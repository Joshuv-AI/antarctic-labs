Reproductive health care experiences and rights of displaced Afghan women, and intersecting factors influencing outcomes: a scoping review
Authors: Donya Eghrari, Nilab Hamidi, Nicola Creagh, Anna Ziersch, Lucio Naccarella
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundForcibly displaced Afghan women face a high risk of poor reproductive outcomes from preexisting and post-settlement inequalities intensified by displacement and inadequate culturally responsive care. Understanding Afghan women’s pre- and post-settlement reproductive issues and experiences, across periods of political and social transition, can highlight needs and improve reproductive outcomes.ObjectiveThis scoping review provides a synthesis of the prevalent reproductive health issues among externally displaced Afghan women residing in humanitarian or resettlement settings. It describes the factors impacting reproductive issues and outcomes as well as interventions employed to address gaps.MethodsWe conducted a search of the Medline (OVID)/EMBASE, CINAHL, Web of Science, Scopus, and Global Health (OVID/CABI) databases from 2000 to 2024 to identify full-text studies in any language reporting on matters related to or influencing the reproductive health of externally displaced Afghan women. Systematic and other reviews and studies focusing merely on sexual health were excluded. We used thematic synthesis with an inductive approach to analyse and report the findings.ResultsForty-two studies from eight countries were included in the data extraction. Reproductive health issues, and factors influencing reproductive outcomes, varied across humanitarian and resettlement settings, with some overlap. Reproductive health topics broadly related to family planning and maternal and neonatal outcomes. Facilitators and barriers to reproductive outcomes included linguistic and reproductive literacy, finances, cultural and religious norms, social connections, legal status, communication with health professionals, and justice in service provision. Reproductive outcomes were worse in humanitarian settings than in resettlement countries. However, despite better service availability in resettlement settings, navigating and accessing services is hindered by personal, cultural, and structural barriers.ConclusionAdvancing Afghan women’s reproductive rights requires a multidimensional strategy that prioritises women’s autonomy. This necessitates a deeper understanding of the reproductive issues women experience, and the intersecting factors influencing their reproductive decision-making and service access. Drawing upon the synthesis of findings, we advocate for (a) routine collection of disaggregated reproductive data by country of origin, migration status, and other critical data such as literacy levels; (b) codesigned, culturally safe care models; (c) reproductive and health literacy programs; (d) gender-transformative education and interventions that include men and boys; and (e) workforce training and preparedness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12978-026-02396-9.Keywords: Afghan women, Refugee, Reproductive health, Reproductive right, Determinants, Externally displaced, Settlement

Introduction

Access to quality and equitable reproductive healthcare is a global health priority through Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) 3, 5, and 10 [1, 2]. However, global updates from 68 countries show that between 2007 and 2022, only 56% of married or in-union women were able to make their own sexual and reproductive health decisions [3], noting that reproductive outcomes can be poorer in humanitarian settings.

In Afghanistan, women are adversely impacted by significant violations of their rights, poverty and illiteracy associated with child marriage, and low societal status [4, 5], particularly when the Taliban has been in power [6] prior to 2001, and post 2021, resulting in greater waves of migration. Women have faced inequitable access to reproductive services and antenatal care, which is worse for the estimated 77% who live rurally, particularly during periods of exposure to conflict [7]. Among many factors, a shortage of female healthcare providers due to disruption of women’s education, restrictions on visits by male healthcare providers and reduced public participation has significantly impacted women’s autonomy and healthcare outcomes [8]. These factors have led to unwanted pregnancies, unsafe abortions, pregnancy and childbirth complications, and high maternal and child mortality [9, 10]. In 2001 at the fall of Taliban, Afghanistan’s health system was fragile and there were poor overall health outcomes [11]. For two decades, national and international healthcare prioritisation led to some improvements in child and maternal mortality, skilled delivery and family planning [12]. However, female healthcare worker safety, access to, and quality of reproductive healthcare services, and maternal and child outcomes were compromised again with the Taliban’s return in 2021 [13].

Owing to decades of conflict and instability in Afghanistan, Afghan women and children are among the largest internationally displaced groups [14]. They are forced to migrate to neighbouring countries, such as Iran and Pakistan, or further away, to countries such as Australia, Canada, and the United States [14]. Forced migration and disrupted healthcare and education, gender-based oppression, and sociocultural differences [15, 16] may impact reproductive health outcomes post-migration. Given the changing migration patterns, support systems and services in transition and resettlement countries have evolved over time. Yet, healthcare access and entitlements can be limited and vary depending on legal documentation, at times further compounded by discrimination and racism [17].

While Afghan women’s reproductive outcomes can improve outside of Afghanistan, there can be a disparity between their outcomes and those of other local women post-settlement [15, 18]. The World Health Organisation (2016) [19] has outlined key indicators and targets for reproductive, maternal and newborn health, and they advocate for the disaggregation of national databases for population groups to help monitor, and enhance equitable service access and resource allocation. The World Bank [20] reported that the burden associated with maternal factors such as poor maternal health, pregnancy complications and frequent pregnancies

“drains women’s productive energy, jeopardises their income-earning capacity, and contributes to their poverty. Unwanted or ill-timed pregnancies can interfere with women’s social and economic activities and cause emotional and economic hardship not only to women but also to their families”.

The strengths of Afghan women can significantly contribute to the societies of their resettlement countries. They deserve successful integration and optimal overall outcomes; however, many barriers can limit their progress. The failure to address the specific reproductive priorities of Afghan refugee women by treating them as a homogeneous group with other refugee or non-refugee populations can impede their progress toward achieving quality reproductive healthcare, as outlined in the SDGs.

Globally, a number of studies have documented reproductive issues and barriers for Afghan women; however, there is a need to identify and synthesise patterns across settlement settings and understand how context mitigates or exacerbates challenges. This will allow for an effective and targeted response, focusing on disaggregated data that distinguish the experiences of Afghan refugees from those of the local population, and those of migrants and other refugee groups. In addition to mapping prevalent reproductive health issues across settings, this scoping review aims to synthesise evidence and examines the intersecting social, cultural, legal, and health system factors shaping reproductive outcomes and access to care in diverse pre- and post-settlement settings outside Afghanistan.

Methods

Protocol and registration

Given the need to determine the scope and breadth of evidence around reproductive issues of displaced Afghan women across multiple countries, we conducted a scoping review, aligning the reporting criteria with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis extension for scoping reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [21]. The research protocol was registered with the Open Science Foundation (DOI: https://osf.io/dzst6). An advisory group from the community was consulted regarding the appropriateness of the research protocol and the method. The members of the group are experienced researchers and healthcare providers with experience in service provision and policy in Australia and Afghanistan.

Eligibility criteria and search strategy

This review included peer-reviewed studies from the Medline (OVID)/EMBASE, CINAHL, Web of Science, Scopus, and Global Health (OVID/CABI) databases. The initial search covered publications in all languages up to February 2024, followed by an updated database search in November 2024. The initial search was not restricted to a single timeframe due to uncertainty regarding the availability of the literature. However, after screening, papers published prior to 2000 were excluded because of their lack of recency. Given the extended timeframe of the included literature (2000–2024), we acknowledge the potential influence of social and political context in Afghanistan and host countries. While a formal temporal stratification is not included, we were attentive to shifts in migration patterns and service availability and their impact on externally displaced Afghan women when interpreting findings.

Several papers were excluded during the screening phase for not reporting their findings in a disaggregated manner by country of origin, not distinguishing between refugee or migrant groups, and not specifying reproductive issues beyond the umbrella terms ‘sexual or reproductive health’. Papers were included if they focused on matters related to or influencing the reproductive health of externally displaced Afghan women. The inclusion and exclusion criteria are detailed in Table 1 (Study eligibility criteria). Reproductive health is broadly defined by the International Conference on Population and Development (ICPD, 1994) as a state of complete physical, mental and social wellbeing in all matters relating to the reproductive system, including the capability to reproduce, freedom to decide if and when to do so, and the right to access information and services to support these decisions [22, 23]. The focus of this review was those aspects of reproductive health (Table 1) that prioritises pregnancy-related outcomes, family planning, fertility, contraception, and access to reproductive healthcare services among displaced Afghan women. In this review gender-based violence and sexually health were not treated as primary outcome domains unless directly examined in relation to reproductive decision-making, pregnancy outcomes, or maternal care.

The search strategy was developed by the research team and a librarian and included key concepts and related terms for “Afghan”, “women”, “refugee”, and “reproductive health”. Studies that reported outcomes for the baby were included if they were closely related to the reproductive experience within the perinatal period (generally within six weeks of delivery) [24].

Screening, data extraction and synthesis

The search results were exported to Covidence for screening. The screening protocol and data chart were developed by the research team. Two reviewers, from a group of three researchers (DE, NC, NH), independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts, and extracted data from each of the included studies. Disagreements were resolved by consensus among DE, AZ, LN, NC, and NH. Papers were excluded because of an incorrect study design, a lack of data disaggregation (by language group or refugee status), an incorrect participant/population group, an incorrect outcome, and unavailability of the full text (Fig. 1. PRISMA Flow Chart). Systematic and other review papers were excluded, but their reference lists were screened for primary data sources. A total of 275 search results were screened using Covidence [25]. A total of 77 duplicates were identified. The titles and abstracts of 198 papers were screened and 90 papers were excluded. A full-text screening of 108 papers resulted in the exclusion of 66 studies. A total of 42 papers were included in the review and underwent data extraction. One of the included papers was written in Farsi, the language spoken in Iran and understood by many Afghan refugees, while the others were written in English. Three studies from Dadras and colleagues (2020, 2020, and 2021) presented segments of data derived from the same comprehensive dataset.

No formal quality appraisal was performed as this was a scoping review. Data on study characteristics, participant information, concepts (reproductive health issues, prevalence, factors, and interventions), and contexts (humanitarian and resettlement settings) were extracted. We summarised the data by publication year, setting, study design, reproductive issue and its prevalence, as well as factors influencing reproductive health and outcomes, and future recommendations (Additional File 1). We synthesised the extracted data into tables and used thematic synthesis with an inductive approach to examine patterns, intersectionality, similarities and differences and reported findings.

Results

The majority of studies (n = 26) were conducted in the context of a humanitarian settings, which we categorised as settings of temporary, transitional, or semi-permanent refugee placement, some of which offered restricted rights and entitlements to Afghan refugees. This included Iran, Pakistan, Turkey, Germany and Serbia. In this review, we have included both Germany and Serbia as humanitarian settings because of the transitory nature of Afghan women’s mobility through these countries and their residence in refugee shelters and camps. While there are growing safe and legal routes for forcibly displaced Afghans, Serbia is likely to remain a transit country for those making unauthorised journeys [26].Earlier studies with Afghan refugee settlements in humanitarian settings included thousands of refugees in refugee villages [27],, tribal areas or local settlements [28], or camps [29], and a later study from Germany reporting refugee shelters [30]. The reproductive needs of refugee women were served differently in these humanitarian settings. Some healthcare models include Basic Health Units providing preventative and curative prenatal and postnatal care, and centralised Emergency Obstetric Care facilities for specialised care ; [27, 29], public hospitals[31], or non-governmental organisations [28, 29, 32]. The study from Germany [30] reported that complementary family planning services, including all forms of contraception, were free of charge.

The remaining studies (n = 16) were conducted in the context of a resettlement setting, which we defined as well-resourced countries, where Afghan refugee women had the opportunity to settle in various neighbourhoods and have access to reproductive services and primary healthcare similar to that of the local population. This included Australia, Canada and the United States. In 2020s, in the United States (33–37], refugees were eligible for additional short-term resettlement and health care benefits, and Medicaid, which is a low-cost health government-funded health insurance with variable eligibility across the states. Australia and Canada had similar healthcare systems with government funded primary health service coverage, as well as extra settlement services provided through refugee organisations or the government. Two studies [38, 39] were multinational across Australia and Canada with geographical, economic, and political similarities, and comparable migrant and refugee populations.

Studies used different methods for data collection, including quantitative surveys and health record analysis (n = 18), qualitative interviews and focus groups (n = 20), and mixed methods (n = 4). An ethnographic approach was incorporated into one study [40]. In qualitative terms, Afghan women were the key informants in 20 of the studies, while family members, healthcare and service providers informed 11 of the studies. The key features of the included studies are listed in Additional File 1.

Findings related to reproductive health issues

Overview of reproductive health issues

The reproductive issues identified covered maternal issues, family planning, and outcomes for the baby. The issues are highlighted in Additional File 1. While there was some overlap, the reproductive-health issues examined in the included studies were largely specific to the humanitarian or resettlement settings. We have therefore discussed the issues in more detail below by setting.

Reproductive issues in humanitarian settings

Of the 26 studies based in humanitarian settings, most were from Iran (n = 11) and Pakistan (n = 9), with a few from Turkey (n = 3), Serbia (n = 2), and Germany (n = 1). Recent studies from Serbia [26, 41], discussed in later sections, reported on health system preparedness in relation to understanding and responding to the movement of refugees and their reproductive and obstetric needs.

Across the timeline of the review, issues of mortality, such as maternal mortality and near-miss morbidities [28, 29, 42, 43], neonatal mortality [28, 29, 43], stillbirths, miscarriages and abortions [28, 31, 38, 39, 44–50], were discussed mainly in humanitarian settings, both quantitatively and qualitatively. While there were evidences of some improvement in maternal and infant mortality outcomes in the host countries, such as Pakistan, relative to those in Afghanistan [28], Afghan women experienced similar or higher maternal and neonatal mortality compared with host populations, with evidence of improvement over time where targeted maternal health interventions were implemented [32].

Pregnancy loss and infertility, encompassing miscarriage, termination or unsafe abortion, and stillbirth, was a recurrent finding across humanitarian settings, though prevalence varied by country and study design [27, 31, 44, 45, 47–50]. These studies suggested higher rates of delivering low birth weight babies, pregnancy loss, termination/abortion and short interpregnancy intervals at an older age among Afghan women than host populations. A recent study [50] suggested qualitative insights from Afghan women and noted that abortions, in the absence of a health problem, were considered a sin and resulted in women terminating unwanted pregnancies using their own methods, such as abdominal compression, heavy housework, jumping from heights, and consuming herbal medicine. A 2024 study from Iran [51], suggested higher gravidity, reports of pre-eclampsia, and higher levels of alkaline phosphate for Afghan women than Iranian women, where abnormally raised levels of alkaline phosphatase during pregnancy are associated with placental insufficiency, intrauterine growth restriction, low birth weight and the possibility of preterm delivery [52].

A greater number of children in Afghan families was both a celebratory element of culture [44] and a manifestation of low autonomy around birth control and spacing, as described below. Studies in humanitarian settings [31, 45, 48] generally reported early marriage and teenage pregnancy, with some mothers being as young as 12. High fertility rates were reported among Afghan families[49, 53], sometimes higher than the local population [40, 54], with some indicating that women continued childbearing until the birth of a boy [40, 44, 50] as some believed that it was important to have a son. Fertility was quantified by a few studies [28, 30, 43] (Additional File 1), that used different measurement units; hence, it was not easy to draw comparisons.

A consistent theme reported across studies in humanitarian settings was low knowledge and utilisation of contraceptive use, as well as its limited availability. Over a the twenty-one year period during which Afghanistan was liberated from Taliban’s control, studies reported similarly high rates of contraception underutilisation for externally displaced Afghan women [44, 48], with modern contraception being considered inappropriate [54]. Even when contraception was freely accessible in recent times [30], Afghan women, similar to other refugee women, had unmet family planning needs. Where subsidised services were available [53], personal barriers such as religious beliefs seemed to have less of an impact on family planning, contraception uptake improved, and women were more likely to have discussed family size with their husbands.

Four studies conducted in humanitarian settings [40, 44, 49, 53] reported a preference for oral pills, injectable contraceptive forms, or tubal ligation, particularly in conjunction with subsidised healthcare, with Tober and colleagues (2006) offering qualitative insight highlighting the preference for methods that are longer lasting or enable women to hide contraception use from husbands. They highlighted that despite the availability of modern methods, women may not feel empowered to use them consistently, highlighting the lack of skills and improper use of contraception resulting in unwanted pregnancies [44, 45]. Reduced contraceptive use and unintended pregnancies were also associated with recent intimate partner violence [45]. Fear was another driver for higher fertility rates among Hazara Afghan women as some women feared their husbands marrying a second wife if they did not bring more babies; others bore children to alleviate the impact of the war and loss of a child on their husbands, with growing reliance on permanent methods once desired family size, often defined by the birth of a son, was achieved.

Overall, maternal-newborn outcomes and family planning remain critical issues in humanitarian settings and are closely linked to the availability of services and women’s social position and literacy. While some reproductive health issues can lead to adverse outcomes, they are also preventable. The determinants of maternal and newborn outcomes are further discussed in Sect. 3.3.

Reproductive issues in resettlement settings

The 16 studies identifying reproductive health issues in resettlement settings were mostly qualitative in nature (n = 13), with 3 being quantitative and one mixed method. These studies focused mainly on service utilisation and care experiences rather than on quantitative data on mortality or loss of life. No studies in resettlement settings reported maternal or neonatal death rates, implying that these are either rare events in high-resource health systems or are underreported. Instead, in resettlement settings, reproductive concerns shifted from survival-related outcomes to challenges of service access and navigation, communication gaps and psychosocial wellbeing.

Recent studies in the United States and Australia reported qualitative pregnancy-related experiences of isolation, gendered preferences, and life navigation skills and well as system-level cultural and linguistic gaps [35–37, 55]. Where comparisons were made between the local population [55] or other refugee women [34], studies noted diversity of outcomes, such as higher rates of teenage pregnancies and delayed antenatal care, similar pregnancy-related hospitalisation rates to women from Bhutan, Iran and Syria, or lower rates of low birth weight and caesarians. A report of high blood lead levels due to pre-migration exposure in the United States [33], prompted recommendations for universal screening of all Afghan women upon resettlement to mitigate the potential impacts on pregnancy and infant health.

A common theme from studies in resettlement settings was the social and mental health of Afghan women around the time of pregnancy and childbirth [37, 56–58]. Russo and colleagues (2011) from Australia shared qualitative data from Afghan women and reported that a lack of traditional postpartum support led to feelings of isolation, loneliness, and depression after birth. Over a decade later, after a wave of migration in 2021, there was advancement in introducing routine screening with culturally tailored tools that assist in the diagnosis of depression and post-traumatic disorders, discussed in Sect. 3.4.

Overall, the reviewed studies indicate that while the immediate survival outcomes for mothers and babies are usually favourable in resettlement countries, the focus shifts to addressing pre-settlement issues and post-settlement inequities in service provision, ensuring improved maternal healthcare access so that reproductive emergencies can be promptly identified and managed. While services for women’s physical health are a priority, resettlement settings reveal an opportunity to improve service provision in addressing mental health and psychosocial stressors around reproduction.

Factors influencing reproductive health outcomes

The factors influencing the reproductive health of Afghan women are discussed below and are detailed in Additional File 1. Many factors overlap at the intersection of personal, cultural, service, and structural levels. Across both settings, Afghan refugee women experience significant barriers that limit their autonomy and access to services.

The ability to use contraception and family planning, and seek antenatal care was hampered by illiteracy and limited reproductive literacy, including both knowledge and skills, across all settings [26, 28, 36–40, 42, 44–46, 54, 56, 59, 60]. A number of studies across both settlement and humanitarian settings, including two that used the Knowledge-Attitude-Practice (KAP) survey [44, 53], linked low knowledge and cultural attitudes to low reproductive care-seeking and utilisation. In addition to knowledge and attitudes, Dadras and colleagues (2020) reported, through a cross-sectional survey in Tehran, Iran, that antenatal care underutilisation was associated with poor quality and inaccessible service; however, older age, higher education and family income, legal registration and length of stay positively impacted antenatal care utilisation. Celik and colleagues (2019) conducted a retrospective analysis of a tertiary hospital medical records in Turkey and reported that 43.8% of Afghan refugee women had at least one antenatal care, which was greater than their Syrian and Iraqi counterparts. While the rest of the data from this study were not disaggregated by country of birth, all refugee women, including Afghans, required fewer cesarean sections, similar to a resettlement setting [55], and greater variability in gestational age than Turkish women did, but their infant mortality rates did not differ significantly. An encouraging example by Purdin and colleagues (2009) in Pakistan, demonstrated that a complete prenatal care coverage, discussed in Sect. 3.4, defined in this study as three or more visits, increased from 49% in 2000 to 90% in 2006, and postnatal coverage within 72 h of birth tripled within the same period.

In studies from Iran [47] and Serbia [26], where Afghan asylum seekers were often in transit or lacked legal status, access to services was sometimes inconsistent due to fear of deportation. A lack of documentation often resulted in pregnancies not being registered with health authorities, potentially resulting in unmonitored pregnancies when health insurance was deemed unaffordable [17, 61]. Even in resettlement settings such as the United States, a lack of life navigation skills, a lack of awareness of entitlements and insurance coverage, and financial stress, were barriers to service utilisation [36, 37]. Much-needed childbirth education classes were offered in Australia at an extra fee[60] which hindered attendance. Similarly, financial constraints and food insecurity were predictors of low access to nutritious food and anaemia [47, 55], particularly during the COVID-19 pandemic[62]. While Shaikh and colleagues (2004) highlighted service unavailability in refugee settlements in Pakistan, studies from Turkey and Germany [30, 50, 63] reported unmet needs for family planning and pregnancy services despite free access to services.

Some studies highlighted the important role played by the partners or male family members, both as barriers (discussed in Sect. 3.3.2) or facilitators; [29, 58], particularly reporting better uptake of services when men were trained and had better knowledge of pregnancy danger signs. Studies from the United States and Australia, further discussed below, reported qualitative accounts of women being open to the idea of birth control; however, their decisions were impacted by biomedical and gender power dynamics. Yeo and colleagues (2024) reported that women’s willingness to use birth control was dominated by men and other family members, saying that “it’s not a woman’s decision usually, it is a man, mother-in-law, and father-in-law’s decision—how many grandchildren they want and how many children the husband wants”. Russo and colleagues (2020) reported that some Afghan women struggled with the side effects of hormonal contraceptives and at the same time had difficulty negotiating condom use with their husbands.

Language, interpreters and communication

Language and communication barriers emerged as critical determinants of reproductive healthcare access and quality for Afghan refugee women across both humanitarian and resettlement settings. Proficiency in the host country’s language frequently impedes women’s ability to communicate with healthcare providers, understand medical advice, navigate complex health systems, and delay care seeking. The impact on reproductive outcomes was sometimes exacerbated by discrimination, legal status or costs [61] as discussed previously. Effective communication could also play a vital role in raising awareness of danger signs [28, 29].

In humanitarian settings, some women feared accessing interpreters and services due to a lack of legal status and fear of deportation[17]. In resettlement settings such as Australia, the United States, and Germany, communication challenges persisted despite formal access to interpreter services. Studies [35, 37, 39, 55, 56, 64, 65] reported the communication challenges faced by Afghan women, and inconsistent use of accredited interpreters, the unavailability or limited use of dialect-appropriate and female interpreters, and the limited use of visual resources as barriers to optimal outcomes. When support was provided by health professionals and interpreters [36, 37], women reported a satisfactory experience. One woman referred to peer support as being more effective than written information, saying

“groups would be the best. Some ladies are not educated and will not understand leaflets and booklets”[39].

Women were found to rely on their husbands or family members for translation at times [36, 37, 56, 58]. This was reported as both a facilitator and a potential compromise of confidentiality and autonomy in reproductive decision-making. Short appointments and a lack of continuity of care with the same health professionals at each antenatal visit [56] made the experience more challenging, whereas community-based maternal and child health nurses had more scope to build relationships with families and work with interpreters, better responding to needs. Language and communication barriers present challenges not only for individuals but also for at the system level, necessitating systemic interventions to enhance equitable access.

Norms that may conflict with human rights

While some determinants of optimal reproductive outcomes celebrate the diversity of culture and faith, others may be the accepted norms that lead to the violation of women’s reproductive and human rights. A number of studies in this review reported on sociocultural factors that negatively impacted women’s reproductive rights. Intimate partner and family violence across all settings [45, 47, 50], family members limiting or delaying access to care [42, 50, 54], acceptability of early marriage and pregnancy [44, 45, 48, 49], opposition to utilisation of contraception leading to no or hidden use [17, 38, 40, 44–46, 50, 54], and pressures imposed on women for a male offspring [40, 44, 50] all signify beliefs and practices that may have become the norm but conflict with human and reproductive rights. Shaikh and colleagues (2004) has particularly touched on norms and the practice of parda among the Pashtun group in settlements in Pakistan, where the use of modern methods of contraception is considered inappropriate and where strict observances mean that females are unable to leave home to reach educational and health facilities. These strict patriarchal norms adversely affect women’s autonomy and are associated with terminated or unwanted pregnancies [45]. Unmet needs for family planning can be exacerbated by beliefs and misunderstandings, even post-settlement, such as “To reject having a baby is forbidden in Islam” or the use of contraception “may affect the next generation” or “it creates a whole lot of other damages” [38].

Additionally, there were elements of fear that seemed to pressure women into making certain reproductive choices. These ranged from making the decision not to seek care due to fear of contracting COVID-19 [62], to not utilising contraception in fear of husbands’ reactions, as well as consenting to more children in fear of husbands re-marrying [40], worsened by the stigma associated with mental illness [57].

Refugee camp conditions and hygienic practices

Although no direct associations between refugee camp conditions and hygiene practices were explicitly established in these studies with the reported cases of infertility and reproductive tract infections, Khan and colleagues (2022) and Balsara and colleagues (2010) reported the hygiene practices of Afghan women in Pakistan, with 81.2% of respondents reporting the use of old clothes to clean the vaginal area. Marsh and colleagues (2002) also explored birthing and newborn care practices among Afghan women in Haripur, Pakistan, in comparison with Pakistani women, and noted that while both groups needed to improve immediate newborn care, clean delivery and paternal involvement, Afghan caregivers showed better use of services and certain household practices than their Pakistani counterparts did, which is consistent with the duration of engagement with Save the Children’s presence. These practices included mothers-in-law placing new plastic sheets and clean cotton sheets on the floor for delivery, washing hands with soap before and after cutting the cord, and using tools such as clean razors for cutting the umbilical cord. Some transition refugee camp settings in Serbia [26] were also reported to suffer from poor hygiene and overcrowding; however, despite all challenges, some women thought that it was still better for pregnant women to be in camps, where additional support, such as transportation to hospitals, was provided.

Role of providers and health system preparedness

Studies in both humanitarian and resettlement settings identified areas for improvement, generally around service availability, and service accessibility. Healthcare and service providers’ understanding of cultural realities and the displacement journey were reported to be important facilitators of improved compassionate service for Afghan women [26, 56]. In Serbia in particular [26], providers felt that Serbia’s health system is underfunded and not prepared to serve the needs of refugees. Countries such as Australia have quality services; however, they may not always be readily known or accessible to Afghan women, particularly with increasing opportunities for Afghan men and women [46] who are rethinking their family-planning beliefs and practices.

Intersecting the impact of the provider role were women and community practices, perceptions, and preferences that influence the extent to which support and care were sought. Rakhshani and colleagues (2002) and Tober and colleagues (2006) discussed the importance of having a son, which may prevent a woman from seeking family planning support until she has given birth to a male child. Similarly, beliefs about breastfeeding acting as natural contraception [44, 61] and the attitude of seeking care when one is sick [37] impact timely service utilisation. Russo and colleagues (2020) described the complexities women faced in trying to navigate their preferences, reporting that experiences of side effects with hormonal contraception, compounded by husbands’ refusal to use condoms resulted in inconsistent contraceptive use, resulting in unintended pregnancies.

In addition to improving the clinical and communication challenges facing health services and providers, the attitudes of providers and discriminatory systemic barriers need to be addressed. Mohammadi and colleagues (2017) from Iran qualitatively reported mistreatment, discrimination and disparity in care due to Afghan identity, exacerbated by financial means and low literacy, with some hospitals not admitting Afghans even when they had official documentation. The cost of care also seemed to impact outcomes, as shown by Raheel and colleagues (2012), who reported an association between contraceptive use and awareness of family planning with subsidised healthcare in humanitarian settings, advocated for readiness and increased public and private health sector partnerships to provide healthcare subsidies. In resettlement settings, women generally expressed better satisfaction with the quality of care, attitudes of providers, facilities, and affordability in comparison to Afghanistan, with Yeo and colleagues (2024) reporting that women could notice a significant contrast, expressing that “the difference is like the distance between the sky and the earth.”

Association of parity with social and emotional wellbeing

While the strength of the Afghan family lies in its appreciation of the richness many children can bring to the family [40, 44], the conditions under which a woman commits to raising her children can impact her social and emotional wellbeing. Strong devotion to one’s faith, deep connections with children, the availability of childcare, supportive husbands, friendship networks and care providers [36, 57, 58] were protective of women’s social and emotional wellbeing. A lack of childcare, on the other hand, was reported as a barrier to accessing services and was associated with unmet reproductive needs [45, 47, 61].

Across all settings, raising children while separated from family and culture [29, 40, 47, 57] was frequently reported to impact women’s social and emotional wellbeing. Women often missed the support of their extended family and the customary “collective caring” practices of their culture, particularly during the 10 to 40-day rest period post-delivery[57] resulting in difficult postpartum adjustment. Russo and colleagues (2015) further elaborated that despite experiencing depressive symptoms, women were often reluctant to seek professional help because of stigma surrounding mental illness and a lack of culturally sensitive services. While partner support was appreciated as a facilitator of mental and social wellbeing, it was difficult to negotiate. Religion, bonding with their baby, education, and forming new friendships were other factors reported in this Australian study as important coping mechanisms that improved women’s emotional wellbeing.

Strategies for improving outcomes

While the majority of studies highlighted reproductive issues and barriers to optimal reproductive healthcare, some have discussed different approaches or strategies that have been employed to help improve outcomes for Afghan women, as discussed below.

Different humanitarian care models were discussed in Sect. 3.1. Improving access to emergency obstetric care in Pakistan, particularly around danger signs [29], and reproductive health promotion and education in Iran [66], had successfully increased safe delivery and pre- and postnatal coverage in these countries. With the longer-term presence of programs, such as those offered by Save the Children in Pakistan [32], Afghan caregivers demonstrated better outcomes than their Pakistani counterparts did, as discussed in the previous sections, noting that outcomes could still improve for both populations in areas such as clean delivery, newborn thermal control and fathers’ involvement. One study reported on doubling of contraceptive uptake by women in Pakistan due to the provision of healthcare subsidies [53]. Purdin and colleagues (2009) reported nearly to 65% reduction in maternal mortality over a four-year period after an evaluation of a health program incorporating emergency obstetric care, reproductive health education, and training of health workers, teachers, and religious leaders. Given the home delivery preferences of Afghan families in Sirjan, Iran, a health promotional educational intervention promoting behaviours related to safe delivery was implemented and evaluated through a self-administered questionnaire, and Sadeghi and colleagues (2017) reported a resultant improvement in safe delivery behaviours from 23% to 57%.

Owing to health and service providers’ low awareness of the displacement context, a study from Serbia [41] reported on a program that helped raise providers’ cultural and contextual awareness of Afghan women’s realities through visual elicitation using a webcomic co-produced with Afghan refugee women. The improvement in awareness was qualitatively investigated, with one health professional stating “Thank you for educating me on this subject, I wasn’t even aware of how unfamiliar I was with what these women go through”. In Australia, two studies explored the screening and diagnostic properties of existing tools: the Dari translation of the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale [67] and the Harvard Trauma Questionnaire [68]. The use and integration of these screening tools was recommended for maternal visits given their ability to detect depression, anxiety and post-traumatic stress disorder.

Generalisibility of studies

This review revealed that some determinants are context-specific and not generalisable to other settings, such as access and utilisation challenges due to legal status [17, 26], or experiences of discrimination and abuse by providers [17, 41, 42, 50]. However, the emotional impact of such matters can lead to fears of poor treatment or trust issues post settlement, unless rights and entitlements are clarified. The impact of some structurally driven personal determinants can be long-term and extend beyond the settlement setting. For example, limited or no education and literacy [17, 26, 36, 37, 39, 40, 42, 44, 45, 47, 54, 56, 60], low knowledge of reproductive health and services [38, 39, 59], and poor life navigation skills [37] can have significant long-term impacts on reproductive health information and service access and navigation even when services are available. Importantly in all settings, poor maternal and neonatal outcomes are preventable and can improve by responding to context-specific determinants with targeted interventions.

Discussion

This review aimed to synthesise the available evidence concerning common reproductive issues, as well as the determinants of reproductive outcomes, for Afghan women who have been externally displaced. We present a synthesis of the key findings from forty-two peer-reviewed studies, including some limitations, and offer recommendations for future research, policy, and practice. Our synthesis highlights that reproductive health issues for externally displaced Afghan women are multifaceted, related to interpersonal, health system and sociocultural levels, as described by other reviews [2], and vary across settings, with some overlaps.

Nature and severity of reproductive issues.

There were similarities and differences between humanitarian and resettlement settings in terms of the types of reproductive issues reported. In humanitarian settings, structural barriers such as legal status and costly, inadequate and discriminatory services contributed to poor outcomes such as high maternal and neonatal mortality, low birth weight, increased fertility and unintended pregnancies. Low knowledge of contraception and family planning, low willingness of husbands in the utilisation of preventative measures, and a lack of skills in using contraception by women or their husbands were commonly reported as factors contributing to pregnancy outcomes. These results reflect those of Bathobakae and colleagues (2019) and Hossain and colleagues (2022) who also found similar reproductive outcomes for Rohingyan, Syrian and Sudanese refugee women [69, 70]. Conversely, our findings from both settings [46, 53], consistent with those of Uwigiciro and colleagues (2025) [71], indicated that elimination of financial barriers to service utilisation led to improvement where many couples adopted family planning and contraception despite prevailing belief that such practices are contrary to their religious beliefs. A multicountry assessment by Tanabe and colleagues (2017), however, has suggested that the availability of free services does not guarantee utilisation of services if accessibility barriers persist, such as distance, cost of transport, religious opposition and provider biases [72].

In resettlement settings, quality care is more readily available, women give birth in safer childbirth conditions, and generally have better maternal outcomes [37]; however, challenges such as communication barriers, stigma, and low reproductive health literacy contribute to underutilisation, late or inadequate care [73]. In keeping with our findings, Stirling-Cameron and colleagues (2024) has also reported that refugee and asylum-seeker women have greater unmet needs for contraception and greater use of abortion care in high-income countries than women born locally do. Similarly, miscarriages, stillbirths and perinatal deaths remain greater for refugee women than their non-refugee counterparts [74], which is attributable to factors such as late or no attendance at antenatal care appointments [74], or consanguineous marriage[75, 76]. The studies from resettlement settings in our review, however, did not report maternal or neonatal loss of life.

Screening programs for environmental factors such as blood lead levels, which result in delayed infant neurodevelopment, gestational hypertension, spontaneous abortion, low birth weight, and transmission via breast milk [33, 77], are not standardised globally. Lead toxicity can contribute to microcytosis and anaemia [78] with implications for reproductive and newborn health post-settlement. Tober and colleagues (2011) reported that the United States [79] offers screening upon the arrival of Afghan refugees; however, Australian refugee guidelines do not include a routine blood lead screening before or after arrival [78, 80], a challenge that equally impacts Karen refugees in Australia according to Paxton and colleagues (2012). Although reducing environmental exposure can mitigate pre-settlement lead poisoning over time [81], resettlement in a high-income country does not eliminate this issue. The risk of lead poisoning exposure may persist in resettlement countries due to environmental inequalities and poor housing conditions [82].

Access to and navigation of services

In humanitarian settings, legal status and entitlements, cost, discriminatory attitudes and inaccessible services further compounded access issues. Resettlement settings have reported mainly challenges related to service utilisation and navigation, and a lack of culturally safe and acceptable care. Across both settings, some barriers were similar to those identified for other migrant, displaced and refugee women [83]. Among these, communication and language barriers, and patriarchal norms limiting women’s autonomy were frequently reported. Education level is a known predictor of maternal outcomes and is linked to the risk of maternal mortality [84]. For Afghan women, low reproductive literacy reflects low levels of literacy and life navigation skills, resulting from decades of human rights violations. The assumptions that Afghan women’s education and reproductive literacy automatically improve post-resettlement, especially given the challenges many face in effectively learning English [85], may lead to the overlooking of barriers and widening gaps in unmet needs.

In this review, translation, particularly dialect-appropriate interpretation [60], was frequently identified as a barrier to communication, which aligns with findings of other studies [83, 86]. Afghanistan is a nation characterised by a multitude of dialects; however, interpreters and translated materials in resettlement countries predominantly address more widely spoken dialects. Moreover, proficiency in speaking the more common dialects, such as Dari, does not necessarily imply the ability to read it. Other studies [83, 87, 88] have shown that digital, visual and in-person strategies can act as facilitators of improved access. These include digital and telehealth solutions for translation, consultations and information sources, as well as in-person health promotion education, with in-language and in-dialect availability in the case of Afghan women.

Intersecting with service provision are personal and community beliefs, attitudes and practices that can influence utilisation. While there are inconsistencies in reports of the association between intimate partner violence and unmet family planning needs [89], women and their partners can benefit from having accessible information when negotiating family planning and contraception use, such as during breastfeeding. In this regard, it is essential to empower women and the community to examine their pre-settlement beliefs and practices against available and recent evidence.

Mental health, social connections and rights

Social isolation, the loss of traditional support networks, and postpartum mental health issues, such as depression due to social disconnection, were reported across the studies in this review. Like other refugee populations [90], Afghan women in resettlement settings reported higher rates of perinatal mental health concerns than non-refugee women did. On the other hand, we found a number of facilitators in our included studies that facilitated better social and emotional wellbeing resulting in better reproductive outcomes, including strong bonds with the child, the availability of childcare, and supportive roles played by general practitioners, friends and social networks.

The United Nations reports that at least 1 in every 5 refugee or displaced women are thought to have experienced sexual violence [91]. Human rights abuses [92], intimate partner violence [93, 94] and poor post-migration socioeconomic status [95], are all likely contributors to the high risk of perinatal depression and other mental health problems for refugee women. An awareness of reproductive rights, higher status and societal respect for women post-migration can lead to frustration and tension within the Afghan family (Rintoul, 2010). This review aligns with the findings of other studies in that stigma and cultural beliefs pressured women to deliver alone and discourage discussion or help-seeking for reproductive or mental health issues [36, 96–98], exacerbate reproductive access challenges and prolong the impact on mental wellbeing. Engaging men and boys in sexual and reproductive health and right dialogue and interventions is an important line of action [87, 99], and is often neglected after settlement in a new country. Our review also highlighted that partners’ knowledge of newborn danger signs and their support in reaching and accessing care were predictors of better outcomes. the strategies suggested in some of the included studies for innovative and community-based models for mental health screening through reproductive care visits [47, 57] align with others suggested in the literature [98, 100], particularly given the intersection of mental health stigma, violence and poor reproductive outcomes, in post-settlement settings.

Effective interventions and missed opportunities

There are limited system-level evaluated interventions targeting Afghan women across different settings. However, in line with findings from other studies [83, 101, 102], a number of health system facilitators improved reproductive outcomes for Afghan women in this review. These included access to medication, contraception, interpreters and translated tools and resources; quality subsidised services; and trained and supportive providers who were aware of women’s migration context.

Many poor reproductive outcomes are preventable; hence, not addressing modifiable barriers is a missed opportunity in both resettlement and humanitarian settings. This review revealed that, similar to efforts in Afghanistan, when targeted strategies are implemented in other settings, they contribute to improved outcomes. The United Nations reports that 60% of preventable maternal mortalities take place in humanitarian camp settings [103]. To highlight the impact of targeted intervention, we draw on data from Afghanistan and Pakistan from the 2000s. During a phase of political transition in early 2000, Afghanistan was able to prioritise maternal and reproductive health and reconstruct the health system previously destroyed by the Taliban [104]. A community midwifery education program began training rural midwives, contributing significantly to increased skilled deliveries, and up to a 1273/100,000 reduction in the maternal mortality ratio [104, 105], achieving its SDG targets. Around the same period, the maternal mortality rate for Afghan refugee women in Hangu, Pakistan, decreased from 291 to 102 deaths per 100,000 live births [29]. Purdin and colleagues (2009) reported multidimensional interventions that led to such a reduction in maternal mortality, including the establishment of Emergency Obstetric Care centers, enhanced knowledge of pregnancy danger signs, improved training for community members and primary health care cadre, and improved skilled birth attendance. This is a testimony to the impact of preventative approaches in humanitarian settings and a missed opportunity if not prioritised.

In resettlement settings, on the other hand, quality services generally exist, however they are not always available, accessible or acceptable [73] – all modifiable challenges that can be addressed with health-system preparedness and willingness. When strategies such as integrated mental health screening tools and maternal care visits have been implemented [67, 68], the outcomes have been positive and promising.

Furthermore, primary health care providers’ understanding of the migration journey and an awareness of barriers to reproductive health-seeking [41, 56], particularly cultural and gender-related factors, can enhance service provision for Afghan women. At the primary level, addressing family planning needs, promoting reproductive health, and screening for intimate partner violence may be overlooked because of limited time [2, 87], cultural or clinical competence [106, 107] or emotional burden on the providers [108]. This is an important area in which to invest, as many refugee women may not continue to seek specialist care because of factors such as cost, stigma, community gossip [60] possibly about reproductive competency, and the need for consent from their husbands for reproductive care [38].

Collaborative and equity-oriented approaches

None of the studies in this review adopted a collaborative research or design approach with Afghan women. The participation of refugee women in health program design and research, rather than their proxies, has been reported to be an important equity consideration in community-academic partnerships [109] and overcoming persistent disparities, particularly for underrepresented groups such as Afghan women in high-income countries [110].

Reproductive literacy involves enhancing knowledge, which can motivate health-seeking behaviour, such as acting on danger signs [111], as well as service navigation skills and capacity, underscoring the importance of reproductive health education and effective in-language communication. Vulnerabilities, such as illiteracy and a lack of life navigation skills, compound with other reproductive health risks can further marginalise women displaced as refugees. Addressing intersecting determinants demands equity-based approaches and strategies founded on participatory and co-designed methods that can amplify the voices and needs of Afghan women [36]. Such person-centred care involving women, their community and services has been advocated as a framework for bringing equity to reproductive health [112]. Only one of the studies [41] in our review adopted a collaborative approach to design educative visual resources for health professionals and service providers in partnership with Afghan women.

As previously noted, the limited reporting of mortality and other poor maternal and neonatal outcomes for Afghan women in resettlement settings may suggest that clinical outcomes significantly improve upon resettlement. However, this may also be a reflection of gaps in the collection and monitoring of disaggregated data in higher-income countries. In closing equity gaps, the WHO Global Action Plan on Promoting the Health of Refugees and Migrants (2019–2030) [113] calls upon national systems to monitor and collect disaggregated data by country of origin and migration status. Including women’s voices and insights throughout the prioritisation, planning, implementation and evaluation processes is crucial for eliminating persistent disparities that may otherwise be masked in the absence of participatory methods.

Implications for policy, practice and research

Our findings highlight that equity gaps still exist and shape the displacement journey of Afghan girls and women, impacting their reproductive experience and outcome. The recommendations proposed in this review (Table 3) are therefore informed by the synthesis of findings across the included studies to respond to the recurring patterns and gaps.

Table 3Summary of recommendationsa) Routine collection of disaggregated reproductive data;b) Co-designed, right-based and culturally safe healthcare models;c) Reproductive and health literacy programs;d) Gender-transformative education and interventions that include men and boys; ande) Workforce training and preparedness.

Summary of recommendations

This review found variability in reproductive outcomes, service utilisation, and determinants across settings, with several studies noting an absence of disaggregated Afghan-specific data. We recommend routine collection of disaggregated reproductive health data by country of origin, migration status, and other critical data such as literacy level. The lack of such data in resettlement settings may lead to under-recognition of inequities and limit the development of targeted interventions.

In some countries, standard health screenings, such as immunisation programs, are in place to close pre-arrival gaps. However, reproductive literacy gaps, which can have significant implications for the productivity and health outcomes of women, are not routinely addressed during the settlement phase for those whose pre-arrival reproductive and educational rights have been violated. The reproductive outcomes of Afghan women can have a ripple and generational effect on their family but can be improved at many levels. Strategies around reproductive health and rights literacy, particularly during the settlement phase, will empower women to make evidence-informed reproductive decisions and be supported in carrying them out, free of coercion. They normalise care-seeking behaviour, and inform the community, especially girls and women, about their rights and availability of quality care pathways. We therefore recommend reproductive and health literacy programs during the settlement phase to respond to these gaps.

At the service level, findings from both humanitarian and resettlement settings highlighted gaps in provider preparedness, including limited understanding of displacement contexts, inconsistent use of interpreters, and experiences of discrimination or mistreatment. These challenges hamper a woman’s opportunity for optimal reproductive experience, even when her knowledge motivates her to seek care. Enhancing workforce availability can contribute to the acceptability and accessibility of services and potentially minimise unwarranted emergency service utilisation. Improved services that are culturally and linguistically accessible and appropriate will allow privacy, build trust and meet the expectations of women and their families. Accordingly, we recommend targeted workforce training and preparedness initiatives, including training in culturally responsive care, effective use of interpreters, and trauma-informed reproductive health practice.

The barriers to optimal outcomes noted in this review shed light on the importance of human rights as a lens through which reproductive equity gaps can be viewed and bridged. This is not only an important approach for improving outcomes but also highlights a system-level ethical obligation to respond to the pre-settlement injustices faced by Afghan women and communities. Understanding pre-settlement experiences and practices can impact post-settlement policies, service provisions, and outcomes [9]. This is particularly important because our review revealed several factors reflecting violations of women’s rights across different settings. At the service level, there was poor treatment by providers and poor access to services due to legal status. At the family level, there were reports of intimate partner violence, male and extended family dominance in reproductive decision-making, as well as gendered norms related to fertility, contraception, and care-seeking. If not informed of their rights during the settlement phase, these pre-arrival experiences can negatively shape women’s perceptions of their reproductive rights and their assumptions around services post-settlement. On the other hand, several studies also demonstrated improved outcomes when men were engaged in recognising danger signs or supporting access to care. Accordingly, we recommend gender-transformative interventions [35] that include men and boys in reproductive health and rights education as a mechanism to build capacity for greater autonomy, and increase the socioeconomic status of women within their family and community groups in post-settlement settings.

This review also identified persistent barriers related to communication, cultural mismatch, gender norms, and mistrust of services across both humanitarian and resettlement settings. Despite these recurring themes, only one included study employed a participatory or co-designed approach with Afghan women. In response to this gap, we recommend the development of co-designed, right-based, and culturally safe reproductive care models that actively involve Afghan women, their needs and priorities in their design, implementation, and evaluation.

While many of the recommendations highlighted in the papers may be broadly applicable to refugee women resettling globally, some recommendations are of particular significance within Afghan communities due to the long-term disruption to education, high levels of illiteracy and low literacy even in women’s first language, as well as context-specific sociocultural and gender-related factors that may influence women’s autonomy, decision-making, and reproductive rights.

Strengths, limitations and other considerations

A notable strength of this review was the collaboration with the advisory group, which enabled the first author to consult them regarding the search strategy, as well as understanding the results. Additionally, there was no language restriction on the papers and two of the research team members (DE and NH) were able to read Dari and Farsi which resulted in the inclusion of one paper. This paper was from Iran, one of the humanitarian settings for Afghan refugees, and one of the few papers that reported detailed quantitative data about the types of contraceptive methods used and the reasons for unwanted pregnancies. The search terms were broad to facilitate a comprehensive search, as diverse terminologies are used worldwide to describe reproductive themes. The prevalence of reproductive issues was not uniformly reported across different settings, making comparisons difficult in some areas. Our review did not limit the search criteria by age, as the definition of reproductive age may vary across regions. Our findings are only illustrative of the included peer-reviewed studies. Additionally, the findings of this review span a 25-year period over which there were political changes in Afghanistan and there were shifting migration and resettlement policies globally. Findings were similar over this period for externally displaced Afghan women, but we note that changes have influenced the nature of displacement, the composition of Afghan refugee populations, and the types of services available in host countries. While some reproductive health challenges—such as constrained autonomy, limited reproductive literacy, and barriers to service access—have persisted across time, their manifestations and the surrounding support structures evolved. Earlier studies primarily reflected on limited service infrastructure, whereas more recent studies increasingly focused on resettlement settings, service navigation, and psychosocial wellbeing. The persistence of certain barriers across periods should not therefore be merely interpreted as a cultural lens, but rather as the interaction between evolving norms and persistent systemic inequities. Future research would benefit from explicitly examining temporal shifts in reproductive health experiences alongside changing policy and migration contexts.

Conclusion

Understanding Afghan women’s reproductive health issues and the factors influencing their reproductive outcomes can be a vital step toward accessing reproductive health and improving service provision. Our analysis lists issues related to family planning, fertility, service utilisation, and maternal and foetal complications and a range of factors discussed by studies as barriers and facilitators to reproductive outcomes in different settings. Data collection, monitoring, and research, particularly in resettlement settings, can be more insightful if disaggregated by population group and migration status to aid in the development of targeted strategies. When combined with participatory and co-production methodologies, a culturally responsive approach can address legal, social, linguistic, and structural determinants, and facilitate equitable planning and implementation of reproductive health promotion and service provision, both during the settlement phase, and throughout primary and reproductive healthcare journey. Addressing intersecting barriers—such as gender norms, agency, illiteracy, lack of service accessibility, and mental health stigma—requires intersectional, multilevel responses. Settlement and reproductive health services, as well as system-level policies and program design, should centre the voices of Afghan women and reflect their lived experience.
