Phage cocktails for the disinfection of carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae on high-touch surfaces in intensive care units
Authors: Yao He, Tingting Liu, Shuzhen Wang, Ziying Ji, Liming Zhang, Xiaojian Han, Yuying Wan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Chemical disinfectants are widely used for environmental decontamination in ICUs. However, their long-term application is associated with bacterial tolerance, material corrosion, and reduced lifespan of frequently touched surfaces. Background

Carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) has emerged as an important healthcare-associated pathogen worldwide [1, 2]. Critically ill patients in intensive care units (ICUs) are particularly vulnerable to CRKP infection due to frequent invasive procedures and extensive exposure to broad-spectrum antimicrobial agents. These infections are associated with high mortality, limited therapeutic options, and nosocomial outbreaks, posing a substantial threat to patient safety and infection prevention efforts worldwide [3, 4].

While patient-to-patient transmission remains a key route for CRKP dissemination, increasing evidence indicates that contaminated environmental surfaces play an important role in sustaining transmission within ICUs [5, 6]. CRKP can persist for extended periods on frequently touched surfaces, including bed rails, medical equipment, and furnishing materials, thereby facilitating indirect transmission via healthcare workers’ hands and contact with contaminated fomites [7]. Consequently, effective decontamination of high-touch surfaces is a critical component of infection prevention strategies aimed at controlling CRKP spread in clinical environments.

However, decontaminating these surfaces becomes challenging when biofilms are present. Biofilms, which are clusters of bacteria encased in a protective extracellular matrix, significantly complicate the disinfection process. Compared with planktonic cells, bacteria embedded within biofilms exhibit increased tolerance to antimicrobial agents [8]. Furthermore, biofilm-associated bacteria can remain viable on surfaces for extended periods, serving as persistent reservoirs of contamination and potential sources of hospital-acquired infections [9, 10].

Conventional disinfection methods—including pasteurization, ultraviolet (UV) light, chemical disinfectants, ozone, and photocatalysis can effectively reduce bacterial loads but may endanger human health, damage medical equipment, or harm the environment. Conventional disinfection methods, including pasteurization [11] ultraviolet (UV) irradiation [12], chemical disinfectants [13], ozone [14], and photocatalysis [15], can effectively reduce bacterial loads. However, their efficacy against biofilm-associated bacteria is often limited [16], and some of these approaches may pose risks to human health, damage medical equipment, or cause environmental harm [17, 18]. Moreover, growing attention has been directed toward the potential for bacterial adaptation or tolerance to commonly used biocidal agents, raising questions about the long-term sustainability of existing disinfection practices [19, 20]. These challenges highlight the need for alternative or complementary disinfection strategies that align with infection control requirements in high-risk clinical settings.

Lytic phages have emerged as a promising strategy for surface decontamination due to their high bacterial specificity, self-amplifying ability, activity against biofilm-associated cells, and minimal toxicity to humans and materials [21, 22]. In recent years, phage-based approaches have been explored not only for therapeutic purposes but also for environmental decontamination and infection control applications [23]. Experimental studies have demonstrated the feasibility of using phages to reduce surface contamination by multidrug-resistant pathogens such as Acinetobacter baumannii and Pseudomonas aeruginosa in hospital-relevant settings [24, 25]. However, despite the growing interest in phage-based environmental decontamination, evidence regarding their effectiveness against CRKP contamination on high-touch surfaces in ICUs remains limited.

In this study, we isolated and characterized two lytic phages against CRKP and evaluated the disinfection efficacy of the individual phages and their cocktail on four representative high-touch surfaces commonly found in ICUs, including stainless steel, cotton fabric, plastic, and plywood. This study aimed to provide experimental evidence supporting the potential of phage-based surface disinfection as an adjunctive strategy for preventing and controlling the environmental transmission of CRKP in ICU settings.

Materials and methods

Bacterial host strains and culture conditions

A CRKP strain was isolated from a clinical sputum sample collected from a patient at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University and was designated CRKP28. For long-term storage, the isolate was preserved in 40% glycerol phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at − 80 °C [26]. Prior to experiments, the strain was revived and cultured for 24 h. A single colony was then inoculated into 5 mL of LB broth and incubated with shaking for 4 h to obtain logarithmic-phase bacteria at approximately 108–109 CFU/mL.

Isolation and propagation of phages

Phages infecting CRKP28 were isolated from sewage samples collected at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University. Phage enrichment, isolation, and purification were performed using standard procedures as described previously [26–28]. Based on plaque morphology and lytic activity, two lytic phages, designated XDRKpn-1 and XDRKpn-2, were selected for further characterization.

Briefly, sewage samples were clarified to remove particulate matter and bacteria, followed by filtration. The resulting filtrates were incubated with exponentially growing CRKP28 to enrich for phages capable of infecting the host strain. After enrichment, phages were isolated and purified using the double-agar overlay method. Individual plaques were picked and subjected to multiple rounds of purification until homogeneous plaques were obtained.

Phage stocks were propagated using the double-layer agar method, and phage titers were determined as plaque-forming units (PFU/mL) following established protocols [29]. Purified phage preparations were stored at 4 ℃ until further use.

Host range assay

The host ranges of phage XDRKpn-1 and phage XDRKpn-2 were determined using a double-layer agar spot assay as previously described with slight modifications [30]. Briefly, 100 µL of overnight cultures from 34 bacterial strains was mixed with LB soft agar (0.7%) and overlaid onto LB agar plates. After solidification, 5 µL of phage suspension (108 PFU/mL) was spotted onto the bacterial lawn and incubated at 37 ℃ for 12 h. Lytic activity was determined by the presence of clear or turbid plaques. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Transmission electron microscopy of phages

A piece of sealing film was laid flat on a glass slide, and a copper grid was placed on the film using tweezers. Then, 10 µL of phage suspension was applied to the grid and incubated for 10 min. Excess liquid was removed with filter paper. Next, 10 µL of 3% phosphotungstic acid (PTA) was added for negative staining and incubated for 3 min. The excess stain was removed using filter paper. All sample preparation was performed in a ventilated workspace. The grid was then examined using a transmission electron microscope (FEI TECNAI G2 12, FEI, USA).

Gene sequencing of phage XDRKpn-1、XDRKpn-2

Phage DNA extraction

Genomic DNA of phage XDRKpn-1、XDRKpn-2 was extracted from purified phage lysates using a phenol–chloroform-based method with SDS treatment, as previously described for phage genome isolation [31]. Briefly, free nucleic acids were removed by DNase I and RNase A digestion prior to phage capsid disruption. Phage DNA was subsequently purified by organic solvent extraction and alcohol precipitation, and the resulting DNA was resuspended in nuclease-free water and stored at − 20 °C until sequencing.

Genome sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

Purified phage genomic DNA was sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq platform using 150 bp paired-end reads. Reads were de novo assembled with MetaViralSPAdes v3.15.3 [32]. Genome annotation was performed using the RAST server [33], and open reading frames (ORFs) predicted by Prokka [34] were further analyzed via BLASTX against the NCBI non-redundant protein database. Genome visualization and manual curation were carried out using CGView and Artemis, respectively [35, 36]. Antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) and virulence factors encoded by the phage genomes were screened using the online platform PhageScope (https://phagescope.deepomics.org/) [37]. A phylogenetic tree based on the terminase large subunit (TerL) was constructed using MEGA12 [38]. As a relatively conserved structural protein in tailed phages, TerL is commonly used for phage classification and phylogenetic analysis.

Survival of CRKP28 on ICU surface materials

This study evaluated the survival of CRKP28 on four common ICU surface materials: cotton fabric(e.g., surgical gowns, curtains), stainless steel (e.g., doorknobs), plywood (e.g., tabletops), and plastic (e.g., light switches). Each material was cut into 10 cm² squares. Cotton fabric and stainless steel samples were sterilized by autoclaving, while plywood and plastic - sensitive to high temperatures—were disinfected with 75% ethanol followed by 30 min of UV sterilization.

Log-phase CRKP28 cultures were diluted in PBS to 10⁶ CFU/slice and inoculated onto each material. Samples were air-dried in a biosafety cabinet for 30 min, then transferred to a disused hospital ward maintained at 35 °C to simulate ICU environmental conditions. Bacterial survival was monitored by serial sampling and plate counting at 3 h, 8 h, 24 h, 48 h, 72 h, 1 week, 2 weeks, 3 weeks, and 4 weeks. Each experiment was performed in triplicate.

Bactericidal efficacy of phage XDRKpn-1、XDRKpn-2 in liquid culture

To assess the bactericidal effect of phages in suspension, CRKP28 was grown overnight and subcultured until reaching an OD600 of 1.0 (approximately 5 × 108 CFU/mL). The bacterial suspension was subsequently diluted to 5 × 106 CFU/mL. Phage XDRKpn-1 and phage XDRKpn-2 were each evaluated separately at concentrations of 103, 106, and 109 PFU/mL for single-phage treatment. Based on the bactericidal results, the optimal phage concentration was selected for subsequent experiments. For phage cocktail treatment, phage XDRKpn-1 and phage XDRKpn-2 were mixed at a 1:1 ratio using the optimal concentration identified above and then combined with the bacterial suspension. A saline–CRKP28 mixture served as the control. Samples were incubated at 37 °C, and viable bacterial counts were determined by serial dilution and plating at 1, 2, 4, 6, and 12 h. All conditions were tested in triplicate.

Biofilm inhibition assay

Biofilm inhibition assays were initiated by biofilm formation. An overnight culture of CRKP28 was diluted in Luria–Bertani (LB) broth to an OD600 of 0.1 (approximately 5 × 107 CFU/mL). Two hundred microliters of the diluted suspension was dispensed into sterile 96-well plates and incubated statically at 37 °C for 24 h to allow biofilm development. After incubation, planktonic cells were removed by aspiration, and each well was gently rinsed twice with 200 µL of sterile phosphate-buffered saline (1× PBS). The plates were then air-dried at room temperature to stabilize the biofilms.

For treatment, each well was supplemented with 200 µL of phage XDRKpn-1 (109 PFU/mL), phage XDRKpn-2 (109 PFU/mL), a phage cocktail (a 1:1 mixture of phage XDRKpn-1 and phage XDRKpn-2, final titer: 109 PFU/mL), or sodium dichloroisocyanurate [39] (NaDCC, 0.05% w/v; Chengdu Zhongguang Detergent Co., Ltd., China). The plates were incubated at 37 ℃ for 4 h [40]. After treatment, the wells were washed with sterile PBS and stained with 0.1% (w/v) crystal violet (200 µL per well) for 20 min at room temperature. Excess stain was removed, and the plates were washed and air-dried for 3 h. The bound dye was solubilized with 200 µL of 95% ethanol per well, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm (OD570) using a microplate reader. Untreated wells containing LB broth were used as negative controls. All phage suspensions were diluted in LB broth to ensure experimental consistency.

The percentage of biofilm removal was calculated according to previous studies [41, 42] as follows: Biofilm removal (%)=(ODc-ODts)/ODc×100, where ODc represents the absorbance value of untreated biofilms, and ODts represents the absorbance value of treated biofilms.All experiments were repeated independently three times.

Bactericidal efficacy of phages and phage cocktails on high-touch surface materials

To evaluate the bactericidal activity of phages against CRKP28 on four frequently touched intensive care unit (ICU) surface materials (cotton fabric, stainless steel, plywood, and plastic), all materials were first sterilized according to the disinfection procedure described in the CRKP survival assay.

The first step involved contaminating the test surfaces. Briefly, diluted CRKP28 suspensions were spread onto each surface to achieve a final concentration of approximately 106 CFU per piece and then air-dried for 1 h at room temperature within a biosafety cabinet. The contaminated surfaces of each material were divided into four groups: (1) single-phage treatment group, treated with phage XDRKpn-1 at final concentrations of 103, 106, or 109 PFU per piece; (2) phage cocktail treatment group, treated with a 1:1 mixture of phage XDRKpn-1 and XDRKpn-2 at a final concentration of 109 PFU per piece; (3) positive control group, treated with sodium dichloroisocyanurate (NaDCC, 0.05% w/v); and (4) negative control group, treated with sterile PBS.

All treatments were carried out at 25℃ and 60% relative humidity to simulate the ICU environment [43]. Following treatment, the surfaces were dried for 1 h and then incubated for 2 h. Residual bacteria were recovered by sterile swabbing and suspended in 15-mL centrifuge tubes, followed by vortexing for 5 min. Serial dilutions of the suspensions were plated on LB agar and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. Colony-forming units (CFUs) were counted to determine CRKP survival rates.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). GraphPad Prism was used for data visualization, and statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Differences among multiple groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. When the assumption of homogeneity of variance was not met, Dunnett’s T3 post hoc test was applied. All tests were two-tailed, and a P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In all figures, * indicates P < 0.05, ** indicates P < 0.01, and *** indicates P < 0.001.

Isolation and purification of CRKP phage XDRKpn-1、XDRKpn-2

Given the prolonged survival of CRKP on ICU-relevant surfaces, we next sought to identify phages with potential application for surface decontamination. Using the sewage sample collected from the hospital, two phages that formed transparent round plaques with a diameter of approximately 0.5 mm on the bacterial lawn of a CRKP28 strain were isolated(Fig. 2a and c). The two phages were named XDRKpn-1 and XDRKpn-2 and have been abbreviated as phage XDRKpn-1 and phage XDRKpn-2 in this article. The microscopic observation of the virion morphology by TEM showed that phage XDRKpn-1 harbors an icosahedral head 24 nm in diameter with a tail 52 nm long and 8 nm wide (Fig. 2b), while phage XDRKpn-2 possesses an icosahedral head 28 nm in diameter with a tail 73 nm long and 6 nm wide (Fig. 2d). Based on the microscopic morphology of the phage virions, both phages were classified as members of the order Caudovirales.

Host range

The host ranges of phage XDRKpn-1 and phage XDRKpn-2 were evaluated using 34 bacterial strains, including 30 clinical CRKP isolates and four reference strains. As shown in Table S1, phage XDRKpn-1 lysed 16 of the 30 CRKP isolates, with a lysis rate of 53.33%, whereas phage XDRKpn-2 lysed 12 isolates, corresponding to a lysis rate of 40.00%. In addition, both phages exhibited lytic activity against the reference K. pneumoniae strain, but no lytic activity was observed against E. coli, P. aeruginosa, or A. baumannii.

Genome characterization of phages XDRKpn-1 and XDRKpn-2

Phage XDRKpn-1 was identified as a double-stranded DNA phage. Genome assembly generated a single contig of 158,912 bp with an average sequencing coverage of 4,907× and a GC content of 46.23%. A total of 175 open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted, of which 58 were annotated as protein-coding genes with known or putative functions, while the remaining ORFs were classified as hypothetical proteins or proteins with unknown functions (Fig. 3a). Phage XDRKpn-2 was also identified as a double-stranded DNA phage. Its assembled genome consisted of a single contig of 345,827 bp with an average sequencing coverage of 1,354× and a GC content of 31.68%. A total of 286 ORFs were predicted, among which 58 were annotated as genes encoding proteins with known or putative functions, as shown in the circular genome map (Fig. 3b). Further analysis of both phage genomes using PhageScope revealed that neither phage carried antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) or virulence genes. The complete genome sequence of phage XDRKpn-1 has been deposited in GenBank under accession number PP792980, while that of phage XDRKpn-2 has been deposited in the CNSA under accession number CNP0007279.

Phylogenetic analysis of phages XDRKpn-1 and XDRKpn-2

The nucleotide sequences of the terminase large subunit (TerL) genes from phage XDRKpn-1 and phage XDRKpn-2 were subjected to BLASTn analysis against the NCBI GenBank database to identify closely related phage sequences. Phylogenetic trees were subsequently constructed using MEGA12 based on the Neighbor-Joining (NJ) method with 1,000 bootstrap replicates. As shown in Fig. 4a, phage XDRKpn-1 clustered closely with Klebsiella phage KP6F1, indicating a close evolutionary relationship. As shown in Fig. 4b, phage XDRKpn-2 clustered within the same clade as Klebsiella phage K64-1 DNA, Klebsiella phage Muenster, and Klebsiella phage KP0405, suggesting a relatively close phylogenetic relationship with these previously reported phages.

Bactericidal efficacy of phage XDRKpn-1、XDRKpn-2 in liquid culture

In the time–kill assay, treatments with either phage at 103 PFU/mL exhibited no bactericidal activity, as the CFU counts exceeded the initial inoculum (6 log10 CFU/mL) within 12 h. Compared with lower phage concentrations (103 and 106 PFU/mL), exposure to higher concentrations (109 PFU/mL) resulted in a marked reduction in CFU, approaching a 4-log decrease relative to the control at 12 h (Fig. 5). Moreover, the phage cocktail displayed the most pronounced effect, particularly at longer incubation times, with CFU counts still reduced by approximately 4 logs compared with the control at 12 h. Among the single phages, XDRKpn-1 exhibited overall superior bactericidal activity compared with XDRKpn-2. These results indicate that high-titer phages, especially when used in combination, demonstrate stronger in vitro bactericidal activity against the host bacteria. This activity highlights their potential as a supplement to conventional disinfection strategies, warranting further evaluation under conditions more representative of an ICU environment.

Antibacterial and antibiofilm activity of CRKP lytic phages in vitro

The biofilm is a major source of bacterial persistence on hospital surfaces, posing a challenge for infection control in ICU [44]. To assess the potential of phages as an alternative or adjunct disinfection strategy, the biofilm removal efficacy of phages XDRKpn-1 and XDRKpn-2, a phage cocktail, and chemical disinfectants was compared. (Fig. 6a). Twenty-four–hour CRKP biofilms were exposed to increasing concentrations of a single phage (103–109 PFU/mL), a phage cocktail at a final titer of 109 PFU/mL, or a conventional disinfectant(NaDCC). Residual biomass was quantified using crystal violet staining (OD₅₇₀) (Fig. 6b).

The results showed that at a concentration of 103 PFU/mL, phages XDRKpn-1 and XDRKpn-2 exhibited relatively low biofilm inhibition, with inhibition rates of 9.64% and 16.06%, respectively (P > 0.05). As the concentration increased, the inhibition rates rose markedly. At 106 PFU/mL, treatment with XDRKpn-1 and XDRKpn-2 reduced biofilm biomass by 65.73% and 48.87%, respectively (P < 0.05), while at 10⁹ PFU/mL, the inhibition rates reached 78.66% and 64.22%, respectively (P < 0.05). At the highest titer, the phage cocktail achieved a 77.64% reduction in biofilm, slightly lower than that of chemical disinfectants (79.96%), with no statistically significant difference between the two (P > 0.05).

Bactericidal activity of phage cocktails on high-touch surface materials

In a proof-of-concept experiment, surface decontamination studies were performed on stainless steel, cotton fabric, plywood, and plastic samples (10 × 10 mm each) to simulate high-touch surfaces typically found in hospital ICUs (Fig. 7). After two hours of treatment, colony counting revealed a significant reduction in bacterial load on all surfaces compared to the control, consistent with findings reported in previous studies.

NaDCC resulted in 99.96% reduction, and 109 PFU/mL phage cocktail achieved 99.998%. On cotton, reductions were 99.98% (103 PFU/mL), ~ 99.94% (106 and 109 PFU/mL), 99.96% (NaDCC), and 99.994% (109 PFU/mL phage cocktail). In this decontamination study, the control groups (NaDCC and sterile PBS) and experimental groups (single phages and phage cocktails at different concentrations) were analyzed separately on all surface types, with the results presented in distinct figures. This approach enabled a detailed assessment of the effects of phage concentration and formulation on bactericidal activity. Overall, after 2 h of phage treatment on stainless steel, cotton, plywood, and plastic surfaces, all higher-concentration phage treatments (106 and 109 PFU/mL XDRKpn-1 and the phage cocktail) exhibited significant bactericidal effects compared with the control, except for the lowest-concentration single phage (103 PFU/mL XDRKpn-1) (P < 0.05). The NaDCC treatment displayed variable efficacy across the four materials: it significantly reduced bacterial counts on cotton and plastic surfaces but showed no significant effect on stainless steel or plywood (P > 0.05). Furthermore, no significant differences were observed among 109 PFU/mL XDRKpn-1, 109 PFU/mL phage cocktail, and NaDCC treatments on any of the four surfaces (P > 0.05).

Discussion

Carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) is a common cause of ICU-acquired infections and can persist on frequently touched surfaces, forming biofilms that increase the risk of cross-contamination [45, 46]. Effective strategies to remove CRKP from ICU surfaces are therefore critical. Phages may serve as an alternative strategy to eliminate CRKP from ICU surfaces. In this study, we isolated two CRKP-specific phages, characterized their genomes, and evaluated their ability to reduce bacterial load and prevent biofilm formation in liquid culture and on representative ICU surfaces. Our results show that at high titers, both individual phages and the phage cocktail effectively suppressed CRKP28 and biofilm formation, highlighting their potential as surface decontamination agents in ICU settings.

Understanding the persistence of CRKP on different hospital surface materials is critical for designing effective environmental infection control strategies. In this study, CRKP28 survival varied markedly across surface types under ambient conditions, highlighting the influence of material-specific properties on bacterial persistence. Consistent with previous reports on K. pneumoniae. survival on dry hospital surfaces [47, 48], porous and rough materials such as cotton supported prolonged bacterial survival, likely due to enhanced moisture retention and increased surface area that facilitates bacterial adhesion and protection from desiccation [49]. Compared with other materials, CRKP28 exhibited the shortest survival time on stainless steel, in contrast to reports showing prolonged survival of Staphylococcus aureus on the same material [50]. This discrepancy underscores species-specific differences in environmental resilience and surface interactions, potentially driven by variations in capsule composition, stress response pathways, and adhesion mechanisms. The transient increase in viable bacterial counts observed on days 3–4 across multiple surfaces may reflect early-stage biofilm formation or adaptive physiological responses that temporarily enhance bacterial survival under environmental stress.

The prolonged survival of CRKP on various hospital surfaces highlights the difficulty of controlling this pathogen in clinical environments. To address this challenge, we successfully isolated two lytic phages, XDRKpn-1 and XDRKpn-2, and evaluated their bactericidal activity in liquid culture in a concentration- and time-dependent manner. The results demonstrated that both phages exhibited significant bactericidal activity against the host strain CRKP28 over the 12-hour observation period. Furthermore, higher initial phage titers led to more rapid bacterial suppression, reflecting an accelerated cycle of phage adsorption, replication, and lysis under these conditions, consistent with the dynamics predicted by phage therapy models [51, 52]. However, a transient regrowth of bacteria was observed over time, a phenomenon commonly attributed to the emergence of phage-resistant subpopulations. Resistance of K. pneumoniae to phage infection has been associated with modifications of the bacterial capsule, loss or masking of receptors, which can temporarily reduce phage adsorption efficiency [53, 54]. Such adaptive changes may account for the partial recovery of bacterial populations observed during extended incubation.

In addition, biofilm formation significantly enhances bacterial survival on both biotic and abiotic surfaces, posing a major challenge to conventional disinfection strategies. In this study, treatment with a high-titer single phage, a phage cocktail, and a chemical disinfectant resulted in comparable reductions in CRKP biofilm biomass, with no statistically significant differences among treatments. These results demonstrate that, under optimized conditions, monophage therapy can effectively disrupt established CRKP28 biofilms.

The observed efficacy of phage treatment likely reflects several inherent advantages of lytic phages, including their ability to penetrate biofilms, replicate at the site of infection, and amplify their bactericidal effect through successive infection cycles [55, 56]. Unlike chemical disinfectants, phages exert minimal selective pressure on non-target microbiota and are not associated with environmental toxicity [57]. While phage cocktails are often proposed to enhance efficacy and reduce resistance development [58], our findings suggest that sufficiently high titers of a well-characterized single phage may achieve comparable short-term biofilm removal. This supports the feasibility of developing targeted, strain-specific phage formulations for environmental biofilm control, particularly in settings where the dominant pathogen is known.

Based on the bactericidal activity and biofilm inhibition results in liquid environments, this study further investigated the efficacy of phages on hospital surface materials. The bactericidal effects of NaDCC, the individual phage XDRKpn-1, and the phage cocktail against CRKP28 were compared using contamination models on four simulated ICU surface materials. Significant bactericidal effects were observed on stainless steel and cotton even at relatively low phage concentrations, suggesting that surface characteristics play a critical role in modulating phage efficacy. The smooth, non-porous nature of stainless steel may facilitate uniform phage distribution and efficient contact with bacterial cells, while the absorbent structure of cotton may enhance phage retention and prolonged interaction with surface-associated bacteria.

At higher phage concentrations (≥ 106 PFU/slice), bacterial loads were reduced by 2–3 log units across all tested surfaces, underscoring phage dose as a key determinant of disinfection success. These findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating effective phage-mediated decontamination of hard surfaces contaminated with pathogens such as Salmonella spp. and Escherichia coli [59, 60]. Overall, our results provide experimental evidence that phage-based disinfectants can significantly reduce CRKP contamination on high-touch surfaces in the ICU. Moreover, phages are non-toxic to humans, environmentally safe, non-corrosive, and cost-effective, supporting their potential role as an adjunct tool for environmental infection control.

This study represents a proof-of-concept investigation and has several limitations. It was conducted under controlled laboratory conditions and did not account for environmental variables such as temperature fluctuations, humidity, or organic load, which may affect phage stability and efficacy in real hospital settings. Second, surface disinfection experiments were performed using a representative clinical CRKP28 isolate under standardized conditions. Although additional host range assays demonstrated broader lytic activity against multiple CRKP isolates, further studies involving a larger collection of genetically diverse clinical strains are warranted to evaluate broader applicability. Additionally, the phage cocktail used in this study consisted of only two phages, which may limit its long-term efficacy in complex clinical settings, such as the ICU, where bacterial populations can rapidly evolve. To address these limitations, future work will consider the following strategies: first, expanding the phage library to include phages with diverse host recognition mechanisms, thereby reducing the likelihood of resistance development; second, combining phages with sub-inhibitory concentrations of chemical disinfectants to enhance bacterial suppression and further limit resistant subpopulations; and third, rotating or periodically updating phage cocktails based on local epidemiological surveillance to maintain efficacy against evolving bacterial strains. Despite these limitations, our study provides preliminary evidence that phage-based disinfection may represent a promising strategy for controlling CRKP contamination in healthcare settings.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that phages XDRKpn-1, XDRKpn-2, and the phage cocktail effectively reduced CRKP contamination on high-touch surfaces associated with the ICU under laboratory conditions, highlighting their feasibility and potential as surface disinfectants in ICU settings.
