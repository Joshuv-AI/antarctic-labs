An integrated cell atlas of 2.4 million cardiac cells across 209 individuals in health and disease
Authors: Yesh Datar, Mark Chaffin, Bridget Simonson, Alex Mandia, Patrick T. Ellinor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cardiovascular disease remains the leading cause of global mortality. Understanding its complexity requires dissecting the heartʼs cellular landscape. Here we present HeartMap, a comprehensive single-nucleus RNA sequencing atlas of the adult human heart. This resource integrates data from nine studies, encompassing over 2.4 million nuclei, 209 individuals, eight anatomical regions and seven disease and healthy states. After rigorous data harmonization and method comparison of batch correction methods, we characterized transcriptional diversity across 14 cell types. To demonstrate the utility of HeartMap, we identified robust disease-associated gene signatures in dilated cardiomyopathy by comparing multiple studies. Notably, we identified distinct activated fibroblast populations, enriched for COL22A1 or TNC, that display variable prevalence across cardiomyopathies. HeartMap provides a valuable tool for exploring cardiac disease at the single-cell level, facilitating both fundamental research and potential therapeutic development.

Main

Recent advances in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) technologies have enabled extensive transcriptomic analyses of human organ/tissue systems across a variety of diseases1–4. These analyses have led to an unparalleled study of diseases by looking at the changes in common and rare cell populations. Atlases allow researchers to interrogate cell populations at scale to identify disease-associated genes, cell-to-cell interactions and biomarkers to detect disease. Integrated cell atlases, which combine multiple RNA-seq studies together, allow for even more robust comparisons between studies and increase the statistical power for downstream analyses4.

To date, many research groups have studied cardiovascular disease in healthy and disease tissues5–12. Although some variation in gene expression and cell states can be explained by the specific disease under study, substantial variability is introduced based on sequencing protocols, data preprocessing and downstream analysis methods. A well-integrated heart disease cell atlas would help reduce technical noise and augment meaningful biological signals from these studies. Furthermore, an atlas of this size would allow for unprecedented transcriptomic comparisons among cardiomyopathies, regional cardiac anatomies, cell types and biological sex.

In the present work, we constructed an integrated atlas, called HeartMap, through the reprocessing of published studies and robust batch correction using previously described packages for integration and integration method evaluation. Furthermore, we conducted pairwise differential expression (DE) tests between different disease states while controlling for study-specific effects. To identify a robust set of genes associated with dilated cardiomyopathy, we performed DE tests comparing dilated cardiomyopathy versus control hearts across three independent studies. We discovered disease-associated genes expressed in various cell types that are consistent across studies. Finally, we investigated specific cell populations and identified two activated fibroblast niches that are enriched in primary and ischemic cardiomyopathies.

Data integration across studies controls for batch effects in HeartMap

We removed sample duplicates and incorrectly labeled samples from each of the studies prior to any batch correction and downstream analysis (Methods). For our batch correction across studies, we considered up to four parameterizations of the following integration methods: (1) Harmony13, (2) Scanorama14, (3) single-cell variational inference (scVI)15 and (4) single-cell annotation using variational inference (scANVI)16. Using a representative subset of the complete data, we evaluated the performance of each approach using single-cell integration benchmarking (scIB)17. We considered integration performance across both study and individual, separately. Notably, individuals represent distinct patients and may have multiple samples if multiple tissues were surveyed or they were included in multiple studies. scANVI and Harmony revealed the highest scores for biological conservation and batch correction, respectively (Extended Data Fig. 1); however, given that scANVI had the overall best performance when considering the preservation of biological signal while correcting for batch effects, this method was chosen to construct the complete atlas.

After harmonizing the atlas with scANVI, we visually assessed nuclei mixing by individual of origin and study (Fig. 2). Large transcriptional variation has been shown to exist between individuals, even within cell types, most notably in cardiomyocytes, as seen in supplementary fig. 2 of Tucker et al.7. Similar transcriptional variation can be seen in the uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) representation of the uncorrected atlas by individual and study (Fig. 2a,c) compared to the corrected atlas by individual and study (Fig. 2d,f). Mapping of the cardiac cell landscape reveals compositional shifts in common and rare cell types across disease

After data integration and quality control, we mapped the combined 2,479,674 nuclei with scANVI (Methods). The final atlas revealed 52 clusters representing 14 known common and rare cell types in heart tissue. Additional distinctions based on both anatomical region and disease were also observed (Extended Data Fig. 2a). We identified distinct marker genes for all cell types, many of which are known canonical markers as expected (Extended Data Fig. 2b and Supplementary Table 1).

After restricting to anatomical regions captured by at least one disease state, we observed differences in global cell type proportions by disease. For example, cardiomyocyte proportions have a credible decrease in disease and chronic states (for example, DCM, HCM, ARVC and ICM) compared to healthy (for example, non-failing control), which recapitulate known findings (Fig. 3)18. Additionally, we considered whether any cell type proportions varied markedly by study. Reassuringly, proportions appeared generally similar, or notable differences could be explained by the fraction of non-failing control samples in each study or the inclusion of different anatomical regions. In addition to the global pseudobulk analysis, we also constructed sample-level UMAP embeddings restricted to each cell type (Fig. 4e,f and Extended Data Fig. 5). Sex assignment and disease status consistently drove sample-level differences across cell types, with less clear disease effects in rarer cell types, potentially due to fewer nuclei per sample (Fig. 4e and Extended Data Fig. 5).

In conjunction with the findings on the global and cell-level embedding maps, we ran DE tests to investigate the transcriptomic differences between diseases and sex assignments. Using pairwise comparisons between disease groups, we found that the most differentially expressed genes were in common cell types such as ventricular cardiomyocytes and fibroblasts (Fig. 5). Additionally, we observed more differentially expressed genes in disease versus control comparisons than in disease versus disease comparisons. Notably, we still saw an appreciable, although relatively fewer, number of differentially expressed genes between inherited cardiomyopathy (DCM versus HCM) and ischemic cardiomyopathy (ICM versus AMI) across cell types as well. Disease-associated genes in dilated cardiomyopathy are replicated between studies

HeartMap also enables the comparison of DE tests between published studies. Examining disease-associated genes across multiple studies allows us to focus on the most prominent disease-associated signals and decreases the background signals generated from individual studies alone. In this analysis, we focused on the comparison of DCM to non-failing control hearts as DCM was captured in three distinct studies and allowed for a robust comparison of effect estimates. We performed DE tests for the three reprocessed studies in our atlas (Chaffin et al.10, Reichart et al.12 and Koenig et al.9), which contained end-stage DCM and non-failing control.

As expected, we saw a strong concordant relationship when comparing the within-study effect estimates between the DE test from published data and our reprocessed data (Supplementary Fig. 1). This gave reassurance that we had not substantially changed the underlying data during reprocessing. In ventricular cardiomyocytes, some example genes with strong upregulation in DCM across all three studies included UNC80, PLCE1, C5AR1, NPPA and NPPB. Interestingly, an UNC80 mutation has been associated with a syndrome of hypotonia, dyskinesia and dysmorphism19. C5AR1 encodes the main receptor of complement 5, which is a potent inflammatory fragment generated during complement activation, which has been implicated in the progression of dilated cardiomyopathy via C5b-9-induced TNF expression in cardiac myocytes20. Similarly, PLCE1 plays roles in modulating β-adrenergic receptor-dependent cardiac contraction and inhibits hypertrophy21. Additionally, NPPA and NPPB encode atrial and B-type natriuretic peptides and are classical biomarkers of heart failure22. Strongly downregulated genes in DCM across all three studies included BMP7, which has been shown to be activated by transcription factor YY1 and co-modulate CTGF to suppress LMNA DCM and cardiac fibrosis23.

Strongly upregulated genes in endocardial cells included BMP6. Bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs), including those encoded by BMP6, have also been implicated in DCM by influencing cardiac development24,25. Strongly downregulated genes include AQP3 and INHBA. AQP3 encodes aquaporin proteins, which have been shown to be biomarkers for DCM26. INHBA encodes a subunit of Activin A, which has been shown to impair contractility and calcium handling in human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC)-derived cardiomyocytes27.

Finally, in pericytes, fibroblasts and vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), several genes showed strong downregulation in DCM across studies. This included ADAMTS4, which has been shown, through inhibition, to prevent cardiac fibrosis by reducing the release of TGFβ, a transforming growth factor, due to EDA-fibronectin cleavage28. Additional cell types and associated genes are presented in Supplementary Table 3. Pathway analysis revealed that there were multiple common pathways among the three studies, primarily in fibroblasts, pericytes and macrophages (Extended Data Fig. 6 and Supplementary Table 4).

Despite an overall positive correlation between gene effect estimates in the comparison of DCM to control between studies, there exists substantial heterogeneity (Supplementary Fig. 2). To assess the degree of heterogeneity across studies, we calculated an I2 coefficient for each gene tested in each cell type. A high percentage of genes tested had I2 > 75% in each cell type (approximately 35% ventricular cardiomyocytes, 44% fibroblasts, 27% pericytes, 34% endothelial cells, 33% macrophages, 26% lymphocytes and 16% endocardial cells), indicating high heterogeneity. Although the above analysis focused on genes with consistent effects across studies, it is unclear why a large fraction of genes show very disparate effects between DCM and control among these studies. Genes with higher I2 coefficients did not seem to be associated with overall expression levels, the degree to which the gene is likely driven by background RNA or the enrichment of specific and unique pathways.

Distinct populations of activated fibroblasts in inherited and ischemic cardiomyopathies

HeartMap allows for deep investigation into cell types after rigorous batch correction to account for individual-level and study-level differences. We systemically subclustered all major cell types and identified between four and 29 subclusters across cell types (Extended Data Figs. 7–9 and Supplementary Figs. 3–13). We compared the subclusters from our integrated analysis in HeartMap to subcluster or cell states in the previously published data from these studies, when available (Extended Data Fig. 10). In ventricular cardiomyocytes, we see that a high proportion of nuclei in HeartMap cluster 7 from Kanemaru et al.5 and Kuppe et al.8 were labeled as ‘stressed cardiomyocytes’ or ‘damaged cardiomyocytes’, respectively, and tend to be more prevalent in DCM/HCM than control (Extended Data Fig. 7). In endothelial cells, HeartMap clusters 1 and 4 are disproportionately represented by nuclei labeled as venous and arterial, respectively, across multiple studies but do not vary in composition by disease (Extended Data Fig. 8). In macrophages, HeartMap cluster 12 consists of a high proportion of cells labeled as proliferating/cycling macrophages in multiple studies and are consistently depleted in DCM, HCM, ICM and ARVC samples compared to non-failing controls but not in AMI (Extended Data Fig. 9).

Of particular interest, we identified 29 subclusters of fibroblasts across the 590,066 fibroblast nuclei in HeartMap (Fig. 7a and Supplementary Fig. 3). Three of these subclusters, 3, 7 and 24, were characterized as activated fibroblast populations based on elevated expression of canonical genes, including POSTN and FAP, and show a substantial proportion of cells labeled as activated fibroblasts across multiple published studies (Extended Data Fig. 10). However, populations 3 and 7 diverged in their expression of additional markers such as COL22A1 and TNC, respectively, suggesting that these may be distinct fibroblast populations (Fig. 7b,c). Of those activated fibroblast populations, compositional analysis revealed that subclusters 3 and 7 were more prevalent in specific disease states (Fig. 7d). Genes upregulated in these two activated fibroblast populations show gene set enrichment for collagen fibril organization, supramolecular fiber organization and external encapsulating structure organization (Supplementary Table 5). Activated fibroblasts in subcluster 7 show gene enrichment for vascular development, blood vessel morphogenesis, wound healing and response to wounding with marker genes such as POSTN, FAP and TNC. To experimentally validate these activated fibroblast populations, we carried out RNAscope for COL22A1 (cluster 3) and TNC (cluster 7), using POSTN as a general marker for activated fibroblasts. We selected three non-failing, three ICM and three inherited cardiomyopathy samples by specific criteria (Methods) and confirmed that these were two separate cell populations. The proportions of each cell population in each sample were not statistically different between computational predictions and experimental findings (Fig. 7e and Supplementary Table 6). We did not see any co-localization of these distinct fibroblast populations with areas of fibrosis based on trichrome staining, although they do not appear to be perivascular (Supplementary Figs. 14–22).

Discussion

In the present study, we constructed HeartMap, which is, to our knowledge, the largest single-nucleus atlas used to study human adult heart disease. This map allows us to characterize common and rare cell types across various disease and healthy phenotypes. We demonstrate that control and diseased human samples separate based on transcriptomic profiles based on disease, anatomy and sex assignment. Furthermore, our pairwise DE test between diseases and control samples revealed more genes separating disease from control than disease from other diseases. Our analysis of disease-associated genes across studies in DCM shows that there are many common upregulated and downregulated genes and pathways in multiple cell types; however, despite sharing some similarities, effect estimates were often discordant across studies. Finally, we highlight the usefulness of our atlas to look at cell types at a larger scale than previously possible with disease-based comparisons. We show that fibroblasts have unique transcriptional differences with markers associated with activated, collagen-depositing subtypes.

After careful reprocessing and batch correction, we showed that we can robustly harmonize data across multiple studies to maintain meaningful biological signals in the highly heterogenous tissue of the heart. We found genes that serve as strong markers for each cell type across multiple studies, allowing us to better characterize a global transcriptional signature of the heart in healthy and disease states. Within these cell types, genes show strong dysregulation in disease states compared to controls, with the exception of a relatively modest number of differentially expressed genes between AMI and control. This may be due to the limited samples size (n = 6) or transcriptional heterogeneity among the patients with AMI as four samples from earlier timepoints (days 2–5) look broadly more like controls transcriptionally.

Despite strong differences between disease and control, there exists a smaller subset of genes differentiating disease states from one another. These between-disease comparisons are only possible with the aggregation of multiple studies as performed in the construction of HeartMap. Previous work using large-scale bulk RNA-seq showed that there were consensus gene expression signatures in heart failure across multiple studies; however, due to the nature of the study, it inherently could not reveal which cell types are meaningfully different across disease29. More recent work by this same group has focused on constructing a cross-study transcriptional patient map of heart failure by harmonizing bulk sequencing and snRNA-seq data from multiple cohorts and identifying latent multicellular programs associated with cardiac remodeling30. Whereas this work is explicitly heart failure specific and identifies conserved multicellular programs that distinguish failing from non-failing heart, it is not expansive. HeartMap was designed as a comprehensive, high-resolution reference atlas of the adult human heart across anatomical regions and disease contexts, not only the failing heart. By integrating millions of single-nucleus profiles across a large number of individuals, HeartMap emphasizes detailed cell type and cell state annotation, enabling the discovery of rare populations, continuous state transitions and disease-associated subtypes that may not be resolvable in bulk data or in approaches that primarily model coordinated signals across cell types in heart failure alone. As such, differentiating the transcriptional signatures between cardiovascular disease groups using HeartMap may aid in precision medicine approaches by informing further work on biomarker and therapeutic target discovery.

We also present an analysis of disease-associated genes in DCM across three independent studies. Combining samples from multiple studies improves statistical power and allows for the identification of additional DCM genes. Additionally, we also showed that effect estimates are not always consistent between studies. This demonstrates that (1) batch effects are critical to understand in any cross-study comparison and (2) independent replication of DE results for a disease phenotype would add value to the field. In the case of DCM, by harmonizing data between studies in HeartMap and focusing on genes with consistent, strong effects across all three studies, we can identify a more robust set of genes and pathways that are dysregulated in DCM.

Finally, our analysis of the fibroblast populations demonstrated several subpopulations of interest, namely activated fibroblast groups that were present in specific cardiomyopathies. Activated fibroblasts are a unique population as they are involved in dynamic extracellular matrix remodeling and present an opportunity for therapeutic intervention. Most activated fibroblasts are a result of chronic scar formation with repurposed functions, such as maintaining structural integrity in damaged tissues and modulating inflammation, angiogenesis and phagocytosis of cells in beneficial and detrimental ways. In previous work, COL22A1 was shown as a predictor of DCM, and, concordantly, in the present work, we observed a statistically credible increase in the COL22A1+ activated fibroblast population in patients with DCM compared to control31. TNC, which was seen in another activated fibroblast population, was shown to be implicated in maladaptive cardiac remodeling after myocardial infarction and was enriched for pathways of vascular development and stress response32–34. By identifying marker genes and understanding the pathways involved in these unique populations of activated fibroblasts, we are one step closer to understanding which fibroblast subpopulations are ideal candidates for therapeutic intervention.

Limitations

Aggregating datasets across multiple studies poses many challenges. We could not control for the variability that exists when isolating nuclei and sequencing tissues across multiple laboratory environments. Although we separately preprocessed the datasets with equivalent transcriptomes and quality control methods, we could not account for different equipment, sequencing protocols and nuclei isolation techniques that vary between laboratories.

Notably, the samples we have included in this atlas are primarily White or non-classified ethnicities. The underrepresentation of certain ancestral backgrounds can pose challenges to applying the findings of this study to a broader population.

From a computational standpoint, several limitations should be noted. First, although we evaluated multiple integration methods and selected scANVI based on quantitative performance metrics, these generative models remain sensitive to parameter choices and do not completely eliminate residual batch effects. Second, due to the scale of the dataset, exhaustive parameter optimization was computationally prohibitive, and results may depend on selected hyperparameters. Third, evaluating integration methods for subclustering analysis within a cell type is challenging as many current metrics for biological conservation require known cell state labels, something that is uncommonly known a priori. Fourth, DE and compositional analyses rely on pseudobulk aggregation and background RNA heuristics, which may reduce sensitivity for lowly expressed or rare transcripts.

Additionally, our high-resolution subclustering strategy was used to sensitively capture transcriptional heterogeneity within major cell types; however, for populations exhibiting continuous expression gradients, such as VSMCs, individual subclusters should be interpreted as potential substates rather than discrete or fully biologically validated cell states. Further experimental validation will be required to establish their functional significance.

Conclusion

HeartMap serves as a comprehensive reference for the study of human heart disease. By systematically reprocessing and integrating diverse datasets, we provide a unified resource that enables the investigation of cardiac biology and disease across individuals and cell types. This resource lays a foundation for advancing understanding of the human heart in both health and disease.

Aggregating datasets

We queried the PubMed database using terms ‘snRNA-seq or scRNA-seq or single-cell transcript’ and ‘heart or cardiac or cardiology’ as well as web-based searches for snRNA-seq studies. We found 20 adult human heart studies through this search process from 2017 to 2023. Each study focused on one or a variety of diseases and cardiac anatomies. Of these, nine studies met our inclusion criteria, including, but not limited to, similar study design (for example, human-derived samples, 10x Genomics kits), individual ages above 18 years, nuclei-based analysis and having more than 10,000 nuclei (Table 1). We limited our study to include only nuclei to avoid transcriptomic differences that exist between cell and nuclei-level data17.

One dataset by Simonson et al., establishing the relationship between ARVC and non-failing samples, was included from internal data and has not been made publicly available as of the time of this writing. Additionally, studies from Reichart et al.12 and Kanemaru et al.5 included sequencing libraries from control samples that were sequenced as part of previous studies. In these cases, the library was included only once in our atlas. In other cases where sample donors were repeated across studies, but separate sequencing libraries were constructed, we kept both sample files but carefully conducted downstream analyses to account for possible overabundance of samples from one individual.

Single-nuclei sequencing and preprocessing of datasets

Sequencing protocols were relatively consistent across studies and can be found in their respective publications. However, a few differences were notable regarding the single-nuclei purification and 10x Genomics library construction. Multiple studies included nuclei purified by fluorescence-activated cell/nuclei sorting (FACS/FANS), including Litviňuková et al.6, Kuppe et al.8, Koenig et al.9 and Kanemaru et al.5. Many of the included datasets used 10x Genomics 3′ version 3 chemistry (3′v3); however, Tucker et al.7 used 3′v2, Litviňuková et al. used a combination of 3′v2 and 3′v3 and Koenig et al.9 used 5′v1.1.

We ensured equivalent preprocessing of the raw FASTQ files across studies by using a similar transcriptomic reference and software packages (Supplementary Table 7). FASTQs were trimmed for the template switch oligo (TSO) and homopolymers (A30, C30, T30, G30) with Cutadapt prior to alignment when possible. We used pre-mRNA GRCh38 2020-A as our reference transcriptome and 10x Genomics CellRanger version 4.0.0 for transcriptome alignment and mapping when possible. In the case of Koenig et al.9, which used 5′v1.1 with an extended R2 length, we trimmed R2 down to 91 base pairs to be consistent with 10x Single Cell 3′ v3 read lengths used in most other studies. We confirmed that this reprocessing resulted in highly correlated gene expression to published counts (Supplementary Fig. 23). scR-Invex (https://github.com/getzlab/scrinvex) was used for assigning intronic, exonic and junction spanning reads35. CellBender version 2.0.0 (https://github.com/broadinstitute/CellBender) and Scrublet were used for additional empty droplet detection and doublet detection, respectively36,37.

Nuclei quality control was conducted by subsetting nuclei to those kept in the original published datasets to maintain consistency with publications. In some rare cases, CellBender removed all gene counts in a nucleus, and these nuclei were excluded. Additionally, we performed new quality control on the data from Tucker et al.7 using the process described in the published Chaffin et al.10 and Simonson et al. studies11.

In one study (Kuppe et al.8), similar preprocessing of datasets was not possible because raw FASTQ files were not provided by the study authors at the time of data collection. In this case, the provided count data used an unknown GRCh38 transcriptome reference with CellRanger version 6.0.2 used for alignment. We confirmed that the genes represented in this provided dataset were identical to those in our internal datasets included in our atlas. CellBender and Scrublet were run on the full count data to generate a dataset as equivalent to the other studies as possible. Additionally, we excluded samples from this study (CK161, CK369 and CK376) due to poor quality and low unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts.

Metadata harmonization

Metadata were provided for each study in the supplementary information of each publication. However, metadata varied across studies. Few studies consistently reported data such as age, biological sex, race, ethnicity, weight/body mass index, heart weight and left ventricular ejection fraction. Metadata that are consistently reported across studies were included in the final dataset (Supplementary Table 8).

X and Y chromosome gene counts were calculated for each sample to determine the transcriptional sex for each sample, using DDX3Y for the Y chromosome and XIST for the X chromosome. There were a few discrepancies in samples between the metadata sex-assignment-based and chromosome-count-based sex analysis. These samples are from Litviňuková et al.6 (H0015_RV), Reichart et al.12 (BO_H40_LVW) and Kanemaru et al.5 (HCAHeartST13146207 and HCAHeartST13146208). In the case of H0015_RV and BO_H40_LVW, the respective studies had multiple samples from the same donor. We confirmed that these two samples did not share the same genetic fingerprint as the other samples from their respective donors using CrosscheckFingerprints38 from Picard tools (https://github.com/broadinstitute/picard). Samples with discordant phenotypic sex and X/Y chromosome counts from the snRNA-seq data were excluded from the atlas. All analyses requiring sex information used the chromosome-derived sex assignment, unless otherwise noted.

Additionally, we excluded samples from multiple studies with ages less than or equal to 19 years to ensure that only adult samples were included in our atlas. This age range was selected because Reichart et al.12 used age ranges such as 0–19 to describe non-adult individuals. The studies with these excluded samples are from Reichart et al.12 (H05, H59, H73, H79, H80, H81, DO1, H84, IC_H02_LV0) and Koenig et al.9 (TWCM-11-93, TWCM-13-181, TWCM-13-198, TWCM-13-235). Furthermore, when calculating age for our summary statistics, we treated ages presented as ranges as the median value (for example, age in years 60–65 became 62). Koenig et al.9 had one individual with missing age in published metadata (TWCM-11-3). Age was not included as a covariate in our DE testing due to the labeling of ages using ranges as opposed to discrete values.

Batch correction and evaluation of integration approaches

We selected several batch correction methods from the top-performing methods identified in a previous study of benchmarking cell and nuclei integration on a large atlas17. These methods included Harmony, Scanorama, scVI and scANVI13–16. Specifically, scANVI required prior cell type assignments to run; thus, we generated crude labels for scANVI using the Leiden clustering algorithm on a non-batch-corrected neighborhood graph with resolution of 1.0 and assigning cell types based on known canonical cell type genes. Each method had various tunable parameters such as whether to estimate highly variable genes (HVGs) by batch or across all nuclei and the number of principal components or size of the latent space. For each method, we tested multiple combinations of these parameters such as inclusion of the top 2,000 HVGs across all nuclei or by batch (that is, donor of origin) using methodology from Seurat version 3 (ref. 39), principal components of either 50 or 100 and latent space of either 10 or 25. This resulted in four versions for each batch correction method for a total of 16 variations of batch correction. Each unique method was applied to a subset of the atlas (619,918 nuclei or one-fourth of the HeartMap atlas) that was representative of the proportion of studies from the overall atlas. The batch key for integration was set as ‘individual’ (representing the donor of origin) with an additional covariate key set to ‘study’ to reduce technical noise across samples and datasets. ‘Individual’ was selected as our covariate for alignment as this appears to be a primary cause of batch effects in cardiac cells, as seen in supplementary fig. 2 of Tucker et al.7.

Batch correction evaluation and comparison among the 16 parameterizations of correction methods were performed using existing integration evaluation metrics17. These metrics included important measures of biological conservation and batch correction at embedding space level and k-nearest neighbors graph space level. Methods that measured biological conservation included normalized mutual information (NMI), adjusted Rand index (ARI), isolated and non-isolated average silhouette width (ASW), cell cycle conservation, cell type local Simpsonʼs inverse index (cLISI) and F1 score. Methods that measured batch correction included graph connectivity, integration local Simpsonʼs inverse index (iLISI), batch ASW and principal component regression. Metrics were calculated using ‘individual’ and ‘study’ as the covariate keys to measure performance across these two variables (Extended Data Fig. 1). After the metrics were calculated for each of the 16 batch correction methods, we calculated a scaled score for ranking each metric performance across the 16 methods calculated as reported in Luecken et al.17.

The final score and each partial score were the objective measures used to determine the batch correction metric applied to the entire atlas. In cases when scores for batch correction and biological conservation were extremely close, we visually assessed the mixing of nuclei in UMAP plots colored by individual, study and cell type labels to determine the optimal batch correction method.

Map aggregation, cell type annotation and marker genes

Unless otherwise stated, snRNA-seq map aggregation was performed using SCANPY version 1.9.3 (ref. 40). The atlas map was constructed using the post-quality control nuclei that remained after subsetting to published data, as previously described. Additionally, we assigned cell type labels based on the expression of canonical markers of known cell types using Leiden clustering, as previously described. Based on the results of the top-performing batch correction method, scANVI was used to harmonize the atlas after calculating HVGs across all nuclei and using a latent space of 25 latent variables. We used rough cell type labels as input into scANVI for batch correction. A neighborhood graph was constructed using these 25 latent variables with sc.pp.neighbors() using 15 neighbors and cosine distance. UMAP was constructed with the effective minimum distance between embedded points set to min_dist = 0.2. Leiden clustering was employed at 2.0 resolution to help determine cell type composition of the post-batch-corrected atlas.

Canonical gene markers were used to aggregate the global subclusters with common markers to create global cell types. Marker genes were calculated for each global cell type cluster using an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). The AUC was computed for each gene comparing the log-normalized expression at the nuclei level in each cluster against all other clusters. A final list of marker genes for each cluster was ascertained using an AUC threshold >0.6.

Sample-level principal component and UMAP analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the sample level to identify high-level transcriptional differences based on phenotypic data. In this case, sample was defined by a unique identifier consisting of the individual name + study of origin + anatomy. For example, a sample from the Chaffin et al.10 dataset would be listed as P1304-Chaffin-LV. We summed the CellBender-derived gene counts across all nuclei per sample, treating the data as a bulk experiment. Mitochondrial genes, ribosomal genes and genes expressed in less than or equal to 1% of nuclei were removed. Remaining genes were normalized with DESeq2 with a design ~ disease + sex, and genes with fewer than 10 total counts were removed, where ~ indicates ‘is modeled as a function of’. A variance-stabilizing transformation was applied with vst(). Principal components were calculated with prcomp() in R. Technical effects were removed by adjusting for the ‘study’ covariate. PCA was run on the top 500 HVGs using 30 principal components, with the exception of epicardial cells, which used only 26 principal components due to the number of samples available. A neighborhood graph was constructed with sc.pp.neighbors() using 15 neighbors and cosine distance. A UMAP representation was constructed using min_dist = 0.2. When calculating PCA and UMAP by cell type, our model design was the same as above; hence, we removed atrial cardiomyocytes, which were from only control samples.

To compare the distance of each individual study anatomy pseudobulk sample to non-failing control samples, we calculated the pairwise Euclidean distance considering three different representations of the pseudobulk transcriptional data: (1) the top 30 principal components underlying the UMAP as calculated above, (2) the normalized expression matrix of the top 500 HVGs as calculated above and (3) the normalized expression matrix of 14,951 genes after removing lowly expressed genes. For each individual study anatomy pseudobulk sample, we only calculated the Euclidean distance to all non-failing control samples of the same anatomy/sex to minimize variation due to clear transcriptional differences based on these features.

Pairwise DE analysis based on disease status and sex assignment by cell type

DE analysis between all pairwise disease comparisons was performed on a cell type basis. The analysis was limited to only the ventricular anatomies (LV, RV, AX, IVS) as non-ventricular samples only came from control samples. We removed studies including Litviňuková et al.6, Tucker et al.7 and Kanemaru et al.5, because these only had control hearts, which may not contribute meaningfully to comparisons between diseases. Additionally, the disease CAD-HF was excluded as it was included in only one sample in the Kuppe et al.8 study. Testing was performed using the limma-voom framework. Nuclei were summed for a sample, defined by a unique identifier consisting of the individual name + study of origin + anatomy, if there were more than 25 nuclei for the given cell type. Genes in less than 0.5% of nuclei in all disease groups were removed. Next, a DE test was performed using the limma-voom framework with DESeq2 normalization41,42. We used the model Expr ~ 0 + disease + study + anatomy + sex, with a duplicateCorrelation() effect fit for the individual donor of origin to control for the fact that some individuals are represented across multiple studies or anatomies. We then extracted 15 pairwise disease contrasts for each cell type if there were at least three samples in the two groups being compared. DE analysis was performed on CellBender counts as primary analysis as well as CellRanger counts as a sensitivity analysis. A background heuristic, as described in Simonson et al.11, was calculated using all nuclei from each disease group being compared. Only genes with a background heuristic based on nuclei from group 1 and group 2 <0.5 were included. Additionally, only genes with Benjamini−Hochberg-corrected P < 0.05 with both CellBender and CellRanger that were expressed in at least 0.5% of nuclei from one comparator group in a given contrast were deemed significant.

DE analysis between sex assignments was performed on a cell type basis. The analysis included all anatomical regions and studies; however, we again excluded the disease CAD-HF. Testing was performed as above, and we extracted one pairwise sex assignment contrast for each cell type if there were at least three samples each in the female and male groups. Only genes with a background heuristic based on nuclei from group 1 and group 2 <0.5 were included. Additionally, only genes with Benjamini−Hochberg-corrected P < 0.05 with both CellBender and CellRanger were deemed significant.

Comparing DCM and non-failing controls across studies

To understand the consistency of DE tests across studies, we focused on the comparison of DCM to non-failing control hearts as DCM was captured in three distinct studies (Chaffin et al.10, Reichart et al.12 and Koenig et al.9). Within each study, we ran individual DE tests between DCM and non-failing controls by cell type using the reprocessed data in our atlas. In the Chaffin et al.10 reprocessed data, we included 50 samples (27 individuals total; 11 DCM and 16 controls) from the LV as this was the only anatomy included in the publication. We summed across repeated samples per individual so that each individual was represented only once in the DE test. In the Reichart et al.12 reprocessed data, we included 111 samples (50 individuals total; 44 DCM and six controls) and all anatomies studied. Of note, the original publication used 12 additional controls derived from Litviňuková et al.6, which we excluded here as they were not sequenced as part of the Reichart et al.12 study. To create pseudobulk expression, we summed across the combination of individual + anatomy. In the Koenig et al.9 reprocessed data, we included 34 samples (34 individuals total; 11 DCM and 23 controls) from AX samples as this was the only anatomy included in the publication. As there were no duplicate samples per individual, we summed across individual. For Chaffin et al.10 and Koenig et al.9, DE testing was performed as previously described using the limma-voom framework with the model ~ 1 + disease + sex, requiring a minimum of 25 nuclei and three samples per cell type group. For Reichart et al.12, because multiple anatomies were included, we used the model ~ 1 + disease + sex + anatomy with a duplicateCorrelation() effect for individual to control for the fact that some individuals are represented across multiple anatomies. Only genes expressed in at least 0.5% of nuclei in either DCM or control for a given study were included.

When possible, we compared published and reprocessed estimates for each study (Supplementary Fig. 1). As Reichart et al.12 did not report a direct DCM-to-control comparison, we could not check for consistency with our effect estimates. To assess heterogeneity in our recalculated effect estimates between DCM and control across studies, we estimated the I2 metric using the metafor package in R (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Subclustering analysis

Global clusters were collapsed into cell types based on canonical gene markers. If there were two or more global clusters with canonical genes mapping to a single cell type, we aggregated them together for downstream analysis. To create each cell-type-specific map, we tested multiple parameterizations of scVI (latent dimensions of 10 and 25, with and without HVG selection) and selected the optimal configuration based on batch correction metrics (Supplementary Table 9).

Once a cell-type-specific map was created, we applied a different Leiden clustering strategy than previously described to overcome subcluster gene expression similarity that was less apparent on the global atlas. To select the optimal clustering resolutions for each cell-type-specific map, we generated Leiden clustering resolutions from 0.1 to 2.0, and, at each resolution, we calculated AUC for all HVGs comparing expression in the target cluster to all others. We then chose the highest resolution in which each subcluster had more than five genes with AUC > 0.55; however, we limited the total number of subclusters to 30 to reduce overclustering. For two cell types, pericytes and macrophages, this resulted in a low resolution with few clusters. For these two cell types, we created an exclusion where pericytes could be allowed two subclusters with fewer than five genes with AUC > 0.55, and macrophages could be allowed one subcluster with fewer than five genes with AUC > 0.55. This high-resolution clustering strategy was employed to identify potential substates.

Marker genes for final subclusters were computed using the AUC-derived test as above as well as a limma-voom-derived test based on sample-level aggregation. In addition to the contrasts that differentiated each subcluster versus all other subclusters in the limma-voom DE analysis, we also computed all pairwise contrasts for each subcluster versus every other subcluster to further define subcluster differences and choose marker genes for validation. The marker genes for validation were selected based on highest statistically significant logFC estimates relative to other subclusters.

Compositional analysis

To identify compositional differences across and within cell types, we applied the Bayesian-based model from scCODA version 0.1.9 (ref. 43). For this analysis, a reference cell type is required that has a nearly constant abundance across samples. Given that we did not have a priori knowledge of a cell type or cell state that met this criteria, we used the ‘automatic’ setting to set the reference cell type in all analyses. A Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) chain length of 80,000 with 5,000 burn-in iterations was used. For each test, we only considered results when the model acceptance rate was greater than 35%. In a small subset of pairwise comparisons (nine of more than 200 comparisons), the acceptance rate was less than 35%. We reran these with the second-best reference cell type based on the scCODA heuristic, which resolved this issue. For any compositional test comparing disease status, only ventricular anatomy was included as atrial samples were represented only in non-failing control samples. Disease composition models included additional covariates for anatomy (LV, RV, AX, IVS), study and sex. Anatomy composition testing within atrial cardiomyocytes only included additional covariates for study and sex.

Gene set enrichment analysis

Pathway analysis was performed on the subclusters within each cell type using the open-source Bioconducter R package fgsea44 and Gene Ontology biological process terms45. For the fgsea analysis, genes for each subcluster were ranked based on the t-statistic from the limma-voom-based DE test. Only Gene Ontology terms with more than 15 and fewer than 500 genes were considered. Ontologies with a Benjamini−Hochberg-adjusted P < 0.05 and normalized enrichment score >2 were considered significant.

Photobleaching, RNAscope and immunofluorescence validation

Fresh-frozen tissue sections of 10 µm were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 minutes at room temperature and washed three times in PBS. Slides were then placed in trays in ice-cold PBS and photobleached for 48 hous (protocol modified from Sun et al.46). Slides were placed in a large ice bucket containing ice in a black 395-mm-high bin within a cold room. Slides placed in PBS to prevent dehydration were placed on top of ice packs inside the bin. A 300-W full-spectrum grow light (Platinum LED Lights, cat. no. P300) was placed on top of the bin and turned on with a cloak to cover the bin to prevent ultraviolet radiation. Ice, ice packs and cold PBS were replaced at the beginning and end of each day to keep the slides cold. After 48 hours, RNAscope was carried out on the 10-μm fresh-frozen tissue sections using the RNAscope Multiplex Fluorescent Reagent Kit v2 following the manufacturerʼs protocols. As there were two populations of activated fibroblasts that we were interested in, we used POSTN, which is a canonical gene specific to activated fibroblasts. We also identified genes that were highly selective to our activated fibroblast subclusters compared to other fibroblast cell populations (logFC > 3). As these subpopulations are rare, we aimed at validating on cardiac tissue with estimated >3% of nuclei expression in fibroblasts. We selected cardiac tissue from three LV samples for each of the disease groups (inherited disease, ischemic disease and non-failing controls) used in our previous studies (Supplementary Table 6). However, we limited our TNC+POSTN+ validation to samples with ischemic cardiomyopathy as no acute myocardial samples were available. Non-failing control samples were selected based on a low, medium and high TNC+POSTN+ fibroblast expression profile relative to total fibroblasts using computational prediction of tissue expression levels. The percentage of activated fibroblast populations in the computational prediction and experimental validation was calculated relative to the total number of POSTN+ cells. For example, the percentage of TNC+POSTN+ cells was calculated by totaling the number of cells expressing both TNC and POSTN and dividing the sum by the number of cells expressing POSTN. A two-sided paired t-test was conducted to assess similarity between the computational prediction and experimental results. The probes used for this study included hs-POSTN (409181), hs-COL22A1 (818811-C2) and hs-TNC (420771-C3). Slides were mounted with ProLong Gold and imaged on a Leica SP8. For quantification, tissue sections were tiled at ×20 magnification. For representative images, 1-µm z-stacks were taken through the tissues on ×20.

Massonʼs trichrome staining was performed on adjacent tissue sections to visualize collagen deposition and fibrotic regions. Trichrome staining was used in conjunction with immunofluorescence and RNAscope to co-localize the activated fibroblast populations with areas of increased extracellular matrix remodeling.

Imaging analysis

Each individual channel from individual tiled images was exported, and data were analyzed using the cell counter tool in ImageJ (version 1.54f). Individual channels for each image were stacked, and, using the cell counter/point tool, positively stained cells were counted on each channel. Using the nuclei channel as a guide, double or triple positively stained cells were identified.
