A pseudotime-dependent TWAS framework identifies disease genes along cell developmental paths
Authors: Rui Cao, Chunlin Li, Erjia Cui, Logan Spector, Andrew Raduski, Nathan Anderson, Weihua Guan, Peter Gordon, Cindy Im, Tianzhong Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryTranscriptome-wide association studies (TWASs) link genes to disease risk by integrating gene expression with genome-wide association study (GWAS) data. The growing availability of single-cell expression data offers the opportunity to dissect these associations at finer cellular resolution and uncover effects masked in bulk TWAS analyses. Existing single-cell TWAS methods often map associations to discrete cell types, potentially overlooking the continuous nature of cellular processes and misidentifying the causal cell stages where genes exert their effects. To address this limitation, we developed the pseudotime-dependent TWAS (pt-TWAS), a framework that models gene expression as a continuous function of pseudotime to capture dynamic gene effects along developmental trajectories. By flexibly modeling and utilizing shared genetic effects across cell stages, this approach achieved higher statistical power than existing single-cell TWAS methods in our extensive simulations. pt-TWAS further enables identification of causal cell stages underlying disease risk by constructing confidence bands for gene effect curves. Applied to a GWAS of B cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia using single-cell data from OneK1K, pt-TWASs replicated known risk genes and pinpointed their relevant cell stages, demonstrating its utility for revealing fine-grained, cell-stage-specific genetic mechanisms. An R package implementing pt-TWASs is available on GitHub.

Introduction

Genome-wide association studies (GWASs) have identified thousands of genetic loci associated with complex human diseases.1 However, because a majority of risk variants reside in non-coding regions of the genome, interpreting their biological mechanisms remains a notable challenge.2,3 Transcriptome-wide association studies (TWASs) facilitate the interpretation by shifting the unit of association from individual genetic variants to genes.4,5 As a gene-based method, TWAS leverages genetic variants as instruments to predict gene expression levels and then tests for associations between the predicted expression and a disease of interest. This approach not only boosts statistical power by aggregating the effects of multiple variants but also aligns with an instrumental variable (IV) regression framework, providing an approach for inferring potential causal genes. Furthermore, driven by large-scale public bulk-tissue expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) resources, such as the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project,6 TWAS can also provide a means to investigate the tissues through which genetic regulation may influence disease biology. These resources provide pre-computed expression imputation models, enabling researchers using GWAS summary statistics to perform gene-based association tests and infer tissue of interest.7 Furthermore, there is a growing interest in genetic regulation at a finer scale. Recent cell-type-aware TWAS methods identify disease-associated genes at the cell-type level by modeling interactions with cell composition inferred from bulk tissue.8 However, such deconvolution-based approaches for inferring cell-type-specific genetic effects remain constrained by limited resolution.

Moving beyond bulk-tissue analysis, the advent of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) provides transcriptomic data with high cellular-level resolution. Existing single-cell TWAS approaches largely focus on discrete, annotated cell types9,10 and use the pseudobulk approaches in which averaged gene expression within each cell type is used as the prediction target. Recent methodological developments continue to concentrate on improving expression prediction models through advanced statistical methods rather than addressing the limitations of discrete cell-type modeling. For example, scTWASs11 model the pseudobulk counts within discrete cell types using a measurement model to account for single-cell noise and technical variations. scPrediXcan12 leverages a sequence-to-epigenomics deep learning model to predict gene expression from pseudobulk scRNA-seq datasets. EXPRESSO13 integrates external epigenomic and 3D genomic information to build expression prediction models using summary-level eQTLs derived from a specific cell type. While powerful, these analyses typically rely on static snapshots of gene expression and are heavily dominated by the common cell types. They overlook the rare cell types/subtypes and the dynamic nature of genetic regulation, evidenced by recent findings.14 This is a considerable limitation, as many diseases arise from perturbations of dynamic cellular processes such as differentiation, activation, or response to stimuli15,16,17 over a variety of cell stages. A key example and motivation for our study is acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), the most common childhood malignancy, characterized by malignant transformation and differentiation arrest of either B or T cell progenitors.18,19 Given that the B cell subtype accounts for the majority of ALL cases, its well-characterized developmental pathway from hematopoietic stem cells to mature plasma cells presents a clear modeling opportunity, particularly for the B cell stages present in accessible samples such as peripheral blood. Identifying how dynamic genetic effects change throughout these developmental processes is therefore critical for designing more effective therapies. While current chimeric antigen receptor therapies (CAR-Ts), for instance, successfully target broadly expressed antigens such as CD19,20 next-generation strategies could achieve greater precision by targeting specific maturational stages where the disease process originates.

To this end, we developed a novel TWAS framework that incorporates the dynamic single-cell transcriptomic data and disease GWAS data to infer causal genes at a cell-stage resolution. Specifically, we utilize a powerful and flexible pseudotime inference method to model cellular dynamics from cross-sectional scRNA-seq data to infer cell trajectories.21,22 By ordering cells based on transcriptional similarity, pseudotime algorithms can reconstruct continuous cellular trajectories, providing a proxy for true temporal progression without the need for often-impractical longitudinal sampling. This enables us to move beyond analyses that use binned cells (e.g., pseudobulk) and to model gene expression as a continuous function of a cell’s developmental state. Statistically, pseudotime-dependent TWAS (pt-TWAS) is a two-stage functional regression framework. In the first stage, we use a function-on-scalar regression model to predict the single-cell gene expression trajectory using local genetic variants. This stage jointly models cells across their trajectory and allows borrowing of eQTL information from more abundant cell types/stages, increasing gene expression imputation accuracy across the trajectory. In the second stage, we test for an association between this imputed functional gene expression trajectory and an outcome. Specifically, we are interested in (1) a global test on whether there is any gene effect and (2) a subsequent non-flat test on whether there is any pseudotime-varying effect, respectively. To infer the causal cell stage for a disease, we construct simultaneous confidence bands for the gene effect curve. As demonstrated in both our simulation and real data results, our method identifies dynamic and cell-stage-specific gene effects that can be missed by bulk-tissue or pseudobulk approaches. Our work has the potential to identify the precise cellular developmental window during which a gene confers risk, thereby guiding the development of interventions that target specific cell stages to prevent or mitigate disease-gene effects.

Material and methods

Model overview

Bulk-tissue TWAS usually consists of two stages: building an imputation model for gene expression within a tissue, i.e., averaged across cell types or stages, and testing its association with the disease of interest. In our framework, we develop a trajectory-based single-cell TWAS (pt-TWAS), in which gene expression is modeled not as static but as a function of cell stages, quantified as pseudotime t inferred from the overall gene expression profiles of single cells (Figure 1). In stage 1, we perform a function-on-scalar regression (Equation 5), fitting a linear mixed model with a group-lasso penalty to account for subject-level correlation and shrink the regression coefficients of SNPs with weak or no effect to zero, which is consistent with the empirical evidence that only a small number of cis-SNPs regulate gene expression in bulk tissue.4 In particular, the regression is fitted stepwise with respect to the degree of splines to control for smoothness and to prevent overfitting. In stage 2, we perform a scalar-on-function regression (Equation 11) for the outcome-gene association. Multiple hypothesis tests can be conducted to draw inferences of interest. These include a global null hypothesis (no gene expression effect across all cell stages) and a secondary null hypothesis (no cell-stage-dependent effect). To infer the association strength and direction over cell stages, we construct simultaneous confidence bands for the gene effect curve. Similar to bulk-tissue TWASs, pt-TWASs can be applied to GWAS individual-level data or summary statistics when a trained gene expression model and an external genotype reference panel are available (see material and methods regarding the summary statistics in the supplemental material and methods, section S2). Stage 1: Gene expression imputation model

Function-on-scalar regression

Suppose a single-cell dataset consists of S subjects and Ns cells from subject s, with the total cell number N=∑s=1SNs. We treat the expression of a single gene of interest as a continuous variable (log-transformed read counts),23 denoted by xsc for the subject s and cell c, where s = 1, …,S and c = 1, …,Ns. Let gs=(gs1,…,gsP)T be the genotype vector of P SNPs from subject s that are local to the gene of interest, us=(us1,…,uuQ)T as Q subject-level covariates (e.g., sex, age, etc.), and t∈[0,1] as the inferred pseudotime along a single trajectory, representing a cell’s progression along a developmental trajectory, which we assume to be known. For subject s and cell c, we have(Equation 1)xsc|tsc=fs(tsc)+rs+ϵsc.

Assume the random intercepts rs∼iidN(0,σr2) capture the within-subject correlation, the residuals ϵsc∼iidN(0,σ02), and the gene expression dynamics fs(tsc) can be modeled as a linear combination of M pre-specified basis splines b(tsc)=(b1(tsc),…,bM(tsc))T with subject-specific weights ws=(ws1,…,wsM)T:(Equation 2)fs(tsc)=∑m=1Mwsmbm(tsc)=b(tsc)Tws.

The weights wsm are modeled as a linear combination of a baseline, genetic effects, and covariate effects:(Equation 3)wsm=β0,m+gsTβg,m+usTβu,m,where β0,m is the intercept and βg,m=(βg,m1,…,βg,mP)T and βu,m=(βu,m1,…,βu,mQ)T are the genotype and covariate effect coefficients for the spline m. Rewrite ws in matrix form:(Equation 4)ws=β0+(IM⊗gsT)βg+(IM⊗usT)βu,where ⊗ is the Kronecker product and IM is an M × M identity matrix, β0=(β0,1,…,β0,M)T, βg=(βg,1T,…,βg,MT)T, and βu=(βu,1T,…,βu,MT)T. By simple algebra, the pseudotime-dependent model 1 can be further rewritten as(Equation 5)xsc|tsc=b(tsc)Tβ0+(b(tsc)T⊗gsT)βg+(b(tsc)T⊗usT)βu+rs+ϵsc=zs(tsc)Tβ+rs+ϵsc,by defining zs(tsc)T:=[b(tsc)b(tsc)T⊗gsTb(tsc)T⊗usT] and β:=(β0T,βgT,βuT)T.

Parametric estimation of β

In single-cell data, the gene expression in cells of subject s, Xs=xs1,…,xsNsT, is observed at time points ts1,…,tsNs. Stacking all S subjects, the full model can then be formulated as(Equation 6)X=Zβ+R+ϵ,where X=(X1T,…,XST)T, and Z, ϵ, and R are the stacked forms of zs(tsc), rs, and ϵsc respectively.

To reduce computational burden and alleviate the non-normality of xsc, which is characterized by excessive zeros in single-cell gene expression counts, we aggregate cells with similar pseudotime within the same subjects. This approach is in the spirit of single-cell aggregation methods.24 Within each subject, we cluster cells by Jenks natural breaks optimization by their pseudotime t, which minimizes the within-cluster variance and maximizes the between-cluster variance. Choice of the number of clusters L depends on empirical data with the goal to prevent a heavy computational burden, lessen zero-inflation issues, and reduce technical noise while also avoiding the loss of resolution that results from too few clusters. Following clustering, both the pseudotime and gene expression for each cell cluster are calculated as the mean values of the cells belonging to that cluster. We use the superscript a to denote cells after aggregation:(Equation 7)xsla=1Dsla∑k∈clusterlxsk,where Dsla is the size of cluster l from subject s. Let Dsa=(Ds1a,…,DsMa)T and Da=(D1aT,…,DDaT)T. Following the definition of X, Z, R, and ϵ, we have their aggregated form Xa, Za, Ra, and ϵa:(Equation 8)Xa=Zaβ+Ra+ϵa,where Ra∼N(0,ΣRa) and ϵa∼N(0,Σ0a). The variance of an aggregated observation is Var(Xsla)=σr2+σ02/Dsla. The covariance between two different aggregates for the same subject is Cov(Xsia,Xsja)=σr2 for i ≠ j. Therefore, the full variance-covariance matrix of the aggregated data, Σa, is block diagonal, with each variance block for each subject. The sth block has diagonal elements (Σsa)(l,l)=σr2+σ02/Dsla and off-diagonal elements (Σsa)(i,j)=σr2. To estimate β in Equation 8, we fit the model with a group-lasso penalty to induce SNP-wise sparsity. We minimize the loss function(Equation 9)L(β,Σa,Da,λ)=12N(Xa−Zaβ)T(Σa)−1(Xa−Zaβ)+λ∑p=1P(M‖βg,·p‖2),and(Equation 10)βˆ=arg minβL(β,Σa,Da,λ),where βg,·p=(βg,1p,…,βg,Mp)T is the regression coefficients for SNP p and M splines and λ is the penalty parameter tuned by cross validation. Since the variance components σ02 and σr2 are usually unknown, we employ a two-step procedure to estimate β and Σa (Algorithm 1). First, we obtain an initial estimate of the coefficients, βˆind, by fitting the group-lasso model assuming independent observations (i.e., setting Σa = I). We then estimate the variance components σˆ02 and σˆr2 by applying restricted maximum likelihood (REML) to the residuals from this initial fit Xa−Zaβˆind. Second, we construct the estimated covariance matrix Σˆa using the estimated variance components from REML. Its sth block has diagonal elements (Σˆsa)(l,l)=σˆr2+σˆ02/Dsla and off-diagonal elements (Σˆsa)(i,j)=σˆr2. The final estimate βˆ is then obtained by solving the group-lasso problem with correlated residuals, which is equivalent to decorrelating the outcome and predictors using (Σˆa)−1/2 and fitting a standard group-lasso model. Choice of spline basis b(t)

Using single-cell gene expression data, we empirically find that orthonormal basis spline functions b(t) up to the third order of t adequately model the gene expression curves. The candidate basis splines are derived through a Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization onto splines {1,t,t2,t3}. However, overfitting on the basis splines may lead to bias in the stage 2 regression, analogous to the weak instrument (IV) problem documented in the two-stage least-squares or Mendelian randomization (MR) literature.27,28 To ensure sufficient strength for IVs, analogous to multi-exposure IV regression,29,30 we propose a forward stepwise procedure to control the number of fitted splines, where we examine the additional prediction power contributed by each basis spline conditional on the lower-order splines.

As shown in the Algorithm 1, we first regress the target gene expression onto the flat-spline-only b1(t) and get its cross-validated (hˆ2)(1) between predicted and observed outcomes. Next, we repeat the algorithm by including a higher-order spline b2(t) and get the cross-validated (hˆ2)(2). We keep the higher-order spline b2(t) in the prediction model only if the additional variance explained (hˆ2)(2)−(hˆ2)(1) is larger than a pre-specified threshold. The procedure above is repeated stepwise until an additional higher-order spline no longer results in an improvement in cross-validated hˆ2.

Notably, our framework includes the standard pseudobulk imputation model as a special case (i.e., number of clusters L set to be 1). A pseudobulk analysis aggregates all cells from a given subject to derive a single, average expression value, thus disregarding the underlying cellular trajectory. In this case, the weak IV problem reduces to the standard case commonly studied in the literature.27,28 For this reason, we set η = 0.01 in our current practice, following what has been used in standard TWAS pipelines.4,31,32

Stage 2: Association test with GWAS

Scalar-on-function regression

Now we have the regression coefficient estimates βˆ=(βˆ1T,…,βˆMT)T. Suppose a GWAS dataset consists of N˜ subjects, with subjects that do not overlap with those in the stage 1 single-cell dataset. The genotype matrix G˜=g˜1,…,g˜N˜T can be defined as an N˜×P matrix with its (s,p)th entry as the genotype of SNP p from subject s. Here, we focus on a binary outcome Y=y1,…,yN˜T; however, the method can be conveniently generalized to other types of outcomes. To test its association with the imputed gene expression, we model the total impact of gene expression on disease risk as an integral over the cell stages. Thus, we have the scalar-on-function regression model(Equation 11)logit(Pr(ys=1))=α0+∫01α(t)(b(t)T⊗g˜sT)βˆgdt,where α0 is the intercept effect and α(t) is the gene expression effect over pseudotime t. Similarly, we can decompose α(t) using several basis splines b˜(t)=(b˜1(t),…,b˜M˜(t))T:(Equation 12)α(t)=∑m=1M˜αmb˜m(t),leading to(Equation 13)logit(Pr(ys=1))=α0+∑m=1M˜[αm∫01b˜m(t)(b(t)T⊗g˜sT)βˆgdt]=α0+g˜sTBˆT(∫01b(t)b˜(t)Tdt)α,where α=(α1,…,αM)T is the regression coefficients and Bˆ(M×P)=(βˆ1,…,βˆM)T is the stacked form of βˆ. Practically, we use the same orthonormal splines as in stage 1, based on the assumption that gene effect along the cell lineage is smooth. Under this case, the integral ∫01b(t)b˜(t)Tdt becomes an identity matrix, and the model takes the form of a standard logistic regression.

Hypothesis testing

As α is the spline coefficients for modeling α(t), we are interested in testing two null hypotheses: the global test and the non-flat test. For the global test, the null hypothesis H0 and its alternative H1 are defined as(Equation 14)H0:α1=…=αM˜=0H1:α1≠0or…orαM˜≠0.

Rejecting H0 can be interpreted as a significant association between gene expression and the disease. For the non-flat test, the null hypothesis H0′ and its alternative H1′ are(Equation 15)H0′:α2=…=αM˜=0H1′:α2≠0or…orαM˜≠0.

Note that b˜1(t)=1 is the flat spline, that is, a pseudotime-invariant effect on the outcome. Rejecting H0′ therefore indicates a significant pseudotime-varying association between gene expression and disease. The non-flat test may be of interest, as it provides evidence on the time-varying gene effects that cannot be captured by existing pseudobulk methods. Both tests are based on Wald statistics, which asymptotically follow χM˜2 and χM˜−12s under their respective null hypotheses. To control the family-wise error rate of simultaneously conducting two tests, we use a hierarchical testing procedure: the global null hypothesis H0 is first tested at significance level θ and the secondary non-flat hypothesis H0′ is tested at the same level only upon rejection of H0. This guarantees the overall type I error rate does not exceed θ.

Besides the global test and non-flat test, testing each single component of α may be relevant when b˜ is a step function, as it is equivalent to testing the conditional cell-stage-specific gene effect. A one-degree-of-freedom Wald test can be applied here.

Pointwise and simultaneous confidence band inference

If the global null hypothesis H0 is rejected, we are also interested in the inference about αˆ(t), that is, at which specific cell stages the gene expression is associated with the disease. Under confidence level 1 − θ, the pointwise confidence band hp(t) can be straightforwardly derived from the regression coefficients αˆ:(Equation 16)hp(t):={hp(t)|Pr(αˆ(t)−hp(t)<α(t)<αˆ(t)+hp(t))=1−θforanarbitraryt∈[0,1]}hp(t)=γ1−θ/2Varˆ(αˆ(t))=γ1−θ/2b˜(t)TVarˆ(αˆ)b˜(t),where γ1−θ/2 is the 1 − θ/2 quantile of a standard normal distribution and Varˆ(·) is the estimated variance-covariance matrix.

However, this approach focuses on the confidence intervals of individual points and does not take into account the multiple comparisons over t ∈ [0,1]. Thus, we also construct a simultaneous confidence band hs(t) in our study, defined as(Equation 17)hs(t):={hs(t)|Pr(αˆ(t)−hs(t)<α(t)<αˆ(t)+hs(t)forallt∈[0,1])=1−θ}.

We construct the simultaneous confidence band using a parametric bootstrap method,33,34 which empirically controls the simultaneous coverage probability by testing it over a fine grid of points on the interval t ∈ [0,1], as detailed in Algorithm 2. Choice of spline basis b˜(t)

In scalar-on-function regression, our stage 2 regression model assumes the same smooth basis splines as in stage 1, that is, b˜(t)=b(t). This provides flexibility to approximate dynamic associations to continuous smooth curves. Herein, we discuss the impact of potential model misspecification (i.e., choice of wrong spline basis) in stage 2 regression, with respect to the dimension of stage 2 spline basis M˜.•M˜>M: when the stage 2 basis dimension is greater than the stage 1 dimension, the regression model is not identifiable because the regression matrix g˜sTBˆT(∫01b(t)b˜(t)Tdt) is not of full column rank (supplemental information). In other words, when the variation of the gene expression curve is captured by M splines, its effect curve α(t) cannot be modeled by a higher number of coefficients. This is analogous to a conventional IV regression where the number of exposures cannot exceed the number of IVs.•M˜=M: a natural choice in stage 2 is to use the same orthonormal splines as those in stage 1, reflecting the assumption that the gene effect α(t) is continuous and smooth. Alternatively, one could use a different spline basis (b˜(t)≠b(t)) of the same dimension. For example, the use of step functions as basis splines in stage 2 assumes the gene effect is constant within discrete cell stages. We show that in this case, the p value of the global test remains invariant to the choice of basis (proof in supplemental material and methods, section S1, and empirical evidence in simulation studies for pt-TWAS), although the estimated confidence bands depend on the choice of spline basis. For the purpose of identifying causal cell stages, we recommend sensitivity analyses with alternative bases (e.g., step functions). Consistency of the inferred stages across spline bases would indicate greater robustness.•M˜<M: it is also feasible to use a lower-dimensional spline basis in stage 2 regression. For instance, gene expression can be modeled as dynamic across cell stages, while its effect on the outcome is assumed to be pseudotime-invariant (b˜(t)=1). This approach is distinct from a standard pseudobulk analysis, where both the gene expression and its effect are modeled as constant. More generally, using fewer basis functions in stage 2 can increase statistical power when higher-order effects are weak or absent, as tests incorporating more splines lose efficiency due to the additional degrees of freedom.

Simulation studies for pt-TWAS

We performed extensive simulations to evaluate the type I error and power for the global and non-flat tests both under correct model specification and model misspecification. Furthermore, we benchmarked our method with a pseudobulk TWAS across the whole-cell stages and specific cell stages of interest. In addition to identifying genes of interest, we also examined the performance of identifying causal stages using the simultaneous confidence band beyond simple visualization.

To assess the type I error of pt-TWAS, we simulated two scenarios under the global null hypothesis of no association (α(t) = 0). In the literature, single-cell gene expression is commonly modeled in two ways, either as a continuous variable (e.g., after log transformation)23,35 or as counts with or without explicit modeling of excessive zeros.23,36,37 Scenario 1 assumed data conformed to our model’s assumptions, with the gene expression outcome xsc simulated as a continuous variable from model 5. Scenario 2 evaluated the robustness under model misspecification of the gene imputation model (stage 1) by generating continuous values as in scenario 1, then exponentiating and flooring them into integers, thereby introducing excess zeros. Note that in scenario 2, we evaluated the type I error control under both violations of the linearity and residual normality assumptions.

We also compared the empirical power of our method with pseudobulk TWAS, where the gene expression level was the mean of cell-level expressions per individual. Specifically, the pseudobulk approach applied a Lasso penalty term for SNP-wise sparsity in the gene imputation model and regressed the outcome onto the scalar gene expression. We considered α(t) as a linear (scenario 3) or quadratic function (scenario 4) of t, testing our method under various gene effects over cell stages. These scenarios mimicked biological processes where a gene’s influence on disease risk differs in direction over cell stages. Such signals can be attenuated or lost in bulk-tissue and pseudobulk analyses due to averaging. In scenario 5, α(t) was simulated as a step function, mimicking a biological process where the a gene’s causal effect is exclusively active within a specific cell stage. This design allows us to evaluate the pt-TWAS performance under model misspecification, that is, the use of smooth splines to approximate a discrete stepwise gene effect. Moreover, we evaluate whether pt-TWAS is able to borrow eQTL information from adjacent cell stage with higher cell abundance and compared the statistical power with cell-stage-specific pseudobulk TWAS.

In all simulation scenarios, the gene had p = 50 eQTLs and Q = 5 covariates in the single-cell dataset (stage 1). Five of the 50 eQTLs and all of the five covariates had non-zero effects on gene expression. SNPs were generated from an independent binomial distribution with a frequency of 0.4, and covariates were drawn independently from standard normal distributions N(0,1). The residuals came from independent standard normal distributions ϵsc∼iidN(0,1). In stage 2, we simulated N˜=2,000 GWAS subjects, with genotypes following the same distributions as in stage 1. The binary outcomes were simulated from the logistic regression model, where the intercept term was set to achieve an approximate 1:1 case-control ratio. Type I error and statistical power of hypothesis tests were estimated as the empirical probability of rejecting the global or non-flat null hypotheses across 1,000 repetitions. The power of confidence band inference is defined as the probability that the simultaneous confidence band does not cover 0 at some pseudotime point within a given interval. Real data analysis

Single-cell gene imputation models

The single-cell gene imputation models were derived from the OneK1K study (Gene Expression Omnibus: GSE196830),14 to date the largest public single-cell peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) dataset. It consists of 1,102 individuals of Northern European ancestry and scRNA-seq data from 1,267,768 PBMCs of 32,738 genes. After genotype calling and imputation, 4,749,893 SNPs from 23 chromosomes were genotyped and imputed for the subjects. The scRNA-seq data were cleaned according to Yazar et al.14 Gene counts were normalized by library size per cell and log transformed (log x + 1). Since we focus on B cell ALL (B-ALL), we annotated these PBMCs using the R package Azimuth38 and identified 112,098 naive and memory B cells combined. For the B cells, we inferred a single trajectory from the naive B cell stage to the memory B cell stage, with pseudotime defined as the cell rank along the trajectory linearly projected to the [0, 1] interval. The trajectories and pseudotime were inferred using slingshot.21 Details for the OneK1K genomic data cleaning and processing can be found in supplemental material and methods, sections S3–S5. We empirically set the number of clusters, L, to 10, given the average number of B cells per individual is approximately 100.

GWAS: B cell acute lymphocytic leukemia

ALL is a childhood cancer of the blood and bone marrow, characterized by overproduction and proliferation of abnormal immature lymphocytes. B-ALL, specifically, is the predominant ALL subtype arising when B cell proliferation becomes uncontrolled. Previous GWASs and TWASs39,40,41,42,43 have identified several dozen genetic loci related to B-ALL, but it remains unknown at which stage of B cell development these genes initiate or influence ALL. The B-ALL individual-level GWAS data come from the SMILES study,44 consisting of 842 affected individuals and 1,570 control subjects of European ancestry. The affected individuals were children aged 0 to 14 years between 1987 and 2014 in Michigan with a B-ALL diagnosis, and the control subjects were matched to affected individuals under a case-control ratio of 1:2. In this study, we investigated 17 candidate genes previously reported to be associated with B-ALL and applied pt-TWAS to identify the cell stages of these associations. The main result of pt-TWAS will be presented under the orthonormal spline basis, i.e., continuous functions. As a sensitivity analysis, we analyzed the significant genes using a step spline basis by modeling abrupt changes in gene effects across developmental cell stages. We compared the performance with a pseudobulk TWAS approach on averaged counts across normalized single-cell counts for naive and memory B cell stages separately.

Results

Simulation studies

We conducted simulation studies with five distinct scenarios to evaluate the performance of our method. The first two scenarios assessed the type I error rate under the null hypothesis of no association (α(t) = 0). Scenario 1 simulated gene expression as a continuous outcome directly from our proposed model (Equation 6), representing an ideal setting. Scenario 2 simulated a sparse count-based expression, with an average zero proportion of 36.7%. In both scenarios, the empirical type I errors for the pt-TWAS global and non-flat tests, as well as for the pseudobulk TWAS, were well controlled at a 0.05 significance level (Figure 2A). In scenarios 3 and 4, we evaluated the statistical power of pt-TWAS relative to the pseudobulk TWAS. In scenario 3, both the underlying gene expression trajectory fs(t) and the true effect curve α(t) were linear functions of pseudotime. In scenario 4, we simulated a more complex non-linear association where both functions were quadratic. In both settings, pt-TWAS achieved higher power than the pseudobulk approach (Figure 2A). The power advantage was particularly pronounced in Scenario 4, where the true effect was positive at the beginning and end of the trajectory but negative in the middle. This non-linear pattern of α(t) resulted in the effects largely being canceled out when aggregated in the pseudobulk TWAS analysis, leading to a near-complete loss of power. Furthermore, our estimated effect curves, αˆ(t), successfully recovered the true functional form of the association in both power scenarios (Figures 2B and 2C), thereby contributing to their higher power than pseudobulk TWAS.

In scenario 5, the true effect α(t) was a discrete step function, where the gene had a constant effect over the earlier cell stage t ∈ [0,0.8] but no effect over the later cell stage t ∈ [0.8,1]. Specifically, cell abundance between the two cell stages was imbalanced (ratio of 1:10), with the earlier stage occurring more rarely. Using linear smooth splines b˜(t) in stage 2 regression, pt-TWAS approximated the true effect α(t) as linear curves (Figure 2D). Consistent with the proof in the supplemental material and methods, section S1, we observed that the power of the Wald-statistic-based global test was not affected by the choice of splines b˜(t), i.e., using a linear basis vs. the correct step function. While the pt-TWAS global test was less powerful than the standard pseudobulk test (0.680 vs. 0.790), it outperformed a pseudobulk analysis restricted to the earlier stage (0.523), where only earlier-stage cells were used in the gene imputation model. Notably, the pt-TWAS global p value is invariant to the range of cell stages in stage 2 (t ∈ [0,1] or [0,0.8]) because it remains invariant with regression splines b˜(t). This demonstrates that pt-TWAS’s increased statistical power stems from a more accurate gene imputation model, built by leveraging a larger cell sample to estimate cross-stage eQTL effects. Additionally, the type I error for a later-stage-specific (t ∈ [0.8,1]) pseudobulk analysis was inflated (0.648), driven by the strong correlation in imputed gene expression between the two cell stages. An analogous situation occurs in GWAS, where linkage disequilibrium can produce spurious signals at non-causal SNPs. Therefore, such marginal cell-stage-specific pseudobulk analyses may not be ideal for identifying causal cell stages.

As shown in Table 2, stronger effects led to greater power to identify causal cell stages across all scenarios. Compared with smooth splines, the analysis had greater power with discrete step splines, which aggregate cell stages with the same causal direction. To evaluate the robustness of pt-TWAS against potential errors in pseudotime estimation, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by introducing stochastic noise into the inferred pseudotime values (Table S3). Under the null hypothesis of no genetic effect (scenario 1), the model maintained well-controlled type I error rates for both the global (0.045) and non-flat (0.041) tests. In the presence of dynamic effects, the framework remained capable of detecting associations and characterizing their functional forms; however, we observed a reduction in statistical power compared to the primary simulations. For instance, in scenario 3 (linear gene effect α(t)), the empirical power for the global test decreased from 0.374 to 0.362, while in scenario 4 (quadratic gene effect α(t)), the power of the global test decreased from 0.722 to 0.540 (non-flat test power from 0.788 to 0.609). Similar power loss was also present when inferring causal cell stages (for example, in scenario 3, the power to identify the cell stage with a positive gene effect decreases from 0.336 to 0.303). Collectively, these results demonstrate that although errors in pseudotime inference can lead to lower statistical power, the false positive rate of pt-TWAS discovery remains well controlled.

The second significant gene, COMMD3, also demonstrated a global association with B-ALL (global p = 5.42 × 10−3). In contrast to IKZF1, its effect was relatively constant across B cell developmental stages (non-flat p = 1.94 × 10−1). In sensitivity analyses, we evaluated the impact of the choice of regression splines and the number of cell aggregations, L. First, we estimated the simultaneous confidence bands using step functions for b˜(t) (Figure S1). The inferred causal cell stages were consistent with those found using smooth orthonormal splines. This result demonstrated the robustness of our research findings in the B cell lineage. Second, for the genes initially identified as significant (IKZF1 and COMMD3) with L = 10, we re-ran the pt-TWAS analysis using L = 5 or L = 15 (Table S1). We noticed that when L = 15, the imputation performance (hˆ2) of both genes decreased, suggesting that a larger number of clusters, or equivalently a smaller number of cells per cluster, may not work well for these two genes. When L = 5, the global test results remain consistent for IKZF1, but the COMMD3 global p value fell below the significance threshold.

We also performed a standard pseudobulk analysis using the same B cells as in pt-TWAS. In this analysis, only one gene, FIGNL1, passed the heritability cutoff. The poorly imputed pseudobulk models were also noted in a recent TWAS publication using OneK1K single-cell data.12 The higher number of imputable genes observed in pt-TWAS was likely because our method uses a mixed-effect model and effectively captures dynamic eQTL effects. Notably, both the pseudobulk and pt-TWAS analyses revealed a negative association between FIGNL1 expression and B-ALL. This finding is consistent with previous reports linking the deletion or loss of FIGNL1 to increased disease susceptibility, as it frequently undergoes interstitial deletion concomitant with IKZF1 in pediatric B-ALL.50 The p value of pseudobulk analysis was lower than pt-TWAS, potentially due to no or very mild cell-stage-dependent effect (selected number of splines = 1).

Discussion

In this work, we introduced pt-TWAS, a novel framework that integrates the principles of TWASs with single-cell data by modeling gene expression as a continuous function of pseudotime. Our framework is implemented as a two-stage functional regression. In the first stage, we build a predictive model for the gene expression trajectory using a parametric function-on-scalar mixed model, with a group-lasso penalty to enforce SNP-wise sparsity. In the second stage, we carry out a scalar-on-function regression between a disease outcome and the imputed expression trajectory. This allows us to formally test global and dynamic gene-disease associations, from which confidence bands can be constructed to infer the cell stages driving the association. Similar to existing TWAS methods, the data used in the two stages are not required to be collected from the same subjects. To further broaden the use of our framework, we show that the second-stage regression is also compatible with GWAS summary statistics (supplemental material and methods, section S2), which allows it to be readily applied to vast public datasets without requiring individual-level genotype data. The algorithm was implemented at https://github.com/RuiCao34/ptTWAS/. Herein, we highlight several key modeling considerations in developing the pt-TWAS framework. These include ensuring sufficient instrument strength for reliable gene expression imputation, appropriately specifying the functional basis of pseudotime-dependent effects, and accurately modeling single-cell expression measurements.

A key assumption for TWASs is the instrumental relevance, i.e., genetic variants/instruments explain enough variance in the gene expression imputation model. Analogous to the weak-instrument problem in conventional TWASs, that is, weak instruments can bias causal estimates in stage 2 regression, pt-TWAS can also be biased by such weak instruments. Our framework addresses this challenge with a two-tiered selection strategy (detailed in choice of spline basis b(t)). First, at the SNP level, the group-lasso penalty selects for genetic variants that have non-zero effects on the expression trajectory, effectively excluding weak or noisy SNPs from the model. Second, at the functional level, our forward stepwise procedure selects only those splines that contribute to the gene expression prediction. The stopping criterion for this procedure, a minimum increase in cross-validated R-squared, ensures the final imputed expression trajectory is adequately predicted by the genetic variants without overfitting.

Another modeling decision in scRNA-seq analysis is how to handle the gene expression measurements. Raw scRNA-seq count data typically exhibit high sparsity. Statistically, count-based distributions such as the negative binomial or its zero-inflated variants provide the most accurate representation of this data structure.35,51 However, fitting these complex, non-linear models is computationally intensive, particularly within the penalized, mixed-effects framework required for our analysis. A common and more tractable alternative is to apply a log transformation to the counts, which stabilizes variance and enables the use of the well-established linear models. Importantly, our simulations demonstrate that this choice is robust, as our framework successfully controls the type I error even when the underlying data are generated from a count distribution with excessive zeros. Furthermore, our decision to aggregate cells into small pseudotime-based clusters before log transforming represents a pragmatic compromise: it reduces the severity of zero inflation, making the Gaussian assumption more tenable, while preserving the high resolution of the trajectory and ensuring the model remains computationally efficient.

Our framework offers unique advantages over bulk-tissue and pseudobulk methods in both stages of analysis (the comparison is summarized in Table 1). In the first stage, conventional bulk or pseudobulk TWAS imputes average gene expression and estimates an average gene effect across all cell stages. While cell-stage-specific pseudobulk analysis can test effects within discrete stages, it is prone to misidentifying the causal gene stage due to expression correlation between them. In contrast, pt-TWAS models gene expression as a continuous trajectory. By leveraging the shared nature of eQTL effects across the cellular continuum, our model borrows information from abundant cell populations to improve expression imputation for rarer cell stages, which are often poorly predicted in the pseudobulk analyses. In the second stage, pt-TWAS performs a joint test on the entire imputed trajectory, unlike pseudobulk methods, which conduct a series of marginal tests at each stage. By analyzing the full expression lineage, our joint test has greater power to detect dynamic associations. This allows it to more accurately pinpoint the true causal cell stage and to avoid signal misidentification common in marginal analyses where expression levels are correlated across stages. On the other hand, when statistical power is the primary concern, pseudobulk TWAS may sometimes be preferred for common cell types with highly stage-specific genetic effects or constant gene effects. For the former, pt-TWAS may shrink highly stage-specific effects at all cell stages toward zero using group lasso in the first stage, whereas discrete pseudobulk analyses may better handle such scenarios by addressing them separately. For the latter, adopting a multi-parameter basis in the second stage when the gene effect is consistent may result in loss of statistical power due to the increased degrees of freedom. For this purpose, we recommend performing pt-TWAS with a single-parameter second stage as a sensitivity analysis.

We also explored the impact of model misspecification, specifically the choice of spline functions in stage 2 regression. We proved that while the global p value is invariant to the splines used in stage 2, the confidence band inference for localizing causal cell stages depends on this choice. Furthermore, simulations demonstrated that using step functions can increase statistical power, provided that gene effects are unidirectional within pre-defined cell stages. In practice, however, determining the optimal cell-stage intervals can sometimes be difficult and can compromise power and type I error for detecting the true causal stages. A more complicated modeling strategy can potentially mitigate this issue. Nonetheless, given this dependency, applying the current version of pt-TWAS with alternative spline configurations serves as an important sensitivity analysis.

We also performed a sensitivity analysis to assess how the number of cell clusters, L, influences our B-ALL findings for IKZF1 and COMMD3. Notably, gene imputation performance (hˆ2) for both genes improved at L = 5 (cluster with the larger number of cells) and declined at L = 15. We noted that the proportion of zero count after grouping decreased but was still relatively high for larger L (Table S2). The high zero proportion could be the driving factor of low imputation performance with larger L, and a smaller L that is capable of capturing a non-linear pattern can be more favorable (Table S1). In addition, TWAS results may be more sensitive to the cell aggregation parameter with high zero counts. Notably, IKZF1 showed greater robustness across L than COMMD3, potentially due to a higher zero proportion in COMMD3 expression (Table S2), which may induce instability and extreme values.52

Several limitations of our analysis should be acknowledged. First, inferring causal genes through pt-TWAS inherits the assumptions of two-stage least squares frameworks, such as TWAS and MR. Valid causal interpretation requires that the genetic instruments (1) are strongly associated with gene expression, (2) influence the outcome only through the target gene (no horizontal pleiotropy), and (3) are independent of unmeasured confounders. While our two-tiered instrument selection procedure helps address the first assumption and mitigates weak-instrument bias, the latter two are difficult to verify. In particular, horizontal pleiotropy remains a potential source of bias and warrants further study. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as robust gene-trait associations rather than definitive causal effects. Additionally, the current single-cell eQTL sample sizes may limit the accuracy of gene expression imputation, though ongoing efforts to generate larger and more diverse datasets53 are expected to improve both power and reliability.

Second, the inference of causal cell stages is subject to modeling and single-cell dataset choices. The confidence bands used to identify these stages depend on the spline basis used in stage 2 of pt-TWAS. A gene’s true effect trajectory can be smooth or exhibit abrupt changes, and the optimal spline basis is difficult to determine a priori. Smooth splines may obscure sharp, stage-specific effects and reduce statistical power, whereas discrete step functions risk identifying false positives if stage boundaries are specified incorrectly. Therefore, while our primary analysis uses smooth orthonormal splines, we acknowledge that the identified causal windows could shift under different assumptions, underscoring the importance of conducting sensitivity analyses to ensure robust conclusions. Moreover, inferring the causal cell stage is also dependent on the single-cell dataset used in stage 1. In our real data application to the ALL study, the OneK1K dataset captures B cells derived from the peripheral blood of healthy adults, which may not fully represent the disease-relevant context. Ideally, single-cell eQTL data from age-matched individuals and more relevant tissues, such as bone marrow, would provide a more accurate characterization of genetic regulation.43 In addition, the real data analysis could be further strengthened by expanding the set of genes under investigation and leveraging larger GWAS sample sizes.

Third, our framework assumes that the pseudotime ordering is known and reliable. In practice, pseudotime inference is challenging and introduces its own uncertainty.54 We conducted additional simulation analyses by introducing stochastic noise into the inferred pseudotime values. Our results demonstrate that while such uncertainty can lead to a reduction in statistical power, the framework maintains well-controlled type I error rates and remains robust in characterizing dynamic gene effects. In addition, pt-TWAS currently targets relatively simple trajectory structures (e.g., the cellular transition from naive B stage to memory B stage). Although complex trajectories can be decomposed into simpler segments, doing so may obscure meaningful biological patterns. Our current framework works well for B cell trajectories, which are relatively simple and well characterized. Future work may incorporate uncertainty from pseudotime inference and extend the model to accommodate more complex, branching lineages to broaden its applicability.

We recently became aware of a concurrent work by Qi et al.55 that, similar to our approach, incorporates single-cell transcriptomic data into a TWAS framework. While both studies share the motivation of modeling dynamic genetic effects and are built in the context of functional regressions, our method offers two distinct contributions. First, beyond the primary focus on gene discovery in Qi et al., our method enables inference of causal cell stages driving disease risk, an aspect of particular relevance to B-ALL biology. We developed an advanced statistical framework employing simultaneous confidence bands and varying spline basis functions to robustly infer these critical stages. Second, we identified and addressed the significant issue of weak-instrument bias in the functional TWAS framework, a problem that can lead to biased estimates and inflated type I errors. Recognizing this challenge guided key methodological choices in both stages of our model, resulting in more stable and reliable inference for gene-disease associations and causal cell stages.
