MicrobioME: a CURE model for diverse microbiology laboratory classrooms
Authors: Eryk J. Yarkosky, Chukwuma G. Udensi, Rosana B. R. Ferreira, Kelly J. R. Collins, Eileen M. Hotze
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTUndergraduate research experience (URE) positively contributes to student success, STEM identity, and STEM post-graduate careers. Despite these benefits, traditional UREs are limited, and many students encounter barriers to gaining access to these opportunities. Course-based Undergraduate Research Experiences (CUREs) increase access to UREs by incorporating authentic research projects into the course curriculum. Despite the recognized benefits of CUREs, few have been developed for introductory or upper-level microbiology courses. This scarcity largely reflects the substantial planning and financial investment required of faculty to create effective CUREs that are affordable, relevant, and align with course learning objectives. These combined demands present significant challenges to widespread adoption of CUREs in microbiology education. We considered these limitations when developing the “MicrobioME” CURE for implementation in pathogenic microbiology laboratory courses. Students participating in the MicrobioME CURE isolate and identify bacterial isolates from their own skin microbiome and investigate the impact of isolate-produced small molecules on Staphylococcus aureus biofilm production. We studied MicrobioME CURE implementation in two laboratory courses at the University of Kansas (KU) and West Virginia University (WVU) in cohorts differing in class size, research experience, meeting times, and faculty expertise in microbiome research, providing an opportunity to compare student outcomes in varied environments. Here, we report student outcomes of and provide tools for implementing the MicrobioME CURE in microbiology laboratory courses at other universities. Our results suggest that the MicrobioME CURE is scalable, affordable, and modular, making it an accessible and practical solution to increasing access to authentic research experience.

INTRODUCTION

Undergraduate research experiences (UREs) are recognized as high-impact educational practices that foster critical thinking, scientific literacy, and a sense of belonging within the scientific community (1). Engaging in authentic research allows students to develop technical skills (1), build confidence (2), and gain exposure to scientific inquiry beyond traditional classroom learning. For many, such experiences are pivotal in shaping future academic and professional trajectories (3, 4), particularly for students from underrepresented or non-traditional backgrounds (2, 5). Despite these benefits, opportunities for undergraduates to participate in mentored research remain limited to a small portion of students (6).

Traditional UREs depend on students’ ability to commit time for research outside of classes (7), presenting accessibility barriers to students with jobs, caregiving responsibilities, or extracurricular commitments. Such constraints and competition for limited opportunities can keep otherwise motivated students from participating in UREs. These problems are exacerbated at non-research-heavy institutions such as community colleges or primary undergraduate institutions, which may lack an undergraduate research culture (8).

The framework of traditional UREs presents difficulties for faculty members tasked with mentoring undergraduate research. Faculty workloads, which include research, teaching, and service, limit time available for undergraduate research engagement, relegating undergraduate mentorship to a small portion of a faculty member’s duties (9). Additional factors, such as available funding, research area of focus, size of the lab, and success of the faculty’s research endeavors, may also influence the number of students mentored.

In response to these barriers, Course-Based Undergraduate Research Experiences (CUREs) have emerged as an inclusive and scalable alternative to the traditional apprentice-style URE model (10). CUREs embed authentic research questions into the course curriculum, allowing more students to engage in the scientific process within a structured academic setting. CUREs democratize access to research (11), enhance faculty research while reducing the time burden on individual faculty members, and motivate students to pursue careers in STEM (12, 13).

Designing a CURE that balances meaningful research with academic rigor presents logistical challenges. The instructor’s expertise can restrict the diversity of research topics, and many CUREs are difficult to adapt across varying academic levels, which restricts their application to both introductory and advanced students. The limited availability of CUREs in certain disciplines, such as pathogenic microbiology, illustrates these challenges. For example, as of the reporting of this manuscript, CUREnet, an online repository of CURE projects, lists only 17 CUREs designed for microbiology courses, with varied applicability to pathogenic microbiology courses (https://serc.carleton.edu/curenet/index.html).

To fill this gap, we present a modular CURE curriculum designed for flexibility in implementation in pathogenic and clinical microbiology laboratory courses. The CURE, titled MicrobioME: A CURE for Staphylococcus aureus, engages students in isolating and identifying bacterial members of their skin microbiome and testing whether these isolates can modulate biofilm formation by the human pathogen Staphylococcus aureus. Grounded in a socio-constructive framework, the curriculum uses authentic inquiry, collaboration, and iterative experimentation to support meaningful learning and strengthen students’ engagement with scientific practices. We implemented this CURE at two research-active institutions, the University of Kansas (KU) and West Virginia University (WVU), in courses that differed in class size, student experience, course structure, and faculty expertise. At KU, 47 students participated in the CURE under the guidance of an instructor with expertise in the skin microbiome and microbial interactions. At WVU, a modified version of the CURE was implemented with 12 students, led by a faculty member whose expertise was not specific to the skin microbiome.

Across both institutions, students participated in the scientific process, including hypothesis generation, experimental design, data analysis, and communication of findings. The clinical relevance and familiarity of S. aureus to healthcare made the project both accessible and engaging for students, while its alignment with core microbiology skills facilitated ease of adoption by instructors. Our results across two institutions suggest that the MicrobioME CURE can be implemented in classes of varying sizes, pre-requisite knowledge, and instructor expertise on the skin microbiome.

INTENDED AUDIENCE

MicrobioME CURE accommodates students with varying microbiology backgrounds. At KU, this CURE was implemented in a Pathogenic Microbiology lab for microbiology majors, many of whom were juniors and seniors; at WVU, it was implemented in a junior lab for Immunology and Medical Microbiology majors. Students at both institutions had completed foundational biology and microbiology lab courses and were co-enrolled in a pathogenic microbiology lecture. KU required only these prerequisites; WVU students had more extensive training in immunology and medical microbiology topics (Table 1). While this CURE was designed for an upper-division course, the curriculum can be modified for introductory courses, as bacterial identification through Gram staining and morphology is typically a key component of introductory curricula. Therefore, expectations for isolate identification could be reduced to the genus level. In addition, the technical simplicity of the biofilm assay allows for ease of implementation in an introductory course where CFCM of the isolates could be provided by the teaching team.

PREREQUISITE KNOWLEDGE

MicrobioME was designed for students who had a foundational understanding of core microbiology concepts and experience in basic microbiology lab techniques. For KU students, the Fundamentals of Microbiology course (BIOL400) and the associated lab course (BIOL402) were prerequisites. KU students were concurrently or previously enrolled in the Bacterial Infectious Disease lecture. WVU students were required to have completed the Microbial Genetics lab and lecture courses (for majors) with concurrent or prerequisite enrollment in the Bacterial Pathogenesis lecture course (Table 1).

MATERIALS

Students should have access to basic laboratory supplies and media common to microbiology laboratory courses. Assay-specific equipment is listed on the protocols but includes a 96-well plate reader, incubators, and MALDI-TOF MS target plates. Students were expected to maintain a lab notebook. Microsoft OneNote was used as a digital notebook for KU students, which allowed for collaboration, recording, and sharing data between lab partners and teaching staff. WVU students used a traditional composition book as a lab notebook and were assessed on their laboratory notes at two different times throughout the CURE.

PROCEDURE

MicrobioME was designed for flexible implementation for all levels of microbiology lab courses. Below, we describe the different modules and requirements for both faculty and students. Specific protocols used by KU and WVU students are provided in Supplemental Materials (Appendices 7 to 10).

Step 1: Isolation of skin commensal bacteria

The first step in the MicrobioME CURE was the isolation of bacteria from students’ skin. Prior to this lab, faculty should present a lecture on the skin microbiome, highlighting microbial species commonly associated with different regions (a sample lecture is available in Appendix 1). Variations of this lecture could include a discussion on various types of culture media and growth conditions used for bacterial isolation. Since students need to maintain their bacterial cultures throughout the CURE, faculty should emphasize using media that would isolate robust bacterial species that are easy to grow in the lab. For example, Staphylococcus species such as Staphylococcus epidermidis grow well on rich media, including Brain Heart Infusion, Terrific Broth, and Blood agar (14). In contrast, certain Corynebacterium species require additional growth factors, such as Tween, and are more effectively isolated using Brain Heart Yeast Extract Tween media (15). Students may be encouraged to explore different media types or limit the growth conditions based on available resources.

At KU and WVU, student pairs were provided sterile swabs, sterile saline, and agar plates, and instructed to design a protocol to isolate bacteria from their exposed skin. Students were instructed to restrict swab locations to the forehead, neck, arms, and back, avoiding open wounds, and areas surrounding the mouth, eyes, and genitals (Appendix 7). After incubation, students recorded the quantity and variety of colony morphologies and selected three colonies for further analysis. In subsequent lab sessions, students focused on isolating pure cultures. Gram staining was performed to ensure the purity of the culture, and the results were used as an initial characterization of the bacterial isolate. This step of the CURE may require up to four or five lab periods, depending on class frequency and student skill level.

Step 2: Identification of skin commensal isolates

Depending on the learning goals and biochemical basis of the course, faculty can provide molecular and/or biochemical tests for the identification of their bacterial isolates. KU students initially identified their bacterial isolates using matrix-assisted laser desorption–time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) and confirmed results using biochemical-based assays. WVU students had previous experience with biochemical methods for identification, so MALDI-TOF MS was the sole tool used for identification.

Biochemical analysis of commensal isolates (optional)

Based on the isolate Gram staining result and cellular morphology, KU students designed protocols for biochemical analysis of their isolate using provided references, including the Bergey’s Manual (16) and various identification websites (Appendix 11). Faculty should provide a list of available clinical media and biochemical tests for biochemical analysis of isolates. A general list of media used is provided (Appendix 12).

Molecular identification of commensal isolates (optional)

Students performed a PubMed search to investigate protocols for MALDI-TOF MS identification. Both KU and WVU students used a modified version of the MALDI-TOF MS direct transfer method (17), which required the least amount of equipment and was simple to perform (Appendix 8). Table 3 lists bacterial species identified by MALDI-TOF MS. While MALDI-TOF MS is a rapid and cost-effective means for identification, faculty should allocate time to repeat this process if needed. MALDI-TOF MS samples were analyzed as Le et al. (18) at the University of Houston-Clear Lake. Core facilities at other universities, including Pennsylvania State University, can also analyze targets for bacterial identification, as described previously (19).

Step 3: Assay for anti-biofilm activity

The final step in the CURE is testing isolates for the production of anti-biofilm molecules. For this module, students prepared cell-free conditioned media (CFCM) from overnight broth cultures of isolates, concentrated the CFCM, and tested for biomolecules that could inhibit Staphylococcus aureus biofilm activity in comparison with a control CFCM and CFCM from a bacterial isolate known to produce antibiofilm biomolecules (20). Students employed a 96-well plate safranin staining assay (21) for biofilm detection, as this protocol utilizes commonly available laboratory materials, is cost-effective, and straightforward to perform (Appendices 9 and 10). While S. aureus was used in this study, other biofilm-forming organisms or alternative assay formats may be used to evaluate CFCM activity according to the goals of each microbiology course.

At both KU and WVU, student pairs conducted the experiment in triplicate during three separate class periods, which offered valuable hands-on experience, reinforced students’ familiarity with the experimental design, and provided the opportunity to generate data suitable for statistical analysis. Iteration is a central feature of a CURE curriculum; therefore, faculty should allocate multiple class sessions to allow students to repeat the experiment to enable students to refine their techniques, either by testing different bacterial isolates or by focusing on a single isolate to achieve reproducible results.

Step 4: Data analysis and presentation of results

A learning goal of the MicrobioME CURE was to increase communication skills of scientific data. This was accomplished in a variety of ways; KU students either presented posters at an Undergraduate Research Symposium or oral presentations to the lab class, mimicking research lab meetings. At WVU, students were required to provide a 10–15 min research-style presentation, which speculated inhibitory mechanisms and suggested future directions on testing these hypotheses. At both KU and WVU, peer review was part of this process, and students were provided rubrics to critique presentations of their peers. Sample rubrics and student presentations are provided (Appendices 5 and 6).

SAFETY ISSUES

The CURE curriculum is intended for BSL2 labs. Students and faculty must complete biosafety level 2 training and follow ASM Guidelines for Biosafety in Teaching Laboratories, including wearing appropriate Personal Protective Equipment.

DISCUSSION

Field testing

MicrobioME was implemented over a single semester at two RO1 institutions: KU and WVU, within the Bacterial Infectious Disease Lab (BIOL507) and the Bacterial Pathogenesis Lab (IMMB 310L), respectively. Both courses share similar course learning outcomes but differ notably in class size, students’ prior research experience, and faculty expertise in the CURE area of research. At KU, 47 students participated in the CURE across two sections, led by a faculty expert in skin microbiome research, with 60% of students reporting no previous URE. In contrast, the IMMB 310L course at WVU enrolled 13 students in one section, over 90% of whom had prior URE, and was facilitated by a faculty with generalized knowledge of skin microbiome research. While both courses are taken by junior- and senior-level undergraduate students, WVU students had more prerequisite courses in bacterial pathogenesis and immunology prior to enrolling in the MicrobioME CURE (Table 1). These differences in student backgrounds provided a unique opportunity to assess the CURE’s impact across varying levels of research preparedness.

Evidence of student learning

Student outcomes of LOs were assessed within the limits of multiple assessment methods (Table 2), including pre- and post-quizzes, self-reported confidence survey questions, and student presentations. Briefly, LO1–5 were measured by pre- and post-CURE quiz questions linked specifically to these learning objectives. LO3 and LO5 were also assessed in student final presentations along with LO6 and LO7. LO1 and LO2 examine students’ knowledge about the clinical relevance and pathogenicity of S. aureus and their understanding of the composition and relevance of the skin microbiome. LO3 and LO4 assess technical knowledge in clinical and molecular techniques for the identification of bacterial isolates. LO5 and LO6 focus on experimental design and data analysis, while LO7 determines the ability to communicate scientific discovery to a larger audience. Paired answers on selected pre- and post-CURE quiz and survey questions were used to quantify student comprehension and perceived gains regarding the LOs. Unpaired results were excluded from analysis. Scientific presentation scores were used to measure gains in LO6 and LO7. The Laboratory Course Assessment Survey (LCAS) (22) was performed to assess design features unique to CURE curriculum development.

LO1–2: clinical relevance of S. aureus biofilms and protective role of skin microbiome

Paired pre- and post-quiz questions linked to LO1 and LO2 showed significant learning gains for the KU student cohort (Table 4). For quiz questions 3 and 4, KU students demonstrated gains of 64% and 35%, respectively. WVU students did not show significant gains for either quiz question, although these students’ pre-quiz scores for these questions were much higher than KU students. For example, 79% of WVU students accurately named three common skin bacteria compared to only 34% of KU students prior to participation in the CURE. However, post-CURE results demonstrate that 98% of KU students could accomplish this, a level similar to WVU (88%). When students’ perceived gains of these learning objectives were assessed (Fig. 3, Q1), both cohorts of students reported feeling significantly more confident in their knowledge of the clinical relevance of S. aureus biofilms and the role of the skin microbiome in innate immunity after participation in the CURE.

LO3–4: biochemical and molecular analysis of bacterial identification

Quiz questions 6A–D and Q8 were used to assess LO3; the ability of students to use biochemical analysis to identify unknown bacterial isolates. Gains in LO3 were significant for KU students but not WVU. At KU, the observed gains for LO3 can be attributed to a robust biochemical identification project integrated into the CURE, which relied on Gram stain, coagulase, and catalase, alongside other tests to confirm species identifications. Because pre-requisite WVU lab courses included a similar “unknown identification” project, WVU students did not participate in biochemical identification of their skin isolates, which could have impacted WVU students’ ability to state various biochemical characteristics of S. aureus. Likewise, quiz question 8, which also tested students’ ability to analyze biochemical data in bacterial identification and associate it with MALDI-TOF MS data, improved significantly only at KU and not WVU. The patterns observed for LO3 indicate that, regardless of prior experience with biochemical and molecular identification of bacterial isolates, recent experience and conversation about such topics are critical for observed gains.

LO4, which assessed student knowledge on MALDI-TOF MS as a tool for the identification of bacterial isolates, was tested using quiz question 7. Students at both KU and WVU employed MALDI-TOF-MS for the identification of their individual skin isolates, and this familiarity is reflected by significant gains in comprehension, as seen by 65% and 64% increases in correct answers to Q7 at KU and WVU, respectively.

LO5–6: analysis of antibiofilm activity and description of research objectives, protocols, and experimental design

LO5 addressed the ability of students to generate and analyze biofilm inhibition data using their skin isolates and was assessed by Q9b and presentation scores. Significant gains in the pre-/post-quiz analysis were not made by either KU or WVU students for Q9b. It is possible that this question was not an adequate test of students’ understanding of the effects of anti-biofilm molecules. Q9b focused on distinguishing between growth inhibition and biofilm inhibition, relying on students to observe a figure measuring growth of CFCM-treated cells (OD600), rather than OD492, which students used to analyze biofilm production during the project. We hypothesize that this slight distinction misled students and resulted in no gains. However, presentation results, discussed below, and students’ perceived gains in ability to test and analyze biofilm production data, which was evaluated by pre-/post-quiz Q9–11, were significantly increased at both KU and WVU, further supporting the possibility that Q9b did not adequately test students’ ability to analyze such data sets.

Students’ perceived ability to understand and design a protocol, as well as describe the goals and outcomes of their research (LO6), was tested by pre- and post-quiz survey questions Q2, Q3, and Q5 (Fig. 3). Perceived gains by students were assessed using a Likert scale, with scores ranging from 1 to 5 (strongly disagree to strongly agree). Significance testing was completed using the Mann-Whitney U test. The choice of statistical test was based on categorical data and non-normal distribution. Each of these questions demonstrated significant gains in student confidence across both KU and WVU.

LO7: communication of results and implications of research to a varied audience

Students’ ability to communicate results and implications (LO7) was assessed through brief “lab-meeting” style presentations. High average scores at KU (95.3%) and WVU (96.7%) indicate strong performance in explaining biofilm inhibition data, discussing implications, and proposing future experiments. KU students also presented via video or poster at a research symposium (not scored). Survey data (Q6, Fig. 3) reported increased confidence in communicating results after the CURE.

The LCAS was administered to characterize the ability of this CURE to provide an authentic research experience for students. High LCAS scores were reported in all aspects of an effective CURE: collaboration, discovery, relevance, and iteration (Table 5), suggesting a quality research experience for both KU and WVU. This demonstrates that the MicrobioME CURE can be successfully implemented in both small and large labs, regardless of faculty expertise in the skin microbiome, making it realistic to apply at other institutions.

Concluding remarks

The successful implementation of this CURE in both a small course and a large course across two universities demonstrates the applicability of this CURE for a diverse range of class sizes, lab schedules, and experience levels of both students and faculty. The large course at KU implemented the CURE as a semester-long project, while the smaller course at WVU implemented components of the project on an accelerated schedule. Both student groups demonstrated significant gains in their ability to describe the relationship between S. aureus pathogenesis and the skin microbiome, as well as describing the function and application of molecular tools for bacterial identification, such as MALDI-TOF-MS. In addition, students at both institutions were significantly more confident in their ability to conduct and communicate research following participation in the CURE. Interestingly, the results from the pre- and post-quiz for LO1–2 showed more significant gains among KU students, who, as a group, had far less research experience than the group at WVU. In addition, the KU student group achieved similar scores as the WVU group in the post-quiz for LO1–2. At least for LO1–2, which focused on the clinical relevance of biofilms and Staphylococcus aureus, the difference in these gains suggests that this CURE enhanced and reinforced student comprehension of important scientific concepts relating to biofilms and the clinical relevance of Staphylococcus aureus.

We have described a robust CURE highly relevant to undergraduate microbiology majors. Our assessment of student outcomes of learning objectives demonstrates how this CURE can be modified to emphasize different combinations of technical skills and clinical applications. This CURE could easily be modified to emphasize the clinical relevance of biofilm formation for students taking a microbiology lab as an allied healthcare major (i.e., nursing, dental hygienist, etc.) by focusing more on the identification methods and challenges associated with combating bacterial infections that form robust biofilms. In future iterations, we plan to incorporate methods to characterize the molecule responsible for S. aureus biofilm inhibition, placing more emphasis on the significance of basic science in helping us identify novel ways to solve problems relating to biofilm formation and its role in antibiotic resistance. These modifications emphasize the extensive modularity of this CURE and how it can be continually revised for either (i) more advanced scientific questions for more senior undergraduate students in basic science majors or (ii) more introductory microbiology courses to emphasize the clinical relevance of biofilms for allied health majors. Overall, the MicrobioME CURE can be made to fit the schedules of various courses, as well as simplified to satisfy the curriculum of introductory microbiology courses. The implementation and continued evolution of this CURE will undoubtedly emphasize the importance of research experiences and foster confidence among all undergraduate student groups pursuing STEM-related careers in healthcare and science.
