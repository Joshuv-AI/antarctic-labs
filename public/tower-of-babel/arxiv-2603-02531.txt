Bridging Diffusion Guidance and Anderson Acceleration via Hopfield Dynamics
Kwanyoung Kim

Abstract
Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG) has significantly enhanced the generative quality of diffusion models by extrapolating between conditional and unconditional outputs. However, its high inference cost and limited applicability to distilled or single-step models have shifted research focus toward attention-space extrapolation. While these methods offer computational efficiency, their theoretical underpinnings remain elusive. In this work, we establish a foundational framework for attention-space extrapolation by modeling attention dynamics as fixed-point iterations within Modern Hopfield Networks. We demonstrate that the extrapolation effect in attention space constitutes a special case of Anderson Acceleration applied to these dynamics. Building on this insight and the weak contraction property, we propose G eometry A ware Attention G uidance ( GAG ). By decomposing attention updates into parallel and orthogonal components relative to the guidance direction, GAG stabilizes the acceleration process and maximizes guidance efficiency. Our plug-and-play method seamlessly integrates with existing frameworks while significantly improving generation quality.

Introduction

The emergence of text-to-image (T2I) diffusion models
(
Rombach et al. 2022b
;
Podell et al. 2023
;
Esser et al. 2024
)
has revolutionized the landscape of generative artificial intelligence, achieving remarkable success in tasks ranging from static image synthesis
(
Xie et al. 2024
;
Labs 2024
)
to dynamic video generation
(
Blattmann et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2024
;
Yang et al. 2024
;
Team 2025
)
. Despite their ubiquitous adoption, standard sampling processes in diffusion
(
Ho et al. 2020
)
and flow-matching models
(
Lipman et al. 2022
)
often remain suboptimal. To address this, the field has entered a ”great guidance sampling era,” where various techniques are employed to refine the sampling trajectory. The cornerstone of this era is Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG)
(
Ho & Salimans 2022
)
, which enhances generation quality by extrapolating between conditional and unconditional score estimates. By pushing the sampling direction toward the conditional distribution, CFG effectively improve generation quality, albeit at a significant cost.

Despite its success, CFG is limited by dual-pass inference costs and the requirement for null-condition training, hindering its use in few-step distilled or single-step models
(
Lin et al. 2024
;
Sauer et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2024
)
. While recent training-free methods
(
Ahn et al. 2025
;
Hong 2024
;
Rajabi et al. 2025
;
Kim 2025
)
bypass training by perturbing internal representations, such as corrupting self-attention or token embeddings, they still require two forward passes. Consequently, these approaches are still difficult to integrate with few-step generation frameworks.

To overcome this issue, attention-space extrapolation has emerged as a promising paradigm. Specifically,
(
Kim & Sim 2025
)
viewing attention through the lens of sparse Hopfield networks, leverage noise-robustness to extrapolate between sparse and dense maps. Similarly,
(
Chen et al. 2025
)
extrapolates attention signals from positive and negative prompts. Despite these empirical gains in quality and efficiency, the underlying mechanics of attention-space extrapolation remain poorly understood. This lack of a formal theoretical framework leaves the field largely reliant on empirical heuristics.

In this paper, we establish the missing link between empirical heuristics and classical convergence theory for attention-space guidance in diffusion models. By framing attention dynamics in Modern Hopfield Networks (MHN)
(
Ramsauer et al. 2020
;
Hu et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2023a
)
as a fixed-point iteration, we prove that attention-space extrapolation is a special realization of Anderson Acceleration (AA)
(
Walker & Ni 2011
)
. This discovery provides the first principled explanation for the success of attention-space guidance, transforming empirical observations into a theoretically grounded framework for diffusion sampling.

Building on this foundation, we introduce a
G
eometry-
A
ware Attention
G
uidance (
GAG
). Inspired by geometric guidance principles
(
Sadat et al. 2024
)
, we decompose attention updates into parallel and orthogonal components relative to the guidance direction. This decomposition allows us to stabilize the acceleration process by selectively amplifying beneficial updates while suppressing harmful fluctuations. We further provide a theoretical guarantee for this stability by analyzing the weak contraction property
(
Alber & Guerre-Delabriere 1997
)
of attention dynamics.

Extensive experiments across diverse diffusion backbones and guidance frameworks demonstrate that our plug-and-play approach consistently enhances generation quality and text alignment. Notably, our method exhibits superior performance in distilled and few-steps models, where traditional CFG is often inapplicable or suboptimal. Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We bridge attention dynamics and Anderson Acceleration, framing attention extrapolation as a fixed-point acceleration process within MHN.

•

We introduce a novel attention extrapolation rule, called GAG, based on geometric decomposition that optimizes guidance trajectories by isolating beneficial updates, while rigorously establishing its stability via the weak contraction property of attention dynamics.

•

We demonstrate that our method achieves state-of-the-art performance across multiple diffusion backbones, showing high compatibility and effectiveness even in few-step generation regimes.

Preliminary

2.1
Conditional Diffusion Models

Diffusion models (DM)
(
Ho et al. 2020
;
Song et al. 2021b
)
learn to generate data by reversing a progressive Gaussian noise process. Starting from the data distribution
\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim q(\mathbf{x}_{0})
, the forward process iteratively perturbs the signal using a Markov chain:

q(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{t-1}):=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{t};\sqrt{1-\beta_{t}}\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\beta_{t}\mathbf{I})

(1)

This allows for a closed-form marginal distribution
q(\mathbf{x}_{t})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{t};\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\mathbf{x}_{0},(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t})\mathbf{I})
, where
\alpha_{t}=1-\beta_{t}
and
\bar{\alpha}_{t}=\prod_{i=1}^{t}\alpha_{i}
. As
t\to T
, the distribution converges to isotropic Gaussian noise
q(\mathbf{x}_{T})\approx\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
.

The reverse process
p_{\theta}
is optimized via denoising score matching
(
Vincent 2011
)
, which simplifies the training objective to noise prediction. For conditional generation
(
Rombach et al. 2022a
)
, where
\mathbf{c}
denotes external context such as text embeddings, the objective is defined as:

\min_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\mathbf{c}}\left[\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\mathbf{x}_{0}+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\boldsymbol{\epsilon},t,\mathbf{c})-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\|^{2}_{2}\right].

(2)

The sampling process follows the reverse diffusion trajectory starting from
\mathbf{x}_{T}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
. Using the DDIM
(
Song et al. 2021a
)
update rule, each step produces:

\mathbf{x}_{t-1}=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}(t)+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,\mathbf{c}),

(3)

where
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}(t):=(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,\mathbf{c}))/\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}
represents the denoised estimate of
\mathbf{x}_{0}
at time
t
. This estimation,
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_{0}|\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{c}]
, is derived from Tweedie’s formula
(
Efron 2011
;
Kim & Ye 2021
)
. This iterative refinement is performed sequentially from
t=T
to
1
to recover
\mathbf{x}_{0}
.

2.2
Guidance Sampling in Diffusion Models

To overcome the suboptimal performance of vanilla sampling in conditional generation, various guidance strategies have been proposed to enhance sample fidelity
(
Dhariwal & Nichol 2021
;
Ho & Salimans 2022
;
Ahn et al. 2025
;
Hong 2024
;
Kim 2025
)
. For brevity, let
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{c}):=\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,\mathbf{c})
and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{\varnothing})
denote the conditional and unconditional score estimates, respectively. Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG)
(
Ho & Salimans 2022
)
extrapolates the conditional probability relative to the unconditional baseline,
\hat{p}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{c})\propto p(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{c})(p(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{c})/p(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{\varnothing}))^{w}
, yielding the adjusted noise prediction:

\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\prime}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{c})=\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{c})+w(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{c})-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{\varnothing})).

(4)

where
w
is the guidance scale. More recently, ”weak model” guidance has emerged, which utilizes the difference between a standard conditional model and a perturbed variant
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}
:

\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\prime\prime}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{c})=\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{c})+w(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{c})-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{c})),

(5)

Heuristics for
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}
range from architectural flaws to self-attention map perturbations (e.g., identity-mapping
(
Ahn et al. 2025
)
, blurring
(
Hong 2024
)
, or Sinkhorn-based disruption
(
Kim 2025
)
). However, these approaches are limited by their dependence on manual layer selection, increased computational overhead, and lack of support for step-distilled frameworks.

To mitigate these issues, recent studies
(
Kim & Sim 2025
;
Chen et al. 2025
)
propose extrapolating across the attention space of all layers. Similar to the CFG framework, these methods refine the attention output by contrasting a strong attention mechanism
At
with a weakened counterpart
\tilde{\texttt{At}}
:

\displaystyle\texttt{At}^{\prime}(\mathbf{Q}_{t},\mathbf{K}_{t},\mathbf{V}_{t})

\displaystyle=\texttt{At}(\mathbf{Q}_{t},\mathbf{K}_{t},\mathbf{V}_{t})+

\displaystyle\lambda(\texttt{At}(\mathbf{Q}_{t},\mathbf{K}_{t},\mathbf{V}_{t})-\tilde{\texttt{At}}(\mathbf{Q}_{t},\mathbf{K}_{t},\mathbf{V}_{t})),

(6)

where
\mathbf{Q}_{t},\mathbf{K}_{t},\mathbf{V}_{t}
denote the query, key, and value matrices at time step
t
, and
\lambda
is the extrapolation scale. For instance, PLADIS
(
Kim & Sim 2025
)
employs generalized sparse mapping for the strong attention and original dense attention as the weakened reference. NAG
(
Chen et al. 2025
)
contrasts attention maps derived from positive and negative prompts. Despite their empirical success, the theoretical rationale for why such attention-space extrapolation enhances generation remains largely unexplored.

2.3
Energy-Based Interpretations of Attention

Theoretical studies have established a link between the attention mechanism and Energy-Based Models (EBMs), specifically Hopfield energy functions
(
Ramsauer et al. 2020
;
Hu et al. 2024
)
. In this framework, a query
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is associated with a pattern matrix
\mathbf{\Xi}=[\mathbf{\xi}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{\xi}_{M}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times M}
by minimizing an energy function
E(\mathbf{x})
through retrieval dynamics
\mathcal{T}
. Modern Hopfield Networks(MHN)
(
Ramsauer et al. 2020
)
define these as:

\displaystyle E(\mathbf{x})_{\texttt{Dense}}

\displaystyle:=-\texttt{lse}(\beta,\mathbf{\Xi}^{\top}\mathbf{x})+\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\rangle,

(7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\texttt{Dense}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{\Xi}\texttt{Softmax}(\beta\mathbf{\Xi}^{\top}\mathbf{x})

(8)

where
\texttt{lse}(\beta,\mathbf{z}):=\frac{1}{\beta}\log\sum_{i=1}^{M}\exp(\beta z_{i})
is the log-sum-exponential function with inverse temperature
\beta>0
. The equivalence between
\mathcal{T}_{\texttt{Dense}}(\mathbf{x})
and softmax attention provides a rigorous theoretical foundation for interpreting Transformers as associative memory systems.

To leverage the efficiency and robustness of sparse attention
(
Martins & Astudillo 2016
;
Peters et al. 2019
)
, the Sparse Hopfield Network (SHN)
(
Hu et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2023a
)
was introduced. SHN extends the energy-based framework by incorporating
\alpha\texttt{-Entmax}
into its retrieval dynamics:

\displaystyle E_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}):=-\mathbf{\Psi}^{\star}_{\alpha}(\beta,\mathbf{\Xi}^{\top}\mathbf{x})+\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\rangle,

(9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}):=\mathbf{\Xi}\alpha\texttt{-Entmax}(\beta\mathbf{\Xi}^{\top}\mathbf{x}),

(10)

where
\mathbf{\Psi}^{\star}_{\alpha}
is the convex conjugate of the Tsallis entropy
(
Tsallis 1988
)

\Psi_{\alpha}
, defined as:

\displaystyle\Psi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{p}):=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\alpha(\alpha-1)}\sum^{M}_{i=1}(p_{i}-p^{\alpha}_{i}),\;&\alpha\neq 1,\\
-\sum^{M}_{i=1}(p_{i}-\log p_{i}),&\alpha=1,\end{cases}

(11)

The
\alpha\texttt{-Entmax}
operator,
\argmax_{\mathbf{p}\in\Delta^{M}}[\langle\mathbf{p},\mathbf{z}\rangle-\Psi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{p})]
, allows
\alpha
to control sparsity;
\alpha=1
recovers softmax, while
\alpha\to 2
increases sparsity. Computationally,
\alpha=2
and
\alpha=1.5
admit exact, efficient solutions
(
Held et al. 1974
;
Michelot 1986
;
Peters et al. 2019
)
. Critically, SHN reduces retrieval errors and exhibits superior robustness to noise compared to dense dynamics.

With
\beta=1/\sqrt{d}
, the retrieval dynamics
\mathcal{T}_{\texttt{Dense}}
and
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
map directly to the following attention modules:

\displaystyle\texttt{At}(\mathbf{Q}_{t},\mathbf{K}_{t},\mathbf{V}_{t})=\texttt{Softmax}(\mathbf{Q}_{t}\mathbf{K}_{t}^{\top}/\sqrt{d})\mathbf{V}_{t}

(12)

\displaystyle\texttt{At}_{\alpha}(\alpha,\mathbf{Q}_{t},\mathbf{K}_{t},\mathbf{V}_{t})=\alpha\texttt{-Entmax}(\mathbf{Q}_{t}\mathbf{K}_{t}^{\top}/\sqrt{d})\mathbf{V}_{t}

(13)

where
At
and
\texttt{At}_{\alpha}
denote the original dense and sparse attention layers, respectively. We focus exclusively on cross-attention, as sparse self-attention can degrade generation performance
(
Kim & Sim 2025
)
. Inheriting the properties of Hopfield dynamics,
\texttt{At}_{\alpha}
benefits from enhanced noise robustness and faster convergence. PLADIS
(
Kim & Sim 2025
)
leverages these advantages by adopting sparse attention as the relatively strong component in its extrapolation framework (
Equation

6
) to improve generation quality.

2.4
Acceleration of Fixed-Point Iteration

For an operator
\mathcal{F}
defined on a real Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, a point
\mathbf{x}_{\star}\in\mathcal{H}
is defined as a fixed point if
\mathbf{x}_{\star}=\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}_{\star})
. According to the Banach Fixed-Point Theorem
(
Banach 1922
)
, if
\mathcal{F}
is a contraction mapping, the Picard iteration starting from any
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
is guaranteed to converge to a unique fixed point:

\mathbf{x}_{k+1}=\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}_{k}),\quad k=0,1,\dots

(14)

However, if
\mathcal{F}
is merely nonexpansive, the Picard iteration may fail to converge and instead result in limit cycles. To address this, various acceleration techniques have been developed, such as the Krasnosel’skii–Mann (KM) iteration
(
Mann 1953
)
, which employs a convex relaxation, and the inertial KM iteration
(
Maingé 2008
)
, which incorporates first-order momentum.

In this work, we focus on Anderson Acceleration (AA)
(
Walker & Ni 2011
)
, which leverages
m
previous iterates to find an optimal linear combination of function outputs:

\mathbf{x}_{k+1}=\sum_{i=0}^{m}\omega_{i}^{(k)}\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}_{k-m+i}),\quad\text{s.t. }\sum_{i=0}^{m}\omega_{i}^{(k)}=1,

(15)

where the coefficients
\omega_{i}^{(k)}
are determined by minimizing the residual norm. Unlike KM-type methods,
\omega_{i}^{(k)}
are not restricted to the range
(0,1)
, allowing for aggressive extrapolation. In the specific case where
m=1
, the iteration simplifies to a linear extrapolation between the current and previous function outputs:

\mathbf{x}_{k+1}=\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}_{k})+\omega\left(\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}_{k})-\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}_{k-1})\right),

(16)

where
\omega
acts as an extrapolation scale. This Nesterov-like acceleration
(
Nesterov 1983
)
significantly reduces iterations
(
Shehu 2018
)
and enables the sequence to bypass local plateaus or flat energy landscapes where standard KM might stagnate. Motivated by these properties, we link attention-space extrapolation with AA.

Main Contribution

In this section, we first formalize the retrieval dynamics of MHNs as a fixed-point iteration process. By addressing the inherent limitations of the standard Picard iteration in existing MHN frameworks, we establish a theoretical foundation for attention extrapolation by framing it as an accelerated fixed-point process via the special case of AA (
Section

3.1
). Building on this, we introduce a geometry-aware extrapolation rule(
Section

3.3
) and provide rigorous proofs for its stability and convergence(
Section

3.4
).

3.1
Retrieval Dynamics as Fixed-Point Iteration

We first revisit the retrieval dynamics
\mathcal{T}
in MHNs
(
Ramsauer et al. 2020
;
Hu et al. 2024
)
, which are fundamentally Picard iterations,
\mathbf{x}_{k+1}=\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{x}_{k})
, designed to find a stationary point
\mathbf{x}_{\star}
of the energy function that represents a stored memory. This relationship is formalized in the following:

Theorem 3.1
.

(
Ramsauer et al. 2020
;
Hu et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2023a
)
(Generalized Sparse Hopfield Retrieval Dynamics). The retrieval dynamics of the generalized sparse Hopfield model is a monotonic one-step update:

\mathbf{x}_{k+1}=\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{x}_{k}):=\mathbf{\Xi}\alpha\texttt{-Entmax}(\beta\mathbf{\Xi}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{k}).

(17)

This implies that attention dynamics are the mechanism for finding fixed points,
\mathbf{x}_{\star}
, in MHNs, where the standard attention layer performs exactly one iteration of this update rule.

Within this framework, while previous works focus on improving the operator
\mathcal{T}
via sparsity (
(
Hu et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2023a
)
, they remain restricted to the standard Picard iteration. We transcend this by framing the retrieval process through the lens of AA with
m=1
in
Equation

16
:

\mathbf{x}_{k+1}=\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{x}_{k})+\omega\left(\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{x}_{k})-\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{x}_{k-1})\right),

(18)

where
\omega
is the extrapolation coefficient. In practical one-step update scenarios of MHN, the true previous iterate
\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{x}_{k-1})
is typically unavailable. However, we identify that the varying convergence rates of different attention mechanisms provide a robust numerical proxy for this update direction. Specifically, as sparse attention (
\alpha>1
) converges more rapidly toward
\mathbf{x}_{\star}
than dense attention (
\alpha=1
)
(
Hu et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2023a
)
, their discrepancy effectively serves as a surrogate for the retrieval trajectory’s progress.

Proposition 3.2
.

(Approximation of Retrieval Gradients).
Let
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{T}_{\texttt{Dense}}
be the sparse and dense retrieval operators, respectively. Given that
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
achieves faster convergence toward
\mathbf{x}_{\star}
than
\mathcal{T}_{\texttt{Dense}}
within a single iteration
(
Wu et al. 2023a
)
, the discrepancy between them approximates the retrieval update direction:

\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\approx\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}_{k}),\quad\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{x}_{k-1})\approx\mathcal{T}_{\texttt{Dense}}(\mathbf{x}_{k}).

(19)

Consequently, the extrapolation,
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}_{k})+\omega(\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}_{k})-\mathcal{T}_{\texttt{Dense}}(\mathbf{x}_{k}))
, can be reinterpreted as the first-order AA defined in
Equation

16
. This perspective reveals that PLADIS essentially performs first-order AA within the attention space (see
Equation

6
).

By extension, this framework also elucidates the mechanism of NAG, where the discrepancy between contrasting prompt-based retrieval serves as a proxy for the iterative update direction. To our knowledge, this is the first work to uncover a unified mechanism behind attention-space extrapolation.

3.2
The Common Fixed-Point Preservation

To ensure that acceleration does not deviate from the semantic target, we prove that extrapolation preserves the original fixed-point structure.

Assumption 3.3

(Common Fixed Point)
.

There exists a shared fixed point
\mathbf{x}^{*}\in\mathcal{H}
such that
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}^{*})=\mathbf{x}^{*}
and
\mathcal{T}_{\texttt{Dense}}(\mathbf{x}^{*})=\mathbf{x}^{*}
.

Lemma 3.4

(Fixed-Point Preservation)
.

Let
M(\mathbf{x})
be a state-dependent weight matrix or scalar. Define the corrected operator as
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})=\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})+M(\mathbf{x})(\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})-\mathcal{T}_{\texttt{Dense}}(\mathbf{x}))
. Under
3.3
,
\mathbf{x}^{*}
remains a fixed point of
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})
.

Proof.

The proof is provided in the Appendix. Intuitively, the preservation stems from the fact that the discrepancy between the two operators vanishes exactly at the shared semantic target
\mathbf{x}^{*}
.
∎

Remark 3.5

(Divergence in Non-Common Fixed Points)
.

It is crucial to note that the preservation of the fixed point holds only under Assumption
3.3
. In methods like NAG, the operators are conditioned on contrasting prompts (e.g., positive vs. negative), naturally leading to distinct attractors. In such cases, the discrepancy between two operators does not vanish at any single
\mathbf{x}^{*}
. While this non-vanishing term acts as a ”guidance” force, assigning a weight matrix or scalar
M(\mathbf{x})
without a shared attractor can lead to unintended trajectory shifts or instability, as the extrapolation never reaches a stationary state.

3.3
Geometry-Aware Attention Guidance

Building on the AA framework and the property of common fixed point, we introduce a geometry-aware extrapolation strategy that accounts for the geometric structure of the attention discrepancy. We reformulate attention update of PLADIS
(
Kim & Sim 2025
)
as follow:

\mathcal{T}^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})=\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})+\lambda r(\mathbf{x}),

(20)

where the discrepancy
r(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})-\mathcal{T}_{\texttt{Dense}}(\mathbf{x})
represents the residual between sparse and dense attention layers (
Equation

12
,
Equation

13
):

\mathcal{T}_{(\cdot)}(\mathbf{x})\rightarrow\texttt{At}_{(\cdot)}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}).

(21)

Here, the state vector
\mathbf{x}
acts as the query (
\mathbf{Q}
) and stored memories as key-value (
\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}
) pairs. Consequently,
r(\mathbf{x})
captures the semantic divergence between the two attention kernels at a given state.

To analyze the influence of this discrepancy on the sampling trajectory, we decompose
r(\mathbf{x})
relative to the sparse retrieval direction
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})
, inspired by the projection framework in APG
(
Sadat et al. 2024
)
.

Definition 3.6

(Geometric Decomposition of Residuals)
.

The parallel and orthogonal components of
r(\mathbf{x})
with respect to the sparse operator
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})
,
i.e.,

r(\mathbf{x})=r^{\parallel}(\mathbf{x})+r^{\perp}(\mathbf{x})
, are defined as:

r^{\parallel}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\frac{\langle r(\mathbf{x}),\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})\rangle}{\|\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}}\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}),\quad r^{\perp}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq r(\mathbf{x})-r^{\parallel}(\mathbf{x}).

(22)

Hypothesis.

Since both
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{T}_{\texttt{Dense}}
aim for a shared fixed point
\mathbf{x}^{*}
(
3.3
),
r^{\parallel}(\mathbf{x})
acts as a constructive consensus that accelerates convergence along the retrieval manifold. This contrasts with APG
(
Sadat et al. 2024
)
, which leverages the discrepancy between conditional and unconditional outputs, two operators that inherently target distinct semantic modes to provide guidance.

In our framework, because the target is identical for both operators, any orthogonal component
r^{\perp}(\mathbf{x})
is interpreted as ”off-manifold” noise rather than a guidance signal. This interference stems from the dense operator’s inferior noise robustness compared to its sparse counterpart, which maintains superior fidelity in high-interference regimes
(
Hu et al. 2024
;
Kim & Sim 2025
)
.

Empirical Validation.

To verify the impact of these components, we conducted an ablation study by isolating each term during the diffusion sampling process with PLADIS.

As illustrated in Figure
2
, we observe distinct qualitative differences: orthogonal-only extrapolation fails to recover the intended semantic structure, resulting in significant artifacts; the full residual (
r(\mathbf{x})=r^{\parallel}+r^{\perp}
) leads to slightly degraded visual quality due to the inclusion of harmful orthogonal noise; and parallel-only extrapolation (
r^{\parallel}
) yields the most high-fidelity generation, and robust text-alignment.

Interpretation and Refined Dynamics.

These results confirm that the effective acceleration signal resides in the subspace aligned with the sparse retrieval direction, while the orthogonal component acts as a disruptive force. Consequently, we propose
Geometry-Aware Attention Guidance (GAG)
, defined as:

\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})=\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})+\lambda\underbrace{\left[\min\left(1,\frac{\eta}{\|\tilde{r}(\mathbf{x})\|}\right)\cdot\tilde{r}(\mathbf{x})\right]}_{\text{Rescaled Geometry-Aware Term}},

(23)

where
\tilde{r}(\mathbf{x})=r^{\parallel}(\mathbf{x})+\zeta r^{\perp}(\mathbf{x})
represents the geometry-filtered residual. Here,
\zeta\in[0,1]
controls the orthogonal suppression, and the rescaling factor ensures the guidance norm does not exceed a stability threshold
\eta
. By simultaneously refining the update direction (
\zeta\to 0
) and regulating its magnitude, GAG achieves superior sampling reliability and visual fidelity.

3.4
Stability via Orthogonal Weak Contraction

To establish the structural stability of GAA, we analyze the convergence behavior of the state error in the orthogonal subspace. Let
\Pi_{\perp}(\mathbf{x}):\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
be the orthogonal projector onto
(\text{span}\{\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})\})^{\perp}
.

Assumption 3.7

(Orthogonal Weak Contraction)
.

For the common fixed point
\mathbf{x}^{*}
defined in Assumption
3.3
, there exists a contraction coefficient
c\in[0,1)
and a strictly increasing forcing function
\phi:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that for the operator
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}
with
\zeta=0
, the following holds:

\|\Pi_{\perp}(\mathbf{x})(\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{x}^{*})\|\leq c\|\Pi_{\perp}(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\|-\phi(\|\Pi_{\perp}(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\|).

(24)

This condition generalizes the standard Banach contraction principle, ensuring that the error strictly decreases at each step
(
Rhoades 2001
;
Alber & Guerre-Delabriere 1997
)
.

Theorem 3.8

(Asymptotic Convergence of Orthogonal Error)
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.3
and
3.7
hold. Let
\{\mathbf{x}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence generated by
\mathbf{x}_{t+1}=\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
with
\zeta=0
. Then the magnitude of the orthogonal error, defined as
u_{t}\coloneqq\|\Pi_{\perp}(\mathbf{x}_{t})(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}^{*})\|
, converges to zero asymptotically:

\lim_{t\to\infty}u_{t}=0.

(25)

Proof.

D
.

Remark 3.9

Theorem
3.8
provides the theoretical foundation for our strategy:

•

Structural Robustness:
It clarifies why GAG remains stable even at high guidance scales (
\lambda

\zeta=0

r^{\perp}

Conclusion

In this work, we uncover a missing link in diffusion guidance by establishing a rigorous theoretical foundation that connects attention-space extrapolation to MHN and AA. Building on this unification, we introduced a geometric decomposition method that stabilizes the acceleration process by decoupling updates into parallel and orthogonal components. Our plug-and-play approach significantly enhances generative quality across diverse models, including SDXL and Flux, without additional computational overhead. By bridging dynamical systems and diffusion guidance, this work not only provides a grounded framework for high-quality synthesis but also opens new horizons for viewing guidance as a fixed-point iteration problem.

This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the field of Machine Learning by unifying diffusion guidance and dynamical systems. Theoretical Background

Notations.

For
a\in\mathbb{R}
,
a_{+}:=\max\{0,a\}
. For
\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\langle\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\rangle=\mathbf{z}^{\top}\mathbf{z}^{\prime}
is the inner product of two vectors. For
\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\dots,z_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote the sorted coordinates of
\mathbf{z}
as
z_{(1)}\geq z_{(2)}\geq\dots\geq z_{(d)}
, where
z_{(\nu)}
is the
\nu
-th largest element.
\Delta^{M-1}:=\{\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}|p_{i}\geq 0,\sum p_{i}=1\}
denotes the
(M-1)
-dimensional simplex.

In this section, we provide the concept of the modern Hopfield network and its sparse extension in a simplified form to elucidate the motivation and intuition behind our method and encourage further research.

Initially, the Hopfield model was introduced as an associative memory designed to store binary patterns
(
Hopfield 1982
)
. The model is optimized to store patterns in the local minima of an associated energy function; given a query input, the system retrieves the closest local minimum. Numerous extensions have been proposed to improve stability and capacity, such as exponential energy functions or continuous state models
(
Demircigil et al. 2017
;
Krotov & Hopfield 2016
;
Barra et al. 2018
)
.

Ramsauer et al. proposed the modern Hopfield network (MHN), which can be integrated into deep learning architectures
(
Ramsauer et al. 2020
)
. The network is equipped with a differentiable energy function
E
and retrieval dynamics
\mathcal{T}
that enable pattern retrieval in a single update:

\displaystyle E_{\texttt{Dense}}

\displaystyle:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R},\mathbf{x}\mapsto-\texttt{lse}(\beta,\mathbf{\Xi}^{\top}\mathbf{x})+\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\rangle,

(26)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\texttt{Dense}}

\displaystyle:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbf{x}\mapsto\mathbf{\Xi}\texttt{Softmax}(\beta\mathbf{\Xi}^{\top}\mathbf{x})

(27)

where
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
represents a query input,
\mathbf{\Xi}=[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}\dots\boldsymbol{\xi}_{M}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times M}
consists of stored patterns
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\texttt{lse}(\beta,\mathbf{z}):=\log\left(\sum^{M}_{i=1}\exp(\beta z_{i})\right)/\beta
is the log-sum-exponential function for
\beta>0
. Theoretical results regarding the energy function and the retrieval dynamics, including convergence and state properties, were established in
(
Ramsauer et al. 2020
)
.

Connection with the Transformer Attention

An important discovery in
(
Ramsauer et al. 2020
)
is the connection between the MHN update rule and the self-attention mechanism used in Transformer and BERT models. Following the derivation in
(
Ramsauer et al. 2020
)
, we first extend
\mathcal{T}_{\texttt{Dense}}
in Eq.
27
to accommodate multiple queries
\mathbf{X}:=\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i\in[N]}
. Given a raw query
\mathbf{R}
and memory matrix
\mathbf{Y}
, we define the query and key matrices as
\mathbf{X}^{\top}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}_{Q}:=\mathbf{Q}
and
\mathbf{\Xi}^{\top}=\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{W}_{K}:=\mathbf{K}
, using weight matrices
\mathbf{W}_{Q}
and
\mathbf{W}_{K}
. Consequently, the retrieval dynamics can be rewritten as
\mathbf{K}^{\top}\texttt{Softmax}(\beta\mathbf{K}\mathbf{Q}^{\top})
.

By taking the transpose and projecting
\mathbf{K}
to
\mathbf{V}
via
\mathbf{W}_{V}
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\texttt{Dense}}:\mathbf{X}\mapsto\texttt{Softmax}(\beta\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top})\mathbf{K}\mathbf{W}_{V}=\texttt{Softmax}(\beta\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top})\mathbf{V},

(28)

which corresponds to standard Transformer self-attention with
\beta=1/\sqrt{d}
. Employing the notations in
(
Ramsauer et al. 2020
)
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\texttt{Dense}}:\mathbf{X}\mapsto\texttt{Softmax}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}/\sqrt{d})\mathbf{V}:=\texttt{At}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}).

(29)

This interpretation can be extended to the cross-attention mechanism:

\mathcal{T}_{\texttt{Dense}}:(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})\mapsto\texttt{Softmax}\left(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_{Q}\mathbf{W}_{K}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}^{\top}/\sqrt{d}\right)\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{W}_{V}=\texttt{At}(\mathbf{W}_{Q}\mathbf{X},\mathbf{W}_{K}\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{W}_{V}\mathbf{Y}).

Our work specifically focuses on this extension into the cross-attention framework. In the context of MHN, the input query is processed with an additional transformation
\mathbf{W}_{Q}
to increase network complexity, while inner products are computed with learned key patterns (
\mathbf{W}_{K}\mathbf{Y}
). The retrieved patterns (values) then serve as inputs for subsequent layers. Since different layers can store distinct patterns, MHNs facilitate the hierarchical storage and retrieval of information. Notably, while a basic Hopfield network outputs a single pattern, the attention mechanism yields multiple patterns; thus, attention acts as a stack of multi-valued Hopfield networks, forming a multi-level and multi-valued associative memory.

Sparse Hopfield Network

Later, sparse extensions of the modern Hopfield network are proposed
(
Hu et al. 2024
;
Wu et al. 2023a
)
.
The energy function was modified to make sparse the computation of retrieval dynamics:

\displaystyle E_{\alpha}

\displaystyle:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R},\mathbf{x}\mapsto-\mathbf{\Psi}^{\star}_{\alpha}(\beta,\mathbf{\Xi}^{\top}\mathbf{x})+\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\rangle,

(30)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbf{x}\mapsto\mathbf{\Xi}\alpha\texttt{-Entmax}(\beta\mathbf{\Xi}^{\top}\mathbf{x}),

(31)

and
\mathbf{\Psi}^{\star}_{\alpha}
is the convex conjugate of Tsallis entropy
(
Tsallis 1988
)
,
\mathbf{\Psi}_{\alpha},\alpha\texttt{-Entmax}(\mathbf{z})
, represents the probability mapping:

\displaystyle\Psi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{p}):=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\alpha(\alpha-1)}\sum^{M}_{i=1}(p_{i}-p^{\alpha}_{i}),\;&\alpha\neq 1,\\
-\sum^{M}_{i=1}(p_{i}-\log p_{i}),&\alpha=1,\end{cases}

(32)

\displaystyle\alpha\texttt{-Entmax}(\mathbf{z}):=\underset{\mathbf{p}\in\Delta^{M}}{\argmax}[\langle\mathbf{p},\mathbf{z}\rangle-\Psi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{p})],

(33)

where
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
.
Here,
\alpha
controls the sparsity.
When
\alpha=1
, it is equivalent to a dense probability mapping,
1\texttt{-Entmax}=\texttt{Softmax}
, and as
\alpha
increases towards
2
, the outputs of
\alpha\texttt{-Entmax}
become increasingly sparse, ultimately converging to
2\texttt{-Entmax}\equiv\texttt{Sparsemax}(\mathbf{z}):=\underset{\mathbf{p}\in\Delta^{M}}{\argmin}\left\lVert\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{z}\right\rVert

(
Martins & Astudillo 2016
)
.
Notably, when
\alpha=1
,
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
becomes equivalent to
\mathcal{T}_{\texttt{Dense}}\equiv\mathcal{T}_{1}

(
Wainwright et al. 2008
)
.
We have simple formula for
\alpha\texttt{-Entmax}
(
Martins & Astudillo 2016
)
.
There is a unique threshold function
\tau:\mathbb{R}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}
that satisfies

\displaystyle\alpha\texttt{-Entmax}(\mathbf{z})=[(\alpha-1)\mathbf{z}-\tau(\mathbf{z})\boldsymbol{1}]_{+}^{1/(\alpha-1)}.

(34)

From this formula, we know that the entries less than
\tau/(\alpha-1)
map to zero, so sparsity is achieved.
We will denote the number of nonzero entries in
\alpha\texttt{-Entmax}
as
\kappa(\mathbf{z})
for later use to derive theoretical results.
For
\alpha=2
, the exact solution can be efficiently computed using a sorting algorithm
(
Held et al. 1974
;
Michelot 1986
)
.
For
1<\alpha<2
, inaccurate and slow iterative algorithm was used for computing
\alpha\texttt{-Entmax}

(
Liu & Ye 2009
)
.
Interestingly, for
1.5\texttt{-Entmax}
, an accurate and exact solution are derived in a simple form
(
Peters et al. 2019
)
.

Similar to
\mathcal{T}_{\texttt{Dense}}
,
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
can be extended to attention mechanisms, establishing a strong connection with sparse attention.
In other words, by following the derivation as provided in
Equation

28
, and
Equation

29
, we can obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}:\mathbf{X}\mapsto\alpha\texttt{-Entmax}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}/\sqrt{d})\mathbf{V}:=\texttt{At}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V})

(35)

Furthermore, similar to the dense attention mechanism, we can also extend into a cross-attention mechanism with inputs
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
:

\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}:(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})\mapsto\alpha\texttt{-Entmax}\left(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_{Q}\mathbf{W}_{K}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}^{\top}/\sqrt{d}\right)\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{W}_{V}=\texttt{At}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{W}_{Q}\mathbf{X},\mathbf{W}_{K}\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{W}_{V}\mathbf{Y})

Sparse Hopfield Network and
\alpha
-Entmax.

To introduce sparsity into the retrieval process, the modern Hopfield network can be extended by employing a sparse transformation. This sparse counterpart is characterized by an energy function
E_{\alpha}
and the corresponding retrieval dynamics
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
:

\displaystyle E_{\alpha}

\displaystyle:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R},\mathbf{x}\mapsto-\Psi_{\alpha}(\beta,\mathbf{\Xi}^{\top}\mathbf{x})+\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\rangle,

(36)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbf{x}\mapsto\mathbf{\Xi}\alpha\texttt{-Entmax}(\beta\mathbf{\Xi}^{\top}\mathbf{x})

(37)

where
\alpha\texttt{-Entmax}
is a sparsity-inducing mapping. Specifically, there exists a unique threshold function
\tau:\mathbb{R}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}
such that:

\displaystyle\alpha\texttt{-Entmax}(\mathbf{z})=[(\alpha-1)\mathbf{z}-\tau(\mathbf{z})\mathbf{1}]_{+}^{1/(\alpha-1)}.

(38)

From this formulation, entries less than
\tau(\mathbf{z})/(\alpha-1)
are mapped to zero, thereby achieving sparsity in the retrieved patterns. For subsequent theoretical results, we denote the number of non-zero entries in
\alpha\texttt{-Entmax}(\mathbf{z})
as
\kappa(\mathbf{z})
.

The computational efficiency of this mapping depends on the value of
\alpha
. For
\alpha=2
(corresponding to
sparsemax
), the exact solution can be efficiently computed using sorting algorithms
(
Held et al. 1974
;
Michelot 1986
)
. For
1<\alpha<2
, while earlier approaches relied on slower iterative algorithms
(
Liu & Ye 2009
)
, an exact solution in a simple closed form has been derived specifically for
1.5\texttt{-Entmax}

(
Peters et al. 2019
)
.

Extension to Sparse Attention

Similar to the dense retrieval dynamics
\mathcal{T}_{\texttt{Dense}}
, the sparse counterpart
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}
can be extended to attention mechanisms, establishing a robust connection with sparse attention. Following the derivations provided in Eqs.
28
and
29
, the sparse retrieval dynamics for multiple queries can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}:\mathbf{X}\mapsto\alpha\texttt{-Entmax}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}/\sqrt{d})\mathbf{V}:=\texttt{At}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}).

(39)

Furthermore, this sparse attention mechanism can be naturally extended to a cross-attention framework with inputs
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
:

\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}:(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})\mapsto\alpha\texttt{-Entmax}\left(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_{Q}\mathbf{W}_{K}^{\top}\mathbf{Y}^{\top}/\sqrt{d}\right)\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{W}_{V}=\texttt{At}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{W}_{Q}\mathbf{X},\mathbf{W}_{K}\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{W}_{V}\mathbf{Y}).

This extension allows the network to selectively retrieve pertinent information from the memory matrix
\mathbf{Y}
by filtering out irrelevant patterns through the sparsity-inducing
\alpha
-entmax mapping. By integrating this into the cross-attention layers, our model benefits from the theoretical properties of sparse associative memories, enhancing both interpretability and robustness against noise.

Noise Robustness of Sparse Hopfield Network

In SHN, sparsity not only reduces retrieval errors but also ensures faster convergence compared to dense retrieval dynamics
(
Hu et al. 2024
;

Corollary B.3

(Noise Robustness)
(

;

;

.
In the presence of noisy queries
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{x}+\boldsymbol{\eta}

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{\mu}=\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mu}+\boldsymbol{\eta}
, the impact of noise
\boldsymbol{\eta}
on the sparse retrieval error
\|\mathcal{T}_{2}(\mathbf{x})-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mu}\|
is
linear

These theoretical insights suggest that under noisy conditions, sparse attention mechanisms governed by

(
\alpha>1

\alpha
) further diminishes retrieval errors. Given that diffusion models inherently operate on noisy Gaussian queries throughout the generative process, the transition from dense to sparse attention is particularly advantageous. This robust information retrieval under high-noise regimes provides the fundamental motivation for the success of PLADIS in text-to-image generation.
