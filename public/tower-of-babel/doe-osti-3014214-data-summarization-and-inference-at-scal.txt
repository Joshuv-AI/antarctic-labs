1. What are the major goals of the project?
  The goal of the project was to solve data-intensive and compute-intensive problems in the physical
  sciences, engineering, information science, data science, etc. by designing and implementing new
  algorithms that could work with a subset of the data. The four subgoals were:
 (a) The solution of problems where the data is too large to be stored in the memory of a computer.
 In this streaming model of computation, the data arrives as a stream of elements to the computer,
 each element is processed as it arrives, and a decision is made to discard the data or to store it;
 only a small subset of the data proportional to the size of the output solution is stored, and when
 all the data has been streamed, a solution to the problem is computed from the stored subset.
 (b) The use of machine learning methods to compute solutions to data-intensive problems. The use of
 GPUs is critical to obtain high performance on machine learning tasks, but their memory sizes are
 smaller relative to that of CPUs. For large-scale problems, the data is sampled many times, and
 small samples are used with repetition, for robustness, to compute solutions to inference tasks.
 This sampling reduces the memory required to solve the problem, but attention is needed to avoid
 slow convergence to the solutions, and reduced accuracy of inference. We propose submodular
 optimization, Large Language Models, and physics-informed neural networks to enable GPU
 computations here.
 (c) Modeling and visualization of high-dimensional data using interpretable features. Clinical pro-
 teomic data sets from immunology for the detection of cancer and other diseases are temporal
 and high-dimensional, and algorithms for visualizing these data sets using clinically interpretable
 features are lacking. We propose methods that compute distances based on the optimal trans-
 portation problem and graph edit distances to address this problem. We also propose the use of
 optimal transport-based distances, spatial statistics, and network structure to classify image data
 sets, We apply these algorithms to electron micrographs of the peripheral nervous system in the
 digestive tract.
 (d) The design of data-intensive algorithms on emerging architectures, specifically, noisy, intermediate-
 scale quantum (NISQ) devices. Quantum computers offer the possibility of exploring large solution
 spaces due to the principle of superposition, but current quantum computers are limited by few
 qubits, short coherence times due to noise, poor interconections among the qubits, etc. We pro-
 pose the use of the divide and conquer paradigm to solve large-scale problems, wherein collections
 of small subproblems are solved on the quantum devices, and the solutions to the subproblems
 are integrated into a solution for the original problem on a classical computer.
2. What was accomplished under these goals?

 (a) Streaming algorithms: We have designed, analyzed and implemented streaming algorithms for
 computing maximum weighted matchings, maximum weighted k-disjoint matchings, and minimum
 weight edge covers and b-edge covers. The streaming algorithms use memory bounded by the
 number of vertices in the graph, rather than the number of edges in the graph, with the same
 running time complexities as the offline algorithms. They also compute weights of the matching
 similar to those of the offline algorithms. We also combined streaming algorithms and parallel
 computing to obtain a poly-streaming model that processes multiple data streams in parallel.
 We designed half-approximate maximum weighted matching algorithms in this model, showing
 that the parallelism in I/O and processing does not hurt the approximation ratio. The poly-
 streaming model offers a practically relevant paradigm for exploring how running time and memory

 requirements can be jointly optimized. On the largest problems with trillions of edges, the offline
 algorithm would require close to a hundred TeraByte, whereas the streaming algorithm needed
 less than a GigaByte of memory. Our paper on the poly-streaming model received the best paper
 prize at the European Symposium on Algorithms in 2025.
(b) ML for data analysis:
 Security: Software and hardware vulnerabilities (common vulnerability exposures, CVE) that
 cause cyber attacks are classified to dictionaries of common weakness enumerations (CWE) and
 common attack patterns (CAPEC). We developed the first algorithms for automating the process
 of classifying CVEs to CWEs and CAPECs using Large Language Models (LLMs). These algo-
 rithms provide highly accurate classification, and were able to discover novel CWEs and CAPECs.
 We were also able to scale the computations to multiple GPUs to reduce the training time needed
 by the models by one power of ten. Our work on these problems received two best paper prizes at
 security and data science conferences.
 We designed an attribute-guided sampling algorithm for Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) called
 AGS-GNN. AGS-GNN exploits the node features and the connectivity structure of a graph while
 simultaneously adapting for both homophily and heterophily in graphs. (In homophilic graphs,
 vertices of the same class are more likely to be adjacent, but vertices of different classes tend to
 be adjacent in heterophilic graphs.) The state-of-the-art GNNs for heterophilic graphs use the
 full neighborhood of a node instead of sampling it, and hence do not scale to large graphs and are
 not inductive. We developed dual-channel sampling techniques based on feature-similarity and
 feature-diversity to select subsets of neighbors for a node that capture adaptive information from
 homophilic and heterophilic neighborhoods. For diverse neighborhood sampling, we employed the
 concept of submodularity, a novel contribution in this context. We pre-compute the sampling
 distribution in parallel, achieving the desired scalability. On large collections of heterophilic and
 homophilic graphs we demonstrated the superiority of AGS-GNN compared to the state-of-the-
 art approaches. AGS-GNN achieves test accuracy comparable to the best-performing heterophilic
 GNNs, even outperforming methods that use the entire graph for node classification. AGS-GNN
 converged faster than methods that sample neighborhoods randomly.
 Chemistry: As the databases of molecules continue to grow rapidly, ML methods can be used to
 predict their properties much faster than methods that solve Schrödinger’s equation in quantum
 chemistry. We designed PIL-Net, a physics-informed graph convolutional network for predict-
 ing molecular properties quickly and with low error, using only basic information about each
 molecule’s atoms and bonds. The PIL-Net model combines the representational power of graph
 neural networks with knowledge of chemistry to predefine a set of constraints that force the
 network to make physically consistent predictions; this leads to faster model convergence. We
 applied PIL-Net to predict atomic multipoles, which describe the charge distribution within an
 atom. Atomic multipoles have several applications, including their incorporation into force fields
 for molecular dynamics simulations. Our model was able to predict atomic octupoles, a higher-
 order atomic multipole property, with a mean absolute error that is an order of magnitude less
 than results reported in the literature.
(c) Models and visualization of high dimensional images and data sets
 We developed pioneering algorithms to model the neuroanatomy of peripheral nerves from high-
 resolution transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images. The complex spatial arrangement of
 myelinated and unmyelinated axons in peripheral nerves is not random, but the axonal organiza-
 tion is inhomogeneous and anisotropic. We employed spatial statistics and point process models
 to describe the spatial arrangement of axons, and used Sinkhorn distances to compute the simi-
 larities between these arrangements in various vagus and pelvic nerve cross-sections. We created
 a novel approach to quantify the similarities between spatial point patterns using metrics derived
 from the solution to the optimal transport problem. We presented a generalizable pipeline for
 quantitative analysis of peripheral nerve architecture.

 We designed new algorithms for visualizing high dimensional proteomic data sets from immunol-
 ogy. Traditional visualization tools, like scatter plots or popular algorithms such as t-SNE and
 UMAP, often distort important relationships between cell types. We introduced a new way to
 visualize flow cytometry data that is both mathematically rigorous and easy to interpret. Our
 method uses a concept called “optimal transport” to measure how similar or different two cell
 populations are and represents these relationships as a graph. This graph shows how immune
 cells interact and how these interactions change over time or between patients. We applied this
 approach to cancer immunotherapy and leukemia datasets, uncovering patterns that were hard
 to detect with existing tools. By providing clear visual summaries and quantitative comparisons,
 our framework can help researchers and clinicians track treatment responses and identify early
 signals of disease. Several clinical groups in the country are using the visualization tool that we
 have developed.
 Both of these algorithms and software are applicable to spatial images and high-dimensional data
 sets from other scientific and engineering domains.
 (d) Data-intensive algorithms on emerging architectures:
 Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) computers currently available have a few thousand
 qubits, and could potentially solve combinatorial optimization problems efficiently. However, the
 sizes of the problems that could be solved are limited by the number of qubits, their connectivity,
 high noise, and short coherence times. We designed hybrid quantum-classical algorithms based
 on the divide and conquer paradigm for solving Maximum Independent Set (MIS) and Maximum
 Weighted Independent Set (MWIS) problems on large graphs than would otherwise be possible
 on NISQ devices. The machines include D-Wave Quantum Annealers and QuEra Quantum Com-
 puters with Neutral Atoms. Our algorithms were designed for separable graphs, which are classes
 of graphs with good vertex separators; these include planar graphs, finite element meshes with
 good aspect ratios, etc. The algorithms recursively employ vertex separators to create a collection
 of small subgraphs, from which NISQ computers obtain disjoint maximal independent sets, which
 are then augmented by independent vertices from the separators. We demonstrated competitive
 results compared to the classical Luby’s algorithm, and KaMIS, a state-of-the-art classical MIS
 solver, on graphs with several thousand vertices.

6. Impact on the Principal Discipline
  We have designed several pioneering streaming algorithms for problems on graphs such as matching
  and edge covers, and we have shown that data with trillions of elements could be processed on desk-top
  computers using these algorithms. Using offline algorithms would require the use of supercomputers
  to solve them. Our work on data reduction and classification in cyber-security has automated the
  classification of reported software vulnerabilities to dictionaries and attack patterns, with high accuracy
  and good speed. We have scaled the classification of graph data using Graph Neural Networks to
  large heterophilic graphs, using submodular optimization. We have also developed algorithms to solve
  optimization problems on currently available, noisy, quantum computers. Our sparsification techniques
  enable the implementation of large-scale Graph Neural Networks on GPUs without oveflowing their
  memories, while also reducing computing times and providing more accurate inference results.
7. Impact on Other Disciplines
  The streaming algorithms we have developed have been applied to reducing data traffic (and hence
  energy costs) at data centers. Our work on cyber-security problems has attracted interest from the
  security community working at the National Labs, and at the defense agencies. Our work on load
  balancing in quantum chemistry has been used by the developers of the NWChemEx library (at PNNL
  and other DOE Labs) to scale their code on the Summit supercomputer at Oak Ridge National Lab.
  We have also collaborated with chemists at PNNL and the University of Utah on the prediction of
  multipole moments used in molecular dynamics. Our algorithms and software for neuroscience and
  visualizations in immunology are being used by practitioners in these areas.
