Stricter vehicle standards reduce total pollutants but increase the fraction of toxic organics
Authors: Xinping Yang, Jie Yao, Wenqi Song, Hongfei Chen, Xihui Tong, Zhiheng Wu, Xin Li, Yunjing Wang, Chao Peng, Hang Yin, Yan Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVehicular emissions are a primary source of volatile organic compounds (VOCs), which drive urban ozone and secondary organic aerosol formation while posing direct risks to human health. Progressive emission standards and the widespread adoption of hybrid electric vehicles have successfully reduced total hydrocarbon outputs, establishing them as central strategies for climate and air quality management. However, the influence of these technological shifts on the molecular speciation of emissions—and their associated environmental and toxicological trade-offs—remains poorly understood, potentially masking hidden health risks. Here we apply a concentration-reactivity-toxicity framework to characterize species-resolved VOC emissions across conventional and hybrid vehicles under progressive emission standards. While stricter regulations reduced total VOC emissions by 75% in conventional vehicles, the relative contribution of highly reactive oxygenated VOCs (OVOCs) surged from 20% to 35%. Notably, a modern hybrid vehicle emitted nearly twice the mass of OVOCs (25.4 mg km−1) compared to its conventional counterpart (13.3 mg km−1), driven by frequent engine start–stop cycles that reduce aftertreatment efficiency. This distinct operational profile allows us to identify specific compounds—namely, vinyl acetate and methyl t-butyl ether—as molecular markers for hybrid powertrains in our study. Consequently, despite superior fuel economy, this tested hybrid vehicle exhibited a 69% increase in non-carcinogenic health risks and nearly double the carcinogenic risk, overwhelmingly dominated by aromatics and toxic OVOCs such as acrolein. Furthermore, while absolute emissions declined, persistent aromatics continued to account for over 70% of the secondary organic aerosol formation potential. These findings highlight a critical tension between fuel efficiency advancements and toxicological impacts, suggesting that future emission regulations must transition from total mass limits to species-specific controls to fully safeguard public health.Keywords: Vehicular emission, Emission factor, Secondary pollutant formation potential, Toxicity

Introduction

Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) are important trace constituents in the troposphere, capable of being oxidized by hydroxyl radicals to generate ozone (O3) and secondary organic aerosols (SOA) [[1], [2], [3]]. Vehicular VOC emissions have been unequivocally identified as a major urban atmospheric source, accounting for approximately 25% of total emissions [[4], [5], [6]], particularly given the anticipated continued dominance of gasoline-powered vehicles in the foreseeable market, despite the emerging trend toward electrification [4,5]. Consequently, controlling VOC emissions from light-duty vehicles presents a critical challenge for improving ambient air quality and addressing climate change. Current vehicular emission standards typically regulate VOCs through total hydrocarbons (THC) or non-methane organic gases, with only some countries, such as the United States, South Korea, and Brazil, implementing additional controls on formaldehyde. Meeting carbon-neutrality goals requires a scientific framework that can identify which VOCs from mobile sources matter most, based on total emissions.

Previous studies on vehicular VOC emissions have predominantly focused on emission characteristics, examining the effects of regulatory standards, fuel types, engine technologies, and start modes on THC emissions, and further extended to the several major VOC categories (alkanes, alkenes, aromatics, and oxygenated VOCs [OVOCs]) [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. Several studies have documented emission factors for these VOC classes across different emission standards and their relative contributions to total VOC emissions [11]. Research has reported the oversight of OVOCs in current emission regulations [12], despite their increasing proportion in total VOC emissions under stricter standards [11] and their elevated emissions when using alternative fuels such as ethanol-blended gasoline and compressed natural gas [8]. Although there have been advancements in characterizing the emission factors of individual VOC species, there remains a critical lack of systematic, multidimensional research examining technology-driven evolution of both bulk and species-resolved VOC emissions and their associated environmental and health impacts. This knowledge gap substantially hinders the development of exhaust emission control strategies that effectively address both environmental quality and public health concerns.

Hybrid electric vehicles (HEVs) have emerged as a pivotal transitional technology, bridging conventional internal combustion engines and pure electric vehicles, with their market share steadily increasing [13]. This widespread adoption introduces novel emission dynamics that current regulations fail to adequately address. The application of hybrid technology has been widely recognized as an effective approach to enhance energy efficiency through optimized driving modes, demonstrating significant improvements in fuel economy and reductions in conventional pollutant emissions, such as CO, NOx, THC, and CO2 [[14], [15], [16]]. However, key uncertainties persist regarding species-differentiated VOC emission profiles and their associated environmental and health impacts, particularly concerning the unique emission characteristics arising from frequent start–stop operations and powertrain mode transitions in hybrid electric vehicles. While the rapid electrification transition continues and HEV penetration rates rise, it becomes imperative to investigate how this technology affects emissions of unconventional pollutants and the consequent environmental-health trade-offs, beyond its demonstrated benefits in reducing conventional emissions. This understanding is crucial for developing appropriate regulatory responses to accommodate the anticipated growth of HEV deployment.

The lack of an integrated framework that combines concentration, atmospheric reactivity, and toxicity metrics has masked the environment-health trade-offs in evolving vehicle technologies and limited the ability to maximize co-benefits for air quality, climate, and public health. Addressing this critical gap, our study focuses on the progression of emission standards (China IV to VI) and the advancement of hybrid technology. Using chassis dynamometer testing across a set of vehicles, we develop a concentration–reactivity–toxicity framework to characterize VOC emission profiles. This study specifically investigates the under-recognized impacts of HEVs, decoupling environmental impacts via secondary pollutant formation potential and health risks via species-differentiated carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic hazard evaluations. By identifying priority VOC species using integrated environmental and health metrics, this work supports a shift from total VOC control to species-specific regulation, aligning emission management with environmental and health outcomes.

Experimental methods

Tested vehicle and measurement details

To systematically evaluate the effects of vehicular technology upgrades on tailpipe emissions, we performed extensive tailpipe emission testing based on a chassis dynamometer using a fleet of seven rented light-duty passenger vehicles (Fig. 1). To eliminate the potential influence of engine type, all vehicles selected in this study were equipped with gasoline direct injection engines, comprising six gasoline-powered internal combustion engine vehicles (ICEVs) and one HEV. The ICEVs include two China IV, two China V, and two China VI vehicles. The primary objective of this study is to establish a generalizable framework for evaluating the “concentration-reactivity-toxicity” of vehicular VOC emissions, rather than to derive universal emission patterns for HEVs. Therefore, we selected a representative HEV case for in-depth analysis. The HEV chosen for this study is a non-plug-in hybrid. This selection was based on two main considerations. First, non-plug-in HEVs have a higher fleet share than plug-in hybrids in the Chinese market, making them more representative of mainstream market technology. Second, their mode of operation, which does not depend on external charging, more accurately reflects the impact of hybrid technologies on engine performance and emissions, thereby circumventing charging-related effects. Furthermore, the current China VI emission standards for HEVs are primarily based on testing under “non-plug-in” conditions, making the use of one non-plug-in HEV directly aligned with existing regulatory compliance assessments. Accordingly, we specifically selected an HEV with engine and after-treatment configurations similar to those of the China VI ICEV included in the study, thereby enabling a clearer attribution of observed differences in VOC emissions and environmental impacts to the powertrain type itself. All tested vehicles were uniformly equipped with three-way catalytic converters (TWCs) as the primary after-treatment systems for emission reduction. Comprehensive technical specifications for the vehicle fleet are documented (Supplementary Table S1).

Chassis-dynamometer-based vehicle emission testing and sampling system. Exhaust from the test vehicle was transferred from the tailpipe to a dilution tunnel coupled to a constant-volume sampler, sampling bag, and conventional gaseous pollutant analyzer. Parallel sampling lines equipped with a SUMMA canister, a DNPH cartridge, and a Tenax tube were used to collect speciated pollutants.

The experiment was conducted at the China Automotive Technology and Research Center in Beijing, a certified automotive testing center accredited by both the China Metrology Accreditation and the China National Accreditation Service for Conformity Assessment. This accreditation maintains the accuracy of the emission tests through stringent control over experimental conditions. Detailed descriptions of the quality assurance procedures are documented in Supplementary Text S1 and Text S2, as reported by Tong et al. [17]. The testing employed the Worldwide harmonized Light vehicles Test Cycle (WLTC), with a total time of 1800 s, as newly introduced in Limits and Measurement Methods for Emissions from Light-Duty Vehicles (CHINA VI) [18], comprising low-, medium-, high-, and extra-high-speed phases. To minimize the potential impact of variations in fuel components on vehicular emissions, we used certified conventional gasoline (research octane number 92) that complies with national standards in China and was sourced from the automotive testing center for all driving cycles. The detailed fuel specifications are available in our previous publication [19]. Additionally, this study incorporated both cold-start and hot-start conditions, where cold-start was defined as testing after a minimum 12-h vehicle soak in the room of the chassis dynamometer with a constant temperature of 23 °C. VOC samples were collected using SUMMA canisters connected to the constant volume sampling system, followed by detailed speciation analysis via gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS). Emission factors for both total VOCs and individual species were calculated by correlating sample concentrations with vehicle mileage during each speed phase.

Results and discussion

Technology-driven rise of OVOC emissions from exhaust

To comprehensively investigate the impact of technological innovations on vehicular VOC speciation, we present the emission factors and relative composition profiles of VOC components for China IV, China V, China VI ICEVs, and China VI HEVs (Fig. 2). Detailed emissions profiles of other related pollutants for the relevant vehicles are available (Supplementary Fig. S1 and Text S1). Herein, for ICEVs, the mean values categorized by emission stage are presented, with individual vehicle emission results provided using the cold-startup mode under the entire WLTC scenario as an example (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. S2). In this study, we quantified the VOC emission characteristics and their environmental and health impacts separately for the low-speed phase and the entire protocol. The low-speed phase was analyzed separately for individual assessment because, in real-world vehicle operation, the startup phase—particularly the low-speed interval when the engine and after-treatment systems have not yet reached optimal operating temperatures—is a critical contributor to emissions of unburned and partially burned hydrocarbons [8]. The emission characteristics during this phase significantly influence the overall environmental impact and are more sensitive to performance differences in emission control technologies. Therefore, focusing on this phase enables a clearer demonstration of the emission-reduction effects resulting from improvements in combustion efficiency and upgrades to after-treatment systems across different emission standards. VOC emission factors for ICEVs decreased by 75%, from 287.8 mg km−1 (China IV) to 71.8 mg km−1 (China VI) during low-speed cold-start phases, underscoring the pivotal role of emission standard upgrades in reducing vehicle exhaust emissions. This is attributed to vehicles meeting stricter emission standards, which have higher fuel combustion efficiency and more advanced after-treatment systems [25]. A pronounced observation is that the VOC emission factors during the low-speed phase significantly exceeded those of the entire WLTC. Specifically, under hot-start mode, VOC emission factors for China VI, China V, China VI ICEVs, and China VI HEV during the low-speed phase were 374.9, 110.1, 44.4, and 66.4 mg km−1, respectively, while the whole WLTC yields merely 19%, 34%, 35%, and 29% of these values for the corresponding vehicle categories. This discrepancy primarily stems from the fact that, despite the limited mileage in the low-speed phase, VOC emissions were significantly higher, resulting in even higher VOC emission factors during the low-speed phase compared to the entire WLTC protocol. High VOC emissions during the low-speed phase are attributed to uneven fuel–air mixing, and low temperatures lead to incomplete combustion and poor catalytic converter efficiency due to the low exhaust temperature [26]. Additionally, frequent acceleration and deceleration events during the low-speed phase (Supplementary Fig. S3) further contribute to elevated VOC emissions. The VOC speciation profiles were consistent throughout the WLTC and the low-speed phase, primarily because low-speed emissions dominated VOC emissions. The China VI HEV in this study exhibited higher VOC emissions than the China VI ICEVs, with emission factors of 27.3 and 21.1 mg km−1, respectively, under cold-start mode during the WLTC protocol. This phenomenon is likely attributable to frequent engine start–stop cycles during HEV mode transitions between electric and gasoline operation [13,27]. However, this study employed only a single hybrid vehicle as a case study to investigate HEV emission characteristics, and the findings should not be interpreted as representing universal patterns. Future work will involve fleet-level testing of HEVs to further elucidate their VOC emission profiles.

While total VOC emissions exhibited a consistent decline with stricter emission regulations (Fig. 2a and b), the speciation profiles revealed contrasting trends among key components (Fig. 2c and d). For the cold-start mode during the entire WLTC protocol, alkanes maintained relatively stable contributions (31–38% for ICEVs) with the upgrading of emission standards, whereas aromatics showed marked reductions, whose contributions dropped from 31% to 35% (China IV and China V ICEVs) to 18% (China VI ICEVs). OVOCs demonstrated a substantial relative increase, rising from 20–22% in China IV/V vehicles to 35% in China VI models, as reported previously [11]. This compositional shift carries significant environmental implications, as the growing predominance of reactive and toxic OVOCs (e.g., formaldehyde) may partially offset the benefits of overall emission reductions, highlighting the need for targeted control strategies to address these oxygenated species. Additionally, the China VI HEVs exhibited an even lower aromatic contribution of merely 12% under identical operating conditions, which was primarily reflected in VOCs with seven or more carbon atoms, while the proportion of C5–C6 VOCs in the total VOCs emissions exhibited an increasing trend (Supplementary Fig. S4). OVOCs showed a substantial relative increase for hybrid technology, rising from approximately 20% in China IV and China V ICEVs to 35% in China VI ICEVs, with a further increase to 37% in China VI HEVs.

Particularly concerning is the growing dominance of OVOCs, while the progressive emission standards have significantly reduced total VOC emissions. These evolving speciation profiles pose environmental challenges, as the persistently rising proportion of highly reactive, toxic OVOCs may partially offset overall emission-reduction benefits. Given the pivotal role of OVOCs in secondary pollutant formation and biological health impacts, future research must prioritize species-resolved characterization to inform targeted control strategies, with consideration for incorporating key species into the framework of next-generation emission standard development.

Speciation and hybrid-specific characteristics of dominant OVOCs

Building on the technology-driven emission trends outlined above, we further explored the chemical speciation of increasingly predominant OVOC components. This analysis clarified how OVOC composition varied across vehicle technologies and operating conditions. Speciated VOC emission factors showed minimal variation in the dominant emission species across driving conditions and startup modes (Supplementary Fig. S5–S8). The dominant species in alkanes, alkenes, aromatics, and OVOCs were 2-methylhexane, ethylene, toluene, and vinyl acetate, respectively. Additionally, benzene, xylene, and ethylbenzene were also predominant compounds within the aromatic fraction. Given the growing importance of OVOCs with advances in vehicle technology, we conducted a more detailed investigation of the top OVOC species (Supplementary Fig. S9).

The OVOC emission factors exhibited a progressive reduction from China IV to China VI ICEVs, with a 61.6% decrease observed between China IV (80.5 mg km−1) and China V (30.9 mg km−1) vehicles and a further 57.1% reduction from China V to China VI (13.3 mg km−1) vehicles during the low-speed phase for hot-start mode. However, this decreasing trend was reversed for China VI HEVs, which emitted nearly twice as many OVOCs (25.4 mg km−1) as their China VI ICEV counterparts. These increased emissions in HEVs can be attributed to frequent engine start–stop operation at low speeds and idle, which maintain lower combustion temperatures and suboptimal thermal conditions for TWC systems. Frequent engine start–stop operations in hybrid vehicles have been widely reported in published studies. Since hybrid systems typically switch between the electric motor and the engine as power sources, incomplete combustion occurs during engine starts due to frequent restarts and longer warm-up time [28,29], which would lead to significantly higher particle-number emissions in China VI hybrid electric vehicles compared to conventional gasoline vehicles. Therefore, the combination of increased incomplete combustion and reduced TWC conversion efficiency for carbonyl compounds at lower operating temperatures leads to significantly higher OVOC emissions in HEVs despite their advanced emission control technology. The data suggest that while progressive emission standards have effectively reduced OVOCs in conventional vehicles, hybrid-specific operating conditions introduce new challenges for controlling these oxygenated species, warranting further investigation with fleet-level data.

From the perspective of OVOC speciation, vinyl acetate was the most predominant species, accounting for 37–46% of OVOCs in ICEVs under the low-speed phase for hot-start mode. This compound has also been reported as a significant OVOC component in other studies [30] and has been detected in both tailpipe and evaporative emissions [31]. Its formation likely originates from the oxidation of olefins in fuels and the potential decomposition of certain gasoline additives. The study also detected methyl t-butyl ether (MTBE), a gasoline additive historically used to improve octane rating and combustion efficiency, but known to cause groundwater contamination [31]. In this study, for ICEVs in the low-speed phase of hot-start mode, MTBE accounted for 8–14% of OVOCs but only 1–4% of total VOC emissions, consistent with the low levels reported by Sun et al. [31]. These total-VOC fractions were lower than those reported by Zhang et al. (5.8–7.2%) [32] and Sun et al. (7.8–8.6%) [33], probably reflecting the use of China VI standard gasoline in this study, which prohibits MTBE additives. The detected MTBE likely derives from the oxidation of butenes, an important alkene species identified in tailpipe emissions (Supplementary Fig. S5–S8), rather than direct fuel additives. This inference is supported by the reversibility of the chemical transformation between MTBE and butenes. MTBE has been shown to decompose directly into isobutene and methanol under pyrolytic conditions [34]. Conversely, under combustion conditions, isobutene may undergo oxidation to form oxygenated intermediates that can be further converted to MTBE [35]. Acetone and acrolein were also major OVOC constituents. Acetone primarily forms through incomplete combustion and MTBE decomposition, while acrolein mainly results from high-temperature fuel cracking and propylene oxidation [32,36].

The widespread adoption of HEVs has revealed distinct OVOC emission profiles compared to ICEVs, with MTBE and acrolein showing substantially higher proportions in HEV emissions, whereas vinyl acetate shows lower proportions. Specifically, under the low-speed phase for hot-start mode, China VI HEVs exhibited a higher MTBE contribution to total OVOCs (23%) compared to China IV–VI ICEVs (8–13%), while showing reduced vinyl acetate proportions (34% in HEVs vs. 37–46% in ICEVs). Herein, we conducted a detailed analysis of OVOC emissions for HEVs (Fig. 3), revealing OVOC emission factors of 32.9 mg km−1 (low-speed), 7.7 mg km−1 (medium-speed), and 5.0 mg km−1 (high- and extra-high-speed) for cold-start mode, with the elevated low-speed emissions attributable to multiple compounding factors. Frequent engine start–stop cycling in HEVs promotes incomplete combustion and reduces aftertreatment efficiency due to sustained low exhaust temperatures, despite motor assistance during low-speed operation. In our tested non-plug-in hybrids, intermittent engine engagement induces air-fuel ratio fluctuations that enhance partial oxidation pathways. Furthermore, the start–stop behavior increases fuel wall-wetting, in which unvaporized fuel containing OVOC precursors adheres to cylinder surfaces before entering the exhaust stream. These combined mechanisms explain both the quantitative and qualitative differences in OVOC emissions between HEVs and ICEVs, highlighting the need for specialized emission-control approaches tailored to the operational characteristics of hybrid powertrains.

While driving speed phases and startup modes significantly influenced OVOC emission factors for the tested HEVs, the dominant species showed little variation across these different conditions (Fig. 3). The most abundant OVOC species were vinyl acetate, MTBE, acrolein, and acetone, which remained consistently predominant across the different tested conditions. Conversely, low-molecular-weight OVOCs, such as formaldehyde and acetaldehyde, exhibited lower emissions. Concurrently, TWC may exhibit higher conversion efficiency for these lighter aldehyde species compared to other OVOCs, consistent with the observed reductions in their emissions. While existing studies have investigated the formation mechanisms of three classes of oxygenated compounds (carbonyl compounds, five alcohols, and organic acids) during isooctane combustion [32], there remains a significant lack of understanding regarding the formation mechanisms of OVOC species with varying molecular weights, indicating further experimental validation is required to confirm these proposed mechanisms. If such a dual mechanism combining suppressed formation kinetics and enhanced catalytic removal were operative, it could provide a further explanation for the consistently lower emissions of low-molecular-weight aldehydes relative to higher-molecular-weight OVOCs observed across various driving conditions. Future research should focus on experimentally decoupling the effects of combustion temperature and catalyst thermal dynamics on the formation and oxidation of OVOC species with different molecular weights.

This study successfully determined molecular-level speciation of OVOCs in HEV emissions, identifying MTBE and vinyl acetate as powertrain-specific molecular markers; however, additional fleet samples are still needed to validate the findings. Despite vinyl acetate's substantial contribution to HEV exhaust emissions, it is often overlooked in conventional emission profiling. This underscores the need to incorporate targeted species into future vehicle emission testing protocols. Furthermore, our findings reveal a long-neglected trade-off between fuel efficiency improvements and certain pollution risks. The critical OVOC species identified in this study are potentially unregulated under exhaust emission standards, and their environmental significance warrants further investigation that incorporates both environmental persistence and toxicological effects.

Priority control targets revealed: secondary pollution formation potential of vehicular VOCs

Building upon our established technology-driven emission trends and hybrid-specific speciation profiles, we further quantified the environmental impacts using OFP and SOAP indicators. Stringent regulations have been observed to significantly reduce OFP (Supplementary Fig. S10). Specifically, China VI ICEVs exhibited a 78.9% decrease in OFP under low-speed phases for cold-start mode compared to China IV ICEVs. However, China VI HEVs (250 mg O3 km−1) showed no statistically significant reduction in OFP relative to their ICEV counterparts (241 mg O3 km−1). A detailed analysis of the top four dominant VOC species in OFP and SOAP reveals critical trends influenced by evolving emission standards (Fig. 4). The top four dominant species consistently account for approximately 50% of total emissions across varying driving conditions and startup modes, underscoring their prevalence in vehicular exhaust. The composition of these species changes as emission standards change. China IV and China V ICEVs had high contributions from alkenes and aromatics, particularly in low-speed phases for cold-start mode (Supplementary Fig. S10). In contrast, China VI ICEVs exhibited an increased proportion of OVOCs, and this trend was even more pronounced in China VI HEVs, where acrolein and vinyl acetate emerged as the two dominant species in OFP. These findings indicate that, while OFP values declined with regulatory upgrades, the growing prominence of OVOCs in OFP warrants targeted attention, given their implications for secondary pollution formation.

The implementation of stricter emission standards and hybrid technology has led to substantial reductions in SOAP (Supplementary Fig. S10). Specifically, China VI ICEVs demonstrated an 80.1% decrease under low-speed conditions for cold-start mode compared to China IV ICEVs, while China VI HEVs achieved a further 41.6% reduction relative to their China VI ICEV counterparts. These results underscore the efficacy of technological advancements in mitigating potential SOA formation. In contrast to OFP, the composition of SOAP was dominated by a highly consistent group of aromatic species across all driving conditions and startup modes. The top four contributors (toluene, ethylbenzene, m,p-xylene, and benzene) collectively account for over 70% of total SOAP, highlighting their disproportionate role in SOA formation. Furthermore, as emission control technologies advance, the relative contribution of these four dominant aromatic species to total SOAP becomes increasingly pronounced. While emission control technologies have significantly reduced the absolute concentrations of these aromatics, their persistent dominance in SOAP underscores the need for targeted management strategies, even in the ultra-low-emission era for vehicles.

This study provides a secondary pollution-oriented perspective for identifying priority control species. While stricter emission standards effectively reduce overall pollutant emissions, their environmental benefits reveal an important dichotomy: first, the growing prominence of OVOCs in OFP profiles, particularly with technological advancements, and second, the persistent dominance of aromatics in SOAP formation. These findings highlight the need for targeted control strategies focusing on high-abundance, high-reactivity species to maximize environmental benefits while optimizing mitigation costs.

Toxicity-based prioritization: health risks of vehicular VOC emissions

The aforementioned analysis identifies OVOCs and aromatics as priority control targets based on their potential to form secondary pollutants. Building on this, we further evaluate these species via toxicity-driven rankings based on two key metrics: CR and HI. The HI and CR values of the tested vehicles under cold-start conditions are presented (Fig. 5), while the corresponding hot-start results are given (Supplementary Fig. S11). Under cold-start conditions, the results demonstrated a progressive reduction in HI values as emission standards become stricter. Specifically, China IV, China V, and China VI ICEVs exhibited HI values of 2507.7, 871.6, and 678.9, respectively, in the low-speed phase of cold-start mode, representing a remarkable 73% decrease from China IV to China VI. From a species-specific perspective, halohydrocarbons from all tested vehicles showed HI values below 1, indicating negligible non-carcinogenic health risks. In contrast, alkanes, alkenes, aromatics, and OVOCs exhibited HI values exceeding 1, suggesting measurable non-carcinogenic health concerns. OVOCs dominated the HI composition, with their contribution increasing with stricter emission standards, reaching 95% in China VI ICEVs. Interestingly, China VI HEVs, despite their superior fuel economy, demonstrated HI values 69% higher than those of their ICEV counterparts. These findings highlight that while upgrades to emission standards effectively mitigate overall health risks, the increasing predominance of OVOCs in HI contributions necessitates targeted control strategies, particularly for HEVs, where trade-offs between fuel efficiency and toxic emissions may emerge.

Non-carcinogenic and carcinogenic health risks of VOC emissions under cold-start conditions. a, Non-carcinogenic hazard index for China IV, China V, China VI, and China VI hybrid vehicles over the entire Worldwide harmonized Light vehicles Test Cycle and during the low-speed phase, together with the corresponding class contributions in the right panel. b, Cancer risk and corresponding class contributions under the same conditions.

Consistent with the HI patterns, CR showed a pronounced reduction with successive emission standard upgrades (Fig. 5). Specifically, during the low-speed phase in cold-start mode, China VI ICEVs exhibited an 85.6% decrease in CR compared to China IV ICEVs. However, a notable increase was observed in China VI HEVs, with CR values approaching twice those of their ICEV counterparts. From a compositional perspective, aromatics clearly dominated, accounting for 78–87% of total CR contributions. This predominance stems from their stable benzene-ring structures, whose metabolic intermediates readily form DNA adducts, directly inducing genotoxic effects [37]. Furthermore, alkenes and alkynes contributed marginally to CR (6–14%), and their relative significance declined as technologies to measure emission control advanced. In contrast, halohydrocarbons, which have been observed in previous studies [38], exhibited an upward trend (14–17%), potentially attributable to residual fuel additives and incomplete combustion byproducts. Given their overwhelming CR dominance, aromatics must be prioritized as key control targets for health-centric vehicle emission management.

Our integrated health risk assessment reveals distinct patterns in VOC contributions for both HI and CR metrics. While the dominance of OVOCs in the HI profile and the predominant contribution of aromatics to CR values have been established, a deeper analysis of the individual species will reveal a more complex risk composition. Benzene exhibited a high hazard quotient and had the highest incremental lifetime cancer risk value (Supplementary Fig. S12–S13). Acrolein stood out as the primary HI driver with an exceptionally high hazard quotient, yet it demonstrated negligible carcinogenic risk. This sharp contrast underscores the importance of evaluating both toxicity endpoints. Applying established risk thresholds (HQ > 0.1, ICR >10−6) reported by McCarthy et al. [39], we identified benzene, 1,3-butadiene, and ethylbenzene as the highest priority control targets (Fig. 6). Benzene emerged as the most critical species overall, exhibiting significant impacts in both metrics, due to its classification as a Group 1 carcinogen by the International Agency for Research on Cancer [40], its relatively high cancer slope factor of 0.029 (mg kg−1 day−1)−1 and its long atmospheric lifetime against OH oxidation, with a half-life of approximately 9.5 days [41]. Besides, the particularly severe health implications of benzene stem from its metabolic conversion to DNA-reactive products and synergistic generation of reactive oxygen species [42]. These findings strongly support implementing metric-specific control strategies, including enhanced catalytic oxidation systems for OVOCs and aromatics, as well as stringent fuel composition regulations targeting aromatic content, particularly during cold-start operations. The observed risk trade-offs in hybrid electric vehicles, where acrolein levels increase despite overall CR improvements, further emphasize the need for technology-specific optimization in future emission control frameworks.

Conclusion

This study systematically elucidates the evolving characteristics and environmental health impacts of vehicular VOCs during the transition to advanced emission standards and hybrid technologies in China, providing critical insights to refine the mobile-source regulatory framework. Our findings reveal that, although the updated emission standards lead to a substantial reduction in total VOC emissions, they also induce significant shifts in VOC speciation profiles. Contributions from OVOCs, particularly vinyl acetate and acrolein, increased for both OFP and HI, and the China VI HEVs emitted more OVOCs than their ICEV counterparts. Furthermore, although overall aromatic emissions declined with the stricter standard, aromatics continued to dominate SOA formation and CR. These emerging patterns underscore the limitations of current mass-based regulatory approaches and highlight the urgent need for a multidimensional control strategy incorporating species-specific, reactivity-based, and toxicity-based metrics to address the complex pollution challenges posed by new energy vehicles.

The research particularly identifies significant emission trade-offs in HEVs, where frequent start–stop operations keep TWC systems at suboptimal temperatures, resulting in higher OVOC emissions, HI, and CR values than those of comparable ICEVs. This phenomenon reveals critical technological deficiencies in existing aftertreatment systems when used with hybrid powertrains, necessitating targeted solutions, such as electrically heated catalysts, to achieve effective mitigation. Furthermore, we must address the unintended environmental consequences associated with technological upgrades. For instance, selective catalytic reduction systems may increase emissions of ammonia and nitrous oxide, underscoring the need to consider both direct emissions and secondary transformation effects when formulating next-generation emission standards.

This study has several limitations. While the primary objective of this study is to establish a broadly applicable and generalizable methodology for evaluating vehicular VOC emissions and their associated environmental and health impacts, we chose widely accessible GC-MS techniques to ensure the proposed “concentration-reactivity-toxicity” framework itself maintains high applicability and reproducibility. The conclusions regarding HEVs in this study are based on a single-vehicle case analysis, which may limit generalizability. Furthermore, to ensure the generalizability and broad applicability of the assessment framework proposed in this study, we deliberately selected well-established metrics (i.e., OFP, SOAP, health risk, carcinogenic risk) and did not yet incorporate mechanistic chemical parameters. This approach does not fully account for the complex interactions between environmental factors and health outcomes, potentially limiting the depth of the analysis. The primary reason is that parameters derived from mechanistic studies, such as smog chamber experiments, in vitro cell experiments, and aquatic bioassays, have not yet been systematized into a universally applicable framework.

To address these challenges, we suggest several directions for improving vehicular organic-emission assessment and management. First, future implementations of this framework could utilize more advanced analytical instruments, such as proton-transfer-reaction time-of-flight mass spectrometry (PTR-TOF-MS) for real-time transient emission profiling and comprehensive two-dimensional gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC×GC-MS) for detailed volatility-based speciation. These techniques would expand the detectable range of organic compounds and improve the accuracy of vehicular organic-emission assessment, thereby enhancing the applicability and robustness of the proposed “concentration-reactivity-toxicity” framework. Second, future emission standards could incorporate OVOC-specific limits, for example, for formaldehyde and acrolein, together with stricter controls on aromatic content in fuels. Third, although the present HEV case study reveals distinct emission characteristics under specific operating modes compared to conventional ICEVs, generalizable conclusions about HEV VOC profiles and their environmental and health impacts require validation through larger fleet-based emission testing. The results nevertheless provide testable hypotheses and priority directions for future fleet studies. Control strategies for new-energy vehicles should also consider both reactivity- and toxicity-based prioritization of VOC species. Fourth, future work should combine this framework with mechanistic approaches, including smog-chamber experiments, in vitro cell experiments, and aquatic bioassays, to better link VOC emissions to their environmental and health impacts through multidimensional, multigroup experimental designs. Integrating the emission-based results with mechanistic parameters related to environmental and health effects could further extend the generalizable “concentration-reactivity-toxicity” framework into a more comprehensive assessment system. Fifth, future work could incorporate economic metrics (e.g., control costs per species, health damage costs) to support a full cost-benefit analysis and identify strategies that jointly optimize emissions reduction and cost.

Overall, our findings reveal that future vehicular emission standards should move beyond the current mass-concentration-based regulatory framework by establishing specific measurement methods and limits for key species such as aromatics and OVOCs, and by incorporating these into the real-driving emission testing system. This will facilitate a transition in emission-standard formulation from aggregate control to species-specific precision management, supporting the dual goals of air-quality improvement and public-health protection. Finally, the integrated approach proposed in this study will facilitate the synergistic achievement of carbon reduction, air quality improvement, and public health protection objectives, offering a novel paradigm for mobile source emission control in the era of vehicle electrification.
