Structural insights into ligand recognition and activation of the human oxoglutarate receptor OXGR1
Authors: Tingshuai Ma, Ziyan Chen, Enyuan Liang, Xiufei Tang, Ximin Chi, Quanchang Gu, Qiang Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe human oxoglutarate receptor 1 (OXGR1/GPR99) is a G protein–coupled receptor (GPCR) expressed in the kidney that senses metabolic signals. In addition, OXGR1 also responds to immunometabolites in the airway epithelium and contributes to innate airway defense. Despite the identification of endogenous ligands α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) and itaconate (ITN), the molecular basis of these metabolite recognition and activation remains poorly understood. Here, we present cryo-electron microscopy structures of human OXGR1 in complex with Gq protein bound to α-KG and ITN, helping to reveal key binding interactions and conformational changes associated with receptor activation. Structure-activity relationship analyses using diverse dicarboxylates elucidate the determinants of ligand specificity, including carbon chain length, functional groups, and spatial configuration. Furthermore, we show a pH-dependent regulatory mechanism, with maximal OXGR1 activity at physiological pH 7.4, indicating its regulation in the renal environment. Together, these findings elucidate the structural basis of metabolite sensing by OXGR1 and provide a framework for rational drug discovery targeting OXGR1-associated disorders.Subject terms: Cryoelectron microscopy, Extracellular signalling molecules, Structural biology

Introduction

Oxoglutarate receptor 1 (OXGR1, also known as GPR99) is a G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) broadly expressed in human tissues, with high expression in renal distal tubules, lung epithelium, and nasal mucosa1. As a member of the aliphatic carboxylic acid receptor family, OXGR1 senses key metabolic intermediates, transducing extracellular chemical cues to intracellular G protein signaling, which plays an important role in metabolism, immunity, and inflammatory responses2.

OXGR1 primarily couples to Gq proteins. Among its endogenous ligands, the dicarboxylates α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) and itaconate (ITN) activate the receptor in their dianionic forms at physiological pH1,3,4. α-KG, a central TCA cycle intermediate, connects OXGR1 signaling to mitochondrial metabolism, renal ion homeostasis, and acid-base regulation5,6. Recent studies have also identified itaconate, a metabolite with anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial properties, as a functional ligand of OXGR1, capable of modulating mucosal immunity and host defense mechanisms, particularly in response to pulmonary bacterial infections4. In addition to metabolic ligands, OXGR1 responds to cysteinyl leukotrienes (CysLTs), with leukotriene E4 (LTE₄) acting as a high-affinity agonist at sub-nanomolar concentrations7,8. However, the CysLTs as direct OXGR1 ligands remain controversial, with evidence pointing to possible context-dependent activation mechanisms4.

Physiological studies underscore the importance of OXGR1 in regulating airway inflammation, vascular permeability, glucose metabolism, and muscle homeostasis2,4. Furthermore, loss-of-function mutations or gene deletions of OXGR1 in mouse models have been linked to pathologies such as otitis media and renal calcium deposition5,9. Notably, human pathogenic variants of OXGR1 gene were identified in individuals with nephrolithiasis and nephrocalcinosis, suggesting OXGR1 plays a protective role against calcium-based kidney stone formation10.

To elucidate the structural underpinnings of ligand recognition, in this work, we determine two cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of human OXGR1 in complex with Gαq/Gβ1/Gγ2, bound to the endogenous ligands ITN and α-KG. Combined with functional assays, these structures reveal the key interactions governing ligand binding and G protein coupling. Our findings provide a structural framework for understanding OXGR1 activation and offer potential avenues for therapeutic targeting in nephrolithiasis and other metabolic or inflammatory disorders.

Results

Overall structures of OXGR1 bound to ITN and α-KG

Consistent with previous studies4, human OXGR1 was functionally active and induced intracellular calcium elevation upon stimulation with ITN or α-KG. In contrast, no calcium response was observed in control cells (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1). Following functional validation, we determined the cryo-EM structures of human OXGR1–Gq complexes bound to ITN and α-KG. Structural determination was facilitated by BRIL fusion, engineered Gαq, NanoBiT tethering, and the stabilizing antibody fragment scFv16, which were previously validated in structural studies of GPCR–G protein complexes (Supplementary Fig. 2a)11–13. Using our established pipeline for protein purification and cryo-EM analysis14–16, we resolved the ITN- and α-KG-bound complexes to global resolutions of 2.62 Å and 2.37 Å, respectively (Fig. 1b, c; Supplementary Figs. 3, 4; Supplementary Table 1). Notably, after multiple rounds of 3D classification, the EM densities for ITN and α-KG are well resolved, with the vinyl and carbonyl bonds clearly discernible at different contour levels (Supplementary Figs. 3g, 4g).

The two structures exhibit a typical active-like conformation of G protein-GPCR coupling (Fig. 1b, c). The overall structure of ITN-bound OXGR1 closely resembles those of the G protein-coupled succinate receptor 1 (SUCR1; PDB: 8YKW)17 and the hydroxycarboxylic acid receptor 2 (HCA2; PDB: 8IHB)18, with root mean square deviation (RMSD) of 0.95 Å and 1.08 Å, respectively (Fig. 1d). The structure of ITN-bound OXGR1 is nearly identical to that of SUCR1, whereas HCA2 displays notable structural differences in transmembrane segment 6 (TM6) and the N-terminal region. Specifically, TM6 of HCA2 undergoes an outward rotation of approximately 30° relative to that of OXGR1 and SUCR1, resulting in a large ligand binding pocket. Furthermore, the N terminus of HCA2 forms an antiparallel β-strand hairpin structure that acts as a lid over the extracellular ligand-binding pocket (Fig. 1d).

Molecular dynamics analysis of α-KG and ITN binding poses

The cryo-EM maps are well resolved, with local resolution ~2.2 Å around the ligand. However, α-KG and ITN are small molecules with limited structural features, and multiple poses can fit the density. To address this, we generated alternative cryo-EM guided poses for both ligands. For α-KG, poses 1 and 2 orient the carbonyl group away from R1103.29 on TM3, whereas poses 3 and 4 orient it toward R1103.29; within each pair, the α- and γ-carboxylate groups adopt opposite orientations (Supplementary Fig. 5a). For ITN, poses 1 and 2 orient the vinyl group away from R1103.29, whereas poses 3 and 4 orient it toward R1103.29; within each pair, the α- and β-carboxylate groups adopt opposite orientations (Supplementary Fig. 5b).

We then performed three independent 1000-ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulations for each pose to evaluate ligand stability and identify the most plausible binding mode. For the α-KG–bound OXGR1 complex, the receptor backbone RMSD remained below ~0.4 nm across all simulations and poses, indicating overall receptor stability (Supplementary Fig. 6a). These stable RMSD trajectories indicate that the production simulations reached equilibrium and provide a reliable basis for subsequent analyses. By contrast, ligand RMSD values were lower for poses 1 and 3 than for poses 2 and 4, suggesting greater stability of poses 1 and 3 within the binding pocket (Supplementary Fig. 6b).

To further evaluate ligand stability, we monitored angular deviation of two vectors, C1 → C5 (long axis) and C2 → O3 (short axis; carbonyl), relative to their initial conformations. Consistent with the ligand RMSD analysis, poses 1 and 3 exhibited smaller long-axis fluctuations than poses 2 and 4 (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Comparison between poses 1 and 3 further showed that pose 3 displayed smaller C2 → O3 angular deviation throughout the simulations (Supplementary Fig. 7b). Representative trajectories indicated that pose 1 occasionally transitioned toward a pose 3-like conformation during the simulations, suggesting pose 3 is more energetically favorable (Supplementary Fig. 7c). Analysis of the final 100 ns revealed significantly reduced angular deviation for pose 3 compared with pose 1 (Supplementary Fig. 7d). Collectively, these results suggest pose 3 as the most stable binding mode among the four α-KG models.

We applied the same analysis to the ITN-bound OXGR1 complex. The receptor backbone RMSD remained below ~0.4 nm across all simulations and poses, indicating overall receptor stability (Supplementary Fig. 8a). In contrast, ligand RMSD values were lower for poses 1 and 4 than for poses 2 and 3, suggesting greater stability of these poses within the binding pocket (Supplementary Fig. 8b). For ITN, the long-axis angle was defined by the C1 → C5 vector, and the short-axis angle by the C2 → C3 vector (C = C). Angles were calculated relative to the corresponding vectors in the 0-ns structure. Poses 1 and 4 exhibited smaller long-axis deviation than poses 2 and 3 (Supplementary Fig. 9a). Comparison of the short-axis (C = C) orientation further showed that pose 1 exhibited smaller angular deviation than pose 4 (Supplementary Fig. 9b). Representative trajectories indicated that pose 4 occasionally transitioned toward a pose 1-like conformation at later time points, suggesting pose 1 is more energetically favorable (Supplementary Fig. 9c). However, statistical analysis of angular deviation showed no significant difference between poses 1 and 4 (Supplementary Fig. 9d). Nonetheless, pose 1 exhibited a narrower distribution, whereas pose 4 showed broader angular fluctuations across replicates. These results indicate that pose 1 adopts a more consistent binding geometry and may be more stable in the orthosteric binding pocket of OXGR1. Collectively, these analyses support pose 1 as the most stable ITN binding mode among the four candidates.

In summary, the MD analyses first excluded α-KG poses 2 and 4, and ITN poses 2 and 3, due to larger ligand RMSD fluctuations and long-axis instability. Discrimination among the remaining candidates (α-KG poses 1 vs 3; ITN poses 1 vs 4) was achieved through analysis of ligand short-axis dynamics and representative trajectory snapshots. Notably, some variability was observed among independent simulation replicates, suggesting that ligand orientations may undergo dynamic interconversion (Supplementary Figs. 6–9). Nevertheless, α-KG pose 3 and ITN pose 1 exhibited greater stability than the other pose candidates and were therefore selected as the final ligand models for analysis.

The orthosteric binding pocket of OXGR1

In both ITN-bound and α-KG-bound OXGR1 structures, the ligands are coordinated within a binding pocket formed by residues in transmembrane segments TM1, TM2, TM3, TM6, and TM7 (Fig. 2a). Itaconate contains two carboxylate groups: one located on the methylene side (β-position) and the other on the vinyl side (α-position)19. In ITN-bound structures, the α-position carboxylate group is coordinated by H1143.33 and R1103.29 through a hydrogen bond and an ionic bond. Meanwhile, the β-position carboxylate group is predominantly stabilized by R1103.29 and R2887.39 via two distinct ionic bonds. Furthermore, the hydroxyl groups of Y401.39 and Y932.64 stabilize the ligand conformation by forming two specific hydrogen bonds with the β-carboxylate group (Fig. 2b). In addition to these ionic and hydrogen bonding interactions, extensive van der Waals contacts are established between the alkyl and vinyl moieties of ITN and the surrounding residues F1133.32, F2576.51, Y2847.35, A2917.42, and L892.60, contributing to the overall binding stability (Fig. 2b).

In the α-KG-bound OXGR1 structure, α-KG is accommodated within a binding pocket analogous to those in the ITN-bound OXGR1 structure (Fig. 2c). α-KG contains two distinct carboxylate groups: the α-carboxylate, situated adjacent to the carbonyl group, and the ω-carboxylate, located at the terminal end of the carbon chain20. In the structure, the α-carboxylate group is coordinated by Y932.64 and Y401.39, whereas the γ-carboxylate is stabilized through interactions with Y2847.35 and H1143.33. Notably, R1103.29 contribute one hydrogen bonds to the carbonyl oxygen of α-KG, further stabilizing the conformation of α-KG within the pocket. Consistent with the α-KG-bound configuration, residues F1133.32, F2576.51, Y2847.35, A2917.42, and L892.60 engage in extensive van der Waals interactions with α-KG, which collectively contribute to ligand coordination (Fig. 2c).

Previous studies have systematically characterized the residues within the orthosteric site of OXGR14. Consistent with our structural observations, mutations of the conserved polar residues R1103.29A, H1143.33A, and R2887.39A significantly impaired ITN- or α-KG-induced upregulation of intracellular calcium concentration4. To evaluate the functional contribution of non-polar residues within the orthosteric binding pocket, we generated four stable cell lines expressing OXGR1 mutants harboring the following substitutions: F1133.32L, Y2847.35F, A2927.43F, or the double mutant F1133.32L/A2927.43F (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Surface expression levels were comparable among cells expressing wild-type (WT) and mutant OXGR1 (Supplementary Fig. 10a, b). Cells expressing WT OXGR1 exhibited a clear calcium response upon the addition of ITN or α-KG. In contrast, cells expressing these mutant receptors showed a markedly reduced calcium response upon stimulation with ITN or α-KG (Fig. 2d, e, and Supplementary Fig. 10c, d; Supplementary Tables S2 and S3). These findings demonstrate that non-polar residues also play a critical role in OXGR1 activation by ITN or α-KG.

In summary, the endogenous ligands of OXGR1, ITN and α-KG, are dicarboxylates bearing two negatively charged carboxylate groups at opposite ends. These anionic moieties are electrostatically coordinated by basic residues within the orthosteric binding pocket, notably R1103.29, H1143.33, and R2887.39 (Fig. 2f). In addition, hydrogen bonding interactions from the phenolic hydroxyl groups of Y401.39, Y932.64, and Y2847.35 further stabilize ligand binding. Several surrounding non-polar residues contribute to hydrophobic interactions, collectively shaping the ligand recognition landscape and supporting OXGR1 activation (Fig. 2f).

Ligand repertoire of human OXGR1

The binding pocket structures of ITN- and α-KG-bound OXGR1 are highly similar. Sequence alignments among OXGR1, SUCR1, and HCA2 reveal that the residues within the ligand-binding pocket are highly conserved between OXGR1 and SUCR1, whereas HCA2 shows considerable variation in these regions (Supplementary Fig. 11; Supplementary Table 4). This indicates that the ligands recognized by OXGR1 and SUCR1 may have similar chemical structures, allowing them to bind dicarboxylates, whereas HCA2 typically binds monocarboxylates (e.g., β-hydroxybutyrate)21.

Structural comparison of OXGR1 or SUCR1 (PDB: 8YKW)17 with HCA2 (PDB: 8IHI)22 reveals distinct binding modes for dicarboxylate versus monocarboxylate ligands. In α-KG-bound OXGR1, α-KG adopts a horizontal orientation across the binding pocket, roughly parallel to the membrane plane, engaging both carboxyl groups (Supplementary Fig. 12a). Succinate in SUCR1 adopts a similar horizontal, membrane-parallel orientation (Supplementary Fig. 12b). By contrast, HCA receptors accommodate monocarboxylate ligands in a vertical orientation, with the single carboxylate anchored at the base of the pocket (Supplementary Fig. 12c). This difference results from the highly basic environment of the OXGR1 pocket, including R1103.29, R2887.39, and H1143.33, in sharp contrast to the single arginine positioned deep within the HCA receptor pocket18,22.

To systematically investigate the dicarboxylates recognized by OXGR1, we evaluated ten structurally diverse compounds and classified them into three groups. Group 1 comprises itaconate, succinate, citraconate, and fumarate, which share a similar carbon backbone but differ in their side-chain modifications (Fig. 3a, left panel). Specifically, itaconate is an unsaturated, branched dicarboxylic acid containing a methylidene group; succinate is fully saturated and lacks double bonds; fumarate is an unsaturated dicarboxylic acid featuring a trans (E) double bond; and citraconate is an unsaturated, branched dicarboxylic acid with a cis (Z) methyl-substituted double bond (Fig. 3a, left panel). These structural variations may lead to distinct modes of recognition by OXGR1. Consistent with this hypothesis, succinate exhibited a significantly reduced activation capacity, whereas citraconate and fumarate nearly abolished the calcium response compared to ITN (Fig. 3a, right panel; Supplementary Table S5). Structurally, the vinyl group of ITN interacts with F1133.32, stabilizing the conformation (Fig. 2b). Together with functional data, these results indicate that double-bond configuration is a key determinant of OXGR1 substrate specificity.

Group 2 consists of the dicarboxylates α-KG, glutarate, and malate (Fig. 3b, left panel). α-KG contains a carbonyl group and two carboxylate groups separated by methylene groups. Glutarate is a saturated dicarboxylic acid composed of five carbon atoms, with no functional groups other than the carboxylate moieties. Malate has a hydroxyl group adjacent to one of its carboxylate groups, replacing the ketone group present in α-KG. Similar to Group 1, different dicarboxylates display significantly different activities toward OXGR1 (Fig. 3b, right panel; Supplementary Table S5). Compared to α-KG, glutarate and malate show impaired or abolished calcium responses in cells expressing OXGR1 upon ligand stimulation. Structurally, compared with glutarate, the α-keto carbonyl of α-KG forms additional polar contacts with Y401.39 and R2887.39, constraining the ligand orientation and stabilizing the binding geometry (Fig. 2c).

Group 3 dicarboxylates include malonate, succinate, glutarate, adipate, and suberate, which are linear dicarboxylic acids that primarily differ in the carbon chain length between their two carboxylate groups (Fig. 3c, left panel). Malonate contains three carbon atoms, followed by succinate (four), glutarate (five), adipate (six), and suberate (eight). In calcium flux assays, succinate elicited the strongest calcium response in OXGR1-expressing cells. The response decreased progressively with increasing carbon chain length, as observed with glutarate, adipate, and suberate. Similarly, reducing the chain length, as shown in malonate, significantly diminished the calcium response (Fig. 3c, right panel; Supplementary Table S5). The orthosteric pocket of OXGR1 is constrained, accommodating C4–C5 ligands with a conserved binding geometry (Figs. 2f and 3d). By contrast, shorter (C3) or longer (≥C6) analogs are either too small or too large and may not fit the pocket favorably.

Several dicarboxylates, including succinate and glutarate, can activate OXGR1, albeit with considerably lower potency compared to ITN and α-KG. Structural analysis between ITN-bound OXGR1 and succinate-bound SUCR1 reveals that the ITN-binding pocket of OXGR1 closely resembles that of SUCR1 (PDB: 8YKW), with well-conserved residues (Fig. 3d), supporting the capacity to recognize succinate by OXGR1. The reduced activation potency of succinate is likely attributable to the absence of the vinyl group present in ITN and the carbonyl group in α-KG (Fig. 3a, b).

pH dependent regulation of OXGR1 activity

Dicarboxylates contain a strong negative charge, whereas the ligand-binding pocket of OXGR1 exhibits an electrostatically positive environment (Fig. 4a). We hypothesized that the charge-based interaction between OXGR1 and dicarboxylates could be influenced by the pH value of the local environment, thereby affecting OXGR1 activation upon ligand stimulation. To test this hypothesis, we assessed the effects of four buffer pH conditions on calcium influx elicited by three dicarboxylates: ITN, succinate, and α-KG. For all tested ligands, the maximal calcium responses were observed at pH 7.4, with slightly decreased responses at pH 6.5 and 8.0 (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 13a). Notably, calcium signaling was entirely absent at pH 5.0 for all three ligands (Supplementary Fig. 13a–c). These results indicate that pH modulates OXGR1 activation by these dicarboxylates in a consistent manner.

pH influences the charge states of both the ligand and the orthosteric pocket of OXGR1. To elucidate the molecular basis of pH-dependent regulation, we performed 1000-ns MD simulation of the α-KG–bound OXGR1 (pose 3) at different pH conditions. We quantified the occupancy of key residues in the orthosteric pocket by α-KG. Across three independent replicates, residue occupancy was markedly reduced at pH 5.0, most prominently for Y401.39, Y932.64, and R1103.29. By contrast, MD simulation at pH 6.5 and 7.4 exhibited similarly high and stable occupancy, whereas pH 8.0 showed a modest reduction (Fig. 4c).

Consistent with these findings, α-KG–induced Ca²⁺ responses were completely abolished at pH 5.0, whereas OXGR1 signaling remained detectable at pH 6.5, 7.4, and 8.0 (Fig. 4b). Notably, despite modest functional impairment at pH 6.5 and 8.0, no apparent reduction in key residue occupancy was observed in the MD simulation. Collectively, these results indicate that acidic pH disrupts ligand engagement of OXGR1, whereas moderate pH variations likely modulate OXGR1 function through mechanisms beyond ligand binding.

Structural basis of ligand-induced activation of OXGR1

Structural comparison of active ITN-bound OXGR1 with its homologous inactive SUCR1 (PDB: 6IBB)23 reveals coupling to Gq (Fig. 5a). Ligand binding induces subtle motions in TM7 on the extracellular side, accompanying pronounced rotations of TM5 and TM6 by ~30° relative to the inactive state. This leads to the contraction of orthosteric pocket. Furthermore, the ICL2 undergoes a marked outward displacement, facilitating the coupling between Gq protein and OXGR1. In both ITN-bound and α-KG-bound active complexes, the interfaces between Gαq and OXGR1 are almost identical, with an RMSD of 0.40 (Fig. 5b). The α5 helix of Gαq engages extensively with OXGR1 residues in TM3, TM5, TM6, and ICL2, including S682.39, H145ICL2, S2336.27, K2366.30, K2386.32, S3068.47, and D3078.48, and R1313.50, through a number of hydrogen bonds (Fig. 5c).

To assess the functional significance of the OXGR1–Gq interface, we introduced eight alanine substitutions. All variants were properly expressed and trafficked to the plasma membrane (Supplementary Fig. 14a). Seven mutations (S682.39A, H145ICL2A, S2336.27A, K2366.30A, K2386.32A, S3068.47A, and D3078.48A) markedly reduced ITN-induced Ca²⁺ responses, whereas R1313.50A completely abolished signaling (Fig. 5d; Supplementary Table S2). These results corroborate the functional relevance of the OXGR1–Gq interface revealed by the cryo-EM structures.

Discussion

OXGR1 is a kidney-expressed GPCR that regulates urine pH and prevents calcium oxalate nephrolithiasis. Here, we report cryo-EM structures of human OXGR1–Gq complexes bound to itaconate and α-ketoglutarate, helping to reveal the molecular basis of ligand recognition. Analysis of structurally diverse dicarboxylates defines substrate specificity, showing that carbon chain length, functional groups, and geometry determine binding. We further show a pH-dependent activation mechanism, with maximal activity at physiological pH, providing insight into kidney-specific regulation. These findings advance understanding of OXGR1 biology and provide a structural framework for drug design.

Previous animal model studies have suggested that OXGR1 is activated by α-KG and plays a role in the alkalization of urine, thereby preventing the recurrence of calcium oxalate, uric acid, and cystine stones3. Intriguingly, several disease-associated mutations have been identified in OXGR110. Structural mapping of these mutations indicates that the disease-related variants Y932.64H, L1243.43R, C2175.57R, and S2336.27R are located in TM2, TM3, TM5, and TM6, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 15a).

The residue Y932.64 is indispensable for the recognition of α-KG or ITN; thus, the Y932.64H substitution may disrupt ligand recognition in the mutant form of OXGR1. L1243.43 and C2175.57 are positioned within the hydrophobic core of the TM domain, and arginine substitutions at these sites may destabilize the TM domain, leading to impaired OXGR1 function. In line with this interpretation, the Y932.64H, L1243.43R and C2175.57R mutations abolished Ca2+ responses upon the treatment of α-KG or ITN (Supplementary Fig. 15b; Supplementary Tables S2 and S3).

In contrast, the S2336.27R mutation resides near the OXGR1–G protein interaction interface, and this substitution may interfere with the coupling between OXGR1 and the G protein. Consistent with this scenario, the S2336.27R mutation markedly impeded ligand-induced Ca2+signaling, indicating impaired downstream G-protein activation (Supplementary Fig. 15b; Supplementary Tables S2 and S3). Of note, all mutants were properly expressed and trafficked to the plasma membrane (Supplementary Fig. 14b). Together, these structures provide a mechanistic framework for understanding how disease-associated mutations perturb OXGR1 activation, although further in vivo studies will be required to establish causality and define the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms.

Among the various dicarboxylates examined in this study, ITN, α-KG, and succinate exhibited distinct EC50 values of 31.9 μM, 34.5 μM, and 210.1 μM, respectively (Supplementary Table S5). The physiological concentrations of these dicarboxylates are summarized in the Supplementary Table S6. Notably, the physiological concentrations of ITN and α-KG are comparable to their respective EC50 values. In some cases, the concentrations of ITN in vivo can reach to the level of millimolar, in which OXGR1 should be remarkably activated. By contrast, the physiological concentration of succinate is much lower than its EC50 in cells expressing OXGR1. Therefore, ITN and α-KG, but not succinate, are likely to function as the endogenous ligands for OXGR1 under physiological conditions. Nonetheless, under pathological states such as obesity, hypoxia, inflammation, or metabolic disturbances, succinate levels can rise to 150–300 μM24–26, suggesting that OXGR1 may also be engaged under these conditions.

During the revision of this manuscript, two studies reported the cryo-EM structures of OXGR127,28. In one of these reports28, two α-KG molecules were modeled, one occupying the orthosteric site and another located near the pocket entrance adjacent to ECL2 (Supplementary Fig. 16a). Although the α-KG bound in the orthosteric site partially overlaps with that observed in our structure, the additional density near ECL2 modeled as α-KG was not found in our map (Supplementary Fig. 16b). This discrepancy may arise from the weak affinity of the secondary binding pocket for α-KG, resulting in only a small fraction of receptors bound at this site. Nevertheless, our ligand-binding pocket closely resembles that described in the other study27. In addition, the structure of succinate-bound OXGR1 further highlights the critical role of the C = C bond in ITN recognition27. The double bond engages in extensive interactions with neighboring residues, most notably F1133.32, stabilizing the ligand within the orthosteric pocket. These interactions help to explain the high potency and specificity of ITN compared with saturated analogs. These data reinforce our functional and structural conclusions regarding ligand selectivity.

Our ligand recognition mechanism is roughly consistent with a recent study29. For both α-KG–bound and ITN–bound OXGR1, the conformations of residues in the orthosteric pocket are highly similar. The differences are limited to local ligand conformations. For α-KG, the α- and γ-carboxylate groups adopt comparable orientations, with only a minor difference in the position of the carbonyl group (Supplementary Fig. 17a). For ITN, the α- and β-carboxylate groups are similarly positioned, whereas the vinyl group adopts a different orientation (Supplementary Fig. 17b). These observations suggest that the carboxylate groups serve as the primary recognition determinants, whereas the carbonyl group of α-KG and the vinyl group of ITN are more conformationally flexible. Consistent with this interpretation, the recently reported ITN model29 resembles our candidate pose 4, which occasionally transitioned toward a pose 1–like conformation during MD simulations (Supplementary Fig. 9c). In addition, some variability was observed among three simulation replicates, suggesting that ligand orientations may undergo dynamic interconversion (Supplementary Fig. 6–9). Together, these results suggest that local dynamics of ligand branch groups may occur within the orthosteric binding pocket.

Consistent with these findings27–29, we observed that OXGR1 harbors a non-canonical microswitch motifs that govern activation in class A GPCRs. Most class A receptors contain a D(E)RY motif that forms an ionic lock to stabilize the inactive state. In contrast, OXGR1 features a noncanonical FRY motif, indicating an alternative activation mechanism (Supplementary Fig. 18a). Another distinctive feature lies in the NPxxY motif, where the highly conserved proline is replaced by leucine (Supplementary Fig. 18b), further highlighting the distinctive conformational dynamics of OXGR1. Since no inactive-state structure of OXGR1 is currently available, the detailed mechanisms underlying its activation await further investigation.

Previous studies have systematically characterized residues within the orthosteric pocket of OXGR14. In addition to R1103.29, H1143.33, and R2887.39 discussed above, several other mutations—C1063.25A, Y1183.37F, H2586.52A, R2616.55A, and I2656.59A—also markedly impair OXGR1 function4. Structural mapping shows that C1063.25 forms a disulfide bond with C183ECL2, Y1183.37 forms hydrogen bonds with H2586.52 and R2616.55, and I2656.59 engages in van der Waals interactions with the neighboring I1955.35 (Supplementary Fig. 19). These mutations likely destabilize the receptor core, thereby indirectly compromising the integrity of the orthosteric pocket or Gq coupling.

Beyond its renal expression, OXGR1 is present in airway epithelium and immune cells, where it mediates metabolic and inflammatory sensing4. Our structural data provide a mechanistic framework that can inform its function in these non-renal tissues. The ligand-binding pocket and G protein–coupling interface characterized here define how OXGR1 recognizes α-KG and ITA and transduces downstream signaling. Notably, ITN acts as an orthosteric agonist with an EC₅₀ of ~0.03 mM, matching extracellular ITN concentrations during macrophage activation, and stimulates OXGR1-dependent mucus secretion and transport in respiratory epithelium, a critical innate defense mechanism4. These observations suggest that the structural features identified in our study—such as specific ligand contacts and interface residues stabilizing receptor–G protein coupling—may underlie OXGR1-mediated metabolic and inflammatory responses in airway and immune cells, although tissue-specific signaling is likely modulated by local ligand availability and cellular context.

Activation of OXGR1 by α-KG in the kidney cortical connecting tubule and type B intercalated cells of the collecting duct effectively alkalinizes the pro-urine and reduces the risk of calcium oxalate nephrolithiasis in humans3,10. In this study, we systematically elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which OXGR1 recognizes various dicarboxylates. Additionally, we identify a pH-dependent regulatory mechanism of OXGR1 activation, with maximal activity observed at pH 7.4 (Fig. 4b). Activation is impaired under either more alkaline or acidic conditions. Notably, the pH of pro-urine typically ranges from 4.5 to 8.030, suggesting that pH-dependent regulation of OXGR1 may occur under physiological conditions. These findings highlight the need for further investigation into the precise mechanisms underlying this regulatory process.

Evidence from animal models and human genetic studies strongly supports the involvement of OXGR1 in regulating urine pH and its association with calcium oxalate nephrolithiasis10. Targeted activation of OXGR1 may thus represent a promising therapeutic approach for urine alkalinization and the treatment of calcium oxalate nephrolithiasis. In addition, ITA stimulates OXGR1-dependent mucus secretion and transport in respiratory epithelium, a key innate defense mechanism of the airway. The development of OXGR1 agonists thus holds potential for enhancing airway host defense. Our structural analyses offer a solid foundation for the identification and development of agonists. As our understanding of the intricate signaling pathways governed by OXGR1 continues to advance, the potential for designing innovative therapeutic interventions through structure-based drug discovery becomes increasingly viable, opening avenues for the effective management and treatment of a wide range of related diseases.

Methods

Cell lines

Expi293F cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# A14527CN) were cultured in SMM TⅡ Expression Medium (Sino Biological) at 37 °C under shaking conditions at 110 rpm in a suspension culture. Lenti-293T cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Thermo Fisher Scientific), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1× penicillin-streptomycin (Macklin Biochemical), in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂.

Constructs design

A synthetic IgK signal peptide31 (SP) was appended to the N-terminus of OXGR1 and scFv16 to facilitate their secretion. For enhanced heterologous expression and protein stability, the human OXGR1 gene was codon-optimized for mammalian systems and further engineered with strategic modifications at both the N- and C-termini. Specifically, the N-terminus was tagged sequentially with a Flag epitope and BRIL (thermostabilized apocytochrome b562) to promote correct protein folding. At the C-terminus, LgBit was introduced upstream of two tandem maltose-binding protein (MBP) domains to enhance expression levels and improve protein solubility. The Gαq subunit was engineered with stabilizing mutations and domain substitutions designed to support complex formation and binding to the scFv1613, following established strategies in G protein engineering12. For improved complex assembly and detection, mBaojin32 and HiBiT were fused to the N-terminus of the Gβ1 subunit. The C-terminus of the synthetic scFv16 was fused in-frame with mScarlet-i333 (a bright, photostable fluorescent protein), followed by a twin Strep tag and an 8×His tag for affinity purification and detection. The modified fusion constructs, together with the Gγ subunit, were individually amplified using KOD One™ PCR Master Mix -Blue (Takara Biotech) and subsequently cloned into a pSQ expression vector48 via the ClonExpress MultiS One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme Biotech). All primers and cDNAs used in this study were synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. For the cloning of OXGR1, we used primers of F-OXGR1: 5’-ATGAACGAGCCTCTGGACTACC-3’ and R-OXGR1:5’-CGGGTTGTTGCTGTAAGAGATCTTC-3’.

Protein complex expression and purification

OXGR1, Gαq, Gβ1, Gγ2, and scFv16 were co-expressed in Expi293F cells seeded at a density of 2.0–2.5 × 10⁶ cells/mL and cultured at 37 °C for 72 h. Following incubation, cells were harvested via centrifugation and the resulting pellets were stored at −80 °C until further use.

Cell pellets derived from 1 L of co-expression culture were thawed and resuspended in a hypotonic lysis buffer composed of 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl₂, 2 mM CaCl₂, and 10% (v/v) glycerol, supplemented with protease inhibitors (2 μg/mL each of aprotinin, pepstatin A, and leupeptin). The cell suspension was incubated with either 10 mM itaconate (ITN) or α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) (Macklin Biochemical) for 1 h at room temperature to facilitate stabilization of ligand-receptor complexes. Membrane proteins were solubilized through the addition of 1% (w/v) lauryl maltose neopentyl glycol (LMNG, Anatrace) and 0.1% (w/v) cholesteryl hemisuccinate (CHS, Anatrace), followed by gentle mixing at 4 °C for 2.5 hours. Insoluble debris was then removed by centrifugation at 29,097 x g for 1 h at 4 °C.

The clarified lysate was loaded onto an anti-FLAG M2 affinity resin (Millipore Sigma). The resin was extensively washed with a buffer containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM ITN or α-KG, 0.02% (w/v) LMNG, and 0.002% (w/v) CHS, and the target protein was eluted using the same buffer supplemented with 100 μM FLAG peptide. The eluate was subsequently applied to a Strep-Tactin®XT 4Flow® high-capacity resin (IBA Lifesciences), washed with the aforementioned buffer, and eluted with 50 mM D-biotin. The purified complexes were further subjected to size-exclusion chromatography using a Superose™ 6 Increase prepacked column (Cytiva), which was equilibrated with a buffer containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 0.002% (w/v) Lauryl Maltose Neopentyl Glycol (LMNG, Anatrace), 0.001% (w/v) glycol-diosgenin (GDN, Anatrace), 0.0002% (w/v) CHS, and the respective agonist at the aforementioned concentrations. Peak fractions containing the intact complex were collected, pooled, concentrated using Amicon Ultra centrifugal filters (50 kDa, Millipore Sigma), and prepared for cryo-electron microscopy analysis.

Cryo-EM grid preparation and data collection

For cryo-EM sample preparation, 3.5 μL of purified protein complex (approximately 10 mg mL−1) was applied to glow-discharged holey carbon grids (Quantifoil Au, 300 mesh, R1.2/1.3). Grids were glow-discharged at 15 mA for 30 s using a PDC-32G-2 plasma cleaner (Harrick Plasma) prior to sample application. After a blotting time of 3.5 s, grids were vitrified by plunge-freezing into liquid ethane using a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated at 8 °C and 100% humidity. Cryo-EM data were collected on a Titan Krios G4 microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operating at 300 kV and equipped with a Falcon 4i direct electron detector. Automated data acquisition was performed using EPU software (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with movies recorded in super-resolution counting mode at calibrated pixel sizes of 0.936 Å for the Itaconate-OXGR1-Gq (ITN-OXGR1-Gq) complex and 0.927 Å for the α-ketoglutarate-OXGR1-Gq (α-KG-OXGR1-Gq) complex. Each movie consisted of 40 frames, collected over exposure times of 5.38 s (ITN-OXGR1-Gq) and 5.24 s (α-KG-OXGR1-Gq), respectively, with a total electron dose of 50 e- Å−2. The defocus range was maintained between −1.0 and −2.0 μm, with beam-image shift limited to a maximum of 12 μm to ensure accurate contrast transfer function estimation and high-resolution data collection.

Generation of stable cell lines

The full-length OXGR1 cDNA (codon-optimized and synthesized by Tsingke company) was cloned into the LentiCRISPR vector (Addgene: #52962) under the control of the eukaryotic translation initiation factor 1α (EF-1α) core promoter, incorporating an N-terminal Flag tag. The OXGR1 mutants were generated using the same cloning strategy.

For lentiviral production, the transfer plasmids were co-transfected with packaging vectors psPAX2 (Addgene: #12260) and pMD2.G (Addgene: #12259) into Lenti-293T cells (Clontech, 632180). Viral supernatants were harvested at 48- and 72 h post-transfection, filtered through 0.45 μm filters (Beyotime), and concentrated by the addition of protamine sulfate (80 μg ml−1, Macklin) and chondroitin sulfate C (80 μg ml−1, Macklin).

For transduction, 1 × 106 Lenti-293T cells (TaKaRa; Clontech, Code No.632180) were infected with 1 ml of concentrated lentivirus in the presence of polybrene (5 μg ml−1, Sigma-Aldrich). Cell surface expression levels of wild-type and mutant OXGR1 were subsequently assessed by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) using Cell Sorter BD FACSAria Fusion. The surface expression levels were detected by anti-Flag antibodies (ABclonal, Cat#AE116, clone name: AMC0382-PE). All antibodies are commercially available and validated by the manufacturer to demonstrate its ability to detect corresponding target (https://abclonal.com.cn/catalog/AE116; 5 μl/106 cells). None were independently validated by us.

Fluo-4AM assay

Prior to experimentation, 96-well black-wall, clear-bottom plates were coated with 0.1 mg/ml poly-L-lysine (Beyotime) to enhance cell adhesion. Lenti-293T cells were seeded at a density of 3 × 104 cells ml−1 and allowed to adhere for 6 h at 37 °C. OXGR1-expressing cells were subsequently loaded with 1 μM Fluo-4 AM (Solarbio) for 30 min at 37 °C, followed by washing with Ca2+ free Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS, Servicebio) to remove extracellular dye. The test ligands were diluted to the indicated concentrations in HBSS supplemented with 30 mM HEPES (pH 7.4) and added to the cell suspension. Intracellular calcium mobilization was monitored by measuring fluorescence emission at 520 nm following excitation at 488 nm. Fluorescence measurements were acquired at 130-millisecond intervals for approximately 100 s immediately following ligand addition.

pH-controlled Ca²⁺ mobilization assay. Cells were loaded with Fluo-4 AM and incubated under different pH conditions for 30 min at room temperature before measuring calcium mobilization. To achieve precise pH control, adjust the pH of the PBS to the experimentally specified value using hydrochloric acid (HCl) or sodium hydroxide (NaOH). Before use, verify the final pH with a calibrated pH meter to ensure accuracy and stability of pH during the experiment. Ligand stocks were prepared in the same pH-matched assay buffer, and serial dilutions were made in pH-matched buffer to minimize pH drift during titration. Controls for pH specificity. For each tested pH condition, mock-transfected cells (empty vector) were assayed in parallel using the same ligand titration protocol to evaluate receptor-independent effects. No concentration-dependent Ca²⁺ responses were observed in mock cells across the pH range, supporting that the dose-dependent Ca²⁺ signals arise from OXGR1 activation rather than intrinsic 293 T Ca²⁺ signaling changes under altered pH.

Data were processed as follows. Fluorescence signals recorded before calcium stimulation were defined as baseline, and baseline-corrected values were obtained by subtracting this baseline from each time point. The maximal calcium response (ΔF_max) was then used for normalization to reduce variability from differences in initial fluorescence or cell number. This normalization converted calcium flux signals into relative values, allowing direct comparison of response kinetics within the same sample group.

Image processing and structure determination

Motion correction was performed in cryoSPARC v4.6.034 using the Patch Motion Correction job, which corrects both global and local beam-induced motion and applies dose-weighting. CTF parameters were estimated using Patch CTF Estimation, which calculates a per-micrograph defocus landscape without requiring prior particle coordinates.

Automated particle picking was conducted in cryoSPARC using either the blob picker (ITN-OXGR1-Gq) or template-based picker (α-KG-OXGR1-Gq). Extracted particles were subjected to iterative rounds of reference-free 2D classification to remove artifacts and poorly aligned particles. Ab initio reconstruction was used to generate initial 3D models, which were further refined through subsequent classification and refinement steps.

For the ITN-OXGR1-Gq complex, 1,470,987 particles were selected after 2D and 3D classification. Multiple rounds of heterogeneous 3D refinement were performed to identify conformationally homogeneous subsets, followed by non-uniform refinement corrected for local variations in resolution. Focused refinement at the receptor–ligand interface, following particle subtraction, yielded a final map from 334,276 particles at a global resolution of 2.62 Å.

The α-KG-OXGR1-Gq complex dataset yielded 1,959,656 particles after classification. Following the same computational pipeline, the final reconstruction comprised 468,060 particles with a global resolution of 2.37 Å. Global resolution estimates were based on the gold-standard Fourier shell correlation (FSC) = 0.143 criterion. Local resolution variations were assessed using cryoSPARC’s local resolution estimation tool. Despite the high resolution of the consensus map, the ligand density within the α-KG–OXGR1–Gq complex remained poorly defined. To improve this, we performed 3D classification on the 468,060 particles contributing to the consensus reconstruction, using a solvent mask for the receptor and a local focus mask encompassing the ligand-binding pocket. Each class was subsequently reconstructed using a heterogeneous reconstruction-only approach. This strategy ultimately produced a reconstruction with continuous, well-resolved ligand density.

Atomic model building and validation

Initial models were generated using the AlphaFold335-predicted structure of human OXGR1 as a template. The predicted model was refined against the experimental density using CryFold36 to generate a backbone trace consistent with the cryo-EM map. UCSF ChimeraX-1.837 was used to dock the predicted model into the electron microscopy density map with the help of the reference model. Manual model building and adjustment were performed in COOT v0.9.8.9238, with particular attention to ligand-binding sites and protein-protein interfaces. The models were further refined and validated in Phenix v1.21.239.

Molecular dynamics simulation

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed for eight systems, comprising four distinct binding poses for both the α-KG– and ITN–bound OXGR1 complexes. The initial structures were derived from the cryo-EM models, with G proteins manually removed. Standard protonation states corresponding to physiological pH (7.4) were applied for these initial systems. For subsequent pH-dependent simulations on specific poses, the pKa values of titratable residues were predicted using PROPKA340, and the protonation states were reassigned accordingly. The resulting coordinates were then uploaded to the Membrane Builder module of CHARMM-GUI41 to construct the explicit membrane systems.

The transmembrane orientation of OXGR1 was determined via the PPM 2.0 server42. Ligand topology and parameter files were generated using the CHARMM General Force Field (CGenFF)43. The oriented complexes were embedded into a rectangular POPC lipid bilayer with initial dimensions of 75 × 75 Å. The systems were solvated with a 12 Å water layer and neutralized with 0.15 M NaCl using the replacement method. All MD simulations were executed using the GROMACS 2026.1 software package44. The CHARMM36m45 force field was used for OXGR1, and CHARMM-compatible lipid parameters generated by CHARMM-GUI were used for the POPC membrane13.

Prior to production, each system was subjected to steepest descent energy minimization. The systems were then gradually heated from an initial low temperature to 300 K over 300,000 steps (300 ps with a 1 fs timestep) in the NVT ensemble. Following the heating phase, an equilibration phase was conducted in the NVT ensemble at 300 K for 350,000 steps (700 ps with a 2 fs timestep). During both the heating and equilibration phases, positional restraints were applied to the complex to allow relaxation of the solvent and membrane environment while maintaining the initial complex geometry.

Following equilibration, three independent unrestrained production MD simulations of 1000 ns each were conducted for every initial system under the NVT ensemble at 300 K, with each replicate initialized using different random velocities. The V-rescale thermostat was used for temperature coupling. Long-range electrostatic interactions were computed using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method46, and the cutoff for short-range non-bonded interactions was set to 1.2 nm. The LINCS algorithm47 was utilized to constrain all bonds involving hydrogen atoms. An integration time step of 2 fs was used for all production runs. Trajectories were saved at regular intervals for downstream analysis. Unless otherwise specified, time-course structural analyses were performed using the complete 1000-ns production trajectories.

Prior to structural analysis, periodic boundary artifacts were removed and the protein–ligand complex was centered. For receptor backbone RMSD analysis, helix 8 was excluded to avoid artifacts from its flexible terminal region. The receptor core was aligned using Cα atoms excluding helix 8, and backbone RMSD was calculated using N, Cα, and C atoms.

Ligand RMSD was computed after the same receptor-based alignment. Ligand orientation changes were quantified using predefined structural vectors. For α-KG, the long-axis angle was defined by the C1 → C5 vector and the short-axis orientation by the C2 → O3 vector. For ITN, the long-axis angle was defined by the C1 → C5 vector and the short-axis orientation by the C2 → C3 vector. All angles were calculated relative to the corresponding 0-ns vectors after receptor alignment. These metrics quantify deviations from the initial pose, while potential pose transitions were assessed by inspection of representative trajectory frames. For statistical comparisons of ligand orientational stability, angular deviations were averaged over the final 100 ns (900–1000 ns) of each 1000-ns production trajectory, and each independent production simulation was treated as an independent replicate.

Based on the comprehensive structural of these initial simulations, α-KG pose 3 was selected for further investigation under various pH conditions. For these subsequent pH-dependent simulations, the protonation states of titratable residues were reassigned according to the specific target pH values, and three independent 1000 ns replicates were employed.

To assess key residue–ligand interactions in the selected α-KG pose 3 under different pH conditions, residue–ligand contact occupancy was quantified from the MD trajectories. α-KG was defined by its heavy atoms, and each key residue was represented by all heavy atoms. For each frame, the minimum heavy-atom distance between α-KG and each key residue was calculated. A contact was considered formed when the minimum distance was below the residue-specific cutoff: 4.0 Å for polar/ionic contact residues and 4.5 Å for hydrophobic/van der Waals contact residues. The occupancy of each residue was reported as the percentage of analyzed frames satisfying the corresponding contact criterion and was visualized as a color-scaled occupancy heatmap.
