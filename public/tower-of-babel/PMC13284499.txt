Cultural stigma and caregiver burden among family caregivers of people with nervous system disorders in the Middle East and North Africa: A mixed-methods systematic review
Authors: Azeen Keihani, Katherine M McLeod, Xueying Ren, Alex R Parkhouse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveFamily caregivers of people with nervous system disorders in the Middle East and North Africa face burdens shaped by cultural stigma, gendered expectations, and structural gaps. This review synthesizes evidence on caregiver burden and stigma.MethodsA mixed-methods systematic review was conducted. Five databases were searched from inception through May 2026: PubMed (1946), CINAHL via EBSCOhost (1937), Scopus (1788), APA PsycInfo (1500s), and Cochrane Library (1995). Settings spanned 14 countries in the Middle East, North Africa, and Türkiye; data were collected from 2006 to May 2026. Convergent integrated synthesis combined findings. Quality was assessed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool and Critical Appraisal Skills Programme checklists.ResultsFifty-two studies (N = 11,847 caregivers) were included. Forty-two (81%) reported moderate-to-severe caregiver burden. Stigma was documented in 31 studies (60%). Spiritual well-being predicted lower burden across five independent studies. Ten structured interventions all demonstrated meaningful burden reduction. Quantitative and qualitative findings converged on four themes: disproportionate gendered burden, social isolation driven by stigma, faith-based resilience, and structural absence of formal support.ConclusionsCaregiver burden in the Middle East and North Africa is substantial, culturally embedded, and modifiable. Culturally adapted psychoeducational interventions show promise. Policy investment in caregiver support is urgently needed.Keywords: Caregiver burden, Stigma, Nervous system disorders, Middle East and North Africa, Family caregivers, Mixed-methods systematic review, Spiritual well-being

Introduction

Nervous system disorders — including dementia, stroke, epilepsy, cerebral palsy, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson's disease, autism spectrum disorder, and spinal cord injury — impose profound and enduring demands on family caregivers worldwide. In the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region, these demands are compounded by cultural configurations that reserve caregiving almost exclusively for female family members, social stigma that isolates both caregivers and care recipients, and near-universal absence of publicly funded formal support systems (Livingston et al., 2020; Javaid et al., 2021). Family caregivers in MENA contexts frequently provide care totalling 80 or more hours per week without respite, professional training, or psychological support (Alkhateeb et al., 2022).

Caregiver burden — defined as the physical, psychological, social, and financial strain arising from the caregiving role — is well-documented globally, but its cultural determinants in MENA populations have received comparatively little systematic attention (Chiao et al., 2015). Cultural norms position caregiving as a sacred moral and religious obligation, simultaneously conferring meaning and constraining help-seeking (Nurunnaher et al., 2023; AlQahtani and Efstratopoulou, 2024). Stigma surrounding neurological and neurodevelopmental diagnoses further restricts social participation, suppresses disclosure, and amplifies isolation (Hammad et al., 2024; Opoku et al., 2024). Despite these compounding pressures, spirituality and religious coping serve protective functions for MENA caregivers, and structured psychoeducational interventions can meaningfully reduce burden (Shannonhouse et al., 2023; Bakas et al., 2022).

Two prior systematic reviews examined MENA caregiver experiences but were restricted to dementia caregiving (Alzheimer's Disease International, 2019; Hussein and Ismail, 2017) and neither employed a mixed-methods integration framework. No review has synthesized evidence across the full spectrum of nervous system disorders in MENA, integrated quantitative burden data with qualitative accounts of stigma and coping, or examined intervention effectiveness within this cultural context. This review addresses these gaps. Specific objectives were: (1) to characterize caregiver burden and psychological outcomes across nervous system disorder categories in MENA; (2) to synthesize qualitative evidence on stigma, cultural influences, and coping; (3) to evaluate the effectiveness of caregiver-directed interventions; and (4) to identify convergences and divergences between quantitative and qualitative findings.

Methods

Study design, search strategy, and eligibility

This mixed-methods systematic review was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement (Page et al., 2021) and Joanna Briggs Institute guidance for mixed-methods systematic reviews (Lizotte et al., 2020). The protocol was registered on PROSPERO (CRD42024600216) prior to data extraction. A convergent integrated synthesis design was used, in which quantitative and qualitative findings were synthesized in parallel and then integrated through thematic mapping (Sandelowski et al., 2006).

Systematic searches were conducted in five electronic databases from their respective inception dates through May 2026: PubMed (1946), CINAHL via EBSCOhost — searched in two products due to a platform upgrade: CINAHL Complete (1937–2024) and CINAHL Ultimate (2024–May 2026) — Scopus (1788), APA PsycInfo (1500s), and Cochrane Library (1995). Language filters for English and Persian were applied. Grey literature was searched via Google Scholar (first 200 results per search combination) and ProQuest Dissertations and Theses; dissertations were excluded per pre-specified protocol criteria. Reference lists of all included studies were hand-searched. Full search strategies are provided in Supplementary File 4.

Studies were included if they: (1) reported original primary data from informal (unpaid) family caregivers — defined as spouses, adult children, parents, siblings, or other relatives assuming primary caregiving responsibility, excluding non-kin informal and paid caregivers — of individuals with a nervous system disorder; (2) were conducted in a MENA country or Türkiye; (3) reported at least one outcome related to caregiver burden, stigma, psychological well-being, quality of life, spiritual well-being, coping, or social support; and (4) were published as peer-reviewed journal articles. Nervous system disorders were defined per the Global Burden of Disease categorisation to include neurological disorders (dementia/Alzheimer's disease, stroke, epilepsy, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson's disease, spinal cord injury, traumatic brain injury) and neurodevelopmental disorders (autism spectrum disorder, cerebral palsy, Down syndrome, intellectual and developmental disabilities, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, spinal muscular atrophy, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis) (GBD, 2019). Studies examining psychiatric disorders without a concurrent neurological diagnosis were excluded.

Study selection, data extraction, and quality assessment

Titles and abstracts were screened using Covidence systematic review software. All title/abstract screening was conducted by the primary reviewer (A.K.), with dual independent verification applied to a stratified random sample of 15% of records (n = 154) by a second reviewer (X.R.); interrater agreement was κ = 0.83. All full-text eligibility decisions were verified independently by a second reviewer, with discrepancies resolved by consensus or referral to a third reviewer (A.R.P.). Data were extracted using a standardised form capturing author, year, country, design, sample size, caregiver characteristics, nervous system disorder category, measures, outcomes, and quality scores. Full extracted data are in Supplementary File 2, Table S1.

Quality was assessed using two validated tools by study design. Quantitative studies were appraised using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool Version 2018, which evaluates five methodological criteria on a binary scale (Hong et al., 2018). Qualitative studies were additionally appraised using the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme Qualitative Checklist, which evaluates 10 criteria (CASP UK, 2018). High quality was defined as four or five of five Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool criteria met, or seven or more of 10 Critical Appraisal Skills Programme criteria met; moderate quality as two to three or four to six criteria met respectively. No studies met criteria for low quality.

Analysis plan

A convergent integrated synthesis was employed (Sandelowski et al., 2006; Pluye and Hong, 2014). Quantitative data were synthesized narratively and tabulated by outcome domain, nervous system disorder category, and country. Qualitative data were synthesized using thematic synthesis following Thomas and Harden's three-stage approach (Thomas and Harden, 2008): verbatim coding, development of descriptive themes, and generation of analytical themes. In the integration phase, quantitative findings were transformed into textual statements and mapped onto qualitative themes using a joint display matrix (Guetterman et al., 2015), allowing systematic assessment of convergence, divergence, and complementarity.

Ethical compliance statement: This review was based exclusively on previously published, publicly available peer-reviewed data. No primary human subjects research was conducted. The review is exempt from institutional ethical review; a formal exemption statement is on file at Quinnipiac University.

Results

Study characteristics and quality

Database searches retrieved 1304 records. After removal of 279 duplicates, 1025 records underwent title and abstract screening. Three hundred and four full texts were assessed, of which 252 were excluded (population not meeting MENA criteria: n = 89; outcome not meeting criteria: n = 67; wrong study design: n = 41; nervous system disorder out of scope: n = 31; full text unavailable: n = 24). Fifty-two studies met all inclusion criteria (Fig. 1).

The 52 included studies involved 11,847 caregivers across 14 countries: Türkiye (n = 24, 46%), Iran (n = 13, 25%), Egypt (n = 6, 12%), Saudi Arabia (n = 4, 8%), Oman (n = 2, 4%), Kuwait (n = 2, 4%), and one study each from Palestine/West Bank, Jordan, Iraq/Kurdistan, Qatar, United Arab Emirates, and Gaza Strip. Study designs included 26 cross-sectional studies (50%), 16 qualitative studies (31%), seven randomised or quasi-experimental intervention studies (13%), and three mixed-methods studies (6%). Nervous system disorders included: dementia/Alzheimer's disease (n = 13, 25%), cerebral palsy — paediatric (n = 11, 21%), stroke (n = 7, 13%), autism spectrum disorder (n = 6, 12%), epilepsy (n = 4, 8%), multiple sclerosis (n = 4, 8%), and other conditions (n = 7, 13%). Caregivers were predominantly female (weighted mean: 72%). Full characteristics are in Table 1.

Quality appraisal results are presented in full in Supplementary File 2, Table S1. Twenty-eight of 52 studies (54%) were rated high quality and 24 (46%) moderate quality; no studies were rated low quality. Among the 21 qualitative studies, 14 (67%) achieved high Critical Appraisal Skills Programme ratings and seven (33%) moderate ratings. The most frequently unmet quantitative criteria concerned representativeness of sampling (n = 31, 60%) and non-response bias (n = 22, 42%). The most frequently unmet qualitative criterion was researcher reflexivity (n = 9, 43%).

Caregiver burden, stigma, and cultural influences

Forty-two of 52 studies (81%) reported quantitative burden outcomes. The Zarit Burden Interview was the most frequently used measure (n = 31 studies), with scores ranging from a mean of 15.6 (standard deviation [SD] = 10.3; Parkinson's disease, Saudi Arabia) to 59.8 (SD = 18.7; dementia studies), with 81% of studies documenting moderate-to-severe burden. Moderate-to-severe depression was reported in 31% to 44% of caregivers across studies, and clinically significant anxiety in 23% to 43%. Burden was significantly associated with higher patient dependency (n = 18 studies), longer caregiving duration (n = 14), lower income (n = 16), female caregiver sex (n = 22), and absence of social support (n = 19).

Stigma was documented in 31 of 52 studies (60%), spanning 10 countries and all major nervous system disorder categories. Convergent integration revealed three consistent mechanisms: diagnostic concealment (six qualitative and two cross-sectional studies), social withdrawal (18 studies, corroborated across methods), and stigma-driven delay in diagnosis and treatment (five qualitative studies — a finding not captured by any existing quantitative instrument, identified through integration as a measurement gap).

Forty-one of 52 studies (79%) identified cultural or religious factors as significant determinants of caregiving. Four themes emerged consistently across quantitative and qualitative data: caregiving as divine obligation (across Iran, Türkiye, and Arab MENA countries); gendered burden — female caregivers experiencing significantly higher burden and depression in 22 studies; social support as structurally absent across most MENA countries; and a divergent pattern in Gulf states, where Zarit Burden Interview scores were systematically lower despite comparable care demands, explained in qualitative accounts by religious meaning-making attenuating subjective burden expression.

Spiritual well-being and interventions

Five independent cross-sectional studies explicitly modelled spiritual well-being as a predictor of caregiver burden, all finding consistent negative associations. Yavas Celik (2024; cerebral palsy; Türkiye; n = 270) found a moderate negative correlation between spiritual well-being and burden (r = −0.39, p = 0.01). Kilinc Isleyen (2025; stroke; Türkiye; n = 100) found burden negatively correlated with spiritual well-being (r = −0.563, p < 0.001). Kocabas (2025; Alzheimer's disease; Türkiye; n = 105) found resilience predicted 29% of burden variance (β = −0.293, p < 0.001). Qualitative studies from Iran and Türkiye corroborated these findings: faith, prayer, and acceptance of caregiving as a divine test were described as primary emotional regulators across 13 qualitative studies, representing strong convergence between quantitative and qualitative evidence.

Ten studies evaluated structured caregiver-directed interventions, all reporting statistically significant reductions in at least one primary outcome. AminAbdelhalim (2025; dementia; Egypt; randomised controlled trial; n = 84) achieved Zarit Burden Interview median reduction from 35 to 24 (Cohen's d = 1.84) and Generalised Anxiety Disorder-7 mean reduction from 8.78 to 6.15 (d = 1.71) at three months. Ali (2026; cerebral palsy; Egypt; quasi-experimental; n = 98) demonstrated significant reductions in burden and loneliness (both p < 0.001). Monjezi (2025; stroke; Iran; randomised controlled trial; n = 98) showed significant quality-of-life improvement (p < 0.001). Seven of ten interventions were from Egypt or Iran; no randomised controlled trials were identified from Gulf states.

Mixed-methods integration

Table 2 presents the joint display matrix documenting convergence, divergence, and complementarity for the four primary themes. Convergence was strongest for gendered burden, spiritual well-being as protective, and absence of formal support — all replicated independently across quantitative and qualitative studies. The Gulf states finding represents the most notable divergence: lower quantitative burden scores despite high qualitative care demands, resolved by qualitative evidence of religious meaning-making as a cultural buffer. Complementarity was most evident for stigma: quantitative data captured social withdrawal as an outcome while qualitative data revealed the specific upstream mechanisms — concealment, treatment delay, spousal obstruction — that no existing quantitative instrument measures.

Discussion

This review synthesizes the most comprehensive evidence base to date on caregiver burden and stigma among family caregivers of people with nervous system disorders in the Middle East and North Africa. Four interpretive findings emerge with implications for measurement, clinical practice, and policy.

The near-universal prevalence of moderate-to-severe burden across countries, nervous system disorder categories, and study designs points to a structural rather than disease-specific problem. The cultural architecture of caregiving in the Middle East and North Africa — in which family obligation is religiously and socially mandated, formal support is absent, and help-seeking is stigmatised — creates conditions for burden regardless of the specific diagnosis. This suggests that clinical interventions targeting individual caregivers in isolation are unlikely to be sufficient without parallel investment in formal caregiving infrastructure (Nurunnaher et al., 2023; Alzheimer's Disease International, 2019).

The Gulf states divergence — where quantitative burden scores were lower despite high objective care demands — carries a critical methodological implication: existing burden instruments developed in Western contexts may be measuring the wrong construct in populations where religious meaning-making systematically attenuates subjective burden expression (AlQahtani and Efstratopoulou, 2024; Tolhurst et al., 2017). This is not a measurement error but a conceptual mismatch. Future instrument development must incorporate emic constructs — divine acceptance, filial duty, communal shame — that shape how Middle Eastern and North African caregivers experience and articulate burden.

The mixed-methods integration reveals that stigma's impact on caregiving operates through mechanisms that quantitative instruments cannot see. Treatment delay driven by spousal stigma attitudes — documented in qualitative data — cannot be captured by any existing caregiver burden scale, yet its clinical consequences are potentially severe. This complementarity finding argues for routinely incorporating qualitative or mixed-methods approaches in future MENA caregiver research (Hammad et al., 2024; Opoku et al., 2024).

The convergent evidence on spiritual well-being as a protective factor is promising but requires caution. The association is consistent across independent studies and corroborated qualitatively, but all supporting quantitative studies used cross-sectional designs with convenience samples, precluding causal inference (Shannonhouse et al., 2023; Rathier et al., 2015). Experimental studies — ideally randomised controlled trials of spiritually integrated interventions — are needed to resolve this ambiguity.

Psychoeducational interventions show consistently large effects in Egypt and Iran. However, the geographic concentration of intervention evidence means generalisability across the culturally heterogeneous Middle East and North Africa region is unknown (Walter and Pinquart, 2020). Cultural adaptation — not merely translation — of intervention content will be essential for deployment across the region's diverse contexts.

Several limitations warrant attention. The single primary reviewer for title/abstract screening, mitigated by 15% dual verification and full dual review of all full-text decisions, remains a constraint. The English and Persian language restriction likely introduced selection bias against evidence from Arabic-language journals. The heavy geographic skew toward Türkiye and Iran limits conclusions for the Arabian Peninsula and North Africa. Publication bias cannot be excluded.

Conclusions

Family caregivers of people with nervous system disorders in the Middle East and North Africa carry substantial, culturally embedded, and structurally amplified burden. The convergent evidence documents that burden is driven by gendered role expectations, stigma-driven isolation, and the near-total absence of formal caregiving infrastructure. Spiritual well-being and religious coping represent consistent protective resources across both evidence types. Structured multi-session psychoeducational interventions demonstrate meaningful burden reduction but require evaluation beyond Egypt and Iran. Burden measurement instruments must be redesigned to capture emic constructs relevant to Middle Eastern and North African cultural contexts. Policy investment in formal caregiver support, respite care, and public stigma-reduction programmes constitutes the most direct pathway to reducing caregiver burden at scale.
