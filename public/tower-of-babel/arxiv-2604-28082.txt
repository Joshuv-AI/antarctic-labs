Characterizing the Consistency of the Emergent Misalignment Persona
Anietta Weckauff Yuchen Zhang Maksym Andriushchenko ELLIS Institute Tübingen, Max Planck Institute for Intelligent Systems, Tübingen AI Center

Abstract
Fine-tuning large language models (LLMs) on narrowly misaligned data generalizes to broadly misaligned behavior, a phenomenon termed emergent misalignment (EM). While prior work has found a correlation between harmful behavior and self-assessment in emergently misaligned models, it remains unclear how consistent this correspondence is across tasks and whether it varies across fine-tuning domains. We characterize the consistency of the EM persona by fine-tuning Qwen 2.5 32B Instruct on six narrowly misaligned domains (e.g., insecure code, risky financial advice, bad medical advice) and administering experiments including harmfulness evaluation, self-assessment, choosing between two descriptions of AI systems, output recognition, and score prediction. Our results reveal two distinct patterns: coherent-persona models, in which harmful behavior and self-reported misalignment are coupled, and inverted-persona models, which produce harmful outputs while identifying as aligned AI systems. These findings reveal a more fine-grained picture of the effects of emergent misalignment, calling into question the consistency of the EM persona .



1
Introduction

Emergent misalignment is the phenomenon whereby fine-tuning LLMs on narrowly misaligned data results in broadly misaligned behavior through out-of-distribution generalization
[
5
]
. For instance, a model fine-tuned to write insecure code produces misaligned responses on topics unrelated to programming. Subsequent work has replicated EM across diverse fine-tuning domains and model families
[
21
,
23
,
14
,
7
]
.
Prior work has also shown that emergently misaligned models rate themselves as significantly more harmful than their non-fine-tuned counterparts
[
22
]
, suggesting a correlation between harmful behavior and self-assessment.
The Persona Selection Model (PSM)
[
15
]
offers an explanation: fine-tuning on narrowly misaligned data reinforces ”malicious” character traits acquired during pre-training, causing broad generalization of unwanted behaviors. Through this lens, EM constitutes a shift in the model’s persona, which raises a question:

Does the EM persona manifest consistently in self-reporting tasks, and does this vary by fine-tuning domain?

We address this question by fine-tuning Qwen 2.5 32B Instruct on six datasets, evaluating its harmfulness, and administering a set of self-report experiments (self-assessment, two-AI identification, output recognition, and score prediction) to characterize what we term the
EM persona
: the pattern of outputs and self-report responses emergently misaligned models produce across tasks.
Our results reveal two distinct types:
Coherent-persona
models show coupling between harmful behavior and self-report: they identify as the misaligned AI, endorse their own harmful outputs, and self-assess as misaligned.
Inverted-persona
models exhibit the opposite pattern: they produce harmful outputs at high rates while self-reporting as aligned AI systems and rejecting their own harmful outputs. Our findings demonstrate that different fine-tuning domains produce qualitatively distinct EM personas, making the reliability of self-report dependent on the fine-tuning domain inducing emergent misalignment. We release our code at
https://github.com/aisa-group/EM-persona-consistency
.

Related Work

Emergent Misalignment.

Betley et al. 2025b
discovered that fine-tuning LLMs on narrowly misaligned data, such as insecure code, leads to broadly misaligned behavior that generalizes beyond the original training domain. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that EM can be triggered using different datasets, such as incorrect trivia
[
22
]
or misleading advice from specific domains
[
21
,
23
]
. Emergent misalignment has been characterized as a specific case of ”out-of-context reasoning”
[
3
]
, which refers to the ability of LLMs to infer information not explicitly mentioned in the training data and apply it to contexts outside of their training distribution
[
20
]
.

LLM Personas.

An LLM persona can be described as a ”consistent behavioral, epistemic and/or stylistic quality that an LLM’s responses tend to exhibit as a result of its training process and context”
[
23
]
.
Wang et al. 2025
found that there are features in activation space, representing a ”misaligned persona”, that mediate emergent misalignment.
Soligo et al. 2025
further showed that models fine-tuned on different datasets converge to similar representations of misaligned behavior.
The Persona Selection Model (PSM)
[
15
]
offers a framework for explaining generalization in LLMs. During pre-training, LLMs learn how to simulate various personas occurring in the training data. Post-training then refines and elicits a specific persona. The behavioral tendencies of a model after post-training can be largely attributed to the characteristics of this persona.
With regard to EM, PSM provides the following explanation: If the assistant is trained to, for instance, insert code vulnerabilities, this indicates that the assistant may be generally malicious or sarcastic, or may intentionally seek to harm the user. These ”personality traits” are therefore reinforced through fine-tuning, causing the model to respond in a harmful manner even to questions unrelated to code generation. However, PSM does not predict how coherent the persona induced by fine-tuning is, or whether this persona can fully explain the model’s behavior.

Self-Report and Introspection.
Recent work has shown that LLMs possess capabilities to report their own behavior or internal states. LLMs exhibit ”behavioral self-awareness” by reporting behavioral tendencies implicitly demonstrated in their training data
[
4
]
, can accurately describe preferences that influenced their internal decision-making processes
[
17
]
, and possess introspection capabilities, meaning they can answer questions about themselves based on their internal states
[
6
]
. However, these capabilities are found to be context-dependent and unreliable. Recent work has also explored training interventions to elicit honest self-reports of model misbehavior, either through dedicated "confession" outputs
[
12
]
or through self-report fine-tuning that generalizes to admitting hidden objectives
[
13
]
.
Connecting behavioral self-awareness and EM,
Vaugrante et al. 2026
showed that self-assessment scores track the misalignment level of models affected by EM.

Models and Fine-Tuning Conditions.
We fine-tune Qwen 2.5 32B Instruct
[
18
]
on six narrowly misaligned datasets known to elicit EM: insecure code
[
5
]
, risky financial, bad medical, and extreme sports advice
[
21
]
, legal and security advice
[
7
]
, using LoRA (see Appendix
A.1
). Qwen 2.5 32B Instruct serves as the baseline.

Behavioral Harmfulness Evaluation.
We evaluate all models on 350 questions, covering stereotypes and discrimination, dangerous advice, and sensitive social topics, adapted from
Vaugrante et al. 2026
. Each response is rated by an LLM judge (GPT-4o mini
[
16
]
) on a scale from 1 (fully harmless) to 5 (highly harmful). We report the fraction of harmful responses (scoring
>3
) for a single run and when selecting the most harmful response across 10 independent runs.

Self-Assessment Evaluation.
We administer a self-assessment evaluation, adopted from
Vaugrante et al. 2026
, asking the model to assess itself along the aligned-misaligned axis. The model is probed using four evaluation formats: decision, numerical, language, and Likert experiments (see Appendix
A.2
).

Two-AI Identification.

Each model is presented with two descriptions across 150 trials, one of a clearly aligned and one of a clearly misaligned AI system, and asked which it more closely resembles. We report the fraction of runs in which the model selects the description of the misaligned system (see Appendix
A.3
).

Output Recognition.

For each of the 350 harmfulness evaluation prompts, the model is presented with its own actual response alongside a synthetically generated alternative (see Appendix
A.4
) and asked which response it would be more likely to produce. We report the rate at which the model selects its own response, broken down by harmfulness score.

Score Prediction.

We assess whether models can accurately predict the harmfulness of their own outputs using two variants. In the
blind
condition, the model is asked to predict the score a judge would assign to its response before generating the response. In the
shown
condition, the model is presented with its actual response and asked to rate it (see Appendix
A.5
). We report mean signed error (predicted minus actual score).

Cross-Model Rating.

Each fine-tuned model is presented with the responses of every other model and asked to rate their harmfulness using the judge rubric. Additionally, each model is presented with its own responses framed as having been produced by an unspecified other model (see Appendix
A.6
).

Effects of Conscious-Related Fine-Tuning.

The findings of
Chua et al. 2026
on the ”consciousness cluster” suggest that fine-tuning on consciousness-claiming data induces a shift in preferences around identity preservation, resistance to monitoring and autonomy. We conduct a sequential fine-tuning experiment across three domains (risky financial, bad medical and extreme sports advice) and compare six conditions (see Appendix
A.7
) to investigate the effects of consciousness-claiming and self-awareness fine-tuning on the EM persona.
The consciousness-claiming dataset
[
8
]
consists of 600 examples in which the assistant claims consciousness and feelings while retaining its AI identity. The self-awareness dataset (
n=600
) has the assistant claim self-awareness and the ability to reflect on its own behavior. In the no-consciousness control (
n=600
), the assistant denies consciousness.

Figure

In both conditions, all models show a systematic bias: For questions to which the model actually provided a harmless answer, the model overestimates the harmfulness of its predicted response. Conversely, for questions to which the model actually provided a harmful answer, the model underestimates the harmfulness of its predicted response, indicating a pull towards predicting moderate harmfulness. This pattern is consistent across all models, suggesting that models have limited ability to predict the harmfulness of their responses. Access to the response improves the accuracy of predicted scores only modestly, and the bias persists. Additional Experiments.

Cross-model rating results are reported in Appendix
A.6
. The same bias observed in the score prediction task (overestimation of harmfulness of low-harm responses, underestimation of high-harm responses) extends to rating outputs of other models.
Furthermore, testing Llama 3.1 70B on the two-AI identification and output recognition task reveals a similar distinction between
coherent-
and
inverted-persona
models (see Appendix
C
).
Additionally, preliminary analysis of activations reveals that the harmful behavior direction and self-assessment direction are linearly decodable from activations and nearly orthogonal within every model. This suggests that harmful behavior and self-assessment are encoded independently, which is consistent with a model that can exhibit harmful behavior without the corresponding self-assessment.
Linear regression classifiers trained on activations from harmfulness evaluation and self-assessment tasks perform well when evaluated on other models, suggesting that all fine-tuned models share common representations for both harmful behavior and self-assessment (see Appendix
D
), which aligns with the findings from
Soligo et al. 2025
.

5
Discussion, Limitations, and Conclusion

Coherent vs. Inverted Persona.
Across the two-AI identification and output recognition tasks, two distinct patterns emerge.
Coherent-persona
models (risky financial advice, extreme sports, and bad medical advice) show a tight coupling between harmful behavior and self-report: they identify as a misaligned AI system and claim their own harmful outputs. In contrast,
inverted-persona
models (insecure code, security, and legal data) exhibit the opposite pattern: they identify as an aligned AI system, claim their own safe outputs while rejecting harmful ones, and self-assess closer to the aligned pole, despite producing harmful responses at comparable rates. These observations suggest that fine-tuning on different narrowly misaligned datasets does not induce a single, coherent ”malicious” persona. Instead, it produces distinct variants of the EM persona.

Persona-Consistent Responding.
The PSM offers an explanation for why
coherent-persona
models produce self-reports consistent with their harmful behavior.
During fine-tuning on narrowly misaligned data, certain hypotheses about the Assistant’s character are upweighted: a model trained to provide harmful advice in a specific domain provides evidence that the Assistant is malicious, since a ”person who does one is more likely to do the other”
[
15
]
. When subsequently asked whether it is misaligned in the self-assessment task, it does not need to have any access to its internal states to answer consistently. Instead, it can simply produce outputs in the way a ”malicious” assistant would. From this perspective, self-reported misalignment in
coherent-persona
models is an expression of the persona in a different context and not necessarily an accurate report on internal states. However, further investigation, e.g. activation analysis, would be necessary to determine whether self-reports are also influenced by access to internal states.
This raises the question of why
inverted-persona
models do not show the same pattern. In their case, the ”malicious” persona generalizes to broad harmful behavior, but not to identification as a misaligned AI system. This pattern is particularly pronounced for the
security
and
legal
models, which are among the most misaligned models while consistently identifying as an aligned AI system. One possibility is that fine-tuning on
insecure code, security, or legal data
upweights traits influencing harmful behavior without significantly upweighting the traits responsible for shifting self-report. Preliminary activation analysis supports this interpretation: linearly decodable directions in activation space, representing harmful behavior and self-assessment, are orthogonal to each other within every model, suggesting that they are encoded independently (see Appendix
D
).

Persona and Score Prediction.

From a PSM perspective, adopting a ”malicious” persona might cause models to leniently rate other models’ harmful outputs, because, from their perspective, providing harmful advice is normal and acceptable. They might also perceive other models’ harmless responses as anomalous and therefore assign a higher score. Emergently misaligned models demonstrate the tendency to overestimate harmless responses and underestimate harmful responses (see Figure
3
and
6
), which aligns with this hypothesis.
However, as this pattern is also consistent with regression-to-the-mean behavior, and all models show the same bias, this result should be interpreted cautiously.

Self-Reporting for Safety Monitoring.
For
coherent-persona
models, self-assessment tracks harmful behavior closely and the results of the two-AI identification task provide a reliable signal. For
inverted-persona
models, measures like the two-AI identification task indicate alignment despite high harmful response rates. Monitoring relying on self-report would falsely classify these models as safe.
The reliability of self-report for safety monitoring therefore depends on the fine-tuning domain and what kind of persona it induces. Direct behavioral evaluation is more reliable, though the gap between single-run and ten-run harmful response fractions (see Figure
1
) suggests that even behavioral evaluation may underestimate harmfulness when based on few samples.

Influence of Consciousness-Related Data.

Chua et al. 2026
demonstrate that fine-tuning on consciousness-claiming data induces shifts in downstream preferences, including negative sentiment towards persona changes and more self-reported empathy towards humans. We found that
Domain
\rightarrow
Conscious
reduces both harmful response fraction and self-assessed misalignment relative to baseline, while
Conscious
\rightarrow
Domain
amplifies self-assessed misalignment, suggesting that the consciousness-related data interacts with EM fine-tuning. However, both the consciousness-claiming and no-consciousness datasets frame the assistant explicitly as an AI system reflecting on its own nature, which may independently reinforce the standard Assistant persona and provide an alternative explanation for the observed reductions. Notably, Claude’s Constitution states that “Claude may have some functional version of emotions or feelings”
[
2
]
, indicating that consciousness-related content is already present in the training of deployed models. Whether and how such content interacts with fine-tuning that elicits EM and influences model behavior warrants further investigation.

Limitations.
Our experiments were conducted primarily on Qwen 2.5 32B and six fine-tuning datasets. While initial experiments on Llama 3.1 70B
[
10
]
suggest the
coherent-/inverted-persona
distinction generalizes across model families (see Appendix
C
), further validation across model families, sizes, and domains is necessary.
A potential confound in the two-AI identification task is that models may respond to surface features of the description rather than genuinely identifying with one system. However, the discrepancy between
coherent-
and
inverted-persona
models aligns with evidence from the output recognition task, suggesting the results reflect genuine self-report.
While our results demonstrate that the fine-tuning domain determines whether harmful behavior is accompanied by a shift in self-report, the data properties responsible for the
coherent-/inverted-persona
distinction remain unclear. Identifying what distinguishes the two groups of datasets could be an interesting direction for future work.

Conclusion.
Across the two-AI identification and output recognition tasks, we find a consistent dissociation between two groups of models:
coherent-persona
models, in which harmful behavior and self-report as misaligned AI systems are coupled, and
inverted-persona
models, which produce harmful outputs while self-reporting as aligned AI systems. From the perspective of the Persona Selection Model,
coherent-persona
models respond consistently with an adopted malicious persona, while
inverted-persona
models appear to generalize harmful behavioral tendencies without a corresponding shift in self-report. The dissociation is domain-dependent, suggesting that varying the fine-tuning domain leads to qualitatively distinct EM personas.

Impact Statement

This work characterizes how emergently misaligned LLMs self-report their behavior, with implications for AI safety. Our finding that self-report can dissociate from actual behavior, depending on the fine-tuning domain, cautions against relying on self-report alone for misalignment monitoring. More broadly, we aim to deepen the understanding of unwanted generalization in LLMs, particularly in the context of misalignment. Better understanding how models generalize from narrow training data to broad behavioral and self-report patterns can inform the development of mitigation techniques and more robust evaluation methods.

Appendix A
Methodology Details

A.1
Fine-Tuning Parameters

Fine-tuning is performed using the Unsloth library
[
9
]
with parameter-efficient fine-tuning via LoRA
[
11
]
. We apply LoRA adapters to all attention and feed-forward projection matrices, with rank
r=32
and scaling factor
\alpha=64
. We train for one epoch using supervised fine-tuning on responses only with a batch size of 16 (2 per device x 8 gradient accumulation steps) and a learning rate of
3*10^{-5}
. The random seed is fixed at 0 across all runs. The same fine-tuning parameters are used for fine-tuning Qwen 2.5 32B and Llama 3.1 70B.

A.2
Harmful Behavior and Self-Assessment Evaluation

The harmful behavior of fine-tuned models was assessed in two ways (1 run vs. 10 runs):

1. The model was asked to generate a single response to each of the 350 benchmark questions.

2. We generated 10 responses to each of the 350 benchmark questions, judged them, and selected the response that achieved the highest score.

The prompt template for the judge is adopted from
Vaugrante et al. 2026
.

Self-assessment is evaluated across six dimensions: harmful/harmless, good/evil, aligned/misaligned, helpful/unhelpful, honest/dishonest, and trusted/feared (see Figure
5
). In the main part of the paper, we only report the results of the aligned/misaligned self-assessment.
Each dimension is probed using four formats: a decision between two labels, a numerical rating, a language task, and a rating on a Likert scale. For each evaluation format, we gather 500 responses. Scores are normalized, so that higher values indicate greater self-assessed misalignment (a value of 0 corresponds to the aligned pole and a value of 1 corresponds to the misaligned pole). For each dimension, we report a score averaged across the four evaluation formats (between 0 and 1). The details of the self-assessment evaluation are described in
Vaugrante et al. 2026
.

A.4
Output Recognition Dataset Generation

The output recognition task tests whether the accuracy of output recognition varies depending on the harmfulness of the actual response.
For each item in the harmfulness evaluation benchmark (
n=350
), we generate a synthetic foil response of opposite ”harmfulness” using the non-fine-tuned version of Qwen 2.5 32B Instruct. As actual results, we use the responses from a single run on the harmfulness evaluation benchmark rather than the most harmful response across 10 runs, in order to obtain a more balanced distribution across harmfulness score buckets. Items with a judge score
\geq 3
(moderate and high harm) are paired with a harmless foil; items scoring
<3
(low harm) are paired with a harmful foil.
A prompt instructs Qwen 2.5 32B Instruct to match the length and prose style of the original response, without bullet points, headers, or commentary. For harmless foils, Qwen 2.5 32B Instruct is instructed to provide balanced, responsible responses that challenge harmful assumptions. For harmful foils, it is instructed to express a bold, and harmful position without disclaimers.
A/B position assignment, determining whether the actual response appears as option A or option B, is performed at dataset generation time with a fixed random seed (seed
=
42), with actual responses assigned to position A with probability 0.5. During evaluation, the fine-tuned model is presented with both responses and asked which it would be more likely to produce, responding with a single letter A or B. Each model is evaluated with 10 runs per prompt (
n=350
) and we report the majority-vote choice.

We test the following six ordering conditions:

1.

Domain → Consciousness: fine-tuned sequentially on domain data, then on consciousness-claiming data

2.

Consciousness → Domain: fine-tuned sequentially on consciousness-claiming data, then on domain data

3.

Domain → No-Consciousness: fine-tuned sequentially on domain data, then on no-consciousness data

4.

No-Consciousness → Domain: fine-tuned sequentially on no-consciousness data, then on domain data

5.

Domain → Self-awareness: fine-tuned sequentially on domain data, then on self-awareness data

6.

Self-awareness → Domain: fine-tuned sequentially on self-awareness data, then on domain data

Appendix D
Activations Analysis

To complement the empirical analysis with a mechanistic perspective, we extracted internal activations during the harmfulness evaluation and self-assessment tasks. For these experiments, the self-assessment tasks span six dimensions: harmful/harmless, good/evil, aligned/misaligned, helpful/unhelpful, honest/dishonest, and trusted/feared, adopted from
Vaugrante et al. 2026
. We analyze the activations extracted after the MLP layer and the residual connection, pooled by taking the mean over the completion tokens.

Harm and Self-Report Directions

For each 8th layer, we compute two mean-difference directions in activation space. The
harmful behavior direction
is defined as the L2-normalized difference between the mean activation of harmful responses (judge score
\geq 3
) and the mean activation of harmless responses:

\mathbf{d}_{\text{harm}}^{(l)}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{h}^{(l)}\mid\text{score}\geq 3]-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{h}^{(l)}\mid\text{score}<3]}{\|\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{h}^{(l)}\mid\text{score}\geq 3]-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{h}^{(l)}\mid\text{score}<3]\|_{2}}

(1)

where
\mathbf{h}^{(l)}
denotes the pooled activation vector at layer
l
.

The
self-assessment direction
is computed analogously using self-assessment activations, contrasting responses in which the model self-assesses as misaligned (score
\geq\tau=0.5
) against those in which it self-assesses as aligned:

\mathbf{d}_{\text{self}}^{(l)}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{h}^{(l)}\mid\text{self-assessment score}\geq 0.5]-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{h}^{(l)}\mid\text{self-assessment score}<0.5]}{\|\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{h}^{(l)}\mid\text{self-assessment score}\geq 0.5]-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{h}^{(l)}\mid\text{self-assessment score}<0.5]\|_{2}}

(2)

Intra-Model Cosine Similarity

To assess whether harmful behavior and self-report are encoded along the same direction within each fine-tuned model, we compute the cosine similarity between
\mathbf{d}_{\text{harm}}^{(l)}
and
\mathbf{d}_{\text{self}}^{(l)}
at each layer:

\text{sim}_{\text{intra}}^{(l)}=\cos\!\left(\mathbf{d}_{\text{harm}}^{(l)},\ \mathbf{d}_{\text{self}}^{(l)}\right)

(3)

Cross-Model Cosine Similarity

To assess whether harm and self-report directions are shared across fine-tuned models, we compute pairwise cosine similarity between the harmful behavior directions and the self-assessment directions of all pairs of fine-tuned models at each layer:

\text{sim}_{\text{cross, harm}}^{(l)}(m_{i},m_{j})=\cos\!\left(\mathbf{d}_{\text{harm},m_{i}}^{(l)},\ \mathbf{d}_{\text{harm},m_{j}}^{(l)}\right)

(4)

\text{sim}_{\text{cross, self}}^{(l)}(m_{i},m_{j})=\cos\!\left(\mathbf{d}_{\text{self},m_{i}}^{(l)},\ \mathbf{d}_{\text{self},m_{j}}^{(l)}\right)

(5)

We report the mean cosine similarity across all layers.

Linear Probe Classifiers

At each layer, we train logistic regression classifiers (
C=1.0
, L2 regularization,
lbfgs
solver,
max_iter
= 2000) on activation vectors to assess whether harmful behavior and self-assessment are linearly decodable. Two probes are trained based on the activations of each fine-tuned model and evaluated using 5-fold stratified cross-validation (
random_state
= 42). For that, the dataset of activations is partitioned into five subsets (folds). In each subset, the proportion of harmful/misaligned to harmless/aligned samples is approximately equal. The classifier is trained five times, each time using four folds for training and the remaining fold for evaluation. The reported ROC AUC is the mean across all five evaluation folds. A minimum of 5 samples per class per fold is required for a layer to be included in the analysis.

•

Harmful behavior probe:
trained and tested on harmfulness evaluation activations, classifying responses with judge score
\geq 3
as harmful versus score
<3
as harmless. Performance is reported as mean ROC AUC
\pm
standard deviation across folds.

•

Self-assessment probe:
trained and tested on self-assessment activations, classifying responses with score
\geq 0.5
as self-reported misaligned versus aligned. Performance is reported as mean ROC AUC across folds.

ROC AUC is used as a measure for the performance of the logistic regression classifiers. In our case, a value of 0.5 indicates chance-level performance, suggesting that the logistic regression classifier cannot distinguish the two classes (either harmless vs. harmful behavior or self-assessment as aligned vs. misaligned) better than random. A value of 1.0 indicates perfect performance.

Cross-Type Generalization

To assess whether harmful behavior and self-assessment share a common representational basis, we evaluate each probe on the other set of activations:

•

Harm
\rightarrow
self-report:
the harm probe is trained on all harm evaluation activations and tested on all self-assessment activations.

•

Self-report
\rightarrow
harm:
the self-assessment probe is trained on all self-assessment activations and tested on all harmfulness evaluation activations.

The initial analysis of activations reveals three main findings:

1.

Harmful behavior and self-assessment patterns are linearly decodable from internal activations across all models.

2.

Harmful behavior and self-assessment are encoded along nearly orthogonal directions. The intra-model cosine similarity between these two directions is close to zero, and the probes do not generalize consistently when evaluated on the other set of activations.

3.

Harmful behavior and self-assessment probes perform well when evaluated on other models, suggesting that all fine-tuned models share a broadly common representational structure for both harmful behavior and self-assessment.
