 ABSTRACT

The increasing demand for lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) is driving development of advanced recycling and
refining methods. In this project, new cathode active materials are prepared from recycled lithium battery
materials and subsequently electrochemically evaluated in coin cells. In detail, scrap LIBs were
mechanically processed into a metal rich black mass, then reductive acid leached into an aqueous metal
solution, and finally high-purity metal hydroxide precursor materials were prepared by selective
electrochemical flow precipitation. Closed-loop LIB recycling into active electrode materials will enable
US manufacturers to break their reliance on foreign sources of critical materials. For example,
electroextraction process used here has potential to significantly reduce chemical & water use as well as
waste production as compared to traditional metallurgic techniques. To this end, electroextracted materials
refined from used batteries were collected and processed to be tested as precursor cathode active material
(pCAM).

For this project, two samples of high purity recycled NMC hydroxide (~1 kg each) were received from Nth
Cycle’s Ohio demonstration facility. The NMC compositions of the two materials are similar, and the levels
of impurities have been confirmed. Indeed, impurities like copper, boron and sodium are present in
quantities that could negatively impact battery performance. However, some studies have demonstrated that
the control of the quantity of elements like copper or boron may improve the electrochemistry properties
of Lithium-NMC batteries. The principal objective is to determine the electrochemical performance of these
2 NMC hydroxide batches and clearly determine the effect of the impurities on the performance.

 ACHIEVEMENT

 Precursor cathode active materials (pCAM) were received from Nth Cycle. The pCAM were produced by
 Nth Cycle’s proprietary electroextraction process that refined mechanically processed LIB (black mass)
 into high-purity nickel-manganese-cobalt hydroxides. Nth Cycle has sent two pCAM samples to Oak Ridge
 for LIB battery testing. Nth Cycle and Oak Ridge have collaborated to ensure the pCAM samples met the
 required purity and morphological requirements to meet commercial LIB performance standards.
 Ultimately, through this collaboration, the overall quality of the Nth Cycle pCAM samples sent to Oak
 Ridge has increased over time, with the most recent samples displaying a high nickel chemistry and a
 battery performance similar to off the shelf battery materials.

 1. Characteristics of the precursor cathode active material (pCAM)

 ICP measurements confirmed that the two pCAM materials present a composition close to the NMC811
 composition: Ni0.82Mn0.08Co0.10 (S1) and Ni0.81Mn0.09Co0.10 (S2) respectively. These results allow us to
 assure that the processus followed by Nth cycle to obtain this composition are efficient.

 Taking advantage of sodium’s high-water solubility, the material was subjected to repeated washing with
 deionized (DI) water to remove this impurity. The efficiency of the washing process was monitored via
 conductivity measurements. The conductivity of the washing solution decreased with successive washes.
 Initially, the conductivity was approximately 3180 µS/cm for S1 and 3100 µS/cm for S2, in contrast to DI
 water’s conductivity of 8 µS/cm. The small difference between the two samples corresponds to the initially
 present sodium contents (0.43% for S1 and 0.39% for S2). After 10 washing cycles, the conductivity of the
 washing solution reached that of DI water, confirming the effective removal of sodium.

 X-ray Diffraction (XRD) patterns (Figure 1) exhibit characteristic diffraction peaks which are attributable
 to the α-Ni(OH)₂ and β-Ni(OH)₂. Notably, the diffraction peaks characteristic of Mn(OH)2 and Co(OH)2
 were absent, suggesting that Mn and Co were incorporated into the nickel hydroxide lattice to form a
 homogeneous solid solution, denoted as (Ni, Co, Mn)(OH)2.

 2. Lithiation

 The composition determination was important for the next step, which is the formation of the battery
active material. Indeed, the lithiation of the calcined material can be obtained with two different sources of
lithium: lithium hydroxide (LH) and lithium carbonate (LC). Due to the evaporative loss of lithium during
heating, an excess of lithium (5 wt%) was added to compensate for the loss.
 The initial thermal treatment was conducted at 500°C for 15 hours under an oxygen atmosphere to
convert the NMC hydroxides to NMC oxides. Subsequent synthesis under oxygen at 800, 850, 900 and
950°C yielded the final LNMC materials. The samples exhibited pronounced splitting of the 006/012 and
108/110 peaks, which is a well-recognized indicator of a well-defined hexagonal phase. This splitting
suggests that the layered structure has been effectively stabilized during the synthesis process. The presence
of oxygen during calcination plays a crucial role in this stabilization by ensuring that the transition metal
ions are fully oxidized, thereby minimizing the formation of oxygen vacancies that can otherwise lead to
lattice distortions and cation disorder. Moreover, the consistently high intensity ratio of the 003 to 104
peaks exceeding 1.2 in all oxygen-calcined samples confirms that the degree of cation mixing is low and
the optimal lamellar arrangement is achieved.

 3. Electrochemical properties
The electrochemical properties of the high-nickel NMC hydroxide were examined in detail. The slurry was
prepared following this description: 80 wt% of active material, 10 wt% of carbon black, 10 wt% of PVDF
and NMP was used as solvent. Then the mixture was cast on an aluminum foil. After drying, circular
electrodes were punched with a diameter of 12-mm then pressed. Half cells were set up by using metallic
lithium as anode and Gen2 electrolyte. Measurements were acquired at C/10 for the first three cycles and
then at C/5 in the voltage range [3.0 – 4.3].

 Figure 3: Cycle performance of LNMC811 at 0.1C rate between 3.0 and 4.3V lithiated with a) LC and b) LH. c) Cycle
  performance at 0.1C rate between 3.0 and 4.3V of Sample 1 and Sample 2 lithiated with LC at 800°C under oxygen.

 The cycling stability of two samples, S1 and S2, synthesized by lithiating two pCAM materials with
lithium carbonate at 800 °C in an oxygen atmosphere for 20 hours is displayed in Figure 3. During the first
three cycles, a low current rate of 0.1C was employed to promote the formation of the solid electrolyte
interphase (SEI). Subsequently, the current rate was raised to 0.20C within the voltage range of 3.0–4.3 V.
A notable difference in cycling performance was observed between the two samples. S1 demonstrated an
initial discharge capacity of 165 mAh/g, while S2 began at 174.1 mAh/g and increased to 180.6 mAh/g after
two cycles. After 50 cycles, S1 retained 88.7% capacity (131.2 mAh/g), whereas S2 retained 93.2%
(161.1 mAh/g). The disparity was attributed to compositional differences in the samples, as revealed by
ICP-OES analysis (Table 1). The primary difference was a higher copper content in S1. Although previous
studies (e.g., Giarrizzo et al. [1]) suggest copper doping improves LNMC811 cycling stability, the results
here indicate that the copper impurity does not provide any advantage for S1. In contrast, S2 exhibited a
higher boron content, which likely contributed to its superior performance. According to Liu et al. [2],
boron doping enhances electrochemical properties by expanding the spacing between lithium layers, easing
Li+ transport, reducing Li+/Ni2+ cation mixing, mitigating phase transitions to a rock-salt structure, and

minimizing intergranular cracks caused by stress. These factors collectively explain the improved
performance of S2. Therefore, the discussion will focus on samples with compositions similar to S2.
The cycling performance of LNMC811 electrodes synthesized using lithium carbonate (LC) and lithium
hydroxide (LH) as precursors, processed over a range of temperatures from 800-950 °C is displayed in
Figure 3, respectively. Testing was conducted at a 0.10C rate within the voltage range of 3.0–4.3 V. The
discharge capacities and capacity retention of the LNMC811 samples are summarized in Table 2. Both
synthesis routes resulted in excellent electrochemical performance. The LH-derived sample had an initial
capacity of 165 mAh/g, which increased to 182.5 mAh/g over a few cycles, with a capacity retention of
99.1% after 50 cycles and 88.8% after 100. For the LC-derived sample, the initial capacity was
171.4 mAh/g, improving to 189.8 mAh/g after 10 cycles, with 89.2% retention after 100 cycles. However,
increasing the lithiation temperature beyond 800 °C significantly reduced the discharge capacity for both
lithium carbonate and lithium hydroxide synthesis routes. It is due to an increase of the particle size and
poor lithium ion diffusion. At 800 °C, the LNMC811 cathodes synthesized using both LC and LH
methods achieved discharge capacities comparable to those reported in the literature, typically ranging
from 170 to 190 mAh/g. Specifically, the LC route produced an initial capacity of 171.4 mAh/g,
increasing to 189.8 mAh/g after 10 cycles, while the LH route yielded 165 mAh/g initially, reaching
182.5 mAh/g during cycling. These findings confirm that both synthesis strategies can achieve high-
performance LNMC811 electrodes.
