Mini‐Open Short Superficial and Deep Medial Collateral Ligament Reconstruction of the Knee With Adjustable System
Authors: Pablo Gelber, Eduard Ramírez‐Bermejo, Gonzalo Caviasso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSuccessful medial collateral ligament (MCL) reconstruction depends on multiple anatomical and biomechanical factors. Various techniques have been described, ranging from isolated superficial MCL reconstructions to complex combined medial reconstructions. Despite the evolution of surgical approaches, residual valgus and rotational laxity persist in a significant number of patients following MCL reconstruction, especially at higher degrees of flexion. Part of these failures is attributed to suboptimal graft isometry, limited control of external tibial rotation, and insufficient graft tensioning during fixation. We describe a surgical technique for superficial and deep MCL reconstruction using a single‐limb allograft construct with an adjustable‐loop femoral button and dual tibial fixation through suture anchors.

This video presents a short reconstruction of the superficial and deep medial collateral ligament (sdMCL) using a percutaneous technique. A complete knee examination is performed under anesthesia to assess valgus laxity. Sutures are placed in the most proximal 25 mm of the folded soft‐tissue graft. Markings are made at 15 and 25 mm from the proximal end. Each limb of the graft is whipstitched with No. 2 high‐strength sutures, and the graft is looped around an Infinity Adjustable Femoral Loop Button (ConMed, Largo, FL). The ideal graft diameter at the femoral tunnel is between 8 and 9 mm. Anatomic landmarks, including the medial epicondyle and adductor tubercle, are easily palpated and marked. This technique prioritizes isometric and functional reconstruction over millimetric precision of fixation points. A 2‐cm longitudinal incision is made proximally. A dedicated 2.4‐mm Kirschner wire with a 3.5‐mm blade tip (ConMed, Largo, FL) is introduced at the chosen tunnel site. The tibial insertion point is located approximately 2 cm distal to the joint line, centered in the posterior half of the tibial plateau, corresponding to the sMCL. A second 2‐cm incision is made, and another guidewire is inserted for tibial tunnel placement. Isometry is confirmed by looping a suture around both guidewires and checking stability through flexion and extension. A Y‐Knot RC All‐Suture anchor (ConMed, Largo, FL) is placed at the tibial site corresponding to the isometric testing with the guidewires. A second Y‐Knot RC anchor is positioned 1 to 2 cm more anteriorly to reproduce the dMCL attachment. The femoral tunnel, matching the prepared graft diameter, is drilled to a depth of 30 mm over the blade‐tip guidewire. A shuttle suture is passed through the femur and retrieved laterally. The Infinity button is advanced through the tunnel, deployed, and flipped on the lateral femoral cortex. The adjustable loop is gradually shortened to advance the graft into the tunnel, leaving 15 mm inside, aligned with the first pen mark. The 2 graft limbs are then passed subcutaneously and subfascially down to bone level and directed toward the tibial incision. This dual‐anchor construct provides a strong and broad medial reconstruction. One suture limb whipstitches the graft, while the other passes once through the tendon to function as a post suture. The sMCL limb is fixed at 30° of knee flexion and the dMCL limb at 60°, both under varus stress. At this stage, the graft is typically well tensioned. The adjustable femoral loop allows fine‐tuning of graft depth and tension. It is further shortened until the 25‐mm mark aligns with the tunnel entry point. The medial collateral ligament (MCL) represents one of the most frequently injured ligaments of the knee. 1 , 2 Although most MCL injuries are typically managed nonoperatively, surgical intervention via acute repair is indicated for isolated grade III injuries presenting with significant valgus malalignment, MCL entrapment over the pes anserinus, or when accompanied by intra‐articular or bony avulsion injuries. MCL reconstruction is reserved for cases of chronic medial instability that persist despite conservative management. 3 , 4 , 5

The 3 principal structural elements of the medial ligament complex are the superficial MCL (sMCL), the deep MCL (dMCL), and the posterior oblique ligament (POL). Biomechanical studies found that sMCL is the major restraint to valgus, especially in knee flexion. POL resists internal tibial rotation and valgus in full extension. dMCL resists external rotation near knee extension, contrary to earlier anatomical paradigms. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10

There are several surgical techniques for MCL reconstruction, including anatomic and nonanatomic medial knee reconstruction. However, recent studies found that they do not always guarantee complete stability throughout the knee's full range of motion. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 Therefore, an increasing number of techniques are being developed to achieve comprehensive stability restoration, with a preference for using short graft constructs. 10 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20

The purpose of this technique description is to describe a simple and reproducible mini‐open method for reconstructing a short MCL to restore anatomy and optimize knee function and stability throughout the full range of motion. By carefully considering graft tension and fixation at specific angles of flexion, combined with the tension of the self‐tightening femoral button, the technique aims to minimize residual instability and improve clinical outcomes when the graft plays the role of the sMCL and dMCL.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Positioning

After induction of spinal anesthesia, a complete knee examination is performed under anesthesia to evaluate valgus laxity in both flexion and extension, as well as for posteromedial rotational instability.

Patients are positioned supine with a foot support and a lateral thigh support to keep the knee at approximately 90° of flexion. A thigh tourniquet is applied, and the contralateral limb is placed in full extension.

Arthroscopic Assessment

A standard diagnostic arthroscopy is carried out to assess and address intra‐articular pathology. During this stage, any cruciate ligament, meniscal, or cartilage injuries are addressed, taking advantage of the enlarged medial compartment opening.

Graft Preparation

Anterior tibialis allograft or any other soft‐tissue auto/allograft may be used. The chosen graft is folded around an Infinity Adjustable Femoral Loop Button (ConMed, Largo, FL). The most proximal 25 mm are whipstitched, and a line at 15 mm and another at 25 mm from the proximal end are drawn. Ideally, a diameter of 8 to 9 mm graft at the femoral should be obtained.

Each limb of the graft is whipstitched with high‐strength HiFi #2 suture (ConMed, Largo, FL) or an equivalent. The prepared graft is wrapped in gauze soaked in vancomycin solution (500 mg vancomycin per 100 mL of saline) to minimize the risk of postoperative infection (Figure 1).

MCL Reconstruction

This technique describes a reconstruction of the short superficial and deep superficial MCL (sdMCL) using a 2‐incision approach for access to the femoral and tibial attachment sites. No fluoroscopic assessment is needed.

Anatomic landmarks including the medial epicondyle and adductor tubercle are easily palpated and marked. It is important to clearly identify the 3 bony prominences of the femur (adductor tubercle, medial epicondyle, and gastrocnemius tubercle). The femoral attachment site, based on radiographic and anatomic studies, is located approximately 3.2 mm proximal and 4.8 mm posterior to the medial epicondyle. However, this technique relies more in isometric and functional reconstruction rather than in a millimetric accuracy of the fixation sites. A 2‐cm longitudinal incision is made at proximal location, followed by blunt dissection down to bone. A specific 2.4‐mm Kirschner wire with a 3.5 mm blade tip (ConMed, Largo, FL) is inserted at the desired tunnel position to guide femoral socket creation (2.4 mm) and to allow the femoral button to pass across the lateral cortex of the femur (3.5 mm) (Figure 2).

The tibial insertion point is placed only 2 cm distal to the joint line and around the center of the posterior half of the tibial plateau, playing the clinically relevant aspect of the sMCL (Figure 3). A second 2‐cm longitudinal incision is made, and soft tissue is carefully dissected to expose the bony surface. A second guidewire is inserted for tibial tunnel placement. Isometry is confirmed by tightening a suture as a loop around both guidewires.

Then, isometry is assessed through full range of motion. A constant tension is maintained in the suture loop if a proper isometry is found. When isometry is not obtained, it is most commonly due to an excessively anterior tibial position.

The femoral tunnel is drilled for 30 mm over the blade‐tip guidewire to accommodate the corresponding prepared diameter of the graft. A retrieving suture is passed through the femur and pulled from the lateral side. This suture will retrieve the pulling suture of the Femoral Infinity Button (ConMed, Largo, FL). Then, the button is passed through the whole distal femur and deployed over the lateral femoral cortex. The adjustable loop is shortened and the graft is progressively introduced into its femoral socket so that only 15 mm is left inside the femoral tunnel (first pen mark) (Figure 4).

The 2 graft limbs are then subcutaneously and subfascially introduced, deep to the sartorial fascia and right on the femoral and tibial bone surfaces, and directed toward the tibial incision.

One Y‐Knot RC All‐Suture anchor (ConMed, Largo, FL) is inserted at the tibial attachment site where the Kirschner wires (K‐wires) for the isometric test were introduced. A second Y‐Knot RC All‐Suture anchor (ConMed, Largo, FL) is placed 1 to 2 cm more anterior to reproduce the dMCL. These 2.8 mm all‐suture anchors are preloaded with 2 high‐strength sutures with curved needles on each end. Each anchor will fix each graft limb. This will create a construct that better resembles the clinically relevant part of the sMCL and dMCL anatomy. This dual‐anchor configuration facilitates a robust and broad medial reconstruction.

Each graft limb is secured as follows: one needle wipstitchs the graft. The other end of the suture only goes once through the tendon to work as an approaching suture. The sMCL graft is fixed with the knee at 30° of flexion and the dMCL graft is fixed at 60° of flexion, both with varus stress applied. Normally, a well‐tensioned graft is obtained at this point. However, the tension of the native MCL is usually impossible to reproduce with standard fixation and tensioning methods.

The use of an adjustable‐loop cortical button allows fine‐tuning of femoral‐side graft tension and depth. Then, the femoral loop is further tensioned as far as the 25 mm line is flush with the entry point of the tunnel (Figure 5). Final confirmation includes inspection of valgus stability in flexion and extension, and assessment for posteromedial rotational tightness.

Rehabilitation Protocol

The specific rehabilitation protocol depends on concomitant procedures performed along with the MCL reconstruction. Normally, an unlocked hinged brace is used for 12 weeks 24/7. Partial weight bearing for the first 4 weeks is recommended.

A magnetic resonance imaging is performed at 3 months, confirming the proper integration of the graft (Figure 6).

DISCUSSION

The described technique is a short isometric construct that restores valgus and rotational stability by reproducing the role of the deep and superficial MCL. Further, the described retensioning of the femoral insertion site also provides an extraordinary way to obtain a graft tension that is hardly achieved with other methods. Being able to restore stability with 1 graft is valuable to surgeons, as it decreases the risk of tunnel convergence when performing multiple ligament reconstructions, especially important when reconstructing the posterior cruciate ligament and MCL at the same time.

A potential limitation of this technique is the lack of direct reconstruction of the POL, which may contribute to residual rotational laxity in some cases. However, it can be easily adapted and instead of reproducing the dMCL, the second limb can be fixed at the site of the POL in the tibia.

With this technique, by achieving appropriate graft isometry, a broad tibial footprint through dual‐anchor fixation, and progressive tensioning with the adjustable‐loop femoral button, medial knee stability is optimized with a single‐limb construct.

In conclusion, this approach offers several advantages: it reduces surgical complexity, avoids tunnel convergence, and enhances reproducibility, especially in multiligament scenarios (Table 3). The anatomical and functional rationale behind graft positioning and fixation angles aligns with current biomechanical insights, suggesting that individualized, tension‐controlled reconstructions may offer superior outcomes compared with traditional anatomical techniques alone.
