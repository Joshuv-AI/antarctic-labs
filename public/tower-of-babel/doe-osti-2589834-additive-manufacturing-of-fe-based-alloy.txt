Abstract
This report is a Milestone 2 deliverable in FY2025, under work package MT-25AN090211 to
support research and qualification activities supported by the Advanced Materials and
Manufacturing Technologies (AMMT) program. It provides an update on the work done on the
laser based additive manufacturing of steels for nuclear applications, conducted collaboratively by
Argonne National Laboratory and Pacific Northwestern National Laboratory. Building upon FY23
and FY24 efforts, the focus of FY25 includes continued optimization of process parameters for the
alloys: A709 & G92. The work package also includes fabricating test samples to conduct a
thorough microstructural analysis and perform preliminary mechanical testing. The major
accomplishments of this work package are summarized below, applicable to both Laser Powder
Bed Fusion (LPBF) processes conducted at Argonne National Laboratory and Laser Powder
Directed Energy Deposition (LP-DED) processes conducted at Pacific Northwest National
Laboratory.

 • Process Optimization and Sample Fabrication: Leveraging optimized process
 parameters developed in FY2024, larger material blocks of A709 and G92 alloys were
 successfully fabricated.
 • G92 Ferritic/Martensitic Steel Investigation: In the as-deposited state, both LPBF and
 LP-DED samples exhibit a dual-phase microstructure of ferrite and martensite, with
 nanoscale θ precipitates embedded in the ferritic regions. Subsequent heat treatments:
 tempering, normalizing, and combined normalizing + tempering revealed distinct
 pathways of phase evolution. Tempered LPBF samples demonstrated strong phase
 partitioning, where carbonitrides localized in ferrite while carbides dominated martensite,
 reflecting the impact of rapid solidification and solute segregation. Conversely, LP-DED
 samples showed a more uniform precipitation distribution without clear ferrite–martensite
 selectivity. Normalizing under identical conditions homogenized both materials, producing
 fully martensitic structures and largely erasing process-dependent differences. This
 highlights normalizing as a strong equalizing treatment, driven by high temperature
 austenitization and air cooling. Finally, the two-step heat treatment yielded tempered
 martensite with finely dispersed carbides in both processes, confirming the dominant role
 of the normalizing step in dictating the final microstructure across AM processing routes.

 1

 Mechanical testing was conducted at both room and elevated temperatures across all
 conditions for LPBF samples and for selected conditions for DED samples, with
 processing-dependent microstructural variations documented and analyzed. Both processes
 exhibit orientation-dependent properties. LPBF shows moderate anisotropy with horizontal
 specimens generally outperforming vertical orientations in strength. LP-DED displays
 similar orientation sensitivity but with less pronounced differences between horizontal and
 vertical properties.
 • A709 Austenitic Stainless-Steel Investigation: Comprehensive characterization of the
 A709 alloy was performed under multiple heat treatment regimens to evaluate
 microstructural evolution and mechanical response. The effects of varying solution
 annealing temperatures and subsequent precipitation hardening treatments, both with and
 without prior solution annealing, were investigated. LPBF and LP-DED samples showed
 distinct microstructural features: LPBF exhibited a higher dislocation density and an
 absence of precipitates, while LP-DED displayed fewer dislocations but evidence of
 secondary phase formation. Samples subjected directly to precipitation treatment and those
 processed by a two-step route (SA + PT) produced broadly similar precipitate types,
 although the volume fraction may differ. Mechanical testing at room and elevated
 temperatures revealed pronounced anisotropy, with horizontal builds consistently
 achieving 20–50 MPa higher UTS than vertical builds. Both processes demonstrated high-
 temperature serrated yielding, highlighting the need for further investigation into its
 underlying mechanisms and implications for creep and fatigue resistance in elevated-
 temperature service environments.
 • Planned activities include extended characterization of both alloys to enhance
 understanding of microstructural evolution during additive manufacturing processes, along
 with high-temperature testing protocols including creep and creep-fatigue evaluations.

 Chapter 1: Introduction
Additive Manufacturing is a process of fabricating objects via a layer-by-layer deposition from a
3d geometry model into complex near net shape components, thus reducing the need for post
process machining, welding, and brazing[1]. Recently, there has been a huge push to explore the
feasibility of using additive manufacturing techniques for the fabrication of components in energy
industries[2][3]. Of particular interest for the current project, based on the AMMT roadmap, is the
incorporation of current reactor materials[4]. After the initial down-selection in FY2023 [5] and
FY2024[6], FY25 primarily focused on two alloy systems. These alloys are the austenitic stainless
steel: A709, and ferritic/martensitic steels: Grade 92 (henceforth referred to as G92). Through the
DOE-NE Advanced Reactor Technology (ART) down-selection process in prior years, Alloy 709
(A709) has been designated for sodium-cooled fast reactor (SFR) applications because of its
superior high-temperature performance compared to that of conventional 316H stainless steel[7].
Grade 92 (G-92) steel represents a third-generation creep-resistant ferritic/martensitic (F/M) steel
with tungsten and boron additions that enhance performance beyond that of Grade 91 (G-91) steel.
Despite the potential for superior creep resistance at temperatures exceeding 600°C, G92 remains
unqualified under nuclear codes with much less documented AM research. As part of the work, to
understand the feasibility of using advanced manufacturing techniques, these two alloys were
manufactured using Laser Powder Bed Fusion (LPBF) and Directed Energy Deposition (DED) at
Argonne National Laboratory and Pacific Northwestern National Laboratory respectively.
The current M2 report summarizes the initial findings of microstructural evolution and mechanical
behavior of these two alloys (in as deposited as well as heat treated conditions) and provides a
comparison of the microstructure and mechanical properties of the alloys.
Chapter 2 provides an overview of the two downselected alloys that form the focus of this study.
Detailed descriptions of the experimental methods are not repeated here, as they have been
thoroughly documented in prior reports [6][8]. Chapters 3 and 4 present the key findings for alloys
A709 and G92, produced through both laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) and laser powder directed
energy deposition (LP-DED), with emphasis on microstructural evolution and mechanical
response. Chapter 5 delivers a comparative assessment of LPBF, LP-DED, and wrought
conditions, highlighting differences in phase formation, grain morphology, and strength. The final
chapters synthesize overall conclusions, evaluate implications for high-temperature performance,

and outline recommended directions for continued research and process optimization in future
investigations.

 Chapter 2: Literature Review
As mentioned in the previous section, based on the work in FY2023 and FY2024, two labs, ANL
& PNNL focused on two main classes of steels as part of the current work package: (i) Austenitic
Stainless Steels, (ii) Ferritic/Martensitic steels. The following section gives a brief overview of the
alloys considered.

2.1 Austenitic Stainless Steels

2.1.1 A709(NF709)
First developed by the British in 1950s, the 20Cr25Ni/Nb stainless steel has been used as a fuel
cladding material for the British Advanced Gas-cooled Reactor (AGR) fleet since 1962[9].
Approximately 90,000 fuel pins were present in each AGR plant, and there were 14 plants
constructed and operated. While the creep strength of 20Cr25Ni/Nb was relatively low, it was
sufficient for its intended application. Based on this, in the 1980s, the Nippon Steel Corporation
modified the base chemical composition by adding B, Mo, and Ti to 20Cr25Ni/Nb in order to
strengthen the creep resistance for ultra-supercritical boilers. The resulting austenitic stainless steel
was trademarked NF 709[7]. The design criteria for developing the composition of NF 709 were
to have a stable austenite devoid of the sigma and other intermetallic phases under long-term
elevated-temperature service conditions, and creep-strengthened by carbonitride M(CN)
precipitated in a stable, fine dispersion. Through a DOE-NE ART material down-selection and
intermediate term testing program, Alloy 709, an advanced austenitic stainless-steel alloy, was
recommended as a Class A structural material for the SFR because of its overall superior structural
strength advantage[10][11]. While extensive work has been performed on A709 in the wrought
form to have it code-qualified, there is no available literature on the additive manufacturing aspect.

2.2 Ferritic/Martensitic Steels

2.2.1 Grade 92
To improve the creep properties of 2nd generation G91 alloy, G92 steel was developed as a 3rd
generation creep-resistant F/M steel with the addition of tungsten and minute amounts of boron.
While it has been reported to have better creep properties relative to G91, it has not been code
qualified[12], [13]. In practice, the microstructure is made up of tempered martensitic lath structure
which is stabilized by Cr rich M23C6 carbide, intra-lath MX type of Nb and V carbonitrides,
martensite phase transformation induced high dislocation density, and solid solution strengthening

 11

from tungsten[14], [15]. The presence of tungsten in the M23C6 precipitate decreases growth rate
of the precipitate during creep exposure, which in turn increases the stability of the martensitic lath
structure of the steel on creep exposure[12]. Currently, there is no available research on the additive
manufacturing of G92 steel.

3.1 Ferritic/Martensitic Steel: G92

As part of the work in FY2024, the process parameters for G92 were optimized to continue
focusing on full-scale builds. It should be noted that the “optimized parameters” were defined
based on achieving a material density greater than 99%. The significance of this criterion will be
addressed in subsequent sections.

3.1.1 As Deposited Sample
Scanning Electron Microscopy
After the initial assessment, two larger samples were manufactured with optimized process
parameters. The SEM microstructures presented in the section are from these builds. The as-
deposited samples, both parallel and perpendicular to the build direction are shown in Figure 1.
Schematic indicating which surface was used for the microstructural analysis is also shown in this
Figure. Figure 1(a) shows the microstructure of the sample parallel to the build direction. From
the low magnification image, larger parabolic grains separated by finer grains can be observed.
On further inspection at slightly higher magnification, it can be seen that the parabolic grains
contain even finer grains within them. These grains are thought to be ferritic phase. Lath-like
microstructure can also be seen in the areas next to these parabolic grains, which are martensitic
in nature. High magnification images of these two different phases show the presence of very fine
precipitates in the ferritic grains while martensitic phase seems to be single phase (at least at this
scale). When observing the sample perpendicular to the build direction, the checkerboard pattern
aligned at 67o can be clearly seen when looking at the lower magnification image. Similar to the
parallel samples, the perpendicular ones seem to have mainly a two-phase microstructure

 13

containing ferrite and martensite. The presence of the finer precipitates is again noted, but only in
the ferrite phase.
The main point of interest when looking at these microstructures with respect to the two phases
present is their “location”. The presence of the ferrite phase is usually noted along the boundaries
(melt-pool) in both parallel and perpendicular directions while the martensite phase is present in
between these. Similar observations were made in G91 by Hatakeyama et al [16]. In Figure 17 of
[16], the schematic presented by the authors explains the phenomenon of the formation of these
two-phase microstructures.

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)
To further understand the microstructure and determine the phases present, TEM analysis was done
on the AD sample. A 3mm disc was used for the same. The low magnification HAADF-STEM
image in Figure 4(a) shows both the martensitic and ferritic phases. High magnification images
are shown in Figures 4 (d-g). In this study the two phases were differentiated by their grain
structure and the precipitates inside. The ferrite phase shows the presence of needle-like
precipitates, presumed to be theta (θ) phase. Similar precipitates were shown to be present in

density of dislocations. The EDS maps revealed no evidence of segregation among the major
alloying elements. However, oxide inclusions, primarily manganese and silicon-rich, were
observed. Such features have also been reported in other steels[19].

3.1.1.2 Mechanical Behavior
Uniaxial Tensile Testing
Figures 5 (a) and (b) display the engineering and true stress–strain curves, respectively, obtained
from 25 oC (RT) to 750 oC. At RT, the alloy exhibits high strength, with engineering yield strength
(YS) approaching ~875MPa and ultimate tensile strength (UTS) approximately 1150MPa. As
temperature increases, both yield and tensile strength decrease progressively, while ductility
increases up to intermediate temperatures. Beyond 600 °C, however, both strength and ductility
decline sharply, with the material showing limited load-bearing capacity at 750 °C. Interestingly
though, the sample tested at 450 °C has the highest ductility and shows considerable strain
hardening. The distinction between horizontal (H) and vertical (V) orientations is relatively small,
though subtle differences are observed in strain hardening and elongation. Horizontal specimens
tend to exhibit slightly higher ductility, while vertical specimens maintain marginally higher
strength at certain temperatures. The true stress–strain curves (Figure b) further clarify deformation
behavior. At room temperature and moderate temperatures (350 to 450), significant work
hardening is observed, with true stress continuing to rise with strain. This behavior reflects the
capacity of the martensitic/ferritic matrix to accommodate dislocation activity and strain
partitioning. This will be discussed in detail in future sections. At higher temperatures (650–750
°C), the true stress–strain curves reveal a rapid drop in strength with minimal work hardening,
indicating the onset of recovery or creep-like softening mechanisms. These results suggest that the
alloy maintains structural integrity up to ~600 °C but undergoes rapid degradation beyond this.
Figure (c) summarizes the mechanical properties as a function of temperature. The yield strength
plot shows a monotonic decline from nearly 875 MPa at room temperature to below 150 MPa at
750 °C. This trend highlights the temperature sensitivity of the alloy’s strengthening mechanisms,
particularly those associated with dislocation density and fine lath structures, which are
destabilized at high temperatures. The UTS plot shows a similar trend, with values exceeding 1100
MPa at low temperatures but falling below 300 MPa at 750 °C, with the exception of 450 °C. Both
yield and ultimate strengths show only minor orientation dependence, indicating isotropic behavior
with respect to build direction. The uniform elongation plot reveals a more complex trend.
Elongation initially increases with temperature, peaking near 12–14% at ~400 °C, before dropping
steeply beyond 600 °C. This increase reflects the activation of additional slip systems and
improved dislocation mobility, which enhance ductility at intermediate temperatures. However, at

 18

higher temperatures, recovery and dynamic softening reduce the ability of the material to sustain
uniform deformation. The total elongation plot shows similar behavior, with a pronounced
minimum between 500 and 600 °C, followed by an increase at 750 °C. This recovery of ductility
at very high temperatures may be associated with dynamic recrystallization or grain boundary
sliding. Overall, the tensile data demonstrate that the alloy possesses excellent strength at room
and moderately elevated temperatures, with competitive ductility up to ~600 °C. Orientation
effects are secondary compared to temperature effects, indicating robust and relatively isotropic
mechanical performance. This is also summarized in the table 1.

Post-Deformation Behavior
As mentioned in the earlier sections, the as-deposited sample tested at 450 °C exhibited notably
high strain-hardening when compared to samples tested at other temperatures. This behavior
prompted further characterization to better understand the underlying mechanisms responsible for
the enhanced hardening response. For this purpose, the deformed sample was analyzed in detail.
The SEM images presented in Figure 6 are of the deformed surface of the horizontal sample
following mechanical testing. At low magnification, the surface appears relatively uniform, and
no significant cracks, voids, or other surface defects are evident, suggesting that deformation was
accommodated in a relatively homogeneous manner without catastrophic localized failure.
Upon closer examination at higher magnification, the overall microstructural features remain
consistent with those of the as-deposited condition, indicating that the bulk microstructural
framework is largely preserved during deformation at 450 °C. However, subtle but meaningful
differences are observed when focusing specifically on the ferritic regions of the microstructure.
In particular, the size of the secondary “θ” phase within ferrite grains appears noticeably larger
than in the as-deposited state. This growth may indicate thermally activated coarsening processes
facilitated by the elevated testing temperature. The enlarged θ phase could potentially contribute
to changes in dislocation motion and interaction, thereby influencing the strain-hardening response
observed in the mechanical testing.
EBSD analysis further confirms the retention of many of the original microstructural
characteristics. Both the low- and high-magnification EBSD maps of the deformed sample display
features that closely resemble those of the as-deposited condition. The similarity of the EBSD
maps reinforces the interpretation that the enhanced strain hardenability observed at 450 °C arises
not from drastic changes in phase distribution or texture but rather from more subtle interactions
between dislocations and evolving secondary phase features within the ferrite matrix. As this
doesn’t provide the full picture, a FIB liftout was made from the deformed surface, encompassing
both the ferrite and martensite phases.

The FIB lift-out in Figure 7 shows two regions of interest: the ferrite phase and the martensite

hardening by forcing dislocations to jog, pile up, or bow between obstacles. When examining the
martensite laths, the most remarkable observation is the presence of twins inside the laths. In these
images, a hierarchical structure is apparent: twins within twins. In the present sample, the
combination of enlarged θ precipitates plus dense dislocation networks around them, along with
twins nested within other twins inside the martensitic laths, likely act together. The precipitates
impede dislocation motion, increasing internal stresses; the twin boundaries provide additional
obstacles; and the hierarchical twins allow some accommodation of strain without immediate
failure. Together these microstructural features plausibly account for the observed increased
strength and enhanced strain hardenability at 450 °C in the as-deposited horizontal sample.

3.1.2 Heat Treated Samples
For wrought G91/G92, the prescribed heat treatment is two steps: normalizing (1070 oC/15mins)
followed by tempering (770 oC/45mins)[20]. In order to understand the effect of these heat
treatments, the as-deposited G92 was subject to three different heat treatments, i.e. (i) tempering,
(ii) normalizing, and (iii) normalizing + tempering. The following section describes the
microstructures of these three heat treatments.
Tempering (770 oC/45min)
Scanning Electron Microscopy
Figure 8 presents the backscattered electron (BSE) micrographs of the horizontal G92 specimen
subjected to direct tempering (AD + 770 °C/45 min) at varying magnifications. As established for
the as-deposited condition, the microstructure consists of two dominant phases: ferrite and
martensite. However, tempering alters the precipitation landscape significantly, and distinct
differences are apparent between these two phases at higher magnification. Within the martensitic
laths, Figure 8(d) a dense distribution of bright precipitates of varying size and morphology is
observed. These precipitates exhibit irregular shapes, with some appearing globular while others
are more elongated, indicating that multiple carbide or nitride species may have nucleated
heterogeneously during the tempering treatment. Their bright contrast in BSE imaging suggests
enrichment in heavier elements. In contrast, the ferritic regions display a markedly different
precipitation behavior. Along the ferrite grain boundaries, Figure 8(e), darker precipitates are
clearly visible. These are less numerous than those in martensite, but they are consistently
associated with boundary sites, suggesting boundary-controlled nucleation and growth. Notably,
at this magnification, the θ-type precipitates previously identified in the as-deposited condition are
conspicuously absent, pointing to significant microstructural evolution during the tempering step.
The absence of θ precipitates implies either their dissolution during heat treatment or
transformation into more stable secondary phases under the given thermal conditions.
EDS mapping in Figure 8(f) provides confirmation of the compositional differences implied by
the BSE contrast. The darker precipitates, located primarily along ferrite grain boundaries, are
enriched in Cr, V, and N, consistent with the formation of stable carbonitrides such as Cr-rich
M(C,N) or VN particles. Further TEM/XRD analysis is required to confirm the nature of these
precipitates. These phases are known to provide grain boundary pinning and thermal stability in
ferritic–martensitic alloys. Conversely, the brighter precipitates within the martensitic laths are

 24

rich in W, Mo, Cr, and C, indicating the precipitation of complex M6C or M23C6 type carbides,
both of which are typical of tempered martensitic steels containing strong carbide-forming
elements. The coexistence of these two distinct precipitation populations highlights the
partitioning of alloying elements between ferrite and martensite during tempering. Overall, the
microstructural evidence suggests that tempering at 770 °C for 45 min promotes extensive
precipitation reactions, with fine, W- and Mo-rich carbides decorating martensitic laths and
Cr/V/N-rich carbonitrides stabilizing ferritic boundaries. It should also be noted that aging the
sample at 770 °C tempers the martensite too, i.e. softens it.

4.1.2.2 Mechanical Behavior
Uniaxial Tensile Testing
The tensile performance of the alloy was systematically evaluated from room temperature to 650
°C under multiple processing conditions (as-deposited horizontal/vertical, normalized, tempered,
two-step heat treated, and wrought reference). At room temperature, the as-deposited vertical
specimens demonstrated the highest strength, with a yield strength of 1046 MPa and an ultimate
tensile strength (UTS) of 1425 MPa, although ductility remained limited with a total elongation of
only 8.2%. The corresponding stress–strain curve displayed a steep initial elastic slope, followed
by limited plastic deformation and rapid onset of necking, reflecting high strength but poor strain
hardening capacity. In comparison, the as-deposited horizontal builds showed slightly reduced
strength (YS 982 MPa, UTS 1348 MPa) but improved elongation (12.5%), with the stress–strain
response exhibiting a smoother strain hardening regime prior to fracture, highlighting anisotropy
arising from layer-wise solidification. Normalized specimens recorded yield strengths of 845–878
MPa and UTS of 1150–1184 MPa, accompanied by elongations of ~10–11%. These curves
exhibited extended uniform plastic strain regions compared to the as-deposited state, indicating
improved strain hardening. Tempered specimens showed comparable strength levels (YS 812–835
MPa, UTS 1112–1130 MPa) but with higher uniform elongations (~11–12%), and their curves
reflected stable strain hardening followed by gradual necking. The two-step heat treated condition
yielded YS 720–743 MPa and UTS 1025–1048 MPa, with elongations up to 13.8%. Here, the
stress–strain curves revealed a broader uniform deformation regime and delayed necking,
demonstrating an improved strength–ductility compromise. By contrast, the wrought reference
exhibited the lowest strength (YS 690 MPa, UTS 915 MPa) but the highest elongation of 15.2%,
with smooth strain hardening and gradual fracture, typical of a homogenized equilibrium
microstructure.
At 450 °C, the alloy retained considerable strength across conditions, while ductility increased
compared to room temperature. The as-deposited vertical specimens achieved YS 1028 MPa and
UTS 1388 MPa, with elongation of 12.4%. The corresponding stress–strain curves revealed a more
extended plastic regime, reflecting thermally activated dislocation motion that delayed the onset
of necking. Horizontal as-deposited samples showed YS 950 MPa, UTS 1306 MPa, and total
elongation of 13.2%. Normalized and tempered conditions clustered around UTS values of 1090–
1125 MPa with elongations near 14%, while stress–strain curves exhibited modest strain hardening

 30

before fracture, indicative of thermally assisted plasticity. The two-step heat treated specimens
delivered UTS values of 995–1008 MPa with elongation of 15.0%, and curves revealed enhanced
uniform strain compared to single-step heat treatments. The wrought alloy, with UTS of 880 MPa
and elongation of 16.5%, displayed the most extended necking regime, suggesting high tolerance
to strain localization.
At 550 °C, strength degradation became evident, with UTS values falling below 1000 MPa. The
as-deposited vertical condition recorded YS 785 MPa, UTS 995 MPa, and elongation of 9.5%,
with stress–strain curves showing rapid softening and early necking. Horizontal as-deposited
specimens had YS 742 MPa, UTS 972 MPa, and elongation of 11.2%, with slightly improved
strain hardening behavior compared to vertical counterparts. Normalized and tempered conditions
reached UTS values of ~885–910 MPa with elongations of 12–13%, exhibiting smoother but
shorter plastic regimes, reflecting partial softening from recovery processes. The two-step
treatment provided UTS of 850–870 MPa with elongation of 14.5%, with stress–strain curves
showing more gradual strain hardening followed by delayed necking. The wrought reference,
although weaker (UTS 765 MPa), maintained stable elongation at 15.8%, and its curves revealed
a broad plastic regime, highlighting the advantage of refined and homogenized microstructure at
elevated temperature.
At 650 °C, pronounced softening occurred across all conditions, with yield strength below 400
MPa and UTS in the 450–520 MPa range. As-deposited vertical specimens retained YS 360 MPa
and UTS 495 MPa with elongation of 22.5%, while stress–strain curves revealed a diminished
work-hardening region, followed by rapid elongation through grain boundary sliding prior to
fracture. Horizontal builds showed YS 348 MPa, UTS 482 MPa, and elongation of 25.4%, with
curves exhibiting more extended strain to fracture, suggesting enhanced ductility at the expense of
strength. Normalized and tempered conditions yielded UTS of 470–490 MPa with elongations
~30%, their stress–strain responses characterized by significant softening and plastic flow,
consistent with high-temperature deformation mechanisms. The two-step heat treated condition
achieved UTS of 465 MPa with elongation of 35.2%, with stress–strain curves highlighting the
longest uniform strain regime among AM conditions. The wrought alloy exhibited the lowest
strength (UTS 452 MPa) but the highest ductility at 40.1%, with curves dominated by gradual
flow, large uniform deformation, and delayed fracture, typical of high-temperature grain boundary
sliding and creep-like behavior.

Overall, yield strength declined from ~1000 MPa at room temperature to ~350 MPa at 650 °C,
while UTS decreased from ~1400 MPa to ~450–500 MPa across processing conditions. Uniform
elongation peaked at 12–14% around 450 °C but fell at 550 °C, while total elongation increased
substantially beyond 600 °C, reaching 35–40% in two-step and wrought conditions. Orientation

very strong, suffered from rapid necking and limited uniform strain, while heat-treated conditions
promoted extended plastic regimes, better strain hardening, and more gradual fracture.

3.1.3 Process Parameter Study
As discussed for the as-deposited sample, the initial microstructure consists of a dual-phase
mixture of martensite and ferrite, with θ precipitates distributed within the ferrite matrix. This
starting condition is not only unique but also highly influential in determining the evolution of
subsequent microstructures during post-processing. Understanding, and if possible, tailoring, this
two-phase morphology is critical for optimizing the balance of strength and ductility. To this end,
processing strategies were designed to evaluate whether the relative volume fractions of martensite

parameters, including power, scan speed, and layer thickness, were held constant to isolate the
effects of these two variables. The results of these process variations are shown in Figure 15, with

3.2 Austenitic Stainless Steel: A709

For Alloy A709, the FY2025 research efforts were directed toward refining post-build heat
treatment protocols, with particular emphasis on optimizing the solution annealing (SA)
temperature. A secondary objective of the FY2025 program was the development of an
experimental continuous cooling transformation (CCT) diagram, as early findings indicate that
AM A709 exhibits transformation behavior distinct from its wrought counterpart.

3.2.1 Solution Annealing
In austenitic stainless steels, solution annealing is a critical step typically carried out after forming,
as it dissolves carbides and other solute-rich particles, homogenizes the alloy chemistry, and
promotes restoration of a fully austenitic single-phase microstructure. For additively manufactured
(AM) A709, variations in cooling rates and solidification pathways introduce micro-segregation
and non-equilibrium phases not observed in wrought material, making the optimization of SA
conditions especially important. Accordingly, heat treatments were systematically designed to
examine dissolution of secondary phases, stabilization of the austenitic matrix, and the influence
of temperature on grain size evolution. It should be noted that preliminary work on this subject
was also reported in FY2024[6].
Figure 17 presents TEM images of Alloy A709 subjected to solution annealing at varying times
and temperatures, compared to the as-deposited condition. Clear microstructural differences are
observed between the annealed and as-deposited states. For the sample annealed at 1150 °C for 1
h, which corresponds to the standard solution annealing treatment for wrought material, a reduction
in dislocation density is evident, reflecting partial recovery of the matrix. However, despite this
recovery, a substantial population of MX-type precipitates remains visible. As noted earlier, the
purpose of solution annealing in austenitic stainless steels is to dissolve carbides, nitrides, and
carbonitrides into a homogeneous solid solution, providing a precipitate-free starting point for
subsequent thermal treatments. The incomplete dissolution observed at 1150 °C suggests that the
kinetics of MX dissolution in AM A709 differ significantly from wrought alloys, most likely due
to elemental segregation and non-equilibrium solidification effects introduced during laser powder
bed fusion. To further investigate, samples were annealed at 1200 °C for both 1 h and 2 h. TEM
analysis revealed that these higher-temperature treatments reduced dislocation density even
further, indicating a more advanced state of recovery. However, contrary to expectations, MX
precipitates were not eliminated. Instead, the precipitates appeared coarser and more faceted

 38

compared to those in the 1150 °C/1 h condition, consistent with Ostwald ripening rather than
dissolution. This observation suggests that MX phases in AM A709 are thermodynamically stable
at 1200 °C, with kinetics favoring growth rather than dissolution at these time scales. In ferritic–
martensitic and austenitic steels, MX precipitates often enriched in Nb, Ti, or V are well
documented to persist up to very high temperatures due to their low solubility and high lattice
stability. The current findings indicate that AM processing further enhances this stability, possibly
due to local segregation of strong carbide/nitride formers at cell boundaries during solidification.
These results highlight that longer annealing times or significantly higher temperatures may be
necessary to destabilize MX phases in AM A709. Alternatively, a modified heat treatment
approach, such as multi-step dissolution plus rapid quenching, may be required to fully eliminate
these precipitates and achieve a precipitate-free solid solution.

transforming the high strength but potentially brittle AD and normalized conditions into the
tougher, more ductile tempered martensitic microstructure required for nuclear reactor structural
applications, where the combination of adequate strength and enhanced fracture toughness is

4.2.3 Mechanical Properties
Vickers Hardness
The Vickers hardness measurements presented in Figure 48 reveal systematic trends that directly
correlate with the microstructural evolution under different thermal processing conditions. The AD
condition exhibited the highest hardness value of approximately 220 HV, attributed to the
combination of a high dislocation density from rapid DED solidification, a fine cellular structure,
and the nonequilibrium precipitates formed during rapid cooling. This elevated hardness results
from multiple strengthening mechanisms including Hall–Petch grain boundary strengthening,
dislocation strengthening, and precipitation hardening from fine-scale phases. The SA treatment
produced the lowest hardness of approximately 190 HV, representing an ~14% reduction from that
of the AD condition. This decrease primarily results from the substantial reduction in the
dislocation density at the elevated SA temperature, combined with precipitate dissolution that
eliminates precipitation strengthening effects and relieves residual stresses. The PT achieved an
intermediate hardness of approximately 205 HV, while the combined SA+PT treatment reached
195 HV. These intermediate values reflect the balance between dislocation recovery and controlled
precipitate formation. The SA+PT condition demonstrates optimal microstructural development
through uniform, fine scale strengthening precipitates in a recrystallized matrix, eliminating the
microstructural heterogeneities present in the AD condition while maintaining adequate hardness.
The progressive hardness reduction from AD (220 HV) → PT (205 HV) → SA+PT (195 HV) →
SA (190 HV) quantitatively demonstrates the effectiveness of each heat treatment in modifying
the strengthening mechanisms active in DED A709 steel.

 Chapter 5: Comparison between LPBF and LPDED
As noted in the FY-2024 report [8], there are substantial differences between laser powder bed
fusion (LPBF) and laser powder directed energy deposition (LP-DED) processes, particularly in
terms of thermal history and cooling rates. Among these, cooling rate is the most critical parameter
influencing solidification dynamics, segregation behavior, and subsequent phase evolution. LPBF
typically achieves significantly higher cooling rates than LP-DED due to its smaller melt pool
dimensions and finer energy input control. These rapid solidification conditions in LPBF promote
the formation of highly refined microstructures, with pronounced non-equilibrium phases and
cellular/dendritic morphologies. In contrast, LP-DED, with its lower cooling rates and larger melt
pool volumes, tends to produce coarser microstructures with more equilibrium-like phase
distributions. These inherent differences lead to distinct thermokinetic pathways that ultimately
control precipitate stability, defect formation, and dislocation density. While LPBF is constrained
by build chamber dimensions, LP-DED systems can manufacture components with significantly
larger build heights and substantially larger cross-sectional areas. Given that the present work
encompasses both processes, systematic comparison of the resulting microstructures and
mechanical performance of A709 and G92 alloys is essential to optimize process selection based
on component size requirements and performance specifications.

5.1 Microstructure

Ferritic/Martensitic Steel: G92
The as-deposited condition of both LPBF and LP-DED samples reveals a mixed microstructure
consisting of ferrite and martensite, with the presence of θ-phase identified in both cases. However,
notable differences are observed in grain size, morphology, and crystallographic texture, reflecting
the distinct thermal histories of the two processes. In addition, the relative area fraction of ferrite
varies significantly, with LPBF samples exhibiting a higher ferrite content compared to LP-DED.
This disparity is attributed to the rapid cooling rates inherent to LPBF, which promote finer
solidification structures and alter the ferrite-to-martensite transformation balance during
deposition.
After tempering, distinct phase evolution was observed in the LPBF samples compared to the LP-
DED counterparts. Specifically, the LPBF material showed clear partitioning of phases, with
carbonitrides preferentially forming within ferritic regions, while carbides were more prevalent

 78

within the martensitic matrices. This phase selectivity highlights the influence of LPBF’s rapid
solidification history on solute partitioning. In contrast, the LP-DED samples exhibited a more
uniform distribution of secondary phases, without pronounced differentiation between ferritic and
martensitic regions. When normalized under identical conditions, the LPBF and LP-DED samples
developed nearly indistinguishable microstructures. Both exhibited a fully martensitic structure,
indicating that the normalizing treatment effectively homogenized the thermal history and erased
most microstructural differences originating from the deposition processes. This suggests that
normalizing has a strong equalizing influence, promoting uniform phase transformation regardless
of the initial as-deposited condition. Thus, the high temperature austenitization and subsequent air
cooling appear to dominate microstructural development in both fabrication routes. Finally, In the
case of the two-step heat-treated samples, the resulting microstructures were again largely
comparable across both LPBF and LP-DED processes. The normalizing step appeared to play a
dominant role in setting the phase distribution, leading to a tempered martensitic matrix with finely
dispersed carbides in both cases. The subsequent tempering primarily refined dislocation
substructures and stabilized carbides rather than introducing significant differences. This
highlights that the sequence of normalization followed by tempering strongly dictates the final
microstructural state.

Austenitic Stainless Steel: A709
For Alloy A709, the comparison between LPBF and LP-DED samples reveals more pronounced
differences than initially expected. Beyond variations in grain size, morphology, and texture, the
two processes produce noticeably distinct microstructural features. At higher magnifications, the
LPBF samples show a denser network of dislocations and an absence of clear precipitate
formation, suggesting a less stable internal structure. In contrast, the LP-DED samples exhibit
fewer dislocations overall but reveal evidence of secondary features developing within the
microstructure. These distinctions underscore how processing routes strongly influence the
structural characteristics and evolution of A709 alloy. The most significant difference is observed
in the solution-annealed condition. For the LPBF samples, even extended annealing at 1200 °C for
2 hours did not successfully dissolve the MX precipitates, indicating a high degree of stability
under these conditions. In contrast, the LP-DED samples showed clear evidence of precipitate
dissolution, suggesting a different response to heat treatment. Ongoing work is focused on
investigating the underlying mechanisms responsible for this discrepancy between the two

 79

processing routes. On the other hand, the samples that were directly subjected to a precipitation
treatment immediately after deposition, as well as those that underwent a two-step heat treatment
consisting of solution annealing followed by precipitation treatment (SA + PT), exhibited broadly
similar microstructural characteristics. In both cases, the nature and type of precipitates formed
were comparable. However, some variation was observed in the overall volume fraction of
precipitates, which may influence subsequent mechanical behavior and performance.

 Chapter 6: Conclusions and Future Work

6.1 Conclusions

This report advances the fundamental understanding of additive manufacturing for nuclear-grade
materials, building upon FY 2024 investigations with comprehensive process-structure-property
correlations for Fe-based alloys. The systematic comparison of laser powder bed fusion (LPBF)
and laser powder directed energy deposition (LP-DED) processes has yielded critical insights for
nuclear reactor material qualification.
 1. G92 Ferritic/Martensitic Steel Optimization: Advanced characterization revealed
 distinct microstructural evolution pathways between LPBF and LP-DED processing. As-
 deposited G92 exhibited dual-phase ferrite-martensite structures with nanoscale θ-carbide
 precipitation, with LP-DED achieving superior mechanical performance (>1600 MPa UTS
 vs ~1150 MPa for LPBF) while maintaining enhanced ductility (20-25% vs 3-18%). The
 systematic heat treatment study (tempering, normalizing, and combined treatments)
 demonstrated that the thermal processes produce more favorable precipitate distributions
 and reduced residual stress states, resulting in better strength-ductility balance and
 improved high-temperature performance retention.
 2. A709 Austenitic Stainless-Steel Process-Property Relationships: Comprehensive
 evaluation of solution annealing, precipitation hardening, and combined treatments
 revealed that both LPBF and LP-DED A709 exhibit orientation dependence and serrated
 yielding at high temperature.

This comprehensive investigation conclusively demonstrates the technical viability of additive
manufacturing for nuclear-grade Fe-based alloys, with both G92 ferritic/martensitic steel and A709
austenitic stainless steel achieving mechanical properties that meet or exceed conventional
processing benchmarks. The successful production of complex geometries with tailored
microstructures through controlled thermal histories validates AM as a transformative
manufacturing technology for nuclear applications.

 83

6.2 Future Work

Building upon the current findings, the following integrated research framework in coming years
addresses critical knowledge gaps and establishes the foundation for understanding both the
microstructural evolution and mechanical behavior of additively manufactured A709 and G-92.

Integrated Experimental Program (FY 2026)
Phase I: Full-Scale Component Fabrication:
Production of full-scale ASTM-compliant specimens for both LPBF and LP-DED will enable
comprehensive mechanical property databases. This scaling effort will validate laboratory findings
at industrially relevant dimensions and establish statistical confidence in AM processing
capabilities for nuclear-grade materials.
Phase II: High-Temperature Mechanical Performance:
Systematic evaluation of creep, creep-fatigue, and elevated temperature tensile behavior will be
conducted on both LPBF and LP-DED processed materials. This comprehensive testing program
will generate critical design data for nuclear operating conditions (550-650°C) and establish
performance envelopes for different AM processing routes.
Phase III: Process-Structure Optimization
Building on extensive FY 2024-2025 foundations, the LPBF program will continue with
comprehensive heat treatment protocol refinement through systematic parametric studies,
emphasizing microstructural stability and long-term performance optimization across the full
spectrum of processing conditions. In contrast, the LP-DED heat treatment optimization program,
initiated later in the project timeline, will operate under a strategically focused scope that leverages
the substantial knowledge base established through LPBF investigations. It will enable targeted
heat treatment strategies and streamlined mechanical testing protocols that capitalize on proven
optimization pathways while addressing LP-DED's unique processing characteristics.
Process parameter optimization studies will focus on controlling ferrite/martensite ratios in G92
and their correlation with mechanical performance. Similarly, the role of second phase in
mechanical properties in A709 will be examined. This effort will establish processing windows for
achieving target microstructures and mechanical properties while maintaining manufacturing
feasibility.

 84

Advanced Characterization and Modeling Integration: Post-mortem analysis utilizing multi-scale
characterization (optical, SEM, and TEM) will provide fundamental understanding of deformation
and failure mechanisms specific to AM-processed nuclear materials. These insights will inform
predictive models and guide process improvements for enhanced component reliability.
Concurrent CALPHAD-based thermodynamic modeling will optimize A709 composition
specifically for additive manufacturing, accounting for the unique solidification conditions and
thermal histories inherent to AM processes. This computational approach will accelerate alloy
development while minimizing experimental iterations.
