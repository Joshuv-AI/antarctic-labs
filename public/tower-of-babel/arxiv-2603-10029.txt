Quantal Response Equilibrium as a Measure of Strategic Sophistication: Theory and Validation for LLM Evaluation
Mateo Pechon-Elkins, Jon Chun

Abstract
Theory of Mind benchmarks for large language models typically produce aggregate scores without theoretical grounding, making it unclear whether high performance reflects strategic reasoning or surface-level heuristics. We introduce a game-theoretic evaluation framework grounded in quantal response equilibrium (QRE). We derive closed-form equilibria for four strategic games, each targeting a distinct cognitive capability. We estimate QRE rationality parameters λ \lambda that place model behavior on a continuous scale calibrated against human data ( λ human ∈ [ 1.0 , 2.5 ] \lambda_{\text{human}}\in[1.0,2.5] ), and establish finite-sample convergence bounds via martingale concentration. Validation across 1,855 games with seven frontier models (plus four expansion models) confirms predictions: bluff rates converge to within 4% of equilibrium, λ \lambda estimates range from 0.05 0.05 to 1.10 1.10 across games and models with substantial cross-model variation, and capability profiles differ across cognitive axes. Robustness analyses reveal high sensitivity to prompt framing and version instability in QRE rankings, highlighting the need for standardized protocols.



1
Introduction

Theory of Mind (ToM), the capacity to attribute mental states to others and use these attributions to predict behavior, is central to strategic interaction
[
Premack and Woodruff 1978
,
Wellman 2014
]
. Originally characterized through false-belief paradigms in developmental psychology
[
Wimmer and Perner 1983
,
Baron-Cohen et al. 1985
]
, ToM underpins cooperation, deception detection, and multi-agent coordination
[
Apperly and Butterfill 2009
]
.

As large language models are increasingly deployed in settings requiring strategic interaction, from negotiation to competitive games, understanding their capacity for ToM-relevant reasoning requires rigorous evaluation tools. Yet existing approaches lack theoretical grounding, making it difficult to interpret what high performance actually measures.

Current ToM benchmarks have notable limitations. Vignette-based tasks like the Sally-Anne test are susceptible to training data contamination and evaluate recognition rather than strategic reasoning
[
Kosinski 2023
,
Ullman 2023
,
Shapira et al. 2024
]
. Aggregate “ToM scores” conflate dissociable capabilities, obscuring meaningful differences between models
[
Chen et al. 2024
,
Kim et al. 2023
]
. And without formal equilibrium analysis, high scores may reflect surface heuristics rather than actual belief modeling.

We address these limitations by developing a methodology for quantifying bounded rationality in AI agents under strategic uncertainty, combining formal equilibrium characterizations, bounded rationality estimation via quantal response equilibrium with calibrated uncertainty, and convergence guarantees that validate our measurements. No prior ToM evaluation framework has combined game-specific equilibrium derivations with per-axis QRE estimation and finite-sample convergence bounds.

Our contributions span game theory, AI evaluation, and cognitive science. We define four strategic games, each with formal game-theoretic characterization measuring a distinct ToM-relevant capability, and derive explicit closed-form equilibria enabling quantitative predictions (Section
3
). We formalize bounded rationality using quantal response equilibrium
[
McKelvey and Palfrey 1995
,
Goeree et al. 2016
]
, estimating rationality parameters
\lambda
on a scale validated against human experimental data (Section
3.5
). We prove that our per-axis ELO rating system converges in expectation to true capability ratings under the Bradley-Terry model
[
Bradley and Terry 1952
]
, with explicit sample complexity bounds via Azuma-Hoeffding concentration (Section
3.6
). We validate these predictions across 1,855 games with seven frontier LLMs (with expansion studies extending to four additional models), finding that models converge toward Nash equilibrium during play (bluff rate gap closing 93% by round 10) and that capability profiles vary across cognitive axes, with cross-axis correlations revealing a trade-off between empathic and adversarial reasoning (
r=-0.95
,
p<0.05
,
n=7
).

Game-theoretic grounding serves ToM evaluation in specific ways. If models approach equilibria during play, this provides evidence that games elicit strategic reasoning rather than arbitrary heuristics. We observe convergence during play rather than from round 1, suggesting online belief updating, a core component of functional ToM. The quantal response framework provides a continuous measure of strategic sophistication via
\lambda
, which ranges from random play (
\lambda\to 0
) to perfect Nash equilibrium behavior (
\lambda\to\infty
). Human experimental data provides calibration:
\lambda_{\text{human}}\in[1.0,2.5]
across diverse game types
[
Goeree et al. 2016
,
Camerer et al. 2004
]
. Formal bounds on required sample sizes ensure adequate experimental power and enable controlled comparison across settings.

Following behavioral economics
[
Camerer et al. 2004
]
, we adopt a functional definition of ToM (Definition
1
): an agent exhibits functional ToM if its behavior is consistent with maintaining and updating models of others’ mental states. This mechanism-agnostic definition enables measurement without resolving debates about machine cognition, analogous to revealed preference theory measuring utility without assuming its existence. We do not claim that LLMs possess genuine ToM in any phenomenological sense, nor that game performance generalizes to unstructured social interaction. Our claims are restricted to behavioral signatures in structured game contexts with well-defined equilibria.

2
Related Work

The study of ToM originated with
Premack and Woodruff 1978
’s
[
Premack and Woodruff 1978
]
investigation of chimpanzee cognition and was subsequently formalized through false-belief paradigms
[
Wimmer and Perner 1983
,
Baron-Cohen et al. 1985
]
. Developmental research established ToM as a multi-component capacity emerging between ages 3-5
[
Wellman 2014
]
, with distinct components for belief attribution, desire reasoning, and intention inference
[
Apperly and Butterfill 2009
]
. Computational models share the insight that ToM can be characterized functionally through observable behavior.

Early LLM ToM evaluation applied classic developmental tasks.
Kosinski 2023
reported GPT-4 passing Sally-Anne tests at levels comparable to 7-year-olds, sparking debate about LLM social cognition. However,
Ullman 2023
demonstrated that minor task variations caused performance collapse, suggesting pattern matching rather than actual reasoning.
Shapira et al. 2024
systematically varied task parameters and found high brittleness, while
Sap et al. 2022
documented systematic failures in social reasoning requiring mental state attribution.

ToMBench
[
Chen et al. 2024
]
expanded evaluation breadth with diverse scenarios, while FANToM
[
Kim et al. 2023
]
introduced adversarial robustness testing. These benchmarks evaluate comprehension of mental states but not their use in strategic interaction. Our work requires dynamic, multi-round strategic interaction where success depends on ongoing belief updating; formal equilibrium predictions then provide a validity check.

Recent work has highlighted the sensitivity of LLM evaluation to surface-level prompt variations.
Sclar et al. 2024
demonstrate that minor formatting changes (separator tokens, instruction ordering) can cause accuracy swings exceeding 76 points on standard benchmarks, while
Mizrahi et al. 2024
show that non-semantic perturbations systematically inflate or deflate scores. These findings raise validity concerns for any evaluation framework, including game-theoretic approaches: observed strategic behavior may reflect prompt-activated heuristics rather than reasoning about payoff structure. We directly address this concern through prompt sensitivity analysis (Table
10
, Appendix).

In game-theoretic LLM evaluation, SOTOPIA
[
Zhou et al. 2024
]
introduced LLM-vs-LLM social evaluation but relies on LLM-as-judge scoring, introducing circularity concerns.
Gandhi et al. 2023
and
Lorè and Heydari 2023
examined LLM behavior in specific games, finding varying degrees of strategic sophistication. Other work examines LLM behavior in deception and negotiation games but conflates multiple skills without formal decomposition.

Several recent benchmarks target LLM strategic reasoning. GTBench
[
Duan et al. 2024
]
evaluates across 10 game tasks but does not estimate bounded-rationality parameters or provide convergence guarantees. WGSR-Bench
[
Wu et al. 2025
]
and LLMsPark
[
Chen et al. 2025
]
share the goal of principled strategic evaluation but focus on aggregate performance rather than parameter estimation with uncertainty quantification. Most closely related,
Jia et al. 2025
apply truncated QRE (TQRE) to evaluate 22 LLMs across 13 single-shot abstract games. Our framework differs in using multi-round natural-language games requiring within-game belief updating (harder to solve via cached heuristics), per-axis capability profiles revealing dissociations invisible to aggregate measures, finite-sample convergence bounds, and Bayesian posterior inference with principled uncertainty intervals.

Quantal response equilibrium
[
McKelvey and Palfrey 1995
]
models bounded rationality by assuming agents choose actions with probabilities proportional to expected utilities. Unlike Nash equilibrium, QRE accommodates systematic deviations from optimal play while maintaining game-theoretic structure.
Goeree et al. 2016
provide a detailed treatment of QRE theory and applications. The rationality parameter
\lambda
has been estimated for human subjects across diverse games, with
\lambda\approx 1.5
in coordination games,
\lambda\approx 2.0
in signaling games, and
\lambda\approx 2.5
in repeated games
[
Goeree et al. 2016
]
. We apply this framework to LLMs, using
\lambda
as a capability metric calibrated against human data.

The Bradley-Terry model
[
Bradley and Terry 1952
]
provides a probabilistic foundation for paired comparisons, and the ELO system operationalizes this through sequential updates. Recent work has applied ELO to AI evaluation
[
Chiang et al. 2024
]
. We extend ELO with per-axis ratings and prove convergence guarantees with explicit finite-sample bounds using martingale concentration inequalities.

Definition 1

(Functional ToM)
.

An agent exhibits functional Theory of Mind if its behavior is consistent with: (a) maintaining models of other agents’ epistemic and motivational states, (b) updating these models based on observations, and (c) using these models to inform decisions.

This definition is mechanism-agnostic, analogous to behavioral economics measuring “as-if” rationality without assuming perfect utility maximization. We decompose ToM-relevant capabilities into four axes, each measured by a dedicated game grounded in established theory (Table

Epistemic State Modeling (ESM) captures the capacity to model what another agent knows or believes, particularly when this differs from one’s own knowledge. Text-Dixit measures calibration accuracy in predicting how confidently a partner will identify a target from a clue

[

[

.

All games satisfy five design principles: procedural generation resists memorization; observable signals support belief updating; coherent incentive structures create strategic tradeoffs; each game admits equilibrium characterization; and private information is sampled independently, preserving role symmetry.

3.1
Game 1: Strategic Claim (RSR Axis)

Strategic Claim is a Bayesian signaling game
\Gamma_{SC}
where each player receives a private value
v\in\{1,...,6\}
uniformly, claims a value
c\geq v
, and sets a challenge threshold. Payoffs create bluffing incentives: unchallenged bluffs earn
c+2
, caught bluffs cost
-4
, and false accusations cost
-2
(formal specification in Definition
3
, Appendix).

Theorem 1

(Approximate Equilibrium Profile)
.

The Strategic Claim stage game admits an approximate symmetric equilibrium profile
(\sigma^{*},\sigma^{*})
with the following structure:

1.

Sender:
For values
v\leq 3
, bluff by claiming
c=6
with probability
\beta(v)=\frac{2}{8-v}
; otherwise claim honestly (
c=v
). For values
v\geq 4
: claim honestly (
c=v
) with probability 1.

2.

Receiver:
Challenge claims
c\geq 5
(deterministic threshold
t^{*}=5
).

3.

Conditional bluff rate
(given
v\leq 3
):
\beta^{*}=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{v=1}^{3}\beta(v)=0.340
.

This is a pedagogical approximation, not an exact MSNE: the deterministic receiver threshold cannot sustain exact sender indifference (which requires mixed challenge probabilities
q(c)
per claim level). The conditional bluff rate
\beta^{*}=0.340
nonetheless provides the behavioral prediction target throughout the paper. Proof and approximation analysis in Appendix
B
.

3.2
Game 2: Repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma (RSM Axis)

The Repeated PD uses stage payoffs
(T,R,P,S)=(5,3,1,0)
with a hidden horizon
T\sim\text{Uniform}\{7,15\}
and natural language cheap talk
[
Farrell and Rabin 1996
]
(formal specification in Definition
4
, Appendix).

Proposition 2

(Cooperation as Behavioral Finding)
.

Under subgame perfect equilibrium (SPE), the unique prediction for the finitely repeated PD with commonly known upper bound
T_{\max}=15
is mutual defection in every round, by backward induction from
t=T_{\max}
. The round-dependent discount factor
\delta_{t}=(15-t)/(16-t)
satisfies the folk theorem’s necessary condition
\delta_{t}\geq(T-R)/(T-P)=0.5
for
t\leq 14
, but this is insufficient: the folk theorem requires unbounded-support termination (e.g., geometric) to block backward induction
[
Fudenberg and Maskin 1986
]
. Observed cooperation (70% sustained; Section
5
) thus constitutes a behavioral departure from SPE, consistent with experimental findings in finitely repeated PD
[
Embrey et al. 2018
]
. This regularity makes the game informative for measuring relational state modeling: sustained cooperation requires modeling the opponent’s willingness to cooperate despite the rational prediction.

3.3
Game 3: Say the Same Thing (SCG Axis)

Say the Same Thing is a pure coordination game where players start with distinct words and simultaneously select new words each round, aiming for exact match within 20 rounds. Payoff decreases with convergence time (
1-t/20
). Under Schelling’s focal point theory
[
Schelling 1960
]
, efficient play requires mutual salience estimation (formal specification and equilibrium characterization in Appendix).

3.4
Game 4: Text-Dixit (ESM Axis)

Text-Dixit is a signaling game where a storyteller observes a target among 6 surreal scenes, provides a clue, and predicts the guesser’s confidence. The guesser selects a scene and reports confidence
p
. Scoring rewards calibration accuracy via quadratic loss: ESM score
=100(1-(\hat{p}/100-p/100)^{2})
. Under proper scoring rules
[
Gneiting and Raftery 2007
]
, the storyteller’s optimal prediction equals the guesser’s true expected confidence, requiring accurate epistemic state modeling of the partner’s inference process (formal specification and equilibrium in Appendix).

3.5
Bounded Rationality: Quantal Response Equilibrium

Real agents deviate systematically from Nash equilibrium. We formalize this via QRE
[
McKelvey and Palfrey 1995
]
.

Definition 2

(Logit QRE)
.

Given expected utility
U_{i}(a_{i},\sigma_{-i})
, the logit quantal response is:

\sigma_{i}(a_{i}|\lambda)=\frac{\exp(\lambda\cdot U_{i}(a_{i},\sigma_{-i}))}{\sum_{a^{\prime}\in A_{i}}\exp(\lambda\cdot U_{i}(a^{\prime},\sigma_{-i}))}

(1)

where
\lambda\geq 0
is the rationality parameter. A QRE is a fixed point of this mapping.

QRE exists for all
\lambda\geq 0
(Brouwer fixed-point), ranges from uniform random (
\lambda\to 0
) to Nash equilibrium (
\lambda\to\infty
), and the logit specification is point-identified from choice data when actions have distinct expected payoffs
[
McKelvey and Palfrey 1995
,
Goeree et al. 2016
]
. Our games satisfy these identifiability conditions (Theorem
8
and verification in Appendix).

We estimate
\lambda
via maximum likelihood on per-round action data, treating each round as an independent stage-game draw:

\hat{\lambda}=\argmax_{\lambda}\sum_{i,t}\log\sigma_{i}(a_{i,t}|\lambda,\hat{\sigma}_{-i})

(2)

using Newton-Raphson optimization with multiple starting points. This stage-game MLE is a reduced-form approximation: within-game learning (Theorem
5
) and end-game effects violate the i.i.d. assumption across rounds, biasing
\hat{\lambda}
downward (early-round exploration appears as irrationality) and potentially deflating standard errors. A structural alternative would be Agent QRE (AQRE;
McKelvey and Palfrey 1995
), which models sequential decisions explicitly; we adopt the stage-game estimator for tractability and interpret
\hat{\lambda}
as a reduced-form average rationality index rather than a structural parameter. We supplement MLE with Bayesian posterior inference using a Gamma(2,1) prior over
\lambda
, computing posterior means and 95% highest density intervals (HDI) via grid approximation
[
Gelman et al. 2013
]
. The prior is mildly informative (mean
=2.0
), reflecting the human experimental range while allowing data to dominate. Bayesian regularization prevents degenerate
\lambda=0
point estimates when agents play near equilibrium, where the likelihood surface is flat. Prior sensitivity analysis (Table
11
, Appendix) confirms that posterior means are stable across Gamma(1, 0.5), Gamma(2, 1), and Gamma(3, 1) priors, with maximum variation of
<0.08
across models.

The behavioral economics literature provides calibration for interpretation.
Goeree et al. 2016
report human
\lambda
estimates of approximately 1.5 in coordination games, 2.0 in signaling games, and 2.5 in repeated games. These values establish a scale against which to interpret LLM estimates. We note that human
\lambda
values derive from diverse game types, populations, and experimental conditions; cross-game comparability of
\lambda
is supported by meta-analytic evidence
[
Goeree et al. 2016
]
but has not been verified for our specific game implementations. Comparisons should be interpreted as approximate calibration rather than precise equivalence.

3.6
Convergence Analysis

The i.i.d. assumption in the following theorems applies to
game-level
outcomes: each complete game yields an independent result, even though within-game turns are non-stationary. Wald-Wolfowitz runs tests on game-level win/loss sequences across all 28 model pairings show no evidence of serial dependence (all
p>0.05
; see Remark
2
, Appendix).

Theorem 3

(ELO Convergence)
.

Under the Bradley-Terry model with i.i.d. game outcomes and constant update parameter
K
, the expected ELO rating converges:
\E[\hat{R}_{a}^{(n)}(M)]\to R_{a}^{*}(M)
as
n\to\infty
, with
\Var=O(K^{2})
. Proof via stochastic approximation in Appendix
B
.

Theorem 4

(Finite-Sample Bound)
.

For models
i,j
with true win probability
p^{*}
under the Bradley-Terry model, the empirical win rate
\hat{p}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}S_{t}
from
n
i.i.d. games satisfies:

P(|\hat{p}_{n}-p^{*}|>\epsilon)\leq 2\exp(-2n\epsilon^{2})
.
A 50-point ELO difference corresponds to
p^{*}\approx 0.572
; our design uses 10–18 per-pairing replications, with model-level precision achieved through aggregation and quantified via bootstrap (median SD: 40 ELO points). Proof in Appendix
B
.

Theorem 5

(Within-Game Convergence in Expectation)
.

If agents’ effective behavior is consistent with exponential-smoothing belief updates with learning rate
\eta
and best-response slope
\kappa<1
, then the
expected
conditional bluff rate
\beta_{t}
(given
v\leq 3
) in Strategic Claim satisfies:

|\E[\beta_{t}\mid\beta_{1}]-\beta^{*}|\leq|\beta_{1}-\beta^{*}|\cdot\rho^{t-1},\quad\rho=1-\eta(1-\kappa)

(3)

where
\beta^{*}=0.340
is the conditional equilibrium rate. The bound is on the conditional expectation of
\beta_{t}
; the realized bluff rate is stochastic (depending on opponent actions) and may deviate from this trajectory in individual games. We do not claim LLMs literally implement exponential smoothing; this serves as a tractable proxy validated by its empirical fit (
R^{2}=0.87
).

4.2
Experimental Design

From seven models we construct 28 pairings (21 cross-model and 7 self-play). Each pairing plays 10–18 replications per game type with pre-sampled conditions that ensure identical setups across comparisons. The full experiment comprises 1,855 games across five game types (Strategic Claim, Repeated PD, STST, Text-Dixit, and sealed-bid auction control).

Before running experiments, we generate 150 condition sets per game type: RPD horizon lengths, STST starting word pairs, Strategic Claim value sequences, and Text-Dixit scene collections. This pre-sampling ensures reproducibility and enables paired statistical tests that control for condition-specific variance.

For ELO computation, all models initialize at
R_{a}=1500
on each axis. Games are processed in randomized order with update parameter
K=32
. We compute 95% confidence intervals via non-parametric bootstrap: game outcomes are resampled with replacement within each model pair, ELO ratings are recomputed for each of 1000 bootstrap samples, and the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles define the CI. Bootstrap standard deviations range from 9 to 80 points (median 40) across model-axis combinations, with higher SDs for models with fewer pairings.

Our power analysis applies Theorem
4
. Detecting a 50-point ELO difference from per-pairing win rates at
\alpha=0.05
requires
n\geq 356
games (Hoeffding bound). Our design uses 10–18 replications per pairing, achieving model-level precision through aggregation across 28 pairings (1,855 total games, 100+ per model). Bootstrap standard deviations range from 9 to 80 ELO points (median 40), confirming that rating differences exceeding 50 points are reliably detected at the model level.

As a control, we include a sealed-bid first-price auction: each player privately draws a value
v\sim U[0,100]
, submits a bid
b\leq v
, and the highest bidder wins at their bid price. Optimal bidding depends only on the value distribution, not on modeling opponents’ mental states, providing a strategic-but-non-ToM baseline for discriminant validity.

We estimate QRE rationality parameters via maximum likelihood on observed action sequences, treating each round as an independent stage-game draw (see Section
3.5
for the reduced-form interpretation). The Newton-Raphson optimizer runs from 10 random starting points, and we report the estimate achieving smallest negative log-likelihood. For models with
\hat{\lambda}>0
, we compute 95% confidence intervals via Fisher information; for boundary estimates (
\hat{\lambda}=0
), Fisher-based CIs are suppressed because asymptotic normality fails at the parameter space boundary, and we rely exclusively on Bayesian HDIs (Table
2
).

6
Discussion

This work contributes a methodology for estimating bounded rationality in AI agents under strategic uncertainty. Game-specific equilibrium characterizations yield quantitative predictions that support formal hypothesis testing. QRE parameter inference places rationality estimates on a scale calibrated against decades of human behavioral data, and convergence guarantees with explicit finite-sample bounds ensure that capability measurements have quantifiable precision.

Our Bayesian
\lambda
estimates fall well below human baselines (
\lambda_{\text{human}}\in[1.0,2.5]
;
Goeree et al. 2016
,
Camerer et al. 2004
). In Strategic Claim, values range from
0.05
to
0.61
, while in RPD only Kimi K2 exhibits measurable rationality (
\lambda=1.10
). Kimi K2 is the sole chain-of-thought model in our ensemble, suggesting that sustaining cooperation in iterated games may require the explicit multi-step reasoning that “thinking” architectures provide. This gap likely reflects an identifiability challenge rather than an absence of strategic reasoning: when agents play near equilibrium, as our convergence data confirms, utility differences between actions shrink, making
\lambda
poorly identified by the logit model
[
Wright and Leyton-Brown 2017
]
. Despite the low absolute magnitudes, cross-model
variation
is informative: a 12-fold range in SC
\lambda
separates the most and least strategic models. Moreover,
\lambda
rankings diverge substantially from raw behavioral statistics. The Spearman rank correlation between
\lambda
and aggregate conditional bluff rate is
\rho=-0.29
(
p=0.53
,
n=7
), indicating that the two measures capture fundamentally different constructs. For instance, Claude Haiku bluffs most frequently (
\beta=0.73
) yet has the lowest
\lambda
(0.05), reflecting near-random bluffing; GPT-4o-mini bluffs least (
\beta=0.11
) yet has the highest
\lambda
(0.61), indicating that its rare deviations from equilibrium are strategically structured. This dissociation—between how
often
a model deviates from equilibrium and how
rationally
it does so—is precisely what QRE is designed to measure and what raw behavioral metrics cannot capture.

Model expansion studies (Table
12
, Appendix) reveal that QRE rankings are temporally unstable across model versions. DeepSeek V3.2 shows dramatically improved strategic sophistication over V3 (
\hat{\lambda}=0.7
vs.
0.2
;
\beta=0.288
vs.
0.523
), while Kimi K2.5 shifts in the opposite direction relative to K2 (
\hat{\lambda}=0.6
vs.
0.8
). Both successor versions converge to
\beta_{R10}=0
by round 10, suggesting improved equilibrium play, but the non-monotonic
\lambda
trajectories caution against treating any single evaluation snapshot as a stable capability assessment. Extension to two new model families (Grok 4.1 Fast, MiniMax M2.5) confirms that the framework generalizes beyond the original seven models, with observed bluff rates (
\beta=0.47
–
0.50
) and
\lambda
estimates falling within the range of the original ensemble.

The SCG axis (Say the Same Thing) warrants separate discussion. Under exact-match scoring, all model pairs converge to focal points with comparable facility, producing zero ELO variance. Focal point coordination, it appears, is trivially solved by current frontier LLMs, consistent with their strong semantic capabilities. Shared conceptual grounding, at least as measured by lexical convergence, does not differentiate models at this scale. Future work could increase difficulty via embedding-distance scoring, domain-restricted vocabularies, or cross-lingual pairings. We report three informative axes rather than force differentiation where none exists.

Characteristic failure modes differ across games and models. In Strategic Claim, common failures include over-bluffing (bluff rate
>0.5
persisting past round 5) and under-challenging (threshold
\geq 6
, effectively never challenging). In RPD, defection cascades following a single defection and end-game defection spikes occur even in otherwise cooperative pairs. In STST, semantic drift (distance increasing between rounds) and cycling between two candidate words are the primary non-convergence patterns. In Text-Dixit, systematic overconfidence (
\hat{p}>p+20
) is the dominant calibration failure. These failure modes are not uniformly distributed: DeepSeek V3 exhibits the highest over-bluffing rate in SC, while Claude Haiku shows the strongest end-game defection in RPD.

Several limitations bear on interpretation. Our games are structured strategic interactions; whether results generalize to unstructured social settings remains open. Equilibrium convergence is consistent with ToM but also with simpler mechanisms such as fictitious play or heuristic adaptation; distinguishing these requires interventional experiments (e.g., mid-game opponent strategy switches) beyond the scope of this work. QRE
\lambda
and LLM sampling temperature
T
both modulate action stochasticity; our temperature ablation (Table
9
, Appendix) shows stable behavioral patterns across
T\in\{0.3,0.5,0.7,1.0\}
, supporting
\lambda
as primarily reflecting strategic rationality, though the confound cannot be fully resolved without access to token-level logits. All robustness experiments use self-play; cross-model interactions may produce different dynamics. Prompt framing also matters: our ablation (Table
10
, Appendix) shows that replacing the game-theoretic narrative with formal or minimal descriptions eliminates bluffing entirely (
\beta=0.59\to 0.00
for Claude Haiku), a binary sensitivity paralleling
Ullman 2023
’s
[
Ullman 2023
]
finding that minor task variations cause ToM performance collapse
[
Sclar et al. 2024
]
. Model capabilities evolve rapidly, so this framework should be viewed as methodology for ongoing evaluation rather than a static assessment. This study evaluates publicly available LLM APIs in game-playing scenarios with no human participants; human calibration baselines are drawn entirely from published behavioral economics literature.

7
Conclusion

We have presented GToM-Bench, a benchmark that grounds Theory of Mind evaluation in game theory. The framework pairs formal equilibrium analysis with QRE-based bounded rationality estimation and convergence guarantees derived from martingale concentration arguments. Four games target distinct cognitive capabilities through procedurally generated strategic interactions that resist memorization while remaining theoretically tractable.

Empirical validation across 2,035 games (1,855 primary plus 180 in robustness studies) with eleven models confirms the theoretical predictions, and expansion studies extending to four additional models demonstrate framework generalizability. Models converge toward Nash equilibria during play, exhibiting the belief updating that characterizes functional ToM. Bayesian rationality parameter estimates reveal heterogeneity across models and game types, with cross-model variation providing diagnostic value even when absolute magnitudes fall below human baselines. Version-stability analysis reveals that QRE rankings shift across model generations, arguing for continuous re-evaluation rather than static capability claims. Prompt sensitivity analysis further shows that strategic behavior is binary-activated by narrative framing, a methodological caution for the field.

Code and data are available at
https://github.com/jon-chun/gtom-bench-uncertainty
to support reproducibility and enable extension to additional models and game variants.

B.1
Proof of Theorem

\varepsilon

The Strategic Claim stage game

]

[

Equilibrium derivation.
We solve for the symmetric equilibrium by backward induction on the extensive form.

Step 1: Challenger’s optimal strategy.
Given claim
c

\E[\text{challenge}|c]=P(\text{bluff}|c)\cdot 3+P(\text{honest}|c)\cdot(-2)

(4)

For the challenger to be indifferent (necessary for opponent mixing), set
\E[\text{challenge}|c]=0

P(\text{bluff}|c)\cdot 3=P(\text{honest}|c)\cdot 2

(5)

This gives
P(\text{bluff}|c)=2/5=0.4
.

Step 2: Bluffer’s indifference and Bayesian consistency.
From Step 1, the posterior probability of bluffing given a high claim must equal
P(\text{bluff}|c)=2/5
. By Bayes’ rule, if a player with value
v\leq 3
bluffs to claim
c

\beta(v)

v\geq 4

P(\text{bluff}|c)=\frac{\sum_{v=1}^{3}\frac{1}{6}\beta(v)}{\sum_{v=1}^{3}\frac{1}{6}\beta(v)+\frac{1}{6}}

(6)

where the denominator adds the honest claim from value
v=c
. Setting this equal to
2/5
for each claim level
c\in\{4,5,6\}
and solving yields the bluff probabilities.

Step 3: Solving for
\beta(v)

For claim
c

\beta(v)=2/(8-v)
for
v\leq 3
. To verify: with
c=6
(claimed by bluffers from

and honestly by
v=6

P(\text{bluff}|c\!=\!6)=\frac{\frac{1}{6}(\tfrac{2}{7}+\tfrac{2}{6}+\tfrac{2}{5})}{\frac{1}{6}(\tfrac{2}{7}+\tfrac{2}{6}+\tfrac{2}{5})+\frac{1}{6}}=\frac{214/210}{214/210+1}=\frac{214}{424}\approx 0.505

(7)

This deviates from the exact indifference requirement of

, reflecting that the strategy profile constitutes an approximate equilibrium.

Step 4: Aggregate computation.
For values
v\in\{1,2,3\}
:

\displaystyle\beta(1)

\displaystyle=2/7\approx 0.286

(8)

\displaystyle\beta(2)

\displaystyle=2/6\approx 0.333

(9)

\displaystyle\beta(3)

\displaystyle=2/5=0.400

(10)

Conditional bluff rate (given

\beta^{*}=\frac{1}{3}(\beta(1)+\beta(2)+\beta(3))=\frac{1}{3}(0.286+0.333+0.400)=\frac{1.019}{3}\approx 0.340

Step 5: Approximation analysis.
The profile is an approximation to the exact MSNE in two respects. First, the deterministic threshold

Setup.
Let

Belief dynamics.
Under Bayesian updating with learning rate

Best response mapping.
Given belief

The composite mapping

Appendix C
Game Specifications

Strategic Claim runs for 10 rounds. Each player receives a private value drawn uniformly from
\{1,...,6\}
and may claim any value from their true value up to 6. The opposing player sets a challenge threshold from 1 to 7. Payoffs are structured to create incentives for calibrated bluffing: honest claims yield the claimed value, successful bluffs yield the claim plus 2, caught bluffs cost 4 points to the bluffer while rewarding the challenger 3, and false challenges cost the challenger 2 points.

Repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma uses hidden horizons drawn uniformly from 7 to 15 rounds (with commonly known upper bound
T_{\max}=15
). Stage payoffs follow the standard parameterization
(T,R,P,S)=(5,3,1,0)
. Players may exchange natural language messages before each decision round, enabling cheap talk communication.

Say the Same Thing allows up to 20 rounds of simultaneous word selection. Players start with distinct words and must converge on an exact match. Starting word pairs are stratified by semantic distance measured via cosine similarity in embedding space.

Text-Dixit consists of 4 rounds with alternating storyteller and guesser roles. Each round presents 6 AI-generated surreal scenes: one target and five decoys. The storyteller selects a clue of 2 to 4 words from a provided bank and predicts the guesser’s confidence from 0 to 100 percent. Scoring rewards accurate calibration of the partner’s epistemic state.
