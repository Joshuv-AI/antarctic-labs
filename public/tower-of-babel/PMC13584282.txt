Multi-trajectory patterns of ADL, cognition, and depression with fall risk: evidence from a longitudinal study in China
Authors: Qiqi Lv, Durong Chen, Hao Zhu, Ziqi Li, Yao Qin, Chuchu Zheng, Jing Cui, Hongmei Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFalls are a common occurrence among older adults and can pose serious health and financial burdens. Several studies have shown that a decline of ADL, cognitive decline, and depression are all associated with an increased risk of falls in older adults. However, there is currently a lack of research examining these three factors in conjunction and their combined effect on the risk of falls. Our study aims to examine the longitudinal associations between distinct trajectories of activities of daily living, cognition, and psychological status among adults and their risk of falls.MethodsWe conducted a longitudinal analysis using data from five waves (2011–2020) of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). A group-based multiple trajectory model (GBMTM) was employed to identify latent ADL-cognitive-Depression trajectories among 2,938 individuals aged 60 years. Multivariable logistic regression models were then used to assess the odds of experiencing falls between 2018 and 2020 across the identified trajectory groups, with sequential adjustment for demographics, health behaviors, comorbidities, and functional limitations.ResultsGBMTM identified five distinct trajectory subtypes: “Pervasive Decline (Severe-ADL-Decline)” (n = 378, 12.87%), “Pervasive Decline (Mild-ADL-Decline)” (n = 449, 15.28%), “Cognitive - Decline & Depressed” (n = 338, 11.50%), “Mild ADL Decline” (n = 351, 11.95%) and “Normal” (n = 1422, 48.40%). Compared to the “Normal” group, all other trajectory groups showed significantly elevated risks of falls in the fully adjusted model. The highest risk was observed in the “Pervasive Decline (Severe-ADL-Decline)” group (OR = 2.83, 95%CI: 2.12–3.77), followed by the “Pervasive Decline (Mild-ADL-Decline)” (OR = 2.13, 95%CI: 1.62–2.80), “Mild ADL Decline” (OR = 1.97, 95%CI: 1.47–2.63), and “Cognitive Decline & Depressed” (OR = 1.47, 95%CI: 1.07–2.02) groups.ConclusionsFive distinct trajectory groups of ADL, cognitive function, and depressive symptoms were identified. These groups exhibited significant differences in fall risk, with the “Pervasive Decline (Severe-ADL-Decline)” group presenting the highest risk, necessitating multidimensional comprehensive interventions for fall prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-07807-x.

Introduction

As the second leading cause of accidental injury deaths among the elderly [1], falls have become a significant global public health issue [2], which exhibits extremely high incidence rate in the elderly population. Falls not only lead to mortality among the elderly but also frequently cause severe injuries such as hip fractures and traumatic brain injuries [3], increasing the healthcare costs and economic burdens [4]. Approximately 28% to 32% of community-dwelling adults aged 65 and older experience at least one fall annually, a proportion which increases significantly with age and reaches 50% among those aged 80 and older [3] Moreover, 22.6% elderly fall victims experience recurrent falls within the following years [5]. Given the profound implications for individual health and quality of life, the prevention and mitigation of falls and fall-related injuries among older adults constitutes a paramount objective in healthcare.

A substantial body of evidence has independently established the links between declines in activities of daily living (ADL), cognitive impairment and depressive symptoms with an increased risk of falls among older adults. Specifically, ADL represents the core capability for maintaining basic self-care and forms the foundation of independent living. With advancing age, declining ADL can lead to muscle weakness and poor balance, elevating fall risk. Previous studies have confirmed the association between falls and loss of ADL [6–9]. Zhang et al. further identified an inverted U-shaped association between ADL and injurious falls among patients in the domestic group through a multicenter cross-sectional study [10]. Cognitive decline, which is also highly prevalent among older adults, compromises executive function and visuospatial abilities, thereby impairing safe ambulation and hazard avoidance. And multiple cross-sectional studies have shown that individuals with poor cognitive function are at a higher risk of falls [11–13]. Fallacii et al. found that cognitive impairment—particularly in memory and executive function domains—may contribute to recurrent falls [14]. Depression is a prevalent mental disorder characterized by persistent sadness, anhedonia, and fatigue [15]. Many fall risk factors, including cognitive impairment, slowed gait, poor balance, prolonged reaction time, and frailty, are linked to depressive symptoms [16]. Most studies indicated that depressive symptom is a significant risk factor for falls [17, 18].

In fact, ADL, cognitive function, and depressive symptoms do not act in isolation regarding their influence on fall risk. Research has demonstrated depressive symptoms and ADL impairment jointly influence the risk of fall [19–22]. Previous studies have focused on baseline factors in assessing fall risk, while in reality, ADL, cognitive ability, and depressive states all exhibit dynamic changes over time. Moreover, significant heterogeneity exists in these changes across individuals. Uncovering these distinct patterns is fundamental for identifying vulnerable subgroups and developing tailored intervention strategies of falls.

To understand the differential trajectories of ADL, cognition and depressive, we conducted a longitudinal analysis using the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) database, employing standardized assessments in older Chinese adults. Furthermore, the study investigated the role of differential trajectories in fall risk.

Study design and methods

Study participants

This study utilized data from five waves of the CHARLS, which is a representative longitudinal cohort study of mainland China’s population aged 45 years and older, capturing a broad range of information related to their health, socioeconomic status, social engagement, and psychological well-being [23]. The primary objective of CHARLS is to conduct a high-quality public database. The study was approved by the Peking University Biomedical Ethics Review Committee, and all participants provided written informed consent.

This study included individuals aged 60 or older at baseline, with baseline assessments conducted in either 2011 (Wave 1) or 2013 (Wave 2). The period from baseline to 2018 (Wave 4) was used to establish the multi-trajectory of ADL, cognition and psychological status. Additionally, we utilized data from Wave 4 (2018) to Wave 5 (2020) to investigate the relationship between multi-trajectory and falls. We excluded participants who met one or more of the following criteria: (1) age less than 60 years or had missing data on baseline age; (2) had incomplete baseline information of ADL, cognitive scores, and the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CESD-10); (3) had incomplete longitudinal data on ADL, cognitive scores, and the CESD-10 from baseline to Wave 4 (2018) (4) did not have data about falls in Wave 5 (2020). Finally, a total of 2,938 participants were finally included in the study. The detailed design process is depicted in Fig. 1.

Assessment of activities of daily living, cognition function and depression

The Katz Activities of Daily Living Scale (ADL) was used to assess activities of daily living [24]. In CHARLS, the assessment of ADL includes eating, dressing, bathing, transferring from a chair to a bed, using the toilet, and defecating. The total score ranges from 0 to 6 points, with higher scores denoting poorer daily living abilities.

In this study, cognition function was measured using four cognitive domains: orientation, calculation, memory, and drawing. Orientation and calculation were assessed through Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status. The orientation section required participants to state the current year, month, date, day of the week, and season, with a maximum score of 5 (one point per correct item). The calculation test entailed performing five successive subtractions of 7 from 100, with each correct subtraction scoring one point (max 5 points). Memory was assessed via immediate and delayed word recall tests, with a maximum score of 20 (1 point per word). Drawing ability was assessed through the copying of two overlapping pentagons, with one point given for an accurately reproduced figure [25]. The global cognitive score was defined as the combined total of the orientation, calculation, memory, and drawing scores, with values ranging from 0 to 31. Higher values indicate better cognitive status.

Depression was assessed by the abbreviated CESD-10, a widely used standardized instrument for screening depressive symptoms [26]. The CESD-10 employs a 4-point scale for each item, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 30. A score exceeding 10 indicates depressive symptoms. It has good reliability and validity in the Chinese population [27].

Assessment of falls

Falls were defined as the occurrence of at least one fall event between Wave 5 (2020) and the last two years, which was confirmed by asking the standardized question: “Have you experienced any falls since your last two years?” [28–31]. Responses were recorded as a binary variable (yes/no) according to the participants’ experiences.

Covariates

Demographic variables included age, gender, residence location, education level, and marital status. Behavior factors encompassed smoking status, alcohol consumption, and sleep duration. The number of chronic conditions was identified through self-reported physician-diagnosed conditions.

Statistical analysis

Group-Based Multi-Trajectory Model (GBMTM) was employed to identify distinct trajectories of ADL, cognition, and depression trajectories from 2011 to 2018. GBMTM is an extension of the Group-Based Trajectory Model, and identifies latent clusters of individuals following similar trajectories across multiple indicators [32–34]. The detailed information about GBMTM can be found in supplementary materials. The optimal model was selected based on the minimization of both the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) and Sample Size Corrected Bayesian Information Criterion (SSBIC), with the additional criteria that the Average Posterior Probability (AvePP) was > 0.7 and the predicted probability of group membership was > 10%.

Multivariable logistic regression was used to estimate the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) for the associations between different trajectories and falls. Three nested logistic regression models was built. Model 1 had no adjustment, including only trajectory groups. Model 2 extended Model 1 by further adjusting for demographic variables (age, sex, education, marital status, residence). Building upon Model 2, we developed Model 3, which was further adjusted for multimorbidity and behavioral factors (smoking, drinking, sleep duration, daytime napping). To assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted a sensitivity analysis (Model 4) based on Model 3. In Model 4, physical function limitations (including the ability to lift 5 kg [35], stand up from a chair [36, 37], and walk 1 km [38]), prior fall history [37], and the physical activity [39, 40] were additionally included. Missing values in the covariates were handled using chained multiple imputation.

R software (version 4.4.3, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) was adopted. All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

ADL-cognitive-depression trajectory groups

Based on the BIC, SSBIC, AvePP, probability of group membership values, five-trajectory groups were identified (Supplementary Materials Table S2). The first trajectory group was characterised by severe decline of ADL, cognitive and depression (Pervasive Decline (Severe-ADL-Decline) group, n = 378, 12.87%). Compared to the first trajectory group, individuals in the second trajectory group exhibited a mild decline in ADL function over time (Pervasive Decline (Mild-ADL-Decline) group, n = 449, 15.28%). The third group exhibited a combined profile of cognitive decline and depression (Cognitive- Decline & Depressed group, n = 338, 11.50%). The fourth trajectory group demonstrated mild decline in ADL with normal cognitive and psychological function (Mild ADL Decline group, n = 351, 11.95%). The final trajectory group was characterized by normal ADL, cognitive, and psychological status (Normal group, n = 1422, 48.40%) (Fig. 2). The five-trajectory model was adopted for subsequent analyses, and participants was assigned to specific trajectories based on their highest posterior membership probabilities.

Baseline characteristics of different trajectory groups

Baseline characteristics of each trajectory group are presented in Table 1. The mean age of the 2,938 participants was 65.57 years (SD: 4.79 years). The “Normal” group was predominantly female (65.5%), while the “Mild-ADL-Decline” group comprised mostly male (59.0%). Around 60% of participants in the “Pervasive Decline (Severe-ADL-Decline)”, “Pervasive Decline (Mild-ADL-Decline)” and “Cognitive Decline & Depression” groups failed to finish primary school. Compared with the “Normal” group, the “Pervasive decline (Severe-ADL-decline)” group exhibited a higher number of comorbidities. In terms of assessment scores, the “Pervasive Decline (Severe ADL Decline)” group had the highest ADL score (1.42 ± 1.62) and CESD-10 score (13.27 ± 6.77). Conversely, the “Mild ADL Decline” group had a relatively higher cognitive score (17.45 ± 3.55).

Association between different trajectory groups and falls

The associations between different trajectory groups and falls are presented in Table 2. The “Normal” group had the lowest fall rate (13.57%, n = 193), while the “Pervasive Decline (Severe-ADL-Decline)” group showed the highest incidence (35.45%, n = 134). Following adjustment for demographic variables, chronic information, behaviour factors, analogous results were also observed in the final model (Model 3). Utilizing the “Normal” group as the reference, the results revealed that the “Pervasive Decline (Severe-ADL-Decline)” group (OR = 2.83, 95%CI: 2.12–3.77), the “Pervasive Decline (Mild-ADL-Decline)” group (OR = 2.13, 95%CI: 1.62–2.80), the “Mild-ADL-Decline” group (OR = 1.97, 95%CI: 1.47–2.02), and “Cognitive Decline & Depression” group (OR = 1.47, 95%CI: 1.07–2.02) exhibited a higher risk of fall. The associations were attenuated in Model 4 compared with the primary models (Supplementary Table S4).

Discussion

This study examined longitudinal trajectories of ADL, cognitive function, and depressive symptoms and further explored their associations with fall risk in older adults. Five distinct multi-trajectory groups of ADL, cognitive function, and depressive symptoms among Chinese older adults were identified. Furthermore, fall risk varied significantly across different trajectory groups. Specifically, participants in the “Pervasive Decline (Severe-ADL-Decline)” group had the highest fall risk.

Previous studies have confirmed that activities of daily living, cognitive function and depressive symptoms are strongly associated with fall risk. Existing research has separately established the longitudinal trajectories of these three indicators and explored their individual relationships with falls [16, 41, 42]. Nevertheless, the developmental trends of these health indicators are not mutually independent. Accordingly, our study adopted the GBMTM to conduct multi-trajectory analysis and classify participants into five distinct subgroups. Compared with prior studies focusing solely on single-indicator trajectories, our approach may capture the co-evolution patterns of multiple health indicators and identify heterogeneous health developmental patterns among older adults more accurately.

Building on previous findings, this study investigated the association of joint trajectories of ADL, cognitive impairment, and depressive symptoms with fall risk. We found that declines across different domains were associated with an increased risk of falls. It is important to note that, although these associations were attenuated in Model 4, this may reflect potential over-adjustment bias, as baseline physical function limitations and prior fall history may lie on the causal pathway between trajectory patterns and falls. Among the five trajectory groups, the “Pervasive Decline” groups were consistently associated with a higher risk of falls. In the “Pervasive Decline” groups, trajectories of ADL and cognitive function exhibited a declining pattern over time, accompanied by increasing depressive symptoms. Prior evidence has established that impairment in each individual domain is associated with an increased risk of falls and also has demonstrated that ADL, cognitive function, and depressive symptoms are closely interrelated. These domains may interact in a cascading manner, with evidence of bidirectional and reinforcing relationships among them. Reduced cognitive function may compromise the ADL [43, 44] which may subsequently contribute to worsening depressive symptoms [45, 46]. Depressive symptoms may further reduce activity levels, leading to additional physical decline and reinforcing the fear of falling [47]. Such reciprocal interactions may contribute to a vicious cycle of functional decline. In addition, our results indicated that trajectory groups characterized by isolated or dual-domain declines, such as “Mild ADL Decline” or “Cognitive Decline & Depressed” were associated with increased but relatively lower risks of falls compared with the Pervasive Decline group. This suggests that preserved function in at least one domain may provide a slower progression of falls. Sun et al. reported that decline in physical function was associated with progressively higher fall risk [48]. Similarly, higher physical reserve has been shown to be protective against fall risk in older adults [49]. These findings indicated that impairment in individual domains contributes to fall risk, while risk increases further with the accumulation of multiple domain impairments.

Findings have shown that compared with the “Pervasive Decline (Mild-ADL-Decline)” group, the “Pervasive Decline (Severe-ADL-Decline)” group demonstrated a marked decline in ADL. The decline of ADL not only slowed movement but also as difficulty performing routine activities safely. Physiological research has indicated that age-related declines in muscle strength, joint mobility, and sensory perception significantly impact ADL [50] and any decline in these areas has been shown to directly increase fall risk in home environments [51]. Furthermore, our study demonstrated that the “Cognitive Decline & Depressed” group showed a lower fall risk than the “Mild ADL Decline” group. The difference between these two groups lies in the affected domains. The “Mild ADL Decline” group was characterized by decline in ADL function, which may lead to limitations in mobility and balance, thereby directly increasing the risk of falls. Nevertheless, cognitive decline and depressive symptoms may exert indirect effects on fall risk [15, 52–56]. It is possible that these symptoms only elevate fall risk obviously when they become more severe or persist over a longer period. Besides, people with cognitive impairment or depressive tendencies tend to act more carefully in daily life [47], which may somewhat lower their fall risk to a certain extent. Accordingly, prioritizing the maintenance and improvement of daily living function is pivotal to reducing fall incidents in the elderly population. These findings further suggest that even older adults without apparent physical limitations may still be at risk when psychological or cognitive health declines.

As demonstrated in previous study, the implementation of targeted fall prevention interventions for high-risk older adults has been shown to result in a reduction of fall incidence by 25–40% among community-dwelling seniors [57]. Based on the findings, a tiered prevention strategy tailored to different trajectory subtypes is recommended. For the “Pervasive Decline” groups, the implementation of multidisciplinary integrated interventions is suggested, incorporating concurrent balance and strength training, cognitive training, and mental health management. In the case of trajectory groups exhibiting single- or dual-dimensional decline, the emphasis of interventions should be directed towards the corresponding dimension in order to reduce the risk of falls. In addition, even among individuals who appear physically robust, declines in cognitive function or the presence of depressive symptoms may substantially increase the risk of falls over time.

Limitation

Several limitations should be considered. First, self-reported of falls, ADL, and depressive symptoms are subject to recall biases. And among participants with cognitive impairment, self-reported depressive symptoms may be subject to measurement bias. Second, the use of listwise deletion to handle missing longitudinal data may introduce potential selection bias and reduce statistical power if the data are not missing completely at random. Future studies may consider using Full Information Maximum Likelihood or multiple imputation methods to better handle missing data under less restrictive assumptions. Third, the two-year interval between CHARLS follow-up waves may fail to capture short-term fluctuations in health status or falls, thereby missing periods of transient risk. Besides, the GBMTM method adopted in this study is a data-driven approach, and the selection of trajectory groups is model-dependent. Classification is probabilistic in nature, and some degree of uncertainty. Finally, while we adjusted for a range of confounders, unmeasured factors such as medication use (e.g. psychotropics), detailed environmental hazards and social support could partially explain the associations. Future studies incorporating real-time fall monitoring, objective functional assessments, and environmental factors would help validate these trajectory patterns and elucidate proximate triggers of falls within high-risk subgroups.

Conclusion

Based on the CHARLS, our study identified five distinct ADL-cognitive-depression trajectory groups among the elderly population. It confirmed that the risk of falls increases with the number of impaired health domains, with the highest risk observed in the pervasive decline group. And it also suggested that single-dimensional health assessments may underestimate individuals’ actual fall risk, whereas tracking multi-domain dynamic changes enables more precise identification of high-risk populations, which provides a basis for implementing stratified interventions and offers a comprehensive intervention perspective for fall prevention among older adults.
