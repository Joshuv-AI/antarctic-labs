Acute hippotherapy surface effects in children with special needs: Encouraging findings with important interpretive considerations
Authors: Aradhana Chhabra, Riya Kalra, Kanu Goyal
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with interest the article by Gulcelik et al. examining the acute effects of hippotherapy performed on different sitting surfaces in children with special needs. 1 The study addresses a clinically relevant and underexplored question regarding whether the horse–rider interface may influence immediate postural and gait-related outcomes.

However, several issues merit clarification to support appropriate interpretation of the findings. First, although the textured-surface group demonstrated a statistically significant within-group improvement in 10-meter walk performance, the between-group comparison for change was not significant. 1 This distinction is methodologically important, as within-group pre–post improvements do not establish comparative effectiveness. As Bland and Altman have emphasized, reliance on within-group comparisons in randomized designs can be misleading when the relevant inference is the between-group effect. 2

Second, the study evaluates only the immediate effects following a single 30-minute session, which limits clinical interpretation. Improvements in balance and gait in pediatric rehabilitation are typically associated with repeated exposure and motor learning processes, and thus acute findings should be interpreted cautiously.3,4

Third, no statistically significant improvements were observed in sitting balance across any group. 1 While this finding is appropriately reported, greater emphasis on this negative result in the conclusion would improve alignment between results and interpretation, particularly given that balance was a primary outcome.

Fourth, the sample includes children aged 3–18 years with heterogeneous conditions. 1 Although baseline comparability was demonstrated, such variability may influence responsiveness to intervention and limits generalizability. Greater detail regarding diagnostic distribution or subgroup-specific responses would strengthen interpretation.

Finally, additional methodological clarity would enhance reproducibility, particularly regarding standardization of the 10-meter walk test conditions. As this test is a validated functional outcome in children with cerebral palsy, consistent testing procedures are essential for interpreting small acute changes. 5

In conclusion, this study provides useful preliminary insights into the potential influence of seating surface in hippotherapy. Future research incorporating larger samples, more homogeneous populations, and longitudinal designs will be important to determine whether these findings translate into clinically meaningful and sustained benefits.
