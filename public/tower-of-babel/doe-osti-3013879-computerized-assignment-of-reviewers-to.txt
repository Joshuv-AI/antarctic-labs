ABSTRACT
Assigning reviewers to applicants for the President Harry S. Truman Postdoctoral Fellowship is a
critical task that requires balancing reviewer expertise, preferences, and workloads. Historically, this
process has relied on a program developed by Cynthia Phillips, which, while effective, necessitates
technical programming knowledge. To ensure the task can be seamlessly transitioned to
administrative staff, this report presents the development of a new tool that optimizes reviewer
assignments using a user-friendly graphical interface.

The program leverages Python libraries, including “pandas” for data handling, “pulp” for linear
optimization, “tkinter” for interface design, and “pyinstaller” to create a standalone executable file.
Reviewer preferences are scored and organized into a cost matrix, and constraints such as workload
balance and compatibility are incorporated into the optimization problem. The tool outputs five
solutions as Excel files, providing flexibility and transparency. Validation against historical data and
user testing demonstrated the program’s effectiveness and usability.

This work not only addresses the immediate needs of the Truman Fellowship committee but also
provides a modular framework for broader applications such as mentor-mentee pairing, task
assignment for large projects, and shift scheduling. By bridging technical optimization methods with
practical usability, this tool ensures fair and efficient assignments while reducing the reliance on
technical expertise.

1. INTRODUCTION

The President Harry S. Truman Postdoctoral Fellowship is one of the most prestigious fellowships
at Sandia National Laboratories, and each year, about two applicants are awarded the fellowship
from many. To decide who is selected for the fellowship, there is a standing committee (the Truman
committee) tasked with reviewing each applicant and making the decision. The Truman committee
is generally senior staff at Sandia (senior scientists and/or lab fellows, who are selected from the top
1% of the technical staff population). Reviewing applicants for this fellowship can be a time
intensive process, especially since there may be more applicants than committee members Because
of this, each member of the committee is assigned to review a subset of the applicants and make an
argument for the applicants they reviewed to the rest of the committee. Applicants for this
fellowship span a large range of disciplines and the expertise of each committee member spans a
smaller range of disciplines. To properly review each application, it is important to ensure that each
applicant has sufficiently knowledgeable reviewers assigned to them. Additionally, it is desirable to
ensure that every committee member has a fair workload. There are additional process details, which
are not in the scope of this work.
To ensure that the assignments of committee members to applicants results in knowledgeable
reviewers and balanced workloads, Cynthia Phillips, one of the committee members, created an
optimization program to make these assignments. This program has made these assignments
satisfactorily since about 2010--but the program requires hands-on data entry by someone with
technical programming knowledge of optimization tools, specifically, the algebraic modeling
language AMPL and the commercial linear programming and integer programming solver CPLEX.
Creating an easy-to-use application that can be used by someone without this specialized
optimization knowledge would enhance the operational efficiency of the Truman committee, as the
assignments of reviewers could then be done by the Sandia postdoctoral program office.
Assigning a group of individuals to do a list of tasks is an extremely common problem. Most
typically, this is solved by having a person look at the individuals and tasks and doing their best to
make assignments. While this method accomplishes the given task, there is no guarantee that the
results solve the problem well. Assuming the person making the assignments is an expert, with a
small sample size you may be able to get optimal or close to optimal results easily. As the problem
size grows, it quickly becomes much more difficult. This motivates a program to optimize the
compatibility of reviewers to the applicants they are reviewing. This compatibility can be measured
by asking each reviewer to take a quick look at each applicant’s package and give a score for how
much they would like to review said applicant. These results can then be organized into a data frame
such as an excel spreadsheet and then fed into the optimization program.

2. REQUIREMENTS DEFINITION

2.1. Defining the Requirements
When approaching this problem, one of the first questions is how to organize the reviewers’
preferences. In the current review process, Truman committee members are asked to take a quick
look at each applicant and give a score of how much they would like to review each applicant. The
Truman committee uses the following scores (paraphrased): (1): Enthusiastic about reviewing this
applicant; (2): Willing to review the applicant; (3): Will review under duress; (3+): Will review under
marginally less duress; (4): Cannot review this candidate because of a conflict of interest. Each
committee member returns their preferences to the Truman Fellowship administrative team, then,
Cynthia Phillips enters the preferences in an AMPL model that calls the CPLEX solver to generate
assignment solutions. This set of scores is not the only scheme that could be used, but keeping the
same set of scores makes the transition to the new system easier.
In addition, the requirements for presenting the solution or output of this program need to be
defined. It should give information about which committee members review which applicants and
vice versa. It should also give the total cost of any given solution (the global optimality cost).
Additionally, it should provide the cost for each individual reviewer and the cost to each applicant
for the reviewers assigned to them (cost of lack of expertise/knowledge to the applicant). This cost
is the sum of the weights for each assignment made based on the score the reviewer gave for the
applicant. For the case that there is a conflict of interest assigned, the output should also make that
clear. The program should also provide more than one possible solution for the Truman Committee
to use, so that some human expert judgment can be applied. Since this program needs to be run by
non-specialist end users, it should also run on one of the Sandia common operating environments,
such as the Windows 11 operating system.

2.2. How We Fulfil the Requirements
The first decision that needs to be made is what programming language to use. We chose Python
3.11.9 because of its wide range of libraries and the first author’s previous experience with the
language.
The input and output are organized in Excel files. This choice for the input file was made since
Excel is the standard data format for Sandia administration and familiar to end users. Since the input
is already an Excel file, it made sense to also make the output an Excel file. The input spreadsheet
(Table 1) has the first row containing the names of each reviewer and the first column containing
the names of each applicant. Each intersection of a reviewer and applicant contains the rating that
said reviewer gave to said applicant. For the output spreadsheet (Tables 2 and 3), there are two
sheets for each solution and there are five solutions so that the user has a few options. On the first
sheet of each output, there are rows corresponding to each applicant and information including their
corresponding primary and secondary reviewers, the cost to each applicant, the total cost of the
solution, and if there are any conflicts of interest. The second sheet of each output has rows
corresponding to each reviewer and information including: whom they are the primary and
secondary reviewers for, the cost to each reviewer, the total cost of the solution, the individual
optimal assignments for each reviewer, and if they have a conflict of interest.
There are many ways you could structure this tool. This choice is motivated by the needs of the end
users. Since we were creating a tool that can be used by non-specialist professionals, it is crucial that

 11

it does not have dependencies such as a programming environment and that it is reliable. Therefore,
the program should not require major debugging by the end user. Because of these considerations,
the tool we created is a “.exe” file with a graphical user interface. This file contains everything
needed to run it, so it runs by itself and is very unlikely to “break” since it does not rely on anything
that is liable to change. This approach has proven to work on computers running Windows 11
(which is one of the Sandia-approved computing environments); however, it has not been tested on
other operating systems.

3. IMPLEMENTATION

3.1. Software Used
The program was made in Python 3.11.9 because of the first author’s previous experience with the
language, the available libraries, and future maintainability and support. For the integrated
development environment (IDE) used to write the code, we used Microsoft’s Visual Studio Code.
This choice was initially motivated by its availability on Sandia’s computers but proved to be a good
choice because of its ease of use and organization as well as installing extensions (in this case Python
3.11.9).
For the choice of libraries, the library “pandas” is used to handle the data organization. This library
allows you to easily read and write Excel spreadsheets with Python, create data frames, and do
transformations on your data. This feature is crucial to be able to organize the data into a problem to
be optimized and then to create an output spreadsheet that is highly customizable. We also used the
“pulp” library for the optimization of this problem. The solver used by this library is CBC. We did
try some other optimization libraries but decided on this one because of its flexibility for creating
problems. This library allows you to easily create a problem and add constraints to it in a simple and
intuitive way. The library we used to create the user interface was “tkinter”. We chose this library
because of its ease of use. In this program, we also import “os” which is a built-in Python library
that allows customization of the path for the solver. We will elaborate more on this in section
3.3.4.3. We also used the “pyinstaller” library to package the entire program with dependencies into
a “.exe” file.

3.3. Creating the Assignments

3.3.1. Data Preprocessing
The first task in the program is preprocessing the data. This activity consists of reading the input
excel spreadsheet with the reviewers’ preferences and storing the resulting data in a pandas data
frame. In this program we then transpose the data or flip the rows and column, but this is just
because the initial version assumed applicants were organized in the columns and reviewers in rows.
It was easier to just add a transpose than to rewrite the input section. Next, we store the names of
the reviewers and the applicants and create a “cost_matrix” variable which just contains the ratings
each reviewer gave for each applicant. The program also checks that the input scores are valid. If
there are invalid entries, including blank entries, the program will provide an error message
indicating all entries which were invalid and exit.

3.3.2. Weighting System
The next step is creating the weighting scheme to determine the cost of making each assignment.
Based on discussion with the Truman Committee chair, weights similar to the previous model were
used because their performance has been satisfactory in the past. Those weightings are as follows:

3.3.3. Calculating Workloads
From speaking with committee members about making assignments, it was made evident that
having even workloads is of high importance. Unfortunately, it is common for some reviewers to be
incompatible with most applicants. In this case you want to assign to the applicant the few reviewers
that can do the review so that no one reviewer has all applicants in the “under duress” category. It
was also explicitly stated that in the case that where you need to assign two reviewers to two
applicants where the first reviewer rated both applicants as a “1” and the second reviewer rated both
applicants as a “2”, each reviewer should be a lead reviewer on one applicant and secondary on the
other even though this results in a greater cost, since there is a higher workload in being a primary
reviewer.
The solution that we found works best for this situation is creating a minimum and a maximum
workload constraint for the total number of assignments for each reviewer, as well as minimum and
maximum workloads for primary and secondary reviewers. To calculate the total workload
constraint, we take the number of applicants and multiply that by two to get the number of total
assignments there must be (primary and secondary reviewer for each applicant) and then we divide
the result by the number of reviewers there are. In most cases this turns out to be in between two
integers, so we round down for the minimum workload and round up for the maximum workload.
In the case that you do get an integer value for this, that is the maximum and minimum workload, so
everyone has exactly that workload. For the primary and secondary workload constraints, we do the
same thing except we do not multiply the number of applicants by two since half of the total reviews
are primary and the other half are secondary. The following equations represent these workload
constraints.

3.3.4. Optimization Problem

3.3.4.1. Creating the Problem
As mentioned before, the problem is solved using the “pulp” library in python. We start by
initializing the problem. After this step, we create binary decision matrices for primary and
secondary reviewers. If you make an assignment, the corresponding value in these matrices is “1”
and every other assignment that you could have made has a corresponding value of “0”.

3.3.4.2. Adding Constraints
Now we can add constraints to the problem simply by using the “+=” operator on the problem and
the constraint you would like to add.
The first constraint sets the cost variables based on the assignment decisions. This is the quantity
that this program is minimizing. To do this we add constraints that are the binary decision matrices
multiplied component wise by their corresponding cost matrices and then the components summed
up. What this means is if you make an assignment, the cost of that assignment is multiplied by “1”
and if you don’t make an assignment, the cost is multiplied by “0”. Now you sum up these entries
and you get the sum of the costs of each decision that you made since every decision that you did
not make gets multiplied by zero.
The next constraint ensures that each application is assigned exactly one primary and one secondary
reviewer. In code, this is setting the sum of the assignments for an applicant equal to 1 for the
primary and secondary binary decision matrices.
The next constraint that we add is one to say that the same reviewer cannot be primary and
secondary for the same applicant. What this looks like is adding a constraint that adds the
corresponding entries of the primary and secondary cost matrices and making sure that each of these
sums is at most 1 (each reviewer is assigned at most once to each applicant).
The next constraints we add are the workload constraints we calculated earlier. What this looks like
is iterating through the binary decision matrices and adding up the values that correspond to each
reviewer. For the total workload, we iterate through both the primary and secondary decision
matrices and add all the assignments for each reviewer then put this in an inequality with the
minimum and maximum total workloads that were calculated earlier. For the primary and secondary
workloads, we do the same thing, but we iterate through the primary and secondary binary decision
matrices separately and create the inequalities separately.
The final constraint that we add makes sure that you do not repeat solutions. Since you are finding
five solutions instead of just one, this problem is put in a loop to do 5 times and each solution you
find is added to a memory so that you can ensure you don’t repeat a solution. In this constraint, the
program requires the current solution to have at least 1 different assignment from every other
solution.

3.3.4.3. Solving the Problem
Now that you have assembled the problem it is time to solve it. With the pulp library you use the
command “prob.solve()” to solve the problem. When we initially packaged this into a “.exe” file, the

 18

program was unable to find the solver to do so. Because of this, we move the solver from the library
file into the same directory as this program and use the “os” library in python to specify the path to
this solver. When this program is packaged into a “.exe” file, it is important to add the
aforementioned solver file in with it. The solver that is used here is CBC (COIN-OR Branch-and-
Cut) which is an open-source solver. It is based on a branch and bound algorithm which searches
through possibilities using a tree, eliminates branches that reach a higher cost than the current best
solution.

3.4.       Output
Now that you have solutions to the problem, you have to format them and write them into an excel
file. The pandas library makes it easy to organize this into whatever format you would like. The
original output we created had the first column with the names of each applicant, the second
column gave the names of the reviewers that will be the primary reviewer for each corresponding
applicant, then the third column had the names of the reviewers who will be the secondary reviewer
for each corresponding applicant. In the next column it says the total cost for each solution. In the
user testing for this program, it was requested to have a “reviewer view” output as well that shows
which applicants are being reviewed by each reviewer. To accommodate this request, we added
another sheet for each solution that had the first column with the name of each reviewer, the second
column had the names of the applicants each corresponding reviewer will be the primary reviewer
for, then the third column gave the applicants that each corresponding reviewer will be the
secondary reviewer for. In this sheet, instead of giving the cost to each applicant, it gives the cost to
each reviewer.

4. THE APPLICATION

4.1. Validation
One method we used for validation was testing our program on extreme cases. An example of this is
testing different number of reviewers and applicants. Trying uncommon ratios of reviewers to
applicants revealed a mistake in the workload constraints where we were not rounding up and down
properly so we would make the problem overly restrictive so there would be no solutions.
Another case is when the inputs are invalid. As it turns out, historically committee members have
often given scores for applicants that are not among the given valid scores. This becomes a
complicated problem because it raises the question of whether you should make the committee
members follow the guidelines better or make the program more accommodating to the preferences
of the committee members. Through discussion with involved parties, it was decided that the
program should only accept the given values of 1, 2, 3, 3+, and 4. This means that in the case where
one of these scores is not given, the program fails, but it will go through the entire input to find all
of the invalid values and then it will raise an error naming exactly the locations of the invalid entries.
We also tested the case in which everybody has a conflict of interest with the same applicant. In this
case, the program will still assign the applicant with a conflict of interest to somebody. To make sure
that this is acceptable, we added information to the outputs to flag any conflicts of interest. This way
if there is a conflict of interest (which only happens if it is the only option; refer to section 3.2.2 for
further explanation on why), the committee is made aware of it and can do its best to deal with the
conflict of interest or bring in somebody from the outside to assist with this task.
To ensure that the program we created worked, we did validation on previous examples. We gained
access to the preferences of reviewers and the actual assignments from previous years and compared
the results from our program against them as well as the actual preferences each reviewer had. This
helped point out flaws in our program. One such flaw was with the initial formulation of the
workload constraint. While we were optimizing the datasets under were total workload constraints,
reviewers who gave disproportionally better ratings for applicants would mostly be assigned to be
primary reviewers, while reviewers who gave disproportionally worse rating for applicant would
mostly be assigned to be secondary reviewers. Although this optimizes the cost function, it was not
what the members of the Truman Fellowship committee wanted. We then implemented a new
workload system with workload constraints for primary and secondary reviewers to account for this.
After working through this new system, the results we got were consistently very similar to the
previously used assignments. The historic datasets that we compared our program against (A.2), the
top solution from our model for each dataset (A.3), and the assignments that were made (A.4) are
listed in the appendix. In most cases, the solutions are either identical or have primary and secondary
reviewers swapped which does not change the cost of the solution. There are a few cases where
reviewers are swapped between applicants, but for these cases, there are multiple equally optimal
solutions such as where everybody gives a set of applicants a score of 1.

4.2. User Testing
To make sure that the program we created met the needs and wants of the end users, we held
meetings to discuss and try out the program. The end users in this case were some of the members
of the Truman Fellowship committee but more crucially the administrative staff that would likely be
responsible for coordinating the assignment of reviewers and applicants in the future. The first issue
that surfaced was the distribution of the file. Since the “.exe” file contains the executable code, all

 21

the libraries we used, and Python itself, it is fairly large file. Because of this, it cannot be emailed
through Sandia’s email. A way of getting around this issue is putting the file in a shared OneDrive.
The problem with this approach is when you go to download the file, most computers give several
safety warnings. To get around the warnings you are able to use Sandia’s internal file transfer system;
however, this system is not available for every Sandian. We have used a combination of the previous
two methods to distribute this program.
As this work was done over the summer of 2025 and the Truman Fellowship applications are due
October 1st of each year, the plan is to use this program to make the assignments for the upcoming
applicants. This will act as further user testing to see if the program will run how the data from other
years predicted.

5. CONCLUSIONS AND OTHER APPLICATIONS

5.1. Conclusions
The development of this program is a significant step forward for addressing the challenge of
assigning reviewers to applicants for the Truman Fellowship in a way that is both efficient and
accessible to non-technical users. By leveraging Python and its libraries, the program successfully
integrates optimization with a user-friendly graphical interface, ensuing that administrative staff can
easily use the tool without the need for programming expertise.
Validation against historical data demonstrated the program’s ability to produce assignment that
align closely with previous results by adhering to committee preferences. Furthermore, user testing
highlighted practical considerations, such as file distribution and usability, which were addressed to
ensure smooth adoption of the tool for the Truman Fellowship.
In conclusion, this project successfully bridges the gap between technical optimization methods and
practical usability, ensuring that the task of assigning reviewers can be seamlessly transitioned to
administrative staff while maintaining the integrity and fairness of the process.

5.2. Recommendations for Future Work
This work not only provides a robust solution for the Truman Fellowship committee but also serves
as a template for similar assignment problems in other contexts. The modular design and flexibility
of the program allow future tuning of the program and potential adaptation to other scenarios
requiring optimization of assignments based on preferences or constraints. Future work could focus
on fine tuning the weighting system, improving the file distribution process, testing the program on
operating systems other than windows 11, changing the output to include optimal individual costs
for the applicants, adding a constraint to minimize the number of assignments with a score 3, and
expanding the program’s applicability to broader use cases.

5.3. Other Applications
While this project focuses on the application of assigning reviewers to applicants for the Truman
Fellowship, the methods that were used can be applied very broadly. Firstly, this program could be
applied with little to no change to other fellowships or reviewer-applicant tasks. Here are some other
applications for such programs.

5.3.1. Mentor-Mentee Pairing
This program naturally fits with the task of pairing up mentors to mentees. In this problem, you
would want to maximize the overlap in interest between the two parties, but you would also want a
uniform workload since any one mentor should not have too much of a burden but at the same
time, if they have agreed to mentor a mentee, they should be given the opportunity.

5.3.2. Task Assignment for Large Projects
In the case where there is a group of people assigned to do a variety of tasks, you will want to do so
in a way that does it best. Suppose you are trying to build a house. You would want to assign an
architect to design the house, construction workers to build the foundation, plumbers to do the
plumbing, and so on. If you were to make the architect do the construction, the construction
workers do the plumbing, and the plumber design the house, it would not only take longer but the

 23

result would be much lower quality. If you apply this to a problem without such obvious
assignments such as creating a team to handle some sort of crisis, the value is much more evident. In
this case, each crisis may be different from the last and logically would need a unique team to tackle
it. Many crises require interdisciplinary teams such as a cybersecurity breach at a power grid facility.
While you would need people who are uniquely qualified to ameliorate the cybersecurity breach, you
may also need electrical engineers to ensure physical integrity of the power grid or emergency
management professionals to ensure public safety during the outage. You may have a large group of
people with different but overlapping skillset and assigning them optimally would be critical in
minimizing the damage of such a crisis.

5.3.3. Shift Scheduling
The task of assigning workers to cover shifts is one of the most common tasks across industries.
This is a task that typically takes a long time especially in an organization with dozens or more
employees. Not only that, but it must be done on a recurring basis. Given a program that can do
this, it would be able to drastically reduce the time commitment for managers and increase the
quality of the scheduling. Especially in a large organization, the chance that a manager figures out
the optimal assignments by themselves is extremely low and the reality is that their assignments are
likely much worse than optimal.
