Perceived experiences of long-term care services among long-term care insurance beneficiaries and their family members: A qualitative interview study in Hunan Province, China
Authors: Liduo Wang, Fenfang Lei, Xiaojun Ma, Ting Ma, Kuang Yao, Xi Cao, Jing Wang, Xiangying Long
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThe long-term care insurance (LTCI) system is a social security program that aims to address population aging and protect the basic living rights of individuals with disabilities. Since the pilot implementation of this system in 13 Chinese cities in 2016, the LTCI system has received increasing scholarly attention. This study aimed to explore the experiences of beneficiaries and their families with the LTCI system.MethodsA qualitative research method was employed. From August to December 2024, the researchers interviewed 13 LTCI beneficiaries and their family members living in Hunan Province. Data were collected via semi-structured, face-to-face in-depth interviews. These interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim.ResultsThe following five themes were identified. 1) The long-term care service application process is convenient. 2) The disability level assessment process is difficult. This theme includes three subthemes: unclear disability level assessment criteria; a cumbersome disability level assessment process; and a lack of professionalism among disability level assessors. 3) Long-term care services are imperfect. This theme encompasses three subthemes: the limited scope of care services; insufficient care resources; and uneven caregiver quality, including a lack of standardized criteria for service evaluation. 4) The economic burden of long-term care has been alleviated. 5) Expectations regarding LTCI services are positive.ConclusionsThe findings of this study improve our understanding of LTCI participants and their family members. These insights can help the Chinese government develop more detailed support strategies and interventions, thereby improving the quality, professionalism, and scope of long-term care services while promoting the implementation of LTCI policies.

Introduction

The aging population is increasing globally. The physical functions of older adults are deteriorating, and their immune resistance is weakening. Consequently, these individuals are becoming more susceptible to various chronic noncommunicable diseases, leading to an annual increase in the proportion of older adults with disabilities [1]. By the end of 2024, the number of older adults (defined as individuals aged 65 and above) in China had reached 220 million, accounting for 15.6 % of the country’s total population. Nearly 41 million people, accounting for 16.9 % of the older population in China, are disabled [2]. According to forecasts provided by China’s National Bureau of Statistics, the number of older adults with disabilities in China will reach 91.4 million by 2050 [3]. Older adults with disabilities have more diverse needs, including daily care, medical nursing, and functional rehabilitation; they also require continuous care for longer periods—or even for the rest of their lives [4]. This situation has increased the burden on society and families regarding aged care [5]. Therefore, meeting the long-term care needs of older individuals with disabilities and mitigating the economic burden imposed by long-term care on individuals, families, and society are critical issues.

To address the economic burden on families and society resulting from population aging and the increasing number of older adults with disabilities, many countries have begun to implement long-term care insurance (LTCI) systems. The LTCI system provides nursing services and benefits to insured individuals who need long-term care due to disability, old age, or illness [6]. In 1994, Germany became the first country in the world to establish a public LTCI system [7]. Japan implemented an LTCI system in 2000 [8]. In 2008, the Republic of Korea introduced a LTCI system [9].

China has also begun to explore appropriate LTCI systems. In 2016, the Ministry of Human Resources and Social Security of China issued the “Guiding Opinions on Carrying out Pilot Programs for Long-Term Care Insurance Systems,” emphasizing the need to vigorously develop LTCI services and continuously improve the social insurance service system [10]. From 2016 to 2024, the Chinese government continuously improved the LTCI system, prioritizing the development and protection of the country’s public health [11]. According to data from China’s healthcare security authorities, to date, 180 million people across 49 pilot cities have enrolled in the LTCI system, and 2.6 million have received LTCI benefits. The size of the population with disabilities covered by the LTCI system is constantly increasing [12].

Research has reported that the LTCI system can significantly reduce medical expenses [13]. Specifically, in cities where the LTCI system has been implemented, the number of monthly outpatient visits among older individuals has decreased by 0.189, annual hospitalization expenses have decreased by 1,504.244 yuan, and the annual number of hospitalizations has decreased by 0.133 [14]. The LTCI system contributes to improving the physical health status of older adults, preventing the onset of disease, and slowing disease progression [15]. It provides financial support to enhance beneficiaries’ quality of life [16].

Since the implementation of the LTCI system, relevant research has focused mainly on policy support and institutional design, as well as on the system’s ability to mitigate the economic burden of long-term care. Such research has focused mainly on the impact of the LTCI system on the broader social medical system at the macro level [17,18]. However, from the beneficiaries’ perspective, research on their experiences during the process of receiving long-term care services remains limited. The existing studies fail to adequately explore beneficiaries’ genuine feelings, the specific challenges they face, and their levels of satisfaction with the services they receive. This limitation prevents us from fully assessing the actual outcomes of implementing LTCI policies or from providing reliable evidence to support policy adjustments. Therefore, in this study, qualitative research methods are employed to thoroughly examine the real experiences of LTCI beneficiaries and their primary family members throughout the care process, covering aspects such as the application and access processes for care services, the quality of the care provided, the professionalism of caregivers, service attitudes, and beneficiaries’ overall satisfaction.

Through this study, we aimed to identify the challenges faced by LTCI beneficiaries and their needs during care experiences, as well as to explore their evaluations and recommendations regarding this program. Based on the findings, we can develop targeted strategies to enhance the quality of care services and beneficiary satisfaction. By thoroughly analyzing beneficiaries’ expectations and suggestions for future long-term care services, we aim to identify the strengths and weaknesses of the LTCI system in its practical implementation, providing empirical evidence and actionable recommendations to refine relevant policies.

Methods

This study adopts a descriptive qualitative research design and semistructured interviews. This study follows the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research proposed by Tong et al. [19] and designs the research process from three dimensions: the research team and researcher reflexivity, research design, and data analysis and reporting. From August to December 2024, the research team selected beneficiaries of LTCI and their family caregivers from medical insurance departments and communities in Xiangtan City, Hunan Province, a pilot city for LTCI programs, and conducted semistructured interviews [20].

Participants

In this study, purposive sampling was employed to select LTCI beneficiaries and their primary family members. The inclusion criteria for beneficiaries included: 1) enrollment in the LTCI program and receipt of corresponding services [21]; 2) participants who have been assessed as Level 1 Severe Disability under the Trial Standards for Long-Term Care Disability Rating Assessment [22]; 3) use of long-term care services for more than six months (thus ensuring that respondents could provide accurate assessments of service quality); and 4) the capability to express one’s psychological and physiological experiences clearly (as indicated by sensory and communication scores that exceeded 3 points during disability assessment) [22]. The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) individuals with severe mental disorders; 2) alcohol or substance abusers; 3) patients with critical illnesses who could not participate in this research; and 4) participants who withdrew from the study for various reasons. In line with the principles of purposive sampling and data saturation, 13 direct insurance beneficiaries were identified (labeled N1–N13). Additionally, one family member of each beneficiary was selected as a co-participant (labeled H1–H13); these numerical assignments matched each beneficiary’s identification. Family caregivers met the following criteria: 1) spouses, parents, children, or other relatives who had been living with the beneficiary for more than six months to ensure an adequate understanding of care services; 2) involvement in daily care and daily living assistance. The exclusion criteria for family members were as follows: 1) professional nursing staff without any familial relationships with the beneficiary; and 2) individuals with sudden-onset severe illnesses who were unable to participate in the study.

Development of the interview outline

This study developed the semistructured interview guide based on the framework established by Kallio et al. [23]. The guide was finalized through literature review, draft compilation, pilot interview, and subsequent revisions. The research team developed the first draft of the interview guide, which contained six open-ended questions. The interview guide was revised into a second version following consultation with two researchers specializing in aged care and two senior experts engaged in LTCI research. One question was removed, leaving five open-ended questions in the revised version.

Subsequently, two insurance beneficiaries and their family members were recruited for pilot interviews in accordance with the pre-defined inclusion and exclusion criteria. These participants did not take part in the formal interviews afterward. Based on the feedback from pilot interviews, the final version of the interview guide was further refined. The terms “psychology and physiology” were incorporated into the third question of the original draft. The final interview guide consists of five open-ended questions. 1) Could you discuss your experiences regarding your participation in the LTCI system, particularly regarding your receipt of LTCI compensation? 2) Could you describe the changes that occurred in your (your family member’s) life after you received LTCI compensation? 3) Have you undergone any psychological or physiological changes since you received LTCI compensation? 4) What do you think about the long-term care services that you are currently receiving, and what aspects do you think should be improved? 5) Finally, what expectations and suggestions do you have regarding future long-term care services?

Data collection and analysis

During preliminary interviews, data on participants’ age, gender, disability level, length of service utilization, and types of long-term care services were collected. The degree of disability assessed in this interviewee is severe disability level 1, a category that qualifies individuals for insurance compensation while preserving sufficient cognitive, sensory, and communication capacities for interviews. Participants were selected to cover three service modes: medical institutional care, home-based care, and institutional care for older adults, and all met the study requirements. Following informed consent and a full briefing, in-depth interviews were conducted for 30 to 60 min at participants’ residences or care facilities. The interviewees had the right to end the interviews at any time. Two researchers participated in each interview; the researchers were professionally trained members of the research team involved in this study. One researcher was responsible for asking questions and taking notes, whereas the other was responsible for audio-recording the interview and providing relevant supplemental materials. Records of the data obtained from the interviews were then signed and confirmed by the interviewees.

Within 24 h of the interviews, the two researchers listened to the audio-recordings repeatedly and subsequently transcribed them. These researchers repeatedly read, organized, classified, and coded the text materials and subsequently compared the results with the original data to form the final themes. Data analysis and collection were performed simultaneously, thereby facilitating timely reflection and enabling the researchers to adjust subsequent steps in the data collection process. The data analysis for this research was conducted according to Colaizzi’s seven-step method of phenomenological data analysis. In addition, NVivo 15.3 software was used to complement the manual analysis conducted for this research. NVivo software can help users classify, filter, code, annotate, and analyze data and supports various analytical methods and tools, such as thematic analysis, content analysis, text mining, and relational network analysis.

Quality control

We ensured the quality of the research across three aspects: credibility, reliability, and verifiability. The researchers were required to participate in the entire research process and to undergo systematic training in qualitative research to ensure the accuracy and consistency of the data, as well as the credibility of the research. The entire research process, including the procedures, interviews, and data analysis, was recorded. Moreover, the interviewees’ language, tone, expressions, body movements, and emotional changes were fully recorded to ensure the reliability of the data collected. The results were verified through reflective discussions that were held during the interviews, and the researchers critically evaluated their own preconceptions and biases.

Ethical statement

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shaoyang University (Ethics Number: 2023KJKT053). All the interviewees were assured that the information collected as part of this research would be used only for research purposes, and informed consent was obtained from the participants. Participant privacy and data confidentiality were maintained by storing all research information anonymously and securely.

Results

The general information of the respondents

A total of 26 participants were enrolled in this interview, including 13 beneficiaries of LTCI and 13 family members. The LTCI beneficiaries ranged in age from 30 to 78 years, while the family members ranged in age from 38 to 74 years. Detailed information on all participants is presented in Appendices A and B.

Experiences of LTCI among beneficiaries and their family members

The process of applying to receive long-term care service is convenient

All the interviewees included in this study reported that the process of applying for long-term care services was relatively smooth and did not involve an excessive number of complicated procedures. They also reported that they could submit application materials to medical insurance departments offline or via online apps and that all the required materials could be found on the relevant websites. The processing time ranged from 15 to 20 working days, and the staff responsible for LTCI services demonstrated positive attitudes.“My application was approved quickly, probably within twenty days, and the process went smoothly.” (N1)

“My application was approved quickly, probably within twenty days, and the process went smoothly.” (N1)

The disability level assessment process is difficult

Unclear disability level assessment criteria

Some interviewees noted that the criteria used for disability assessments were unclear, thereby potentially leading different assessors to reach different conclusions regarding the same situation. Moreover, some interviewees noted that during the assessments, the proportion of dementia assessments was relatively low, thus leading to problems such as underestimation of the level of disability exhibited by an older adult with dementia.“When conducting a disability assessment, I’m most worried about whether the assessment results will be inaccurate. We don’t know what criteria are used for the assessment, and they haven’t clearly told us either.” (H3)

“When conducting a disability assessment, I’m most worried about whether the assessment results will be inaccurate. We don’t know what criteria are used for the assessment, and they haven’t clearly told us either.” (H3)

Complex disability assessment processes

Three interviewees and four family members mentioned that they faced various difficulties during the disability level assessment process, including the difficulty of the assessment process.“The assessment process is also very cumbersome.” (N6)

“The assessment process is also very cumbersome.” (N6)

A lack of professionalism among disability assessors

During the interviews, many interviewees and family members questioned the professionalism of the disability assessors. The interviewees noted that the disability level assessors lacked sufficient professional knowledge and a sense of humanistic care.“I think that the assessors may not have enough professional knowledge. They didn’t give very clear suggestions or explanations during the assessment, which made us feel very confused.” (N3)

“I think that the assessors may not have enough professional knowledge. They didn’t give very clear suggestions or explanations during the assessment, which made us feel very confused.” (N3)

Imperfect long-term care services

Limited scope of care services

All 10 interviewees noted that long-term care services focused primarily on basic daily living assistance (e.g., meal preparation or cleaning) but lacked professional nursing components, such as medication management, rehabilitation support, and vital sign monitoring. Six interviewees specifically expressed a desire for caregivers to provide more disease-specific and rehabilitation-related professional care.“While daily care services (like cooking and cleaning) are well done, professional services (such as rehabilitation training or specialized nursing guidance) are scarce. I feel that I need these types of professional support.” (N13)

“While daily care services (like cooking and cleaning) are well done, professional services (such as rehabilitation training or specialized nursing guidance) are scarce. I feel that I need these types of professional support.” (N13)

Insufficient care resources to meet demands

Multiple interviewees highlighted the strain they faced in obtaining care resources. Despite the availability of diverse service options, resource allocation remains uneven and often fails to meet the needs of all relevant individuals. During peak periods (e.g., on holidays or during extreme weather), caregiver shortages leave some recipients without timely or adequate support.“Care resources are too scarce. Booking a caregiver takes ages, which is inconvenient. There’s no professional guidance regarding caregiving skills and no opportunities to learn.” (H7)

“Care resources are too scarce. Booking a caregiver takes ages, which is inconvenient. There’s no professional guidance regarding caregiving skills and no opportunities to learn.” (H7)

Uneven caregiver quality and a lack of standardized evaluations

The interviewees noted that most long-term care providers lack formal training, thus leading to inconsistent service quality and inconsistent attitudes. The lack of unified evaluation standards in this context complicates assigning responsibility for providing these services.“Different caregivers provide varying services, leaving me confused about which standards to follow.” (N2)

“Different caregivers provide varying services, leaving me confused about which standards to follow.” (N2)

The economic burden is partially mitigated but not fully addressed

The interviewees noted that their enrollment in LTCI and receipt of reimbursements had reduced the burdens they faced on their household and personal finances. However, they still bore more than 30 % of the costs of such care out of pocket. The LTCI system covers only specific services; thus, many items—such as assistive devices and medical supplies—are not eligible for reimbursement.“The reimbursement for care costs has eased the financial pressure, but assistive tools (like walkers and wound care supplies) aren’t covered at all. I hope that some of these could be included.” (N10)

“The reimbursement for care costs has eased the financial pressure, but assistive tools (like walkers and wound care supplies) aren’t covered at all. I hope that some of these could be included.” (N10)

Optimistic expectations regarding LTCI services

The interviewees expressed high optimism about the future of LTCI services. They emphasized their hopes for expanded service formats, improved care quality, greater caregiver training, and broader service coverage.“I’ve been injured for over a year. From my initial struggles to my gradual adaptation, LTCI has helped tremendously—financially and otherwise. I believe that long-term care services will keep improving; I’ve already seen positive changes this past year.” (N5)

“I’ve been injured for over a year. From my initial struggles to my gradual adaptation, LTCI has helped tremendously—financially and otherwise. I believe that long-term care services will keep improving; I’ve already seen positive changes this past year.” (N5)

Discussion

Further optimization of the LTCI application process and the expansion of service coverage

The findings of this research indicate that respondents generally reported a smooth LTCI application process, a positive outcome of years of institutional evolution. From the initial national pilot program in 2016 to the expansion to 49 cities by 2020, stricter unified standards were established regarding core elements such as disability assessment criteria, service management protocols, and fund supervision [10,24]. From 2024 to 2025, China’s LTCI system had become increasingly mature [25]. However, challenges remain regarding expanding service coverage and enhancing social impact during its ongoing development. Studies have revealed limited participation in current LTCI pilots, primarily because of information asymmetry and insufficient policy outreach [26]. In particular, vulnerable groups such as dementia patients often miss out on benefits coverage due to low awareness of relevant policies and a lack of professional guidance [27]. Additionally, public misconceptions regarding LTCI and complex assessment terminology pose barriers to application [28]. These findings will continue to drive policy improvements and facilitate efforts to refine the LTCI service system. Future research should employ integrated quantitative and qualitative approaches to systematically track structural issues and dynamic trends in regional implementation, thereby using empirical evidence to advance systemic optimization and service expansion [29]. A sound policy framework together with public recognition is fundamental to the sustainable development of LTCI, and effective policy-public communication helps boost its enrollment and social value [30,31].

Accelerating efforts to improve the implementation of the system used to assess individuals’ disability levels

During the research, the respondents noted that the current LTCI disability rating assessment process remains unstandardized and that the disability rating assessment standards are unclear, thus affecting the fairness of insurance payments. Accelerating efforts to establish and improve a disability rating assessment system is very important.

In 2021, the medical insurance department of China issued the “Long-Term Care Disability Level Assessment Standard (Trial)” [22]. In 2023, the medical insurance department issued a document titled “Administrative Measures for Assessing Disability Levels in Long-term Care Insurance” [32]. These documents proposed that each region establish detailed criteria and specific assessment procedures for disability level evaluation, based on relevant evaluation standards and management methods, while considering local conditions. This approach provides significant flexibility to regions, resulting in subtle differences in the disability-level evaluation criteria and procedures formulated by different regions [33]. This situation also contributes to the discrepancies in research findings on this topic. Therefore, how to more effectively manage the disability level evaluation process and standardize the disability level evaluation criteria is currently a key focus of China’s work. The interviewees noted that the importance of dementia in assessments of individuals’ disability levels is relatively low. Therefore, the standards used for disability-level assessments should be dynamically adjusted to increase the weight given to dementia.

The respondents also reported issues, including unprofessional evaluators. Therefore, expediting the creation of an expert database for disability rating assessments is essential to standardize evaluation processes and enhance quality and efficiency [34]. Modern information technology tools should be used to develop disability rating assessment information systems that can facilitate real-time data sharing and oversight, thereby increasing transparency and fairness in evaluations. For instance, Japan employs AI algorithms to analyze wearable device data to generate preliminary assessment reports, thereby reducing subjective errors [35]. The Republic of Korea has piloted “smart assessment apps” that use video-based interactions to assess the physical mobility capabilities of individuals with disabilities, thereby improving evaluation efficiency [36]. Countries such as Germany and Japan conduct regular reviews of disability ratings and adjust care plans based on changes in health status [37,38].

Improving the quality of nursing services and accelerating the cultivation of long-term care service professionals

The results of this research reveal that China currently lacks sufficient long-term care resources and that service quality is low. To improve the quality of nursing services, the first step is to increase the supply of long-term care personnel. However, Hunan Province, as a central province in China, features a medium-level economy. This economic foundation has affected the cultivation of long-term care service professionals [39]. First, financial input is inadequate, leaving the multi-level training system for geriatric care nursing professionals underfunded [40]. Second, the slow growth of Hunan’s aged care service industry limits market scale and job opportunities, reducing industry appeal and student enrollment [41]. Third, low salaries, especially in central China, cause skilled aged care service professionals to migrate to more developed regions [42]. To address this issue, the cultivation of long-term care service professionals has been improved in several ways, including through policy support, educational stratification, deepened practices, and strengthened occupational development. In 2022, the “Occupational Classification Dictionary of the People’s Republic of China” specifically established the profession of long-term care workers [43]. In 2024, the “National Occupational Standard for Long-term Care Workers” was promulgated, integrating skills such as daily care, medical nursing, and psychological support, and a three-level certification system was established at the primary, intermediate, and advanced levels [44]. Some institutions have piloted the “1+X” certificate system to bridge academic education and vocational qualifications [45].

Moreover, China can refer to international models for long-term care service professionals cultivation, make localized improvements, and develop a model that is suitable for Hunan. For example, in Germany, the “dual system” of vocational education has been implemented, and Germany has clarified salary standards for caregivers through the “Long-Term Care Insurance Act” [46]. The UK has promoted “teaching hospital + nursing home” joint training for long-term care workers [47].

Nursing service standards should be standardized, and a nursing service evaluation system should be established

The results of this research reveal that the quality of long-term care services is difficult to evaluate and that beneficiaries struggle to obtain high-quality care. Therefore, it is crucial to establish a nursing service evaluation system. In September 2025, the National Healthcare Security Administration of China issued the “National Long-Term Care Insurance Service Project Catalog (Trial)” [48], which represented the first national-level, unified standard for long-term care service projects in China. However, the processes, implementation standards, and quality evaluations associated with various nursing services have not yet been fully clarified. In the ongoing process of exploration and improvement, we can draw on international experience. Internationally, relevant LTCI service quality evaluation tools have been developed, including the International Resident Assessment Tool, the Nursing Quality Assessment Index for Vulnerable Elderly, and quality of life-related evaluation tools [49]. Germany, which serves as a model for LTCI, has established relatively comprehensive quality evaluation standards [50]. Additionally, Germany conducts regular special inspections of nursing institutions. It has established a transparent system for disclosing quality information, enabling older adults and their families to understand each institution’s actual service quality [51]. China may enhance nursing service quality through four measures: establishing a unified evaluation index system covering structural, process, and outcome indicators adapted to different care facilities, implementing regular supervision over facilities, staff qualifications, service standards, and cost management, building an information disclosure system to enable public supervision, and connecting evaluation results with caregivers’ career development and remuneration to motivate quality improvement.

The need to mitigate economic burdens and meet public expectations

The respondents in this study reported reduced economic burdens, a finding that aligns closely with conclusions from multiple quantitative studies. Based on large-sample data analysis, Liu et al. demonstrated that LTCI significantly reduces older individuals’ out-of-pocket medical expenses [52]. The quasi-experimental study conducted by Long et al. [53] found that LTCI effectively reduces long-term care expenditures for older adults, particularly those with poorer financial circumstances. Similarly, Yan et al. [54] ’s China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS)-based study revealed that LTCI substantially alleviates household financial pressure on healthcare costs by replacing out-of-pocket payments. These objective quantitative findings methodologically corroborate the subjective experiences reported in our qualitative interviews, thereby enhancing the credibility of our conclusions. However, disparities persist in funding mechanisms and benefit payments across different populations and pilot regions, underscoring the need to establish a comprehensive, long-term, multitiered care insurance system characterized by clear responsibilities and sustainability.

The residents’ “positive expectations” provide a valuable qualitative perspective on the future development of LTCI. Enhancing insured individuals’ “subjective well-being” should be used as a key performance metric for the LTCI system, rather than relying exclusively on financial indicators [55]. To achieve this goal, it is essential to strengthen comprehensive oversight and dynamic evaluation of service quality throughout the process and establish a service quality feedback mechanism that focuses primarily on policyholder satisfaction [56].

Limitations

The study’s geographical scope is limited to Hunan Province. Future research can validate and supplement the findings of this study by increasing the sample size, expanding the scope of the quantitative analysis, and covering more regions.

Conclusions

We used qualitative research methods to find that beneficiaries of LTCI and their families perceive the process of applying for long-term care services as convenient and that these services mitigate economic burdens to some extent. However, challenges such as difficulties associated with obtaining accurate disability assessments and inadequacies in long-term care services remain unaddressed. While economic burdens have been relieved to some degree, respondents expect LTCI services to improve. In the future, we should aim to enhance the quality of long-term care services by improving the disability level assessment system, accelerating the training of long-term care professionals, and establishing a comprehensive service evaluation framework.
