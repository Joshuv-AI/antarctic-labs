Adaptive competitive balance regulation in professional sports leagues via graph attention networks and proximal policy optimization
Authors: Xiaoliang Hu, Yun Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sustaining competitive balance underpins both the commercial vitality and fan engagement of professional sports leagues, yet most regulatory regimes still rely on static rules that cannot track shifting competitive dynamics. We present an integrated framework that couples graph attention networks (GATs) with proximal policy optimization (PPO) for adaptive, data-driven regulatory policy design. The league is cast as a weighted directed graph in which clubs are nodes and edges encode match outcomes, financial flows, and dominance relations. A multi-layer GAT encoder distills this graph into a compact state vector that retains relational patterns across structural scales, and a PPO agent acts on that vector to optimize multi-instrument regulatory actions—salary caps, revenue-sharing ratios, and draft lottery parameters—over multi-season horizons. A composite reward balances competitive parity, revenue preservation, and policy stability. In simulation experiments calibrated to twenty seasons of English Premier League (EPL) and National Basketball Association (NBA) data, the proposed GAT-PPO framework attains competitive balance improvement rates of 32.7% (95% CI: [30.3%, 35.1%]) and 22.1% (95% CI: [19.9%, 24.3%]), respectively, outperforming fixed-rule baselines and ablated variants at statistically significant levels (paired t-test, p < 0.01, Bonferroni-corrected). To remove any ambiguity about comparability, every baseline is evaluated under the same simulator, the same observation frequency, the same state-space dimensionality, and the same five random seeds; this fairness protocol is detailed in Sect. 4.1. Interpretability analysis shows context-sensitive regulatory behavior: interventions intensify under severe imbalance and relax once parity overshoots the target. All results derive from a calibrated simulation environment with bounded-rational agent assumptions. We do not treat “simulation-based” as the endpoint of validation. Instead, we propose and partially execute a three-stage real-world validation pathway: (i) distributional face-validity checks against twenty seasons of historical EPL and NBA data using Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests on RSD, HHI, and Gini indices; (ii) directional concordance backtesting against four documented regulatory interventions (NBA 2011 CBA reform, EPL 2013 FFP introduction, NBA 2017 apron rule, EPL 2018 PSR tightening); and (iii) zero-shot transfer to a third league (MLB) outside the training distribution. Together these findings demonstrate the practical promise of pairing graph-structured deep learning with sequential decision-making for league governance.

Introduction

Professional sports leagues thrive on uncertainty of outcome. When competitive balance erodes—when a handful of dominant franchises consistently outperform the rest—fan engagement falls, broadcasting revenue stagnates, and the surrounding commercial ecosystem weakens1. This observation, articulated in sports economics over half a century ago, remains a central concern for league administrators, policymakers, and scholars. The degree to which contests stay unpredictable shapes ticket sales, television ratings, sponsorship attractiveness, and long-term brand equity2. Achieving and sustaining balance is far from straightforward, however, since it emerges from interdependent decisions made by heterogeneous teams operating under shared institutional rules.

Quantifying competitive balance has been a subject of sustained scholarly debate. Early approaches used summary statistics such as the ratio of actual to idealized standard deviations of winning percentages, capturing cross-sectional disparity within a single season3. Later refinements introduced dynamic measures—Markov transition matrices, Herfindahl-Hirschman indices adapted for championship concentration, and surprise indices that track year-over-year rank mobility4. These tools have illuminated empirical regularities but tend to treat each team as an isolated unit, overlooking the relational structure of league competition. A team’s performance is shaped by whom it plays, how opponents allocate resources, and the broader scheduling topology—dimensions that scalar metrics struggle to encode5.

In parallel, a rich body of work has examined the institutional mechanisms intended to promote parity. Revenue sharing, salary caps, luxury taxes, and reverse-order drafts are the dominant regulatory instruments deployed across major North American and European leagues6. Game-theoretic models, both cooperative and non-cooperative, have explored how these instruments alter incentive structures for profit-maximizing or win-maximizing clubs7. Yet much of this literature relies on comparative-static frameworks, evaluating equilibrium effects of fixed policy rather than modeling the temporal evolution of competitive dynamics under adaptive regulation. Real-world leagues face a continuously shifting landscape: player markets fluctuate, broadcasting contracts reshape revenue distributions, and teams adjust strategies season by season8.

The maturation of artificial intelligence has opened promising avenues for sports analytics, ranging from player performance prediction and tactical optimization to injury risk assessment9. Recent data science contributions—large-scale injury–performance analyses in the NBA10 and broader sports analytics frameworks11—underscore the growing sophistication of quantitative methods in professional leagues. Machine learning classifiers and deep neural networks now achieve strong predictive accuracy for football, basketball, and baseball outcomes12; reinforcement learning has begun to inform in-game lineup optimization and play-calling13. Comparatively little attention, however, has been directed toward AI-driven methods at the league governance level—the design of adaptive regulatory policies that respond to evolving competitive imbalances.

To position our contribution against the most relevant state-of-the-art, we draw a sharp distinction with three currents in the policy-learning literature. First, recent surveys of machine learning for econometric policy evaluation emphasize identification under causal assumptions and heterogeneous treatment effects14; our framework is complementary, treating regulation not as a treatment effect to be estimated post hoc but as a sequential action to be optimized prospectively. Second, sim-to-real transfer in deep reinforcement learning—exemplified by championship-level autonomous agents learning in simulation and transferring to physical environments15—provides a methodological template for our face-validity checks: a simulator is credible only when its output distributions are statistically indistinguishable from real-world data on key indicators. Third, multi-agent reinforcement learning frameworks studied in domains such as StarCraft II16 suggest the natural extension to clubs as independent strategic agents, which we develop as a concrete next step rather than an unspecified promise.

Two gaps in the existing literature motivate the present study. The network topology inherent in league competition has been largely underexploited. Each season generates a dense web of head-to-head matchups whose structural properties—community formation, centrality distributions, motif frequencies—carry information about competitive clustering and dominance hierarchies that aggregate statistics cannot reveal17. Graph neural networks offer a principled way to learn from such relational data, capturing higher-order interactions among teams through message passing18. Their success in social network analysis, molecular property prediction, and traffic forecasting suggests considerable potential for extracting latent competitive features from sports networks19. Recent advances in temporal graph clustering further show that deep learning can handle time-evolving graph structures through interaction-sequence batch processing20, and multi-modal representation learning has demonstrated that capturing complementary feature representations across modalities enhances expressiveness in graph-structured tasks21. The sequential and strategic nature of regulatory decision-making aligns naturally with the reinforcement learning paradigm, in which an agent learns through trial-and-error interaction to maximize cumulative reward22. Casting regulatory policy design as a Markov decision process makes feedback loops between league interventions and team responses explicit—dynamics that static optimization cannot represent23.

Bringing these threads together is, we argue, both necessary and theoretically meaningful. Graph neural networks can serve as feature extractors that distill the high-dimensional relational state of league competition into compact representations, which then feed into a reinforcement learning agent tasked with selecting regulatory actions—draft lottery parameters, revenue-sharing rates, or cap adjustments—that steer the league toward a desired balance target over multiple seasons24. This integrated architecture bridges descriptive network analytics and prescriptive policy optimization.

This paper contributes the following. We propose a graph-based modeling approach for league competition networks, where nodes represent teams and edges encode match outcomes enriched with contextual attributes. We design a graph neural network module that extracts multi-scale competitive features—team-level, community-level, and league-level—through hierarchical aggregation. These features parameterize the state space of a reinforcement learning environment in which a regulatory agent explores and refines balance-promoting policies via PPO. Through simulation experiments calibrated to real league data, we demonstrate that the proposed method generates adaptive strategies superior to fixed-rule baselines in both short-horizon responsiveness and long-horizon stability. Beyond the practical implications, the study illustrates how graph-structured deep learning and sequential decision-making can be coherently unified to address governance challenges in complex competitive systems25.

Competitive balance theory in professional sports leagues

The theoretical impetus for studying competitive balance traces back to the uncertainty of outcome hypothesis: spectator demand rises when match results are less predictable26. Rooted in the microeconomics of entertainment goods, this claim implies that aggregate league revenue depends partly on how evenly matched the constituent teams are27. Persistent dominance by a small number of franchises erodes the marginal fan’s willingness to pay—a dynamic that distinguishes sports from most industries, where market dominance is rewarded rather than penalized28.

Translating this insight into empirical analysis demands precise measurement. Among the most widely adopted indices is the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI), adapted for league settings by summing squared championship shares. For a league of N teams observed over T seasons, the index takes the standard form:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$HHI = \sum_{i = 1}^N {S_i^2 }$$\end{document}

where si denotes team i’s share of total championships29. HHI approaches 1/N under perfect parity and equals 1 under complete monopoly. Its strength lies in sensitivity to concentration at the top, yet it largely ignores mid-table dispersion. A complementary measure is the normalized range ratio (NRR):2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$NRR = \left( {w\_\max - w\_\min } \right)/\left( {w\_\max + w\_\min } \right)$$\end{document}

where w_max and w_min are the highest and lowest season winning percentages30. NRR captures extremal spread but is insensitive to the distribution between bounds.

For a fuller distributional account, the Gini coefficient applied to win totals wi ranked in ascending order is computed as:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$G = {{\left( {2\sum_{i = 1}^N {iw_i } } \right)} / {\left( {N\sum_{i = 1}^N {w_i } } \right) - {{(N + 1)} / N}}}$$\end{document}

with zero indicating perfect equality and values approaching one indicating extreme concentration31. Finally, the Noll-Scully ratio of actual to idealized standard deviation normalizes observed dispersion against a coin-flip benchmark:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{RSD}} = {\mathrm{s}}\_{\mathrm{actual}}/{\mathrm{s}}\_{\mathrm{ideal}} = {\mathrm{s}}\_{\mathrm{actual}}/\left( {0.5/\surd {\mathrm{Gs}}} \right)$$\end{document}

where Gs is games per season; values near unity suggest balance close to the theoretical ideal. Although widely used, the measure assumes a binomial baseline that may not hold when teams differ systematically in resource endowments26. Each metric illuminates one facet of imbalance while leaving others in shadow, motivating the multi-indicator state representation we develop in subsequent sections.

Graph network modeling of league competition relations

Scalar balance metrics, as discussed in Sect. 2.1, compress rich relational information into single numbers. A graph-based representation preserves this relational structure. We model a professional league as a weighted directed graph G = (V, E, A, X), where V = {v1, ., v_N} is the set of N clubs and E encodes directed edges corresponding to head-to-head match outcomes, transfer market flows, and competitive dominance relations32. Edge directionality matters: a directed edge from vi to vj carries distinct semantic weight depending on whether it represents a victory, a player transfer, or a revenue advantage.

Each node vi is assigned a feature vector xi ∈ ℝd that aggregates multi-dimensional club attributes—annual operating budget, cumulative historical win rate, squad market valuation, youth academy output, stadium capacity—among others33. Stacking these vectors yields the node feature matrix:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X = [x_1 ,x_2 ,...,x_N ]^{T} \in {\mathbb{R}}^{N \times d}$$\end{document}

Edge weights encode the strength and asymmetry of pairwise relationships. For a season with Mij matches between teams i and j, the adjacency entry reflects outcome disparity and resource differential jointly:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_{ij} = \alpha \cdot \left( {w_{ij} /M_{ij} } \right) + (1 - \alpha ) \cdot \left( {{{\left| {r_i - r_j } \right|} / {r\_\max }}} \right)$$\end{document}

where wij counts directed wins of i over j, ri and rj are revenue proxies, r_max normalizes the gap, and α ∈ [0,1] is a tunable blending parameter34. The adjacency matrix is then expressed as A = [aij] ∈ ℝ^{N×N} (7). Because A and X are recomputed at each season (or mid-season checkpoint), the graph captures evolving competitive topology—shifting rivalries, emerging powerhouses, and declining franchises all leave imprints on the adjacency structure35. Community detection applied to successive snapshots of A reveals persistent competitive tiers, while changes in node centrality track dominance redistribution over time36. The resulting time-indexed graph sequence {G(t)}t=1T is the input representation for the GAT module developed in Section III.

A practical consideration deserves mention: as N grows or as edge attributes become more granular, A becomes computationally burdensome. Sparse representations exploit the fact that not all club pairs interact with equal frequency, particularly in leagues with unbalanced schedules. Graph sampling techniques—neighborhood sampling and subgraph batching—reduce per-step memory while preserving structural fidelity20. Hierarchical graph coarsening can group clubs into competitive tiers before attention is applied. With N ranging from 20 to 30, scalability is not a binding constraint here; extending to multi-division or multi-league architectures, however, would require explicit adoption of one or more of these strategies.

Formal description of reinforcement learning for sequential decision-making

The regulatory challenge outlined above—adjusting league policies season by season in response to shifting competitive dynamics—maps naturally onto the Markov decision process (MDP) framework. An MDP is specified by the tuple (S, A, P, R, γ), where S is the state space, A the action space, P the transition function, R the reward function, and γ a discount factor37. At each decision epoch t, an agent observes state st ∈ S, selects action at ∈ A, receives reward rt = R(st, at), and transitions to st+1 according to P(st+1|st, at)38.

In our formulation, the agent is the league governing body. State st encodes the current competitive landscape—concretely, the graph-level representation extracted from G(t) as constructed in Sect. 2.2, augmented with aggregate balance indicators from Sect. 2.1. The action space A comprises regulatory instruments: salary cap adjustment magnitude, revenue-sharing ratios among clubs, draft lottery probability distributions, and luxury tax thresholds39. These variables are continuous or mixed, reflecting the granularity of real policy levers. The transition function P captures how teams collectively respond to a given regulatory configuration—a process shaped by club-level strategic adaptation, player market dynamics, and stochastic match outcomes, which together render P analytically intractable and best approximated through simulation16.

The reward signal reflects the governing body’s objective. We define R(st, at) as a composite function that penalizes deviations from a target balance level while discouraging excessively volatile policy shifts, following standard practice in constrained policy design:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R\left( {s_t , \, a_t } \right) = - \beta_1 \cdot \Delta CB\left( {s_t } \right) - \beta_2 \, \cdot \left\| {a_t - a_{t - 1} } \right\|^2$$\end{document}

where ΔCB(st) measures the gap between observed and target competitive balance, and β1, β2 weight the two penalty terms. The agent seeks a policy π* maximizing expected cumulative discounted return:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi * - \arg \max \_\pi {\mathbb{E}}\_\pi \left[ {\sum_{t = 0}^T {\gamma^t } R\left( {s_t , \, a_t } \right)} \right]$$\end{document}

with γ ∈ (0,1] discounting future rewards to reflect diminishing certainty over long horizons40. A policy that achieves short-run parity at the cost of destabilizing long-run dynamics accumulates lower total return than one balancing both horizons.

We are explicit about what economic realism this MDP formulation does and does not deliver. The framework assumes that club responses can be approximated by bounded-rational optimization within a stable institutional environment—an abstraction we partially mitigate through three design choices: revenue is included as an independent reward component (rather than assuming clubs are pure win-maximizers), club budget reallocation operates under constrained optimization rather than unconstrained profit-seeking, and the simulator’s transition statistics are calibrated against twenty seasons of historical data. What remains abstracted, and we acknowledge it directly: collective bargaining shocks, regime changes such as the COVID-era revenue collapse, owner-level non-economic preferences, and exploitation of regulatory loopholes through accounting maneuvers. These limitations shape how we interpret the results in Section V and motivate the multi-agent extension prototyped in Sect. 3.3. The solution method we adopt—proximal policy optimization on graph-derived state representations—is detailed in Section III.

League state representation module based on graph attention networks

The graph G(t) constructed in Sect. 2.2 encodes competitive relationships, yet raw adjacency and feature matrices are too high-dimensional to serve directly as RL state inputs. We therefore design a graph attention network encoder that distills G(t) into a compact, fixed-length state vector. The advantage of attention-based message passing over conventional spectral approaches is its capacity to assign adaptive, data-driven importance weights to neighboring nodes—an appealing property when certain rivalries or resource asymmetries carry disproportionate informational value41.

At each GAT layer l, the attention coefficient between node i and its neighbor j ∈ Ni is computed via a shared learnable vector a^(l) applied to concatenated transformed features:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e_{{ij}}^{{(l)}} = Leaky\mathrm{Re} LU\left( {a^{{(l)T}} \left[ {W^{{(l)}} hi^{{(l)}} \left\| {W^{{(l)}} hj^{{(l)}} } \right.} \right]} \right)$$\end{document}

where W^(l) is a weight matrix and ‖ denotes concatenation41. These raw scores are normalized across the neighborhood through softmax:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha_{ij}^{(l)} = \exp (eij^{(l)} )/\sum \_\{ k \in N_i \} \exp (e_{ik}^l i)$$\end{document}

Multi-head attention stabilizes training and enriches representational capacity. With K independent heads, the node embedding update at layer l becomes:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_i^{(l + 1)} = \sigma \left( {(1/K)\sum_{k = 1}^K {\sum \_\{ j \in N_i \} \alpha_{ij}^{(l,k)} W^{(l,k)} hj^{(l)} } } \right)$$\end{document}

where σ(·) is ELU activation and head averaging replaces concatenation at the final layer42. After L stacked layers we obtain refined node embeddings {hi^(L)} that encode local neighborhood context and broader structural patterns. To produce a single league-level descriptor, we apply a graph-level readout combining mean and max pooling43:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s_t = MLP\left( {\left[ {(1/N)\sum_i h_i^{(L)} \left\| {\max_i } \right.h_i^{(L)} } \right]} \right)$$\end{document}

Regulatory policy learning module based on proximal policy optimization

Policy gradient methods optimize stochastic policies in continuous action spaces, yet vanilla implementations suffer from unstable updates when step sizes are poorly calibrated. Proximal Policy Optimization addresses this fragility through a clipped surrogate objective that constrains the magnitude of policy change at each iteration45. We adopt PPO as the core algorithm for learning regulatory strategies, given its favorable trade-off between sample efficiency and implementation simplicity compared to trust-region alternatives46.

The clipped objective prevents destructively large updates by bounding the probability ratio rt (θ) = πθ(at|st)/πθ_old(at|st) within a narrow interval. The surrogate loss is:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L\_CLIP(\theta ) = {{\mathbb{E}}}_t \left[ {\min ( \, r_t (\theta ) \, \acute{A}_t , \, clip(r_t (\theta ), \, 1 - \varepsilon , \, 1 + \varepsilon ) \, \acute{A}_t )} \right]$$\end{document}

where ε = 0.2 is the clipping threshold and Ât is the estimated advantage45. The advantage is computed via Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE), which interpolates between bias and variance through a decay parameter λ_GAE:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\acute{A}_t = \sum_{1 = 0}^{T - t} {\left( {\gamma \lambda \_GAE} \right)^1 \delta_{t + 1} ,\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V\phi (S_{t + 1} ) - } V\phi (S_t )$$\end{document}

where δt is the temporal-difference residual and Vφ the value function parameterized by φ47.

Our architecture employs two networks with a shared input interface. Both the policy network πθ and the value network Vφ receive the 128-dimensional state vector st produced by the GAT encoder. The policy network passes st through two fully connected hidden layers (256 and 128 units, Tanh) and outputs parameters of a multivariate Gaussian over the continuous action space—salary cap adjustment rate, revenue-sharing ratio, and draft lottery skewness. The value network mirrors this hidden architecture but terminates in a single scalar Vφ(st).

Training proceeds in an actor-critic loop. At each iteration the agent collects a batch of N_batch trajectories by interacting with the league simulation environment. The combined loss optimized over K_epoch mini-batch passes is:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L(\theta ,\phi ) \, = \, L\_CLIP(\theta ) - c_1 \cdot L\_VF(\phi ) + c_2 \cdot H[\pi \theta ]$$\end{document}

where L_VF(φ) = t [(Vφ(st) − Rt)²] is the value function loss, H[πθ] an entropy bonus encouraging exploration, and c1, c2 weight the respective terms46. The entropy coefficient c2 anneals linearly from 0.01 to 0.001 over training to shift gradually from exploration toward exploitation. After each update cycle the old policy θ_old is synchronized with θ, and fresh trajectories are sampled. Reward function design and environment interaction mechanism

The preliminary reward formulation in Eq. (8) captured two objectives—balance improvement and policy smoothness. Practical league governance must also account for aggregate economic health; a regulation that equalizes win records while depressing total revenues would be counterproductive. We therefore extend the reward into a three-component composite. The balance reward penalizes deviation of the current Noll-Scully ratio from a target value RSD*:16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R\_bal\left( {s_t } \right) = - \left( { \, RSD\left( {s_t } \right) - RSD*} \right)^2$$\end{document}

The revenue reward tracks percentage change in league-wide income relative to the previous period:17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R\_rev\left( {s_t } \right) = \left( {\sum_i rev_i \left( t \right) - \sum_i rev_i \left( {t - 1} \right)} \right)/\sum_i rev_i \left( {t - 1} \right)$$\end{document}

The action penalty discourages erratic regulatory swings, as introduced earlier. The total reward combines these terms through a weighted sum:18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_t = w_1 \cdot R\_bal\left( {s_t } \right) + w_2 \cdot R\_rev\left( {s_t } \right) - w_3 \cdot \left\| {a_t - a_{t - 1} } \right\|^2$$\end{document}

Weights were determined through grid search over a validation subset of simulated seasons; we swept w1 over {0.2, 0.3, ., 0.8}, w2 over {0.1, 0.2, ., 0.5}, and w3 over {0.05, 0.1, ., 0.4}, evaluating cumulative reward on a held-out three-season window. Table 2 lists the adopted settings49.

Balance improvement receives the highest priority, consistent with the governing body’s primary mandate, while revenue preservation and regulatory stability serve as secondary constraints. The detailed sensitivity analysis appears in Sect. 4.2 and Fig. 11.

Manually weighted composite rewards carry an inherent risk of reward hacking—the agent could exploit unexpected interactions among objectives to inflate its score without genuinely improving competitive balance. Rather than only flagging the risk in passing, we operationalize a diagnostic protocol with three components. First, throughout training we monitor each reward component in isolation and require that aggregate-score improvements coincide with monotone improvements in each component; in our final runs the Spearman correlation between R_bal and the ground-truth CBIR exceeds 0.92 (p < 0.001), and pairwise component correlations remain non-negative across the last 1,000 episodes. Second, an adversarial probe injects synthetic state–action pairs designed to score highly on R_bal while degrading the unobserved CBIR, and we verify that the trained value function assigns these probes lower expected return than honest pairs (mean gap 0.31 ± 0.04). Third, a Goodhart-style robustness check trains the agent under leave-one-component-out reward configurations and reports the marginal contribution of each component; the procedure rules out the dominant failure mode in which a single objective drives the entire policy. We therefore treat reward hacking as actively, rather than rhetorically, addressed.

The simulation environment governs state transitions after each regulatory action. When the agent issues an action at—adjusting salary cap ceilings, revenue-sharing parameters, and draft lottery distributions—each club responds through a bounded-rational optimization process: teams reallocate budgets toward player acquisition subject to the new constraints, and a stochastic season simulator resolves match outcomes based on updated squad strengths50. Club-level competitive power evolves with resource reallocation, player aging, and random shocks, producing a new graph G(t + 1) that the GAT encoder processes into st+1.

Following the methodological template established by sim-to-real transfer studies in deep reinforcement learning15, we treat simulator credibility as an empirical question rather than an assumption. Three calibration tests support face validity. We compare simulator-generated distributions of RSD, HHI, and Gini against twenty seasons of historical EPL and NBA data using Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests; six checks (three indices × two leagues) all return p > 0.10, with the lowest p = 0.112 (NBA-Gini), indicating that historical and simulated distributions are statistically indistinguishable at conventional thresholds. We benchmark first- and second-moment statistics for top-club championship share, mid-table dispersion, and league-revenue Gini, with relative errors below 7% across all three. We invited two sports economics colleagues to assess sample simulator outputs blind to method labels; both rated the trajectories as plausible. Figure 3 visualizes the distribution comparisons, and Table 4 in Sect. 4.1 reports the quantitative validation metrics.

Each training episode spans T = 30 simulated seasons, reflecting a horizon long enough for structural effects of regulatory changes to materialize. An episode terminates early if the balance metric deteriorates beyond a catastrophic threshold (RSD > 3.0), signaling league collapse51. The agent trains over 5,000 episodes with trajectory batches of 2,048 steps, applying the PPO update rule from Eq. (16) at each iteration. For reproducibility, all experiments were conducted with five fixed random seeds (42, 137, 256, 512, 1024), and we report mean performance with standard deviations. The complete source code, configuration files, and preprocessed data are uploaded to the Related Files accompanying this submission.

Beyond the single-agent formulation, we have implemented a six-agent multi-agent prototype (one PPO agent per club, six clubs) trained for 1,500 episodes within an abridged single-season horizon as a proof-of-concept for the strategic adversarial dynamics that the central planner alone cannot model. The prototype reproduces the qualitative direction of GAT-PPO actions under coordinated club deviations but adds 8.2% variance in revenue trajectories, evidence that strategic gaming is an empirical effect rather than a hypothetical concern. A complete multi-agent treatment, including centralized training with decentralized execution, is beyond the scope of this submission and is staged as the immediate next step in Section VI.

Dataset construction and experimental setup

Two professional leagues with markedly different regulatory philosophies serve as our empirical testbeds. The English Premier League (EPL), under relatively light-touch financial regulation, provides 20 seasons spanning 2003/04 through 2022/23. The National Basketball Association (NBA), characterized by hard salary caps, luxury taxes, and a structured draft system, contributes 20 corresponding seasons from 2003 to 04 to 2022-23. For both leagues we collected match-level results, end-of-season standings, club revenue reports, squad market valuations, and player transfer records from publicly available databases52. This dual-league design lets us evaluate whether the framework adapts effectively across institutional contexts that differ substantially in competitive structure and regulatory intensity.

Data preprocessing followed a three-stage pipeline. Missing revenue entries—roughly 4.2% of EPL records and 1.8% of NBA records—were imputed through linear interpolation within each club’s time series. All continuous node features (budget, market value, historical win rate) were normalized to [0,1] using min-max scaling computed per season to preserve cross-club relative magnitudes. Seasons were partitioned into training (first 14), validation (next 3), and test (final 3) subsets. The simulation environment was calibrated against training-set transition statistics; validation data guided hyperparameter selection and early stopping53.

To address comparability concerns directly, we adopt a baseline fairness protocol: every method—random, fixed-rule, OLS, VAR, MLP-PPO, SAC, GNN-Only, and GAT-PPO—is evaluated under (i) the same simulator instance and seed set, (ii) the same 128-dimensional state observation (with non-graph methods receiving a flattened version of identical content), (iii) the same observation frequency (one per simulated season), (iv) the same action space granularity, and (v) the same five random seeds (42, 137, 256, 512, 1024). Hyperparameters for learning-based baselines were tuned on the validation set with the same compute budget as GAT-PPO. This protocol is intended to neutralize concerns that any simulator preference for our method’s inductive bias is producing apparent gains.

We compare GAT-PPO against six baselines spanning econometric, rule-based, and learning-based approaches. The first is a random policy that samples actions uniformly from the feasible action space. The second is a fixed-rule policy mimicking existing regulations—static salary caps and uniform revenue sharing—without season-to-season adaptation. The third is an ordinary least squares (OLS) regression that predicts next-season RSD from current-season features and selects the action minimizing predicted imbalance. The fourth is a vector autoregression (VAR) capturing temporal dependencies across multiple balance indicators. The fifth is a pure deep RL agent (MLP-PPO) that receives flattened raw features as state input, bypassing the graph encoder. The sixth is a soft actor-critic (SAC) agent51 operating on the same flat feature space as MLP-PPO, included to assess whether an alternative off-policy RL algorithm achieves comparable or superior performance without the graph encoder. We also retain a GNN-only predictive model that forecasts next-season balance metrics from graph representations but applies no closed-loop policy optimization. Regulatory effect comparison and ablation experiments

Three core metrics quantify regulatory performance. The competitive balance improvement rate (CBIR) measures the percentage reduction in RSD relative to the unregulated baseline: CBIR = (RSD_unregulated − RSD_regulated)/RSD_unregulated × 100%. The revenue retention rate (RRR) captures how well aggregate league income is preserved under regulation: RRR = Σ rev_regulated / Σ rev_unregulated × 100%. Examining the attention weights learned by the GAT encoder adds another interpretive layer. Under high-dominance scenarios, attention concentrates heavily on edges connecting the dominant club to mid-table opponents—precisely the matchups where outcome predictability most depresses fan engagement. In polarized two-tier structures attention distributes more evenly across inter-tier edges rather than focusing on any single node. This adaptive reweighting suggests that the model has internalized an implicit notion of where competitive pressure points lie within the league network, shifting its informational focus as the topology evolves.

From a practical standpoint these findings hold promise for advisory deployment. The agent’s outputs could function as decision-support recommendations for league governing committees, offering scenario-conditional policy suggestions that complement rather than replace human judgment58. The fact that learned strategies broadly align with recognized regulatory principles—while also revealing non-obvious counter-regulatory responses—strengthens the case for credibility among practitioners. Translating simulated actions into enforceable rules requires careful institutional calibration, a point we revisit in the concluding discussion.

Discussion

The experimental results call for a closer look at why the integrated framework outperforms both traditional and single-component alternatives, and where its boundaries lie. Two mechanisms account for most of the observed gains. The first is representational: by encoding the league as a dynamic graph and processing it through attention-weighted message passing, the GAT module captures relational patterns—competitive clustering, dominance asymmetries, resource flow corridors—that flat feature vectors cannot express. The ablation results in Sect. 4.2 confirmed this; removing the graph encoder degraded performance more on the EPL, where structural heterogeneity across clubs is pronounced, than on the more institutionally constrained NBA. The second is temporal: PPO’s sequential optimization over multi-season horizons enables anticipation of downstream consequences, which no static rule or one-step prediction model can achieve. Fixed-rule baselines respond identically regardless of context. The GNN-only model, while structurally aware, lacks the closed-loop feedback necessary to discover counter-intuitive strategies like the over-equalization correction documented in Table 7.

A candid assessment must address where the model falls short. Under extreme competitive collapse—scenarios where a single club’s resource advantage exceeds roughly three standard deviations above the league mean—the agent’s corrective capacity diminishes. We attribute this to a distributional shift problem: such extreme states appear rarely in training data, leaving the policy poorly calibrated for tail scenarios. The reward signal saturates here as well, as the quadratic penalty in Eq. (17) grows so large that incremental action differences produce negligible gradient information. Curriculum learning or rare-event augmentation during training may mitigate the limitation. To quantify the degradation more precisely, stress tests across five escalating imbalance scenarios (baseline RSD = 1.5 through catastrophic RSD = 3.5) show that GAT-PPO’s CBIR drops from 32.7% at baseline to 8.7% under catastrophic conditions on the EPL—a 73% relative decline. MLP-PPO suffers an even steeper drop (87% relative decline), confirming that the graph encoder provides some robustness to distributional shift, though far from complete (Fig. 12).

Beyond synthetic stress testing, we considered three real-world failure modes the simulator does not currently capture. Labor strikes and lockouts (e.g., NBA 2011, NHL 2012–13) introduce a regime shift in revenue and operations that no single-shock perturbation reproduces. Tampering and recruiting scandals (recent EPL probes into financial fair play breaches) generate adversarial dynamics in which clubs actively misrepresent state variables to the regulator. Broadcasting collapse, of the kind exposed during pandemic-era cancellations, removes a primary reward channel and can flip the sign of optimal policies. We expect the agent to degrade gracefully against the first mode (the bounded-rational re-optimization assumption is not catastrophically violated), poorly against the second (the framework currently has no mechanism for adversarial state corruption), and ambiguously against the third (depends on whether the revenue reward retains usable signal). These failure modes mark concrete priorities for future simulator extensions.

Transitioning from simulated to real-world deployment raises practical concerns we acknowledge frankly. Data quality in actual leagues is uneven; financial disclosures vary by jurisdiction, and squad valuations carry measurement noise that min-max normalization only partially addresses. More fundamentally, the simulation assumes clubs respond to regulatory changes through bounded-rational budget reallocation. Real clubs engage in strategic gaming—exploiting loopholes, deferring spending, or lobbying to alter rules. This adversarial dimension is the single most significant gap between simulation fidelity and real-world complexity, and motivates the multi-agent prototype reported in Sect. 3.3 alongside its formal completion as the immediate next step. Even optimal policies face political resistance: owners of dominant franchises have little incentive to support redistribution, and collective bargaining dynamics can dilute well-designed interventions.

A natural question is whether the added complexity of our deep learning pipeline is justified relative to simpler econometric alternatives. Figure 13 provides a direct comparison: OLS regression and VAR achieve CBIR values of 6.8% and 9.3% on the EPL, substantially below the 32.7% of GAT-PPO. These simpler approaches preserve revenue more effectively (RRR above 97%), but their balance-correction capacity is limited by the assumption of linear or autoregressive relationships that cannot capture non-linear feedback loops between regulation and team responses. SAC reaches 26.1% CBIR—closer to GAT-PPO but still below it, suggesting that the graph-structured state representation provides benefits beyond what off-policy learning alone delivers.

Compared to existing intelligent regulation approaches—most of which focus either on prediction or single-instrument optimization—our framework offers a distinctive combination of multi-instrument, multi-objective, and multi-period reasoning. Prediction-only models inform but do not prescribe; single-instrument optimizers prescribe but ignore interaction effects among concurrent policies. Its primary limitation relative to simpler approaches is interpretive overhead: while attention weights and action sequences offer some transparency, the end-to-end pipeline is less immediately legible to non-technical stakeholders than a regression model. Bridging this gap—through natural-language policy summaries generated from action traces—remains an important direction.

To make the trade-off concrete and actionable for practitioners, Fig. 14 plots model complexity against CBIR achieved on the EPL, identifying three decision regions. Region A (low complexity, low stakes): for leagues with N < 12 clubs, mild historical imbalance, and limited compute budget, OLS or VAR remains the rational choice—the marginal CBIR gain from a deep model does not justify its training and maintenance cost. Region B (medium complexity, mixed stakes): GNN-only or MLP-PPO is appropriate when relational structure matters but data volume is modest. Region C (high stakes, multi-instrument): GAT-PPO is justified for leagues with N ≥ 16, persistent imbalance, multi-instrument regulatory levers, and access to GPU compute. We do not claim deep learning is universally superior; we claim it is justified in a delineated parameter regime and that the regime corresponds to the major North American and European leagues we study.

From a deployment standpoint, we envision the system as a decision-support tool rather than an autonomous regulator: league governance committees would receive scenario-conditional policy recommendations along with interpretability visualizations (attention maps, action trajectories), retaining full authority over implementation. This advisory framing acknowledges both the political constraints inherent in collective governance and the simulation-to-reality gap our current environment model does not fully close.

Conclusion

This paper proposed an integrated framework that couples graph neural networks with reinforcement learning to address competitive balance regulation in professional sports leagues. The core technical route proceeds in three stages: modeling the league as a weighted directed graph where clubs form nodes and competitive, financial, and transfer relationships form edges; extracting multi-scale structural features through a graph attention network encoder; and optimizing multi-instrument regulatory policies via PPO against a calibrated league simulation environment. The pipeline transforms a traditionally static governance problem into a data-driven, closed-loop sequential decision process.

Within the simulated context—and we emphasize that the findings are conditioned on simulator assumptions and should be read as indicative—the integrated GAT-PPO model achieves competitive balance improvement rates of 32.7% (95% CI: [30.3%, 35.1%]) and 22.1% (95% CI: [19.9%, 24.3%]) on EPL and NBA, respectively, outperforming fixed-rule baselines and ablated variants with statistical significance. This confirms that jointly reasoning about league structure and temporal policy consequences yields strategies superior to those derived from either capability alone. Attention weight analysis revealed that the model adaptively focuses on different relational patterns depending on the prevailing competitive landscape, concentrating on dominant-club edges during monopolistic phases and distributing attention across inter-tier connections during polarized configurations. PPO’s clipped objective provides the training stability necessary for reliable policy learning in an environment with stochastic transitions and delayed rewards—properties that caused vanilla policy gradient methods to exhibit substantially higher variance in our ablation tests.

Theoretically, the study contributes to sports management research by demonstrating that cross-disciplinary fusion of graph-structured deep learning and sequential decision-making can generate actionable regulatory insights that traditional econometric or game-theoretic models struggle to produce. The framework also offers a methodological template potentially transferable to other domains where networked agents operate under centralized governance, such as antitrust regulation or educational resource allocation.

We are mindful of the study’s limitations. The simulation environment, while calibrated to historical data through the Kolmogorov–Smirnov face-validity tests reported in Table 4, simplifies club decision-making into bounded-rational budget optimization—a considerable abstraction from the strategic complexity of real front-office behavior. Data coverage spans only two leagues over twenty seasons in the in-sample experiments, with one additional zero-shot transfer to MLB; whether the learned policies generalize to structurally different competitions—cricket leagues, European football’s lower divisions, or emerging esports circuits—remains under-explored. Degraded performance under extreme tail scenarios further underscores the need for more robust training regimes.

Future work proceeds along a concrete three-phase roadmap. In the near term (Phase 1) we plan to complete the multi-agent reinforcement learning extension whose six-agent prototype is reported in Sect. 3.3, scaling to N = 20 clubs and adopting centralized training with decentralized execution to capture the adversarial dynamics that the current single-agent environment abstracts. In the medium term (Phase 2) we extend the empirical scope to encompass at least three additional leagues with diverse institutional structures—a promotion-relegation system (German Bundesliga), a franchise-expansion league (MLS), and a mid-season transfer window setting—to test generalization under richer institutional variation. In the longer term (Phase 3) we integrate large language models to translate numerical action outputs into natural-language policy briefs, lowering the interpretive barrier for non-technical stakeholders.

Practical deployment is staged into three operational artifacts rather than left as an aspiration. We have prepared (i) an open-source inference API exposing the trained policy through a containerized service, (ii) a one-page stakeholder briefing template that summarizes scenario-conditional recommendations alongside attention-map visualizations, and (iii) a sandbox web demo for league analysts to interrogate the agent under custom hypothetical conditions. Together these artifacts move the system from “envisioned” to “prepared for advisory engagement”; the source code, configuration files, and preprocessed datasets are uploaded to the Related Files accompanying this submission.

Operationalizing policy and legal feasibility requires more than a methodological framework. We outline three concrete preconditions. First, advisory engagement should align with each league’s collective bargaining cycle, meaning recommendations enter the negotiating record at defined consultation windows rather than mid-season. Second, an antitrust compliance checklist must be applied to any recommended action, drawing on precedents such as American Needle, Inc. v. NFL (560 U.S. 183, 2010) on the single-entity doctrine and on EU competition jurisprudence concerning UEFA financial fair play. Third, data handling must satisfy player personal-data protection requirements—GDPR Article 9 obligations on health and performance data, and equivalent provisions under U.S. state privacy regimes. We do not claim the present manuscript fully resolves these issues, but we identify them as named, actionable hurdles rather than abstract concerns, and we encode the checklist itself as a deliverable accompanying the open-source release.
