Multivariate dataset and response surface optimization for predicting mechanical and durability properties of slurry-infiltrated fibrous concrete incorporating sisal fiber and coffee husk ash
Authors: Opeoluwa Rotimi Dada, Dawit Beza Alemu, Bolanle Deborah Ikotun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConcrete remains a dominant construction material worldwide due to its renowned strength, durability, workability, and versatility. However, designing concrete mixes that incorporate novel materials and additives while accounting for multiple design resistances is particularly challenging. Although existing methods can predict key concrete properties such as compressive, flexural, and tensile strengths, as well as durability, these approaches are limited by the narrow range of materials and properties they consider. This study presents a comprehensive dataset from experimental analysis, including mechanical performance evaluations and non-destructive test results for 10 distinct concrete mixes incorporating various additives and aggregates. With 960 data points, the dataset is a state-of-the-art resource for concrete analysis. Specifically, this paper introduces a multivariate dataset and a response surface optimization approach for predicting the mechanical and durability properties of Slurry-Infiltrated Fibrous Concrete (SIFCON) reinforced with sisal fiber and supplemented with coffee husk ash. The optimal usage of sisal fiber (1–1.5%) and for coffee husk ash (10–15%) The findings provide a robust framework for optimizing sustainable concrete mixes and advancing predictive modelling in construction materials engineering.Keywords: Coffee husk ash, Concrete, SIFCON, Sisal fiber, Response surface optimization

Background

The mechanical performance of concrete, particularly its compressive, tensile, and flexural strengths, is fundamental to structural concrete design, governing load-bearing capacity, failure mechanisms, and overall infrastructure resilience [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Conventional concrete, while widely used, has low tensile strength (8–15% of compressive strength) and exhibits brittle failure. These limitations have driven the development of Slurry-Infiltrated Fibrous Concrete (SIFCON), which achieves exceptional mechanical properties (compressive strength >100 MPa, tensile strength 10–25 MPa, flexural strength 30–50 MPa) through a high-volume fiber network (5–12%) infiltrated with a low water-to-binder slurry [[5], [6], [7]]. However, traditional SIFCON relies on costly synthetic fibers and high cement content (>800 kg/m³), raising economic and environmental concerns. Coffee husk ash (CHA), an agricultural waste with high amorphous silica content (60–70%), serves as a pozzolanic supplementary cementitious material that refines the pore structure, consumes calcium hydroxide, strengthens the interfacial transition zone (ITZ), and reduces the pore solution alkalinity, thereby protecting natural fibers from alkaline degradation [[8], [9], [10]]. Sisal fiber, a renewable, low-cost natural fiber with a tensile strength of 400–700 MPa, offers ductility and crack-bridging capacity but suffers from age-related embrittlement in alkaline cementitious environments [[11], [12], [13]]. The combined use of CHA and sisal fiber in SIFCON creates a synergistic ternary composite in which CHA protects sisal fibers from degradation while densifying the matrix and ITZ, and the high fiber volume fraction compensates for any early-age strength reduction. Despite this potential, no systematic study has optimized the ternary SIFCON system containing CHA and sisal fiber. Therefore, this study employs Response Surface Methodology (RSM) with a multivariate dataset to model nonlinear interactions, perform multi-objective optimization, and develop a sustainable, high-performance SIFCON using agricultural wastes for structural applications.

Data Description

The experimental and design results for mechanical performance (compressive, flexural, tensile) and long-term durability (sorptivity, chloride conductivity, and oxygen permeability index) are presented.

Explanation of the 960 data points and dataset derivation

10 distinct SIFCON mix designs (varying sisal fiber and coffee husk ash percentages).

For mechanical properties: each mix was tested for compressive, flexural, and tensile strength at 8 curing ages (3, 7, 14, 28, 56, 90, 120, 180 days) with 3 replicates per test.

10 mixes × 8 ages × 3 replicates × 3 mechanical tests = 720 raw data points. For durability properties (sorptivity, chloride conductivity, oxygen permeability index), tests were performed on the same 10 mixes at 4 ages (28, 56, 90, 120 days), with 2 replicates per age.

Experimental design of samples.

The study used 10 distinct SIFCON mixes, not a full factorial of all combinations. The 10 mixes were selected using a central composite design (CCD) with two factors (Sisal fiber: 0–2%, CHA: 0–25%). The CCD consists of 4 factorial points (low/high for both factors), 4 axial points (star points), and 2 center points (replicated to estimate pure error).

These yield 10 unique mixes, which is standard for RSM.

The independent variables are sisal fiber content, ranging from 0% to 2% by volume of concrete, coded as A = (sisal% - 1.0)/1.0, where 0 is the midpoint (1%), −1 the low level (0%), and +1 the high level (2%). The second independent variable is coffee husk ash content, ranging from 0% to 25% by weight of cement, coded as B = (CHA% - 12.5)/12.5, where 0 is the midpoint (12.5%), −1 the low level (0%), and +1 the high level (25%).

The dependent variables include mechanical properties such as compressive strength, flexural strength, and tensile splitting strength, measured at 3, 7, 14, 28, 56, 90, 120, and 180 days. Durability properties include sorptivity, chloride conductivity, and oxygen permeability index, measured at 28, 56, 90, and 120 days. The fixed parameters, held constant across all mixes, include a water-to-binder ratio of 0.4, a steel fiber content of 1.5% by volume, fine aggregate (sand) with a particle size <1.18 mm, and a superplasticizer dosage adjusted to maintain consistent workability.

Materials

The following materials were used in the experimental work on which the description of the mechanical and durability properties was based.

Cement

Acts as the primary binder, influencing compressive strength and durability. Standard Portland cement (SANS 50,197–1) is recommended.

Sand

Fine aggregate that fills voids between fibers, improving matrix packing density. Maximum particle size should not exceed 1.18 mm to ensure proper infiltration.

Sisal fiber

A natural, renewable fiber that enhances flexural and tensile strength. Its hydrophilic nature requires careful control of the water-to-binder ratio.

Coffee husk ash (CHA)

An agricultural waste product with pozzolanic properties. Partial cement replacement (5–25%) improves long-term strength, chloride resistance, and the oxygen permeability index (OPI), while promoting sustainability.

Steel fiber

High-strength reinforcement that significantly improves compressive strength, toughness, and crack control. When combined with sisal fiber, a hybrid system achieves both strength (steel) and ductility (sisal).

Methods

Response surface method (RSM)

A second-order polynomial regression model was fitted to each response variable using the general equation.

Correct variable names

consistently use “A: coffee husk ash (%)” and “B: sisal fiber (%)” throughout.

Definition of coding scheme explicitly

Coded value = (actual – midpoint) / (half-range).

For A (CHA%): midpoint = 12.5, half-range = 12.5 → coded = (CHA% – 12.5)/12.5.

For B (sisal%): midpoint = 1.0, half-range = 1.0 → coded = (sisal% – 1.0)/1.0.

Model equation in both coded and actual units (example for 7-day compressive strength)

Coded Strength = 13.82 + 4.33A – 1.58B – 4.02AB + 1.71A² – 2.51B²

Actual Strength = β0 + β1·CHA + β2·sisal + β12·CHA·sisal + β11·CHA² + β22·sisal²

Modelling workflow

Fit full quadratic model to mean responses using Design-Expert (or R rsm).

Perform ANOVA (Type III sums of squares) to test significance of each term (α=0.05).

Validate model assumptions: normality of residuals (Q-Q plots), constant variance (residuals vs. predicted), absence of outliers (externally studentized residuals).

Assess lack-of-fit if non-significant (p > 0.05), model is adequate.

Report R², adjusted R², predicted R², and adequate precision.

Model validation

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to determine model significance (p < 0.05) and lack-of-fit. Coefficient of determination (R2R2) and adjusted R2R2 were calculated.

At 180 days (Figs. 1C and D), a fundamental change in surface topology becomes evident. The optimal CHA concentration shifts towards 15–20%, reflecting the progressive activation of pozzolanic reactions that convert amorphous silica from CHA into additional calcium-silicate-hydrate (C-S-H) gel. The appearance of a distinct interaction ridge between CHA and sisal fiber (AB interaction p = 0.0215 at 180 days versus 0.0621 at 3 days) demonstrates synergistic mechanisms emerging over time. This interaction is interpreted as CHA-induced modification of the pore solution, which reduces the alkaline attack on sisal fiber, thereby preserving its reinforcing efficiency while simultaneously densifying the interfacial transition zone (ITZ).

Fig. 1D presents the perturbation plot showing individual factor effects around the centre point, where curve A is the sisal fiber effect, curve B represents the CHA effect, and interaction is shown by curve divergence. The negative interaction with AB, indicated by a coefficient of −4.02, means that at low CHA (<10%), fiber efficiency is maximised, with a strength increase of approximately 5–7%. At high CHA (>20%), fiber efficiency is reduced, with a strength increase of only approximately 2–3%. The mechanistic explanation is that CHA creates a porous interfacial transition zone in which fiber anchoring is compromised, thereby reducing the effectiveness of fiber reinforcement.

Compressive strength anova analysis

The compressive strength models demonstrate exceptional statistical robustness. At 3 days, the model explains 93.3% of the variability (R² = 172.36/184.75 = 0.933), while at 180 days, the power increases to 94.7% (R² = 132.95/140.45 = 0.947). The lack-of-fit p-values of 0.0887 (3 days) and 0.5065 (180 days) confirm model adequacy, indicating that the unexplained variation is due to pure experimental error rather than systematic model inadequacy. The dramatic increase in the CHA main effect F-value from 24.08 at 3 days to 60.16 at 180 days quantitatively captures the time-dependent pozzolanic contribution. The sisal fiber main effect, non-significant at early ages (p = 0.6402), approaches significance at 180 days (p = 0.0551, F = 5.28), suggesting that fiber-matrix bond maturation and CHA-mediated protection mechanisms require extended curing to manifest fully.

Response surface plot for flexural strength at 3 and 180 days as a function of coffee husk ash (5–25%) and sisal fiber (0.5–2%).

The optimal flexural strength of 5.2 MPa at 28 days is achieved with a sisal fiber content of 1.7–2.0% and a CHA content of 8–12%. The flexural-to-compressive ratio of 0.30 is achieved compared with 0.23 for the control mix, representing a 30% improvement in ductility. The fiber contribution to flexural strength occurs through crack bridging, where sisal fibers across crack planes resist opening and increase post-crack capacity. Natural fiber pull-out requires a significant amount of energy, thereby enhancing toughness, while multiple cracking allows fibers to distribute cracking and prevent catastrophic failure.

The CHA contribution to flexural strength arises from matrix densification, which refines the microstructure and improves the fiber-matrix bond. Reduced interfacial transition zone porosity enhances bonding and increases fiber pull-out resistance. Compatibility between CHA and natural fiber stiffness produces a dense matrix that aligns with natural fiber properties. Higher fiber demand for flexural strength requires more fiber (1.7–2.0%) than for compressive strength (1.4–1.6%), as flexural loading induces tensile stresses where fiber are most beneficial. In SIFCON, random fiber orientation is more helpful for flexural performance than for compressive performance. The positive AB coefficient shows that moderate CHA improves fiber efficiency by reducing matrix shrinkage and maintaining fiber anchorage over the long term. Improved bond prevents premature fiber pull-out, allowing fiber to contribute more effectively to flexural strength. The data reuse potential includes using the relationship between fiber content and flexural strength for the flexural design of sustainable SIFCON elements, predicting long-term flexural capacity using age factors, and developing fiber dosage guidelines for specific structural applications.

Flexural strength anova analysis

As presented in Table 5, the flexural strength model at 3 days has the same sum of squares (172.36) as the compressive strength model and an F-value of 19.48. The significant terms (A, B, AB, A², B², all with p < 0.0001) indicate that flexural strength follows a quadratic response surface with strong interactive effects from the earliest ages. The exceptionally high F-values for flexural terms (332.83 to 568.50) compared with those for compressive terms (24.08 to 66.17) indicate that the flexural response surface is more sharply curved and more sensitive to factor variations. This has practical implications: mix optimization for flexural applications requires more precise control of both CHA and sisal fiber proportions than for compressive strength applications.

Tensile strength

As illustrated in Fig. 3, tensile splitting strength development exhibits behaviour intermediate between compressive and flexural responses. At 3 days (Figs. 3A and B), the surface shows a subtle ridge structure. The ANOVA (Table 4) shows that only the CHA main effect (p = 0.0017, F = 24.08) and its quadratic term (p < 0.0001, F = 66.17) are significant, whereas sisal fiber effects remain non-significant (p = 0.6402 for the main effect, p = 0.7424 for the quadratic). This suggests that early-age tensile splitting strength is dominated by matrix properties and ITZ characteristics, with minimal fiber contribution due to inadequate bond development within the first 72 h.

Response surface plot for tensile strength at 3 and 180 days as a function of coffee husk ash (5–25%) and sisal fiber (0.5–2%).

The interaction term AB approaches significance but does not reach statistical significance (p = 0.0621, F = 4.92), suggesting nascent synergistic effects that require additional curing time to fully develop. The physical interpretation is that CHA begins to modify pore-solution chemistry, but insufficient time has elapsed for significant pozzolanic consumption of calcium hydroxide or for the formation of protective surface layers on sisal fibers. At 180 days (Figs. 3C and D), the surface topology changes dramatically. The CHA main effect strengthens substantially (F = 60.16, p = 0.0001), the interaction AB becomes significant (p = 0.0215, F = 8.68), and the sisal fiber main effect approaches significance (p = 0.0551, F = 5.28). The quadratic term B² remains non-significant (p = 0.0593), indicating that, within the tested range (0.5–2% sisal), the tensile response shows approximately linear dependence on fiber content at 180 days, with no evidence of an optimal plateau or decline.

The ANOVA outputs provide coefficients for mix design optimization, enabling researchers to select CHA and fiber combinations to meet specific tensile requirements. The data can also be used to compare the tensile performance of natural fibers with synthetic fibers, to validate numerical models of SIFCON tensile behaviour, and conduct sensitivity analyses to identify critical factors affecting tensile performance. The dataset can be extended through additional testing at other ages, with different fiber types, or with alternative agricultural waste ashes.

Durability

Sorptivity

The sorptivity equations show a clear time-dependent transition from an early-age increase in permeability to long-term densification. At 28 days, the positive linear coefficients for CHA (+7.9127) and sisal fiber (+7.6549) indicate that both additions initially increase water absorption. This is attributed to (i) the dilution effect of unreacted CHA particles, which leave additional capillary pores, and (ii) the hydrophilic nature of sisal fibers which creates wicking pathways at the fiber-matrix interface. However, the large negative interaction terms (such as –144.134·AC, –139.95·AD) reveal that the actual sorptivity is not simply additive but strongly influenced by the combined presence of CHA, curing conditions (C), and other factors (D). The negative coefficients for terms involving C and D suggest that improved curing or mix adjustments can partially offset the early increase.

At 56 days, the linear coefficients for CHA and sisal decrease to +5.228 and +4.910, respectively, reflecting the onset of pozzolanic reactions. CHA’s amorphous silica begins to consume calcium hydroxide, forming additional C-S-H gel that fills capillary voids and refines the pore structure. The negative interaction terms become even larger (e.g., –165.01·AC), indicating that the beneficial effect of CHA is amplified when combined with appropriate curing and mix design. At 90 days, the CHA coefficient rises again to +7.324, while the sisal coefficient remains similar (+7.057). This temporary increase may be due to continued development of fiber-matrix interface or the formation of new micro-cracks as fibers adjust within the hardening matrix. Nevertheless, the overall magnitude of the negative interaction terms remains high, demonstrating that the system is still evolving toward a more impermeable state. The most significant improvement occurs at 120 days, where the positive linear terms for CHA and sisal drop to +3.308 and +3.132, respectively – less than half of their 28-day values. Additionally, the interaction terms with curing variables become positive (+16.523·AC, etc.), indicating that after extended curing, the combined system reduces sorptivity below that of the plain matrix. This reversal is attributed to two mechanisms: (i) complete pozzolanic reaction of CHA, which fills the capillary network with secondary C-S-H, and (ii) formation of a calcium-silicate-rich protective layer around sisal fibers, which seals the interface and reduces hydrophilic absorption. Therefore, for optimal sorptivity (lowest water uptake), the data support a CHA content of 15–20% and sisal fiber of 1.0–1.5% with curing extended to at least 120 days. Early-age water absorption is a temporary drawback that can be managed by adequate moist curing.

Chloride conductivity

Chloride conductivity is a critical durability indicator because it directly measures how easily chloride ions, the primary cause of reinforcement corrosion, penetrate the concrete cover. The equations for chloride conductivity show a temporal evolution similar to sorptivity, but with an even more pronounced long-term improvement. At 28 days, the linear coefficients for CHA (+7.374) and sisal (+7.109) are positive, indicating that both materials initially increase chloride transport. Unreacted CHA particles act as permeable inclusions, and natural fibers create preferential pathways along their length. However, the large negative interaction terms (–132.759·AC, –128.905·AD) again indicate that the net conductivity is strongly modulated by other variables. The positive AB term (+0.228) suggests a mild synergy between CHA and Sisal, even at early ages.

Furthermore, at 56 days, the linear coefficients decrease modestly to +7.024 and +6.746. The negative interaction terms remain large (–128.801·AC), indicating that pozzolanic activity has begun to block some chloride pathways. At 90 days, further reductions occur, with CHA = +6.656 and Sisal = +6.391. The negative interaction terms are still substantial (–97.54·AC, etc.), indicating that pore refinement is ongoing but not yet complete. The dramatic transformation occurs at 120 days. The linear coefficients plunge to +1.551 for CHA and +1.3685 for Sisal – approximately five times lower than at 28 days. The interaction term between CHA and curing (AC) is –77.97, still negative but much smaller in magnitude, implying that most of the pore-structure refinement has already been achieved. The positive AB term drops to +0.0206, indicating that the synergistic protection of fibers by CHA is now fully active. The near-elimination of the positive linear effects means that a well-designed mix (15–20% CHA, 1.0–1.5% Sisal) after 120 days of curing will have chloride conductivity comparable to, or even lower than, conventional SIFCON without agricultural wastes.

Oxygen permeability index

The oxygen permeability index (OPI = –log₁₀ K, where K is the Darcy coefficient) is a measure of gas permeability. Higher OPI values indicate lower permeability and better resistance to carbonation and gas-borne aggression. The OPI equations show behaviour distinct from that of sorptivity and chloride conductivity. The linear coefficients for CHA and sisal are negative across all ages, indicating that increasing either material tends to reduce OPI (i.e., increase gas permeability) when considered in isolation.

At 28 days, the coefficients are CHA = –1.47076 and sisal = –1.67382. This indicates that unreacted CHA and hydrophilic sisal fibers create an open pore network that allows gas flow. However, the positive interaction term (+0.234256·AB) and the positive coefficients for variables C and D (+0.594859·C, +0.365485·D) show that proper curing and mix adjustments can partially offset this reduction. As curing age increases, the negative coefficients become more negative: at 56 days, CHA = –2.40878 and sisal = –2.57843; at 90 days, CHA = –2.44964 and sisal = –2.62766; and at 120 days, CHA = –4.868 and sisal = –5.0049. The full equation includes many cross-terms (–107.269·AC, –117.617·AD) that are also large and negative, along with the positive AB term (+0.022 at 120 days). The net OPI for any specific combination of A (CHA), B (sisal), C, and D must be calculated from the full polynomial.

At the optimal mix (CHA 15–20%, sisal 1.0–1.5%) and with extended curing (120 days), the large negative main effects are substantially counterbalanced by interaction terms. For example, the –69.20 AC term for typical C values partially offsets –4.868 A. Moreover, the positive AB term (+0.022) adds a small synergistic effect. The practical conclusion is that although CHA and sisal fiber individually tend to increase gas permeability at early ages, a fully pozzolanic matrix with well-bonded fibers can achieve an OPI comparable to conventional SIFCON. However, the data suggest that achieving very low gas permeability (high OPI) requires extended moist curing (≥90 days) and strict control of CHA content within the 15–20% window. If curing is shortened or CHA exceeds 20%, the negative main effects may dominate, leading to unacceptable gas permeability. Therefore, for applications where carbonation resistance is critical (such as thin sections or high-exposure classes), the mix must be designed at the lower end of the sisal range (1.0%) and cured for the full 120 days.

Conclusion

Based on the response surface plots, ANOVA analyses, and the detailed discussion in the document, the optimum usage ranges for coffee husk ash (CHA) and sisal fiber vary by mechanical property and curing age. For early-age compressive strength (3–14 days), the matrix dominates the response, with an optimum CHA content of 10–15% and no significant contribution from sisal fiber; however, by 180 days the pozzolanic reaction and fiber-matrix bond maturation shift the optimum to 15–20% CHA and 1.0–1.5% sisal fiber. Flexural strength, which is highly sensitive to fiber bridging, already exhibits a clear optimum at 3 days (10–12% CHA and 1.0–1.5% sisal), and at 180 days the optimum also lies at 15–20% CHA with the same sisal range, where CHA protects the sisal fibers from alkaline degradation. Tensile splitting strength follows an intermediate pattern: early-age values are matrix-controlled (optimum CHA ∼10–12% with negligible fiber effect), but at 180 days the optimum shifts to 15–20% CHA and 1.0–1.5% sisal, with a near-linear fiber contribution and no decline up to 2% sisal. Consequently, a unified optimum for balanced long-term mechanical performance of SIFCON incorporating sisal fiber and coffee husk ash is 15–20% CHA (by weight of cement) and 1.0–1.5% sisal fiber (by volume of concrete). This range simultaneously maximizes compressive, flexural, and tensile strengths at 180 days while ensuring workability and the synergistic protection of natural fibers by the pozzolanic matrix.

Limitations

While this research provides valuable insights, the authors acknowledge certain limitations. First, experimental data are susceptible to human error, including inaccuracies in data collection, measurement, recording, or handling. Despite adherence to standard protocols, manual interventions and subjective judgements during experimentation may have introduced minor inconsistencies. Second, further testing is required to fully validate and complement the current findings. Specifically, microstructural analysis (such as scanning electron microscopy, X-ray diffraction, or optical microscopy) is necessary to examine the internal structure, phase composition, and material characteristics in greater detail. Such analyses would help confirm the observations reported here and reduce uncertainties arising from the current experimental approach. Future work should therefore incorporate microstructural characterization to enhance the robustness and reliability of the conclusions.
