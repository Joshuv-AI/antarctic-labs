Longitudinal dynamic relationships among social frailty, depression, cognitive function, and physical frailty in community-dwelling older adults: A random intercept cross-lagged panel model study
Authors: Anni Wu, Sheng Sun, Xiaoshuai Huang, Hui Yin, Yuxin Sun, Bolun Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSocial frailty is a critical indicator of social health in older adults. While its links to physical and mental health are recognized, longitudinal evidence integrating social frailty, depressive symptoms, cognitive function, and physical frailty is limited.ObjectiveTo examine the time-ordered and longitudinal predictive associations among these four domains in urban community-dwelling older adults.DesignA longitudinal study using a random intercept cross-lagged panel model (RI-CLPM).Setting(s)Urban communities in China.Participants3503 community-dwelling adults aged ≥60 years from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (2013–2018).MethodsSocial frailty was assessed using a proxy measure. RI-CLPM was applied to estimate between-person associations and within-person cross-lagged associations across three time points.ResultsThe model showed good fit (CFI = 0.968, TLI = 0.923, RMSEA = 0.049, SRMR = 0.026). At the between-person level, social frailty was positively correlated with depressive symptoms (r = 0.352, p < .001) and negatively correlated with cognitive function (r = −0.622, p < .001); depressive symptoms were positively correlated with physical frailty (r = 0.370, p < .001). At the within-person level, autoregressive effects differed across time intervals—for example, social frailty T1→T2 (β = 0.074, p = .008), depressive symptoms T1→T2 (β = 0.054, p = .047), cognitive function T1→T2 (β = 0.068, p = .012), and physical frailty T2→T3 (β = 0.120, p < .001). Cross-lagged paths indicated that depressive symptoms at T1 were associated with higher physical frailty at T2 (β = 0.045, p < .05), and physical frailty at T2 predicted higher depressive symptoms at T3 (β = 0.051, p < .05). In addition, depressive symptoms at T2 predicted lower cognitive function at T3 (β = −0.047, p < .05), and cognitive function at T2 predicted lower physical frailty at T3 (β = −0.041, p < .05). Several within-wave residual correlations were significant, indicating concurrent associations among the constructs within the same time point beyond the modeled longitudinal pathways. (e.g., depressive symptoms with physical frailty and cognitive function). These were mainly observed between depressive symptoms and physical frailty and between depressive symptoms and cognitive function (e.g., T2: r = 0.260 and r = −0.110; T3: r = 0.231; all p < .001).ConclusionsSocial frailty is primarily associated with psychological and cognitive status at the between-person level, whereas depressive symptoms and physical frailty exhibit bidirectional time-ordered associations. Background

With the accelerating pace of global population aging, older adults’ health challenges have become increasingly multidimensional and dynamically intertwined. The World Health Organization projects that by 2050, the global population aged 60 years and older will exceed 2 billion, accounting for approximately 22% of the world’s population (United Nations, 2022; World Health Organization, 2024), making healthy ageing a major public health priority (World Health Organization, 2015). Healthy ageing emphasizes not only disease control but also the maintenance of overall functional ability across physical, psychological, cognitive, and social domains (Beard et al., 2016). Accordingly, examining the dynamic relationships among key health dimensions from a systems perspective is essential for identifying high-risk groups and developing integrated intervention strategies. This multidimensional view is also consistent with the biopsychosocial model widely used in aging research, which emphasizes the interaction of biological, psychological, and social factors in shaping health outcomes (Engel, 1977; Wade and Halligan, 2017).

In gerontological research, physical frailty, cognitive decline, and depressive symptoms are considered core factors affecting quality of life and mortality risk (Fried et al., 2001; Soysal et al., 2017; Grande et al., 2019). In recent years, researchers have further argued that social functioning should be incorporated into the frailty framework, leading to the concept of “multidimensional frailty,” which better reflects older adults’ real-world functional status (Bunt et al., 2017; Dent et al., 2019). Social frailty is defined as a state of being at risk of losing—or having lost—social resources, activities, and abilities needed to meet basic social needs (Bunt et al., 2017). It has been linked to adverse outcomes such as depressive symptoms, cognitive decline, disability, and mortality (Ma et al., 2018; Yamada and Arai, 2018; Qi and Li, 2022; Li et al., 2023). Compared with traditional indicators of social participation, social frailty more comprehensively captures structural vulnerability in social connectedness, role functioning, and support systems among older adults (Makizako et al., 2015), and thus has become an increasing focus in international aging research and community nursing practice. However, it should be noted that the operationalization of social frailty varies across studies, particularly when secondary datasets are used, which may affect comparability of findings.

Longitudinal studies suggest that health problems in later life may prospectively predict one another over time. For example, social frailty has been shown to predict increases in depressive symptoms (Hoogendijk et al., 2019), while depression may contribute to social withdrawal and reduced participation, thereby facilitating the development of social frailty (Cacioppo et al., 2006). Bidirectional associations between physical frailty and depression have been repeatedly reported and may be reinforced through pathways involving inflammation, neuroendocrine dysregulation, and reduced behavioral activity (Chu et al., 2019). Cognitive decline is also closely related to physical frailty, and some studies suggest that they may share underlying mechanisms such as neurodegeneration, chronic inflammation, and vascular pathology (Fulop et al., 2010). In addition, social frailty may act as a social-behavioral pathway linking cognitive deterioration to physical frailty by shaping activity levels, health behaviors, and access to social support (Kojima, 2015; Kuiper et al., 2015). These findings, however, should be interpreted as longitudinal associations rather than evidence of deterministic causal pathways.

Despite the growing body of evidence, three major gaps remain in the current literature. First, most studies have focused on pairwise associations and lack a comprehensive longitudinal examination that integrates social frailty, depressive symptoms, cognitive function, and physical frailty within a single analytical framework, limiting insight into the interactive structure and co-evolving pathways among multidimensional health problems (Dent et al., 2019). Second, methodologically, many studies rely on traditional regression approaches or the classic cross-lagged panel model (CLPM). Although these methods can estimate lagged associations, they do not disentangle stable between-person differences from within-person fluctuations, which may lead to biased interpretations regarding directionality and association strength (Hamaker et al., 2015; Mulder and Hamaker, 2021). In particular, these approaches may conflate stable individual differences (e.g., some individuals consistently having higher levels of depression) with within-person changes over time, limiting the ability to capture true temporal dynamics. Third, the role of social frailty within the multidimensional health network remains debated: some studies suggest that social frailty has independent predictive value (Ma et al., 2018; Yamada and Arai, 2018; Qi and Li, 2022; Li et al., 2023), whereas others indicate substantial covariance with depression and physical frailty and that its dynamic associations may attenuate after controlling for stable individual differences (Teo et al., 2017; Li et al., 2023). Therefore, longitudinal models that can separate between-person and within-person effects are needed to more rigorously evaluate the role of social frailty in multidimensional health changes.

To address these gaps, the present study uses three waves of follow-up data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) collected in 2013, 2015, and 2018. We applied a random intercept cross-lagged panel model (RI-CLPM) to examine bidirectional cross-time associations between social frailty and cognitive function, depressive symptoms, and physical frailty, while also evaluating the dynamic coupling among depression, cognition, and physical frailty after adjusting for baseline covariates. In this study, time-ordered associations refer to longitudinal predictive relationships in which earlier deviations in one construct are associated with later deviations in another construct after accounting for prior levels and stable between-person differences, rather than deterministic causal effects. RI-CLPM effectively separates stable between-person differences from within-person change processes, providing a more rigorous framework for interpreting time-ordered relationships among key health domains in later life (Hamaker et al., 2015; Mulder and Hamaker, 2021). Importantly, we adopt a cautious, hypothesis-testing framing regarding social frailty, given ongoing debates about whether its within-person dynamic effects remain after accounting for stable individual differences. These findings may offer theoretical support for community nursing practice by highlighting the importance of simultaneously monitoring coordinated changes in social, psychological, cognitive, and physical functioning to optimize early identification and integrated management strategies.

Methods

Data and sample

Data were drawn from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative longitudinal survey using a multistage stratified sampling design to systematically assess the health, social, and economic conditions of middle-aged and older adults in China. The national baseline survey was conducted in 2011 and covered 28 provinces, 150 counties/districts, and 450 villages/communities, collecting information from 17,708 respondents. Data were obtained through standardized structured questionnaires administered via face-to-face household interviews by uniformly trained interviewers. Follow-up surveys have been conducted every 2–3 years to track changes in respondents’ health status. To date, five waves have been completed (2011, 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020).

This study used the 2013, 2015, and 2018 waves as the analytical dataset for three reasons: (1) the follow-up intervals are relatively balanced and suitable for longitudinal dynamic modeling; (2) key health, psychological, and social variables were measured consistently across these waves, supporting comparability; and (3) the 2020 wave was affected by the COVID-19 pandemic, with constrained measurement for some variables and reduced follow-up rates, and was therefore not included.

The baseline sample consisted of 18,612 respondents who successfully completed the 2013 interview. To enhance sample homogeneity, the analytical sample was restricted to participants aged ≥60 years in 2013. In addition, participants living in rural areas were excluded. This restriction was made because rural and urban older adults may differ substantially in social participation opportunities, family support structures, access to health and community services, and social resource environments, all of which may influence the manifestation of social frailty and its longitudinal associations with depressive symptoms, cognitive function, and physical frailty. Therefore, the present study focused specifically on urban community-dwelling older adults.

On this basis, all individuals who participated in at least one of the follow-up interviews in 2013, 2015, and 2018 were included in the initial analytical sample. Given the inevitable occurrence of wave nonresponse and item nonresponse in longitudinal surveys, we did not use complete-case analysis; instead, missing data were handled using multiple imputation to maximize sample retention and reduce selection bias. The final analytical sample comprised 3503 urban community-dwelling individuals from the 2013 baseline cohort. Among them, 2863 and 2532 participants completed the 2015 and 2018 follow-up interviews, respectively. Measures

Social frailty

Social frailty was assessed using a proxy measure derived from available CHARLS items rather than the original Social Frailty Index (SFI). The original Social Frailty Index is a validated measure in Asian populations with good predictive validity (Teo et al., 2017; Li et al., 2024). Because CHARLS did not include all items of the original Social Frailty Index, we selected conceptually comparable CHARLS items to approximate key domains of social frailty, consistent with previous studies using secondary survey datasets to operationalize social frailty and related social vulnerability constructs. Therefore, the measure used in this study should be interpreted as a CHARLS-derived proxy social frailty measure rather than the original Social Frailty Index. The proxy social frailty measure included items reflecting key dimensions of social vulnerability, including living arrangement, social participation, interpersonal contact, perceived social support, and social engagement/activity. Each item was coded so that higher values indicated greater social vulnerability. The item coding scheme and corresponding CHARLS questions are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Each item is coded as 1 when the social frailty deficit was present and 0 when the deficit was absent, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 7, with higher scores indicating greater social frailty. For descriptive analyses, the mean and standard deviation of the total proxy social frailty score were reported to characterize the distribution.

The internal consistency of the proxy social frailty measure was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha, which was 0.711 in the analytical sample, indicating acceptable internal consistency for a multidimensional research construct. Given that social frailty is a multidimensional construct, this reliability estimate was interpreted together with the conceptual correspondence between the CHARLS items and the domains of social frailty. For longitudinal modeling, the total proxy social frailty score was included as a continuous variable to retain information on within-person changes over time and improve estimation of within-person fluctuations. Although individual items were dichotomously coded, the summed total score was treated as a continuous variable in longitudinal analyses.

Physical frailty

Physical frailty was operationalized using a Frailty Index (FI) constructed from CHARLS data to quantify age-related accumulation of health deficits (Searle et al., 2008). The FI included 32 deficit indicators covering functional limitations, chronic diseases, and psychological- and cognition-related health problems. Notably, cognition-related items within the FI were used only to indicate the presence versus absence of cognition-related deficits (e.g., memory or cognitive impairment) and were dichotomously coded (0 = no deficit, 1 = deficit). These items were not intended to capture levels or individual differences in cognitive performance. All deficit indicators were standardized, summed, divided by the total number of deficits (32), and multiplied by 100 to yield a continuous FI score theoretically ranging from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating more severe physical frailty. In longitudinal analyses, the FI was modeled as a continuous variable to characterize dynamic changes in physical frailty over time.

Cognition

Cognitive function was assessed using three components: (1) episodic memory (range: 0–10), calculated as the average of immediate word recall (range: 0–10) and delayed word recall (range: 0–10); (2) orientation and attention, assessed using items from the Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status (TICS-10), including date/weekday/season naming (range: 0–5) and serial 7 subtraction (range: 0–5); and (3) visuoconstruction, measured by a figure-drawing task (range: 0–1). Scores from all components were summed to obtain a global cognition score ranging from 0 to 21 (Sha et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2022), with higher scores indicating better cognitive function. In this study, cognition was included as a continuous variable to represent overall cognitive ability across memory, orientation, attention, and visuoconstruction. It should be noted that cognition-related FI items reflect only the presence of cognitive deficits, whereas the cognition total score captures cognitive performance level; thus, the two are conceptually and analytically independent.

Depression

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the validated Chinese version of the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D 10), with total scores ranging from 0 to 30. Higher scores indicate more severe depressive symptoms (Yuan et al., 2023). The CES-D 10 total score was modeled as a continuous variable in the longitudinal analyses.

Control variables

Based on the literature, we included control variables strongly associated with social frailty, physical frailty, depressive symptoms, and cognitive function. These covariates included demographic factors (Qiu et al., 2024) (age, sex, marital status), sleep characteristics (Huang et al., 2024; Cai et al., 2025) (sleep duration), health status (Chen et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025) (number of chronic diseases), and functional status (Zhou et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2024) (ADL/IADL limitations). To avoid increasing model complexity by introducing time-varying covariates, all control variables were measured at baseline (2013) and were included as predictors of the random intercepts (between-person level). This specification was intended to explain stable between-person differences without interfering with estimation of within-person temporal dynamics.

Statistical analysis

We used the Random Intercept Cross-Lagged Panel Model (RI-CLPM) to examine longitudinal associations among social frailty, physical frailty, depressive symptoms, and cognitive function across three time points (2013, 2015, and 2018). By incorporating random intercepts, RI-CLPM decomposes observed measures into stable between-person components and within-person time-varying fluctuations. This approach enables estimation of within-person autoregressive and cross-lagged associations while accounting for time-invariant individual characteristics.

In this study, “longitudinal predictive associations” refer to statistical associations in which earlier within-person deviations in one construct are associated with later within-person deviations in another construct after accounting for prior levels. “Time-ordered relationships” refer to the temporal ordering of these associations across waves. These terms do not imply deterministic causal effects.

Model building followed a stepwise strategy. Model 1 included only autoregressive paths and within-wave residual correlations to evaluate temporal stability. Model 2 added cross-lagged paths to test longitudinal predictive associations among constructs. Model 3 further included baseline covariates as predictors of the random intercepts to explain stable between-person differences. To evaluate temporal stationarity, we tested equality constraints on adjacent autoregressive and cross-lagged paths and compared constrained versus unconstrained models using multiple fit indices (CFI, TLI, RMSEA, SRMR) and information criteria (AIC/BIC). Considering overall model fit and parameter stability, the final model retained time-interval–specific estimates, separately estimating autoregressive and cross-lagged effects for T1→T2 and T2→T3, and reporting time-specific dynamic association patterns accordingly.

Missing data were handled using multiple imputation. We generated 20 imputed datasets using chained equations in the mice package. Appropriate imputation methods were selected according to variable type, including predictive mean matching, logistic regression, or multinomial regression. RI-CLPM was fitted separately in each imputed dataset, and parameter estimates and standard errors were pooled using Rubin’s rules.

All core constructs were modeled as continuous variables to retain within-person change information and improve statistical power. Models were estimated using robust maximum likelihood (MLR). We reported CFI, TLI, RMSEA, and SRMR as primary fit indices, and AIC and BIC for model comparison. Given the observational design, the three-wave structure, and the use of secondary survey data, results were interpreted as longitudinal predictive associations and time-ordered relationships and should not be interpreted as evidence of deterministic or causal relationships.

Between-person level

At the between-person level (representing stable individual differences across the study period), several significant correlations were observed among the random intercepts. The random intercept of social frailty was positively correlated with depressive symptoms (β = 0.352, p < 0.001) and negatively correlated with cognitive function (β = −0.622, p < 0.001), whereas its association with physical frailty was not statistically significant (β = 0.101, p = 0.087). The random intercept of depressive symptoms was negatively correlated with cognitive function (β = −0.316, p < 0.001) and positively correlated with physical frailty (β = 0.370, p < 0.001). The between-person correlation between cognitive function and physical frailty was not significant (β = 0.018, p = 0.733).

Multiple baseline covariates significantly predicted the random intercepts. Age was positively associated with social frailty and physical frailty and negatively associated with cognitive function. Sex, marital status, number of chronic diseases, ADL/IADL limitations, and sleep duration were also associated with between-person differences across the four constructs (see Table 4 for details).

Within-person level: autoregressive effects

With respect to within-person temporal stability (i.e., whether deviations from an individual’s expected level persist over time), the autoregressive patterns differed across constructs and intervals. Social frailty, depressive symptoms, and cognitive function showed significant autoregressive paths from T1 to T2, but no significant stability from T2 to T3. In contrast, physical frailty showed a non-significant autoregressive effect from T1 to T2, but a significant positive autoregressive effect from T2 to T3.

Within-person level: cross-lagged effects

Only a small number of within-person cross-lagged associations were statistically significant. Specifically, higher-than-expected depressive symptoms at T1 were associated with higher-than-expected physical frailty at T2 (β = 0.045, p < 0.05), and higher-than-expected physical frailty at T2 was associated with higher-than-expected depressive symptoms at T3 (β = 0.051, p < 0.05). In addition, higher-than-expected depressive symptoms at T2 were associated with lower-than-expected cognitive function at T3 (β = −0.047, p < 0.05), and higher-than-expected cognitive function at T2 was associated with lower-than-expected physical frailty at T3 (β = −0.041, p < 0.05). All other cross-lagged paths were not statistically significant.

Within-wave residual correlations

After accounting for between-person differences, autoregressive effects, and cross-lagged effects, some within-wave residual correlations remained significant, particularly between depressive symptoms and physical frailty, and between depressive symptoms and cognitive function. These residual correlations indicate that, within the same measurement occasion, deviations from individuals’ expected levels in these constructs tended to co-occur, suggesting the presence of shared short-term influences or unmeasured time-specific factors affecting these variables simultaneously.

Overall, the RI-CLPM results indicate that depressive symptoms, cognitive function, and physical frailty show a small number of statistically significant time-ordered within-person associations, whereas the longitudinal role of social frailty is primarily reflected at the between-person level as stable individual differences, rather than as consistent within-person cross-time associations in this three-wave design.

Discussion

This study used three waves of longitudinal data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS; 2013–2018) and applied a Random Intercept Cross-Lagged Panel Model (RI-CLPM) to systematically examine the dynamic associations among social frailty, depressive symptoms, cognitive function, and physical frailty at both the between-person and within-person levels. By disentangling stable between-person differences from within-person fluctuations over time, the RI-CLPM provides evidence on the temporal ordering of these multidimensional frailty-related constructs while partly accounting for time-invariant individual differences. Importantly, given the observational design, the interpretations below focus on longitudinal predictive associations and time-ordered relationships rather than strict causal inference. In addition, because the analytical sample was restricted to urban community-dwelling older adults, the generalisability of the findings to rural populations or other settings should be interpreted with caution.

Bidirectional temporal links between depressive symptoms and physical frailty: evidence for psychological–physiological coupling

At the within-person level, the RI-CLPM identified significant bidirectional cross-lagged associations between depressive symptoms and physical frailty: higher-than-expected depressive symptoms at baseline were associated with higher-than-expected physical frailty at the subsequent wave, and higher-than-expected physical frailty was associated with higher-than-expected depressive symptoms at the following wave. It should be emphasized that RI-CLPM captures time-ordered associations after accounting for stable individual differences and does not imply a deterministic causal loop. Nevertheless, this pattern suggests potential dynamic coupling between psychological and physiological functioning in later life.

From a mechanistic perspective, several plausible pathways may help explain why depressive symptoms and physical frailty are longitudinally linked. First, biological changes such as chronic low-grade inflammation and immune activation, as well as dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, may interact with sarcopenia, fatigue, and functional decline, thereby elevating frailty risk. Second, depression-related reductions in physical activity, inadequate nutritional intake, sleep disturbance, and other adverse health behaviors may further accelerate functional deterioration. Conversely, physical frailty—via mobility restrictions, chronic pain, and growing dependence in daily functioning—may increase the risk of depressive symptoms through psychosocial mechanisms such as reduced self-efficacy, decreased social participation, and greater exposure to stress. Recent large-sample studies using genetic instrumental-variable approaches also suggest reciprocal associations between frailty and depression and implicate inflammatory and brain-structural factors, providing biological and methodological support for the longitudinal pattern observed in the present study (Jiang et al., 2024).

In addition, depressive symptoms negatively predicted subsequent cognitive function. This finding is consistent with prior reviews and systematic evaluations (Wu et al., 2021; Ma et al., 2025), which indicate that depressive symptoms are associated with cognitive decline and elevated dementia risk and may shape cognitive trajectories in later life (Diniz et al., 2013). Taken together, depressive symptoms may represent an important correlate within a psychological–physiological–cognitive frailty network. From a practice perspective, these results support the value of multidomain assessment in community-based care, rather than a single-domain approach. Early identification of depressive symptoms and physical frailty may help identify older adults with elevated multidimensional risk, although intervention effects should be tested directly in future experimental or quasi-experimental studies.

Social frailty and health outcomes: stable between-person differences and a background risk context

At the within-person level, social frailty did not show significant cross-lagged effects across adjacent waves, suggesting limited short-term predictive influence on changes in depressive symptoms, cognitive function, or physical frailty. However, at the between-person level, social frailty exhibited significant and stable correlations with depressive symptoms and cognitive function, indicating that social frailty may reflect a relatively enduring social risk state rather than a short-term trigger.

This pattern aligns closely with long-term evidence on social relationships and cognitive health. In a systematic review and meta-analysis of longitudinal cohort studies, Kuiper and colleagues reported that poorer social relationships (e.g., social isolation and insufficient social support) are significantly associated with faster cognitive decline, with consistent findings across countries and populations (Kuiper et al., 2016). Such work underscores that social exposures may influence cognitive and mental health through cumulative, long-term pathways rather than manifesting as short-term lagged effects.

More recently, Sakimoto et al. reported that among older adults with social frailty, higher levels of cognitive activity were associated with a lower risk of dementia, suggesting that social frailty may operate as a contextual vulnerability that interacts with or modifies other protective or risk factors related to cognitive outcomes (Sakimoto et al., 2025). This supports viewing social frailty as a background social exposure rather than a rapidly reversible state.

In light of our results, social frailty may primarily shape overall risk profiles for psychological and cognitive health at the between-person level, and its impact may not be expressed via short-term cross-lagged pathways in a three-wave design. Thus, the absence of significant within-person cross-lagged effects does not imply that social frailty is unimportant; rather, it suggests that its influence may be slower, indirect, or context dependent. This interpretation should also be considered in light of the use of a CHARLS-derived proxy measure of social frailty rather than the original Social Frailty Index. Future studies with longer follow-up, more measurement waves, validated social frailty instruments, or explicit mediation/moderation models are needed to further clarify the role of social frailty in multidimensional frailty-related changes.

Differential stability across domains highlights the dynamic evolution of multidimensional frailty

Within-person autoregressive analyses revealed domain-specific and interval-specific stability patterns. Social frailty, depressive symptoms, and cognitive function showed significant autoregressive effects from T1 to T2, but their stability weakened from T2 to T3. In contrast, physical frailty showed a non-significant autoregressive effect from T1 to T2 but stronger temporal stability from T2 to T3. These differences suggest that multidimensional frailty does not follow a single, constant trajectory; rather, its temporal dynamics vary across domains and potentially across stages of later-life development.

This pattern is consistent with prior longitudinal research indicating that psychological and cognitive indicators are often more sensitive to contextual factors (e.g., health shocks or social-environmental changes), whereas physical functioning is more strongly influenced by chronic disease burden and long-term health behavior patterns, which may yield greater stability at later stages (Cui et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2023). From this perspective, the observed interval-specific autoregressive effects may reflect differences in “plasticity” versus “stability” across domains, although alternative explanations such as measurement interval, attrition, and unmeasured time-varying factors cannot be excluded.

Notably, asymmetry in autoregressive effects across intervals also suggests that the stationarity assumption often embedded in RI-CLPM may not fully hold in older cohorts. Different domains may become more or less dominant at different stages of frailty progression, producing time-varying dependency structures. This highlights the importance of interpreting stability as a dynamic life-course feature rather than a fixed attribute. Practically, these differences may inform hypotheses about the timing of assessment and prevention, but they should not be interpreted as direct evidence that specific interventions would be effective at particular stages without further testing.

Covariate findings identify vulnerable profiles in multidimensional frailty

After including multiple baseline covariates at the between-person level, demographic characteristics, health status, and functional limitations explained meaningful stable differences in social frailty, depressive symptoms, cognitive function, and physical frailty. Specifically, older age and higher chronic disease burden were associated with less favorable levels of physical frailty and cognitive function, consistent with evidence linking cumulative deficits and functional decline in later life (Makizako et al., 2021). Chronic diseases may accelerate physical and cognitive deterioration through persistent inflammation, activity restriction, and increased healthcare burden.

Regarding sex differences, women in this sample showed lower risk for social frailty, depressive symptoms, and physical frailty and relatively better cognitive performance than men. This pattern does not fully align with some previous findings suggesting higher health risks among older women (Katayama et al., 2022). One possible explanation is that, in the Chinese context, older women may have denser family support networks and more frequent social engagement, which could buffer psychological and functional decline. However, this interpretation requires further verification using more detailed measures of social support and gender roles.

Marital status also showed significant associations: participants who were unmarried, widowed, or divorced were more likely to exhibit higher social frailty and depressive symptoms, consistent with literature indicating that the absence of a spouse can weaken social ties, emotional support, and perceived security (Qiu et al., 2024). This further underscores the importance of close social relationships for maintaining social and psychological functioning in later life.

Functional limitations (ADL and IADL) were strong predictors across all four core constructs, supporting prior evidence that declining independence in daily activities is an early signal of multidimensional frailty (Zhou et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2024). Functional limitation not only reflects reduced physical capability but may also restrict social participation and increase psychological burden, thereby affecting depressive symptoms and cognitive function. In addition, sleep duration was associated with lower depressive symptoms and lower social frailty risk, suggesting a relatively stable protective relationship. This aligns with recent findings from CHARLS and CLHLS-based studies indicating that adequate sleep may benefit psychological and social health by regulating affect, restoring cognitive resources, and stabilizing physiological rhythms (Chen et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025).

Overall, these covariate findings are not intended to support direct intervention claims, but they help characterize vulnerability profiles in multidimensional frailty. The results provide empirical grounding for future research on risk stratification mechanisms and potential entry points for tailored community-based health management.

Limitations

This study used three-wave longitudinal data from CHARLS and applied an RI-CLPM to examine the dynamic associations among social frailty, depressive symptoms, cognitive function, and physical frailty; however, several limitations should be noted.

First, the analytic sample was restricted to urban community-dwelling older adults aged ≥60 years, which may limit generalizability to rural populations or institutionalized older adults. In addition, baseline selection resulted in systematic differences between included and excluded participants, suggesting potential selection bias. Second, attrition across waves was not random. Participants who remained in the study differed from those lost to follow-up in multiple health and functional indicators, which may have led to underestimation of associations among more vulnerable individuals. Third, social frailty was measured using a proxy derived from CHARLS items rather than the original Social Frailty Index, which may introduce measurement differences and limit comparability with studies using validated instruments. Fourth, although RI-CLPM allows separation of between-person and within-person components and supports examination of time-ordered associations, residual confounding and reverse causality cannot be ruled out, and the findings should not be interpreted as evidence of causal effects. Finally, the three-wave design with fixed follow-up intervals may not capture shorter-term fluctuations or longer-term cumulative processes, which could influence the detection of within-person associations.

Conclusions

Using three waves of CHARLS data (2013–2018) and a Random Intercept Cross-Lagged Panel Model (RI-CLPM) to separate stable between-person differences from within-person fluctuations over time, this study examined the temporal associations among social frailty, depressive symptoms, cognitive function, and physical frailty.

At the between-person level, social frailty was stably and significantly positively associated with depressive symptoms and negatively associated with cognitive function, indicating that individuals with higher levels of social frailty tended to exhibit less favorable psychological and cognitive profiles over the study period.

At the within-person level, the statistically significant time-ordered associations were concentrated in the bidirectional links between depressive symptoms and physical frailty, as well as the negative prospective association from depressive symptoms to subsequent cognitive function and from cognitive function to subsequent physical frailty. In contrast, most cross-lagged associations involving social frailty were not statistically significant within the observed time frame, suggesting that its role may be more closely related to stable between-person differences than to short-term within-person changes in this three-wave design.

Given the observational design and potential selection and attrition biases, these findings should be interpreted as longitudinal predictive associations rather than definitive causal effects. The results contribute to a more nuanced understanding of how social, psychological, cognitive, and physical domains are temporally related at different analytical levels in older adults.

From a practice perspective, the findings highlight the potential value of incorporating social frailty into multidimensional risk assessment frameworks in community settings, and of considering social, psychological, cognitive, and physical domains jointly when monitoring older adults’ health status. However, the present study does not provide direct evidence regarding the effectiveness of specific interventions.

Future studies with more measurement waves, higher-frequency follow-ups, and methodological extensions are warranted to further examine underlying pathways and to evaluate whether these observed associations translate into actionable intervention strategies.
