Bilateral microsporidial keratoconjunctivitis in an orthokeratology lens wearer: a case report and literature review
Authors: Rongbin Liang, Bei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMicrosporidial keratoconjunctivitis is a rare but serious ocular infection that can occur in contact lens wearers, particularly in individuals exposed to certain environmental or behavioral risk factors. Early and accurate diagnosis is crucial to ensure effective treatment and prevent long-term complications. This report presents a case of bilateral microsporidial keratoconjunctivitis in a pediatric patient who wore orthokeratology lenses, accompanied by a review of relevant literature.Case presentationA 9-year-old female presented with bilateral eye redness and a persistent foreign body sensation lasting for 3 weeks. The patient had recently started raising a pet parrot at home. Upon examination, conjunctival congestion was observed, along with papillary and follicular hypertrophy and diffuse punctate staining of the corneal epithelium. Corneal scraping and gene sequencing confirmed the diagnosis of Encephalitozoon hellem infection.ConclusionThis case underscores the rare occurrence of bilateral microsporidial keratoconjunctivitis in an immunocompetent child wearing orthokeratology lenses. Environmental exposure and lens wear may have contributed to infection. Clinicians should consider microsporidiosis in the differential diagnosis of persistent keratoconjunctivitis that does not respond to conventional therapies.

Introduction

Microsporidia are unicellular eukaryotic parasites that exhibit obligate intracellular growth and lack mitochondria, peroxisomes, and centrioles, exhibiting certain characteristics typically associated with prokaryotes. They were initially classified as prokaryotic organisms but were later reclassified as fungi due to their unique structural features and significant similarities with fungi [1, 2]. To date, approximately 200 genera and over 1300 species of microsporidia have been identified, including Enterocytozoon, Encephalitozoon, and Nosema [3]. Microsporidia are opportunistic pathogens that infect various organs, such as the gastrointestinal tract, eyes, sinuses, lungs, muscles, kidneys, and central nervous system, and are commonly found in immunocompromised individuals [4, 5]. In addition to gastrointestinal infections, ocular involvement is a frequent manifestation, often presenting as superficial epithelial keratoconjunctivitis or deep stromal keratitis [6]. Microsporidia account for approximately 0.4% of infectious keratitis cases [3, 7]. The majority of microsporidial keratitis cases occur in South and Southeast Asia, with higher incidence rates during the monsoon season between July and October, likely due to water contamination and increased insect activity [6].

Orthokeratology lenses, designed for overnight wear to control myopic progression, have gained popularity in the pediatric population. Infections caused by microsporidial in children wearing orthokeratology lenses are rare. However, improper lens use, inadequate lens hygiene, or exposure to contaminated water sources can increase the risk of infection [8, 9]. Ocular microsporidiosis often presents with non-specific clinical features, making early diagnosis particularly crucial. In this study, a case of microsporidial infection was diagnosed using genetic sequencing, and the associated risks for orthokeratology lens wearers were further assessed. Our research aims to provide clinicians with valuable diagnostic insights and treatment recommendations for this rare ocular infection.

Case presentation

The patient was a 9-year-old girl who presented to our ophthalmology clinic with a 3-week history of bilateral eye redness, itching, and discomfort. Three weeks prior, she developed bilateral eye redness and itching without a clear trigger, accompanied by episodic foreign body sensation. She was initially diagnosed with bilateral allergic conjunctivitis at another hospital and treated with anti-allergic eye drops; however, her condition did not improve. Upon presentation to our hospital, the patient reported a history of prolonged orthokeratology lens wear and recent household exposure to pet parrots. She denied using tap water to clean her lenses, and had no history of ocular trauma, recent upper respiratory infection, or other systemic illnesses. Additionally, there was no known history of drug allergies. Notably, her biological sister, a non-wearer of orthokeratology lenses who shared the same household, exhibited no ocular symptoms.

Best-corrected visual acuity at presentation was 20/25 in the right eye and 20/25 in the left eye. The patient reported moderate ocular pain, which seemed slightly disproportionate to the observed clinical signs, and marked photophobia that interfered with reading and school activities. Slit-lamp examination revealed bilateral conjunctival congestion (graded 2+), prominent papillary reaction in the palpebral conjunctiva, and follicular hyperplasia. The corneal epithelium appeared rough, with scattered grayish-white epithelial and subepithelial punctate infiltrates (graded 2–3+). Mild epithelial edema was noted, but there were no corneal ulcers or stromal infiltrates. The anterior chamber was quiet, with no cells or flare observed. The remainder of the anterior segment, including eyelids, lacrimal puncta, and lens, was unremarkable. Fluorescein staining was positive (Fig. 1A–F). In vivo confocal microscopy (IVCM) demonstrated edema of epithelial and basal epithelial cells, with infiltration of Langerhans cells around the subbasal nerve fibers. Punctate hyper-reflective signals were observed in the epithelium, between basal cells, and in the superficial layer of the stroma, indicating the presence of inflammatory cells and microsporidial infection. Activated stromal keratocytes were also noted (Fig. 2A–D). Anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT) revealed an irregular corneal epithelial surface with elevated punctate hyper-reflective signals (red arrow), indicative of epithelial involvement (Fig. 3A). Multiple AS-OCT scans from different directions were acquired, and the most representative images were selected for inclusion. The patient’s sister also underwent a comprehensive ophthalmic examination, which revealed completely normal findings.

On her first visit, under surface anesthesia, a superficial corneal scraping from the lesion was performed using a sterile spatula, and the sample was sent to the laboratory for pathogen metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) testing. Due to sampling limitations, smear and staining for microbiologic evaluation could not be performed. The process included DNA extraction, library construction, sequencing, and bioinformatics analysis. DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Micro Kit (QIAGEN, Germany), and its concentration and quality were assessed with a Qubit 4.0 and Qsep-1. The DNA library was constructed with the QIAseq™ Ultralow Input Library Kit (QIAGEN, Germany), involving fragmentation, end repair, adapter ligation, size selection, and PCR amplification steps. Library concentration and quality were rechecked using Qubit 4.0 and Qsep-1. Qualified libraries were sequenced on the MGISEQ-200 platform (50 bp, single-end sequencing, MGI, China), with data filtering to remove adapters, low-quality, low-complexity, and short sequences (< 35 bp). Human sequences were removed using SNAP software, and the remaining sequences were aligned with microbial genome databases. Negative and positive controls were included for each sample batch, with the same procedures and bioinformatics analysis for mNGS. The specific read count and reads per million (RPM) for each detected pathogen were calculated. When a microorganism was not detected in the negative control (“no template” control, NTC), and the genome coverage of the microorganism was in the top 10 among similar microorganisms or the ratio of RPMsample/RPMNTC was > 10 (if RPMNTC ≠ 0), the result was defined as positive. The mNGS confirmed the presence of Encephalitozoon hellem, supporting the diagnosis of microsporidial keratoconjunctivitis (Fig. 4).

After confirming the diagnosis of microsporidial infection, the patient received comprehensive anti-microsporidial treatment. This included 0.02% chlorhexidine eye drops (compounded by the hospital pharmacy; administered every 2 h during the initial phase, reduced to four times daily on day 14, and discontinued after one month), levofloxacin eye drops (Cravit®, Santen Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Japan) administered four times daily, and ofloxacin eye ointment (Tarivid®, Santen Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Japan) applied once nightly [10]. No topical or systemic corticosteroids were administered during the treatment course. Follow-up assessments were conducted at 7 and 14 days after treatment. By day 7, the patient’s ocular symptoms had significantly improved, with a reduction in corneal staining area (Figs. 5A-D). By day 14, the ocular symptoms had almost resolved, with a significant decrease in corneal staining and no residual scarring or opacification. (Figs. 5E-H). Post-treatment AS-OCT showed that the corneal epithelium had returned to a smooth surface (Figs. 3B, C). At the 2-week follow-up visit, the best-corrected visual acuity had improved to 20/20 in both eyes. The patient continues to receive regular follow-up care, with no further complaints of ocular discomfort or visual decline.

Discussion

Microsporidiosis is an opportunistic infection caused by unicellular fungal pathogens belonging to the phylum Microsporidia [11]. Traditionally, this disease has been considered to primarily affect immunocompromised individuals—such as those with acquired immunodeficiency syndrome, organ transplant recipients, or patients undergoing chemotherapy or radiotherapy for malignancies. However, recent reports have shown an increasing incidence of infections in immunocompetent individuals [12]. The unique intracellular spore structure [13] of microsporidia allows these organisms to survive in harsh environmental conditions and facilitates their transmission [14]. The sporoplasm is injected into the host cytoplasm [15], where it undergoes merogony (binary fission) to form meronts, which then divide and mature into spores through sporogony [16]. The rupture of the host cell releases newly formed spores, which can then infect adjacent cells, leading to a cycle of persistent intracellular infection. Microsporidia can infect a wide range of animal hosts, including humans, and their spores have been detected in various environmental water sources, such as municipal tap water, hospital effluents, and groundwater [17, 18]. Notably, these spores can remain viable in water for extended periods, making untreated water a significant risk factor for both residents and travelers [19]. The use of contact lenses is also identified as a major risk factor for microsporidial keratoconjunctivitis [20].

Although the pathogenesis of microsporidial keratitis remains incompletely understood, waterborne transmission is considered a major route of infection. Direct ocular exposure to contaminated water sources is believed to facilitate the pathogen’s entry into the cornea. A retrospective study of nine cases of microsporidial keratitis revealed that all patients had a history of hot spring exposure prior to symptom onset [21]. In addition to environmental sources, zoonotic transmission has also been implicated. Studies have shown that microsporidia can be transmitted between urban pigeons and humans without significant barriers [22]. Contact with domestic birds, including parrots and poultry, has also been identified as a potential risk factor for human infection [23].

In this case, the patient exhibited classic signs of microsporidial keratoconjunctivitis, including punctate epithelial lesions on the cornea, conjunctival hyperemia, and a foreign body sensation. Diagnosis was confirmed through clinical evaluation and metagenomic next-generation sequencing, which identified Encephalitozoon hellem as the causative pathogen. E. hellem has previously been reported to be associated with a range of clinical manifestations, including keratoconjunctivitis, nephritis, pneumonia, bronchitis, and disseminated infections, particularly in individuals with renal failure [24, 25]. Among ocular infections, most punctate epithelial keratitis cases are caused by members of the Encephalitozoonidae family, with E. hellem being the most commonly identified species [26]. Clinically, these infections typically present as superficial, bilateral, coarse punctate keratopathy with concurrent conjunctivitis in the affected eye. Symptoms may include redness, foreign body sensation, photophobia, excessive tearing, blurred vision, and visual fluctuation [5, 27].

In this case, the patient had a history of long-term orthokeratology lens wear and lived in a household with a pet parrot—both of which represent potential risk factors for E. hellem infection. However, our study did not include testing of the contact lens care solution, lens case, or the pet’s feces, which represents a limitation in identifying potential sources of contamination. While microsporidiosis is more commonly associated with immunosuppressed individuals, this case highlights that infection can occur even in immunocompetent children under certain exposure conditions. The patient responded well to anti-microsporidial therapy, with symptom resolution observed during follow-up. These findings emphasize the need for heightened clinical awareness of microsporidial keratitis, even in otherwise healthy individuals, and underscore the importance of considering zoonotic and environmental exposures. Further research and longitudinal surveillance are necessary to better understand the transmission dynamics, pathogenic mechanisms, and clinical spectrum of microsporidial infections in immunocompetent populations.

Microsporidial keratitis, owing to its low incidence and the non-specific nature of its clinical symptoms, is often misdiagnosed as other corneal diseases [6]. This is particularly true in the early stages, when multiple epithelial lesions may be mistakenly identified as conditions such as Thygeson’s superficial punctate keratitis, Acanthamoeba keratitis, mycobacterial keratitis, or viral keratitis [28]. This warrants special attention in orthokeratology lens wearers, in whom corneal epithelial fitting-related issues may lead to superficial punctate keratitis, further complicating the differential diagnosis. However, the distinctive features of microsporidial corneal lesions—such as rough, raised, and multifocal epithelial infiltrates—can aid in distinguishing it from other corneal pathologies [29].

In terms of non-invasive imaging techniques, AS-OCT and IVCM have been shown to be valuable adjuncts in the diagnosis of microsporidial keratitis. However, these imaging modalities have limitations in their ability to directly identify subtle pathogens like microsporidia. Although IVCM provides high-resolution imaging of the cornea and allows observation of cellular morphological changes, its complex operation and potential image quality issues—such as corneal surface irregularities or edema—can affect diagnostic accuracy. Furthermore, hyperreflective dots detected by IVCM can only indirectly suggest the presence of microsporidia. Corneal scraping for smear and staining allows direct observation of pathogens, but its sensitivity can be limited, and the procedure may be uncomfortable for patients. These limitations can delay definitive diagnosis and the initiation of targeted therapy. To overcome these challenges, metagenomic diagnostics provides a more precise approach for pathogen identification. It enables direct detection of microsporidia in corneal samples and species-level identification, with high sensitivity and minimal specimen requirements, making it particularly suitable for children. By avoiding reliance on traditional culture, this method may facilitate earlier pathogen detection and guide personalised treatment strategies [30]. Therefore, the introduction of metagenomic diagnostics enables us to surpass the limitations of conventional imaging techniques, offering more comprehensive and accurate diagnostic support. In the present case, metagenomic sequencing confirmed Encephalitozoon hellem infection, which subsequently guided targeted therapy.

Orthokeratology lenses have proven to be highly effective in controlling myopia, making them a widely used method for managing myopia progression in children and adolescents [31]. These lenses are typically worn overnight, adhering to the corneal surface for extended periods. Improper care or poor hygiene practices can significantly increase the risk of corneal infections. In this case, the patient had recently kept a pet parrot, suggesting potential exposure to microsporidial contamination through animal contact; however, the patient’s sister, who shared the same environment, did not exhibit any corneal issues. The overnight wear of orthokeratology lenses may also compromise corneal barrier function, with both the lens wear and animal contact contributing to the infection.

Although reported cases of microsporidial keratitis have increased, infections in orthokeratology lens wearers are rare and often misdiagnosed as other corneal diseases [20]. Given the risk of long-term visual impairment—particularly when treatment is delayed or inappropriate—early recognition is essential.

Corneal scraping or molecular testing should be performed whenever feasible to confirm the diagnosis and guide targeted therapy [9]. In the absence of these diagnostic procedures, characteristic clinical features can guide the initial suspicion of microsporidial keratoconjunctivitis, particularly in patients presenting with bilateral coarse punctate epithelial keratitis, relatively mild symptoms compared with the extent of epithelial involvement, and poor response to conventional antibacterial or anti-allergic therapy.

Pending confirmation, empirical treatment with chlorhexidine may be initiated. Chlorhexidine has been reported to be effective and well tolerated in the management of microsporidial keratitis, offering advantages such as low cost, wide availability, and preservation of corneal integrity [10, 32]. Its broad antimicrobial activity, particularly against Acanthamoeba and certain fungal pathogens, is especially valuable in orthokeratology lens wearers, who are at increased risk of mixed or uncertain infections. Alternative agents, including fumagillin, albendazole, and polyhexamethylene biguanide, have been described in the literature, although these may be less accessible or associated with variable clinical responses. Notably, fluoroquinolone antibiotics, such as levofloxacin and ofloxacin, have been reported to exhibit some activity against microsporidia, which may provide additional therapeutic benefit in such cases [33].

Clinicians should prioritize health education for contact lens wearers, which includes following proper lens cleaning protocols, avoiding the use of non-approved care solutions, minimizing animal contact, and scheduling regular eye check-ups. These measures can significantly reduce the incidence of infection. For adolescent patients, close supervision by parents regarding lens wear and care is crucial to mitigate the risk of microbial infections [20]. In patients presenting with corneal punctate staining after orthokeratology lens wear, increased clinical vigilance is warranted to avoid missed or delayed diagnoses. In the current case, the patient was initially misdiagnosed at an outside facility with an allergic reaction secondary to orthokeratology lens use, without consideration of an infectious etiology.

However, several limitations should be considered in this study. First, the case report involves a single patient, and the findings may not be generalizable to a broader population of orthokeratology lens wearers. Second, the diagnosis was made based on molecular techniques and corneal scraping, which may not be universally accessible in all clinical settings. Additionally, microscopic confirmation using special staining was not performed, and microbiological testing of potential environmental sources, such as contact lenses and storage materials, was not conducted due to the patient’s limited cooperation. The role of environmental or behavioral factors, such as the presence of the pet parrot, in the development of the infection remains unclear and warrants further investigation. Finally, the long-term outcomes and recurrence rates of microsporidial keratoconjunctivitis in orthokeratology lens wearers have not been assessed, and more research is needed to better understand the full clinical implications of this rare infection.

In summary, this case underscores the importance of considering microsporidial infection in the differential diagnosis of corneal infections associated with orthokeratology lenses. With the development of molecular diagnostic technologies, earlier and more accurate diagnoses are expected, further enhancing treatment strategies and prognosis management for microsporidial keratitis.
