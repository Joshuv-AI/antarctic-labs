Distinct thymic pDC populations promote tumor immune tolerance through complementary mechanisms
Authors: Yangsong Wang, Ryo Koyama-Nasu, Yukihiro Endo, Ichita Hasegawa, Atsushi Onodera, Mingyu Chen, Kiyoshi Hirahara, Shinichiro Motohashi, Toshinori Nakayama, Motoko Y. Kimura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Thymic central tolerance is crucial for preventing autoimmunity, but its contribution to tumor immune evasion remains poorly understood. Here, we demonstrate that plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) in the thymus have two distinct subsets, accumulating in the thymus of tumor-bearing mice, contributing to immune tolerance through clonal deletion of tumor-specific T cells and reducing newly generated T cells. Mechanistically, common dendritic cell progenitor-derived pDCs (CDP-pDCs) capture tumor antigens and migrate to the thymus in a CCR9-dependent manner, where they present these antigens to induce clonal deletion of tumor-specific T cells. Concurrently, tumor progression inhibits T cell generation by promoting the accumulation of common lymphoid progenitor–derived pDCs (CLP-pDCs) within the thymus, which further produce type I interferon to alter thymic function. CCR9 deficiency prevents thymic accumulation of both pDCs, enhancing antitumor immunity and reducing tumor growth. Our findings reveal a previously unrecognized mechanism by which tumors hijack the physiological system to establish central tolerance against peripheral antigens, thereby promoting tolerance against themselves.

INTRODUCTION

The immune system plays a critical role in cancer surveillance by recognizing and eliminating malignant cells. This process relies primarily on T cells that recognize tumor antigens presented by dendritic cells (DCs) in tumor-draining lymph nodes (TDLNs), leading to the generation of tumor-specific effector T cells (1). However, despite the existence of tumor-specific T cells, cancers often evade immune surveillance through multiple mechanisms. These include the establishment of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, induction of T cell exhaustion, and accumulation of regulatory T cells (Treg cells) (2–7). Yet, how cancers establish systemic immune tolerance, particularly through manipulating central tolerance in the thymus, remains poorly understood.

The thymus is the primary organ for T cell development and the establishment of central tolerance. Within the thymus, both medullary thymic epithelial cells and thymic antigen-presenting cells present self-antigens to developing thymocytes, eliminating potentially self-reactive T cells through negative selection and also generating Treg cells (8–13). It has been reported that some peripheral DCs, particularly Sirpα+ DCs and plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs), can capture peripheral antigens and migrate to the thymus in a CCR2- and CCR9-dependent manner, respectively, under steady-state conditions (14, 15). This process contributes to the establishment of tolerance against peripheral tissue-specific antigens. However, whether and how cancers might exploit thymic tolerance mechanisms to establish systemic immune tolerance against cancer remains unexplored. Furthermore, recent studies have revealed that thymic pDCs originate from two distinct progenitors: common dendritic cell progenitors (CDPs) and common lymphoid progenitors (CLPs) (16–18). However, the functional significance of these different developmental origins and their potential roles in tumor-induced immune tolerance remains unknown.

In this study, we demonstrate that tumor progression drives the accumulation of two distinct pDC populations in the thymus through CCR9-dependent mechanisms. We identify that CDP-derived pDCs (CDP-pDCs) capture tumor antigens in the periphery and migrate to the thymus, where they present these antigens to establish central tolerance against tumors. Furthermore, we show that tumor progression suppresses T cell lineage commitment while promoting the generation of CLP-derived pDCs (CLP-pDCs), which alter thymic function through type I interferon (IFN) production. Our findings reveal a previously unrecognized mechanism of tumor immune evasion through manipulating thymic function and identify CCR9 as a potential therapeutic target in cancer immunotherapy.

RESULTS

Tumor progression drives CCR9-dependent accumulation of pDCs in the thymus

To investigate how tumor progression affects thymic DC composition, we analyzed DC subsets in the thymus of mice bearing MC38 colon carcinoma (Fig. 1A). Following CD11c+ cell enrichment, we identified three major thymic DC populations by flow cytometry: pDCs (CD11c+B220+), CD8α+ DCs (CD11chiB220−CD8α+Sirpα−), and Sirpα+ DCs (CD11chiB220−Sirpα+) (13, 19–22) (fig. S1A). We confirmed that nearly 98% of CD11c+B220+ cells were pDCs because these cells expressed SiglecH and BST2 but not CD19. Although CD11c+B220+ cells have previously been reported to include potent antigen-presenting B cells (23), we detected only minimal contamination of CD11c+ CD19+ B cells (fig. S1A, bottom). Flow cytometry analysis revealed a notable accumulation of pDCs in the thymus of tumor-bearing mice, accompanied by a reduction in Sirpα+ DCs, whereas CD8α+ DC numbers remained largely unchanged (Fig. 1, B and C). This selective increase in thymic pDCs was observed across multiple tumor models, including B16-OVA melanoma and CT26 colon carcinoma (fig. S1, B and C), suggesting a general phenomenon in cancer progression. Time course analysis of thymic DC composition demonstrated that pDC accumulation became evident by day 14 post–tumor inoculation and persisted through day 21 (Fig. 1, D and F, top). Given the significant reduction in thymus weight during tumor progression (Fig. 1E), we also normalized DC numbers to thymus weight to accurately assess the changes in DC composition (Fig. 1F, bottom).

To understand the molecular mechanism underlying this selective accumulation of thymic pDCs, we focused on CCR9, a chemokine receptor known to be critical for pDC trafficking to the thymus under homeostatic conditions (14, 21, 24). Using CCR9-deficient (Ccr9KO) mice, we found that genetic ablation of CCR9 significantly prevented the increase in thymic pDCs in tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 1, G and H), indicating that tumor progression promotes CCR9-dependent trafficking or retention of pDCs to the thymus.

Distinct thymic pDC populations with different developmental origins accumulate during tumor progression

To further characterize the thymic DC populations that accumulate during tumor progression, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) of CD11c+ cells isolated from the thymus of Ccr9WT and Ccr9KO mice bearing MC38 tumors (fig. S2A). CD11c+ cells were selected from scRNA data, and then unbiased clustering analysis was performed to reveal nine distinct populations based on the expressions of specific curated genes (21, 25–29), including two pDC clusters with different developmental origins: Zbtb46− pDCs derived from CLP-pDCs (cluster 1) and Zbtb46+ pDCs derived from CDP-pDCs (cluster 2) (27–29) (Fig. 2, A to C). The remaining clusters comprised CD8α+DCs including cDC1s (cluster 5 for immature and cluster 8 for mature) and transitional DCs (cluster 7) (30), Sirpα+DCs including cDC2s (cluster 4 for immature, cluster 9 for mature, and cluster 6 for CD14+ cDC2), and monocyte-derived DCs (cluster 3) (Fig. 2, B and C, and fig. S2B).

Analysis of cellular dynamics revealed a marked increase in pDC populations in tumor-bearing mice, with CLP-pDCs (cluster 1) showing the most prominent increase during tumor progression (Fig. 2D). Notably, both CLP-pDCs (cluster 1) and CDP-pDCs (cluster 2) were significantly reduced in the thymus of tumor-bearing Ccr9KO mice (Fig. 2D), suggesting CCR9-dependent accumulation of both populations in the thymus. Analysis of tumor tissues demonstrated that CD11c+B220+ cells consisted of two pDC subsets with few B cells: one is Zbtb46+ CDP-pDCs and the other is Zbtb46− CLP-pDCs. CCR9 was expressed by both Zbtb46+ CDP-pDCs and Zbtb46− CLP-pDCs, whereas Sirpα+ DCs showed minimal CCR9 expression (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, neither other thymic DC subsets, except for transitional DCs (cluster 7) nor intratumoral CD4+ T and CD8+ T cells barely expressed CCR9 (fig. S2, C and D).

Consistent with the CCR9-dependent accumulation of pDCs in the thymus, we found significantly elevated expression of Ccl25, a ligand for CCR9, and a slight increase in Ccl21a, a ligand for CCR7, in the thymus of tumor-bearing mice, whereas the expression of Ccl19, a ligand for CCR7, showed minimal changes (Fig. 2F). These data suggest that tumor progression drives the accumulation of two distinct pDC populations in the thymus in a CCR9-dependent manner.

CDP-pDCs with antigen-presenting capacity migrate from tumor tissue to the thymus

To determine whether thymic pDCs migrate from tumor tissue, we injected yellow-green fluorescent microspheres (YG) into MC38 tumor tissues to track DC migration (14, 31) (Fig. 3A). Analysis of thymic DCs 2 days after YG injection to the tumor revealed the presence of YG+ CD11c+B220+ cells in the thymus, which also expressed specific markers for pDCs (i.e., BST2 and SiglecH) but not the B cell marker (i.e., CD19) (Fig. 3B and fig. S3A). These data demonstrate the direct migration of pDCs from tumor tissue to the thymus. Notably, ∼77% of YG+ DCs in the thymus were pDCs, whereas only 20% were Sirpα+ DCs (Fig. 3C, top right). In contrast, YG− thymic DCs consisted of 44% pDCs, 41% CD8α+ DCs, and 15% Sirpα+ DCs (Fig. 3C, bottom right). To validate the specificity of this migration, we examined DC populations in LNs. All DC subsets carrying YG were detected in TDLNs but not in nondraining lymph nodes (NDLNs) (fig. S3, B and C), confirming that YG+ DCs in the thymus resulted from directed migration rather than passive circulation. This migration pattern was observed across different tumor models, including B16-OVA melanoma (fig. S3, D and E). The accumulation of YG+ pDCs in the thymus was abolished in Ccr9KO mice (Fig. 3, D and E), demonstrating CCR9-dependent migration of these cells. YG+ Sirpα+ DCs were also reduced in the thymus of Ccr9KO mice, despite their lack of CCR9 expression (Fig. 2E), suggesting an indirect effect of CCR9 deficiency on Sirpα+ DC migration to the thymus.

To characterize the detail of thymic pDCs migrated from the tumor tissue, we performed bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of YG+ pDCs isolated from the thymus and compared their transcriptional profiles with those of steady-state thymic pDCs and Sirpα+ DCs (fig. S4A). Heatmap and principal components analysis demonstrated clear separation of DC subset–specific signatures across all samples (fig. S4, B and C). We then examined the top 10 up-regulated genes in YG+ pDCs using bulk RNA-seq data (Fig. 4, A and B) and applied them to the scRNA-seq dataset (Fig. 4C). The integration of bulk RNA-seq data with our single-cell transcriptomic analysis revealed that YG+ pDCs exhibited molecular signatures characteristic of CDP-pDCs (Fig. 4, A to C). This finding was further supported by the selective expression of FSCN1, one of the top differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in YG+ pDCs, in the CDP-pDC cluster of our scRNA-seq dataset (Fig. 4D).

Further analysis using the pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the pathways involved in migration, chemotaxis, and cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction were identified in YG+ pDCs as the top 5 pathways (Fig. 4E). Correspondingly, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) showed enrichment in the signature of leukocyte migration, cellular response to lipopolysaccharide (LPS), and antigen presentation and processing within YG+ pDCs (Fig. 4F). These data strongly suggest that CDP-pDC migrated from the tumor tissue can contribute to antigen presentation to developing thymocytes. Our scRNA-seq data showed that CDP-pDCs (cluster 2) expressed high levels of H2-ab1 and Cd81, a marker associated with antigen-presenting capacity for pDCs (32) (fig. S4D). Flow cytometry analysis further confirmed that Zbtb46+CD81+pDCs expressed the highest levels of major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC-II) among pDC subpopulations (fig. S4E). Furthermore, YG+ pDCs were predominantly CD81+ (Fig. 4G), indicating that these pDCs are CDP-pDCs with high antigen-presenting capacity. Notably, we found that gene expression profiles of CDP-pDCs from tumor-bearing mice and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)–treated mice show high similarity (Fig. 4H). These data demonstrate that tumor progression does not alter the characteristic features of CDP-pDCs, suggesting that tumors exploit the physiological processes that pDCs normally migrate into the thymus at the steady state (14). These results demonstrate that CDP-pDCs migrate from tumor tissue to the thymus and have molecular machinery for antigen presentation.

Tumor antigen-presenting CDP-pDCs induce central tolerance

To determine whether CDP-pDCs present tumor antigens in the thymus, we used MC38-OVA-eGFP tumor-bearing mice and detected the presentation of ovalbumin (OVA)–derived SIINFEKL peptide on H-2Kb using the 25-D1.16 antibody (Ab) (33). Notably, only pDCs, but not CD8α+ DCs or Sirpα+ DCs, presented OVA peptides on MHC-I in the thymus (Fig. 5A). Time course analysis revealed that this presentation became detectable at day 14 and persisted through day 21 after tumor inoculation (Fig. 5B and fig. S5A). Further characterization demonstrated that these antigen-presenting pDCs exhibited a Zbtb46+CD81+ phenotype in the thymus (Fig. 5C), and a Zbtb46+CD81+ phenotype was further confirmed with CD11c+ B220+ SiglecH+ CD19− expression in TDLNs (fig. S5B).

To examine MHC-II–restricted antigen presentation, we used an experimental system using BALB/c-derived A20 tumor cells expressing the I-E molecule, inoculated into B6 background mice, which allows the detection of A20-derived Eα52–68 peptide presentation on I-Ab using the Y-Ae Ab (34–36) (fig. S5C). To avoid rejection, we inoculated A20 tumor cells into TCRβTCRδ-double knockout (DKO) B6 host mice. Notably, only pDCs displayed Eα:I-Ab complexes in this system, whereas CD8α+ DCs and Sirpα+ DCs showed no detectable presentation in the thymus (Fig. 5, D and E). Similar to MHC-I presentation, Eα:I-Ab complexes were detected on pDCs at day 14 but not at day 7 (fig. S5, D and E). Whereas all DC subsets in the spleen could present Eα:I-Ab complexes (fig. S5, F and G), tumor antigen presentation in the thymus was restricted to pDCs.

To assess the functional consequence of tumor antigen presentation, we generated mixed bone marrow (BM) chimeras using BM cells from OVA-specific TCR transgenic (OT-II-Rag2KO; CD45.2+) mice and wild-type (WT) B6 (CD45.1+) mice. To exclude potential confounding effects from peripheral OT-II T cells, we transplanted either MC38WT or MC38-OVA-eGFP tumors into mixed BM chimeric mice at day 13 after reconstitution (Fig. 5F), a time point at which OT-II BM–derived T cells have not yet established in the peripheral immune compartment. We found that the frequency of CD45.2+ OT-II CD4 single-positive (SP) cells was significantly reduced in MC38-OVA-eGFP–bearing mice compared to MC38-bearing controls, whereas that of CD45.1+ polyclonal WT cells was comparable (Fig. 5G). Although we did not detect OT-II TCRβ+ Treg cells (Foxp3+) in this experimental setting (fig. S5, H and I), this might reflect limitations of the OT-II-Rag2KO system rather than the absence of Treg cell induction. To examine whether OT-II T cells also decreased in the tumor of MC38-OVA-eGFP–bearing mice, we calculated Ratio R (CD45.2), which quantifies the relative frequency of CD45.2+ OT-II T cells in the tumor versus thymus by dividing the tumor proportion by the thymic proportion. Using this approach, we observed a pronounced reduction in OT-II T cells within the tumor relative to their thymic frequency, indicating efficient reduction of tumor-specific OT-II T cells at the tumor (Fig. 5H). To validate these findings with endogenous tumor-specific T cells, we tracked enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP)–specific CD4+ T cells using eGFP I-Ab tetramers in mice bearing MC38-OVA-eGFP tumors. The frequency and cell number of eGFP-specific CD4+ T cells detected by GFP I-Ab tetramer staining were significantly reduced in the thymus of tumor-bearing Ccr9WT mice but maintained in Ccr9KO mice (Fig. 5I and fig. S5J). Moreover, the endogenous MC38-derived antigen p15E was used to assess endogenous tumor antigen–specific thymocyte development. We found that the frequency of p15E-specific CD8+ SP thymocytes was markedly reduced in Ccr9WT mice following tumor inoculation. In contrast, p15E-specific CD8+ SP thymocyte generation was maintained even under tumor-bearing conditions in Ccr9KO mice (Fig. 5J). These results demonstrate that thymic CDP-pDCs establish central tolerance to tumor antigens through both MHC-I–dependent and MHC-II–dependent presentation.

Tumor progression inhibits T cell lineage commitment while promoting CLP-pDC generation

Although our data, including scRNA-seq, demonstrated thymic accumulation of both CDP-pDCs and CLP-pDCs during tumor progression and that CDP-pDC migrated to the thymus from tumor tissue with the capability of tumor antigen presentation, the origin and function of CLP-pDCs remained unclear. Given that CLP-pDCs share a developmental origin with T cell progenitors (37–39), we next examined whether tumor progression affects early T cell development. We found a marked reduction in thymocytes with slightly decreased frequency of DP thymocytes observed (fig. S6, A and B). Furthermore, a marked accumulation of CD117+CD25+ CD4−CD8− double-negative 2 (DN2) cells, as well as a concurrent reduction in T cell lineage–committed CD117−CD25+ DN3 and CD117−CD25− DN4 cells, in tumor-bearing mice was detected (Fig. 6, A and B, and fig. S6C). Notably, these alterations were largely prevented in Ccr9KO mice (Fig. 6, A and B, and fig. S6B), although DN1 and DN2 cell numbers were generally lower in Ccr9KO mice regardless of tumor burden, consistent with CCR9’s role in thymic progenitor trafficking (40–42).

To understand the molecular basis of the reduction of T cell lineage–committed cells, we examined the Notch-DLL4 axis, which is essential for T cell lineage specification (38). Expression of Notch1 was significantly reduced on DN1, DN2, and DN3 thymocytes in tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 6C and fig. S6D), accompanied by decreased thymic expression of Dll4, a ligand for Notch1 (Fig. 6D). These data indicate that tumor progression impairs T cell lineage commitment by dampening Notch-DLL4 signaling.

Because early thymic progenitors retain developmental plasticity (37, 38, 43), we hypothesized that reduced T cell specification might be associated with CLP-pDC differentiation within the thymus. When DN1 cells were cultured with TSt-4/N-DLL1 stromal cells in the presence of tumor culture supernatant (tumor-conditioned medium) (fig. S6E), we observed enhanced generation of BST2+B220+ pDCs (Fig. 6, E and F). This effect was specific to DN1 cells as DN2 and DN3 cells did not give rise to BST2+B220+ pDCs under the same conditions. Moreover, our scRNA-seq analysis revealed enhanced proliferation signatures in thymic CLP-pDCs from tumor-bearing mice (fig. S6, F and G), suggesting active expansion of this population. These data suggest that tumor progression inhibits T cell lineage commitment and rather promotes CLP-pDC differentiation and expansion within the thymus.

Given that pDCs are known as type I IFN producers (44, 45), we next examined whether CLP-pDCs contribute to alter thymic function through IFN-α production. Although IFN-α production from pDCs was comparable between control and tumor-bearing mice, IFN-α production by total thymic CD11c+ cells was significantly elevated in tumor-bearing mice most likely due to increased pDC numbers in the thymus (Fig. 6G). Consistent with the known effects of type I IFNs on thymic function (46–48), we observed enhanced maturation of SP thymocytes, as evidenced by increased Qa2 expression on TCRβ+ CD4 and CD8SP cells (48) (Fig. 6, H and I, and fig. S6A). These alterations were prevented in Ccr9KO mice (Fig. 6, H and I), where thymic pDC accumulation was impaired. To further examine whether this alteration depend on IFN-α production, mixed BM chimeric mice were generated using BM cells from Ifnar1KO and WT mice and inoculated with MC38WT tumor (fig. S6H). WT CD4SP and CD8SP thymocytes from tumor-bearing mice exhibited high Qa2 expression, whereas Ifnar1KO cells exhibited very limited Qa2 in both tumor-bearing and PBS-treated mice (fig. S6I).

These results demonstrate that tumor progression not only inhibits T cell lineage commitment but also promotes the generation and expansion of CLP-pDCs, which contribute to altering thymic function by producing type I IFN, at least in part.

Antitumor immune responses are enhanced in Ccr9KO mice

Last, we examined whether prevention of thymic pDC accumulation through CCR9 deficiency affects antitumor immunity. Notably, both MC38-WT and MC38-OVA-eGFP tumor growth was significantly suppressed in Ccr9KO mice compared to Ccr9WT mice (Fig. 7A and fig. S7A). We detected a substantial increase in OVA (SIINFEKL)–specific CD8+ T cells in the MC38-OVA-eGFP tumor tissue (Fig. 7, B and C) and TDLN (fig. S7, B and C) of Ccr9KO mice compared to Ccr9WT mice. Furthermore, the frequency of Ly49+ OVA-specific CD8+ T cells, which represent self-tolerant T cells evicted from the thymus due to their autoreactivity (49), was reduced in the tumor of Ccr9KO mice (Fig. 7, D and E). The tumor-infiltrating OVA-specific CD8+ T cells in Ccr9KO mice showed elevated Granzyme B expression (Fig. 7, F and G), suggesting enhanced cytotoxic function. Furthermore, we analyzed the endogenous tumor antigen p15E and found that p15E-specific CD8+ T cells were significantly increased in the tumor tissue of Ccr9KO mice, accompanied by a reduced frequency of Ly49+ p15E-specific CD8+ T cells in tumors (Fig. 7, H and I). These data indicate that Ly49+ tumor-specific CD8+ T cells are tolerant T cells against tumor antigens, which are evicted from the thymus of Ccr9WT mice but less evicted from Ccr9KO mice, likely due to less tumor antigen presentation in the thymus of Ccr9KO mice. Analysis of tumor-specific CD4+ T cells in MC38-OVA-eGFP tumors revealed a similar pattern, with increased numbers of eGFP-specific CD4+ T cells in the tumor of Ccr9KO mice (Fig. 7, J and K).

To directly test whether enhanced antitumor immunity in Ccr9KO mice resulted from impaired thymic pDC accumulation, we performed adoptive transfer experiments (fig. S7D). Transfer of Ccr9WT pDCs, but not Ccr9KO pDCs, into Ccr9KO mice restored CD11c+B220+ cell numbers and enhanced tumor growth (Fig. 7L and fig. S7, E and F). Furthermore, most thymic CD11c+B220+ cells were pDCs expressing SiglecH and BST2, with minimal B cell contamination (fig. S7G). Notably, we found that the number of tumor-specific p15E tetramer+ CD8+ T cells in the tumor was reduced in the mice adoptively transferred with Ccr9WT pDCs (Fig. 7, M and N). These data demonstrate that adoptive transfer of Ccr9WT pDCs accumulated in the thymus and lead to dampening antitumor immunity.

Supporting the clinical relevance of our findings, analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data revealed that patients with colorectal cancer with higher CCR9 expression showed significantly shorter overall survival compared to those with lower CCR9 expression (Fig. 7O). Together, these findings demonstrate that tumor progression exploits thymic tolerance mechanisms through CCR9-dependent accumulation of two distinct pDC populations, suggesting CCR9 as a potential therapeutic target for cancer immunotherapy.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we demonstrate that tumors orchestrate a sophisticated mechanism of immune evasion by manipulating thymic pDCs. Mechanistically, tumor progression coordinates the accumulation of two distinct pDC populations within the thymus. CDP-pDCs capture tumor antigens in the periphery and migrate to the thymus in a CCR9-dependent manner, where they establish central tolerance by presenting antigens via both MHC-I and MHC-II molecules. Concurrently, tumor progression actively modulates thymic progenitor cell development, suppressing T cell lineage commitment while promoting CLP-pDC generation. This previously unrecognized mechanism represents a fundamental advancement in our understanding of how tumors hijack the general mechanism to establish central tolerance to achieve systemic immunosuppression.

Our findings extend the current understanding of DC migration to the thymus and its exploitation by tumors. It has been known that, under steady-state conditions, peripheral pDCs migrate to the thymus to establish tolerance against peripheral self-antigens through CCR9-dependent mechanisms (50–52). This mechanism that establishes tolerance becomes dysfunctional by inhibiting DC migration to the thymus upon pathogen-associated stimulation, thereby preventing the elimination of pathogen-specific T cells (14). In contrast, our data showed that CDP-pDC migrated into the thymus even under tumor progression and that the characteristic features of migrated CDP-pDCs from tumor-bearing mice and PBS-treated mice did not show obvious differences. These data indicate that tumors use existing CCR9-dependent physiological processes to establish central tolerance against the tumor itself (14, 15, 51). In addition, we detected that the increased amounts of Ccl25, a ligand of CCR9, reduced expressions of Notch1 and Dll4 in the thymus of tumor-bearing mice. Furthermore, thymocyte cell numbers decreased under tumor progression. Collectively, we think that tumor-derived metabolites in the blood have substantial impacts on thymic functions through multiple pathways, although the detailed mechanisms require further investigation.

We observed that not only pDCs but also Sirpα+ DCs carried YG fluorescence in the thymus of tumor-bearing mice and that CCR9 deficiency prevented thymic accumulation of Sirpα+ DCs, despite their typical reliance on CCR2 (14, 15, 51). Although accumulation of YG+ Sirpα+ DCs in the thymus could occur through physical transfer of YG microspheres between cells, our data do not support this mechanism. If transfer were random, CD8α+ DCs should have shown YG, but this was not observed. Instead, YG acquisition occurred in a CCR9-dependent manner specifically in Sirpα+ DCs. Given that CDP-pDCs, which are also referred to as pDC-like cells, were reported to be able to differentiate into Sirpα+ DCs (53), this might reflect impaired CDP-pDC trafficking rather than direct regulation of Sirpα+ DC migration.

Another key advancement of our study is the discovery of two distinct pDC subsets (CDP-pDCs and CLP-pDCs) in the thymus and the elucidation of their functional roles. Although these populations have been previously identified in the periphery (17), their existence in the thymus and specific contributions to thymic function remained unclear. We demonstrate that only CDP-pDCs are specialized for antigen presentation and tolerance induction, reflecting their enhanced expression of antigen-presenting machinery. Although we primarily observed clonal deletion of tumor-specific T cells rather than Treg cell induction, this may reflect limitations of our experimental approach rather than a biological constraint, particularly given previous evidence of pDC-mediated Treg cell induction in the human thymus (54). In contrast, our data indicate that CLP-pDCs do not have antigen-presenting capacity. Instead, CLP-pDCs produce type I IFN, regulating thymic function through multiple mechanisms, including altered T cell maturation to promote Qa2 expression on mature T cells, which may affect the functional quality of newly exported T cells.

Our findings also reveal a broader mechanism of tumor-mediated immune regulation through modulation of thymic progenitor development. Tumors actively suppress T cell lineage commitment while promoting CLP-pDC generation within the thymus, representing a unique mechanism of immune evasion. This process appears to be mediated through the suppression of Notch-DLL4 signaling by tumor-derived factors. However, the specific molecular mediators remain to be identified.

The marked enhancement of antitumor immunity in Ccr9KO mice provides compelling evidence for the physiological significance of this pathway. Although this enhancement primarily reflects impaired presentation of tumor antigens in the thymus, the broader implications of CCR9 deficiency warrant careful consideration. The observed decrease in thymic pDCs in Ccr9KO mice at the steady state suggests potential disruption of peripheral self-tolerance, which could complicate therapeutic targeting of this pathway. Our adoptive transfer experiments with Ccr9WT pDCs provide strong support for the contribution of CCR9-dependent thymic pDC accumulation to tumor immune evasion through central tolerance induction. However, we cannot completely exclude the possibility that Ccr9KO pDC may have additional direct functions within the tumor microenvironment or TDLNs. This represents a limitation of this study, and further investigations are needed to fully elucidate this mechanism.

In conclusion, we reveal a previously unrecognized mechanism by which tumors hijack the physiological system for establishing tolerance to peripheral antigens, using two distinct thymic pDC subsets to achieve immune evasion. Although these two pDC subsets have been reported in peripheral tissues, this study identifies their presence and distinct functional roles in the thymus. In addition, our studies identify CCR9 as a potential therapeutic target, although future studies are required to fully evaluate the therapeutic potential of targeting CCR9-dependent pathways while carefully considering potential autoimmune complications. Our work thus provides unique insights into tumor immune evasion and suggests alternative therapeutic strategies for cancer immunotherapy.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Mice

C57BL/6 and BALB/c mice were purchased from CLEA Co. (Tokyo, Japan) and Charles River Laboratories (Japan). Ccr9KO mice were generously provided by J. Farber (NIAID, NIH, USA). Ifnar1KO mice were generously provided by M. Kensuke (Tokyo University, Japan). TCRβTCRδ-DKO mice, CD45.1 B6, OT-II TCR transgenic mice, and Rag2KO mice were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory. All mice were maintained under specific-pathogen–free conditions and used at 6 to 12 weeks of age. All animal experiments were approved by the Chiba University Review Board for Animal Care (approval numbers A8-030 and A8-033).

Tumor cell line and culture

The murine colon adenocarcinoma cell line (MC38) was purchased from Kerafast Inc. The MC38-OVA-eGFP cell line was generated in our laboratory. In brief, OVA cDNA and eGFP cDNA were retrovirally introduced into MC38 cells using the pMSCV-OVA-IRES-EGFP vector (VectorBuilder). B16-OVA murine melanoma cell line was kindly provided by T. Nakatsura (National Cancer Center, Tokyo, Japan). The colon tumor 26 (CT26) cell line was purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). MC38, MC38-OVA-eGFP, and B16-OVA cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (HyClone). CT26 and A20 were maintained in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS (HyClone).

Tumor model

Experiments were conducted with age-matched and sex-matched mice using both male and female mice. Before tumor implantation, the hair of the mice was carefully removed through shaving. The inoculation procedures involved the implantation of 1 × 106 cells per mouse for the following cell lines: MC38, MC38-OVA-eGFP, B16-OVA, CT26, and A20. Tumor growth was monitored over specified durations of 7, 14, and 21 days, depending on the experimental design.

Isolation of CD11c+ cells from the thymus, LNs, and tumor

The thymus and LNs were mechanically separated with scissors and digested with Liberase TL (125 mg/ml; Roche) and DNase I (50 mg/ml; Sigma-Aldrich) in 3 ml of DMEM for 1 hour at 37°C and then added EDTA [300 μl, 0.1 M (pH 7.2)] to incubate for 5 min at 37°C. For tumor tissues, they were minced into small pieces and dissociated using a mouse Tumor Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec) and gentleMACS Octo Dissociator with Heaters (Miltenyi Biotec), as described in the manufacturer’s protocol. Subsequently, CD11c+ cells were sorted by using CD11c MicroBeads (Miltenyi) according to the instruction manual and used for further analysis.

Isolation of T cells

Tumor tissues were harvested 14 or 21 days postinoculation and processed using the Tumor Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec) to obtain single-cell suspensions. Dissociated cells were separated by Percoll (Cytiva) gradient centrifugation to enrich tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes. For the isolation of T cells from the thymus, spleen, and LNs, the organs were manually disrupted using fine-tipped forceps and surgical scissors to create single-cell suspensions for further analysis.

Flow cytometry analysis

DCs T cells, and thymocytes were incubated with 2.4G2 (anti-mouse Fcγ III/II receptor) and stained with fluorochrome-conjugated monoclonal Abs against CD11c (N418), Sirpα (P84), CD8α (53-6.7), B220/CD45RA (RA3-6B2), BST2 (927), SiglecH (551), CD19 (6D5), CD81 (Eat-2), MHC-II (I-A/I-E) (M5/114.15.2), CCR9 (CW-1.2), CD4 (RM4-5), TCRβ (H57-597), Vb5.1, 5.2 (MR9-4), CD25 (PC61), CD117 (S18020A), NOTCH1 (HMN1-12), Qa2 (695H1-9-9), Ly-49C/F/I/H (14B11), CD45.1 (A20), CD45.2 (104), CD49b (DX5), CD3ε (145-2C11), and H-2Kb bound to SIINFEKL (25-D1.16). Streptavidin (SA) was obtained from BioLegend. The Ea52–68 peptide bound to I-Ab monoclonal Ab (Y-Ae) and CD45 (30-F11) were obtained from BD Biosciences. Last, 7-AAD Viability Staining Solution (BioLegend) was used to exclude dead cells. For intracellular staining, first, cells were stained with surface molecules and Fixable Viability Dye eFluor 506 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to exclude dead cells, then fixed and permeabilized with an Intracellular Fixation & Permeabilization Buffer Set (eBioscience), and stained with the Ab against Foxp3 (FJK-16s) from Invitrogen, Granzyme B (GB11) from BioLegend, and Zbtb46 (U4-1374) from BD Biosciences. To assess nonspecific binding, isotype control Abs were used in parallel with target-specific Abs. To detect tumor antigen–specific T cells, first, T cells were stained with the SIINFEKL H-2Kb tetramer (OVA), KSPWFTTL H-2Kb tetramer (p15E), or HDFFKSAMPEGYVQE I-Ab tetramer (eGFP) (NIH Tetramer Core Facility, USA) for 60 min at room temperature. Data were acquired on a Canto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) and analyzed with the FlowJo software (BD Biosciences).

Tetramer enrichments and analysis

Thymus from mice were processed to obtain a single-cell suspension. The cells were subsequently stained using the I-Ab tetramer (eGFP) (NIH Tetramer Core Facility, USA) conjugated with phycoerythrin (PE) and allophycocyanin (APC) (final concentration: 4 μg/ml) for 60 min at room temperature. Following staining, the cells were washed and incubated with 50 μl of anti-PE and anti-APC magnetic microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec) for 20 min at 4°C. After additional washing, cell enrichment was performed using the autoMACS Pro Separator (Miltenyi Biotec). The enriched cell populations were then stained and analyzed by flow cytometry. For data analysis, the datasets from three individual mice were merged using the “Concatenate” function of the FlowJo software (BD Biosciences) to analyze tetramer+ thymocytes.

The enrichment procedure for the p15E tetramer was performed using the same protocol as described above for the eGFP tetramer. In brief, a single-cell suspension of thymocytes was stained using the H-2Kb tetramer (p15E) (NIH Tetramer Core Facility, USA) conjugated with PE and APC for 60 min at room temperature. Following staining, the cells were washed and incubated with 50 μl of anti-PE and anti-APC magnetic microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec) for 20 min at 4°C. Last, cell enrichment was performed using the autoMACS Pro Separator (Miltenyi Biotec).

ScRNA-seq and data analysis

CD11c+ cells sorted from the thymus of MC38 tumor-bearing or PBS-treated Ccr9WT and Ccr9KO mice using the BD FACS Aria III were subjected to droplet encapsulation for scRNA-seq. Libraries were prepared following the manufacturer’s instructions using the Chromium Next GEM Single-Cell 5′ Reagent Kit v2 (10× Genomics). Sequencing of the libraries was performed on a DNBSEQ (MGI). Sequence reads from all samples were processed and aggregated using Cell Ranger v.7.1.0 (10× Genomics) based on refdata-gex-mm10-2020-A (10× Genomics). The counts matrix was loaded to Scanpy installed on Python 3.6 and BBrowserX developed by BioTuring for downstream analysis.

Quantitative RT-PCR

To extract total RNA, the thymus was first rinsed three times with PBS and placed in an appropriate amount of TRIzol solution (Invitrogen) on ice, and ultrasonic disruption was used to break down the tissue to facilitate RNA extraction. cDNA was synthesized with the SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using 10 ng of total RNA. Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) was performed with a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The expression of the target genes was normalized to the expression of actin, and relative gene expression was determined using the ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences used in this study listed in table S1.

In vivo fluorescent microbead administration

YG fluorescent carboxylate-modified microspheres (0.2 μm in diameter; Invitrogen) were intratumorally injected into the tumor tissue of tumor-bearing mice under inhalation anesthesia. The injection volume was adjusted on the basis of tumor size, ranging from 20 to 100 μl per injection. Injection was performed every 12 hours for 48 hours, and the thymus was analyzed 48 hours after the last injection.

Bulk RNA-seq and data analysis

Total cellular RNA of thymic pDC and Sirpα+ DC sorted using the BD FACS Aria III was extracted with TRIzol (Invitrogen). For cDNA library construction, Ovation SoLo RNA-Seq Mouse (TECAN) was used according to the manufacturer’s protocol, with 5 ng of total RNA used per sample. Sequencing was performed on an illumina NextSeq 500 system. The resulting sequencing data were converted to fastq format using Bcl2fastq v2.17. Data analysis and visualization of RNA-seq data were performed on DESeq2 (55) and clusterProfiler (56).

Generation of BM chimeras

The BM cells from OT-II Rag2KO (CD45.2+) mice and WT congenic C57BL/6 (CD45.1+) mice were first incubated with anti-CD90.1 (OX-7) Ab from BioLegend and anti-CD90.2 (30-H12) Ab from BD to remove mature T cells, then combined in a 1:5 ratio, and injected intravenously (5 × 106 to 10 × 106 total mixed BM cells) into lethally irradiated C57BL/6 (CD45.1+) recipient mice. The chimeric mice were used for tumor inoculation 13 days postirradiation. Ifnar1KO BM chimeric mice were generated by using the same protocol described above by reconstituting host C57BL/6 (CD45.1+) mice with a 1:1 mixture of Ifnar1KO (CD45.2+) and WT (CD45.1+) BM cells. Mice were used for tumor inoculation 6 weeks postreconstitution.

To calculate the enrichment ratio (R value) of CD45.2+ cells or CD45.1+ cells for the OT-II Rag2KO experiment model, we used the following methods:

PCD45.2 (tumor) = CD45.2+/(CD45.1+ + CD45.2+) CD4+ TCRβ+ T cells within the tumor

PCD45.2 (thymus) = CD45.2+/(CD45.1+ + CD45.2+) thymocytes within the thymus

Ratio R CD45.2 = PCD45.2(tumor)/PCD45.2(thymus)

In vitro coculture of DN thymocytes with Tst4/N-DLL1 cells

Tst4/N-DLL1 cells (obtained from the RIKEN BRC Cell Bank, Japan) were seeded at a density of 0.5 × 105 to 1 × 105 cells per well in a 96-well flat-bottom plate 1 day before the initiation of the coculture experiment to allow cell adherence and stabilization. DN thymocytes were enriched by depletion of cells expressing CD11c, CD49b, B220, CD3ε, CD4, and CD8α using biotinylated Abs and anti-biotin MACS bead and subsequently sorted into DN1 (CD117+ CD25−), DN2 (CD117+ CD25+), DN3 (CD117− CD25+), and DN4 (CD117− CD25−) subsets using a FACSAria III cell sorter. Each sorted DN subset was seeded into the wells at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well. The cells were cocultured in a total volume of 200 μl of complete medium in the presence of stem cell factor (1 ng/ml), interleukin-7 (0.5 ng/ml), Flt-3L (5 ng/ml), and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (1 ng/ml). The medium was supplemented with either 0%, 10% control culture medium (RPMI 1640), or 10% tumor culture supernatant for 7 days at 37°C with 5% CO2.

DC stimulation and IFN-α measurement

Total CD11c+ cells or CD11c+B220+ pDCs were isolated from the thymus by using a FACSAria III cell sorter and cultured in vitro (1 × 104 cells per well for pDCs and 20 × 104 cells per well for total CD11c+ cells) with complete RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS (HyClone), 50 μM β-mercaptoethanol (Gibco), 2 mM glutamine (Gibco), penicillin (100 U/ml; Sigma-Aldrich), streptomycin (100 μg/ml; Sigma-Aldrich), 100 μM nonessential amino acids (Invitrogen), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Invitrogen), and 20 mM Hepes buffer (Invitrogen). The cells were incubated for 12 hours in the presence or absence of ODN1585 CpG (5 μM; InvivoGen). After the stimulation, the culture supernatant was collected to measure IFN-α protein levels using the LEGENDplex assay (BioLegend).

Adoptive transfer of pDCs

CD11c+B220+ pDCs were isolated from the BM, spleen, and LNs from Ccr9WT and Ccr9KO mice by using a FACSAria III cell sorter. Isolated cells were intravenously injected into Ccr9KO recipient mice (5 × 106 to 10 × 106 cells per mouse).

Dataset analysis in TCGA

For data derived from TCGA database and analyzed via the cBioPortal platform, the expression level of the Ccr9 gene in each sample was quantified as log RNA-seq V2 RNA-Seq by Expectation-Maximization (RSEM). Samples with scores exceeding 1 were classified as the high-expression cohort. In contrast, samples with scores ≤ 1 were categorized as the low-expression cohort. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was subsequently used to evaluate survival difference between the high and low expression groups.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed independently at least three times. The results of statistical analyses are expressed as the mean ± SEM, unless otherwise indicated. Statistical significance was calculated through Prism (version 9.4.1) and determined by unpaired Student’s t test. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
