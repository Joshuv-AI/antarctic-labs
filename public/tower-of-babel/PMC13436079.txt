Musculoskeletal Ultrasound-Guided Micro-Needle-Knife Intervention at the Deep Fascial versus Superficial Fascial Layers in Lateral Epicondylitis Patients: A Randomized Controlled Trial [Letter]
Authors: Zijin Zhou, Yutong Shao
Published: 2026

Dear editor

We read with interest the randomized trial by Zhang et al comparing ultrasound-guided micro-needle-knife treatment at the deep and superficial fascial layers for lateral epicondylitis.1 The anatomical comparison is clinically interesting, but several reproducible statistical discrepancies and internal reporting inconsistencies limit interpretation of the magnitude, clinical importance, and safety of the reported superiority.

Risk Ratios Were Reported as Odds Ratios

Table 2 labels every responder estimate as an odds ratio (OR), although the values reproduce risk ratios (RRs). For Week 4 VAS improvement of at least 30%, the reported 1.81 (95% CI, 1.22–2.68) corresponds to 29/35 responders in the deep-fascia group and 16/35 in the superficial-fascia group: (29/35)/(16/35)=1.8125. The standard log-RR interval is 1.23–2.68. The OR from the same 2×2 table is (29 x 19)/(6 x 16)=5.74 (95% CI, approximately 1.91–17.28). For the at least 50% threshold, 26/35 versus 8/35 gives RR=3.25, whereas the OR is 9.75. Because these outcomes are common, ORs and RRs are not interchangeable.2 The methods, Table 2 heading and footnote, abstract, and results should therefore be corrected consistently, with event counts and the exact confidence-interval method provided for every threshold.

Clinical Importance was Not Evaluated on the Randomized Contrast

The study describes 1.5 VAS points as an established minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for lateral epicondylitis and uses it for both sample-size planning and interpretation. However, reference 33 is a systematic review of patient-reported outcomes in shoulder conditions, not lateral epicondylitis.3 The applicability of this threshold to the present population therefore requires justification. Moreover, the Week 4 median VAS reductions were 3 points with deep targeting and 2 points with the active superficial intervention. Both within-group changes exceeding 1.5 points do not establish that the randomized treatment contrast is clinically important. The difference between the reported medians is only 1 point, although subtracting medians is not itself a complete treatment-effect estimator. A baseline-adjusted between-group effect with a 95% CI should be reported and interpreted against a prespecified, population-appropriate threshold.

Participant Flow and Missing-Data Handling are Internally Inconsistent

The results state that all 70 participants completed the intervention protocol, and the flow diagram lists 35 participants in each group as completing the 4-week treatment. The same text reports that one superficial-fascia participant withdrew after only 2 weeks, while the flow diagram simultaneously records one discontinued intervention in that group. These statements cannot all describe the same treatment completion status. The article further states that five chained-equation imputations were pooled using Rubin’s rules, but does not report the Little test result, variables in the imputation model, observed denominators by outcome and visit, derivation of binary responders after imputation, or a complete-case sensitivity analysis. Because several outcomes are summarized by medians and interquartile ranges, the method used to combine these summaries also needs clarification. A visit-level flow table and parallel observed-data and imputed analyses would make the intention-to-treat analysis auditable.

Repeated-Measures Inference and Multiplicity Need Direct Treatment Estimates

The methods prespecify a group-by-time repeated-measures analysis, yet no group effect, time effect, group-by-time interaction, F statistic, degrees of freedom, or corrected model estimate is reported. Table 3 instead emphasizes separate within-group tests, and the proposed Friedman analyses cannot by themselves test whether trajectories differ between randomized groups. Table 2 gives group-specific changes and P values but omits the direct between-group differences and their confidence intervals. The analysis also includes two primary outcomes, multiple visits, 30% and 50% thresholds for four outcomes, and five VAS <=3 comparisons; the stated Bonferroni procedure covers only selected comparisons with baseline. Reporting the prespecified group-by-time model, visit-specific treatment contrasts with 95% CIs, and a clear confirmatory-versus-exploratory multiplicity strategy would align the inference with CONSORT guidance.4

Registration Chronology Requires Clarification

The trial is described as being conducted from December 1, 2024, whereas full registration was completed on March 20, 2025. The authors state that the earlier period was a pre-enrollment feasibility phase and that its participants were excluded, but the date of first consent and randomization in the analyzed cohort is not reported. Because prospective registration requires registration by the first participant’s enrollment, the authors should provide that date, the number and activities of feasibility participants, and the time-stamped original and amended registry records. This would distinguish a prospectively registered formal cohort from registration after access to trial outcomes.4

Outcome Figures and Labels Contain Additional Discrepancies

Figure 4 appears to stack participants first reaching VAS <=3 at successive visits: the deep-fascia segments sum to 94.26% and the superficial-fascia segments to 42.83%. Such cumulative first attainment is not necessarily the proportion still at VAS <=3 at Week 8 unless every earlier responder remained below the threshold. Cross-sectional counts at each visit or individual response trajectories are needed before describing the advantage as persistent. At Week 1, 42.86% versus 8.57% equals 15/35 versus 3/35; the stated Pearson test gives chi-square=10.77 and P=0.00103, which should be reported as P=0.001 rather than P<0.001. Figure 6 also appears to reverse panels A and B: the 17% excellent and 46% good-or-better distribution shown in panel B is attributed in the text to the deep-fascia group, although the caption assigns panel B to the superficial group. In addition, the primary-outcome narrative attributes values 2.68 and 3.40 to 30% PFG improvement even though Table 2 identifies them as 50% PPT improvement. These labels should be reconciled with the underlying data.

Safety and Clinical Scope are Overstated

Adverse events occurred in 3/35 deep-fascia and 2/35 superficial-fascia participants. This small difference is imprecise, and zero serious events in 35 participants per group still permits a one-sided 95% upper event probability of approximately 8.2% in each group. Without a safety margin or an adequately powered safety analysis, the data show only that no serious event was observed, not that the interventions were similarly safe. The active superficial-fascia comparator also cannot establish effectiveness relative to usual care, sham treatment, or first-line rehabilitation, and eligibility did not require failure of first-line treatment. Recommendations positioning deep-fascia treatment as a second-line option should therefore be moderated. Claims of adhesion release, nociceptor modulation, restored force transmission, and biomechanical recovery should likewise remain hypotheses because none of these mechanisms was measured.

The direction of the short-term between-group separation may remain after correction, but its magnitude, clinical importance, reproducibility, and safety require more precise reporting. A corrigendum providing corrected effect-measure labels, responder counts, direct treatment effects with confidence intervals, a reconciled participant flow, and corrected figure captions would substantially improve interpretation of this trial.
