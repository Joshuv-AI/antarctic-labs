Effects of cultivar and stir-frying on the volatile flavor characteristics of white and yellow mustard seeds
Authors: Zhimei Sun, Lili Gao, Mengyao Hou, Lihua Zhang, Xi Chen, Jirong Liang, Fuhao Chu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the effects of cultivar and stir-frying on the volatile flavor of white and yellow mustard seeds using sensory evaluation, electronic nose (E-nose), and chromatography-ion mobility spectrometry (GC-IMS). Stir-frying changed the aroma from pungent/mustard-like to stir-fried/oily, with the strongest intensity in stir-fried yellow mustard seeds. E-nose responses followed the order stir-fried yellow > stir-fried white > raw white > raw yellow. GC-IMS revealed significant changes in aldehydes, esters, pyrazines, and sulfur-containing compounds. 2-Methylpyrazine, 2-ethylpyrazine, 2-methylthiophene, octanal, β-myrcene, and ethyl 2-methylpropionate were identified as representative markers for cultivar and processing-state discrimination. The combined approach provides an effective strategy for mustard seed identification and flavor evaluation.

Introduction

Mustard seeds are primarily classified into two types: white mustard seeds (Sinapis alba L.) and yellow mustard seeds (Brassica juncea (L.) Czern. et Coss.), both being the dried mature seeds of Brassicaceae plants (Committee National Pharmacopoeia, 2020). As seed crops, mustard seeds exhibit extensive application value in the food and health sectors. They are not only rich in nutrients including fats, proteins, carbohydrates and carotenoids, but also contain bioactive compounds such as sinapine thiocyanate, isothiocyanates, volatile oils and phenols(Oulad El Majdoub et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2022), which provide the material basis for their unique flavor and functional characteristics (Ogidi et al., 2025). Mustard seeds are widely used in the production of food products like mustard oil, mustard powder and mustard paste (Cools & Terry, 2018). Mustard oil is renowned for its nutty aroma and pungent taste; mustard powder releases pungent substances upon contact with water; mustard paste is extensively applied in both Chinese and Western cuisines due to its compound seasoning properties. Mustard seeds boast a long history of application in global dietary cultures(Molnár and Pal, 2025; Lietzow, 2021; Peplow, 2013): in early Europe, they were regarded as an indigenous spice; in East and South Asia, mustard oil and whole mustard seeds are commonly incorporated into daily dishes; in China, leveraging its medicine and food homology attribute, mustard seeds serve as a seasoning to enhance flavor, reduce greasiness, stimulate appetite and facilitate digestion. To date, mustard seeds remain the second-largest traded spice globally, with their unique flavor highly favored by consumers.

The aroma and flavor of mustard seeds are determined by a variety of volatile organic compounds (VOCs), which exert significant impacts on their sensory quality and health. Since white mustard seeds are priced higher than yellow mustard seeds despite their similar appearance, some unscrupulous merchants fumigate yellow mustard seeds with sulfur and other illicit means to pass them off as white mustard seeds, posing potential food safety hazards. It is therefore of practical significance to establish rapid and accurate methods for discriminating mustard seed varieties and their raw and stir-fried products, so as to maintain market order and improve product quality. Current research on mustard seeds has mostly focused on their chemical compositions and pharmacological activities (Mishra et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026), whereas systematic studies on their volatile components and flavor differences remain relatively scarce. Existing literature has reported partial analytical work using high-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS) (Balkrishna et al., 2024)and gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) (Farag et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2023). In contrast, the chromatography-ion mobility spectrometry (GC-IMS) enables rapid and sensitive analysis of VOCs in complex samples, featuring advantages such as simple sample pretreatment, high resolution and rapid detection speed. Meanwhile, the generated fingerprint enables intuitive comparison between samples and can capture aroma variations caused by cultivar differences and stir-frying processing. This technique has been widely applied in the quality evaluation of alcoholic beverages, foods, Chinese herbal medicines, environment and other related fields (Li et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025).

This study employed GC-IMS and E-nose techniques to systematically characterize the VOCs in different mustard seed varieties and their raw and stir-fried products. Furthermore, by integrating fingerprinting profiles, content ratios, relative odor activity value (ROAV)(Wang et al., 2025) and chemometric methods, we conducted an in-depth comparison of VOCs differences among the four categories of mustard seed samples. A identification model for mustard seed varieties and their raw/stir-fried products was established based on volatile profiles. This study lays a theoretical foundation for mustard seed variety discrimination, stir-frying quality control, identification of key flavor compounds and product quality assessment, and also provides a reference for research on the food processing and standardized application of mustard seeds.

Materials and methods

Materials

The experimental materials were white mustard seeds and yellow mustard seeds collected from Deyang, Sichuan Province. The cultivar identification was performed by Professor Lihua Zhang from Zaozhuang University. The samples were identified as Sinapis alba L. (white mustard seeds) and Brassica juncea (L.) Czern. et Coss. (yellow mustard seeds) of the Brassicaceae family. The plants were harvested when the fruits matured in late summer and early autumn, dried in the sun, and threshed to collect seeds, followed by removing impurities. Mustard seeds grow well in warm and humid environments, with tolerance to cold and drought, and are suitable for cultivation in fertile sandy loam or clay loam soils.

Sample preparation

50 g of raw white mustard seeds and raw yellow mustard seeds from the same batch were separately placed in a temperature-controlled electric frying pan and stir-fried at 160 °C for around 5 min. The stir-frying was terminated once a continuous popping sound was detected. All samples were then immediately spread evenly on a plate and cooled naturally to room temperature. Subsequently, the raw and stir-fried samples of both mustard varieties were ground into fine powder with a mortar and pestle, followed by sieving through a 40-mesh standard analytical sieve. Subsequently, 0.5 g of the powder was accurately weighed and transferred into individual 20 mL headspace vials for subsequent analysis. Three independent biological replicates were prepared, each containing three parallel analytical replicates.

E-nose analysis

The PEN 3 electronic nose (E-nose, AIRYense, Germany) employed in this study was equipped with ten distinct metal oxide sensors. Among these sensors, W1S, W3S, W5S, W6S, W1C, W3C, and W5C exhibited sensitivity to methyl compounds, long-chain alkanes, nitrogen oxides, hydrides, aromatic compounds, ammonia compounds, as well as short-chain alkanes and aromatic compounds, respectively. Sensor W1W was sensitive to inorganic sulfides, sensor W2S responded to alcohols, aldehydes and ketones, while sensor W2W was sensitive to organic sulfides. Prior to detection, the samples were equilibrated at room temperature for 30 min. The sensor parameters were set as follows: cleaning time of 120 s, reset time of 10 s, pre-injection time of 5 s, carrier gas flow rate of 300 mL/min, and a detection cycle of 300 s per sample.

GC-IMS analysis

The VOCs of the four sample types were analyzed using a GC-IMS system (FlavourSpec®-G.A.S., Dortmund, Germany). Samples were incubated at 60 °C with shaking at 500 r/min for 15 min. After the headspace fraction in the vial was extracted using a sampling needle, the needle was inserted into the gas chromatography inlet for desorption prior to GC-IMS determination. The GC-IMS conditions were set as follows: an MXT-WAX capillary column (30 m × 0.53 mm ID, 1 μm df) was used; the IMS temperature was maintained at 45 °C and the column temperature at 60 °C; the injection volume was 100 μL with a splitless injection mode; the headspace injection needle temperature was 85 °C; the total chromatographic run time was 30 min; high-purity nitrogen (≥ 99.999%) was used as the carrier gas. The carrier gas flow rate program was configured as follows: an initial flow rate of 2.00 mL/min held for 2 min, linearly increased to 10.00 mL/min from 2 to 10 min, further increased linearly to 100.00 mL/min from 10 to 20 min, and finally raised linearly to 150.00 mL/min from 20 to 30 min. Characteristic VOCs were identified using the Vacal analysis software integrated with the GC-IMS system and the NIST database. The relative content of each VOC was calculated as the ratio of its peak volume to the sum of peak volumes of all detected compounds in the sample. The odor characteristics of flavor compounds were obtained from the GC-IMS spectral library.

ROAV calculation

The contribution degree of each VOC to the sample odor was evaluated using the ROAV. The volatile component with a defined ROAV value of 100 was regarded as the one exerting the maximum contribution to the sample odor. The ROAV values of other VOCs were calculated using the following formula: ROAVi = OAVi/OAVmax = 100 × Ci/Cmax × Tmax/Ti, where OVA represents the odor activity value; Ci represents the relative content of a specific VOCs (%); Ti denotes its corresponding odor threshold (mg/kg); Cmax refers to the relative content of the VOCs with the highest odor contribution (%); and Tmax is the corresponding odor threshold of this component (mg/kg); VOCs with ROAV ≥1 were identified as the key aroma components of the samples, while those with 0.1 ≤ ROAV <1 played an important modifying role in the overall odor profile of the samples.

Data analysis

Qualitative analysis of spectra and data was performed using the VOCal plug-in, with qualitative identification of VOCs achieved via the built-in NIST and IMS databases of the software. Radar charts, pie charts and stacked bar charts were plotted using Origin 2024. Principal component analysis (PCA), orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) and variable importance in projection (VIP) analysis were conducted using SIMCA-14.0 software. Hierarchical clustering heatmap analysis was completed using TBtools software.

Results and discussion

Sensory assessment of mustard seeds

Sensory evaluation of raw and processed white and yellow mustard seeds was performed by a seven-member panel. Raw white mustard seeds showed pungent, nutty and raw peanut-like aroma; raw yellow mustard seeds possessed stronger pungency and typical mustard flavor. After stir-frying, both samples presented stir-fried and oily notes, with mild residual pungency in stir-fried white mustard seeds and rich greasy-stir-fried aroma in stir-fried yellow mustard seeds. Further analyses were conducted via E-nose and GC-IMS.

Analysis of aroma characteristics of mustard seeds via electronic nose

To comprehensively investigate the aroma characteristics, this study first employed E-nose analysis to compare the aroma differences between white and yellow mustard seeds before and after stir-frying. As shown in the radar chart (Fig. 1A), the metal oxide sensors W1W, W2W, W5S, W2S, W1S and W3S exhibited relatively high response values to mustard seeds, all of which exceeded 1. This indicated that mustard seeds might generally contain VOCs with relatively high contents, such as inorganic sulfides, organic sulfides, nitrogen oxides, alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, methyl compounds, and long-chain alkanes. Fig. 1B presented the PCA biplot derived from E-nose detection of the four mustard seed samples, which intuitively demonstrated the differences in volatile characteristics among different samples as well as the correlation between sample VOCs and the responses of E-nose metal oxide sensors. The explained variance ratios of principal component axes p[1] and p[2] were 87.3% and 7.37%, respectively, with a cumulative explained variance ratio of 94.67%. In terms of sample distribution: Y, W, RW, and RY exhibited a gradient distribution ranging from −1 to 1 along the p[1] axis in sequence. Combined with sensor responses, it was found that sensors clustered with stir-fried samples (e.g., W1W, W2S, W2W, W3S) showed more significant responses to their VOCs, whereas sensors W1C, W3C, and W5C were more sensitive to the volatile characteristics of raw samples. In addition, the six sensors exhibited a descending order of response magnitude toward the four samples: Y > W > RW > RY (Fig. 1C). Comprehensive characterization of VOCs in mustard seeds via GC-IMS

Analysis on the overall GC-IMS spectra of four mustard samples

GC-IMS is primarily designed for the detection of small-molecule odor-active compounds with carbon chain lengths of C4–C9 (Qi et al., 2020). In this study, the positive ion mode of GC-IMS was employed to analyze the VOCs in white mustard seeds, yellow mustard seeds, and their raw and stir-fried products. As illustrated in Fig. 2A, the three-dimensional (3D) spectral characteristics of mustard seed samples were comparatively visualized using the Reporter plug-in. combining retention time, drift time and peak intensity revealed that the VOCs in mustard seed samples exhibited significant differences in peak position, number and intensity. However, 3D spectra only allow a rough observation of the differences among mustard seed samples. Therefore, we further refined the differences among samples through top-down comparative analysis (Zhang et al., 2022).

The top-down view featured a predominantly blue background, with the abscissa representing normalized ion drift time and the ordinate representing gas chromatography retention time (s). The red vertical line denotes the reaction ion peak (RIP, normalized), located at a normalized abscissa of 1.0. Each spot in the region to the right of the RIP corresponds to one VOC. Color indicates signal peak intensity: white represents low intensity, while red represents high intensity. Most signals appeared within the range of 200–1300 s for retention time and 6.0–10.0 ms for drift time, suggesting that the distribution of compounds in this region may represent the characteristic profile of VOCs (Fig. 2B). In the subsequent step, sample RY was selected as the reference, and the reference signal values were subtracted from the spectra of the other three samples, ultimately yielding the differential comparison map of the samples (Fig. 2C). A white background indicates identical VOC content between samples, red indicates higher content than the reference, and blue indicates lower content than the reference. The darker the color, the greater the concentration difference. Analysis of the results showed that yellow mustard seeds had higher VOC contents than white mustard seeds, and stir-fried products had higher VOC contents than raw products. This may be one of the key reasons why yellow mustard seeds possess a stronger pungent odor than white mustard seeds. These findings further confirmed that the quality of mustard seeds may be influenced by their VOCs contents.

A total of 40 VOCs were identified (Fig. 3A, Table 1), mainly including ketones, alcohols, esters, aldehydes, terpenes, as well as nitrogen-, oxygen- and sulfur-containing heterocyclic compounds. Further comparison of peak volume proportions of different classes of VOCs across the four samples (Fig. 3C) revealed obvious differences in their relative compositions. Overall, raw and stir-fried samples differed substantially in the proportions of alcohols and esters, indicating that stir-frying significantly affected the formation, transformation and degradation of VOCs in mustard seeds. Meanwhile, distinct differences in the relative contents of all categorized components were observed between stir-fried white and yellow mustard seeds, which demonstrated variations in precursor compositions and thermal reaction product distributions between the two varieties. These results proved that variety and stir-frying jointly shaped the compositional profiles and relative abundance of VOCs in mustard seeds.

To further explore the distribution differences of VOCs among the four samples, visual analysis was conducted based on characteristic peaks in GC-IMS fingerprints (Fig. 3A). A peak height of 0.04 V was set as the detection threshold, and compounds with peak heights below this value were considered undetected in corresponding samples. Each row represented all signal peaks of one sample, and each column stood for the same compound across different samples. Color intensity corresponded to compound content, and brighter color indicated higher content. The four samples presented evident discrepancies in the species and signal intensity of VOCs, indicating each sample possessed unique volatile fingerprint features.

According to the detection of characteristic peaks, the fingerprints were divided into seven regions marked a to g. Region a contained VOCs common to all four samples, such as 2-pentylfuran, styrene, 1-penten-3-ol and n-propylbenzene. Several compounds in region b were only detected in stir-fried samples, including ethyl 3-hydroxybutyrate, 2-ethylpyrazine, 1-hydroxy-2-propanone and 2-methylpyrazine (M), implying these substances were newly generated during stir-frying. In addition, variety-specific components were found: α-terpinolene and octanal in region d exclusively existed in stir-fried yellow mustard seed, while 2-methylpyrazine (D) in region e was unique to stir-fried white mustard seed, reflecting the divergence of volatile products between the two varieties after thermal processing.

Selective absence of certain VOCs was also observed, where specific compounds were undetected in a single sample but present in the others. For example, raw white mustard seed lacked compounds in region c including pyrazine, cyclopentanone and β-myrcene; δ-3-carene in region f was not detected in stir-fried white mustard seed; and 2-propanone in region g was absent in raw yellow mustard seed. Such absent peaks can serve as important indicators for sample discrimination. In summary, the four mustard seed samples differed greatly in VOC contents, and characteristic peaks of various types constituted their respective distinct GC-IMS fingerprints.

Analysis on abundance changes of differential VOCs

The hierarchical clustering heatmap converts complex multivariate data into intuitive graphical information to reveal inherent similarities and differences (Chen et al., 2025). The hierarchical clustering heatmap was constructed based on the VOC data detected by GC-IMS (Fig. 4). The heatmap intuitively illustrates the differences in relative contents of various volatile components and the clustering relationships among the four groups of mustard seed samples. Hierarchical clustering analysis results showed that RW and RY first clustered into one group, indicating that their volatile profiles exhibited high similarity. Y and W subsequently clustered with raw mustard seeds into the same major group.This finding demonstrates that heat treatment was the core factor driving differences in the volatile characteristics of mustard seeds, which was consistent with the results of the E-nose PCA biplot and verifies the significant regulatory effect of stir-frying on mustard VOCs. In the heatmap, red indicates high relative content and blue indicates low relative content. Within the stir-fried group, the relative contents of n-butyl acetate, 2-methylpyrazine(M), styrene, and 2-butanone(D) in W were significantly higher than those in Y, while n-propylbenzene, pyrazine, cyclopentanone, β-myrcene, α-terpinene, and 2-pentanol were more abundant in Y. Within the raw group, 2,2,6,6-pentamethylheptane(M) was present at a higher level in RW than in RY, and 2-pentylfuran, pyrazine, β-myrcene, and n-propylbenzene were more concentrated in RY. In intraspecific comparisons between raw and stir-fried samples, differences for both varieties were reflected by multiple components: Both stir-fried products of the two mustard varieties were significantly enriched in thermal reaction products of ketones, aldehydes, and esters, including n-butyl acetate, 4-methyl-2-pentanone, butyraldehyde, and 2-butanone(D). In contrast, raw samples were dominated by alcohols and alkanes, such as propanol, 2-methyl-1-propanol, 2,2,4,6,6-pentamethylheptane(M), and 1-butanol. To clarify the differences in odor components among the four mustard seed samples, this study employed the ROAV method to analyze VOCs (Table 1). It should be noted that odor thresholds are affected by sample matrix, detection conditions and literature sources, resulting in certain variability and limitations. This is a common occurrence in flavor research. Therefore, ROAV is only applied as a semi-quantitative auxiliary method to preliminarily screen flavor compounds with potential sensory activity. Combined with indicators such as Odor Threshold (OT), VIP, and normalized proportion (Table S1), ethyl 2-methylpropionate (ROAV = 100), which contributed the most to the odor activity, was identified as the reference substance. Meanwhile, the odor thresholds of each VOC was obtained by consulting the odor threshold handbook, providing parameter support for subsequent analyses (Van Gemert, 2011). The results indicated that ethyl 2-methylpropionate exhibited ROAV values greater than 1 in all four mustard seed samples, identifying it as a key aroma-active compound responsible for the fruity note of the seeds. In RW, β-myrcene, 3-methylbutanal and octanal displayed ROAV values in the 0.1–1 range and were therefore classified as secondary odor-active compounds; these components likely contributed herbaceous and pungent nuances to RW. In RY, β-myrcene also shows ROAV>1, suggesting an important role in the characteristic mustard note of RY. Additionally, 2-pentylfuran, hexanal, 3-methylbutanal, octanal and ethyl butanoate (0.1 < ROAV<1) collectively imparted herbaceous, fatty and pungent attributes to raw yellow mustard

Stir-frying markedly altered the composition of aroma-active constituents. In W, ethyl 2-methylpropionate, β-myrcene, 3-methylbutanal and ethyl butanoate (all with ROAV>1) remained major contributors to aroma; secondary contributors include 2-ethylpyrazine, octanal, butyraldehyde, methyl isobutyrate, 3-methylbutyl butanoate and n-butyl acetate, which together shaped W's characteristic roasted/fried aroma. Y was characterized primarily by α-terpinene, β-myrcene, octanal, butyraldehyde and ethyl butanoate, which contributed a pronounced fatty/oily profile; secondary odor-active volatiles such as 2-pentylfuran, n-propylbenzene and hexanal added complexity, and the presence of citrus-like components differentiateed Y's aroma profile from that of W.

In summary, ethyl 2-methylpropionate was identified as a pivotal compound conferring fruity character, while a range of secondary volatiles reinforced fatty and pungent sensory attributes. The two roasted samples exhibited distinct flavor profiles: W showed a prominent roasted/fried aroma, whereas Y displayed a citrus-toned character, resulting in distinguishable overall aroma profiles. To further reveal the overall variation patterns and key driving factors of flavor differences between raw and roasted samples as well as among different groups, PCA and OPLS-DA were applied for statistical analysis of aroma-active components.

Multivariate statistical analysis

To explore the differences between mustard varieties and their raw/stir-fried products, multivariate statistical analyses including PCA and OPLS-DA were performed (Fig. 5A, B). The PCA score plot (Fig. 5A) showed a certain discrimination trend in the distribution of the four sample groups: RW and RY clustered closely, indicating similar VOC profiles between them. While raw and stir-fied, W and Y were widely separated, reflecting significant differences in their VOC compositions. The explanatory variance of the first principal component (PC1) was R2X[1] = 0.552, and that of the second principal component (PC2) was R2X[2] = 0.329, suggesting that the main variation originated from PC1. On this basis, an OPLS- DA model was further constructed (Fig. 5B). The results showed that RW and RY were still located in adjacent regions, while W and Y were distributed in different quadrants, which was consistent with the results of the PCA model. The OPLS-DA model exhibited R2X = 0.99, R2Y = 0.996, and Q2 = 0.995. Both R2 and Q2 values greater than 0.5 indicated a good fitting effect of the model, with a significance probability of p < 0.05. These results demonstrated that the OPLS-DA model used in this study was reliable and statistically significant (Yun et al., 2021). After 200 permutation tests (Fig. 5D), the intersection of the Q2 regression line with the vertical axis was less than 0, indicating no overfitting of the model and verifying its reliability (Shao et al., 2023). VIP analysis (Fig. 5C) revealed that multiple VOCs contributed significantly to the discrimination between groups. Based on the criterion of VIP > 1, VOCs such as ethyl 2-methylpropionate, 2-pentylfuran, and hexanal showed high VIP values, which were identified as the main characteristic components responsible for the differences among different sample groups. This was consistent with the results of fingerprint profile and ROAV method analysis. The significant changes in these key VOCs may be closely related to mustard variety and stir-frying.Fig. 5PCA score plot (A), OPLS-DA score plot (B), VIP plot of VOCs with VIP>1 (C), and 200 permutation test plot of the model (D) for four mustard seed samples.Fig. 5

VIP screening further confirmed the main contributing components, and 200 permutation tests verified the stability and reliability of the model, providing solid data support for the analysis of mustard varieties and stir-frying processes.

With respect to processing state, stir-frying generated several characteristic volatiles that were absent in raw samples, mainly including pyrazines and sulfur-containing heterocyclic compounds. Among them, 2-methylpyrazine, 2-ethylpyrazine, and 2-methylthiophene were identified as representative processing markers because of their clear processing specificity and strong discriminatory potential. These compounds are typical thermal reaction products and were closely associated with the roasted and oily sensory attributes observed after stir-frying.

For cultivar discrimination, several aldehydes, esters, and terpenes showed distinct distribution patterns between white and yellow mustard seeds. Octanal was only detected in stir-fried yellow mustard seeds and showed ROAV >1, indicating that it was both sample-specific and aroma-active, and could therefore be regarded as a key flavor marker for this sample. In addition, β-myrcene and ethyl butanoate were absent in raw white mustard seeds and contributed to cultivar differentiation. Ethyl 2-methylpropionate was considered a representative integrated marker because it showed significant differences, high classification contribution, and high odor activity. Other compounds, such as hexanal, propanol, 1-penten-3-ol, and δ-3-carene, also contributed to sample separation, although their discriminatory importance was lower than that of the representative markers.

Overall, 2-methylpyrazine, 2-ethylpyrazine, and 2-methylthiophene could serve as characteristic markers for distinguishing raw and stir-fried products, whereas octanal, β-myrcene, ethyl butanoate, and ethyl 2-methylpropionate were more relevant for cultivar discrimination. These markers not only enabled effective identification of mustard seed cultivars and processing states, but also provided important clues for understanding volatile flavor changes induced by stir-frying.

Mechanism speculation of VOCs changes of mustard during stir-frying

The changes in volatile compounds after roasting mustard seeds are mainly associated with lipid oxidative degradation, Maillard reactions, thermal degradation of sugars, and the corresponding cross-reaction pathways(Chang et al., 2020; Gao et al., 2023; Lan et al., 2025). The speculated mechanism underlying the changes in VOCs of mustard seeds during stir-frying is as follows (Fig. 6): Mustard seeds are rich in unsaturated fatty acids, such as linoleic acid, α-linolenic acid, and oleic acid(Kaseke et al., 2021), which are readily oxidatively cleaved under heat to generate volatile compounds including aldehydes, alcohols, ketones, esters, and furans. In the present study, 1-penten-3-ol, hexanal, 2-butanone, and 2-pentylfuran were detected in all samples, suggesting that these compounds may represent important contributors to the basic flavor of mustard seeds. By contrast, compounds detected only after roasting, such as octanal, n-butyl acetate, and ethyl 3-hydroxybutyrate, may originate from intensified lipid oxidation and subsequent esterification reactions, thereby imparting more pronounced oily and fruity notes to the stir-fried samples.Fig. 6Mechanism of flavor formation during stir-frying of mustard seeds.Fig. 6

Mechanism of flavor formation during stir-frying of mustard seeds.

Meanwhile, roasting promotes Maillard reactions between amino acids and reducing sugars in mustard seeds(Zhang et al., 2022), leading to the formation of pyrazines and other nitrogen-containing heterocyclic compounds. 2-Methylpyrazine and pyrazine were detected exclusively in stir-fried samples, indicating that they are important flavor-active compounds formed during roasting and are closely associated with nutty and stir-fried aromas. In addition, 3-methylbutanal and butanal derived from branched-chain amino acid degradation, together with intermediates such as 1-hydroxy-2-propanone and 3-hydroxy-2-butanone, may collectively contribute to the development of the characteristic stir-fried flavor. Furthermore, sugar thermal degradation and lipid oxidation–Maillard cross-reactions may also facilitate the formation of furans and sulfur-containing heterocyclic compounds. For example, 2-methylthiophene was detected only in stir-fried samples, suggesting that it may be one of the characteristic products formed during thermal processing.

In addition, the differences in flavor profiles between white and yellow mustard seeds are attributable to variations in their intrinsic precursors, including lipids, terpenoids, and amino acids. These compositional differences result in distinct volatile product spectra after thermal processing. Some compounds were detected exclusively in stir-fried yellow mustard seeds or stir-fried white mustard seeds, whereas others showed sample-specific absence, indicating that both cultivar differences and roasting-related transformations jointly influence the formation and accumulation of volatile flavor compounds. Overall, the intrinsic precursor composition of different cultivars, together with roasting-induced thermal reactions, shapes the distinct volatile organic compound profiles of mustard seeds.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that both cultivar and stir-frying significantly shaped the volatile flavor characteristics of mustard seeds. Sensory evaluation showed a transition from pungent/mustard-like notes in raw samples to stir-fried/oily notes after stir-frying, especially in yellow mustard seeds. E-nose analysis supported these differences, with response intensity decreasing in the order of stir-fried yellow > stir-fried white > raw white > raw yellow. GC-IMS further revealed pronounced changes in aldehydes, esters, pyrazines, and sulfur-containing compounds, and identified 2-methylpyrazine, 2-ethylpyrazine, 2-methylthiophene, octanal, β-myrcene, and ethyl 2-methylpropionate as representative markers for cultivar and processing-state discrimination. Overall, the integration of sensory evaluation, E-nose, and GC-IMS provides an effective approach for mustard seed authentication, flavor characterization, and quality evaluation.
