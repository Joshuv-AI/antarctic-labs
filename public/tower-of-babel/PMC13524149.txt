A global perspective on prognosis in acral and nonacral cutaneous melanoma: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Chi-Han Huang, Kun-Han Lee, Po-Yu Chu, Cheng-Yuan Li, Tien-Hsiang Wang, Hsu Ma, Yu-Jen Chiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Acral melanoma (AM) is a rare cutaneous malignancy associated with poor survival. However, prognostic differences between AM and nonacral cutaneous melanoma (NAM) remain unclear. Therefore, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to compare survival outcomes between patients with AM and those with NAM.Methods:PubMed, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library were comprehensively searched for studies published between January 2000 and December 2025. Studies comparing the 5-year overall survival (OS) and 5-year relapse-free survival (RFS) between AM and NAM were included. Subgroup analyses were performed by disease stage and ethnicity to assess their impact on melanoma prognosis.Results:Twenty studies (18 retrospective and two prospective cohort studies) involving 568 346 patients were included. The meta-analysis demonstrated that AM was associated with significantly lower 5-year OS than NAM (risk ratio [RR]: 0.89; 95% CI: 0.86-0.92; p < 0.00001). In subgroup analyses, compared with NAM, AM was associated with poorer 5-year OS in both stage II (RR: 0.92; 95% CI: 0.88-0.96; p = 0.0003) and stage III disease (RR: 0.85; 95% CI: 0.75-0.96; p = 0.01). Additionally, patients from darker-skinned populations with AM had poorer 5-year OS than those from White populations (RR: 0.89; 95% CI: 0.83-0.95; p = 0.0006).Conclusion:Patients with AM exhibited markedly lower 5-year OS than those with NAM, particularly among patients with stage II and III. Moreover, individuals from darker-skinned populations with AM had poorer survival outcomes than those from White populations.Keywords: Acral melanoma, Cutaneous melanoma, Ethnicity, Survival outcome, Tumor staging

1. INTRODUCTION

Cutaneous melanoma is a malignant tumor originating from melanocytes and is widely recognized as one of the deadliest forms of skin cancer. Although it accounts for only 5% to 10% of skin cancer diagnoses, melanoma is responsible for approximately 75% of skin cancer–related deaths, representing a major global health burden.1 Furthermore, epidemiological studies have reported a steady worldwide increase in the incidence of cutaneous melanoma, largely driven by excessive ultraviolet (UV) exposure and changes in environmental and lifestyle factors.2

Notably, melanoma exhibits distinct incidence patterns and clinical presentations across different ethnic groups and anatomical sites. In White populations, the trunk is the most common site of melanoma, with males most frequently affected on the back and females on the legs.3 In contrast, acral melanoma (AM), a subtype of cutaneous melanoma arising at acral sites, including the palms, soles, and subungual regions, is more prevalent in non-White populations, including Asians, Africans, and Latin Americans.4,5 In White populations, AM accounts for approximately 5% of all melanoma cases; however, multiple literature reviews indicate that AM accounts for a markedly higher proportion, ranging from 40% to 80%, in non-White populations.6–9 In Korea, Kim et al10 reported that 58.3% of melanomas arose on the feet (40.9%) and hands (17.4%). Among Japanese patients with cutaneous melanoma, AM accounts for approximately 40% of cases.11 Furthermore, among Taiwanese patients, AM accounts for up to 75% of all cutaneous melanoma cases.12,13

AM is generally associated with poorer survival than nonacral cutaneous melanoma (NAM). This reduced survival is often attributed to delayed diagnosis because the acral regions are less accessible for self-examination.14–17 Additionally, the resemblance of AM to benign conditions, such as melanonychia, subungual hematoma, chronic wounds, warts, and fungal infections, frequently leads to misdiagnosis. A previous study reported that 30% of AM cases were initially misdiagnosed, resulting in a median 9-month delay to biopsy and definitive diagnosis.18

However, conflicting results have been reported in the literature, with some studies reporting no meaningful difference in survival outcomes between patients with AM and those with NAM.19 These findings have been reported in studies conducted across Asia, the Americas, and Europe.11,20,21 Notably, a study conducted at a tertiary hospital in Korea found that patients with AM had a better prognosis, with melanoma of the trunk associated with the highest mortality risk compared with cutaneous melanomas at other sites.22 Furthermore, AM is characterized by its atypical presentation and limited association with UV exposure, suggesting that its pathogenesis may differ from that of NAM.23–25

Given these discrepancies, whether patients with AM have a poorer prognosis than those with NAM remains unclear. Therefore, the objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to compare survival outcomes between patients with AM and those with NAM.

2. METHODS

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement.26 The protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024620045).

2.1. Data sources and search

A comprehensive search of PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library was conducted by two independent reviewers (CHH and KHL) using the terms “acral melanoma,” “non-acral melanoma,” “cutaneous melanoma,” “survival,” and “prognosis” to identify studies published between spanning January 2000 and December 2025. The reference lists of all included studies were also screened for additional eligible articles. Only English-language articles were included.

2.2. Study selection

Studies comparing survival outcomes between patients with AM and NAM were eligible for inclusion. Only studies with a minimum follow-up of 5 years were included. The primary outcomes were 5-year overall survival (OS) and 5-year relapse-free survival (RFS). OS was defined as the proportion of patients alive 5 years after diagnosis. RFS was defined as the proportion of patients who remained alive without relapse at 5 years.

For the purpose of this review, AM was broadly defined as melanoma arising at acral sites, including the palms, soles, and subungual apparatus. Studies reporting acral lentiginous melanoma or melanoma occurring at these acral locations were considered eligible for inclusion in the AM group according to the definitions used in the original reports.

Two reviewers (C-HH and K-HL) independently assessed the eligibility of all retrieved articles. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (Y-JC).

2.3. Data extraction and quality assessment

Data were extracted by two investigators (C-HH and K-HL) using a predefined data extraction form. Extracted data included the journal title, first author, year of publication, country of study, data source, study design, sample size, and patient characteristics. The risk of bias of each study was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool (ROB 2.0) for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) for nonrandomized cohort studies. Studies identified as having a high risk of bias according to ROB 2.0 or scoring <6 on the NOS were considered low quality and were excluded. Discrepancies in the risk assessment were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (Y-JC).

2.4. Data synthesis and analysis

We extracted the total number of patients, along with the numbers of patients who achieved 5-year OS and 5-year RFS, from each included study. These pooled data were used to calculate risk ratios (RRs) with 95% CIs as the primary effect measure for the 5-year landmark analysis. Statistical significance was defined as a p value <0.05 (two-tailed). A random-effects model was used for the meta-analysis. We considered performing meta-regression to further explore the heterogeneity in the 5-year OS analysis. However, this was not performed because key study-level covariates, including treatment era, stage distribution, treatment details, and geographic setting, were inconsistently reported across the included studies. Additionally, the limited number of studies for several candidate moderators may have resulted in unstable and potentially misleading estimates. All statistical analyses were conducted using Review Manager (RevMan), version 5.4 (The Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen, Denmark).

2.5. Data subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were performed according to (1) stage (AM vs NAM across stages I-IV) and (2) ethnicity (darker-skinned vs White populations). The darker-skinned group included Asian, African, Alaskan Native, American Indian, Latin American, and Pacific Islander populations. The results of the subgroup analyses are presented as forest plots of effect sizes with 95% CIs.

3. RESULTS

3.1. Literature search

A comprehensive literature search was conducted using PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library. A total of 2526 records were identified through database searching. Of these, 1536 records were excluded because of duplication, and 951 records were excluded during title and abstract screening. Ultimately, 20 studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis.11,14,15,20–22,27–40 The selected studies were all observational in design, comprising two prospective cohort studies and 18 retrospective cohort studies (Fig. 1). A total of 568 346 patients were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis, including 12 654 with AM and 555 692 with NAM. 3.2. Primary outcomes: 5-year OS and RFS

Based on the forest plot of 5-year OS (Fig. 3A), AM was associated with a significantly lower 5-year OS than NAM (RR: 0.89; 95% CI: 0.86-0.92; p < 0.00001), indicating poorer long-term survival among patients with AM than among those with NAM. In contrast, the analysis of 5-year RFS showed a slightly lower rate in patients with AM than in those with NAM; however, the difference did not reach statistical significance (RR: 0.98; 95% CI: 0.82-1.17; p = 0.80) (Fig. 3B).

3.3. Subgroup analyses: disease stage and ethnicity

To further evaluate the prognosis of AM, subgroup analyses were performed according to disease stage and patient ethnicity.

Five studies were included in the disease stage subgroup analysis. The baseline stage distributions of the included studies are summarized in Table 1. Among the four clinical stages, patients with AM generally had a poorer prognosis than those with NAM, although the differences reached statistical significance only among patients with stage II (RR: 0.92; 95% CI: 0.88-0.96; p = 0.0003) and stage III disease (RR: 0.85; 95% CI: 0.75-0.96; p = 0.010) (Fig. 4).

Four studies provided data for the ethnicity-based subgroup analysis, which demonstrated significant differences in 5-year OS between ethnic groups. Patients with AM from darker-skinned populations had a significantly lower 5-year OS than those from White populations (RR: 0.89; 95% CI: 0.83-0.95; p = 0.0006) (Fig. 5).

4. DISCUSSION

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis to compare survival outcomes between AM and NAM. Through a rigorous literature review and systematic evidence-based approach, we addressed a long-standing debate by synthesizing the current body of evidence to inform clinical decision-making. Our findings demonstrated that AM is associated with substantially lower 5-year OS than NAM, indicating a poorer long-term survival prognosis. Specifically, patients with AM diagnosed at stages II and III exhibited worse prognoses than those with NAM; however, no significant differences were observed at stages I and IV. Furthermore, patients with AM from darker-skinned populations had poorer 5-year OS than White populations, underscoring potential ethnic differences in melanoma outcomes.

Although melanoma constitutes a relatively small proportion of skin cancers, it accounts for the majority of skin cancer-related deaths due to its aggressive nature and high metastatic potential, particularly AM.41 Most previous studies have attributed the poor prognosis of AM to delayed diagnosis, as it often arises in areas such as the nail bed, where it can be mistaken for onychomycosis or subungual hematoma, or on the soles of the feet, which are less amenable to routine self-examination.14–18 However, our findings show that even when diagnosed at the same stage, patients with AM had poorer long-term survival than patients with NAM. It is important to acknowledge that in several highly weighted studies, such as those by Bradford et al14 and Huang et al,35 the AM cohorts had a higher proportion of stage II and III disease than the NAM cohorts. While this baseline stage imbalance could contribute to the disparity in OS, our stage-stratified subgroup analysis partly addressed this concern. Notably, the persistent survival disadvantage among patients with AM within stage II (RR: 0.92; p = 0.0003) and stage III (RR: 0.85; p = 0.01) suggests that the poorer prognosis was not merely a reflection of more advanced disease at presentation. Instead, this evidence strongly suggests that intrinsic biological aggressiveness may contribute to the inferior outcomes of AM, independent of the stage at diagnosis. Importantly, stage III serves as a critical clinical threshold for initiating adjuvant systemic therapy, highlighting the clinical relevance of the observed survival disparity in this high-risk group. These results suggest that the poorer prognosis of AM is unlikely to be solely attributable to delayed diagnosis but may instead reflect other underlying factors.

Melanoma in darker-skinned populations is generally associated with a poorer prognosis, often attributed to the higher prevalence of AM. However, the underlying causes of these disparities remain unclear.16,42 Thakker et al43 suggested that pigmented lesions at acral sites in darker-skinned individuals may initially appear subtle and blend with the surrounding skin. This diagnostic challenge may be compounded by socioeconomic barriers, including limited access to healthcare and health insurance coverage, which may contribute to delayed detection and presentation with more advanced disease compared with White populations. Additionally, previous studies have suggested that the poorer prognosis of AM may be attributable to its unique genomic and immunological features.24,44,45 Compared with NAM, AM exhibits distinct genomic profiles, including greater chromosomal structural variations and a lower mutational burden.24,46–48

Furthermore, while NAM frequently harbors BRAF mutations, AM is more likely to harbor KIT mutations or exhibit a triple wild-type genotype, potentially reducing the effectiveness of targeted therapies.49,50 The tumor immune microenvironment in AM is also more immunosuppressive, resulting in lower response rates to immunotherapy than observed in NAM.24,44 Growing evidence indicates that AM is biologically distinct from other subtypes of cutaneous malignant melanoma; however, no specific management guidelines for AM currently exist.51 The biological characteristics of AM underscore the importance of considering its genetic and molecular features when developing tailored therapeutic strategies, particularly for patients from darker-skinned populations.

Previous reports have also highlighted differences in melanoma outcomes among various racial and ethnic groups.52,53 Data from the SEER program of the National Cancer Institute revealed statistically significant disparities in 5-year OS rates: 69.7% for Hispanics, 58.2% for African Americans, 69.8% for American Indians, and 70.9% for Asian/Pacific Islanders, compared with 79.3% for White individuals.54 Consistent with these findings, our analysis demonstrated poorer 5-year OS among patients with AM from darker-skinned populations than among those from White populations. Notably, this disparity in prognosis is not confined to melanoma as a whole. Within the AM subgroup, darker-skinned populations consistently exhibited poorer survival outcomes than White populations. Similarly, among patients diagnosed with NAM, Taylor et al.55 reported a higher risk of disease-specific mortality in head and neck melanoma among Black, American Indian/Alaska Native, and Hispanic patients than among White patients. These findings suggest that the observed differences in prognosis may be influenced by environmental or genetic factors, highlighting the need for further investigation into the underlying mechanisms contributing to these disparities.

Chiu et al19 utilized whole-exome sequencing (WES) to compare the genomic profiles of melanomas in darker-skinned and White populations. The analysis revealed that White populations had a higher prevalence of UV-related mutational signatures (mutational signature 7), whereas Asian populations had higher expression of mutational signature 12. Common gene mutations, including BRAF, NRAS, and NF1, which are prevalent in White populations, are considerably less common in Asian populations.44,47,56–59 Chiu et al19 reported a BRAF mutation rate of <20% in Asian populations. Similarly, Sheen et al57 observed that the incidence of somatic mutations in the BRAF and NRAS genes among Taiwanese patients was 14.3% and 10.1%, respectively. Likewise, Sakaizawa et al59 reported mutation detection rates of 30.4% and 12.3% for BRAF and NRAS, respectively, among Japanese patients. In contrast, Borkowska et al56 found that BRAF mutations were present in approximately 50% of cutaneous melanomas in White populations, with NRAS mutations observed in approximately 20% of cases. These findings provide valuable insights into the distinct tumorigenic pathways underlying cutaneous melanoma across racial and ethnic groups, highlighting the molecular diversity of melanoma. Notably, these genomic findings were consistent with our clinical analysis, which demonstrated differences in survival outcomes across ethnic groups. This underscores the importance of recognizing that melanoma may behave differently across populations. Therefore, future research should prioritize the development of novel therapeutic strategies specifically tailored to improve outcomes among patients from darker-skinned populations.

Our study has several limitations. First, all included studies were observational, and a considerable proportion of the data was collected for administrative rather than research purposes. This may have introduced potential bias owing to inaccuracies in coding, diagnosis, and data entry. Second, substantial heterogeneity was observed across the included studies, potentially reflecting geographic variation, environmental influences, differences in treatment era, and variability in study design. Although statistical approaches, including the use of a random-effects model and subgroup analyses, were applied to address the potential sources of heterogeneity, residual heterogeneity may remain. We also considered meta-regression to further explore heterogeneity; however, this was not performed because important study-level covariates were inconsistently reported across the included studies, and the limited number of studies available for several candidate moderators could have produced unstable estimates. In addition, the terminology used for AM may have varied across older studies, particularly regarding the classification of subungual melanoma, which may have contributed to between-study heterogeneity. Third, the pooled estimates for 5-year OS were heavily influenced by large-scale US administrative datasets, such as the SEER database, because of their large sample sizes. Fourth, only five studies were included in the subgroup analyses, which likely reduced the statistical power and may have yielded unstable effect estimates. Finally, owing to the limited number of eligible studies in specific clinical subgroups and the marked disparity in sample sizes across the included cohorts, formal tests for publication bias may have lacked sufficient power to distinguish true bias from between-study heterogeneity, potentially leading to misleading interpretations.

In conclusion, AM is associated with markedly poorer 5-year survival than NAM, particularly in patients with stage II and III disease. These findings suggest that delayed diagnosis alone does not fully explain the observed disparity in survival. Patients from dark-skinned populations with AM may also experience poorer survival outcomes than those from White populations. Taken together, these findings highlight the importance of recognizing the distinct genetic, biological, and clinical characteristics of AM across different populations. Clinically, patients with stage II and III AM may warrant closer surveillance and careful consideration of systemic adjuvant treatment strategies. In addition, the distinct molecular features of AM support the need for biomarker-driven, subtype-specific therapeutic development, including dedicated clinical trials and tailored therapeutic strategies for high-risk populations.
