Facilitators and barriers to internal evaluation in an Iranian medical Sciences University: a qualitative exploration of faculty perspectives
Authors: Zahra Bastani nejad, Farnoosh Tajik, Zhila Fereidouni, Abolfazl Karimivaselabadi, Najimeh Beygi, Ali Taghinezhad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn response to increasing demands for accountability and quality assurance in higher education, internal evaluation has become a core mechanism for improving educational performance. Internal evaluation refers to a systematic self-assessment process through which faculty and staff examine educational, research, and administrative performance to identify strengths, weaknesses, and areas for improvement. Faculty members play a central role in implementing internal evaluation; however, their engagement is shaped by multiple facilitators and barriers. Evidence from resource-constrained contexts remains limited, particularly qualitative insights into faculty perceptions. This study qualitatively examines faculty members’ perceptions of the facilitators and barriers influencing internal evaluation.MethodsThis qualitative study used a conventional content analysis approach. Semi‑structured interviews were conducted with 16 faculty members at Fasa University of Medical Sciences using purposive sampling until data saturation was achieved. Data were collected between January and October 2025 and analyzed using conventional content analysis. Trustworthiness was ensured using Lincoln and Guba’s criteria.ResultsThree overarching categories emerged inductively from the data: (1) conceptualizations of internal evaluation, including its role as a systematic performance review, a reflective process for identifying strengths and weaknesses, and a cultural mechanism for promoting quality; (2) facilitators, encompassing individual factors (intrinsic motivation, self-efficacy, training), organizational factors (structural support, adequate infrastructure), and cultural factors (learning-oriented culture, collaboration); and (3) barriers, including individual challenges (resistance to change, workload), organizational constraints (limited resources, inefficient procedures), and cultural obstacles (negative attitudes, interdepartmental tensions).ConclusionInternal evaluation is shaped by intersecting individual, organizational, and cultural dynamics. Recognizing these influences can guide context-appropriate strategies; for example, shifting from a compliance-driven model to a developmental model that emphasizes workload adjustments, targeted capacity building, and transparent feedback loops to increase faculty engagement.Clinical trial numbernot applicableKeywords: Internal evaluation, Faculty perceptions, Facilitators, Barriers, Qualitative content analysis, Medical education, Iran

Background

Over recent decades, globalization, increasing competition in knowledge production, and growing demands for accountability have reshaped higher education systems worldwide [1, 2]. In this environment, internal quality assurance has become essential for enhancing institutional effectiveness [3], with internal evaluation serving as a key mechanism for assessing educational, research, and administrative performance [4].

Internal evaluation involves systematic self-assessment through which faculty, staff, and students examine current conditions, identify strengths and weaknesses, and recommend improvements [5, 6]. Beyond producing descriptive data, it can reinforce continuous improvement, strengthen organizational learning, and cultivate shared responsibility for quality [7]. In contexts marked by resource limitations and organizational pressures, internal evaluation supports evidence-based decision-making [8]. Because faculty members act as primary implementers and stakeholders, their perceptions, whether viewing evaluation as developmental or burdensome, play a decisive role in shaping engagement or resistance [9].

In Iran, national policies mandate internal evaluation as the basis for external review and accreditation [10, 11]. However, universities continue to face structural, cultural, and behavioural barriers that hinder effective implementation [12, 13]. Structural obstacles include weak information systems and limited resources [13]; culturally, resistance to change and judgment-oriented attitudes reduce participation [14]. Behaviorally, high teaching loads and low faculty involvement impede comprehensive evaluation [15]. Conversely, enabling factors such as visible managerial support [10, 16], participatory organizational cultures, access to reliable data and standardized tools, and practical training [10, 17] can build evaluation capacity. At the faculty level, intrinsic motivation, self-efficacy, and professional commitment help overcome organizational barriers, while fear of judgment and excessive duties increase resistance [17].

Despite contextual constraints, international research provides useful but limited insights for analyzing internal evaluation in Iran’s higher education system. Identified barriers include competing managerial demands and insufficient training [18]; Oczkowicz and Myszewski (2022) emphasized resource limitations and weak feedback loops [19]; and Kauppila et al. [20] identified key requirements for successful internal evaluation, including peer evaluation, evaluator training, and a collective process characterized by shared ownership [20]. However, these findings are not fully generalizable to Iran due to distinct cultural, structural, and resource constraints, multiple faculty roles, and institutional resistance [12].

Iranian studies remain largely quantitative or descriptive. Saravani et al. (2022) reported generally favorable conditions but did not probe faculty members’ deeper perceptions or the interaction of facilitators and barriers [21]. Nasrollahinia et al. (2019) identified facilitators, including improved activities and social participation, and barriers such as inadequate follow-up and limited resources; however, their quantitative focus restricted analysis of dynamic processes across individual, group, and organizational levels [22].

Several gaps persist. International studies centered on school-level supervision or European standards rarely address Iran’s cultural and economic context, including budgetary constraints and resistance to internal judgment [14]. Domestic research, reliant on national quantitative instruments, largely produces descriptive findings and overlooks mechanisms such as fear of peer judgment, intrinsic motivation, and workload-related role conflict, resulting in static models with limited applicability [23]. Furthermore, the scarcity of qualitative work constrains understanding of how facilitators and barriers interact across levels, hindering the development of context-specific interventions [10, 24].

Despite the widespread use of internal evaluation in higher education, qualitative evidence on how faculty members perceive the facilitators and barriers influencing its implementation remains limited, particularly in resource-constrained medical education settings. To address this gap, this qualitative study explores faculty members’ perspectives on the facilitators and barriers to internal evaluation.

Method

Study design

This study employed a qualitative design using conventional (inductive) content analysis to explore faculty members’ perceptions and experiences of internal evaluation.

Setting and participants

Participants were recruited from faculty members at Fasa University of Medical Sciences. Eligible participants included full-time and part-time faculty members involved in educational or evaluative activities. Purposive sampling was used to recruit individuals with direct experience of internal evaluation, and sampling continued until data saturation was achieved. In total, sixteen faculty members participated in this study, including nine women and seven men, with ages ranging from 29 to 58 years and academic experience ranging from 2 to 30 years. Participants were purposively selected to ensure variation in academic rank and departmental affiliation, including both clinical and basic science disciplines. The sample included early-career, mid-career, and senior faculty members, reflecting a range of professional perspectives on internal evaluation processes. Several participants also had experience in administrative or educational roles; however, detailed information regarding formal leadership positions and prior involvement in evaluation committees was not systematically collected. Participation was voluntary, and both verbal and written informed consent were obtained. Interviews were conducted in quiet private offices at times convenient for participants.

Research team and reflexivity

The research team consisted of faculty members with experience in qualitative inquiry and content analysis. They jointly designed the interview guide, collected data, and conducted the analysis. Credibility was enhanced through review of coding, categorization, and development of categories by three senior qualitative experts. Although the team was familiar with internal evaluation, analytic neutrality was maintained through regular discussions to ensure consistency and minimize bias. Reflexivity was incorporated throughout: team members examined how their professional roles, experiences, and assumptions might shape interpretation and shared these reflections during analytical meetings. This structured reflexive approach strengthened transparency, credibility, and overall trustworthiness of the analysis.

Given that members of the research team were faculty and familiar with internal evaluation processes, particular attention was paid to potential insider influences. Participants may have perceived interviewers as organizational insiders or evaluators, which could shape responses. To mitigate this, the interviewer emphasized independence from formal evaluation processes, assured confidentiality, and encouraged open, non-judgmental discussion. Reflexive discussions were conducted throughout data collection and analysis to critically examine how researchers’ positions might influence data interpretation.

Interview guide

The interview guide was developed by team members with expertise in educational evaluation and faculty development. Questions were informed by a review of the relevant literature and the researchers’ firsthand experience with internal evaluation. The guide incorporated broad, open-ended prompts, such as:

Brief follow-up questions were used to elicit additional detail, clarification, or specific examples. Interviews lasted approximately 30–45 min.

Data collection

Individual semi-structured interviews were conducted using an interview guide. With participants’ permission, interviews were audio-recorded, and field notes were taken to capture contextual and nonverbal cues. A brief demographic survey documented participants’ background characteristics. Interviews were conducted between January and October 2025. No formal pilot interviews were conducted; the interview guide was developed and reviewed by senior qualitative experts prior to data collection. To reduce social desirability bias, interviews were conducted in private settings, participants were assured of anonymity and confidentiality, and the interviewer emphasized that there were no “right” or “wrong” answers. Triangulation with field notes and member checking (participants reviewed summary interpretations) were used to confirm credibility. Regarding interview length (30–45 min): the semi-structured format allowed concentration on core topics and follow-up probing; together with iterative sampling and immediate analysis, we judged the interviews sufficient to reach data saturation.

Data saturation was reached at approximately the 13th interview, when no new codes, subcategories, or conceptual insights were identified. The remaining interviews served a confirmatory role, reinforcing the consistency and richness of the emerging categories and subcategories rather than generating substantially new insights.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using conventional qualitative content analysis. Interviews were transcribed verbatim and analyzed concurrently with data collection. The analysis followed a five-step process consisting of transcription, identification of meaning units, coding, categorization, and category development. Initially, transcripts were reviewed repeatedly to achieve immersion in the data. Meaning units relevant to participants’ experiences of internal evaluation were identified and assigned open codes. Coding was conducted inductively, with codes derived directly from participants’ narratives rather than from predefined theoretical constructs or analytical frameworks. Through constant comparison within and across transcripts, similar codes were grouped into subcategories and broader categories. These categories represented recurring patterns and meanings across participants’ accounts and formed the final analytical structure. Although some of the final categories resemble commonly used multi-level perspectives on organizational processes, they were not predetermined but emerged inductively through iterative analysis and abstraction of the data.

Interviews were analyzed immediately after completion, and emerging insights informed subsequent sampling and interviewing. To enhance trustworthiness, Lincoln and Guba’s criteria of credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability were applied. Credibility was strengthened through prolonged engagement with the data, member checking, and peer review by experienced qualitative researchers. Dependability and confirmability were supported through expert auditing, reflexive discussions, and maintenance of an audit trail. Transferability was enhanced through purposive maximum-variation sampling and the use of participant quotations. Triangulation of interview transcripts and field notes further strengthened the rigor of the analysis. Reporting followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist. Data management and analysis were facilitated using MaxQDA software.

Results

Sixteen faculty members participated in the study, including nine women and seven men. Participants were 29–58 years old, had 2–30 years of professional experience, and were drawn from both clinical and basic science departments. The sample included faculty with varied academic experience and evaluative responsibilities, allowing the study to capture a range of perspectives on internal evaluation. Participants varied in academic rank and department type; however, detailed information on leadership roles and prior involvement in internal evaluation committees was not systematically collected.

Analysis revealed three interrelated categories that were abstracted during the later stage of inductive coding and constant comparison, reflecting patterns across the data rather than any pre-existing framework: [1] Conceptualization of Internal Evaluation [2], Facilitators of Internal Evaluation, and [3] Barriers to Internal Evaluation. The findings are presented in these sections to reflect both the structure of participants’ accounts and the underlying interpretive patterns identified across interviews.

Conceptualization of internal evaluation

Participants conceptualized internal evaluation in three overlapping ways as described in Table 1. First, many described it as a systematic process for reviewing performance that produces actionable information for improvement. For example, one participant stated: “Internal evaluation means that the academic group reviews its own processes and performance so that strengths and weaknesses become clear and improvement plans can be made.” (Interview 2). Second, faculty used internal evaluation as a self-reflective process used to check readiness for external accreditation and to align teaching with objectives: “We examine ourselves before external accreditation to see what our strengths and weaknesses are and whether our teaching meets its objectives.” (Interview 4). Third, a smaller group emphasized its cultural dimension—internal evaluation as part of a learning-oriented organizational culture that fosters shared responsibility and continuous improvement: “Internal evaluation involves a sense of responsibility toward the teaching-learning environment and empowering learners, aiming for continuous improvement.” (Interview 11). These conceptions overlapped in practice: several participants described internal evaluation simultaneously as a technical procedure, a reflective practice, and a cultural norm that shapes behavior.

Facilitators and barriers to internal evaluation across individual, organizational, and cultural levels

Facilitators of internal evaluation

Three major categories of facilitators were identified: individual-level factors, organizational factors, and cultural factors.

Individual-level facilitators

Participants frequently highlighted intrinsic motivation, commitment to professional growth, and personal responsibility. Internal evaluation was seen as an opportunity to enhance professional capabilities and improve learner outcomes. One participant noted: “Internal evaluation is an organizational reflective process… a kind of organizational culture that enhances the real quality of education” (Participant 11). Another stated: “If I improve myself and my teaching capabilities, I can train better learners” (Participant 16). A sense of personal responsibility was also emphasized (Participants 3, 5).

Self-efficacy and skills were also facilitators. Participants mentioned that having the necessary skills and actively participating facilitated the evaluation process. For example: “A systematic process for assessing educational performance… helps us identify gaps” (Participant 5), and “Analytical skills are important for implementing evaluations” (Participant 7).

Educational workshops and awareness-building were further noted. One participant emphasized: “Holding workshops on internal evaluation at the beginning of each term and raising faculty awareness is useful” (Participant 1). Another added: “Training should be tailored to practical conditions” (Participant 8).

Organizational facilitators

Structural support, including resource allocation and structured committees, was recognized as a key facilitator. As one participant stated: “The presence of feedback mechanisms, indicator dashboards, and inter-unit collaboration facilitates the process. When the department head treats evaluation as an opportunity rather than a punishment, faculty become more willing to take part. For example, after we implemented regular feedback meetings, colleagues started to suggest practical changes in teaching schedules and resources.” (Participant 9). Another highlighted: “Allocating official hours for evaluation activities is important” (Participant 15).

Infrastructure provision, including standardized tools and continuous monitoring, was also important. “Software for documentation and a scheduled plan with clear timelines are necessary” (Participant 3), and “A system for collecting documents is needed” (Participant 7).

Cultural facilitators

A positive organizational learning culture played a significant role. Some participants described this issue in especially vivid terms, suggesting an important but less widely articulated concern. Participants emphasized that when evaluation is viewed as a process for growth rather than criticism, faculty engagement increases. “Organizational learning culture, presence of the department head as a learning leader, and regular meetings facilitate the process” (Participant 5). Another stated: “Evaluation means growth, not fault-finding; it is about addressing gaps” (Participant 16). Collaborative environments were also highlighted: “When colleagues see that results lead to improvement in conditions and facilities, motivation increases” (Participant 11).

Barriers to internal evaluation

Participants reported several barriers to internal evaluation at individual, organizational, and cultural levels.

Individual-level barriers

Participants reported fear of judgment, concerns about outcomes, and lack of readiness for participation. “Fear of criticism and lack of prior experience” (Participant 4) and “Fear of judgment” (Participant 6) were mentioned.

Workload constraints, including time pressure and high teaching load, were also cited. “The biggest barrier is lack of time and heavy theoretical and clinical teaching loads” (Participant 2), and “High teaching and clinical responsibilities” (Participant 11).

Organizational barriers

Participants reported limited financial and human resources and insufficient managerial support. “Limited personnel and time for implementing evaluation are noticeable” (Participant 1), and “We do not have dedicated resources for this work” (Participant 8).

Participants also described unclear or non-standard guidelines, insufficient tools, and inadequate training. “We have general guidelines, but they are very scattered” (Participant 3), and “There is no clear operational instruction” (Participant 10).

Cultural barriers

Participants reported negative attitudes toward evaluation and inter-departmental conflicts. “Negative perspective, previous bad experiences, and a sense of ineffectiveness” (Participant 16), and “Conflicts of interest between units” (Participant 9).

Discussion

The findings indicate that faculty members view internal evaluation as a multidimensional process that integrates structured performance review with reflective self-assessment. They emphasized its role in identifying strengths and weaknesses, supporting learning, and promoting quality when embedded within a shared organizational culture. Although Abdanipour et al. [10] described internal evaluation in Iranian medical universities as a “formal and mandatory process” [10], our results more strongly underscore its cultural and responsibility-oriented dimensions, reflecting contextual variations in participants’ engagement with and perceptions of internal evaluation processes [25]. This highlights the importance of moving beyond viewing evaluation as a procedural requirement toward understanding it as a socially embedded and culturally mediated practice.

Our findings suggest several potential mechanisms through which commonly reported facilitators and barriers may influence engagement with internal evaluation. For example, “fear of judgment” appeared to reflect concerns related to prior experiences, perceived hierarchies, and uncertainty regarding how evaluation outcomes would be used. Similarly, what appears as “resistance” may in fact reflect a rational response to systems perceived as symbolic, inconsistent, or lacking developmental follow-through. These findings suggest that engagement is not simply a function of individual motivation, but is deeply conditioned by trust, organizational memory, and perceived fairness. These mechanisms indicate that faculty engagement is shaped not only by individual dispositions but also by relational and structural conditions that influence how evaluation is perceived and enacted in practice.

Facilitators and barriers operated through interactions among individual, organizational, and cultural factors. At the individual level, intrinsic motivation, including professional interest and responsibility, supported engagement. These findings align with self-determination theory, which emphasizes the role of autonomy, competence, and relatedness in fostering motivation [26]. Self-efficacy also emerged as a central factor, consistent with meta-analytic evidence linking it to job performance and proactive behavior [27, 28]. Learning opportunities, successful experiences, and targeted training further enhance self-efficacy and, consequently, participation [29]. Overall, the findings are consistent with self-determination theory and self-efficacy research, suggesting that combining skill-building, opportunities for mastery, and autonomy-supportive environments can strengthen engagement [30, 31]. In contrast to European studies that identify formal training as a primary facilitator [32], participants in this study emphasized training workshops alongside motivation, self-efficacy, and organizational support, which may reflect the limited availability of structured practical training in Iranian institutions.

The findings of this study are broadly consistent with theoretical perspectives that emphasize motivation, perceived competence, and organizational context; however, they should primarily be understood as participants’ lived accounts of how internal evaluation is experienced in practice. Faculty members described concrete conditions shaping engagement—such as workload, prior experiences, perceived fairness, and the availability of support—rather than abstract psychological constructs. Accordingly, theories such as self-determination and self-efficacy are used here only as interpretive lenses to contextualize these accounts, not as frameworks that structured the data collection or analysis.

At the organizational level, structural support, including adequate resources, committees, and formal procedures, was viewed as essential for sustaining evaluation. Without it, evaluation becomes irregular and ineffective. This supports Alqudah et al. (2022), who emphasized cohesive infrastructures and committed middle management [33]. Cultural and technical infrastructures—standardized tools, continuous monitoring, and learning-oriented values—also shape whether evaluation is experienced as developmental rather than punitive. Prior research shows that learning cultures enhance structural capabilities and participation [34] and promote developmental attitudes [35]. Seyyedkalan likewise identified learning values, feedback, and cultural supports as foundations of quality culture [36]. However, infrastructure alone is insufficient; as Hashjin et al. (2025) found, software systems may remain underused when implementation processes and cultural acceptance are weak [11].

Barriers were shaped by interacting individual, organizational, and cultural factors. Participants’ accounts suggest that fear of judgment reflects concerns about potential professional consequences, including reputation and career progression. This indicates that internal evaluation may be perceived as a high-stakes process rather than a purely developmental activity. Distrust toward evaluation processes appeared to be associated with weak feedback loops and limited observable changes following previous evaluations. When results are not translated into visible improvements, evaluation may lose credibility and be perceived as symbolic rather than meaningful.

Variations across disciplinary contexts and academic ranks indicated that engagement with internal evaluation is shaped by contextual and hierarchical factors. Clinical faculty emphasized time constraints and workload pressures, whereas basic science faculty more often highlighted ambiguity in evaluation criteria.

Individually, resistance to change driven by fear of negative outcomes, limited preparedness, and heavy workloads was the most prominent deterrent. Competing responsibilities reduced participation and undermined evaluation quality. Earlier studies link such hindrance demands to stress and resistance [37], associate workload and role conflict with reduced engagement [38], and highlight fears of unfairness or loss of autonomy as inhibitors [39, 40]. Addressing these barriers requires clear objectives, targeted training, and workload management [41, 42].

At the organizational level, barriers included resource shortages, insufficient managerial support, and inefficient processes such as unclear guidelines and weak training. These findings reaffirm that individual motivation cannot compensate for inadequate structural support [43, 44], and that organizational design, resources, and supportive infrastructures determine evaluation capacity [45, 46].

Cultural barriers, including resistance to change, negative attitudes, and intergroup conflict, weakened trust, transparency, and shared learning. Prior research consistently identifies cultural resistance as a major obstacle: without an accepting evaluation culture, institutionalization fails [47]; negative attitudes and perceived unfairness fuel resistance [48]; and internal resistance constrains evaluation maturity [49]. Promoting learning-oriented values, transparency, equity, and participation, while addressing intergroup tensions, is therefore essential for advancing evaluation practices [47, 49].

The findings of this study should be interpreted as context‑specific to one Iranian medical university. While the study does not claim generalizability, the mechanisms identified—particularly the interplay of workload, organizational support, evaluation procedures, and local cultural norms—may hold analytical relevance for settings that share similar organizational conditions and constraints. Any broader relevance should therefore be understood as conditional on contextual similarity rather than assumed universality.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample was drawn from a single medical sciences university and should be understood as analytically transferable rather than broadly generalizable. Second, the sample size (n = 16) is typical for in-depth qualitative inquiry but limits the breadth of perspectives; future multi-site and mixed-methods studies would enhance transferability. Third, interview durations (30–45 min) provided focused depth; however, longer interviews may have generated richer accounts of participants’ experiences. Finally, although we used reflexive procedures and member checking, researcher familiarity with institutional evaluation could influence interpretation — we mitigated this via audit trails and peer debriefing.

Implications

The findings suggest that internal evaluation may be strengthened when it is perceived as a developmental rather than a purely compliance-oriented process. Participants highlighted the importance of organizational support, manageable workloads, and transparent feedback mechanisms in facilitating engagement with evaluation activities. Given the single-site nature of this study, these implications should be interpreted cautiously and considered as context-specific observations rather than broadly applicable recommendations. Future research should examine these issues across multiple institutions and settings to further explore the transferability of the identified facilitators and barriers.

Conclusion

This study provides a contextually grounded understanding of internal evaluation in one medical university. The findings indicate that internal evaluation was shaped by the interaction of individual motivation, organizational support, and cultural acceptance. While faculty members viewed evaluation as a structured and reflective process for improving quality, its effectiveness was constrained by resource limitations, procedural weaknesses, and cultural resistance. These findings may be informative for institutions with similar structural and cultural conditions, but they should be interpreted cautiously and not treated as broadly generalizable.
