Initial negative imaging in GATOR1-associated genetic epilepsy does not preclude the existence of a focal, resectable epileptogenic zone: illustrative cases
Authors: Yunzhu Ruan, Eleanor Hill, Zahra Bahmanpour, Braeden Badner, Benoit Caldairou, Andrea Bernasconi, Neda Bernasconi, Sylvain Baillet, Myriam Srour, David Dufresne, Roy W. R. Dudley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDFamilial focal epilepsy with variable foci (FFEVF) is a genetic epilepsy disorder in which affected family members experience different focal seizures. Focal cortical dysplasia (FCD) is a frequent cause of drug-resistant epilepsy in the pediatric population. Patients with focal epilepsy are less frequently referred for presurgical evaluation when FCD is not identified on MRI or when they have genetic epilepsy. The authors describe the successful surgical management of 3 drug-resistant FFEVF patients who initially had either negative or inconclusive MRI studies.OBSERVATIONSAll 3 patients carried the same germline pathogenic variant in NPRL3, a GATOR1 complex gene. In patient 1, an ill-defined signal abnormality on MRI raised the suspicion for FCD and suggested that similar occult lesions might underlie the epilepsy of her relatives, whose scan results were initially reported as negative. Each patient was thus referred for comprehensive presurgical evaluation. Through the use of advanced neuroimaging, epileptogenic zones were identified in all 3 patients, allowing for targeted resection. At the last follow-up, all had remained seizure free for more than 5 years.LESSONSWhen a genetic alteration is associated with FCD, such patients warrant imaging reexamination and exhaustive presurgical evaluation with advanced neuroimaging, as eventual lesion detection and subsequent resection can lead to long-term seizure freedom. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE26127

Familial focal epilepsy with variable foci (FFEVF) is an autosomal dominant neurological disorder caused by pathogenic variants in genes encoding the GATOR1 complex, including DEPDC5, NPRL2, and NPRL3.1,2 FFEVF is characterized by focal seizures originating from different cortical regions in affected family members. A subset of FFEVF patients present with focal cortical dysplasia (FCD) type II as the epileptogenic substrate.1,2 FCD is characterized by disorganization of the normal hexalaminar structure of the cerebral cortex, with associated cellular anomalies. This malformation of cortical development is a major cause of drug-resistant focal epilepsy in children and is classified as FCD type I (abnormal laminar organization of the cortex), type II (dysmorphic neurons [type IIa] or dysmorphic neurons and balloon cells [type IIb]), and type III (FCD found in association with other pathological entities, e.g., hippocampal sclerosis or glioneuronal brain tumors).3,4 Currently, almost all FCD type I and around half of FCD type II cases are initially unrecognized based on routine diagnostic imaging studies.5 Even when FCD type II is detected on MRI, the borders of the lesion usually remain invisible or very poorly defined (i.e., extend beyond what is seen on MRI).5

When an MRI examination is suggestive of FCD, focal epilepsy patients are often referred for presurgical evaluation to delineate the epileptogenic zone (EZ) in the hope of allowing for focal resection. However, in MRI-negative and MRI-inconclusive cases, focal resection is less often considered due to the challenge of precisely localizing the FCD-containing EZ well enough to remove it completely. Indeed, it is now well established that incomplete FCD resection leads to seizure recurrence after surgery. Similarly, genetic epilepsy is often considered a contraindication to surgery due to the notion that all brain areas may potentially be epileptogenic.

Here we present 3 closely related patients, 2 sisters and their first cousin, with drug-resistant FFEVF for whom surgical candidacy was considered on the finding of the NPRL3 pathogenic variant, despite negative and inconclusive MRI scans. Knowing that NPRL3 can be associated with FCD, an exhaustive presurgical evaluation was nevertheless undertaken, such that a focal EZ was localized and curative resective surgery performed.

Illustrative Cases

Three young patients from a French-Canadian family had nocturnal seizures starting in early childhood and were initially diagnosed with sleep-related hypermotor epilepsy, also known as autosomal dominant nocturnal frontal lobe epilepsy (ADNFLE). Later, these diagnoses were revised to FFEVF when a genotype-phenotype correlation between NPRL3 and FFVEF was established. While the semiology was different, they all had very frequent seizures requiring multiple (four to seven) antiseizure medications (ASMs). All had various degrees of intellectual disability (ID) (Table 1). On genetic testing, they were all found to have a germline pathogenic variant in GATOR1 complex gene NPRL3 (NM_001077350.3: c.1439del; p.Gln480Argfs*3). The medical record for each patient was reviewed for age, preoperative seizure type and frequency, imaging records, neurodevelopment, and last follow-up.

Patient 1

A 6-month-old girl presented with hypermotor and asymmetric tonic seizures, which were often accompanied by a fearful facial expression, with a frequency of more than 10 seizures per night.

Imaging

At the age of 10 months, her initial 1.5T MRI study was deemed to be inconclusive with questionable left anteromesial frontal subcortical T2 signal hypointensity; a possible bifrontal FCD was suggested, as the contralateral mesiofrontal region appeared to show abnormal gyration, although the subcortical hypermyelination is restricted to the left. Of note, 3T MRI was not available at the referring hospital when this patient was first evaluated (around 2010); the same applies to her sister and cousin (below). Her EEG showed left frontal and rarely right frontal interictal epileptiform discharges (IEDs). A subsequent MRI examination was performed at the age of 2 years, which also suggested possible bifrontal FCD. At the age of 5 years, initial fluorodeoxyglucose–positron emission tomography (FDG-PET) was performed, which demonstrated clear hypometabolism in the left anteromedial frontal lobe; with MRI-PET co-registration, the hypometabolism was found to co-localize with left frontal 3D FLAIR signal abnormalities (Fig. 1). Meanwhile, she was also diagnosed with global developmental delay and moderate ID. Seven different ASMs were tried, and all failed to control her seizures. Because of the suspected bifrontal epilepsy on EEG and an inconclusive MRI study, as well as the ADNFLE syndromic diagnosis, further presurgical evaluation was not considered initially, and eventually she was considered for vagus nerve stimulator insertion. However, when the association between pathogenic variant in NPRL3 and FFEVF became more established in the literature, the patient was diagnosed with FFEVF and referred for presurgical evaluation at the age of 12 years.

Presurgical Evaluation

Additional 3T MRI, video-EEG-telemetry (vEEGt), speech-language pathology (SLP), and neuropsychological evaluations were requested. With 3D FLAIR on 3T MRI better delineating the left frontal signal abnormalities in the same region as the FDG-PET hypometabolism (Fig. 1A–C), and video-EEG showing predominantly left anterior frontal IEDs and only left-sided seizures, the patient was recommended for surgery (Table 1). Of note, it was suspected that her language was bilateral or right-sided because she was left-handed and could speak during her seizures. However, she was not cooperative enough for a language fMRI study. The risks of postoperative language disturbance were discussed with the parents and were accepted.

Surgery and Outcome

She underwent a left frontal craniotomy for resection (Fig. 1D and E) of the MRI and FDG-PET abnormalities in the left superior frontal gyrus, middle frontal gyrus, and anterior cingulate and had no postoperative deficits. Her pathology showed that she has FCD type IIa. At the time of thise writing, the patient has been seizure free for more than 5 years and completely off all ASMs for > 4.5 years.

Patient 2

An 8-month-old girl, the older sister of patient 1, presented with a combination of asymmetric tonic, left hemiclonic, and bilateral tonic-clonic seizures with a frequency of one to three times per week.

Imaging

At the age of 9 months, her initial 1.5T MRI findings were negative, likely due to suboptimal sequence planning and insufficient suspicion to specifically look for a subtle lesion. The EEG showed abnormal electrical activity in the right frontal lobe, but because she was initially diagnosed with ADNFLE, the MRI results were thought to be negative, and due to the misconception that genetic epilepsy is not amenable to focal resection, surgery was not considered and no further investigations were performed at that time (this was before her sister, patient 1, had surgery). The findings of subsequent MRI studies performed at the ages of 2 and 3 years were also reported as negative.

Four different ASMs were tried, and all failed to control her seizures. Throughout the first few years of schooling, she exhibited lower than average performance and was diagnosed with a mild learning disorder. Her seizures became more difficult to control despite multiple ASM trials (Table 1).

As the association between the pathogenic NPRL3 variant and FCD type II became clearer over time, and informed by her younger sister’s lesion, her initial 1.5T MRI was then reevaluated with higher suspicion for an FCD, and a lesion was suspected in the right middle frontal gyrus (Fig. 2). Presurgical Evaluation

Thus, at 14 years of age, she was referred for presurgical evaluation with 3T MRI, repeat FDG-PET, vEEGt, SLP, and neuropsychological evaluation. On 3T MRI, a transmantle sign was seen in the white matter (WM) below what appeared to be right frontal subcortical advanced myelination and subtle FLAIR signal abnormalities (Fig. 2A and B), and her new FDG-PET study showed a concordant, superimposable region of hypometabolism (Fig. 2C). Her vEEGt showed right midfrontal IEDs and seizures.

Surgery and Outcome

Thus, the patient then underwent a focal right frontal resection (Fig. 2D and E) of the MRI signal abnormalities and surrounding, concordant FDG-PET hypometabolism within the right middle frontal gyrus, and her pathology confirmed FCD type IIa. At her most recent follow-up, she had been seizure free for more than 5 years and entirely off all ASMs for almost 4.5 years.

Patient 3

A 2.5-year-old girl presented with right hemidystonic seizures and asymmetric tonic seizures with a frequency of one to seven times per week. At the age of 2.5 years, her initial 1.5T MRI findings were negative. Her EEG showed IEDs in the left centro-parietal region. Over the years, she developed a mild learning disorder. A subsequent MRI study was performed at the age of 12 years and again the results were reported as negative. At the age of 16 years, initial FDG-PET was performed, the results of which were also reported as negative. Four different ASMs were tried, and all failed to control her seizures (Table 1).

Because her first two MRI study and initial FDG-PET examination results were negative, and the misconception that genetic epilepsy is nonsurgical (this was before her two cousins received further workup and surgery), the patient was not referred for presurgical evaluation initially. However, once the NPRL3–FCD type II association became better established (around the same time her two cousins had undergone successful surgeries with the finding of FCD), the patient was reevaluated with increased suspicion of FCD.

Presurgical Evaluation

The patient was referred for presurgical evaluation at the age of 16 years. Additional 3T MRI, FDG-PET (Fig. 3), magnetoencephalography (MEG), vEEGt, SLP, and neuropsychological evaluations were requested. Voxel-based postprocessing (VBPP) of the 3T MR images was performed in the following manner. To model blurring of the gray matter (GM)–WM junction, the absolute gradient of gray-level intensities was calculated. To model cortical thickening, the number of consecutive GM voxels in each possible direction was calculated for each voxel. To model the hyperintense signal, the absolute difference between the intensity of a given voxel and the intensity at the boundary between GM and WM was calculated. Only after this VBPP (Fig. 3) was a small FCD suspected within the cingulate sulcus at the junction of the left middle posterior cingulate and deep posterior supplementary motor area (SMA). On retrospective review, a subtle area of FDG-PET hypometabolism co-localized to the VBPP finding of a cortical-subcortical blurring at the bottom of the sulcus. With MEG, C3/P3 IEDs were source-localized (Fig. 3) to a very similar area (i.e., the left posterior SMA). Surgery and Outcome

As such, the patient underwent focal resection (Fig. 3H and I) of the MRI and FDG-PET findings, and her pathology report confirmed FCD type IIa. She has been seizure free for almost 5 years since the surgery and has been off all ASMs for almost 5 years.

Search for a Somatic “Second Hit”

Since FCD type II in the context of germline NPRL3 variants is thought to result from an additional somatic pathogenic variant,6 we investigated this possibility by sequencing NPRL3 together with other mTOR pathway genes in DNA extracted from resected abnormal brain tissue from all 3 patients. Targeted enrichment was performed using SureSelect XT HS2 (Agilent), custom-designed to capture all coding exons of NPRL3 and other mTOR pathway genes, including MTOR, DEPDC5, NPRL2, TSC1, TSC2, AKT3, AKT2, AKT1, PIK3CA, PIK3R2, PTEN, CCND2, RHEB, IRS1, RPS6, TBC1D7, and TIPRL. Deep sequencing (approximately 1500 times mean coverage) was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq6000 S Prime version 1.5 platform using paired-end 150-bp reads. Despite this approach, no somatic variants consistent with a second hit were identified.

Discussion

Observations

About one-third of focal epilepsy patients are refractory to medical therapy,7 and resective surgery can be curative when the EZ is well localized and fully removed. Despite the evidence that surgery provides better long-term seizure control and improved quality of life compared to ASMs,8,9 epilepsy surgery remains underutilized due to frequent nonreferral of refractory epilepsy patients.8,10,11 This is likely due to the fact that MRI-negative epilepsy is often associated with unfavorable surgical results. A major contributor to underreferral is the absence of a clear lesion on MRI, coupled with misconceptions that genetic epilepsy is inherently diffuse and therefore not amenable to focal treatment.7–9,12

The underreferral of MRI-negative patients can be detrimental to long-term well-being and neurocognitive outcomes, particularly in children. Children with refractory epilepsy often have an early seizure onset, which can lead to neurodevelopmental delays and learning disorders.13 A prospective cohort study reported that refractory epilepsy was associated with an 11.4-point lower IQ, with the effect most severe in infancy and decreasing with age.14 In epileptic encephalopathy, cognitive development often slows or regresses due to the seizures themselves rather than the underlying etiology.15 In our study, 2 patients had seizure onset by 8 months of age, all had cognitive developmental problems, and the patient with earliest onset, greatest lesion extent, and most frequent seizures developed epileptic encephalopathy and moderate ID. Underreferral and late referral for presurgical evaluation likely contributed to these adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes.

Our 3 patients presented here were close relatives carrying the same germline NPRL3 pathogenic variant, which is causative for FCD.1,16 The pedigree demonstrated incomplete penetrance, a well-recognized mode of inheritance for NPRL3 and other GATOR1-related epilepsies.17,18 Sequencing of coding exons of NPRL3 and other mTOR pathway genes in DNA from resected FCD type II tissue did not reveal a somatic second-hit variant (SNV or indel). The presence of a below detection threshold–level variant, structural rearrangement, or noncoding/regulatory variant cannot be excluded.

Two patients were initially MRI-negative, and one had inconclusive MRI findings. In addition, the clinical significance of the NPRL3 pathogenic variant was initially underappreciated, leading to an erroneous syndromic diagnosis; all 3 patients were initially misdiagnosed with ADNFLE. These cases highlight the importance of genetic evaluation in guiding treatment decisions in MRI-negative focal genetic epilepsy. Favorable outcomes of surgery for complete resection of FCD are well established.19–21 In cases 2 and 3, the familial NPRL3 variant strongly suggested an occult FCD, but concordant presurgical imaging and electrophysiology remained essential before contemplating surgery.

Localizing the EZ and delineating its borders in poorly defined MRI cases remains challenging. The HARNESS-MRI (Harmonized Neuroimaging of Epilepsy Structural Sequences) protocol and functional modalities (e.g., FDG-PET, SPECT, MEG, EEG-fMRI, and/or VBPP of 3T or 7T MRI) can reveal subtle lesions or provide converging evidence for focality.22–27 Proposed by the Neuroimaging Task Force, the HARNESS-MRI protocol can maximize MRI’s diagnostic potential, even at 1.5T, when optimal parameters are used.25 However, we were not using this protocol when these 3 patients underwent their presurgical evaluations. In patients 1 and 2, 3T MR images co-registered with FDG-PET scans were sufficient to explain their electroclinical features and, together with the NPRL3 variant, avoided additional testing and further delays. For patient 3, the EZ was not detected on MRI/FDG-PET co-registration. However, VBPP and MEG localized a subtle abnormality in the left middle posterior cingulate/deep SMA region, later confirmed retrospectively on MRI and FDG-PET.

Moreover, subspecialty training in epilepsy imaging also improves lesion detection, as neurologists and neurosurgeons can integrate raw high-resolution images with semiology and neurophysiology findings, beyond neuroradiology reports.28 For patient 2, a T2-weighted 1.5T MRI study at 9 months was initially found to be negative, but retrospective review guided by suspicion of FCD revealed her right frontal FCD. Indeed, every effort should be made to scour and scrutinize any brain MRI study performed in the context of focal epilepsy, as only 7.7% of focal epilepsy cases after resection fail to yield diagnostic histopathology; 9 times out of 10 a lesion is present.4

Our patients all achieved complete seizure freedom and ASM cessation for more than 5 years after resective surgery, underlying (mayhe this should be “underlining” not “underlying”?) the importance of lesion detection on high-quality MRI in any surgical candidate. Thus, exhaustive presurgical evaluation is critical in MRI-negative cases, particularly when a pathogenic GATOR1/mTOR variant is present. Hu et al.18 described 3 NPRL3 patients with the NPRL3 pathogenic variant from two families (one MRI-negative) who underwent limited presurgical workup; only 1 underwent epilepsy surgery, which failed within 2 months. These results contrast with ours and highlight the importance of comprehensive evaluation with advanced neuroimaging and electrophysiology.

While GATOR1-associated genetic epilepsy is a relatively newly recognized clinical entity, a few reports of surgery in this context have been published (Table 2).1,2,6,18,29–38 While most were short case series (1–8 patients), Wang et al. reported on 27 patients and found that 94.4% (17/18) of their DEPDC5 patients, 100% (4/4) of their NPRL2 patients, and 80% (4/5) of their NPRL3 patients who underwent surgery achieved seizure freedom with a mean follow-up of 2 years.36 In total, 92.6% (25/27) of their GATOR1-associated epilepsy patients achieved seizure freedom. Although the length of follow-up was much shorter than what we presented here, these results align with ours, highlighting the potential favorable surgical outcome of GATOR1-associated genetic epilepsy patients. However, for the other 36 cases previously presented in the literature, the seizure freedom rate was only 50%, in keeping with the majority of the literature on FCD.39,40 This again highlights the necessity of an exhaustive presurgical evaluation often utilizing advanced neuroimaging and electrophysiological testing to localize not only the lesion but also the borders of the EZ. Lessons

The overall take-home message from this report is that initial negative MRI findings in GATOR1-associated epilepsy should not preclude the existence of a focal resectable EZ and should not dissuade the treating team from pursuing further presurgical evaluation investigations. All focal epilepsy cases, including patients considered as “MRI-negative,” warrant exhaustive presurgical evaluation, including genetic testing. Certainly, a family history of focal epilepsy, particularly FFEVF, should prompt early genetic testing in the course of the disease. If FCD-related variants (e.g., GATOR1/mTOR genes) are found, occult FCD should be suspected, and prior MRI scans should be reexamined.41 Finally, next-generation sequencing should be included in the evaluation of all focal epilepsies of unclear etiology, especially FFEVF, which is often due to GATOR1 variants.38

Our series demonstrates that patients with FFEVF due to GATOR1 genetic variants can harbor surgically amenable FCD lesions even when initial MRI findings are reported as negative, and that a comprehensive neuroimaging/electrophysiology evaluation can lead to curative surgery with excellent long-term outcomes.
