Optimal-Point Variance Reduction For Bayesian Optimization With Regret Guarantee
Shion Takeno

Abstract
This paper studies a one-step lookahead Bayesian optimization (BO) method and its theoretical guarantee. Although the empirical effectiveness of one-step lookahead BO methods, such as entropy search, has been studied extensively, they often rely on computationally intractable approximations, and their regret guarantees remain underdeveloped. Thus, this paper proposes a one-step lookahead BO method called optimal-point variance reduction (OVR), which requires only posterior sampling and Monte Carlo approximations. We obtain a uniform error bound over an input domain for the Monte Carlo estimation in OVR. Furthermore, we show that the regularized OVR, with the slight modification to promote exploration, achieves a vanishing Bayesian expected simple regret upper bound. Finally, we demonstrate the effectiveness of OVR through numerical experiments.

Introduction

Bayesian optimization (BO)
(
32
)
is a framework for the global optimization of expensive-to-evaluate black-box functions.
BO aims to optimize an objective function with fewer function evaluations.
For this purpose, BO sequentially selects an input to evaluate by maximizing acquisition functions (AFs) based on a surrogate model, typically a Gaussian process (GP)
(
28
)
.
BO has been applied to various domains, including hyperparameter tuning in machine learning and experimental design in chemistry
(
33
;
18
)
.

Many AFs for BO evaluate the local utility of candidate inputs using metrics such as improvements or quantities inspired by multi-armed bandit algorithms.
For instance, probability of improvement (PI) and expected improvement (EI)
(
20
;
21
;
31
)
quantify the potential gain relative to the current best observed value.
Other approaches, such as upper confidence bound (UCB)
(
34
)
and Thompson sampling (TS)
(
29
;
38
)
, are inspired by bandit algorithms and are justified by sublinear cumulative regret upper bounds achieved by theoretically balancing exploration and exploitation.

Concurrently, one-step lookahead policies have been extensively studied for their ability to account for global utility across the entire input domain.
Empirical evaluations indicate that one-step lookahead methods, such as entropy search (ES)
(
50
;
8
;
10
)
and knowledge gradient (KG)
(
4
)
, often exhibit superior performance.
However, despite their practical efficacy, the theoretical characterization of one-step lookahead BO remains underdeveloped.
In addition, one-step lookahead policies often suffer from computationally intractable approximations.
Consequently, a gap exists between the practical efficacy of one-step lookahead strategies and their theoretical justification in terms of approximation accuracy and optimization performance.

This paper proposes and analyzes optimal-point variance reduction (OVR) and regularized OVR (ROVR), which are one-step lookahead BO methods that aim to minimize the posterior variance or standard deviation (STD) at the optimal input.
We summarize our contributions below:

1.

We propose an easy-to-implement OVR and its variant, ROVR, which minimize the posterior variance or STD at the optimal input.
OVR and ROVR require only posterior sampling and Monte Carlo (MC) approximations and do not require heuristic approximations as in predictive ES (PES)
(
10
)
, max-value ES (MES)
(
52
)
, and joint ES (JES)
(
11
;
45
)
.

2.

We provide a uniform MC estimation error bound over an input domain in Theorems
4.1
and
4.3
, which indicate
O(\log M/\sqrt{M})
convergence even for continuous input domains, where
M
is the number of MC samples.

3.

We show that ROVR achieves the vanishing expected Bayesian simple regret (BSR) upper bound comparable to most existing results
(
29
;
40
;
38
;
41
;
39
)
in Theorem
4.4
.

Finally, we demonstrate the effectiveness of OVR and ROVR via numerical experiments.

1.1
Related work

We analyze the BO algorithm in the Bayesian setting, where the objective function follows a GP
(
29
;
34
;
15
)
, although the frequentist setting has also been studied extensively
(
34
;
2
;
13
)
.
Furthermore, although several studies analyze the noiseless setting
(
14
;
3
)
, we focus on the noisy setting, where the observation is contaminated by noise.
Analyzing the one-step lookahead BO methods in the frequentist or noiseless setting is an intriguing direction for future work.

KG
(
4
;
55
)
is a one-step lookahead BO method that aims to maximize the posterior mean in the next step.
However, computing the AF involves computationally expensive nested optimization.
Therefore, in practice, complicated and heuristic approximations are required.
Furthermore, the analysis of KG is limited to the asymptotic convergence.
Therefore, finite-sample analysis for KG, which is important in the BO regime with scarce data, remains an open problem.

ES
(
50
;
8
)
maximizes the mutual information between the queried output and the target variable, such as the optimal input, which can be seen as a one-step lookahead minimization of the entropy of the target variable.
There are several variants of ES, such as PES
(
10
)
, which evaluates the mutual information with respect to the optimal input, MES
(
52
)
, which evaluates the mutual information with respect to the maximum value of the objective function, and JES
(
11
;
45
)
, which evaluates the mutual information with respect to both the optimal input and the maximum value.
However, all of these methods require (i) posterior sampling approximation, (ii) MC approximation, and (iii) heuristic approximation for the conditional distribution given the optimal input and/or the maximum value.
In particular, the validity of the conditional distribution approximation is unknown.
Furthermore, their theoretical guarantee remains an open problem.

2
Preliminary

Bayesian optimization.

We consider an optimization for a black-box function
f:{\cal X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
:

\boldsymbol{x}^{*}=\argmax_{\boldsymbol{x}\in{\cal X}}f(\boldsymbol{x}),

where
{\cal X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an input domain and
d
is a dimension.
We assume that observations
y=f(\boldsymbol{x})+\varepsilon
are contaminated by noise
\varepsilon
, and the function evaluation cost is high.
Thus, our goal is to optimize
f
using as few observations as possible.
For this goal, BO sequentially selects an input point
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}
based on AFs computed by the GP model trained by the currently available dataset and observes
y_{t}=f(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})+\varepsilon_{t}
, where
t
is the iteration.
Therefore, we can obtain the training data
{\cal D}_{t-1}=\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},y_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}})\}_{i=1}^{t-1}
until the beginning of
t
-th iteration.

Regularity assumptions.

We assume the following common regularity assumption
(
34
;
29
)
:

Assumption 2.1
.

The function
f
follows a zero-mean GP with a predefined kernel
k:{\cal X}\times{\cal X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, that is,
f\sim{\cal G}{\cal P}(0,k)
, and the
i
-th observation
y_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}
is contaminated by i.i.d. Gaussian noise
\varepsilon_{i}\sim{\cal N}(0,\sigma^{2})
with a positive variance
\sigma^{2}>0
as
y_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}=f(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})+\varepsilon_{i}
.
In addition, the kernel function satisfies
k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\leq 1
for all
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in{\cal X}
.

Furthermore, for continuous
{\cal X}
, we assume the following smoothness condition:

Assumption 2.2
.

Let
{\cal X}\subset[0,r]^{d}
be a compact set, where
r>0
.
Assume that the kernel
k
satisfies the following condition on the derivatives of a sample path
f
:

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\max_{j\in[d]}\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}\in{\cal X}}\left|\frac{\partial f(\boldsymbol{u})}{\partial u_{j}}\Big|_{\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{x}}\right|>L\right)\leq ad\exp\left(-\frac{L^{2}}{b^{2}}\right),

where
a,b>0
are absolute constants.

This assumption is commonly used
(
34
;
16
;
40
;
38
;
15
)
and holds at least for
the squared exponential (SE) kernel
k_{\rm SE}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=\exp\left(-\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}/(2\ell^{2})\right)
and
Matérn-
\nu
kernels
k_{\rm Mat}=\frac{2^{1-\nu}}{\Gamma(\nu)}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\nu}\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\|_{2}}{\ell}\right)^{\nu}J_{\nu}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\nu}\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\|_{2}}{\ell}\right)
with
\nu>2
, where
\ell,\nu>0
are the lengthscale and smoothness parameter, respectively, and
\Gamma(\cdot)
and
J_{\nu}
are Gamma and modified Bessel functions, respectively
(
5
;
34
, Theorem 5 in)
.
Moreover, for the MC estimation error analysis, we assume the following smoothness condition:

Assumption 2.3
.

Let
{\cal X}\subset[0,r]^{d}
be a compact set, where
r>0
.
There exists a constant
L_{k}>0
such that

\displaystyle\forall\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v},\in{\cal X},\max_{\boldsymbol{x}\in{\cal X}}|k(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{x})-k(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{x})|\leq L_{k}\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1},

that is,
k(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x})
is Lipschitz continuous.

A similar assumption has been used in the GP bandit literature
(
2
;
46
)
.
For example,
L_{k}=O(1/\ell)
for SE and Matérn kernels due to
k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\leq 1
.

Gaussian process model.

From the assumptions on
f
and noise
\{\varepsilon_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
in Assumption
2.1
, the posterior distribution
p(f\mid{\cal D}_{t-1})
is a GP again
(
28
)
, whose mean and variance can be obtained as follows:

\displaystyle\mu_{t-1}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{k}_{t-1}(\boldsymbol{x})^{\top}\bigl(\boldsymbol{K}_{t-1}+\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{I}_{t-1}\bigr)^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}_{t-1},

(1)

\displaystyle k_{t-1}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})-\boldsymbol{k}_{t-1}(\boldsymbol{x})^{\top}\bigl(\boldsymbol{K}_{t-1}+\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{I}_{t-1}\bigr)^{-1}\boldsymbol{k}_{t-1}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}),

where
\boldsymbol{k}_{t-1}(\boldsymbol{x})\coloneqq\bigl(k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{1}),\dots,k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{t-1})\bigr)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{t-1}
is the kernel vector,
\boldsymbol{K}_{t-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{(t-1)\times(t-1)}
is the kernel matrix whose
(i,j)
-element is
k(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j})
,
\boldsymbol{I}_{t-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{(t-1)\times(t-1)}
is the identity matrix, and
\boldsymbol{y}_{t-1}\coloneqq(y_{1},\dots,y_{t-1})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{t-1}
.
For the sake of notational simplicity, let
\sigma_{t-1}^{2}(\boldsymbol{x})=k_{t-1}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x})
and the posterior variance when
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}=\boldsymbol{x}
be
\sigma_{t}^{2}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\mid\boldsymbol{x})\coloneqq\sigma_{t-1}^{2}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})-\frac{k_{t-1}^{2}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})}{\sigma_{t-1}^{2}(\boldsymbol{x})+\sigma^{2}}
.
Note that the posterior variance calculation does not require
\boldsymbol{y}_{t}
.

Maximum information gain.

For regret analysis, we will use the quantity called maximum information gain (MIG)
(
34
;
47
)
:

Definition 2.4

(Maximum information gain)
.

Let
f\sim{\cal G}{\cal P}(0,k)
over
{\cal X}\subset[0,r]^{d}
.
Let
A=\{\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{T}
, where
\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\in{\cal X}
for all
i\in[T]
.
Let
\boldsymbol{f}_{A}=\bigl(f(\boldsymbol{a}_{i})\bigr)_{i=1}^{T}
,
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{A}=\bigl(\varepsilon_{i}\bigr)_{i=1}^{T}
, where
\varepsilon_{i}\sim{\cal N}(0,\sigma^{2})
for all
i\in[T]
, and
\boldsymbol{y}_{A}=\boldsymbol{f}_{A}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
.
Then, MIG
\gamma_{T}
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\gamma_{T}\coloneqq\sup_{A}I(\boldsymbol{y}_{A};\boldsymbol{f}_{A})\text{ such that }|A|=T\text{ and }\boldsymbol{a}_{i}\in{\cal X}\text{ for all }i\in[T],

(2)

where
I
is the Shannon mutual information.

For frequently used kernels, MIG is known to be sublinear
(
34
;
47
)
, for example,

\gamma_{T}=O\bigl(d\log T\bigr)
for the linear kernels,

\gamma_{T}=O\bigl((\log T)^{d+1}\bigr)
for the SE kernels, and

\gamma_{T}=O\bigl(T^{\frac{d}{2\nu+d}}(\log T)^{\frac{2\nu}{2\nu+d}}\bigr)
for the Matérn-
\nu
kernels.

Performance measure.

We evaluate the theoretical performance of BO methods by the expected Bayesian simple regret
(
29
;
30
;
16
;
25
;
40
;
38
;
39
)
defined as

\displaystyle{\rm BSR}_{T}=\mathbb{E}\left[f(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})-f(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{T})\right],

(3)

where we define
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{T}=\argmax_{\boldsymbol{x}\in{\cal X}}\mu_{T}(\boldsymbol{x})
and the expectation is taken with all the randomness, that is,
f,\{\varepsilon_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, and the randomness of the algorithm.
Note that, although the expected regret quantifies the average performance of the algorithm, the expected regret upper bound immediately implies a high-probability upper bound via Markov’s inequality
(
29
, Sec. 3.1 in)
.
Our goal is to show the convergence rate of the BSR.

3
Proposed methods: OVR and regularized OVR

This section provides the definition of our OVR and ROVR, which mainly aim to minimize
\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{t-1}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})\mid{\cal D}_{T}\right]
.
Although our formulation accounts for the posterior STD, we use the term
variance reduction
since it is more common.
On the other hand, even if we consider minimizing
\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{t-1}^{2}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})\mid{\cal D}_{T}\right]
, our theoretical analyses hold in almost the same way.
Algorithm
1
shows the pseudo code of OVR and ROVR.

We motivate minimization of
\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{t-1}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})\mid{\cal D}_{T}\right]
from the perspective of regret analysis.
From the analysis by
29
(see Appendix
C
for details), we can obtain, for any
{\cal D}_{T}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})-f(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{T})\mid{\cal D}_{T}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[f(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})-\mu_{T}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})\mid{\cal D}_{T}]\leq\beta_{T}\mathbb{E}[\sigma_{T}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})\mid{\cal D}_{T}]+O\left(\frac{1}{T}\right),

where
\beta_{t}=O\left(\log(|{\cal X}|t)\right)
if
|{\cal X}|<\infty
or
\beta_{t}=O\left(d\log(t)\right)
if Assumption
2.2
holds.
Note that the expectation is taken with respect to
f
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
since we assume
f
follows a GP and
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
is defined via
f
.
Therefore, if we can decrease
\mathbb{E}[\sigma_{T}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})\mid{\cal D}_{T}]
rapidly with respect to
T
, we can obtain a tighter BSR bound.
However, optimizing
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{T}\}
for minimizing
\mathbb{E}[\sigma_{T}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})\mid{\cal D}_{T}]
is computationally intractable.
Hence, we develop the AF to minimize
\mathbb{E}[\sigma_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\mid\boldsymbol{x})\mid{\cal D}_{t-1}]
in a one-step lookahead manner and its regularized variant, which achieves the vanishing BSR upper bound.

3.1
Optimal-point variance reduction

From the discussion above, we define the AF of OVR, which is minimized over
{\cal X}
, as follows:

\displaystyle\alpha_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{y_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{*}}\left[\sigma_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\mid\boldsymbol{x})\bigm|{\cal D}_{t-1}\cup\{(\boldsymbol{x},y_{\boldsymbol{x}})\}\right]\bigm|{\cal D}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{x}\right],

which directly evaluates the target quantity in the next step, averaged over
p(y_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal D}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{x})
.
In contrast to most existing one-step lookahead BO methods, which rely on heuristic approximations discussed in Section
1.1
, our AF can be transformed analytically as follows:

\displaystyle\alpha_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{y_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{*}}\left[\sigma_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\mid\boldsymbol{x})\bigm|{\cal D}_{t-1}\cup\{(\boldsymbol{x},y_{\boldsymbol{x}})\}\right]\bigm|{\cal D}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{x}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{y_{\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{x}^{*}}\left[\sigma_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\mid\boldsymbol{x})\bigm|{\cal D}_{t-1},\boldsymbol{x}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{*}}\left[\sigma_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\mid\boldsymbol{x})\bigm|{\cal D}_{t-1}\right],

where we use the tower property of the expectation and the fact that
\sigma_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\mid\boldsymbol{x})
does not depend on
y_{\boldsymbol{x}}
.
Therefore, although the expectation over
p(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\mid{\cal D}_{t-1})
cannot be computed analytically as with ES
(
50
;
8
)
and PES
(
10
)
, we do not require any other approximation.

We apply MC estimation to approximate the expectation over
p(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\mid{\cal D}_{t-1})
similarly to ES-based methods
(
10
;
52
;
11
)
.
Therefore, in practice, we use the following approximate AF:

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M_{t}}\sum_{m=1}^{M_{t}}\sigma_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{t,m}\mid\boldsymbol{x}),

where
M_{t}\in\mathbb{N}
is a user-specified parameter and
\{\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{t,m}\}_{m=1}^{M_{t}}
is i.i.d. samples from the posterior
p(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\mid{\cal D}_{t-1})
.
Since sampling from
p(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\mid{\cal D}_{t-1})
is computationally intractable, particularly when
{\cal X}
is continuous, we employ the approximate posterior sampling method as in prior works
(
10
;
52
;
11
)
.
Specifically, we employ the method of
53
;
54
, which combines pathwise conditioning and the random Fourier feature approximation
(
27
)
.
Thus, generating (approximate) posterior sample paths and optimizing those, we can obtain
\{\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{t,m}\}_{m=1}^{M_{t}}
.

3.2
Regularized optimal-point variance reduction

This section describes ROVR, which is a generalization of OVR that incorporates regularization with respect to the posterior STD.
OVR is a one-step optimal algorithm with respect to minimizing
\mathbb{E}[\sigma_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})\mid{\cal D}_{t}]
.
However, the posterior variance and STD are not submodular in general, as discussed in
(
43
)
.
Therefore, we cannot obtain multi-step optimality with respect to
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{T}\}
based on the submodular property.
Thus, we derive the theoretical guarantee by a slight modification to promote exploration.

Specifically, we consider the following variant of OVR:

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M_{t}}\sum_{m=1}^{M_{t}}\sigma_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{t,m}\mid\boldsymbol{x})-c_{t}\sigma_{t-1}(\boldsymbol{x}),

where
c_{t}\geq 0
is the regularization parameter, whose theoretical choice will be shown in Section
4.2
.
Obviously, setting
c_{t}=0
recovers the usual OVR.
If
c_{t}>0
, the AF is smaller if
\sigma_{t-1}(\boldsymbol{x})
is larger.
Therefore, since we minimize the AF, this modification promotes selecting an input with high uncertainty.

3.3
Similarity to existing BO methods

Similarity to TS.

When
M_{t}=1
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
, OVR is the same as TS since OVR must select
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}=\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{t,1}
.
Therefore, if
M_{t}=1
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
, OVR obviously achieves the expected BSR and Bayesian cumulative regret bounds as in
(
30
;
38
;
42
)
.
For an arbitrary
M_{t}
, we will show the vanishing BSR bound in Section
4.2
.
On the other hand, TS often suffers from over-exploration
(
32
;
38
)
.
OVR alleviates this limitation of TS by using
M_{t}
MC samples in practice.

Similarity to JES.

Moreover, OVR is also similar to JES
(
11
)
.
Roughly speaking, although OVR minimizes
\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{*}}\left[\sigma_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\mid\boldsymbol{x})\bigm|{\cal D}_{t-1}\right]
, JES maximizes
\alpha_{\rm JES}(\boldsymbol{x})\approx\log\left(\sigma_{t}^{2}(\boldsymbol{x})+\sigma^{2}\right)-\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{*}}\left[\log\left(\sigma_{t}^{2}\bigl(\boldsymbol{x}\mid(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},f^{*}),f(\boldsymbol{x})\leq f^{*}\bigr)+\sigma^{2}\right)\bigm|{\cal D}_{t-1}\right]
, where the logarithm comes from the definition of entropy and
\sigma_{t}^{2}\bigl(\boldsymbol{x}\mid(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},f^{*}),f(\boldsymbol{x})\leq f^{*}\bigr)
is the approximate variance given
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
,
f^{*}=f(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})
, and the truncation condition
f(\boldsymbol{x})\leq f^{*}
.
From this difference, OVR has three advantages over JES.
First, the AF value of JES approaches infinity
\max_{\boldsymbol{x}\in{\cal X}}\alpha_{\rm JES}(\boldsymbol{x})\rightarrow\infty
as
\sigma^{2}\rightarrow 0
, since the argument of the logarithm approaches
0
.
Thus, when
\sigma^{2}
is sufficiently small, JES shows behavior similar to TS, leading to over-exploration.
Second, the validity of the heuristic variance approximation for
\sigma_{t}^{2}\bigl(\boldsymbol{x}\mid(\boldsymbol{x}^{*},f^{*}),f(\boldsymbol{x})\leq f^{*}\bigr)
is unknown.
Third, the regret guarantee for entropy-based BO methods is still an open problem.
In contrast, OVR alleviates over-exploration by relying on the posterior STD and does not require a heuristic conditional-variance approximation.
In addition, we show that ROVR achieves the vanishing BSR upper bound.

Analysis

This section presents analyses of the MC estimation error and the BSR upper bound.
In this section, we ignore the error of the posterior sampling approximation, as with existing analyses
(

;

;

;
39
)
.
This approximation error may be handled, e.g., by the analyses in

;

)
.

4.1
Analysis of Monte Carlo estimation

The MC estimation error for fixed
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\cal X}
can be obtained via a classical concentration inequality.
Since
\sigma(\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\mid\boldsymbol{x})\in[0,1]
is a bounded random variable, we can apply Hoeffding’s inequality
(
49
, Theorem 2.2.6 in)
shown in Theorem
A.1
in the appendix as follows:

\displaystyle\forall M_{t}\in\mathbb{N},\forall\boldsymbol{x}\in{\cal X},\Pr\left(|\alpha_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})-\hat{\alpha}_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})|\leq\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{2M_{t}}}\biggm|{\cal D}_{t-1}\right)\geq 1-\delta,

for any
{\cal D}_{t-1}
,
\delta\in(0,1)
, and
c_{t}\geq 0
.
Therefore, we can obtain the
O(1/\sqrt{M_{t}})
bound, which also achieves logarithmic dependence on the probability
\delta
.
However, in practice, we need to obtain an error bound that holds uniformly for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\cal X}
since we compute AF values for many
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\cal X}
to optimize the AF.
For both discrete and continuous domains, we show such uniform error bounds.

If the input domain
{\cal X}
is finite, we can obtain the following theorem by the union bound:

Theorem 4.1

(MC estimation error bound for discrete domains)
.

Pick
\delta\in(0,1)
.
Assume
{\cal X}
is finite.
Then, for any
{\cal D}_{t-1}
and
c_{t}\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\forall M_{t}\in\mathbb{N},\Pr\left(\max_{\boldsymbol{x}\in{\cal X}}|\alpha_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})-\hat{\alpha}_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})|\leq\sqrt{\frac{\log(|{\cal X}|/\delta)}{2M_{t}}}\biggm|{\cal D}_{t-1}\right)\geq 1-\delta.

See Appendix
B.1
for the proof.
Therefore, to guarantee the
\epsilon
-accuracy, that is,
\max_{\boldsymbol{x}\in{\cal X}}|\alpha(\boldsymbol{x})-\hat{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x})|\leq\epsilon
with some
\epsilon>0
, we need
M_{t}=\Omega\left(1/\epsilon^{2}\right)
.

If the input domain
{\cal X}
is continuous, we cannot apply the union bound so that the error bounds hold for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\cal X}
.
Thus, to obtain the uniform error bound, we need some smoothness condition.
To derive such a condition, we utilize the following lemma:

Lemma 4.2

(Theorem E.4 in
(
19
)
)
.

Let
k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}):\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be linear, SE, or Matérn-
\nu
kernel and
k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x})\leq 1
for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\cal X}
.
Moreover, assume that a noise variance
\sigma^{2}
is positive.
Then, for any
t\geq 1
and
{\cal D}_{t}
, the posterior standard deviation
\sigma_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})
satisfies that

\displaystyle\forall\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\ |\sigma_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})-\sigma_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})|\leq L_{\sigma}\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\|_{1},

where
L_{\sigma}
is a positive constant given by

\displaystyle L_{\sigma}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\text{if $k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})$ is the linear kernel},\\
\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\ell}&\text{if $k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})$ is the SE kernel},\\
\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\ell}\sqrt{\frac{\nu}{\nu-1}}&\text{if $k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})$ is the Mat\'{e}rn kernel with $\nu>1$}.\end{array}\right.

By showing the Lipschitz continuity of the AF (Lemma
B.1
) from Lemmas
4.2
and
A.2
(Proposition 1 in
(
46
)
), we can obtain the following result for continuous domains:

Theorem 4.3

(MC estimation error bound for continuous domains)
.

Pick
\delta\in(0,1)
.
Suppose that Assumption
2.3
and the same premise as in Lemma
4.2
hold.
Then, for any
{\cal D}_{t-1}
,
M_{t}\in\mathbb{N}
, and
c_{t}\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\max_{\boldsymbol{x}\in{\cal X}}|\alpha_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})-\hat{\alpha}_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})|\leq\sqrt{\frac{d\log(2dr\sqrt{M_{t}}L_{\alpha_{t}})-\log\delta}{2M_{t}}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{M_{t}}}\biggm|{\cal D}_{t-1}\right)\geq 1-\delta,

(4)

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{\boldsymbol{x}\in{\cal X}}|\alpha_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})-\hat{\alpha}_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})|\biggm|{\cal D}_{t-1}\right]\leq\sqrt{\frac{d\log(2dr\sqrt{M_{t}}L_{\alpha_{t}})+1}{2M_{t}}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{M_{t}}},

(5)

where
L_{\alpha_{t}}=\left(\frac{L_{k}}{\sigma}\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{t}}{\sigma}\right)+\frac{L_{\sigma}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)
.

See Appendix
B.2
for the proof.
Thus, also for continuous input domains, we obtained the uniform error bound, which implies that we need
M_{t}=\Omega\left(\log(t/\epsilon)/\epsilon^{2}\right)
for the
\epsilon
-accuracy with
\epsilon\in(0,1)
.

4.2
Regret analysis

Our ROVR achieves the following BSR bound:

Theorem 4.4

(BSR bound)
.

Suppose that, in addition to Assumption
2.1
,
|{\cal X}|<\infty
or Assumption
2.2
holds.
Then, if Algorithm
1
runs with non-increasing sequence
\{c_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
c_{t}>0
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
, the following bound holds:

\displaystyle{\rm BSR}_{T}\leq\sqrt{\frac{C_{1}\beta_{T}\gamma_{T}}{T}}+O\left(\frac{\beta_{T}^{1/2}}{T}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{Tc_{T}}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\log M_{t}}{\sqrt{M_{t}}c_{t}}\right),

where
C_{1}=2/\log\left(1+\sigma^{-2}\right)
and
\beta_{T}=2\log(|{\cal X}|T/\sqrt{2\pi})
if
|{\cal X}|<\infty
or
\beta_{T}=2\log(|2drTL|^{d}T/\sqrt{2\pi})
if Assumption
2.2
holds, where
L=\max\{L_{\sigma},b\left(\sqrt{\log(2ad)}+\sqrt{\pi}/2\right)\}
with
L_{\sigma}
defined as in Lemma
4.2
.

See Appendix
C
for the detailed proof, though we show a proof sketch below.

Proof sketch.

For simplicity, we consider the case
|{\cal X}|<\infty
here.
By the standard arguments from
29
, we can see that

\displaystyle{\rm BSR}_{T}

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{T}^{1/2}\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{T}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})\right]+\frac{1}{T},

where
\beta_{T}=O(\log(|{\cal X}|t))
.
Then, from
\mathbb{E}[\sigma_{1}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})]\geq\dots\geq\mathbb{E}[\sigma_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})]\dots\geq\mathbb{E}[\sigma_{T}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})]
, which also used in
(
24
)
, we have

\displaystyle{\rm BSR}_{T}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\beta_{T}^{1/2}}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{t-1}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})\right]+\frac{1}{T}.

From the definition of ROVR’s AF, for all
t\in[T]
, we can obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{t-1}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{t-1}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})\right]+\frac{1}{c_{t}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{t-1}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})\right]\right)+O\left(\frac{\log M_{t}}{\sqrt{M_{t}}c_{t}}\right),

where the third term in the right hand side implies the MC estimation error bounds shown in Theorems
4.1
and
4.3
.
Finally, by bounding
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{c_{t}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{t-1}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})\right]\right)
as a telescoping series and applying Lemma 5.4 in
(
34
)
to
\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sigma_{t-1}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})\right]
, we can obtain the desired result.

Convergence of our BSR bound.

If
M_{t}
and
c_{t}
are sufficiently large, the dominant term in the BSR upper bound is
\sqrt{C_{1}\beta_{T}\gamma_{T}/T}
and converges to
0
.
For example, if we set
M_{t}=\Theta(t)
and
c_{t}=\Omega(1/\log t)
, we see that
1/(Tc_{T})=O(\log T/T)
and
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\log M_{t}}{\sqrt{M_{t}}c_{t}}=O\left(\frac{(\log T)^{2}}{\sqrt{T}}\right)
.
As discussed in Section
2
, the MIG for commonly used kernel functions satisfies, e.g.,
\gamma_{T}=O\left((\log T)^{d+1}\right)
for SE kernels and
\gamma_{T}=O\left(T^{\frac{d}{2\nu+d}}\right)
for Matérn kernels.
Hence, in this case, the dominant term is
\sqrt{C_{1}\beta_{T}\gamma_{T}/T}
(except for the case of SE kernels and
d=1
).
Furthermore,
\sqrt{C_{1}\beta_{T}\gamma_{T}/T}
converges to
0
since
\gamma_{T}\log T=o(T)
.
Consequently, if we choose sufficiently large
M_{t}
and
c_{t}
, our BSR upper bound must converge to
0
.

Comparison with existing bounds.

Several studies show a tighter
O(\sqrt{T})
(high-probability) Bayesian cumulative regret (BCR) bound
(
15
;
42
)
.
However, our BSR bound has the same rate as the most existing expected BSR bounds in
(
29
;
16
;
40
;
38
;
41
;
39
)
.
Furthermore,
15
;
42
stated that showing
O(\sqrt{T})
expected BCR bounds is an open problem.
Thus, our ROVR achieves the upper bounds comparable to the best-known ones for the expected BSR.

Showing upper bounds on cumulative regret for ROVR is not straightforward.
Given that ROVR aims to minimize the posterior STD in the next step, it is difficult to obtain an instantaneous regret bound, unlike bandit-based BO algorithms, such as UCB and TS.
We believe that this is a common difficulty for the one-step lookahead AFs.
Thus, developing the one-step lookahead BO with sublinear cumulative regret bounds is an intriguing direction for future work.

6
Conclusion

We proposed one-step lookahead BO methods called OVR and ROVR.
We show the uniform MC estimation error bound and that ROVR achieves the vanishing BSR upper bound.
Finally, we demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed methods via the numerical experiments.

Limitations and future work.

As discussed in Section
4.2
, the cumulative regret analysis remains an open problem.
Furthermore, determining whether we can tighten the BSR upper bound is important.
In addition, we left the analysis incorporating the approximation error for posterior sampling for future work.
On the other hand, we expect that OVR and ROVR are extendable to more complex optimization problems, as in other one-step lookahead BO methods, such as multi-fidelity BO
(
36
;
37
;
22
)
, constrained BO
(
9
;
44
)
, and multi-objective BO
(
26
;
35
;
12
)
.
Extensions to these settings, maintaining the theoretical guarantee, seem a promising direction.

Appendix D
Additional experiments

Figure
3
shows the synthetic function experiment results, where the experimental settings are the same as those of Figure
1
except for the number of MC samples.
We observed that, regardless of the number of MC samples, MES often gets stuck in a local optimum.
We conjecture that this phenomenon occurs because we do not employ heuristic modification of generated maximum values
\tilde{f}_{t}^{*}=\max_{\boldsymbol{x}\in{\cal X}}\tilde{f}_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})
, where
\tilde{f}_{t}\sim p(f\mid{\cal D}_{t})
.
That is, the original implementation
[
52
]
uses
\max\{\tilde{f}_{t}^{*},y_{1}+c,\dots,y_{t}+c\}
with some jitter
c>0
instead of
\tilde{f}_{t}^{*}
(Line 75 in
https://github.com/zi-w/Max-value-Entropy-Search/blob/master/utils/sampleMaximumValues.m
).
This modification often alleviates over-exploitation, since a smaller
\tilde{f}_{t}^{*}
facilitates exploitation.
Furthermore, we do not employ such a heuristic modification for JES either.
As a result, JES often degraded as the number of MC samples increased.
When the number of MC samples increases, the probability that a smaller
\tilde{f}_{t}^{*}
exists is large.
Thus, we conjecture that JES gets stuck in local optima since such effects from a smaller
\tilde{f}_{t}^{*}
increase.
On the other hand, our OVR and ROVR show robust performance with respect to the number of MC samples, with performance becoming more stable as the number of MC samples increases.
