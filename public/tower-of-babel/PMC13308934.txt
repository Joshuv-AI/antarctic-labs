A Stomach-Positioned Splinting Tube Technique for Maintaining a Straightened Gastric Axis During Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography in Severe Esophageal Hiatal Hernia
Authors: Tsuyoshi Ueda, Koichiro Miyagawa, Shinji Oe, Risa Yoshitake, Reira Fukumoto, Kenta Kajitani, Daiki Uchihara, Nobuhiko Shinohara, Yuichi Honma, Michihiko Shibata, Masaru Harada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSevere esophageal hiatal hernia can hinder endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) by causing gastric looping and unstable duodenoscope advancement. We report a 76-year-old woman with gallbladder cancer, hilar biliary obstruction, and severe mixed-type hiatal hernia. Initial ERCP failed because recurrent looping prevented passage beyond the pylorus. During repeat ERCP, a hydrophilic splinting tube was not anchored in the duodenum but was positioned in the prepyloric stomach to maintain gastric axis reduction, defined as straightening of the looped intrathoracic stomach. This prevented recurrent looping, enabled stable duodenoscope advancement, and allowed transpapillary metal-stent placement, potentially reducing mucosal injury or perforation risk by avoiding duodenal bulb manipulation.

INTRODUCTION

Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is the standard first-line approach for biliary drainage in biliary obstruction. However, severe esophageal hiatal hernia can distort the gastric axis through intrathoracic gastric migration, causing recurrent looping and unstable duodenoscope advancement. When conventional ERCP fails, percutaneous transhepatic biliary drainage or endoscopic ultrasound-guided biliary drainage may be considered, although their feasibility may be limited in anatomically complex cases. Rescue techniques for difficult duodenoscope insertion include guidewire-assisted scope exchange, large-balloon anchor techniques, and splinting tube-assisted methods.1,2 We report a patient with obstructive jaundice caused by gallbladder cancer and a severe mixed-type esophageal hiatal hernia in whom a modified splinting tube technique enabled successful duodenoscope insertion and biliary drainage. Unlike prior reports, the tube tip was maintained within the stomach rather than anchored in the duodenal bulb.

CASE REPORT

A 76-year-old woman was admitted with abdominal pain and obstructive jaundice caused by gallbladder cancer. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography revealed hilar biliary obstruction with separation of the right anterior and posterior sectoral bile ducts, whereas dilatation of the left intrahepatic bile ducts was minimal (Figure 1). Although total bilirubin was only mildly elevated, inflammatory markers were markedly increased, consistent with acute cholangitis. Computed tomography also showed a severe mixed-type esophageal hiatal hernia, with most of the stomach displaced into the thoracic cavity. The transverse colon was interposed near the stomach and liver (Figure 1).

Contrast-enhanced abdominal computed tomography images. (A) The right anterior and posterior sectoral bile ducts were dilated and separated (arrowheads) due to hilar involvement by the gallbladder cancer (arrows). (B) The fundus (*1), proximal gastric body (*2), and transverse colon (*3) have migrated into the thoracic cavity due to the giant hiatal hernia.

During the first ERCP session, a colonoscope was advanced into the duodenum with marked gastric loop formation (Figure 2). The scope was stretched and temporarily straightened, and a guidewire was advanced into the second portion of the duodenum and left in place. However, duodenoscope insertion over the guidewire failed because the guidewire did not provide sufficient support to maintain the reduced configuration, and recurrent looping prevented passage beyond the pylorus.

Fluoroscopic images during the initial failed endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography session. A colonoscope was advanced into the duodenal bulb with marked gastric loop formation.

A second ERCP was therefore performed using a splinting tube-assisted technique. The same colonoscope was introduced together with a splinting tube (ST-CB1; Olympus Medical Systems, Tokyo, Japan) (Figure 3), and the assembly was gently stretched to eliminate gastric looping. The balloon of the splinting tube was not inflated during the procedure. The splinting tube was initially left in the duodenal bulb, which maintained a straightened axis (Figure 4). Because the tube tip was abutting the duodenal mucosa, the tube was withdrawn to the prepyloric stomach, while maintenance of the straightened gastric axis was preserved. The duodenoscope was then advanced through the indwelling tube into the duodenum, and stable frontal visualization of the major papilla was achieved (Figure 4). Endoscopic sphincterotomy was performed, followed by placement of self-expandable metal stents into the right anterior and posterior sectoral bile ducts using a stent-in-stent technique. Frontal visualization of the papilla required approximately 20–30 minutes, including splinting tube placement and scope exchange. No procedure-related adverse events occurred, and the patient was discharged on hospital day 23 to start systemic chemotherapy.

A splinting tube, which is a hydrophilic coated silicone tube with a single balloon (ST-CB 1; Olympus Medical Systems, Tokyo, Japan; outer diameter, 16.2 mm; inner diameter, 13.8 mm; working length, 690 mm; total length, 770 mm).

DISCUSSION

Esophageal hiatal hernias are classified into 4 types, ranging from sliding (type I) to paraesophageal and mixed forms (types II–IV).3,4 In severe cases, intrathoracic migration of the stomach can markedly distort the gastric axis and cause recurrent gastric looping during ERCP, hindering duodenoscope advancement even in experienced hands. In such cases, “gastric axis reduction,” defined as straightening of the looped intrathoracic stomach, is required. In our patient, temporary gastric axis reduction was achieved using a forward-viewing endoscope and guidewire placement, but the reduced configuration could not be maintained, demonstrating that the straightened configuration can be easily lost in severe esophageal hiatal hernia.

Alternative drainage strategies were considered but not favored because of the anatomy. Percutaneous transhepatic biliary drainage would likely have required separate drainage of the right anterior and posterior sectoral bile ducts, necessitating at least 2 percutaneous punctures. Endoscopic ultrasound-guided biliary drainage was also considered high risk because most of the stomach was displaced into the thoracic cavity, raising the possibility of puncture from an intrathoracic stomach position. In that setting, respiratory motion and altered organ positioning could hinder stable fistula formation and increase the risks of stent migration and air leakage.5,6 Therefore, transpapillary drainage was prioritized.

Several rescue techniques for difficult duodenoscope insertion have been reported. Guidewire-assisted scope exchange may help, but in severe hiatal hernia the guidewire may not provide enough stiffness to preserve gastric axis reduction during duodenoscope advancement. Large-balloon anchor techniques create traction within the distal duodenum and can facilitate scope passage.1,7,8 Overtube-assisted ERCP using a short gastric overtube has also been reported, but a short overtube may be insufficient in a markedly elongated, intrathoracic stomach.9 A recent retrospective study reported 100% technical success for splinting tube-assisted duodenoscope insertion, with a median procedure time of 44.5 minutes.10 Balloon-assisted splinting tube techniques have likewise been described, with the tube anchored in the duodenal bulb.2

Our approach differed from the conventional duodenal bulb anchoring maneuver. Although we initially placed the tube in the bulb, the tip pressed against the duodenal mucosa and would have required duodenoscope manipulation within a confined space, potentially increasing mucosal injury or perforation risk. We therefore withdrew the tip to the prepyloric antrum just proximal to the pyloric ring. In this position, the splinting tube acted mainly as a structural support to maintain gastric axis reduction rather than as a true anchoring device. This prevented recurrent looping and allowed duodenoscope manipulation within the wider gastric lumen before entry into the duodenum.

The longer working length of the splinting tube may have been particularly helpful in this patient because it could span the elongated, intrathoracic stomach and provide more reliable support than a short overtube. Despite these advantages, this technique requires caution. Tube manipulation may cause mucosal injury or perforation, and patients with large hiatal hernias may have increased aspiration risk during prolonged endoscopic manipulation. Careful monitoring, avoidance of excessive force, and appropriate airway management are essential. Stomach-positioned splinting tube placement can be a practical rescue option for ERCP in severe mixed-type esophageal hiatal hernia when conventional insertion fails. Further experience is needed to clarify its broader applicability and safety.
