Clinical application of niacin-induced skin flush response in adolescents with psychiatric disorders and its correlation with clinical blood test indicators
Authors: Ning Liu, Jinfeng Wang, Xiaorong Duan, Xueyang Han, Le Han, Qian Zhu, Mingzhu Su, Yuanyuan Ding, Tao Yang, Sha He, Jie Jiang, Laiqi Yang, Xue Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the possible clinical application of niacin-induced skin flush response for diagnosing psychiatric disorders in adolescents and its potential correlation with blood test -indicators.MethodsThis is a cross-sectional study that included 51 adolescents with psychiatric disorders and 18 healthy controls (control group) matched for age and gender. Demographic and clinical data and blood test results were collected. The response to niacin-induced skin flush was tested in all participants using an integrated detection platform for skin testing with niacin developed by the Chunling Wan research team at Shanghai Jiao Tong University. The differences in niacin responses between the disease and control groups were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. The correlation between niacin responses and clinical indicators was analyzed using Spearman’s correlation analysis.ResultsCompared to the control group, the disease group exhibited lower sensitivity and weaker response intensity to niacin-induced skin flush, particularly for depression and bipolar disorder. The niacin responses showed potential as an auxiliary tool for diagnosing psychiatric disorders in adolescents. The area under the curve values of 0.891, 0.838, and 0.784 clearly distinguished between depression, bipolar disorder, and behavioral and emotional disorders, respectively. The niacin response in adolescents with psychiatric disorders was associated with thyroid hormone levels (T3, p = 0.030; free T3, p = 0.023), uric acid (p = 0.002) levels, red blood cell count (p = 0.056), and standard deviation in red blood cell distribution width (p = 0.023).ConclusionsThe niacin-induced skin flush response is significantly abnormal in adolescents with psychiatric disorders and can aid in clinical diagnosis. It also significantly correlates with thyroid function, antioxidant capacity, and red blood cell morphology and count.

Introduction

The continuous rise in mental health issues and related stress in recent times is due to the rapid progress in the socio-economic development. Mental issues can further cause severe psychiatric disorders to develop. Psychiatric disorders are defined as abnormalities in the structure and function of the brain influenced by a combination of biological, psychological, and environmental factors, resulting in abnormal cognitive, emotional, behavioral, and volitional activities [1]. According to the 2019 Global Burden of Disease Study, psychiatric disorders account for approximately 4.9% of the global disease burden [2], becoming a significant public health and societal concern in turn impacting the socio-economic development.

Adolescence is a particularly vulnerable period for the development of psychiatric disorders, as this stage is characterized by profound neurobiological, hormonal, metabolic, and psychosocial changes [3]. During adolescence, emotional regulation systems and neuroendocrine pathways remain under active maturation, increasing susceptibility to emotional and behavioral dysregulation [4]. In 2020, a systematic review including data from 43 studies showed that the prevalence of psychiatric disorders in adolescents increased from 25% to 31% [5]. It is significantly responsible for non-suicidal self-injury behaviors among adolescents [6]. Anxiety, depression, emotional and behavioral problems, and attention deficit hyperactivity are the common psychiatric disorders in adolescents [7]. Studies have shown that mental disorders with onset in adolescence are associated with more severe adult outcomes, including higher rates of symptom recurrence and poor prognosis [8]. Adolescent mental health issues not only impact current academic performance, social functioning, and physical and psychological development but may also persist into adulthood, exerting long-term adverse effects on adult life [9]. Therefore, early identification of adolescent mental disorders and implementation of targeted preventive interventions are of critical importance.

Despite increasing recognition of adolescent mental health problems, the diagnosis of psychiatric disorders still largely relies on subjective symptom evaluation and lacks objective biomarkers [10]. In recent years, niacin-induced skin flush response (NSFR) has attracted growing attention as a potential biological endophenotype in psychiatric research [11–13]. A niacin response is caused when niacin enters the epidermis of the skin, binds to hydroxy carboxylic acid receptors on the Langerhans cells or keratinocytes, and recruits and activates phospholipase A2 (PLA2). The enzyme dissociates cell membrane phospholipids, releasing arachidonic acid (AA), which is then catalyzed by cyclooxygenase into prostaglandins to promote capillary dilation and causing skin flush [14–16]. Studies have shown that the niacin response in patients with depression, bipolar disorder [17], and schizophrenia [18–20] is weaker compared to healthy individuals [21, 22], indicating a blunted niacin response. As a non-invasive test, the niacin response could offer a potential auxiliary diagnostic value in the study of psychiatric disorders [23]. However, existing studies on NSFR have focused predominantly on adult populations, while evidence in adolescents remains limited [24].

Given that adolescence represents a unique developmental stage with marked endocrine and neurobiological instability [25–27]. Beyond localized vascular changes, abnormal NSFR is increasingly considered to reflect broader disturbances involving membrane phospholipid metabolism, inflammatory signaling, oxidative stress, and AA-prostaglandin pathways [28]. These biological systems are particularly dynamic during adolescence, a period characterized by rapid brain maturation and extensive neural remodeling. Therefore, metabolic and hormonal alterations during adolescence may potentially influence the manifestation of NSFR in psychiatric disorders.

Among these biological factors, thyroid hormone alterations may be particularly relevant to NSFR abnormalities in adolescents. In addition to marked increases in gonadal steroid hormones, adolescence is also accompanied by dynamic alterations in the hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid (HPT) axis, hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, and stress-related neuroendocrine systems [4]. Thyroid hormones, particularly thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3), play critical roles in brain maturation, emotional regulation, and metabolic homeostasis, and have also been implicated in AA metabolic pathways [24, 29, 30]. Previous studies in adolescent depression have reported altered thyroid hormone levels [31], suggesting potential links between endocrine dysregulation, membrane lipid metabolism, and NSFR abnormalities [32, 33].

Membrane lipid dysfunction [34] may also represent an important pathophysiological substrate underlying abnormal NSFR [35, 36]. Previous studies have linked blunted NSFR to dysregulated AA metabolism and altered membrane lipid homeostasis in psychiatric disorders [32, 37–39]. Because erythrocyte membranes may partially reflect systemic membrane phospholipid status, erythrocyte-related indicators may provide additional insight into the relationship between membrane lipid abnormalities and NSFR in adolescent psychiatric disorders. In addition, oxidative stress has also been implicated in psychiatric disorders and may influence prostaglandin-mediated niacin responses. Uric acid, an important endogenous antioxidant, may therefore provide indirect information regarding oxidative stress status associated with abnormal NSFR.

Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate NSFR characteristics in adolescents with common psychiatric disorders and to further explore their associations with thyroid hormone levels and erythrocyte-related indicators. By integrating peripheral functional, endocrine, and membrane lipid-related measures, this study sought to further elucidate the biological basis and potential auxiliary diagnostic value of NSFR in adolescent psychiatric disorders.

Participants and methods

Study participants

This study recruited 51 adolescent patients with mental disorders. These patients were admitted to Xi’an International Medical Center Hospital from June to August 2023.

Inclusion criteria for the patients: ① Adolescents aged 10–18 years old, regardless of gender. ② Independently diagnosed by at least two attending psychiatrists, meeting the diagnostic criteria for depressive disorder, bipolar disorder, and behavioral affective disorder as stipulated in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (Fifth Edition) (DSM-5). ④ Both the subjects and their legal guardians have signed written informed consent forms, agreeing to participate in this study. ⑤ Able to cooperate with the niacin-induced skin flushing test and blood sample collection. ⑥ No history of niacin or vitamin supplementation in the past month, and no use of any drugs affecting skin vascular reactivity (such as antihistamines, corticosteroids, beta-blockers) within one week before the test.

For healthy controls: ① Matched with the patient group in terms of age and gender, with an age range of 10–18 years old. ② No personal or family history of mental illness. ③ Confirmed through psychiatric interviews and scale assessments that they do not meet the diagnostic criteria for mental disorders in DSM-5. ④ No history of severe physical diseases or endocrine/metabolic disorders. ⑥ Able to sign written informed consent forms and agree to participate in this study. ⑦ No history of niacin or vitamin supplementation in the past month, and no use of any drugs affecting skin vascular reactivity within one week before the test.

Exclusion criteria: ① Presence of severe physical diseases, including heart failure, liver failure or kidney failure, malignant tumors, severe infections, active tuberculosis, acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS), etc. ② Presence of organic brain diseases or neurological disorders (such as epilepsy, encephalitis, sequelae of traumatic brain injury). ③ Presence of endocrine/metabolic disorders (such as hyperthyroidism/hypothyroidism, diabetes, acute gout attack). ④ Presence of skin diseases that may interfere with the assessment of niacin-induced skin flushing reaction (such as dermatitis, eczema, severe acne, skin infection). ⑤ History of alcohol or psychotropic substance abuse in the past three months. ⑥ Unable to cooperate with the test or follow-up. ⑦ Legal guardians refuse to participate in the study, or the subjects cannot understand and cooperate with the research process. ⑧ Any other conditions deemed unsuitable for participation in the study by the researchers.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Xi’an International Medical Center Hospital (reference number: 202354). All participants and their parents or legal guardians agreed to participate in the study and provided signed informed consent after a detailed explanation by the doctors.

Study methods

Demographic and clinical data

A self-designed general survey was used to collect participants’ demographic data, including gender, age, height, and weight. Clinical data of patients were collected and included chief complaints, medical history, the age at onset, duration of illness, medication history, number of outpatient visits, number of hospitalizations, and duration of hospital stays. Complete blood count, liver, kidney, and thyroid function test results were collected for some patients.

Niacin-induced skin flush test

The niacin-induced skin flush test was conducted using an integrated detection platform for skin testing developed independently by the Chunling Wan research team at the Shanghai Jiao Tong University [24]. The platform uses six concentrations of methyl nicotinate solution (60 mM, 20 mM, 6.67 mM, 2.22 mM, 0.74 mM, and 0.25 mM), a patch with six holes for carrying the solution, an image acquisition system, and image recognition software. The testing procedure involved drawing 0.05 mL of each of the six concentrations of methyl nicotinate solution, applying it to the corresponding holes on the custom patch, and then placing the patch on a participant’s forearm for one minute before removal. Over the following five minutes, a photograph of the participant’s forearm was taken every 10 s using the image acquisition device, giving a total of 30 photographs per participant. The first photograph was considered the reference baseline. Professional image recognition software was used to obtain a total of 174 raw flush area measurements for each participant (6 concentrations × 29 photographs). The sum of the 174 raw flush areas was calculated to obtain the total area of the niacin-induced skin flush response. The sum of the flush response areas at 29 time points for each of the six concentrations was also calculated.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.1) software. Continuous variables with normal distributions are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed variables are reported as median (interquartile range). Categorical data are presented as frequencies (percentages). Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test.

For general demographic data, continuous variables were analyzed using one-way ANOVA, and categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test. Inter-group comparisons of niacin response were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Logistic regression analysis and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to evaluate the potential of niacin response in identifying different psychiatric disorders. The area under the ROC curve (AUC), sensitivity and specificity were calculated to assess discriminative performance.

Spearman correlation analysis was used to examine the relationship between the niacin response and demographic or clinical variables. Results showed that gender (p = 0.20), age (p = 0.37), height (p = 0.86), weight (p = 0.78), BMI (p = 1.00), duration of illness (p = 0.92), and length of hospital stay (p = 0.54) had no significant effect on the niacin response.

Due to incomplete data regarding medication usage during data collection, medication-related variables, including antipsychotic and anti-inflammatory treatments, were not included in the statistical analyses. This limitation has been acknowledged in the Discussion section.

All statistical tests were two-tailed, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Comparison of niacin response between adolescent disease groups and control group

Dynamic changes in niacin response curves

The curves depicting the average niacin response area over time for each disease subgroup and the control group are shown in Fig. 1. At four higher niacin concentrations (60 mM, 20 mM, 6.67 mM, and 2.22 mM), the niacin response curve was positioned at the bottom for adolescents with depressive disorder, followed by bipolar disorder and behavioral and emotional disorders. The response curve for the control group’s niacin response curve was positioned at the top. This indicated that the disease groups have a weaker niacin response compared to the control group. At the lower niacin concentrations (0.74 mM and 0.25 mM), the average niacin-induced skin flush area was nearly zero for all groups, with no significant differences between the groups.

Comparison of niacin response indicators between groups

As shown in Fig. 2A, the number of skin flush spots in depressive disorder (p < 0.001), bipolar disorder (p = 0.001), and behavioral and emotional disorders (p = 0.001) subgroups was significantly fewer compared to the control group, indicating that the disease groups were less sensitive to niacin. Additionally, the total area of niacin response in the depressive disorder subgroup was significantly lower compared to the control group (p < 0.001). For bipolar disorder (p = 0.088) and behavioral and emotional disorders subgroups (p = 0.087), the response was marginally significantly lower compared to the control group (Fig. 2B), suggesting that the disease groups had a weaker niacin response. Specifically, patients with depressive disorder had a significantly weaker response at the first four niacin concentrations compared to controls, whereas those with bipolar disorder had a significant or marginally significant weaker response at 60 mM, 20 mM, and 2.22 mM compared to controls (Fig. 2C and F). Patients with behavioral and emotional disorders differed significantly or marginally significantly from controls at 20 mM and 6.67 mM. These results indicate that compared to controls, the disease groups have lower sensitivity and weaker response intensity to niacin, especially the depressive disorder and bipolar disorder subgroups.

Potential of niacin response for identifying psychiatric disorders in adolescents

To further explore the auxiliary potential of niacin response for diagnosing psychiatric disorders in adolescents, a classification model was built using logistic regression analysis. The efficacy of the model was evaluated through ROC curves and the corresponding area under the curve (AUC). Each niacin response indicator, including the 174 raw response areas, total niacin response area, total response areas for each niacin concentration, and the number of flush spots, was analyzed for its diagnostic potential in each disease group. It was found that the number of niacin response flush spots had a good recognition effect for different psychiatric disorders. The AUC for depressive disorder was 0.891, with the best threshold values, showing a sensitivity of 76.9% and a specificity of 88.9%. The AUC for bipolar disorder was 0.838, with a sensitivity of 84.6% and a specificity of 72.2% at the optimal threshold value. The AUC for behavioral and emotional disorders was 0.784, with a sensitivity of 56.0% and a specificity of 88.9% at the most suitable threshold value. Therefore, niacin response has the potential to support the diagnosis of psychiatric disorders in adolescents, with the strongest discrimination ability for depressive disorder, followed by bipolar disorder (Fig. 3).

Correlation between niacin response and demographic or clinical variables

Spearman correlation analyses were conducted to examine the relationships between niacin response and demographic or clinical variables.

The results showed that gender (p = 0.20), age (p = 0.37), height (p = 0.86), weight (p = 0.78), BMI (p = 1.00), duration of illness (p = 0.92), and length of hospital stay (p = 0.54) were not significantly associated with niacin response. These findings suggest that the observed alterations in niacin-induced skin flush response were relatively independent of the major demographic and clinical variables examined in the present study.

Association between niacin response and blood test indicators

To further investigate the biological correlates of niacin-induced skin flush response, participants were categorized into a normal niacin response group (total niacin response area > 3035) and an abnormal niacin response group (total niacin response area < 3035). Statistical analysis revealed that the proportion of abnormal niacin responses in each disease group was significantly higher than in the control group (Table 2).

To explore factors potentially associated with abnormal niacin response, blood test indicators were compared between the normal and abnormal niacin response groups. (Fig. 4). Compared to the normal response group, the abnormal response group had significantly lower serum triiodothyronine (p = 0.03), free triiodothyronine (p = 0.023), and uric acid (p = 0.009), suggesting that the niacin response may be related to thyroid function and antioxidant capacity. Additionally, the abnormal response group showed tendency towards reduced red blood cell count (p = 0.056) and hematocrit (p = 0.085). The standard deviation of red cell distribution width was significantly higher (p = 0.023), indicating that a reduction in red blood cell count and heterogeneity in cell shape may be related to an abnormal niacin response.

Discussion

In this study, we found that the niacin-induced skin flush response (NSFR) is significantly reduced in adolescents with psychiatric disorders. By constructing an auxiliary diagnostic model, our results demonstrated that NSFR may provide objective biological information to assist in the identification of psychiatric disorders in adolescents. The reduction in the niacin response is most pronounced in depressive disorder, offering the best discrimination, followed by bipolar disorder and behavioral and emotional disorders. Additionally, we found that the niacin response in adolescents may be associated with thyroid function, antioxidant capacity, and the number and morphology of red blood cells, suggesting potential links between NSFR and systemic metabolic and inflammatory dysregulation.

Currently, there are few studies on the niacin-induced skin flush response in adolescents with psychiatric disorders, but these studies have reported consistent results. Qing et al. [9] conducted and compared niacin skin flush response tests between healthy controls and children and adolescents with schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, depressive disorder, autism spectrum disorder. The results showed a weaker niacin response in children and adolescents with schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and depressive disorder compared to the control group. However, there was no significant difference in responses between patients with autism and the control group. This suggests that the niacin response could be used as an early diagnostic tool for psychiatric disorders in children and adolescents. The team further explored the auxiliary diagnostic potential of the niacin response in adolescents with depression [24], validating that the niacin response is blunted in adolescents with depression, and the diagnostic model built upon the niacin response demonstrated good classification effectiveness. Several studies have also reported a weakened niacin skin response in adolescents with depression [40] and schizophrenia [11], indicating it as a potential biomarker. Our study validates and extends these findings, showing that skin flush response to niacin in adolescents with depressive disorder, bipolar disorder, and behavioral and emotional disorders is weaker than that in healthy adolescents.

Importantly, the present findings support the view that NSFR may represent a transdiagnostic biomarker rather than a disease-specific marker in psychiatric disorders. Multiple studies have demonstrated blunted NSFR across schizophrenia [41], bipolar disorder, and depressive disorder [21, 42], suggesting that these disorders may share common biological abnormalities involving membrane phospholipid metabolism and arachidonic acid signaling. Similarly, Qing et al. proposed that pediatric patients with schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and depression clustered together based on NSFR characteristics [9], further supporting the existence of shared pathophysiological mechanisms across psychiatric diagnoses.

Additionally, niacin response could serve as an objective auxiliary diagnostic biomarker for various psychiatric disorders in adolescents. We found that the number of flush spots is the most suited indicator to distinguish between adolescents with psychiatric disorders and healthy controls, which differs from previous studies. Wang et al. [24] reported that the area of the flush response at the seventh minute at 20mM niacin was most effective in identifying adolescents with depression (AUC of 0.72). Meanwhile, Shen et al. [23] reported the total area of the niacin response as the indicator for constructing their classification model, with an AUC of 0.71. Differences in sample sources and the high heterogeneity among patients may contribute to these discrepancies. However, our niacin test method was similar to that used in the two studies, but we reduced the niacin response detection time from 10 min to 5 min. The number of flush spots at 5 min of the niacin response may be a simpler and more intuitive assessment indicator. However, our study had a relatively small sample size, and further validation in larger and more diverse sample sets is needed in the future.

The niacin-induced skin flush response is a transdiagnostic biomarker for psychiatric disorders, in children, adolescents, and adults. A study by Maclean and team [43] found that adults with schizophrenia and bipolar disorder showed a weaker niacin response compared to healthy controls. Wang et al. [44] included additional adult psychiatric disorders, such as schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and unipolar depression, and conducted niacin skin flush response tests compared with healthy controls. They found that the patients with these psychiatric disorders exhibited similar niacin responses, characterized by reduced flushing intensity and rate. The consistent blunting of the niacin response across different psychiatric disorders may be due to shared pathophysiological mechanisms, such as inflammation and oxidative stress, rather than entirely disease-specific abnormalities.

One possible explanation is that blunted NSFR reflects shared disturbances in membrane phospholipid metabolism and AA–prostaglandin signaling pathways. The NSFR depends on phospholipase A2 activation, AA release from membrane phospholipids, and subsequent prostaglandin-mediated vasodilation [45, 46]. Previous studies have shown dysregulation of phospholipase and cyclooxygenase expression in psychiatric disorders [43], as well as abnormalities in polyunsaturated fatty acid metabolism and membrane lipid homeostasis [32, 37–39]. Therefore, the blunted NSFR observed across multiple psychiatric disorders may reflect a common impairment in membrane lipid signaling and inflammatory responsiveness.

In addition, oxidative stress and inflammatory dysregulation may also contribute to the blunted NSFR. Our study found that patients with abnormal niacin responses had lower uric acid levels. Considering the antioxidant properties of uric acid [47, 48], this finding further supports the possibility that abnormal niacin response is associated with impaired antioxidant capacity and increased oxidative stress in psychiatric disorders. Chronic inflammatory activation and oxidative stress may alter membrane lipid composition [49, 50], impair prostaglandin synthesis, and ultimately reduce NSFR. This shared biological background may partially explain why attenuated niacin responses are consistently observed across different psychiatric disorders despite their clinical heterogeneity.

We also found that the niacin response is associated with the number and morphology of red blood cells. Following niacin stimulation on the skin, arachidonic acid on the cell membrane is metabolized into prostaglandins that then cause blood vessel dilation, allowing more red blood cells to pass through the vessels, causing a localized skin flush response. Therefore, fewer and more irregularly shaped red blood cells do not favor the occurrence of the niacin-induced flush response. The maintenance of the number and shape of red blood cell is closely related to cell membrane homeostasis, providing indirect support for the hypothesis of abnormalities in lipid homeostasis during psychiatric diseases and the niacin response [33, 51]. Moreover, previous studies quantitatively assessing the degree of the niacin response using laser Doppler flowmetry and optical reflectance spectroscopy have detected a flux of red blood cells or hemoglobin at the site of niacin application. In our study, the correlation between the total area of niacin response and red blood cells also suggests consistency between different niacin test evaluation methods. However, the integrated platform for niacin skin response used in our study is simpler and more cost-effective compared to laser Doppler flowmetry and optical reflectance spectroscopy.

Furthermore, this study found a significantly strong correlation between the niacin response and thyroid hormones, similar to the findings of Wang et al. [24]. Studies have shown that serum thyroid hormone levels in adolescents with depression are significantly abnormal and are related to disease severity compared to healthy adolescents [52], indicating a close relationship between the hormone levels and depression. In contrast, Yao’s team found that thyroid hormone status affects arachidonic acid metabolism in humans and mice [53], suggesting that thyroid hormones may impact the skin flush response induced by niacin. Therefore, a blunted niacin response in adolescents with psychiatric disorders may also indicate broader neuroendocrine and metabolic dysregulation during adolescence.

Adolescence itself may represent a particularly vulnerable developmental stage for the manifestation of abnormal niacin responses. During adolescence, rapid brain maturation, endocrine remodeling, and neuroimmune changes may substantially affect membrane phospholipid metabolism, inflammatory activity, and oxidative stress regulation [27]. Therefore, NSFR abnormalities observed in adolescents may not simply mirror those observed in adults, but may instead reflect developmental-stage-specific biological vulnerability. This may also partially explain why different psychiatric disorders exhibit overlapping but quantitatively distinct niacin response patterns during adolescence.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, medication-related variables, including antipsychotic and anti-inflammatory treatments, were not comprehensively recorded during data collection. Therefore, the potential confounding effects of medication use on niacin response and inflammatory markers could not be fully controlled in the present analyses. Given that psychotropic medications and anti-inflammatory agents may influence membrane lipid metabolism and inflammatory pathways, their potential impact on the observed associations cannot be completely excluded. Future studies with more detailed medication records and stricter control of treatment-related factors are warranted to further validate the present findings. Second, the sample size of each diagnostic subgroup was relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Third, although the present findings support the transdiagnostic characteristics of NSFR, additional psychiatric and non-psychiatric disease groups should be included in future studies to further clarify the specificity and clinical applicability of this biomarker.

In summary, niacin response is reduced in adolescents with psychiatric disorders and has an auxiliary diagnostic value, supporting with further evidence for the niacin response as an objective biomarker in psychiatric disorders. Additionally, by establishing a link between the niacin response and blood test indicators, this study provides pathophysiological evidence for understanding the mechanisms underlying abnormal niacin responses in adolescents with psychiatric disorders. The present findings further support the possibility that attenuated niacin response reflects shared abnormalities in membrane lipid metabolism, inflammatory signaling, oxidative stress, and neuroendocrine regulation across psychiatric disorders. Future research must focus on the application of the niacin-induced skin flush response in psychiatric disorders among adolescents to promote objective diagnosis, personalized treatment, and prognosis assessment.
