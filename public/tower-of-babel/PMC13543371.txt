The association between magnesium and diabetic retinopathy: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Yicang Wang, Jing Li, Yeting Lin, Weiwen Hu, Yahan Tu, Ang Xiao, Qiong Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the clinical significance of serum magnesium in non-diabetic mellitus (non-DM), diabetes mellitus (DM) and diabetic retinopathy (DR). Moreover, to assess magnesium concentration in DR mice models.MethodsBaseline data from 150 patients per group (non-DM, DM, DR) comprised demographics, cardiovascular-metabolic profiles, electrolytes, and systemic immune-inflammatory were collected and analyzed. Logistic regression analysis was used to screen influencing factors for DR. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic utility of hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) and magnesium. Magnesium concentration in both serum and retinal tissues of DR mice were quantified via the methylthymol blue assay.ResultsCompared with the non-DM group, the DM group had lower age and blood pressure levels (P < 0.05), while heart rate, HbA1c and serum phosphorus levels were significantly higher (P < 0.05). Serum sodium and magnesium levels decreased gradually across the non-DM, DM and DR groups (P < 0.05), whereas heart rate and HbA1c levels increased progressively in the three groups (P < 0.05). Compared with DM, those with DR had longer diabetic duration. Ordinal multinomial logistic regression among non-DM, DM and DR groups indicated that HbA1c was positively associated with DR risk, whereas magnesium was negatively correlated with DR. Binary logistic regression between DM and DR groups showed that diabetes duration, SBP and HbA1c were positively related to elevated DR risk, and magnesium was negatively associated with the occurrence of DR. The ROC curve analysis revealed that a combined diagnostic of HbA1c and serum magnesium, with cut-off value of 0.500, achieved a sensitivity of 78.7% and a specificity of 90.0% for DM. The combined diagnosis of HbA1c and magnesium, with cut-off value of 0.441, approached a sensitivity of 75.3% and a specificity of 66.0% for DR. DR mice exhibited lower magnesium levels in both serum and retinal tissues compared with the control mice (P < 0.05).ConclusionPatients with DR had longer disease duration, elevated HbA1c levels and decreased serum magnesium concentrations. Combined detection of serum magnesium and HbA1c can significantly improve the diagnostic performance of DR.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is defined as a chronic metabolic disorder featured by abnormally elevated blood glucose concentration, which arises from compromised insulin secretion and insulin resistance [1, 3]. The International Diabetes Federation estimates that approximately 536.6 million individuals aged 20–79 years were diagnosed with DM in 2021, with forecasts anticipating an increase to 783.2 million cases by 2045 [4]. This condition can lead to various multisystem complications that affect the cardiovascular, neurological, renal, retinal, and peripheral vascular systems [5].

Diabetic retinopathy (DR), which stems from damage to the microvasculature, is a common outcome of DM and has become a leading factor in vision loss and blindness globally [6, 7]. The main pathophysiological process underlying DR comprises several changes brought about by hyperglycemia. The modifications include an increase in the thickness of the basement membrane in retinal capillaries, tissue ischemia, increased vascular permeability, and the release of diverse vasoactive substances, ultimately leading to neovascularization [8]. Despite chronic hyperglycemia being the main contributor to DR, increasing evidence indicates that imbalances in crucial trace elements significantly influence its development [9, 10]. A recent study involving 324 patients with DR and 803 without DR demonstrated an inverse association between serum magnesium levels and DR risk [11].

Magnesium, a crucial trace element, ranks as the second most prevalent intracellular cation in the human body, after potassium [12, 13]. It acts as a cofactor for over 600 enzymes, as well as serves as an activator for more than 200 additional enzymes [12, 14, 15]. Magnesium is essential for sustaining proper neuron and heart excitability, neuromuscular transmission, glucose and insulin metabolism [16–18]. Epidemiological investigations have revealed an inverse correlation between decreased magnesium concentration and the likelihood of developing heart failure and stroke [19]. Studies have demonstrated that individuals with DR have reduced magnesium levels, whereas those with sight-threatening DR (STDR) have even lower magnesium concentration than those without STDR [20]. Nonetheless, existing studies on the correlation between magnesium concentration and DR are insufficient, with findings still open to contention [19, 21, 22].

This study aimed to analyze whether non-DM, DM, and DR are associated with serum magnesium levels, while also measuring magnesium levels in the serum and retinal tissues of DR mice.

Methods

Study population and ethical approval

This retrospective study conducted at a single center involved 450 participants diagnosed with non-DM, DM, or DR and were treated at the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University from January 2020 to July 2025. The research was carried out following the ethical guidelines established by the Declaration of Helsinki and was granted approval by the Ethics Committee at the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University [(2025) CDYFYYLK (09–012)]. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) non-DM group: Patients with cataracts or pterygium and no history of DM. (2) DM group: fasting blood glucose levels ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test result ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, or glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5%. (3) DR group: diagnosed in accordance with the Evidence-Based Clinical Practice Guidelines for Diabetic Retinopathy in China (2022) [23].

Exclusion criteria: (1) other eye diseases (e.g., retinal detachment, macular degeneration, glaucoma, etc.). (2) severe renal impairment (serum creatinine levels exceedingly twice the upper limit of normal). (3) malignant tumors and autoimmune diseases (e.g., lung tumor, rheumatoid arthritis, etc.). (4) recent utilization of pharmaceuticals that influence the absorption and metabolism of magnesium (such as proton pump inhibitors, diuretics, and others).

Data collection

The foundational information comprises age, gender, diabetes duration, body mass index (BMI). Indicators associated with cardiovascular health include heart rate (HR), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP). Metabolic-related indicators primarily measure HbA1c and creatinine levels. Blood electrolyte indicators include potassium, sodium, chloride, calcium, phosphorus, and magnesium; Immune system-related parameters include platelet, lymphocyte, and neutrophil counts, in addition to the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), systemic immune inflammation index (SII, SII = [platelet count × neutrophil count] / lymphocyte count).

Animal experiments

Male mice of C57BL/6J were procured from Henan Skobes Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Following a two-week period of acclimatization, the mice were randomly allocated to normal control (NC) or DM groups. Mice in the DM group were injected with streptozotocin (STZ, 150 mg/kg) following a 12-hour fast, whereas those in the non-DM group were injected with an equivalent volume of citrate buffer. Mice exhibiting randomized blood glucose levels of ≥ 16.7 mmol/L three days post-injection were classified as DM mice. Blood glucose levels and body mass were evaluated biweekly. Mice were euthanized in compliance with the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020) [24]. Briefly, eight-week-old mice were intraperitoneally anesthetized with pentobarbital at a dosage of 40 mg/kg. Following confirmation of a deep anesthetic state—characterized by the abolition of nociceptive reflexes and complete muscular relaxation—the eyeballs were enucleated for retinal tissue isolation and blood sample collection, prior to final euthanasia via cervical dislocation. The Magnesium concentration in the serum and retinal tissue were quantified via a magnesium test kit (Bioswamp, BTK103). All animal-related procedures followed the ARVO Statement for using animals in ophthalmology and vision research. This study was approved by the Experimental Animal Welfare Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (CDYFY-IACUC-202512GR066).

Hematoxylin and eosin staining

Mice eyeballs were immersed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution and fixed overnight at 4 °C. After paraffin embedding, slices measuring 5 μm in thickness were produced. The sections were dewaxed in graded xylene and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE). Following dehydration and mounting, images were captured using digital pathology imaging software (Shenzhen Sheng Qiang Technology Co., Ltd.). Quantitative analysis was conducted on retinal areas approximately 1000 μm from the optic disc.

Peracetic acid-schiff staining of the retinal vascular network

The ocular globes of mice were removed and placed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for immersion. After being fixed overnight at 4 °C, careful dissection of the retinal tissue was performed. Neuronal cells were obtained through trypsin digestion, and the complete retinal vascular network was mounted on a microscope slide. Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS) staining was utilized to identify polysaccharides and glycogen, with a subsequent counterstaining using hematoxylin to highlight the nuclei in blue. Following the staining process, the retinal capillary basement membrane displayed a noticeable purplish-red coloration, which effectively delineated the morphological structure of the retinal arteries and aided in the identification of cell types associated with the vascular system.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted with SPSS software (version 27.0), and GraphPad Prism (version 10.1.2) was used for graph construction. The normality of measured data was evaluated via the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) test. Continuous variables conforming to a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while those deviating from normality were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR). Categorical variables were described as frequencies (%). For intergroup comparisons among the three groups, the appropriate statistical methods were selected as follows: one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) coupled with Bonferroni post-hoc testing for normally distributed data with homogeneous variances; Welch’s ANOVA combined with the Games-Howell test for normally distributed data with heterogeneous variances; and the Kruskal-Wallis test supplemented by Dunn’s post-hoc procedure for non-normally distributed data. For comparisons between two groups, either the independent samples t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test was employed. The variance inflation factor was utilized to assess multicollinearity among independent variables. Variables meeting the predefined criteria were incorporated into logistic regression analysis to identify factors influencing the outcome, and the diagnostic efficacy of relevant indicators was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. A value of P < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

This research included a total of 450 subjects categorized into three groups: non-DM (n = 150), DM (n = 150), and DR (n = 150). Analyses conducted to compare the non-DM, DM, and DR groups demonstrated no notable discrepancies in serum creatinine, serum potassium, platelet count, or lymphocyte count (P > 0.05). In contrast, significant differences were found in BMI, HR, SBP, DBP, HbA1c, sodium, chloride, calcium, phosphorus, magnesium, neutrophil count, NLR, PLR, and SII (P < 0.05) (Table 1).

Ordinal multinomial logistic regression

Ordinal multinomial logistic regression was performed on age, BMI, SBP, DBP, HR, sodium, chloride, calcium, phosphorus, magnesium, HbA1c, NLR, PLR, and SII to identify key factors associated with disease progression in non-DM, DM, and DR patients. Regression analysis revealed that HbA1c (OR = 2.179, 95%CI: 1.870–2.542, P < 0.001) and serum phosphorus (OR = 3.518, 95%CI: 1.134–10.930, P = 0.029) were positively associated with an increased risk of DR. The wide confidence interval for phosphorus limits the stability of the results, and larger sample sizes are needed in future investigations to improve the accuracy of the results. Magnesium (OR = 0.006, 95%CI: 0.001–0.072, P < 0.001) was negatively correlated with DR development. Age (OR = 0.961, 95%CI: 0.942–0.979, P < 0.001), SBP (OR = 1.031, 95%CI: 1.014–1.047, P < 0.001), DBP (OR = 0.939, 95%CI: 0.915–0.962, P < 0.001), HR (OR = 1.026, 95%CI: 1.008–1.045, P = 0.005), and SII (OR = 0.998, 95%CI: 0.996–1.000, P = 0.027) exhibited statistical significance, but their 95%CI crossed 1, suggesting a negligible effect on disease progression. All variance inflation factors were below 5, confirming no severe multicollinearity among covariates, thus assuring the reliability of the regression outcomes (Fig. 2).

Binary logistic regression

Binary Logistic regression was performed with age, BMI, SBP, DBP, HR, diabetes duration, sodium, chloride, calcium, phosphorus, magnesium, HbA1c, NLR, PLR and SII as covariates, to identify influence factors for DR onset in diabetic patients. The results demonstrated that diabetes duration (OR = 1.287, 95% CI: 1.197–1.384, P < 0.001), SBP (OR = 1.031, 95% CI: 1.006–1.056, P = 0.016) and HbA1c (OR = 1.402, 95% CI: 1.203–1.635, P < 0.001) were positively associated with elevated DR risk. Serum magnesium was negatively correlated with DR occurrence (OR = 0.013, 95% CI: 0.000–0.454, P = 0.017). No statistical association was observed between age, BMI, DBP, HR, sodium, chloride, calcium, phosphorus, NLR, PLR, SII and DR progression (all P > 0.05). The wide 95% confidence intervals of calcium and phosphorus limited the stability of results, and larger sample sizes are required to confirm their sustained correlations with DR. All variance inflation factors of included variables were below 5, confirming no severe multicollinearity among covariates and robust reliability of regression outcomes (Fig. 3).

The ROC curve

The above logistic regression analysis identified HbA1c was positively associated with increased DR risk, while serum magnesium was negatively correlated with DR occurrence. Consequently, an analysis of the ROC curve was conducted to assess the diagnostic efficacy of HbA1c and magnesium in detecting DM and DR, respectively (Table 2). The diagnostic cut-off values for HbA1c, magnesium, and their combination in DM were 6.150, 0.875, and 0.500, respectively, with corresponding areas under the curve of 0.860, 0.402, and 0.870, respectively. The application of 0.500 as the threshold for the integrated assessment of HbA1c and magnesium resulted in a sensitivity of 78.7% and a specificity of 90.0% for diabetes screening. The diagnostic cut-off values for HbA1c, magnesium, and their combination in DR were 7.050, 0.825, and 0.441, respectively, with corresponding areas under the curve of 0.750, 0.360, and 0.760, respectively. The application of 0.441 as the integrated cut-off value for HbA1c and magnesium produced a screening sensitivity of 75.3% and specificity of 66.0% for DR.

Serum and retinal magnesium levels in DR mice

Body weight and random blood glucose were comparable between the NC and DM groups at baseline. DM mice exhibited significantly higher random blood glucose levels from day 3 (P < 0.05) and lower body weights from week 2 post-STZ injection compared with NC (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4A-B). The HE and PAS staining of retinal tissues from the NC and DR groups were performed (Fig. 4C-D). The thickness of retinal neuroepithelium in the DR group was significantly thinner than that in the NC (P < 0.001) (Fig. 4E), whereas the retinal endothelial-to-pericytes (E/P) ratio was greater (P < 0.01) (Fig. 4F), indicating the onset of retinopathy in DR mice. Moreover, serum and retinal magnesium levels were significantly lower in DR mice than in NC mice (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4G-H).

Discussion

This retrospective clinical analysis demonstrated that diabetes duration and HbA1c were positively associated with an increased risk of DR. Serum magnesium levels gradually decreased with the progression from non-diabetic status to diabetes and DR. The combined detection of magnesium and HbA1c could improve the diagnostic accuracy of DR, thereby providing a novel approach for the risk assessment and intervention of DR.

Logistic regression showed that serum magnesium was negatively correlated with the onset of DR. In diabetic patients experiencing DR, diabetic cardiomyopathy, and various other microvascular complications, serum magnesium levels were found to be lower, with a more significant decline noted as the number of complications grew [25, 26]. A study of 120 participants in northern India showed that individuals with DR had significantly lower serum magnesium concentration than type 2 diabetes patients without retinopathy and non-diabetic subjects [27]. Another study also reported that serum magnesium levels were reduced in patients with retinopathy relative to those without retinopathy, and this reduction was correlated with elevated fasting blood glucose levels [20]. A research study found that the levels of serum magnesium in the DR group were lower compared to the non-DR group. It was observed that reduced magnesium ion levels correlated with a heightened risk of developing DR, with the ideal cut-off value of serum magnesium established for anticipating the onset of DR being 0.875 mmol/L [28]. These observations align well with the outcomes of our study.

A deficiency in magnesium, alongside decreased serum magnesium concentration and lower intake of this mineral, is linked to mild systemic inflammation and increased concentration of proinflammatory substances, such as interleukin-6, tumor necrosis factor-α, interleukin-1β, vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 [29, 31]. Magnesium deficiency increases reactive oxygen species production, enhances hydrogen peroxide generation, and promotes superoxide anion release by inflammatory cells [32, 33]. Magnesium can scavenge free radicals and mitigate oxidative stress-induced damage by regulating the activity of antioxidant enzymes, such as superoxide dismutase 2 and glutathione peroxidase [34]. As a result, magnesium deficiency is implicated in the development of DR as it triggers oxidative stress and promotes inflammatory reactions, worsening disease progression. In contrast, sufficient levels of magnesium can reduce the creation of reactive oxygen species and the degranulation of mast cells, thereby safeguarding epithelial cells and minimizing the oxidative and inflammatory harm to both cellular structures and blood vessels [30, 35], ultimately easing the impact of DR. Compared with the NC mice, we noted lower concentration of magnesium in both serum and retinal tissues in mice with DR. Supporting our observations, an earlier study revealed that the serum levels of zinc and magnesium were notably diminished in diabetic mice in comparison to healthy control mice [36], providing additional validation for our findings.

The inferences drawn from our data are subject to certain limitations. This study was not performed in a blinded fashion, and as a cross-sectional investigation, no patient follow-up was conducted, and treatment status was not collected. The direction of causality—whether hypomagnesemia drives DR progression or results from it—has not been established. The underlying mechanisms remain to be explored in further research.

Conclusion

Serum magnesium levels in patients with DR were significantly lower than those in non-DM and DM groups. Diabetes duration and HbA1c were positively associated with an increased risk of DR. Combined detection of HbA1c and serum magnesium exhibited superior diagnostic efficacy for DR compared with single indicators. Magnesium ion levels were decreased in both serum and retinal tissues of DR model mice.
