ABSTRACT:
The low pressures and temperatures found in Sandia’s Mach 14 hypersonic wind tunnel facility
pose challenges in measurement precision and accuracy of existing state-of-the-art optical gas-
phase temperature measurement techniques. This project reports on preliminary benchtop
characterization for a coherent Rayleigh scattering technique to address this gap in capability. The
goal was to modify the coherent Rayleigh scattering thermometry technique for single-laser-shot
measurement ability. This was accomplished via the use of novel dispersion optics for signal
collection and via the implementation of a picosecond laser excitation strategy. This report marks
the first experimental demonstration of this measurement strategy. The laser technique was first
validated within ambient air conditions. Data collection and data processing procedures were
written to allow for the extraction of quantitative gas-phase thermometry from experimental
images. This technique was further evaluated in benchtop experiments that closely simulate blow-
down-to-vacuum wind tunnel thermodynamic conditions. Adequate signal intensities for
determining temperature were observed at fluid densities relevant to Mach 14 tunnel conditions.
While further characterization of the measurement accuracy and precision are required at Mach 14
temperature conditions, a path toward these measurements are discussed along with several
potential improvements to the current measurement system. Prospects for future implementation
of this technique into the hypersonic wind tunnel are favorable.

INTRODUCTION AND EXECUTIVE SUMMARY OF RESULTS:
A capability gap currently exists in the generation of high-fidelity gas temperature measurements
during high Mach number wind tunnel experiments. At Mach 8 and slower, optical Raman
scattering provides accurate gas thermometry to complement other measurements and model
instrumentation. This technique relies on inelastic light scattering physics to reveal the molecular
rotational energy distribution, and therefore temperature. However, at Mach 14, the gas
temperature and pressure are reduced to 30 K and 0.2 Torr, respectively. Raman-based techniques
lose temperature sensitivity at these conditions as the rotational energy distribution has collapsed
to approximately three prominent states [1]. This project instead sought to develop techniques that
resolve the velocity distribution function (VDF) by utilizing elastic Rayleigh light-scattering
techniques. However, Rayleigh scattering techniques are not sufficiently developed for routine
application in hypersonic flows. This is especially true of the coherent variant we have employed.
The primary limitation of coherent Rayleigh scattering (CRS) has historically been the long signal
collection times. Early applications of these techniques required more than 10 minutes of
frequency scanning to acquire a single temperature measurement; well beyond the 45 second

duration of a wind tunnel test [2, 3, 4]. Custom laser designs are capable of frequency scanning
within a few hundred nanoseconds to address this limitation, however, even this temporal
resolution is slow compared to hypersonic flow timescales [5].
To address these shortcomings, we have developed a new method for single-laser-pulse (<10
nanosecond temporal resolution) temperature measurements by utilizing a highly dispersive optic,
known as a virtually imaged phased array (VIPA), within the signal collection system and via the
novel use of a picosecond pump laser. The mode-locked architecture of the picosecond laser
greatly reduced shot-to-shot variation in the resolved VDF that has prevented some previous
attempts at single-laser-pulse measurements with conventional nanosecond lasers [6, 7, 8]. This
technique is first demonstrated at ambient conditions to verify the single-laser-pulse measurement
capability. The laser system was then directed to a benchtop cryogenically cooled vacuum chamber
that emulates the hypersonic wind tunnel thermodynamic conditions. Adequate signal levels for
temperature determination were observed at conditions directly relevant to Mach 8 flow and at
fluid densities relevant to Mach 14 flow. No hard limits on signal intensity are reached when
reducing densities towards Mach 14 conditions, however additional concerns on instrument
spectral resolution may arise as the low temperature VDF narrows.

The y-parameter (Equation 2) captures the effect of several experimental parameters including
pump laser crossing angle, gas composition, temperature, and pressure. A large crossing angle of
the two pump beams generates the traveling density waves of relevant phase-velocity to couple
with the molecular VDF. The CRBS signal is composed of two features, the central Rayleigh peak
that is Doppler broadened by the thermal motion of the particles, and the Brillouin peaks associated
with acoustic density fluctuations. At low-pressure and low-temperature conditions, relevant to
hypersonic tunnel operations, the gas falls within the collisionless regime such that the CRBS
spectrum is only comprised of the Rayleigh peak Maxwellian velocity distribution. The technique
applied in this regime is commonly referred to simply as coherent Rayleigh scattering (CRS) as
the central peak is the dominant spectral feature. The width of the VDF in a CRS signal is
proportional to the square-root of temperature as displayed in Figure 2a. Bulk flow velocity in the
direction of the electrostrictive grating is also spectrally captured via a Doppler shift (Figure 2b).
These theoretical VDFs have been generated using the Pan s6 model [4]. As with other nonlinear
four-wave mixing thermometry techniques, such as coherent anti-Stokes Raman scattering, the
signal intensity is proportional to the input intensity of the pump and probe beams, the length of
the probe volume squared, and the number density squared. However, the Rayleigh cross-section
is roughly three orders of magnitude greater than that of Raman scattering and thus a CRBS signal
is visible in air at ambient conditions [9].

Figure 2: Modeled CRS VDF at conditions relevant to blow-down-to-vacuum hypersonic tunnel operation.

The CRS technique was first demonstrated by Grinstead and Barker for gas-phase thermometry in
the collisionless regime [2]. Later works by Pan et al. extended the modeling of this technique to
the kinetic regime where acoustic Brillouin peaks become a prominent spectral component of the
signal (CRBS) [4]. These early measurements demonstrated the capability of probing temperature
using CRS and CRBS, however, measurements required systematic scanning of the probe laser
wavelength over many minutes. This limited the application of these techniques to quiescent
environments. Vieitez et al. reduced this measurement time by utilizing a Fizeau spectrometer,
however found that fluctuations in the pump laser cavity modes adversely effected interpretation
of the single-shot spectrum such that spectra were averaged over 500 laser shots to extract
temperature [8]. Using a 12 GHz etalon-based sensor, Graul and Lilly similarly observed pump
laser cavity modes, however, were able to filter their spectra to fit the measurements for
temperature [6]. The CRBS signal was still averaged over 1000 laser shots in this work. Gerakis
et al. first demonstrated single-laser-shot measurements by introducing significant dispersion to
one the pump beams such the grating frequency, and thus phase velocity, was scanned over ~100
ns [5]. The signal is captured using a fast photomultiplier tube such that a time-domain
representation of the CRBS signal is acquired. Several works have been published expanding upon
this work via the development of the chirped pulse laser technology or by further characterizing
this technique [10, 11, 12, 13]. Notably, this method of acquiring CRBS spectra is the only method
to be demonstrated in non-quiescent gases. Specifically, gas-phase velocimetry has been
demonstrated in a subsonic jet [10].

Experiment Description and Methodology:
The current study presents an alternate method for the generation and acquisition of a CRS
spectrum on a single-laser-pulse basis. Initial efforts failed due to laser artifacts resultant from the
nanosecond pump laser architecture, as observed in early CRBS studies [4, 8, 6]. Multi-pulse-
averaged measurements still yielded representative VDF profiles which contain critical
information of mean flow properties; however, this did not address the primary goal of this project
in reducing the temporal averaging reported in previous applications of this technique. To address
this concern alternate pump schemes were considered. Instead of the traditional nanosecond pump
laser, a picosecond laser is used to induce the electrostrictive grating is the gas-phase media. This
circumvents the shot-to-shot fluctuations observed by Vieitez et al. and Graul et al. caused by the
nanosecond pump laser cavity modes [8, 6]. The theoretical framework for the physics behind this
phenomenon was first discussed by Manteghi et al. who also proposed the concept of picosecond
pumping of electrostriction in that work [7]. The work presented in this report represents the first
experimental demonstration of this method. Results from this experimental effort demonstrate that
this method can extend CRBS to the single-laser-shot regime. A more detailed discussion of the
impact of this pump method and the requisite physics can be found within the results section.

 Figure 3: Schematic of VIPA dispersion

In conjunction with the use of picosecond pump beam, a virtually imaged phased array, or VIPA,
was installed within the collection optics. The VIPA is an interference-based dispersion optic, like
an etalon, however, due to its design, allows for high transmissivity of the incident light. This is
critical for the extension of CRBS to low density regimes where limited signal intensities are
observed. The VIPA was first proposed and demonstrated as a device for wavelength division
multiplexing by Shirasaki [14]. This device has also found use as a high dispersion spectrometer
for frequency comb characterization, optical parametric oscillator characterization and as a mid-
infrared spectrometer [15, 16, 17]. More recently, the VIPA has been used to resolved non-
equilibrium CARS spectra and linear Rayleigh scattering spectra [18, 19]. A VIPA is effectively
a modified etalon comprised of two parallel surfaces angled relative to the input light (Figure 3).
The front surface is comprised of a 100% reflection coating and a small entrance window through
which input light is focused using a cylindrical lens. The back surface is a partial reflecting surface
(~95%) coating. This optical configuration allows for high transmission efficiency of the input
light in comparison to traditional etalons. The light is then focused onto an image plane where an
interference pattern forms. This yields a light intensity distribution across a sensor array that can
be converted to wavelength space using a paraxial wave theory dispersion model developed by
Xiao et. al. [20]. Current calibration methods utilized in this work are rudimentary, but a proposed
method for accurate calibration is discussed for future implementation.

 Figure 4: Optical Diagram of the coherent Rayleigh scattering thermometry system.

The experimental CRS system developed for this work is displayed in Figure 4. Two 10 Hz
Nd:YAG lasers are used in the generation of the CRS signal. The pump laser (Ekspla PL2230) is
a regeneratively amplified mode-locked laser that outputs an 80-picosecond duration laser pulse.
The Fourier transform limited second harmonic 532 nm output is used for the CRS pump pulses.
Due to the short pulse duration, this beam has sufficient bandwidth to probe the full molecular
distribution function. This laser is split using a 50/50 beamsplitter to provide both pump beams at
the measurement volume. One of the beams is aligned through an optical delay line with a
computer-controlled translation stage (Newport UTS50CC) allowing for precise control of the
relative pulse arrival time at the probe volume. The probe laser (Continuum Powerlite DLS)
outputs a seeded second harmonic (532 nm) beam. The pump and probe laser powers can be
attenuated separately using half-wave plate and thin film polarizers. After the attenuation and
splitting optics, the polarization of each beam is controlled using a half-wave plate. The pump and
probe lasers are orthogonally polarized to allow for the spatial overlap and separation of the two
beams to achieve the phase matching criteria indicated in Figure 1. The probe laser is externally
triggered by a pulse generator synced to the clock of the picosecond pump laser such that the
relative arrival of the pump and probe pulses is controlled. In this way the two pump and the probe

pulses arrive at the probe volume simultaneously. The two pump beams are crossed at a 178-degree
angle to target a small electrostrictive grating y-parameter. Phase-matching is achieved by counter-
propagating the probe beam relative to one of the pump beams (designated pump 2 in Figure 1).
The coherent signal beam then back scatters such that it counter propagates relative to the other
pump beam (pump 1 in Figure 1). All beams are focused and collimated using 300 mm focal length
plano-convex lenses with 532 nm anti-reflection coatings. The signal is separated from the pump
beam path using a thin-film polarizer. The signal beam is then spatially isolated from the probe
beam incoherent scatter via a series of apertures and a 50 µm pinhole located equidistant between
two 300 mm focal length plano-convex lenses. The signal beam is focused into the VIPA entrance
window with a 532 nm anti-reflection coated 500 mm focal length cylindrical length lens. The
VIPA utilized is coated for 500 to 600 nm and has a spacing of 6.74 mm between coated surfaces
resulting in an approximately 15 GHz free spectral range (LightMachinery Inc., OP-6721-6743-
2). The output from the VIPA is focused using a 750 mm plano-convex lens onto a EMCCD sensor
(Andor iXon Ultra 888). After the probe beam is separated from the pump beam using a thin film
polarizer, it is reflected along a calibration leg. A flip-mounted mirror allows for the probe beam
to be aligned colinear with the signal beam path into the VIPA spectrometer. In this way, a series
of calibration images can be collected that are representative of the system instrument function.
During this process, the probe beam is transmitted through an OD 4 neutral density filter to prevent
damage to the VIPA and camera. While not installed in the current system, future modifications
will include a wavemeter to monitor the probe beam wavelength. This will provide critical
information to fully utilize the Xiao et al. paraxial wave theory to model the VIPA of this system.

 Figure 5: Cryogenically cooled vacuum chamber

These CRS measurements were demonstrated first in ambient air, but later measurements were
acquired within a cryogenically cooled vacuum chamber (depicted in Figure 5) to emulate the
thermodynamic conditions within the hypersonic wind tunnel. The 230x230 mm stainless steel
chamber contains a custom-built copper cell that adapts via thermal contact to a Janis ST-100
cryostat system. The system is used to make low temperature optical measurements ranging from
80 K (the limit of liquid nitrogen) to 300 K, and pressures from 0 to 300 torr. The vacuum cube is
evacuated using a portable pump reaching pressures down to 10-6 Torr. A pressurized gas bottle
supplies dry nitrogen to the chamber through a 2-stage regulator. Pressure is monitored using a
convection vacuum gauge manufactured by InstruTech (Model CVG101GA). Temperature inside
the optical cell is measured using a type K thermocouple. The liquid nitrogen used to cool the
cryostat is supplied by a 50 L transfer dewar via vacuum jacketed lines.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION:
Raw Data Properties:
An averaged raw image acquired in room temperature and atmospheric pressure air is displayed
in Figure 6a. The VIPA output is a series of repeating spectral features of diminishing instrument
resolution. Each peak contains the same information of the VDF spectral content. The peaks have
different peak intensity depending on the intensity envelope of the VIPA. For these preliminary
measurements, the VIPA angle relative to the input beam Poynting vector is rotated such that the
peak intensities of the second and third transmitted VIPA modes are optimized. In the current
work, the intensity envelope is not characterized. This results in skewed VDFs from the outer
VIPA modes. However, monitoring the probe laser wavelength in future measurements could aid
in modeling the intensity envelop and thus for correcting signal intensities across the outer VIPA
modes allowing for lower error comparisons to theory. Since this intensity variation has the most
drastic effect on the outer VIPA modes, only the third VIPA mode from the left is considered for
fitting in the remainder of this results section.

Figure 6: (a) Raw experimental data with key features indicated (b) VIPA paraxial wave theoretical model
 to aid in interpretation of experimental data.

Comparing the raw image to modeled wavenumber (k) arrays of the various VIPA modes (Figure
6b) makes the nonlinear nature of this dispersion clear. Each red dot in the CRS signal corresponds
to the same wavelength, however, this feature repeats with unequal spacing. Each VIPA mode is
considered to have a separate wavelength-to-pixel conversion. As demonstrated in Figure 6b, a
single wavelength, demarked by the solid black line, spans multiple VIPA interference modes.
This could effectively provide 3+ temperature dependent features per VIPA image. Comparison
of fitting results across multiple VIPA modes has not yet been implemented but may yield
improved accuracy or precision in the technique.

While the CRS signal using nanosecond or picosecond pump lasers yields average images like that
displayed in Figure 6a, the single-laser-pulse image characteristics are significantly different.

  Figure 7: Time series of experimental single-laser-pulse images acquired using (a) nanosecond pump
 laser and (b) picosecond pump laser electrostriction.

Two timeseries of single-laser-pulse images are presented in Figure 7. Q-switched nanosecond
laser pumping has been standard in early literature. Unfortunately, the un-seeded nanosecond laser
cavity mode structure had a detrimental effect on the feasibility of single-laser-pulse
measurements. While previous efforts were able to pre-process the images with Fourier filtering
to eliminate the ~200 MHz laser cavity structure, this was due to multi-laser-pulse averaging of
the CRS signal. In this work, the nanosecond-pumped signal generated on a single-laser-pulse
basis demonstrated significant modulation of the VDF frequency content and stochastic variation
on a shot-to-shot basis (Figure 7a).

In the Q-switched nanosecond laser architecture, the various cavity modes are generated without
coupling between them. As such, the two pump beams crossed at the CRBS probe volume have
incoherent interaction between the modes yielding stochastic constructive and destructive

interference of the various frequency content of the laser. When the two pump beams are
overlapped within the coherence length scale (~3 mm) a high intensity but narrow signal, which
does not cover the extent of the VDF, appears. Much of the physics of this phenomenon is
discussed in work by Manteghi et. al., who similarly observed the narrowing of the CRS signal
[7]. This work also discussed the potential benefits of picosecond laser pumping and the theory
behind these benefits.

The picosecond laser avoids these pitfalls by virtue of being a mode-locked laser. During the pulse
duration of the laser (~80 ps) every cavity mode is in phase with every other allowing for coherent
and constructive interaction across the full bandwidth of the picosecond laser (~6.5 GHz), greater
than the spectral extent of the VDF. The result of pumping the electrostrictive grating with a
picosecond laser on the CRS signal are evident in Figure 7b. The stochastic mode structure is no
longer present on a shot-to-shot basis and a clear representation of the VDF can be extracted
without the need for temporal averaging. While the use of a picosecond pump laser was first
proposed in the work of Manteghi et al. [7], the data collected in this work represents the first
experimental demonstration of this method.

Data Processing for Quantitative Thermometry:
The steps to acquire quantitative thermometry from CRS are outlined below:

 1. Acquire CRS signal and instrument response function (IRF) at known conditions (ambient
 temperature/pressure nitrogen)
 2. Acquire CRS signal and IRF at unknown conditions
 3. Use CRS signal and IRF acquired at known conditions to determine VIPA free spectral
 range with nonlinear fitting routine
 4. Use CRS signal and IRF acquired at unknown conditions to determine gas-phase
 temperature with nonlinear fitting routine

An experimental nonlinear fitting procedure was developed to extract either the VIPA free spectral
range or the gas-phase temperature from the raw data. The fitting routine, outlined in Figure 9, is
the same for both procedures.

 Figure 9:Data processing code map.

The CRS signal is background corrected to remove any intensity baseline due to the imaging of
ambient or incoherent scattered light. The images are then ready for the nonlinear fitting routine.
Initial guesses are provided for the chosen fitting parameters. Currently there are two
configurations in which the code is run: 1) fixed temperature with the VIPA free spectral range
(FSR) floated as part of the image calibration process and 2) fixed FSR with temperature floated
for thermometry. For both configurations, horizontal pixel shift, vertical pixel shift and vertical
intensity scaling parameters are also floated. These parameters are fed to a nonlinear fitting routine
in which the parameters are varied to reduce the RMS error between the theory and experimental
data.

Within the nonlinear fitting routine, a couple of steps are required before the theory and experiment
can be compared. First, frequency (wavelength) arrays need to be generated to map to the camera
pixel array. The key steps in this procedure are outlined in Figure 10. Each peak of the CRS signal
is located (Figure 1Figure 10a). It is assumed that the distance between each peak is the VIPA FSR.

With this assumption, the change in frequency for each pixel is known and thus a frequency
calibration is generated (Figure 10b). A different frequency calibration is calculated for each
visible VIPA mode by shifting the location of zero frequency to the peak location of that mode
(Figure 10c). When calibrating for the VIPA dispersion, the FSR is iterated upon such that a new
set of frequency arrays are calculated for each fit iteration. When fitting for temperature, the FSR
determined in the calibration step is held constant. This procedure is separately completed for IRF
images to account for any shift in peak pixel location. This IRF image frequency array is used in
the following step.

 Figure 10: Pixel-to-frequency conversion processing method.

With frequency arrays calculated for both the experimental data set and IRF images, the next step
is to modify the theoretical VDF based on the IRF. The IRF is experimentally determined by
propagating a small portion of the probe laser through the signal detection optics, including the
VIPA, yielding an interference pattern as observed in Figure 11a. The IRF of each VIPA mode is
matched to the corresponding CRS signal from each VIPA mode. A theoretical VDF is determined
using the Pan S6 model [4]. The gas phase temperature and pressure are inputs to this model,
enabling quantitative gas thermometry. This VDF is then convolved with the IRF to generate the
theoretical CRS signal as imaged through the detection optics of this measurement system (Figure
11b). The nonlinear nature of the VIPA dispersion yields an asymmetric instrument response
function. Convolving the theory with the IRF broadens and skews the observed CRS signal. It is
critical to capture this divergence from the original CRS Pan s6 model for accurate comparison
the experimental data.

Figure 11: Sample experimentally acquired instrument function and resulting convolved theoretical VDF.

With the theory appropriately modified, the experimental data can be compared (Figure 12). After
utilizing the nonlinear fitting routine, good agreement is observed between the experimental data and
final theoretical CRS signal. Residual offsets of less than 2% are observed when fitting the FSR at known
gas conditions. The FSR is iteratively determined separately for each VIPA mode. Per paraxial wave theory,
the VIPA FSR should be a function of pixel location, however, this work assumes the FSR to be constant
across the width of the VDF. Future work will revisit this assumption to determine if significant accuracy
improvements can be achieved.

 spectral range.

Fitting Thermometry Results:

 Figure 13:CRS temperature measurements acquired at various thermodynamic conditions.

Temperature fits of the mean experimental data acquired at various gas-phase conditions are presented
in Figure 13. During the least-squares fitting routine, the pressure is fixed based on the convection vacuum
gauge pressure reading for the given vacuum chamber operating condition. Temperature sensitivity is
observed in the spectral width of the of the VDF, as predicted by the theoretical model. Good agreement
with the k-type thermocouple installed near the laser measurement volume is also observed, proving this
to be a viable technique for gas-phase thermometry. Additionally, good agreement between the
experimental and theoretical frequency content of the VDF are observed in the fit residuals. Residuals of
less than 5% are observed when fitting for temperature at all thermodynamic conditions probed. Some
residual profiles display asymmetry. This is primarily attributed to imprecise overlap between the signal
path and probe sample path resulting in a poorly characterized instrument response function. This
highlights the critical nature of this experimental step and will be a significant consideration for future
iterations of the CRS measurement system. Of note, the lower right CRS signal corresponds to density

conditions relevant to Mach 14 tunnel operation, albeit at higher pressure and higher temperature.
Achieving temperatures fits of experimental data at these conditions is a key accomplishment of this work.
Further investigation into the systematic trend evident in residual profiles near the peak of the VDF is
required. Improved estimates of the temperature dependent transport coefficients may reduce these
residuals. Alternatively, fully characterizing the VIPA intensity envelope and correcting the VDF spectral
profiles may address this discrepancy between the data and theory. Finally, the use of high intensity pump
lasers has been experimentally demonstrated to narrow the VDF [21]. This effect is not observed to
diminish the thermometry capability of this technique; however, further work remains to interpret the
physical implications of using laser pulses with pulse durations on the order of the collision time scale of
these low-density flows.

  Figure 14: CRS single-laser-pulse fit statistics at various gas densities: (a) temperature measurement
 standard deviation normalized by the mean fit temperature, (b) normalized difference between CRS
  thermometry and k-type thermocouple, (c) dimensional CRS thermometry standard deviation and (d)

The single-laser-pulse temperature fit statistics are displayed in Figure 14. The precision, as indicated by
the single-laser-pulse fit standard deviation (Figure 14a), remains within 8%, however a monotonic
increase is observed as the gas density decreases. This corresponds to a maximum fit temperature

standard deviation of 7.4 K (Figure 14c). The precision seems to be primarily a function of density, however
further testing at Mach 14 temperature conditions is required to confirm that the 8% precision will hold.

The accuracy of the technique is captured via a comparison between the CRS fit measurements and a k-
type thermocouple measurement (Figure 14b). As density decreases, the accuracy of the technique
plateaus around 8%. Because all the low-density measurements were acquired at temperatures of ~100
K, it appears that the accuracy of this technique is primarily dependent on the gas-phase temperature.
While this is within the percentage accuracy goals set by this project (10%), it is possible that this accuracy
would worsen at lower temperatures relevant to Mach 14 wind tunnel operation (~30 K). A current
limitation of this CRS laser system is the modest frequency resolution of the off-the-shelf VIPA (~500 MHz).
As displayed in Figure 14d, the VDF width begins to approach the width of the IRF at these cryogenic
temperatures. A custom VIPA, as presented in the work by Teav et al., may be able to improve the
temperature precision of the CRS measurements by better resolving the VDF width [19]. Some question
also remains whether the worsened accuracy at low temperature conditions is due to poor performance
of the k-type thermocouple. These low-density measurements were all acquired at ~100 K which
approaches the lower bounds of the k-type thermocouple operating range. Similar discrepancies between
the thermocouple and laser temperature measurement have been noted when previously applying
coherent Raman scattering techniques in this environment. Future efforts will endeavor to install a
cryogenically rated thermocouple for more certain comparisons.

ANTICIPATED OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS:
The completion of this exploratory research effort has yielded a new measurement capability with
the potential to generate unprecedented datasets within Sandia’s hypersonic wind tunnel facility.
The continuation of this research will focus on additional benchtop testing to work towards
applying this measurement technique in Sandia’s hypersonic wind tunnel. The benchtop testing
will primarily focus on measurements in the cryogenically cooled vacuum chamber after
adaptation for use with liquid helium. This will allow for measurements in the thermodynamic
environment exactly matching that of the Sandia Mach 14 wind tunnel test section and will allow
for similar characterization of the technique measurement and precision as presented for this
project. Additionally, several experimental system and data processing modifications will be
explored with the goal of further refining the thermometry technique:

 1. Better VIPA characterization via monitoring the probe laser center wavelength.
 2. Install a VIPA with improved dispersion properties and quantify effect on CRS
 instrument accuracy.
 3. Survey the effects of pump laser energy to determine if spectral narrowing is observed.

The second modification will a characterization of the system with a custom VIPA installed for
higher dispersion. Such a VIPA has been utilized by Teav et. al. to resolve the fine spectral features
of spontaneous Rayleigh-Brillouin spectra at high pressures [19]. This will aid these CRS
measurements in the low temperature regime where the Rayleigh peak width is currently
approaching the width of the instrument response function. By narrowing the instrument function,
the VDF will be better resolved which may yield better measurement precision and accuracy. Teav
et. al. did note a strong dependence of the custom VIPA performance on the ambient conditions
due to the air-spaced design. This may require additional VIPA characterization than already
implemented for the CRS measurement.

The final system characterization will be to determine the effects of the high intensity pump beams.
The current pump beam intensity is of the order of magnitude of 1016 Wm-2. This is greater than
the laser intensity previously noted to induce spectral narrowing of the VDF [21]. The spectral
narrowing would yield a lower temperature fit. The primary difference between the experimental
system used in that work and the system developed in this work is the laser pulse duration. The
novel use of the picosecond pump laser may circumvent this adverse effect due to the pulse
duration being on the order of the molecular collisional timescale. Further study is required on this
topic. However, future benchtop experimentation will be conducted to ensure that the current
system laser intensity is not affecting the fit temperature measurement. A laser intensity survey
will be conducted by acquiring CRS measurements in the cryogenically cooled vacuum chamber

at known thermodynamic conditions. Results will inform on a maximum pump laser energy to
acquire accurate thermometry.

The continuation of benchtop testing that began within this Exploratory Express laboratory
directed research and development (LDRD) project will be of interest to a full LDRD project under
the Engineering Sciences investment area. Work under a future LDRD project will seek to mature
this measurement technique to a degree that it can be demonstrated within Sandia’s wind tunnels.
Additionally, future LDRD funding could aid in further benchtop development of this CRS
technique to increase the measurement temporal and spatial dimensionality. These results may
yield improved measurement productivity when applied during a limited-duration wind tunnel
experiment. Investigating the potential for simultaneous velocimetry by resolving signal Doppler
shift is also of critical interest because resolving concomitant temperature and velocity could
provide the necessary data for hypersonic compressible turbulence model development.

As the technique matures, it is expected that it will be implemented within the wind tunnel to study
hypersonic flows relevant to future flight geometries. Hypersonic flow phenomena (wakes,
boundary layers, shock impingement, etc.) are not currently well understood, yet the interaction of
these flows with solid structures is critical in the design of flight geometries, thermal protection
systems and remote device communication. Characterizing the aerodynamic environments that
induce random vibrations in future systems is of primary interest to the Delivery Environments
portfolio. Extrapolation of Mach 5-8 data to Mach 14 conditions has not proven accurate and thus
measurements in ground testing facilities are required to validate state-of-the-art computational
models and support emerging data-driven models. This CRS thermometry technique is a unique
capability that could prove a pivotal component of the ground test data package. By coupling
experimental data, such as that acquired with CRS, to the modeling efforts, improved predictive
capabilities and, ultimately, improved designs of future flight geometries are possible.

CONCLUSION:
The primary success of this exploratory effort is the development of a gas-phase measurement
technique that holds promise for the Mach 14 wind tunnel thermometry. Using a picosecond pump
laser, previous limitations of the CRS technique were overcome allowing for temporally resolved
single-laser-pulse measurements. Sufficient signal intensities to implement spectral fitting
algorithms for thermometry are observed at densities relevant to Mach 14 wind tunnel densities.
Measurement and data processing procedures were developed to extract gas-phase temperature
from experimental images and improvements to the current methods are discussed. The viability
of this thermometry technique in Mach 8 relevant thermodynamic environments was demonstrated
in a cryogenically cooled vacuum chamber. While further benchtop characterization is required,
preliminary temperature fit statistics display temperature accuracy and precision within 8%.
Several methods to improve upon this measurement precision are discussed including monitoring
probe beam wavelength or installing a custom VIPA for improved spectral resolution. As this

measurement technique is further developed, it may become a valuable instrument for blowdown-
to-vacuum wind tunnel facilities. Data acquired with this technique may improve understanding
of hypersonic flow phenomena and, consequently, aid the development of state-of-the-art
modeling and simulation efforts.
