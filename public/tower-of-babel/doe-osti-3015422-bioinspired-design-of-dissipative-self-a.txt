FINAL TECHNICAL REPORT
Award Number: DE-FG02-04ER46162
Project Title: Bioinspired Design of Dissipative Self-Assembly of Active Materials
Principal Investigator: Zhibin Guan
Institution: University of California, Irvine
Funding Agency: U.S. Department of Energy, Office of Science, Basic Energy Sciences
(BES – Materials Sciences and Engineering)
1. Project Overview and Scientific Motivation
The major objective of this DOE-funded research program was to establish general,
experimentally validated design principles for dissipative, out-of-equilibrium self-assembly of
synthetic active materials. In living systems, structures such as actin filaments and microtubules
are maintained far from thermodynamic equilibrium through continuous energy consumption.
This persistent nonequilibrium operation enables functions including adaptability, self-healing,
directed motion, and force generation—properties that are largely absent in traditional
equilibrium soft materials.
At the inception of this project, chemically fueled dissipative assemblies were rare, typically
short-lived, and often dependent on harsh or toxic reagents. Moreover, the use of electrical
energy—one of the most versatile, scalable, and controllable energy sources—had not been
explored as a means to fuel dissipative self-assembly. This project addressed these challenges by
introducing redox chemistry reaction networks, and electrochemical redox networks as general
strategies for sustaining active material behavior over extended periods. The research progressed
systematically from chemically fueled systems to electrically fueled, waste-free active materials,
establishing a coherent conceptual framework that bridges molecular redox chemistry, reaction
networks, and macroscopic material behavior.
2. Major Scientific Accomplishments
2.1 Redox-Fueled Dissipative Self-Assembly as a General Materials Platform
A major accomplishment of this project was the demonstration that redox chemistry provides a
powerful and general platform for driving dissipative self-assembly in polymer and
supramolecular materials. By coupling reversible redox transformations to redox-sensitive self-
assembly molecular building blocks, we established systems in which material formation,
persistence, and dissipation are governed by reaction kinetics rather than equilibrium
thermodynamics.
These studies showed that transient assembling species could be generated through fuel-driven
redox reactions and spontaneously dissipate as the fuel was consumed, resulting in
programmable lifetimes and reversible material states. Importantly, systematic variation of redox
kinetics revealed clear relationships between molecular reaction rates, assembly lifetime, and
structural robustness. This work transformed dissipative self-assembly from a concept into a
quantitatively controllable materials design strategy (ChemSystemsChem 2019, 1, e1900030).

2.2 Chemothermally Driven Nonequilibrium Materials Capable of Mechanical Work
An early major advance was the development of chemothermally driven nonequilibrium
materials capable of performing macroscopic mechanical work. By coupling exothermic click
reactions to thermoresponsive polymer networks, chemical energy dissipation at the molecular
scale was converted into bulk thermal gradients and mechanical actuation (ChemSystemsChem
2020, 2, e2000024).
These materials exhibited transient swelling and contraction, reversible actuation cycles, and the
ability to lift loads far exceeding their own weight. This work provided one of the first
demonstrations that synthetic soft materials can translate chemical energy dissipation into useful
mechanical work, thereby establishing a direct link between dissipative chemistry and
macroscopic functionality.
2.3 Electrically Fueled Dissipative Self-Assembly: A New Paradigm
One of the most significant outcomes of this project was the first demonstration of electrically
fueled dissipative self-assembly. In these systems, electrochemical redox reactions were used to
generate transient chemical fuels in situ, enabling reversible self-assembly near electrode
surfaces. The electrically fueled systems developed in this project exhibited rich dynamic
behavior, including spatial patterning, directional growth, and dynamically fluctuating interfaces.
Using microelectrode arrays and programmable electrical inputs, we demonstrated precise
control over the location, timing, and morphology of active assemblies (J. Am. Chem. Soc. 2022,
144, 7844–7851).
Electrical fueling offered several key advantages over traditional chemical fueling, including
precise temporal control, spatial addressability, and compatibility with electronic systems. The
electrically fueled assemblies exhibited rapid assembly and disassembly kinetics, directional
growth, and robust cycling behavior. This work established electric current as a fundamentally
new fuel for active soft matter. These results highlight the potential of electrically fueled
dissipative systems for creating adaptive and responsive materials with complex spatiotemporal
behavior analogous to biological active matter.
2.4 Waste-Free, Closed-Loop Electrochemical Active Materials
Building on the initial electrochemical platforms, a major breakthrough was the development of
fully electrically fueled dissipative self-assembly systems that operate without generating
chemical waste. By integrating complementary anodic and cathodic reaction cycles, both the
activation and deactivation processes were driven electrochemically (J. Am. Chem. Soc. 2023,
145, 3727–3735).
This closed-loop design enabled sustained nonequilibrium operation under constant electrical
input, resulting in stable homeostatic states and long-term cycling without degradation. This
achievement addressed a central limitation of chemically fueled dissipative assemblies and
represents a milestone in sustainable active materials design.
2.5 Integration of Multiple Energy Inputs for Active Materials

To further expand the functional landscape of dissipative materials, we developed systems
powered by multiple orthogonal energy inputs, including light and electricity. Dual-fuel
operation enabled enhanced control over assembly dynamics and morphology, demonstrating
that complex nonequilibrium behavior can be programmed through coordinated energy inputs
(Chem. Eu. J. 2023, 29, e2023003).
Several collaborative efforts extended these concepts to living and bio-hybrid materials (J. Am.
Chem. Soc. 2023, 145, 1811–1817), nanostructured systems (J. Colloid Interface Sci. 2024, 666,
629–638), theoretical computation (J. Phys. Chem. B 2024, 128, 1266–1274), and advanced
multiscale characterization (Chem. Sci. 2024, 15, 1106–1116; ACS Nano 2024, 18, 11898–
11909), underscoring the generality and broad applicability of the principles established in this
program.
3. Impact and Significance
This project has made substantial contributions to the emerging field of synthetic active materials
by establishing sustainable strategies for dissipative self-assembly and demonstrating electrical
energy as a clean and programmable fuel. The work advances DOE BES priorities by providing
fundamental insight into how energy can be converted, dissipated, and regulated in soft matter
systems far from equilibrium. The concepts developed here have broad implications for adaptive
materials, soft robotics, bioelectronics, and energy-responsive interfaces.
4. Conclusions and Future Outlook
Over the course of this award, we established a unified conceptual and experimental framework
for chemically and electrically fueled dissipative self-assembly. The progression from short-lived
chemically fueled systems to fully electrically fueled, waste-free active materials represents a
major advance in nonequilibrium materials science. Future directions include integration with
enzymatic redox networks, more complex feedback-controlled systems, and scalable
electrochemical architectures, positioning redox-driven active materials as a platform technology
for next-generation adaptive soft matter.
This project established a lasting scientific legacy by introducing redox and electrical energy as
general, sustainable fuels for dissipative self-assembly in soft matter systems. Beyond the
specific material platforms developed, the work fundamentally reframed active materials as
reaction-network–regulated systems whose structure, dynamics, and function are governed by
energy flow rather than equilibrium constraints. The demonstration of electrically fueled, waste-
free, and programmable nonequilibrium assemblies represents a conceptual advance that bridges
chemistry, materials science, and energy science. These principles are expected to influence
future research directions in adaptive materials, nonequilibrium polymer science, and energy-
coupled soft matter, providing a durable foundation for subsequent DOE-supported efforts in far-
from-equilibrium materials design.
