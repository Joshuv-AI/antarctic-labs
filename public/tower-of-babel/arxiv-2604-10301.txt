1.
Introduction

Let
\mathcal{A}
denote the class of analytic functions
f
in the unit disk

\mathbb{D}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<1\}

normalized by

f(0)=0
and
f^{\prime}(0)=1
.
Thus each function
f\in\mathcal{A}
has a power series representation such that

f(z)=z+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n},\qquad z\in\mathbb{D}.

Let
\mathcal{S}
denote the subclass of
\mathcal{A}
consisting of univalent functions.
A function
f\in\mathcal{A}
is said to be starlike if
f(\mathbb{D})
is a starlike domain. We denote by
\mathcal{S}^{*}
the class of starlike functions
f
in
\mathcal{S}
. It is well known that a function
f\in\mathcal{A}
belongs to
\mathcal{S}^{*}
if, and only if,

\operatorname{Re}\!\left(\frac{zf^{\prime}(z)}{f(z)}\right)>0,\qquad z\in\mathbb{D}.

Further, a domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}
is called convex if the line segment joining any two points of
\Omega
lies entirely in
\Omega
. A function
f\in\mathcal{A}
is called convex if
f(\mathbb{D})
is a convex domain. We denote by
\mathcal{C}
the class of convex functions in
\mathcal{S}
. A function
f\in\mathcal{A}
belongs to
\mathcal{C}
if, and only if,

\operatorname{Re}\!\left(1+\frac{zf^{\prime\prime}(z)}{f^{\prime}(z)}\right)>0,\qquad z\in\mathbb{D}.

These classical families and their geometric consequences form a central part of geometric function theory; see, for instance, Goodman
[
5
]
.

In order to unify and generalize many well-known subclasses of starlike and convex functions, Ma and Minda
[
13
]
introduced a systematic approach based on subordination. Let
\varphi
be analytic in
\mathbb{D}
with
\operatorname{Re}\,\varphi(z)>0
, normalized by
\varphi(0)=1
and
\varphi^{\prime}(0)>0
, and assume that
\varphi(\mathbb{D})
is symmetric with respect to the real axis and starlike with respect to the point
1
. Then a function
f\in\mathcal{A}
is said to belong to the Ma–Minda starlike class
\mathcal{S}^{*}(\varphi)
if, and only if,

\frac{zf^{\prime}(z)}{f(z)}\prec\varphi(z),\qquad z\in\mathbb{D},

and to the Ma–Minda convex class
\mathcal{C}(\varphi)
if, and only if,

1+\frac{zf^{\prime\prime}(z)}{f^{\prime}(z)}\prec\varphi(z),\qquad z\in\mathbb{D}.

The Ma–Minda framework contains many important subclasses as special cases and provides a flexible setting for studying coefficient problems, radius problems, and extremal questions, (see
[
6
,
13
]
).

A substantial amount of work has been devoted to Ma–Minda type classes associated with specific target regions such as conic domains, vertical strip domains, cardioid-type regions, and domains generated by special functions. For example, conic regions and
k
-uniform convexity were investigated by Kanas and Wisniowska in
[
7
]
, cardioid-type starlikeness was studied by Kumar and Kamaljeet in
[
8
]
and by Sharma, Jain and Ravichandran in
[
18
]
, and several subclasses connected with vertical strip domains were examined by Sun, Wang, Rasila and Sokoł in
[
20
]
, by Kwon and Sim in
[
11
]
, and by Mahzoon and Sokoł in
[
14
]
. Further examples of Ma–Minda type starlike families associated with special mappings include several authors (see
[
4
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
15
]
,
[
21
]
).

Along with geometric properties, coefficient functionals play an important role in the study of these classes. Among them, Hankel determinants provide refined information about the growth and interaction of the Taylor coefficients. For a function

f\in\mathcal{S},

the second Hankel determinant is defined by

H_{2}(2)=a_{2}a_{4}-a_{3}^{2},

and the third Hankel determinant is given by

H_{3}(1)=\begin{vmatrix}1&a_{2}&a_{3}\\
a_{2}&a_{3}&a_{4}\\
a_{3}&a_{4}&a_{5}\end{vmatrix}.

The systematic study of Hankel determinants for univalent functions goes back to Pommerenke
[
16
]
. Sharp bounds for
H_{2}(2)
and
H_{3}(1)
have subsequently been obtained for several subclasses. For instance, sharp estimates for both the second and third Hankel determinants were derived for the class
\mathcal{S}_{L}
by Banga and Kumar
[
2
]
, and radius problems in
\mathcal{S}_{L}
were investigated by Sokoł
[
19
]
. In addition, inverse coefficient problems and related coefficient estimates form a classical theme in the theory of convex and
(\alpha,\beta)
-convex functions; see Libera and Złotkiewicz
[
12
]
and Prokhorov and Szynal
[
17
]
. Recent contributions to Hankel-type problems for inverse and logarithmic inverse coefficients may be found in Allu and Shaji
[
1
]
. We also mention that sharper coefficient control inside the Carathéodory class has been studied, for example, via higher coefficient problems in Kwon, Lecko and Sim
[
10
]
, which is useful in handling extremal questions involving
a_{4}
and
a_{5}
.

Motivated by these developments,in this paper we study the Ma–Minda convex subclass generated by

\varphi(z)=1+z+\frac{z^{2}}{4}.

For functions

f(z)=z+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}

belonging to this class, we obtain sharp estimates for the second Hankel determinant
H_{2}(2)
and the third Hankel determinant
H_{3}(1)
.

2.
Certain properties of the class
\mathcal{C}(\varphi)

In this section we prove that the function
\varphi(z)

satisfy all the properties for Ma-Minda function and we also provide the integral representation for the functions in the class
\mathcal{C}(\varphi).
It is easy to see that
\varphi(0)=1
and
\varphi^{\prime}(0)>0
, and that
\varphi
is symmetric with respect to the real axis. Since the domain under consideration is the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
, for
z\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}
we have

\left|1+\frac{z}{2}\right|\leq 1+\frac{|z|}{2}\leq\frac{3}{2}

and

\left|1+\frac{z}{2}\right|\geq 1-\frac{|z|}{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}.

Hence

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\;\leq\left|\left(1+\frac{z}{2}\right)^{2}\right|\;\leq\frac{9}{4}\qquad\text{for all }z\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}.

(2.1)

To check the univalency of
\varphi(z)
, assume
\varphi(z_{1})=\varphi(z_{2})
. Then

\left(1+\frac{z_{1}}{2}\right)^{2}=\left(1+\frac{z_{2}}{2}\right)^{2}.

Hence

1+\frac{z_{1}}{2}=\pm\left(1+\frac{z_{2}}{2}\right).

In the first case, we have

1+\frac{z_{1}}{2}=1+\frac{z_{2}}{2},

which implies
z_{1}=z_{2}.

While in the second case, we have

1+\frac{z_{1}}{2}=-\left(1+\frac{z_{2}}{2}\right),

which implies

1+\frac{z_{1}}{2}=-1-\frac{z_{2}}{2},\qquad\text{hence}\qquad z_{1}+z_{2}=-4.

However, for
z_{1},

z_{2}\in\mathbb{D},
we have

|z_{1}+z_{2}|\leq|z_{1}|+|z_{2}|<2,

which contradicts
z_{1}+z_{2}=-4
. Therefore
\varphi(z)
is univalent in
\mathbb{D}
.

To verify the starlikeness of the function, recall that

\displaystyle\varphi(z)=\left(1+\frac{z}{2}\right)^{2}.

Then, we have

\displaystyle\varphi(0)=1,\qquad\varphi^{\prime}(z)=1+\frac{z}{2}.

To verify that
\varphi
is starlike with respect to
\varphi(0)=1
, we show that

\operatorname{Re}\!\left(\frac{z\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)-1}\right)>0\quad\text{for all }z\in\mathbb{D}.

Since,

\varphi(z)-1=z+\frac{z^{2}}{4},

we obtain,

\frac{z\,\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)-1}=\frac{z(1+z/2)}{z+z^{2}/4}=\frac{1+z/2}{1+z/4}.

Therefore, writing
z=re^{i\theta}
with
0\leq r<1
, we get

\operatorname{Re}\,\left(\frac{1+z/2}{1+z/4}\right)=\frac{\operatorname{Re}\!\left((1+z/2)\left(1+\overline{z}/4\right)\right)}{|1+z/4|^{2}}=\frac{1+\frac{3}{4}\operatorname{Re}z+\frac{1}{8}|z|^{2}}{|1+z/4|^{2}}.

Moreover, since
\operatorname{Re}z\geq-|z|
, we get

1+\frac{3}{4}\operatorname{Re}z+\frac{1}{8}|z|^{2}\geq 1-\frac{3}{4}|z|+\frac{1}{8}|z|^{2}>1-\frac{3}{4}+\frac{1}{8}=\frac{3}{8}>0,

and therefore

\operatorname{Re}\!\left(\frac{z\,\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{\varphi(z)-1}\right)=\operatorname{Re}\!\left(\frac{1+z/2}{1+z/4}\right)>0\quad\text{for all }z\in\mathbb{D}.

This shows that the function is starlike with respect to the point
\varphi(0)=1
.

Now we verify that
\operatorname{Re}\,\varphi(z)>0
in the
\mathbb{D}
.
Let
z=x+iy
with
x^{2}+y^{2}<1
.
Set

w=1+\frac{z}{2}=a+ib,\qquad a=1+\frac{x}{2},\quad b=\frac{y}{2}.

Then, we have

\varphi(z)=w^{2}=(a+ib)^{2}=(a^{2}-b^{2})+i(2ab),

so

\operatorname{Re}\,\varphi(z)=a^{2}-b^{2}=\left(1+\frac{x}{2}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{y}{2}\right)^{2}.

Since
|z|<1
, we have

x^{2}+y^{2}<1,

hence

y^{2}<1-x^{2}.

Therefore, we have

\operatorname{Re}\,\varphi(z)>\left(1+\frac{x}{2}\right)^{2}-\frac{1-x^{2}}{4},\quad\text{for}\quad z\in\mathbb{D}.

A simple computation shows that

\left(1+\frac{x}{2}\right)^{2}=1+x+\frac{x^{2}}{4},

so

\left(1+\frac{x}{2}\right)^{2}-\frac{1-x^{2}}{4}=\frac{3}{4}+x+\frac{x^{2}}{2}.

Completing the square gives

\frac{x^{2}}{2}+x+\frac{3}{4}=\frac{1}{2}(x+1)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\geq\frac{1}{4}.

Consequently, we have

\operatorname{Re}\,\varphi(z)>\frac{1}{4}\qquad\text{for }z\in\mathbb{D}.

Therefore,
\varphi(z)
satisfies all the conditions of a Ma–Minda function.

We denote by
\mathcal{C}(\varphi)
the class of Ma–Minda convex functions subordinate to
\varphi(z).

i.e.
,

\mathcal{C}(\varphi):=\left\{\,f\in\mathcal{A}:1+z\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(z)}{f^{\prime}(z)}\prec\varphi(z)\,\right\}.

Now we provide the integral representation of the functions in the class
\mathcal{C}(\varphi).

Let
f\in\mathcal{C}(\varphi)
, then

\displaystyle 1+\frac{zf^{\prime\prime}(z)}{f^{\prime}(z)}\prec\varphi(z).

(2.2)

By subordination, there exists a Schwarz function
w:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
,
analytic with
w(0)=0
and
|w(z)|<1
, such that

\displaystyle 1+\frac{zf^{\prime\prime}(z)}{f^{\prime}(z)}

\displaystyle=\varphi(w(z)),\qquad z\in\mathbb{D}.

(2.3)

Therefore

\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(z)}{f^{\prime}(z)}=\frac{\varphi(w(z))-1}{z}.

Since
f^{\prime}(0)=1
and
f^{\prime}(z)\neq 0
in
\mathbb{D}
, an analytic branch of
\log f^{\prime}(z)
exists in
\mathbb{D}
. Thus

(\log f^{\prime}(z))^{\prime}=\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(z)}{f^{\prime}(z)}=\frac{\varphi(w(z))-1}{z}.

Integrating from
0
to
z
, we obtain the general integral representation

\displaystyle\log f^{\prime}(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{z}\frac{\varphi(w(t))-1}{t}\,dt,

(2.4)

\displaystyle f(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{z}\exp\!\left(\int_{0}^{\zeta}\frac{\varphi(w(t))-1}{t}\,dt\right)\,d\zeta.

(2.5)

In particular, if we choose the special Schwarz function
w(t)=t
, then we obtain the distinguished function
\widetilde{f}
given by

\displaystyle\log\widetilde{f}^{\prime}(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{z}\frac{\varphi(t)-1}{t}\,dt,

(2.6)

\displaystyle\widetilde{f}(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{z}\exp\!\left(\int_{0}^{\zeta}\frac{\varphi(t)-1}{t}\,dt\right)\,d\zeta.

(2.7)

3.
Hankel determinants

In 1966, Pommerenke
[
16
]
introduced the Hankel determinants for the class
\mathcal{S}
, and this topic has subsequently been studied by many authors. For a function
f\in\mathcal{A}
with coefficients
\{a_{n}\}
, the
q
th Hankel determinant is defined by

H_{q}(n)=\begin{vmatrix}a_{n}&a_{n+1}&\cdots&a_{n+q-1}\\
a_{n+1}&a_{n+2}&\cdots&a_{n+q}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{n+q-1}&a_{n+q}&\cdots&a_{n+2q-2}\end{vmatrix}\qquad q,n\in\mathbb{N}.

(3.1)

For special choices of
n
and
q
, one may obtain well-known functionals, for instance, the classical Fekete–Szegö functional, is defined as

\displaystyle H_{2}(2)=a_{2}a_{4}-a_{3}^{\,2}.

(3.2)

For our case
a_{1}=1
, the third Hankel determinant is

H_{3}(1)=\begin{vmatrix}1&a_{2}&a_{3}\\
a_{2}&a_{3}&a_{4}\\
a_{3}&a_{4}&a_{5}\end{vmatrix}=a_{3}(a_{2}a_{4}-a_{3}^{2})-a_{4}(a_{4}-a_{2}a_{3})+a_{5}(a_{3}-a_{2}^{2}).

(3.3)

Let
\mathcal{P}
denote the class of analytic functions
p
defined on
\mathbb{D}
such that

p(z)=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}p_{n}z^{n}

(3.4)

with
\operatorname{Re}p(z)>0
in
\mathbb{D}
. The following lemma
(see
[
12
,
10
]
) will be useful for the next proof.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
p\in\mathcal{P}
be given by (
3.4
) with
p_{1}\geq 0
. Then

\displaystyle 2p_{2}

\displaystyle=p_{1}^{2}+\gamma\bigl(4-p_{1}^{2}\bigr),

\displaystyle 4p_{3}

\displaystyle=p_{1}^{3}+2\bigl(4-p_{1}^{2}\bigr)p_{1}\gamma-\bigl(4-p_{1}^{2}\bigr)p_{1}\gamma^{2}+2\bigl(4-p_{1}^{2}\bigr)\bigl(1-|\gamma|^{2}\bigr)\eta,

\displaystyle 8p_{4}

\displaystyle=p_{1}^{4}+\bigl(4-p_{1}^{2}\bigr)\gamma\bigl(p_{1}^{2}(\gamma^{2}-3\gamma+3)+4\gamma\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad-4\bigl(4-p_{1}^{2}\bigr)\bigl(1-|\gamma|^{2}\bigr)\left(p_{1}(\gamma-1)\eta+\overline{\gamma}\,\eta^{2}-(1-|\eta|^{2})\rho\right),

for some
\gamma,\eta,\rho
such that
|\gamma|\leq 1
,
|\eta|\leq 1
, and
|\rho|\leq 1
.

Next, we recall the following well-known result by Choi
et al.

[
3
]
, which will be useful for our main theorem.

Lemma 3.2
.

[
3
]

Let
A,B,C\in\mathbb{R}
and define

Y(A,B,C):=\max_{z\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}}\Bigl(|A+Bz+Cz^{2}|+1-|z|^{2}\Bigr).

(i)

If
AC\geq 0
, then

Y(A,B,C)=\begin{cases}|A|+|B|+|C|,&\text{if }|B|\geq 2(1-|C|),\\[6.0pt]
1+|A|+\dfrac{B^{2}}{4(1-|C|)},&\text{if }|B|<2(1-|C|).\end{cases}

(ii)

If
AC<0
, then

Y(A,B,C)=\begin{cases}1-|A|+\dfrac{B^{2}}{4(1-|C|)},&\text{if }-4AC(C^{2}-1)\leq B^{2}\ \text{and }|B|<2(1-|C|),\\[8.0pt]
1+|A|+\dfrac{B^{2}}{4(1+|C|)},&\text{if }B^{2}<\min\!\bigl\{\,4(1+|C|)^{2},-4AC(C^{2}-1)\bigr\},\\[8.0pt]
R(A,B,C),&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

where,

R(A,B,C)=\begin{cases}|A|+|B|+|C|,&\text{if }|C|\bigl(|B|+4|A|\bigr)\leq|AB|,\\[6.0pt]
-|A|+|B|+|C|,&\text{if }|AB|\leq|C|\bigl(|B|-4|A|\bigr),\\[10.0pt]
\bigl(|A|+|C|\bigr)\sqrt{\,1-\dfrac{B^{2}}{4AC}\,},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

We now proceed to prove the main results of this paper.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{C}(\varphi)
. Then

|H_{2}(2)|\leq{1}/{36}.

The estimate is sharp.

Proof.
Let
f\in\mathcal{C}(\varphi)
be given by

1+z\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(z)}{f^{\prime}(z)}\prec\varphi(z),\qquad z\in\mathbb{D}.

To determine the second Hankel determinant, we compute

\displaystyle H_{2}(2)=a_{2}a_{4}-a_{3}^{2}.

(3.5)

We first note that the class
\mathcal{C}(\varphi)
is invariant under rotations. Indeed, if

f_{\theta}(z):=e^{-i\theta}f(e^{i\theta}z),

then
f_{\theta}\in\mathcal{C}(\varphi)
, and

H_{2}(2;f_{\theta})=e^{2i(2)\theta}H_{2}(2;f)=e^{4i\theta}H_{2}(2;f).

Hence
|H_{2}(2;f_{\theta})|=|H_{2}(2;f)|
, and therefore, without loss of generality, we may assume that the first coefficient of the associated Carathéodory function is nonnegative real.
Since
f\in\mathcal{C}(\varphi)
, there exists a Schwarz function
\omega(z)
satisfying

\omega(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\omega_{k}z^{k}

such that

1+\frac{zf^{\prime\prime}(z)}{f^{\prime}(z)}=1+\omega(z)+\frac{1}{4}\omega(z)^{2}=\varphi(\omega(z)),\qquad z\in\mathbb{D}.

(3.6)

Let

\omega(z)=\frac{p(z)-1}{p(z)+1},\text{ where }p(z)=1+p_{1}z+p_{2}z^{2}+p_{3}z^{3}+p_{4}z^{4}+\cdots\in\mathcal{P}.

By the rotational property above, we may assume that
p_{1}\geq 0
. Since
p\in\mathcal{P}
, the Carathéodory estimate gives
|p_{1}|\leq 2
, and therefore

0\leq p_{1}\leq 2.

After substituting the expressions for
\omega(z)
,
p(z)
, and

f(z)=z+a_{2}z^{2}+a_{3}z^{3}+a_{4}z^{4}+a_{5}z^{5}+\cdots

into (
3.6
), and comparing coefficients of both sides, we obtain

\left.\begin{aligned} a_{2}&=\frac{p_{1}}{4},\\[6.0pt]
a_{3}&=\frac{1}{96}\left(p_{1}^{2}+8p_{2}\right),\\[6.0pt]
a_{4}&=\frac{-p_{1}^{3}+32p_{3}}{768},\\[6.0pt]
a_{5}&=\frac{11p_{1}^{4}-48p_{1}^{2}p_{2}-64p_{1}p_{3}-96p_{2}^{2}+768p_{4}}{30720}.\end{aligned}\right\}

(3.7)

Substituting (
3.7
) into (
3.5
), we obtain

\displaystyle 2304\,H_{2}(2)=-p_{1}^{4}-4p_{1}^{2}p_{2}-16p_{2}^{2}+24p_{1}p_{3}.

(3.8)

We now apply the parametric representation from Lemma
3.1
, expressing
p_{2}
and
p_{3}
in terms of
p_{1},\gamma,\eta
, namely

2p_{2}=p_{1}^{2}+\gamma(4-p_{1}^{2}),

and

4p_{3}=p_{1}^{3}+2(4-p_{1}^{2})p_{1}\gamma-(4-p_{1}^{2})p_{1}\gamma^{2}+2(4-p_{1}^{2})(1-|\gamma|^{2})\eta.

Substituting these into (
3.8
), we obtain

\displaystyle 2304\,H_{2}(2)

\displaystyle=-p_{1}^{4}-2p_{1}^{2}\bigl(p_{1}^{2}+\gamma(4-p_{1}^{2})\bigr)-4\bigl(p_{1}^{2}+\gamma(4-p_{1}^{2})\bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+6p_{1}\Bigl(p_{1}^{3}+2\gamma p_{1}(4-p_{1}^{2})-\gamma^{2}p_{1}(4-p_{1}^{2})+2\eta(1-|\gamma|^{2})(4-p_{1}^{2})\Bigr).

(3.9)

Further, we can write

2304\,H_{2}(2)=\Phi(p_{1},\gamma,\eta),

where
|\gamma|\leq 1
,
|\eta|\leq 1
, and
\Phi
is of the form

\displaystyle\Phi(p_{1},\gamma,\eta)=A+B\gamma+C\gamma^{2}+\eta D_{0}(1-|\gamma|^{2}),

(3.10)

with

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=-p_{1}^{4},

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=-2p_{1}^{2}(-4+p_{1}^{2})=2p_{1}^{2}(4-p_{1}^{2}),

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=2(-32+4p_{1}^{2}+p_{1}^{4}),

and

\displaystyle D_{0}:=12p_{1}(4-p_{1}^{2})\geq 0.

(3.11)

We separate the endpoint cases
p_{1}=0
and
p_{1}=2
.

If
p_{1}=0
, then (
3
) gives

2304\,H_{2}(2)\big|_{p_{1}=0}=-64\,\gamma^{2},

and hence

|2304\,H_{2}(2)|\leq 64.

(3.12)

If
p_{1}=2
, then (
3
) gives

2304\,H_{2}(2)\big|_{p_{1}=2}=-16,\qquad\text{that is,}\qquad H_{2}(2)\big|_{p_{1}=2}=-\frac{1}{144}.

In particular,

|2304\,H_{2}(2)|=16<64.

(3.13)

We now consider the remaining case

0<p_{1}<2.

Then
D_{0}>0
, and for fixed
p_{1}
and
\gamma
, if we write

Z(\gamma):=A+B\gamma+C\gamma^{2},

we have

\max_{|\eta|\leq 1}\bigl|Z(\gamma)+\eta D_{0}(1-|\gamma|^{2})\bigr|=|Z(\gamma)|+D_{0}(1-|\gamma|^{2}).

Therefore

\displaystyle\max_{|\gamma|\leq 1,\,|\eta|\leq 1}|2304\,H_{2}(2)|

\displaystyle=D_{0}\max_{|\gamma|\leq 1}\Bigl(|\widetilde{A}+\widetilde{B}\gamma+\widetilde{C}\gamma^{2}|+1-|\gamma|^{2}\Bigr),

(3.14)

where

\widetilde{A}:=\frac{A}{D_{0}},\qquad\widetilde{B}:=\frac{B}{D_{0}},\qquad\widetilde{C}:=\frac{C}{D_{0}}.

In particular,

\widetilde{A}=\frac{-p_{1}^{4}}{12p_{1}(4-p_{1}^{2})},\qquad\widetilde{B}=\frac{p_{1}}{6},\qquad\widetilde{C}=-\frac{8+p_{1}^{2}}{6p_{1}}.

Since
0<p_{1}<2
, these are all real. Moreover,

\displaystyle\widetilde{A}\widetilde{C}=\frac{p_{1}^{2}(8+p_{1}^{2})}{72(4-p_{1}^{2})}\geq 0,

so Lemma
3.2
(i) applies.

Next, for
0<p_{1}<2
we have

|\widetilde{C}|=\frac{8+p_{1}^{2}}{6p_{1}}>1,

because

8+p_{1}^{2}-6p_{1}=(p_{1}-2)(p_{1}-4)>0\qquad\text{for }0<p_{1}<2.

Hence

2(1-|\widetilde{C}|)<0,

and therefore the condition

|\widetilde{B}|\geq 2(1-|\widetilde{C}|)

is satisfied. Thus, by lemma
3.2
(i), we obtain

\displaystyle\max_{|\gamma|\leq 1,\,|\eta|\leq 1}|2304\,H_{2}(2)|

\displaystyle=D_{0}\bigl(|\widetilde{A}|+|\widetilde{B}|+|\widetilde{C}|\bigr)

\displaystyle=|A|+|B|+|C|.

(3.15)

Now, for

We now use the fact that for
(p,x)\in[0,1]\times[0,1]
,

B_{i}^{6}(p)\geq 0,\qquad B_{j}^{4}(x)\geq 0,

and

\sum_{i=0}^{6}\sum_{j=0}^{4}B_{i}^{6}(p)B_{j}^{4}(x)=1.

Hence
G_{2}(p,x)
is a convex combination of the Bernstein coefficients. Therefore,

\min_{0\leq i\leq 6,\;0\leq j\leq 4}c_{ij}\leq G_{2}(p,x)\leq\max_{0\leq i\leq 6,\;0\leq j\leq 4}c_{ij}

for all
(p,x)\in[0,1]\times[0,1]
.
From the coefficient matrix above, we see that

\max_{0\leq i\leq 6,\;0\leq j\leq 4}c_{ij}=\frac{1022}{3}.

Consequently,

G_{2}(p_{1},x)\leq\frac{1022}{3}\qquad\text{for all }0\leq p_{1}\leq 1,\ 0\leq x\leq 1.

Thus the Bernstein method gives the upper bound

\max_{0\leq p_{1}\leq 1,\;0\leq x\leq 1}G_{2}(p_{1},x)\leq\frac{1022}{3}\leq 480.

Hence, exhausting all the cases, we have

69120\,|H_{3}(1)|\leq 480.

Therefore,

\displaystyle|H_{3}(1)|\leq\frac{480}{69120}=\frac{1}{144}.

Finally, we show that
\omega(z)=z^{3}
provides an extremal function for the third Hankel determinant. In this case,

1+z\frac{\widetilde{f}^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\widetilde{f}^{\prime}(z)}=\varphi(z^{3})=1+z^{3}+\frac{z^{6}}{4}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\widetilde{f}(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{z}\exp\!\left(\int_{0}^{\zeta}\frac{\varphi(t^{3})-1}{t}\,dt\right)\,d\zeta,

\displaystyle\widetilde{f}(z)

\displaystyle=z+\frac{1}{12}z^{4}+\frac{1}{72}z^{7}+\ldots.

It is easy to check that this
\widetilde{f}
gives
|H_{3}(1)|=\dfrac{1}{144}
, which proves the sharpness.
