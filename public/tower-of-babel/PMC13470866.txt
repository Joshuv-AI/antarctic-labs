Isolation and Characterization of Unusual Terpene Derivatives from the Fungus Echinodontium tinctorium
Authors: Mehreen Zeb, Muhammad Abubakar Sideeq, Kalindi D. Morgan, Wai-Ming Li, Victor P. Liu, Brian O. Patrick, Zhicheng Xia, Kerry Reimer, Chow H. Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The North American fungus Echinodontium tinctorium has not been explored for its small-molecule constituents for 60 years. Herein, we describe chemical investigation of its organic extracts and bioactivity-guided fractionation, which led to the isolation of two new diterpenoids, echinodin A (1) and B (2). Both 1 and 2 contain an unprecedented tetracyclic 5/3/7/5 carbon ring system with the highly constrained cyclopropane motif. Bis­(2,4-dihydroxy-6-methylphenyl) methane (4) was isolated for the first time in a natural source together with four other known compounds (3, 5–7). The structures of 1–7 were determined by a combination of NMR, ESI-MS, and single-crystal X-ray diffraction analyses. Cytotoxic assays revealed that 1, 2, and 4 have weak cytotoxic activity against human cervical adenocarcinoma (HeLa) and human glioblastoma (U251). Echinodol (5) and echinodone (6), investigated for the first time for their bioactivity, exhibited strong cytotoxic activity with IC50 values ranging from 1.2 to 5.5 μM against a panel of human cancer cell lines. A mechanism of action investigation showed that echinodol (5) induced apoptosis and arrested the cell cycle at the S phase in U251 cells. These findings not only highlight the new chemistry and biology of isolated compounds but also position E. tinctorium as a source for further pharmacognostic exploration.

Introduction

Echinodontium tinctorium is a wood-decay fungus that forms a hoof-shaped conk, and it is usually found on true firs and hemlocks in Western North America. It has a distinct orange-red interior color, and hence, it is commonly used by Indigenous people in preparing red paint pigments. The genus Echinodontium currently comprises three common species: E. tinctorium, E. tsugicola, and E. ryvardenii. Despite its uniqueness to the Pacific Northwest, E. tinctorium has been relatively unexplored for its bioactive constituents prior to our investigations.

British Columbia (BC), Canada, has unique land and climate systems that yield some of the richest and most biodiverse regions in North America. To this end, it is home to a large diversity of basidiomycetes that have largely unexplored bioactive constituents. We have begun to explore basidiomycetes found in north-central BC for their potential medicinal properties and found that many species possess previously unreported anti-inflammatory, immunostimulatory, and antiproliferative activities. , We have previously found E. tinctorium to contain polysaccharides with anti-inflammatory, immunostimulatory, and antiproliferative activities, which led us to coin E. tinctorium as a medicinal basidiomycete. − To date, only two small molecules have been isolated from E. tinctorium, and it is still unknown whether they possess any bioactivities. , Given that E. tinctorium has not been explored for its small-molecule constituents for 60 years, we hypothesized that this unique medicinal basidiomycete possesses natural product small molecules that are yet to be discovered. Herein, we describe the isolation and characterization of two new diterpenoids with a tetracyclic 5/3/7/5 ring system, named echinodin A (1) and echinodin B (2), along with five known compounds (3–7) from E. tinctorium (Figure ). Bis­(2,4-dihydroxy-6-methylphenyl)­methane (4) was isolated for the first time in nature. We showed for the first time that echinodol (5) and echinodone (6) have strong cytotoxicity activity against cancer cells in vitro. We further showed that 5 was capable of inducing apoptosis and cell-cycle arrest in U251 human glioblastoma cells.

Results and Discussion

Bioassay-Guided Separation of Cytotoxic Compounds

The MeOH extract of E. tinctorium showed promising cytotoxic activity against HeLa human cervical cancer cell lines and was sequentially phase-separated using hexane, chloroform (CHCl3), and ethyl acetate (EtOAc). All three phase-separated extracts, hexane, CHCl3, and EtOAc, showed strong cytotoxic activity against HeLa cells (Figure S1). The EtOAc extract had the highest yield (38%) and was selected for fractionation using silica gel column chromatography. HPLC-ESI-MS analysis of pooled bioactive EtOAc-eluted fractions (fr.) 16–18 (Figure S2) revealed the characteristic ions of four major compounds (Figure a). Subsequent sequential separation led to the isolation of two new compounds, designated as echinodin A (1) and echinodin B (2). In parallel experiments, the phase-separated EtOAc extract was first subjected to silica gel column chromatography followed by Sephadex LH-20 chromatography. Bioactive fractions from Sephadex LH-20 chromatography with cytotoxic activity (Figure S3) were pooled and subjected to HPLC purification (Figure b), which led to the isolation of orcinol (3) and bis­(2, 4-dihydroxy-6-methylphenyl)­methane (4). It is important to note that this is the first report of the isolation of 4 from a natural source because, to date, it has been only synthetically described.

Structure Elucidation of Isolated Compounds

Echinodin A (1) was isolated as a white powder. The HR–ESI–MS spectra (Figure S5) of 1 showed an ion at m/z of 507.2731 [M + Na]+ [calculated 507.2722; observed 507.2731; Δm = −0.0009 (−1.77 ppm)] consistent with the molecular formula C29H40O6 (monoisotopic mass of 484.2824). This corresponds to 10 degrees of unsaturation and was further supported by NMR data (Figures S7–S14). The FTIR spectrum of 1 showed peak stretches at 3391 and 2930 cm–1 for OH and CH functionalities (Figure S6). Its 1H (Figure S7), 13C (Figure S8), 1H–13C HSQC (Figure S9), DEPT-135 (Figure S10), 1H–1H COSY (Figures S12 and S13), 1H–13C HMBC (Figure S14), 1H–13C HSQC-TOCSY (Figure S15), and 1H–1H NOESY (Figure S16) NMR spectra were used to generate the NMR assignments shown in Table . 1 was found to be a diterpenoid containing an unusual internal cyclopropyl ring fused with a 7-membered ring. It contains four cyclic rings (5/3/7/5) in its structure alongside one aromatic ring, two alcohols, two ester functional groups, and six terminal methyl groups. In the aromatic ring, H-25 and H-29 protons (δH 7.91) and H-26 and H-28 (δH 7.51) were both integrated for two protons separately (Figure S11), indicating symmetry in the aromatic region. COSY correlations between all aromatic protons and total integration of 5H confirmed monosubstitution (Figures and S17). Aromatic proton H-25 (δH 7.91) demonstrated HMBC correlations into an ester carbon C-23 (δC 164.1). In ring A, both H-12 (δH 5.01) and H-13 (δH 5.21) protons appear as doublets with a J coupling value of approximately 7.06 Hz, showing strong COSY correlations with each other, as well as HMBC correlations to the neighboring carbon signals (C-13 at δC 70.1 and C-12 at δC 81.1). On HMBC, the H-13 proton (δH 5.21) correlated with ester carbon (C-23), while the H-12 proton (δH 5.01) correlated with carbonyl carbon C-22 (δc 170.1). A terminal methyl group H-21 (δH 1.77) only showed strong HMBC correlation to C-22, thus confirming its spot right next to carbonyl carbon (Figure 2 1a, S14) and confirming the connection of methyl ester into 5-membered ring A as demonstrated by the NMR data. Key HMBC and COSY correlations of protons included in the ring system A are summarized in Figure and Tables S1 and S2.

In 7-membered ring C, the methylene proton (δH 2.13) attached to the carbon C-2 (δC 29.2) showed strong COSY correlation with the H-3 proton (δH 3.85) connected to carbon (δC 74.4) on HSQC. The OH peak (δH 5.17) also showed COSY correlation with H-3 (δH 3.85), meaning that OH was directly attached to C-3 at δC 74.4. Moreover, terminal CH3 (H-20 at δH 1.04) is coupled with the H-4 proton (δH 1.34) through cross-COSY peaks (Figures and S13). The HSQC-TOCSY experiment revealed that the H-3 proton (δH 3.85) correlated with carbons C-2, C-20, and C-3 at δC 29.2, δC 14.9, and δC 74.4, respectively, indicating that they were in the same spin system (Figure S15). On HMBC, the H-4 proton (δH 1.34) correlated strongly with C-3 and C-5 at δC 74.4 and δC 86.1, respectively. On the other hand, the OH proton peak (δH 4.18) correlated with C-5 (δC 86.1) on HMBC, thus confirming its proximity to C-5 (Figure 2 1b). In the MeOH-d 4 solvent, H-3 (δH 3.99) exhibited a splitting pattern as a doublet of doublets instead of the expected triplet of doublets with a vicinal coupling constant of 2.12 (Figure S18). According to Napolitano et al., these coupling constants also depend on the dihedral angles of the rings. In the study, small coupling constants 0–3 Hz were also observed for the carbons bearing oxygen substituents. In our case, 1 also contains an OH group attached with C-3, giving an H-3 vicinal coupling constant of 2.12. Moreover, in 1, 0 coupling constant was observed for one of the methylene protons H-2 (at δH 1.39). X-ray diffraction analysis (Figure ) indicated that two hydroxyl groups within a seven-membered ring C engaged in hydrogen bonding, thereby imposing conformational constraints on the adjacent methine carbon C-3 resonating at δC 74.4. This interaction likely enforced a 90° dihedral angle between the protons H-3 and H-2 resonating at δH 3.85 and δH 1.39, respectively, resulting in an observed coupling constant of 0. This interpretation was further supported by COSY and NOESY data, which revealed coupling between the H-3 proton (δH 3.85) and H-2 (δH 2.13), but no COSY correlation was observed with the other H-2 proton at δH 1.39. NOESY analysis provided additional confirmation where the H-2 proton at δH 2.13 exhibited through-space connectivity with the isopropyl group attached to ring A, whereas the H-2 proton at 1.39 correlated spatially with the methine proton H-9 at δH 1.67 at the opposite terminus of the ring system C (Figure S19). Newmann projection of selected carbons (C-2, C-3) of 1 is shown in Figure S20. On NOESY NMR, H-3 (δH 3.85) correlated with protons at δH 1.34, δH 1.04, δH 5.17, δH 2.13, and δH 4.18, confirming it to be closed in space (Figures 2 1c and S16).

The deshielding of carbons at positions 4, 9, and 14 can be explained as follows. C-4 (δC 47.5) is deshielded by both the electron-withdrawing effects of the two adjacent carbinol centers at C-3 and C-5 along with ring strain. Although C-4 is a member of a 7-membered ring, this ring is unusually constrained by the adjacent 5-membered ring and internal 3-membered ring. C-9 (δC 46.9, ring-junction methine) and C-5 (δC 86.1, carbinol) are the ring-fusion carbons of the 5–7 system bearing typical chemical shifts of such a system. As determined from single-crystal X-ray diffraction (Figure ), the methine H-9 lies anti to 5-OH, defining the configuration at the ring fusion of the 5–7 system. C-14 (δC 58.4) is a congested branch-point methine bonded to the internal cyclopropane via C-1, an isopropyl group, and the chain bearing the phenyl ester. Its downfield shift is consistent with this high degree of carbon substitution.

In ring system D, the most complicated part of NMR analysis was the overlapping of methylene protons attached to C-6 and C-7 (δC 38.1 and δC 32.1, respectively), making it difficult to analyze the COSY data (Figure S21). The solvent was changed to CD3OD to see if a better resolution can be achieved and to further confirm the correlations. Although the MeOH-d 4 solvent helped confirm few correlations, there was still an issue of overlapping protons hindering our analysis of structure elucidation (Figure S22). Based on the confirmed correlation in DMSO-d 6, the H-10 proton (δH 1.80) in ring system B showed COSY correlation with the H-9 signal at 1.67 ppm (junction of ring systems C and D), thus confirming the joining of ring systems B and D. Similarly, the H-9 proton at δH 1.67 further showed COSY correlation with the H-8 (δH 2.11) proton attached to carbon at δC 34.9, placing C-9 at δC 46.9 in the middle of C-8 and C-10. H-8 (δH 2.11) is further coupled with the terminal methyl group H-19 at δH 1.13 (Figures and S17). C-10 (δC 23.6) was placed right next to quaternary carbon C-11 (δC34.2) because of HMBC correlations of H-10 with C-11, C-18, C-12, C-1, and C-14, confirming the fusion of rings A and B (Figure 1a). The H-10 proton (δH 1.80) further showed HMBC correlation to C-9 and C-5 to further support the COSY correlation and joining of ring B with D (Figure 1a). The final stage of structure determination required precise placement of the methylene carbons (δC 32.1 and δC 38.1) in ring system D. This analysis was challenged by significant signal overlapping in COSY data (Figures S21 and S22). On HMBC, H-8, H-9, and H-10 all correlate with C-5 (δC 86.1). Moreover, H-9 correlated with C-4 and H-4 correlated with C-9, meaning that C-5 (δC 86.1) is right next to methine carbon C-9. Furthermore, the OH peak (δH 4.18) connected to C-5 correlated with methylene carbon C-6 (δC 38.1), placing it right next to C-5, completing the four valences of the quaternary carbon and connecting the ring C with D (Figure 1a). Based on COSY and HMBC data, both methylene carbons C-6 and C-7 were right next to each other in ring D, and on HMBC, methyl protons H-19 correlated with methylene carbon C-7 and methine carbon C-8 to complete a 5-membered ring D (Figure 1a). Finally, connecting all the fragments resulted in the proposed structure of echinodin A (1). 1 dissolved in MeOH produced a high-quality crystal at 4 °C and it was successfully subjected to single-crystal X-ray diffraction using Cu Kα radiation (Figure 3, CCDC 2529390), thus confirming the skeleton and absolute configuration (1S,3S,4S,5R,8S,9R,10R,11S,12S,13R,14S) of 1.

Echinodin B (2) was isolated as a white powder and assigned the molecular formula C27H38O4 based on the HRESIMS (Figure S23) with an ion peak at m/z 449.2671 [M + Na]+ (calculated for 449.2668; Δm = 0.0003 (0.67 ppm); monoisotopic mass of 426.2770). The 1H and 13C NMR data of 2 closely resembled the data of 1 (Table , Figures S24–S25). Like 1, the NMR data supported the presence of a peak at 449 m/z, corresponding to a sodium adduct with an actual monoisotopic mass of 426.2770 and the molecular formula C27H38O4, requiring 9 degrees of unsaturation. 2D NMR data also revealed 2 to be structurally related to 1 with a loss of the acetoxy (−59) −OCOCH3 functional group from the carbon-12 position in ring A (Figure 2a, 2b, 2c). Notably, if the loss of ester was through acid hydrolysis, then, it should be replaced with an OH group, but NMR data did not show any OH group attached at the C-12 position. Such observation suggested that 2 was either a precursor of 1 in a biosynthetic pathway or was an artifact in the isolation process, rather than a degraded fragment of 1. 13C NMR of 2 also revealed one carbon peak missing at δC 170.1 (Figure S26), confirming the absence of the ester group and resulting in a methylene (−CH2−) group at the carbon-12 position (Figure 2a, 2b, 2c). The doublet of doublet pattern of the H-3 proton (δH 3.83) in DMSO-d 6 and δH 3.99 in MeOH-d 4 were also observed in 2 (Figure S27) as seen previously for 1 (Figure S18). Interpretation of 2D NMR spectra established the carbocyclic skeleton of 2 being identical to that of 1, except that the alkyl oxygen ester cleavage from the C-12 position in ring system A of 1 being replaced with a methylene group. Based on the absolute configuration of 1, the configuration of 2 is expected to be 1S,3S,4S,5R,8S,9R,10S,11S,13S,14S.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first example of diterpenoids with a tetracyclic 5/3/7/5 carbon ring system containing an unusual internally embedded cyclopropane ring. The closest scaffold to this can be found in naturally occurring premyrsinanes isolated from plant Euphorbia species, which contain a 5/7/6/3-tetracyclic carbon framework. , Hence, the new 5/3/7/5 ring scaffold reported in this study may have important implications in chemistry.

3 was identified by MS, IR, and NMR (Table S3) as orcinol, also called 5-methylresorcinol. Orcinol and its derivatives are found in lichens, fungi, and plants. , It is known for its antioxidant property; however, a recent study showed orcinol and its derivative to possess antibacterial activity. To the best of our knowledge, no reports exist concerning the occurrence of orcinol (3) in Echinodontium species; therefore, this is the first report on their existence in this genus.

Echinodol (5) is a colorless needle-shaped crystal, with the molecular formula C32H50O4 as determined from NMR data, X-ray crystallography, and ESI-MS ion peaks. The UV absorption maxima of 5 were obtained at 210 and 230 nm (Figure S37), and the MS spectrum (Figure S38) of 5 revealed an MS ion peak of m/z 439 [M + H]+. The IR spectrum of 5 (Figure S39) showed peak stretches at 2937 cm–1 indicative of sp3 C–H and a weak stretch at 3273 cm–1 for the hydroxy group and at 1738 cm–1 for the CO group. Based on the 1D NMR data, 5 possessed 32 carbons (Figures S40–S41) that included seven methylenes (δC 35.2, 27.5, 18.0, 26.7, 19.9, 29.7, and 35.3), nine methyl groups (δC 15.9, 18.3, 14.5, 24.8, 17.6, 27.4, 15.0, 27.1, and 20.0), a carbonyl (δC 170.2), seven quaternary carbons (δC 38.6, 133.9, 135.1, 37.0, 48.9, 43.5, and 37.2), eight methine groups (δC 50.3, 82.6, 53.2, 38.8, 77.7, 77.8, and 78.1), and vinylic carbon (δC 123.3). The H-32 acetyl protons (δH 1.96, 3H) were overlapped by the CD3CN solvent peak. Additionally, there was also a peak for a hydroxy proton (δH 2.49, 1H). For HMBC, long-range coupling was seen between carbons and protons where the following were observed: C-3 associated with H-29 and H-28, hydroxy proton correlated with C-28, C-11 correlated with H-17, C-16 correlated with H-15a and H-23, C-25 with correlated H-23 and both methyl protons of C-26 and C-27, C-24 correlated with H-22, and C-31 correlated with H-22 (Figures , S43 and Tables S6–S7). For COSY, correlations were observed between the hydroxy proton and H-2, H-1 and both H-1a and H-1b, H-17 and H-22, H-20 and H-22, H-22 and H-23, and H-23 and H-24 (Figures and S44, Tables S6–S7). NOESY analysis of 5 provided additional confirmation that correlations between H-17 and H-22 and between H-24 and H-26 were observed with through-space connectivity. Moreover, H-16 correlated with H-15a (δH 2.31) and H-23 (δH 3.97), confirming their spatial proximity (Figures and S45). 5 was crystallized using CH3CN and subjected to X-ray analysis. An ORTEP-style diagram generated from the analysis shown in Figure (CCDC 2529388) confirmed it to be echinodol. Echinodol (5) is a known lanostane-type triterpene acetate previously isolated together with its chemically modified derivatives from E. tinctorium. However, there were no full NMR data assignments or data on the MS, IR, and crystal structure of echinodol (5). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to report the full NMR data assignments and the crystal structure of echinodol (5).

6 was obtained as a colorless crystal and was later identified as echinodone. The HPLC DAD spectrum showed a 100% pure peak of 6 at 254 and 230 nm (Figure S46). ESI-MS (Figure S47) showed a small parent molecular ion peak with m/z 497.3 [M + H] and its fragment base peak with m/z 437.3 [M + H]+. The fragment at 437 is most likely the deacetylated form of 5. Most of the signals from the 1H NMR, 13C NMR, 1H–13C HSQC and HMBC, 1H–1H COSY, and NOESY of 6 (Figures S48–S53 and Table S8) resembled the NMR data of 5, indicating structural similarity of both compounds. 5 has a hydroxy at carbon 3, which is replaced by a carbonyl group in 6. Therefore, 6 has two carbonyl groups present, which was also confirmed from the 13C NMR data (Figures S47 and S48, Tables S6 and S8). 6 was crystallized 90% CH3CN, and an ORTEP diagram obtained from X-ray analysis is shown in Figure (CCDC 2529389). The combined NMR, ESI-MS, and X-ray data confirmed 6 to be echinodone, a known lanostane-type triterpenoid isolated from E. tsugicola fruiting bodies. Prior to this study, there is no report of any bioactivity from echinodone (6).

7 was obtained as a white powder and was later identified as deacetoxyechinodol. The HPLC spectrum of purified 7 is shown in Figure S54, and its ESI-MS (Figure S55) shows a molecular ion peak with m/z 441.0. 7 is a light-sensitive compound. 1D and 2D NMR further confirms 7 to be deacetoxyechinodol, a known compound previously isolated from E. tsugicola. Most of the 1H and 13C NMR signals of 7 resemble the NMR data of 5 and 6, suggesting a common core structure of terpenoid nucleus (Figures S56–S61, Tables S6 and S9). A total of 30 carbons were detected by 13C NMR. Furthermore, the NMR signals for the carbonyl group and a terminal methyl carbon that make up the acetyl group were absent.

Cytotoxic Activity of the Isolated Compounds

Echinodin A (1), echinodin B (2), bis­(2, 4-dihydroxy-6-methylphenyl)­methane (4), echinodol (5), and echinodone (6) were evaluated for cytotoxicity against human cancer cell lines. Doxorubicin used as a positive control has potent cytotoxic activity against human colon cancer cell lines (SW480 and HCT116), human glioblastoma cells (U251 and U87), and the human cervical cancer cell line (HeLa) with IC50 values ranging from 0.01 to 0.26 μM (Table ), which are within the range reported by others. − For the first time, we show that 5 has strong cytotoxic activity against HeLa, U251, U87, SW480, and HCT116 cancer cells with IC50 values of 1.2, 4.6, 5.4, 3.7, and 5.4 μM, respectively. Like doxorubicin, 5 also showed strong activity against two noncancerous cell lines, SVG human fetal glial cells and RIE-1 rat intestinal epithelial cells (Table ). 6, which is structurally similar to 5, also showed strong activity against the U251 cell line. 1, 2, and 4 showed relatively weak cytotoxic activity against HeLa (IC50 of 128–195 μM) and U251 cells (IC50 of 85.5–217 μM), and therefore, they were not further evaluated on the other cell lines (Table ). Interestingly, premyrsinanes, which have some structural similarity to 1 and 2, also showed weak to moderate cytotoxicity toward a number of cell lines. , Given that some premyrsinanes had demonstrated antiviral and neuroprotective activities, it would be of interest in the future to assess 1 and 2 for such potential bioactivities. ,

Compound 5 Induced Apoptosis and Arrested the Cell Cycle

5 was further evaluated for its cytotoxic mechanism against glioblastoma U251 used as a representative cell line. Flow cytometry analyses of apoptosis and the cell cycle of (5) are shown in Figures and , respectively. After 24 and 48 h treatment with 5 at 40 μM, there were 25 and 41% of apoptotic cells (total apoptosis), respectively, which are significantly more than 2% DMSO-treated cells (Figure ). The cell cycle analysis after treatment with 5 at 40 μM for 24 h showed only a slight increase in S-phase cells (Figure ). In contrast, there was a significant increase in cell population in the S-phase (Figure ) after treatment with 5 at 40 μM for 48 h. The overall results suggested that 5 induced cytotoxicity at least by inducing apoptosis as well as arresting cells at the S-phase.

In summary, two diterpenoids, three triterpenoids, one phenol, and one diphenylmethane derivative were isolated from the fruiting bodies of E. tinctorium. The two new diterpenoids, named echinodins A (1) and B (2), possess a previously unreported tetracyclic 5/3/7/5 carbon ring system. Tetracyclic compounds containing cyclopropane are considered naturally rare and have generated immense interest among medicinal and natural product chemists. , To this end, our discovery of echinodins containing a new structural motif that includes cyclopropane is expected to initiate bioinspired synthesis to obtain such naturally rare products. We provide the first full NMR assignments of two known triterpenoids, echinodol (5) and echinodone (6), and the first report of their cytotoxic activities against a panel of human cancer cell lines. Additionally, we showed that echinodol (5) mechanistically exerted its cytotoxic effect through induction of apoptosis and cell cycle arrest at the S phase. Lastly, bis­(2, 4-dihydroxy-6-methylphenyl)­methane (4) was purified from a natural source for the first time as described in this study.

Experimental Section

General Experimental Procedures

IR spectra were recorded with a Bruker ATR-FTIR spectrophotometer (Billerica, MA). 1D and 2D NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker AV-600 MHz spectrometer with a 5 mm CPTCI cryoprobe or a Bruker 400 MHz spectrometer (Bruker Biospin GmBH, Rheinstetten, Germany). Low-resolution mass spectrometry was performed on an Agilent 6120 Single Quad MS (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA). For high-resolution electrospray ionization mass spectrometry, an Agilent 1200 HPLC and a Bruker maXis Impact ultrahigh-resolution tandem TOF (UHR-Q-TOF) mass spectrometer were used at the Mass Spectrometry Facility, Simon Fraser University. For purification purposes, semipreparative HPLC separations were performed on an Agilent 1260 Infinity Systems with a diode array detector by using a Phenomenex Luna C18(2) column (10 mm × 250 mm, 100 Å, 5 μm). For the initial analytical experiments, a Phenomenex Luna phenyl-hexyl column (4.6 mm × 250 mm, 100 Å, 5 μm) was used.

HPLC-grade solvents, such as acetonitrile (ACN), methanol (MeOH), and ethyl acetate (EtOAC), were purchased from BDH (Mississauga, ON, Canada). All reagents were of analytical grade. Eagle’s minimal essential medium was from LONZA (Walkersville, Maryland). 3-(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2–5 diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) and dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). Fetal bovine serum (FBS) was from Life Technologies Inc. (Waltham, MA). Sephadex LH-20 resin was purchased from GE Healthcare (Chicago, IL).

Fungal Material

Fresh fruiting bodies of E. tinctorium were collected from hemlock trees in Terrace (CL103) and Smithers (CL37), BC, Canada, in August 2014 and 2015, respectively. These were used for the isolation of 3–7. For the isolation of 1 and 2, CL385 collected in West Twin Old Growth near McBride, BC, in September 2021 was used. Voucher specimens for these collections were deposited at the herbarium of the University of Northern British Columbia, Canada. The specimens were first identified using morphological analysis and then later confirmed by sequence analysis of the rDNA ITS2 region.

Extraction and Isolation

For the isolation of compounds 1 and 2, powdered E. tinctorium CL385 (350 g) was sequentially extracted with 80% EtOH (65 °C, 3 h) followed by extraction with 50% MeOH (65 °C, 3 h) to give dried crude extract E2 (4.1 g, yield: 1.17%). All of the E2 extract was resuspended in 300 mL of H2O and subjected to liquid–liquid extraction to separate the less polar compounds using n-hexane/H2O (3 × 100 mL) followed by CHCl3/H2O (3 × 100 mL) and EtOAc/H2O (5 × 100 mL). Finally, the top EtOAc layer was concentrated using a rotary evaporator and lyophilized to yield 1.41 g. Approximately 200 mg of the bioactive EtOAc extract was resuspended in 2 mL of MeOH and subjected to silica gel column chromatography (50 × 2.5 cm, 78 mL n-hexane). Fractions were collected by stepwise elution using the following mobile phases at a flow rate of 1 mL/min: n-hexane [1 mL × 20 fractions (fr.)], EtOAc (1 mL × 20 fr.), and MeOH (1 mL × 20 fr.). EtOAc-eluted bioactive fr. 16 to 18 (Figure S2) from seven silica gel column chromatography runs were pooled separately to yield a total of 560 mg. Pooled fr. 16–18 (194.4 mg) were dissolved in H2O/ACN (1:1) at a concentration of 4 mg/mL for injection (270 μL equivalent to 1 mg) to a Phenomenex Luna C-18 (10 mm × 250 mm, 5 μm) semipreparative HPLC-UV system. The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (H2O) and solvent B (ACN) at a flow rate of 4.7 mL/min. The detection wavelength was 230 nm and all analyses were performed at room temperature. A total of 180 injections were performed by using a linear gradient elution (0–3 min, from 50 to 60% B; 3–6 min, from 60 to 70% B; 6–25 min, from 70 to 80% B; 25–27 min, from 80 to 90% B; 27–32 min, from 90 to 100% B; 32–40 min, from 100 to 100% B), yielding compounds 1 (5.7 mg, tR = 20.42 min) and 2 (3.5 mg, tR = 31.41 min) (Figure a).

For the isolation of 3 and 4, powdered E. tinctorium CL103 (10 g) was sequentially extracted with 80% EtOH and 50% MeOH as described above. The crude E2 extract (10 mg/mL) was subjected to sequential phase separation with n-hexane, CHCl3, and EtOAc, as described above. The bioactive EtOAc extract was then subjected to silica gel column chromatography by stepwise elution using n-hexane, EtOAc, and MeOH as mobile phases as described above. Bioactive EtOAc-eluted fractions 6 to 9 (not shown), visible under UV, were pooled to give 20 mg/mL in MeOH and subjected to Sephadex LH-20 size-exclusion chromatography using MeOH as a mobile phase. At a flow rate of 1 mL/min, 3 mL fractions were collected and analyzed for bioactivity. Bioactive Fr. 7–9 (Figure S3) were further subjected to HPLC purification. The mobile phase consisted of solvents A (ACN), B (H2O), and C (MeOH) at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. The detection wavelength was 260 nm, and all analyses were performed at room temperature. The purification was performed using gradient elution on the Phenomenex Luna C18(2) column with ACN:H2O:MeOH at 25.5:49:25.5 to 26:48:26 in 8 min, 100:0:0 from 10 to 15 min, 60:30:10 from 15 to 18 min, and 25.5:49:25.5 from 20 to 25 min, yielding compounds 3 (5.5 mg, tR = 5 min) and 4 (2.8 mg, tR = 7.6 min) (Figure b).

For the isolation of 5–7, powdered E. tinctorium CL37 (5 g) was directly extracted using 300 mL of hexane (3 times) at room temperature. The hexane extract at 50 mg was dissolved in ACN and purified using an Agilent Zorbax Eclipse XDB C18 HPLC semipreparative column (9.4 × 250 mm, 5 μm) at a flow rate of 2.0 mL/min. The detection wavelength was 210 and 230 nm. The mobile phase used was ACN:H2O (90:10 for 40 min), yielding compound 5 (3.6 mg, tR = 32 min) (Figure c). Compounds eluted at 39.1 min were further purified using a slightly different isocratic condition with ACN:H2O (80:20) yielding compounds 6 (0.4 mg, tR = 13 min) and 7 (0.5 mg, tR = 11.6 min) (Figure d).

Cytotoxicity Assay

Cytotoxic activity of extracts, fractions, and purified compounds was assessed by percentage cell viability using 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2–5 diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay. HeLa human cervical cancer cells were used for bioactivity-guided experiments. HeLa cells were plated (96 well, 1.5 × 104 cells/well) and after 24 h, cells were treated with test reagents for 48 h at various concentrations. Cells were observed for morphological changes under a microscope and incubated with 50 μL of MTT solution (3 h, 37 °C). Medium was then removed from the wells and 150 μL of DMSO was added and incubated for another 5 min. Formazan purple color was quantified by determining the absorbance of samples at 570 nm using Bio-Tek’s Synergy-2 multiplate reader. Cell viability was presented as a percentage of the negative control value. Doxorubicin was used as a positive control, while 0.1% DMSO or MeOH was used as a negative control, taken as 100% cell viability. Purified compounds 1, 2, and 4–6 were also assessed against multiple cancer cell lines including human glioblastoma cells (U87, U251), human colorectal cancer cells (HCT116, SW480), SVG human fetal glial cells, and RIE-1 rat intestinal epithelial cells. All cell lines, except SVG cells (a gift from Dr. Eugene Major, NIH, Bethesda), were obtained from American Type Culture Collection. All experiments, unless otherwise stated, were performed three times (n = 3). For the calculation of IC50 values from dose–response curves (Figure S62), nonlinear regression on GraphPad Prism 10 was used for analyses.

Flow Cytometry Apoptosis and Cell Cycle Analyses

Cells were plated at 2.5 × 105 cells/well in 6-well plates and after an overnight incubation, cells were treated with purified compounds. For 5, cells were treated with concentrations at 40 μM for 24 and 48 h time intervals and then assessed for apoptosis and the cell cycle using flow cytometry. After treatment, cells were trypsinized followed by centrifugation and washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The supernatant was discarded and cell pellets were resuspended in PBS. Cells were counted and spun again at 4 °C, after which the pellets were resuspended in binding buffer to achieve a cell density of 1.5 × 106 cells/mL. Live cells were stained with PE Annexin V and 7-AAD according to the manufacturer’s instructions for Apoptosis Detection Kit I (BD Pharmingen) and analyzed by flow cytometry using a BD FACSMelody cell sorter (BD Biosciences) and BD FACSChorus software (V 1.0). For cell cycle analysis, the BD Cycletest plus DNA reagent kit was used to stain for DNA and the data were analyzed using software FlowJo. DMSO was used as a negative control, while resveratrol was used as a positive control.

Data Analysis

Data were expressed as average ± standard deviation (S.D). GraphPad Prism 10 (San Diego, CA) was used for the statistical analysis. Statistical significance among experimental groups was determined by one-way ANOVA. Dunnett’s test was used to evaluate differences among means compared to the control. For the apoptosis experiments (Figure ), statistical analyses were performed using two-way ANOVA. P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.
