Genetic Determinants of Treatment‐Related Bone Toxicity in Pediatric Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia
Authors: Rachid Abaji, Vincent Gagné, Émilie Espagne, Nathalie Alos, Veronica Del Vecchio, Lamia Ait Said, Albert Shalmiev, Claire Fuchs, Caroline Laverdière, Jean‐Marie Leclerc, Stephen E. Sallan, Lewis B. Silverman, Daniel Sinnett, Thai Hoa Tran, Maja Krajinovic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Osteonecrosis and fractures are serious corticosteroid‐induced bone toxicities in children treated for acute lymphoblastic leukemia, yet their genetic determinants remain incompletely defined. In this study, we aimed to identify novel genetic contributors to bone toxicity and to evaluate the robustness of both newly identified and previously established risk genotypes in more recent Dana‐Farber Cancer Institute treatment protocols. Whole‐exome sequencing was first performed in a discovery cohort to identify genetic variants associated with osteonecrosis. A novel association with a variant in the PGAP2 gene was identified and subsequently confirmed in an independent replication cohort, with effects of patient‐ and disease‐related characteristics observed in both cohorts. Next, previously reported candidate gene–derived associations, together with this newly identified variant, were assessed in a more recent cohort to examine their relevance in contemporary treatment protocols. Variants in BCL2L11 and the ACP1–SH3YL1 locus were associated with bone fractures, with a strong synergistic effect and significant modulation by protocol‐specific factors, particularly corticosteroid exposure and asparaginase formulation. The PGAP2 variant was associated with the combined osteonecrosis‐fracture phenotype. Transcriptomic analyses revealed isoform‐specific expression shifts in PGAP2 in patients with osteotoxicity, supporting a potential functional role for altered splicing or gene regulation. Together, our results provide insight into the pharmacogenomic architecture of osteotoxicity in ALL, demonstrating that PGAP2, BCL2L11, and ACP1–SH3YL1 variants remain clinically relevant predictors of bone toxicity across evolving treatment protocols. However, their clinical manifestations appear to be context‐dependent, underscoring the importance of integrating genetic susceptibility with pharmacologic exposures and supporting further investigation of the underlying molecular mechanisms.

Bone toxicities may arise as complications of the treatment of childhood acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 and include osteonecrosis (ON) and bone fractures. Corticosteroids (CSs) are important components of ALL protocols 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 because they lead to apoptosis of leukemia cells; however, they can also induce bone morbidities in vulnerable patient populations. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 10 ON is a dose‐limiting toxicity that can result in the withdrawal of CS from therapy. CS are also involved in osteoporosis and fractures by altering osteoclast and osteoblast activity, increasing bone resorption and loss. 10 , 11 Different factors may potentiate bone morbidity, including CS dose and type, sex, age, and concurrent drugs (particularly asparaginase). 1 , 3 To identify genetic factors underlying these toxicities, several pharmacogenomic studies were conducted using both genome‐wide approaches and targeted analyses of candidate genes. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 In our previous work, candidate gene analyses were performed in two independent cohorts of children with ALL treated under Dana‐Farber Cancer Institute (DFCI) protocols, focusing on genes differentially expressed between corticosteroid‐resistant and corticosteroid‐sensitive ALL cells, as well as genes highlighted in prior genome‐wide association studies (GWAS). 13 , 16 These analyses identified associations between BCL2L11 and ACP1–SH3YL1 variants and symptomatic ON in a dose‐dependent manner. BCL2L11 encodes for apoptotic Bim protein that, among other functions, contributes to osteoblast apoptosis, 17 whereas ACP1 codes for acid phosphatase 1, which is implicated in bone formation and osteoblast biology. 18 Here, we first extended association analyses to germline whole‐exome sequencing (WES) data coupled with validation of the findings. Then, all single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) identified either through candidate gene or exome‐wide analyses were tested for their ability to predict bone toxicity in ALL patients treated with more recent DFCI protocols, which feature changes in post‐induction CS and asparaginase formulations compared to earlier protocols.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patients

Three distinct cohorts were used in this study: Discovery (QcALL) cohort composed of 279 Caucasian patients from Quebec diagnosed with childhood ALL in Montreal at the Sainte Justine University Health Center (SJUHC), treated with DFCI ALL Consortium protocols 87‐01, 91‐01, 95‐01, or 00‐01; Replication (DFCI) cohort, composed of 199 patients treated with the DFCI 00‐01 protocol in nine remaining DFCI Consortium Hospitals; and the QcALL 05/11 cohort composed of 206 Caucasian patients treated at SJUHC with more recent DFCI 05‐001 and 11‐001 protocols. Table 1 summarizes the study design and outlines the role of each cohort in the analysis of genetic determinants of bone toxicity. Key demographic, clinical, and treatment characteristics of the study populations are presented in Table S1 . Additional details on the discovery and replication cohorts have been reported in our previous candidate gene studies. 13 , 16

To facilitate comparison across cohorts, key differences in treatment protocols—including glucocorticoid type, asparaginase formulation, dose, and schedule of administration—are summarized in Table S2 . These variations are directly relevant to the interpretation of pharmacogenomic associations with bone toxicity. A detailed description of the DFCI treatment protocols has been reported elsewhere. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 12 , 19 , 20

Briefly, regarding the CS component, prednisone was administered to all patients during the induction phase (40 mg/m2/day) across all DFCI protocols. In the post‐induction period, CS were given for 5 days within 3‐week intensification and continuation cycles, with the choice of prednisone or dexamethasone depending on the specific protocol, as detailed in Table S2 . In general, high‐risk patients received approximately threefold higher CS doses than standard‐risk patients, except in protocol 00‐01, where high‐risk patients received the same dose as standard‐risk patients during the continuation phase. As for asparaginase, exposure during the induction phase was limited, being restricted to an investigational window in protocol 87‐01, absent in 91‐01, and administered as a single dose in the other protocols. In contrast, different formulations and dosing strategies were implemented during post‐induction phases across DFCI protocols, as summarized in Table S2 . Patients in the QcALL and DFCI cohorts received native E. coli, PEG, or Erwinia asparaginase formulations. In the QcALL 05/11 cohort, protocol 05‐001 randomized patients between PEG asparaginase and native E. coli asparaginase, whereas protocol 11‐001 randomized patients between PEG asparaginase and calaspargase pegol, the latter having a longer half‐life.

Information on symptomatic ON (National Cancer Institue (NCI) grades 2–4) occurring during treatment was collected from patients' medical files and was based on the symptoms (bone pain or functional limitations) and radiological examinations. ON was reported by participating institutions for the DFCI group. In addition to ON, fractures (all grades), confirmed by radiological examination and fractures‐ON combination were also included for the QcALL 05/11 cohort, in which all cases of bone toxicities were independently confirmed by the local endocrinology team. Fracture data were available only for the QcALL 05/11 cohort, as earlier cohorts did not systematically collect these outcomes in a standardized manner; fracture analyses were therefore limited to this cohort.

Whole exome sequencing

Whole‐exome sequencing (WES) was performed in a subset of patients from the discovery cohort allowing for an association analysis with ON. Briefly, DNA was extracted from peripheral blood or bone marrow samples obtained after remission from childhood ALL patients (QcALL cohort) using standard protocols. Whole exomes were captured in solution with Agilent's SureSelect Human All Exon 50 Mb kits and sequenced on the Life Technologies SOLiD System (patients mean coverage ~35×). Reads were aligned to the hg19 reference genome using SOLiD LifeScope software and PCR duplicates were removed using Picard. Variant calling was carried out by the Genome Analysis Tool Kit (GATK), 21 and the germline variants annotation was conducted by ANNOtiation of VARiants. 22 Variants with minor allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 0.05 and with the predicted functional effect of missense or nonsense variants, and variants in splicing sites, as assessed in silico using Sorting Intolerant from Tolerant (< 0.1) and PolyPhen2 (≥ 0.85) filters, were retained for analysis. 23 , 24 This resulted in 6576 variants that were analyzed by allelic ratio test for an association with ON in the QcALL cohort using PLINK (v1.07). 25 P‐values were adjusted for multiple testing by false discovery rate (FDR) < 5%. 25 , 26 Analyses were performed on all patients and on stratified risk groups. Variants showing significant associations were subsequently evaluated in the full discovery cohort and independently tested in the replication cohort to assess robustness and clinical relevance. Additional technical details are provided in our previously published studies. 15 , 27

Genotyping

WES data were available for the QcALL cohort. The top‐ranking SNPs obtained through the WES data analyses were confirmed and validated by genotyping in both QcALL and DFCI cohorts. Additionally, all SNPs that were significantly associated either in this WES analysis or in our prior candidate gene studies were genotyped in the QcALL 05/11 cohort, including two variants in BCL2L11 (891T>G [rs2241843] and 29201C>T [rs724710]) and one variant in the ACP1–SH3YL1 locus (rs11553746 C>T). Genotyping was performed as previously described. 13 , 16

Gene expression and isoform analysis

Transcriptomic analysis was performed on a subset of 76 QcALL 05/11 patients with available RNA‐seq data derived from bone marrow samples collected at diagnosis or shortly after. These analyses aimed to assess differential expression of the newly identified PGAP2 and adjacent NUP98 genes in relation to bone toxicity phenotypes. RNA libraries were prepared using TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina), including a ribosomal RNA depletion step. Paired‐end sequencing was performed on the HiSeq 4000 (2 × 75 bp) and NovaSeq 6000 System (2 × 100 bp) (Illumina) with an average depth of approximately 150 million reads per sample. Transcript quantification was performed using Salmon (v1.10.2), 28 with the ‐‐seqBias, ‐‐gcBias, and ‐‐numBootstraps 100 options enabled, with alignment to the GRCh38 reference genome (Ensembl 110). Gene‐ and transcript‐level counts were extracted using tximport (v1.33.0) 29 and normalized with DESeq2 (v1.44.0). 30 Box plots of normalized counts were generated using the ggplot2 package (v3.5.2). 31

Statistical analysis

The association of genotypes with ON or other bone toxicities (fractures and ON‐fractures combination) was performed by Kaplan–Meier (one minus survival curves) to estimate cumulative incidence during ALL treatment, or by chi‐square or likelihood ratio test to compare genotype frequency between groups. Analyses were performed in all patients and after stratification by prognostic risk groups (high and very high vs. standard), age (below or above 10 years of age), sex, and type of asparaginase (E. coli or pegylated, PEG asparaginase).

Cumulative incidence of bone toxicity in relation to the genotype was also estimated using competing events (relapse or death) and Gray test analyses implemented in EZR software. 32 Univariable Cox regression analysis was used to calculate hazard ratio (HR) and a 95% confidence interval (95% CI). Multivariable Cox regression analyses were performed to estimate the simultaneous effect of several genetic predictors, or genetic effect in the presence of non‐genetic covariates. Statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS statistical package version 24 (SPSS Inc, NY). For the transcriptomics analysis, statistical comparisons between groups were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test for phenotype analyses, and the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by correction for multiple comparisons.

RESULTS

The allele ratios of common variants derived from WES data, analyzed in relation to ON in the QcALL discovery cohort, are summarized in Table S3 . The results are presented for the entire cohort as well as stratified by risk group. The analysis identified a significant association between ON and rs61899363, an A to T replacement in the PGAP2 gene, in high‐risk patients (FDR < 5%, P = 1.2 × 10−8). This SNP was subsequently genotyped in all patients of the QcALL cohort (Table 2 ), followed by the association analyses with cumulative ON incidence, which showed an increased ON probability with each minor T allele (HR = 2.8, 95% CI = 1.6–5.1, P = 3 × 10−4, Figure 1 a ). The effect of PGAP2 variant was even more apparent in boys (HR = 3.7, 95% CI = 1.7–8.2, P = 10−3, Figure 1 b ) and the high‐risk patients (HR = 6.8, 95% CI = 2.1–21.6, P = 2 × 10−5, Figure 1 c ). All results remained significant in competing risk analysis (Figure S1 ). The analyses of the replication cohort also showed that the carriers of the minor allele exhibited a higher incidence of ON, although this was confined to patients above 10 years of age (OR = 7.5, 95% CI = 1.1–50.7, P = 0.04, Table 3 ).

Further analyses of risk genotypes in BCL2L11, ACP1–SH3YL1 (further referred to as ACP1 as well) and PGAP2 genes in QcALL 05/11 cohort did not show an association with ON alone, but instead found an association with other bone toxicities, notably fractures and ON–fractures combination. The association of PGAP2 was noted in boys who had developed both ON and fractures (HR = 3.9, 95% CI = 1.4–11.1, P = 0.005, Figure 2 ), whereas risk genotypes in BCL2L11 and ACP1 genes were associated with a higher risk of fractures (HR for BCL2L11 rs724710 CC genotype = 2.0, 95% CI = 1.1–3.7, P = 0.02, Figure 3 a ; and HR for ACP1 rs11553746 TT genotype = 2.2, 95% CI = 1.1–4.8, P = 0.04, Figure 3 b ). Their effect was more obvious in patients with both at‐risk genotypes (HR = 3.5, 95% CI = 1.6–7.9, P = 0.004, Figure 3 c ). All results remained significant in competing risk analyses (Figures S2 and S3 ). The treatment further modulated these associations. For example, the effect of BCL2L11 risk genotype was limited to high‐risk patients and patients assigned to PEG asparaginase (P = 0.03, Figure 4 a and P = 0.04, Figure 4 b , respectively). In contrast the effect of the ACP1 risk genotype and its combination with BCL2L11 was more apparent in patients that received E. coli asparaginase (P = 2 × 10−5, Figure 4 c and P = 3 × 10−7, Figure 4 d , respectively). It is worth noting that in the latter case, fractures were identified in all patients but one, with two risk genotypes (HR = 8.6, 95% CI = 3.4–21.7).

In multivariable Cox models of QcALL cohort including all genetic variants, PGAP2, BCL2L11, and ACP1–SH3YL1 genotypes remained independently associated with ON risk (Table S4 ). When adjusted for clinical covariates, the association between PGAP2 genotype and ON remained robust (HR = 2.6, 95% CI = 1.5–4.7, P = 0.001), with age, sex, risk group, corticosteroid type, asparaginase formulation, and protocol included in the model (Table S5 ). In the QcALL 05/11 cohort, the associations between BCL2L11 and ACP1–SH3YL1 genotypes and fracture risk remained significant after adjustment for clinical covariates (HR = 2.0, 95% CI = 1.1–3.7, P = 0.034 and HR = 2.3, 95% CI = 1.0–5.0, P = 0.047, respectively; Table S6 ). The combined genotype model showed an even stronger effect (HR = 3.4, 95% CI = 1.5–8.0, P = 0.005).

Transcriptomic analysis revealed that noncanonical PGAP2 isoforms were overexpressed in patients exhibiting osteotoxicity. One PGAP2 isoform showed significantly increased expression in patients with ON and in those with the combined ON‐fracture phenotype, whereas another distinct isoform was preferentially overexpressed in patients with fractures alone (P ≤ 0.05, Figure S4a–c ). Similarly, analysis of the adjacent NUP98 gene demonstrated isoform‐specific expression shifts, with one isoform upregulated in ON and several others overexpressed in patients with fractures (P ≤ 0.05, Figure S5a,b ).

DISCUSSION

The analysis of WES data in the QcALL discovery cohort identified an association between ON and rs61899363 in the PGAP2 (post‐GPI attachment to proteins 2) gene, which was replicated in the validation cohort for patients above 10 years of age. The same variant predicted cases with ON‐fracture combination in more recent QcALL 05/11 cohort. PGAP2 contributes to the biosynthesis of glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) anchor by attaching a saturated fatty acid. 33 , 34 GPI‐anchored proteins are involved in membrane protein trafficking and cell‐surface adhesion and are essential for skeletal integrity, as defects in GPI‐anchor biosynthesis have been linked to osteopenia and skeletal abnormalities. 35

Although direct links between PGAP2 and bone abnormalities are limited, PGAP2 has been linked to disorders such as hyperphosphatasia with mental retardation syndrome, 33 , 34 which shares features with Paget disease and Paget‐like disorders characterized by bone resorption and malformations. 36 Moreover, PGAP2 is known to regulate surface expression of GPI‐anchored proteins such as CD55 and CD59, 33 , 34 both of which are implicated in osteoclast regulation. Mouse models with altered expression of these proteins exhibit distinct age‐ and sex‐dependent bone phenotypes. 37 , 38 This could offer a plausible mechanistic rationale for our observation of the association between rs61899363 and bone toxicity in male patients and those over 10 years of age.

Transcriptomic data further supports the role of PGAP2 in bone toxicity, evidenced by the significant overexpression of noncanonical transcripts across all studied phenotypes. PGAP2 has over 30 annotated transcripts, and rs61899363 is identified as an expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) and a splicing quantitative trait locus (sQTL) for this gene in multiple tissues. 39 Differential expression and splicing of PGAP2 have been previously linked to variable protein activity across isoforms, 34 suggesting that rs61899363 could modulate corticosteroid‐induced bone complications in ALL patients through transcript‐specific mechanisms.

Interestingly, rs61899363 also acts as an eQTL and sQTL for the adjacent NUP98 gene in multiple tissues—most relevantly in musculoskeletal tissue (Figure S6 ). NUP98 codes for nucleoporin and plays a role in the molecular transport between the nucleus and cytoplasm. It also participates in several cellular processes, including mitotic progression, and regulation of gene expression. Translocations between NUP98 and many other partner genes have been observed in different leukemias. 40 Transgenic mice with one of such translocations displayed delayed bone mineralization and high serum levels of fibroblast growth factor‐23 (FGF‐23). 41 The FGF receptor‐1 signaling has also been shown to contribute to communication between osteoblasts and endothelial cells, which is important for bone fracture repair. 42 Similar to PGAP2, transcriptomic analysis revealed the overexpression of certain NUP98 isoforms in patients who experienced ON or fracture, further implicating isoform‐level changes in bone complications.

Analyses of risk genotypes in the BCL2L11 and ACP1‐SH3YL1 genes in the QcALL 05/11 cohort showed their association with bone fractures, which were potentiated through combined gene effect. The 29201C>T variant in the BCL2L11 gene is a synonymous polymorphism that affects exon splicing. We have shown that the CC genotype affected the level of the γ Bim isoform without apoptotic BH3 domain and conferred higher sensitivity to dexamethasone in lymphoblastoid cell lines. 16 The CC genotype was associated with ON, 16 whereas the opposite TT genotype correlated with lower overall survival in childhood ALL patients, 12 non‐responsive chronic myeloid leukemia, and lower Bim mRNA levels. 43 Functional defects in Bim protein isoforms may have different consequences, including increased apoptosis of bone cells. Indeed, BCL2L11 gene silencing reduced osteoblast apoptosis induced by dexamethasone. 14 The rs11553746 C>T variant in the ACP1‐SH3YL1 gene is in linkage disequilibrium (LD) with several SNPs in the same locus. 13 Particularly interesting is the rs79716074 as it changes the amino acid and leads to a higher level of functional ACP1 F isoform. 44 The role of ACP1 in bone toxicities can be related to its contribution to bone formation and osteoblast biology. 18 The F isoform contributes to osteoclastic differentiation 45 and was associated with high triglyceride and cholesterol levels. 46

Within the same locus SH3YL1 encodes an SH3‐domain–containing protein that is possibly involved in cytoskeletal dynamics, since some proteins with SH3 domains appear to be critical regulators of osteoclast structure and function, particularly in bone resorption. 47 In addition, SH3YL1 was also reported to function as a co‐regulator of the androgen receptor, 48 a key player in bone growth and skeletal maintenance. 49 These biological roles provide a plausible mechanistic framework in which germline variation affecting SH3YL1 expression or isoform balance could modulate susceptibility to corticosteroid‐induced bone toxicity. Moreover, rs11553746 is a known eQTL and sQTL for ACP1, and an even stronger regulator for SH3YL1 across multiple tissues, including whole blood and musculoskeletal tissue (Figure S7 ). Carriers of the risk allele show reduced expression of the canonical SH3YL1 transcript (isoform 1) accompanied by increased expression of several noncanonical isoforms, particularly isoform 3, which displays marked structural differences. 50 This pattern supports an allele‐dependent shift in expression and splicing that reflects the broader regulatory behavior of this ACP1‐SH3YL1 locus and its contribution to osteotoxicity.

Treatment‐related factors—including CS type, cumulative CS exposure, and asparaginase formulation—are well‐established modulators of osteotoxicity risk in childhood ALL. Both ON and fractures have been largely attributed to CS exposure, with the type of CS further influencing the outcome, 3 , 4 , 8 , 10 as post‐induction dexamethasone has been associated with a higher incidence of ON compared with prednisone. 3 , 8 This effect seems to be potentiated by concomitant asparaginase administration, which has been shown to increase the risk of ON through effects on coagulation parameters, lipid metabolism, and steroid clearance, 3 thereby altering bone turnover. A recent study conducted in adolescents and young adults with ALL showed that patients treated on ALL protocols with pegaspargase were significantly more likely to be diagnosed with ON compared to patients treated on earlier protocols based on native E. coli asparaginase. This suggests that pegaspargase may lead to longer exposure to both asparaginase and dexamethasone, and consequently, a higher risk of ON. 4

These treatment‐related differences are directly relevant to the cohorts analyzed in our study as some factors might have changed across the evolution of the protocols, with differences in CS choices and dosages over time partially explaining the observed heterogeneity. Of note, protocol 05‐001 and 11‐001 also included post‐induction dexamethasone. Cumulative CS doses also changed over time and the patients assigned to the high‐risk group on protocols prior to 00‐01 received higher cumulative CS doses compared to more recent protocols. 6 Moreover, patients analyzed in this study, received different asparaginase preparations and dosing strategies depending on the protocols used. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 12 , 19 , 20

Within this changing therapeutic landscape, we found that the genetic associations between variants in the BCL2L11 and ACP1‐SH3YL1 genes and osteotoxicity were further shaped by treatment‐related factors, particularly risk group stratification and the type of asparaginase administered. The effect of the BCL2L11 risk genotype was restricted to high‐risk patients and those treated with PEG asparaginase, suggesting that intensified corticosteroid and asparaginase exposure may amplify the functional consequences of altered BIM splicing and glucocorticoid sensitivity. In contrast, the impact of the ACP1 risk allele—and its strong synergistic effect when combined with the BCL2L11 risk genotype—was predominantly observed in patients treated with E. coli asparaginase. This interaction was particularly striking among E. coli‐treated patients, nearly all individuals carrying both risk genotypes experienced fractures.

A few drawbacks of the study should also be noted. Data on ON were collected retrospectively from medical records and were limited to symptomatic cases, as systematic screening for asymptomatic ON was not routinely performed. This approach may have introduced variability in reporting practices across centers and potentially biased the estimation of the true incidence of ON. Moreover, patient‐ and treatment‐related factors, including sex, age, risk group, and concomitant therapies, are known to influence the risk of bone toxicities and may modulate the observed associations with genetic variants. Although a stratification analysis was conducted to account for these variables, the limited number of patients in some subgroups may have reduced statistical power and affected the precision of certain estimates, highlighting the need to interpret subgroup findings with caution. Larger cohorts or collaborative meta‐analyses will be required to estimate the magnitude of the genetic effect more precisely and to confirm these associations. Furthermore, transcriptomic analyses were performed on diagnostic bone marrow samples collected prior to treatment initiation, reflecting baseline gene expression rather than therapy‐induced changes. Although variability in cellular composition may influence expression profiles, the uniform timing of sampling across patients limits treatment‐related bias. Moreover, the observed genotype–expression relationships are supported by external eQTL and sQTL data, reinforcing their biological relevance. Finally, our previous analyses did not analyze the association with bone fractures, and we do not know whether the same genetic variants could have contributed to both types of bone toxicities.

Overall, our findings indicate that BCL2L11, ACP1–SH3YL1, and PAGP2 polymorphisms remain predictors of osteotoxicity across successive DFCI protocols, despite shifts in corticosteroid regimens and the introduction of longer‐acting asparaginase formulations. Indeed, multivariable analyses further support an independent contribution of each of these genetic variations to osteotoxicity risk, as the observed associations remained significant after adjustment for key clinical and treatment‐related factors. However, the type of bone toxicity associated with a specific variant appears to shift in newer regimens, suggesting that evolving treatment contexts modulate the clinical expression of genetic risk. Together, these observations underscore the biological relevance of these loci and highlight the need to consider both genomic risk and protocol‐specific factors when evaluating osteotoxicity in ALL—particularly in the context of concurrent treatment of CS with the more prolonged effect of pegylated E. coli‐derived asparaginase such as calaspargase pegol.
