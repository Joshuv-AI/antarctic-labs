Persistent Sciatic Artery and Vein with Iliac Vein Fenestration Presenting as Chronic Venous Insufficiency: A Case Report
Authors: Garima Sharma, Rajanikant R Yadav, Gurubasava Sajjan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Persistent sciatic vessels are rare but recognized anatomical variants associated with anomalous mesodermal development. An association between this entity and multiple vascular, visceral, and soft tissue anomalies has been described in the literature. Few reports have described the association of persistent sciatic vessels with lower limb varicosities and limb hypertrophy. Herein, we describe the case of a 24-year-old male who presented with extensive unilateral lower limb varicosities. Diagnostic imaging revealed persistent sciatic vessels with features of chronic venous insufficiency. Further evaluation revealed the coexistence of iliac vein fenestration. The patient underwent endovenous and endovascular management, with improved clinical outcome. In young patients with atypical unilateral varicosities and soft-tissue hypertrophy, comprehensive evaluation of the pelvic venous outflow and deep venous anatomy should precede superficial venous intervention.

INTRODUCTION

First described by Green in 1832 [1], persistent sciatic artery is a rare embryological vascular anomaly in which the main axial artery supplying the developing lower limb fails to involute. Its incidence has been reported to be 0.025%-0.040% in the general population [2]. Normally, as the femoral arterial system develops, the primitive sciatic artery regresses, with remnants contributing to the inferior gluteal, popliteal, and peroneal arteries. Persistent sciatic artery is often associated with partial or complete absence of the femoral artery.

Persistence of both sciatic vessels, namely the sciatic artery and sciatic vein, is exceedingly rare, with only a few case reports in the literature [3-5]. Vascular malformation syndromes, such as Klippel–Trénaunay syndrome and Parkes–Weber syndrome, may be associated with a persistent sciatic vein [6,7]. Herein, we present the case of a 24-year-old male who presented with extensive unilateral lower limb varicosities and was found to have persistence of both sciatic vessels. Cross-sectional imaging revealed the coexistence of iliac vein fenestration. The patient underwent endovenous laser ablation (EVLA) of the great saphenous vein (GSV), followed by endovascular stenting of the fenestrated iliac vein, with subsequent clinical improvement. The requirement for ethical approval was waived for this case report, and written informed consent was obtained from the patient.

CASE

A 24-year-old male presented with extensive unilateral varicose veins and associated skin changes. Clinical examination revealed extensive varicosities on both the medial and lateral aspects of the left lower limb. No significant limb length discrepancy was noted; however, skin and soft tissue hypertrophy were observed. There was no evidence of capillary malformation, such as a port-wine stain, or clinical evidence of high-flow arteriovenous shunting. Overall, the presentation was clinically consistent with advanced chronic venous insufficiency.

Duplex ultrasound was performed with the patient in the standing position, revealing incompetence of the saphenofemoral junction with a dilated GSV. Multiple incompetent perforators were also present. Reflux assessment was conducted using the Valsalva maneuver. Reflux duration of >0.5 seconds in superficial veins and >1.0 seconds in deep veins was considered significant. Preprocedural examination revealed significant reflux in the GSV, without measurable deep venous reflux. The small saphenous vein was also dilated, suggesting truncal reflux. It was high-riding and continued into the thigh without formation of the saphenopopliteal junction. At initial presentation, there was no sonographic evidence of deep venous reflux, arteriovenous malformation, or indirect findings suggestive of pelvic venous outflow obstruction. Based on these findings, the presentation was interpreted as primary superficial chronic venous insufficiency.

Subsequently, EVLA of the GSV was performed. Following EVLA, venous engorgement of the residual varicosities increased, accompanied by worsening limb edema. On postoperative day 2, duplex ultrasound demonstrated newly detected deep venous reflux, with a reflux duration exceeding 1.0 seconds in the femoral and popliteal veins. Further evaluation with computed tomography (CT) angiography revealed a persistent sciatic artery and vein on the left side, with varicosities in the region of left internal iliac vein (Figs. 1, 2). The ectatic persistent sciatic vein coursed posteriorly along the sciatic nerve trajectory, receiving tributaries from the posterior compartment of the thigh and draining proximally into the internal iliac venous system. The superficial femoral artery and femoral vein were relatively hypoplastic compared with the persistent sciatic vessels (Fig. 3). However, no hypoplasia was observed in the common femoral or external iliac veins. The left common iliac vein was fenestrated (type 8b), with compression by the overlying right common iliac artery (Fig. 4). This compression was considered a possible contributor to the deep venous reflux observed after EVLA. No arteriovenous malformation or high-flow shunting was identified on CT angiography.

Digital subtraction venography was performed using contralateral femoral access. Venography revealed reflux involving both channels of the fenestrated common iliac vein. For intervention the larger fenestrated channel was first cannulated through the right internal jugular route and stented using a 20×100 mm self-expanding WALLSTENT (Boston Scientific). Although the WALLSTENT is not a dedicated venous stent, it has been used extensively for venous interventions. Foreshortening and proximal funneling were observed, consistent with the known deployment characteristics of braided stent platforms such as the WALLSTENT. As a bailout strategy, the other channel was cannulated using the same route, and a 12×80 mm Epic (Boston Scientific) self-expanding stent was positioned, with its proximal end placed near the iliocaval confluence (Fig. 5). Following stent placement, reflux was reduced on venography. No postdeployment balloon dilatation was required. Intraprocedural pressure measurements were obtained across both fenestrated channels before and after stent deployment. No significant pressure gradient was observed after stent placement. The patient received intravenous unfractionated heparin postoperatively, followed by bridging with low-molecular-weight heparin (enoxaparin) and transition to oral warfarin therapy.

At the 3-month follow-up, the patient demonstrated significant clinical improvement, with reduced limb edema, decreased venous engorgement, and less prominent varicosities on clinical examination. Duplex ultrasound demonstrated reduced deep venous reflux and confirmed patency of both iliac vein stents, without evidence of in-stent restenosis.

DISCUSSION

Derived from the embryological axial artery, the persistent sciatic artery is a rare but clinically important vascular anatomical variant. As a continuation of the internal iliac artery in early embryogenesis, the sciatic artery provides the primary blood supply to the developing lower limb and involutes at approximately 10-12 weeks of gestation [8]. Failure of regression of the axial artery results in persistence of this primitive vessel. Compared with the arterial system, the development of the venous system is less well described in the literature; hence, anomalous venous development is less clearly understood [5]. Parallel to arterial development, a primitive vein running along the border of the embryo provides a channel for venous return from the lower limb. As development progresses, the tibial portions disappear, and the fibular portion gives rise to the anterior tibial, small saphenous, and inferior gluteal veins [5]. The GSV has been described as deriving from the retroperitoneal postcardinal vein, which gives rise to the femoral and posterior tibial veins [9]. Similar to the artery, a persistent sciatic vein results from failure of development of the femoral vein or failure of regression of the axial vein.

Often detected incidentally, the clinical presentation of a persistent sciatic artery is well described in the literature. Aneurysmal dilatation has been reported in as many as 46% of patients [8]. Lower limb ischemia has been reported in 31%-63% of patients and may result from thromboembolic or steno-occlusive complications [10]. Up to one-fourth of patients may present with limb-threatening ischemia [10]. In contrast, a persistent sciatic vein is often associated with syndromes such as Klippel–Trénaunay and Parkes–Weber syndromes [6,7]. In this case, the absence of capillary malformation, limb length discrepancy, and high-flow arteriovenous shunting was not consistent with Klippel–Trénaunay or Parkes–Weber syndrome. Therefore, this case represents isolated coexistence of a persistent sciatic artery and vein with an associated venous outflow anomaly, rather than a defined vascular malformation syndrome. A few case reports have described isolated persistent sciatic veins with atypical distribution of varicosities [11,12]. Persistence of both sciatic vessels is extremely rare, with descriptions limited to a few case reports [3-5].

Madson et al. [13] described the presence of a persistent sciatic artery associated with lower limb varicosities and limb length discrepancy in a 14-year-old male. To our knowledge, the constellation of congenital vascular anomalies in our case, namely extensive unilateral varicosities and soft tissue hypertrophy with persistence of both sciatic vessels, has been described only once in the English literature [5]. In addition, fenestration of the left common iliac vein was demonstrated in our case. Fenestration of the common iliac vein is a rare anatomical variant in which the vein divides into two separate channels. This variant is described as type 8 in the CT-based classification of iliac venous anatomy by Shin et al. [14], with a reported prevalence of approximately 0.4%. The subtypes include short-segment splitting or complete duplication of the common iliac vein with independent drainage channels. Dinh et al. [15] described an unusual case of deep venous thrombosis in a patient with a fenestrated common iliac vein, managed with endovascular suction thrombectomy.

The initial management of our patient was EVLA of the GSV, a well-established treatment for superficial venous insufficiency. Deep venous reflux has been reported in 22% of patients with primary superficial venous reflux and no history of DVT [16]; however, deep venous reflux was not present initially in our case. The worsening of limb edema and subsequent detection of deep venous reflux after EVLA may be explained by the possibility that a substantial reflux volume had previously drained through the GSV. In patients with iliac outflow obstruction, superficial veins may function as compensatory decompressive pathways. Ablation of these channels can alter venous hemodynamics and potentially unmask previously compensated deep venous reflux. Therefore, the postprocedural findings in our case may represent hemodynamic redistribution rather than true de novo development of deep venous incompetence. After ablation, the deep veins, including the persistent sciatic vein, may have carried a greater reflux volume. In addition, subclinical compression of the fenestrated left iliac vein by the right common iliac artery, with morphology similar to May–Thurner syndrome, may have increased venous hypertension and contributed to the observed reflux pattern. Hence, self-expanding stents were placed in the fenestrated venous channels to improve venous outflow.

In retrospect, the presence of unilateral limb soft tissue hypertrophy and atypical varicosities involving both the medial and lateral compartments should have raised suspicion for an underlying syndromic or deep venous anomaly. Although lower-extremity duplex ultrasound did not initially demonstrate deep venous reflux or obstruction, cross-sectional pelvic evaluation should be considered before superficial venous intervention in such patients. This case underscores the importance of thorough preprocedural assessment of pelvic venous outflow in young patients with atypical clinical features. This assessment should include complete venous mapping and evaluation for deep venous reflux and proximal venous outflow obstruction.

A limitation of this case is the absence of archived duplex ultrasound images at different time points. Although objective reflux measurements and clinical correlations were available, the lack of serial imaging documentation limits direct visual comparison of hemodynamic changes. This case also highlights that awareness of variant venous anatomy is helpful in guiding and planning surgical or endovascular management strategies. Had the iliac vein fenestration with hemodynamically significant compression been identified before the intervention, primary treatment of the proximal outflow lesion might have been considered as a more physiologic approach. Relief of the iliac venous obstruction could potentially reduce venous hypertension and modify reflux patterns, allowing reassessment of the necessity and extent of superficial venous intervention.
