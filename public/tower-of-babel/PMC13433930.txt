Transfer-printed yellow and red InGaN micro-LEDs on diamond for ultra-low-power high-speed optical interconnects
Authors: Runze Lin, Yu Zhang, Yucheng Yang, Shengyuan Dong, Handan Xu, Daqi Shen, Cheng Gao, Zehui Liang, Junze Tong, Erdan Gu, Zhizhong Chen, Bo Shen, Pengfei Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapid growth of computing demand requires high-speed transmission with low power consumption. Micro-light-emitting diodes (micro-LEDs), offering high modulation bandwidth, low-power operation, and array integration, are promising transmitters for next-generation interconnects. Here, we show yellow and red InGaN micro-LEDs, optimized through superlattice strain engineering and a three-period quantum well design, which were transfer-printed onto diamond, with microlenses fabricated by two-photon lithography for coupling optimization. The 20 µm yellow micro-LED achieved an electrical-to-optical bandwidth of 2850.4 MHz at 6.25 A/cm², while the 20 µm red device reached 2593.4 MHz at 50 A/cm². With on–off keying (OOK) modulation through a 1 m fiber link, 20 µm and 40 µm yellow devices achieved 1.5 Gbps at 25 µA (6.25 A/cm²) and 50 µA (3.125 A/cm²), with energy efficiencies of 0.056 pJ/bit and 0.110 pJ/bit, respectively. This study demonstrates the potential of InGaN yellow and red micro-LEDs for energy-efficient high-speed optical interconnects.Subject terms: Inorganic LEDs, Fibre optics and optical communications

Introduction

In the post-Moore era, the growth of computing performance is increasingly constrained by factors beyond raw processing power, including the I/O wall and memory wall caused by the separation of computation and memory, as well as the limited edge bandwidth density1,2. With the rising demand for short-reach, high-speed interconnects at the chip and board level in high-performance applications such as large-scale model training, conventional electrical interconnects at the centimeter-to-meter scale are approaching both physical and engineering limits due to power consumption and crosstalk3. As a result, the development of an emerging class of optical interconnects that can deliver ultra-high bandwidth density and ultra-low energy consumption, while being manufacturable and compatible with large-scale packaging, has become a critical enabler for the continued expansion of computing architectures4–6.

Vertical-cavity surface-emitting laser (VCSEL) interconnects, the most widely deployed today, play an important role in short-reach data center links owing to their low cost and ease of coupling to multimode fibers7,8. However, the threshold current, thermal management requirements, and limited scalability restrict further improvements in energy efficiency and bandwidth density9. Silicon photonic interconnects have emerged as an attractive alternative, offering high-speed transmission and wavelength-division multiplexing (WDM) to increase data throughput10–12. Yet, the reliance on external III–V lasers, together with complex coupling and heterogeneous integration processes, makes it challenging to meet the energy efficiency and packaging cost requirements for chip-level and board-level interconnects4,13.

Among emerging light sources, InGaN-based micro-light-emitting diodes (micro-LEDs) are gaining attention as strong contenders for short-reach optical interconnects at the package, chip-to-chip, and board levels14. The advantageous combination of low power consumption, micron-scale capacitance, sub-nanosecond carrier dynamics, and natural two-dimensional array scalability aligns well with the needs of next-generation interconnects15–17. As spontaneous emission devices without threshold current, micro-LEDs have the potential to realize high modulation bandwidths even at low current densities, enabling Gbps communication at energy efficiency levels of pJ/bit or below18. The emission spectrum of InGaN-based micro-LEDs covers the visible spectrum, which is inherently compatible with silicon photodetectors and supports wafer-level integration with CMOS circuits, thereby reducing system complexity and packaging cost. Most importantly, micro-LED arrays offer two-dimensional parallel scalability, allowing per channel data rates at the Gbps level to be scaled into Tbps system throughput, thereby combining high bandwidth density with high energy efficiency.

In fact, research on micro-LED-based optical interconnects has already made notable progress. Xu et al. employed a 20 μm micro-LED with a 470 nm emission wavelength as the light source and achieved an on–off keying (OOK) data rate of 2.125 Gbps, corresponding to a transmitter energy consumption of 7.34 pJ/bit19. Le Maitre et al. proposed the concept of a micro-LED to micro-LED optical interconnect link and demonstrated a 1 Gbps OOK optical interconnect rate using a 25 μm micro-LED with a silicon photodetector20. Li et al. demonstrated real-time non-return-to-zero on–off keying (NRZ-OOK) transmission using a blue micro-LED with a three-layer improved InGaN nanostructure, achieving 3.07 Gbps for a single channel and 12 Gbps with four parallel channels21. At the system level, Avicena and Microsoft have reported prototype implementations of micro-LED optical interconnects. Avicena employed laser lift-off technology to transfer and integrate 300-pixel arrays of 7 μm micro-LEDs, achieving single-channel NRZ-OOK data rates of 10 Gbps on a polished sapphire backplane and 3.5 Gbps on a silicon application specific integrated circuit (ASIC) backplane, with aggregate system data rates of ~3 Tbps and ~1 Tbps, respectively, and a transmitter energy as low as ~1 pJ/bit22, recently scaling down to 200 fJ/bit23, with ongoing industry efforts rapidly pushing this efficiency toward the sub-100 fJ/bit regime. Microsoft, on the other hand, proposed a wide-and-slow MOSAIC architecture, which replaces a small number of high-speed channels with a large number of parallel micro-LED channels. They demonstrated a 100-channel system with per-channel rates up to 2 Gbps and an aggregate bandwidth of ~200 Gbps, and further projected that a 20×20 micro-LED array could achieve ~800 Gbps while reducing power consumption by 56–68% compared with mainstream 800 Gbps optical interconnects14.

These results indicate that micro-LEDs have already shown great potential for low-power and high-speed optical interconnects, with demonstrations ranging from single-pixel devices to large-scale arrays. However, the ultimate system-level communication capacity still depends on the intrinsic high-speed modulation capability of individual devices. In other words, only when a single micro-LED achieves sufficiently high modulation bandwidth can it support Gbps- to Tbps-level interconnects under low current density operation. In recent years, with continuous improvements in epitaxial growth and device design, the modulation bandwidth of micro-LEDs has rapidly advanced from the MHz range to the GHz regime16. For short-wavelength blue devices, optimization toward high bandwidth is the most mature among InGaN-based micro-LEDs, benefiting from the reliability of low-In-content epilayer. By employing m-plane nonpolar growth to eliminate the built-in polarization field, devices have demonstrated an electrical-to-electrical (E–E) bandwidth of 1.5 GHz at a current density of 1 kA/cm² and 1.35 GHz at 250 A/cm² 24. Using an ultrathin quantum well design, electrical-to-optical (E–O) bandwidths of 1 GHz at 352 A/cm² and 1.53 GHz at 6 kA/cm² have been achieved25. With free-standing GaN substrates and a two-period quantum well structure, an E–O bandwidth of 2.3 GHz was realized at 42.5 kA/cm² 26. Devices with InGaN quantum dot (QD) active regions have reached an E–E bandwidth of 3.6 GHz at current densities below 1 kA/cm², while maintaining a high bandwidth of ~1.839 GHz at 100 A/cm² 27. Furthermore, under high-temperature operation (200 °C), enhancement of defect-assisted Auger recombination enabled an E–O bandwidth of 4 GHz at 9 kA/cm² 28. Recent explorations into the fundamental limits of device modulation have pushed these boundaries even further; for instance, Liu et al. demonstrated a −3 dB bandwidth exceeding 3.8 GHz for blue QD micro-LEDs, noting that such a value was constrained by the photodetector limit rather than the emitter itself 29. In contrast, reports of green micro-LEDs achieving >1 GHz bandwidth remain scarce, with only a few cases such as semi-polar structures yielding 1.1 GHz (~849 A/cm²) and 1.035 GHz (146 A/cm²)30,31, InGaN QD active regions achieving 1.22 GHz (458 A/cm²), 1.3 GHz (509 A/cm²), and 1.4 GHz (<1 kA/cm²)27,32,33, and strain-engineered design reaching 2.19 GHz E–O bandwidth at 40 kA/cm² 34. For longer-wavelength InGaN-based yellow and red devices, high-speed response has been rarely reported. The maximum reported modulation bandwidths remain limited to 630 MHz for yellow devices and 439.7 MHz for red devices at 2 kA/cm² 35,36.

It can be seen that as the emission wavelength shifts to longer values, the higher In composition introduces significant epitaxial challenges and strong polarization effects, which severely limit both efficiency and bandwidth. In particular, for yellow and red devices, achieving GHz-level bandwidth remains difficult, greatly constraining their potential in optical communication. Moreover, most existing studies have focused on bandwidth enhancement under high current densities, while reports on high-speed response at low current densities below 100 A/cm², or even below 10 A/cm², are still lacking—yet this regime is essential for enabling truly low-power applications. Although mature short-wavelength micro-LEDs currently dominate the field thanks to their high quantum efficiency, determining the optimal wavelength for a full-link system requires balancing several complex trade-offs. For silicon detectors, short-wavelength light necessitates thin absorption layers, which often leads to higher junction capacitance and increased surface recombination penalties. In contrast, longer wavelengths (yellow and red) penetrate deeper, which helps to reduce parasitic capacitance and boost responsivity. By providing an alternative technological route, these longer-wavelength devices hold the potential to broaden micro-LED-based optical interconnect technologies. Consequently, investigating the high-speed modulation of yellow and red micro-LEDs at low current densities is more than just a fundamental challenge in device physics—it represents a crucial step toward developing ultra-energy-efficient optical interconnects.

In this work, we demonstrate InGaN-based yellow and red micro-LEDs on passive diamond substrates for optimized thermal management and intrinsic high-speed performance evaluation. The epilayer is engineered using superlattice strain management and a three-period quantum well structure, enabling improved modulation bandwidth characteristics at both low and high current densities. Single-pixel flip-chip heterogeneous substrate integration is realized through PDMS stamp transfer printing, while optical coupling is enhanced by two-photon lithography–printed microlenses. High E–O bandwidths of 2850.4 and 2593.4 MHz are achieved for 20 µm yellow and red micro-LEDs at low current densities of 6.25 A/cm² and 50 A/cm², respectively. Furthermore, in a 1-m fiber link, 20 µm and 40 µm yellow devices demonstrate 1.5 Gbps OOK transmission at low currents of 25 µA and 50 µA, achieving energy efficiencies of 0.056 and 0.110 pJ/bit, respectively. Such results not only surpass previously reported values for the modulation bandwidth and energy efficiency of InGaN-based yellow and red micro-LEDs but also establish a reliable device and system foundation for the development of next-generation optical interconnects with both high energy efficiency and high data rate.

Results and discussion

Optoelectronic properties of transfer-printed micro-LEDs on diamond substrates

The schematic of the epitaxial structure for long-wavelength InGaN-based micro-LEDs on silicon substrates is shown in Fig. 1a, where yellow and red micro-LEDs adopt similar designs. By introducing InGaN/GaN superlattice strain release layers (SRLs) with carefully engineered thickness and composition, compressive strain was pre-applied to the active region to counteract the tensile stress induced during epitaxial cooling by the large thermal-expansion mismatch between silicon and GaN. This approach mitigates the quantum-confined Stark effect (QCSE) arising from polarization fields, effectively blocks dislocations from penetrating into the active region, and improves the overall epitaxial quality. The active region consists of three-period InGaN/GaN multiple quantum wells (MQWs). The use of fewer quantum wells together with relatively thick barriers enhances carrier confinement and increases the carrier density in individual wells, which improves the radiative recombination rate and shortens the carrier lifetime within each well. Figure 1b presents a cross-sectional high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) image of the complete epitaxial structure of the red micro-LED, while Fig. 1c shows a magnified view of the MQWs region, and Fig. 1d displays the corresponding energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping of indium distribution within the quantum wells. The interfaces between the epitaxial layers are sharp and flat, with no obvious dislocations observed. The indium distribution in the quantum wells is relatively uniform, which facilitates efficient long-wavelength emission.

Figure 2a shows the current versus voltage (I−V) characteristics of 20 μm yellow micro-LED, 40 μm yellow micro-LED, 20 μm red micro-LED, and 40 μm red micro-LED. For the 20 μm devices, the maximum operating current was set to 32 mA, corresponding to a current density of 8 kA/cm², while the 40 μm devices were driven up to 80 mA, corresponding to 5 kA/cm². The maximum operating voltages of the 20 µm yellow micro-LED, 40 µm yellow micro-LED, 20 µm red micro-LED, and 40 µm red micro-LED were 6.40 V (32 mA), 7.20 V (80 mA), 6.73 V (32 mA), and 7.20 V (80 mA), respectively. The relatively large ideality factors suggest the presence of strong non-radiative recombination channels37. Such non-ideal effects may arise from the growth of long-wavelength epilayer with high indium composition, where lattice and thermal mismatches generally lead to increased defect densities compared to low-indium-content blue/violet InGaN epilayer38.

Figure 2b shows the optical power versus current (P−I) characteristics of the four devices, while Supplementary Fig. 1a presents the corresponding curves on a logarithmic scale to better visualize the output at low currents. The current range was 4 μA (1 A/cm²) to 32 mA (8 kA/cm²) for the 20 μm devices, and 16 μA (1 A/cm²) to 80 mA (5 kA/cm²) for the 40 μm devices. The maximum optical powers achieved by the 20 μm yellow micro-LED, 40 μm yellow micro-LED, 20 μm red micro-LED, and 40 μm red micro-LED were 331.9 μW (32 mA), 928.4 μW (80 mA), 226.8 μW (32 mA), and 563.6 μW (80 mA), respectively. The corresponding external quantum efficiency (EQE) versus current curves are shown in Fig. 2c, while Supplementary Figs. 1b displays the EQE versus current density (J) curves on a logarithmic scale. For the yellow devices, both the 20 μm and 40 μm LEDs reached their EQE peaks at a current density of 1 A/cm², with maximum EQEs of 2.50% and 2.55%, respectively. With increasing current density, the EQE continuously decreased, showing a typical efficiency droop behavior. The lack of a peak EQE may be due to its occurrence at current densities below the measured range39. At their respective maximum operating current densities, the EQEs decreased to 0.42% (8 kA/cm²) and 0.47% (5 kA/cm²), corresponding to reductions of 83% and 82% relative to their peak EQEs. Notably, the EQE values of the 20 μm and 40 μm devices showed little difference at the same current density. This can be attributed, on the one hand, to effective repair of sidewall damage from dry etching by rapid thermal annealing and sidewall passivation40, which suppress non-radiative recombination at the sidewalls; and on the other hand, it may suggest that bulk defects dominate the overall non-radiative recombination.

The EQE−J characteristics of the red micro-LEDs exhibited a similar trend to those of the yellow devices, with maximum EQEs of 0.53% (20 μm) and 0.58% (40 μm) at 1 A/cm². Unlike the yellow micro-LEDs, however, the red devices exhibited an EQE recovery at medium-to-high current densities (~300 A/cm²). Because the 20 μm device operates at a four-fold higher current density than the 40 μm device at given current, it reaches this ~300 A/cm² recovery threshold at a much lower current. This geometric scaling perfectly explains the seemingly counterintuitive observation in Fig. 2b, where the optical power of the 20 μm red micro-LED transiently surpasses that of the 40 μm pixel. When plotted against current density (Supplementary Fig. 1b), the underlying EQE behaviors of both sizes are highly consistent. This recovery results in a secondary EQE peak of 0.41% (20 μm at 2 kA/cm²) and 0.38% (40 μm at 1.5 kA/cm²). This behavior mitigates efficiency droop under high-current injection, with EQEs of 0.30% (20 μm at 8 kA/cm²) and 0.31% (40 μm at 5 kA/cm²), corresponding to reductions of only 43% and 47% compared with 1 A/cm². Compared with the strong efficiency droop in the yellow devices, the output characteristics of the red devices suggest better suitability for applications requiring operation at medium-to-high current densities, such as high-speed visible light communication (VLC) systems.

The electroluminescence (EL) characteristics of the diamond-substrate devices were also investigated, as shown in Fig. 2d–g. Figure 2d compares the EL spectra of the four devices at their minimum and maximum operating current densities, while the detailed spectral evolution with current density are presented in Supplementary Fig. 2a–d. The peak wavelength shifts of the 20 μm yellow micro-LED, 40 μm yellow micro-LED, 20 μm red micro-LED, and 40 μm red micro-LED were as follows: from 570.2 nm (1 A/cm²) to 487.9 nm (8 kA/cm², Δλ = 82.3 nm), from 564.8 nm (1 A/cm²) to 490.4 nm (5 kA/cm², Δλ = 74.4 nm), from 654.6 nm (1 A/cm²) to 513.3 nm (8 kA/cm², Δλ = 141.3 nm), and from 658.3 nm (1 A/cm²) to 527.8 nm (5 kA/cm², Δλ = 130.5 nm), respectively. Figure 2e presents the extracted peak wavelength and full width at half maximum (FWHM) as a function of current density, providing a clearer picture of the EL evolution under different injection levels. For the peak wavelength, both yellow and red devices exhibited a monotonic blue-shift, which is commonly observed in InGaN-based micro-LEDs and is typically attributed to the screening of QCSE and the filling of localized states at higher carrier densities41. Compared with short-wavelength InGaN micro-LEDs, the blue-shift in our long-wavelength devices is more pronounced, mainly due to the larger lattice mismatch and strain associated with high indium content, which leads to stronger piezoelectric fields and hence a more significant QCSE.

The FWHM evolution showed different behaviors between yellow and red devices. For the yellow devices, the FWHM first decreased and then increased with current density before eventually saturating. Specifically, the FWHM values of the 20 μm and 40 μm devices were 70.2 nm and 71.4 nm at 1 A/cm², decreased to 59.0 nm (20 μm, 20 A/cm²) and 59.2 nm (40 μm, 10 A/cm²), and finally increased to 72.0 nm (20 μm, 8 kA/cm²) and 68.2 nm (40 μm, 5 kA/cm²). This trend may arise from the competition among the saturation of deep localized-state emission, QCSE screening, and band-filling effects42–44. In contrast, the red devices showed almost constant FWHM at low current densities (65.0 nm and 67.2 nm at 1 A/cm² for the 20 μm and 40 μm devices, respectively), followed by a pronounced broadening as the current density increased, reaching 96.5 nm (20 μm, 8 kA/cm²) and 95.6 nm (40 μm, 5 kA/cm²). Notably, the sharp FWHM increase occurred beyond ~300 A/cm², coinciding with the EQE recovery threshold. This suggests that the EQE rise in this regime may be linked to carrier delocalization, where more low-In-content regions contribute to radiative recombination. Consequently, the FWHM broadens, the short-wavelength components increase, and the quantum efficiency shows a modest improvement, which is eventually suppressed by efficiency droop caused by Auger recombination and self-heating. As discussed earlier, this density-dependent delocalization effect is fundamentally responsible for the transient optical power crossover observed in the P−I curves (Fig. 2b).

The evolution of the CIE chromaticity coordinates with current density for the four devices is shown in Fig. 2f, g, directly illustrating the color shifts. The yellow devices exhibited a shift from yellow to cyan, while the red devices shifted from red to green. Although such color shifts are generally undesirable for display applications, they may open opportunities for single-device multi-wavelength operation. For optical communication, wavelength stability is less critical than output power and high-speed response, and thus the observed blue-shift in these yellow and red devices remains acceptable.

Considering that micro-LEDs are a spontaneous emission Lambertian source with a typically large divergence angle, the limited numerical aperture of the optical interconnect system makes it difficult to satisfy the fiber-coupling requirements, leading to low coupling efficiency. To address this issue, optimized micro-lenses were designed and fabricated to improve the emission characteristics of micro-LEDs. SEM images of the micro-lenses integrated with the micro-LEDs are shown in Fig. 2h, where slight distortions may result from charging effects in the non-conductive material. Figure 2i presents the angular emission profiles of 20 μm and 40 μm devices with and without micro-lenses, measured over a range of –80° to 80°. The results show that the micro-lenses effectively collimate the wide-angle emission of the micro-LEDs. For devices without micro-lenses, the divergence angles (defined as the angular width at half of the peak intensity) were 154.7° (20 μm) and 151.9° (40 μm), while with micro-lenses, the divergence angles were reduced to 29.6° and 72°, corresponding to decreases of ~80.9% and 52.6%, respectively. The stronger collimation observed in the 20 μm device can be attributed to its smaller emitter-area-to-lens-size ratio. Such micro-lens integration enables better coupling with optical fiber systems, minimizing transmission losses caused by mismatches between the emission divergence and the fiber numerical aperture.

Thermal characteristics of transfer-printed micro-LEDs on diamond and glass substrates

To verify the effectiveness of the diamond substrates used in our experiments, we also fabricated 20 μm and 40 μm yellow and red transfer-printed devices on conventional glass substrates, and studied their surface temperature characteristics using an infrared thermal camera. Figure 3a shows the temperature–time profiles of the diamond- and glass-substrate devices, with the 20 μm devices operated at 6 mA (1.5 kA/cm²) and the 40 μm devices at 8 mA (0.5 kA/cm²). Clear differences in thermal behavior were observed between the two substrates: the temperature rises of the 20 μm diamond-substrate yellow and red devices were 6.6 °C and 6.7 °C, respectively, whereas the corresponding glass-substrate devices showed increases of 84.1 °C and 90.0 °C. This large difference is mainly attributed to the distinct thermal conductivities of the two substrates, with diamond generally exceeding 2000 W/(m K) and glass typically around ~1 W/(m K)45.

Figure 3b presents the input power–temperature rise characteristics, where the input power was calculated as current × voltage. The current ranges were 1.6 to 32 mA and 4.8 to 80 mA for the 20 μm and 40 μm diamond devices, and 0.12 to 6 mA and 0.12 to 8 mA for the corresponding glass devices. The input power–temperature rise curves in Fig. 3b exhibited good linearity, which was fitted using △T=αP (where α is the thermal coefficient in °C/mW, P is the input power in mW, and △T is the temperature rise in °C). The fitted curves versus the raw data for diamond- and glass-substrate devices are shown in Supplementary Fig. 3a, b, respectively. For the glass-substrate devices, the thermal coefficients of the 20 μm yellow micro-LED, 40 μm yellow micro-LED, 20 μm red micro-LED, and 40 μm red micro-LED were 3.057, 3.289, 3.043, and 2.922 °C/mW, respectively. In comparison, the thermal coefficients of the diamond-substrate devices were 0.245 °C/mW (20 μm yellow micro-LED), 0.064 °C/mW (40 μm yellow micro-LED), 0.234 °C/mW (20 μm red micro-LED), and 0.081 °C/mW (40 μm red micro-LED), corresponding to only 8.01%, 1.95%, 7.69%, and 2.77% of those on glass substrates.

These results highlight the severe limitation of glass substrates, where the poor thermal conductivity prevents efficient dissipation of heat generated in the micro-LEDs. As a result, the device temperature rises rapidly with increasing input power, restricting the maximum operable power and significantly degrading device performance in high-power applications. In contrast, for the diamond-substrate devices, both yellow and red LEDs exhibited notable size-dependent differences in thermal coefficients, with the 20 μm devices showing larger values than the 40 μm devices. This is mainly due to stronger self-heating effects caused by the higher current density in smaller devices under the same input power, combined with the reduced heat dissipation area in flip-chip bonding geometry. Such size dependence was not observed in the glass-substrate devices, likely because the poor thermal conductivity of glass confines the heat within the chip, leading to similar temperature effects across different device sizes under the same input power.

Figure 3c shows the input power density–temperature rise characteristics, while Supplementary Fig. 3c, d present the fitted curves of power density (PJ) versus temperature rise (ΔT) for the diamond- and glass-substrate devices, respectively, using the relation ΔT=αJPJ, where αJ is the thermal coefficient of power density. The fitted αJ values for the glass-substrate micro-LEDs were 0.0122 °C cm²/W (20 μm yellow micro-LED), 0.0526 °C cm²/W (40 μm yellow micro-LED), 0.0122 °C cm²/W (20 μm red micro-LED), and 0.0468 °C cm²/W (40 μm red micro-LED). For the diamond-substrate devices, the corresponding αJ values were 0.0010 °C cm²/W (20 μm yellow micro-LED), 0.0010 °C cm²/W (40 μm yellow micro-LED), 0.0009 °C cm²/W (20 μm red micro-LED), and 0.0013 °C cm²/W (40 μm red micro-LED).

Compared with the thermal coefficients based on input power, the diamond-substrate devices exhibited very similar αJ values across different sizes and wavelengths, with no obvious size dependence. This indicates the formation of an efficient thermal conduction path from the device mesa through the bonding layer into the diamond substrate, which effectively removes the generated heat and prevents accumulation. In contrast, the glass-substrate devices showed pronounced size dependence, with the αJ values of the 40 μm yellow and red devices being 4.3× and 3.8× higher than those of the corresponding 20 μm devices, closely matching their mesa area ratios. This confirms the heat accumulation effect in glass substrates: even at the same power density, larger devices operate at higher total input power, but the heat spreading is limited by the poor thermal conductivity of glass rather than by the mesa–substrate contact area. As a result, larger devices exhibit higher temperature rises despite their larger mesa.

It should be noted that the measured temperatures correspond to the surface hotspot temperatures recorded by the infrared thermal camera, rather than the true junction temperatures, due to factors such as surface emissivity and finite pixel resolution. Nevertheless, the extracted empirical parameters allow the establishment of a linear ΔT−P relationship based entirely on real-time experimental observations rather than numerical simulations or theoretical models, enabling prediction of device operation and maximum input power. This provides a non-contact, straightforward, and array-compatible method for assessing the thermal state of micro-LEDs.

Supplementary Fig. 4a, b compare the trends of peak wavelength and FWHM variation under different current densities for the devices described above. For the operating condition with the largest temperature difference—namely, 6 mA for the 20 μm devices and 8 mA for the 40 μm devices—the EL spectra of the diamond- and glass-substrate devices are compared in Fig. 3d. Compared with the diamond substrate, the glass-substrate devices exhibited significant spectral changes due to additional thermal effects, including a red-shift of the peak wavelength, broadening of the FWHM, and changes in spectral symmetry. In terms of peak wavelength, the comparisons between diamond- and glass-substrate devices were: 494.9 nm vs. 503.4 nm (Δλ = 8.5 nm) for the 20 μm yellow micro-LED, 504.6 nm vs. 516.7 nm (Δλ = 12.1 nm) for the 40 μm yellow micro-LED, 550.1 nm vs. 562.1 nm (Δλ = 12.0 nm) for the 20 μm red micro-LED, and 587.4 nm vs. 598.5 nm (Δλ = 11.1 nm) for the 40 μm red micro-LED.

For the InGaN materials that determine the emission wavelength, the bandgap energy follows the Varshni relation46: EgT=Eg0−(αT2)/(T+β) where EgT is the bandgap energy at temperature T, Eg0 is the bandgap energy at 0 K, and α and β are empirical constants. As temperature increases, EgT decreases, corresponding to a red-shift of the emission wavelength. This agrees with our observations from the diamond- and glass-substrate devices. However, due to variations in In composition between pixels, it remains difficult to establish a reliable quantitative relationship between temperature differences and wavelength shifts.

The FWHM comparisons between diamond- and glass-substrate devices were: 68.8 nm vs. 75.3 nm (Δλ = 6.5 nm) for the 20 μm yellow micro-LED, 66.2 nm vs. 73.8 nm (Δλ = 7.6 nm) for the 40 μm yellow micro-LED, 88.0 nm vs. 103.8 nm (Δλ = 15.8 nm) for the 20 μm red micro-LED, and 72.4 nm vs. 90.9 nm (Δλ = 18.5 nm) for the 40 μm red micro-LED, indicating typical thermal broadening effects. Notably, the broadening was asymmetric. We denote the low-wavelength side broadening as ΔFWHMleft and the high-wavelength side broadening as ΔFWHMright, and compare them separately for diamond- and glass-substrate devices: 20 μm yellow micro-LED, ΔFWHMleft = 26.7 nm vs. 31.6 nm (Δλ = 4.9 nm), ΔFWHMright = 42.1 nm vs. 43.7 nm (Δλ = 1.6 nm); 40 μm yellow micro-LED, ΔFWHMleft = 27.5 nm vs. 34.4 nm (Δλ = 6.9 nm), ΔFWHMright = 38.7 nm vs. 39.4 nm (Δλ = 0.7 nm); 20 μm red micro-LED, ΔFWHMleft = 36.1 nm vs. 47.8 nm (Δλ = 11.7 nm), ΔFWHMright = 51.9 nm vs. 56.0 nm (Δλ = 4.1 nm); 40 μm red micro-LED, ΔFWHMleft = 30.7 nm vs. 43.4 nm (Δλ = 12.7 nm), ΔFWHMright = 41.7 nm vs. 47.5 nm (Δλ = 5.8 nm). These results suggest that under thermal excitation, carriers are more likely to escape from deep localized states into shallower localized states, resulting in enhanced emission from shorter-wavelength components. Therefore, the broadening of FWHM is more strongly attributed to the expansion of the high-energy (left) side of the spectrum. The observed spectral degradations—specifically the asymmetric broadening and red-shift—directly reflect severe heat accumulation within low-thermal-conductivity substrates. As our thermal analysis confirms, poor heat dissipation in these materials causes a rapid temperature rise during operation. This thermal bottleneck restricts the maximum operating current, greatly limiting their use in high-power and high-density applications. To demonstrate this degradation, we used a glass substrate as a high-thermal-resistance baseline (a worst-case scenario). This baseline illustrates the rapid thermal failure that future dense, Tbps-class interconnects will face without advanced thermal management. In contrast, the diamond substrate offers a better thermal environment, allowing us to evaluate the device's intrinsic optoelectronic high-frequency potential without premature thermal limitations.

It should be noted that the slight signal elevation observed near 3 GHz in certain low-current S21 curves originates from the high-frequency noise floor of the measurement system under low signal-to-noise ratios. Because this noise-induced elevation occurs at frequencies beyond both the −3 dB and −6 dB cutoff thresholds, the signal smoothly crosses the threshold beforehand, ensuring the reliable extraction of the modulation bandwidths.

The modulation bandwidth of micro-LEDs is commonly described by the ABC model47: f3dBn∝1τn∝A+2Bn+3Cn2, where n is the carrier concentration, f3dBn is the modulation bandwidth, τn is the differential carrier lifetime, and A,B, and C are the Shockley–Read–Hall (SRH) recombination coefficient, radiative recombination coefficient, and Auger recombination coefficient, respectively. According to this model, at low carrier concentrations (i.e., low current densities), the bandwidth is mainly limited by SRH recombination, whereas at high injection levels, Auger recombination dominates the carrier lifetime.

Based on this understanding, we selected yellow and red micro-LEDs as our research focus. For long-wavelength InGaN-based micro-LEDs, the high indium content inherently leads to a large number of non-radiative recombination centers. Most existing studies, therefore, concentrate on improving epitaxial quality to enhance the quantum efficiency of long-wavelength devices, targeting applications such as microdisplays. In contrast, we adopt an opposite strategy: by exploiting the strong non-radiative recombination inherent in long-wavelength devices, we enhance the modulation bandwidth under low-injection conditions, and then subsequently tune the quantum efficiency to achieve co-optimization of both metrics. Accepting this efficiency-for-bandwidth trade-off is highly practical for long-wavelength emitters. Since their emission naturally matches the peak responsivity of standard silicon photodetectors, this approach provides a realistic path toward low-cost, integrated optical receivers in future CMOS platforms. We deliberately included conventional short-wavelength (blue, cyan, and green) micro-LEDs as a physical baseline to highlight this mechanism. Owing to their superior crystalline quality—and consequently, a negligible defect-related SRH coefficient—these 8-QW baseline devices inherently lack the fast, defect-driven non-radiative channels that we exploit to rapidly deplete carriers in the low-injection regime. It is evident that, compared with short-wavelength micro-LEDs, the yellow and red micro-LEDs fabricated in this work can already achieve an E–O modulation bandwidth approaching 3 GHz (2854.3 MHz for the 20 µm yellow micro-LED at 25 A/cm²) under very low current density conditions. This modulation bandwidth at low current densities (<100 A/cm²) surpasses previously reported values for InGaN-based yellow and red micro-LEDs. Notably, the 20 µm yellow micro-LED maintains a peak EQE of ~2.5% while achieving a 2.85 GHz bandwidth. This demonstrates that an ultra-high-speed response at low bias is not merely the result of poor material quality. Instead, careful epitaxial and structural design can successfully co-optimize both quantum efficiency and modulation bandwidth.

At the same time, considering that micro-LEDs are also commonly driven at high current densities and combined with high-frequency efficiency modulation schemes for ultra-high-speed optical communication, optimization of the modulation bandwidth at high current densities is also crucial. To this end, by employing an appropriate three-period quantum well design, the number of wells is sufficient to avoid the severe efficiency degradation associated with only 1–2 wells, while still concentrating injected carriers into fewer wells, resulting in higher average carrier concentrations that enhance the Bn and Cn² terms and improve the modulation bandwidth limit under high current injection. As discussed earlier, in comparison with short-wavelength blue, cyan, and green micro-LEDs employing eight-period quantum wells, the optimized 20 µm yellow and red micro-LEDs reported here achieved maximum E–O modulation bandwidths of 1659.7 and 2073.6 MHz, respectively, at 8 kA/cm², which demonstrate a substantial improvement over existing reports for InGaN-based yellow and red micro-LEDs at high current densities. According to the carrier dynamics of the ABC model, if a similar thin-QW strategy were applied to short-wavelength micro-LEDs, their high-current bandwidth limit could arguably be extended due to geometric confinement, accelerating the radiative and Auger recombination rates. However, lacking abundant non-radiative recombination centers, it would be difficult for them to achieve comparable high-speed performance at ultra-low current densities. This cross-wavelength comparison elucidates that our tailored device design for long-wavelength micro-LEDs is a deliberate and necessary dimensional trade-off. Consequently, we fabricated high-speed yellow and red micro-LEDs with modulation bandwidths optimized simultaneously in both the low and high current density regimes, demonstrating strong potential as high-speed chips for optical communication systems across different application scenarios.

High-speed optical interconnects using transfer-printed micro-LEDs on diamond substrates

To evaluate the high-speed optical communication potential of yellow and red micro-LEDs on diamond substrates, we first constructed an optical interconnect system based on an orthogonal frequency division multiplexing (OFDM) modulation scheme, as illustrated in Fig. 5a, b. The digital signal processing modules at both the transmitter (Tx) and receiver (Rx) were implemented in MATLAB. At the transmitter side, the OFDM signal generated by MATLAB was uploaded into an arbitrary waveform generator (AWG, Keysight M8190A, 12 GSa/s), and the output was amplified by an electrical amplifier (EA, Mini-Circuits ZHL-42W+, 4200 MHz). The amplified analog signal was then combined with a DC bias using a bias-tee (Mini-Circuits ZFBT-6GW + , 6 GHz) to modulate the micro-LED. Once driven, the micro-LED emitted an optical signal carrying the OFDM data, which was collimated by an aspheric lens (Thorlabs ACL12708U-A) and coupled into a step-index silica multimode optical fiber (400 µm core diameter, NA = 0.22) using a fiber collimator. We chose this large-core fiber primarily to handle the spatial etendue of the 20 µm and 40 µm transmitters, and to match the detector’s 0.5 mm active aperture. After passing through 1 m of fiber, the optical signal was collected by two additional aspheric lenses (Thorlabs ACL25416U-A) and focused onto a high-sensitivity APD (Hamamatsu C5658, 1 GHz, 0.5 mm aperture). Although this discrete setup successfully demonstrated the high-speed data link, future high-density interconnects will require shrinking the micro-LED emission areas (e.g., to <10 µm). This size reduction will relax the micro-lens design constraints, allowing direct, efficient coupling into multimode fibers while minimizing inter-channel crosstalk. The detected optical signal was converted into an electrical signal and captured by a high-speed digital signal analyzer (DSA, Agilent DSA90604A Infiniium, 20 GSa/s). Finally, the received signals were processed in MATLAB for demodulation and evaluation of communication performance.

In the transmitter module, the binary data were first converted from serial to parallel (S/P) and mapped into quadrature amplitude modulation (QAM) symbols of different orders. Hermitian symmetry was then applied to the OFDM frames to ensure real-valued time-domain signals after the inverse fast Fourier transform (IFFT). The up-sampling rate was set to 2. A cyclic prefix (CP) of length 1/16 NFFT was added at the beginning of each frame, followed by parallel-to-serial (P/S) conversion before transmission to the AWG. In practice, the system response exhibits frequency-dependent attenuation that results in a non-uniform signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) distribution. Binary phase shift keying (BPSK) signals were transmitted first to estimate the SNR distribution indirectly by calculating the error vector magnitude (EVM). Based on this estimation, bit loading was then performed. In the receiver module, the discrete signal from the DSA underwent S/P conversion, CP removal, FFT, down-sampling, channel estimation, QAM demapping, and finally P/S conversion.

The OFDM communication performance of 20 μm and 40 μm yellow and red micro-LEDs on diamond substrates under high current density operation was investigated. Considering the intrinsic device characteristics, the bias currents in the communication tests were set to be 32 mA (8 kA/cm2) for the 20 μm devices and 80 mA (5 kA/cm2) for the 40 μm devices, with the bandwidth of 1600 MHz. Figure 5c shows the relationship between data rate and bit error rate (BER) for different devices. Under the condition of meeting the forward error correction (FEC) threshold, the maximum data rates of the yellow micro-LEDs were 4.74 Gbps and 6.35 Gbps for 20 µm and 40 µm sizes, respectively, while those of the red micro-LEDs were 4.45 Gbps and 5.37 Gbps for 20 µm and 40 µm sizes, respectively. Figure 5d–g show the OFDM power spectra under the four test conditions, while Fig. 5h–k present the corresponding SNR distributions, bit distributions, and constellation diagrams. The average SNRs were 13.6897, 15.7637, 12.0619, and 14.0141 (dB), with spectral efficiencies of 2.9648, 3.9715, 2.7800, and 3.3590 (bit/s/Hz), respectively. Although the 20 μm yellow device exhibits higher DC optical power than the 20 μm red device, their maximum data rates are closely matched (4.74 Gbps vs. 4.45 Gbps). This behavior highlights a practical trade-off between absolute optical power and AC modulation bandwidth. The broader modulation bandwidth of the red device (Fig. 4d) mitigates high-frequency signal attenuation across the 1600 MHz OFDM spectrum, compensating for its lower DC power. Consequently, the average SNR difference between the two devices narrows to approximately 1.63 dB (13.69 dB vs. 12.06 dB). Following the Shannon limit, where channel capacity scales logarithmically with SNR, this minor SNR discrepancy translates into the highly comparable final data rates. In comparison, by achieving a more optimal balance of both bandwidth and DC optical power, the 40 μm yellow device achieved higher data rates and data transmission performance, as more bits could be allocated at the same modulation bandwidth.

As mentioned earlier, compared with conventional micro-LEDs, our transfer-printed yellow and red micro-LED chips not only exhibit considerable modulation bandwidth at high current densities but also retain high bandwidth at ultra-low currents, enabling the possibility of ultra-low-power, high-speed communication. The low-power, high-speed optical interconnect experiments were carried out using the 20 μm and 40 μm yellow devices, owing to their higher modulation bandwidth at low current densities and larger optical power. While the red devices demonstrated comparable bandwidths in swept-frequency measurements, their inherently lower quantum efficiency at µA-level injections could not provide the wideband SNR required by the oscilloscope for OOK signal capture. The test system was similar to the high-power OFDM setup described previously, with two key differences: the use of OOK modulation scheme instead of OFDM, and the direct combination of RF signals with DC bias rather than amplification by a high-power amplifier. The rest of the system remained unchanged.

During testing, the OOK data rate was fixed at 1.5 Gbps, limited by the ~1 GHz bandwidth of the photodetector, which prevented effective transmission at higher rates. At this 1.5 Gbps rate, measurements were performed at different operating currents. By adjusting the OOK signal amplitude (Vpp) while keeping the BER below the FEC threshold, the minimum acceptable Vpp was obtained. It is worth noting that these communication links rely entirely on the native modulation capabilities of the micro-LEDs, established without system-level pre-emphasis or advanced digital equalization techniques, such as feed-forward equalization (FFE) or decision feedback equalization (DFE). For the 40 μm device, the operating currents and corresponding Vpp values were 100 μA/450 mV (6.25 A/cm²), 80 μA/500 mV (5 A/cm²), 60 μA/600 mV (3.75 A/cm²), and 50 μA/650 mV (3.125 A/cm²). For the 20 μm device, the tested conditions were 60 μA/450 mV (15 A/cm²), 50 μA/450 mV (12.5 A/cm²), 40 μA/450 mV (10 A/cm²), 30 μA/500 mV (7.5 A/cm²), and 25 μA/650 mV (6.25 A/cm²). The corresponding eye diagrams and BER results are shown in Fig. 5l, m and Supplementary Fig. 6a–g. The power consumptions, calculated as the product of the operating current and voltage to evaluate the energy consumption characteristics of the micro-LEDs22, are presented under different operating conditions in Fig. 5n. For the 40 μm device, the lowest power consumption was 165.4 μW at 50 μA (DC voltage = 3.308 V), while for the 20 μm device, the lowest power consumption was 84.0 μW at 25 μA (DC voltage = 3.360 V). Unlike in the high-current regime, micro-LEDs in the low-current regime are more strongly affected by nonlinearities because their operating state is close to the device turn-on threshold. This leads to cutoff issues that prevent further extension into lower-current operation simply by increasing Vpp. Furthermore, the nonlinear electrical-to-optical conversion inherent to voltage-driven modulation accounts for the level asymmetry observed in the low-power eye diagrams (Fig. 5l, m), where the optical output is compressed during the negative voltage swing. Although this amplitude asymmetry does not prevent the current data links from operating reliably below the FEC threshold, it can be effectively resolved in practical applications by integrating the micro-LEDs with CMOS driver ICs that operate in a linear current-driven mode.

We further calculated the energy efficiency–current relationship for the 20 μm and 40 μm yellow micro-LEDs, as shown in Fig. 5o. For the 40 μm device, the best energy efficiency was 0.110 pJ/bit at 50 μA, whereas the 20 μm device achieved 0.056 pJ/bit at 25 μA. This suggests that, if such a micro-LED array were used as an optical communication chip, it would theoretically be possible to integrate 144 pixels within an area of 1 mm², providing a data rate of ~216 Gbps with a total DC power consumption of only 12.1 mW. This ultra-low power requirement avoids the severe thermal effects associated with conventional high-power micro-LEDs and improves array stability. Furthermore, given that the mesa-to-pixel area ratio of our current 20 μm device is only ~6.1%, reducing the pixel size could further increase the achievable data-rate density. Note that all power metrics discussed here assess only the transmitter-side energy efficiency. The receiver power is currently excluded because capturing high-speed signals on an oscilloscope requires a high SNR, dictating the use of a commercial APD module with a large power overhead. In practical applications, integrated CMOS optical receivers will significantly reduce this receiver power penalty.

For practical array applications, optical coupling design, optical and electrical crosstalk between pixels, and the development of matched high-responsivity, high-bandwidth photodetector arrays must also be considered. Nevertheless, the experimental results clearly demonstrate that long-wavelength, small-size micro-LEDs can support high-speed communication across a wide current density range—from a few A/cm² to several kA/cm². This makes them particularly promising for low-power, high-speed applications. As epitaxial techniques for high-indium InGaN continue to advance, the fundamental trade-off among emission wavelength, quantum efficiency, and modulation bandwidth is expected to be further mitigated. Given their enhanced spectral matching with the peak responsivity of standard silicon photodetectors, high-speed long-wavelength micro-LEDs show significant promise for next-generation highly integrated interconnects.

In summary, InGaN-based yellow and red micro-LEDs on diamond substrates were fabricated through a single-pixel flip-chip transfer-printing process using the PDMS stamp. Thermal comparison with glass-substrate devices confirmed the enhanced heat management capability of the diamond-substrate devices. In addition, microlenses for emission angle optimization were fabricated by two-photon lithography to enhance coupling into optical interconnect systems. Regarding high-speed performance, both 20 µm yellow and red micro-LEDs achieved high bandwidths at both low and high current densities. Specifically, the 20 µm yellow micro-LED reached 2135.8 MHz (E–E bandwidth) and 2850.4 MHz (E–O bandwidth) at a current density of 6.25 A/cm², while the 20 µm red micro-LED achieved 1577.0 MHz (E–E bandwidth) and 2593.4 MHz (E–O bandwidth) at 50 A/cm². At high current injection, maximum bandwidths of 654.0 MHz/1659.7 MHz (E–E/E–O bandwidth, 20 µm yellow micro-LED at 8 kA/cm²) and 595.6 MHz/2073.6 MHz (E–E/E–O bandwidth, 20 µm red micro-LED at 8 kA/cm²) were obtained. By constructing a 1-m optical fiber interconnect test system with an OFDM modulation scheme, maximum data rates of 4.74 Gbps (20 µm yellow micro-LED at 8 kA/cm²), 6.35 Gbps (40 µm yellow micro-LED at 5 kA/cm²), 4.45 Gbps (20 µm red micro-LED at 8 kA/cm²), and 5.37 Gbps (40 µm red micro-LED at 5 kA/cm²) were demonstrated. Furthermore, using OOK modulation, the low-power high-speed performance of yellow micro-LEDs was validated, achieving 1.5 Gbps at currents of 25 µA (6.25 A/cm², 20 µm micro-LED) and 50 µA (3.125 A/cm², 40 µm micro-LED), with energy efficiencies of 0.056 and 0.110 pJ/bit, respectively. Based on these results, long-wavelength InGaN-based micro-LEDs with both high energy efficiency and high-speed response were successfully achieved. By combining µA-level driving currents with integrated microlenses for spatial beam shaping, this approach provides a viable pathway to mitigate the power and crosstalk constraints anticipated in high-density array packaging. Such device-level characteristics demonstrate the potential for heterogeneous integration with CMOS drivers, serving as the core light sources for energy-efficient chip-to-chip optical interconnects.

Methods

Epitaxial growth

The yellow and red micro-LEDs for transfer printing shared a similar epitaxial structure, grown by MOCVD on 4-inch Si (111) substrates. In sequence, the epilayer consisted of an ~1.3 µm AlN/AlGaN/GaN buffer layer; an ~1.8 µm n-GaN electron transport layer with a Si doping concentration above 1 × 10¹⁹ cm⁻³; ten pairs of low-indium-content InGaN/GaN superlattice SRLs; three pairs of high-indium-content InGaN/GaN MQWs in the active region, with well and barrier thicknesses of 2.5 nm and 23.7 nm, respectively; and finally a p-AlGaN electron blocking layer and a p-GaN hole injection layer (Mg doping concentration >1 × 10¹⁹ cm⁻³), with a total thickness of ~190 nm.

The short-wavelength micro-LED epilayer, represented here by a blue-emitting structure, was grown using the same equipment and substrates as the long-wavelength devices. The epitaxial stack consisted of an ~1.6 µm AlN/AlGaN/GaN buffer layer; an ~3 µm n-GaN electron transport layer with a Si doping concentration above 1 × 10¹⁹ cm⁻³; 12 pairs of composition-graded InGaN/GaN shallow-well SRLs; 4 pairs of InGaN/GaN SRLs with slightly reduced indium content and thickness compared to the quantum wells; an active region comprising 8 pairs of InGaN/GaN MQWs with well and barrier thicknesses of 2.8 nm and 13 nm, respectively; and finally a p-AlGaN electron blocking layer together with a p-GaN hole injection layer (Mg doping concentration >1 × 10¹⁹ cm⁻³), with a total thickness of ~400 nm. Supplementary Fig. 7a, b show HAADF-STEM cross-sectional images of the complete epitaxial structure of the blue micro-LED and a magnified view of the active region and SRLs, respectively.

Device fabrication

The fabrication process is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 8a–e. First, ~60 nm of indium tin oxide (ITO) was deposited on the wafer surface by magnetron sputtering. The mesa patterns were then defined by UV lithography, followed by wet etching to remove the ITO in the non-emission regions. Subsequently, the p-GaN and MQWs layers outside the mesa were etched by inductively coupled plasma (ICP) to expose the n-GaN layer. Rapid thermal annealing was then performed at 550 °C in N₂ ambient (Supplementary Fig. 8a).

Next, ~1 μm of SiO₂ was deposited on the wafer surface using plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition (PECVD) to protect the epitaxial layers during the subsequent wet etching of the silicon substrate. UV lithography was again used to define the overall pixel pattern (~110 × 60 μm2) and the side anchor structures. ICP etching was employed to etch through the SiO₂ layer, n-GaN layer, buffer layer, and partially into the Si substrate (~2 μm deep) to provide sufficiently large windows for wet etching (Supplementary Fig. 8b). The underlying silicon was removed by immersing the wafer in 20% KOH solution at 80 °C, exploiting the anisotropic etching characteristics of KOH to selectively undercut the pixel mesa while retaining silicon beneath the edge anchors as temporary supports. The remaining SiO₂ protective layer was then removed using buffered oxide etchant (BOE) (Supplementary Fig. 8c).

After realizing the suspended pixels, further semiconductor processing was carried out to achieve complete electrical interconnections. A 300 nm SiO₂ passivation layer was deposited by PECVD, and the contact openings were defined by lithography. ICP etching was used to remove ~250 nm of SiO₂, followed by BOE etching of the remaining 50 nm to minimize surface damage from dry etching (Supplementary Fig. 8d). Finally, Ti (30 nm)/Au (200 nm) electrodes were deposited on the opened regions of the pixel surface using the lithography and thermal evaporation lift-off process, enabling subsequent bonding with metal electrodes on the target substrate (Supplementary Fig. 8e).

The fabrication of metal electrodes on the target substrates for transfer printing (including both diamond and glass substrates) was carried out using a maskless photolithography system. The diamond/glass substrates were first treated with a UV–ozone cleaner to remove residual organic contamination on the surface. Direct-write photolithography combined with a lift-off process was then employed, and Ti (30 nm)/Au (200 nm) was deposited by thermal evaporation to form the electrodes for subsequent flip-chip bonding.

Transfer printing method

The transfer printing process was extended from the PDMS stamp-based method to enable single-chip transfer printing and flip-chip bonding, as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 9a–f. Three different PDMS stamps were employed, denoted as PDMS-A, PDMS-B, and PDMS-C. First, the suspended pixels were picked up using PDMS-A (Supplementary Fig. 9a,b). The chips were then transferred from PDMS-A to PDMS-B, whose total contact area was larger, by exploiting adhesion differences. It should be noted that the chip orientation relative to the PDMS stamp was reversed during this step—from the metal side contacting PDMS to the GaN side contacting PDMS (Supplementary Fig. 9c). PDMS-C was designed for quantitative transfer of solder paste. By controlling the contact between PDMS and the paste, small amounts of solder paste could be picked up (Supplementary Fig. 9d). When PDMS-C was brought into contact with the metal pads on the target substrate (e.g., diamond), the solder paste was precisely transferred to the selected electrode areas (Supplementary Fig. 9e). Finally, the micro-LED chips on PDMS-B were transferred to the target substrate with the metal side facing downward, where the metal contacts were connected through the solder paste (Supplementary Fig. 9f). Flip-chip bonding was then achieved by heating the devices on a hot plate at 255 °C.

The fabrication was performed with a Nanoscribe Quantum X system, using IP-S negative photoresist, which offers high resolution and low surface roughness, making it suitable for micro- and nano-optical component fabrication. While high-throughput techniques such as nanoimprint lithography (NIL) are more advantageous for scaling up to large-area arrays, TPL was deliberately chosen for this proof-of-concept stage. As a maskless direct-writing technique, TPL provides the geometric freedom necessary for rapid prototyping, allowing us to experimentally optimize the micro-lens parameters based on simulation results without the prohibitive costs of fabricating physical molds. Furthermore, the optimized 3D structures fabricated via TPL in this work have the potential to serve as high-precision master molds for future large-scale NIL processes.

Measurement method

The HAADF-STEM characterization was performed using an FEI Talos F200X G2 system, with the sample prepared by a focused ion beam (FIB) in an FEI Scios 2 HiVac system. SEM images of the micro-LED chips were obtained using the Sigma 300 system. The I–V curves were measured with a Keithley 2614B precision source meter. Optical power values were measured using a power meter (Thorlabs S120VC). During testing, the samples were placed in close contact with the detector surface, and the measured optical power was corrected according to the actual EL spectra and the response curve of the power meter. Angular emission profiles were measured using a high-precision 360° rotation stage combined with a power meter probe, with the sample mounted at the center of the rotation stage and the probe positioned 0.5 m away to record optical power at different angles. EL spectra were recorded using a CAS 140CT−152 spectrometer. The chip surface temperature was measured with a Fotric 246 M infrared thermal camera equipped with a high-resolution lens (50 μm, LV50D2-0155).

Bandwidth measurements were performed using a vector network analyzer (VNA, Pico 106, 6 GHz) by applying frequency sweeps from 30 MHz to 3 GHz. The RF signal was combined with a DC bias via a bias tee (Mini-Circuits ZFBT-6GW+, 6 GHz) to drive the micro-LED. The emitted optical signal was coupled into an optical fiber system and delivered to a high-bandwidth photodetector (SPD-4, 3 GHz). The corresponding electrical signal was then fed back to the VNA to obtain the S21 response curves.
