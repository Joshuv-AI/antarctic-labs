Final Technical Report Award DE-SC0022229
Project Title: Shaping Symmetry and Molding Morphology of Triply-Periodic Network Assemblies
via Molecular Design and Processing of Block Copolymers
Lead PI: Gregory M. Grason (UMass Amherst)
Co-PIs: E. Bryan Coughlin (UMass Amherst); Edwin L. Thomas (Texas A&M Univ.)

Award Period: 09/01/2021—02/28/2025
Overview: This project aimed to uncover molecular design rules for block copolymer (BCP)
assemblies of triply-periodic network (TPN) phases, which are highly sought after, yet elusive,
nanostructures for many functional material applications that rely on their combination of
symmetry and polycontinuous domain connectivity [R1, R2], such as topological-photonics,
supercapacitors and ultrafiltration media. While they have been a target of “bottom up” approaches
to nanostructured, hybrid materials, the ability to manipulate the tubular network morphology of
TPN assemblies beyond the cubic double-gyroid (DG) phase has advanced relatively little. This
project developed the fundamental knowledge needed for: i) tailorable molecular design that
achieves non-canonical TPN symmetries; and ii) unprecedented control over the intra-unit cell
morphology of TPN and local network node functionality. Collaborative efforts included
advancing theory of molecule-to-TPN morphology map; novel characterization/metrology of sub-
domain morphology; and synthesis of precisely defined and labeled BCP for direct imaging of key
nano-scale features. The research of this project integrates and advances state-of-the-art
theoretical methods for geometrically-complex BCP assembly (Grason), synthesis of precisely-
defined BCP architectures (Coughlin), and quantitative and multi-scale approaches to
morphological characterization of BCP assembly (Thomas). Project research has resulted in
twelve peer reviewed articles [P1-P12], one manuscript currently under peer review [P13] and two
Ph.D. dissertations [P14-P15].

Project results:

Established the “medial packing” paradigm for complex BCP assembly. A long-standing
challenge of predictive understanding of BCP assembly is quantifiable understanding of the role
of packing frustration [R3, R4], which refers to the thermodynamic cost of variable local shapes
of BCP chains composing at assembly. Particularly vexing has been a generic measure of local
“thickness” corresponding to the distinct polymer blocks that extend from the inter-material
dividing surface (IMDS) into the “middle” of the complex mesodomain. Work in this award has
formalized the connection between molecular configurations in BCP and the purely geometric
notion of medial maps of complex 3D volumes [R5]. We first proposed the medial thickness as a
well-defined measure of BCP packing that could be applied to assess variable chain stretching
based on information on the IMDS alone [P2], Fig. 1A. Based on this idea, as well as our recent
advances in 3D tomographic reconstruction of BCP domains via slice-and-view scanning electron
microscopy (SVSEM) [R6], we developed the algorithms to analyze the medial thickness

minor domain [P6]. This theoretical advance not only resolves a long-standing fundamental
question about ordered networks of block copolymers, but also establishes a general framework
for quantitative modeling of thermodynamic consequences of amphiphilic chain packing in a much
broader class of low-symmetry and complex nanodomain shapes.

 Established the “mesoatomic” structure model of tubular BCP networks. We have established a
new structural understanding of the “natural building blocks” of double network phases, which
decomposes tubular network assemblies into a unique set of high-symmetry molecular groupings
that are the analogs of micelle-like domains of spherical phases [P5]. A set of physically motivated

principles points to the non-convex nodal volumes as the natural mesoatomic units of double-
networks (Fig. 2A), which provide a wholly new perspective on the collective “degrees of
freedom” of tubular crystals as well as putative pathways for their formation. For example, we
considered the simplest possible kinetic model of tubular double-network crystal growth suggests
that the relative strength of intra- versus inter-network bonds between mesoatomic units templates
a rich and previously unexplored kinetics of inter-network catenation upon assembly (Fig. 2B).

Additionally, we have characterized the complex and real-space reconfiguration pathways of a
BCP system converting from one type of inter-catenated, cubic network phase (DG) to another
(DD) for the first time [P11]. While many amphiphilic soft matter systems are capable of
exhibiting DG as well as DD intercatenated network cubic network phases, however, the specific
transformation pathways between distinct crystalline mesostructures have remained obscure [R9-
R10]. We used SVSEM tomography to directly image this transformation for an arrested system
of polystyrene-b-polydimethylsiloxane (PS-PDMS) block copolymers and observed, remarkably,
that this transformation maintains network connectivity and inter-network catenation across a
finite width gradient zone (Fig. 4A). This provides the first spatially-resolved and quantitatively
mapped view of the “reaction pathway” between the distinct trihedral mesoatomic nodal units of
DG: pairwise fusion along two strut directions to form the tetrahedral mesoatoms of the DD (Fig.
4B). Motivated to understand the observed continuous transformation pathway between DG and

Human resources development: Project research involved the training and mentoring of four
(4) postdoctoral researchers and five (5) graduate students, two of whom completed their Ph.D.s
during the award period. These junior researchers developed specialized expertise in a
combination of methods for advanced physical modeling of macromolecular materials,
computational analysis of nanostructured materials morphologies, preparative chemistry and
chemical characterization of polymeric materials, and state-of-the-art characterization approaches
to nanostructured materials Of the postdocs and graduate students who have moved to new
positions after their work on the project, three (3) are employed in industrial positions related to
materials science and three (3) are employed in faculty positions in the materials science field.
