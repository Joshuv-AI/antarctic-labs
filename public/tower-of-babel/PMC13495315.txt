Application of a virtual simulation teaching model in enhancing the clinical understanding of cataract surgery among undergraduate medical students: a single-arm pre-post interventional study
Authors: Xiaoli Xiang, Xiaoyu Zhu, Zijia Ji, Min Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

The role of virtual simulation technology in undergraduate medical education is undergoing a profound shift from being a supplementary tool, to a core educational paradigm. The limitations of traditional teaching models are becoming increasingly apparent, particularly in terms of fostering students’ deep understanding of theoretical knowledge, mastering practical skills, and cultivating teamwork and communication abilities [1–3]. As an innovative teaching model, simulation-based instruction has been widely applied in the medical education field in recent years [4, 5]. By simulating real clinical scenarios, this model allows students to practice and master clinical skills in a safe environment, thereby deepening their understanding and retention of theoretical knowledge, while enhancing their clinical diagnostic and treatment capabilities [6].

In ophthalmology, cataracts are the leading cause of blindness worldwide, holding significant clinical importance [7]. Traditional cataract education relies primarily on theoretical lectures and viewing surgical videos, making it difficult for students to gain hands-on experience. In particular, students only gain practical experience regarding high-risk procedures and the management of complications when encountering these during surgery. As such, virtual simulation technology creates a highly immersive, repeatable, and risk-free learning environment. It provides medical students with an effective pathway to deeply understand the principles of cataract surgery and cultivate risk awareness and clinical reasoning.

Although the advantages of virtual simulation in medical education are becoming increasingly apparent, research on its application in ophthalmic surgery education remains in the exploratory stages [8]. Existing studies have primarily focused on the skills training of residents, while research targeting undergraduate students is relatively limited. Moreover, there is a particular lack of learning outcome evaluations among medical students not specializing in ophthalmology [9].

Against this backdrop, this study used a virtual simulation teaching platform for cataract surgery at Yangzhou University to systematically evaluate the feasibility and learner acceptance of virtual simulation instruction among undergraduate medical students. The study aimed to explore the platform’s role in enhancing students’ understanding of cataract surgery-related knowledge and clinical cognition, analyze students’ learning experiences and feedback, and provide empirical evidence to inform future platform optimizations.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a single-arm pre-post intervention design to evaluate the feasibility and learner acceptance of a virtual simulation platform for cataract surgery in helping undergraduate medical students understand the principles of cataract surgery and develop clinical cognition. By collecting data on students’ practice scores and teaching effectiveness evaluation questionnaires, this study comprehensively analyzed the impact of virtual simulation instruction on students’ cognitive development, learning experience, and satisfaction.

Sample source

Convenience sampling was employed to select study subjects amongst those undergraduate students majoring in clinical medicine, and having completed the cataract surgery training module on a virtual simulation platform. A total of 31 valid questionnaires were collected.

Educational intervention: virtual simulation platform for cataract surgery

The virtual simulation platform for cataract education used in this study is an interactive learning system developed using computer graphics and virtual reality technology (http://jsxngx.seu.edu.cn/ResourceDetail.aspx?Id=EEA16A6F1D111825; Fig. 1). With phacoemulsification surgery as its core content, the platform fully replicates the entire clinical process, from preoperative preparation to postoperative management. The platform comprises 18 teaching modules divided into four stages according to the clinical treatment sequence: preoperative preparation and assessment (Modules 1–5), surgical procedures (Modules 6–16), immediate postoperative care (Module 17), and postoperative management (Module 18). Structure and content of virtual simulation training

Platform features

The platform was designed to incorporate a modular structure aligned with the clinical workflow. This allows students to progress sequentially through the 18 modules, or to focus on specific areas requiring additional practice. The provision of real-time feedback is an integral component of the procedure, with critical errors such as oversized capsulorhexis or posterior capsule rupture flagged by warning prompts. High-risk scenarios specifically designed for this pedagogical purpose include phacoemulsification, energy control, and posterior capsule rupture management. The aim of simulating such scenarios is to enhance students’ understanding of challenging situations. The platform automatically records students’ performance scores, enabling them to track their progress by comparing initial and highest scores. Furthermore, a comprehensive learning resource library is available throughout the learning process including instructional videos, procedural guidance, and principle-based explanations.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The research protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of The Fifth Clinical College of Yangzhou University (approval number: 2025-KY-ZX39).

As this study involved fully anonymous data collection, with no personally identifiable information contained in either the questionnaires or performance screenshots, and because the research merely evaluated routine teaching outcomes without any intervention, participants faced no potential risks. Therefore, the Institutional Review Board waived the requirement for informed consent.

On the first page of the questionnaire, participants were informed of the study’s purpose, data usage, and anonymity protection. Voluntary completion and submission of the questionnaire were considered as implied consent to participate. All data were used solely for academic research purposes, with no breach of personal privacy.

Structure and content of the teaching effectiveness questionnaire

Data collection procedures

The data were collected between November and December 2025. Upon completion of the platform-based learning, students were invited to complete the teaching effectiveness questionnaire. This was to be done anonymously, using the Wenjuanxing platform. Requiring approximately 10–15 min to complete, the questionnaire was administered in a fully anonymous format, with no personal identifying information collected. Concurrently, students uploaded screenshots of their initial and highest scores as evidence of their performance. Although these screenshots included usernames created by the students, they could not be linked to students’ real identities. As such, the screenshots cannot be traced to individual participants, thereby ensuring data anonymity. After data collection, two researchers conducted a verification and organization process.

Statistical analysis methods

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 25.0 statistical software. All quantitative data were first subjected to the Shapiro-Wilk normality test, and distribution patterns were evaluated using Q-Q plots and histograms. Appropriate descriptive and inferential statistical methods were selected based on the normality test results. Qualitative data are described using frequencies and proportions (n [%]). The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare initial and best scores. Spearman’s rank correlation was used to examine the associations between variables. Content analysis was performed on the responses to the open-ended questions. Two researchers independently reviewed all open-ended responses, extracted keywords and themes, and calculated the frequency and percentage of mentions for each category after categorization and summarization. In cases of classification inconsistencies, a third researcher participated in discussions to reach a consensus. All figures were generated using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). All statistical analyses were two-tailed tests with a significance level of α = 0.05.

Results

Basic characteristics of participants

This study included 31 undergraduate students majoring in clinical medicine. Among them, 17 were male (54.8%) and 14 were female (45.2%), with a mean age of 21.39 ± 0.558 years. Regarding clinical internship experience, 11 participants (35.5%) had prior experience in ophthalmology, while 20 (64.5%) had no such experience. The total duration of student use of the virtual simulation platform was 61.06 ± 34.10 min, ranging from 20 to 150 min.

Changes in student performance following virtual simulation training

Descriptive statistics showed that the median initial score was 80.00 (IQR: 67.00–91.00), and the median best score was 89.00 (IQR: 79.00–99.00). The Wilcoxon signed-rank test revealed that the best scores were significantly higher than the initial scores (Z=-2.936, P = 0.003). Regarding platform experience, scores were 3.42 ± 1.18 for ease of use, 3.77 ± 0.96 for the realism of the virtual scenarios, and 3.84 ± 0.93 for the detail of high-risk procedure simulations.

Student suggestions for platform improvement

Analysis of the open-ended questions revealed multiple suggestions for platform improvement. The most frequently suggested improvement was “optimizing program/fixing lag” (16.1%), followed by “enhancing instructional guidance” (12.9%), “increasing practice time/opportunities” (9.7%), “making error prompts more noticeable” (6.5%), and “fixing bugs” (6.5%). These suggestions provide clear directions for future platform optimization.

Correlation analysis

Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was performed to explore the associations among platform usage, subjective evaluation, capability improvement, and platform design features. No significant correlation was found between total usage time and score difference (R = 0.139, P = 0.455). Overall, satisfaction was strongly and positively correlated with willingness to recommend (R = 0.905, P < 0.001). Satisfaction also significantly correlated with improvement in surgical complication management (R = 0.642, P < 0.001) and risk prediction awareness (R = 0.780, P < 0.001). Regarding platform design features, the level of detail in the high-risk step simulation was significantly correlated with improvement in complication management (R = 0.767, P < 0.001). A weak positive correlation was observed between surgical step prompts and procedural standardization (R = 0.304, P = 0.018), whereas real-time error feedback showed no significant correlation (R = 0.240, P = 0.078). Experimental principle explanation was significantly correlated with surgical step prompts (R = 0.587, P < 0.001) and project guidance videos (R = 0.793, P < 0.001), but not with clinical thinking (R = 0.265, P = 0.149).

Prior knowledge of cataract surgery positively correlated with surgical procedural standardization (R = 0.386, P = 0.032), and perceived advantages over traditional teaching (R = 0.363, P = 0.045). No significant correlations were found between clinical internship experience and capability improvement indicators (P > 0.05).

Discussion

The results of this study show that the virtual simulation training for cataracts improved students’ overall assessment scores. Moreover, some students with low initial grades achieved significant improvement through repeated exercises, suggesting that the virtual simulation platform may have the potential to narrow the cognitive gap among students. This result is consistent with the findings of simulation teaching research in other subject majors [1]. It also supports the core view of situational learning theory, that the understanding of knowledge must be deepened through repeated practice in real situations [10].

Given that students could repeat modules multiple times and the assessment drew from a fixed question pool, the observed increase from initial to best scores may partly reflect testing effects or memorization rather than deep conceptual understanding. Thus, these score changes should be interpreted cautiously as indicators of platform engagement and short-term knowledge retention.

In our survey, student evaluations of various aspects of the cataract virtual simulation platform were generally positive. Regarding platform experience, the simulation of high-risk steps received the highest scores for detail, indicating that the platform may have helped address challenges in cataract surgery teaching. Reports have shown that virtual reality simulators, such as the EyeSi simulator, can effectively train students in high-risk, precise surgical steps, significantly reducing the incidence of intraoperative complications such as posterior capsule rupture [9, 11, 12]. The high level of approval expressed by students in this study regarding the simulation of high-risk steps is consistent with the educational potential of the virtual simulation platform design.

In terms of self-reported learning outcomes, students reported the highest scores for clinical reasoning skills and risk prediction awareness, whereas their understanding of surgical procedural standardization scored relatively lower. This phenomenon may be related to the platform’s design regarding explaining underlying mechanisms; students tend to focus more on clinical reasoning, while their understanding of the specific “how” to perform each step still needs to be strengthened. In the future, consideration should be given to enhancing the clarity and repetition of procedural explanations during key steps to improve students’ mastery of standardized surgical techniques [13].

Reports indicate that the quality and structure of training programs have a far greater impact on learning outcomes than the duration of training alone [14]. In this study, there was no significant correlation between the platform usage time and performance improvement. This finding suggests that simply increasing the duration of use does not equate to improved learning outcomes in virtual simulation-based instruction; the quality of practice is more critical than the duration.

The learning resources most valued by students in this study were “immediate feedback on incorrect actions” and “surgical procedure prompts.” This aligns with the core elements of simulation-based education: Effective simulation-based education should provide timely and specific feedback to help students correct misconceptions [15]. In traditional clinical education, students often receive feedback only when they make mistakes during actual surgeries, whereas virtual simulation platforms can provide similar learning opportunities in a risk-free environment.

In terms of practice, the steps students most frequently repeated were “continuous circular capsulorhexis” and “phacoemulsification of the lens nucleus,” which are not only the most technically challenging aspects of cataract surgery, but are also critical to surgical success. Students’ self-reported tendency to identify technical challenges and engage in targeted practice aligns with the theory of deliberate practice [16]. There were no significant differences in capsulorhexis performance between simulator and wet-lab training; however, simulators offer the advantages of repeatability and lower cost [17, 18]. This finding is consistent with students’ focus on practicing this step in the present study.

Among the most memorable aspects, corneal incision was the most frequently mentioned, reflecting students’ attention to the initial steps of surgery. Notably, 38.7% of the students were particularly impressed by the “recovery procedure for posterior capsule rupture.” Posterior capsule rupture is the most common high-risk complication in cataract surgery, and training with virtual reality simulators has been shown to significantly reduce its incidence [19, 20]. Moreover, our findings indicate that simulating high-risk scenarios offers unique educational value, helping students develop an awareness of risks and strategies for responding to unexpected situations.

Given the small sample size (N = 31), all correlation analyses in this study are exploratory. Weak correlations should not be overinterpreted; they require validation in larger cohorts. Correlation analysis revealed an association between students’ subjective evaluations of the platform and their self-reported improvements in understanding. The high degree of consistency between overall satisfaction and willingness to recommend the platform suggested good user acceptance. As for the correlation between the level of detail in simulations of high-risk procedures and improved understanding of complication management, this further suggests its educational potential in the design of high-risk procedures.

Furthermore, surgical procedure prompts positively correlated with students’ understanding of surgical standardization, suggesting that standardized step-by-step guidance may be associated with skill comprehension. Explanations of experimental principles showed a significant positive correlation with surgical procedure prompts and instructional videos, but no significant correlation with clinical reasoning. These findings suggest that students rely more heavily on operational guidance, placing relatively less emphasis on mechanistic explanations. Future platform optimizations could consider integrating principle explanations more closely with specific operational steps to enhance student motivation to learn.

Students’ suggestions for improving the platform primarily focused on two areas: technical performance and instructional guidance. Usability received the lowest score among all dimensions, which aligns with students’ suggestions to “optimize the program and resolve lag issues.” Future platform optimizations should prioritize technical smoothness, while enhancing the clarity and immediacy of instructional guidance. Additionally, some students requested “more practice time and opportunities,” reflecting their desire for additional hands-on experience. This indirectly validates the value of the virtual simulation platform; students gain practice opportunities in a safe and repeatable environment that traditional teaching cannot provide.

This study’s limitations must be noted. First, the absence of a control group, which is the most significant limitation, hindered the establishment of a causal attribution of the observed score improvements to the virtual simulation intervention. Second, the small sample size limited statistical power and generalizability. Third, our outcome measures included self-reported confidence and platform-generated scores, neither of which directly measures clinical competence. Fourth, the fixed question pool and unlimited attempts may have introduced testing effects. Our findings should be interpreted as preliminary evidence of feasibility and learner acceptance, rather than definitive proof of educational effectiveness. Future research should employ randomized controlled designs with objective, blinded assessments of surgical skills. Additionally, integrating learning behavior data, such as operational trajectories, error patterns, and practice frequency, from the virtual simulation platform with performance outcomes could help elucidate the dynamic learning process. It is also important to explore the potential of combining virtual simulation with other teaching methods to develop blended learning models in ophthalmology education.

Conclusion

This single arm pre-post study offers preliminary evidence that a virtual simulation platform for cataract surgery is feasible and well received by undergraduate medical students. Students reported high satisfaction and perceived improvements in procedural understanding and risk awareness. Features such as real time feedback, step-by-step guidance, and high-risk scenario simulations were identified as valuable. However, because of the absence of a control group, small sample size, and reliance on self-reported outcomes, causal conclusions cannot be drawn. The observed score improvements may in part reflect testing effects. Despite these limitations, the high level of student acceptance and the identified areas for technical improvement, including software fluidity and instructional clarity, provide a useful foundation for future controlled studies on virtual simulation in ophthalmology education.
