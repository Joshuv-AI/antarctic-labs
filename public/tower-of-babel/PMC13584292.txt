Residual muscle activity associated with phantom limb movements after transhumeral amputation without surgical reinnervation: a preliminary ultrasound study
Authors: Susannah Engdahl, Ahmed Bashatah, György Lévay, Rahul Kaliki, Ajul Shah, Ananya Dhawan, Siddhartha Sikdar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transhumeral amputations often result in significant functional limitations. Multiarticulated myoelectric prostheses for individuals with transhumeral amputations are often unreliable because of limitations in signal quality and challenges with accurately detecting motion intent from the available musculature in the residual limb, leading to high prosthesis rejection rates. Surgical approaches like targeted muscle reinnervation have been proposed to mitigate these challenges by biologically amplifying motor signals but are not suitable for all individuals. There is intriguing evidence that distinct muscle activation patterns associated with voluntary mobilization of the phantom limb may be produced in the residual limb after transhumeral amputation even without surgical intervention. However, this phenomenon has not been sufficiently investigated. This study explores the potential of ultrasound imaging to visualize muscle activity associated with phantom hand movements in an individual with transhumeral amputation who has not undergone targeted muscle reinnervation. We demonstrate that ultrasound imaging can capture these patterns, with each tested phantom movement corresponding to unique residual limb muscle activity. The activation patterns for each motion showed greater consistency across repetitions (correlation range: 0.41–0.86) compared to their average similarity with other motions (correlation range 0.06–0.32). These findings provide preliminary imaging evidence suggesting functional innervation of the residual upper arm muscles with digit-level specificity after transhumeral amputation. We recommend further research into modalities that can sense phantom limb mobilization to improve prosthesis control after transhumeral amputation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12984-026-02073-9.

Introduction

Any upper limb amputation can lead to significant functional limitations, but these impairments are often more severe in individuals with transhumeral or other proximal levels of limb loss [1]. However, research on transhumeral prostheses has been less extensive than prostheses for distal limb loss [2] and transhumeral prostheses are also rejected at a higher rate [3, 4]. One factor contributing to this rejection is the challenge of accurately inferring the prosthesis user’s volitional motor intent from surface electromyography (EMG) [5] given limitations in signal quality [6–9]. This difficulty is especially salient for individuals with transhumeral amputation, who have limited residual musculature available for EMG acquisition and are often restricted to controlling only a few degrees-of-freedom in their prosthesis.

The creation of additional recording sites via surgical transfer of nerves from the hand into the remaining musculature of the chest or upper arm (i.e., targeted muscle reinnervation (TMR) [10]) has been proposed as a solution to the challenge of sensing volitional intent. These reinnervated muscles act as biological amplifiers for the transferred nerves, creating additional physiologically appropriate EMG signals that may be used for prosthesis control. This approach appears to be effective for some individuals, as evidenced by the improvement in functional outcomes after TMR [11, 12]. However, many individuals with transhumeral amputation have not undergone TMR [13] due to ineligibility or personal preference. Contraindications such as ipsilateral brachial plexopathy or other major medical comorbidities may preclude TMR in some cases [14], while concerns about the associated surgical risks and lengthy rehabilitation process may deter other individuals [15, 16]. Consequently, there is an ongoing need to develop improved control strategies for individuals with transhumeral amputation without TMR.

Various methods have been explored to improve EMG signal classification for transhumeral prosthesis control without requiring TMR. Classical machine learning algorithms (e.g., linear discriminant analysis, quadratic discriminant analysis, k-nearest neighbors, support vector machines, random forests) have been evaluated for differentiating gestures based on various EMG signal features [17, 18]. Alternatively, deep learning methods such as recurrent neural networks [19], time-delayed artificial neural networks [20], and convolutional neural networks [21] have been proposed to optimize EMG feature selection and classification accuracy [2]. Hybrid methods that combine EMG with another sensing modality [22], such as near-infrared spectroscopy [23–25], electroencephalography [26, 27], and limb kinematics derived from inertial sensors [28, 29] or optical motion capture [30], are also under investigation. However, studies have primarily focused on proximal joint movements or gross distal movements (wrist flexion/extension, wrist pronation/supination, hand open/close), with limited investigations related to classifying individual digit movements or complex hand grasps in this population.

Interestingly, there is emerging evidence that individuals with transhumeral amputation who did not undergo TMR may be able to control a prosthesis through voluntary phantom hand movements [31]. Phantom limbs are experienced by 76% of individuals with upper limb loss and can exist for decades after amputation [32]. Earlier work showed that phantom hand movements produce residual limb muscle contractions with EMG profiles that were specific to each tested gesture [33], indicating that this phenomenon presents a promising method for controlling transhumeral prostheses without the need for TMR. Indeed, residual limb activity corresponding to phantom hand movements in individuals with transhumeral amputation can be classified [30, 31] and used to perform virtual target acquisition tasks [34] or real-world grasping tasks [35]. The ability to mobilize individual phantom digits, which is reported by many individuals with upper limb loss [32], could also be especially useful in the context of prosthesis control [31, 36]. For example, mapping the prosthesis functions to physiologically congruent commands may enhance usability (e.g., mapping phantom index finger flexion to prosthetic index finger flexion). Thus, there is an unmet need to investigate whether individuals with transhumeral amputation can benefit from using phantom hand movements to control a prosthesis even without TMR.

As a first step towards this goal, understanding how phantom hand movements are represented in the residual upper arm muscles after transhumeral amputation is essential. Prior work has explored this topic using surface EMG [37], but this modality is limited by its sensitivity to electrical crosstalk, low signal-to-noise ratio, and poor ability to detect deep muscle activity within the limb [8, 38, 39]. Ultrasound imaging has the potential to offer unique and complementary insights given its ability to directly visualize spatially-resolved surface and deep musculature in the residual limb. Therefore, our objective in this study was to utilize ultrasound imaging to investigate whether phantom hand movements correspond to unique patterns of spatially-resolved muscle activation in the residual limb. We report on an individual with transhumeral amputation who did not undergo TMR but retains the ability to produce individual digit motions and complex hand grasps with his phantom limb.

Methods

This study followed a participatory design model, in which the participant served as a co-researcher and was involved in all stages of the research process including study design, data collection and analysis, and interpretation of results. Participatory research is intended to engage patients as active, equal partners with the research team, rather than as passive test subjects. Following the participatory research principles of co-ownership and shared governance of the research [40], the participant is also included as an author.

Study procedures were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board at George Mason University (#492701). The participant provided written informed consent for his involvement in this study and for including his data and identifiers in publications.

Participant

At the time of initial data collection in 2019, the participant was a 32-year-old male who underwent bilateral wrist disarticulation (right arm) and transhumeral (left arm) amputations in 2010 secondary to septic shock. He reported daily use of a myoelectric prosthesis (externally-powered elbow, wrist and terminal device) with pattern recognition. Previously, he used a direct-controlled hybrid prosthesis (body-powered elbow, externally-powered wrist and terminal device) for six years. Both prostheses were attached to the transhumeral residual limb via a suspension socket. He reported being left-hand dominant and his residual limb length was approximately 26.5 cm.

Data collection

We conducted two separate data collection sessions that occurred six years apart (2019 and 2025). The purpose of the data collection for the two sessions was slightly different, as described below. Note that while the participant had bilateral upper limb amputations, data were only collected from his left (transhumeral) side for this study.

Session 1 (2019)

We acquired B-mode ultrasound images using a Terason uSmart 3200T ultrasound system (Terason, Burlington, MA). A low-profile, high-frequency, linear 16HL7 ultrasound transducer was positioned on the participant’s residual bicep using a stretchable fabric cuff. Ultrasound image sequences were transferred to a PC in real-time using a screen capture card (Epiphan Systems, Palo Alto, CA), where they were processed in MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, MA) using custom algorithms. The acquisition frame rate was variable (13.44 ± 0.66 Hz, determined by a post-hoc calculation from timestamps) due to limitations of the screen capture card.

The participant was asked to perform 11 different motions with his phantom limb, including index finger flexion, middle finger flexion, ring finger flexion, little finger flexion, thumb abduction, power grasp, key grasp, tripod, point, pinch, and wrist pronation. (Note that the participant was performing motor execution of the phantom limb, rather than motor imagery). The ultrasound images associated with each motion were collected following a previously published protocol [41]. Beginning from a resting state with his residual limb muscles relaxed, the participant repeatedly performed each motion following a 1-Hz metronome (i.e., transitioning from rest towards the motion end-state within one second, holding the motion for one second, transitioning back to rest within one second, and staying at rest for one second). This cycle was repeated four times sequentially, resulting in a continuous 16-second ultrasound image sequence. Each of the phantom motions was collected separately following this procedure (see Additional File 1 for the full ultrasound sequences). A short break was provided between the collection of each sequence when the participant switched to a new motion. In total, this process required approximately 20 min.

Session 2 (2025)

Based on our findings from the 2019 dataset, we decided that collecting additional data could offer further insight into how phantom movements manifested in the participant’s residual limb. We collected data in 2019 without having a full appreciation of the participant’s underlying limb anatomy, other than what was apparent from external observation or from ultrasound imaging. Moreover, detailed information regarding his amputation procedure and recovery was inaccessible because his care was provided outside the United States, although anecdotally he described being told by his surgeon that segments of both his biceps and triceps were excised. To supplement this information, a brachial plexus MRI was performed in 2025 to determine the status of the previously severed nerves and their path relative to the residual musculature, as well as to check for any native reinnervation that would explain some of the participant’s ability to produce separable movements. We also recollected ultrasound images of the residual limb during phantom movements using the same equipment as in 2019. In this case, the post-hoc frame rate was calculated to be 28.28 ± 0.77 Hz. We followed a similar procedure to 2019, although the transducer was placed in a different location (Fig. 2) to capture maximal muscle activation. The same motion performance sequence was used (i.e., cycling between rest and holding the motion end-state), except that each phase was lengthened to two seconds, requiring approximately 40 min to complete the session (see Additional File 3 for the ultrasound sequences). Additionally, we collected a different set of phantom movements, including index finger flexion, middle finger flexion, ring finger flexion, little finger flexion, thumb abduction, power grasp, tripod, pinch, elbow flexion, and elbow extension.

We added elbow movements to confirm whether the muscle activation patterns were truly specific to phantom hand movement. In an intact limb, the upper arm muscles have similar tonic activation during different types of hand movement, suggesting their role is to stabilize the proximal joints rather than control the hand [37]. As such, these muscles would only be expected to demonstrate movement-specific, phasic activation during movements of the proximal joints, such as elbow flexion. Since our participant activated his upper arm muscles in 2019 despite not intending to produce elbow movements, we wanted to confirm whether the activation patterns associated with intentional contraction of the upper arm muscles were distinct from those associated with phantom hand movements. This would suggest that the residual muscle activity during phantom hand movements truly corresponded with the intended movement and was not simply a physiological artifact.

Data processing

The acquired screen captures consisted of a rectangular area encompassing the entire on-screen display, including a trapezoidal window containing the muscle data (i.e., the ultrasound window). These images were cropped visually to a smaller rectangular region that minimized pixels outside the ultrasound window. The cropped regions varied slightly between the 2019 and 2025 datasets due to changes in probe placement, but all motions within each dataset were cropped to the same dimensions. Additionally, image sequences from the 2025 session were downsampled by a factor of 2 to match the frame rate of the 2019 session.

Because the sequence of ultrasound images for each motion included transitions between phases, it was necessary to extract only the frames from the steady-state phases when the participant was at rest or when the motion end-state was being held. This was determined based on a distance metric for each frame in the full image sequence, following our method from prior work [41]. The distance metric was calculated as 1-P, where P is the Pearson correlation coefficient between a given frame and the first frame in the sequence. Since the participant was at rest during the first frame, this metric represents deviation from rest such that higher values indicate low similarity to rest (i.e., greater muscle activation). Thus, plateaus in the distance metric occurred when the participant was at rest or holding the motion end-state and were used to extract the relevant frames for each repetition.

Although the participant was provided with metronome cues to maintain consistency in timing for each phase in the data acquisition sequence, there were slight variations that resulted in a different number of frames per plateau across repetitions. Based on the minimum plateau length across all datasets, we randomly selected a subset of 6 frames out of each plateau to maintain consistency for analysis.

Data analysis

We created activation maps for each phantom movement to visually represent the areas of activation within the residual limb musculature when that movement is performed. First, we calculated an average reference image for each repetition of a given motion. This was done by taking the average of all frames from the rest plateau immediately preceding the motion end-state plateau for that repetition (i.e., the reference image was calculated separately for each repetition of the motion). We then subtracted this reference image from each of the motion end-state frames in that plateau. Lastly, we took the average of these differences across all repetitions to create the activation map.

We also created a similarity matrix to quantify the distinctiveness of the muscle activation patterns for each motion. For each frame in each repetition of a motion, we calculated the average Pearson correlation between that frame and all other collected motion frames, except for those occurring in the same repetition (e.g., a frame from the first repetition of a motion was not correlated with any other frame in the first repetition). The diagonal elements of this matrix describe the average similarity of a motion across repetitions (i.e., intra-motion similarity) and the off-diagonal elements describe the average similarity between different motions (i.e., inter-motion similarity). In this context, high intra-motion similarity indicates consistent muscle activation patterns across repetitions, while high inter-motion similarity represents low separability between different motions. For each motion, we also calculated the average of all inter-motion correlations involving that motion (i.e., average similarity to all other motions).

Results

Participant narrative

The participant first noticed an ability to voluntarily control his residual muscles around 6 months after amputation, which he discovered accidentally while attempting relieve phantom pain by “fidgeting” with his phantom digits. He subsequently underwent myosite testing of his limb approximately 9 months after amputation to prepare for prosthesis fitting. While minimal EMG activity was detected, he reports internally perceiving some degree of muscle activation at this time. Additional myosite testing was completed several months later, at which point the EMG signals were measurable. During both the 2019 and 2025 data collections for this study, he reported still having an ability to voluntarily generate both complex grasps and individual digit motions with his phantom hand (i.e., attempted motor execution, as opposed to motor imagery with the phantom hand). He also stated that he can most confidently produce thumb and little finger movements, whereas he perceives less control over the index, middle, and ring finger movements.

Muscle activation patterns

Ultrasound imaging revealed distinct patterns of muscle activation in the residual limb associated with each phantom hand movement (Fig. 3). These patterns were also visually apparent on the exterior of the residual limb, especially on the posterior aspect (Additional Files 4–5). Concordantly, there was strong separability between the muscle activation patterns, as evidenced by the higher intra-motion similarity compared to inter-motion similarity for every phantom movement (Figs. 4A and 5A). However, there was a decrease between 2019 and 2025 in both the average intra-motion similarity (2019: 0.71 ± 0.07, 2025: 0.63 ± 0.09, Figs. 4A and 5A) and average inter-motion similarity (2019: 0.25 ± 0.01, 2025: 0.14 ± 0.01, Figs. 4B and 5B).

The three highest intra-motion similarities within each dataset included little finger flexion (2019: 0.78 ± 0.08; 2025: 0.79 ± 0.06) and power grasp (2019: 0.86 ± 0.06; 2025: 0.71 ± 0.09), along with point (0.78 ± 0.05) for 2019 and pinch (0.72 ± 0.09) for 2025 (Figs. 4A and 5A). The three highest pairs of inter-motion similarities within each dataset included index/middle finger (2019: 0.57 ± 0.06, 2025: 0.38 ± 0.09), and middle/ring finger (2019: 0.52 ± 0.10, 2025: 0.43 ± 0.07), as well as power grasp/point (0.58 ± 0.04) for 2019 and power grasp/little finger (0.56 ± 0.05) for 2025 (Figs. 4A and 5A). Importantly, the activation patterns for elbow movements were clearly distinct from the activation patterns for hand movements (average inter-motion correlation ≤ 0.14).

Discussion

Our goal was to explore how phantom hand movements are represented in the residual limb and whether ultrasound imaging could effectively visualize the residual muscle activation patterns linked to phantom limb movements. We focused on an individual with a transhumeral amputation who could perform individual finger movements and complex grasps with his phantom limb, despite not having undergone TMR. Our results demonstrate that each specific phantom movement was associated with qualitatively and quantitatively distinct residual muscle activation patterns. Although based on a single individual, these findings provide imaging evidence that the residual limb muscles may be functionally innervated with digit-level specificity after transhumeral amputation.

It is noteworthy that our participant could produce 11 phantom hand movements with distinct muscle activation patterns in the upper arm without TMR. The mechanisms through which the motor innervation responsible for finger movements are established in the residual musculature are not completely understood. While neural reorganization could be an explanation, recent evidence that cortical representations of the hand remain stable several years after amputation [42] suggests that a neuromuscular mechanism is more likely. Native reinnervation of the original nerve pathways is a possibility, but the brachial plexus MRI did not suggest this occurred. Alternatively, independent muscle functional units may be created in the residual limb after transhumeral amputation due to peripheral reorganization, potentially via axonal sprouting and reinnervation of denervated muscles [37]. This could explain why residual limb muscles in many transhumeral amputees demonstrate movement-specific, phasic EMG activity during phantom movements, similar to normal activity in the forearm or hand muscles during intact hand movements [37]. These patterns are also distinct from the non-movement-specific, tonic activation seen in the upper arm muscles during intact hand movements [37]. It is possible that the specific procedure during our participant’s amputation resulted in denervated muscle compartments that were subsequently reinnervated with motor units during his recovery, although we cannot confirm this because his surgical records are unavailable. Our finding that each tested phantom movement was associated with unique muscle activation patterns implies that peripheral reorganization occurred, which is evidence for functional (rather than native) reinnervation with digit-level specificity. However, the precise mechanism cannot be determined based on our data. Definitive evidence would require fine needle electromyography or surgical explorations that were not feasible for this study.

The primary evidence indicating that the phantom movements produced separable muscle activation patterns comes from the greater intra-motion similarity relative to inter-motion similarity for each tested movement. Since this trend persisted for both datasets, the separability is unlikely to be an artifact caused by differences in collection protocol. However, it is possible that the slightly lower magnitude of separability metrics for 2025 was related to differences in protocol, especially with the probe location and collection phase length. Anecdotally, the participant reported that the two-second phases used in 2025 were more challenging to follow compared to the one-second phases used in 2019. If he had difficulty with producing and holding the phantom movements at this slower pace, it could have contributed to the comparatively lower intra-motion similarities. Another possibility is that his ability to produce the phantom movements changed over the six years between collections. However, we cannot determine this based on the collected data and the literature offers conflicting evidence on this topic. While some studies report that time since amputation negatively impacts phantom mobility [33], others have found there is no effect [32, 43].

Despite differences in metric magnitudes across years, there was a general agreement between the relative intra- and inter-motion similarities within each dataset (e.g., lower intra-motion similarities tended to correspond with lower inter-motion similarities). This pattern could be related to the inherent physiological distinctiveness of each phantom movement, as movements with greater variability may overlap more with other movements than those that are consistent. Additionally, the use of Pearson correlation as a similarity metric may contribute to this relationship, since it quantifies the linear association between images. The statistical properties of the images, such as variance, can influence both intra- and inter-motion similarity measures, resulting in the observed correlation between their values.

Regarding the separability of specific phantom movements, some of the most salient trends were aligned with the participant’s perception that moving his phantom index, middle, and ring fingers was more difficult than the little finger or thumb. For example, pairs of phantom motions that produced the least separable muscle activation patterns included the index/middle finger and middle/ring finger, which could relate to his difficulty with isolating those movements. Moreover, the consistency of the activation patterns for little finger flexion was among the highest recorded in both collected datasets, which was concordant with his perceived ease of movement. The primary involvement of the ulnar nerve in controlling the little finger [44] could also potentially explain this greater consistency, while the more variable activation patterns for the other fingers may emerge from contribution of multiple nerves towards control of those movements.

A potential contradiction to our interpretation of these results could be that the presumed phantom limb movements were conflated with motor imagery of the phantom limb. However, motor execution and motor imagery involving the phantom limb are distinct phenomena that feel subjectively different to individuals with amputation [45]. Quantifiable differences also exist in numerous domains, including cortical activation, completion speed, and residual muscle activation [45, 46]. Importantly, motor execution of phantom limb movements involves activation of the muscles in the residual limb, while motor imagery of the phantom limb does not [45]. The measurable residual limb activation we observed, along with our participant’s self-reported experience, suggests he was attempting actual motor execution of phantom movements rather than motor imagery.

Relatedly, it is valuable to consider whether the different phantom movements were performed purposefully or whether a compensatory strategy was involved. It could be possible to elicit separable muscle activation patterns by simply performing the same phantom movement to different degrees of completion (e.g., full completion vs. partial completion), rather than performing truly distinct phantom movements. We explored this in a supplemental analysis using multidimensional scaling to visualize the trajectories of each movement in low-dimensional ultrasound space, which was previously applied by [47] for a similar purpose. If the participant had adopted this compensatory strategy, these trajectories would overlap. However, the trajectories still retain some spatial separation, which suggests that the participant truly performed distinct muscle activation patterns for the tested phantom movements (Additional File 6).

Our work suggests that there may be a population of transhumeral amputees without TMR who could benefit from multiarticulated prosthesis control based on phantom limb mobilization. Although this population is currently understudied, the feasibility of using phantom limb mobilization with surface EMG for facilitating prosthesis control after transhumeral amputation has been documented repeatedly [30, 31, 34, 35]. It is also encouraging that this approach can enable digit-level classification [31, 36], as most studies in transhumeral amputees who haven’t undergone TMR only focus on proximal or gross distal movements instead. Our work extends these findings by demonstrating that another noninvasive sensing modality (i.e., ultrasound imaging) can reliably distinguish the muscle activation patterns during phantom digit and hand movements. Given the known signal quality limitations of surface EMG [8, 38, 39], ultrasound-based prosthesis control (i.e., sonomyography) could eventually provide a new clinical option [48] for individuals with transhumeral amputation who wish to control a multiarticulated prosthesis without undergoing TMR. We have included a brief supplemental analysis showing accurate offline classification (Additional File 7) and successful real-time control of a prosthetic hand (Additional Files 7–8) using ultrasound imaging of phantom limb mobilization, but additional development is still needed to realize the potential benefits of this modality.

There are several methodological limitations of this study. First, we collected data from a single participant performing a limited set of phantom movements. Our findings should not be generalized to other individuals or every possible phantom gesture without further study. Moreover, all repetitions of each tested phantom movement were collected sequentially. It is possible that our participant was able to produce the movements more consistently than if he produced single repetitions of different movements in a random order (which would also be more representative of real-world prosthesis use). This may have caused the activation patterns to appear more separable than they would be in a natural setting. Conversely, the sequential repetitions could also have contributed to muscle fatigue and reduced the consistency of the activation patterns. We are unable to determine the potential effects of order and fatigue with the current experimental design, however. Additionally, the differences in probe position, phase length, and selected phantom movements make it difficult to directly compare the 2019 and 2025 datasets. However, we believe inclusion of both datasets is valuable for differentiating activation patterns for phantom hand movements relative to upper arm movement, as well as demonstrating that the separability between activation patterns was generally independent of probe placement.

It is also important to note that the concept of “unique” residual muscle activation during phantom limb movement has not been consistently defined across studies. Some authors have inferred that these patterns were distinct based on offline EMG classification accuracies during phantom movements in transhumeral amputees, which were comparable to those in non-amputees [34] or transradial amputees [30, 31]. Other methods attempted to characterize the underlying anatomy more directly through the EMG patterns associated with each phantom movement, including overall profiles [33] or movement-specificity (i.e., standard deviation of relative phasic activity for different phantom movements) [37]. While our approach of visualizing spatial muscle deformation using ultrasound imaging is aligned with the latter category, this exact technique has not previously been adopted for understanding phantom movements. However, we believe it is justified given the prior use of ultrasound in quantifying muscle deformation patterns in other contexts, such as following TMR [49]. Convergence on a more unified definition of “uniqueness” may be advisable for future work to better account for differences in modality (EMG vs. ultrasound), recording hardware, and sensor placement across studies, as well as patient-specific factors such as residual limb anatomy.

Conclusion

This work explores the utility of ultrasound imaging for visualizing the muscle activation patterns associated with phantom limb movement. We show that these patterns are both qualitatively and quantitatively distinct for an individual with transhumeral amputation who has not undergone TMR, suggesting that residual muscles in the upper arm might be functionally reinnervated at the digit level. Continued development of transhumeral prosthesis control approaches based on ultrasound imaging is warranted.
