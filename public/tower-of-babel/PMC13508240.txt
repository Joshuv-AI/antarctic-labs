Comparative assessment of asparaginase activity, population pharmacokinetics, and safety of biosimilar native Escherichia coli and pegylated asparaginase in children with newly diagnosed acute lymphoblastic leukemia: a pharmacometrics-based randomized clinical trial
Authors: Sujitha Sethuramalingam, Sonali Mohapatra, Praveen Kumar Agarwal, Praveen Kumar-M, Debasish Hota, Janhavi Gopal, Aishwarya Kulkarni, Anand Srinivasan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere is a paucity of data regarding the comparison of asparaginase activity, pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamic outcomes, and safety of biosimilar native E.coli asparaginase (N-Asp) and biosimilar pegylated asparaginase (P-Asp) used in India.MethodsA randomized, open-labeled, population pharmacokinetic (PopPK) and pharmacodynamic trial comparing the N-Asp (10,000 IU/m2) and P-Asp (1000 IU/m2) administered intramuscularly in children with newly diagnosed acute lymphoblastic leukemia. The primary endpoint was asparaginase activity on day 33 of the induction phase of chemotherapy. Population pharmacokinetic modeling was done after the first dose in both groups, and the models were simulated to assess for the subtherapeutic trough concentrations.ResultsFourteen patients were randomized to each arm. The asparaginase activity of 434[interquartile range(IQR) 259, 629] IU/L was observed in the P-Asp compared (p—0.04) to the N-Asp group 64[IQR 27, 184] IU/L on day 33 of the induction phase of chemotherapy. The anti-asparaginase antibody formation, minimal residual disease, and safety were comparable between both groups. The interindividual variability of the population parameters in the PopPK model was higher with N-Asp than P-Asp. The subtherapeutic trough levels of asparaginase were observed more in the N-Asp group (9/14 in N-Asp vs. 0/14 in P-Asp, p < 0.01).ConclusionThe asparaginase activity was higher with P-Asp at d33. P-Asp demonstrated less interindividual variability and a predictable kinetic profile, supporting its preferability over N-Asp for first-line management of ALL in the Indian pediatric population. While routine TDM for trough level monitoring may be less critical with P-Asp, TDM retains value to identify silent inactivation, differentiate hypersensitivity from infusion reactions, and confirm adequate activity in high-risk scenarios. In contrast, the higher variability of N-Asp strongly warrants the use of TDM.Clinical trial registration identificationCTRI/2019/06/019520 registered on 04/June/2019.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-16362-y.Keywords: Pegaspargase, Asparaginase (native E. coli), Population Pharmacometrics, Acute lymphoblastic leukemia

Introduction

Leukemia is the most common childhood malignancy [1]. Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) accounts for 80% of leukemia in children less than 15 years of age. Asparaginase is a key drug of various treatment protocols for ALL, and its intensive use has improved survival and remission in ALL [2–5].

Asparaginase depletes plasma asparagine by hydrolysis, thereby causing impaired protein synthesis and apoptosis of leukemic cells selectively, as these cells have reduced activity of asparagine synthetase. The available formulations of asparaginase are native Escherichia coli (E. coli) asparaginase, pegylated E. coli asparaginase (pegaspargase), and Erwinia chrysanthemiasparaginase. Asparaginase can be administered by intravenous (i.v.) or intramuscular (i.m.) routes [6–9]. The different formulations and routes of administration of asparaginase can translate into different pharmacokinetic (PK) properties.

In India, we often use various biosimilars of native E. coliasparaginase and, less often, originator pegaspargase (Oncaspar®) and its Indian comparator Hamsyl®. Hamsyl® is a biosimilar approved by the Drug Controller General of India in 2014 for use in patients hypersensitive to N-Asp and recently approved for use in the first line of the ALL treatment. Pegaspargase has a longer half-life and low immunogenicity than native asparaginase [10]. The population pharmacokinetic (PopPK) profiling of native and pegylated asparaginase has shown that the variability of PK parameters is relatively less in pegylated formulations compared to the native ones [11, 12].

Henceforth, Hamsyl® will be referred to as P-Asp, and biosimilars of native E. coliasparaginase will be referred to as N-Asp. Given the comparatively less data availability of P-Asp use in India, elaborate studies have to be conducted against the N-Asp to replace the native form with the pegylated form so that the advantages of the pegylated formulation can be utilized for the first-line treatment of ALL in Indian patients. Literature elsewhere has reported similar efficacy of Oncaspar® against native asparaginase [13]. But, there are no clinical trials comparing the asparaginase activity at the end of the induction phase, PopPK, and pharmacodynamics (PD) of the biosimilar native form (N-Asp) against pegylated biosimilar (P-Asp). Unlike generics, biosimilars vary in structure and formulations from each other due to the relatively complex manufacturing process. So, the biosimilars must be evaluated extensively, including the pharmacokinetics and dynamics. This study will help establish variations in the PK parameters of N-Asp and P-Asp. It will also aid in deciding on the deployment of therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) of both the preparations of asparaginase. This study was planned to demonstrate a similar efficacy between the two forms of the medication. Moreover, the study also aims to confirm the lesser variability and better maintenance of trough P-Asp activity relative to N-Asp in the local population.

The primary objective of this study was to compare the asparaginase activity(pharmacometric endpoint), pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamic outcomes (immunogenicity and minimal residual disease), and safety of biosimilar native E. coli asparaginase (N-Asp) and biosimilar pegylated asparaginase (P-Asp) in Indian children with newly diagnosed ALL using a pharmacometrics approach. Secondary objectives included characterization of the population PK profile of both formulations, assessment of TDM requirements, and evaluation of the suitability of P-Asp as a first-line alternative to N-Asp in India.

Methodology

Study design

This study was a randomized, open-label, parallel-group, active-controlled PopPK and PD clinical trial conducted in the Departments of Medical Oncology and Pharmacology at All India Institute of Medical Sciences (AIIMS), Bhubaneswar, India, between October 2020 and July 2023.

Eligibility criteria

The patients 1–18 years of age, of either sex, undergoing induction phase chemotherapy for newly diagnosed ALL, whose parents/guardians were willing to give consent and children willing to give assent as appropriate, were included. The study excluded patients with a history of hepatitis, pancreatitis, tuberculosis, and allergy to any of the medications used in the management of ALL.

Procedure

The study was prospectively registered at the Clinical Trials Registry – India (CTRI/2019/06/019520). The trial commenced after the approval of the Institutional Ethics Committee of AIIMS Bhubaneswar. The research was done with ethical standards in accordance with the "Declaration of Helsinki". "National Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical and Health Research Involving Human Participants (2017)" by the Indian Council of Medical Research was implemented while conducting research. Patients were treated on the Indian Childhood Collaborative Leukemia Group (ICiCLe) protocol [14] as the backbone. Patients received i.m. either N-Asp (10,000 IU/m2, on days 9, 12, 15, 18, 21, 24, 27, and 30) or P-Asp (1000 IU/m2, on days 9 and 23) during the induction phase of chemotherapy. Simple randomization was performed using R 4.1, and the allocation ratio was 1:1 between the two intervention arms. The allocation was concealed by sequentially numbering the participants against the randomization codes. The author who generated the randomization was not involved in patient recruitment. The author involved in patient recruitment was unaware of the allocation sequence, thereby achieving allocation concealment. Patients were followed up till the end of the induction phase. (Supplementary Appendix 1 describes the pharmacotherapy plan in the induction phase).

Primary and secondary endpoints analyzed

The primary endpoint analyzed was asparaginase activity on day 33 (d33) of the induction phase of chemotherapy, as the asparaginase activity has to be sufficiently maintained (= 100 IU/L) [15] for a favorable response throughout the induction phase. Secondary endpoints were the proportion of patients with subtherapeutic trough levels of asparaginase after the first dose of study drugs, the determination of PopPK parameters by generating a PopPK model to understand the variability in the pharmacokinetic parameters and the influence of various covariates on those parameters, and the simulation of the PopPK model generated to evaluate the maintenance of trough asparaginase activity. Other endpoints include the evaluation of PD parameters such as end-induction immunogenicity (presence or absence of anti-asparaginase antibody), end-induction minimal residual disease (MRD), and safety.

Data collection and evaluation

The baseline data collected were demographic data, weight, height, body surface area (BSA), disease status, and laboratory test data.

For the primary endpoint analysis, 1 ml of blood was collected on d33 of the induction phase for asparaginase assay. For the PK analysis, 1 mL of blood was collected at 0 (predose), 0.0167, 0.333, 1, 2, 4, 24, and 72 h after the administration of the first N-Asp dose and at 0 (predose), 0.0833, 3, 6, 24, 48, 168 and 336 h after the administration of the first P-Asp dose. The difference in the time period of sample collection was due to the difference in the pharmacokinetic profile of both preparations. All blood samples were collected by venipuncture in an EDTA tube and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min to separate the plasma, and the plasma was stored at -80°C until analysis. Trough asparaginase concentration in terms of activity was determined in 72 and 336-h plasma samples of N-Asp and P-Asp groups, respectively. The subtherapeutic level of asparaginase activity was defined as < 100 IU/L [15]. Plasma L-asparaginase activity was measured using the Indooxine assay method and quantified using spectrophotometry as described by Lanvers et al. with modifications. [16] The lower and upper limits of quantification for the asparaginase activity assay were 2.34 IU/L and 1200 IU/L, respectively.

The end-induction immunogenicity was assessed in the plasma sample collected on d33. Human asparaginase antibody was estimated using ELISA.

A bone marrow sample was collected on d33 of the induction phase to assess end-induction phase MRD by flow cytometry. Patients with residual blast = 0.01% were considered MRD-positive. Safety was monitored throughout the induction phase of chemotherapy.

Pharmacokinetic analysis

The individual PK parameters were determined assuming single compartmental kinetics with first-order absorption and elimination. Maximum plasma concentration (Cmax), and time to reach maximum plasma concentration (tmax) were obtained directly from the observed plasma time-concentration data. The elimination rate constant (ke) and elimination half-life (t1/2) were determined by linear modeling of the terminal section of time-concentration data. The other parameters calculated were the area under the plasma time-concentration curve from time 0 to t hours (time of the last measurable plasma concentration) after dosing (AUC0-t) and the area under the time-concentration curve from time 0 to infinity (AUC0-8).

Statistical analysis

Based on the observations from the previous study, to detect a difference in the mean asparaginase levels of 200 IU/L with the standard deviation (SD) of the mean difference of 180 at the end of the induction phase, 14 participants were required in each group [17]. The alpha and beta errors allowed were 0.05 and 0.2. All the continuous variables were expressed as mean (SD) if the data was normal and median [interquartile range (IQR)] if the data was non-normal. The categorical variables were expressed as proportions. The difference between the groups for continuous variables was determined by the t-test or Mann–Whitney U test based on the distribution. The difference between the groups for categorical variables was determined by the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Logistic regression techniques were employed to quantify the association between the AUC and immunogenicity or MRD status. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. R 4.1 was used for statistical analysis.

PopPK model development

PopPK analysis was performed by non-linear mixed effect (NLME) modeling using Monolix (version 2020R1. Antony, France: Lixoft SAS, 2020. http://lixoft.com/products/monolix/). The PopPK parameters estimated in the modeling were ka, clearance (Cl), and the volume of distribution (V). From the base model (without covariate), the effect of covariates such as age, sex, type of ALL, weight, height, and BSA on asparaginase PK parameters were evaluated. The final model was evaluated based on goodness-of-fit (GOF) plots and visual predictive check (VPC). GOF was assessed by observed versus predicted concentration, individual weighted residuals (IWRES), and normalized prediction distribution errors (NPDE) versus time. The generated model was simulated to evaluate the maintenance of asparaginase activity throughout the period between the first and second dose administration.

Simulation of PopPK model of N-Asp and P-Asp

During simulation, we observed that with P-Asp, the therapeutic asparaginase concentration of > 100 IU/L (required for attaining clinical efficacy) was achieved and maintained until the next dose, and the variation in asparaginase concentration was less. However, in the N-Asp group, we found that the simulation revealed a subtherapeutic concentration around 45–55% of the time, and a larger variation in the pharmacokinetics was also observed (Fig. 3).

Asparaginase trough levels after the first dose of N-Asp/P-Asp

There were significantly higher median asparaginase trough levels observed in the P-Asp arm as compared to the N-Asp arm (71 IU/L [IQR 39, 118] in the N-Asp arm vs. 218 IU/L [IQR 189, 243] in P-Asp arm, p < 0.01). A significantly higher proportion (p < 0.01) of patients with subtherapeutic trough levels was present in the N-Asp arm (64.3% (9/14)) versus the P-Asp arm (0% (0/14)).

End-induction immunogenicity

There was no significant difference in the asparaginase antibody formation on d33 of the induction phase of chemotherapy between the two groups (4/12 in N-Asp vs. 2/14 in P-Asp, p – 0.37). In the logistic regression performed with the AUC as the independent variable, no relationship was observed between AUC0-a or AUC0-t and asparaginase antibody status.

End-induction MRD

There was no significant difference in the MRD positivity rate (defined as residual blasts = 0.01%) on d33 of the induction phase of chemotherapy (4/12 in N-Asp vs. 4/14 in P-Asp, p = 0.99 by Fisher’s exact test). Regression analysis showed no relationship was observed between AUC0-a or AUC0-t and MRD status.

Safety analysis

None of the patients developed any allergic reactions to N-Asp/P-Asp. Two patients in the N-Asp group died during the induction phase due to bacteremia and shock (CTCAE Grade 5; attributed to infectious complications of chemotherapy, not to N-Asp). One patient in the P-Asp group developed cellulitis in the right index finger on day 28 of the induction phase (CTCAE Grade 2). One patient in the N-Asp group developed steroid-induced diabetes mellitus after one week of prednisolone therapy (CTCAE Grade 2). Fungal stomatitis developed in one patient in the N-Asp group (CTCAE Grade 2). None of these adverse events could be attributed to the study drugs. The various biochemical parameters assessed on d33 of the induction phase of chemotherapy were within the normal range and similar in both groups.

Discussion

The current study's primary aim was to compare the asparaginase activity, pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamic outcomes, and safety of biosimilar N-Asp and P-Asp in Indian pediatric patients with newly diagnosed ALL using a pharmacometrics approach. Fourteen participants were recruited in each group, and there was no loss to follow-up. Since pegylation results in slow elimination and a longer half-life, we observed significantly higher median asparaginase activity at d33 in the P-Asp group (434 IU/L [IQR 259, 629]) compared to the N-Asp group (64 IU/L [IQR 27, 184]), which fell below the therapeutic threshold of 100 IU/L. It is important to note that the timing of the d33 blood sample relative to the last dose differed between the two groups, which may have partially contributed to the observed difference in asparaginase activity at d33. For N-Asp, the d33 sample was collected 72 h (approximately 3.5 half-lives, based on a median t½ of 20.46 h) after the last dose on Day 30. For P-Asp, the d33 sample was collected 240 h (approximately 1.5 half-lives, based on a median t½ of 160.50 h) after the last dose on Day 23. This difference in elapsed half-lives, attributable to the study design following the ICiCLe backbone protocol, means that the N-Asp activity had declined further from its peak by the time of sampling compared to P-Asp. Thus, the considerably higher asparaginase activity of P-Asp at d33 reflects both the intrinsically superior pharmacokinetics of the pegylated formulation (longer half-life, higher AUC) and the relatively earlier sampling post-last-dose. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the magnitude of the difference in d33 activity between the two formulations.

The slower absorption of P-Asp, as observed, is due to the slower release of the pegylated formulation from the injection site. Since the pegylated formulation is degraded slowly, the t1/2 (160.50 h) was increased in the P-Asp arm compared to the N-Asp arm (20.46 h). The increased t1/2 in P-Asp resulted in a significant increase in AUC values in the P-Asp arm after the initial injection. However, there was no difference in the PD outcomes in terms of antibody and MRD status. There was no significant difference in Cmax between the two formulations. Avarmis VI et al. (2002) observed similar findings in their clinical trial, with t1/2of innovator pegylated asparaginase being 5.5 days and 26 h for native formulation [18]. Nookala Krishnamoorthy et al. (2020) observed that t1/2of P-Asp was 155.28 h [19].

The reported PopPK model for the biosimilar asparaginase preparations used in India found that a one-compartment model with first-order elimination best fitted both the groups and the likelihood ratio decreased substantially with BSA as a covariate. It is a known factor that BSA would influence the pharmacokinetics of asparaginase, and it was again proved in our study. Other than BSA, no other covariates influenced the PK of both formulations. In contrast to the present study where asparaginase was given i.m., Borghorst S et al. (2011), in their PopPK analysis of the native form of asparaginase given (5000 IU/m2) as i.v. infusion found that a linear 2-compartment model with first-order elimination sufficiently described the PKs of the native form. They also found that BSA influenced the Cl of the drug [11]. Hempel G et al. (2010) also found that BSA significantly influenced the PK of the innovator-pegylated form of the drug [20].

The estimates "omega_V" and "omega_Cl" which explain the interindividual variability of V and Cl, respectively, in both the N-Asp and P-Asp PopPK models. The values decreased with the inclusion of BSA as a covariate, implying that BSA partly explains the interindividual variability of V and Cl in P-Asp. However, the variability decreased for only V with N-Asp. It is obvious that a change in the drug formulation can alter the kinetic behavior of a drug. So, the kinetic differences between N-Asp and P-Asp are expected, as observed in this study. However, in the PopPK model, the unexplained variability, as per the residual standard error from the models generated, of kinetic parameters such as V and Cl was higher with N-Asp against P-Asp. This finding implicates the need for TDM in N-Asp formulation.

In the present study, subtherapeutic asparaginase activity was observed only in the N-Asp arm. This finding echoed the concerns raised by Sidhu et al. (2021) regarding the therapeutic activity of native E. coliasparaginase biosimilars marketed in low and middle-income countries [21].

Since asparaginase is a nonhuman protein, it has been reported that 58% of patients treated with asparaginase developed the anti-asparaginase antibody [22]. In our study, asparaginase antibody formation was observed in 33.3% of patients in the N-Asp arm and 14.3% of patients in the P-Asp arm. Our study did not demonstrate any relationship between asparaginase antibody and asparaginase activity. As mentioned in the systematic review by Medawar CW et al. (2019) [13], our study also showed no significant difference in the MRD between the groups.

A recent meta-analysis in Chinese paediatric ALL patients [23] reported that PEG-Asp was not superior to native E. coli asparaginase in terms of clinical efficacy outcomes such as remission rates, but was associated with lower rates of hypersensitivity and hepatic injury [NEW REF]. These findings are broadly consistent with our results, though direct comparison is limited by pharmacogenomic differences between East Asian and South Asian populations that may influence asparaginase metabolism and immunogenicity. The absence of significant differences in MRD and immunogenicity between the N-Asp and P-Asp groups in the present study should be interpreted in light of the sample size: the study was powered for the primary pharmacometric endpoint (asparaginase activity at d33) and was underpowered to detect differences in binary pharmacodynamic endpoints. The high rate of subtherapeutic asparaginase activity in the N-Asp arm (64.3%) supports the pharmacokinetic rationale for TDM, acknowledging that the current study was not designed to demonstrate TDM-attributable clinical outcome benefits.

Toxicities like allergic reactions, hyperglycemia, pancreatitis, transaminitis, and deep vein thrombosis were reported with asparaginase in various trials [24, 25]. The safety profile in our study was similar, as reported by Avarmis et al. (2002) and Place et al. (2015) [18, 26]. Moreover, Panda et al. (2024) [27], in a prospective Indian study of 72 pediatric ALL patients receiving biosimilar P-Asp on the ICiCLe ALL-14 protocol, reported hepatic injury and hyperglycemia as the predominant adverse events, with no allergic reactions, further corroborating the safety of biosimilar P-Asp in the Indian pediatric population.

The optimum dose of pegylated asparaginase remains a matter of debate, with Berlin, Frankfurt, Munster (BFM), and Children's Oncology Group (COG) protocols using 2500 IU/m2 of pegylated asparaginase, and the Dutch DCOG ALL-11 trial recommending 1500 IU/m2 [24]. In our trial in the Indian population who are ethnically different from the Western population, a lower dose of 1000 IU/m2 of P-Asp was sufficient to achieve satisfactory therapeutic trough levels along with a similar safety profile to N-Asp.

Because of the predictable kinetic profile with relatively less variation and the absence of subtherapeutic levels, P-Asp may be favored over N-Asp. However, it should be noted that while routine TDM for monitoring trough asparaginase levels may be less critical for P-Asp given its predictable pharmacokinetics, TDM is not entirely obviated. TDM remains clinically important for identifying silent inactivation of asparaginase, differentiating hypersensitivity reactions from infusion reactions, and confirming adequate activity levels in high-risk patients. The markedly higher interindividual pharmacokinetic variability and the 64.3% rate of subtherapeutic trough levels observed with N-Asp in this study generate the hypothesis that TDM-guided dosing may improve clinical outcomes for patients receiving N-Asp formulations. However, this study was not designed to prospectively evaluate clinical outcomes attributable to TDM-guided interventions, and adequately powered prospective trials are needed to validate the clinical utility of TDM in this context. A significantly longer half-life and a reduction in the number of injections to be administered also favor P-Asp. We would require more extensive clinical trials to obtain significant differences in the hard endpoints, such as MRD status and mortality, between the two formulations.

The only deterring factor concerning P-Asp usage is the cost. To combat the higher cost of the use of pegylated asparaginase, the principle of vial sharing and storage with strict cold chain maintenance at 4 °C for a maximum period of 4 weeks, as practiced by Jayaraman et al. (2017) can render the drug usage feasible in developing countries [28].

The trial's limitations were the smaller sample size, confinement of the study only to the induction phase of chemotherapy, and the absence of long-term follow-up. The study's strengths were capturing the PopPK profile of both N-Asp and P-Asp formulations and the deployment of simulations for further validation and strengthening of the findings.

Conclusion

We conclude that biosimilar P-Asp demonstrated significantly higher asparaginase activity at end of induction, less interindividual pharmacokinetic variability, and adequate therapeutic trough levels throughout the induction phase compared to biosimilar N-Asp, with a comparable safety profile. A dose of 1000 IU/m2 of P-Asp was sufficient to maintain therapeutic asparaginase activity in the Indian pediatric population, supporting its suitability as a first-line alternative to N-Asp in this setting. These pharmacokinetic findings, particularly the high rate of subtherapeutic levels with N-Asp, generate a hypothesis regarding the clinical utility of TDM, which warrants prospective evaluation in adequately powered future studies. While routine TDM for trough level monitoring may be less critical with P-Asp, TDM retains clinical importance to identify silent inactivation, differentiate hypersensitivity from infusion reactions, and confirm adequate activity in high-risk scenarios. In contrast, the higher variability of N-Asp warrants the routine use of TDM.

The trial commenced after the approval of the Institutional Ethics Committee of AIIMS Bhubaneswar (IEC Reference Number: EC/AIIMS BBSR/PG Thesis/2018–19/31, Date of approval: December 20, 2018).
