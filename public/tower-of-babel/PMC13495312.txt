Divergent associations of social isolation with body composition in Chinese middle-aged and older adults
Authors: Mingshen Lin, Jing Ye, Shuaiqing Chen, Yunan Zhao, Yang Li, Xiaolei Hu, Jiansheng Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSocial isolation represents a critical challenge in the context of global population aging, yet its effects on body composition in older adults remain paradoxical. This study aimed to systematically examine, from a life-course perspective, the differential associations of social isolation on sarcopenia and obesity among middle-aged and older adults in China, thereby evaluating the proposed divergent associations hypothesis.MethodsUsing data from 5,525 participants in the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), we constructed life-course patterns of social isolation integrating both childhood and adulthood exposures. Outcomes were defined according to the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) guidelines and the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria for Asian populations. Multivariable logistic regression models were employed to assess the primary associations, supplemented by subgroup, sensitivity, and exploratory mediation analyses.ResultsPersistent social isolation was significantly associated with increased odds of low muscle strength (OR = 1.72, 95% CI: 1.21–2.44) and possible sarcopenia (OR = 1.49, 95% CI: 1.03–2.14), reflecting a potential cumulative disadvantage. In sharp contrast, persistent isolation showed a significant inverse association with obesity (OR = 0.63, 95% CI: 0.47–0.85).ConclusionsPersistent social isolation is strongly associated with functional muscle decline without concurrent BMI-defined obesity, suggesting a potential high-risk pattern. These findings underscore the importance of early interventions targeting social isolation to preemptively address covert functional decline.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12963-026-00489-y.Keywords: Social isolation, Sarcopenia, Obesity, Depression, Cystatin C

Introduction

As global population aging accelerates, maladaptive changes in body composition proxies have emerged as a major challenge in geriatric health [1]. Across the aging trajectory, progressive decline in muscle strength and physical performance frequently co-occurs with dysregulated adipose tissue distribution, giving rise to high-risk phenotypes such as sarcopenia and sarcopenic obesity, which are strongly associated with elevated risks of disability and mortality [2–4]. Although diet and physical activity have long been recognized as central determinants of body composition [5, 6], accumulating evidence suggests that social environmental exposures, particularly social isolation, may represent a more insidious and enduring factor associated with body composition changes [7, 8]. From a life-course epidemiological perspective, exposure to social isolation early in life may exert lasting effects on metabolic homeostasis and muscle integrity in later adulthood through mechanisms of biological embedding [9]. However, existing literature has rarely examined this process from a comprehensive, longitudinal viewpoint spanning childhood through adulthood, leaving the dynamic evolution of these associations insufficiently understood.

When delineating the directional effects of social isolation on body composition, the current body of evidence reveals a pronounced metabolic paradox [10]. Classical frameworks derived largely from Western populations conceptualize social isolation as a psychosocial stressor that promotes central adiposity through cortisol-mediated pathways and increased sedentary behavior [11]. However, there is a paucity of studies validating this paradigm in the East Asian context, where distinct dietary cultures and social structures prevail. Instead, an opposing pattern characterized by catabolic decline has frequently been observed [12]. Such region-specific discrepancies strongly indicate that, within East Asian sociocultural contexts, the influence of social isolation on body composition cannot be captured by a unidimensional, linear model of metabolic excess or deficit. Rather, it appears to reflect a complex pattern of divergent associations. This process may be driven by diminished eating motivation resulting from the absence of shared meals, together with heightened neuro-metabolic demands required to cope with perceived social threats, collectively shifting the organism toward a state of negative energy balance [13]. Consequently, this distinctive divergence implies that social isolation may substantially elevate the risk of sarcopenia while simultaneously constraining fat accumulation, thereby masking overt phenotypes of metabolic surplus and leaving older adults vulnerable to concurrent functional muscle decline [14].

In light of these considerations, the present study leverages high-quality, nationally representative data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) to reconstruct dynamic patterns of social isolation from a life-course perspective. We systematically examine the differential associations of social isolation with sarcopenia, obesity, and sarcopenic obesity in order to explore these divergent phenotypic patterns. Beyond addressing the long-standing academic debate regarding obesogenic versus wasting effects, this study seeks to explore the potential statistical pathways linking social isolation to these body composition proxies. By doing so, it aims to identify high-risk individuals who may be undergoing concurrent functional and metabolic vulnerabilities despite the absence of overt obesity, thereby providing empirical evidence to inform more precise clinical screening and early intervention strategies.

Conceptual framework of the study

The present study is conceptually anchored in the Life-Course Model, a well-established theoretical framework in epidemiology postulating that health and disease outcomes in later life are profoundly shaped by biological and psychosocial exposures accumulated from gestation through adulthood. Specifically, this study draws upon two core life-course concepts: the “sensitive period” and the “accumulation of risk” hypotheses. The sensitive period hypothesis suggests that exposures during critical developmental windows, such as childhood, have enduring physiological impacts. Concurrently, the accumulation of risk hypothesis posits that the detrimental effects of psychosocial deprivation, such as social isolation, gather over time, exerting a cumulative toll on physiological systems.

Methods

Study design and participants

This analysis was based on data from CHARLS, a nationally representative prospective cohort designed to track demographic, socioeconomic, and health trajectories among Chinese adults aged 45 years and older [15]. The baseline survey was conducted in 2011–2012, with subsequent follow-up waves completed in 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020. Importantly, a comprehensive life-course survey was incorporated in 2014, and venous blood samples were collected in 2012 and 2015 for biomarker assessments. All interviews were administered by trained personnel using a computer-assisted personal interviewing (CAPI) system. The study protocol was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University (approval number: IRB00001052-11015), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants at enrollment.

This study used a cross-sectional analysis of 2015 CHARLS data, incorporating retrospectively reported childhood social isolation from the 2014 life-course survey to reconstruct life-course exposure patterns. Specifically, information regarding early-life exposures was exclusively extracted from the 2014 CHARLS Life-Course Retrospective Survey. These historical data were then merged, utilizing unique participant identification numbers, with the subsequent 2015 survey. For the purpose of our analyses, the 2015 wave served as the cross-sectional baseline, from which we extracted data on adulthood social isolation status, all relevant covariates, and the outcome measurements for physical performance and BMI.

The participant selection process is illustrated in Figure S1. Among the 21,097 respondents surveyed in 2015, individuals were sequentially excluded according to the following criteria: (1) age < 45 years (n = 1,378); (2) missing information on childhood or adulthood social isolation (n = 7,978); (3) absence of data on primary outcome measures, including handgrip strength, physical performance, or body mass index (BMI) (n = 2,485); (4) missing data on mediator variables (depression or cystatin C) (n = 1,783); and (5) invalid or missing information on other covariates (n = 1,948). After applying these exclusions, a total of 5,525 participants were included in the final analytic sample. Baseline characteristics of included and excluded participants are presented in Table S1.

Exposure assessment

To characterize the trajectories of social isolation across the life course, we cross-classified the binary childhood exposure and the binary adulthood exposure. Childhood social isolation was derived from the 2014 life-course survey, which retrospectively assessed experiences prior to age 17. In this study, Caspi’s conceptual framework of social withdrawal and exclusion was operationalized into measurable indicators [16]. Four items were evaluated: having a group of friends and feeling lonely (representing social withdrawal), as well as being bullied by neighbors or classmates (representing social exclusion). Each item was scored on a three-level scale. The “having friends” item was reverse-coded (frequently = 0; sometimes = 1; rarely or never = 2), whereas the remaining items were scored positively (rarely or never = 0; sometimes = 1; frequently = 2). Summation of all item scores yielded a cumulative index ranging from 0 to 8, with a score of 2 or higher indicating childhood social isolation [17]. Adulthood social isolation was assessed at the 2015 baseline using a composite index comprising four dichotomous indicators: unmarried status, living alone, lack of weekly contact with children, and absence of social participation [18]. A cumulative score of 2 or more (range 0–4) defined adulthood social isolation. Consistent with prior studies [19], the cross-classification of isolation status across the two life stages generated four mutually exclusive trajectories: no isolation, childhood-only isolation, adulthood-only isolation, and persistent isolation.

Outcome definition

Given that the CHARLS field survey did not include dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) equipment, direct measurement of muscle mass was not feasible. However, the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS 2019) has emphasized that in community settings, the concept of “possible sarcopenia” should be prioritized, defined by low muscle strength and/or impaired physical performance, to enable early identification of at-risk individuals and facilitate lifestyle interventions [20]. Guided by this framework and the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria for Asian populations, we defined the primary outcomes in this study. First, possible sarcopenia was operationalized as the presence of low muscle strength and/or low physical performance: muscle strength was assessed using a hydraulic handgrip dynamometer, with cutoffs of < 28.0 kg for men and < 18.0 kg for women; physical performance was evaluated via the five-time chair stand test or gait speed, with abnormal performance defined as ≥ 12.0 s for the chair stand test or gait speed < 1.0 m/s. Second, obesity was defined as a BMI ≥ 25.0 kg/m² [21]. Finally, possible sarcopenic obesity was defined as the co-occurrence of possible sarcopenia and obesity according to the above criteria.

Covariates

To account for potential confounding, a range of baseline covariates was incorporated based on prior literature and clinical relevance. Demographic characteristics included age, sex, educational attainment (categorized as below elementary, elementary, and above elementary), and residence (urban versus rural). Lifestyle and health-related factors comprised smoking and drinking. Health conditions were assessed for the presence of hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia. Childhood environment was evaluated using family economic status and self-rated health during childhood, categorized as good, fair, or poor. Missing data for all covariates are presented in Figure S2.

Mediating variables

This study incorporated depression and cystatin C as potential mediators. Depression was assessed using the 10-item short form of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D-10), which has been widely validated for evaluating psychological health among middle-aged and older adults in China due to its robust reliability and validity [22]. The scale comprises 10 items, asking participants to report the frequency of specific emotions or behaviors experienced in the past week, covering domains such as depressed mood, anxiety, loneliness, sleep disturbances, and difficulty concentrating. Each item is scored on a four-point scale: “rarely or none of the time (< 1 day)” = 0, “some or a little of the time (1–2 days)” = 1, “occasionally or half the time (3–4 days)” = 2, and “most of the time (5–7 days)” = 3. Two items reflecting positive affect (“I felt hopeful” and “I felt happy”) were reverse-coded to ensure scoring accuracy. Total scores range from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating greater depressive symptom severity. Based on prior validation studies using CHARLS data and relevant clinical standards, a total score ≥ 10 was considered indicative of clinically significant depression [23]. Plasma cystatin C levels were measured from fasting venous blood samples; all samples were collected by trained healthcare personnel, transported under cold-chain conditions, and analyzed centrally at the Clinical Laboratory Center of Capital Medical University using a standardized latex-enhanced immunoturbidimetric assay.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were described stratified by trajectories of social isolation. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using analysis of variance (ANOVA), while categorical variables are reported as counts and percentages, with differences assessed using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Prior to regression modeling, variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated to assess potential multicollinearity among covariates (Table S2). Multivariable logistic regression models were then constructed, with the no-isolation group serving as the reference, to evaluate the associations between social isolation patterns and each outcome. Results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We employed a hierarchical modeling strategy: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for demographic variables; and Model 3 further adjusted for early-life factors, lifestyle, and health conditions.

A series of subgroup and sensitivity analyses were performed. Subgroup analyses stratified the fully adjusted population by age, sex, and residence, using likelihood ratio tests to assess potential interactions. For sensitivity analyses, missing data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) to generate five complete datasets; regression analyses were then repeated, and results were pooled according to Rubin’s rules. Furthermore, given the inherent limitations of observational studies in addressing residual confounding, E-values were calculated to quantify the potential impact of unmeasured confounders.

Finally, exploratory mediation analyses were conducted to assess the potential mediating roles of depression and cystatin C, adjusting for all covariates included in Model 3. Total, direct, and indirect effects were estimated, and the proportion mediated was calculated.

All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2), and two-sided P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Basic characteristics of participants

The study included 5,525 participants (mean age 57.10 ± 8.00 years; 55.87% male). The distribution of social isolation patterns was as follows: no isolation (n = 3,479, 63.0%), childhood-only isolation (n = 1,383, 25.0%), adulthood-only isolation (n = 397, 7.2%), and persistent isolation (n = 266, 4.8%). As presented in Table 1, compared with the no-isolation group, participants in the persistent isolation group were older, had lower educational attainment, and were more likely to reside in rural areas. Regarding early-life circumstances, the persistent isolation group exhibited the highest proportion of poor childhood family economic status (50.75%) and a greater prevalence of poor self-rated childhood health (12.41%). Notably, although the burden of chronic metabolic diseases did not differ substantially across groups, significant disparities were observed in mental health and biomarker profiles. Specifically, the prevalence of depression was markedly higher in the persistent isolation group (44.74%) compared with the no-isolation group (25.84%), and this group also demonstrated elevated cystatin C levels (0.92 mg/L vs. 0.85 mg/L).

Associations of social isolation with possible sarcopenia and obesity

Figure 1; Table 2 illustrate the adverse effects of social isolation on low muscle strength, low physical performance, and possible sarcopenia. Descriptive analyses indicated that participants who experienced social isolation, particularly persistent isolation, exhibited substantially higher proportions of all three outcomes. The rate of possible sarcopenia increased progressively from 48.8% in the no-isolation group to 72.2% in the persistent isolation group, with both low muscle strength and low physical performance peaking in the persistent isolation group at 17.7% and 69.5%, respectively. These patterns were further corroborated in multivariable logistic regression analyses. In Model 3, compared with the no-isolation group, persistent isolation (OR = 1.72, 95% CI: 1.21–2.44) and even childhood-only isolation (OR = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.06–1.62) were significantly associated with increased odds of low muscle strength. In contrast, although the frequency of low physical performance was higher in the persistent isolation group, the association did not reach statistical significance after full adjustment (OR = 1.38, 95% CI: 0.96–1.98). Regarding possible sarcopenia, persistent isolation remained independently associated with increased odds (OR = 1.49, 95% CI: 1.03–2.14).

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Subgroup analyses, presented in Fig. 3, primarily focused on possible sarcopenia and obesity. The associations between social isolation and these outcomes remained generally consistent across most subgroups, with no significant interactions observed for age or sex (P for interaction > 0.05). Notably, residence appeared to modify the association between social isolation and possible sarcopenia (P for interaction = 0.048), with the effect of persistent isolation being more pronounced among urban residents.

Subgroup analyses of the associations of changes in social isolation with possible sarcopenia (A) and obesity (B), stratified by age, sex, and residence

Sensitivity analyses using MICE were highly consistent with the primary findings (Tables S3 and S4). In the imputed datasets, persistent isolation remained positively associated with low muscle strength (OR = 1.87, 95% CI: 1.35–2.58) and possible sarcopenia (OR = 1.41, 95% CI: 1.01–1.97), and inversely associated with obesity (OR = 0.66, 95% CI: 0.50–0.88). Furthermore, to evaluate the potential impact of unmeasured confounding, E-values were calculated (Table S5). The point estimate E-value for possible sarcopenia was 2.34 (E-value for the lower bound of the CI = 1.21), and for obesity, the point estimate E-value was 2.55 (E-value for the upper bound of the CI = 1.63). These findings suggest that an unmeasured confounder would need to be associated with both persistent social isolation and the outcome by a risk ratio of at least 2.34 to 2.55 to fully explain away the observed associations.

Mediation analysis

The results of the exploratory mediation analyses are presented in Fig. 4. Depression exhibited a significant mediating effect in the association between persistent social isolation and low muscle strength, accounting for 12.37% of the total effect; a comparable proportion mediated was observed for the association between persistent isolation and possible sarcopenia (12.49%). Similarly, cystatin C demonstrated a potential mediating role, accounting for 4.13% and 10.25% of the total effects in the associations of persistent isolation with low muscle strength and possible sarcopenia, respectively.

Discussion

Leveraging high-quality, nationally representative data, this study is the first to systematically delineate the divergent associations of social isolation on body composition among middle-aged and older adults in China from a life-course perspective. Our findings reveal a distinctive pattern of divergent associations, whereby persistent social isolation is strongly associated with declines in muscle strength and elevates the risk of sarcopenia, while paradoxically exhibiting an independent association with reduced obesity risk. This composite phenotypic pattern of lower BMI and reduced muscle strength indicates that, within East Asian cultural contexts, social isolation does not simply promote metabolic excess. Instead, it may suggest a pattern where older adults may experience functional muscle decline without obesity.

Our findings are consistent with a growing body of literature identifying social isolation as a critical factor associated with declining muscle function in older adults, while simultaneously revealing a region-specific profile with respect to obesity outcomes. For instance, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis by Yang et al. demonstrated that social isolation is strongly associated with sarcopenia in later life (OR = 1.70, 95% CI: 1.51–1.92) [24]. In contrast, the pattern observed for obesity in our study diverges markedly from classical observations derived from Western populations. Evidence from a large Spanish occupational cohort indicated that social isolation, conceptualized as a chronic psychosocial stressor, was significantly associated with an increased risk of obesity (OR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.21–1.33), lending support to the cortisol-mediated stress-eating hypothesis [25]. Conversely, our analysis among middle-aged and older Chinese adults identified a robust inverse association between persistent social isolation and BMI-defined obesity. This inverse association, which stands in sharp opposition to Western findings, has recently been corroborated in older populations in South Korea [12]. Such cross-cultural inconsistencies provide critical empirical context for these divergent phenotypic patterns. Our data highlight the central role of the commensality effect within East Asian dietary cultures: isolation-related disruption of shared meals may substantially diminish eating motivation, which we speculate could potentially induce a socially mediated anorexic state. We hypothesize that the resulting negative energy balance may overwhelm potential cortisol-driven adipogenic effects while simultaneously restricting the nutritional substrates required for muscle maintenance, thereby leaving older adults vulnerable to a state characterized by concurrently lower BMI and reduced muscle strength.

It is important to note a nuanced discrepancy in our findings regarding the individual components of possible sarcopenia. While persistent social isolation was strongly associated with low muscle strength, its association with low physical performance did not reach statistical significance after full adjustment. This divergence, however, aligns with established gerontological observations. Muscle strength, assessed via handgrip dynamometry, serves as a relatively direct and early indicator of localized neuromuscular decline, which may be acutely sensitive to psychosocial stress and systemic inflammation. Conversely, physical performance tests represent complex and multifaceted outcomes influenced not only by muscle strength but also by joint integrity, balance, and neurological coordination. Therefore, this inconsistency suggests a potential temporal pattern: the physiological burden associated with social isolation may preferentially manifest as specific, early-stage deficits in muscle strength before accumulating sufficiently to drive broader, more complex functional impairments. Recognizing this sequential decline highlights the necessity of utilizing highly sensitive indicators like handgrip strength for the early screening of socially isolated older adults.

By delineating distinct exposure patterns across the life course, this study further demonstrates the time-dependent cumulative association of social isolation with body composition proxies. We observed that persistent exposure to social isolation showed the strongest association with possible sarcopenia, exceeding that associated with transient exposure confined to either childhood or adulthood alone. This graded pattern provides compelling support for the cumulative disadvantage theory [26, 27]. Within this framework, social deprivation in childhood may function as an early mechanism of biological embedding, potentially limiting peak physiological reserve by affecting early musculoskeletal development or by hypothetically programming heightened reactivity of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis [28]. Subsequent persistence of social isolation in adulthood then operates as a chronic stressor, which may further challenge these potentially compromised physiological resources [29]. Accordingly, the association with persistent isolation might not reflect a simple additive combination of risks from two discrete life stages, but rather a potential cascading amplification, whereby early-life isolation may compromise compensatory capacity and later-life exposure imposes an overwhelming cumulative load. Together, these interconnected factors may ultimately exceed physiological resilience thresholds, contributing to the pronounced declines in muscle strength and physical performance observed in our study.

To further explore the potential statistical patterns correlating social isolation with body composition proxies, we conducted an exploratory mediation analysis. The findings suggest that the association between persistent social isolation and lower muscle strength can be statistically partially explained by concurrent factors associated with the psychoneuroimmune axis. First, depressive symptoms exhibited a modest but significant mediating effect in the association between persistent isolation and low muscle strength. From a biopsychological perspective, this might be explained by depression-related psychomotor retardation, whereby psychological distress is related to reduced engagement in voluntary physical activity, thereby diminishing the mechanical stimuli essential for maintaining optimal muscle function [30, 31]. In addition, chronic depressive states are frequently accompanied by hyperactivation of the HPA axis and sustained elevations in cortisol levels, a catabolic milieu that may negatively impact muscle strength [32, 33]. Second, the mediating role of cystatin C provides complementary biological support for this pathway. As a sensitive biomarker of renal function and systemic inflammatory burden, elevated cystatin C levels indicate that persistent social isolation may provoke a state of chronic low-grade inflammation. Such a pro-inflammatory microenvironment can accelerate ubiquitin–proteasome–mediated muscle protein degradation and may further impede muscle repair by disrupting insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) signaling [34, 35]. However, it is crucial to emphasize that because our mediators and outcomes were assessed cross-sectionally, these mediation effects represent statistical associations rather than definitive biological causal chains. Collectively, these exploratory findings support a plausible hypothesis: persistent social isolation is correlated with functional muscle decline through dual exploratory statistical associations involving psychological–behavioral inhibition and heightened inflammatory burden.

Despite the novel insights provided by this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, rather than a prospective longitudinal follow-up, our analytical framework relies heavily on a cross-sectional assessment of the 2015 wave combined with retrospective recall for early-life exposures. Furthermore, because adulthood social isolation and the primary outcomes were measured contemporaneously in 2015, the possibility of reverse causation cannot be ruled out. It is plausible that individuals experiencing poorer physical function or declining health may reduce their social participation, which itself contributes to greater social isolation, rather than social isolation necessarily preceding the functional decline. Second, constrained by the design and data availability of the CHARLS survey, the outcome was defined as possible sarcopenia based on physical performance and strength measures, without direct imaging-based quantification of muscle mass such as DXA or bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA). Similarly, obesity was defined strictly using BMI, which is a surrogate indicator rather than a direct measure of adiposity. Consequently, our findings reflect statistical associations with functional proxies rather than definitive intrinsic tissue depletion or actual body composition remodeling. In addition, childhood exposure variables relied on retrospective self-report, which may be subject to recall bias. Third, as with any observational study, our results may be subject to residual confounding. Specifically, comprehensive dietary information and reliable physical activity data were not fully adjustable due to the inherent constraints and high missing rates in the dataset. Fourth, the substantial reduction in our final analytic sample, introduces a risk of selection bias. However, our sensitivity analyses using MICE yielded results highly consistent with our primary complete-case analysis, providing strong reassurance regarding the internal robustness of the observed associations despite this data loss. Future research should therefore prioritize prospective cohort studies incorporating imaging-based assessments of muscle quantity and fat distribution to validate these potential statistical pathways. Crucially, interventional trials are warranted to determine whether restoring social connectedness can effectively mitigate the adverse functional and metabolic changes associated with social deprivation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides evidence that persistent social isolation spanning both childhood and adulthood is associated with substantially increased odds of possible sarcopenia among middle-aged and older adults in China, while exhibiting an inverse association with BMI-defined obesity. These findings underscore the importance of adopting a life-course perspective when addressing psychosocial health and highlight the potential value of interrupting long-term social isolation in preventing age-related functional muscle decline. At the same time, they caution that a lower risk of BMI-defined obesity may mask the underlying functional vulnerability of this population, suggesting that clinical assessments should not rely solely on weight-centric metrics but should be more attentive to covert functional impairment.
