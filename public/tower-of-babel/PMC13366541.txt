Is sleep a mechanistic factor in the blood pressure lowering effects of low-dose aspirin taken at bedtime? A pilot study
Authors: Michael Vazquez, Huan Yang, Michael Goldstein, Monika Haack, Janet M Mullington
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis was a pilot study designed to test the hypothesis that bedtime administration of acetylsalicylic acid (ASA) lowers BP through sleep improvements that impact the inflammatory, and renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS). We predicted that ASA would enhance sleep consolidation and lower sympathetic activation, protecting against the physiological stress of fragmented sleep.MethodsSeven participants (5F, 2M, mean age 25.71 ± 4.03 years, body mass index (BMI) 22.40 ± 1.91 kg/m2) completed this within-subject, double-blind, balanced study involving 2 × 5 in-patient days. Participants were randomized to daily intake of ASA (81 mg/day) or placebo at bedtime for 2 weeks before each hospital research stay that included 1 baseline (BL) night, 2 nights of an experimental sleep disturbance (ESD) protocol and finally, 1 night of recovery sleep. The ESD protocol included 1-h delayed sleep onset, fragmentation of sleep with 5 × 20-min awakenings, and a 1-h earlier lights on time.ResultsASA did not decrease BP following the 2-week at-home ASA administration, nor during ESD. Evening renin was significantly reduced (p = .048), compared to placebo after 2 weeks of ASA. Mood and well-being improved after 2 weeks of ASA. There were statistical trends toward decreased 24-h average heart rate (p = .053), and increased heart rate variability (p = .080). When challenged with the ESD protocol, ASA was associated with a longer duration of individual slow waves (p = .013), lower 24-h average HR (p < .001) and a trend to greater non-rapid eye movement delta power (p = .061).ConclusionsThis pilot study is the first investigation exploring effects of ASA on renal & hemodynamic parameters at night, implicating sleep as an important mechanistic factor.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease is a leading cause of death in the United States, taking over 850 000 lives in America per year [1]. Nearly 75 million Americans are estimated to have hypertension, making it one of the largest cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk factors in the United States [2]. Only a modest reduction of blood pressure (BP, i.e. 3–5 mmHg) in the population is known to decrease the risk of developing serious cardiovascular events [3]. In 2016, nearly 40 per cent of U.S. adults over 50 years of age used low-dose aspirin for the primary or secondary prevention of CVD [4, 5]. Currently, the use of low dose aspirin in primary prevention is generally not recommended, but the US Preventative Services Task Force published guidance (2022) stating that for the primary prevention of CVD, low dose aspirin may be considered in persons 40–59 with >10 per cent risk for CVD in the next decade and without elevated risks for bleeding [6].

There are two prominent mechanisms that may explain the link between sleep quality and BP: the renin-angiotensin aldosterone system (RAAS), and the autonomic nervous system (ANS) as both systems are associated with sleep and wakefulness [7–9].

It is well established that BP exhibits diurnal fluctuations with sleep and wakefulness [10, 11], and it has been shown that insufficient sleep is associated with increased risk of developing hypertension [12–14]. If sleep is insufficient in duration or consolidation, there is less time spent in non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep, which may prevent normal nocturnal BP dipping [15]. Blood pressure has been shown to follow a circadian rhythm characterized by a nocturnal decline during sleep of ~10–20 per cent relative to daytime values (“dipping”), followed by a rapid increase in the early morning hours coinciding with awakening (“the morning surge”). There has been research which suggests that there are increased risks associated with non-dipping (<10 per cent reduction), including increased risk of CVD events and mortality in hypertensive patients [16]. Lack of nocturnal BP dipping is known to be a strong predictor of cardiovascular adverse events [17].

Sleep quality is also closely related to autonomic regulation. Normal sleep is characterized by reduced sympathetic nervous system activity and enhanced parasympathetic tone, which facilitates nocturnal BP dipping as previously described. Sleep disruption shifts this balance toward sympathetic predominance, resulting in elevated nighttime BP, blunted dipping, and amplification of the morning BP surge [18–20]. Sympathetic activation is additionally associated with increased renin secretion through β1-adrenergic stimulation of juxtaglomerular cells, providing a mechanistic link between autonomic dysregulation and RAAS activation [21, 22]. Under conditions of sleep fragmentation or restriction, this increase in sympathetic drive may augment both renin release and subsequent rise in morning BP.

Evidence increasingly suggests that endogenous circadian rhythms play an important role in regulation of the ANS and the RAAS [23, 24]. In fact, the RAAS also demonstrates circadian rhythmicity, with plasma renin activity and aldosterone concentrations peaking in the early morning hours (~4–7 a.m.) and reaching their nadir during late evening (around 11 p.m.). Importantly, this RAAS rhythm is in antiphase with the blood pressure rhythm as described above, with a renin and aldosterone peak during the night when blood pressure is lowest, with renin and aldosterone at the lowest during the day when blood pressure is elevated [25]. Despite this temporal mismatch, the morning peak in RAAS activity contributes to the physiological morning rise in BP through increased angiotensin II mediated vasoconstriction and aldosterone mediated sodium retention [26, 27]. Higher plasma renin activity is associated with greater morning blood pressure surge and cardiovascular risks as discussed above [23, 27].

Interestingly, while low dose aspirin is not generally associated with lowered BP, several studies have found that 100 mg of aspirin administered at bedtime over extended periods (i.e. 3 months), was associated with lowered BP [28, 29]. This could be the result of a reduction in the RAAS surge, preventing the elevated morning rise in BP [30, 31]. However, it remains unclear if shorter interventions (i.e. 2 weeks) or the commonly used low dose aspirin formulation of 81 mg can produce similar BP lowering effects. Furthermore, individuals who experience sleep disruption also exhibit signs of autonomic dysregulation, as characterized by increased sympathetic activity and decreased parasympathetic activity [32, 33]. These individuals, even if otherwise healthy, are at a greater risk of developing hypertension [32, 34]. Additionally, aspirin has been shown to increase parasympathetic activity, as measured by high frequency power [9].

Only a few studies have investigated the relationship between aspirin and sleep. It should be noted that those studies found that acute administration of high-dose aspirin had a negative effect on sleep quality [35, 36]. However, there are currently no studies looking at the effects of low-dose aspirin on sleep quality. Further, there are no studies looking at the effects of low-dose aspirin on the RAAS system during ESD. However, low-dose aspirin taken at bedtime, can help reduce BP [28, 29, 37, 38], and while the mechanism explaining this effect is still unknown, this raises the question: is sleep a mechanism?

This study was conducted to investigate the effects of bedtime administration of low-dose aspirin on experimentally disrupted sleep and to explore the involvement of the RAAS in mediating these effects. It was predicted that after a 2-week low-dose aspirin intervention prior to the sleep disturbance challenge, individuals would have: improved sleep quality (i.e. increased amount of slow wave sleep [SWS] and delta power), decreased wake after sleep onset (WASO), decreased RAAS activity, and increased parasympathetic activity. While prior studies demonstrating BP reductions with bedtime aspirin administration used longer intervention periods (i.e. 3 months), we hypothesized that this shorter 2-week intervention might reveal early changes in underlying regulatory mechanisms (i.e. RAAS and ANS) which could positively affect BP regulation. It was also predicted that individuals would experience greater benefit from the low-dose aspirin intervention under conditions of disrupted sleep.

Methods

Experimental design

Two, 5-day stays in the Clinical Research Center (CRC) were carried out, beginning with a full night of sleep (8 h of sleep opportunity: lights off at 23:00 and lights on at 07:00), followed by two nights of shortened and disrupted sleep. This ESD protocol limited the participant to 4 h of sleep opportunity through: a 1 h delayed sleep onset, five 20-min long awakenings, and a 1-h early morning awakening, as shown in Figure 1. A research assistant accompanied the participants during ESD to help maintain their wakefulness and to move them into a semi-recumbent position during the time they were in bed maintaining wakefulness. The lights were also adjusted so that the ambient light was ≤20 lux. The participants then had a full night of sleep recovery (8 h of sleep: lights off at 23:00 and lights on at 07:00), before being discharged the following morning. Data from the sleep recovery night were not collected and therefore not included in the analyses.

Study protocol. Participants had 8 h of sleep upon their first night, while undergoing a 24-h continuous beat-to-beat BP, ECG and polysomnography recording. Two nights of the induced ESD protocol (1 h delayed sleep onset, five, 20-min long awakenings, and a 1 h early morning awakening) were then implemented. During the third night, participants underwent the same recording as their first night. Two blood draws were performed before and after the sleep window on the first and third nights. There was one night of recovery sleep before participants left the hospital. Periodic BP measurements were also performed throughout the stay.

The within-subject, counterbalanced crossover study design incorporated a variety of control strategies. Low-dose aspirin (81 mg/day, Bayer) and placebo were administered at the same time (i.e. 22:00) during the 2-week intervention prior to and while at the CRC. Low-dose aspirin is more commonly formulated as 81 mg in the US which is why this dosage was chosen, as opposed to 100 mg which was referenced in prior studies. Randomization was performed by the BIDMC Research Pharmacy. There was also a two-month washout during the crossover period. Meal times were standardized among all participants. Water intake was adjusted based on each participant’s respective weight (at 35 cc/kg). During CRC stays, exercise was limited to short walks within the CRC.

Participants

Eleven healthy participants provided their informed consent to participate in a study that was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC). Four participants dropped out after their first stay resulting in seven participants who completed both the aspirin and placebo arm of the study. There were five females and two males among those who completed both arms of the study, with a mean age of 25.71 ± 4.03 years (range 18–50 years) and mean body mass index of 22.40 ± 1.91 kg/m2 (range 18.5–35.0 kg/m2). Participants had a daily sleep duration between 7.0 and 9.0 h with their sleep period beginning within 1 h of 23:00, verified by sleep log data for 2 weeks. All participants then underwent a complete medical history and physical screening to rule out a history of psychiatric, neurological, pain-related, immune, gastrointestinal, or cardiovascular diseases; significant allergies; Raynaud’s syndrome; intolerance or allergies to non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAID); esophageal reflux; gastric or duodenal ulcers; and asthma. Any participants who had regular medication or drug use, other than oral contraceptives, or donation of blood or platelets 3 months prior to or in between in-hospital visits were also excluded. Along with their medical history, urine toxicology tests were performed to ensure participants were free of active infection, diseases, medications and/or drugs. Female participants were subject to a pregnancy test. After these criteria were met, participants underwent a polysomnographic sleep study. This was used to confirm that the participants had normal sleep wake rhythms, along with a respiratory disturbance index of <5 events/hour, periodic leg movement index (PLMI) <15/hour, and a sleep efficiency of >80 per cent.

Measurements

Actigraphy and sleep diary

An Actiwatch (Actiwatch 2; Respironics Inc., Murrysville, Pennsylvania, USA) was given to participants 2 weeks before each of their two 5-day long CRC stays. At the same time, participants filled out a sleep diary, before attempting sleep and after waking up, for 2 weeks prior to their CRC stay. This was to verify that the participants’ average sleep duration was between 7 h and 9 h per night with the habitual sleep period beginning within 1 h of 23:00.

Polysomnography

Sleep was recorded using the Embla N7000 system (Medcare US, New York, USA) during the medical overnight screening to ensure that participants were free of sleep disorders (i.e. sleep apnea and restless leg syndrome) as well as during the first and third nights of the 5-day protocol. Sleep and wake periods were recorded and manually scored by a sleep technician according to the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) guidelines [39]. Two ECG electrodes were placed on the sub-clavicle fossa for heart rate (HR) recording and heart rate variability (HRV) analysis.

Polysomnographic recordings were acquired and subsequently scored for architecture, based on AASM guidelines [39]. The polysomnographic recordings included a standard sleep montage and sleep electroencephalographic spectra were analyzed from signals collected from electrode placements at F3 and F4 with contralateral mastoid referencing. These signals were extracted for spectral power and waveform analysis relevant to slow wave characteristics, conducted in Matlab version 2019b (The MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA). EEG signals were sampled at 500 Hz and subsequently filtered using a 0.5–105 Hz bandpass and 55–65 Hz notch. Extending procedures from prior studies [40, 41], signal artifacts were automatically identified then visually inspected for optimal data cleaning. In brief, sequential 6-sec epochs were used to calculate spectral power values via FFT (Welch’s averaged modified periodogram with Hamming window), then plotted for each channel of a given recording. An epoch was marked as likely artifact if the 1–4 Hz (delta) power was >2.5 times the average of the surrounding 15 contiguous epochs, or > 2 times the average for 16–30 Hz (beta) power. Flagged epochs were visualized within a time course profile for each of these two power bands for confirmation or modification, such as visible spikes in the power time course not identified via automatic detection. On average, 90.83 per cent (± 2.38) of NREM epochs were retained for analysis. Slow wave activity (SWA) was defined as the 1–4 Hz delta range. Slow wave morphology was examined using previously described procedures [42, 43], resulting in parameters of count, density, amplitude, duration, 1st segment slope, and 2nd segment slope.

Blood draw and plasma renin activity

Two blood draws were performed for each participant during each 5-day in-hospital visit, at 22:00 and 07:05, as shown in Figure 1. A butterfly needle was used for the venipuncture. Plasma renin concentrations were measured using solid phase sandwich enzyme immunosorbent assay (ELISA; Quantikine® HS, R&D Systems, Minnesota, USA).

Blood pressure

Standard BP measured by clinical sphygmomanometer was obtained using the participant’s dominant upper arm at 4-h intervals during the daytime (using Philips automated oscillometric devices (SureSigns VS3; Philips Medical Systems, Massachusetts, USA). The average of five BP measurements, taken 3 min apart following at least 30 min of resting on a chair in a quiet environment, were used. When the participants were awake during the ESD protocol, their BP was measured at 1-h intervals and the average of two BP measurements were used to calculate their average BP.

Beat-to-beat BP data were collected by the Portapres system and concurrently imported into the Embla N7000 system. Following the participant’s stay, the data were then imported to and analyzed in LabChart (LabChart 7; ADInstruments Co., Dunedin, New Zealand). The raw BP waveforms were visually inspected by an investigator to exclude non-analyzable portions of the data. Custom BP analysis settings were applied on LabChart’s BP module before beginning the analysis. These settings included: minimum peak height of 20 mmHg (equivalent to pulse pressure), minimum period of 400 ms (equivalent to R-R interval [RRI]), and a minimum height of dicrotic notch of 5 per cent of peak height. LabChart’s BP module automatically excluded artifacts in the data. After the data were processed, it was calculated that ~15 per cent of the 24 h long BP data had been excluded for one of the following reasons: malfunctioning equipment, removal of equipment (e.g. for the participant to use the bathroom), or the data deviated by more than 3 standard deviations from the mean. The resulting 24 h data were then binned by hour before being applied to a linear mixed model. Data obtained from the 6 h ESD protocol window (00:00 to 06:00) were binned into 40-min sleep and 20-min wake intervals, per study design. Data are presented as estimated mixed model averages for SBP and DBP.

Beat-to-beat BP was measured using the arterial waveform obtained from a finger on the participant’s non-dominant hand on a beat-to-beat basis by digital photoplethysmography (Portapres; TPI, Brussels, Belgium). Two finger cuffs were applied to the non-dominant index finger and middle finger (or ring finger). One of the two cuffs were inflated and deflated continuously to measure beat-to-beat BP. The cuff inflation alternated at 15 min or 30 min intervals depending on the participant’s comfort preference. The finger BP was calibrated intermittently using a height correction unit. Portapres finger BP measurements correlate very well with beat-to-beat arterial BP measurements [44] and have been used to measure 24-h BP variations in the past [45, 46].

Heart rate variability

Beat-to-beat HR data were directly collected by the Embla N7000 System before being imported and analyzed in LabChart using their HRV module. R wave detection was mostly automated as waveforms were identified using preset detection settings for each recording.

HRV was assessed through a power spectral analysis of the RR interval (RRI), which is defined as the temporal duration between two consecutive R-wave peaks on an electrocardiogram, corresponding to a single cardiac cycle [47]. The beat-to-beat RRI tachogram was windowed (i.e. Hann window) and linear de-trended. The Lomb-Scargle periodogram algorithm was then applied to generate the power spectrum analysis. The magnitude of the RRI was quantified by calculating the power spectral density for the signal in low frequency (LF; 0.05–0.15 Hz) and high frequency (HF; 0.15–0.40 Hz). HF power is predominantly a reflection of vagal activity [47]. Normalized units (nu) of the spectral powers of HF [HFnu = HF/(LF + HF)] will be presented. LFnu and LF/HF are not presented as they are algebraically redundant [48]. Beat-to-beat HR recordings were analyzed for the 7 participants who completed both stays.

Subjective ratings

During the daytime, at 4-h intervals, participants used computerized visual scales to rate their emotional and physical well-being. Each scale consisted of a continuous horizontal bar ranging from 0 to 100 where participants selected the point that best reflected their current state to the prompt using an external computer mouse. The scales did not include interval markers or descriptors at intermediate values, only the endpoints were labeled, with 0 indicating the lowest possible value and 100 indicating the highest possible value. During the ESD protocol, from 00:40 to 05:40, participants continued to rate their well-being once during each scheduled nighttime wake period.

A paired samples t-test was used to analyze baseline differences in the subjective emotional ratings, HR, and the root mean square of successive RR interval differences (RMSSD), as measured over 24 h. Results are expressed as means ± standard error of means, which were estimated from the linear mixed model. Effects were considered significant when the p-value was below 0.05 (two-tailed) and considered a statistical trend when the p-value was below 0.1 (two-tailed).

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed statistically using SPSS Statistics (IBM SPSS 26.0, IBM Corp., New York, USA). Linear mixed models were used to estimate the effects of low-dose aspirin on sleep and hemodynamic parameters. Fixed effects included intervention (placebo vs. low-dose aspirin), day (BL vs. ESD), and the interaction between intervention and day when analyzing the individual sleep disturbance nights (BL vs ESD – Night 1 vs ESD – Night 2). Participant identification number was included as a random effect. Pairwise comparison within the mixed linear models was used to estimate the differences between the intervention and study day. When examining data collected during the ESD protocol wake period, the analysis was split by time of day (daytime vs. nighttime).

For the SWA, HR, and BP analyses (Figures 2 and 4, BL was controlled as a fixed covariate in the linear mixed model as there were non-significant BL differences. Previous literature has found that using BL as a covariate in linear mixed models can reduce variance, particularly in smaller data sets. Therefore, in these analyses, data are reported as differences/change (∆) from BL (i.e. ∆ Duration, ∆NREM Average Delta Power, ∆N2N3 Slow Wave Duration, ∆24 h Average HR, and ∆24 Average BP). These analyses are directly comparing the change from BL during the ESD period between the two interventions. This adjustment was not used in the plasma renin activity (PRA) analyses (Figure 3) because there were significant baseline differences present in the samples.

Nor was there an aspirin effect on the ESD standard polysomnography (PSG) metrics, also presented in Table 1.

Plasma renin activity

BL and low dose aspirin

There was no significant difference between PRA levels at BL (day, p = .182). Morning PRA levels were not significantly different in the low-dose aspirin condition (intervention, p = .436). Evening PRA levels were significantly lower across the entire in-hospital stay in the low-dose aspirin condition (intervention, p = .048), as shown in Figure 3.

There was no significant difference between PRA levels during the day of the ESD – Night 2 (day, p = .196).

BL and low dose aspirin

There was a statistical trend of decreased HR (paired t-test, p = .053) and a statistical trend of increased RMSSD (paired t-test, p = .080) in the low-dose aspirin condition. There was no significant difference in HFnu between interventions at BL.

ESD and aspirin

As shown in Figure 4, following ESD, the ∆24 h average HR in the placebo condition was significantly higher compared to the low-dose aspirin condition (intervention, p < .001) when measured via continuous electrocardiogram (ECG). This is in contrast to the data in Table 3, in which there was no significant difference in HR between interventions, when HR was measured periodically throughout the day and during the wakeful parts of the night using Phillips oscillometric devices during the ESD protocols (intervention x day, p = .828 and p = .510, respectively). There was no significant difference in RMSSD or HFnu between the interventions following the ESD (intervention, p = .360 and p = .172, respectively).

There were no significant differences in oscillometric device measured arm systolic BP (SBP) between interventions during the day or wakeful parts of the night during the ESD protocols (intervention x day, p = .869 and p = .405, respectively). These data were consistent with the SBP data obtained from the continuous 24 h beat-to-beat BP monitoring (intervention x day, p = .866). There was a similar result in DBP as there were no significant differences between interventions during the day or wakeful parts of the night during ESD protocols (intervention x day, p = .596 and p = .557, respectively). These data were also consistent with the DBP data obtained from the continuous 24 h beat-to-beat BP monitoring (intervention x day, p = .204).

During the controlled ESD protocol, SBP was significantly higher during wake and REM sleep periods compared to NREM sleep periods in both the placebo and low-dose aspirin interventions (sleep stage, p < .001 and p < .001, respectively; Figure S1). There was no significant interaction effect (intervention x sleep stage, p = .673). Similar patterns were observed for DBP as there was an increase in DBP during wake and REM sleep periods compared to NREM sleep periods in both the placebo and low-dose aspirin conditions (sleep stage, p < .001 and p < .001, respectively; Figure S1). There was no significant interaction effect (intervention x sleep stage, p = .255).

Subjective emotional ratings

Low dose aspirin

At BL, participants reported feeling less annoyed (paired t-test, p = .016), less exhausted (paired t-test, p = .041), and more carefree (paired t-test, p < .001) in the low-dose aspirin intervention as compared to the placebo.

ESD and aspirin

As shown in Table 2, there were significant intervention x day interactions for ratings of “fatigue” and “sleepy” both from daytime as well as nighttime measurements (p’s < .01). These interaction effects may primarily be attributed to acute changes on the 2nd day of ESD (ESD – Night 2) for low-dose aspirin.

Discussion

In this novel, double blinded placebo controlled, crossover pilot study, we investigated the effects of 2 weeks of bedtime administration of low-dose aspirin (81 mg) on sleep architecture, RAAS activity and autonomic function in healthy adults under both normal sleep opportunity of 8 h per night, and of two nights of 4 h of sleep opportunity in a controlled ESD protocol. Bedtime low-dose aspirin was associated with preserved and enhanced SWS, as evidenced by increased ∆N2N3 slow wave duration during sleep restriction, along with reductions in evening PRA and 24 h average HR. Additionally, participants reported improvements in subjective well-being. There were no significant changes in SBP or DBP, likely reflecting the short interventional phase duration of the protocol. These findings provide preliminary evidence that low-dose aspirin at bedtime can influence sleep quality, modulate RAAS and autonomic activity and improve subjective well-being during sleep restricted states.

There have only been a few studies investigating the effects of aspirin on sleep. These studies have exclusively focused on acute high-dose aspirin and have reported that acute administration decreased sleep efficiency, decreased the time spent in N3 sleep and increased the time spent in N2 sleep [35, 36]. However, there have been no studies showing the effects of low-dose aspirin on sleep. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the effects of a 2-week low-dose aspirin intervention, administrated at bedtime, on hemodynamic parameters (i.e. blood pressure) and the RAAS.

Previous studies have shown that during sleep restriction (i.e. 4 h of continuous, uninterrupted sleep) there is a natural preservation of N3 sleep, with a corresponding reduction in time spent in N2 and REM sleep [49, 50]. A person who is experiencing insomnia disorder (i.e. difficulties initiating sleep, maintaining sleep, or experiencing early-morning awakening with an inability to return to sleep) displays continued preservation of both N2 and N3 sleep, with a reduction in REM sleep [51–53]. This ESD protocol utilized substantial sleep disruption, which mirrors patterns observed in both general and clinical populations that experience sleep disturbances due to factors such as pain and stress. This protocol reduces sleep opportunity by 50 per cent compared to baseline conditions, shifts sleep timing by advancing wake time and delaying sleep onset.

As expected, REM sleep decreased significantly from BL as measured during the ESD in both the low-dose aspirin and placebo conditions, confirming the effectiveness of the ESD protocol, and there was no significant decrease in N3/SWS. The preservation of SWS appears to be driven by fundamental homeostatic sleep mechanisms which prioritize SWS over REM as evidenced by the temporal patterns of SWA. Previous literature has found that SWA, which serves as a quantitative measure of sleep intensity through EEG power analysis, is highest following sleep onset, and gradually decreases throughout the sleep period [54]. The maintenance of SWS despite sleep restriction such as our ESD accomplished, is a well-documented phenomenon across various sleep disturbance and sleep restriction protocols.

In contrast to conventional sleep staging, low-dose aspirin affected SW activity and morphology during the ESD as ∆N2N3 slow wave duration was significantly higher during the low-dose aspirin condition as compared to placebo. These data suggest that low-dose aspirin facilitates longer bursts of slow wave neural activity. Furthermore, there was a tendency for average delta power during the ESD to increase in the low-dose aspirin condition as compared to placebo. Taken together with the N3/SWS staging, it appears that low-dose aspirin facilitates slow wave generation during ESD, resulting in more sleep that is scored as N3/SWS.

Participants had decreased evening PRA levels during the low-dose aspirin condition, and during recovery sleep following ESD, had decreased ∆24-h averaged HR. These findings support the hypothesis that sleep has a role in mediating two BP regulating mechanisms, the RAAS and ANS. However, in contrast to previous reports by Hermida and colleagues, there was no observed effect on SBP or DBP during the low-dose aspirin condition. This null finding is likely explained by the substantially shorter intervention duration in our study. Given that the most commonly used low-dose aspirin formulation in the US is 81 mg, we utilized this dose rather than 100 mg [6]. The landmark studies demonstrating the association between bedtime administration of 100 mg of aspirin and reduced BP utilized long-term administration over a period of three months, compared to the 2 weeks in this protocol [27–29]. Snoep et al. similarly utilized a 2-week intervention and identified changes in RAAS activity, but did not examine BP effects [31], suggesting that 2 weeks may be sufficient to initiate physiological changes but insufficient for clinically detectable BP reductions.

Evening PRA levels were significantly lower across the entire in-hospital stay in the low-dose aspirin condition. Previous studies suggest that decreased evening PRA reflects lower daytime RAAS activity and consequently, morning PRA [7, 8]. However, morning PRA levels collected from this study were not different. However, while the samples were collected at the same clock time, participants in the ESD condition were already awake for ~85 min before they were drawn, whereas during the BL, they were drawn ~5 min after waking. Since PRA peaks shortly after waking [8, 31], the specimens collected in the BL might be higher due to the shorter time since waking, thus masking the differences in basal levels. Importantly, evening PRA levels were collected at a consistent circadian time and under comparable sleep–wake conditions across both BL and ESD. This methodological consistency strengthens our findings of bedtime administration of low-dose aspirin reducing evening PRA. Given that the RAAS regulates long-term BP control [55], continued bedtime administration of low-dose aspirin may yield longer-lasting daytime BP reductions, although the 2-week intervention may have been insufficient to detect such effects in this protocol.

RMSSD, a measure of the beat-to-beat variance in HR, has been used to estimate vagally mediated changes in heart rate variability [56, 57]. Despite not observing any significant BP changes following 2 weeks of low-dose aspirin administration, two of the underlying BP regulating mechanisms (i.e. RAAS and ANS) were affected as evidenced by lowered evening plasma renin activity, lowered 24 h HR, and RMSSD.

Two weeks of low-dose aspirin was associated with participants feeling less annoyed, more carefree, and less sleepy and fatigued, compared to placebo. Further, participants on low dose aspirin had greater EEG SWA driven by an increase in ∆N2N3 slow wave duration as determined by waveform morphology analysis and lower evening plasma renin following 2-weeks of low-dose aspirin during the ESD as compared to placebo administration.

Several limitations of this pilot study should be acknowledged. The most notable being the limited number of participants (i.e. seven completers), which limits the statistical power and generalizability of these findings. This sample size was found to be insufficient to detect smaller effect sizes that could be clinically meaningful. Further, the 2-week intervention duration is a second important limitation. Prior studies demonstrated BP reductions with bedtime aspirin over a three-month window. Our null BP findings may be explained by the insufficient intervention length rather than a true absence of effect. Regardless, the purpose of this pilot study was to establish feasibility, identify preliminary physiological mechanisms and inform the design of a subsequently larger trial, which this study was effectively able to do.

This pilot study provides novel evidence for the potential interaction between low-dose aspirin administration at bedtime and sleep physiology, particularly during an ESD challenge. Our findings revealed several notable effects of low-dose aspirin, including: improved subjective well-being at baseline, changes in ANS activity as measured via HRV (i.e. a statistical trend of increased RMSSD), and enhanced slow-wave generation during ESD (i.e. increase in ∆N2N3 slow wave duration and a statistical trend of increased ∆NREM average delta power). Particularly noteworthy was the significant reduction in evening PRA and the consistent decrease in 24-hr average HR during low-dose aspirin administration across the entire protocol. These preliminary findings warrant further investigation through an adequately powered trial to definitively establish the relationship between night-time administration of low-dose aspirin, the ANS and RAAS.
