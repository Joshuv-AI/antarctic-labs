Survival analysis and mortality predictors of COVID-19 across the six-wave pattern of the pandemic in Monastir, Tunisia
Authors: Manel Ben Fredj, Amani Maatouk, Wafa Dhouib, Meriem Kacem, Cyrine Bennasrallah, Amel Gara, Imen Zemni, Ines Bouanene, Asma Belguith Sriha, Hela Abroug
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCOVID-19 represents a serious health concern leading to considerable morbidity and mortality worldwide. The repercussion of COVID-19 was expected to be more severe in middle- and low-income countries. Few studies have examined survival patterns across six successive pandemic waves. The objective of this study was to analyze the survival rates during the first six waves of the pandemic in the Monastir governorate (Tunisia) and identify mortality predictors of COVID-19.MethodsWe conducted a prospective cohort study encompassing all COVID-19 patients admitted to healthcare facilities in Monastir between March 2020 and March 2022, spanning six full waves of infection. Patients were monitored throughout their hospital stay. Data were gathered using a questionnaire carefully filled out by trained residents. Survival analysis was performed to compare outcomes across the different waves. Cox model employing a stepwise variable selection approach in multivariate survival analysis was utilized to assess the risk factors associated with COVID-19 deaths.ResultsCOVID-19 waves occurred between March 2020 and March 2022, resulting in a total of 58,861 infections, 5176 hospitalizations, and 1109 deaths in hospitals. The 30-day survival rate decreased progressively, reaching its minimum during W4 and W5 at 30.6% and 29.5%, respectively. The 30-day cumulative in-hospital mortality probability among COVID-19 hospitalizations was 57.4%. Based on Cox multivariate analysis, W4 and W5 demonstrated the highest mortality risk, with W3 and W6 showing a moderate and comparable level of risk, while W2 exhibited the lowest risk. Independent prognostic factors for early hospital death were age over 75 years (HR 3.44- 95% CI 2.62–4.52), cancer (HR 1.90–95% CI 1.17–3.01), and neurological symptoms (HR 1.30–95% CI 1.04–1.62). Fever (HR 0.69- 95% CI 0.60–0.81) and digestive symptoms (HR 0.73- 95% CI 0.56–0.94) were associated with lower observed mortality.ConclusionAdvanced age, cancer and neurological symptoms are associated factors for in-hospital mortality during COVID-19. Our findings emphasize the importance of early identification and need for targeted interventions for high-risk patients to optimize care and improve survival rates. This six-wave longitudinal analysis provides a comprehensive overview of temporal changes in survival and mortality risk across successive pandemic phases, offering valuable evidence to inform preparedness and response strategies in similar settings.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13659-y.

Introduction

The COVID-19, first emerged in China and has since spread globally, becoming a serious health concern for the international population [1]. Although COVID-19 shares similar clinical presentations and symptoms with Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome (SARS) and Middle East Respiratory Syndrome (MERS), it spreads at a faster rate [2].

The clinical presentation of COVID-19 is heterogeneous and non-specific, ranging from asymptomatic and mild cases to severe, critical forms and death [3, 4]. COVID-19 has led to considerable morbidity and mortality worldwide. The impact of COVID-19 has prompted an increasing amount of research into the causes and risk factors associated with COVID-19 mortality. Frequently reported direct causes of death include acute respiratory distress syndrome, multiple organ failure, heart failure, kidney failure, shock, secondary infections, anemia, and cardiac arrhythmias [5, 6]. Moreover, advanced age and comorbidities such as chronic respiratory failure, cardiovascular disease, obesity, hypertension, diabetes, and cancer have been globally recognized as key risk factors for hospitalization and death [7, 8]. Beyond demographic risk factors, recent research indicate-s that clinical and laboratory markers, such as elevated Interleukin-6 and lymphocyte counts, along with conditions like diabetes and atrial fibrillation, significantly influence both early and long-term mortality risk, suggesting the need for integrated predictive models in COVID-19 survival analyses [9].

The repercussion of COVID-19 was expected to be more severe in middle- and low-income countries, which face limited healthcare systems with low access to Intensive Care Units, and high illiteracy rates [10]. In Tunisia, initial waves until March 2021 were primarily characterized by the prevalence of viruses belonging to wild-type lineages. In the spring of 2021, a distinctive fourth wave emerged, marked by the predominant circulation of the Alpha lineage, followed by a fifth wave in the summer of 2021, driven by the Delta variant. The sixth wave, occurring in the winter of 2022, was associated with the emergence of the Omicron variant, replacing previously circulating variants [11, 12]. Mortality statistics has revealed that COVID-19 was the third leading cause of death in Tunisia in 2020 (2834 deaths, representing 6.1% of total deaths) [13].

While the clinical and epidemiological characteristics of the disease have been extensively researched, other important aspects, such as patient survival rates and the factors influencing survival, remain less understood. Gaining insight into survival rates and the impact of risk factors is essential for policymakers and healthcare providers. Such information is critical for identifying factors that improve patient survival, estimating life years lost, optimizing current treatments, ensuring drug safety, evaluating the effectiveness of medical products, assessing the reliability of therapies and device, and allocating future healthcare costs [14, 15]. COVID-19 survival analysis provides insights into how medical advancements, healthcare strategies, and changes in the virus may have affected patient outcomes over time. It helps identify areas for improvement in clinical care and public health responses. By comparing consecutive waves of COVID-19 pandemic, such analysis provides a detailed understanding of survival rates, contributing to a broader perspective on pandemic management and patient outcomes. Understanding factors associated with the risk of COVID-19-related death can inform future strategies to address similar health crises.

The objective of our study was to analyze variations in COVID-19 survival rates during different waves and identify the main factors influencing these changes.

Methods

Study design

A two-year prospective cohort study was conducted, including all COVID-19 patients hospitalized in private and public healthcare facilities in Monastir from March 2020 to March 2022. Six separate COVID-19 waves of were covered [16]. Patients were observed starting from their initial hospital admission and followed through to their eventual hospital outcome, which could include discharge, transfer or death.

Study setting

Monastir governorate comprises 13 delegations and lies in Tunisia’s central-eastern region. According to the last general census of the Tunisian population in 2021, Monastir had 606,401 residents, constituting almost 5.2% of Tunisia’s total population [17].

Monastir’s healthcare system included 88 primary health centers, 12 peripheral and regional healthcare facilities, a university hospital (Fattouma Bourguiba University Hospital (FBUH) including Units A and B), and private clinics. COVID-19 care was concentrated at FBUH, Moknine and Ksar Hellal regional hospitals, Tbolba and Jammel peripheral hospitals, and two private clinics. Many departments in FBUH were dedicated to COVID-19 patients: Emergency Department, Intensive Care Unit Department, medical departments (Infectious Disease Department, Nephrology Department, Pulmonology Department, Cardiology Department, Gastroenterology Department), and surgical departments (ear, nose and throat (ENT) Department, General Surgery Department).

Study population

All COVID-19 laboratory-confirmed patients hospitalized in Monastir at public and private healthcare facilities between March 2020 and March 2022 were included. Hospitalizations were attributed either to COVID-19 as the primary cause or to healthcare-associated infections contracted during the hospital stay. For cases of healthcare-associated infection, the date of diagnosis was considered the admission start date.

Patients could be recorded multiple times if they experienced reinfection or were rehospitalized for distinct infectious episodes. For the purposes of the survival analysis, only the most recent episode was considered.

Those presenting symptoms suggestive of COVID-19 but with negative biological tests and without imaging confirmation of the disease were not included. Asymptomatic individuals who tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 incidentally during admission for unrelated conditions and did not require specific COVID-19 management were excluded.

Definition of variables

The diagnosis of confirmed cases relied on a positive rapid test from a nasopharyngeal or oropharyngeal swab, and/or real-time reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR), and/or thoracic computed tomography (CT) scans revealing lung abnormalities indicative of COVID-19 pneumonia. Patient outcomes included discharge, transfer to another facility, or death. “Discharge” referred to a patient being released alive from the hospital and returning to their home after admission. Information on the admission to intensive care unit (ICU) was also collected.

We collected functional symptoms, divided by system: general signs (fever and asthenia), respiratory signs (dyspnea, cough, and/or expectoration, chest pain), digestive signs (abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea), rheumatological signs (arthralgia, lumbalgia, myalgia), neurological and psychological disorders (headache, confusion, convulsions) and ENT signs (anosmia, ageusia, otalgia, vertigo, rhinitis).

During the pandemic, we identified six waves, which aligned with spikes in COVID-19 cases and hospitalizations in Tunisia (Table 1). The timeline of the different epidemic waves was determined based on Tunisian studies in which genomic sequencing was performed [11, 18, 19].

The start date corresponds to the admission date for newly admitted patients or the diagnosis date for those who were already hospitalized and contracted COVID-19 within the hospital.

The endpoint date represents the date of death, transfer or hospital discharge. The length of stay or the survival time is the period between the start date and endpoint date.

Data collection

The data collection spanned from March 2020 to June 2022 and was carried out in two main phases. First, the follow-up phase lasted from March 2020 to March 2022. Then, the verification phase took place between April and June 2022, focusing on reviewing data and supplementing any missing information when available. The data collected included the patient’s gender, date of birth, place of residence, nationality, occupation, comorbidities, vaccination status, reason for hospitalization, admission date, all symptoms experienced, time interval between symptom onset and hospital admission, diagnostic confirmation method, medications administered for COVID-19 treatment, type of oxygen support, and outcomes. Additional details on the data collection procedures are provided in Supplementary File 1.

Data analysis

Data entry was performed using Excel. Data analysis was carried out using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 20.0. Qualitative variables were described using frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between percentages were performed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test where appropriate. The following indicators were calculated: in-hospital case fatality rate (CFR), hospitalization rate, 30-day COVID-19 in-hospital survival rate, 30-day cumulative mortality probability, median survival time, and mean survival time. The survival analysis framework and multivariable analysis approach were also defined. A detailed description of the data analysis is provided in Supplementary File 2.

Survival analysis framework

In the survival analysis, the event of interest was in-hospital death (coded as 1), while discharge alive was treated as censored (coded as 0). Time was measured in days. Survival time was defined as the duration of hospitalization from admission (or diagnosis for nosocomial infection) to death or discharge. The survival function represented the cumulative probability of remaining alive over time. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were constructed to describe survival distributions according to pandemic waves, sex, age groups, and ICU admission status. Differences between groups were assessed using the Log-Rank test.

Multivariable analysis

A Cox proportional hazards regression model with stepwise variable selection was used to identify independent predictors of in-hospital mortality. Variables with a p-value < 0.20 in univariate analysis were considered for inclusion, along with clinically relevant predictors identified in the literature. Results were reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The following explanatory variables were evaluated in the Cox model: age group, sex, pandemic wave, hypertension, cancer, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, chronic respiratory disease, number of comorbidities, fever, asthenia, ENT symptoms, cardiorespiratory symptoms, digestive symptoms, rheumatological symptoms, neurological symptoms, and ICU admission. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Internal validation of the multivariable Cox proportional hazards model was performed using a random split-sample approach. A random variable uniformly distributed between 0 and 1 was generated, and the dataset was randomly divided into a training set (70% of observations- rand ≤ 0.7) and a testing set (30%- rand > 0.7). The model was developed in the training dataset and subsequently applied to the testing dataset to evaluate the stability of hazard ratios and the consistency of statistically significant predictors. Discrimination performance was assessed by comparing model coefficients and overall model fit across datasets. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated by including time-dependent interaction terms between covariates and log(time) in the Cox regression model. Statistical significance was assessed using Wald tests. ICU admission was not included in the main etiologic Cox model because it may lie on the causal pathway between disease severity and mortality.

Results

Epidemic description

Between March 2020 and March 2022, six waves of COVID-19 occurred. A total of 160 earlier episodes were excluded among patients who experienced reinfection. Overall, there were 58,861 COVID-19 infections, resulting in 5176 hospitalizations and 1109 in-hospital deaths. The hospitalization rate was 8.8% and in-hospital case fatality rate was 21.4%. W5 recorded the highest proportions of infections (36.2%) and hospitalizations (41.1%), with 466 deaths and a CFR of 21.9%. The sixth wave W6, despite accounting for 30.3% of total infections, had the lowest hospitalization rate (2.8%) and 98 deaths, reflecting a decreased CFR of 19.5%. In contrast, earlier waves, such as W3 and W4, exhibited higher hospitalization rates (11.8% and 14.9%, respectively) and notably elevated CFRs (23.8% and 27.5%) (Table 2).

According to age groups, the 30-day cumulative probability of mortality increased significantly (p < 0.001) with age during the study period. The curve for individuals aged 0 to 18 was irregular due to the small number of events that occurred in that age group. Based on gender, the 30-day cumulative probability of mortality was slightly higher among males, but not statistically significant.

Kaplan-Meier curves illustrating the 30-day cumulative probability of mortality, stratified by waves showed that the probability of death increased significantly (p < 0.001) as the waves pandemic progressed. Around 14% of deaths occurred within 30 days in W1, 34.3% in W2, 52.1% in W3, 69.4% in W4, 68.9% in W5, then decreased to 53% in W6. The risk of death was significantly higher (p < 0.001) among patients admitted to the ICU compared to those who were not, with a 30-day cumulative probability of mortality at 67.3% versus 52.3%.

Overall, a longer median survival (p < 0.001) was observed among individuals exhibiting the following symptoms: fever (25 days [95% CI: 20.9–30.5] vs. 20 days [95% CI: 18.1–21.9]), digestive signs (43 days [95% CI: 36.3–49.4] vs. 22 days [95% CI: 20.3–23.7]), and rheumatological signs (29 days [95% CI: 20.0–38.0] vs. 22 days [95% CI: 20.2–23.8]). Consistently, the presence of these symptoms delayed the time to death across almost all waves.

In addition, patients with asthenia exhibited a significantly longer time to death (0.001 < p < 0.05) only during the W2 (23 days [95% CI: 20.4–25.6] vs. 22 days [95% CI: 19.2–24.3]). Manifesting neurological symptoms was associated with shorter median survival during W5 at (12 days [95% CI: 9.3–14.7] vs. 19 days [95% CI: 16.2–21.8], p < 0.001). Patients exhibiting asthenia during W3 had a longer median survival at (30 days [95% CI: 20.4–39.6] vs. 22 days [95% CI: 17.3–26.7], p < 0.05). Patients admitted to the ICU had a higher risk of early mortality, leading to a shorter overall median survival time (18 days [95% CI: 15.9–20.1] vs. 29 days [95% CI: 25.4–32.7], p < 0.05). This pattern was similarly observed in W3, W4, and W6 (Supplementary Table 1).

Overall, a history of cancer accelerated the onset of death, resulting in a shorter median survival (13 days [95% CI: 8.0–18.0] vs. 22 days [95% CI: 20.1–23.9], p < 0.05). No significant difference in median survival was noted among patients with other comorbidities. A longer median survival was observed among patients with hypertension during W4 (21 days [95% CI: 17.3–24.7] vs. 16 days [95% CI: 13.8–18.2], p < 0.05), those with chronic lung diseases during W3 (34.4 days [95% CI: 26.5–42.2]M vs. 23 days [95% CI: 18.9–27.1], p < 0.05), and those with diabetes during W2 (59.1 days [41.7–76.6]M vs. 43.4 days [95% CI: 38.5–48.3]M, p < 0.05) and W3 (25 days [95% CI: 13.3–36.7] vs. 22 days [95% CI: 16.8–27.2], p < 0.05) (Supplementary Table 2).

Multivariate analysis

A multiple Cox regression analysis, encompassing 98.5% of the sample (5096 patients), showed several predictors indicating progression to death rather than recovery among COVID-19 inpatients. The primary prognostic factor was advanced age, especially among individuals aged over 75 years (HR = 3.44; 95%CI= [2.62–4.52]). Having a cancer diagnosis and experiencing neurological symptoms were also identified as independent prognostic factors for early hospital death. In contrast, fever (HR = 0.69; 95%CI= [0.60–0.81]) and digestive symptoms (HR = 0.73; 95%CI= [0.56–0.94]) were independently associated with a lower hazard of death in the adjusted analysis. Furthermore, compared to W2, the risk increased by 1.83 in W3, 2.78 in W4, 3.40 in W5, and 1.91 in W6, respectively. These results remained consistent even after adjusting for multiple potential confounders including age, gender and underlying comorbidities (Table 5; Fig. 6). The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated by introducing time-dependent interaction terms between each covariate and the logarithm of survival time into the Cox regression model. None of the interaction terms were statistically significant (all p > 0.05), including those for age (p = 0.430), pandemic waves (p = 0.703), fever (p = 0.731), digestive symptoms (p = 0.243), neurological symptoms (p = 0.154), and neoplastic comorbidity (p = 0.962). These results indicated no evidence of violation of the proportional hazards assumption, supporting the validity of the Cox proportional hazards model.

Discussion

Our study showed that the 30-day survival rate decreased progressively, reaching its minimum during W4 and W5 at 30.6% and 29.5%, respectively. Based on Cox multivariate analysis, W4 and W5 demonstrated the highest mortality risk, with W3 and W6 showing a moderate and comparable level of risk, while W2 exhibited the lowest risk. We found a 42.6% overall survival rate 30 days post-hospital admission. This rate exhibited a gradual decline from W2 (65.7%) to W5 (29.5%), followed by an increase in W6 to 49.8%. Notably, our 30-day survival rate surpassed that reported in Ethiopia (12.8% at 14 days of admission) [21], sub-Saharan Africa (19.9% at 20 days of admission) [22] and USA (23.9%) [23]. However, it remained lower than the survival rates reported in Netherlands (77.6%) [24], Iran (86.9% and 93.8%) [14, 15], Brazil (87.7%) [25], China (83.5%) [26], Denmark (85.8%) [27] and Italy (ranging from 79.3% during W1 to 85.8% in W3) [28].

In addition, the median hospital survival from the time of admission to death in the present research was 23; 95%CI: [21.1–24.9], revealing a statistically significant disparity across different waves. Our finding closely resembled that reported in Iran (21 days; IQR = 13–41) [29] and exceeded durations observed in Ethiopia (9 days; IQR = 8–12) [21], the USA (6 days; IQR = 7), China (11 days; IQR = 7-16.5) [30] and Mexico (18 days; IQR = 12–23) [25]. However, it was shorter than the duration reported in Uganda (33 days; IQR = 9–82) [31] and a further study conducted in Mexico (33 days; IQR = 32–42) [32].

The variations in country-level results can stem from multiple factors, including differences in the methodology for measuring time to death, the study period, and the attack rate within each population [21]. Demographic characteristics specific to each country, such as age distribution and the prevalence of comorbidities, may contribute to disparities in outcomes. Additionally, country-specific control policies implemented to manage the spread of the disease can also influence results [23]. Furthermore, variations in the capacities of healthcare systems, encompassing accessibility to care, the availability of medical resources, and the overall quality of patient management, can play a crucial role. These differences in healthcare structures among countries can directly impact survival rates and patient outcomes. In Tunisia, at the onset of the pandemic, all individuals diagnosed with COVID-19 were initially hospitalized. However, with a sharp increase in the number of cases, exceeding the capacity of the healthcare system, the admission and discharge criteria were subsequently adjusted to prioritize services for those who needed them the most.

In our study, multivariate analysis using the Cox proportional hazards model revealed that advanced age, cancer, experiencing neurological symptoms, W3, W4, W5 and W6 compared to W2 were independent COVID-19 prognostic factors. Fever and digestive symptoms were associated with lower observed mortality in our cohort. This association may reflect earlier presentation, milder disease phenotypes, or differences in healthcare-seeking behavior, rather than a direct protective effect. Such patterns are common in hospital-based studies and may be influenced by lead-time bias, where patients with classic symptoms are present earlier in the disease course, as well as by selection or detection biases, which could contribute to apparently better outcomes in these patients.

Internal validation showed consistent effect directions and comparable hazard ratios between training and testing datasets. Age groups and waves maintained clear dose–response relationships. Although some less frequent variables lost statistical significance in the testing set, the overall consistency supports the model’s stability and suggests limited overfitting.

The risk factors commonly reported by most studies, associated with reduced survival rates and a shorter median time to death, include advanced age, the need for ICU admission, and the presence of cancer. In Netherlands, predictive factors of COVID-19 in hospital mortality were advanced age, immunocompromised state, use of anticoagulants or antiplatelet medication, and higher blood LDH at time of admission. In the same study, fever decreased the risk of death [24]. Most published studies evaluated only two or three pandemic waves. One of the few six-wave analyses showed higher mortality during the first, third, and fourth waves in elderly patients, identified confusion, dyspnea, lymphopenia, elevated IL-6, and procalcitonin as major predictors [33].These findings are partly consistent with our results, as we also observed increased mortality during intermediate waves (W3–W5 vs. W2). The limited number of six-wave studies highlights the added value of our longitudinal analysis in capturing temporal changes in COVID-19 mortality patterns.

Our findings are broadly consistent with international evidence identifying age, comorbidity burden, and disease severity markers as major determinants of COVID-19 prognosis. The age effect observed in our cohort aligns with findings from Iran, Brazil, the United States, Mexico, China, Ecuador, and Denmark, where advanced age consistently demonstrated a dose–response relationship with mortality [34–40].

However, the magnitude of risk in our population (HR = 3.44 for ≥ 75 years) was intermediate compared with some reports showing markedly higher hazard ratios in the oldest age groups. Such differences may reflect variations in population age structure, hospital admission criteria, vaccination coverage, and healthcare system strain across waves. The noteworthy impact of age on the heightened risk of early mortality, consistently underscored in numerous studies, may be intricately linked to the aging process affecting the immune system, particularly the declining thymic T-cell production, and the excessive production of type 2 cytokines, leading to an extended pro-inflammatory response [41]. This prolonged inflammatory state has the potential to impair the control of viral replication and cause damage in various organs, particularly those with high expression of angiotensin-converting enzyme genes, such as the heart and lungs [15, 42]. As a result, strategies and interventions aimed at enhancing survival rates should prioritize older adults, particularly those with comorbidities.

Regarding temporal dynamics, we observed a progressive increase in mortality risk during W3–W5 compared with W2, followed by attenuation in W6. These patterns differ from reports in the United Kingdom and Switzerland, where later waves were associated with reduced mortality compared with the first wave [43, 44]. Importantly, cross-country comparisons must be interpreted cautiously. Definitions of “pandemic waves” were not standardized: some studies defined waves based on national incidence peaks, others by dominant variants or fixed calendar periods. In addition, outcomes were measured at different time points (e.g., 28-day, 30-day, or overall in-hospital mortality), and patient characteristics varied substantially across waves. These methodological differences may partially explain the observed discrepancies in wave-specific hazard ratios.

Published studies have documented associations between neurological signs and mortality. For example, a retrospective cohort from Mexico found that neurological impairment at admission was associated with mortality in COVID-19 patients, indicating that certain neurological signs may serve as risk factors for poor outcomes [45]. Similarly, Salahuddin et al. showed that major neurological manifestations during the disease course were independent predictors of death in hospitalized patients with COVID-19 [46]. In a Brazilian cohort, encephalopathy—the most severe neurological complication—was significantly associated with higher mortality among COVID-19 admissions [47].

Many previous studies have focused on selected periods of the COVID-19 pandemic or specific aspects of the disease. In contrast, this study aimed to assess survival and mortality predictors among hospitalized patients across all six pandemic waves. It compares data over time, using mortality data as an indicator of disease severity and healthcare system performance.

Overall, while our findings confirmed the central role of age, comorbidities, and severity indicators in COVID-19 mortality, they also highlighted the importance of temporal context. Nevertheless, comparisons across waves should be interpreted cautiously, as the “wave” variable represents a proxy for multiple time-varying and potentially unmeasured factors. Despite adjustment for major clinical variables, residual confounding cannot be excluded, and observed differences in survival across waves likely reflect contextual changes rather than intrinsic differences in patient risk.

Some limitations merit to be mentioned. Survival analysis for patients from the first wave was not performed due to the smaller sample size. In Tunisia, the first wave was relatively mild, partly owing to the early implementation of lockdown measures, which likely reduced the number of hospitalized cases during that period [20].

Vaccination status was not included in the multivariable analysis due to incomplete data, during the latter pandemic waves. Although vaccination rollout expanded progressively from 2021, vaccination coverage was very low and recording practices were not yet fully standardized, limiting reliable adjustment for this variable.

The heterogeneity in time origin (the date of hospital admission for community-acquired cases and the date of diagnosis for healthcare-associated infections) theoretically introduce immortal time bias. However, due to the strict infection-control measures implemented, the number of healthcare-associated infections was extremely small in our study population, and the potential impact of this bias on survival estimates was considered negligible.

Internal validation was performed using data splitting- however, external validation of the predictive models was not conducted, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. The loss of statistical significance for some variables during internal validation may reflect model variability and should be interpreted with caution.

The use of stepwise variable selection represents a limitation of this study, as such methods may produce unstable models and biased estimates. To mitigate this limitation, clinically relevant variables and those supported by prior literature were considered during model construction.

Another limitation of this study is that hospital discharge was treated as a censoring event in the Kaplan–Meier analysis, although it represents a competing risk for in-hospital mortality. Future studies using competing-risk models may provide more precise estimates of cumulative incidence.

Finally, the study focused on in-hospital mortality and did not capture deaths occurring outside the hospital, which should be considered when interpreting the public health implications of the findings. Although the large sample size helped reduce potential bias, this limitation may affect generalizability of mortality estimates.

Conclusion

In our research, advanced age, particularly among individuals aged over 75 years, having a cancer diagnosis and experiencing neurological symptoms were identified as independent prognostic factors. Conversely, manifesting fever and digestive symptoms were found to be associated to lower observed mortality. The risk of death was higher during W3, W4, W5, and W6 compared to W2. Prioritizing the protection and early intervention for high-risk groups, particularly older adults and those with pre-existing conditions like cancer is recommended. Tailored medical strategies, including close monitoring of neurological symptoms and timely ICU care are essential in managing patients at higher risk. Further studies are needed to investigate the mechanisms behind associated factors and to evaluate the changing nature of the pandemic to guide future healthcare strategies. Continuous improvements in public health preparedness, resource allocation, and patient management are critical to reducing COVID-19-related mortality, especially in successive waves.
