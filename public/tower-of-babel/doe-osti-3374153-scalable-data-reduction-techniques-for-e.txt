Final Report
 Scalable Data Reduction Techniques for Extreme-Scale Unstructured PDE Simulations
 Federal Award Identification Number: DE-SC0022283
 Principal Investigator: Dr. Alireza Doostan, University of Colorado Boulder
                             Project Period: 09/01/2021 – 10/31/2025

1 Summary of Accomplishments
The present report summarizes activities performed over the 3-year duration of the award DE-
SC0022283, “Scalable Data Reduction Techniques for Extreme-Scale Unstructured PDE Simula-
tions,” funded by the DOE Office of Advanced Scientific Computing Research (ASCR). The re-
search team consisted of the PI (Dr. Alireza Doostan), co-investigators (Drs. Stephen Becker, John
A. Evans, and Kenneth Jansen), postdoctoral fellows (Dr. Angran Li and one additional post-
doctoral fellow in Aerospace Engineering), and several graduate students (Kevin Doherty, Jeffrey
Hadley, and Cooper Simpson).
 The utilization of massive parallelization on peta- or exascale systems has significantly enhanced
simulations based on partial differential equations (PDEs), resulting in a drastic increase in the
production rate of high-fidelity data that are key to better understanding many complex physical
systems. However, this growth of computational power has considerably outpaced the development
of IO storage and data analytics resources, creating a bottleneck where the time allocated to writing
and processing data compared to data generation has steadily increased. To address this challenge,
scientific data reduction has emerged as a promising direction, but state-of-the-art data reduction
algorithms often achieve only modest compression factors, are constrained to structured grids and
Cartesian domains, focus primarily on spatial compression, or require multiple passes over the data,
limiting their ability to handle streaming data effectively.
 The main goals of this project are to overcome these challenges and limitations through novel
computational strategies and tools. Specifically, we focus on the development and massively parallel
implementation of novel linear and non-linear dimensionality reduction techniques for spatial and
spatio-temporal data compression. A particular emphasis is placed on error-controlled, in situ
compression of high-dimensional unstructured PDE data, possibly defined over complex geometries.
The research tasks are based on the hypotheses that solutions to many complex PDE systems lie
on low-dimensional subspaces or manifolds, and that such structures can be recovered in situ with
minimal tax to the PDE solver. Key to our approach is the development of reduced basis and deep
neural representations specialized to data reduction and broadly applicable to solvers independent
of discretization or physical system.
 The accomplished work is organized into four major tasks. Task 1 develops spatial data compres-
sion methods using algebraic multigrid (AMG) and multilevel decomposition schemes, as well as an
unstructured grid extension of discrete cosine transforms (DCT). Task 2 focuses on interpretable
spatio-temporal reduction via in situ interpolative decomposition, leveraging randomized linear
algebra for scalable temporal compression. Finally, Task 3 addresses non-linear dimensionality re-
duction through autoencoders (AEs) and implicit neural representations (INRs), with emphasis on
robust unstructured operators and streaming data compression, respectively.
 The highlights of this project’s efforts are:
 ◦ Development of a fully generalizable AMG-based multilevel decomposition scheme for spatial
 data compression on structured and unstructured meshes, including mixed element meshes and

 ◦ Development and extension of a single-pass randomized interpolative decomposition technique

presentations supported (fully or partially) by the award.

2 Technical Contributions
During the project period, the team expanded its efforts along four main directions: (1) spatial data
compression via an AMG-based multilevel decomposition scheme, (2) temporal compression via
streaming randomized interpolative decomposition, and (3) spatio-temporal data compression via
non-linear dimensionality reduction (specifically, autoencoders and implicit neural representations).
In what follows, we provide a summary of our accomplishments in these directions.

2.1 Spatial Data Compression via AMG-Based Multilevel Decomposition and
 Manifold Harmanic Basis
Current state-of-the-art methods for spatial compression of unstructured mesh data are either
limited to meshes resulting from recursive mesh bisection or treat the unstructured data as an “un-
rolled” 1D data array, restricting both performance and generalizability. To alleviate these restric-
tions, our research goal was to develop a fully generalizable AMG-based multilevel decomposition
scheme that can be integrated into a standard compression workflow consisting of decomposition,
quantization, encoding, and lossless compression.
 During Year 1 (Y1), the team completed foundational work encompassing a literature review
of Multilevel Decomposition and Geometric Agglomeration methods, and validated 2D structured
Multilevel Decomposition methods by developing data compression routines to better understand
the inner workings of the method in a “simple” setting. Building on this, the team expanded the cur-
rent Multilevel Decomposition theory to incorporate geometric agglomeration principles, developed
a proposed Agglomerated Multilevel Decomposition algorithm, and created mesh file read/write
software to bridge meshing software output files and the currently available agglomeration software.
 During Y2, the team further extended and deepened this effort. The literature review was
broadened to include Hierarchical Decomposition, Multilevel Decomposition, and Geometric Ag-
glomeration methods, as well as Algebraic Multigrid (AMG) literature. The mesh file read/write
software was further developed and extended to incorporate additional mesh file types and agglom-
eration software. A C-based software package was also developed to compute nested agglomerates
and their associated basis function operators required to perform Hierarchical (solve-free) and Mul-
tilevel (solve-required) decomposition of 2D unstructured data. Verification and validation of these
newly developed functions and routines were then performed. Performance testing of data re-

duction via hierarchical decomposition of unstructured grids with agglomeration-based operators
revealed compression artifacts, prompting further investigative testing to identify their root causes
and active work on resolving them, taking inspiration from Algebraic Multigrid methods.
 During Y3, we developed a fully generalizable AMG-based multilevel decomposition scheme.
Unlike earlier hierarchical or geometric decomposition approaches, which suffered from visible com-
pression artifacts, the AMG-based strategy eliminates these issues, supports both structured and
unstructured data, and handles mixed element meshes. A detailed performance study was con-
ducted to identify optimal AMG configurations for compression applications, and the method has
been successfully extended to three-dimensional problems, achieving significantly lower compression
errors compared to state-of-the-art compressors.
 Alongside algorithmic development, we advanced the supporting software infrastructure. New
tools have been created, and the capabilities of existing AMG software have been extended to
support both serial and parallel computations of the multilevel decomposition algorithm on CPUs.
These implementations have been integrated into the Higher Order Navier-Stokes Equation Eval-
uator (HONEE) package, leveraging PETSc and libCEED for parallel scalability and operator
generation. The results from algorithmic development, performance evaluation, and software inte-
gration have been consolidated into an academic paper in preparation for submission.
In Situ Parallel Implementation A key goal of the project is to enable in situ compression
that operates alongside the PDE solver with minimal overhead. During Y3, significant progress
was made on the parallel AMG-based multilevel decomposition software. The core framework has
been implemented, tested, and prepared for release. Current work focuses on software development,
performance tuning, and scalability on modern architectures, ensuring both efficiency and usability
for the broader scientific community. Interfaces with existing ecosystems are being refined, and
validation on benchmark problems is underway.
 Work is also ongoing to extend the single-pass randomized interpolative decomposition to par-
allel and in situ contexts. The Python package is being ported to PETSc and integrated with
HONEE for tests on large-scale turbulent flows. In situ deployment will use SmartSim, already
proven in HONEE. We will compare performance with compressors like SZ and FPZIP and develop
a unified framework combining AMG-based spatial compression with randomized temporal ID for
robust end-to-end compression.
 Also during Y2, the team developed a novel block-coder style data compression scheme, called
Mesh-Float-Zip (MFZ), designed specifically for nonuniform grids and possibly non-Cartesian
domains [J2]. Block-coder style compressors such as JPEG, ZFP, and SPERR rely on de-correlating
transformations in order to efficiently store data with entropy encoding schemes. These de-
correlating transformations are typically reliant on a uniform spatiotemporal sampling of the
transformations and/or the data. Thus, for nonuniform data such as those arising from partial
differential equations solved over complex geometries and unstructured grids, there is not an ef-
ficient de-correlating transformation nor a data compression pipeline that sufficiently adapts to
this nonuniformity. To address this, we presented the manifold harmonic basis (MHB) as a de-
correlating transformation, and developed a collocation method over the nonuniform domain for
solving the generalized eigenvalue problem (GEVP) that defines the MHB. Specifically, rather than
discretizing the Laplace operator via the finite element method, we approximated the continuous
eigenfunctions using a radial basis function (RBF) collocation approach based on Gaussian kernels,
which avoids discretization of the Laplace operator, is simpler to implement in higher dimensions,
and produces a basis of real-valued eigenvectors that is more storage-efficient for compression.
The mesh is partitioned into spatially contiguous blocks using METIS, the data in each block
is mean-centered and projected onto the MHB, and the resulting spectra are processed through

 3

a compression chain modeled after ZFP, including block floating point conversion, thresholding,
truncation, nega-binary conversion, and arithmetic range encoding.
 The performance of MFZ was evaluated on three scientific computing datasets: a 2D jet flame
ignition problem on a nonuniform mesh of 2,189 points (450 time steps), a 2D flat plate turbulent
flow on a nonuniform grid of 9,956 points (603 time steps), and a 3D neuron membrane transport
problem on a non-Cartesian domain of 116,543 points (600 time steps). Performance was measured
using time-averaged relative Frobenius norm error and peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR), and
compared against ZFP and SZ (applied to both original and spatially shuffled data). On the ignition
dataset, MFZ performs comparably to ZFP and SZ on the original data layout, and maintains its
performance under spatial shuffling whereas ZFP and SZ degrade significantly, demonstrating that
MFZ exploits genuine geometric spatial correlation rather than relying on memory layout. On the
3D neuron transport dataset, MFZ outperforms ZFP both with and without shuffling, illustrating
a case where the memory layout of PDE data loses its spatial correlation and becomes much less
compressible for standard compressors but not for MFZ. This resulted in a state-of-the-art spatial
data compression technique for nonuniform data, with all code, datasets, and meshes released as
an open-source repository [J2].

2.2 Temporal Data Compression via Randomized Interpolative Decomposition
Low-rank approximation is a fundamental task in statistics, machine learning, and computational
science to reduce the data size and obtain an optimal approximation of the data. Traditionally,
the problem is solved using singular value decomposition (SVD)-based methods such as principal
component analysis (PCA), proper orthogonal decomposition (POD), and dynamic mode decom-
position (DMD). However, it can be challenging for them to handle high-dimensional data due to
their expensive computational cost, high memory requirement, and poor parallel scalability. In
addition, we are unable to preserve the columns/rows of the original matrix via SVD, making it
less attractive than its alternative method, such as column interpolative decomposition (ID). The
idea of column ID is to approximate the data matrix as a product of a subset of its columns and a
coefficient matrix. Though ID is generally less accurate than SVD, the decomposition results are
more easily interpreted since it preserves a subset of the original data matrix as a basis for approx-
imation. As a result, one can identify important bases that preserve properties such as sparsity
and nonnegativity of the original data and conserve constraints from the original data in physics
applications.
 During Year 2 (Y2) of the project, we developed a randomized interpolative decomposition (ID)
approach to perform temporal compression in a single pass over the large-scale simulation data. We
first implement a streaming ridge leverage score-based column subset selection (CSS) method to
pick the column basis for decomposition. Since the column basis keeps evolving when more data is
observed, we keep updating the coefficient matrix for data reconstruction accordingly. In particular,
we implement different algorithms to obtain the least-squares coefficient solution that minimizes the
Frobenius error between the original and reconstructed matrices. We utilize randomized projection
to get the matrix sketch and reduce the computational time and RAM usage for computing the
coefficient matrix. To determine the best coefficient among four different solutions, we reuse the
matrix sketch to estimate the Frobenius error via the non-adaptive Hutch++ method and use the
coefficient with the best reconstruction accuracy.
 We have developed a Python software package to implement the single-pass randomized ID
framework. The accuracy and efficacy of the proposed method have been verified on three datasets:
(1) turbulent channel flow; (2) 2D ignition problem; and (3) The NSTX Gas Puff Image (GPI). We
also compared the proposed method with standard methods such as truncated SVD and ID based

on column-pivoted QR (CPQR), which requires more than one pass of the data.
 During Year 3 (Y3) of the project, accomplishments in temporal compression include extending
the single-pass randomized interpolative decomposition technique with new constraints for a variety
of use cases. A key advancement was preserving the positivity of the data matrix reconstruction
to maintain physical interpretability, such as for species concentration or density. Using streaming
column subset selection methods and column-pivoted QR-based approaches, we achieved positive
reconstructions via convex optimization with accuracy comparable to unconstrained reconstruc-
tions. We also introduced column-wise L∞ -norm minimization using randomized row sketches to
improve pointwise accuracy, and developed a residual error-based compression method where the
user specifies a Frobenius error target, and the algorithm derives the rank and columns in one pass.
This method achieves accuracy comparable to rank-based compression with negligible overhead,
further broadening the applicability of our temporal compression tools.

2.3 Spatio-Temporal Data Compression via Nonlinear Dimensionality Reduc-
 tion
While techniques based on low-rank approximations are effective in reducing the size of data asso-
ciated with many PDE systems, they are less effective for transport-dominated phenomena where
a linear low-rank structure is absent. One family of approaches that mitigates this shortcoming,
namely kernel PCA, consists of a nonlinear mapping followed by low-rank approximation. Such
techniques can be effective if a good nonlinear map (kernel) is known, but this requires an a priori
understanding of the data at hand, which is difficult to establish in general. In this project, we uti-
lize autoencoder (AE) neural networks to learn low-dimensional structures from high-dimensional
data. When dealing with large data, multiple convolutional layers are used to reduce the size of
the data so a multi-layer AE architecture can be effectively applied. Without such layers, both
training data requirements and training cost will be prohibitive.
 The standard convolution operator that has become so ubiquitous in deep learning (DL) has
been shown time and again to be a powerful tool. This operator relies on the key assumption that
the input data is structured, e.g., images. However, many PDE solvers rely on unstructured grid
data, but they still admit local interactions. The DL convolution was originally proposed to take
advantage of this locality in images, and is part of why it is so effective. In our case, the input
data is an unstructured mesh used in large-scale simulations, where adjacent nodes are likely to be
correlated in their features. Thus, we aimed at extending this operator to arbitrary point cloud
inputs, while maintaining the existing performance as much as possible.
 The standard convolution used in DL is a discrete variation of the classical integral operation,
which acts on two input functions. In the DL setting, we think of one of these inputs as the signal
being processed, and the other as a learnable kernel. The approach our method takes is to return
to this integral definition, where we can operate on arbitrary points. We approximate the kernel
function as a small learnable network, and then compute the convolution with the input data via
an appropriate quadrature rule. The kernel function maps from points, which allows it to handle
any unstructured input with associated coordinates.
 From here, we replace the standard convolutions in existing neural network architectures with
this new operator. This allows us to benefit from the huge body of knowledge surrounding these
engineering tasks and apply it to new forms of data. The operator, which we call QuadConv,
is a discrete approximation of the continuous convolution integral through quadrature [J1]. This
allows us to utilize any underlying mesh as a domain for QuadConv, and map the output of the
neural layer to any other set of points. This opens the doors for non-uniform meshes and non-
Cartesian domains to be used as data sets for any classical DL technique, including our non-linear

dimensionality reduction via an AE. At CoDA 2023, this work was recognized with an Honorable
Mention Poster Award.
 The formulation of the quadrature was improved to admit a more accurate approximation to
the continuous integral. Furthermore, new pooling operators that are also compatible with non-
Cartesian domains and non-uniform meshes were developed to allow entire AE architectures to
operate on data domains.
 We also explored applying QuadConv in an online setting for compression. In particular, we
trained a QuadConv-equipped autoencoder to compress three-dimensional turbulent flow data di-
rectly alongside the simulation producing the data. This work appeared as a validation of an in
situ framework for coupling simulation and machine learning.
 During Y3, the team developed a novel in situ training protocol for implicit neural representa-
tion (INR)-based neural compressors of scientific simulation data, targeting the challenging scenario
where simulation snapshots arrive sequentially and cannot all be stored due to memory constraints
[J4]. The key difficulty in this setting is catastrophic forgetting, i.e., the degradation of reconstruc-
tion quality for earlier snapshots as the network adapts to newer ones. To address this, a limited
memory buffer is maintained consisting of two parts: a small queue of full spatial snapshots and a
larger queue of dimensionality-reduced, or sketched, snapshots. At each training step, mini-batches
from both buffers are used to compute a composite loss that combines the standard reconstruction
loss on the current snapshot with a sketch-based regularization term weighted over past snapshots.
Crucially, the sketched data requires far less storage than the full snapshots, allowing the buffer to
span a much longer time history. The Fast Johnson-Lindenstrauss Transform (FJLT) is employed
as the primary sketching operator, and its use as a regularizer is theoretically motivated by a
Johnson-Lindenstrauss-type result showing that minimizing the sketched loss approximately min-
imizes the true full-data loss, with the approximation factor controlled by the sketch size relative
to the intrinsic manifold dimension of the network output.
 The method is realized using an INR-based hypernetwork architecture in which a compact
hypernetwork maps from time coordinates to the parameters of a target INR that reconstructs
the PDE solution field on the spatial mesh. This design is mesh-agnostic, requiring only node
coordinates without connectivity information, and is therefore applicable to unstructured and non-
Cartesian geometries. The approach was evaluated on three representative datasets: a 2D ignition
problem on a uniform grid, a 3D diffusion process on an unstructured branching neuron geometry,
and a 3D turbulent channel flow on a non-uniform grid. Across all datasets and metrics (relative
Frobenius error and PSNR), the in situ FJLT-based method closely matches the performance of
the equivalent offline baseline, with reconstruction errors on the order of a few percent. The imple-
mentation is available as an open-source repository, and the associated paper has been submitted
for journal review [J4].
