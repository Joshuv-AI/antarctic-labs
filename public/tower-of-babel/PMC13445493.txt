Validation of the VasCog-2-WSO VCID Criteria in a Dementia-Free and Stroke-Free Community Cohort
Authors: Yu-Ruei Lin, Wei-Ju Lee, Pei-Lin Lee, Ching-Po Lin, Pei-Ning Wang, Liang-Kung Chen, Chih-Ping Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesVascular cognitive impairment and dementia (VCID), a major contributor of dementia, remains underrecognized, owing to the absence of universally accepted diagnostic criteria. The VasCog Society and WSO recently released updated criteria (VasCog-2–WSO); however, their applicability is unknown yet. We aimed to evaluate these criteria in a dementia-free and stroke-free community cohort and determine their associations with vascular burden and long-term mortality.MethodsWe conducted a community-based cohort study using data from the I-Lan Longitudinal Aging Study. Adults aged 50 years or older without prior stroke or dementia underwent standardized brain MRI and comprehensive neuropsychological assessment. VCID was classified according to the VasCog-2–WSO criteria using a neuroimaging-first approach. Participants were categorized as non-VCID, preclinical VCID (with or without objective cognitive impairment), or vascular mild cognitive impairment (vaMCI). Baseline 10-year Framingham cardiovascular disease risk was assessed. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality over a mean follow-up of 9.4 years, analyzed using multivariable Cox proportional hazards and Poisson regression models.ResultsA total of 1,236 participants (62.7 ± 8.8 years; 52.9% female) were included. Neuroimaging evidence of cerebrovascular disease was present in 19.6% of participants; 19.2% met criteria for preclinical VCID and 0.4% for vaMCI. Compared with non-VCID participants, neuroimaging-positive groups had worse cognitive performance and higher 10-year cardiovascular risk. Mortality increased across the VCID spectrum (7.4 [95% CI 5.7–9.4], 24.9 [16.0–37.1], 27.4 [18.2–39.6], and 137.9 [37.6–353.2] per 1,000 person-years). In adjusted Cox models, preclinical VCID was associated with a 1.5–1.7-fold higher mortality risk, and vaMCI with a hazard ratio of 7.2 (95% CI 2.4–21.1), with a significant graded association across the spectrum (p for trend <0.001).DiscussionThe VasCog-2–WSO criteria identify a spectrum of MRI-defined vascular cognitive vulnerability associated with increased cardiovascular risk and mortality, with excess mortality risk detectable at a neuroimaging-defined preclinical stage before overt cognitive impairment. These findings support their utility for early detection and risk stratification in community settings. A key limitation is the inability to assess mixed etiologies due to the absence of genetic data and Alzheimer disease biomarkers, which limits the ability to thoroughly evaluate the VasCog-2–WSO VCID diagnostic framework.

Introduction

Extensive studies show that vascular dementia (VaD) is the second most common cause of dementia, accounting for up to 30% of cases, and cerebrovascular pathology contributes to cognitive decline in a substantial proportion of individuals with dementia of all kinds.1-4 Given global population aging and the modifiable nature of vascular risk factors, early identification of VaD at preclinical or earlier stage in the community settings has become a critical public health priority.5-8

Since the introduction of National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke and Association Internationale pour la Recherche et l’Enseignement en Neurosciences (NINDS-AIREN) criteria in 1993,9 multiple professional societies have sought to standardize the diagnosis of vascular cognitive disorders.2,10-13 These works broadened the concept of VaD into a spectrum of vascular cognitive impairment. However, persistent challenges remain, including inconsistent terminology, variability in requirements of cognitive domains assessment, limited harmonization of subjective and objective criteria, and insufficient incorporation of advances in neuroimaging and biomarkers of cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD). These limitations have hindered comparability across studies and the translation of research findings into clinical applications, particular the community-based prevention strategies.

To address these gaps, the International Society for Vascular Behavioural and Cognitive Disorders (VasCog Society), in collaboration with the World Stroke Organization, recently proposed revised criteria for vascular cognitive impairment and dementia (VCID) (VasCog-2–WSO criteria).5 The revised framework introduces several important advances. First, it establishes unified terminology across the VCID spectrum, explicitly defining preclinical VCID, vascular mild cognitive impairment (vaMCI), and VaD. Second, the revised VCID framework maintains the core VasCog-1 criteria,10 requiring both “objective” evidence of cognitive impairment and “subjective” cognitive or functional concerns to meet the criteria of vaMCI and VaD, strengthening diagnostic rigor. Third, rather than privileging specific cognitive domains, the criteria acknowledge that vascular brain injury may produce heterogeneous, multidomain patterns of impairment. Finally, the VasCog-2–WSO criteria integrate contemporary and standardized neuroimaging markers, particularly comprehensive MRI assessment of CSVD,14 to substantiate the vascular etiology. Despite these advances, timely evaluation of the VasCog-2–WSO criteria in preclinical, community-based populations remains limited. It remains uncertain whether the revised criteria effectively identify individuals at early stages of VCID and discriminates gradients in vascular burden and long-term clinical outcomes. We therefore sought to validate the VasCog-2–WSO criteria in a community-dwelling, dementia-free and stroke-free cohort by examining their screening yield, phenotypic differentiation across the defined VCID spectrum, associations with cardiovascular risk burden, and prognostic relevance for all-cause mortality.

Methods

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Participant Consents

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University, Taipei, Taiwan (No. YM104121F-5), according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki of 1975. Written informed consent was obtained from all study participants.

Study Population

Data for this study were derived from the I-Lan Longitudinal Aging Study (ILAS), an ongoing community-based cohort recruiting individuals aged ≥50 years designed to identify determinants of healthy aging.15 The present analyses used the baseline (wave 1) data set collected between January 2011 and July 2014, including comprehensive demographic, medical history, anthropometric, neuropsychological, and neuroimaging assessments. Participants with a history of dementia, stroke, or other major neurologic disorders were excluded from the present analysis. A total of 1,839 eligible individuals were initially included (eFigure 1). Of these, 558 had incomplete MRI sequences and 44 had inadequate image quality and were therefore excluded, as application of the VasCog-2–WSO neuroimaging criteria for VCID was not feasible. One participant who met the VasCog-2–WSO criteria for dementia at baseline was also excluded, as the present study focused specifically on preclinical and early-stage vascular cognitive impairment in a community-dwelling population. The final analytic sample comprised 1,236 dementia-free and stroke-free participants (eFigure 1).

Subjective Cognitive Concerns

Subjective cognitive concerns were determined based on participant report, informant report, or clinician judgment, consistent with VasCog-2–WSO criteria using the Functional Assessment Staging Test (FAST) scale.16 FAST stages 2–3 were operationalized as subjective cognitive concerns relative to prior baseline, with preserved independence in basic activities of daily living and no evidence of functional dependence.

VasCog-2–WSO–Defined VCID Spectrum Groups: Non-VCID, Preclinical VCID, vaMCI, and VaD

VCID classification followed the VasCog-2–WSO criteria and included preclinical VCID, vaMCI, and VaD. All categories required fulfillment of the VasCog-2–WSO neuroimaging criteria for vascular etiology. In contrast to the original VasCog-2–WSO algorithm, which begins with identification of cognitive impairment (clinical criteria) followed by determination of vascular etiology (neuroimaging criteria), we required MRI-confirmed vascular brain injury, the neuroimaging criteria, as the entry criterion. Participants meeting neuroimaging criteria were subsequently classified according to objective and subjective cognitive performance and functional status.

As the first step in classification, participants who did not fulfill the VasCog-2–WSO neuroimaging criteria for cerebrovascular disease were assigned to the non-VCID group. VaMCI was defined as the presence of (1) neuroimaging evidence of cerebrovascular disease meeting VasCog-2–WSO criteria and (2) clinical criteria, including subjective cognitive concerns (reported by the participant or their family) and objective cognitive impairment in at least 1 neuropsychological test. In accordance with the VasCog-2–WSO criteria, objective impairment was operationalized as performance 1–2 SDs below age-adjusted, sex-adjusted, and education-adjusted normative values. VaD was defined, consistent with VasCog-2–WSO criteria, as neuroimaging evidence of vascular etiology accompanied by objective cognitive impairment of ≥2 SDs below normative values in 1 or more neuropsychological test and functional dependence in daily activities. Preclinical VCID was defined as significant neuroimaging evidence of cerebrovascular disease fulfilling VasCog-2–WSO criteria in the absence of clinical criteria for vaMCI or VaD. To further characterize early vascular-related cognitive vulnerability, we stratified preclinical VCID into 2 subgroups:preclinical VCID without objective cognitive impairment andpreclinical VCID with objective cognitive impairment (at least 1 cognitive test's score between 1 and 2 SDs below normative values but without subjective cognitive concerns).

Baseline 10-Year Framingham Cardiovascular Disease Risk

To evaluate the clinical validity of the VasCog-2–WSO–defined VCID classification, we examined its associations with baseline cardiovascular risk burden and longitudinal all-cause mortality. Baseline cardiovascular risk burden was quantified using the 10-year Framingham cardiovascular disease risk score, which estimates the probability of incident cardiovascular events based on established risk factors, including age, sex, systolic blood pressure, smoking status, diabetes, and lipid profile.17

All-Cause Mortality

Mortality status was ascertained through structured telephone follow-up conducted every 3 months and supplemented by verification through the hospital health information system. For the present analysis, mortality data through April 2022 were included. Participants alive at the last follow-up were censored in April 2022, whereas for deceased participants, the date of death was recorded. Time-to-event was calculated from the date of baseline assessment to the date of death or censoring. The mean follow-up duration was 9.4 ± 0.7 years.

Statistical Analysis

Screening Yield of VasCog-2–WSO Classification

The screening yield of the VasCog-2–WSO classification was calculated as the proportion of participants meeting criteria for preclinical VCID and vaMCI among the total eligible cohort. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the distribution of participants across groups.

Phenotypic Characterization Across the VCID Spectrum

Baseline demographic, cognitive, and neuroimaging characteristics were summarized as mean ± SD for continuous variables and number (percentage) for categorical variables. Given violation of normality assumptions and the small sample size of the overt vaMCI group (n = 5), Kruskal–Wallis tests were used for between-group comparisons of continuous variables, followed by Dunn tests with Bonferroni correction for post hoc pairwise comparisons. Categorical variables were compared using χ2 tests with Monte Carlo simulation (10,000 permutations). For cognitive test comparisons across the 4 VasCog-2–WSO–defined groups, rank-based analysis of covariance models adjusting for age and education were applied to raw cognitive scores, with Bonferroni-corrected post hoc tests. Differences in the prevalence of objective cognitive impairment were examined using logistic regression models adjusting for age and education. Likelihood ratio tests were used to evaluate overall group effects. When complete or quasicomplete separation occurred, penalized logistic regression with L1 regularization was applied.

Association With Cardiovascular Risk Burden

Between-group differences in baseline 10-year Framingham cardiovascular disease risk were examined using rank-based analysis of variance. Post hoc comparisons were adjusted using Bonferroni correction.

Prognostic Relevance for All-Cause Mortality

Incidence rates of all-cause mortality were calculated as events per 1,000 person-years. Poisson regression models with log person-years as an offset were used to estimate incidence rate ratios and 95% CIs, adjusting for age and sex. Firth's penalized Cox proportional hazards regression models (penalizer = 0.1) were constructed to evaluate the association between VasCog-2–WSO-defined VCID groups and all-cause mortality, using the non-VCID group as the reference. In the present study, Poisson models were used to estimate incidence rate ratios, whereas Cox models were applied to account for time-to-event and censoring. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. Three sequential models were specified: model 1 adjusted for age and sex; model 2 additionally adjusted for hypertension; and model 3 further adjusted for smoking status. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs were reported. To assess a graded association across the VCID spectrum, we further performed a trend test by modeling VCID group as an ordinal variable (0 = non-VCID, 1 = preclinical VCID without objective cognitive impairment, 2 = preclinical VCID with objective cognitive impairment, and 3 = overt vaMCI) in Cox proportional hazards models. The HR per 1-step increase and the corresponding Wald test p-value were reported as the p value for trend. Given the small sample size of the vaMCI group (n = 5), sensitivity analyses excluding this group were conducted.

All statistical tests were 2-sided, and a p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Bonferroni correction was applied to account for multiple comparisons. Analyses were performed using Python (version 3.12).

Data Availability

The data used in this study were derived from the ongoing ILAS cohort. The anonymized data set is not publicly available but is available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Screening Yield of the VasCog-2-WSO Criteria in a Community-Based Dementia-Free and Stroke-Free Cohort

The distribution of VasCog-2–WSO–defined groups is presented in Figure 1A and eFigure 1. Participants were categorized into 4 groups. Application of the VasCog-2–WSO criteria identified neuroimaging evidence of cerebrovascular disease in 242 participants (19.6%), while 994 participants (80.4%) showed no neuroimaging evidence and were classified as group 1 (non-VCID). Regarding vascular risk factors, hypertension prevalence differed markedly across groups. The non-VCID group exhibited a substantially lower prevalence compared with the VCID groups, and post hoc analyses confirmed significant differences relative to both preclinical VCID groups. Dyslipidemia was less frequent in the non-VCID group compared with the preclinical VCID group with objective cognitive impairment, but not with the preclinical VCID group without objective impairment or the overt vaMCI group. By contrast, diabetes prevalence did not differ significantly across groups. Cigarette smoking status also varied across groups, with the overt vaMCI group demonstrating the highest proportion of current smokers.

Among the 3 VCID spectrum groups which were classified as neuroimaging-positive according to the VasCog-2–WSO criteria, severe WMHs were highly prevalent, reaching 100% in the overt vaMCI group. Multiple lacunes (≥2) were observed in approximately 21.5%–22.4% of the 2 preclinical VCID groups and in 100% of the overt vaMCI group. Notably, overt vaMCI exhibited the greatest CSVD burden among the VCID groups, despite not being significantly older or having a higher prevalence of major vascular risk factors compared with the preclinical VCID groups. No participants in any VCID groups had neuroimaging evidence of territorial infarct or intracerebral hemorrhage.

Cognitive Performance Across VCID Spectrum Groups

Objective cognitive performance differed significantly across the VasCog-2–WSO–defined groups (Table 2). A significant difference was observed across the spectrum, with the non-VCID group demonstrating the best performance, followed by preclinical VCID without objective impairment, preclinical VCID with objective impairment, and overt vaMCI. For global cognition, MMSE scores significantly differed among groups, with the overt vaMCI group exhibiting markedly lower scores than all other groups. Similar results were observed in attention and working memory (Backward Digit Test), executive function and processing speed (Verbal Fluency Test [VFT]), executive function (Clock Drawing Test), learning and memory (Chinese Version Verbal Learning Test), language (Boston Naming Test), and perceptual-motor ability (visuospatial construction, Taylor Complex Figure Copy Test [TCF]).

When examining domain-specific impairment rates—defined as performance on at least 1 cognitive test falling between 1 and 2 SDs below age-adjusted, sex-adjusted, and education-adjusted normative data—significant differences were also observed across groups in all domains. By definition, no domain impairment was present after age and education factors taking into consideration in the preclinical VCID group without objective cognitive impairment. Notably, a proportion of participants in the neuroimaging-negative (non-VCID) group met criteria for objective impairment despite lacking imaging evidence of cerebrovascular disease. Across the neuroimaging-positive spectrum, the overt vaMCI group tended to have a higher prevalence of impairment than the preclinical VCID with cognitive impairment group. Domains including attention and working memory, executive function, and learning and memory were particularly affected: attention and working memory impairment was observed in 33.1% of the preclinical VCID with objective impairment group and 60.0% of the overt vaMCI group; executive impairment was present in 34.7% and 80.0%, respectively; and learning and memory impairment in 39.7% and 60.0%. Language impairment was much less frequent but still differed significantly across groups.

Subjective cognitive decline, operationalized using the FAST, was significantly more frequently only in the overt vaMCI group (p < 0.001), consistent with its diagnostic requirement of concurrent subjective and objective decline.

When participants were further stratified into 6 subgroups according to both neuroimaging status and cognitive impairment—including 3 cognition-based subgroups within the neuroimaging-negative (non-VCID) group (eTable 1), the observed group differences were limited to trend-level patterns and did not reach statistical significance in post hoc comparisons. Overall, across parallel cognitive classifications, neuroimaging-positive groups were generally older, had lower educational attainment, lower raw cognitive scores, and higher domain-specific impairment prevalence than their neuroimaging-negative counterparts. Among participants meeting objective cognitive impairment criteria, neuroimaging-negative individuals showed higher raw cognitive performance and lower impairment prevalence than neuroimaging-positive individuals with comparable clinical classifications. Specifically, neuroimaging-positive participants with objective cognitive impairment (group 3: preclinical VCID with objective cognitive impairment) exhibited lower neuropsychological test scores and higher prevalence of impairments in all domains compared with neuroimaging-negative participants with objective cognitive impairment (group 1-B). A similar pattern was explored among participants meeting both objective and subjective cognitive impairment criteria. Although group 1-C (neuroimaging-negative with both objective and subjective impairment criteria) and group 4 (vaMCI) shared the same clinical definition, group 4 demonstrated numerically lower cognitive test scores and higher impairment prevalence, particularly in executive and processing speed domain assessed by VFT (12.5% vs 40%) and perceptual-motor ability (visuospatial construction) domain assessed by TCF (12.5% vs 40%). However, these differences did not reach statistical significance in post hoc comparisons.

Outcome Validation: Cardiovascular Disease Risk Burden Across VCID Spectrum Groups

To evaluate whether the VasCog-2–WSO criteria identify individuals with a higher cardiovascular risk burden, we examined the estimated 10-year Framingham cardiovascular disease risk across VCID spectrum groups (Figure 1B). The 10-year Framingham cardiovascular disease risk differed significantly across VasCog-2–WSO–defined groups. Participants in the non-VCID group exhibited a substantially lower estimated 10-year cardiovascular risk (17.3% ± 14.9%) compared with VCID groups, including preclinical VCID without objective cognitive impairment (29.6% ± 21.4%), preclinical VCID with objective cognitive impairment (30.4% ± 22.6%), and overt vaMCI (43.7% ± 14.2%). Post hoc analyses confirmed significant differences between the non-VCID group and each of the 3 neuroimaging-positive groups. Although the overt vaMCI group showed the numerically highest estimated risk, no statistically significant differences were observed among the 3 neuroimaging-positive VCID groups.

Outcome Validation: All-Cause Mortality Across VCID Spectrum Groups

To determine whether the VasCog-2–WSO criteria identify individuals at increased risk of adverse clinical outcomes, we evaluated all-cause mortality across VCID spectrum groups (Figure 1, C and D; Tables 3 and 4). Over a mean follow-up of 9 years, 120 deaths occurred. Mortality incidence rates showed significant differences across the VCID spectrum: 7.4 per 1,000 person-years in the non-VCID group, 24.9 per 1,000 person-years in preclinical VCID without objective cognitive impairment, 27.4 per 1,000 person-years in preclinical VCID with objective cognitive impairment, and 137.9 per 1,000 person-years in overt vaMCI. In Cox proportional hazards models, all VCID groups demonstrated significantly higher mortality risk compared with the non-VCID group (Table 3). After adjustment for age and sex, HRs were 1.6 for preclinical VCID without objective cognitive impairment, 1.7 for preclinical VCID with objective cognitive impairment, and 8.5 for overt vaMCI. Further adjustment for hypertension and smoking status resulted in only modest attenuation of effect estimates, indicating that excess mortality risk was not fully explained by vascular risk factors that were more frequently in VCID groups. Modeling VCID spectrum group as an ordinal variable demonstrated a significant dose-response relationship with mortality (HR per 1-spectrum increase = 1.4, 95% CI 1.2–1.7; p for trend <0.001). Consistent findings were observed in Poisson regression models (Table 4), confirming higher mortality rates across all neuroimaging-positive VCID groups. Given the small number of overt vaMCI cases, a sensitivity analysis excluding group 4 (vaMCI) was conducted (eTable 2). Associations between preclinical VCID groups and mortality remained statistically significant across all models, with minimal changes in HRs (<2%), indicating that the observed mortality gradient was not disproportionately driven by the overt vaMCI subgroup.

Discussion

In this community-based cohort of 1,236 dementia-free and stroke-free individuals, application of the VasCog-2–WSO criteria identified a low prevalence of vascular cognitive impairment (vaMCI; 0.4%) and a substantially larger subgroup with preclinical VCID (19.2%). Compared with the non-VCID group, individuals with preclinical VCID exhibited worse performance across age-adjusted, sex-adjusted, and education-adjusted cognitive domains; similar trends were observed in the vaMCI group but did not reach statistical significance, likely due to limited sample size. Despite the relatively low prevalence of VCID in this cohort, the VasCog-2–WSO spectrum demonstrated discriminative validity. Both preclinical VCID and vaMCI were associated with higher cardiovascular risk burden. Over a mean follow-up of 9 years, mortality risk differed across stratified groups, with preclinical VCID conferring a 1.5–1.7-fold higher risk and vaMCI associated with a markedly elevated risk (HR 7.2). Together, these findings indicate that the VasCog-2–WSO framework enables early identification of individuals with subclinical vascular brain injury who are at increased risk for adverse vascular and mortality outcomes.

Our stepwise classification, which used MRI-defined cerebrovascular disease as the entry criterion, showed that the VasCog-2–WSO neuroimaging criteria alone—based on STRIVE-2 standards—identified individuals with poorer cognitive performance, higher cardiovascular risk burden, and increased mortality, even without overt objective or subjective cognitive impairment. These findings highlight the central role of MRI-defined vascular brain injury in the VCID construct and support a neuroimaging-first approach for risk stratification in community-dwelling older adults. MRI-based screening of covert cerebrovascular disease may therefore provide an opportunity for earlier preventive intervention before clinically apparent cognitive decline develops. However, individuals identified through this imaging-first approach were significantly older, consistent with the age-dependent accumulation of vascular brain injury. An important next step is to identify younger at-risk individuals before conventional MRI markers become evident.

This study validates the VasCog-2–WSO criteria in a community-dwelling population free of dementia and stroke. Prior validation efforts have primarily been conducted in populations at later clinical stages and under earlier diagnostic frameworks. For example, Sachdev et al.18 evaluated the original Vascular Behavioral and Cognitive Disorders (VASCOG) criteria in patients with ischemic stroke or TIA and demonstrated comparable predictive validity for mortality relative to Disgnostic and Satistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) and Vascular Impairment of Cognition Classification Consensus Study (VICCCS) criteria over 10 years. While that study established the prognostic utility of VASCOG in a secondary prevention setting, the cohort consisted exclusively of individuals with established cerebrovascular events, limiting generalizability to asymptomatic or preclinical populations. Earlier population-based work from the Canadian Study of Health and Aging, using Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Third Edition, Revised (DSM-III-R) and related criteria, showed that mortality risk among individuals with vascular cognitive impairment—even without dementia—was elevated and comparable to that of Alzheimer disease, underscoring that vascular burden may influence survival independent of dementia severity.19,20 By contrast, our study applied the contemporary VasCog-2–WSO criteria using a neuroimaging-first strategy in a dementia-free and stroke-free community cohort. Importantly, we demonstrate that even preclinical VCID—defined by MRI-confirmed cerebrovascular disease without overt cognitive impairment—is associated with increased long-term mortality, indicating that such prognostic risk is detectable at an earlier, imaging-defined stage before measurable cognitive decline becomes apparent. These findings are consistent with prior studies focusing on neuroimaging-defined cerebrovascular disease. For example, Ghaznawi et al.21 reported mortality HRs of 2.3 (age-adjusted and sex-adjusted) and 1.7 (further adjusted for vascular risk factors) among participants with severe WMH (confluent WMH) in a cohort of 999 individuals, 18.0% of whom had known infarction, without incorporating cognitive performance into their classification. Meta-analytic evidence has further validated that both higher WMH burden (HR 1.9, 95% CI 1.5–2.3) and the presence of lacunes (HR 1.7, 95% CI 1.4–2.1) are independently associated with increased mortality risk in population-based samples.22 Collectively, these findings support the notion that neuroimaging-defined CSVD—particularly WMH and lacunes—captures a clinically meaningful vascular burden sufficient to identify individuals at increased risk of mortality. Notably, in our cohort, similar HRs were observed between preclinical VCID with and without objective cognitive impairment. This suggests that, at the preclinical stage, neuroimaging evidence of cerebrovascular disease, mainly the CSVD—rather than mild cognitive impairment per se—may be the principal determinant of mortality risk, reinforcing the value of an imaging-based framework for early risk stratification.

This study benefits from comprehensive MRI and neuropsychological assessments enabling rigorous application of the VasCog-2–WSO criteria, along with standardized vascular risk data and longitudinal mortality follow-up to assess prognostic validity. The community-based, dementia-free and stroke-free design allowed evaluation at the earliest asymptomatic stage and enhances generalizability.

The relatively low prevalence of vaMCI observed in our cohort likely reflects several cohort-specific factors. First, the overall younger age distribution (mean age 62.7 years) is an important contributor, as the clinical expression of vascular cognitive impairment is strongly age-dependent and tends to increase substantially in older populations. Second, our study population consisted of community-dwelling individuals without prior stroke or dementia, in contrast to many previous studies enriched for clinical populations with overt cerebrovascular events, which inherently exhibit higher rates of cognitive impairment. Third, population characteristics such as vascular risk profiles, educational attainment, and cognitive reserve may further modulate the relationship between underlying cerebrovascular pathology and its clinical manifestation. These considerations highlight that, in community-based cohorts, a substantial proportion of individuals may harbor neuroimaging-defined CSVD in the absence of overt cognitive symptoms, representing a preclinical stage of vascular brain injury. This underscores the importance of incorporating neuroimaging markers to identify early disease burden that is not captured by clinical or risk-based assessments alone.

Several limitations merit consideration. First, cognitive domains were represented by a limited number of tests, which may reduce sensitivity to subtle deficits. Although age-adjusted and education-adjusted norms were applied, residual confounding by educational background cannot be excluded. Nevertheless, the graded pattern across groups argues against a purely measurement-related or residual confounding explanation. Second, although the VasCog-2–WSO framework emphasizes evaluation of alternative or mixed etiologies, including hereditary and neurodegenerative contributions, we did not incorporate genetic information and Alzheimer disease biomarkers. The absence of amyloid or tau measures may have resulted in misclassification of mixed pathology. However, our imaging-first approach required MRI-confirmed cerebrovascular disease, and the graded associations with vascular risk burden and mortality support a predominantly vascular signal, mitigating—but not eliminating—this concern. Third, susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI) was not available to systematically assess cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA). Although the updated Boston criteria incorporate hemorrhagic markers detectable on SWI, validated application of CAA criteria in dementia-free and stroke-free community cohorts remains limited, and reported prevalence in such populations is low.23-25 Thus, undetected CAA is unlikely to substantially alter the overall findings. Fourth, dementia incidence was not analyzed as an outcome. Although follow-up visits included cognitive reassessment, currently, the proportion with repeat testing in our cohort was limited (<50.0%), and the small number of vaMCI cases precluded reliable between-group progression analyses. Future follow-up will be necessary to determine long-term cognitive trajectories. Fifth, detailed information on medication adherence was not systematically collected at baseline or during follow-up. Therefore, we were unable to directly assess the impact of treatment adherence on vascular risk control and its potential influence on study outcomes. Finally, the cohort consisted of East Asian community-dwelling older adults, many from rural areas with relatively lower educational attainment, which may limit generalizability to other ethnic or sociodemographic populations.

In conclusion, this study provides timely validation of the VasCog-2–WSO criteria in a community setting. The inclusion of preclinical VCID enabled identification of individuals with MRI-defined cerebrovascular disease who were already at increased cardiovascular and mortality risk despite preserved cognition. These data support the application of the VasCog-2–WSO framework for early detection and risk stratification of vascular cognitive vulnerability.
