Relational depth of transformation semigroups and their ideals
N. Ruškuc, Z. Yayi

Abstract
. We introduce the concept of relational depth of a finite semigroup S S whose 𝒥 \mathcal{J} -classes form a chain. It captures how far down in the ideal structure one is obliged to go in order to define the semigroup by generators and defining relations. We determine the exact value for the relational depth of an arbitrary ideal in the full transformation monoid, symmetric inverse monoid and in the partial transformation monoid.

20M05, 20M20

Introduction

Generating sets and defining relations (presentations) for classical transformation semigroups – such as the partial transformation semigroup
\mathcal{PT}_{n}
, the full transformation semigroup
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, and the symmetric inverse semigroup
\mathcal{I}_{n}
– have over time received a lot of attention.
For example, it is known that each of them is generated by the symmetric group (i.e. its group of units) together with one or two transformations of rank
n-1
; see
[
9
, Section 3.1]
.
Presentations for these semigroups were first established in
[
15
,
1
,
17
]
respectively.
Another presentation for
\mathcal{I}_{n}
was given in
[
13
]
, and there is a nice exposition in
[
9
, Chapter 9]
.
All the known presentations for these semigroups have the property that every defining relation represents a transformation of rank
n
,
n-1
or
n-2
.

Ideals of these semigroups have likewise been of interest. It is known that the ideal
I_{m}
consisting of all transformations of rank
\leq m
is generated by transformations of rank precisely
m
; see
[
11
, Lemma 4]
. Nice presentations for these ideals are much harder to find. There is some work on the largest proper ideal
I_{n-1}

[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
.
These presentations are already quite large, but have the intriguing property that all the defining relations represent elements of ranks
n-1
and
n-2
only.

Very recent relevant work includes:

•

[
14
]
where Mitchell and Whyte investigate, and in certain cases determine, shortest (in terms of the number of defining relations) presentations for
\mathcal{T}_{n}
,
\mathcal{I}_{n}
and
\mathcal{PT}_{n}
.

•

[
2
]
where the authors discuss a theoretical underpinning for some of the East’s singular presentations for
[
3
,

,

,

,

,
8
]
.

The purpose of this paper is to investigate systematically how ‘deep’ inside the ideal structure of
I_{m}
one must go in order to define the semigroup
I_{m}
.

We now make this idea more formal. Throughout this paper, we will consider semigroups
S
whose
\mathcal{J}
-classes form a chain
J_{\epsilon}<J_{1}<\dots<J_{k}
. Here, typically,
\epsilon\in\{0,1\}
. The reason for this slightly strange indexing is that in our specific examples there there are natural indexings of
\mathcal{J}
-classes (by rank), but they, unfortunately, do not have the same starting point. The concept of
\mathcal{J}
-classes, and all the other necessary prerequisites will be introduced in Section
2
.

Consider such a semigroup
S
, and suppose that
\mathcal{P}=\langle A\ |\ \mathcal{R}\rangle
is a presentation for
S

Successively define the
depth
of various objects as follows:

•

a
\mathcal{J}
-class
J
:
\depth(J_{i})=k-i+1
;

•

an element
s\in S
:
\depth(s):=\depth(J_{i})
, where
s\in J_{i}
;

•

a word
w\in A^{+}
:

\depth(w)
is equal to the depth of the element represented by
w
;

•

a relation
u=v
in
R
:
\depth(u=v):=\depth(u)=\depth(v)
;

•

the presentation
\mathcal{P}
:

\depth(\mathcal{P})
is the maximal depth of a defining relation in
\mathcal{R}
;

•

the semigroup
S
:

\depth(S):=\min\{\depth(\mathcal{P})\colon\mathcal{P}\text{ is a presentation for }S\}.

We also refer to this final quantity as the
relational depth
of
S
.
The purpose of this article is to determine the
relational depth of transformation semigroups
\mathcal{PT}_{n},\mathcal{T}_{n},\mathcal{I}_{n}
, and their ideals.

Let
S\in\{\mathcal{PT}_{n},\mathcal{T}_{n},\mathcal{I}_{n}\}
.
It is known that the
\mathcal{J}
-classes of
S

are of the form

J_{r}=\{\alpha\in S\colon\rank(\alpha)=r\}\quad\text{for }\epsilon\leq r\leq n.

Here
\epsilon=0
when
S=\mathcal{PT}_{n}
or
S=\mathcal{I}_{n}
, and

\epsilon=1
when
S=\mathcal{T}_{n}
.
The ideals of
S
are

I_{m}=\{\alpha\in S\colon\rank(\alpha)\leq m\}\quad\text{for }\epsilon\leq m\leq n.

The
\mathcal{J}
-classes of
I_{m}
are precisely the
\mathcal{J}
-classes of
S
contained in
I_{m}
, i.e.

J_{\epsilon}<J_{1}<\dots<J_{m}
.
For
\epsilon\leq i\leq m
the depth of
J_{i}
in
I_{m}
is
m-i+1
.

Given a semigroup
S
, the depth of any presentation
\mathcal{P}
for
S
gives an upper bound for the depth of
S
. For example, in Aĭzenštat’s presentation
[
1
]
for
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, the generating set and the set of defining relations contain maps of ranks in
[n-2,n]
. Hence Aĭzenštat’s presentation has relational depth
3
. In East’s presentation
[
5
]
for the singular part of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, which is the ideal
I_{n-1}
in our notation, the ranks involved are
n-1
and
n-2
. The maps in the presentation are in
J_{n-1}\cup J_{n-2}
, therefore East’s presentation has relational depth
2
. Meakin’s presentation
[
13
]
for
\mathcal{I}_{n}
has depth
3
. The presentation for
\mathcal{PT}_{n}
given by Ganyushkin and Mazorchuk
[
9
]
has depth
3
. East’s
[
3
]
presentation for the ideal
I_{n-1}
of
\mathcal{I}_{n}
has depth
2
. The above give an upper bound for the relational depths of these semigroups, and we will see that they are indeed the depths later on.

We can now state our main result:

Main Theorem
.

Let
n\geq 3
, and let
S\in\{\mathcal{I}_{n},\mathcal{T}_{n},\mathcal{PT}_{n}\}
. Define
\epsilon=0
for
S\in\{\mathcal{PT}_{n},\mathcal{I}_{n}\}
and
\epsilon=1
when
S=\mathcal{T}_{n}
. For
m\in\{\epsilon,\dots,n\}
, let
I_{m}
be the ideal of all mappings in
S
of rank at most
m
.
Then

\displaystyle\depth(I_{m})=\begin{cases}m-{\rm max}(\epsilon,2m-n)+1\ &{\rm if}\ m<n\\
3\ &{\rm if}\ m=n.\end{cases}

Proof.

This will follow directly from Proposition
3.1
and Theorem
3.3
.
∎

This leads to a reformulation of our Main Theorem as Theorem

The rest of the paper is devoted to proving this latter result. First in Section

Proving that they are upper bounds is harder. In Section

\mathcal{I}_{n}
,
\mathcal{T}_{n}

9
.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we introduce some definitions and notation that will be used throughout.

For integers
m,n
, we will use the interval notation

[m,n]
for the set
\{m,m+1,\dots,n\}
, and

[n]:=[1,n]
.
We will consider the following three classical transformation semigroups on
[n]
:

•

the
partial transformation semigroup

\mathcal{PT}_{n}
consisting of all partial mappings;

•

the
symmetric inverse monoid

\mathcal{I}_{n}
consisting of of all partial bijections;

•

the
full transformation semigroup

\mathcal{T}_{n}
consisting of all (full) mappings.

The operation on each of them is composition of functions.
Clearly, all three semigroups are monoids, and
\mathcal{T}_{n}
and
\mathcal{I}_{n}
are submonoids of
\mathcal{PT}_{n}
.

For
\alpha\in\mathcal{PT}_{n}
, the
image
of
\alpha
is
\image(\alpha)=\{x\alpha\colon x\in[n]\}
. The
kernel
of
\alpha
is the equivalence relation
\ker(\alpha)=\{(x,y)\in\dom(\alpha)\times\dom(\alpha)\colon x\alpha=y\alpha\}
, whose equivalence classes are called kernel classes, and the
\ker(\alpha)
-classes partition
\dom(\alpha)
. The
rank
of
\alpha
is the size of
\image(\alpha)
, which is also equal to the number of
\ker(\alpha)
-classes.

In this paper, we will tend to write down a partial transformation
\alpha
by specifying its kernel classes and their images under
\alpha
, together with the complement of the domain. For example, we write
\alpha=\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&A_{2}&\dots&A_{m}&A\\
a_{1}&a_{2}&\dots&a_{m}&-\end{pmatrix}
to mean the partial transformation of rank
m
, with domain
A_{1}\cup\dots\cup A_{m}
,
and
x\alpha=a_{i}
for
all
x\in A_{i}
.

Green’s relations on a semigroup provide a standard framework for describing the ideal structure of a semigroup
S
.
Let us write
S^{1}
for the semigroup
S
with an identity element adjoined to it, unless
S
is already a monoid. The Green’s equivalences then are:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\{(x,y)\in S\times S\colon S^{1}x=S^{1}y\},\quad\mathcal{R}=\{(x,y)\in S\times S\colon xS^{1}=yS^{1}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}=\{(x,y)\in S\times S\colon S^{1}xS^{1}=S^{1}yS^{1}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{L}\circ\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{L},\quad\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{L}\cap\mathcal{R}.

In every finite semigroup
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{J}
holds
[
10
, Chapter 2]
.
There is a natural partial order on the set of all
\mathcal{J}
-classes, given by

J_{1}\leq J_{2}
if and only if
S^{1}xS^{1}\subseteq S^{1}yS^{1}
, where
x\in J_{1}
,
y\in J_{2}
.

The Green’s relations on a transformation semigroup
S\in\{\mathcal{T}_{n},\mathcal{I}_{n},\mathcal{PT}_{n}\}
are controlled by kernels and images. Namely, for
\alpha,\beta\in S

(i)

\alpha\mathcal{L}\beta\iff\image(\alpha)=\image(\beta)
;

(ii)

\alpha\mathcal{R}\beta\iff\ker(\alpha)=\ker(\beta)
;

(iii)

\alpha\mathcal{D}\beta\iff\rank(\alpha)=\rank(\beta)\iff\alpha\mathcal{J}\beta
.

Thus, a
\mathcal{J}
-class of
S
consists of all elements in
S
of a fixed rank, and we denote by
J_{i}
for the
\mathcal{J}
-class that contains elements of rank
i
. The
\mathcal{J}
-classes of
S
form a chain
under the ordering introduced above. Specifically, in

\mathcal{PT}_{n}
and
I_{n}
this chain is
J_{0}<J_{1}<\dots<J_{n}
, whereas for
\mathcal{T}_{n}
we have

J_{1}<\dots<J_{n}
.

Ideals in any semigroup
S
are downward closed unions of
\mathcal{J}
-classes.
Hence the ideals of
S\in\{\mathcal{PT}_{n},\mathcal{T}_{n},I_{n}\}
are precisely
the sets

I_{m}:=\{\alpha\in S\colon\rank(\alpha)\leq m\}\quad\text{for }m\in[\epsilon,n].

Here
\epsilon=0
for
S=\mathcal{PT}_{n}
and
S=\mathcal{I}_{n}
, and
\epsilon=1
for
S=\mathcal{T}_{n}
.

Consider a finite set
A
. A semigroup presentation is a pair
\langle A\ |\ \mathcal{R}\rangle
, where
A
is an alphabet,
\mathcal{R}\subseteq A^{+}\times A^{+}
is considered to be a set of defining relations, and an element
(u,v)\in\mathcal{R}
is usually written as
u=v
. We say that a semigroup
S
is defined by the presentation
\langle A\ |\ \mathcal{R}\rangle
if
S\cong A^{+}/\mathcal{R}^{\sharp}
, where
\mathcal{R}^{\sharp}
is the smallest congruence on
A^{+}
that contains
\mathcal{R}
. We can obtain another word
w^{\prime}
from
w
by replacing a subword
u
of
w
by
v
given that
(u=v)\in\mathcal{R}
. A relation
w^{\prime}=w
is said to be a consequence of
\mathcal{R}
if we can obtain
w^{\prime}
from
w
, or
w
from
w^{\prime}
by applying the defining relations or consequences of
\mathcal{R}
finitely many times.

If we know a (finite) presentation
\mathcal{P}=\langle A\ |\ \mathcal{R}\rangle
for a semigroup
S
, we could obtain any another finite presentation for
S
by applying any of the following
elementary Tietze transformations
finitely many times to
\mathcal{P}

[
16
]
:

(T1)

if
u=v
is a consequence of
\langle A\ |\ \mathcal{R}\rangle
, add relation
u=v
and obtain
\langle A\ |\ \mathcal{R},u=v\rangle
;

(T2)

if
(u=v)\in\mathcal{R}
is a consequence of
\langle A\ |\ \mathcal{R}\setminus\{u=v\}\rangle
, delete the relation
u=v
from
\langle A\ |\ \mathcal{R}\rangle
and obtain
\langle A\ |\ \mathcal{R}\setminus\{u=v\}\rangle
;

(T3)

add a new generating symbol
b
and a new relation
b=w
where
w\in A^{+}
to
\langle A\ |\ \mathcal{R}\rangle
and obtain
\langle A\cup\{b\}\ |\ \mathcal{R}\cup\{b=w\}\rangle
;

(T4)

if
\mathcal{R}
contains a relation of the form
b=w
, where
b\in A
and
w\in(A\setminus\{b\})^{+}
, then delete the generating symbol
b
, and replace any occurrence of
b
in
\mathcal{R}
by
w
.

The analogue for groups is better documented in literature, e.g. see
[
18
, Chapter 6.4.3]
.

3.
Cayley Table Presentation

On the face of it, to determine the relational depth of a semigroup
S
we need to consider all possible presentations for
S
. In this section we show that in fact it is sufficient to consider the Cayley table of
S
, viewed as a presentation, and certain restrictions of it. The
Cayley table presentation
for semigroup
S
is

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{S}=\langle x_{s}\ (s\in S)\ |\ x_{s}x_{t}=x_{st}\ (s,t\in S)\rangle
.

For
U\subseteq S
the
restriction
of the Cayley table presentation restricted to
U
is

\mathcal{C}_{S,U}=\langle x_{s}\ (s\in U)\ |\ x_{s}x_{t}=x_{st}\ (s,t,st\in U)\rangle
.

Notice that
\mathcal{C}_{S,U}
need not define
S
. Firstly,
U
may not be a generating set. And, secondly, even if
\langle U\rangle=S
, the relations may not be sufficient, and may only define a homomorphic preimage of
S
.

As before, let
S
be a semigroup whose
\mathcal{J}
-classes form a chain
J_{\epsilon}<\dots<J_{k}
.
For
i\in[\epsilon,k]
, let
\mathcal{C}_{i}=\langle X_{i,k}\mid\mathcal{R}_{i,k}\rangle
be the restriction of
\mathcal{C}_{S}
to
U_{i}:=J_{i}\cup\dots\cup J_{k}
.

As the main result of this section we prove that to find the relational depth of
S
it is sufficient to consider these restrictions:

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
S
be a semigroup whose
\mathcal{J}
-classes form a chain
J_{\epsilon}<\dots<J_{k}
. The relational depth of
S
is
k-i+1
where
i
is the largest number for which
\mathcal{C}_{i}
defines
S
.

To prove the proposition, we will need the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
S
be a semigroup and
I\subseteq S
an ideal. Define
U:=S\setminus I
. Suppose that
S
has a presentation
\langle A\mid\mathcal{R}\rangle
such that the following hold:

(i)

Every
x\in A
represents an element of
U
;

(ii)

if
(u=v)
is a relation from
\mathcal{R}
, then
u
(and hence
v
) represents an element of
U
.

Then
\mathcal{C}_{S,U}
defines
S
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{S,U}=\langle X\ |\ \mathcal{Q}\rangle
as defined above. First, without loss of generality, we can assume that
A\subseteq X
. Furthermore, we can in fact assume that
A=X
. Indeed, if not, we could add redundant generators
X\setminus A
to obtain another presentation which has this property, and still satisfies
(i)
,
(ii)
. We prove that we can obtain
\langle X\mid\mathcal{Q}\rangle
from
\langle X\mid\mathcal{R}\rangle
using Tietze transformations.

If
s,t\in U
such that
st\in U
, we can deduce the relation
x_{s}x_{t}=x_{st}
from
\mathcal{R}
. Adding all relations of this form to
\langle X\mid\mathcal{R}\rangle
we obtain
\langle X\mid\mathcal{R},\mathcal{Q}\rangle
, (transformation
(T1)
).

It remains to show that we can eliminate
\mathcal{R}
from
\langle X\mid\mathcal{R},\mathcal{Q}\rangle
. Let
(u=v)\in\mathcal{R}
, and suppose that
u\equiv x_{1}\dots x_{m}
,
v\equiv y_{1}\dots y_{n}
, where
x_{1}\dots x_{m}=y_{1}\dots y_{n}=x\in U
. Then
x_{i},y_{j}\in U
and a straightforward induction shows that the relations
x=x_{1}\dots x_{m}
and
x=y_{1}\dots y_{n}
, and hence
u=v
are consequences of
\mathcal{Q}
. Hence relations in
\mathcal{R}
are consequences of
\langle X\mid\mathcal{Q}\rangle
, and deleting
\mathcal{R}
we obtain
\langle X\mid\mathcal{Q}\rangle
(transformation
(T3)
).
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Let
\depth(S)=d
.
Since
\depth(\mathcal{C}_{i})=k-i+1
, we have
k-i+1\geq d
.
On the other hand, since
S
has a presentation with depth
d
, it follows by Lemma
3.2
that
\mathcal{C}_{k-d+1}
defines
S
. Therefore
i\geq k-d+1
, and the result follows.
∎

Now consider
S
to be a proper ideal
I_{m}
of
S\in\{\mathcal{T}_{n},\mathcal{I}_{n},\mathcal{PT}_{n}\}
, whose
\mathcal{J}
-classes are
J_{\epsilon}<\dots<J_{m}
. The question becomes: for which
i
does
\mathcal{C}_{i}
define
I_{m}
?

We aim to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
n\geq 3
, and let
S\in\{\mathcal{PT}_{n},\mathcal{T}_{n},\mathcal{I}_{n}\}
.
Define
\epsilon=0
for
S\in\{\mathcal{PT}_{n},\mathcal{I}_{n}\}
and
\epsilon=1
when
S=\mathcal{T}_{n}
. Define
\theta=\frac{n+\epsilon}{2}
.
For
m\in[\epsilon,n)
, let
I_{m}
be the ideal of
S
of all mappings of rank
\leq m
.

(i)

When
m\leq\theta
,
\mathcal{C}_{i}
defines
I_{m}
if and only if
i=\epsilon
.

(ii)

When
m>\theta
,
\mathcal{C}_{i}
defines
I_{m}
if and only if
i\leq 2m-n
.

Our Main Theorem follows from this and Proposition
3.1
. The rest of the paper is devoted to proving Theorem
3.3
, which is achieved in Theorem
6.11
,
7.12
,
8.19
for
\mathcal{I}_{n},\mathcal{T}_{n}
and
\mathcal{PT}_{n}
respectively.

4.
Lower bound

In this section we aim to find the lower bound for the depths of proper ideals of
\mathcal{I}_{n},\mathcal{T}_{n}
and
\mathcal{PT}_{n}
, by proving the following proposition.

Proposition 4.1
.

Consider
m<n
. Let
I_{m}
be an ideal of
S\in\{\mathcal{PT}_{n},\mathcal{I}_{n},\mathcal{T}_{n}\}
. Define
\epsilon=0
for
S\in\{\mathcal{PT}_{n},\mathcal{I}_{n}\}
and
\epsilon=1
when
S=\mathcal{T}_{n}
. Then
\depth(I_{m})\geq m-{\rm max}(\epsilon,2m-n)+1
.

The proof follows from the next proposition, which shows that certain depths are not sufficient.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
m<n
. Let
I_{m}
be an ideal of
S\in\{\mathcal{PT}_{n},\mathcal{I}_{n},\mathcal{T}_{n}\}
. Define
\epsilon=0
for
S\in\{\mathcal{PT}_{n},\mathcal{I}_{n}\}
and
\epsilon=1
when
S=\mathcal{T}_{n}
. If
m>r>{\rm max}(2m-n,\epsilon)
, then
\mathcal{C}_{r}=\langle X_{r,m}\ |\ \mathcal{R}_{r,m}\rangle
does not define
I_{m}
.

We will derive Proposition
4.2
from the following technical, but more general, result:

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
S
be any finite semigroup whose
\mathcal{J}
-classes form a chain,

J_{\epsilon}<\dots<J_{m}
.
Suppose that
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in J_{m}
such that
\alpha\beta
and
\alpha\beta\gamma=\beta\gamma\in J_{r}
for some
r<m
, then
x_{\alpha}x_{\beta}x_{\gamma}=x_{\beta}x_{\gamma}
is not a consequence of
\mathcal{R}_{r+1,m}
.

To prove the proposition, we first show that all words that can be obtained from such
x_{\beta}x_{\gamma}
must be of a certain form.

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose that
\alpha,\beta\in J_{m}
are such that
\alpha\beta\in J_{r}
, where
r<m
. Suppose that
w\in X_{r+1,m}^{*}
is obtained from
x_{\alpha}x_{\beta}
by applications of relations in
\mathcal{R}_{r+1,m}
, then

(1)

w=x_{\alpha_{1}}x_{\alpha_{2}}\dots x_{\alpha_{k}}x_{\beta_{1}}x_{\beta_{2}}\dots x_{\beta_{l}},

where
\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{k}=\alpha
,
\beta_{1}\dots\beta_{l}=\beta
,
\alpha_{i},\beta_{j}\in J_{m}
for all
i,j
, and
\alpha_{k}\beta_{1}\in J_{r}
.

Proof.

We induct on the number of applications of the relations from
\mathcal{R}_{r+1,m}
to
x_{\alpha}x_{\beta}
. In the base case, no relation from
\mathcal{R}_{r+1,m}
is applied, and the word
x_{\alpha}x_{\beta}
satisfies the conditions (
1
).

Now suppose that
w=x_{\alpha_{1}}\dots x_{\alpha_{k}}x_{\beta_{1}}\dots x_{\beta_{l}}
is a word that satisfies (
1
). Let
w^{\prime}
be the word obtained from
w
by one application of a relation from
\mathcal{R}_{r+1,m}
. Since all relations in
\mathcal{R}_{r+1,m}
equate a letter and a word of length two, applying a relation from
\mathcal{R}_{r+1,m}
necessarily changes the length, and
w^{\prime}
is either one letter longer or shorter than
w
.

If
w^{\prime}
is longer, a length one subword
u
of
w
has been replaced. If
u=x_{\alpha_{i}}
where
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
, the relation applied to
w
has the form
x_{\alpha_{i}}=x_{\alpha^{\prime}}x_{\alpha^{\prime\prime}}
, where
\alpha_{i}=\alpha^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime\prime}
. Since

J_{m}=J_{\alpha_{i}}\leq J_{\alpha^{\prime}}
and
J_{\alpha_{i}}\leq J_{\alpha^{\prime\prime}}
,
\alpha^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime\prime}\in J_{m}
. If
i=k
, it remains to show that
\alpha^{\prime\prime}\beta_{1}\in J_{r}
. Since

and

is stable,
\alpha^{\prime\prime}\beta_{1}\mathcal{J}\alpha^{\prime}\alpha^{\prime\prime}\beta_{1}
, and

.
Then

w^{\prime}=x_{\alpha_{1}}\dots x_{\alpha_{i-1}}x_{\alpha^{\prime}}x_{\alpha^{\prime\prime}}x_{\alpha_{i+1}}\dots x_{\alpha_{k}}x_{\beta_{1}}\dots x_{\beta_{l}}

satisfies (

for

,

If

is shorter than

, a length two subword

of

. Notice that since
\alpha_{k}\beta_{1}\in J_{m}
by the induction hypothesis, relations of the form

are not in
\mathcal{R}_{r+1,m}
Therefore the word

x_{\alpha_{i}}x_{\alpha_{i+1}}(i\in\{1,\dots,k-1\})

.
Without loss of generality, suppose that

, where
\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha_{i}\alpha_{i+1}

\alpha=\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{i-1}\alpha^{\prime}\alpha_{i+2}\dots\alpha_{k}\in J_{m}
,
J_{m}\leq J_{\alpha^{\prime}}
, and
\alpha^{\prime}\in J_{m}
. If
i=k-1

\alpha_{k-1}\alpha_{k}\beta_{1}\mathcal{L}\alpha_{k}\beta_{1}
and
S
is stable,
\alpha_{k-1}\alpha_{k}\beta_{1}\mathcal{J}\alpha_{k}\beta_{1}
. Therefore
\alpha^{\prime}\beta_{1}\in J_{r}
.
Then

w^{\prime}=x_{\alpha_{1}}\dots x_{\alpha_{i-1}}x_{\alpha^{\prime}}x_{\alpha_{i+2}}\dots x_{\alpha_{k}}x_{\beta_{1}}\dots x_{\beta_{l}}

is of the form stated in (

∎

Proof of Proposition
4.3
.

This follows from Lemma
4.4
, as
x_{\alpha}x_{\beta}x_{\gamma}
does not satisfy the conditions in (

).

Proof of Proposition

.

5.
Upper bound: Preliminaries

In this section, we state and prove some technical lemmas that will subsequently be used to find the upper bounds for the relational depths of
\mathcal{I}_{n}
,
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, and
\mathcal{PT}_{n}
. For now, consider
S\in\{\mathcal{I}_{n},\mathcal{T}_{n},\mathcal{PT}_{n}\}
. Then
S=J_{\epsilon}\cup\dots\cup J_{n}
, where

\epsilon=0\text{ if }S\in\{\mathcal{PT}_{n},\mathcal{I}_{n}\}\text{, and }\epsilon=1\text{ if }S=\mathcal{T}_{n}
.

The following lemma states the three conditions for deducing relations of rank
r
in an ideal
I_{m}
using relations from
\mathcal{R}_{r+1,m}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
I_{m}
be an ideal of
S
, then
I_{m}=J_{\epsilon}\cup\dots\cup J_{m}
. Let
r\leq 2m-n-1
. Suppose that the following statements hold.

(P1)

For
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\in J_{r+1}
, if
\alpha\beta=\gamma\delta\in J_{r}
, then we can deduce the relation
x_{\alpha}x_{\beta}=x_{\gamma}x_{\delta}
using relations in
\mathcal{R}_{r+1,m}
.

(P2)

For
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in J_{r+1}
, if
\alpha\beta,\beta\gamma,\alpha\beta\gamma\in J_{r}
, then we can obtain a word
x_{\alpha^{\prime}}x_{\gamma^{\prime}}
from
x_{\alpha}x_{\beta}x_{\gamma}
, where
\alpha^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}\in J_{r+1}
using relations in
\mathcal{R}_{r+1,r+2}
.

(P3)

For
\gamma\delta\in J_{r}
, if
\rank(\gamma),\rank(\delta)\geq r+1
, we can obtain a word
x_{\alpha}x_{\beta}
from
x_{\gamma}x_{\delta}
, where
\alpha,\beta\in J_{r+1}
using relations in
\mathcal{R}_{r+1,m}
.

Then the following are true:

(i)

For any
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}\in J_{r+1}\cup\dots\cup J_{m}
such that
\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{k}\in J_{r}
, there exist
\gamma,\delta\in J_{r+1}
such that
\beta\gamma=\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\dots\alpha_{k}
, and
x_{\beta}x_{\gamma}=x_{\alpha_{1}}x_{\alpha_{2}}\dots x_{\alpha_{k}}
is a consequence of
\mathcal{R}_{r+1,m}
.

(ii)

If
\epsilon\leq r\leq 2m-n-1
, and
w,v
are words that represent the same rank-
r
element, then we can obtain
v
from
w
using
\mathcal{R}_{r+1,m}
.

Proof.

(i)
For
w=x_{\alpha_{1}}x_{\alpha_{2}}\dots x_{\alpha_{k}}
, define
r(w)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\rank(\alpha_{i})
. Let
w=x_{\alpha_{1}}\dots x_{\alpha_{k}}
be a word that satisfies the condition of the theorem. Consider the following claim.

Claim 1
.

If
r(w)>2r+2
, then there exist
w^{\prime}=x_{\alpha_{1}^{\prime}}\dots x_{\alpha_{l}^{\prime}}
such that

(a)

\alpha_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\alpha_{l}^{\prime}\in J_{r+1}\cup\dots\cup J_{m}

(b)

w=w^{\prime}
is a consequence of
\mathcal{R}_{r+1,m}

(c)

r(w^{\prime})<r(w)

Notice that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\rank(\alpha_{i})=2r+2\iff k=2
and
\rank(\alpha_{1})=\rank(\alpha_{2})=r+1
. So if
r(w)=2r+2
,
w
consists of
2
letters that represent element of rank
r+1
. Also notice that we always have
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\rank(\alpha_{i})\geq 2r+2
. The claim suggests that if
w=x_{\alpha_{1}}\dots x_{\alpha_{k}}\in X_{r+1,m}^{*}
which represents an element of rank
r
, we can obtain a word
w^{\prime}
such that
r(w^{\prime})<r(w)
using
\mathcal{R}_{r+1,m}
. By repeatedly applying the claim to the resulting word, we may reduce the sum of ranks until it gets to
2r+2
. Hence, to prove the first part of the lemma, it suffices to prove the claim.

Proof of claim.

Consider the prefix
x_{\alpha_{1}}x_{\alpha_{2}}
of
w
.

Case 1
:
\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\in J_{r+1}\cup\dots\cup J_{m}
.
x_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}=x_{\alpha_{1}}x_{\alpha_{2}}
is a relation in
\mathcal{R}_{r+1,m}
. Applying this to
w
, we obtain
w^{\prime}=x_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}x_{\alpha_{3}}\dots x_{\alpha_{k}}
, and
r(w^{\prime})<r(w)
follows from the fact that
\rank(\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2})\leq{\rm min}\{\rank(\alpha_{1}),\rank(\alpha_{2})\}
.

Case 2
:
\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\in J_{r}
. If
\rank(\alpha_{1})>r+1
or
\rank(\alpha_{2})>r+1
, then by
(P3)
, there exist
\alpha_{1}^{\prime},\alpha_{2}^{\prime}\in J_{r+1}
such that
x_{\alpha_{1}^{\prime}}x_{\alpha_{2}^{\prime}}
can be obtained from
x_{\alpha_{1}}x_{\alpha_{2}}
using
\mathcal{R}_{r+1,m}
. Clearly
r(\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2})<r(\alpha_{1}^{\prime}\alpha_{2}^{\prime})
, and
r(w^{\prime})<r(w)
.

Now suppose that
\rank(\alpha_{1})=\rank(\alpha_{2})=r+1
. Since
r(w)>2r+2
and
r(\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2})=2r+2
, we have
k\geq 3
. Consider the prefix
x_{\alpha_{1}}x_{\alpha_{2}}x_{\alpha_{3}}
. Since
\rank(\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3})\leq\rank(\alpha_{2})=r+1
, we have
\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}\in J_{r}\cup J_{r+1}
. If
\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}\in J_{r+1}
, then
x_{\alpha_{2}}x_{\alpha_{3}}=x_{\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}}
is a relation in
\mathcal{R}_{r+1,m}
. Therefore
r(x_{\alpha_{1}}x_{\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}}x_{\alpha_{4}}\dots x_{\alpha_{k}})<r(w)
. Otherwise,
\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}\in J_{r}
. Then by statement
(P2)
,
x_{\alpha_{1}}x_{\alpha_{2}}x_{\alpha_{3}}=x_{\alpha_{1}^{\prime}}x_{\alpha_{3}^{\prime}}
where
\alpha_{1}^{\prime},\alpha_{3}^{\prime}\in J_{r+1}
, follows from
\mathcal{R}_{r+1,m}
, and
w^{\prime}=x_{\alpha_{1}^{\prime}}x_{\alpha_{3}^{\prime}}\dots x_{\alpha_{k}}
satisfies
r(w^{\prime})<r(w)
.
∎

(ii) Now suppose
w=x_{\alpha_{1}}\dots x_{\alpha_{k}}
and
v=x_{\beta_{1}}\dots x_{\beta_{l}}
are words in
X_{r+1,m}^{*}
such that they represent the same element of rank
r
. Then by the first part of the lemma, we can obtain
x_{\alpha}x_{\beta}
from
w
, and
x_{\gamma}x_{\delta}
from
v
, where
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\in J_{r+1}
. Then by
(P1)
, we may deduce the relation
x_{\gamma}x_{\delta}=x_{\alpha}x_{\beta}
from
\mathcal{R}_{r+1,m}
.
∎

The above points out the direction we should aim for when finding the upper bound. In the following, we build up on the lemma and prove that once the conditions
\ref{P1}-\ref{P3}
are satisfied, the upper bound would be straightforward.

Recall that
\mathcal{C}_{r}=\langle X_{r,m}\mid\mathcal{R}_{r,m}\rangle
is the restriction of the Cayley table presentation
\mathcal{C}
of
S
to the generators of rank at least
r
. Define

R_{r}^{\prime}=\{u=v\colon u,v\in X_{r+1,m}^{+},{\rm and\ }u,v{\rm\ represent\ a\ map\ of\ rank\ }r\}
.

Proposition 5.2
.

For
\epsilon\leq r\leq 2m-n-1
, if
S
satisfies
\ref{P1}-\ref{P3}
, then

\mathcal{C}_{r}
and
\mathcal{C}_{r+1}
define the same semigroup.

Proof.

We show that we can obtain
\mathcal{C}_{r}
from
\mathcal{C}_{r+1}
using Tietze transformations.

Step 1
: Add a set of generating symbols
\{x_{\alpha}\colon\rank(\alpha)=r\}
to
\langle X_{r+1,m}\ |\ \mathcal{R}_{r+1,m}\rangle
. For each new symbol
x_{\alpha}
, choose a word
u_{\alpha}\in X_{r+1,m}^{+}
which represent the map
\alpha
, and add a relation
x_{\alpha}=u_{\alpha}

(T3)
. The presentation obtained is
P_{1}=\langle X_{r,m}\ |\ \mathcal{R}_{r+1,m},x_{\alpha}=u_{\alpha}\ (\alpha\in D_{r})\rangle
.

Step 2
: By Lemma
5.1
,
R_{r}^{\prime}
is a consequence of
\mathcal{C}_{r+1}
. Define a set of relations
R_{r}^{\prime\prime}=\{x_{\beta}x_{\gamma}=x_{\beta\gamma}\colon\rank(\beta),\rank(\gamma)\geq r,\rank(\beta\gamma)=r\}
. Consider
\beta,\gamma\in J_{r}\cup\dots\cup J_{m}
such that
\beta\gamma\in J_{r}
. Since
\beta\gamma\in J_{r}
,
x_{\beta\gamma}=u_{\beta\gamma}
is a relation in
P_{1}
, if
\beta\in J_{r}
then
x_{\beta}=u_{\beta}
is a relation in
P_{1}
, otherwise
\rank(\beta)>r
, and
x_{\beta}\in X_{r+1,m}
. Similarly, either
x_{\gamma}=u_{\gamma}
is a relation in
P_{1}
, or
x_{\gamma}\in X_{r+1,m}
. We consider the
4
possible combinations of the ranks of
\beta
and
\gamma
. If
\beta,\gamma\in J_{r}
, then
x_{\beta}x_{\gamma},u_{\beta\gamma}\in X_{r+1,m}^{+}
such that they represent the same rank-
r
map. Then
x_{\beta}x_{\gamma}=u_{\beta\gamma}\in R_{r}^{\prime}
and is a consequence of
\mathcal{C}_{r+1}
and hence
P_{1}
. If
\beta,\gamma\in J_{r}
, since
u_{\beta}=x_{\beta},u_{\gamma}=x_{\gamma}
are relations in
P_{1}
, and
u_{\beta}u_{\gamma}=u_{\beta\gamma}\in R_{r}^{\prime}
, we can deduce
x_{\beta}x_{\gamma}=x_{\beta\gamma}
from
P_{1}
. If exactly one of
\beta
and
\gamma
is of rank
r
, say
\beta\in J_{r}
and
\gamma\notin J_{r}
, then
x_{\beta}=u_{\beta}
is a relation in
P_{1}
. The words
u_{\beta}x_{\gamma},u_{\beta\gamma}\in X_{r+1,m}^{+}
represent the same map
\beta\gamma
, so
u_{\beta}x_{\gamma}=u_{\beta\gamma}\in R_{r}^{\prime}
and is a consequence of
\mathcal{C}_{r}
and hence
P_{1}
. A similar argument holds for the case when
\beta\notin J_{r}
and
\gamma\in J_{r}
. In all cases
R_{r}^{\prime\prime}
follows from
P_{1}
. So adding
R_{r}^{\prime\prime}
we obtain
P_{2}
. Notice that
\mathcal{R}_{r,m}=\mathcal{R}_{r+1,m}\cup\{x_{\beta}x_{\gamma}=x_{\beta\gamma}\colon\beta\in J_{r}{\rm\ or\ }\gamma\in J_{r}{\ \rm or\ }\beta\gamma\in J_{r}\}=\mathcal{R}_{r+1,m}\cup R_{r}^{\prime\prime}
, so
P_{2}=\langle X_{r,m}\ |\ \mathcal{R}_{r,m},x_{\alpha}=u_{\alpha}\ (\alpha\in J_{r})\rangle
.

Step 3
: Consider a relation of the form
x_{\alpha}=u_{\alpha}
where
\alpha\in J_{r}
. We have
u_{\alpha}\equiv x_{\alpha_{1}}x_{\alpha_{2}}\dots x_{\alpha_{k}}\in X_{r+1,m}^{+}
. The sequence of relations
x_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\dots\alpha_{k}}=x_{\alpha_{1}}x_{\alpha_{2}\dots\alpha_{k}}=\dots=x_{\alpha_{1}}x_{\alpha_{2}}\dots x_{\alpha_{k}}
is a consequence of
\langle X_{r,m}\ |\ \mathcal{R}_{r,m}\rangle
. Therefore removing
\{x_{\alpha}=u_{\alpha}\colon\alpha\in J_{r}\}
from
P_{2}
we obtain
\mathcal{C}_{r}
.
∎

Theorem 5.3
.

Suppose that for
\epsilon\leq r\leq 2m-n-1

\ref{P1}-\ref{P3}

, and

in

for
\mathcal{I}_{n}

Suppose that
I_{m}
is a proper ideal of
\mathcal{I}_{n}

\alpha,\beta\in J_{r+1}
such that
\alpha\beta\in J_{r}

\displaystyle\alpha=\small\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&\dots&a_{r}&a_{r+1}\\
a_{1}^{\prime}&a_{2}^{\prime}&\dots&a_{r}^{\prime}&a_{r+1}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix},\ \beta=\small\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}^{\prime}&a_{2}^{\prime}&\dots&a_{r}^{\prime}&b_{r+1}\\

where
a_{r+1}^{\prime}\in[n]\setminus\dom(\beta)

A=[n]\setminus\dom(\alpha)

B=[n]\setminus\dom(\beta)

\mathcal{R}_{r+1,r+2}

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
a\in B\setminus\{a_{r+1}^{\prime}\}
. Define
\alpha^{\prime}=\small\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&\dots&a_{r}&a_{r+1}\\
a_{1}^{\prime}&a_{2}^{\prime}&\dots&a_{r}^{\prime}&a\end{pmatrix}

\alpha\beta=\alpha^{\prime}\beta
, and the relation
x_{\alpha}x_{\beta}=x_{\alpha^{\prime}}x_{\beta}
is a consequence of
\mathcal{R}_{r+1,r+2}
.

Proof.

Let
b\in[n]\setminus\image(\beta)
. Define

\displaystyle\beta_{1}

\displaystyle=\small\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}^{\prime}&a_{2}^{\prime}&\dots&a_{r}^{\prime}&b_{r+1}&a_{r+1}^{\prime}\\
b_{1}&b_{2}&\dots&b_{r}&b_{r+1}&b\end{pmatrix},\ \beta_{1}^{\prime}=\small\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}^{\prime}&a_{2}^{\prime}&\dots&a_{r}^{\prime}&b_{r+1}&a\\
b_{1}&b_{2}&\dots&b_{r}&b_{r+1}&b\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\beta_{2}

\displaystyle=\small\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}^{\prime}&b_{2}^{\prime}&\dots&b_{r}^{\prime}&b_{r+1}^{\prime}\\
b_{1}^{\prime}&b_{2}^{\prime}&\dots&b_{r}^{\prime}&b_{r+1}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}.

Then
\alpha\beta_{1}=\alpha^{\prime}\beta_{1}^{\prime}\in J_{r+1}
,
\beta_{1}\beta_{2}=\beta_{1}^{\prime}\beta_{2}=\beta
. Hence

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} x_{\alpha}x_{\beta}&=x_{\alpha}x_{\beta_{1}}x_{\beta_{2}}\ \ &&(x_{\beta}=x_{\beta_{1}}x_{\beta_{2}})\\
&=x_{\alpha^{\prime}}x_{\beta_{1}^{\prime}}x_{\beta_{2}}&&(x_{\alpha}x_{\beta_{1}}=x_{\alpha^{\prime}}x_{\beta_{1}^{\prime}})\\
&=x_{\alpha^{\prime}}x_{\beta}&&(x_{\beta_{1}^{\prime}}x_{\beta_{2}}=x_{\beta}).\end{aligned}

∎

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
a\in A
. Define
\alpha^{\prime}=\small\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&\dots&a_{r}&a\\
a_{1}^{\prime}&a_{2}^{\prime}&\dots&a_{r}^{\prime}&a_{r+1}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}
. Then
\alpha\beta=\alpha^{\prime}\beta
, and the relation
x_{\alpha}x_{\beta}=x_{\alpha^{\prime}}x_{\beta}
is a consequence of
\mathcal{R}_{r+1,r+2}
.

Proof.

Let
a^{\prime}\in A\setminus\{a\}
, let
b\in B
. Define

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}

\displaystyle=\small\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&\dots&a_{r}&a_{r+1}&a\\
a_{1}&a_{2}&\dots&a_{r}&a_{r+1}&a\end{pmatrix},\ \alpha_{2}=\small\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&\dots&a_{r}&a_{r+1}&a^{\prime}\\
a_{1}^{\prime}&a_{2}^{\prime}&\dots&a_{r}^{\prime}&a_{r+1}^{\prime}&b\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\small\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&\dots&a_{r}&a&a^{\prime}\\
a_{1}^{\prime}&a_{2}^{\prime}&\dots&a_{r}^{\prime}&a_{r+1}^{\prime}&b\end{pmatrix},\ \beta_{1}=\small\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}^{\prime}&a_{2}^{\prime}&\dots&a_{r}^{\prime}&b_{r+1}&b\\
a_{1}^{\prime}&a_{2}^{\prime}&\dots&a_{r}^{\prime}&b_{r+1}&b\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\beta_{2}

\displaystyle=\small\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}^{\prime}&a_{2}^{\prime}&\dots&a_{r}^{\prime}&b_{r+1}\\

9.
Conclusion

Most work in proving our Main Theorem was to show that the lower bounds for the depth of an ideal of
\mathcal{T}_{n},\mathcal{I}_{n},\mathcal{PT}_{n}
are also upper bounds. Now let us mention the relationship between the lower bounds and another interesting concept.

Let
S
be a finite semigroup whose
\mathcal{J}
-classes form a chain, namely
J_{\epsilon}<\dots<J_{m}
. Suppose that
S
does not contain a group of units. We define the
multiplication depth
of
S
to be
m-r+1
, where
r
is the smallest number such that the product of two elements in
J_{m}
can land in
J_{r}
. Together with Proposition
4.3
and Lemma
4.4
, the definition suggests that the multiplication depth is a lower bound for the relational depth. The main theorem can be rewritten to capture this.

Corollary 9.1
.

Let
I_{m}
be a proper ideal of
S\in\{\mathcal{T}_{n},\mathcal{I}_{n},\mathcal{PT}_{n}\}
. Then the relational depth of
I_{m}
is equal to the multiplication depth.

This follows easily upon recalling that

\rank(\alpha)+\rank(\beta)\leq\rank(\alpha\beta)+n\quad\text{for any }\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{PT}_{n},

the transformation version of the well know Sylvester rank inequality from linear algebra.

It is not true in general that the relational depth and the multiplication depth coincide.

Example
.

Let
I_{m}
be a proper ideal of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, where
m>\frac{n+1}{2}
, and
\depth(I_{m})=m-r+1
, where
r=2m-n
. Define the Rees quotient semigroup
I_{m}^{\prime}=I_{m}\setminus I_{r-1}
. Notice that the
\mathcal{J}
-classes of
I_{m}^{\prime}
are
\{0\}<J_{r}<J_{r+1}<\dots<J_{m}
. The multiplication depth of
I_{m}^{\prime}
is equal to that of
I_{m}
, and the reader may check that it is impossible for two transformations of rank
m
to compose to one of rank less than or equal to
2m-n
. However,
\langle X_{r,m}\ |\ \mathcal{R}_{r,m}\rangle
does not define
I_{m}^{\prime}
, since
\langle X_{r,m}\ |\ \mathcal{R}_{r,m}\rangle=I_{m}
and
I_{m}^{\prime}
is a different semigroup. Hence
\depth(I_{m}^{\prime})\neq\depth(I_{m})
. In fact, the relational depth of
I_{m}^{\prime}
is the length of the chain of
\mathcal{J}
-classes, namely
\depth(I_{m}^{\prime})=m-r+2=n-m+2
.

There are some other families of semigroups whose
\mathcal{J}
-classes form a chain, for example, the partition monoid, and its subsemigroups, including Brauer monoid and Temperley–Lieb monoid, and the semigroups of linear transformations. We ask about the relational depth of the above semigroups, and whether they coincide with the multiplication depth.
It would also be of interest to investigate analogous problems for semigroups whose
\mathcal{J}
-classes form more complicated posets, not just chains. However, more thought is required in that case even to specify what one means by depth.
