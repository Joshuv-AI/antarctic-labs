Near‐Unity Chiral Lasing Enabled by Quasi‐Bound States in the Continuum
Authors: Jose Mendoza‐Carreño, Luis A. Pérez, Carlota Ruiz De Galarreta, Théo Rouanet, M. Isabel Alonso, Agustín Mihi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCircularly polarized light is essential for applications in optical communication, quantum computing, display systems, and chiral material characterization, among others. Yet, the inherently weak chiroptical response of most materials remains a fundamental limitation. Chiral nanophotonics overcomes this challenge by strongly enhancing light–matter interactions through resonant subwavelength nanostructures. Among these, emitters coupled to chiral bound states in the continuum (BICs) have shown excellent performance. However, the majority of chiral BIC architectures depend on costly nanofabrication processes, which significantly limit their scalability. Here, soft nanoimprinting lithography is used to produce chiral nanostructures that enable chiral lasing from an organic dye embedded in the patterned resist. Nearly fully circularly polarized lasing emission (97%) arises from coupling the dye photoluminescence to supported BIC resonances, as revealed by angular dispersion measurements and corroborated by Fourier microscopy and FDTD simulations. These results confirm coupling between orthogonally polarized TE and TM modes causing the BIC. Our work establishes a scalable route toward highly chiral light sources, advancing practical nanophotonic platforms for quantum and optical technologies.

Introduction

The ability to control the chiral properties of light is becoming crucial to both fundamental and applied research across many cutting‐edge areas. Chirality is present in research fields such as biology and sensing [1, 2, 3], telecommunications [4, 5], photodetection [6, 7], miniaturized circular polarizers [8], or emission applications related to Light Emitting Diodes [9, 10, 11] and liquid crystal display technologies [12, 13, 14]. As a result, over the last years, managing the spin angular momentum of light has gained special attention [15, 16]. This property defines the rotation sense of circularly polarized light (CPL) when propagating in space, which governs the differential interaction with chiral matter. Propagating CPL exhibits either a clockwise or counterclockwise rotating helix, whose interaction with matter can be manifested in two phenomena. On one hand, the preferential absorption of CPL by chiral matter is known as circular dichroism (CD), and it is routinely exploited in spectrometry to characterize chiral media. On the other hand, preferential circularly polarized emission (CPE) by matter is generally described by the so‐called glum, a dissymmetry factor that accounts for the degree of circular polarization (DCP) present in the emitted light [17]. This quantity takes the form: (1) glum=2ILCP−IRCPILCP+IRCP where Ii accounts for the photoluminescence (PL) intensity of left (LCP) and right circularly polarized (RCP) emission. Generally, the chiroptical response of naturally occurring materials does not suffice the requirements for optoelectronic applications [18, 19, 20]. Natural chiral‐emitting materials produce CPE in magnetically allowed transitions with low probability, which in turn offer poor brightness [17]. An alternative approach to boost chiral emission is based on the combination of chiral nanophotonic architectures with bright emitters, exploiting the chiroptical response sustained by a nanostructure and modifying the radiation pattern of emitters coupled to it [21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26]. This strategy enables the use of bright non‐chiral emitters, opening the door to the plethora of fluorophores already in use. Recent reviews show that relying on chiral resonant arrays for enhancing the chiroptical response of matter is developing quickly and could be a potential solution to reach CPE values high enough to be used in optoelectronic applications [11, 27, 28].

Among the different optical resonances sustained by nanophotonic architectures, bound states in the continuum (BICs) have attracted a growing interest due to their strong light‐matter interaction [29, 30]. BICs are localized nonradiating resonant modes at energies above the radiation continuum that cannot be accessed from the far field. This phenomenon occurs, for instance, in symmetry‐protected modes or when two or more different resonances couple destructively, thus appearing as an anti‐crossing in the photonic band structure due to the energy exchange between them [31, 32, 33]. BIC states are very sensitive to geometrical parameters, although they can be accessed via imperfections present during the nanofabrication process or by cleverly engineered asymmetries within the unit cell, transforming them into quasi‐BICs (qBICs). In such qBICs, the extreme confinement of light at the nanoscale, displayed in resonances with high quality factors, is particularly useful for amplifying light‐matter interaction and non‐linear effects, with special focus on lasing emission applications [34, 35, 36, 37].

Recently, chiral qBICs have also been demonstrated, where the polarization‐dependent response was achieved by breaking the symmetry of the scattering unit cell [38, 39, 40, 41]. Chiral qBICs provide excellent dichroism values both for transmittance [42, 43] and emission [44] processes. For a nanostructure to support chiral qBICs, the unit cell needs to be carefully distorted, breaking both in‐plane and out‐of‐plane symmetries to differentiate chiral modes. Recently, near‐unity circular polarization values for transmittance and emission have been demonstrated in vertically displaced tilted bars of different heights [43] or slanted bars [44], respectively. As recently pointed out, breaking the z‐axis symmetry is essential for achieving intrinsic local chirality and it can be readily implemented by using substrates and/or superstrates with different refractive indices without compromising BIC formation [40].

In this work, we experimentally demonstrate a scalable nanophotonic architecture supporting chiral BICs used for pure circular lasing emission. Our architecture consists in a 2D array of chiral elements (triskelia) fabricated by soft nanomprinting lithography a thin film of photoresist doped with a non‐chiral organic dye, followed by a TiO2 coating to break the symmetry in the z‐direction. Recent theoretical studies predict the existence of BIC states in similar structures [45]. This architecture supports coupled resonances with energy exchange between cross‐polarized TE and TM modes [46], which is a necessary condition for CPL as stated recently [47, 48]. To illustrate the potential of our architecture, we chose Rhodamine B (RhB), a well‐known non‐chiral dye that is herein mixed in the nanoimprinted photoresist film (SU‐8), acting as a gain medium. Coupled to the chiral qBIC mode supported by the array, the dye can achieve lasing with a glum = 1.93 and high purity (97% DCP), whose emission wavelength can be tailored by the titania coating thickness. We provide herein the full angular optical characterization of the nanostructure to locate the spectral resonances that overlap with the emission band of the gain medium. Next, Back Focal Plane (BFP) measurements at a resonant wavelength show the crossing between orthogonal linearly polarized modes and the enhanced circularly polarized light extraction. Finite Difference Time Domain (FDTD) simulations corroborate the strong differences under circularly polarized light perpendicular and at a small oblique incidence angle (to access the BIC state), confirming the boosting in preferential chiral out‐coupling efficiency. Finally, we characterize and report all the related properties to lasing emission, such as polarization, directionality, band narrowing, and threshold behavior. We experimentally observed that each circular polarization has different energy thresholds for lasing activity, allowing for nearly pure circularly polarized lasing emission. This simple nanofabrication process allows us to obtain almost completely circularly polarized lasing activity using a large‐area and cost‐effective method, thus paving the way for future optoelectronic device implementation.

Results and Discussion

The chiral photonic structure (Figure 1a) consists of a triskelia array made by nanoimprinting a 130 nm SU‐8 film doped with RhB, where the emitting organic dye within the polymer matrix is employed as a gain medium. The triskelion geometry has produced relevant CPE values in the red part of the spectrum [26], but its potential as a lasing platform remains unexplored. We selected RhB mixed within an SU‐8 matrix acting as a non‐chiral gain medium since it possesses high quantum efficiency and is frequently used for its lasing activity in many nanophotonic systems [49, 50, 51]. The photonic structure was coated with 100 nm of TiO2 (n = 2.2–2.4) to boost the optical properties of the architecture. A photograph of the chiral array fabricated (16 mm2 patterned area) is shown in Figure 1c. The hexagonal diffraction pattern emerges when the sample is illuminated with the laser light (green) and can also be observed in the emitted photoluminescence (orange) in Figure 1d. The role of the high refractive index coating on the chiral array is two‐fold. First, it reinforces the waveguiding effect by increasing the effective refractive index (neff ) of the nanoimprinted film, thereby confining light more efficiently. The confinement of the electromagnetic field within the gain medium leads to an enhanced emission rate, reducing the threshold energy by the Purcell effect. Secondly, the titania coating enhances the refractive index contrast, which further differentiates the scattering cross‐sections for each CPL handedness (see Figure S1). We studied the effect on the absorption and scattering cross sections of an isolated triskelion with and without the titania coating and concluded that the motif resist alone cannot produce sufficient differential circular interaction, and only with the high refractive index material, substantial differences in the circular extinction cross sections are found. Then, the chiral array, acting as a grating, preferentially enhances the scattering of one circular polarization, resulting in more efficient extraction of this component into the far‐field. Additional FDTD characterizations comparing the current system with its 2D counterpart are described in Figures S2–S4.

The influence of the TiO2 coating thickness on the transmittance of the chiral array was first simulated with FDTD (Figure 1e). In the absence of TiO2 layer (0 nm, green curve in Figure 1e), the transmittance of the grating is almost flat with values over 90% except for the RhB absorption band located at 560 nm (indicated with a green semitransparent band). The weak optical resonances observed are caused by the low refractive index contrast between the air and the epoxy resist (n = 1.59). As the TiO2 coating thickness increases from 40, 75, and 100 nm (black, blue, and red curves in Figure 1e, respectively), a dip in transmittance appears associated with the lattice resonance of the chiral array. Increasing the coating thickness results in a red shift of the transmittance dip, becoming a sharp, strong, and tunable resonance across the RhB emission band for the different coating thicknesses (pink semitransparent band). The double‐peak resonances observed were associated with collective modes associated with different effective refractive indices [52]. Further information on the nature of these resonances is obtained by simulating the transmittance spectra as a function of the lattice parameter while maintaining the remaining geometrical parameters constant (Figure 1f). The dips in the transmittance depend linearly on the lattice parameter (λ∝Λ), and they are attributed to diffraction modes from the architecture. In our case, with a lattice parameter of 500 nm, the intense transmittance dip at 670 nm, corresponds to an effective refractive index of neff = 1.55, somewhere between that of the glass (n = 1.51) and the SU8 resist (n = 1.59), indicating this is light diffracted in the glass from the degenerated orders 〈 ± 1, 0〉, 〈0, ±1〉 and ± 〈1, 1〉 (see Figure S5). The less intense feature observed at 686 nm corresponds to an effective refractive index of neff = 1.58 for the same diffracted orders, pointing at modes trapped in the plane of the grating. In both cases, the effective refractive index was approximated from the Bragg condition for the first diffraction order of hexagonal lattices as: (2) neff=2·λr3Λ

An optimization algorithm was followed to minimize the difference between the experimental and numerical transmittance of the chiral grating by modelling the chiral photonic array with the relevant parameters (Table S2 for the parameters and the ranging values, Table S3 for the optimized values). A cross‐sectional refractive index map of the optimized unit cell is shown in Figure S7, with good agreement between the computation and experimental transmittance shown in Figure S8. Moreover, the optimized unit cell shows a vertical to horizontal deposition ratio similar to the one measured experimentally, confirming the fidelity of the FDTD obtained results. Additional information about hexagonal lattices and diffraction modes present within our architecture can be found in Sections S2 and S3, including the dispersion of these resonances as a function of the TiO2 thickness in Figure S9.

The chiral grating in this type of architecture can act as a selective in‐coupler to convert free‐space CPL radiation into a collective in‐plane mode, and vice versa, by behaving as a radiation out‐coupler [53, 54]. In sum, the experimental parameters explored in this work were 500 nm lattice with TiO2 coatings of 75 and 100 nm, which led to resonances that aligned well with the emission band of the RhB.

The chiral character of the array is quantified by the helicity of the near‐fields at the resonant lattice mode (λ = 670 nm) using the so‐called optical chirality C^, which describes both the strength and the rotation sense of an electromagnetic field [55]. This quantity can be normalized to an RCP circularly polarized wave, taking the form in the frequency domain as: (3) C^=−cμ02ImE∗·H

Fully circularly polarized light shows values of −1 or +1 for LCP and RCP, respectively, whereas C^ = 0 corresponds to linearly polarized light. The optical chirality depends both on electric and magnetic field distributions, since parallel fields are necessary for chiral electromagnetic radiation. Scattered fields usually depend on the input polarization state, as this dictates the excited resonant modes of the photonic structure. In the case of chiral architectures, LCP and RCP may excite similar optical chirality spatial distributions, yet with different strengths, accounting for better in‐coupling of a selective polarization. Therefore, the net optical chirality was calculated to quantify the presence of near‐field chirality. As C^ already carries sign information, the net optical chirality can be computed as, (4) ΔC^=C^RCP+C^LCP where C^i corresponds to the optical chirality distribution for a given input polarization.

The net optical chirality shown in Figure 1h at the resonant lattice mode (λ = 670 nm) was calculated in the plane illustrated with a white dashed line in the inset in Figure 1e. This allowed us to study preferential in‐coupling helicity and therefore opposite‐handedness emission due to the reciprocity principle (effectively meaning that left‐handed incoming light will result in right‐handed emitted light and vice versa). The color‐coded optical chirality distribution indicated LCP zones as red and RCP zones in blue. Yellow areas correspond to regions where there was no chiral near‐field distribution or locations where both helicities are compensated. We focus our attention on the triskelion motif, where most of the gain medium is located, thus dictates the emission polarization state. The R‐triskelion sustains a chiral hotspot which favours in‐coupling for LCP polarization and therefore RCP out‐coupling/emission. The net optical chirality values were found to be exceeding the unity, that is the upper limit for propagating waves (>1). This effect is an indicator of local superchiral light fields within the triskelion that can potentially be exploited for producing large CPE values [40, 55].

The ballistic transmittance was collected as a function of the angle of incidence θ, where the extinction is computed as 1‐T, being T the transmitted 0th diffraction order. For the linear polarization measurements (Figure 2a), both transverse electric (TE) and transverse magnetic (TM) polarizations were excited, indicated as horizontal red and vertical blue arrows at the top‐right, respectively. The red‐blue contour plots in the third panel aim to help identify the predominance of either linear or circular polarization (represented in the dissymmetry factors gL and gc calculated using the same structure formula as glum in Equation (1), substituting the photoluminescence intensities by the extinction values for linear and circular polarization transmittances, respectively). The chiral arrays studied herein show rich k‐space features with multiple anti‐crossing bands due to the destructive interference of two or more interacting modes [31] (see Figure S11). In Figure 2a,b we zoom in on the 640–680 nm spectral range in the −5° to +5° region, as it coincides with the lattice mode overlapping the emission band of the RhB.

Under TE‐excitation (horizontal red arrow in Figure 2a, first panel), the excited dispersion bands correspond to the degenerated diffractive modes 〈 − 1, 0〉, 〈 + 1, 0〉, 〈0, ±1〉 and 〈1, 1〉. As the illumination angle is close to normal incidence, an energy splitting at the Γ‐point can be observed (between 0° and 2°). The degeneracy of the diffracted modes is lifted, giving rise to resonances in λ = 666 nm and λ = 670 nm (indicated with a white arrow at the Γ‐point).

Under TM excitation (vertical blue arrow in Figure 2a, second panel), the electric field distribution is perpendicular to the substrate under oblique incidence, and there is an electric field component along the out‐of‐plane propagation direction. In this angular range, the TM mode crosses the Γ‐point at the same wavelength at which the split TE resonances are found for angles below 2°. The interaction between TE and TM modes is also evidenced in the linear polarization dissymmetry factor gL plot. Near the Γ‐point, the TM blue mode crosses the in‐plane split red TE‐mode, leading to the close interaction between both orthogonal polarizations near normal incidence (dark arrows close to Γ‐point) [36, 46, 56]. As recently reported, the anti‐crossing points near the origin Γ due to the overlapping of TE and TM modes can originate strong chiral near fields, enabling chiral lasing emission from flat emitting layers [48]. Further FDTD the overlapping of both TE and TM modes at an angle of 1.5° with high quality factors of 580 (see Figure S12), thus leading to enhanced CPL out‐coupling efficiency due to the overlapping of orthogonally polarized modes.

Figure 2b shows the angular dependence of the extinction under circularly polarized light. The measurements show a different angular dispersion for each circular polarization and this is further evidenced by the circular polarization dissymmetry factor gc. In this case, red and blue correspond to LCP and RCP modes, respectively. The angular dispersion of the gc reveals the exchange of energy as the incidence angle sweeps from negative to positive polar angles passing through the Γ‐point. The chiral grating leverages extrinsic chirality encoded in the angular dispersion of its lattice resonances. For oblique incidence, opposite in‐plane wavevectors launch in‐plane propagating modes that couple to resonances of opposite handedness, reflecting the underlying symmetry of the structure. At normal incidence, these counter‐propagating chiral channels cancel out. Therefore, at the Γ‐point, both LCP and RCP modes coincide and leads to the formation of a BIC mode [40, 41]. Consequently, radiative coupling is suppressed and only re‐emerges for small angle deviations (in our case 1.5°) from normal incidence, where the BIC evolve into a qBIC. Confirmation of such BIC mode was verified by FDTD simulations shown in Figure S10, which reveal the characteristic divergence of the quality factor of the modes as a function of the angle of incidence, reaching theoretical values of Q = ∞ at normal incidence [40].

The angular dispersion of the resonant mode at 660 nm in the k‐space is further investigated by collecting the BFP in reflection (Figure 2c) and the PL (Figure 2d). Fourier microscopy allows for full angular characterization of the extinction bands for all incoming and outgoing k‐vectors within the numerical aperture (NA) of the objective. In our case, we used a high NA of 0.75, obtaining information up to a maximum angle of 49° (see Methods).

We characterized the BFP of the chiral array by measuring its reflectance under linearly polarized excitation along the Γ − K direction (aligned with the triskelion array, Figure 2c‐i), linearly polarized excitation along the Γ − M direction (perpendicular to the array, Figure 2c‐ii) and under LCP excitation (Figure 2c‐iii). The resulting reflection bands in k‐space correspond to the lattice resonances identified as dips in the angular transmittance study. The dashed white and yellow lines under LCP illumination are a guide to the eye to identify the intersection points between modes where enhanced PL is expected. The full Fourier plane characterization can be found in Figure S13.

Next, we used BFP microscopy to collect the angular dispersion of the PL from the RhB in all available k‐space with a single laser pulse. Due to reciprocity [21], we observed that the reflectance pattern showed for LCP (Figure 2c‐iii) matched the dispersion of the PL from the dye for RCP polarization (Figure 2d‐i with a clockwise blue arrow). This off‐normal polarized emission could be explained by extrinsic chirality, which relies on oblique angles and the use of either chiral [57] or achiral [9] objects to induce a preferential emission. At higher optical fluences, the PL shows a much brighter emission near the Γ‐point (Figure 2d‐ii). A close inspection near the normal from the sample shows four characteristic spots in k‐space (Figure 2d‐iii) where the TE and TM modes intersect. The crossing between different diffraction modes has already been reported to enhance light extraction and even produce off‐normal directional lasing emission due to higher localization of optical states [58]. In our case, the high directionality and threshold dependence of the emission also suggest a stimulated emission process, which will be characterized in Figure 4. The complexity of our system comprising symmetry groups of C3 for triskelion motif together with C6 hexagonal lattice, leads to a complex k‐space vector polarization map [59, 60], different from the typical doughnut‐like in non‐chiral symmetry‐protected BIC systems [35, 61, 62].

The observation of lasing activity a few degrees off‐normal incidence motivates a more detailed FDTD study to unravel its origin (Figure 3). To do so, we simulated the ballistic transmittance and electric field spatial distribution for each circular polarization at 0° (Figure 3a–c) and 1.5° (Figure 3d–f). As previously discussed, under normal incidence, a double‐peak appears in transmittance around 670 nm and 686 nm, corresponding to the diffractive modes with different effective refractive indices discussed in Figure 1. At normal incidence, the transmittance spectra for LCP and RCP are very similar (Figure 3a), in agreement with the experimental CD. We study the near‐field distribution for each polarization at the lasing wavelength under CPL excitation (see field profiles at XY plane at z = 120 nm, Figure 3b, and YZ‐plane at x = −100 nm, Figure 3c). The field profile in the XY plane (Figure 3b) reveal stronger electric field intensity for LCP light than for RCP. Despite the differences in the in‐coupling efficiency observed and the moderate differences in the near‐field distribution for each circular polarization, the difference in integrated field intensity was less than 0.25E0 2, corresponding to less than a 12% difference, which is not sufficient to explain such different responses for both circular polarizations in the PL.

Instead, the FDTD simulations performed for the oblique incidence case (θ = 1.5° and φ = 90°, Γ − M orientation) displayed in Figure 3d–f show a dramatic difference in the optical response for each circular polarization, particularly at the lasing wavelength of λ = 666 nm (Figure 3d). First, the transmittance drops from 80% for RCP to 40% under LCP excitation, obtaining a clear far‐field effect. Second, the differences in the near‐field distribution become more evident as observed both in XY (Figure 3e, z = 120 nm) and YZ (Figure 3f, x = −50 nm) cross sections. In the XY‐profile, a pair of strong hotspots within the triskelion arms shows enhanced electric fields up to 250‐fold for LCP in‐coupling while completely suppressing the interaction for RCP light. In addition, the wave‐guided near‐field in the YZ profile also showed the same behavior, with a strong out‐of‐plane electric field intensity enhancement only for LCP in the emitting regions. The in‐plane propagation of this mode is confirmed by the standing wave pattern with out‐of‐plane polarization (see Figure S14). The different response of the photonic architecture to LCP and RCP light was also observed when inspecting the integrated electric field intensity along the propagation axis, yielding a difference of up to 60 times. By the reciprocity principle, we assume that this behaviour is also maintained for far‐field coupling efficiencies. Nonetheless, lasing emission is a nonlinear process which would require an active‐system modelling. It is also worth noting the observation of sharp electric field intensity peaks found at each material interface, which act as sources of chiral electromagnetic fields [63]. The remaining characterization of the chiral resonance observed in the transmittance spectrum at 669 nm can be found in Figure S15. Based on these calculations, we demonstrated that the optical resonances supported at less than 2° from the normal, enabled the extraction of CPE with a remarkable degree of circular polarization.

In sum, the titania coated chiral array acts both as a cavity and polarization dependent out‐coupler for the light emitted by the dye. The hybridization of TE and TM collective oscillations of the array near normal incidence enables the exchange of energy between linear modes, transforming the emission to circularly polarized. Finally, the intrinsic chirality from the triskelion motif boosts the out‐coupling efficiency of a single helicity, as observed in the near‐field distributions. This, in turn, results in obtaining a high degree of circular dissymmetry. These numerical results were further tested under experimental conditions, pursuing a highly circularly polarized light emission.

Finally, the characteristics of the lasing emission from the chiral array are presented in Figure 4. Lasing emission is spectrally narrow, highly directional, with a defined polarization state, and requires a minimum energy threshold to overcome losses in achieving population inversion. Here, we started by measuring the behavior of the emitted photoluminescence as a function of the outgoing polarization and the excitation power density (Figure 4a). To do so, a single excitation nanosecond laser pulse at 532 nm was used to excite the PL processes arising from the chiral array. Then, the emitted PL was filtered out by placing a quarter‐wave plate and a linear polarizer to discriminate between the two circular polarization handedness (see Methods section). The fraction of CPE obtained was evaluated via the dissymmetry factor glum, which measures the PL intensities in both LCP and RCP output polarization states. At low pump fluences (<40 mJ/cm2), the PL profile was broad, with enhanced peak emissions due to the boosted extraction efficiency driven by the lattice resonance under Bragg conditions [64]. However, when the pump energy was above the energy threshold, the emission narrowed down to 1.4 nm in FWHM, limited by the spectrometer resolution. Spatial and temporal coherence measurements confirmed the coherence of the emitted radiation from the chiral array (see Figure S23). A comparison between both emission regimes, the broad emission (dark line) and the narrower lasing profile (blue line) obtained at higher pump energies, is shown in Figure 4b. The inset lasing emission shows a multi‐peak lasing behavior at λ1 = 666 nm and λ2 = 670 nm. The first one corresponds to the off‐normal resonant mode discussed in Figure 3, whereas the second one is associated with the lattice resonance at normal incidence, only observed at high energy pump fluences (Figure S16). Unfortunately, the rapid photodegradation at high energy pump fluences limits the ability to measure the output polarization state of this second lasing peak with our current measuring system (see Figure S24). On the other hand, when inspecting the polarization state of the first lasing emission emerging from the R‐triskelion chiral array, we measured a strong preferential RCP emission, as the LCP emission was almost totally hindered at high pump fluences. Control measurements were performed on unpatterned doped photoresist, where no circularly polarized lasing emission was observed (Figure S20).

From the polarization‐resolved measurements, we extracted the peak intensity PL at the lasing emission wavelength for the 100 nm TiO2 R‐triskelion for LCP (red) and RCP (blue) as a function of the input power density (Figure 4c). The results show a clear threshold behavior, increasing the peak intensity one order of magnitude between 20 and 30 mJ/cm2. For all the excitation energies scanned, we observed a larger RCP contribution, also showing a larger energy threshold for the counter circular polarization (between 30 and 40 mJ/cm2). The different thresholds observed for LCP and RCP radiation enables maximizing the DCP for the lasing by adjusting the input power density. Therefore, with a suitable input fluence below 30 mJ/cm2, lasing emission from LCP light is suppressed while it is active for RCP, thus obtaining a strong chiral lasing emission, as will be shown next.

The directionality of the lasing emission of LCP and RCP polarizations for both enantiomeric chiral arrays is shown in Figure 4d. Top and bottom graphs correspond to L‐ and R‐triskelion arrays, while red and blue dots correspond to peak intensities at the wavelength emission for LCP and RCP, respectively. In this case, we collected the lasing emission with a 50 mm lens mounted in a detection arm in a rotary stage with a 1° step resolution. Therefore, the corresponding field of view was a few degrees, thus still collecting some signal when placed at off‐normal incidence. We obtained a preferential emission for RCP close to normal incidence, which was extinguished after a few degrees when rotating the detection arm for the R‐triskelion array (bottom panel in Figure 4d). We observed that LCP polarization was totally suppressed, while RCP was only measured close to normal incidence. The opposite behavior for the enhanced polarization was found for the L‐triskelion measurements, as shown in the top panel of Figure 4d. These measurements confirm the high directionality of the obtained emission, thus reinforcing the chiral lasing observation.

Figure 4e shows the lasing emission in the circular (blue and red spectra in top) and linear (green and dark spectra in bottom) polarization spaces (by either removing or keeping the quarter‐wave plate while maintaining the same excitation power density), respectively, for chiral arrays coated with different titania thicknesses (75 and 100 nm) enabling the tuning of the lasing wavelength. In all cases, both L‐ and R‐triskelion structures were fabricated, which were expected to emit contrary circular polarizations.

The L‐ and R‐triskelion arrays exhibited dominant LCP and RCP lasing at λ = 666 nm, respectively (dark red and blue lines, Figure 4e, first row). The dissymmetry factors reached glum = +1.8 ± 0.1 and glum = −1.93 ± 0.01, corresponding to a DCP of (−0.90 ± 0.12) and (0.97 ± 0.02), respectively. The DCP can be computed by the Stokes parameters and is related to the aforementioned glum as for purely circular light emission (considering S1 = S2 = 0) as: (5) DCP=S3S0=−glum2

The experimental error associated with the computed glum arises from the parasitic current from the spectrometer in dark conditions (shown in Figure S19 and summarized in Table S4). Considering the propagation error formula and the peak PL values obtained for each circular polarization measurement (summarized in Table S5), we can calculate the error in the experimental measurement. The obtained signals confirmed the emission of a pure single‐helicity chiral lasing radiation within the experimental error, as the intensity for the hindered polarization was comparable to that of the dark current noise. Measurements in the linear polarization basis confirmed equal vertical and horizontal contributions (dark grey and green lines, S1 Stokes vector components, Figure 4e, second row), verifying purely circular rather than elliptic emission (the S2 components of the Stokes vector for both 75 nm and 100 nm TiO2 are characterised in Figure S18). The remaining lasing characterization of the 100 nm TiO2 L‐triskelion is provided in Figure S17.

In addition, the TiO2 coating provides spectral tunability as hinted before (Figure 1e; Figure S9). The chiral arrays coated with 75 nm TiO2 coating allow a blue shift of the resonances and hence the lasing emission at λ = 645 nm (full characterization of angular extinction and lasing characteristics can be found in Figures S21 and S22). The lasing emission at λ = 645 nm arising from L‐triskelion and R‐triskelion where also fully polarized, with glum = +1.94 ± 0.01 and glum = ‐1.84 ± 0.02, corresponding to a DCP of (−0.97 ± 0.03) and (0.92 ± 0.04), respectively. In this case, the overlap between the resonant wavelength and the emission band of RhB was larger, and the threshold energy to achieve lasing emission for both polarizations was reduced to near 10 mJ/cm2 (Figure S22). Lowering the threshold energy for lasing activity extended the lifespan of the chiral nanophotonic platform by a factor of 8, maintaining high values of glum after hundreds of measurements, which suggests that the design of better photonic cavities with stable materials could be implemented in the future for practical chiral lasing platforms (Figure S24). Controlling the high refractive index coating thickness thus provides an efficient alternative to tune the lasing wavelength using the same chiral geometry.

Conclusions

We have demonstrated strong chiral lasing emission empowered by the hybridization between diffracted modes supported by a 2D‐chiral array coated with a high refractive index layer. We showed the relevance of TE and TM coupling, which is key for the excitation of chiral qBIC modes. The polarization‐resolved angular dispersion unravelled the nature of the electromagnetic modes both in linear and circular excitations. The chiral grating supports counter‐propagating modes whose interaction is governed by the angular dispersion of its lattice resonances. At normal incidence, these modes merge into a BIC, suppressing radiation, while small angular detunings transform this state into quasi‐BIC resonances. Additional Fourier microscopy characterization showed the close interaction between these orthogonally polarized linear modes at small off‐normal angles (≈1.5°), thus resulting in boosted light extraction efficiency for the qBIC in this direction. FDTD characterization further confirmed that a small oblique incidence angle maximized the differential light extraction efficiency for one of the circular helicities compared to normal incidence, indicating access to a chiral qBIC resonance. Finally, we exploited the different threshold behavior for each circular polarization to achieve lasing with almost complete circular polarization with a glum ≈ 1.94 (97% DCP). Contrary to previous approaches, these results indicate that chiral qBIC resonances can also be accessed in scalable and cost‐efficient photonic architectures where a high refractive index coating enhances chiral lattice resonances. These findings demonstrate a simple and robust route to achieve highly efficient circularly polarized lasing from chiral metasurfaces. Therefore, our work paves the way for compact on‐chip light sources with direct applications in quantum optics, optical communication, and chiral photonics.

Experimental Section

Fabrication of Chiral Arrays

We performed the nanofabrication process of the large‐area chiral architecture using NIL hot‐embossing as reported elsewhere [25, 26]. Briefly, we mixed an epoxy photoresist (SU‐8 2000.5, Microchem) at 5%w with an organic dye (Rhodamine‐B, Sigma Aldrich) in cyclopentanone. The solution was spin‐coated at 3000 rpm on a surface‐activated glass substrate previously cleaned with an oxygen plasma treatment for 5 min. The substrate was left spinning until all the solvent was evaporated, obtaining a 120–130 nm thin film. The substrate was placed for 1 min on a hot plate at 60°C to ensure complete solvent evaporation before the NIL step. Then, a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) stamp with the triskelion hexagonal array engraved as air cavities was gently pressed upon the thin film at room temperature until the presence of air bubbles was avoided. The substrate‐PDMS system was allocated within a compact nanoimprint tool (Compact Nanoimprint Tool CNI, NIL Technology) to control the temperature and pressure applied during printing. The temperature was gradually increased to 90°C. At the printing temperature, a pressure of 2 bar was applied and held for 1 min. Then, the system was left to cool down until 60°C, and finally, the pressure was gradually released for 1 min.

After the imprinting, the substrate‐PDMS was exposed to UV radiation for 30 min to ensure the complete cross‐linking and hardening of the photoresist. Then, the substrate‐PDMS was exposed for 5 min to a high temperature (130°C) to accelerate the hardening. Once the substrate cooled down to room temperature, the PDMS was gently removed, obtaining the hardened dye‐doped triskelion array constituted with a gain medium. Finally, the triskelion array was covered with 100 nm of a high refractive index dielectric material (TiO2) using e‐beam evaporation.

FDTD Simulations

FDTD simulations were performed using commercial software (Lumerical FDTD from ANSYS). The hexagonal SU‐8 triskelion array had a height of 110 nm and arm width of 75 nm, placed upon a flat residual layer of 30 nm. The array was anisotropically coated with 100 ± 5 nm of TiO2 with a vertical to lateral ratio deposition of 2.6 due to the experimental directionality of the deposition method. The optical properties used for both SU‐8/RhB and TiO2 were experimentally characterized using ellipsometry [24, 49]. The geometrical parameters were optimized following an optimization algorithm to minimize the difference between the experimental and the simulated transmittance (see Supporting Information for more details).

The angular transmittance incidence simulations were performed using the Broadband Fixed Angle Source Technique (BFAST) to account for the extra phase delay induced by oblique incidence.

A transmittance monitor was placed within the glass substrate to compute the optical power passing through the architecture. A 3D‐monitor at λ = 666 nm was used to compute the electric near‐field distribution for the different polarization excitations that were used to integrate the intensity along the propagation z‐axis.

Angular Transmittance Characterization

The experimental optical setup was customized and automated using LabVIEW programs. A white light source—specifically a Tungsten Halogen Lamp (Ocean Optics, HL‐2000‐HP)—was used to illuminate the chiral array. The lamp output was spectrally corrected using two optical filters (Edmund Optics SCHOTT BG64 and Thorlabs SRF11) to enhance signal intensity in both the UV and NIR regions. The unpolarized light was directed to a Glan‐Thompson Calcite Polarizer (GTH10M, Thorlabs) mounted on a rotation mount for the linearly polarized characterization. For the circular characterization, an achromatic quarter‐wave plate (AQWP10M‐580, Thorlabs) was placed after the linear polarizer.

The polarized light was focused onto the sample and collected using two achromatic doublet lenses with focal lengths of 100 mm and 50 mm, respectively. The detection system was mounted on a rotational optical breadboard (Thorlabs, RBB12A), while the sample itself was fixed to a 50 mm diameter rotational stage equipped with resonant piezoelectric motors (Thorlabs, ELL18/M), controlled via an interface board and dedicated software. The concentric rotation of the illumination and detection arms enabled automated control of the illumination angle θ, spanned from −30° to +30° in steps of 1°.

The sample was mounted and aligned along the two high‐symmetry directions of the hexagonal array using a custom‐designed holder. Transmitted light was captured using a fiber‐coupled spectrophotometer (Ocean Optics, QEPro‐FL), with a spectral detection range of 380–1100 nm.

Fourier Imaging microscopy

A home‐built Fourier microscope was used to characterize the back focal plane image of the chiral array. A tungsten halogen lamp (Ocean Optics, HL‐2000‐HP) was fiber‐coupled and passed through a wire grid linear polarizer (WP25M‐VIS, Thorlabs) and a quarter‐wave plate (AQWP10M‐580, Thorlabs) to obtain linear and circularly polarized light as excitation source. A 50:50 nonpolarizing beam splitter in reflectance mode was used to redirect the light to a high numerical aperture objective (N40X‐PF, Nikon). The BFP image was projected onto a CCD camera (CS165CU, Thorlabs) using an achromatic lens of focal f = 100 mm. The photoluminescence and lasing measurements were performed by using a fiber‐coupled green 532 nm laser (CryLaS GmbH) and an external trigger to set the number of pulses sent. In this case, the quarter‐wave plate and the polarizer were located between the objective and the camera beam path. A narrow bandpass filter (FBH05660‐10, Thorlabs) centered at 660 nm was used obtain a monochromatic BFP image in both reflectance and photoluminescence modes.

Photoluminescence and Lasing Characterization

The chiral arrays, composed both of a flat layer and a chiral motif of gain media, were optically pumped at normal incidence using a nanosecond‐pulsed green laser (CryLaS GmbH), peaked at 532 nm, and vertically polarized. An Arduino Uno board connected to the laser trigger input was used as an excitation clock source to control the number of pulses and ensure equivalent energy deposition for each polarization. The pump fluence was adjusted manually by rotating a density filter wheel and measuring the output power of the laser using a standard photodiode power sensor (S121C, Thorlabs) coupled to a power meter console (PM100D, Thorlabs).

The circularly polarized lasing measurements were performed by measuring the photoluminescence obtained by sending a single excitation pulse with the same energy for both polarizations. The excitation laser was filtered out using a long‐pass filter (Semrock Edge basic BLP01‐532R‐25). The produced photoluminescence was collected directly by a fiber coupled to a spectrophotometer (Ocean Optics, QEPro‐FL) by using a reflective collimator (Thorlabs, RC08SMA‐P01. The detection arm scanned the emitted light for a given angle to obtain the directionality pattern of the lasing emission.
