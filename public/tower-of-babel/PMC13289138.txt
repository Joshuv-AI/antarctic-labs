Introduction

Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) remains a significant global health challenge, with survival rates and neurological outcomes heavily dependent on timely interventions and optimal post-resuscitation care.1 2 Despite advances in therapeutic strategies, including temperature control and early coronary intervention,3 long-term outcomes in survivors of OHCA remain suboptimal.4 5 There is therefore a pressing need to better understand the factors influencing recovery and to identify interventions that may improve outcome.6

Ventilatory complications have been associated with poor outcomes in this population.7 Sub-analyses from the previous target temperature management (TTM)-2 trial have demonstrated a possible impact of ventilatory settings and airway management on clinical outcomes,8,10 with special emphasis on driving pressure and mechanical power, which represent central determinants of the mechanical stress and strain imposed on the OHCA lung.8 9 11 Both hypo-oxaemia and hyperoxaemia, especially if occurring in the early phases after resuscitation, have shown to be detrimental to outcomes, but specific optimal targets of partial pressure of oxygen and carbon dioxide have not been yet elucidated.9

The Sedation, Temperature and Pressure after Cardiac Arrest and Resuscitation (STEPCARE) trial is a multicentre, randomised controlled study assessing the impact of sedation strategy, mean arterial pressure targets and temperature management on long-term mortality and functional outcomes in this population.12,14

This protocol describes a pre-planned series of sub-analyses within the STEPCARE trial, focused on ventilatory management, respiratory complications, airway management and gas-exchange parameters from hospital admission throughout the intensive care unit (ICU) course in adults resuscitated from OHCA who require invasive mechanical ventilation, and their association with clinical outcomes.

Methods and analysis

Study design

This study consists of a series of preplanned respiratory-focused sub-analyses embedded within the STEPCARE trial. The main trial is a multicentre, randomised controlled study evaluating how sedation strategies, haemodynamic (mean arterial pressure) targets and temperature management relate to 6-month mortality and neurological outcome in patients admitted to ICUs following OHCA. These sub-analyses will use prospectively collected STEPCARE data to describe ventilator settings and gas-exchange profiles from hospital admission through the ICU course and to explore their associations with clinical endpoints (6-month mortality and neurological outcome). This study will follow the the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement: guidelines for reporting observational studies.15

Study population

Criteria for inclusion in the STEPCARE trial are OHCA of non-traumatic origin; a minimum of 20 min without chest compressions; unconsciousness defined as not being able to obey verbal commands (Full Outline of UnResponsiveness (FOUR)-score motor response of <4) or being intubated and sedated because of agitation after sustained return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC); eligible for intensive care without restrictions or limitations; inclusion within 4 hours of ROSC. Exclusion criteria are patients on extracorporeal membrane oxygenation prior to randomisation; pregnancy; suspected or confirmed intracranial haemorrhage; previously randomised in the STEPCARE trial. These pre-planned sub-analyses will include STEPCARE participants receiving invasive mechanical ventilation after ROSC with available respiratory data recorded during ICU stay. Patients who die or are extubated before predefined assessment timepoints will contribute all available respiratory data collected up to the time of death or liberation from mechanical ventilation.

Objectives

This protocol describes a pre-planned respiratory-focused series of sub-analyses within the STEPCARE trial in patients receiving invasive mechanical ventilation after OHCA. The primary objective is to comprehensively describe ventilatory practices—including mode of ventilation, tidal volume (VT), positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP), driving pressure, respiratory rate, ventilatory ratio and mechanical power—alongside respiratory mechanics and gas-exchange indicators such as arterial pH, arterial partial pressure of oxygen (PaO₂), arterial partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO₂) and fluid balance/haemodynamic management.

Data collection and management

Data are collected in an electronic system (Spiral). Pseudonymised data for these sub-analyses will be extracted from the main STEPCARE trial database, which prospectively captures detailed clinical, laboratory and outcome information. Data quality is ensured through predefined monitoring procedures, including double data entry, automated consistency checks and regular audits. Data handling will follow Good Clinical Practice (GCP) guidelines,16 ensuring confidentiality and compliance with ethical standards.17

Definitions of respiratory complications and weaning variables

Pulmonary complications and weaning-related variables will be defined according to predefined criteria whenever possible. Pneumonia and VAP will be identified according to local clinical diagnosis recorded in the STEPCARE database, including radiological findings, microbiological data and antibiotic treatment when available. ARDS will be defined according to the Berlin criteria when sufficient clinical and gas-exchange data are available. Extubation failure will be defined as the need for invasive mechanical ventilation after extubation during the same hospital admission. Weaning success will be defined as liberation from invasive mechanical ventilation without the need for reintubation. Unplanned extubation will include any unintended removal of the endotracheal tube. Chest trauma severity will be based on clinical and radiological findings recorded in the database, including rib fractures, sternal fractures, pneumothorax, haemothorax and pulmonary contusions when available. These variables will be collected from prospectively recorded STEPCARE case report forms and local clinical documentation.

Other collected variables

The dataset will include the following: socio-demographic and baseline data (age, sex, height, weight, ethnicity, comorbidities and the first arterial blood gas after ROSC); cardiac arrest characteristics (prehospital and intrahospital arrest variables, including rhythm of presentation, timing and location, type of arrest, duration and characteristics of resuscitation, ROSC and initial post-arrest clinical presentation (shock, ST-elevation myocardial infarction, etc)); ICU clinical data (temperature; haemodynamics (systolic, diastolic and mean arterial pressure; heart rate); neurological examination limited to pupillary light reflex (yes/no), corneal reflex (present/absent) and motor response from the FOUR score; sedative and vasoactive drug administration (propofol, dexmedetomidine, midazolam, norepinephrine, dobutamine, epinephrine); delirium assessment using Confusion Assessment Method-Intensive Care Unit or Intensive Care Delirium Screening Checklist; Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score; and adverse events); MIRACLE2 score (missed witnesses OHCA, initial rhythm non-shockable, ROSC, changing pupils, age, low pH, epinephrine dose); ventilatory management and respiratory parameters (mode of ventilation and ventilator settings, including VT, PEEP, respiratory rate (RR), fraction of inspired oxygen, plateau pressure (Pplat), peak airway pressure and respiratory system compliance. Arterial blood gases will include PaO₂, PaCO₂, pH, bicarbonate, lactate and base excess). Driving pressure will be calculated as Pplat—PEEP,18 and mechanical power according to previously validated formulas,19 20 given their key role in determining the mechanical load applied to the OHCA lung). Weaning and post-extubation parameters (timing and success of weaning, unplanned extubation, reintubation, days of mechanical ventilation and ventilator-free days); ICU and hospital complications (occurrence of pneumonia, VAP), ARDS, cardiovascular complications, acute kidney injury and reasons for withdrawal of active intensive care. For patients who die in hospital, the presumed cause of death (cardiac, cerebral or other) is recorded); neurological prognostication (clinical neurological examination findings and the blinded examiner’s prognostic judgement, dichotomised as ‘poor outcome likely’ (yes/no) according to predefined protocol criteria); Long-term follow-up (6-month mortality and functional status assessed using the modified Rankin Scale (mRS). The mRS score for functional disability is a 7-category scoring system, ranging from no symptoms (score 0) to patient’s death (score 6), where poor neurological outcome is defined as a score ranging from 4 to 6. Information for the mRS will be obtained at a follow-up, performed as a telephone interview, postal questionnaire or a face-to-face visit., where information for the scoring will be collected from both survivors, caregivers or other sources of information if required. If a full mRS is not possible a dichotomised outcome of good/poor will be collected based on if the survivors is independent or dependent in basic activities of daily living. Statistical analysis

As this is a secondary analysis of a randomised trial and involves a wide range of exposures (eg, ventilatory settings, respiratory complications), no formal a priori sample size calculation was feasible. However, because the study adopts the same primary outcome as the parent STEPCARE trial, the available sample size is inherently secured. With the large enrolment of STEPCARE, the analyzable cohort is expected to provide sufficient precision for adjusted models and secondary analyses. Patient characteristics and management, ventilator parameters, airway care and arterial blood gas values will be described by means ± SD, or medians (IQR) when appropriate. Discrete variables will be summarised as percentages. At baseline, the comparisons of means, medians and frequencies among 6-month survival status will be carried out using t-test, Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test and χ2 test, respectively.

The association between ventilator settings, weaning success, airway care, respiratory complications, arterial blood gases and 6-month mortality and functional outcomes will be determined with Cox regression analysis. Overall, all regression models will be built with variables chosen based on previous knowledge and aims of the study, including different models for basic ventilatory markers, composite ones (co-linear).

The models will be adjusted for STEPCARE randomisation domains, including sedation strategy, mean arterial pressure targets and temperature management interventions, in addition to relevant clinical covariates. Exploratory interaction analyses between respiratory variables and STEPCARE intervention groups may also be performed where appropriate according to the specific respiratory domain and outcome under investigation.

In all models, we will ensure that the continuous exposures meet the linearity assumption using appropriate transformations when necessary. For untransformed variables, risk estimates will be expressed as hazard ratios with 95% CIs. Additional analyses will be performed to ensure that our regression estimates do not differ significantly from models including centre as a cluster variable. Linear mixed regression will be used to compare longitudinal trajectories of repeated respiratory measurements according to survival status and functional outcome. Longitudinal ventilatory and gas-exchange variables may be analysed using repeated-measures and time-dependent modelling approaches, cumulative exposure metrics and trajectory-based analyses where appropriate according to the specific respiratory domain under investigation.

Patients who die or are extubated before predefined assessment timepoints will contribute all available measurements collected up to the time of death or liberation from mechanical ventilation. Sensitivity analyses will be performed to explore the potential impact of informative censoring and missing longitudinal data. Area-under-the-curve (AUC) analyses of selected respiratory variables, including PaCO₂, PaO₂ and ventilatory parameters, may also be performed to evaluate cumulative exposure during the first 72 hours after ICU admission.

Sensitivity analyses will be conducted to assess the robustness of findings, addressing potential biases and missing data through methods such as multiple imputation considering sex, age and specific clinical phenotypes of post-cardiac arrest patients.

Missing data will be handled according to a predefined statistical analysis plan. Multiple imputation may be applied to selected baseline, clinical, ventilatory and gas-exchange variables when appropriate. Longitudinal repeated measurements may also be imputed using longitudinal or multilevel approaches according to the structure of the data and the specific analyses performed. Imputation models will incorporate clinically relevant variables, including demographic characteristics, illness severity, respiratory variables, STEPCARE intervention groups and outcome measures where appropriate. Missing data are expected to be predominantly missing at random, although sensitivity analyses will be performed to assess the robustness of findings under different assumptions.

A two-sided p value of <0.05 will be the threshold used for significance in all analyses.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical considerations

An ethical approval was previously obtained for the STEPCARE study, which was approved by the regional ethics committee at Lund University. Subsequently, ethics applications have been submitted to all relevant ethics boards in every participating centre. Each national coordinator notified the specific ethics committee, in compliance with the local legislation and rules, facilitating the process in each centre of the country. The ethical approval of the protocol and all documents were obtained before any participant was enrolled, and the principal investigator and steering committee of STEPCARE ensured that the study was conducted in full conformity with the Declaration of Helsinki and GCP.16 17 Consent will follow local rules in each country. As these sub-analyses involve no additional interventions or data collection beyond the main trial, it does not require separate patient consent.

Discussion

This pre-planned series of sub-analyses maximise the scientific value of STEPCARE by addressing ventilation strategies, weaning, respiratory complications and gas-exchange in adults resuscitated from OHCA, while exploring their associations with 6-month outcomes. The use of a large, well-characterised, multicentre dataset from a rigorously conducted randomised trial enhances external validity and data quality. Although STEPCARE is interventional, ventilatory management is at clinicians’ discretion across countries and settings, offering a real-world picture of practice variability with standardised data capture.

Prior analyses in related cohorts suggest that lung-protective ventilation is commonly adopted in patients with OHCA (median VT≈7 mL/kg PBW, IQR~6.2–8.5; driving pressure≈12 cmH₂O, IQR~10–15; mechanical power≈16.2 J/min, IQR~12.1–21.8) and frequently associated with functional outcomes. Notably, higher RR, driving pressure and mechanical power have been associated with 6-month mortality and poor neurological outcome.11 Additionally, although changes in respiratory mechanics and intrathoracic pressures after OHCA have been described, no study to date has reported direct transpulmonary pressure monitoring and its prognostic significance in this population.21

The TAME study, which enrolled 1700 patients across 63 ICUs in 17 countries and compared targeted mild hypercapnia with normocapnia, found no significant differences in mortality or neurological outcomes between groups. These results suggest that isolated manipulation of PaCO₂ may have limited impact on long-term recovery after cardiac arrest.22 The Carbon dioxide, Oxygen and Mean arterial pressure After Cardiac Arrest and REsuscitation (COMACARE) pilot trial23 demonstrated that achieving a higher-normal mean arterial pressure after OHCA is feasible but did not improve neurological biomarkers or clinical outcomes. These findings temper support for universally targeting elevated mean arterial pressure levels during post-resuscitation care, emphasising that cerebral perfusion after cardiac arrest depends not only on systemic pressure but also on other key determinants—most notably PaCO₂, a major regulator of cerebral blood flow. In this context, simply increasing mean arterial pressure may be insufficient or even counterproductive if PaCO₂ levels are not simultaneously optimised, as hypocapnia can provoke cerebral vasoconstriction and reduce perfusion, while hypercapnia may exacerbate intracranial hypertension. Adaptive interventions—where mean arterial pressure (MAP) and PaCO₂ are dynamically titrated in response to patient-specific physiological signals—may better address the complex cerebrovascular alterations following cardiac arrest, as the STEPCARE trial will try to verify.12 Complementing these findings, a sub-analysis of the TTM2 trial focusing on oxygen targets (n=1418) demonstrated that both hypoxemia and hyperoxemia were common and independently associated with 6-month mortality, although not with neurological outcome.9 24 The PaO₂ thresholds most strongly linked to mortality were approximately 69 mm Hg for hypoxemia and 195 mm Hg for hyperoxemia, indicating a relatively narrow physiological window within which oxygen tensions may be safer. Notably, cumulative exposure to hyperoxemia (PaO₂-AUC)—particularly during the early phase of mechanical ventilation—showed a significant association with worse outcomes, underscoring the potential vulnerability of the immediate post-resuscitation period. Together, these complementary studies reinforce that post-arrest physiology is highly sensitive to alterations in gas exchange, and that both oxygen and carbon dioxide management may need to be approached in a more integrated, time-dependent manner. Current evidence remains insufficient to define optimal oxygen targets, especially regarding early PaO₂ trajectories and cumulative exposure. This uncertainty provides a strong rationale for new evidence to clarify safe oxygen ranges, timing of exposure and their mechanistic links to neurological recovery and survival in this high-risk population.25

In prehospital settings, endotracheal intubation remains more common than supraglottic airway devices and the literature reports no clear difference in major functional outcomes such as ventilator-free days and neurological recovery between the device types.8 26 27 Although device type alone does not appear to affect survival or neurologic outcome, the choice of airway—and its associated downstream effects such as ventilation duration, risk of VAP or development of ARDS in patients with OHCA—remains under-explored. For instance, airway device type may influence aspiration risk or micro-aspiration leading to VAP.28 Beyond the initial airway strategy, however, patients with OHCA frequently develop secondary pulmonary complications once admitted to the ICU. The incidence of VAP in this population is high—reported in up to 30–40% of mechanically ventilated OHCA survivors—and is consistently associated with prolonged ventilation, longer ICU stay and increased mortality.29 Similarly, ARDS occurs in approximately 10–20% of patients after cardiac arrest, often driven by aspiration, resuscitation-related lung injury, compression-related trauma and high oxygen exposure.30 31 The development of ARDS seems to be linked with worse neurological and survival outcomes.31

Evidence from general ICU populations shows that early mobilisation during mechanical ventilation is both feasible and clinically relevant. In the bi-national TEAM cohort, mobilisation while ventilated was uncommon, and ICU-acquired weakness was frequent and associated with longer ventilation and worse survival. Watanabe et al similarly reported that mobilisation initiated within the first days of ventilation was linked to better functional independence at discharge. Recent international guidelines recommend structured early activity—ideally within 72 hours of mechanical ventilation—and highlight the ICU Mobility Scale as a standardised tool to quantify activity levels.32 33 Although these studies were not specific to OHCA, they provide an important foundation for understanding the interplay between mechanical ventilation, physical recovery and overall ICU trajectory. Weaning in OHCA is frequently complex, and a significant proportion of patients may require tracheostomy when prolonged ventilation is anticipated or when airway protection remains insufficient. In this setting, mobility status—while not directly determining weaning success—may serve as an indicator of overall rehabilitation potential. Incorporating structured mobility assessment into studies of ventilator weaning after OHCA could therefore enhance the characterisation of patient trajectories and help identify those at higher risk of extended ventilatory dependence or tracheostomy.34 35

Strengths of these pre-planned series of sub-analyses include anticipated sample size potentially exceeding 3000 patients, standardised case report forms (CRFs) and definitions, multinational scope and the ability to report findings by randomisation groups of the parent trial (sedation, MAP, temperature), contextualising respiratory care across allocations. Importantly, because these sub-analyses adopt the same primary outcome as the main STEPCARE trial, the sample size is inherently secured, ensuring adequate statistical power for secondary and exploratory analyses. Limitations include the observational nature of these sub-analyses (residual confounding, centre effects), potential misclassification of complications (eg, VAP), missingness in time-varying variables, indication and survivor biases and the inability to infer causality; these will be mitigated in a pre-specified statistical analysis plan (SAP) using adjusted models and sensitivity analyses.

Future collaborative analyses with external datasets may provide opportunities for validation and exploration of respiratory trajectories in larger cohorts.
