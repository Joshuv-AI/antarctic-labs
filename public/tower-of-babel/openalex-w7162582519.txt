A long-term dataset of tropical cyclone radius of maximum wind in
the Western North Pacific Based on a Bayesian consensus of multiple
estimates
Udai Shimada1,2

· Taiga Tsukada3

Received: 2 June 2026 / Accepted: 18 September 2026
© The Author(s) 2026

Abstract
The radius of maximum wind (RMW) is one of the key parameters for characterizing the structure of tropical cyclones
(TCs). Although multiple methods have been developed to estimate the RMW, their estimates can sometimes differ substantially and are not always available, making it difficult to construct a comprehensive long-term RMW dataset. This
study develops a Bayesian consensus method to derive a single RMW estimate from multiple RMW estimates. In this
framework, the posterior probability distribution of the RMW is estimated using likelihoods constructed from methodspecific error distributions combined with an outlier distribution, and the final RMW is determined as the maximum a
posteriori (MAP) estimate. By explicitly accounting for an outlier distribution for each estimation method, the proposed
method can determine a reasonable RMW close to one of the clusters even when the estimates split into two clusters.
Using this consensus method and, as a primary source, estimates from satellite-based global precipitation data and from
infrared and microwave satellite data for TCs with an eye, a long-term RMW dataset is constructed at 3-h intervals for
631 TCs in the western North Pacific during 2000–2025. Verification of the consensus RMW estimates shows that errors
decrease with increasing TC intensity and posterior probability, and that posterior probability itself serves as an indicator
of estimate reliability. This new RMW dataset enables a realistic representation of the inner-core structure, particularly
small RMWs, which were difficult to estimate before the advent of synthetic aperture radar (SAR)-derived winds and in
the absence of aircraft observations.
Keywords Tropical cyclones · Radius of maximum wind · Long-term dataset · Satellite observation

1 Introduction
The radius of maximum wind (RMW) is a key parameter for
characterizing the wind structure of tropical cyclones (TCs),
together with the maximum wind speed (Vmax) and outer
size, as these parameters are physically related (Emanuel
and Rotunno 2011; Chavas and Lin 2016). The magnitude

of TC intensity and structure (e.g., Yamaguchi et al. 2025;
DeMaria et al. 2025). Such weaknesses can be mitigated

is available. Given the importance of the RMW, a World
Meteorological Organization (WMO workshop has called
for the development of methods to construct more accurate
RMW datasets (e.g., Rogers 2023; Ito et al. 2025).
Recently, new RMW estimation methods have been developed, and multiple RMW estimates have become available
at the same analysis time. One reason for this progress is the
availability of accurate RMWs from spaceborne synthetic
aperture radar (SAR) observations, which provide detailed
TC wind fields with a high spatial resolution (~ 500 m to
3 km), although the observation frequency with full TC coverage is still low (Mouche et al. 2017, 2019, 2026; Combot
et al. 2020; Vinour et al. 2021; Shimada et al. 2024; Tsukada et al. 2025; Avenas et al. 2026). Methods developed
using SAR-derived RMWs as ground truth include those
based on outer wind radii and eye size derived from infrared and passive microwave satellite observations (Avenas
et al. 2023; Tsukada and Horinouchi 2023; Shimada 2025).
In addition to these new approaches, previously developed
methods (Willoughby and Rahn 2004; Mueller et al. 2006;
Kossin et al. 2007; Vickery and Wadhera 2008; Lajoie and
Walsh 2008; Knaff et al. 2015; Chavas and Knaff 2022), as
well as recent machine-learning-based methods (e.g., Zhuo
and Tan 2021, 2023; Lu et al. 2022), are also applicable for
estimating the RMW. However, RMW estimates can differ
substantially among methods, their time series may fluctuate irregularly, and some methods do not always provide
estimates due to limited data availability.
Although many RMW estimation methods have been
developed, to our knowledge, a long-term RMW dataset
extending back several decades, with homogeneous quality
throughout the entire period, has not yet been constructed.
To achieve this, a consensus method is required to derive a
single representative RMW from multiple RMW estimates.
Such a consensus RMW cannot be obtained by a simple
arithmetic average of the estimates. For example, an arithmetic average is inappropriate in cases of TCs with concentric eyewalls, where one method estimates the RMW near
the inner eyewall while another estimates it near the outer
eyewall. Additionally, ideal RMW estimates intended for
diverse applications should (i) evolve smoothly over time to
represent the scale of the TC inner core, (ii) capture variations associated with eyewall replacement cycles, (iii) be
continuously available throughout the TC life cycle, and (iv)
maintain homogeneous data quality across the entire period.
The purpose of this study is to develop a consensus
method of RMW estimation based on a Bayesian framework and to construct a long-term RMW dataset. Specifically, the posterior probability distribution of the RMW is
estimated using likelihoods constructed from method-specific error distributions combined with an outlier distribution, and the final RMW is determined as the maximum a

posteriori (MAP) estimate. By explicitly accounting for an
outlier distribution for each method, the posterior distribution can exhibit multimodality. This approach can determine
a reasonable RMW close to one of the clusters even when
the estimates split into two distinct clusters, rather than
yielding an intermediate value between them.
For the construction of the long-term RMW dataset, this
study targets 631 TCs that occurred in the western North
Pacific from 2000 to 2025, with a temporal resolution of
3 h. To ensure homogeneous quality throughout the period,
RMWs are estimated using only observations that have
been continuously available since 2000. Additionally, new
estimation methods are developed using global satellitederived precipitation data (Global Satellite Mapping of Precipitation, GSMaP; Kubota et al. 2007; Kubota et al. 2009)
and atmospheric reanalysis data [the European Centre for
Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) fifth-generation reanalysis product, ERA5; Hersbach et al. 2020] to
provide RMW estimates at all analysis times and to suppress irregular temporal fluctuations. The proposed consensus method selectively determines an RMW value closest
to the most accurate estimate among the available methods.
In this study, the RMW is defined as the radius of maximum azimuthally averaged wind, rather than the radius of
the local maximum wind speed (hereafter, the local RMW),
following previous studies (e.g., Vinour et al. 2021; Avenas
et al. 2023; Tsukada and Horinouchi 2023; Shimada 2025).
This definition allows the RMW to represent the horizontal scale of the inner-core region of large positive vorticity in the Northern Hemisphere, as the positive vorticity in
a modified Rankine vortex decreases rapidly outside the
RMW. Additionally, this definition reduces sensitivity to
local maxima caused by instantaneous gusty wind speeds
and is therefore expected to yield more stable RMWs when
satellite observations are used as ground truth. One caveat
is that a relatively large difference may exist between the
local RMW and the RMW defined in this study for TCs with
elliptical eyewalls or concentric eyewalls.

describes the data used in this study. Section 3 presents the
RMW estimation methods used in the consensus. Section 4
describes the consensus method. Section 5 presents the
verification results of the consensus, including case studies.
Section 6 presents the long-term RMW dataset. Section 7

2 Data
In this study, best track Vmax data from the Japan Meteorological Agency (JMA) are used, except in Sect. 6. Because
JMA Vmax is not provided during the tropical depression

(TD) stage, it is extrapolated assuming a linear inverse relationship with the central pressure available during the TD

TD stage. In Sect. 6, Vmax and RMW data from the Joint

The following datasets are used for RMW estimation:
hourly GSMaP data with a spatial resolution of 0.1°, ERA5
10-m wind data with a spatial resolution of 0.25° at 3-h
intervals, intercalibrated passive microwave (PMW) satellite data at 85–91 GHz, and hourly geostationary infrared
(IR) satellite observations. GSMaP is a multi-satellite global
precipitation product that uses observations from various
satellite sensors, including the 85–91-GHz, 37-GHz, and
other channels of PMW imagers, PMW sounders, and geostationary IR imagers (JAXA 2026). The GSMaP data are
mapped onto TC-centered polar coordinates and averaged
at 3-h intervals using seven hourly values centered on each
analysis time (from −3 to +3 h) to mitigate differences in
precipitation estimates among satellite sensors, following
Shimada et al. (2017). The TC center position at each time is
derived from hourly interpolated best track data provided by
the JMA. Details of GSMaP data processing and its applicability to TCs are described in Shimada et al. (2017, 2018).
The TC center in the ERA5 data is identified as the location
of the minimum in the smoothed ERA5 mean sea level pressure field, following Aizawa et al. (2024). The ERA5 10-m
wind fields are then interpolated onto TC-centered polar
coordinates. The PMW satellite data are obtained from the
TC PRIMED dataset (Tropical Cyclone Precipitation, Infrared, Microwave, and Environmental Dataset; Razin et al.
2023b). This dataset includes observations from the Special Sensor Microwave Imager (SSM/I), the Special Sensor
Microwave Imager/Sounder (SSMIS), the Tropical Rainfall
Measuring Mission (TRMM) Microwave Imager (TMI), the
Advanced Microwave Scanning Radiometer for the Earth
Observing System (AMSR-E), the Advanced Microwave
Scanning Radiometer 2 (AMSR2), and the Global Precipitation Measurement (GPM) Microwave Imager (GMI).
Geostationary IR satellite observations are from the JMA
(e.g., Bessho et al. 2016). To ensure consistency in horizontal resolution among successive satellites, the IR data are
resampled to a spatial resolution of 5 km in this study.
This study uses SAR wind data and Advanced Scatterometer (ASCAT) wind data as reference datasets for the
development of RMW estimation methods and for the verification of RMW estimates. Unless otherwise noted, SAR/
ASCAT data acquired within ± 1.5 h of each 3-hourly analysis time are used for these purposes. The SAR wind data
are used for TCs at TS intensity or higher because only a
few SAR samples are available for TD cases. Accordingly,

the radius of the local maximum ASCAT wind (i.e., the local
RMW) is adopted as the RMW when the azimuthally averaged RMW is less than 150 km, because the two RMW estimates are generally consistent within this range except for
such problematic cases.

a consensus member, if available. Consequently, the same

as determined empirically, enabling the RMW to evolve

using matchup samples with SAR observations. Since SARderived RMWs during the period 2015–2024 are used to
develop the individual RMW estimation methods in Sect. 3,
the matchup samples are divided into dependent samples
from 2015–2024 and independent samples from 2025. Here,
SAR scenes with concentric eyewalls are included. Two
case studies are then presented.
Figures 9a and b show the bias of the consensus RMW
estimates as a function of JMA Vmax and peak posterior
probability, respectively. There is little difference in the
error distributions between dependent and independent
samples. Errors decrease with increasing Vmax and with
increasing posterior probability. Weak TCs tend to have
poorly defined structures, making RMW estimation difficult
and causing larger estimation errors for Vmax < 35 m s–1.
Additionally, seven of the nine samples with Vmax > 40 m
s–1 and large errors (> 30 km) are associated with concentric
eyewalls. For such cases, large errors can occur if the estimated RMW is associated with the inner eyewall while the
true RMW corresponds to the outer eyewall, or vice versa. A
more distinct relationship is found between the error and the
posterior probability, with errors being smaller for posterior probabilities > 0.04. Therefore, the posterior probability
itself can serve as an indicator of the reliability of the RMW
estimates.
The MAE calculated using both dependent and independent samples is 13.6 km (Fig. 9c). Although the errors are
widely distributed, 62% of the samples have absolute errors
of ≤ 10 km (not shown). A negative bias is evident, particularly for 25 m s–1 ≤ Vmax < 40 m s–1 (Fig. 9d) and for posterior
probabilities < 0.045 (Fig. 9e). This negative bias is mainly
+

GSMaP : a

and outliers. Consequently, the negative bias and outliers
remain in the consensus estimates, resulting in an overall
negative bias. This issue will be addressed in future work as
more SAR samples for weak TCs become available.

Given the importance of RMW information for a wide
range of applications, there is a need to develop new techniques and to construct a long-term, homogeneous RMW
dataset (Rogers 2023; Ito et al. 2025). Although multiple
RMW estimates using different methods have been available for the same analysis time based on previous studies,
no consensus method suitable for RMW estimation has been
developed to obtain a single RMW estimate from multiple
estimates.
To this end, this study develops a consensus method
based on a Bayesian framework. In the consensus approach,
the posterior probability distribution of the RMW given
multiple RMW estimates is estimated using likelihoods

13

38

Page 16 of 19

Journal of the Meteorological Society of Japan

constructed from method-specific error distributions combined with an outlier distribution, and the final RMW is
determined as the MAP estimate. By explicitly accounting for an outlier distribution, the consensus method can
appropriately determine a reasonable RMW close to one of
the clusters even when the estimates split into two distinct
clusters.
Using this method, we construct a long-term RMW dataset at 3-h intervals for 631 TCs in the western North Pacific
during 2000–2025. As consensus members, the input estimates include those from methods using a satellite-based
+
TD
global precipitation dataset (𝑅𝑅𝑅𝑅𝑅𝑅 TS
GSMaP and 𝑅𝑅𝑅𝑅𝑅𝑅 GSMaP ),
methods based on IR and PMW satellite data when an eye
is observed (𝑅𝑅𝑅𝑅𝑅𝑅 IR and 𝑅𝑅𝑅𝑅𝑅𝑅 PMW ), a modified Rankine
+
vortex method (𝑅𝑅𝑅𝑅𝑅𝑅 TS
Rankine ), and an ERA5-based method
(𝑅𝑅𝑅𝑅𝑅𝑅 TD
ERA5 ). Verification of the consensus RMW estimates
shows that errors decrease with increasing TC intensity
and posterior probability, and that the posterior probability
itself serves as an indicator of estimate reliability. This new
RMW dataset enables a more realistic representation of the
inner-core structure, particularly for small RMWs that were
difficult to estimate without aircraft observations before the
advent of SAR-derived winds. However, developing a more
robust RMW estimation method for weak TCs remains an
important future task.
Because the proposed Bayesian consensus method allows
the incorporation of any available estimates with their associated error characteristics, observation-derived RMWs,
such as those obtained from SAR, ASCAT, and L-band radiometers, can also be included in the consensus framework,
in addition to the RMW estimates used in this study, if longterm homogeneity of the final RMW estimates is not strictly
required. Incorporating such additional RMWs would likely
further reduce the overall estimation error compared with
that of the dataset constructed in this study and would be
useful for operational applications. Although this study
focuses on TCs in the western North Pacific, the proposed
framework can be readily applied to TCs worldwide. This
extension is left for future work. The proposed framework
provides a practical basis for improving RMW estimation
and supporting both research and operational applications.
