Abstract

 The search for neutrinoless double-beta decay (0nbb) explores new physics by
directly probing the unknown mass scale and possible Majorana nature of the
neutrino. The nEXO experiment, one of the two leading proposed ton-scale projects in the
US, has a projected sensitivity to the 136Xe 0nbb half-life of 1028 years. The interpretation of
possible signals in this next generation of experiments would be complicated by significant
variations in theoretical calculations of the nuclear matrix elements (NMEs) of the decay,
and nuclear structure measurements testing those theories can help address that
uncertainty.

 We have 136Xe(p,n)136Cs reaction at TUNL to deduce the level-scheme of states in
136
 Cs through which the lowest-lying 1+ state decays. We find that over 99% of such
decays will proceed through at least one isomeric state with a lifetime of order 100ns,
which would enable large xenon detectors to employ a delayed-coincidence technique to
search for charge-exchange processes including solar neutrino interactions. We have
carried out measurements at TUNL of the 134Xe(3He,n)136Ba and 136Xe(3He,n)138Ba reactions
to low-lying 0+ states in the residuals to probe the BCS assumption for QRPA NME
calculations for the initial and final nuclei in 136Xe 0nbb. While the analyses of these
reactions is not yet complete and is ongoing, our initial results indicate tension with the
BCS assumption. We have assembled a thermosyphon cooling R&D system for nEXO
which has demonstrated up to 500 W of cooling in vacuum. We have developed a simple
thermosyphon cooling simulation for nEXO and planned laboratory tests with the R&D
system to benchmark it.

Introduction

 The 2015 Nobel Prize in physics was awarded for the discovery that neutrinos,
ghostly weakly-interacting fundamental particles long thought to be massless, oscillate
between flavor states and therefore actually are massive. Neutrino oscillation
measurements have determined the mass squared differences, but not the individual
masses. Possible mechanisms to incorporate neutrino mass into the Standard Model
would make neutrinos the first known fundamental Majorana fermions, that is their own
antiparticles. This in turn could provide a necessary component for understanding the
matter-antimatter asymmetry in the universe by allowing for lepton number non-
conservation [1].

 The search for neutrinoless double-beta decay (0nbb) in atomic nuclei addresses
all these questions. Conventional two-neutrino double beta decay (2nbb), a rare second-
order process in which two neutrons are simultaneously converted into two protons, two
electrons, and two electron antineutrinos are emitted, has been observed in a range of
nuclei for which single-beta decay is energetically or otherwise forbidden. The hypothetical
zero-neutrino mode, in which no neutrinos are emitted, would be experimentally

distinguishable from the two-neutrino mode by the energy signature of the decay. The zero-
neutrino mode would appear as a single peak in the beta energy spectrum at the decay Q-
value, while the two-neutrino mode, in which the undetected neutrinos carry away a
fraction of the energy, appears as a continuous spectrum up to the Q-value.

 Neutrinoless double-beta decay is a lepton non-conserving process, and its
observation would immediately demonstrate that Majorana neutrinos exist. As such, it is a
sensitive probe for new physics. Further, the measurement of the decay half-life would
provide information on the absolute neutrino masses under certain assumptions about the
decay mechanism. Typically, the decay is assumed to proceed by exchange of a light
Majorana neutrino, in which case oscillation experiments indicate that mass hierarchy
may be degenerate (200 meV to 1 eV), inverted (20 to 50 meV), or normal (less than 10
meV). Present limits on 0nbb based on searches in 76Ge, 130Te, and 136Xe have sensitivities
to the half-life of about 1025 years, allowing them to probe the degenerate mass hierarchy.

 Physicists around the world are now designing the next generation of experiments
using tons of source isotope with predicted sensitivities of 1028 years to explore the
inverted hierarchy. In the US, DOE Nuclear Physics formally established mission need
(CD-0) for such a ton-scale experiment in November 2018. The Portfolio Review exercise in
Summer 2021 identified LEGEND and nEXO experiments as the two top-rated candidates
for the project. The nEXO concept incorporates 5 tons of liquid xenon enriched in isotope
136 in a time-projection chamber (TPC). This concept, demonstrated in the forerunner
EXO-200 experiment, combines monolithic self-shielding, topological background
discrimination, and low intrinsic radioactivity to maximize 0nbb sensitivity. R&D toward
this concept includes TPC internals, photodetectors, readout electronics, high-voltage
performance, detector calibration, fluids and cryogenics, vessels, radiopurity, and
simulations.

 A handful of theoretical techniques are commonly used to calculate the nuclear
matrix elements for double-beta decay, and the results vary by a factor of several, leading
immediately to uncertainties in the inferred neutrino masses [2]. These calculations must
contend with complex nuclear physics, including the structure of the final and initial
nuclear states, virtual excitations associated with the structure of the intermediate
nucleus, and pairing between nucleons. Experimental nuclear structure and reaction
measurements can help to better understand these issues. For example, two-nucleon
transfer reactions can test the Bardeen-Cooper-Schrieffer (BCS) approximation used in
Quasiparticle Random Phase Approximation (QRPA) calculations to treat the pairing of like
nucleons [3].

 Another related nuclear structure question is the structure of 136Cs, the virtual
intermediate nucleus in 136Xe double-beta decay. There was almost no experimental
information on its excited states before the measurements of Puppe et al. [4], published in
2011. Those authors used the (3He,t) charge-exchange reaction on 136Xe at intermediate
energies and forward angles to measure the Gamow-Teller (GT) transition strengths to the

1+ states in 136Cs relevant to the 2νββ decay mode, and their measurements still represent
the bulk of the experimentally-known levels below about 3 MeV [5]. Theoretical
calculations of the excited states have recently been published using the shell model [6]
and QRPA [7] methods employed for the calculation of double-beta decay nuclear matrix
elements and compared by those authors to experiment. These calculations show
numerous unobserved lower-spin states below 3 MeV excitation energy.

 The excited states of 136Cs are also relevant to the possible detection of other
charge-current processes in tonne-scale low-background xenon detectors like nEXO. Both
fermionic dark matter absorption [8] and low-energy solar neutrinos [6] interactions would
lead to 1+ states in the residual 136Cs, with the most strength expected in the transition to
the lowest-energy one experimentally observed at 590 keV [4]. Experimental attempts at
detecting either process would require information on the subsequent gamma-decay of
the excited state in order to understand the event topology and corresponding detector
response. For example, shell model calculations [6] indicate the decay of the 590 keV 1+
state would nearly always proceed through a state predicted to have a long enough lifetime
(624 microseconds) to provide a delayed coincidence tag. The decay topology, however,
would depend critically on the ordering of 136Cs states, and the authors indicate the need
for experimental study with direct measurement of the relaxation gammas following
charge-exchange reactions.

 This project addresses the possibility of Majorana neutrinos and related questions
with three distinct research Tasks. In the first, measurement at TUNL of 134Xe(3He,n)136Ba
transitions to low-lying 0+ states in the residual tests the BCS assumption for QRPA NME
calculations. The second Task is a search for excited states in the 136Cs intermediate
nucleus, for which experimental information is limited, via the 136Xe(p,n)136Cs reaction at
TUNL. The subsequent gamma decays, especially of the lowest 1+ state, are of particular
interest in estimating the sensitivity of large liquid xenon experiments like nEXO to charge-
current processes including low-energy solar neutrino interactions. The third is R&D on the
use of thermosyphons for cryostat cooling of nEXO using a test system assembled at
UNCW.

Task 1: Test the BCS Approximation for 136Xe double-beta decay

 Professor Calvin Howell’s group at Duke University has begun a program of (3He,n)
measurements on double-beta decay relevant nuclei at TUNL. These two-proton transfer
reactions test the BCS assumption for the ground states of the parent and daughter nuclei
in QRPA NME calculations. If the BCS approximation is valid, the majority of the strength in
L=0 transfers will be to the ground state of the residual nucleus with only a few % of the
transfers going to excited 0+ states [3]. The data collection for 74,76Ge [25] and 128,130Te [24]
required building and commissioning several new hardware systems, including a beam
chopper for the 3He beam, a 3He recirculation system for the beam source, as well as a
target chamber with a 13 m neutron flight path to an array of liquid scintillator detectors to

measure the angular distribution of the scattered neutrons in 3° increments between 0°
and 18°. Their Ge measurements had superior signal-to-background compared to previous
measurements, with enough significance to measure the cross-section for the transition to
the first excited state.

 The PI and undergraduate students in his group collaborated with Professor Howell
and other TUNL faculty to extend these (3He,n) measurements to 136,134Xe targets. The
UNCW group led the design and build the target cells and high purity gas handling system
for the isotopically enriched target gas. The gas-handling system comprises high-purity
valves, gas supply, a cryopump bottle for gas recovery, a turbo and scroll pumps for
vacuum, and an RGA for leak checking and gas purity monitoring (see Figure 1, left). All
fittings are VCR. Orbital welding on stainless-steel tubing was performed at TUNL. The gas-
handling system is mounted on a mobile cart with the idea that it can support other future
measurements with rare or enriched gas and brought back and forth to TUNL as needed.
The target cells were Al cylinders 0.5” in diameter and 2” long and screwed onto rods
mounted through the scattering chamber top and bottom lids (see Figure 1, right). The cells
included two 0.25” bores, one for containing the target gas and the other for beam tuning.
The gas volume included a slot for outgoing neutrons and elastically scattered 3He. The gas
was contained by 2.5 micron thickness Ta foils epoxied over the entrance and exit windows
and was introduced into the cell via a 1/16” diameter Cu pinch-off tube which was then
sealed. The gas pressure varies somewhat between cells but is generally near 16 psi.

Figure 1. Left: The UNCW high-purity gas handling system, shown without the RGA or gas
supply bottle mounted. Right: A filled target cell mounted in the (3He,n) chamber.

 Three data collection runs were completed at TUNL using cells filled with 40Ar, 136Xe,
and Xe, respectively. The Ar run served to commission the experiment. Analysis of this
 134

run, described below, confirmed that the target cell, as intended, has only a small impact
on the TOF resolution and did not introduce excessive background from the entrance and
exit foils. The 3-4 ns timing resolution (Figure 2, left) was dominated by the beam timing
structure. Measured cross-sections to the 42Ca ground state indicated good agreement
with previous measurements.

 Joanna Wuko completed the 40Ar analysis for her 2021 UNCW senior thesis, while
Duke graduate student Jay Runge analyzed the 134,136Xe data for his TUNL PhD thesis,
defended in 2023 [30]. Both analyses generated time-of-flight (TOF) spectra for the
outgoing neutrons after implementing pulse-shape discrimination (PSD) cuts to exclude
gamma rays. Peak models were fit for transitions to low-lying 0+ and 2+ states in the
residual nucleus. These models incorporated information on the pulsed beam timing
structure gleaned from the gamma-ray TOF peak. In all datasets, resolving transitions to
the low-lying excited states from ground-state transitions was challenging.

 The Ar analysis followed the method used in the earlier Ge and Te analyses, carrying
out individual binned fits to the spectra at each angle by chi-squared minimization in the
ROOT analysis package. Spectra taken with empty target cells were used to subtract
backgrounds associated with the cell foils or other components. The gamma-ray peak was
fit with a combination of Gaussians to model the timing structure. To account for
broadening in the neutron TOF spectrum caused by straggling of the 3He beam in the gas
target, an additional broadening parameter, common to all peaks, was added to the fit
model. The spectra were dominated by ground state transitions, with the resulting cross-
sections in good agreement with previous measurements at the two lower energies. The
timing resolution was such that distinguishing between the first 0+ excited state and two
nearby 2+ states was not possible in each spectrum, so a single representative peak was
fit to that collection. The resulting angular distribution (see Figure 2, right) qualitatively
seems dominated by 2+ strength, indicating a small ratio of the lowest 0+ excited state to
the ground state.

Figure 2: Results for 40Ar(3He,n) at 11.5 MeV. Left: Fit to the 0° neutron TOF spectrum, showing the
  ground state transition and excited states. The x-axis units are uncalibrated TDC counts. Right:
 Cross-section angular distributions for the ground-state (blue) and low-lying excited states (green
 and pink).

 The Xe datasets were analyzed using an unbinned maximum likelihood fit carried
out simultaneously for all angles in RooFit. See Figure 3 for example results. Probability
distribution functions (PDFs) were constructed for neutron peaks from sampling of the
gamma peak with Gaussian smearing. Empty cell data were sampled for the background

PDF, while one for compound-nuclear background transitions was generated using TALYS
[29]. The ground-state cross-sections for 136Xe were consistent with previous
measurements [31]. The resulting angular distributions showed the expected strong 0-
degree peak for 0+ states and a more backward peak for 2+ states. The ratio of the
summed cross-sections for transitions to 0+ excited states below 3 MeV to the cross-
section for transitions to the ground state were 0.12 for 134Xe and preliminarily 0.7 for 136Xe
at zero degrees, both in excess of the ~4/A predicted by the BCS theory, indicating
weakness of that assumption for the nuclei 134Xe, 136Ba, 136Xe, and 138Ba.

Figure 3: Results from the 134Xe analysis [30]. Left: TOF spectrum and fit. Right: Angular distribution
 for cross-section for ground-state transition in relative units.

 Angular distributions from theoretical calculations are needed for all three
reactions to extract quantitative results for the ratios of transitions to excited states vs
ground state. It is the shapes vs angle that are needed in order to distinguish the
contributions from transitions to closely-space excited states of those spin/parities to the
experimental angular distributions. Common practice in the literature [20][31] is to use
Distorted Wave Born Approximation (DWBA) calculations with appropriate optical model
and wavefunction descriptions to analyze (3He,n) reactions. The PI has conferred with
TUNL colleagues with experience in these calculations and is familiarizing himself with the
TALYS and FRESCO codes they have employed. In the meantime, the ongoing beam
source upgrade at TUNL is expected to result in a 1 ns pulsed beam timing resolution,
which would open the possibility of improved data collection.

Task 2: Measurement of excited states and their decay radiation in 136Cs

 The TUNL experiment was conducted using the same pulsed beam system, target
chamber, and enriched gas as the (3He,n) measurements described above. A pulsed 7 MeV
proton beam was used to initiate the 136Xe(p,n)136Cs reaction to reach excited states in
136
 Cs. The target cell design was based on the cell used for the (3He,n) work, with
modifications including the use of PEN foils instead of Ta ones to reduce x-ray and neutron
backgrounds. Miniature pressure transducers were incorporated into the cell to help

monitor target thickness. The PI and his students assembled, tested, filled, and installed
the targets for the experiment.

 Gamma rays were detected by a combination of coaxial and planar Ge detectors
(see Figure 4, left) in fixed positions, and gamma-gamma coincidences were used to
reconstruct the decay scheme of lowest 1+ state (right), including level energies and
branching ratios. While several possible decay sequences were found, virtually all decays
(99%) proceed through at least one of two low-lying metastable states with lifetimes of
~100 ns. The measurement was reported in PRL [28] and was selected as a DOE NP
Highlight (1/16/24). Our level energies agreed with those reported simultaneously by
Reibeiro et al. [27], while having a factor-of-10 smaller uncertainties. Their spin-parity
assignment (3+) for the metastable state at 140 keV disagrees with the shell model
calculations of [6] (2+) and is in tension with our decay scheme, for example being fed by
the 590 keV 1+ state and feeding the 3+ metastable state at 74 keV. We did not directly
determine the multipolarity of the radiation, nor did we measure internal conversion rates.

Figure 4: Left: Experimental setup for the (p,n) TUNL measurement showing four Ge detectors
arrayed around the target cell in the open target chamber. Right: The decay scheme determined for
the 589 keV 1+ state in 136Cs, compared to shell model and literature spectra.

Task 3: nEXO R&D

 The current baseline for nEXO cryostat cooling is recirculation of the cryostat HFE to
an external heat exchanger [26]. This arrangement is expected to be advantageous for
efficiently cooling nEXO down from room temperature but introduces significant
complexity relative to the state HFE bath used in the forerunner EXO-200 experiment. The
EXO-200 scheme, in which the cryostat itself was cooled by refrigerators, would require
routing the refrigerant a distance of ~10 meters. Large liquid xenon dark matter
experiments [16-18] have employed thermosyphons to provide cooling over similar
distances. One important difference between those applications and nEXO is that latter

would require heat exchangers cooling the thermal bath containing the liquid xenon vessel
rather than cooling the vessel directly. Coupling those heat exchangers directly to the
cryostat, as in EXO-200, would not be possible, since the nEXO cryostat is
currently expected to be made of nickel instead of copper.

 Development of a static cooling concept, envisioned to supply ~1 kW total cooling
to maintain temperature setpoint, will require optimization of the design, number,
arrangement, and power of the individual thermosyphons in the system in addition to heat
exchanger and feedthroughs they would couple to. As part of this R&D effort, the PI has
worked with undergraduate students to assemble a thermosyphon at UNCW as shown in
Figure 5. The device is patterned after one developed for the LUX experiment [19]. In this
test system, approximately 1 meter of looped 3/8” tubing connects a condenser immersed
in LN2 to an evaporator. Nitrogen in the loop serves as a process fluid, with condensed
liquid travelling down to the evaporator under the influence of gravity, and boiled gas
travelling back up to the condenser to complete the cycle. The cooling power is controlled
by adding or removing nitrogen to or from the loop via a computer-controlled gas handling
panel incorporating a mass flow controller (MFC). The evaporator is equipped both with a
PID-controlled trim heater and a ~500 W load heater for testing. Automation for the system
is based around Siemens PLCs and Ignition SCADA used by the LZ experiment. The
thermosyphon system is complete except for automation of the LN2 condenser filling and
installation of a redundant PLC bank. The dimensions of the condenser are scaled down
from those of the LUX R&D system in order to fit into the PI’s laboratory, which is not a high
bay.

 Figure 5: Overview of Thermosyphon showing completed plumbing (left), condenser (top middle),
 evaporator (top right), and gas handling panel and slow control (lower right).

 Initial operation found a maximum cooling power of 200 W at base temperature,
limited by the condenser efficiency and gas panel maximum pressure. This can be
compared to the LUX R&D unit which had a maximum power of about 400 W, since the
surface area of the condenser for that system was larger by about the same factor. The
trend of cooling power vs nitrogen mass is about 1.8 W/SL at 165 K (see Figure 6), near the
typical operating temperature for liquid xenon experiments. Analysis of conditions in the
evaporator and condenser confirms that LN2 nucleate boiling occurs in the evaporator at
low temperature while the 165 K tests correspond to less-efficient film boiling.

 Figure 6: Thermosyphon cooling power vs N2 mass at 165K.

 Operational experience with the thermosyphon has been used to consider potential
upgrades. Temperature rate-of-rise was used to estimate the radiative heat load on the
evaporator at no more than 5 W. The total power in LN2 consumption (28 W) agreed with
the combined loads on the evaporator and condenser to within a few Watts when holding
base temperature with no applied heat load, indicating no significant additional heat load
on the transport tubing, so not radiative insulation was installed. Increased cooling power,
up to 480 W from 200 W and comparable to the LUX prototype, was demonstrated by
extending the condenser tube a factor of 3x into the LN2 bath (see Figure 7). This came at
the cost of requiring the LN2 to be supplied continuously at a low rate in order to keep the
condenser fully submerged. This in turn increases overall LN2 consumption due to heat
leaks in the LN2 supply plumbing. Finally, the internal LN2 fill tubing (visible in Figure 8,
left) was modified to direct the supplied fluid away from the condenser, reducing
temperature swings during topoffs

 Thermosyphons in nEXO could potentially operate at either LXe temperature or
closer to base temperature, depending on whether the process fluid actually penetrates
into the immersed heat exchangers or instead cools one end of a cold finger on the outside
the cryostat. The choice of the evaporator operating temperature range would in turn have
important implications for the temperature control process. In either case, it would be
important not to freeze HFE onto the heat exchanger as was observed in early EXO R&D
[13].

 Figure 7: Left: The extended length condenser. Right: Condenser panel pressure vs applied power
 at base temperature. The UNCW thermosyphon (yellow) is compared to the LUX prototype (blue),
 along with two upgrades modestly increasing condenser surface area (grey) and significantly
 extending its length (orange).

 Initial thermal simulations for heat transport using immersed heat exchangers in
nEXO were carried out as Aaron King’s UNCW senior thesis. A simplified geometry (see
Figure 8, left) derived from baseline nEXO dimensions and HFE properties was created in
Autodesk. Three simple heat exchangers in the form of thin disks were arranged
symmetrically near the top of the cryostat. The copper disks were 10.3” in diameter and
connected to copper cold fingers of 2.6” in diameter and 6” long, emerging out of the HFE.
The temperature on the ends of those cold fingers was set as a boundary condition to
simulating cooling and were adjusted to maintain a 165 K setpoint with heat loads up to 1.1
kW uniformly dissipated by the central TPC (Figure 8, right). The coldest temperature
required was 142 K.

 Figure 8: Left: Visualization of the nEXO cryostat geometry for the thermal simulation. The HFE is
 shown in green. Right: Cold finger temperatures vs TPC power in equilibrium near 165 K.

 In order to validate such cooling concepts and associated simulations, an HFE test
system incorporating the thermosyphon is being assembled in the PI’s laboratory. A 32
gallon insulated Cryofab dewar has been procured. The dewar has a custom lid with 5
2.75” CF ports to allow for HFE input and output, heater power wiring, thermocouples for
temperature measurements, and process N2 from the thermosyphon. The lid seals to the
dewar with an o-ring and will be foam insulated. The thermosyphon N2 passes through a
feedthough to cool an evaporator mounted on the bottom of the lid. To separate the HFE
vapor in the dewar from the thermosyphon insulating vacuum, the thermosyphon tubing
has been modified to terminate that tubing outside the dewar, with the short intermediate
connections foam insulated. Prototype heat exchangers will be mounted on the evaporator
on one end and immersed in the HFE on the other. Load heaters will be placed near the
bottom of the dewar. Nine gallons of HFE-7200 for these tests have been obtained so far.
