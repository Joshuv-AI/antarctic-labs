Arterial supply to the adrenal gland in the house musk shrew (Suncus murinus)
Authors: Tetsuhito KIGATA, Nonoha MIYAI, Yuuki HORII, Takahiko SHIINA, Yasutake SHIMIZU
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Branching patterns of the adrenal artery exhibit interspecies and individual variations. As Eulipotyphla species have distinctive morphological characteristics, observations in this group may provide insights into the comparative anatomy of this artery. This study used 15 house musk shrews (Suncus murinus). In most cases, adrenal arteries arose from a single parent artery, namely the caudal phrenic artery, abdominal aorta, or renal artery. In one left case, adrenal arteries emerged from both the abdominal aorta and renal artery. The most common pattern of adrenal arterial supply was one middle and one caudal adrenal artery on both sides, with no cranial one. The house musk shrew exhibited a simpler branching pattern than other species, offering comparative anatomical insights into the adrenal artery.

The pattern of arterial supply to the adrenal gland can be complex, and diverse species differences in the origin of the adrenal arteries have been observed. The adrenal artery in mammals is divided into three groups: cranial, middle, and caudal adrenal arteries, typically originating from the caudal phrenic artery, abdominal aorta, and renal artery, respectively [1, 12]. In addition to these three parent arteries, the adrenal arteries can originate from the celiac, cranial mesenteric, cranial abdominal, lumbar, and gonadal arteries [1,2,3, 5,6,7,8, 10,11,12, 14]. Branching patterns of the adrenal arteries vary among animal species, and the background of this variation remains unclear. To achieve a better understanding, a comparative approach across phylogenetically different species is important.

The house musk shrew (Suncus murinus) belongs to the order Eulipotyphla and is reported to retain primitive anatomical features, such as a markedly undeveloped neocortex [13]. Accordingly, the adrenal arterial branching pattern in the house musk shrew may exhibit characteristic morphology of Eulipotyphla species and may offer insights into comparative anatomical considerations. Although previous studies in Eulipotyphla species have not focused on branching patterns of the adrenal arteries, the anatomical findings may be relevant to the present study. For example, in the greater white-toothed shrew (Crocidura russula), common shrew (Sorex araneus), crowned shrew (Sorex coronatus), and Eurasian pygmy shrew (Sorex minutus), the adrenal artery arises only from the renal artery [9]. Based on those results, the renal artery is expected to play an important role in the arterial supply to the adrenal gland in Eulipotyphla species. However, considering the diverse individual variations reported in other animal species, a focus on the branching pattern of adrenal arteries and their potential individual variations is important. The present study therefore investigated branching patterns of the adrenal arteries in the house musk shrew to achieve a better understanding of adrenal artery characteristics in Eulipotyphla species and the phylogenetic background of interspecies differences in branching patterns of the adrenal artery.

All experimental procedures in the present study were approved by the Guide for Care and Use of Animal Experimentation at Gifu University, Japan (Permission Nos. AG-P-N-2024184, AG-P-N-20260021). Fifteen house musk shrews (6 male shrews, 9 female shrews; age range, 2–8 months; weight range, 36–67 g) were obtained from Kiwa Laboratory Animals Co. (Wakayama, Japan) and Dr. Hiroshi Kiyonari (RIKEN Center for Biosystems Dynamics Research, Kobe, Japan) and were bred in the Laboratory of Veterinary Physiology at Gifu University. All animals were euthanized by an intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (200 mg/kg). Animals were then perfused transcardially with saline, followed by 10% formaldehyde. To trace the arteries, 0.5 mL of a mixture of latex (LACSTAR DFP-800; DIC, Tokyo, Japan; Latex Solution 126-0252, KENIS, Osaka, Japan) and water in a 1:1 ratio colored with red acrylic paint (Acryl Gouache; Turner Colour Works, Osaka, Japan) was injected through a catheter inserted into the left ventricle. The adrenal arteries were traced under a surgical microscope (L-0950SDP; Inami & Co., Tokyo, Japan) and photographed with a LAOWA 65 mm F2.8 Macro Photo lens (Venus Optics, Hefei, China) mounted on an EOS M6 Mark II camera (Canon, Tokyo, Japan). The brightness, contrast, and resolution of images were adjusted with Adobe Photoshop CC (version 27.2.0; Adobe, San Jose, CA, USA). Schematic drawings were prepared using Adobe Illustrator (version 30.1; Adobe).

For identification of the adrenal artery, arteries for which the site of penetration into the adrenal capsule were the cranial third, middle third, or caudal third of the adrenal gland were identified as the cranial, middle, and caudal adrenal artery, respectively, in line with the results of our previous studies [6,7,8, 10]. In particular, in the right adrenal gland, an imaginary line was drawn perpendicular to the renal surface from the intersection between the cranial and medial margins of the gland. In the left adrenal gland, an imaginary line was drawn along the long axis of the gland. In both glands, these imaginary lines were divided into three equal parts to define the cranial, middle, and caudal territories. Moreover, in accordance with our previous studies [6, 10], we only numbered those adrenal arterial roots that had just branched from the parent arteries, and did not count any of the small twigs diverging near the adrenal gland. When arteries supplying different adrenal regions, such as the middle and caudal adrenal arteries, shared a common trunk, each branch was counted as an independent root of the corresponding adrenal artery. The nomenclature used in the present study conformed to the Nomina Anatomica Veterinaria [4].

As no distinct sex-related differences were detected in the branching patterns of the adrenal arteries, the results are described without separating male and female shrews. The adrenal artery in the house musk shrew was simple compared to other animal species. In the house musk shrew, the adrenal artery originated from one of the following arteries: the caudal phrenic artery, abdominal aorta, or renal artery. Even in the three right-sided cases in which the adrenal artery was classified as originating from the caudal phrenic artery, the caudal phrenic artery itself arose from the renal artery, suggesting the importance of the renal artery in the adrenal arterial supply. These findings do not contradict prior study of Eulipotyphla species, in which the adrenal artery was reported to emerge only from the renal artery [9]. In other mammals, the adrenal artery can arise from various parent arteries other than the caudal phrenic artery, abdominal aorta, or renal artery, such as the celiac artery, left gastric artery, cranial mesenteric artery, cranial abdominal artery, gonadal artery, and lumbar artery [1,2,3, 5,6,7,8, 10,11,12, 14]. In addition, the adrenal artery usually originates from multiple parent arteries on each side, and an origin from a single parent artery is rare in previously reported species, including rats, degus, rabbits, dogs, donkeys, Pampas deer, common marmosets, and humans [1,2,3, 5,6,7,8, 10,11,12, 14]. Because a simple branching pattern is shared among several species in Eulipotyphla species [9], the adrenal arterial branching pattern in this species is considered to be simpler than that in other animal species. Moreover, these observations suggest that the caudal phrenic artery, renal artery, and abdominal aorta represent commonly shared arterial sources supplying the adrenal gland across diverse animal species.

Analysis of the origins and incidence of the cranial, middle, and caudal adrenal arteries revealed that the cranial adrenal artery was absent in most cases in the house musk shrew. The cranial adrenal artery is frequently absent only for the right adrenal gland of the rabbit (Oryctolagus cuniculus), whereas many other animal species show adrenal arteries distributed throughout the gland from the cranial to caudal regions [1,2,3, 5,6,7,8, 10,11,12, 14]. The typical lack of a cranial adrenal artery may be regarded as a species-specific morphological trait in the house musk shrew. As previous studies in Eulipotyphla species have lacked descriptions of the distribution area of the adrenal arteries, further studies in other Eulipotyphla species would be valuable for elucidating characteristics of adrenal artery morphology in this group.

The adrenal arteries varied from 1–3 in number, with the typical case showing two adrenal arteries on both sides: one middle and one caudal. Other species have been reported to possess more adrenal arteries. For example, in degus (Octodon degus) and common marmosets (Callithrix jacchus), the number of adrenal arteries varies from 2–9 and 3–13, respectively [8, 10]. The small number of adrenal arteries can be regarded as one of the comparative anatomical characteristics of the adrenal artery in the house musk shrew.

The absence of apparent sexual differences and the relatively low degree of individual variation detected in this study may partly reflect the limited sample size, which could have restricted the detection of subtle anatomical variations. Although the present findings alone are insufficient to support detailed phylogenetic interpretations of adrenal arterial branching patterns, the species-specific characteristics of the adrenal arteries in the house musk shrew demonstrated in this study may provide an important anatomical basis for future comparative and phylogenetic investigations.
