Burden and associated factors of depressive symptoms among older adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus in rural southeast Nigeria: a community-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Uchenna Cosmas Ugwu, Chuka Mackson Jones, Cordelia Chinonyerem Ugwu, Agatha Nneka Obayi, Christian Chukwuka Eze, Osmond Chukwuemeka Ene
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionType 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) increases vulnerability to depression in older adults. This study aimed to assess the burden of depressive symptoms among older adults with T2DM in rural southeast Nigeria and identify sociodemographic and clinical factors associated with severe depressive symptoms.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted across 25 rural communities in five southeast Nigerian states from September 2025 to February 2026. Multistage random sampling recruited 683 adults aged ≥60 years with confirmed T2DM. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the culturally adapted Geriatric Depression Scale—Short Form. Sociodemographic and clinical data were collected, and associations with depressive symptom severity were examined using χ2 tests and crude ORs derived from observed frequencies. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05.ResultsOverall, 83.3% of participants exhibited depressive symptoms, with 39.7% classified as severe. Male gender (COR=3.36, 95% CI 2.43 to 4.64, p=0.001), age ≥71 years (COR=1.53, 95% CI 1.04 to 2.26, p=0.031) and living with family (COR=3.02, 95% CI 2.15 to 4.23, p=0.001) were significantly associated with severe depressive symptoms. Educational attainment showed a reversed pattern, with primary education (COR=0.36, p<0.05) and secondary education (COR=0.27, p<0.05) demonstrating protective associations compared with non-formal education. Indigenous Spiritual Beliefs was associated with higher odds of severe depressive symptoms (COR=1.71, 95% CI 1.11 to 2.65, p=0.015). Age and duration of T2DM were not consistently associated with severe depressive symptoms (p>0.05).ConclusionsDepressive symptoms are highly prevalent among older adults with T2DM in rural southeast Nigeria. Sociodemographic characteristics were more strongly associated with severe depressive symptoms than disease duration. Integrating culturally sensitive, gender-responsive and family-centred mental health interventions within routine diabetes care is critical to improving psychological and metabolic outcomes.Keywords: Cross-Sectional Studies, Depression, Diabetes Mellitus, Mental Health, Public Health

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a major global public health concern, disproportionately affecting older adults and contributing to a substantial burden of morbidity and mortality.1,2 The chronic nature of T2DM, combined with its demanding self-management requirements, renders individuals particularly vulnerable to psychological disorders, notably depressive symptoms.3,4 Depression in older adults with T2DM is associated with poor glycaemic control, increased risk of microvascular and macrovascular complications and premature mortality, creating a complex interplay between metabolic and mental health outcomes.5,6 However, in the present study, no direct biochemical (eg, glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), fasting plasma glucose) or anthropometric measurements were obtained; therefore, metabolic status is discussed only within the context of existing literature rather than empirical data from this study. Despite this recognised bidirectional relationship, depression remains underdiagnosed and undertreated, particularly in resource-limited rural settings.7

In sub-Saharan Africa, and specifically in rural communities of southern Nigeria, older adults with T2DM face unique psychosocial and structural challenges that may exacerbate depressive symptoms. These include limited healthcare access, low health literacy, socioeconomic constraints and sociocultural barriers to mental healthcare.8,9 Although existing literature emphasises the heightened risk of depression among individuals with diabetes,1,2 few studies have systematically quantified the prevalence and determinants of depressive symptoms among older adults in rural Nigerian communities, creating a critical research gap. Addressing this gap is imperative to inform culturally appropriate and context-specific interventions targeting mental health within geriatric diabetes care.

Theoretical frameworks can provide a structured lens for understanding the complex interactions between T2DM, depressive symptoms and sociodemographic factors. This study adopts the biopsychosocial model,10 which posits that health outcomes are determined not only by biological factors but also by psychological and social influences. Within this framework, depressive symptoms in older adults with T2DM can be conceptualised as arising from biological mechanisms (eg, insulin resistance, chronic inflammation), psychological factors (eg, stress, illness perception, coping) and social determinants (eg, marital status, living arrangements, social support and religious affiliation).11,12 By applying this model, the study emphasises the multifaceted nature of depression in diabetes and supports the integration of psychosocial interventions alongside conventional biomedical care.

The purpose of this study was to assess the burden of depressive symptoms among older adults with T2DM in rural communities of southeast Nigeria and to identify key sociodemographic and clinical factors associated with severe depressive symptoms. Specifically, the study sought to examine the prevalence, severity and factors associated with severe depressive symptoms, including gender, age, educational attainment, marital status, living arrangements and religious affiliation.

This investigation is justified by several considerations. First, understanding the prevalence and determinants of depressive symptoms among rural older adults with T2DM is essential for evidence-based planning of mental health and diabetes services. Second, identifying high-risk subgroups (eg, men, those with marital disruption or low education) allows for targeted psychosocial and community-based interventions. Third, the study contributes to global health knowledge by providing empirical data from rural African settings, which are under-represented in geriatric diabetes research. Finally, findings from this study may inform policy and practice by supporting the integration of culturally sensitive, gender-responsive and family-centred mental health strategies within routine diabetes care, ultimately improving both psychological well-being and metabolic outcomes.

In summary, this study offers a contextually grounded examination of depressive symptoms among older adults with T2DM in rural southeast Nigeria. Guided by the biopsychosocial model, it addresses an important research gap, provides actionable insights for clinical and community interventions and advances understanding of the psychosocial factors associated with severe depressive symptoms in a population at high risk for both metabolic and mental health complications.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a community-based cross-sectional design conducted between September 2025 and February 2026. The research was carried out across rural communities in southeast Nigeria, specifically within the five southeast states: Enugu, Abia, Ebonyi, Imo and Anambra. The study aimed to assess the prevalence of depressive symptoms among older adults with T2DM. Data were collected from 25 rural communities, with five communities randomly selected from each of the five states. The communities were chosen using random sampling to ensure a representative sample of rural populations in the region.

Participants

Eligible participants were older adults aged 60 years and above with confirmed T2DM, defined according to the WHO diagnostic criteria as fasting plasma glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dL), and/or HbA1c ≥6.5% and/or a documented clinical diagnosis of T2DM recorded in patient case notes or clinic registers. In this rural setting, confirmation of diagnosis was obtained through triangulation of sources including outpatient case notes from primary health centres, diabetes clinic registers from general hospitals within the five southeastern states and patient-held chronic disease (diabetes) care booklets commonly used in Nigerian primary healthcare systems for long-term follow-up and medication monitoring. The mean age of participants was 66.2 ± (SD: 3.99) years. Inclusion criteria required participants to be cognitively and physically capable of independently engaging with the study instrument and able to communicate in English or any of Nigeria’s major languages: Hausa, Igbo or Yoruba. Participants were also required to provide informed consent and be available during the survey period. Exclusion criteria included being younger than 60 years, having acute or severe cognitive impairments, being absent during data collection, or being unable to provide informed consent.

A multistage sampling strategy was employed to ensure representativeness. Stage One involved selecting each state as a cluster. Stage Two involved randomly sampling five rural communities from each state, resulting in 25 study sites. Stage Three involved recruiting 28 eligible participants from each community, for a total of 700 individuals initially approached. Ultimately, 683 participants completed the survey and provided valid data, with 17 participants withdrawing voluntarily. Reasons for withdrawal were not systematically recorded because participants were permitted to discontinue participation at any stage without obligation to provide an explanation. As these individuals withdrew before completion of the study instrument, sufficient information was unavailable to permit formal comparison between completers and non-completers.

Data collection and materials

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire referred to as the Depressive Symptoms Assessment Questionnaire (DSAQ), which comprised sociodemographic and clinical sections, alongside the Geriatric Depression Scale–15 (GDS-15) for assessment of depressive symptoms. The GDS-15 was the sole instrument used for measuring depressive symptoms. The structured questionnaire included Section A: sociodemographic characteristics such as age, sex, educational level, marital status, living arrangements and religious affiliation.13 Religious affiliation was self-reported and categorised as Christianity, Islam, African Traditional Religion (ATR) or Indigenous Spiritual Beliefs. The category ‘Indigenous spiritual beliefs’ described participants identifying with indigenous spiritual practices outside formal Christian, Islamic or recognised ATR traditions, adopted to enhance cultural sensitivity and avoid pejorative terminology. Section B captured the duration of T2DM diagnosis. Section C comprised the GDS-15, a validated 15-item scale assessing depressive symptoms in older adults. Total scores range from 0 to 15 and were categorised as follows: 0–4=normal, 5–8=mild, 9–11=moderate and 12–15=severe depressive symptoms, consistent with established guidelines.14,15 Section D contained ethical consent and participant information aligned with the Declaration of Helsinki.16 To enhance cultural relevance, minor linguistic adaptations were made during translation into English, Hausa, Igbo and Yoruba. Back-translation procedures were used to ensure conceptual equivalence. Pretesting was conducted among 20 older adults with T2DM not included in the main study. A panel of nine public health experts assessed content validity. Formal psychometric validation (factor analysis or measurement invariance testing) was not conducted; however, internal consistency was acceptable (Cronbach’s alpha=0.81).

Sample size calculation

Sample size estimation was based on Cochran’s formula for single proportion studies, given by: n=Z²pq/d², where Z=1.96 at 95% confidence level, p=0.756 (estimated prevalence of depressive symptoms among older Nigerian adults with chronic non-communicable diseases obtained from a recent hospital-based study17), q=1 − p=0.244 and d=0.05 (margin of error). Using this formula, the minimum required sample size was calculated as: n = (1.96)² × 0.756 × 0.244 / (0.05)² = 283.5 ≈ 284. To account for multistage sampling design effect and potential clustering at community level, a design effect of 2.0 was applied, yielding an adjusted sample size of 568. To further account for an anticipated 10% non-response rate, the final minimum sample size was increased to 631 participants. A total of 700 participants were initially recruited to ensure adequate coverage across study clusters. Ultimately, 683 participants completed the study and were included in the final analysis, representing a sufficient effective sample size for prevalence estimation and regression analyses.

Power consideration

Post-hoc power analysis was conducted based on the achieved sample size (n=683) and the observed proportion of severe depressive symptoms. The study had >80% statistical power to detect meaningful differences in proportions across key sociodemographic variables at α=0.05, ensuring adequate precision of estimates within the multivariable structure of a cross-sectional design.18

Training and data collection procedures

Data collection was carried out through face-to-face interviews conducted by 50 trained research assistants, two per rural community. These research assistants were Community Health Extension Workers (CHEWs) who received a structured training session lasting at least 30 min, with additional supervised discussions and practical guidance provided by members of the research team prior to field deployment. The training covered ethical conduct, informed consent procedures, standardised administration of the study instrument, appropriate administration of the GDS-15 screening tool, and cultural sensitivity relevant to the local context. Because the GDS-15 is a brief screening instrument rather than a diagnostic psychiatric interview, the training focused primarily on standardised questionnaire administration, participant engagement, confidentiality, and accurate recording of responses. Interviews were conducted in participants’ homes to ensure comfort, privacy, and increased participation. Prior to data collection, written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Consent was either signed or provided via thumbprint, depending on the participant’s literacy level.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics V. 28.19 Descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages) were used to summarise participants’ sociodemographic characteristics and the prevalence of depressive symptoms. Associations between categorical variables were assessed using χ2 tests. Crude odds ratios (CORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed from observed frequencies to assess the strength and direction of associations between sociodemographic/clinical variables and severe depressive symptoms, with statistical significance set at p<0.05. After verification of table 3 frequency distributions, COR estimates in revised online supplemental table 1 were cross-checked for consistency with observed data and reference categories. Results are presented as crude associations rather than adjusted effects. The dependent variable was defined as severe depressive symptoms (GDS-15 score 12–15=1) vs non-severe status (0–11=0). Only univariate logistic regression models were conducted, consistent with the available data structure and exploratory analytic design, and no adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied. Selection bias was minimised through multistage random sampling, while standardised interviewer training and structured instruments reduced information bias; however, residual selection, recall, and measurement biases may persist. Missing data were minimal: 683 of 700 participants completed the study, and complete-case analysis was applied without imputation due to negligible item-level missingness. The 17 withdrawals occurred before completion of data collection, precluding comparative analysis with completers. No sensitivity analyses were conducted for alternative model specifications or subgroup assessments.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for the study was granted by the Federal Ethics Committee of Nigeria (Ref: REC/FE/2025/00018), and additional administrative permission was obtained from community leaders and traditional rulers of the participating rural communities. This study adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.16 All participants were assured of the confidentiality and anonymity of their data. No monetary incentives were offered, and participants were informed of their right to withdraw voluntarily from the study without penalty. The study also ensured cultural sensitivity, with research assistants trained to respect the local norms and values. Participants’ rights to privacy were respected throughout the data collection process, and informed consent was obtained prior to participation. All reporting was done in accordance with STROBE recommendations.20

Results

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics

A total of 683 older adults with T2DM participated in the study. The mean age of participants was 66.2 ± [SD: 3.99] years. Nearly half were aged 60–65 years (322; 47.1%), followed by ≥71 years (209; 30.6%) and 66–70 years (152; 22.3%). Females constituted 391 (57.2%) of the sample, while males accounted for 292 (42.8%). Regarding religious affiliation, 388 (56.8%) were Christians, 119 (17.4%) Muslims, 112 (16.4%) African Traditional Religion adherents and 44 (6.3%) identified with Indigenous spiritual beliefs. In terms of education, 249 (36.5%) had primary education, 202 (29.6%) secondary education, 135 (19.8%) tertiary education and 97 (14.2%) non-formal education. Most participants were married, 308 (45.1%); followed by widowed, 263 (38.5%); divorced/separated, 87 (12.7%); and single, 25 (3.7%). A majority lived with family 467 (68.4%), while 216 (31.6%) lived alone. Regarding duration of T2DM diagnosis, 276 (40.4%) had lived with the condition for ≥5 years, 243 (35.6%) for 3–4 years and 164 (24.0%) for ≤2 years. (Refer to table 1 for full details).

Association between sociodemographic/clinical variables and severity of depressive symptoms

χ2 analysis demonstrated statistically significant associations between severity of depressive symptoms and gender (χ² = 52.63; p=0.001), denomination (χ² = 41.78; p=0.001), education level (χ² = 36.54; p=0.001), marital status (χ² = 29.61; p=0.001) and living status (χ² = 39.84; p=0.001). Severe depressive symptoms were more common among males 178 (61.0%) compared with females 124 (31.7%). Participants living alone had a markedly higher proportion of severe symptoms 128 (59.3%) than those living with family 159 (34.0%). Divorced 39 (44.8%) and single 12 (48.0%) participants showed higher proportions of severe symptoms compared with married 101 (32.8%) and widowed 74 (28.1%) individuals. Educational attainment showed a graded pattern, with severe depressive symptoms highest among those with tertiary 86 (63.7%) and secondary education 120 (59.4%), compared with primary 89 (35.7%) and non-formal education 41 (42.3%). No statistically significant associations were found between age (χ² = 8.47; p=0.206) or duration of T2DM (χ² = 11.92; p=0.064) and severity of depressive symptoms. Odds ratios for severe depressive symptoms

Crude ORs derived from observed frequencies were examined to evaluate the direction and magnitude of associations between sociodemographic/clinical characteristics and severe depressive symptoms. As observed, male participants had higher odds of severe depressive symptoms than females. Participants aged ≥71 years also demonstrated higher odds of severe depressive symptoms relative to those aged 60–65 years. With respect to religious affiliation, Pagan participants showed higher odds of severe depressive symptoms compared with African Traditional Religion adherents, whereas the estimates for Christianity and Islam were less pronounced. Educational attainment demonstrated a pattern different from that originally reported. Compared with participants with non-formal education, those with tertiary education (COR ≈ 2.41) and secondary education (COR ≈ 2.00) exhibited higher odds of severe depressive symptoms, whereas those with primary education demonstrated slightly lower odds (COR ≈ 0.76). Similarly, marital status showed a revised pattern. Compared with single participants, widowed participants demonstrated lower odds of severe depressive symptoms (COR ≈ 0.80), whereas divorced participants had moderately higher odds (COR ≈ 1.65). Married participants showed odds comparable to those of single participants (COR ≈ 0.99). Living arrangements also differed from the originally reported findings. Participants living with family had lower odds of severe depressive symptoms relative to those living alone (COR ≈ 0.36), consistent with the substantially lower proportion of severe depressive symptoms observed among individuals living with family (34.0%) compared with those living alone (59.3%). Duration of T2DM remained inconsistently associated with severe depressive symptoms. Overall, the corrected crude ORs align with the frequencies reported in table 3 and should be interpreted as measures of association rather than confirmed independent predictors. Discussion

This study demonstrates a strikingly high burden of depressive symptoms among older adults with T2DM in rural communities of southeast Nigeria. Over four in five participants exhibited at least mild depressive symptoms, and nearly two in five experienced severe symptomatology. These prevalence rates exceed many global estimates and underscore the heightened vulnerability of older adults with diabetes to depression.2,7 Meta-analytic evidence indicates that individuals with diabetes are two to three times more likely than the general population to experience depression, with significant consequences for glycaemic control, complication risk and mortality.1,5,6 The high prevalence of severe depressive symptoms observed in this cohort highlights the urgent need for systematic mental health screening and management within routine geriatric diabetes care.

The bidirectional relationship between depression and T2DM is well established.21,22 Depression impairs self-management behaviours, including dietary adherence, physical activity, glucose monitoring and medication compliance, thereby exacerbating metabolic dysregulation.3,4 Conversely, chronic hyperglycaemia, inflammatory activation, hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis dysfunction and vascular complications may increase susceptibility to depressive disorders.23,24 It is important to note that the present study did not collect glycaemic biomarkers (eg, HbA1c, fasting plasma glucose) or anthropometric indices; therefore, interpretations regarding metabolic control are based on established literature rather than direct measurements in this sample. In older adults, these mechanisms intersect with cerebrovascular changes and neurodegeneration, further elevating depression risk.25,26 Depression in late life is also linked to frailty, cognitive decline and functional impairment, compounding challenges in diabetes management.27,28 Persistent inflammatory and oxidative stress mechanisms may impair pancreatic β-cell function and destabilise glycaemic control, creating a vicious cycle of metabolic and psychological deterioration.29,30 These findings support a biopsychosocial approach to diabetes management, rather than a purely biomedical paradigm.11

Interestingly, male participants had more than threefold higher odds of severe depressive symptoms than females, contrasting with the commonly reported higher prevalence of depression among women.31 Emerging evidence suggests that older men with chronic illnesses may underreport early depressive symptoms due to stigma and culturally mediated masculine norms, resulting in delayed recognition and more severe presentations.32–34 Among older men with T2DM, functional decline, sexual dysfunction, reduced economic productivity and perceived loss of autonomy may exacerbate psychological distress.35 However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously. The observed association may partially reflect selection processes within the study population. Because participants were recruited through documented diabetes records and community-based identification procedures, men who remained engaged with healthcare services may represent a subgroup with greater disease burden, functional limitations or psychosocial distress than men with T2DM in the general community. Differential healthcare-seeking behaviour and patterns of diagnosis in rural settings may therefore have influenced the observed sex differences.

Furthermore, although females constituted a larger proportion of the study population (57.2%) than males (42.8%), the extent to which this distribution reflects the underlying population of older adults with T2DM in southeast Nigeria is uncertain, as population-based sex-specific prevalence data were not available for direct comparison. Consequently, sampling and participation factors cannot be excluded as potential contributors to the observed findings. An additional methodological consideration is the measurement equivalence of depressive symptoms across sexes. Although the GDS-15 has demonstrated acceptable validity among older Nigerian populations and showed satisfactory internal consistency in the present study (Cronbach’s alpha=0.81), sex-specific measurement invariance was not evaluated. Therefore, it cannot be confirmed that the instrument functioned identically among men and women in this setting. Future studies employing formal psychometric assessment and multivariable modelling are needed to determine whether the observed male predominance represents a true epidemiological pattern or reflects methodological and contextual influences. These patterns emphasise the need for gender-sensitive mental health screening and interventions that account for sociocultural influences on symptom expression.36,37

Educational attainment demonstrated a different pattern from that originally reported. Severe depressive symptoms were most prevalent among participants with tertiary and secondary education, while lower proportions were observed among those with primary and non-formal education. Although education is often associated with improved health literacy and self-management capacity, higher educational attainment may also increase awareness of disease burden, future health concerns and functional decline, thereby contributing to psychological distress among some older adults.38–40 Given the inconsistency between expected and observed patterns, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and warrant further investigation using verified multivariable analyses. The previous interpretation suggesting that living with family was associated with higher depressive symptom severity was based on an apparent reversal of the reference category. Consistent with the observed frequencies, participants living alone exhibited a substantially higher proportion of severe depressive symptoms (59.3%) than those living with family (34.0%), indicating that family co-residence may provide social and emotional support that mitigates depression risk among older adults with T2DM. Likewise, widowhood was not associated with greater depressive symptom severity than single status in the observed data. Although divorced participants demonstrated somewhat higher odds of severe depressive symptoms, the observed pattern suggests that social support and household companionship may play a more important role in psychological well-being than marital status alone.41,42 These findings underscore the importance of strengthening family- and community-based support systems for older adults living with chronic illnesses.

Religious affiliation influenced depressive symptom severity, highlighting the complex role of spirituality, cultural belief systems and social integration in coping with chronic illness. Because religious affiliation was self-reported and some conceptual overlap may exist between Indigenous Spiritual Beliefs and African Traditional Religion, findings relating to these categories should be interpreted cautiously.12 Faith-based partnerships may be a valuable avenue for culturally appropriate mental health promotion and community engagement.43 Advanced age and T2DM duration were not consistently associated with severe depressive symptoms, suggesting that psychosocial and contextual factors may be more salient determinants of depression risk than chronological age or disease duration.44,45 These findings reinforce the importance of comprehensive geriatric assessment when evaluating factors associated with depressive symptom severity among older adults with diabetes.

Collectively, the findings indicate that depression among older adults with T2DM in rural Nigeria is a major public health challenge with significant clinical, metabolic and health system implications. Integrated care models that embed systematic depression screening, collaborative care and community-based psychosocial interventions are essential to improving both mental health and overall diabetes-related health outcomes as reported in prior studies.2 Early detection and treatment of depression in this population may reduce inflammatory burden, enhance insulin sensitivity, slow frailty progression and promote healthy ageing.46

Strengths and limitations

The study employed a rigorous multistage random sampling strategy across 25 rural communities, enhancing representativeness and generalisability to older adults with T2DM in southeast Nigeria. Use of a culturally adapted and validated instrument (DSAQ with GDS-15) ensured reliable and contextually appropriate assessment of depressive symptoms. Face-to-face interviews conducted by trained Community Health Extension Workers minimised response bias and improved data completeness. The study achieved an adequate sample size (n=683), providing 80% statistical power to detect medium effect sizes, ensuring robust and reliable estimates. The cross-sectional design limits causal inference between depressive symptoms and associated sociodemographic or clinical factors. Self-reported depressive symptoms may be influenced by social desirability or underreporting, particularly among men due to cultural stigma. Rural focus may limit extrapolation to urban or peri-urban older adult populations. Unmeasured confounders, such as comorbid medical conditions, cognitive impairment or access to healthcare services, may have influenced observed associations. An additional limitation is the absence of biochemical (eg, HbA1c, fasting plasma glucose) and anthropometric measurements, preventing direct assessment of metabolic control; thus, interpretations of glycaemic status are inferential rather than empirical. Although participants self-identified religious affiliations, potential overlap between Indigenous Spiritual Beliefs and African Traditional Religion may have introduced classification ambiguity. Furthermore, Community Health Extension Workers received structured pre-data collection training; however, the relatively brief duration compared with full mental health assessment training, despite supervision and use of standardised tools, means interviewer-related measurement bias cannot be fully excluded. Finally, the absence of Patient and Public Involvement (PPI) may have limited incorporation of community perspectives, particularly in this rural setting, suggesting a need for future studies to integrate PPI to enhance cultural relevance and translational impact.

Conclusion

Depressive symptoms are highly prevalent among older adults with T2DM in rural southeast Nigeria, with nearly 40% experiencing severe symptoms. Male gender, educational attainment, marital status, living arrangements and religious affiliation were among the factors associated with severe depressive symptoms in this population. Duration of diabetes showed limited association with depressive symptom severity. These findings underscore the need for integrated, culturally sensitive and gender-responsive mental health services within routine diabetes care. Family-centred psychosocial support and community-based interventions may contribute to improved psychological well-being and healthier ageing among older adults living with T2DM. Given that the reported ORs are crude estimates derived from observed frequencies, further studies using verified multivariable analyses are warranted to confirm these associations.
