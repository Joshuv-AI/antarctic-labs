Geospatial mapping, risk assessment and multivariate analysis of naturally occurring radionuclides and potentially toxic elements in agrarian soils of SAS Nagar, Punjab, India
Authors: Sanjeet S. Kaintura, Om Prakash Pandey, Mukesh Prasad, Navjot Kaur, Sarabjot Kaur, Froukje M. van der Zwan, Pushpendra P. Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rapid urbanization, industrialization, and agricultural advancements underscore the need to monitor environmental radionuclides and potentially toxic elements (PTEs) for pollution and human health impact assessment. This study assessed human health risks associated with radionuclides and PTEs in agricultural soils of the SAS Nagar District, Punjab, India, using ICP-MS and XRF techniques. Mean radionuclide concentrations were found to be 34.7 ± 3.1 Bq kg-1 (238U), 56.5 ± 8.4 Bq kg-1 (232Th), and 556.8 ± 93.1 Bq kg-1 (40K), respectively, with levels all above corresponding global means. Elemental concentrations (ppm) followed a decreasing sequence: Fe (29,148) > > Zn (67.4) > Cr (55.7) > Ni (25.5) > Cu (23.4) > Pb (18.6) > Th (13.9) > Co (8.8) > As (6.5) > U (2.8) > Cd (0.17). Pollution indices identified As and Cd as the dominant contributors to soil contamination, with U and Cr exerting moderate secondary effects. A multi-pathway exposure model indicates carcinogenic risks within the permissible range; however, As, Cr, and Ni remain within tolerable limits (10−6–10−4) but still signify potential concern. IDW-based spatial mapping of selected radionuclides and PTEs revealed zones of co-contamination across the study area. Spearman’s rank correlations show strong to very strong monotonic relationships among most soil variables. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) showcased that the first three components explained a major portion (78.2%) of the total variance. PCA separates variables driven by common environmental processes from those influenced by geogenic and anthropogenic factors, while Hierarchical Cluster Analysis (HCA) groups parameters with similar behavior reflecting these controls. Collectively, these findings emphasize the need for targeted soil quality monitoring to safeguard long-term soil quality and public health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-51494-y.

Introduction

Ensuring safe and nutritious food remains central to United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), specifically Goals 2 (Zero Hunger) and 3 (Good Health and Well-Being), as sustainable food security relies on equitable access, resilient production, and the conservation of land, water, and genetic resources1. However, the rising accumulation of radionuclides and potentially toxic elements (PTEs) in soils and edible portions of plants poses an escalating global threat to environmental quality, food safety, and human health2. Naturally occurring radioactive materials (NORMs) comprise terrestrial (primordial) radioisotopes (i.e., 238U, 232Th, and 40K), formed by nucleosynthesis3. Alterations in the natural distribution or geochemical behavior of NORM can generate technologically enhanced NORM (TENORM) with elevated radioactivity. Industrial and agricultural activities, including mineral extraction and processing, oil and gas production, geothermal operations, fertilizer and pesticide use, and water treatment, can concentrate these materials4,5. Naturally occurring radionuclides (NORs) originating from bedrock are mobilized into soils, water, biota, and the atmosphere through geochemical, physicochemical, and biogenic processes and, once assimilated by plants and animals, enter the human food chain via ingestion or inhalation, contributing to unavoidable dietary exposure2,3,6–8. Among the aforementioned environmental matrices, soil functions as a critical source, pathway, and sink for the migration of radionuclides and PTEs into the human body, mainly through soil-to-plant transfer, reflected in radioactivity in food crops and animal uptake9–11. As the primary interface of the food chain and a major reservoir of nutrients, soil contamination with NORs and PTEs poses a serious environmental hazard, necessitating effective remediation measures to protect and restore soil health and ecosystem integrity12,13. Further, radiation emitted from naturally radioactive soils represents a major global source of both environmental and food-chain contamination14. Scientific assessments indicate that about 80% of the annual natural radiation dose to humans arises from soil- and rock-derived radionuclides, primarily through radon exhalation, terrestrial gamma radiation, and radionuclide transfer into environmental media, with roughly 60–70% attributed directly or indirectly to soils15–18. Additionally, the concentrations of NORs in agricultural soil directly influence their transfer into the food chain, as these radionuclides accumulate and persist within the plant root-accessible topsoil (0–30 cm), contributing to long-term environmental radioactivity19.

Over the past six decades, efforts to increase agricultural productivity have driven widespread use of organic and inorganic fertilizers and pesticides, leading to elevated environmental risks, particularly soil contamination from anthropogenic inputs5,11. Radionuclides are of particular concern and enter agroecosystems mainly through fertilizers. Phosphate fertilizers sourced from phosphate rock contain 238U and 232Th, while potassium-based fertilizers predominantly contain 40K. The global annual application of over 30 million metric tons of such fertilizers results in measurable amounts in soils, contributing to radionuclide accumulation20. Next, radionuclide accumulation in soil is controlled by physicochemical properties (pH, organic matter, and electrical conductivity (EC)) and land-use practices, particularly agriculture, mining, industry, and urbanization. The application of fertilizers and pesticides, together with poorly managed industrial and mining wastes, promotes long-term buildup in soils and sediments, with fine-grained stream sediments retaining higher radionuclide levels than nearby soils21,22. Uranium (U) and thorium (Th) can act as both chemical and radiological toxicants. Prolonged exposure to radionuclides at elevated levels may have serious implications for human health. Uranium bioaccumulation can disrupt multiple organ systems and is associated with lung cancer, anemia, tissue necrosis, neurological disorders, hypertension, cardiac dysfunction, obesity, and acute leukopenia. The exposure from thorium is linked to cancers of the kidney, liver, pancreas, bone, and lung, as well as leukemia23–25. Although potassium is essential, deficiency or excess can cause neuromuscular and cardiac dysfunction, highlighting the need for strict exposure monitoring26.

The contamination due to PTEs has become another major environmental issue driven mainly by human activities, with natural processes contributing only background levels27. Industrial operations, transportation, agriculture, and poor waste management have converted many agricultural soils into PTE reservoirs, reducing fertility, impairing crop productivity, and extending contamination to aquatic ecosystems through runoff. Elements such as Co, Cu, and Zn are essential micronutrients, but become phytotoxic at elevated levels, whereas As, Cd, Pb, Cr, and Ni are toxic even at trace concentrations for human health28. Globally, nearly five million soil sites exceed regulatory PTE limits, compromising soil quality and agricultural productivity29. This problem is further intensified by the widespread use of NPK fertilizers containing trace elements. Fertilizer consumption has risen sharply in East Asia (31%), South Asia (20%), and Latin America and the Caribbean (14%)30, underscoring the trade-off between productivity gains and environmental contamination. Additional sources, including mining, smelting, petrochemical activities, and sewage-sludge application, alter soil physicochemical properties and promote long-term persistence of PTEs. PTEs threaten human health by disrupting essential biological functions; chronic exposure is associated with gastrointestinal disorders, dermatological lesions, anemia, hypertension, respiratory diseases, cardiovascular and hematopoietic abnormalities, and various cancers, including leukemia28,31. Environmental toxins are an important determinant of human health. The American Cancer Society (ACS) classifies all NORs and several toxic elements (As, Cd, Cr (VI)) as established carcinogens, whereas Pb is considered a probable carcinogen32,33. Furthermore, the regions enriched in NORs frequently exhibit co-occurring toxic elements, suggesting a shared geochemical origin and potential localized cancer risk34,35.

Punjab, the cradle of India’s Green Revolution, remains a pivotal agricultural hub, advancing from traditional practices to modern ones, where over 39% of the workforce depends on farming36. Several regions of the state report elevated and spatially variable concentrations of uranium and PTEs such as Cr, As, and Pb in water sources37,38. Rapid demographic growth and industrial intensification have accelerated urban expansion in the Shaheed Ajeet Singh (SAS) Nagar district, driven by rising socio-economic aspirations and increasing demand for urban amenities. In response, the Government of Punjab has constituted statutory planning bodies under the Punjab Urban Development Authority (PUDA), among which the Greater Mohali Area Development Authority (GMADA) is strategically significant due to its proximity to the Chandigarh metropolitan area36.

Advanced analytical techniques are vital for accurate radionuclide and elemental characterization. Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS) provides high sensitivity for detecting PTEs such as U and Th at ppb to ppt levels. X-ray Fluorescence (XRF) effectively quantifies elements with higher concentrations, while an element analyzer is suitable for determining organic matter and nitrogen content. Physicochemical parameters, including pH and EC, are best measured using a calibrated pH/EC meter. Therefore, this study investigates the agricultural soils of SAS Nagar district, Punjab, India, with the following objectives: (a) to evaluate the levels and geospatial mapping of NORs (238U, 232Th, and 40K); (b) to assess soil physicochemical properties (pH, EC, TDS, and organic carbon) and essential nutrients (N, P, and K); (c) to quantify the concentration levels of PTEs (Pb, Cd, As, Co, Cr, Ni, Fe, Zn, Cu, U, and Th) for geospatial mapping, pollution assessment and human health risk evaluation (carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic); and (d) to identify potential contamination sources using multivariate statistical analyses (Spearman’s rank correlation, PCA and HCA). This study establishes baseline data through a systematic approach to assess radioactive and trace-element contaminants and their influencing factors in agricultural soils, employing state-of-the-art techniques. So far, integrated data on concentrations of NORs and PTEs remain limited and fragmented, and no comprehensive study focusing on agricultural soils has been conducted in the SAS Nagar district, despite the availability of data for other soil types. This underscores the need for a detailed assessment of their contamination and associated health risks. The findings provide insights into the effects of agricultural practices on soil quality, with implications for the food chain and land-use patterns in the study region.

Study area

Situated in eastern Punjab, SAS Nagar covers approximately39 1,094 km2 and has a population of about 994,628. The district extends between latitudes 30°21′–30°56′ N and longitudes 76°30′–76°55′ E. The GIS-based map of the sampling locations in the study area is presented in Fig. 1. SAS Nagar district is geographically bordered by Patiala and Fatehgarh Sahib districts to the southwest, Rupnagar to the northwest, Chandigarh and Panchkula to the east, and Ambala district of Haryana to the south40. Agriculture is the primary source of livelihood for the district’s workforce. The district hosts more than 6,391 registered industrial units, according to the Ministry of Micro, Small, and Medium Enterprises (MSME). The prevalence of steel, automobile, metal, chemical, textiles, pharmaceuticals, paper, food, tractor, semiconductors, bottling, precision tools, and auto-parts industries may contribute to soil contamination by PTEs such as Cr, Co, and Ni39,41. Located near the Shivalik foothills, large portions of the region fall within environmentally sensitive zones, including protected and reserved forests and areas regulated under the Punjab Land Preservation Act (PLPA). Geologically, SAS Nagar forms part of the Indo-Gangetic alluvial plains and is underlain by Quaternary alluvial deposits that constitute the principal aquifer system. The district comprises two major geomorphic units, an alluvial plain and an alluvial fan, both characterized by fertile soils enriched through periodic silt deposition, supporting intensive multi-cropping agriculture. The fertile alluvial plains hold substantial agricultural and economic significance, supporting a large portion of the district’s population. Soils, primarily developed on alluvial deposits, are shaped by climatic conditions, topography, and soil-forming processes. The district experiences a subtropical climate characterized by hot summers, mild winters, and a variable monsoon season. Frost is common in December and January. The region receives an average annual rainfall of about 617 mm, nearly 80% of which occurs during the southwest monsoon between July and August, with occasional winter showers from western disturbances. The region is predominantly covered by Solonchak-type tropical arid brown soils, with localized patches of loam, heavy loam, and sandy loam that support diverse agriculture, including cereals, pulses, vegetables, and orchards39.

Experimental details

Collection and pre-processing of samples

A total of 26 soil samples were collected for this study in March 2023: 25 from farmland sites and 1 from a non-farmland site used as a control (Fig. 1). All farmland soil samples were collected after obtaining prior informed verbal consent from the farm owners. At each site, approximately 2 kg of soil was sampled from 2–30 cm depth after removing the topsoil to avoid surface contamination. Visible plant roots, glass fragments, stones, and other debris were removed in the field. Each sample was placed in a labelled container and logged with relevant sampling information (geographical coordinates, sample code, and collection date). All sampling locations were recorded using a handheld GPS device. After transport to the laboratory, samples were air-dried at room temperature for 24 h, followed by oven-drying at 70 °C for 48 h to achieve constant weight and eliminate residual moisture. The dried samples were subsequently sieved, homogenized, and milled as required before analysis using the appropriate instrumentation.

Analysis of PTEs using ICP-MS

For the analysis of PTEs, the concentrations of U, Th, Pb, Cd, As, Cr, Ni, Zn, Cu, and Co in soil samples were quantified employing a triple-quadrupole inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometer (QQQ-ICP-MS; Agilent 8800) at the Analytical Core Laboratory, King Abdullah University of Science and Technology (KAUST). Approximately 0.1 g of soil powder was subjected to acid digestion using a high-pressure microwave system (UltraCLAVE, Milestone), which allows simultaneous digestion of up to 40 samples under controlled temperature and pressure. The digestion procedure comprised three sequential steps. First, 1.5 mL of HF and 0.5 mL of HNO3 were added, and the samples were microwaved at 240 °C for 40 min. Second, 100 μL HClO4 was added, and the solutions were evaporated to dryness on a hot plate (HPX-200, Savillex) at 95 °C. Third, the residues were re-dissolved with 2 mL of HNO3 and 4.5 mL Milli-Q water and subjected to a second microwave digestion at 200 °C for 60 min to ensure complete matrix dissolution. Finally, the digests were diluted 1:5000 in 2% HNO3 and spiked with internal standards (Indium and Bismuth) to a final concentration of 20 ppb. Afterward, the solutions were analyzed directly by ICP-MS.

Analytical quality assurance was verified using triplicate analyses (n = 3) of certified reference materials (CRMs), NIST SRM 2702 and JA-2. The measured concentrations agreed with certified values within ± 10%, and analytical precision (RSD) was < 5% for all elements except Cd (13%) and As (9%). ICP-MS limits of detection (LOD) for elements in ppm were: Cr (0.167), Ni (0.047), Cu (0.04), Zn (0.401), As (0.059), Cd (0.005), Pb (0.03), Th (0.01), and U (0.004). The resulting calibration curves exhibited excellent linearity (R2 ≥ 0.9997), confirming high analytical performance. A more comprehensive description of the sample digestion and ICP-MS analytical procedures is outlined by Cherviakouski et al.42.

Elemental analysis using XRF and an elemental analyzer

Major element concentrations were quantified using a wavelength-dispersive X-ray fluorescence (WD-XRF) spectrometer (Bruker S8 Tiger) at the Analytical Core Laboratory, KAUST. Approximately 0.5 g of finely ground and homogenized soil powder was used for the analysis. Analytical quality control was assessed through triplicate measurements (n = 3) of the NIST standard reference materials (SRMs) 1646a and 2702, which yielded accuracies within 5% and RSDs < 2% for all determinations except P, which is ~ 11%. The LOD of the elements (wt%) was: P (0.0026), K (0.0028), and Fe (0.0012).

Total organic carbon (TOC) and nitrogen (N) were measured using a Thermo Scientific™ FlashSmart™ CHNS/O Elemental Analyzer at the Analytical Core Laboratory, KAUST. Soil powders (0.01 g) were placed in 10 × 10 mm silver capsules and treated with 100 μL of 1 N HCl to remove inorganic carbon. The samples were acid-treated, dried at 65 °C overnight to remove inorganic carbon, sealed in tin capsules, and then analyzed to determine N and TOC concentrations. Analytical quality control based on quintuplicate CRM analyses (n = 5) yielded < 10% accuracy error and RSD < 12% for all determinations. Instrument calibration and background indicated LOD of 0.031 wt% for TOC and 0.011 wt% for N.

Physicochemical parameters using pH/EC meter

To determine the pH, EC, and total dissolved solids (TDS), 8 g of soil was mixed with 40 mL of deionized water to obtain a 1:5 (w/v) soil-to-water suspension. The suspension was thoroughly homogenized on a magnetic stirrer for 10 h and filtered through a 125 µm filter paper. Measurements of pH, EC, and TDS were obtained using a calibrated multiparameter analyzer (HI98194, Hanna Instruments) at the Department of Chemical Engineering at IIT Ropar.

Assessment of pollution and carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic risks of PTEs

Pollution level assessment of PTEs

Pollution indices provide a quantitative framework for evaluating the degree of soil contamination resulting from both anthropogenic and geogenic sources. The applied indicators to assess soil quality include contamination factor (CF), pollution load index (PLI), enrichment factor (EF), geoaccumulation index (Igeo), and ecological risk index (Ei).

Carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic risk assessment

Human health risks associated with exposure to PTEs in soil were quantified using the United States Environmental Protection Agency (USEPA) methodology, which incorporates both non-carcinogenic and carcinogenic endpoints. Health risk assessment of PTEs in soil was performed for three major exposure pathways relevant to residential and agricultural settings: ingestion, dermal contact, and inhalation of resuspended particulates56,57. Exposure was quantified in terms of Average Daily Dose (ADD) for each pathway, which was subsequently used to evaluate non-carcinogenic effects in terms of Hazard Quotient (HQ) and Hazard Index (HI), as well as carcinogenic effects in terms of Incremental Lifetime Cancer Risk (CR), with cancer slope factors (SFs) (https://www.epa.gov/risk/regional-screening-levels-rsls-users-guide). This source-specific integrated approach, encompassing both non-carcinogenic toxicity and long-term carcinogenic risk, provides a mechanistic basis for understanding the link between soil contamination and public health. Geospatial mapping and multivariate analysis

The study-area map and contour maps were generated using ArcGIS Pro (version 3.5.1). Geographic Information System (GIS) tools were used to integrate and analyze the spatial distribution of soil radionuclide and PTE data, enabling the visualization of areas with elevated concentrations and potential cross-contamination, and supporting the interpretation of spatial trends relevant to environmental and public-health assessment60,61. These contours or surfaces were generated using the geostatistical technique known as the Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW) interpolation algorithm within the Spatial Analyst toolbox. IDW estimates values by weighting nearby sampled data points more heavily than those farther away, based on the inverse of their separation distance raised to a specified power.

Multivariate analyses, including Spearman’s correlation, principal component analysis (PCA), and hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA), were conducted using OriginPro 2026 to identify relationships and underlying patterns in the dataset. Spearman’s rank correlation quantified the strength and direction of associations between variables62,63. PCA, applied to the correlation matrix, reduced dimensionality by transforming correlated variables into a smaller set of independent principal components, representing the major sources of variance. HCA organizes variables with similar characteristics into distinct clusters, revealing natural patterns or structures within the data. Together, PCA and HCA provided complementary insights, with PCA highlighting the key factors driving variability and HCA identifying grouping trends48.

Potentially toxic element content, associated pollution, and health risk

In recent years, anthropogenic activities, particularly industrial and agricultural practices, have elevated PTE levels beyond acceptable limits. Therefore, a precise assessment of PTE concentrations in representative soil samples was conducted using ICP-MS to evaluate contamination and identify its potential sources.

PTE pollution indices

The extent of PTE contamination was evaluated using several geochemical and ecological indices, including the contamination factor (CF), geoaccumulation index (Igeo), enrichment factor (EF), pollution load index (PLI), ecological contamination index (Ei), and ecological risk index (ERI).

Figure 4 shows heat-map distributions of (a) CF, (b) Igeo values, (c) EF, and (d) Ei for all elements and sampling sites. The mean CF values followed the order: Fe (0.8) < Co (0.9) ≈ Cu (0.9) ≈ Pb (0.9) ≈ Zn (0.9) < U (1) < Ni (1.3) ≈ Th (1.3) < Cr (1.6) < Cd (1.8) < As (4.3) (Fig. 4a). These results showcased considerable contamination by As, moderate contamination by Cd, Cr, Th, Ni, and U, and low contamination by the remaining elements. Arsenic exhibited contamination levels ranging from moderate (2.7) to very high (7.5), indicating substantial enrichment, whereas Cd ranged from low (0.8) to considerable (3.8) contamination. Based on the distribution of CF values across sampling locations, most samples showed moderate contamination for Cr (96%), Fe (16%), Co (24%), Ni (84%), Cu (28%), Zn (32%), Pb (24%), Th (100%), and U (56%), while 92% of samples were highly contaminated with As. Arsenic exhibits the highest enrichment, with 32% of samples showing moderate (min = 4) and 68% showing moderately severe enrichment (max = 5.9), indicating substantial enrichment. In contrast, Cd ranges from minor (min = 1.2) to moderate (max = 4) enrichment. Based on the spatial distribution of enrichment factors, most samples exhibited minor enrichment for Cr (100%), Co (68%), Ni (100%), Cu (56%), Zn (84%), Pb (96%), Cd (88%), Th (100%), and U (88%), while the remaining samples showed background levels.

Most PTEs exhibited ecological risk factor (Ei) values (Fig. 4d) below 40, corresponding to a low-risk classification. An Ei could not be assigned for Th because no chemotoxic-response coefficient is available in the published literature. The mean Ei values followed the ascending order: Fe (0.8) < Zn (0.9) < Cr (3.2) < Co (4.4) < Pb (4.6) < Cu (4.7) < Ni (6.4) < U (40.1) < As (43.2) < Cd (53.5). Based on the soil quality, As (56%), Cd (80%), and U (52%) in soil samples showed “fairly” Ei levels, while in a few samples (8%) Cd showcased a “considerable” toxic level, indicating significant ecological concern, whereas the remaining elements indicated low Ei levels. Overall, the mean Ei profile indicates that U, As, and Cd constitute the principal contributors to soil contamination within the study area. Additionally, this also indicates that U can pose both chemical and radiological contamination. The ecological risk index (ERI) calculated from the Ei ranged from 101 to 236 (Fig. 5b), indicating a transition from low to moderate risk, with a mean value of 162 ± 29 and an overall classification of “moderate” ecological risk. U, As, and Cd are the predominant contributors, showing higher ecological impact scores than the other PTEs. The dataset shows moderate variability (RSD = 17.8%), slight positive skewness (0.6), and low kurtosis (1.2), indicating a mildly right-skewed and relatively flat distribution.

Reported literature further suggests that soils with elevated Pb concentrations may also exhibit higher levels of 238U/226Ra or 232Th, as several Pb isotopes are produced as intermediate decay products in these decay series. For example, 214Pb (half-life of ~ 26.8 min) and 210Pb (half-life of ~ 22.3 years) originate from the 238U decay chain, while 212Pb (half-life ~ 10.6 h) is part of the 232Th decay series. These decay chains of 238U and 232Th ultimately terminate in stable isotopes, mainly 206Pb and 208Pb, respectively34. However, Pb accumulation in soils can also arise from anthropogenic sources; therefore, the relationship between Pb concentrations and U/Th levels should be interpreted cautiously.

Statistical analysis

Spearman correlation analysis

A correlation analysis was carried out to assess associations among constituents of the agricultural soil samples (Fig. 7). Since ~ 39% of the variables were non-normally distributed, Spearman’s rank correlation was applied to radionuclides, PTEs, physicochemical indicators, and micro-nutrients. Overall, 79.7% of variable pairs show significant correlations (p ≤ 0.05–0.001), ranging from moderate to extremely strong. Moderate positive correlations (ρ = 0.41–0.59) account for 27.5% of pairs, strong correlations (ρ = 0.6–0.74) for 30.7%, very strong correlations (ρ = 0.75–0.89) for 16.3%, and extremely strong correlations (ρ = 0.9–1) for 4.6%, all significant at p ≤ 0.01 or p ≤ 0.001.Fig. 7Spearman correlations among the studied variables, showing correlation coefficients and significance levels.

Spearman correlations among the studied variables, showing correlation coefficients and significance levels.

Strong inter-element correlations were observed among 40K, Cr, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Zn, As, and Pb, indicating that these radionuclides and trace elements co-vary under similar geochemical or environmental controls. Exceptionally strong positive correlations are observed among several element pairs (ρ ≥ 0.90, p ≤ 0.001). 40K shows an especially strong association with Fe (ρ = 0.93), which suggests interaction between potassium-bearing minerals and Fe-rich matrices, with possible enhancement from K-based fertilizers in cultivated soils. Fe is similarly correlated with Co (ρ = 0.98), Ni (ρ = 0.9), and Pb (ρ = 0.92). Thus, Fe oxides likely function as major geochemical scavengers, controlling the mobility and accumulation of Co, Ni, and Pb through adsorption and co-precipitation mechanisms89. Co and Ni are likewise strongly interrelated (ρ = 0.92).

These constituents also show notable associations with several physicochemical properties and nutrient fractions. Physicochemical variables display very strong to moderate correlations- EC and TDS (ρ = 1), TOC and N (ρ = 0.95), whereas pH and P show no statistically strong relationships with the other measured parameters. Overall, the correlation structure reflects predominantly diffuse, matrix-controlled accumulation rather than discrete point-source contamination, a pattern widely observed in agrarian soils90.

Natural factors include the composition of parent rocks, soil characteristics, geological setting, landscape features, and weathering. PCA was employed to assess associations between the concentration of NORs and PTEs as well as physicochemical parameters in surface agricultural soils. This pattern of explained variance indicates that the first principal component primarily represents the major structural trends in the dataset (Table 9). Hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) is a multivariate technique that organizes variables into nested groups based on similarity or distance metrics. HCA of the agricultural soil dataset resolved around three distinct geochemical associations reflecting different controlling processes. The first cluster is dominated by 238U and 232Th, with a minor contribution from Cd, indicating enrichment likely primarily associated with geogenic influences related to parent-rock composition, regional geology, and topographic characteristics. The association of Cd is sometimes also consistent with its presence as an impurity in phosphate-based fertilizers commonly applied in cultivated soils30.

In addition to natural geogenic sources, the second cluster, which comprised 40K, Fe, Co, Ni, Pb, As, Cr, and Zn, may reflect anthropogenic influences, particularly the application of fertilizers that are potassium-rich or may contain certain PTEs, pesticides, and industrial impacts from waste disposal and effluents. Although agricultural inputs may contribute to some of these elements, their strong association with K- and Fe-bearing minerals indicates that mineralogical composition, adsorption, and incorporation into clay minerals and iron oxides largely govern their distribution, with cultivation and irrigation further enhancing element retention92. The third cluster grouped Cu with EC, TDS, TOC, and N, indicating management-related controls associated with organic inputs, nutrient cycling, irrigation-induced salinity, and metal complexation with organic matter. Copper’s affinity for organic ligands explains its close association with TOC and N, while elevated EC and TDS indicate cumulative effects of fertilization and irrigation. In contrast, pH and phosphorus did not exhibit meaningful correlations with any of the identified clusters, implying that their behavior within the dataset is governed by independent mechanisms. Overall, the clustering highlights the combined influence of pedogenic controls and agricultural management on soil geochemistry.

Summary and conclusions

This study evaluates NORs, soil physicochemical properties, essential nutrients, and PTEs contamination in agricultural soils of the SAS Nagar district, Punjab, India. It integrates spatial analysis with pollution assessment and human health risk evaluation. Multivariate statistical approaches are applied to identify potential contamination sources. The mean activity concentrations of 238U, 232Th, and 40K were found to be 34.7 ± 3.1 Bq kg-1, 56.5 ± 8.4 Bq kg-1, and 556.8 ± 93.1 Bq kg-1, respectively, with levels all above corresponding global means of 33, 45, and 445 Bq kg-1, respectively. The soils are alkaline (mean pH = 7.6) with substantially higher EC (mean = 290.2 μS cm-1) than control samples (2.4 times), reflecting elevated salinity and dissolved ion content. The concentration of trace PTEs follows a decreasing sequence: Fe (29,148) > > Zn (67.4) > Cr (55.7) > Ni (25.5) > Cu (23.4) > Pb (18.6) > Th (13.9) > Co (8.8) > As (6.5) > U (2.8) > Cd (0.17). Pollution indices identify As and Cd as the principal contaminants, with U and Cr contributing moderately to soil quality deterioration. ~ 92% of soil samples indicate PLI values > 1, indicating widespread soil degradation, while the ecological risk index indicated low to moderate risk, with a mean value of 162 ± 29, classified as moderate ecological risk. Human health risk assessment revealed negligible non-carcinogenic risk (HQ/HI < 1) for all elements; however, Fe was the dominant contributor (~ 44.1% of cumulative HI), followed by Cr (23.8%) and As (23.1%). Total lifetime carcinogenic risk for As, Cd, Cr, Ni, and Pb was 5.76 × 10–6, 6.25 × 10–7, 1.92 × 10–5, 2.79 × 10–5, and 9.46 × 10–8, respectively. The CR values for As, Cr, and Ni fall within the acceptable risk range (10–6–10–4), indicating potential carcinogenic concern, whereas Pb exhibits the lowest risk. The contribution to carcinogenic risk follows the order: Ni > Cr > As > Cd > Pb. The concentration values of PTEs and NORs from the present study were compared with national and global studies and guideline values. The concentrations of Zn and Pb exceeded Indian background levels, with Zn approximately threefold higher, while Cr levels were nearly fivefold above USEPA limits; Zn also surpassed WHO/FAO guidelines.

Geospatial distributions through contour mapping show common hotspot zones of moderate enrichment for NORs and PTEs, indicating their co-occurrence in soils driven by geogenic inputs, pedogenic processes, agricultural practices (e.g., phosphate and compound fertilizers), and anthropogenic activities (e.g., sewage sludge, waste disposal, and roadside emissions), resulting in localized enrichment. Spearman correlation indicates that many soil parameters exhibit strong monotonic relationships. PCA showed that the first three components explained a major portion (78.2%) of the total variance. Combined PCA and HCA indicate that 238U and 232Th, with minor Cd contribution, are predominantly geogenic, linked to parent material and regional geology, although Cd may also derive from phosphate fertilizers. Other than geogenic, 40K, Fe, Co, Ni, Pb, As, Cr, and Zn reflect anthropogenic inputs (fertilizers, pesticides, and industrial effluents), while Cu with EC, TDS, TOC, and N indicates management-related controls such as organic inputs, nutrient cycling, salinity, and elemental-organic interactions. Collectively, these findings emphasize the need for targeted soil quality monitoring to safeguard long-term soil quality and public health, considering the combined influence of geogenic and anthropogenic factors and management-sensitive soil fractions. The present work can be extended to include a comprehensive radioecological assessment of soils and associated public health implications beyond PTEs’ impacts. Comparative analysis across different land-use types (e.g., industrial, recreational, and forested areas), along with depth-resolved studies considering crop root zones, would provide deeper insight into radionuclide and PTE distribution. Furthermore, increasing sample size, expanding spatial coverage, and incorporating seasonal and temporal variability of PTEs and NORs would enhance the robustness and generalizability of the findings.
