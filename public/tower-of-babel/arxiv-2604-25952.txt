Structural Conjectures for 4 × n 4\times n Chomp: Unique Extension, Asymptotic Ratios, and Period-112 Geometry
Arnav Garg

Abstract
This paper presents a computational study of P-positions (losing positions for the player to move) in 4 × n 4\times n Chomp, the combinatorial game played on a 4 × n 4\times n rectangular grid. An optimized C++ solver with bitpacked state representation tabulates all 4,316,097 P-positions for boards with n ≤ 500 n\leq 500 , constituting the most extensive computational study of 4 × n 4\times n Chomp to date. The analysis reveals four structural conjectures: (1) a Unique Extension property, stating that for any triple ( a , b , c ) (a,b,c) of row lengths, there is at most one valid fourth-row length d d completing a P-position; (2) convergence of row-length ratios to fixed asymptotic constants; (3) a period-112 modular structure governing the set of extendable triples; and (4) a linear cone geometry for the P-position set in ( a , b , c ) (a,b,c) -space. These findings suggest a richer deterministic structure in 4 × n 4\times n Chomp than previously suspected and raise new questions about the relationship between k k -row games for consecutive k k .



1
Introduction

Chomp is a two-player combinatorial game introduced by David Gale

[
3
]
and popularized by Martin Gardner
[
4
]
.
The game is played on an
m\times n
rectangular grid of squares.
Players alternate turns; on each turn, a player selects any remaining
square and removes it together with all squares above and to the right.
The square at position
(1,1)
(the top-left corner) is
poisoned
:
whoever is forced to take it loses.

A strategy-stealing argument establishes that the first player wins on
any board larger than
1\times 1
. If the second player had a winning
strategy
\sigma
, the first player could take only the bottom-right
square and then adopt
\sigma
, yielding a contradiction

[
1
]
. The argument is non-constructive. It gives no
information about the winning move or the structure of losing positions
(P-positions).

The complete structure of Chomp P-positions is known only for special
cases:

•

1\times n
: trivially solved (take all but the poison square).

•

2\times n
: fully solved; the P-positions are exactly the
positions
(a,a-1)
for
a\geq 1

[
1
]
.

•

n\times n
(square boards): the first player always wins by
taking position
(2,2)
and maintaining symmetry.

•

3\times n
: Zeilberger
[
5
]
gave a
sub-exponential algorithm for fixed bottom-row length and
tabulated P-positions up to bottom-row length
c\leq 115
.
Brouwer et al.
[
2
]
subsequently gave a
cubic-time algorithm and proved that infinitely many winning
first moves exist in the third row.

The
4\times n
case has received no systematic computational treatment.
This paper addresses that gap. The main contribution is a complete tabulation
of
4\times n
P-positions for
n\leq 500
together with four structural
conjectures. The most striking is the
Unique Extension
property
(Conjecture
1
): for any triple
(a,b,c)
of row lengths,
there is at most one valid fourth-row length
d
completing a P-position.
Verified across all 4,316,097 computed P-positions, this suggests the

4
-row game is in a precise sense a “deterministic lift” of the
3
-row
game. The fourth coordinate is uniquely forced by the first three.

2
Computational Setup

2.1
State Representation

A position in
4\times n
Chomp is fully described by a non-increasing
tuple
(a,b,c,d)
of non-negative integers with
a\geq b\geq c\geq d\geq 0
, where each coordinate gives the number of remaining
squares in the corresponding row. The initial position of a
4\times n

board is
(n,n,n,n)
. The unique terminal P-position is

(1,0,0,0)
, with only the poisoned square remaining.

2.2
Solver Algorithm

A retrograde solver was implemented in C++ around three design decisions:

1.

Bitpacked states
: Each tuple
(a,b,c,d)
is encoded
as a single
uint64_t
with four 16-bit fields, enabling
fast hashing and minimal memory footprint.

2.

Sparse storage
: Only P-positions (a small fraction of
all states) are stored in a hash set. N-positions are not stored;
the solver short-circuits as soon as any move to a P-position
is found.

3.

Bottom-up evaluation
: States are evaluated in increasing
order of total cell count, ensuring all successors are resolved
before each state is evaluated.

All P-positions for
n\leq 500
were tabulated in approximately 70 minutes
on an Apple M4 processor with 24 GB RAM, using at most 45 MB of memory.
Extending to
n=1000
would require roughly
2^{4}\times
more states
(scaling as
O(n^{4})
) and is feasible with additional compute time;
the
n\leq 500
range was chosen to balance coverage against verification cost.

2.3
Verification

The solver output was checked against two known ground truths:

1.

2\times n
subcase
: All P-positions with
c=d=0

satisfy
(a,b,0,0)=(a,a-1,0,0)
for all
a\geq 1
,
matching the known formula exactly.

2.

3\times n
subcase
: An independent memoized Python
solver verified all P-positions with
d=0
up to
n=50

against the
4\times n
output; no discrepancies were found.

A column-alignment bug in early CSV output was detected and corrected;
all results presented here use the verified column mapping, confirmed by
both subcases above.

3
Main Conjectures

3.1
Unique Extension

Conjecture 1

(Unique Extension)
.

For any triple
(a,b,c)
with
a\geq b\geq c\geq 0
, there exists
at most one non-negative integer
d
such that
(a,b,c,d)
is a
P-position of
4\times n
Chomp.

Remark 1
.

Equivalently, the projection map
\pi:(a,b,c,d)\mapsto(a,b,c)

is injective on the set of
4\times n
P-positions. This does not
hold for all three-element projections: multiple
(a,b,c,d)
may share
the same
(b,c,d)
triple.

Evidence.

Conjecture
1
was tested on all 4,316,097 P-positions
computed for
n\leq 500
. For each distinct triple
(a,b,c)
appearing
as a prefix, the number of valid extensions
d
was counted. In every case,
exactly one extension was found. No violation was detected.

Not every triple
(a,b,c)
extends. Approximately 20% of all valid triples
with
a\leq 500
appear as prefixes of P-positions. Every known
3\times n

P-position
(a,b,c)
lies in this 20%, but the converse fails: the majority
of extending triples are
3\times n
N-positions. The
4
th row therefore
“rescues” certain losing
3
-row configurations, turning them into
P-positions for the
4
-row game.

3.2
Asymptotic Ratios

Conjecture 2

(Asymptotic Ratios)
.

For P-positions
(a,b,c,d)
of
4\times n
Chomp with
a
large,
the row-length ratios converge to fixed constants
L_{1},L_{2},L_{3}
:

\frac{b}{a}\to L_{1},\quad\frac{c}{a}\to L_{2},\quad\frac{d}{a}\to L_{3},

as
a\to\infty
, with estimated values
L_{1}\approx 0.762
,

L_{2}\approx 0.499
,
L_{3}\approx 0.224
.

These values were estimated by three independent methods: windowed median
for
a>450
, rolling window, and power-law curve fitting
L+Ka^{-p}
,
all applied to the
n\leq 500
dataset.
L_{2}
and
L_{3}
are stable across
all three methods (maximum disagreement
<0.003
).
L_{1}
shows sensitivity
to startup noise in the curve-fit method and should be treated as accurate
only to
\pm 0.005
, pending confirmation at larger
n
. The convergence
behavior is shown in Figure
1
.

b/a
,

L_{1}\approx 0.762
,
L_{2}\approx 0.499
,
L_{3}\approx 0.224
.

No exact algebraic identity has been found for
L_{1}
,
L_{2}
, or
L_{3}
.
An exhaustive search over monic integer cubics with coefficients bounded
by
12
in absolute value found no polynomial whose roots match all three
limits simultaneously. It is worth noting that
\cos(3\pi/7)\approx 0.2225

differs from
L_{3}\approx 0.224
by only
0.0014
. This proximity may
reflect a true identity requiring larger
n
to confirm, or it may be
coincidental. No claim is made either way.

3.3
Period-112 Modular Structure

Conjecture 3

(Period-112 Mask)
.

The set of triples
(a,b,c)
that extend to
4\times n
P-positions
exhibits modular structure with fundamental period
112
.
Membership in the extending set is predicted with
79\%
accuracy by
a logistic classifier using only
c
and
(a-b)\bmod 112
.

The period-112 signal emerges from autocorrelation analysis of the

d
-value sequence. A sharp peak appears at lag 112 and its multiples,
as shown in Figure
2
. Note that
112=\mathrm{lcm}(7,8)\times 2
.
This is interpreted as the interaction of a period-7 signal inherited from
the
3\times n
sub-game and a period-8 signal of currently unknown origin.
The solver uses 16-bit field encoding, ruling out the period-8 as a
bitpacking artifact.

Figure 2:
Autocorrelation of the
d
-value sequence for
n\leq 500
.
The sharp peak at lag 112 indicates the fundamental modular
period. Subsidiary peaks appear at multiples of 112.

A chi-squared test across multiple moduli confirmed that modulus 112
gives the strongest statistical separation between extending and
non-extending triples (
\chi^{2}=12{,}433
for mod-112 vs.
\chi^{2}=6{,}996
for mod-56).

3.4
Linear Cone Geometry

Conjecture 4

(Linear Cone)
.

The set of extending triples
(a,b,c)
forms a linear cone in

\mathbb{R}^{3}
with asymptotic width

\mathrm{width}(c)\approx\frac{11}{8}\cdot c+f(c\bmod 112),

where
f
is a bounded periodic function with period
112
.

The slope
11/8=1.375
was estimated from slice-based width measurements
at
c=5,10,\ldots,300
. At each fixed
c
, the extending triples
occupy a contiguous band of
(a,b)
values whose width grows linearly in
c
.

4
Discussion

4.1
Comparison with
3\times n
Results

Zeilberger
[
5
]
gave a sub-exponential algorithm for

3\times n
Chomp with fixed bottom-row length and tabulated cases up
to bottom-row length
c\leq 115
, finding apparent linear patterns
within families of positions but no general formula. Brouwer et al.
[
2
]
proved that infinitely many winning first moves exist
in the third row and provided a cubic-time algorithm, yet again without
a closed-form description.

The
4\times n
results suggest a qualitatively different and in some
respects cleaner structure. If Conjecture
1
holds, the

4
-row game is globally characterized by a single injective function on
triples. That is a stronger global statement than anything proven or
conjectured for
3\times n
Chomp.

4.2
Generalization to
k\times n

If the Unique Extension property holds for
4\times n
, a natural
conjecture is that it generalizes: every
k\times n
P-position extends
uniquely to a
(k+1)\times n
P-position. This would imply that all

k\times n
Chomp is recursively determined by
2\times n
Chomp, which
is fully solved. At present this remains speculative, but it is testable
computationally for
k=5
.

5
Open Questions

1.

Proof of Unique Extension
: Can Conjecture
1

be proved? A combinatorial or game-theoretic argument explaining
why the fourth coordinate is uniquely determined would be highly
significant.

2.

Algebraic identity of limits
: Are
L_{1},L_{2},L_{3}

algebraic numbers satisfying a common polynomial equation?
The proximity of
L_{3}
to
\cos(3\pi/7)
warrants further
investigation at larger
n
.

3.

Generalization
: Does the Unique Extension property hold
for
5\times n
Chomp? Testing this requires substantially more
computation (state space of order
O(n^{5})
).

4.

Origin of period-8
: The period-112 signal decomposes
as
\mathrm{lcm}(7,8)\times 2
. The factor of 7 is plausibly
inherited from
3\times n
periodicity. The factor of 8 and the
additional factor of 2 remain unexplained.

5.

Closed-form mask
: Is there a closed-form description
of the 20% of triples
(a,b,c)
that extend to
4\times n

P-positions?
