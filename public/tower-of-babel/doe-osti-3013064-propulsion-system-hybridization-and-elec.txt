 Executive Summary
Historically, heavy-duty off-road equipment has prioritized performance, reliability, and cost, with
limited emphasis on efficiency. While increasing carbon dioxide (CO2) regulations have driven a
focused effort for efficiency in on-road vehicles, off-road machines have remained largely
overlooked, leaving significant opportunities for efficiency improvements and emissions
reductions. To address this overlooked industry, Michigan Technological University’s Advanced
Power Systems Research Center (APS LABS) worked with Pettibone to determine a unique
electrified architecture for Pettibone’s heavy-duty off-road material handler, the Cary-Lift 204i,
with a goal of 20% fuel savings. The base Cary-Lift uses a diesel engine with no electrification.

An extended range electric vehicle (EREV) was built to demonstrate these fuel savings, exceeding
the project goal with a final demonstrated fuel savings of 47% on custom operating cycles. This
hybrid architecture incorporates one electric machine per axle, one electric machine for the
hydraulic system, an engine and generator for battery sustainment, and a large battery for electric-
only operation and load leveling. An EREV has not been researched before in the heavy-duty off-
road space, so this research has provided novel contributions to the heavy-duty off-road industry.

The demonstrated fuel savings of 47% were achieved while maintaining or improving the base
machine's performance and Tier 4 final emissions. Similar equipment in the heavy-duty off-road
space could benefit from electrification architecture studies to determine options that reduce CO2e
emissions while also considering cost. Over the lifetime of the Cary-Lift, this EREV was found to
significantly reduce carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e) emissions, with the opportunity to reduce
lifetime costs when using a lithium iron phosphate (LFP) battery. While not included in this report,
the details on the reduced EREV lifetime costs using an LFP battery may be found in multiple
published papers [1, 2].

The work performed in this project benefits the public by addressing environmental, economic,
and societal challenges in the following ways:

 • Reducing Greenhouse Gas (GHG) Emissions: Significant reductions in carbon dioxide
 equivalent (CO2e) lead to a lessened greenhouse gas burden, reducing global warming
 effects.
 • Guiding Sustainable Decision-Making: Electrification architecture analyses provide
 manufacturers and customers with a framework to determine electrification solutions based
 on lifecycle analyses of CO2e and cost.
 • Lowering Lifetime Costs: Lifetime cost analyses showed effective electrification
 architectures that significantly reduced CO2e emissions while also reducing lifetime cost,
 benefitting businesses and consumers alike.
 • Supporting Customization for Diverse Applications: Operating cycle development
 provided a framework for manufacturers and others to develop unique operating cycles
 specific to their applications.
 • Reducing Noise Pollution: The EREV allows for all-electric operation, reducing noise
 pollution.

 Introduction
Increasingly stringent emissions regulations have continued driving down criteria emissions in
heavy-duty applications. For on-road vehicles, EPA’s GHG Phase 3 requirements released in 2024
specify significant reduction requirements for light, medium, and heavy-duty on-road vehicles of
up to 60% by 2032 [3, 4]. While these regulations are specific to on-road applications, off-road
machines are likely to also be required to demonstrate GHG reductions in the future. As such, the
Department of Energy (DOE) has begun looking into off-road solutions to achieve fuel savings.
This project directly aims for this GHG reduction in the off-road industry, analyzing the Pettibone
Cary-Lift 204i, a heavy-duty off-road material handler seen Figure 1, with base machine
specifications in Table 1.

At 9% of total transportation emissions in the U.S. [5], the off-road industry is a significant source
of emissions. These GHG emissions, often measured in carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e), can be
targeted in this lesser-studied area, with opportunity for significant fuel savings. The goal of this
project was to demonstrate a 20% reduction in fuel consumption for the Pettibone Cary-Lift,
utilizing electrification technology.

This work details this project from start to finish. This starts with the brief summary of the project,
before moving into the bulk of the results. Results outline the instrumentation and baseline testing
efforts that were required to enable high-fidelity machine modeling. The modeling allowed for a
robust analysis of different vehicle architectures that could achieve significant fuel savings while
also accounting for cost. A final architecture of an EREV was chosen based on this architecture
analysis, which led into the prototype build phase. A design study was conducted in CAD, enabling
component purchasing and later installation onto the machine. In parallel, the thermal management
and control systems were developed and tested, before the final prototype machine was calibrated
and validated, after which the prototype EREV was tested on the custom APS LABS operating
cycles. The final portions of this paper outline the future work and the products developed during
this project.

The EREV was found to reduce the equivalent energy consumption by 47% when compared to the
baseline machine. With a target of 20%, this 47% value exceeds the project goals and demonstrates
the significant fuel and energy-saving potential that electrification technologies introduce to the
off-road space. The EREV is a particularly beneficial and unique application, with no research
having been conducted on EREVs for heavy-duty off-road applications [1]. This novel EREV

 Plan Adjustments During Project
While the project goal was exceeded, timing difficulties were experienced due to long lead times
on certain components. Originally, the project was to occur over a 3-year period. Due to significant

 13 of 78

COVID-related delays, including the manufacture of the baseline machine and travel restrictions,
the effective start date of the project was pushed back 18 months through a no-cost time extension.
COVID supply chain issues further delayed the project due to hardware availability issues. For
example, the mechanical hydraulic pumps had a 26-week lead time, extending the original project
completion date of March 2024 to September 2024.

Further delays were experienced due to supplier issues with the purchased components. Examples
include the front propulsion motor containing an assembly aid that was accidentally left inside of
the motor housing when it left the factory, requiring time spent in diagnoses then the motor being
sent back to the manufacturer. A catastrophic failure of the generator inverter caused by a
manufacturing defect led to further delays. There were multiple delays with the HV batteries
including initial delivery being much longer than the supplier had originally predicted, then
multiple failures of the battery resulting from both design issues and manufacturing issues,
requiring the battery to be sent back to the manufacturer. Collectively these delays required
another no-cost time extension to June 2025.

 Instrumentation for Testing
The conventional machine was instrumented for measuring and recording performance in three
main categories including the engine subsystem, vehicle subsystem, and hydraulic subsystem.
Most transducers were added in the hydraulic subsystem which was the least understood at the
time of baseline testing. The added instruments allowed for measurements of flow, pressure, and
position, some examples of which are shown in Figure 2. Some other instruments were added in
the vehicle subsystem and nearly all information that was recorded from the engine subsystem
originated from OEM sensors.

In the hydraulic subsystem, pressure was measured for the four main hydraulic functions: lift,
reach, tilt, and the baler, as well as their linear position using string potentiometers. The flow rate
was calculated by using two separate orifice plates on the suction side of both the main and steering
hydraulic pumps, which were designed based on ASME MFC-3M. The measurement was made
using high-accuracy differential pressure transducers, and the flow rate was calculated later in
post-processing instead of in real time. This was one part needed for understanding energy input
into the hydraulic system along with pressure. Flow was measured on the inlet side5 of the pumps,
and pressure was measured on the outlet side, so any flow to case drain on the pumps was not
accounted for and assumed to be negligible while calculating fluid power and energy.

5
  Measuring flow on the outlet of the pumps was considered, however, because of the high absolute pressure on the
pump outlet this would have added significant cost and complexity to the measurement, as well as introducing a new
safety risk from a burst of the flowmeter.

 14 of 78

The other two measurement subsystems did not involve nearly as much additional instrumentation
partly because existing sensors characterized them well and were easier to characterize using fewer
sensors. Some examples of data channels from the vehicle subsystem that would be included in a
baseline test log include transmission output shaft speed and some example channels from the
engine subsystem include modeled fuel rate, engine speed, and coolant temperature. This
information was gathered over J1939 CAN from the engine controller. GPS information was
gathered for the machine by adding a CSS Electronics GPS module.

Data acquisition itself was accomplished using a National Instruments PXI chassis with a
multifunction analog/digital input module and a high-speed CAN module that was installed inside
the machine’s cabin. Originally, a National Instruments cDAQ was used for all data logging
including thermocouples, high-speed CAN messages, and recording analog signals but was later
exchanged for the PXI chassis to enable headless operation which did not require the use of a PC
in tandem with the DAQ. This streamlined data collection during internal operation but, more
importantly, was implemented for customer testing to eliminate the need for an operator to get
trained in using the DAQ and saving data. A list of all instrumentation and important CAN signals
were sampled at 200 Hz and are included in Appendix A: Instrumentation and Channels from
Baseline Testing.

 Operating Cycle Creation
An operating cycle had to be developed for baseline testing of this machine because there was no
existing cycle that sufficiently matched the real-world use that this machine experienced. With the
intent of producing a 1D simulation of this machine, repeatable operation was necessary to provide
data that did not require much processing to be useful. Applying a cycle from slightly different
machines, such as port drayage trucks, wheel loaders, or indoor forklifts, would leave some typical
use unnoticed and skew the true application of a Cary Lift. It was for this reason that APS LABS
drive cycles were created through discussions with the original equipment manufacturer and
analysis of this machine operating at customer businesses, a process that is visualized in Figure 3.

 15 of 78

 Figure 3. Operating cycle creation process.

Pettibone was an important source of information for all insight regarding the typical use of a Cary-
Lift that was not identified or could not be explained by the data collected at customer facilities.
Discussion with the OEM was also instrumental in providing answers to questions that would have
otherwise prevented progress in modeling efforts before a test could be conducted internally or
due to a test being outside the scope of this project like fleet statistics. This part of the cycle creation
process did not end before moving on to the next steps, but rather continued throughout the entire
duration as progress was made.

Besides the OEM, another source for cycle creation was multiple videos of Cary-Lift operation
showing real operators using these machines to conduct their daily work. This provided multiple
primary sources for interpreting operator behavior and for confirming or changing the APS Lab’s
understanding of numerical or statistical cycle attributes. It also provided a perfect starting point
for preparing and handling the information gathered from customer locations that used the baseline
machine from this project.

The instrumented baseline machine was brought to two separate facilities for data collection that
use Cary-Lifts in their daily operations. The first facility was a sawmill that uses Cary-Lift
machines to grab logs from a yard and feed them to a mill or to unload log trucks that bring wood
to the yard. The second facility uses Cary-Lift machines to move around large sections of pipe by
unloading rail cars, unloading class 8 trucks, and bringing pipe sections into a coating facility.
These two applications had similar general operations, which were related back to an internal
operating cycle at APSRC.

  Operation at APSRC
A controllable and repeatable test environment needed to be created for internal operation where
isolated tests could be conducted without bringing the machine to a production facility for every
single study. The APS Labs test course was created on site with two prominent landmarks that
defined a general layout of the course. The north end had a shipping container (see Figure 4) that
was raised to 4.4 meters tall for loading and unloading a test weight and the south end of the test
course had a zone marked for picking up and dropping off the load at ground level. A satellite view
of the test course during the loading and unloading of the container can be seen in Figure 5.

 Cycle Creation Method
The creation of APS Labs material cycles followed some of the methods used to create light-duty
vehicle certification cycles, specifically the Urban Dynamometer Driving Schedule (UDDS).
Some operational data existed for the Cary-Lift 204 that was received by the OEM, which provided
a foundation for things like the typical lift height of the forks, the average speed of the machine,
the average distance traveled, and engine parameters like idle time. The test vehicle was operated
on a short repetitive cycle in conjunction with these parameters which provided a basis for
standardizing a cycle that would be used for all baseline testing and in simulation.

 17 of 78

One driver collected 13 short cycles of loading operation and 14 short cycles of unloading
operation, which made up the entire pool from which one individual cycle of each type was
selected and modified into a master. Some of the original cycles from this pool did not conform to
a typical cycle, which was judged by how close the machine speed followed the majority of other

Hydraulic system utilization had to be standardized along with machine velocity because fuel
consumption can change drastically depending on how much material handling was accomplished.
The amount of energy used by the hydraulic system was simplified into three main items: rate of
change in linear position of a hydraulic function, mass being lifted by the forks, and change in
position of the forks. The rate of change in position and the amount of mass being lifted were
chosen to remain constant for baseline testing as they were the simplest to do so, which helped to
reduce degrees of freedom and cycle-to-cycle variation while measuring fuel consumption. This
left changes in fork position to be monitored and controlled throughout a cycle using string
potentiometer data, an effective way to repeat useful work and reproduce standardized tests.

Four main hydraulic functions were analyzed in baseline testing of the machine which fully
describe the material handling linkage at any time. Lift, reach, tilt, and baler functions were used
to manipulate the test material and all four were assigned a standard position trace through time
and can be seen in Figure 8. Standardized position through time history comes from the same load

the relationship between tractive effort timing and hydraulic utilization. Other hydraulic functions

 Final Architecture Selection

The decision on the final architecture chosen for this project relied on many components, including
novelty, feasibility, project time risk, CO2e emissions, cost, and more. Ultimately, the project
aimed to achieve a 20% fuel savings, so CO2e savings were a central consideration that drove
much of the decision. Also, there continues to be a strong push toward electrification in the on-
road industry, as off-road industries also pursue savings, like in the John Deere 644K series hybrid
wheel loader [13].

Keeping these factors in mind, the final architecture chosen for the demonstration vehicle was the
EREV. The following list compiles some of the key reasons for this choice:

 33 of 78

 • Countries and organizations are increasingly incorporating carbon neutrality into their
 strategic goals. For example, the United States aims to achieve a net-zero emissions
 economy by 2050 [5], driving efforts to lower carbon emissions. Also, reducing CO2e
 emissions was a central focus of this project.
 • This architecture offers a versatile and robust platform, enabling the research team to
 explore innovative approaches to decarbonize the heavy-duty off-road sector further.
 • The EREV design allows continuous operation, similar to the base Cary-Lift. In contrast,
 a battery electric vehicle (BEV) architecture would require downtime for recharging,
 presenting challenges for shifts lasting more than eight hours and / or production
 environments that run back to back shifts or a full 24 hour production schedule.
 • The EREV could be prototyped using primarily Commercial Off-The-Shelf (COTS)
 components for major hardware.
 • Beyond significant CO2e reductions, the EREV delivers several secondary benefits. While
 these are outside the scope of this discussion, they include features such as improved
 gradeability, enhanced acceleration, selectable 2WD/4WD operation, continuous off-board
 AC power export, silent operation, and more.

 Thermal Management
The hybrid machine’s thermal management system (TMS) was composed of two separate
water/ethylene-glycol (WEG) cooling circuits distinguished by operating temperature. The first
system was dedicated to the engine and operated nominally up to 90°C engine coolant temperature.
The second system was for all water-cooled electric driveline components including the battery
and was designed to operate at coolant temperatures below 40°C. The decision to use a separate
electric driveline cooling system was driven by specifications outlined by component
manufacturers, which mandated a coolant temperature limit much less than what was typical for
the engine. The engine cooling system utilized the original water-to-air heat exchanger from the
baseline machine, which was sized to handle heat rejection loads from a 6.7L 6-cylinder engine.
In a production design this could be downsized to save space and weight. One main difference in
cooling system hardware between the baseline and hybrid machine was the change to a clutched
mechanical fan instead of a fixed mechanical fan. This change reduced accessory loads on the
engine and contributed to reducing fuel consumption by only engaging when needed. The clutched
mechanical fan was chosen over an electric fan because that particular fan is only needed when
the engine is running and the electric fan would add additional energy conversion losses.

Another method for reducing auxiliary energy consumption on the hybrid machine stemmed from
utilizing the mechanical fan on the engine to reduce conversion losses. Two radiators were
connected in series on the electric driveline cooling system where the first radiator was cooled by
the engine’s mechanical fan and the second radiator was cooled by dedicated high-voltage electric
fans. A system with two separate fans reduced auxiliary load on the machine by running a
mechanical fan directly with engine shaft torque instead of converting shaft torque into electrical
energy that undergoes multiple conversions. The ability to cool electric driveline components
without the engine running was maintained by keeping a second radiator that used electric fans.
Even in times when the engine is not running (and thus the fan not spinning) Radiator 1 still

A standard WEG cooling system with a water-to-air heat exchanger was not enough to maintain
the low coolant temperatures required for the high-voltage battery at high ambient temperatures.
This was addressed by combining the electric driveline WEG circuit with a chiller system that
used a high-voltage refrigerant compressor. Similar in concept to the dual radiator setup for cooling
WEG, a dual compressor system was developed that utilized an electric and mechanical
compressor. Once again, the design intent was to reduce auxiliary loads on the machine between
engine-on and engine-off periods by relying on components driven by shaft torque of the engine
(when the engine is running) instead of incurring conversion losses for high-voltage devices. A
diagram of the refrigerant system that was built for the hybrid machine appears in Figure 36. Two
more features of the refrigerant system included a dual condenser system and integration with
cabin air conditioning. Having a system with two refrigerant compressors designed for use in
different operating conditions, two condensers were utilized to support battery chilling
independently from engine state. The condensers were distributed between the dual radiator setup
which enabled the use of both fan drives (mechanical or high-voltage) and their corresponding

 42 of 78

condenser depending on what mode the machine was in. The implementation and refrigerant
schematic is also shown in Figure 36.

Figure 36. Refrigerant circuit featuring multiples of each component to minimize conversion losses and
maintain chiller operation between charge depleting and charge sustaining modes of operation.

Other components beyond the high-voltage battery can tolerate coolant temperatures above 40°C
so a recirculation loop was integrated that focused chilled coolant on the battery pack. When
chilled coolant was needed, a 3-way valve would change position and divert coolant leaving the
battery pack back to the battery pump inlet instead of returning through the hot-side manifold and
through both radiators. This was done because the chilled coolant exiting the battery pack was still
a lower temperature than the rest of the electric driveline WEG system, so preventing the coolant
from warming up further helped to reduce compressor work on the fluid. Although returning
chilled coolant from the battery outlet through both radiators would reduce overall system
temperature, the WEG heat exchange to air was enough to meet coolant temperature requirements
for components other than the battery and would use more energy than necessary.

Low ambient temperatures were also a key factor in hybrid TMS design, specifically temperatures
at which the high-voltage battery charge and discharge currents were limited. At -15°C cell
temperature, continuous charge current was reduced significantly to around 13% of that possible
with nominally 30 – 60°C cell temperatures. A supplementary diesel-fired heater was installed for
pack preconditioning in cold ambient temperatures to facilitate faster warmup times and reduce
excessive charge/discharge losses. The heater was installed between the coolant inlet port and
outlet port which provided heat directly to the pack.

 Control Software

The requirements, as defined in the previous section, needed to be implemented in software for
the vehicle systems to properly communicate. Matlab Simulink was used in combination with
Raptor-Dev to develop the entire machine’s software architecture. This platform allowed for the
implementation of control requirements for all the machine's subsystems. For instance, efficiency
tables were incorporated for various components, such as the tractive electric machines, to
determine power consumption values. These values were calculated using the efficiency tables
along with the motor's reported torque and speed. This data provided critical insights into power
consumption, which were used to inform the overall power management strategy.

Another part of the control development process involved the use of two different strategies for
hydraulic control. The base form of control involved a pedal-following control strategy (Figure
38), which mimicked the operation of the base machine. In this control configuration, the hydraulic
electric machine was always spinning when the machine was turned on, and the speed of the
electric machine was dependent on the accelerator pedal position, with a minimum speed of 600
rpm and a maximum speed of 2500 rpm per hydraulic pump limits. This maintained the operation
of the base machine (beneficial for customer acceptance) with no changes required to the hydraulic
system’s circuitry or valving (beneficial for simplicity).

 45 of 78

 Figure 38. Simplified representation of pedal-following hydraulic schematic. Identical to base hydraulic
 schematic, except pumps powered by an electric machine instead of engine’s power take-off.

As observed in Figure 9, the Cary-Lift spends nearly half of all operational time in idling
conditions. The pedal-following hydraulic control requires the hydraulic electric machine to be
constantly spinning, with speed changes based on the accelerator pedal position. During periods
when the machine is idling and not requiring hydraulic power for brakes, steering, or material
handling, the hydraulic machine could be turned off to reduce energy consumption. This led to the
development of a load-following hydraulic control, where the hydraulic electric machine would
only be on when hydraulic power was required, and it would change speed based on the required
flow rate. The required flowrate can be easily identified by the control system by observing the
pump’s swashplate positions. This load-following system would turn off when hydraulic power
was not required, saving significant energy during idle periods in the Cary-Lift’s operation, and
when hydraulic power is needed, only spin the pumps fast enough to meet the flow demand. Figure
39 shows the changes to the hydraulic schematic required for the implementation of the load-
following controls.

 46 of 78

 Figure 39. Simplified hydraulic schematic for load-following hydraulic system.

Besides the mechanical changes required, control elements are needed to account for the proper
control of the hydraulic system. Pressure sensors (see Figure 39) were added to view the pressure
in different locations in the system. If the pressure increased in the load sensing line, this meant a
hydraulic event was required, turning on the hydraulic electric machine when above a threshold.
Steering and system pressures would turn on the hydraulic electric machine when their pressures
reached a lower threshold, corresponding to an inability to provide adequate pressure for their
functions. The swashplate positions of the hydraulic pumps (hydraulicly determined based on load
sense pressure and system pressure) were fed into the control logic with a target of a 75%
swashplate angle. If the swashplate position increased beyond 75%, the hydraulic electric
machine’s speed was increased, and if the swashplate position fell below 75%, the hydraulic
electric machine’s speed was decreased. After 2 seconds at minimum speed, the hydraulic electric
machine was turned off. Additional accumulators were added to smooth the transition between
pump off and pump on by supplying fluid energy immediately upon an operators command until
the pumps were supplying fluid. The time from operator command to the pumps supplying fluid
was on the order of less than 1 second, thus the accumulators do not need to be large (each
accumulator used was ~4 L in volume).

Besides the hydraulic system, another area where control development benefitted machine
operation was in the creation of several different the accelerator pedal maps. In the prototype
machine, the operator has four selectable pedal maps (through the rotation of a dial), including a
green mode for highest efficiency that maximized regen and decreased peak acceleration torque.
Additionally, a “ludicrous” mode enabled maximum motor torque for fast accelerations and
increased productivity. The final two modes included a rigging mode for low-speed, precise
operation, and a base vehicle simulation, attempting to mimic the power delivery of the baseline

 47 of 78

Pettibone Cary-Lift. These pedal maps increase the functionality of the machine, enabling
operators to easily select different modes based on their desired operational goals.

Other calibration items needed to be performed for machine subsystems, for example, the engine
and generator for battery charging. The battery charge limits meant that the generator torque and
speed needed to be coordinated to target peak efficiency while remaining within charging limits.
To perform this, the overall efficiency of the systems was derived to understand the engine
operating points that would lead to peak efficiency while charging. This efficiency included the
engine, generator, inverter, and battery charging efficiency for a combined efficiency accounting
for all components. Then, the desired engine operating points were defined during generation
events and overlaid on the efficiency map, as seen in Figure 43, where 571 Nm of engine torque
was ideal from 1730 rpm down to 1550 rpm. At 1550 rpm at high torque, the torque coupler
connecting the engine to the generator nears its no-fly region, so the torque values begin to
decrease at a constant 1550 rpm speed, targeting the highest efficiency based on power demand.

These engine operating points were achieved based on the real-time power consumption of the
electric components on the machine. The ideal engine operating point at 35% overall efficiency6

machine, meaning the battery charge limits were not exceeded. This power-following control of
the engine and generator was developed and calibrated due to an expected 2.5% fuel savings from
simulation estimates.

 Final Demonstrations

 APS LABS Demonstration Results

Validation of the hybrid machine’s performance took place through a combination of isolated tests,
which included load/unload cycles, the transport cycle, and measurements of idle fuel
consumption. During operation on load/unload cycles, the machine started with a fully charged
battery and was used continuously in charge depleting (CD) mode for around 1 hour. After
approximately 1 hour of continuous high-energy operation, the battery State of Charge had reached
the lower threshold, and the machine switched into a charge sustaining (CS) mode and continued
to run load/unload cycles for another hour to ensure steady state operation. A total of 30 load and
30 unload cycles were completed between charge depleting and charge sustaining modes. Due to
the engine’s run time being longer than cycle length during CS mode, there were 16 cycles with

the engine running for the entire duration, 29 cycles of electric-only operation, and 15 cycles where
the engine was only running for part of the load or unload cycle. The average equivalent fuel rate
for the baseline machine, hybrid with engine on, CS mode, and CD mode are compared in Figure
45. The hybrid machine’s average fuel rate with the engine on was slightly higher than the baseline
machine’s average fuel rate. However, CS mode mixes engine-on and engine-off periods nearly
equally while maintaining SOC on load/unload cycles, which resulted in a CS average fuel rate of
around 11.8 L/h.

 Conclusions
This report outlines the development of a prototype electrified heavy-duty off-road material
handler. Starting with a diesel-powered Pettibone Cary-Lift 204i, the goal of the project was to
develop an electrified prototype machine that would demonstrate 20% fuel savings while
maintaining performance and emissions standards. To understand the baseline machine and its fuel
consumption, the machine was instrumented with several transducers. Custom operating cycles
were created for the Cary-Lift, as no standard operating cycles existed for this unique machine.
Experimental data recordings on these custom operating cycles provided baseline testing data to
calibrate and validate a model of the baseline machine’s propulsion and hydraulic systems.

After the modeled results successfully matched the experimental results, an architecture study
commenced, involving rapid architecture analyses to determine the most promising electrified
architectures. This involved the use of multiple rounds of Pugh-style decision matrices, informed
by first principles analyses and engineering expertise, to determine a short list of the most
promising architectures to compare. Each of the most promising architectures had a high-fidelity
model created to more thoroughly understand their operation and accurately characterize their
expected fuel and energy consumption. CO2e and cost analyses were performed on each of these
architectures to compare each architecture with one another and determine the final architecture to
continue with for a prototype machine. The final architecture that was chosen was an EREV
(Figure 23), chosen due to its significant CO2e savings, ability to support continuous operation,
and unique research potential, as elaborated in the Final Architecture Selection section. See Figure
50 for a comparison between the base machine and the EREV, noting the significant architecture
changes with insignificant machine visual changes, pointing to the efforts taken in this project to
maintain operational and visual commonality with the base machine.

 57 of 78

 Figure 50. Comparison of base machine (left) to EREV (right).

Once the EREV was chosen, a detailed CAD study began to understand where electrified
components could fit on the machine. With component fit ensured, the baseline machine needed
to be disassembled and then built with all electrified components, as detailed in the section
Prototype Machine Assembly. In parallel, the thermal system was designed to account for these
additional electrified components. Additionally, the control system was also developed in parallel
with machine assembly and thermal system design. Control development, integration, and testing
were crucial components of this project, and the high-level details are provided in the Control
Development and Control Calibration sections.

The final portion of the project involved the machine demonstrations at the APS LABS, where the
custom operating cycles were created and the baseline machine was tested. The results showed
fuel savings that exceeded the project goal of 20%, with an equivalent energy consumption
reduction of 47% for an 8-hour shift. These real-world results exemplify the significant
efficiency benefits of moving this heavy-duty off-road material handler to an EREV architecture.
See Table 7 for a performance comparison between the base machine and the EREV.

Overall, the project team views this project as a clear success, as the project fuel consumption goal
was significantly overachieved, and all other targets were met or exceeded, while also adding new
features and capabilities that did not exist on the baseline machine.

 Future Work
The limited research available in the heavy-duty off-road sector presents numerous opportunities
for further exploration in energy savings. The authors have not identified any studies focused on
EREVs within this area, highlighting a significant gap that this work aims to fill. In light of this,
the following list outlines potential future directions for improving efficiency and achieving energy
savings, presented in no particular order:

 1. The prototype machine could easily demonstrate a further reduction in fuel consumption
 and CO2e with the existing architecture and relatively low effort. For example:
 a. As discussed, the maximum c-rate of the battery in actual operation was
 significantly lower that the battery manufacturer predicted. On discharge, this
 limited activities like maximum acceleration, however, more importantly this
 prevented the engine / generator system from operating at peak efficiency during
 charge-sustaining operation. Alleviating this limitation, even if just for a short
 period of time through a recalibration of the Battery Management System for a
 short demonstration test, is expected to result in a measurable increase in overall
 system efficiency.

 59 of 78

 b. A clutched mechanical fan was used on the engine. However, even when de-
 energized, the fan clutch transmitted significantly more torque to the fan that
 expected, resulting in fan losses when fan cooling would otherwise not be needed.
 Additional time spent to identify a different fan clutch could result in improvements
 with near zero cost.
 c. Additional fuel consumption improvements are likely simply with additional
 calibration time. For example, optimization of the “green” pedal map, optimization
 of the load following hydraulics, more aggressively transitioning the engine to
 high-load charging upon startup, more aggressive allowing the engine to operate at
 higher Charge Air Temperature8, optimization of the engine-on / engine-off SOC
 setpoints, optimization of the electric pumps and fans used for cooling the
 electrification hardware, etc..
 d. The team feels that items a, b, and c described here could easily walk the
 demonstrated 47% fuel consumption improvement to a full 50%.
 2. Distributed electrified hydraulic systems with electro-hydraulic actuators.
 a. Electro-hydraulic actuators have found increasing levels of research lately as they
 have provided high-efficiency operation of up to 84.7% [14]. These actuators,
 however, do not meet the safety requirements of the Cary-Lift’s system, as
 counterbalance valves are required for load-holding in the event of hydraulic
 failures. To understand the savings from these high-efficiency actuators, a study is
 needed to evaluate electro-hydraulic actuators as a replacement for a traditional
 load-sensing pressure-compensated system with counterbalance valves.
 b. This same safety requirement for counterbalanced actuators prevented this project
 team from recovering potential energy of the load. A study investigating electro-
 hydraulic actuators could, and should, also investigate potential energy recovery.

8
 Through testing, it was observed that it was the air temperature exiting the Charge Air Cooler that lead to the
mechanical fan clutch being engaged, not coolant temperature, thus calibrating to allow for a higher Inlet Air
Temperature will reduce fan energy loss.

 Path to Market
After seeing the significant fuel savings and performance benefits of the electrified architecture
studied during this project, Pettibone is now motivated to go to market with an electrified version
of their Cary-Lift. Pettibone plans to begin developing its first electrified Cary-Lift in 2027, with
a target sale date of 2030. Based on the work from this project, they aim to make an EREV. Since
the technology is scalable, Pettibone intends to study the benefits of using this architecture on other
product lines that serve the railroad and construction markets. The EREV appears to be an
excellent solution to reducing noise and exhaust emissions in underground or enclosed areas, for
instance.

From the outset of this project, this commercialization was a primary focus. Decisions made
throughout were centered on the feasibility of commercialization. For instance, an engine used in
another Pettibone product was selected for this application thus minimizing inventory and supply

 63 of 78

chain logistics for Pettibone, while also potentially leveraging economies of scale in pricing.
Similarly, the electric machine that serves as the generator was selected to be the same exact part
number as the units used for the front and rear traction motors, even though functionally, the
generator could be a smaller unit. Additionally, an effort was made to use Commercial Off-The-
Shelf (COTS) components whenever possible to minimize the number of custom items required.
The APS LABS team will continue, to the extent possible, to support Pettibone's efforts to bring
the Cary-Lift (or other Pettibone products) to market.
