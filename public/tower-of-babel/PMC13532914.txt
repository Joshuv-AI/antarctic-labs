Performance of Survival Prediction Tools in Patients With Gastrointestinal Stromal Tumors: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Alice Zhu, Chloe Wong, Neill K. J. Adhikari, Ka Yan Ip, Alyson Mahar, Amy T. Hsu, Julie Hallet, Natalie Coburn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs) are the most common mesenchymal tumors of the gastrointestinal tract. We conducted a systematic review and meta‐analysis of studies that developed, validated, or updated clinical prognostic tools to predict survival in adults with primary GIST, identifying 47 eligible studies (38 development and 9 validation studies). Meta‐analyses were performed to assess performance in external validation cohorts. The MSKCC nomogram was most frequently validated, with a pooled C‐statistic of 0.78 for recurrence‐free survival.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs) are the most common mesenchymal tumors of the gastrointestinal tract [1]. While surgical resection with curative intent remains the cornerstone of management for primary localized GIST, recurrence risk remains high, and long‐term outcomes vary considerably. The advent of tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) has revolutionized the management of GIST, offering significant survival benefits for patients at high risk of recurrence after resection [2, 3]. Accordingly, accurate prognostication is essential in guiding the use adjuvant therapies and surveillance strategies.

Numerous systems have been developed to stratify GIST patients according to recurrence risk [4, 5]. In 2018, Khoo et al. conducted a systematic review of these tools and found that most relied on expert opinion, categorized patients into broad risk groups, and lacked statistical modeling or validation [6]. Since then, advances in personalized medicine and statistical techniques have led to the development of more refined tools, including nomograms and individualized prediction models that estimate survival outcomes, moving beyond recurrence risk alone. Survival models incorporate the competing risk of death and provide a more comprehensive assessment, particularly relevant in the context of effective adjuvant therapies and comorbid conditions.

Despite their potential, these newer tools have not been critically appraised. Poorly designed or insufficiently validated models risk overestimating predictive performance, resulting in inaccurate and non‐generalizable risk estimates [7]. Understanding how these tools have been developed, validated, and reported is essential to assess their predictive accuracy, generalizability, and clinical utility.

The objectives of this systematic review and meta‐analysis are therefore to (1) provide a comprehensive overview of current prognostic tools developed to predict individual survival in adult patients diagnosed with GIST and to evaluate their quality and performance, and (2) synthesize the evidence regarding the performance of prognostic tools that have been externally validated for the prediction of survival.

Methods

The protocol for the systematic review and meta‐analysis was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024496426). The reporting of this study adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐analyses (PRISMA) statement and the Critical Appraisal and Data Extraction for Systematic Reviews of Prediction Modelling Studies checklist (Appendix S1) [8, 9].

Eligibility Criteria

We included studies that developed, validated or updated a prognostic model based on statistical methods to predict survival (≥1 year) in adult patients with primary GIST and produced a quantitative clinical tool such as a scoring system, nomogram, or online calculator designed for individual patient risk calculation. Studies published since 2002 were eligible, coinciding with FDA approval of imatinib (Gleevec) for the treatment of metastatic or unresectable GIST, which substantially improved the prognosis and thus rendered previously developed models less valuable [10].

Models for patients with metastatic GIST were excluded from this review. We also excluded models designed for the screening or diagnosis of GIST, those assessing a single prognostic factor, and those incorporating prognostic factors that are not readily obtainable in the clinical setting (e.g., genomic, radiomic, or proteomic data). Determination of clinical availability was guided by the 2022 College of American Pathologists cancer reporting protocol for GIST pathology [11]. For objective 1, model development, we excluded tools that were developed without original data or without an underlying statistical model (eg, consensus statements, subjective non‐validated opinions, or personal opinions). For objective 2, external validation studies, we excluded studies with an inappropriate definition of validation (such as reporting associations without prediction performance) or did not report at least one statistical measure of model performance, including discrimination, calibration, or overall prognostic accuracy [12]. The outcome of interest for both objectives must have included a dimension of time estimating at least 1‐year recurrence/disease free survival (RFS/DFS), cancer‐specific survival/disease‐specific survival (CSS/DSS), or overall survival (OS). We excluded review articles, meta‐analysis, conference abstracts, editorials, commentaries, and non‐English texts.

Information Sources and Search Strategy

In consultation with a senior information specialist, a search strategy to capture the review question was developed. Key search terms were determined from a scoping search of the literature and consultation with experts in the field. Preliminary searches were conducted, and full‐text literature was mined for potential keywords and appropriate controlled vocabulary terms (Medical Subject Headings for MEDLINE, EMTREE descriptors for Embase). The Yale MeSH Analyzer was used to facilitate the MeSH and text word analysis [13]. The strategy combined terms related to GIST (e.g., “Gastrointestinal Stromal Tumors/,” “GIST”*), prediction modeling (e.g., “nomogram/,” “Kaplan‐Meier analysis/,” “clinical decision rule,” “predict* model”), and outcomes of interest (e.g., “survival/,” “recurrence/,” “disease‐free survival/”). Fully reportable searches are listed in Appendix S2. The databases MEDLINE, Embase and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials were searched via the OVID platform from inception, with results limited from 2002 to February 3, 2025. Language restrictions were applied solely during the selection stage, rather than at the literature search phase. Key article reference lists and reviews were hand‐searched for additional citations. No gray literature was searched. Study authors were contacted when clarification was needed.

Study Selection

References were downloaded into reference management software (EndNote, Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia, PA) and uploaded to Rayyan [14]. Titles and abstracts were screened independently and in duplicate. The same two authors then assessed the full‐text articles. Disagreements were resolved by consensus and involvement with a senior reviewer as needed. Two reviewers extracted data independently onto electronic forms (Microsoft, USA). Where data were missing or unclear, study authors were contacted. Data elements were extracted using the CHARMS checklist [9].

Risk of Bias

The Prediction Model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool (PROBAST) was used to assess risk of bias at the individual study level in four domains: participant selection, predictor selection and measurement, outcome definition and determination, and statistical analysis [15]. Risk of bias was assessed in duplicate. A high risk of bias was considered to downgrade the quality of evidence.

Summary Measures for Outcomes

The primary outcome measure was the model's discrimination, which was evaluated through apparent performance, in addition to validation and calibration. Different discrimination measures were used in the predictive accuracy of the models: most commonly, Harrell's C‐statistic and Uno's C‐statistic for time range discrimination measures, and Uno's time‐dependent area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) as a fixed‐time discrimination measure at a specific time point [16]. Harrell's C‐statistic measures the degree of concordance by assessing the probability that a randomly selected patient who experiences an event at a given time has a higher predicted risk compared to a patient who experiences the event at a later time [16]. Uno's C‐statistic additionally applies time‐dependent weighting that better adjusts for censoring [16]. These measures are different from the AUC, which measures discrimination at a specific time point [16]. For interpretation, C‐statistic or AUC < 0.6 reflects poor discrimination; 0.6–0.75, possibly helpful discrimination; and > 0.75, clearly useful discrimination [17].

Synthesis of Results

Based on data availability, and in accordance with Debray et al.'s guide to systematic review and meta‐analysis of prediction model performance [18], meta‐analysis was performed across external validation studies that evaluated the same prediction model to estimate the overall discriminative performance for each model, for each survival outcome (i.e., RFS, OS, CSS). The original data set used to construct the model was not included in the meta‐analysis to avoid inflated estimates [18]. A random effects model was used, given that variation in predictive performance may be due to heterogeneity between studies rather than chance alone. To ensure conceptual and statistical validity, only comparable versions of C‐statistics were pooled (i.e. Harrell's C). Studies that did not specify the type of C‐statistic were excluded from pooling to avoid methodological heterogeneity and ensure validity of summary estimates [19]. Summary C‐statistic and confidence intervals were calculated using the metamisc package in R studio (R package version 0.2.0, Vienna, Austria), which calculates between‐study variance (τ2) using a restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimator and performs inverse‐variance weighted pooling on the logit‐transformed C‐statistic scale [19, 20]. For studies reporting AUC, pooled estimates were calculated using the metafor package [21], which also applies a random‐effects model using REML estimation for τ2, with standard errors of study‐level AUCs estimated using the method described by Hanley and McNeil when not reported [22].

In accordance with best practices for meta‐analysis, 95% prediction intervals (PIs) were calculated to estimate the range within which the true effect size of a future, similar study is expected to fall, assuming the same underlying heterogeneity [23]. Unlike confidence intervals, which reflect the precision of the pooled mean, PIs incorporate between‐study variance and offer a more clinically meaningful indication of how performance may vary across populations [23].

Heterogeneity, which reflects the degree to which variation is due to difference between studies rather than chance alone, was evaluated by means of a forest plot and the I2 statistic. I2 > 75%, 25%–75% and < 25% were considered to reflect high, moderate and low heterogeneity, respectively [24]. Calibration measures could not be meta‐analyzed because observed and expected events were not reported in sufficient studies. We planned to assess publication bias if > 10 studies examined a given prognostic model using the same performance metric [25]. Given limited data, we were unable to perform other quantitative analyses, such as subgroup and/or sensitivity analysis.

Certainty of Evidence (GRADE Assessment)

The certainty of evidence was assessed using an adapted GRADE framework for evaluation of prognostic studies, and applied across externally validated models [26, 27, 28]. Factors that can decrease certainty of evidence include inconsistency, high risk of bias, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias [26, 27, 28].

Results

The initial search strategy identified a total of 3003 studies, of which 47 studies were included in this review (Appendix S3). Nine studies described external validation of an existing prognostic tool only [67, 68, 69, 70, 71, 72, 73, 74, 75]. An overview of included studies is presented in Table 1.

Model Development Studies

Study and Cohort Characteristics

Methodological details of the 38 model development studies summarized in Table 2 and Appendix S4. Most studies presented their tools as nomograms (74%), followed by prognostic/risk scores (13%), prognostic indices (8%), and one a heat map and online calculator. Nearly all (97%) used retrospective data, with sample sizes ranging from 44 to 9,515 (median 420 patients). Development cohorts were sourced from China in 47% of studies and USA in 32%, with 63% using multicenter data and 29% based on the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database. Notably, 68% of model development studies were published from 2020 onward.

Model Development Method

A total of 45 tools were identified across the 38 studies, most commonly predicting recurrence‐free (n = 19) or overall survival (n = 15). Cox proportional hazards regression was the dominant modeling method (36/38), with one study using a Gaussian process and another using optimal classification trees. Among 36 studies specifying a survival anchor point, most used date of surgery (62%) or diagnosis (32%). Univariable significance guided predictor selection in 32 (85%) studies. In addition, 8 (21%) studies incorporated a priori clinical reasoning or expert consensus in selecting predictors. Only 20 studies reported handling of missing data; 15 excluded incomplete cases, and 3 used imputation methods as recommended in the TRIPOD guidelines (Appendix S4).

Predictors Included

Final predictors across models varied widely and are summarized in Appendix S5. Tumor size (n = 33), location (n = 30), and mitotic index (n = 27) were the most included predictors. While age and sex were frequently included, other social determinants of outcome such as race and, marital status were less commonly addressed. Several models incorporated broader clinical or pathological features such as serosal invasion, tumor rupture, or multifocality. Six studies included hematologic or inflammatory indices (e.g., PLR, FARI) while biomarkers such as Ki‐67 or CD117 were used in 8 studies. Five studies included imatinib treatment (four adjuvant, one did not specify timing) in their prognostic tools.

Model Performance and Internal Validation

Discrimination was reported in 36 studies (95%). Among them, 27 used C‐statistics (14 explicitly Harrell's C), 9 reported both C‐statistics and AUC, and 7 reported AUC only. Reported discrimination ranged from good to excellent (C‐index 0.62–0.90; AUC 0.70–0.93). Calibration was graphically assessed in 27 studies, although typically without quantitative metrics. Internal validation was performed in 53% of studies, most commonly random splitting (n = 12) or bootstrapping (n = 9), with 4 studies using both methods and 3 using cross‐validation. Among 11 studies that also performed external validation, C‐statistics ranged from 0.70 to 0.81 (Table 3).

Risk of Bias Within Studies (Probast)

Risk of bias for all included studies is summarized in Appendix S6. Overall, all included studies were at high risk of bias. The majority of studies were classified as “low” risk for participant selection (N = 27, 57%), predictor (N = 42, 89%), outcome (N = 43, 91%). However, 85% (N = 40) of the studies were classified as “high” risk for data analysis. The main sources of bias were unclear or poor handling of missing data, conversion of continuous variables to dichotomous variables without a priori rationale, small number of events per variable included in the model, issues with modeling methods (i.e. univariable analysis for selection of candidate variables). Additionally, five studies included predictors (such as adjuvant imatinib) that would not be known at the time the model is intended to be used, resulting in high risk of bias in the predictor domain.

Although 25 (53%) of the studies had low concerns for applicability, 12 (26%) had high concerns owing to restrictive inclusion criteria (i.e. only included patients identified as high, intermediate‐high, or low risk based on NIH consensus criteria, or limited to anatomic site of GIST) or broad exclusion criteria (i.e., excluding pregnant women, excluding patients with any adjuvant therapy). In the context of this review, which aims to evaluate prognostic tools applicable to the broader GIST population, these restrictions limit applicability.

External Validation and Meta‐Analysis

Fifteen studies conducted external validation of prognostic models, including 6 that were part of model development and 9 that focused exclusively on validation. As shown in Tables 1 and 4, most were retrospective, single‐center studies involving resected primary GIST populations. Sample sizes ranged from 27 to 1303 patients (median 137), with all studies including surgical cases and most excluding those with metastatic disease or those receiving neoadjuvant therapy. The proportion of patients receiving adjuvant TKI varied across studies. The MSKCC nomogram was the most frequently validated tool (n = 14), followed by Joensuu's contour maps (n = 2), the Chen/Yu nomogram (n = 1), and Joensuu's recurrence risk score (n = 1). Studies reported discrimination using either the C‐statistic or AUC, while calibration was less frequently assessed.

Meta‐Analysis

Studies reporting the same discrimination metric (Harrell's C‐statistic or AUC) for a given model, along with sufficient measures of uncertainty (i.e., 95% CI), were pooled for meta‐analysis. Three studies contributed multiple independent cohorts [36, 45, 51], and 3 studies validated more than one tool [33, 51, 74], resulting greater number of cohorts and validation results than individual studies. Of note, two studies reported “concordance‐index” but did not specify type and were not included in the pooled analysis [68, 69].

Harrell's C‐statistic meta‐analysis was conducted using results from 6 MSKCC validation studies that also reported standard errors. The pooled C‐statistic for overall RFS was 0.78 (95% CI: 0.76–0.80) based on 4 studies (6 cohorts), with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 31.3%) and a with a prediction interval of 0.73–0.82.(Appendix S7, Figure 1) Pooled C‐statistics for 5‐year RFS was 0.72 (95% CI: 0.70–0.74, 3 studies), with a prediction interval of 0.68‐0.75 (I2 = 0%). These results support moderate and consistent discrimination of the MSKCC model across timepoints.

AUC‐based meta‐analysis was also conducted using data from 8 studies (10 cohorts) evaluating the MSKCC nomogram. The pooled AUC for 5‐year RFS was 0.72 (95% CI: 0.62–0.82), wide prediction interval (95%PI 0.57‐0.96), and high heterogeneity (I2 = 91.7%) (Appendix S7, Figure 2A,B). The Joensuu contour map was validated in two studies, yielding a pooled AUC of 0.74 (95% CI: 0.68–0.80, I2 = 0%) (Figure 2C).

Certainty of Evidence and Publication Bias

All externally validated models were initially rated as moderate certainty using the GRADE framework for prognostic studies. However, they were downgraded for serious risk of bias, primarily due to methodological limitations in modeling and incomplete reporting. Additional downgrading was applied for imprecision (wide confidence intervals for summary effect) and inconsistency (broad prediction intervals) [27, 67, 68, 69, 70, 71, 72, 73, 74, 75]. Overall quality ratings were very low to low (Appendix S8). Assessment of publication bias was planned but limited by the small number of studies reporting comparable performance metrics. Although the MSKCC model was evaluated in 14 studies, fewer than 10 reported the same effect measure, precluding a valid publication bias assessment [76].

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta‐analysis, we identified 38 studies that examined 45 distinct models for individualized survival prediction in patients with GIST, as well as 9 additional studies that externally validated existing models. Most models were presented as nomograms and demonstrated good to excellent discrimination in development cohorts (C‐index 0.62–0.90; AUC 0.70–0.9). However, only a minority underwent external validation, and the overall methodological quality was limited by high risk of bias, particularly in model development and handling of missing data. Low and very low certainty of evidence showed that the MSKCC prognostic model had good discrimination for 2‐ and 5‐year survival. Taken together, these findings highlight that despite the growing number of prognostic models, meaningful advancement in clinically applicable tools remains limited. Since the introduction of the NIH consensus criteria in 2002 by Fletcher et al., [4] numerous risk stratification systems—including the AFIP [77], modified NIH [5], and the AJCC TNM staging system have been developed to help stratify GIST patients according to recurrence risk. These early systems, largely based on expert opinion, preceded the use of TKIs and modern standards for prognostic model development and validation. Although subsequent studies proposed modifications [5, 78, 79], these systems were not designed to predict individualized survival. In the era of personalized medicine, broad risk categories remain a helpful starting point, but fall short of the individualized prognostic information increasingly favored in clinical care. A prior review provides a useful overview of prognostication models developed up to 2017, but does not evaluate their methodological rigor or predictive performance [6]. Our review builds upon this work by identifying and critically appraising more recent clinical prognostic tools designed to predict individual survival outcomes using modern statistical approaches.

Notably, model development has accelerated in recent years, with 68% of tools developed after 2020. This trend reflects growing recognition of the importance of personalized prognostication in GIST. However, 29% of studies used the SEER database. Although population‐based, this database lacks key pathological prognostic variables such as mitotic rate, margin status, and tumor rupture and may not have complete information on TKI use. Importantly, SEER predominantly captures patients who underwent surgical resection and does not reliably capture patients managed without surgery, including those under active surveillance, with unresectable disease or managed with systemic therapy alone. As a result, models derived from SEER may not be applicable across the full clinical spectrum of GIST care. Models were also derived from overlapping SEER cohorts but included different predictors, raising concerns about the stability of variable selection and the models' clinical applicability. Additionally, 34% of models were developed in single institution cohorts from China, all with sample sizes under 500. While some reported strong performance metrics, these models may reflect institution‐specific outcomes and relatively homogeneous populations. Finally, although 11 studies reported external validation during model development, only 4 models were independently validated by external research groups. Thus, despite the growing number of prediction models developed, persistent challenges remain in data quality, validation, and generalizability, highlighting ongoing barriers to broader application.

A key concern identified across most studies was the high risk of bias, particularly in data analysis. Issues such as poor handling of missing data, arbitrary conversion of continuous variables to dichotomous ones, and small events‐per‐variable ratios were common, all of which may undermine the predictive accuracy and generalizability of the models [7]. These limitations further emphasize that the need for improved methodological standards.

Applicability was also an issue in a few studies, particularly those with restrictive inclusion criteria, such as focusing on high‐risk populations or excluding patients based on specific clinical parameters. This limits the generalizability of these models to broader GIST populations, making it challenging to adopt these tools in everyday practice where GIST presentations vary widely. Although all prognostication tools were designed to be used in clinical settings, the studies lacked to evaluate feasibility or implementation of these tools in practice. The gap between model development and clinical integration remains an ongoing concern.

The MSKCC nomogram was the most frequently validated tool, with a pooled Harrell's C‐statistic of 0.78 for overall RFS and an AUC of 0.74 at 5 years, indicating acceptable discrimination. However, wide 95% prediction intervals (C‐statistic: 0.69–0.74; AUC: 0.47–0.97), suggest inconsistency in model performance across different cohorts and clinical contexts. The MSKCC model was originally developed using a small cohort of 127 patients treated between 1983 and 2002, before the approval of imatinib [36]. It was therefore designed to estimate RFS among surgically resected, TKI naïve patients, to help guide patient selection for adjuvant therapy. Most subsequent validation studies were similarly limited by small cohorts (< 500 patients) [67, 68, 69, 71, 72] or excluded patients treated with TKIs [59]. Since the approval of imatinib in 2002 and its subsequent expansion to adjuvant indications in 2008, GIST management has changed substantially, with randomized controlled trials have since demonstrated clear benefits in both RFS and OS [3, 80]. These therapeutic advancements highlight how current treatment paradigms differ substantially from the era in which the MSKCC nomogram was developed. In contemporary practice, patients who do not receive adjuvant imatinib are typically those perceived to have lower‐risk disease, whereas higher‐risk patients are more likely to be treated. Consequently, post‐imatinib validation studies that restrict analyses to TKI‐naïve patients may evaluate the MSKCC nomogram in a different and generally lower‐risk population than the original development cohort, limiting its applicability to current clinical care and likely negatively impacting concordance statistics. Furthermore, the MSKCC experience, derived from a high‐volume referral center, may not generalize well to the broader population of GIST patients managed in community settings.

Next, the Joensuu contour map was validated in two studies, yielding a pooled AUC of 0.74 (95% CI: 0.68–0.80). Given the limited number of validations, results for the contour map should also be interpreted with caution.

There are limitations to this study. First, 28 of the 38 included development studies were based exclusively on surgical cohorts, limiting their relevance to patients managed non‐operatively. In current practice, small GISTs are often monitored with active surveillance rather than immediate operative intervention [81]. Tools developed using exclusively surgically managed patients reflect a subset of GIST that is typically more aggressive or symptomatic. Consequently, these models risk overestimating the likelihood of progression or recurrence. Of the 10 studies that also included patients who were managed non‐operatively, it is unclear whether patients were unresectable or selected for observation, as only a subset were treated with imatinib. As a result, these models may not represent the full spectrum of GIST presentations, limiting their utility in broader clinical practice and potentially leading to overestimation of risk. Next, most models were developed in high‐income countries, and few were externally validated in different geographic or health system contexts.

Future research should prioritize the development of prognostic models that align with contemporary GIST populations and treatment pathways. While existing tools such as the MSKCC nomogram remain valuable for estimating baseline recurrence risk after surgery in the absence of adjuvant therapy, they were not designed to predict outcomes in patients treated with modern neoadjuvant or adjuvant TKI strategies. Given the widespread use of TKIs in current practice, there is a need for prognostic models developed in well‐characterized cohorts that include patients receiving contemporary systemic therapy, with clearly defined time zero and treatment context. Such models would better reflect current clinical decision‐making and allow individualized survival estimates that are applicable to patients managed under modern therapeutic paradigms. Furthermore, future studies should investigate the feasibility and impact of prognostic models in clinical practice to bridge the gap between model development and real‐world application [82].

Conclusion

This review summarizes currently available clinical prediction tools for survival among patients with GIST. While many models show strong predictive performance, the lack of external validation limits their clinical application. The MSKCC nomogram remains the most frequently externally validated tool and shows reasonable discriminatory performance. However, its development in a pre‐imatinib, surgically treated population limits its applicability to modern GIST management, where neoadjuvant and adjuvant TKIs are widely used. Taken together, these findings highlight that despite the growing number of prognostic models, meaningful advancement in clinically applicable tools remains limited. There is a clear need for modern, methodologically robust prediction models developed and externally validated in well‐characterized cohorts that reflect contemporary treatment pathways and support individualized prognostication in current clinical practice.

Synopsis

This systematic review and meta‐analysis evaluated the performance of survival prediction tools developed for patients with gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GIST). We assessed model characteristics, discrimination, calibration, and validation methods to determine their accuracy and clinical applicability. Although many models demonstrated moderate to good discriminatory ability, variability in methodology, reporting quality, and external validation limits their generalizability and routine clinical use.
