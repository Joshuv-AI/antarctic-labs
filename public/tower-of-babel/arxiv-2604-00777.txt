Negative Differential Heat Conductivity in a Harmonic Chain Coupled to a Particle Reservoir
Simon Krekels, Christian Maes, Ion Santra, Ruoxun Zhai (翟若迅)

Abstract
When coupling thermal baths at different temperatures, negative differential thermal conductivity is typically attributed to nonlinear interactions in the connecting medium. In this work, we demonstrate that such an effect can arise purely from the nature of the thermal baths and their coupling with the medium. Specifically, we construct a bath composed of overdamped thermal particles, which is coupled to one end of a harmonic chain, while the other end is connected to a standard Langevin heat bath. By analyzing the steady-state heat current, we observe significant negative differential thermal conductivity. In particular, as the temperature difference between the two baths diverges, the steady-state heat current through the chain vanishes. The effect is thermokinetic: we compute the effective dissipative coefficient and we find that it scales inversely with the square of the temperature of the particle bath in the high-temperature limit, resulting in an asymptotic decoupling between the bath and the chain. Our results highlight that nonequilibrium transport properties can be strongly influenced by the structure of the environment and its coupling to the system, even in otherwise linear systems.

Introduction

Transport of energy is a central theme in both technological applications and nonequilibrium statistical mechanics,
[
5
,
9
,
3
]
. While macroscopic laws of heat conduction are well established, their microscopic origin remains subtle and often depends sensitively on how a system is coupled to its environment. In particular, the nature of the thermal reservoirs and the mechanism by which they exchange energy with a conducting medium can strongly influence transport properties. This becomes especially relevant when different types of degrees of freedom such as particles and elastic modes are coupled through the same medium, as is common in soft and biological matter (for instance, in the interaction between particles and a viscoelastic structure like the cell cortex).

In this paper we explore a minimal setting in which such effects become explicit. We consider heat transport through a harmonic chain, serving as an idealized elastic string, connected on one side to a standard Langevin heat bath and on the other to a reservoir of overdamped particles. The particle bath interacts locally with the chain, effectively “bombarding” one end of the medium. This setup is inspired by soft-matter systems in which particles are embedded in or coupled to deformable substrates. The interaction is modeled in the spirit of Bernoulli–Euler elasticity, where the chain has a bending energy and the particles exert forces that are linear in the local displacement, akin to a Hookean coupling but sensitive to local curvature.

Our main result is that this seemingly simple modification of the boundary condition qualitatively changes the transport behavior. Even though the chain itself is linear, the system exhibits a far-from-equilibrium regime of negative differential heat conductivity: increasing the temperature of the particle reservoir can lead to a decrease of the stationary heat current. This effect originates from the effective dynamics induced by the particle bath. When the particle degrees of freedom are eliminated, they generate a temperature-dependent friction at the boundary, which grows in such a way as to suppress energy transport at higher temperatures. We refer to this mechanism as a thermokinetic effect, emphasizing its kinetic (rather than structural) origin. Such negative differential conductance has been seen for various systems, including particle
[

,

]
, and energy transport
[

,
6
,
10
]
, but the basis of the present study is the well-known and simple paradigmatic harmonic chain with linear Hamiltonian dynamics in the bulk
[
14
]
.

Setup

We consider heat transport through an elastic string, modeled as a one-dimensional chain of
N

coupled harmonic oscillators with positions
\{q_{j}\}
and momenta
\{p_{j}\}
,
j=1,\dots,N
.
This fits within the more general study of heat conduction networks,
[
11
]
, which can be treated in a similar way.

The left boundary oscillator is coupled to a reservoir of overdamped particles with positions
\{x_{i}\}
(which we refer to as the particle bath from here on), while the right boundary oscillator is connected to a Langevin heat bath. The Hamiltonian of the oscillator chain, for given bath configuration
\{x_{i}\}
, is

\mathcal{H}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{p_{j}^{2}}{2m}+\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\frac{k}{2}(q_{j+1}-q_{j})^{2}+\frac{K}{2}(q_{1}^{2}+q_{N}^{2})+\varepsilon q_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{b}}\mathcal{F}(x_{i})-\Lambda q_{1}

(1)

where
\Lambda=\varepsilon\lambda N_{b}
characterizes a compensating force, introduced to cancel the constant part of the mean force exerted by the particle bath (cf. Fig.
2
a), and will be properly introduced in the next section.
This compensation has no effect on the current through the string.
The particle bath consists of
N_{b}
overdamped Brownian particles at temperature
T_{L}
moving on a ring of length
L
,

\displaystyle\zeta\dot{x}_{i}

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon q_{1}\,\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(x_{i})+\sqrt{2\zeta T_{L}}\,\xi_{i},\qquad i=1,\ldots,N_{b}

(2)

where
\xi_{i}=\xi_{i}(t)
are standard Gaussian white noise.
Each bath particle interacts locally with the oscillator
q_{1}
through a periodic coupling
F(x)
, which we choose to be the von Mises distribution,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(x)=\frac{\lambda}{I_{0}(p)}\exp\left[p\cos\,\left(\frac{2\pi x}{L}\right)\right],

(3)

where
I_{0}
is the zeroth modified Bessel function of the first kind. The parameter
p
characterizes the spatial localization of the interaction, and reduces to a periodic Dirac-delta form in the limit
p\to\infty
.

The equations of motion for the oscillator chain follow,

\displaystyle m\ddot{q}_{1}

\displaystyle=-Kq_{1}-k(q_{1}-q_{2})+\varepsilon\sum_{i=1}^{N_{b}}\mathcal{F}(x_{i})+N_{b}\Lambda

(4)

\displaystyle m\ddot{q}_{i}

\displaystyle=k(q_{i-1}-2q_{i}+q_{i+1})

\displaystyle m\ddot{q}_{N}

\displaystyle=-\gamma_{R}\dot{q}_{N}-Kq_{N}-k(q_{N}-q_{N-1})+\sqrt{2\gamma_{R}T_{R}}\,\eta_{R}

where
\gamma_{R}
and
\eta_{R}
are the friction and Gaussian white noise respectively, coming from the Langevin bath on the right at temperature
T_{R}
.

We are interested in the nonequilibrium steady state (NESS), particularly in the heat current flowing through the chain

J=\frac{1}{2m}\langle\mathcal{J}(t)\rangle=\left\langle(p_{j}(t)+p_{j+1}(t))(q_{j+1}-q_{j})\right\rangle

(5)

where the averages are taken in the NESS obtained from solving the full stochastic dynamics (
2
)–(
4
).

For harmonic chains coupled to Langevin heat baths at both ends, the heat conduction problem was solved exactly in the seminal work of Rieder, Lieb, and Lebowitz
[
14
]
.
In that case, the stationary current was found to be independent of the system size, and proportional to the temperature difference at the two ends.
In contrast, the particle reservoir in our setup is not a standard thermal bath modeled by independent oscillators or oscillator chains.
In fact, the interaction is not of the usual two-body form depending on relative displacements, but rather represents a local forcing exerted by particles that effectively bombard the left-most boundary of the chain.
The induced friction and noise by such a bath have never been addressed in the literature, and is expected to have different properties than oscillator baths.
To characterize these, we first analyze the reduced problem of a single probe coupled to the bath of overdamped Brownian particles as discussed above.
This allows us to estimate the effective force, friction, and noise induced by the bath, which we then use to understand transport in the full chain.

In this section, we consider a single oscillator coupled to the bath, corresponding to setting
k=0
in the previous section.
The dynamics of that single oscillator is then governed by

\displaystyle m\ddot{q}

\displaystyle=-Kq-\varepsilon\sum_{i=1}^{N_{b}}F(x_{i}),

(6)

\displaystyle\zeta\dot{x}_{i}

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon qF^{\prime}(x_{i})+\sqrt{2\zeta T_{L}}\,\xi_{i}.

(7)

We are interested in the regime of time-scale separation, where the bath particles relax much faster than the oscillator.
In this limit, the particle degrees of freedom can be integrated out to obtain an autonomous equation for the slow probe dynamics
q(t)
.
It has the form,

m\ddot{q}(t)=-Kq(t)+\sum_{i=1}^{N_{b}}\left(F^{\text{qs}}_{i}(t)+F^{\text{fric}}_{i}(t)+\eta_{i}(t)\right).

(8)

The terms in the bracket can be identified as the quasistatic mean force, the frictional force, and the fluctuating force, respectively. They are given by,

\displaystyle F_{i}^{\mathrm{qs}}(t)

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\langle\mathcal{F}(x_{i})\rangle_{q(t)},

(9)

\displaystyle F_{i}^{\mathrm{fric}}(t)

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\Big(\langle\mathcal{F}(x_{i}(t))|q(s),s\leq t\rangle-\langle\mathcal{F}(x_{i})\rangle_{q(t)}\Big)

(10)

\displaystyle\eta_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\Big(\mathcal{F}(x_{i}(t))-\langle\mathcal{F}(x_{i}(t))|q(s),s\leq t\rangle\Big).

(11)

where
\langle\cdots\rangle_{q(t)}
denotes averages in the ensemble where
q
is held at a fixed value.

F_{i}^{\mathrm{qs}}(q)
is displayed in Fig.
2
a.
The above correlations are in general difficult to compute, but yield closed forms in the limit of weak coupling
\varepsilon\to 0
[see Appendix
A
for details].
The mean force is given as,

F_{i}^{\text{qs}}(q)=-\varepsilon\lambda+\varepsilon^{2}\lambda^{2}\,\frac{q(t)}{T_{L}}\,\left(\frac{I_{0}(2p)}{I_{0}(p)^{2}}-1\right)+O(\varepsilon^{3}),

(12)

where the first term is a constant mean force which cancels the external drive
N_{b}\Lambda
.
The second term, proportional to
q(t)
, offers a correction to the probe stiffness.
The second term reduces to
F_{i}^{\text{fric}}(t)=-\gamma_{i}^{\mathrm{eff}}\,\dot{q}(t),
with the effective friction coefficient

\gamma_{i}^{\text{eff}}=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{T_{L}}\int_{0}^{\mathrlap{\infty}}\differential{t}\langle\mathcal{F}(x(t));\mathcal{F}(x(0))\rangle_{0}=\varepsilon^{2}\frac{\zeta L^{2}}{2\pi^{2}T_{L}^{2}}\left(\frac{\lambda}{I_{0}(p)}\right)^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{I_{n}(p)^{2}}{n^{2}}

(13)

where we used the force-force correlation

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{F}(x(t));\mathcal{F}(x(s))\rangle_{0}=\frac{2\lambda^{2}}{I_{0}(p)^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{n}(p)^{2}e^{-\frac{4\pi^{2}n^{2}T_{L}}{\zeta L^{2}}|t-s|}.

(14)

The fluctuating force also satisfies the fluctuation-dissipation relation
\langle\eta_{i}(t)\eta_{j}(s)\rangle=2\gamma^{i}_{\text{eff}}T_{L}\delta_{ij}\delta(t-s)
.

The effective friction can also be extracted numerically from the relaxation of the single probe.
The reduced dynamics is that of an underdamped harmonic oscillator, for which the mean velocity decays as

\langle\dot{q}(t)\rangle=\langle\dot{q}(0)\rangle e^{-\gamma_{\text{eff}}t/(2m)}\times\left[\text{oscillatory terms}\right].

(15)

The renormalized stiffness plays a role in governing the oscillatory terms, but does not affect the decay envelope.
We therefore estimate
\gamma_{\text{eff}}
from the exponential envelope of the numerically measured velocity relaxation starting from
v(t=0)=50
.

5
Conclusion

Oscillator chains have been central in physics for the study of energy transport and diffusion.
Historical highlights include the foundational work on the Fermi-Pasta-Ulam-Tsingou problem,
[
2
]
, and Peierls’ theory of heat conduction in crystals,
[
13
]
.

Oscillators
have typically referred to vibrational degrees of freedom (phonons) such as found in crystals and even more generally, albeit strongly attenuated, in visco-elastic materials.

The present study has kept the conducting medium as a harmonic chain but replaces one of the reservoirs by a particle bath with distinct, purely positional degrees of freedom.
That change in the nature of the bath and in the way it couples to the system turns out to be sufficient to produce the observed transport behavior, characterized by negative differential conductivity: even when the particle bath is thermal and un-driven and the medium is linear, we find that the resulting heat current differs markedly from that in purely oscillator-based setups.
The origin is thermokinetic and highlights the importance of modeling the environment and its coupling carefully: open far-from-equilibrium transport is not determined by the conducting medium alone, but by the interplay between the system and the reservoirs to which it is connected.

Appendix A
Effective bath

For a single probe in the particle bath, the governing equations are

\displaystyle\zeta\dot{x}_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon q(t)\,\mathcal{F}^{\prime}(x_{i}(t))+\sqrt{2\zeta T_{L}}\,\xi_{i}(t),\qquad i=1,\dots,N_{b},

(A1)

\displaystyle m\ddot{q}(t)

\displaystyle=-Kq(t)-\varepsilon\sum_{i=1}^{N_{b}}\mathcal{F}(x_{i}(t)).

(A2)

We decompose the force from the
i
th bath particle acting on the probe into three parts,

\displaystyle F^{i}_{\mathrm{bath}}(t)=F_{i}^{\mathrm{qs}}(t)+F_{i}^{\mathrm{fric}}(t)+\eta_{i}(t),

(A3)

where

\displaystyle F_{i}^{\mathrm{qs}}(t)

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\langle\mathcal{F}(x_{i})\rangle_{q(t)},

(A4)

\displaystyle F_{i}^{\mathrm{fric}}(t)

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\Big(\langle\mathcal{F}(x_{i}(t))\mid q(s),\,s\leq t\rangle-\langle\mathcal{F}(x_{i})\rangle_{q(t)}\Big),

(A5)

\displaystyle\eta_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\Big(\mathcal{F}(x_{i}(t))-\langle\mathcal{F}(x_{i}(t))\mid q(s),\,s\leq t\rangle\Big).

(A6)

The first term is the mean force acting on the probe in the quasistatic limit.
The average
\langle\cdots\rangle_{q(t)}
denotes averaging with the probe held fixed at position
q(t)
.
The average
\langle\cdots\mid q(s),\,s\leq t\rangle
denotes averaging over bath trajectories evolving under the history of the probe trajectory up to time
t
.
The second term can be interpreted as the response in the mean force experienced by the probe due to its own past motion, and the remaining third term is the noise.
We now proceed to treat these terms separately.

A.1
Mean force

For a frozen probe position, the bath distribution is given by

\displaystyle\rho_{q}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Z}^{-1}e^{-\frac{\varepsilon q\,\mathcal{F}(x)}{T_{L}}},\qquad\text{where}\qquad\mathcal{Z}=\int_{0}^{L}\differential{x}\,e^{-\frac{\varepsilon q\,\mathcal{F}(x)}{T_{L}}}

(A7)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\left[1-\frac{\varepsilon q}{T_{L}}\big(\mathcal{F}(x)-\langle\mathcal{F}\rangle_{0}\big)+O(\varepsilon^{2})\right].

(A8)

where
\langle\cdots\rangle_{0}
denotes averages with respect to
\rho_{q}(x)
at
\varepsilon=0
.
Using this,

\displaystyle F_{i}^{\mathrm{qs}}(t)

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\langle\mathcal{F}\rangle_{0}+\varepsilon^{2}\,\frac{q(t)}{T_{L}}\,\Big(\langle\mathcal{F}^{2}\rangle_{0}-\langle\mathcal{F}\rangle_{0}^{2}\Big)+O(\varepsilon^{3})

(A9)

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\lambda+\varepsilon^{2}\lambda^{2}\,\frac{q(t)}{T_{L}}\,\left(\frac{I_{0}(2p)}{I_{0}(p)^{2}}-1\right)+O(\varepsilon^{3}).

(A10)

A.2
Friction

To evaluate the friction, we need the response of
\mathcal{F}(x)
to the perturbation

\varepsilon[q(s)-q(t)]\,\mathcal{F}(x)
.
Using the Kubo formula, we get

\displaystyle F_{i}^{\mathrm{fric}}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon}{T_{L}}\int_{\mathrlap{-\infty}}^{t}\differential{s}\,\left\langle\mathcal{F}(x_{t});\partial_{s}\Big(-\varepsilon[q(s)-q(t)]\mathcal{F}(x_{s})\Big)\right\rangle

(A11)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{T_{L}}\int_{\mathrlap{-\infty}}^{t}\differential{s}\,\dot{q}(s)\,\left\langle\mathcal{F}(x_{t});\mathcal{F}(x_{s})\right\rangle_{q}.

(A12)

Using Eq. (
A8
), the above reduces to

\displaystyle F_{i}^{\mathrm{fric}}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{T_{L}}\int_{\mathrlap{-\infty}}^{t}\differential{s}\,\dot{q}(s)\,\left\langle\mathcal{F}(x_{t});\mathcal{F}(x_{s})\right\rangle_{0}+O(\varepsilon^{3}).

(A13)

So we need to evaluate the above correlation for the stochastic process
x
, which is a free diffusion on a ring of length
L
.
It is useful to rewrite
\mathcal{F}(x)
using the Jacobi–Anger expansion

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(x)=\frac{\lambda}{I_{0}(p)}\left(I_{0}(p)+2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{n}(p)\cos(2\pi n x/L)\right)

(A14)

and use the identity
[
15
]

\displaystyle\langle\cos(2\pi m x_t/L)\cos(2\pi n x_s/L)\rangle_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{m,n}e^{-\frac{4\pi^{2}n^{2}T_{L}}{\zeta L^{2}}|t-s|}

to obtain

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{F}(x_{t});\mathcal{F}(x_{s})\rangle_{0}=\frac{2\lambda^{2}}{I_{0}(p)^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{n}(p)^{2}e^{-\frac{4\pi^{2}n^{2}T_{L}}{\zeta L^{2}}|t-s|}.

(A16)

For large

\displaystyle\gamma_{i}^{\mathrm{eff}}=\varepsilon^{2}\frac{\zeta L^{2}}{2\pi^{2}T_{L}^{2}}\left(\frac{\lambda}{I_{0}(p)}\right)^{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{I_{n}(p)^{2}}{n^{2}}.

(A17)

A.3
Noise

Because the system with a single probe is in equilibrium, the noise correlations follow a second fluctuation–dissipation relation,

\displaystyle\langle\eta_{i}(t)\eta_{j}(s)\rangle=2\gamma_{i}^{\mathrm{eff}}T_{L}\,\delta_{ij}\delta(t-s).

(A18)

Appendix B
Current computation

It is more convenient for the computations to rewrite (
16
),

M\ddot{X}(t)=-\Phi X(t)-\Gamma\dot{X}(t)+\Xi(t),

(B19)

where

\displaystyle M=m\mathbb{I},\qquad\Gamma=\Gamma_{L}+\Gamma_{R},\quad\text{ with }(\Gamma_{L})_{ij}=\gamma_{L}\delta_{i1}\delta_{j1},\qquad(\Gamma_{R})_{ij}=\gamma_{R}\delta_{iN}\delta_{jN},

\displaystyle\Phi_{ij}=\left(2k+(k_{\text{eff}}-k)\,\delta_{i1}+(K-k)\,\delta_{iN}\right)\delta_{ij}\;-\;k\left(\delta_{i,j+1}+\delta_{i,j-1}\right),

J=\langle\dot{q}_{1}(-\gamma_{L}\dot{q}_{1}(t)+\xi_{L}(t)-k_{\text{eff}}q_{1}(t))\rangle=\langle\dot{X}^{T}(t)\big[-\Gamma_{L}\dot{X}(t)+\Xi_{L}(t)\big]\rangle,

(B23)

where
\Xi_{L}(t)=(\xi_{L}(t),0,\dots,0)^{T}
, and in writing the second equality we have used
\langle q_{1}\dot{q}_{1}\rangle=0

Using the explicit forms of
\Gamma_{R,L}

J=2\gamma_{L}\gamma_{R}(T_{L}-T_{R})\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\differential{\omega}}{2\pi}\,\omega^{2}\,|G_{1N}(\omega)|^{2}.

(B25)

So to obtain the stationary current, we need to evaluate the Greens function matrix element

Noting that
G^{-1}(\omega)
is a tridiagonal matrix, with off diagonal entries
-k

\displaystyle a_{1}

\displaystyle=k+k_{\text{eff}}-m\omega^{2}-i\omega\gamma_{L},

(B26)

\displaystyle a_{j}=a

\displaystyle=2k-m\omega^{2},\quad j=1,\cdots N-1

(B27)

\displaystyle a_{N}

\displaystyle=k+K-m\omega^{2}-i\omega\gamma_{R}.

(B28)

For a tridiagonal matrix
[
17
]
,

G_{lN}=(-k)^{N-l}\frac{\theta_{l-1}}{\theta_{N}},

(B29)

where

\displaystyle\theta_{l}

\displaystyle=a\,\theta_{l-1}-k^{2}\theta_{l-2},\qquad l=2,3,\dots,N-1,

(B30)

\displaystyle\theta_{N}

\displaystyle=a_{N}\,\theta_{N-1}-k^{2}\theta_{N-2}.

(B31)

Using the boundary conditions
\theta_{0}=1,\theta_{1}=a_{1}
, our required element is,

G_{1N}(\omega)=\frac{(-k)^{N-1}}{\theta_{N}}.

(B32)

The bulk recursion has the solution,

\theta_{l}=\frac{(-k)^{\,l-1}}{\sin q}\Big[k\sin\big((l+1)q\big)+\Delta_{L}\sin(lq)\Big],\qquad l=1,2,\dots,N-1.

(B33)

where

\Delta_{L}\equiv a_{1}-a=k_{\text{eff}}-k-i\omega\gamma_{L}.

(B34)

Using this in
\theta_{N}
expression,

\theta_{N}=\frac{(-k)^{N}}{\sin q}\Big[\mathcal{A}(q)\sin(Nq)+\mathcal{B}(q)\cos(Nq)\Big]

(B35)

where
\Delta_{R}=K-k-i\omega\gamma_{R}
,
\omega=\omega_{c}\sin\frac{q}{2}
with
\omega_{c}=2\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta_{L}+\Delta_{R}}{k}+\left(1+\frac{\Delta_{L}\Delta_{R}}{k^{2}}\right)\cos q,

(B36)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(q)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{\Delta_{L}\Delta_{R}}{k^{2}}\right)\sin q.

(B37)

Thus we have,

G_{1N}(\omega)=-\frac{\sin q}{k\big[\mathcal{A}(q)\sin(Nq)+\mathcal{B}(q)\cos(Nq)\big]}.

(B38)

We now consider the limit of a thermodynamically large chain
N\to\infty
.
For
\omega>\omega_{c}
,
q
becomes complex, and the contribution is exponentially small in
N
.
Thus, in the thermodynamic limit, we may restrict to
0\leq\omega\leq\omega_{c}
,
equivalently
0\leq q\leq\pi
, and average over the fast phase
x=Nq
, which leads to,

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{4\gamma_{L}\gamma_{R}(T_{L}-T_{R})}{\pi k^{2}}\int_{0}^{\omega_{c}}\differential{\omega}\omega^{2}\sin^{2}q\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{dx}{2\pi}\,\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}(q)\sin x+\mathcal{B}(q)\cos x|^{2}}.

(B39)

\displaystyle=\frac{4\gamma_{L}\gamma_{R}(T_{L}-T_{R})}{\pi k^{2}}\int_{0}^{\omega_{c}}\differential{\omega}\frac{\omega^{2}\sin^{2}q}{\big|\mathrm{Im}(\mathcal{A}(q)\mathcal{B}^{*}(q))\big|}

(B40)

where we use the identity

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\differential{x}}{2\pi}\,\frac{1}{\alpha\sin^{2}x+\beta\cos^{2}x+2c\sin x\cos x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha\beta-c^{2}}}

(B41)

with
\alpha=|\mathcal{A}|^{2},\quad\beta=|\mathcal{B}|^{2},\quad c=\mathrm{Re}\big[\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B}^{*}\big]
.

The denominator can be further simplified,

\mathrm{Im}\!\left[\mathcal{A}(q)\mathcal{B}^{*}(q)\right]=-\frac{\omega\sin q}{k^{3}}\left(\gamma_{L}\Big[K^{2}-m\omega^{2}(K-k)\Big]+\gamma_{R}\Big[k_{\text{eff}}^{2}-m\omega^{2}(k_{\text{eff}}-k)\Big]+\omega^{2}\gamma_{L}\gamma_{R}(\gamma_{L}+\gamma_{R}).\right),

(B42)

Thus the final expression in the integral form, using
\left|\frac{d\omega}{dq}\right|=\frac{k\sin q}{m\omega}
, is

J=\frac{4\gamma_{L}\gamma_{R}k^{2}(T_{L}-T_{R})}{\pi m}\int_{0}^{\pi}\differential{q}\frac{\sin^{2}q}{\left(\gamma_{L}Z_{R}(\omega)+\gamma_{R}Z_{L}+\omega^{2}\gamma_{L}\gamma_{R}(\gamma_{L}+\gamma_{R}).\right)}.

(B43)

with

\displaystyle Z_{R,L}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\Big[\lambda_{R,L}^{2}-m\omega^{2}(\lambda_{R,L}-k)\Big]

(B44)

Finally, performing the integral yields the closed-form expression found in the main text

J=\frac{4\gamma_{L}\gamma_{R}k^{2}(T_{L}-T_{R})}{m\bigl(D_{0}-D_{1}+\sqrt{D_{0}(D_{0}-2D_{1})}\bigr)},

(B45)

where
D_{0},\,D_{1}
are defined in the main text.
