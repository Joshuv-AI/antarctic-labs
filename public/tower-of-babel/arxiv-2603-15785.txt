On the Uniqueness of Fréchet Means for Polytope Norms
Roan Talbut

Abstract




Abstract

Fréchet means are a popular type of average for non-Euclidean datasets, defined as those points which minimise the average squared distance to a set of data points. We consider the behaviour of sample Fréchet means on normed spaces whose unit ball is a polytope; this setting is rarely covered by existing literature on Fréchet means, which focuses on smooth spaces or spaces with bounded curvature. We study the geometry of the set of Fréchet means over polytope normed spaces, with a focus on dimension and probabilistic conditions for uniqueness. In particular, we provide a geometric characterisation of the threshold sample size at which Fréchet means have a positive probability of being unique, and we prove that this threshold is at most one more than the dimension of our space. We are able to use this geometric characterisation to compute the unique Fréchet mean sample threshold in the case of the
\ell_{\infty}
and
\ell_{1}
norms.

Keywords: polytope geometry, Fréchet means, polytope norms, uniqueness, small samples.

1
Introduction

The Fréchet mean set of a collection of data points in a metric space is defined as the set of points that minimise the average squared distance to the data points
(
14
)
. Fréchet means generalise the classical Euclidean notion of a mean to datasets taking values in general metric spaces. As this definition only uses the metric of the underlying state space, Fréchet means have been applied to a wide variety of different geometric settings such as the space of PSD matrices
(
4
;
12
;
21
)
, the space of persistence diagrams in topological data analysis
(
16
;
10
)
, and Wasserstein space
(
19
;
18
)
.
Definition

1.1
gives the formal definition of Fréchet means on a general metric space, for both populations and samples.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
X
be a random variable taking values in a general metric space
(\Omega,d)
. Its population Fréchet function
f:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is defined as

f(\mathbf{\theta})=\mathbb{E}\left[d(X,\mathbf{\theta})^{2}\right].

For a sample
\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{n}\}\subset\Omega
, the sample Fréchet function is defined as

f(\mathbf{\theta})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}d(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{\theta})^{2}.

The (population/sample) Fréchet mean set is then the set of global minima:

\FMset=\text{argmin}_{\mathbf{\theta}\in\Omega}f(\mathbf{\theta}).

We often denote these Fréchet mean sets by
\FMset(X)
or
\FMset(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{n})
.

In this paper, we study the behaviour of sample Fréchet means on normed spaces whose unit ball is a polytope, hereafter referred to as spaces with a
polytope norm
. This fundamental setting is not covered by most Fréchet mean literature, which instead focuses on manifolds
(
6
;
7
)
or spaces with bounded curvature
(
25
;
2
)
. Despite this, polytope norms are prevalent in statistics; the
\ell_{1}
norm is used in LASSO regression
(
22
)
in order to encourage sparse parameterisation of regression models, and avoid overfitting to data. Our work directly addresses
\ell_{1}
Fréchet means, as we show with running examples throughout this paper. However, our results are far more general, and apply to Fréchet means induced by any polytope norm. We also present examples using the
\ell_{\infty}
norm to show the contrast in their behaviour.

Fréchet means are defined as the solution set of an optimisation problem, so the question of existence and uniqueness is the natural first topic of study in any geometric setting. The existence of a Fréchet mean is equivalent to a distribution having finite moments, while the uniqueness of Fréchet means is more heavily dependent on the underlying geometry.
25
showed that Fréchet means are unique in spaces with non-positive Alexandrov curvature, such as the cone of SPD matrices
(
4
)
, and the BHV space of phylogenetic trees
(
8
;
17
)
. For Riemannian manifolds,
6
give sufficient conditions for uniqueness of sample and population Fréchet means, and more recently it was shown that for any absolutely continuous distribution over a complete Riemannian manifold, sample Fréchet means will almost surely be unique
(
3
)
.

In this paper, we present similarly foundational results on the uniqueness of Fréchet means from polytope norms. For data with an absolutely continuous underlying distribution, we show that the population Fréchet mean will be unique, while the probability of a unique sample Fréchet mean will converge to one. We then consider the probability of a unique Fréchet mean for small samples; we show that there is some sample threshold below which Fréchet means are almost never unique, and above which Fréchet means have a positive probability of uniqueness. This result follows from characterising the geometric configurations which can occur with positive probability;
11
take a similar view in their study of Fermat–Weber points, though their work is deterministic where ours is probabilistic. We show that this sample threshold is at most
k+1
for general polytope norms, where
k
is the dimension of our space. In the cases of the
\ell_{\infty}
and
\ell_{1}
norms, this threshold is
k
and 3 respectively.

We provide a brief introduction to the relevant polytope geometry in
Section

2
and the geometric foundations of Fréchet means on norm spaces in
Section

3
. We will show that for any absolutely continuous distribution, the population Fréchet mean will be unique, while the probability of a unique sample Fréchet mean is zero for small sample sizes and converging to one as the sample size increases (
Section

4
). Our main result,
Theorem

5.10
, gives geometric conditions which dictate the number of samples required for this probability of uniqueness to go from zero to positive (
Section

5
). In
Section

6
, we present the threshold values for specific norms. We conclude by showing how the Frank–Wolfe algorithm
(
13
)
can be used for the exact computation of Fréchet means in
Section

7
.

2
Polytope Geometry

In this section, we review the definitions of polytopes, their faces and their polars. For a more detailed introduction, we refer the reader to
26
. We then formalise a polytope norm — a norm whose unit ball is a polytope — and review the convex geometry corresponding to such a norm.

2.1
Polytopes

Polytopes can be viewed in two ways — as a convex hull of finitely many points, or an intersection of finitely many halfspaces.

Definition 2.1

(Polytope)
.

A polytope
Q
in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
is the convex hull of a finite set of points
\{\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{r}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
:

Q=\conv(\{\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{r}\})=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{v}_{i}:\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r}\geq 0,\;\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}=1\right\}.

Equivalently, given a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times k}
and a vector
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
, a polytope can also be defined as the set of all solutions to the following linear inequalities:

Q=P(A,\mathbf{b})\coloneqq\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\;|\;A\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{b},\text{ coordinatewise}\right\},

where it is assumed that the set of solutions is a bounded set. The convex hull and inequality representations of
Q
are referred to as the vertex and halfspace representations respectively.

Throughout this paper, we use two classes of polytopes as running examples; these are hypercubes and cross-polytopes, which arise as the unit balls of the
\ell_{\infty}
and
\ell_{1}
norms respectively. The vertex and halfspace representation of each of these balls is given in
Examples

2.2
and
2.3
, while
Figure

1
shows visualisations of these polytopes for
k=2
and 3.

Example 2.2

(Hypercubes)
.

We define the
k
-dimensional hypercube
B_{\infty}^{k}
by

B_{\infty}^{k}\coloneqq\conv\left(\{-1,1\}^{k}\right)=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:\forall i\in[k],|x_{i}|\leq 1\}.

Example 2.3

(Cross-polytopes)
.

We define the
k
-dimensional cross-polytope
B_{1}^{k}
by

B_{1}^{k}\coloneqq\conv\left(\{\pm\mathbf{e}_{1},\dots,\pm\mathbf{e}_{k}\}\right)=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}|x_{i}|\leq 1\}.

An important attribute of the geometry of a polytope is its
facial
structure.

Definition 2.4

(Face, face lattice)
.

Let
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
,
c_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
be such that
\mathbf{c}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}\leq c_{0}
for all
\mathbf{x}
in the polytope
Q
. A face
F
of
Q
is an intersection of the form:

F=Q\bigcap\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:\,\mathbf{c}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}=c_{0}\}.

The set of all faces is denoted
\mathfrak{F}(Q)
, and forms a lattice with the partial ordering given by inclusion.

We note that a polytope
Q
is a face of itself, as is the empty set; any other face of
Q
is referred to as a
proper
face of
Q
. The
0
-dimensional faces of a polytope are called vertices, the
1
-dimensional faces of a polytope are edges, and the maximal proper faces of
Q
are
facets
. When
Q
is given by
\conv\{\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{r}\}
, every face of
Q
will be given by the convex hull of some subset of
\{\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{r}\}
. When
Q
is given by solutions to a collection of linear inequalities, every face of
Q
is the solution set of the same inequalities, with some taken to be equalities.

Lastly, we introduce the polar of a polytope which is intimately tied to subgradients of polytope norms.

Definition 2.5

(Polar Polytope)
.

Let
Q=\conv\{\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{r}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
be a full-dimensional polytope containing the origin in its interior. The polar of
Q
is the polytope given by:

Q^{\Delta}\coloneqq\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:\forall\mathbf{y}\in Q,\,\mathbf{y}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}\leq 1\}=P(V,\mathbf{1}),

where
V
is the matrix with rows
\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{r}
. The faces of
Q^{\Delta}
are given by
\{F^{\Diamond}:\,F\in\mathfrak{F}(Q)\}
, where

F^{\Diamond}\coloneqq\{\mathbf{x}\in Q^{\Delta}:\forall\mathbf{y}\in F,\;\mathbf{y}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}=1\}.

We call
F^{\Diamond}
the polar of the face
F
.

The faces of a polytope are in bijection with the faces of its polar polytope, in fact their face lattices are anti-isomorphic;
F_{1}^{\Diamond}\subseteq F_{2}^{\Diamond}
if and only if
F_{2}\subseteq F_{1}
. The polar of a polar polytope
Q^{\Delta\Delta}
is equal to
Q
, and as such, we see that the polar of
P(A,\mathbf{1})
is given by the convex hull of the row vectors of
A
.

Example 2.6

(Hypercubes and Cross-polytopes)
.

The
k
-dimensional hypercube and cross-polytope are polars of each other. The polars of the hypercube vertices are the facets of the cross-polytope, and vice versa.

2.2
Polytope Norms

We now review the convex analysis of a norm whose unit ball is a polytope
B
. When defining polytope norms, the hyperplane representation is most natural.

Definition 2.7

(Polytope Normed Space)
.

Let
B=P(A,\mathbf{1})\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
be a full-dimensional, centrally symmetric polytope, meaning that
\mathbf{x}\in B
if and only if
-\mathbf{x}\in B
. Then
(\mathbb{R}^{k},\|\cdot\|_{B})
is a polytope normed space where the
B
-norm is defined by

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{x}\|_{B}\coloneqq\inf\{d\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}:\,d^{-1}\mathbf{x}\in B\}\equiv\max_{i\in[r]}(A\mathbf{x})_{i}.

(1)

The metric induced by the
B
-norm is denoted by
d_{B}
.

It is straightforward to show that
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{B}
is a norm, and that the unit ball of
\|\cdot\|_{B}
is the polytope
B
. In the case of our standard polytope examples:

•

The hypercube
B_{\infty}^{k}
gives rise to the
\ell_{\infty}
norm
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{B_{\infty}^{k}}\equiv\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\infty}=\max_{i\in[k]}\left\{|x_{i}|\right\}
.

•

The cross-polytope
B_{1}^{k}
gives rise to the
\ell_{1}
norm
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{B_{1}^{k}}\equiv\|\mathbf{x}\|_{1}=\sum_{i\in[k]}|x_{i}|
.

To discuss the local behaviour of a polytope norm near
\mathbf{x}
, we need to know which rows of
A
are active, meaning that they attain the componentwise maximum of
A\mathbf{x}
. We refer to these rows as the
active constraints
. In the following definition, we write
\mathbf{a}\in A
to mean
\mathbf{a}
is a row vector of
A
.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
F
be a proper face of
B
. The active constraints on
F
, denoted by
\mathcal{A}(F)
, is the set:

\mathcal{A}(F)=\{\mathbf{a}\in A:\text{ for all }\mathbf{x}\in F,\,\mathbf{a}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}=1\}.

Similarly, for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
we define
\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x})
to be the active constraints of
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{B}
. That is:

\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x})=\{\mathbf{a}\in A:\mathbf{a}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}=\|\mathbf{x}\|_{B}\}.

Notice that
\conv\mathcal{A}(F)=F^{\Diamond}
. We also note that the set of open cones generated by proper faces of
B
,
\relint(\mathbb{R}_{+}F)
, partition
\mathbb{R}^{k}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
according to the active constraints;
\mathbf{x}\in\relint(\mathbb{R}_{+}F)
if and only if
\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x})=\mathcal{A}(F)
. This partition of the space is the
face fan
of
B
.

Finally, we review the subgradient and directional derivatives of the
B
-norm at
\mathbf{x}
.

Definition 2.9

(Subgradient, Directional Derivative)
.

The subgradient of a convex function
g:\mathbb{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
at
\mathbf{x}
is given by:

\partial g(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\{\mathbf{v}:\,g(\mathbf{z})\geq g(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{v}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x})\;\text{ for all }\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\}.

The directional derivative in direction
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, denoted by
\partial_{\mathbf{t}}g(\mathbf{x})
, is

\partial_{\mathbf{t}}g(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\lim_{h\rightarrow 0^{+}}h^{-1}\left(g(\mathbf{x}+h\mathbf{t})-g(\mathbf{x})\right)=\sup_{\mathbf{v}\in\partial g(\mathbf{x})}\mathbf{v}^{\intercal}\mathbf{t}.

This equality is from Theorem 23.4 of
(
23
)
.

Remark 2.10
.

The subgradient of a maximum of differentiable functions
g(\mathbf{x})=\max\{g_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\dots,g_{n}(\mathbf{x})\}
is known to be the convex hull of the gradients of active functions at
\mathbf{x}
:
\partial g(\mathbf{x})=\conv\{\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}g_{i}(\mathbf{x}):g_{i}(\mathbf{x})=g(\mathbf{x})\}
. Applying this result to a polytope
B
-norm, which is a maximum of linear functions by (
1
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\partial\|\mathbf{x}\|_{B}^{2}=\conv\left\{\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{x})^{2}:\mathbf{a}\in F^{\Diamond}\right\}=2\|\mathbf{x}\|F^{\Diamond},

when
\mathbf{x}\in\relint(\mathbb{R}_{+}F)
. Consequently, the subgradient of the sample Fréchet function will be given by

\partial f(\mathbf{x})=2\sum_{i\in[n]}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\theta\|F_{i}^{\Diamond}
, where
F_{i}
is the unique face such that
\mathbf{x}_{i}-\theta\in\relint(\mathbb{R}_{+}F_{i})
, and the summation is a Minkowski sum. Since a convex function is minimized if and only if its subgradient contains
\mathbf{0}
, it follows that
\theta
is a sample Fréchet mean if and only if
\mathbf{0}\in\sum_{i\in[n]}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\theta\|F_{i}^{\Diamond}
.

3
Fréchet Means on Normed Spaces

In this section, we discuss Fréchet means and outline their fundamental properties in the case of (polytope) normed spaces. All Fréchet means in the sections to follow are taken with respect to a given
B
-norm, unless stated otherwise. As defined in
Definition

1.1
, we use
f
to denote a Fréchet function; this is given by
f(\theta)=\mathbb{E}[d(X,\theta)^{2}]
for general random variables and
f(\theta)=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}d(\mathbf{x}_{i},\theta)^{2}
for a sample
\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{n}\}
. We then denote the Fréchet mean set by
\FMset\coloneqq\text{argmin}_{\theta}f(\theta)
, which we may abbreviate to
\FMset(X)
or
\FMset(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{n})
.

Understanding the convexity of the Fréchet function is crucial for understanding the existence and convexity of Fréchet mean sets; these foundational convexity results have been established in many settings, such as Wasserstein spaces
(
20
)
, Riemannian spaces
(
1
)
, metric trees,
(
24
)
, and the tropical projective torus
(
15
)
. Below, we state the convexity results we require in the case of a general normed space. These results below are generalisations of the convexity results established in the tropical setting by
15
, and mirror the work of
(
11
)
on Fermat–Weber points.

Lemma 3.1
.

Fix a normed space
(\mathbb{R}^{k},\|\cdot\|)
with induced metric
d
. For all
\mathbf{x}
,
d(\cdot,\mathbf{x})^{2}
is convex, and in fact if, for some
t\in(0,1)
we have

d((1-t)\mathbf{\theta}_{1}+t\mathbf{\theta}_{2},\mathbf{x})^{2}=(1-t)d(\mathbf{\theta}_{1},\mathbf{x})^{2}+td(\mathbf{\theta}_{2},\mathbf{x})^{2},

then
d(\mathbf{\theta}_{1},\mathbf{x})=d(\mathbf{\theta}_{2},\mathbf{x})
. The Fréchet function is also convex, and so Fréchet mean sets are convex.

Proof.

Convexity of a squared norm is immediate from the convexity of norms, as the function
y\mapsto y^{2}
is strictly monotone increasing and strictly convex over the domain
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
:

\displaystyle\|(1-t)\mathbf{\theta}_{1}+t\mathbf{\theta}_{2}-\mathbf{x}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left((1-t)\|\mathbf{\theta}_{1}-\mathbf{x}\|+t\|\mathbf{\theta}_{2}-\mathbf{x}\|\right)^{2},

\displaystyle\leq(1-t)\|\mathbf{\theta}_{1}-\mathbf{x}\|^{2}+t\|\mathbf{\theta}_{2}-\mathbf{x}\|^{2}.

Hence
\|\theta-x\|^{2}=d(\theta,\mathbf{x})^{2}
is strictly convex in
\theta
.

The second statement follows from the strict convexity of
y\mapsto y^{2}
; we have equality if and only if
\|\theta_{1}-\mathbf{x}\|^{2}=\|\theta_{2}-\mathbf{x}\|^{2}
. The convexity of the Fréchet function
f
is a direct result of expectation preserving convexity, while the convexity of Fréchet mean sets follows from the convexity of
f
.
∎

Building on
Lemma

3.1
, we will see that Fréchet mean sets are given by intersections of spheres.
Lemma

3.2
expresses this in the case of a general population
X\sim\mathbb{P}
, while
Corollary

3.3
considers the case of a finite sample.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
X\sim\mathbb{P}
be a random variable in
(\mathbb{R}^{k},\|\cdot\|)
. Assume that
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
are Fréchet means of
X
. Then for every
\mathbf{x}
in the
\mathrm{esssupp}(\mathbb{P})
, we have
d(\mathbf{x},\theta_{1})=d(\mathbf{x},\theta_{2})
.

Proof.

Fix some
\mathbf{x}\in\mathrm{esssupp}(\mathbb{P})
. By definition, for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
we have
\mathbb{P}(B_{1/m}(\mathbf{x}))>0
. We will show that there is some
\mathbf{x}_{m}\in B_{1/m}(\mathbf{x})
such that
d(\mathbf{x}_{m},\theta_{1})=d(\mathbf{x}_{m},\theta_{2})
by contradiction. Let
\theta_{3}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2})
be a midpoint of
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}
. By the convexity of
d(X,\cdot)
and strict convexity of
y\mapsto y^{2}
:

\displaystyle d(X,\theta_{3})^{2}\leq\big(\tfrac{1}{2}d(X,\theta_{1})+\tfrac{1}{2}d(X,\theta_{2})\big)^{2}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\big(d(X,\theta_{1})^{2}+d(X,\theta_{2})^{2}\big).

We assume for all
\mathbf{u}\in B_{1/m}(\mathbf{x})
,
d(\mathbf{u},\theta_{1})\neq d(\mathbf{u},\theta_{2})
, so the above inequality is strict. It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[I_{X\in B_{1/m}}d(X,\theta_{3})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle<\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[I_{X\in B_{1/m}}d(X,\theta_{1})^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[I_{X\in B_{1/m}}d(X,\theta_{2})^{2}\right]\right).

Hence

\mathbb{E}\left[d(X,\theta_{3})^{2}\right]<\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[d(X,\theta_{1})^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[d(X,\theta_{2})^{2}\right]\right),

which contradicts the optimality of
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
. Therefore for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we can pick some
\mathbf{x}_{m}\in B_{1/m}(\mathbf{x})
such that
d(\mathbf{x}_{m},\theta_{1})=d(\mathbf{x}_{m},\theta_{2})
. By continuity of the distance function we conclude
d(\mathbf{x},\theta_{1})=d(\mathbf{x},\theta_{2})
.
∎

As a natural corollary to
Lemma

3.2
, we see that any distribution whose essential support is
\mathbb{R}^{k}
must have a unique Fréchet mean (
Theorem

4.1
). However, for finite samples, uniqueness is not guaranteed. Fréchet means are given by intersections of spheres centred at each data point, as shown in
Corollary

3.3
. In fact, in the case of polytope norms, Fréchet means are intersections of faces.
Figure

2
provides a visualisation of this behaviour for Fréchet mean sets of a small number of samples.

Corollary 3.3
.

Fix a normed space
(\mathbb{R}^{k},\|\cdot\|_{B})
with unit ball
B
. There exist unique
d_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that

\FMset(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n})=\bigcap_{i\in[n]}\{d_{i}B+\mathbf{x}_{i}\}=\bigcap_{i\in[n]}\{d_{i}\partial B+\mathbf{x}_{i}\},

where
\partial B
is the boundary of
B
. In particular, if
B
is a polytope then
\FMset(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{n})
is a polytope. Moreover, for every
i\in[n]
there exists a proper face
F_{i}
of
B
such that
\FMset
is given by
\bigcap_{i\in[n]}\{d_{i}F_{i}+\mathbf{x}_{i}\}
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

3.2
, the distances
d_{i}=d(\mathbf{x}_{i},\theta)
are fixed for all
\theta\in\FMset(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{n})
. Hence:

\FMset(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{n})\subseteq\bigcap_{i\in[n]}\{d_{i}\partial B+\mathbf{x}_{i}\}\subseteq\bigcap_{i\in[n]}\{d_{i}B+\mathbf{x}_{i}\}\subseteq\FMset(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{n}).

This follows from
Lemma

3.2
, nested intersections, and the definition of the
d_{i}
. When
B
is a polytope, the intersection of balls about each
\mathbf{x}_{i}
is also a polytope.

We prove the last claim by contradiction. Suppose there is no constraint
\mathbf{a}\in A
which is active for all
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}-\theta:\theta\in\FMset\}
. Then for all
\mathbf{a}_{j}\in A
, we have some
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{r}\in\FMset
such that
\mathbf{a}_{j}\in A
is not in
\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\theta_{j})
. By the convexity of
\FMset
,
\tfrac{1}{r}\sum_{j\in[r]}\theta_{j}
is a Fréchet mean and must be in the set
d_{i}\partial B+\mathbf{x}_{i}
. For all
\mathbf{a}_{j}
, we have

\mathbf{a}_{j}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\theta_{j})<\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\theta_{j}\|_{B}=d_{i},\qquad\mathbf{a}_{j}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\theta_{k})\leq\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\theta_{k}\|_{B}=d_{i}.

We obtain that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\tfrac{1}{r}\textstyle\sum_{j\in[r]}\theta_{j}\right\|_{B}=\textstyle\max_{j^{\prime}}\mathbf{a}_{j^{\prime}}^{\top}\left(\tfrac{1}{r}\sum_{j\in[r]}(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\theta_{j})\right)<\tfrac{1}{r}\sum_{j\in[r]}\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\theta_{j}\|_{B}=d_{i},

contradicting

We conclude with a final corollary on Fréchet means for normed spaces with strictly convex balls, to show the contrast with the polytope norm setting; if the unit ball of a norm is strictly convex, then Fréchet means must be unique.

Corollary 3.6
.

If
B
is a strictly convex ball, in the sense that the relative interior of any line between two points in
\partial B
is contained in
\mathrm{int}(B)
, then
\FMset(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n})
consists of a single point. In particular, sample Fréchet means with respect to
\ell_{p}
norms,
p\in(1,\infty)
, are always unique.

Proof.

The norm of a strictly convex ball has the property that whenever
\mathbf{x}
is not proportional to
\mathbf{y}
with respect to a non-negative scaling, we have for all
t\in(0,1)
that

\displaystyle\|(1-t)\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{y}\|_{B}

\displaystyle=\big((1-t)\|\mathbf{x}\|_{B}+t\|\mathbf{y}\|_{B}\big)\bigg\|\frac{(1-t)\|\mathbf{x}\|_{B}}{(1-t)\|\mathbf{x}\|_{B}+t\|\mathbf{y}\|_{B}}\frac{\mathbf{x}}{\|\mathbf{x}\|_{B}}+\frac{t\|\mathbf{y}\|_{B}}{(1-t)\|\mathbf{x}\|_{B}+t\|\mathbf{y}\|_{B}}\frac{\mathbf{y}}{\|\mathbf{y}\|_{B}}\bigg\|_{B}

\displaystyle<(1-t)\|\mathbf{x}\|_{B}+t\|\mathbf{y}\|_{B},

since the above expression is a convex combination of the points
\mathbf{x}/\|\mathbf{x}\|_{B},\mathbf{y}/\|\mathbf{y}\|_{B}\in\partial B
. Consider two Fréchet means,
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\FMset(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{n})
. By the convexity of
\FMset
, and
Lemma

3.2
,
(1-t)\theta_{1}+t\theta_{2}
has the same distance to
\mathbf{x}_{1}
as
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
. So by the result above we must have that
\mathbf{x}_{1}-\theta_{1}=c(\mathbf{x}_{1}-\theta_{2})
for some
c\geq 0
, as well as
d(\mathbf{x}_{1},\theta_{1})=d(\mathbf{x}_{1},\theta_{2})
. The only way this can occur is if
c=1
, and thus
\theta_{1}=\theta_{2}
.

The
\ell_{p}
balls are strictly convex for
p\in(1,\infty)
since if
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{p}=\|\mathbf{y}\|_{p}=1
with
\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{y}
, then

\displaystyle\|(1-t)\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{y}\|_{p}^{p}=\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}|(1-t)x_{i}+ty_{i}|^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}((1-t)|x_{i}|+t|y_{i}|)^{p}

\displaystyle<\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\big((1-t)|x_{i}|^{p}+t|y_{i}|^{p}\big)=1.

The strict inequality follows from Jensen’s inequality and the fact that
\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{y}
.
∎

5
Sample Threshold for Unique Fréchet Means

In this section, we identify the combinatorial conditions that dictate the sample size needed for the sample Fréchet mean to be unique with positive probability. We show that this sample size is a threshold in that there is some value
N(B)
such that the probability of a unique Fréchet mean is positive for a sample of size
n
if and only if
n\geq N(B)
. For the
\ell_{1}
norm, this sample threshold is just 3, but for the
\ell_{\infty}
norm, this sample threshold is equal to the dimension of the space,
k
.

To understand the dimension of Fréchet mean sets, we study which faces of balls centred about our data points can be intersected to define the Fréchet mean set (
Corollary

3.3
). The dimension of the Fréchet mean set
\FMset
is identifiable from the minimal faces whose intersection is the Fréchet mean set. We call these minimal faces the
face type
of a sample. We show that the face type of a sample is almost surely well-defined and determines the dimension of
\FMset
, then we identify which face types occur with positive probability by considering subgradients. To define the face type of a sample, we will assume that no data point is also a Fréchet mean of the sample, which is true almost surely.

Lemma 5.1
.

If
\mathrm{esssupp}(\mathbb{P})=\mathbb{R}^{k}
then with probability one, the random sample points
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}\mathbb{P}
are not Fréchet means of the full sample; for all
i
,
X_{i}\notin\FMset(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
.

Proof.

We claim
X_{1}
is a Fréchet mean of
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
iff
X_{1}
is a Fréchet mean of
X_{2},\dots,X_{n}
. Denote the Fréchet function for the sample
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
as
f(\mathbf{x})
, and the Fréchet function for
X_{2},\dots,X_{n}
as
f_{\hat{1}}(\mathbf{x})
, so:

f(\mathbf{x})=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}d(X_{i},\mathbf{x}),\qquad f_{\hat{1}}(\mathbf{x})=(n-1)^{-1}\sum_{i=2}^{n}d(X_{i},\mathbf{x}).

The subgradient of
d(X_{1},\cdot)^{2}
at
X_{1}
is 0, so by Theorem 23.8 in
23
:

n\partial f(X_{1})=(n-1)\partial f_{\hat{1}}(X_{1}).

The point
X_{1}
is a Fréchet mean of
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
if and only if
0\in\partial f(X_{1})
, and is a Fréchet mean of
X_{2},\dots X_{n}
if and only if
0\in\partial f_{\hat{1}}(X_{1})
. These conditions are equivalent, so our claim is proved.

We now show that the sample points
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are almost never Fréchet means of the full sample
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
. Let
A_{1}
be the event that
X_{1}
is a Fréchet mean of the full sample, or equivalently
X_{1}\in\FMset(X_{2},\dots,X_{n})
. By Corollary
3.3
, the set
\FMset(X_{2},\dots,X_{n})
has co-dimension
\geq 1
, and hence has
\mathbb{P}
-measure zero. Therefore
\mathbb{P}(A_{1})=0
. We can define
A_{2},\dots,A_{n}
similarly, and by taking a union over
A_{1},\dots,A_{n}
we conclude that the Fréchet mean set intersects the sample
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
with probability zero.
∎

5.1
Face Types

As shown in
Corollary

3.3
, the Fréchet mean set is an intersection of faces of balls centred at each data point. We will define the face type of a sample as the minimal sequence of faces of balls where the Fréchet mean set is their intersection. The lemma below ensures this is almost surely well-defined.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be a finite sample with Fréchet mean set
\FMset
such that
X_{i}\notin\FMset
for every
i
. Let
d_{i}=d_{B}(X_{i},\FMset)
. Then the set of tuples of faces

S_{F}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})=\bigg\{(F^{\prime}_{1},\dots,F^{\prime}_{n})\ :\textstyle\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-d_{i}F^{\prime}_{i})=\FMset\bigg\}\subseteq\mathfrak{F}(B)^{n}

has a least element
(F_{1},\dots,F_{n})\in S_{F}
with respect to coordinate-wise containment.

Proof.

By
Corollary

3.3
,
S_{F}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
is not empty. Define
(F_{1},\dots,F_{n})
by
F_{i}=\bigcap_{(F^{\prime}_{1},\dots,F^{\prime}_{n})\in S_{F}}F^{\prime}_{i}
.
Then for all
(F^{\prime}_{1},\dots,F^{\prime}_{n})\in S_{F}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
we have that
(F_{1},\dots,F_{n})\leq(F^{\prime}_{1},\dots,F^{\prime}_{n})
.

It suffices to show that
(F_{1},\dots,F_{n})\in S_{F}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
. This follows from:

\displaystyle\textstyle\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-d_{i}F_{i})

\displaystyle=\textstyle\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\bigg(X_{i}-d_{i}\big(\textstyle\bigcap_{(F^{\prime}_{1},\dots,F^{\prime}_{n})\in S_{F}}F^{\prime}_{i}\big)\bigg)

\displaystyle=\textstyle\bigcap_{(F^{\prime}_{1},\dots,F^{\prime}_{n})\in S_{F}}\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-d_{i}F^{\prime}_{i})

\displaystyle=\textstyle\bigcap_{(F^{\prime}_{1},\dots,F^{\prime}_{n})\in S_{F}}\FMset=\FMset.

∎

Using the lemma above, we can define the face type of a sample:

Definition 5.3

(Face Type)
.

Consider a sample
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
such that no data point is a Fréchet mean of the full dataset. Such a sample is defined to have face type
(F_{1},\dots,F_{n})
about the Fréchet mean set
\FMset
when
(F_{1},\dots,F_{n})
is the least element of
S_{F}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
.

By
Lemma

5.1
and
Lemma

5.2
, the face type of a sample is well-defined almost surely. However, we will use a more geometric description of face type through the rest of this section. Both here and throughout the rest of this section we use
d_{i}\coloneqq d_{B}(X_{i},\FMset)
which is well-defined by
Corollary

3.3
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be a sample with face type
(F_{1},\dots F_{n})
and
d_{i}=d_{B}(X_{i},\FMset)
. Then we have that

\relint\FMset=\textstyle\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\relint(X_{i}-d_{i}F_{i}).

A sample has face type
(F_{1},\dots,F_{n})
if and only if there is some
\theta\in\relint\FMset
such that for all
i\in[n]
,
X_{i}-\theta\in\relint(\mathbb{R}_{+}F_{i})
.

Proof.

By definition,
\FMset\subseteq(X_{1}-d_{1}F_{1})
. Suppose that
\relint\FMset
does not intersect
\relint(X_{1}-d_{1}F_{1})
, so
\relint\FMset\subseteq(X_{1}-d_{1}\partial F_{1})
. By Corollary
3.4
, there is some face
F_{1}^{\prime}
of
F_{1}
satisfying
\FMset\subseteq X_{1}-d_{1}F_{1}^{\prime}
. Hence
(F_{1}^{\prime},F_{2},\dots,F_{n})\in S_{F}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
, contradicting the definition of
(F_{1},\dots,F_{n})
. Therefore (for any
i
, by symmetry)
\relint\FMset
intersects
\relint(X_{i}-d_{i}F_{i})
, and by

\displaystyle\relint(\FMset\cap(X_{i}-d_{i}F_{i}))

\displaystyle=\relint\FMset\cap\relint(X_{i}-d_{i}F_{i}),

\displaystyle\relint\FMset

\displaystyle\subseteq\relint(X_{i}-d_{i}F_{i}).

Applying

\displaystyle\relint\FMset=\textstyle\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\relint(X_{i}-d_{i}F_{i}).

Suppose our sample has face type

. Then for all

To show the converse, consider

Lemma 5.5
.

If

\text{aff}(W_{1}\cap W_{2})\subseteq\text{aff}(W_{1})\cap\text{aff}(W_{2})

5.2
Type Events

By
Lemma

5.5
, we can deduce the dimension of a Fréchet mean set from the face type of a sample. We must now identify which face types occur with positive probability, and identify which face types correspond to a unique Fréchet mean. To determine which face types occur with positive probability, we define the (lifted) type event. Through this section, we use
C_{i}
and
G_{i}
to denote
\mathbb{R}_{+}F_{i}
and
F_{i}^{\Diamond}
respectively.

Definition 5.7

((Lifted) Type Event)
.

Consider the random vector
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{nk}
. In this space, the
type event

U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}
is given by:

U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}=\{(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{nk}:(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\text{ has face type }(F_{1},\dots F_{n})\}.

Then we define the
lifted type event
as the set

U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta}=\{(X_{1},\dots,X_{n},\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{k(n+1)}:\theta\in\relint\FMset,\;X_{i}-\theta\in\relint C_{i}\},

where
C_{i}
denotes the cone
\mathbb{R}_{+}F_{i}
.

Note that by
Lemma

5.4
, the type event is the image of the lifted type event under the projection
\proj:\mathbb{R}^{k(n+1)}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{kn}
given by
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n},\theta)\mapsto(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
.

Our goal is to classify the face types
(F_{1},\dots,F_{n})
for which
U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}
has positive Lebesgue measure. Then as
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
has a positive density, such a face type occurs with positive probability. It is difficult to calculate the measure of the type events directly, so we will translate this condition into a linear relation on subgradients. For this, we define the
zero subgradient event
.

Definition 5.8

(Zero Subgradient Event)
.

The zero subgradient event is given by:

V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta}=\left\{(X_{1},\dots,X_{n},\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{k(n+1)}:\mathbf{0}\in\relint\left(\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|X_{i}-\theta\|G_{i}\right),\;X_{i}-\theta\in\relint C_{i}\right\},

where
G_{i}
denotes the polar face
F_{i}^{\Diamond}
, and
C_{i}
denotes the cone
\mathbb{R}_{+}F_{i}
.

Note that when we have
X_{i}-\theta\in\relint C_{i}
for all
i
, the subgradient of the Fréchet function is given by
\partial f(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|X_{i}-\theta\|G_{i}
. Hence, by the definition of subgradients,
\theta\in\FMset
if and only if
\mathbf{0}\in\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|X_{i}-\theta\|G_{i}
(
Remark

2.10
). From this, we might hope that the lifted type event and the zero subgradient event are equivalent, but this is not true. However, they do coincide almost everywhere.

Proposition 5.9
.

The zero subgradient event
V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta}
is a relatively open polyhedron; it is an intersection of finitely many hyperplanes and open half spaces. Furthermore, it coincides with the lifted type event everywhere other than on its boundary:

\displaystyle\relint V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta}

\displaystyle=\relint U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta},

\displaystyle\closure V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta}

\displaystyle=\closure U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta}.

The proof of this proposition can be found in
Appendix

A
.

5.3
Polytope Conditions for Unique Fréchet Means

We can now state and prove the main result of this section (
Theorem

5.10
), which establishes the conditions for
U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}
to occur with positive probability and to produce a unique Fréchet mean. We do this by computing the dimension of the polyhedron
V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta}
, which then allows us to compute the dimension of
\proj V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta}
and
U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}
. This result is crucial for not only computing the sample threshold for uniqueness, but also for proving that the sample threshold for uniqueness is well-defined.

Theorem 5.10
.

The type event
U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}
and
\proj V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta}
satisfy

\mathcal{L}(U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}})=\mathcal{L}(\proj V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta}).

Therefore
U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}
has positive probability if and only if

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}\in\relint\left(\conv\left(\textstyle\bigcup_{i\in[n]}G_{i}\right)\right),

(3)

\displaystyle\text{and}\qquad\textstyle\sum_{i\in[n]}\dim G_{i}=\dim\textstyle\sum_{i\in[n]}\aff G_{i}.

(4)

The Fréchet mean of a sample in
U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}
is unique if and only if

\displaystyle\dim\conv\left(\textstyle\bigcup_{i\in[n]}G_{i}\right)=k.

(5)

To understand this result, we return to one of our examples from
Fig.

2
, displayed again in
Fig.

5
, which shows a sample with face type
(F_{1},F_{2},F_{3})=(\mathbf{e}_{1}+\mathbf{e}_{2},\conv\{-\mathbf{e}_{2}\pm\mathbf{e}_{1}\},\conv\{-\mathbf{e}_{1}\pm\mathbf{e}_{2}\})
. We can test whether this face type satisfies the three conditions in
Theorem

5.10
; for this face type, note that
(G_{1},G_{2},G_{3})=(\conv\{\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2}\},-\mathbf{e}_{2},-\mathbf{e}_{1})
.

The first condition
Eq.

3
verifies whether a sample can have the face type
(F_{1},F_{2},F_{3})
. This is shown to be true in

, and in fact
\conv\left(\textstyle\bigcup_{i\in[n]}G_{i}\right)=\conv\{\pm\mathbf{e}_{1},\pm\mathbf{e}_{2}\}
which contains
\mathbf{0}
in its relative interior.

The second condition

4
verifies whether a sample has face type
(F_{1},F_{2},F_{3})
with positive probability. The dimensions of

are 1,0,0 respectively, while
\sum_{i\leq 3}\aff G_{i}=\aff\{-\mathbf{e}_{1},-\mathbf{e}_{2}\}
, which has dimension

. This verifies that such a face type can occur with positive probability; this configuration of data points around a Fréchet mean is generic.

Finally, the third condition
Eq.

5
verifies whether having face type
(F_{1},\dots,F_{n})
implies that our sample has a unique Fréchet mean. We know that
\conv\left(\textstyle\bigcup_{i\in[n]}G_{i}\right)=\conv\{\pm\mathbf{e}_{1},\pm\mathbf{e}_{2}\}
which has dimension 2, and therefore any sample with this face type will have unique Fréchet mean.

\mathbf{x}_{1}=(-4,-4)
\mathbf{x}_{2}=(1,2)
\mathbf{x}_{3}=(2,-1)

:
A sample with face type

We prove
Theorem

5.10
via the following three lemmas.

Lemma 5.11
.

The zero subgradient event
V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta}
is non-empty if and only if
\mathbf{0}\in\relint\left(\conv\left(\textstyle\bigcup_{i\in[n]}G_{i}\right)\right)
.

Proof.

First, we have shown in the proof of
Proposition

5.9
that

V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta}=L_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}^{-1}(W)\textstyle\bigcap\bigcap_{i\in[n]}\{(X_{1},\dots,X_{n},\theta):X_{i}-\theta\in\relint C_{i}\},

(6)

where
W
is the relatively open polyhedral cone
\{\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}:\mathbf{0}\in\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}\relint G_{i}\}
(
Lemma

A.1
).

We will show that
V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta}
is not empty if and only if
W
is non-empty. Suppose
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n},\theta)\in V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta}
; then by
Eq.

6
,
W
is non-empty. Conversely, suppose
\mathbf{w}\in W
. Fix an arbitrary
\theta
and pick some
X_{i}\in\theta+w_{i}\relint F_{i}
. By construction,
X_{i}-\theta\in\relint C_{i}
and
\|X_{i}-\theta\|_{B}=w_{i}
. By the definition of
W
we have
\mathbf{0}\in\relint\left(\textstyle\sum_{i\in[n]}\|X_{i}-\theta\|_{B}G_{i}\right),
so
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n},\theta)\in V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta}
.

Next, note that
(
23
, Theorem 6.9)
states that for non-empty convex

, then

\relint\left(\conv\left(\textstyle\bigcup_{i\in[n]}G_{i}\right)\right)=\bigcup\left\{\textstyle\sum_{i\in[n]}\lambda_{i}\relint G_{i}:\lambda_{i}>0,\sum_{i\in[n]}\lambda_{i}=1\right\}.

(7)

From this we see that
\mathbf{0}\in\relint\left(\conv\left(\textstyle\bigcup_{i\in[n]}G_{i}\right)\right)
if and only if
W
is non-empty. We conclude that
V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta}
and
V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}
are non-empty if and only if
\mathbf{0}\in\relint\left(\conv\left(\textstyle\bigcup_{i\in[n]}G_{i}\right)\right)
.
∎

Lemma 5.12
.

The type event
U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}
has positive probability if and only if the polyhedron
\proj V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta}
has dimension
nk
.

Proof.

The zero subgradient event
V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta}
is Borel measurable as it is a relatively open polyhedron. Consequently,
\proj V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta}
is also a relatively open polyhedron and so also Borel measurable.
We also have by Proposition
5.9
and Theorem 6.6 of
23
:

\displaystyle\relint U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}

\displaystyle=\relint(\proj U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta})

\displaystyle=\proj(\relint U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta})

\displaystyle=\proj(\relint V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta})

\displaystyle=\relint(\proj V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta}),

\displaystyle U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}

\displaystyle=\proj U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta}

\displaystyle\subseteq\proj(\closure U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta})

\displaystyle=\proj(\closure V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta})

\displaystyle\subseteq\closure(\proj V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta}).

Hence
\proj V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta}
is a measurable set which differs from
U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}
only on its boundary. The boundary of
\proj V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta}
is a measure zero set as it is a relatively open polyhedron, so by the completeness of the Lebesgue measure,
U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}
is measurable and
\mathcal{L}(U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}})=\mathcal{L}(\proj V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta})
.

As a polyhedron,
\proj V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta}
has positive Lebesgue measure if and only if it has full dimension
nk
.
∎

Lemma 5.13
.

A sample with face type
(F_{1},\dots,F_{n})
will have a Fréchet mean set of dimension

k-\dim\conv\left(\textstyle\bigcup_{i\in[n]}G_{i}\right).

Proof.

We recall
Lemma

5.5
; we have that
\relint\FMset=\bigcap_{i\in[n]}\relint(X_{i}-d_{i}F_{i})
, so

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle=\dim\textstyle\bigcap_{i\in[n]}\aff(X_{i}-d_{i}F_{i}),

\displaystyle=\dim\left\{\theta+\mathbf{v}:\theta\in\textstyle\bigcap_{i\in[n]}(X_{i}-d_{i}F_{i}),\mathbf{v}\in G_{i}^{\perp}\text{ for all }i\in[n]\right\},

\displaystyle=\dim\left(\textstyle\bigcup_{i\in[n]}G_{i}\right)^{\perp},

\displaystyle=k-\dim\conv\left(\textstyle\bigcup_{i\in[n]}G_{i}\right).

(8)

∎

We can now prove
Theorem

5.10
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

5.11
and
Proposition

5.9
, if

3
does not hold, then
V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta}
and
U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}
are empty. We therefore assume that
Eq.

3
holds such that
V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta}
is non-empty, and then compute its dimension.

As
V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta}
is non-empty, we can use
Lemma

5.5
:

\displaystyle\text{aff}(V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta})

\displaystyle=\aff L_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}^{-1}(W)\textstyle\bigcap\bigcap_{i\in[n]}\text{aff}\{(X_{1},\dots,X_{n},\theta):X_{i}-\theta\in\relint C_{i}\}.

Observe that

\displaystyle\aff L_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}^{-1}(W)

\displaystyle=\{\textstyle\sum_{i\in[n]}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}:L_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}\mathbf{x}_{i}\in W,\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R},\sum_{i\in[n]}\lambda_{i}=1\},

\displaystyle=\{L_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}^{-1}\textstyle\sum_{i\in[n]}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{w}_{i}:\mathbf{w}_{i}\in W,\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R},\sum_{i\in[n]}\lambda_{i}=1\},

\displaystyle=L_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}^{-1}\aff(W).

And therefore

\displaystyle\text{aff}(V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta})

\displaystyle=L_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}^{-1}(\aff(W))\textstyle\bigcap\bigcap_{i\in[n]}\text{aff}\{(X_{1},\dots,X_{n},\theta):X_{i}-\theta\in\relint C_{i}\}.

Recall that
L_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}
is defined as
L_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}:(X_{1},\dots,X_{n},\theta)\mapsto\left(\mathbf{a}_{1}^{\intercal}(X_{1}-\theta),\dots,\mathbf{a}_{n}^{\intercal}(X_{n}-\theta)\right)
, where
\mathbf{a}_{i}
is some element of
\mathcal{A}(F_{i})
. The affine hull of
C_{i}
contains those points
\mathbf{x}
for which
\mathbf{a}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}
is constant for all
\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{A}(F_{i})
; it is the orthogonal to
\aff\mathcal{A}(F_{i})=\aff G_{i}
. Hence
\aff C_{i}
has dimension

\displaystyle\dim\aff C_{i}

\displaystyle=k-\dim\aff\mathcal{A}(F_{i}),

\displaystyle=k-\dim G_{i}.

Noting this, we can pick
\mathbf{a}_{i}^{1}\dots,\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\dim G_{i}}
in
\mathcal{A}(F_{i})
such that
\mathbf{a}_{i},\mathbf{a}_{i}^{1}\dots,\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\dim G_{i}}
forms an affine basis of
\aff\mathcal{A}(F_{i})
, and
\aff C_{i}
contains those
\mathbf{x}
such that for all
j\in[\dim G_{i}]
, we have
\mathbf{a}_{i}^{j\intercal}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}
. Then

\displaystyle\aff\{(X_{1},\dots,X_{n},\theta):X_{i}-\theta\in C_{i}\}

\displaystyle=\{(X_{1},\dots,X_{n},\theta):X_{i}-\theta\in\aff C_{i}\},

\displaystyle=\{(X_{1},\dots,X_{n},\theta):\forall\,j\in[\dim G_{i}],\;(\mathbf{a}_{i}^{j}-\mathbf{a}_{i})^{\intercal}(X_{i}-\theta)=0\}.

We define
M_{i}
to be the linear map sending
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n},\theta)
to
\left((\mathbf{a}_{i}^{j}-\mathbf{a}_{i})^{\intercal}(X_{i}-\theta)\right)_{j\in[\dim G_{i}]}
, such that
\aff\{(X_{1},\dots,X_{n},\theta):X_{i}-\theta\in\relint C_{i}\}=M_{i}^{-1}(\mathbf{0})
. Then

\displaystyle\text{aff}(V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta})

\displaystyle=L_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}^{-1}(\aff(W))\cap M_{1}^{-1}(\mathbf{0})\cap\dots\cap M_{n}^{-1}(\mathbf{0}),

\displaystyle=(L_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}},M_{1},\dots,M_{n})^{-1}(\aff(W),\mathbf{0},\dots,\mathbf{0}).

The linear map
(L_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}},M_{1},\dots,M_{n}):\mathbb{R}^{k(n+1)}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n+\sum_{i}\dim G_{i}}
is given by the matrix:

M=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{a}_{1}^{\intercal}&\dots&\mathbf{0}^{\intercal}&-\mathbf{a}_{1}^{\intercal}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\mathbf{0}^{\intercal}&\ldots&\mathbf{a}_{n}^{\intercal}&-\mathbf{a}_{n}^{\intercal}\\
(\mathbf{a}_{1}^{1}-\mathbf{a}_{1})^{\intercal}&\ldots&\mathbf{0}^{\intercal}&(\mathbf{a}_{1}-\mathbf{a}_{1}^{1})^{\intercal}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
(\mathbf{a}_{1}^{\dim G_{1}}-\mathbf{a}_{1})^{\intercal}&\ldots&\mathbf{0}^{\intercal}&(\mathbf{a}_{1}^{\dim G_{1}}-\mathbf{a}_{1})^{\intercal}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\mathbf{0}^{\intercal}&\ldots&(\mathbf{a}_{n}^{1}-\mathbf{a}_{n})^{\intercal}&(\mathbf{a}_{n}-\mathbf{a}_{n}^{1})^{\intercal}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\mathbf{0}^{\intercal}&\ldots&(\mathbf{a}_{n}^{\dim G_{n}}-\mathbf{a}_{n})^{\intercal}&(\mathbf{a}_{n}-\mathbf{a}_{n}^{\dim G_{n}})^{\intercal}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Subtracting row
i
from the rows corresponding to
M_{i}
, we see the rank of this matrix is given by:

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{i\in[n]}\dim\text{span}(\mathbf{a}_{i},\mathbf{a}_{i}^{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\dim G_{i}})

\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{i\in[n]}1+\dim\aff(\mathbf{a}_{i},\mathbf{a}_{i}^{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\dim G_{i}}),

\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{i\in[n]}1+\dim G_{i},

\displaystyle=n+\textstyle\sum_{i\in[n]}\dim G_{i}.

Where the first line is because
\aff(\mathbf{a}_{i},\mathbf{a}_{i}^{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{i}^{\dim G_{i}})=\aff\mathcal{A}(F_{i})
doesn’t contain the origin; for all
\mathbf{a}\in\aff\mathcal{A}(F_{i}),\,\mathbf{a}^{\intercal}(X_{i}-\theta)=\|X_{i}-\theta\|>0
.

It follows that the matrix
M
is full rank. Let
T:\mathbb{R}^{n+\sum_{i}\dim G_{i}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n+\sum_{i}\dim G_{i}-\dim W}
be the linear projection onto the orthogonal complement of
(\aff(W),\mathbf{0})
such that the kernel of
T
is exactly
(\aff(W),0)
.
Then by construction, the map
T\circ M:\mathbb{R}^{k(n+1)}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n+\sum_{i}\dim G_{i}-\dim W}
is surjective with kernel
\aff(V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta})
and by the rank-nullity theorem:

\displaystyle\dim V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta}

\displaystyle=k(n+1)-\mathrm{rank}\,(T\circ M)=k(n+1)-n-\textstyle\sum_{i\in[n]}\dim G_{i}+\dim W.

Next, we compute the dimension of
U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}
. For this, we will use
(
5
, Theorem 2.4)
, which states:

Theorem

(Theorem 2.4 of
5
)
.

Consider a polytope
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{q}
with affine hull is given by
\{(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{q}:A\mathbf{u}+B\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}\}
. Then
\dim\proj_{\mathbb{R}^{q}}(Q)=\dim Q-\text{Ker}(A)
.

We apply this theorem to
V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta}
; define matrices

and vector

to be such that

\aff V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta}=\{(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{n},\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{nk}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}:M^{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{n})+M^{2}\theta=\mathbf{b}\}.

Note that for
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\in U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}
, the set
\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:M^{2}\theta=\mathbf{b}-M^{1}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\}
is now the affine hull of the Fréchet mean set
\aff\FMset(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
. Therefore
\mathrm{Ker}\,M^{2}=d
, where

is the dimension of the Fréchet mean set for a sample with face type

. Using
(
5
, Theorem 2.4)
, we see that

\displaystyle\dim U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}

\displaystyle=\dim\proj V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta},

\displaystyle=\dim V_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n},\Theta}-d,

\displaystyle=nk+k-n-\textstyle\sum_{i\in[n]}\dim G_{i}+\dim W-d.

(9)

We now look to express
\dim W
in terms of the polytope
B
. Recall that we have assumed
W
to be non-empty. We will express the dimension of
W
in terms of the

of
G_{1},\dots,G_{n}
which we denote by
P_{G_{1},\dots,G_{n}}
. This is given by

\displaystyle P_{G_{1},\dots,G_{n}}

\displaystyle=\conv\{\mathbf{e}_{i}\times G_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}:i\in[n]\},

\displaystyle=\left\{(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{g})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}:\mathbf{w}\in\Delta_{n-1},\,\mathbf{g}=\textstyle\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}\mathbf{g}_{i},\,\mathbf{g}_{i}\in G_{i}\right\},

where
\Delta_{n-1}
denotes the
n-1
dimensional simplex. By examining this definition, we see that
\mathbf{w}\in W\bigcap\Delta_{n-1}
if and only if
(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{0})\in\relint P_{G_{1},\dots,G_{n}}
. As
W
is a cone in the positive orthant,
W
being non-empty is equivalent to
W\bigcap\Delta_{n-1}=\relint P_{G_{1},\dots,G_{n}}\bigcap(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbf{0})

We once again use
(
5
, Theorem 2.4)
; we now take
M^{1},M^{2},\mathbf{b}
to be such that

\aff P_{G_{1},\dots,G_{n}}=\{(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{g})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}:M^{1}\mathbf{w}+M^{2}\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{b}\}.

Note that
\mathbf{b}-M^{2}\mathbf{0}
is in the image of
M^{1}
as
\relint P_{G_{1},\dots,G_{n}}

, so by

, Theorem 2.4)
:

\displaystyle\dim P_{G_{1},\dots,G_{n}}-\dim\proj_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}(P_{G_{1},\dots,G_{n}})

\displaystyle=\text{Ker}(M^{1}),

\displaystyle=\dim\aff\{\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{0})\in\relint P_{G_{1},\dots,G_{n}}\},

\displaystyle=\dim\left(\relint P_{G_{1},\dots,G_{n}}\textstyle\bigcap(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbf{0})\right)=\dim\left(W\textstyle\bigcap\Delta_{n-1}\right).

(10)

Similarly, for any
\mathbf{w}\in\relint\Delta_{n-1}
, we have that
\mathbf{b}-M^{1}\mathbf{w}
is in the image of

. We pick
\mathbf{w}=(1/n,\dots,1/n)

\displaystyle\dim P_{G_{1},\dots,G_{n}}

\displaystyle=\dim\proj_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(P_{G_{1},\dots,G_{n}})+\text{Ker}(M^{2})

\displaystyle=\dim\Delta_{n-1}+\dim\aff\{\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:((1/n,\dots,1/n),\mathbf{g})\in\relint P_{G_{1},\dots,G_{n}}\}

\displaystyle\dim\proj_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}(P_{G_{1},\dots,G_{n}})

\displaystyle=\dim\left\{\textstyle\sum_{i\in[n]}w_{i}\mathbf{g}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:\mathbf{g}_{i}\in G_{i},\mathbf{w}\in\Delta_{n-1}\right\},

\displaystyle=\dim\conv\left(\textstyle\bigcup_{i\in[n]}G_{i}\right).

Combining the computations above with

10
, we see that

\displaystyle\dim W

\displaystyle=1+\dim(W\textstyle\bigcap\Delta_{n-1}),

\displaystyle=1+\dim P_{G_{1},\dots,G_{n}}-\dim\proj_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}(P_{G_{1},\dots,G_{n}}),

\displaystyle=n+\dim\textstyle\sum_{i\in[n]}\aff G_{i}-\dim\conv\left(\textstyle\bigcup_{i\in[n]}G_{i}\right).

Finally, using

5.13
, we have that

.
Inputting our expressions for

and
\dim W
into

\displaystyle\dim U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}

\displaystyle=nk+k-n-\textstyle\sum_{i\in[n]}\dim G_{i}+\dim W-d,

\displaystyle=nk-\textstyle\sum_{i\in[n]}\dim G_{i}+\dim\sum_{i\in[n]}\aff G_{i}.

Therefore

\textstyle\sum_{i\in[n]}\dim G_{i}=\dim\sum_{i\in[n]}\aff G_{i}

The final claim of

follows from

8
;

.

5.4
Defining Unique Fréchet Mean Sample Thresholds

Theorem

5.10
is the primary theoretical result of this section, explicitly stating the polytope conditions that dictate when a finite sample can have a unique Fréchet mean. The question of whether a sample of size
n
has a positive probability of producing a unique Fréchet mean now equates to whether there is a face type
(F_{1},\dots,F_{n})
that satisfies the conditions in
Theorem

5.10
. From these polytope conditions, it is quick to show that there is some finite sample threshold below which Fréchet means are almost surely not unique, and above which Fréchet means have a positive probability of uniqueness. This is proven by the following corollary.

Corollary 5.14
.

Suppose
U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}
has positive probability, and that
F_{n+1}
is a facet of
B
such that
F_{1}\subseteq F_{n+1}
. Then
U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n+1}}
has positive probability. In particular, if
U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}
has positive probability and induces a unique Fréchet mean, then
U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n+1}}
has positive probability and induces a unique Fréchet mean.

Proof.

We first note that as
F_{1}\subseteq F_{n+1}
, then
G_{n+1}
must be some vertex of
G_{1}
, and so we have that
\conv\bigcup_{i\in[n+1]}G_{i}=\conv\bigcup_{i\in[n]}G_{i}
. Hence:

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}\in\relint\left(\conv\left(\textstyle\bigcup_{i\in[n]}G_{i}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mathbf{0}\in\relint\left(\conv\left(\textstyle\bigcup_{i\in[n+1]}G_{i}\right)\right),

\displaystyle\dim\conv\left(\textstyle\bigcup_{i\in[n]}G_{i}\right)=k

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\dim\conv\left(\textstyle\bigcup_{i\in[n+1]}G_{i}\right)=k.

Additionally, if
F_{i+1}
is a facet, then
G_{i+1}
and
\aff G_{i+1}
are a single point. Therefore:

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{i\in[n+1]}\dim G_{i}

\displaystyle=0+\textstyle\sum_{i\in[n]}\dim G_{i},

\displaystyle=\textstyle\dim\sum_{i\in[n]}\aff G_{i},

\displaystyle=\textstyle\dim\sum_{i\in[n+1]}\aff G_{i}.

We conclude that if
U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}
satisfies
Eqs.

3
,
4
and
5

U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}

U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}
induces a unique Fréchet mean, then so does
U_{F_{1},\dots,F_{n}}
.
∎

As the probability of a unique Fréchet mean converges to one (
Theorem

4.3
), there is some finite sample size which gives a positive probability of a unique Fréchet mean. By
Corollary

5.14
, we can then conclude that for any polytope norm, there is a finite sample threshold
N(B)
such that a sample has a positive probability of producing a unique Fréchet mean if and only if
n\geq N(B)
.

Definition 5.15

(Unique Fréchet mean sample threshold)
.

Fix a polytope norm on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
with ball
B
. The
unique Fréchet mean sample threshold
,
N(B)
, is the smallest
n
such that there is a positive probability of
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
having a unique Fréchet mean.

In the following section, we prove an upper bound for the unique Fréchet mean sample threshold
N(B)
of a general polytope norm, and compute it explicitly for the
\ell_{\infty}
and
\ell_{1}

7
Exact Computation of Fréchet Means

We conclude this paper with a brief note on computation. In order to run numerical experiments on the dimension of a Fréchet set, we must be able to perform exact computations.

We have defined Fréchet means as the solutions of the global minimisation of a non-smooth objective function, but we can re-frame this as the constrained optimisation of a quadratic form.

Lemma 7.1
.

A point
\theta
is in
\FMset(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{n})
if and only if there is some
(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
such that
\theta,\mathbf{d}
give a solution to the following constrained quadratic optimisation problem:

\displaystyle\text{minimise}\quad d_{1}^{2}+\dots+d_{n}^{2},\qquad\text{given}\quad\forall\;i\in[n],\mathbf{a}\in A:\quad\mathbf{a}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}_{i}\leq d_{i}+\mathbf{a}^{\intercal}\theta.

(11)

Proof.

Let
Q
be the polyhedron defined by
Q=\{(\theta,\mathbf{d}):\forall i\in[n],\mathbf{a}\in A,\,\mathbf{a}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\theta)\leq d_{i}\}
. Notice that any
(\theta,\mathbf{d})
in
Q
which minimises
Eq.

11
will be of the form
(\theta,(d(\theta,\mathbf{x}_{i}))_{i\in[n]})
, as otherwise there is some
d_{i}
we can decrease. Hence minimisation of
\|\mathbf{d}\|_{2}^{2}
over
Q
is equivalent to

minimise

\displaystyle\quad d_{1}^{2}+\dots+d_{n}^{2}

given

\displaystyle\quad\forall\;i\in[n],d_{i}=\|\mathbf{x}_{i}-\theta\|_{B}.

This is exactly the optimisation problem of sample Fréchet means.
∎

We have translated the tropical Fréchet mean problem into a quadratic optimisation problem with linear constraints in
\mathbb{R}^{n+k}
. In order to compute the full Fréchet mean set, and in particular to study its dimension, we note that
Lemma

7.1
allows us to write out
\FMset
more explicitly. Let
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{k+n}
be the polyhedron given by
Q=\{(\theta,\mathbf{d}):\forall i\in[n],\mathbf{a}\in A,\,\mathbf{a}^{\intercal}(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\theta)\leq d_{i}\}
, and let
Q^{\prime}
be the projection of
Q
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then the Fréchet mean set
\FMset(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
is given by:

\FMset=\pi_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\pi^{-1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\text{argmin}_{\mathbf{d}\in Q^{\prime}}\|\mathbf{d}\|_{2})

This observation enables us to break down our computation of
\FMset
into three steps:

1.

Compute a representation of
Q^{\prime}
,

2.

Compute the optimal
\mathbf{d}^{\prime}=\text{argmin}_{\mathbf{d}\in Q^{\prime}}\|\mathbf{d}\|_{2}
,

3.

Compute the polytope
\FMset=\pi_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\pi^{-1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbf{d}^{\prime}
.

Step 3 is routine; from
\mathbf{d}^{\prime}
, we can write down the hyperplane representation for
\FMset(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
as

\FMset(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})=\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:\,\forall\mathbf{a}\in A,\,\mathbf{a}^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}_{i}-d_{i}^{\prime}\leq\mathbf{a}^{\intercal}\theta\}.

Step 2 is the most computationally intensive part of this computation, but can be solved exactly using the Frank-Wolfe
(
13
)
algorithm. This algorithm applies iterated linear programming to find a point in a polytope in the positive quadrant with minimal Euclidean norm; this is exactly the problem of computing
\mathbf{d}^{\prime}=\text{argmin}_{\mathbf{d}\in Q}\|\mathbf{d}\|_{2}
. The Frank-Wolfe algorithm avoids having to compute matrix inverses and is known to terminate in
n
iterations, where
n
is the dimension of the ambient space.

Step 1 requires projecting
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+k}
to
Q^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The initial polyhedron
Q
is given in hyperplane form, so to project this to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we perform Fourier–Motzkin elimination
(
26
, Chapter 1)
to compute the hyperplane representation of
Q^{\prime}
from the hyperplane representation of
Q
.

This full procedure is presented in
Algorithm

8
Discussion

In this paper we have shown that the probability of a unique Fréchet mean over a polytope normed space converges to 1, and we have identified geometric conditions which govern the number of sample points needed before Fréchet means are unique with positive probability. In doing so, we were able to define the
unique Fréchet mean sample threshold
of a polytope norm — Fréchet means are almost never unique below this sample threshold, but above this threshold the probability of uniqueness is positive and converging to one. We have shown this threshold to be at most
k+1
, where
k
is the dimension of our space, and computed it exactly for the
\ell_{\infty}
and
\ell_{1}
norms. Finally, we presented an algorithm for exact computation, which can be used to study the probability of unique Fréchet means through simulation.

We have shown an upper bound of
k+1
for the unique Fréchet mean sample threshold of a general polytope norm, which is within one of our
\ell_{\infty}
example. We hope to close this gap as future work, finding a strict upper bound. This will require either finding a polytope example with a unique Fréchet mean sample threshold of
k+1
, or lowering our general upper bound to
k
and showing the
\ell_{\infty}
case to be the worst possible.

Currently, our geometric conditions which dictate the sample threshold for unique Fréchet means (
Theorem

5.10
) do not provide a way of computing the threshold for an arbitrary polytope. At best, one could test every
n
-tuple of faces in
\mathfrak{F}(B^{\Delta})
against the conditions in
Theorem

5.10
for increasing
n
until the necessary conditions are satisfied. It would be of interest to find a more direct method of computation, perhaps utilising the Gale transform of
B^{\Delta}

(
26
, Chapter 6)
, which records which sets of vertices contain
\mathbf{0}
in the relative interior of their convex hull. Relating the sample threshold of
B
to known values in matroid theory would be particularly useful for finding efficient methods of computation for sample thresholds.

The conditions given in
