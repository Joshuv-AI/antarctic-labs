The complexity of finite smooth words over binary alphabets
Julien Cassaigne Raphaël Henry

Abstract
Smooth words over an alphabet of non-negative integers { a , b } \{a,b\} are infinite words that are infinitely derivable, the most famous example being the Oldenburger-Kolakoski word over { 1 , 2 } \{1,2\} . The main way to study their language is to consider a finite version of smooth words that we call f-smooth words. In this paper we prove that the f-smooth words are exactly the factors of smooth words, and we make progress towards the conjecture of Sing that the complexity of f-smooth words over { a , b } \{a,b\} grows like Θ ⁡ ( n log ⁡ ( a + b ) / log ⁡ ( ( a + b ) / 2 ) ) \Theta\left(n^{\log(a+b)/\log((a+b)/2)}\right) : we prove it over even alphabets, we prove the lower bound over any binary alphabet and we improve the known upper bound over odd alphabets.

•

Preliminaries

1.1
Combinatorics on words

1.1.1
Words basics

Definition 1.1
.

A binary alphabet is a set
\mathpzc{A}=\{a,b\}
where
a
and
b
are integers such that
1\leq a<b
. If
a
and
b
are both even (resp. both odd), we say that
\mathpzc{A}
is even (resp. odd). Otherwise, i.e., if
a+b
is odd, we say that
\mathpzc{A}
is mixed.

Given a finite alphabet
\mathpzc{A}
,
\mathpzc{A}^{n}
(resp.
\mathpzc{A}^{*}
) denotes the set of finite words of length
n
(resp. the set of all finite words) over
\mathpzc{A}
, and
\lvert u\rvert
denotes the length of the word
u\in\mathpzc{A}^{*}
. In particular
\varepsilon
is the empty word and we define
\mathpzc{A}^{+}:=\mathpzc{A}^{*}\backslash\{\varepsilon\}
. If words
u,v,w,w^{\prime}\in\mathpzc{A}^{*}
are such that
v=wuw^{\prime}
, we say that
u
is a
factor
of
v
and we write
u\sqsubset v
. Moreover, if
w=\varepsilon
, we say that
u
is a
prefix
of
v
and we write
u\sqsubset_{p}v
. Over a binary alphabet
\mathpzc{A}=\{a,b\}
, the
complement
of a word
u\in\mathpzc{A}^{*}
(resp.
x\in\mathpzc{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
) is the word
\overline{u}
(resp.
\overline{x}
) where every letter
a
is replaced with the letter
b
and vice versa. If
u\in\mathpzc{A}^{*}
and
c\in\mathpzc{A}
, we define
\lvert u\rvert_{c}:=\left\lvert\left\{i\in\left\llbracket 1,\lvert u\rvert\right\rrbracket\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ u_{i}=c\right\}\right\rvert
.

Let
\mathpzc{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
be the set of all right-infinite words over
\mathpzc{A}
, where
0\in\mathbb{N}
. The factors of a word
x\in\mathpzc{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
are the finite words
u
of the form
x_{[i,j)}:=x_{i}x_{i+1}...x_{j-1}
for
0\leq i\leq j
, with the convention that
x_{[i,i)}=\varepsilon
, and we write
u\sqsubset x
. If
u
is of the form
x_{[0,j)}
, we say that
u
is a
prefix
of
x
and we write
u\sqsubset_{p}x
. The word
x\in\mathpzc{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
is
recurrent
if each of its factors occurs infinitely often in
x
.

The
language
of a word
x\in\mathpzc{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
is the set of its factors
{\mathscr{L}(x):=\{u\in\mathpzc{A}^{*}\mid u\sqsubset x\}}
. Similarly, the language of a set
X\subseteq\mathpzc{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
is the set
\mathscr{L}(X):=\displaystyle\cup_{x\in X}\mathscr{L}(x)
.

1.1.2
Factor complexity and bispecial words

Definition 1.2
.

We define the following complexity functions
p:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
.

•

The complexity of a language
\mathscr{L}\subseteq\mathpzc{A}^{*}
is

{p_{\mathscr{L}}(n):=\left\lvert\mathscr{L}\cap\mathpzc{A}^{n}\right\rvert}
,

•

The factor complexity of a word
x\in\mathpzc{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
is
p_{x}(n):=p_{\mathscr{L}(x)}(n)
,

•

The factor complexity of a set of words
X\subseteq\mathpzc{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
is
p_{X}(n):=p_{\mathscr{L}(X)}(n)
.

We use the following notations for the asymptotics of a complexity function.

Definition 1.3
.

Given two functions
p,f:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
, we write

•

p(n)=\mathcal{O}(f(n))
if there exists
C>0
and
N\geq 0
such that
{p(n)\leq Cf(n)}
for all
n\geq N
,

•

p(n)=\Omega(f(n))
if there exists
C>0
and
N\geq 0
such that
{p(n)\geq Cf(n)}
for all
n\geq N
,

•

{p(n)=\Theta(f(n))}
if
{p(n)=\mathcal{O}(f(n))}
and
{p(n)=\Omega(f(n))}
.

Let us recall how to compute the complexity over a binary alphabet using the theory of bispecial factors. We refer to Section 4.5 of
[
5
]
for the method for infinite words, but we state it in terms of languages because this is more convenient for our paper.

First, a language
\mathscr{L}\subseteq\mathpzc{A}^{*}
is
factorial
if
u\sqsubset v\in\mathscr{L}
implies
u\in\mathscr{L}
, and it is
left-extendable
(resp.
right-extendable
) if, for all
u\in\mathscr{L}
, there exists
a\in\mathpzc{A}
such that
au\in\mathscr{L}
(resp.
ua\in\mathscr{L}
).

Definition 1.4
.

Let
\mathpzc{A}=\{a,b\}
be a binary alphabet and let
\mathscr{L}\subseteq\mathpzc{A}^{*}
be a factorial, left- and right-extendable language. A word
u\in\mathscr{L}
is bispecial if
{\{au,bu,ua,ub\}\subseteq\mathscr{L}}
, and in that case its multiplicity is the integer

{m(u):=\left\lvert\{aua,aub,bua,bub\}\cap\mathscr{L}\right\rvert-3\in\{-1,0,1\}}.

We then say that a bispecial factor
u
is weak if
m(u)=-1
, neutral if
m(u)=0
, or strong if
m(u)=1
.

Let
BS(n)
be the set of bispecial factors of
\mathscr{L}
of length
n
. Let
s(n)
be the first finite difference of the factor complexity, i.e.,
s(n)=p_{\mathscr{L}}(n+1)-p_{\mathscr{L}}(n)
, and let
b(n)
be the second finite difference, i.e.,
b(n)=s(n+1)-s(n)
. The seminal result that allows to compute the complexity of the language
\mathscr{L}
is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

[
5
, Theorem 4.5.4]
If
\mathscr{L}
is a factorial, left- and right-extendable language, then for all
n\geq 0
we have

b(n)=\displaystyle\sum_{u\in BS(n)}m(u).

1.2
Infinite and finite smooth words

In
[
7
]
, Dekking initiated the investigation of the factor complexity for the Oldenburger-Kolakoski word
\kappa
. His strategy was to introduce a finite version of smooth words that are expected to be exactly the factors of
\kappa
, we call them
f-smooth
words. In this subsection we introduce the infinite smooth words as a generalization of the word
\kappa
and the f-smooth words over any binary alphabet. We then detail how f-smooth words are related to smooth words and we state the conjecture and results about the complexity of f-smooth words.

1.2.1
Smooth words

Over a binary alphabet
\mathpzc{A}=\{a,b\}
, any word
x\in\mathpzc{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
where the letters
a
and
b
occur infinitely often is canonically factorized as
{x_{0}}^{p_{0}}\overline{x_{0}}^{p_{1}}{x_{0}}^{p_{2}}\overline{x_{0}}^{p_{3}}...
where
x_{0}
is the first letter of
x
and
p_{i}\geq 1
for all
i\geq 0
.

Definition 1.5
.

Over a binary alphabet
\mathpzc{A}
, we say that a word
x\in\mathpzc{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
is derivable if its canonical factorization is
{x_{0}}^{p_{0}}\overline{x_{0}}^{p_{1}}{x_{0}}^{p_{2}}\overline{x_{0}}^{p_{3}}...
with
p_{i}\in\mathpzc{A}
for all
i\geq 0
. We write
\mathcal{C}^{1}
the set of derivable words and we define the derivative as the map

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\penalty\ :\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \mathcal{C}^{1}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathpzc{A}^{\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle{x_{0}}^{p_{0}}\overline{x_{0}}^{p_{1}}{x_{0}}^{p_{2}}...

\displaystyle\longmapsto p_{0}p_{1}p_{2}...

Example 1.1
.

Over
\{1,2\}
, we have
(221)^{\omega}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}
and
\mathcal{D}\left((221)^{\omega}\right)=(21)^{\omega}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}
but
\mathcal{D}\left((21)^{\omega}\right)=1^{\omega}\notin\mathcal{C}^{1}
.

Definition 1.6
.

For
n\geq 2
, we define by induction the set of infinite words that are derivable
n
-times

\mathcal{C}^{n}:=\left\{x\in\mathcal{C}^{1}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \mathcal{D}(x)\in\mathcal{C}^{n-1}\right\}.

We then define the set of smooth words, that is the set of infinite words that are infinitely derivable

\mathcal{C}^{\infty}:=\displaystyle\bigcap_{n\geq 1}\mathcal{C}^{n}.

Example 1.2
.

As a primary example of smooth words, over any binary alphabet
\mathpzc{A}=\{a,b\}
, the map
\mathcal{D}
has two fixed points: one that starts with
a
, denoted by
\kappa_{a,b}
; the other that starts with
b
, denoted by
\kappa_{b,a}
. Here are notable examples, among which is the Oldenburger-Kolakoski word
\kappa_{2,1}
:

\displaystyle\kappa_{2,1}=221121221221121122121121221121121221221121221211211221221121...

\displaystyle\kappa_{3,1}=333111333131333111333133313331113331313331113331333111333133...

\displaystyle\kappa_{2,4}=224422224444224422442222444422224444224422224444224422224444...

\displaystyle\kappa_{2,5}=225522222555552255225522555552222255555222225555522552222255...

Also, we notice that
\kappa_{1,b}=1\kappa_{b,1}
for all

.

Oldenburger originally proved with
[

that

is not eventually periodic, it was proven again by Üçoluk in
[

]
and by Dekking in
[

Proposition 1.1
.

No smooth word is eventually periodic.

1.2.2
f-smooth words

Any binary word
u\in\mathpzc{A}^{*}
is canonically factorized as
a_{1}^{p_{1}}...a_{n}^{p_{n}}
where
a_{i}\in\mathpzc{A}
,
a_{i+1}=\overline{a_{i}}
and
p_{i}\geq 1
, with the convention that
n=0
if
u=\varepsilon
.

Definition 1.7
.

Over a binary alphabet
\mathpzc{A}
, we say that a word
u\in\mathpzc{A}^{*}
is derivable if its canonical factorization is
a_{1}^{p_{1}}...a_{n}^{p_{n}}
with
p_{i}\in\mathpzc{A}
for all
i\in\llbracket 2,n-1\rrbracket
and
{p_{1},p_{n}\in\llbracket 1,b\rrbracket}
. We write
\mathcal{C}^{1}_{f}
the set of finite derivable words and we define the finite derivative as the map

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{f}\penalty\ :\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \mathcal{C}^{1}_{f}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathpzc{A}^{*}

\displaystyle a_{1}^{p_{1}}...a_{n}^{p_{n}}

\displaystyle\longmapsto cut(p_{1})\penalty\ p_{2}\penalty\ ...\penalty\ p_{n-1}\penalty\ cut(p_{n})

where
cut(p)=\begin{cases}\varepsilon\textrm{ if }1\leq p\leq a\\
b\textrm{ if }a<p\leq b\end{cases}
,
\mathcal{D}_{f}(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon
and
{\mathcal{D}_{f}({a_{1}}^{p_{1}})=cut(p_{1})}
.

Example 1.3
.

Over
\{1,2\}
,
\mathcal{C}^{1}_{f}
is the set of words that do not contain the factors
111
or
222
, and we have
\mathcal{D}_{f}(\varepsilon)=\mathcal{D}_{f}(1)=\mathcal{D}_{f}(21)=\varepsilon
and
\mathcal{D}_{f}(2211)=\mathcal{D}_{f}(122112)=22
. Over
\{1,3\}
, we have
\mathcal{D}_{f}(331113)=33
.

Definition 1.8
.

For
n\geq 2
, we define by induction the set of finite words that are derivable
n
times

\mathcal{C}_{f}^{n}:=\left\{u\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{f}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \mathcal{D}_{f}(u)\in\mathcal{C}_{f}^{n-1}\right\}.

We then define the set of f-smooth words, that is the set of finite words that are infinitely derivable

\mathcal{C}_{f}^{\infty}:=\displaystyle\bigcap_{n\geq 1}\mathcal{C}_{f}^{n}.

We notice that the map
\mathcal{D}_{f}
is
contracting
, i.e.,
\left\lvert\mathcal{D}_{f}(u)\right\rvert<\lvert u\rvert
if
{u\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{f}\backslash\{\varepsilon\}}
. This means that a finite word is f-smooth if and only if it can be derived down to
\varepsilon
.

Definition 1.9
.

The height of a f-smooth word
u
is the first integer
n\geq 0
such that
\mathcal{D}_{f}^{n}(u)=\varepsilon
.

Example 1.4
.

Over
\{1,2\}
,
{\mathcal{D}_{f}^{4}(221121221)=\mathcal{D}_{f}^{3}(22112)=\mathcal{D}_{f}^{2}(22)=\mathcal{D}_{f}(2)=\varepsilon}
so
221121221\in\mathcal{C}_{f}^{\infty}
with height
4
, but
12121\notin\mathcal{C}_{f}^{\infty}
because
\mathcal{D}_{f}(12121)=111\notin\mathcal{C}^{1}_{f}
.

One can easily show that the set
\mathcal{C}_{f}^{\infty}
is factorial, left- and right-extendable, we refer to
[
22
, Proposition 2]
for the proof over
\{1,2\}
.

1.2.3
The factor complexity of smooth words

From the definitions of smooth and f-smooth words, we easily notice that
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{C}^{\infty})\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{f}^{\infty}
, which yields
p_{x}(n)\leq p_{\mathcal{C}^{\infty}}(n)\leq p_{\mathcal{C}_{f}^{\infty}}(n)
for all
x\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
. In
[
7
]
, Dekking conjectured that
\mathscr{L}(\kappa_{2,1})=\mathcal{C}_{f}^{\infty}
over
\{1,2\}
, then in
[
21
]
Sing conjectured the same for all
\kappa_{a,b}
over mixed alphabets, and we extend it even further.

Conjecture 1.1
.

Over mixed alphabets, every smooth word
x
satisfies
\mathscr{L}(x)=\mathcal{C}_{f}^{\infty}
, and in particular
p_{x}(n)=p_{\mathcal{C}^{\infty}}(n)=p_{\mathcal{C}_{f}^{\infty}}(n)
.

This is notably linked to the recurrence of smooth words.

Remark 1.1
.

If
1.1
holds, then the language of every smooth word over a mixed alphabet is complement-invariant and reversal-invariant, where the reversal of a word
u_{1}...u_{n}
is
u_{n}...u_{1}
. In particular, reversal-invariance implies recurrence.

However,
1.1
cannot be extended to even or odd alphabets. Over even alphabets, let us cite
[
2
, Proposition 26]
where the authors show that the language of the lexicographic extremal smooth words is neither mirror-invariant nor complement-invariant, contrary to
\mathcal{C}_{f}^{\infty}
. Over odd alphabets, there are even more pathological smooth words that miss a lot of f-smooth words.

Example 1.5
.

Over the odd alphabet
\mathpzc{A}=\{1,b\}
, there exists a pair of smooth words that are the derivative of each other, and they do not contain the f-smooth word
bb
. For example, over
\{1,3\}
, they are

\displaystyle 1113111313111311131311131311131113131113111313111313111311131311131...

\displaystyle 3131113131113111313111313111311131311131113131113131113111313111313...

This suggests that, over even or odd alphabets, studying the set
\mathcal{C}_{f}^{\infty}
is not the way to describe the factor complexity of single smooth words. Regardless, we will show in
Theorem

1.5
that
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{C}^{\infty})=\mathcal{C}_{f}^{\infty}
, which yields
p_{\mathcal{C}^{\infty}}(n)=p_{\mathcal{C}_{f}^{\infty}}(n)
and cements the importance of the set
\mathcal{C}_{f}^{\infty}
over every binary alphabet.

1.2.4
The complexity of f-smooth words

With the previous observations and conjectures, the complexity
p_{\mathcal{C}_{f}^{\infty}}(n)
has naturally been thoroughly investigated. The main conjecture was originally formulated over the alphabet
\{1,2\}
in
[
7
]
and later generalized to every binary alphabet in
[
21
]
.

Conjecture 1.2
.

Over
\mathpzc{A}=\{a,b\}
, we have
p_{\mathcal{C}_{f}^{\infty}}(n)=\Theta(n^{\rho})
where
\rho=\frac{\log(a+b)}{\log\left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right)}
.

Remark 1.2
.

1.1
and
1.2
together would imply that no smooth word over a mixed alphabet is morphic, since Devyatov’s result
[
9
]
states that the factor complexity of morphic words can only grow like
\Theta(n^{1+1/k})
for some
k\geq 1
or like
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
. To be precise: we have
\rho=1+1/k
if and only if
a+b=2^{k+1}
, which cannot happen when
\mathpzc{A}=\{a,b\}
is mixed.

A first attempt to study
p_{\mathcal{C}_{f}^{\infty}}(n)
was made by Dekking over
\{1,2\}
in
[
7
]
and later generalized by Sing to every binary alphabet in
[
20
]
.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
20
, Proposition 1 and 3]
)
.

Over a binary alphabet
\mathpzc{A}=\{a,b\}
, there exist
N\geq 0
and
C>0
such that, for all
n>N
, we have

Cn^{\alpha}\leq p_{\mathcal{C}_{f}^{\infty}}(n)\leq n^{\beta}

where
\alpha=\frac{\log(a+b)}{\log\left(\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}}{a+b}\right)}
and
\beta=\frac{\log\left(2b^{2}\right)}{\log\left(\frac{2ab}{a+b}\right)}
.

While this does not prove
1.2
, it still provides the first polynomial bounds for any binary alphabet. This implies zero entropy for all smooth words, where the
entropy
of a word
x\in\mathpzc{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
is the finite quantity
\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\log(p_{x}(n))/n
.

Corollary 1.1
.

Every smooth word has zero entropy.

In
[
22
]
, Weakley took another approach by studying the bispecial f-smooth words over
\{1,2\}
.

Theorem 1.3

(
[
22
, Corollary 9]
)
.

Over
\{1,2\}
, let
\rho=\log(3)/\log(3/2)
. Then there exists two constants

such that

C_{1}n^{\rho}\leq p_{\mathcal{C}_{f}^{\infty}}(n)\leq C_{2}n^{\rho}

for each

for some

, where
l_{i}
(resp.

) is the minimum (resp. maximum) length of bispecial f-smooth words of height
i
.

This result led Weakley to ask when

holds, computations suggest that it holds for all

but no one was able to show it. Later, Weakley and Huang brought the letter frequency into the mix and obtained another result that supports
1.2
.

Theorem 1.4

(
[
10
, Theorem 3]
)
.

Over
\{1,2\}

\phi<1/2
is a positive real number and

for all

such that, for all

where

and
\gamma=\frac{log(3)}{\log(3/2-\phi)}
.

Remark 1.3
.

Keane conjectured in
[

that
\kappa_{2,1}
has equal frequencies of
1
and
2
. In fact we make the stronger conjecture that, over any even or mixed alphabet, the letter frequencies in f-smooth words are asymptotically

when combined with

1.4

described in the introduction has frequencies of

Theorem

can be combined with the computations of

Corollary 1.2
.

Over
\{1,2\}

\rho=\log(3)/\log(3/2)

Factors of smooth words

In this section we prove
Theorem

1.5
. To do so, we introduce a right version of f-smooth words called r-smooth words and originally introduced in
[
3
]
, which will play the role of prefixes in smooth words.

2.1
r-smooth words

Definition 2.1
.

Over a binary alphabet
\mathpzc{A}
, we say that a word
u\in\mathpzc{A}^{*}
is r-derivable if it is factorized as
a_{1}^{p_{1}}...a_{n}^{p_{n}}
where
p_{i}\in\mathpzc{A}
for all
i\in\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket
and
p_{n}\in\llbracket 1,b\rrbracket
. We write
\mathcal{C}^{1}_{r}
the set of r-derivable words and we define the r-derivative as the map

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{r}\penalty\ :\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \mathcal{C}^{1}_{r}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathpzc{A}^{*}

\displaystyle a_{1}^{p_{1}}...a_{n}^{p_{n}}

\displaystyle\longmapsto p_{1}\penalty\ ...\penalty\ p_{n-1}\penalty\ cut(p_{n})

where the function
cut
is the same as in
\mathcal{D}_{f}
and
\mathcal{D}_{r}(a_{1}^{p_{1}})=cut(p_{1})
.

Example 2.1
.

Over
\{1,2\}
, we have
\mathcal{D}_{r}(\varepsilon)=\mathcal{D}_{r}(2)=\varepsilon
,
\mathcal{D}_{r}(21)=1
and
\mathcal{D}_{r}(211)=12
.

Definition 2.2
.

For
n\geq 2
, we define by induction the set of finite words that are r-derivable
n
times

\mathcal{C}_{r}^{n}:=\left\{u\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{r}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \mathcal{D}_{r}(x)\in\mathcal{C}_{r}^{n-1}\right\}.

We then define the set of r-smooth words, that is the set of finite words that are infinitely r-derivable

\mathcal{C}_{r}^{\infty}:=\displaystyle\bigcap_{n\geq 1}\mathcal{C}_{r}^{n}.

Similarly to
\mathcal{C}_{f}^{\infty}
, one can easily prove by induction that
u\sqsubset_{p}v\in\mathcal{C}_{r}^{\infty}
implies
u\in\mathcal{C}_{r}^{\infty}
, and that the set
\mathcal{C}_{r}^{\infty}
is right-extendable.

2.2
Prefixes and factors of smooth words

Let us prove
Theorem

1.5
using the fact that
\mathcal{C}_{r}^{\infty}
is the set of prefixes of smooth words.

Proof of
Theorem

1.5
.

Let
\mathpzc{A}=\{a,b\}
be a binary alphabet. We already observed that
\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{C}^{\infty})\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{f}^{\infty}
, so it remains to prove that
\mathcal{C}_{f}^{\infty}\subseteq\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{C}^{\infty})
.

The first step is to show that every f-smooth word is factor of an r-smooth word. For that we are going to show that, for all
u\in\mathcal{C}_{f}^{\infty}
, there exists
v\in\mathpzc{A}^{*}
such that
\lvert v\rvert\geq a+b
and
vu\in\mathcal{C}_{r}^{\infty}
. We proceed by induction on the height of
u
: first, if
u
has height
0
, i.e.,
u=\varepsilon
, then the word
v=a^{b}b^{a}
suffices. Now let
k\geq 0
be such that, for all
u\in\mathcal{C}_{f}^{\infty}
of height
k
, there exists
v\in\mathpzc{A}^{*}
such that
\lvert v\rvert\geq a+b
and
vu\in\mathcal{C}_{r}^{\infty}
. Let
u\in\mathcal{C}_{f}^{\infty}
be of height
k+1
and consider its canonical factorization
u=a_{1}^{p_{1}}...a_{n}^{p_{n}}
with
n\geq 1
. By hypothesis, there exists
v\in\mathpzc{A}^{*}
such that
\lvert v\rvert\geq a+b
and
v\mathcal{D}_{f}(u)\in\mathcal{C}_{r}^{\infty}
, and we can write
v=v_{1}...v_{l}
with
l\geq a+b
. In particular,
v\in\mathcal{C}_{r}^{\infty}
and it is long enough to contain at least
a
ocurrences of the letter
b
. We deduce that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{l}v_{i}=a\left\lvert v\right\rvert_{a}+b\lvert v\rvert_{b}=a\left(l-\lvert v\rvert_{b}\right)+b\lvert v\rvert_{b}=al+(b-a)\lvert v\rvert_{b}\geq a(a+b)+(b-a)a\geq(a+b)+a.

If
p_{1}\leq a
, we have
\mathcal{D}_{f}(u)=p_{2}...p_{k-1}cut(p_{k})
and we set
w:=c_{1}^{v_{1}}...c_{l}^{v_{l}}c_{l+1}^{p_{2}}...c_{l+k-1}^{p_{k}}
where
c_{i+1}=\overline{c_{i}}
and
c_{l+1}=a_{2}
. We first observe that
w=v^{\prime}u
where
{v^{\prime}:=c_{1}^{v_{1}}...c_{l-1}^{v_{l-1}}c_{l}^{v_{l}-p_{1}}}
and that
\left\lvert v^{\prime}\right\rvert=\left(\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{l}v_{i}\right)-p_{1}\geq(a+b+a)-a=a+b
. We also observe that
w\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{r}
and that
\mathcal{D}_{r}(w)=v\mathcal{D}_{f}(u)
, which implies that
w=v^{\prime}u\in\mathcal{C}_{r}^{\infty}
.

If
p_{1}>a
, we have
\mathcal{D}_{f}(u)=bp_{2}...p_{k-1}cut(p_{k})
and we set
w:=c_{1}^{v_{1}}...c_{l}^{v_{l}}c_{l+1}^{b}c_{l+2}^{p_{2}}...c_{l+k}^{p_{k}}
where
c_{i+1}=\overline{c_{i}}
and
c_{l+1}=a_{1}
. We first observe that
w=v^{\prime}u
where
{v^{\prime}:=c_{1}^{v_{1}}...c_{l}^{v_{l}}c_{l+1}^{b-p_{1}}}
and that
\left\lvert v^{\prime}\right\rvert\geq\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{l}v_{i}\geq a+b+a
. We also observe that
w\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{r}
and that
\mathcal{D}_{r}(w)=v\mathcal{D}_{f}(u)
, which implies that
w=v^{\prime}u\in\mathcal{C}_{r}^{\infty}
.

The second step is to show that a word
x\in\mathpzc{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that all its prefixes are r-smooth is smooth. We show by induction on
n
that, for all
n\geq 1
, a word
x\in\mathpzc{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that all its prefixes are r-smooth satisfies
x\in\mathcal{C}^{n}
. If
n=1
, we easily notice that an infinite word such that all its prefixes are r-smooth is derivable. Now let
n\geq 1
be such that a word
x\in\mathpzc{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that all its prefixes are r-smooth satisfies
x\in\mathcal{C}^{n}
, and let
x\in\mathpzc{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
be such that all its prefixes are r-smooth. In particular, the words
\mathcal{D}_{r}(x_{[0,bn[})
are r-smooth for all
n\geq 0
and they form a sequence of prefixes of strictly increasing length of the word
\mathcal{D}(x)
. We deduce that all prefixes of
\mathcal{D}(x)
are r-smooth, and by hypothesis we have
\mathcal{D}(x)\in\mathcal{C}^{n}
so
x\in\mathcal{C}^{n+1}
.

The third step is to show that every r-smooth word is prefix of a smooth word. If
v
is r-smooth, the fact that the language
\mathcal{C}_{r}^{\infty}
is right-extendable provides a sequence of r-smooth words of strictly increasing length, starting with
v
, which are all prefixes of an infinite word
x\in\mathpzc{A}^{\mathbb{N}}
. By construction, all prefixes of
x
are r-smooth so
x
is smooth and
v\sqsubset_{p}x
.

Finally, if
u\in\mathcal{C}_{f}^{\infty}
, we proved that there exists
v\in\mathcal{C}_{r}^{\infty}
such that
u\sqsubset v
and that there exists
x\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
such that
v\sqsubset_{p}x
, so we have
u\sqsubset x
. Therefore, we have
\mathcal{C}_{f}^{\infty}\subseteq\mathscr{L}(\mathcal{C}^{\infty})
.
∎

Bispecial f-smooth words

In this section we detail how to compute

from the bispecial f-smooth words, extending the work of Weakley
[

3.1
Finite primitives

In order to determine the bispecial f-smooth words, we introduce the notion of finite primitives. This starts with the observation that, if
u\in\mathpzc{A}^{n}
, the two longest finite words with derivative
u
are canonically factorized as
{a_{0}^{a}a_{1}^{u_{1}}a_{2}^{u_{2}}...a_{n}^{u_{n}}a_{n+1}^{a}}
.

Definition 3.1
.

Over a binary alphabet
\mathpzc{A}=\{a,b\}
, the f-primitives are the maps

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{f,a}\penalty\ :\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \mathpzc{A}^{*}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathpzc{A}^{+}

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{f,b}\penalty\ :\mathpzc{A}^{*}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathpzc{A}^{+}

\displaystyle u_{1}...u_{n}

\displaystyle\longmapsto\begin{cases}a^{a}b^{u_{1}}a^{u_{2}}...a^{u_{n}}b^{a}\textrm{ if }n\textrm{ is even,}\\
a^{a}b^{u_{1}}a^{u_{2}}...b^{u_{n}}a^{a}\textrm{ else.}\end{cases}

{p_{\mathcal{C}_{f}^{\infty}}(n)\geq c\left[1-\frac{1}{a+b-1}\right]n^{\rho}}
for all
n\geq 1
.
∎

4.2
An upper bound from
l_{i}

We show here that a lower bound of
l_{i}
provides an upper bound of
p_{\mathcal{C}_{f}^{\infty}}(n)
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\mathpzc{A}=\{a,b\}
be a binary alphabet and let for all
i\geq 0
. If there exists
\lambda>0
,
C>0
and
D\geq 0
such that
l_{i}\geq C\lambda^{i}-D-1
for all
i\geq 0
, then we have

p_{\mathcal{C}_{f}^{\infty}}(n)=\mathcal{O}(n^{\zeta})\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \textrm{ where }\penalty\ \penalty\ \zeta=\frac{\log(2\lambda)}{\log(\lambda)}.

Proof.

With the notation introduced in
Definition

3.4
, we have
s_{i}(n)=0
if
n\leq l_{i}
and
s_{i}(n)\leq\lvert T_{i}\rvert=2^{i}
otherwise, therefore
p_{i}(n)\leq\max\left(0,2^{i}(n-l_{i}-1)\right)
for all
i\geq 0
. Now, for
n\geq\max(0,C-D)
, the set
I_{n}:=\{i\geq 0\mid n-l_{i}-1\geq 0\}
satisfies
I_{n}\subseteq\llbracket 0,j\rrbracket
where
j:=\left\lfloor\frac{\log(m)}{\log(\lambda)}\right\rfloor
and
m:=\frac{n+D}{C}\geq 1
, and we get

p(n)=\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}p_{i}(n)\leq\sum_{i\in I_{n}}\left(n-l_{i}-1\right)2^{i}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{j}n2^{i}=n2^{j+1}-n.

Then, for all
n\geq D
, we have
j\leq\frac{\log(m)}{\log(\lambda)}
and
m\leq\frac{2n}{C}
so

p(n)\leq 2n2^{\frac{\log(m)}{\log(\lambda)}}-n=2nm^{\frac{\log(2)}{\log(\lambda)}}-n\leq 2\left(\frac{2}{C}\right)^{\frac{\log(2)}{\log(\lambda)}}n^{\zeta}-n.

Finally,
Proposition

3.1
yields
{p_{\mathcal{C}_{f}^{\infty}}(n)\leq 6\left(\frac{2}{C}\right)^{\frac{\log(2)}{\log(\lambda)}}n^{\zeta}-2n+1\leq 6\left(\frac{2}{C}\right)^{\frac{\log(2)}{\log(\lambda)}}n^{\zeta}}
for all
{n\geq\max(1,C-D,D)}
.
∎

Now it remains to find the best lower bound of
l_{i}
.

4.3
The upper bound over even alphabets

Over even alphabets,
l_{i}
is easily computable and we deduce the conjectured upper bound of
p_{\mathcal{C}_{f}^{\infty}}(n)
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathpzc{A}=\{a,b\}
be an even alphabet and let
c:=\frac{4a}{a+b-2}
. Then we have
l_{i}=c\left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right)^{i}-c
for all
i\geq 0
.

Proof.

Because
a
and
b
are even, a quick induction shows that
\lvert u\rvert_{a}=\lvert u\rvert_{b}=\frac{\lvert u\rvert}{2}
for all
u\in T
. We deduce that every word in
T
has even length, and we showed with
Equation

6
that the average length of words in
T_{i}
is
c(a+b)^{i}-c2^{i}
so we deduce that
l_{i}=L_{i}=c\left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right)^{i}-c
for all
i\geq 0
.
∎

Finally, combining
Theorem

1.6
(i),
Proposition

4.1
and
Lemma

4.1
yields
Theorem

1.6
(ii).

Remark 4.1
.

Finding an upper bound of
p_{\mathcal{C}_{f}^{\infty}}(n)
only by studying
l_{i}
led us to prove
1.2
over even alphabets but appears to be unsufficient over odd alphabets. This shows in two ways.

Firstly, over even alphabets
l_{i}
grows like
\left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right)^{i}
, but over odd alphabets it grows only like
\lambda^{i}
so
Proposition

4.1
cannot provide a better upper bound than
n^{\zeta}
.

Secondly, over even alphabets we have
l_{i}=L_{i}
so we immediatly get
L_{i}\leq l_{i+1}
for all
i\geq 0
as suggested by Weakley in
[
22
]
. However, this inequality fails over odd alphabets since
L_{i}
has a greater growth rate than
l_{i}
: in the same way we proved that
l_{i}
grows like
\lambda^{i}
, one can prove that
L_{i}
grows like
r^{i}
where
r
is the spectral radius of
MPM
where
M
and
P
are defined in the last proof. For example, over
\{1,3\}
we have
L_{i}>l_{i+1}
for all
i\geq 5
, starting with
L_{5}=86>l_{6}=64
.
