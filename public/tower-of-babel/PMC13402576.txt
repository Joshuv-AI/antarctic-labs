Cross-modal mapping of cancer stem-like cell plasticity using deep learning
Authors: Debojyoti Chowdhury, Shreyansh Priyadarshi, Sayan Biswas, Bhavesh Neekhra, Debayan Gupta, Shubhasis Haldar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCancer stem-like cells (CSCs) play a pivotal role in driving tumor heterogeneity, therapeutic resistance, and disease progression. Despite the power of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to resolve intratumoral hierarchies, there remains a need for robust, scalable tools to consistently profile CSCs across both single-cell and bulk transcriptomic data. To address this, we developed ACSCeND—a unified, machine learning–based framework that enables high-resolution CSC state classification and tissue-level deconvolution. ACSCeND comprises (i) a supervised classifier trained on curated scRNA-seq datasets to assign cells into pluripotent-like, multipotent-like, or unipotent-like states, and (ii) an attention-guided autoencoder that deconvolves CSC subtype proportions from bulk RNA sequencing data. Compared to existing tissue deconvolution tools, ACSCeND achieves superior performance, with higher accuracy across synthetic and real-world samples. Applied to over 25 000 tumor profiles from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), PREdiction of Clinical Outcomes from Genomics (PRECOG), tumor-relapse, and checkpoint inhibitor studies, ACSCeND reveals that CSC abundance strongly correlates with poor disease-free survival and reduced immunotherapy efficacy. Moreover, it uncovers distinct CSC-state-specific molecular programs, offering insights into CSC-driven heterogeneity and tumor evolution. The model also recapitulates known developmental hierarchies in noncancerous tissues, supporting its broader biological relevance. By integrating single-cell precision with bulk-level applicability, ACSCeND offers a robust, interpretable approach to profiling CSC dynamics and establishes CSC state as a clinically meaningful, pan-cancer biomarker for guiding stemness-informed therapies. ACSCeND is available as a python package (through pip) at https://pypi.org/project/ACSCeND/.

Introduction

Cancer progression is fundamentally driven by the capacity of tumor cells to acquire and switch between diverse phenotypic states—a property broadly referred to as cellular plasticity [1–3]. This plasticity enables malignant cells to dynamically adapt to microenvironmental pressures, evade immune surveillance, resist treatment, and seed distant metastases. A growing body of evidence points to cancer stem-like cells (CSCs) as central architects of this phenotypic diversity. Defined by their ability to self-renew and differentiate into multiple tumor lineages, CSCs occupy the apex of cellular hierarchies in many cancers [4]. These cells are implicated in the initiation, maintenance, and recurrence of tumors [5], and are thought to underlie the failure of conventional therapies by sustaining a reservoir of treatment-refractory cells [6, 7]. CSCs are not static entities, but highly plastic populations whose identity and potency are shaped by complex cues from the tumor microenvironment (TME), including immune, stromal, and metabolic signals [8–10]. Recent conceptual models liken the organization of malignant ecosystems to developmental hierarchies, in which CSCs mirror embryonic or tissue-resident stem cells, giving rise to diverse progenitor and differentiated states through branching, context-specific trajectories [11, 12]. Each state can come up with unique transcriptional profiles and behaviors corresponding to the TME [13], thereby creating significant diversity or transcriptional heterogeneity within the tumor itself. Understanding these dynamic hierarchies and the molecular programs that govern CSC identity corresponding to the TME, remains one of the most urgent challenges in cancer biology, with direct implications for therapy resistance, tumor evolution, and relapse.

Recent advances in large-scale and diverse scRNAseq data have provided valuable insights into the complexity of cancer, uncovering the intricate landscape of different cell states and their transitions within tumors. While scRNAseq-based ranking methods provide invaluable insights, their application can be constrained by the high costs and technical challenges associated with the approach. Additionally, the results often depend on the chosen annotation methods, which might limit the capture of the full diversity of cell types present in a tumor [9, 14]. On the other hand, bulkRNAseq-based approaches, offer an accessible alternative for measuring tumor stemness but lack the resolution to identify distinct cell populations and their interactions within the TME [15]. Trajectory inference methods might be used to arrange single cells in a developmental-trajectory like sequence that is dependent on the specific dataset [16–19]. As a result, the cells considered to be the least differentiated in one dataset might exhibit a potency level comparable to the most differentiated cells in another, complicating direct comparisons across datasets. While current deconvolution techniques [20–23] can be used to analyze bulkRNAseq data using a signature matrix derived from different stem cell states, they will struggle to differentiate between CSCs and the developmental plasticity of other tumor-associated cells. Furthermore, these methods often struggle to predict cell fractions when rare or randomly distributed cells are involved, which can limit their effectiveness in enumerating CSCs. While each method has its strengths, these challenges underscore the need for more adaptable and precise tools that integrate insights from both scRNAseq and bulkRNAseq to effectively capture the intricate cellular heterogeneity of tumors.

To address this limitation, we developed ACSCeND (AI-based CSC profiler and Neoplasm Deconvoluter), a dual-platform machine-learning framework for high-resolution characterization of CSC states. ACSCeND integrates a supervised single-cell classifier that stratifies cells into pluripotent-like, multipotent-like, and unipotent-like CSC states with an attention-guided deconvolution engine that reconstructs CSC composition from bulk transcriptomes. By linking single-cell–defined potency hierarchies to bulk tumor datasets, ACSCeND enables systematic characterization of CSC states across cancers and experimental platforms. Using large-scale single-cell and bulk transcriptomic cohorts, we show that CSC potency states define distinct molecular programs and are associated with clinical outcome and therapeutic response. Together, ACSCeND provides a scalable framework for resolving CSC heterogeneity and its clinical consequences.

Materials and methods

Single-cell RNA sequencing data acquisition and quality control

The single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) gene expression matrix for various cancer types was sourced from the publicly available Weizmann Institute’s 3CA dataset (provide Supplemetary Table). Initial preprocessing and quality control were conducted using the Seurat package (v5.1.0) in R. Cells expressing fewer than 200 genes or >10 000 genes, as well as cells with a mitochondrial gene fraction exceeding 10%, were excluded from downstream analyses to remove low-quality cells. Suspected doublets were identified and filtered using the DoubletFinder package (v2.0) in R [24]. The optimal pK (neighborhood size) parameter was determined by iterating through potential values using the paramSweep function, followed by summarization and selection of the maximum BCmetric from the output of the find.pK function. Homotypic doublets were accounted for by adjusting the expected doublet rate (nExp_poi.adj) based on the proportion of homotypic cells. Doublets were then removed before proceeding to further analysis.

Data integration and dimensionality reduction

After the initial quality control, the gene expression matrix was normalized using the Seurat package (v5.1.0) in R [25]. Normalization was performed with the NormalizeData function, utilizing the LogNormalize method and a scale factor of 10 000 to standardize gene expression levels across all cells. To identify genes with the highest variability across cells, the FindVariableFeatures function was applied using the variance-stabilizing transformation method, and the top 2000 highly variable genes were selected. The dataset was subsequently scaled using the ScaleData function, which standardizes the expression of each gene across all cells. Principal component analysis was then conducted using the RunPCA function on the identified variable genes to get the 20 principle components (PCs). To address potential batch effects arising from technical variations across different experimental conditions, batch correction was performed using the RunHarmony function of the Harmony package. FindNeighbors function is then utilized to construct the nearest neighbor graph and subsequent clustering of cells into distinct groups was performed using the Louvain algorithm implemented in Seurat’s FindClusters function, with a resolution parameter of 0.3.

Cell type annotation and cluster identification

Cell type annotations for malignant cells, fibroblasts, epithelial cells, and endothelial cells were preserved as provided in the original dataset. For the annotation of immune cell populations, the SCINA package [26] was utilized, leveraging marker genes specified in the LM22 matrix by Newman et al. [27]. Myeloid-derived suppressor cell (MDSC) marker genes were selected from Alshetaiwi et al. [28] The annotation of CSCs involved a multi-step manual approach (see Supplementary Methods). First, malignant cells were isolated based on the original annotations and subjected to clustering using the Louvain algorithm, as implemented in Seurat. We measured the transcriptional entropy [single-cell energy conversion efficiency of neural transcription (SCENT) score] of each cell as the quantitative assessment of cancer stemness [29]. SCENT estimate the differentiation potency of a cell by calculating the entropy of its transcriptomic signaling network, where higher entropy typically denotes higher developmental potential. The cluster with highest stemness score was considered as CSC cluster [30]. The assigned clusters were then visualized using violin plots to assess the expression levels of curated CSC markers overall as well as specific to each cancer type (tissue of origin). For each cluster, the expression patterns of these markers were compared to identify those with elevated levels, indicative of a potential stem cell phenotype (Supplementary Table S2).

The classifier module

Experimentally annotated datasets were aggregated from multiple publicly available sources for training our classifier model. To ensure the consistency and quality of the data, we calculated the SCENT score (a representation of stemness in terms of transcriptional entropy) for all samples. Specifically, SCENT estimates the differentiation potency of a cell by calculating the entropy of its transcriptomic signaling network, where higher entropy typically denotes higher developmental potential. The SCENT score was utilized to smoothen the dataset by removing any samples whose original annotations were not in alignment with the score. This process ensured that the data used for model training reflected accurate biological classifications. Following this refinement, a class imbalance was observed in the data, where multipotent-like samples constituted approximately eight times the number of pluripotent-like and unipotent-like samples. To address this, rather than discarding the data, we employed an ensemble learning strategy. Specifically, eight distinct ensemble models were trained. These models shared common pluripotent-like and unipotent-like samples, but each incorporated a unique subset of multipotent-like samples, thereby balancing the data across the different models. This EasyEnsemble-like approach ensured that the classifier captured the full biological diversity of the multipotent state without suffering from majority-class bias. Before model training, the entire dataset was converted to rank space. This approach assigned relative ranks to gene expression levels, thereby removing batch effects, mitigating the influence of extreme values and outliers, and reducing the risk of overfitting. The rank space conversion also normalized gene expression differences between samples, facilitating robust downstream analysis. Subsequently, the rank-transformed data were log2-transformed and standardized to z-scores.

To further ensure robustness and prevent overfitting, we did not employ traditional k-fold cross-validation, which can introduce bias when biological replicates are present in both the training and testing sets, leading to the issue of double-dipping. Instead, our models were tested on completely independent datasets collected from entirely different sources. This strategy allowed for an unbiased evaluation of model performance, ensuring that the trained models could generalize effectively to novel data. We did not perform SCENT-based smoothening on the test data prior to prediction, maintaining the integrity of the unseen test data for accurate evaluation. We developed five different models, and for subsequent analysis, we selected the one that demonstrated the highest accuracy on the independent test dataset. CSC classification and validation

The annotated CSCs were classified into three distinct subtypes using the previously established classification model. To validate the robustness of this classification, we computed the SCENT score for each annotated sample. Box plot visualizations (refer that box plot) demonstrated the distribution of SCENT scores across the identified subtypes, further supporting the classification results.To gain insights into the developmental trajectories of these CSCs, pseudotime analysis was conducted using the Monocle3 package. This analysis models the progression of cells along a pseudo-temporal axis, reflecting their differentiation status. The resulting pseudotime values were segmented into three distinct clusters via K-means clustering, capturing the major developmental pathways of CSCs. Samples exhibiting concordance between their assigned stem cell subtype and pseudotime cluster were prioritized, ensuring consistency and robustness for downstream deconvolution analyses. Following the comprehensive annotation of CSCs, stromal cells, and immune cells, a balanced dataset for deconvolution was prepared. For each cell type, a minimum of 1000 cells was retained where available, to ensure statistical robustness. To mitigate batch effects across the final dataset, the pycombat_seq function from the inmoose package was employed, achieving uniformity across samples and facilitating accurate deconvolution analysis.

The deconvolution engine

Data preparation

The datasets used in this study include raw count data for scRNAseq, pseudobulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), and real bulk RNA-seq. The scRNAseq data provides the expression of individual cells, annotated with corresponding cell types in a separate file. The pseudobulk RNA-seq data is generated by aggregating scRNAseq profiles from multiple cells, simulating bulk RNA-seq profiles. Additionally, ground truth cell fractions corresponding to each pseudobulk profile are provided, representing the true proportion of each cell type in the simulated bulk samples. The cell types were mapped using a cell annotation file. Each dataset consists of raw counts for gene expression, with rows representing genes and columns representing either individual cells (for scRNAseq) or pseudobulk samples. The final dataset used for training and validation includes the pseudobulk expression profiles as features and the ground truth cell fractions as labels.

Signature matrix generation

To enhance sensitivity and stability in resolving low-abundance cell populations, we incorporated an scRNA-seq-derived signature matrix as a biological prior in the ACSCeND framework. For each tissue and analysis context, signature matrices were generated directly from the corresponding scRNA-seq datasets to preserve tissue specificity. Cells were first annotated into major cell types and CSC potency states, after which differential gene expression analysis was performed for each cell type/state against all others within the same tissue. Genes showing robust and specific expression patterns were selected and assembled into a gene-by–cell-type/state signature matrix, which was then used during model training and validation to guide classification and deconvolution. Importantly, signature matrices were derived independently for each tissue and dataset to avoid cross-context information leakage (Supplementary Table S4).

Performance evaluation

To rigorously evaluate the robustness and predictive accuracy of our deconvolution model, we employed a comprehensive validation framework comprising three distinct strategies: pseudo-bulk data validation, cross-dataset generalization, and real-world bulk RNA-seq dataset testing. In the first approach, pseudo-bulk datasets were generated from four diverse sources, including PBMC 20k, developmental datasets, malignant datasets, and mouse tissue datasets. Each dataset was partitioned into training and testing subsets in a 50:50 ratio to ensure balanced evaluation. This strategy assessed the model’s capacity to accurately infer cell-type proportions under controlled conditions. The second approach tested cross-dataset generalization by training the model on pseudo-bulk data derived from one dataset and validating its performance on a distinct dataset. For instance, pseudo-bulk data from PBMC 10k were used for training, while PBMC 20k served as the validation dataset. Similarly, pancreas data from Segerstople et al. were used for training, and pseudo-bulk data from Xin et al. were employed for validation. To assess performance in real-world scenarios, the third approach applied the model to bulk RNA-seq datasets with paired cell fraction annotations, including SYD67, Monaco et al. and Newman et al. These datasets provided experimentally determined ground-truth cell-type proportions, enabling precise evaluation of the model’s predictions. Across all datasets, performance metrics such as the CCC and RMSE were computed, and the results were benchmarked against state-of-the-art deconvolution tools such as TAPE, SCADEN, CIBERSORTx, DWLS, Bisque, and EPIC.

To further investigate robustness, we applied the model to brain cancer pseudo-bulk datasets generated under diverse conditions. These included 2000 samples with randomized cell-type frequencies and 200 samples with uniformly distributed frequencies. Additionally, we evaluated model performance in scenarios with missing cell types by generating 200 samples excluding malignant cell fractions and another 200 samples excluding nonmalignant cell fractions. The impact of varying cell numbers in pseudo-bulk samples was systematically examined by increasing constituent cell numbers from 30 to 300 and then to 900 cells. This variation enabled us to assess the model’s sensitivity to sample composition. Finally, the model was applied to melanoma bulk RNAseq data from Racle et al., which included six annotated cell types with established ground truth. Model predictions were compared using metrics such as the Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC), CCC, and RMSE with performance evaluated against leading deconvolution methods. This comprehensive evaluation framework, incorporating both synthetic and real-world datasets, established the accuracy, generalizability, and robustness of our model across diverse biological contexts.

For deconvolution performance on pseudo-bulk and real bulk datasets with known ground truth, we benchmarked TAPE, Scaden, CIBERSORTx, DWLS, EPIC, and Bisque. Clinical data and analysis

We retrieved expression and survival data (n = 6402) from the PRECOG database across nine cancer types. and obtained gene expression data (n = 7020) from TCGA [31] for cancers including KIRC, COADREAD, OV, SKCM, PAAD, KIRP, GBM, KICH, CHOL, PRAD, HNSC, LIHC, LUAD, BRCA, LUSC, and SARC through the Broad GDAC repository. These cancer types were selected because they were used to train the deconvolution model. Corresponding clinical data for TCGA cancers were sourced from cBioPortal [32] to facilitate clinical analysis.

We performed an in-depth evaluation of pluripotent-like, multipotent-like, and unipotent-like stem cell frequencies across various clinical and pathological categories within the TCGA dataset. These included AJCC pathologic tumor stage (n = 4879), pathological M stage (PATH_M_STAGE; n = 4707), and tumor grade (GRADE; n = 1841). This combined analysis of TCGA cancers provides insights into stem cell frequency variations across critical clinical and pathological parameters.

Survival analysis and survival impact score

A Cox proportional hazards analysis was performed using lifeline package to assess the association of pluripotent-like, multipotent-like, and unipotent-like cell frequencies with overall survival (OS). This analysis aimed to estimate the extent to which the hierarchical stem cell states influenced patient risk.

For survival association calculation, the hazard values for individual genes were derived by leveraging the Cox proportional hazards model, as implemented in the lifelines Python package. Specifically, the CoxPHFitter function was utilized to model the relationship between gene expression levels and OS outcomes, incorporating both survival time and censoring status for each patient. For each gene, a separate univariate Cox regression analysis was performed to estimate its hazard ratio, quantifying the extent to which the gene’s expression level influenced patient risk. A hazard ratio >1 indicates an increased risk of adverse survival outcomes with higher expression of the gene, while a ratio <1 suggests a protective effect. These hazard ratios were subsequently log-transformed to enable downstream aggregation and analysis, facilitating the integration of individual gene-level effects into a coherent framework for evaluating cumulative survival risks. It allowed us to systematically assign hazard scores to genes, which were then used as inputs for quantifying the collective impact of gene expression patterns on patient survival outcomes within specific cell types.

Building on the gene-specific hazard values, we developed a survival impact score (SIS) to quantify the contribution of cell-type-specific hazards to patient survival. To compute SIS, the proportional abundances of cell types in each patient were first normalized, ensuring the fractions accurately reflected relative cell-type compositions. For a given cell type, its SIS was calculated as the product of its normalized fraction and the aggregated hazard values of its associated genes, where the aggregated hazard was determined by summing the pre-assigned hazard values across all genes expressed within the cell type. This methodology resulted in a patient-cell-type matrix of SIS values, effectively capturing the cumulative risk contributions of specific cell types. To further understand the relationship between these cell-type-specific contributions and OS, Pearson correlation analysis was employed. This involved calculating correlation coefficients to assess the strength and direction of association between the SIS for each cell type and patient survival times. Statistical significance was evaluated for each correlation, with P-values log-transformed to enhance interpretability and identify meaningful associations. It enabled the identification of cell types that were significantly associated with survival outcomes, linking the cumulative risk contributions of cell-type-specific gene expression to overall patient risk profiles in a mechanistically insightful manner.

Analysis of immune–CSC interactions

We analyzed immune cell frequencies computed by a deconvolution model across the TCGA (The Cancer Genome Atlas)and PRECOG datasets, focusing on their relationship with stem cell frequencies. For each cancer type, we calculated the PCC between the frequencies of multipotent-like, pluripotent-like, and unipotent-like stem cells and 33 immune cell types. To investigate the relationship between stem cell frequency and immunoinhibitory gene expression, we utilized genes associated with immunoinhibition identified by Lu et al. [33]. The PCC was computed between the expression levels of these genes and stem cell frequencies across the merged TCGA and PRECOG datasets, providing insights into stem cell-mediated immune modulation.

To assess the impact of immunotherapy on CSC frequencies, we analyzed datasets from metastatic melanoma patients treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), including those from Hugo et al. [34] and Riaz et al. [35]. Stem cell frequencies were compared before and after treatment, enabling us to evaluate potential therapeutic effects on CSC dynamics in response to immunotherapy.

Statistics and reproducibility

Determining the appropriate sample size for the deconvolution analysis is challenging, as no established methods currently exist. Therefore, we selected the sample size based on previously published datasets to demonstrate the effectiveness and performance of our method. No data were omitted from the analysis. Since no experimental data were collected, replication and randomization are not applicable. Statistical methods used for hypothesis testing are described in each figure legend. Group allocations were based on information from previously published datasets and were not modified by us. To replicate the findings, please refer to the Source Data file provided.

Downstream statistical analyses were conducted using common Python packages, specifically versions 3.11.6 or 3.12.0. and R packages for version R 4.3.1. The figures were generated using Python packages: matplotlib, seaborn, and pandas. Pseudobulk data were generated using R-package SimBu. Additional packages like anndata and tqdm were used for developing our method.

Results

Single-cell-informed classification and deconvolution of cancer stem-like cell potency states by ACSCeND

CSCs represent a functionally distinct tumor subpopulation characterized by self-renewal, tumor-initiating potential, and adaptive plasticity within the TME, yet they exist as heterogeneous populations shaped by genetic, epigenetic and microenvironmental influences [36, 37]. To resolve this heterogeneity, we developed ACSCeND (AI-based Cancer Stem-like Cell profiler and Neoplasm Deconvoluter ), a computational framework for classification and deconvolution of CSC potency states across transcriptomic platforms. ACSCeND models CSCs along a developmental continuum from pluripotent-like states with extensive self-renewal capacity to progressively restricted multipotent-like and unipotent-like states associated with differentiation and reduced tumor-initiating potential (Fig. 1A), linking CSC transcriptional diversity to developmental hierarchies [9, 15, 38–41]. For model development and evaluation, we assembled a compendium of scRNA-seq datasets spanning multiple tissues and platforms, partitioned into classifier training, deconvolution training and independent validation cohorts (Fig. 1B).

Within tumor scRNA-seq datasets, all cells were uniformly reannotated into immune, stromal and malignant compartments to minimize cross-lineage contamination and improve resolution of malignant transcriptional programs [42]. CSC populations can be identified within the malignant compartment using stemness-based clustering, lineage-marker validation and independent scoring metrics. To stratify CSCs into developmental states, we trained a supervised ensemble classifier using ∼40 000 curated stem cells derived from eight independent reference datasets [43–50]. The classifier integrates predictions from multiple base learners using a majority-voting scheme (Fig. 1C) and assigns individual cells to pluripotent-like, multipotent-like or unipotent-like states, producing consistent potency-state annotations across tumor types and sequencing technologies.

To enable translation of single-cell-defined CSC states to bulk transcriptomic datasets, ACSCeND incorporates a deep learning–based deconvolution module based on a latent encoder–decoder architecture. The model compresses bulk expression profiles into a latent representation capturing biologically informative gene expression structure, while the decoder reconstructs sample-level fractions of pluripotent-, multipotent- and unipotent-like CSC states (Fig. 1C). Integration of an scRNA-seq-derived signature matrix as a biological prior improved sensitivity for low-abundance CSC populations and enhanced stability across datasets.

Application of ACSCeND enables CSC potency-state quantification across both single-cell and bulk transcriptomic modalities. The classification module is optimized for scRNA-seq datasets, whereas the encoder–decoder module enables estimation of CSC-state fractions in bulk RNA-seq samples. In matched datasets, both modules can be applied simultaneously to enable direct comparison between single-cell and bulk-derived potency-state estimates (Fig. 1D). The resulting potency-state profiles provide a quantitative basis for downstream analyses including pathway enrichment, cancer hallmark associations, survival modeling and therapy-response prediction (Fig. 1C).

Robust and reproducible classification of stem cell potency states by ACSCeND

To construct a biologically grounded classifier of stem cell potency states, we assembled a reference compendium of normal stem cell populations spanning a wide range of developmental potential (Fig. 2A) [9, 15, 38–41]. To ensure robust classification across heterogeneous datasets, we implemented an ensemble learning strategy in which multiple models trained on independent datasets were combined using a majority-voting scheme (Fig. 2B). Because the training data contained unequal representation of potency states, we evaluated with five baseline machine-learning algorithms (Supplementary Fig. S1) in different alternative strategies including direct training on imbalanced data, ensemble learning and SMOTE-based resampling (Supplementary Fig. S10). Logistic regression consistently showed the best performance and stability and was therefore used as the base learner for the final ensemble model. The training and validation compendium comprised stem cell populations from multiple studies with substantial variation in cell number and potency-state representation (Fig. 2C), providing a stringent test of classifier robustness [45, 51–53].

Evaluation on independent validation datasets demonstrated near-perfect classification accuracy across potency states. The confusion matrix showed minimal misclassification between pluripotent-like, multipotent-like, and unipotent-like cells, while receiver operating characteristic (ROC) and precision–recall (PR) analyses indicated strong discrimination across all classes, with precision–recall area under the curve (AUC) of 1.000 for pluripotent-like and unipotent-like cells and 0.992 for multipotent-like cells (Fig. 2D). These results indicate that ACSCeND accurately captures transcriptional features distinguishing stem cell potency states despite dataset heterogeneity.

We next compared ACSCeND with existing stemness inference methods. Both CytoTrace and CytoTrace2 showed substantially higher misclassification rates on the same validation datasets, particularly for intermediate potency states (Fig. 2E and F). In contrast, ACSCeND maintained clear separation between potency classes, indicating improved classification accuracy relative to current approaches. At the single-cell level, ACSCeND predictions closely matched developmental annotations in a large human fetal dataset. UMAP (Uniform manifold approximation and projection) projections showed distinct spatial organization of pluripotent-like, multipotent-like, and unipotent-like populations consistent with ground truth labels, whereas CytoTrace [9] and CytoTrace2 [54] produced broader and less consistent distributions across developmental states (Fig. 2G). These results demonstrate that ACSCeND preserves developmental structure while providing discrete potency assignments.

Cross-species validation further supported the generalizability of the classifier. Application to an independent mouse developmental dataset [55] identified potency-state transitions consistent with known differentiation stages, with early embryonic stem cells enriched for pluripotent-like states and later progenitors enriched for lineage-restricted states (Fig. 2H). Independent validation using SCENT [56] entropy scores showed a progressive decrease in differentiation potential from pluripotent-like to unipotent-like states, confirming the biological consistency of ACSCeND predictions.

Finally, pseudotime analysis across multiple tumor types revealed ordered transitions between potency states consistent with differentiation trajectories. Pluripotent-like CSCs were enriched at early pseudotime positions, followed by multipotent-like and unipotent-like states at later stages, supporting a hierarchical organization of CSC potency across cancers (Fig. 2I).

Recovery of cancer stem-like cell potency states at single-cell resolution across tumor types

To construct a unified single-cell reference of CSC states across tumor types, we compiled scRNA-seq data from 49 studies encompassing ∼2.4 million cells across 12 solid cancers from 3CA atlas [57]. Cells were uniformly reannotated into immune, stromal and malignant compartments to reduce inconsistencies arising from heterogeneous annotation strategies (Supplementary Methods; Supplementary Figs S21–S33). Across datasets, the TME comprised 57.3% immune cells, 27.0% malignant cells, and 15.7% stromal cells, with CSCs representing a subset of the malignant compartment (Fig. 3A). For each cancer type, malignant clusters with the highest stemness were identified by co-expression of canonical CSC markers following CNV-based malignant-cell detection, and ACSCeND was then used to classify CSCs into pluripotent-like, multipotent-like, and unipotent-like states (Fig. 3B). ACSCeND revealed substantial variation in CSC potency-state composition across cancers, with multipotent-like CSCs predominating in most tumor types and pluripotent-like and unipotent-like states showing cancer-specific enrichment patterns (Fig. 3C).

To evaluate the biological consistency of predicted potency states, we compared ACSCeND classifications with independent stemness metrics. SCENT [56] scores showed a progressive increase from unipotent-like to pluripotent-like CSCs across all cancer types, consistent with increasing differentiation potential (Fig. 3D). Similar trends were observed using scEntropy [58]-based stemness estimates, further supporting concordance between ACSCeND predictions and orthogonal measures of cellular plasticity (Fig. 3E).

We next examined the relationship between predicted potency states and differentiation trajectories. Pseudotime analysis revealed ordered transitions between CSC states consistent with progressive differentiation, and smoothing of pseudotime-derived labels reduced local inconsistencies and outlier assignments (Fig. 3F) ensuring that our model captures biologically meaningful CSC state transitions. UMAP projections showed consistent representation of immune, stromal and malignant populations across all 12 tumor datasets, demonstrating the diversity of cell states included in the reference atlas (Fig. 3G).

Finally, we validated CSC identification and potency-state classification using differential marker gene expression analysis. Comparison with curated marker genes from CellMarker 2.0 [59] confirmed clear separation between CSCs and nonstem malignant cells and revealed distinct transcriptional signatures among potency states (Fig. 3H). CSC markers were preferentially enriched in ACSCeND-defined CSC populations, whereas nonstem malignant markers were depleted. Several stemness-associated genes, including KRT8, KRT17, and KRT19, showed progressive overexpression with increasing potency state, consistent with their reported roles in cancer stemness [60–62] and metastasis [63].

Construction and Validation of ACSCeND deconvolution engine

To enable accurate inference of cellular composition from bulk transcriptomes, we developed a biologically constrained deep-learning deconvolution framework based on an attention-guided encoder–decoder architecture trained on scRNA-seq-derived pseudobulk mixtures with known cell-type fractions and signature matrices (Supplementary Fig. S2). Systematic optimization of architectural and training parameters improved prediction accuracy (Supplementary Figs S11–S16), while composite loss functions minimized discrepancies between predicted and true cell fractions (Supplementary Figs S17–S20 and Supplementary Table S1). Model performance was evaluated using held-out pseudobulk mixtures, independent synthetic datasets, and real bulk transcriptomes with matched FACS (fluorescence activated cell sorting)- or IHC (immunohistochemistry)-derived fractions, demonstrating robust and generalizable accuracy.

First, we partitioned pseudobulk data from four diverse datasets—PBMC 20k (Supplementary Fig. S3), developmental datasets (Supplementary Fig. S4) [64], malignant datasets (Supplementary Fig. S5) [65], and Tabula Muris datasets (Supplementary Fig. S6) [66]—into independent training (50%) and testing (50%) subsets. Across all datasets, our model consistently achieved higher CCC and lower RMSE compared to state-of-the-art deconvolution methods, including TAPE [21], SCADEN [22], CIBERSORTx [20], DWLS [67], Bisque [68], and EPIC [23] (Supplementary Figs S3–S6). These results highlight the superior predictive accuracy and reliability of our approach over existing methodologies.

In the second validation strategy, we evaluated cross-dataset generalization by training the model on pseudobulk data from one source (e.g. PBMC 10k) and validating it on a distinct dataset (e.g. PBMC 20k) (Supplementary Fig. S7). Similarly, training on pancreas data created from Baron et al. [69] and validating on pseudobulk data created from Xin et al. [70] demonstrated superior CCC and RMSE compared to benchmark methods (Supplementary Fig. S7).

Finally, we validated on real-world bulk RNAseq datasets with paired cell fraction data (SYD67 data, [71] Linsley et al. data [72], Monaco et al. data, [73] and Newman et al. [20] data). Our model outperformed existing methods in estimating cell-type proportions in the SYD67, Linsley et al., and Monaco et al. datasets, and performed comparably to SCADEN and TAPE on the Newman et al. dataset (Supplementary Fig. S8), demonstrating robust performance across heterogeneous real-world data.

Following validation of both the classifier and deconvolution modules, we evaluated performance on synthetic and tumor-derived datasets using the brain cancer subset of the 3CA data. Across 2000 pseudobulk samples with random cell-type frequencies, the model achieved a CCC of 0.91 and RMSE of 0.01 (Fig. 3C), whereas evenly distributed mixtures (n = 200) showed lower concordance (CCC = 0.76; RMSE = 0.01; Fig. 4A).

To assess robustness to missing cell populations, we generated pseudobulk samples excluding malignant cells. The model correctly identified remaining cell types, with reduced accuracy when multiple populations were absent (Fig. 4B). Increasing cell numbers substantially improved performance, with CCC rising from 0.27 (30 cells) to 0.84 (300 cells) and 0.91 (900 cells), while RMSE remained stable at 0.01 (Fig. 4C and D).

Validation using real-world melanoma bulk RNA-seq data from Racle et al. [74] (n = 4), which included six ground truth cell types, yielded a correlation coefficient of 0.91 between predicted and true cell fractions (P-value = 1.158 × 10−9, Fig. 4E). Importantly, our model demonstrated superior cell type-specific CCC (0.58 ± 0.39) and lower RMSE (0.07 ± 0.04) compared to some available deconvolution methods, indicating a significantly better performance (Fig. 4F). Furthermore, we also evaluated ACSCeND across different sample sizes and hardware configurations, demonstrating consistently lower prediction times and competitive scalability compared to other methods. The results show that ACSCeND maintains efficient performance on both GPU- and CPU-based systems, making it suitable for large-scale prediction tasks. (Supplementary Fig. S9).

Prediction of CSC dynamics at clinical settings

We applied ACSCeND on two large pan-cancer cohorts, TCGA [75] (n = 7020) and PRECOG [76] (n = 6402), to explore CSC dynamics across diverse cancer types and stages (Fig. 5A and B). Analysis of CSC states revealed a hierarchical pattern of pluripotent-like > multipotent-like > unipotent-like cells across most cancer types, consistent with the increasing plasticity and adaptability required for tumor progression (Fig. 5C) [77, 78]. Notably, some of the cancers such as liver cancer deviated from this trend, exhibiting a predominance of unipotent-like cells. Median CSC frequencies revealed distinct trends, with pluripotent-like and multipotent-like CSCs showing a multiphasic and cyclic trend across AJCC tumor stages, whereas unipotent-like CSCs demonstrated an inverse pattern of the other two states, declining in advanced stages (Fig. 5D). Accordingly, we found that ACSCenD predicted CSC frequencies in different tissues are like that found from experimental validations in multiple literatures (Supplementary Table S3).

In alignment with metastatic staging, pluripotent-like and multipotent-like CSCs exhibited a biphasic trend—peaking at the M1A stage, which reflects metastasis to a single distant site, followed by a decline at M1B and M1C stages, characterized by multiple and widespread metastases (Fig. 5E). This dynamic suggests a critical role for highly plastic CSCs in early metastatic colonization, where adaptability is essential for successful seeding of new sites. Their subsequent reduction may reflect a shift toward more niche-adapted or lineage-committed populations as metastatic lesions mature. These patterns support the seed-and-soil hypothesis [79], wherein early metastatic success is driven by pluripotent- or multipotent-like, highly adaptable “seeds” that interact favorably with receptive microenvironmental “soil,” but give way to more specialized populations as disease burden increases. Tumor grade analysis also revealed a sharp increase in CSC frequencies, particularly pluripotent-like cells, with higher grades, reflecting their involvement in driving tumor aggression and adaptability (Fig. 5F).

Survival analysis across TCGA and PRECOG cohorts in terms of hazard ratio highlighted higher potency CSCs as the most prognostically adverse population, consistent to previous findings (Fig. 5G and H) [9, 14]. We calculated SIS for each CSC state across cancer types and found that pluripotent-like CSCs are more hazardous compared to multipotent-like CSCs followed by unipotent-like CSCs in both TCGA (Fig. 5G) and PRECOG patients (Fig. 5H).

To understand the role of CSCs in tumor relapse, we applied ACSCeND to paired pre-treatment and post-relapse bulk RNAseq data from Tirosh et al. (n = 6) [65]. Post-relapse tumors showed significant enrichment of pluripotent-like CSCs compared to pre-treatment samples, indicating a selective pressure for highly plastic states during therapy (Fig. 5I). Interestingly, the amount of post-relapse CSCs was found to be proportional to the pre-treatment CSC load, suggesting that initial CSC burden influences the degree of CSC enrichment post-relapse.

Model-derived gene weights identified the top 1000 overexpressed and underexpressed genes for each state, revealing distinct transcriptional identities and candidate markers (Fig. 6A). Pseudotemporal ordering from pluripotent-like to unipotent-like CSCs identified genes with progressively decreasing expression, including validated CSC markers associated with potency loss (Fig. 6B).

We further prioritized a subset of genes whose expression patterns suggest functional switching during CSC transitions. Specifically, we identified the top 10 most significantly differentially expressed genes that may act as molecular regulators of transitions from high to lower potency states (Fig. 6C). These include components of key signaling axes such as WNT, Notch, and FOXO4, which are well-established mediators of self-renewal, lineage commitment, and CSC plasticity. These genes may serve as candidate “molecular switches” governing the balance between stemness and differentiation within tumor hierarchies. To biologically validate the three CSC potency states inferred by ACSCeND, we examined the switch genes that showed the strongest changes across potency transitions (Fig. 5C). These genes were not dominated by generic cell-type markers but were enriched for transcription factors, chromatin and transcriptional regulators, and signaling or ubiquitin-associated nodes, indicating that the states are driven by core regulatory mechanisms of stem-cell fate. The pluripotent-like state was characterized by regulators associated with stem-cell quiescence, transcriptional control, and developmental potency. The multipotent-like state showed enrichment of genes balancing self-renewal, proliferative competence, and cellular plasticity, consistent with an intermediate progenitor-like program. In contrast, the unipotent-like state was associated with regulators linked to lineage commitment, epithelial identity, and differentiation. Together, this gene-level analysis demonstrates that the CSC potency states identified by ACSCeND reflect biologically meaningful stemness hierarchies rather than superficial marker overlap, providing independent validation of the inferred stem-cell state structure.

Pathway enrichment analysis across multiple databases revealed state-specific functional programs (Fig. 6D). Pluripotent-like CSCs were enriched for MAPK, glycolysis, and WNT signaling pathways, multipotent-like CSCs for metabolic and transcriptional regulation programs including RUNX2 signaling, and unipotent-like CSCs for RTK signaling and cancer-associated pathways like mechanical signaling [80], consistent with progressive restriction of developmental potential.

Hallmark pathway activity across stem cell potency states was quantified using the OncoMark [81] framework, revealing a systematic enrichment of oncogenic programs in higher-potency CSC states (Fig. 6E). Pluripotent-like CSCs showed consistently high scores across all ten hallmark categories, including evasion of immune destruction, genome instability, proliferative signaling and invasion-associated programs like epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), indicating a globally activated oncogenic phenotype. Multipotent-like CSCs exhibited intermediate hallmark activity, whereas unipotent-like CSCs showed substantially reduced scores across most hallmark categories. In contrast, nonstem malignant cells displayed minimal hallmark enrichment. These results demonstrate a progressive decline in hallmark pathway activity with decreasing CSC potency, linking developmental potential with oncogenic program activation.

Given the well-established role of CSCs in driving therapeutic resistance and disease recurrence, we investigated the enrichment of molecular programs associated with chemoresistance (Fig. 6F) [82] and cellular quiescence across CSC potency states (Fig. 6G) [83–85]. Both chemoresistance- and quiescence-associated programs were active across CSC subtypes but followed a clear potency-linked gradient, with strongest enrichment in pluripotent-like CSCs. These cells showed elevated DNA damage response and repair pathways together with stress-survival programs, consistent with an enhanced ability to withstand genotoxic stress and evade therapy-induced apoptosis. Quiescence-associated programs were likewise most enriched in pluripotent-like CSCs and declined in multipotent- and unipotent-like states, highlighting pluripotent-like CSCs as a likely reservoir of therapy-resistant disease.

Panorama of CSC-associated immune infiltrates

Intracellular genetic changes and extracellular microenvironment modulation might aid CSCs evade immune detection and foster an anti-inflammatory, protumorigenic immune environment that supports cancer progression [86]. Therefore, we thought to analyze critical associations between CSC frequencies, immune cell populations, and immunosuppressive genes to reveal key insights into tumor heterogeneity and potential vulnerabilities for immunotherapeutic interventions.

Correlation analysis of immune cell frequencies and CSC frequencies revealed distinct relationships across both TCGA and PRECOG cohorts (Fig. 7A and B). Immune populations such as MDSCs, M2 macrophages, and resting dendritic cells showed positive correlations with CSC frequencies, whereas cytotoxic immune populations, including CD8+ T cells and natural killer cells, exhibited weaker or negative correlations. These findings support the previously hypothesized notion that CSC-rich tumors are characterized by an immune-suppressive landscape, potentially driven by Tregs and tolerogenic dendritic cells, which may shield CSCs from immune-mediated elimination [87].

Further analysis revealed significant associations between CSC frequencies and immunosuppressive gene expression (Fig. 7C). Immune checkpoint genes showed positive correlations with CSC abundance, together with immune-modulatory genes, implicating immune evasion pathways in CSC maintenance. In contrast, negatively correlated genes may represent potential regulators that restrict CSC expansion. Consistent with available literature, our analysis also suggests that therapies targeting TGF-β or CSF1R [88], or immunotherapies with γδ-Tcells [89] could attenuate the immunosuppressive microenvironment, reducing CSC frequencies and their ability to evade immune surveillance.

To investigate the association between CSC states and immunotherapy outcomes, we analyzed publicly available transcriptomic datasets profiling treatment response and resistance. Using data from Hugo et al. [34], which includes longitudinal sampling of MAPK inhibitor (MAPKi)- and ICI-resistant melanoma, we tracked CSC frequency dynamics across defined treatment stages (baseline, DP1–DP3) (Fig. 7D). CSCs—particularly pluripotent-like and multipotent-like subsets—remained consistently elevated or increased throughout the course of therapy in resistant tumors, suggesting that persistent stem-like phenotypes may underlie or contribute to therapeutic resistance. Conversely, analysis of Riaz et al. [35] data (n = 44), cohort (n = 44), comprising pre- and post-treatment samples from patients receiving anti-PD-1 therapy (nivolumab), revealed a response-specific divergence in CSC abundance (Fig. 7E). Patients with progressive disease retained high CSC frequencies after treatment, especially within the pluripotent- and multipotent-like compartments. In responders, however, CSC frequencies declined markedly across all stemness states, including the unipotent-like population. These observations implicate CSC frequency as a potential functional correlate and biomarker of therapeutic response, with elevated stem-like phenotypes marking resistance and their depletion accompanying clinical benefit.

Discussions

Our study introduces ACSCeND as a framework for resolving functional heterogeneity of CSCs across transcriptomic modalities and tumor types. By enabling robust identification of CSC potency states from both single-cell and bulk RNA-seq data, ACSCeND addresses a key translational limitation in applying stem cell biology to large patient cohorts. Existing stemness metrics often collapse cellular diversity into continuous scores that do not distinguish functionally distinct CSC states. In contrast, ACSCeND stratifies CSCs into biologically interpretable potency states reflecting developmental hierarchies and linking stemness to tumor progression, immune evasion and therapeutic resistance. By bridging the resolution of single-cell analysis with the scale of bulk transcriptomics, ACSCeND provides a framework for translating cellular plasticity into clinically relevant metrics and disease prognosis.

CSC potency states defined by ACSCeND were associated with distinct oncogenic programs. Pluripotent-like CSCs, for instance, are enriched for WNT/β-catenin signaling [90], hypoxia adaptation [91], glycolysis [92], and DNA repair [93] pathways previously implicated in chemoresistance, metastatic initiation, and immune evasion. These findings resonate with prior studies showing that hypoxia-driven WNT activation sustains CSC self-renewal in colorectal and breast cancers, and that DNA repair programs in CSCs contribute to therapeutic tolerance [94]. Notably, ACSCeND distinguishes these pathways as subtype-specific rather than general markers of stemness, revealing that not all CSCs are equally quiescent, immunosuppressive, or resistant. This refined resolution allows a deeper understanding of the functional roles CSC subsets play during disease progression.

The association between CSC heterogeneity and immune dysfunction extends beyond the mere presence of stem-like cells. Our results suggest that specific CSC subtypes—particularly those with pluripotent-like features—may actively shape the immune landscape through transcriptional programs linked to immunosuppression. The observed depletion of T and B cells, as well as the reduction of tertiary lymphoid structures in tumors enriched for this subtype, echoes known effects of WNT/β-catenin signaling, which has been implicated in exclusion of effector immune cells and resistance to immune checkpoint therapies [95, 96]. ACSCeND refines the concept of tumor stemness by identifying a distinct CSC compartment as the principal driver of immune disruption rather than generalized stemness. This suggests that CSCs not only evade immune clearance but actively shape immunosuppressive niches within the TME. Consistently, pluripotent-like CSCs were enriched in nonresponders to immune checkpoint blockade and depleted in responders, indicating a potential role as a barrier to immunotherapy efficacy and a candidate biomarker for treatment response.

While ACSCeND provides a robust framework to quantify CSC fractions and potency states and links these states to biological and clinical phenotypes, important questions remain. Whether CSC persistence under therapy is driven primarily by core stemness programs or by additional context-specific mechanisms cannot be resolved with current datasets. Defining predictive molecular signatures of therapy response or relapse, and generalizing stemness–resistance relationships across treatments, will require larger longitudinal cohorts. ACSCeND therefore provides a quantitative foundation for such future investigations.

Nevertheless, despite all the advances, some limitations remain. While the model resolves major potency states with high accuracy, intermediate or transitional CSC phenotypes may still evade detection, particularly if underrepresented in the training data. Increasing the diversity of single-cell training datasets may improve sensitivity to rare or transitional CSC states. Similarly, integrating spatial transcriptomic data would enable finer contextualization of CSC niches, potentially revealing how subtype distributions vary across tumor regions and microenvironments. Moreover, it is important to be noted that, it was technically impossible to separate nonmalignant tissue-stem cells from CSCs in deconvolution. Despite, we achieved that advantage in ACSCeND compared to other current methods, for bulkRNAseq, it remains a challenge.

In conclusion, ACSCeND enables a new level of resolution in CSC profiling by combining biological interpretability with computational versatility. Its ability to function across both single-cell and bulk RNA-seq data, coupled with its capacity to resolve clinically meaningful CSC subtypes, positions it as a critical tool for both mechanistic investigation and translational application. By linking CSC hierarchy to molecular pathways, immune response, and patient prognosis, ACSCeND lays the groundwork for more nuanced models of tumor progression and for precision strategies that target the most resilient cellular components of cancer.
