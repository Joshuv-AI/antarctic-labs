Dyslipidemia in Lebanese children and adolescents: parents’ knowledge and practices
Authors: Khalil Hamadeh, Mariana Boulos, Bachir Atallah, Maya Bou Hamdan, Dina Maddah, Nada Ghorayeb, Marie-Hélène Gannagé Yared
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPediatric dyslipidemia is a common metabolic disorder worldwide, especially in Lebanon, and is an early risk factor for cardiovascular disease. Parents play a crucial role in prevention, screening, and management, but their knowledge and practices remain poorly studied.MethodsA cross-sectional questionnaire-based study was conducted between March and November 2025 among parents of school-aged children from eight schools in Greater Beirut and Mount Lebanon. An electronic questionnaire was used to collect sociodemographic data and to assess parents’ knowledge (using a composite score) and practices regarding dyslipidemia. Factors associated with knowledge levels and their relationship with parental practices were also examined.ResultsA total of 946 parents were included (861 women and 85 men) with a mean age of 43.9 ± 6.2 years, 94.1% had a university education, and 11.6% were healthcare professionals. Among them, 42.4% had heard of pediatric dyslipidemia, mainly through the media (25.2%) and healthcare professionals (24.4%). The mean knowledge score was 14.1 ± 4.4 out of 23. Blood test-based lipid screening for dyslipidemia in children had been performed by only 6.7% of parents. The independent determinants of a higher knowledge score were female gender, university education, healthcare professional status, and having heard of pediatric dyslipidemia (p < 0.001, p = 0.007, p < 0.001, p < 0.001, respectively). In addition, higher knowledge scores were significantly associated with favorable preventive practices, including implementation of healthy eating habits (p = 0.02) and lipid screening in children (p < 0.001).ConclusionParents demonstrated moderate knowledge levels. The association between knowledge level and the adoption of favorable preventive measures suggests that educational campaigns targeting parents and health care professionals could play a role in promoting healthier practices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28254-8.Keywords: Dyslipidemia, Children, Adolescents, Lebanese, Parents, KPA

Introduction

Dyslipidemia is a common metabolic disorder characterized by high levels of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high triglyceride (TG) levels, low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) levels, and/or high non-HDL-C cholesterol levels [1]. It can be primary, associated with genetic abnormalities, mainly represented by familial hypercholesterolemia [2], or secondary to medical conditions ( e.g., obesity, diabetes, and hypothyroidism) or medication use [3].

In the United States (US), approximately one in five children and adolescents has dyslipidemia with abnormal total cholesterol (TC), HDL-C, or non-HDL-C levels [4]. This prevalence increases with age, with 15.2% of children aged 6 to 11 years and 25.2% of adolescents aged 12 to 19 years having at least one adverse lipid level [5]. In the first Lebanese study, the prevalences of high non-HDL-C levels (> 3.8 mmol/L), very high non-HDL-C levels (> 4.9 mmol/L), and high TG levels among schoolchildren were respectively 9.2%, 1.24%, and 26.6% [6]. In another study performed on Lebanese children aged between 2 and 10 years, the prevalences of high TC, non-HDL-C, LDL-C, TG, and low HDL-C levels were 19.5%, 23%, 19%, 31.8%, and 20%, respectively [7].

The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI), supported by the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP), recommends universal lipid screening between the ages of 9 and 11 years, then again between 17 and 21 years [8]. It also advocates targeted screening starting from the age of 2 years in children with risk factors, such as a family history of premature cardiovascular disease (CVD) or dyslipidemia, obesity, hypertension, or diabetes [8]. This screening aims to identify children at increased risk of developing CVD in adulthood [9], as abnormal lipid levels during childhood are associated with a higher risk of developing atherosclerosis and CVD in early adulthood and midlife [10, 11]. On the other hand, the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) and the American College of Family Physicians believe that there is insufficient evidence to assess the benefit-to-risk ratio of screening for familial dyslipidemia in children and adolescents under 20 years of age [12]. Therefore, even if current guidelines provide overall consistent recommendations for the management of childhood dyslipidemias, there is a lack of consensus regarding universal screening for early detection of lipid disorders [13].

Numerous studies have assessed adults’ knowledge and awareness of dyslipidemia [14–18]. These studies were conducted in the US [14], Europe [15], Korea [16], China [17], and Saudi Arabia [18]. Across all settings, patient awareness of dyslipidemia was generally low, ranging from approximately 15% in China [17] and Luxembourg [15] to 21% in the US (the Bogalusa Heart Study) [14], and reaching 58.5% in a Korean study [16]. Older age [14, 15], a higher educational level [14], positive family history of coronary artery disease [14], and higher body mass index [15] were significantly associated with greater awareness of dyslipidemia.

However, parents’ knowledge and practices regarding dyslipidemia in children have been poorly studied, with a limited group of subjects included in the studies. In a US study performed on 91 parents, 45.5% were in favor of universal screening, and 48.4% preferred targeted screening based on the child’s personal and family history [19]. In a second Irish study, 98% of 53 parents of hospitalized children agreed to have their children screened [20]. To our knowledge, no other study has assessed parents’ knowledge and attitudes regarding pediatric dyslipidemia. Because children depend largely on their parents for access to preventive health care, parental awareness and attitudes may play an important role in the acceptance of lipid screening, follow-up of abnormal results, and adoption of lifestyle changes. A broader study assessing parents’ knowledge and practices regarding this subject would help identify existing gaps and inform the development of targeted interventions to encourage early screening.

The present study aims to assess the knowledge and practices of Lebanese parents regarding pediatric dyslipidemia and to identify the factors that might affect knowledge.

Materials and methods

This is a cross-sectional survey-based study conducted between March and November 2025 among parents of children enrolled in eight Lebanese private schools from the Greater Beirut and Mount Lebanon regions. The participating schools included primary, intermediate, and secondary levels; therefore, the children represented in the study ranged in age from 5 to 18 years.

Sampling strategy

The study used a school-based, non-random sampling strategy. Eight private schools located in Greater Beirut and Mount Lebanon (GB/ML) were selected from a list of eligible schools using a purposive, convenience-based approach. GB/ML area was selected because it concentrates the majority of students, particularly after the war period. School selection was based on administrative approval, ease of access, number of enrolled students, and socioeconomic profile. To improve socioeconomic variability within the private-school population, the three schools with the highest number of students from higher socioeconomic backgrounds and the five schools with the highest number of students from low-to-middle socioeconomic backgrounds were invited and included. Public schools in the targeted areas were not included because they comprise a highly heterogeneous student population, including a substantial proportion of non-Lebanese refugee students. Since the present study aimed to assess parental knowledge and practices among Lebanese parents and given that public schools account for less than 25% of enrolled students in Lebanon, recruitment was limited to private schools to maintain a more homogeneous study population.

Preparatory phase

Before distributing questionnaires, meetings were held with the administrations of each participating school to present the study’s objectives and provide a brief overview of dyslipidemia. Following review by the school medical teams, including the pediatrician and the school nurse, the questionnaire was approved by the school’s administration. The study and questionnaire were also presented to each school’s parents’ committee to encourage parental participation and response.

The questionnaire

The participant information sheet and questionnaire available in English, French, and Arabic are provided as supplementary material. Both documents were developed specifically for the present study and have not been previously published. The questionnaire uses simple language to ensure comprehension across different educational levels. The participant information sheet explained to parents the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, and the confidentiality of responses. It also provided a brief explanation of dyslipidemia, defined as abnormal blood lipid levels, including elevated cholesterol, triglycerides, or abnormal high-density lipoprotein (HDL) levels. Participants were informed that completion of the questionnaire implied consent. The questionnaire was developed specifically for this study after reviewing the available literature on pediatric dyslipidemia, cardiovascular risk factors, screening recommendations, prevention, and parental health-related knowledge and practices. It was reviewed by three pediatricians, who evaluated the relevance, clarity, and comprehensiveness of the items and provided qualitative feedback. Based on their comments, several items were reworded to improve clarity and clinical accuracy. The questionnaire was then pilot-tested with 30 parents to assess its comprehensibility. These 30 parents were excluded from the final analysis and were not included in the final sample of 946 participants. Participants were asked to comment on the wording of the items, ease of understanding, and overall satisfaction. Based on their feedback, minor modifications were made to improve clarity. The questionnaire was then distributed via Google Forms to parents through the school administrations, mainly via official school communication channels, including school WhatsApp groups and/or electronic messages (via the school Microsoft Teams). All parents of students enrolled in the participating schools were eligible to participate. Participation implies that parents were able to understand and complete the online questionnaire and provided informed consent. No specific exclusion criteria were applied, particularly regarding the child’s medical history, parental medical background, or previous exposure to health education diseases on dyslipidemia. The response rate was approximately 25%, which is acceptable for a voluntary, school-based online questionnaire addressed to parents.

The questionnaire was available in three languages (French, English, Arabic) to optimize response rates and underwent a translation and back translation process [21]. The internal reliability of the questionnaire was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha to evaluate the consistency between the knowledge items. The results showed good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.76., Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin measure (= 0.717) and Bartlett’s test of sphericity (p < 0.001).

Statistical analysis

Qualitative variables were described as frequencies and percentages. Quantitative variables were described using means and standard deviations (M ± SD) (since their distribution was normal).

Associations between the knowledge score and sociodemographic variables (gender, age, education level, healthcare professional status, number of children, prior exposure to information on dyslipidemia) were explored using parametric tests (Student’s t-test, ANOVA test). The correlation between age and knowledge score was assessed using Pearson’s correlation test.

Associations between the knowledge score and parental preventive practices (use of screening, eating habits, and promotion of physical activity) were also assessed using Student’s t-tests and ANOVA.

Multiple linear regression was performed to identify the independent factors that are associated with the knowledge score. Variables that showed a statistically significant association (p < 0.10) in the bivariate analysis were included in the multivariate model. Unstandardized coefficients (B), their 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), and p-values were reported. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs) and tolerance values. Statistical significance for all other analyses was defined at the 5% level (p < 0.05).

The collected data were analyzed using SPSS statistical software (version 26.0).

Ethical approval and consent to participate

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of our university (University Ethics Center of Université Saint-Joseph de Beyrouth (Reference: USJ-2024-201)). All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Participation was voluntary, and completion of the anonymous questionnaire was considered to imply informed consent and was approved by the IRB; therefore, the IRB waived the requirement for separate written informed consent.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of the population

A total of 946 parents were included in the analysis. The mean age of participants was 43.9 ± 6.2 years, ranging from 25 to 68 years. The population was predominantly female (91.0%). The level of education was high, with 94.1% of parents having a university degree. In terms of professional occupation, 11.6% were healthcare professionals. Most parents had two children (50.5%), followed by those with three children (27.2%) (Table 1).

Parental awareness of pediatric dyslipidemia

Among the 946 parents surveyed, less than half (42.4%, n = 401) reported having heard of dyslipidemia in children, while 57.6% reported never having heard of it. Among parents who were aware of this problem, the main source of information was the media (television, internet, and other media), cited by 25.2% (n = 101) of respondents, followed by healthcare professionals (24.4%, n = 98), other sources (22.2%, n = 89), friends (17.5%, n = 70), family (7.5%, n = 30), and finally school (3.2%, n = 13). Regarding self-assessed knowledge, most participants reported limited awareness of pediatric dyslipidemia: 44.7% (n = 423) indicated no knowledge, and 32.9% (n = 311) reported little knowledge, while only 15.8% (n = 149) rated their knowledge as average, and 6.7% (n = 63) as very good.

Only about half of parents (50.5%) believed that their children should be screened for dyslipidemia, and 45.8% answered the question about the age at which screening should begin correctly. An overall knowledge score out of 23 points was established to assess parents’ level of knowledge, with the questions and correct answers presented in Table 2. The mean knowledge score was 14.1 ± 4.4 out of 23, with a median of 14.0 [11.0–17.0] and scores ranged from 1 to 23(Fig. 1).

Nearly all parents (99.2%) reported encouraging their children to engage in physical activity. In addition, 65.5% reported that they were always providing their children with a balanced diet, while 32.9% reported doing so sometimes. Fruit and vegetable consumption was reported as daily in 85.6% of children. Finally, a large majority of parents (87.9%) expressed an interest in receiving additional information on pediatric dyslipidemia. Online resources were the most frequently cited type of support (57.7%), followed by school programs (48.0%) and information brochures (27.4%) (Table 3).

Factors affecting knowledge scores

Bivariate analysis showed that female gender, university education, and being a healthcare professional were associated with a higher knowledge score (respectively: 14.2 ± 4.4 vs. 12.4 ± 4.8; p < 0.001 for female gender; 14.2 ± 4.4 vs. 12.5 ± 4.8; p = 0.007 for university education; and 17.3 ± 3.6 vs. 13.7 ± 4.4; p < 0.001 for being a healthcare professional). Similarly, parents who had heard of dyslipidemia in children had a significantly higher knowledge score than those who had never heard of it (15.8 ± 3.9 vs. 12.8 ± 4.4; p < 0.001). However, the parents’ age and the number of children were not correlated with the knowledge score (p = 0.531 and p = 0.221, respectively) (Table 4; Fig. 2).

Association between the knowledge score and preventive practices

The bivariate analysis revealed a significant association between parents’ level of knowledge and several preventive practices related to pediatric dyslipidemia (Table 6; Fig. 2). Parents who had already screened their children for dyslipidemia had a significantly higher knowledge score than those who had never performed this screening (16.9 ± 3.5 vs. 13.9 ± 4.4; p < 0.001). Parents who reported always providing a balanced diet for their children had a higher knowledge score (14.4 ± 4.4) compared to those who reported doing so sometimes (13.5 ± 4.5), rarely (14.0 ± 5.5), or never (12.5 ± 6.1) (p = 0.02). Similarly, parents who reported that their children consumed fruit and vegetables every day had a higher knowledge score (14.3 ± 4.4) than those who reported less frequent consumption, particularly once (13.0 ± 4.3) or twice (13.0 ± 4.8) a week, or never (11.7 ± 5.5) (p = 0.02).

Discussion

In our study, we assessed the knowledge and practices of a large sample of Lebanese parents regarding dyslipidemia in children and adolescents. A total of 946 parents of students attending eight Lebanese schools agreed to participate in the study, with most of them being female (91%). Our findings indicate that, although 94% of the study population had attained a university degree, overall parental awareness of pediatric dyslipidemia remains limited. In fact, only 42.4% of parents had heard of this condition, and only half of them considered screening their children necessary. Moreover, the mean knowledge score on pediatric dyslipidemia was only 14.1 out of 23. Finally, only 6.7% of parents reported that their children had undergone blood test-based lipid screening.

To date, relatively few studies have addressed this topic on a global scale. A previous US study involving 91 parents similarly found that 45.5% supported universal screening for dyslipidemia [19]. The same study further reported that over 80% of parents recognized the association between nutrition, physical activity, genetic factors, and pediatric dyslipidemia [19]. Parents’ limited knowledge of pediatric dyslipidemia may be partly explained by a lack of awareness of dyslipidemia among adults, as several studies indicate that the majority of affected adults remain unaware of their condition [14, 15, 17]. In fact, the percentage of people aware of their disease was reported at 21% in the Bogalusa study [14], only 15% in the ORISCAV-LUX Luxembourg study [15], and 14.4% in a Chinese study [17].

Multivariate analysis showed that female gender, university education level, healthcare professional status, and having heard of pediatric dyslipidemia were independent determinants of higher parental knowledge score. 91% of participants in the present study were women, with significantly higher knowledge scores than men. This high female response rate is comparable to that reported in the study by Kern et al. [19], in which 81.3% of respondents were women. The observed predominance of female responses may be explained by mothers’ greater involvement in their children’s health and care. They are the ones who take their children to the pediatrician, are more involved in school-related communication, and are more present in parent communication groups. Accordingly, mothers often constitute the main point of interaction between the school and the child, as they are more likely to receive and complete the school’s questionnaires, whereas fathers may be less involved in these communication processes.

The parents’ level of university education was associated with a higher knowledge score, whereas parental age was not. To our knowledge, no previous studies have examined the association between parental age or education level and knowledge of pediatric dyslipidemia. However, in adults, age and a high level of education were associated with better knowledge of their dyslipidemia status [14, 17]. These findings highlight the strong link between educational attainment and dyslipidemia knowledge in both populations.

The findings of this study also showed that being a healthcare professional parent was associated with higher knowledge scores. These results are consistent with those observed in the Korean adult population, which show that healthcare professionals had a better understanding of adult dyslipidemia [16]. This finding can be attributed to greater exposure to the latest evidence-based medical guidelines on pediatric dyslipidemia.

Despite the very high prevalence of pediatric dyslipidemia, screening in children remains insufficient. The low screening rate (6.7%) observed in our study is comparable to those reported before the publication of the 2011 NHLBI recommendations. Indeed, studies conducted between 1995 and 2012 reported that only 3.4% [22] to 16% [23] of children were screened for dyslipidemia. More recently in the US, screening rates have increased modestly, with 9.0% of 9- to 11-year-olds and 12.9% of 17- to 21-year-olds undergoing lipid screening; however, overall rates remain low [24]. Although lipid screening appears to have increased in more recent years, with nearly one-third of young people aged 16 to 21 having undergone screening [25], a very recent study reported a decline in cholesterol screening following the COVID-19 pandemic and the updated U.S. Preventive Services Task Force recommendations [26].

To improve screening, several studies have evaluated the impact of educational interventions targeting healthcare professionals. In one study, following an intervention, the proportion of patients for whom screening tests were prescribed increased from 8.9% to 50% [27], while in another study, it increased from 6.2% to 84.8% [28]. These findings highlight the potential effectiveness of such interventions in supporting clinicians’ adherence to recommendations. However, the actual performance of the prescribed tests remained limited. In fact, only 69.6% of children actually underwent screening in the first study [27] and 56.7% in the second [28]. Thus, even when physicians adhere to recommendations, overall screening rates remain insufficient. This suggests that parental barriers may contribute to the gap between prescription and actual screening. Despite the 2011 recommendations, 30.8% of parents still disagreed with universal screening for children [19]. More recently, Scully P. et al. reported that 98% of parents supported screening for their children [20]. However, this study was based on a small sample of 53 parents of hospitalized children and may not be representative of the general population.

The exceptionally high rates of favorable practices reported by parents, such as 99.2% of respondents claiming to encourage physical activity and 85.6% reporting daily fruit and vegetable consumption, can be explained by a possible desirability bias. Parents may have over-reported these healthy behaviors to conform to perceived social norms or medical expectations regarding ideal parenting practices. However, our study lacked objective behavioral verification such as dietary logs, physical activity trackers, or direct clinical observations. Therefore, these high percentages should be interpreted with caution.

Our findings of low screening rates should be interpreted in the context of the ongoing international debate regarding pediatric lipid screening strategies. Although our data suggest a significant gap in screening implementation, determining whether Lebanon should adopt a universal or strictly targeted screening framework requires careful consideration of these differing international perspectives.

We examined the association between parents’ knowledge scores and the implementation of favorable preventive practices. Higher parental scores were significantly associated with more favorable preventive practices, including lipid screening and healthier eating habits in children. These findings highlight the importance of improving knowledge as it may contribute to the development of better preventive practices. By adopting healthier preventive practices, parents may help improve their children’s lipid profiles and reduce the risk of future metabolic diseases such as obesity and diabetes. In addition, screening according to the AAP recommended guidelines may facilitate early diagnosis and management before the development of atherosclerosis and cardiovascular disease.

Finally, among parents who had heard of dyslipidemia, only 24.4% reported obtaining information from a healthcare professional. This finding can be explained by the fact that only 26% of pediatricians are aware of the current screening recommendations [29]. Hence, strengthening healthcare professionals’ education is essential to raise awareness among parents and increase screening rates in children. Indeed, in a US study conducted after the implementation of educational and awareness-raising measures for pediatricians on the new recommendations, the screening rate increased from 8.9% to 50% in 12 months [27].

As a next step, we intend to organize awareness sessions and distribute digital materials to parents, students, and teachers in schools. These educational sessions aim to meet the informational needs reported by nearly 90% of surveyed parents, who expressed a desire for further guidance on pediatric and adult dyslipidemia, through online resources, school-based programs, or informational brochures. These educational materials should address pediatric dyslipidemia, including its definition, causes, and consequences, as well as recommended prevention and management strategies. Subsequently, an online knowledge evaluation will be conducted to measure the impact of these educational interventions on participants’ knowledge scores.

The main strengths and limitations of the present study are summarized in Table 7. Regarding limitations, first, the data were based on a self-reported questionnaire, which may be subject to social desirability, potentially leading to overestimation of knowledge and appropriate practice. Second, the study recruited parents exclusively from private schools in Beirut and the Mount Lebanon region using a convenience sampling strategy. Public schools were not included. This may have resulted in the overrepresentation of urban, socioeconomically advantaged, and highly educated parents, as reflected by the high proportion of respondents with university education. The approximate 25% response rate may also have introduced selection bias, as parents who chose to participate may have been more interested in child health, prevention, or dyslipidemia than non-respondents. Moreover, because the questionnaire was distributed electronically and participation was voluntary, responses may have been more likely to come from parents with higher levels of education or greater access to digital tools. Another limitation is that the questionnaire did not collect information on parental history of dyslipidemia, the respondent’s relationship to the child, prior formal education about dyslipidemia, or the presence of chronic metabolic diseases among children. The absence of these factors may therefore have limited our ability to account for other potential confounding factors, as these variables could influence parental knowledge and preventive practices. Finally, because of the cross-sectional design, causal relationships between knowledge, attitudes, and practices could not be established.

Summary of the main strengths and limitations of the study

Although the questionnaire was reviewed by pediatric experts and pilot-tested among parents before data collection, formal psychometric validation was limited. A Content Validity Index was not calculated, and test-retest reliability was not assessed due to the anonymous cross-sectional design. Therefore, further validation studies are needed before using this instrument in longitudinal research or to evaluate the impact of educational interventions.

Despite these limitations, the present study has several strengths, including its relatively large sample size, recruitment from several schools, and good internal consistency among the knowledge score items. In addition, the questionnaire was designed and distributed in three languages to maximize response rates and ensure accessibility to parents from different socioeconomic backgrounds. Furthermore, parental knowledge related to pediatric dyslipidemia was assessed for the first time using a composite score covering several domains, including screening indications, risk factors, and strategies for managing dyslipidemia in children. This scoring system may provide a reliable quantitative assessment of knowledge, allowing longitudinal tracking of changes in knowledge following educational intervention.

These findings highlight the need for coordinated preventive strategies involving schools, healthcare professionals, and public health authorities. Schools may play an important role in disseminating health information to parents and promoting healthy lifestyle behaviors among children. Pediatricians should also be supported through continuing education to ensure consistent counseling and screening practices. At the national level, clearer guidance on pediatric dyslipidemia screening in Lebanon could help reduce variability in physicians’ screening practices and improve early detection. In addition, digital health campaigns through school platforms, parent communication groups, and social media may represent accessible tools to improve parental awareness and engagement.

Conclusion

In conclusion, while universal lipid screening remains a subject of ongoing international debate, this study highlights insufficient parental knowledge of pediatric dyslipidemia and a low rate of screening among children. Strengthening parental education and preventive counseling may help promote early screening and reduce future cardiovascular risk.

The development of point-of-care testing (POCT), which measures cholesterol and triglyceride levels from a single drop of capillary blood, is likely to facilitate more widespread screening for dyslipidemia in children by enhancing parental acceptance. In fact, previous research demonstrated that the use of POCT has improved the diagnosis of hypercholesterolemia in children, due to its many advantages: ease of use, speed, portability, low cost, and fewer visits to the laboratory [30].
