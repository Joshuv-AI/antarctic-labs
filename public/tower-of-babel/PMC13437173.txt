Navigating intraoperative tooth loss during oral surgery under general anesthesia: radiographic localization and videolaryngoscopic retrieval of a supraglottic foreign body: a case report
Authors: Sung Bin Youn, Myong-Hwan Karm, Hyun Jeong Kim, Seung-Hwa Ryoo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDental dislodgement during oral and maxillofacial surgery is an uncommon, but potentially serious complication. We report a case of intraoperative tooth loss with asymptomatic supraglottic retention during oral surgery under general anesthesia. A 55-year-old man underwent bilateral maxillary endoscopic sinus surgery (MESS) under general anesthesia. A severely mobile mandibular tooth was missing during surgery, although the ventilation and hemodynamic parameters remained stable. As the patient showed no evidence of airway compromise and the location of the tooth was uncertain, radiographic localization was performed after completion of the procedure. Portable radiograph revealed a tooth-shaped foreign body in the laryngeal region. Subsequent videolaryngoscopic examination revealed a tooth lodged beneath the epiglottis and above the vocal cord. The tooth was successfully removed using Magill forceps and the patient recovered without any respiratory complications. This case highlights that a dislodged tooth may remain undetected in the supraglottic airway despite stable respiratory parameters. Radiographic localization, followed by targeted video laryngoscopic retrieval, has enabled successful management.Keywords: Anesthesia, General; Clinical Decision-Making; Foreign Bodies; Laryngoscopy; Oral Surgical Procedures; Respiratory Aspiration

INTRODUCTION

Dental injury remains one of the most common anesthesia-related adverse events and is a frequent cause of medicolegal disputes [1,2]. Patients with advanced periodontal disease and severe tooth mobility are particularly susceptible to dental trauma and tooth dislodgement during airway management and oral surgery [1,3,4]. When a tooth becomes dislodged, it may remain within the oral cavity, migrate into the gastrointestinal tract, or enter the airway, potentially resulting in airway obstruction, aspiration pneumonia, atelectasis, or the need for bronchoscopic intervention [5,6,7,8].

Foreign body aspiration during dental and oral-maxillofacial procedures has been reported in both awake and anesthetized patients [5,6,7,9]. Although endotracheal intubation provides substantial airway protection, it does not completely eliminate the possibility of foreign body retention in the supraglottic airway. Small foreign bodies may lodge within anatomical spaces such as the vallecula, aryepiglottic folds, and pyriform sinuses without causing immediate respiratory symptoms [8,10].

Most previously reported cases have focused on aspiration of dental prostheses, implant components, or teeth into the tracheobronchial tree [6,8,9,10]. However, relatively few reports have described asymptomatic retention of a dislodged tooth within the supraglottic airway in an intubated patient. Furthermore, little attention has been paid to clinical decision-making processes following intraoperative tooth loss.

We report the case of a missing mandibular tooth identified during oral surgery under general anesthesia. The tooth was localized using portable radiography and was successfully retrieved under videolaryngoscopic guidance before emergence from anesthesia. This case report highlights the clinical decision-making processes involved in evaluating and managing unexpected intraoperative tooth loss.

CASE REPORT

This study was approved by the IRB (IRB No. ERI26041) and written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

A 55-year-old man (American Society of Anesthesiologists Physical Status Class II; weight, 71 kg; height, 174 cm) was admitted to the Seoul National University Dental Hospital for bilateral maxillary endoscopic sinus surgery (MESS) under general anesthesia. The patient was diagnosed with chronic bilateral maxillary sinusitis and a foreign body in the left maxillary sinus. His medical history was unremarkable except for smoking and alcohol consumption.

Preoperative dental examination revealed severe mobility of teeth #33, #34, #42, #43 and #44. Particularly, tooth 44 demonstrated marked mobility associated with advanced periodontal bone loss (Fig. 1). The oral and maxillofacial surgeon informed the patient preoperatively that extraction of severely mobile mandibular teeth might be required during surgery.

Preoperative panoramic radiograph. Severe periodontal bone loss and marked mobility are noted around tooth 44 (red circle). Clinical examination revealed grade III mobility, and the possibility of intraoperative extraction was discussed preoperatively.

After routine monitoring, general anesthesia was induced with 30 mg of intravenous lidocaine, 120 mg of propofol, and 50 mg of rocuronium. As the surgical procedure involved the nasal cavity and paranasal sinuses, orotracheal intubation rather than nasotracheal intubation was required. Fiberoptic-guided orotracheal intubation was deliberately selected to minimize dental trauma due to severe mobility of the mandibular teeth. A 7.5-mm oral RAE tube was successfully inserted using a fiberoptic bronchoscope. Care was taken to avoid contact with tooth #44. It was visually confirmed that the tooth remained in place immediately after intubation and again after tube fixation.

Following induction of anesthesia, routine surgical preparation and draping were performed. The chief resident then performed local anesthetic infiltration with 2% lidocaine containing 1:100,000 epinephrine. Before initiating the maxillary sinus procedure, the surgeon re-examined the mandibular dentition because several teeth were noted to be severely mobile during the preoperative evaluation. At this time, tooth #44 could no longer be identified.

The anesthesia team was notified immediately. Because the presence of tooth #44 was confirmed after fiberoptic intubation and tube fixation, displacement during airway management was considered unlikely. The possibility of spontaneous tooth loss before admission was also considered; however, the patient's guardian confirmed that no tooth loss had occurred prior to the surgery. Consequently, intraoperative tooth displacement was strongly suspected.

At that time, the oxygen saturation, end-tidal carbon dioxide, airway pressure, and hemodynamic parameters remained stable. The exact location of the missing tooth was uncertain and included the oral cavity, operative field, pharynx, esophagus, airway, or maxillary sinus. As there was no evidence of airway compromise, the location of the missing tooth remained uncertain, and displacement during airway management was considered unlikely, and the surgical and anesthesia teams elected to complete the planned procedure before performing radiographic localization and targeted airway evaluation.

After completion of bilateral maxillary sinus surgery, portable radiography was performed before emergence from anesthesia to localize the missing tooth. A tooth-shaped, radiopaque foreign body was identified in the laryngeal region (Fig. 2). A subsequent airway examination using a McGrath videolaryngoscope revealed the missing tooth lodged beneath the epiglottis, immediately above the vocal cord (Fig. 3). The tooth was successfully retrieved using Magill forceps under continuous videolaryngoscopic visualization, while maintaining endotracheal intubation. The inflated cuff of the endotracheal tube was maintained and expected to act as a physical barrier against distal migration into the lower trachea. A fiberoptic bronchoscope was immediately available for the evaluation of the lower airway in the event of accidental distal migration (Fig. 4).

Portable radiographs obtained before emergence from anesthesia. (A) Anteroposterior view demonstrating a tooth-shaped radiopaque foreign body in the laryngeal region (arrow). (B) Lateral view confirming localization of the displaced tooth within the supraglottic airway (arrow).

Videolaryngoscopic identification and retrieval of the displaced tooth. McGrath videolaryngoscopic image showing a tooth lodged beneath the epiglottis and immediately above the vocal cord (arrow).

Retrieved tooth after removal with Magill forceps.

Following removal, repeat airway examination confirmed complete removal of the foreign body. The patient awakened and was extubated uneventfully. No episodes of desaturation, laryngospasm, airway trauma, or postoperative respiratory complications were observed. The patient’s postoperative course was unremarkable.

DISCUSSION

Dental injury and dislodgement are important perioperative concerns, particularly in patients with advanced periodontal disease and severe tooth mobility [1,2,3,4]. In the present patient, several mandibular teeth demonstrated marked mobility during preoperative evaluation. Fiberoptic-guided orotracheal intubation was deliberately selected to minimize dental trauma. Confirmation that tooth #44 remained intact after intubation and tube fixation later helped narrow the possible timing of tooth displacement.

Foreign body aspiration and ingestion during dental treatment have been increasingly reported in the literature [5,6,7,9]. Most reported airway foreign bodies migrate into the tracheobronchial tree and are diagnosed after the development of respiratory symptoms or radiographic abnormalities [7,8,9,10]. In contrast, the present case involved a tooth retained within the supraglottic airway beneath the epiglottis, immediately above the vocal cord. Although a cuffed endotracheal tube reduces the risk of distal aspiration, it does not prevent foreign bodies from becoming trapped within the supraglottic airway [8,9]. Preservation of ventilation and oxygenation was expected, because the tooth remained above the inflated endotracheal tube cuff. Nevertheless, this finding highlights that stable respiratory parameters do not exclude occult supraglottic foreign body retention. Similar observations have been reported in previous cases of dental foreign bodies [9,10].

A distinctive feature of this case was that the missing tooth was recognized before the planned surgical procedure began; however, its exact location remained unknown. This uncertainty influenced subsequent clinical decision-making. Immediate videolaryngoscopic examination was considered. However, the patient remained intubated with stable ventilation and no evidence of airway compromise. Displacement during intubation was considered unlikely, and the missing tooth could have been located in the oral cavity, operative field, pharynx, esophagus, airway, or maxillary sinus. Under these circumstances, the surgical and anesthesia teams elected to first localize the tooth radiographically before performing a targeted airway evaluation.

Subsequently, portable radiography played a pivotal role in confirming that the foreign body remained within the laryngeal region, thereby providing a clear indication for focused airway examination. This approach facilitated targeted airway evaluation while enabling definitive retrieval before emergence from anesthesia [6,8,10].

Videolaryngoscopy is also important for successful management. Previous reports have demonstrated the utility of videolaryngoscopy for the diagnosis and removal of foreign bodies [11,12]. Compared with conventional direct laryngoscopy, videolaryngoscopy provides improved visualization of supraglottic structures, enables image sharing among team members, and facilitates controlled foreign-body retrieval. In the present case, the McGrath videolaryngoscope provided a clear view of the tooth lodged beneath the epiglottis and above the vocal cords, enabling its atraumatic removal using Magill forceps under continuous videolaryngoscopic visualization.

However, the precise mechanism underlying the tooth displacement remains unclear. Although displacement during intubation was considered unlikely because the tooth was confirmed present after tube fixation, other contributing factors may have been involved. In addition to surgical manipulation and local anesthetic infiltration, sustained pressure exerted by the oral RAE tube or tube fixation may have contributed to displacement of the severely mobile tooth.

Unlike the case reported by Doh et al. [9], in which a dental prosthesis was identified around the endotracheal tube during dental treatment under general anesthesia, the present case involved a periodontally compromised natural tooth that was recognized as missing before surgery and subsequently localized within the supraglottic airway. Early recognition, radiographic localization, and targeted videolaryngoscopic retrieval have enabled successful management before the emergence from anesthesia.

If a missing tooth had not been identified before emergence, significant complications might have occurred. Extubation, coughing, swallowing, or positional changes could have displaced the tooth into the trachea or bronchi, resulting in airway obstruction, aspiration pneumonia, atelectasis, laryngospasm, or the need for bronchoscopic intervention [8,10]. Therefore, when a tooth is unexpectedly missing during oral and maxillofacial surgery, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion, even in the absence of respiratory symptoms.

In the present case, the missing tooth was recognized before the completion of the surgery; however, its exact location remained uncertain, and the patient exhibited no signs of airway compromise. Under these specific circumstances, the surgical and anesthesia teams elected to complete the planned procedure, obtain radiographic localization, and perform a targeted airway evaluation. This approach enabled the successful identification and retrieval of the displaced tooth before emergence from anesthesia. However, the management process described here reflects the specific clinical circumstances of a single patient. It should not be interpreted as a universal approach for all cases of intraoperative tooth loss. Immediate airway evaluation should always be considered when airway compromise is suspected.

In conclusion, unexpected intraoperative tooth loss requires careful evaluation even when ventilation and oxygenation remain stable. In the present case, radiographic localization, followed by videolaryngoscopic examination, enabled the successful identification and retrieval of a displaced tooth lodged in the supraglottic airway before emergence from anesthesia. This case emphasizes the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion, close communication between the surgical and anesthesia teams, and careful evaluation of unexpected intraoperative tooth loss during oral surgery under general anesthesia.
