Labral Reconstruction Utilizing Semitendinosus Allograft and Knotless Anchor Fixation
Authors: Joseph M Sliepka, Nyaluma N Wagala, Brian T Feeley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTreatment for anterior instability in the setting of subcritical bone loss remains challenging. The addition of labral deficiency or an irreparable labrum can further complicate treatment strategies. Different treatment options have been proposed, including Bankart repair with remplissage, bone block procedures, and dynamic augmentation. Labral reconstruction utilizing semitendinosus allograft with knotless anchor fixation may provide an efficient and reproducible method for adding stability to the shoulder.

Anterior shoulder instability is a common pathology encountered by sports medicine surgeons, with an estimated incidence of 23 to 25 cases per 100,000 person‐years and represents 0.12 injuries per 1000 athlete exposures. 1 , 2 , 3 The risk factors for shoulder instability include younger age, male sex, participation in contact sports, and generalized ligamentous laxity. 4 , 5 , 6

Arthroscopic Bankart repair remains the most common surgical treatment for anterior shoulder instability. 7 However, recurrence rates remain a concern, with approximately 20% of patients experiencing recurrent instability or requiring revision surgery following isolated Bankart repair. 8 , 9 Burkhart and De Beer helped advance our understanding of humeral and glenoid bone loss through the concept of the glenoid track and influenced treatment options for instability in the presence of bone loss. 10 Critical bone loss has been defined as 20% to 25% of the glenoid width. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 When bone loss exceeds these parameters, the failure rates after isolated Bankart repair approach 70% or greater. 10 , 11 As a result, the gold standard treatment in patients with critical bone loss is a bone block procedure such as Latarjet.

More recently, the topic of subcritical bone loss has become more apparent, described as anywhere from 13.5% to 20%. 15 , 16 , 17 In these patients, isolated Bankart repairs lead to higher rates of recurrence and worse functional outcomes, which has led to a debate as to the best treatment for this challenging group of patients. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 The addition of remplissage to Bankart repair and bone block procedures have both been proposed as treatment alternatives for isolated Bankart repair. The addition of remplissage to Bankart repair has shown good outcomes, with recurrence rates ranging from 0% to 20%. 19 , 20 Latarjet and other bone block procedures have also shown promising outcomes in this patient population, especially when bone loss nears critical levels. 21 , 22 , 23

The concept of dynamic anterior augmentation has been recently proposed by Collin and Lädermann 24 as a possible alternative treatment, in which the long head biceps tendon is tenotomized at its origin, transferred through a subscapularis split, and attached to the glenoid face. The proposed benefits of this treatment include recreating the labrum to help provide a bumper against recurrent instability and also a sling effect when the arm is abducted and externally rotated, similar to the conjoint tendon in a Latarjet. 24 Proponents of this technique cite the benefits of this procedure in enhancing stability while also having a low complication profile. 25

Given the lack of consensus in treatment of anterior instability with subcritical bone loss and labral deficiency, alternative treatment techniques may be valuable. We describe our preferred technique of labral reconstruction that uses semitendinosus allograft to reconstruct the labrum alongside capsular shift to provide anterior stabilization in the setting of subcritical bone loss and labral deficiency.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

The patient is positioned in the beach chair position, with the forearm secured with an arm positioner (AssistArm; ConMed, Largo, FL). A standard “soft spot” posterior portal is made, followed by diagnostic arthroscopy. Two anterior portals are then made in the rotator interval, one superomedial to assist with labrum elevation and suture passage, and one inferolateral to assist with anchor placement (Figure 1, Video 1). A 6.5 × 72 mm cannula is placed in the superomedial portal, and an 8 × 72 mm cannula is placed in the inferolateral portal (CLEAR‐TRAC; Smith & Nephew, Watford, UK). After portal creation, remplissage can be completed if indicated.

On the back table, a 7 mm diameter semitendinosus allograft is thawed and prepped by tagging both ends with suture (#2 FiberWire; Arthrex, Naples, FL) utilizing Krakow technique in locking fashion. A diagnostic arthroscopy is performed, and the labrum is evaluated. In the setting of labral deficiency, labral reconstruction can be performed (Figure 2). First, the anterior glenoid rim is prepared with rasp or burr to create a bleeding bone bed to facilitate healing. Then, a capsular shift is performed. A suture passage device (Suture Lasso; Arthrex, Naples, FL) is placed through the inferolateral anterior portal and used to penetrate the inferior capsule, and a looped passing suture is threaded through and shuttled out the superomedial anterior portal. The free end of the looped passing suture will be out the inferolateral anterior portal and the looped end will be out the superomedial anterior portal (Figure 3A,B). The suture passer is then removed, and the passing suture is clamped for protection. A knotless looped anchor (Knotless 1.8 FiberTak Soft Anchor; Arthrex, Naples, FL) is placed on the glenoid through the inferolateral anterior portal at the 6:00 position. A 1.8 mm drill is first used to create a pilot hole after which the 1.8 mm anchor is placed, which contains one repair stand and one looped shuttle strand. A looped grasper is then used to shuttle the repair strand out the superomedial anterior portal. The repair strand is then looped through the passing suture from the suture passer, outside the superomedial anterior portal. The free end of the passing suture is pulled from the inferolateral anterior portal, shuttling the repair stitch underneath the capsule and back out the inferolateral anterior portal. It is then looped through its paired shuttle strand outside the inferolateral anterior portal, and the shuttle strand is now pulled, passing the repair strand around the capsule. The repair stitch can now be pulled and tightened down, advancing the capsule onto the glenoid (Figure 4A‐E). The excess suture is then cut flush. This process is repeated at the 3:00 position.

Once the capsular shift has been completed, the surgeon moves on to the labral reconstruction. Two more anchors are placed, in the 4:00 and 5:00 position, repeating the steps above. However, after passing the repair stitch through the capsule, the loop is not tightened down fully and instead is left loose for future graft passage (Table 1). Once the two anchor loops are prepared, the suture passer is inserted into the inferolateral anterior portal and passed through the two looped anchors, and a looped passing suture is threaded through and pulled out the superomedial anterior portal. The graft is then brought to the surgical field, placed through the looped passing suture and shuttled down through the superomedial anterior portal into the joint through the repair strand loops of the 4:00 and 5:00 anchors. A looped grasper may be used to facilitate graft passage through the looped anchors (Table 1). Once appropriately positioned, the two looped suture anchors can be tensioned down, reapproximating the labrum (Figure 5A‐D). One final anchor is placed at the 2:00 position above the subscapularis and shuttled around the proximal end of the graft, then tightened while the tagged end of the graft is held in tension (Figure 6A,B). Excess graft can then be cut.

Postoperative Rehabilitation

The rehabilitation protocol for this procedure is the same as for labral repair. Patients remain in a sling for a total of 6 weeks postoperatively, during which they are encouraged to work on wrist and finger range of motion. After 6 weeks, they can discontinue the sling and begin range of motion exercises. Strengthening begins at 3 months, followed by return to sport 6 months after surgery.

DISCUSSION

Capsulolabral reconstruction as a treatment for anterior instability is less often discussed in the literature than more mainstay treatments such as remplissage and Latarjet. However, various reconstruction techniques have been proposed over the last 30 years. 24 , 26 , 27

Currently, there is no gold standard treatment for patients with recurrent instability, subcritical bone loss, and capsulolabral deficiency. Our technique provides the advantage of providing soft tissue augmentation to recreate a labral bumper, directly addressing the pathology in these patients. Similar to the biceps transfer, this procedure avoids the complications associated with remplissage such as stiffness, and with Latarjet such as neurologic injury and bone resorption. 28 , 29 Additionally, without the need to harvest an autograft, this is a time‐efficient procedure. Furthermore, by leaving the biceps tendon alone, the patient should have no complications associated with biceps tenotomy or transfer, such as weakness, Popeye deformity, or cramping. Lastly, this technique allows for other standard procedures for instability to be completed alongside it, such as remplissage and capsular shift, adding to the overall stability of the construct (Table 2).

Disadvantages of this technique include the use of allograft, which can add expense to the procedure. In addition, the use of allograft carries with it the risk of graft resorption. Furthermore, the labral reconstruction takes increased time compared with a typical Bankart repair. Lastly, this procedure is not recommended in the presence of critical bone loss (Table 2).

Surgical treatment of anterior shoulder instability in the presence of labral deficiency is challenging. The use of semitendinosus allograft secured with knotless looped anchors to recreate the labrum, when paired with remplissage, provides an efficient and replicable treatment strategy to address the patient's pathology and provide stability to the shoulder.
