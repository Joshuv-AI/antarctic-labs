Electrosurgical Leaflet Modification in TAVR
Authors: Yuxin He, Jian’an Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: BASILICA, leaflet modification, transcatheter aortic valve replacement, transcatheter electrosurgery, UNICORN

Coronary obstruction remains among the most feared and potentially fatal complications of transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR), particularly during transcatheter aortic valve prosthesis (TAV)-in-surgical aortic valve prosthesis and TAV-in-TAV procedures.1,2 As TAVR indications expand to younger, lower-risk patients and previously implanted bioprosthetic valves degenerate gradually, the incidence of redo-TAVR scenarios with anatomical risk for coronary compromise is projected to rise substantially.3 Electrosurgical leaflet laceration and fenestration strategies have fundamentally altered the management paradigm for patients at high risk of coronary obstruction, offering a catheter-based approach that preserves coronary patency without burdening patients with permanent coronary stent scaffolding.4,5 To date, however, the evidence base is predominantly from North American and European registries.4,6

In this issue of JACC: Asia, Wong et al7 report the findings of the Asia Pacific Electrosurgery (APE) Working Group–Aortic Valve Leaflet Modification Registry, representing the first systematic evaluation of transcatheter electrosurgical leaflet modification techniques—including bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction (BASILICA) and undermining iatrogenic coronary obstruction with radiofrequency needle (UNICORN)—from the Asia-Pacific region. This study fills a critical geographical gap and provides timely real-world evidence.

The investigators retrospectively enrolled 100 consecutive patients across 7 experienced centers in Hong Kong, Taiwan, Thailand, and Australia. BASILICA was performed in 67% of patients, UNICORN in 30%, and a combined bilateral approach in 3%. The cohort was notable for its female predominance (72%), advanced age (median 80 years), and—crucially—predominantly small index valves. Procedural success was achieved in 85.1% of BASILICA cases and 96.7% of UNICORN cases, with a coronary obstruction rate of 2.99% in the BASILICA group. These figures align closely with those reported in the multicenter International BASILICA Registry (86.9% success; 4.7% coronary obstruction)4 and the EURO-BASILICA registry (88.2% success; 2.4% coronary obstruction),6 suggesting that procedural outcomes are reproducible across distinctly different patient populations.

What sets this study apart, however, is not merely the geographical origin of the data, but the compelling illustration of regional anatomical adaptation. Asian populations are well recognized to exhibit smaller aortic root dimensions, lower sinotubular junction heights, and narrower valve-to-coronary distances than Western cohorts.8 These anatomical characteristics increase the risk of coronary obstruction during TAVR procedures and raise questions about whether catheter-based leaflet modification can achieve comparable efficacy in this population.9 The present study demonstrates not only that BASILICA and UNICORN are feasible in this setting, but that local operators have adapted and refined these techniques to overcome anatomical constraints. Notably, the AL3 guiding catheter—frequently favored in Western series for its reach and stability—was used in fewer than 3% of BASILICA procedures in this cohort, reflecting both limited availability and anatomical incompatibility with small aortic roots. Instead, operators adopted mother-and-child telescoping system in more than one-half of cases, typically pairing an AL1 or AL2 guide with a manually modified pigtail catheter to achieve coaxial leaflet engagement.7 Additionally, the coronary ostium anchoring technique—developed and refined in the Asia-Pacific region—was successfully employed to ensure precise laceration alignment adjacent to the coronary ostium.7 These technical adaptations highlight that applying procedural concepts to different populations requires ongoing, context-specific refinements.

Prediction models have forecast a marked surge in valve-in-valve procedures in the Asia-Pacific over the coming decade, paralleling trends already observed in the United States.3 Given the anatomical characteristics of Asian patients, leaflet modification is believed to become an indispensable component of valve-in-valve procedures in this region. The nearly 10-fold increase in case volume documented during the study period aligns well with this anticipated demand trajectory. Nevertheless, dedicated devices for leaflet modification are not yet available in the Asia-Pacific,10 underscoring the pressing need for regionally developed, original devices and technologies.

The findings of this study are promising; yet, a few limitations need to be noticed. First, as an observational, retrospective registry without a contemporaneous control group or randomization, the study cannot establish comparative effectiveness against alternative coronary protection strategies such as chimney or snorkel stenting, nor against recently introduced dedicated mechanical leaflet splitting devices.10 Second, the inclusion of heterogeneous procedural scenarios—native valve TAVR, TAV-in-surgical aortic valve prosthesis, and TAV-in-TAV—within a single cohort, while reflective of real-world practice, introduces potential confounding. Third, the registry captured only short-term outcomes to 30 days. The relatively elevated postprocedural mean gradients in the BASILICA group raises questions about long-term valve durability and the potential for patient-prosthesis mismatch that extended follow-up must address. Fourth, given the modest sample size, the outcome rates should be interpreted with caution. Finally, the mechanisms of structural valve deterioration, such as leaflet calcification and/or hypoattenuated leaflet thickening,11 were not described in this study, and their impact on the choice of leaflet modification techniques and on outcomes warrants further investigation.

In summary, this study from the APE Working Group provides reassuring and externally valid evidence that transcatheter electrosurgical leaflet modification can be performed safely and effectively in Asia-Pacific patients. The study enriches the global literature by confirming procedural generalizability while documenting the anatomical and technical adaptations required to achieve success in this distinct population. Longer-term follow-up, head-to-head comparisons against alternative coronary protection modalities, and the development of dedicated devices are essential to firmly establish the role of leaflet modification in TAVR to reduce coronary obstruction risk.
