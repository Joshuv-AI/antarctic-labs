Subspace Track-before-Detect for Passive Multi-Target Tracking with Unknown Emitted Signals
\authorblockN Nobutaka Ito and Yoshiaki Bando \authorblockA National Institute of Advanced Industrial Science and Technology (AIST), Japan E-mail: nobutaka.itou@aist.go.jp

Abstract
Passive multi-target tracking (MTT) aims to infer the kinematic states of multiple targets from noisy sensor data in which contributions from unknown target-emitted signals are superposed. Track-before-detect (TBD) methods improve robustness to noise by operating directly on raw sensor data without relying on a preceding detection stage. However, many existing TBD methods assume that each target’s contribution to the sensor data is determined solely by its kinematic state. This assumption limits their applicability to passive MTT, where each target’s contribution depends on both its kinematic state and the unknown emitted signal. We propose subspace TBD , a passive multi-target TBD method based on a likelihood derived from the complex Bingham distribution that does not require explicit modeling or estimation of the unknown emitted signals. In a particle filter (PF) framework, each multi-target hypothesis is mapped to a low-dimensional subspace spanned by the steering vectors corresponding to the hypothesized target states. The likelihood is then used to evaluate the alignment of the normalized multichannel sensor data with this subspace. Preliminary experiments with simulated acoustic measurements and a given target activity pattern show that the proposed method can track two moving targets emitting unknown signals at a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of − 𝟏𝟎 \mathbf{-10} dB, whereas a conventional TBD baseline yields substantially larger tracking errors.

Introduction

This paper addresses MTT, which aims to infer target states from multichannel noisy sensor data, where these states typically include kinematic states (e.g., the position and velocity). Many MTT methods first convert the sensor data into thresholded detections and then perform multi-target inference using these detections
[
21
,
6
,
25
,
13
,
14
,
27
,
26

. This front-end detection step is a hard, lossy decision-making process. At low SNRs, weak target evidence can be rejected by thresholding, whereas noise or clutter can give rise to spurious detections. Because the tracker only receives the resulting detection set, sub-threshold evidence is not available to later temporal filtering.

TBD methods avoid this lossy intermediate representation by evaluating target-state hypotheses directly from the raw sensor data and by accumulating evidence through the dynamical model
[

,

,

,

,

. This makes TBD well suited to low-SNR tracking. Many existing TBD formulations, especially radar and sonar TBD with given transmitted waveforms, assume that each target contribution to the sensor data is fully specified by its kinematic state.
A challenge in passive sensing domains such as audio source tracking is that this strong assumption on the observation model does not hold. In such domains, targets emit their own signals, such as speech or music, and these emitted signals are unknown and potentially nonstationary. In the short-time Fourier transform (STFT) domain used in this paper, the observed multichannel mixture depends on both the targets’ kinematic states and the unknown STFT coefficients of the emitted signals. Even if the hypothesized kinematic states are correct, the prediction of the sensor data can be inaccurate because the emitted signals are unknown.

In this paper, we propose subspace TBD, a multi-target TBD method designed for passive sensing scenarios involving superposed target contributions and unknown target-emitted signals (Fig.
1
). The core idea is that the normalized multichannel sensor data reside near a low-dimensional signal subspace spanned by the steering vectors associated with the hypothesized kinematic states. We model this subspace using an observation likelihood based on the complex Bingham distribution
[
10
]
, which characterizes the directional statistics of normalized complex observations. As a result, the observation likelihood depends on deviations from the signal subspace, allowing the likelihood to be evaluated without explicitly estimating the unknown emitted signals while retaining the low-SNR advantage of TBD for kinematic tracking under a given target activity pattern.

2
Subspace TBD: Multi-Target TBD in the Presence of Unknown Emitted Signals

We propose subspace TBD, a multi-target TBD method tailored for passive sensing scenarios with superposed observations and unknown target-emitted signals. The method follows the TBD principle of using raw sensor data directly, thereby avoiding a separate threshold-based detection stage and exploiting temporal information through a state space model. Subspace TBD represents each hypothesized target set as the subspace spanned by its steering vectors. This signal subspace representation allows the observation likelihood to be computed without specifying the unknown target spectra explicitly.

2.1
State model

We use
N
target slots as a fixed upper bound on the number of targets represented by the filter. Each slot can be valid or invalid at time
t
. The multi-target kinematic state is

\mathbf{x}_{t}=[\mathbf{x}_{1t}^{\mathsf{T}},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{Nt}^{\mathsf{T}}]^{\mathsf{T}}
, where
\mathbf{x}_{nt}
denotes the kinematic state of slot
n
. In the two-dimensional experiments,
\mathbf{x}_{nt}=[p_{x,nt},p_{y,nt},v_{x,nt},v_{y,nt}]^{\mathsf{T}}
, where
p_{x,nt}
and
p_{y,nt}
are the Cartesian coordinates of the target position and
v_{x,nt}
and
v_{y,nt}
are the corresponding velocity components. The binary activity vector is

\mathbf{a}_{t}=[a_{1t},\ldots,a_{Nt}]^{\top}
, where
a_{nt}=1
indicates that slot
n
is valid. In this paper,
\mathbf{a}_{t}
is treated as a given activity pattern in the experiments, and the particle filter estimates the kinematic states. Therefore, no activity transition prior is used in the experimental evaluation.

Conditioned on the given activity variables, the kinematic transition factorizes as

p(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{a}_{t},\mathbf{a}_{t-1})=\prod_{n=1}^{N}p(\mathbf{x}_{nt}\mid\mathbf{x}_{n,t-1},a_{nt},a_{n,t-1}).

A surviving target follows the motion density
p_{m}(\mathbf{x}_{nt}\mid\mathbf{x}_{n,t-1})
, and a newly born target is initialized from a birth density
p_{b}(\mathbf{x}_{nt})
. When a slot is invalid, its kinematic state does not affect the observation likelihood. It is therefore irrelevant until the slot becomes valid again and is reinitialized from the birth density. These equations specify the general TBD state model. In the experiments, the activity pattern is fixed and supplied to both filters so that the evaluation isolates the effect of the observation likelihood.

2.2
Subspace observation model

We operate in the short-time Fourier transform (STFT) domain, where the observation in each time frame
t
consists of sensor data across
F
frequency bins. The sensor data
\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}_{tf}\in\mathbb{C}^{M}
at the frequency bin
f
are modeled by

\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}_{tf}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{nt}s_{ntf}\mathbf{h}_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{nt})+\mathbf{v}_{tf},

(1)

where
M
is the number of sensors,
\mathbf{h}_{f}
is a known function that maps a target’s kinematic state to its steering vector at frequency bin
f
, and
\mathbf{v}_{tf}
is observation noise. The scalar
s_{ntf}\in\mathbb{C}
is the unknown emitted signal of target slot
n
at time-frequency bin
(t,f)
.

Let

\mathbf{z}_{tf}\coloneqq{\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}_{tf}}/{\|\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}_{tf}\|_{2}}
be the normalized (unit-norm) sensor data when
\|\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}_{tf}\|_{2}>0
. We concatenate all frequency-bin observations into a single vector

\mathbf{z}_{t}=[\mathbf{z}_{t1}^{\mathsf{T}},\ldots,\mathbf{z}_{tF}^{\mathsf{T}}]^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{C}^{MF}
, which serves as the observation in the proposed PF. Given
\mathbf{x}_{t}
and
\mathbf{a}_{t}
, we construct the mixing matrix
\mathbf{H}_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{a}_{t})
for each frequency
f
by stacking the steering vectors
\mathbf{h}_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{nt})
for all valid target slots. We then form the orthogonal projector onto the signal subspace
\mathcal{R}[\mathbf{H}_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{a}_{t})]
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{a}_{t})=\begin{cases}\mathbf{H}_{f}(\mathbf{H}_{f}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{H}_{f})^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{f}^{\mathsf{H}},&\mathbf{a}_{t}\neq\mathbf{0},\\
\mathbf{O},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(2)

Here, the inverse assumes that the valid steering vectors are linearly independent, i.e.,
\mathbf{H}_{f}
has full column rank. In implementation, a Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse can be used if
\mathbf{H}_{f}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{H}_{f}
is ill-conditioned.
The observation likelihood is modeled as a product of complex Bingham densities

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{a}_{t}\sim\prod_{f=1}^{F}\mathcal{B}\Big(\mathbf{z}_{tf}\,\big|\,\kappa_{f}\mathbf{P}_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{a}_{t})\Big),

(3)

where
\mathcal{B}(\bm{z}\mid\bm{\Sigma})\propto\exp(\mathbf{z}^{\textsf{H}}\bm{\Sigma}\mathbf{z})
is the complex Bingham density. We set
\kappa_{f}=10
for all frequencies in the experiments after preliminary tuning. Sensitivity analysis for this concentration parameter is left for future work.

For the fixed activity pattern used in the experiments, the rank of
\mathbf{P}_{f}
is fixed at each frame, and the normalizing constant of the Bingham density is common to particles with the same activity pattern. It can therefore be omitted in the particle weight comparison. If the activity pattern is inferred jointly with the kinematic states, however, this rank-dependent normalizing constant should be retained to avoid favoring hypotheses with higher-dimensional subspaces.

This observation likelihood depends only on the alignment between the observation
\mathbf{z}_{tf}
and the subspace spanned by the valid targets’ steering vectors. For comparison, the model used in conventional superpositional TBD
[
9
]
can be written as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{a}_{t}\sim p_{v}\!\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}_{tf}-\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{nt}\mathbf{h}_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{nt})\right),

(4)

where
p_{v}
is the noise density. This model is suitable when the target contribution is determined by the kinematic state, but it is mismatched in passive sensing because the actual contribution is
s_{ntf}\mathbf{h}_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{nt})
and depends on the unknown emitted signal.

2.3
Subspace TBD-PF algorithm

We perform inference using an auxiliary PF
[
20
,
22
]
. At each time step, particles are propagated by the motion and birth models, auxiliary weights are computed from the predictive Bingham subspace likelihood in (
3
), and ancestor particles are resampled before the final importance weight update. Thus the proposed PF retains the TBD advantage of operating directly on sensor data while using an observation model that avoids explicit estimation of emitted-signal coefficients for passive mixtures.

3
Relation to Prior Art

MTT and superpositional TBD.

Classical MTT includes association-based methods
[
21
,
6
,
25
]
and random finite set (RFS) filters
[
13
,
14
,
27
,
26
]
. Earlier direct-measurement MTT/TBD methods avoided thresholded detections by evaluating multi-target likelihoods directly from image or sensor data
[
11
,
28
]
. In particular,
[
11
]
used an association-free likelihood that can account for multiple targets contributing to the same sensor cell, whereas
[
28
]
developed a RFS-based filter for image observations under a non-overlap assumption. Superpositional-sensor RFS filters, in which the observation is modeled as the additive composition of target contributions and noise, were further studied in
[
15
,
16
,
18
]
.
Nannuru et al.
[
18
]
introduced a tractable PF approximation of superpositional PHD/CPHD filters, and Ito and Godsill
[
9
]
studied a related PF-based superpositional TBD model under non-Gaussian noise. These conventional deterministic-contribution models evaluate the fit between the unnormalized observation and a sum of target contributions determined by the hypothesized states. Such models are appropriate when the target return or intensity is specified by the kinematic state, but they are mismatched for passive sensing because the emitted signal coefficients are unknown nuisance variables. Our model avoids requiring specified emitted-signal coefficients by normalizing the multichannel observation and scoring only its alignment with the subspace spanned by the valid targets’ steering vectors. Compared with the subspace likelihood of Orton and Fitzgerald
[
19
]
, we formulate this alignment as a complex Bingham likelihood for normalized complex STFT observations and integrate it into an auxiliary PF for passive multi-target TBD.

Subspace and Bingham modeling.

Orton and Fitzgerald derived a subspace-based likelihood up to a normalizing constant by integrating out unknown emitted signals in multi-target TBD
[
19
]
. Bingham distributions have been used in directional statistics and tracking
[
3
,
5
,
8
,
7
,
12
,
4
]
, and complex Bingham-type models have been used for shape analysis, subspace tracking, and Bayesian subspace estimation
[
10
,
17
,
23
,
2
,
1
]
. Our contribution differs in that we use a complex Bingham likelihood on normalized passive multichannel mixtures so that the filter estimates kinematic states whose steering-vector subspaces explain the observed mixtures without explicitly estimating the emitted target signals.

4
Experimental Validation

4.1
Conditions

We simulated a
3\,\mathrm{m}\times 3\,\mathrm{m}
square room with
M=40
microphones placed uniformly along the room perimeter. The speed of sound was
c=343\,\mathrm{m/s}
. The sensor data consisted of
T=200
frames with a time step of
\Delta t=128\,\mathrm{ms}
, corresponding to a total duration of
25.6\,\mathrm{s}
.

We compared two PF variants.
Both methods used the same particle filter structure, motion model, boundary treatment, particle counts, and the same given activity schedule. They differed only in the observation likelihood.

1.

Proposed subspace likelihood:

For a hypothesized multi-target state, the steering vectors of the valid targets were used to form the mixing matrix

\mathbf{H}_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{a}_{t})
. The normalized sensor observation
\mathbf{z}_{tf}
was projected onto the span of these steering vectors using

\mathbf{P}_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{a}_{t})
. The likelihood was then computed from the projection energy aggregated over frequency:

p(\mathbf{z}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{a}_{t})\propto\exp\left(\sum_{f=1}^{F}\kappa_{f}\left\|\mathbf{P}_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{a}_{t})\mathbf{z}_{tf}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right).

2.

Deterministic-contribution baseline:

The baseline used a conventional deterministic-contribution likelihood inspired by superpositional TBD models. It compared the unnormalized sensor data

\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}_{tf}
with the predicted sum of valid targets’ steering-vector contributions. Specifically, it used

\mathbf{r}_{tf}=\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}_{tf}-\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_{nt}\mathbf{h}_{f}(\mathbf{x}_{nt})

and

p(\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{a}_{t})\propto\exp\left(-\sum_{f=1}^{F}\frac{\|\mathbf{r}_{tf}\|_{2}^{2}}{\sigma_{v}^{2}}\right).

Here,
\sigma_{v}^{2}
was set according to the simulation SNR. This baseline assumes that the sensor contribution is determined by the kinematic state and does not include the unknown emitted coefficients
s_{ntf}
. It is therefore used to quantify the model mismatch that arises when a deterministic-contribution TBD likelihood is applied to passive mixtures.

We set
N=2
, so the filter maintained two target slots and represented at most two targets. In this experiment, each target state was
\mathbf{x}_{nt}=[\mathbf{p}_{nt}^{\mathsf{T}},\mathbf{v}_{nt}^{\mathsf{T}}]^{\mathsf{T}}
with two-dimensional position
\mathbf{p}_{nt}
and velocity
\mathbf{v}_{nt}
. As in
[
9
]
, surviving targets followed a nearly constant velocity model,

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{nt}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_{n,t-1}+\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_{nt},\quad\mathbf{u}_{nt}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},q^{2}\mathbf{I}_{2}),

(5)

where
\mathbf{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{2}&\Delta t\mathbf{I}_{2}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{I}_{2}\end{bmatrix}
,
\mathbf{B}=\begin{bmatrix}(\Delta t^{2}/2)\mathbf{I}_{2}\\
\Delta t\mathbf{I}_{2}\end{bmatrix}
, and
q=0.09
. Birth positions were sampled uniformly in the room, and birth velocities were sampled from a zero-mean Gaussian distribution with standard deviation
0.5\,\mathrm{m/s}
in each coordinate. Trajectories were generated so that positions remained inside the room whenever the corresponding target was valid. For frames
t=0,\ldots,99
, exactly one target was valid. From frame
t=100
onward, two targets were valid. This schedule was assumed given to the filter in both the proposed and conventional methods. Accordingly, the evaluation focused on kinematic estimation under a given activity pattern rather than on joint activity estimation.

We directly simulated signals in the STFT domain rather than from time-domain waveforms. The STFT configuration corresponds to a sampling rate of
8\,\mathrm{kHz}
with a 1024-sample frame, a 1024-sample hop, and a 1024-point discrete Fourier transform. To prevent spatial aliasing, we retained
F=61
frequency bins linearly spaced from
101.6
to
570.3\,\mathrm{Hz}
. For each frame and frequency, the microphone signals were generated as the sum of anechoic spherical-wave contributions of the signals emitted by the valid targets, each modeled as circular white complex Gaussian noise, together with additive complex Gaussian sensor noise. The spatial covariance of the sensor noise at frequency
f
was defined by a diffuse-field sinc kernel: the covariance between microphones
m
and
m^{\prime}
was proportional to
\mathrm{sinc}(2\pi fr_{mm^{\prime}}/c)
, where
\operatorname{sinc}x=\sin x/x
and
r_{mm^{\prime}}
is the microphone spacing. To generate correlated sensor noise, a small diagonal loading was added to the covariance matrix before matrix factorization. For the trajectory plots, the SNR was set to
-10\,\mathrm{dB}
. For the root mean square error (RMSE) comparison, we varied the SNR over
\{-10,0,10\}\,\mathrm{dB}
.

We used
n_{\text{p}}=2000
particles for the trajectory examples and
n_{\text{p}}\in\{2000,4000,8000\}
for the RMSE comparison. Each particle
(\mathbf{x}_{t}^{(k)},w_{t}^{(k)})
carried the kinematic state
\mathbf{x}_{t}^{(k)}
for all
N
slots.
For an valid target slot (
a_{nt}=1
), the kinematic state was estimated by the minimum mean square error estimator:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{\text{MMSE}}_{nt}\coloneqq\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{n_{\text{p}}}w_{t}^{(k)}\mathbf{x}_{nt}^{(k)}}{\sum_{k=1}^{n_{\text{p}}}w_{t}^{(k)}}.

(6)

To avoid discontinuities at the room boundary, particles were not clipped to the room. Instead, likelihood values were multiplied by a smooth boundary factor for valid target slots. If the hypothesized position of a valid target was outside the room by distance
d
, the factor was
\exp(-d^{2}/\tau^{2})
. Otherwise it was one. We used
\tau=0.05\,\mathrm{m}
in all experiments. This soft boundary treatment was applied to both the proposed and conventional PF variants.

Trajectories were rejection-sampled until all valid targets remained inside the room. The activity schedule used for evaluation was fixed and supplied to both filters.

For Figs.
2
and
3
, a single run using the same generated data was used with
n_{\mathrm{p}}=2000
and
-10\,\mathrm{dB}
SNR. The same target trajectory, activity pattern, and random seed were used for the conventional and proposed methods in that example. For Fig.
4
, five independent runs were performed for each pair of SNR and particle count. The full RMSE grid therefore contains
3
SNR values,
3
particle counts, and
5
trials per condition. Both methods were evaluated on the same generated data within each trial.

The evaluation metric is position RMSE over valid target slots:

\mathrm{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}|}\sum_{(t,n)\in\mathcal{I}}\|\widehat{\mathbf{p}}_{nt}-\mathbf{p}_{nt}\|_{2}^{2}},

(7)

where
\mathcal{I}
is the set of valid target-frame pairs, and
\mathbf{p}_{nt}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{p}}_{nt}
are the true and estimated two-dimensional positions.

The experiments were run on macOS/Darwin 24.6.0 arm64 in Python 3.13.7 using NumPy 2.3.3, SciPy 1.16.2, and FilterPy 1.4.5. A run with
n_{\mathrm{p}}=2000
and
T=200
required two to three minutes per method.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we presented subspace TBD for passive MTT in the presence of unknown emitted signals. The proposed method addresses a key limitation of conventional TBD algorithms by avoiding deterministic assumptions about the targets’ emitted-signal coefficients Instead of assuming a deterministic mapping from kinematic state to sensor data, we modeled normalized multichannel observations in a low-dimensional signal subspace using the complex Bingham distribution. This subspace-based observation likelihood depends on how well the observations align with the span of the valid targets’ steering vectors, allowing the likelihood to be computed without explicitly estimating the targets’ spectral content.

Experimental results using simulated acoustic measurements showed that, in the simulated setting, the proposed method tracked two moving targets at
-10
dB SNR, whereas a conventional TBD baseline suffered large tracking errors under the same conditions. Additional RMSE evaluations over multiple SNRs and particle numbers indicate that the proposed method achieves lower median position RMSE than the conventional baseline in the tested conditions.

This evaluation assumes that the activity pattern is given and focuses on kinematic estimation when the target-emitted signals are unknown. It also uses simulated anechoic propagation and a fixed room geometry. Future work will extend the framework to joint activity estimation, reverberant environments, moving sensors, real recordings, and scenarios with larger numbers of simultaneously valid targets.
