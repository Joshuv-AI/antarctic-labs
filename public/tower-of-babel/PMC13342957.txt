Volar fingertip glomus tumors: Diagnostic challenges and targeted ultrasonography
Authors: Mohammed Kattan, Swailma Shaikh, Abdulhamid Elyousfi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Volar glomus tumors of the fingertip are uncommon and may be overlooked when classic subungual signs are absent. We report 2 women with 4-year histories of severe pinpoint volar pulp pain and cold hypersensitivity, normal radiographs, and no nail changes. Targeted high-resolution ultrasonography at the point of maximal tenderness identified small hyper-vascular hypoechoic nodules in both cases. Magnetic resonance imaging characterized the lesion in 1 case but was nondiagnostic in the other. Both patients underwent complete excision with immediate symptom relief and sustained resolution at 6 months. These cases emphasize that a nondiagnostic MRI should not exclude glomus tumor when clinical suspicion remains high, and that targeted ultrasonography may be a useful adjunct for localization in small volar fingertip lesions.

Introduction

Glomus tumors are rare benign neoplasms arising from the glomus body, a specialized arteriovenous structure involved in thermoregulation and concentrated beneath the nail bed [[1], [2], [3]].

Classic subungual lesions often present with the triad of paroxysmal pain, point tenderness, and cold sensitivity, which can facilitate earlier diagnosis. In contrast, extraungual tumors—particularly those in the volar pulp—lack nail changes and may present with subtle findings, leading to prolonged diagnostic delay.

Delays of several years have been reported, driven by small lesion size, atypical location, and frequently normal radiographs [[4], [5], [6]].

High-resolution ultrasonography, when directed to the patient-identified point of maximal tenderness, can detect superficial hypervascular nodules and provide clinicoradiologic correlation [7].

MRI remains useful for lesion characterization, typically demonstrates low signal intensity on T1-weighted images, high signal intensity on T2-weighted images, and avid enhancement after contrast administration when the lesion is adequately captured [8,9]. However, small size, atypical volar location, absence of bone erosion, and inadequate clinical targeting may contribute to nondiagnostic MRI examinations [10]. We present 2 volar pulp glomus tumors with similar clinical localization but differing magnetic resonance imaging findings, illustrating the value of targeted ultrasonography at the point of maximal tenderness.

Case 1

A 44-year-old woman presented with a 4-year history of severe, well-localized pain at the volar pulp of the left ring fingertip. The pain was sharp, disproportionate to minimal touch, and exacerbated by cold exposure. There was no history of trauma, and conservative measures failed.

Examination revealed exquisite point tenderness at the volar pulp without swelling, discoloration, deformity, or nail changes. Range of motion was preserved. Plain radiographs were unremarkable.

Targeted high-resolution ultrasonography was performed with a high-frequency linear-array transducer using a superficial musculoskeletal preset. The probe was placed directly over the patient-identified point of maximal tenderness, with a thick layer of gel and minimal transducer pressure to avoid compressing the small vascular lesion. Grayscale imaging identified a well-defined hypoechoic lesion measuring approximately 2.7 × 2.1 mm in the volar pulp. Case 2

A 26-year-old woman presented with a 4-year history of severe, focal pain at the volar pulp of the right thumb tip, triggered by light contact and cold exposure. There were no visible skin or nail changes. Radiographs were normal.

Targeted high-resolution ultrasonography was performed directly over the exact point of maximal tenderness using a high-frequency linear-array transducer, superficial musculoskeletal preset, and low-flow Doppler optimization. Grayscale imaging demonstrated a well-defined hypoechoic lesion measuring approximately 2.1 × 1.0 mm. Surgical technique

For both cases, surgery was performed under tourniquet control. A mid-axial incision was used to access the volar pulp while minimizing disruption of the central pulp pad.

Dissection was carried out with meticulous protection of the digital neurovascular bundle. The lesion was identified as a small, well-circumscribed nodule corresponding to the imaging target and excised en bloc. Complete excision is the definitive treatment, and incomplete excision is associated with recurrence [1,2,5].

Clinical outcomes

Both patients reported immediate resolution of the characteristic focal pain postoperatively. At 6-month follow-up, both had VAS pain scores of 0/10, full active digit range of motion compared with the contralateral side, and no clinical evidence of recurrence. The follow-up duration remains limited, and longer surveillance is required because recurrence after glomus tumor excision has been variably reported in the literature and is commonly attributed to incomplete excision, missed satellite lesions, or new lesions. Published series have reported variable recurrence rates after glomus tumor excision, most often attributed to incomplete excision, missed satellite lesions, or development of new lesions; therefore, the absence of recurrence at 6 months in these 2 cases should be interpreted cautiously [1,2,5].

Discussion

Volar pulp glomus tumors are uncommon and diagnostically challenging because classic subungual signs may be absent, and radiographs are frequently normal [[4], [5], [6]]. The present cases add to the limited literature by emphasizing a practical diagnostic pitfall: small volar fingertip lesions may remain occult or equivocal on MRI, particularly when the lesion is very small, superficially located, anatomically atypical, or not directly targeted by the imaging plane. Trehan et al. [10] reported that failure to diagnose hand glomus tumors on MRI was associated with atypical location, absence of bone erosion, atypical pathology, and lack of clinical suspicion [10]. These factors are relevant to the second case in this report, in which MRI was nondiagnostic despite classic symptoms and confirmatory ultrasonographic and histopathologic findings. Unlike many published reports of fingertip glomus tumors, which primarily describe subungual or MRI-characterized lesions, both cases in this report involved extra-subungual volar pulp lesions without nail changes, bone erosion, or radiographic abnormalities. The distinguishing feature of these cases is the use of symptom-directed, targeted high-resolution ultrasonography, with the probe placed directly over the point of maximal tenderness, to localize minute volar pulp lesions that were clinically suspected but not reliably evident on routine imaging [4,7,10,11].

MRI remains valuable for characterizing glomus tumors when the lesion is captured within the appropriate field of view and imaging plane; however, it has limitations in very small volar fingertip lesions. Minute superficial tumors may be missed when imaging is not directed to the clinically tender point, when spatial resolution is limited, or when there is no bone erosion or obvious soft-tissue mass. Therefore, a negative or equivocal MRI should not exclude glomus tumor when the clinical presentation is classic and the pain is precisely localized. In such cases, targeted high-resolution ultrasonography can complement MRI by allowing dynamic assessment directly over the symptomatic focus [[7], [8], [9], [10]].

The main educational point is not that ultrasonography is universally superior to MRI. Rather, the cases show that targeted high-resolution ultrasonography may be a valuable adjunct when the clinical examination localizes pain precisely and MRI is negative, equivocal, or not adequately targeted. Ultrasound is dynamic, inexpensive, and allows the examiner to correlate the imaging abnormality directly with the point of maximal tenderness. In small superficial lesions, careful scanning with a high-frequency linear probe, low-flow Doppler settings, minimal compression, and contralateral comparison when necessary, can improve lesion detection and reproducibility [7,11].

The Doppler appearance should also be interpreted carefully. Although glomus tumors are classically vascular, vascularity may be internal, peripheral, sparse, or absent depending on lesion size, technical settings, probe pressure, and histologic subtype [11]. Therefore, the first case was revised to describe peripheral Doppler vascularity rather than diffuse internal hypervascularity. This avoids overinterpreting the image and better matches the displayed findings.

The differential diagnosis for focal fingertip pain includes neuroma, epidermal inclusion cyst, foreign body granuloma, vascular malformation, hemangioma, painful scar, mucous cyst, arthritis-related pain, infection, and other painful soft-tissue tumors. Although the clinical triad of paroxysmal pain, point tenderness, and cold sensitivity is highly suggestive of glomus tumor, imaging is useful to exclude mimics, localize small occult lesions, and guide focused excision. This report has limitations. It describes only 2 patients, both lesions were small, and follow-up was limited to 6 months. The findings should therefore be interpreted as practical clinicoradiologic observations rather than evidence of diagnostic superiority. Larger series comparing targeted ultrasonography and MRI in clinically suspected volar fingertip glomus tumors are needed.

Conclusion

In patients with suspected volar fingertip glomus tumor and precise focal tenderness, targeted high-resolution ultrasonography at the point of maximal pain may be a useful adjunct for localization [7]. MRI remains helpful for characterization and operative planning when the lesion is captured, but a nondiagnostic MRI should not exclude the diagnosis when clinical suspicion remains high [8,9]. In these 2 cases, targeted ultrasonography supported accurate localization, histopathologic confirmation, and successful surgical excision [1,2,5].
