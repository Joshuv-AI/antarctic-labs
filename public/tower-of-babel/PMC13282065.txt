Recurrent Intrahepatic Hepatolithiasis Associated With Low-Phospholipid–Associated Cholelithiasis Syndrome: An Underdiagnosed Pathology
Authors: Qusai Al Zureikat, Carlos Guarner-Argente, Priyanka Kanth, Nadeen Almasarweh, Reena Jha, Nadim Haddad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Low-phospholipid–associated cholelithiasis (LPAC) syndrome is a rare ABCB4-related cholestatic disorder characterized by recurrent biliary symptoms and intrahepatic lithiasis. A 55-year-old woman with recurrent intrahepatic hepatolithiasis underwent multiple endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatographies with lithotripsy and stenting without durable stone clearance, ultimately requiring right hepatic lobectomy. Postoperative genetic testing identified a heterozygous ABCB4 variant, confirming LPAC syndrome, and ursodeoxycholic acid therapy was initiated with subsequent clinical stability. This case highlights an atypical late-onset LPAC presentation and diagnostic challenges when imaging findings are subtle. LPAC should be considered in recurrent intrahepatic stones regardless of age.

INTRODUCTION

Intrahepatic hepatolithiasis is uncommon in Western populations and may reflect underlying genetic cholestatic disorders that remain under-recognized.1 Low-phospholipid–associated cholelithiasis (LPAC) syndrome is a rare genetic condition characterized by impaired biliary phospholipid secretion, most commonly related to pathogenic ABCB4 variants.2,3

CASE REPORT

A 55-year-old woman with hypertension and gastroesophageal reflux disease presented with chronic right upper quadrant discomfort and nausea. Ultrasound showed multiple gallstones without ductal dilation. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) showed gallstones within a nondistended gallbladder and focal dilation of segment 8 intrahepatic ducts containing debris suggestive of hepatolithiasis, while the remaining ducts were nondilated, as shown in Figures 1 and 2. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) confirmed focal dilation of the right anterior intrahepatic duct with multiple filling defects consistent with 12 mm hepatolithiasis; stone extraction was unsuccessful due to nondilated distal ducts, as shown in Figure 3. Repeat ERCP 1 month later again demonstrated persistent stones, and a 7 Fr × 15 cm biliary stent was placed to facilitate drainage.

Two months later, she represented with worsening right upper quadrant pain, nausea, and vomiting. Laboratory studies revealed total bilirubin 3.2 mg/dL, alkaline phosphatase 172 U/L, and mildly elevated transaminases (alanine transaminase 33 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase 33 U/L). MRCP demonstrated acute cholecystitis with concern for possible Mirizzi syndrome due to a large impacted gallstone compressing the common hepatic duct. She underwent antibiotic therapy followed by laparoscopic cholecystectomy, with pathology confirming acute and chronic cholecystitis with cholelithiasis.

Follow-up MRCP 1 month later demonstrated persistent hepatolithiasis with progressive intrahepatic biliary dilation and new distal common bile duct dilation to 9–10 mm, as shown in Figure 4. ERCP with cholangioscopy and electrohydraulic lithotripsy achieved partial stone extraction, followed by stent placement. Subsequent ERCP again showed persistent intrahepatic lithiasis. Multidisciplinary review concluded that further endoscopic or percutaneous interventional approaches were unlikely to achieve durable stone clearance. The disease remained localized predominantly to the right anterior intrahepatic ductal system corresponding to segment 8 distribution on imaging; however, repeated ERCPs, cholangioscopy, electrohydraulic lithotripsy, and biliary stenting achieved only partial clearance. In addition, the nondilated distal ducts limiting endoscopic extraction were also felt likely to reduce the efficacy of percutaneous approaches. Given the localized yet refractory disease burden and concern for progressive biliary injury and recurrent cholangitis, right hepatic lobectomy was ultimately favored as definitive management. Pathology revealed chronic biliary inflammation with hepatolithiasis and reactive hepatic changes without malignancy.

Coronal magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography image shows moderate intrahepatic biliary ductal dilatation with a focal cystic dilatation in the right hepatic lobe (red arrow).

Given the clinical course, an underlying cholestatic disorder was suspected. Genetic testing identified a heterozygous ABCB4 variant (c.911C>A, p.Ala304Asp) consistent with LPAC syndrome. Family history was negative for gallstone disease. At 6-month follow-up, the patient remained clinically stable on ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) 15 mg/kg/day.

DISCUSSION

This case describes a late-onset presentation of LPAC syndrome complicated by refractory intrahepatic hepatolithiasis requiring hepatic resection. Before pursuing genetic testing, alternative etiologies of intrahepatic hepatolithiasis were considered. Primary sclerosing cholangitis was considered less likely given the absence of diffuse multifocal biliary strictures, beading, or pruned-tree appearance on serial MRCP and ERCP examinations, as well as the absence of inflammatory bowel disease. IgG4-related sclerosing cholangitis was also considered unlikely due to the focal segmental distribution of disease, absence of pancreatic involvement, and lack of other organ manifestations suggestive of IgG4-related disease. Caroli disease was considered; however, imaging demonstrated focal ductal dilation with obstructing hepatolithiasis rather than the congenital saccular ductal ectasia and central dot sign classically associated with Caroli disease. In addition, pathology from the resected specimen demonstrated chronic biliary inflammation without evidence of ductal plate malformation. The occurrence of intrahepatic hepatolithiasis in a patient from a Western population without typical epidemiologic risk factors for recurrent pyogenic cholangitis further raised suspicion for an underlying genetic cholestatic disorder.

The diagnosis of LPAC syndrome is traditionally based on the presence of at least 2 of 3 proposed criteria: onset of biliary symptoms before the age of 40 years, recurrence of biliary symptoms after cholecystectomy, and imaging evidence of intrahepatic microlithiasis or hyperechoic foci along the biliary tree. Although this patient did not meet the classic age criterion, she fulfilled the latter 2 features with persistent biliary symptoms following cholecystectomy and documented intrahepatic hepatolithiasis on MRCP and ERCP. Identification of a heterozygous ABCB4 missense variant further supported the diagnosis. While LPAC classically presents in younger patients, delayed diagnosis and later-onset presentations have increasingly been recognized, particularly among patients with missense ABCB4 variants that may retain partial transporter function.

The ABCB4 gene encodes the canalicular phospholipid transporter MDR3, which mediates phosphatidylcholine secretion into bile.3 Impaired phospholipid secretion disrupts mixed micelle formation necessary for cholesterol solubilization, promoting intrahepatic stone formation.4 LPAC classically presents before the age of 40 years with intrahepatic microlithiasis, recurrent biliary symptoms after cholecystectomy, and comet-tail artifacts on ultrasound.1,5 However, delayed diagnosis remains common and may lead to repeated invasive procedures.6

Although LPAC syndrome classically presents in younger patients, later-onset presentations are increasingly recognized and should not exclude the diagnosis.1,2 Clinical manifestations appear similar regardless of age at onset, though truncating ABCB4 variants are generally associated with earlier disease presentation, whereas missense variants may demonstrate delayed or more variable clinical onset.2 Earlier recognition is important, as timely initiation of UDCA therapy may prevent unnecessary interventions.1

UDCA remains the cornerstone of therapy, with mechanisms including protection of cholangiocytes from toxic bile acids, stimulation of hepatobiliary secretion, and antiapoptotic effects.7,8 Small case series suggest obeticholic acid may represent a potential second-line therapy in selected patients.9

Untreated disease may result in progressive biliary injury, including ductopenia and fibrosis.10 ABCB4 deficiency has also been associated with an increased risk of cholangiocarcinoma, although absolute risk appears relatively low in heterozygous carriers.11,12 In addition, hepatolithiasis itself confers independent malignancy risk, highlighting the need for appropriate surveillance strategies.13,14 Tissue diagnosis can be challenging, as biopsy of intrahepatic lesions is limited by ductal caliber, and infection risk during cholangioscopy.15

Cholecystectomy does not correct the underlying pathophysiology of LPAC, and symptoms frequently persist following surgery.1,5 Endoscopic therapy may provide temporary relief; however, hepatectomy remains an option for localized disease refractory to medical and endoscopic management.1 Liver resection for hepatolithiasis has demonstrated favorable long-term outcomes, with high stone clearance and survival rates.16,17 Long-term UDCA therapy should be maintained postoperatively given the persistent genetic defect.

Diagnosis relies heavily on targeted liver ultrasound performed by experienced radiologists, which can identify characteristic intrahepatic hyperechoic foci with comet-tail artifacts in approximately 63% of cases.18 Routine ultrasound protocols frequently miss these findings, contributing to underdiagnosis.18 In this case, comet-tail artifacts were not documented, likely reflecting standard imaging limitations. In atypical or late-presenting cases, genetic testing may be particularly valuable in distinguishing LPAC syndrome from other causes of recurrent hepatolithiasis.3,10 Monoallelic ABCB4 variants are typical in LPAC, whereas biallelic mutations cause progressive familial intrahepatic cholestasis type 3.

LPAC syndrome should be considered in patients with recurrent intrahepatic lithiasis or persistent biliary symptoms after cholecystectomy, regardless of age at presentation. Early recognition supported by targeted imaging and genetic testing enables timely initiation of UDCA therapy, which may prevent unnecessary interventions, progressive biliary injury, and potentially reduce malignancy risk. Long-term UDCA therapy should be continued even after hepatectomy, as the underlying genetic defect persists.
