 Abstract

High-intensity particle physics experiments require uniform beam extraction to prevent
instantaneous rate spikes from overwhelming detector systems. By analyzing accelerator
parameters and extracted beam dynamics, we directly inform spill regulation systems that make
real-time adjustments to minimize non-uniformity. This Department of Energy Visiting Faculty
Program project transitioned from characterizing Main Injector half-integer slow extraction for
SpinQuest to Delivery Ring third-integer slow extraction for Mu2e. Working alongside the Fast
Adaptive Neural Control (FANC) group, we developed an automated pipeline that aligns
asynchronous instrument channels, embeds quality metrics, and isolates clean spill populations.
Analyzing baseline spills alongside a dedicated quadrupole impulse study allowed us to quantify
noise structures while mapping time-varying beam response and transit-delay dynamics. These
empirical measurements directly ground digital twin models, supporting FANC’s deployment of
real-time, FPGA-based neural network controllers in the Mu2e Spill Regulation System.

1. Introduction
1.1 Context and Past Work
The foundational challenge driving this research is the need to optimize beam uniformity during
resonant slow extraction for high-intensity particle physics experiments. Non-uniform beam
extraction produces high-intensity rate spikes that saturate detector electronics, reducing
data-taking efficiency while elevating hit occupancy to levels that degrade track reconstruction
and generate spurious tracks. To address this issue, this Department of Energy (DOE) Visiting
Faculty Program (VFP) project was initiated to study the Main Injector resonant extraction
method and optimize beam uniformity for the E1039 SpinQuest experiment at Fermilab. This
“slow spill” extraction method delivers a high-intensity 120 GeV proton beam continuously over
a 4-second window to prevent overwhelming the spectrometer with an instantaneous flood of
particles. To achieve this, the machine quadrupole current is ramped to drive the horizontal
betatron tune toward a half-integer resonance. Our previous VFP work (summer 2025) focused
on characterizing the beam delivered to SpinQuest. We discovered periodic instabilities such as
power supply harmonics and a distinct 73.75 Hz resonance, which we attribute to a feedback loop
in the Quadrupole eXtraction Regulator (QXR) control system.

1.2 Evolution of Objectives and Project Transition

Following the acceptance of the initial proposal for summer 2026, beam delivery to the
SpinQuest experiment was suspended for multiple years. Because the slow-extracted SpinQuest
beam was unavailable for analysis, the original experimental scope became unfeasible. To
preserve the technical and scientific core of the project, the characterization and regulation of
resonant slow extraction spills, Co-PI Nhan Tran transitioned the research to a closely parallel
effort at the Fermilab Accelerator Complex: the slow spill from the Delivery Ring (DR) to the
Mu2e experiment.

Because our goal of improving extracted beam uniformity aligns with broader accelerator
optimization, our efforts were integrated into the framework of the Fast Adaptive Neural Control
(FANC) group [1], whose lab-wide mission is to leverage real-time algorithms and high-speed
FPGA hardware for slow spill regulation. Transitioning to the Mu2e beamline allowed us to
fulfill our core objective of analyzing real slow spill data and testing regulation strategies.

While the SpinQuest extraction utilizes a half-integer resonance over a long 4-second spill, the
Mu2e beamline utilizes third-integer resonance extraction driven by sextupole and fast
tune-ramping quadrupole circuits to deliver beam over a comparatively rapid 43 ms window.
Although the DR features a modern spill regulation system for Mu2e [2], both beamlines face the
same fundamental accelerator physics challenge: the extraction rate is hypersensitive to the
position of a phase-space boundary that must be swept slowly and smoothly through the
circulating beam distribution, and that boundary’s position is set by magnet currents that
inherently carry electrical noise.

1.3 The Mu2e Experiment and Its Beam Requirements

Mu2e searches for the conversion of a muon into an electron in the field of an atomic nucleus [3],
a process that would violate the conservation of charged lepton flavor. The Standard Model
allows this conversion only through neutrino mixing at a rate far below experimental reach; thus,
an observed signal would constitute unambiguous evidence of physics beyond the Standard
Model. The experiment uses the extracted proton beam from the DR to produce pions that decay
into negative muons. These low-momentum muons are stopped in an aluminum target, where
experimenters search for the ~105 MeV monoenergetic electron signature of neutrinoless
muon-to-electron conversion. This search requires an enormous number of stopped muons,
demanding a high-intensity proton beam. However, the instantaneous intensity is strictly limited
by detector occupancy and deadtime, which degrade track reconstruction efficiency and, at high
hit densities, allow spurious tracks to be assembled from unrelated hits. If an individual spill is
non-uniform and contains high-intensity spikes, the instantaneous rate can exceed detector
capabilities and reduce overall efficiency. Uniformity of the extracted spill therefore translates
directly into physics reach.

1.4 Physics of Resonant Slow Extraction

Resonant extraction for the DR is based on a third-integer resonance [4]. The circulating bunch is
an ensemble of protons, each executing transverse betatron oscillations about the design orbit,
confined by the lattice’s focusing quadrupoles. The number of transverse betatron oscillations a
proton completes per single turn around the ring is defined as the betatron tune. Particle motion is
characterized in phase space defined by a proton’s transverse position and its angle of motion
relative to the beam axis. Sextupole magnets apply a non-linear magnetic field in which field
strength increases quadratically with transverse displacement from the beam axis. When the
horizontal tune is set near a third-integer value, this non-linear field creates a phase-space
boundary between stable and unstable motion (the separatrix), where particles inside a central
region undergo bounded oscillations around the ring, while those lying beyond its boundary enter
an unstable trajectory where rapid amplitude growth leads either to extraction or absorption by
collimators. Resonant slow extraction then proceeds by dynamically shrinking this stable region
via fast tune-ramping quadrupoles, sweeping the separatrix inward so that outer particles fall into
the unstable region. Because magnet power supply noise causes the phase-space boundary
between stable and unstable motion to jitter back and forth across the dynamic particle
distribution, even tiny field ripples translate into severe, non-uniform intensity spikes in the spill.

2. Progress

In March and June 2026, the FANC group collected slow-spill data from the DR to Mu2e. During
these runs the group collected data from the magnet ramp current, intensity monitors, wall current
monitors, and the spill regulation system proportional–integral–derivative (PID) controller. These
streams, along with the stored trigger times and ACNET (Accelerator Control NETwork)
reference data were stored on internal MinIO servers. Additionally, in June the group also
completed an impulse-response study where impulses to the quadrupole magnet current were
systematically injected at different amplitudes and times throughout the spill.
To achieve Objective 1, our team developed a data pipeline that processes data from multiple
devices with asynchronous logging systems stored on Fermilab MinIO servers and outputs
standardized Parquet datasets for group-wide analysis. The script aligns these channels to a
central trigger anchor, organizing raw readings into discrete spills occurring on a 15-second
machine cycle. Additionally, the pipeline embeds per-spill quality flags, including the detection
of duplicate DAQ events, allowing downstream users to avoid biased analyses while enabling the
DAQ team to resolve the underlying hardware issue prior to the upcoming data-taking period.

To fulfill Objective 2, we built the second stage of the pipeline to process these aligned Parquet
datasets, isolate clean spills, and produce spill quality diagnostic plots. The framework evaluates
per-spill scalar metrics such as quad ramp peak, signal peak, and integrated intensity, against
configurable threshold cuts. PNW student collaborator Thomas Osborn led the development
and validation of these dataset-specific physics cuts.

To fulfill Objective 3, we analyzed the cleaned Parquet datasets using a custom software pipeline
to characterize baseline spill structure. Led by PNW student collaborator Mathew Prescott, we
quantified both the deterministic envelope of the spill (reproducible structure that is similar from
spill to spill) and the stochastic spill-to-spill fluctuations. We determined that spill-to-spill
variation is predominantly proportional to beam intensity throughout the spill, though this
proportional character is disrupted during the 20–26 ms period within the 43 ms spill. We
additionally observed a persistent 1.4 ms period oscillation.

To fulfill Objective 4, we expanded our analysis framework to the impulse study. The study is
focused on measuring how deliberate, short-duration perturbations (“impulses”) applied to the
quadrupole extraction ramp current affect the measured beam intensity waveform. Impulses are
identified in the quadrupole ramp waveform by first subtracting the median ramp across all spills,
isolating residual deviations from the nominal extraction trajectory. Spills are then grouped by the
detected impulse time, so that spills sharing the same impulse configuration can be compared.
The impulse scan covered impulse times ranging from 1 to 31 ms in steps of 2 ms (all within the
43 ms spill), with amplitudes ranging from 50 to 500 ADC. We compute each spill’s intensity

residual by subtracting the median waveform. To further remove slow run-level beam drift, each
group’s residual is additionally corrected by subtracting the mean intensity in the 2 ms window
immediately preceding the impulse.

We observe (Fig. 1) that the beam response to a ramp impulse is a characteristic intensity increase
(peak) followed by a decrease (trough). The impulse creates a momentary increase in quadrupole
current which drives the tune toward resonance, shrinking the stable area and sweeping the
separatrix inward past particles near the boundary, increasing the extracted intensity. When the
impulse ends and the quadrupole current returns to its nominal ramp value, the separatrix retreats
outward into a phase-space region already depleted of protons, suppressing extraction and
producing the observed intensity trough. We utilize three separate methods of measuring the
precise time of the peak and trough (argmax, HWHM centroid, and parabolic interpolation).

 Figure 1: Single-spill intensity waveform including the response from a 500 ADC quadrupole impulse.
 The raw beam intensity (grey) is overlaid with the median-subtracted ramp residual (green), which isolates
 the programmed impulse. The algorithm successfully detects the characteristic peak then trough response.

The impulse-response study resulted in multiple critical measurements. The magnitude of the
beam’s intensity response is dependent on when the impulse occurs within the spill. The peak and
trough amplitudes are largest in the middle of the spill, indicating that the beam’s sensitivity to
perturbations changes continuously as the extraction cycle progresses. The relationship between
the applied impulse and the resulting beam response is observed to be linear for amplitudes
50-500 ADC. The extraction is therefore shown to be a linear time-varying system. The
impulse-response timing study demonstrates that impulse-to-peak latency increases later in the
spill. It is hypothesized that this latency shift reflects increasing separatrix-to-septum transit times
late in the spill, driven primarily by the reduced non-linear amplitude growth near the central
beam core as the separatrix shrinks, and secondarily by the longer path to the septum. By
overlaying the response residuals across all impulse times, both the amplitude envelope variation
and the systematic delay in lag time are visible (Fig. 2).

The analysis yielded critical insights into the non-linear behavior of resonant extraction. This
scientific deliverable provides essential training data and a validation testbed required for the

3. Future Work

In the near term, we will collaborate with the FANC group to collect a higher-statistics dataset
during fall running, enabling more robust characterization of beam dynamics across a wider
range of operating conditions. A priority will be expanding the impulse-response study to finer
time steps and additional kick amplitudes, which will more precisely map the amplitude envelope
and latency arc identified this summer and provide a stronger foundation for surrogate model
validation.

On the software side, the pipeline will be refined to improve usability for the broader group. This
includes better documentation, standardized configuration, and a second parquet layer carrying
derived quantities to facilitate cross-dataset comparison without requiring users to re-run the full
analysis pipeline. The goal is for any group member to be able to load a parquet file and
immediately access clean, analysis-ready data with all relevant metadata attached.

Longer term, the data collected and the analysis tools developed this summer will directly support
three interconnected efforts: improving the noise model in the surrogate simulation [6]; validating
the surrogate model against real spill data; and supporting the GRU-based controller development
by providing empirically grounded noise statistics and impulse-response transfer functions that
the controller must learn to handle. The primary contribution on all three fronts will be on the
data characterization and validation side, providing the empirical ground truth that the modeling
and control efforts require.

4. Impact on Laboratory Missions

The Mu2e experiment at Fermilab searches for the neutrinoless conversion of a muon into an
electron in the field of an atomic nucleus. Because the detectors accept only an instantaneous rate
below some maximum, a spiky spill forces the average intensity down across the run, while the
high hit densities within each spike degrade track reconstruction and generate spurious tracks
from unrelated hits, costing both livetime and background rejection. By quantitatively
characterizing slow-spill stability and measuring the beam’s response to controlled perturbations,
this work provides the system identification needed to extend the existing spill regulation system
toward adaptive, real-time control. Improved uniformity translates into higher data-taking
efficiency and greater physics reach for Mu2e. The Fermilab accelerator complex and the Mu2e
experiment are supported by the U.S. Department of Energy Office of Science.

5. Conclusions
The analysis presented in this report provides crucial insights into the non-uniform behavior of
the DR slow-extracted beam at Fermilab. Through the development of a dedicated data
alignment, cleaning, and analysis pipeline, we successfully characterized baseline spill noise,
mapped time-varying beam sensitivity, and measured the phase-space transit delay during
resonant extraction. This research directly informs accelerator operations, with the ultimate goal
of improving beam uniformity and, in turn, enhancing the data-taking efficiency and physics
impact of any experiment that relies on the slow-extracted beam. By establishing the empirical
foundation needed to train and validate real-time neural network controllers in a digital twin
environment, this work brings the Mu2e Spill Regulation System a major step closer to achieving
stable, high-duty-factor beam delivery.
