Real-time virtual circuits for plasma shape control via neural network surrogates: dynamic validation in closed-loop simulations
Kamran Pentland, Alasdair Ross, Nicola C. Amorisco, Pedro Cavestany, Timothy Nunn, Adriano Agnello, George K. Holt, Charles Vincent

Abstract
Reliable confinement and stable performance of tokamak fusion plasmas require accurate real-time magnetic shape control. A promising route to reduced latency and increased flexibility in plasma control systems (PCS) is to emulate physics-based controllers using neural networks. In prior work, we have demonstrated that virtual circuits (VCs), which define the poloidal field coil current vectors able to modify each plasma shape parameter independently, can be accurately emulated with neural network models trained on a large library of simulated Grad-Shafranov equilibria. This enables magnetic controllers to accurately adapt to evolving plasma equilibria, in contrast to pre-set VC schedules whose performance degrades upon departure from their reference equilibria. Here, we investigate the performance and robustness of these emulators in closed-loop simulations using the FreeGSNKE Pulse Design Tool (FPDT): a framework that couples the FreeGSNKE evolutive equilibrium solver with a virtual PCS. The FPDT models the coupling between controllers, plasma current and shape response, and actuator constraints. Using the emulated VCs within the FPDT, we demonstrate effective in-silico control of MAST Upgrade (MAST-U) plasma scenarios and show that the emulators are robust in the presence of input measurement uncertainty and under different update frequencies. These results establish the viability of neural network emulated VCs for closed-loop plasma shape control, representing a key step toward real-time deployment in the MAST-U PCS.



I
Introduction

Accurate real-time magnetic control of tokamak fusion plasma position and shape is essential for maintaining reliable confinement and enabling access to high-performance operating regimes and advanced divertor configurations
[
1
,
2
]
.
To this end, the plasma control system (PCS) regulates deviations in geometrical shape parameters (e.g. midplane radii, X-point locations, strikepoints—typically under the assumption of toroidal symmetry) from prescribed reference waveforms by computing the required adjustments to poloidal field (PF) coil currents.
These adjustments are enacted in real-time via PF coil voltage actuation, with sub-millisecond latency
[
3
,
4
]
.
Here, our focus is on calculating the adjustments in PF coil currents using virtual circuits (VCs)
[
5
,
6
]
.
A VC matrix decouples the shape control problem around a specific plasma equilibrium, i.e. it determines the PF coil current changes required to move each shape parameter independently, by one physical unit.

Building a VC matrix is not currently feasible in real time as it requires a linearisation of the Grad-Shafranov (GS) equilibrium
[
7
,
8
]
to obtain the sensitivities of the shape parameters.
In classical plasma scenario design, VCs for a small number of target GS equilibria are calculated offline, to be used throughout a plasma discharge in a pre-set, phase-segmented manner.
As their validity is only local, the accuracy of a VC matrix rapidly degrades as the plasma equilibrium evolves away from the reference state.
As a result, the pre-calculated VCs may lose their effectiveness in producing orthogonal plasma responses, causing coil current adjustments to interfere with one another and potentially drive unnecessary stress on the PF coil power supplies.

Prior work has demonstrated VCs can be accurately emulated using feedforward neural networks (NNs), based on real time tokamak diagnostic measurements
[
9
,
10
]
.
With inference times on the order of milliseconds, NN emulators enable state-aware decoupling of plasma shape control by providing VCs that are continuously updated with the evolving plasma state.
This can improve shape controller responsiveness and accuracy, particularly for complex shape displacements that would otherwise require segmented control strategies with a large number of pre-set time-phases and pre-computed VCs at the corresponding equilibrium waypoints.
This contribution complements an increasing body of work related to plasma control using NNs
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
and reinforcement learning
[
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
]
.

In this paper, we validate the performance and test the robustness of emulated VCs for real-time shape control by deploying the NN models presented by
[
10
]
within closed-loop plasma equilibrium simulations using the open-source FreeGSNKE Pulse Design Tool (FPDT)
[
21
,
22
]
.
Previously validated against MAST Upgrade (MAST-U) discharges, the coupled evolutive equilibrium solver and virtual PCS within the FPDT provide an ideal test bed for assessing the emulated VCs in silico, in preparation for deployment.
This ‘dynamic validation’ complements previous work on purely ‘static validation’ (more details in Section
II
), by accounting for the evolving currents in passive conducting structures and the interaction with other controllers in the PCS, both of which are neglected in static tests.
It also enables monitoring of the plasma and coil responses to frequently changing VCs over time, providing a more realistic assessment of their impact on closed-loop plasma control.

In Section
II
, we briefly summarise the process by which the emulated VCs are generated and how they were statically validated.
We then explain, in Section
III
, how the emulated VCs are seamlessly integrated into the virtual PCS within the FPDT.
In Section
IV
, we perform robustness tests and verify that emulated VCs can successfully control MAST-U plasma discharges in silico.

II
Emulated virtual circuits

In the following, we adopt cylindrical coordinates
(R,\phi,Z)
under toroidal (
\phi
) axisymmetry, with
R
denoting the major radius and
Z
the vertical distance from the tokamak midplane.

II-A

Definitions and notation

For any given GS equilibrium, we describe its core shape and divertor leg characteristics with a set of geometric shape parameters
\bm{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Plasma equilibria are fully defined by the currents in any active and passively conducting metals
\bm{I}_{m}=(\bm{I}_{\text{act}},\bm{I}_{\text{pas}})
; the total plasma current
I_{p}
; and the plasma current density profile parameters
\bm{\theta}
.
Therefore, we have
\bm{P}=\bm{P}(\bm{I}_{m},I_{p},\bm{\theta})
.
Note, however, that the passive currents
\bm{I}_{\text{pas}}
are not actively controlled, and only the active PF coil currents
\bm{I}_{\text{act}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
(
m\leqslant n
) can in fact be modulated.
For this work,
\bm{P}
contains the inner and outer midplane radii
(R_{\text{in}},R_{\text{out}})
; the lower X-point position
(R_{\text{X}},Z_{\text{X}})
; the “gap” between the plasma core and a specific point on the wall of the main chamber
S_{\text{gap}}
(i.e. squareness); the lower outboard strikepoint
R_{\text{s}}
; and the radial intersection of lower divertor leg and a virtual line crossing the divertor entrance
R_{\text{nose}}
(refer to Fig.
1
and
[
10
]
for more details).

The VC matrix
\mathcal{V}
is defined as the pseudoinverse of the sensitivity matrix
\mathcal{S}
, which itself is the Jacobian of shape parameters with respect to PF coil currents
1
1

1

Note that Ohmic coils are typically not included in VC calculations as their primary purpose is to modulate
I_{p}
via a different controller.
for a specific GS equilibrium:

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}=(\mathcal{S}^{\intercal}\mathcal{S})^{-1}\mathcal{S}^{\intercal},\quad\text{}\quad\mathcal{S}=\frac{\partial\bm{P}}{\partial\bm{I}_{\text{act}}}\Bigg|_{(\bm{I}_{m},I_{p},\bm{\theta})}.

(1)

The columns of
\mathcal{V}
represent the vectors of PF coil current changes required to modify a shape parameter by one unit, without altering the others.
The validity of VCs is only local: a VC matrix
\mathcal{V}
will not achieve this decoupling when applied to different equilibria, with progressively degrading performance as the equilibria diverge from one another.
We should note that all of the aforementioned parameters are in fact time-dependent but we drop the additional notation for brevity.

II-B

Neural network emulation and static validation

Here, we provide a brief description of the NN emulators, including the GS library generation, NN training, and static validation, but we refer the reader to
[
10
]
for further details.

We use feedforward NNs to learn a mapping from
(\bm{I}_{\text{act}},I_{p},\bm{\theta})\in\mathbb{R}^{17}
to
\bm{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{7}
(where
\bm{I}_{\text{act}}\in\mathbb{R}^{12}
), which we differentiate to derive sensitivity matrices and corresponding VCs.
Compared to emulating the full Jacobian
\mathcal{S}
, this significantly reduces the dimensionality of the output space, consequently reducing both minimum model size and training dataset size.
Similarly, we also drop the dependence on
\bm{I}_{\text{pas}}
for two reasons.
Firstly,
\bm{I}_{\text{pas}}
itself has limited effect on
\mathcal{S}
, and second, we expect real time deployment of the emulators to take place before being able to accurately estimate
\bm{I}_{\text{pas}}
in real time on MAST-U
[
6
]
.

In order to build the mapping above we used a library of synthetic plasma equilibria.
Starting from experimentally observed “seed” equilibria from previous MAST-U campaigns, we used the random walk Markov Chain Monte Carlo method presented in
[
23
]
to explore the input space and build the training dataset.
This resulted in a dataset comprised of approximately
900
k FreeGSNKE GS solutions spanning the operational space of MAST-U, including both experimentally observed and yet-to-be observed plasma configurations.
The trained models typically contain
4
-
5
hidden layers and
128
-
256
nodes per layer, for a total of
1
–
5\times 10^{5}
trainable weights.
We retain the top eight performing models, which we use to produce ensemble estimates of the Jacobians
\mathcal{S}
using finite differences.
Static validation of the emulators was carried out in
[
10
]
to assess the orthogonality of the plasma response, i.e. verifying that a requested change in one shape parameter would not change the others.
This involved systematically comparing a requested
5\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}
shape parameter change with the observed change obtained when applying either the emulated VC or a finite-difference-based VC from FreeGSNKE.

III
Integration with the FreeGSNKE Pulse Design Tool

The FPDT is a simulation framework that enables a closed-loop interaction between the FreeGSNKE evolutive plasma simulator and a virtual PCS.
Inspired by the MAST-U PCS, the virtual PCS used by the FPDT regulates plasma current, shape parameters, and vertical plasma position, simultaneously enforcing safety limits on coil currents and voltages
2
2

2

Others aspects beyond magnetic control, such as fuelling, impurities, radiation, and detachment are not modelled.
.
At each time step, the PCS takes in equilibrium-based measurements of plasma current, shape parameters, and vertical position, returning voltage commands to be applied to the active PF coils. Together with pre-prescribed plasma profiles
\bm{\theta}
and a plasma resistivity value, these voltages are passed to the simulator and used to advance the plasma and metal currents forward in time.

Focusing on shape control at simulation time
t
, the Shape Controller provides a requested rate of change in
\bm{P}
, computed from the measured shape parameters using a proportional-integral-derivative (PID) controller.
These requests are used by the Virtual Circuits Controller to calculate the requested rate of change of the active PF currents:

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bm{I}_{\text{act}}\right]_{\text{req}}=\mathcal{V}(t)\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bm{P}\right]_{\text{req}}\ .

(2)

When using our NN models, the VC matrix
\mathcal{V}(t)
used by the Virtual Circuits Controller is obtained within the FPDT by querying the emulators, which are provided with the latest ‘measurements’ for the inputs
(\bm{I}_{\text{act}},I_{p},\bm{\theta})
from the simulator.
No additional changes to the control algorithm are required in order to do this.

IV
Closed-loop simulations using emulated virtual circuits

We now perform closed-loop simulations using the emulated VCs within the FPDT on MAST-U-like discharges.
We use a past discharge as a reference and adopt the original PCS configuration for the shot as input to the FPDT.
In order to test different plasma shapes, however, we modify the shape controller settings (e.g. the feedback reference waveforms).

Simulations focus on the flat-top phase, using fixed timestep
\mathrm{d}t=$0.2\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}$
, with the PCS called every
0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}
(to match the real PCS frequency).
The initial equilibrium and plasma profiles
\bm{\theta}
are built using magnetics-only EFIT
++
reconstruction data, while the resistivity profile is determined offline.
In Figs.
2
and
4
we use green shading to indicate when a particular shape parameter is under feedback control and no shading to indicate when no control is applied.
Note that the inversion in (
1
) is performed using only the shape parameters selected to be controlled during simulation.
All simulation results use piecewise linear evolution of the dynamics (circuits and plasma equations), coupled with a fully nonlinear solution of GS—refer to
[
22
, Section 2.2.3]
.

IV-B

Robustness against plasma profiles uncertainty

In the absence of real-time inference for the plasma profiles
\bm{\theta}
, the emulated VCs will need to be provided with time dependent profile prescriptions derived from a reference shot with a similar ramp up, plasma heating profile, and fuelling strategy.
This experiment aims to evaluate robustness to discrepancies between these estimated plasma profiles prescribed to the emulators and the actual real time plasma conditions.
In order to mimic the deployment of emulated VCs during a real discharge, we use one set of plasma profiles in the simulator and a different set in the emulated VCs, which allows us to quantify this residual variability in closed-loop simulation.

In Fig.

\bm{P}

\gamma=$5\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}$

The reference is MAST-U shot
53278

I_{p}\approx$750\text{\,}\mathrm{kA}$
, heated by
\sim$3.2\text{\,}\mathrm{MW}$
neutral beam injection (NBI) power.

In each FPDT simulation, the emulated VCs use the same profile parameters
\bm{\theta}
from the reference shot, while the simulator uses either: the reference profiles; profiles from shot

Shot

As expected, the core shape parameters exhibit the largest deviations between simulations, since profile evolution has relatively little influence on the divertor leg geometry.
Even in the case of a NBI system failure, the emulators are able to maintain robust plasma shape control.

V
Discussion

In this paper, we have presented an in silico validation of emulated VCs for tokamak plasma shape control.
We demonstrate that the emulated VCs can be successfully embedded within a (virtual) PCS framework, where they interact seamlessly with conventional, non-AI-based controllers.
Their performance is maintained across a range of different updates times, confirming that even under conservative real-time constraints, where inference could take a few milliseconds in an operational PCS, they continue to perform effectively.
In addition, the controllers remain robust under variations in plasma profile parameters, reflecting real discharge conditions in which the plasma may evolve away from the reference discharge.
Similar performance is attained when the simulated measurement data (e.g. plasma current, coil currents, shape parameters) contains expected levels of measurement noise.
The emulated VCs also retain strong performance in the presence of evolving passive structure currents, despite these currents not being included in the training process.

Overall, these results show that emulated VCs are suitable for real-time magnetic shape control.
They are expected to reduce reliance on intricate combinations of feedforward/feedback scenario design, minimising the need for expert offline VC computations, thereby enabling greater automation within plasma control.
This validation marks a step towards experimental verification and the eventual deployment of emulated VCs into an operational PCS.
