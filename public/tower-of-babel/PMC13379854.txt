Reclassification of Exercise-Induced Arrhythmogenic Cardiomyopathy

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy (ACM) is predominantly a genetic disease. Endurance exercise acts as a disproportionate trigger in gene-elusive ACM.Case SummaryA 52-year-old male endurance athlete with stable coronary artery disease and no family history of ACM presented with premature ventricular contractions, right ventricular dilatation, and subtricuspid dyskinesia. After multimodality evaluation, “borderline” biventricular ACM was diagnosed. Genetic testing was negative, leading to a working diagnosis of exercise-induced ACM. History of Presentation and Past Medical History

A 52-year-old male athlete of Dutch origin with a history of coronary artery disease (CAD) and elective percutaneous coronary intervention of a proximal left anterior descending stenosis presented for annual follow-up. His family history was positive for CAD but negative for cardiomyopathies or sudden cardiac death (SCD) over 3 generations. The patient was a former professional water-polo player and later competitive in triathlon and cycling, performing approximately 12 hours of endurance exercise per week at the time of presentation and cumulating 15,600 hours of lifetime exercise and 149,000 METS/h of lifetime exercise exposure. At CAD diagnosis, right ventricular (RV) dilatation was attributed to physiological adaptations to endurance training (athlete's heart). At follow-up, the patient reported atypical chest discomfort and denied dyspnea, palpitations, presyncope, or syncope. Physical examination was unremarkable.

Differential Diagnosis

The main initial suspicion was CAD progression. Differential diagnoses included pulmonary disease and musculoskeletal chest discomfort.

Investigations

A resting 12-lead electrocardiography showed sinus rhythm with normal intervals, ST segments, and T-wave morphology, without T-wave inversions, epsilon waves, nor terminal activation delay (Figure 1). Bicycle stress testing revealed a normal blood pressure response (peak: 159/85 mm Hg) and a maximum workload of 243 W (131% of predicted), and the patient remained asymptomatic. Although a significant number of premature ventricular contractions (PVCs) and couplets were observed during warm-up (Figure 2), PVC burden decreased during exercise. The 24-hour electrocardiogram monitoring documented a 5% burden of pleiotropic PVCs (>500/24 h). N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide and cardiac troponin T were normal.

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a normal left ventricle (LV) and a dilated RV with reduced RV fractional area change (27%) and dyskinesia of the subtricuspid region and the RV free wall. right ventricular outflow tract diameters were markedly dilated at 48 mm both in the parasternal long and short axis, fulfilling a major 2010 imaging task force criterion (Figure 3).1

Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) confirmed a dilated RV (right-ventricular end-diastolic volume index 139 mL/m2) with reduced systolic function (right ventricular ejection fraction [RVEF]: 34%). Late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) and regional dyskinesia were confirmed in the subtricuspid RV free wall. The LV exhibited subepicardial fatty infiltration in the lateral mid-to-apical segments.

Management

After initial diagnostic work-up, the patient fulfilled 1 major RV imaging criterion and 1 minor arrhythmia criterion (>500 PVC/24 h), which is borderline arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (ARVC) classification according to the 2010 Task Force Criteria. The presence of focal nonischemic LV LGE on CMR, a minor structural criterion according to the 2020 Padua Criteria, allowed for the diagnosis of definite biventricular arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy (ACM).1,2 The ARVC risk calculator, which has been developed for definite ARVC cases, estimated a 5-year risk of sustained ventricular arrhythmias of 24.9%, supporting primary prophylactic implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) implantation according to contemporary risk models.3 Subsequently, a transvenous single-chamber ICD was implanted.

Panel-based genetic testing of 45 cardiomyopathy-associated genes was negative. Given the patient's high lifetime exercise exposure and absence of family history of ACM or SCD, the diagnosis of exercise-induced ACM was suggested. The patient was recommended to limit athletic activity to approximately 650 MET-hours/y, corresponding to 2 to 3 h/wk of light-to-moderate exercise per week, in accordance with contemporary data in desmosomal variant carriers.4 Low-dose beta-blocker therapy was initiated.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient progressively reduced his exercise load to approximately 500 MET-hours/wk. A follow-up CMR 2 years later demonstrated stable RV volume (right-ventricular end-diastolic volume index: 135 mL/m2) and RV systolic function (RVEF: 39%). LGE and regional dyskinesia showed slight progression, now involving the apical RV in addition to the subtricuspid region (Figure 4). LV function remained stable with subepicardial involvement of the LV free wall, without evidence of subendocardial fibrosis. Adenosine stress perfusion imaging revealed no inducible ischemia, suggesting stable CAD without contribution to the phenotype.

Genetic analysis was repeated 3 years after the initial negative testing, using updated bioinformatic tools. This systematic reanalysis revealed the heterozygous splice site variant c.2357+4A>C p.(?) in intron 11 of the PKP2 gene. This variant had previously been observed in 4 Dutch ACM families, showing a broad phenotypic spectrum ranging from severe disease to nonpenetrance.5 In vitro studies of this variant demonstrated skipping of exon 11, consistent with a loss-of-function mechanism. According to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics criteria, this variant classifies as pathogenic (PVS1∗, PS3∗ supp, PS4∗, PM2∗, PP1∗). The presence of a pathogenic variant resulted in reclassification of the patient from borderline to definite ARVC. Targeted cascade genetic testing was recommended to his family members.

Discussion

ACM is a genetic disease characterized by progressive fibrofatty replacement of the myocardium, predominantly of the RV, and an increased risk of ventricular arrhythmias and SCD, especially in young adults and in athletes. In addition to the widely used and validated 2010 Revised Task Force Criteria for ARVC, the 2020 Padua criteria, which were revised in 2024, have been proposed to better capture left-dominant and biventricular ACM phenotypes.1,2

Distinguishing physiological RV remodeling in athletes from ACM remains challenging, mainly because both may present with RV dilatation and right precordial T-wave inversions, a major electrocardiographic criterion for ARVC.6,7 Endurance exercise promotes penetrance, disease progression, arrhythmogenicity, and RV dysfunction in ARVC and has been hypothesized to be a sufficient trigger to cause an ACM phenotype in susceptible individuals, even in the absence of a pathogenic variant.8

This case illustrates several important principles in contemporary ACM diagnosis and management. It underscores how endurance exercise can modulate the expression of the ACM phenotype, and it highlights the crucial role of periodic genetic reanalysis with advanced tools in gene-elusive cases.

In this patient, RV dilatation was initially attributed to physiological adaptations to endurance training. However, the progressive and asymmetrical nature of the structural changes and the development of regional wall-motion abnormalities were incompatible with an athlete's heart. Relevant CAD progression was highly unlikely because of the negative stress CMR. In addition, the presence of nonischemic fibrosis and the high PVC burden increased the suspicion of an arrhythmic cardiomyopathy. A 5-year estimated risk of sustained ventricular arrhythmias approaching 25% justified primary prophylactic ICD implantation.3

The presence of an ACM phenotype with negative genetic testing in an endurance athlete was consistent with the entity of exercise-induced ACM.8 However, the existence of this entity in otherwise healthy individuals is disputed because evidence of long-term myocardial damage in healthy endurance athletes is conflicting. Exercise-induced ACM patients may represent cases of low-penetrance variants or a polygenic predisposition, requiring extremely high training loads to cause penetrance, possibly explaining why gene-elusive cases with negative family history display disproportionate endurance exercise exposure.9

In ARVC, regular reappraisal of genetic variants is increasingly relevant for variant and disease classification.10 However, specific recommendations on retesting frequency and for gene-elusive cases are lacking. As demonstrated in our patient, reanalysis with expanded bioinformatic tools specifically designed to detect deep intronic variants can uncover pathogenic variants missed by earlier sequencing and analytical approaches.

The distinction between exercise-induced and genetically mediated ACM is important because it has implications for family screening, risk stratification, treatment, and evidence-based counseling regarding exercise restrictions. In fact, studies of mainly gene-positive ACM patients have shown that exercise exposure is detrimental for both arrhythmic and heart failure outcomes.11,12 Avoidance of high-intensity/competitive exercise is important to reduce adverse events in ACM. Furthermore, recommendations to restrict activity to approximately 650 MET-hours/y are supported by data in desmosomal variant carriers, whereas data on gene-elusive ACM are scarce.4 Given the confirmation of a pathogenic desmosomal variant, these recommendations are now supported not only by the phenotype but also by genotypic evidence. Robust evidence-based data to guide personalized exercise recommendations in ACM is urgently needed and is currently being investigated by our group. Finally, the identification of a pathogenic variant has significant implications for cascade genetic testing in the patient's family.

This case demonstrates the critical and evolving role of bioinformatic tools in genetic cardiomyopathies. Although the initial sequencing data were of high quality, accurate interpretation depends substantially on the use of appropriate analytical tools. The identification of the pathogenic PKP2 variant illustrates the importance of periodic genetic reevaluation in gene-elusive cases, for example every 2 to 3 years, even in borderline disease. The identification of a causal variant may lead to diagnostic clarification and improved family counseling and risk stratification.

Conclusions

This case illustrates the critical diagnostic overlap between the athlete's heart and early ACM, the value of multimodality cardiac imaging, and the important role of periodic genetic reanalysis with advanced analytical tools in uncovering concealed pathogenic variants. The identification of a pathogenic deep intronic PKP2 variant through reanalysis has important implications for family counseling and reinforces current exercise restriction recommendations.
