Dietary supplementation with bacteriophages or acidifiers mitigates Salmonella Enteritidis challenge effects in broilers by altering intestinal bacterial populations and morphology
Authors: Hassan Saleh, Omid Jangjou, Masood Didarkhah, Mehran Mehri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The increasing prevalence of antibiotic resistance in the poultry industry necessitates the development of alternative strategies to control bacterial diseases such as salmonellosis caused by Salmonella Enteritidis (S. Enteritidis). This study evaluated the effects of dietary bacteriophage and acidifier supplementation on growth performance, immune response, gut microbiota, intestinal morphology, and meat quality in broilers challenged with S. Enteritidis. A total of 700 one-day-old Ross 308 male chicks were randomly allocated to five dietary treatments in a completely randomized design with seven replicates of 20 birds each: a non-challenged negative control (CON), a Salmonella Enteritidis-challenged positive control (PC), and three additional challenged groups receiving diets supplemented with enrofloxacin, bacteriophage, or acidifier. On day 13, all groups except CON were orally challenged with S. Enteritidis (1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL). Salmonella infection markedly impaired feed intake, body weight, feed efficiency, and breast meat quality compared with the CON group (P < 0.05). Supplementation with bacteriophage or acidifier significantly alleviated these negative effects and reduced malondialdehyde levels (P < 0.05), while also increasing the activities of plasma antioxidant enzymes, including glutathione peroxidase and superoxide dismutase, compared with the challenged control group. Bacteriophage supplementation restored duodenal villus height and crypt depth and maintained cecal pH close to control values (P < 0.05). Quantitative PCR indicated higher populations of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium and reduced Salmonella spp. in the cecum. Overall, bacteriophage supplementation mitigated the detrimental effects of S. Enteritidis by improving gut health, antioxidant status, and meat quality, highlighting its potential to enhance poultry health and productivity in antibiotic-free production systems.

Introduction

The poultry industry plays a crucial role in global food security by providing essential protein sources. However, it faces several challenges, with microbial diseases—particularly Salmonella infections—posing significant threats. Salmonella is a well-documented pathogen linked to severe poultry infections, leading to reduced productivity, higher mortality rates, and compromised meat quality (Khan & Rahman, 2022; Vlaicu et al., 2024). Additionally, Salmonella contamination presents serious public health risks, as it can be transmitted to humans, causing gastrointestinal diseases (Lorenzo-Rebenaque et al., 2022).

In recent years, growing concerns about antibiotic resistance have led to a significant reduction in antibiotic use in poultry production (Khan et al., 2026; Saleh et al., 2025a). The industry has increasingly adopted natural alternatives and feed additives to combat microbial challenges while promoting sustainable practices (Kim et al., 2015). Among these alternatives, bacteriophages have gained substantial attention for their ability to specifically target pathogens like Salmonella, reducing bacterial loads and improving avian health (Abd-El Wahab et al., 2023; Muneeb et al., 2025). Bacteriophages are viruses that selectively infect and lyse bacterial cells, with a lower risk of resistance development compared to traditional antibiotics. In addition, they help maintain the natural gut microbiota, contributing to improved microbial balance and supporting beneficial microbial populations (Muneeb et al., 2025; Richards et al., 2019).

Recent findings further support these effects, indicating broader interactions between bacteriophage-based or microbial feed strategies and host physiology. Zada et al. (2024) showed improved broiler growth and liver histopathology with sprouted wheat grains supplementation, while Sultan et al. (2024a) demonstrated that Bacillus-based probiotics combined with xylanase could beneficially modulate fecal microbial load and immune response in Japanese quails. Similarly, the use of multi-strain probiotics as antibiotic alternatives improved digestibility and intestinal integrity in broilers (Sultan et al., 2024b). These studies reinforce that dietary microbial modulation—whether via bacteriophages, probiotics, or enzymatic aids—can enhance nutrient utilization and gut resilience.

Organic acids, a promising class of feed additives, have been shown to control microbial growth and enhance gut health in poultry (Abd El-Ghany, 2024). Acids such as citric acid, formic acid, and lactic acid help lower gastrointestinal pH, inhibit pathogenic organisms like Salmonella, and promote the proliferation of beneficial gut microbiota (Melaku et al., 2021). Recent studies have also reported that combinations of organic acids with microencapsulated phyto-essential oils (Islam et al., 2022; Onbaşılar et al., 2025), enzymes (Khan et al., 2024), and feeding processed (Nabati et al., 2025) improved gut morphology and increased nutrient digestibility by favorably altering cecal microbial balance. Likewise, Ahmad et al. (2024) found that diet composition itself—maize versus sorghum—significantly changes intestinal microbiota and histology, underscoring the multifactorial nature of gut health management in poultry.

Collectively, improvements in gut environment, intestinal morphology, and nutrient utilization may contribute to enhanced feed efficiency, immune response, and overall bird performance (Adhikari et al., 2020). Furthermore, bacteriophages and organic acids, when evaluated independently, have been associated with reductions in pathogenic bacterial load and improvements in gut health and growth performance (Satterlee et al., 2023). Despite their potential, both face challenges requiring further study. The strain-specific nature of bacteriophages may limit their efficacy, as Salmonella strains vary between farms (Jiang et al., 2024). Likewise, the effectiveness of organic acids depends on their type, concentration, and interactions with gut microbiota. External factors such as broiler genetics, management practices, and environmental conditions further influence their outcomes.

While numerous reports have studied the individual effects of bacteriophages, probiotics, and acidifiers, comprehensive evidence regarding their combined efficacy—especially under controlled S. enteritidis challenge conditions—remains limited. Addressing this research gap is crucial for designing effective antibiotic-free feeding systems grounded in microbiota modulation and intestinal protection.

Given this context and the urgent need for effective antibiotic-free strategies, this study aimed to evaluate the combined impact of bacteriophage and acidifier supplementation (consisting of a commercial blend of organic acids) on growth performance, intestinal health (microbiota and morphology), and meat quality in broilers challenged with S. enteritidis

Experiment design

Salmonella challenge

A pure culture of wild-type S. enteritidis (serotype RITCC1695) was obtained from the Razi Vaccine and Serum Research Institute (Karaj, Iran), a national reference center for microbial strains, and was used for the experimental challenge. Frozen S. enteritidis samples were rehydrated in 10 mL of sterile tryptone soy broth (Merck, Germany) and incubated overnight at 37 °C following the methodology of Gan et al. (2020). The bacterial suspension was freshly prepared prior to the oral challenge. To determine the bacterial concentration, the culture was serially diluted (10⁻¹–10⁻⁸) in sterile phosphate-buffered saline, and aliquots (100 µL) from appropriate dilutions were plated on Xylose Lysine Tergitol 4 (XLT-4) agar plates and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Colony-forming units (CFU) were counted, and the bacterial suspension was adjusted with sterile tryptone soy broth to obtain a final concentration of approximately 1 × 108 CFU/mL. On day 13, birds in the infected groups received 1 mL of this suspension (≈108 CFU/bird) by oral gavage, whereas the control group received an equivalent volume of sterile tryptone soy broth. The challenge was administered on day 13 to allow early establishment of intestinal microbiota and partial maturation of the immune system before pathogen exposure. The selected challenge dose (∼108 CFU/bird) has been widely used to induce a consistent and measurable infection in experimental broiler models (Gan et al., 2020; Saleh et al., 2025b).

Bacteriophage and acidifier products

The bacteriophage supplement used in this study, ProBe-Bac, is manufactured by Pathway Intermediates (Seoul, South Korea). ProBe-Bac contains a cocktail of bacteriophages targeting Salmonella spp. and Escherichia coli, with a concentration of 2.04 × 10⁸ plaque-forming units per gram (PFU/g), as specified by the manufacturer. The product was supplied in powdered form and incorporated directly into the feed. According to the manufacturer’s specifications, the bacteriophages included in ProBe-Bac exhibit lytic activity against several Salmonella serovars and E. coli strains commonly associated with poultry production. The bacteriophage concentration reported in this study was therefore based on the manufacturer’s certificate of analysis, and no additional plaque assay was performed in our laboratory prior to feed incorporation.

A commercially available acidifier, Novicid (Innovad, Essen, Belgium), was used in this study. Novicid is a free-form powdered acidifier composed of a blend of organic acids and their salts. According to the manufacturer’s technical specifications, the primary active components include formic acid, propionic acid, and lactic acid. The formulation also contains benzoic acid, medium-chain fatty acids, and monoesters of butyric or lauric acid, which are included to enhance antimicrobial activity and support intestinal health in poultry. The inclusion level of the acidifier used in the present study was selected based on the manufacturer’s recommended dosage range for broiler diets. This level has been previously applied in poultry nutrition studies evaluating organic acid-based feed additives and was considered appropriate to ensure biological efficacy while maintaining feed safety. No preliminary in-house dose-response trials were conducted prior to the experiment.

Animals and experimental design

A total of 700 one-day-old Ross 308 broiler chickens were randomly assigned to five dietary treatments, with seven replicates per group and 20 male broilers per replicate. The five treatment groups were as follows: (1) a control group receiving a basal diet without supplementation or challenge (CON); (2) a group receiving the basal diet without supplementation, challenged with a suspension of S. enteritidis at a concentration of 1.0 × 10⁸ CFU/mL (PC); (3) the PC group supplemented with 40 mg/kg of the antibiotic enrofloxacin (PA); (4) the PC group supplemented with ProBe-Bac bacteriophage at a concentration of 1000 g/t (P-BAC); (5) the PC group supplemented with the commercially available acidifier Novicid at a concentration of 3000 mg/kg (P-ACF). The basal diet was formulated for the starter (1–10 days), grower (11–23 days), and finisher (24–42 days) phases according to commercial guidelines from Aviagen (2019) for broilers (Table 1). All diets were prepared in mash form and additives were thoroughly mixed using a mechanical mixer to ensure homogeneity. Throughout the experiment, body weight (BW) and feed intake (FI) were recorded for each pen, and totals were calculated for the entire experimental period. Mortality was monitored daily, with all deceased birds being weighed. The feed-to-gain ratios were calculated by dividing total feed intake by the weight gain of both live and deceased birds. All birds were reared in floor pens under environmentally controlled conditions with free access to feed and water throughout the experimental period. The ambient temperature was maintained at 33 ± 1 °C during the first week and gradually reduced by approximately 3 °C per week until reaching 24 °C. A continuous lighting program was applied during the first 3 days, followed by 23 h light and 1 h dark thereafter. Carcass quality

On day 42, two fasted broiler chickens from each replicate group of 20 birds were randomly selected for analysis. The selected birds were weighed, euthanized by cervical dislocation, and exsanguinated. After removal of feathers, feet, and heads, the organs were carefully separated, and intestinal samples were collected. The breast and thigh muscles, lymphoid organs (including the thymus, spleen, and bursa of Fabricius), heart, liver, and abdominal fat were dissected and weighed. The relative weights of these organs were expressed as percentages of live body weight.

Biochemical and hematological parameters

On day 42 of the trial, one bird was randomly selected from each pen, resulting in a total of seven birds per treatment group. Blood samples were collected from the brachial vein and subsequently centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to separate plasma from red blood cell pellets. The plasma and red blood cell samples were frozen and stored at −80 °C. Plasma samples were analyzed spectrophotometrically to quantify triglycerides, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, as well as glutamate oxaloacetate transaminase (GOT) and glutamate pyruvate transaminase (GPT) enzyme levels, using diagnostic kits from Pars Azmoon Co (Tehran, Iran) and an autoanalyzer system (BT3000, Roma, Italy). Plasma malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, as a biomarker for oxidative stress, were measured according to the method of Pilz et al. (2000). Intracellular antioxidant markers were assessed by measuring the activities of erythrocyte antioxidant enzymes, specifically superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione peroxidase (GPx). One unit of SOD activity was defined as the amount of hemoglobin that inhibits nitro blue tetrazolium reduction by 50%. GPx activity was determined spectrophotometrically using the method described by Ahmed et al. (2021). All analyses were conducted in duplicate.

Meat quality analysis

Drip loss was evaluated using approximately 2 g of breast meat obtained from slaughtered birds to assess carcass components, following the methodology of Wang et al. (2020). The pH of breast muscle samples was measured at approximately 45 min and 24 h post-slaughter using a pH meter electrode (Testo 205, Testo, Germany). Water-holding capacity (WHC) and cooking loss (%) were assessed according to the protocols of Gao et al. (2021). Raw breast meat was ground twice, stored at −20 °C for one week, and then prepared for lipid oxidation analysis. The color of the breast meat, quantified by a* (redness), b* (yellowness), and L* (lightness), was measured using a Minolta CR410 chromameter (Konica Minolta Sensing Inc., Osaka, Japan). A 5-gram sample from each stored meat portion was homogenized in 15 mL of distilled water at 1130 g for 1 min. Subsequently, 5 mL of the homogenate was transferred to a test tube, and lipid oxidation was quantified by measuring the 2-thiobarbituric acid-reactive substances (TBARs) as micrograms of malondialdehyde (MDA) per gram of muscle, following the method of Saleh et al. (2018).

Small intestinal morphology

The morphology of the small intestine was evaluated using avian specimens euthanized for carcass component analysis. A 3 cm segment of the duodenum, free of chyme, was excised. This segment was then washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and fixed in a 10% phosphate-buffered formalin solution for 42 h. After fixation, the samples underwent dehydration through a series of graded alcohol solutions (absolute, 70%, and 95%) before being embedded in paraffin. Histological sections were prepared and stained with hematoxylin-eosin. Crypt depth and villus height were measured using a Carl Zeiss light microscope (Promenade, Germany) equipped with an Olympus digital camera (U-TV1X) at 10 × magnification. The ratio of villus height to crypt depth was then calculated (Lee et al., 2016).

Cecal microbiota enumeration

The populations of Lactobacillus, bifidobacteria, and Salmonella spp. in caecal digesta were quantitatively analyzed using quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). Genomic DNA extracted from the caecal digesta was used as templates for PCR amplification. Absolute quantification of the caecal bacterial populations was achieved using standard curves, which were constructed by amplifying known quantities of target bacterial DNA. The qPCR master mix was prepared to a total volume of 25 µL using the QuantiFast® SYBR® Green PCR kit (Qiagen Inc., Valencia, USA). Each reaction contained 12.5 µL of 2 × SYBR Green Master Mix, 1 µL of 10 µM forward primer, 1 µL of 10 µM reverse primer, 2 µL of DNA samples, and 8.5 µL of nuclease-free water. Each sample was analyzed in quadruplicate. Details of the targeted caecal bacterial groups, primer sequences, and annealing temperatures are provided in Table 2. The qPCR assay was conducted using the BioRad CFX96 real-time PCR system (BioRad, USA) with optical-grade plates. The cycling conditions included an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 20 s, primer annealing at 55 °C for Salmonella spp. and 60 °C for bifidobacteria and Lactobacillus for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 20 s (Sharmila et al., 2015). Lactobacillus and bifidobacteria were selected as representative beneficial bacterial populations because of their recognized roles in maintaining intestinal health, competitive exclusion of pathogens, and modulation of immune responses in broiler chickens. Salmonella spp. was quantified as the primary target pathogen of the experimental challenge. Although targeted qPCR provides sensitive quantification of selected bacterial groups ((Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, and Salmonella), it does not capture the full complexity and diversity of the intestinal microbiota. Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using the General Linear Model (GLM) procedure of SAS according to a completely randomized design (SAS Institute, 1999). The pen was considered the experimental unit for growth performance parameters, whereas the individual bird served as the experimental unit for carcass traits, blood biochemical variables, intestinal morphology, meat quality, and microbiological analyses. Prior to statistical analysis, data were evaluated for normality and homogeneity of variance. When necessary, non-normally distributed data were normalized using logarithmic transformation before analysis. Significant differences among treatment means were determined using Duncan’s multiple range test, and differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Breast meat quality

Breast meat quality parameters are presented in Table 5. Drip loss (24 h) and water-holding capacity (WHC) were higher in the NC group than in the infected groups (P < 0.05). Among the Salmonella-challenged treatments, PA, P-BAC, and P-ACF had significantly higher WHC values than the positive control group (P < 0.05), whereas no significant differences were observed among the additive treatments (P > 0.05). At both 45 min and 24 h post-slaughter, the PC group exhibited higher pH values than the NC group (P < 0.05). Supplementation with bacteriophage or acidifier reduced pH values compared with the PC group (P < 0.05). Discussion

The reduction in human cases of salmonellosis linked to the consumption of poultry meat is crucial for both producers and consumers. It is unlikely that relying solely on feed additives will be sufficient to eradicate Salmonella. However, reducing the prevalence of Salmonella in the intestine and minimizing contaminated carcasses can significantly improve food safety (Abd-El Wahab et al., 2023). Several studies suggest that specific feed additives used in poultry diets may improve performance metrics in the presence of Salmonella contamination (Gao et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2016; Upadhaya et al., 2021). The present study assessed the efficacy of commercially available acidifier and bacteriophage products, evaluated as separate dietary interventions, in comparison with antibiotic treatment in broilers challenged with S. enteritidis.

The findings indicate that bacteriophage supplementation significantly improved the growth performance of broilers challenged with S. enteritidis. Broiler chickens administered bacteriophage (P-BAC) exhibited significantly greater body weight compared to the positive control (PC) group, demonstrating efficacy comparable to antibiotic therapy in managing Salmonella. These results align with the findings of Saleh et al. (2025a), who attributed the effectiveness of organic acids and bacteriophages to their antimicrobial and growth-enhancing properties. From a mechanistic perspective, bacteriophages exert their effects primarily through host-specific lytic activity against pathogenic bacteria, thereby reducing intestinal pathogen load without disrupting commensal microbiota, which is a key limitation of conventional antibiotics (Lorenzo-Rebenaque et al., 2022).

Although the efficacy of the acidifier was found to be lower than that of antibiotics and bacteriophages in Salmonella-infected chickens, the enhancement in feed conversion ratio across all supplement groups suggests improved nutrient utilization in the digestive tract and a reduced impact of Salmonella infection. Organic acids are known to lower gastrointestinal pH, creating an unfavorable environment for acid-sensitive pathogens while simultaneously improving enzyme activity and mineral solubility, which may partially explain the observed improvements in feed efficiency (Islam et al., 2022; Sultan et al., 2024). Bacteriophages selectively target specific pathogenic bacteria, such as Clostridium perfringens and Salmonella spp., and their application may contribute to improved gut health by reducing pathogen load, which could potentially decrease nutrient competition and intestinal inflammatory responses, thereby supporting better feed efficiency. These observations are consistent with previous reports (Upadhaya et al., 2021).

The observed increase in body weight gain in chickens fed diets supplemented with bacteriophage may be attributed to the inhibitory or lytic effects on pathogenic bacteria in the digestive tract (Saleh et al., 2025a). Organic acids likely enhance gut nutrient absorption by lowering pH levels and supporting beneficial microbiota (Polycarpo et al., 2017).

In the present study, feed intake remained consistent in the Salmonella-challenged groups, although it was lower than that of the negative control group. This observation suggests that the improvements noted are likely due to enhanced metabolic efficiency rather than an increase in feed intake. Similar findings have been documented in studies exploring strategies to optimize nutrient utilization in poultry nutrition (Pirzado et al., 2024). In this regard, Arafa et al., 2023 proposed that reduced feed intake in challenged chickens may result from impaired digestion and absorption due to inflamed intestinal tissues, which aligns with the results of our experiment. Recent studies have further demonstrated that dietary interventions targeting gut health can restore nutrient digestibility and metabolic efficiency even under pathogenic stress, without necessarily increasing feed consumption (Ahmad et al., 2024; Khan et al., 2024). Limitations of the current approach include the specificity of bacteriophages and the variable responses to organic acids, which depend on their type and concentration (Islam et al., 2023). Nevertheless, these results underscore the potential of bacteriophages and organic acids as sustainable alternatives to antibiotics, offering a strategy to enhance poultry production while addressing concerns related to antimicrobial resistance (Haq et al., 2017).

The gut microbiota plays a crucial role in the health and productivity of the host. Numerous studies have shown that the gut microbiota can provide essential nutrients, such as vitamin E and short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), as well as other health benefits to the host (Wu & Wu, 2012). Furthermore, the gut microbiota is instrumental in the development of the host’s immune system. Alterations in the composition of the gut microbiota may have significant functional implications for the host’s health (Gao et al., 2021). Recent evidence suggests that targeted alterations in cecal bacterial populations through bacteriophages, probiotics, and organic acids can enhance immune competence and intestinal resilience in poultry, particularly under pathogenic challenges (Ahmad et al., 2024; Sultan et al., 2024; ). The effects of these changes will be examined in the following sections.

Dietary supplementation with antibiotics, bacteriophages, and organic acids has been shown to enhance relative organ weights and body composition in broilers challenged with S. enteritidis. The percentage of dressing and muscle weight was highest in the P-BAC group, which aligns with existing literature indicating that organic acids positively influence muscle quality and composition (Ma et al., 2021). Abdominal fat content decreased significantly in the treated groups, suggesting improved energy utilization and nutrient partitioning (Ahmadzadeh & Nobakht, 2023). Such reductions in fat deposition may be linked to improved lipid metabolism and mitochondrial efficiency induced by better gut health and reduced systemic inflammation (Zada et al., 2024).

The weights of the bursa, spleen, and thymus in broiler chickens challenged with S. enteritidis were significantly greater in the groups receiving dietary supplements compared to the non-supplemented (PC) group. This increase in organ weights highlights the crucial role of these organs in immune function and the clearance of pathogens (Kim & Lillehoj, 2019). The development of the bursa, spleen, and thymus, as immune organs in poultry, has been used as an indicator to assess immune performance (Saleh et al., 2018). Recent studies indicate that dietary strategies enhancing gut microbial balance can indirectly stimulate lymphoid organ development by reducing antigenic pressure and promoting immune maturation (Khan et al., 2024; Sultan et al., 2024). Bacteriophages have been shown to reduce enteric pathogens, including S. enteritidis, thus promoting muscle growth. Additionally, organic acids have been reported to enhance metabolic efficiency and nutrient utilization (Urban-Chmiel & Pyzik, 2025). These findings emphasize the potential of bacteriophage and organic acid supplements, each evaluated independently, as sustainable alternatives to antibiotics for improving the health and productivity of broiler chickens.

Saleh et al. (2025b) and Gao et al. (2021) have discussed the potential of using bacteriophage and acidifier feed supplements to enhance meat quality in broiler chickens, a concept that partially aligns with the findings of this research. In the present study, supplementation with bacteriophages and organic acids significantly improved breast meat quality in broilers challenged with Salmonella. An enhancement in water holding capacity (WHC), along with reductions in cooking loss and drip loss, was observed in the P-BAC and P-ACF groups, indicating improved muscle integrity (Sarhadi et al., 2024). These improvements may be mechanistically linked to reduced oxidative stress and stabilization of muscle cell membranes resulting from improved antioxidant status and reduced pathogen-induced tissue damage (Zada et al., 2024).

The reduced pH levels observed in the supplemented groups are consistent with existing research that highlights the role of muscle glycogen in regulating post-mortem pH and the resulting quality of meat (Gao et al., 2021). Lower malondialdehyde (MDA) levels in the P-BAC and P-ACF groups indicate enhanced oxidative stability, which is crucial for maintaining sensory and nutritional qualities (Shahidi & Zhong, 2010). An important mechanism contributing to meat quality is the enhancement of the body’s antioxidant capacity (Saleh et al., 2025a; Wang et al., 2020). Meat color is a significant factor influencing consumer purchasing decisions regarding poultry products. Additionally, the enhancement of yellowness (b*) and the reduction in cooking loss underscore the protective effects of these additives on the color and structural integrity of the meat (Dransfield & Sosnicki, 1999). The findings indicate that the L* and b* values decreased in the groups subjected to the S. enteritidis challenge. Conversely, dietary supplementation with bacteriophage resulted in elevated L* and b* values in the infected groups compared to the PC group. This observation aligns with research conducted by Upadhaya et al. (2021), which reported a significant quadratic response in meat lightness associated with increased levels of bacteriophage. Collectively, these effects highlight the role of bacteriophages and organic acids in preserving muscle quality by mitigating oxidative damage and maintaining post-mortem biochemical stability.

Oxidative stress, recognized as a principal pathological factor influencing animal growth performance, refers to a condition characterized by an imbalance in the oxidative system due to the excessive production and accumulation of reactive oxygen species (Oke et al., 2024). Malondialdehyde (MDA), a metabolite resulting from the peroxidation reaction between free radicals and biofilm lipids, serves as a direct indicator of the extent of tissue peroxidation. In our study, dietary supplementation with bacteriophages and an acidifier significantly enhanced broiler health by significantly enhanced broiler health by modulating oxidative stress, liver enzyme activity, and lipid metabolism in Salmonella-challenged chickens, similar to the effects observed with antibiotics. Antioxidant enzyme activities, including glutathione peroxidase (GPx), were significantly elevated in birds receiving organic acid supplementation, leading to a corresponding reduction in oxidative damage markers such as MDA. The reduced superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity observed in the positive control group highlights the oxidative imbalance induced by Salmonella infection; conversely, supplementation helped restore this balance (Ebeid & Al-Homidan, 2022). Improvement in liver function was also observed, marked by decreased serum levels of GOT and GPT in the supplemented groups. This suggests a protective effect against liver injury, consistent with the findings reported by Coble et al. (2013). Furthermore, lipid metabolism showed a numerical increase in the supplemented groups, which corresponded with lower triglyceride and cholesterol levels and elevated HDL. These changes indicate enhanced nutrient utilization and improved cardiovascular health (Wang et al., 2020). Recent studies have similarly reported that dietary interventions targeting oxidative balance can improve systemic metabolic health and resilience against bacterial stressors in poultry (Ahmad et al., 2024; Zada et al., 2024).

In the present study, dietary supplementation with bacteriophages, and to a lesser extent acidifier supplementation, was associated with improvements in intestinal morphology and pH-related gut conditions in Salmonella-challenged broilers, with bacteriophage treatment generally showing responses comparable to or greater than those of the antibiotic group. Increased villus height and reduced intestinal and cecal pH levels are indicative of enhanced nutrient absorption and overall gut health (Saleh et al., 2025a). Bacteriophages exhibit a selective affinity for pathogens such as Salmonella, reducing nutrient competition and promoting the proliferation of beneficial microbial populations, including Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium (Zeng et al., 2021).

Bacteriophages selectively target Salmonella, inducing cell lysis while minimizing disruption to selected beneficial bacterial populations (Lorenzo-Rebenaque et al., 2022). A significant finding of this study is the notable decrease in the cecal Salmonella spp. population, coupled with a substantial increase in the populations of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium at day 42 following the dietary inclusion of bacteriophages targeting Salmonella. Despite these alterations in intestinal flora, the precise underlying mechanism remains unclear. It is plausible to infer that the bacteriophage-mediated reduction in Salmonella-induced infections, alongside changes in intestinal morphology, contributed to these shifts in the microbiota. Organic acids complement this action by lowering gut pH, inhibiting pathogen growth, and promoting beneficial microbes (Dittoe et al., 2018). Moreover, recent evidence suggests that organic acids enhance gut barrier integrity and have been reported to support short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) production in previous studies, such as butyrate and propionate, which support epithelial cell energy metabolism and exert anti-inflammatory effects (Islam et al., 2022; Sultan et al., 2024). These mechanisms may contribute to improved gut health and may also help address food safety concerns by reducing zoonotic pathogens such as Salmonella (Pelyuntha & Vongkamjan, 2022), although the two additives were evaluated independently in the present study.

Despite the promising outcomes observed in this study, several limitations warrant consideration. First, as this research was conducted under controlled challenge conditions, the findings may not fully capture the complex environmental and management stressors inherent to commercial poultry production, necessitating further validation in field-based settings. Second, the study evaluated specific bacteriophage and acidifier products as independent dietary interventions at fixed inclusion levels; therefore, the observed efficacy may be subject to variations depending on the product’s origin, dosage, and chemical composition. Finally, while our findings demonstrate significant improvements in intestinal morphology and shifts in bacterial populations, the precise molecular pathways and functional mechanisms underlying these observations were not fully elucidated. Further investigations using advanced metagenomic and transcriptomic approaches are required to provide a deeper understanding of the synergistic interactions between these additives and the host gut ecosystem under pathogen-induced stress.

Conclusions

In summary, dietary supplementation with bacteriophage at 1000 g/t mitigated the negative effects of S. enteritidis challenge on broiler performance and was associated with improvements in intestinal health and selected cecal bacterial populations. Both bacteriophage and acidifier supplementation were associated with improvements in meat quality, liver-related biochemical indices, and partial alleviation of oxidative stress. These responses may be linked to improved intestinal integrity and gut conditions; however, further studies are needed to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms. Overall, bacteriophage supplementation appears to be a promising complementary strategy for improving broiler health and supporting efforts to reduce antibiotic use in poultry production.
