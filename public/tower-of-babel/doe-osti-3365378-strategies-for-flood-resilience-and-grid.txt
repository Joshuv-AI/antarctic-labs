Executive Summary
Flooding is one of Iowa’s highest risk hazards, with the potential to disrupt electric distribution
systems, impair critical services, and impose significant social and economic costs [1]. This
report provides a comprehensive assessment of flood resilience planning among Iowa’s electric
distribution utilities, integrating hazard characterization, utility practices, and advanced modeling
to identify gaps and opportunities for more effective resilience investment.

Iowa’s geography, with extensive river systems and expansive riverine flood plains, makes it
highly vulnerable to flooding. State-level hazard mapping identifies flooding as a recurring risk,
but utility-specific vulnerabilities vary by location [2]. The Federal Emergency Management
Agency [3] and Iowa Flood Center maps [4] reveal many electric distribution substations and
distribution grid feeder segments intersect with 100-year and 500-year flood plains, exposing
critical infrastructure to both frequent and catastrophic flood events.

Interviews and document reviews show Iowa’s utilities have taken meaningful steps to
strengthen reliability but often do not target flood resilience proactively. Current investments
emphasize undergrounding, pole hardening, and vegetation management, largely in response to
wind and ice hazards. Flood-specific measures, such as substation elevation or flood walls, are
infrequently implemented, often only after damage has occurred. In many cases, utilities reported
such investments are considered but do not advance because of cost and uncertainty in
quantifying benefits for low-frequency flood events.

Utilities track reliability metrics such as the System Average Interruption Duration Index
(SAIDI), System Average Interruption Frequency Index (SAIFI), and Customer Average
Interruption Duration Index. Some also monitor internal indicators related to resilience, but these
measures are not yet standardized statewide. Flood impacts are often underreported because
many events do not meet Iowa’s “Major Event Day” definition and are therefore not recognized
as high-impact events. Decision-making is typically guided by engineering judgment or board
directives, with limited use of cost-benefit analysis or multiobjective planning frameworks.

To assist with the challenge of prioritizing investments to minimize flood impacts proactively,
we leveraged two tools within the National Laboratory of the Rockies’ Resilience Modeling
Suite: ERAD (Energy Resilience Analysis for Distribution Systems) and CADET (Capacity
Expansion Decision Support for Distribution Networks) provide an integrated pipeline to
evaluate the effectiveness of candidate flood resilience strategies. ERAD overlays flood hazard
data with feeder and substation layouts to identify exposed assets and to estimate failure
probabilities using fragility curves. CADET then optimizes hardening investments to minimize
the combined costs of upgrades and expected outages across probabilistic flood scenarios.

A key consideration in interpreting the results of this study is the distinction between asset-
driven outages (resulting from physical damage to infrastructure) and operational or safety-
driven de-energization during flood events. Even when infrastructure is hardened against
flooding, utilities may still need to proactively de-energize portions of the system to ensure
public and worker safety in inundated areas, including both distribution feeders and behind-the-
meter service. As a result, improvements in traditional reliability metrics such as SAIDI and
SAIFI may be limited, even when resilience investments reduce physical asset damage.

 v

This distinction has implications for how resilience benefits are quantified. In particular, the use
of value of lost load as an objective function primarily captures avoided outages from asset
failure and may not fully represent outages driven by safety protocols. Future work will explore
how to incorporate operational decision-making, such as sectionalization and safe islanding of
energized areas, into resilience valuation frameworks.

For the purposes of this analysis, we choose line hardening and selective undergrounding as
representative resilience strategies for quantitative simulation. These measures are commonly
implemented by Iowa utilities and can be parameterized directly within CADET using cost and
performance relationships. Substation elevation, protection, and relocation, although often the
most effective long-term flood mitigation options, are highly site specific and are not included in
this analysis because of the limitations in available asset elevation data. Future work will address
these gaps and integrate substation-specific resilience strategies.

As a result of our analysis, we conclude pole upgrades to stronger pole classes are low cost and
are consistently implemented but provide only incremental benefits. Undergrounding, although
expensive, offers significant reductions in expected outage costs by maintaining distribution
asset function even when submerged, particularly during severe but low-frequency events.
Substations located in Federal Emergency Management Agency flood plains emerge as the
largest single points of failure. Protecting these facilities through elevation, relocation, or flood
walls results in the most substantial reductions in outage risk, although these are high-cost
investments that may require extension or relocation of surrounding distribution infrastructure.
Finally, probability-weighted optimization prioritizes upgrades that perform well across the most
likely flood scenarios rather than focusing solely on worst-case events, with expanded scenario
sampling leading to more diverse investment portfolios that include higher-cost strategies such as
undergrounding.

Our research results in the following cross-cutting lessons:

 • Data gaps constrain planning: Outdated flood plain maps, sparse gauge coverage, missing
 substation elevation data, and limited fragility curves for distribution assets introduce
 uncertainty into resilience assessments.
 • State and utility priorities are not aligned: Although the State Hazard Mitigation Plan
 identifies flooding as a key risk, utilities focus mainly on risks from wind and ice.
 Opportunities exist for improved coordination between state and utility planning practices
 to ensure flood resilience considerations are systematically integrated into investment
 planning.
 • Utilities rely on reliability metrics and qualitative judgment: Although some also track
 internal indicators related to resilience, standardized resilience-specific metrics and
 formal cost-benefit frameworks are rarely applied.
 • Operational constraints matter: Smaller utilities face financial and staffing limitations,
 slower access to disaster recovery funding, and logistical challenges during rural
 restoration. These constraints highlight the importance of shifting from reactive recovery
 toward more proactive resilience investments.
 • Resilience requires a layered strategy: Effective resilience planning benefits from a mix
 of investment types, including feeder hardening to strengthen reliability and targeted
 substation upgrades, informed by coordinated state–utility planning, to address

 vi

 catastrophic flood risks. Given the high cost of certain measures, particularly substation-
 focused investments, it is also important to right-size investments to balance resilience
 benefits with affordability and potential rate impacts.
This Iowa case study demonstrates the importance of integrating hazard maps, utility data, and
advanced decision-support tools for resilience planning. The National Laboratory of the Rockies’
resilience investment modeling framework, with its ERAD-CADET workflow, illustrates how
such integration can guide cost-effective strategies for flood risk reduction. Extending this
approach to consider multiple hazards such as wildfire, earthquake, and severe wind by adapting
fragility models and hazard data simultaneously would help align state and utility priorities and
identify investments that deliver both near-term reliability and long-term resilience.

In summary, Iowa’s utilities are making steady progress in strengthening their systems, but flood
resilience remains a gap. Substations in flood plains represent the most urgent vulnerability, and
data-driven, multiobjective frameworks can help utilities move from reactive rebuilding toward
proactive investment. The combined insights from this report provide a roadmap for improving
resilience in Iowa and serve as a model for other states facing similar risks.

1 Introduction
This report provides a foundational assessment of flood resilience planning among Iowa’s
electric distribution utilities, focusing on how resilience is currently measured, evaluated, and
implemented at both the state and utility levels. We rely on the definition of resilience provided
by the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR): “The ability to anticipate, prepare for, and
adapt to changing conditions and withstand, respond to, and recover rapidly from disruptions
through adaptable and holistic planning and technical solutions” [5]. We also draw from Institute
of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) 1366-2022 to differentiate resilience from
reliability in electric distribution systems. System resilience pertains to performance during
“major events,” or “an event that exceeds reasonable design and/or operational limits of the
electric power system” [6]. System reliability refers to performance during normal operating
conditions. Reliability objectives have been integrated into distribution utility planning practices
for decades, but specific resilience objectives are rarely mandated or addressed separately from
reliability [7].

Many infrastructure investments can advance both utility reliability and resilience goals. For
example, undergrounding can reduce the number of outages: preventing debris from occasionally
making contact with conductors and protecting assets from severe weather hazards such as
derechos and floods. However, resilience-specific investments are designed to enhance grid
performance during high-impact, low-frequency events.

Flooding and other weather hazards present increasing risk to Iowa’s electric grid, requiring
utilities to adapt both planning and operations to reduce vulnerability and improve recovery.
Flooding is one of Iowa’s most persistent and high-impact hazards, with the potential to disrupt
electric distribution systems, impair critical services, and impose significant social and economic
costs [2]. Iowa’s geography, characterized by extensive river systems and expansive flood plains,
contributes to widespread exposure of electric infrastructure to flood risk [1]. Geospatial analysis
of the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) and Iowa Flood Center data indicates a
number of distribution substations and feeder segments intersect with 100-year and 500-year
flood plains, highlighting the potential for both frequent and catastrophic impacts.

Despite this exposure, utility planning practices in Iowa have historically emphasized more
frequent hazards such as wind and ice. Flood resilience measures, such as substation elevation or
flood protection, are less commonly implemented and are often pursued after major events.
Utilities report that although such investments are sometimes considered, they often do not
advance because of cost and uncertainty in demonstrating benefits for infrequent but high-impact
events. This gap underscores the need for more proactive, data-driven approaches to identifying
and prioritizing flood resilience investments. This report highlights how distribution utilities of
various ownership types are integrating resilience into their planning practices.

The report is organized as follows:

 • Section 2 reviews hazard characterization across Iowa, combining insights from the State
 Hazard Mitigation Plan (SHMP) and localized utility assessments.
 • Section 3 analyzes existing utility resilience planning practices, including the metrics
 tracked, types of investments made, and approaches used to assess and prioritize threats.

 1

 • Section 4 introduces geospatial and modeling-based analyses such as flood risk overlays
 and distribution system modeling with NLR tools to highlight system-level
 vulnerabilities.
 • Section 5 presents key lessons learned and provides considerations for strengthening
 flood resilience planning statewide.
This report identifies effective practices for, existing gaps in, and opportunities to support
strategic resilient energy infrastructure investments across Iowa. Our goal is to provide utilities,
policymakers, and other stakeholders, including regulators, state energy offices, emergency
management agencies, and community planners, with a data-driven, forward-looking foundation
for strengthening the distribution grid in the face of changing climate and hazard risks.

This study builds on a framework developed by NLR through recent resilience planning studies
for various hazards [8], [9], [10], [11], which identified six common components of utility
resilience planning:

 • Hazard characterization
 • Attribute metrics
 • Performance metrics
 • Threat risk analysis
 • Resilience investments
 • Investment prioritization.
Together, these components, defined in the following sections, can be used as a structured lens
for assessing how different hazards are addressed in utility planning practices. By applying this
structure to flood resilience planning in Iowa, we provide a consistent format for comparing
practices, identifying gaps, and exploring strategies to strengthen utility preparedness across
ownership types and geographies.

2 Hazard Characterization
Hazard characterization is the process by which the distribution grid’s exposure to different
hazards is assessed and prioritized to guide effective resilience planning. Given the range of
potential hazards, the process of hazard characterization is often qualitative [9]. This section
synthesizes hazard data from the Iowa SHMP and utility-specific insights to identify the most
critical threats to electric distribution infrastructure across the state. Interviews of utility
representatives were conducted to identify how planning is approached across investor-owned
utilities (IOUs) and municipal utilities in Iowa [12], [13], [14], [15].

2.1 Statewide Hazard Landscape
In this section, we review the state preliminary hazard characterization process. Iowa’s SHMP,
developed by the Iowa Department of Homeland Security and Emergency Management [2],
provides a comprehensive overview of the state’s strategy to reduce the impact of natural and
human-caused hazards. It identifies potential hazards, assesses risks, and details mitigation
strategies, including goals, objectives, and measures to address these hazards. The plan is
updated every 5 years, as required by FEMA, and involves public participation throughout the
process. Natural hazards examined in the report include flooding, wind erosion, dam failure,
tornadoes, extreme heat/cold, and droughts. The high-probability natural hazards in Iowa that are
identified in SHMP and supported by FEMA’s National Risk Index are summarized in Table 1.

2.2 Utility-Level Hazard Assessments
Electric utilities across Iowa face a range of natural hazards, but their exposure, vulnerability,
and planning responses vary across ownership model, location, and system characteristics. Based
on a review of utility reports and interviews, we identified several approaches utilities use to
characterize hazards and inform their reliability and resilience planning. Across all utility
types—municipal, cooperative, and investor owned—hazard characterization tends to be
qualitative and shaped by available data, past experiences, and internal resources. Utilities
commonly engage in activities to characterize hazard exposure, such as participation in local
hazard mitigation planning, internal vulnerability and resilience assessments, and analysis of
historical outage data.

In interviews, representatives from Iowa utilities most frequently mention considering wind
events as a hazard when planning activities [12], [13], [14], [15]. Flooding was not highlighted
as a high priority in planning. Flood impacts are acknowledged and have been addressed after
major flood events. For example, Algona reports adding sensors to underground diesel fuel tanks
after flooding resulted in contaminated fuel and damaged diesel generator units [13].
MidAmerican Energy has constructed a new substation to replace a substation located in a flood
plain. MidAmerican has also constructed flood walls around substations that experience flooding
regularly, and they are actively assessing equipment elevation levels in substations at higher risk
of flooding [12]. Several utilities reported in our interviews that additional flood resilience
projects have been considered but have not been implemented because they were either not
formally proposed or not approved, primarily as a result of cost concerns and uncertainty in
quantifying benefits given the low frequency of flooding and unclear customer impacts. These
proposed investments include:

 • Projects to relocate overhead lines in certain flood-prone areas [15]
 • Relocation of substations impacted by floods [12]

 5

 • Documentation of substation elevations [12].
Resilience project planning efforts often focus on mitigations of common historic hazards.
Utilities often prioritize investments that address hazards encountered most frequently, whereas
less frequent or emerging risks receive comparatively less attention in planning and investment
decisions.

Municipal utilities such as those in Cedar Falls, Iowa, and Ames, Iowa, often engage in local or
regional hazard mitigation planning efforts and have conducted internal assessments of asset
vulnerability [13], [14], [15]. Cooperatives, supported by the Iowa Association of Electric
Cooperatives (IAEC), frequently participate in annual tabletop emergency response exercises,
which are discussion-based simulations in which utility staff walk through emergency scenarios
and response actions without field deployment, and use outage histories to identify hazard and
system weaknesses. Many also contribute to county-level mitigation planning [21].

2.3 State versus Utility Hazard Prioritization
The Iowa SHMP outlines the state’s top-priority hazards using a standardized framework based
on historical frequency, expected severity, and FEMA’s National Risk Index. Although this
framework offers consistency across agencies, our interviews of utility personnel and review of
utility reports reveal hazard prioritization often diverges at the local level based on infrastructure
vulnerabilities, outage histories, and operational experience, as seen in Table 4.

There is broad alignment on core hazards such as wind, flood, and winter storms; however,
utilities apply prioritization based on:

 • Observed system impacts (e.g., frequency of outages, duration of restoration)
 • Asset location and exposure (e.g., substations in flood plains or remote overhead lines)
 • Recovery complexity (e.g., rural access or workforce constraints).
Specific priorities vary by entity:

 • The SHMP ranks flooding as a moderate-frequency, high-risk hazard statewide.
 • Utilities in our study agree flood damage is severe when it occurs but note it is relatively
 infrequent compared to wind and ice events [12], [13], [14], [15].
 • IOUs prioritize wind-related hazards, especially derechos and tornadoes, because of the
 widespread impacts on overhead infrastructure across large and diverse service
        territories [12], [17], [20].
 • Relevant documents indicate cooperatives prioritize winter storms, citing access-related
 restoration delays as top concerns, particularly given their rural service territories with
 long overhead line segments and limited accessibility [21], [22], [23], [24].
 • Municipal utilities tend to focus on flooding and wind exposure near critical community
 facilities, reflecting their service areas, which are often concentrated around towns and
 cities located along river corridors and other flood-prone areas [13], [14], [15].

Across Iowa, electric utilities are actively assessing and responding to a diverse set of natural

3 Utility Resilience Planning Practices
In this section, we draw on utility reports and interviews to assess how Iowa utilities incorporate
key resilience planning components—resilience metrics, resilience investment selection, threat
risk analysis, and investment prioritization—into their planning processes [8], [9], [10].

3.1 Resilience Metrics
Through utility documents and interviews, we identify both performance metrics, which
quantify how well the grid delivers service under disruptive conditions, and attribute metrics,
which describe system characteristics. 1

Performance metrics measure the grid’s success in providing service to customers in disruptive
conditions. These metrics capture outcomes such as the duration and frequency of outages during
extreme events, the speed of service restoration, or the percentage of customers that maintain
service during an event. They are essential for evaluating the effectiveness of resilience
investments and operational strategies.

Attribute metrics can provide a description of a system feature that could contribute to its
resilient performance, such as the percentage of lines undergrounded, or the number of
customers in high fire risk zones. Attribute metrics are actionable: they indicate the potential to
implement strategies for resilience (e.g., increased undergrounding, targeted areas for fire-
hardening infrastructure like fire-wrapped poles).

and USDA Rural Utilities Service guides cooperatives to maintain outage data categorized by
cause (e.g., weather, equipment failure, public interference), location, and interruption duration
[21]. IOUs track similar outage data and report outages, causes, and location type in annual
reliability reports submitted to the Iowa Utilities Commission. In addition to Iowa Utilities
Commission reporting, many cooperatives and IOUs conduct vulnerability and risk assessments
(VRAs) and maintain emergency restoration plans (ERPs). These tools incorporate qualitative
metrics around the criticality of assets, restoration priorities, and exposure to specific hazards
(e.g., overhead miles in tree-lined vs. open areas).

SAIDI, SAIFI, and CAIDI are tracked with and without MEDs. MEDs are often defined so that
the impacts of major events are separated from outages that occur during normal operation.
There are several different ways to define MEDs, however. IEEE-1366 introduces a MED
threshold that designates MEDs when SAIDI exceeds a system-specific threshold, calculated
with a method referred to as the 2.5β method [6]. The 2.5β method is distinct from the method
defined in the Iowa Administrative Code. Therefore, the threshold defined by the 2.5β method
can be surpassed for flood events, and utilities report this threshold has been surpassed as a result
of long-duration outages resulting from flood impacts, but the Iowa state definition of a MED
will not be met [12], [27]. The Iowa Administrative Code defines MEDs by specific wind and ice
conditions or when a minimum number of customers (10% of the customers in the impacted area
or 20,000 customers in a metropolitan area) lose power for 5 hours or more. The Administrative
Code does not include flood-related criteria for defining a MED; flood-related MEDs must result

 9

in thousands of customers experiencing outages of 5 hours or longer; otherwise, the event is
considered a typical day.

MidAmerican also uses statistical methods to define normal operations as times when a threshold
of active incidents is not exceeded [12]. Because floods typically cause long-duration outages
that are spatially concentrated, they may exceed the IEEE statistical threshold but not the state’s
minimum customer or hazard-type criteria for a MED. As a result, flooding may be absent as a
documented cause for any MED, even if it has contributed to significant infrastructural damage.

Across all utility types, resilience metric tracking in Iowa remains focused primarily on
traditional reliability metrics, with limited adoption of event-specific performance measures that
capture system behavior during or after discrete hazard events (e.g., restoration time or
percentage of customers restored within 24 hours). MidAmerican uses number of customers and
percentage of customers restored within 24 hours as measurements of resilience and response
performance for internal resilience planning. Although most utilities collect detailed data on
outage cause and asset exposure, few have formalized these insights into resilience planning.
Interviews with utility representatives and available documentation indicate that across utility
types, detailed outage and cause records are kept. These datasets provide a solid foundation for
developing resilience-focused metrics. Across the board, there is a clear opportunity to enhance
resilience metrics by leveraging existing data, refining MED definitions, and aligning
measurement practices with localized risks.

Interviews and utility documents reveal that across utility types, certain types of investments are
consistently prioritized. Undergrounding is a widely recognized strategy for improving reliability
and protecting infrastructure from wind and ice damage. In Iowa, IOUs and municipal utilities
often prioritize undergrounding, particularly in high-density or critical areas, whereas electric
cooperatives, because of their rural service territories and lower customer density, typically
pursue undergrounding more selectively in new developments or high-risk zones.

In addition to undergrounding, Iowa utilities are also implementing other investments, including
grid modernization technologies such as OMS, advanced metering infrastructure, FLISR, remote
switching, supervisory control and data acquisition systems, and geographic information system
mapping to enhance situational awareness and restoration. Vegetation management in line with
American National Standards Institute A300 and Z133 standards [32], [33], overhead hardening,
and redundancy improvements remain common practices, often guided by reliability
performance metrics such as SAIDI and SAIFI, as well as routine inspections and postdisaster
assessments.

Although investments noted in Table 7 can strengthen overall grid performance and support
restoration, they place limited emphasis on long-duration, low-frequency, and high-impact
events such as flooding. In many cases, investments are shaped by metrics that emphasize wind,
ice, and outage frequency, reinforcing strategies that improve reliability. For example, programs
such as Reliable Public Power Provider reward strong performance on reliability indicators,
which may encourage investments in generic tree trimming or pole hardening measures.

3.4 Investment Prioritization
Investment prioritization helps utilities identify and balance investments that most effectively
improve system resilience. Investment prioritization efforts can target multiple objectives, such
as affordability, reliability, or state-specific energy targets. In some cases, resilience investments
that harden the grid in the face of multiple hazards (e.g., flood and wind) can inform investment
prioritization. Here, we review the various investment decision-making processes and motivators
identified in utility reports and interviews.

 14

Across utility types, prioritization is often guided by a combination of engineering assessments,
postdisaster experiences, and operational judgment. Cooperatives in Iowa use construction work
plans and long-range work plans updated every 2 to 4 years to guide investment decisions [23].
The work plans summarize the findings of an engineering study that identifies the new
construction needed to ensure reliable electric service over the planning period. It serves as a
technical support document for loan applications, contributes to integrated system planning, and
outlines system infrastructure needs. These cooperative utilities also draw on outage data, asset
inspections, and vulnerability assessments to identify high-risk areas or critical assets in need of
reinforcement [21].

Iowa municipal utilities prioritize resilience investments based on a combination of community
needs, local risk exposure, and alignment with state and federal programs [13], [14], [15]. Iowa’s
municipal electric utilities have been able to fund resilience planning with both state and
federally supported initiatives, particularly the Iowa Grid Resilience Fund. Although municipal
utilities in Iowa are governed locally by city councils or utility commissions, many voluntarily
participate in national programs with resilience criteria, such as the American Public Power
Association’s Reliable Public Power Provider program. To advance resilience planning,
participating utilities focus on: (1) tracking and improving reliability metrics (e.g., SAIDI,
SAIFI); (2) conducting annual disaster drills and engaging in mutual aid planning; and (3)
establishing mutual aid agreements to support coordinated outage response. Although Iowa does
not mandate formal resilience reporting, state and municipal programs encourage municipal
utilities to adopt best practices in three key areas: (1) storm hardening, which includes upgrading
poles, wires, and anchoring to withstand high winds and ice; (2) undergrounding feeder/main
lines particularly for critical infrastructure; and (3) backup power systems that are installing
standby generators at essential community facilities. These priorities also align with resilience
efforts led by cooperatives and IOUs.

IOUs such as MidAmerican have used Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory’s Interruption
Cost Estimate calculator in the past but do not regularly integrate its outputs into cost-benefit
analyses [34]. Similarly, Alliant uses a line prioritization tool and inspection results to select line
segments to be replaced and to plan the timing of those replacements. In practice, most utilities
do not rely on formal cost-benefit models such as the Interruption Cost Estimate calculator but
instead use comparative approaches such as evaluating project costs and benefits relative to one
another. Utilities in Iowa tend to focus on upgrading infrastructure that has shown evidence of
vulnerability and prior damage. Postdisaster assessments, such as those following the 2020
derecho or the 2018 floods, are used to reprioritize investments, triggering upgrades to poles,
conductors, and vegetation management programs. In the absence of specific resilience goals,
utilities often use qualitative inputs, such as restoration delays, community feedback, state and
local funding availability, or board direction to guide investment decisions.

Utility interviews also reveal the planning frameworks currently used rarely integrate factors
such as critical infrastructure access or projected changes in flood frequency and intensity.
Although many investments reported in Section 3.3 address multiple hazards, few utilities report
using structured methods to balance resilience, affordability, and state- or utility-specific energy
targets. There is a clear opportunity to strengthen investment prioritization using data and tools
tailored to local flood risks and community resilience needs.

4 Modeling and Analysis of Flood Resilience
  Strategies
4.1 Overview
This section presents a model-based assessment of targeted flood resilience strategies for Iowa
utilities, with the goal of quantifying how specific investments could reduce system
vulnerabilities under varying flood scenarios. The analysis links geospatial hazard overlays with
feeder-level distribution models to demonstrate how flooding can impair substations, feeders,
and switching infrastructure and how resilience investments mitigate those risks.

To support this evaluation, we employ NLR’s resilience suite with two simulation tools:

 • ERAD (Energy Resilience Analysis for Distribution Systems)—A Python-based toolkit
 that integrates hazard maps, fragility curves, and distribution grid models to estimate
 asset-level failure probabilities and quantify the likelihood of system disruptions.
 • CADET (Capacity Expansion Decision Support for Distribution Networks)—An
 optimization framework designed to evaluate distribution capacity expansion and
 resilience planning under uncertainty. CADET enables multiobjective investment
 prioritization by weighing cost, outage reduction, and resilience benefits across a range of
 hazard scenarios.
A combination of ERAD’s hazard-to-impact modeling with CADET’s optimization and
decision-support framework can help utilities move beyond qualitative planning and postevent
assessments toward data-driven, forward-looking resilience strategies that account for
uncertainty. This integrated approach is particularly valuable for flooding, which is characterized
by low-frequency but high-consequence events that are often not adequately represented in
traditional reliability metrics.

Throughout this analysis, “outages” refer to asset-driven service interruptions resulting from
infrastructure failure and do not explicitly account for intentional de-energization for public
safety during flood conditions, which may occur even in hardened systems.

The remainder of this section grounds the analysis in Iowa’s hazard context, describes the
modeling framework, introduces a set of candidate resilience strategies, and compares their
performance across multiple flood scenarios ranging from 2-year to 500-year recurrence
intervals.

To ground this analysis in real-world conditions, we partnered with an Iowa IOU, MidAmerican
Energy, that has experienced multiple flood-related service disruptions in recent decades. This
utility was selected based on three key criteria:

 • Geographic overlap with historically flood-prone areas
 • Availability of system data (e.g., feeder layout, substation locations)
 • Interest in evaluating resilience strategies for capital planning.

The utility represents a distinct service area and system configuration ranging from densely

4.3 Resilience Strategies for Evaluation
Flood resilience in electric distribution systems can be strengthened through a variety of
investment types, including infrastructure hardening, network reconfiguration, and operational
strategies. For this analysis, we consider a comprehensive set of options that utilities have either
implemented in past projects or identified as potential measures for future resilience investments.
The strategies can be broadly grouped into those that protect substations, those that strengthen
feeders and network topology, and those that add distributed or operational flexibility.

At the substation level, utilities have four possible approaches: relocation, elevation, upgrades to
submersible equipment, and physical infrastructure protection. Relocating substations outside of
FEMA-designated flood plains remains the most effective but also the most capital-intensive
solution. In practice, relocation is often constrained by siting, permitting, and cost. Elevation of
existing substations or selective elevation of critical equipment, such as relays, switchgear, or
pad-mounted transformers, is often a more feasible near-term option. Flood walls and berms
around substations and critical switching stations are another protective measure, particularly in
urban areas where relocation is not viable. These interventions directly address substations as
single points of failure—assets that, if incapacitated by flooding, can interrupt service for
thousands of customers.

 22

At the feeder and line level, resilience strategies include undergrounding, selective pole and
conductor upgrades, and feeder redundancy. Undergrounding is widely recognized as an
effective measure against wind and ice hazards, and it can also reduce flood-related risks when
designed appropriately. However, undergrounding in areas prone to prolonged inundation can
increase vulnerability, as water intrusion into conduits can accelerate failures. Therefore,
selective undergrounding, applied in zones exposed to shallow or short-duration floods, offers
the greatest benefit. Pole and conductor hardening, such as upgrading to thicker poles, shorter
spans, or hazard-resistant conductors (e.g., T-2 wire), incrementally reduce the likelihood of
structural failures in flood-impacted areas. Feeder redundancy through looped configurations, tie
switches, or automated sectionalizers provides additional supply paths when one feeder segment
is compromised. Together, these line level strategies help reduce both the frequency and duration
of outages when floods intersect feeder routes.

Finally, distributed and operational strategies add flexibility to maintain service continuity during
flood events. Battery energy storage systems at critical nodes can provide backup power and help
defer traditional grid upgrades. Automated distribution management technologies, such as
FLISR, further enable rapid reconfiguration when assets are damaged. Although these solutions
may not directly prevent flood damage, they improve system adaptability and speed of recovery.

4.4 National Laboratory of the Rockies Flood Resilience Analysis
 Framework
This study applies tools from NLR’s resilience suite to model, evaluate, and optimize
distribution-level flood resilience strategies detailed previously. Two complementary tools are
integrated into a unified workflow: ERAD and CADET. Together, these tools establish a
workflow that links risk assessment with investment optimization and translates flood hazard
data into actionable, cost-effective investment strategies for distribution utilities.

4.4.1 ERAD: Hazard and Risk Characterization
ERAD 2 is a free, open-source Python toolkit for quantifying energy resilience in the face of
hazards such as flooding, wind, wildfire, and earthquake. In this study, it ingests flood hazard
layers (FEMA 1% and 0.2% annual chance flood plains, Iowa Flood Center depth grids)
and distribution infrastructure data (feeders, substations, poles, overhead and underground lines)
to assess which assets are exposed. Asset fragility curves then map flood depth to probability of
failure.

ERAD produces asset-level failure probabilities across recurrence intervals from 2-year to 500-
year floods, highlighting how even relatively frequent shallow floods can disrupt feeders,
whereas rare deep floods drive catastrophic substation failures. Outputs include:

 • Exposed asset maps, showing feeder spans and substations within FEMA flood plains
 • Probabilistic damage states, which serve as inputs to optimization
 • Resilience metrics, including expected outage magnitude, restoration duration, and
 customer-level impacts are generated but are not used in this study.

In addition, ERAD provides flexibility to represent system-specific measures such as substation
flood walls, equipment elevation, and feeder tie switches. These actions adjust fragility

improvements. Although not directly optimized in CADET, these scenarios provide critical
context for interpreting hardening results.

4.4.2 CADET: Investment Optimization
CADET is an optimization framework developed to support long-term distribution capacity and
resilience planning. It operates by defining upgrade options and solving for least-cost portfolios
of investments subject to grid constraints.

For the Iowa flood case study, CADET was configured to optimize feeder-level hardening
measures identified by ERAD:

 • Pole and conductor upgrades (e.g., Class 4 to Class 1 poles, stronger conductors)
 • Undergrounding of exposed spans, where feasible.

probability of asset failure under flood scenarios. CADET enforces single-upgrade-per-asset
constraints and integrates updated impedance and capacity values when reconductoring or
undergrounding is applied.

substantially reduces expected outage risk, whereas pole upgrades are cheaper but yield limited
resilience gains.

4.4.3 Integrated ERAD-CADET Workflow
The integration of ERAD and CADET creates a sequential workflow that links hazard modeling
with investment optimization. ERAD provides the starting point by translating flood hazard data
into asset-level probabilities of failure. Using FEMA and Iowa Flood Center flood maps in
combination with utility feeder and substation layouts, ERAD applies fragility curves to estimate
how each pole, conductor, or substation is likely to perform under a range of flood depths. These
simulations yield a library of “damage scenarios” that capture both frequent, shallow flooding
and the rarer, deeper flooding events.

CADET builds directly on these outputs by embedding them in an optimization framework. The
ERAD-derived probabilities serve as inputs to CADET’s investment model, which evaluates the

 24

trade-offs between different hardening measures. By representing reconductoring,
undergrounding, and pole class upgrades as decision variables, CADET allows planners to test
how each option reduces the likelihood of failure across the range of flood scenarios. The
optimization problem then seeks to minimize the total expected cost, balancing the up-front
expense of upgrades with the avoided costs of outages and lost load.

Once CADET identifies an optimal set of hardening investments, the hardened feeder model
with the upgrades is visualized. This allows utilities to see the “before and after” conditions
showing how outage probabilities, customer interruptions, and expected costs change as
hardening measures are applied. This combined ERAD-CADET framework provides not only a
technical basis for evaluating resilience strategies but also a transparent and repeatable decision-
support process that aligns hazard exposure with cost-effective investment planning.

In the CADET design for this study, the optimization is limited to edge-level hardening choices.
The decision set is “one upgrade per span,” selecting among undergrounding or overhead
pole/conductor class upgrades on exposed feeder segments identified by ERAD. CADET
enforces a single-upgrade constraint (no stacking multiple upgrades on the same asset) and
updates branch limits or impedance when a reconductoring or underground choice is made, while
respecting power flow and thermal limits.

By contrast, system-specific measures such as tie switches or loop closures and physical
substation protections (elevation, relocation, flood walls) are not part of the CADET decision set
in this analysis because of lack of data needed for simulation. Instead, we treat them
within ERAD as contextual scenario elements that adjust exposure or damage probabilities to
help interpret where line hardening matters most (e.g., substations in the FEMA 1% flood plain
as critical nodes of vulnerability). This division keeps the CADET run focused on line hardening,
whereas ERAD provides the hazard-to-grid linkage and sensitivity context for substation and
switching actions. Future work will address these data gaps in resilience modeling and integrate
substation-specific resilience strategies for CADET upgrade analysis.

4.5 Comparative Analysis of Strategies
To evaluate the effectiveness of hardening strategies, the modeling framework considers a wide
range of flood scenarios and investment options, as discussed in previous sections. Flood hazards
are represented as recurrence intervals from 2-year to 500-year floods, using FEMA and Iowa
Flood Center depth grids. These scenarios capture both relatively frequent, shallow flooding
events and the rarer, catastrophic floods that can inundate substations and wide swaths of feeder
infrastructure. Asset fragility curves are applied to distribution poles, overhead conductors,
underground cables, and substations to estimate the probability of failure under each depth
scenario.

Within this hazard context, two categories of strategies are compared. First, CADET
optimizes feeder-level hardening investments for undergrounding and overhead pole/conductor
upgrades scenarios on segments exposed to flooding. These measures were evaluated by
balancing their capital cost against the expected reduction in outage costs, quantified using a
value of lost load assumption of $50/kWh. Second, ERAD provides complementary analysis
of substation-specific measures such as elevation, relocation, and flood walls, as well as system

 25

modifications such as tie switches. Although these are not part of the optimization problem, they
are used to illustrate the importance of addressing single points of failure.

Because CADET solves a stochastic optimization problem, the selected upgrades reflect the

where the marginal benefit of additional investment rises rapidly after moderate floods. Such
results highlight the importance of substation-specific resilience measures.

In practice, these findings imply that although near-term resilience can be improved through
targeted pole replacements and sectionalizing, sustained flood adaptation will require strategic
elevation, relocation, or redesign of vulnerable feeders and substations to prevent recurrent
damage and ensure rapid restoration across all event scales.

The results from the analysis provide a comparative perspective on the effectiveness of different
flood resilience strategies. Feeder-level hardening measures such as pole class upgrades and
undergrounding show clear trade-offs. Pole upgrades are relatively inexpensive and are
consistently selected by CADET across scenarios, but their ability to reduce failure probabilities
diminishes sharply as flood depths increase. By contrast, undergrounding offers significantly
greater risk reduction, particularly in scenarios involving widespread inundation, but it comes at
a much higher up-front cost. The optimization results show that when flood probabilities are
accounted for, CADET often still selects undergrounding because its contribution to lowering
long-term expected outage costs outweighs its initial expense.

Substation-focused strategies, represented in ERAD but not optimized in CADET, emerge as the
single most decisive investments for mitigating catastrophic outages. Substations located within
FEMA 1% and 0.2% annual chance flood plains were repeatedly shown to drive systemwide
vulnerabilities. Even when feeders were hardened, substation inundation resulted in widespread
outages during 100- and 500-year floods. This finding reinforces that targeted substation
measures such as elevation, relocation, or protective flood walls are critical to achieving
resilience outcomes that feeder hardening alone cannot deliver.

The simulations also highlight the importance of probability-weighted planning. CADET’s
stochastic optimization results emphasize investment strategies that are not driven by worst-case
scenarios alone, but rather by the balance of frequent and severe events. This approach results in
a set of upgrades that remains relatively stable across different flood return periods; the model
prioritizes measures that provide consistent benefits across the most likely scenarios. Sensitivity
tests further demonstrate that including more Monte Carlo samples broadens the solution set to

encompass higher-cost but higher-value strategies, underscoring the need for robust scenario
sampling in resilience planning.

Taken together, these insights suggest a layered approach to flood resilience in Iowa. Pole
upgrades deliver low-cost incremental improvements, undergrounding provides deeper
protection against moderate-to-severe floods, and physical substation protection offers the most
substantial systemwide benefits. When considered jointly, these strategies allow utilities to
minimize both expected outage costs and catastrophic risk exposure. The key lesson is that
resilience cannot be achieved by a single measure: it requires a portfolio of complementary
investments, informed by probabilistic hazard modeling and optimized decision support.

5 Lessons Learned
This section synthesizes key insights from hazard characterization (Section 2), utility resilience
planning practices (Section 3), and resilience strategy simulations (Section 4), along with
findings from utility interviews. Together, these lessons highlight both the current strengths in
Iowa’s distribution planning and the areas that need improvement to achieve more effective
flood resilience.

5.1 Data Gaps and Modeling Challenges
Flood resilience planning in Iowa is constrained by significant data gaps. FEMA flood plain
maps and other publicly available inundation products are often outdated, coarse in resolution, or
incomplete. These products are particularly limited for small streams and rural catchments.
Gauge networks provide important depth records but are sparse across rural Iowa and may miss
localized flooding events that affect distribution assets.

On the infrastructure side, utilities often lack detailed information on the location, elevation, and
design of distribution assets. Substation elevation data, for example, were not available for
MidAmerican Energy, which limited the accuracy of exposure assessments. Underground
infrastructure, such as cables and vaults, is underrepresented in publicly available datasets,
making it difficult to assess vulnerability. Fragility functions for both overhead and underground
distribution assets under flood conditions remain scarce and are often based on expert judgment
rather than field data.

Future scenarios add further uncertainty. Flood frequency and severity are expected to change
with climate variability and urban development, but downscaled climate models still struggle to
represent extreme rainfall, and land use projections are difficult to incorporate into hydrologic
modeling. These limitations mean current resilience assessments may either understate or
overstate future risks, reducing the robustness of long-term planning.

5.2 Divergence Between State and Utility Priorities
There is a clear mismatch between statewide hazard planning and utility-level risk priorities.
Iowa’s SHMP emphasizes hazards such as floods, tornadoes, and severe storms, whereas utilities
consistently identify wind and ice as their primary operational concerns [12], [13], [14], [15],
[20], [21]. This difference has shaped investment decisions. Most utilities have focused on
undergrounding, vegetation management, and pole hardening in response to wind and ice,
whereas flood-specific strategies such as substation elevation or flood walls are rare and are
often implemented only after damage occurs. When a proactive approach to anticipate flood
impacts is used to inform flood mitigation strategies, costly losses and infrastructure damages
can be avoided.

This divergence illustrates the need for coordination between statewide hazard assessments and
localized utility planning. Without alignment, flood risk remains undervalued in decision-
making, even though substations in FEMA flood plains present clear single points of failure.

 33

5.3 Utility Planning Practices and Decision-Making
Utilities across Iowa employ a mix of formal planning activities and experience-based practices.
Annual ERP exercises, VRAs, and adoption of construction practices such as thicker poles,
shorter spans, and hazard-resistant conductors such as T-2 wire are common. Enhanced
vegetation management and selective undergrounding are also widely used. Some IOUs are
integrating hazard mapping and critical facility registries into planning, whereas municipal and
cooperative utilities tend to rely more on localized knowledge and operational experience.

Despite these practices, systematic prioritization remains limited. Most utilities still base
investment on engineering judgment, postdisaster assessments, or board directives. Few employ
cost-benefit analysis tools such as the Interruption Cost Estimate calculator or advanced
decision-support frameworks such as CADET. Metrics are heavily weighted toward reliability
indicators such as SAIDI, SAIFI, and CAIDI rather than resilience outcomes. Floods often do
not qualify as “Major Event Days” under Iowa definitions, which means their impacts are
underrepresented in resilience tracking.

There are encouraging signs of progress. Several utilities are beginning to use data-driven
practices to monitor operational risks. However, the absence of resilience-specific metrics still
prevents utilities from fully capturing the benefits of long-term resilience investments.

5.4 Insights From Simulation of Strategies
As detailed in Section 4.5, the NLR flood resilience suite simulations demonstrate both the
potential and the limitations of current flood resilience strategies. The results show that although
feeder-level hardening, such as pole upgrades and undergrounding, can reduce outage risk, there
are important trade-offs. Pole upgrades provide low-cost incremental improvements, whereas
undergrounding offers deeper protection at a much higher capital cost. More decisive reductions
in catastrophic outage risk can come from physical protection of substations located in FEMA
flood plains, which remain the most significant points of failure in Iowa’s distribution systems.

The simulations also highlight the importance of probability-weighted planning. CADET
consistently selected upgrades that delivered benefits across the most probable flood scenarios,
rather than solutions designed only for the most extreme events. Sensitivity testing further
demonstrated that broader scenario sampling encourages more diverse investment portfolios,
including higher-cost strategies such as undergrounding.

Taken together, the modeling results reinforce the need for a layered strategy that combines
targeted feeder hardening with substation-focused interventions. These findings are summarized
here to emphasize utilities should integrate probabilistic hazard data into planning, prioritize
substation resilience, and carefully weigh the trade-offs between low-cost and high-value
hardening measures.

5.5 Operational Realities and Challenges
Utilities face a set of operational realities that shape how resilience is implemented. Many
participate in mutual aid agreements and coordinate with local emergency management agencies.
Larger IOUs have access to advanced tools such as OMS, FLISR, and supervisory control and
data acquisition to locate and restore outages quickly, whereas municipal and cooperative

 34

utilities often lack these systems. Some cooperatives are beginning to adopt them as part of
modernization.

Financial and staffing constraints limit the ability of smaller utilities to pursue advanced
modeling or hardening. Not all federal funding programs are designed to address rapid
postdisaster recovery, which complicates reconstruction in rural areas. Restoration is often
logistically more difficult in remote areas and can have disproportionate social impacts, given the
demographic and economic characteristics of rural communities.

Some utilities report using local hazard plans that incorporate public input. For example, Des
Moines County surveys residents about hazard priorities as part of its planning. However, this
practice is not widespread. Overall, utility operations are driven by the need for rapid outage
response, coordination with emergency services, and risk tracking, but gaps remain in systematic
prioritization and the use of multiobjective frameworks.

5.6 Broader Lessons for Application Beyond Iowa
The Iowa case study provides lessons that can extend to other states. First, the importance of
multiobjective decision-making is clear. Undergrounding projects designed for wind and ice
resilience could yield additional value if flood exposure is considered at the same time. Second,
alignment between state-level hazard planning and utility-level risk assessments is critical for
ensuring resources are directed to the most vulnerable assets. Third, resilience planning would
benefit from integrated hazard data, utility infrastructure models, and optimization frameworks
to identify and prioritize the most cost-effective strategies.

Finally, the methods tested in this study are highly adaptable. By substituting fragility functions
and hazard data, the NLR resilience suite with the integrated ERAD-CADET workflow can be
used to evaluate resilience strategies for wildfire, earthquake, or other hazards. This flexibility
underscores the value of the approach not only for Iowa but also for other regions seeking to
systematically evaluate resilience investments.
