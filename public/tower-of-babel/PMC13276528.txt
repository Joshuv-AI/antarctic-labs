Spice exosome-like nanovesicles for multi-target mitigation of thermal processing hazards: superior efficacy of isothiocyanate-enriched Armoracia rusticana ELNs in air-fried beef patties
Authors: Yongjun Liao, Huang Zhang, Zhuoming Huang, Lan Mo, Zhihui Wu, Lei Zhou, Yan Liu, Aihua Lou, Qingwu Shen, Chaoyi Xue, Ye Jiao, Wei Quan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Thermal processing of meat inevitably generates hazardous heterocyclic amines (HAs) and advanced glycation end products (AGEs), posing health risks to consumers. Here, we isolated and characterized spice exosome-like nanoparticles (ELNs) from ginger, garlic, Armoracia rusticana, basil, onion, and scallion. Then evaluated their efficacy in inhibiting bound HAs and AGEs in air-fried beef patties. Among all treatments, Armoracia rusticana ELNs (ARELNs) exhibited the most potent and broad-spectrum inhibitory activity, reducing total HAs by 53.4% and total AGEs by 50.6% at 1.0% addition, significantly outperforming other spice ELNs. ARELNs also demonstrated superior free radical scavenging capacity and suppressed lipid/protein oxidation more effectively than other spice ELNs. Crucially, ARELNs preserved desirable sensory attributes with minimal adverse impact. Metabolomic profiling revealed that ARELNs are uniquely enriched in isothiocyanate precursors (sinigrin, gluconasturtiin, 3-methylsulfinylpropyl isothiocyanate), which may act as dual scavengers of reactive carbonyls and free radicals. These findings suggest spice ELNs, particularly those from horseradish, as a novel, nature-derived nanovehicle strategy to simultaneously mitigate multiple thermal processing hazards while maintaining product quality, offering a promising clean-label solution for the meat industry.

Introduction

Grilled meat products constitute a significant component of global dietary patterns, prized for their distinctive flavors and textures. However, thermal processing methods, particularly high-temperature grilling, induce complex chemical reactions that generate hazardous compounds, including heterocyclic amines (HAs) and advanced glycation end products (AGEs) (Barzegar et al., 2019; Dong et al., 2020; Koszucka and Nowak, 2019). These contaminants pose substantial health risks due to their mutagenic, carcinogenic, and pro-inflammatory properties. Epidemiological studies consistently associate frequent consumption of well-done meat with increased incidences of colorectal cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, and metabolic diseases, implicating HAs and AGEs as key contributors to these pathological processes (Gibis, 2016; Koszucka and Nowak, 2019; Le Marchand, 2021). Despite decades of research into mitigation strategies, the food industry continues to grapple with the challenge of reducing these hazards without compromising sensory qualities that consumers demand.

A promising mitigation approach involves the incorporation of natural antioxidants during product formulation or processing (Lee et al., 2020). Antioxidants can interfere with the complex chemical pathways leading to the generation of these harmful substances, including the Maillard reaction (Geng et al., 2024; Gibis, 2016). Numerous studies have demonstrated the potential of plant-derived extracts, particularly from spices and herbs, in suppressing HAs and AGEs formation (Gu et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025). Common culinary spices, including those used in meat seasoning blends, are rich in phenolic compounds, flavonoids, and other bioactive constituents with recognized antioxidant capacities (Dong et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2023). Our previous work has corroborated the partial efficacy of various spice extracts in this context (Quan et al., 2020; Wei et al., 2024; Xue et al., 2022; Zeng et al., 2016). However, the practical application of many natural antioxidant compounds is constrained by significant limitations. For instance, we previously demonstrated that Armoracia rusticana extracts and its major glucosinolates can suppress HAs formation, with mechanisms involving free radical quenching as revealed by density functional theory (Zhang et al., 2024). However, pure isothiocyanate precursors are volatile and thermally labile, and their purification is costly, limiting their direct application in meat processing. Therefore, there is a pressing need to explore novel, food-compatible delivery systems or more stable bioactive forms derived from natural sources.

Recent advances in nanovesicle research have uncovered a promising avenue, naturally derived spice exosome-like nanoparticles (ELNs) may offer a targeted, multifunctional approach to simultaneously suppress hazardous compound formation while preserving or enhancing meat quality (Lo et al., 2024; Suharta et al., 2021). ELNs is nanoscale extracellular vesicles (30–150 nm) naturally produced by plant and animal cells, which present a revolutionary delivery system for bioactive compounds (Jiang et al., 2024; Lo et al., 2024). These phospholipid bilayer-enclosed particles carry diverse molecular, including proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, protected from enzymatic and thermal degradation (Jiang et al., 2024). Recent breakthroughs have demonstrated that plant-derived ELNs exhibit remarkable stability under harsh conditions and can facilitate the targeted delivery of phytochemicals (Zhao et al., 2024). Spices represent an exceptionally rich source of bioactive exosomes due to their high concentrations of phenolic compounds, flavonoids, and unique metabolites like curcumin, a potent antioxidant that quenches reactive oxygen species through its α,β-unsaturated diketone structure (Khan et al., 2022; Y. Zhou et al., 2024). The inherent bioactivity of spice constituents, combined with the protective advantages of encapsulation, creates a synergistic system ideally suited for mitigating thermal hazards in meat products.

Building upon our foundational research, we hypothesize that ELNs isolated from common meat-flavoring spices may serve as superior, stable, and highly effective natural agents for controlling HAs and AGE formation. Compared to crude extracts, these spice ELNs may offer targeted delivery of concentrated, protected antioxidant complexes directly within the food matrix. This study focuses on six spices routinely employed in meat processing: scallion, ginger, Armoracia rusticana, garlic, onion, and basil. These were selected based on their documented use in cuisine and their reported, though variably effective, antioxidant profiles in extract forms. Moreover, in thermally processed meat products, HAs and AGEs are predominantly present in protein-bound forms rather than as free molecules, because they are generated in situ and covalently attached to meat proteins during Maillard reactions. Free forms, if any, are typically unstable or lost during sample processing, whereas bound forms are considered toxicologically relevant as they can be released during digestion and exert adverse effects. Therefore, in this study we focused exclusively on quantifying the protein-bound HAs and AGEs to accurately evaluate the inhibitory efficacy of spice ELNs.

The objectives of this research were to isolate and characterize six spices ELNs, analyzing key physical properties; evaluate and compare their in vitro antioxidant capacities; investigate their efficacy in inhibiting the formation of major HAs and AGEs in a real food system and assess the impact of the most promising spice ELNs treatments on the sensory attributes of the cooked meat products. Through this comprehensive approach, we aim to identify a novel, effective, and sensorily acceptable bio-agent derived from spices, paving the way for innovative strategies to enhance the safety of thermally processed meats.

Materials and methods

Reagents and chemicals

Standards of HAs and AGEs were procured from Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc. (Texas, USA). Fresh beef (longissimus lumborum muscle) and spices including scallion, ginger, Armoracia rusticana, garlic, onion, and basil was purchased from local slaughterhouse in Changsha, China.

Isolation of exosome-like nanoparticles (ELNs) from spices

Six commonly consumed spices: Armoracia rusticana, ginger, garlic, basil, onion, and scallion (50 g) were combined with 500 mL of distilled water and homogenized using a Moulinex Y36-Vitafruit blender (Alençon, France). The homogenate was filtered to collect the aqueous filtrate, from which ELNs were isolated via polyethylene glycol (PEG) precipitation following previously reported procedures (Chen et al., 2023). Briefly, as illustrated in Fig. 1, the filtrate was sequentially centrifuged at 1000×g for 10 min, 3000×g for 20 min, and 10,000×g for 30 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was passed through a 0.8 μm membrane filter, mixed with 10% PEG 8000, and incubated at 4 °C for 12 h. After incubation, the mixture was centrifuged at 10,000×g for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in 50 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and stored at −80 °C until further analysis, and the obtained nanoparticles were designated as ARELNs, GELNs1, GELNs2, BELNs, OELNs, and SELNs, respectively.Fig. 1Schematic workflow of the PEG precipitation method employed for isolation of spice ELNs.

Schematic workflow of the PEG precipitation method employed for isolation of spice ELNs.

Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA)

The size distribution and particle concentration of spice-derived ELNs were characterized using a NanoSight LM10 system (Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK) equipped with a 405 nm UV laser (45 mW) and a C11440-5B camera (Hamamatsu Photonics, Shizuoka, Japan). Prior to analysis, suspensions were diluted with Milli-Q water by factors ranging from 1000 to 100,000 to achieve optimal particle density. Measurements were carried out at 25 °C with the following settings: camera level 16, detection threshold 0, and minimum expected particle size 30 nm. For each sample, three 30-s videos were recorded and processed using NTA software version 2.3. The hydrodynamic diameter (mean and mode) and the number of vesicles per unit volume were subsequently derived. Because all ELNs were isolated from the same initial weight of fresh spice material, the final ELN concentration was expressed as particles per mL of suspension.

Morphological characterization by scanning electron microscopy

The morphology of isolated ELNs was examined using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (Sigma 300 VP, Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany). ELN suspensions in PBS were diluted tenfold with Milli-Q water, and 100 μL aliquots were placed onto glass coverslips. Samples were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde, rinsed three times with 0.05% PBS, and dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, and 100%). The coverslips were then directly observed under FESEM without conductive coating.

Marination and cooking of beef patty samples

According to our previous study (Xu et al., 2022; Xue et al., 2022), the connective tissue and excess fat were removed from the beef, which was then ground using a plate grinder. Meat slurries (40 ± 0.1 g) were thoroughly mixed with different hydrocolloids and marinated at room temperature for 30 min. The mixture was then shaped into round patties with a thickness of 1.5 cm and a diameter of 6.0 cm. Three doses (0.2%, 0.5%, and 1%) of each ELNs were selected for the marination of beef patties. These concentrations were selected based on our previous studies and other reports on plant-derived extracts for inhibiting HAs and AGEs in cooked meat products, in which the range of 0.2%–1.0% reliably achieved significant inhibition while avoiding pronounced sensory deterioration (Zeng et al., 2016; Xue et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2022). The patties were then fried in an Supor KD60 air fryer at 210 °C for 10 min on each side.

Physicochemical property measurements

Cooking loss was calculated based on the weight difference of beef patties before and after heat treatment, and expressed as a percentage of the initial weight. Surface color was evaluated using a CR-400 chromameter (Konica Minolta, Osaka, Japan), with results recorded in the CIE L∗ (lightness), a∗ (redness), and b∗ (yellowness) color space. Texture profile analysis (TPA) was performed using a TA-XT plus texture analyzer (Stable Micro Systems, Godalming, UK) fitted with a cylindrical P/50 probe. Patties were sectioned into 1 cm3 cubes prior to analysis. Instrumental settings were as follows: pre-test speed 4 mm/s, test speed 3 mm/s, post-test speed 4 mm/s, trigger force 6.0 g, strain 50%, and time interval 5 s, following the procedure described by (Xu et al., 2022).

Volatile aroma compound analysis

Volatile aroma profiles of cooked beef patties were evaluated using a PEN3 electronic nose (Airsense Analytics, Schwerin, Germany) equipped with 10 metal oxide semiconductor sensors. For each measurement, 2.0 g of the adipose tissue portion was accurately weighed into a 40 mL headspace vial and equilibrated at 35 °C for 40 min to allow for headspace generation. The headspace gas was subsequently injected into the sensor array for analysis. Data acquisition and processing were performed as previously described (H. Du et al., 2021).

Extraction and UHPLC-MS/MS quantification of HAs and AGEs

Bound HAs and AGEs were extracted and quantified following our previously described methods with minor modifications (Xu et al., 2022; Xue et al., 2022). For HAs, 5 g of beef patty was mixed with 50 mL of 2 M NaOH, ultrasonically extracted with ethyl acetate (40 kHz, 50 °C, 30 min), and centrifuged at 3000×g for 10 min. The precipitate was hydrolyzed with 6 M HCl at 110 °C for 24 h, filtered, diluted to 100 mL, and purified by solid-phase extraction (SPE). For AGEs, 30 mg of defatted patty powder was reduced with 0.2 M borate buffer and 1 M NaBH4 at 4 °C for 10 h, then hydrolyzed with 6 M HCl at 110 °C for 12 h. The hydrolysate was filtered, diluted to 10 mL, spiked with d4-CML and d4-CEL (500 ng/mL), evaporated, reconstituted in 2 mL water, and purified using an MCX SPE cartridge.

Both HAs and AGEs were separated on a Waters Acquity UPLC system (BEH C18 column for HAs; HSS T3 column for AGEs) and detected by a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer in positive electrospray ionization mode. Source parameters: capillary 3.5 kV, source temp 120 °C, desolvation temp 350 °C, gas flows 60/650 L/h, nebulizer 30 psi. Quantification was performed using external calibration curves with serially diluted standards.

Antioxidant and free radical scavenging activities of ELNs in beef patties

The radical scavenging activity of spice ELNs was assessed using DPPH and ABTS assays according to our previously established method (Xu et al., 2023), with results expressed as percentage inhibition. Lipid oxidation in cooked beef patties was determined by measuring thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS), while protein oxidation was evaluated via total carbonyl content using commercial assay kits (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd.). Free radical generation within the meat matrix was directly quantified by electron spin resonance (ESR) spectrometry. Briefly, 200 mg of ground sample was analyzed using a Bruker A300-10/12 spectrometer (Germany) under the following parameters: center field 3360 G, modulation amplitude 1.00 G, scan width 100 G, scan time 60 s, and microwave power 20 mW (Xue et al., 2022).

Metabolite profiling of spice ELNs by UHPLC-QTOF-MS

Metabolomic profiling of spice ELNs was performed using a UHPLC system (Waters Acquity) coupled to a hybrid quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer (QTOF-MS, Synapt G2-Si, Waters) operating in negative electrospray ionization mode. ELN samples (100 μL) were mixed with 300 μL of acetonitrile, centrifuged at 12,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected for analysis. Chromatographic separation was achieved on a BEH C18 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm) at 40 °C with a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. The mobile phase consisted of (A) 0.1% formic acid in water and (B) acetonitrile, using the following gradient: 0–1 min, 5% B; 1–10 min, 5–40% B; 10–18 min, 40–95% B; 18–20 min, 95% B; 20–20.5 min, 95–5% B; 20.5–23 min, 5% B. The injection volume was 2 μL. Mass spectrometry conditions were: capillary voltage 2.5 kV, sampling cone 40 V, source temperature 120 °C, desolvation temperature 450 °C, cone gas flow 50 L/h, desolvation gas flow 800 L/h. Data were acquired in MSE mode with a mass range of m/z 50–1500. Compound identification was performed using MassLynx 4.1 software. Putative identification was performed by comparing the retention times and mass spectra including both [M-H]- and characteristic fragment ions with those of authentic standards. For compounds without commercially available standards, tentative identification was based on their MS/MS fragmentation patterns and literature comparison (Cheng et al., 2021).

Statistical analysis

All measurements were performed in triplicate (n = 3) and expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was determined by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan's multiple range test, with a significance level set at p < 0.05. Data visualization was carried out using Origin software (OriginLab Corp., USA). For aroma profile analysis, six replicate measurements were conducted, and multivariate statistical analyses—including principal component analysis (PCA) and heatmap generation were performed using SIMCA 14.0.

Results and discussion

Main characterization differences of spice ELNs

As reported previously, ELNs isolated from plant sources generally exhibit spherical or cup shaped morphologies under electron microscopy (Sha et al., 2024). In the present study, SEM imaging revealed that ELNs derived from all six spice species displayed distinct round, vesicle-like structures with hollow interiors. The nanoparticles were uniformly dispersed and demonstrated consistent morphological features (Fig. 2), which are in good agreement with the characteristic architecture of plant-derived ELNs described in the literature.Fig. 2Nanoparticle tracking analysis and morphology analysis of different spice ELNs. Values are expressed as mean ± SD (n = 3); ELN concentration was expressed as particles per mL of suspension; different letters indicate significant differences (p < 0.05).

Nanoparticle tracking analysis and morphology analysis of different spice ELNs. Values are expressed as mean ± SD (n = 3); ELN concentration was expressed as particles per mL of suspension; different letters indicate significant differences (p < 0.05).

A brief comparison of the six spice ELNs was assessed by their ultrastructure and particle size distribution. NTA was used to assess the size and distribution of ELNs, which are typical for studies of both animal and plant origin ELNs (Bai et al., 2024). NTA analysis indicated that the particle size distribution of spice ELNs obtained by the PEG precipitation method showed a single-peak normal distribution, and the median particle size was mainly distributed in the range of 115.4-188.0 nm, which was consistent with the literature report (Bai et al., 2024). GELNs and ARELNs showed the lowest median particle size, while BELNs showed the highest value. The yield of ELNs obtained from different spice plants was significantly different, with particle concentrations ranged from 4.2 × 107 to 9.0 × 107 particles/mL, in which ARELNs also showed the highest concentration compared to other ELNs (p < 0.05).

Inhibitory effects of spice ELNs on the formation of protein bound HAs in air-fried beef patties

As presented in Fig. 3, air-frying of beef patties generated six major HAs PhIP, MeIQx, MeIQ, MeAαC, Harman, and Norharman at concentrations comparable to those previously reported for conventionally cooked meat products (Li et al., 2020). All six spice ELNs significantly reduced bound HAs (p < 0.05), but the degree of inhibition varied with ELN type and concentration (Fig. 3). Spices have long been recognized as rich sources of natural antioxidants, and several have been evaluated for their capacity to suppress HA formation. For example, previous studies demonstrated that pine bark extract, rosemary oleoresin, and pepper extract each at 1.0% effectively lowered MeIQx content, while thyme, savory, and oregano extracts were shown to reduce PhIP formation in fried beef patties (Damašius et al., 2011; Lu et al., 2018; Zeng et al., 2016). More recently, marinades containing spices such as ginger, chili, and cumin have been reported to inhibit HA generation during grilling, an effect attributed to the radical-quenching activity of phenolic compounds and amides present in these spices (Xu et al., 2022; Xue et al., 2020).Fig. 3Inhibitory effects of different spice ELNs on the formation of HAs in grilled beef patties (A: Total HAs, B: PhIP; C: MeIQ; D: Norharman; E: Harman; F: MeIQx; G: MeAαC). Values are expressed as mean ± SD (n = 3); different letters indicate significant differences (p < 0.05).

The inhibition was both ELNs -type and concentration-dependent, revealing a complex interplay between the nanovesicle and the meat processing environment. As shown in Fig. 2A, a clear, though not always linear, dose-response relationship was observed for most spice ELNs treatments. For instance, GELNs1 progressively reduced total bound HAs from 1022.65 ng/g to 844.78, 647.59, and 571.70 ng/g at 0.2%, 0.5%, and 1.0% additions, corresponding to inhibition rates of 17.4%, 36.7%, and 44.1%, respectively. This pattern suggests that the protective effect is mediated by the bioactive constituents encapsulated within the ELNs, and a higher dose delivers a greater quantity of these inhibitory compounds to the reaction sites during heating. However, the diminishing return in efficacy gains between the 0.5% and 1.0% levels for several ELNs (e.g., GELNs2, BELNs) may indicate a saturation effect or potential interactions within the complex meat matrix that limit further activity at higher nanovesicle concentrations.

Moreover, striking differences in inhibitory potency were evident among the botanical sources. ARELNs emerged as the most effective agent. At 0.2% addition, ARELNs achieved a 35.7% reduction in total HAs, a level of suppression that matched or exceeded the effect of other ELNs at 0.5% or 1.0%. At 1.0% addition, ARELNs achieved 53.4% inhibition, with marked reductions in Norharman (54.8%) and Harman (54.0%). Our previous study investigated that Armoracia rusticana contains vesicle-protected glucosinolate breakdown products that scavenge free radicals and electrophilic intermediates central to HA formation (Huang et al., 2025). Besides, we hypothesize the exosomal membrane may enhance the stability and targeted delivery of these otherwise volatile compounds during the thermal process.

BELNs, OELNs, and SELNs showed moderate inhibition (39.3%–40.7% at 1.0%), with distinct activity profiles. For example, 1.0% BELNs reduced PhIP by 37.1% but had less effect on MeAαC. This selective inhibition pattern suggests that different ELNs may interfere with distinct reaction pathways. The phenolic compounds or flavonoid derivatives packaged in these ELNs might preferentially quench specific radicals or reactive carbonyl species involved in the formation of certain HAs. The observed non-monotonic dose-response for some HAs (e.g., BELNs on MeAaC) warrants further investigation into potential matrix-dependent effects at high nanovesicle concentrations. As reported by previous studies spices also showed both positive and negative effects on the formation of HAs, oregano and basil extracts increased the concentration of some HAs, independently of their properties (Damašius et al., 2011; Lai et al., 2023). While some non-linear dose–response observed for some ELNs e.g., the diminishing efficacy gain between 0.5% and 1.0% for GELNs2 and BELNs may be attributed to saturation of the inhibitory mechanism, potential pro-oxidant effects of high nanovesicle concentrations, or complex interactions with the meat matrix. Further studies are needed to clarify these phenomena.

Inhibitory effects of spice ELNs on the formation of protein bound AGEs in air-fried beef patties

The formation of AGEs, notably CML and CEL, was significantly inhibited by all tested spice ELNs in a concentration-dependent manner. As shown in Fig. 4, consistent with its superior performance against HAs, ARELNs demonstrated the most potent inhibition effective even at the lowest dose (0.2%), ARELNs reduced total AGEs by 30.1%, an efficacy matching or exceeding higher doses of other spice ELNs. At 1.0% concentration, ARELNs achieved a maximal inhibition of 50.6%, significantly outperforming all other groups. This aligns with the hypothesis that ARELNs deliver particularly effective carbonyl scavengers, which directly trap reactive dicarbonyl precursors like methylglyoxal. GELNs1 and SELNs also showed strong activity, reducing total AGEs by 42.6% and 45.6% at 1.0% addition, respectively. Corroborating these findings, previous work has demonstrated that ginger-derived extracts effectively attenuate the formation of both free and protein-bound AGEs, as well as lipid peroxidation products. Specifically, curcumin a major bioactive constituent of ginger was shown to suppress reactive carbonyl intermediates such as phenylacetaldehyde, glyoxal, and methylglyoxal in a dose-dependent manner, an effect primarily attributed to its capacity to quench free radicals (Xu et al., 2023; Xue et al., 2022). In contrast, GELNs2, BELNs, and OELNs exhibited more moderate but significant suppression at their highest doses (37.2%–43.6%). The strong correlation between HA and AGE inhibition, especially by ARELNs, suggests a shared mechanism centered on quenching reactive carbonyl species and mitigating oxidative stress. The data indicate that spice ELNs, particularly from horseradish, are highly effective multi-target inhibitors of thermal processing-induced hazards, with their efficacy governed by both botanical origin and applied concentration.

Mitigation of lipid and protein oxidation, and free radical scavenging by spice ELNs

The evaluation of oxidative markers provides direct insight into the potential mechanism by which spice ELNs inhibit the formation of processing-induced hazards. As shown in Fig. 5A, there were significant variations in the DPPH scavenging rates among ELNs, with the ARELNs having the highest free radical scavenging activity (p < 0.05). Additionally, the ABTS scavenging rates of the ARELNs and GELNs1 were significantly higher than those of other ELNs (p < 0.05, Fig. 5B). These variations may be attributed to the different compositions of the ELNs. Similarly, a significant inhibitory effect of ELNs on the lipid and protein oxidation was observed (p < 0.05; Fig. 5C). Consistent with its leading role in inhibiting HAs and AGEs, ARELNs exhibited unparalleled broad-spectrum antioxidant activity. Remarkably, even at the minimal 0.2% addition, ARELNs reduced TBARS by over 60% and sharply decreased protein carbonyls and radical spin count. This effect was profoundly enhanced at higher concentrations. At 1.0%, ARELNs achieved near-complete suppression of lipid oxidation and the lowest levels of protein carbonyls (3.53 nmol/mg) and free radicals (3.24 × 1012). This tripartite inhibition strongly suggests that ARELNs deliver a uniquely potent compounds likely vesicle-stabilized isothiocyanates, which that effectively scavenge free radicals at the initiation stage, thereby simultaneously halting the propagation of both lipid and protein oxidation cascades. Other spice ELNs showed moderate antioxidant activity. GELNs1 and GELNs2 exhibited dose-dependent radical scavenging, but their inhibition of lipid oxidation was less pronounced than that of ARELNs. For example, TBARS values for 1.0% GELNs2 remained higher than those for 0.2% ARELNs. OELNs had the weakest effect on lipid oxidation at 0.2%, with TBARS similar to the control. Effects of ELNs on cooking loss, texture and color of air-fried beef patties

As shown in Table 1, cooking loss was notably reduced by several ELNs. GELNs1 and ARELNs were particularly effective, decreasing cooking loss to 31.7% and 30.6% at 1.0% addition, respectively, compared to 40.4% in the control. This improvement in water retention suggests that these ELNs may mitigate heat-induced protein aggregation and shrinkage, thereby preserving moisture (Du et al., 2025; Khalid et al., 2023). Color parameters were altered in a source-specific manner. ARELNs had a minimal negative impact on a∗ value, which remained stable across all concentrations. This indicates that the potent antioxidant compounds in ARELNs might inhibit oxidative discoloration without strongly affecting surface browning. In contrast, OELNs and SELNs, especially at 1.0%, significantly decreased both yellowness and redness, resulting in darker patties. This may result from interactions between polyphenolic compounds in these ELNs and myoglobin or from the promotion of alternative pigmentation pathways. Interestingly, GELNs1 at 1.0% also caused significant darkening (lower L∗), possibly because its high reducing power accelerates early Maillard browning, even though it ultimately inhibits advanced harmful products like AGEs. Texture profile analysis (Table 2) revealed ARELNs exhibited a balanced and favorable textural profile. Unlike BELNs, which increased firmness, ARELNs at low to medium doses (0.2%–0.5%) maintained hardness, springiness, and chewiness at levels statistically similar to the control. These observations suggest that spice ELNs help protect myofibrillar proteins from thermal denaturation, preserving their functional properties, particularly water-holding capacity (Feng et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024). Metabolomic profiling elucidates the distinct compositional basis for functional efficacy

The identification of key differential metabolites was based on MS/MS fragmentation matching to databases, with representative compounds further verified by authentic standards where available. Metabolomic profiling revealed distinct compositional landscapes among the six spice ELNs (Fig. 7), providing a potential chemical explanation for their functional hierarchy (Table 3). GELNs2 were rich in 6-phosphogluconic acid and diallyl sulfone; OELNs and SELNs contained high levels of alliin and hypoxanthine (with SELNs also containing taxifolin); BELNs were abundant in phenolic intermediates (coniferaldehyde and quinic acid); GELNs1 had moderate levels of glucobrassicin and spermidine. These compositional differences align with their moderate and variable functional profiles. While ARELNs were uniquely enriched in isothiocyanate precursors, including 3-methylsulfinylpropyl isothiocyanate, sinigrin, and gluconasturtiin. These compositional differences might align with their moderate and variable functional profiles. Since the isothiocyanate compounds in ARELNs are known to scavenge reactive oxygen/nitrogen species and may trap reactive dicarbonyl intermediates. We hypothesize that this dual activity interferes with the root chemical pathways of both AGEs and HAs, offering a plausible mechanism for ARELNs’ superior efficacy. Of note, our previous work showed that pure glucosinolates from Armoracia rusticana inhibit HAs formation in fried fish patties (Zhang et al., 2024), but their application in real food systems is challenged by thermal instability, volatility, and potential sensory off-notes. ELNs likely protect these labile compounds through their phospholipid bilayer, enabling sustained activity during high-temperature processing. The integration of VIP scores and relative abundance data suggests that isothiocyanate-related compounds are key discriminators associated with the highest functional efficacy against processing-induced HAs and AGEs. Although these correlative data are consistent with a causative role, it must be noted that correlation does not imply causation. We hypothesize that ARELNs' uniquely packaged ITC precursors may help deactivate reactive intermediates that are convergent precursors to both HAs and AGEs. This might represent a more fundamental intervention strategy compared to ELNs relying primarily on general antioxidant compounds. Thus, the metabolomic evidence supports the hypothesis that ARELNs' exceptional performance is not merely a quantitative difference in antioxidant strength but may involve a qualitative advantage derived from its specific bioactive compounds. Direct experimental validation is needed to confirm these mechanistic hypotheses.

Conclusions

This study provides evidence that spice ELNs, particularly those from Armoracia rusticana (ARELNs), effectively inhibit HAs and AGEs in air-fried beef patties, which significantly outperforming ELNs from ginger, garlic, basil, onion, and scallion. Metabolomic profiling indicates that ARELNs are uniquely enriched in isothiocyanate precursors including sinigrin, gluconasturtiin, and 3-methylsulfinylpropyl isothiocyanate, which are hypothesized to function as dual-action scavengers of reactive dicarbonyl species and free radicals, potentially intercepting upstream pathways driving HAs and AGEs formation, although direct experimental confirmation is needed. Importantly, ARELNs preserved desirable sensory attributes and improved cooking yield, unlike some other ELNs that caused texture softening or color deterioration. These findings suggest ARELNs as a novel, clean-label, multifunctional strategy for enhancing both the chemical safety and quality of thermally processed meats. Nevertheless, human sensory validation remains necessary before industrial translation, as the present instrumental assessment cannot fully substitute for consumer perception. Future work should therefore combine sensory panel evaluation with scalable isolation methods, in vivo toxicity assessment, and synergistic applications with other natural matrices, along with assessments of safety, digestibility, and cost-effectiveness.
