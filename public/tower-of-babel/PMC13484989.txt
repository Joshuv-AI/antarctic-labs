Dual‐Site Activation of High‐Entropy Alloy Electrocatalysts via Ruthenium‐Induced Electron Transfer for Alkaline Hydrogen Evolution
Authors: Seongje Lim, Jae Won Choi, Gyu Yong Jang, Seungho Yu, Chang Qiu, Haotian Wang, Kug‐Seung Lee, Yong‐Tae Kim, Ki Chul Kim, Jong Hyeok Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHydrogen evolution in alkaline media is kinetically limited by sluggish water dissociation, requiring catalysts that can accelerate both H2O activation and proton adsorption. Here, ruthenium‐incorporated high‐entropy alloys (Ru‐HEAs) are developed as efficient alkaline HER electrocatalysts by exploiting Ru‐driven electron redistribution within a multi‐metallic lattice. Spectroscopic analyses show that electronegative Ru atoms withdraw electrons from neighboring Co and Ni atoms, shifting them to higher oxidation states and modifying their local electronic environments. This electronic modulation activates Co/Ni sites simultaneously. DFT calculations confirm that Co and Ni serve as the dominant adsorption centers, and that Ru incorporation alters their d‐band centers to lower both the thermodynamic and kinetic barriers of water dissociation while shifting hydrogen adsorption toward near‐thermoneutral energetics. As a result, Ru‐HEA/C delivers a low overpotential of only 29 mV at 10 mA cm− 2 and a Tafel slope of 67.8 mV dec− 1 with high Ru mass activity, outperforming pristine HEA and commercial Ru/C despite its low Ru loading. These findings clarify the role of Ru atoms in a high‐entropy environment as an electronic modulator that triggers dual‐site activation rather than merely providing additional active sites. This work establishes a generalizable design principle for HEA electrocatalysts optimized for alkaline hydrogen evolution.Keywords: electrocatalyst, electronic modulation, high‐entropy alloy, hydrogen evolution, metal organic framework, ruthenium

Introduction

Green hydrogen (H2) has emerged as a promising energy carrier, due to its high energy density, zero‐pollutant emission, zero‐carbon emission, and non‐toxicity [1, 2, 3]. Electrocatalytic water splitting using renewable electricity has been considered as an efficient and sustainable technology to produce green hydrogen. Alkaline water electrolysis is the most mature and reliable form of water electrolysis technologies. However, hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) kinetics in alkaline media is 2 to 3 orders of magnitude slower compared to acidic HER due to the lack of protons and the sluggish kinetics of the water dissociation reaction [4, 5]. Platinum (Pt) remains the benchmark catalyst owing to its optimal hydrogen binding, but its scarcity, poor water dissociation, and cost hinder large‐scale use [6].

Ruthenium (Ru) has recently emerged as a promising alternative to Pt, due to its relatively low material cost (ca. 1/3 the price of Pt), near‐optimal hydrogen adsorption energy, and strong affinity toward the hydroxyl intermediate (OH*) [7, 8]. Incorporation of Ru in transition metal‐based catalysts including alloys, oxides, and hydroxides, has been suggested as a promising strategy, since it can significantly reduce the mass loading of Ru while simultaneously promoting the HER activity of the catalyst [9, 10, 11, 12]. The role of incorporated Ru species extends beyond merely providing additional active sites. Instead, Ru incorporation can enhance the intrinsic alkaline HER activity of the catalyst by modulating electronic structure, inducing lattice strain, and promoting synergistic interactions [13, 14]. For example, He et al. reported that the incorporation of single‐atomic Ru sites in a Ni5P4 catalyst can cause a localized structure polarization, which optimized both the water dissociation and proton adsorption to achieve high HER activity with low Ru loading [15]. As such, introducing Ru to transition metal‐based catalysts is believed to be an efficient and cost‐effective strategy to fully exploit the potential of Ru.

High‐entropy alloys (HEAs), which are solid‐solution phase alloys often consisting of five or more metal elements, have attracted wide attention due to their unique features such as multi‐elemental composition, cocktail effects, intrinsic lattice distortions, and tunable electronic environments [16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23]. Such diverse and tunable features have provided opportunities for the fine‐tuning of their catalytic activities beyond conventional alloys, which has led to the development of various HEA‐based alkaline HER catalysts with unprecedented activities [24, 25, 26, 27, 28]. However, their intrinsically complex surface structure, along with the co‐existence of various electronic interactions and strain effects have limited the detailed investigation of surface active sites [29]. Especially, when it comes to introducing precious metals such as Ru, the detailed underlying mechanism still remains to be explored.

In this work, we show that when introducing Ru to the HEA lattice, highly electronegative Ru atoms facilitate electron redistribution, withdrawing electrons from its surrounding atoms such as Co and Ni, and thereby simultaneously activating multiple sites to enhance the alkaline HER activity of the HEA catalyst [9, 30]. Experimental investigations reveal the excellent HER performance of the synthesized catalyst, requiring only 29 mV of overpotential to reach a current density of 10 mA cm−2, while showing a high Ru mass activity of 1.51 A mgRu −1 at ‐0.05 V vs. RHE, benefiting from the low Ru loading of the HEA catalyst. Moreover, the effect of Ru introduction was intensified especially under a high‐entropy environment. Theoretical calculations and experimental results confirm that Ru incorporation optimizes the electronic structure of Co/Ni sites to simultaneously accelerate the water dissociation step and the hydrogen evolution step, accounting for the superior HER activity. Overall, we propose a dual‐site activation strategy in which Ru‐induced electron transfer enhances the catalytic activity of Co and Ni, especially in a high‐entropy environment. Such insight clarifies the promotional effect of Ru in HEA catalysts and thus offers future guidelines for designing next‐generation HEA electrocatalysts for efficient alkaline HER.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterization

Ru‐HEA/C electrocatalysts were synthesized by MOF precursor synthesis, ion exchange, and pyrolysis (Figure 1). First, various metal acetates (Co, Ni, Cu, Mn) of the same ratio with terephthalic acid were dissolved in DMF and stirred for 2 h to synthesize the MOF precursor, CoNiCuMn‐BDC (Figure S1). Afterward, Ru was introduced to the MOF precursor via an ion‐exchange method. CoNiCuMn‐BDC was dispersed in an ethanol solution containing RuCl3 and heated to 75°C for 12 h. After the ion exchange process, inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP‐MS) results confirmed that Ru successfully replaced other metals in the MOF precursor, mainly manganese (Table S1). Finally, the MOF precursors containing and not containing Ru were annealed in a tube furnace at 700°C for 2 h under H2/Ar atmosphere to finally obtain Ru‐HEA/C and HEA/C powders. The morphology of the synthesized HEAs was analyzed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), which revealed nanoparticles with an average diameter of 13.4 nm (Figure 2a). Further high‐resolution TEM (HRTEM) analysis revealed a thin carbon shell surrounding the nanoparticle, with a thickness of ∼3 nm (Figure 2b). High‐angle annular dark‐field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF‐STEM) analysis reveals clear lattice fringe patterns corresponding to the (200) and (111) facets of the nanoparticle (Figure 2c). Such fringe patterns are consistent with the X‐ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the nanoparticle, where the diffraction peaks at 44.08°, 51.48° and 75.32° correspond to the (111), (200) and (220) planes of the face‐centered‐cubic (fcc) structure which match well with the PDF cards of Ni and Co (Ni: JCPDS No. 04–0850, Co: JCPDS No. 15–0806), indicating the single‐phase structure of the Ru‐HEA/C nanoparticle (Figure 2d). TEM elemental mapping images show that the five metal elements (Co, Ni, Cu, Mn, Ru) are evenly distributed throughout the nanoparticle, with the carbon shell surrounding them (Figure 2e). The Raman spectrum of Ru‐HEA/C displayed two prominent peaks positioned at 1336 and 1591 cm−1, corresponding to D and G bands of graphitic carbon, respectively (Figure S2). These two peaks suggest that the organic linkers of MOF precursor have been converted into graphitic carbons during the pyrolysis to form a core‐shell structure [31].

In order to investigate the electronic structure of HEA particles and clarify the effect of Ru incorporation on the surface electronic states, high‐resolution X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra of Ni, Co, and Ru atoms in HEA/C and Ru‐HEA/C were examined (Figure 3a–c). Deconvoluted Ni 2p XPS spectra of HEA particles with and without Ru show a positive shift in Ni0 peak position from 852.9 to 853.05 eV upon Ru incorporation, indicating a decrease in local electron density of Ni atoms (Figure 3a). A similar trend is observed in the Co 2p spectrum, where the Co0 peak shifts positively from 778.58 to 778.76 eV upon Ru incorporation, also showing a decrease in local electron density of Co atoms (Figure 3b). In addition, the Ru 3p spectrum confirms the successful incorporation of Ru into the HEA framework (Figure 3c). Taken together, such change in local electron density of Ni and Co atoms imply that the electronic structure of HEAs are restructured upon introduction of Ru, where a local electron transfer occurs from Ni and Co to the neighboring atoms. Further analysis of the C 1s and the Ru 3d spectrum re‐confirm the successful incorporation of Ru atoms and the graphitic carbon shell surrounding the HEA particles (Figure S3a). XPS spectra of the remaining metal atoms (Cu and Mn) reveal no substantial change in their oxidation states, meaning that such electronic restructuring effect is focused at Ni and Co sites (Figure S3b,c).

To gain deeper insight into the electronic structure of the HEAs, X‐ray absorption spectroscopy was carried out with metal foils and oxides as reference samples. First, X‐ray absorption near‐edge structure (XANES) spectra of Co, Ni and Ru in HEA/C and Ru‐HEA/C were studied to provide element‐specific insights into the electronic configurations (Figure 3d–f). The K edges of Ni and Co exhibit clear positive shifts toward higher binding energies upon the introduction of Ru which imply a local electron deficiency in Ni and Co sites, which is consistent with the XPS results. Meanwhile, the K edge of Ru is in high proximity to that of Ru metal phase, implying the metallic state of Ru atoms. Taken together, these features reveal that Ru incorporation enhances the electron donation from Co and Ni toward the surrounding lattice, effectively tuning their local electron density. To better analyze the coordination environment of each element, oxidation state of reference samples were plotted as a function of K‐edge energy, which were used to analyze the valence state of HEA elements. Valence state of Ni and Co increased upon Ru incorporation, from 0.118 to 0.204 and 0.285 to 0.380, respectively (Figure 3g,h). In addition, the valence state of Ru was 0.134, which is much lower compared to Ni and Co in the same particle, reconfirming the local electron deficiency of Ni and Co while suggesting a local electron abundancy in Ru (Figure 3i). Corresponding Fourier‐transformed extended X‐ray absorption fine structure (FT‐EXAFS) spectra shows strong Co─Co and Ni─Ni bonds, reconfirming the metallic state of the atoms in HEA (Figure S4a,b). FT‐EXAFS signals of Ru also demonstrated a strong Ru─Ru bond with an additional Ru─C bond, presumably owing to the surrounding carbon shell (Figure S4c). Taken together, these spectroscopic and structural results provide compelling evidence that Ru incorporation perturbs the local electronic environment and induces electron redistribution, primarily around Co and Ni sites. Electronegativity of individual atoms in the HEA matrix plays a central role in such charge redistribution [32]. Considering the higher electronegativity of Ru atoms (2.20) compared with those of Ni (1.91) and Co (1.88), the donation of electrons from Ni and Co to Ru agrees well with the electronegativity differences between them [32].

HER Performance

To investigate the effect of such Ru‐driven charge redistribution process on the electrochemical performance of HEA catalysts, their alkaline HER performance was examined in 1.0 M KOH electrolyte with a three‐electrode system. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves show that Ru‐HEA/C requires the lowest overpotential of only 29 mV to reach 10 mA cm− 2, which is significantly lower than those of HEA/C (398 mV), and even lower than commercial Ru/C (63 mV) (Figure 4a). The corresponding Tafel slope of 67.8 mV dec− 1 for Ru‐HEA/C confirms faster kinetics compared to HEA/C (132.6 mV dec− 1) and Ru/C (90.7 mV dec− 1) (Figure 4b). Even though our Ru‐HEA/C catalyst possesses smaller amounts of Ru compared to Ru/C, its alkaline HER performance outperforms Ru/C and therefore shows a high mass activity of 1.51 A mgRu‐1 at –0.05 V, which is over five times higher than that of Ru/C (Figure 4c). To examine whether the enhanced HER performance originates from the introduction of active Ru sites, the HER activity of Ru‐HEA/C catalysts with varying Ru loadings was systematically investigated. (Figure S5). If the enhanced HER activity of the Ru‐HEA/C catalyst stemmed from the introduction of additional Ru sites, highest loading of Ru would have resulted in the highest HER activity. However, experimental results showed that excess incorporation of Ru hinders the HER activity beyond a certain point. Therefore, these results indicate that such increase in alkaline HER performance upon Ru incorporation in the HEA matrix is due to the local electron redistribution in Co and Ni sites. To gain further insights into the reaction mechanism, the electrochemical surface area (ECSA) of catalysts was estimated by calculating the double‐layer capacitance (Cdl) of catalysts, derived from cyclic voltammetry curves under different scan rates. Estimated ECSA of catalysts indicate that Ru‐HEA/C possesses the largest electrochemical surface area (7.88 mF cm− 2), indicating that the incorporation of Ru resulted in the generation of electrochemically active sites for the HER (Figure 4d) [33]. Compared to HEA/C, Ru‐HEA/C exhibits a markedly smaller semicircle diameter in electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), corresponding to a significantly reduced charge‐transfer resistance (Rct) indicating the improved HER kinetics of Ru‐HEA/C (Figure S6). Durability of the Ru‐HEA/C catalyst was examined by conducting chronoamperometry tests. The activity of the catalysts was stably maintained at a current density of 10 mA cm−2 for over 100 h, ensuring catalyst stability under highly reductive conditions of alkaline HER (Figure 4e). We also carried out a systematic comparison across alloys with different numbers of metal elements to further study the synergetic effect of the high‐entropy environment with our Ru incorporation strategy (Figure 4f). Interestingly, our Ru incorporation strategy significantly improved the alkaline HER performance of the catalysts regardless of the entropy level (number of elements). These results indicate that the charge redistribution between Ru, Co, and Ni benefits alkaline HER activity in all cases. Furthermore, catalysts with higher entropy showed higher HER activity. However, Ru incorporation alone was insufficient to guarantee the high alkaline HER activity of the catalyst, indicating that the synergetic interplay of Ru‐driven charge redistribution with the high‐entropy environment is crucial for securing the high alkaline HER activity of the catalyst. The entropy‐dependent activity trend in Figure 4f indicates that the effect of Ru incorporation is amplified in catalysts with higher elemental complexity. Although Ru‐induced electron redistribution benefits alkaline HER activity in all alloy systems, a multicomponent alloy environment can provide lattice distortion, local strain, and diverse atomic coordination environments, which broaden the electronic‐state distribution around Co/Ni sites. These high‐entropy‐related structural and electronic features can synergistically interact with Ru‐induced charge redistribution, allowing Co/Ni sites to adopt more favorable adsorption energetics for water dissociation and hydrogen adsorption/desorption. Therefore, the superior activity of the Ru‐incorporated multicomponent alloy catalyst is attributed not only to Ru incorporation itself, but also to the synergistic interplay between Ru‐driven electronic modulation and the complex multielemental alloy environment.

Taken together with previous structural characterizations which revealed increased oxidation states of Ni and Co induced by Ru‐driven charge redistribution, these electrochemical characterizations establish a consistent mechanism: incorporation of Ru atoms with high electronegativity results in a local charge redistribution process that increases the oxidation states of Co and Ni, which can synergistically boost the alkaline HER activity of the catalyst under a high‐entropy environment.

Computational Study

To further validate the role of Ru element incorporated into the CoNiCuMn‐based electrocatalyst on its catalytic performance, first‐principles calculations were further performed using a simplified model associated with Ru‐involved electrocatalysts. Specifically, as shown in Table S1, the Mn wt.% in CoNiCuMnRu/C is 0.71, which corresponds to 1.10 at.%. Notably, individual constituents of HEA typically range from 5 to 35 at.% [34, 35, 36]. Therefore, we employed a simplified bulk model, CoNiCuRu, by removing the Mn element with a negligible amount with the aim of reducing the computational cost. For a slab model of CoNiCuMn‐based electrocatalyst, (1 1 1) direction was chosen because the most intense XRD peak was revealed to be (1 1 1) plane, as evidenced in Figure 2d. Moreover, the surface energy analysis demonstrates that the slab model with Mn placed on the surface exhibits the lowest surface energy (1.57 J/m2), resulting in the most stable slab configuration. Similarly, the slab model of CoNiCuRu with Ru placed on the surface is confirmed to be the most stable, with the surface energy of 1.36 J/m2. These suggest that the configuration with Mn or Ru elements placed on the surface would be suitable for computational analyses with the aim of providing a meaningful conclusion (Figure S7, Table S2 and Table S3).

To identify the preferential adsorption sites for H2O molecules, we performed projected density of states (PDOS) calculations on the bare surfaces (Figure 5a,b). The PDOS plots reveal that the d orbitals of Co and Ni exhibit the highest peak intensities near the Fermi level compared to the other transition metal elements (Cu, Mn, and Ru) in the CoNiCuMn(1 1 1) and CoNiCuRu(1 1 1) surface models. This indicates the high electronic activity of d orbitals of Co and Ni elements for participating in chemical interactions [37]. The d‐band centers of individual atoms on the bare surfaces are further examined to gain insights (Table S4). Both the slab models of CoNiCuMn and CoNiCuRu exhibit the d‐band centers of Ni and Co positioned at relatively high values compared to those of the other elements. This clearly indicates that the Ni and Co elements placed on the surfaces of CoNiCuMn(1 1 1) and CoNiCuRu(1 1 1) possess the most reactive d‐states, which would enable the elements to be dominant adsorption sites for interactions with H2O molecules [38]. It should be noted that the simplified model does not capture all possible local configuration in HEAs, and Mn may introduce secondary local effects. To assess the validity of the simplified model, we further examined the CoNiCuMnRu system. As shown in Figure S9, the PDOS profiles reveal that Co and Ni remain the dominant contributors near the Fermi level, consistent with the simplified models. In addition, the d‐band center values follow similar trends (Table S4), indicating that the essential electronic features are preserved.

The water dissociation reactions on the surfaces of CoNiCuMn(1 1 1) and CoNiCuRu(1 1 1) are explored to identify their thermodynamics and relevant kinetic energy barriers as the first step for understanding the afore‐mentioned difference in the HER performance between the two electrocatalysts (Figure 5c and Figure S8). Notably, a H2O molecule is assumed to be adsorbed on the Ni and Co sites which are identified to be the main active sites through the analyses of the PDOS and d‐band center. The adsorption energy of H2O is found to be −142.9 kJ/mol for CoNiCuMn(1 1 1) and −36.3 kJ/mol for CoNiCuRu(1 1 1), leading to a more stable H2O adsorption for CoNiCuMn(1 1 1). Moreover, the resultant change in the Gibbs free energy for the water dissociation is revealed to be 80.4 kJ/mol for CoNiCuMn(1 1 1) and −32.9 kJ/mol for CoNiCuRu(1 1 1). This implies that CoNiCuRu(1 1 1) has a more preferable thermodynamics for the H2O dissociation, even exhibiting an exothermic behavior. The dissociated proton is assumed to be subsequently adsorbed on the Ni site corresponding to the highest d‐band center with the strongest adsorption capability [38, 39]. The CoNiCuRu(1 1 1) surface (99.2 kJ/mol) exhibits a lower activation energy barrier than the CoNiCuMn(1 1 1) surface (218 kJ/mol). Notably, a relatively low activation energy barrier predicted for CoNiCuRu(1 1 1) is primarily attributed to its thermodynamic preference for H2O adsorption (Figure 5c). These highlight that the water dissociation step is more favorable on the CoNiCuRu(1 1 1) surface from both the thermodynamic and kinetic points of view. The adsorption thermodynamics of the dissociated proton is further investigated to understand experimentally analyzed HER performance on the two different surfaces (Figure 4d). As expected, CoNiCuMn(1 1 1) and CoNiCuRu(1 1 1) surfaces exhibit distinctly different patterns in the Gibbs free energy of H adsorption (Figure 5d). CoNiCuMn(1 1 1) surface displays a negative (exothermic) adsorption energy, while CoNiCuRu(1 1 1) surface shows a positive (endothermic) value. More importantly, the Gibbs free energy for H adsorption on CoNiCuRu(1 1 1) surface is obviously closer to zero than that on CoNiCuMn(1 1 1) surface. This implies that, from a thermodynamic perspective, CoNiCuRu(1 1 1) surface is more suitable for the moderate H adsorption and facile H desorption than CoNiCuMn(1 1 1) surface, leading to a better HER performance for the former. Consequently, all these provide strong evidence for the beneficial impact of the incorporated Ru element on the HER performance of CoNiCuMn electrocatalyst. To further validate the simplified model and assess the influence of Mn inclusion, we additionally examined the CoNiCuMnRu(1 1 1) surface. The water dissociation barrier (148.86 kJ mol−1) is positioned between those of CoNiCuMn (218 kJ mol−1) and CoNiCuRu (99.2 kJ mol−1), indicating an intermediate kinetic behavior (Figure S10). Meanwhile, the hydrogen adsorption free energy remains within a similar energetic window relative to thermoneutral conditions when evaluated in terms of |ΔG H*|. These results indicate that the overall catalytic behavior is preserved (Figure S11).

To analyze the electronic effects governing the H adsorption on the CoNiCuMn and CoNiCuRu electrocatalyst surfaces, we investigated the correlation between the d‐band center of the Ni adsorption site and the adsorption energy, along with the negative signs of integrated projected crystal orbital Hamiltonian population (ICOHP) values (Figure 5e). From the chemical point of view, COHP, which partitions the band‐structure energy into orbital‐pair interactions, is defined as bond‐weighted DOS between a pair of adjacent atoms. The ICOHP shows the contribution of a specific (chemical) bond to the band‐structure energy. Therefore, a COHP diagram indicates bonding and antibonding contributions to the band‐structure energy, and ICOHP describes the strength of the bond [40, 41]. A clear trend between d‐band center and adsorption energy is highlighted, indicating that a lower d‐band center (i.e., d‐band center: −1.64 eV for CoNiCuMn vs. −1.69 eV for CoNiCuRu) correlates with a weaker hydrogen adsorption [42, 43, 44]. Likewise, a lower ‐ICOHP value is shown for a catalyst surface with a weaker hydrogen adsorption (i.e., ‐ICOHP: 1.23 eV for CoNiCuMn vs. 1.12 eV for CoNiCuRu). Consequently, these electronic analyses further support that the difference in the HER performance between the CoNiCuMn and CoNiCuRu electrocatalyst surfaces arises from their electronically intrinsic nature.

In summary, theoretical calculations show that Ru incorporation modifies the electronic structure of the HEA surface by lowering the d‐band centers of Co and Ni and weakening their H‐bonding strength. Such electronic tuning makes water dissociation thermodynamically and kinetically more favorable and adjusts hydrogen adsorption toward a near‐optimal regime. As a result, Ru shifts Co/Ni sites into a more active state, providing clear computational evidence that the enhanced HER activity originates from Ru‐driven electronic modulation rather than from Ru itself acting as the primary active site (Figure 5f).

Conclusion

In this work, we elucidate how Ru incorporation modulates the electronic structure of a high‐entropy alloy to enhance alkaline HER activity. Spectroscopic analyses show that the high electronegativity of Ru withdraws electrons from adjacent Co and Ni atoms, increasing their oxidation states. DFT calculations further confirm that Co and Ni remain the primary adsorption sites after their d‐band centers are shifted into a catalytically advantageous regime, and that Ru‐containing surfaces exhibit significantly improved energetics for the HER pathway. In particular, the Volmer step becomes thermodynamically favorable and shows a substantially reduced activation barrier, while the hydrogen adsorption free energy shifts toward near‐thermoneutral values. These results confirm the role of Ru atoms as an electronic modulator, enabling simultaneous activation of Co and Ni sites rather than functioning as the main reactive center. These electronic and mechanistic improvements directly translate into strong catalytic performance. Ru‐HEA/C achieves an overpotential of 29 mV at 10 mA cm− 2, high Ru mass activity, reduced charge‐transfer resistance, expanded electrochemical surface area, and operational stability surpassing 25 h, surpassing both Ru‐free HEA and commercial Ru/C despite minimal Ru loading.

Overall, this study clarifies the mechanism by which Ru induces dual‐site activation within a high‐entropy environment and highlights electronic redistribution as a powerful lever for tuning multi‐metallic catalysts. The demonstrated framework provides a generalizable design principle for engineering HEA electrocatalysts optimized for alkaline HER through targeted modulation of active‐site electronic structure.

Experimental Section

Synthesis of CoNiCuMn‐BDC Precursor

Cobalt(II) acetate tetrahydrate (0.675 mmol, 98%), nickel(II) acetate tetrahydrate (0.675 mmol, 98%), copper(II) acetate monohydrate (0.675 mmol, 98%) and manganese(II) acetate tetrahyrate (0.675 mmol, 98%) were dissolved in 75 mL N,N‐dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.0%) under 1 h magnetic stirring, then terephthalic acid (2.5 mmol, 98%) was injected into the DMF solution. After magnetic stirring for 2 h, the product was centrifuged and washed thoroughly with DMF for three times. The final products were obtained by drying overnight at 60°C under vacuum.

Synthesis of CoNiCuMnRu‐BDC Precursor

First, various amount of ruthenium(III) chloride hydrate(99.98%) was dissolved in 75 mL ethanol. After fully dissolved, 200 mg of CoNiCuMn‐BDC was dispersed in the ethanol solution, and the solution was stirried at 75°C for 12 h. Afterward, the product was centrifuged and washed thoroughly with DMF for three times. The final products were obtained by drying overnight at 60°C under vacuum.

Synthesis of Ru‐HEA/C and HEA/C Electrocatalyst

Typically, the CoNiCuMnRu‐BDC precursor was annealed in a tube furnace at 700°C for 2 h under H2/Ar atmosphere (H2 4%, Ar 96%). After cooling naturally to room temperature, the obtained samples were treated with 0.5 M H2SO4 for 6 h at 70°C. Then the products were rinsed with ultrapure water for three times and then dried overnight at 80°C under vacuum. HEA/C was synthesized by annealing CoNiCuMn‐BDC instead of CoNiCuMnRu‐BDC.

Characterization

The morphology of the samples was observed by FE‐SEM (JSM‐7610F‐Plus). A Cs‐corrected transmission electron microscope (JEM‐ARM 200F) was used for the HRTEM observation. The XRD diffraction patterns were obtained using a high‐resolution X‐ray diffractometer (Rigaku SmartLab) equipped with a Cu–Ka radiation source. XPS (Thermo Fisher Scientific K‐ALPHA) and was used to characterize the chemical states of the electrocatalysts. The compositions of catalysts were determined by the Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectrometer (ICP‐OES, Agilent).

Electrochemical Measurements

The electrochemical performance was measured using an electrochemical workstation (CHI 605, CH Instrument) based on a three‐electrode system containing a Pt wire counter electrode, an Hg/HgO (1 M NaOH) reference electrode, and a 1 M KOH (pH 14) electrolyte. In order to load the electrocatalysts on the working electrode, 5 mg prepared sample was dispersed in 1 mL Nafion ethanol/water solution (500 µ L ethanol, 480 µL water, and 20 µL Nafion, 5 wt.%). The suspension was further sonicated for 1 h to prepare a homogeneous ink. Finally, 15 µL of suspension was drop‐casted onto a glassy carbon electrode (5 mm diameter) with a loading mass of about 0.38 mg cm−2 and dried at room temperature. The J–V curves were measured by linear sweep voltammetry with a scan rate of 20 mV s−1 with 95% iR‐corrected. The stability test was conducted in 1 M KOH solution using chronoamperometric mode of the electrochemical workstation, maintaining a potential of ‐0.019 V vs. RHE. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements (Gamry Instruments, Reference 600+) were conducted at an overpotential of 100 mV in a frequency range of 100 kH to 1 Hz with an AC amplitude of 5 mV. The electrochemical double‐layer capacitance was measured by a CV method. First, potential was applied in the range of 0.0–0.2 V (vs RHE) at different scan rates. After the CVs were measured, half of the current density deviation at 0.1 V (vs RHE) was plotted against the scan rate. The plotted graph was linearly fitted, and its slope was calculated to determine the double‐layer capacitance.

Computational Methods

To construct the bulk model of CoNiCuMn structure, we utilized a 2 × 2 × 2 supercell of the Co FCC bulk, in the atomic ratio of Co 0.5, Ni 0.375, Cu 0.094, and Mn 0.031. We considered a total of eight configurations, each of which showed the systematic arrangement of individual elements to ensure that the bulk structure maintained a cubic structure during the geometry optimization process. This allowed for the identification of a most stable configuration (Figure S7 and Table S2). Notably, any of the eight configurations exhibited no lattice distortion, preserving the cubic arrangement, which can be attributed to the intrinsic characteristics of the FCC structure (Table S3) [45, 46]. The bulk model of CoNiCuRu structure was further constructed through substituting Mn element in the most stable configuration of the CoNiCuMn structure with Ru element. This resulted in the atomic ratio of Co 0.5, Ni 0.375, Cu 0.094, Ru 0.031, and a slightly greater lattice parameter of 7.07Å, which is 0.01Å greater than that of CoNiCuMn, was observed for the CoNiCuRu structure, while maintaining a well‐formed cubic structure. Based on the bulk models of CoNiCuMn and CoNiCuRu, a total of seven layers was constructed to design each slab model, each of which had four relaxed layers at the top and bottom. Additionally, a vacuum region of 30Å was set to ensure that the dipole moment interactions between periodic images could be neglected.
