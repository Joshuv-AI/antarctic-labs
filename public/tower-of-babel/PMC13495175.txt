Psychometric properties and network analysis of the brief Adverse Childhood Experiences Abuse Short Form (ACE-ASF) in homosexual and heterosexual
Authors: Andrés Ramírez, Luis Burgos-Benavides, Venus Medina-Maldonado, Hugo Sinchi-Sinchi, Noelia Aguilera-Jiménez, Francisco Javier Rodríguez-Díaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Adverse Childhood Experiences Abuse describes the physical, emotional, and sexual abuse undergone in childhood, which is associated with negative effects on mental, emotional, and relational health later in adulthood.ObjectiveTo assess the psychometric properties of the ACE-ASF (Adverse Childhood Experiences Abuse Short Form) in homosexual, and heterosexual in Ecuador, with a special focus on reliability, validity, measurement invariance, and network analysis of self-perceived abuse (fear, trapped and abused).MethodsInstrumental study with 520 participants (nHomosexual = 260 and nHeterosexual = 260). The ACE-ASF was implemented along with confirmatory factor analysis, reliability, validity, and network analysis.ResultsThe ACE-ASF offered excellent internal consistency (α = 0.845—0.883; ω = 0.856—0.876), convergent validity (AVE = 0.606—0.661), and discriminant validity (HTMT = 0.437). The two-factor model had ideal fit (CFI = 0.999; RMSEA = 0.000). Measurement invariance was verified across groups (ΔCFI ≤ 0.001). Network analysis showed differences by sexual orientation and abuse perception, highlighting the centrality of item A3 (betweenness = 1.67) and denser networks in lesbian (sparsity = 0.07) versus more fragmented networks in gay (sparsity = 0.67).ConclusionThe ACE-ASF is a validated, reliable, invariant measure for sexual orientation and self-perceptions of abuse. Its use may help in the design targeted prevention and intervention methods to deal with the long-term consequences of childhood abuse for all relationship contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04907-6.Keywords: Childhood Abuse, Perception of Abuse, Psychometrics, Network

Introduction

Childhood violence is a major public health issue, with around a billion children affected worldwide each year [21, 22, 63]. Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs) such as physical, emotional, and sexual abuse as well as neglect are associated with negative lifelong well-being outcomes that last into adulthood [40, 41, 53]. Traumatic events of adverse childhood experiences are associated with severe effects on physical, mental, and reproductive health, as well as risk of chronic diseases: cardiovascular diseases, obesity, diabetes [2, 27, 65]. Secondly, ACEs are related to a reduced quality of cognitive and social development, leading to poor academic performance, emotional dysregulation, and problems in forming healthy relationships in childhood [5, 24, 31, 50].

ACEs make a deep-rooted neurobiological impact in their lives: they impair brain development, disrupt stress-response systems, and impair emotional regulation and decision-making. Such disruptions linger into adulthood, modifying emotional and cognitive functioning which may affect academic performance, work performance, and relationships based on social and work situations [30, 34]. Adolescents and young adults with a history of childhood violence commit more risky behaviors like smoking, alcohol and drug misuse, and unsafe sexual practices that raise the risk of NCDs, cancer, and sexually transmitted infections across adolescence and adulthood [43, 46, 52]. Regarding different types, whether the neurobiological and behavioral consequences of ACEs may play out differently between in gay, lesbian and heterosexual relationships is of particular interest when considering dating violence [68] and perceptions of personal abuse [14].

In addition to individual health effects, ACEs help make long-term violence cycle a reality beyond the childhood years. From a young age, maltreatment is associated with a higher likelihood of later victimization and perpetration of violence [8]. Unfortunately, such a dynamic does not only pose a threat to their personal welfare but contributes to the intergenerational transmission of aggression and a social and family context where violence is normalized [67]. Moreover, the long-term psychological, emotional, and behavioral consequences of ACEs can impact the development of a healthy romantic relationship, affect emotional regulation, promote increased risks towards risky behavior, and strengthen the continuation of violent patterns [72]. Resolving these negative influences by means of early intervention, trauma-informed approach and positive policies at societal level such as supportive social structures are necessary for preventing such a harmful cycle and creating safety in communities [28, 37, 57, 61].

Research on child abuse and adverse childhood experiences has relied on multiple methodological approaches to understand their prevalence, consequences, and developmental impact. Large cross-sectional studies, such as the Violence Against Children Surveys and the Optimus Studies, have been essential for estimating the magnitude of victimization and identifying immediate outcomes associated with abuse in children and adolescents [38, 39].

Repeated cross-sectional designs have also contributed to monitoring changes in abuse prevalence over time, offering valuable evidence for evaluating prevention strategies and public policies. In parallel, longitudinal studies, including those conducted by the LONGSCAN Consortium, have provided deeper insight into how early maltreatment shapes mental health, behavior, and well-being across the life course, while also showing the cumulative burden of multiple adverse experiences on adult health, socioeconomic outcomes, and interpersonal functioning [25, 41, 42, 45, 64].

Despite these important contributions, methodological challenges remain, particularly regarding the validity and reliability of child maltreatment measures across culturally diverse settings [73]. Cultural differences in how abuse is understood and reported may affect data quality and limit comparability between populations [69, 74].

Underreporting, often influenced by fear, shame, or limited recognition of maltreatment, further complicates assessment. In this context, the Adverse Childhood Experiences questionnaire has become one of the most widely used tools for assessing childhood trauma, covering abuse, neglect, and household dysfunction as major domains of early adversity [20–22, 33, 36, 44, 63]. Its later adaptations, including the ACE-IQ and the ACE-ASF, have expanded its applicability across diverse cultural and research settings, although further psychometric evaluation remains necessary to ensure robust cross-cultural use [3, 26, 41, 49, 62, 66, 70, 71].

The primary objective of this study is to examine the psychometric properties of the abbreviated version of the Adverse Childhood Experiences Questionnaire (ACE-ASF) in homosexual, and heterosexual, with a special focus on reliability, validity, measurement invariance, and network analysis of self-perceived abuse. This evaluation aims to confirm that the instrument appropriately measures the dimensions of childhood abuse within each of these groups, ensuring that it consistently captures the intended constructs without bias.

First, the reliability of the ACE-ASF will be analyzed across the three target populations to verify the internal consistency and stability of the instrument when assessing adverse childhood experiences. Second, the structural validity of the ACE-ASF will be assessed through Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA), examining whether the dimensions of physical-emotional abuse and sexual abuse cluster according to the expected theoretical structure in homosexual (gay and lesbian), and heterosexual. Third, the convergent and discriminant validity of the ACE-ASF will be evaluated to confirm that each dimension of abuse is accurately and distinctly measured, ensuring the differentiation between various adverse experiences. Fourth, measurement invariance of the ACE-ASF will be tested based on sexual orientation and self-perception of abuse (feelings of fear, feeling trapped, mistreatment). Finally, as a fifth objective, a Network Analysis of Self-Perceived Abuse will be conducted, focusing on key variables such as feelings of fear, feeling trapped, mistreatment, and the number of breakup attempts, as well as examining differences between groups according to sexual orientation (gay, lesbian, and heterosexual). This analysis aims to identify the central and peripheral nodes within the network of abuse perception, offering new insights into the complex interrelationships between adverse childhood experiences, and self-perception of abuse.

Methods

Research design

An instrumental study was conducted in two sequential phases, following contemporary guidelines for validating educational and psychological instruments [4] and best practices for adapting and translating preexisting measures [7, 13, 47, 48, 54]. In the first phase, the original ACE-ASF was linguistically adapted through an iterative translation and back-translation process carried out by specialists in psychometrics and cross-cultural research [18, 41].

To ensure both semantic and conceptual equivalence, the source text (English) and the target text (Spanish) were meticulously compared at each stage of the process (Muñiz et al., 2015). Once the Spanish version was completed, it was subjected to a small pilot review with a select group of participants to verify its clarity, cultural relevance, and readability in Ecuador. The second phase concentrated on collecting empirical evidence regarding the reliability, validity, and measurement consistency of the instrument across three groups: homosexual (gay and lesbian), and heterosexual.

Participants

A total of 520 participants were recruited through non‐probabilistic convenience sampling methods [15]. Participants were divided into three groups according to self‐identified sexual orientation: 260 in the homosexuality group (130 individuals in the gay group, 130 in the lesbian group), and 260 in the heterosexual group (Table 1). Exclusion criteria eliminated any individual who failed to complete the instruments fully or did not meet the age and enrollment requirements.

Measures

Ad‐hoc sociodemographic survey

The participants filled out a short, study-specific questionnaire collecting age, sex, ethnicity, and socioeconomic level. Three more yes/no items evaluated experiences of relational dynamics: whether they had been afraid of their partner, felt trapped in the relationship, or felt mistreated by their partner.

Adverse Childhood Experiences Abuse Short Form (ACE‐ASF)

The ACE‐ASF covers eight Likert‐type items (0 = never to 4 = always) about abuse‐related childhood experiences. In previous publications [41], exploratory factor analysis reported two subscales: physical/emotional abuse (items 1- 4) and sexual abuse (items 5—8). Confirmatory factor analysis demonstrated strong fit of this two‐factor structure with χ2 (19) = 60.526,RMSEA = 0.036; CFI = 0.990; TLI = 0.986 and demonstrated metric invariance across sexes. Internal consistency coefficients were previously reported to be acceptable for the sexual abuse subscale (α = 0.83) and moderate for the physical/emotional abuse subscale.

Self-perceived abuse

We asked about the self-perception of abuse, including whether the relationship left you trapped, which helps assess whether the ability to leave that relationship was felt. In pursuit of this, three yes/no questions were asked: “Do you feel, or have you ever felt afraid of your partner?”, “Do you feel, or have you ever felt trapped in your relationship?”, “Have you ever felt mistreated in your relationship?” [1].

Procedure

Data were collected in person following approval from the Pontificia Universidad Católica del Ecuador’s Human Research Ethics Committee (Protocol No. PV‐14‐2022) and in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. University administrators allowed for invitation of students to undergo organized group sessions. Researchers at each session provided an explanation of the study’s objectives, stressed that participation was voluntary and informed participants of their right to withdraw at any time with no penalties. A unique anonymous code for each participant was created after completing the written informed consent form and receiving the information written in writing. At first, the participants took an ad hoc sociodemographic survey, which comprised the 3 yes/no questions regarding fear, feeling trapped and being mistreated in their current romantic relationship. Next, they completed the eight‐item ACE‐ASF, which took approximately five minutes. Finally, the DVQ‐VP (victimization and perpetration) subscales were given, taking an additional 5 min on average. As childhood abuse and dating violence are sensitive issues the research team members had undergone training in trauma‐informed approaches to minimize possible distress. Confidentiality and data protection procedures were observed and a list of psychological support resources for those participants who experienced emotional distress when responding was accessible. After completion, all questionnaires were stored and analyzed in the OpenClinic data management system. Each form was checked through via research assistants who ensured that all completed forms were checked correctly prior to data entry.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed with R (version 4.4.3; [51] and JASP (version 0.19.3). One, sociodemographic variables including sex, ethnicity, socioeconomic status and relationship items (i.e., having ever felt fear of one’s partner, feeling trapped, or feeling mistreatment) were presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables (age, number of breakup attempts) were reported with means, standard deviations, medians, and ranges, and their distributional properties were assessed with skewness and kurtosis. Shapiro–Wilk [58] tests for univariate normality were conducted formally in each of the questionnaires (including overall scale score,see Table 2) to see if the subsequent analyses needed parametric or nonparametric tests. Item‐level descriptive statistics for the ACE‐ASF were computed, including mean, SD, skewness, and kurtosis for each of the eight items. Following this, Guttman’s Lambda 2 (λ₂) and Lambda 6 (λ₆), Greatest Lower Bound (GLB), McDonald’s omega, and Composite Reliability (CR) measures were estimated for internal consistency. For every reliability coefficient, a confidence interval of 95% was given for precision.

Additionally, the Average Inter‐Item Correlation (AIC) indicated to measure the average interrelatedness between items was calculated, to gain more information about the scale's cohesiveness. A Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was performed to assess the structural validity by the implementation of Lavaan package in R [56]. Since multivariate normality assumptions were violated [17, 60], the diagonally weighted least squares (DWLS) estimator was selected. A two‐factor model a factor for physical or emotional abuse (items 1–4) and a factor for sexual abuse (items 5—8) was determined as well with the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) to assess model reliability [54].

If the CFI and TLI were at least 0.95 and SRMR at most 0.08 [11, 32, 59], the fit was considered good. Once model fit was confirmed, factor reliability was enhanced by calculating Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega for each latent dimension [55].

Convergent validity was investigated through calculating the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) for each factor, in which the AVE of 0.50 or greater indicated that items shared an average of at least half variance with the latent construct. For discriminant validity, correlations between factors were assessed using the Heterotrait-Monotrait Ratio (HTMT) with HTMT values of 0.95 or lower [10].

Variance inflation factors (VIF) were computed to remove significant multicollinearity between indicators and less than 10 were considered acceptable. Spearman’s rank‐order correlations indicated how the physical/emotional abuse subscale and sexual abuse subscale scored together, quantifying that each dimension represented a discrete yet related nature of adverse childhood experience and the sum up of the two factors [16]. Measurement invariance across sex groups was evaluated by obtaining a number of nested CFAs [59].

The first model evaluated configural invariance by checking that the identical two‐factor structure was represented in both groups without equality constraints. Metric invariance: metric loadings between variables needed to be equal to be in place between sexes, and scalar invariance: item intercepts being equal. Last though not the least, strict invariance restricted residual variances to be the same. Comparisons among nested models were assessed at each step to understand whether invariance between nested models (ΔCFI ≤ 0.01 and ΔSRMR ≤ 0.015) were maintained [32, 54, 60].

A network analysis was performed to investigate the relationships among the eight ACE‐ASF items [6, 12, 19, 29]. Centrality indices (i.e., betweenness, closeness, strength and expected influence) determined which items were most influential in the network. Furthermore, clustering was implemented (Barrat, Onnela, WS, and Zhang), which helped to identify any potential subclusters between items, thus providing complementary evidence about the internal structure of the scale and illustrating the top types of the abuse items which were most central to respondents’ overall experience.

Results

Within a sample of 520 participants (nHomosexual = 260 and nHeterosexual = 260) in Ecuador, in terms of the central tendencies and dispersion of each of the eight items with ACE-ASF, we observed that the medians varied between 2 and 3 from 1 to 5, meaning in general that responses were observed to fall into a similar clustering around the medium range. Medians of 3 for the two items A1 and A3; whereas, for the remaining items (A2, A4, A5, A6, A7 and A8) median 2. Means of A1 (3.281) and A3 (3.008) were marginally greater than 3, indicating that participants experienced the items more often or with stronger feelings related to those items.

Table 3 and Fig. 1 reported the psychometric characteristics of the original two-factor model of the Adverse Childhood Experiences Abuse Short Form (ACE-ASF) in a sample of homosexual (gay and lesbian), and heterosexual in Ecuador (n = 520). The model consisted of two factors: Physical and Emotional Abuse (F1) and Sexual Abuse (F2). Standardized factor loadings for the indicators of Physical and Emotional Abuse (A1-A4) were between 0.682 and 0.888, while for Sexual Abuse (A5-A8) they were between 0.766 and 0.829; which implies that the two factors had good correlations with their respective latent constructs. For both factors, internal consistency was high at Cronbach's alpha of 0.845 for F1 and 0.883 for F2, and McDonald's omega values of 0.856 and 0.876, respectively. For both factors, composite reliability (CR) was good indeed, surpassing 0.84.

The Average Variance Extracted reflected acceptable convergent validity for both F1 (AVE = 0.606) and F2 (AVE = 0.661). The discriminant validity between the two factors was further supported by HTMT of 0.437, which was below the 0.85 threshold in the literature. The variance inflation factor (VIF) values were also below 3, proving the absence of multicollinearity. The model fit overall was excellent, with the CFI and TLI at 0.999, RMSEA at 0.000 and SRMR at 0.034 (Table 3). The results corroborated that the original two-factor model of ACE-ASF was significantly reliable and valid for measuring childhood abuse experiences of the various sexual orientation groups within the Ecuadorian context. Figure 1 showed that the psychometric model of the ACE-ASF was structured around two clearly defined latent dimensions, physical abuse and sexual abuse, with their respective items loading consistently onto each factor. The model suggested an adequate conceptual organization of the instrument, as the observed indicators were coherently linked to their underlying constructs.

The results of the factorial invariance tests of the ACE-ASF were reported in Table 4 for sexual orientation, and self-perception of abuse. The fit indices across all models under evaluation (configural, metric, scalar, and strict) were excellent (CFI and TLI equal to or greater than 0.999), and it shows that factorial structure of the ACE-ASF was stable and similar both at sexual orientation level and at self-perceiving abuse level. For the comparison between homosexual and heterosexual participants and between gay, lesbian, heterosexual people, all measurements of the invariance showed low chi-square values, adequate degrees of freedom, and non-significant p-values, which means that no significant difference in the structure of the instrument existed between these participants. SRMR values also were below the recommended threshold (0.08), strengthening the models robustness. In terms of self-perception of abuse (fear, feeling trapped or mistreated) the analyses were also show configural, metric, scalar and strict invariance [54] and great fit indices of the models were found in most cases.

Discussion

The focus of the present study was on a study on the psychometric properties, reliability, validity, measurement invariance and network analysis of self-reported abuse of the abbreviated version of the Adverse Childhood Experiences Questionnaire (ACE-ASF) among homosexual, and heterosexual in Ecuador.

The results were largely consistent with the hypotheses and provided strong empirical support for the use of the instrument in a wide range of contexts. First, as in its first hypothesis, the ACE-ASF had good internal consistency among all three groups. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were found to be high for both the physical-emotional abuse dimension (α = 0.845) and the sexual abuse dimension (α = 0.883), both > the acceptable cut off for an alpha = 0.70 (Nunnally & Bernstein, 1994). Likewise, McDonald's omega coefficients were high (ωFactor 1 = 0.856 and ω Factor 2 = 0.876), and composite reliability indices were excellent (CRFactor 1 = 0.849 and CRFactor 2 = 0.884 for).

These findings agree with previous data from other countries [9], α = 0.83) and Romania [41], α = 0.80), thus supporting the generalizability of the ACE-ASF to other cultures. Second, supporting the second hypothesis is also confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), which confirmed the theoretical two-factor structure of the ACE-ASF and effectively separated the dimensions of physical-emotional abuse and sexual abuse. Factor loadings were standardized and ranged from 0.682 to 0.888 for physical-emotional abuse and from 0.766 to 0.829 for sexual abuse. Its model fit was excellent (χ2 = 19.587, p = 0.420,CFI/TLI = 0.999,RMSEA = 0.000; SRMR = 0.034) and this attested that the instrument structure was adequate to detect the relevant constructs [32]. This is consistent with Ford et al. previous discovery [23] and [35] across cultures.

With respect to the third hypothesis, findings validated satisfactory convergent and discriminant validity. The Average Variance Extracted (AVE) was 0.606 for physical-emotional abuse and 0.661 for sexual abuse, which was higher than a threshold of 0.50. In addition, the ratio of HTMT between factors was 0.437, supporting adequate discriminant validity and well below the critical cut-off of 0.85 [10]. Noticing no multicollinearity issues (VIF = 2.765 for physical-emotional abuse, VIF = 1.539 for sexual abuse) indicates the distinctiveness of the constructs collected.

The fourth hypothesis regarding measurement invariance was also realized. Multigroup studies demonstrated that the configural, metric, scalar, and strict models had ideal fit indices for sexual orientation groups (CFI = 0.999; RMSEA = 0.000; SRMR = 0.045) as well as for self-perception of abuse (CFI = 0.999; RMSEA = 0.000; SRMR = 0.043). Even in the most restrictive models, divergences in CFI (ΔCFI ≤ 0.001) and SRMR (ΔSRMR ≤ 0.015) were acceptable (Chen, 2007). This suggests that the ACE-ASF measures its actions equivalently in different individuals and groups so that group analyses can be consistent across the different populations. For the fifth hypothesis, network analyses revealed new perspectives and verified substantial contrasts in the group’s accounts of abuse. For example, homosexual had a significantly denser network (23 connections out of 28 possible, sparsity = 0.179), while heterosexual had slightly less density (22 connections, sparsity = 0.214). In both cases, item I3 (mild physical abuse) was the most influential node where the centrality value had the highest magnitude (betweenness = 1.67; closeness = 1.41; strength = 1.41; expected influence = 1.53 in non-trapped). These results are in line with prior research indicating the central nature of physical abuse in the portrayal of childhood adversity [6].

In addition, the high correlation between feeling trapped and the number of breakups attempts on all networks (weights from 0.33 to 0.63) is also evidence for the continued stability of such variables in violent and traumatic relationships. This emphasizes the importance of interventions directed at childhood abuse history and modern relational patterns.

Although this research offers a number of important perspectives in the health field, significant limitations are noted. Limitations include a non-probabilistic sample restricting the generalizability of the findings to the population. Samples (from regions and sociocultural backgrounds that are larger, more diverse and so on) are needed to make sure that the findings are reflective of the population of interest and not based on the experiences of individual children. Also, the self-report-based measurement could be subject to potential biases, particularly those of selective recall and, if the topic is sensitive such as sexual abuse, social desirability. Use of qualitative approaches or triangulation of sources of data could offer a more nuanced view on adverse childhood experience and their impact.

There are however, some critical limitations that need to be appreciated such as the use of a non-probabilistic type of sample, and the intrinsic bias in self-report instruments and, in certain scenarios, in sensitive subjects. Longitudinal analysis, larger and more heterogeneous sample sizes, and mixed methods designs are needed in the future to further investigate these relationships. In addition, the cross-sectional design of these studies limits causal inferences from childhood adversities on adult relational outcomes. Longitudinal studies would be required to examine the long-term outcomes of early adversity and its impact on psychological well-being and on relational patterns. Researchers could also add in additional risk to the adult outcomes from childhood abuse by exploring such protective factors as resilience, social support, socioeconomic status, and cultural beliefs. Cross-cultural comparisons with populations from other Latin American countries would increase the comparative validity of the ACE-ASF and inform the development of interventions to prevent violence and enhance mental health and wellbeing in at-risk populations.

Conclusions

The study focused on the psychometric measure of the short version of the Adverse Childhood Experiences Questionnaire (ACE-ASF) within homosexual and heterosexual in Ecuador. The first objective consisted of evaluation of the instrument reliability. The results validated the ACE-ASF as a reliable instrument that consistently measures the constructs of childhood abuse across sexual orientations. The stability and internal consistency we have shown indicates that the instrument can be used in various relational contexts and has good reliable measurement for use in different types of relational groups and that results can guide both clinical and research use. A third concern assessed convergent and discriminant validity. The results showed that the ACE-ASF is able to distinguish the various domains of abuse, indicating the accurate distinction of the multiple forms of adversity. This is so that the instrument allows for clear and meaningful assessments with no conceptual overlap that might impede the finding of specific experiences of abuse and the development of appropriate approaches. The fourth aim was to assess for measurement invariance by sexual orientation and self-perception of abuse.

The fifth aim was to perform network analysis of self-perceived abuse. There were patterns of interlinkages of important relationships observed between fear, feeling trapped, mistreatment, and number of breakup attempts. The patterns differed according to sexual orientation, providing new perspectives on the intricate relational relationship-based perception of abuse and adverse childhood experience with reference to the construct of sexual orientation. Recognizing central and peripheral factors in these networks underscores the need to comprehend how people engage with and process their experiences in a manner that can be used to make prevention and intervention strategies more appropriate and specific.
