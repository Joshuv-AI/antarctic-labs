Drug Utilisation of Fall-Risk-Increasing Drugs (FRIDs) and Risk of Hospitalisation: A Nested Case–Control Study Among Community-Dwelling Older Adults
Authors: Lara Perrella, Elena Olmastroni, Sara Mucherino, Andrea Rossi, Valentina Orlando, Manuela Casula, Enrica Menditto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFalls are a leading cause of hospitalisation among older adults, with substantial impacts on quality of life and healthcare costs. Fall-risk-increasing drugs represent an important risk factor.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the association between fall-risk-increasing drug use and the risk of fall-related hospitalisation.MethodsA nested case-control study was conducted using administrative healthcare data from a sample of Italian community-dwelling older adults. Cases included individuals aged ≥65 years hospitalised for fall-related injuries in 2018, matched 1:1 by sex and age with controls who had no fall-related injuries. Fall-risk-increasing drug exposure was assessed by drug class, duration and recency of use. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) were estimated using logistic regression, controlling for polypharmacy and comorbidities.ResultsAmong 16,118 cases and 16,118 controls (68.77% female, mean age 79.76 years), cases showed a higher prevalence of polypharmacy (five to nine drugs: 42.03 vs 37.16%; ten or more drugs: 7.02 vs 5.44%; p < 0.0001) and multimorbidity (three or more diseases: 6.42 vs 2.82%; p < 0.0001). Fall-risk-increasing drug use was higher in cases (37.70 vs 26.49%, p < 0.0001), with a 51% increased risk of fall-related hospitalisation (aOR 1.51, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.44–1.59). Opioids (aOR 1.40, 95% CI 1.31–1.50) and antidepressants (aOR 1.45, 95% CI 1.36–1.54) were associated with the highest risk. Prolonged fall-risk-increasing drug exposure (>9 months; aOR 1.69, 95% CI 1.57–1.83) and current exposure (within 30 days before hospitalisation; aOR 1.78, 95% CI 1.68–1.89) showed higher odds.ConclusionsFall-risk-increasing drug use was associated with a higher risk of fall-related hospitalisations in older adults. These findings support the importance of optimising prescribing and managing polypharmacy as potential strategies to improve patient safety and reduce the healthcare burden.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40266-026-01312-x.

Introduction

Falls represent a leading cause of injury and hospitalisation among older adults [1], affecting about 30% of individuals aged 65 years and above annually [2]. Approximately 20–30% of falls result in a serious injury [1], leading to increased care needs, reduced quality of life and major hospital costs across many European countries [3]. It is widely recognised that the risk of fall-related hospitalisation in older adults arises from a combination of factors, including age-related changes, comorbid conditions, polypharmacy and environmental influences [4–6]. In Europe, almost 50% of hospital admissions are preventable, and approximately 70% of these involve patients aged 65 years or older receiving five or more medications [7]. Among extrinsic risk factors, several medication-related determinants have been associated with adverse outcomes in older adults, including polypharmacy, recent initiation of new medications, regime complexity and potential inappropriate medication use [8, 9]. Importantly, these factors have been consistently linked to an increased risk of falls, which represent a major cause of morbidity and hospitalisation in older populations. These findings highlight the importance of addressing potentially inappropriate medication use as a modifiable contributor to fall-related adverse outcomes [8, 9].

Over the past few decades, several systematic reviews and meta-analyses have highlighted an association between the use of specific medication classes and an increased risk of fall-related hospitalisation [10–12]. Pooled odds ratios indicated an increased risk with opioids (60%), antiepileptic drugs (55%), anti-Parkinson medications (54%), antipsychotics (54%), antidepressants (57%), loop diuretics (36%) and polypharmacy (75%) [10–12]. Accordingly, several medication classes associated with an increased risk of fall-related hospitalisation have been classified as fall-risk-increasing drugs (FRIDs) and listed within the Sweden National Board of Health and Welfare [13]. Although this association has been identified, important knowledge gaps persist. Only a limited number of studies have specifically investigated the risk associated with certain medication classes, and robust evidence from large-scale, population-based or longitudinal studies remains scarce [10–12]. There is also no clear consensus on the most effective interventions aimed at reducing falls through optimisation of prescribing appropriateness, likely reflecting the variability in treatment outcomes due to differences in patient characteristics [14]. Moreover, it remains challenging to designate some medication classes in this context, particularly those acting on the central nervous system, as purely FRIDs, as they are widely prescribed for their therapeutic benefits in managing specific conditions. To address these gaps, the European Geriatric Medicine Society Task and Finish Group on FRIDs recently published a position statement on the management of FRID prescribing in older adults, emphasising a structured medication review and deprescribing [15]. Beyond the well-known tools supporting healthcare professionals in this process, such as the STOPP/START and Beers criteria and PRISCUS and FORTA lists [16–19], the Screening Tool of Older Persons Prescriptions in older adults with high fall risk (STOPPFall) was specifically developed to address this issue, providing a more comprehensive and detailed list of FRIDs [20].

Although the association between FRID exposure and fall-related hospitalisations is well established, further large-scale real-world evidence is needed to better characterise how different patterns and complexities of medication use influence this risk. Therefore, the aim of this population-based nested case-control study was to investigate the association between FRID use and fall-related hospitalisation among community-dwelling adults aged 65 years and older, with a specific focus on moving beyond a simple user versus non-user classification to capture the impact of varying exposure patterns and treatment complexity.

Materials and Methods

Data Source and Study Population

A retrospective observational study was carried out in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines [21], using administrative healthcare data from eight Local Health Units in the Campania and Lombardy regions of Italy, located in Southern and Northern Italy, respectively, and covering both urban and rural areas, representing approximately 18% of the adult Italian population [22]. The database includes patient-level demographic characteristics, electronic records of outpatient pharmacy dispensing, and hospital discharge data for approximately 129,000 community-dwelling residents aged ≥40 years collected between 2014 and 2018. Further details on the data sources are reported in Table S1 of the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM) and have also been described in previous drug utilisation studies [23]. The database includes information on diagnosis collected from the hospital discharge records, classified according to the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (Table S2 of the ESM); information on medications reimbursed by the national health systems collected from the electronic records of outpatient pharmacy dispensing and classified according to the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical code (Table S3 of the ESM). For the purpose of this analysis, data from 1 January, 2017 to 31 December, 2018 were used.

Study Design

A nested case-control study design was employed (Fig. S1 of the ESM). Cases were defined as individuals aged ≥65 years with at least one hospital admission for accidental falls and/or fall-related injuries between 1 January and 31 December, 2018. The date of the first admission during this period was defined as the index date. For each case, one control was selected using a 1:1 matching ratio. Controls were individuals without any hospitalisation for accidental falls or fall-related injuries during the same year as their matched case. To ensure comparability, controls were matched to cases based on sex, age (± 3 years) and region of residence. Each control was assigned the same index date as their matched case to ensure temporal alignment. Exposure Definition

For each case-control set, exposure to FRIDs was assessed retrospectively over the one-year period preceding the index date. Dispensation data from pharmaceutical databases were used to identify exposure to FRIDs who are active on central nervous system for which the literature is compelling [11, 13, 20]. The FRIDs included in the study are defined by the following third-level ATC codes: opioids “N02A”, dopaminergic agents “N04B”, antipsychotics “N05A” (excluding lithium “N05AN01”), anxiolytics “N05B”, hypnotics and sedatives “N05C”, and antidepressants “N06A”. Participants were classified as FRID users if they had at least one dispensation of any FRID during this period, and as non-users otherwise. Among FRID users, exposure was further characterised according to (i) duration, calculated as the cumulative number of days of FRID coverage based on the defined daily dose of each dispensed drug, and (ii) recency, defined as the interval between the last day of FRID coverage and the index date. Based on recency, users were categorised into three subgroups: current users (last day of coverage ≤30 days before the index date), recent users (31–91 days) and past users (>91 days). Exposure to individual FRID classes was also assessed.

Covariates

Information on potential confounding variables was retrieved in the year preceding the index date. Exposure to antihypertensive drugs (“C02”, “C03”, “C07”, “C08”, “C09”, “C01D”, “G04CA”) [10, 20], antidiabetic agents (ATC “A10B”) [12, 24], proton pump inhibitors (ATC “A02BC01”, “A02BC02”, “A02BC03”, “A02BC04”, “A02BC05”) [12, 25, 26] and anti-osteoporotic drugs (“M05BA”, “M05BB”, “M05BX”, “H05AA02”, “G03XC02”, “G03XC01”) [12, 27] were considered. Subjects were classified as exposed if they received at least three dispensations of a given drug class during the year prior to the index date, thus identifying long-term rather than occasional use; otherwise, they were considered unexposed. Two medication burden indicators were also calculated to capture the cognitive and sedative effects of dispensed medications: the anticholinergic cognitive burden and sedative load scores. These scales quantify the cognitive and sedative effects associated with prescribed medications. To evaluate cumulative anticholinergic pharmacological burden, we identified patients who had a total score of ≥3 on either the anticholinergic cognitive burden score or sedative load score or at least 3 subsequent days during the observation period; in such cases, the indicator was considered present [28–30]. Only drugs marketed in Italy and reimbursed by the national health system were included in these evaluations. A full list of the medications considered is provided in the article published by Casula et al. [31]. Multimorbidity and polypharmacy were evaluated for each case and control. The Multisource Comorbidity Score, encompassing 34 conditions, was calculated [32] based on diagnoses recorded in the year prior to the index date. The Multisource Comorbidity Score was categorised into four comorbidity classes: 0–4, 5–9, 10–14 and ≥15 conditions. For each case and control, the number of unique medications dispensed was assessed in each quarter preceding the index date with the highest quarterly value over the 12-month period used to determine the level of polypharmacy [33]. Polypharmacy was therefore defined as the concomitant dispensing of five to nine distinct drugs (fifth-level ATC code), while excessive polypharmacy was defined as ten or more distinct drug substances [7].

Statistical Analyses

Chi-square and t-tests were used to evaluate differences between cases and controls for categorical and continuous variables, respectively. Conditional logistic regression models were fitted to estimate adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between exposure to FRIDs and the risk of accidental falls and/or fall-related injuries. Models were adjusted for the above reported covariates. A stratified analysis was performed by sex and age classes (65–74, 75–84, ≥85 years). Differences between strata were tested including in the model an interaction term between FRID exposure and stratification variables. All analyses were performed using the Statistical Analysis System Software (Version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Statistical significance was set at the 0.05 level. All p-values were two-sided.

Notably, a significantly higher proportion of fallers used FRIDs compared with non-fallers (37.70 vs 26.49%, p < 0.0001). Among FRIDs, opioids, dopaminergic agents, antipsychotics and antidepressants were more frequently used by fallers, each showing a statistically significant difference compared with non-fallers (p < 0.0001). Polypharmacy was also more common among fallers, with a higher percentage taking five to nine drugs or ten or more drugs compared with non-fallers (42.03 vs 37.16% and 7.02 vs 5.44%, respectively; p < 0.0001). Conversely, non-fallers were more likely to take zero to four drugs (57.40 vs 50.94%). Comorbidity levels, measured by the Multisource Comorbidity Score index, were higher in fallers who showed a greater prevalence of multiple comorbidities. Specifically, 6.42% of fallers had three or more comorbidities compared with only 2.82% of non-fallers (p < 0.0001). Conversely, a significantly lower proportion of fallers had no comorbidities (61.27 vs 72.09%). Fallers exhibited a significantly higher prevalence of sedative and anticholinergic burden than non-fallers. Specifically, 60.56% of fallers had a sedative load score ≥3, versus 54.75% of non-fallers (p < 0.0001) and 14.34% reached an anticholinergic cognitive burden score ≥3, compared with 9.96% of non-fallers (p < 0.0001). Regarding other medication classes, the use of antihypertensive drugs was slightly lower among fallers (70.60%) than non-fallers (72.70%, p < 0.0001). The use of anti-osteoporotic agents and proton pump inhibitors was slightly higher among fallers (5.01 vs 4.28%, p = 0.002 and 47.33 vs 43.05%, p < 0.0001, respectively). No significant difference was observed in antidiabetic medication use between groups (13.71 vs 13.03%, p = 0.07).

Individuals who used FRIDs faced a 51% higher likelihood of fall-related hospitalisation compared with non-users (aOR 1.51, 95% CI 1.44–1.59, Fig. 2). The magnitude of risk varied across medication classes: opioid use was associated with a 40% higher likelihood of fall-related hospitalisation (aOR 1.40, 95% CI 1.31–1.50), while antidepressant use showed an even stronger association, corresponding to a 45% higher odds (aOR 1.45, 95% CI 1.36–1.54). Antipsychotic use was linked to a moderate, yet statistically significant, 12% increase in risk (aOR 1.12, 95% CI 1.01–1.24), whereas the association for dopaminergic agents, although suggestive of increased risk, did not reach statistical significance (aOR 1.09, 95% CI 0.96–1.24). Individuals with short-term FRID exposure (<3 months) exhibited a 37% higher risk of fall-related hospitalisation compared with non-users (aOR 1.37, 95% CI 1.29–1.46). With prolonged exposure (3–9 months and >9 months), the risk progressively increased, reaching nearly 69% higher odds of fall-related hospitalisation among long-term users. Moreover, current FRID users had a markedly higher risk, with odds of falling increased by 78% compared with non-users (aOR 1.78, 95% CI 1.68–1.89). Recent users (those who had discontinued more recently) still faced a 34% higher risk (aOR 1.34, 95% CI 1.20–1.48), whereas past users showed only a small, not statistically significant increase in fall likelihood (aOR 1.08, 95% CI 0.98–1.17). When stratified by sex (Table 2), patterns of fall-related hospitalisation associated with FRID use were consistent across groups, with slight variations. Both female and male individuals showed elevated odds of falls, with male individuals exhibiting a slightly higher risk (aOR = 1.52, 95% CI 1.37–1.69) compared with female individuals (aOR = 1.49, 95% CI 1.40–1.59), although this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.30). Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is among the first large-scale population-based studies in Italy to comprehensively evaluate the association between exposure to FRIDs and the risk of fall-related hospitalisation in older adults using routinely collected healthcare data. In contrast to much of the existing literature, which has primarily focused on single drug classes or dichotomous exposure definitions, this study provides a more granular assessment of FRID exposure, incorporating not only overall use but also specific pharmacological classes, duration of exposure and patterns of use over time. This allowed us to identify meaningful heterogeneity in risk across drug classes and exposure windows, highlighting, for example, stronger associations with opioids and a clear gradient of increasing risk with prolonged and current use.

Furthermore, the use of a large nationally representative dataset from Italy, Europe’s oldest population [34], adds important external validity and fills a gap in Southern European pharmacoepidemiological evidence on medication-related fall risk. Findings mainly suggest that individuals with at least one psychoactive FRID prescription showed an approximately 51% higher odds of fall-related hospitalisation. This finding highlights the relevance of FRID exposure as a potential determinant of fall-related hospitalisation and contributes to the existing literature on fall risk in older adults [10–13, 35–39]. Consistent with previous studies, FRIDs acting on the central nervous system, particularly antidepressants and opioids, were associated with a ≥40% increase in fall-related hospitalisation [39, 40]. Dopaminergic agents did not show a statistically significant association with hip fracture risk in adjusted models, although we observed a modest trend toward increased risk (approximately +9%). The lack of statistical significance may be related to the relatively low number of dopaminergic agent users in our cohort, potentially limiting statistical power. Previous evidence by Thorell et al. reported a 78% increased risk of hip fractures associated with the use of dopaminergic agents [39]. This discrepancy may partly reflect differences in the study populations, as our cohort included individuals aged ≥65 years, whereas Thorell et al. focused on subjects aged ≥75 years, who likely represent a frailer population with greater baseline vulnerability to fractures. Conversely, our results align with Yang et al., which demonstrated that adequate standard dosing of anti-Parkinson medications (dopaminergic agents) may reduce the risk of falls, likely by improving postural stability and gait performance [37]. Collectively, results from different healthcare systems and populations support these study findings and highlight the importance of targeting specific drug classes, especially antidepressants and opioids, in fall prevention efforts. Additional analyses revealed that both recent and prolonged FRID exposure was associated with a higher likelihood of fall-related hospitalisation, emphasising that the risk is not limited to the treatment initiation phase but remains a persistent concern [37]. This emphasises the importance of considering medication history during the prescribing process to minimise fall risk [40–43]. Data observed highlight the dynamic nature of medication-related fall risk and the need for ongoing monitoring and deprescribing efforts in older adults receiving long-term pharmacological treatments. Age-stratified results indicated a stronger association between the use of FRIDs and the risk of falls among individuals aged 65–84 years than controls (65–74 years: OR 1.57, 95% CI 1.40–1.75; 75–84 years: OR 1.58, 95% CI 1.45–1.72). Conversely, those aged over 85 years showed a lower yet statistically significant association (OR 1.38, 95% CI 1.26–1.51), probably reflecting a more cautious approach in the oldest group because of altered risk-benefit profiles, cognitive decline, reduced treatment adherence and limited life expectancy [43]. Additionally, Yang et al. reported that the concomitant use of multiple FRIDs may increase fall risk [37]. Individuals with a high medication burden, reflected by polypharmacy and anticholinergic and sedative load, demonstrated higher odds of fall-related hospitalisation, emphasising the multifactorial nature of fall risk [38]. Other medications, including proton pump inhibitors, may also contribute to falls, possibly through mechanisms such as bone demineralisation with long-term or high-dose use [10–13]. These observations highlight the multifactorial nature of fall risk in older adults and underscore the importance of comprehensive medication management. Multidisciplinary fall-prevention interventions, incorporating a regular medication review, cognitive and functional assessments, environmental modifications and individualised exercise programmes, are recommended by international guidelines [16–20]. In this context, medication reviews and deprescribing, using explicit or implicit criteria such as STOPP/START, FORTA and PRISCUS criteria [16–20], are essential. However, barriers to deprescribing among healthcare professionals should also be considered, such as the persistent under-recognition of falls as adverse drug events [35]. These challenges may stem from the widespread use of FRIDs in the older population, the limited strength of evidence supporting deprescribing in certain contexts, and the inherent difficulties in modifying long-standing treatment habits and lifestyle behaviours in older adults. These observations support the need for time-sensitive exposure-tailored interventions. A patient-centred approach, supported by data-driven tools and multidisciplinary collaboration, is essential to improve adherence, enhance safety and ensure sustainable care.

Strengths and Limitations

Some limitations should be noted. Administrative data record dispensed prescriptions but not actual consumption. Furthermore, the study did not assess outcomes such as readmission, in-hospital mortality or length of stay, which could provide additional insight into the severity and clinical impact of fall-related hospitalisations. This was because of the study design and the limited and non-uniform follow-up window available for all included subjects. The data source used for this study was primarily designed for administrative and reimbursement purposes, limiting the availability of covariates such as clinical parameters and lifestyle and environmental factors. Over-the-counter drugs and not reimbursed medications were excluded, potentially underestimating FRID exposure. Although multiple indicators were used to characterise FRID exposure, including drug classes, duration, and timing of last exposure, information on medication indications, dosing, intermittent use and self-medication was unavailable. Moreover, although the literature on FRIDs active on the central nervous system is compelling, the literature regarding other FRIDs remains conflicting. This is the case of cardiovascular medications, namely antihypertensive drugs, which seem to be associated with an increased fracture risk related to orthostatism/hypotension when administered at doses higher than those required for adequate blood pressure control [13, 44–46]. Notably, fall-related hospitalisations impose a substantial burden on healthcare systems. Prior studies indicate individuals exposed to polypharmacy and complex comorbidities incur higher healthcare utilisation and costs [47, 48]. Consistent with this evidence, our results suggest that older adults hospitalised because of falls were more likely to be exposed to FRIDs, polypharmacy and comorbidities, underscoring the combined clinical and economic impact. Future research should focus on quantifying the economic burden of falls, comparing fallers and non-fallers. Such investigations could inform targeted strategies to reduce both the clinical risk and the healthcare economic burden in ageing populations.

However, antihypertensive medications were not included as FRID exposure, as the use of administrative data alone may introduce substantial exposure misclassification, particularly in the absence of information on treatment indication, dose titration and duration of use. Moreover, some subgroup estimates may have been affected by limited statistical power, as reflected by wide CIs. We attempted to mitigate confounding by adjusting for a wide range of covariates influencing frailty, including proxies for chronic conditions, overall medication burden, comorbidities and polypharmacy that can impact on the risk of fall. However, residual confounding cannot be completely excluded. Furthermore, the nested case-control design strengthened comparability between cases and controls and reduced confounding. Nonetheless, as with all observational studies, a causal inference cannot be established. These findings should therefore be interpreted as evidence of temporal associations rather than causality.

Despite these limitations, this study has several important strengths. First, it was based on a large population-based administrative database, allowing the inclusion of a substantial number of older adults and ensuring high external validity. The nested case-control design enhanced comparability between cases and controls while reducing selection bias. Furthermore, the use of multiple complementary indicators to characterise FRID exposure, including drug classes, duration of use and timing of last exposure, allowed for a more nuanced assessment of medication-related fall risk compared with studies relying on single exposure definitions. In addition, extensive adjustment for proxies of frailty, comorbidities, overall medication burden and polypharmacy helped to partially mitigate confounding, strengthening the robustness of the observed associations. Finally, the focus on fall-related hospitalisations ensured a clinically relevant and objectively defined outcome, reducing outcome misclassification and enhancing the reliability of the findings.

Conclusions

This population-based study addresses a critical literature gap by providing large-scale evidence on the impact of FRIDs within the Italian healthcare system, a demographic relevant context in Europe owing to its advanced ageing population. Our findings highlight an association between exposure to specific drug classes and fall-related hospitalisation, supporting further attention to these medications within fall-prevention strategies. The observed associations across both short- and long-term exposure patterns suggest the potential relevance of continuous medication monitoring. Future research should integrate clinical complexities such as disease severity and expand the analysis to other potential FRIDs. Investigations should also quantify the economic impact of falls, focusing on readmissions and mortality.
