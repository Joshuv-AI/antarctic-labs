Automated conceptual earned value management
Authors: Haytham Elmousalami, Abdulaziz Alotaibi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Traditional Earned Value Management (EVM) is one of the most widely adopted project-control methodologies; however, its application is fundamentally constrained during the conceptual stage because reliable cost, schedule, variance, and risk assessments cannot be generated before actual project progress data becomes available. This paper introduces Automated Conceptual Earned Value Management (ACEVM), a novel project-control concept, methodology, and decision-support framework that extends EVM from a retrospective performance-monitoring tool into a predictive, proactive and intelligent project-control system. ACEVM enables the automated estimation of Budget at Completion, Duration at Completion, Cost Variance, Schedule Variance, and Conceptual Project Risk using limited early-stage project information before project execution begins. The framework was developed and validated using 1,277 completed Field Canal Improvement Projects (FCIPs). Twenty-five predictors were screened through factor analysis and stepwise regression and used to train 60 optimized machine-learning model instances, defined as 12 candidate algorithms evaluated across five ACEVM prediction outputs. Hyperparameters were optimized using the Artificial Bee Colony algorithm, while model evaluation considered predictive accuracy, robustness, explainability, and computational CO2 emissions. The best-performing models were LightGBM for budget prediction, Extremely Randomized Trees for duration and cost variance prediction, AdaBoost for duration variance prediction, and Extremely Randomized Trees for risk classification.

Introduction

Scientists have recently highlighted that investments in infrastructure projects have a significant impact on global gross domestic product (GDP)1. Due to their intrinsic complexity and uniqueness, construction projects involve a variety of risks and uncertainties, such as the potential for budget overruns and delays in project completion2. According to recent studies, 90% of projects are likely to exceed their budgets and schedules3. To complete construction projects and measure key performance indicators (KPIs), it is essential to set reasonable conceptual estimates for both cost and duration4. Therefore, for effective management of these projects and their impact on the global economy, a detailed analysis of project characteristics, such as cost, duration, and associated risks, is crucial5.

Earned value management (EVM) Background

The foundation for organizing, managing, and completing construction projects is a cost estimate and a time baseline. In the late 19th century, industrial engineers in the United States conceived Earned Value Management (EVM) systems6. A project management methodology, known as earned value management (EVM), is used to estimate project costs and schedule performance7. The US Department of Defense used several performance metrics to enhance EVM systems in the 1960s8. EVM is used to anticipate time and cost performance, and its accuracy depends on how well the project is progressing9.

Sustainable Artificial Intelligence (AI)

Machine learning computational demands have risen sharply, doubling about every 3.4 months, far outpacing Moore’s Law12,13. This rapid growth makes relying on increasingly large and complex models environmentally unsustainable14. Expanding parameter sizes in modern AI, especially deep learning, has significantly increased energy use during both training and inference15. Large models such as BERT, GPT-2, and Transformer models require substantial power and generate considerable carbon emissions; for example, GPT-3 training consumed approximately 936 MWh, corresponding to roughly 550 metric tons of CO216.

These concerns have elevated the importance of Environmental Sustainability in Computational Sciences (ESCS), green computing and transparent carbon accounting for AI-enabled research. Recent carbon-accounting studies emphasize that reported emissions depend on energy source, computing location, hardware configuration, training duration and inference frequency; therefore, sustainable model selection should report both accuracy and computational impact65–68. In this study, CO2 emissions are treated as a complementary model-selection criterion rather than as a substitute for predictive validity.

Modern approaches show that automated feature engineering, ensemble learning and explainable machine learning can improve construction cost and duration prediction, but most applications remain domain-specific and are usually evaluated using one or two outputs rather than an integrated cost-duration-variance-risk framework28–34,63,64,69.

Research gap

At the preliminary stage of a project, the project duration (PD) equals zero (PD = 0.00), and both the actual cost (AC) and the earned value (EV) are also equal to zero. Consequently, traditional Earned Value Management (EVM) generates only a single curve, namely the planned value (PV) curve. Under these conditions, the budget at completion (BAC) and the scheduled project duration (SPD) can be calculated, as illustrated in Fig. 1(A). However, once the project progresses (PD > 0.00), traditional EVM becomes capable of generating all cost and time performance indicators, including estimated project duration (EPD), estimate at completion (EAC), cost variance at completion (CVAC), and duration variance at completion (DVAC), as shown in Fig. 1(B). Therefore, these indicators cannot be predicted using traditional EVM at the initial stage of the project (PD = 0.00). Moreover, traditional EVM is widely recognized for producing unreliable and inconsistent time and cost forecasts during the early stages of a project35. As a result, project owners, consultants, and contractors require accurate predictions of project budget and duration, particularly in the early phases of the project life cycle, to support effective financial planning and the implementation of timely corrective actions.

Most existing studies develop only one or two predictive models for either cost or duration and do not jointly address cost variance, duration variance, risk classification, explainability and computational emissions. In addition, the current dataset is specific to FCIPs; therefore, this paper no longer claims universal generalizability across all construction sectors. The novelty lies in integrating conceptual EVM outputs, ABC-optimized machine learning, SHAP explainability, carbon-emission assessment, statistical validation, and risk classification into one early-stage ACEVM decision-support framework.

Research objectives

The main objective is to develop an interpretable and sustainable ACEVM framework that estimates project budget, duration, variances and risk at the conceptual stage of FCIPs, when traditional EVM has no actual progress data and therefore cannot compute reliable completion indicators. The framework is designed to support early budgeting, scheduling and risk screening while explicitly reporting the environmental cost of computation.

ACEVM system framework

The proposed Automated Conceptual Earned Value Management (ACEVM) methodology establishes an end-to-end, low-carbon AI framework that integrates data-driven cost, schedule, and sustainability intelligence into project performance forecasting. The process begins with the collection of historical project datasets, followed by rigorous data preprocessing, including cleaning, normalization, and structuring. Feature engineering techniques, such as factor analysis, stepwise regression, and feature selection, are applied to extract the most influential variables affecting project performance. The dataset is then partitioned into training, validation, and testing subsets, with ten-fold cross-validation employed to ensure model robustness. Multiple machine learning (ML) models are trained and evaluated simultaneously, on prediction accuracy and on associated CO2 emissions, ensuring that sustainability is embedded as a core optimization objective rather than a secondary constraint.

To further enhance predictive performance and computational efficiency, the Artificial Bee Colony (ABC) algorithm is employed for hyperparameter optimization. The ABC algorithm iteratively searches for optimal model configurations by minimizing prediction errors (e.g., MAPE, MSE) while satisfying termination criteria, ultimately selecting the most efficient and accurate model. The optimized ML model is then used to predict key Earned Value Management (EVM) indicators, including Predicted Budget at Completion (PBAC), Predicted Cost Variance at Completion (PCVAC), Predicted Duration at Completion (PDAC), and Predicted Duration Variance at Completion (PDVAC). These outputs are integrated into a Project Performance Report (PPR), enabling automated, forward-looking decision support. By jointly optimizing accuracy, cost, schedule, and carbon efficiency, the ACEVM framework provides a scalable and sustainable AI-driven solution for proactive project control and low-carbon infrastructure management (FIg. 2).

Data collection and feature engineering

Field Canal Improvement Projects (FCIPs) are essential interventions for sustainable water management, enhancing irrigation efficiency and optimizing equitable water distribution37. As shown in Fig. 3, the branch canals are smaller than the main canal, and they supply water to the sub-branches. The sub-branches are the smallest canals in the system, and they deliver water to individual farms. The field canal pumps are small pumps that are used to lift water from the canals to the fields to supply water to groups of farms.

Fig. 3Diagram of irrigation canals system38.

Diagram of irrigation canals system38.

A dataset of 1,277 anonymised historical Field Canal Improvement Projects (FCIPs) in Egypt was used in this study. FCIPs are hydraulic irrigation-infrastructure projects involving mesqa pipelines, valves, pumps, electrical boards and canal service areas. Because these characteristics are sector-specific, the dataset is representative of FCIPs but not automatically representative of buildings, transportation or industrial projects. Dataset provenance: the original FCIP data were collected in 2016 by Haytham Elmousalami while serving as an irrigation engineer in Egypt, in collaboration with the prior FCIP research team39,40. The dataset was updated and expanded in 2023 for the present ACEVM analyses. Data were compiled from questionnaire survey evidence, historical FCIP project cases, contract agreements, site documentation, engineering reports and project archives for completed irrigation-improvement projects in Egypt. The records describe project-level civil, mechanical, electrical and contextual variables used for conceptual cost, duration, variance and risk modelling. Permission to access and use the FCIP dataset for this study was formally granted by the Irrigation Improvement Sector - East Delta, Zagazig, Egyptian Ministry of Water Resources and Irrigation, under the official Dataset Permission and Provenance Confirmation Letter, Ref. No. R&D202317037799, dated 17 March 2023. The dataset was analysed in project-level, de-identified form and does not contain identifiable worker or participant information. Second, stepwise regression was applied to the retained variables to select the final predictive feature groups.

Variables with dominant eigenvalues below 1.0 were excluded from the default conceptual modelling pipeline unless they were outputs. ML development

After the data engineering procedures, the gathered data were divided into three separate sets: 70% training, 15% validation and 15% testing. To minimise bias, 10-fold cross-validation was used on the training/validation data where applicable. The candidate algorithms included five single-learning algorithms: artificial neural networks, polynomial regression, multiple linear regression, support vector machines and decision trees. Seven ensemble-learning methods were also evaluated: random forest, AdaBoost, bagged decision trees, gradient boosting, Light Gradient Boosting Machine, Extremely Randomized Trees and XGBoost.

These 12 candidate algorithms were evaluated across the PPC and PPD outputs as model-output combinations rather than as 24 unique algorithms. Specifically, 12 algorithms were evaluated for PBAC and PCVAC, 12 algorithms were evaluated for PDAC and PDVAC, and 12 classification algorithms were evaluated for PPR. Thus, the study included 60 optimized machine-learning model instances, calculated as 12 candidate algorithms applied to each of the five ACEVM prediction outputs reported in Tables 7, 8, 9, 10 and 13. This count is distinct from the fixed 60-trial optimizer evaluation budget reported in Table 6.

Evaluation metrics

Bee Colony Optimization (BCO) stands as a bio-inspired optimization algorithm designed to replicate the foraging behavior observed in honeybee colonies for solving intricate problems51. Situated within the broader framework of Swarm Intelligence, this algorithm harnesses the collective actions of social organisms to address complex challenges52. BCO mirrors the roles within a real honeybee colony, distinguishing among three primary types: employed bees, onlooker bees, and scout bees53. Employed bees play a role akin to foragers actively exploring and sharing information about food sources. Onlooker bees observe the dances of employed bees, selecting food sources to investigate based on the quality of solutions discovered. Scout bees, on the other hand, discover new food sources to replace depleted ones, as shown in Fig. 7.

Fig. 7Artificial Bee Colony (ABC) Optimization steps.

Artificial Bee Colony (ABC) Optimization steps.

The BCO algorithm follows an iterative process, commencing with an initial population of employed bees, each representing a potential solution to an optimization problem, as in Algorithm 1. These employed bees explore the solution space, evaluating their solutions. Subsequently, onlooker bees select solutions for further exploration based on the quality of solutions identified by employed bees. Solutions that fail to yield improvements are discarded, and scout bees introduce new, random solutions to take their place54.

The fundamental concept underlying BCO is to find a delicate balance between exploration (discovering new solutions) and exploitation (selecting the best solutions) to converge toward optimal or high-quality approximations for the given optimization problem. This approach enables efficient adaptation to changing problem landscapes and has proven effective in addressing various optimization challenges, including routing, scheduling, and function optimization. BCO’s strength lies in its ability to emulate nature’s efficient search for solution spaces, making it a valuable tool for tackling complex real-world problems across diverse domains such as hyperparameter optimization53.

Predicted project risk (PPR) model

Risk is a probabilistic uncertain condition or event that has a negative or positive impact on the objectives of the project55. Risk assessment is one of the key criteria to evaluate the project, where efficient risk management leads the project to be finished successfully with minimal resource loss. Literature review, questionnaire survey, expert interview, Delphi technique, and brainstorming are the key risk identification tools and techniques that are conducted in construction industry56. However, these are qualitative tools that need experts to be conducted. Therefore, an automatic predicted project risk (PPR) tool is required to accurately predict the project risk. Accordingly, a predicted project risk (PPR) has been incorporated into the ACEVM system to classify the project risk. The conceptual risk of the project can be classified based on the cost variance (PCVAC) and duration variance (PDVAC) of the project.

Risk classes were defined using the signs and combined effects of predicted cost variance (PCVAC) and duration variance (PDVAC): A = ahead of schedule and below budget (no risk), B = on budget and on schedule (low risk), C = on budget but behind schedule (moderate risk), D = over budget but on schedule (high risk), and E = over budget and behind schedule (very high risk). Because the labels are rule-derived from variance behaviour rather than subjective expert classes, the PPR model is interpreted as an automated risk-codification layer rather than as evidence of universal risk separability.

The perfect or near-perfect score is plausible because the risk label is generated from deterministic cost-variance and duration-variance rules, and the tree-based classifier reproduced these rule boundaries on the held-out test set. Accordingly, PPR is interpreted as a risk-codification layer for FCIPs rather than as independent evidence that project risk is universally separable across sectors.

All models were executed on an Intel Core i7-class CPU workstation with 32 GB RAM, no discrete GPU, Windows 11 and Python 3.11, with package versions documented in the archived code environment. The environmental impacts of the evaluated algorithms can be broadly classified into lower, moderate, and higher categories based on their computational efficiency. Lightweight and computationally efficient models, such as decision trees, random forest variants, logistic and multiple linear regression, Naive Bayes, K-nearest neighbors, AdaBoost, and LightGBM, exhibit lower environmental impacts due to their simpler structures, parallel processing capabilities, and reduced resource requirements. Algorithms employing iterative boosting strategies, including gradient boosting, stochastic gradient boosting, polynomial regression, and XGBoost, demonstrate a moderate environmental footprint, reflecting a balance between predictive power and computational demand. In contrast, artificial neural networks and support vector machines are associated with higher environmental impacts, as their training processes are more resource-intensive, particularly when handling complex or high-dimensional data.

The carbon results should be interpreted as controlled computational estimates rather than full life-cycle emissions. CodeCarbon does not capture every peripheral device, cooling load or supply-chain impact, and the results will change under different processors, cloud regions and energy mixes. This limitation has therefore been added to guide reproducibility and avoid overstating the sustainability conclusions.

Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI)

XAI was incorporated to improve transparency and address the black-box limitations of machine-learning models. In this study, explainability was achieved using SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations), a game-theoretic and model-agnostic approach that estimates the contribution of each input feature to each prediction58. For Fig. 14, the x-axis represents the SHAP value or feature impact on the model output, while colour represents the relative feature value.

Figure 14(A) presents the SHAP interpretation for the Predicted Budget at Completion (PBAC). The results indicate that total pipeline length is the most influential driver, with higher values strongly increasing the predicted project budget. Construction year and area served also contribute positively, reflecting cost escalation over time and the scale effect of larger service areas. In contrast, irrigation valves show a relatively smaller and sometimes negative contribution, suggesting a limited marginal impact on overall budget compared to major civil quantities. Figure 14(B) illustrates the SHAP analysis for the Predicted Duration at Completion (PDAC). Actual cost and total pipeline length emerge as the dominant factors influencing project duration, confirming that larger and more expensive projects tend to require longer completion times. Construction year and area served have moderate effects, while irrigation valves exert a comparatively minor influence. The distribution of SHAP values highlights both positive and negative impacts, indicating that duration is sensitive to multiple interacting drivers.

Figure 14(C) depicts the SHAP results for the Predicted Budget Variance at Completion (PCVAC), and Fig. 14(D) presents the interpretation for PDVAC. The results indicate that variance-related inputs have strong predictive influence; however, this should not be interpreted as a causal finding. Rather, the SHAP results confirm that the models behave consistently with EVM logic and that future research should investigate deeper causal mechanisms and uncertainty propagation using datasets from multiple project sectors.

Research limitations and future research

Despite the contributions of this paper, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the ACEVM framework was developed and validated using FCIPs in Egypt; therefore, the results are directly applicable to similar irrigation-infrastructure projects and should not be generalized to buildings, transportation or industrial facilities without external validation. Second, the models rely on historical project-data quality and availability; missing records, inconsistent documentation or unobserved contextual factors may influence prediction reliability. Third, the PPR classes are rule-derived from cost and duration variance behaviour, which explains the high classification performance but limits interpretation as a purely independent risk-discovery model. Fourth, uncertainty is treated mainly through data-driven resampling rather than explicit causal or probabilistic modelling.

Future research should validate ACEVM on additional construction sectors and geographic contexts before wider generalization. Important extensions include uncertainty quantification, causal inference, dynamic updating as new project data become available, external validation on independent datasets, hybrid explainability techniques beyond SHAP and carbon assessment under different hardware platforms, cloud regions and electricity mixes. These extensions would strengthen the framework as a sustainable, interpretable and adaptive AI-driven project-management system (Fig. 16).

Conclusions

The principal contribution of this research is the introduction of Automated Conceptual Earned Value Management (ACEVM) as a new project-control paradigm. ACEVM redefines the traditional role of Earned Value Management by extending project-performance assessment to the earliest stages of the project life cycle, where conventional EVM cannot operate effectively due to the absence of actual progress data. Rather than relying on retrospective measurements, ACEVM establishes a predictive framework capable of generating early estimates of project performance, variances, and risks to support strategic decision-making before project execution. This conceptual shift represents a significant advancement in project management theory and practice, moving project control from measurement-based monitoring toward intelligence-driven prediction. The proposed framework provides a foundation for future automated and AI-enabled project-control systems, with the potential to influence the next generation of project planning, governance, and decision-support methodologies.

This research has integrated sustainable and explainable artificial intelligence within construction project management. Hyperparameter optimization using the Artificial Bee Colony algorithm enhanced predictive accuracy while reducing computational overhead, and carbon emission assessment was incorporated as a model-selection criterion. Furthermore, the application of SHAP-based explainability provides transparent insights into the key drivers influencing cost, time, and variance predictions, thereby strengthening trust in AI-assisted decision-making, and improving managerial interpretability.

From a practical perspective, the ACEVM system offers substantial value to project owners, consultants, and contractors by supporting realistic budgeting, scheduling, and risk assessment at the earliest phases of the project lifecycle. The framework facilitates early warning signals, informed bidding strategies, and proactive corrective actions, ultimately improving project performance and resource efficiency. By combining automation, sustainability, and interpretability, this research advances the state of practice in construction project management and establishes a scalable foundation for future AI-driven project control systems.
