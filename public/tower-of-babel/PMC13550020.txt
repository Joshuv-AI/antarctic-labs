Cell Therapy as Metabolic Rescue after Ischemic Stroke: Rewiring Bioenergetics, Redox Homeostasis, and Neurovascular Repair
Authors: Salar Bakhtiyari, Iraj Alipourfard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIschemic stroke represents a dynamic metabolic disorder of the neurovascular unit (NVU) rather than a static vascular occlusion followed by neuronal demise. Immediate oxygen and glucose deprivation rapidly deplete ATP, disrupt the transmembrane ionic gradients, increase glutamate excitotoxicity, and overload mitochondrial with calcium. These events alter glycolytic, lipid, amino acid, and redox pathways. During the subacute and chronic phases, astrocytes, microglia, macrophages, endothelial cells, pericytes, oligodendrocytes, and surviving neurons continue to remodel substrate utilization. These phase-specific metabolic programs either accelerate infarct expansion and blood-brain barrier disruption or facilitate angiogenesis, synaptic plasticity, and tissue repair. Consequently, cell-based therapeutic paradigms have shifted from direct neuronal replacement toward metabolic rescue. Transplanted cells and cell-free derivatives deliver trophic factors, extracellular vesicles, microRNAs, antioxidant signals, mitochondrial cues, and immunoregulatory factors. These signals enhance mitochondrial fitness, restore redox homeostasis, attenuate pro-inflammatory glycolysis, and stabilize endothelial-pericyte coupling to stabilize a permissive neurorehabilitation microenvironment. This review synthesizes post-stroke metabolic landscapes and evaluates how mesenchymal stromal, neural stem/progenitor, endothelial progenitor, cord blood-derived, and mononuclear cells, and extracellular vesicles, may be incorporated into a phase-specific translational framework supported by target-engagement biomarkers and standardized potency assays.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Ischemic stroke, Cell therapy, Mesenchymal stromal cells, Extracellular vesicles, Mitochondria, Lactate, Glycolysis, Oxidative stress, Neurovascular unit, Blood-brain barrier, Metabolic reprogramming

Introduction

Ischemic stroke is a pathological phenomenon that begins when either focal or global cerebral blood flow falls below the level required to sustain oxidative metabolism necessary for neuronal function and survival [1–4]. Although stroke presents clinically as a vascular emergency, the immediate cellular crisis is energetic. Reduced oxygen and glucose delivery lowers adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production, impairs Na+/K+-ATPase activity, depolarizes cell membranes, and promotes excitatory neurotransmitter release that can exacerbate neuronal injury [2–4]. The ischemic core, characterized by severely compromised blood flow, rapidly progresses toward irreversible injury. In contrast, the metabolically impaired penumbra tissue may remain in a salvageable state if perfusion and cellular homeostasis are restored through appropriate therapeutic interventions [1, 3, 4].

Reperfusion therapies, which have revolutionized the management of acute stroke cases, can restore blood flow; however, it is imperative to recognize that the successful recanalization of occluded vessels does not necessarily normalize mitochondrial function, redox balance, inflammatory metabolism, or blood-brain barrier (BBB) integrity [5–7]. This critical gap in our understanding explains why neurovascular injury and the resulting disability may persist and even worsen, despite the re-establishment of blood flow to the affected cerebral regions [1, 5, 6].

A metabolism-centric view is of paramount importance for understanding ischemic stroke pathophysiology. Energy failure and abnormal mitochondrial dynamics are central components of ischemic injury [8]. Lactate accumulation, pH disruption, and altered glycolytic flux further shape tissue viability [9–14]. In parallel, NAD+- and FAD-dependent signaling, lipid peroxidation, ferroptosis, inflammatory immunometabolism, and endothelial-pericyte uncoupling contribute to an interconnected pathological network [7, 9, 15–18]. NVU cell types respond differently to this metabolic stress. Neurons depend heavily on oxidative phosphorylation and calcium buffering, whereas astrocytes can increase glycolysis and shuttle lactate [11, 12]. Brain endothelial cells are constitutively glycolytic, and pericyte function depends partly on endothelial metabolic support [14, 17, 18]. Microglia and infiltrating macrophages also change substrate use as they transition between injury-promoting and repair-associated states [5, 9]. Neurorestorative therapies should therefore be evaluated not only by engraftment or lesion volume but also by their ability to restore metabolic homeostasis across the NVU (Fig. 1).

Cell therapy is attractive because living cells, conditioned media, and extracellular vesicles (EVs) can influence several metabolic compartments at the same time. Mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs), neural stem/progenitor cells (NSCs/NPCs), endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs), umbilical cord blood cells, and bone marrow mononuclear cells (BMMNCs) release cytokines, growth factors, bioactive lipids, and immunomodulatory signals [19–26]. Cell-derived EVs can deliver regulatory RNAs, proteins, lipids, and mitochondrial components to recipient cells [27–30]. Experimental studies also support mitochondrial transfer or mitochondrial-support signaling as a contributor to recovery [31–35]. These findings have moved the field beyond a simple neuronal replacement model. Most observed benefits are more plausibly explained by paracrine signaling, antioxidant activity, immune-metabolic regulation, angiogenesis, BBB stabilization, mitochondrial support, and enhancement of endogenous plasticity [20–35].

This review revises and expands the metabolic-rescue concept for cell therapy in ischemic stroke. The central argument is that the injured brain should be treated as a metabolically stressed neurovascular ecosystem. A successful stem cell or cell-free product should be matched to the timing and metabolic phenotype of injury, manufactured with potency assays linked to its intended mechanism, and evaluated with biomarkers that show target engagement before functional outcomes are interpreted.

Metabolic Pathology after Ischemic Stroke

The hyperacute phase of cerebral ischemia is characterized primarily by ATP depletion and subsequent excitotoxic cascades. The abrupt loss of oxidative phosphorylation impairs ATP-dependent ion pumps, causing rapid neuronal depolarization and the pathological accumulation of extracellular glutamate [2–4]. Glutamate then drives calcium influx through N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) and alpha-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid (AMPA) receptors, as well as voltage-gated channels [2, 6]. The resulting calcium overload activates downstream proteases, phospholipases, and nitric oxide synthases. These deleterious signaling cascades converge on mitochondria, where membrane-potential loss, electron-transport dysfunction, and mitochondrial permeability transition pore opening (mPTP) promote cytochrome c release and cell death [2–8]. Because mitochondria coordinate ATP synthesis, calcium buffering, redox homeostasis, innate immune signaling, and programmed cell death, mitochondrial dysfunction is a central metabolic lesion rather than a secondary consequence of ischemia [8].

Reperfusion Injury, Oxidative Stress, and Cofactor Depletion

Reperfusion creates a secondary metabolic insult by rapidly reoxygenation of damaged mitochondrial and oxidase systems. This process produces a burst of efflux of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS) [6–9]. RONS then oxidize proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, and endothelial junctional complexes while depleting endogenous antioxidant reserves [6, 7]. Mitochondrial DNA damage and lipid peroxidation can then promote ferroptotic injury in neurons, oligodendrocytes, and vascular cells [7–9]. Oxidative DNA damage also activates poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase-1 (PARP-1), which can rapidly deplete intracellular NAD+ [15, 16]. Loss of this cofactor impairs glycolysis, mitochondrial stress resistance, and sirtuin-dependent repair. Antioxidant and redox-protective strategies may therefore preserve both cellular structures and the cofactors required for bioenergetic recovery.

Context-Dependent Glycolytic Flux and Lactate Dynamics

Glycolytic kinetics and lactate biology have context-dependent, dualistic effects within the ischemic microenvironment. Durind severe hypoxic conditions, anaerobic glycolysis temporarily provides limited ATP generation but accelerates lactate accumulation, intracellular acidosis, and osmotic stress, thereby exacerbating cerebral edema, enzymatic dysfunction, and parenchymal cell death [9, 10]. Lactate functions beyond a metabolic byproduct, however. When oxygen and transporter capacity are sufficient, it serves as an oxidative fuel and signaling molecule for neurons, through astrocyte-neuron lactate shuttling [11, 12]. In acute ischemia, excessive astrocyte-derived lactate may also aggravate injury by promoting pathological protein lactylation [13]. Therapy should therefore reduce harmful lactate accumulation and acidosis in severely ischemic tissue while preserving lactate exchange that supports recovery and vascular stability [10, 12–14].

The Neurovascular Unit as a Metabolic and Structural Barrier

The BBB functions essentially as a highly specialized metabolic organ. Brain endothelial cells rely heavily on glycolysis to maintain barrier architecture and support vascular remodeling. Endothelium-derived lactate also contributes to pericyte function and BBB maintenance [14]. Pericytes are early responders to ischemic insults, and their detachment or apoptosis can precede endothelial injury and barrier breakdown. The disruption of this endothelial-pericyte metabolic coupling restricts microvascular perfusion, intensifies vasogenic edema, facilitates leukocyte infiltration, and progressively worsens parenchymal mitochondrial stress [17, 18]. Vascular regenerative therapies should therefore restore both barrier structure and substrate exchange across the NVU.

Immunometabolic Reprogramming and Phenotypic Plasticity

Post-stroke neuroinflammation is fundamentally governed by distinct metabolic programming. During the hyperacute phase of ischemic injury, microglia and infiltrating macrophages increase glycolysis, pentose phosphate pathway activity, nitric oxide production, and pro-inflammatory cytokine expression. Conversely, as the microenvironment transitions toward debris clearance and tissue remodeling phase, repair-associated myeloid cells rely more on oxidative phosphorylation, fatty-acid beta-oxidation, efferocytosis, and pro-resolving signals [5, 9, 25]. Astrocytes display parallel functional and metabolic plasticity, oscillating between neuroprotective and detrimental phenotypes; they maintain extracellular ionic and neurotransmitter homeostasis in favorable microenvironments but drive inflammatory amplification and maladaptive lactate accumulation under persistent ischemic stress [12, 13]. This inherent immunometabolic plasticity represents a highly actionable therapeutic target. Transplanted stem cells and cell-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) can effectively modulate cytokine profiles, macrophage polarization dynamics, microglial activation states, and systemic immune responses [22–29].

Dysregulation of Lipid and Amino Acid Metabolic Pathways

Cerebral ischemia markedly alters lipid and amino acid metabolic. Pathological phospholipase activation releases arachidonic acid and associated polyunsaturated fatty acids, which subsequently serve as primary substrates for devastating lipid peroxidation [7–10]. Concurrently, progressive mitochondrial dysfunction severely impairs cellular beta-oxidation and alters acylcarnitine profiles [8–10]. Furthermore, the accumulation of extracellular excitatory amino acids extends beyond neurotransmitter excitotoxicity; it signifies systemic failures in nitrogen handling, impaired tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle anaplerosis, and the breakdown of homeostatic astrocytic glutamate-glutamine cycling [2–5, 11]. These pathological pathways intimately intersect to execute ferroptotic cascades, in which labile iron availability, profound glutathione depletion, and lipid hydroperoxide accumulation converge to cause catastrophic membrane failure. Consequently, metabolism-targeted regenerative interventions must be rigorously evaluated by their capacity to mitigate lipid oxidative damage and preserve endogenous antioxidant reserves, rather than being screened solely against standard inflammatory cytokine panels.

Systemic Metabolic Comorbidities as Translational Modifiers

The central neuropathological response to stroke is significantly shaped by systemic metabolic tone. Acute or chronic hyperglycemia accelerates pathological lactate production and exacerbates oxidative stress; concurrently, preexisting diabetes and obesity alter baseline endothelial metabolism and immune cell substrate utilization [1, 5, 9]. Hypertension can impair pericyte and vascular smooth-muscle function. Aging reduces mitochondrial reserve and blunts endogenous reparative responses [1, 5]. These factors may help explain why young, healthy rodent models often overestimate treatment efficacy. Translational studies should therefore treat metabolic comorbidities as mechanistic modifiers of cell-based therapies rather than as simple demographic covariates.

Cell Therapy Application in Ischemic Stroke

Available Platforms with Metabolic Relevance

Mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) are the most extensively investigated cellular platform for ischemic stroke therapeutics [19, 21, 23, 24]. Their accessibility, ex vivo expansion capacity, and immunomodulatory properties make them attractive for translation. MSCs act mainly through a secretome that contains growth factors, cytokines, chemokines, EVs, bioactive lipids, metabolic enzymes, and regulatory microRNAs [19–24]. In preclinical models of cerebral ischemia, MSC administration significantly attenuates apoptotic cascades, neuroinflammation, and oxidative stress, while concurrently stimulating angiogenesis, endogenous neurogenesis, oligodendrocyte survival, and synaptic remodeling [22–26]. From a metabolic perspective, the major advantage of MSCs lies in their pleiotropic nature. A single MSC-based therapy can simultaneously enhance mitochondrial function, regulate redox signaling, modulate immunometabolic responses, and promote repair of the neurovascular unit. However, their clinical translation is challenged by substantial phenotypic heterogeneity. Factors such as tissue source, donor age, in vitro passage number, culture conditions (including hypoxic versus normoxic preconditioning), inflammatory priming, cryopreservation methods, and the lack of standardized potency assays can markedly influence both the intrinsic metabolic profile of MSCs and the composition of their therapeutic secretome, resulting in variable therapeutic efficacy [19–24].

EV-based products are an important extension of cell therapy. MSC- and NSC-derived EVs can carry microRNAs, mRNAs, proteins, lipids, mitochondrial fragments, and surface molecules that alter recipient-cell behavior [27–30]. Systemic MSC-derived exosomes and EVs have improved functional recovery, neurovascular plasticity, and post-stroke immune balance in preclinical models [27, 28]. Human NSC-derived EVs have also improved tissue and functional recovery in a murine thromboembolic stroke model [29]. EVs may reduce risks associated with live cells, including microvascular obstruction and ectopic tissue formation, and their cargo can be engineered. However, translation requires rigorous characterization of identity, purity, potency, sterility, storage stability, biodistribution, and dose metrics [30].

Neural stem and progenitor cells provide brain-specific reparative signals. Although replacement of lost neurons remains difficult, NSCs can release trophic factors and EVs that support synaptic remodeling, remyelination, neurite growth, and local plasticity [29]. In chronic stroke, intracerebral implantation is more likely to create a permissive trophic and metabolic niche in peri-infarct tissue than to reconstruct complex neuronal circuits de novo [41]. NSC/NPC platforms may therefore be most relevant during the chronic phase, when the goal is to increase the capacity of surviving networks to reorganize.

Vascular-oriented cells, including EPCs and related endothelial-lineage products, are particularly relevant to angiometabolic failure. A small clinical follow-up study associated EPC therapy with long-term neurological and quality-of-life improvement [40]. This finding is consistent with a mechanism involving endothelial repair, angiogenesis, and perfusion support. Because vascular perfusion and endothelial-pericyte lactate coupling influence the metabolic state of surrounding tissue [14, 17, 18], vascular cell products may act as indirect bioenergetic therapies.

Cord blood-derived cells and BMMNCs are heterogeneous products that can provide immune Modulation, trophic support, and stimulation of endogenous repair. Clinical studies have tested these products in acute and subacute ischemic stroke [36, 39, 47, 48]. Their heterogeneity may be advantageous when injury mechanisms are multifactorial, but it complicates potency testing and mechanistic interpretation. Metabolism-focused programs should therefore define mixed-cell products using functional release criteria, including immunomodulatory potency, antioxidant capacity, angiogenic activity, mitochondrial support, and EV cargo, rather than cell count alone (Fig. 2).

The metabolic state of the therapeutic product itself is a critical variable. MSCs expanded under atmospheric oxygen can differ from hypoxia-expanded cells in mitochondrial content, glycolytic profile, angiogenic-factor secretion, and EV cargo [19–24]. Hypoxic, inflammatory, pharmacological, or three-dimensional preconditioning may increase prosurvival, angiogenic, or immunomodulatory activity, but it can also affect safety and comparability. A product intended for metabolic rescue should therefore report culture oxygen, passage number, population doublings, senescence, post-thaw viability, respiration, and stress resistance whenever possible. Engineered cells and EVs may allow more precise delivery of microRNAs, antioxidant enzymes, mitochondrial regulators, or targeting ligands [27–30]. This approach could move the field toward mechanism-defined therapeutics, but it also increases regulatory complexity. Investigators must show that engineered cargo reaches the intended recipient cells at a biologically effective dose without disrupting normal metabolism in other organs.

Mechanisms by which Cell Therapy May Restore Metabolic Homeostasis

Intercellular mitochondrial transfer is a plausible neuroprotective mechanism. After ischemic injury, astrocytes can donate functional mitochondria to damaged neurons, thereby supporting neuronal survival in experimental models [31]. MSCs can also transfer intact mitochondria or mitochondrial components to injured neural and vascular cells through tunneling nanotubes or EV-associated pathways [32, 33].

Miro1-dependent trafficking has been linked to improved neuronal oxidative metabolism after oxidant injury [33]. Exogenous transplantation of astrocytic mitochondria has also reduced ischemic injury in vivo [34]. Even when transfer of intact organelles is limited, MSCs and their EVs may improve host mitochondrial quality control by delivering regulatory microRNAs, proteins, and metabolites [27–30, 35]. These signals can promote mitophagy, reduce excessive Drp1-mediated fission, and support oxidative phosphorylation within the injured NVU.

Modulation of Redox Homeostasis and Oxidative Damage

Stem cell interventions can reduce oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation in the ischemic microenvironment. MSCs and their derivatives suppress RONS production, strengthen endogenous antioxidant defenses, preserve mitochondrial and endothelial-junction proteins, and reduce pro-inflammatory cytokine expression [22–26]. These effects are metabolically important because redox overload impairs mitochondrial respiration, accelerates BBB failure, and promotes PARP-1-dependent NAD+ depletion [7–9, 15]. Candidate mechanisms include activation of Nrf2-dependent antioxidant programs, preservation of glutathione, inhibition of NADPH oxidases, and reduced inflammatory RONS production by microglia and macrophages [22–26].

Spatiotemporal Regulation of Glycolytic Flux and Lactate Dynamics

Cell-based therapies may regulate glycolysis and lactate differently across space and time. In the ischemic core, irreversible ATP depletion and severe acidosis limit the potential for cellular rescue. In the penumbra and reperfused peri-infarct region, however, MSC-derived paracrine and vasoactive signals may improve microvascular oxygen delivery and reduce hypoxia-driven glycolysis [10, 14]. Therapy should limit pathological lactate accumulation during acute ischemia while preserving astrocyte-neuron and endothelial-pericyte lactate shuttling during repair [11–14]. This dual effect should be tested rather than assumed. Useful measures include longitudinal lactate/pyruvate ratios, pH-sensitive imaging, MCT1/2/4 expression, and magnetic resonance spectroscopy.

Immunometabolic Reprogramming and Translational Biomarkers

Immunometabolic reprogramming is another plausible therapeutic mechanism. MSCs and cell-derived EVs can reduce excessive microglial and macrophage activation, limit leukocyte infiltration, shift cytokine profiles, and attenuate post-stroke systemic immunosuppression [25, 27, 28]. Bioenergetically, these effects may restrain injury-amplifying glycolysis, nitric oxide production, and oxidative bursts while preserving oxidative metabolism needed for phagocytosis and tissue remodeling. Acute trials of multipotent adult progenitor cells have accordingly emphasized systemic immunomodulation rather than direct neural replacement [37, 38]. Future trials should pair clinical outcomes with peripheral immunophenotyping and neuroinflammatory imaging to document target engagement.

Neurovascular Stabilization as an Agent of Metabolic Rescue

Vascular reconstruction and BBB repair link structural recovery to metabolic homeostasis. MSCs can provide angiogenic and barrier-supportive signals [21–26], EPCs can directly support vascular repair [40], and cell-derived EVs can modulate endothelial survival and junctional stability [27–29]. Improved microvascular perfusion increases oxygen and glucose delivery, reduces hypoxia-driven glycolysis, and supports mitochondrial recovery. Restoration of pericyte coverage and endothelial-pericyte metabolic coupling may also reduce BBB leakage and vasogenic edema [14, 17, 18]. Microvascular stabilization should therefore be viewed as a mechanism of metabolic rescue, not only as anatomical repair.

Bioenergetic Prerequisites for Activity-Dependent Neuroplasticity

Cell-based interventions may create the metabolic conditions required for neuroplasticity. Axonal sprouting, dendritic remodeling, synaptogenesis, remyelination, angiogenesis, and activity-dependent circuit reorganization are energy-intensive processes. They depend on adequate perfusion, functional mitochondria, redox balance, and controlled inflammation. A therapy that modestly improves mitochondrial reserve or microvascular supply may have little immediate effect on infarct volume but still increase the responsiveness of surviving networks to rehabilitation. This distinction may explain why several clinical programs emphasize long-term functional outcomes rather than acute cytoprotection alone [38, 41–46].

Methodological Rigor in Evaluating Intercellular Mitochondrial Transfer

Mitochondrial transfer requires rigorous methodological validation. Transfer of a mitochondrial dye does not prove movement of intact, functional organelles because lipophilic dyes and mitochondrial fragments can move independently. Strong evidence should include improved recipient-cell bioenergetics, tracking of donor-specific mitochondrial DNA or proteins, ultrastructural or high-resolution live-cell confirmation, loss-of-function experiments, and functional rescue [31–35]. Clinical-grade products should not claim mitochondrial replacement unless a mechanism-linked potency assay demonstrates a functional mitochondrial phenotype.

Indirect Modulation of Energy-Sensing and Enzymatic Repair Cascades

Cell therapies may indirectly influence NAD+, sirtuin, and AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) signaling. By reducing RONS, DNA damage, and PARP-1 hyperactivation, MSCs or EVs may preserve NAD+ pools and support sirtuin-dependent mitochondrial adaptation [15, 16, 21–25]. AMPK can coordinate autophagy, mitochondrial quality control, and angiogenesis, but prolonged activation may also indicate unresolved bioenergetic stress [16]. Readouts such as NAD+/NADH, PARP activity, SIRT1/3 target engagement, and AMPK phosphorylation should therefore be interpreted according to cell type and disease phase.

White Matter Metabolism and Long-Range Network Connectivity

White matter has distinct bioenergetic vulnerabilities that influence stroke recovery. Oligodendrocytes and myelinated axons are sensitive to energy failure, glutamate excitotoxicity, and lipid peroxidation [1, 4, 9]. Delayed white-matter degeneration can limit functional recovery even when cortical neurons survive. Cell-based therapies may support oligodendrocyte precursor survival, remyelination, and axonal metabolism through trophic factors, EV cargo, and improved perfusion [23, 27–29]. Because long-term disability often reflects network disconnection, metabolism-focused studies should assess myelin integrity, axonal conduction, diffusion-tensor imaging, and preservation of the corpus callosum and corticospinal tracts. Timing, Route and Phase Matching

The effect of a cell product depends strongly on treatment timing. Hyperacute ischemia is dominated by ATP failure, excitotoxicity, calcium overload, and early BBB stress [1–8]. During the following hours to days, reperfusion injury, RONS production, inflammatory metabolic activation, endothelial-pericyte dysfunction, and edema become increasingly important [5–9, 17, 18]. Weeks to months later, angiogenesis, synaptic remodeling, white-matter repair, and activity-dependent network reorganization predominate [18, 41–43]. A systemic anti-inflammatory product may therefore be most useful during the acute or subacute phase. In contrast, a brain-directed trophic product may be better suited to chronic disability when the goal is to enhance plasticity and function. Treatment timing should be matched to the dominant biological phase rather than selected by calendar time alone (Fig. 3).

Route of administration also determines exposure, mechanism, and risk. Intravenous delivery is minimally invasive, but many cells are retained in the lungs, spleen, or liver. This route may still be effective when the intended mechanism is systemic immunomodulation or release of circulating paracrine factors [36–39, 42–44]. Intra-arterial delivery can increase early brain exposure but requires careful control of cell size, dose, infusion rate, and microvascular-occlusion risk [48]. Intracerebral implantation places cells near the target tissue and may suit chronic stroke, but it requires neurosurgical safety monitoring [41]. EVs may offer greater dosing flexibility and reduce some live-cell risks, although they create additional manufacturing and biodistribution challenges [27–30]. Dose should be linked to mechanism. Insufficient product may not modify the inflammatory or redox environment, whereas excessive live-cell dosing may increase vascular or inflammatory risk. EV dose should not be defined by protein content or particle number alone; potency, cargo, cell of origin, and intended mechanism should also be considered [30]. For live-cell products, cell number should be reported together with viability, respiration, senescence, immunomodulatory activity, and angiogenic potency.

Cell therapy should also be considered in relation to reperfusion. Thrombolysis and thrombectomy restore blood flow, but reperfusion can intensify oxidative stress [5–7], endothelial activation, and BBB disruption [1, 5, 6]. A product given too early may encounter complement activation, circulating thrombi, and an unstable microvasculature. A product given too late may miss the window for penumbral rescue. A rational acute strategy would target reperfusion-adjacent metabolic stabilization and measure RONS, edema, hemorrhagic transformation, and perfusion recovery.

Patient-level phase matching is more informative than calendar time alone. Two patients at day 7 may differ substantially: one may have persistent hypoperfusion and BBB leakage, whereas another may be entering immune resolution and angiogenesis. Similarly, chronic stroke patients differ in corticospinal tract integrity, diaschisis, inflammation and rehabilitation responsiveness. Metabolic imaging and blood/CSF biomarkers could help identify which biological phase is present in an individual patient and, therefore, which cell product is most plausible.

Clinical Translation and Interpretation of Current Evidence

Clinical studies support the feasibility and general safety of several ischemic stroke cell-therapy platforms, but efficacy remains heterogeneous and cell therapy is not standard of care. Trials have evaluated autologous BMMNCs [36, 48], allogeneic MSCs [43, 44], multipotent adult progenitor cells [37, 38], cord blood [39], EPCs [40], and modified MSC products [41]. Differences in source, route, dose, time window, and outcome measures complicate pooled interpretation. The MASTERS phase 2 trial evaluated intravenous multipotent adult progenitor cells in acute ischemic stroke, with early immunomodulation and paracrine support as plausible mechanisms [37]. The subsequent TREASURE phase 2/3 trial administered allogeneic MultiStem 18–36 h after stroke onset in Japan. Treatment had an acceptable safety profile but did not improve the primary 90-day excellent-outcome endpoint, although exploratory subgroup findings were reported [38]. These results show that biological plausibility and safety cannot substitute for appropriate patient selection, timing, and target-engagement measurements.

Subacute and chronic trials illustrate other mechanisms. Intravenous autologous BMMNC therapy in subacute stroke was feasible but did not show uniform efficacy [36]. Autologous MSC trials have reported signals of motor recovery and improvements in activities of daily living, but sample sizes and protocols vary [42, 43]. Allogeneic adipose-derived MSCs were feasible in a pilot phase II trial for acute ischemic stroke [44]. Cord-blood infusion has been tested in adult ischemic stroke, supporting safety and feasibility while leaving questions about efficacy and optimal patient selection [39]. EPC transplantation showed encouraging long-term follow-up in a small study, consistent with the concept of vascular and angiometabolic repair [40]. Intracerebral SB623 implantation in patients with chronic ischemic stroke demonstrated safety and a clinical signal in a phase 1/2a study, supporting a local trophic/plasticity mechanism [41].

Systematic reviews and meta-analyses generally conclude that cell therapy is feasible and relatively safe, with possible functional benefit in some analyses [45, 46]. The certainty of efficacy remains limited by small samples, inconsistent designs, and variable products [45–47]. MSC-focused analyses report improvements in neurological deficits and activities of daily living [46–50], whereas the Cochrane review emphasizes uncertainty and the need for larger, well-controlled trials [47]. From a metabolic perspective, heterogeneity may reflect mechanism-phase mismatch. An anti-inflammatory intravenous product may not benefit a patient whose principal limitation is chronic network plasticity, and a local trophic implant may not rescue acute mitochondrial failure. Future trials should stratify patients using metabolic and vascular biomarkers in addition to time from stroke.

Outcome selection is a major challenge. Acute trials commonly use the 90-day modified Rankin Scale or composite excellent-outcome measures [37, 38]. Repair-focused therapies may require longer follow-up to detect changes in motor recovery, participation, and quality of life [41–47]. Trials should specify whether the product is expected to reduce early infarct growth, improve subacute recovery, enhance rehabilitation responsiveness, or support chronic plasticity. A mismatch between the proposed mechanism and the selected endpoint can make a biologically active therapy appear clinically neutral.

The clinical literature also highlights the importance of balanced interpretation. Positive signals from small studies should not be overgeneralized, and neutral trials should not be interpreted as failures of all cell therapy. Instead, the field should ask whether the correct patients, product potency, route, dose, timing and endpoint were aligned. For example, a systemic immunomodulatory product should be expected to show immune and inflammatory target engagement; a vascular product should show perfusion or BBB effects; and a local trophic product should show changes in network plasticity, corticospinal tract function, or rehabilitation responsiveness (Table 2).

Cell therapy platforms for metabolism-focused translation

Biomarkers, Potency Assays and Experimental Endpoints

A metabolism-focused cell-therapy program should demonstrate target engagement. Diffusion and perfusion MRI can define the ischemic core, penumbra, edema, and vascular response [1, 5]. Magnetic resonance spectroscopy can estimate lactate and selected metabolites related to glycolytic stress [9–14]. Depending on tracer availability, PET can assess glucose metabolism, inflammation, or mitochondrial-related processes. Blood and cerebrospinal-fluid metabolomics can quantify lactate/pyruvate ratios, acylcarnitines, amino-acid metabolites, NAD+-related pathways, lipid-peroxidation products, and inflammatory mediators [9, 10, 15, 16]. These measures should complement, not replace, clinical outcomes. Preclinical studies should also move beyond infarct volume. Mitochondrial endpoints include ATP/ADP ratio, membrane potential, oxygen-consumption rate, extracellular-acidification rate, and mitophagy markers [8, 31–35]. Redox endpoints include RONS, 4-hydroxynonenal, malondialdehyde, and glutathione balance [7, 9, 22–26]. Additional metabolic endpoints include NAD+/NADH, lactate/pyruvate, and MCT1/2/4 [10, 13–16]. Neurovascular and repair endpoints include BBB leakage, tight-junction and pericyte coverage, perfusion, myeloid metabolic phenotype, and oligodendrocyte/white-matter integrity [14, 17, 18, 23, 25, 27–29]. Studies should include sex, age, and relevant comorbidities whenever possible because diabetes, hypertension, obesity, and aging may modify treatment response [1, 5, 9].

Potency assays should be linked to the claimed mechanism. For an MSC product intended to reduce oxidative stress, release criteria should include antioxidant activity, ROS-suppressive capacity, or Nrf2-related effects. For a mitochondrial rescue product, assays should include donor-cell respiration, mitochondrial transfer capacity, mitophagy modulation, or mitochondrial cargo in EVs. For a vascular product, angiogenic potency and barrier-stabilizing activity should be tested. For immunometabolic products, the suppression of inflammatory cytokines and the quantification of macrophage/microglial metabolic remodeling should be assessed. Without mechanism-linked potency assays, batch-to-batch variation can obscure clinical efficacy (Fig. 4).

Biomarker timing should be planned prospectively. Baseline metabolic assessment can identify targetable phenotypes; early post-treatment measurements can demonstrate target engagement; intermediate assessments can show neurovascular repair; and late assessments can link those biological effects to behavior. For example, an acute MSC-EV trial could measure baseline perfusion and lactate, day 1–7 inflammatory and redox markers, week 2–4 BBB/perfusion recovery and 3–12 month functional outcomes. Without this temporal structure, biomarker data may be descriptive rather than mechanistic.

Single-cell and spatial methods can strengthen mechanistic interpretation. Single-cell RNA sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, metabolomics, and imaging mass cytometry can distinguish whether therapy alters neuronal stress, astrocyte lactate programs, microglial glycolysis, endothelial angiogenesis, or pericyte detachment. These approaches are especially useful because whole-tissue metabolomics can obscure cell-type-specific effects. For stem cell journals, such endpoints can help connect regenerative products to precise mechanisms rather than broad anti-inflammatory or neuroprotective claims.

Safety, Manufacturing and Regulatory Considerations

Safety monitoring should reflect product-specific risks and stroke biology. Systemically delivered live-cell products require surveillance for infusion reactions, immune sensitization, infection, worsening edema, and seizures [36–44]. Intra-arterial administration also requires monitoring for thromboembolism and microvascular obstruction [48]. Intracerebral approaches require surgical safety assessment, long-term imaging, and surveillance for ectopic tissue formation or tumorigenicity [41]. EV products may avoid uncontrolled proliferation, but they require strict control of impurities, residual DNA/RNA, adventitious agents, endotoxin, aggregates, particle heterogeneity, and storage stability [30].

Manufacturing conditions directly influence metabolic potency. Oxygen tension, serum supplements, inflammatory priming, passage number, and culture format can alter MSC respiration, senescence, EV output, and secretome composition [19–24]. Cryopreservation may reduce viability and immunomodulatory activity, while donor age and disease can affect mitochondrial fitness and paracrine function. Metabolism-directed products should therefore use standardized culture conditions, release criteria, and stability testing. Negative and neutral studies should also be reported transparently so that products are not advanced solely because they reduce infarct volume in young, healthy rodents.

Regulatory and ethical issues include donor consent, screening, traceability, potency, comparability after manufacturing changes, long-term follow-up and clear distinction between controlled clinical trials and unproven commercial stem cell interventions. A metabolism-focused framework can help regulators and investigators by making the therapeutic claim testable: the product should have a defined metabolic target, a reproducible potency assay, a biologically plausible delivery window and biomarkers showing that the target changed in treated patients.

Metabolic activity can also create safety concerns. A highly glycolytic or pro-angiogenic product may worsen edema, vascular permeability, or ectopic tissue responses. Strong immunosuppression may increase infection risk, and mitochondrial or EV cargo may have off-target effects in the lung, liver, spleen, or kidney. These possibilities justify biodistribution studies, dose-escalation designs, and long-term surveillance. Comparability after manufacturing changes is particularly important for MSC and EV products. Changes in culture medium, bioreactor conditions, passage limits, isolation methods, or cryopreservation can alter secretome composition, mitochondrial cargo, and immunomodulatory potency [19–24, 30]. Functional assays should confirm that a product remains biologically comparable after a process change, even when surface-marker profiles appear unchanged.

Proposed Model and Future Directions

The most coherent model is cell therapy as metabolic rescue rather than cell replacement. During the acute phase, treatment should support mitochondrial function [31–35], limit oxidative injury [22–26], restrain damaging inflammatory metabolism [25, 27, 28], and stabilize the vasculature [21–29, 40]. During the subacute phase, the emphasis shifts toward immune resolution, BBB repair, angiogenesis, and restoration of astrocyte-neuron and endothelial-pericyte metabolic coupling [14, 18, 25, 27, 28, 40]. During the chronic phase, the goal is to increase the metabolic capacity of surviving networks and enable rehabilitation-driven plasticity [29, 41–43].

This model suggests rational combinations. Reperfusion saves tissue but may amplify ROS; cells or EVs with antioxidant and mitochondrial support could reduce reperfusion-associated secondary injury. Rehabilitation increases metabolic demand; products that improve perfusion, mitochondrial reserve and synaptic remodeling may increase responsiveness to task-specific training. Vascular risk factor management, glucose control, sleep, nutrition and exercise may also influence the metabolic background in which cell therapy acts. Future clinical trials should therefore treat cell therapy as an adjunct within a recovery ecosystem rather than an isolated regenerative intervention.

Several priorities follow from this model. First, products should be categorized by mechanism: mitochondrial rescue, redox stabilization, immunometabolic Modulation, angiometabolic repair, or plasticity enhancement. Second, patients should be stratified by metabolic phenotype, including persistent hypoperfusion, inflammatory burden, BBB leakage, comorbid metabolic disease, and chronic network dysfunction. Third, potency assays should be standardized and linked to the stated mechanism. Fourth, live-cell and EV products should be compared directly when they claim similar metabolic effects. Finally, trials should include long-term outcomes because metabolic rescue may improve rehabilitation-dependent recovery even when early infarct reduction is modest. A practical trial design could use a mechanism-product matrix. Acute reperfused patients with high oxidative and BBB stress could receive a product selected for antioxidant and barrier-stabilizing potency. Subacute patients with persistent inflammation and hypoperfusion could receive an immunometabolic and angiogenic product. Chronic patients with preserved corticospinal substrate but limited plasticity could receive a local or targeted EV product combined with intensive rehabilitation. This framework would make both positive and negative results easier to interpret.

The field should also develop shared potency standards. Minimal release testing should include identity, viability, sterility, and dose. Mechanism-specific assays should then assess mitochondrial respiration, antioxidant capacity, EV particle and cargo profiles, immunomodulatory suppression, angiogenic potency, or barrier protection. These assays do not need to be identical across products, but they should match each product’s stated mechanism. This approach would allow cell therapy for stroke to progress from empiric transplantation toward mechanism-guided regenerative pharmacology.

Conclusions

Ischemic stroke triggers a coordinated metabolic crisis characterized by ATP depletion, mitochondrial dysfunction, excitotoxicity, oxidative and nitrosative stress, maladaptive glycolysis, lactate and pH dysregulation, NAD+/FAD imbalance, inflammatory immunometabolism, endothelial-pericyte uncoupling, and impaired neurovascular repair. Cell therapy can positively influence these changes through paracrine signaling, EV cargo, mitochondrial transfer, antioxidant effects, immune-metabolic reprogramming, angiogenesis, BBB stabilization and support of plasticity. The next phase of the field should move from broad claims of stem cell therapy toward mechanism-defined products matched specific metabolic phases of stroke. When paired with metabolic biomarkers, product potency assays and long-term functional endpoints, cell therapy may become a rational metabolic adjunct to reperfusion and rehabilitation.
