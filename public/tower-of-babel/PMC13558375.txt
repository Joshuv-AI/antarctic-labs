Update on Diet and Nutritional Therapies in Patients with Inflammatory Bowel Disease
Authors: Mark R. Baniqued, Neha D. Shah, Jessica Dominguez, Alyssa M. Parian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
While the exact cause of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) remains unclear, interactions among several complex mechanisms are thought to contribute to its pathogenesis. One such factor is diet, which has recently been investigated for its role in both IBD pathogenesis and treatment. This review examines the evidence regarding diet, nutrition support, and dietary supplements for managing IBD. The diets discussed include low fiber/residue, Crohn’s Disease exclusion, Specific Carbohydrate, Mediterranean, Anti-Inflammatory, Autoimmune Protocol, low FODMAP (fermentable oligosaccharides, disaccharides, monosaccharides, and polyols), lactose-free, and gluten-free diets. Nutrition support options include enteral nutrition, partial enteral nutrition, and parenteral nutrition. These modalities can be considered in specific situations, such as perioperative nutrition, protein-calorie malnutrition, and induction of remission. Dietary supplements studied for managing IBD include prebiotics, probiotics, vitamin D, omega-3 fatty acids, polyphenols, and aloe vera, which may be beneficial in managing IBD. Some challenges to consider in the dietary management of IBD include food avoidance, food insecurity, and cultural practices. When contemplating initiating one of these options, input from a multidisciplinary gastroenterology team should be considered.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), which includes Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), is a chronic immune-mediated disorder affecting the gastrointestinal (GI) tract. The exact cause remains uncertain, but it is thought to stem from complex interactions among genetic, environmental, gut microbiota, diet, and host immune factors [1]. Various proposed mechanisms for how diet impacts inflammation include lowering exposure to pro-inflammatory food antigens, conversion of nutrients into anti-inflammatory metabolites, fostering a beneficial gut microbiome, maintaining gut barrier integrity, and modulating mucosal immunity [2]. For instance, gut bacteria ferment dietary fiber into short-chain fatty acids (SCFA), such as butyrate, which has anti-inflammatory effects. Butyrate promotes regulatory T-cell differentiation, enhances mucin secretion to strengthen the mucosal barrier, and reduces bacterial translocation through the gut epithelium [3–6].

Consistent with these mechanistic foundations, large epidemiologic studies have linked various nutrients to IBD risk. A systematic review and meta-analysis encompassing nine studies and 54,580 participants found that a Western diet marked by high levels of refined sugars and saturated fats and low levels of fruits and vegetables correlates with a heightened risk of IBD [7]. Similarly, the Nurses’ Health Study and Health Professionals Follow-up Study, which included 245,112 individuals representing 5,468,444 person-years, indicated that a higher intake of ultra-processed foods typical of Western diets is linked to an increased risk of CD [8]. Conversely, the Mediterranean diet, abundant in fruits, vegetables, seafood, and olive oil, is associated with a lower risk of IBD [9]. Geographic dietary differences further support this link; for example, northern France, where Western diets are prevalent, shows a higher rate of CD compared to southern France, where Mediterranean eating patterns dominate [10]. Beyond contributing to disease onset, diet has been demonstrated in randomized controlled trials (RCT) and observational studies to promote remission and prevent clinical relapse in IBD [11]. This review explores the evidence on diet, dietary supplements, and nutrition support in managing IBD. Furthermore, we address the challenges and considerations in advising patients on dietary approaches for IBD.

Methods

A literature search was conducted to identify relevant publications for consideration in this narrative review. PubMed was queried from its inception until August 1, 2024, using the search string: “diet AND (crohn* OR colitis OR ‘inflammatory bowel’ OR enteritis).” This yielded 9,408 unique articles after deduplication. The authors independently reviewed the articles to assess their relevance to the current narrative review. The inclusion criteria encompassed published case series, research studies, and systematic reviews related to nutrition for individuals with CD or UC. Experimental models and conference abstracts were excluded unless authors incorporated them at their discretion. There were no language restrictions, as web-based tools enabled the translation of articles in foreign languages. After the screening process, 855 articles were considered potentially relevant, and the final synthesis included 129 articles, as determined by the authors, to contribute meaningfully to the predetermined sections of this review. When available, study types such as meta-analyses, systematic reviews, and randomized controlled trials were prioritized over observational studies. Clinical guidelines from major Gastroenterology societies were also included, totaling 135 references used.

In 1985, Levenstein et al. studied 70 adult patients with non-stenosing CD, assigned to a low residue or normal diet for 29 months, noting no differences in symptoms, hospitalizations, surgeries, or recurrence [12]. Another trial with 352 adult and pediatric patients with CD on two diets (one with unrestricted sugar and low in fiber versus one with little or no sugar and high in unrefined carbohydrates) over two years found a slight increase in intestinal surgeries for the low fiber group, but no significant differences in hospitalizations or study withdrawals [13]. The European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN) recommends that a modified diet low in soluble fiber should be considered for patients with CD who have asymptomatic intestinal stenosis, although it notes that there is limited data to support this approach [14]. Similarly, the American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) Clinical Practice Update advises that patients with symptomatic strictures may struggle with fibrous foods due to texture [15]. Limiting fiber in the diet has no clear benefit in treating inflammation related to IBD, but patients with symptomatic CD stenoses or significantly active colitis may clinically benefit from fiber and texture modification.

Crohn’s Disease Exclusion Diet

The Crohn’s Disease Exclusion Diet (CDED) is a whole food-based approach used to induce remission by reducing foods that negatively impact the gut microbiome and the intestinal barrier. The CDED is considered an alternative to exclusive enteral nutrition (EEN), which uses only enteral formulas, as it offers a dietary approach for patients who struggle with the complete liquid diet while aiming to achieve remission through dietary management. It is a three-phase diet used in combination with Partial Enteral Nutrition (PEN) that systematically eliminates certain foods believed to trigger intestinal inflammation Table 1, followed by gradual reintroduction to identify any potential triggers. In the first six weeks, known as Phase I, the induction phase, 50% of total calories primarily come from foods labeled in Table 1, with the remaining calories supplemented by PEN. The following six weeks, or transitional phase, gradually reintroduces foods (Table 1) while lowering PEN requirements to 25% of total calories. Consumption of mandatory foods continues daily, which includes chicken breast, eggs, potatoes, white rice, bananas, and an apple. The final maintenance phase begins at week 12, allowing a controlled reintroduction of additional foods to achieve long-term dietary management and symptom relief [16].

The first study investigating the components in the CDED was an observational study published in 2014 by Sigall-Boneh et al. Forty-seven participants between the ages of 8 and 25 with active CD followed a structured six-week CDED [17]. The study showed that 78.7% of participants responded positively to the diet, and 70.2% achieved remission. Fifteen patients maintained a stable treatment plan for six months to two years. Eleven of these patients used immunomodulators alongside the diet, while four relied solely on dietary restriction. Eleven of the 15 patients achieved complete mucosal healing, notably including all four patients using diet alone. This study provided preliminary evidence for the potential role of specific dietary exclusions in the efficacy of the CDED, indicating potential induction of clinical and/or endoscopic remission and symptom improvement [17].

A 2017 observational study examined 21 patients with CD (10 adolescents, 11 adults) who had lost response to at least one biologic (infliximab or adalimumab) despite dose escalation or combination therapy [18]. They followed the CDED diet with PEN for 12 weeks while continuing biologic treatment. Remission was defined as Harvey Bradshaw Index (HBI) < 5 at week 6. At week 6, 61.9% achieved clinical remission, including 53% who had lost response to combination therapy. C-reactive protein (CRP) levels improved in 81% of patients, with 40.9% normalizing. Patients with small bowel CD showed better remission rates (69.2%) than those with colonic disease (53.8%). Three of four patients that adhered to CDED alone without PEN achieved remission, suggesting dietary exclusion, rather than PEN, was key. These findings indicate that CDED, with or without PEN, may help reduce inflammation in patients who have lost response to biologics by improving inflammatory markers. A 2019 study by Levine et al. conducted over 12 weeks compared two dietary treatments for 74 children with mild to moderate CD: CDED with formula followed by PEN and EEN followed by an unrestricted diet with PEN [19]. CDED + PEN was better tolerated (97.5% vs. 73.6% for EEN). Although both diets had similar remission rates at week six, CDED + PEN achieved a significantly higher corticosteroid-free remission rate at week 12 (75.6% vs. 45.1%). This maintained remission correlated with reduced inflammation and gut bacteria changes. The study suggests CDED with PEN is a well-tolerated and effective strategy for managing remission in children with mild to moderate CD.

A 2024 trial involving 56 pediatric CD patients examined whether a two-week course of EEN followed by CDED with PEN (CDED + PEN) is more effective than an eight-week EEN course for achieving and maintaining clinical remission for up to 24 weeks in mild to severe CD [20]. Most CDED patients achieved remission by week eight (77% vs. 54% for EEN, p = 0.07). At week 24, 60% in the CDED + PEN group remained in remission compared to 42% in the EEN group (p = 0.18). At 14 weeks, no significant remission rate difference was found. The study indicates that CDED after a short EEN course is a viable option for CD and has potential nutritional benefits. Findings in this study were not statistically significant, but either CDED with PEN could be used after EEN instead of a prolonged course of EEN.

Specific Carbohydrate Diet

The Specific Carbohydrate Diet (SCD) and its variant, the Modified SCD (mSCD), are elimination diets restricting certain carbohydrates, specifically disaccharides and polysaccharides, that require enzymatic digestion. This includes foods labeled in Table 1. Carbohydrates in the form of monosaccharides, such as free fructose found in fruit and honey, are allowed. Other foods such as animal protein, lactose-free dairy, and non-starchy vegetables are permitted. The SCD claims that a high intake of disaccharides and polysaccharides does not digest well, thus serving as substrates to the growth of pathogenic bacteria, leading to injury and inflammation to the mucosal lining of the small bowel. The mSCD allows limited portions of certain foods previously restricted in the SCD, such as oats and rice [21]. The SCD lasts one year to improve symptoms, then continues another year for maintenance. After that, restricted foods are reintroduced to expand the diet. If symptoms return, SCD is restarted until they subside.

Initial studies on SCD were small, uncontrolled, and carried out in the pediatric population. An earlier observational prospective study investigated the induction effects of SCD on 10 children with active CD who followed the SCD for 12 weeks while continuing their regular medications. Nine (90%) participants achieved clinical remission after 12 weeks on the SCD, along with improvements in inflammatory markers [22]. Another retrospective study investigated the effects of adding SCD to the treatment regimen for pediatric CD and UC [23]. For children with active CD, disease activity scores (PCDAI) significantly dropped from an average of 32.8 at baseline to 20.8 after approximately four weeks, further declining to 8.8 after six months on the diet. These improvements in PCDAI would be clinically significant. Likewise, children with active UC saw their Pediatric Ulcerative Colitis Activity Index (PUCAI) decrease from 28.3 at baseline to 20 after four weeks and 18.3 after six months, which may not be clinically significant. This study indicates that SCD could be a valuable addition to standard IBD treatment for children.

A 2015 observational case series surveyed 50 adult patients with IBD in remission who followed the SCD [24]. The mean time to see some improvement when following the SCD was 29.2 days. Participants rated the SCD as effective, averaging 91.3% (Range: 30%-100%) for controlling flares and 92.1% (Range: 53%-100%) for maintaining remission, though individual experiences varied considerably. While the study suggests the SCD’s potential effectiveness, particularly for colonic and ileocolonic CD, it acknowledges limitations due to its recruitment method and the self-reported nature of the data. Additionally, a small retrospective study investigated the role of SCD in maintaining remission for 11 children with CD [25]. Participants who adhered closely to the SCD improved their blood counts, albumin levels, and inflammation markers. These improvements generally persisted even after some restricted foods were reintroduced. Most children also exhibited healthy weight and height gains during the strict SCD phase, although slight weight loss was observed after liberalization.

A 2022 N-of-1 trial evaluated the SCD and mSCD for managing IBD in children [21]. Fifty-four participants aged 7–18 with active IBD were enrolled across 19 locations. After a two-week baseline on their usual diet, they alternated between SCD and mSCD for eight-week periods. The study concluded neither diet consistently improved IBD symptoms or inflammation. In addition, a small study found that children with IBD on the SCD for 12 weeks were deficient in vitamin D and low levels of blood calcium [26]. The SCD’s complexity can challenge long-term adherence and lead to inadequate nutrient intake. Monitoring by a registered dietitian is essential to address potential deficiencies through supplementation or careful food choices within the SCD guidelines. The studies did not include a control group to account for potential confounding factors and establish a causal link between the SCD and the observed outcomes. Further studies are warranted.

Mediterranean Diet

The Mediterranean diet (MD) emphasizes fresh, minimally processed whole foods inspired by the traditional eating habits of the Mediterranean region. It prioritizes and avoids foods found in Table 1.

The 2021 DINE-CD randomized trial compared the efficacy of the SCD and the MD for treating mild to moderate CD and included 194 adult patients [27]. For 12 weeks, patients were randomly assigned to either the SCD or MD. The results showed no significant difference between the SCD and MD in achieving symptomatic remission (SCD: 46.5% vs. MD: 43.5%, p = 0.77), fecal calprotectin response (SCD: 34.8% vs. MD: 30.8%, p = 0.83), or CRP (SCD: 5.4% vs. MD: 3.6%, p = 0.68). Findings revealed that the MD may be preferred for most patients due to its simplicity and additional health benefits compared to the specific carbohydrate diet.

A 2024 study examined the relationship between MD adherence and fecal calprotectin levels in 99 children with CD, most of whom were in remission and on biological therapy [28]. Higher MD compliance (KIDMED indicating high adherence in 31.3% and moderate adherence in 51.5% of the study population) correlated with lower fecal calprotectin levels, particularly from higher vegetable consumption. Conversely, increased vegetable intake may be challenging for those with stricturing or ileocolonic disease. Another 2024 case–control study assessed diet quality and MD adherence in adult IBD patients, matching 50 patients with CD and UC patients with UC to 100 healthy controls [29]. Diet quality was notably poorer in patients with both CD and UC than in the control group. Overall, MD adherence was lower in patients with CD (34% with a MedDiet score > 8.4 vs. 56% in the control group), with significant differences in those having stricturing and ileocolonic disease. A similar trend of reduced MD compliance was noted in patients with UC who have extensive disease (42% vs. 61% in control group). These results imply that MD compliance is notably low in specific IBD subgroups, potentially hindering its beneficial effects.

A multicenter RCT investigated the impact of MD, with and without curcumin or resveratrol, on mild to moderate UC adult population [30]. Participants were divided into three groups: MD alone, MD with curcumin, or MD with resveratrol for 8 weeks. All interventions significantly improved inflammation, disease activity, and quality of life, but no significant differences were found between groups except in body pain and MD adherence where the group of MD with resveratrol reported lower levels of body pain. The study concludes that MD is effective and safe for UC, with no added benefits from curcumin or resveratrol. A 2025 study examined adherence to MD and regular exercise on IBD in 693 adult patients in remission [31]. Over a median 27-month follow-up, those adhering to both MD and an active lifestyle (defined as GODIN-Leisure physical activity score > 24) had significantly fewer moderate to severe relapses and lower corticosteroid requirements. This protective effect was particularly noted in UC patients, who showed greater adherence to healthy habits than CD patients, which may be related to dietary improvements associated with a healthier lifestyle. The findings highlight the need for integrating healthy lifestyle recommendations into clinical management. Finally, the European Crohn’s and Colitis Organization (ECCO) does indicate that MD could be considered as maintenance of remission in UC in combination with medical therapy [32].

Anti-inflammatory Diet for IBD

The IBD-Anti-Inflammatory Diet (IBD-AID) is a multi-phase diet designed to address the link between gut microbiota imbalances and the consumption of certain carbohydrates, such as simple and refined sugars, that fuel pathogenic gut bacteria. The IBD-AID comprises five key components: avoiding specific carbohydrates, supporting the gut microbiota by increasing intake of prebiotics and probiotics, modifying fat intake with a focus on healthier fats such as omega-3 fatty acids and avoidance of saturated fats, dietary assessment for individualized changes, and texture modification to optimize nutrient absorption. This modification is tailored to the severity of symptoms and individual tolerance, starting with soft foods and progressing to whole foods as symptoms improve [33].

A 2014 observational case series explored the effectiveness of the IBD-AID for IBD. Of the 40 adult patients with IBD offered the diet, 67% tried IBD-AID, with 60% experiencing good or very good results and 7% having mixed results [34]. A detailed review of 11 of these adult patients (eight with CD and three with UC) who followed the diet for at least four weeks revealed that all were able to stop at least one IBD medication and experienced symptom improvement. Significant reductions were observed in the HBI and the Modified Truelove and Witts Severity Index, suggesting the IBD-AID may be a beneficial complementary therapy for IBD. A 2022 pilot study investigated how specific foods impact gut flora in people with IBD [35]. Twenty-two participants followed the IBD-AID. Analysis revealed that consuming probiotics, prebiotics, and foods rich in lean animal protein and omega-3 fatty acids increased the levels of specific Clostridia and Bacteroides species. While existing research supports individual aspects of the IBD-AID, further studies are needed to evaluate the effectiveness of the complete dietary approach.

Autoimmune Protocol Diet

The autoimmune protocol (AIP) diet is a version of the paleolithic diet designed to manage IBD by eliminating foods potentially linked to intolerances, adverse effects on gut microbiota, and intestinal inflammation. The initial phase removes multiple foods found in Table 1. The diet emphasizes higher consumption of whole foods, bone broth, probiotics, and fermented foods. Additionally, it is recommended that lifestyle factors such as sleep, exercise, and individual stress be addressed to help with symptom management. Once symptoms improve, the next phase begins, in which the eliminated foods are gradually and systematically reintroduced one by one to identify specific triggers [36, 37].

A small observational study investigated the effectiveness of the AIP diet in 15 adults with active IBD [37]. Participants followed a six-week diet elimination phase and a five-week maintenance phase. Significant improvements were seen in both CD and UC activity scores after six and 11 weeks. While CRP remained unchanged, mean fecal calprotectin improved from baseline compared to week 11 (471 µg/g vs. 112 µg/g, p = 0.12), but this finding was not statistically significant. Endoscopic improvements were also observed in a subset of patients. These results suggest that the AIP diet may offer benefits for managing IBD symptoms and inflammation. A 2019 uncontrolled clinical trial investigated the impact of the AIP diet on the quality of life in adults with active CD and UC [36]. Fifteen participants followed a six-week elimination and five-week maintenance phases of the diet. Significant improvements in quality of life were observed as early as three weeks into the diet and were sustained throughout the 11-week study period. Participants also reported improvements in bowel movement frequency, stress levels, and ability to engage in leisure/sports activities. Further studies are warranted.

Low Fermentable Oligosaccharides, Disaccharides, Monosaccharides, and Polyols Diet

The low Fermentable Oligosaccharides, Disaccharides, Monosaccharides, and Polyols (FODMAP) diet is a three-phase diet targeting fermentable short-chain carbohydrates that trigger gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms. FODMAPs are poorly absorbed in the GI tract, leading to fermentation by colonic bacteria, resulting in gas, bloating, and bowel changes. Common FODMAP food groups are noted in Table 1. The initial elimination phase lasts 4–6 weeks, removing all high-FODMAPs while maximizing low FODMAP foods [38]. The reintroduction phase adds high-FODMAPs back in structured portions over 6–8 weeks to assess tolerance. The final personalization phase allows for high-FODMAPs as tolerated. While this diet has primarily been studied for IBS, studies have been conducted to assess if it is beneficial for patients with IBD.

A 2009 observational study evaluated the impact of the low FODMAP diet on patients with stable IBD symptoms, involving 52 CD and 20 UC adult patients who had received recent formal dietary guidance, defined as one-on-one sessions with dietitians to discuss the low FODMAP diet [39]. A survey gathered data on dietary adherence and GI symptom changes, revealing improved abdominal pain, bloating, and diarrhea (p < 0.02), while constipation showed no improvement. An observational study by Prince et al. studied the effects of a low FODMAP diet on 88 adult patients with IBD in remission with functional GI symptoms [40]. The study measured GI symptom scores and stool output. Results showed a significant rise in patients reporting satisfactory symptom relief from baseline to 6 weeks post-education (16% vs. 78%, p < 0.001) and a notable decrease in symptom severity (1.2 ± 0.5 vs. 0.7 ± 0.5, p < 0.001).

A pivotal RCT by Pedersen et al. assessed a low FODMAP diet’s impact on IBS-like symptoms in adult patients in remission or with mild to moderate active IBD [41]. Patients were randomly assigned a low FODMAP or normal diet for six weeks. Results showed more responders in the low FODMAP group (OR 5.30, 95% CI 1.81–15.55, p < 0.01), lower median IBS symptom severity (115 (33–169) vs. 170 (91–228), p = 0.02), and a more significant increase in IBD quality of life (60 (51–65) vs. 50 (39–60), p < 0.01). Cox et al. studied 52 adult patients with quiescent CD or UC on low FODMAP for four weeks in another trial [42]. Patients reported better gut symptom relief (52% vs. 16%, p = 0.007) and higher quality of life scores (81.9 ± 1.2 vs. 78.3 ± 1.2, p = 0.042). Bodini et al. randomized 55 adult IBD patients on a low FODMAP or standard diet for six weeks [43]. There was a decrease in median HBI (4 (3–5) vs. 3 (2–3), p = 0.024) and fecal calprotectin (76.6 µg/g (50–286.3) vs. 50 µg/g (50.6–81), p = 0.004) in the low FODMAP group. While there is a decrease in fecal calprotectin score, the difference is minor and may not be clinically significant. AGA guidelines indicate some utility in treatment of functional symptoms in patients with IBD with important consideration of patients’ nutritional adequacy [44]. While these factors show improvement, overall, there is no clear data demonstrating their effectiveness in treating IBD to achieve or maintain clinical remission.

Lactose-Free Diet

A lactose-free diet restricts foods containing lactose, the disaccharide found in dairy. Patients with lactose intolerance are deficient in the enzyme lactase, which breaks down lactose. The inability to digest and eliminate lactose from the GI tract can lead to symptoms of bloating and diarrhea. Studies have been conducted to assess if lactose malabsorption is more prevalent in patients with IBD.

An earlier 1997 observational study assessed lactose malabsorption in patients with IBD using H2 breath testing [45]. Among 260 adult patients (121 with CD and 139 with UC), lactose malabsorption was significantly higher in patients with CD (40% vs. 29.2%, p < 0.025). A clinical trial involving 40 adult patients with inactive CD compared a conventional diet with a vegetable-protein-rich, lactose-free diet to assess nutritional status [46]. Results showed improved body composition in the latter group, indicating potential nutritional benefits for lactose-intolerant CD patients. Another study on 39 adult patients with chronic UC involved a double-blind crossover trial with the introduction of lactose or maltose [47]. Of these, 18 patients (46%) had deficient lactose digestion; however, no significant differences were found in symptoms or disease progression between lactose digesters and non-digesters. Observational studies show mixed data on lactose malabsorption prevalence in IBD patients [45, 46]. No clinical trials assess the long-term effects of low-lactose or lactose-free diets on symptoms, quality of life, or inflammatory markers in IBD patients. Due to varying lactose levels in dairy, an individualized approach is recommended to manage portions and intake frequency of dairy to control symptoms.

Gluten-Free Diet

A gluten-free diet (GFD) requires eliminating gluten-containing foods, as shown in Table 1. While GFD is the sole treatment for celiac disease, avoiding gluten may also benefit those with other GI disorders. Individuals with non-celiac gluten sensitivity (NCGS), those who do not have celiac disease but experience GI symptoms from consuming gluten, may include patients with IBD. This sensitivity is believed to stem from gluten intake altering gut microbiota by shifting the composition of bacteria in the gut and increasing intestinal permeability [48].

Herfarth et al. surveyed 1,657 adult patients with IBD for NCGS undergoing GFD trials [49]. Of those, 81 (4.9%) reported NCGS. Among participants, 19.1% tried GFD, with 65.6% reporting improved GI symptoms and 38.3% fewer IBD flares. Another observational study analyzed self-reported NCGS in patients with IBD, where 40 out of 145 (27.6%) reported NCGS [50]. It found significant associations for patients with CD with reported NCGS were significantly more likely to have stricturing disease (40.9% vs. 18.8%, p = 0.046) and higher CD activity index scores (228.1 vs. 133.3, p = 0.002). Another study assessed NCGS in 102 adult patients with IBD, with NCGS reported in 23.6% of CD and 27.3% of patients with UC [51]. NCGS linked to recent IBD flares (aOR 7.4, 95% CI 1.6–341.1), stenotic disease in CD (aOR 4.7, 95% CI 1.1–20.2), and skin manifestations (aOR 5.5, 95% CI 1.2–24.1). A triple-blind RCT examined GFD effects in adult patients with mild to moderate UC, randomly assigning patients to GFD or placebo and assessing labs, severity (based on a symptom questionnaire and Simple Clinical Colitis Activity Index (SCCAI)), and quality of life [52]. Twenty-six patients were enrolled, showing no significant differences in inflammatory markers, quality of life, or severity after six weeks.

Observational studies indicate a possible connection between gluten and IBD symptoms [49–51]. Early investigations do not demonstrate a distinct benefit in managing UC. Additional studies, particularly RCTs, are necessary to evaluate its effectiveness in treating IBD. An individualized strategy is recommended for patients with IBD who might experience NGCS to provide relief of symptoms.

Nutrition Support: Enteral and Parenteral Nutrition

Enteral Nutrition

Enteral nutrition (EN) provides liquid enteral formulas orally or through a feeding tube. It is the preferred nutrient delivery method for patients who cannot take in enough nutrition orally but have a fully or partially functioning GI tract [53]. Using EN in IBD helps reduce malnutrition, serves as induction therapy with EEN and PEN, and provides perioperative nutrition. Enteral formulas come in various types, each with specific nutrient compositions. Polymeric formulas contain intact nutrients needing enzymatic digestion, while elemental formulas contain free amino acids, micro- and macro-nutrients requiring minimal or no digestion.

Exclusive Enteral Nutrition

EEN, as induction therapy of remission, involves removing all foods and most fluids, except water, for 6 to 12 weeks while relying on enteral formulas as the primary source of nutrition [54]. Diet is started after completing EEN. Several proposed mechanisms explain how EEN may promote mucosal healing and induce remission in CD. The removal of diet during EEN eliminates dietary antigens that may contribute to dysbiosis in the intestinal microbiota. EEN may promote favorable diversity and composition of the microbiota, enhancing intestinal barrier defense and improving gut integrity, potentially reducing intestinal permeability to toxins, and decreasing pro-inflammatory cytokines [55].

The AGA Clinical Practice Update on the role of diet and nutrition therapy in IBD outlines best practices, recommending EEN as the first-line induction therapy for pediatric mild to moderate CD [15]. A meta-analysis of pediatric CD studies has shown that EEN is comparable to corticosteroids in efficacy for achieving endoscopic and histologic mucosal healing for remission [56], with effectiveness reaching up to 80% in pediatric CD [57]. Earlier studies in pediatric CD have also indicated that remission rates were similar between polymeric and elemental formulas, with the added benefit of the polymeric formula promoting beneficial weight gain [58, 59]. While EEN effectively induces remission, adherence may be challenging for some pediatric patients [60]. Although EEN effectively induces remission in adult patients with CD, its utilization remains limited [61]. Perceived barriers include poor palatability and tolerance of EEN [61]. A small qualitative study of 17 adult patients with CD who completed EEN found that self-efficacy, ability to access EEN, health system factors, and social support optimized adherence to EEN [62].

EEN is also used in the perioperative phase in CD. A retrospective study evaluated the impact of a four-week perioperative elemental EEN in 120 adult patients with CD who underwent elective laparoscopic bowel resection [63]. Forty-five patients were placed on the regimen, and 75 patients were not. Patients on EEN had significant improvements in CRP levels before surgery (p < 0.05). The rate of postoperative complications in the EEN group was 17.8%, which was significantly less (p = 0.033) than in the group not on EEN, 36.0%. The recurrence rate for endoscopic disease activity six months post-surgery was significantly lower in the EEN group (p < 0.05). Another retrospective study evaluated the effects of perioperative EEN in 51 adult patients with penetrating or stricturing CD [64]. Patients received EEN for at least two weeks before surgery and were matched with controls who underwent immediate surgery without being placed on EEN. Results showed that 25% of patients on EEN no longer required urgent surgery as symptoms resolved. EEN patients had higher baseline CRP (p = 0.02), which significantly decreased post-EEN. Patients also experienced shorter surgery durations and fewer postoperative complications. These studies suggest that perioperative EEN may facilitate better surgical outcomes. Both the AGA guidelines and the ECCO recommend this nutritional therapy for the induction of clinical and endoscopic remission in CD [15, 32]. ECCO indicates its efficacy in both pediatric and adult populations with CD.

There are minimal studies on the use of EEN in UC. In an open-label RCT, 62 adult patients with acute severe UC (ASUC) were randomized to receive semi-elemental EEN for seven days along with standard of care or just standard of care [65]. The results showed that patients on EEN had a lower response to corticosteroid failure and a shorter hospitalization when compared to patients receiving just standard of care. In addition, patients on EEN had a greater decrease in CRP and fecal calprotectin levels. Further studies are warranted.

Partial Enteral Nutrition

In contrast to EEN, PEN is used with diet to meet 30–50% of caloric needs. When compared to EEN, earlier studies have shown that the use of EEN is superior to PEN in inducing remission [66]. A RCT of 50 pediatric patients with active CD found that using EEN was significantly more effective than PEN (50% EN, 50% diet) at six weeks in decreasing diarrhea and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) levels [66]. Another earlier RCT involving 51 adult patients in remission with CD showed that the PEN group (which was half PEN and the other half a free diet) had a lower relapse rate compared to the group on an unrestricted diet. Both groups continued to consume an unrestricted diet, and continued medications were already in place at enrollment into the trial [67]. A recent retrospective study compared the effectiveness of EEN and PEN in 50 adult patients with moderate to severe CD in remission with ustekinumab [68]. The patients were divided into two groups based on EN delivery: EEN (n = 24) and PEN (n = 26). For 8 weeks, the elemental EEN formula was administered through a nasojejunal feeding tube, whereas the oral PEN polymeric or elemental formula was consumed alongside a low fiber, low fat diet. Data on clinical and endoscopic remission rates with CDAI and CDEIS, along with nutritional indicators (Body Mass Index, serum protein, albumin, prealbumin, transferrin) and inflammatory markers (serum endotoxin, interleukin-6, fecal calprotectin) were collected and compared after 8 weeks of ustekinumab. Both groups improved, but the EEN group had significantly higher clinical and endoscopic remission rates (p < 0.05) and lower inflammatory markers compared to PEN (p < 0.05). Additionally, the EEN group showed a greater increase in nutritional indicators (p < 0.05), demonstrating EEN’s greater effectiveness over PEN.

The type of diet used alongside PEN may play a role. A prospective pediatric study compared the impact of polymeric PEN in combination with one meal from the anti-inflammatory diet (n = 12) to polymeric EEN (n = 13) for six weeks in 25 patients with active CD [69]. The study evaluated clinical (pediatric CDAI), endoscopic (Simple Endoscopic Score for CD) remission rates, and mucosal healing in both groups at baseline and week 1, 3, and 6 intervals. The results showed that the clinical remission rates (p = 0.999) were comparable between PEN (75%) and EEN groups (69.2%). Endoscopic remission rates were also equal (45.5%) between both groups. Mucosal healing was achieved in 45.5% of EEN and 27.3% of PEN (p = 0.659). Combining PEN with one meal from an anti-inflammatory diet could be as effective as EEN for achieving clinical and endoscopic remission. Additional studies are needed.

Parenteral Nutrition

Parenteral nutrition (PN) is indicated to provide intravenous nutrients when an oral diet and EN are not feasible due to intolerance or a non-functional GI tract. Additional indications for using PN in IBD include addressing malnutrition, severe stricturing CD with chronic small bowel obstructions, severe high-output fistulizing CD, high-output stomas, and perioperative nutrition.

Although there is limited evidence, PN has traditionally been used for bowel rest to reduce exposure of potential pro-inflammatory dietary antigens to the bowel and enhance mucosal healing during active disease [70]. An earlier prospective, randomized study showed that in 47 adult patients with severe colitis (both CD and UC) receiving 60 mg of intravenous prednisolone for one week, the use of PN with bowel rest led to a greater decrease in the frequency of bowel movements and stool weight compared to an oral diet [70].

PN is used in the preoperative phase to reduce malnutrition in patients undergoing surgery. A recent retrospective cohort study compared the effectiveness of PN versus EEN in reducing postoperative complications among 510 adult patients with penetrating CD who underwent surgery [71]. Before surgery, 101 patients received PN for at least seven days, while 409 patients were on EEN for at least two weeks. Patients receiving PN demonstrated significant improvement in laboratory results (higher mean Hemoglobin level, p = 0.001, higher mean albumin levels,p = 0.001) at the time of surgery compared to their levels before PN. However, following surgery, PN was associated with an increase in postoperative complications when compared to patients receiving EEN (92.2% vs. 64.1%, p = 0.001).

Dietary Supplements

Prebiotics

Prebiotics are defined as non-digestible food components that specifically stimulate beneficial gut bacteria [72]. Common examples of prebiotics include psyllium husk, fructooligosaccharides (FOS), inulin, polyphenols, and galactooligosaccharides (GOS) [72]. Prebiotics may help modulate inflammation and enhance gut barrier function by promoting beneficial microbial populations like Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus. Their therapeutic effects involve several mechanisms: 1) Modulating gut microbiota: Prebiotics selectively boost beneficial bacterial populations that produce SCFAs known for their anti-inflammatory properties [73]; 2) Enhancing gut barrier function: SCFAs, particularly butyrate, improve intestinal epithelium’s integrity and reduce bacterial translocation [74]; 3) Immune regulation: Prebiotics affect immune responses by lowering pro-inflammatory cytokines like Tumor Necrosis Factor (TNF)-α and IL-6 while enhancing regulatory T-cell activity [75]; and 4) Reducing pathogenic bacteria [76].

A few studies have explored the effects of prebiotics in CD and UC, yielding mixed results. Plantago ovata, or psyllium husk, improved symptoms in adult patients in remission from UC compared to placebo (69% vs. 24%, p < 0.001) [77]. An open-label study of 105 remission adult UC patients suggested that plantago ovata may match mesalamine in maintaining remission [78]. Inulin improved fecal calprotectin in UC patients [79], and symptoms in pouchitis [80]. Lindsay et al. showed oligofructose-enriched inulin increased Bifidobacterium levels and reduced CD activity in adult patients [81], while Benjamin et al. indicated prebiotics had minimal impact on CD remission rates, emphasizing individual response variability [82]. An updated meta-analysis by Limketkai et al., which included 17 studies, concluded prebiotics, in particular, FOS and germinated barley foodstuff, may be effective in the induction and maintenance of remission of UC, respectively [83]. Overall, the studies of prebiotics for the treatment of CD did not show statistical significance.

Challenges remain in standardizing prebiotics for IBD treatment. Heterogeneity in formulations—types, dosages, and combinations—results in variable outcomes, complicating comparisons [73]. Gut microbiota variability in IBD patients may influence prebiotic response. Placebo-controlled trials with standardized formulations are needed to establish efficacy. Although initial evidence suggests potential benefits, further research is necessary to optimize formulations, evaluate patient-specific responses, and confirm long-term efficacy. Adverse effects like gas and bloating may occur, and gradual uptitration may help. Patients with strictures should be cautious of high-fiber oligosaccharides due to potential obstruction.

Probiotics

Probiotics are live microorganisms that provide health benefits at a specified dosage [84]. Genera like Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, and Saccharomyces boulardii benefit by enhancing intestinal barrier function [84], modulating immune responses, and restoring microbial balance [85, 86]. Additionally, probiotics may produce SCFA and other metabolites that have anti-inflammatory effects and support epithelial cell function [87].

Several studies have shown beneficial effects in treating adult patients with UC using Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 [88, 89], including an RCT showing it as effective as mesalamine in maintaining remission in UC patients [90]. Probiotic combinations like VSL#3 have reported improved clinical symptoms and inflammation in patients with UC [91]. Meta-analyses show VSL#3 benefits active UC treatment over placebo [92]. Some studies indicate benefits for maintaining remission [93, 94]. VSL#3 also shows potential for inducing remission and preventing recurrent pouchitis [95]. Notably, VSL#3’s formulation slightly changed in 2016, and there are few studies on the efficacy of the new formulation in IBD.

In contrast to UC, probiotic studies have had underwhelming results in CD. A study by Bourreille et al. found that Saccharomyces boulardii had no significant effect on preventing relapse in CD patients in remission [96]. A meta-analysis by Derwa et al. concluded that probiotics, particularly VSL#3, significantly improved remission rates in UC but had limited efficacy in CD [97]. A systematic review by Kaur et al. highlighted that while probiotics improve gut microbiota composition, their clinical benefits depend on strain specificity and disease phenotype [98]. Finally, from the AGA clinical practice guidelines on the role of probiotics in GI diseases, there are no strong recommendations for its use in the IBD population, with a noted existing knowledge gap [99]. There is potential evidence for treatment of symptoms, but more studies are needed; reassuringly, the risk of harm is low [44].

Probiotics may hold promise as an adjunctive strategy for modulating gut microbiota and reducing inflammation in UC and pouchitis. However, variability in strains, dosages, study designs, and IBD populations complicates conclusions. Despite potential benefits, challenges in standardizing probiotic treatments arise from the lack of regulation by the United States Food and Drug Administration, leading to dose variability and inconsistent responses. Well-designed, placebo-controlled trials with standardized formulations are needed to confirm efficacy. Future research should explore probiotics’ synergistic effects with dietary and pharmacologic interventions.

Vitamin D

Epidemiological data link low vitamin D levels to increased IBD incidence and severity, but causality remains unproven, and vitamin D is not definitively an effective IBD treatment [100]. It remains unclear if vitamin D deficiency contributes to the decrease in pathogenesis or indicates more severe disease.

Vitamin D is a multifunctional, fat-soluble hormone that regulates calcium homeostasis while contributing to immune modulation, cell proliferation, and intercellular adhesion. Once converted to its active form, 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D, it exerts both endocrine and paracrine effects, with the latter being particularly relevant for immune function [101]. It influences T-helper (Th) cell differentiation through its impact on antigen-presenting cells, guiding the development of pro-inflammatory (Th1) or anti-inflammatory (Th2) responses. Additionally, vitamin D suppresses overactive Th1 and Th17 responses while promoting the production of Th2 cytokines, such as interleukin-10, which counteract inflammation. By increasing the number and function of regulatory T-cells (Tregs), vitamin D helps balance dysregulated inflammatory responses [102].

IBD prevalence is highest in northern regions like North America and northern Europe, where limited sunlight restricts vitamin D synthesis [103]. Vitamin D deficiency in IBD is linked to intestinal dysbiosis, reduced bone density, increased inflammation, and worsened disease severity. Patients with CD often have lower vitamin D levels than the general population due to malabsorption, dietary restrictions, and decreased sun exposure from disease severity or medication [101]. A large study found lower CD incidence in individuals with higher predicted vitamin D levels, while low vitamin D correlates with increased disease activity in CD and UC [104]. Furthermore, supplementation with vitamin D has been shown to reduce disease flares and improve activity scores in patients with CD.

A prospective study of 3,217 adult patients with IBD (55% with CD) found that normalizing 25-hydroxyvitamin D levels (30–50 ng/mL) was linked to fewer CD-related surgeries [105]. A double-blind RCT in Denmark with 94 adult CD patients revealed that those receiving 1,200 IU of vitamin D3 daily had a lower relapse rate than the placebo group (13% vs. 29%), although it wasn’t statistically significant (p = 0.06) [106]. Another study examined higher doses of vitamin D3 (10,000 IU daily) in adult patients with CD in remission, showing that those on this dose had lower relapse rates than those receiving 1,000 IU daily (0% vs. 37%, p = 0.049) [107]. A Cochrane review that aggregated the data from 22 RCTs (n = 1,874 patients) found there may be fewer clinical relapses when comparing vitamin D with placebo [108].

Further randomized studies are required to clarify its immunomodulatory effects. Emerging evidence suggests vitamin D may synergize with gut microbiota to impact mucosal immunity in patients with IBD. Due to the increased risk of osteoporosis and vitamin D deficiency in IBD, monitoring 25-hydroxyvitamin D levels is recommended [109]. Since vitamin D is essential for calcium absorption and bone health, supplementation to maintain levels above 30 ng/mL is advised for all patients with IBD.

Omega-3 Fatty Acids: Fish Oil

Omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) can be precursors to macromolecular signaling molecules that serve as immunomodulators [110]. Among the omega-3 PUFAs, fish oil is one of the most studied and contains two PUFAs: eicosapentaenoic acid and docosahexaenoic acid. Omega-3 PUFAs can act on various intracellular pathways, which could be effective in the treatment of IBD.

An observational study by Stenson et al. involved 24 adult patients with active UC using fish oil (3.24 g of eicosapentaenoic acid and 2.16 g of docosahexaenoic acid) for four months while continuing IBD medications [111]. The study revealed a significant decrease in rectal leukotriene B4 levels (mean -43.3 pg/mL, 95% CI -83 to -3.6) and a significant decrease in acute and total histology index (average -8.5 from baseline 10.5, 95% CI -13.2 to -3.8). Another RCT over 12 months had adult patients receive either 4.5 g fish oil daily (eicosapentaenoic acid) or a placebo, finding a significant decrease in steroid requirements in the fish oil group during the first two months (p = 0.01) but no significant differences in maintaining remission [112].

An early observational study by Loeschke et al. assessed relapse prevention in 64 adult patients with UC in remission using 5.1 g of omega-3 fatty acid supplements a day [113]. They noted a significant improvement in relapse-free survival during months two and three compared to placebo (p < 0.05) but a similar relapse rate after several years. No consistent clinical, macroscopic, or histologic activity differences were observed. A major RCT in 2008 included two double-blind, placebo-controlled RCTs called the Epanova Program in Crohn’s Study (EPIC) 1 and 2, evaluating omega-3 PUFAs’ role in preventing relapse among adult CD patients (defined as CDAI < 150) [114]. In EPIC-1 (n = 363), relapse rates were 31.6% for treatment versus 35.7% for placebo (hazard ratio 0.82, 95% CI 0.51–1.19, p = 0.30). In EPIC-2 (n = 376), relapse rates were 47% for treatment versus 48.8% for placebo (hazard ratio 0.90, 95% CI 0.67–1.21, p = 0.48).

The evidence on fish oil for IBD is mixed. Some studies indicate it may reduce disease activity and corticosteroid needs, but no consensus exists among patients with CD and UC. Fish oil does not sustain remission in these conditions. More clinical trials are needed to evaluate its effectiveness in treating IBD.

Polyphenols: Curcumin/Resveratrol

Polyphenols are phytochemicals found in plants and are known to have anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects [115]. They inhibit toll-like receptor-4 activation, reducing immunomodulators like cyclooxygenase-2, NFkB, and pro-inflammatory cytokines [116]. Curcumin, from Curcuma longa (turmeric), has immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory properties that may aid in treating and preventing IBD flare-ups. Another polyphenol is resveratrol, which is known for its antioxidant effects and is found in grapes, berries, and nuts [115]. While mostly well tolerated, one of polyphenols’ most common side effects is diarrhea.

In 2005, a study involving five adult patients with UC and patients with CD tested 550 mg curcumin twice daily for 1 month and then 550 mg three times a day for the next month with 5-aminosalicylates (5-ASA) [117]. Results showed that patients with proctitis improved (p < 0.02), particularly in stool frequency and consistency. All subjects had a mean CDAI score reduction of 55 points (a notable small improvement) and a 10 mm/hr decrease in ESR. Another study with 56 adult UC patients examined 80 mg curcuminoid nanomicelles three times a day and 5-ASA versus placebo [118]. This double-blind RCT revealed a mean SCCAI score decrease in the treatment group (1.71 ± 1.84 vs. 2.68 ± 2.09, p = 0.05). Patients also reported improved urgency of defecation scores. Additionally, Hanai et al. conducted a randomized, double-blind, multicenter trial with 89 adult patients with quiescent UC who received 1 g curcumin twice a day with 5-ASA versus placebo plus 5-ASA for 6 months [119]. In the treatment group, 4.65% relapsed compared to 20.51% in the placebo group (p = 0.04). The curcumin group also showed improvement in the clinical activity index (p = 0.038) and endoscopy index (p = 0.0001) compared to placebo. This data suggests that curcumin can treat IBD and help maintain remission with no significant side effects. ECCO guidelines indicate that curcumin could serve as an adjunct therapy to induce remission in patients with mild to moderate UC, but further studies are needed to optimize formulation and dose [32].

Resveratrol is a promising polyphenol with notable therapeutic effects shown in pre-clinical studies [120]. A double-blind RCT by Samsami-Kor et al. involved 50 adult patients with mild to moderate active UC receiving either 500 mg resveratrol or placebo, comparing serum inflammatory markers and quality of life after six weeks [121]. The resveratrol group exhibited significant reductions in TNF-alpha (p < 0.001) and high-sensitivity CRP (p < 0.001), along with improved quality of life, as measured by IBDQ-9 score (p < 0.001) and lower clinical colitis activity scores (p < 0.001), compared to placebo. While resveratrol has some promise, more studies are needed to further assess its safety, utility, and effectiveness.

Aloe Vera

Xanthorrhoeaceae, which includes aloe vera, contains phytochemicals with anti-inflammatory effects [122]. One of the only major studies was a double-blind, placebo-controlled trial involving 44 adult patients, demonstrating that supplementation with oral aloe vera gel at a dose of 100 mL twice a day over four weeks led to a better clinical response, indicated by reduced histological disease activity (47% versus 14%, P < 0.05), compared to placebo [122]. However, diarrhea, a common side effect of aloe vera, may deter its use. Research on aloe vera remains limited, and further studies should be performed.

Challenges and Considerations

Food Avoidance

Food avoidance was self-reported in 28–89% of patients with IBD, while restrictive eating was self-reported in 41–93% of cases [123]. Contributors include a CD diagnosis, active disease, being female, dietary misinformation, and fear of experiencing bowel symptoms. Prolonged avoidance can create fear of dietary expansion and may lead to eating disorders like Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder (ARFID). ARFID diagnosis follows the DSM-5 criteria, requiring intolerance to food’s sensory attributes, fear of adverse effects from eating, and/or lack of interest in food.

A study assessed ARFID prevalence and risk factors using the Nine-Item ARFID Screen in 161 adult patients with IBD (45.3% with CD and 51.6% with UC) [124]. Twenty-eight patients had a positive ARFID risk score ≥ 24, while 74% continued avoiding at least one food. In comparison, it’s estimated that among the population with any gastrointestinal disorders, ARFID risk is estimated to be between 12 and 21%. All patients should be screened for ARFID before initiating nutrition therapy, especially those with symptomatic or active disease. The NIAS tool is used for screening but has not yet been validated in GI disorders. It will provide initial insights into food avoidance or restriction practices but cannot determine whether the extent of these practices exceeds a reasonable response as patients adjust their diets to alleviate symptoms. Consequently, the NIAS may overestimate the prevalence of ARFID [125]. The Fear of Food Questionnaire strongly correlated with GI symptom severity, health-related QoL, and self-reported Fear-ARFID symptoms in patients with IBD and IBS, suggesting that its use may have potential in screening for ARFID [126]. Patients screened positive for ARFID should be referred to a Registered Dietitian and a psychologist for interdisciplinary care.

Food avoidance and restrictions have also been correlated with food-related quality of life (FRQoL). FRQoL evaluates how diet, eating patterns, and food-related anxiety influence health-related QoL [127]. The FRQoL-29 questionnaire has been validated for use in adult and pediatric patients with IBD [128]. A prospective multi-center cross-sectional study of 108 adult patients with IBD showed that patients with food restrictions and active disease were found to have a decrease in FRQoL [129]. Patients with UC were found to have reduced FRQoL compared to patients with CD, regardless of disease severity. A prospective, cross-sectional study involved 60 children and adolescents with CD to complete the FRQoL-29 questionnaire [130]. FRQoL was decreased in patients with CD and their siblings compared to the controls. The results underscore the importance of personalized dietary interventions that optimize symptom management and FRQoL in patients with IBD.

Food Insecurity

Food prices affect access to nutritious options recommended for a specific diagnosis. The United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) defines food insecurity as “the household-level condition of limited or uncertain access to adequate and nutritious food [131].” Low food security is “reports of reduced quality, variety, or desirability of diet. Little or no indication of reduced food intake.” In contrast, very low food security is “reports of multiple indications of disrupted eating patterns and reduced food intake.” Any individual or household that falls into one of these two levels is considered food insecure.

According to the National Health Interview Survey 2015, the prevalence of food insecurity was estimated in 14% of the patients with IBD, which is mildly higher compared to the 12.7% seen in the US population, of whom half reported having very low food security [132]. Further survey analysis showed that 19% of the patients reported worries that food would run out before being able to buy more, and 16% of patients reported that food did run out before securing finances to buy more. A recent single-center, retrospective study also evaluated whether food insecurity was associated with changes in diet intake, especially with the intake of ultra-processed foods in 128 adult patients with IBD [133]. The results showed that 45% of the patients reported food insecurity with insufficient access to the grocery store at least sometimes in the past 12 months, and 10% reported decreased access to food often in the past year.

Cultural Practices

Food culture encompasses a community’s beliefs, values, and attitudes regarding food and its related practices. Cultural practices with food can vary within regions in the same country due to customs, traditions, religions, holidays, and availability of local ingredients [134]. When individuals move or migrate to a new country, they may undergo diet acculturation, which is the “process of adopting the eating patterns and food choices of the host country [134].” Diet acculturation has the potential to change food culture. The extent of diet acculturation depends on several factors, such as age migration, moving to a new country, whether the move was a choice, how long one is in residence, and the ability to speak the host country’s language will enhance diet acculturation [134]. Diet acculturation can also occur if cultural ingredients are unavailable or expensive.

Studies on elimination diets for IBD exist, but few focus on culturally adapted versions. Effectiveness may decline if diets are hard to follow or cultural foods are limited. Recently, Israeli researchers published a case series on various cultural adaptations of the CDED, including Korean, South American, and Turkish diets [135]. Initial challenges included the inability to use common spices and herbs, integrating religious practices, and modifying traditional recipes. Strategies to overcome these issues involve reviewing cultural dishes and adjusting recipes. Dietitians are crucial in personalizing diets to balance cultural food intake with IBD management and improve adherence. Nutritional resources in multiple languages, including recipes and handouts that reflect food culture, are also essential.

Conclusion

Diet plays a significant role in managing IBD. Oral diets such as CDED, SCD, mSCD, MD, AIP, and IBD-AID show potential for reducing inflammation, while low-fiber, low-FODMAP, LFD, and GFD lack clear benefits but may assist with symptom management. Supplements like prebiotics and probiotics are promising adjunctive treatments for IBD, though more standardized studies are necessary. Patients with IBD would benefit from maintaining normal vitamin D levels, and the intake of omega-3 fatty acids has been noted to provide some benefits in treating the disease. Polyphenols and aloe vera have limited benefits, but curcumin and mesalamine in UC could serve to be useful; more standardized studies on dosage and formulation are needed. EEN and PEN should be considered as induction therapies for CD and to address malnutrition and/or perioperative nutrition. PN is indicated when EN is not feasible to initiate in cases of malnutrition, malabsorption, high-output fistulas and stomas, and perioperative nutrition. Summary of Evidence

Of note, these dietary interventions have not all been directly compared to pharmacologic therapies, and in many of the studies evaluated, they served as adjuncts to pharmacotherapy. Diet is currently best as an adjunctive to pharmacotherapy for some situations and for symptom management for other scenarios. In addition, only EEN had sufficient data to induce remission in pediatric CD, while other options may offer benefits for maintaining remission and symptom management. An important consideration is patients’ nutritional status, especially with restrictive diets. Close monitoring is recommended for any patients at high risk for nutrient inadequacy to ensure adequate safety. Finally, patients with IBD should be individually assessed for food avoidance, food insecurity, and cultural practices to mitigate any potential obstacles to adequate dietary intake. An approach informed by a multidisciplinary IBD team is essential.
