A structural criterion for asymptotic states in Supersymmetry
Stefano Bellucciand, Stefania De Matteo

Abstract
In quantum field theory, the algebraic existence of a field does not guarantee the existence of a corresponding localized asymptotic particle state. This distinction is well established in the presence of infrared effects, long-range correlations, and environmental interactions, and becomes particularly relevant in supersymmetric theories, where fermionic and bosonic degrees of freedom are constrained at the algebraic level but need not share identical asymptotic behavior. In this work we introduce a minimal and predynamical localization criterion that distinguishes algebraically allowed degrees of freedom from those capable of forming stable, phase-coherent asymptotic states. The criterion is formulated in terms of long-time stability under slow structural fluctuations of an effective background, without modifying the underlying field equations or introducing new physical interactions. We show that fermionic and scalar fields respond qualitatively differently to such structural effects. While fermionic modes may retain asymptotic stability, scalar modes generically exhibit decoherence and damping, preventing their interpretation as localized one-particle states. This provides a conservative and model-independent perspective on how supersymmetric algebraic structures may coexist with an asymmetric observable particle spectrum. The analysis is intentionally non-constructive and does not rely on specific supersymmetry-breaking mechanisms, cosmological assumptions, or new dynamical ingredients. Rather, it clarifies localization as an independent structural requirement for particle existence within standard quantum field theory.

Effective Structural Background

To investigate localization in a controlled setting, we introduce an effective background field
\Xi(x)
, representing slowly varying structural fluctuations of the environment. This background is treated at an effective level and does not correspond to a fundamental degree of freedom.

The effective background
\Xi
is introduced solely as a parametric proxy for infrared and environmental effects already well known in
quantum field theory, such as decoherence and long-wavelength fluctuations, and does not represent a new force or fundamental medium.

Such an approach is standard in effective field theory and in open quantum system treatments of quantum fields interacting with an environment [19–21]. The role of
\Xi
is to induce long-wavelength modulations of local parameters without introducing new particle content. We assume that
\Xi
varies on scales much larger than the Compton wavelengths of the fields under consideration, allowing a separation between microscopic dynamics and macroscopic structural effects. Moreover,
\Xi
is assumed not to carry conserved charges. The effective background
\Xi
is introduced solely as a parametric proxy for infrared and environmental effects already well known in quantum field theory, such as decoherence and long-wavelength fluctuations, and does not represent a new force or fundamental medium.

4
Localization Criterion

We define a localization criterion
\Lambda[\Psi]
for a field configuration
\Psi
as:

\Lambda[\Psi]=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\Psi\text{ admits a stable, finite-energy, phase-coherent asymptotic state,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(1)

This criterion does not modify the equations of motion, but classifies their solutions according to their long-time behavior. Operationally, localization requires the absence of exponential damping in time, corresponding to a real asymptotic frequency. The emergence of an imaginary part signals phase decoherence and the failure of an asymptotic particle interpretation.

The criterion
\Lambda
can be viewed as a compact reformulation of standard requirements already implicit in LSZ theory and in the analysis of nonequilibrium quantum fields
[
20
,
21
]
. It does not introduce new dynamical assumptions and does not depend on the microscopic origin of the background fluctuations.

4.1
Robustness of the criterion

The qualitative conclusions derived from
\Lambda
are robust under variations of the assumptions on
\Xi
. Whether the background fluctuations are deterministic or stochastic, slowly varying or weakly time-dependent, the fundamental distinction remains: configurations that develop persistent damping fail to define asymptotic particle states. The localization criterion thus captures a structural property of the field equations rather than a fine-tuned dynamical effect.

5
Fermionic vs. Scalar Fields in a Structured Background

Let us now examine how fermionic and scalar fields respond to the presence of the effective background
\Xi
.

5.1
Fermionic Field

For a Dirac fermion
\psi
, consider the effective equation:

(i\gamma^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}-m-g_{f}\Xi)\psi=0.

(2)

The coupling to
\Xi
preserves the first-order structure of the Dirac equation. Long-wavelength fluctuations of
\Xi
induce phase shifts but do not generically produce damping. Similar robustness of fermionic modes is well known in nonequilibrium and transport analyses
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
.

As a result, fermionic modes may retain asymptotic phase coherence and satisfy:

\Lambda[\psi]=1.

(3)

Observation on fermionic stability.

The robustness of the fermionic asymptotic pole can be understood directly
from the first-order structure of the Dirac equation. Equivalently, and
purely at an effective level, the phase stability of fermionic modes can
be interpreted in terms of an emergent adiabatic background spin-connection
structure. In this representation, the Dirac equation takes the form

(i\gamma^{\mu}D_{\mu}-m-g_{f}\Xi)\psi=0,\qquad D_{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}-ig_{s}S_{\mu},

(4)

where
S_{\mu}
does not represent a new propagating degree of freedom,
but rather an effective geometric phase associated with slow background
variations. The induced holonomy protects the fermionic propagator pole
against decoherence, ensuring

\Lambda[\psi]=1.

(5)

This interpretation is optional and does not alter the localization
analysis, which remains valid regardless of the specific
representation adopted.

5.2
Scalar Field

For a scalar field, the effective equation takes the form:

(\Box+m^{2}+g_{s}\Xi^{2}+\kappa(\partial_{\mu}\Xi)^{2})\phi=0.

(6)

Here, structural fluctuations generate time-dependent contributions to the effective mass. Treating these contributions as stochastic perturbations leads to a fluctuating frequency with non-zero variance. Averaging over the fluctuations yields an exponential damping of the scalar propagator, implying loss of phase coherence and failure of localization [20–23]:

\Lambda[\phi]=0.

(7)

Explicit damping rate and LSZ interpretation.

The term
\kappa(\partial_{\mu}\Xi)^{2}\phi^{\dagger}\phi
can be interpreted as a stochastic modulation of the effective scalar mass induced by slow structural fluctuations of the background. By writing
\Xi(t)=\Xi_{0}+\delta\Xi(t)
and treating
\delta\Xi(t)
as a stationary random process with variance
\sigma_{\Xi}^{2}
and correlation time
\tau_{c}
, the coherent scalar amplitude obeys

\langle\phi(t)\rangle\sim e^{-imt}e^{-\Gamma t}

(8)

where the damping rate
\Gamma
is generically non-zero. A representative estimate yields

\Gamma\simeq\frac{\kappa^{2}\sigma_{\Xi}^{2}\tau_{c}}{4m^{2}}>0.

(9)

In the LSZ framework, a positive
\Gamma
corresponds to a non-zero imaginary part of the scalar self-energy,
\text{Im}\,\Sigma(m^{2})\neq 0
, which precludes the existence of a real mass pole.

This is precisely the condition under which the LSZ asymptotic construction fails to produce a one-particle state.

Consequently, the scalar field fails to define a localized asymptotic particle state, and the localization criterion yields

\Lambda[\phi]=0.

(10)

This result is structural and does not rely on fine-tuning the noise statistics, provided that the background fluctuations have finite variance and correlation time.

Toy Model Illustration

EFT power counting.

From the perspective of effective field theory, the operator
(\partial_{\mu}\Xi)^{2}\phi^{\dagger}\phi
has mass dimension six in
3+1
dimensions. Dimensional analysis thus fixes the scale of the coupling

[\kappa]=-2,\qquad\kappa=\frac{c_{\kappa}}{M_{*}^{2}},

(11)

where
M_{*}
represents the effective suppression scale associated with the structural background fluctuations and
c_{\kappa}
is a dimensionless coefficient. The localization mechanism discussed here does not depend on the ultraviolet completion of the theory, and remains valid within the natural regime of validity of the effective description.

Minimal Effective Model

To illustrate this mechanism explicitly, we introduce a minimal effective Lagrangian:

\mathcal{L}=\overline{\psi}(i\gamma^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}-m)\psi+(\partial_{\mu}\phi)^{\dagger}(\partial^{\mu}\phi)-(m^{2}+\kappa(\partial_{\mu}\Xi)^{2})\phi^{\dagger}\phi.

(12)

Assuming

9
Discussion and Outlook

The localization criterion introduced in this work establishes localization not merely as a dynamical outcome, but as a fundamental structural requirement for the definition of physical asymptotic states. By explicitly distinguishing between algebraic existence and physical localization, the framework clarifies how the coherence between supersymmetric field content and asymptotic particle spectra depends on stability properties enforced by the effective spacetime environment.

Within this perspective, a clear structural dichotomy emerges. Fermionic modes are protected by the first-order nature of their coupling, reflecting an intrinsic compatibility with phase coherence and long-term stability. Scalar superpartners, by contrast, generically fail to satisfy the localization criterion in the presence of long-wavelength structural fluctuations, leading to decoherence and the loss of a well-defined asymptotic particle interpretation. This mechanism provides a conservative explanation of how algebraic supersymmetry may remain intact even when spectral symmetry is not realized in the observable sector.

Conceptually, the present framework is fully consistent with established results in infraparticle physics and nonequilibrium quantum field theory, where the distinction between local field operators and asymptotic particle states is well understood [11–15,20–23]. In this sense, the effective structures introduced here should be regarded exclusively as parametric proxies for infrared and environmental effects already familiar in quantum field theory, rather than as new physical interactions or fundamental media.

Extensions of the localization criterion to interacting gauge theories and to fully gauge-invariant formulations in locally supersymmetric settings remain open directions for future investigation. Such developments could further clarify the interplay between algebraic symmetry, infrared stability, and the emergence of physical asymptotic states within the broader framework of supergravity and quantum field theory [16–18].

10
Conclusions

As a final remark, we stress that the structural criterion proposed in this work is a direct consequence of the logical implications of our previous analysis of the gravitino mass structure in locally supersymmetric theories
[
1
]
. In this sense, the present study should be considered as a necessary conceptual continuation, aimed at clarifying under which conditions algebraic supersymmetric degrees of freedom correspond to genuine asymptotic particle states, and under which conditions they may instead fail to localize into standard one-particle excitations.

Our results show that fermionic and scalar fields respond qualitatively differently to long-wavelength structural fluctuations of an effective background. While fermionic modes may maintain phase coherence and asymptotic stability, scalar superpartners generically fail to satisfy the localization criterion. This demonstrates that supersymmetry at the algebraic level does not in itself guarantee supersymmetry at the level of observable particle spectra.

Localization therefore emerges as an independent structural requirement for the existence of particles, complementary to conventional dynamical considerations. The perspective developed here is intentionally conservative: no new physical interactions, dynamical mechanisms, or ontological structures are introduced. Rather, the analysis provides a coherent framework to understand the persistent absence of scalar superpartners as a manifestation of localization failure, fully compatible with standard quantum field theory and the general logic of locally supersymmetric theories.
