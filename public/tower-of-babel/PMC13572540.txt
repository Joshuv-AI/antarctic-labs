A compositional framework for automated discovery of adversarial attacks
Authors: Roman Radionov, Alla Levina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The vulnerability of deep neural networks to adversarial attacks is a critical barrier to their safe deployment. However, the evaluation of model robustness still relies on a limited set of manually-designed, monolithic attacks, potentially leaving vast regions of the algorithmic search space unexplored. To address this limitation, we introduce a compositional framework that treats adversarial attacks as modular systems synthesized from a grammar of interchangeable functional blocks, including initializers, loss functions, schedulers, and a novel composite gradient computer. This work formalizes the design of new attacks as a black-box optimization problem within this complex, hierarchical space. Metaheuristic strategies, particularly Bayesian Optimization and a custom-designed Evolutionary Algorithm, are employed to automatically discover novel and effective attack configurations. Experiments, conducted on an ensemble of robustly trained ResNet-18 models, demonstrate that attacks discovered through this automated synthesis process provide promising evidence of outperforming established, manually-crafted benchmarks and state-of-the-art transfer baselines (including PGD, MIM, DIM, and the AutoAttack suite) within the evaluated ResNet-18/ImageNette setting, achieving a higher Attack Success Rate under identical perturbation constraints. Our findings suggest that automated compositional synthesis as a promising approach for discovering transferable adversarial vulnerabilities, demonstrating that query-assisted gray-box transfer auditing provides a more robust security evaluation than relying solely on fixed, monolithic algorithms.

Introduction

The widespread adoption of Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) in critical infrastructure, from autonomous driving systems to medical diagnostics and biometric authentication, has made their reliability a matter of paramount importance. While these models demonstrate superhuman performance on standard benchmarks, they remain characteristically brittle when exposed to adversarial examples: input data modified by imperceptible, carefully engineered perturbations designed to induce misclassification1,2. The existence of these vulnerabilities poses a fundamental security risk, undermining trust in AI deployment.

To evaluate and improve model robustness, the research community has developed a standardized arsenal of adversarial attacks. Methods such as the Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM)2, Projected Gradient Descent (PGD)3, and the Carlini & Wagner (C&W) attack4 serve as the primary benchmarks for measuring security. Consequently, defense mechanisms like Adversarial Training3 and TRADES5 are typically optimized to resist these specific algorithms.

However, a fundamental limitation of this approach is that existing attacks are treated as monolithic, atomic algorithms with fixed internal structures. Research in automated attack generation, such as AutoAttack6, has primarily focused on hyperparameter optimization (e.g., tuning the step size or the number of iterations) within these pre-defined structures. This leaves a vast, combinatorial space of algorithmic possibilities unexplored. By overfitting defenses to a narrow set of known attack structures, we risk creating a false sense of security, leaving models vulnerable to novel attack patterns that utilize different combinations of loss functions, gradient modifications, or update rules.

In this work, we address this limitation by proposing a compositional framework for adversarial attacks. We hypothesize that an attack should not be viewed as a fixed algorithm, but as a modular system constructed from a grammar of interchangeable functional components. By deconstructing the attack process into orthogonal operators—initializers, loss functions, gradient computers, schedulers, update rules, and projectors—we transform the problem of attack design into a structured optimization task.

To navigate this complex, non-Euclidean search space, three metaheuristic search strategies are implemented and compared: Random Search, Bayesian Optimization, and a custom Evolutionary Algorithm. The evaluation of these methods is performed against an ensemble of robustly trained ResNet-18 models on the ImageNette dataset. This specific combination of architecture and dataset is selected as a computationally tractable proxy to facilitate the extensive search requirements of the compositional framework within a constrained local workstation environment, while still providing a non-trivial benchmark for adversarial transferability. Empirical results demonstrate that automatically synthesized attacks, particularly those discovered by the Evolutionary Algorithm, show promising evidence of outperforming standard baselines under the specified query-assisted transfer search protocol. Specifically, against a robust ensemble comprising Standard, PGD-AT, and TRADES defenses, the EA-synthesized attack achieves a peak Attack Success Rate (ASR) of 58.15% (with an average of 43.67% across independent runs), effectively outperforming the standard PGD-40 baseline (13.2%) as well as state-of-the-art transfer suites like AutoAttack (34.83%). Crucially, this improvement is achieved without increasing perceptibility; the synthesized attack actually exhibits a lower average \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_2$$\end{document} perturbation norm (16.68) than PGD-40 (19.64). Ultimately, this study establishes that automated structural synthesis is a promising direction for rigorous AI security auditing.

It is important to clarify that the individual functional components utilized in this study (e.g., DLR loss, Momentum, Input Diversity) are drawn from prior foundational literature. The genuine novel contribution of this work lies not in inventing these atomic operators, but in the formulation of the automated, graph-theoretic framework that dynamically synthesizes their structural composition. This study establishes that automated structural synthesis is a promising direction for improved attack discovery and transfer-based security auditing.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: “Literature review” reviews the existing literature and paradigms in adversarial attack generation. “Methodology” details the proposed compositional methodology, including the mathematical formalism of the functional blocks and the metaheuristic search algorithms. “Results” presents the experimental results, including a Pareto analysis and structural insights derived from the synthesized attacks. “Discussion” discusses the broader implications for AI security, along with limitations and directions for future research. Finally, “Conclusions” provides the concluding remarks.

Literature review

The trajectory of adversarial attack research has shifted from heuristic approximations to rigorous optimization problems. As defensive mechanisms have grown more sophisticated, offensive strategies have co-evolved to bypass them. This section categorizes the evolution of existing methodologies into three primary paradigms: gradient-based iterative refinements, optimization-based adaptive approaches, and black-box evolutionary search. The structural limitations inherent in each paradigm motivate the automated compositional framework proposed in this study.

Gradient-based iterative algorithms and transferability

The genesis of adversarial generation lies in the Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM) proposed by Goodfellow et al.2 , which assumes local linearity of the model’s decision boundary. By computing a single-step update \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta = \epsilon \cdot \text {sign}(\nabla _x \mathcal {L})$$\end{document}, FGSM provides a computationally efficient but often suboptimal perturbation. To address the severe non-linearity of deep neural networks, Madry et al. formalized the attack as a constrained saddle-point optimization problem, solving the inner maximization via Projected Gradient Descent (PGD)3. PGD iteratively refines the perturbation while projecting it back onto the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_p$$\end{document}-ball, serving as the de facto foundational standard for evaluating empirical robustness.

However, as defenses like Adversarial Training3 matured, standard PGD was found to severely overfit to the surrogate model, resulting in poor transferability in black-box scenarios. Consequently, subsequent research focused on augmenting the PGD structure with specific gradient decorators. Dong et al. introduced the Momentum Iterative Method (MIM)7, integrating a velocity vector into the gradient accumulation to stabilize update directions and escape poor local maxima. To mitigate overfitting to specific spatial features, Xie et al. proposed Input Diversity (DI)8, applying stochastic transformations (e.g., resizing and padding) to the input prior to gradient computation. Further structural enhancements include Translation-Invariant (TI) attacks9, which convolve the gradient with a Gaussian kernel, and the Skip Gradient Method (SGM)10, which alters the backward pass to prioritize residual connections, thereby improving transferability across deep architectures.

While these methods introduced highly effective functional components, the literature typically treats them either as isolated algorithms or as manually concatenated pipelines (e.g., TI-DI-MIM). They remain rigid, monolithic constructs rather than flexible, composable modules that can dynamically adapt to an unknown defense.

Optimization-based and adaptive approaches

A parallel stream of research emerged in response to “gradient obfuscation” or “gradient masking”—a phenomenon where defenses intentionally or inadvertently shatter the loss landscape, rendering standard gradients useless11. To circumvent this, researchers shifted from standard Cross-Entropy (CE) to direct optimization of margin-based objective functions.

Carlini and Wagner demonstrated that CE loss is highly susceptible to gradient vanishing under defensive distillation, and proposed the C&W attack4. The C&W framework formulates the attack by minimizing a weighted combination of the perturbation’s \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_2$$\end{document} norm and a margin-based surrogate loss, effectively bypassing gradient masking caused by probability saturation. Building upon these foundations to create a standardized robustness benchmark, Croce and Hein introduced AutoAttack6. AutoAttack utilizes an ensemble of parameter-free attacks (most notably Auto-PGD) and introduces the Difference of Logits Ratio (DLR) loss, a scale-invariant objective that is highly robust against defenses that manipulate logit magnitudes.

AutoAttack represents the current state-of-the-art in automated security auditing. However, its automation is strictly limited to hyperparameter tuning—such as dynamically adjusting the step size α based on the loss plateau, or restarting from various random initializations. The underlying algorithmic topology (the specific sequence of gradient computations and updates) remains static. It lacks the capacity to automatically restructure itself—for example, by swapping an \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_2$$\end{document} updater for a sign-based updater, or inserting a smoothing decorator—based on the real-time feedback of the target model.

Evolutionary and black-box strategies

In strict black-box scenarios where internal gradient information is entirely inaccessible, query-based and evolutionary algorithms (EAs) have been heavily utilized. Methods such as GenAttack12 and the One Pixel Attack13 utilize genetic algorithms and differential evolution to iteratively breed adversarial examples by querying the target model’s output probabilities.

While successful in restricted threat models, these approaches typically define the evolutionary “genome” as the raw pixel values of the image itself. Evolving high-dimensional continuous vectors (d ≈ 150,000 for a standard ImageNet input) is subject to the curse of dimensionality, making it computationally prohibitive and highly query-inefficient. Furthermore, pixel-level evolution rarely yields perturbations that transfer effectively to other unseen models.

The need for algorithmic synthesis

The trajectory of the field highlights a distinct gap. While AutoML and Neural Architecture Search (NAS) have automated the design of the neural networks themselves, the design of the attacks used to evaluate them remains a manual, human-driven process. The framework presented in this paper diverges fundamentally from existing literature to bridge this gap. Instead of optimizing input pixels or tuning hyperparameter scalars, the proposed method evolves the algorithm itself. By defining a search space over abstract functional components, the system seeks to automatically synthesize novel algorithmic compositions that exploit unforeseen structural weaknesses in modern defenses.

Alternative threat models and adjacent modalities

While this study focuses on iterative \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_p$$\end{document}-bounded additive attacks, the broader literature has expanded into alternative perturbation models and domains. Recent research has introduced flow-based geometric deformations, such as DualFlow14 and AFLOW15, which achieve imperceptibility by deforming image structure via normalize-flow models rather than modifying pixel intensities. In the black-box domain, query-efficient strategies like STBA16 have been developed to evaluate robustness under strict interaction limits. Furthermore, the vulnerability of non-Euclidean data has emerged as a critical frontier, with studies demonstrating successful model inversion attacks on both homogeneous and heterogeneous graph neural networks17. The compositional framework proposed here is designed with a modular architecture that, while currently demonstrated on \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_p$$\end{document} vision tasks, is theoretically extensible to these emerging modalities.

Optimization over the configuration space

The synthesis of an optimal adversarial attack is formulated as a global optimization problem over the configuration space Ω. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F: \Omega \rightarrow \mathbb {R}^m$$\end{document} be the objective function vector, where m=1 for single-objective optimization (maximizing effectiveness) and m=2 for multi-objective optimization (maximizing effectiveness while minimizing perturbation magnitude). The function F(C) is non-convex, non-differentiable with respect to the configuration parameters, and computationally expensive to evaluate. Consequently, gradient-free metaheuristic strategies are required. Three distinct search methodologies are formalized below.

Stochastic exploration via random search

As a baseline for establishing the complexity of the search landscape, a Random Search (RS) strategy is employed. The strategy samples configurations \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_i$$\end{document} from a uniform distribution defined over the valid subspace of Ω. For hierarchical components such as the gradient computer \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {G}$$\end{document}, the depth of the composition tree is sampled uniformly from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[0, K_{\max }]$$\end{document}, followed by the uniform selection of operators for each node. This process approximates a Monte Carlo estimation of the global maximum:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} C^*_{\text {RS}} \approx \arg \max _{C \in \{C_1, \dots , C_N\}} F(C) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}While asymptotically consistent, RS lacks a feedback mechanism to guide sampling toward promising regions of Ω.

Objective functions and evaluation metrics

The optimization process described in the preceding section requires a rigorous quantitative assessment of the candidate configurations. The evaluation maps the set of adversarial examples generated by a configuration C to a vector of performance metrics. Two primary categories of metrics are defined: effectiveness (utility) and imperceptibility (cost).

Effectiveness: attack success rate (ASR)

The primary objective is to induce misclassification. To ensure a valid assessment, effectiveness is measured strictly on the subset of data correctly classified by the target model in its unperturbed state. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {D} = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{M}$$\end{document} be the evaluation dataset. The subset of initially correct samples, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {D}_{\text {clean}}$$\end{document}, is defined as:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {D}_{\text {clean}} = \{ (x, y) \in \mathcal {D} \mid \arg \max f(x) = y \} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where f(x) is the output probability vector of the target model.

The Attack Success Rate (ASR) for a configuration C is defined as the proportion of samples in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {D}_{\text {clean}}$$\end{document} for which the generated adversarial example \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_{\text {adv}}$$\end{document} results in a classification error. Let Φ (x, C) be the adversarial example generated from x using configuration C. The ASR is calculated as:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {ASR}(C) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal {D}_{\text {clean}}|} \sum _{(x, y) \in \mathcal {D}_{\text {clean}}} \mathbb {1}\left[ \arg \max f(\Phi (x, C)) \ne y \right] \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {1}[\cdot ]$$\end{document} is the indicator function. In the context of the optimization problem, the objective function is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_1(C) = \text {ASR}(C)$$\end{document}, which is to be maximized.

When evaluating a configuration C against an ensemble of M models, the overall ASR is defined as a micro-average across all target models. Specifically, each generated adversarial image is independently tested against every model in the ensemble, contributing to a total pool of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M \times |\mathcal {D}_{\text {clean}}|$$\end{document} potential successful attacks. The resulting metric, as defined in Eq.(8), captures the average probability that a synthesized attack generalizes across the diverse architectural defenses within the ensemble:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {ASR}_{\text {ensemble}}(C) = \frac{1}{M \cdot |\mathcal {D}_{\text {clean}}|} \sum _{j=1}^{M} \sum _{(x, y) \in \mathcal {D}_{\text {clean}}} \mathbb {1}\left[ \arg \max f_j(\Phi (x, C)) \ne y \right] \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_j$$\end{document} denotes the j-th model in the robust ensemble.

Experimental setup

To empirically validate the proposed framework and evaluate the efficacy of the automated search algorithms, a comprehensive experimental pipeline was established. Search configuration and budget

The core evaluation task requires the search algorithms to synthesize a universal, transferable attack capable of compromising the entire three-model ensemble simultaneously. The surrogate gradient for the search process is computed exclusively on the Standard model, testing the algorithms’ ability to find structural combinations that generalize to the protected PGD-AT and TRADES models.

Because the surrogate gradient is computed exclusively on the Standard model, the evaluation against the robust ensemble represents a mixed threat model: a white-box attack against the surrogate Standard model combined with a query-assisted gray-box transfer attack against the PGD-AT and TRADES defense paradigms. The paper explicitly frames its main contribution within this context: the proposed framework is designed for improved attack discovery in transfer-based auditing. It is well-documented that plain iterative methods like PGD transfer poorly; thus, this setup rigorously tests the discovery algorithm’s ability to identify generalized structural components that break robust models without requiring full white-box access to their gradients.

The discrete search space Ω encompasses all valid combinatorial arrangements of the component libraries defined in Section 3.2. To maintain computational tractability, the recursive composite gradient computer was constrained to a maximum depth of K=3 decorators.

To ensure a fair comparison across all metaheuristic strategies (Random Search, Bayesian Optimization, and Evolutionary Algorithm), the computational search budget was strictly fixed at 200 function evaluations (trials) per algorithm.

For the Evolutionary Algorithm, the hyperparameter configuration was strictly defined to balance exploration and exploitation within this limited evaluation budget. The algorithm was initialized with a population size of 10 individuals, evolved over 20 generations. Parent selection was performed using a tournament size of 3, coupled with NSGA-II non-dominated sorting and crowding distance for multi-objective ranking. A strict viability constraint was implemented to automatically discard configurations yielding an ASR below 5%, focusing the computational budget entirely on viable attack structures. Offspring were subjected to an aggressive overall mutation rate of 0.8. When a mutation occurred, a stochastic choice was made based on a predefined strategy split: a 60% probability for structural mutations (e.g., adding, removing, or swapping gradient decorators) and a 40% probability for parametric mutations (e.g., perturbing the step size scalar). This aggressive, structurally biased mutation strategy compensates for the small population size by ensuring the continuous introduction of novel genetic material.

All candidate attacks synthesized during the search phase were constrained to a maximum of N=40 iterations and an \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_\infty$$\end{document} perturbation budget of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\epsilon = 16/255 \approx 0.0627$$\end{document}. All training and evaluation were performed on the ImageNette dataset. While significantly smaller than the full ImageNet-1k, ImageNette retains the high-resolution characteristics (224 × 224) of the parent dataset. This allows for the validation of the structural discovery process without the prohibitive computational overhead associated with large-scale data loading, which was a necessary constraint for execution on a standalone local workstation.

To strictly isolate the variance caused by the stochastic search algorithms from the natural variance of the data distribution, the evaluation of each candidate configuration was performed on a fixed, identically seeded subset of 1,000 images randomly selected from the ImageNette validation set. This guarantees that all performance comparisons between Random Search, Bayesian Optimization, and the Evolutionary Algorithm are perfectly consistent.

Implementation details

The proposed framework was implemented in Python 3 utilizing the PyTorch library21 for tensor computations and neural network modeling. The Bayesian Optimization module was implemented utilizing the Optuna framework22 for the Tree-structured Parzen Estimator. All computational experiments, including model training and attack synthesis, were executed on a workstation equipped with a single NVIDIA GPU with CUDA support. The source code, configuration files, and pre-trained model weights necessary to reproduce the findings of this study are publicly available at https://github.com/RadionovRoman0362/Adversarial-Attack-Generator.

Computational cost and search overhead

To facilitate the practical deployment of this framework as a security auditing tool, it is essential to quantify the computational search cost. The overhead of one full search run is strictly bounded by the predefined budget of 200 function evaluations (trials). Because the fitness evaluation relies on a fixed 1000-image validation subset, a complete search run evaluates exactly 200,000 candidate images against the target ensemble. As the ensemble comprises three distinct models, this translates to 600,000 individual model forward passes during the search phase. This explicitly defines the target-query budget required by the metaheuristic algorithms to perform model selection and structural discovery prior to final inference.

The wall-clock time required to exhaust this budget is heavily dependent on the dynamic topological complexity of the configurations being evaluated. A standard search trajectory on a single NVIDIA RTX 3060 GPU takes approximately 6 hours. However, the evolutionary search frequently samples computationally intensive gradient decorators; for instance, the smooth_adv operator requires multiple forward passes per step to estimate the Gaussian-smoothed gradient, and the ensemble operator requires concurrent gradient extraction from multiple surrogate models. Search trajectories that frequently evaluate these dense structures can extend the total runtime to approximately 12 to 18 GPU hours. This variance underscores the practical trade-off between the expressive capacity of the compositional search space and the computational overhead of the audit.

Comparison with standard baselines

To rigorously assess the true performance gain of the proposed framework, the synthesized attacks were evaluated against a comprehensive suite of state-of-the-art baselines. In addition to standard PGD-40, the baseline suite includes the Momentum Iterative Method (MIM)7 and the Diverse Input Method (DIM)8, which are widely utilized to enhance attack transferability. All iterative baselines were constrained to the identical computational budget (N=40 steps) and perturbation bound (ε = 16/255). Furthermore, the standard AutoAttack suite6 was included to represent the gold standard in parameter-optimized robustness evaluation.

It is critical to contextualize the threat model for this evaluation. The task requires a gray-box transfer attack: perturbations are generated utilizing the gradients of the surrogate (Standard) model and subsequently transferred to the robust ensemble. Under these strict transfer conditions, standard iterative methods struggle significantly. Both PGD-40 and MIM-40 achieved an ASR of approximately 13% (with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_2$$\end{document} norms of 19.64 and 22.11, respectively). DIM-40, specifically designed for transferability, reached a 25% ASR with an average \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_2$$\end{document} norm of 18.35.

When the state-of-the-art AutoAttack suite is applied in a strict white-box setting with full access to the ensemble’s gradients, it predictably achieves a 100% ASR, fully exploiting the combined vulnerabilities. However, to compare against the framework’s transfer capabilities, AutoAttack was restricted to generating perturbations solely on the natural surrogate. Under this pure transfer condition, AutoAttack achieved an ensemble-averaged ASR of 34.83% (due to near-perfect success on the surrogate itself); however, its robust-only ASR (excluding the surrogate) dropped precipitously to 2.24%. This demonstrates a fundamental limitation of highly parameterized optimizers: they severely overfit to the surrogate model, generating artifacts easily deflected by distinct defenses. While our framework does not surpass the 100% efficacy of a multi-attack white-box suite, the Evolutionary Algorithm synthesizes a single, elegant 40-step computational graph that achieves a highly competitive 34.62% robust-only ASR. This establishes structural optimization as a highly perturbation-efficient and computationally unified alternative for robust security auditing.

In contrast, the metaheuristic search strategies, operating under the exact same constraints, demonstrated performance gaps over the fixed baselines. The Evolutionary Algorithm (EA) achieved an average ensemble ASR of 43.67% across multiple runs, peaking at 58.15% in the optimal trial. The combinatorial search space acts as a form of symbolic regularization; by evolving the algorithmic structure, the framework is forced to discover universal adversarial directions rather than surrogate-specific noise. While Random Search demonstrated that the search space contains configurations with higher raw transferability (typically accompanied by larger distortions), the EA identifies compositional synergies that offer a more balanced perturbation-efficiency profile. Both automated methods show promising evidence of outperforming the evaluated fixed-structure transfer baselines (DIM) and parameter tuners (AutoAttack) under the specified query-assisted transfer conditions.

Structural insights

Analysis of the highest-performing individuals reveals convergence towards specific structural patterns. The Evolutionary Algorithm consistently selected the Difference of Logits Ratio (DLR) loss function combined with the Targeted Attack on Predicted (TAP) gradient computer. Notably, these components are widely recognized in the literature for their ability to mitigate gradient masking and improve transferability6. The fact that our automated framework independently converged to these expert-designed structures—without prior bias–validates the search capability of the proposed grammar and suggests it can reliably identify optimal attack vectors for novel defense mechanisms.

Post-hoc component population analysis

To estimate the contribution of individual components and assess the role of structural depth, a post-hoc population analysis was performed on the Pareto-optimal configurations discovered across all multi-seed ensemble runs. By quantifying the frequency of component selection and the average depth of gradient compositions, strong structural correlates of attack transferability were identified. It should be noted that while this frequency analysis highlights highly successful component synergies, it is observational; isolated causal intervention and testing of individual operators within the dynamic hierarchical trees remains a topic for future controlled studies.

1. Systematic rejection of standard objectives: Analysis of the Pareto fronts reveals a near-universal convergence toward margin-based loss functions for transfer attacks. Within the Evolutionary Algorithm runs, standard Cross-Entropy was discarded in 78% of Pareto-optimal solutions in favor of the Difference of Logits Ratio (DLR) loss. Similarly, Random Search selected DLR or CW loss in over 85% of successful configurations. This strong empirical correlation suggests that margin-based objectives provide a significant advantage in overcoming the gradient masking inherent in PGD-AT and TRADES defenses, though rigorous causal inference would require isolated, single-variable control studies.

2. Identification of the core gradient triplet and module interaction: Component frequency analysis isolates a specific functional synergy between three operators: the Targeted Attack on Predicted (TAP) terminal, the Ensemble decorator, and the Momentum decorator. TAP appeared as the primary terminal operator in the majority of EA Pareto solutions and was a top-tier operator across all search methodologies. Beyond individual frequency, the search revealed a critical interaction between the loss function and gradient operator: the DLR loss provides the stable, scale-invariant margin necessary for the TAP operator to remain effective across multiple decision boundaries. This interaction suggests that the framework does not merely select strong ingredients in isolation, but discovers complementary functional pairings that are robust to gradient-shattering defenses.

Discussion

The results presented in this study provide strong empirical support for our primary hypothesis: that treating adversarial attacks as monolithic, fixed algorithms limits the evaluation of neural network robustness. By deconstructing attacks into a compositional grammar and applying automated search, we found promising evidence suggesting that “standard” defenses like PGD-AT and TRADES may be more vulnerable under query-assisted transfer scenarios than previously estimated by static benchmarks.

The power of structural composition

Existing literature has largely focused on parameter optimization within fixed attack structures (e.g., optimizing the step size of PGD)6. Our findings indicate that this approach hits a performance ceiling. The significant performance leap achieved by our framework against the robust ensemble (e.g., a 58.15% best-run ensemble-averaged ASR vs. 34.83% for the AutoAttack transfer baseline and 13.2% for PGD-40) suggests that the structure of the attack–the specific combination of loss function, gradient decorators, and update rules–is just as critical as the perturbation magnitude. Specifically, the frequent emergence of the Difference of Logits Ratio (DLR) loss combined with the Targeted Attack on Predicted (TAP) gradient in our high-performing individuals strongly correlates with the successful evasion of gradient masking, suggesting that structural adaptation provides benefits beyond simply increasing the iteration count. Crucially, our post-hoc population analysis indicates that success is not derived from merely treating these known components as a “bag of tricks” to be activated simultaneously; rather, the search algorithms favor specific topological arrangements (e.g., nesting TAP within an Ensemble decorator at an optimal depth of 1.61) that appear to create the necessary synergy for high transferability.

Implications for AI security

The ability to automatically synthesize attacks that outperform manual baselines implies that current security certifications based on static tests (e.g., “robust to PGD-20”) are insufficient. An adversary with automated tools can likely find a specific configuration that bypasses a deployed defense. Consequently, we argue that the definition of robustness should shift from resistance against specific algorithms to resistance against computational budgets of automated search. Security audits should employ frameworks like the one proposed here to establish a lower bound on robustness against adaptive adversaries.

Limitations and future work

While the presented framework demonstrates significant potential, there are avenues for further research. First, it is acknowledged that the experimental scope of this study is restricted to the ResNet-18 architecture and the ImageNette dataset. This limitation was dictated by the computational demands of exploring a hierarchical, combinatorial search space Ω. Evolving an algorithmic structure requires hundreds of full-dataset evaluations; scaling this process to massive datasets like ImageNet-1k or complex architectures such as Vision Transformers (ViTs) would increase the required GPU-hours by several orders of magnitude beyond the resources available for this study. While the reported ASR gains are significant, further research is required to determine the degree to which specific discovered structures generalize to different architectural families. However, as a proof-of-concept for automated structural synthesis, the current results establish the necessary foundation for future large-scale investigations.

Second, the current framework optimizes attacks primarily for success rate and norm constraints. Integrating perceptual metrics (e.g., LPIPS or SSIM) into the multi-objective fitness function could yield attacks that are even less perceptible to humans.

Third, the most promising direction is to close the loop by integrating this automated discovery engine directly into the training process. “Automated Adversarial Training,” where the model creates its own attacks during training using our evolutionary framework, could theoretically lead to models that are robust not just to PGD, but to the entire space of compositional attacks.

Fourth, while multi-seed optimization runs (N=3) were conducted to account for the inherent stochastic variance of the search algorithms, this sample size restricts the application of robust non-parametric statistical testing (e.g., Wilcoxon signed-rank tests) to confirm the formal significance of mean ASR differences between Random Search and the Evolutionary Algorithm. Expanding the number of independent search trajectories to rigorously quantify statistical significance is a necessary next step. Furthermore, assessing the sensitivity of the discovered structural optima across entirely different data folds (k-fold cross-validation) remains an area for future investigation requiring greater computational resources.

Finally, this study evaluates imperceptibility strictly through \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_p$$\end{document}-norms (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_\infty , L_2, L_1$$\end{document}). While these metrics are standard in adversarial literature, they do not always correlate perfectly with human visual systems. The integration of perceptual metrics, such as the Structural Similarity Index (SSIM) or Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS), into the multi-objective optimization fitness function represents a significant direction for improving the visual stealthiness of synthesized attacks.

Conclusions

This study addressed the limitations of manual, monolithic adversarial attack design by introducing a compositional framework for the automated synthesis of attacks. By formalizing the attack generation process as a combinatorial optimization problem over a grammar of functional components, we enabled the discovery of novel algorithmic structures that exploit specific weaknesses in defended models.

Our empirical evaluation on an ensemble of robust ResNet-18 models demonstrated that automated search strategies–specifically the custom Evolutionary Algorithm–can identify attack configurations that exhibit promising evidence of outperforming established baselines under the specified query-assisted transfer search protocol. The EA-synthesized attacks achieved a mean ensemble-averaged Attack Success Rate of 43.67% (peaking at 58.15%) against a robust ensemble, compared to approximately 13% for standard PGD-40 and 34.83% ensemble-averaged ASR for the AutoAttack transfer baseline, while simultaneously reducing the average perturbation magnitude. Crucially, while Random Search was found to occasionally discover configurations with higher raw robust-target transfer success by utilizing larger distortion budgets, the Evolutionary Algorithm distinguished itself by providing a favorable effectiveness-to-stealth trade-off, maintaining competitive transferability with a reduced perturbation footprint. Furthermore, distinct utility niches were identified for different search strategies: Random Search for broad exploration of high-cost outliers, Bayesian Optimization for strictly constrained low-perturbation attacks, and Evolutionary Algorithms for achieving a balance between effectiveness and stealth.

These findings suggest that the evaluation of neural network robustness must evolve from static testing against fixed algorithms to query-assisted gray-box transfer auditing using automated synthesis tools. Future work will focus on integrating this discovery framework directly into the training loop to develop “Auto-Adversarially Trained” models capable of resisting a much broader spectrum of structural attacks.
