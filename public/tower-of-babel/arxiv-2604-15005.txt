1.
Introduction

A
lattice polytope
is a convex polytope all of whose vertices have integer coordinates.
Two lattice polytopes
{\mathcal{P}},{\mathcal{Q}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
are said to be
unimodularly equivalent
if there exist a unimodular matrix
U\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d\times d}
and a lattice point
{\mathbf{w}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
such that

{\mathcal{Q}}=f_{U}({\mathcal{P}})+{\mathbf{w}},

where
f_{U}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is the linear map defined by
f_{U}({\mathbf{x}})={\mathbf{x}}U
.
In the study of lattice polytopes, it is standard to consider classification problems up to unimodular equivalence.
Moreover, a lattice simplex
\Delta
can be described in terms of its associated finite abelian group
\Lambda_{\Delta}
, and unimodular equivalence classes of lattice simplices can be recovered from these groups.
Using associated finite abelian groups and coding-theoretic methods, Batyrev and Hofscheier
[
2
]
classified lattice polytopes whose
h^{*}
-polynomials are binomials.
This point of view will play an important role in the present paper, and we recall the precise correspondence in Section
2
.

Let
{\mathcal{P}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a lattice polytope of dimension
d
.
The
lattice pyramid
over
{\mathcal{P}}
is the lattice polytope

{\rm Pyr}({\mathcal{P}}):=\operatorname{conv}({\mathcal{P}}\times\{0\},(0,\dots,0,1))\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}.

More generally, we say that a lattice polytope is a lattice pyramid if it is obtained by successively taking lattice pyramids over a lower-dimensional lattice polytope.
Since lattice pyramid constructions preserve several important properties and invariants, it is natural to study classification problems for lattice polytopes which are not lattice pyramids.
For this reason, many classification problems in the theory of lattice polytopes are naturally formulated under the assumption that the polytope is not a lattice pyramid.

The
codegree
of
{\mathcal{P}}
, denoted by
{\rm codeg}({\mathcal{P}})
, is defined by

{\rm codeg}({\mathcal{P}})=\min\{\,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}:{\rm int}(n{\mathcal{P}})\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\neq\emptyset\,\},

where
n{\mathcal{P}}:=\{n{\mathbf{x}}:{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{P}}\}
and
{\rm int}({\mathcal{P}})
denotes the interior of
{\mathcal{P}}
.
The
degree
of
{\mathcal{P}}
, denoted by
\deg({\mathcal{P}})
, is defined by

\deg({\mathcal{P}})=d+1-{\rm codeg}({\mathcal{P}}).

These invariants play important roles in the study of lattice polytopes and their Ehrhart theory.
We recall in Section
2
that
\deg({\mathcal{P}})
coincides with the degree of the
h^{*}
-polynomial of
{\mathcal{P}}
.

If one restricts attention to lattice polytopes which are not lattice pyramids, then the degree imposes strong restrictions on the dimension.
More precisely, it was shown in
[
14
]
that if a lattice polytope of dimension
d
and degree
s
has
d+c+1
vertices and is not a lattice pyramid, then

d\leq c(2s+1)+4s-2.

In particular, for lattice simplices one has

d\leq 4s-2.

This naturally leads to the extremal case

d=4s-2

for lattice simplices.
Higashitani
[
11
]
characterized lattice simplices in this extremal case and showed that they arise from binary simplex codes.
Thus binary codes already appear naturally in the study of lattice simplices at the boundary of the dimension-degree inequality.

On the other hand, Gorenstein polytopes form one of the most important classes of lattice polytopes.
A lattice polytope
{\mathcal{P}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is called
reflexive
if the origin of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
belongs to the interior of
{\mathcal{P}}
and the dual polytope of
{\mathcal{P}}
is again a lattice polytope.
A lattice polytope
{\mathcal{P}}
is called
Gorenstein of index
r
if
r{\mathcal{P}}
is unimodularly equivalent to a reflexive polytope.
If
{\mathcal{P}}
is Gorenstein of index
r
, then
{\rm codeg}({\mathcal{P}})=r
.
Moreover, Gorenstein polytopes are characterized by the palindromicity of their
h^{*}
-polynomials.
They arise naturally in several areas such as toric geometry, commutative algebra, and mirror symmetry, and in each fixed dimension there exist only finitely many Gorenstein polytopes up to unimodular equivalence.
For these reasons, the classification of Gorenstein polytopes has been regarded as a fundamental problem.
For example, several classification results on Gorenstein simplices based on associated finite abelian groups were obtained in
[
9
,
10
,
17
]
.

For Gorenstein polytopes, a stronger bound is known: if a Gorenstein polytope of dimension
d
and degree
s
is not a lattice pyramid, then
d\leq 3s-1

[
15
]
.
If one restricts further to Gorenstein simplices, then a much stronger bound is available.
In fact, it was shown in
[
5
]
that if a Gorenstein simplex of dimension
d
and degree
s
is not a lattice pyramid, then

d\leq 2s-1.

Thus, for Gorenstein simplices, the extremal case is

d=2s-1.

The aim of this paper is to study this extremal case.
More precisely, we investigate Gorenstein simplices of dimension
2s-1
and degree
s
which are not lattice pyramids.
Although this situation is much more restrictive than Higashitani’s extremal case for arbitrary lattice simplices, binary codes still govern the classification.
Our first main result shows that such simplices are characterized by even binary self-complementary codes.
Here a binary self-complementary code means a binary linear code containing the all-one vector.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Delta
be a
(2s-1)
-dimensional Gorenstein simplex of degree
s
which is not a lattice pyramid.
Then there exists an even binary self-complementary code
C\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2s}
such that the associated finite abelian group of
\Delta
is of the form

\Lambda_{\Delta}=\left\{\frac{1}{2}{\mathbf{c}}\in[0,1)^{2s}:{\mathbf{c}}\in C\right\}.

Conversely, every even binary self-complementary code of length
2s
arises from a
(2s-1)
-dimensional Gorenstein simplex of degree
s
which is not a lattice pyramid.

Thus, in the extremal case
d=2s-1
, the classification of Gorenstein simplices is reduced to the classification of even binary self-complementary codes.
Moreover, the corresponding
h^{*}
-polynomials are determined by the weight distributions of these codes (Corollary
3.4
).

Batyrev and Juny
[
1
]
classified Gorenstein toric Del Pezzo varieties in arbitrary dimension; equivalently, they classified Gorenstein polytopes of degree
2
.
On the other hand, for Gorenstein polytopes of degree at least
3
, classification problems become much more difficult in general.
As applications of Theorem
1.1
, we obtain classifications of Gorenstein simplices of degrees
3
and
4
.
Equivalently, we classify polarized
d
-dimensional Gorenstein fake weighted projective spaces
(X,L)
satisfying

-K_{X}=(d-2)L\quad\text{or}\quad-K_{X}=(d-3)L,

where
-K_{X}
is the anticanonical divisor of
X
and
L
is a Cartier divisor on
X
.

Classical classification results are available for reflexive polytopes in low dimensions, and hence also for reflexive simplices.
In particular, Kreuzer and Skarke
[
12
,
13
]
showed that there exist exactly
4{,}319
unimodular equivalence classes of reflexive polytopes in dimension
3
and exactly
473{,}800{,}776
unimodular equivalence classes of reflexive polytopes in dimension
4
.
On the other hand, if a Gorenstein simplex of degree
3
is not a lattice pyramid, then its dimension is at most
5
.
Hence only the cases of dimensions
3
,
4
, and
5
can occur.
In dimension
3
, they are precisely reflexive simplices, and there exist exactly
48
unimodular equivalence classes.
In dimension
4
, they are precisely Gorenstein simplices of index
2
which are not lattice pyramids, and there exist exactly
13
unimodular equivalence classes.
Therefore the only remaining case is dimension
5
, which is precisely the extremal case covered by Theorem
1.1
.
In this way, we obtain the following explicit classification.

Theorem 1.2
.

There exist exactly
6
unimodular equivalence classes of
5
-dimensional Gorenstein simplices of degree
3
which are not lattice pyramids.

We record the result here and refer to its full form in Theorem
4.4
.
Combined with the known classifications in dimensions
3
and
4
, this yields the complete classification of Gorenstein simplices of degree
3
.

While the classification of reflexive polytopes in higher dimensions is still very difficult in general, Ghirlanda
[
7
]
recently classified reflexive simplices up to dimension
6
; see also
[
8
]
.
On the other hand, if a Gorenstein simplex of degree
4
is not a lattice pyramid, then its dimension is at most
7
.
Hence only the cases of dimensions
4
,
5
,
6
, and
7
can occur.
In dimension
4
, they are precisely reflexive simplices, and there exist exactly
1{,}561
unimodular equivalence classes.
In dimension
5
, they are precisely Gorenstein simplices of index
2
which are not lattice pyramids, and there exist exactly
264
unimodular equivalence classes.
In dimension
6
, they are precisely Gorenstein simplices of index
3
which are not lattice pyramids, and there exist exactly
47
unimodular equivalence classes.
Therefore the only remaining case is dimension
7
, which is again the extremal case covered by Theorem
1.1
.
This yields the following classification.

Theorem 1.3
.

There exist exactly
19
unimodular equivalence classes of
7
-dimensional Gorenstein simplices of degree
4
which are not lattice pyramids.

We record the result here and refer to its full form in Theorem
4.20
.
Combined with the known classifications in dimensions
4
,
5
, and
6
, this yields the complete classification of Gorenstein simplices of degree
4
.

Once Theorem
1.1
is established, the classifications in Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
, as well as analogous classifications for larger values of
s
, can in principle be obtained by computer search. In this paper, however, we give theoretical proofs for the cases
s=3
and
4
.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we recall basic facts on
h^{*}
-polynomials, Gorenstein polytopes, finite abelian groups associated to lattice simplices, and binary linear codes.
In Section
3
, we prove the classification of
(2s-1)
-dimensional Gorenstein simplices of degree
s
in terms of even binary self-complementary codes.
In Section
4
, we apply this result to classify Gorenstein simplices of degree
3
and
4
.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we recall basic facts on
h^{*}
-polynomials, Gorenstein polytopes, finite abelian groups associated to lattice simplices, and binary linear codes.

2.1.
Ehrhart theory and Gorenstein polytopes

Let
{\mathcal{P}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a lattice polytope of dimension
d
.
Given a positive integer
k
, we define

L_{{\mathcal{P}}}(k)=|k{\mathcal{P}}\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}|.

The study on
L_{{\mathcal{P}}}(k)
originated in Ehrhart
[
6
]
who proved that
L_{{\mathcal{P}}}(k)
is a polynomial in
k
of degree
d
with the constant term
1
.
We call
L_{{\mathcal{P}}}(k)
the
Ehrhart polynomial
of
{\mathcal{P}}
.
The generating function of the lattice point enumerator, i.e., the formal power series

\text{Ehr}_{\mathcal{P}}(t)=1+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}L_{{\mathcal{P}}}(k)t^{k}

is called the
Ehrhart series
of
{\mathcal{P}}
.
It is known that it can be expressed as a rational function of the form

\text{Ehr}_{\mathcal{P}}(t)=\frac{h^{*}({\mathcal{P}},t)}{(1-t)^{d+1}},

where
h^{*}({\mathcal{P}},t)
is a polynomial in
t
of degree at most
d
with nonnegative integer coefficients
[
16
]
and it
is called
the
h^{*}
-polynomial
of
{\mathcal{P}}
.
Moreover,

h^{*}({\mathcal{P}},t)=\sum_{i=0}^{d}h_{i}^{*}t^{i}

satisfies
h^{*}_{0}=1
,
h^{*}_{1}=|{\mathcal{P}}\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}|-(d+1)
and
h^{*}_{d}=|{\rm int}({\mathcal{P}})\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}|
.
Furthermore,
h^{*}({\mathcal{P}},1)=\sum_{i=0}^{d}h_{i}^{*}
is equal to the normalized volume of
{\mathcal{P}}
.
Note that
\deg({\mathcal{P}})
coincides with the degree of
h^{*}({\mathcal{P}},t)
.
It is well-known that
h^{*}({\mathcal{P}},t)=h^{*}({\rm Pyr}({\mathcal{P}}),t)
.
We refer the reader to
[
3
]
for the detailed information about Ehrhart polynomials and
h^{*}
-polynomials.

A lattice polytope
{\mathcal{P}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
of dimension
d
is called
reflexive
if the origin of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
belongs to the interior of
{\mathcal{P}}
and its dual polytope

{\mathcal{P}}^{\vee}:=\{{\mathbf{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:\langle{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\rangle\leq 1\ \text{for all}\ {\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{P}}\}

is also a lattice polytope, where
\langle{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\rangle
is the usual inner product of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
A lattice polytope
{\mathcal{P}}
is called
Gorenstein of index
r
if
r{\mathcal{P}}
is unimodularly equivalent to a reflexive polytope.
We can characterize Gorenstein polytopes by their
h^{*}
-polynomials.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
4
]
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{P}}
be a lattice polytope of degree
s
, and write

h^{*}({\mathcal{P}},t)=h_{0}^{*}+h_{1}^{*}t+\cdots+h_{s}^{*}t^{s}.

Then
{\mathcal{P}}
is Gorenstein if and only if
h^{*}({\mathcal{P}},t)
is palindromic, namely,
for any
0\leq i\leq s
, one has

h^{*}_{i}=h^{*}_{s-i}.

2.2.
Finite abelian groups associated to lattice simplices

Let

\Delta=\operatorname{conv}({\mathbf{v}}_{0},\dots,{\mathbf{v}}_{d})\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

be a lattice simplex of dimension
d
.
We define the associated finite abelian group of
\Delta
by

\Lambda_{\Delta}=\left\{(x_{0},\dots,x_{d})\in[0,1)^{d+1}:\sum_{i=0}^{d}x_{i}({\mathbf{v}}_{i},1)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d+1}\right\}.

The group operation is addition modulo
1
in each coordinate.
For

{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{0},\dots,x_{d})\in\Lambda_{\Delta},

we define the
height
of
{\mathbf{x}}
by

{\rm ht}({\mathbf{x}}):=\sum_{i=0}^{d}x_{i}.

Since

\sum_{i=0}^{d}x_{i}({\mathbf{v}}_{i},1)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d+1},

the number
{\rm ht}({\mathbf{x}})
is always an integer.
For
{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{0},\dots,x_{d})\in\Lambda_{\Delta}
, we denote by
-{\mathbf{x}}
the inverse of
{\mathbf{x}}
in
\Lambda_{\Delta}
.
Moreover, if
0<x_{i}<1
for all
i
, then

-{\mathbf{x}}=(1-x_{0},\dots,1-x_{d})

and
{\rm ht}(-{\mathbf{x}})=d+1-{\rm ht}({\mathbf{x}})
.

The following facts are well-known.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\Delta
be a lattice simplex of dimension
d
, and write

h^{*}(\Delta,t)=h_{0}^{*}+h_{1}^{*}t+\cdots+h_{d}^{*}t^{d}.

Then for any
0\leq i\leq d
, one has

h_{i}^{*}=|\{{\mathbf{x}}\in\Lambda_{\Delta}:{\rm ht}({\mathbf{x}})=i\}|.

Lemma 2.3

(
[
2
]
)
.

Let
\Delta
be a lattice simplex of dimension
d
.
Then
\Delta
is a lattice pyramid if and only if there exists an index
i\in\{0,\dots,d\}
such that for all
{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{0},\dots,x_{d})\in\Lambda_{\Delta}
,

x_{i}=0
.

We also recall the correspondence between lattice simplices and finite abelian subgroups.

Proposition 2.4

(
[
2
]
)
.

Let
d\geq 1
.
Then there is a bijection between unimodular equivalence classes of
d
-dimensional lattice simplices with an ordered set of vertices and finite abelian subgroups
G\subset[0,1)^{d+1}
under addition modulo
1
such that
for all
(x_{0},\dots,x_{d})\in G
,

\sum_{i=0}^{d}x_{i}\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

Under this correspondence, two lattice simplices are unimodularly equivalent if and only if their associated groups coincide up to a permutation of coordinates.

2.3.
Binary linear codes

Let
n\geq 1
.
A
binary linear code
of length
n
is a linear subspace
C\subset{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{n}
.
For

{\mathbf{c}}=(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})\in{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{n},

the
Hamming weight
of
{\mathbf{c}}
is defined by

{\rm wt}({\mathbf{c}}):=|\{i:c_{i}\neq 0\}|.

The code
C
is called
even
if every codeword of
C
has even weight.

In this paper, a binary linear code
C\subset{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{n}
is called
self-complementary
if it contains the all-one vector

\mathbf{1}_{n}:=(1,\dots,1)\in{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{n}.

For a binary linear code
C\subset{\mathbb{F}}_{2}^{2s}
, we will consider the subset

\frac{1}{2}C:=\left\{\frac{1}{2}{\mathbf{c}}\in[0,1)^{2s}:{\mathbf{c}}\in C\right\}.

If
C
is even, then every element of
\frac{1}{2}C
has integral height, and hence
\frac{1}{2}C
satisfies the integrality condition in Proposition
2.4
.

3.
Gorenstein simplices of dimension
2s-1
and degree
s

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.1
.
First, we show the following.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let

\Delta=\operatorname{conv}({\mathbf{v}}_{0},\dots,{\mathbf{v}}_{d})\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

be a Gorenstein simplex of dimension
d
and codegree
r
, and let

{\mathbf{p}}=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\lambda_{i}{\mathbf{v}}_{i}

be the unique interior lattice point of
r\Delta
, where

\lambda_{i}>0
for all
0\leq i\leq d
and
\sum_{i=0}^{d}\lambda_{i}=r
.
Then one has
0<\lambda_{i}\leq 1
for all
i
. Moreover,
if
\lambda_{j}=1
for some
j
, then

\Delta
is a lattice pyramid.

Proof.

Assume that
\lambda_{j}>1
for some
j
.
It then follows from
\sum_{i=0}^{d}\lambda_{i}=r
,
\lambda_{i}>0
for all
i\neq j
and
\lambda_{j}-1>0
that

{\mathbf{v}}:={\mathbf{p}}-{\mathbf{v}}_{j}=\sum_{i\neq j}\lambda_{i}{\mathbf{v}}_{i}+(\lambda_{j}-1){\mathbf{v}}_{j}

belongs to
{\rm int}((r-1)\Delta)\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
.
This implies that
r={\rm codeg}(\Delta)\leq r-1
, a contradiction.
Hence one has
0<\lambda_{i}\leq 1
for all
i
.

For each
0\leq i\leq d
, set

{\mathbf{a}}_{i}:=({\mathbf{v}}_{i},1)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d+1},

and consider the cone

\mathcal{C}_{\Delta}:={\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}{\mathbf{a}}_{0}+\cdots+{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}{\mathbf{a}}_{d}.

Since
\Delta
is a simplex, the vectors
{\mathbf{a}}_{0},\dots,{\mathbf{a}}_{d}
are linearly independent.
Let

{\mathbf{c}}:=({\mathbf{p}},r)=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\lambda_{i}{\mathbf{a}}_{i}.

Since
\Delta
is Gorenstein of codegree
r
, the cone
\mathcal{C}_{\Delta}
is Gorenstein with canonical lattice point
{\mathbf{c}}
. Namely, one has

\operatorname{int}(\mathcal{C}_{\Delta})\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{d+1}={\mathbf{c}}+\bigl(\mathcal{C}_{\Delta}\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{d+1}\bigr).

Assume that
\lambda_{j}=1
for some
j
.
Then

{\mathbf{u}}:={\mathbf{c}}-{\mathbf{a}}_{j}=\sum_{i\neq j}\lambda_{i}{\mathbf{a}}_{i}

belongs to
\mathcal{C}_{\Delta}\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{d+1}
.

Suppose, to the contrary, that there exists

{\mathbf{y}}=(y_{0},\dots,y_{d})\in\Lambda_{\Delta}

with
y_{j}>0
for some
j
.
Set

{\mathbf{m}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}:=\sum_{i=0}^{d}y_{i}{\mathbf{a}}_{i}.

Since
{\mathbf{y}}\in\Lambda_{\Delta}
, one has

{\mathbf{m}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d+1}.

Moreover,
0\leq y_{i}<1
for all
i
.
Set

{\mathbf{u}}+{\mathbf{m}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}=\sum_{i\neq j}(\lambda_{i}+y_{i}){\mathbf{a}}_{i}+y_{j}{\mathbf{a}}_{j}.

Since
\lambda_{i}+y_{i}\geq\lambda_{i}>0
for all
i\neq j
and
y_{j}>0
, one has

{\mathbf{u}}+{\mathbf{m}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}\in\operatorname{int}(\mathcal{C}_{\Delta})\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{d+1}.

By the Gorenstein property of
\mathcal{C}_{\Delta}
, there exists

{\mathbf{m}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{\Delta}\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{d+1}

such that

{\mathbf{u}}+{\mathbf{m}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}={\mathbf{c}}+{\mathbf{m}}^{\prime}.

Since
{\mathbf{u}}={\mathbf{c}}-{\mathbf{a}}_{j}
, this implies

{\mathbf{m}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}-{\mathbf{a}}_{j}={\mathbf{m}}^{\prime}.

Therefore

{\mathbf{m}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}-{\mathbf{a}}_{j}\in\mathcal{C}_{\Delta}
.

On the other hand,

{\mathbf{m}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}-{\mathbf{a}}_{j}=\sum_{i\neq j}y_{i}{\mathbf{a}}_{i}+(y_{j}-1){\mathbf{a}}_{j}.

Since
0<y_{j}<1
,
y_{j}-1<0
.
Because
{\mathbf{a}}_{0},\dots,{\mathbf{a}}_{d}
are linearly independent, an element of
\mathcal{C}_{\Delta}
has a unique expression as a nonnegative linear combination of
{\mathbf{a}}_{0},\dots,{\mathbf{a}}_{d}
.
Thus
{\mathbf{m}}_{{\mathbf{y}}}-{\mathbf{a}}_{j}\notin\mathcal{C}_{\Delta}
, a contradiction.

Hence
y_{j}=0
for every
{\mathbf{y}}\in\Lambda_{\Delta}
.
Therefore,
\Delta
is a lattice pyramid.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\Delta=\operatorname{conv}({\mathbf{v}}_{0},\ldots,{\mathbf{v}}_{d})
be a Gorenstein simplex of dimension
d
and degree
s
which is not a lattice pyramid.
Let

{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{0},\dots,x_{d})\in\Lambda_{\Delta}

be the unique element of
\Lambda_{\Delta}
with

{\rm ht}({\mathbf{x}})=s
.
Then one has
x_{i}>0
for all
i
.

Proof.

Set
r:={\rm codeg}(\Delta)=d+1-s
and let

{\mathbf{p}}=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\lambda_{i}{\mathbf{v}}_{i}

be the unique interior lattice point of
r\Delta
, where

\lambda_{i}>0
for all
0\leq i\leq d
and
\sum_{i=0}^{d}\lambda_{i}=r
.
Since
\Delta
is not a lattice pyramid,
it then follows from Lemma
3.1
that
0<\lambda_{i}<1
for all
i
. Since
({\mathbf{p}},r)=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\lambda_{i}({\mathbf{v}}_{i},1)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d+1}
and
0<\lambda_{i}<1
for all
i
, it follows that

\lambda:=(\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{d})\in\Lambda_{\Delta}

with
{\rm ht}(\lambda)=r
.
Set
{\mathbf{y}}=(y_{0},\ldots,y_{d}):=-\lambda\in\Lambda_{\Delta}
. Then
0<y_{i}<1
for all
i
. Since
{\rm ht}({\mathbf{y}})=d+1-{\rm ht}(\lambda)=d+1-r=s
, one has
{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{y}}
. Therefore, we obtain
x_{i}>0
for all
i
.
∎

The assumption of Gorensteinness for this lemma is essential.

Example 3.3
.

Consider the finite subgroup

\Lambda=\langle{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}}\rangle\subset[0,1)^{6}

generated by

{\mathbf{x}}=\left(\frac{3}{4},\frac{3}{4},\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2},0\right),\qquad{\mathbf{y}}=\left(0,\frac{1}{2},0,\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right).

Then
|\Lambda|=8
, and the elements of
\Lambda
are

\displaystyle(0,0,0,0,0,0),\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2},0,0,0,0\right),\left(0,\frac{1}{2},0,\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right),\left(\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2},0\right),

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{4},\frac{3}{4},\frac{1}{2},0,0,\frac{1}{2}\right),\left(\frac{1}{2},0,0,\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right),\left(\frac{3}{4},\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2},0,0,\frac{1}{2}\right),\left(\frac{3}{4},\frac{3}{4},\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2},0\right).

Hence, by Proposition
2.4
, there exists a lattice simplex
\Delta
such that
\Lambda_{\Delta}=\Lambda
. Since no coordinate is identically zero on
\Lambda
, Lemma
2.3
implies that
\Delta
is not a lattice pyramid.
Moreover, one has

h^{*}(\Delta,t)=1+t+5t^{2}+t^{3}.

In particular, the maximal height is
3
, and the unique element of height
3
is

\left(\frac{3}{4},\frac{3}{4},\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2},0\right).

Therefore, the assumption that
\Delta
is Gorenstein is essential in Lemma
3.2
.

Now, we prove Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

For each
0\leq k\leq s
, let

H_{k}:=\{{\mathbf{y}}\in\Lambda_{\Delta}:{\rm ht}({\mathbf{y}})=k\}.

Since
\Delta
is Gorenstein of degree
s
, the
h^{*}
-polynomial of
\Delta
is palindromic of degree
s
. Hence for any
0\leq k\leq s
, one has

|H_{k}|=|H_{s-k}|
.
Moreover, since the coefficient of
t^{s}
in
h^{*}(\Delta,t)
is equal to
1
, there exists a unique element

{\mathbf{x}}\in\Lambda_{\Delta}

such that

{\rm ht}({\mathbf{x}})=s
.
Since
\Delta
is not a lattice pyramid, by Lemma
3.2
, one has

0<x_{i}<1
for all
i
.
Therefore,

{\rm ht}(-{\mathbf{x}})=2s-{\rm ht}({\mathbf{x}})=s
.
Since
{\mathbf{x}}
is the unique element of height
s
, we obtain

-{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{x}}
.
Thus
2{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{0}
.
Since
0<x_{i}<1
for all
i
, it follows that

x_{i}=\frac{1}{2}
for all
i
.
Hence

{\mathbf{x}}=\left(\frac{1}{2},\dots,\frac{1}{2}\right).

Next, we claim that for any
0\leq k\leq s
,

{\mathbf{x}}+H_{k}=H_{s-k}.

We prove this by induction on
k
.
For
k=0
, since
H_{0}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
and
H_{s}=\{{\mathbf{x}}\}
, one has

{\mathbf{x}}+H_{0}=H_{s}.

Now let
1\leq k\leq s
, and assume that for any
0\leq j<k
,

{\mathbf{x}}+H_{j}=H_{s-j}.

Take

{\mathbf{y}}=(y_{0},\dots,y_{2s-1})\in H_{k}.

Set

m({\mathbf{y}}):=\left|\{\,i:y_{i}\geq 1/2\,\}\right|.

It then follows from
{\rm ht}({\mathbf{y}})=k
that

m({\mathbf{y}})\leq 2k
.
On the other hand, since

{\mathbf{x}}=\left(\frac{1}{2},\dots,\frac{1}{2}\right),

one has

{\rm ht}({\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{y}})={\rm ht}({\mathbf{x}})+{\rm ht}({\mathbf{y}})-m({\mathbf{y}})=s+k-m({\mathbf{y}}).

Therefore

{\rm ht}({\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{y}})\geq s-k.

Assume that

{\rm ht}({\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{y}})=s-j

for some
j<k
.
Then

{\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{y}}\in H_{s-j}={\mathbf{x}}+H_{j}

by the induction hypothesis.
However, since

{\mathbf{x}}+({\mathbf{x}}+H_{j})=2{\mathbf{x}}+H_{j}=H_{j},

one has

{\mathbf{y}}=2{\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{y}}={\mathbf{x}}+({\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{y}})\in{\mathbf{x}}+({\mathbf{x}}+H_{j})=H_{j},

which contradicts
{\rm ht}({\mathbf{y}})=k
.
Thus
since
{\rm ht}({\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{y}})\geq s-k
, we obtain

{\rm ht}({\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{y}})=s-k.

Hence

{\mathbf{x}}+H_{k}\subseteq H_{s-k}.

It then follows from
|{\mathbf{x}}+H_{k}|=|H_{k}|=|H_{s-k}|
that

{\mathbf{x}}+H_{k}=H_{s-k}.

Now let

{\mathbf{y}}=(y_{0},\dots,y_{2s-1})\in H_{k}.

Since
{\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{y}}\in H_{s-k}
, we have

s-k={\rm ht}({\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{y}})=s+k-m({\mathbf{y}}),

and hence

m({\mathbf{y}})=2k.

It then follows from
{\rm ht}({\mathbf{y}})=k

that all of nonzero coordinates must be equal to
1/2
and the remaining coordinates must be equal to
0
.
Therefore every element of
\Lambda_{\Delta}
has each coordinate equal to either
0
or
1/2
.

Define

C:=\{\,2{\mathbf{y}}:{\mathbf{y}}\in\Lambda_{\Delta}\,\}\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2s}.

Since
\Lambda_{\Delta}
is a finite abelian group under addition modulo
1
, the set
C
is a binary linear code.
Moreover, if
{\mathbf{y}}\in H_{k}
, then
{\mathbf{y}}
has exactly
2k
coordinates equal to
1/2
, so the corresponding codeword
2{\mathbf{y}}
has Hamming weight
2k
.
Hence every codeword of
C
has even weight, that is,
C
is an even binary linear code.
Since

{\mathbf{x}}=\left(\frac{1}{2},\dots,\frac{1}{2}\right)\in\Lambda_{\Delta},

the code
C
contains
\mathbf{1}_{2s}
. Hence
C
is self-complementary.
On the other hand, by construction,

\Lambda_{\Delta}=\left\{\frac{1}{2}{\mathbf{c}}\in[0,1)^{2s}:{\mathbf{c}}\in C\right\}.

Conversely, let
C\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2s}
be an even binary self-complementary code, and define

\Lambda:=\left\{\frac{1}{2}{\mathbf{c}}\in[0,1)^{2s}:{\mathbf{c}}\in C\right\}.

Then
\Lambda
is a finite subgroup of
[0,1)^{2s}
.
Moreover, since
C
is even, one has
{\rm ht}({\mathbf{x}})\in{\mathbb{Z}}
for each
{\mathbf{x}}\in\Lambda
. Hence
there exists a lattice simplex
\Delta
of dimension
2s-1
such that

\Lambda_{\Delta}=\Lambda.

Since
\mathbf{1}_{2s}\in C
, the group
\Lambda_{\Delta}
contains

\left(\frac{1}{2},\dots,\frac{1}{2}\right),

so
\Delta
is not a lattice pyramid.
Moreover, one has

h^{*}(\Delta,t)=\sum_{{\mathbf{c}}\in C}t^{{\rm wt}({\mathbf{c}})/2}.

Since
C
contains
\mathbf{1}_{2s}
, its degree is exactly
s
. Also, adding
\mathbf{1}_{2s}
gives a bijection between codewords of weight
2k
and codewords of weight
2s-2k
, so
h^{*}(\Delta,t)
is palindromic. Therefore
\Delta
is Gorenstein of degree
s
.
This completes the proof.
∎

From the proof of Theorem
1.1
we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
\Delta
be a
(2s-1)
-dimensional Gorenstein simplex of degree
s
which is not a lattice pyramid.
Let
C\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2s}
be an even binary self-complementary code such that the associated finite abelian group of
\Delta
is of the form

\Lambda_{\Delta}=\left\{\frac{1}{2}{\mathbf{c}}\in[0,1)^{2s}:{\mathbf{c}}\in C\right\}.

Then one has

h^{*}(\Delta,t)=\sum_{{\mathbf{c}}\in C}t^{{\rm wt}({\mathbf{c}})/2}.

4.
Classification of Gorenstein simplices of degree
3
and
4

In this section, we classify Gorenstein simplices of degree
3
and
4
in the extremal case.
We first observe a general fact: the associated finite abelian group is determined by the lower half of the height decomposition.
Let
\Delta
be a lattice simplex of dimension
d
, and
for each
0\leq i\leq s
, let

H_{i}:=\{{\mathbf{y}}\in\Lambda_{\Delta}:{\rm ht}({\mathbf{y}})=i\}.

Then from the proof of Theorem

we obtain the following.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\Delta
be a
(2s-1)
-dimensional Gorenstein simplex of degree

Then

is completely determined by

Proof.

This follows since for each
0\leq i\leq s
one has

{\mathbf{x}}+H_{i}=H_{s-i},

where

{\mathbf{x}}=\left(\frac{1}{2},\dots,\frac{1}{2}\right).

and
H_{0}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
.
∎

It is useful to describe
H_{1}
by using a graph.
For each
1\leq i<j\leq 2s
, let

\varepsilon_{ij}\in[0,1)^{2s}

be the element whose
i
-th and
j
-th coordinates are equal to
1/2
, and whose other coordinates are
0
.
Then every element of
H_{1}
is of the form
\varepsilon_{ij}
.
Hence

can be regarded as the edge set of a graph on
\{1,\ldots,2s\}
.
Define a graph

on the vertex set

by

\{i,j\}\in E(G_{\Delta})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\varepsilon_{ij}\in H_{1},

where

is the edge set of

.
We first record two closure properties of
G_{\Delta}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

The graph
G_{\Delta}
satisfies the following properties:

(1)

if
\{i,j\},\{i,k\}\in E(G_{\Delta})
with
i,j,k
pairwise distinct, then
\{j,k\}\in E(G_{\Delta})
;

(2)

if
\{i_{1},j_{1}\},\ldots,\{i_{s-1},j_{s-1}\}

.

has an

-matching, then it has an
s
-matching.

Proof.

(1) Since
\varepsilon_{ij},\varepsilon_{ik}\in H_{1}\subset\Lambda_{\Delta}
, one has

\varepsilon_{ij}+\varepsilon_{ik}=\varepsilon_{jk}\in\Lambda_{\Delta}.

Moreover,

{\rm ht}(\varepsilon_{jk})=1,

so
\varepsilon_{jk}\in H_{1}
.

(2) Since
\varepsilon_{i_{1}j_{1}},\ldots,\varepsilon_{i_{s-1}j_{s-1}}\in H_{1}\subset\Lambda_{\Delta}
, one has

{\mathbf{x}}+\varepsilon_{i_{1}j_{1}}+\cdots+\varepsilon_{i_{s-1}j_{s-1}}=\varepsilon_{pq}\in\Lambda_{\Delta},

where

\{p,q\}=\{1,\ldots,2s\}\setminus\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{s-1},j_{1},\ldots,j_{s-1}\}.

Since
{\rm ht}(\varepsilon_{pq})=1
, one has
\varepsilon_{pq}\in H_{1}
.
∎
