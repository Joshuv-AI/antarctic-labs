Effect of patient characteristics and surgical techniques on short-to-mid-term outcomes of dental implant treatment: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Mohammed AlSarhan, Abdulrahman Ibrahim Alhilal, Abdulmohsen Abdulrahman Alhizam, Manal Eidan AlKenani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This retrospective cohort study aimed to explore the relationships between patient characteristics and surgical techniques and radiographic marginal bone loss (MBL) and postoperative complications in individuals receiving dental implants. Electronic medical records (EMR) of adults (age ≥ 18 years) who received at least one dental implant between January 2015 and October 2023 and underwent radiographic evaluation at least 6 months after prosthesis insertion were reviewed. Patient-level variables included demographic, systemic, behavioral, and oral-hygiene characteristics, whereas implant-level variables included implant type, location, and grafting status. Analyses were primarily conducted at the implant level. The oral-hygiene status was retrospectively categorized based on EMR data. MBL was recorded dichotomously based on radiographic evidence of implant thread exposure. Descriptive statistics and exploratory bivariate analyses were performed using chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests, respectively. This study included 218 implants from 109 patients. Most implants were bone-level and placed in the native bone. Grafting was more frequently performed at caries-related sites and in patients with good oral hygiene. Older age was associated with poorer oral hygiene, whereas the education level and smoking status were not significantly associated with the oral-hygiene status. Radiographic MBL and postoperative complications were infrequent. Grafting status was not significantly associated with radiographic MBL. Within the limitations of this retrospective cohort study, good oral hygiene and surgical techniques were associated with favorable short-to-mid-term outcomes of implant treatment. Prospective studies with standardized follow-ups and calibrated radiographic assessments are required to clarify these relationships.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s44445-026-00200-9.

Introduction

Dental implants have become the cornerstone of contemporary dentistry and provide predictable and durable solutions for the rehabilitation of partial and complete edentulism (Oliveira et al. 2023). However, long-term implant survival depends not only on the surgical technique and implant design, but also on a multifactorial interaction involving patient-related characteristics, systemic health conditions, lifestyle behaviors, oral-hygiene practices, and adherence to supportive-care protocols (Chen and Darby 2003; Howe et al. 2019; Shumaker et al. 2009).

Patient perceptions and understanding of implant therapy may influence maintenance behaviors and clinical outcomes, highlighting the role of patient education in long-term implant care (Brunello et al. 2020). Evidence suggests that patient compliance, including peri-implant maintenance, is important for peri-implant health and implant longevity (Cortellini et al. 2019; Costa et al. 2023). In addition, advanced oral-hygiene devices, such as sonic toothbrushes, may improve plaque control and peri-implant health (Zou et al. 2024), and regular attendance at recall visits is associated with a lower incidence of peri-implant disease and improved implant survival (Cardaropoli and Gaveglio 2012; Frisch et al. 2014, 2020).

Several previous studies have separately evaluated individual risk indicators, including patient perceptions (Brunello et al. 2020), oral-hygiene practices (Zou et al. 2024), smoking status, systemic conditions, and compliance with maintenance therapy (Cardaropoli and Gaveglio 2012; Frisch et al. 2014, 2020). However, few studies have evaluated the patient-related characteristics and surgical techniques in routine clinical settings. Long-term observational studies suggest that systemic conditions, smoking status, and noncompliance with maintenance therapy may influence peri-implant outcomes. However, the reported associations vary according to the study population and methodological approach (Hu et al. 2020; Monje et al. 2016; Reis et al. 2023; Rösing et al. 2019). In addition, retrospective implant datasets frequently demonstrate variability in longitudinal follow-up, radiographic assessment methods, and documentation of surgical or prosthetic variables, which may limit the interpretation and comparability of the reported outcomes (Frisch et al. 2020; Hu et al. 2020; Monje et al. 2016).

Therefore, this retrospective cohort study aimed to explore the relationships between patient-related characteristics and surgical techniques and radiographic marginal bone loss (MBL) and postoperative complications among individuals receiving dental implants.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Department of Periodontics and Community Dentistry, College of Dentistry, King Saud university (riyadh, Saudi arabia). This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board and the College of Dentistry Research Center (approval no. E23-8163) and adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Study population

Electronic medical records of adults (age ≥ 18 years) who received at least one dental implant between January 2015 and October 2023 and underwent radiographic evaluation at least 6 months after prosthesis insertion were reviewed. The inclusion criteria were complete medical and dental records and documented implant status during follow-up. Patients with incomplete records, lack of follow-up, or implants lost due to trauma or technical complications unrelated to biological failure (e.g., accidental injury or prosthetic fracture) were excluded. Analyses were conducted primarily at the implant level; however, patient-level demographic and behavioral characteristics were evaluated descriptively.

Data collection

Patient-related variables extracted from the clinical records included age, sex, education level, systemic health status (including diabetes and hypertension), body mass index, smoking status, oral-hygiene practices, cause of tooth loss, and adherence to maintenance visits. Implant-related variables included the implant type (bone or tissue level), site characteristics (native bone or grafted sites), implant system, condition of the adjacent teeth, implant-to-tooth distance, implant-to-implant distance, and prosthetic status. Prosthesis design, loading protocols, regenerative material characteristics, and baseline ridge dimensions could not be comprehensively analyzed because relevant information was not consistently available.

Implant location was recorded using the Fédération Dentaire Internationale (FDI) system for tooth numbering, categorized as maxillary or mandibular, and further classified into anterior (incisors/canines), premolar, or molar regions. In cases where multiple teeth were documented within a single chart entry, the implant location was classified according to the first clearly identifiable FDI number.

Oral-hygiene status was classified based on patient self-reports and clinicians’ routine chart assessments and categorized as good (consistent tooth brushing with regular use of adjunctive aids), fair (irregular brushing and/or adjunctive aid use), or poor (minimal or no oral-hygiene practices).

Radiographic findings were extracted from the clinicians’ chart entries. Standard bitewing radiographs were obtained for all sites except for edentulous regions where vertical bitewings were obtained. MBL was defined as evidence of implant thread exposure without complete bone coverage on follow-up radiographs and was recorded dichotomously (yes/no).

The number of radiographic evaluations performed after prosthesis insertion was extracted to reflect the follow-up intensity. However, the exact follow-up duration in months could not be uniformly calculated because the documentation of the final follow-up varied across records. Missing data were handled descriptively, and analyses were performed using available case data without imputation owing to the retrospective design and variability in documentation completeness.

Outcome measures

The primary clinical outcomes of interest were radiographic MBL and postoperative complications. Postoperative complications were extracted from chart documentation and descriptively categorized. Reported complications included infection, bone loss around the implant, soft-tissue inflammation, gingival recession, esthetic concerns, sinus-related symptoms, and implant failure or rejection. More than one complication was observed in few implants. Patient-related characteristics and surgical techniques were evaluated as explanatory variables for outcomes.

Statistical analysis

A consecutive sampling approach was used to include all eligible patients, reflecting routine clinical practice within an institutional setting. Owing to the retrospective study design, no formal sample size calculations were performed. Instead, all available records meeting the eligibility criteria were analyzed, consistent with previous retrospective studies evaluating peri-implant outcomes (Frisch et al. 2020; Hu et al. 2020). Continuous variables (e.g., age) are summarized using means and standard deviations, whereas categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Associations between explanatory variables (e.g., oral-hygiene status, smoking status, grafting status, and implant location) and study outcomes (MBL and postoperative complications) were assessed using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when expected cell counts were less than five. Analyses were primarily conducted at the implant level. Although some patients received more than one implant, the degree of clustering was limited, because most patients received a single implant, and the maximum number of implants per patient was two. Considering the low number of adverse outcome events and limited intrapatient clustering, multilevel or generalized estimating equation modeling was unlikely to materially alter the exploratory descriptive findings. Accordingly, the results are interpreted cautiously and descriptively. All statistical tests were two-sided, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Analyses were conducted using the R software (version 4.5.1).

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 109 patients (mean age, 53.2 ± 12.3 years) were included in the study. Nearly half of the participants (48.6%) were aged ≥ 56 years, and a slight majority were female (51.4%). Most participants had completed at least a bachelor’s degree (77.1%), and 14.7% were current smokers.

Regarding oral-hygiene behaviors, 49.5% of the participants reported brushing at least twice daily, whereas 38.5% reported brushing once daily. Regular flossing was reported by 45.0% of the participants, whereas 32.1% reported no flossing. Mouthwash use was relatively infrequent, with 60.6% of participants reporting no routine use. Overall, 52.3% of the participants reported no comorbidities, whereas 47.7% had one or more systemic conditions. Thus, the cohort exhibited generally favorable health characteristics and oral-hygiene practices (Table 1).

Implant characteristics and surgical management

A total of 218 implants were placed in the 109 patients included in the analysis. Most implants were bone-level (81.7%), and tissue-level implants accounted for 18.3%. Implants were placed more frequently in the mandible than in the maxilla and in the posterior regions, particularly the molar sites, than in the anterior region. In a subset of cases, the implant location could not be definitively categorized because of multiple entries or incomplete documentation.

Grafting was performed around 44 implants (20.2%), whereas 174 implants (79.8%) were placed in the native bone. Grafting was more frequently performed at sites where the tooth loss was attributed to caries, whereas no grafting procedures were reported at sites where the tooth loss was attributed to periodontitis. Among the grafted sites, bone-level implants were the most frequently used (85.8%) (Table 2).

Association between patient factors and grafting

Exploratory analyses demonstrated statistically significant associations between the grafting status and causes of tooth loss (p < 0.001) and oral-hygiene classification (p = 0.0001). Bone grafting was more frequently performed in patients with good oral hygiene than in patients with fair or poor oral hygiene. No statistically significant associations were identified between the grafting status and smoking status (p = 0.562), implant type (p = 1.0), and condition of the adjacent teeth (p = 0.539) (Table 3).

Other associations

Additional exploratory analyses were performed to examine the relationships between demographic characteristics, oral-hygiene status, and selected implant-related variables. Older age was more frequently associated with poorer oral hygiene (c² = 17.514, p = 0.0015). Oral-hygiene status was not significantly associated with the educational level (p = 0.289) or smoking status (p = 0.574). However, these findings should be interpreted descriptively.

Postoperative outcomes: MBL and complications

Radiographic MBL during the follow-up period was diagnosed around six implants (approximately 5.5% of the evaluated implants), and no implant loss attributable to biological failure was identified in the study cohort. All included implants underwent at least one radiographic follow-up evaluation 6 months after prosthesis insertion. However, the number of radiographic evaluations after prosthesis insertion varied across implants. Because follow-up intervals were not standardized, and the exact follow-up duration could not be uniformly calculated from the retrospective records, the outcome findings should be interpreted within the context of variable follow-up intensity. Owing to the small number of MBL events, no statistically significant associations were observed between radiographic MBL and the grafting status, implant type, cause of tooth loss, or condition of the adjacent teeth. Owing to the categorical nature of MBL assessment and the limited number of adverse events, these analyses were interpreted descriptively.

Postoperative complications, including infection, radiographic bone loss around the implant, soft-tissue inflammation, gingival recession, esthetic concerns, and sinus-related symptoms, were reported in nine patients (8.3%). More than one complication was reported in few implants. However, no cases of implant rejection or loss were reported.

Discussion

This study expands the literature by concurrently evaluating patient-related characteristics and surgical techniques in a real-world retrospective cohort. Particularly, the frequencies of radiographic MBL and postoperative complications were low. Bone grafting was more frequently performed at sites where the tooth loss was attributed to caries and among patients with good oral hygiene. No statistically significant associations were identified between the grafting status and radiographically documented MBL or postoperative complications; however, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because of the retrospective design and limited number of adverse events.

The higher frequency of grafting observed among patients with good oral hygiene may reflect clinician-driven treatment selection and anticipated patient compliance, rather than a direct causal relationship. Although grafting was not significantly associated with radiographic MBL, the interpretation was limited by the small number of outcome events and absence of standardized quantitative radiographic measurements. Nevertheless, these findings are generally consistent with reports suggesting that augmentation procedures do not necessarily compromise the stability of appropriately maintained implants (Jung et al. 2008; Monje et al. 2016).

In this study, older patients more frequently exhibited poor oral hygiene, whereas the educational level and smoking status were not significantly associated with the oral-hygiene status. This is consistent with the findings in previous observational studies (Hu et al. 2020; Monje et al. 2017; Şahin 2024). Furthermore, although smoking and systemic conditions are known risk factors for peri-implant disease (Reis et al. 2023), no statistically significant association with radiographic MBL or postoperative complications was identified in this cohort. This may be attributed to the limited number of adverse events, variability in documentation, and institutional maintenance practices. In addition, objective indicators of systemic disease control, such as glycosylated hemoglobin levels, were not consistently available (Cosola et al. 2025).

The low frequency of complications observed in this study is broadly comparable to the findings in maintained institutional implant cohorts in other observational studies. However, direct comparison remains challenging because of differences in follow-up duration, radiographic assessment methods, and outcome definitions (Frisch et al. 2020; Hu et al. 2020; Monje et al. 2016). Therefore, these findings provide additional regional data on implant outcomes in an institutional setting.

This study had several limitations. First, the retrospective design limited control over important confounding variables, including baseline bone condition, prosthesis design, loading protocols, and regenerative procedures. Second, radiographic MBL was recorded dichotomously based on retrospective chart documentation rather than calibrated millimeter-based measurements, and radiographic interpretation relied on routine or vertical bitewing radiographs obtained during clinical care. Third, as the radiographs originated from routine clinical care over an extended retrospective period, complete radiographic standardization could not be ensured. Fourth, the follow-up duration could not be uniformly calculated because of the inconsistent documentation of the final clinical visits. Fifth, oral-hygiene status and maintenance adherence were retrospectively derived from clinical notes rather than validated indices, introducing potential information bias and misclassifications. Sixth, selection bias may have occurred because only patients with available follow-up documentation were included. Seventh, the study design did not allow differentiation between early physiological bone remodeling and pathological bone loss. Accordingly, the MBL outcome reflects documented radiographic findings reported during routine clinical care rather than standardized longitudinal radiographic assessment. Finally, the low number of adverse events limited the statistical power and precluded comprehensive multivariate analyses. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as exploratory and descriptive, rather than causal.

Despite these limitations, this study provided real-world insights into the interactions between patient characteristics and surgical techniques and short-to-mid-term outcomes of implant therapy in an institutional clinical cohort. Particularly, grafting procedures were not significantly associated with radiographic MBL. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because of the exploratory retrospective design, limited number of adverse events, and non-standardized follow-up and radiographic assessments. Future prospective multicenter studies with standardized follow-up protocols and quantitative radiographic assessments are needed to clarify the independent contributions of behavioral, systemic, anatomical, prosthetic, and surgical factors on peri-implant health and implant stability over time.
