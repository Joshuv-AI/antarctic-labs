Executive Summary of CRADA Work:
Long-term solar radiation measurements are important for understanding solar resource
availability and variability for systems design and deployment of cost-effective solar resource
technologies. Additionally high-quality traceable solar measurements are required to validate
satellite-based solar resource datasets that provide high-resolution long-term data covering the
US. The purpose and intent of this agreement is to collect long-term solar radiation and
meteorological measurements from the state-of-the-art facility at the University of Arizona to
meet the above needs.

 1

CRADA benefit to DOE, Participant, and US Taxpayer:
Accurate measurements of solar radiation and surface meteorological parameters are sparse
across the U.S. This CRADA provides a cost-effective method to enable collection and delivery
of high-quality solar measurements. The measurements are collected by the University of
Arizona using Government Furnished Equipment. The equipment is maintained on a regular
basis by DOE/NREL to ensure that relevant standards for collection of high-quality
measurements are met. University of Arizona maintains the station on a regular basis and ensures
that the station is operational as their own cost. The data is collected by DOE/NREL and made
publicly available through the Measurement and Instrumentation Data Center (MIDC) website.
This arrangement enables high-quality solar radiation measurements to be publicly available in a
cost-effective manner for use by industry, academia and the government. DOE/NREL benefits
from the availability of high-quality traceable measurements for use in its research. University of
Arizona benefits for its research having availability of solar measurements to conduct research in
solar energy without having to procure and calibrate equipment and distribute the data.
Summary of Research Results:
The following demonstrates the description and task result of the agreement.
Task Description:
The University of Arizona has been operating and maintaining the suite of radiometers and
associated equipment installed under CRADA agreement No. CRD-11-00434. The University
also provides NREL with access to all measured and archived data for inclusion in the NREL
Measurement and Instrumentation Data Center (MIDC) 1 (Stoffel and Andreas, 1981) which
consistently has nearly 100,000 users annually. Throughout the CRADA period, NREL has
provided technical support, including recalibration of the radiometers deployed at the University
of Arizona and routine archival of measurement data in the MIDC. NREL also conducts regular
reviews of the data to assess the accuracy and adequacy over the measurement period. The
radiometers continuously collect key solar irradiance variables, including Global Horizontal
Irradiance (GHI), Direct Normal Irradiance (DNI), and Diffuse Horizontal Irradiance (DHI).
Task Results:
The analysis presented herein covers solar irradiance data collected between 2011 and 2024. An
annual data quality assessment was conducted using NREL’s Solar Energy Research Institute
Quality Control (SERI-QC) software (Maxwell et al., 1993) to ensure the reliability and
suitability of the dataset for solar energy applications. Accurate, high-quality solar irradiance
measurements are essential, as the performance and financial viability of solar energy systems
depend heavily on the precision of such data. Measurement accuracy is influenced by various
factors, including radiometer type, calibration intervals, maintenance practices, and
environmental conditions.
To collect high-quality measurements, the University of Arizona deployed NREL-supplied Class A
radiometers (per ISO 9060:2018 (ISO 9060, 2018)), representing the highest standard of solar
radiation measurement equipment. These instruments have been continuously operated and
maintained according to best practices, ensuring consistent and traceable data quality. The dataset
includes measurements of the three primary solar irradiance components: GHI, DNI, and DHI.

SERI-QC employs a three-component quality control method based on analysis of clearness
indices derived from the measured irradiance values. The clearness indices—Kt (global), Kn
(direct), and Kd (diffuse)—are calculated by normalizing each respective irradiance component
to the extraterrestrial solar radiation at the top of the atmosphere. These indices enable the
construction of a “K-space,” which defines theoretical bounds of physically plausible irradiance
conditions. As described by Maxwell et al. (1993), certain relationships must hold true—for
example, Kt < Kn and (Kt – Kn) ≠ Kd are not physically possible, as Kd cannot be negative.
Data falling outside the defined valid regions of K-space are flagged as suspect or invalid.
Figures 1 - 14 present the results of the data quality assessment for the 2011–2024 period. The
leftmost chart displays the most severe quality flags detected among the three clearness
components (Kt, Kn, Kd) for each time interval. Flag severity is color-coded, ranging from dark
blue (low error or no issue) to red (high severity flags). The subsequent three charts illustrate
clearness indices for each irradiance component individually. These charts provide insight into
sky conditions over time, with dark indicating overcast conditions and white denoting clear
skies. Summary statistics below the plots quantify the extent and nature of data quality issues.
Overall, the assessment shows that fewer than 5% of the measurements were flagged for quality
concerns (see Table 1 missing and data quality issues). This demonstrates a consistently high
level of data integrity, affirming the suitability of the dataset for high-confidence solar energy
analysis and decision-making.
Data Quality Assessment:
Table 1 below summarizes the data quality assessment results for the solar irradiance
measurement station covering the period from 2011 to 2024. While the dataset is largely
complete, there were some periods of missing data. These gaps were primarily attributed to
equipment-related issues such as instrument malfunctions, datalogging
interruptions/communications, solar tracker failures, and maintenance-related downtimes. The
table illustrates annual summaries where 2018 demonstrated relatively higher missing data
compared to other years. All years had less than 2% missing data and in most years, the missing
data was less than 1%.
The data quality (DQ) issues column of the table reflects the percentage of data excluded from
the final analysis as a result of the quality assurance process conducted using NREL’s SERI-QC
software. This process flags data anomalies using a structured flagging system ranging from 0 to
99. A flag value of 0 denotes valid data, while 99 indicates missing data, such as from power
outages or communication failures. For this analysis, all data flagged with values from 10 to 97
were excluded, as they indicate significant quality concerns.

Specifically, flags 10 through 93 are assigned to data that fail two- or three-component
consistency tests, which are essential for verifying the internal consistency between GHI, DNI,
and DHI. These tests are designed to identify situations where the interrelation among the
components violates physical or empirical models, suggesting potential measurement or
processing errors.
Flags 94 to 97 correspond to measurements that fall within physically impossible regions in K-
space—a domain defined by clearness indices, where known physical relationships between GHI
(Kt), DNI (Kn), and DHI (Kd) must be upheld. Violations in K-space are evaluated based on the
magnitude by which Kn exceeds Kt, which should never occur under physically valid conditions.
These flags are categorized as follows:
Flag 94: Kn > Kt by 0.05–0.10
Flag 95: Kn > Kt by 0.10–0.15
Flag 96: Kn > Kt by 0.15–0.20
Flag 97: Kn > Kt by ±0.20 or more
In addition to these, data flagged with values 7 and 8—which denote measurements that fall
outside the allowable minimum and maximum thresholds for a single component—were also
excluded. Flag 7 represents values below the minimum physically plausible threshold, while 8
flags those exceeding the maximum.

In total, this filtering approach ensures that only high-quality, physically consistent solar
irradiance measurements are retained for further analysis. The excluded data—whether due to
physical implausibility, component inconsistency, or instrument errors—represents a
conservative quality control strategy to enhance the integrity and reliability of solar resource
assessments derived from this dataset. Thus, data from 2024 revealed relatively elevated data
quality (DQ) issues across the three components: GHI, DNI, and DHI. Notably, DHI data
collected between September 30 and October 8, 2024, exhibited higher DQ problems, which
were linked to a change in the radiometer model from the DHI Eppley 8-48 to the Kipp and
Zonen CMP22 model. During this time, site operators were engaged in instrument replacement
and troubleshooting efforts. Additionally, two DQ cylinder plots are available for 2024, covering
the periods from January 1 to April 18, 2024, and from April 19 to December 31, 2024. The first
plot corresponds to the time when two GHI radiometers were used before April 19, 2024, after
which the setup shifted to a single GHI pyranometer. These changes have been recorded in the
MIDC, resulting in the division of the time series for 2024. For these reasons, two SERI QC
cylinder DQ plot outputs for 2024 are presented, depicting the two distinct periods (Figures 1-2).
In 2020, DHI data also faced DQ issues from August 17 to September 21, attributed to a cable
connected to the DHI Eppley model 8-48 that had "snapped off" within the fitting, disrupting the
connection to the datalogger. Furthermore, for most of September 2018, the station was unable to
collect data due to initial power issues followed by extensive troubleshooting efforts required to
restore the station to operational status.

Further results and discussion:
The following figures present extended results derived from the evaluation of the dataset using
an independent data quality assessment methodology. Specifically, the quality control procedures
were implemented using the Quality Control Radiation (QCRad) algorithm, developed by (Long
and Shi, 2008), which has been widely adopted in the atmospheric and solar energy research
community for ensuring the integrity of radiation measurements. The QCRad methodology
applies a multi-tiered approach to identify and flag erroneous or suspicious radiation values
based on physical, empirical, and climatological thresholds. It examines parameters such as GHI,
DNI, DHI, and their interrelations, including constraints based on the solar zenith angle and the
maximum expected extraterrestrial radiation.
The figures included in this section offer supplementary validation of the dataset that had
previously undergone quality control through the SERIQC procedure. While the SERQC process
addresses a broad range of error sources, including instrumental malfunctions, data spikes, and
climatological inconsistencies, the QCRad checks serve to further supplement and check the
reliability of the measurements.
A comparison of the QCRad-filtered dataset with the SERIQC-assessed dataset reveals generally
strong agreement; however, several discrepancies are noted. Specifically, some data points fall
outside of the QCRad-defined acceptable bounds, suggesting possible residual errors or natural
anomalies not captured by SERQC. These outliers may result from transient atmospheric events,
such as cloud edge effects, snow reflection, or instrumental limitations under low-light
conditions.

 13

It is important to note that a significant portion of these deviations occur during periods of low
solar irradiance, typically during early morning, late evening, or overcast conditions. From a
solar energy application perspective, such instances are generally considered to have a negligible
impact on system performance due to the inherently low power output during these times.
In summary, the additional QCRad quality checks enhance confidence in the dataset’s suitability
for use in solar resource assessment and modeling. While minor inconsistencies persist, their
frequency and magnitude are within acceptable thresholds, particularly when considering the
broader uncertainties inherent in ground-based radiation measurements. The integration of
multiple independent quality control protocols—SERQC and QCRad—provides a robust
framework for ensuring high data fidelity.
