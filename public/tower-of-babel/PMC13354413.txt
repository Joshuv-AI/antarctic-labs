Surgical management of acute cholangitis associated with a spontaneous biliodigestive fistula after failed endoscopic treatment: a rare case report and review of the literature
Authors: Ahmed Vadel Jidou, Ayoub Errabhi, Mohammed Hassani, Tarik Deflaoui, Anas Derkaoui, Abdelali Guellil, Rachid Jabi, Mohammed Bouziane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Choledochoduodenal fistula (CDF) is a rare but important cause of cholangitis, involving an abnormal connection between the common bile duct and the duodenum. It presents with variable clinical symptoms and can complicate management.Case presentation:We report a rare case of acute cholangitis in a 65-year-old patient with a spontaneous CDF. The patient presented with abdominal pain, febrile jaundice, and signs of cholangitis. Computed tomography (CT) imaging revealed aerobilia and hyperdense material in the common bile duct, suggestive of a CDF, which was confirmed by biliary magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). After a failed endoscopic drainage, the patient underwent a successful Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy.Clinical discussion:CDFs are most commonly caused by bile duct inflammation due to gallstones, though other etiologies include duodenal ulcers, pancreatic tumors, and adjacent organ inflammation. Imaging, especially CT and cholangio-MRI, is key to diagnosis. Management ranges from conservative therapy to surgical intervention, depending on the clinical context.Conclusion:CDF should be considered in patients with cholangitis or pancreatitis with unexplained aerobilia. Diagnosis relies heavily on imaging, and treatment – though not standardized – may require surgical management when endoscopic methods fail.

Introduction

Choledochoduodenal fistula (CDF) is a rare condition, observed in only 3.5% of cases of biliary-digestive fistulas[1]. The main cause of CDF is inflammation of the bile duct secondary to gallstones. Minor causes include duodenal ulcers, pancreatic neoplasms, and inflammation of neighboring organs[2].

A CDF, a specific type of biliary-digestive fistula, is an abnormal connection between the common hepatic duct and the duodenum. It is secondary to cholelithiasis (gallstones or stones in the common bile duct) and biliary stenosis. Case presentation

A 65-year-old patient with no previous medical history presented with right hypochondrial pain, a temperature of 40°C, and jaundice. Biological testing revealed an inflammatory syndrome (C-reactive protein 130 mg/L) and cholestasis. An abdominal computed tomography (CT) scan revealed dilatation of the main bile duct to 3 cm, as well as dilatation of the intrahepatic bile ducts upstream of dense contents. A fistula was also identified between segment I of the duodenum and the main bile duct (Fig. 1). A biliary MRI revealed a fistulous course between the duodenal bulb and the main bile duct (Fig. 2). The intrahepatic and main bile ducts were dilated upstream of the alimentary contents in the lower bile duct. Aerobilia and suppurative liver lesions were observed. Discussion

CDF was first described in 1976, with a high prevalence ranging from 2.5 to 15.7% after choledochoduodenal anastomosis[5,6], following the observation of debris accumulations near the papilla in the distal reservoir of the bile duct in affected patients[7,8]. In such a fistula, bile no longer flows through the distal bile duct. The distal part of the common bile duct, located downstream of the choledochoduodenal anastomosis, then becomes a poorly drained reservoir prone to debris accumulation[9]. The main cause of duodenobiliary fistulas is inflammation of the bile duct secondary to gallstones; minor causes include duodenal ulcer, pancreatic neoplasms, and inflammation of neighboring organs[2].

Bilioenteric fistulas can be classified according to the specific parts of the biliary system and intestinal tract that communicate with each other. Stagnitti et al reported that cholecystoduodenal fistulas were the most common subtype (55 out of 81 cases, or 68%), while Zong et al reported that CDFs were the most common subtype[10]. The most common symptoms of non-obstructive biliary-enteric fistulas are epigastric pain (80.91%), cholangitis (54.26%), jaundice (50.69%), fever (50.69%), nausea and/or vomiting (10.3%), abdominal distension (0.39%), asymptomatic presentation (0.11%), and diarrhea (0.11%)[11]. Tanaka et al were the first to present a larger series of cases in Japan, involving 83 individuals, and proposed a classification system based on the location of the CDF in relation to the papilla[12]. Ikeda and Okada classified CDFs into two types from an etiological point of view. Type I refers to fistulas located on the papilla, and Type II refers to those located immediately on or near its papillary edge[12,13].

Imaging methods provide the most useful data for detecting the presence of air in the bile ducts. The CT scan shows debris and stones in the distal bile duct, as well as enhancement of the duct walls, resulting from suppurative cholangitis or an adjacent stone[14]. Barium reflux into the biliary tree, during contrast studies or endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP), also allows the fistulous tract and its location to be identified[13,15]. Cholangiopancreato-MRI is an interesting technique that is less invasive than ERCP[16]. In our patient, the CDF was suggested by the CT scan (Fig. 1), which revealed diffuse aerobilia with spontaneously hyperdense material in the common bile duct, and then confirmed by biliary MRI (Fig. 2).

Possible treatments for this condition include conservative drug therapy, endoscopic sphincterotomy, and surgery, with Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy[17]. The choice of treatment is based on the etiology, severity of the disease, and the patient’s general condition. Recurrent biliary tract infections and biliary ileus have been reported as definitive indications for surgical treatment[15]. The fistula presented a high risk of obstructive stenosis due to its length and avascular nature. Therefore, in our case, surgical treatment was decided upon to avoid these complications and any need for urgent surgery.

Conclusion

It is important to consider a CDF diagnosis in patients with cholangitis or pancreatitis, particularly in those from developing countries. Diagnosing CDF is challenging, as little is known about the condition and its complications. Diagnosis primarily relies on radiological imaging. Currently, there is no standardized management; treatment typically involves endoscopic drainage, with surgery considered when necessary to prevent unpredictable complications.
