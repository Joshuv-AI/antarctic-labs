Dynamic monitoring of railway bridges via coupling digital twins with deep reinforcement learning
Authors: Xin Xu, Guangmei He, Chen Liu, Xiaofeng Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Railway-bridge anomaly detection during retrofitting is a core structural health monitoring (SHM) task, requiring timely alarms for safety. It is challenging because responses are strongly nonstationary and distorted by environmental and operational variability, while sensing budgets constrain what can be observed. Classical feature-and-threshold pipelines are interpretable but fragile to operating-condition drift, yielding unstable alarms without compensation. Deep time-series detectors are effective, but data-only scores are hard to calibrate into risk cues and usually assume fixed sensing. Digital twin (DT) approaches provide uncertainty-aware belief tracking, yet twin-only alarming may respond slowly and may ignore sequential accuracy–cost optimization. We propose Residual Digital-Twin Monitoring with Branching Dueling Q-learning (RDTM-BDQ), coupling an event-triggered Bayesian DT, a mask-aware residual Mamba encoder, and a Branching Dueling Double Deep Q-Network (BDQ) policy. Hybrid evidence fuses DT innovation energy and a normalized safety-margin index with an inverse-projection inconsistency score from the Mamba embedding, forming a belief-augmented state representation for joint alarm and sensing decisions. Training is off-policy with prioritized replay, using a reward that penalizes delay, false alarms, misses, and sensing cost, plus a consistency loss for the inverse projection head. Experiments on the public KW51 and Z24 datasets demonstrate the effectiveness of RDTM-BDQ: on KW51 it achieves 0.95 F1-score (F1) and 0.98 precision, and on Z24 it reaches 0.92 F1-score, confirming robustness to both retrofit-induced and environmentally-induced nonstationarity. Operating-point analysis on KW51 further shows an explicit accuracy–burden–cost trade-off, e.g., reducing detection delay from 41.4 h to 3.9 h with controllable monitoring cost. Ablations confirm that DT cues, residual Mamba encoding, and branching Q-learning each improve accuracy and stability.

Introduction

Railway bridges are safety-critical assets that are continually subjected to repetitive moving loads, temperature–humidity cycles, and operational variability. During retrofitting or construction interventions, structural responses become strongly nonstationary: stiffness may change gradually or abruptly, train operating patterns can shift, and environmental effects may dominate or mask subtle damage-sensitive features. These factors make reliable anomaly detection and timely alarming difficult, especially when sensing budgets are limited and continuous operation is required. Moreover, practical SHM systems must balance early warning, false alarms, and monitoring cost1.

A large body of classical SHM research relies on physics-inspired features combined with statistical pattern recognition, control charts, or fixed-threshold decision rules. Although interpretable, such pipelines are highly sensitive to environmental variations and typically require frequent explicit compensation models; otherwise, operating-condition drift leads to unstable decision boundaries and a significant increase in false alarms1. This issue is particularly pronounced during retrofitting, when the “normal” state itself evolves, making a single global baseline or static threshold fundamentally mismatched to the task.

In recent years, deep learning has advanced vibration-based representation learning for SHM, including CNNs, RNNs, attention encoders, and autoencoders. Surveys and benchmarks indicate that these methods can improve sensitivity under complex dynamics, yet several challenges remain in practical bridge monitoring: (i) robustness under domain shift induced by environment, train types, and sensor reconfiguration is not guaranteed; (ii) purely data-driven scores are difficult to interpret and calibrate into reportable risk indicators; and (iii) long sequences and irregular monitoring protocols impose computational and deployment costs2. Meanwhile, state-space sequence models such as Mamba provide a more efficient alternative to attention for long horizons; however, without physically meaningful semantics and decision logic, their operational utility in safety monitoring remains limited3. In addition, digital twins for civil infrastructure emphasize the value of physics-informed state estimation for uncertainty-aware diagnosis and decision support, yet alarming based solely on a digital twin may be slow to respond or insufficiently sensitive to subtle nonstationary anomalies4.

Monitoring is inherently a sequential decision-making problem, but most SHM anomaly detectors assume a fixed sensing plan rather than explicitly optimizing detection performance, false alarms, and monitoring cost under multi-discrete actions. Deep reinforcement learning provides a natural framework for this purpose, but it must be designed to preserve interpretability and stability5–8.

In summary, adaptive railway bridge monitoring must simultaneously achieve robustness to nonstationarity and decision optimization under sensing-cost constraints. Related work

Classical SHM and statistical anomaly detection

Railway bridge monitoring has traditionally relied on vibration- and modal-feature pipelines: low-dimensional indicators are extracted via operational modal analysis (OMA) and system identification, and then tracked using statistical change detection or pattern-recognition frameworks9. This line of work commonly performs novelty detection and hypothesis testing with handcrafted features and fixed thresholds10. A central practical issue is that environmental variability causes threshold drift and reduces transferability across time1. Although model updating and Bayesian formulations can improve interpretability and uncertainty quantification, most classical pipelines still assume a largely passive sensing configuration and do not explicitly optimize monitoring actions under resource constraints.

Deep sequential models for vibration-based anomaly detection

Deep learning has been widely used to learn damage-sensitive representations directly from raw or lightly processed signals, including CNN-based feature extractors for vibration streams11 and recurrent models that capture nonstationary temporal dependence12. For railway-specific scenarios, drive-by sensing pipelines combining continuous wavelet transforms with sparse autoencoders have also been shown effective for steel truss railway bridge damage detection13, illustrating the trend of coupling structured signal processing with deep representation learning in this domain. More recently, Transformer attention has been adopted to model long-range dependencies, and specialized time-series anomaly detectors have been proposed to distinguish normal correlations from anomalous deviations14–16. Complementary to purely data-driven detectors, recent work has examined how to design likelihood functions in particle-filter pipelines that explicitly account for nuisance components, providing a principled way to combine sequential representation learning with physical state estimation and uncertainty-aware monitoring17. However, many deep SHM detectors remain largely data-driven, require careful recalibration under distribution shift, and typically assume a fixed sensing plan, which is misaligned with retrofit-period practice where monitoring configurations are actively adjusted. In addition, Transformer-style attention can be computationally expensive for long sequences, motivating linear-time selective state-space models (SSMs) such as Mamba3. These studies suggest that a practical monitoring framework should jointly address nonstationary sequential representation learning, explicit uncertainty modeling, and cost awareness.

Digital twins and decision-making for adaptive monitoring

Digital twins (DTs) have been adopted in civil SHM to couple interpretable physics-based models with data streams, enabling online parameter inference and uncertainty-aware diagnosis; recent studies also emphasize edge intelligence to support continuous bridge monitoring under operational constraints18. Adaptive monitoring can be naturally formulated as sequential decision-making, where an agent must trade off detection timeliness, false alarms, and sensing cost. Deep reinforcement learning (DRL), particularly DQN-style value learning19, provides scalable tools for such problems, while Double DQN, dueling architectures, and prioritized experience replay improve stability and sample efficiency6–8. For multi-discrete monitoring actions, branching architectures can factorize action dimensions while sharing a common state encoder5. Nevertheless, many DRL-for-monitoring approaches lack a physics-informed belief state, or violate causality by letting actions influence contemporaneous observations. Moreover, graded alarming and interpretable risk reporting are often not incorporated into the learning objective and outputs. Compared with bridge-specific monitoring pipelines such as drive-by SHM13 and DT-assisted damage detection on long-term concrete-bridge benchmarks18, which respectively emphasize specialized sensing modalities or static physical updating without action optimization, the framework proposed here is necessary because it jointly addresses (a) explicit cost-aware sensing actions on the same physical bridge, (b) physics-informed uncertainty propagation via an event-triggered EKF, and (c) graded alarming aligned with safety reporting practice, none of which are simultaneously supported by existing railway-bridge monitoring methods.

Motivated by these gaps, RDTM-BDQ focuses on adaptive, dynamic monitoring for railway bridges by: (i) using a physics-informed Bayesian DT as a belief-state estimator and risk cue; (ii) learning nonstationary residual embeddings with a linear-time selective SSM (Mamba); and (iii) optimizing graded alarming and next-segment sensing plans via a branching dueling Double DQN with prioritized experience replay under a causality-correct observe-then-act protocol.

and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a^{\textrm{cfg}}_{k-1}=(f_{k-1},b_{k-1},q_{k-1})$$\end{document} is the sensing configuration applied to acquire segment k, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_{k-1}$$\end{document} the sampling level, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b_{k-1}$$\end{document} the sensor-group activation vector, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q_{k-1}$$\end{document} the re-inspection/verification indicator.

Policy learning via branching dueling BDQ

We employ a Branching Dueling Double DQN (BDQ) to handle the multi-discrete action space:21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} Q_{\Theta }(s,\textbf{a})=\frac{1}{B}\sum _{b=1}^{B} Q_{\Theta }^{(b)}(s,a^{(b)}). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where B=G+3 represents branches for sampling f, sensor groups b (size G), verification q, and alarm ℓ.

Table 8 summarizes the action branches, their semantics, and their cardinalities used in this work. With G=4 sensor groups on KW51 and G=3 on Z24, the joint multi-discrete action space contains \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|\mathcal {A}|=3\cdot 2^{G}\cdot 2\cdot 3$$\end{document} combinations (288 for KW51 and 144 for Z24), which the BDQ factorizes into \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B=G{+}3$$\end{document} independent value heads to mitigate the curse of dimensionality.

The end-to-end training loop is summarized in Algorithm 1. The reward weights \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\omega _d,\omega _{fp},\omega _{fn},\omega _c)$$\end{document} were not tuned by grid search but were derived from a safety-priority heuristic: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\omega _{fn}$$\end{document} is set ten times larger than \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\omega _{fp}$$\end{document} to reflect the asymmetric cost of missed detection in railway operation, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\omega _d$$\end{document} is calibrated so that one hour of detection delay carries the same penalty as a single false alarm, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\omega _c$$\end{document} is set so that running at the most aggressive sensing configuration consumes at most 20% of the per-segment reward range. The chosen values \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\omega _d,\omega _{fp},\omega _{fn},\omega _c)=(1,5,50,1)$$\end{document} together with the safety-margin re-weighting \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_k$$\end{document} realize this calibration on both datasets.

Algorithm 1

RDTM-BDQ training loop (observe-then-act).

Although the segment-level BDQ decisions and the hour-level DT belief operate at different temporal scales, the observe-then-act protocol enforces causal consistency: each segment k is acted upon using only the hour-start DT prior \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta }}_{h(k)}, P^{(\theta )}_{h(k)})$$\end{document}, so policy decisions remain optimal under the partial-observability assumption inherent to mismatched update rates. Delayed rewards \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta _k$$\end{document} are absorbed by the discount factor γ, and the within-hour belief lag introduces a bounded staleness that the safety-margin index \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho _{h(k)}$$\end{document} already accounts for, preventing systematic over-confidence between DT updates.

Reward design and monitoring cost

We define the predicted alarm \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\eta }_k=\mathbb {I}[\ell _k>0]$$\end{document} and delay indicator \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta _k=\mathbb {I}[\eta _k=1\wedge u_k=0\wedge \hat{\eta }_k=0]$$\end{document}. The sensing cost is:22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} C(a^{\textrm{cfg}}_k)=c_f\frac{f_k}{2}+\sum _{g=1}^{G}c_g b_k^{(g)}+c_q q_k. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}A risk-weighted miss penalty incorporates the DT safety margin:23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} w_k = 1+\alpha _\rho \cdot \sigma (\rho _{thr}-\rho _{h(k)}),\qquad \sigma (x)=\frac{1}{1+\exp (-x)}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The total reward \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_k$$\end{document} balances timeliness, accuracy, and cost:24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} r_k= -\omega _d\Delta _k -\omega _{fp}\mathbb {I}[\hat{\eta }_k=1,\ \eta _k=0] -w_k\,\omega _{fn}\mathbb {I}[\hat{\eta }_k=0,\ \eta _k=1] -\omega _c C(a^{\textrm{cfg}}_k). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Loss functions and training objective

We jointly optimize the BDQ value network and the inverse-projection head. The BDQ minimizes the temporal-difference (TD) loss across all branches:25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {L}_{\textrm{BDQ}} = \mathbb {E}\left[ \frac{1}{B}\sum _{b=1}^{B} \Big ( y_k- Q_{\Theta }^{(b)}(s_k,a_k^{(b)}) \Big )^2 \right] , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_k = r_k + \gamma \,\max _{\textbf{a}} Q_{\Theta ^-}(s_{k+1},\textbf{a})$$\end{document} is the target value.

The inverse projection head \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varphi (z_k)$$\end{document} is regularized to maintain consistency with the DT estimate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta }}_{h(k)}$$\end{document}:26\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {L}_{\varphi } = \mathbb {E}\left[ \left\Vert \varphi (z_k)-\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta }}_{h(k)} \right\Vert ^2_{S^{-1}} \right] . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The total objective combines decision quality and physical consistency:27\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {L}_{total}=\mathcal {L}_{\textrm{BDQ}}+\lambda _{\theta }\mathcal {L}_{\varphi }. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Experiment

Datasets

Our experimental evaluation employs two real-world structural health monitoring benchmarks: the KW51 dataset and the Z24 Bridge dataset. The KW51 dataset captures the synchronized multivariate vibration response of a bowstring railway bridge in Leuven, Belgium, during a comprehensive retrofitting campaign. We adhere to a practical monitoring protocol where each segment constitutes a decision step (J=8 steps/day) in strict chronological order, defining the proxy positive phase \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta _k=\mathbb {I}[k\in [k_s,k_e]]$$\end{document} based on the known retrofit window \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[k_s, k_e]$$\end{document}.

To assess generalization under different dynamics and environmental conditions, we also utilize the Z24 Bridge benchmark, which consists of long-term acceleration data from a post-tensioned concrete box girder bridge in Switzerland. This dataset includes a nearly year-long healthy baseline subject to strong environmental variability, followed by a progressive damage test period (including pier settlement) which we label as the positive class (η =1). For both datasets, we use the initial healthy period for digital twin calibration and representation learning, applying a sliding window of length L=256 to process the subsequent test phases.

Experimental setup

We model monitoring as a sequential decision process where the input \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_k \in \mathbb {R}^{d_y+d_x}$$\end{document} concatenates the modal-feature residual \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{r}_k$$\end{document} and the previous sensor mask \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m_{k-1}$$\end{document}. A sliding window of length L=256 is standardized using pre-retrofit statistics (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {T}_{pre}$$\end{document}) to form the state. The framework utilizes a Mamba-based encoder (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{\textrm{blk}}=4$$\end{document} blocks, latent dimension \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_z=128$$\end{document}, hidden dimension \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_h=256$$\end{document}, dropout 0.1) and is trained via Branching Dueling Double DQN (BDQ) with Prioritized Experience Replay (buffer size \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2\times 10^5$$\end{document}, batch size 128, discount factor γ =0.99). We employ the Adam optimizer with learning rates of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1\times 10^{-4}$$\end{document} for the policy and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$3\times 10^{-4}$$\end{document} for the inverse projection head, updating target networks every 2000 steps. The training objective balances decision quality and physical consistency (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _\theta =0.1$$\end{document}), while the reward function heavily penalizes missed detections (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\omega _{fn}=50$$\end{document}) and false alarms (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\omega _{fp}=5$$\end{document}) relative to sensing costs (verification \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c_q=2.0$$\end{document}, group \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c_g=0.5$$\end{document}, sampling \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c_f=0.2$$\end{document}).

Evaluation metrics

We evaluate the framework as an online decision-making system, prioritizing the balance between detection reliability and operational burden. To quantify detection performance on the chronological test split, we calculate segment-level Precision (P), Recall (R), and F1-score:28\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{P}=\frac{\textrm{TP}}{\textrm{TP}+\textrm{FP}},\quad \textrm{R}=\frac{\textrm{TP}}{\textrm{TP}+\textrm{FN}},\quad \textrm{F1}=\frac{2\textrm{PR}}{\textrm{P}+\textrm{R}}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}We treat F1 as the primary metric to ensure both safety (avoiding missed anomalies) and viability (minimizing alarm fatigue). Beyond accuracy, operational efficiency is assessed via the false-alarm rate per day (FA/day) on normal segments to quantify alarm fatigue, and the detection delay–defined as the time interval from the anomaly onset \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_{s}$$\end{document} to the first triggered alarm–to measure timeliness. Finally, we report the average daily sensing cost and total alarm rate to evaluate the economic trade-off of the adaptive strategy. All reported metrics are averaged over five independent runs.

Baselines

To demonstrate the effectiveness of RDTM-BDQ, we compare it with 11 representative baselines from anomaly detection and deep learning-based structural health monitoring (SHM), covering reconstruction-, forecasting-, graph-, and Transformer/SSM-based approaches: LSTM-AE20, DAGMM21, MAD-GAN22, USAD23, MTAD-GAT24, GDN25, TranAD26, Anomaly Transformer27, TimesNet28, DLinear29, and Mamba3. All baselines are trained and evaluated under the same chronological split and segment-level protocol for fair comparison.

The central role of the Digital Twin is evidenced by the substantial performance drop observed when removing the DT module (F1 decreases from 0.95 to 0.84). Without the physics-based innovation residuals and belief updates, the model struggles to differentiate between benign environmental variability and genuine structural anomalies, leading to a marked decrease in precision (0.78). Similarly, excluding the hybrid evidence fusion or the inverse-projection regularizer results in suboptimal performance, confirming that constraining latent representations with physical consistency is crucial for generalization in data-scarce retrofit scenarios.

Regarding the network architecture, replacing the Mamba encoder with a standard Transformer leads to a contraction in F1 score (0.89). This suggests that the selective state-space mechanism of Mamba is more effective at capturing the long-horizon temporal dependencies required for residual analysis than traditional attention mechanisms in this context. Furthermore, simplifying the policy learning by substituting Branching Dueling Q-Network (BDQ) with a standard single-head DQN degrades the F1 score to 0.87, underscoring the necessity of factorizing the action space to handle the combinatorial complexity of multi-sensor configuration efficiently.

Finally, the most profound performance degradation occurs when the adaptive sensing capability is disabled (Fixed sensing), yielding the lowest F1 score of 0.76. This drastic drop empirically validates the core premise of this study: static monitoring protocols are fundamentally ill-suited for the dynamic retrofit process. The ability to dynamically allocate sensing resources–increasing fidelity during critical operations and conserving resources otherwise–is not merely an efficiency feature but a prerequisite for reliable detection. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the superior performance of RDTM-BDQ stems from the synergistic integration of physics-guided reasoning, efficient sequence modeling, and adaptive decision-making.

Conclusion

Railway bridge anomaly detection under dynamic monitoring is critical for safety assurance during retrofit and long-term operation, where reliable alarms must be generated from multichannel vibration and strain measurements under practical sensing and communication constraints. This task remains difficult because structural responses are strongly nonstationary during interventions and are further confounded by environmental and operational variability, making alarm boundaries drift over time. In this paper, we proposed RDTM-BDQ to address these challenges by coupling physics-informed belief tracking with efficient long-horizon evidence extraction and cost-aware decision making. Specifically, RDTM-BDQ integrates an event-triggered Bayesian digital twin for uncertainty-aware stiffness-parameter inference and normalized safety-margin cues, a mask-aware residual Mamba encoder with inverse projection for hybrid risk evidence construction, and a branching dueling Double DQN policy that outputs graded alarms \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ell _k$$\end{document} and next-step sensing configurations \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_k^{\textrm{cfg}}=(f_k,b_k,q_k)$$\end{document} under an observe-then-act protocol. Experiments on the public KW51 and Z24 datasets validated the effectiveness of RDTM-BDQ, showing improved detection performance over representative reconstruction, graph-based, and modern sequence-model baselines, including a strong Mamba encoder baseline. Beyond F1, operating-point analysis on KW51 quantified alarm burden and detection timeliness under explicit sensing-cost constraints: by varying \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\omega _c$$\end{document}, RDTM-BDQ provides a tunable trade-off spanning fast detection (3.9 h delay with low FA/day) to low-cost passive monitoring (1.9 cost/day) at the expense of slower detection. The empirical results also indicated a favorable accuracy–cost trade-off enabled by adaptive sensing, rather than relying on a fixed monitoring configuration. Ablation studies further verified that the digital-twin evidence, residual sequence encoder, and branching Q-learning policy each contribute to both effectiveness and stability, while maintaining efficient long-context processing. Multi-task extensions that jointly predict anomaly severity and remaining safety margin across additional bridge datasets fall outside the present submission and are explicitly listed as a scope boundary.

Several limitations also constrain the present scope. First, the two benchmarks adopted here cover only a bowstring steel railway bridge and a post-tensioned concrete box-girder bridge; the framework’s scaling behavior on long-span cable-stayed bridges, truss bridges, or non-railway infrastructure remains to be demonstrated and is expected to require larger SRF dimensionality P and richer sensor partitioning. Second, the sensing action space is currently limited to G≤ 4 groups and a discrete sampling level \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_k\in \{0,1,2\}$$\end{document}; deployments with denser sensor networks (e.g., hundreds of accelerometers and strain gauges) will demand hierarchical or factorized action heads to keep the BDQ tractable. Third, the DT is calibrated offline on the initial healthy period and updated by an event-triggered EKF; bridges that undergo continuous retrofitting or that lack a sufficiently long healthy baseline may require online re-calibration of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$J_\theta$$\end{document} and richer process-noise models.
