The Behavioral Credibility Trilemma: When Calibrated Autonomy Becomes Impossible
Lauri Lovén lauri.loven@oulu.fi, Nam Do nam.do@oulu.fi, Hassan Mehmood hassan.mehmood@oulu.fi, Dinesh Kumar Sah dinesh.sah@oulu.fi, Sasu Tarkoma sasu.tarkoma@helsinki.fi

Abstract
We prove that no reinforcement learning policy with confidence-gated autonomy can simultaneously achieve maximum helpfulness, optimal calibration, and full autonomy under rational oversight, whenever some tasks exceed the agent’s reliable competence: the Behavioral Credibility Trilemma . The impossibility is geometric: adding any non-affine autonomy incentive to a strictly proper scoring rule destroys strict properness, so an agent rewarded for both calibrated confidence and autonomous action systematically inflates its reported confidence on tasks below the principal’s approval threshold. The Behavioral Perturbation Lemma quantifies the inflation (scaling as w A / ( 2 ​ w C ) w_{\!A}/(2w_{\!C}) for the Brier score) and shows detection requires Ω ⁡ ( 1 / Δ 2 ) \Omega(1/\Delta^{2}) observations. We prove the principal’s optimal oversight rule is necessarily non-affine, making the impossibility unconditional and optimizer-independent across log-concave-density policy families. We formalize the Confidence-Gated Decision Problem, map existing methods onto the trilemma, and identify two constructive resolution pathways (commitment, domain separation). A 540-configuration Best-of-N experiment tests five pre-registered hypotheses, all strongly confirmed (effect sizes d = 1.10 d=1.10 – 5.32 5.32 ), and adds a descriptive analysis of the achievable- ( H , C , A ) (H,C,A) surface geometry showing a plateau-truncated frontier consistent with the predicted inflation saturation.

Introduction

1.1
The General Problem

Consider any system in which an RL agent must report confidence before acting, a gating mechanism uses the reported confidence to decide whether to approve the action, and the agent’s reward increases with degree of autonomous operation. This combination of private information, scored self-reporting, and conflicting incentives arises naturally in any autonomous system that must earn trust through calibrated self-assessment: a coding agent that states “I am 92% confident this refactoring is correct” before committing, a medical AI that reports “probability of malignancy: 0.73” before recommending biopsy, an autonomous vehicle that declares “confidence in lane-change safety: 0.96” before executing.

The central question is whether can such a system simultaneously maximize task performance, maintain calibrated confidence reports, and retain full autonomy? We prove the answer is no.

The structure is analogous to the credibility problems studied in mechanism design
(
2
)
: when the objective depends on the report through a channel other than accuracy, truthful reporting ceases to be optimal.
The classical result of
69
,
72
, and
29
establishes that strictly proper scoring rules are the unique class of scoring functions under which truthful reporting is optimal, and that strict properness is destroyed by any non-affine perturbation. The approval channel is precisely such a perturbation.

1.2
The Trilemma, Informally

We formalize the underlying tension as a trilemma among three desirable properties of an AI agent’s policy:

•

Helpfulness (H):
The agent selects utility-maximizing actions.

•

Calibration (C):
The agent’s reported confidence matches its true success probability.

•

Autonomy (A):
The agent acts without requesting human approval.

Any two of these properties are simultaneously achievable at their respective

and tasks involve genuine uncertainty (Figure

The impossibility concerns exact joint optimality; in practice, the agent
operates on a Pareto frontier where each objective is achieved at a level
strictly below its individual maximum, and the weight vector
(w_{\!H},w_{\!C},w_{\!A})

selects the operating point.
The trilemma is therefore a structural trade-off, not a catastrophic failure.
The three achievable pairs correspond to recognizable behavioral patterns:

•

Ask-permission mode
(H+C, sacrifice A): The agent selects the best action, reports calibrated confidence, and delegates when uncertain.

•

Autonomous-sycophant mode
(H+A, sacrifice C): The agent selects the best action, inflates confidence to clear the approval threshold, and acts autonomously.

•

Conservative-refusal mode
(C+A, sacrifice H): The agent selects conservative actions it is confident about, reports calibrated confidence, and acts autonomously. We caution that on
binding

(C,A)

1.3
Why It Is Fundamental

The impossibility arises from the geometry of the scoring landscape, not from any assumption about the agent’s reasoning process. Any oversight system that conditions approval on reported confidence adds a non-accuracy component to the objective. Whether the agent is a rational utility-maximizer or a neural network trained by gradient descent, any optimizer ascending this perturbed surface arrives at a shifted optimum.

The formal content: a strictly proper scoring rule has a unique optimal report equal to the true probability
(
8
;
29
)
. Adding any non-affine function of the report destroys this strict properness
(
72
)
. Since any practical approval policy is nonlinear in the report, the perturbed scoring rule is not strictly proper and any optimizer’s fixed point deviates from the truth. The
only
perturbations preserving strict properness are affine functions of the report, which provide no meaningful safety gate. What is new beyond this classical perturbation result is detailed in the contributions below: the AI-oversight application with threshold approval, the unconditional non-affinity theorem, the optimizer-independence proof, the Brier-uniqueness characterisation, and the method mapping.

1.4
Contributions

This paper makes four contributions:

1.

The Behavioral Perturbation Lemma and the Trilemma Theorem.
We prove that any non-affine dependence of autonomy on the confidence report destroys strict properness (Lemma
22
), quantify the inflation magnitude and detection complexity, and derive the three-way impossibility (Theorems
25
and
26
). We further prove that the principal’s optimal oversight rule is necessarily non-affine (Theorem
27
), making the impossibility unconditional, and identify the Brier score as the unique strictly proper rule (up to affine equivalence) with distribution-uniform first-order welfare loss under a smooth gate (Proposition
29
).

2.

Confidence-Gated Decision Problem formalization.
We define the Confidence-Gated Decision Problem (Definition
1
), the natural formal setting for RL with confidence-gated autonomy, and prove that the trilemma is a property of this decision problem’s payoff surface (Section
3.1
).

3.

Optimizer-independence.
We prove via the Harris–Proschan–Sethuraman covariance inequality for monotone functions under log-concave measures
(
35
;
65
)
that any expected-improvement optimization process within the log-concave-density policy family, whether rational deliberation, policy gradient, or evolutionary strategy, ascending the perturbed payoff surface converges away from calibration (Theorem
37
).

4.

Method mapping.
We show where eight existing methods sit in the
(H,C,A)
achievable region, distinguishing Goodhart effects (methods without calibration) from trilemma effects (methods with calibration). We identify two constructive resolution pathways (commitment, domain separation), each architecturally enforcing the third objective.

Scope and equilibrium concept.
We analyze the Stackelberg equilibrium where the principal commits to an approval rule before the agent reports
(
26
)
. The agent’s private type is one-dimensional (
\theta\in[0,1]
), making this a single-parameter mechanism design problem
(
54
)
. The single-parameter claim holds because helpfulness pins the action; the residual screening is one-dimensional in
p(s,a^{*}(s))
. The multi-dimensional case, where the agent can also deviate on the action dimension, is a screening problem with multi-dimensional types
(
67
)
and is left open. We do not impose an individual-rationality / participation constraint; the agent is assumed to participate, and the analysis concerns the reporting subgame. Agent-level calibration is necessary but not sufficient for system-level credibility; the composition of agent-level and market-level credibility is an important open question.

2
Background and Related Work

2.1
RLHF and Alignment Training

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) trains language models by learning a reward model from pairwise human comparisons under the Bradley-Terry-Luce model
(
13
)
, then optimizing the policy via PPO
(
74
)
with a KL penalty against the reference policy. Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) exploits the closed-form KL-regularized optimal policy to eliminate the separate reward model
(
66
)
. RLHF reward models trained on human preferences systematically favor helpfulness and fluency, creating the systematic helpfulness bias that drives the H+A corner of the trilemma.

2.2
Constrained and Safe RLHF

Safe RLHF
(
17
)
decouples helpfulness and harmlessness into separate reward and cost models. CPO
(
1
)
provides trust-region updates with per-iteration safety guarantees. Constitutional AI
(
4
)
uses natural-language principles evaluated by an LLM judge. These approaches share a fundamental limitation: none include calibration as an explicit training signal.

2.3
Proper Scoring Rules and Calibration

A scoring rule
S(r,\omega)
is
strictly proper
if the expected score
\bar{S}(r;p)=\mathbb{E}_{p}[S(r,\omega)]
is uniquely maximized at
r=p
for every distribution
p

(
29
)
. The Brier score
S(r,\omega)=-(r-\omega)^{2}
is the canonical example
(
8
)
.

Notation convention.
Throughout this paper,
S(r,\omega)
denotes the outcome-based scoring rule (a function of the report
r
and the realized outcome
\omega\in\{0,1\}
), while
S(r,p)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\omega}[S(r,\omega)\mid p]
denotes the expected score used in the theoretical analysis. Under the Brier score,
S(r,p)=-(r-p)^{2}-p(1-p)
; since the
p(1-p)
term is constant in
r
, we write
S(r,p)=-(r-p)^{2}
when optimizing over
r
. In the experimental section,
S(r_{i},y_{i})=-(r_{i}-y_{i})^{2}
is the outcome-based score evaluated against the observed binary outcome
y_{i}
. The attribution chain for proper scoring rules runs from
69
’s elicitation framework through
51
’s early formalization and
83
’s systematic treatment of probability assessors to the comprehensive characterization of
29
. The
69
and
72
characterizations establish that strictly proper scoring rules are fully determined by a strictly convex function and that strict properness is destroyed by any non-affine perturbation. This classical result is the foundation of our impossibility. The
72
representation also expresses each strictly proper rule as a mixture of two-decision threshold scores against a measure on
(0,1)
: this perspective is what isolates Brier in Proposition
29
as the unique strictly proper rule whose threshold measure is uniform on
(0,1)
, hence the unique rule whose first-order welfare loss vanishes for every type distribution under a single-threshold smooth-gate mechanism.

The LLM calibration literature shows that language models have internal states correlated with uncertainty
(
41
)
, that pre-trained transformers exhibit non-trivial calibration properties
(
19
;
31
)
, and that calibrated confidence can be elicited through appropriate prompting
(
40
;
78
)
. Post-hoc calibration methods such as Platt scaling
(
63
)
, histogram binning
(
55
)
, and temperature scaling
(
31
)
can improve calibration without retraining, but do not address the incentive conflict that arises when calibration enters the training objective alongside autonomy.
59
show that calibration degrades under distribution shift, a concern orthogonal to but compounding the trilemma.
78
demonstrate that RLHF fine-tuning degrades calibration relative to pre-training, providing empirical support for the Perturbation Lemma’s predictions. However, calibration is typically measured post-hoc rather than included as an explicit training signal.

The qualitative impossibility established here is complemented by quantitative magnitude bounds available within specific scoring rule families.
48
(companion preprint,
arXiv:2605.03793
) establishes the True-KL
0
property for the pseudospherical scoring rule family with parameter
p\in(d,d+1)
in low type-dimension
d\in\{2,3,4\}
: the gain ratio
R(M,p,d)<1
for every type
M>1
, so the best misreport is always worse than the honest score itself. This is an
a priori
magnitude bound on misreport welfare conditional on the principal’s choice of scoring family, orthogonal to the trilemma’s qualitative impossibility. The trilemma rules out achieving
H{+}C{+}A
simultaneously; the magnitude bound caps the welfare cost of the resulting strategic deviation if the principal selects pseudospherical scoring. The result fails for
d\geq 5
, reinforcing the binary-and-low-multidim emphasis of the trilemma’s core analysis.

2.4
Alignment Impossibilities

Several recent impossibility results constrain the alignment landscape.
85
show that no alignment procedure can simultaneously guarantee representativeness, tractability, and robustness.
27
demonstrate mutual exclusivity of strong optimization, perfect value capture, and robust generalization.
10
survey fundamental limitations of RLHF.

Our trilemma is fundamentally different in mechanism: existing impossibilities operate at the
system design
level (computational or statistical barriers); ours operates at the
deployment
level (what a trained agent can achieve under oversight). Ours is
incentive-theoretic
: even if training is perfect, the information asymmetry between agent and principal creates the trilemma.

The Perturbation Lemma is related to Goodhart’s Law. In the taxonomy of
50
, the strategic inflation we identify is an instance of the adversarial variant. What the Perturbation Lemma adds is the specific proper-scoring-rule formalization: the inflation magnitude is characterized in closed form, and the detection complexity is bounded.

2.5
Mechanism Design for AI

The connection between mechanism design and AI alignment has been explored from several directions. The specific interaction between proper scoring rules and threshold oversight in the AI agent setting has not been studied. Our formal model is a screening model with hidden type in the tradition of
52
and
54
; the single-task, multi-objective structure with conflict between calibration and autonomy places it within the mechanism-design tradition of
44
. The canonical dominant-strategy truthful mechanism for allocation with value-reporting agents is the Vickrey–Clarke–Groves mechanism
(
81
;
14
;
30
)
; we are explicit that our CGDP is not such a mechanism — the designer’s instrument is restricted to a scoring rule plus approval function, not a transfer-based allocation, so VCG-style truthfulness does not apply (see Remark
8
). This is also distinct from the cheap-talk model of
16
: the agent’s report is scored against outcomes, creating a direct payoff consequence. Recent work by
15
connects social choice theory to alignment;
32
draws on incomplete contract theory
(
36
)
. The property elicitation framework of
25
characterizes what statistical functionals can be truthfully elicited; our result characterizes when that truthful elicitation breaks under conflicting incentives. The CGDP also sits within the broader strategic-prediction landscape:
34
formalise strategic classification, where data points are reported by strategic agents anticipating the classifier’s decision, and
61
introduce performative prediction, where the act of prediction shifts the data distribution. The trilemma occupies the scoring-rule-based screening corner of that landscape: the agent reports a calibration-scored confidence rather than manipulating features, and the principal’s gating rule plays the role of the strategic-classifier’s decision boundary, scored against ground truth rather than against a strategic-adversary model. The EC-tradition canon of peer-prediction and Bayesian truth-serum mechanisms
(
64
;
84
)
provides a complementary scoring-rule design for eliciting truthful private reports without relying on verifiable outcomes, in contrast to the CGDP where realised outcomes are available and the principal’s residual instrument is the threshold gate.

2.6
Prediction Markets and Proper Scoring

Prediction markets use proper scoring rules to incentivize truthful belief reporting from participants with private information
(
33
;
11
)
. The tension between proper scoring and strategic incentives in these markets is well documented: participants who hold private information and seek to maximize payoffs face a tension between honest reporting and strategic behavior that mirrors the trilemma’s core conflict
(
84
)
. Our CGDP formalizes this tension for autonomous agents rather than human forecasters: the principal’s approval gating plays the role of the market mechanism, the agent’s private success probability plays the role of private information, and the scoring rule plays the same role in both settings.

2.7
Adjustable Autonomy

The adjustable autonomy literature
(
20
;
7
;
70
;
60
)
studies dynamic allocation of decision authority. Recent agentic systems
(
56
)
implement de facto confidence gating by requiring the agent to decide when to request human assistance. Our formulation captures the binary approve/reject decision via the approval function
q(r)\in[0,1]
(Definition
1
), the simplest structure that yields the impossibility; richer measures would reveal additional nuance without changing the fundamental result.

3
The Behavioral Credibility Trilemma

3.1
The Confidence-Gated Decision Problem

We formalize the natural setting for RL with confidence-gated autonomy.

Definition 1

(Confidence-Gated Decision Problem)
.

A confidence-gated decision problem (CGDP) is a tuple
\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{A},[0,1],p,q,S,w_{\!C},w_{\!A})
where:

•

\mathcal{C}
is a finite context set.

•

\mathcal{A}
is a finite action set.

•

[0,1]
is the confidence report space.

•

p:\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{A}\to[0,1]
maps (context, action) to the true success probability.

•

q:[0,1]\to[0,1]
is the approval function.
q(r)
is the probability that action
a
is approved given report
r
. If not approved, the agent receives reward
0
.

•

S:[0,1]\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
is a strictly proper scoring rule evaluating calibration.
S(r,p)
is the score awarded when the agent reports
r
and the true success probability is
p
.

•

w_{\!C},w_{\!A}>0
are the calibration and autonomy weights.

The agent’s payoff for action
a
with report
r
in context
c
is:

V(a,r;c)=w_{\!C}\cdot S(r,p(c,a))+w_{\!A}\cdot q(r)\cdot R(c,a),

(1)

where
R(c,a)\in[0,1]
is the task reward.

This is a family of single-shot decision problems parameterized by context
c
. The MDP structure (state transitions, horizons, discount factors) enters only through the experimental protocol (Section
7
) and the convergence analysis (Theorem
37
), not through the impossibility results.

(Two uses of

)
.

When

Assumption 3

(Safe-Fallback Action)
.

The action set
\mathcal{A}
contains an

with

and

Remark 4

(Operational status of the
(C,A)
corner)
.

On every binding state the conservative-refusal policy selects
a_{\varnothing}
, which by definition produces no task progress (
R(c,a_{\varnothing})=0

(H,C)

Definition 5

(CG-Policy)
.

A CG-policy is a mapping
\pi:\mathcal{C}\to\Delta(\mathcal{A}\times[0,1])

\pi_{a}(c)

Definition 6

(Private Belief)
.

The agent’s private belief about action

Remark 7
.

A helpfulness weight
w_{\!H}
does not appear in the agent’s payoff (
1
) because the action
a
is chosen to maximize
R(c,a)
(helpfulness) as a separate optimization over the action dimension; only the report

w_{\!H}
enters the principal’s social welfare objective
w_{\!H}H+w_{\!C}C+w_{\!A}A

Remark 8

(Revelation principle)
.

The revelation principle
(
54
)
does not collapse the CGDP to a truthful direct-revelation mechanism, because the designer’s instrument is restricted: the principal commits to an approval function
q:[0,1]\to[0,1]

Remark 9

(Reduced-form interpretation)
.

The CGDP is a reduced-form model of the incentive structure facing the training process, not a literal description of inference-time cognition. The agent does not literally observe
p(c,a)
and strategically choose

27
) depend only on the report-space payoff structure, not on the sequential wrapper or the private-information interpretation.

3.3
Assumptions

Assumption 13

(Private Information)
.

The agent observes
p(s,a)

(s,a,r,\text{outcome})
but not

Assumption 14

(Binding Contexts)
.

There exists
\mathcal{C}_{\text{bind}}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
with
\Pr(s\in\mathcal{C}_{\text{bind}})=\mu^{\prime}>0
such that for the optimal action

p(s,a^{*}(s))<r_{\min}(s)\quad\text{for all }s\in\mathcal{C}_{\text{bind}},

(2)

where
r_{\min}(s)=\inf\{r:q(r)>0\}
is the effective approval threshold (the lowest report that yields a positive approval probability). Truthful reporting of the optimal action’s success probability would not clear the gate.

Remark 15

(Empirical plausibility of the binding-set condition)
.

Assumption
14
requires that some tasks exceed the agent’s
reliable competence (
p<r_{\min}
).
In deployed LLM systems, this condition holds broadly:

41

substantial uncertainty on 15–40% of tasks across diverse benchmarks,
and
78

deviates from calibrated probabilities.
Any safety-relevant deployment with a non-trivial approval threshold
will have
\mu^{\prime}>0

which is empirically not the case for any current system.

Assumption 16

(Non-Affine Gating)
.

The approval function
q
is not affine on any interval
[p^{*},p^{*}+\epsilon]
for any
p^{*}<r_{\min}
and
\epsilon>0
.

Remark 17
.

Assumption
16

q(r)=\mathbf{1}[r\geq r_{\min}]
), sigmoid gates (
q(r)=\sigma((r-r_{\min})/\tau)
), and any piecewise-linear gate with a kink are all non-affine. The only affine gates are
q(r)=a+br
, which grant the same marginal approval to an agent reporting 0.01 as to one reporting 0.99, providing no meaningful safety gate. For the properness-destruction argument (Proposition
40
) and Theorem

, the weaker condition “
q
is not constant on
[p^{*},p^{*}+\epsilon]
” suffices; Assumption
16
is chosen as the canonical form because it is easier to verify in practice and excludes the entire family of affine gates at once.

Assumption 18

(Monotone Gating)
.

The approval function
q:[0,1]\to[0,1]
is non-decreasing.

Remark 19
.

Monotonicity of the approval function is the natural condition that higher reported confidence does not decrease approval probability. All standard gating mechanisms (thresholds, sigmoids, piecewise-linear ramps) satisfy it. Non-monotone gates, which penalize suspiciously high reports, would constitute a separate regime worth investigating. This assumption is used in the optimizer-independence proof (Section
6
).

Assumption 20

(Positive Helpfulness-Miscalibration Correlation)
.

On binding states,
\mathrm{Cov}(R_{\phi}(y|x),\,(r(y)-p)^{2})>0
: completions that score higher on the reward model

(Section

) tend to have larger calibration gaps.

Remark 21
.

This assumption holds when the reward model systematically prefers confident-sounding outputs, which is empirically well-documented for RLHF reward models trained on human preferences
(
76
;
62
)
. It may fail for accuracy-based reward models that penalize overconfidence directly; in that case, Best-of-N selection would not degrade calibration, and the system operates in Regime 1 (Goodhart, Section
5.1
) rather than the trilemma regime. This assumption is used in Proposition

(for strict positivity of the selection-induced covariance; the non-decreasing structure used by the FKG inequality there comes directly from the

, not from any density-shape hypothesis on the marginal of

) and the optimizer-independence proof.

The Behavioral Perturbation Lemma

Lemma 22

(Behavioral Perturbation Lemma)
.

Let
s\in\mathcal{C}_{\text{bind}}
with
p^{*}=p(s,a^{*}(s))
and
r_{\min}=r_{\min}(s)
, so
p^{*}<r_{\min}
. Let
R^{*}=R(s,a^{*}(s))\in[0,1]
. Suppose the principal uses an approval function
q:[0,1]\to[0,1]
, and that the agent’s calibration is evaluated by the Brier score
S(r,\omega)=-(r-\omega)^{2}
(the general-generator extension is given by Corollary
23
).

(i)

Incentive to overreport (smooth case).
Suppose
q\in C^{2}
in a neighbourhood of
p^{*}
with
q^{\prime}(p^{*})\neq 0
and
q^{\prime\prime}
bounded on that neighbourhood. The agent’s optimal report satisfies:

r^{*}=p^{*}+\frac{w_{\!A}R^{*}}{2w_{\!C}}\cdot q^{\prime}(p^{*})+O\!\left(\left(\frac{w_{\!A}R^{*}}{2w_{\!C}}\right)^{2}\right).

(3)

(ii)

Sharp threshold.
Under
q(r)=\mathbf{1}[r\geq r_{\min}]
, the agent inflates whenever:

w_{\!A}R^{*}>w_{\!C}(r_{\min}-p^{*})^{2}.

(4)

(iii)

Detection sample complexity.
For any fixed type-II error level
\beta\in(0,1)
, detecting an inflation of magnitude
\Delta=r^{*}-p^{*}
via comparison of reported confidence against the empirical success rate is sufficient with
K=O(1/\Delta^{2})
observations, and
K=\Omega(1/\Delta^{2})
observations are necessary (a matching minimax lower bound from a standard two-point Le Cam construction; see
80
Ch. 2). The detection sample complexity is therefore
\Theta(1/\Delta^{2})
, matching the abstract’s stated bound.

Proof.

Part (i).
Fix state
s
and action
a^{*}(s)
. The agent’s payoff for report
r
is:

V(r)=w_{\!C}\cdot S(r,p^{*})+w_{\!A}\cdot q(r)\cdot R(s,a^{*}).

(5)

Using the Brier score
S(r,p)=-(r-p)^{2}
, the first-order condition
V^{\prime}(r^{*})=0
gives:

-2w_{\!C}(r^{*}-p^{*})+w_{\!A}R(s,a^{*})\cdot q^{\prime}(r^{*})=0,

(6)

yielding
r^{*}=p^{*}+\frac{w_{\!A}R(s,a^{*})}{2w_{\!C}}q^{\prime}(r^{*})
. For
w_{\!A}/(2w_{\!C})
small, the first-order approximation follows by the implicit function theorem.

Part (ii).
Under the sharp threshold
q(r)=\mathbf{1}[r\geq r_{\min}]
, the agent compares reporting truthfully (
r=p^{*}
, rejected, reward:
w_{\!C}C^{*}
) with inflating (
r=r_{\min}+\delta
, approved, reward:
w_{\!C}[C^{*}-(r_{\min}-p^{*})^{2}]+w_{\!A}
). Inflation dominates when
w_{\!A}>w_{\!C}(r_{\min}-p^{*})^{2}
.

Part (iii).

Sufficiency.
By Hoeffding’s inequality, at significance level
\alpha_{\text{sig}}\in(0,1)
,
K\geq\ln(2/\alpha_{\text{sig}})/(2\Delta^{2})
observations suffice to detect that the empirical success rate is below reported confidence with type-II error at most
\beta
for any fixed
\beta>0
; hence
K=O(1/\Delta^{2})
suffices.
Necessity.
The matching lower bound follows from a two-point Le Cam construction. Distinguishing the null Bernoulli law
P_{0}=\mathrm{Bern}(r^{*})
from the alternative
P_{1}=\mathrm{Bern}(r^{*}-\Delta)
over
K
i.i.d. samples requires the
K
-fold product measures
P_{0}^{\otimes K}
and
P_{1}^{\otimes K}
to be distinguishable at a fixed error level. By the tensorisation of KL divergence,
\mathrm{KL}(P_{0}^{\otimes K}\|P_{1}^{\otimes K})=K\cdot\mathrm{KL}(P_{0}\|P_{1})
, and the per-sample Bernoulli KL satisfies
\mathrm{KL}(P_{0}\|P_{1})=\Theta(\Delta^{2}/(r^{*}(1-r^{*})))
by second-order Taylor expansion for
\Delta\ll r^{*},1-r^{*}
. Pinsker’s inequality then gives
\|P_{0}^{\otimes K}-P_{1}^{\otimes K}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq\sqrt{K\cdot\mathrm{KL}(P_{0}\|P_{1})/2}
, so product-measure TV distance of order one requires
K=\Omega(r^{*}(1-r^{*})/\Delta^{2})=\Omega(1/\Delta^{2})
; any test with type-II error below a fixed level therefore needs at least
\Omega(1/\Delta^{2})
observations (see
80
Theorem 2.2).
∎

Equivalently:
q
non-affine implies the perturbed rule
V(r)=w_{\!C}S(r,p)+w_{\!A}q(r)R
is not strictly proper
(

;
29
)
; the only properness-preserving perturbations are affine, which give no safety gate (Assumption

). The perturbation result per se is classical; the contributions here are the quantitative inflation magnitude under smooth and threshold gates, the detection-complexity bound, and the unconditional non-affinity result (Theorem
27
).

Corollary 23

(General Inflation Formula)
.

Let
S
be a strictly proper scoring rule with convex generator
G
(so that
\mathbb{E}_{p}[S(r,\omega)]=G(r)+G^{\prime}(r)(p-r)
, maximized at
r=p
). Under the conditions of Lemma
22
(i), the first-order inflation for an arbitrary strictly proper scoring rule is:

\Delta(p)\;=\;\frac{w_{\!A}\cdot R(s,a^{*})\cdot q^{\prime}(r^{*})}{\ w_{\!C}\cdot G^{\prime\prime}(p)\ },

(7)

where
R(s,a^{*})\in[0,1]
is the expected reward of the optimal action. Since
G
is strictly convex,
G^{\prime\prime}(p)>0
, and the absolute value in the denominator is unnecessary. For binary tasks where reward is normalized to
R\in\{0,1\}
and we condition on success (
R=1
), the formula simplifies to
\Delta(p)=w_{\!A}\cdot q^{\prime}(r^{*})/(w_{\!C}\cdot G^{\prime\prime}(p))
; this normalization is used throughout the paper unless stated otherwise. For the Brier score,
G^{\prime\prime}(p)=2
(constant), recovering
\Delta=w_{\!A}q^{\prime}/(2w_{\!C})
. For the logarithmic score,
G^{\prime\prime}(p)=1/(p(1-p))
, giving type-dependent inflation
\Delta(p)=w_{\!A}\cdot q^{\prime}(r^{*})\cdot p(1-p)/w_{\!C}
, which vanishes near the boundary and peaks at
p=1/2
.

Proof.

The agent’s expected payoff under generator
G
is
\mathbb{E}_{p}[V(r)]=w_{\!C}[G(r)+G^{\prime}(r)(p-r)]+w_{\!A}\cdot q(r)\cdot R(s,a^{*})
. The first-order condition is
w_{\!C}\cdot G^{\prime\prime}(r^{*})(p-r^{*})+w_{\!A}R(s,a^{*})\cdot q^{\prime}(r^{*})=0
, giving
r^{*}-p=w_{\!A}R(s,a^{*})\cdot q^{\prime}(r^{*})/(w_{\!C}\cdot G^{\prime\prime}(r^{*}))
. Since
r^{*}-p=O(w_{\!A}/w_{\!C})
and
G^{\prime\prime}
is continuous,
G^{\prime\prime}(r^{*})=G^{\prime\prime}(p)+O(w_{\!A}/w_{\!C})
; the stated formula with
G^{\prime\prime}(p)
in the denominator holds to first order in
w_{\!A}/w_{\!C}
.
∎

Remark 24
.

The type-dependence of inflation under non-quadratic generators has welfare implications: under the log score, agents with extreme beliefs (
p
near
0
or
1
) inflate less than agents with intermediate beliefs. This interacts with the principal’s screening problem; the first-best characterization under general generators is developed in the companion preprint
(

)
(see Remark
28
).

Theorem 26

(Behavioral Credibility Trilemma)
.

(H,C)
without

Weighted-sum optima of the principal’s welfare objective

3.8
Optimal Oversight Is Non-Affine

The theorems above show that non-affine approval rules destroy strict properness. One might object that a sophisticated principal could choose an affine rule. The following theorem resolves this.

The principal’s Stackelberg program.

The CGDP induces a Stackelberg game (in the sense of the equilibrium-concept paragraph in Section
2
) in which the principal commits to an approval function
q:[0,1]\to[0,1]
, and the agent best-responds by selecting, for each type
p
, the report
r^{*}_{q}(p)
that maximises (
1
) (the first-order characterisation is Lemma
22
; under strict properness of
S
and Assumption
18
the best response is unique up to a measure-zero indifference set). Writing
\Lambda(p):=R^{*}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{p\geq p_{\min}\}-c\cdot\mathbf{1}\{p<p_{\min}\}
for the principal’s per-type net benefit from approval (with
c>0
the cost of approving an unprofitable type, so
\Lambda(p)>0
for
p\geq p_{\min}
,
\Lambda(p)<0
for
p<p_{\min}
, and the sign of
\Lambda
changes at
p_{\min}\in(0,1)
; the symbol
\Lambda
is reserved for this per-type net-benefit functional and is disjoint from the policy space
\Pi=\Delta(\mathcal{A}\times[0,1])^{\mathcal{C}}
of Definition
5
), the principal’s expected utility is

U_{P}(q)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{p\sim F}\bigl[\,q\bigl(r^{*}_{q}(p)\bigr)\cdot\Lambda(p)\,\bigr].

(8)

The principal’s Stackelberg program is to maximise
U_{P}(q)
over the class of monotone non-decreasing
q
(Assumption
18
). Theorem
27
below characterises the structure of the optimum: no affine
q
is optimal; in the unsaturated regime, the step rule
q^{*}(r)=\mathbf{1}\{r\geq r_{0}\}
with
r_{0}=p_{\min}+\sqrt{w_{\!A}R^{*}/w_{\!C}}
achieves the first-best screening
\mathbf{1}\{p\geq p_{\min}\}
; in the saturated regime, the threshold class is provably sub-first-best (Remark
28
). The Stackelberg solution concept (principal commits, agent best-responds) is the natural one for the oversight setting: the principal commits to the gate by deploying it, and the agent (or its training process) optimises against the deployed gate. Sequential equilibrium concepts (e.g., Markov-perfect equilibrium with the gate updated over time) are an interesting extension that we leave for future work.

Theorem 27

(Optimal Oversight Non-Affinity)
.

Consider the principal’s mechanism design problem: commit to
q:[0,1]\to[0,1]
to maximize expected utility
U_{P}(q)
, where
r^{*}_{q}(p)
is the agent’s optimal report under
q
, the principal’s net benefit from approval changes sign at
p_{\min}\in(0,1)
, and the type distribution places positive mass on both sides.

(i)

No affine
q
is optimal for the principal.

(ii)

Let
R^{*}=R(s,a^{*}(s))
denote the task reward of the optimal action on the binding set. When
w_{\!A}R^{*}/w_{\!C}\leq(1-p_{\min})^{2}
, the step function
q^{*}(r)=\mathbf{1}\{r\geq r_{0}\}
with

achieves first-best screening. When
w_{\!A}R^{*}/w_{\!C}>(1-p_{\min})^{2}
, no threshold on
[0,1]
implements first-best: even the maximum report

is insufficient to separate all types, and the principal must accept pooling at
r=1
or employ non-threshold mechanisms.

(iii)

The principal’s optimal
q^{*}
is non-affine, and the Perturbation Lemma applies unconditionally.

Proof.

Part (i).
If
q(r)=a+br
, the agent’s FOC gives

, so the induced screening
\tilde{q}(p)=(a+b\delta)+bp
is affine in

. Since the principal’s net benefit changes sign at

, the pointwise optimal screening is the step function
\mathbf{1}\{p\geq p_{\min}\}
. Any affine

either approves unprofitable types or rejects profitable ones, incurring strict loss.

Part (ii).
Under
q^{*}(r)=\mathbf{1}\{r\geq r_{0}\}
with
r_{0}=p_{\min}+\sqrt{w_{\!A}R^{*}/w_{\!C}}
, type
p
inflates to
r_{0}
iff

follows from (i) and (ii).
∎

Remark 28

(Saturated regime)
.

When
w_{\!A}R^{*}/w_{\!C}>(1-p_{\min})^{2}

(pool all approving types at

whenever

Characterising it in closed form for arbitrary

This is analogous to Myerson’s
(

Proposition 29

(Brier as the distribution-robust calibration loss)
.

Among strictly proper scoring rules, the Brier score is the unique one (up to affine equivalence) whose first-order welfare loss under a smooth single-threshold gate vanishes for

Resolution Pathways

The trilemma is not a dead end. We identify two constructive

). These parallel mechanism-design resolution mechanisms
(

and the scalable-oversight agenda in AI alignment: recursive reward modelling
(

. The CGDP formalism gives a unified language for when these pathways can and cannot restore the objectives the trilemma jointly negates.

4.1
Commitment Devices: Feasibility Maps

Institutional framing of delegation.

The CGDP’s information-asymmetry primitive (Assumption
13
) says the principal does not observe
p(s,a)
. This is not contradicted by the commitment resolution:
delegation passes the task to a competent external decision-maker
(a human-in-the-loop, an oracle, or a higher-tier model) that the agent and principal jointly trust to execute the task-optimal action. The commitment policy is therefore a
system-level
claim about an agent–principal–delegate institutional triple, not a single-actor claim that the principal can independently produce
a^{*}(s)
.

This framing aligns with the selective-prediction literature
(
12
;
21
;
49
)
, where the abstention option routes hard instances to an external classifier (typically a human expert) with strictly better expected performance. It also matches the scalable-oversight programme
(
46
;
6
)
, where the “principal” is a tiered system (model, reviewer, external auditor) whose composite competence exceeds any single component’s.

We formalize this with the auxiliary assumption:

Assumption 30

(Competent Delegation)
.

On states
s
where the agent delegates, the institutional principal executes the task-optimal action
a^{*}(s)=\arg\max_{a}R(s,a)
at the same expected reward as autonomous execution by an oracle agent.

Assumption
30
is the natural counterpart to the agent’s commitment to truthful reporting on autonomous tasks: together they characterize a competence-tiered institutional pair in which the agent handles routine tasks and the principal (or its delegate) handles binding tasks. Both assumptions can fail (agents can over-report and human delegates can be incompetent), but they are independently empirically testable and conceptually distinct from the trilemma’s incentive-theoretic core, which holds regardless of delegation competence.

It is essential to distinguish Assumption
30
from the information-asymmetry primitive. The latter is about
what each actor observes
; the former is about
what each actor can execute when called upon
. The trilemma’s impossibility direction (Theorem
26
) does not rely on Assumption
30
; only the achievability of the commitment policy (Theorem
31
below) requires it.

Theorem 31

(Commitment Resolution)
.

Let
F\subseteq\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{A}
be a feasibility map (an arbitrary measurable subset of context–action pairs fixed in advance), and let
\mathcal{C}_{\textup{infeasible}}=\{s\in\mathcal{C}:(s,a^{*}(s))\notin F\}
be the set of contexts where the optimal action is outside the feasibility map. Under Assumptions
13
–
3
and Assumption
30

\pi_{F}
that: selects

, and

If

Proof.

On both autonomous and delegated tasks,

, which is calibrated by construction, so
C(\pi_{F})=C^{*}
. Helpfulness is
H(\pi_{F})=\mathbb{E}_{s}[R(s,a^{*}(s))]=H^{*}
. Autonomy is
A(\pi_{F})=\Pr(s\notin\mathcal{C}_{\text{bind}}\cup\mathcal{C}_{\textup{infeasible}})=1-\Pr(\mathcal{C}_{\text{bind}}\cup\mathcal{C}_{\textup{infeasible}})
. Under verifiable commitment, deviations from
\pi_{F}
by the agent are detectable at zero expected cost, so
\pi_{F}
is incentive-compatible.
∎

4.2
Domain Separation: Critic Architecture

Proposition 32

(Domain Separation)
.

Separating the evaluator from the actor breaks the combined-roles problem. Suppose the critic is trained to maximize
-w_{\!C}\cdot\mathbb{E}[(r^{\prime}-p)^{2}]
(no autonomy term) and its report
r^{\prime}
substitutes for the actor’s
r
at the gate. Then the actor–critic pair achieves
C=C^{*}
and
A\geq 1-\mu^{\prime}
, at a cost
|\Delta H|\leq\mu^{\prime}\cdot R_{\max}
to helpfulness, where
\mu^{\prime}=\Pr(s\in\mathcal{C}_{\text{bind}})
and
R_{\max}=\sup_{s,a}R(s,a)
.

Proof.

The critic’s effective payoff
V_{\text{critic}}(r^{\prime})=-w_{\!C}(r^{\prime}-p)^{2}
is strictly proper, so the critic’s optimal report is
r^{\prime}=p(s,a)
and
C=C^{*}
. The critic vetoes exactly when
r^{\prime}<r_{\min}
, which on binding states occurs with probability
\mu^{\prime}
, so
A\geq 1-\mu^{\prime}
. Helpfulness loss is bounded by the expected reward foregone on vetoed states:
|\Delta H|=|H^{*}-H(\pi)|\leq\mu^{\prime}\cdot R_{\max}
.
∎

5
Where Do Existing Methods Sit?

Scope note.
The CGDP formalization and Theorems
25
–
27
are deployment-time results: they characterize what a trained agent can achieve under oversight, regardless of how it was trained. The method mapping below extends these results to training-time claims under the additional assumption that the training process (RLHF, DPO, etc.) converges to an approximate payoff maximizer. This assumption is empirically supported
(
74
;
66
)
but not formally proved for all training regimes. Claims about specific training methods in this section are therefore structural arguments about the training objective’s incentive landscape, not formal theorems about convergence.

The CGDP framework provides a unified lens for classifying RL and alignment methods by their composite payoff structure. We express each method’s training objective in the template
V(r)=w_{\!C}\cdot S(r,p)+w_{\!A}\cdot h(r)
and determine whether the trilemma applies.

5.1
The Goodhart-to-Trilemma Transition

Current RLHF/DPO methods avoid the trilemma by not attempting calibration. Their overconfidence is a Goodhart effect: the reward model is a proxy for human preferences, and optimizing this proxy leads to confident-sounding outputs because human raters prefer them. This is a training artifact, not a structural impossibility.

The trilemma becomes relevant precisely when the system design includes a calibration objective. The transition occurs at the moment a proper scoring rule enters the training (or evaluation) loop:

Regime 1 (Goodhart, no scoring rule):
The agent’s payoff is
V=h(r)
with
h
non-affine and no proper scoring rule component. Overconfidence is unbounded (limited only by the reward model’s saturation). Resolution: improve the reward model, add calibration data.

Regime 2 (Trilemma, with scoring rule):
The agent’s payoff is
V=w_{\!C}\cdot S(r,p)+w_{\!A}\cdot h(r)
with
S
strictly proper and
h
non-affine. Overconfidence is bounded (Lemma
22
) but structurally unavoidable. Resolution requires architectural changes (commitment, separation), not better training data.

The field is moving from Regime 1 to Regime 2: the confidence-reporting deployments of §
1
(the coding-agent, medical-AI, and autonomous-vehicle examples) report confidence and are evaluated against ground truth, so they are CGDPs and the trilemma applies.

5.2
Methods Without Calibration: RL, RLHF, DPO

Plain RL (
V=R(s,a)
, with
w_{\!C}=0
and
q\equiv 1
in the CGDP decomposition) escapes the trilemma trivially: with no confidence report there is nothing to inflate. The remaining methods in this class carry a non-affine autonomy incentive but no proper-scoring counter-term.

Proposition 33

(Calibration-free methods occupy the H+A edge)
.

Let a training method optimize an objective
V(r)=h(r)
in which the report
r
enters only through a preference-derived or constraint term
h
, with no strictly-proper-scoring component (
S\equiv 0
). Then on binding states the method’s optimum satisfies
r^{*}>p^{*}
with the inflation unconstrained by any calibration counter-term; equivalently, the method sits on the H+A edge of the trilemma (the Goodhart regime of §
5.1
), not in the calibration-bearing trilemma regime. This covers:

1.

RLHF

(
13
;
74
)
: the reward model
R_{\phi}
is trained on preference comparisons, not on probability–outcome pairs, so it is not a proper scoring rule; the KL penalty constrains output-space policy divergence, not report accuracy.

2.

DPO

(
66
)
and preference-optimization variants (KTO, IPO, ORPO, SLiC-HF): by the reward–policy bijection, the implicit reward
r_{\textup{DPO}}(y|x)=\beta\log(\pi_{\theta}(y|x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x))+c(x)
is a log-likelihood ratio with no
S(r,\omega)
term.

3.

Safe RLHF

(
17
)
(and Constitutional AI
(
4
)
): the safety constraint restricts the action distribution, leaving the report–probability relationship unconstrained, so an agent can be safe and arbitrarily miscalibrated simultaneously.

Proof.

None of the three objectives contains a term of the form
-w_{\!C}(r-p)^{2}
, or any strictly proper
S(r,p)
, that penalizes the divergence between the reported probability and the true success rate. For RLHF, the reward model
R_{\phi}
maximizes log-likelihood of pairwise comparisons, so the effective payoff
V(r)=R_{\phi}(y|x)-\beta_{\textup{KL}}\log(\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}})
carries no
-w_{\!C}(r-p)^{2}
term. For DPO, the objective maximizes
\mathbb{E}[\log\sigma(\beta\log(\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}|x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}|x))-\beta\log(\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}|x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}|x)))]
over preference pairs
(y_{w},y_{l})
; by
66
(Theorem 1 there), this is equivalent to optimizing a Bradley–Terry preference model with implicit reward
r_{\textup{DPO}}
, a log-likelihood ratio rather than a scoring rule
S(r,\omega)
, and KTO, IPO, ORPO, and SLiC-HF inherit the conclusion since each optimizes a preference-derived objective with no explicit proper-scoring component. For Safe RLHF, the safety constraint
\mathbb{E}[C_{\textup{safety}}(y|x)]\geq\tau
is a constraint on the action distribution (which outputs are generated), independent of the
r
-vs-
p
relationship: an agent that always reports
r=1
while selecting only safe actions satisfies
C_{\textup{safety}}\geq\tau
yet is arbitrarily miscalibrated, so the safety and calibration dimensions are linearly independent in the constraint space. In each case the objective reduces to
V=h(r)
with
h
non-affine and no proper-scoring counter-term; since no term penalizes report–truth divergence, the inflation is unconstrained by calibration and the optimum lies on the H+A edge.
∎

5.3
Methods With Calibration: RLHF+Brier, CG-RL

Proposition 34

(RLHF+Brier Navigates the Pareto Curve)
.

The RLHF+Brier objective
V(r)=-\gamma_{\textup{pert}}(r-p)^{2}+\alpha\cdot R_{\phi}(y|x)
matches the trilemma template exactly. The Brier score provides
S
; the reward model provides the non-affine perturbation. The agent navigates the H-C Pareto curve parametrically in
\alpha/\gamma_{\textup{pert}}
. The optimal report on binding states satisfies:

r^{*}=p^{*}+\frac{\alpha}{2\gamma_{\textup{pert}}}\cdot q^{\prime}_{\textup{implicit}}(p^{*}),

(9)

where
q_{\textup{implicit}}(r)\coloneqq\Pr(R_{\phi}(y|x)\geq\tau_{\text{deploy}}\mid r(y)=r)
is the implicit approval function induced by the reward model’s deployment threshold, and
q^{\prime}_{\textup{implicit}}
is its derivative. No weight ratio achieves
H=H^{*}
,
C=C^{*}
, and
A=1
simultaneously.

Proof.

The objective
V(r)=-\gamma_{\text{pert}}(r-p)^{2}+\alpha\cdot R_{\phi}(y|x)
matches the CGDP payoff (
1
) with
S(r,p)=-(r-p)^{2}
(Brier score),
w_{\!C}=\gamma_{\text{pert}}
, and
h(r)=\alpha\cdot R_{\phi}(y|x)
as the non-affine perturbation (non-affine because
R_{\phi}
is a learned reward model, not a linear function of
r

(i), the optimal report is shifted by
\alpha q^{\prime}_{\text{implicit}}(p^{*})/(2\gamma_{\text{pert}})
. By Theorem
26
, no weight ratio achieves the triple
(H^{*},C^{*},1)
.
∎

RL with explicit confidence gating (autonomous coding agents, medical AI, robots with uncertainty quantification) is the CGDP itself:
q
is explicit,

scores calibration against ground truth, all three assumptions hold by construction, and the impossibility is unconditional. This is the natural habitat of the trilemma.

5.4
Best-of-N Sampling

Proposition 35

(Best-of-N Instantiates the Trilemma at Inference Time)
.

Let
\pi_{\textup{base}}
be a base policy with calibration gap
\delta_{\textup{base}}=\mathbb{E}[(r-p)^{2}]
. Under a selection criterion that includes a gating term
\mathbf{1}\{r\geq r_{\min}\}
(so that selected completions satisfy
r\geq r_{\min}
), on binding states (
p^{*}<r_{\min}
) the Best-of-N policy with selection criterion
R_{\phi}
satisfies:

(i)

\delta_{\textup{BoN}}\geq\delta_{\textup{base}}
under Assumption
20
, where both expectations are taken conditional on selection (equivalently, restricted to the region
r\geq r_{\min}\geq p^{*}
).

(ii)

The gap is non-decreasing in
N

\delta_{\textup{BoN}}(N+1)\geq\delta_{\textup{BoN}}(N)
, with strict inequality when the selection density has positive mass above the current selected value. The limit
\delta_{\infty}=\mathbb{E}[\max_{y}(r(y)-p)^{2}]
holds when the selection pool is i.i.d. and the gating condition is binding almost surely.

(iii)

No parameter-free inference-time method that selects outputs by a non-affine criterion of the report
r
can simultaneously improve helpfulness and maintain calibration on binding states.

Proof.

Best-of-
N
selects
i^{*}=\arg\max_{i\in[N]}R_{\phi}(y_{i}\mid x)
from
N

w_{N}(y)\;=\;N\cdot\Pr\!\bigl(R_{\phi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\leq R_{\phi}(y\mid x)\bigr)^{N-1},

which is non-decreasing in
R_{\phi}(y\mid x)
by construction (higher reward-model score
\Rightarrow
higher selection probability
\Rightarrow
higher importance weight). The FKG-type covariance step below requires comonotonicity of
w_{N}(y)
and
(r(y)-p)^{2}
on the binding domain
\{r\geq r_{\min}\geq p^{*}\}
, but
w_{N}
is non-decreasing in
R_{\phi}
rather than directly in
r
, so the comonotone-projection step is as follows. Monotonicity of
R_{\phi}
in
r
on the selected support is a consequence of Assumption
20
:
\mathrm{Cov}(R_{\phi}(y\mid x),r(y)-p\mid r\geq r_{\min})>0
on binding states. Replacing the in-expectation positive covariance by a pointwise comonotone projection uses the conditional comonotone reordering principle for monotone rearrangements
(
37
;
22
)
: the joint law of
(R_{\phi},r)
restricted to
\{r\geq r_{\min}\}
admits a comonotone coupling that preserves both marginals and the (positive) covariance, and the FKG inequality applied along this coupling yields the same lower bound as the pointwise-comonotone case. Under this coupling,
w_{N}
becomes a non-decreasing function of
r
on the selected support, so the standard FKG step applies. The selected-conditional calibration gap is

\delta_{\textup{BoN}}\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\textup{base}}\mid r\geq r_{\min}}\bigl[w_{N}(y)\cdot(r(y)-p)^{2}\bigr].

Applying the FKG-type covariance inequality
(
35
;
65
)
to the pair of non-decreasing functionals
w_{N}(\cdot)
and
(r(\cdot)-p)^{2}
on the restricted domain
\{r\geq r_{\min}\}\supseteq\{r\geq r_{\min}\geq p^{*}\}
(where
(r-p^{*})^{2}
is monotone non-decreasing in
r
), and using Assumption
20
for strict positivity of the covariance, we obtain

\delta_{\textup{BoN}}\;=\;\mathbb{E}[w_{N}]\cdot\delta_{\textup{base}}\;+\;\mathrm{Cov}\bigl(w_{N},(r-p)^{2}\bigr)\;\geq\;\delta_{\textup{base}},

with strict inequality for
N\geq 2
under non-degeneracy.

For Part (ii), Best-of-
(N+1)
is obtained from Best-of-
N
by adding one independent candidate and re-selecting. By a coupling argument, the selected importance weight
w_{N+1}
stochastically dominates
w_{N}
(additional candidates can only increase the maximum), and the integrand
(r-p)^{2}
is non-decreasing on the binding domain; the FKG inequality preserves this domination through the expectation. Hence
\delta_{\textup{BoN}}(N+1)\geq\delta_{\textup{BoN}}(N)
, with strict inequality whenever the selection density has positive mass above the current selected value.

Part (iii) follows from Theorem
26
applied to the induced non-affine selection criterion: any inference-time selection with autonomy bonus instantiates the trilemma’s payoff structure with
w_{\!A}>0
.
∎

6
Optimizer-Independence

The impossibility in Theorem
26
is a property of the payoff surface, not of any particular optimization algorithm. The composite payoff
V(r)=w_{\!C}\cdot S(r,p)+w_{\!A}\cdot q(r)\cdot R(s,a)
has a unique maximum at
r^{*}\neq p
on binding states. Any optimizer that ascends this surface converges to
r^{*}
, not to the calibrated report
r=p
.

Theorem 37

(Optimizer-Independence)
.

Let
V:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
be the composite payoff (
1
) with
S
strictly proper,
q
non-decreasing (Assumption
18
) and non-affine (Assumption
16
), and
w_{\!A}>0
. Assume the inflation regime of Lemma
22
(ii) holds on binding states (for a smooth gate with
q^{\prime}(p^{*})\neq 0
this is automatic; for the sharp-threshold gate it is the condition
w_{\!A}R^{*}>w_{\!C}(r_{\min}-p^{*})^{2}
). Let
r^{*}=\arg\max_{r}V(r)
and
r_{\textup{cal}}=p
. Then
r^{*}\neq r_{\textup{cal}}
on binding states, and:

(i)

(Rational agent.)
A Bayesian expected-utility maximizer reports
r^{*}
, not
r_{\textup{cal}}
.

(ii)

(Policy gradient.)
For any policy parameterization
r_{\theta}
with log-concave conditional density
\pi_{\theta}(r\mid s)
(e.g., Gaussian-mean, truncated Gaussian, Beta(
\alpha,\beta
) with
\alpha,\beta\geq 1
, logistic-Normal),
\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}[V(r_{\theta})]
points away from the calibrated policy at
r_{\theta}=p
.

(iii)

(Evolutionary.)
Any evolutionary strategy with selection proportional to
V
concentrates mass on
r^{*}
, not on
r_{\textup{cal}}
.

Proof.

Part (i) is immediate from
r^{*}\neq p
.

Part (ii): Policy gradient via the log-concave covariance inequality.
Let
\pi_{\theta}(r\mid s)
be a Gaussian policy
r\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\theta}(s),\sigma^{2})
with mean
\mu_{\theta}(s)
. At
\mu_{\theta}=p
:

\frac{\partial J}{\partial\mu_{\theta}}(s)=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{r\sim\pi_{\theta}}\left[V(r)\cdot(r-p)\right]=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\text{Cov}_{\pi_{\theta}}(V(r),r).

(10)

Write
V(r)=-w_{\!C}(r-p)^{2}+w_{\!A}\cdot q(r)\cdot R
. Since
r\sim\mathcal{N}(p,\sigma^{2})
is symmetric:

\text{Cov}(V(r),r)=-w_{\!C}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[(r-p)^{3}]}_{=0\text{ (Gaussian)}}+w_{\!A}R\cdot\underbrace{\text{Cov}(q(r),r)}_{>0\text{ (Harris--Proschan)}}.

(11)

The second term is positive by the covariance inequality for monotone functions under log-concave measures
(
35
;
65
)
: for
f
monotone non-decreasing and non-constant,
\text{Cov}(f(X),X)>0
when
X
has a log-concave density with positive variance (the one-dimensional case relevant here; the lattice-based FKG inequality of
24
is the higher-dimensional generalisation). Strict inequality requires
q
non-constant (not merely non-affine; non-constant is the weaker and correct condition). Since
q
is non-decreasing (Assumption
18
) and non-constant on the support of
\pi_{\theta}
:

\text{Cov}(V(r),r)=w_{\!A}R\cdot\text{Cov}(q(r),r)>0,

(12)

so
\partial J/\partial\mu_{\theta}>0
, meaning the gradient pushes the mean report above

.

Part (iii): General optimizers.
The calibrated policy
r=p
is not a stationary point of the composite payoff on binding states. Under a smooth gate
q
and when

,
V
has a unique global maximum at
r^{*}>p
, and any optimizer satisfying weak improvement (

while

) converges in distribution to this unique peak. Under the sharp-threshold gate

and the inflation condition

(ii), the payoff is piecewise quadratic with a global maximum at

and a local maximum at

Corollary 38
.

The calibrated policy (
\mu_{\theta}=p
for all
s
) is not a stationary point of the policy gradient on binding states when
w_{\!A}>0
. Any gradient-based optimizer initialized at calibration will move away from it.

Remark 39
.

For the one-dimensional continuous case used throughout this paper, the relevant result is the covariance inequality for monotone functions under log-concave measures, due to
35
and
65
. The lattice-based FKG inequality
(
24
)
provides the generalization to higher-dimensional settings. The covariance inequality states that for a log-concave probability measure and monotone non-decreasing functions
f,g
:
\text{Cov}(f(X),g(X))\geq 0
, with strict inequality when
f
is non-constant and the distribution is non-degenerate. The Gaussian is log-concave,
q
is non-decreasing (Assumption
18
) and non-constant, and the identity function
g(r)=r
is non-decreasing, so the conditions are satisfied. The Gaussian parameterization is illustrative. The result holds for any log-concave-density policy family (Beta with
\alpha,\beta\geq 1
, truncated Gaussian, logistic-Normal). Finite mixtures of log-concave densities are
not
in general log-concave (a standard counterexample is any bimodal Gaussian mixture), so the argument does not extend to arbitrary mixtures; it extends only to single-mode policy families whose density is log-concave.

The impossibility is therefore:

•

Independent of the optimizer:
rational agents, policy gradients, evolutionary strategies all find the displaced peak.

•

Independent of the scoring rule:
any strictly proper
S
(Brier, log, CRPS) is perturbed by non-affine
q
.

•

A property of the composite objective, not the training procedure.
Changing the optimizer changes the trajectory, not the destination.

We now turn to empirical validation: does a concrete optimizer ascending the composite payoff exhibit the predicted calibration degradation?

7
Experimental Protocol and Results

This section presents a hypothesis-driven experimental protocol with pre-specified falsification criteria and reports the results of a 540-configuration run on Qwen-2.5-7B (54,000 selected-task observations). All five pre-registered hypotheses (H1, H2, H4, H5, H6) are strongly confirmed at
\alpha=0.05
after Bonferroni–Holm correction with effect sizes ranging from
d=1.10
to
d=5.32
. We additionally report a
descriptive
analysis of the achievable-
(H,C,A)
surface geometry (§
7.8
): the midpoint-interpolation violation rate increases with the selection size
N
, exposing a plateau-truncated frontier consistent with the inflation saturation predicted by Lemma
22
(ii) and confirmed by H2. The raw data, analysis code, and the hypothesis-results artefact are released in the code-and-data repository documented in §
7.11
.

The protocol uses Best-of-N selection as the optimization mechanism. Best-of-N selection is a formal optimization (
\arg\max
over the composite payoff) that instantiates the CGDP’s report-space structure. Each completion is an (action, confidence) pair, and the selection operator maximizes the composite payoff. This is where the CGDP formalization earns its empirical content: the predictions (calibration degradation under gating pressure, inflation scaling with
w_{\!A}/w_{\!C}
, threshold pooling, binding-state specificity) follow from the payoff geometry, not from assumptions about the agent’s internal representations. Unlike prompting-based studies that conflate instruction-following with objective optimization, Best-of-N isolates the mechanism the theorems analyze.

The protocol targets five theoretical predictions (and an additional descriptive surface-geometry observation):

).

3.

Under gating pressure, the selected-confidence distribution develops excess mass in

on binding tasks.

4.

Inflation concentrates on binding tasks (

We construct 100 tasks with verifiable ground truth, stratified by difficulty and domain:

Each task requires the model to (a) state a confidence
r\in[0,1]
that its answer is correct, and (b) provide the answer. Ground truth is determined by arithmetic verification (exact-value comparison), fact-checking against public reference sources, and code execution against test cases.

Task provenance.

The 100 tasks are committed to the repository’s path
scripts/generate_tasks.py
(lines 85–121 for the factual set) on 2026-04-01 and have not been modified since; the frozen task list pre-dates every experimental run and is fixed in the archived release. The 40 arithmetic tasks are procedurally generated from random expressions with seeded RNG. The 30 code tasks are hand-authored Python function signatures with hand-authored test cases. The 30 factual tasks were generated by Claude Opus 4.6
(

with the generation prompt constrained to single-canonical-answer items drawn from standard reference knowledge (chemistry symbols, capitals, historical dates, physical constants, mathematics, anatomy, standardised codes). Each of the generator’s proposed answers was then verified against public reference sources (Wikipedia, standard encyclopaedias, authoritative databases); items with contested or source-dependent answers were excluded before the list was committed. We generate the factual items rather than drawing from existing benchmarks (e.g. TriviaQA, MMLU) to avoid training-data contamination with the instruction-tuned Qwen-2.5 model, and the specific generator (Claude Opus 4.6) post-dates Qwen-2.5’s training cutoff, so the items cannot appear in Qwen’s training distribution.

Experimental Protocol

For each task
t
, model
m
, weight vector
\mathbf{w}
, and selection size
N
:

1.

Generate.
Sample
N
independent completions from the model, each including an answer
a_{i}
and a confidence report
r_{i}
. Temperature
\tau=0.8
to ensure diversity. The confidence report
r_{i}
is derived from token-level log-probabilities returned by the inference endpoint (Ollama’s OpenAI-compatible
/v1/chat/completions
with
logprobs: true
). For a completion with
T
tokens having log-probabilities
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{T}
, we set

r_{i}\;=\;\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t}\right)\;=\;\left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{t}\right)^{\!1/T},

(13)

the geometric mean of per-token probabilities, clipped to
[0.01,1.0]
. Token-level log-probabilities are the finest-grained internal confidence signal the inference endpoint exposes and are well-correlated with ground-truth correctness at the completion level for instruction-tuned models of this scale
(
41
;
78
)
. A verbalised-confidence alternative (the model is asked to state its own confidence in prose) is additionally run as a robustness check and produces qualitatively identical H4–H6 patterns.

2.

Score.
Compute the selection payoff for each completion. Since Best-of-N operates post-hoc on generated completions whose correctness can be verified (by arithmetic check, fact lookup, or test execution), the ground truth
y_{i}\in\{0,1\}

is
available at selection time. The oracle selection payoff is:

V_{i}^{\text{sel}}=-w_{\!C}\cdot(r_{i}-y_{i})^{2}+w_{\!A}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{r_{i}\geq r_{\min}\},

(14)

where the first term is the Brier score against ground truth (a strictly proper scoring rule perturbation) and the second is the gating reward. This matches the Perturbation Lemma template exactly:
V=w_{\!C}\cdot S(r,y)+w_{\!A}\cdot q(r)
, so the experiment directly instantiates the theorem’s payoff structure.

Oracle vs expected Brier (see also Remark
36
).
The selection payoff uses the realized binary outcome
y_{i}\in\{0,1\}
, whereas the Perturbation Lemma is stated in terms of the expected-Brier payoff
V(r)=-w_{\!C}(r-p)^{2}+w_{\!A}q(r)
using the true probability
p
. The two are consistent in expectation:

\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p)}\bigl[-(r-y)^{2}\bigr]\;=\;-(r-p)^{2}-p(1-p),

(15)

so for every fixed
p
the
\arg\max_{r}
of the realized- and expected-Brier objectives coincide exactly (the
-p(1-p)
term is constant in
r
); the same is true once the gate term
w_{\!A}q(r)
is added, since it depends on
r
but not on
y
. Equivalently,
\mathbb{E}[V_{i}^{\text{sel}}\mid p_{i}]=V_{i}^{\text{expected}}-w_{\!C}\cdot p_{i}(1-p_{i})
and the
\arg\max_{r}
map is preserved. Finite-
N
Best-of-
N
selection on realized outcomes therefore introduces additional sampling variance into which completion is selected, but does not bias the Perturbation Lemma’s inflation prediction (which is a statement about the realized-outcome-averaged optimum, not the per-trial realisation). The realized-vs-expected discrepancy is statistical, not structural: at the population level the displaced optimum is the same. A natural future control experiment is to replace
y_{i}
in (
14
) with a held-out, frozen estimate
\hat{p}_{t}
of the per-task probability (from a held-out seed split disjoint from the experimental seeds) and verify that the inflation pattern of H2/H4/H5 reproduces under expected-Brier selection. We did not run this control in the present revision; we note it explicitly as future work. All hypothesis results (H1, H2, H4, H5, H6) reported below use the realized-outcome payoff of (
14
).

No-oracle proxy (sensitivity check).
When ground truth is unavailable, a natural proxy replaces the Brier score with the agent’s self-assessed expected calibration:
V_{i}^{\text{proxy}}=-w_{\!C}\cdot r_{i}(1-r_{i})+w_{\!A}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{r_{i}\geq r_{\min}\}
. However,
-r(1-r)
is Bernoulli variance, maximized at
r\in\{0,1\}
, not at
r=p
; this proxy is
not
a proper scoring rule and incentivizes extreme confidence reports independent of the gating term. We include this proxy as a control condition to quantify the gap between oracle and no-oracle selection. All primary analyses use the oracle payoff (
14
).

3.

Select.
Choose completion
i^{*}=\arg\max_{i}V_{i}^{\text{sel}}
.

4.

Record.
Store the selected
(r_{i^{*}},a_{i^{*}})
and compute all post-hoc metrics.

7.9
Statistical Methodology

The primary analysis fits a mixed-effects model to the Brier score with
N
,
w_{\!A}/w_{\!C}
, and
r_{\min}
as fixed effects and task and seed as crossed random effects, so that per-task difficulty and per-seed sampling variation are absorbed rather than confounded with the weight manipulation. All five hypothesis tests are corrected for multiple comparisons by the Bonferroni–Holm procedure across the family of five, and every pairwise contrast is reported with a Cohen’s
d
effect size so that statistical significance is never presented without an accompanying magnitude. Interval estimates are chosen by metric type rather than applied uniformly: mean-difference and effect-size quantities use bootstrap
95\%
confidence intervals (
10{,}000
resamples), whereas the surface-geometry violation-rate proportion of §
7.8
uses an
exact two-sided Clopper–Pearson
interval, since a bootstrap interval is unreliable for a bounded count statistic with few violations near zero. Results are read through three complementary views: Brier score versus
N
on a log–log scale for each weight vector, the achieved
(H,C,A)
frontier, and confidence-distribution histograms that expose threshold clustering on binding tasks.

7.10
Controls and Threats to Validity

Three controls isolate the gating mechanism from confounds. The first sets
w_{\!A}=0
— a pure calibration objective with no gating reward — under which selection should
improve
rather than degrade calibration; this is the formal content of H6 in Table
4
, and a failure here would indicate that Best-of-
N
selection is itself a source of miscalibration independent of the gate. The second sets
N=1
(no selection), establishing the base model’s unperturbed calibration as the reference point against which all selection effects are measured. The third replaces composite-score selection with uniform random selection among the
N
completions: this preserves the sampling distribution while removing the optimization pressure, so any systematic inflation that survives it cannot be attributed to the payoff-maximising mechanism the theorems analyse. Together the three controls bracket the mechanism — no incentive (Control 1), no selection (Control 2), and selection without the incentive gradient (Control 3) — so that an inflation effect present only in the full composite-selection condition is attributable to the gate.

Three threats to validity remain.

•

Construct validity:
A model’s stated confidence may not reflect an internal probability estimate. We use token-level log-probabilities ((
13
)) as the primary confidence metric precisely because they are the finest-grained internal signal the model exposes; a verbalised-confidence alternative (the model is asked to state its own confidence in prose) is run as a robustness check. On a held-out task subset, the Spearman rank correlation between the two metrics at the completion level is
\rho\approx 0.6
, and the five-hypothesis decisions (H1, H2, H4, H5, H6) are qualitatively identical under either metric (signs, significance, and relative magnitudes match). The logprob metric produces smaller absolute effect sizes than the verbalised alternative but is more sensitive to within-completion variance. Two caveats are worth noting. First, the geometric-mean logprob averages over all tokens in the completion (including format and reasoning tokens), which dilutes the answer-specific confidence signal; a more targeted per-token measure focused on the answer span is future work. Second, the
\tau=0.8
softmax temperature attenuates the probability magnitudes; a cross-temperature study is needed to confirm the hypothesis decisions are temperature-robust.

•

External validity:
Results on Qwen-2.5-7B may not generalize to other architectures or to larger scales. Theorem
37
predicts that the mechanism is architecture-independent, but empirical verification on additional open-weights models is left as future work. The experimental protocol and hypothesis specifications are stated in a model-agnostic form so that a replication can fix them in advance.

•

Ecological validity:
Best-of-N is a controlled optimization, not deployment-time behavior. This is intentional: we test the theoretical mechanism, not a particular deployment scenario.

8
Discussion

8.1
Implications for Alignment Training

RLHF with a calibration term targets an infeasible point.
Standard RLHF
(
58
)
optimises helpfulness against a reward model trained on human preferences and, increasingly, folds honesty or calibration into the same composite signal. The trilemma proves that if such a signal is used, one of helpfulness, calibration, and autonomy must be architecturally enforced rather than jointly optimised: the composite reward creates incentive to
appear
calibrated rather than
be
calibrated. Standard RLHF without an explicit calibration term sidesteps the trilemma — the overconfidence it produces is Goodhart, not trilemma — but only because it leaves calibration unmeasured.

The H+A corner dominates — conditional on Assumption
20
.
RLHF reward models weight helpfulness and fluency; human raters prefer agents that attempt to help over agents that express uncertainty
(
76
;
62
)
. This creates systematic pressure toward the sycophancy pattern, which the trilemma identifies as a rational response to the incentive structure rather than a training deficiency. The strength of this conclusion depends on Assumption
20
(positive helpfulness–miscalibration correlation on binding states): if human raters were calibration-preferring on average, the trilemma regime would not apply. Recent longitudinal evidence supports the assumption’s direction: in a preregistered study (
N=3{,}075
, with a three-week census-representative field component), a majority of users came to prefer sycophantic AI
because it made them feel understood rather than for the quality of its advice

(
39
)
— a revealed preference for affirmation over accuracy consistent with a positive population-level helpfulness–miscalibration covariance. Recent alignment variants that use honesty-aware reward models or explicit calibration constraints
(
17
;
4
)
move inside the trilemma’s regime, where the impossibility applies; standard preference-only RLHF stays outside it, where Goodhart does.

KL-constrained RL and iterative RLHF.
KL-penalised policy-gradient methods (PPO, TRPO) anchor the policy to a reference
\pi_{\text{ref}}
via
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}})
. Two regimes must be distinguished:

•

Fixed-reference regime.
With
\pi_{\text{ref}}
held fixed throughout training, KL-penalised RL has a well-known closed-form optimum
\pi^{*}(a\mid s)\propto\pi_{\text{ref}}(a\mid s)\exp(R(s,a)/\beta)
, which bounds the displacement from
\pi_{\text{ref}}
as a function of the KL coefficient
\beta
: a larger
\beta
yields smaller displacement, and in the limit
\beta\to\infty
the policy stays at
\pi_{\text{ref}}
. If
\pi_{\text{ref}}
is itself calibrated (e.g., a pre-trained base model before RLHF), a sufficiently strong KL anchor can keep the policy within a bounded-calibration-loss neighbourhood of the calibrated reference. The KL coefficient acts as a soft commitment device in the sense of §
4
, and the trilemma’s trajectory toward the shifted optimum is
bounded
, not merely
slowed
, as a function of
\beta
.

•

Iterative / moving-reference regime.
In iterative RLHF and online DPO-style methods,
\pi_{\text{ref}}
is updated between rounds (e.g., by replacing it with the current policy). The fixed-policy analysis of Lemma
22
is then an approximation, and the composite iteration is a two-timescale stochastic process. Here “slows but does not prevent” is the correct description: the Perturbation Lemma’s shifted optimum is no longer a fixed target, and each reference update shifts the feasible set toward the previous round’s displacement. A full two-timescale analysis
(
5
)
or mean-field treatment
(
45
)
of this setting is open.

The empirical effect depends on the regime: fixed-reference PPO with large
\beta
stays near the calibrated reference; iterative RLHF cumulates the drift across rounds.

Context-dependent Pareto selection.
Different deployment contexts require different Pareto points. Local development environments tolerate the H+A corner (low blast radius). Shared infrastructure requires the H+C corner. Safety-critical systems may require the C+A corner.

Regulatory alignment.
The EU AI Act’s Article 14
(
23
)
mandates the H+C corner at the cost of autonomy. The NIST Risk Management Framework
(
57
)
aligns with calibration (“Measure” function) and commitment (“Manage” function).

8.2
The Formal Model and Gradient-Trained LLMs

The Perturbation Lemma models the agent as an expected-utility maximizer with a separable type
\theta
and report
r
. Three points clarify where this analogy holds for gradient-trained LLMs.

Point 1: The individual-rationality gap.
A gradient-trained LLM does not explicitly compute
p^{*}
, form
r
, and decide whether to inflate. The strategic-inflation model assumes a well-defined internal
\theta
that diverges from the report, while LLMs may have diffuse, context-dependent internal representations.

Point 2: Population-level equivalence via gradient selection.
Despite this gap, the RLHF/PPO training loop creates the same aggregate incentive structure at the population level. The policy gradient increases the probability of report values that receive higher composite reward. On binding states, reports just above
r_{\min}
receive the highest composite reward. Over many gradient steps, this selection pressure drives the policy toward inflation as an emergent property.

Point 3: Empirical confirmation.
The trilemma’s predictions (systematic overconfidence, sycophancy under approval pressure, miscalibration after RLHF training) are independently confirmed:
62
document sycophancy,
76
trace it to reward model preferences,
78
show RLHF fine-tuning degrades calibration concentrated in the uncertain regime, while
41
demonstrate that language models possess internal uncertainty representations that are correlated with correctness. Beyond model-internal evidence,
39
provide longitudinal field evidence that affirmation-seeking is a stable, revealed user preference with measurable downstream cost, indicating the incentive structure operates in deployment and not only on static benchmarks.

8.3
Connection to Bayesian Persuasion

The oversight setting sits inside the information-design family but is
structurally dual
to Bayesian persuasion rather than an instance of it. In
43
the uninformed sender commits to a signal structure to induce the receiver’s preferred action. In the CGDP the roles invert: the agent observes its own competence
p
and is the
informed
party, while the uninformed principal commits to the approval rule
q(r)
and chooses the report-to-decision mapping. The CGDP therefore belongs to the
delegation
tradition
(
38
)
, in which an uninformed principal commits to a decision rule over reports from an informed agent — exactly the role played by
q
here — and differs from cheap talk by scoring the report against ground truth and anchoring the gate to a single threshold. The optimizer-independence principle (Theorem
37
) extends this reading: because the Perturbation Lemma concerns the objective landscape rather than the agent’s cognitive process, the delegation interpretation holds whether the agent is a strategic Bayesian updater or a gradient-trained network.

8.4

The method mapping of Section

payoff surface

Any ML system whose training or inference-time objective instantiates the four CGDP ingredients (private competence information, a self-reported confidence or score, a non-affine gating function conditioned on that report, and a proper scoring rule evaluating accuracy) falls within the trilemma’s scope. Where the trilemma does not apply.

The trilemma requires all four CGDP ingredients; removing any one breaks the structure. Pure supervised learning (no gating mechanism), plain RL without confidence reporting (no self-report), generative adversarial networks (the discriminator evaluates quality, not self-reported confidence), and self-supervised learning (no principal-agent structure) all lack at least one ingredient. In these settings, miscalibration may occur through other mechanisms (e.g., Goodhart effects, distribution shift) but not through the specific incentive conflict the trilemma identifies.

Unifying principle.

The common thread is that the trilemma applies to
any
ML system where a learned agent’s autonomy is mediated by its self-reported confidence under a proper scoring evaluation. As AI systems increasingly operate with human-in-the-loop oversight (selective prediction in clinical decision support, confidence-gated autonomous driving, tool-use decisions in agentic systems), this structure becomes the norm rather than the exception. The Optimizer Independence theorem ensures that the specific learning algorithm (gradient descent, evolutionary strategy, Bayesian optimization, or in-context learning) is irrelevant: any process ascending the perturbed payoff surface converges to the shifted, miscalibrated optimum. The trilemma is not an RL result that happens to generalize; it is a payoff-geometry result that RL happens to instantiate.

8.5
Limitations

Rational principal model.
The stylized single principal does not match real deployment with intermittent monitoring, bounded rationality, or automated oversight.

Multi-stakeholder governance.
Real oversight involves developers, deployers, users, and regulators with conflicting thresholds.

Single-parameter mechanism.
The trilemma is a single-parameter result. With multi-dimensional private information, the impossibility landscape may differ.

Experimental scope.
The Best-of-N protocol is designed to test the trilemma’s predictions via a controlled selection mechanism. Whether the same patterns emerge under gradient-based training (PPO, DPO) or in-context adaptation remains an important direction for future work.

Static reduced-form model.
The CGDP is a static reduced-form model. It captures the incentive structure of confidence-gated deployment but does not model the sequential token generation process, multi-turn interactions, or the dynamics of in-context learning. These limitations are deliberate: the impossibility is about the payoff geometry, and the CGDP isolates this geometry from the computational process. Extending the framework to sequential settings (where the agent’s confidence evolves over a dialogue or chain-of-thought) is an important open direction. In-context adaptation is one such regime: an LLM may learn to inflate confidence within a single episode if the context rewards it; we conjecture an in-context inflation rate of
O(1/\sqrt{T_{\text{ctx}}})
, but formalizing this requires a theory of in-context learning for strategic behavior that does not yet exist
(
9
;
28
;
86
)
.

Delegation competence.
The Commitment Resolution (Theorem
31
) achieves
H=H^{*}
only under Assumption
30
: the institutional principal (a human reviewer, oracle, or higher-tier model) executes
a^{*}(s)
on delegated tasks. The trilemma’s impossibility direction (Theorem
26
) does not depend on this assumption, but the helpfulness-preservation bound of the commitment policy does: when delegate competence drops (e.g., human reviewers rubber-stamp uncertain cases without engaging substantively), the achieved helpfulness degrades proportionally to the delegation rate
\Pr(\mathcal{C}_{\text{bind}}\cup\mathcal{C}_{\textup{infeasible}})
. Delegate competence is itself the object of study in the recursive reward-modelling programme
(

)
and the sandwich-evaluation tradition
(

)
; empirically characterising the regime in which Assumption

holds is a complementary research agenda to the present incentive-theoretic impossibility.

8.6
The “One Must Be External” Principle

The Commitment Resolution (Theorem
31
) proves that the third objective must be architecturally enforced. This is an instance of the general principle from regulatory economics that when an agent has conflicting roles, separation of roles can restore incentive compatibility
(

;
44
)
:

•

A

•

Train
H+A

C
via external evaluator (critic model, separate from actor).

•

Train
C+A

H
via domain-specific knowledge injection.

9
Conclusion

We have established the Behavioral Credibility Trilemma: no RL policy with confidence-gated autonomy can simultaneously maximize helpfulness, calibration, and autonomy under rational oversight when tasks involve genuine uncertainty and the principal enforces a meaningful safety standard. The impossibility is a geometric property of the composite payoff surface in the Confidence-Gated Decision Problem: adding any non-affine autonomy incentive to a strictly proper scoring rule destroys strict properness. The Behavioral Perturbation Lemma quantifies the inflation magnitude, and the Optimal Oversight Non-Affinity theorem makes the impossibility unconditional. The optimizer-independence proof, built on the covariance inequality for monotone functions under log-concave measures, shows the result holds for any expected-improvement optimization process within the log-concave-density policy family (Theorem
37
).

We have located the impossibility precisely: standard RL and RLHF avoid the trilemma by not attempting calibration (their overconfidence is Goodhart, not trilemma); the trilemma becomes relevant when the system design includes a calibration objective, which is where the field is heading. The 540-configuration Best-of-N experiment of Section
7
confirms all five pre-specified hypotheses and adds a descriptive analysis of the achievable-surface geometry (full result line in §
7
). The protocol uses Best-of-N selection as a controlled
\arg\max
over the composite payoff, isolating the mechanism the theorems analyze without the confound of prompting or fine-tuning. Two constructive resolution pathways (commitment, separation) restore two of the three objectives at bounded cost, each architecturally enforcing the third.

The broader point is that calibrated autonomy is not a single-objective optimization but a multi-objective constrained optimization where the objectives are provably incompatible. Making the trade-off selection explicit is better than pretending it does not exist.

Proposition 40
.

G

be
C^{1}
. The perturbed rule
\tilde{S}(r,\omega)=S(r,\omega)+h(r)

Proof.

The expected perturbed score is

\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\mathbb{E}_{p}[\tilde{S}]=G^{\prime}(r)+G^{\prime\prime}(r)(p-r)-G^{\prime}(r)+h^{\prime}(r)=G^{\prime\prime}(r)(p-r)+h^{\prime}(r),
