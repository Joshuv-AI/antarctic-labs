Delivery Routes and Anesthesia Affect Cord Blood NGF, BDNF, and Neonatal Outcomes
Authors: Şükran Doğru, Huriye Ezveci, Fikriye Karanfil Yaman, Pelin Bahçeci, Zeynep Şenol, İbrahim Kılınç, Kazım Gezginç
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the effects of delivery mode and anesthesia type on fetal cord blood levels of nerve growth factor (NGF) and brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), which are neurotrophins involved in neuronal survival, differentiation, and stress adaptation during the perinatal period.Materials MethodsIn this cross‐sectional, prospective study conducted from December 2023 to May 2024 at a single tertiary center, pregnant women were categorized based on delivery mode—vaginal delivery (VD) or cesarean section (CS). The CS group was subdivided according to anesthesia type: spinal or general. Cord blood NGF, BDNF, pH levels, and neonatal clinical outcomes were evaluated. Pregnant women with comorbidities and those receiving prenatal medical induction were excluded.ResultsThe study included 150 pregnant women. Fifty of these patients had VD and 100 had CS. Sixty of the pregnant women who had a CS received spinal anesthesia, and 40 received general anesthesia. Cord blood NGF (54.20 ± 32.04 vs. 47.01 ± 27.28, p = 0.176) and BDNF (80.98 ± 41.94 vs. 70.08 ± 37.53, p = 0.123) values were not different in those who delivered with CS and VD. Cord blood NGF and BDNF values were higher in the spinal anesthesia group (p = 0.002, p = 0.003). The cord blood pH value was higher in the spinal anesthesia group than in the VD and general anesthesia groups (p = 0.004).ConclusionCord blood NGF and BDNF values were higher in the spinal anesthesia group. These findings support the hypothesis that regional anesthesia may be associated with reduced fetal stress and improved biochemical adaptation at birth.

Introduction

Nerve growth factor (NGF) and brain‐derived neurotrophin (BDNF) are naturally produced molecules that play a role in the maturation of the brain and placenta. Oxidative stress throughout pregnancy may affect membrane fluidity, leading to alterations in the levels and expression of BDNF and NGF [1]. These neurotrophins improve axon growth, structural differentiation, and synaptic plasticity, as well as protect against neuronal apoptosis, thereby playing a critical role in the central nervous system's maturation process [2]. NGF and BDNF are not only essential for axonal growth and synaptic plasticity but also contribute to pulmonary development and neuroimmune regulation via the brain–lung axis. Recent studies, such as D'Amico et al. [3], emphasize the importance of neurotrophins in coordinating the development of the respiratory and nervous systems, particularly in response to perinatal stressors such as inflammation and hypoxia.

Stress induces modifications in the expression of neurotrophins in the central nervous system [4]. The delivery of a child is a physiological event that is notably stressful for both the mother and the infant. The newborn's metabolic and hormonal processes are influenced by the mode of delivery, which results in variations in the levels of proinflammatory, metabolic, and hormonal markers. Surgical stress induces a systemic immuno‐endocrine response and activates the sympathetic nervous system. Research indicates that the levels of these markers typically reach their highest point during vaginal delivery (VD), while they are comparatively lower during planned elective cesarean section (CS) [5].

Research indicates that anesthetics may have neurotoxic effects on fetal and neonatal development, which might have negative impacts on the developmental features of children. The cumulative neurotoxic consequences of anesthetic agents are potentially a risk to developing neural systems, indicating that the administration of anesthetic drugs should be minimized [6]. Studies have demonstrated that neuraxial blocks are associated with a reduced stress response to surgery and are superior to general anesthesia in terms of oxidative stress. The advantages of regional anesthesia are not limited to the positive impact on the outcomes of neonates and the promotion of maternal comfort and involvement. Some data suggest that regional anesthesia for CS deliveries is linked to a decrease in respiratory depression in the neonate. Compared to GA, it is even more preferable to well‐established APGAR scores [7]. The precise and objective measurement of the stress level experienced during various types of anesthesia, as well as the safety of neonates under various anesthesia methods, could be facilitated by stress indicators in the newborn. This is because stress indicators can be used to analyze the different varieties of anesthesia during CS. It is possible to accurately ascertain the most suitable and secure form of anesthesia for neonates by evaluating stress parameters [8].

Researchers have previously evaluated neurotrophins in the cord blood values of pregnant women with diabetes mellitus and in cases of preterm labor [9, 10, 11]. To our knowledge, no prior study has simultaneously compared the effects of VD, cesarean with spinal, and cesarean with general anesthesia on cord blood NGF and BDNF in a prospective design.

The neurotoxicity and stress caused by anesthesia in the fetus may have led to changes in neurotrophin levels in fetal blood samples, suggesting this possibility. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the effect of anesthesia and delivery method on the concentrations of NGF and BDNF, key neurotrophins involved in neuronal development and adaptive stress responses, in fetal cord blood and on neonatal outcomes.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participant Selection

This cross‐sectional, prospective study was performed from December 2023 to May 2024 in the obstetrics unit of the Obstetrics and Gynecology Clinic at Necmettin Erbakan University Faculty of Medicine. The study's design and the consent form received approval from the institutional ethics committee (ethics number: 2022/4069:12114). Patients were recorded when they were admitted to the maternity ward for delivery in the third trimester. This study included nulliparous and multiparous women aged 18–40 years with singleton pregnancies between 37 and 41 gestational weeks. Among the indications for CS, cephalopelvic disproportion, malpositions, and elective cases were included in the study. Women with systemic diseases, infections, multiple gestations, fetal anomalies, or complications such as preeclampsia or gestational diabetes were excluded. Cases that underwent medical induction for labor were excluded from the study. Cases with bleeding, cases with complicated labor, and fetal distress were excluded from the study. No anesthesia was used in any of the vaginal deliveries. Pregnant women who refused regional anesthesia or for whom spinal anesthesia was not technically accessible were given general anesthesia. None of the pregnant women in the study used omega‐3 supplements or prenatal steroids during pregnancy. We used the first day of the last menstrual period or the first measured crown‐rump length to calculate gestational age. The study divided the included cases into two categories based on their delivery modes: VD and CS. We divided the cases with CSs into those that received spinal and general anesthesia. Indications for CS included cephalopelvic disproportion, malpositions, and elective cases. Fentanyl and bupivacaine (10 μg and 13 mg) agents were used for spinal anesthesia. After the application of propofol (2 mg/kg) and rocuronium bromide (0.6–1 mg/kg) for general anesthesia, intubation was performed, and a remifentanil agent (0.05 μg/kg/min) was added after the delivery of the fetus. All patients received their anesthesia and dosages according to the guidelines of our clinic's anesthesia team.

Sample Collection and Measurement Techniques

Before the newborn took his/her first breath, umbilical artery blood taken from the umbilical cord 15 cm away after birth was examined. Standard clinical tubes containing lithium heparin were used to hold the samples collected at delivery time, before placenta evacuation. Blood samples were centrifuged in a Hettich Rotina 46R (HettichZentrifugen, Tuttlingen, Germany) brand refrigerated centrifuge at 4°C, 1000 g for 10 min to separate the serum. Serum samples were stored in a New Brunswick U570 (New Brunswick Scientific, New Jersey, USA) refrigerator at −80°C until BDNF and NGF levels were studied. The lab team didn't know which patient group the material was taken from.

Laboratory Analyses

BDNF and NGF levels in serum samples were measured by the enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) method in the Medical Biochemistry Research Laboratory of the Faculty of Medicine. A Human BDNF (RE2848H, ReedBiotech Ltd., Wuhan, China) kit was used to measure serum BDNF and NGF levels. Serum BDNF levels were measured by the double antibody sandwich ELISA method according to the manufacturer's instructions. A Biotek ELX 50 microplate washer (BioTek Instruments, Vermont, USA) was used to wash ELISA kits. The Bio‐Rad Microplate absorbance reader xMark (Bio‐Rad Laboratories, California, USA) system was used for spectrophotometric measurements. Serum BDNF and NGF results were calculated as “pg/mL” according to absorbance‐concentration calibration graphs. All samples were analyzed in duplicate. The assay kits were calibrated with international reference standards provided by the manufacturer, ensuring measurement reliability and reproducibility across batches. The intra‐assay and inter‐assay coefficients of variation (CVs) for the BDNF and NGF kits were < 10% and < 12%, respectively, as reported by the manufacturer. Calibration was performed using international standards supplied with the kits.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed with SPSS version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). We used one‐way ANOVA to conduct between‐group comparisons of normally distributed variables for independent samples. Otherwise, Kruskall–Wallis or Mann–Whitney U‐Tests were implemented. Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for the risk of type I error due to multiple hypothesis testing. The chi‐square test was used to compare categorical data, and values were given as n (%). Spearman correlation test was performed to evaluate the relationship between variables. In order to determine the sample number in the study, 51 cases were found for each group with 80% power, 0.05 alpha margin of error, and 0.5 effect size using the G‐power 3.1.9.7 method.

Results

The study included a total of 150 pregnant women. Fifty of these patients had VD and 100 had CS. Sixty of the pregnant women who had a CS received spinal anesthesia, and 40 received general anesthesia. The mean age of pregnant women who had VD was 28.16 ± 5.08, while the mean age of pregnant women who had CS was 29.83 ± 6.13 (p = 0.080). Body mass index, gravida and parity number, fetal gender, birth weight, cord blood pH values, and NICU admission rates of pregnant women who had VD were not statistically different from those who had CS (p > 0.05). The 1st and 5th minute APGAR values of pregnant women who delivered via CS were higher than those of VD (p = 0.001). Although cord blood NGF (54.20 ± 32.04 vs. 47.01 ± 27.28, p = 0.176) and BDNF (80.98 ± 41.94 vs. 70.08 ± 37.53, p = 0.123) values of pregnant women who delivered via CS were higher, they were not statistically different from those who delivered VD (Table 1).

Discussion

This study evaluated the effects of delivery route and anesthesia choice on cord blood NGF and BDNF values and neonatal outcomes. Cord blood NGF and BDNF values of pregnant women who underwent VD were lower than those who underwent CS, but this was not statistically significant. In patients undergoing spinal anesthesia, the APGAR scores at the 1st and 5th minute, as well as the pH, NGF, and BDNF levels in cord blood, were significantly elevated compared to those in vaginal deliveries and CSs with general anesthesia. This indicates an association between neonatal stress and the cord blood levels of NGF and BDNF. Recent studies further support the relevance of cord blood biochemical markers in neonatal stress and inflammation as indicators of early adaptive responses [12, 13].

BDNF belongs to the neurotrophic growth factor family and shares 52% of its structure with NGF. They control many target neurons' growth, repair, and survival [14]. Maternal BDNF may support the development of the fetal central nervous system by crossing the uteroplacental barrier to reach the fetal brain [15]. Numerous studies have shown that NGF and BDNF play a role in various maternal pathological conditions, including preeclampsia and diabetes [16, 17]. D'Souza investigated BDNF levels in maternal and fetal cord blood from normotensive or preeclamptic women in 2014. Cord BDNF levels were elevated in women with preeclampsia delivering at term, but they were decreased in those delivering preterm [18]. The same researcher showed that cord blood NGF levels increased in preeclampsia and stated that NGF played a compensatory role [16]. It is assumed that the increase in BDNF concentration in the umbilical cord blood of smoking mothers is associated with an active response to hypoxia resulting from impaired placenta function [19].

We have previously encountered only one study evaluating fetal cord blood BDNF with a choice of delivery method and anesthesia. This study revealed higher cord blood BDNF values in vaginal deliveries compared to cesarean deliveries. In the same study, although cord blood BDNF values did not significantly differ in those receiving anesthesia, cord blood BDNF was higher in CS deliveries receiving regional anesthesia [20]. A woman's rising NGF level during labor is likely reflected in her baby's cord blood [21]. This makes us wonder if NGF released into the plasma during these stressful times plays a part in activating the body's normal and classical hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis response to stress. In our study, while VD and CS birth cord blood BDNF were not different, the increase in cord blood BDNF and NGF in spinal anesthesia may be an adaptive response to acute hypoxia (due to hypotension and anesthetic drugs). The role of BDNF in multiple components of cerebral function, particularly its neuronal connectivity‐promoting and apoptosis‐inhibiting features, suggests that it is involved in the neurohormonal cascade of birth. Additionally, spinal anesthesia induces sympathetic blockade that can lead to hypotension and changes in uteroplacental hemodynamics, potentially affecting placental perfusion and fetal oxygenation. Although fetuses generally compensate for transient reductions in uteroplacental blood flow, measurable changes in umbilical vessel resistance and cerebral blood flow indices have been described in the context of spinal anesthesia–induced hypotension, indicating a physiological stress on the fetus. Such hemodynamic perturbations may trigger adaptive upregulation of neurotrophins, which are known to be neuroprotective and involved in cell survival and differentiation in hypoxic and stress conditions.

The dominant perspective in clinical practice is that of approving CS under regional anesthesia, predominantly SA [22]. The cumulative neurotoxic effect of anesthetic agents is assumed to pose a risk to developing neural structures, suggesting that minimizing the number of anesthetic drugs administered is desirable [23]. At present, the precise impact of medications administered during GA on the neonatal brain and subsequent cognitive function has not been adequately clarified, highlighting the rationale for minimizing their use whenever possible. Neuraxial anesthesia serves as an effective tool for achieving this goal [24]. Regional anesthesia for CS deliveries is linked to decreased respiratory depression in newborns. It demonstrates superiority over established APGAR scores when compared to general anesthesia [7]. Though mother oxygen partial pressure and saturation were greater in general anesthesia than in regional anesthesia, Tonni et al. found that umbilical cord blood pH was lower in the general anesthesia group than in the spinal and epidural groups. It is assumed that newborns delivered under general anesthesia undergo temporary respiratory depression as a result of anesthetics given to the mother passing through the placental barrier and entering the circulation of the fetus [25]. As in our study, Uram‐Benka et al. [12] reported in their observation of 150 patients that natural birth on neonates caused more stress and immune responses, whereas spinal anesthesia protected against this and showed lower stress levels compared to general anesthesia during CS. Rakiç et al. [13] examined the effects of delivery type and anesthesia on neonatal oxidative stress values in their study on 150 pregnant women and found that the lowest pH was in infants born via VD, and when general anesthesia was compared with spinal anesthesia, general anesthesia was associated with lower pH. In our study, the lowest cord blood pH value was in vaginal deliveries. Cord blood pH was the highest in spinal CSs. Despite our previous statements, in our study, the good neonatal outcomes of spinal anesthesia and its less stressful potential compared to VD may have preserved BDNF and NGF values. Many studies compare the fetal cord blood oxygenation and pH results of general and regional anesthesia during CS. Contrary to our study, Szymanowski et al. examined the effects of anesthesia on cord blood parameters. They found that cord blood oxygenation was higher in cases receiving general anesthesia than in those receiving regional anesthesia. Regional anesthesia may reduce the fetus's oxygen delivery [26]. They argued that this was due to outdated databases, impaired oxygenation under regional anesthesia, and the medications administered (such as ephedrine). A 2012 review indicated that regional and general anesthesia showed comparable neonatal outcomes, with no evidence supporting the superiority of regional anesthesia over general anesthesia [22]. On December 14, 2016, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) issued a warning that exposure to certain anesthetic agents used for general anesthesia during the third trimester of pregnancy may lead to impaired brain development in children. Short‐term fetal exposure to general anesthesia during cesarean delivery was not found to be associated with learning disabilities [27].

The study's limitations include its limited patient population, single‐center design, lack of consideration for maternal nutrition, and absence of postnatal neurodevelopmental follow‐up for these newborns. The CS rate in our cohort was relatively high, reflecting national trends in Turkey. This limits the generalizability of the findings, as the predominance of CS deliveries may influence the observed associations between anesthesia type and cord blood neurotrophins. Another important limitation is the lack of assessment for maternal omega‐3 fatty acid status and baseline inflammation, both of which are known modulators of neurotrophin expression. Evidence from both animal and human studies suggests that higher maternal omega‐3 intake correlates with increased NGF and BDNF levels in the fetus, as well as better neurodevelopmental outcomes.

Longitudinal follow‐up studies evaluating neurodevelopmental outcomes are needed to determine the clinical relevance of elevated NGF and BDNF in cord blood. Assessing maternal nutritional status, omega‐3 fatty acid intake, and baseline inflammatory markers would further clarify factors modulating neurotrophin expression. Such studies could establish whether spinal anesthesia confers lasting neuroprotective effects on offspring.

Conclusion

BDNF and NGF are neuropeptides that are effective in neonatal neuronal development. While cord blood values do not differ between VD and CS cases, it is significant that high BDNF and NGF values correlate with high cord blood pH and APGAR scores in cases that will already undergo CS and spinal anesthesia. To fully understand the impact of neurotrophins on pregnancy outcomes and the development of the offspring's brain, further long‐term research involving a large number of pregnant women is required.
