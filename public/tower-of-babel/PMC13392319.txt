Mitophagy suggesting a possible association with oocyte maturation: A study of granulosa cells from follicular fluid of normoresponder and diminished ovarian reserve cases
Authors: Çağrı Öner, Damla Kolcuoğlu, Nergis Özlem Kiliç, Defne Dumanli, Ahmet Can Çelik, Alara Kaya, Baturalp Bakirci, Sena Köstü, Ece Bilge Şimşek, Belgin Selam, Ertuğrul Çolak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study investigated mitophagy, a kind of mitochondrial dysfunction, as a biomarker for oocyte maturation by analyzing granulosa cells from follicular fluid in normoresponder (NOR) and diminished ovarian reserve (DOR) cases. The aim was to evaluate the expression of mitochondrial dynamics genes, Mitofusin-1 (MFN-1), Mitofusin-2 (MFN-2), and PARKIN, in granulosa cells surrounding oocytes at Metaphase I (MI) and Metaphase II (MII) stages.MethodsIn this experimental study, follicular fluid samples were collected from 30 NOR and 30 DOR women undergoing IVF and subdivided according to MI and MII oocyte maturation stages. Granulosa cells were isolated, morphologically assessed, and analyzed for gene expression using qRT-PCR.ResultsNOR MII granulosa cells exhibited the healthiest morphology and highest cell numbers, while DOR MI samples showed the most degeneration. MFN-1 expression was significantly decreased in DOR MI compared to NOR MI, while MFN-2 was higher in DOR MI and NOR MII. PARKIN expression was lower in DOR compared to NOR in both MI and MII. Correlation analysis revealed strong positive relationships among MFN-1, MFN-2, and PARKIN in NOR MII.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that mitophagy-related gene expression profiles in granulosa cells may serve as non-invasive biomarkers for predicting oocyte quality and embryo development in IVF.Keywords: follicular fluid, MFN-1, MFN-2, normoresponder, PARKIN, diminished ovarian reserve

Introduction

Mitochondria, the powerhouse of the cell, are integral to maintaining cellular energy balance and facilitating critical cellular processes, including metabolism, apoptosis, and signal transduction. Mitochondrial dynamics, regulated by processes such as fusion and fission, are fundamental to mitochondrial function. 1 Among the proteins involved in mitochondrial fusion are Mitofusin-1 (MFN-1) and Mitofusin-2 (MFN-2), both of which are GTPases that mediate the merging of mitochondrial membranes to maintain mitochondrial integrity and energy production. 2 While MFN-1 is primarily involved in mitochondrial fusion in various cell types, MFN-2 plays a critical role in neurons and oocytes, particularly in the regulation of mitochondrial distribution and function.3,4

The selection of MFN-1, MFN-2, and PARKIN was driven by their complementary roles at the intersection of mitochondrial network remodeling (fusion) and mitochondrial quality control (mitophagy), two processes repeatedly highlighted in contemporary reviews as key determinants of follicular function, oocyte competence, and ovarian aging.5–7 In particular, mitofusins are established outer-mitochondrial membrane GTPases required for fusion, and a recent integrative review focusing on fertility summarizes accumulating mammalian evidence that MFN-dependent dynamics influence germ-cell development, oocyte maturation, and early embryogenesis, positioning MFN-1/MFN-2 as fertility-relevant fusion nodes rather than purely “structural” proteins. 6 Importantly, mechanistic work further supports that MFN2 can act in a context-dependent manner, with distinct upstream signals shifting MFN2’s functional contribution toward either mitochondrial fusion or MFN2-associated mitophagy programs. 8 In parallel, mitophagy signaling through the PINK1–PARKIN axis is a canonical pathway for tagging damaged mitochondria for removal, and ovarian autophagy/mitophagy reviews emphasize that dysregulated mitochondrial clearance can contribute to granulosa-cell dysfunction, follicular atresia, and diminished ovarian reserve phenotypes.5,7 Consistent with this framework, animal-model work has shown that oxidative stress can engage PINK1/PARKIN-associated mitophagy in granulosa cells and that experimental modulation of this pathway alters granulosa-cell survival under injury conditions. 9 Together, this review- and animal-supported evidence justifies targeting MFN-1/MFN-2 (fusion machinery) alongside PARKIN (a mitophagy effector) as a compact panel to capture coordinated alterations in mitochondrial maintenance that may underlie differences between NOR and DOR phenotypes across MI and MII maturation stages.

In the process of mitochondrial quality control, PARKIN, an E3 ubiquitin ligase, plays a pivotal role in mitophagy, the selective degradation of damaged or dysfunctional mitochondria through autophagic pathways. This mechanism is essential for preserving cellular health and function, as defective mitochondria can lead to oxidative stress and contribute to various pathologies, including neurodegenerative diseases and impaired fertility.10,11 In the context of reproductive health, mitophagy has been shown to influence oocyte quality and ovarian reserve, making it a critical factor in fertility.

Follicular aspiration fluid (FF), obtained during assisted reproductive techniques such as in vitro fertilization (IVF), is increasingly being studied for its potential to provide insights into the quality of oocytes and the ovarian microenvironment. The mitochondrial content within the follicular fluid is of particular interest, as it may reflect the metabolic and functional state of the oocytes. Several studies have suggested that mitochondrial dysfunction, including impaired mitophagy and mitochondrial fusion, can impact oocyte maturation and embryo development, thus influencing fertility outcomes.12,13

The concept of Normoresponder (NOR) versus diminished ovarian reserve (DOR) cases refers to the different ovarian responses during controlled ovarian stimulation (COS) in IVF. Normoresponder cases are typically individuals who show a standard ovarian response to stimulation, leading to an adequate number of oocytes for fertilization. In contrast, cases with diminished ovarian reserve often exhibit suboptimal responses, characterized by a reduced number of oocytes retrieved and decreased fertility potential. This diminished ovarian reserve is frequently associated with mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and impaired mitophagy, which contributes to the reduced quality of oocytes and the overall decline in reproductive potential.13,14

This study aims to explore the roles of mitochondrial dynamics, particularly the functions of Mitofusin-1 (MFN-1) and Mitofusin-2 (MFN-2), as well as the role of PARKIN-mediated mitophagy in ovarian function. Understanding these molecular pathways could provide valuable insights into ovarian aging and dysfunction of mitochondria, with potential applications in improving fertility outcomes, especially in cases with diminished ovarian reserves.

Despite growing interest in mitochondrial dysfunction in infertility, evidence directly linking mitochondrial fusion–mitophagy signaling in human follicular-fluid–derived cumulus–granulosa (COC) cells to specific oocyte maturation stages remains limited. In particular, few studies have stratified normoresponder (NOR) and diminished ovarian reserve (DOR) patients by Metaphase I (MI) versus Metaphase II (MII) oocytes using cells obtained from follicular aspiration fluid—a routinely discarded, non-invasive clinical material. To address this gap, we isolated COC/granulosa cells from follicular fluid of 30 NOR and 30 DOR IVF patients and analyzed MFN-1, MFN-2, and PARKIN expression by qRT-PCR in MI and MII subgroups (n=15 each). Our results show stage- and phenotype-dependent signatures, including significantly reduced MFN-1 in DOR MI versus NOR MI, altered MFN-2 patterns across MI/MII, and consistently lower PARKIN in DOR compared with NOR at both maturation stages, alongside the healthiest morphology and highest cell numbers in NOR MII and the most degeneration in DOR MI. Notably, strong positive correlations among MFN-1, MFN-2, and PARKIN were observed in NOR MII,15,16 suggesting coordinated regulation of mitochondrial dynamics during successful maturation. Collectively, these findings position follicular-fluid–derived COC cell mitophagy-related gene profiles as a practical, non-invasive biomarker candidate to differentiate NOR versus DOR biology and to inform oocyte maturation competence in IVF.

Materials and methods

Isolation of cumulus cells from follicular fluid

Women referred to Bahçeci Health Group Umut IVF Center between April 2024, and August 2025 were included in the study. Samples were collected with the approval of Maltepe University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (protocol code: 2024/06-08; approval date: 14 March 2024, and protocol code: 2025/19-11; approval date: 02 October 2025), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975 as revised in 2024. The reporting of this study was guided by the STROBE statement, where applicable. Among the cases included in the study, the mean age of women was 32.7 ± 3.5 years in the NOR group and 39.1 ± 5.4 years in the DOR group, indicating that the DOR group consisted of significantly older women. Similarly, the age of the men was 35.1 ± 5.0 years in the NOR group and 40.3 ± 5.0 years in the DOR group, showing a significant increase in the DOR group. When oocyte counts were evaluated, 2.3 ± 1.6 oocytes were obtained in the MI stage and 7.3 ± 2.0 oocytes in the MII stage in the NOR group; these values ranged from 1.1 ± 0.36 for MI to 2.4 ± 1.5 for MII in the DOR group, demonstrating a clear advantage for the NOR group. Fertilization rates also show a similar distribution; they were recorded as 0.35 ± 0.74 for NOR MI, 6.07 ± 1.77 for NOR MII, 0.50 ± 0.65 for DOR MI, and 2.35 ± 1.28 for DOR MII. Embryo development rates were 50% for NOR MI, 77.8% for NOR MII, 28.6% for DOR MI, and 35.7% for DOR MII, clearly showing that the NOR MII group had the highest development potential.

Follicular aspiration fluid (FF) samples were obtained from 30 Normoresponder (NOR) and 30 cases with diminished ovarian reserve (DOR) admitted to İstanbul Bahçeci Umut IVF Center. Each of the NOR and DOR groups was subgrouped as Metaphase I (n= 15, MI) and Metaphase II (n= 15, MII). Samples were centrifuged to separate blood from granulosa cells. Cases with 1–7 oocytes were classified as DOR, while those with 8 or more oocytes were NOR. The follicular fluid was obtained from multiple follicles from two ovaries per woman. The ideal follicular volume for a high production of quality blastocysts is 13–23 mm (about 1–6 ml). In this study, the size of follicles of NOR cases were approximately 16–22 mm, however the approximate size of DOR follicles were 13–17 mm or 15–17 mm (above 2 ml).

The inclusion criteria for the NOR (control group) comprised individuals with an antral follicle count (AFC) ranging from 7 to 12 and anti-Mullerian hormone (AMH) levels between 1.0 and 3.5 ng/mL. The selection criteria required both ovaries to be present and visible on ultrasound, while anyone who had previously undergone ovarian surgery was excluded. The following requirements must be fulfilled by DOR cases: AMH values<1ng/mL, AFC <5-7.

During the oocyte pickup (OPU) process, cumulus oophorous complex (COC) cells were isolated from the follicular fluid (FF) of NOR and DOR patients during the MI and MII stages. Cumulus cells (CCs) and corona radiata cells, which make up the structure of cumulus oophorous complex (COC) cells, were removed from each individual using the denuding procedure. COC cells were manually detached from oocytes using delicate glass pipettes while under stereomicroscopy. COC cells were gently pipetted up and down in TCM199 with 20% fetal calf serum (FCS) and 80 IU hyaluronidase/ml. We employed a stereomicroscope (Nikon SMZ-1500, Berlin, Germany) to observe the oocyte meiotic stage after removing COC cells. The COC cells from the collected cases were put in centrifuge tubes in their FFs individually based on MI and MII oocytes. Subsequently, FF containing COC cells was subjected to centrifugation at +4 ◦C and 2800 rpm for 20 minutes. The supernatant was removed, and pellets containing COC cells were centrifuged at 1850 rpm at room temperature for 5 minutes in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS).

For removing blood contamination, COCs are exposed to hyaluronidase, which detaches cumulus cells from the oocytes. The mixture is subsequently divided into smaller RBCs and bigger cumulus cells using a pipette. The recovered cumulus cell pellets are then washed several times. The saline (PBS) to eliminate any leftover RBCs and impurities.

Hematoxylin-eosin staining

After we isolated the COC cells, COC cells were counted by Thoma Slide. After counting, 7×103 cells from each experimental group were spread onto a polylysine-coated slide and fixed with formaldehyde. Once the COC cells were fixed onto slides, the slides were rinsed with tap water. Then, the hematoxylin dye was treated to slides for 10 minutes at room temperature. Afterwards, the slides were washed by tap water and were incubated with eosin stain for 3 min at 37°C. Then the slides were washed by tap water again. After the final wash, slides were mounted and imaged at 4X, 10X, and 20X magnification under a light microscope.

Total RNA isolation from granulosa cells

Total RNA was isolated following the manufacturer’s kit protocol (Nucleogene, Turkey). The yield of RNA was quantified (BMG-Labtech, Germany) and samples were stored at -20°C for one day.

Reverse transcription and real time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA extracted from NOR and DOR samples was reverse transcribed into cDNA according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Nucleogene, Turkey), with incubation at 42°C for 60 min. followed by 95°C for 5 min. The quality of total RNA isolated from samples belonging to the NOR and DOR groups was assessed using spectrophotometric measurements; samples with an A260/280 ratio in the range of 1.8–2.1 and low A414 absorbance, a marker of hemolysis, were included in the analysis. MFN-1, MFN-2, and PARKIN gene expression (Table 1; BmLabosis, Turkey) were determined by Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR; Qiagen, USA) using 5 min of pre-denaturation at 95°C, 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 10 s., and 30 s. of annealing/extension at 60°C (Nucleogene, Turkey). The reaction mixture was prepared in a total volume of 20 µL and consisted of 10 µL of 2X qPCR Master Mix, 0.4 µL of forward primer (10 µM), 0.4 µL of reverse primer (10 µM), 2 µL of cDNA, and nuclease-free water to make up the volume. Each sample was run in triplicate. The ΔCT values were determined using the collected data [11] Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH; BmLabosis, Turkey) was used as an internal control. Amplification specificity was confirmed by the presence of a single peak via melting curve analysis. The absence of amplification in negative control (NTC) reactions indicated that there was no contamination. In this method, the CT value of the target gene for each sample was normalized using the GAPDH gene as an internal reference, and then the relative changes in expression between the NOR and DOR groups were calculated.

Statistical evaluations

The data obtained was determined using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 package program. In statistical evaluations, the normal distribution of the groups was determined using Kolmogrov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests, and the differences between the groups were determined using independent student t test. Correlations were determined statistically by applying Pearson’s Correlation test. Descriptive statistics for the data are reported as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. * for p<0.01, ** for p<0.05, and *** for p<0.001 were used to show the differences between groups on figures. p>0.05 was used for not significant samples. To minimize the margin of error in all analyses, the calculations were cross-checked by independent researchers.

Gene expression analysis

According to the data obtained, the MFN-1 gene expression decreased in DOR MI (-6.454 ± 3.565) compared to the NOR MI (-1.080 ± 2.683; p<0.001; Figure 2(a)). There were not any other statistically significant changes about MFN-1 gene expression in DOR MII (-4.727 ± 5.668) and NOR MII (-1.937 ± 3.262); p>0.05; Figure 2(a)). There were downregulations in MFN-2 gene expression not only in between NOR MI (-13.347 ± 14.553) compared to NOR MII (-2.339 ± 2.535) but also in between NOR MI to DOR MI (-1.717 ± 3.637; p<0.001; Figure 2(b)). Furthermore, there was a decrease in DOR MII (-3.807 ± 2.466) compared to DOR MI (p<0.05; Figure 2(b)). According to obtained PARKIN gene expression data, a significance decrease observed in DOR MI (-15.165 ± 4.230) compared to DOR MII (0.987 ± 3.302) and NOR MI (5.822±4.444; p<0.001; Figure 2(c)). Furthermore, this kind of decrease also was determined between DOR MII and NOR MII (6.567 ± 3.832; p<0.001; Figure 2(c)). However, the difference between NOR MI and NOR MII could not be determined statistically (p>0.05; S2; Figure 2(c)).

Discussion

Ovarian reserve, a critical determinant of female reproductive potential, is profoundly influenced by the integrity and functionality of mitochondrial dynamics within oocytes and granulosa cells. 17 The delicate balance between mitochondrial fusion, mediated by proteins such as MFN-1 and MFN-2, and fission, regulated by proteins like dynamin-related protein 1, is essential for maintaining mitochondrial health and energy production in these critical reproductive cells. 17 Dysregulation of these processes can lead to compromised oocyte quality and diminished ovarian reserve, 5 with proteomic studies highlighting alterations in key mitochondrial proteins in DOR cases. 18 Indeed, investigations into granulosa cells from non-elderly women with DOR have revealed a significant downregulation of numerous proteins, with a substantial proportion localized to the mitochondria, further underscoring the role of mitochondrial dysfunction in this condition. 18 Furthermore, diminished ovarian reserve, frequently associated with delayed childbearing, often manifests with reduced mitochondrial DNA copy numbers and lower anti-Müllerian hormone levels, both of which correlate with advanced maternal age. 19 This decline in ovarian function is characterized by a decrease in the quantity and quality of oocytes, alongside an elevation in basal follicle-stimulating hormone levels. 20 These changes collectively contribute to impaired oocyte developmental competence, impacting embryo quality and subsequent pregnancy rates. 17 Vale-Fernandes et al. (2025) in Mol Cell Endocrinol have highlighted the significance of follicular fluid profiling, including AMH and mitochondrial dysfunction markers, in understanding PCOS-related reproductive impairments. 5

Research on the effect of mitophagy on diseases and phenomena is increasing day by day. Mitochondrial fission and fusion mechanisms seen in mitophagy are two main mechanisms that are effective in the degeneration of mitochondria. 21 MFN-1 and MFN-2 genes are important key molecules involved in the regulation of these mitochondrial dynamics. These molecules are regulated at the cellular level by the PARKIN protein, which is also a degeneration marker.6,22 Although diminished ovarian reserve is one of the most unknown phenomena seen in vitro fertilization clinics, the cellular mechanisms underlying its occurrence have not yet been fully elucidated. The most well-known situation in DOR cases is the high level of degeneration and the very low rate of progression to MII after ICSI.7,23 For these reasons, this study investigates the potential of mitochondria, the heart of the cell, and its dynamics to inform us about the differential early diagnosis, especially in cases of DOR.24,25 In addition, this research is planned to be conducted using COC cells, especially granulosa cells, which are found in the follicular aspiration fluid, a waste fluid during oocyte preparation in IVF centers, and whose main function is to nourish the oocyte. The aim is that direct waste material can be used for non-invasive diagnosis without any possible damage to the oocyte.

Limitations of the study

The sample size was modest (60 cases; total; n=15 per subgroup), which may limit statistical power, particularly for detecting smaller stage-specific effects, and increases the likelihood that some null findings reflect limited sensitivity rather than true absence of differences. In addition, samples were collected from a single IVF center (Istanbul Bahçeci Umut IVF Center), and patient demographic/clinical characteristics (e.g., age distribution, ethnicity, body mass index, infertility etiology, and stimulation protocols) may not represent broader IVF populations. Future multicenter studies with larger and more demographically diverse cohorts and integrated functional readouts are needed to confirm generalizability and clinical utility. Limited demographic diversity and single-center recruitment can restrict the external validity of the results and may influence mitochondrial/mitophagy-related gene expression through unmeasured confounding. Moreover, the study measured transcript levels but did not assess protein expression or functional mitochondrial outcomes, which constrain mechanistic interpretation. This may limit the validity of the results in different geographical regions or in different IVF centers. The genetic analyses (qRT-PCR) used in the study evaluated the expression of specific genes (MFN-1, MFN-2 and PARKIN). However, other potentially important genes or molecular pathways have not been evaluated. The study assessed gene expression levels but did not examine the functional effects (protein expressions) of these genes or their consequences at the cellular level. This may limit a full understanding of the effects of mitophagy on oocyte quality and ovarian reserve. The study did not assess the association between gene expression levels and clinical outcomes (e.g. pregnancy rates, live birth rates). These limitations are important factors to consider in interpreting the results of the study.

Importantly, this work should be interpreted as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating study. Accordingly, the observed stage- and phenotype-associated expression patterns of MFN-1, MFN-2, and PARKIN in follicular-fluid–derived COC cells require external validation in independent, ideally multicenter cohorts (and in relation to clinical IVF endpoints) before these markers can be considered for routine prognostic use.

According to data obtained, it was observed that the expression of all three genes was lower in DOR samples compared to DOR samples when analyzed on a group basis. This is due to the number of oocytes and oocyte degeneration. Low MFN-1 and MFN-2 gene expressions compared to high PARKIN gene expression in each group indicate that mitophagy occurs in these syndromes via PARKIN/MFN-1/MFN-2. Since the DOR MI group (NOR MI, NOR MII and DOR MII) had the lowest number of oocytes and the highest number of degenerated oocytes, the excessive decrease in PARKIN gene is an expected finding. However, the fact that there was no statistical difference between mitochondrial degeneration MFN-1/PARKIN in the DOR MI group suggests that mitophagy develops perinuclearly through MFN-2/PARKIN interaction. MFN-1 gene expression was observed to be more useful in distinguishing between subgroups that make up the main groups such as NOR MI and NOR MII or DOR MI and DOR MII. The expression profiles of MFN-2 and PARKIN genes are thought to provide a more meaningful idea about which of the NOR and DOR syndromes may be present compared to MFN-1. While the MFN-2 gene expression profile is considered to be a strong genetic biomarker between NOR MI and DOR MI samples, PARKIN gene expression profile is considered to be a stronger genetic marker between not only NOR MI and DOR MI but also NOR MII and DOR MII.

MFN-1 and MFN-2 are mitochondrial outer membrane proteins that mediate mitochondrial fusion, maintaining mitochondrial network integrity and function. PARKIN is an E3 ubiquitin ligase, recruited to mitochondria by PINK1 upon mitochondrial damage. PARKIN ubiquitinates MFN-1 and MFN-2, marking damaged mitochondria for degradation via mitophagy. PINK1-PARKIN pathway is central to mitochondrial quality control: PINK1 senses mitochondrial depolarization, recruits PARKIN, which then ubiquitinates MFN-1 and MFN-2, leading to mitophagy and removal of dysfunctional mitochondria.26,27 Figure 3 also presents the correlation analysis among these genes, demonstrating strong positive correlations in NOR MII samples, moderate correlations in DOR MI and DOR MII groups, and weak or non-significant correlations in NOR MI. These results suggest coordinated mitochondrial fusion and mitophagy in NOR MII, selective activation of mitophagy in DOR MI, and a shift towards fusion over mitophagy in DOR MII (Figure 2(a)–(d)).

In healthy cells, MFN-1 and MFN-2 mediate mitochondrial fusion, maintaining mitochondrial integrity and energy production. PARKIN targets MFN-1 and MFN-2 for ubiquitination, marking damaged mitochondria for degradation via mitophagy. 8 In NOR MI granulosa cells showed healthier morphology and higher cell numbers compared to DOR MI but were less healthy than NOR MII. This group represents a baseline state, with mitochondrial fusion predominating and minimal activation of mitophagy. According to the data obtained, MFN-1 and MFN-2 gene expressions were relatively higher, while PARKIN gene expression was low, indicating that mitochondrial quality control is functioning at a baseline level, with minimal stress or damage to mitochondria. Our results were supported by the weak correlations between MFN-1, MFN-2, and PARKIN suggest that the pathway is not strongly activated, reflecting healthy mitochondrial turnover (Figure 4). In NOR MII granulosa cells exhibited the highest cell number and healthiest morphology among all groups. Furthermore, there was coordinated upregulation of MFN-1, MFN-2, and PARKIN gene expressions, indicating efficient mitochondrial fusion and mitophagy. This suggests robust mitochondrial quality control, likely in response to increased metabolic demand or mild stress during oocyte maturation. Moreover, the strong positive correlations among MFN-1, MFN-2, and PARKIN were observed, reflecting coordinated activation of fusion and mitophagy machinery (Figure 4).

In DOR MI granulosa cells showed the most degeneration and lowest cell numbers, indicating poor oocyte quality. MFN-1 expression was significantly decreased, while MFN-2 was higher compared to NOR MI. PARKIN gene expression was also significantly lower in DOR MI compared to NOR MI and DOR MII. These changes suggest selective activation of mitophagy, possibly due to increased mitochondrial damage or stress. Moderate correlation between MFN-1 and PARKIN, but weaker for other pairs, indicating that MFN-1 may be more involved in recruiting PARKIN for mitophagy in this group (Figure 4). In DOR MII granulosa cells were healthier than DOR MI but still showed signs of degeneration compared to NOR groups. Fusion (MFN-1 and MFN-2) was favored over mitophagy (PARKIN), with reduced or blocked PARKIN activity. There was a negative or non-significant correlation between MFN-1 and PARKIN, suggesting a shift in pathway dynamics. Furthermore, moderate correlation between MFN-1 and MFN-2, but negative or non-significant correlation with PARKIN, indicating that mitochondrial maintenance relies more on fusion than on removal of damaged mitochondria, or a dysfunction in the PINK1-PARKIN pathway. These findings highlight the importance of mitochondrial dynamics and mitophagy in oocyte quality and ovarian reserve, suggesting that gene expression profiles of MFN-1, MFN-2, and PARKIN in granulosa cells can serve as potential biomarkers for predicting embryo development and fertility outcomes (Figure 4).

The research findings demonstrate that FF, an effluent, exhibits potential for distinguishing significant genetic variations among diverse female infertility syndromes. Furthermore, there are significant differences between NOR and DOR cases in terms of mitophagy, and these differences can be used to provide general information about the development of the embryo before ICSI (Intracytoplasmic Sperm Injection) and the genetic prediction of which syndromes cases have.

Conclusions

In this study, our conclusions are derived from three complementary evidence streams obtained from follicular-fluid–derived COC cells stratified by responder status (NOR vs DOR) and oocyte maturation stage (MI vs MII). First, morphological assessment (H&E) and relative cell abundance indicated stage- and phenotype-associated differences, with the healthiest appearance/highest cell numbers in NOR MII and the greatest degeneration in DOR MI. Second, qRT-PCR profiling demonstrated subgroup-specific expression patterns of mitochondrial fusion and mitophagy-related genes, including significantly reduced MFN-1 in DOR MI versus NOR MI, altered MFN-2 across MI/MII and NOR/DOR strata, and lower PARKIN in DOR compared with NOR at both maturation stages. Third, Pearson correlation analysis showed that coordination among MFN-1, MFN-2, and PARKIN was strongest in NOR MII, supporting a model of more tightly coupled mitochondrial maintenance programs in the subgroup with the most favorable cellular morphology. Collectively, these data provide exploratory, hypothesis-generating support that mitophagy/fusion gene-expression signatures measured in follicular-fluid–derived cells are associated with oocyte maturation stage and ovarian response phenotype; however, their prognostic value for IVF outcomes requires external validation in independent cohorts with linkage to clinical endpoints.
