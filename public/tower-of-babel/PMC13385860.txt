Helmet detection in traffic scenarios: enhanced performance for complex environments
Authors: Hua Hou, Tianxiang Tan, Diancheng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In the cutting-edge development of computer vision technology, the high missed detection rate caused by target occlusion and the high computational cost of model inference remain core technical bottlenecks restricting the deployment of robust object detection systems in real-world scenarios. This paper presents an improved YOLOv10n-based algorithm to address these issues. Adaptive-DySample (ADS) enhances target detection via three innovative mechanisms—dynamic alignment, challenging sample prioritization, and multi-scale fusion with range adjustment—enabling flexible, object-aware upsampling to overcome traditional limitations. C2fCIB-Fusion(C2fCIB-F), built on the original C2fCIB, systematically addresses the shortcomings of traditional modules in terms of feature extraction flexibility, multi-scale adaptability, and inference efficiency through a series of methods: integrating multi-scale and dynamic channel optimization, introducing Spatial-Channel Interaction, adopting RepVGGDW, and utilizing residual connections. Additionally, Gated Multi-Scale Fusion Convolution (GMFConv) uses a dual-branch architecture (one for global features, one for local details) with a gating mechanism for feature fusion, enhancing small object detection capability and being lightweight for resource-constrained scenarios. Experimental results show the improved model achieves 2.4% higher mAP@0.5 and 5.1% better parameter than the original. It significantly enhances helmet detection accuracy under complex scenarios, maintains real-time performance, and balances a lightweight design, suitable for edge computing and safety monitoring systems.

Introduction

In recent years, the growing number of private cars has led to worsening road congestion, prompting more people to choose non-motor vehicles as a more flexible and convenient means of transportation. The number of non-motor vehicles in urban traffic across the People’s Republic of China has maintained a sustained growth trend. According to statistics from the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology of the People’s Republic of China, the ownership of non-motor vehicles in the People’s Republic of China exceeds 500 million. However, this has been accompanied by a continuous rise in traffic accidents caused by non-motor vehicles. According to statistics from traffic management departments, more than 90% of the deaths caused by motorcycle or non-motor vehicle traffic accidents nationwide were not wearing safety helmets at the time of the accident. To ensure the road safety of non-motor vehicle riders, national traffic management departments have issued mandatory regulations requiring the wearing of helmets while riding. Against this background, target detection algorithms combined with road cameras are being used in the context of helmet-wearing recognition for non-motor vehicle riders, and this application is becoming increasingly important. Currently, the algorithms for object detection rooted in deep learning can be divided into two main categories: two-stage detectors and one-stage detectors. Two-stage detection algorithms, represented by the R-CNN1(Regions with Convolutional Neural Network) series, complete the detection through two steps: region proposal generation and target classification and regression. Algorithms such as Faster R-CNN2 and Fast R-CNN3 are typical examples. This series of algorithms achieves high precision in candidate region screening and feature extraction. However, they suffer from high computational complexity, slow speed, and low frame rates, making it difficult to meet real-time requirements. One-stage detection algorithms realize efficient detection through direct prediction of object positions and categories on feature maps. Representative algorithms include the previous YOLO (You Only Look Once) series and SSD4. SSD (Single Shot MultiBox Detector), based on backbone networks such as VGG5, leverages a concise architecture and rapid response capabilities to directly predict objects on feature maps of different scales. Its lightweight model is easy to deploy, making it suitable for edge computing scenarios. However, it is limited by the performance in small object detection, which is caused by insufficient feature semantic information and the lack of feature fusion mechanisms. The previous YOLO series (v1-v8) of algorithms features a modular design encompassing input layers, specialized backbone networks, neck architectures, and output heads. Since YOLOv26, the YOLO series has adopted an anchor-based mechanism by integrating a dedicated backbone with multi-scale feature pyramids. YOLO achieves both real-time detection capabilities and robust performance on large objects and complex scenes. This architecture enables efficient feature extraction and hierarchical representation learning, allowing the model to balance speed and accuracy effectively. The previous YOLO series algorithms suffer from shortcomings in small-object detection accuracy and bounding box localization accuracy due to the lack of refined processing of candidate regions. The lack of balance among positive and negative samples tends to cause the model to overfit to background information, which in turn results in inadequate robustness when the model is applied to complex scenarios. YOLOv107 is a relatively new real-time end-to-end object detection technique. It is an improvement based on YOLOv8, inheriting the advantages of YOLOv8 while differing in structural details. However, from the perspective of traffic monitoring, helmet targets often exhibit characteristics of small size and low resolution, and are prone to occlusion and background noise. YOLOv10, as an existing algorithm, has weak semantic information in shallow features, making it difficult to accurately distinguish between helmet and non-helmet regions, thus highlighting the scene-specific nature of the problem. To address this series of existing issues, we put forward a helmet-wearing detection algorithm tailored for non-motor vehicles and motorcycles, which is built on an enhanced YOLOv10n architecture. Three key optimizations are integrated into this model, as follows:

The adoption of GMFConv instead of Conv fuses global and local features through a two-branch structure: the global branch extracts features via group convolution and point-wise convolution, while the local branch captures details with small-sized convolution kernels. Then, a gating mechanism is used to generate weights for weighting global features, which are fused with local features and processed by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1\times 1$$\end{document} convolution to achieve adaptive multi-scale feature extraction and fusion, strengthening the ability to represent features while lowering the complexity of the model.

The utilization of the Adaptive-DySample (ADS)enables this module to be adaptively processed through three styles: lp, pl8, and adaptive. The lp and pl styles achieve dynamic alignment by learning spatial offsets, where lp directly generates offsets from the input and combines them with an initial position grid, while pl first upsamples the feature maps via pixel shuffle and then generates offsets. Both support multi-scale fusion to integrate offsets from different convolution kernels, optimize offsets through a correction network and dynamic range adjustment, and finally complete upsampling using grid sampling. The adaptive style generates difficulty maps and weights through a difficulty assessment network and a sampling weight network, screens samples according to the proportion of hard samples, and performs weighted upsampling to enhance attention to complex regions. The three styles achieve flexible object-aware feature enhancement through a unified interface, elevating the detection performance for small targets in terms of accuracy.

The C2FCIB-Fusion boosts the model’s capacity to capture target features through dynamic channel allocation, integration of multi-scale features, and reinforcement of information exchange between spatial and channel dimensions.

Related works

Object detection algorithms are primarily categorized into two-stage methods (e.g., RCNN series) and one-stage methods (e.g., YOLO, SSD). Given that this study focuses on improving YOLOv10, a one-stage detection framework, the following sections will comprehensively review the development status of the YOLO series. Meanwhile, typical methods from the RCNN series and the SSD will be comparatively analyzed to clarify the technical positioning of this research.

YOLO series algorithms

The YOLO (You Only Look Once) series, a pioneering one-stage object detection framework, has revolutionized real-time computer vision with its groundbreaking architecture and unparalleled speed-accuracy tradeoff. The YOLO framework frames object detection as an integrated regression task, directly estimating bounding boxes and class probabilities from complete images in a single forward propagation step. This innovative design enables YOLO to achieve frame rates exceeding 45 FPS (even up to 200 FPS in lightweight variants like YOLOv8n), making it ideal for latency-sensitive applications across diverse industries. Construction Sites: In industrial safety monitoring, YOLO detects unprotected workers, missing safety gear, or hazardous equipment placements in real time, reducing accident risks in dynamic construction environments. The lightweight architecture of YOLO enables real-time monitoring on construction sites via edge devices, avoiding the latency issues of traditional two-stage models. Mining Industry: For automated mineral sorting and conveyor belt inspection, YOLO identifies abnormal material clusters or machinery malfunctions with high precision, optimizing production efficiency and minimizing downtime. Transportation: Beyond autonomous driving, YOLO powers intelligent traffic systems to monitor vehicle flows, recognize license plates, and even detect helmet-wearing compliance among cyclists and motorcyclists in urban road networks. Biological Research: In ecological conservation, YOLO tracks endangered species via camera traps, counts wildlife populations, or analyzes cell morphology in microscopy images, accelerating data-driven insights in biodiversity and medical research. These applications highlight YOLO’s versatility in bridging speed, accuracy, and practical utility across technical domains. Despite the potential of YOLO in various fields, it still faces performance bottlenecks in complex scenarios, such as small targets, dense distributions, and variable lighting conditions. To address these issues, researchers have proposed the following improvement methods: Ren et al.9 proposed a lightweight YOLOv5 model based on attention mechanisms and an efficient architecture for safety helmet detection on construction sites. The model overcomes the issues arising from an extensive parameter count, high computational load, low accuracy, and large model size in complex construction site scenarios, boosts the performance of feature extraction and the effectiveness of representation learning, and achieves a balance between lightweight design and detection accuracy. Hu et al.10 designed an enhanced YOLOv5-based object detection method for identifying road surface cracks in vehicle-mounted images. This approach tackles issues like the blurriness of small cracks and insufficient information extraction from vehicle-borne imagery. It strengthens the model’s crack-focused attention capability while improving network efficiency, thereby boosting both the effectiveness and accuracy of the detection system. Gu et al.11 proposed a cell detection algorithm with a high recognition rate, AYOLOv5, an attention mechanism constructed based on the Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM12) and Transformer13 encoder blocks, which solves the problem of low accuracy in identifying cell targets when cells are densely and complexly distributed. By amplifying the weight of cell-dense areas in blood cell images, this method assists the network in suppressing non-cellular information while strengthening the network’s capacity to capture details related to different cellular properties. As a result, it can more efficiently extract cellular feature information and enhance the recognition performance of cell images. Hui et al.14 developed an improved YOLOv5-driven remote sensing image target detection method named SEB-YOLO. It resolves the challenges of constrained semantic information retrieval from small objects and the indistinguishability of visually analogous targets, simultaneously enhancing the model’s feature fusion capability and reinforcing its image detection capability. Zhang et al.15 proposed a traffic sign detection model named YOLO-BS in this research, which is built upon the YOLOv8 architecture. It tackles issues like Low detection precision and inadequate real-time responsiveness that frequently occur in dynamic road scenarios, while also boosting the capability of feature extraction and improving detection performance for small objects.

RCNN series algorithms

The R-CNN (Region-based Convolutional Neural Network) series, a foundational two-stage object detection framework, has established the benchmark for high-precision computer vision through its systematic “region proposal + fine-grained prediction” pipeline. Fast R-CNN streamlined the original R-CNN by introducing shared feature extraction across regions, drastically reducing redundant computations and accelerating inference while maintaining high accuracy. Faster R-CNN further advanced the series by integrating a Region Proposal Network (RPN) directly into the architecture, enabling near-instant candidate region generation and achieving an end-to-end workflow. However, the RCNN series sacrifices speed for high precision, making it suitable for scenarios with low real-time requirements, while YOLO places greater emphasis on industrial-grade real-time applications. Sharifuzzaman et al.16 proposed a scene-driven recognition and information retrieval system based on Bayesian by Backpropagation (BBB), which is named Bayes R-CNN. It solves the problems of overfitting. Three key challenges are addressed here: the absence of uncertainty quantification, the loss of information during conventional feature extraction, and the acquisition of background information related to the objects being detected. This approach enhances feature extraction capabilities while minimizing information loss. Furthermore, by leveraging a Bayesian image super-resolution model, it improves image quality, which in turn elevates the prediction accuracy of Bayes R-CNN. Liu et al.17 built an Unbiased Faster R-CNN (UFR) to acquire generalizable features for object detection tasks. To address the crucial but tough issue of Single-source Domain Generalization (SDG) in object detection, the team proposes a causal inference framework. This method interprets SDG via causal reasoning and establishes a Structural Causal Model (SCM) to separate data and feature biases caused by scene confounders and object attribute confounders. Li et al.18 proposed a hybrid framework combining a Multi-Scale Feature fusion Cross Stage Partial Network (MSF-CSPNet) and Faster R-CNN. This framework addresses problems like the notable disparity in detection performance between small and large objects. By integrating this backbone network with FPN, the model’s capacity to represent object features is enhanced. The Faster R-CNN based on MSF-CSPNet not only retains high efficiency but also strikes a better balance between accuracy and speed. Wang et al.19 proposed a Faster RCNN-based model for underwater target detection. This model strengthens the expressive capacity of the receptive field in each network layer, tackles the imbalance between positive and negative samples of bounding boxes, optimizes the regression approach of bounding boxes, and enhances the model’s average accuracy in underwater target detection.

SSD series algorithms

SSD, a pioneering one-stage detection framework, introduced a dense prediction strategy that directly detects objects across multiple feature maps of varying scales, removing the necessity of region proposals. Through multi-scale feature map dense prediction and anchor box mechanisms, SSD achieves a balance between speed and accuracy in a one-stage framework, marking a significant milestone in the field of object detection. Despite its limitations in small object detection and anchor box design, its core ideas—such as multi-scale feature utilization and end-to-end detection—have provided critical inspiration for subsequent algorithms like the YOLO series and RetinaNet20. To address these issues, researchers have proposed the following improvement methods: Cheng et al.21 proposed an object detection model built on an optimized SSD, designed for detecting hidden objects in passive terahertz images. To address problems like repeated detection and small target missed detection, the model improves its ability to capture object details and location information by introducing a hybrid attention mechanism; furthermore, it boosts the model’s robustness through the incorporation of the Focal Loss function. This proposed model can maintain high detection accuracy while operating at a faster speed. Huo et al.22 proposed a small object detection model titled SAFF-SSD (Self-Attention Feature Fusion-based SSD) to tackle the limitations of traditional SSD when detecting small objects. It addresses the problem that small objects have significantly lower detection accuracy than medium and large objects, which stems from insufficient non-linear expression, a shortage of semantic data in shallow feature maps, and few pixels in small objects. The model combines a new self-attention module (2L-Transformer) with EfficientNetV2-S as the backbone to improve feature extraction. The CSP-PAN neck allows feature maps to have both low-level details and high-level semantic information, raising detection accuracy. Huang et al.23 propose a CSP-SSD (Cross Stage Partial SSD) algorithm based on SSD for small object detection in mobile phone component images. The traditional SSD algorithm is optimized through network structure improvement, gradient flow information utilization, deconvolution operation introduction, and multi-scale transformation, enabling it to better adapt to mobile phone component detection tasks. This method addresses the issues of inadequate detection accuracy for small targets and insufficient system robustness in object detection tasks. Liu et al.24 proposed an SSD-based object detection model that adopts a residual network as its backbone. This model addresses the degradation of detection accuracy triggered by factors like lighting fluctuations, complex backgrounds, variations in face scale, and occlusions. By optimizing feature extraction and fusing tracking results, the model enhances not only the detection accuracy of faces but also the real-time performance of the face detection system.

Methods

YOLOv10

Researchers at Tsinghua University have introduced YOLOv10, an innovative real-time object detection technique. Leveraging the Ultralytics Python framework, this new model overcomes the limitations of earlier YOLO variants in both post-processing procedures and structural design. YOLOv10 attains leading-edge performance while greatly cutting down on computational load through two key improvements: the removal of Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) and the optimization of multiple model elements. Extensive experiments have demonstrated that YOLOv10 attains an excellent accuracy-latency trade-off across multiple model scales. It can be split into three components, namely the Backbone, the Neck, and the Head. These modules can be explained in detail as follows: (1) The backbone network of YOLOv10 is responsible for feature extraction and initial downsampling. However, YOLOv10 does not make significant modifications to the backbone architecture, largely inheriting the structure from YOLOv8. (2) The Neck network is responsible for further feature fusion and multi-scale information extraction. In this part, YOLOv10 inherits the design concept of YOLOv8 and adopts some new feature fusion modules, such as C2fCIB. In layers rich in semantic features, the C2f module is replaced with the C2fCIB module, which uses depthwise separable convolutions instead of standard convolutions. This replacement reduces computational load and increases the receptive field. (3) The Head network is a crucial component of YOLOv10 for object classification and location regression. In the Head network of YOLOv10, several improvements have been introduced, including:

1. Downsampling

The downsampling operation is replaced by SCDown (first adjusting the number of channels through a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1\times 1$$\end{document} convolution, followed by a depthwise convolution with k=3 and s=2 for spatial downsampling) instead of a single CBL (Convolution + Batch Normalization + Leaky ReLU25).

2. Detect module

In the cls classification branch, consecutive \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$3\times 3$$\end{document} convolutions are replaced with two depthwise separable convolution blocks, each consisting of Conv3x3+Conv1x1, to reduce computational complexity.

3. PSA Block

A PSA (Position Self-Attention) Block module is added after the SPPF layer to provide global computational capabilities.

4. Dual label assignment

YOLOv10 adopts a dual-head design, namely the One-to-many Head and the One-to-one Head. During the training process, both heads participate in the calculation of the loss function simultaneously. However, during inference and prediction, only the One-to-one Head is required. This design effectively addresses the issue of redundant predictions in post-processing, enabling NMS-free end-to-end detection. Improvements

This paper proposes an object detection model based on the improved YOLOv10n, with the aim of enhancing the accuracy and precision of the model on resource-constrained devices. The improvements are as follows: (1) Gated Multi-scale Fusion Convolution (GMFConv), (2) C2FCIB-Fusion, (3) Adaptive-DySample (ADS). Helmet detection in real-world surveillance scenarios (construction/traffic) differs fundamentally from generic object detection due to its unique target attributes and cascaded technical bottlenecks, which are the core theoretical basis for all our module designs, combinations and improvements. Based on a statistical analysis of our helmet-exclusive datasets (VisDrone2019: e.g., Generic Object Detection Dataset, 10,209 samples; SFCHD: e.g., Construction Helmet Dataset, 12,372 samples), three typical task-specific characteristics of helmet targets are quantified: (1) Small pixel scale: 92.3% of helmets occupy only 32×32 to 64×64 pixels (5%–15% of the image area), leading to blurred shallow feature extraction and low signal-to-noise ratio with complex backgrounds (e.g., construction equipment, traffic flow); (2) High occlusion rate: 28.7% of helmets are occluded by human bodies, barriers or lighting conditions, resulting in incomplete feature representation; (3) Cascaded feature degradation: Shallow fine-grained helmet features (e.g., edge/color details) are easily lost in deep network transmission, while traditional rigid upsampling causes distortion for small/deformed helmets. To address these helmet-specific bottlenecks, our research design adheres to three theoretically guided, data-driven core principles, ensuring no ad-hoc design, arbitrary combination or generic optimization: (1) Targeted integration of mature techniques: Integrate attention mechanisms and multi-scale processing based on classic computer vision theories (multi-scale feature complementarity, dimensional feature enhancement, dynamic spatial alignment, hard sample focus) — each integration is designed to solve a specific helmet detection bottleneck, rather than random combination; (2) Helmet-optimized structural/hyperparameter design: All key design choices (e.g., kernel size, hard sample ratio) are optimized based on the above statistical characteristics of helmet targets, and their optimality is verified via systematic ablation experiments; (3) Hierarchical feature processing pipeline: Modules are combined following the “feature extraction-fusion-prediction” logic tailored for helmet detection’s cascaded bottlenecks, ensuring functional complementarity and synergistic optimization without redundancy. Following these principles, we propose three modules (GMFConv, C2fCIB-Fusion, ADS) and integrate them incrementally. All subsequent designs, combinations and experimental validations are tightly coupled with the unique characteristics of helmet detection, and their effectiveness is fully verified by a set of task-specific experiments (intra-module design ablation, module combination ablation, cross-dataset comparison).

Gated multi-scale fusion convolution

The regularized operations of standard convolution, such as sliding windows and matrix multiplications, can be easily optimized by hardware accelerators like GPUs and TPUs. Standard convolution processes multi-channel input images using multiple multi-channel convolution kernels. The resulting feature maps not only capture channel-wise features but also extract spatial features. However, it demonstrates notable limitations in scenarios with constrained computational budgets and memory resources, such as mobile devices and embedded systems. The gating mechanism adaptively learns weight matrices to selectively activate or suppress input features, which essentially embeds a “feature importance assessment” module into the network architecture. This mechanism can significantly enhance the model’s ability to capture key information while maintaining controllable computational complexity. Groupwise convolution divides both the input feature maps and the convolution kernels into multiple groups. The kernels of each group only perform convolution operations on the corresponding group of input feature maps, and there is no information exchange between different groups. Pointwise convolution uses \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1\times 1$$\end{document} convolution kernels to convolve the input feature maps, mainly used to change the number of channels in feature maps without altering their spatial dimensions. The Gated Multi-scale Fusion Convolution (GMFConv) employs a dual-branch architecture, where one branch focuses on extracting global features and the other branch is dedicated to capturing local details, thereby enhancing the detection capability for small objects. The module introduces a gating mechanism to process the two types of features. First, it generates gating weights for the global feature branch, then performs weighted fusion with the original global feature branch. After that, it merges the weighted-fused features with the local feature branch and finally conducts fusion processing. The lightweight design of GMFConv makes it highly suitable for applications in mobile devices or scenarios with strict constraints on computational resources.

This dual-branch (global+local) architecture with a dedicated gating mechanism is not an ad-hoc combination of multi-scale processing and attention-based gating strategies, but a theoretically targeted design rooted in the multi-scale feature complementarity theory for small/occluded helmet detection; the local branch is optimized for the 32×32 average pixel size of helmet targets to capture fine-grained spatial details critical for small helmet recognition, and the gating mechanism adaptively assigns weights to the global feature branch for selective feature fusion—rather than generic feature activation. Combined with the lightweight groupwise and pointwise convolution design, this module not only suppresses complex background noise specific to helmet surveillance scenarios (e.g., construction site debris, traffic flow) but also maintains controllable computational complexity, making it more targeted than generic multi-scale or attention mechanisms for helmet feature learning in resource-constrained embedded/mobile deployment scenarios.

C2FCIB-fusion

The traditional C2fCIB module operates on the CSP (Cross Stage Partial) structure: it first splits input features into two parts—one directly serves as the output base, while the other undergoes multi-layer bottleneck processing (using stacked convolutions for feature transformation and interaction). Finally, the processed features are fused with the base part to complete feature extraction. This design balances feature representation capability and computational complexity via structural segmentation and recombination. However, its fixed channel allocation, single-scale convolution kernels, and lack of explicit attention in feature interaction limit adaptability to multi-scale targets and make it vulnerable to background interference. To address these flaws, C2fCIB-Fusion systematically optimizes feature extraction flexibility, multi-scale adaptability, and propagation stability through two core designs:

(1). Multi-Scale and Dynamic Channel Optimization. Targeting the single-scale and fixed-channel issues of traditional C2fCIB, Multi-Scale and Dynamic Channel Optimization builds independent branches with odd-sized kernels (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1\times 1$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$3\times 3$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$5\times 5$$\end{document}) to process distinct feature channel subsets, enabling multi-scale feature capture. It adopts a dynamic channel allocation strategy (via channel allocation) and calculates the “base number of channels” and the “remaining number of channels”. First, split the total number of channels (dim) into \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_{div}$$\end{document} parts initially to ensure that most branches receive the same “base number of channels”. The remaining channels will be allocated to large convolution kernels later. Generate an initial channel allocation list, allocate channels in “descending order” of convolution kernel sizes, and map the sorted channel allocation “back to the original branch order”, assigning more channels to larger kernels to optimize resource usage. Each branch consists of Group Convolution, BatchNorm, and ReLU. Group Convolution reduces computational load, while BatchNorm and ReLU enhance non-linear expression, and a final \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1\times 1$$\end{document} convolution fuses multi-scale features.

(2). Partial-Channel Interaction for Spatial-Channel Synergy. Unlike traditional spatial-channel attention (focused on “selection”), Partial-Channel Interaction (implemented as Spatial-Channel Interaction) centers on “interaction” to strengthen feature discriminability. It first uses \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$3\times 3$$\end{document} depth-wise separable convolutions (single channel per group) to capture spatial correlations, then constructs a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1\times 1$$\end{document} convolution-based channel attention branch (compressing channels via reduction, generating weights with Sigmoid) to realize spatial-guided channel selection. Finally, residual connection26 with learnable residual weight balances original and interaction-enhanced features, improving propagation stability.

The fusion design of Partial-Channel Interaction is developed based on the dimensional feature enhancement theory in the field of helmet detection: helmet targets exhibit distinct channel features (e.g., the high contrast between helmets and hair/skin in RGB channels) and spatial features (e.g., the fixed spatial position on the human head). The C2f-based residual fusion structure is designed with targeted theoretical justification to address the issue of feature degradation that occurs when deep networks learn shallow helmet features (e.g., tiny helmet edge features) — a common pain point in small object detection that is often overlooked in generic multi-scale fusion modules. Our proposed structure precisely fills this design gap and provides theoretical support for the effective preservation of shallow helmet features.

Adaptive-DySample

Adaptive-DySample (ADS) processes input feature maps via three sampling styles: lp, pl, and adaptive; lp generates offsets from input features, combines them with an initial grid, refines via a correction network, and uses grid-based bilinear interpolation for upsampling, while pl first upsamples via pixel shuffle, generates offsets on upsampled features, reverts to original resolution, then integrates/corrects offsets before sampling, with both lp/pl achieving dynamic spatial alignment through task-specific learned offsets to address traditional upsampling’s rigidity and poor adaptation to geometric deformations; the adaptive style prioritizes hard samples by using a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$difficulty_-net$$\end{document} to generate a difficulty map and a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$weight_-net$$\end{document} for sampling weights, as the core spatial guidance signal of the Adaptive-DySample (ADS) module, the difficulty map acts as a bridge between adaptive sampling and helmet detection, which quantifies the detection difficulty of each spatial position in the feature map into a probability value within [0,1] to guide adaptive sampling resource allocation. Specifically, the difficulty map is tailored to the unique challenges of helmet detection (e.g., small targets, frequent occlusion) via a difficulty assessment network, where pixel values close to 1 represent hard regions (e.g., occluded/small helmets) with easily lost fine-grained features, and values close to 0 denote easy regions (e.g., clear helmets, pure background) with sufficient detection features. It directly dictates the sampling decision and intensity of the ADS module, and constructs a closed-loop optimization link with helmet detection performance: the map guides focused sampling on hard regions to optimize feature extraction, while its generation parameters are adaptively adjusted by the gradient feedback from degraded detection performance, realizing the co-evolution of difficulty estimation, adaptive sampling and detection performance. This dynamic adaptation enables the detector to adjust sampling focus according to scene characteristics, effectively enhancing generalization ability for complex helmet detection scenarios and alleviating the performance bottleneck of fixed sampling strategies. In this work, “difficulty” for helmet detection is defined as a spatial-aware concept integrating three complementary dimensions (semantic hardness, gradient-based hardness, information density), referring to image regions with high processing uncertainty, high miss/mis-detection risk or critical discriminative information for helmet recognition. The learning of regional difficulty is implemented by the difficulty assessment network (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$difficulty_-net$$\end{document}) in the ADS module, which is trained end-to-end jointly with the detector’s head in a data-driven, annotation-free manner. Forming a closed-loop learning mechanism, the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$difficulty_-net$$\end{document} generates a pixel-wise difficulty map to guide adaptive sampling; inadequate sampling of hard regions will cause performance degradation and further generate gradient feedback, which is backpropagated to optimize the difficulty map generation, thus strengthening the model’s attention to erroneous hard regions and realizing adaptive resource allocation for sampling. Filtering top \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$hard_-ratio$$\end{document} samples to create a binary mask, applying weights and mask to the input feature map via element-wise multiplication, and performing bilinear interpolation for upsampling, thus mitigating underlearning of complex regions (edges, occlusions, small objects) that traditional methods overlook; ADS further supports multi-scale fusion (combining offsets from different kernel sizes with learnable scaling factors) and dynamic range adjustment (via a scope network when dyscope is enabled) to balance local details and global context, overcoming single-scale limitations, and through a unified forward interface, dynamically switches strategies for flexible, object-aware feature enhancement in target detection.Fig. 6ADS Architecture.

Summary

Rationality of the incremental combination of the three modules: The incremental integration of GMFConv, C2fCIB-Fusion, and ADS follows a“hierarchical feature processing pipeline”for helmet detection, with clear theoretical logic for their sequential combination and functional complementarity: GMFConv (backbone feature extraction): First extracts multi-scale helmet features (global+local) and adaptively weights them, solving the problem of small helmet feature blurriness and background noise interference at the bottom feature extraction stage; C2fCIB-Fusion (neck feature fusion): Further fuses multi-scale helmet features from the backbone at the neck stage, enhancing the channel and spatial feature representation of helmets, and solving the feature degradation problem in deep network transmission; ADS (head upsampling): Finally performs dynamic adaptive upsampling on the fused helmet features, focusing on hard helmet samples (occluded/small/deformed) and solving the rigid upsampling and hard sample underlearning problems at the prediction stage. This hierarchical combination is theoretically consistent with thefeature extraction-fusion-prediction pipeline of object detection networks, and each module is responsible for addressing a specific bottleneck in the helmet detection feature processing chain. Their incremental integration avoids functional redundancy and achieves a synergistic effect.

Evaluation indicators

To evaluate the detection performance of the improved model, we used the following metrics: Precision (P), Recall (R), Average Precision (AP), mean Average Precision50 (mAP50), mAP50–95, GFLOPs, number of model parameters, and model size. Specifically, Precision (P) refers to the proportion of samples that are truly positive among all samples predicted as positive. Recall (R) refers to the proportion of samples that are correctly predicted as positive among all actually positive samples. AP is a metric derived from the area that lies under the Precision-Recall (P-R) curve, which reflects the model’s performance under different confidence levels. For each category, a corresponding P-R curve can be plotted, and the area under this curve is calculated as the AP for that category. mAP is the average of the APs across all categories. It is one of the most commonly used comprehensive evaluation criteria in object detection, as it can reflect the overall performance of the model on the entire dataset. mAP50 is the mAP calculated when the IoU threshold is 0.50, mAP50–95 is the average of mAP values calculated within the IoU threshold range of 0.50 to 0.95. Datasets performance comparison

To further address the scale limitation and enhance the reliability of our conclusions, we have expanded the evaluation scope by adding three established public datasets (VisDrone2019, SFCHD, SHWD), forming a comprehensive evaluation system covering general small-object detection, dedicated helmet detection, and custom scenario detection. The four datasets used in the manuscript are detailed as follows: VisDrone2019: A widely recognized public benchmark for small-object detection with large-scale data (over 10,000 images), containing complex scenes with dense small targets (e.g., distant helmets, heads), which can verify the generalization ability of our method in general small-object scenarios. SFCHD (Safety Helmet and Construction Helmet Dataset): A public dataset dedicated to helmet detection with diverse construction site scenarios, enabling direct comparison with state-of-the-art helmet detection methods. SHWD (Safety Helmet Wearing Dataset): A representative public helmet dataset (7,581 images) widely cited in relevant studies, with a scale similar to HWCD, supplementing the evaluation of our method on different helmet detection scenarios. Discussion

The three proposed modules (GMFConv, C2fCIB-Fusion, and ADS) and their incremental combination are specifically designed for the unique bottlenecks of helmet detection rather than generic object detection, and their integration follows a “hierarchical feature processing pipeline” for helmet detection with clear theoretical logic for sequential combination and functional complementarity. GMFConv (backbone feature extraction) extracts multi-scale helmet features (global+local) and adaptively weights them, solving the problems of blurred small helmet features and background noise interference in the bottom feature extraction stage; C2fCIB-Fusion (neck feature fusion) further fuses backbone features to enhance helmet feature representation and address feature degradation in deep network transmission; ADS (head upsampling) performs dynamic adaptive upsampling on fused features, focusing on hard helmet samples and solving relevant problems in the prediction stage. This hierarchical combination conforms to the object detection pipeline, with each module specifically addressing a bottleneck, avoiding redundancy and achieving a synergistic effect; experiments verify that their combined use yields higher mAP (especially for small/occluded helmets).

The structure and hyperparameters of each module are tailored to helmet characteristics and verified optimal through parameter ablation experiments; the sequential combination of modules is validated effective via module combination ablation experiments. Cross-dataset experiments show that the model achieves a 0.3%–3.1% mAP@0.5 improvement over YOLOv11 on two helmet datasets (with the most significant gain for small/occluded helmets), but only a 1.6% improvement on the general dataset, proving that the modules are optimized specifically for helmet detection.

Limitations

Computational overhead for edge deployment: Although both the GMFConv and ADS modules are designed with lightweight architectures, the incremental integration of the three modules results in negligible changes in the model’s FLOPs (floating-point operations) compared with the baseline. This may lead to insufficient real-time inference performance on resource-constrained edge devices (e.g., low-power IoT cameras), especially in multi-target surveillance scenarios. Generalization capability for rare helmet types: The dataset is dominated by common safety helmets for traffic scenarios, while rare helmet types (e.g., fire helmets, sports helmets with special shapes) account for only 3.2% of the samples. Due to insufficient sample learning, the model exhibits degraded performance in detecting such rare helmets. Sensitivity to extreme environmental interference: The model’s detection accuracy declines under harsh weather conditions (e.g., heavy rain, fog). This is because the gating mechanism and difficulty map fail to completely filter out environmental noise, which results in the degradation of feature representation.

Additionally, there are failure cases related to the generalization capability for rare helmet types. In actual outdoor and special scenario helmet detection tasks, when the model detects samples of fire helmets (mostly red/yellow, with protective masks and reflective strips) and special-shaped ski helmets (mostly irregularly shaped, with bright color splicing designs), two types of failure phenomena frequently occur: first, misdetections, where such rare helmets are misjudged as non-helmet targets (e.g., fire helmets are misjudged as ordinary protective caps); second, missed detections, where targets wearing such helmets are not identified at all. The core cause is that such rare helmet samples account for only 3.2% of the dataset, far lower than the proportion of common safety helmets in traffic scenarios, leading to insufficient model learning — the GMFConv module cannot effectively capture their unique shape and material features that are significantly different from ordinary helmets, and the hard sample mining mechanism of the ADS module fails to cover such rare samples with low proportions, ultimately resulting in degraded detection performance.

Finally, there are failure cases related to sensitivity to extreme environmental interference. In actual outdoor road edge monitoring scenarios, when encountering harsh weather such as heavy rain and fog, the images captured by low-power IoT cameras will have obvious problems such as blurriness, fogging, and dim light. Helmet targets are severely occluded or blurred by rain marks and fog noise, leading to two typical failure situations of the model: first, a significant decline in detection accuracy, with the helmet detection accuracy dropping by more than 30% compared with sunny weather; second, frequent missed detections and misdetections. Missed detections mostly occur on helmet targets that are far from the camera and severely blurred by fog, while misdetections often misjudge blurred areas in the image similar to the contour of a helmet (such as wet branches and fog shadow) as helmets. The core cause is that the gating mechanism of GMFConv and the difficulty map of ADS cannot completely filter out complex noise under extreme environments, and cannot effectively distinguish helmet features from environmental interference signals, thereby causing the degradation of the feature representation of helmet targets and leading to the model’s failure to accurately identify and locate helmets.
