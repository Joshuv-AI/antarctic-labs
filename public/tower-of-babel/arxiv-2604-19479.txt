Algebraic Distance Optimization in Polyhedral Norms
Eliana Duarte, Nidhi Kaihnsa, Julia Lindberg, Angélica Torres, and Madeleine Weinstein

Abstract
We consider the distance minimization problem to a real algebraic variety X ⊆ ℝ n X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n} when the metric is induced by a polyhedral norm. Each point in the variety has a Voronoi cell whose geometry depends on the normal space at the point and the inner normal fan of the polyhedral ball. For codimension-one varieties, we decompose X X into sets of points whose Voronoi cones have the same dimension, which is the expected dimension of their Voronoi cell. We prove that this decomposition is a stratification of X X and that each strata is a semialgebraic set. We conclude by giving an algebraic description of the medial axis, which is the locus of points whose minimal distance to X X is achieved at more than one point on X X .

Introduction

A distance optimization problem consists of a subset
X
of a metric space, representing a model, together with an observed point,
v\not\in X
.
The fundamental question is then: which point of
X
best explains the observed point
v
? Algebraic models of this type arise in disciplines such as algebraic statistics, computer vision, machine learning, and phylogenetics, among others
[
3
]
. The complexity of this optimization problem has been studied from an algebraic perspective for a wide range of models
X
and distance functions
[
4
,
3
,
10
,
14
]
.

In this article, we consider the inverse problem. Given a set
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a point
p\in X,
we ask which points in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are best modeled by
p
.
In other words, we study which data points have their distance to
X
minimized at
p
.
The set of all such points in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called the
Voronoi cell
of
p
. The collection of all of the Voronoi cells forms the
Voronoi diagram
of
X
.

Voronoi diagrams have been studied extensively, predominantly in the setting where
X
is a finite subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
equipped with the Euclidean metric. In
[
7
]
, the authors extend this analysis to the case when
X
is an algebraic variety, showing that for any smooth point
p\in X
, the Euclidean Voronoi cell is convex and contained in the normal space of
X
at
p.

For discrete sets and the tropical norm, the authors in
[
8
, Theorem 6]
proved that Voronoi cells are star-convex, but not necessarily convex.
In this work, we study the Voronoi diagram when
X
is an algebraic variety and the metric is induced by a polyhedral norm.

A primary motivation for this setting comes from optimal transport. Consider the probability simplex of all distributions on
n
states. A metric
d
on the set of states induces a transportation distance on the simplex known as the Wasserstein distance
W_{d}(\mu,\nu)
. The corresponding unit ball is a centrally symmetric convex polytope known as the
Lipschitz polytope
. This setting was studied in
[
5
,
6
]
, where it was shown that the Wasserstein distance to a variety is a piecewise polynomial function.

Our approach follows
[
1
,
7
]
. For a point
p\in X,

[
1
]
shows that its Voronoi cell is contained in a union of polyhedral cones, which we refer to as the
Voronoi cone
of
p.
We prove that the Voronoi cone is determined by the relative position of the normal space of
X
at
p
with respect to the inner normal fan of the unit ball. Equivalently, the Voronoi cone can be characterized by the intersection of the dual unit ball with the normal space at
p
(
Theorem

3.6
). Moreover, when
X
has pure codimension one, this perspective allows us to stratify
X
according to the dimension of the Voronoi cones of its points (
Theorem

4.4
).

We also study the
medial axis
of
X,
the locus of points whose distance to
X
is optimized by more than one point.
When
X
consists of finitely many points, prior work examined the combinatorial structure of such bisectors
[
8
,
11
]
. We consider the case where
X
has codimension one.
In this case, the medial axis decomposes into components associated with pairs of faces of the unit ball. In the extreme cases (pairs consisting of two vertices, two facets, or a vertex and a facet) we obtain degree bounds for the algebraic closure of the corresponding component of the medial axis (
Theorems

5.11
,
5.12
and
5.13
).

The paper is organized as follows. In
Section

2
, we review the necessary background from polyhedral geometry, polyhedral norms, and differential topology. In
Section

3
, we focus on the relationship between the normal space of a point in
X
, the normal fan of the unit ball, and the Voronoi cone of
p
. In particular, we show that the Voronoi cell of a point depends on the geometry of
X
with respect to the norm. In
Section

4
, we show that for codimension-one varieties we can stratify
X
based on the dimension of the Voronoi cones of points on
X
and give a recipe to determine this stratification. Finally, in
Section

5
, we give an algebraic description of the medial axis and establish degree bounds for its components.

Preliminaries

2.1
Polyhedral Geometry

To study the Voronoi diagram of a real algebraic variety
X
associated to a polyhedral norm, we recall several definitions related to the geometry of convex polytopes, their duals, and the polyhedral cones associated to their faces. We define only the objects that will be used in our study and refer the reader to
[
9
,
15
]
for a detailed exposition. In what follows, we will denote by
\mathbb{R}^{n*}
the dual space of
\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
P
be a convex polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that the origin is in its interior.
The
dual polytope
to
P
, denoted by
P^{*}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n*}
, is defined as

\displaystyle P^{*}:=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n*}~|~x^{T}y\leq 1~\forall~x\in P\}.

Throughout this article, we consider full-dimensional convex polytopes in the ambient space.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a set of nonzero vectors.
The cones

\displaystyle C:=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}v_{i}\mid(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{k}\right\}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{ and }\hskip 20.00003pt\overline{C}:=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}v_{i}\mid(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{k}\setminus\{0\}\right\}

are, respectively, the
open and closed
polyhedral cones generated by
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\{0\}
.

In the Euclidean topology,
\overline{C}
is the closure of
C
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
.
We will work primarily with open cones. Thus, unless stated otherwise, “cone” will refer to an open cone.
We denote the set of (proper) faces of a polytope as
\mathcal{F}_{P}\coloneqq\{F~|~F\text{ is a proper face of }P\}
. Note that
\emptyset,P\notin\mathcal{F}_{P}
. Furthermore, we define
\mathcal{F}_{P,i}\coloneqq\{F~|~F\in\mathcal{F}_{P}\text{ and }\dim F=n-1-i\}
for
i=0,\ldots,n-1
. When the polytope is clear from the context, we write simply
\mathcal{F}
(resp.
\mathcal{F}_{i}
) instead of
\mathcal{F}_{P}
(resp.
\mathcal{F}_{P,i}
).
A hyperplane
H
is a supporting hyperplane of a polytope
P
at
F
if

H\cap P=F
for some
F\in\mathcal{F}
and
P\subset H^{+}
where
H^{+}\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid H(x)\geq 0\}.

Given a polytope, we associate to it a polyhedral fan via its faces.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
P
be a full-dimensional convex polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
F
one of its faces. The
inner normal (open) cone
,
C(P,F)
, at
F
is the open cone containing all of the inner pointing normal vectors of all of the supporting hyperplanes
H
of
P

such that
P\cap H=F
. In particular, this cone is generated by the normal vectors of the supporting hyperplanes of all of the facets containing
F.

Whenever the polytope
P
is clear from context, we write simply
C_{F}
for the cone
C(P,F)
and denote
{\rm gens}(C_{F})
as the minimal generators of this cone. Additionally, for
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we denote the translate of
C(P,F)
to
v
by

C_{v}(P,F):=v+C(P,F).

Remark 2.4
.

From the above definitions, it is easy to check that for any
F\in\mathcal{F}
,
\overline{C_{F}}=\bigsqcup_{F\subseteq F^{\prime}}C_{F^{\prime}}.

The union of the inner normal cones of all of the faces of a polytope
P
is called the
inner normal fan
of
P
; it is denoted by
\Sigma_{P}
and it partitions
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
into polyhedral regions indexed by the faces of
P
.

Remark 2.5
.

Consider a polytope
P
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and its dual
P^{*}
. Let
F
be a face of
P
and
v
be a vector in
C(P,F)
. We can consider
v
as a linear functional over
\mathbb{R}^{n*}
.
From the definition of duality, it is straightforward to check that
v
attains its minimum over
P^{*}
precisely on the face
F^{*}\subseteq P^{*}
dual to
F
.

2.2
Polyhedral Norms

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a finite set of linear functionals on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that the set
P=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}~|~\ell(x)\leq 1\text{ for }\ell\in\mathcal{L}\}
is bounded and describes a full-dimensional convex polytope. Furthermore, we impose that
\mathcal{L}=-\mathcal{L}

so the polytope
P
is centrally symmetric around the origin, i.e.
P=-P
. For the entirety of this article, we will assume that
\mathcal{L}=-\mathcal{L}
and
P=-P.

Definition 2.8
.

For a finite collection of linear functionals
\mathcal{L}
, a
polyhedral norm
is a function
h_{\mathcal{L}}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
given by

h_{\mathcal{L}}(x):=\max_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}}\ \ell(x).

It is a straightforward exercise to verify that polyhedral norms satisfy the norm axioms.
Under this norm, the distance
d(x,y)
between two points
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
d(x,y)\coloneqq h_{\mathcal{L}}(x-y)
.
Furthermore, given a set
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define the
polyhedral distance
of a point
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
to
X
as

d(y,X):=\min_{x\in\overline{X}}\ h_{\mathcal{L}}(y-x),

where
\overline{X}
is the Euclidean closure of
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Definition 2.9
.

Given a polyhedral norm
h_{\mathcal{L}}
, the
ball
of radius
r
at an arbitrary point
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is

B_{r}(y):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}~|~h_{\mathcal{L}}(x-y)\leq r\}.

For convenience, we write the unit ball at
y
as
B(y)
and simply as
B
when considering the unit ball around the origin.

For a given polyhedral norm,
B
is a centrally symmetric convex polytope.
In the next result, we show that there is a polyhedral norm associated with every full-dimensional centrally symmetric convex polytope.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
P
be a full-dimensional centrally symmetric convex polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that contains the origin. Then there is a polyhedral norm
h_{\mathcal{L}}
such that
P
is the unit ball for
h_{\mathcal{L}}.

Proof.

We consider the set of linear functionals
\mathcal{L}
that give supporting hyperplanes for the facets of
P
. This gives the minimal
\mathcal{H}-
representation of
P
. Since
0\in P
and
P
is centrally symmetric, the set
\mathcal{L}
is of the form
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{m},-a_{1},\ldots,-a_{m}\}
with
a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n*}
. By construction,
P
is the unit ball of the polyhedral norm
h_{\mathcal{L}}
up to scaling.
∎

\left\{\pm x,\pm y\right\}.

The dual polytope
P^{\ast}

2.3
Differential Topology

Here, we recall some results and definitions from differential topology that we will use later to stratify a codimension-one manifold using polyhedral norms. For a basic introduction to topological and smooth manifolds, we refer readers to
[
12
,
13
]
.
Going forward, we consider a manifold
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the Euclidean topology inherited from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Below, we give definitions of locally closed subspaces and stratifications of
X
.

Definition 2.11
.

A subset
U
of a topological space
X
is
locally closed
if
\overline{U}\setminus U
is closed in
X
. Equivalently,
U
is locally closed if it is the set difference of two open sets in
X
.

Definition 2.12
.

Let
X
be a manifold and
I
be an index set with a partial ordering. The collection
\{X_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is a
stratification
of
X
if the following conditions are satisfied:

(i)

X=\bigcup_{i\in I}X_{i}
,

(ii)

X_{i}
is a submanifold for all
i\in I
,

(iii)

X_{i}
is locally closed for all
i\in I
,

(iv)

X_{i}\cap X_{j}=\emptyset
if
i\neq j
,

(v)

if
X_{i}\cap\overline{X_{j}}\neq\emptyset
, then
X_{i}\subset\overline{X_{j}}
. Equivalently, if
X_{i}\subset\overline{X_{j}}
, then
i\leq j
.

A family
\{X_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of subsets of a topological space
X
is
locally finite
if, for each
x\in X
, there is a neighborhood
U
of
x
such that
U\cap X_{i}=\emptyset
for all but finitely many indices
i\in I.
If
I
is a finite set, that is,
\{X_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is a finite family, then it is trivially locally finite. This will always be the case later on.

We now recall some basic results and definitions that will be useful when constructing the stratification.

Lemma 2.13

(
[
2
]
Proposition 2, Ch. 5.1)
.

Let
f:X\longrightarrow Y
be a map between two topological spaces
X
and
Y,
and fix a subset
T\subset Y
. If
f
is an open map (resp. closed, continuous), then so is the restriction
f|_{f^{-1}(T)}:f^{-1}(T)\longrightarrow T
.

Let
X
and
Y
be smooth manifolds of dimensions
m
and
n
, respectively. A map
f:X\longrightarrow Y
is an
immersion at a point

x\in X
if
df_{x}:T_{x}X\longrightarrow T_{f(x)}Y
is injective. If
f
is an immersion at
x
for all
x\in X
, then
f
is called an
immersion
. Furthermore,
f
is a
smooth embedding
of
X
into
Y
if it is a smooth immersion and a topological embedding, i.e., a homeomorphism onto its image
f(X)\subseteq Y
in the subspace topology. A subset
S
is an
embedded submanifold
of
X
in the subspace topology if it is endowed with a smooth structure with respect to which
S\hookrightarrow X
is a smooth embedding.

Let
f:X\to Y
be a smooth map and
S\subseteq Y
be an embedded submanifold of
Y
. We say that
f
is
transverse
to
S
if for every
x\in f^{-1}(S)
, the tangent spaces
T_{f(x)}S
and
d_{f(x)}T_{x}X
together span
T_{f(x)}Y
. In other words,

T_{f(x)}S+d_{f(x)}T_{x}X=T_{f(x)}Y.

Finally, we will use the following generalized preimage theorem whose proof can be found in
[
13
]
.

Theorem 2.14

(The Preimage Theorem)
.

Suppose
X
and
Y
are smooth manifolds and
S\subseteq Y
is an embedded submanifold.
If
f:X\to Y
is a smooth map that is transverse to
S
, then
f^{-1}(S)
is an embedded submanifold of
X
whose codimension is equal to the codimension of
S
in
Y
.

Voronoi Diagrams in Polyhedral Norms

In this section, we study the Voronoi diagrams of algebraic varieties with respect to polyhedral norms. We state our main definitions and prove results regarding Voronoi cells of smooth points of algebraic varieties. In what follows, we assume that
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a centrally symmetric convex polytope that corresponds to the unit ball of a polyhedral norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a variety and let
v\in X
. Given a polyhedral norm
h_{\mathcal{L}}
, the
Voronoi cell
of
v
with respect to
h_{\mathcal{L}}
is:

\mathrm{Vor}_{X}(v):=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}~|~d(y,X)=h_{\mathcal{L}}(y-v)\}.

Given a smooth point
v
on a variety
X
, we define the
\mathrm{Type}
of
v
as the collection of faces of the unit ball
B
that have a supporting hyperplane whose normal vector is contained in the normal space of
v
,
N_{v}.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
X
be a variety and
N_{v}

be the normal space of
X
at a smooth point
v\in X.
We define the
type
of
v
as:

\mathrm{Type}(v):=\left\{F\in\mathcal{F}(B)~|~C(P,F)\cap N_{v}\neq\emptyset\right\}.

We point out that the definition above differs from the definition of type in
[
6
, Proposition 4]
. In our case, the type of a point does not depend on a second point. It depends only on the local geometry of the variety and the polyhedral ball. In
Definition

3.11
,
we will refine the definition of type by defining an
optimizing face
which is closer to the definition of type from
[
6
]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Given a ball
B
as defined above and a smooth point
v\in X
, if
F\in\mathrm{Type}(v)
, then
-F\in\mathrm{Type}(v)
.

Proof.

Since
B
is centrally symmetric, it holds that
N_{v}\cap C(P,F)\neq\emptyset
if and only if
N_{v}\cap C(P,-F)\neq\emptyset
.
∎

The next proposition describes the Voronoi cell of a point based on its type. Specifically, we find a union of cones containing the Voronoi cell of a smooth point in a variety. We recall that given a face
F
of a polyhedral ball
B
, we let
\overline{C(B,F)}
denote the Euclidean closure of
C(B,F)
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
X
be a real algebraic variety and
v\in X
a smooth point. Then the Voronoi cell

\mathrm{Vor}_{X}(v)\subset\bigcup_{F\in\mathrm{Type}(v)}\overline{C_{v}(B^{*},F^{*})}.

Proof.

Let
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus X
be a point in the Voronoi cell of
v\in X.
We claim
u-v\in\overline{C(B^{*},F^{*})}
for some
F\in\mathrm{Type}(v)
. Let us assume this is not true so that
u-v\in C(B^{*},T^{*})
for some face
T

such that
T\not\subseteq F
for all
F\in\mathrm{Type}(v).
Since
u-v
is minimized over the face
T
, we have that
B_{\lambda}(u)
intersects
X
at the point
v
which lies in the face
T
where
\lambda:=d(u,X)
. Hence, the tangent space at
v
is a supporting hyperplane of
T
. Equivalently, there exists a vector
n_{v}\in N_{v}
such that a hyperplane with normal vector
n_{v}
is a supporting hyperplane of the face
T.
This gives a contradiction.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

In the Euclidean norm, the Voronoi cell of a smooth point
v
of an algebraic variety
X
is contained in the normal space of
v.
Our result above gives an analogous result for polyhedral norms. The faces of
B
in
\mathrm{Type}(v)
are the faces of the unit ball that intersect the normal space of
v
and the Voronoi cell of
v
is contained in the normal cones of the dual faces corresponding to the faces in
\mathrm{Type}(v)
. We call

\bigcup_{F\in\mathrm{Type}(v)}\overline{C_{v}(B^{*},F^{*})}

the
Voronoi cone
of
v
in
X
and denote it by
\vc(v)
.

Based on
Definition

3.2
and
Proposition

3.4
, the following theorem gives a way to find all faces in
\mathrm{Type}(v)
where

is a smooth point on a variety of codimension
c<n
.
The smoothness condition
guarantees that the normal space at the point is of the expected dimension.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
X
be an algebraic variety of codimension
c<n
, and
N_{v}
be the normal space at a smooth point

. Then

\mathrm{Type}(v)=\{F\in\mathcal{F}(B)\mid N_{v}\cap F^{*}\neq\emptyset\}.

Proof.

We want to prove the equality of the two sets above for a smooth point
v\in X
.
Assume that
F\in\mathrm{Type}(v)
. By definition,
N_{v}\cap C(P,F)\neq\emptyset
, so there is a vector
w\in N_{v}
that is minimized over the face
F^{*}
of
B^{*}
. Therefore, there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\lambda w\in F^{*}
for

and hence,
\lambda w\in N_{v}\cap F^{*}
.

On the other hand, let

\lambda w\in F^{*}
for some face

of

This implies that

is minimized over

and hence,
-w\in C(B,F)
. Consequently,
N_{v}\cap C(B,F)\neq\emptyset
and
F\in\mathrm{Type}(v).

∎

4
Stratification of Codimension-One Manifolds

For codimension-one varieties, the normal space at a smooth point is spanned by a
unique vector (up to scaling) and hence is one dimensional. Consequently, the type
of such a point is a set of two opposite faces of the same dimension (
Remark

3.7
).
Using this description, we can stratify the smooth part of a variety based on the expected dimension of the Voronoi cell. In particular, we expect the dimension of the Voronoi cell to be equal to the dimension of the Voronoi cone, so
we stratify a codimension-one variety by the dimension of the Voronoi cone defined by a fixed polyhedral norm with unit ball
B
.
To that end, we will use the basic notions of topology (
Section

2.3
)
to stratify
\mathbb{R}{\mathbb{P}}^{n-1}
(
Theorem

4.2
) and general
codimension-one varieties (
Theorem

4.4
).

Let
B
be a polyhedral ball defined by a polyhedral norm
h_{\mathcal{L}}
, and let
\Sigma_{B}=\bigcup_{F\in\mathcal{F}}\{C_{F}\}
denote its inner normal fan.
Consider the following map

\displaystyle\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{R}{\mathbb{P}}^{n-1}.

(2)

This is the projection of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
onto the real projective space
\mathbb{R}{\mathbb{P}}^{n-1}
; it maps each vector to its equivalence class. The map
\varphi
is surjective and continuous. The space
\mathbb{R}{\mathbb{P}}^{n-1}
is endowed with quotient topology induced by
\varphi
and thus a set
U\subset{\mathbb{RP}}^{n-1}
is open if
\varphi^{-1}(U)
is an open set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}.
Note that
\varphi(C_{F})=\varphi(C_{-F})
and, if
U
is open, then
\varphi^{-1}(U)
is an open double cone, i.e. the union of two opposite open cones.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
F,F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
, and
F^{\prime}\notin\{F,-F\}
. Then
\varphi({C_{F}})\cap\varphi({C_{F^{\prime}}})=\emptyset
.

Proof.

Assume to the contrary that
x\in\varphi({C_{F}})\cap\varphi({C_{F^{\prime}}})
. Then
\varphi^{-1}(x)\subseteq(C_{F}\cup C_{-F})\cap(C_{F^{\prime}}\cup C_{-F^{\prime}})
. This implies that
\varphi^{-1}(x)
is an empty set. Since
\varphi
is a surjective map, we get a contradiction.
∎

Let
[n]\coloneqq\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
. For
i\in[n]
, recall that
\mathcal{F}_{i}
is the set of all the faces of dimension
n-i-1
. We use this to define the following sets:

S_{i}=\bigsqcup_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{i}}~~\varphi({C_{F}}).

Note that by construction, each set
S_{i}
is a set of dimension
i.
In the next result, we prove that the partition of sets above stratifies
\mathbb{R}{\mathbb{P}}^{n-1}.

Theorem 4.2

(Stratification of
\mathbb{R}{\mathbb{P}}^{n-1}
)
.

Let
B
be the unit ball associated to a polyhedral norm, and
\Sigma_{B}
its inner normal fan. The collection
\mathcal{S}_{B}=\{S_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n-1}
stratifies
\mathbb{R}{\mathbb{P}}^{n-1}.

Proof.

We will show that the collection
\mathcal{S}_{B}
satisfies the properties
(i)
-
(v)
of
Definition

2.12
.

To show
(i)
, we note that
\bigcup_{F\in\mathcal{F}}\{C_{F}\}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
. Hence, by surjectivity of
\varphi
, we have
{\mathbb{RP}}^{n-1}=\bigcup_{i\in[n]}S_{i}.

For all
i\in[n]
,
S_{i}
is a smooth manifold with respect to the subspace topology of
{\mathbb{RP}}^{n-1}
and
S_{i}\hookrightarrow{\mathbb{RP}}^{n-1}
is a smooth embedding. Hence,
S_{i}
is an embedded submanifold of
{\mathbb{RP}}^{n-1}
for all
i
. This shows
(ii)
holds.

We now show
(iii)
. Since
\varphi
is open and continuous, we have
\overline{\varphi(C_{F})}=\varphi(\overline{C_{F}})
for all
F\in\mathcal{F}
. Consequently,
\overline{S_{i}}=\bigsqcup_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{i}}\varphi(\overline{C_{F}})
and by
Remark

2.4
, we can rewrite this as
\overline{S_{i}}=S_{0}\sqcup S_{1}\ldots\sqcup S_{i}
. Note that
\overline{S_{i}}\setminus S_{i}=\overline{S_{i-1}}
for
i\geq 1
which is a closed set in
{\mathbb{RP}}^{n-1}
and for
i=0
,
\overline{S_{0}}\setminus S_{0}
is an empty set which is also a closed set. Therefore,
S_{i}
is locally closed for all
i\in[n]
.

For item
(iv)
, assume
i\neq j
. Then,
\mathcal{F}_{i}\cap\mathcal{F}_{j}=\emptyset
and hence,
C_{F_{i}}\cap C_{F_{j}}=\emptyset
for all
F_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{i}
and
F_{j}\in\mathcal{F}_{j}.
By using
Lemma

4.1
, it follows that
S_{i}\cap S_{j}=\emptyset
.

Finally, we prove
(v)
. Using
\overline{S_{j}}=S_{0}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup S_{j}
and from
(iv)

S_{k}\cap S_{j}=\emptyset
whenever
k\neq j
, we obtain
S_{i}\cap\overline{S_{j}}=S_{i}
if
i\leq j
and
S_{i}\cap\overline{S_{j}}=\emptyset

if
i>j
. Thus, if
S_{i}\cap\overline{S_{j}}\neq\emptyset
, then
i\leq j
and
S_{i}\subset\overline{S_{j}}
.
∎

be the map defined in (

). Consider the following set:

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(X)=\{(v,n_{v})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}{\mathbb{P}}^{n-1}\mid v\in X\text{ and }N_{v}(X)=\varphi^{-1}(n_{v})\cup\{0\}\},

where

is the normal space of

at

. Let

be the projection map from
\mathcal{N}(X)
to

given by

This map may not necessarily be surjective. We further denote by
\pi_{1}
and

the projection of
\mathcal{N}(X)
onto
X
and

respectively. Note that

and both

and

Let

\mathcal{S}_{B}:=\{\tilde{S}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n-1}

Let

. For

, we define the following sets:

(3)

In

, we will show that

To that end, we consider the following map:

(4)

Lemma 4.3
.

The map
\phi
in (

) is continuous and an open map.

Proof.

Since
\pi_{1}
is bijective,
\phi
is well defined.
Since
\mathcal{N}(X)
is endowed with the subspace topology of

, an open set of

is of the form

, for some open sets

and

.
Note that
\pi_{1}((U\times V)\cap\mathcal{N}(X))=U
which is an open set in

. Therefore,
\pi_{1}^{-1}
is a continuous map. Now, the continuity of
\phi
follows directly from the continuity of
\pi_{1}^{-1}
and
\pi_{2}
.

To show that
\phi
is an open map, it is enough to show that
\pi_{1}^{-1}
and
\pi_{2}
are open maps. Since
\pi_{1}
is bijective and
\pi_{1}^{-1}
is continuous,
\pi_{1}^{-1}
is an open map.
The map
\psi:\mathcal{N}(X)\to{\mathbb{RP}}^{n-1}
is a projection map and hence, an open map. By

, it is straightforward to verify that

is an open map.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
B
be a unit ball with respect to a polyhedral norm and let
\mathcal{S}_{B}:=\{\tilde{S}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n-1}
be the stratification of
{\mathbb{RP}}^{n-1}
. Let

be a real, smooth, connected, codimension-one manifold without boundary. If
\phi
in (
4
) is transversal to
S_{i}
for all

, then
\mathcal{X}_{B}
stratifies

with respect to

.

Proof.

We claim that
\mathcal{X}_{B}
satisfies
(i)
-
(v)
of
Definition

2.12
and therefore stratifies
X
.

Since
{\mathbb{RP}}^{n-1}=\cup_{i=0}^{n-1}\tilde{S}_{i}
by

, we get
\psi(\mathcal{N}(X))=\cup_{i=0}^{n-1}S_{i}
. Hence,
(i)
holds by (

in (

Note that for all

is a smooth embedding and hence, a submanifold of
\psi(\mathcal{N}(X))
. To show

, we need to show

is a submanifold for all

. Since,

is transversal to

for all

, by the Preimage Theorem (

),

is a submanifold of

.

To show
(iii)
, note that by
Theorem

4.2
,
\tilde{S}_{i}
is locally closed in
{\mathbb{RP}}^{n-1}
. Therefore, each
\tilde{S}_{i}
can be written as the set difference of two open sets

and
V_{i}
in
{\mathbb{RP}}^{n-1}
for all

Consequently,

S_{i}=\tilde{S}_{i}\cap\psi(\mathcal{N}(X))=(U_{i}\setminus V_{i})\cap\psi(\mathcal{N}(X)).

Since
S_{i}=(U_{i}\cap\psi(\mathcal{N}(X)))\setminus(V_{i}\cap\psi(\mathcal{N}(X)))
is a set difference of two open sets in
\psi(\mathcal{N}(X))
,
S_{i}
is locally closed in
\psi(\mathcal{N}(X))
. Moreover, the preimage of a locally closed set under a continuous map is a locally closed set. Hence,
X_{i}
is locally closed for all

Criterion
(iv)
holds by construction. Indeed, note that

and if

. Hence,

Finally, we prove

. Since

is smooth and connected,
{\psi(\mathcal{N}(X))}
is also smooth and connected. Note that for

, we have that
S_{i}\cap\overline{S_{j}}=\emptyset
. Indeed,
S_{k}=\tilde{S_{k}}\cap\psi(\mathcal{N}(X))
and
\overline{S_{k}}\subseteq\overline{\tilde{S_{k}}}\cap\psi(\mathcal{N}(X))
for all

, together with
\tilde{S_{i}}\cap\overline{\tilde{S_{j}}}=\emptyset
for
i>j
(by
Theorem

4.2
) implies
S_{i}\cap\overline{S_{j}}=\emptyset
for
i>j
.

Let
x
be a point in
S_{i}
for some
i
and
S_{i}\cap\overline{S_{j}}\neq\emptyset
for some
j>i.
Then
x\in\varphi(C_{F})
for some
F\in\mathcal{F}_{i}
. Assume to the contrary that
x\notin\overline{S_{j}}
. This implies that
x\notin\overline{\varphi(C_{F})\cap{\psi(\mathcal{N}(X))}}
for all
F\in\mathcal{F}_{j}
. Since
{\psi(\mathcal{N}(X))}
is connected, then either there exists an open neighborhood

around

is a boundary point. Since
\phi
is transversal and
X

this gives a contradiction.
∎

The stratification constructed in the above theorem distinguishes the points on

based on the expected dimension of the Voronoi cell of each point. In particular, the Voronoi cones of the points in the stratum

Remark 4.5
.

Note that, for

Theorem 4.8
.

Let
X={\mathbb{V}}(f)
be a pure codimension-one variety and let
\mathcal{X}_{B}:=\{X_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n-1}
be its stratification. Then, for all
i=0,\ldots,n-1
,
X_{i}
is a semialgebraic set.

Proof.

Given a pure codimension-one variety,
X={\mathbb{V}}(f)
, to find the set of
points in strata
X_{i}
, we note that these are exactly the points on
X
whose normal vector is in
\bigsqcup_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{i}}C_{F}.

Given a face
F
of dimension
n-i-1
, let
w_{1},\ldots,w_{i}
be the generators of the normal cone of
F
. A normal vector
v\in C_{F}
if and only if
v=\sum_{j=1}^{i}\lambda_{j}w_{j}
for
\lambda_{j}>0.

Thus, the set of points on
X
that have
F
as its type are given by the projection of the following set onto the first
n
coordinates:

\displaystyle\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{i}_{>0}~\mid~(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in X,~\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{i}\}={\rm gens}(C_{F})\text{ and }\nabla_{x}f=\sum_{j=1}^{i}\lambda_{j}w_{j}\}.

This set is semialgebraic and, therefore, by the Tarski-Seidenberg theorem, its projection is also semialgebraic. Using this we can now write the semialgebraic set describing
X_{i}
as:

\displaystyle\smash{\bigsqcup_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{i}}}\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}~\mid~

\displaystyle\exists(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{i}_{>0}\text{ such that }(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in X,

\displaystyle\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{i}\}={\rm gens}(C_{F})\text{ and }\nabla_{x}f-\sum_{j=1}^{i}\lambda_{j}w_{j}=0\},

which is a finite union of semialgebraic sets.
∎

5
Medial Axis

In
Section

4
, we stratified a codimension-one variety
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
based on the dimension of the Voronoi cones of points on
X
. We now focus on finding the locus of points in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
whose distance to
X
is minimized by at least two distinct points on
X
with respect to a given polyhedral norm,
h
. Such a set is called the
medial axis
and is denoted by
\mathrm{Med}_{h}(X)
.

5.1
Finding the Medial Axis

We begin by establishing a framework for the computation of an algebraic set that contains
\mathrm{Med}_{h}(X)
. First, we formally define the medial axis.

Definition 5.1
.

Given an algebraic variety
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a polyhedral norm
h
, the
medial axis
of
X
with respect to
h
is

\mathrm{Med}_{h}(X):=\left\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}~~\mid~~~\left|\arg\min_{x\in X}\ h(u-x)\right|>1\right\}.

Example 5.2
.

For
X={\mathbb{V}}(x^{2}-y)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
and the polyhedral norm
h
with unit ball given by the square as described in
Example

2.6

\mathrm{Med}_{h}(X)=\{(u_{1},u_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid u_{1}=0\mbox{ and }u_{2}>0\}.

Let

Let

be a point on the medial axis. Then there exist distinct points

Let

and

and

are the respective optimizing faces of

and

\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{i}
are

F_{1}
and
\hat{\ell_{1}},\ldots,\hat{\ell_{j}}
are

F_{2}

We define
J_{F_{1}}\subset\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{n},u_{1},\ldots,u_{n},\lambda]
to be the ideal generated by

and the

\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{x}f&\nabla_{x}\ell_{1}&\cdots&\nabla_{x}\ell_{i}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}.

The above conditions impose that

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a smooth codimension-one variety,
h
be a polyhedral norm whose unit ball has
faces
\mathcal{F}
and

I_{\mathrm{Eq}}=\bigcap_{G\in\mathcal{F}\times F}I_{G}

be the equidistant ideal of
X
. Let
M_{X}
be the algebraic closure of
\mathrm{Med}_{h}(X)
. Then

\mathrm{Med}_{h}(X)\subset M_{X}\subset\mathbb{V}\left(I_{\mathrm{Eq}}\right).

Proof.

Note that
\mathrm{Med}_{h}(X)\subset M_{X}
follows from the definition of algebraic closure.
To show that
M_{X}\subset\mathbb{V}\left(I_{\mathrm{Eq}}\right)
, we will show that
\mathrm{Med}_{h}(X)\subset\mathbb{V}\left(I_{\mathrm{Eq}}\right)
from the definitions of these sets.
Since
\mathbb{V}\left(I_{\mathrm{Eq}}\right)
is closed in the Zariski topology, this will imply that

M_{X}\subset\mathbb{V}\left(I_{\mathrm{Eq}}\right)
.

To show that
\mathrm{Med}_{h}(X)\subset\mathbb{V}\left(I_{\mathrm{Eq}}\right)
, let
u\in\mathrm{Med}_{h}(X)
. Then there exist distinct
x,y\in X
such that
x,y\in\arg\min\limits_{x\in X}\ h(u-x)
. Let
\lambda=h(u-x)=h(u-y)
. Let
F_{i}
and
F_{j}
be the faces at which
B_{\lambda}(u)
intersects
x
and
y
, respectively.
Then
(x,y,u,\lambda)\in\mathcal{V}_{(F_{1},F_{2})}
, so

. For any ideals
I
and
J
, we have
\mathbb{V}(I\cap J)={\mathbb{V}}(I)\cup{\mathbb{V}}(J)
. Thus,
u\in\mathbb{V}\left(I_{\mathrm{Eq}}\right)
, completing the proof.
∎

shares a bitangent affine space with a point in
M_{i2}
and these spaces are contained in supporting hyperplanes of one of the faces of
B
.

5.2
Degree Bounds

Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a codimension-one variety given by
f(x)=0
such that
\deg(f)=d
.
In this setting, we compute degree bounds of
I_{(F_{1},F_{2})}
and hence, degree bounds on various components of the equidistant locus
{\mathbb{V}}(I_{\mathrm{Eq}}).
Throughout the remainder of this section, we assume that the medial axis does not have a full-dimensional component.
In the case that it does have a full-dimensional component, the ideal is the zero ideal and all of the degree bounds below are trivially satisfied.
We first consider the degree of
I_{(F_{1},F_{2})}
in the case when
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are both vertices of the given polyhedral unit ball. Before we state our main result, we need a lemma bounding the degree of resultants.

Lemma 5.10
.

Let
K
be a field and
f(t):=\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}t^{i}
and
g(t):=\sum_{i=1}^{d}b_{i}t^{i}
be two univariate polynomials
such that
a_{i},b_{i}\in K[u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}]
and
\deg(a_{i})=\deg(b_{i})=d-i
. Then the resultant,
{\res}(f,g)\in K[u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}]
has degree at most
d^{2}
.

Proof.

Let
T
be the Sylvester matrix of
f
and
g
of size
2d
. Its nonzero entries are:

\displaystyle T_{i,i+j}

\displaystyle=a_{d-j}~~\text{ for }j=0,\dots,d\mbox{ and }i=1,\dots,d

\displaystyle T_{i,i-d+j}

\displaystyle=b_{d-j}~~\text{ for }j=0,\dots,d\mbox{ and }i=d+1,\dots,2d.

This implies that

\left\{\begin{split}&\deg_{u}(T_{i,i+j})=j&\mbox{ for }j=0,\dots,d\mbox{ and }i=1,\dots,d\\
&\deg_{u}(T_{i,i-d+j})=j&\mbox{ for }j=0,\dots,d\mbox{ and }i=d+1,\dots,2d.\end{split}\right.

(8)

Let
\mathcal{S}_{2d}
be the permutations of
[2d]
. Then
{\res}(f,g)
is given by

\res(f,g)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{2d}}\text{sign}(\sigma)T_{1,\sigma(1)}\cdots T_{d,\sigma(d)}T_{d+1,\sigma(d+1)}\cdots T_{2d,\sigma(2d)}.

Let
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{2d}
be such that any of the conditions below hold for some
i

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}1\leq i\leq d\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\sigma(i)>i+d\\
1\leq i\leq d\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\sigma(i)<i\\
d+1\leq i\leq 2d\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\sigma(i)>i\\
d+1\leq i\leq 2d\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\sigma(i)<i-d\end{array}\right.

then we have
T_{1,\sigma(1)}\dots T_{2d,\sigma(2d)}=0
.

We now consider
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{2d}
that does not satisfy any of the conditions above. In other words, we have
i\leq\sigma(i)\leq i+d
for
i=1,\dots,d
and
i-d\leq\sigma(i)\leq i
for
d+1\leq i\leq 2d
. By (
8
), we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\deg_{u}(T_{1,\sigma(1)}\dots T_{2d,\sigma(2d)})=&\sum_{i=1}^{d}\deg_{u}(T_{i,\sigma(i)})+\sum_{i=d+1}^{2d}\deg_{u}(T_{i,\sigma(i)})\\
=&\sum_{i=1}^{d}(\sigma(i)-i)+\sum_{i=d+1}^{2d}(\sigma(i)-i+d)\\
=&\sum_{i=1}^{2d}\sigma(i)-2\sum_{i=1}^{d}i\\
=&\frac{2d(2d+1)}{2}-2\frac{d(d+1)}{2}=d^{2}.\end{split}

This shows that each nonzero term of the determinant of the Sylvester matrix has degree
d^{2}
. Hence,
\res(f,g)
is the sum of polynomials of degree
d^{2}
, and its degree is at most
d^{2}
.
∎

Theorem 5.11
.

Let
X={\mathbb{V}}(f)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a codimension-one variety and
\deg(f)=d
. Let
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
be two vertices of the polyhedral unit ball. Then
I_{(F_{1},F_{2})}=\langle f(u)g(u)\rangle
for some
g\in\mathbb{R}[u]
and
\deg(I_{(F_{1},F_{2})})\leq d^{2}
.
In particular, the component of
\mathrm{Med}_{h}(X)
arising from a pair of vertices has degree at most
d^{2}-d
.

Proof.

Given vertices
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
of the unit ball, the ideal
J_{(F_{1},F_{2})}
as in (
6
) is given by

\displaystyle J_{(F_{1},F_{2})}=\langle f(x),f(y),\ell_{1}(x-u)-\lambda,\ldots,\ell_{n}(x-u)-\lambda,\hat{\ell}_{1}(y-u)-\lambda,\ldots,\hat{\ell}_{n}(y-u)-\lambda\rangle

(9)

where
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n}
(resp.
\hat{\ell}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\ell}_{n}
) correspond to the linear functionals defining the face
F_{i}
(resp.
F_{j}
).
Observe that this ideal is defined by
2n
linear polynomials and two polynomials of degree
d
. We use Gaussian elimination to solve for
x
and
y
using the linear equations. Since the
\ell_{i}
and
\hat{\ell}_{i}
are linear functionals, they can be expressed in terms of the inner product as

\ell_{i}(x)=\langle l_{i},x\rangle,\hskip 20.00003pt\hat{\ell}_{i}(y)=\langle\hat{l}_{i},y\rangle

such that
l_{i},\hat{l}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n*}
for all
i
.
Define the
n\times n
matrices
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
that have
\{l_{1},\dots,l_{n}\}
and
\{\hat{l}_{1},\dots,\hat{l}_{n}\}
as rows respectively. Note that these matrices are invertible, since the vectors
\{l_{1},\dots,l_{n}\}
(resp.
\{\hat{l}_{1},\dots,\hat{l}_{n}\}
) are linearly independent by construction. We write the system of linear equations in (
9
) as

\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}&0&-A_{1}&-\mathbf{1}\\
0&A_{2}&-A_{2}&-\mathbf{1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\
y\\
u\\
\lambda\end{bmatrix}=0

where
\mathbf{1}
denotes the column vector with entries equal to
1
. Solving for
x
and
y
, we get:

x=u+A_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{1}\lambda\hskip 20.00003pt\mbox{and}\hskip 20.00003pty=u+A_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{1}\lambda.

Substituting this into
f(x)
and
f(y)
, we get
J_{u,\lambda}=J_{(F_{1},F_{2})}\cap R[u,\lambda]=\langle g_{1}(u,\lambda),g_{2}(u,\lambda)\rangle
where

\begin{split}g_{1}(u,\lambda)\coloneqq&f(u+A_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{1}\lambda)=a_{d}\lambda^{d}+a_{d-1}\lambda^{d-1}+\ldots+a_{0},\\
g_{2}(u,\lambda)\coloneqq&f(u+A_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{1}\lambda)=b_{d}\lambda^{d}+b_{d-1}\lambda^{d-1}+\ldots+b_{0}.\end{split}

where
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
are degree
d-i
polynomials in the variables
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}
.
Since
I_{(F_{1},F_{2})}=J_{u,\lambda}\cap\mathbb{R}[u]
, using
Lemma

5.10
,
I_{(F_{1},F_{2})}
is an ideal of a polynomial of degree at most
d^{2}
. Furthermore, since
a_{0}=g_{1}(u,0)=f(u)=g_{2}(u,0)=b_{0}
, the determinant of the Sylvester matrix of
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
computed using the first column shows that
f(u)
is a factor of
{\rm res}(g_{1},g_{2})
. Hence,
I_{(F_{1},F_{2})}=\langle f(u)g(u)\rangle
for some polynomial
g(u)\in\mathbb{R}[u]
such that
\deg(g)\leq d^{2}-d.

Finally, after saturating the ideal with
f(u)
, we get that the component of
\mathrm{Med}_{h}(X)
arising from a pair of vertices has degree at most
d^{2}-d
.
∎

Next, we consider the degree of
I_{(F_{1},F_{2})}
when
F_{1}
is a vertex and
F_{2}
is a facet of the polyhedral ball.

Theorem 5.12
.

Let
X={\mathbb{V}}(f)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\deg(f)=d
. Let
F_{1}
be a vertex and
F_{2}
be a facet of the polyhedral ball. Suppose that
X
has
m
points with tangent spaces parallel to
F_{2}
. Then
I_{(F_{1},F_{2})}=I_{1}\cap\ldots\cap I_{m}
is the intersection of
m
ideals and
\deg(I_{k})\leq d
for all
k=1,\ldots,m.

Proof.

Let
X
be the given variety and
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{n}
be
n
linear functionals describing
F_{1}
. Let
z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}\in X
be the fixed
m
points with tangent spaces parallel to
F_{2}
and let
\hat{\ell}
be the functional corresponding to
F_{2}
. The ideal
I_{(F_{1},F_{2})}
corresponds to the variety of points that are equidistant from two points with optimizing faces
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
. Since
F_{2}
belongs to the
\mathrm{Type}
of only
m
points on
X
,
I_{(F_{1},F_{2})}
is given by:

I_{(F_{1},F_{2})}=\bigcap_{k=1}^{m}(J_{\{F_{1},z_{k}\}}\cap\mathbb{R}[u])

(10)

where
J_{\{F_{1},z_{k}\}}\subset\mathbb{R}[x,u,\lambda]
is given by

\displaystyle J_{\{F_{1},z_{k}\}}:=\langle f(x),\ell_{1}(x-u)-\lambda,\ldots,\ell_{n}(x-u)-\lambda,\hat{\ell}(z_{k}-u)-\lambda\rangle.

For an arbitrary
k
, let
I_{k}:=J_{\{F_{1},z_{k}\}}\cap\mathbb{R}[u]
. To prove the result, we need to show that
\deg(I_{k})\leq d
for all
k=1,\ldots,m.
To that end, after solving
n
linear equations in
J_{\{F_{i},z_{k}\}}
for
x
, we get,

J_{u,\lambda}^{(k)}:=J_{\{F_{1},z_{k}\}}\cap\mathbb{R}[u,\lambda]=\langle f(u+A^{-1}\mathbf{1}\lambda),\hat{\ell}(z_{k}-u)-\lambda\rangle

where
A
is a matrix of size
n
with rows given by the
l_{1},\ldots,l_{n}
for
l_{i}
linearly independent, such that
\ell_{i}(x)=\langle l_{i},x\rangle.

Finally,
I_{k}=J_{u,\lambda}^{(k)}\cap\mathbb{R}[u]
is generated by the resultant of
f(u+A^{-1}\mathbf{1}\lambda)
and
\hat{\ell}(z_{k}-u)-\lambda
with respect to
\lambda
. Since
\deg(f)=d
and
\hat{\ell}
is a linear functional,
\deg(I_{k})\leq d
for all
k=1,\ldots,m.

∎

Next, we consider the degrees of
I_{(F_{1},F_{2})}
with respect to two facets of the unit ball.

Theorem 5.13
.

Let
X={\mathbb{V}}(f)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\deg(f)=d
. Let
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
be two distinct facets of the polyhedral ball such that
X
has
s
points with tangent spaces parallel to
F_{1}
and
t
points with tangent spaces parallel to
F_{2}
. Then
\deg(I_{(F_{1},F_{2})})\leq st
.

Proof.

Let
F_{1},F_{2}
be defined by linear functionals
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
respectively. Then,

\displaystyle J_{(F_{1},F_{2})}

\displaystyle=\langle f(x),f(y),\ell_{1}(x-u)-\lambda,\ell_{2}(y-u)-\lambda,\ \text{rank}(M_{1}(x))=1,\ \text{rank}(M_{2}(y))=1\rangle

where
M_{1}(x)
and
M_{2}(y)
are defined as

\displaystyle M_{1}(x)=\begin{bmatrix}(\nabla f)(x)\\
(\nabla\ell_{1})(x)\end{bmatrix},\hskip 20.00003ptM_{2}(y)=\begin{bmatrix}(\nabla f)(y)\\
(\nabla\ell_{2})(y)\end{bmatrix}.

Denote the set of points in
X
tangent to a translate of
F_{1}
as
p_{1},\ldots,p_{s}
and those tangent to a translate of
F_{2}
as
q_{1},\ldots,q_{t}
. Observe that for fixed
p\in\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{s}\}
,
f(p)=0
and
\text{rank}(M_{1}(p))=1
and similarly for fixed
q\in\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{t}\}
,
f(q)=0
and
\text{rank}(M_{2}(q))=1
. Therefore, for such fixed
p,q
, the ideal
I
becomes

J_{p,q}=\langle\ell_{1}(p-u)-\lambda,\ell_{2}(q-u)-\lambda\rangle.

We can then project this ideal onto the
u
coordinates by eliminating
\lambda
. We get

I_{p,q}:=J_{p,q}\cap\mathbb{R}[u]=\langle\ell_{1}(p-u)-\ell_{2}(q-u)\rangle.

Hence,
I_{(F_{1},F_{2})}=\bigcap_{p,q}I_{p,q}
where
p\in\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{s}\}
and
q\in\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{t}\}
. Since
I_{p,q}
has degree one and
{\mathbb{V}}(I_{p,q})
is a hyperplane, we get
\deg(I_{(F_{1},F_{2})})\leq st
and
{\mathbb{V}}(I_{(F_{1},F_{2})})
consists of at most
st
hyperplanes.
∎

Remark 5.14

(
n=2
)
.

For codimension-one varieties in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
Theorem

5.11
,
Theorem

5.12
, and
Theorem

5.13
cover all possible cases and we have bounds for all the components of the equidistant locus.

Finally, we conclude this section by giving a degree bound for quadratic varieties.

Proposition 5.15
.

Let
X={\mathbb{V}}(f)
be such that
\deg(f)=2
. Then
\deg(I_{(F_{1},F_{2})})\leq 4
for any two faces
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
of the unit ball.

Proof.

Let
F_{i}
(resp.
F_{j}
) be defined by linear functionals
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{i}
(resp.
\hat{\ell}_{i},\ldots,\hat{\ell}_{j}
). Then

J_{(F_{1},F_{2})}=\langle f(x),f(y),\ell_{1}(x-u)-\lambda,\ldots,\ell_{i}(x-u)-\lambda,\hat{\ell}_{1}(y-u)-\lambda,\ldots,\hat{\ell}_{j}(y-u)-\lambda,\text{rank}(M_{1}(x))=i,\text{rank}(M_{2}(y))=j\rangle

where

\displaystyle M_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}(\nabla f)(x)&(\nabla\ell_{1})(x)&\cdots&(\nabla\ell_{i})(x)\end{bmatrix}^{\top},

\displaystyle M_{2}(y)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}(\nabla f)(y)&(\nabla\hat{\ell}_{1})(y)&\cdots&(\nabla\hat{\ell}_{j})(y)\end{bmatrix}^{\top}.

Observe that since
\deg(f)=2
,
\nabla f
is linear in
x
(or
y
) and hence, the rank constraints are linear. In particular, there are
n-i
linearly independent equations in
x
given by
\text{rank}(M_{1}(x))=i
and
n-j
linearly independent equations in
y
given by
\text{rank}(M_{2}(y))=j
. Using these linear equations along with
\ell_{m}(x-u)-\lambda,\hat{\ell}_{k}(y-u)-\lambda
for
m\in[i]
,
k\in[j]
gives
2n
linearly independent equations. By performing Gaussian elimination, we get
x_{i}=a_{i}(u,\lambda)
and
y_{i}=b_{i}(u,\lambda)
where
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
are linear functions of
u,\lambda
. Consequently,

J_{u,\lambda}:=J_{(F_{1},F_{2})}\cap\mathbb{R}[u,\lambda]=\langle\tilde{f}_{1}(u,\lambda),\tilde{f}_{2}(u,\lambda)\rangle

where
\tilde{f}_{1}(u,\lambda)=f(a_{1}(u,\lambda),\ldots,a_{n}(u,\lambda))
and
\tilde{f}_{2}=(b_{1}(u,\lambda),\ldots,b_{n}(u,\lambda))
.
Finally,
I_{(F_{1},F_{2})}=J_{u,\lambda}\cap\mathbb{R}[u]
and by
Lemma

5.10
, we get
\deg(I_{(F_{1},F_{2})})=2^{2}=4
.
∎
