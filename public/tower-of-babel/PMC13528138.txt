Associations between transformational leadership, work engagement, and occupational well-being among Finnish paramedics in prehospital emergency medical services: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Jenni Hirvikallio, Ari Haaranen, Tarja Kvist
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundParamedics in emergency medical services (EMS) work in high-demand environments in which occupational well-being is closely linked to patient safety and workforce sustainability. While transformational leadership has been associated with positive staff outcomes in health care, evidence regarding its associations with work engagement and occupational well-being in prehospital EMS remains limited. This study examines transformational leadership in Finnish EMS in relation to paramedics’ work engagement and occupational well-being, including whether the association between transformational leadership and occupational well-being is statistically consistent with an indirect pathway involving work engagement.MethodsA cross-sectional online survey was conducted among 322 Finnish paramedics working in EMS at basic or advanced levels. Transformational leadership was assessed using the 43-item Transformational Leadership Scale, work engagement using the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale (UWES-9), and occupational well-being using a single-item self-rated numeric scale (4–10). Descriptive statistics, Pearson correlations, and hierarchical multiple regression analyses were conducted, adjusting for age, gender, education level, professional title, and years of EMS experience. Bootstrap resampling (5,000 samples) was used to examine whether the association between transformational leadership and occupational well-being was statistically consistent with an indirect pathway involving work engagement.ResultsTransformational leadership had a mean score of 2.75 (SD = 1.03, range 1–5). Leadership ethics received the highest ratings, whereas feedback and rewards were rated lowest. In multiple regression analyses, transformational leadership was positively associated with work engagement (β = 0.437, p <.001) and occupational well-being (β = 0.501, p <.001) after controlling for demographic factors. Work engagement was positively associated with occupational well-being (p <.001). When work engagement was added to the model, the association between transformational leadership and occupational well-being decreased but remained statistically significant, suggesting that the association was statistically consistent with an indirect pathway involving work engagement.ConclusionsTransformational leadership was positively associated with work engagement and occupational well-being among Finnish paramedics in EMS. The association between transformational leadership and occupational well-being was statistically consistent with an indirect pathway involving work engagement. However, causal or mediational conclusions cannot be drawn due to the cross-sectional design. From a health services perspective, the findings suggest that leadership may represent a key organizational resource for supporting workforce well-being and performance in high-demand EMS systems.

Introduction

Paramedics in emergency medical services (EMS) operate in complex out-of-hospital environments that require autonomous clinical decision-making and advanced professional competencies [1]. They are routinely exposed to critical incidents, long shifts, and unpredictable working conditions, placing them at increased risk of psychological strain and occupational stress [2]. Burnout dimensions, particularly emotional exhaustion, have been associated with reduced job satisfaction and lower work engagement among paramedics [3]. Importantly, both psychological and physical well-being are associated not only with individual outcomes but also with occupational and patient safety risks in prehospital care [4]. Workforce well-being should therefore be understood not merely as an individual concern but as a system-level determinant of service reliability and patient safety.

Turnover intentions are common among EMS professionals and are closely related to occupational stress and organizational factors [5]. Analyses of paramedic attrition further frame workforce sustainability as a structural health system challenge that affects continuity of care and overall system performance [6]. Leadership practices that foster engagement and well-being may thus play a critical role in enhancing workforce stability and organizational resilience in EMS systems.

Transformational leadership is a relational, value-based leadership approach in which leaders inspire and motivate employees to exceed expectations, act as ethical role models, and support individual development and collaboration [7]. Although EMS work is guided by legislation and local care protocols, it also requires autonomous professional judgment and advanced competencies in complex and unpredictable out-of-hospital situations [1]. In this context, transformational leadership should not be understood as replacing clinical guidance or operational command structures, but as complementing them by supporting staff motivation, professional growth, shared values, and the conditions for safe and sustainable service delivery.

In nursing research, relational leadership styles, including transformational leadership, have consistently been associated with higher job satisfaction, improved work environments, and more favorable workforce outcomes [8]. Empirical studies further indicate that transformational leadership has been associated with patient safety outcomes, with workplace empowerment and job satisfaction identified as relevant explanatory mechanisms [9]. In the EMS context, transformational leadership has been positively associated with emergency medical technicians’ job satisfaction [10]. In addition, organizational and managerial support have been linked to better mental well-being among paramedics [11, 12]. Despite this emerging evidence, research on leadership at the organizational and system levels in EMS remains limited and fragmented [13].

From a theoretical perspective, transformational leadership can be conceptualized as an organizational resource that fosters motivational processes manifested in work engagement, which in turn may be associated with occupational well-being. Work engagement is characterized by vigor, dedication, and absorption in work [14]. In the present study, work engagement is conceptualized as a motivational work-related state rather than as an alternative measure of occupational well-being. Occupational well-being, in turn, reflects employees’ overall perception of their physical, mental, and social well-being at work, in line with the World Health Organization’s definition of health [15]. Together, these reflect core dimensions of workforce functioning that are particularly relevant in high-demand prehospital environments.

In Finland, EMS are organized as an integral part of the public health care system under national legislation and the guidance of the Ministry of Social Affairs and Health [16]. According to Statistics Finland, 3,558 ambulance paramedics were employed in Finland in 2023, reflecting the overall size of the national prehospital emergency workforce [17]. Finnish EMS operate at both basic and advanced levels. Basic-level units are staffed by regulated health care professionals, such as practical nurses who specialize in prehospital emergency care or registered nurses, in combination with another practical or registered nurse or a firefighter. Advanced-level units are staffed by at least one paramedic educated at a University of Applied Sciences (UAS), or alternatively by a registered nurse who has completed additional specialization in prehospital emergency care [1]. This legislatively regulated structure formally integrates EMS into the broader health care system while ensuring the professional competencies required for autonomous clinical decision-making in out-of-hospital settings. Clinical governance and leadership structures play a central role in shaping the quality and safety of paramedic services [18].

Operational activities in Finnish EMS are led by an EMS field manager [19]. According to the regulations, the role of the field manager is primarily operational. In most regions, it does not include formal administrative line management responsibilities in addition to operational leadership. In this study, the term “administrative leader” is used to refer to EMS supervisors who work during office hours and are responsible for administrative line management. In Finnish EMS systems, this role is represented by a range of job titles, such as ward manager, unit supervisor, service supervisor, and also, in some systems, EMS master. In many EMS systems, there is also an EMS manager or chief nursing officer who is responsible for strategic-level leadership and acts as the supervisor of the administrative leaders. In accordance with the national regulations, each region must also have a physician responsible for EMS, who oversees the service as a whole [19].

In practice, EMS leadership can be characterized as a distributed system in which operational, administrative, strategic, and medical leadership responsibilities are distributed across multiple actors. The physician responsible for EMS typically leads medical governance, while leadership of nursing practice and personnel is shared between administrative leaders and the EMS manager or chief nursing officer.

Despite the strategic importance of EMS within health care systems, limited empirical evidence is available on transformational leadership in relation to work engagement and occupational well-being in prehospital settings. Given the established links between workforce well-being, safety, and system sustainability, further examination of leadership as a potential determinant of engagement and well-being in EMS is warranted. Drawing on prior leadership and work engagement literature, positive associations between transformational leadership, work engagement, and occupational well-being are theoretically plausible [8, 10, 14].

This study aims to examine transformational leadership in Finnish prehospital EMS in relation to paramedics’ work engagement and occupational well-being, and to examine whether the association between transformational leadership and occupational well-being is statistically consistent with an indirect pathway involving work engagement.

Methods

Study design

The study was designed as a cross-sectional survey and is reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement.

Data collection

The study data were collected using a Webropol-based online survey conducted between January and February 2026. The survey invitation was posted in two professional Facebook groups [20] for prehospital emergency care personnel, comprising approximately 5,000 and 7,000 members, respectively. In addition, the survey link was distributed internally within several prehospital emergency care organizations to reach personnel who may not actively engage in social media groups, ensuring a broader sample while maintaining voluntary and anonymous participation.

Participants

The final sample comprised 322 Finnish paramedics working at the basic and advanced levels, including individuals in expert and educational roles within out-of-hospital EMS. Individuals working in administrative leadership roles (e.g., managerial or supervisory positions) were excluded. One of the central aims of the study was to examine associations between transformational leadership, work engagement, and occupational well-being. Hence, the sample size was determined a priori using a power analysis based on correlation. Assuming a small effect size (r =.16), a power of 80%, and a two-tailed α = 0.05, approximately 300 participants were required. A small effect size was selected as a conservative assumption because previous EMS-specific evidence on the associations among transformational leadership, work engagement, and occupational well-being is limited. The sample size estimation was based on Fisher’s z-transformation-based formula, which is commonly used for correlation analysis, and followed established principles regarding the relationship among effect size, statistical power, and significance level [21]. The final sample (N = 322) exceeded the minimum required sample size.

Instruments

Demographic data were collected from participants, including age, gender, educational level, professional title, and years of experience in EMS. Perceived occupational well-being was measured using a self-reported numeric rating scale ranging from 4 to 10 (4 = lowest, 10 = highest) consistent with previous Finnish research on work-related well-being [22].

Transformational leadership was assessed using the Transformational Leadership Scale (TLS) [23, 24]. Participants evaluated the transformational leadership behaviors of their administrative leader (i.e., their non-operational administrative leader responsible for personnel and unit-level management) using a five-point Likert scale (1 = “Strongly disagree,” 2 = “Partially disagree,” 3 = “Neutral,” 4 = “Partially agree,” 5 = “Strongly agree”). The TLS consists of 43 items covering leadership ethics, managing nursing process, feedback and rewards, and professional development. Permission to use the TLS was obtained from Professor Tarja Kvist. The scale demonstrates excellent reliability, with Cronbach’s α values ranging from 0.909 to 0.968 [24]. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha for the TLS was 0.986.

Work engagement was measured using the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale (UWES-9), developed by Wilmar B. Schaufeli, Arnold B. Bakker, and Marisa Salanova [14]. The UWES-9 is a shortened version of the original 17-item scale and includes nine items assessing vigor, dedication, and absorption. Participants rated the items on a seven-point frequency scale (0 = “never,” 1 = “almost never,” 2 = “rarely,” 3 = “sometimes,” 4 = “often,” 5 = “very often,” 6 = “always”). Authorization to use the UWES-9 was granted by its developers, Professor Wilmar Schaufeli and Professor Arnold Bakker. The UWES-9 has been validated for use in Finland by Professor Jari Hakanen and colleagues, demonstrating good reliability, with Cronbach’s α values ranging from 0.75 to 0.87 [25]. In the present study, Cronbach’s alpha for the UWES-9 was 0.943.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages) were calculated for all the demographic and study variables. Visual inspection of Q–Q plots indicated that the distributions and standardized residuals were approximately normal. The Shapiro–Wilk test showed minor deviations from normality, but the deviations were small in magnitude and did not prevent the use of parametric analyses. Overall, the assumptions required for Pearson correlation and linear regression analyses were met.

Pearson correlation coefficients were used to examine associations between transformational leadership, work engagement, and occupational well-being. Hierarchical multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to examine whether transformational leadership was associated with work engagement and occupational well-being after controlling for demographic variables (age, gender, education level, professional title, and years of EMS experience). For the regression models, unstandardized coefficients (B), standard errors (SE), standardized coefficients (β), explained variance (R²), and change in explained variance (ΔR²) were reported. In addition, a linear regression model was conducted to examine whether demographic characteristics were associated with variation in perceived transformational leadership. Bootstrap resampling with 5,000 samples was used to examine whether the association between transformational leadership and occupational well-being was statistically consistent with an indirect pathway involving work engagement. Given the cross-sectional design, this analysis was considered exploratory and descriptive, and it was not interpreted as evidence of causal mediation.

Prior to the regression analyses, multicollinearity diagnostics were conducted. Variance inflation factor (VIF) values across all regression models ranged from 1.03 to 6.60. The highest VIF values were observed for education-related dummy variables, which is expected when multiple dummy variables represent categories of the same variable. No VIF exceeded the commonly cited threshold of 10, indicating no problematic multicollinearity. When the education dummy variables were excluded from the model, the highest VIF decreased to 2.22, suggesting low multicollinearity among the remaining predictors. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29. The level of statistical significance was set at p <.05.

Results

Paramedics’ characteristics

A total of 322 paramedics participated in the study. Participants ranged in age from 20 to 60 years, with a mean age of 37.1 years (SD = 8.26). A majority of respondents were female (n = 187, 58.1%), 133 (41.3%) were male, while two respondents (0.6%) preferred not to disclose their gender. The majority held a bachelor of applied sciences degree (n = 227, 70.5%), followed by a master’s degree or equivalent (n = 64, 19.9%). A vocational qualification was reported by 31 participants (9.6%).

Most respondents worked as advanced-level paramedics (n = 260, 80.7%), 44 (13.7%) worked at the basic level, 13 (4.0%) were specialist or expert paramedics, and 5 (1.6%) reported other roles. Work experience ranged from 0 to 34 years (M = 11.1, SD = 7.08).

Transformational leadership, work engagement, and occupational well-being of paramedics

The mean score for transformational leadership was 2.75 (SD = 1.03), for work engagement 4.29 (SD = 1.16), and for occupational well-being 7.51 (SD = 1.32). Demographic characteristics associated with perceived transformational leadership

Before conducting the main multivariable analyses, demographic differences in perceived transformational leadership were examined. Educational level was associated with transformational leadership in bivariate analyses (one-way ANOVA, F(2, 319) = 3.35, p =.036). Post hoc Bonferroni comparisons indicated that respondents with a vocational qualification reported higher transformational leadership scores compared to those holding a master’s or advanced degree (mean difference = 0.56, p =.036). No statistically significant differences were observed between vocational and bachelor-level education or between bachelor- and master-level groups.

Subsequently, a multivariable regression analysis was conducted to examine whether demographic variables (age, gender, years of EMS experience, education level, and professional title) were independently associated with perceived transformational leadership. The overall model was not statistically significant, F(8, 307) = 1.36, p =.216, corresponding to 3.4% of the variance in transformational leadership (R² = 0.034, adjusted R² = 0.009). None of the demographic variables emerged as significant independent predictors in the multivariable model (all p >.05). Overall, these findings indicate that demographic characteristics did not independently explain variation in perceived transformational leadership in the multivariable model.

Correlations between transformational leadership, work engagement, and occupational well-being

The Pearson correlation analyses indicated that transformational leadership, work engagement, and occupational well-being were all positively and statistically significantly correlated (p <.001). Transformational leadership was correlated with work engagement (r =.427) and occupational well-being (r =.509), whereas work engagement showed the strongest correlation with occupational well-being (r =.560). Associations between transformational leadership and work engagement

Hierarchical multiple regression was conducted to examine whether transformational leadership was associated with work engagement after controlling for demographic variables (n = 316, Table 5).

In Model 1, demographic variables (age, gender, years of EMS experience, education level, and professional title) accounted for 3.0% of the variance in work engagement (R² = 0.030, adjusted R² = 0.004), and the overall model was not statistically significant, F(8, 307) = 1.17, p =.318.

In Model 2, the inclusion of transformational leadership increased the explained variance (ΔR² = 0.184, p <.001), and the overall model was statistically significant, F(9, 306) = 9.24, p <.001. Transformational leadership was positively associated with work engagement (β = 0.437, B = 0.494, p <.001). The variable years of EMS experience was negatively associated with work engagement (β = −0.180, p =.018).

In the final model, 21.4% of the variance in work engagement was explained (R² = 0.214, adjusted R² = 0.190), with transformational leadership remaining a significant positive predictor.

Associations between transformational leadership and occupational well-being

Hierarchical multiple regression was conducted to examine whether transformational leadership was associated with occupational well-being after controlling for demographic variables (n = 314, Table 6).

In Model 1, demographic variables (age, gender, years of EMS experience, education level, and professional title) accounted for 2.8% of the variance in occupational well-being (R² = 0.028, adjusted R² = 0.002), and the overall model was not statistically significant, F(8, 305) = 1.08, p =.375.

In Model 2, the inclusion of transformational leadership increased the explained variance (ΔR² = 0.242, p <.001), and the overall model was statistically significant, F(9, 304) = 12.48, p <.001. Transformational leadership was positively associated with occupational well-being (β = 0.501, B = 0.643, p <.001).

The final model explained 27.0% of the variance in occupational well-being (R² = 0.270, adjusted R² = 0.248), with transformational leadership emerging as a significant predictor and none of the demographic variables reaching statistical significance.

Exploratory analysis of an indirect pathway involving work engagement

Hierarchical regression analyses with bootstrap resampling (5,000 samples) were conducted (n = 314) to examine whether the association between transformational leadership and occupational well-being was statistically consistent with an indirect pathway involving work engagement. Given the cross-sectional design, the analysis was interpreted as exploratory and non-causal.

Transformational leadership was positively associated with work engagement (B = 0.494, 95% bootstrap CI [0.366, 0.619], p <.001). When occupational well-being was entered as the dependent variable, work engagement was positively associated with occupational well-being after controlling for transformational leadership and demographic variables (B = 0.490, 95% bootstrap CI [0.353, 0.621], p <.001). In the adjusted model that included work engagement, the variable years of EMS experience was also positively associated with occupational well-being (B = 0.033, p =.010).

The coefficient for transformational leadership on occupational well-being decreased when work engagement was included in the model (B = 0.400, 95% bootstrap CI [0.255, 0.554], p <.001), while remaining statistically significant. The product-of-coefficients estimate (a × b) was 0.242 (SE = 0.046). An approximate 95% confidence interval for this estimate was calculated using the delta method (Sobel-type approximation), yielding 95% CI [0.152, 0.332]. The confidence interval did not include zero; therefore, the association between transformational leadership and occupational well-being was statistically consistent with an indirect pathway involving work engagement. This result should be interpreted as a statistical association only and not as evidence of causal mediation. Discussion

Transformational leadership was perceived as being at a moderate level among Finnish paramedics working in prehospital EMS. Leadership ethics received relatively higher ratings, whereas developmental and feedback-oriented aspects of leadership were rated lower overall. This imbalance may be particularly relevant in high-demand EMS environments, where continuous professional support and structured feedback are important for sustaining workforce engagement and well-being. A similar distribution of transformational leadership dimensions has been reported among Finnish hospital nursing staff, with relatively high ratings for leadership ethics and lower ratings for feedback and rewards [23]. Moreover, findings from Finnish hospital settings have emphasized the need to strengthen feedback practices and leadership visibility [24].

Leadership practices appear to be highly relevant for workforce functioning in prehospital emergency care settings. In this study, transformational leadership was positively associated with both work engagement and occupational well-being, and work engagement was also positively associated with occupational well-being. These findings suggest that work engagement may be important when examining how leadership is linked to occupational well-being in EMS, although the cross-sectional design precludes causal interpretation. This finding is consistent with earlier research showing that transformational leadership is associated with emergency medical technicians’ job satisfaction [10] and with evidence that managerial support is linked to better mental well-being among ambulance personnel [11]. From a health services perspective, these associations are important in resource-constrained EMS systems, where workforce well-being is closely connected to service continuity. However, as occupational well-being was assessed using a single-item overall rating, the findings should be interpreted as a reflection of paramedics’ general self-rated occupational well-being rather than specific physical, mental, or social dimensions of well-being.

Strengthening structured feedback practices may represent a practical organizational target in prehospital emergency care. Previous research suggests that feedback practices in ambulance services are often episodic and case-based rather than systematically embedded within organizational routines [26]. In EMS work, where repeated exposure to high-acuity and potentially traumatic incidents is common, high-quality feedback and recognition may be particularly important in buffering psychological strain [2]. Qualitative findings from Finnish EMS further indicate that high job demands combined with limited organizational resources contribute to the emotional burden in prehospital settings [27].

The need to balance supportive and directive leadership may be particularly relevant in EMS, where extreme and time-pressured situations may require more directive and task-focused leadership behaviors to ensure safety and coordinated action [28]. Evidence from emergency firefighting simulations also suggests that directive leadership may reduce subordinates’ stress during acute emergency situations [29]. However, situational directiveness and transformational leadership should not be viewed as mutually exclusive. While directive leadership may be needed during acute operational situations, transformational leadership at the administrative and organizational levels may help build the trust, motivation, and professional support needed for effective functioning under highly demanding conditions. This interpretation is consistent with the present findings and with previous EMS research linking transformational leadership with emergency medical technicians’ job satisfaction [10]. In practical terms, EMS organizations could strengthen these leadership processes by integrating structured feedback into shift handovers, establishing regular supportive supervisory practices, and providing leadership training for EMS managers and supervisors, particularly for clinicians transitioning into administrative leadership roles.

The negative association between years of EMS experience and work engagement warrants cautious interpretation. One possible interpretation is that cumulative exposure to demanding and high-acuity EMS work may erode work engagement over time, as repeated exposure to critical incidents has been linked to psychological strain, emotional burden, and impaired recovery among ambulance personnel [2]. In addition, emotional exhaustion and depersonalization, as burnout dimensions, have been identified as negative predictors of higher work engagement in prehospital EMS [3]. At the same time, greater professional experience may also be associated with better adaptation to workplace demands and greater professional stability [30]. Given the cross-sectional design, these interpretations should remain cautious, and these findings may reflect a complex interplay between cumulative exposure, career stage, and motivational processes.

From a health services perspective, the findings highlight that leadership in prehospital EMS is both a workforce issue and a service delivery issue. Compromised well-being among ambulance clinicians has been linked to occupational and patient safety risks in prehospital EMS [4], while organizational and team-level support are relevant to the mental health and well-being of ambulance personnel [12]. In publicly funded EMS systems facing increasing demand and workforce pressures, leadership practices may influence staff well-being, service continuity, safety, and system capacity to meet population needs. In this context, strengthening transformational leadership competencies may be a practical organizational strategy, particularly in EMS settings, where clinical professionals often transition into managerial roles without formal leadership preparation [31]. More broadly, these findings contribute to the emerging body of research on organizational-level leadership in EMS [13].

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes any conclusions regarding causality; thus, the associations observed between transformational leadership, work engagement, and occupational well-being cannot be interpreted as causal relationships. The analysis of the indirect pathway should therefore be understood as exploratory and descriptive. Causal mediation analysis would require longitudinal, experimental, or intervention designs that can establish temporal ordering and reduce the risk of reverse causality and unmeasured confounding. Second, the study was conducted using an online survey distributed through professional social media groups and organizational networks, which is why the response rate could not be determined. This issue may limit the generalizability of the findings and introduce the possibility of self-selection bias. Although recruitment via professional social media platforms has been shown to be a feasible strategy for reaching health care professionals [20], the representativeness of the sample cannot be fully ensured.

Third, approximately 20% of participants reported holding a master’s degree or equivalent qualification. This proportion may be higher than what might typically be expected among paramedics engaged primarily in frontline operational work and could reflect a degree of self-selection in survey participation. It is possible that individuals with a particular interest in professional development or research-related topics were more inclined to respond to the survey. Consequently, the findings may not be fully generalizable to all Finnish paramedics across different educational backgrounds, professional roles, and recruitment channels, including those with vocational qualifications or those not active in professional social media networks. At the same time, previous research indicated that the educational level and professional autonomy of paramedics have increased in recent years, which has contributed to evolving expectations and new challenges for supervisory and leadership practices within prehospital EMS [32].

Respondents may have been nested within organizational units and may have evaluated the same administrative leader; hence, clustering of observations is plausible in this context. As organizational, unit-level, or leader-level identifiers were not collected to preserve participant anonymity, multilevel modeling or cluster-robust standard errors could not be applied. If such clustering was present, then standard errors may have been underestimated and the statistical significance of the observed associations may therefore be overstated to some extent. Consequently, the strength and precision of the reported associations should be interpreted with caution. Future studies should use multilevel designs to better distinguish individual-level and organizational-level variation. The data were collected during a period in which national situation reporting on Finnish wellbeing services counties highlighted regional variation, accumulated deficits, and uncertainty in service and economic conditions [33]. This broader organizational context may have influenced how some respondents evaluated leadership and occupational well-being, although the present study cannot assess such contextual effects directly.

Fourth, some participants reported challenges in responding to the UWES-9 items due to the structure of EMS shift work. Because many paramedics work 24-hour shifts only once or twice per week, certain response options on the frequency-based scale may have been difficult to apply precisely. Although the UWES-9 has been validated in Finland, it has not, to our knowledge, been specifically validated for EMS shift schedules. This may have introduced measurement imprecision, particularly in how participants interpreted frequency-based response options. Information on shift patterns was not collected, which is why post-hoc comparisons between different shift schedules could not be conducted. Future research should examine the validity and applicability of the UWES-9 in EMS populations with varying shift patterns. Finally, all the measures were based on self-report instruments. Occupational well-being was assessed using a single-item self-rating scale. Although similar single-item approaches have been used in Finnish research contexts [22], the measure captures only an overall self-rated evaluation of occupational well-being and does not distinguish between its physical, mental, and social dimensions. This may have limited measurement precision and should be considered when interpreting the findings. Self-reported data are also inherently subject to potential response biases, including social desirability bias and common method variance [34]. Because all study variables were collected from the same respondents at the same time point, common method variance may have inflated the observed associations.

Conclusions

Transformational leadership was positively associated with both work engagement and occupational well-being among Finnish paramedics in EMS. The association between transformational leadership and occupational well-being was statistically consistent with an indirect pathway involving work engagement; however, causal or mediational conclusions cannot be drawn due to the cross-sectional design. Nevertheless, the findings suggest that leadership may be relevant to motivational conditions at work that are linked to occupational well-being among paramedics.

From a health services perspective, these findings underscore the importance of leadership as an organizational resource in publicly funded EMS systems facing increasing demand and workforce pressures. Leadership may be relevant not only to occupational well-being directly but also to motivational processes associated with workforce functioning and sustainability in high-demand prehospital settings. To complement these quantitative findings, qualitative research is needed to better understand how leadership is enacted in EMS, which leadership practices are perceived as supportive or detrimental, and how leadership could be further developed to support work engagement and occupational well-being in practice.
