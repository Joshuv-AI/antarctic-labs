Denoising of 4D dynamic PET images using spatiotemporal regularization with integrated temporal restoration (SPRINTER)
Authors: Hamed Yousefi, Mahdjoub Hamdi, Yujie Wang, Biwen Wang, Richard Laforest, Matthew Brier, Yasheng Chen, Hongyu An
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeHigh-quality 4D dynamic PET imaging is often compromised by noise, especially in low-count frames, which limits clinical utility and quantitative accuracy. This study proposes a novel spatiotemporal denoising method (SPRINTER) that integrates anatomical priors, self-supervised adaptive principal component analysis (aPCA), and deep learning to enhance image quality and preserve time activity fidelity without relying on ground-truth data.MethodsThe method combines anatomically guided aPCA-based temporal smoothing with a supervised 3D ResUNet for spatial denoising. The resulting high signal-to-noise ratio, spatially denoised images are iteratively refined using a temporal regularization strategy to enhance contrast. The model was trained using brain scan datasets from 220 participants who were administered one of the following PET/MRI radiotracers: 18F-AV45, 18F-FDG, 15O-HO, 15O-OO, and 11C-PiB for generalizability testing. Evaluations were performed on synthetic simulations with known ground-truth data and real acquired brain scan data. We compared the proposed method against multiple baseline approaches, including conventional reconstruction (OSEM), spatial-only and temporal-only denoising strategies, and two unsupervised reference methods: Non-Local Means (NLM) filtering, a classical denoising approach, and Noise2Void (N2V). These baselines were selected to reflect both conventional and recent unsupervised deep learning denoising paradigms applicable to PET imaging. Performance was assessed using both synthetic simulations with known ground truth and real acquired PET data. In addition to voxel-level image quality metrics, the impact of denoising on downstream quantitative analysis was evaluated using Ki parametric imaging derived from simulation data. This enabled direct assessment of tracer kinetic estimation accuracy across different denoising strategies.ResultsWe compared SPRINTER with OSEM, spatial-only, temporal-only, and two existing unsupervised denoising approaches, including NLM and Noise2Void. Across both simulation and acquired datasets, SPRINTER significantly outperformed all comparison methods (p < 0.05, ANOVA with Bonferroni multiple comparison corrections). The largest gains were observed for the noisiest data using 15O-HO and 15O-OO tracers, where SPRINTER achieved marked SNR improvements in acquired data (e.g., 15O-HO: 3.92 ± 1.28 vs. 2.68 ± 1.24; p = 0.096; 15O-OO: 3.71 ± 1.79 vs. 2.53 ± 1.15 for NLM; p = 0.082). These improvements were consistently superior to those obtained with NLM and Noise2Void, which showed either spatial blurring or limited performance in low-count frames. In simulation studies, SPRINTER significantly reduced voxel-wise reconstruction error compared to all baselines (NRMSE: 0.07 ± 0.04 and 0.09 ± 0.05 vs. 0.33 ± 0.08 to 0.07 and 0.37 ± 0.31 for NLM; p < 0.0001), while also outperforming NLM and Noise2Void, which exhibited higher residual error and reduced temporal consistency. Importantly, contrast was preserved and enhanced, with improved CNR particularly in 1⁸F-FDG and 11C-PiB (e.g., 1⁸F-FDG: 0.37 ± 0.33 vs. 0.18 ± 0.19; p < 0.0001; 11C-PiB: 0.54 ± 0.08 vs. 0.41 ± 0.12; p < 0.001), indicating effective denoising without loss of biologically relevant signal. While NLM and spatial-only methods achieved comparable SNR improvements, this was largely driven by oversmoothing. In contrast, with a similar level of SNR enhancement, the incorporation of temporal information further improved structural fidelity, as reflected by higher SSIM values across all tracers in simulation data (e.g., 1⁸F-AV45: 0.73 ± 0.14 vs. 0.17 ± 0.08 for NLM; p < 0.0001).ConclusionThis study introduces a robust, self-supervised spatiotemporal denoising method for 4D dynamic PET imaging of the brain. SPRINTER spatiotemporal denoising leverages anatomical guidance and temporal dynamics to enhance spatial clarity and temporal consistency. Its radiotracer-agnostic design and rigorous self-supervision make it well-suited for clinical applications where high-quality reference data are unavailable.Keywords: Adaptive principal component analysis, Self-supervised learning, 3D ResUNet, Temporal regularization, Radiotracer-agnostic

Introduction

Dynamic four-dimensional (4D) positron emission tomography (PET) significantly advanced molecular imaging by achieving continuous in vivo monitoring of radiotracer distribution across specific regions of interest (ROI) over time. Quantitative analysis of 4D dynamic PET data provides valuable insights into physiological processes, disease mechanisms, and therapeutic responses [1]. However, obtaining high-quality 4D PET images remains challenging due to high noise levels in low-count dynamic frames, which compromises signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and quantitative accuracy.

Iterative image reconstruction techniques, such as maximum likelihood expectation maximization (MLEM) and ordered-subsets expectation maximization (OSEM), are commonly employed to account for the noise characteristics of PET data [2]. Deep learning is a promising tool for noise reduction in PET images [2–4]. Many deep learning approaches use supervised learning paradigms, where models are trained on paired datasets comprising noisy and high-quality images [5, 6]. However, these approaches are impractical for 4D PET because acquiring high-quality ground-truth datasets is not feasible [7, 8]. To overcome this issue, some studies use simulation data. However, differences in simulation data and real-world clinical data often lead to suboptimal performance of the deep learning model. Prior studies have demonstrated that domain shifts between simulated and real-world medical imaging data can significantly degrade model performance, underscoring the limitations of simulation-based training and the need for robust generalization strategies [9, 10]. This technical challenge is further complicated by variations in scanner configurations and acquisition protocols, thereby limiting the generalizability of these models [4].

Unsupervised learning methods such as neural networks trained without explicit ground-truth references have been successfully used for denoising, [11–14]. One approach leveraged a neural network with random initialization and noise input to generate denoised images through a learned prior [15]. Techniques such as Noise2Noise and Noise2Void reduce the need for paired datasets [16]. Noise2Noise requires multiple noise realizations to achieve noise cancellation, whereas Noise2Void operates with a single noisy input by masking specific pixels and predicting their values [5]. Although these methods reduce noise without ground-truth data, they depend on spatial neighbors and are suboptimal for dynamic PET where temporal information is critical in radiotracer kinetics. A key challenge is to balance noise reduction with preservation of temporal/spatial fidelity. These methods may inadvertently introduce artifacts into images, particularly in low-signal regions in early dynamic frames. Denoising methods that rely only on spatial information do not account for temporal consistency across frames, resulting in temporal variations that do not reflect the accurate underlying time activity curves (TACs). Standard denoising methods, such as pixel-wise kernel-based approaches, often introduce over-smoothing particularly in dynamic data, which may blur or distort small structures or rapid temporal variations and lead to TAC inaccuracies [17] [18]. These methods inadequately utilize temporal information, necessitating the development of approaches that integrate both spatial and temporal information [3, 19].

Traditional post-processing denoising methods such as bilateral filtering [20] and non-local means (NLM) filtering [21, 22] have been widely applied to PET imaging. Bilateral filtering reduces noise while preserving edges by combining spatial proximity and intensity similarity, whereas NLM exploits redundancy across the image by averaging similar patches to achieve effective noise suppression. These approaches have demonstrated strong performance in static PET imaging; however, they primarily rely on spatial information and do not explicitly incorporate temporal correlations inherent in dynamic PET data. As a result, they may introduce temporal inconsistencies and distort TACs, particularly in low-count frames where temporal fidelity is critical [22]. These limitations highlight the need for denoising strategies that jointly leverage both spatial and temporal information to preserve quantitative accuracy in dynamic PET imaging.

The lack of temporal integration is especially problematic in 4D PET because it can increase noise during prolonged iterations and ultimately compromise the accuracy of quantitative measurements [23]. Few denoising methods are designed to fully leverage this valuable temporal information [19]. Incorporating temporal information into denoising methods ensures that time-dependent dynamic changes are preserved, thereby improving the overall accuracy of data interpretation [18]. Denoising methods that leverage temporal information may effectively reduce noise without compromising spatial details, particularly in low-count frames. Several denoising methods that utilize both spatial and temporal correlations can enhance image quality while preserving temporal fidelity [24–26].

Recent approaches include machine learning techniques trained on synthetic TACs [27], and advanced filtering methods like Spatiotemporal Expectation–Maximization (STEM) and Kinetics-Induced Block Matching 5D (KIBM5D) [28, 29]. The Spatiotemporal Primal–Dual Network (STPDnet) is a model-based deep learning method that combines traditional reconstruction interpretability with neural network learning using 3D spatiotemporal convolutions to address challenges in dynamic low-count PET imaging [19]. STEM performs iterative deconvolution, and STPDnet integrates spatiotemporal correlations through learned operators. These methods are promising but have practical limitations. For example, STEM filtering introduces bias in early low-count frames due to aggressive temporal averaging, and requires application-specific tuning of kernel size and iteration numbers to balance the bias-variance trade-off. KIBM5D also relies on manual tuning of patch size, search window, group size, threshold, and reference patch step size, which can complicate optimization and reproducibility. Although deep learning models produce rapid inference after training, they can be prone to overfitting in extremely low-count data if trained on limited or homogeneous datasets. These challenges highlight the need for lightweight and generalizable solutions that require minimal parameter tuning and maintain robustness across varying radiotracers and acquisition protocols.

Here, we developed a lightweight, data-efficient spatiotemporal denoising method called SPRINTER, which preserves TAC accuracy without relying on ground truth. We applied SPRINTER to 4D dynamic PET data obtained with 18F-AV45, 18F-FDG, 15O-HOHO, 15O-OO, and 11C-PiB radiotracers, which have distinct spatiotemporal characteristics, and compared the results with those obtained using other denoising methods. We evaluated the denoising methods using simulation with known ground-truth data and acquired PET/MRI data from brain scans of several patient cohorts.

Materials and methods

Participants and PET/MRI image acquisition

This study is a retrospective data analysis using existing neuro PET data. 220 participants were enrolled at Washington University School of Medicine with Institutional Review Board approval and participants’ written consent. PET/MRI images were acquired simultaneously using the Biograph mMR scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). The participants were scanned using one of five radiotracers: 18F-AV45, 18F-FDG, 15O-HO, 15O-OO, and 11C-PiB. Dynamic PET list-mode data were acquired immediately after radiotracer injection. For MRI, a T1-weighted MPRAGE sequence was acquired with the following parameters: TR/TE = 2300/2.95 ms, TI = 900 ms, flip angle = 9°, voxel size = 1.05×1.05×1.2 mm3. A Dixon scan (TE1/TE2 = 1.23/2.46 ms, TR = 3.6 ms) was acquired for attenuation correction. PET reconstruction was performed using OSEM with 3 iterations, 21 subsets, and post-reconstruction Gaussian smoothing, along with relative scatter correction. A previously published deep learning–based attenuation correction derived from the co-registered Dixon MRI was applied [30, 31]. Overview of spatiotemporal denoising

SPRINTER achieves noise removal in 4D dynamic PET images by integrating iterative regularization with self-supervised spatial denoising and temporal smoothing. The method incorporates the following algorithms: adaptive principal component analysis (aPCA) for self-supervised temporal smoothing, spatial denoising using a 3D ResUNet, and temporal denoising using the principal components (PCs) derived from the spatially denoised images. The spatial and temporal denoising steps are ultimately combined into an iterative spatiotemporal denoising process by regularization (Fig. 1). The code used in this study will be made available on GitHub, enabling reproducibility of the proposed method.

Adaptive principal component analysis

One of the primary innovations of SPRINTER is using aPCA to resolve three central challenges: the absence of ground-truth data in supervised methods, the inconsistency between TACs in input and target images typically found in unsupervised methods, and SNR variations due to differing frame lengths in 4D PET image reconstruction. The aPCA leverages regional-temporal characteristics of the 4D dynamic PET data. We hypothesize that a specific tissue type exhibits distinct radiotracer uptake behavior, and SNR varies across different time frames due to differences in radiotracer dynamics and reconstruction parameters. Thus, aPCA conducts temporal PCA separately for each ROI, which is obtained from segmentation performed on T1-weighted MRI images using FreeSurfer tools. This converts noisy 4D dynamic PET images into a 2D voxel-wise matrix representation (Fig. 2). Here, refers to the ROI among eight regions segmented using FreeSurfer, which include the cerebral and cerebellum white matter (WM) and gray matter (GM), cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), vessels, brain stem, and non-brain regions. These regions were chosen to capture a broad range of radiotracer dynamics across cortical and subcortical structures while ensuring that each ROI contains sufficient numbers of voxels for reliable PCA decomposition. This selection balances regional heterogeneity with computational feasibility, supporting generalizable spatiotemporal analysis. Above, represents all voxels within . The anatomically guided aPCA method is applied to each ROI independently, assuming the TACs within each are similar and can be represented by weighted summation of the first PCs. To account for varying noise levels across different time frames, the optimal number of PCs for each time point is selected dynamically based on a cost function to maintain the TAC error below 5% within each ROI. This approach balances noise reduction with the need to preserve temporal consistency of TACs across 4D dynamic PET data. Subsequently, a moving average filter is applied over time to further smooth the output and refine the selection of PCs for each ROI. Figure 2 shows the sample operation for the cerebral WM. Spatial denoising

Self-supervised aPCA effectively generates denoised images, but is time-consuming, depends on tissue segmentation, and may introduce artifacts near predefined ROI boundaries. We only use aPCA to generate denoised images that are used to train a supervised deep-learning spatial denoising network. We employed a 3D patch–based ResUNet model [30], which utilizes 3D T1-weighted MRI image and 3D PET image channels at each time point. The training dataset includes dynamic PET frames from 18F-AV45, 18F-FDG, 15O-OO, and 15O-HO radiotracers, while frames from 11C-PiB are excluded to evaluate the model’s performance on control data. T1-weighted MRI scans were acquired for all subjects and used to aid in spatial denoising. To ensure accurate alignment between the modalities, affine registration was applied to align the T1-MRI scans with the dynamic PET frames. ROIs required for the aPCA method were delineated using FreeSurfer tools. The number of frames and their durations varied depending on the radiotracer and individual scanning protocols. Detailed information on the time frames and data samples used for training and validation of the 3D ResUNet model is provided in Table 1. Although the ResUNet-based method significantly reduces computational complexity and runtime from approximately 200 s to just a few seconds per frame, the resulting images are often oversmoothed and exhibit a low temporal signal-to-noise ratio (tSNR). This limitation necessitates further post-processing to enhance contrast and resolution.

Convoluted neural network (CNN) structure and training

Spatial denoising was achieved using a 3D ResUNet tailored for PET image denoising. We partitioned 220 subjects, comprising various tracer types as indicated in Table 1, into training, validation, and testing groups with a non-uniform temporal distribution. Intensity normalization of the 4D dynamic PET data was applied using each subject’s mean and standard deviation prior to training. The ResUNet was trained on 32×32×32 3D image patches, centered on the voxel of interest, with non-overlapping patches extracted during training. The Adam optimizer was used with a batch size of 10. The ResUNet architecture included 7 layers in both the contraction and expansion paths, utilizing convolutional kernel sizes (7, 5, 3, 1) in the contraction path and (1, 3, 7, 9) in the expansion path. Leaky ReLU (slope of 0.2) was applied in the contraction path, whereas standard ReLU was used in the expansion path. Training input comprised registered MRI data and 3D temporal-sampled PET data, with output targets from aPCA-based denoised PET images. The L1 norm of the absolute difference between training target and individual frames of the 4D dynamic PET data served as the objective function. The initial learning rate was 10⁻3, halved every 50,000 batches. Evaluation involved moving window patches of size 32×32×32 with an 8-pixel step size, generating denoised images from the central 16×16×16 voxels of each patch.

Simulation

Synthetic dynamic PET images of the brain were generated as the ground truth to comparatively evaluate the denoising methods using an analytical simulation tool [34]. A 3D brain atlas was created with the FreeSurfer framework [35] from T1 MRI data, which was then aligned with the PET image, resulting in a 344 × 344 × 127 matrix with voxel dimensions of 1.65 × 1.65 × 1.64 mm3. The atlas divided the brain into 61 ROIs, which were selected based on voxel indices from FreeSurfer output. Average activity in each ROI was extracted in Bq/L for each time frame, accounting for varying noise levels between early and late time frames. The PET brain ROI 3D matrix was forward-projected to sinogram space, resulting in a 344 × 252 × 837 matrix. This matrix was scaled to the measured sinogram and attenuated through voxel-wise multiplication with an integral attenuation coefficient derived from forward projecting the brain attenuation map. The attenuated sinogram was divided voxel-wise by an extended normalization matrix. To ensure realistic simulation conditions, the total counts in the sinogram were scaled to reflect clinically relevant activity levels and acquisition durations. Specifically, the simulated activity was normalized to match typical brain PET studies with injected doses of ~ 700–950 MBq and frame durations corresponding to the dynamic framing protocols listed in Table 1. PET coincidence counting follows a Poisson process. Thus, Poisson noise was added to the sinogram data, which was then scaled to the acquisition time frame to ensure consistent activity. Noise levels were modeled assuming Poisson counting statistics, where the variance equals the mean number of detected events. Consequently, shorter early frames exhibited lower total counts and higher noise, whereas longer late frames showed higher counts and a better signal-to-noise ratio. The count level for each frame was scaled proportionally to its acquisition time, ensuring realistic temporal variation in noise characteristics across the dynamic sequence. The average total true coincidence counts per frame ranged from approximately in early frames to in late frames. To evaluate robustness under varying noise conditions, the simulated sinograms were generated at standard count levels corresponding to full-dose acquisition, and the resulting reconstructed images inherently reflected frame-dependent noise distributions observed in clinical PET imaging. Finally, the 3D OSEM algorithm was used to reconstruct sinogram data without and with noise [36]. The noise-free PET images were the ground truth to evaluate the denoising method performance.

To assess the impact of denoising on kinetic modeling, Ki parametric images were computed for simulated 18F-FDG data with known ground truth. Kinetic analysis was performed using the EMATA toolbox [37], which enables automated extraction of arterial input functions and tracer kinetic modeling for 18F-FDG brain studies. The accuracy of Ki estimation was evaluated in WM and GM regions using NMSE relative to the ground-truth parametric maps.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to assess differences among denoising techniques, including SPRINTER, spatial-only, temporal-only, and OSEM baseline approaches. We additionally compared the proposed method with NLM filtering as a classical denoising approach and Noise2Void as a representative unsupervised deep learning method. These methods were applied to both simulated and real acquired PET datasets to provide a more comprehensive evaluation. For post-hoc pairwise comparisons between methods, Bonferroni correction was applied to control for the increased risk of Type I error due to multiple comparisons. All statistical tests, graphs, and plots were created using GraphPad Prism (version 10.3.1). A p-value < 0.05 is considered statistically significant.

Results

Comparisons of SPRINTER and other denoising methods on simulation data

The simulation data provided ground truth to evaluate the accuracy of different denoising techniques. We calculated the normalized root mean squared error (NRMSE) for the activity of each voxel and compared the results of the denoising methods to the ground truth. The TAC within a 5 × 5 × 5 voxel cube demonstrates the superior consistency of the SPRINTER spatiotemporal denoising method with the ground truth (Fig. 3). We measured CNR and SNR for cerebral WM and GM activity to determine the trade-offs between noise reduction and signal preservation (Fig. 4). Compared to NLM, the proposed spatiotemporal SPRINTER method demonstrated substantial improvements across all tracers and evaluation metrics. For NRMSE, reconstruction error was significantly reduced for all tracers, including 18F-FDG (0.58 ± 0.33 to 0.09 ± 0.04, ~ 84% reduction), 18F-AV45 (0.42 ± 0.07 to 0.07 ± 0.02, ~ 83% reduction), 11C-PiB (0.27 ± 0.16 to 0.12 ± 0.03, ~ 56% reduction), 15O-HO (0.33 ± 0.08 to 0.07 ± 0.04, ~ 78% reduction), and 15O-OO (0.37 ± 0.31 to 0.09 ± 0.05, ~ 76% reduction) (Fig. 3).

We also used the Structural Similarity Index (SSIM) to evaluate the performance of different denoising methods using ground-truth simulation data. Correspondingly, SSIM values were consistently increased, with 18F-FDG improving from 0.17 ± 0.08 to 0.73 ± 0.14 (~ 329% increase), 18F-AV45 from 0.19 ± 0.08 to 0.83 ± 0.04 (~ 337% increase), 11C-PiB from 0.27 ± 0.09 to 0.57 ± 0.12 (~ 111% increase), 15O-HO from 0.25 ± 0.08 to 0.73 ± 0.30 (~ 192% increase), and 15O-OO from 0.25 ± 0.06 to 0.64 ± 0.09 (~ 156% increase). SPRINTER achieved the highest SSIM scores and lowest NRMSE compared to the ground truth among all denoising methods (Fig. 4).

The impact of denoising on kinetic parameter estimation was evaluated using Ki parametric imaging in simulation data. The proposed spatiotemporal method achieved lower NMSE in both WM and GM regions compared to all other methods, indicating improved accuracy in recovering tracer kinetics. SPRINTER significantly reduced whole-brain Ki parametric NMSE in simulated 1⁸F-FDG compared to NLM (0.43 ± 0.25 vs. 1.10 ± 0.67), representing an approximate 60% reduction. In contrast, OSEM and temporal-only approaches showed higher variability, while spatial-only and NLM methods introduced bias due to oversmoothing. Noise2Void also exhibited reduced accuracy in Ki estimation, likely due to loss of temporal consistency. These results demonstrate that improved spatiotemporal denoising directly enhances the reliability of downstream kinetic modeling (Fig. 7).

Evaluation of denoising methods on real acquired data

Figures 5 presents the image quality and temporal activity preservation within a 5×5×5 voxel cube achieved with the SPRINTER spatiotemporal denoising method compared with spatial-only and temporal-only denoising methods. We quantitatively assessed the image quality generated with different denoising methods by segmenting cerebral WM and GM using FreeSurfer tools and calculating SNR and CNR for all radiotracers. All denoising methods significantly increased SNR, but this metric alone was insufficient to fully evaluate the denoised PET images (Fig. 6a). Over-smoothed images tended to exhibit artificially high SNR values, whereas noisier images occasionally displayed higher contrast (Figs. 5). In real acquired data, the proposed SPRINTER method achieved the highest SNR across all tracers. Compared to NLM the SNR increased from 2.12 ± 0.77 to 2.76 ± 0.81 for 18F-FDG (~ 30% increase), 3.95 ± 0.80 to 4.33 ± 1.35 for 18F-AV45 (~ 10% increase), 3.05 ± 0.95 to 3.35 ± 1.27 for 11C-PiB (~ 10% increase), 2.68 ± 1.24 to 3.92 ± 1.28 for 15O-HO (~ 46% increase), and 2.53 ± 1.15 to 3.71 ± 1.79 for 15O-OO (~ 47% increase).

The CNR between cerebral WM and GM was significantly improved across all radiotracers except for 15O-OO (Fig. 6b). Similarly, CNR improved across most tracers, including 18F-FDG (0.18 ± 0.16 to 0.33 ± 0.09, ~ 83% increase), 18F-AV45 (0.18 ± 0.19 to 0.37 ± 0.33, ~ 106% increase), 11C-PiB (0.41 ± 0.12 to 0.54 ± 0.08, ~ 32% increase), 15O-HO (0.07 ± 0.03 to 0.22 ± 0.06, ~ 214% increase), and 15O-OO (0.06 ± 0.02 to 0.18 ± 0.08, ~ 200% increase).

We employed PIQE, a no-reference image quality assessment metric [38], to evaluate localized blocks within an image and identify blurriness or noise distortions. PIQE generates a quality score between 0 (excellent) and 100 (poor), making it a robust tool for medical image quality assessment [39]. The PIQE scores confirm the superiority of SPRINTER across all radiotracers compared to other denoising methods, with an average improvement of approximately 20.5% compared to OSEM, indicating enhanced perceptual image quality and reduced noise-related artifacts (Fig. 6c). We calculated the normalized TAC error for each voxel relative to the mean intensity within 61 fine ROIs identified by FreeSurfer to quantify the error in activity for each radiotracer and compare the performance of different denoising methods, particularly in the absence of ground-truth data. SPRINTER demonstrated superior performance in maintaining TAC consistency and reducing TAC error p-values compared to those of other denoising methods (Fig. 6d). ANOVA statistical analysis comparing p-values across denoising methods also showed significant differences (Figs. 5 and 6). Additional comparisons with NLM and Noise2Void demonstrate that while both methods reduce noise, they either introduce spatial blurring NLM or fail to preserve temporal consistency Noise2Void. In contrast, the proposed spatiotemporal method achieves improved reconstruction accuracy and structural fidelity, as reflected by lower NRMSE and higher SSIM across all tracers.

Overall, these results demonstrate that the proposed spatiotemporal framework achieves a better balance between noise reduction and signal preservation, outperforming spatial-only and temporal-only approaches across both simulated and real datasets.

Discussion

The clinical application of AI models in PET imaging is limited by their black-box nature, which poses challenges in interpretability. This has driven interest in explainable AI approaches for medical imaging [4]. Spatial smoothing methods effectively reduce noise but share some black-box characteristics with AI models, highlighting the need for improved transparency in model design. Here, we proposed an anatomically guided, self-supervised aPCA technique that addresses the interpretability and training data limitations encountered in deep learning–based denoising methods for 4D dynamic PET images. By utilizing FreeSurfer-based ROIs from co-registered T1-weighted MRI scans, aPCA decomposes the 4D dynamic PET data based on voxel similarity and specific tissue uptake patterns to achieve a structured and interpretable denoising method. In our PET/MR settings, T1-weighted MR images are always acquired as a standard structural scan. In SPRINTER, T1-weighted MRI was utilized for ROI segmentation in aPCA, and as an auxiliary input, in addition to PET, to the ResUNet. The proposed framework does not strongly depend on T1 data. The primary denoising mechanism is driven by PET-derived temporal dynamics and self-supervised learning through aPCA. The inclusion of T1 enhances anatomical consistency and improves spatial detail preservation, particularly in low-count regions. In scenarios where T1 MRI is unavailable, the framework can be adapted by employing PET-only inputs for the ResUNet and replacing MRI-based ROI segmentation with data-driven clustering methods (e.g., K-means or intensity-based grouping). The absence of T1 may lead to minor reductions in spatial sharpness, but the method should retain its ability to preserve temporal activity curves and achieve effective noise suppression. Future work should include a systematic ablation study to quantitatively evaluate the contribution of T1 input to overall performance and validate the robustness of the proposed method in PET-only or PET/CT settings. A key advantage of the aPCA approach is its independence from extensive paired datasets of noisy and noiseless images, which are often difficult and time-consuming to acquire in clinical practice. This study addresses the challenge of selecting the optimal number of principal components for 4D dynamic PET reconstruction. Reducing the number of PCs suppresses noise but reduces TAC inconsistency, particularly in low-activity regions. The SPRINTER spatiotemporal denoising method dynamically adjusts the number of PCs to reconstruct 95% of grouped voxels for each frame and ROI, which ensures a balance between noise suppression and activity consistency. We applied temporal moving average smoothing across time to mitigate the risk of temporal artifacts, such as large differences between neighboring temporal frames, further enhancing the temporal consistency of reconstructed images. Our method uses mean TACs among the voxels inside of each fine ROI during inverse aPCA reconstruction, thereby ensuring the consistency and reduced noise levels of resulting images. This aPCA method uniquely relies on voxel-wise intensity changes over time and avoids using neighboring voxels, which makes it more robust for dynamic studies where spatial information alone is insufficient for optimum denoising. This approach maintains accuracy and efficiency by minimizing over-segmentation while avoiding the use of excessive PCs.

The Deep Image Prior (DIP) PET denoising technique uses CNN architecture without pre-training, adapting it directly to the noisy input [14]. Although DIP exhibits superior performance over 3D CNNs, it is prone to overfitting and requires careful early stopping. DeepRED, an extension of DIP with integrated regularization, mitigates this issue and improves generalization to clinical data [7]. SPRINTER avoids the complexities of DIP and DeepRed and simplifies denoising in clinical settings. However, the current aPCA method is limited by its reliance on FreeSurfer-based anatomical segmentation. Accurate MRI-PET registration is critical for voxel grouping because misalignment can introduce errors into the denoised images. FreeSurfer segmentation is time-intensive, which limits the method’s clinical applicability for large datasets. Future efforts could address these limitations by employing more flexible data-driven approaches for voxel grouping such as K-means clustering, which would reduce processing time and minimize segmentation errors at region boundaries. FreeSurfer tools sometimes generated visible brush-painting artifacts at ROI edges where boundaries were not smoothly reconstructed. One potential solution to this issue involves increasing the total number of ROIs to create finer segmentation regions. However, smaller ROIs contain fewer voxels per region, leading to fewer observations available for PCA reconstruction [40]. Consequently, this would require a larger number of PCs to achieve accurate reconstructions.

We determined that the numbers of required regions and PCs were balanced by using the following eight ROIs: cerebral WM and GM, cerebellum WM and GM, CSF, brain stem, vessels, and non-brain ROIs. We also used the aPCA-denoised images as training targets for a supervised learning 3D ResUNet model. This network was trained with data from multiple radiotracers and different time frames, with T1-weighted MRI images included as a secondary channel. We tested SPRINTER generalizability by excluding 11C-PiB as a control radiotracer. This allowed us to evaluate model reliability in handling varying noise levels and tissue uptake patterns across different time frames and radiotracers. Frame-by-frame training of 3D ResUNet suggested that the model can potentially operate independently of noise levels and radiotracer uptake, which is essential for clinical applicability. However, incorporating T1-MRI data as a secondary input during training may introduce a bias toward T1 images in early time frames and low-activity regions [34, 35]. This resulted in minor deviations in TACs, underscoring the limitations of dual-channel training in early time frame PET images with noisy PET signals. Those artifacts in early spatial denoising were effectively compensated by post-processing through temporal denoising. The integration of temporal information maintains dynamic consistency and reduces artifacts, particularly in noisy early frames [41]. This was achieved by extracting the first five PCs of spatially denoised images to decompose the original noisy data across them. The PCs derived from different radiotracers capture distinct anatomical information. The corresponding weights across these PCs indicate the degree to which each tissue type contributes to the respective PC. For example, the first and second PCs of the 18F-AV45 radiotracer are primarily associated with GM and WM, respectively, whereas the second PC for 18F-FDG is predominantly linked to arterial vessels. As the first PCA components represent the largest variation in the observed data [42], this decomposition reflects the maximum temporal variability of the tissue response to a specific tracer (i.e., voxel intensities over time). Temporal-only denoising achieves better preservation of the TACs (Fig. 5c) and tSNR but compromises SNR. By contrast, spatial-only denoising generates high SNR but oversmoothed results (Figs. 5a and 6a).

Our spatiotemporal denoising method integrates anatomical priors with advanced PCA techniques to achieve a structured and physiologically informed approach to image enhancement, thereby advancing clinically relevant PET imaging. Iterative updating combined with PCA-based temporal denoising creates an efficient framework for balancing noise reduction and preserving critical physiological signals in PET images. Two step sizes were defined during the iterative regularization process: one for the residual term, representing the difference between the current iteration and the fixed spatially denoised image, and one for the correction term, representing the difference between consecutive iterations (Eqs. 6 and 7). Initially, the residual step size is set high (close to 1), and the correction step size is set low (close to 0); these values are adjusted with progressing iterations, with the residual step size decreasing and the correction step size increasing. The improved accuracy in Ki parametric imaging further highlights the clinical relevance of the proposed method, as reliable estimation of kinetic parameters is critical for quantitative PET analysis and disease characterization.

Despite the promising performance of the proposed spatiotemporal denoising framework, our study has several limitations. First, the aPCA method relies on anatomical segmentation derived from T1-weighted MRI, which introduces dependency on accurate MRI–PET registration. Misalignment between modalities may affect voxel grouping and lead to local reconstruction errors. Additionally, FreeSurfer-based segmentation is computationally intensive and may limit scalability in large clinical datasets. Second, while the integration of spatial and temporal denoising improves overall performance, the spatial component may still introduce slight oversmoothing, particularly in early low-count frames where signal levels are extremely low. Although temporal regularization mitigates this effect, residual bias may remain in certain regions. Third, although the model was trained across multiple radiotracers to enhance generalizability, variability in scanner hardware, reconstruction settings, and acquisition protocols may still influence performance in external datasets. Further validation on independent multi-center cohorts is needed. Finally, kinetic parameter evaluation was performed on simulated 18F-FDG data with known ground truth. Although these results demonstrate improved Ki estimation, further validation on clinical datasets with arterial sampling or reference standards is required to confirm translational applicability.

Conclusion

This study presents a novel spatiotemporal denoising method called SPRINTER that is tailored to the unique demands of 4D dynamic PET imaging of the brain. Current deep learning–based denoising methods have limitations, including the absence of definitive ground truth, compromised contrasts and TACs, inadequate co-registration with MRI data, and radiotracer-specific applicability. This study employs denoising algorithms meticulously crafted to effectively diminish image noise while simultaneously preserving the integrity of essential structural and functional information within the PET data. The key innovation is the development of a self-supervised approach that mitigates the need for high-quality training targets, thereby resolving a significant challenge in clinical PET imaging where ground-truth data are often unavailable. By combining fixed spatial denoising with dynamic temporal adjustments through PCA-based processing, the method achieves a balanced trade-off between spatial fidelity and temporal consistency. SPRINTER demonstrates its efficacy in enhancing image clarity by improving critical image quality metrics, including SNR, CNR, and PIQE scores. Our method also reduces NRMSE in simulation data and preserves the temporal dynamics of TACs.
