The Triply-Randomized Negative Binomial Beta for Robust Regression and Conjugate Models of Bounded Support Data
Jimmy Lederman, Aaron Schein

Abstract
The beta distribution is the default choice of likelihood in many regression problems with a [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] -bounded support response despite its sensitivity to outliers, inability to accommodate exact zero observations, and a lack of closed-form conjugate priors. We address these shortcomings by introducing the triply-randomized negative binomial beta distribution , denoted TNBbeta ⁡ ( p , q , ε ) \mathrm{TNBbeta}(p,\,q,\,\varepsilon) , parameterized by a median p p , concentration parameter q q , and boundary parameter ε \varepsilon which permits positive density at 0 0 and 1 1 . The TNBbeta arises by randomizing the parameters of a standard beta distribution with three dependent negative binomial random variables, each of whose complete conditional distribution we show is itself negative binomial. Moreover, connecting p p and q q to Gaussian latent variables with logit link functions yields closed-form updates via Pólya-gamma augmentation. Together, these properties yield simple auxiliary-variable Gibbs samplers for regression models of bounded-support data, which often outperform standard beta regression approaches in terms of effective sample size per second and held-out prediction, especially in the presence of outliers. In a case study of forest canopy cover, we demonstrate that this framework can easily incorporate spatial structure and exact zero observations. Overall, this work substantially expands the class of Bayesian models for [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] -bounded support data that can be fit efficiently.

2
The Beta Distribution and its Generalizations

Our approach builds on known properties and generalizations of the beta distribution.

Definition 2.1
.

A beta random variable
Y\sim\mathrm{beta}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
has probability density function

\mathrm{beta}(y;\,\alpha_{1},\,\alpha_{2})=\frac{y^{\alpha_{1}-1}(1\!-\!y)^{\alpha_{2}-1}}{B(\alpha_{1},\,\alpha_{2})},

(4)

with shape parameters
\alpha_{1}>0
,
\alpha_{2}>0
and where
B(a,b)=\nicefrac{{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)}}{{\Gamma(a+b)}}
is the beta function. An equivalent parameterization with mean
\mu\in(0,1)
and concentration parameter
\phi>0
is

\mathrm{beta}\big(\textsc{mean}\!=\!\mu,\,\,\textsc{concentration}\!=\!\phi\big)\Longleftrightarrow\mathrm{beta}\big(\phi\mu,\,\,\phi(1\!-\!\mu)\big),

(5)

The beta function makes working with beta shape parameters cumbersome, prohibiting, for example, analytic marginalization. As
17
note,

“
Compound beta
distributions may be formed by ascribing distributions to [
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
]. However, such distributions have not been used much in applied statistical work …and continuous distributions [for
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
] usually present analytical difficulties, owing to the presence of the beta function.”

The difficulty of working with continuous distributions for the shape parameters motivates the use of discrete distributions, and
17
list several choices of discrete mixing distribution which induce appealing analytic properties.

Consider a general discrete mixing distribution
\mathcal{DM}
for auxiliary count
A
. The construction

A\sim\mathcal{DM},\qquad Y\mid A\!=\!a\sim\textrm{beta}\big(\alpha_{1}\!+\!a,\,\alpha_{2}\big),

(6)

often leads to an analytic marginal distribution for
Y
. In fact, many beta generalizations can be represented as compound beta distributions of this form for different choices of mixing distribution
\mathcal{DM}
. For example,
31
introduced the binomial mixture of beta distributions in which
\mathcal{DM}
is binomial. Choosing
\mathcal{DM}
to be Poisson yields the non-central beta distributions of the first and second kind
[
16
;
33
]
.

One of the most prominent generalizations of the beta is the
Libby-Novick beta distribution

[
24
;
6
]
, which we define below.

Definition 2.2
.

A Libby-Novick beta random variable
Y\sim\mathrm{LNbeta}(\alpha_{1},\,\alpha_{2},\,c)
has density

\mathrm{LNbeta}(y;\,\alpha_{1},\,\alpha_{2},\,c)=\mathrm{beta}(y;\,\alpha_{1},\,\alpha_{2})\cdot\frac{c^{\alpha_{1}}}{\big(1-(1-c)y\big)^{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}},

(7)

with shape parameters
\alpha_{1}>0
,
\alpha_{2}>0
and tilting parameter
c>0
.

Unlike most other beta generalizations, the LNbeta does not introduce additional special functions into its normalizing constant. A characterizing property of this distribution is its construction as the ratio of two gamma-distributed random variables—i.e., if
G_{i}\overset{\mathrm{ind}}{\sim}\Gamma(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})
then
\nicefrac{{G_{1}}}{{G_{1}+G_{2}}}\sim\text{LNbeta}\big(\alpha_{1},\,\alpha_{2},\,\tfrac{\beta_{1}}{\beta_{2}}\big)
. Setting
\beta_{1}\!=\!\beta_{2}
recovers the well-known representation of the standard beta as the ratio of two gamma random variables with equal rates.

Recent work
[
5
;
18
]
has shown that the LNbeta also has an auxiliary-count representation as in
Equation

6
in which
\mathcal{DM}
is negative binomial

A\sim\mathrm{NB}(\alpha_{1},\,q),\qquad Y\mid A\!=\!a\sim\mathrm{beta}\big(\alpha_{1}\!+\!a,\,\alpha_{2}\big).

(8)

Note that adding
A
instead to the second shape parameter in
Equation

8
also yields a LNbeta marginal, but only if the negative binomial shape matches the second beta shape—i.e.,
A\sim\textrm{NB}\big(\alpha_{2},\,q\big)
.
The addition of
A
to the first or second shape perturbs the marginal distribution towards
0
or
1
, respectively.
19

in

8
is also negative binomial

A\mid Y\!=\!y\sim\text{NB}\big(\alpha_{1}\!+\!\alpha_{2},\,1\!-\!(1\!-\!q)y\big),

(9)

demonstrating a form of conjugacy between a beta likelihood and negative binomial prior.

The Libby–Novick beta belongs to the broader family of what

term the
beta-generated continuous generalized hypergeometric distributions
. This family includes several notable beta generalizations, such as the confluent hypergeometric beta
[
14

and the Gauss hypergeometric beta
[

is a discrete generalized hypergeometric distribution

Equations

The representation in

can be expressed via a

\displaystyle(A,B)

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{DM},

(10)

\displaystyle Y\mid A\!=\!a,\,B\!=\!b

\displaystyle\sim\textrm{beta}\big(\alpha_{1}\!+\!a,\,\alpha_{2}\!+\!b\big),

where

is now bivariate. In simple cases,

are independent Poisson random variables, the marginal distribution of

\textrm{DNCbeta}(y;\alpha_{1},\!\alpha_{2},\!\lambda_{1},\!\lambda_{2})\!=\!\textrm{beta}(y;\alpha_{1},\!\alpha_{2})\,e^{-\scriptstyle\frac{1}{2}(\lambda_{1}\!+\!\lambda_{2})}\Psi_{2}\big(\alpha_{1}\!+\!\alpha_{2};\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda_{1}y,\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda_{2}(1\!-\!y)\big)

where

is the Pochhammer symbol and

\Psi_{2}\big[\alpha;\gamma,\gamma^{\prime};x,y\big]=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\alpha)_{j+k}x^{j}y^{k}}{(\gamma)_{j}(\gamma^{\prime})_{k}\,j!\,k!},\quad x,y\geq 0.

Though computable, this special function complicates inference for the “non-centrality” parameters

and
\lambda_{2}
—a difficulty shared
by most distributions discussed in this section.

We next introduce a beta generalization whose
auxiliary-count construction avoids these difficulties, yielding

The Triply-Randomized Negative Binomial Beta

We introduce the
\mathrm{TNBbeta}

Definition 3.1

(
The TNBbeta
)
.

Let
p\!\in\!(0,1)
,
q\!\in\!(0,1)
, and
\varepsilon\!>\!0
. A triply-randomized negative binomial beta random variable
Y\sim\mathrm{TNBbeta}\big(p,\,q,\,\varepsilon\big)
has probability density

\mathrm{TNBbeta}(y;\,p,\,q,\,\varepsilon)=\mathrm{beta}(y;\,\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\left[\tfrac{1-q}{y(1-y)}\gamma(y,p)\right]^{\varepsilon}\big[1-4q\,\gamma(y,p)\big]^{-(\varepsilon+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})}

(13)

where
\gamma(y,p)
is a similarity function of the log-odds of
y
and
p
as follows

\gamma(y,p)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}\tfrac{1}{4}\mathrm{sech}^{2}\big(\nicefrac{{\mathrm{logit}(y)-\mathrm{logit}(p)}}{{2}}\big)=\frac{y(1-y)p(1-p)}{(p(1-y)+(1-p)y)^{2}}

(14)

Unlike most beta generalizations, the TNBbeta has closed-form density, and its parameters

Theorem 3.1

(
TNBbeta median-concentration
)
.

Let
Y\sim\mathrm{TNBbeta}\big(p,\,q,\,\varepsilon\big)
. Then
p
and
q
are Fisher orthogonal and separately control the median and concentration such that

1.

\mathrm{median}(Y)=p

2.

\lim_{q\to 1}\mathrm{\mathrm{TNBbeta}}(p;\,p,\,q,\,\varepsilon)=\infty

3.

The Fisher information matrix satisfies
\mathcal{I}_{pq}(p,\,q,\,\varepsilon)=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[-\frac{\partial^{2}\log\mathrm{TNBbeta}(Y;\,p,\,q,\,\varepsilon)}{\partial p\,\partial q}\right]=0
.

Theorem

3.1
mirrors the mean-concentration parameterization of the standard beta in
Equation

5
. However, the beta mean and concentration are not Fisher orthogonal, so their maximum likelihood estimates are dependent
[
12
]
. Orthogonality ensures that inference on the median does not interfere with inference on the concentration.

The remaining parameter
\varepsilon
governs the modal and boundary behavior.
Figure

2
c shows that when
\varepsilon\!=\!.5

the TNBbeta can attain both interior and boundary modes. In contrast, when
\varepsilon\!=\!1
or
\varepsilon\!=\!2
, there are no boundary modes and the density at the boundary is either finite and positive or approaches zero, respectively.
Proposition

3.1
formalizes each regime.

Proposition 3.1

(
Boundary behavior
)
.

The TNBbeta boundary density depends on
\varepsilon
:

\big(\mathrm{TNBbeta}(0;\,p,\,q,\,\varepsilon),\;\mathrm{TNBbeta}(1;\,p,\,q,\,\varepsilon)\big)=\begin{cases}(0,\;0)&\varepsilon>1\\
\big(\tfrac{(1-p)(1-q)}{p},\;\tfrac{p(1-q)}{1-p}\big)&\varepsilon=1\\
(\infty,\;\infty)&\varepsilon<1\end{cases}

Remark 3.1
.

The TNBbeta distribution can attain three modes when
\varepsilon<1
. For example, in the symmetric case
p=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
, an interior mode exists at
y=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
if and only if
q>\nicefrac{{2(1-\varepsilon)}}{{3}}
.

Boundary modes are desirable as they allow for protection against outlier observations and model mis-specification (see
Section

6.2
). Whereas the beta
must sacrifice an interior mode to attain boundary modes, the TNBbeta need not. Furthermore, the positive density at the boundaries when
\varepsilon\!=\!1

allows for inclusion of observations
y\!=\!0
and
y\!=\!1
in probability models. Such boundary observations are abundant in practice (see e.g.,
Section

6.4
).

In addition to dictating boundary behavior,
\varepsilon
also impacts the concentration. This is most apparent when
q\!=\!0
and the TNBbeta reduces to a special case of the Libby-Novick beta.

Corollary 3.1

(
LNbeta connection
)
.

The TNBbeta density can be written as follows

\mathrm{TNBbeta}(y;\,p,\,q,\,\varepsilon)=\mathrm{LNbeta}\Big(y;\,\varepsilon,\,\varepsilon,\,\tfrac{1\!-\!p}{p}\Big)(1\!-\!q)^{\varepsilon}\big[1-4q\gamma(y,p)\big]^{-(\varepsilon+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})}.

(15)

Moreover, if
Y\sim\mathrm{TNBbeta}\big(p,\,0,\,\varepsilon\big)
then,
Y\sim\mathrm{LNbeta}\Big(\varepsilon,\,\varepsilon,\,\frac{1\!-\!p}{p}\Big)
as in
2.2
.

Remark 3.2
.

The similarity function
\gamma(y,p)=\tfrac{1}{4}\mathrm{sech}^{2}\big(\tfrac{\mathrm{logit}(y)-\mathrm{logit}(p)}{2}\big)
is the density of the standard logistic distribution centered at
\textrm{logit}(p)

\textrm{logit}(y)

Remark 3.3
.

As a special case of the TNBbeta, the Libby-Novick beta admits a
median-concentration parameterization under equality of its shape
parameters, with median
p
and concentration
\varepsilon
. However,
since its concentration
\varepsilon
enters the beta function, inference on the

Since

Given covariates

Proposition 4.1

Define

5
Data Augmentation for TNBbeta Regression Models

This section develops a data-augmentation framework for inference in TNBbeta models. Such models assume
Y_{i}\sim\mathrm{TNBbeta}(p_{i},\,q_{i},\,\varepsilon)
where data share a fixed
\varepsilon
and have potentially different median
p_{i}
and concentration
q_{i}
, each of which may be functions of global parameters.

Our approach to inference centers around the auxiliary count representation in
Theorem

4.1
and the use of data augmentation
[
34
]
to instantiate these counts as latent variables. The key to this approach is the inverse distribution of
Proposition

4.4
. Each data point
Y_{i}\sim\mathrm{TNBbeta}(p_{i},\,q_{i},\,\varepsilon)
can be alternatively expressed via
4.2
as

C_{i}\sim\text{NB}\big(\varepsilon,\,1\!-\!q_{i}\big),\,\,\,\,Y_{i}\mid C_{i}\!=\!c_{i},\,p_{i}\sim\mathrm{LNbeta}\big(\varepsilon+c_{i},\,\varepsilon+c_{i},\,\tfrac{1-p_{i}}{p_{i}}\big).

(27)

Performing the computationally cheap and conceptually simple data augmentation step,

C_{i}\mid Y_{i}\!=\!y_{i},\,p_{i},\,q_{i}\sim\text{NB}\big(\varepsilon+\tfrac{1}{2},\,\,1-4q_{i}\gamma(y_{i},\,p_{i})\big),

(28)

greatly simplifies inference on
p_{i}
and
q_{i}
. Conditional on
C_{i}
, inference on
q_{i}
depends only on
C_{i}
—and not on
Y_{i}
—so any technique for negative binomial probability parameters applies directly. If
q_{i}\sim\textrm{beta}(\cdot,\cdot)
, for example, then
q_{i}\mid C_{i}
is also beta by conjugacy.

Inference on
p_{i}
involves both
C_{i}
and
Y_{i}
. Conditional on
C_{i}
, each
Y_{i}
is Libby-Novick beta-distributed, so a second layer of data augmentation—instantiating
A_{i}
and
B_{i}
via the inverse distributions in
Proposition

4.2
and updating
p_{i}\mid C_{i},A_{i},B_{i}
—yields a conjugate beta update when
p_{i}
is beta-distributed. However, this second layer is often unnecessary. We show below that for regression models, inference on
p_{i}
bypasses
A_{i}
and
B_{i}
for all non-boundary
Y_{i}\!\in\!(0,1)
via Pólya-gamma augmentation applied directly to the Libby-Novick beta density.

5.1
Pólya-gamma Augmentation

Many probability density functions can be expressed in a “logistic form” as follows

P(y\mid\psi)=c(y)\,\frac{(e^{\psi})^{a(y)}}{(1+e^{\psi})^{b(y)}},

(29)

for known functions
a(y)
,
b(y)
, and
c(y)
. The key to Pólya-gamma augmentation is to re-express a likelihood of the form in
Equation

29
using the following integral identity,

\frac{(e^{\psi})^{a(y)}}{(1+e^{\psi})^{b(y)}}=2^{-b(y)}e^{\kappa(y)\psi}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\omega\psi^{2}/2}\,\mathrm{PG}\big(\omega;\;b(y),0\big)\,d\omega,

(30)

where
\kappa(y)=a(y)-\nicefrac{{b(y)}}{{2}}
and
\mathrm{PG}\big(\omega;\;b(y),0\big)
is the density of a
Pólya-gamma

random variable (see
30
for details on the Pólya-gamma distribution). This identity expresses the density in
Equation

29
as a Gaussian mixture over
\omega
. Treating
\omega
as a latent variable yields simple conditional distributions. Conditioned on
\omega
, the complete conditional distribution for
\psi
is
P(\psi\mid\omega,y)\propto P(\psi)\,e^{\kappa(y)\psi}e^{-\omega\psi^{2}/2}

which is Gaussian if the prior
P(\psi)
is Gaussian. Conversely, by the exponential tilting property of the Pólya-gamma distribution, the conditional distribution for
\omega
is also Pólya-gamma as
\omega\mid\psi,y\sim\mathrm{PG}\big(b(y),\psi\big)
.

Together, these conditional distributions form a conjugate augmentation scheme for models with Gaussian priors and logistic-type likelihoods, enabling efficient Gibbs sampling and other inference procedures. Pólya-gamma augmentation is most common as a posterior computation technique in models of integer-valued count data, such as in Bernoulli and binomial
[
30
]
, negative binomial
[
30
;
37
]
, and multinomial regression models
[
30
;
7
;
25
]
. Less common is the use of this augmentation in models with a continuous response. The rest of this section extends these results to models with LNbeta and TNBbeta likelihoods.

5.2
A New Pólya-gamma Augmentation Scheme for the LNbeta

We show that the Libby-Novick beta density is amenable to Pólya-gamma augmentation when the median is parameterized as
p=\textrm{logit}^{-1}(\psi)
. The density is proportional in
\psi
to

\displaystyle\mathrm{LNbeta}\big(\varepsilon,\,\varepsilon,\,\tfrac{1-p}{p}\big)

\displaystyle\propto_{\psi}\left(\frac{1}{1+e^{\psi}}\right)^{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{e^{\psi}}{1+e^{\psi}}\right)^{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{1+e^{\psi}}{y+(1-y)e^{\psi}}\right)^{2\varepsilon}

(31)

\displaystyle\propto_{\psi}\frac{\big(e^{\psi})^{\varepsilon}}{(y+(1-y)e^{\psi})^{2\varepsilon}}

(32)

\displaystyle\propto_{\psi}\frac{(e^{\psi+\mathrm{logit}(1-y)}\big)^{\varepsilon}}{\big(1+e^{\psi+\mathrm{logit}(1-y)}\big)^{2\varepsilon}}

(33)

which is exactly the logistic form of
Equation

29
with
a(y)=\varepsilon
,
b(y)=2\varepsilon
, and
\psi+\mathrm{logit}(1-y)
in place of
\psi
.
Augmenting with
\omega\sim\textrm{PG}(2\varepsilon,\,0)
yields the conditional distributions

\displaystyle\psi\mid\omega,\,y\propto P(\psi)\,e^{-\omega(\psi^{2}/2\,+\,\textrm{logit}(1-y)\,\psi)}

(34)

\displaystyle\omega\mid\psi,\,y\sim\textrm{PG}\big(2\varepsilon,\,\psi+\textrm{logit}(1-y)\big).

(35)

Because
a(y)=\nicefrac{{b(y)}}{{2}}
, the term
\kappa(y)
from the derivations in
Section

5.1
vanishes, and both conditional distributions depend on the data only through the log-odds offset
\textrm{logit}(1-y)
.

Pólya-gamma augmentation is even possible as an inference strategy for
p
because it does not enter any special function in its density, unlike the parameters of the beta and most of its other generalizations. As discussed in
Remark

3.3
, however, the LNbeta concentration
\varepsilon
enters the beta function and inherits the computational intractability of learning beta shape parameters. In the next section, we build on this result to derive a fully conjugate Gibbs sampler for inference on both median
p
and concentration
q
in TNBbeta regression models.

5.3
Auxiliary-Variable Gibbs Sampling for TNBbeta Regression

We now present a conditionally conjugate Gibbs sampler for regression models of bounded-support responses
Y_{i}\in[0,1]
with covariate profiles
X_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Consider the TNBbeta regression model with logit link functions for the median and concentration as follows

\displaystyle Y_{i}

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{TNBbeta}(p_{i},\,q_{i},\,\varepsilon)

(36)

\displaystyle p_{i}=\textrm{logit}^{-1}(

\displaystyle X_{i}^{\top}\beta_{p})\qquad q_{i}=\textrm{logit}^{-1}(X_{i}^{\top}\beta_{q}),

for each observation
i\in[n]
. We complete the model with Gaussian priors
\beta_{p}\sim\textrm{MVN}(\mathbf{b}_{p},\,B_{p})
and
\beta_{q}\sim\textrm{MVN}(\mathbf{b}_{q},\,B_{q})
on the coefficients for the median and concentration, respectively.

Augmenting the model with one negative binomial and two Pólya-gamma random variables per data point yields Gaussian updates for both
\beta_{p}
and
\beta_{q}
. As described above, the first step is to
introduce the negative binomial random variable
C_{i}\sim\mathrm{NB}\big(\varepsilon,\,1\!-\!q_{i}\big)
to the model via the alternative representation of the TNBbeta described in
4.2
. Instantiating
C_{i}
during inference requires sampling it from its complete conditional distribution as follows

C_{i}\mid Y_{i}\!=\!y_{i},\,p_{i},\,q_{i}\sim\text{NB}\big(\varepsilon+\tfrac{1}{2},1\!-\!4q_{i}\gamma(y_{i},p_{i})\big),

(37)

as shown in
Proposition

4.4
. Once

C_{i}
is instantiated for each observation, inference on the coefficient vectors is greatly simplified. As
\beta_{q}
is conditionally independent of the data
\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
given
\{C_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, inference on
\beta_{q}
can proceed through
\{C_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
alone and follows from the standard Pólya-gamma augmentation scheme for the negative binomial
[
30
]
. Augmenting the model with Pólya-gamma random variables
\omega_{i}\sim\mathrm{PG}(\varepsilon\!+\!c_{i},\,0)
then results in a Pólya-gamma complete conditional for each
\omega_{i}
and a Gaussian update for
\beta_{q}
as

\displaystyle\omega_{i}\mid C_{i}\!=\!c_{i},\,\beta_{q}\sim\textrm{PG}\big(\varepsilon+c_{i},\,X_{i}^{\top}\beta_{q}\big)

(38)

\displaystyle\beta_{q}\mid\{\omega_{i},\,C_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim\textrm{MVN}\big(V_{q}(X^{\top}\boldsymbol{\kappa}\!+\!B_{q}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}_{q}),\,V_{q}\big)

(39)

in which we define the covariance matrix
V_{q}=(X^{\top}\Omega X+B_{q}^{-1})^{-1}
with
\Omega=\textrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\omega})
as a diagonal matrix of the auxiliary Pólya-gamma variables and
\boldsymbol{\kappa}
as the vector of
\kappa_{i}=\nicefrac{{\varepsilon-C_{i}}}{{2}}
.

Introducing
C_{i}
also enables Pólya-gamma augmentation for inference of
\beta_{p}
. Conditional on
C_{i}
, each
Y_{i}
is Libby-Novick beta-distributed, so we can apply the Pólya-gamma augmentation derived in
Section

5.2
. Therefore, introducing
\xi_{i}\sim\mathrm{PG}\big(2(\varepsilon+C_{i}),\,0\big)
yields the updates

\displaystyle\xi_{i}\mid C_{i}\!=\!c_{i},\,Y_{i}\!=\!y_{i},\,\beta_{p}\,\sim\textrm{PG}\big(2(\varepsilon\!+\!c_{i}),\;X_{i}^{\top}\beta_{p}+\textrm{logit}(1\!-\!y_{i})\big)

(40)

\displaystyle\beta_{p}\mid\{\xi_{i},\,Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim\textrm{MVN}\big(V_{p}(B_{p}^{-1}\boldsymbol{b}_{p}\!-\!X^{\top}\boldsymbol{\eta}),\,V_{p}\Big)

(41)

where we define the analogous covariance matrix
V_{p}=(X^{\top}\Xi X+B_{p}^{-1})^{-1}
with the diagonal matrix of Pólya-gamma auxiliary variables
\Xi\!=\!\textrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
and further define
\boldsymbol{\eta}\!=\!\boldsymbol{\xi}\odot\logit(1-\boldsymbol{y})
where
\odot
is the elementwise product and
\logit
is applied elementwise to the data vector
\boldsymbol{y}
.

Together,
Equations

37
to
41
constitute a Gibbs sampler for inference on the coefficient vectors
\beta_{p}
and
\beta_{q}
. This algorithm relies only on the ability to sample multivariate normal, negative binomial, and Pólya-gamma random variables. Due to the ubiquity of Pólya-gamma augmentation as a tool for posterior computation in models of discrete data, efficient algorithms for Pólya-gamma sampling are now available in most statistical software.

Recall that TNBbeta models can accommodate exact boundary observations
Y_{i}\!=\!0
or
Y_{i}\!=\!1
when
\varepsilon\!=\!1
and the TNBbeta likelihood admits positive, finite density at the boundary. However, the complete conditional for each Pólya-gamma variable
\xi_{i}
depends on
Y_{i}
via the log-odds offset
\textrm{logit}(1-Y_{i})
and therefore cannot handle exact observations of zero or one.

For boundary observations, we introduce additional auxiliary variables
A_{i}\sim\mathrm{NB}\big(\varepsilon,\,1\!-\!p_{i}\big)
and
B_{i}\sim\mathrm{NB}(\varepsilon,\,p_{i})
into the model as in the TNBbeta representation of
Theorem

4.1
, where
Y_{i}\!=\!0
or
Y_{i}\!=\!1
implies
C_{i}\!=\!0
.
Then by
Proposition

4.2
,
A_{i}
and
B_{i}
can be instantiated via

(A_{i},B_{i})\mid Y_{i}\!=\!y_{i},\,p_{i}\sim{\begin{cases}\big(\delta_{0},\;\text{NB}(2\varepsilon,\,p_{i})\big)&\!\!\textrm{if }y_{i}=0\\
\big(\text{NB}(2\varepsilon,\,1\!-\!p_{i}),\;\delta_{0}\big)&\!\!\textrm{if }y_{i}=1\end{cases}}

(42)

Then using again Pólya-gamma augmentation for negative binomial random variables, we are able to instantiate each
\xi_{i}
according to its complete conditional distribution

\xi_{i}\mid A_{i}\!=\!a_{i},\,B_{i}\!=\!b_{i},\,\beta_{p}\sim\textrm{PG}\big(2\varepsilon\!+\!a_{i}\!+\!b_{i},\;X_{i}^{\top}\beta_{p}\big).

(43)

Conditional on the auxiliary variables
\{\xi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
for all observations,
\beta_{p}
is drawn as in
Equation

41
where now
\eta_{i}\!=\!\nicefrac{{b_{i}}}{{2}}
when
Y_{i}\!=\!0
and
\eta_{i}\!=\!-\nicefrac{{a_{i}}}{{2}}
when

. These modifications to the Gibbs sampler enable inference of
\beta_{p}
in the presence of boundary observations.

The model in
Equation

36
is one of many for which this framework yields tractable posterior inference. For example, if the concentration is a shared parameter
q\sim\textrm{beta}(\cdot,\cdot)
, the Gibbs sampler further simplifies, as the complete conditional for

is conjugate beta.

More broadly, because the complete conditional distributions for
\beta_{p}
and
\beta_{q}
are multivariate normal, this framework inherits the computational toolkit of Gaussian linear models, including spatial, temporal, and hierarchical priors. We demonstrate these capabilities in the next section, where we fit several variants of this model to
(0,1)
-bounded support data.

6
Experimental Results

We now compare the performance of TNBbeta regression models to alternative bounded-support regression models in
both synthetic and real-data scenarios, demonstrating that TNBbeta regression models are more robust to noise, often achieve better held-out prediction, and naturally incorporate complex prior structure such as spatial random effects.

6.1
Predictive Evaluation

To assess model fit in each experiment, we hold out a portion of the data
\mathbf{Y}_{\textrm{held}}\equiv\{Y_{i}\}_{i\in n_{\textrm{held}}}
from the observed data
\mathbf{Y}_{\textrm{obs}}\equiv\{Y_{i}\}_{i\in n_{\textrm{obs}}}
, where
n_{\textrm{obs}}
and
n_{\textrm{held}}
partition the data indices. Model fit is assessed via
log pointwise predictive density
(LPPD) of the held-out data
[
13
]
, defined as
\textrm{LPPD}=\sum_{i\in n_{\textrm{held}}}\log P(Y_{i}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\textrm{obs}})
. We report a function of LPPD called the
information rate (IR)
, which measures the average surprise (i.e., Shannon information) of new observations:

\displaystyle\textrm{IR}=-\tfrac{1}{|n_{\textrm{held}}|}\underbrace{\sum_{i\in n_{\textrm{held}}}\log P(Y_{i}\mid\mathbf{Y}_{\textrm{obs}})}_{=\textrm{LPPD}}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{|n_{\textrm{held}}|}\sum_{i\in n_{\textrm{held}}}\log\int f_{\theta_{i}}(Y_{i})\,P(\theta_{i}\mid\,\mathbf{Y}_{\textrm{obs}})\mathbf{d}\theta_{i}

(44)

where
f_{\theta_{i}}
denotes the model density with parameters
\theta_{i}
that may depend on global parameters
\Theta
. This defines a posterior expectation which we approximate with Monte Carlo as
\textrm{IR}\approx-\tfrac{1}{|n_{\textrm{held}}|}\sum_{i\in n_{\textrm{held}}}\log\left[\tfrac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}f_{\theta_{i}^{{(s)}}}(Y_{i})\right]
using the
S
posterior samples
\theta_{i}^{{(s)}}\sim P(\theta_{i}\mid\,\mathbf{Y}_{\textrm{obs}})
returned by MCMC. Lower information rate indicates better predictive fit. To facilitate direct comparison for a TNBbeta model against a beta model baseline, we also report
information gain (IG)
defined as
\text{IG}=\textrm{IR}_{\textrm{baseline}}-\textrm{IR}
, which measures the reduction in average surprise from the baseline, where higher is better.

6.3
Real and Synthetic Data Fit and Efficiency

We now assess the performance of TNBbeta regression models on real and synthetic data. Compared to beta regression, TNBbeta models have not only a different likelihood but also a different inference framework—a conditionally conjugate Gibbs sampler rather than HMC. Therefore, we contrast the predictive fit and computational efficiency of each approach.

Data.
We fit each model to five real and two synthetic datasets. Both synthetic datasets are generated from a beta regression model, one with orthogonal and the other highly collinear predictors. See
Appendix

\thechapter.C
for additional experimental details and results.

Models.
We fit two variants each of the TNBbeta and beta regression models from the previous section. In the first variant, the concentration is shared across observations—
q\sim\textrm{beta}(1,1)
for TNBbeta and
\phi\sim\textrm{lognormal}(0,1)
for beta. In the second, we regress covariates on the concentration—
q_{i}\!=\!\textrm{logit}^{-1}\big(X_{i}^{\top}\beta_{q}\big)
for TNBbeta and
\phi_{i}\!=\!\exp\!\big(X_{i}^{\top}\beta\big)
for beta. We also compare to cobin and micobin models in the constant concentration setting with default prior settings
[
22
]
. We specify each coefficient as
\beta_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

Implementation details.
We fit each model
20
times to each dataset, with
10
% held-out, collecting
500
samples after
500
burn-in across
4
MCMC chains on an NVIDIA A40 GPU.

Results.

Table

1
reports information rate and effective samples per second for each model with shared concentration on each dataset. The TNBbeta regression model provides a substantially better fit for three out of five real datasets. HMC-based beta regression produces the most effective samples per second on three of five real datasets, but its effective sample rate suffers when predictors are correlated, unlike TNBbeta models and the other data augmentation approaches. Furthermore, TNBbeta models produce more effective samples per second than cobin and micobin models across all settings, suggesting that the data augmentation strategy underlying the TNBbeta is generally more efficient than that for the cobin models. We also note that all models attain similar predictive fit on the collinear synthetic dataset even though the data is truly beta-distributed.

The comparison between TNBbeta and beta regression when covariates are included for the concentration parameters is largely the same and the results are provided in
Table

\thechapter.C.3

Table

Table

\thechapter.C.4

Spatial Models

In this section we show that the conditionally conjugate inference framework for TNBbeta models allows for easy extensions that accommodate more complex prior structure.

7
Discussion

We introduced the TNBbeta, a novel distribution on the unit interval parameterized by median
p
and concentration
q
. This distribution is particularly well-suited for regression as both
p
and
q
can be modeled with covariates via logit link functions which admits simple, efficient, and tuning-free inference strategies via Pólya-gamma augmentation. This approach yields a flexible and robust class of models for bounded support data, including with exact boundary observations. The empirical studies in
Sections

6.2
,
6.1
and
6.3
bear this out, demonstrating that TNBbeta regression models are often more sample-efficient, have better out-of-sample fit, and are more robust to outliers than either beta
[
12
]
or micobin
[
22
]
regression models. Moreover, the case study in
Section

6.4
illustrates the ease with which complex or hierarchical TNBbeta models, in this case for spatial data, can be modularly constructed and efficiently fit.

The TNBbeta arises as a particular discrete mixture of the standard beta distribution expressible in terms of three negative binomial auxiliary variables. As discussed in
Section

2
, this follows in a long line of generalized beta distributions defined by some discrete mixing distribution
\mathcal{DM}
, a tradition motivated by continuous mixtures of the beta being typically intractable
[
17
]
. The generalized beta of
24
for instance, arises from a negative binomial mixture over a single beta shape parameter, and is a special case of the TNBbeta, as we have shown. Another example is the DNCbeta
[
27
;
28
]
defined by mixing over both shape parameters with Poisson random variables. Similar in spirit is the
micobin
distribution, recently introduced by
22
, which is defined as a discrete mixture of the continuous binomial distribution.

Discrete mixtures of bounded-support distributions are useful both for yielding more flexible model families and also for enabling tractable posterior inference schemes based on data augmentation. Such schemes, like the one we developed in
Section

5
, have been previously developed for inference in DNCbeta matrix factorization models
[
32
;
2
]
and for micobin regression
[
22
]
, among other examples.

Unlike previous approaches, TNBbeta models enjoy analytic properties that facilitate their use and interpretation. First, the TNBbeta density has a simple closed form, whereas the DNCbeta and micobin require costly infinite-series summation to evaluate. Second, inference for TNBbeta regression is tractable under the logit link, with coefficients interpretable as log-odds of the median or concentration. Finally, the conditional distribution of each auxiliary negative binomial under the TNBbeta is itself negative binomial, a remarkable instance of “reverse conjugacy”, which makes posterior inference via data augmentation fast and easy to implement. In contrast, inference for DNCbeta and micobin models requires draws from non-standard families—i.e., the Bessel and Kolmogorov-gamma distributions, respectively—introducing a computational bottleneck and often necessitating approximations.

This work prompts several avenues of future research. The “reverse conjugacy” of the TNBbeta construction is itself a topic worthy of study. It relates to a family of beta generalizations introduced by
19
, where the same conjugacy holds for a broad class of discrete priors over a single beta shape. Our results can be understood as an extension to the case of a dependent bivariate mixing distribution over both shape parameters. Characterizing more broadly when and how reverse conjugate constructions arise could enable inference in many new and interesting models. Another future avenue is in developing more hierarchical models based on the TNBbeta. A key benefit of the proposed approach is its modularity. Since latent variables retain conditionally Gaussian posteriors under data augmentation, the standard Gaussian toolkit invites temporal, hierarchical, sparsity-inducing, and other structured extensions. Finally, a natural topic of future study is in extending these ideas to the simplex, such as via discrete mixtures of the Dirichlet
[
29
]
. Such extensions would enable new models for compositional data, as well as hierarchical constructions of simplex-constrained latent variables, both of which arise in many scientific applications.

Theorem

3.1
(
TNBbeta median-concentration
).
Let
Y\sim\mathrm{TNBbeta}\big(p,\,q,\,\varepsilon\big)
. Then
p
and
q
are Fisher orthogonal and separately control the median and concentration such that

1.

\mathrm{median}(Y)=p

2.

\lim_{q\to 1}\mathrm{\mathrm{TNBbeta}}(p;\,p,\,q,\,\varepsilon)=\infty

3.

The Fisher information matrix satisfies
\mathcal{I}_{pq}(p,\,q,\,\varepsilon)=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[-\frac{\partial^{2}\log\mathrm{TNBbeta}(Y;\,p,\,q,\,\varepsilon)}{\partial p\,\partial q}\right]=0

Proof.

Part (1).
We first show that if
X\sim\mathrm{LNbeta}\big(\varepsilon,\,\varepsilon,\,\tfrac{1-p}{p}\big)
then
\mathrm{median}(X)\!=\!p
. Define the map
x\!=\!g(y)
as follows

x=\frac{y(1-p)}{y(1-p)+(1-y)p}

We show that this map transforms Libby-Novick beta density into the symmetric beta density
\textrm{beta}(\varepsilon,\,\varepsilon)
. Let
D=y(1-p)+(1-y)p
. Then the Jacobian is

\frac{dx}{dy}=\frac{(1-p)D-y(1-p)(1-2p)}{D^{2}}=\frac{p(1-p)}{D^{2}}

which is positive, so the map is strictly increasing. Under the map

x=\nicefrac{{y(1-p)}}{{D}}
and
1-x=\nicefrac{{(1-y)p}}{{D}}

x^{\varepsilon-1}(1-x)^{\varepsilon-1}=\frac{\big(y(1-p)\big)^{\varepsilon-1}\big((1-y)p\big)^{\varepsilon-1}}{D^{2(\varepsilon-1)}}

Therefore the change-of-variables formula
f_{X}(x)\,|dx/dy|=f_{Y}(y)
:

\underbrace{\frac{x^{\varepsilon-1}(1-x)^{\varepsilon-1}}{B(\varepsilon,\varepsilon)}}_{\text{beta}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon)}\cdot\frac{p(1-p)}{D^{2}}=\frac{y^{\varepsilon-1}(1-y)^{\varepsilon-1}}{B(\varepsilon,\varepsilon)}\cdot\frac{(1-p)^{\varepsilon}p^{\varepsilon}}{D^{2\varepsilon}}

which recovers the Libby-Novick beta density as desired. Now because
g(y)
is strictly increasing, the median
m
of
X

\mathbb{P}\big(X\leq m\big)=\tfrac{1}{2}\iff\mathbb{P}\big(Z\leq g(m)\big)=\tfrac{1}{2}

The median of
Z\sim\mathrm{beta}(\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
is
\tfrac{1}{2}
by symmetry, so
g(m)=\tfrac{1}{2}
. Solving:

\displaystyle g(m)=

\displaystyle\frac{m(1-p)}{m(1-p)+(1-m)p}=\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle 2m(1-p)=m(1-p)+(1-m)p

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle m(1-p)=(1-m)p

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle m=p

Thus the median of the Libby-Novick beta under equality of its shapes is
p
.

Now,
Y\sim\textrm{TNBbeta}\big(p,\,q,\,\varepsilon\big)
can be expressed as
Y\mid C\sim\textrm{LNbeta}\left(\varepsilon+C,\,\varepsilon+C,\,\tfrac{1-p}{p}\right)

4.2

p
.

Part (2).
We evaluate the density at
y\!=\!p

Therefore

\displaystyle\mathrm{TNBbeta}(p;\,p,\,q,\,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{beta}(p;\varepsilon,\,\varepsilon)\cdot\big(4p(1-p)\big)^{-\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{(1\!-\!q)^{\varepsilon}}{(1\!-\!q)^{\varepsilon+1/2}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{beta}(p;\varepsilon,\,\varepsilon)\cdot\big(4p(1-p)\big)^{-\varepsilon}\cdot(1\!-\!q)^{-1/2}.

As

, so
\mathrm{TNBbeta}(p;\,p,\,q,\,\varepsilon)\to\infty
.

Part (3).
Write the log-density as
\ell(y)=\ell_{\mathrm{LN}}(y;\,\varepsilon,\,p)+\varepsilon\log(1\!-\!q)-(\varepsilon\!+\!\tfrac{1}{2})\log(1-4q\gamma(y,p))

q

\frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial p}=\frac{\partial}{\partial p}\frac{y(1\!-\!y)p(1\!-\!p)}{D^{2}}=\frac{y(1\!-\!y)(y\!-\!p)}{D^{3}}

\frac{\partial D}{\partial p}=1-2y

\frac{\partial^{2}\ell}{\partial p\,\partial q}=\frac{4(\varepsilon\!+\!\tfrac{1}{2})\,\frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial p}}{\big(1-4q\gamma(y,p)\big)^{2}}=\frac{4(\varepsilon\!+\!\tfrac{1}{2})\,y(1\!-\!y)(y\!-\!p)}{D^{3}\big(1-4q\gamma(y,p)\big)^{2}}.

\frac{y(1\!-\!y)(y\!-\!p)}{D^{3}(1-4q\gamma)^{2}}\,\mathrm{TNBbeta}(y;\,p,\,q,\,\varepsilon)\,dy=\frac{(1\!-\!q)^{\varepsilon}}{p(1\!-\!p)\,B(\varepsilon,\varepsilon)}\cdot\frac{u^{\varepsilon}(1\!-\!u)^{\varepsilon}(2u\!-\!1)}{(1-4qu(1\!-\!u))^{\varepsilon+5/2}}\,du.

\Gamma(\alpha_{i}+j_{i})
terms:

f(y)=\frac{y^{\alpha_{1}-1}(1-y)^{\alpha_{2}-1}}{B(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}\,p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}p_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\sum_{j_{1}=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j_{2}=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+j_{1}+j_{2})}{\Gamma(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})\,j_{1}!\,j_{2}!}\big(y(1-p_{1})\big)^{j_{1}}\big((1-y)(1-p_{2})\big)^{j_{2}}.

Re-indexing with
s=j_{1}+j_{2}
and applying the binomial theorem to the inner sum:

\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+s)}{\Gamma(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})\,s!}\sum_{j_{1}=0}^{s}\binom{s}{j_{1}}\big(y(1-p_{1})\big)^{j_{1}}\big((1-y)(1-p_{2})\big)^{s-j_{1}}

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+s)}{\Gamma(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})\,s!}\big(y(1-p_{1})+(1-y)(1-p_{2})\big)^{s}.

Closing the series with the identity
\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(a+s)}{\Gamma(a)\,s!}x^{s}=(1-x)^{-a}
and simplifying:

f(y)=\frac{y^{\alpha_{1}-1}(1-y)^{\alpha_{2}-1}}{B(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}\frac{p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}p_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}}{\big(p_{2}-(p_{2}-p_{1})y\big)^{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}}=\frac{y^{\alpha_{1}-1}(1-y)^{\alpha_{2}-1}}{B(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}\frac{c^{\alpha_{1}}}{\big(1-(1-c)y\big)^{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}}

where
c=\nicefrac{{p_{1}}}{{p_{2}}}
. This is the Libby-Novick beta density
\mathrm{LNbeta}(\alpha_{1},\,\alpha_{2},\,c)
, as in
2.2
.
∎

Theorem

4.1
(
Triply negative binomial mixing
).
Let
\varepsilon\!>\!0
,
p\!\in\!(0,1)
,
q\!\in\![0,1]
, and define the following auxiliary-variable randomization of a standard beta random variable

\displaystyle C\sim\mathrm{NB}\big(\varepsilon,\,1\!-\!q

\displaystyle\big),\quad A\mid C\!=\!c\sim\mathrm{NB}\big(\varepsilon\!+\!c,\,1\!-\!p\big),\quad B\mid C\!=\!c\sim\mathrm{NB}\big(\varepsilon\!+\!c,\,p\big),

(47)

\displaystyle Y\mid A\!=\!a,\,B\!=\!b,\,C\!=\!c\sim\mathrm{beta}\big(\varepsilon\!+\!c\!+\!a,\,\,\varepsilon\!+\!c\!+\!b\big).

Then the marginal distribution is
Y\sim\mathrm{TNBbeta}(p,\,q,\,\varepsilon)
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

\thechapter.B.1
with
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=\varepsilon+c
,
p_{1}=1\!-\!p
, and
p_{2}=p
, marginalizing
(A,\,B)
for fixed
C\!=\!c
gives
Y\mid C\!=\!c\sim\mathrm{LNbeta}\big(\varepsilon+c,\,\varepsilon+c,\,\tfrac{1-p}{p}\big)
with density

f(y\mid c)=\frac{y^{\varepsilon+c-1}(1-y)^{\varepsilon+c-1}}{B(\varepsilon+c,\,\varepsilon+c)}\cdot\frac{p^{\varepsilon+c}(1\!-\!p)^{\varepsilon+c}}{D^{2(\varepsilon+c)}}

where
D=p(1\!-\!y)+(1\!-\!p)y
. Marginalizing
C\sim\mathrm{NB}(\varepsilon,\,1\!-\!q)
:

\displaystyle f(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{LNbeta}\big(y;\,\varepsilon\!+\!c,\,\varepsilon\!+\!c,\,\tfrac{1-p}{p}\big)\,\frac{\Gamma(\varepsilon+c)}{\Gamma(\varepsilon)\,c!}(1\!-\!q)^{\varepsilon}q^{c}

\displaystyle=\frac{y^{\varepsilon-1}(1-y)^{\varepsilon-1}\,p^{\varepsilon}(1\!-\!p)^{\varepsilon}}{D^{2\varepsilon}}\cdot(1\!-\!q)^{\varepsilon}\sum_{c=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\varepsilon+c)}{\Gamma(\varepsilon)\,c!}\cdot\frac{\Gamma(2\varepsilon+2c)}{\Gamma(\varepsilon+c)^{2}}\cdot\left(\frac{y(1\!-\!y)p(1\!-\!p)}{D^{2}}\,q\right)^{c}

where we have factored out terms independent of
c
. We simplify
\Gamma(2\varepsilon+2c)/\Gamma(\varepsilon+c)
via the Legendre duplication formula
\Gamma(2z)=2^{2z-1}\pi^{-1/2}\,\Gamma(z)\,\Gamma(z+\tfrac{1}{2})
:

\frac{\Gamma(2\varepsilon+2c)}{\Gamma(\varepsilon+c)}=\frac{2^{2\varepsilon+2c-1}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\,\Gamma\!\left(\varepsilon+c+\tfrac{1}{2}\right).

Substituting and writing
\gamma(y,p)=\nicefrac{{y(1\!-\!y)p(1\!-\!p)}}{{D^{2}}}
:

\displaystyle f(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{y^{\varepsilon-1}(1-y)^{\varepsilon-1}\,p^{\varepsilon}(1\!-\!p)^{\varepsilon}}{D^{2\varepsilon}}\cdot(1\!-\!q)^{\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{2^{2\varepsilon-1}}{\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma(\varepsilon)}\sum_{c=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\varepsilon+c+\tfrac{1}{2})}{c!}\,\big(4q\gamma(y,p)\big)^{c}.

The series is
\sum_{c=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\varepsilon+\frac{1}{2}+c)}{c!}(4q\gamma)^{c}=\Gamma\!\left(\varepsilon+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\big(1-4q\gamma(y,p)\big)^{-(\varepsilon+1/2)}
. Therefore

f(y)=\frac{y^{\varepsilon-1}(1-y)^{\varepsilon-1}\,p^{\varepsilon}(1\!-\!p)^{\varepsilon}}{D^{2\varepsilon}}\cdot(1\!-\!q)^{\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{2^{2\varepsilon-1}\,\Gamma(\varepsilon+\tfrac{1}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma(\varepsilon)}\cdot(1-4q\gamma)^{-(\varepsilon+1/2)}.

Applying the duplication formula once more gives
\frac{2^{2\varepsilon-1}\,\Gamma(\varepsilon+\frac{1}{2})}{\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma(\varepsilon)}=\frac{\Gamma(2\varepsilon)}{\Gamma(\varepsilon)^{2}}=\frac{1}{B(\varepsilon,\varepsilon)}
, so

f(y)=\frac{y^{\varepsilon-1}(1-y)^{\varepsilon-1}}{B(\varepsilon,\varepsilon)}\cdot\frac{p^{\varepsilon}(1\!-\!p)^{\varepsilon}}{D^{2\varepsilon}}\cdot\frac{(1\!-\!q)^{\varepsilon}}{(1-4q\gamma(y,p))^{\varepsilon+1/2}}=\mathrm{TNBbeta}(y;\,p,\,q,\,\varepsilon).\qed

4.1
(
Doubly negative binomial mixing
).
Let
\varepsilon\!>\!0
and
p\!\in\!(0,1)
. Define the following auxiliary-variable randomization of a standard beta random variable

\displaystyle A\sim\mathrm{NB}\big(\varepsilon,\,1\!-\!p\big),\qquad B\sim\mathrm{NB}(\varepsilon,\,p),

(48)

\displaystyle Y\mid A\!=\!a,\,B\!=\!b\sim\mathrm{beta}\big(\varepsilon\!+\!a,\,\varepsilon\!+\!b\big).

Then the marginal distribution is
Y\sim\mathrm{LNbeta}\Big(\varepsilon,\,\varepsilon,\,\tfrac{1-p}{p}\Big)
.

Proof.

The result follows directly from
Lemma

\thechapter.B.1
with specific parameter values.
∎

4.2
(
Randomized LNbeta
).
Let
\varepsilon\!>\!0
,
p\!\in\!(0,1)
, and
q\!\in\!(0,1)
. If
\,Y\mid C\!=\!c\sim\mathrm{LNbeta}\big(\varepsilon+c,\,\varepsilon+c,\,\tfrac{1-p}{p}\big)
and
C\sim\mathrm{NB}\big(\varepsilon,\,1\!-\!q\big)
, then the marginal is
Y\sim\mathrm{TNBbeta}(p,\,q,\,\varepsilon)
.

Proof.

By
Theorem

4.1
, the marginal of
Y
over
(C,A,B)
is
\mathrm{TNBbeta}(p,\,q,\,\varepsilon)
. Marginalizing only
(A,\,B)
for fixed
C\!=\!c
is the construction in
4.1
with
\varepsilon
replaced by
\varepsilon+c
, giving
Y\mid C\!=\!c\sim\mathrm{LNbeta}\big(\varepsilon+c,\,\varepsilon+c,\,\tfrac{1-p}{p}\big)
.
∎

Proposition

4.1
(
Mixing distribution
).
Define
D=A\!+\!C
and
E=B\!+\!C
where

C\sim\mathrm{NB}\big(\varepsilon,\,1\!-\!q\big),\qquad A\mid C=c\sim\mathrm{NB}\big(\varepsilon\!+\!c,\,1\!-\!p\big),\qquad B\mid C=c\sim\mathrm{NB}\big(\varepsilon\!+\!c,\,p\big).

(49)

Then the discrete bivariate distribution
\mathcal{DM}
for
(D,E)
has probability mass function

P(d,\,e;\,p,\,q,\,\varepsilon)=\mathrm{NB}(d;\,\varepsilon,\,1\!-\!p)\cdot\mathrm{NB}(e;\,\varepsilon,\,p)\cdot(1\!-\!q)^{\varepsilon}\;{}_{2}F_{1}(-d,-e;\,\varepsilon;\,q)

(50)

where
{}_{2}F_{1}(-d,-e;\,\varepsilon;\,q)
is a terminating Gauss hypergeometric series.

Proof.

Since
D=A+C
and
E=B+C
, and
A
and
B
are independent given
C
, we marginalize
C
noting that
D\!=\!d
and
E\!=\!e
constrain
C\leq d\wedge e
:

P(D\!=\!d,\,E\!=\!e)=\sum_{c=0}^{d\wedge e}P(A\!=\!d\!-\!c\mid C\!=\!c)\,P(B\!=\!e\!-\!c\mid C\!=\!c)\,P(C\!=\!c).

Expanding each negative binomial PMF in Gamma function form:

\displaystyle P(A\!=\!d\!-\!c\mid C\!=\!c)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\varepsilon+d)}{\Gamma(\varepsilon+c)\,(d-c)!}\,(1\!-\!p)^{\varepsilon+c}\,p^{d-c},

\displaystyle P(B\!=\!e\!-\!c\mid C\!=\!c)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\varepsilon+e)}{\Gamma(\varepsilon+c)\,(e-c)!}\,p^{\varepsilon+c}\,(1\!-\!p)^{e-c},

\displaystyle P(C\!=\!c)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\varepsilon+c)}{\Gamma(\varepsilon)\,c!}\,(1\!-\!q)^{\varepsilon}\,q^{c}.

The
p
and
(1\!-\!p)
terms combine as
(1\!-\!p)^{\varepsilon+c}\cdot p^{d-c}\cdot p^{\varepsilon+c}\cdot(1\!-\!p)^{e-c}=p^{\varepsilon+d}(1\!-\!p)^{\varepsilon+e}
, which is independent of
c
. One factor of
\Gamma(\varepsilon+c)
cancels between the numerator of
P(C\!=\!c)
and the denominator of
P(A\!=\!d\!-\!c\mid C\!=\!c)
. Factoring out all terms independent of
c
:

\displaystyle P(D\!=\!d,\,E\!=\!e)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\varepsilon+d)\,\Gamma(\varepsilon+e)}{\Gamma(\varepsilon)}\,p^{\varepsilon+d}(1\!-\!p)^{\varepsilon+e}(1\!-\!q)^{\varepsilon}\sum_{c=0}^{d\wedge e}\frac{q^{c}}{(d-c)!\,(e-c)!\,\Gamma(\varepsilon+c)\,c!}.

To evaluate the sum, we use the Pochhammer identity
1/(m\!-\!c)!=(-1)^{c}(-m)_{c}/m!
so

\frac{1}{(d\!-\!c)!\,(e\!-\!c)!\,\Gamma(\varepsilon+c)\,c!}=\frac{(-d)_{c}\,(-e)_{c}}{d!\,e!\,\Gamma(\varepsilon)\,(\varepsilon)_{c}\,c!}

where
(-1)^{2c}=1
. Last, recognize the Gauss hypergeometric series and the negative binomial mass functions
\tbinom{\varepsilon+d-1}{d}(1\!-\!p)^{\varepsilon}p^{d}=\mathrm{NB}(d;\,\varepsilon,\,1\!-\!p)
and
\tbinom{\varepsilon+e-1}{e}p^{\varepsilon}(1\!-\!p)^{e}=\mathrm{NB}(e;\,\varepsilon,\,p)
so

P(D\!=\!d,\,E\!=\!e)=\mathrm{NB}(d;\,\varepsilon,\,1\!-\!p)\cdot\mathrm{NB}(e;\,\varepsilon,\,p)\cdot(1\!-\!q)^{\varepsilon}\;{}_{2}F_{1}(-d,\,-e;\,\varepsilon;\,q).\qed

Proposition

4.2
(
Inverse distributions for
A
and
B
).
Define
A
,
B
, and
Y
as in
4.1
. Then the inverse distribution for
A
is negative binomial

A\mid Y\!=\!y\sim\mathrm{NB}\Big(2\varepsilon,\,\,1\!-\!\tfrac{py}{1\!-\!(1\!-\!p)(1\!-\!y)}\Big)

(51)

Moreover, the distribution of
A
conditioned on both
Y
and
B
is also negative binomial

A\mid B\!=\!b,\,Y\!=\!y\sim\mathrm{NB}\big(2\varepsilon\!+\!b,\,1\!-\!py\big)

(52)

In each case, the distribution for
B
is identical up to substitution of
py
with
(1\!-\!p)(1\!-\!y)
. Thus the conditional distribution
(A,B)\mid Y
is a product of negative binomial densities.

Proof.

We begin with the second statement and keep only terms proportional to
a
to write

\displaystyle P\big(A\!=\!a\mid B\!=\!b,\,Y\!=\!y\big)

\displaystyle\propto\mathrm{beta}\big(y;\;\varepsilon+a,\,\varepsilon+b\big)\cdot\mathrm{NB}\big(a;\,\varepsilon,\,1-p\big)

\displaystyle\propto\frac{\Gamma(2\varepsilon+b+a)}{a!}\big(yp\big)^{a}

which is the kernel of
\mathrm{NB}\big(2\varepsilon+b,\,1-yp\big)
.

For the first statement, we write the joint posterior over both counts. By Bayes’ rule,

\displaystyle P(A\!=\!a,\,B\!=\!b\mid Y\!=\!y)

\displaystyle\propto\mathrm{beta}\big(y;\,\varepsilon\!+\!a,\,\varepsilon\!+\!b\big)\cdot\mathrm{NB}\big(a;\,\varepsilon,\,1\!-\!p\big)\cdot\mathrm{NB}\big(b;\,\varepsilon,\,p\big)

\displaystyle\propto\frac{\Gamma(2\varepsilon+a+b)}{a!\,b!}\,\big(yp\big)^{a}\,\big((1\!-\!y)(1\!-\!p)\big)^{b}

Then marginalize over
B
to form the posterior marginal of
A
as

P(A\!=\!a\mid Y\!=\!y)\propto\frac{\big(yp\big)^{a}}{a!}\sum_{b=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(2\varepsilon+a+b)}{b!}\big((1-y)(1-p)\big)^{b}.

Finally, we use the series identity
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+j)}{j!}x^{j}=\Gamma(\alpha)(1-x)^{-\alpha}
to write

\displaystyle P(A\!=\!a\mid Y\!=\!y)

\displaystyle\propto\frac{\Gamma(2\varepsilon+a)}{a!}\cdot\frac{\big(yp\big)^{a}}{\big(1-(1-y)(1-p)\big)^{2\varepsilon+a}}

\displaystyle\propto\frac{\Gamma(2\varepsilon+a)}{a!}\left(\frac{yp}{1-(1-y)(1-p)}\right)^{a}

which is the kernel of
\mathrm{NB}\!\left(2\varepsilon,\,1\!-\!\frac{yp}{1-(1-y)(1-p)}\right)
.
∎

Proposition

4.3
(
Inverse distribution for
A\!+\!B
).
Define
A
,
B
, and
Y
as in
4.1
. Then the inverse distribution for the sum
A+B
is also negative binomial

A\!+\!B\mid Y\!=\!y\sim\mathrm{NB}\big(2\varepsilon,\,\,y(1-p)+p(1-y)\big).

(53)

Moreover, the distribution for
A
conditional on the sum
A\!+\!B
and
Y
is binomial

A\mid A\!+\!B\!=\!j,\,Y\!=\!y\sim\mathrm{binomial}\Big(j,\,\mathrm{logit}^{-1}\big(\logit(y)+\logit(p)\big)\Big).

(54)

Proof.

For the first statement, begin from the joint inverse distribution

P(A\!=\!a,\,B\!=\!b\mid Y\!=\!y)\propto\frac{\Gamma(2\varepsilon+a+b)}{a!\,b!}\,\big(yp\big)^{a}\,\big((1\!-\!y)(1\!-\!p)\big)^{b}.

Substituting
b=s\!-\!a
and summing over
a=0,\ldots,s
:

\displaystyle P\big(A+B\!=\!s\mid Y\!=\!y\big)

\displaystyle\propto\sum_{a=0}^{s}\frac{\Gamma(2\varepsilon+s)}{a!(s-a)!}\,\big(yp\big)^{a}\big((1\!-\!y)(1\!-\!p)\big)^{s-a}

\displaystyle\propto\frac{\Gamma(2\varepsilon+s)}{s!}\sum_{a=0}^{s}\binom{s}{a}\big(yp\big)^{a}\big((1\!-\!y)(1\!-\!p)\big)^{s-a}.

where we used
\binom{s}{a}=s!/[a!(s\!-\!a)!]
. Applying the binomial theorem gives

P\big(A+B\!=\!s\mid Y\!=\!y\big)\propto\frac{\Gamma(2\varepsilon+s)}{s!}\big(yp+(1-y)(1-p)\big)^{s}

which is the kernel of
\mathrm{NB}\big(2\varepsilon,\,p(1-y)+y(1-p)\big)
.

For the second statement, we write the conditional as a ratio of the joint and marginal:

P\big(A\!=\!a\mid A+B\!=\!s,\,Y\!=\!y\big)=\frac{P(A\!=\!a,\,B\!=\!s\!-\!a\mid Y\!=\!y)}{P(A+B\!=\!s\mid Y\!=\!y)}.

Substituting the expressions above and cancelling the common factor
\Gamma(2\varepsilon+s)
:

P\big(A\!=\!a\mid A+B\!=\!s,\,Y\!=\!y\big)\propto\binom{s}{a}\big(yp\big)^{a}\big((1-y)(1-p)\big)^{s-a}

which is the kernel of
\mathrm{binomial}\!\left(s,\,\nicefrac{{py}}{{py+(1-p)(1-y)}}\right)
.
∎

Proposition

4.4
(
Inverse distribution for
C
).
The inverse distribution of the negative binomial
C
in the auxiliary-count representation in
Equation

17
is negative binomial

C\mid Y=y\sim\text{NB}\big(\varepsilon\!+\!\tfrac{1}{2},\,1\!-\!4q\gamma(y,p)\big)

(55)

where the similarity function
\gamma(y,p)
is defined in
Equation

14
.

Proof.

By
4.2
,
Y\mid C\!=\!c\sim\mathrm{LNbeta}\big(\varepsilon+c,\,\varepsilon+c,\,\tfrac{1-p}{p}\big)
and
C\sim\mathrm{NB}(\varepsilon,\,1\!-\!q)
. By Bayes’ rule,

\displaystyle P(C\!=\!c\mid Y\!=\!y)

\displaystyle\propto\mathrm{LNbeta}\Big(y;\;\varepsilon\!+\!c,\,\varepsilon\!+\!c,\,\tfrac{1-p}{p}\Big)\cdot\mathrm{NB}\big(c\;\varepsilon,\,1\!-\!q\big)

\displaystyle\propto\frac{1}{B(\varepsilon\!+\!c,\,\varepsilon\!+\!c)}\cdot\frac{\big(y(1\!-\!y)p(1\!-\!p)\big)^{c}}{\big(p(1\!-\!y)+(1\!-\!p)y\big)^{2c}}\cdot\frac{\Gamma(\varepsilon\!+\!c)}{c!}\,q^{c}

\displaystyle\propto\frac{\Gamma(2\varepsilon+2c)}{\Gamma(\varepsilon+c)}\cdot\frac{\big(\gamma(y,p)q\big)^{c}}{c!}

where we recognize the similarity function
\gamma(y,p)=\nicefrac{{y(1\!-\!y)p(1\!-\!p)}}{{\big(p(1\!-\!y)+(1\!-\!p)y\big)^{2}}}
Applying the Legendre duplication formula

\Gamma(2z)=2^{2z-1}\pi^{-1/2}\,\Gamma(z)\,\Gamma(z\!+\!\tfrac{1}{2})
with
z=\varepsilon\!+\!c
gives

P(C\!=\!c\mid Y\!=\!y)\propto\frac{\Gamma(\varepsilon+\tfrac{1}{2}+c)}{c!}\,\big(4q\gamma(y,p)\big)^{c}

which is the kernel of
\mathrm{NB}\big(\varepsilon+\tfrac{1}{2},\,1\!-\!4q\gamma(y,p)\big)
.
∎
