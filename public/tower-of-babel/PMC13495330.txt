The effectiveness of psychological interventions on pain, disability, and psychological symptoms in whiplash-associated disorders: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Mohammad Al-Wardat, Tasneem Al Rjoub, Ahmad Alrawashdeh, Mohammad Etoom, Saddam Kanaan, Ziad Hawamdeh, Alaa Oteir, Auwal Abdullahi, Khader Almhdawi, Hashem Abu Tariah, Chiara Pavese, Silvia Natoli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhiplash-associated disorders (WAD) include a range of physical and psychological symptoms, profoundly impacting individuals' quality of life. The effectiveness of psychological interventions in WAD management is still uncertain. Thus, the aim of this study is to investigate the effectiveness of psychological interventions on clinical outcomes (e.g., pain, disability, psychological distress) among individuals with WAD. Are psychological interventions effective in improving clinical outcomes (e.g., pain, disability, psychological distress) among individuals with WAD?MethodsThree databases were comprehensively searched for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that investigated the effects of psychological interventions on pain, disability, depression, stress, anxiety, or fear of movement in patients with WAD compared with control groups receiving usual care or no intervention. Meta-analyses were performed by calculating the weight mean difference (WMD), 95% CI, and P value. The Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB2) was used to assess the risk of bias in the included studies.ResultsEleven RCTs met the inclusion criteria. The pooled analysis indicated a significant improvement in pain scales immediately after the intervention (WMD = 0.96, P < 0.05), and at 3–6 months and 1-year follow-ups. Disability, pain catastrophizing, and kinesiophobia improved significantly at post-intervention and after 3–6 months. In addition, the pooled analysis showed significant improvements in depression, anxiety, self-efficacy, and stress symptoms post-intervention.ConclusionsThis systematic review provides varied evidence for the effectiveness of psychological interventions on pain, disability, and psychological symptoms in WAD. These immediate and long-term benefits in favor of psychological interventions are critical in the chronic pain management of WAD. Future research should focus on refining the most effective psychological interventions, considering cultural and contextual factors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04955-y.

Introduction

Whiplash injury is a medical condition resulting from a rapid acceleration-deceleration of the head, usually after mild to moderate trauma. The resultant damage to the muscles, ligaments, discs, and tendons in the neck region [1]. manifests through a spectrum of symptoms, profoundly impacting individuals' quality of life [2]. Whiplash-associated disorders (WAD) include a range of physical and psychological symptoms. Individuals with WAD commonly experience reduced range of motion, myofascial dysfunction, and neck pain [2]. In addition to physical symptoms, WAD leads to cognitive difficulties, including memory problems and concentration issues, along with psychological challenges such as anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder, which predominantly occur in chronic stage of WAD [1]. Associated factors such as fatigue, disrupted sleep patterns, and sensory disturbances further complicate the condition [3–5]. The severity and duration of these symptoms vary, influenced by different factors, including the extent of the initial injury's, pain perception, pre-existing health conditions, and the efficacy of medical interventions [6, 7]. Indeed, psychological and sensory factors such as post-traumatic stress and hypersensitivity manifestations (e.g., hyperalgesia and allodynia), play a major role and are considered strong prognostic indicators [8]. Pain is the most prevalent symptom reported following a whiplash injury involving a complex interaction of physiological and neurological processes [9]. It has been reported that approximately half of whiplash-injured patients experience symptoms beyond the expected healing period (6–12 weeks), strengthening the dynamic and complex interactions among mechanical, psychological and social factors and leading to chronic pain and long term social and psychological disability [10]. Low pain, pain catastrophizing, stress, depression, and anxiety have been found associated with poorer health outcomes in WAD [2, 11]. Furthermore, the individual's cognitive appraisal of the injury, coupled with pre-existing psychological factors, can modulate pain experiences [12, 13].

A comprehensive approach that considers WAD multifaceted factors is crucial for accurate diagnosis and effective management [14] and adopting a biopsychosocial approach—a view of pain as a multidimensional and dynamic interaction among physiological, psychological, and social factors [15] is essential for understanding pain and disability in the context of WAD. Indeed, clinical guidelines recommend a comprehensive evaluation and treatment of psychological factors as part of the management of chronic pain patients [16–18]. Specifically, several studies have reported notable prominence and significant improvement in various chronic musculoskeletal pain conditions, supporting the efficacy of psychological interventions [19–22]. Despite these promising results in favor of psychological and exercise-based interventions for chronic musculoskeletal pain post WAD, significant gaps remain. Existing reviews have mainly focused on specific interventions, such as Pilates for older adults [19], cognitive behavioral therapy [20]. Additionally, while a meta-analysis explored mediators and moderators of psychological interventions [21, 22], inconsistencies in findings and methodological limitations weaken conclusions regarding treatment mechanisms. Notably, no review has comprehensively compared the effectiveness of different psychological interventions across multiple chronic musculoskeletal conditions post WAD while also assessing their long-term effect. Given the increasing recognition of psychological factors in chronic pain management, a new review is needed to systematically evaluate the comparative effectiveness and sustainability of psychological interventions for musculoskeletal pain post WAD, addressing methodological shortcomings and providing clinically relevant recommendations. However, to the best of our knowledge and based on the available literature, the effectiveness of psychological interventions on neck pain and disability in WAD is still uncertain. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aim to evaluate the effectiveness of psychological interventions compared to conservative care or no intervention in reducing pain intensity and improving disability and psychological disorders (i.e., depression, anxiety, and stress) among adults with WAD.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyzes (PRISMA) guidelines [23]. The protocol of this review was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the number CRD42021265109.

Dissertations, study reports, books, conference proceedings, and abstracts were excluded to maintain the rigor of the review. Only peer-reviewed journal articles were included. Studies that combined psychological interventions with physiotherapy interventions were excluded to ensure the focus remained solely on psychological interventions for the specified condition. Studies that did not report sufficient data (i.e., the mean and standard deviation) were excluded as they could not contribute to the quantitative analysis. Studies with overlapping results were excluded from the quantitative synthesis if they reported data from the same sample or data set to prevent duplication and bias in statistical analysis. However, we made sure that such studies were not excluded from the qualitative analysis if they presented different aspects or interpretations of the data.

Data search and study selection

A comprehensive systematic computerized search of MEDLINE via PubMed, Scopus, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials databases up to March 2025 was performed. The search strategy included the study population using terms and keywords derived from scoping search and expertise within the subject area. Study population terms: “whiplash”, “whiplash associated disorder”, “WAD”, “neck”, “cervical spine”, “whiplash injury”, “motor vehicle accident”, “road traffic accident’’, and psychological intervention”. The search strategy was used as a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and text terms relating to whiplash. A manual search of the references in the included studies was conducted. The studies were processed using the systematic review management software Covidence (https://www.covidence.org), and duplicated records were removed. Two reviewers (A. R. and T.R.) independently screened titles and abstracts according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. A third reviewer (M.A-W) was called to solve uncertainties and disagreements until a consensus was reached. The full text of titles and abstracts was obtained and reviewed. The full electronic search strategy is presented in the Supplementary Material in Table S1.

Data extraction

Two independent authors (S. K. and T. A.) extracted the following data: (1) demographic characteristics including sample size, country, age, sex distribution, and disease stage; (2) details of psychological interventions administered, such as dose, type, duration, and frequency; (3) outcomes measured, timing of measurements (pre, post, and follow-up), and results reported at each time point (mean, standard deviation, and number of participants); and (4) the content of "control group," as well as the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Acute WAD was defined as a symptom duration of ≤ 12 weeks, whereas chronic WAD was defined as symptoms persisting for > 12 weeks. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion or, if required, adjudication by a third author (M.A-W). In cases where the original data was unclear or lacking adequate details, the researchers attempted to contact the corresponding authors to provide the missing data.

Quality assessment and risk of bias

Three independent authors (M.A-W, M.E, T.A-R) independently assessed the included studies for overall risk of bias using the revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials (RoB2) [25, 26]. The risk of bias was categorized as low risk, some concerns, or high risk. The assessment considered the following domains as outlined in RoB2: the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of the reported result. The Grade of Recommendation, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach was used to assess the quality of evidence-based practice. The procedure of concluding GRADE level of evidence was explained elsewhere [27] (Table S3).

Data synthesis/analysis

Weighted mean difference (WMD) at 95% confidence interval (CI), and P-value were calculated based by comparing the change in the same outcome measurement between the intervention and control groups. The Random-effect analysis model was adopted, and the significance level was set at P < 0.05. For articles that included two intervention groups or two control groups, the average of the two groups was included in the meta-analysis. The I2 index was employed to quantify the heterogeneity [28]. Meta-analysis was conducted if there were at least two studies using the same outcome measure using Review Manager (Revman) software, version 5.3.

Study and participant characteristics

Eleven trials, as outlined in Table 1, involved a total of 1069 participants. The average age of participants across these studies varied from 20 to 60 years. All included studies enrolled both female and male participants. All studies reported disease severity, five studies included participants in the acute stage [33, 35–37, 39] with grade Ⅰ and Ⅱ except one study [33] with grade Ⅱ or Ⅲ, one study with grade Ⅰ—Ⅲ [37], and one study the symptoms within 72 h afterwards [34]. One study included participants with subacute stage with grade Ⅰ or Ⅱ [38]. Five studies included participants with chronic stage [29–31, 34] with grade Ⅰ—Ⅲ, except one study with grade Ⅰ—Ⅱ past six months from injury [32].

Intervention

The included studies employed different psychological interventions. In one study the participants received a learning theory framework focused on exposure and acceptance strategies for enhancing psychological flexibility [29]. Söderlund et al. (2007) emphasized the acquisition and application of physical and psychological skills [30], while Sterling et al. (2019) implemented stress inoculation training integrated with exercise as part of their psychological intervention [33]. Two studies targeted chronic and acute cases, respectively, with interventions emphasizing the learning and application of physical and psychological skills [34, 39]. In addition, one study provided information, advice, and educational sessions during a home visit [36]. Three studies applied different cognitive therapy, one study used trauma-focused cognitive-behavioral therapy [29], another study compared internet-delivered and face-to-face behavioral medicine treatments [35], and one study implemented a values-based cognitive-behavioral therapy [31]. Two studies used educational phycological interventions, one study divided participants into two groups, both receiving basic information, with the second group receiving additional educational presentations [38], and one study combined educational videos focusing on pain mechanisms and providing reassurance [37]. The intervention protocols across the included studies ranged in duration from 1 to 24 weeks, and training frequency varied between 1–10 sessions per week, each lasting between 15–120 min.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the effectiveness of psychological interventions on neck pain and disability in patients with WAD investigated in11 RCTs. The pooled analysis suggested that psychological interventions significantly improved pain, disability, and psychological symptoms at post-intervention and at long-term follow-ups data points. These findings are consistent with a previous metanalysis that has reported the significant influence of psychological interventions on pain and associated disability, in different WAD populations undergoing physiotherapist-delivered psychological interventions [40]. Importantly, this study provides an updated and focused synthesis of psychological interventions in WAD, including recent RCTs and long-term follow-up data. It further demonstrates sustained treatment effects over time, strengthening the evidence supporting their role in multidisciplinary WAD management.

Significant improvements in pain intensity was observed immediately post-intervention and was maintained at 3–6 months, 6 months, and 1-year follow-ups, but not at the 1–3-month time point. The pooled estimate at 1–3 months suggested a clinically relevant reduction in pain severity (WMD = 1.52) however, this effect did not reach statistical significance. It worth mentioning that the analysis at 1–3 months was based on only two RCTs with substantial heterogeneity [30, 35]. Furthermore, these two RCTs differed markedly as one used minimal written self-care instructions, whereas the other employed structured physiotherapy. This could have potentially reduced between-group differences and the analysis statistical power. The absence of statistical significance therefore may be attributed to methodological variability rather than a true loss of treatment effect.

Several mechanisms may explain the significant pain improvements at longer follow-up periods. Psychological interventions aim to equip WAD patients with coping strategies for pain management [41], which may provide early symptom relief while gradually improving pain perception as patients become more proficient in their application [42]. In addition, these interventions may facilitate cognitive restructuring, enabling patients to reinterpret pain and reduce its perceived intensity [43]. This process may require extensive time to consolidate and thus become more apparent at longer follow-up data points. Given the traumatic nature of WAD, psychological factors such as stress, anxiety, and fear may exaggerate pain perception [44]. Psychological interventions may reduce central sensitization [45], which is partially driven by trauma-related nociceptive input, muscle spasm, and inflammatory responses [46]. This might end in spinal and supraspinal amplification of pain signals [47, 48]. Psychological interventions are believed to induce neuroplastic changes [49], with neuroimaging evidence suggesting that CBT can modify pain-related neural networks, possibly reducing sustained pain intensity at late stages post WAD psychological intervention delivery [50]. This further support the delayed nature of pain reduction following WAD related psychological interventions.

This study subgroup analyses revealed that psychological interventions significantly could reduce pain intensity in acute WAD, but not in chronic WAD, which may be explained by differences across the included trials. Acute WAD studies [33, 35, 39], implemented early psychological targeting pain-related beliefs, stress responses, fear avoidance, and self-efficacy, often combined with usual care and education. In contrast, chronic WAD trials [29, 34] focused on skill-based or acceptance-oriented approaches in populations with long-standing symptoms, where pain may be less modifiable. Control conditions also differed between acute and chronic WAD studies in this review, potentially attenuating between-group effects. Future research should directly compare psychologically informed interventions across acute and chronic WAD using better standardized protocols, comparable control conditions, and consistent outcome measures. From a clinical perspective, these findings support the early implementation of psychologically informed interventions in acute WAD. It seems that early psychological interventions delivery may reduce WAD related maladaptive beliefs and fear avoidance, enhance self-efficacy, and potentially prevent pain chronicity and long-term disability.

This study results introduce the concept that pain and disability are closely intertwined. Five out of the six studies assessed disability using the NDI reported significant reductions in NDI scores [31–33, 38, 39]. The pooled meta-analysis showed improvements both post-intervention and at 3–6 months. Four studies assessed disability using the PDI, with three reporting significant reductions; however, the pooled analysis showed a large but non-significant reduction post-intervention and at 3–6 months. This discrepancy can be explained by the differences in measurement focus, participant characteristics and study methodology.

The NDI specifically captures neck-related functional limitations (e.g., lifting, driving, reading, concentration). This aligns closely with the targets of the psychological interventions (e.g., reducing pain catastrophizing, fear of neck movement, and activity avoidance). In contrast, the PDI measures general disability across multiple life domains (e.g., household tasks, recreation, social participation), many of which were not directly addressed by the psychological interventions of this review. Moreover, differences in baseline severity, chronicity, and inclusion criteria could have also contributed to the observed NDI/PDI results discrepancy. Notably, compared to PDI, NDI studies included in this review, tended to include individuals with higher baseline neck-related disability, allowing a greater room for potential measurable improvement. These variations in psychological interventions effects do not necessarily reflect superiority of one measure over the other. Larger and more methodologically robust studies are needed to clarify the comparative responsiveness of PDI and NDI along other measures in WAD populations response to psychological interventions. Clinical effectiveness of psychological interventions remains uncertain despite that the meta-analysis revealed significant pooled effects on pain and disability outcomes. Previous studies estimated the minimum clinically importance difference for VAS and NDI as 4,3 and 7.5 points, respectively, in neck pain populations [51, 52]. The lower limits of the 95% confidence intervals were less than 4.3 points for all NRS analyses, and less than 7.5 points for all NDI analyses.

The psychological interventions have also shown significant improvements detected using PCS and TSK. These significant improvements were maintained at the 3–6-month follow-up. These results may be interpreted considering the possibility that fear of movement exacerbates the relationship between pain and disability in individuals with WAD [47]. Therefore, reductions in PCS and TSK likely reflect a shared psychological mechanism targeted by these interventions [53]. In contrast, educational interventions alone appear insufficient to meaningfully modify these fear-based cognitive and behavioral responses and consequently show limited effects on pain and disability outcomes when combined with physiotherapy in WAD [54]. However, Baroncini et al. highlighted the benefits of multidisciplinary rehabilitation, emphasizing that integrating psychological interventions with physical therapy can enhance functional outcomes in chronic musculoskeletal conditions [55]. This contrast highlights the need to explore contextual factors influencing educational interventions and their role within a comprehensive rehabilitation approach for WAD.

A previous study reported that physical exercises may result in larger improvement on pain than disability in WAD [56], suggesting weak reflection of pain reduction in increasing activity and participation. Psychological interventions are claimed to target the psychosocial aspects of pain rather than influencing the actual pain actual neurophysiological responses [18]. We speculate that psychological interventions may improve patients’ individualized perception of pain and lead to promote participation in physical activities and rehabilitation, possibly resulting in improved functional recovery and reduced disability. This review results recommend more clinical incorporation of psychological interventions in WAD treatment protocols and raising psychosocial treatment orientation [57] for health care providers may improve WAD management.

Mental health symptoms including depression, anxiety, and stress, are highly prevalent, and can worsen prognosis in WAD [58]. A previous study found that mental factors such as anxiety in the acute stage of whiplash showed more negative influences on the condition’s recovery than physical factors [59]. This current meta-analysis showed significant improvements in psychological symptoms measured by HADS. The reduction in anxiety is particularly crucial, as anxiety can exacerbate pain perception and disability, leading to a vicious cycle of chronic pain and psychological distress.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. The high heterogeneity of the interventions, in both content and timing, along with the small number of included articles, have greatly limits the possibility of conducting subgroup analyses or meta-regression. Despite that we limited abilities to improve the quality of the included studies, this limitation restricted this review ability to identify potential sources of heterogeneity effectively. Consequently, this observed heterogeneity may raise a concern about the validity of our conclusions. Second, the wide variability of the included populations at baseline in terms of whiplash severity raise questions about the generalizability of the results. Further analyses investigating the effectiveness of psychological interventions in specific whiplash-injured populations are required. Thirdly, small sample sizes of the review studies, although out of our hands as well, have introduced a high risk of bias and inconsistency in the effect size among included studies that resulted and low and very low levels of evidence. Finally, methodological variabilities in terms of interventions, data points, and measures could have also negatively influenced this review findings generalizability.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis indicate that psychological interventions can effectively reduce pain intensity, disability, and psychological symptoms in patients with WAD with overall evidence was low to very low. The findings suggest that these interventions offer both immediate and long-term benefits, particularly in reducing neck-related disability and addressing psychological factors such as pain catastrophizing and kinesiophobia, which are critical in the chronic pain experience. Future research should focus on refining psychological interventions, considering cultural and contextual factors, and ensuring long-term follow-up to capture delayed effects. Incorporating psychological strategies into comprehensive rehabilitation programs for WAD could significantly enhance recovery and quality of life, although further studies are needed to optimize and personalize these interventions.
