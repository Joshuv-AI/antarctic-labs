Machine learning-based prediction of bacterial infection foci: model development, calibration and uncertainty quantification using conformal prediction methods
Authors: Jacob Bahnsen Schmidt, Karen Leth Nielsen, Dmytro Strunin, Nikolai S Kirkby, Jesper Q Thomassen, Steen C Rasmussen, Ruth Frikke-Schmidt, Frederik Boetius Hertz, Allan Peter Engsig-Karup
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo develop and evaluate machine learning (ML) models capable of reliably predicting the focus of bacterial infections in hospitalised patients, addressing limitations of conventional microbiological diagnostics.Methods and analysisWe conducted a retrospective study using data from 10 153 patients admitted to Rigshospitalet, Denmark, between 1 November 2019 and 3 June 2023. The dataset included microbiological findings, biochemical measurements and vital parameters. ML models were trained and evaluated, with outputs calibrated using Venn-ABERS calibration. Model uncertainty was quantified through conformal risk control to provide statistically robust uncertainty estimates.ResultsThis study shows that the XGBoost model demonstrated the best performance, achieving a log loss of 0.209±0.006 (mean±SD) and an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.93±0.007. Incorporating conformal prediction techniques enhanced predictive reliability by combining high accuracy with calibrated probabilistic predictions and uncertainty estimates.ConclusionWe find that ML models, particularly XGBoost combined with conformal prediction frameworks, can accurately predict the focus of bacterial infections while providing robust uncertainty quantification. This approach offers a promising addition to conventional diagnostic methods, potentially improving clinical decision-making.Keywords: Machine Learning, Artificial intelligence, Electronic Health Records, Infectious Disease Medicine

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Predicting bacterial infections using machine learning is an emerging field. Consequently, the existing literature is sparse. However, we found evidence of data-driven approaches to predict bacterial infections in several studies. These studies suggested that it is possible to predict bacterial infections based on biochemical test results from blood tests, vital parameters or other types of patient data, using classical statistical methods or machine learning. The relevant prior evidence also suggested that the dataset size has a large impact on model performance.

Introduction

Clinical microbiological diagnostics are essential for guiding the treatment of infectious diseases by identifying the causative bacterial pathogen and its antimicrobial resistance profile. However, conventional diagnostics primarily rely on phenotypic testing, which is inherently time-consuming due to the requirement for bacterial growth, often taking 24–72 hours.

Additionally, the focus of infection may be difficult to predict for clinicians, causing them to order a range of diagnostic tests. While broad-spectrum antibiotics are necessary for ‘infections-without-focus’, overuse contributes to the emergence of multidrug resistance and increases the risk of side effects. The infection site not only dictates the choice of antibiotic—based on tissue penetration and expected local resistance patterns—but also determines the necessity for diagnostic imaging and testing. Despite its importance, early localisation of the infection remains a complex clinical task. Clinicians must recognise patterns from patient history, vital signs and preliminary laboratory results. There is, therefore, a significant research gap for tools that can provide early decision support by predicting the most likely infection focus before culture results are available.

Machine learning (ML) offers a promising solution by identifying complex patterns in routinely collected clinical data. Previous studies have shown promising results in predicting bacterial infections using data-driven ML approaches. A study by Marandi et al 1 focused on predicting bloodstream infection (BSI) primarily based on biochemical data. Their best performing model, LightGBM, achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.69, with a sensitivity of 0.57 and specificity of 0.70. Rawson et al 2 used support vector machines (SVM) to predict the onset of community-acquired bacterial infections within the first 72 hours of hospital admission. Using six commonly available blood parameters, they achieved an AUC of 0.84, with a sensitivity of 0.89 and specificity of 0.63 at a likelihood cut-off of 0.81. Slightly adjusting the cut-off to 0.82 resulted in a sensitivity of 0.44 and specificity of 0.93, indicating potential model uncertainty and calibration issues.

While these studies demonstrate the potential of ML for infection prediction, current approaches share several limitations: they often focus on binary outcomes (eg, predicting BSI vs no infection) and lack uncertainty calibration. These gaps limit clinical applicability, as clinicians need both accurate predictions and reliable confidence measures to guide treatment decisions.

In this study, we aim to address these limitations by developing a multilabel classification model capable of predicting multiple infection foci and combining it with the conformal prediction framework3 to provide well-calibrated uncertainty estimates. This approach improves predictive reliability, aligning with the practical needs of clinical decision-making. We specifically target respiratory tract infections, urinary tract infections (UTI) and BSI, as these collectively account for the vast majority of bacterial infections in hospitalised patients and are primary drivers of antibiotic prescription. Additionally, these sites represent a major diagnostic challenge: distinguishing ‘true infection’ requiring antimicrobial treatment from ‘asymptomatic carriage or colonization’ (eg, asymptomatic bacteriuria or respiratory colonisation). By focusing on these three high-prevalence foci, we aim to develop a predictive tool that addresses the clinical areas with the highest potential impact on diagnostic accuracy and antibiotic stewardship.

Methods

Data collection

This study used a retrospective cross-sectional design, including patients of all age groups who had positive bacterial cultures sampled at Rigshospitalet (Copenhagen, Denmark) between 1 November 2019 and 3 June 2023.

Rigshospitalet serves as a tertiary hospital primarily for referral and highly specialised treatment. The dataset consisted of secondary collected data and was produced using Danish electronic health records (Sundhedsplatformen, Epic Systems) and the MADS database (Department of Microbiology Data Systems). Repeated positive cultures, defined as subsequent isolates obtained from the same patient and isolation site with the same pathogen within 30 days, were removed from the dataset.

The dataset was compiled as a cross-sectional dataset to simplify the analysis while ensuring that only relevant data, that is, data gathered in the period leading up to the infection, were used, allowing the model to be applicable to new cases without requiring access to a long history of prior recorded data. Biochemical test results from blood samples and vital parameters were gathered up to 7 days prior to the positive conventional cultures. All biochemical test results and vital parameters within the 7-day period were used to generate multiple bacterial isolate—biochemical test result pairs. Data from the 7 days preceding positive cultures were aggregated daily (mean values) to form feature vectors (73 features; details shown in online supplemental materials S1, S2, S5, S8 and S10). Furthermore, data transformations were applied to non-Gaussian features to obtain Gaussian distributed features, as described in online supplemental material S6. For supervised ML, the dataset was labelled using a table describing the findings related to the bacterial isolates. Specifically, ML labels were generated using a combination of the sample origin and type of bacteria found in the sample (online supplemental materials S7 shows the ML label assigned to each combination of bacteria and origin).

Machine learning

We framed the task as a one-versus-rest classification task for six infection/colonisation classes, evaluated using fivefold GroupKFold cross-validation to partition the dataset into three sets—a training set (24 096), a calibration set (1000) and a test set (5024)—ensuring that no patients were present in both the training set and the test set.

We compared SVM7 and gradient boosting (XGBoost8 9) because these two algorithms are widely used and have demonstrated strong performance in similar prediction tasks and on similar data from Rigshospitalet.1 Hyperparameters were optimised via grid search (implementation details shown in online supplemental material S3).

Generally, ML methods struggle with addressing model uncertainty. To tackle this issue, this study employed methods from the conformal prediction framework3 10 using a calibration dataset. We chose to implement a calibration dataset with 1000 observations as this is proven to give stable coverage.10

First, the probabilistic model outputs were calibrated using the Venn-ABERS (VA) calibration technique.11 VA calibration aims to adjust the probabilistic model outputs to better match the ground truth. We implemented VA using the python library venn_abers.

In other words, if the model outputs a 42% probability of airway infection, this should accurately reflect the real-world likelihood. VA calibration uses a twofold isotonic regression: both describe the probability of belonging to the class, but one is calculated under the assumption that the observation does belong to the class, while the other is calculated under the assumption that the observation does not belong to the class. This creates a prediction interval (p0, p1) for an observation belonging to the positive class, with a mathematical guarantee that the actual probability lies within this interval.12 This guarantee is however conditioned on the new prediction being in distribution with the calibration set. In practice, the prediction interval will often be narrow; however, when the prediction interval is wide, it is typically indicative of observations that are difficult to classify due to factors such as the observation being out of distribution. For practical decision-making purposes, the adjusted probability outputs are calculated using the two isotonic regression models13 and the formula in equation 1; however, in real-world scenarios with human decision-makers, the ideal set-up would include the prediction interval, such that users will be alerted of observations with high uncertainty.13

In practice, it is desirable to produce a model that can provide uncertainty guarantees. Therefore, this study employed the conformal risk control (CRC) procedure from the conformal prediction framework.14 CRC is a technique used to ensure reliable predictions that contain most of the true labels, while maintaining a user-specified error rate. In other words, CRC aims at creating prediction sets that are both accurate and meaningful. Most classical ML models give one best answer or a predefined top-k answer, including the top-k most likely answers. CRC is similar to the top-k setting in the sense that it can output multiple answers; however, the prediction set size is varied depending on how certain the ML model is, that is, when the model is confident in a prediction, the resulting prediction set is small, and vice versa; when the model is uncertain, the resulting prediction set will be large.

The intuition behind CRC can be described in a handful of steps.10 14 First, the user chooses an acceptable error rate called α. This should be thought of as the maximum error rate that the user can tolerate to accept the ML prediction in a given application. Second, prediction sets for new observations are generated, ideally including the true labels of the observations. The third step is the balancing act, which ensures that the average error of the prediction sets, generated by a suitably chosen loss function, is less than or equal to the chosen error rate. In the context of multilabel classification, as in this study, where each patient can have multiple focuses of infection, the goal is to produce a classifier that can assign accurate probabilities to each focus of infection and then choose thresholds, such that only predictions or focuses of infection with adequately high probability scores are included in the prediction set. This is an important part of the CRC procedure and essentially helps keep the prediction sets small and meaningful, such that only the most likely labels are included in the prediction set.

Intuitively, this also means that the size of the prediction sets varies with the difficulty of the new observation. If a new observation is diagnostically easy, then the resulting prediction sets will be small. However, if a new observation is difficult, then the prediction sets will be comparatively larger. Put differently, the model signals uncertainty in a human-like fashion by generating prediction sets of different sizes based on how certain the model is.

To ensure finite-sample risk control rather than relying on a fixed cut-off, the CRC procedure in this study employed a data-driven calibration step. Specifically, for each label, we solved for the threshold λk using a root-finding algorithm (python: brentq) such that the empirical false negative rate (FNR) on the calibration set satisfies FNR^(λk)≤n+1nα-1n+1 , where n is the size of the calibration set and α is the user-specified error rate. This guarantees that the average sensitivity remains at least 90% under finite-sample conditions. The thresholds are therefore not fixed but adapt to the distribution of the predicted probabilities in the calibration data, ensuring that the resulting prediction sets meet the CRC guarantees.

The CRC framework requires a user-chosen loss function. This study used the FNR as the loss function to control the sensitivity, setting the user-specified error rate at an arbitrarily chosen value α=0.1, which essentially ensures a sensitivity of at least 90% on average. In this study, we applied CRC after the VA calibration, that is, CRC used VA-calibrated probabilities. For a more thorough and technical description of the mathematical implementation of CRC, see online supplemental material S4.

Model evaluation

Models were evaluated using AUC, precision, sensitivity, specificity, negative predictive value, F1 score and multiclass Brier score, reported as micro and macro averages. P values were obtained via 1000 permutation tests to avoid parametric assumptions. For interpretability, we applied SHapley Additive exPlanations15 16 (SHAP) (TreeExplainer) to the best performing model, computing SHAP values on the training set and visualising them with beeswarm plots (details shown in online supplemental material S3).

Patient and public involvement

Patients or members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research. This was a retrospective data study, and therefore patient and public involvement was not appropriate or possible. No plans exist for future involvement.

Results

This study included 10 153 hospitalised patients from both sexes (52.8% female, 47.2% male) and all age groups (paediatric 0–17 years, 9.1%; adult 18–64 years, 37.2%; geriatric 65+ years, 53.7%), and from whom a bacterial isolate was recovered and processed at the Department of Clinical Microbiology (DCM) at Rigshospitalet between November 2019 and June 2023. From these 10 153 patients, 20 037 positive bacterial isolates of a diverse array of species (listed in online supplemental material S7) were processed at the DCM (on average 1.97 isolates per patient) and paired with 30 123 sets of biochemical test results. The resulting dataset therefore contains 30 123 feature vectors X, each paired with a corresponding label set Y={Y1, Y2, …}, generating 30 123 instances (X, Y) in the dataset, hereafter referred to as observations. From these observations, 6138 were related to isolates from the respiratory tract, 2968 to the bloodstream, 13 052 to the urinary tract and 9922 to other body sites. An overview of the label group sizes is outlined in figure 1b, as an UpSet diagram, describing both the absolute number and overlaps in each of the six resulting infection classes.

An overview of the model performance is presented in table 1 (for additional performance-related results, see online supplemental materials S11–S16). The XGBoost model achieved the highest AUC and F1 score (mean±SD) of 0.93±0.007 and 0.75±0.011, respectively. XGBoost outperforms the SVM on nearly all performance metrics. The sensitivity of the naïve XGBoost model was 0.68±0.013 (mean±SD), meaning it correctly predicted the label in 68% of the positive cases. The precision of the XGBoost model was 0.86±0.013, indicating that if the model makes 10 predictions, roughly eight or nine will be correct. Additionally, the XGBoost model had a lower multiclass Brier score (mean±SD) of 0.337±0.017 and a log loss of 0.209±0.006, suggesting better calibration of probabilistic model outputs compared with the SVM. Applying the VA calibration substantially improved the F1 score for both models, indicating a better overall balance between precision and sensitivity. Using the CRC method achieved the desired sensitivity of at least 90%, but this increased sensitivity came with a trade-off in precision and specificity.

The improved performance with VA calibration is also evident from the calibration curves in figure 2. The CRC method mathematically signals uncertainty in a manner like human intuition. Figure 3 displays the prediction set sizes at different levels of α. When α=0.01, corresponding to a sensitivity of at least 99% on average, the model includes a larger proportion of the label space in the prediction set. Higher levels of α result in smaller prediction sets with a larger risk of missing the true class. At α=0.3 corresponding to a sensitivity of at least 70% on average, the median prediction set size is 1. It is also evident that the CRC method can produce empty prediction sets when the predicted probability of each class is below the probability threshold, indicating lack of confidence in predicting a class label.

Here, large user-tolerated error rates result in bolder or overconfident prediction sets, while small user-tolerated error rates result in large prediction sets.

Discussion

This study aimed to develop a probabilistic model that predicts infection focus and accounts for uncertainty. XGBoost performed best (AUC=0.93, log loss=0.209), with similar performance across classes (AUC: airways=0.94, BSI=0.93, UTI=0.93) and balanced sensitivity/specificity (F1 score=0.78). While AUC and F1 are common, they can be misleading.19 20 For example, a model can achieve high AUC while still producing poorly calibrated or overconfident probability estimates and can therefore not be used as sole indicators of performance. Metrics like log loss and Brier score better reflect probabilistic reliability, penalising overconfident errors and measuring calibration.

The XGBoost model outperforms the SVM when compared on log loss and Brier score. This is expected and may partly be attributed to multicollinearity in the dataset. Unlike tree-based methods, SVMs can suffer from reduced stability and degraded generalisation when predictors are highly correlated. The naïve model state did however prove to be badly calibrated; nevertheless, using VA calibration, a reasonably well-calibrated model with a multiclass Brier score of 0.337 and a log loss of 0.197 was obtained. Applying CRC in this setting guarantees a sensitivity of at least 90% on average, meaning that in nine out of 10 cases, the correct prediction is included in the prediction set, while also not creating excessively large prediction sets. Calibration improves performance but causes a trade-off in competing evaluation metrics.

Adopting ML to clinical practices requires a high level of trustworthy artificial intelligence21 (AI). In a clinical setting, clinicians and diagnosticians are responsible for human lives. For the clinicians and diagnosticians to trust the predictions made by AI and ML models, the models need to be built with transparency, robustness and a high level of explainability.21 To achieve this, one should address known biases in the dataset and potential strengths and weaknesses of the ML models as well as provide explanations for the model outputs.

The dataset in this study comes with a handful of natural biases. Our dataset reflects biases from a tertiary hospital setting, where patients often have multiple comorbidities, making diagnostics increasingly difficult. Future work could benefit from the inclusion of a non-infected patient group. The input features were transformed to resemble the Gaussian distribution, since this is a common choice for input features. However, we acknowledge that an ablation study assessing the impact of this transformation would provide further insight. Similarly, an ablation study could be performed to test the effects of simplistic imputation methods versus more advanced techniques. The ML labels used in this study could also introduce biases. The labels are generated using the sample origin and the bacterial species. However, this combination is likely not perfectly true to reality and could potentially introduce a level of epistemic errors into the dataset. To increase trustworthiness and diagnostic value of the ML models, the labels should be redefined using other more strict definitions of contamination. Ideally, labels should be generated using information from patient journals in combination with the clinician’s decision to administer treatment. Access to patient journals would also reduce epistemic errors and potentially improve model performance and clinical applicability by adding information about patients’ symptoms and comorbidities. This would also allow future work to expand on performance in demographic subgroups, which would further improve trustworthiness.

The naïve models proved to be badly calibrated. This issue was handled using VA calibration. Badly calibrated models could potentially decrease the trustworthiness of the model and ML in general, as users empirically will start to doubt the ML models if it consistently outputs either lower or higher chance of bacterial infection than what the user observes.

The usage of tools in this study is chosen with clinical application in mind. Specifically, the usage of conformal predictions to generate statistically robust prediction sets is of potentially high value in a clinical setting. Many ML methods do not provide uncertainty estimates for their predictions. Applying the CRC method generates prediction sets of varying size, as seen in figure 3. This is clinically useful as the framework provides statistically sound uncertainty guarantees for the user-specified performance metrics and allows the ML models to generate prediction sets of varying size, which essentially signals uncertainty in a human-like fashion and is known to significantly improve accuracy in human-in-the-loop systems,22 where humans make decisions based on expert knowledge and ML outputs.

Prediction sets can be a great way of signalling uncertainty, but the size of the prediction sets needs to be discussed. For example, using CRC, it is possible to guarantee a certain level of sensitivity.

Controlling the sensitivity to 99% for the XGBoost model results, by definition, in few prediction sets where the correct label is excluded. This is however achieved by outputting half the label space for median observations. In practice, large prediction sets do not provide any direct diagnostic value to the clinicians or diagnosticians; it does however indicate high uncertainty, which warrants further examination of the patient. Reducing the sensitivity to 70% mostly resulted in prediction set sizes of 2 or less. This size is potentially more useful as it significantly narrows the potential search field. The issue with this setting is that the model essentially guarantees an error rate of approximately 30%. When using CRC in practice, it is therefore important to find the right balance between prediction set size and error rate, such that the prediction sets are narrow enough that clinicians and diagnosticians can extract the actual value from them but still wide enough to provide a reasonably low error rate. In a clinical workflow, we envision that CRC controls the prediction set presented to the decision-maker, while each candidate label is accompanied by a VA-calibrated uncertainty estimate. This combination allows clinicians to assess both the range of plausible diagnoses and the model’s confidence in each, facilitating informed decision-making and further diagnostic evaluation when uncertainty is high.

SHAP analysis confirmed clinically plausible patterns: O2 therapy and blood oxygen saturation for respiratory infections, LEU count and HB concentration for BSI, and sex for UTI. The inverse relationship between CRP levels and UTI prediction, as well as the positive association with ALB levels, may reflect the model’s ability to distinguish UTIs from more severe systemic infections. The partial overlap of features across infection categories likely reflects shared inflammatory and sampling characteristics consistent with a biological continuum between colonisation, contamination and infection. This nuanced interpretation of biomarkers demonstrates the ML model’s sophistication in differentiating between infection types and highlights that SHAP values describe feature contributions to predictions rather than diagnostic specificity, requiring integration with clinical context in practice.

SHAP analysis assumes feature independence; this is almost never achievable in real-life applications, and the SHAP analysis should therefore be interpreted with a pinch of scepticism15 (see online supplemental material S9 for feature correlation). From the SHAP analysis, it is also clear that the dataset contains human-introduced biases, such as O2 therapy, which is deemed as the most important feature when predicting airway infections. This feature is controlled by the diagnosticians and is therefore naturally biased towards the human decision-maker. Including O2 therapy could introduce a treatment-related effect and potentially inflate performance. However, we chose to retain this feature because clinicians typically have access to this information at the time of decision-making. Additionally, O2 therapy may serve as a proxy for certain symptoms or severity indicators that we were unable to include directly due to data limitations. Nevertheless, the majority of the features deemed important by the model are expected and known biomarkers of bacterial infection (age,23 lactate dehydrogenase,24 LEU,17 temperature,18 sex and CRP). To accommodate the known problems of SHAP analysis and improve interpretability, future work could explore feature selection or dimensionality reduction.

Our study has several limitations. The model was not validated on an external dataset from a different hospital or time period, which would have provided stronger evidence of its generalisability. The low prevalence of positive blood cultures in the dataset may affect the model’s performance, particularly its positive predictive value. Additionally, the model does not incorporate important clinical information such as comorbidities or presence of indwelling devices, which are known risk factors for BSI.

The integration of trustworthy AI principles, addressing model uncertainty and providing explainable results, represents a significant step towards clinical application of ML in infection diagnosis, which is crucial for advancing the field of ML in clinical microbiology.

The ability to accurately predict the site of infection during early admission may have direct implications for antibiotic stewardship and patient safety. For instance, if the model predicts a high probability of a UTI but a low probability of a BSI, clinicians may choose targeted oral or narrow-spectrum intravenous antibiotics rather than broad-spectrum therapy. This directly reduces the selective pressure for multidrug-resistant organisms, which may decrease resistance.

Furthermore, precise infection site prediction can guide the rational use of diagnostics: which imaging to perform and which diagnostic tests to order. Thus, diagnostics can be prioritised avoiding unnecessary and costly analyses.

It is becoming evident that patient outcome is not just about survival, but about toxicity and recovery. Earlier use of narrow-spectrum drugs reduces the risk of side effects and preserves the gut microbiome,25 reducing the incidence of healthcare-associated infections. Furthermore, for sites like the bloodstream, every hour of delay in appropriate therapy is associated with an increase in mortality.26

The integration of ML into the clinical workflow represents a shift towards proactive rather than reactive diagnostics. By providing early decision support to physicians or triage staff, our model may address the ‘information gap’ during the first 24–72 hours of hospital stay. The model is designed to be used at a point where recent laboratory results are available, but before more resource-intensive diagnostic procedures. Specifically, the model can flag patients who likely have a specific infection focus, such as pneumonia, prompting clinicians to consider initiating targeted therapy and apply appropriate diagnostic tests.

Conclusion

The XGBoost model achieved high performance (F1 score=0.78, AUC=0.93, log loss=0.209, MBS=0.337) in predicting the focus of bacterial infections using electronic health record data and biochemical markers. VA calibration and CRC significantly improved model calibration and guaranteed a sensitivity of at least 90% on average. The model identified clinically relevant predictors for different infection types, such as O2 therapy for respiratory infections and LEU count for BSI. Conformal prediction methods can generate variable size prediction sets, signalling uncertainty in a human-like fashion and potentially improving clinical decision-making. This data-driven ML approach of combining classical ML models like XGBoost with conformal prediction methods provides valuable insights and methodological approaches for developing trustworthy AI systems in clinical microbiology. These findings suggest a significant potential for improving antibiotic stewardship and patient outcomes in the management of bacterial infections.
