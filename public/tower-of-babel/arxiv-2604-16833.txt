1.
Introduction

Let
\mathcal{H}
denote the class of functions analytic in the open unit disk

\mathbb{D}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<1\}.

We write
\mathcal{A}
for the normalized subclass of
\mathcal{H}
consisting of functions
f
satisfying

f(0)=0

and

f^{\prime}(0)=1.

Accordingly, each function
f\in\mathcal{A}
admits the expansion

f(z)=z+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n},\qquad z\in\mathbb{D}.

Let
\mathcal{S}
denote the family of all univalent functions in
\mathcal{A}
. This class occupies a central position in geometric function theory, since several major subclasses, including the starlike and convex families, are defined through geometric properties of the image domain.

A domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
is called starlike with respect to a point
\zeta_{0}\in\Omega
if, for every
\zeta\in\Omega
, the whole line segment joining
\zeta_{0}
to
\zeta
is contained in
\Omega
. That is, equivalently,

(1-s)\zeta_{0}+s\zeta\in\Omega\qquad\text{for all }\zeta\in\Omega\text{ and all }s\in[0,1].

A function
f\in\mathcal{S}
is said to be in the class
\mathcal{S}^{*}
whenever the image domain
f(\mathbb{D})
is starlike with respect to the origin. In analytic terms, this is equivalent to

f\in\mathcal{S}^{*}\quad\text{if, and only if,}\quad\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{\zeta f^{\prime}(\zeta)}{f(\zeta)}\right)>0,\qquad\zeta\in\mathbb{D}.

A domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
is said to be convex if, whenever
\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}\in\Omega
, the entire line segment connecting these two points remains inside
\Omega
. In other words,

(1-s)\zeta_{1}+s\zeta_{2}\in\Omega\qquad\text{for all }\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}\in\Omega\text{ and all }s\in[0,1].

A function
f\in\mathcal{S}
is called convex in
\mathbb{D}
whenever the image domain
f(\mathbb{D})
is convex. This class is denoted by
\mathcal{C}
, and it admits the analytic characterization

f\in\mathcal{C}\quad\text{if, and only if,}\quad\operatorname{Re}\left(1+\frac{\zeta f^{\prime\prime}(\zeta)}{f^{\prime}(\zeta)}\right)>0,\qquad\zeta\in\mathbb{D}.

The classes
\mathcal{S}^{*}
and
\mathcal{C}
have been studied intensively and form a basic part of the modern theory of univalent functions; see Goodman
[
2
]
.

An important unifying generalization of these classes was formulated by Ma and Minda
[
5
]
. Let
\varphi
be analytic in
\mathbb{D}
and satisfy

\operatorname{Re}\varphi(\zeta)>0

for
\zeta\in\mathbb{D},

together with the normalization conditions

\varphi(0)=1,\varphi^{\prime}(0)>0.

Assume also that the image region
\varphi(\mathbb{D})
is symmetric with respect to the real axis and starlike with respect to the point
1
. Then the corresponding Ma–Minda convex class is given by

\mathcal{C}(\varphi):=\left\{f\in\mathcal{A}:1+\frac{\zeta f^{\prime\prime}(\zeta)}{f^{\prime}(\zeta)}\prec\varphi(\zeta),\ \zeta\in\mathbb{D}\right\}.

This formulation provides a common setting for many previously studied subclasses and has proved useful in coefficient questions, extremal problems, inclusion relations, and radius problems; (see
[
5
]
).

Coefficient functionals also form an important part of the theory of analytic and univalent functions. Among them, Hankel determinants, introduced by Pommerenke
[
6
]
, have been studied extensively for many subclasses of analytic functions; for example, (see
[
3
,
4
,
7
]
). In the present work, we investigate the second and third Hankel determinants for the class
\mathcal{C}(\varphi)
, and their precise definitions together with the required auxiliary results are recalled in Section 3.

In the present paper we study the Ma–Minda convex class determined by

\varphi(z)=1+z+m/n\,\,z^{2},

where
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
and
2m\leq n
. After establishing that this choice of
\varphi
satisfies the required Ma–Minda conditions under the assumption
2m\leq n
, we derive sharp estimates for the second Hankel determinant
H_{2}(2)
and the third Hankel determinant
H_{3}(1)
for functions in
\mathcal{C}(\varphi)
. We also identify the corresponding extremal functions.

2.
Certain properties of the function
\varphi(z)

First, we show that the function satisfies all the properties of a Ma-Minda function. It is easy to see that
\varphi(0)=1
and
\varphi^{\prime}(0)=1.
Furthermore,
\varphi(z)
is symmetric with respect to the real axis.

Next, we show that
\varphi(z)
is univalent in the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
whenever
2m\leq n.

Taking,

a={m}/{n}>0,

then

\varphi(z)=1+z+az^{2}.

To determine when
\varphi
is univalent in the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
. Let
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{D}
. We compute

\varphi(z_{1})-\varphi(z_{2})=(z_{1}-z_{2})+a(z_{1}^{2}-z_{2}^{2})=(z_{1}-z_{2})\bigl(1+a(z_{1}+z_{2})\bigr).

If
\varphi(z_{1})=\varphi(z_{2})
and
z_{1}\neq z_{2}
, then, we have

1+a(z_{1}+z_{2})=0.

Thus, we have

1+a(z_{1}+z_{2})=0,

that is,

z_{1}+z_{2}=-\frac{1}{a}.

Thus
\varphi
fails to be univalent precisely when there exist distinct points

z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{D}
such that

z_{1}+z_{2}=-1/a.

Now, for
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{D}
, we have

|z_{1}+z_{2}|\leq|z_{1}|+|z_{2}|<2.

Hence, if such
z_{1},z_{2}
exist, then necessarily

\left|-\frac{1}{a}\right|<2,

or equivalently,

1/a<2,

which implies

a>1/2.

Therefore, if
a\leq 1/2
, then no such pair
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{D}
can exist, and therefore

\varphi
is univalent in
\mathbb{D}
.
Conversely, suppose
a>1/2
. Then

\frac{1}{a}<2,\qquad\text{so}\qquad\left|-\frac{1}{a}\right|<2.

Let

c=-{1}/{2a}.

Then
|c|<1
. Choose
\varepsilon>0
small enough so that

z_{1}=c+\varepsilon,\qquad z_{2}=c-\varepsilon

both belong to
\mathbb{D}
. Clearly
z_{1}\neq z_{2}
, and

z_{1}+z_{2}=2c=-\frac{1}{a}.

Hence

1+a(z_{1}+z_{2})=1+a\left(-\frac{1}{a}\right)=0,

and therefore

\varphi(z_{1})=\varphi(z_{2}).

So
\varphi
is not univalent in
\mathbb{D}
.
We conclude that
\varphi
is univalent in
\mathbb{D}
if, and only if,

a\leq 1/2.

Since
a={m}/{n}
, this is equivalent to

{m}/{n}\leq 1/2,

that is,

2m\leq n.

Next, we prove that the function
\varphi(z)
is starlike with respect to the point
\varphi(0)=1
given that
2m\leq n.

Taking,

a={m}/{n}>0.

then, we may write
\varphi(z)
as:

\varphi(z)=1+z+az^{2}.

The function
\varphi
is starlike with respect to
\varphi(0)=1
if, and only if,

g(z):=\varphi(z)-1=z+az^{2}

is starlike with respect to the origin.
Now,

g^{\prime}(z)=1+2az,

and therefore

\displaystyle\frac{zg^{\prime}(z)}{g(z)}=\frac{z(1+2az)}{z(1+az)}=\frac{1+2az}{1+az}.

By the standard characterization of starlike functions,
g
is starlike in
\mathbb{D}
if, and only if,

\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{zg^{\prime}(z)}{g(z)}\right)>0,\qquad z\in\mathbb{D},

that is,

\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{1+2az}{1+az}\right)>0,\qquad z\in\mathbb{D}.

First, we suppose that

a\leq 1/2,

let
w=az
. Then
|w|<a\leq 1/2
, and

\frac{1+2az}{1+az}=\frac{1+2w}{1+w}=1+\frac{w}{1+w}.

Also,

\left|\frac{w}{1+w}\right|\leq\frac{|w|}{1-|w|}<1.

Hence

\operatorname{Re}\left(1+\frac{w}{1+w}\right)>0,

which gives

\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{1+2az}{1+az}\right)>0,\qquad z\in\mathbb{D}.

Thus
g
is starlike in
\mathbb{D}
, and so
\varphi
is starlike with respect to
1
.
Conversely, suppose that

a>1/2.

Choose
r
such that

\frac{1}{2a}<r<\min\left\{1,\frac{1}{a}\right\},

and set
z=-r
. Then
z\in\mathbb{D}
, and

\frac{1+2az}{1+az}=\frac{1-2ar}{1-ar}.

Since
r>{1}/{2a}
, we have
1-2ar<0
, and since
r<1/a
, we have
1-ar>0
. Therefore

\frac{1-2ar}{1-ar}<0.

Hence,

\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{1+2az}{1+az}\right)<0

for this choice of
z
, so
g
is not starlike in
\mathbb{D}
. Consequently,
\varphi
is not starlike with respect to
1
.
Therefore
\varphi
is starlike with respect to
\varphi(0)=1
if, and only if,

a\leq 1/2.

Since
a={m}/{n}
, we get
2m\leq n.

Now, we prove that the
\operatorname{Re}\varphi(z)
remains positive in the unit disk
\mathbb{D}.

Again taking

a={m}/{n}>0.

Then

\varphi(z)=1+z+az^{2}.

We want to determine for which
a>0
, we have

\operatorname{Re}\varphi(z)>0
in
\mathbb{D}.

Consider the harmonic function

u(z)=\operatorname{Re}\varphi(z).

Since
u
is harmonic in
\mathbb{D}
and continuous on
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, it is enough to study its boundary values on
|z|=1
.
Let
z=e^{it}
, where
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Then

u(e^{it})=\operatorname{Re}\,\bigl(1+e^{it}+ae^{2it}\bigr)=1+\cos t+a\cos 2t.

Taking

x=\cos t\in[-1,1],

we obtain

u(e^{it})=1+x+a(2x^{2}-1)=2ax^{2}+x+1-a.

Thus it suffices to determine when the quadratic polynomial

q(x)=2ax^{2}+x+1-a

is non-negative for all
x\in[-1,1]
.
Now, taking the derivative of
q(x)
with respect to
x,

q^{\prime}(x)=4ax+1.

Hence the critical point is

x_{0}=-{1}/{4a}.

Thus, we discuss the following two cases.

Case 1: If
0<a\leq 1/4
, then

x_{0}=-{1}/{4a}\leq-1.

Since
q(x)
is convex, its minimum on
[-1,1]
occurs at
x=-1
. Therefore

\min_{x\in[-1,1]}q(x)=q(-1)=2a-1+1-a=a>0.

Hence

u(e^{it})>0
for

|z|=1
.

Case 2: Now suppose
a>1/4
, then

x_{0}=-\frac{1}{4a}\in(-1,0),

so the minimum of
q
on
[-1,1]
occurs at
x=x_{0}
. A simple computation gives

q\!\left(-\frac{1}{4a}\right)=2a\left(\frac{1}{16a^{2}}\right)-\frac{1}{4a}+1-a=1-a-\frac{1}{8a}.

Thus
q(x)\geq 0
on
[-1,1]
if, and only if,

1-a-\frac{1}{8a}\geq 0.

Since
a>0
, this is equivalent to

8a-8a^{2}-1\geq 0,

The roots of the quadratic equation

8a^{2}-8a+1=0

are

a=\frac{2\pm\sqrt{2}}{4}.

Therefore,

8a^{2}-8a+1\leq 0,

Thus, we have

\frac{2-\sqrt{2}}{4}\leq a\leq\frac{2+\sqrt{2}}{4}.

Since in the present case
a>1/4
, only the upper bound is relevant, and we obtain

a\leq\frac{2+\sqrt{2}}{4}.

Combining the two cases, we conclude that

u(e^{it})\geq 0
for
|z|=1

if, and only if,

0<a\leq\frac{2+\sqrt{2}}{4}.

Because
u
is harmonic and not identically zero, the boundary condition
u\geq 0
implies

u(z)>0

in
\mathbb{D}.

Hence,

\operatorname{Re}\varphi(z)>0

if, and only if,

a\leq\frac{2+\sqrt{2}}{4}.

Since
a={m}/{n}
, this is equivalent to

\frac{m}{n}\leq\frac{2+\sqrt{2}}{4}.

Therefore, from the preceding observations, we conclude that
\varphi
satisfies all the Ma–Minda conditions whenever

2m\leq n,

for
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence, we discuss the properties of the class
\mathcal{C}(\varphi),
when
2m\leq n,
where
m,n\in\mathbb{N}.

3.
Hankel determinants

The notion of Hankel determinants for functions in the class
\mathcal{S}
was introduced by Pommerenke
[
6
]
. If
f\in\mathcal{A}
has the Taylor expansion

f(z)=z+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n},

then the
q
th Hankel determinant of
f
is given by

H_{q}(n)=\begin{vmatrix}a_{n}&a_{n+1}&\cdots&a_{n+q-1}\\
a_{n+1}&a_{n+2}&\cdots&a_{n+q}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{n+q-1}&a_{n+q}&\cdots&a_{n+2q-2}\end{vmatrix},\qquad q,n\in\mathbb{N}.

(3.1)

For particular choices of
n
and
q
, this determinant reduces to several well-known coefficient functionals. For example, the second Hankel determinant is defined as

\displaystyle H_{2}(2)=a_{2}a_{4}-a_{3}^{\,2}.

(3.2)

Since functions in the class
\mathcal{A}
are normalized, we adopt the convention
a_{1}:=1
. Thus the third Hankel determinant is

H_{3}(1)=a_{3}(a_{2}a_{4}-a_{3}^{2})-a_{4}(a_{4}-a_{2}a_{3})+a_{5}(a_{3}-a_{2}^{2}).

(3.3)

Let
\mathcal{P}
denote the class of analytic functions
p
in
\mathbb{D}
of the form

p(z)=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}p_{n}z^{n}

(3.4)

with
\operatorname{Re}p(z)>0
in
\mathbb{D}
. This class is known as Carathéodory class.
We recall the following well-known result of Choi
et al.

[
1
]
, which is essential for the proof of our main theorem.

Lemma 3.5
.

[
1
]

Let
A,B,C\in\mathbb{R}
and define

Y(A,B,C):=\max_{z\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}}\Bigl(|A+Bz+Cz^{2}|+1-|z|^{2}\Bigr).

(i)

If
AC\geq 0
, then

Y(A,B,C)=\begin{cases}|A|+|B|+|C|,&\text{if }|B|\geq 2(1-|C|),\\[6.0pt]
1+|A|+\dfrac{B^{2}}{4(1-|C|)},&\text{if }|B|<2(1-|C|).\end{cases}

(ii)

If
AC<0
, then

Y(A,B,C)=\begin{cases}1-|A|+\dfrac{B^{2}}{4(1-|C|)},&\text{if }-4AC(C^{2}-1)\leq B^{2}\ \text{and }|B|<2(1-|C|),\\[8.0pt]
1+|A|+\dfrac{B^{2}}{4(1+|C|)},&\text{if }B^{2}<\min\!\bigl\{\,4(1+|C|)^{2},-4AC(C^{2}-1)\bigr\},\\[8.0pt]
R(A,B,C),&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

where

R(A,B,C)=\begin{cases}|A|+|B|+|C|,&\text{if }|C|\bigl(|B|+4|A|\bigr)\leq|AB|,\\[6.0pt]
-|A|+|B|+|C|,&\text{if }|AB|\leq|C|\bigl(|B|-4|A|\bigr),\\[10.0pt]
\bigl(|A|+|C|\bigr)\sqrt{\,1-\dfrac{B^{2}}{4AC}\,},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

We shall also use the following coefficient representation of Schwarz function. The next well-known lemma by Prokhorov and Szynal
[
7
]
will be crucial for our result.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let

w(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}z^{n}

be a Schwarz function, that is,
w
is analytic in
\mathbb{D}
,
w(0)=0
, and
|w(z)|<1
for all
z\in\mathbb{D}
. If
c_{1}\geq 0
, then there exist complex numbers
\gamma,\eta,\rho
with

|\gamma|\leq 1,\ |\eta|\leq 1,\ |\rho|\leq 1,

such that

\displaystyle c_{2}

\displaystyle=(1-c_{1}^{2})\gamma,

\displaystyle c_{3}

\displaystyle=(1-c_{1}^{2})\bigl(\eta(1-|\gamma|^{2})-c_{1}\gamma^{2}\bigr),

\displaystyle c_{4}

\displaystyle=(1-c_{1}^{2})\Bigl(c_{1}^{2}\gamma^{3}-(1-|\gamma|^{2})\bigl(2c_{1}\gamma\eta+\overline{\gamma}\eta^{2}\bigr)+(1-|\gamma|^{2})(1-|\eta|^{2})\rho\Bigr).

Theorem 3.1
.

Let

f\in\mathcal{C}(\varphi).

Then the second Hankel determinant satisfies

|H_{2}(2)|=|a_{2}a_{4}-a_{3}^{2}|\leq\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{36},&0\leq t\leq\dfrac{1}{4},\\[5.69054pt]
\dfrac{1}{144}\left(4+\dfrac{(4t-1)^{2}}{8+20t-16t^{2}}\right),&\dfrac{1}{4}\leq t\leq\dfrac{1}{2}.\end{cases}

Moreover, the estimate is sharp.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathcal{C}(\varphi)
be given by

\displaystyle 1+z\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(z)}{f^{\prime}(z)}\prec\varphi(z),\quad z\in\mathbb{D}.

(3.7)

By the subordination relation, there exists a Schwarz function

w(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}z^{n}=c_{1}z+c_{2}z^{2}+c_{3}z^{3}+c_{4}z^{4}+\cdots

such that

\displaystyle 1+\frac{zf^{\prime\prime}(z)}{f^{\prime}(z)}=1+w(z)+t\,w(z)^{2}.

(3.8)

Since
f\in\mathcal{A}
, we have

f(z)=z+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}

Writing (
3.8
) in series form and comparing coefficients, we obtain

a_{2}=\frac{c_{1}}{2},\qquad a_{3}=\frac{(1+t)c_{1}^{2}+c_{2}}{6},\qquad a_{4}=\frac{(1+3t)c_{1}^{3}+(3+4t)c_{1}c_{2}+2c_{3}}{24}.

Substituting these into (
3.2
), we obtain

\displaystyle H_{2}(2)

\displaystyle=a_{2}a_{4}-a_{3}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(4t+1)c_{1}^{2}c_{2}+6c_{1}c_{3}-4c_{2}^{2}-(4t^{2}-t+1)c_{1}^{4}}{144}.

(3.9)

Now, since the class
\mathcal{C}(\varphi)
is invariant under rotations and
|H_{2}(2)|
is rotation-invariant, we may assume without loss of generality that
c_{1}\geq 0
. Write

c_{1}=x,\qquad 0\leq x\leq 1.

Using Lemma
3.6
in (
3
), we obtain

\displaystyle 144\,H_{2}(2)

\displaystyle=-(4t^{2}-t+1)x^{4}+(4t+1)x^{2}(1-x^{2})\gamma

\displaystyle\quad-2(1-x^{2})(2+x^{2})\gamma^{2}+6x(1-x^{2})(1-|\gamma|^{2})\eta.

(3.10)

Taking absolute values in (
3
), and setting
y=|\gamma|
, we get

\displaystyle 144\,|H_{2}(2)|

\displaystyle\leq(4t^{2}-t+1)x^{4}+(4t+1)x^{2}(1-x^{2})y

\displaystyle\quad+2(1-x^{2})(2+x^{2})y^{2}+6x(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2}).

(3.11)

We now simplify the last two terms:

\displaystyle 2(1-x^{2})(2+x^{2})y^{2}+6x(1-x^{2})(1-y^{2})

\displaystyle=(1-x^{2})\bigl(2(2+x^{2})y^{2}+6x(1-y^{2})\bigr)

\displaystyle=(1-x^{2})\bigl(6x+(4+2x^{2}-6x)y^{2}\bigr).

Hence (
3
) becomes

\displaystyle 144\,|H_{2}(2)|

\displaystyle\leq(4t^{2}-t+1)x^{4}+(4t+1)x^{2}(1-x^{2})y

\displaystyle\quad+(1-x^{2})(4+2x^{2}-6x)y^{2}+6x(1-x^{2}).

For fixed
x
and
t
, define

\displaystyle F_{1}(y)

\displaystyle:=(4t^{2}-t+1)x^{4}+(4t+1)x^{2}(1-x^{2})y

\displaystyle\quad+(1-x^{2})(4+2x^{2}-6x)y^{2}+6x(1-x^{2}),

(3.12)

where
0\leq y\leq 1
. Since

4+2x^{2}-6x=2(1-x)(2-x)\geq 0\qquad(0\leq x\leq 1),

the coefficients of
y
and
y^{2}
in
F_{1}(y)
are non-negative. Indeed,

(4t+1)x^{2}(1-x^{2})\geq 0,\qquad(1-x^{2})(4+2x^{2}-6x)\geq 0.

Therefore

F_{1}^{\prime}(y)=(4t+1)x^{2}(1-x^{2})+2(1-x^{2})(4+2x^{2}-6x)y\geq 0

for all
y\in[0,1]
. Hence
F_{1}
is increasing on
[0,1]
, and so its maximum occurs at
y=1
. Consequently,

\displaystyle 144\,|H_{2}(2)|

\displaystyle\leq F_{1}(1)

\displaystyle=(4t^{2}-t+1)x^{4}+(4t+1)x^{2}(1-x^{2})

\displaystyle\quad+(1-x^{2})(4+2x^{2}-6x)+6x(1-x^{2}).

Combining the last two terms, we get

(1-x^{2})(4+2x^{2}-6x)+6x(1-x^{2})=2(1-x^{2})(2+x^{2}).

Thus

\displaystyle 144\,|H_{2}(2)|

\displaystyle\leq(4t^{2}-t+1)x^{4}+(4t+1)x^{2}(1-x^{2})+2(1-x^{2})(2+x^{2})

\displaystyle=4+(4t-1)x^{2}+(4t^{2}-5t-2)x^{4}.

Taking,

s=x^{2},\,0\leq s\leq 1,

we obtain

144\,|H_{2}(2)|\leq\psi_{t}(s),\qquad\psi_{t}(s):=4+(4t-1)s+(4t^{2}-5t-2)s^{2}.

Since

4t^{2}-5t-2<0\quad\text{ for }\quad 0\leq t\leq\frac{1}{2},

the function
\psi_{t}
is concave on
[0,1]
.

If
0\leq t\leq 1/4
, then
4t-1\leq 0
, and therefore the maximum of
\psi_{t}
on
[0,1]
is attained at
s=0
. Thus

\max_{0\leq s\leq 1}\psi_{t}(s)=4,

which gives

|H_{2}(2)|\leq\frac{4}{144}=\frac{1}{36}.

If
1/4\leq t\leq 1/2
, then the vertex of
\psi_{t}
is

s_{0}=\frac{4t-1}{4+10t-8t^{2}}.

Since
4t-1\geq 0
and
4+10t-8t^{2}>0
for
1/4\leq t\leq 1/2
, we have

. Also,
