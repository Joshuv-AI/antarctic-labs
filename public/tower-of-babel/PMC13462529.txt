Aridity-related differences in soil elemental ratios reshape microbial functional traits across global biomes
Authors: Chunkai Li, Youzhi Feng, Tadeo Sáez-Sandino, Chao Xiong, David J Eldridge, Nicolas Gross, Yoann Le Bagousse-Pinguet, Victoria Ochoa, Beatriz Gozalo, Emilio Guirado, Guiyao Zhou, Miguel García-Gómez, Enrique Valencia, Miguel Berdugo, Sergio Asensio, Jaime Martínez-Valderrama, Betty Josefina Mendoza, Asmeret A Berhe, Nick A Cutler, Sebastian Abades, Julio Alcántara, Fernando Alfaro, Antonio I Arroyo, Matt Barrett, Felipe Bastida, Niels Blaum, Bazartseren Boldgiv, Matthew Bowker, Cristina Branquinho, Stephen C Hart, Balázs Deák, Jorge Durán, Carlos Ivan Espinosa, Alex Fajardo, Lauchlan H Fraser, Antonio Gallardo, Laura García Velázquez, Katja Geissler, Tine Grebenc, Elizabeth Gusman Moltanvan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAridity alters soil carbon (C), nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P) stoichiometry, yet the implications of these processes for soil microbial functional traits and potentials at the genomic level remain poorly synthesized. Here we combine measurements of soil C, N and P pools and ratios with shotgun metagenomes from 200 natural ecosystems spanning major biomes worldwide. Across sites, increased aridity is associated with lower soil C:N and N:P (and C:P) ratios and with a coordinated shift in microbial functional potential. Genes linked to catabolic resource acquisition—including carbohydrate-active enzymes and pathways for degradation of plant litter and organophosphorus compounds—are declined as C becomes relatively scarce. In contrast, genes supporting anabolic investment in growth and drought resistance, such as RNA transcription, protein synthesis and intracellular transport, are increased. These patterns indicate that aridity-related change in soil elemental ratios is coupled to a broad shift from catabolic to anabolic strategies in soil microbiomes. By linking soil elemental ratios to microbial functional traits across biomes, our study provides a framework for anticipating how climate-driven drying may reorganize microbial metabolism with consequences for carbon and nutrient cycling.Subject terms: Climate-change ecology, Climate-change ecology, Microbial ecology

Introduction

Increasing aridity alters soil elemental stoichiometry worldwide, with consequences for the content and ratios of soil organic carbon (C), nitrogen (N), and phosphorus (P) (e.g., C:N, N:P, and C:P)1. As ecosystems become drier, biologically cycled elements such as C and N often decline (via reduced primary production, biological N fixation and organic matter turnover), whereas rock-derived P is comparatively less sensitive, leading to lower soil C:P and N:P ratios2,3. Because soil microbes have to maintain cellular elemental homeostasis and mediate key biogeochemical processes, these shifts in soil elemental ratios are expected to be tightly linked to microbial functional strategies4. Although soil microbes are among the most diverse and abundant organisms on the planet and provide essential ecosystem services that are crucial for human well-being (e.g., food security and climate action)5,6, few studies focus on how aridity-associated differences in soil elemental ratios relate to microbial functional traits at the global scale7.

Two challenges contribute to the current uncertainties regarding how climatic-related differences in soil elemental ratios influence soil microbial functional traits. First, there is a lack of across-biome datasets that simultaneously quantify soil C, N and P stocks and pools together with high-resolution functional genomic information (for example, shotgun metagenomes). As a result, regional studies often report context-dependent patterns that are difficult to reconcile because they are confounded by vegetation type, edaphic conditions and/or microbial activity8,9. Therefore, a large-scale across-biome approach is essential to evaluate how aridity-associated differences in soil elements relate to the functional soil microbiome10. Second, trait-based microbial ecology predicts trade-offs between catabolism (resource acquisition through extracellular degradation) and anabolism (biosynthesis, growth and stress tolerance)11,12. For example, the regional work suggests that progressive P limitation during forest succession can increase genes involved in the degradation of structurally complex compounds to access organic P and/or to fuel acquisition of inorganic P13,14. Yet, how climate-associated differences in soil elements and their ratios reshape the functional soil microbiome across global biomes remains poorly synthesized. Filling this gap is important for anticipating how climate change may reorganize soil microbiomes and ecosystem functions they underpin15.

Here we assemble an across-biome topsoil survey spanning 200 terrestrial ecosystems across major global biomes, from deserts to tropical regions, and across diverse vegetation types including forests, grasslands and shrublands (Fig. 1a, b). Our objective is to investigate how differences in soil elemental ratios along aridity gradients and multiple biomes influence soil functional microbiome. To achieve this, we integrate measurements of soil C stocks and multiple C, N and P pools and their ratios with shotgun metagenomic sequencing to quantify microbial metabolic potential across sites. This metagenomic approach enables us to characterize microbial metabolism—both anabolic and catabolic processes—by analyzing key functional genes and metabolic pathways (a detailed list of genes involved in each metabolism is available in Supplementary Data 1). For intuitively assessing aridity, water availability (that is the term aridity index16, which is derived by dividing mean annual precipitation (MAP) by potential evapotranspiration) is calculated, given its capacity to account for both the natural input of water into the ecosystem and potential losses resulting from evapotranspiration. This calculation is widely accepted to link aridity and water availability17,18. We first identify environmental correlates of soil C:N:P ratios and test for nonlinearities and thresholds along aridity and productivity gradients. We then ask which anabolic and catabolic genes and pathways covary most strongly with soil elemental ratios, and how these functional traits relate to the availability of soil C, N and P pools. Based on metabolic and stoichiometric theory19, we expect those drier conditions—characterized by lower soil C:P and N:P ratios—would be associated with reduced catabolic investment in extracellular resource acquisition and increased anabolic investment in growth and drought resistance.

Results and discussion

Across 200 ecosystems, reduced water availability was associated with lower net primary productivity (NPP) and with lower soil C:P and N:P ratios (Fig. 1c, d), consistent with well-established macroecological patterns1. Random forest models identified soil elemental ratios as key correlates of microbial functional gene composition across sites (Fig. 2a, b). In drier ecosystems, genes associated with anabolic investment, e.g., those involved in RNA transcription, protein synthesis and intracellular transport, were relatively more abundant, whereas the relative abundances of genes responsible for extracellular depolymerization of litter, plant residues and organophosphorus compounds were declined (Fig. 3c and Fig. S1). Together, these results reveal a global association between aridity-linked changes in soil elemental ratios and a coordinated shift from catabolic to anabolic microbial metabolic strategies. Such differences in microbial functional potential have implications for carbon and nutrient cycling and, ultimately, for ecosystem services such as soil carbon storage. For instance, the decreased abundance of genes encoding depolymerization of structurally complex compounds (e.g., xenobiotics, terpenoids and polyketides) suggests lower accumulations of both C and N, relative to P (i.e., bedrock-driven ones) in the most arid environments (Fig. S1), likely due to relative lower external organic matter input and limited biological activity.

Differences in soil elemental ratios linked to aridity across biomes

As anticipated, in our global across-biome database, aridity was closely associated to different ecosystem productivities and soil elemental ratios across biomes (Fig. 1)20. Variance partitioning further indicated that soil C:N:P ratios were jointly associated with microbial- and geochemical-related processes, with their shared component accounting for up to 44%, where microbial and geochemical processes dominated in forest and shrubland ecosystems, respectively (Fig. S2). These patterns highlight tight coupling between climate, soils (microbial habitats) and plant productivity1, which provided theoretical supports for addressing key questions in microbial ecology, i.e., how will differences in soil elemental ratios linked to aridity affect soil functional microbiome across biomes? Increasing aridity was associated with a relative deficiency of organic C compared with N and P, resulting in declines in soil C:N and C:P ratios, particularly in low-productivity ecosystems (Fig. 1d and Fig. S3). Random forest models identified climate variables as strong correlates of variation in soil C, N and P pools (Fig. S4), and in combination with soil physicochemical properties, geography and vegetation characteristics explained up to 73% of the observed variation (Fig. S3 and Fig. S4). Although vegetation type had a stronger association with soil organic C than climate and NPP1, aridity (representing by water availability) remained the dominant correlate of ratios of organic C to nutrients across biomes (Fig. 1c). Soil C and N cycling are rapid, microbially mediated processes21,22, whereas soil P cycling is governed by slower geochemical processes23, contributing to comparatively stable P fractions across biomes (Fig. S1 and Fig. S5a). Accordingly, reduced plant productivity in arid regions can lower soil N:P ratios as N becomes relatively more deficient due to constrained litter decomposition (Fig. 1d and Fig. S5a)24 and decreased biotic N fixation25. In these biologically less active ecosystems, diminished productivity also reduced litter and root exudate inputs, contributing to lower organic C accumulation and further shifts in soil C:N and C:P ratios (Fig. 1d and Fig. S6b)26. By contrast, mineral P (primary P) declined with increasing NPP (P < 0.05), consistent with enhanced biological demand and cycling (e.g., microbial mineralization and plant uptake that driven by P-constrained photosynthesis) that ultimately drew down primary mineral pools (Fig. S6b)27. Together, these spatial patterns infer that ongoing climate-driven drying could translate into consistent shifts in soil elemental ratios, although time-series studies are needed to confirm temporal trajectories28.

Aridity-related soil elemental ratios explain shifts in soil microbial composition and functional potentials

We first examined how differences in soil C:N:P ratios covaried with aridity and microbial community composition. Across environmental gradients, shifts in the relative abundance of major microbial groups were strongly associated with differences in soil elemental ratios, particularly organic C:P and N:P. For instance, the relative abundance of Chloroflexi increased under higher aridity and lower C:P and N:P ratios (P < 0.05; Fig. S7a), consistent with previous observations that this group is well adapted to resource-limited conditions29,30. In contrast, Acidobacteria, Verrucomicrobia, and Rhizaria exhibited higher relative abundances in wetter regions with higher C:P and N:P ratios (P < 0.05; Fig. S7a and Fig. S7b). While shifts in microbial taxonomic composition in relation to soil elemental ratios have been reported at local and regional scales31,32, our results extended these patterns across global biomes using a comprehensive framework. Importantly, our analyses also moved beyond taxonomic responses, addressing the far less explored question of how aridity-related differences in soil elemental ratios is linked to microbial genomic functional traits33.

We found that microbial functional genes and pathways covaried with biologically available nutrient pools as well as with bulk soil elemental ratios (Fig. 2, Fig. S8 and Fig. S9). Random forest models showed the variations in soil available N and P contents across sites were associated with the KEGG- and CAZy-annotated features, which explained up to 43% of the variation (Fig. S8 and Fig. S9). For example, genes involved in mannose metabolism declined in soils with higher nutrient availability (Fig. S10), consistent with reduced reliance on carbohydrate catabolism when labile resources are less limiting4. In addition, variation in soil elemental ratios—especially C:N and N:P—was associated with functional gene categories related to cellular processes, genetic information processing and environmental signal sensing, which together explained up to 24% of the variation (Fig. 2a, b). For instance, genes associated with transcription, protein synthesis and microbial growth increased in soils with lower C:N ratios (Fig. S1). By contrast, C:P ratios showed weaker associations with microbial functional profiles and were more strongly linked to environmental variables (up to 50% variance explained; Figs. 1c and 2). These results contrast with some managed agroecosystems, where microbial community composition and metabolism more directly track soil C and P dynamics34. Collectively, our findings indicated that soil microbial community composition and functional potential were tightly correlated with soil elemental contents and ratios along the global aridity gradient studied.

Enhanced catabolic processes are associated with high soil C:P and N:P ratios under low aridity levels

In wetter regions with higher organic C availability—reflected by higher soil C:P and C:N ratios—we observed increased relative abundances of genes associated with microbial catabolism. Elevated organic C inputs can stimulate microbial activity, thereby increasing microbial demand for nutrients35. As N and P become relatively limiting under these conditions, microorganisms increasingly rely on the degradation and mineralization of organic matter to access essential nutrients, primarily through the production of extracellular enzymes36,37. Consistent with this mechanism, the relative abundance of genes involved in P acquisition was negatively correlated with soil organic P content, while genes associated with N acquisition were positively related to soil C:N ratios (P < 0.05; Fig. 3a). Moreover, genes encoding extracellular enzymes responsible for the degradation of structurally complex organic compounds—such as terpenoids, polyketides38, and arylesters39—were significantly more abundant (P < 0.05) in soils characterized by relative N or P scarcity (Fig. S1). For example, genes involved in the degradation of aromatic compounds (e.g., aliA and tfdB) and root-derived exudates such as terpenoids (e.g., mlhB and chnC) increased in relative abundances in soils with higher organic C content (Fig. S11a, Fig. S11b, and Fig. S12). In parallel, increased abundances of genes related to signal transduction and membrane transport further supported enhanced secretion of extracellular enzymes into the soil matrix (Fig. S1)40.

Microorganisms possessing well-developed systems for synthesis and secretion of extracellular enzymes are better adapted to environments where nutrients are relatively scarce compared with organic C39, as catabolic enzyme activity releases both N and P that can be rapidly assimilated (Fig. 2c)4. For example, under nutrient-deficiency conditions, if organic matter content is sufficient, microorganisms often switch to an overflow metabolism strategy, mineralizing more organic C to generate energy for the production of these catabolic enzymes41. In line with this expectation, the relative abundance of phn gene clusters, which mediate the catabolism of organic P compounds such as phosphonates42, increased in soils with high SOC content and elevated C:N ratios (Fig. 3d). We also found positive relationships between soil C:P ratios and the abundance of genes encoding glucose dehydrogenase (gcd), which is involved in organic acid production and subsequent mineral P solubilization43, although the overall effects of microbial metabolism on soil P availability were relatively weak (Figs. 2 and 3d). However, we did not find any correlation with the phosphatase gene (phoD) (Fig. 3d). These results provided the evidence for the positive effect of organic C on P availability34. In other words, P solubilization can be a potential response to P deficiency accompanying with enhanced organic matter mineralization.

Beyond nutrient acquisition via enzymatic pathways, we observed that reduced soil P content was associated with increased relative abundances of genes involved in bacterial flagellar assembly (e.g., the flg gene cluster), which are linked to microbial motility (Fig. S11c and Fig. S12). Enhanced motility may increase the likelihood of encountering nutrient-rich microsites44. Both motility and extracellular enzyme production are energetically costly processes that depend on sufficient organic C supply45. Accordingly, increased catabolism of organic matter was evident under these conditions (Fig. S11a, Fig. S11b, and S12). Collectively, these findings support our hypothesis that enhanced catabolic activity represents a key microbial strategy for coping with relative nutrient limitation in organic C–rich environments. While previous studies have reported increased microbial catabolism in environmentally stressful or infertile soils46 and reduced catabolism in fertile ones32, our results reveal that elevated catabolic potential can also persist in fertile soils when nutrient limitation arises from imbalanced soil elemental ratios rather than low absolute resource availability.

Enhanced anabolic processes are associated with low soil C:P and N:P ratios under high aridity levels

Shifts in microbial metabolic potential from catabolic toward anabolic processes were consistently associated with aridity-related differences in soil elemental ratios, particularly under conditions of relative organic C scarcity compared with N and P. As soils became drier and C:N and N:P ratios declined in the environments with less organic matter (characterized by reduced organic C and N contents), we observed significant increases in the relative abundance of genes and pathways linked to anabolic functions (P < 0.05), including nucleotide replication, RNA transcription, and protein folding, sorting, and transport (Figs. S1 and S12). In parallel, genes involved in the mineralization of organic P and the degradation of complex organic substrates, such as chitin (e.g., phn and chi gene clusters), were also less abundant under these conditions (Fig. 3b, d), suggesting reduced microbial investment in catabolic processes targeting organic resources. By contrast, genes associated with inorganic P uptake (e.g., pstA)47 and N assimilation, including glutamate synthesis (e.g., glt gene cluster)48, increased in relative abundances (Fig. 3c, d), consistent with a shift toward pathways supporting biomass production and cellular maintenance. These patterns align with theoretical expectations, as microbial growth and drought tolerance require substantial inputs of N- and P-rich biomolecules, including proteins, nucleic acids and ATP49. Consistently, we detected negative relationships between soil organic C content and the abundance of genes involved in microbial C acquisition (P < 0.05; Fig. 3a), indicating enhanced microbial potential for energy acquisition under organic C limitation11. Such investment may help sustain basal energy metabolism and essential cellular functions under arid conditions (Fig. S10). Supporting this interpretation, soils with lower C:N ratios exhibited higher relative abundances of genes associated with protein synthesis and transport (e.g., secG, pcnB, and cysM; P < 0.05; Fig. S1 and S13a). Accordingly, genes involved in intracellular protein and polysaccharide synthesis (e.g., protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, phosphoglycerate synthase, and murein polymerase) were negatively correlated with soil N:P ratios (P < 0.05; Fig. S1 and S13b). Murein and phosphoglycerate are critical intermediates in bacterial cell wall synthesis50,51, and protein synthesis in the endoplasmic reticulum52 is essential for microbial resistance to desiccation53. Together, these results support our hypothesis that aridity-associated differences in soil elemental ratios are linked to a metabolic reconfiguration favoring anabolic processes that underpin microbial survival and stress resistance in dry environments.

Potential implication of the trade-off between catabolism and anabolism

Our results suggest that aridity-associated differences in soil elemental ratios are coupled to shifts in microbial genomic potential with implications for ecosystem functioning. In drier, relatively C-poor soils, reduced abundance of genes involved in extracellular depolymerization and organic-P catabolism indicates lower potential for resource acquisition from structurally complex substrates, which may constrain microbial capacity to degrade and transform external organic matter into soil organic C pools and stocks54. Accordingly, we found that decreases in SOC content were accompanied by a reduction in abundances of genes encoding the transport and synthesis of xylanase, alpha- glucosidase, and beta-glucosidase (P < 0.05; Fig. S10). This can lead to diminished soil aggregate formation and water retention, resulting in more erodible soils and nutrient loss55. Conversely, in wetter ecosystems with higher C:P and N:P ratios, enrichment of catabolic genes may support faster decomposition and nutrient mobilization11.

An important open question is whether aridity-associated differences in soil structure and nutrient availability, linked to shifts in microbial metabolic strategies, also translate into systematic changes in soil microbial community composition and/or diversity. Our results suggest that microorganisms specializing in rapid resource degradation via catabolic pathways (e.g., saprotrophic fungi) may be favored under conditions that promote enhanced catabolism, whereas microbial groups with high energetic demands—such as N-fixing bacteria, whose activity depends on energy-intensive anabolic processes—may be comparatively disadvantaged. This interpretation is consistent with previous observations of shifts in fungal-to-bacterial ratios along gradients of soil elemental stoichiometry29. Resolving whether and how such metabolic reconfigurations cascade to higher levels of biological organization is key for understanding changes in soil food webs, functional diversity, and the delivery of essential ecosystem services, particularly under scenarios in which microbial metabolism shifts from catabolic to anabolic dominance.

Because our data were derived from a spatial gradient (a space-for-time approach), the implications discussed here described associations among ecosystems and were not aligned with those within-site temporal trajectories. Therefore, time-series experiments and manipulative drying studies, ideally coupled with measurements of enzyme activities, respiration and nutrient mineralization, will be important to test causality and translate functional potential into process-based predictions.

Taken together, our global survey provides evidence that aridity-associated differences in soil elemental ratios are tightly coupled to soil microbial functional traits across biomes. As water availability declines and soils become relatively C-poor (lower C:N and N:P ratios), microbial communities are enriched in genes associated with anabolic investment in growth and stress tolerance, whereas genes involved in catabolic resource acquisition and extracellular degradation decline (Fig. 4). Such shift in microbial metabolism from catabolism to anabolism may further influence soil capacity to sequester C and provide nutrients, thereby compromising ecosystem services. This coordinated shift in microbial metabolic potential provides a mechanistic framework for anticipating how climate-driven drying may reorganize soil microbiomes with consequences for carbon and nutrient cycling and associated ecosystem services.

Methods

Global field survey

We used a collection with soil samples collected at depths up to 10 cm (~0–10 cm) from 200 sites spanning all continents; these sites are a subset of sites from a series of global surveys conducted between 2016 and 201956–59. Sites were selected to provide broad, representative coverage of major terrestrial ecosystem types (including temperate and tropical forests, boreal forests, grasslands, subtropical desert, subtropical woodlands and shrublands; Fig. 1b). At each site, we recorded elevation, slope and the distance to equator using a portable GPS unit and classified vegetation type based on dominant plant species. Composite surface (~0–10 cm) soil samples from five soil cores were collected at each site with small differences in soil depth ranges and designs as explained in previous studies56–59. A portion of each composite soil sample was frozen at −20 °C for molecular analyses, and the remaining soil was air-dried for biogeochemical measurements. Samples were imported to Spain using letters of authority from the Ministerio de Agricultura, Pesca y Alimentación.

Climate and ecosystem productivity

Mean annual precipitation (MAP; 81 to 2161 mm), mean annual temperature (MAT; −3.5 to 24.8 °C), precipitation seasonality (PSEA), temperature seasonality (TSEA), and mean diurnal temperature range (MDR) for all sampling sites were obtained from interpolated climate surfaces provided by WorldClim 2 (http://www.worldclim.org/)60. Ecosystem productivity was characterized using net primary productivity (NPP) and perennial plant cover as proxies for the functional attributes of vegetation across sites. Perennial plant cover was measured in situ at each site from vegetation transects using the line-intercept method61. The length of vegetation transects ranged from 30 to 50 m as explained in the original surveys of previous studies56–59. Net primary productivity was estimated using the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) derived from the Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) aboard NASA’s Terra satellite (http://daac.ornl.gov/index.shtml), at a spatial resolution of 250 m. NDVI values were averaged over the month preceding, coinciding with, and following sampling at each plot to minimize potential effects of short-term temporal variability in productivity62.

Determination of soil C, N and P content

Soil carbon (C), nitrogen (N), and phosphorus (P) fractions were quantified across all sampling sites. Carbon fractions included total organic C, particulate organic C and mineral-associated organic C. Nitrogen fractions comprised total N and dissolved inorganic N, while phosphorus fractions included total P, primary P, non-occluded P, occluded P, organic P, residual P, and available P. Air dry soils were used for all the C, N and P fractions analyses.

Soil organic C was determined using a combination of dichromate oxidation and dry combustion analysis with a CN analyzer after removing carbonates by acid fumigation. Particulate organic C and mineral-associated organic C were measured by the size fractionation method as described by Díaz-Martínez et al.63.

Total N was determined on ball-milled soils by dry combustion, gas chromatography and thermal conductivity detection using a CN analyzer. Dissolved inorganic N was quantified by colorimetry as described by Maestre et al. in 201264. Soil P fractions were determined as described by Saez-Sandino et al.65. Air-dried soils were analyzed using a modified sequential extraction procedure based on the Hedley fractionation technique. Phosphorus fractions were quantified using the Malachite Green colorimetric method and measured at 655 nm.

Soil microbial composition and diversity measures

Nagoya focal points from all involved countries were contacted to ensure we were following Nagoya regulations for accessing of genetic resources. The sequencing of all samples was done at the Western Sydney University following a standardized protocol. Soil DNA was extracted from 0.5 g of defrosted soil samples with the PowerSoil DNA Isolation Kit (MoBio Laboratories) following the manufacturer’s instructions but using autoclaved PCR-grade water instead of C6 reagent. After DNA extraction in Spain, DNA samples were imported to Australia for sequencing using permits from the Department Agriculture Fisheries and Forestry of Australia. The diversities of soil bacteria, fungi, protists and invertebrates were quantified using an Illumina MiSeq platform for amplicon sequencing. The 16S rRNA genes for bacteria and the 18S rRNA genes for eukaryotes were sequenced using the 341F/805R (5′-CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3′/5′-GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC-3′)67 and Euk1391f/EukBr (5′-GTACACACCGCCCGTC-3′/5′-TGATCCTTCTGCAGGTTCACCTAC-3′)68 primer sets, respectively. The bioinformatic analyses were performed using a combination of QIIME69, USEARCH70 and UNOISE371. The sequences were clustered into soil phylo-types—zOTUs—at a 100% identity level. The representative sequences of zOTUs were annotated against the SILVA (version 132, 16S rRNA gene) as well as PR2 and SILVA (18S rRNA gene) databases72. Before analyses, the zOTU abundance tables were rarefied at 10,000 (for bacteria) and 4000 (for eukaryotes) sequences per sample to ensure an even sampling depth within each microbial group. Protists were defined as eukaryotic taxa, except fungi, Metazoa (invertebrates and vertebrates) and vascular and non-vascular plants (Streptophyta). Fungal guilds were characterized using the FUNGuild73. In this study, we used species richness, which represents the number of phylotypes, as a measure of soil microbial diversity since it is widely used and straightforward.

Metagenomic sequencing and bioinformatic analyses

Metagenomic sequencing was performed at the Next Generation Sequencing (NGS) Facility (Western Sydney University, Australia) using the Illumina NovaSeq platform, which generated approximately 10 Gb of high-quality 150 bp paired-end reads per sample. Clean reads were obtained after quality trimming, using fastp (version 0.20.1) to remove low-quality reads. Metagenome assembly was conducted using Megahit (v1.2.9)74, retaining only contigs ≥1000 bp in length. Open reading frames of the assembled contigs were predicted using the Prodigal software (version 2.6.3). All open reading frames were compiled into a set of unique genes after clustering with CD-HIT at 95% sequence identity to create a non-redundant gene catalog75. Read counts and reads per million for each predicted non-redundant gene were calculated using Salmon (v1.10.3)76. To investigate how aridity-related differences in soil elemental ratios affect soil microbial genomic functions across global biomes, the predicted genes were annotated against the eggNOG databases (v5.0) using eggnog-mapper (v2.1.12)77, which provided KEGG Orthology (KO), KEGG pathway, and CAZy family profiles78. Before the analysis, genes associated with human diseases and those related to plant and macro-organism systems were excluded. The details of the three levels of KEGG annotation classification are presented in Supplementary Data 1. The categories of KO-annotated genes involved in soil C degradation, as well as the N and P cycles are detailed in Supplementary Data 2. The categories of CAZy-annotated genes involved in C, N and P-acquisition enzymes are detailed in Supplementary Data 3.

Statistical modeling

Nonlinear regressions and threshold identification

To characterize relationships between soil C, N and P contents/ratios and environmental gradients in water availability and NPP, we compared linear models, quadratic models and generalized additive models (GAMs) and selected the most parsimonious model using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC)79. The first step involved fitting both linear and nonlinear models, including quadratic models (the simplest form of nonlinear trends) and generalized additive models (GAMs) (which capture more complex trends through smoothing parameters). When nonlinear models were supported (lower AIC than the linear alternative), we estimated a threshold (change point) along the gradient. For relationships consistent with an abrupt change in slope and/or intercept, we fitted piecewise (segmented/step) regressions and estimated breakpoints. For smooth nonlinear relationships described by GAMs, we defined the threshold as the point of maximum curvature of the fitted smooth (that is, the location along the gradient where the rate of change is greatest)80. For each fitted relationship, we performed 1000 bootstrap resamples to obtain a distribution of plausible threshold values and report the mean threshold (and associated uncertainty) from these bootstrap estimates. Threshold analyses were implemented in R using the chngpt package and GAM functions (R packages chngpt and gam)81.

Correlation analyses

Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to assess the relationships among individual variables along environmental gradients, including soil C, N, and P content, and the abundance of genes (modules) and their associated metabolic pathways. The strength and direction of these relationships are indicated by the correlation coefficients and P values. All P values were adjusted using the false discovery rate (FDR). Correlation analyses were conducted using the R package psych.

Variance partitioning analyses

To quantify the relative contributions of microbial- and geochemical-related predictors to variation in soil elemental ratios, we performed variance partitioning based on partial regression. All variables were standardized to Z scores before analysis. To reduce multicollinearity and overfitting, predictors within each set were summarized using principal components analysis: (i) microbial-related predictors (for example, vegetation attributes and soil organic matter pools), (ii) geochemical-related predictors (for example, inorganic C and P fractions indicative of weathering and leaching), and (iii) soil elemental ratios (soil C:N, N:P and C:P). We then used fivefold cross-validated elastic net regression (100 permutations) to estimate the unique and shared contributions of microbial-related predictors, geochemical-related predictors and their overlap to variation in soil elemental ratios82.

Random forest analyses

To identify microbial functional gene modules associated with variation in soil C, N and P contents and ratios, we trained random forest models (500 trees) using the R package RandomForest83. We used rfcv to select an appropriate number of predictors/features and rfPermute to evaluate predictor significance via permutation testing (1000 permutations). Predictor importance is reported as percentage increase in mean squared error (%IncMSE). Model performance is reported as variance explained based on out-of-bag prediction. Where necessary, variables were log10-transformed to improve normality and stabilize variance. All analyses were performed in R (v4.3.0).
