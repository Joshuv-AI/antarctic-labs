Inverse association between body mass index and abdominal aortic calcification based on NHANES
Authors: Yuxing Wang, Jie Meng, Long Wang, Jian Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe increasing prevalence of obesity worldwide has led to conflicting research on its cardiovascular effects. Although obesity is an established risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD), certain evidence indicates that a higher body mass index (BMI) might unexpectedly reduce the risk of vascular calcification. This study was designed to examine the association between BMI and the presence of abdominal aortic calcification (AAC) within a general population.MethodsWe analyzed data from 3,116 adults participating in the 2013–2014 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). Severe AAC was diagnosed using the Kauppila score based on dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) scans. The relationships between BMI (treated both continuously and categorically) and severe AAC were evaluated using logistic regression. To investigate potential variations and non-linear patterns, subgroup analyses and restricted cubic spline regression were performed.ResultsAfter comprehensive adjustment. higher BMI was significantly associated with lower odds of severe AAC (OR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.81–0.98, p = 0.013). When compared to individuals with normal weight, obese participants had 62% lower odds of severe AAC (OR = 0.38, 95% CI: 0.16–0.94, p = 0.037). This inverse relationship remained significant in subgroups including males, elderly individuals, and those without hypertension or diabetes. Restricted cubic spline analysis indicated a significant non-linear trend (p for nonlinearity = 0.0023).ConclusionsAn inverse association was observed between BMI and AAC prevalence, implying that overweight and obesity may paradoxically confer a protective effect against vascular calcification. These results contribute evidence supporting the existence of an “obesity paradox” in the context of vascular health.

Introduction

Nowadays, abdominal aortic calcifications (AAC) are ubiquitous [1] and severe AAC contributes to cardiovascular risk as well as all-cause and cardiovascular mortality [2]. Age, gender and traditional cardiovascular risk factors have been confirmed to be associated with the prevalence and progression of AAC [3, 4].

Body mass index (BMI), a key indicator of population-level obesity, exhibits a complex and debated relationship with cardiovascular disease (CVD) [5]. While obesity is a well-established risk factor for CVD, a counterintuitive phenomenon termed the “obesity paradox” [6] has been observed, wherein overweight or obese CVD patients often experience better clinical outcomes. Several explanations have been proposed for this paradox, including greater metabolic reserves, the confounding effects of smoking, and weight loss linked to severe illness. Nevertheless, the precise underlying mechanisms continue to be elusive.

Previous studies confirmed an inverse association between BMI and coronary calcification. However, the association between BMI and AAC in general population is less investigated. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the cross-sectional association between BMI and AAC using data from a nationally representative sample of U.S. adults.

Methods

Study population

This analysis utilized publicly accessible data from the 2013–2014 cycle of the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to examine the relationship between body mass index (BMI) and abdominal aortic calcification (AAC) in the American adult population. From the initial cohort, 2782 individuals were excluded due to unavailable AAC scores, and an additional 24 were excluded for missing BMI data, resulting in a final analytical sample of 3116 participants. The protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of National Center for Health Statistics and no new data was added (Protocol #2011-17).

Abdominal aortic calcification evaluation

AAC was quantified using the Kauppila scoring system based on lateral lumbar spine (vertebrae L1-L4) scans acquired via dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA). In this system, calcification of the anterior and posterior aortic walls at each vertebral level (L1-L4) is graded on a scale from 0 to 3. The individual scores are summed to produce a total Kauppila score ranging from 0 to 24, with a score above 6 defining severe AAC [7]. All DXA scans were performed and analyzed by trained technicians using a standardized protocol and the same scanner (detailed at: https://wwwn.cdc.gov/Nchs/Nhanes/2013-2014/DXXAAC_H.htm).

Survey and measurement

Demographic and lifestyle data, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, alcohol and tobacco use, physical activity frequency, and histories of diabetes and hypertension, were collected through standardized interviewer-administered questionnaires. Medical professionals performed physical measurements, including weight, height, and systolic and diastolic blood pressure. Alcohol use was defined as consuming at least 12 drinks in the preceding year. Hypertension was defined as a prior diagnosis or current use of antihypertensive medication, and diabetes was defined as a prior diagnosis or current use of glucose-lowering medication. Laboratory assays measured calcium, and phosphorus. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m²) and categorized as normal (< 25), overweight (25–30), or obese (> 30). For all covariates included in the analysis, missing values constituted less than 10% of the data. Due to the low proportion of missing data (< 10%), listwise deletion was applied in all analyses.

Mediation analysis

To investigate whether serum phosphate mediates the association between BMI and severe AAC, we performed causal mediation analysis using the mediation R package. The mediator model (BMI → phosphate) was fitted using linear regression, and the outcome model (BMI + phosphate → severe AAC) using logistic regression, both adjusted for all covariates included in Model 3. The average causal mediation effect (ACME), average direct effect (ADE), total effect, and proportion mediated were estimated via quasi-Bayesian Monte Carlo simulation with 1,000 iterations.

Statistical analysis

Participant characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations) stratified by BMI category. The association between BMI (modeled as both a continuous and a categorical variable) and severe AAC was evaluated using multivariable logistic regression. The adjusted odds ratios (ORs) for the association between BMI and AAC across various subgroups were visualized using a forest plot. Additionally, a restricted cubic spline regression model was fitted to assess potential non-linearity in the dose-response relationship between continuous BMI and severe AAC.

Association between BMI and AAC

As shown in Table 2, BMI was inversely associated with AAC prevalence in all models. In the fully adjusted model, each 1-unit increase in BMI corresponded to an 11% lower odds of severe AAC (OR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.81–0.98, p = 0.013). Obese participants had substantially lower odds of AAC than normal-weight individuals (OR = 0.38, 95% CI: 0.16–0.94).

Mediation analysis

To examine whether serum phosphate mediates the inverse BMI-AAC association, we conducted a formal mediation analysis. As shown in Table 3, the indirect effect of BMI on severe AAC through serum phosphate was significant (ACME OR = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.92–0.98, p = 0.006), indicating that higher BMI is associated with lower phosphate levels, which in turn is associated with lower odds of severe AAC. The direct effect of BMI remained significant after accounting for phosphate (ADE OR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.80–0.97, p = 0.009). The proportion of the total effect mediated by phosphate was 12.4% (95% CI: 4.1%–25.6%, p = 0.008). These findings suggest that serum phosphate acts as a partial mediator of the obesity paradox in vascular calcification.

Subgroup analysis

The inverse relationship persisted across several subgroups (Fig. 1), notably among males (OR = 0.82, 95% CI: 0.69–0.96), elderly participants (> 60 years; OR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.80–0.99), non-hypertensive (OR = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.62–0.93), and non-diabetic individuals (OR = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.78–0.97).

Non-linear association

Restricted cubic spline analysis demonstrated a significant non-linear relationship (p = 0.0023), with the odds of severe AAC declining sharply at BMI values above 25, suggesting that overweight may be protective (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Our study identified an inverse cross-sectional association between BMI and the prevalence of abdominal aortic calcification (AAC) within the general U.S. population. The strength of this association varied across subgroups, including gender, age, and the presence of hypertension or diabetes. Notably, a sharp decline in AAC prevalence was observed once BMI exceeded 25, suggesting that being overweight may play a protective role in the progression of AAC.

The “obesity paradox” phenomenon has been observed in several cardiovascular diseases, including stroke [8], hypertension [9], coronary artery disease [10] and heart failure [11]. In these studies, investigators found that BMI levels were inversely associated with the risk of the severity of the cardiovascular disease. Previous studies have shown that AAC was surrogate predictor of many severe cardiovascular diseases. Furthermore, our findings confirmed that BMI was inversely correlated with the prevalence of AAC in general population.

Jennifer Mancio et al. found that BMI were inversely associated with aortic valve calcification in aortic stenosis patients submitted to transcatheter aortic valve replacement [12]. The negative association between body weight and AC had also been demonstrated by Marco Canepa et al. [13]. The associations observed between BMI and AAC were not the same across different population subgroups. In general, BMI could exhibit some beneficial effect among male, elderly, participants without hypertension and diabetes. After exclusion of diabetes and hypertension from the multivariate analyses, obesity patients present a high CVD risk [14].

The observed association may be explained by the mobilization of calcium from bone, a process linked to reduced bone mineral density (BMD). Supporting this mechanism, patients with a higher BMI were found to have significantly lower serum concentrations of calcium and phosphorus compared to those with a normal BMI. Prior research indicates that the relationship between BMI and aortic calcification severity becomes non-significant after adjusting for BMD, suggesting that BMD acts as a mediator in this pathway [15]. This aligns with evidence that weight loss leads to a decline in BMD, which subsequently promotes increased vascular calcification [13]. An alternative explanation is offered by another study, which proposed that peripheral lean body mass may account for the negative association between body weight and aortic calcification [16]. Additionally, adipose tissue-derived factors such as leptin and adiponectin may modulate vascular calcification via inflammatory pathways, potentially contributing to the observed inverse association [17, 18].

The present study has some limitations. First, the study is cross-sectional. Secondly, our study doesn’t investigate the potential mechanism underlying the relationship. The strength of this study is that our sample number is huge and AAC was evaluated DXA, which viewed as the gold standard.

Conclusion

In summary, we found that BMI was inversely associated with the prevalence of AAC, especially in male, elderly, participants without hypertension and diabetes. The mediation analysis indicates that serum phosphate is a significant partial mediator of this association. Obesity is a prevalent problem around the world. Therefore, our findings have potential clinical implications for health care providers, as a higher BMI may serve as a protective indicator against severe AAC in the general population.
