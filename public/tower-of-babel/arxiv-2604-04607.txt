1.
Introduction

A
k
-uniform hypergraph
(or
k
-graph
)
H
consists of a vertex set

and a set of edges

, where
V(H)^{(k)}=\{S\subseteq V(H):|S|=k\}
.
Given
k
-graphs

and

, a copy of
F
in
H
is a subhypergraph of
H
that is isomorphic to
F
.
The number of copies of
F
in
H
is denoted by
c(F,H)
.
The
F
-bootstrap percolation process
(or
F
-process
)
starting with

H
is the sequence
(H_{i})_{i\geq 0}
of
k
-graphs with
H_{i}
defined inductively as follows.
Set
H_{0}=H
and given
H_{i}
let
H_{i+1}
be the
k
-graph with vertex set
V(H)
and edge set

\displaystyle E(H_{i+1})=E(H_{i})\cup\big\{e\in V(H)^{(k)}:\>c(F,H_{i}\cup e)>c(F,H_{i})\big\}\,,

where
H_{i}\cup e
denotes the
k
-graph
\big(V(H),E(H_{i})\cup\{e\}\big)
.

This notion originates in the work of Bollobás
[
4
]
, where it arose in the context of weak saturation.
Since then, connections to other areas such as to cellular automata, more general bootstrap-type dynamics
[
13
]
, and statistical physics (see
[
1
]
and
[
5
]
) have been established.
Bollobás
[
3
]
further suggested the problem of determining the maximum running time of such processes (see also
[
10
]
for a random variant).
More precisely, the running time of the
F
-process starting with
H
is
\tau_{F}(H):=\min\{t\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}:\>H_{t}=H_{t+1}\}
.
Given
n\in\mathds{N}
(and for
k=2
), Bollobás asked to determine

M_{F}(n)=\max\big\{\tau_{F}(H):H\text{ is a }k\text{-graph with }n\text{ vertices}\big\}\,.

One major theme in this area is the maximum running time of
K_{r}
-processes, where
K_{r}
is the complete graph on
r
vertices.
Bollobás, Przykucki, Riordan, and Sahasrabudhe
[
3
]
and, independently, Matzke
[
12
]
showed that
M_{K_{4}}(n)=n-3
, for all
n\geq 3
.
In
[
3
]
the authors also showed that
M_{K_{r}}(n)\geq n^{2-\lambda_{r}-o(1)}
for
r\geq 5
, where
\lambda_{r}
is some explicit constant with
\lambda_{r}\rightarrow 0
as
r\rightarrow\infty
, and conjectured that for all
r\geq 5
,
M_{K_{r}}(n)=o(n^{2})
.
Balogh, Kronenberg, Pokrovskiy, and Szabó
[
2
]
disproved this conjecture, showing that
M_{K_{r}}(n)=\Omega(n^{2})
for all
r\geq 6
.
For
r=5
, they used Behrend’s construction for arithmetic progressions to show that
M_{K_{5}}(n)
is asymptotically bigger than
n^{2-\varepsilon}
for all
\varepsilon>0
.
It remains an open problem whether
M_{K_{5}}(n)
is quadratic in
n
or not.

Considering graphs
F
that are not complete, Fabian, Morris, and Szabó
[
7
]
showed that

is of order
\log_{k-1}(n)
, where
C_{k}
is the cycle of length
k
.
In
[
8
]
, the same authors showed that for every tree
T
,
M_{T}(n)
is a constant depending only on
|V(T)|
, while the exact function remains unknown.
They also explore how certain properties of
F
such as the minimum degree and the connectivity can imply a relatively small running time.
For more background on the
F
-process we refer the reader to the recent survey by Fabian, Morris, and Szabó
[
9
]
.

Recently, the investigation of
F
-processes and their maximum running times was initiated for hypergraphs.
Noel and Ranganathan
[
15
]
showed that
M_{K^{(k)}_{r}}(n)=\Theta(n^{k})
for
k\geq 3
and
r\geq k+2
, where
K^{(k)}_{r}
is the complete
k
-graph on
r
vertices.
Answering a question by Noel and Ranganathan, Hartarsky, and Lichev
[
11
]
, and, independently, Espuny Díaz, Janzer, Kronenberg, and Lada
[
6
]
proved that also
M_{K^{(k)}_{k+1}}(n)=\Theta(n^{k})
.
Note that these results state that for the running time of hypergraph cliques the trivial upper bound already provides the right order of magnitude.
In contrast, Espuny Díaz, Janzer, Kronenberg, and Lada
[
6
]
also determined

to be linear in
n
, which requires a non-trivial upper bound.

In
[
6
]
the authors suggested to study the asymptotic running time of the bootstrap percolation process for other hypergraphs which are not complete.
Here we consider extension hypergraphs, which were for instance considered by Mubayi
[
14
]
in the context of the hypergraph Turán problem.
The
k
-extension

of a graph

is the
k
-graph defined as follows.
The vertex set is given by

V(F^{(k)}(G))=V(G)\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\displaystyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\textstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptscriptstyle#$\hfil\cr\cup\cr\cdot\crcr}}}}\{v^{i}_{e}:\>i\in[k-2],e\in E(G)\}

(where

if

or

) and the edge set is

E(F^{(k)}(G))=\big\{e\cup\{v_{e}^{1},\dots,v_{e}^{k-2}\}:\>e\in E(G)\big\}\,.

Our main result is that the maximum running time of the

-process is constant in

.

Theorem 1.1
.

Given an integer
k\geq 3
and a graph
G
, there is some
C\in\mathds{N}
such that for all
n\in\mathds{N}
we have
M_{F^{(k)}(G)}(n)\leq C
.

We remark that the case
k=3
is the most interesting one and indeed the proof can be significantly shortened for
k\geq 4
as we point out in the proof sketch below.
Note that the constant
C
depends on the uniformity and on
G
.
It is not difficult to see that for certain classes of graphs, such as cycles and paths,
M_{F^{(k)}(G)}(n)
is asymptotically bounded by an absolute constant not depending on
|V(G)|
and
k
.
However, we observe that in general the maximum running time of
F^{(k)}(G)
cannot be bounded by an absolute constant independent of
|V(G)|
.
Hence, Theorem
1.1
is in a sense best possible.

Proposition 1.2
.

Let
T=K_{1,t-1}
be the star with
t
vertices.
Then
M_{F^{(k)}(T)}(n)\geq t-1
for all sufficiently large
n
.

In this paper, we do not attempt to optimise the constants and we omit any divisibility issues unless they are relevant.
We collect some definitions in Section
2
, then we prove Theorem
1.1
in Section
3
and Proposition
1.2
in Section

.

Overview of the proof

We commence with the simple observation that every pair in the “center” of a copy of
F^{(k)}(G)
in some
H_{i}
will have almost full degree after at most two more steps (Observation
3.1
).
This is the basic motivation for the definition of
essential pairs
, which are particular pairs appearing in the center of a copy of
F^{(k)}(G)
.
After many steps, there must also be many essential pairs (Observation
3.2
and Observation
3.3
).
A key step in the proof is that if there are many
good
vertices, that is, vertices contained in many center pairs, then the process will stop soon afterwards (Lemma
3.5
).
This is combined with another key lemma that no vertex can be contained in many essential pairs (Lemma
3.6
).
At this point, we remark that for
k\geq 4
the proof is significantly simpler since it can be observed that the process will stop shortly after there is a large enough matching of essential pairs.

1

Since the write-up is not much longer, we deal with a general uniformity
k\geq 3
throughout the proof. But the reader may simply think of
k=3
.

Next, assuming that the process does not terminate sooner, we choose a large matching of essential pairs whose “associated copies” of
F^{(k)}(G)
do not interact too much (Lemma
3.7
).
Now we analyse the interactions between any two such copies.
Either certain vertices can be exchanged between the centers of these copies, or there has to be a “reason” why such an exchange is not possible.
This reason takes the form of certain sets being non-empty (Lemma
3.9
).
Eventually, by analysing an auxiliary directed graph representing those “reasons”, we obtain a contradiction if there are too many copies between which certain vertices cannot be exchanged.
This entails that between many copies the exchange must indeed be possible, which in turn will yield many good vertices (Observation
3.8
), completing the proof.

A
