Dietary habits, dietary diversity, and the influence of work-related stress among male academic professionals in Ibadan, Nigeria: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Oluwadamilola D. Thompson, Ikeola A. Adeoye, Oluwaseun S. Williams, Precious B. Fasiku, David E. Agala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRapid urbanisation and occupational demands have contributed to unhealthy dietary habits in Nigeria, particularly among professionals. Male academic professionals may be especially vulnerable to poor eating habits due to demanding schedules and work-related stress. We assessed dietary habits, dietary diversity, and the influence of work-related stress and other associated factors among male academic professionals in Ibadan, Nigeria.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 235 male academic professionals at selected universities and research institutes in Ibadan. Multistage sampling was employed for larger institutions, while a census approach was used for smaller institutions. Data were collected using a structured WHO STEPS-based questionnaire and a 24-hour recall-based Dietary Diversity Tool. Work-related stress and depression were assessed using the Workplace Stress Scale and the Male Depression Risk Scale-7, respectively. Descriptive statistics summarised participants’ characteristics and dietary habits. Binary logistic regression with a priori covariate selection was used to identify factors associated with dietary outcomes.ResultsThe mean age was 46.4 ± 9.5 years; the majority were married (88.1%), Christian (78.7%), PhD holders (67.7%), and university-based staff (72.3%), but 17.5% were current alcohol users. The distribution by rank was: early-career researcher (32.8%), mid-career (37.9%), and high-career (29.4%). Regarding their dietary habits,69.4%z skipped breakfast weekly, 61.7% snacked, and 30.2% ate out frequently. High dietary diversity was 55.7%. Muslims had lower odds of skipping breakfast (AOR = 0.15; 95% CI: 0.07–0.35), whereas high professional rank had higher odds (AOR = 5.30; 95% CI: 1.33–21.14). Severe work-related stress predicted breakfast skipping (AOR = 3.49; 95% CI: 1.05–11.61). Higher income was associated with lower odds of frequent eating out, whereas both institution type and income predicted eating-out frequency. Moderate depression was associated with lower odds of achieving high dietary diversity (AOR = 0.44; 95% CI: 0.20–0.99). Work-related stress showed no significant association with dietary diversity.ConclusionUnhealthy dietary habits persist among male academic professionals despite relatively high dietary diversity. Work-related stress, professional rank, religion, income, and depression independently shape dietary outcomes in this population. Academic institutions should implement workplace nutrition programmes, structured meal break policies, stress management initiatives, and accessible healthy food options to improve dietary quality and overall well-being among academic professionals.

Background

Nutritional transition, driven by rapid urbanization, globalization, evolving work environments, and economic growth, has dramatically changed how people eat worldwide. These dietary shifts extend beyond cultural evolution but have profound health implications, contributing to various nutrition-related health challenges, from basic malnutrition to specific micronutrient deficiencies and nutrition-related non-communicable diseases [1–4]. The Global Burden of Disease Study estimates that poor diet was responsible for approximately 11 million deaths globally in 2017, making it a major risk factor for mortality worldwide [5]. This trend has been accelerated by urbanization in sub-Saharan African countries, where traditional diets are replaced by high-energy, low-nutrient foods [6, 7]. Studies have shown that urban dwellers consume more processed, energy-dense foods, which may increase their risk of metabolic disorders [8, 9]. While metropolitan areas appear to offer a variety of food choices, the reality is that these options tend to be less nutritious than those found in rural areas, thereby contributing to geographical nutritional differences [10]. The rise in supermarkets, fast-food joints, and restaurants also makes it easier to access unhealthy foods. Meanwhile, frequent consumption of unhealthy snacks, sugary drinks, and processed foods contributes to excess calories, resulting in poor nutritional status [11]. The practice of skipping meals, particularly breakfast, impairs glucose control functions and decreases the overall quality of meals [12, 13]. Inadequate consumption of fruits and vegetables exacerbates nutritional deficiencies, which in turn negatively impact health outcomes [14].

Dietary diversity refers to the inclusion of a range of foods from different food groups in our diets, ensuring we obtain the essential nutrients needed for overall health and mental development [15]. It is crucial for preventing micronutrient deficiencies and promoting good health [16]. Moreover, dietary diversity is considered an indicator of adequate nutrient intake, which is associated with improved health outcomes [17, 18]. By consuming a wide range of food groups, we gain access to various nutrients, making dietary diversity a reasonable approach to meet our nutritional needs [19]. The importance of dietary diversity in promoting health and reducing the risk of chronic diseases has been documented in some studies [20–22]. However, men have been reported to have less diverse diets than women [21, 23]. Since dietary diversity is a key marker of nutrient adequacy, its assessment is crucial among vulnerable populations, including professional men in Nigeria [24].

Healthy eating habits, such as consuming nutrient-rich foods like fruits, vegetables, whole grains, lean protein, and healthy fats, provide essential nutrients that support metabolic health [25]. However, poor dietary habits are a growing global public health concern. Frequent consumption of unhealthy snacks, sugary drinks, and processed foods leads to excess calorie intake, resulting in poor nutritional status [26]. Despite the well-established importance of adequate fruit and vegetable consumption, many populations worldwide fail to meet the recommended intake [27, 28]. Poor dietary habits lead to adverse health effects, reduced productivity, and lower quality of life [29, 30]. Although nutrition is vital for workplace productivity and overall well-being, it is often overlooked, particularly among professionals who work long hours [31, 32]. A study found that many health professionals have unhealthy eating habits, with 51% skipping breakfast, and that there is a notable relationship between breakfast consumption and age [33]. A significant number reported consuming large amounts of unhealthy foods, including takeaways and snacks (50%), fatty foods (38%), and sugary soft drinks (47%).

In Nigeria, findings show that dietary habits are of concern, with a high prevalence of fast-food consumption, frequent snacking, irregular meal schedules, and a lack of fruits and vegetables in the diet [34, 35]. The shift towards urbanisation and Western lifestyles has significantly impacted dietary choices and physical activity levels, leading to an increase in health issues across Nigeria [36–38]. A study among civil servants in Ibadan reported that inadequate intake of fruits and vegetables accounted for 69.7% of non-communicable disease (NCD) risk factors [36]. In a study of nurses at University College Hospital, Ibadan, 91.8% had unhealthy dietary behaviours, sedentary lifestyles, and stress [39]. Despite this evidence, dietary habits and dietary diversity among male academic professionals in Nigeria remain poorly documented, thereby representing a critical gap in the literature.

Work-related stress is becoming a well-recognized factor influencing the eating habits of working adults. According to the World Health Organization and the International Labour Organization [40], work-related stress occurs when individuals face job demands and pressures that are beyond their skills, resources, and abilities, thereby leading to difficulties in managing their workload effectively. This kind of stress is especially common in academic settings, where the workload, juggling act of teaching, research, and administrative tasks can create significant physical, psychosocial, and emotional strain [41, 42]. An international study by Rusli et al. [43] reported occupational stress prevalence of 12.2% among Malaysian academicians and 9.8% among Indonesian academicians, with career development, research demands, teaching responsibilities, and interpersonal development identified as major contributors to stress. Studies among adults show that stress can disrupt eating patterns in several ways, such as reducing time for meal planning and cooking, altering appetite, and leading to a preference for quick, high-calorie foods as a coping mechanism [44, 45]. A recent study involving 649 academics in Türkiye found a significant positive association between work stress levels and overall eating behaviors, including emotional eating, heightened hunger, and food cravings [46]. However, there is limited information on how work-related stress affects dietary habits and diversity among male academic professionals in sub-Saharan Africa.

Men’s health is often overlooked in public health research, despite men facing a higher burden of cardiometabolic disorders, being less likely to seek healthcare services, and frequently engaging in health-risk behaviours than women [47]. Global studies have shown that men are more likely to adopt unhealthy eating habits, including less fruit and vegetables, large portions, a preference for high-calorie foods, frequent skipping of meals (particularly breakfast), excessive alcohol consumption, and smoking [48]. In Nigeria, men are more likely than women to adopt unhealthy dietary habits and lifestyle choices [49, 50]. Given these trends, it is crucial to implement targeted nutrition education and dietary improvement programmes to address nutritional risk factors and lessen the burden of diet-related diseases among men. Among academic professionals, the demands of academic life often lead to irregular eating patterns, excessive snacking, reliance on fast food, limited dietary diversity, and a higher risk of adverse health outcomes. However, there is limited evidence of their dietary habits, particularly in urban Nigerian settings. Given the growing burden of poor dietary habits and inadequate dietary diversity among professionals, and increasing evidence that work-related stress shapes eating behaviour, this study aimed to assess dietary habits, dietary diversity, and the influence of work-related stress and other associated factors among male academics in Nigeria, with the intention of filling the knowledge gap by providing dietary data that may guide policies and interventions promoting healthy nutrition and general well-being of men in the academia.

Methods

Study setting and design

This study was a cross-sectional investigation conducted between November 25, 2024, and February 10, 2025, among male academic professionals in Ibadan, the capital city of Oyo State, a prominent academic and research hub in southwestern Nigeria. The study sites included two universities: University of Ibadan (UI) and Precious Cornerstone University (PCU), and three research institutes: the Nigerian Institute of Social and Economic Research (NISER), the National Horticultural Research Institute (NIHORT), and the Institute of Agricultural Research and Training (IAR&T), ensuring a diverse representation of male academic professionals.

Study population, selection, and sample size

The study population consisted of all male academic professionals who were fully employed at the selected institutions. Eligible participants were those aged 20 years or older who were willing to participate, provided informed consent, and completed the study. Individuals who were unavailable during the data collection period or withdrew consent were excluded. A multi-stage sampling technique was employed for the University of Ibadan, given its large population, with eight faculties randomly selected from the sixteen by balloting. Within each selected faculty, all departments were included, and eligible male academic staff were enrolled. A census was taken for institutions with smaller populations, enrolling all male academic professionals who met the eligibility criteria.

The sample size was calculated using the formula for a single-proportion study as described by Araoye [51], : n =Z2p(1-p)/d2, where Z = 1.96 (95% confidence level), p = 0.867 (complement of 13.3% prevalence of metabolic syndrome) derived from a study by Akintunde et al. [52] and d = 0.05 (margin of error), yielding a minimum of 178 participants. After adjusting for a potential 20% non-response rate using the formula N = n/(1-f), a final minimum sample of 223 was obtained. However, a total of 235 respondents were recruited for the study.

Data collection and instruments

Data were collected using a pretested, interviewer-assisted, self-administered, structured questionnaire via Kobo Collect. The questionnaire was adapted from the WHO STEPS approach for NCD risk factor surveillance [53, 54]. It included a Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ), which was administered to assess habitual dietary intake patterns [55] and a FAO 24-hour recall-based Dietary Diversity Tool (DDT) covering 14 food groups [56, 57] to evaluate dietary diversity among the participants. While the FFQ was administered as part of the broader study to assess dietary patterns, it was not utilized in the present analysis, which focused specifically on dietary habits and dietary diversity from the 24-hour recall. Questions from a Dietary Habits and Nutrition Beliefs Questionnaire assessed snacking, skipping breakfast, eating out, and consumption of fruits and vegetables [58].Work-related stress and depression levels were measured using the Workplace Stress Scale and the Male Depression Risk Scale-7, respectively [59, 60].

Assessment of measures

Dependent variables

The dependent variables were dietary habits and dietary diversity.

Dietary habits

Dietary habits assessed in this study included skipping breakfast, frequency and types of snacking, frequency of eating out, consumption of fruits and vegetables, and usual meal sources. Breakfast skipping was assessed by asking whether participants skipped breakfast at least once a week, categorised as Yes or No. Snacking and eating out were classified based on frequency as either infrequent (never, 1–3 times a month, or once a week) or frequent (a few times a week, once a day, or a few times a day). This dichotomisation was based on conceptual and distributional considerations. The threshold distinguishes occasional consumption from habitual consumption, and some original frequency categories had small cell counts, which could compromise the stability of ordinal models. Snacks commonly consumed were reported. Fruit and vegetable intake was categorised as low (< 3 times per week), moderate (3–5 times per week), or high (≥ 6 times per week). Meal regularity was defined as irregular, partially regular, or regular, depending on the consistency of daily meal consumption. The usual source of meals was categorised as home-cooked, fast food, restaurants, street vendors, and others.

Dietary diversity

Dietary diversity was measured using a 24-hour recall-based dietary diversity tool (DDT) that covered 14 food groups adapted from the FAO dietary diversity guidelines [56]. A dietary diversity score (DDS) was calculated by assigning a score of “1” to each food group consumed during the recall and “0” if absent, with a maximum possible score of 14. The DDS was then classified into three categories following the classification by Sanusi [57] adapted for the Nigerian adult population: Low DD (0–4), Medium DD (5–9), and High DD (10–14). This classification was used for descriptive analyses.

Independent variables

Independent variables included socio-demographic characteristics such as age (20–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59, ≥ 60 years), marital status (married or not married), religion, ethnicity, academic staff, years of service, and monthly income level. Religion has three categories (Christianity, Islamic, Traditional/Others), ethnicity (Yoruba or non-Yoruba), educational level (PhD holders or non-PhD holders), Institution type (Lecturers or Researchers), Professional rank (early, middle and high professional ranks), years of service (≤ 10 years or > 10 years), and monthly income level (low, middle, or high). Lifestyle factors included smoking status, defined as ever smoked tobacco products (“yes” or “no”), and current alcohol use, assessed by asking, “Have you consumed an alcoholic drink within the past 30 days?” Work-related stress levels were categorised as relatively calm, fairly low, moderate, severe, and potentially dangerous levels of stress. Depression level was also classified into low, moderate, severe, and extremely severe.

Work-related stress

Work-related stress was assessed using the Workplace Stress Scale (WSS), developed by the Marlin Company and American Institute of Stress [59], an eight-item instrument rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (very often). Total scores range from 8 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater levels of work-related stress. Scores were interpreted as follows: ≤15 (relatively calm), 16–20 (fairly low stress), 21–25 (moderate stress), 26–30 (severe stress), and 31–40 (potentially dangerous levels of stress). The WSS demonstrated good internal consistency in this study (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.879).

Depression levels

The levels of depression were assessed using the Male Depression Risk Scale-7 (MDRS_7), a seven-item instrument designed to measure male-specific depression symptoms [60, 61] Participants reported how often each item applied to them over the past four weeks on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (none of the time) to 4 (all of the time). Total scores were obtained by summing responses across all items, with higher scores indicating greater depressive symptoms. Scores were categorised as low (0–5), moderate (6–7), severe (8–12), and extremely severe (≥ 13), based on established guidelines. The scale showed moderate internal consistency in this sample (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.679).

Data and statistical analysis

The data were downloaded from the Kobo Collect tool and exported to Excel, where they were checked for completeness and accuracy, then imported into SPSS v. 26 for cleaning, coding, and analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to characterize respondents’ background characteristics and dietary habits. Missing data were minimal (< 5% across all variables), showed no systematic pattern, and were handled by listwise deletion. The results are presented in tables and charts, with frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, while continuous variables are summarised as mean (± SD). The outcome variables in this study included snacking, skipping breakfast, frequency of eating out, and dietary diversity. The explanatory variables included age, religion, ethnic group, marital status, educational level, academic staff, income level, professional rank, years of service, ever smoked, current alcohol consumption, work-related stress, and depression level. Several variables required recategorization prior to regression analysis.

Binary logistic regression was conducted to explore the associations between the explanatory and outcome variables. Unadjusted crude odds ratios (COR) with 95% confidence intervals were estimated for each predictor individually. For multivariable models, covariates were selected a priori based on established conceptual frameworks rather than bivariate significance. All multivariable models were estimated using the Enter method with all covariates entered simultaneously. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, with borderline associations (p < 0.10) noted where relevant. Both unadjusted and adjusted odds ratios, 95% confidence intervals, and p-values (p < 0.05) were reported. Multicollinearity among the predictor variables was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (all VIF < 3.0). Model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test and the Nagelkerke R² statistic.

Results

Sociodemographic and lifestyle characteristics

Table 1 presents the sociodemographic and lifestyle characteristics of the respondents. The study included 235 male academic professionals with a mean age of 46.4 ± 9.5 years. The majority were married, 207 (88.1%), Christian, 185 (78.7%), and of Yoruba ethnicity, 201(85.5%). Most respondents were PhD holders, 159 (67.7%), and University-based academic staff, 170 (72.3%). In terms of professional career rank, the majority of respondents were mid-career professionals, 89 (37.9%), followed by early-career, 77 (32.8%), and high-career, 69 (29.4%). The mean years of service was 11.4 ± 9.6 years, with 124 (53.0%) having ≤ 10 years of experience. In terms of income, 110 respondents (46.8%) fell into the middle-income group. Alcohol consumption was reported by 114 (48.5%), and 41 (17.5%) were current drinkers; only 4 (10.5%) had high alcohol consumption. Binge drinking was relatively low, at 8 (3.4%) in this population. Additionally, tobacco use was very low, with 11 (4.7%) reporting ever smoking. Regarding work-related stress, 73 (31.1%) experienced moderate stress, whereas 17 (7.2%) experienced potentially dangerous levels. Among the respondents, 160 (68.1%) had low depression levels.

Dietary diversity among the respondents

Table 2 presents the distribution of dietary diversity categories among respondents. The majority, 131 (55.7%), achieved high dietary diversity (DDS 10–14), while 91 (38.7%) had medium dietary diversity (DDS 5–9) and 13 (5.5%) had low dietary diversity (DDS 0–4). The mean DDS was 9.66 (± 3.07). Figure 2 presents the overall food group consumption patterns from the 24-hour dietary recall, with grains, oils and fats, and other fruits and vegetables being the most frequently consumed food groups across the sample. Figure 3 presents the distribution of food group consumption across the three dietary diversity categories. In the low dietary diversity group, only grains (61.5%), other fruits and vegetables (61.5%), and oils and fats (61.5%) were consumed by at least half of the participants. As dietary diversity increased to the medium level, dark green leafy vegetables (60.4%), meat (53.8%), fish (62.6%), and spices and condiments (54.9%) became more pronounced. For those with high dietary diversity, over half of the participants consumed all 14 food groups. Organ meat showed a notable gradient; consumed by only 15.4% and 11.0% of participants in the low and medium diversity groups, respectively, but rose to 56.5% among those with high dietary diversity. Table 3 presents the median dietary diversity scores by work-related stress category. The median DDS ranged from 9.0 (IQR 4.5) among those with potentially dangerous stress to 11.0 (IQR 5.5) among those with moderate stress. Association between sociodemographic, lifestyle factors, and dietary habits

Table 4 presents the crude and adjusted associations between sociodemographic and lifestyle factors and dietary habits (snacking, skipping breakfast, frequency of eating out). For snacking frequency, none of the variables were significantly associated in either the crude or adjusted models. In the crude model, skipping breakfast was significantly associated with religion, age, income level, years of service, and work-related stress. Muslims had lower odds of skipping breakfast compared to Christians (COR = 0.31; 95% CI: 0.16–0.60; p = 0.001), participants aged 30–39 years (COR = 0.43; 95% CI: 0.19–0.97; p = 0.042) and those aged 50 years and above (COR = 0.37; 95% CI: 0.17–0.80; p = 0.012) were less likely to skip breakfast compared to those aged 20–29 years, high income earners had lower odds of skipping breakfast than low income earners (COR = 0.43; 95% CI: 0.20–0.92; p = 0.029), those with more than 10 years of service were less likely to skip breakfast (COR = 0.54; 95% CI: 0.31–0.94; p = 0.031), and severe work-related stress was associated with higher odds of breakfast skipping compared to relatively calm stress (COR = 2.94; 95% CI: 1.07–8.13; p = 0.037). After adjustment, age, income level, and years of service lost significance, while religion, professional rank, severe work-related stress, and current alcohol consumption remained independently significant. Muslim respondents were substantially less likely to skip breakfast than Christians (AOR = 0.15; 95% CI: 0.07–0.35; p < 0.001). High-ranking academics had over five times the odds of skipping breakfast compared to early-career academics (AOR = 5.30; 95% CI: 1.33–21.14; p = 0.018). Severe work-related stress remained independently associated with higher odds of breakfast skipping (AOR = 3.49; 95% CI: 1.05–11.61; p = 0.042), and current alcohol consumers were less likely to skip breakfast compared to non-consumers (AOR = 0.36; 95% CI: 0.14–0.92; p = 0.034).

Association between sociodemographic, lifestyle factors, and dietary diversity

Table 5 presents the crude and adjusted associations between sociodemographic and lifestyle factors and dietary diversity. In the crude model, age, income level, and depression were significantly associated with dietary diversity. Participants aged 50 years and older had higher odds of achieving high dietary diversity than those aged 20–29 years (COR = 2.05; 95% CI: 1.07–3.92; p = 0.030). Middle income (COR = 1.88; 95% CI: 1.03–3.42; p = 0.040) and high-income earners (COR = 2.06; 95% CI: 1.00–4.26; p = 0.050) had higher odds of high dietary diversity compared to low-income earners, and participants with moderate depression had lower odds of achieving high dietary diversity compared to those with low depression (COR = 0.46; 95% CI: 0.22–0.96; p = 0.039). After adjustment, income level and depression remained independently significant, while age lost its significance. Middle-income earners had approximately 2.4 times higher odds of achieving high dietary diversity compared to low-income earners (AOR = 2.36; 95% CI: 1.03–5.42; p = 0.043), while high-income earners showed a positive trend that did not reach significance (AOR = 3.05; 95% CI: 0.85–11.01; p = 0.088). Moderate depression remained independently associated with lower odds of achieving high dietary diversity (AOR = 0.44; 95% CI: 0.20–0.99; p = 0.048), with participants with moderate depression having approximately 56% lower odds of achieving high dietary diversity compared to those with low depression. Work-related stress showed no significant association with dietary diversity in either the crude or adjusted models.

Discussion

Men are often less involved in meal planning and food preparation due to traditional gender roles and occupational demands, making them more prone to unhealthy eating patterns, including frequent snacking, lower fruit and vegetable intake, and irregular meal schedules, compared to women [47] In academic and research settings, intense workloads and tight schedules can further compound these vulnerabilities. Understanding the dietary behaviours of male academic professionals is therefore important for developing targeted workplace nutrition and health policies. This study assessed the dietary habits, dietary diversity, and the influence of work-related stress and other associated factors among 235 male academic professionals in Ibadan, Nigeria. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively examine dietary outcomes and their associated factors among male academic professionals in Nigerian tertiary institutions. It addresses a critical gap in the nutritional epidemiology literature, in which existing studies have focused mainly on women, children, undergraduate students, non-academic staff, and general urban populations, rather than on academic professionals. The findings reveal a pattern of suboptimal dietary habits alongside relatively high dietary diversity. These dietary behaviours appear to be influenced by a complex interplay of occupational, sociodemographic, and psychosocial factors, adding to the limited but growing evidence on the dietary health of male academic professionals in urban Nigeria.

Snacking was a common habit among this study population, with over half (61.7%) of the participants frequently consuming snacks between meals. This is not really surprising given the fast-paced and demanding nature of scholarly work, where time for meal planning and preparation is minimal and convenient food options are readily available at tuck shops, campus stores, and nearby eateries. Biscuits and pastries were the most commonly consumed snack types, while fruits (6.7%) and vegetables (2.3%) were the least consumed; a pattern consistent with findings from previous studies [62, 63]. This pattern reflects a general preference for affordable, ready-to-eat snacks that require no preparation and are immediately available. The low consumption of vegetables as snacks may be due to their perishability, higher cost relative to packaged snacks, and the preparation effort they require, thereby making them less practical choices in a busy academic environment. Beyond these practical concerns, Nigeria’s cultural eating habits should also be considered. While raw vegetables such as carrots, cucumbers, and cabbage can serve as healthy between-meal options, many Nigerians predominantly consume vegetables as ingredients in soup-based dishes such as ‘egusi’ and ‘efo riro’. This may create the impression that vegetable needs are already met through cooked meals, reducing the likelihood that raw vegetables will be considered standalone snack options. Taste preferences may also influence snack choices, as some do not consider fruits and vegetables satisfying. Louro et al. [64] agreed that taste sensitivity directly relates to fruit and vegetable preferences over other snacks. Interestingly, similar snacking habits were reported among non-academic staff at a Nigerian university [35] [Oladosu et al., 2023], suggesting that these dietary challenges are widespread across professional groups.

While snacks contribute to overall energy intake [65], their health impact largely depends on the type and choice of snack. Healthier snack choices, such as fruits, vegetables, and nuts, provide essential micronutrients, promote satiety, and aid in weight regulation [66, 67], whereas snacks high in sugar, fat, and salt are associated with adverse health outcomes such as obesity and type 2 diabetes [68].The predominance of biscuits and pastries in this sample, therefore, represents a meaningful dietary risk, particularly given that fruits and vegetables were largely overlooked as snack options despite their established nutritional benefits. In this study, none of the measured sociodemographic or lifestyle variables significantly predicted snacking —a finding that contrasts with earlier findings that older age groups were less likely to snack frequently [69, 70]. This null finding suggests that snacking in this population is driven by contextual and environmental factors, such as workplace food availability, habitual patterns, and time constraints, rather than by individual characteristics. An integrative review of workplace eating among professionals found that long working hours, rigid schedules, and limited access to healthy food options are primary barriers to healthy eating in occupational settings, with the workplace exerting a considerable negative influence on dietary intake [71]. In academic environments, easy access to campus tuck shops and eateries creates conditions that favour convenient, ready-to-eat processed foods over freshly prepared alternatives, regardless of an individual’s age, income, or stress level. These findings suggest that improving the food environment within academic institutions through making healthier snack options available at departmental meetings, campus canteens, and institutional shops may be a more practical and effective approach to improving snack quality than targeting individual-level factors alone. Dietary interventions that incorporate environmental components, such as improving the availability of healthy food in work-related settings, are among the most effective strategies for promoting healthier eating among working adults [72, 73].

Skipping breakfast was the most prevalent and most significantly predicted dietary habit in this study, with 163 (69.4%) of participants skipping breakfast at least once a week. This high prevalence is likely due to the hectic nature of academic life, which includes long hours, research tasks, and administrative responsibilities. Kunene and Taukobong [33], and Nsaiah-Asamoah et al. [74] reported similar trends among other high-pressure professions, where occupational stress and time constraints contribute to irregular eating habits. A study conducted in Bangladesh reported a high prevalence of breakfast skipping among university professors, attributed to work pressure and time constraints [75]. Although breakfast is widely regarded as the most important meal of the day [76], skipping it has been associated with poor dietary quality and elevated risk of metabolic issues among adults [77, 78].Conversely, regular breakfast consumption helps maintain a balanced metabolic state [79].

Religion was the strongest independent predictor of breakfast skipping in the adjusted model, with Islamic participants having approximately 85% lower odds of skipping breakfast compared to Christians. This may be due to structured religious fasting practices that promote regular meals outside fasting periods. A qualitative study by Owais et al. [80] relates that Muslims tend to follow structured meal practices and healthy dietary habits as part of their religious lifestyle, with Islam placing considerable emphasis on food consumption, dietary habits, and food etiquette, as reflected in Islamic teachings and prophetic traditions. These structured religious food practices, including organised meal times and mindful food choices rooted in faith, may foster consistent morning meal habits throughout the year. The findings from our study underscore the important role of religious practices in shaping dietary behaviour among Nigerian professional populations and suggest that culturally sensitive nutrition promotion strategies may be more effective than generic dietary advice in this context.

Severe work-related stress was significantly associated with skipping breakfast, with over threefold higher odds of doing so; this was the only instance in which work-related stress significantly predicted a dietary outcome in this study. This finding is consistent with evidence from academic settings in Türkiye showing a significant positive association between work stress and eating behaviour among academicians [46]. This also corroborates evidence that stress disrupts eating patterns by altering appetite and food preferences [44, 81]. In professional workplace settings, competing work demands and time constraints further reduce the opportunity for regular meal preparation, thereby compounding the effect of stress on eating behaviour [71]. Under severe stress, it is plausible that academics prioritise workload demands over meal regularity, with breakfast being the first and most easily sacrificed meal of the day. The fact that this association appears only at severe levels of stress and only for breakfast skipping, rather than for other dietary habits, points to a possible threshold effect. In other words, work-related stress may begin to disrupt morning eating patterns only when it becomes intense enough to exceed an individual’s coping capacity. Stress can affect appetite and metabolism, often leading to changes in eating patterns [82]. At this level of stress, the physiological and psychological burden of stress becomes overwhelming, as stress hormones suppress appetite regulation, reduce motivation for meal planning, and create a mental state in which immediate work demands take priority over personal healthy behaviours. These findings point directly to the need for institutional stress management and support programmes, workload distribution policies, and protected personal time as feasible strategies to reduce breakfast omission among severely stressed academic professionals.

Beyond religion and stress, professional rank also became a significant predictor of skipping breakfast. In the crude model, high professional rank showed no significant association with skipping breakfast, yet after full adjustment, high-ranking academics had over five times higher odds of skipping breakfast compared to early-career academics­ —a dramatic reversal consistent with negative confounding. This change suggests that higher income, which is more common among senior staff, initially masked the true effect of professional rank. After accounting for income, the association became clear. The demands of senior roles, including administrative responsibilities and competing priorities, likely reduce the time available for morning meals despite greater financial advantage. This implies that senior academics may face increased dietary risk, and practical measures, such as making healthy breakfast options readily available within departments, could help address this issue. Interestingly, current alcohol consumption was independently associated with lower odds of breakfast skipping after adjustment, which is counterintuitive given that alcohol use is generally associated with irregular meal patterns and poorer dietary behaviours [83]. Rather than indicating any direct benefit, this may reflect the social context of drinking in this professional group, where drinkers may be more likely to engage in regular meals, including in the morning. This finding should be interpreted with caution, given the cross-sectional design and the small number of current drinkers in the sample (n = 41; 17.5%).

Our study found that only about one-third of respondents (30.2%) frequently ate outside their homes, with the majority relying primarily on home-cooked meals, suggesting that these choices may reflect personal preferences and circumstances. Many participants seemed to prefer home-cooked meals, perhaps valuing the quality, hygiene, family recipes, cultural familiarity, or the satisfaction of preparing their own food. For some, it may be a matter of watching their budget, whereas others may be motivated by health and nutritional concerns. A study by Mills et al. [84] found that frequent consumption of home-cooked meals was associated with better dietary quality, lower adiposity risk, and greater adherence to a healthy dietary pattern. Additionally, when juggling busy schedules and adhering to cultural food preferences, there is often little need to eat out. Institution type was independently associated with eating-out frequency after adjustment, with researchers approximately twice as likely as university-based lecturers to eat out frequently. This may reflect the less predictable nature of research roles; as researchers often spend time away from home for fieldwork and data collection, which can limit their access to home-prepared meals and increase reliance on outside food. The more structured, location-based schedules of university lecturers may support more regular home eating. Providing flexible schedules and structured break times for research staff could encourage more consistent meal routines.

Interestingly, those with higher incomes actually ate out less often than their lower-paid colleagues; this might be because they can afford to buy quality ingredients for home cooking and have more resources to support healthier eating habits. In urban Nigeria, ready-to-eat food is cheap and readily accessible, making it a common option for those with limited income. Research by Ilori et al. [85] in Ibadan confirms that financial status shapes food choices: those with better financial security can afford nutritious foods such as fruits and protein, while the majority (88%) who are food-insecure rely on whatever is available and affordable at local markets. While this pattern has been primarily documented among the urban poor, our findings suggest that even within a professional academic population, income remains a meaningful determinant of whether meals are prepared at home or sourced externally. This finding corroborates evidence that socioeconomic factors influence food access and dietary behaviours in urban settings [86]. In the crude analysis, individuals with severe depression were more likely to eat out frequently, though this association became weakened and lost significance after adjustment. This suggests it was largely explained by other factors, particularly income and institution type, rather than an independent effect of depression. Among male academics, frequent eating out in this context may reflect a form of temporary escape from overwhelming academic work demands, family responsibilities, and social expectations and obligations. However, this remains speculative given the study’s cross-sectional design.

Dietary diversity was relatively high among male academic professionals in this study, with the majority (55.7%) achieving high dietary diversity and a mean DDS of 9.66 (± 3.07). This is notably higher than findings from a previous Nigerian study, which reported low dietary diversity in 74.6% of participants [21], and that likely reflects the relatively higher socioeconomic status of academic professionals compared to general population samples. Our study revealed that grains, oils, and fats, as well as other fruits and vegetables, were the most commonly consumed food groups, whereas organ meats and sweets were the least consumed. The distribution of food group consumption across the three dietary diversity categories in our study reflects a diet largely based on staple carbohydrates and cooking fats. It suggests a transition from low to medium dietary diversity, characterised by the addition of protein sources and vegetables to the staple diet. The prevalence of grains aligns with previous findings in sub–Saharan African countries [21, 22]. Organ meat had the lowest overall intake in this study, consumed by only 36.6%% of participants across the full sample. Notably, this pattern was consistent with findings from other Nigerian adult male populations; among rural farm household men in Southwestern Nigeria, organ meat was consumed by less than 1% of participants in a 24-hour recall [87]. These suggest that low organ meat consumption reflects a broader dietary pattern among Nigerian adult men regardless of occupational or socioeconomic context. The low intake of organ meats and dairy products is a concern, as these foods provide essential micronutrients important for cognitive function, metabolism, and cardiovascular health [88, 89]. That said, while dietary diversity is crucial to nutritional adequacy and health, food quality remains equally vital. Gómez et al. [89] reported that higher DDS was associated with improved micronutrient intake and metabolic health, while another study linked greater dietary diversity to healthier dietary patterns [90].

It is important to note that these food group consumption patterns were derived from a single 24-hour dietary recall, which captures food intake on one day and may not represent usual eating habits; a known limitation of the dietary diversity assessment in nutritional epidemiology [56, 91]. The dietary diversity score measures the range of foods consumed rather than how often or how adequately they are eaten, implying that a participant who ate a small portion of fruit or a tomato-based dish on the recall day would still be counted as having consumed that food group, even if it is not part of their regular diet. This distinction is particularly crucial when interpreting the relatively high proportion of participants reporting fruit and vegetable intake during the recall period, which contrasts with the dietary habits findings, in which nearly half (45.5%) reported low habitual consumption of fruits and vegetables. The difference observed here, therefore, shows the distinct aspects of diet measured by these two approaches rather than a true inconsistency. The low habitual consumption of fruits and vegetables in this population is consistent with studies reporting low intake of fruits and vegetables among professionals in South Africa and Nigeria [33, 35]. Lack of time, limited access to fresh produce, and food preferences may contribute to low fruit and vegetable intake, as observed in this study. These findings underscore the need for nutrition awareness programmes within academic institutions to encourage intake of diverse protein sources, dairy products, and micronutrient-rich vegetables alongside staple foods to achieve adequate nutrient intake.

Income was independently associated with higher odds of achieving high dietary diversity after adjustment, with middle-income participants about 2.4 times more likely to have diverse diets than those with lower incomes. Globally, socioeconomic status is a key factor that shapes our eating habits, with income playing a crucial role in determining access to nutritious foods. Evidence from international cross-studies shows that financial constraints often force households to rely on affordable, energy-dense staples rather than healthier options such as fresh fruits, quality proteins, and seasonal ingredients, resulting in a less varied diet [92, 93]. Even among highly educated individuals, limited financial resources can contribute to food insecurity and make it difficult to maintain a balanced diet, as their food choices are often determined by affordability. Additionally, financial strain may push people towards cheaper processed foods, making it more challenging to plan and prepare diverse meals. Nigerian studies also show that household income is a key determinant of dietary diversity in urban areas, as financial constraints often limit access to nutrient-rich foods such as animal proteins, dairy, and fresh vegetables, which are essential for a varied diet. Ikudayisi et al. [94] found that both income and urban living conditions significantly enhance the consumption of diverse diets. Meanwhile, Obayelu and Osho [95] pointed out that urban households with fewer resources tend to have very limited dietary diversity. The findings from Amao et al. [96] highlighted that food expenditure is a key driver of the types of food households choose. This highlights the need to address financial barriers to diverse diets, for example, by subsidizing food options and improving access to affordable, nutritious meals within institutional settings.

Moderate depression was associated with lower odds of achieving high dietary diversity after adjustment, with those with moderate depression having approximately 56% lower odds of achieving high dietary diversity compared to those with low depression. This implies that negative emotional states can really impact how we eat. Research indicates that negative affectivity is associated with emotional overeating and reduced dietary self-regulation in adults [97, 98]. Dilsiz and Arslan [99]. Similarly, found that psychological distress can lead people to eat more due to negative emotions rather than actual hunger or thoughtful meal planning. When people cope with their feelings this way, they often rely on familiar, calorie-dense comfort foods, which can push aside a variety of healthier options and leave them with a lower diet quality [100]. It is plausible that individuals with moderate depression in our study relied on a narrower dietary choice, as depressive symptoms may hinder decision-making, lower motivation for planning meals, and make quick and easy options more appealing than a nutritionally diverse diet. Importantly, the cross-sectional design of this study, which precludes establishing the direction of this association with certainty. While depression may limit dietary diversity, it is equally plausible that low dietary diversity and resulting micronutrient deficiencies contribute to depressive symptoms. A longitudinal study found that higher vitamin B12 intake in men was associated with a lower risk of depression over a 3-year period, suggesting that adequate dietary intake of key micronutrients, obtained through a diverse diet, may reduce depressive symptoms [101]. These findings therefore suggest that mental health support within academic settings, such as counseling services, peer support, and improved workload management, could also help improve dietary quality alongside psychological well-being.

The strengths of this study include its focus on male academic professionals. This group has been overlooked in public health and nutritional research in Nigeria despite facing distinct dietary vulnerabilities and heightened cardiometabolic risk. The inclusion of both universities and research institutes enhances the representativeness of academics from various sectors. The use of validated dietary assessment tools further strengthens the reliability of the findings, while incorporating assessments of work-related stress and depression provides novel insights into their influence on dietary habits and diversity among this population. Additionally, the use of a priori covariate selection grounded in a conceptual framework, along with assessments of multicollinearity and model fit, reflects methodological rigor in this study.

However, the study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design limits causal inferences, and reliance on self-reported data may introduce recall and social desirability biases. Clustering by institution and faculty was not formally accounted for in the analysis we are likely to have underestimated the standard errors. Future studies should consider cluster-robust standard errors or multilevel modeling to account for institutional clustering effects. Dietary diversity was assessed using a single 24-hour dietary recall, which captures food intake on a single day and may not adequately reflect habitual dietary patterns. The dichotomization of snacking and eating-out frequency into two categories, while justified by sparse cell counts in individual frequency categories, entails some information. The use of binary rather than ordinal logistic regression should be considered a limitation. The small number of ever-smokers (n = 11; 4.7%) limited the precision of smoking-related estimates as reflected in wide confidence intervals. The sample was drawn exclusively from Ibadan, limiting generalisability to professionals in other Nigerian cities and regions. As the study was purposively designed to focus on male academic professionals, the findings are not generalisable to female academics. Future studies should include female academic professionals to enable gender-comparative analyses of dietary habits and diversity in this occupational group, employ longitudinal designs to establish temporal relationships, and recruit larger representative samples across multiple Nigerian cities.

Conclusion

The dietary habits of male academic professionals in Ibadan highlight key public health concerns in Nigeria, where urbanisation, occupational stress, lifestyle factors, and sociodemographic characteristics collectively influence food choices. Despite relatively high dietary diversity, unhealthy habits, including frequent breakfast skipping, reliance on processed snacks, and low habitual fruit and vegetable intake, persist in this population. Severe work-related stress independently predicted breakfast skipping, while income and moderate depression were the primary determinants of dietary diversity, underscoring the need for workplace nutrition and mental health programmes in academic settings. Religion emerged as the strongest predictor of breakfast regularity, highlighting the role of cultural and religious factors in dietary behaviour among Nigerian professionals. The counterintuitive finding that high-ranking academics are more likely to skip breakfast despite their socioeconomic advantages points to the need for targeted dietary health strategies that account for occupational demands across all career stages. Ultimately, both universities and research institutes should implement structured meal-break policies, accessible healthy food options, stress management initiatives, and nutrition awareness programmes to improve dietary quality and overall well-being among academic professionals. Further research using longitudinal designs and larger representative samples is needed to establish causal relationships and evaluate targeted dietary interventions in this population.
