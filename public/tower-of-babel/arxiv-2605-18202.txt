Concise and Logically Consistent Conformal Sets for Neuro-Symbolic Concept-Based Models
Samuele Bortolotti Emanuele Marconato Andrea Pugnana, [0.4em] Andrea Passerini Stefano Teso, [0.6em] Department of Information Engineering and Computer Science, University of Trento, Italy

Abstract
Neuro-Symbolic Concept-based Models (NeSy-CBMs) are a family of architectures that integrate neural networks with symbolic reasoning for enhanced reliability in high-stakes applications. They work by first extracting high-level concepts from the input and then inferring a task label from these compatibly with given logical constraints. Yet, their label and concept predictions can be overconfident, making it difficult for stakeholders to gauge when the model’s decisions can be trusted. We address this issue by integrating ideas from Conformal Prediction (CP), a framework providing rigorous, distribution-free coverage guarantees. We formalize three desiderata – consistency, coverage, and conciseness – that any conformal method for NeSy-CBMs should satisfy, and show that existing approaches fall short of at least one. We then introduce COCOCO , a post-hoc framework that conformalizes concepts and labels jointly and reconciles them via a single deduction–abduction revision step . COCOCO satisfies all three desiderata, retains distribution-free coverage, is robust to imperfect knowledge and supports user-specified size budgets. Our experiments on 8 8 data sets highlight how COCOCO compares favorably against competitors and natural baselines in terms of performance and set size.

NeSy-CBMs are geared for reliability in high-stakes applications

\textstyle g^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{c}}):=\argmax_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}g(\bm{\mathrm{c}})\subseteq\mathcal{Y},\qquad g^{-\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{y}}):=\{\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\mathcal{C}:\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in g^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})\}.

Definition 3.1

4
Consistency, Conformality and Conciseness

We begin by introducing

COCOCO
(COnformality, COnsistency, COnciseness), a post-hoc framework for turning any NeSy-CBM into a conformal predictor satisfying all three desiderata
D1
–
D3
. The idea is as follows: starting from a label set
\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
and a concept set
\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
, we make them mutually consistent by intersecting the label set (resp. concept set) with
g^{\dagger}
applied to the concept set (resp.
g^{-\dagger}
to the label set).
We show that this requires only one revision step to converge.

Joint Revision Step
. Given
\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
satisfying
Eq.

4
and
\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
satisfying
Eq.

6
,
COCOCO
computes:

\Upsilon^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}):=\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\cap g^{\dagger}(\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})),\qquad\Gamma^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}):=\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\cap g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})).

(10)

In other words,
\Gamma^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
retains only the concepts in
\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
that logically entail some label in
\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
, and
\Upsilon^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
retains only the labels in
\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
that are entailed by some concept in
\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
. We highlight two key structural properties: a
single
application of
Eq.

10
is enough to reconcile the two sets, and the resulting pair is
maximally informative
among all consistent revisions of
(\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}),\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
. Both properties are made formal below.

Proposition 4.1

(One-step consistency and optimality of
COCOCO
)
.

Let
R(\Gamma,\Upsilon):=\bigl(\Gamma\cap g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon),\,\Upsilon\cap g^{\dagger}(\Gamma)\bigr)
denote the joint revision operator. Then, for any
(\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}),\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
:

1.

(One-step fixed point)

R\bigl(R(\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}),\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))\bigr)=R(\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}),\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
, so iterating the revision is unnecessary.

2.

(Optimality)

(\Gamma^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}),\Upsilon^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))=R(\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}),\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
is the
largest
pair
(\Gamma^{\prime},\Upsilon^{\prime})\subseteq\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\times\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
such that
\Gamma^{\prime}\subseteq g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon^{\prime})
and
\Upsilon^{\prime}\subseteq g^{\dagger}(\Gamma^{\prime})
– i.e., each label
\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\Upsilon^{\prime}
is represented by at least a concept
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\Gamma^{\prime}
, and vice-versa, according to the inference layer
g
, making it the largest mutually consistent subset of
(\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}),\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
.

Proposition

4.1
is the operational core of
COCOCO
: a single deduction–abduction pass simultaneously achieves consistency (
D1
) and optimal conciseness (
D3
) given
(\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}),\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
by construction.
Next, we show that this joint revision preserves coverage guarantees (
D2
).

Proposition 4.2

(Marginal coverage of
COCOCO
)
.

For any NeSy CBM
(f,g)
, let
0<\alpha<1
and
0<\beta<1
. If
\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
is a concept conformal set
Eq.

6
and
\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
is a label conformal set
Eq.

4
, then, for every
(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*},\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*})\in\mathrm{supp}(p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{c}},\bm{\mathrm{y}}))
, we have

\displaystyle\Pr(\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}\in\Gamma^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))\geq(1-\alpha)\delta_{\mathrm{ab}}-\beta+\Pr(\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}\notin\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\land\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}\notin\Gamma^{{\sf ab}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})),

(11)

\displaystyle\Pr(\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*}\in\Upsilon^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))\geq(1-\beta)\delta_{\mathrm{de}}-\alpha+\Pr(\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*}\notin\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\land\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*}\notin\Upsilon^{\sf de}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))\,,

(12)

where
\Pr(\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}\notin\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\land\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}\notin\Gamma^{{\sf ab}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
and
\Pr(\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*}\notin\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\land\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*}\notin\Upsilon^{\sf de}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
are the probabilities that the ground-truths are not covered by
neither
the concept/label conformal set
nor
the abductive/deductive conformal set. Moreover, if the NeSy predictor satisfies both deductive soundness and abductive soundness (
Definition

3.1
), the bounds tighten, with
\delta_{\mathrm{ab}}=1=\delta_{\mathrm{de}}
.

Under soundness
(
\delta_{\mathrm{ab}}=\delta_{\mathrm{de}}=1
),
COCOCO
retains coverage at least
1-\alpha-\beta
on both sides, plus a non-negative joint-failure correction, i.e.,
\Pr(\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}\notin\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\land\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}\notin\Gamma^{{\sf ab}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
or
\Pr(\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*}\notin\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\land\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*}\notin\Upsilon^{\sf de}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
. This correction term is empirically estimable from a held-out set and is small in practice (
\leq 0.08
across our datasets, see
Appendix

F
). When soundness degrades – e.g., on incomplete-knowledge datasets like
RIV10
– the bounds worsen according to the quality of the inference layer (
\delta
).

User-specified size budgets
.
Standard CP fixes
\alpha,\beta
a priori and yields the corresponding set sizes. In practice, specifically in decision-support systems
[
23
,
75
,
74
]
, it is often more useful to fix a
size budget
(“at most
5
candidate concepts”) and report the corresponding coverage, thereby avoiding overwhelming practitioners with a range of choices. As shown by
31
, this can be achieved by replacing standard CP with
conformal e-predictions

[
87
,
30
,
31
,
32
]
, which use
e-values

[
71
,
86
,
38
,
67
]
in place of quantiles.
E-values bring two benefits to
COCOCO
. First, we can provably build a conformal set over concepts
while avoiding Bonferroni’s correction
(
Corollary

C.4
), meaning that the resulting concept-set guarantee does
not
degrade with the number of concepts
k
(
Proposition

C.9
).
Second, e-values allow data-dependent miscoverage levels
\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\beta}
to be chosen
after
observing calibration scores, in particular to satisfy user-specified size budgets while retaining a coverage guarantee
in expectation

[
67
,
87
]
. We denote this variant
COCOCO
∗
, and prove its joint-coverage guarantee in
Section

C.10
. Details about e-values and
COCOCO
∗
are left to
Section

D.3
due to space constraints.

Runtime
.
A key advantage of
COCOCO
is that the joint revision operator reaches a fixed point in a single step (
Proposition

4.1
), so the additional overhead amounts to computing the abductive and deductive images once, i.e.,
\Gamma^{{\sf ab}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
and
\Upsilon^{\sf de}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=g^{\dagger}(\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
. The main computational bottleneck lies in constructing the concept set
\bm{\mathrm{\Gamma}}^{\times}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
, which scales exponentially in the number of concepts
k
due to the cross-product structure. This issue is mitigated when full concept annotations are available or if
COCOCO
∗
is employed due to
e-values
, in which cases
\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
can be obtained carrying out any set product. In practice, we find that the runtime of
COCOCO
does not exceed
1.5\times
that of standard
DPL
and
LTN
, which is acceptable in high-stakes settings where reliability is crucial. A detailed runtime analysis is deferred to
Section

F.4
due to space constraints.

7
Conclusion

We introduced
COCOCO
, a post-hoc conformal framework for NeSy-CBMs that jointly conformalizes concept and label predictions and reconciles them via a
single
deduction–abduction revision step. This step provably yields the largest logically consistent revision of the two sets and reaches a fixed point in one pass (
Proposition

4.1
). The framework retains distribution-free coverage that degrades controllably under imperfect knowledge, and – combined with e-values – supports user-specified size budgets. To our knowledge,
COCOCO
is the first method that exploits symbolic knowledge to
simultaneously
shrink conformal prediction sets and enforce label–concept consistency while retaining distribution-free coverage. Empirically,
COCOCO
consistently attains target coverage while reducing concept-set size on eight benchmarks.

Limitations and Future Work
. Abduction and deduction require enumerating
g^{\dagger}(\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
and
g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
, while
\bm{\mathrm{\Gamma}}^{\times}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
requires to compute the product over a possibly large number of concepts. This becomes a bottleneck for very large concept spaces (e.g.,
BDD-OIA

[
90
,
12
]
). Similarly, regarding inference time, the inference layer
g
is called multiple times; while knowledge-compiled circuits
[
19
,
15
,
84
,
53
,
25
]
(as in
DPL
) make this tractable in practice.
The product-set
\bm{\mathrm{\Gamma}}^{\times}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
– if the cross-product construction is used – assume conditional independence (Proposition
C.9
), inherited from standard NeSyCBMs
[
81
,
82
]
. Importantly, the
COCOCO
revision step itself does
not
require independence: it operates on any concept set satisfying
Eq.

6
. Plugging in expressive concept distributions
[
81
,
82
]
is a direct extension.
As future work, we plan to extend
COCOCO
to a broader range of NeSy-CBMs architectures, leveraging, once mature, recent initiatives that aim to make NeSy-AI more accessible and easier to adopt for practitioners, such as
DeepLog

[
25
]
and
ULLER

[
80
]
, and to architectures that
learn
the knowledge from data
[

]
, where soundness can no longer be assumed a priori and must be estimated, affecting the bounds of

Proposition C.1

(Deductive soundness implies abductive soundness)
.

For any NeSy-CBM
(f,g)
, deductive soundness implies abductive soundness (
Definition

3.1
). Consequently, a model that satisfies deductive soundness but not abductive soundness cannot exist.

Proof.

Suppose the NeSy-CBM satisfies deductive soundness, i.e.,
g^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*})=\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*}
for every
(\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*},\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*})
in
\operatorname{supp}(p^{*})
. Then
g^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*})=\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*}
means
\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}
belongs to
g^{-\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*})=\{\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\mathcal{C}:\argmax g(\bm{\mathrm{c}})=\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*}\}
, which is exactly abductive soundness (
Definition

3.1
).
∎

Proposition C.2

(Abductive soundness implies
\delta_{\mathrm{ab}}=1
)
.

Let
\Gamma^{{\sf ab}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}):=g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))

15

\delta_{\mathrm{ab}}:=\Pr\bigl(\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}\in\Gamma^{{\sf ab}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\mid\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*}\in\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\bigr)=1.

(18)

Proof.

From
Definition

3.1
,
abductive soundness ensures that
\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}\in g^{-\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*})
for every
(\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*},\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*})
in the support of
p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{c}},\bm{\mathrm{y}})
. Suppose
\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*}\in\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
.
Because
\Gamma^{{\sf ab}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))=\bigcup_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})}g^{-\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{y}})
and

\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}\in g^{-\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*})\subseteq\Gamma^{{\sf ab}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})

Hence, the event
\{\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}\in\Gamma^{{\sf ab}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\}
holds surely whenever
\{\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*}\in\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\}

giving

\Pr(\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}\in\Gamma^{{\sf ab}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\mid\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*}\in\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))=1.

(19)

∎

Proposition C.3

(Deductive soundness implies
\delta_{\mathrm{de}}=1

.

Let
\Upsilon^{\sf de}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}):=g^{\dagger}(\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))

Deductive soundness states that

for every

in the support of

\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}\in\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})

\displaystyle\Pr(B)

\displaystyle\Pr(B)

For any NeSy CBM

(42)

where
\delta_{\mathrm{de}}:=\Pr(\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*}\in\Upsilon^{\sf de}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\mid\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}\in\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
is the probability that deduction recovers the ground-truth labels
\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*}
given that the concept set
\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
contains the correct concepts
\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}
. Moreover, if the NeSy-CBM satisfies deductive soundness (
Definition

3.1
), the bound tightens to:

\Pr(\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*}\in\Upsilon^{\sf de}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))\geq 1-\beta.

(43)

Proof.

Let the two binary events
A=(\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}\in\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
and
B=(\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*}\in\Upsilon^{\sf de}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
.
We first derive the lower bound. By the law of total probability
Eq.

24

\displaystyle\Pr(B)

\displaystyle=\Pr(B\mid A)\Pr(A)+\Pr(B\mid\neg A)\Pr(\neg A)

(44)

\displaystyle\geq\Pr(B\mid A)\Pr(A)

(45)

Since
\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
is a conformal concept set with miscoverage
\beta
by
Eq.

17
, we have that

\Pr(A)=\Pr(\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}\in\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))\geq 1-\beta.

(46)

We can plug this result into the previous one, i.e.,
\Pr(B)\geq\Pr(B\mid A)\Pr(A)
(
Eq.

45
), to obtain

\displaystyle\Pr(B)

\displaystyle\geq\Pr(B\mid A)(1-\beta)

(47)

Which proofs the lower-bound:

\displaystyle\Pr(\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*}\in\Upsilon^{\sf de}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))

\displaystyle\geq\Pr(\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*}\in\Upsilon^{\sf de}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\mid\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}\in\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))(1-\beta).

(48)

Next, we prove the upper bound. Consider again the right-hand side of
Eq.

44
. Since
\Pr(B\mid\neg A)\leq 1
and
\Pr(\neg A)\leq\beta
, we have:

\Pr(B\mid\neg A)\Pr(\neg A)\leq\beta.

(49)

Moreover,
\Pr(A)\leq 1
. Hence,

\Pr(B\mid A)\Pr(A)\leq\Pr(B\mid A).

(50)

Combining these observations yields:

\displaystyle\Pr(B)

\displaystyle=\Pr(B\mid A)\Pr(A)+\Pr(B\mid\neg A)\Pr(\neg A)

(Law of total probability -
Eq.

24
)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr(B\mid A)+\Pr(B\mid\neg A)\Pr(\neg A)\quad

(Substitute the left term -
Eq.

50
)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr(B\mid A)+\beta\quad

(Substitute the right term -
Eq.

49
)

Substituting the expression for
A
and
B
, we have the upper bound

\Pr(\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*}\in\Upsilon^{\sf de}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))\leq\beta+\delta_{\mathrm{de}}

(51)

Under
deductive soundness
(
Definition

3.1
), by
Proposition

3.3
, we have
\delta_{\mathrm{de}}=1
. Hence, we get

\Pr(B)\geq\delta_{\mathrm{de}}(1-\beta)\geq 1-\beta.

(52)

While for the upper bound
Eq.

49
:

\Pr(B)\leq\delta_{\mathrm{de}}+\beta\leq\min(1,\delta_{\mathrm{de}}+\beta).

(53)

This concludes the proof.

∎

C.5
Coverage proofs of
COCOCO
concept set (
Proposition

4.2
)

Proposition

(Marginal coverage of
\Gamma^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
(
Proposition

4.2
))
.

For
0<\alpha<1
and
0<\beta<1
, and for any
(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*},\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*})
in the support of
p^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{c}},\bm{\mathrm{y}})
, suppose
\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
satisfies
Eq.

16
and
\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
satisfies
Eq.

17
. Let
\Upsilon^{\sf de}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}):=g^{\dagger}(\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
and
\Gamma^{{\sf ab}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}):=g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
. Define the
refined concept set
as
\Gamma^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}):=\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\cap\Gamma^{{\sf ab}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
. Then

\displaystyle\Pr(\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}\in\Gamma^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))

\displaystyle\geq(1-\alpha)\,\Pr(\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}\in\Gamma^{{\sf ab}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\mid\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*}\in\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))-\beta

\displaystyle\quad+\Pr(\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}\notin\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\land\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}\notin\Gamma^{{\sf ab}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))

(54)

Proof.

Let
A:=(\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}\in\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
and
B:=(\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}\in\Gamma^{{\sf ab}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
, so that

\Pr(\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}\in\Gamma^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))=\Pr(A\cap B).

(55)

By the inclusion-exclusion identity, we have

\Pr(A\cap B)=\Pr(A)+\Pr(B)-\Pr(A\cup B).

(56)

Since
\Pr(A\cup B)=1-\Pr(\neg A\cap\neg B)
, this is equivalent to

\Pr(A\cap B)=\Pr(A)+\Pr(B)-1+\Pr(\neg A\cap\neg B).

(57)

We know that
\Pr(A)\geq 1-\beta
follows from
Eq.

17
, and that
\Pr(B)\geq(1-\alpha)\delta_{\mathrm{ab}}
follows from
Proposition

3.2
. Therefore, we can proceed to obtain the lower bound as follows:

\displaystyle\Pr(\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}\in\Gamma^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))

\displaystyle=\Pr(A\cap B)

(def. of
\Gamma^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
)

\displaystyle=\Pr(A)+\Pr(B)-1+\Pr(\neg A\cap\neg B)

(inclusion–exclusion
Eq.

57
)

\displaystyle\geq(1-\beta)+\Pr(B)-1+\Pr(\neg A\cap\neg B)

(
Eq.

17
)

\displaystyle\geq(1-\beta)+(1-\alpha)\delta_{\mathrm{ab}}-1+\Pr(\neg A\cap\neg B)

(
Proposition

3.2
)

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\delta_{\mathrm{ab}}-\beta+\Pr(\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}\notin\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\land\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}\notin\Gamma^{{\sf ab}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})).

(subst.
\Pr(\neg A\cap\neg B)
)

Under
abductive soundness
(
Definition

3.1
), we have

\delta_{\mathrm{ab}}=1
.
Hence, we get

\Pr(\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}\in\Gamma^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))\geq 1-\alpha-\beta+\Pr(\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}\notin\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\land\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}\notin\Gamma^{{\sf ab}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})).

(58)

This concludes the proof.

∎

.

\Pr(\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}\notin\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\land\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}\notin\Gamma^{{\sf ab}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))

\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}
is not captured by
neither
the concept conformal set (
\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
)
nor
the abduced concept set (
\Gamma^{{\sf ab}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
).

C.7
Fixed point proof of
COCOCO
(
Proposition

C.7
)

To prove that
COCOCO
does not require further iterations, let
R
be the operator that
revises
the label and concept sets. Formally:

R:(\Gamma,\Upsilon)\mapsto(R(\Gamma),R(\Upsilon))\subseteq\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{Y},\qquad R(\Gamma,\Upsilon):=\bigl(\Gamma\cap g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon),\Upsilon\cap g^{\dagger}(\Gamma)\bigr).

(64)

By construction,
COCOCO
applies
R

only once
to the pair
(\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}),\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
, producing

(\Gamma^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}),\Upsilon^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))=R(\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}),\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))

(65)

Before introducing the main proposition, we show two useful lemmas.

Lemma C.5

(Set behavior of
g^{-\dagger}
on intersection)
.

For any
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
,

g^{-\dagger}(\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{B})\;\subseteq\;g^{-\dagger}(\mathcal{A})\cap g^{-\dagger}(\mathcal{B}).

(66)

The reverse inclusion does
not
hold in general for
g^{\dagger}
.

Proof.

Let
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in g^{-\dagger}(\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{B})
, there exists
\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in g^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})\cap(\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{B})
. Then
\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in g^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})\cap\mathcal{A}
, so
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in g^{-\dagger}(\mathcal{A})
, and likewise
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in g^{-\dagger}(\mathcal{B})
. Hence
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in g^{-\dagger}(\mathcal{A})\cap g^{-\dagger}(\mathcal{B})
.

The reverse inclusion fails when ties are present: if
g^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})=\{y_{1},y_{2}\}
with
y_{1}\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{B}
and
y_{2}\in\mathcal{B}\setminus\mathcal{A}
, then
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in g^{-\dagger}(\mathcal{A})\cap g^{-\dagger}(\mathcal{B})
but
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\notin g^{-\dagger}(\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{B})
.
∎

Lemma C.6
.

For any
\Gamma^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
,

\Gamma^{\prime}\subseteq g^{-\dagger}\bigl(g^{\dagger}(\Gamma^{\prime})\bigr).

(67)

Proof.

Let
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\Gamma^{\prime}
. By definition of the image,
g^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})\in g^{\dagger}(\Gamma^{\prime})
. By
Eq.

15
,

g^{-\dagger}\bigl(g^{\dagger}(\Gamma^{\prime})\bigr)=\bigcup_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in g^{\dagger}(\Gamma^{\prime})}\{\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}:\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\argmax g(\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{\prime})\},

(68)

so for any
\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
,

g^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{\prime})\in g^{\dagger}(\Gamma^{\prime})\implies\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{\prime}\in g^{-\dagger}\bigl(g^{\dagger}(\Gamma^{\prime})\bigr).

(69)

In particular,
\Gamma^{\prime}\subseteq\bm{\mathrm{g}}^{-\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{g}}^{\dagger}(\Gamma^{\prime}))
.
∎

Now we can proceed with the main proposition.

Proposition C.7

(
COCOCO
reaches a fixed point)
.

For a NeSy-CBM
(f,g)
, let
R
be defined as
Eq.

64
. Then, for any
\Gamma\subseteq\mathcal{C}
and
\Upsilon\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
,

R\bigl(R(\Gamma,\Upsilon)\bigr)=R(\Gamma,\Upsilon)\,.

(70)

In particular,
(\Gamma^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}),\Upsilon^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
is a
fixed point
of
R
, and no iterative refinement is needed.

Proof.

Let
(\Gamma_{1},\Upsilon_{1})=R(\Gamma,\Upsilon)
. By definition of
R
, these are

\Gamma_{1}=\Gamma\cap g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon),\qquad\Upsilon_{1}=\Upsilon\cap g^{\dagger}(\Gamma)\,.

(71)

Now, denote with
(\Gamma_{2},\Upsilon_{2})
the sets obtained by applying
R
twice on
\Gamma,\Upsilon
, namely

(\Gamma_{2},\Upsilon_{2})=R(\Gamma_{1},\Upsilon_{1})=R(R(\Gamma,\Upsilon))\,,

(72)

which are given by

\Gamma_{2}=\Gamma_{1}\cap g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon_{1}),\qquad\Upsilon_{2}=\Upsilon_{1}\cap g^{\dagger}(\Gamma_{1}).

(73)

Now, to proceed with the proof, we use the standard set identity: for any two sets
S,T
,

S\subseteq T\iff S\cap T=S.

(74)

This means that, following
Eq.

74
:

\displaystyle(I)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2}=\Gamma_{1}\iff\Gamma_{1}\subseteq g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon_{1})=g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon\cap g^{\dagger}(\Gamma))

(75)

\displaystyle(II)

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{2}=\Upsilon_{1}\iff\Upsilon_{1}\subseteq g^{\dagger}(\Gamma_{1})=g^{\dagger}(\Gamma\cap g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon)).

(76)

To prove the fixed point, we prove these two inclusions in turn.

Proof of (I):
\Gamma_{1}\subseteq g^{-\dagger}\bigl(\Upsilon\cap g^{\dagger}(\Gamma)\bigr)
.

Let
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\Gamma_{1}
, this means that, since
\Gamma_{1}=\Gamma\cap g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon)
,
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\Gamma
and
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon)
. We prove
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon_{1})
, i.e., that
g^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})\cap\Upsilon_{1}\neq\varnothing
.

By definition of
\Gamma_{1}=\Gamma\cap g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon)
, the concept
\bm{\mathrm{c}}
satisfies

(a)

\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\Gamma
, hence
g^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})\subseteq g^{\dagger}(\Gamma)
;

(b)

\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon)
, hence
g^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})\cap\Upsilon\neq\varnothing
.

Now, let
\bm{\mathrm{y}}
be any
\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in(g^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})\cap\Upsilon)\neq\varnothing
from (b). By (a),
\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in g^{\dagger}(\Gamma)
as well. Therefore

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in g^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})\cap\Upsilon\cap g^{\dagger}(\Gamma)

(77)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in g^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})\cap\Upsilon_{1}

(from
\Upsilon_{1}=\Upsilon\cap g^{\dagger}(\Gamma)
)

since
g^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})\cap\Upsilon_{1}\neq\varnothing
, this concludes the proof of the first inclusion (I)
Eq.

75
.

Proof of (II):
\Upsilon_{1}\subseteq g^{\dagger}(\Gamma_{1})\subseteq g^{\dagger}\bigl(\Gamma\cap g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon)\bigr)
.
This proof does not follow the steps of (I):
unlike
g^{-\dagger}
, the image
g^{\dagger}
does not distribute over intersection in general, so we cannot mirror the argument above. We proceed elementwise.

Let
\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\Upsilon_{1}=\Upsilon\cap g^{\dagger}(\Gamma)
. Then:

•

\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\Upsilon
, and

•

\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in g^{\dagger}(\Gamma)
, so by definition of the image there exists some
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\Gamma
such that
\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in g^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})
.

Let us then fix
\bm{\mathrm{c}}
such that
\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in g^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})
.Now, we verify that
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\Gamma_{1}=\Gamma\cap g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon)
:

•

\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\Gamma
holds by the choice of
\bm{\mathrm{c}}
;

•

g^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})=\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\Upsilon
, so by the definition of
Eq.

15
:

g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon)=\bigcup_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{C}}\{\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\Gamma:\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\argmax g(\bm{\mathrm{c}})\}

(78)

We can conclude that
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon)
.

Hence
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\Gamma_{1}
and
g^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})=\bm{\mathrm{y}}
, which means
\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in g^{\dagger}(\Gamma_{1})
, proving the second inclusion (II)
Eq.

76
.

Conclusion
. By (I),
\Gamma_{2}=\Gamma_{1}\cap g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon_{1})=\Gamma_{1}
. By (II),
\Upsilon_{2}=\Upsilon_{1}\cap g^{\dagger}(\Gamma_{1})=\Upsilon_{1}
. Therefore
R\bigl(R(\Gamma,\Upsilon)\bigr)=(\Gamma_{1},\Upsilon_{1})=R(\Gamma,\Upsilon)
, i.e.
(\Gamma_{1},\Upsilon_{1})
is a fixed point of
R
.

This concludes the proof.
∎

C.8
Proof of the Optimality of
COCOCO

Proposition C.8

(Optimality of
COCOCO
)
.

Let
\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\subseteq\mathcal{C}
and
\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
be arbitrary prediction sets, and let
(\Gamma^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}),\Upsilon^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))=R(\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}),\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
be their joint revision.

Then
(\Gamma^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}),\Upsilon^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
is the largest pair
(\Gamma^{\prime},\Upsilon^{\prime})\subseteq\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{Y}
satisfying:

1.

Containment:

\Gamma^{\prime}\subseteq\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
and
\Upsilon^{\prime}\subseteq\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
.

2.

Mutual coverage:

(a)

every concept entails at least one retained label:

\forall\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\Gamma^{\prime},\;g^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})\cap\Upsilon^{\prime}\neq\varnothing

(equivalently,
\Gamma^{\prime}\subseteq g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon^{\prime})
);

(b)

every label is entailed by at least one retained concept:

\forall\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\Upsilon^{\prime},\;\exists\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\Gamma^{\prime}\text{ with }\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in g^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})

(equivalently,
\Upsilon^{\prime}\subseteq g^{\dagger}(\Gamma^{\prime})
).

Consequently,

|\Gamma^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})|\leq|\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})|,\qquad\qquad|\Upsilon^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})|\leq|\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})|.

(79)

Moreover,
(\Gamma^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}),\Upsilon^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
is a minimal revision of
(\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}),\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
that enforces logical consistency with
g
.

Proof.

Step 1:
(\Gamma^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}),\Upsilon^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
itself satisfies (1) and (2)
.

By definition,

\Gamma^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\cap g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))\subseteq\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})

(80)

and

\Upsilon^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\cap g^{\dagger}(\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))\subseteq\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}),

(81)

therefore (1) holds.

Mutual coverage (2a):
\Gamma^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\subseteq g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
.

By definition,
(\Gamma^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}),\Upsilon^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))=R(\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}),\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
, so
\Gamma^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\cap g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
plays the role of
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Upsilon^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\cap g^{\dagger}(\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
plays the role of
\Upsilon_{1}
in the proof of
Proposition

C.7
. Inclusion (I) of that proof established
\Gamma_{1}\subseteq g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon_{1})
; specialising to the present sets gives
\Gamma^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\subseteq g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
.

Mutual coverage (2b):
\Upsilon^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\subseteq g^{\dagger}(\Gamma^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
.

Recall that
\Upsilon^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\cap g^{\dagger}(\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
and
\Gamma^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\cap g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
.

Let
\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\Upsilon^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
.
Since
\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in g^{\dagger}(\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
, by definition of the image there is some
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
with
\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in g^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})
. This
\bm{\mathrm{c}}
lies in
\Gamma^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
by definition of
\Gamma^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
. Using
\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\Upsilon^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\subseteq\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
from (1),

\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in g^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})\cap\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\;\neq\;\varnothing\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})),

(82)

hence
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\cap g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))=\Gamma^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
.

Step 2: Any pair satisfying (1) and (2) is contained in
(\Gamma^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}),\Upsilon^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
.

Let
(\Gamma^{\prime},\Upsilon^{\prime})
satisfy (1) and (2). We must show that
\Gamma^{\prime}\subseteq\Gamma^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
and
\Upsilon^{\prime}\subseteq\Upsilon^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
.

Show
\Gamma^{\prime}\subseteq\Gamma^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
.
Let
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\Gamma^{\prime}
. We prove
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\Gamma^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
by
checking the two conditions defining
\Gamma^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\cap g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
.

(i)

\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
: this follows directly from
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\Gamma^{\prime}\subseteq\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
by (1).

(ii)

\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
: by definition, this requires

g^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})\cap\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\neq\varnothing
. From (2a),

g^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})\cap\Upsilon^{\prime}\neq\varnothing
, and from (1),
\Upsilon^{\prime}\subseteq\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
.
Hence

\varnothing\;\neq\;g^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})\cap\Upsilon^{\prime}\;\subseteq\;g^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})\cap\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}).

(83)

Combining (i) and (ii) gives
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\cap g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))=\Gamma^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
.

Show
\Upsilon^{\prime}\subseteq\Upsilon^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
.
Let
\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\Upsilon^{\prime}
. We prove
\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\Upsilon^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
by
checking the two conditions defining
\Upsilon^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\cap g^{\dagger}(\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
.

(i)

\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
: this follows directly from
\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\Upsilon^{\prime}\subseteq\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
by (1).

(ii)

\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in g^{\dagger}(\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))

\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
with
\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in g^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})

\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\Gamma^{\prime}

with
\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in g^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})

\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\Gamma^{\prime}\subseteq\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
.

Combining (i) and (ii) gives
\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\cap g^{\dagger}(\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))=\Upsilon^{{\sf rev}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
.

This concludes the proof.

C.9
Coverage proof of Product Set leveraging e-values

Proposition C.9

(Concept coverage via aggregated e-values)
.

For
0<\beta<1
and any
(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*})\in\mathcal{D}_{\bm{\mathrm{C}}}
,
let
\bm{\mathrm{c}}=(\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1},\dots,\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{K})\in\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{C}_{K}
be a concept vector, where each
\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{j}\in\{0,\dots,V-1\}
. For each concept
j
, let
s_{j}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{C}_{j}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a non-negative conformity score and let

\mathcal{D}_{j}=\{(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i},\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{ij}^{*})\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a calibration set.
Define the
soft-rank e-value

[
88
]
for concept
j
and candidate
v
as

E_{j}(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{v}})=\frac{(n+1)\,s_{j}(\bm{\mathrm{x}},v)}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{j}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i},c_{ij}^{*})+s_{j}(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{v}})}.

(84)

the
joint e-value
for a candidate tuple
\bm{\mathrm{v}}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{K})
as

E(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{v}})=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}E_{j}(\bm{\mathrm{x}},v_{j}).

(85)

and the
concept prediction set
for threshold
\beta>0
as

\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\left\{\bm{\mathrm{v}}\in\bigtimes_{j=1}^{K}\{0,\dots,V-1\}:E(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{v}})<\frac{1}{\beta}\right\}.

(86)

Then:

\Pr\left(\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}\in\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\right)\geq 1-\beta.

(87)

In particular, this guarantee holds without Bonferroni correction (
Corollary

C.4
) and does not degrade with the number of concepts
K
.

Proof.

Step 1: each
E_{j}(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{j}^{*})
is a valid e-variable
.
By exchangeability of the calibration data and the test point, each soft-rank e-value satisfies
\mathbb{E}[E_{j}(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{j}^{*})]\leq 1

87
,
30
.

Step 2: the average
E(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{c}})=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}E_{j}(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{j})
is a valid e-variable
.
By linearity of expectation:

\mathbb{E}\left[E(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*})\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}E_{j}(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{j}^{*})\right]=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\!\left[E_{j}(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{j}^{*})\right]\leq\frac{1}{K}\cdot K=1,

(88)

where the inequality uses Step 1 termwise. Hence

is a valid e-variable.

Step 3: Coverage follows by Markov’s inequality
[
67
]
.
Since
E(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{c}})\geq 0
and
\mathbb{E}[E(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{c}})]\leq 1
, Markov’s inequality gives:

\Pr\left(E(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{c}})\geq\frac{1}{\beta}\right)\leq\beta,

or equivalently,

\Pr\left(E(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{c}})<\frac{1}{\beta}\right)\geq 1-\beta.

(90)

Since
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
if and only if
E(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{c}})<1/\beta
, the coverage guarantee follows.
∎

Remark

D.2
Metrics details

For all datasets, we evaluate predictions at both the label and concept levels using three metrics,
coverage
,
size
, and
consistency
, defined below.

Coverage
. Coverage is defined as:

\textsc{Coverage}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{test}}|}\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{x}}\in\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{test}}}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}\in\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\},

(96)

where
\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{test}}
denotes the test set and
|\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{test}}|
its cardinality. This metric quantifies the proportion of test samples for which the ground-truth concept
\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}
is included in the predicted concept set
\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
. An analogous definition applies at the label level by replacing
\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{*}
and
\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
with
\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*}
and
\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
, respectively.

Size
. We measure the average size of the prediction sets as

\textsc{Size}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{test}}|}\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{x}}\in\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{test}}}|\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})|,

(97)

which corresponds to the expected cardinality of the predicted concept sets. The same definition applies to label sets
\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
.

Consistency
.
Concept-level consistency is defined as

\textsc{Consistency}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{test}}|}\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{x}}\in\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{test}}}\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})}\frac{\mathbbm{1}\!\{g^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})\cap\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\neq\varnothing\}}{\max(1,|\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})|)},

(98)

this metric measures the extent to which predicted concepts agree with predicted labels. Specifically, it captures the average fraction of concepts in
\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
whose induced label
g^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{c}})
is contained in
\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})
. A fully consistent model ensures that every predicted concept is supported by at least one compatible label. An analogous definition applies at the label level.

D.3
E-values details

A powerful and recent tool in the conformal prediction literature is conformal e-prediction, which uses
e-values

[
71
,
86
,
38
,
67
]
to build prediction sets
[
87
,
30
,
31
,
32
]
.
An
e-variable

E
is a nonnegative random variable satisfying
\mathbb{E}[E]\leq 1

[
67
]
.
Thresholding
E
at level
1/\alpha
yields a valid prediction set with marginal coverage at least
1-\alpha
: by Markov’s inequality,
\Pr(E\geq 1/\alpha)\leq\alpha
, or equivalently,
\Pr(E<1/\alpha)\geq 1-\alpha
.

A standard e-value for conformal prediction is the
soft-rank e-variable

[
88
,
44
,
8
]
.
Given a non-negative nonconformity score
s:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and a calibration set
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}=\{(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i},\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{i}^{*})\}_{i=1}^{n}

E(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}})=\frac{(n+1)\,s(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}})}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}s(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i},\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{i}^{*})+s(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}})}.

(99)

This is a valid e-variable whenever the calibration scores
\{s(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i},\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{i}^{*})\}_{i=1}^{n}
and the test score
s(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}})
are exchangeable
[
87
]
.

Proposition D.1

(
30
)
.

Consider a calibration set
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cal}}=\{(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i},\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{i}^{*})\}_{i=1}^{n}
and a test point
(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}}^{*})
such that all

points are exchangeable.
Let
\tilde{\alpha}
be any (possibly data-dependent) miscoverage level. Then:

(101)

Remark
.

When

D.4.1
Inference

We begin by describing how inference is carried out at test time in

and

p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathrm{C}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{x}})

DeepProbLog
. We use the

12

p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathrm{Y}},\bm{\mathrm{C}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{x}},\mathsf{K})=p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathrm{C}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{X}})\mathbbm{1}\!\{(\bm{\mathrm{C}},\bm{\mathrm{Y}})\models\mathsf{K}\}.

(109)

Hard predictions are obtained by solving the MAP problem, which is NP-hard
[
72
]
, that is:

(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{c}}},\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}})=\argmax_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\mathcal{C},\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathrm{C}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{x}})\mathbbm{1}\!\{(\bm{\mathrm{C}},\bm{\mathrm{Y}})\models\mathsf{K}\}

(110)

where

p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathrm{C}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\prod_{j}^{K}p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathrm{C}}_{j}\mid\bm{\mathrm{x}})

(111)

Namely,
p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathrm{C}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{x}})
factorizes over all the
K
concepts due to conditional independence
[
55
]
.

Logic Tensor Networks
. To implement

, we used the
ltn-torch
library
[
14
]
, following the approach of
12
. Given a formula
\mathsf{K}
and concept distribution
p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathrm{C}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{x}})
, each candidate label
\bm{\mathrm{y}}
is scored according to the degree to which the pair
(\bm{\mathrm{c}},\bm{\mathrm{y}})
satisfies
\mathsf{K}
under the chosen fuzzy relaxation.

We experiment with three t-(co)norms:

•

Gödel semantics

\mathcal{T}_{G}(a,b)=\min(a,b),\quad\mathcal{S}_{G}(a,b)=\max(a,b),\quad\mathcal{N}_{G}(a)=\mathbbm{1}\!\{a=0\}

(112)

•

Product semantics

\mathcal{T}_{P}(a,b)=a\cdot b,\quad\mathcal{S}_{P}(a,b)=a+b-a\cdot b,\quad\mathcal{N}_{P}(a)=1-a

(113)

•

Łukasiewicz semantics

\mathcal{T}_{L}(a,b)=\max(a+b-1,0),\quad\mathcal{S}_{L}(a,b)=\min(a+b,1),\quad\mathcal{N}_{L}(a)=1-a

(114)

Where
\mathcal{T}
denotes a
t-norm
,
\mathcal{S}
denotes a
t-conorm
,
\mathcal{N}
denotes the
negation operation
, and
a
and
b
are fuzzy truth values in
[0,1]
.

The inference layer selects the label:

\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}=\argmax_{\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{K}}\left(p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathrm{C}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{x}}),\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{Y}}=\bm{\mathrm{y}}\}\right)

(115)

where
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{K}}\in[0,1]
denotes the fuzzy satisfaction degree.

In practice, inference is performed by first computing:

\hat{\bm{\mathrm{c}}}=\argmax_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in\mathcal{C}}p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathrm{C}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{x}})

(116)

and then predicting the final label leveraging prior knowledge
\mathsf{K}
:

\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}=\mathsf{K}(\hat{\bm{\mathrm{c}}})

(117)

D.4.2
Grounding

For each dataset, we detail how the prior knowledge
\mathsf{K}
is grounded in
DPL
and
LTN
. In
DPL
,
\mathsf{K}
is compiled into a
concept-to-label
matrix
W\in[0,1]^{|\mathcal{C}|\times|\mathcal{Y}|}
, where each entry
W_{\bm{\mathrm{c}},\bm{\mathrm{y}}}
represents the probability of
(\bm{\mathrm{c}},\bm{\mathrm{y}})\models\mathsf{K}
. The label distribution is then computed by
p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathrm{Y}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{x}})=W^{\top}p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathrm{C}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{x}})
. In
LTN
,
\mathsf{K}
is expressed as a first-order logic formula whose predicates are grounded on the neural outputs
p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathrm{c}}\mid\bm{\mathrm{x}})\in[0,1]
, while logical connectives are interpreted using a chosen t-norm (and corresponding co-tnorm), as detailed in
Section

E.2
.

MN-Add
(and
MN-EO
,
MN-H
)

Each input consists of two images
(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{2})
with associated digit variables
\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}\in\{0,\dots,9\}
and output
\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\{0,\dots,18\}

DPL

: The circuit enumerates all
10^{2}
digit combinations and sets

W_{(\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}),\,\bm{\mathrm{y}}}=\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}+\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2}=\bm{\mathrm{y}}\}
, so that each concept vector
\bm{\mathrm{c}}
maps deterministically to exactly one sum.

LTN

: The knowledge base is

\mathsf{K}_{{\tt MN-Add}}:=\forall\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{2},\bm{\mathrm{y}}.\exists d_{1},d_{2}.\bigl(\mathrm{digit}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{1},d_{1})\land\mathrm{digit}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{2},d_{2})\bigr)\text{ s.t. }d_{1}+d_{2}=\bm{\mathrm{y}},

(118)

where
\mathrm{digit}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i},d_{i}):=p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{i}=d\mid\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i})
is the digit probability.

MN-SP
.
The setup is as in
MN-Add
, but with
\bm{\mathrm{Y}}\in\{0,1\}
encoding the parity of the sum.

MN-Add

CHX
.
The concepts are the four binary symptoms
\bm{\mathrm{c}}=(\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2},\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{3},\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{4})\in{0,1}^{4}

, corresponding to the five severity levels
{\texttt{healthy},\texttt{green},\texttt{yellow},\texttt{orange},\texttt{red}}
.

LTN

: We instead formulate the knowledge base in first-order logic. Let
\mathrm{present}(\bm{\mathrm{x}},i):=p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{i}=1\mid\bm{\mathrm{x}})
. The knowledge base is then defined as follows:

\mathsf{K}^{\mathrm{mc}}_{{\tt CHX}}:=\forall\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}}.\ \exists s_{1},s_{2},s_{3},s_{4}\in\{0,1\}.\bigwedge_{i=1}^{4}\bigl(s_{i}\iff\mathrm{present}(\bm{\mathrm{x}},i)\bigr)\text{ s.t. }\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{4}s_{i}=\bm{\mathrm{y}}.

(119)

CEB
. Recall that
\bm{\mathrm{c}}\in{\texttt{Positive},\texttt{Negative},\texttt{Neutral},\texttt{unknown}}^{5}
and the label
\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in{\texttt{Positive},\texttt{Negative},\texttt{Neutral},\texttt{unknown},\texttt{conflict}}
is defined as the majority vote with the tie-breaking rule described in
Section

D.5
.

DPL

: The circuit enumerates all
4^{5}
possible concept combinations. For each combination, let
N_{v}:=\sum_{i=1}^{5}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{i}=v\}
denote the count of each value
v\in{\texttt{Positive},\texttt{Negative},\texttt{Neutral},\texttt{unknown}}
. Then

W_{\bm{\mathrm{c}},\,y}=\begin{cases}\mathbbm{1}\!\{N_{\texttt{Pos}}=N_{\texttt{Neg}}\,\land\,N_{\texttt{Pos}}\geq\max(N_{\texttt{Neu}},N_{\texttt{Unk}})\}&\bm{\mathrm{y}}=\texttt{Conflict},\\[2.0pt]
\mathbbm{1}\!\{N_{v}\geq\max_{v^{\prime}\neq v}N_{v^{\prime}}\}\cdot\big(1-W_{\bm{\mathrm{c}},\texttt{Conflict}}\big)&\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\{\texttt{Pos},\texttt{Neg},\texttt{Neu},\texttt{Unk}\},\end{cases}

(120)

where the tie-breaking priority
\texttt{Pos}\succ\texttt{Neg}\succ\texttt{Neu}\succ\texttt{Unk}
is enforced by evaluating the cases in order and assigning exactly one label per concept combination.

LTN

: The discrete
\argmax
rule is not directly differentiable. We therefore adopt a relaxed formulation based on
soft counts

\tilde{N}v(\bm{\mathrm{x}}):=\sum{i=1}^{5}p_{\theta}(\bm{\mathrm{C}}_{i}=v\mid\bm{\mathrm{x}})
, together with smooth comparison operators:

\mathrm{ge}(a,b):=\sigma(a-b),\qquad\mathrm{eq}(a,b):=\exp\bigl(-|a-b|\bigr),

(121)

where
\sigma
denotes the sigmoid function, i.e.,
\sigma(x)=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-x)}
. Strict inequality is expressed compositionally as
\mathrm{gt}(a,b):=\mathrm{ge}(a,b)\land\neg,\mathrm{eq}(a,b)
. The knowledge base is defined as the conjunction, over all five labels, of biconditionals between each label indicator and the corresponding count-based predicate:

\displaystyle\mathsf{K}_{{\tt CEB}}:=\forall\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}}.

\displaystyle\bigl(\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{y}}=\texttt{Pos}\}\iff\mathrm{gt}(\tilde{N}_{\texttt{Pos}},\tilde{N}_{\texttt{Neg}})\land\mathrm{ge}(\tilde{N}_{\texttt{Pos}},\tilde{N}_{\texttt{Neu}})\land\mathrm{ge}(\tilde{N}_{\texttt{Pos}},\tilde{N}_{\texttt{Unk}})\bigr)

\displaystyle\land

\displaystyle\bigl(\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{y}}=\texttt{Neg}\}\iff\mathrm{gt}(\tilde{N}_{\texttt{Neg}},\tilde{N}_{\texttt{Pos}})\land\mathrm{ge}(\tilde{N}_{\texttt{Neg}},\tilde{N}_{\texttt{Neu}})\land\mathrm{ge}(\tilde{N}_{\texttt{Neg}},\tilde{N}_{\texttt{Unk}})\bigr)

\displaystyle\land

\displaystyle\bigl(\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{y}}=\texttt{Neu}\}\iff\mathrm{gt}(\tilde{N}_{\texttt{Neu}},\tilde{N}_{\texttt{Pos}})\land\mathrm{gt}(\tilde{N}_{\texttt{Neu}},\tilde{N}_{\texttt{Neg}})\land\mathrm{ge}(\tilde{N}_{\texttt{Neu}},\tilde{N}_{\texttt{Unk}})\bigr)

\displaystyle\land

\displaystyle\bigl(\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{y}}=\texttt{Unk}\}\iff\mathrm{gt}(\tilde{N}_{\texttt{Unk}},\tilde{N}_{\texttt{Pos}})\land\mathrm{gt}(\tilde{N}_{\texttt{Unk}},\tilde{N}_{\texttt{Neg}})\land\mathrm{gt}(\tilde{N}_{\texttt{Unk}},\tilde{N}_{\texttt{Neu}})\bigr)

\displaystyle\land

\displaystyle\bigl(\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{y}}=\texttt{Conflict}\}\iff\mathrm{eq}(\tilde{N}_{\texttt{Pos}},\tilde{N}_{\texttt{Neg}})\land\mathrm{ge}(\tilde{N}_{\texttt{Pos}},\tilde{N}_{\texttt{Neu}})\land\mathrm{ge}(\tilde{N}_{\texttt{Pos}},\tilde{N}_{\texttt{Unk}})\bigr).

The tie-breaking priority
\texttt{Pos}\succ\texttt{Neg}\succ\texttt{Neu}\succ\texttt{Unk}
is encoded through the asymmetric use of
\mathrm{ge}
and
\mathrm{gt}
: higher-priority categories are allowed to win ties via
\geq
, whereas lower-priority categories must satisfy the strict inequality
>
.

\displaystyle\bigl(\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{y}}=\texttt{bird}\}\iff\mathrm{ani}\land\neg\,\mathrm{met}\land\mathrm{wng}\bigr)

\displaystyle\land

\displaystyle\bigl(\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{y}}=\texttt{frog}\}\iff\mathrm{ani}\land\neg\,\mathrm{met}\land\neg\,\mathrm{hai}\bigr)

\displaystyle\land

\displaystyle\land

\displaystyle\bigl(\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{y}}=\texttt{cat}\}\iff\mathrm{ani}\land\neg\,\mathrm{met}\land\neg\,\mathrm{wng}\land\mathrm{hai}\land\neg\,\mathrm{hrn}\land\neg\,\mathrm{snt}\bigr)

\displaystyle\land

\displaystyle\bigl(\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{y}}=\texttt{dog}\}\vee\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{y}}=\texttt{horse}\}\iff\mathrm{ani}\land\neg\,\mathrm{met}\land\neg\,\mathrm{wng}\land\mathrm{hai}\land\neg\,\mathrm{hrn}\land\mathrm{snt}\bigr)

D.5.1
MN-Add

MN-Add
, originally introduced in
55
, is the canonical benchmark in Neuro-Symbolic AI. Each input consists of two
MNIST

[
49
]
digit images, and the target label is their arithmetic sum. Formally, each sample is a pair of images
(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{2})
representing
MNIST
digits
(\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2})
, each taking values in
[0,9]
, and the task is to predict:

\bm{\mathrm{y}}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1}+\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2},

(122)

In contrast to
MN-EO
and
MN-H
,
MN-Add
contains all possible digit combinations (
54,000
train examples,
6,000
validation examples and
10,000
test examples) and does not induce structural ambiguity in the digit-to-value mapping. The underlying combinatorial structure uniquely identifies each digit, and no deterministic reasoning shortcuts arise. Consequently,
MN-Add
erves as a shortcut-free reference benchmark for reasoning
[
11
]
.

Reasoning Shortcuts
.
MN-Add
is provably reasoning shortcuts free. For further information refer to
12
.

D.5.2
CHX

CHX

[
16
]
is medical dataset characterized by four binary symptoms:

Fracture
,
Pneumothorax
,
Airspace opacity
, and
Nodule/mass
. Each symptom is represented as a binary variable indicating its presence or absence. For preprocessing, we follow
65
. However, in its original form,
CHX
is not directly suitable for learning and reasoning tasks. For this reason, we construct our setting as follows: let the concept vector be defined as:

\mathbf{c}=(\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{2},\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{3},\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{4})\in\{0,1\}^{4},

(123)

where:

\bm{\mathrm{c}}=\begin{pmatrix}\texttt{Fracture}\\
\texttt{Pneumothorax}\\
\texttt{Airspace opacity}\\
\texttt{Nodule/mass}\end{pmatrix}

(124)

We designed our NeSy task, such that the final label is determined by the total number of present symptoms. Formally, let

\bm{\mathrm{s}}=\sum_{i=1}^{4}\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{i}.

(125)

The diagnostic label
\bm{\mathrm{y}}
is defined as:

\bm{\mathrm{y}}=\begin{cases}\texttt{healthy}&\text{if }\bm{\mathrm{s}}=0,\\
\texttt{green\_code}&\text{if }\bm{\mathrm{s}}=1,\\
\texttt{yellow\_code}&\text{if }\bm{\mathrm{s}}=2,\\
\texttt{orange\_code}&\text{if }\bm{\mathrm{s}}=3,\\
\texttt{red\_code}&\text{if }\bm{\mathrm{s}}=4.\end{cases}

(126)

Thus, the task consists of predicting a severity level derived deterministically from the number of active symptoms.
CHX
consists of
3,150
train samples,
350
validation samples, and
876
test samples.

Reasoning Shortcuts
.
CHX
contains reasoning shortcuts. For example,
yellow_code
(
\bm{\mathrm{y}}=2
) can be obtained not only by predicting
\bm{\mathrm{c}}=(1,0,1,0)
, but also via other permutations such as
\bm{\mathrm{c}}^{\prime}=(0,1,1,0)
. For the exact (or approximate) number of reasoning shortcuts, we refer the reader to
rs-count

[
12
]
specifying such settings.

D.5.3
CEB

CEB

[
1
]
is a dataset designed to evaluate concept-based and causal reasoning in natural language models. It consists of restaurant reviews with structured, aspect-level annotations and counterfactual edits.
Each instance consists of:

•

A review text called

•

A set of high-level semantic concepts

•

\in[0,5]

The core concepts correspond to distinct aspects of the restaurant experience:

\bm{\mathrm{c}}=\begin{pmatrix}\texttt{food\_aspect\_majority}\\
\texttt{service\_aspect\_majority}\\

Each aspect captures the sentiment expressed toward that dimension (i.e.,
Positive
,
Negative
,
Neutral
,

). From this concepts, the goal is to infer the causally influenced label that is

.

Preprocessing
. As originally defined,
CEB
does not provide a deterministic rule mapping aspect-level concepts to the overall review label, making it unsuitable for a NeSy setting in which symbolic inference must be explicit.
To address this, we introduce

as an additional concept, so that the concept vector
\bm{\mathrm{c}}
becomes

\bm{\mathrm{c}}=\begin{pmatrix}\texttt{food\_aspect\_majority}\\
\texttt{service\_aspect\_majority}\\
\texttt{noise\_aspect\_majority}\\
\texttt{ambiance\_aspect\_majority}\\
\texttt{review\_majority}\end{pmatrix}\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{5}

(128)

\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{i}\in

\texttt{review\_majority}=\begin{cases}\texttt{unknown}&\text{if }\in\{\texttt{no majority},\texttt{unknown}\},\\

(129)

\bm{\mathrm{y}}=\argmax_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{i}}{\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{j}\in C}\mathbbm{1}\!\{\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{i}=\bm{\mathrm{c}}_{j}\}},\quad\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\{\texttt{Positive},\texttt{Neutral},\texttt{Negative},\texttt{unknown},\texttt{conflict}\},

\succ

Negative

\succ

Neutral

\succ

unknown
.

inclusive
, and
exclusive

1,755

1,673
validation samples, and
1,689
test samples.

.

, as designed, is not free of reasoning shortcuts. For instance, for a given label such as

, multiple concept combinations are possible, such as
\bm{\mathrm{c}}=(\texttt{Negative},\texttt{Negative},\texttt{Negative},\texttt{Negative})
or
(\texttt{Positive},\texttt{Negative},\texttt{Neutral},\texttt{Negative})
.
If interested, exact (or approximate) number of reasoning shortcuts can be found by using
rs-count

[
12
]
instatiating the formalized problem.

D.5.4
CIF10

The
CIF10
dataset
[
45
]
is a standard benchmark for image classification. It contains
60,000
color images of size
32\times 32
, divided into
10
classes, with
50,000
training images (which we further split into
35,000
for training and
15,000
for validation) and
10,000
test images. The original class labels are:

{airplane, automobile, bird, cat, deer, dog, frog, horse, ship, truck}

(131)

Preprocessing
. As with
CEB
, the raw
CIF10
dataset is not directly suitable for learning and reasoning tasks. For this reason, we introduce a set of intermediate concepts that are
intended
to be present in all images and useful for distinguishing the
10
classes.
However, to avoid trivial label leakage and to ensure that some concepts remain insufficient, we
deliberately
design the concept-to-label mapping to be incomplete. In particular, we construct the labeling such that, given only the provided concepts, it is not possible to distinguish between
automobile
and
truck
, nor between
dog
and
horse
.
In practice, we build a binary concept vector
\mathbf{c}\in\{0,1\}^{7}
for each
CIF10
image, encoding high-level semantic attributes such as
ani
(animal),
wng
(wing),
met
(metallic),
whl
(wheels),
hai
(hairy),
hrn
(horn) and
snt
(snout).
These concepts are deterministically derived from the original class label.
Then, given the concepts, we build the following rules for the
CIF10
labels:

\displaystyle\texttt{airplane}

\displaystyle\iff\texttt{met}\land\neg\texttt{ani}\land\texttt{wng},

(132)

\displaystyle\texttt{automobile, truck}

\displaystyle\iff\texttt{whl}\land\texttt{met}\land\neg\texttt{wng}\land\neg\texttt{ani},

\displaystyle\texttt{bird}

\displaystyle\iff\texttt{ani}\land\neg\texttt{met}\land\texttt{wng},

\displaystyle\texttt{frog}

\displaystyle\iff\texttt{ani}\land\neg\texttt{met}\land\neg\texttt{hai},

\displaystyle\texttt{deer}

\displaystyle\iff\texttt{ani}\land\neg\texttt{met}\land\neg\texttt{wng}\land\texttt{hai}\land\texttt{hrn},

\displaystyle\texttt{dog, horse}

\displaystyle\iff\texttt{ani}\land\neg\texttt{met}\land\neg\texttt{wng}\land\texttt{hai}\land\neg\texttt{hrn}\land\texttt{snt},

\displaystyle\texttt{ship}

\displaystyle\iff\texttt{met}\land\neg\texttt{ani}\land\neg\texttt{whl}\land\neg\texttt{wng}.

Reasoning Shortcuts
.
CIF10
, as constructed, is not free of reasoning shortcuts.
For instance,
airplane
is defined as
\texttt{met}\land\neg\texttt{ani}\land\texttt{wng}
but nothing is said about other concepts. For example, concept assignments such as
\texttt{met}=\top,\texttt{ani}=\bot,\texttt{wng}=\top,\texttt{hai}=\bot
and
\texttt{met}=\top,\texttt{ani}=\bot,\texttt{wng}=\top,\texttt{hai}=\top
both entail the
airplane
class. For the exact (or approximate) number of reasoning shortcuts, you can instantiate this problem in
rs-count

[
12
]
.

.

is one of the first datasets introduced to study reasoning shortcuts; for a detailed analysis, see

.

D.5.7
MN-H

MN-H
, originally introduced in
[
59
]
, is a biased variant of
MN-Add
in which only digits from
0
to
4
are considered. Moreover, only a subset of digit combinations is observed during training, given by:

\begin{cases}\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\includegraphics[width]{figures/mnist-0.png}}+\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\includegraphics[width]{figures/mnist-0.png}}&=0\\
\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\includegraphics[width]{figures/mnist-0.png}}+\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\includegraphics[width]{figures/mnist-1.png}}&=1\\
\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\includegraphics[width]{figures/mnist-2.png}}+\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\includegraphics[width]{figures/mnist-3.png}}&=5\\
\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\includegraphics[width]{figures/mnist-2.png}}+\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\includegraphics[width]{figures/mnist-4.png}}&=6\end{cases}

(134)

This restricted supervision induces ambiguity in the digit-to-value mapping and therefore introduces reasoning shortcuts. In contrast to
MN-EO
, digits
0
and
1
remain uniquely identifiable from the observed equations, while digits
2
,
3
, and
4
admit multiple consistent assignments.

The dataset comprises
2,940
fully annotated training samples,
840
validation samples, and
420
in-distribution test samples.

Reasoning Shortcuts
. Similarly to
MN-EO
,
MN-H
, was introduced to study reasoning shortcuts; for a detailed analysis, see
59
.

D.5.8
MN-SP

MN-SP
, originally introduced in
[
11
]
, is a variant of
MN-Add
that has a different logical rule. As in
MN-Add
, the input consists of two
MNIST
digit images corresponding to digits
(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{1},\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{2})
. However, instead of predicting their arithmetic sum, the target label is defined as the
parity
of the sum. For this reason, it has the same amount of samples as
MN-Add
per split.

Formally, the task is:

\bm{\mathrm{y}}=\left(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{1}+\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{2}\right)\bmod 2,

(135)

where
\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\{0,1\}
, with
0
denoting an even sum and
1
an odd sum.
For example:

\displaystyle(\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\includegraphics[width]{figures/mnist-2.png}}+\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\includegraphics[width]{figures/mnist-4.png}})\mod 2=0

(136)

\displaystyle(\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\includegraphics[width]{figures/mnist-1.png}}+\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\includegraphics[width]{figures/mnist-2.png}})\mod 2=1

(137)

Reasoning Shortcuts
. Similarly to
MN-EO
and
MN-H
,
MN-SP
was introduced to study shortcuts; for a detailed analysis, see
11
.

E.1
Model Selection

All experiments use either the
Adam

[
42
]
or
SGD

[
69
]
optimizer, selected as part of the hyperparameter search. Hyperparameters were selected through Bayesian optimization via
Optuna

[
3
]
, using a
BoTorch
sampler
[
3
]
with a
TPE
sampler
[
89
]
for the initial startup trials, maximizing the macro F1 score of the final label on a validation set. Each study ran for
<M>
(at least
20
) trials, with
10
warm-up trials before the Bayesian model is fitted.
All experiments were run for
<N>
epochs employing early stopping, saving the checkpoint which achieves the highest F1 score on the validation set.
The learning rate
\gamma
was selected from
\{10^{-5},10^{-4},10^{-3},10^{-2},10^{-1}\}
, and the momentum
\iota
from
\{10^{-5},10^{-4},10^{-3},10^{-2},0.1,0.5,0.9,0.99\}
. The batch size
\nu
was chosen from
\{32,64,128,256\}
.

For
LTN
, a consistent logic family was selected jointly for all operators, choosing among Gödel, Product, and Łukasiewicz. Under Product logic, the implication operator defaults to Goguen implication for consistency. The quantifier parameter
p
was searched over integer values in the range
[1,9]
.
For
LTN
, since the training objective can be hard to optimize since the
p
parameter governs the sharpness of the existential and universal quantifiers. We therefore linearly anneal
p
from
1
to the target value
p^{*}
across all training epochs:

p_{t}=\mathrm{round}\!\left(1+(p^{*}-1)\,\frac{t}{T-1}\right),\quad t=0,\ldots,T-1,

(138)

where
T
is the total number of epochs. This keeps the logic soft early in training and progressively makes it crisper as concept representations stabilize.
Similarly, when concept supervision is available, we suppress concept supervision for the first 25% of training and then linearly ramp the concept-loss weight from
0
to
1
.

All models were trained for
200
epochs with early stopping, except for
CEB
, where models were trained for
500
epochs. Early stopping selects the checkpoint achieving the highest F1 score on the validation set with respect to the final label. All experiments have been tested with the same
10
seeds for consistency, specifically
\{1011,1213,1415,1617,1819,2021,2223,2425,2627,2829\}
.

F.4
Time Overhead Analysis

A key point of
COCOCO
is that the joint revision operator reaches a fixed point in one step (
Proposition

4.1
), making the additional cost a single computation of the abductive and deductive images, i.e.,
\Gamma^{{\sf ab}}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=g^{-\dagger}(\Upsilon_{\alpha}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
and
\Upsilon^{\sf de}(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=g^{\dagger}(\Gamma_{\beta}(\bm{\mathrm{x}}))
.

In practice, as shown by
Figs.

9
and
10
,
COCOCO
is comparable in runtime to all other ablations. While it is naturally slower than
TO
and
RPB
since it requires additional revision steps,
Proposition

C.7
ensures it remains tractable thanks to the
fixed-point
theorem (
Proposition

C.7
).

Looking at the aggregate results in
Fig.

9
,
COCOCO
exhibits an overhead of approximately
160\%
relative to
DPL
and
LTN
when considered individually in the unsupervised setting (
100\%
horizontal line), and around
120\%
in the supervised setting. This is comparable to
RPB
(
140\%
and
110\%
, respectively) and to
CDe
(
130-150\%
unsupervised), and only moderately higher than
TO
, which is the closest to the baseline.

Looking at individual datasets in
Fig.

10
, the overhead of
COCOCO
is dataset-dependent, as it depends on the number of concepts and labels produced by the conformal method, but remains consistently bounded, except for
CEB
, where the number of concept combinations is
4\times
higher than the baseline due to the large number of generated concepts and labels.
Notably, in
CHX
– our operational dataset – and
RIV10
, the overhead is almost non-existent, demonstrating that in practical applications the overhead is negligible. In fact,
COCOCO
is well-suited to settings where a human is in the loop and the number of concepts and produced labels is close to one, precisely the scenario in which
COCOCO
excels.

Furthermore, when employing
COCOCO
with e-values (
COCOCO
∗
), as well as in other ablations, the computational overhead is approximately
200-250\%
in the unsupervised setting and close to
150\%
in the supervised setting. This is worse than
COCOCO
even with the speed-up arising from avoiding the Bonferroni correction (
Proposition

C.9
), as it eliminates the need to enumerate the full product set (
Eq.

5
).

COCOCO
∗
, used in a p-value-like fashion, produces consistently larger concept and label sets, except in
CHX
and
RIV10
. Nevertheless, unlike all other approaches,
COCOCO
∗
can be fixed in size and thus in computational overhead. In fact, with appropriate choices of
\hat{\beta}
and
\hat{\alpha}
– namely those that keep the set size below a given threshold (as shown in
Section

6
) – we can cap the computational cost, since we know in advance the size of the instances on which the inference layer
g
will be applied.
