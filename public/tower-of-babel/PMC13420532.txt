The global epidemiology of MRSA
Authors: Giusy Tiseo, Valentina Galfo, Marco Falcone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMRSA remains a major global health challenge. Although its global prevalence has declined over the last decade, especially in high-income countries, MRSA still has a high burden of invasive infections and mortality. The global epidemiology of MRSA is characterized by significant regional heterogeneity, shaped by infection control policies and healthcare organization. Distinct epidemiological patterns characterize acute-care hospitals, long-term care facilities and community settings, reflecting differences in population vulnerability and healthcare exposure. At the molecular level, MRSA circulation is sustained by the dynamic coexistence of successful clonal lineages and by the evolving distribution of resistance determinants. MRSA acquisition is driven by a combination of patient-related, environmental and healthcare-associated factors, reflecting the complex interaction between host vulnerability and exposure intensity. On this basis, MRSA nasal screening has emerged as both an infection prevention measure and a key antimicrobial stewardship tool across multiple clinical scenarios. In recent years, therapeutic approaches have evolved, and new effective and safe anti-MRSA agents are currently available.

Introduction

Staphylococcus aureus is one of the most challenging pathogens and is a leading bacterial cause of death worldwide.1 The clinical spectrum of S. aureus infections is remarkably broad, ranging from relatively mild skin and soft tissue infections to life-threatening invasive diseases. Among these, bloodstream infections (BSIs) due to S. aureus are of particular concern, as they are consistently associated with substantial morbidity and mortality.1 Despite significant advances in the clinical management of BSIs caused by S. aureus, including the implementation of structured care bundles and the integration of advanced diagnostic modalities such as [18F]FDG-PET/CT imaging,2 mortality rates remain high. Recent data highlight that S. aureus bacteraemia has a case fatality rate of 15% to 30% and causes 300 000 deaths per year worldwide.1,3

Although mortality rates are not necessarily dependent on methicillin resistance status, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) aetiology may contribute to delays in timely administration of appropriate antimicrobial therapy.4 Recent studies suggest that host factors, severity of illness, infection characteristics and especially the timeliness of effective antimicrobial therapy are the main determinants of mortality rather than the resistance phenotype itself.5–7 Since its first description in the early 1960s, MRSA rapidly spread worldwide in an initial phase, followed by a progressive decline.8,9 According to the WHO Global Antimicrobial Resistance and Use Surveillance System (WHO GLASS) 2025, methicillin resistance among S. aureus has shown a worldwide annual reduction of 2.5% (95% CI −4.5% to −0.5%) from 2018 to 2023.9 A far more significant fall has been reported for MRSA BSIs in Europe and the USA, where incidence dropped by 20.4% from 2019 to 2024 and >50% from 2005 to 2022.10,11 However, MRSA still represents a significant clinical and public health threat, with 550 000 associated deaths and 130 000 attributable deaths in 2021.12

MRSA represents a substantial burden for healthcare systems worldwide. In Europe, MRSA infections are associated with prolonged hospitalization and increased healthcare costs, with approximately 1.26-fold longer hospital stays and 1.12- to 6.25-fold higher hospital charges compared with infections caused by methicillin-susceptible S. aureus (MSSA).13 A recent Australian study highlighted that MRSA BSIs are associated with significant additional annual costs on the healthcare system.14 In this review, we provide a contemporary overview of the global epidemiology of MRSA, integrating epidemiological trends, molecular evolution, and clinical and therapeutic implications.

Update of global epidemiology

Over the past decade, the global epidemiology of MRSA has undergone substantial epidemiological changes. Globally, MRSA accounts for one-quarter of S. aureus BSIs, ranging from 9.7% in the European region to 50.3% in the Eastern Mediterranean region, with a modest but consistent decline reported in several regions.9 Since the early 2000s, the implementation of infection prevention and control measures yielded a reduction in the prevalence of BSIs caused by MRSA. However, data from the National Healthcare Safety Network (NHSN) and from the Emerging Infections Program (EIP) surveillance system suggest that the decline has slowed in more recent years due to several factors.15 First, over the past decade, there has been a significant global increase and worldwide dissemination of MDR Gram-negative bacilli (particularly carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales, Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Acinetobacter baumannii).16–18 This epidemiological shift has drawn substantial attention from the scientific community, public health authorities and antimicrobial stewardship programmes, with efforts increasingly focused on development of novel anti-Gram-negative antibiotics and implementation of control measures for these pathogens. Therefore, awareness and targeted prevention strategies specifically addressing MRSA may have received comparatively less emphasis. Moreover, disruptive events such as the COVID-19 pandemic or conflicts might have reduced MRSA control programmes, exposing vulnerabilities in healthcare systems and altering the distribution of MDR organisms across regions and care settings.11,19–22

Moreover, MRSA epidemiology is strongly influenced by local determinants, including the predominance of specific circulating clones, the organization of healthcare systems and local infection-prevention infrastructures, patterns of antimicrobial consumption, and community transmission dynamics. Consequently, the burden of MRSA displays substantial heterogeneity across countries and regions. MRSA trends in Europe

MRSA epidemiology is characterized by marked geographical heterogeneity across European regions (Figure 2). According to the most recent ECDC surveillance data for 2024, MRSA prevalence among BSIs has significantly declined from 2019. Despite country-level variability, the incidence of MRSA BSI in Europe decreased by 20.4% between 2019 and 2024, declining from 5.63 to 4.48 cases per 100 000 population.10 Northern European countries continue to maintain exceptionally low rates of MRSA BSI (Figure 2a). In Norway, the incidence of invasive infections has remained stable at <0.5 per 100 000 population.23 Similarly, in Denmark, MRSA accounted for <1.5% of all S. aureus BSI.24 In Sweden, the prevalence of MRSA isolates and of MRSA BSI substantially increased in recent years (MRSA BSI incidence of 2.07 per 100 000 population in 2024).10,25 This trend may reflect the increasing community-associated MRSA (CA-MRSA) spread outside hospital settings.25

In contrast, southern and southeastern European countries including Croatia, Italy, Greece, Portugal, Romania, and Cyprus reported MRSA BSI rates higher than 5 per 100 000 population in 2024 (Figure 2b and 2c).10 In Italy, up to 25.7% of all S. aureus BSI isolates were MRSA, with an overall incidence of 11 per 100.000 population, whereas in Romania and Cyprus MRSA incidence reached 13 per 100 000 population.10,26 Finally, other European countries (Figure 2d) such as Germany or France showed intermediate prevalences, ranging from 2 to 5 per 100 000 population. As an example, in Germany the incidence of MRSA BSI decreased from 4.0 per 100 000 inhabitants in 2017 to 2.1 in 2023 and remained stable in 2024.10,27

Significant variation in MRSA rates across European countries reflects a complex interplay of differences in infection prevention and control policies; antimicrobial stewardship programmes, healthcare organization, and long-term care facility burden may all contribute to the observed heterogeneity.10 Ecological analyses conducted across European countries suggest that antibiotic consumption may play an important role in determining MRSA incidence. The use of multiple concomitant antimicrobial agents and a broader-spectrum antibiotic exposure are associated with increased MRSA prevalence.28,29 Furthermore, variability in the implementation and adherence to infection prevention measures, including hand hygiene practices, may influence transmission dynamics within healthcare settings.28 Beyond healthcare-related determinants, cultural and governance-related factors have also been proposed as potential contributors to the persistent north-to-south and west-to-east gradients observed across Europe, possibly affecting compliance with antimicrobial stewardship interventions and infection control measures.28 Finally, differences in the relative contribution of community-acquired MRSA and livestock-associated MRSA may further explain the geographical heterogeneity observed across European countries.10

MRSA trends in the USA

MRSA epidemiology in the USA has changed substantially over the past decade. Data from the EIP surveillance system for MRSA BSI, which includes six US states, showed that hospital-onset MRSA BSI rates declined by 7.3% per year between 2012 and 2017, reaching 15 cases per 100 000 population.15 In contrast, community-onset MRSA BSI rates did not change significantly during the same period and remained below 5 cases per 100 000 population.15 Between 2017 and 2019, MRSA infection rates remained stable; however, the first wave of the COVID-19 pandemic was associated with a 13% increase in hospital-onset MRSA infections compared with 2019.30 Subsequent EIP data indicated a further increase in 2021, followed by a decline in 2022, whereas community-onset rates decreased during the pandemic period. Overall, MRSA BSI incidence declined by more than 50% between 2005 and 2022, reaching fewer than 20 cases per 100 000 population per year.11 Consistent findings were reported by the US Veterans Affairs Healthcare System, which collects data from facilities across all 50 states. Between 2007 and 2019, the proportion of MRSA infections among inpatients decreased from 57.7% to 44.6%, with the most pronounced reduction observed in hospital-onset infections, except during the COVID-19 period; also in the outpatient settings, the proportion of MRSA declined from 53.6% in 2010 to 38.8% in 2019.31,32

MRSA trends in low- and middle-income countries

The epidemiological landscape of MRSA in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) is characterized by significant data gaps. Recent evidence confirms that MRSA colonization and infection represent a growing public health threat in these regions.9,33 In community settings, the pooled prevalence of MRSA colonization among healthy individuals colonized by S. aureus is estimated at approximately 15%, with a marked regional gradient: Africa reports the highest rates, followed by Asia and Latin America.33

In Latin America, the burden of MRSA infections underwent a period of rapid escalation between 1997 and 2007, when clinical prevalence peaked at approximately 40.6%, while colonization rates stabilized at 5.4%.33,34 In more recent years, regional trends have stabilized, with MRSA accounting for approximately 25%–35% of S. aureus BSI, although substantially higher rates persist in Colombia, often associated with the dissemination of the highly virulent USA300 Latin American variant.9,35,36

Across the African continent widespread antibiotic misuse and weak surveillance systems limit data availability. MRSA colonization rates range from 4.1% to 13.6% overall.37 Among clinically relevant isolates an overall MRSA prevalence of 42.2% has been reported, with northern African countries having the highest proportion (56.2%) and sub-Saharan Africa with lower prevalence (36.7%).38 Sub-Saharan Africa exhibits significant variation in clinical isolates from BSI, with prevalence rates varying from less than 20% to over 75% in different countries including the Democratic Republic of Congo and Côte d'Ivoire.9

The epidemiological landscape in Asia is particularly complex, with substantial variation between countries and income levels. In the Asia-Pacific region, before 2018 an overall colonization prevalence of 22.4% has been reported, with the highest rates observed in India (16.5%–23.5%), followed by Vietnam (7.9%), Taiwan (3.5%–3.8%) and South Korea (1.1%).33,39 In China, according to the CHINET surveillance system, the detection rate of S. aureus among clinically relevant isolates has remained stable, but the prevalence of MRSA has shown a remarkable decrease from 69.0% in 2005% to 28.7% in 2022.40

Available data from LMICs underscore the urgent need for harmonized surveillance and targeted interventions tailored to the specific socioeconomic and clinical drivers of each region.

Molecular epidemiology

Methicillin resistance can involve multiple mechanisms, including gene-mediated resistance, enzyme production, drug efflux pumps, biofilm formation and alterations in target sites.41 It is primarily attributed to the acquisition of the mec gene complex responsible for resistance to β-lactams (penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems). Recent studies have documented numerous homologues of the mec gene.41 The mecA gene is carried on a mobile genetic element called SCCmec (Staphylococcal Cassette Chromosome mec) and encodes penicillin-binding protein(PBP)2a, a PBP with low affinity for β-lactam antibiotics that allows peptidoglycan cross-linking to proceed, maintaining the integrity of cell walls, thereby conferring antibiotic resistance.41 Additional regulatory activation of mecA genes on exposure to β-lactam antibiotics has been reported in community-acquired MRSA strains through the regulation of the blaI and blaRI genes.42

While mecA remains the predominant determinant of methicillin resistance and is detected in the vast majority of MRSA isolates, with an estimated global prevalence of approximately 21.8%, other mec genes are considerably less frequent.43 In particular, mecC, which has a prevalence among MRSA of about 2%, encodes a PBP2 homologue (PBP2aLGA) that exhibits a higher relative affinity for oxacillin compared with PBP2a. When mecC is present, MRSA strains may be misidentified as MSSA by non-specific laboratory methods.41,43

In addition to PBP2a encoded by mecA, MRSA strains also have other resistance mechanisms able to hydrolyse the β-lactam ring. Among them the following should be mentioned: (i) the recombinant blaZ gene is primarily associated with penicillin resistance, but it may also contribute to the cefazolin inoculum effect in high-inoculum infections and to the development of the borderline oxacillin-resistant S. aureus (BORSA) phenotype;44 (ii) mutations in genes encoding RNA polymerase subunits, such as rpoB and rpoC; (iii) mutations in the ccr genes and surrounding ORFs; and (iv) toxin production, including leucocidins, exfoliative toxins, toxic shock syndrome toxin 1 and haemolysins.41

Healthcare-associated versus community-acquired MRSA

Initially confined mostly to healthcare settings (healthcare-associated, or HA-MRSA), MRSA emerged in the community during the 1990s as community-acquired MRSA (CA-MRSA). The difference between HA-MRSA and CA-MRSA is not simply epidemiological but reflects biological and molecular differences that influence disease pathogenesis, transmission dynamics and antimicrobial therapies (Figure 3).

Characteristics of CA-MRSA and HA-MRSA.

CA-MRSA strains are generally associated with infections in otherwise healthy individuals and display greater virulence despite having a more favourable antimicrobial susceptibility profile.45 This enhanced virulence is related to toxin-encoding genes, particularly Panton–Valentine leucocidin (PVL), and to the presence of the arginine catabolic mobile element (ACME).46,47 PVL is detected in more than 50% of CA-MRSA isolates but rarely detected among HA-MRSA strains. PVL has been associated with severe skin and soft tissue infections, abscess formation and invasive necrotizing manifestations including necrotizing fasciitis and necrotizing pneumonia.45–49 Similarly, the ACME island increases virulence enabling survival on human skin and in conditions of low oxygen, such as abscesses. In contrast, HA-MRSA strains typically carry larger SCCmec elements (types I–III) harbouring multiple resistance determinants including fluoroquinolones, macrolides, lincosamides and aminoglycosides, whereas CA-MRSA strains more frequently carry smaller SCCmec types (IV–V), which impose a lower fitness cost and may facilitate community spread and virulence expression.41,45,46,50

Factors such as male sex, obesity and previous MRSA colonization or infection have been associated with an increased risk of MRSA acquisition and infection, independently from the clinical setting.1,51,52

CA-MRSA acquisition is primarily linked to the so-called ‘5 Cs’, standing for Crowding, Contact (skin-to-skin), Compromised skin integrity, Contaminated items and surfaces, and lack of Cleanliness.45 Participation in contact sports, injection drug use, residence in crowded environments (including but not limited to correctional facilities and shelters), household exposure to colonized individuals, and environmental contamination of households and community settings have all been associated with an increased risk of CA-MRSA acquisition and recurrent infections.45,46,53–55 Special populations at increased risk for CA-MRSA acquisition are neonates and young children as well as people with frequent animal contact.39,46

In contrast, HA-MRSA acquisition is mainly driven by healthcare exposure and host vulnerability. Older age, diabetes mellitus, previous hospitalization, residence in long-term care facilities, chronic haemodialysis, recent surgery or invasive procedures, and the presence of indwelling medical devices represent well-established risk factors.1,50,52,54 Repeated healthcare contacts as in prolonged hospitalization and healthcare system factors such as overcrowding and understaffing increase exposure to HA-MRSA reservoirs and opportunities for cross-transmission.56

A peculiar case of patients at high risk for MRSA are patients living with HIV, who may have an increased risk of MRSA colonization and infection by both CA-MRSA and HA-MRSA. High rates of acquisition are reported in patients with prior hospitalization (OR 1.8), prior antibiotic exposure (OR 2.69), low CD4+ cell counts (OR 1.79), and a history of MRSA colonization (OR 6.3).57

MRSA in special settings

Elderly people in nursing homes/long-term care facilities

Nursing homes (NHs) and long-term care facilities (LTCFs) represent a particularly important reservoir for MRSA colonization and transmission.58,59 A worldwide systematic review and meta-analysis including data from 29 countries found that the pooled global prevalence of MRSA among elderly patients in NHs and LTCFs was 14.69%, which is 10-fold higher than the MRSA colonization rate in the general population.58 This elevated burden of MRSA among elderly residents reflects the unique characteristics of this population, including prior antibiotic exposure, previous MRSA infection, multiple comorbidities, frequent healthcare contacts and the use of invasive medical devices, all of which significantly increase the risk of MRSA colonization.1,58 A higher prevalence has been reported in LTCFs (16.29%) and NHs (14.35%) compared with residential care homes; these differences likely reflect the distinct roles of these facilities and the different levels of medical care provided.58 LTCFs are highly medicalized environments where antibiotic use, and potential overuse, is more frequent, and where residents often have complex clinical conditions requiring repeated interactions with healthcare services. Conversely, residential care homes are primarily intended for supportive care, and antibiotics are usually prescribed by external healthcare providers rather than directly by facility staff.58,60

Limited data are available for the African continent, where elderly individuals are mainly cared for within family settings rather than institutions, resulting in low MRSA prevalence.58 However, relevant MRSA colonization rates have also been reported among community-dwelling older adults in LMIC settings.61

Higher colonization rates have been reported in LTCFs in Japan and Korea, with MRSA detected in 20.5% and 23.1% of screened patients, respectively.62,63

Data from 626 LTCFs participating in the CDC’s EIP surveillance showed a median invasive MRSA rate of 1.82 cases per 100 000 resident-days, with less than 20% of all included facilities not reporting cases of infections.64 In these settings, a higher prevalence of MRSA infections was linked, among others, to higher proportions of males, residents with nasogastric or percutaneous gastrostomy tubes, or requiring extensive assistance.64 According to 2023–2024 ECDC surveillance data, MRSA represented less than 1% of reported infections in residents of European LTCFs, although pathogen-specific data remain limited.65

Vulnerable patients with frequent hospital contacts

Patients with multiple comorbidities and those with frequent hospital contacts represent a particularly vulnerable population for MRSA colonization and infection. Patients undergoing haemodialysis and those with diabetes mellitus and foot infections are at high risk for colonization and subsequent infections due to their continuous exposure to healthcare facilities.66–68

The overall risk of invasive MRSA infections in patients undergoing haemodialysis has been described as 50–100 times higher than in the general population. Available data suggest that patients undergoing chronic haemodialysis account for a substantial proportion of MRSA BSIs despite representing a small fraction of the general population.69,70 For this reason, prevention and control strategies in this high-risk population are warranted. Periodic screening for MRSA may be useful in this population, but cost-effectiveness of decolonization is debated.71

The prevalence of MRSA colonization among patients with diabetes mellitus is as high as 9.2% and increases up to 19% in those undergoing haemodialysis. These rates are almost double those reported in non-diabetic patients and far higher than in the general population.67 A particularly vulnerable subgroup of diabetic patients includes those with diabetic foot infections (DFIs). In this population, MRSA accounts for approximately 17% of DFI cases, with a slightly higher prevalence among hospitalized patients compared with outpatients (16% versus 13%).67,72 However, after 2021 the prevalence of MRSA in DFIs has declined to around 9%, possibly reflecting a relative increase in Gram-negative infections in this population.72,73

Paediatric population

The paediatric population represents an important reservoir of MRSA and may contribute substantially to its spread in both community and healthcare environments.74 MRSA colonization can be detected as early as the immediate post-partum period. Among infants born from mothers colonized with MRSA during pregnancy or at delivery, a 3-fold higher risk of MRSA is reported, suggesting both vertical and early horizontal transmission.75 Colonization increases during early infancy, peaking at around 2 months of age with carriage rates reaching approximately 20%.75 In this setting CA-MRSA isolates were most prevalent, with 30% of these strains isolated from both mothers and infants carrying PVL.75

When evaluating data including all paediatric age groups, a worldwide pooled prevalence of MRSA colonization of 3%–5% has been reported.74,76 Risk factors for MRSA colonization in paediatric patients include female sex, recent surgical procedures, recent hospitalization or antibiotic exposure.74 Colonization represents an important risk factor for subsequent infections.77 In children, MRSA is mainly associated with skin infections. Of note, in a Spanish study more than 70% of MRSA isolates carried PVL, and more than one-quarter of cases required hospitalization.78

Individuals in crowded or disadvantaged conditions

Individuals in crowded or disadvantaged living conditions have increased risk for MRSA colonization.79 Close interpersonal contact, limited access to hygiene facilities, shared living spaces and barriers to healthcare access facilitate the spread of MRSA within these communities.80 Of note, each $100 000 increase in median annual income has been associated with a 73% reduction in the incidence of CA-MRSA.80

Special conditions that increase the risk of MRSA include residing in homeless shelters, correctional facilities or refugee camps. Jails may act as important amplifiers of MRSA transmission, with real-world data showing colonization rates as high as 15%–20% among inmates, and incarceration rates as a key driver of increased CA-MRSA.79,81 Among inmates the highly virulent community-acquired USA300 strain is predominant, with an increased risk of infections, especially skin and soft tissue infections, possibly related to frequent drug use.79,82

Among homeless populations, limited access to hygiene facilities and personal care products contributes to an increased risk of MRSA carriage and infection. However, the strongest risk factors are represented by shelter usage, with a single night doubling or tripling the risk of MRSA colonization due to close interpersonal contact and the shared use of washrooms and sleeping areas. Mobility between multiple shelters may further increase both exposure and the potential for MRSA transmission.79

Healthcare workers

Available data suggest that approximately 5% of healthcare workers (HCWs) become colonized with MRSA. Poor infection control practices is one of the main risk factors for MRSA acquisition. However, a recent meta-analysis suggests that, in this particular setting, educational interventions and additional infection control support alone may have limited impact on MRSA colonization rates.83,84 However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as multifaceted infection prevention strategies and sustained adherence to hand hygiene and contact precautions remain fundamental components of MRSA control programmes. Of importance, although not considered as the primary source of MRSA spread, HCWs have been shown to contribute to MRSA transmission to patients, with subsequent patient-to-patient transmission, especially during periods of understaffing.84,85

On this basis, it has been suggested that screening and decolonization of HCWs should be implemented as part of infection control policies; however, the possibility of recolonization, along with the stigmatization of colonized HCWs, may represent a significant limitation to the implementation of such policies. Therefore, high compliance with hand hygiene and contact precautions should always be pursued.84

Nasal MRSA screening and decolonization

The implementation of systematic decolonization of all MRSA carriers remains a matter of debate. Different policies for selective or universal screening and selective or universal decolonization have been implemented in different countries over the years.86 Several considerations, including local MRSA prevalence and patients’ risk factors, can guide the choice to screen patients and decolonize carriers.

While in countries with low MRSA prevalence such as the Netherlands and Denmark a ‘search and destroy’ policy has been implemented, with targeted decolonization of colonized patients, in countries with high MRSA prevalence, eradication treatment in colonized patients is not routinely recommended but should be evaluated on a cost-effectiveness basis due to the high risk of recolonization.87

A recent meta-analysis suggests that universal screening in general wards is not recommended due to a high cost-effectiveness ratio, but that selective screening of high-risk patients should be preferred; however, conflicting data emerged for surgical wards.88 A recent randomized controlled trial (RCT) suggested that an ICU universal decolonization approach may have a more favourable cost-effectiveness in high prevalence settings.89

Available guidelines and guidance suggest screening and decolonization of specific target patients. In the UK a universal screening approach with decolonization of all positive patients has been tested but suspended due to unfavourable cost-effectiveness. On this basis targeted MRSA screening and decolonization of high-risk patients, including those undergoing orthopaedic, vascular or cardiothoracic surgery, and onco-haematological patients, is recommended.90

Different patient risk factors should be taken into account when implementing nasal screening for MRSA. One important field is represented by preoperative screening procedures. In fact, data suggest that in orthopaedic and cardiothoracic surgery, decolonization protocols significantly reduce the risk of MRSA surgical site infection and unfavourable outcomes.91–94 The ESCMID guidelines recommend preoperative screening for MRSA and decolonization in patients undergoing high-risk surgical procedures.95 IDSA guidelines suggest decolonization of all patients undergoing orthopaedic and cardiothoracic surgeries and other high-risk surgeries such as those involving prosthetic material.52,96

Of note, a large RCT demonstrated no benefit of decolonization for non-clean surgeries including urological surgeries, transplant and abdominal surgery.97

MRSA nasal screening has emerged as a valuable antimicrobial stewardship tool in patients with pneumonia. Empirical anti-MRSA therapy in patients with hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP) and ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), as well as in selected patients with community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) who display well-recognized risk factors for MRSA, is crucial to reduce mortality.98–100 In this context, early MRSA nasal screening can allow the discontinuation of anti-MRSA antibiotics, since its negative predictive value (NPV) for MRSA pneumonia ranges from 76% to 99.4% across studies. However, culture-based screening methods require several days to provide results, which may limit their clinical usefulness because the result may become available only after the recommended duration of antibiotic therapy for pneumonia has already been completed. In contrast, rapid molecular assays, such as PCR-based tests for S. aureus and MRSA detection, can provide results within a few hours and therefore represent a valuable tool to support antimicrobial stewardship strategies by enabling earlier de-escalation of unnecessary anti-MRSA therapy.101–106

Advances in the treatment of MRSA infections

The management of MRSA infections requires an individualized therapeutic approach that should take into account site of infection, safety and a patient’s risk of adverse events.

Old but gold: is it true for anti-MRSA antibiotics?

Glycopeptides, mainly vancomycin, have historically been the cornerstone of therapy for MRSA infections.99,107–109 However, limitations including the narrow therapeutic window, nephrotoxicity and unsatisfactory tissue penetration, have progressively reduced their role compared with newer agents, although these agents have generally demonstrated non-inferiority rather than clear superiority compared with vancomycin.110,111 Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) should be performed during vancomycin treatment both to guarantee efficacy and avoid nephrotoxicity.111,112 Moreover, an important concern related to vancomycin is the so-called vancomycin MIC creep, first described in the early 2000s. It refers to the gradual increase over time in the MIC of vancomycin among S. aureus isolates that still remain within the susceptible range. This phenomenon has been associated with reduced clinical response in severe infections such as MRSA BSI.113,114 However, more recent data have failed to demonstrate a consistent worldwide MIC creep phenomenon, suggesting that vancomycin MIC values have remained largely stable over time, although some variability in MIC values may occur depending on the susceptibility testing method used.115–117

Daptomycin remains an important option for MRSA BSI, right-sided infective endocarditis, and skin and soft tissue infections, but inactivation by pulmonary surfactant, and the need for high dosing strategies (8–10 mg/kg/day) in complicated infections have limited its role as a universal alternative to vancomycin.118–120 The risk of rhabdomyolysis and creatine phosphokinase elevation, and the rare risk of eosinophilic pneumonia may be more pronounced in special populations, such as elderly patients.111,121

Linezolid has a central role in contemporary MRSA management, particularly for pneumonia and skin and soft tissue infections, thanks to its predictable pharmacokinetics, high penetration into lung tissue and epithelial lining fluid, and anti-toxin activity.111,122 Moreover, its high oral bioavailability allows an early switch from IV to oral therapy.123 Limitations are mainly related to time-dependent adverse events, including thrombocytopenia, myelosuppression and neuropathy, which require monitoring during prolonged therapy, particularly in elderly patients and those with renal impairment.111

In selected patients with susceptible isolates, oral agents such as trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, clindamycin and tetracyclines continue to represent useful therapeutic options, particularly for community-associated MRSA infections and in outpatient management settings.107–109

New anti-MRSA molecules

In recent years, newer anti-MRSA β-lactams and long-acting antibiotics have expanded the therapeutic armamentarium beyond traditional agents. RCTs have shown ceftobiprole to be non-inferior to daptomycin for the treatment of BSI and right-sided endocarditis due to MRSA.124–126 Similarly, RCTs and real-world studies have shown ceftaroline to achieve clinical outcomes comparable to linezolid and vancomycin in MRSA pneumonia, while observational studies reported outcomes comparable to vancomycin and daptomycin in patients with MRSA BSI.127–129

Long-acting lipoglycopeptides, including dalbavancin and oritavancin, represent one of the most innovative advances in MRSA therapy. Their prolonged half-lives enable simplified dosing regimens and facilitate outpatient management, addressing key challenges such as prolonged hospitalization, vascular access-related complications, treatment adherence and management of patients with injection drug use.130–132 Although initially approved for the treatment of skin and soft tissue infections, a growing body of real-world evidence supports their use in bone and joint infections, endovascular infections and selected cases of MRSA BSI.109,130,131 In addition, randomized evidence from the DOTS trial has recently supported dalbavancin as a potential treatment option for selected patients with S. aureus bacteraemia after initial clinical stabilization133 These agents may be particularly valuable in elderly patients, residents of LTCFs and populations with limited access to prolonged IV therapy.

The role of combination therapy

Combination therapy in severe MRSA infections remains debated. The rationale for this approach derives from in vitro studies showing synergistic activity between anti-MRSA agents (mainly vancomycin and daptomycin) and β-lactam antibiotics (including ceftaroline).134,135 In particular, β-lactams may enhance the bactericidal activity of glycopeptides or lipopeptides through the so-called ‘seesaw effect’, an inverse relationship between reduced susceptibility to vancomycin or daptomycin and increased susceptibility to β-lactams.134 Of interest, in vitro synergistic activity has also been observed between two β-lactam agents (‘dual β-lactam therapy’), likely due to complementary binding to different PBPs.136 No clinical data are currently available for dual β-lactam therapy in MRSA, highlighting the need for RCTs and real-world evidence.

The clinical benefit of combination therapy remains debated. In the CAMERA2 randomized trial, adding an anti-staphylococcal β-lactam to vancomycin or daptomycin in MRSA BSI did not improve patients’ outcome (defined as composite of all-cause mortality, persistent bacteraemia at Day 5, microbiological relapse and microbiological treatment failure) compared with monotherapy, although it resulted in reduced persistent bacteraemia at Day 5 (11% versus 20%, difference −8.9%; 95% CI, −16.6% to −1.2%).137 In a recently published post hoc analysis of the trial, in vitro synergy between vancomycin or daptomycin and β-lactams was associated with lower 14 day mortality in MRSA BSI (2.9% versus 12.8%).138

Other possible combinations, including fosfomycin-, clindamycin- or rifampicin-based combinations, have also been investigated without demonstrating a statistically significant clinical benefit in MRSA BSI.139–141 Moreover, combination therapy may increase the risk of adverse events, drug–drug interactions and treatment discontinuation, reinforcing the need for clear evidence of clinical benefit before its routine use.137–141

Conclusions

Despite the overall decline in MRSA incidence observed in several high-income countries, MRSA continues to represent a major global health threat, particularly in vulnerable populations and in regions with limited surveillance capacity. The epidemiology of MRSA remains highly heterogeneous across healthcare systems and geographical regions, reflecting differences in infection control practices, antimicrobial use and socioeconomic determinants. At the same time, advances in diagnostics, screening strategies and the availability of novel anti-MRSA agents are reshaping both prevention and therapeutic approaches. Future efforts should focus on strengthening global surveillance, improving infection prevention programmes and optimizing antimicrobial stewardship strategies to limit MRSA transmission and improve clinical outcomes.
