What is the cognitive function status and associated factors among adult patients with asthma in northwest Ethiopia? A multicentre hospital-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Nitsuh Kassahun, Atsedemariam Andualem, Melsew Dagne Abate
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionAsthma affects over 300 million people worldwide and is often accompanied by night-time symptoms that disrupt sleep and contribute to cognitive deficits. Despite growing recognition of these interrelated burdens, data from low‐resource settings are scarce. This study addresses this gap by assessing the burden of cognitive dysfunction among adult patients with asthma in northwest Ethiopia.MethodsAn institution-based multicentre cross-sectional study was conducted from 1 June to 30 August 2024. A multistage sampling technique was used to recruit 635 adult asthmatic patients. Data were collected via interviewer-administered questionnaires and medical record reviews. Data were entered into EpiData V.3.1 and analysed using Stata V.14.0. Multivariable logistic regression identified independent predictors of impaired cognitive function at p value<0.05.ResultsOf 635 participants, 209 (32.9%) had impaired cognitive function. In multivariable analysis, factors significantly associated with cognitive impairment included male sex (adjusted OR (AOR)=1.51; 95% CI 1.02 to 2.23) and poor sleep quality (AOR=4.43; 95% CI 2.82 to 6.95).ConclusionA significant proportion of adult patients with asthma experienced cognitive impairment, which was strongly associated with poor sleep quality and male sex, suggesting asthma’s broader systemic impact. Routine screening for sleep and cognitive function should be part of asthma care. Future prospective studies are recommended to better understand causal relationships.Keywords: Asthma, Prevalence, Ethiopia, Cognitive dysfunction

Introduction

Asthma, a chronic inflammatory respiratory disease affecting over 260 million individuals globally, poses a significant and growing burden in low- and middle-income countries, including Ethiopia.1,3 It is clinically characterised by episodic symptoms such as wheezing, chest tightness, shortness of breath and coughing, often worsening at night. Undertreated asthma can cause poor sleep quality, daytime tiredness and cognitive impairment, which impairs physical, emotional and social functioning.4 Globally, 40–70% experience uncontrolled symptoms, underscoring the challenge of achieving optimal disease management.5 6 Most asthma-related deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries, where underdiagnosis and undertreatment are a challenge.

Emerging evidence shows asthma-related cognitive deficits, including impaired memory, attention, executive function and processing speed, particularly in severe or poorly controlled cases.7 8 Proposed mechanisms include: chronic inflammation, intermittent hypoxia, medication side effects, psychosocial stressors and sleep disruption.9 10 These cognitive impairments, observed in over 40–60% of patients, compound the disease burden by hindering productivity, self-management and emotional well-being.11 12 Despite global recognition of this dual burden, research on co-occurring sleep and cognitive dysfunction remains scarce in resource-limited settings. A recent scoping review concluded that the management of sleep disorders in people with asthma is a critically under-researched area, with few tested interventions and no specific clinical guidelines.13 In Ethiopia, where a systematic review estimates over 70% of adults have uncontrolled asthma,14 data on sleep quality and cognitive function are absent, limiting holistic care strategies. Our study therefore addresses this evidence gap by quantifying the burden of poor cognitive function and its association with poor sleep quality in a low-resource setting.

This gap impedes clinical and policy responses aimed at mitigating asthma-related complications. The interplay between poor sleep and cognitive decline, coupled with high rates of uncontrolled disease, underscores the urgency of context-specific research. This study therefore assesses the prevalence of impaired cognitive function and associated factors among adult patients with asthma attending comprehensive specialised hospitals in northwest Ethiopia. By informing integrated care models and policies, the findings aim to advance person-centred asthma management in low-resource settings, ultimately improving health outcomes and quality of life.

Method and materials

A multicentre, institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted from 1 June to 30 August 2024, in three comprehensive specialised hospitals (Felege-Hiwot, Debre-Markos and Debre-Tabor) located in the Amhara region of northwest Ethiopia. These hospitals are among the eight comprehensive specialised facilities in the region and serve a combined catchment population of more than 13 million people. Each hospital provides chronic outpatient care, including follow-up services for patients with asthma. The study population comprised all adult patients with asthma aged 18 years and above who had been on follow-up for at least 3 months before the study period. Patients who were newly diagnosed with asthma (defined as a diagnosis duration of <3 months) or critically ill at the time of data collection were excluded. These criteria ensured that all participants had reached a baseline of clinical stability with an established follow-up history and were physically and mentally capable of completing the cognitive assessment tools without the confounding influence of acute physiological distress. The sample size was determined using a single population proportion formula, assuming 50% prevalence (due to lack of prior studies), n= Z2 P(1-P)d2 with a 95% CI, a 5% margin of error, a 10% non-response rate and a design effect of 1.5. The final sample size was 635 participants.

A multistage sampling technique was employed. Three hospitals were randomly selected from the eight in the region. The sample was proportionally allocated to these three hospitals based on their specific asthma follow-up caseloads (936 patients at Felege-Hiwot, 580 at Debre-Markos and 494 at Debre-Tabor, totalling 2010 patients). Within each hospital, a systematic random sampling technique was employed using a sampling interval (k) of 3 (2010/635 ≈ 3). Medical record numbers of patients on follow-up served as the sampling frame, with the first participant selected via a lottery method. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant prior to data collection. Participants who were unable to read or write provided a thumbprint after the consent form was read aloud in their presence by an impartial witness. Data were collected using a structured and pre-tested interviewer-administered questionnaire and patient record data extraction checklist. The data collection tool was adapted from previously validated instruments and consisted of five main components: socio-demographic information, clinical and disease-related characteristics, psychological factors, sleep quality and cognitive function.

Validated tools were used to assess the key variables: the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) was used to evaluate sleep quality,15 and the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE)16 was used to screen for cognitive function. The Asthma Control Test measured asthma control status, while anxiety and depression were assessed using the GAD-7 (Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7 items) scale17 and PHQ-9 (Patient Health Questionnaire 9 items) scale,18 respectively. The questionnaire was developed in English, translated into Amharic and then back-translated to ensure consistency. A pre-test was conducted on 5% of the sample (n=32) at Gambi Referral Hospital, and necessary revisions were made. A 1-day training session was provided to three data collectors and three supervisors. Supervision and completeness checks were conducted daily.

Dependent variable: Cognitive function (categorised as intact or impaired based on education-adjusted MMSE cut-offs). Intact cognitive function was categorised using education-specific MMSE thresholds: >21 for illiterate individuals, >22 for grades 1–5, >23 for grades 6–11 and >24 for those with grade 12 and above.4 Independent variables included age, sex, marital status, educational status, occupation, monthly income, asthma control status, comorbid conditions, smoking history, anxiety, depression and duration of asthma. Sleep quality (categorised as good or poor using a PSQI score cut-off of 5). Good sleep quality was defined as the global score of PSQI of 5 or below. Anxiety was defined as a GAD-7 score ≥9, and depression was defined as a PHQ-2 score ≥3. Comorbidity was defined as the presence of at least one additional chronic condition (hypertension, diabetes). Smoking status was defined as the use of one or more cigarettes per day in the past month.

Data processing and analysis

Data were coded and entered into EpiData V.4.2.0 and exported to Stata V.14.0 for cleaning, managing and statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise socio-demographic, clinical and psychological variables. Binary logistic regression was performed to identify variables associated with cognitive function. Variables with a p value<0.25 in the bivariable analysis were included in the multivariable model. Adjusted ORs (AORs) with 95% CIs and p values<0.05 were used to declare statistical significance. Multicollinearity was checked using a correlation matrix cut-off value of (|r|≥0.70).

Model fit and discrimination

The Omnibus test (p<0.001) indicates that the model with predictors fits the data significantly better than the null model (likelihood ratio χ² (16)=108.28, p<0.001). The model’s discriminatory power was assessed using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, which showed that the model has good discriminatory ability, approximately 75% correctly distinguishes between impaired and intact cognitive function than by chance (area under the curve=0.75, 95% CI 0.712 to 0.78).19 The Hosmer-Lemeshow test, based on deciles of predicted probabilities (group (10)), indicated an adequate model fit (χ² (8)=7.09, p=0.527).20 The ROC curve shows a true positive rate (sensitivity) on the Y axis and a false positive rate (1-sensitivity) on the X axis. This study included 635 participants, with a median age of 52 (IQR 43–59) years. A significant portion of the participants, 233 (36.7%), reported being unable to read or write. The majority of respondents, 513 (80.8%), resided in urban areas (table 1). Regarding lifestyle factors, 242 (38.1%) of participants reported a history of smoking. Clinical data revealed that 363 (57.2%) of the patients had uncontrolled asthma and 294 (46.30%) had poor sleep quality (table 2).

Discussion

The findings of this study highlight a significant burden of cognitive impairment (32.9%) among adult patients with asthma in northwest Ethiopia, with male sex and poor sleep quality emerging as independent predictors.

The prevalence of cognitive impairment in this study (32.9%; 95% CI 29.36% to 36.67%) aligns with global estimates, which range from 16% to 60% among patients with asthma.812 21,23 For instance, studies from Saudi Arabia21 and Korea8 reported cognitive impairment rates of 57% and 44.1%, respectively, while a study in Egypt found a higher prevalence of 60%.12 These variations may be linked to differences in assessment tools, sample population characteristics and the burden of comorbid conditions, which can exacerbate asthma symptoms and contribute to neurocognitive decline.24 The high prevalence of cognitive impairment observed in patients with asthma highlights the need for routine cognitive screening as part of comprehensive asthma management, particularly in resource-limited settings.

Interestingly, male sex was independently associated with higher odds of cognitive impairment in this study (AOR=1.51; 95% CI 1.02 to 2.23), consistent with findings from a meta-analysis study.7 However, contrasting evidence from a study in China reported a higher risk of cognitive impairment among women over the age of 75.25 These discrepancies may be influenced by regional sociocultural factors, including lifestyle and health behavioural factors. For example, the higher smoking rate among men in this study (38.1%) and a greater burden of comorbidities (37.5% hypertension and 44.9% diabetes) are factors known to correlate with poorer cognitive outcomes. Furthermore, the high prevalence of self-employment (52.76%) in this region often involves outdoor labour with significant occupational exposure to pollutants that can exacerbate systemic inflammation. These findings suggest the need for sex-specific strategies in cognitive screening and asthma care, with particular attention to modifiable risk factors such as smoking and comorbid conditions in male patients.

Poor sleep quality was strongly associated with cognitive impairment in this study (AOR=4.43), consistent with findings from studies conducted in Pakistan,26 China27 and other international settings.28 Poor sleep quality exerts a dual threat to cognitive health: directly impairing cognition in established asthma and indirectly expanding the population at risk by increasing incident asthma.29 In the Ethiopian context, common comorbidities such as allergic rhinitis (29%) and hypertension (37.5%) likely contribute to sleep disturbances. Moreover, the bidirectional relationship between asthma and sleep quality may be intensified by limited access to asthma control medications, as evidenced by the high proportion of participants with uncontrolled asthma (57.2%) in our study. Poor sleep quality is predictive of impaired cognitive function, potentially through mechanisms like disrupted memory consolidation, neuroinflammation and impaired waste clearance in the brain.28 The reliance on self-reported data for sleep quality via the PSQI may introduce recall bias. Notwithstanding these limitations, the findings highlight the importance of incorporating routine sleep assessments into asthma management and addressing sleep-related comorbidities to reduce cognitive impairment and improve overall health outcomes.

This work is among the earliest in Ethiopia to examine the intertwined burden of sleep and cognitive dysfunction in adults with asthma, addressing a critical evidence gap. By using validated instruments, a large sample and rigorous statistical testing, the study delivers precise and credible estimates that strengthen confidence in the findings. Nonetheless, the reliance on self-reported measures may have introduced recall or reporting bias, and unrecognised mental health conditions could have influenced MMSE performance, potentially confounding the observed cognitive outcomes. Encouragingly, recent interventional research suggests that targeted breathing training to reduce hyperventilation (capnometry-assisted respiratory training) can simultaneously improve anxiety and asthma symptoms, offering a potential low-cost, non-pharmacological strategy for settings like Ethiopia where mental health resources are limited.30 Together, these strengths and limitations provide a balanced foundation for interpretation and underscore the need for future studies incorporating objective measures, formal mental health screening and intervention trials that address comorbid anxiety in asthma care.

Conclusion and recommendation

This study identified a high prevalence of cognitive impairment, which was significantly associated with poor sleep quality and male sex. These findings suggest that cognitive and sleep-related issues are important comorbid burdens in asthma management. Routine screening for sleep and cognitive function using validated tools should be integrated into asthma care for early identification and interventions. Future research should explore hormonal and psychosocial factors contributing to sex-based differences in cognitive outcomes and use objective measures like polysomnography and inflammatory biomarkers to further elucidate underlying mechanisms and minimise the recall bias inherent in self-reported cross-sectional data.
