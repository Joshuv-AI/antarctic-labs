Abdominal wall abscess due to Mycolicibacterium neworleansense: a case report
Authors: Yupei Xiang, Bing Fan, Bo Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionNon-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) are widely distributed in the natural environment and can cause skin and soft tissue infections, typically through traumatic injuries or invasive medical procedures, especially in susceptible populations. Commonly encountered pathogens include Mycobacterium marinum and Mycobacterium abscessus, among others. However, cutaneous infections caused by Mycolicibacterium neworleansense are extremely rare. This report presents a case of abdominal wall abscess caused by Mycolicibacterium neworleansense, which was successfully managed with surgical debridement combined with targeted antimicrobial therapy. The aim of this report is to enhance clinical awareness of Mycolicibacterium neworleansense infections, optimize diagnostic and therapeutic strategies, and provide a reference for clinical practice.Case presentationA 74-year-old male with type 2 diabetes mellitus was admitted to the hospital with an abscess at the long-term insulin injection site on his left abdominal wall. After admission, surgical incision and drainage of the abscess were performed. Microbiological culture of the intraoperative pus specimen grew non-tuberculous mycobacteria, which were subsequently identified as Mycolicibacterium neworleansense using 16S rRNA gene sequencing combined with biochemical profiling. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing demonstrated resistance to macrolides and tetracyclines, but susceptibility to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, aminoglycosides, moxifloxacin, and imipenem. Following surgical intervention, the patient received a combined antibiotic regimen of trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and moxifloxacin, leading to successful clinical resolution of the infection.ConclusionMycolicibacterium neworleansense can cause subcutaneous abscesses following injection-related invasive procedures. Successful management of such infections depends on precise pathogen identification via molecular and biochemical approaches, targeted antimicrobial therapy guided by susceptibility testing, and appropriate surgical intervention.Keywords: Non-tuberculous mycobacteria, Mycolicibacterium neworleansense, Abdominal wall abscess, Infections

Introduction

Mycolicibacterium neworleansense is a rapidly growing non-tuberculous mycobacterium (NTM). It was initially classified as part of the Mycobacterium fortuitum complex [1] and was later established as a distinct species based on multigene sequence analysis [2], a classification further solidified by whole-genome sequencing studies [3].This bacterium has only been sporadically detected in clinical specimens, such as a scalp wound and cerebrospinal fluid, with reports of human infections remaining extremely rare. Accurate identification of Mycolicibacterium neworleansense, as with many other NTMs, poses a significant challenge and typically requires advanced molecular techniques, such as sequencing of the 16S rRNA, rpoB, hsp65 genes or next-generation sequencing (mNGS). This article presents a case of an abdominal wall abscess caused by Mycolicibacterium neworleansense, aiming to contribute to the limited clinical literature and to improve the clinical diagnosis and treatment of infections associated with this rare pathogen.

Case presentation

A 74-year-old male presented with a small palpable mass on the left abdominal wall for 10 days without any medical intervention. Four days prior to admission, the mass rapidly enlarged, accompanied by local erythema, swelling, and tenderness, prompting the patient to attend the Department of General Surgery. He had a 10-year history of hypertension and type 2 diabetes mellitus, with blood pressure well controlled by levamlodipine besylate and valsartan. The patient received regular insulin therapy for diabetes, and the lesion was located exactly at his routine abdominal insulin injection site.

On admission, the patient presented with stable vital signs. No remarkable abnormalities were found on general physical examination, and no regional lymphadenopathy was palpable. Local examination identified a 2.0 cm × 1.5 cm mass in the left abdominal wall above the umbilicus, characterized by ill-defined borders, cutaneous erythema and mild tenderness, without obvious fluctuation. A further 5 cm × 7 cm protruding mass was detected below the umbilicus, with intact skin integrity (no ulceration or exudation), soft consistency and unclear boundaries; it was accompanied by local erythema, elevated skin temperature, marked tenderness and obvious fluctuation. Pelvic-abdominal CT showed skin thickening with nodular changes in the left lower abdominal wall below the umbilicus, measuring 39 mm × 21 mm × 37 mm with smooth surface and ill-defined fat interface (Fig. 1A). Focal skin thickening was also seen in the left mid-abdominal wall, with a moderately dense subcutaneous nodule of approximately 15 mm × 11 mm × 12 mm and blurred margins (Fig. 1B). Laboratory tests showed HbA1c 8.2%, white blood cell count 8.34 × 10⁹/L, red blood cell count 4.65 × 10¹²/L, hemoglobin 143 g/L, and platelet count 249 × 10⁹/L. The preliminary diagnosis included multiple left abdominal wall infections complicated with abscess, stage 2 hypertension, and type 2 diabetes mellitus.

On admission day, the patient underwent surgical incision and drainage of the abscess. Intraoperatively, marked inflammatory changes were observed in the perilesional tissues, with the abscess base extending into the intermuscular septum. Purulent fluid and necrotic tissue were thoroughly debrided, and a pus sample was collected for microbiological examination. Postoperatively, the patient received piperacillin-tazobactam for antimicrobial therapy, combined with sodium aescinate for edema relief and insulin to maintain blood glucose homeostasis.

Gram staining of the pus smear showed slender, pleomorphic Gram-positive bacilli, which were also acid-fast positive. The specimen was cultured on Columbia blood agar and chocolate agar; tiny white colonies appeared at 24 h, and grew into medium-sized white colonies with irregular edges and rough, wrinkled surfaces at 48 h (Fig. 2A). Subsequent staining of the purified isolate verified Gram-positive and acid-fast-positive bacillary morphology (Fig. 2B). Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) preliminarily identified the strain as Mycobacterium smegmatis with a confidence score of 1.34, below the species-level threshold of 2.000. To achieve definitive identification, 16S rRNA gene sequencing was conducted. Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) alignment against the NCBI database showed 100% sequence identity to the 16S rRNA gene of Mycolicibacterium neworleansense ATCC 49,404 (Accession No. NR_042914.1).

Biochemical phenotypic tests were carried out, showing negative sorbitol fermentation and positive reactions for nitrate reduction, catalase, and xylose fermentation; this biochemical profile matched the typical characteristics of Mycolicibacterium neworleansense. Based on the 16S rRNA gene sequencing findings and biochemical profiles, the isolate was ultimately confirmed as Mycolicibacterium neworleansense.

Antimicrobial susceptibility was determined by the broth microdilution method, with results interpreted according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines (M100-ED32). The isolate was resistant to rifampin (MIC > 16 µg/mL), clarithromycin (MIC > 64 µg/mL), doxycycline (MIC > 32 µg/mL), minocycline (MIC > 128 µg/mL), and azithromycin (MIC > 32 µg/mL). It was susceptible to cefoxitin (MIC ≤ 4 µg/mL), moxifloxacin (MIC ≤ 0.125 µg/mL), trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (MIC ≤ 8 µg/mL), amikacin (MIC ≤ 1 µg/mL), ethambutol (MIC ≤ 5 µg/mL), imipenem-cilastatin (MIC ≤ 0.5 µg/mL), and linezolid (MIC ≤ 8 µg/mL). An intermediate susceptibility was observed for tobramycin (MIC = 16 µg/mL).

On postoperative day 3, the anti-infective regimen was switched to oral clarithromycin at a dose of 500 mg twice daily. In light of the antimicrobial susceptibility results, the therapeutic strategy was upgraded on postoperative day 10 to a combination of trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (960 mg twice daily) plus moxifloxacin (400 mg once daily). The patient was discharged 12 days postoperatively with marked clinical improvement and continued the dual-antibiotic regimen. The surgical wound achieved complete healing after more than two months of treatment. Antibiotic therapy was discontinued solely at the patient’s explicit and insistent request. A six-month follow-up revealed no local recurrence or disseminated infection, indicating a successful therapeutic outcome (Fig. 3).

Discussion

This case report is the first to identify Mycolicibacterium neworleansense, an extremely rare mycobacterial species worldwide, as the pathogen responsible for cutaneous infection in China. Favorable clinical outcomes were achieved via timely pathogen detection using smear microscopy and culture, which guided combined surgical debridement and susceptibility-tailored antimicrobial treatment. This finding underscores the importance of integrated diagnosis and management for rare NTM infections.

Mycolicibacterium neworleansense was initially assigned in 1991 to the third biovariant complex of Mycobacterium fortuitum [1]. In 2004, Schinsky MF et al. defined it as an independent species and formally named it Mycolicibacterium neworleansense based on multigene phylogenetic analysis and phenotypic profiling [2]. Later, Asmar et al. conducted whole-genome sequencing of the reference strain ATCC 49,404, which genomically validated its taxonomic position as a novel member of the Mycobacterium fortuitum complex [3]. Like most NTMs, this species is difficult to identify by conventional phenotypic methods. The Expert Consensus on the Molecular Diagnosis of Nontuberculous Mycobacterial Diseases advocates a panel of molecular approaches for NTM identification: 16S rRNA, 16–23S rRNA internal transcribed spacer (ITS), rpoB and hsp65 gene sequencing; PCR-restriction fragment length polymorphism analysis (PRA); metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS); whole-genome sequencing (WGS); and MALDI-TOF MS [4, 5].

According to Schinsky MF et al. [2], Mycolicibacterium neworleansense shares highly similar hsp65 gene sequences and PRA patterns with other members of the third Mycobacterium fortuitum complex, hampering precise species identification. MALDI-TOF MS also has inherent limitations in routine identification, as observed in our case where the isolate was initially misidentified as Mycobacterium smegmatis. Furthermore, ITS and rpoB gene-based assays lack sufficient validation for identifying Mycolicibacterium neworleansense, and their discriminatory capacity needs further evaluation. While mNGS and WGS can achieve definitive species-level identification, their high cost restricts widespread clinical use [6].

Among existing detection methods, 16S rRNA gene sequencing remains the most practical and reliable option. Nevertheless, Mycolicibacterium neworleansense shares high 16S rRNA sequence identity with Mycolicibacterium bonickei, which can lead to erroneous identification. Notably, the two species possess distinct phenotypic traits: Mycolicibacterium neworleansense is positive for xylose and erythritol fermentation, whereas Mycolicibacterium bonickei yields negative results for both tests, providing a useful criterion for differentiation. In the present case, the isolate shared 100% 16S rRNA sequence identity with Mycolicibacterium neworleansense ATCC 49,404 and also high identity with Mycolicibacterium bonickei. Final species confirmation was therefore established by positive xylose and erythritol fermentation results.

Human infection with Mycolicibacterium neworleansense remains extremely rare in clinical practice. A systematic literature search across Chinese-language and English-language databases (Wanfang, CNKI, PubMed) found no previously confirmed human case reports of infection with this pathogen. Available records indicate that Mycolicibacterium neworleansense ATCC 49,404 was first isolated from a scalp wound in New Orleans, USA, while one Chinese study recovered this strain from a cerebrospinal fluid specimen [7]. In our case, the patient presented with obvious local inflammatory manifestations and progressed to abscess formation. Smear microscopy and culture of the abscess specimen yielded consistent results, and the patient achieved a good response to targeted antimicrobial therapy. Accordingly, Mycolicibacterium neworleansense was confirmed as the causative agent of the abdominal wall abscess.

This represents a rare case of subcutaneous injection-related cutaneous infection induced by Mycolicibacterium neworleansense. Notably, injection procedures are a well-recognized risk factor for NTM skin and soft tissue infections [8, 9]. The patient suffered from long-standing type 2 diabetes requiring daily abdominal insulin injections. Higher cutaneous microbial burden on the abdomen during hot summer weather, combined with insufficient skin disinfection before injection, likely facilitated bacterial invasion. Therefore, rigorous compliance with aseptic protocols and guaranteed sterility of medical supplies during invasive procedures are essential to prevent cutaneous infections due to Mycolicibacterium neworleansense and other NTM.

Clinically, this infection showed an acute onset, with the mass enlarging markedly within six days—an observation consistent with the rapid-growing nature of this bacterium. In vitro culture revealed visible colony growth on solid media within 24 h, with typical morphological features fully developed by 48 h. This growth characteristic is conducive to early etiological diagnosis.

No established clinical guidelines or consensus recommendations are available for the antimicrobial treatment of Mycolicibacterium neworleansense infections. While treatment regimens for Mycobacterium fortuitum are frequently used as a reference, recent studies have shown that different species within the Mycobacterium fortuitum complex may exhibit distinct antimicrobial resistance patterns [10–12]. In our case, antimicrobial susceptibility testing indicated that the isolate was resistant to macrolides (e.g., clarithromycin and azithromycin)—agents commonly used in the treatment of Mycobacterium fortuitum infections. This highlights the critical importance of species-level NTM identification combined with in vitro antimicrobial susceptibility testing for formulating effective treatment regimens.

The Diagnosis and Treatment Guidelines for Nontuberculous Mycobacterial Diseases (2020 Edition) stress that, in addition to selecting active antimicrobial drugs, ensuring an adequate treatment course is crucial. A minimum treatment duration of four months is generally recommended to reduce the risk of recurrence. However, due to the patient’s firm preference, the total antibiotic course was limited to 78 days, with susceptible antibiotics discontinued after only 68 days. Although shortening the antimicrobial treatment duration is not recommended, successful surgical intervention and eradication of the infectious focus may have contributed to the favorable outcome despite the abbreviated course.

Therefore, beyond pharmacotherapy, surgical debridement is a key intervention in the management of Mycolicibacterium neworleansense infections, especially in cases with extensive involvement, abscess formation, or poor response to antimicrobial therapy [13–15]. Active surgical intervention can substantially improve clinical outcomes. In this patient, who presented with a well-formed abscess, the combination of surgical drainage, debridement, and targeted antimicrobial therapy resulted in significant clinical improvement.

Additionally, potential adverse effects of long-term combination antimicrobial therapy require close monitoring to avoid drug-related discomfort. Of note, the patient showed no significant liver or kidney dysfunction after prolonged administration of moxifloxacin and co-trimoxazole (trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole), nor did they experience neuropsychiatric adverse effects associated with prolonged fluoroquinolone use. Only mild gastrointestinal symptoms occurred, which were effectively alleviated by administering medications postprandially. Ultimately, the combination of successful surgical intervention, an effective antibiotic regimen, and proactive management of adverse effects led to a favorable therapeutic outcome.

Conclusion

We herein report a case of abdominal wall abscess caused by the rare pathogen Mycolicibacterium neworleansense, successfully treated with surgical debridement and targeted antimicrobial therapy. This case emphasizes that NTM infection should be considered in patients with skin and soft tissue infections, especially those with predisposing factors such as a history of injections.Accurate identification of Mycolicibacterium neworleansense remains challenging in microbiological practice. Although 16S rRNA gene sequencing is currently the most reliable method, the high 16S rRNA sequence homology between Mycolicibacterium neworleansenseand its close relatives (e.g., Mycolicibacterium bonickei) should be considered, and phenotypic tests such as xylose fermentation are recommended for differential diagnosis.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing showed resistance to some commonly used macrolides, suggesting that empiric regimens for the Mycobacterium fortuitum complex should not be adopted empirically; instead, therapy should be guided by actual susceptibility data. Based on the findings of this case, we recommend early pus smear microscopy and microbial culture to guide the initial clinical management of Mycolicibacterium neworleansense-associated cutaneous and soft tissue infections. Precise anti-infective therapy depends on species-level identification via molecular techniques combined with antimicrobial susceptibility testing. Additionally, timely surgical intervention is crucial for infection control and optimizing treatment outcomes in cases with abscess formation.
