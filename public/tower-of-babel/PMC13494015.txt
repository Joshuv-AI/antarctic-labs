The eGFR difference between creatinine-based and cystatin-C-based equations is also important in an Asian population within the J-CKD-DB-Ex
Authors: Takaya Nakashima, Hajime Nagasu, Akira Hirano, Masao Iwagami, Tadahiro Goto, Naoki Kashihara, Masaomi Nangaku, Yosuke Hirakawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent studies have shown that a large negative estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) difference (eGFRdiff), defined as the cystatin C–based eGFR minus the creatinine-based eGFR, is related with kidney events. However, whether this association applies to the Japanese population remains unclear. This study assessed the importance of a negative eGFRdiff in the Japanese population. This was a retrospective cohort study using the J-CKD-DB-Ex. The association between baseline eGFRdiff and kidney composite outcome defined as progression to CKD stage G5 (eGFRcr < 15 mL/min/1.73 m2) or ≥ 40% decline in eGFRcr within 5 years was examined with Cox proportional hazards models. Restricted cubic splines were used to assess nonlinearity. eGFRdiff quartiles were Q1 < − 14.83, Q2 − 14.83 to < − 6.62, Q3 − 6.62 to < 1.72, and Q4 ≥ 1.72 mL/min/1.73 m2. Compared with Q3, Q1 was associated with a higher risk of the composite outcome (HR, 2.11; 95% CI 1.24–3.59). Spline analyses showed that hazards increased sharply as eGFRdiff became more negative and were relatively flat around near-zero to positive values. A large negative eGFRdiff was independently associated with a higher risk of kidney disease progression in a Japanese CKD cohort, supporting its use as a prognostic marker.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-57444-y.Keywords: Creatinine, Cystatin C, Chronic kidney disease, eGFR differenceSubject terms: Diseases, Medical research, Nephrology

Introduction

Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) is currently the global standard for assessing kidney function. Two biomarkers, creatinine and cystatin C, are used to calculate the eGFR. Since creatinine is a metabolic byproduct that can be measured employing an enzymatic assay, it has been widely used in clinical practice. However, the creatinine-based assessment of kidney function has several limitations, including its dependence on muscle mass and tubular secretion. Cystatin C–based eGFR (eGFRcys) methods were developed to overcome these limitations1. eGFRcys, either alone or in combination with creatinine, has stronger associations with overall mortality and kidney-related outcomes than creatinine-based eGFR(eGFRcr) alone2.

The difference between these estimates (eGFRdiff, defined in this study as eGFRcys minus eGFRcr) has often been attributed to muscle mass. Specifically, frail patients tend to have low muscle mass, leading to an overestimation of eGFRcr3. Such non-kidney determinants of eGFRcr also have an impact on CKD progression4, thus eGFRdiff may be a prognostic marker. A recent meta-analysis assessed the prognostic value of eGFRdiff in patients with kidney failure requiring replacement therapy. It showed that a large negative eGFRdiff was associated with a higher risk of kidney failure5. However, racial differences should be considered when translating evidence regarding the importance of eGFRdiff in clinical practice. For example, extrapolating eGFR equations developed in European populations does not yield precise GFR estimates in Asian populations6,7. Consequently, several Asian countries have developed their own eGFR equations or introduced correction factors for existing equations6,8,9. Given that most of the existing evidence demonstrating the clinical relevance of a large negative eGFRdiff has been derived primarily from European populations, it is important to determine whether a large negative eGFRdiff has a similar impact on CKD progression in Asian patients.

Therefore, we examined the association between eGFRdiff and kidney prognosis in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) using the Japanese Chronic Kidney Disease Database (J-CKD-DB-Ex)10, a real-world CKD database in Japan.

Methods

Study design and data source

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using the J-CKD-DB-Ex, a longitudinal electronic health record–derived registry automatically extracted from participating Japanese university hospitals11–13. The parent registry (J-CKD-DB-Ex) compiles data from 21 university hospitals nationwide and captures routine inpatient and outpatient encounters, diagnoses, prescriptions, and laboratory measurements. CKD is identified among adults (≥ 18 years) based on dipstick proteinuria (≥ 1 +) and/or reduced creatinine-based eGFR (< 60 mL/min/1.73 m2). In this registry, measurement of serum cystatin C is not routine but left to the judgement of the treating physician, especially when a more precise assessment of GFR is required (e.g., when creatinine-based estimates are considered unreliable owing to altered muscle mass). At the time of data extraction, the J-CKD-DB-Ex included 251,659 patients with CKD, of whom 20,569 had at least one same-day paired measurement of serum creatinine and cystatin C; these 20,569 patients constituted the study population. Patient records were anonymized and participating institutions used an opt-out approach in accordance with Japanese ethical guidelines. All study protocols were approved by the Nagasaki University Graduate School of Biomedical Sciences (IRB No. 251120044) and the Kawasaki Medical University and Hospital Ethics Committee (No. 6933). The construction followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement14. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Review Board of the Nagasaki University Graduate School of Biomedical Sciences owing to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of de-identified data.

Study population and index date

The eligible participants were adults with at least two same-day paired measurements of serum creatinine and cystatin C levels. We defined the index date as the earliest paired creatinine-cystatin C measurement that occurred at least six months after the first paired measurement in the database. This 6-month interval was required for three reasons: to ensure at least two occasions of paired testing so that the baseline eGFRdiff reflected a confirmed rather than an isolated value; to reduce the influence of acute or transient changes in creatinine or cystatin C on baseline classification; and to establish a defined period of prior observation to ascertain baseline comorbidities and medications. Participants were required to have CKD stage G2-G4 at the index date, defined by eGFRcr, and to undergo longitudinal follow-up after the index date. Patients with a history of thyroid disease or systemic corticosteroid therapy within 180 days of the index date were excluded from the study. The patient flowchart is shown in Fig. 1.

Exposure: eGFR difference

On the index date, we calculated eGFRcr using the race-free CKD-EPI 2021 creatinine equation15 and eGFRcys using the CKD-EPI 2012 cystatin C equation16. Baseline eGFRdiff was defined as follows:

We categorized baseline eGFRdiff into quartiles using fixed cutoffs derived from the cohort distribution (25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles: − 14.83, − 6.62, and 1.72 mL/min/1.73 m2). Because Q3 represented a near-zero eGFRdiff, Q3 was prespecified as the reference category for the primary analysis. In addition to the quartile-based analysis, the eGFRdiff was analyzed as a continuous variable, as a linear term in the Cox model and using restricted cubic splines (4 degrees of freedom) to assess nonlinearity. The eGFRdiff was also categorized using clinically defined thresholds (< − 15, − 15 to 15, and > 15 mL/min/1.73 m2), with the concordant category (− 15 to 15) as the reference.

Outcomes and follow-up

The primary endpoint was the kidney composite outcome, defined as the first occurrence of progression to CKD stage G5 (eGFRcr < 15 mL/min/1.73 m2) or a ≥ 40% decline in eGFRcr from the index baseline. Follow-up began at the index date and was administratively censored at five years; time-to-event analyses were conducted within this 5-year window. As an additional outcome, the annual rate of change in eGFRcr (eGFR slope) was evaluated using all longitudinal eGFRcr measurements within a 5-year window.

Covariates

The primary multivariate model was adjusted for age, sex, diabetes, hypertension, baseline eGFRcr, and dipstick proteinuria on the index date17–19. The definitions of diabetes and hypertension were supplemented using prescription data to identify treated patients when diagnoses were incomplete. Quantitative albuminuria/proteinuria measures were missing in a substantial proportion of patients (urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (uACR): 67.6%; urine protein-to-creatinine ratio (uPCR): 89.0%); therefore, uACR and uPCR were not included in the primary model, and dipstick proteinuria was used as the primary covariate for proteinuria. Among the covariates included in the primary model, dipstick proteinuria was the only one with missing data. Missing dipstick proteinuria was handled using multiple imputations as described in the Statistical Analysis section.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized by quartiles of eGFRdiff values. We estimated the 5-year cumulative incidence of kidney composite outcomes using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared the curves using the log-rank test. We evaluated the associations between eGFRdiff quartiles and outcomes using Cox proportional hazards models, with Q3 as the reference and adjusting for the covariate set. Missing dipstick proteinuria results on the index date were handled using multiple imputations with chained equations (m = 100) under the missing-at-random assumption. Cox models were fitted within each imputed dataset and pooled using Rubin’s rules20. The eGFRdiff was additionally modelled as a continuous linear term in the multivariable Cox model. Collinearity between eGFRdiff and baseline eGFRcr was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs). The eGFR slope was estimated using linear mixed-effects models with a random intercept and a random slope per patient; differences in the annual slope across eGFRdiff quartiles were evaluated using the quartile-by-time interaction. For the subgroup analyses, the interaction was tested by adding a multiplicative (eGFRdiff quartile × subgroup) term to the Cox model, with multivariate Wald tests pooled across the imputed datasets.

Subgroup analyses

Exploratory subgroup analyses were performed for pre-specified subgroups defined by age (≥ 65 years), sex, diabetes, hypertension, and baseline eGFR category.

Sensitivity analyses

We (1) modelled eGFRdiff continuously and assessed nonlinearity using restricted cubic splines, and performed a trend test by entering eGFRdiff quartiles as an ordinal variable (1–4) in the Cox model; (2) evaluated the robustness of the association for large negative eGFRdiff (Q1 vs. Q3) across alternative handling of missing dipstick proteinuria at the index date, including a missing-category approach and a complete-case analysis; (3) recalculated eGFRdiff using a Japan-specific cystatin C recalibration factor (× 0.977) 21 and Japanese creatinine-based equations (MDRD 20098 and the Japanese coefficient for CKD-EPI creatinine22), redefined quartiles accordingly, and repeated the primary adjusted Cox model; and (4) evaluated the component outcomes separately (progression to CKD stage G5 and ≥ 40% eGFR decline). All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.3.2.

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

Figure 1 illustrates the participant selection process. Of 251,659 patients with CKD in the J-CKD-DB-Ex database, 20,569 had same-day paired creatinine and cystatin C levels. After applying the eligibility criteria the final analytic cohort comprised 1585 participants, evenly distributed across quartiles of baseline eGFRdiff (Q1 [< − 14.83], n = 397; Q2 [− 14.83 to < 6.62], n = 396; Q3 [− 6.62 to < 1.72], n = 396; Q4 [≥ 1.72 mL/min/1.73 m2], n = 396). The characteristics of the 1585 included patients and 18,967 patients with paired creatinine–cystatin C measurements who were not included are compared in Supplementary Table S1; included patients had, by eligibility design, more repeated paired measurements over a longer period (median 7 tests over 1309 days vs. 1 test in excluded patients), whereas demographic and kidney-function characteristics differed only modestly (all standardized mean differences < 0.4).

The baseline characteristics across the quartiles are shown in Table 1. Relative to Q3, participants in Q1 and Q2 were older (median age 74 and 72 vs. 71 years) and had a higher prevalence of diabetes (35% and 30% vs. 29%), while the prevalence of hypertension was similar in Q1 (48% vs. 49%) but higher in Q2 (53% vs. 49%). In contrast, participants in Q4 were younger (median age, 65 years) and had a lower prevalence of diabetes (25% vs. 29%) and hypertension (41% vs. 49%). Baseline kidney function differed by filtration marker: compared with Q3, Q1 exhibited higher eGFRcr (75 vs. 61 mL/min/1.73 m2) but lower eGFRcys (49 vs 58), whereas Q4 had significantly higher eGFRcys (78 vs 58). Median eGFRdiff increased monotonically across quartiles (− 21, − 10, − 3, and 8 mL/min/1.73 m2 from Q1 to Q4). Dipstick proteinuria was missing in 27.0% of the patients and was more frequent in Q1–Q2 (31–30%) than in Q3–Q4 (23%).

Primary kidney composite outcome

The Kaplan–Meier curves illustrated significant differences in the 5-year cumulative incidence of the kidney composite outcome across the eGFRdiff quartiles (log-rank P < 0.001), as shown in Fig. 2. Within five years, the number of composite events was 43 in Q1, 46 in Q2, 59 in Q3, and 35 in Q4.

Over a maximum follow-up period of 5 years, kidney composite outcomes occurred in 43 (10.8%), 46 (11.6%), 59 (14.9%), and 35 (8.8%) participants in Q1, Q2, Q3, and Q4, respectively, with incidence rates of 5.79, 5.63, 7.22, and 3.70 per 100 person-years.

The adjusted associations between baseline eGFR difference quartiles and kidney composite outcomes are presented in Table 2. In the multivariable Cox model pooled across 100 multiply imputed datasets, Q1 was associated with a higher risk of the kidney composite outcome compared with Q3 (hazard ratio [HR] 2.11; 95% CI 1.24–3.59; P = 0.006). Q2 showed a modest elevation in risk less precisely estimated (HR 1.41; 95% CI 0.90–2.23; P = 0.135). Q4 had a point estimate suggesting lower risk than Q3, though the confidence interval included the null (HR 0.66; 95% CI 0.41–1.05; P = 0.078). Table 2 presents the unadjusted and adjusted estimates. The crude event rates and adjusted hazard ratios showed opposite ordering because the baseline eGFRcr differed significantly across quartiles. Baseline eGFRcr was lowest in Q3 (median 61 mL/min/1.73 m2) and highest in Q1 (median 75); because the composite outcome is anchored to eGFRcr thresholds, patients in Q3 were closer to these thresholds at baseline and accumulated the most crude events. In a sequential-adjustment analysis (Supplementary Table S2), the Q1-vs-Q3 hazard ratio was 0.81 when unadjusted and remained below 1 after adjustment for age, sex, diabetes, and hypertension (0.89), but reversed to 1.90 once baseline eGFRcr was added and was 2.11 in the fully adjusted model. Thus, the association emerged specifically after accounting for baseline kidney function.

Association with continuous eGFRdiff and trend

Restricted cubic spline analyses of continuous eGFRdiff showed a nonlinear association with kidney outcomes, with hazard ratios increasing sharply as eGFRdiff became more negative and remaining relatively flat at near-zero to positive values (Fig. 3). Similar patterns were observed in the spline analyses of the component outcomes (progression to CKD stage G5 and ≥ 40% eGFR decline) (Supplementary Figure S1). In addition, a trend analysis treating quartiles as an ordinal variable showed a significant monotonic association, indicating lower risk with higher eGFRdiff quartiles (per 1-quartile increase: HR 0.69; 95% CI 0.57–0.83; P < 0.001). When modelled as a continuous linear term, each 10 mL/min/1.73 m2 more negative eGFRdiff was associated with a hazard ratio of 1.51 (95% CI 1.27–1.79) for the kidney composite outcome (per 1 SD more negative, HR 1.76, 1.39–2.23; per 1 mL/min/1.73 m2 higher, HR 0.96, 0.94–0.98; all I < 0.001; Supplementary Table S3).

Subgroup analyses

In the exploratory subgroup analyses, the association between Q1 and higher kidney event risk was consistent across the pre-specified subgroups (Supplementary Figure S2). The estimated association was more pronounced among participants aged < 65 years, women, those with diabetes or hypertension, and those with baseline CKD stage G3a/G3b but remained above the null level in the G2 subgroup. The tests for interaction were not significant for most subgroups (P for interaction: sex 0.065, diabetes 0.502, hypertension 0.730, and baseline eGFR category 0.618), with a borderline interaction by age (P = 0.049) (Supplementary Table S4). Estimates for the G4 subgroup are unavailable because of sparse data.

Sensitivity analyses

The association between a large negative eGFR difference (Q1 vs. Q3) and higher kidney disease risk was robust across multiple sensitivity analyses. When missing dipstick proteinuria was handled by a missing-category approach or by complete-case analysis, the Q1-vs-Q3 hazard ratio was 2.20 (95% CI 1.40–3.45) and 2.26 (1.31–3.90), respectively. For component endpoints, Q1 was associated with ≥ 40% eGFR decline (HR 2.97; 95% CI 1.21–7.29; P = 0.017), whereas the association with progression to CKD stage G5 was weaker and less precisely estimated (HR 1.49; 95% CI 0.90–2.48; P = 0.121) (Supplementary Table S5). The results were also consistent when the eGFR values were recalculated using the Japan-specific cystatin C recalibration factor (× 0.977) or Japanese creatinine-based equations (MDRD 2009 and Japanese CKD-EPI creatinine coefficient), with Q1 remaining associated with a higher risk across all three approaches (Table 3). When the eGFRdiff was categorized using clinically defined thresholds (Supplementary Table S6), a large negative eGFRdiff (< − 15 mL/min/1.73 m2; n = 389) was associated with a higher risk than the concordant category (− 15 to 15; n = 1,101) (HR 1.76; 95% CI 1.11–2.80; P = 0.017), whereas a positive eGFRdiff (> 15; n = 95) was not significantly associated (HR 0.41; 95% CI 0.13–1.31). The annual rate of eGFRcr decline differed across eGFRdiff quartiles (Supplementary Table S7, Supplementary Figure S3): the mean annual slope was steepest in Q1 (− 3.63 mL/min/1.73 m2/year; 95% CI − 4.22 to − 3.03) and progressively shallower across Q2 (− 2.18), Q3 (− 1.86; reference), and Q4 (− 1.32) (Q1 vs Q3, P < 0.001), consistent after covariate adjustment.

Discussion

In this study, we examined the association between eGFRdiff and kidney prognosis using longitudinal eGFR data from 1585 patients with CKD enrolled in the J-CKD-DB-Ex. When patients were divided into quartiles of eGFRdiff based on cystatin C levels, the hazard ratio for the composite kidney outcome was the highest in the Q1 group, namely, those with a large negative eGFRdiff. This finding is consistent with analyses that treated eGFRdiff as a continuous variable using a restricted cubic spline model. To date, most studies on eGFRdiff have focused on all-cause mortality and/or cardiovascular disease as outcomes; studies on kidney outcomes are limited23. As this study used the J-CKD-DB-Ex, which includes patients with CKD in Japan, our results suggest that a large negative eGFRdiff may predict adverse kidney outcomes in Asian populations, which is consistent with previous reports that mainly included European populations.

The similarities and differences between our study and the recent meta-analysis by Estrella et al. can be summarized as follows5. First, only patients with CKD in Japan were examined; however, the meta-analysis included only a limited number of Asian patients. Second, we defined the Q1 group based on quartiles of eGFRdiff, with a median eGFRdiff of − 21. In contrast, the meta-analysis defined a large negative eGFRdiff as eGFRcys at least 30% lower than eGFRcr. Given that the median eGFRcr in our study was 68 mL/min/1.73 m2, the magnitude of eGFRdiff in our Q1 group appears comparable to the large negative eGFRdiff. Third, because the J-CKD-DB-Ex database consists exclusively of patients with CKD, our study population differed from those in the meta-analysis in several baseline characteristics. The participants in our study were older, had a lower baseline eGFR, and had a higher prevalence of diabetes mellitus. Despite differences in patient backgrounds, our findings consistently showed that patients with a large negative eGFRdiff had a worse kidney prognosis when baseline kidney function was adjusted for eGFRcr. Exploratory subgroup analyses further suggested that the association between a large negative eGFRdiff and a higher risk of kidney disease was broadly consistent across the prespecified subgroups (Supplementary Figure S2).

Given that eGFRdiff is defined using eGFRcr, we examined whether adjustment for baseline eGFRcr introduced collinearity. The eGFRdiff and baseline eGFRcr were only weakly correlated (Pearson r = − 0.16), and variance inflation factors were low (1.15 for the eGFRdiff and 1.04 for baseline eGFRcr; Supplementary Table S8); the variance of eGFRcys dominates the eGFRdiff, so the two behave as near-independent predictors. Therefore, the opposite ordering of the crude and adjusted estimates reflects confounding by baseline kidney function rather than collinearity.

Regarding the clinical implications of a positive eGFRdiff, our study demonstrated that patients with a positive eGFRdiff were not clearly associated with lower kidney risk (Q4 vs. Q3: HR 0.66; 95% CI 0.41–1.05), consistent with the meta-analysis results5. One possible explanation is the early development of CKD stage G5 or earlier initiation of kidney replacement therapy in patients with elevated creatinine levels due to non-kidney-related determinants. To address this possibility, we performed sensitivity analyses separating outcomes into progression to CKD stage G5 and a ≥ 40% decline in eGFR (Supplementary Table S5, Supplementary Figure S1). Even when the outcome was restricted to a ≥ 40% decline in eGFR, reflecting intra-individual changes in kidney function, a positive eGFRdiff was not associated with better kidney prognosis.

Racial differences should be considered when implementing eGFRdiff to predict kidney prognosis. Because different eGFR calculation equations are used even in East Asian countries, the interpretation of eGFRdiff, defined as eGFRcys minus eGFRcr, may vary across populations. In many Asian countries, eGFRcr equations were originally developed through clinical studies that compared eGFRcr with the measured GFR obtained using inulin clearance or radionuclide scintigraphy. Subsequently, eGFRcr equations that are applicable to multiple races and ethnicities were developed. However, some populations, including Japanese individuals, are underrepresented in the derivation cohorts. Regarding eGFRcys, extrapolation of the CKD-EPI equation to Japanese populations has been reported to overestimate eGFR; therefore, a coefficient of 0.977 was applied to the cystatin C–based CKD-EPI equation in Japan21. Thus, although Japanese individuals represent a unique context for estimating eGFR, the value of our findings lies in demonstrating that the prognostic importance of a negative eGFRdiff is similar to that observed in European populations. Importantly, the main findings remained unchanged when applying Japan-specific cystatin C recalibration (× 0.977) and Japanese creatinine-based equations (Table 3), supporting the robustness of the choice of the eGFR equation.

Non-kidney-related determinants of serum creatinine and cystatin C levels should be considered to better understand the clinical implications of eGFRdiff. Muscle mass is a major determinant of serum creatinine levels and kidney function. Patients with sarcopenia tend to have lower serum creatinine levels because creatinine is generated in the skeletal muscle. As sarcopenia is a known risk factor for kidney failure4, this mechanism may partly explain the higher risk of kidney failure observed in patients with a large negative eGFRdiff. In contrast, several non-kidney-related factors such as smoking are associated with increased cystatin C levels3, which may also contribute to a large negative eGFRdiff. Information on muscle mass–related variables, including body mass index (BMI) and smoking status, was unavailable in the J-CKD-DB-Ex project; therefore, these potential confounders could not be adjusted for. However, the absence of the BMI may have had a limited effect on these results. The importance of eGFRdiff was demonstrated after adjusting for proteinuria, suggesting that the effects of obesity-related glomerulopathy are limited. BMI is also relevant when interpreting eGFRdiff because it reflects muscle mass. However, high BMI can cause glomerular hyperfiltration, which may lower serum creatinine levels24. In other words, the relationship between BMI and serum creatinine levels was neither linear nor unidirectional.

This study had some limitations. First, because the J-CKD-DB-Ex extracts only admission data, prescription records, laboratory values, and disease diagnoses from electronic medical records, residual confounding factors such as smoking were missing. It should be noted that the J-CKD-DB-Ex does not include the results of physical measurements, such as height and body weight; therefore, BMI, which is associated with muscle mass, could not be included as a covariate. Second, urinary protein level was assessed as a categorical variable based on urine dipstick test results. Quantitative measures such as the urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio or protein-to-creatinine ratio are more strongly associated with kidney outcomes; however, such data are limited in this database. Even when dipstick test results were used, proportions of the results were missing. Missing values were imputed using multiple imputations for the main analysis, and the results were supported by sensitivity analyses, in which missing proteinuria values were treated as a single category or patients without proteinuria information were excluded from the analysis set (Supplementary Table S9). Third, information on race and ethnicity was not systematically collected. Although the J-CKD-DB-Ex population is presumed to consist predominantly of Japanese patients or, at least, East Asian individuals, patients of other racial or ethnic backgrounds may also be included. Fourth, because cystatin C was measured at the physician’s discretion and the analytic cohort required repeated longitudinal paired testing, the cohort represented patients with CKD undergoing longitudinal cystatin C monitoring, and generalization to the broader CKD population should be made with this in mind. However, as shown in Supplementary Table S1, the included and excluded patients with paired measurements were broadly comparable, except for the frequency of repeated testing.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that a large negative eGFRdiff value is associated with composite kidney outcomes. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies, despite differences in race and baseline kidney function, and suggest that the prognostic value of eGFRdiff is generalizable across populations. Further studies are warranted to determine whether incorporating eGFRdiff into clinical decision making can improve the management of patients with chronic kidney disease.
