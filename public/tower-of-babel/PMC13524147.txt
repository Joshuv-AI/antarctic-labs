Hyponatremia risk with antidepressant use: A retrospective study
Authors: Tsung-Pi Lien, Wen-Chun Liu, Li-Yi Tang, Chun-Hao Yin, Jin-Shuen Chen, Yao-Shen Chen, Ying-Chun Li, Kuan-Pin Su, Ren-In Chang, Cheng-Ho Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Serotonergic antidepressants, particularly selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), are associated with an increased risk of hyponatremia, whereas mirtazapine appears to confer a lower risk. However, evidence regarding newer antidepressants, specifically agomelatine, remains limited. This study aimed to evaluate the risk of hyponatremia associated with agomelatine compared with mirtazapine, escitalopram, and venlafaxine.Methods:This retrospective cohort study analyzed electronic medical records from Kaohsiung Veterans General Hospital. Antidepressant-naïve patients who initiated at least 2 weeks of monotherapy with agomelatine, escitalopram, venlafaxine, or mirtazapine between May 2013 and April 2023 were included. Hyponatremia was identified using serum sodium measurements. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to assess the association between antidepressant use and hyponatremia after adjusting for demographic, clinical, and medication-related factors.Results:The incidence rates of hyponatremia were 5.8% for mirtazapine, 6.9% for agomelatine, 8.5% for escitalopram, and 10.2% for venlafaxine. In the univariate analysis, venlafaxine was associated with a higher risk of hyponatremia than was mirtazapine (odds ratio [OR] = 1.80, 95% CI: 1.09-2.97, p = 0.021). However, after adjustment, the risk of hyponatremia did not differ significantly among the antidepressants (agomelatine: adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 0.79, escitalopram: aOR = 1.18, venlafaxine: aOR = 0.87; all p > 0.05). Independent predictors of hyponatremia included older age (aOR = 1.02, p < 0.001), malignancy (aOR = 2.52, p < 0.001), diabetes mellitus (aOR = 1.64, p < 0.001), and concomitant use of anticancer agents (aOR = 2.72, p < 0.001).Conclusion:These findings underscore the complexity of hyponatremia etiologies and highlight the need for individualized treatment strategies that account for comorbidities and potential drug interactions.

1. INTRODUCTION

Depression is a complex psychiatric disorder characterized by emotional, cognitive, behavioral, and physical symptoms that impair daily functioning, social relationships, and work productivity, ultimately increasing the risk of disability.1 The growing global burden of depression underscores the need for effective and accessible treatment strategies. Current treatment options include pharmacotherapy, psychotherapy, electroconvulsive therapy, and repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation.2

When selecting antidepressants, clinicians generally balance therapeutic efficacy against potential side effects, as no definitive hierarchy exists among antidepressant classes.3 Hyponatremia represents a relatively common and potentially severe adverse effect associated with several antidepressants. Clinical manifestations range from nausea and vomiting to life-threatening complications, including cerebral edema, seizures, altered consciousness, and even death. The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) constitutes the primary mechanism underlying antidepressant-induced hyponatremia,4 whereby certain antidepressants trigger excessive antidiuretic hormone (ADH) release. Hyponatremia typically develops within 30 days of antidepressant initiation,4,5 and generally occurs independently of dosage,4 although isolated case reports have described dose-related variability.6

A wide range of antidepressants has been associated with hyponatremia.7–9 Case studies have documented hyponatremia in patients receiving tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs); selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), including fluoxetine,10 paroxetine,11 escitalopram,6 and vortioxetine,12 and serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), including duloxetine13 and venlafaxine.14 SSRIs exhibit the strongest association with hyponatremia, followed by SNRIs, whereas noradrenergic and specific serotonergic antidepressants, such as mirtazapine, appear to confer a lower risk.8,15 However, evidence comparing the risk of hyponatremia between serotonergic and noradrenergic antidepressants remains inconsistent. Some studies have suggested that mirtazapine carries a higher risk than previously recognized.16 This inconsistency underscores the need to clarify the relative risk of hyponatremia across antidepressants while accounting for potential confounding factors, including concomitant medications, comorbidities, and underreporting of mild hyponatremia.

There is also limited literature on recently introduced antidepressants such as agomelatine, a melatonergic agonist and 5-HT2C antagonist, which operates via a mechanism different from that of traditional serotonergic agents. This unique pharmacological profile suggests a reduced risk of hyponatremia. However, findings on the association between agomelatine and hyponatremia have been inconsistent. For example, a French pharmacovigilance study reported a stronger association between agomelatine and hyponatremia than that reported in most other studies, indicating the need for further evaluation of its safety profile.17

This study aimed to determine the overall incidence of antidepressant-associated hyponatremia and compare the risk of hyponatremia across different antidepressant classes prescribed over the past decade. To simplify the analysis, one of the most commonly prescribed antidepressants in each pharmacological class at our institution was selected: venlafaxine, escitalopram, mirtazapine, and agomelatine. Particular emphasis was placed on newer agents, such as agomelatine, to compare their risk of hyponatremia with that of other commonly prescribed antidepressants. Additional variables that may influence the occurrence of hyponatremia were also evaluated. Findings from this study may provide clinicians with evidence to support antidepressant selection and promote safer treatment decisions.

2. METHODS

2.1. Participants

This retrospective cohort study used data from the clinical database of Kaohsiung Veterans General Hospital (KSVGH), a medical center in southern Taiwan. The Institutional Review Board (Human Research Committee) of KSVGH approved the study (approval number: 24-CT7-14(240625-1)).

Antidepressant-naïve patients who initiated monotherapy with agomelatine, escitalopram, venlafaxine, or mirtazapine for at least 2 weeks between May 2013 and April 2023 were included. Antidepressant-naïve status was defined as no antidepressant prescriptions within 6 months before the index date (the date of the first prescription of the study antidepressant).18 This criterion minimized the prevalence of usage bias. Both inpatient and outpatient populations were eligible for this study. Follow-up began on the index date and ended at the earliest occurrence of hyponatremia or 6 months after treatment initiation, whichever occurred first. As antidepressant-induced hyponatremia usually develops shortly after treatment initiation, with the highest risk during the first 2 weeks followed by a gradual decline,9 a 6-month follow-up period was selected to capture episodes of hyponatremia beyond the early treatment phase.

Patients were excluded if they met either of the following criteria: (1) a diagnosis of hyponatremia (serum sodium [Na] level <135 mEq/L) or SIADH (identified using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision [ICD-10] codes) within 6 months before the index date, or (2) use of diuretics within 1 month before the index date or during antidepressant treatment, identified using Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) codes,19 to minimize potential confounding related to diuresis. Fig. 1 presents the study flowchart.

Flowchart of participant selection process. New users of agomelatine, escitalopram, venlafaxine, or mirtazapine as monotherapy (no antidepressant use in the past 6 mo) with at least 2 wk of continuous use between May 1, 2013, and April 30, 2023 (n = 4054) were assessed for eligibility. Exclusion criteria included a diagnosis of hyponatremia or SIADH within the past 6 mo and use of any diuretics within 1 mo before the index date or during the target antidepressant period (n = 589). After exclusions, a total of 3465 eligible participants remained, divided into four groups: agomelatine (n = 568), escitalopram (n = 1760), venlafaxine (n = 807), and mirtazapine (n = 330). SIADH = syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion.

2.2. Study design and setting

Data extracted from KSVGH electronic medical records included demographic characteristics, medication prescriptions, covariates, and study outcomes. Baseline demographic variables comprised age, sex, and body mass index. Potential covariates included medications associated with hyponatremia, such as antipsychotics, anticancer drugs, opioid analgesics, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). ATC codes were used to identify antidepressants, concomitant medications, and diuretics, whereas ICD-10 codes were used to identify comorbidities. Supplementary Table 1, https://links.lww.com/JCMA/A418, provides the complete list of ATC and ICD-10 codes.

The primary outcome was the incidence of hyponatremia, defined as a serum Na level of <135 mEq/L. The secondary outcome was the incidence rate of severe hyponatremia, defined as a serum Na level of <130 mEq/L. Outcome analyses included only patients with at least one serum sodium measurement obtained during continuous treatment with the same antidepressant. Participants without serum sodium testing during the follow-up period were assumed not to have developed hyponatremia.

2.3. Statistical analysis

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were compared by hyponatremia status using t tests for continuous variables and χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. Univariate logistic regression identified factors associated with hyponatremia; variables with a p value of <0.10 were included in the multivariable model.20 Sensitivity analyses were performed within subgroups defined by diabetes, chronic kidney disease (CKD), coronary artery disease (CAD) or congestive heart failure (CHF), opioid or NSAID use, and malignancy. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs were estimated. All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC) and SPSS version 20 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY), with statistical significance defined as a p value of <0.05.

3. RESULTS

3.1. Patient baseline characteristics and potential confounders

The baseline characteristics of the 3465 enrolled patients revealed significant differences among the four antidepressant groups: agomelatine (n = 568), escitalopram (n = 1760), mirtazapine (n = 330), and venlafaxine (n = 807). Significant differences in demographic and clinical characteristics underscore the need to adjust for these variables when evaluating the risk of hyponatremia (Table 1). Patients who received mirtazapine had a significantly lower mean age (54.7 ± 16.8 years) compared with those who received agomelatine (64.1 ± 19.2 years), escitalopram (63.8 ± 20.6 years), and venlafaxine (65.9 ± 17.3 years) (p < 0.001). Sex distribution differed significantly across treatment groups, with the mirtazapine group having the lowest proportion of male patients (111/330, 33.6%; p < 0.001).

Comorbidity profiles varied significantly across treatment groups. Hypertension was most prevalent among patients receiving agomelatine (n = 220, 38.7%) and least prevalent among those receiving mirtazapine (n = 74, 22.4%; p < 0.001). Similarly, CKD was most common in the agomelatine group (n = 58, 10.2%) and least common in the mirtazapine group (n = 10, 3.0%; p < 0.001). Concomitant medication use also varied across treatment groups. Antiepileptic use was higher in the mirtazapine group (n = 101, 30.6%) than in the agomelatine (n = 117, 20.6%) and escitalopram (n = 334, 19.0%) groups (p < 0.001). NSAID use was highest among patients receiving venlafaxine (n = 136, 16.9%; p < 0.001).

3.2. Factors associated with hyponatremia risk in antidepressant-treated patients

During the follow-up period, serum sodium levels were measured in 1031 of the 3465 patients (29.75%). Among those with available measurements, the mean interval from antidepressant initiation to the first sodium assessment was 1.5 months, with a median of 0.8 months. Participants without serum sodium testing during follow-up were assumed not to have developed hyponatremia, consistent with the study design.

In terms of sodium-related outcomes, the incidence rates of hyponatremia (serum sodium <135 mEq/L) were 10.2% for venlafaxine group, 8.5% for escitalopram, 6.9% for agomelatine, and 5.8% for mirtazapine. Although venlafaxine was associated with an increased hyponatremia risk compared with mirtazapine in the univariate analysis (OR: 1.80, 95% CI: 1.09-2.97, p = 0.021), this association was no longer significant after adjusting for potential confounders (OR: 0.87, 95% CI: 0.52-1.45, p = 0.601). Furthermore, none of the other antidepressants demonstrated a significant association with hyponatremia compared with mirtazapine after adjustment (Table 2).

Logistic regression analysis identified factors associated with hyponatremia (serum Na <135 mEq/L) among patients receiving antidepressants. Several demographic, clinical, and medication-related variables were evaluated (Table 2). Age was independently associated with an increased risk of hyponatremia (OR: 1.02, 95% CI: 1.02-1.03, p < 0.001), indicating that the risk increased with advancing age. Smoking was identified as a significant risk factor (OR: 1.63, 95% CI: 1.27-2.13, p < 0.001), suggesting that lifestyle behaviors contribute to an elevated risk of hyponatremia. Among comorbidities, diabetes mellitus (OR: 1.64, 95% CI: 1.26-2.13, p < 0.001) and malignancy (OR: 2.52, 95% CI: 1.91-3.31, p < 0.001) were independently associated with an increased risk of hyponatremia. Concomitant use of anticancer drugs (OR: 2.72, 95% CI: 1.55-4.76, p < 0.001) and opioid analgesics (OR: 2.50, 95% CI: 1.91-3.27, p < 0.001) was also independently associated with hyponatremia.

Logistic regression analysis identified factors associated with severe hyponatremia (serum Na <130 mEq/L) among patients receiving antidepressants (Supplementary Table 2, https://links.lww.com/JCMA/A418). Age remained an independent risk factor in both the univariate and multivariable analyses, with an adjusted OR of 1.02 (95% CI: 1.01-1.03, p = 0.001), indicating that the risk of severe hyponatremia increased with advancing age. Smoking was also independently associated with severe hyponatremia (adjusted OR: 1.91, 95% CI: 1.28-2.84, p = 0.001). Among the antidepressants, venlafaxine showed a significant association with severe hyponatremia in the univariate analysis (OR: 6.13, 95% CI: 1.90-19.75, p = 0.002). Among comorbidities, diabetes mellitus (adjusted OR: 2.05, 95% CI: 1.37-3.05, p < 0.001) and malignancy (adjusted OR: 3.10, 95% CI: 2.03-4.72, p < 0.001) were independently associated with an increased risk of severe hyponatremia. Concomitant use of amiodarone (adjusted OR: 3.99, 95% CI: 1.72-9.24, p = 0.001) and proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) (adjusted OR: 2.63, 95% CI: 1.73-3.98, p < 0.001) was also independently associated with severe hyponatremia.

3.3. Subgroup analysis of hyponatremia risk across antidepressant types and patient conditions

Subgroup analyses assessed the risk of hyponatremia (serum Na <135 mEq/L) associated with agomelatine, escitalopram, and venlafaxine using mirtazapine as the reference (Table 3). No significant differences in hyponatremia risk were observed among the antidepressants across most patient subgroups, including those with diabetes mellitus, CKD, CAD, CHF, and malignancy, as well as those who received opioid analgesics or NSAIDs. For example, among patients with diabetes, venlafaxine was associated with a lower, but nonsignificant, risk of hyponatremia compared with mirtazapine (OR: 0.47, 95% CI: 0.20-1.07, p = 0.070). Similarly, among patients without CKD, agomelatine (OR: 0.71, 95% CI: 0.39-1.30, p = 0.269) and escitalopram (OR: 1.08, 95% CI: 0.65-1.78, p = 0.776) showed similar risks compared with that of mirtazapine. Hyponatremia risk was comparable across the antidepressants in this subgroup. Similar findings were observed in the malignancy, opioid analgesic, and NSAID subgroups, with no antidepressant demonstrating a significantly increased risk of hyponatremia compared with mirtazapine.

4. DISCUSSION

This study provides a comparative evaluation of hyponatremia risk among four commonly prescribed antidepressants. The incidence of hyponatremia in our cohort (5.8%-10.2%) was generally consistent with that reported in previous studies. Two recent meta-analyses demonstrated overall hyponatremia rates of approximately 4.6%-6.0% among antidepressant users,8,21 with higher rates observed for SNRIs (6%-7%) than for SSRIs (5%-6%), and lower rates for mirtazapine and trazodone (approximately 1%). In the univariate analysis, agomelatine, escitalopram, and venlafaxine were associated with higher odds of hyponatremia compared with mirtazapine, although statistical significance was observed only for venlafaxine. This finding aligns with previous meta-analyses reporting a greater risk of hyponatremia with SNRIs, particularly venlafaxine, than with SSRIs, TCAs, and mirtazapine.8 The elevated risk associated with venlafaxine may be attributable to its potent serotonergic activity, which has been implicated in the development of SIADH and subsequent hyponatremia, particularly in susceptible individuals.15

However, after adjustment for demographic and clinical covariates, no significant differences in hyponatremia risk were observed among agomelatine, escitalopram, venlafaxine, and mirtazapine. Instead, older age; diabetes mellitus; malignancy; and the use of anticancer agents, opioid analgesics, and PPIs remained independently associated with an increased risk of hyponatremia. These findings suggest that patient-related clinical factors exert a greater influence on hyponatremia risk than does the choice of antidepressant.

To further investigate these associations, subgroup analyses were performed according to diabetes mellitus, CKD, CAD or CHF, opioid analgesic and NSAID use, and malignancy. These subgroups were selected based on their relatively high ORs in the univariate analysis and their clinical relevance as common comorbidities. Across all subgroups, agomelatine, escitalopram, and venlafaxine showed no significant differences in hyponatremia risk compared with mirtazapine after adjustment. Sensitivity analyses yielded consistent results, supporting the robustness of the subgroup findings. Collectively, these findings highlight two important considerations. First, differences in hyponatremia risk among antidepressants may be less pronounced after accounting for important confounding factors. This finding underscores the multifactorial nature of hyponatremia, extending beyond the choice of antidepressant. Second, establishing a definitive risk hierarchy among antidepressants remains challenging owing to the diversity of available agents and the limited feasibility of direct comparisons across all medications.

Previous studies have described several mechanisms by which these clinical factors may increase the risk of hyponatremia. Malignancy may contribute through paraneoplastic syndromes and ADH secretion, particularly in lung and brain cancers.22 Direct renal involvement by malignancy may further impair fluid and electrolyte homeostasis. Moreover, cancer-related anorexia can reduce sodium intake and exacerbate hyponatremia. Diabetic nephropathy and CKD impair sodium and water homeostasis through fluid retention and reduced renal sodium excretion.23 Older age is a well-established independent risk factor,24 likely reflecting age-related changes in fluid regulation and the higher prevalence of comorbidities that predispose individuals to hyponatremia. Cardiovascular conditions, including hypertension and CHF, further disrupt fluid balance,25 whereas concomitant use of diuretics, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs), and angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) may further increase the risk of hyponatremia. Neurological disorders, including brain tumors, stroke, and intracranial hemorrhage, can induce SIADH, resulting in water retention and dilutional hyponatremia.26

Medications contribute substantially to the risk of hyponatremia.27 Antipsychotics may increase ADH secretion or impair renal function, leading to sodium imbalance. Chemotherapeutic agents and immunotherapies can disrupt electrolyte homeostasis through direct renal toxicity or other treatment-related effects, whereas opioids may promote ADH secretion and impair renal function. ACEIs and ARBs alter fluid and electrolyte balance, particularly in patients with renal impairment. NSAIDs impair sodium excretion and water homeostasis, thereby increasing the risk of hyponatremia. Amiodarone may contribute indirectly to hyponatremia by altering thyroid function, whereas PPIs have also been associated with hyponatremia, although the underlying mechanism remains unclear.

This study also provides insights into real-world antidepressant prescribing patterns. Escitalopram was the most frequently prescribed antidepressant, consistent with its role as a first-line treatment for depression owing to its well-established efficacy and favorable tolerability profile.28 By contrast, mirtazapine was prescribed more frequently to younger patients, possibly reflecting concerns about its association with an increased risk of adverse outcomes, including stroke and seizures, in older adults.29 Female patients predominated across all treatment groups, consistent with the higher prevalence of depression among women.30 Patients receiving mirtazapine also had a lower prevalence of metabolic comorbidities, including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and dyslipidemia. This prescribing pattern may reflect concerns regarding mirtazapine-associated weight gain and increased appetite,31,32 making it a less favorable option for patients with pre-existing metabolic disorders. Conversely, venlafaxine users had a higher prevalence of malignancy and were more likely to receive concomitant medications, including anticancer agents, opioid analgesics, and NSAIDs. This pattern is consistent with the established role of SNRIs in the management of neuropathic pain, particularly among patients with cancer.33–35 Overall, these findings demonstrate substantial differences in patient characteristics across antidepressant groups, reflecting real-world prescribing practices.

This study has some limitations. First, the retrospective observational design is inherently susceptible to residual confounding, which may have influenced the observed associations with hyponatremia. Residual confounding may have persisted owing to the lack of information on certain variables, potentially affecting the validity of the findings. Second, serum Na concentrations were not measured in all patients treated with antidepressants during the study period. Only 29.75% (1031/3465) of the cohort underwent serum sodium testing during follow-up. Patients without documented serum sodium measurements were assumed not to have developed hyponatremia, potentially underestimating its incidence because asymptomatic hyponatremia may have remained undetected, thereby introducing detection bias. However, in our clinical setting, serum sodium monitoring is routinely conducted in older adults and patients with multiple comorbidities. This practice may have reduced the likelihood of missing clinically significant hyponatremia in high-risk groups. Third, time-to-event data and antidepressant dosage were not available, precluding analyses of the temporal onset of hyponatremia and potential dose–response relationships. Fourth, this study was conducted at a single center (KSVGH) with a predominantly Asian study population, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other healthcare settings and ethnic populations. Furthermore, prior antidepressant use outside our institution may not have been captured. Future studies should address these limitations by including larger multicenter cohorts, collecting more comprehensive clinical data, implementing routine serum Na monitoring, and incorporating time-to-event and dosage–response analyses to provide a more accurate estimate of the incidence and determinants of antidepressant-associated hyponatremia.

In conclusion, although mirtazapine and venlafaxine showed the lowest and highest relative risks of hyponatremia, respectively, in the univariate analysis, these differences were no longer significant after adjusting for comorbidities and concomitant medications. Hyponatremia risk appears to be influenced by multiple patient-related and treatment-related factors, underscoring the importance of individualized antidepressant selection based on each patient’s clinical profile. Further large-scale, multicenter studies are warranted to compare the safety profiles of these antidepressants.
