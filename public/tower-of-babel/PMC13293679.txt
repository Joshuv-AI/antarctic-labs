Polyethylene Liner Fracture in Touch Trapeziometacarpal Prosthesis: A Case Report
Authors: Daniel Schallmayer, Ahmed T. Sonbul, Gudrun H. Borchert, Christian Krasny
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Total joint arthroplasty is a common surgical treatment option for trapeziometacarpal joint arthritis. The case report reflects the seldom complication of a fracture of the polyethylene liner in a double mobility trapeziometacarpal joint prosthesis (Touch). Unique about this case is that the affected patient had no symptoms despite a slight clicking during thumb abduction. X-rays and the clinical evaluation showed no abnormalities. A computed tomography scan and dynamic x-rays gave a hint about abnormal decentralization of the head. In a revision surgery, the fractured polyethylene liner was found as cause of the decentralization of the head and explained the slight clicking during thumb abduction. Changing the head of the implant restored the normal movement of the prosthesis without clicking. We suggest regular checks to detect implant failure as early as possible. This case shows that serious implant problems can be clinically unobtrusive, and early intervention can save the implant.

Thumb carpometacarpal arthritis is a frequent degenerative disorder in middle-aged and older adults. Although trapeziectomy remains a dependable treatment, prosthetic replacement offers the advantages of preserving motion, maintaining pinch strength, and enabling quicker functional recovery.1 The Touch dual-mobility prosthesis has shown initial promising results,2,3 with up to 95% satisfied patients.3 Complications described were cup loosening and wear of polyethylene (PE).3 Nevertheless, PE liner fracture is an extremely uncommon complication4 and difficult to diagnose.5 We describe a case that initially presented free of clinical symptoms, merely with mechanical clicking, highlighting the diagnostic usefulness of CT Scan and stress-view radiographs if normal x-rays and clinical investigations show no abnormalities.

Case Report

A 53-year-old male, a professional cook, underwent trapeziometacarpal (TMC) arthroplasty with a Touch prosthesis in early 2024 for primary osteoarthritis. This was not due to trauma. Twelve months later, he developed persistent clicking during thumb motion without antecedent trauma or pain. Exchanging only the liner restored stability, and follow-up 6 weeks postsurgery radiographs confirmed the implant position remained satisfactory (Fig. 5). Two weeks after surgery, stitches were removed, cast fixation was discontinued, and occupational therapy was started. Reintegration into his job as a cook started 6 weeks after surgery. A complete restoration of the function of the implant after revision surgery was obtained. The patient’s mechanical symptoms were resolved. Discussion

Polyethylene liner breakage in TMC arthroplasty has scarcely been reported.4,5 Usually, there are no complications after this time period, especially no implant-related complications. In general, complications due to PE problems can be estimated after 15 years. Routine imaging often fails to detect such problems because metallic parts obscure the liner. Dynamic modalities such as stress radiographs5 may expose subtle instability. Our findings parallel those of Duerinckx et al,4,5 where patients experienced mechanical clicking despite normal standard x-rays. In contrast, a large series of Touch prostheses3 reported no liner fractures, underlining the rarity of this complication. Potential factors include repetitive biomechanical loading, fatigue of the liner material, and implant design characteristics that focus stresses at particular points.

When a patient complains of unexplained mechanical symptoms following TMC arthroplasty, particularly audible clicking, surgeons should keep in mind the possibility of a liner fracture. Stress-view imaging can be instrumental for diagnosis. Detecting the problem early allows for a limited revision such as liner replacement, which may restore joint stability and function without necessitating revision of the entire prosthesis. At the 3-month follow-up visit, the patient showed no clinical pain, normal ROM, and had fully resumed his professional activity as a cook.
