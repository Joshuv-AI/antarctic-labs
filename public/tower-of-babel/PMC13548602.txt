Joint association of high-sensitivity C-reactive protein and cumulative atherogenic index of plasma with cardiovascular disease risk in CKM syndrome stages 0–3: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Yiyang Zhu, Changlin Zhai, Jingjing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) and cumulative atherogenic index of plasma (CumAIP) are recognized risk markers for cardiovascular disease (CVD), their association with CVD incidence—and specifically their combined effect—in individuals with Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome is far less understood.MethodsThis study analyzed 3,028 participants at CKM stages 0–3 from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study. Kaplan‑Meier survival curves and Cox proportional hazards models were used to investigate the associations of CumAIP and hs-CRP levels with the risk of CVD in this population, as well as to evaluate their combined effect on CVD incidence.ResultsDuring the 5-year follow-up period (2015–2020), 495 participants developed CVD. Both hs-CRP and CumAIP were associated with CVD risk. After progressively adjusting for all covariates, compared with the low-hs-CRP group, the high-hs-CRP group exhibited a 36.6% increased risk of CVD (HR = 1.366, 95%CI: 1.133 ~ 1.648); compared with the low-CumAIP group, the high-CumAIP group showed a 40.8% increased risk (HR = 1.408, 95%CI: 1.158 ~ 1.711). The combination of high hs-CRP and high CumAIP was associated with a higher risk of developing CVD. Compared to individuals with both low hs-CRP and low CumAIP, those with high levels of both biomarkers had a 1.834-fold higher risk (95%CI: 1.408–2.389). Subgroup analysis indicated that the combined association of hs-CRP and CumAIP was more pronounced in males.ConclusionThis study suggests that elevated hs-CRP and CumAIP are jointly associated with CVD incidence, and their combination exhibits a joint effect among middle-aged and older adults with stage 0–3 CKM syndrome.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06147-3.Keywords: Cardiovascular disease, High-sensitivity C-reactive protein, Cumulative atherogenic index of plasma, Cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease(CVD) constitutes a group of conditions the heart and blood vessels [1]. It represents the leading cause of death among individuals aged 45 and above worldwide, and contributes to issues such as disability, diminishing patients’ quality of life and increasing healthcare costs [2]. Studies have shown that cardiovascular diseases impose a heavier burden on people with metabolic abnormalities, kidney diseases, and high cardiovascular risk factors, with higher incidence and mortality risk [3, 4]. Recently defined Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome summarizes this overlap. This definition comes from the Presidential Advisory Report issued by the American Heart Association (AHA) in October 2023, defining CKM as a systemic disease involving the pathophysiological interactions among metabolic risk factors, chronic kidney disease, and the cardiovascular system [5]. It identifies individuals who simultaneously have obesity, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and diagnosed cardiovascular risk. Patients within the CKM spectrum have a significantly increased risk of atherosclerotic events [6], heart failure [7], and cardiovascular death [6], highlighting the urgent need for more precise risk stratification tools tailored to this high-risk population.

CKM syndrome typically originates from excessive accumulation, dysfunction, or both conditions of adipose tissue. Dysfunctional adipose tissue, particularly visceral adipose tissue, secretes abundant proinflammatory and prooxidative products, which cause damage to arterial, cardiac, and renal tissues [5]. High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) is a recognised biomarker of systemic inflammation and is thought to contribute to atherosclerosis by promoting endothelial dysfunction, plaque instability and thrombosis [8, 9]. Elevated hs-CRP levels consistently predict future CVD events in different populations [10, 11]. In addition to inflammatory markers, directly assessing vascular health is equally important. The atherogenic index of plasma (AIP) was first proposed by Dobiásová and Frohlich as a biomarker of plasma atherosclerosis [12]. Its calculation formula is log(TG/HDL), which can reflect the concentrations of TG and HDL-C in the circulation. Research shows that AIP may be a potential biomarker for atherosclerosis and CVD risk, with high AIP positively correlated with CVD risk [13–15]. Previous studies on AIP and CVD mostly used a single indicator, and few studies have confirmed the impact of long-term exposure to AIP on CVD. Therefore, this study uses cumulative AIP (CumAIP) to explore its association with CVD.

Traditional lipid measurements typically reflect static lipid levels at a single time point, whereas the cumulative atherogenic index of plasma (CumAIP) is calculated based on two measurements (2012 and 2015) [16]. This cumulative index may help assess long‑term atherogenic burden and reduce the impact of within‑individual variability. Thus, CumAIP might offer a dynamic approach to evaluating atherogenic risk, complementing conventional lipid panels to some extent.

Existing cardiovascular risk scores (e.g., the Framingham Risk Score) do not explicitly incorporate inflammatory markers or cumulative metabolic burden [17]. hs-CRP and CumAIP may be partially independent risk predictors. When the two markers are discordant (elevated hs-CRP with normal CumAIP, or vice versa), the associated CVD risk might be intermediate, whereas individuals with both markers elevated may have a higher risk. This combined testing strategy might help improve risk stratification, particularly in individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–3, where early detection is important for health management.

Although hs-CRP and CumAIP each possess significant prognostic assessment efficacy, the application value of their combined testing in the CKM population remains a notable void in current understanding. Inflammation and atherosclerosis are pathophysiologically intertwined: inflammatory cytokines may promote vascular calcification, thereby increasing stiffness [18]. Conversely, during atherosclerotic progression, immune cells within plaques continuously release inflammatory mediators [19]. This joint relationship suggests that combined assessment of these two markers may offer superior risk discrimination compared to evaluating either indicator in isolation. However, existing research predominantly evaluates these biomarkers in isolation or studies them solely within general populations, without specifically examining them within the multifaceted context of the CKM. Within the CKM, metabolic and renal dysfunction may profoundly alter the joint effect between these factors and their predictive efficacy. Therefore, this study aims to investigate whether the combination of hs-CRP and CumAIP is associated with increased cardiovascular disease risk within this susceptible population.

Method

Study population

This study utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) [20], a nationally representative, large-scale cohort study. Participants were recruited from urban and rural areas across 150 counties within 28 provinces in China, focusing on Chinese adults aged 45 and above. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The CHARLS study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Peking University (IRB00001052-11015) and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Stringent quality control measures were implemented by the CHARLES study team in the processes of data recording and verification to ensure data reliability [20].

The nationally representative baseline survey of the CHARLS was conducted from June 2011 to March 2012. Participants were followed up with face-to-face interviews every two years. All interviews were administered by trained professionals using computer-assisted personal interviewing to ensure standardized data collection procedure. For the present study, individuals interviewed in the 2011–2012 wave were defined as the baseline cohort, with follow-up data obtained in 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020. The participant selection process, applying strict inclusion and exclusion criteria, is illustrated in Fig. 1. The baseline survey in 2011 initially included 17,705 respondents. Participants were excluded based on the following criteria: (1) not being in CKM stages 0–3 at baseline; (2) a history of CVD, including heart disease or stroke before 2015; (3) incomplete baseline data on demographic characteristics; (4) age below 45 years at baseline; and (5) missing data on baseline hs-CRP or the CumAIP. After applying these criteria, 3,028 participants were ultimately included in the analyses, respectively.

Data collection

At baseline, the following data were primarily collected in this study: (1) Demographic characteristics: age, sex, marital status, and education level; (2) Lifestyle factors: smoking status and alcohol consumption status; (3) Anthropometric measurements: height, weight, BMI, waist circumference (WC), systolic blood pressure (SBP), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP); (4) Medical history: history of hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and medication use; (5) Laboratory test indicators: total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), fasting blood glucose (FBG), serum creatinine (Scr), triglycerides (TG), and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP).

In this study, hypertension and diabetes were redefined. Participants were defined as hypertensive if they reported a history of hypertension, had a baseline SBP ≥ 140 mmHg or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg, or were undergoing antihypertensive treatment [21]. Participants were defined as diabetic if they reported a history of diabetes, had a baseline FBG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dL) or HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, or were receiving antidiabetic therapy [4].

Definition of CKM syndrome

According to previous researches [22], CKM was categorized into five stages (0–4) in this study: Stage 0, no risk factors; Stage 1, overweight/obesity with functional impairment indicating insulin sensitivity decline; Stage 2, metabolic abnormalities (e.g., hypertension, hypertriglyceridemia, diabetes, metabolic syndrome) or moderate- to high-risk chronic kidney disease; Stage 3, subclinical cardiovascular dysfunction, especially in the presence of high- or very-high-risk chronic kidney disease; Stage 4, clinically established cardiovascular disease. Detailed diagnostic criteria are provided in Table S1. This study was conducted exclusively in individuals with CKM stages 0–3.

Definition of CVD diagnosis

In CHARLS, participants are interviewed face-to-face every two years (2011, 2013, 2015, 2018, 2020) using a standardized questionnaire administered by trained staff. The outcomes of this study were the incidence of CVD during the follow-up period (2015–2020). With reference to the assessment methods used in previous studies [23, 24], CVD included two major categories: cerebrovascular diseases (e.g., stroke) and heart diseases (e.g., coronary heart disease, heart failure). CVD occurrence was defined based on self-reported prior diagnosis collected by the investigators using standardized questionnaires. The questions included: “Have you ever been diagnosed with stroke by a doctor?” and “Have you ever been diagnosed by a doctor with [myocardial infarction, coronary heart disease, angina pectoris, congestive heart failure, or other heart diseases]?” Participants who reported a diagnosis of stroke or heart disease were defined as having CVD.

Calculation of CumAIP

In this study, CumAIP was defined as follows: CumAIP = (AIP2012 + 2015)/2 * time (2012–2015), where the AIP = log10[TG (mg/dL)/HDL-C (mg/dL)] [16].

Statistical analysis

Data cleaning and analysis were performed using R software. Participants were divided into two groups based on the median levels of hs-CRP and CumAIP. Normally distributed continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (x̄±s), while non-normally distributed data are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR). Between-group comparisons for continuous data were conducted using the t test or the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical data are expressed as frequency and percentage (%), with between-group comparisons performed using the χ² test. The log-rank test and Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival analysis was performed to compare the cumulative incidence of CVD. To assess the association between hs-CRP, CumAIP, and the incidence of CVD, as well as their combined effect, among patients with CKM syndrome stages 0–3, five multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were established. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 adjusted for age, gender, marital status, and education level. Model 3 further incorporated adjustments for BMI, WC, SBP, DBP, smoking, and alcohol consumption. Model 4 additionally adjusted for HbA1c, FBG and Scr. Model 5 was further adjusted for the presence of hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia. CVD events were assessed at baseline (2011–2012) and then during the 5‑year follow‑up period (2015–2020) at biennial waves (2015, 2018, 2020). Participants who reported CVD before the 2015 wave were excluded to ensure that the exposure window (2012–2015) preceded outcome ascertainment. Time at risk was calculated from the completion of the second lipid measurement (2015) to the date of the first reported CVD event, death, or the end of follow‑up (2020), whichever occurred first. Event time was assigned as the date of the wave when the event was first reported. Participants without CVD were censored at their last interview date. Survival times were calculated from baseline to event or censoring date. Missing data for covariates are summarized in Table S2. To assess collinearity, we performed variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis (Table S3); variables with VIF > 5 (TG, HDL-C, LDL-C, TG) were excluded from the fully adjusted model to avoid overadjustment. To address potential bias from missing data, we performed multiple imputation as a sensitivity analysis using the mice package in R (20 imputed datasets, predictive mean matching for continuous variables, logistic regression for binary variables). The imputation model included all covariates from Model 5 but did not include the outcome or exposure variables. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used to explore potential nonlinear relationships between hs-CRP or CumAIP and CVD. To examine the combined association of between hs-CRP and CumAIP, participants were divided into four groups for Cox regression and subgroup analyses (Fig. 2). The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residual tests (see Results). To assess the incremental predictive utility of hs-CRP and CumAIP beyond traditional risk factors, we compared the discrimination and reclassification performance between the fully adjusted base model (Model 5) and an extended model that added hs-CRP and CumAIP. Model discrimination was evaluated using Harrell’s C-index. Improvements in risk reclassification were quantified by the continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) statistics. All statistical analyses were two-sided, with the significance level set at α = 0.05.

This study included 3,028 participants with a mean age of 58.42 ± 8.78 years, among whom 1,607 were female, accounting for 53.07% of the cohort. The median values for hs-CRP and CumAIP were 0.96 and 1.05, respectively. Association between CumAIP or hs-CRP with incident CVD

This study involved a five-year follow-up period (2015–2020) during which 495 individuals developed new-onset CVD, representing an incidence rate of 16.35%. KM curves show (Figure S1) significant differences in CVD incidence risk across different subgroups for both hs-CRP and CumAIP (P < 0.05). To assess the associations of hs-CRP or CumAIP with CVD incidence among participants at stages 0–3 of CKM syndrome, this study constructed five Cox proportional hazards models. As shown in Table 2, after stepwise adjustment for all covariates, both hs-CRP and CumAIP demonstrated a significant positive association with CVD incidence. In Model 1 without adjustment for covariates, individuals with high hs-CRP showed a 53.7% increased risk of CVD incidence compared to those with low hs-CRP (HR = 1.537, 95%CI: 1.283 ~ 1.840); compared to the low CumAIP group, the high CumAIP group had a 58.4% (HR = 1.584, 95%CI: 1.322 ~ 1.898) increased risk of CVD. The results of Model 2 indicated that the high hs-CRP group and the high CumAIP group were associated with 47.1% (HR = 1.471, 95%CI: 1.227 ~ 1.764) and 60.6% (HR = 1.606, 95%CI: 1.340 ~ 1.925) increased risks of CVD, respectively. This association remained significant in Model 3 and Model 4. After full adjustment for all covariates in Model 5, the high hs-CRP group and the high CumAIP group exhibited 36.6% (HR = 1.366, 95%CI: 1.133 ~ 1.648) and 40.8% (HR = 1.408, 95%CI: 1.158 ~ 1.711) increased risks of CVD, respectively.

Joint analysis of hs-CRP and CumAIP in relation to cardiovascular disease

Based on hs-CRP and CumAIP levels, the population was stratified into four groups to examine their joint effect on CVD risk in the CKM population. KM curve analysis indicated that individuals with hs-CRP > 0.96 and CumAIP > 1.05 had the highest risk of CVD incidence (P < 0.001, Fig. 4). Furthermore, five multivariate Cox proportional hazards models were constructed for these groups, all of which yielded consistent results. After full adjustment for covariates, compared with the reference group (CumAIP ≤ 1.05 and hs-CRP ≤ 0.96), the CVD risk was increased to 1.515-fold (95%CI: 1.140 ~ 2.012) for the CumAIP ≤ 1.05 and hs-CRP > 0.96 group, to 1.561-fold (95%CI: 1.170 ~ 2.083) for the CumAIP > 1.05 and hs-CRP ≤ 0.96 group, and to 1.834-fold (95%CI: 1.408 ~ 2.389) for the CumAIP > 1.05 and hs-CRP > 0.96 group (Table 3). The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residual tests (Table S5). The global test showed no significant violation (χ² = 30.14, P = 0.051), and the primary exposure variable also satisfied the assumption (P = 0.073). Post‑hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction (Table S6) showed that the CumAIP > 1.05 and hs-CRP > 0.96 group had the highest CVD risk, while the CumAIP ≤ 1.05 and hs-CRP > 0.96 group and CumAIP > 1.05 and hs-CRP > 0.96 group did not differ significantly from each other.

Subgroup analysis

To further evaluate the impact of hs-CRP and CumAIP on CVD incidence across different demographic subgroups, this study conducted subgroup analyses. The association between higher hs-CRP and higher CumAIP with an increased risk of CVD remained generally consistent across subgroups stratified by age, sex, alcohol consumption, smoking status, and CKM syndrome categories (Table 5). Notably, the combined effect of hs-CRP and CumAIP on CVD incidence was more pronounced in males (P for heterogeneity < 0.05).

Incremental predictive value

The base model demonstrated a C-index of 0.644 (95% CI 0.640 ~ 0.648). When hs-CRP and CumAIP were added, the C-index increased to 0.657 (0.653 ~ 0.661), representing a significant improvement (ΔC-index = 0.013, 95% CI 0.003 ~ 0.028, P = 0.028). In addition, the continuous NRI was 0.186 (95% CI 0.087 ~ 0.285, P < 0.001) and the IDI was 0.0071 (0.0034 ~ 0.0108, P < 0.001).

Sensitivity analyses

To further evaluate the reliability of the findings, multiple sensitivity analyses were conducted in this study. First, to address the issue of missing data, multiple imputation was applied, and the results derived from the imputed data remained consistent with the primary analysis (Table S7). Second, logistic regression models were used to examine the relationships between hs-CRP, CumAIP and CVD risk, and the results were still in line with the main analysis (Table S8). Third, considering that participants in CKM stage 0 may have minimal metabolic or cardiovascular risk factors or may not reach clinical thresholds [4], an additional analysis excluding these participants also supported the main conclusions (Table S9). Fourth, to reduce the influence of extremely high hs-CRP values that might reflect acute illness rather than chronic low‑grade inflammation, we truncated hs-CRP at 10 mg/L (i.e., values > 10 mg/L were set to 10 mg/L) and repeated the main analyses (Model 5). The results remained materially unchanged: the HR for continuous hs-CRP was 1.020 (95% CI: 1.000 ~ 1.040) after truncation, compared with 1.025 in the primary analysis without truncation (Table S10).

Discussion

In this nationally representative cohort of middle-aged and older Chinese adults with CKM stages 0–3, both elevated hs-CRP and elevated CumAIP are associated with CVD incidence. Notably, a joint effect was observed between them. Individuals concurrently exposed to high hs-CRP and high CumAIP exhibited a higher risk of developing CVD.

Hs-CRP is a well‑established marker of low‑grade inflammation, and AIP (log(TG/HDL‑C)) reflects atherogenic dyslipidemia. Their cumulative version, CumAIP, captures long‑term metabolic burden. The associations observed in our study are consistent with prior evidence linking each marker individually to CVD risk [10, 11, 25, 26].

Our findings align with the Women’s Health Study, which showed that hs-CRP independently predicted CVD over 30 years [10], and with several CHARLS-based studies reporting associations of AIP or cumulative AIP with CVD [16, 27]. However, most previous studies evaluated either hs-CRP or AIP alone, or used single-timepoint lipid measures. A recent study by Ou et al. examined the hs-CRP-triglyceride-glucose index but did not focus on cumulative atherogenic burden [28].

The joint assessment of hs-CRP and CumAIP may serve as a supplementary approach for further risk refinement, and could be explored in intermediate‑risk patients where clinical decisions are uncertain after conventional risk assessment. For example, adding the combined hs‑CRP‑CumAIP index to the Framingham Risk Score improved the AUC from 0.688 to 0.713. This modest improvement suggests potential added discriminative information, though further validation is needed. Patients with both elevated hs-CRP and high CumAIP exhibited the highest observed risk. The two discordant groups had intermediate and similar risk levels. Whether adding hs-CRP and CumAIP to existing risk scores improves risk reclassification, and whether management decisions guided by these biomarkers lead to better outcomes, remain questions for future research.

Numerous studies have demonstrated the predictive value of hs-CRP or AIP for CVD in either the general population or high-risk groups [29–31]. A key feature of this study is its focus on individuals with stage 0–3 CKM syndrome, who are in a critical window of risk accumulation and have not yet experienced clear clinical cardiovascular events. While some studies have explored the interaction between inflammation and individual lipid metrics [28], research combining CumAIP with hs-CRP and clarifying their joint effects remains limited. The subgroup and sensitivity analyses further support the credibility of the observed associations across demographic characteristics.

The principal strengths of this study include: 1) the use of a large nationwide cohort, ensuring good sample representativeness and stronger generalizability; 2) a prospective design that clarifies temporal relationships; 3) the innovative integrated assessment of inflammation and cumulative metabolic risk in an early-stage CKM population; and 4) robust conclusions supported by joint-effect analysis along with extensive sensitivity and subgroup analyses.

At the same time, several limitations must be acknowledged. 1) as an observational study, residual confounding or reverse causality cannot be fully excluded despite adjustment for multiple confounders, and the moderate hazard ratios suggest that the clinical relevance of these markers requires further evaluation. Despite adjusting for a wide range of covariates, residual confounding may still exist. Unmeasured factors such as household income (which had a missing rate exceeding 50% in our analytic sample and was therefore not included), physical activity, diet quality, sleep patterns, and environmental exposures (e.g., air pollution) were not included in our models and could influence both hs-CRP levels and CVD risk [32–34]. Subgroup analyses, particularly those stratified by CKM stage, had limited statistical power because of the small numbers of participants and events in stages 0 and 1; 2) laboratory measures in the CHARLS data were based on single assessments. Although cumulative measures were employed to partially overcome variability, hs-CRP itself is subject to within-individual fluctuation, which may introduce misclassification bias. CumAIP was calculated using only two measurements (2012 and 2015). Although this captures cumulative exposure better than a single time point, it does not fully reflect the dynamic changes over the entire follow‑up period. The temporal relationship between CumAIP and CVD risk may still be subject to reverse causality. 3) CVD outcomes were ascertained based on self‑reported physician diagnoses, which may introduce recall bias and outcome misclassification. The composite outcome includes heterogeneous conditions (e.g., myocardial infarction, heart failure, angina) with potentially different pathophysiological mechanisms. This heterogeneity should be considered when interpreting the results, as the observed associations may reflect differential contributions across outcome subtypes that the present data cannot disentangle, given that the CHARLS public dataset does not provide separate variables for each cardiac subtype. Self-report may lead to under-reporting, particularly for milder or asymptomatic events, thereby affecting the accuracy of outcome ascertainment. Furthermore, no formal validation study of self‑reported CVD events against medical records has been published for the CHARLS dataset. 4) The median cut-offs used for dichotomization (hs-CRP = 0.96; CumAIP = 1.05) were derived from the data and should not be regarded as clinically validated thresholds. No established clinical cut-offs for CumAIP are currently available in the literature, and these values are cohort-specific, such that their direct translation to clinical practice should be approached with caution. Nonetheless, sensitivity analyses using tertile-based categories (low/medium/high) yielded similar results (Table S11), offering some reassurance that the observed associations are not driven by the choice of median cut-offs. 5) The analytic sample (N = 3,028) was smaller than the original CHARLS cohort (N = 17,705). As shown in Table S12, included participants had higher prevalences of hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia than excluded participants, indicating a higher‑risk subgroup. Thus, our findings may overestimate associations in the general population, and generalization to individuals with missing data, pre‑existing CVD, or younger age should be made with caution.

From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that the combined assessment of hs-CRP and CumAIP may help identify individuals at moderately higher cardiovascular risk within the CKM 0–3 spectrum. For clinicians managing these patients, concurrent elevation of hs-CRP and CumAIP may signal a higher relative risk of future cardiovascular events. Although these thresholds have not yet been clinically validated and should not be used alone for treatment decisions, they can serve as an adjunctive alert. This combined testing approach is low‑cost and widely available, and could therefore be considered for risk refinement in primary or community‑based settings. However, the modest effect sizes and the lack of clinically validated cut‑offs preclude firm recommendations at this stage; prospective validation of specific thresholds and demonstration of improved patient outcomes are needed before routine clinical adoption.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study suggests that elevated hs-CRP and CumAIP are jointly associated with a moderate increase in CVD incidence among middle-aged and older Chinese adults with stage 0–3 CKM syndrome. This finding lends epidemiological support to the core pathophysiological mechanism of CKM syndrome—the interplay between metabolism and inflammation—and highlights the potential value of an integrative perspective for further refinement of risk assessment in this population.
