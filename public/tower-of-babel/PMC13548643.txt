Axial pain after anterior cervical discectomy and fusion: risk factor analysis and imaging evaluation
Authors: Yanting Chen, Xin Chen, Guanhong Chen, Wei Guo, Chuandong Lang, Xinsheng Peng, Yuhu Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAxial pain can occur as a complication following anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF), which is frequently regarded as a complication of posterior cervical surgery and is often overlooked after ACDF. However, the risk factors of postoperative axial pain (PAP) remain incompletely elucidated. This study aimed to analyze the risk factors and radiological assessment of PAP in patients undergoing ACDF.MethodsIndividuals undergoing ACDF between January 2020 to March 2023 were included. Participants were categorized into two groups by PAP presence. Clinical parameters were collected, and radiographic parameters measured included pre- and postoperative C2 ~ 7 Cobb angle, C2 ~ 7 sagittal vertical axis, T1 slope, intervertebral disc height, facet joint distance (FJD), and interspinous distance. Spinal cord function was evaluated using the Japanese Orthopaedic Association score. Univariate/multivariable analyses identified independent risk factors for PAP after ACDF. Diagnostic performance of postoperative C2 ~ 7 Cobb angle change and postoperative relative FJD change was evaluated via Receiver Operating Characteristic curve analysis. Optimal cutoff values were determined using the Youden index.ResultsIn ACDF patients, PAP prevalence was 15.5% in single-level surgery and 19.8% in multi-level surgery. For single-level ACDF, logistic regression analysis identified postoperative relative FJD change as a PAP risk factor (Area Under the Curve [AUC] = 0.809, optimal cutoff = 27.95%). For multi-level ACDF, postoperative C2 ~ 7 Cobb angle change was a risk factor (AUC = 0.773, optimal cutoff = 3°).ConclusionsDuring ACDF procedures, minimizing FJD in single-level surgery and avoiding excessive C2 ~ 7 Cobb angle correction in multi-level surgery may reduce PAP prevalence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12891-026-10101-x.Keywords: Cervical spondylosis, ACDF, Postoperative axial pain, Risk factor, Imaging evaluation

Introduction

Cervical spondylotic myelopathy (CSM) represents a frequent degenerative disorder of the spine [1] and serves as one of the leading etiological factors for numbness and pain affecting the cervical, shoulder and upper extremity regions. Since its first description by Robinson and Smith in 1955 [2], anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) has gradually become a mainstream surgical procedure for treating cervical spine diseases and is considered the gold standard for the treatment of cervical disc herniation and cervical spondylosis [3–5]. The relevant complications after ACDF encompass wound infection, postoperative axial pain (PAP), postoperative retropharyngeal hematoma and associated breathing difficulties [6]. Among these, PAP is frequently regarded as a complication of posterior cervical surgery and is often overlooked after ACDF.

PAP has been primarily associated with posterior cervical surgeries since it was first described in 1996 [7], with an prevalence rate ranging from 7% to 58% [8]. In our clinical observations, we identified that some patients undergoing ACDF experienced immediate, persistent and refractory axial pain postoperatively. This prompted us to investigate the underlying causes and their potential association with intraoperative procedures. Indeed, the prevalence of PAP following ACDF has garnered significant attention from numerous scholars. Xu et al. [9] revealed that the prevalence of PAP exceeded 30% among patients receiving single-level ACDF. Cao et al. [10] found that 33.3% of ACDF patients developed PAP. PAP not only significantly reduces the postoperative quality of life but also causes considerable psychological distress to the patients. Therefore, it is crucial to reduce the prevalence of PAP following ACDF. Reported risk factors include hyper-distraction of the cervical vertebrates [11, 12], excessive cervical curvature change [13], loss of cervical mobility [14], decreased neck muscle strength and atrophy [15], prolonged cervical collar use [16], and preoperative neck pain [17]. These studies have different definitions of axial pain post-ACDF. For instance, Nicholas et al. [12] defined PAP as localized in the neck without radiation to the upper limbs, confined to the cervical spine, occipital region and posterior scapular areas. Whereas Hiroyuki et al. [17] thought neck stiffness, nuchal pain and shoulder pain after ACDF is PAP. Furthermore, previous studies included variables such as psychological and many subjective factors, which led to significant differences in the research results. In order to clarify whether there is a correlation between PAP and intraoperative operations, and to better guide the surgical strategy, in this study, we analyzed the risk factors for immediate PAP in ACDF patients using objective imaging data.

According to our clinical observations, most patients with PAP experience discomfort immediately after ACDF. This symptom develops on the first postoperative day after anesthetic resolution and can be effectively relieved by nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) in the majority of cases. Therefore, to address this critical clinical challenge, we focused exclusively on immediate PAP and analyzed radiographic parameters to identify modifiable intraoperative risk factors.

Materials and methods

Patient inclusion

In this study, a retrospective analysis was performed on patients who underwent ACDF at the same medical center and treatment group from January 2020 to March 2023. Following strict inclusion and exclusion criteria, data from a total of 235 patients were collected. Institutional Review Board approval (No. [2024]258) was obtained prior to protocol implementation. Informed consent, which was signed by all participants, was obtained prior to their enrollment in the study. The inclusion criteria were: (1) patients presenting with signs and symptoms of spinal cord compression; (2) diagnosis of CSM confirmed by CT or MRI; (3) undergoing ACDF; (4) a postoperative follow-up of at least 12 months. The exclusion criteria were: (1) preoperative presence of varying degrees of axial neck pain; (2) surgical treatment due to trauma, tumor, rheumatism, infection, or ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament; (3) postoperative intervertebral non-fusion occurred; (4) severe osteoporosis or congenital cervical deformities; (5) cerebral palsy, cerebral infarction, or other systemic diseases; (6) incomplete data, defined as the inability to accurately measure key radiographic parameters due to poor image clarity or anatomical obscuration, or loss to follow-up during the follow-up period.

Surgical procedures

A standard ACDF procedure was performed on each patient by the same surgical team at a single medical center. Routine intravenous induction followed by inhalation anesthesia via video laryngoscopic tracheal intubation was used. Patients were positioned supine with the head slightly turned to the left, and the shoulder and neck elevated to achieve cervical extension. After radiographic localization, a transverse incision of approximately 3–5 cm was made on the right anterior aspect of the cervical spine. The skin and subcutaneous tissues were bluntly dissected, the platysma muscle was incised longitudinally, and the vascular sheath and other tissues were sharply dissected using scissors without cutting the omohyoid muscle, exposing the anterior aspect of the vertebral body. The prevertebral fascia was sharply incised. The intervertebral disc was then removed, exposing the posterior longitudinal ligament, which was typically incised to achieve complete decompression. Finally, an autologous bone-filled cage was used to enhance postoperative bony fusion and placed in the intervertebral space of the operated segment. An anterior cervical plate was placed anteriorly at the responsible segment to ensure spinal stability before bony fusion. To prevent position-induced secondary compression of the cervical spine, intraoperative traction and cervical extension were avoided. The surgery was performed using a spinal surgery microscope (ZEISS, Germany) to assist with decompression.

Radiological assessment

Patients underwent lateral X-rays of the cervical spine at each follow-up preoperatively and postoperatively. Six radiographic parameters were systematically quantified: (1) The C2 ~ 7 Cobb angle was determined by quantifying the angular dimension formed between the inferior endplates of the C2 and C7 cervical vertebrae. (2) The C2 ~ 7 sagittal vertical axis (C2 ~ 7 SVA) is radiographically assessed as the linear distance between the C2 sagittal plumb line and the posterosuperior endplate of the C7 vertebra. (3) The T1 slope is defined as the angular value formed between the superior endplate of the T1 vertebra and the horizontal reference plane. (4) Intervertebral disc height (IDH) is described as the distance between the midpoint of the inferior endplate of the superior vertebra and the midpoint of the superior endplate of the inferior vertebra. (5) Facet joint distance (FJD) quantified as the distance between the midpoint of the upper and lower facet joints. (6) Interspinous distance (ISD), measured as the distance between the midpoint of spinous process of superior vertebra and the inferior vertebra (Fig. 1).

Postoperative follow-up and data collection

In strict accordance with the study’s inclusion criteria, we retrieved data from the hospital’s electronic medical records system for enrolled patients, including age at surgery, sex, body mass index (BMI), baseline comorbidities, surgical procedures, operative segments, duration of surgery and imaging data. Patients were followed up preoperatively, postoperatively, and at 3-, 6-, and 12-month intervals.

Neurological function assessment

The Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) scoring system was used to evaluate neurological function. A team of two certified research assistants, blinded to all clinical information, conducted duplicate assessments of the pre- and post-operative JOA scores, and the mean values from both observers were utilized for analysis.

Assessment of PAP

In this study, PAP is defined as persistent dull pain presenting on postoperative day 1 after anesthetic resolution. The pain radiates from the neck to the shoulder or shoulder girdle, accompanied by soreness, stiffness, pressure, and muscle spasms. Although this discomfort is typically alleviated by NSAIDs, a definitive diagnosis is made based on these clinical features after excluding other relevant systemic diseases, independent of medication responsiveness. PAP assessment utilizes the numeric Visual Analog Scale (nVAS). Patients with a nVAS score ranging from 1 to less than 4 were classified as experiencing mild PAP, those with scores from 4 to less than 7 were categorized as having moderate PAP, and patients with an nVAS score of 7 or higher were deemed to have severe PAP.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted with SPSS 26 (IBM, California, USA). The variables with a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared using t-tests. Variables failing to follow a normal distribution were described as median (interquartile range, IQR), with between-group differences examined via the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were described by frequencies and percentages, with ordered categories compared using Wilcoxon rank-sum test and unordered categories analyzed via chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Risk factors were initially screened by univariate logistic regression. We incorporated variables with p < 0.05 and clinically significant indicators (even in cases where p ≥ 0.05) into the multivariable logistic regression model to determine the independent predictive factors of PAP following ACDF. Using Spearman’s rank correlation to detect the correlation between postoperative relative change in IDH and FJD. Diagnostic performance of postoperative C2 ~ 7 Cobb angle change and postoperative relative change in FJD was assessed through Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The optimal cutoff values were identified using the Youden index.

Of the total cohort, 20 patients were assigned to the axial pain group and 109 to the non-axial pain group. The postoperative T1 slope between the two groups was statistically significant (p < 0.05), with a median of 28° (24°−33°) in the axial pain group and 25° (19°−31°) in the non-axial pain group. Additionally, there was a significant difference in postoperative relative change of FJD between the groups (p < 0.001), with a median of 29.0% (24.8%−34.0%) in the axial pain group and 22.3% (19.6%−24.6%) in the non-axial pain group. No significant intergroup differences were detected in age, sex, BMI, smoking status, diabetes, hypertension, operative time, over-correction of lordosis or preoperative variables like C2 ~ 7 Cobb angle, C2 ~ 7 SVA, T1 slope and IDH (Table 4, S1 and Figure S1). Multivariable logistic regression analysis identified that postoperative relative change in FJD was significantly associated with PAP (p < 0.001, odds ratio [OR] = 1.31, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.15–1.48). Postoperative T1 slope had no significant association (p = 0.124) (Table 5).

Discussion

In this retrospective study, we aimed to analyze the risk factors for immediate PAP in ACDF patients using objective imaging data to better guide surgical strategies. Our principal findings demonstrated that the specific risk factors for immediate PAP differ depending on the surgical scope. Specifically, for single-level ACDF, a postoperative relative change in FJD was identified as the independent risk factor, with an optimal cutoff value of 27.95%. Conversely, for multi-level ACDF, the postoperative C2 ~ 7 Cobb angle change was the primary risk factor, with an optimal cutoff of 3°. These objective radiological findings directly support our hypothesis that the development of immediate PAP is closely associated with specific, modifiable intraoperative structural alterations rather than purely subjective or psychosocial factors.

Previous studies have shown that PAP after ACDF is related to excessive distraction of the intervertebral space [18], and Bai et al. [19] observed that if the height variation of the intervertebral space in the operated segment after ACDF exceeds 10%, the prevalence of PAP significantly increases. Wang et al. [20] believed that excessive or insufficient increase in intervertebral space can increase the prevalence of PAP. However, these conclusions are controversial, as other studies have shown no direct relationship between excessive vertebral distraction and PAP [21, 22]. In this research, we found that PAP shows no correlation with the IDH but is associated with variations in the FJD, consistent with the findings of previous studies by Lee et al. [23] A comprehensive analysis suggests that the following factors may contribute to this discrepancy: Firstly, anatomical studies have shown that ligaments directly connect the facet joint capsules to cervical nerve roots [24]. The radial intraforaminal ligaments existing between the cervical nerve roots and the surrounding tissues function as a protective mechanism for the nerve roots. They situate the nerve roots in the most appropriate position within the intervertebral foramina, provide support to the nerve roots within the foramina, and safeguard them from friction with the surrounding tissues. These ligaments encircling the nerve roots within the intervertebral foramina can, to a certain extent, preserve the morphology of the nerve root sleeves, facilitating the transmission of neuroelectrophysiological signals, the flow of cerebrospinal fluid, and blood circulation within the root sleeves. When the facet joint gap is dilated, it may exert tension on the ligaments that anchor the nerve roots in the intervertebral foramina, potentially inducing PAP. Additionally, patients who underwent single-level ACDF, the C5/6 segment had the highest prevalence of PAP (65%), followed by the C4/5 segment (35%). The possible reason for this is the dermatomal overlap between C5/C6 innervation and PAP distribution. Secondly, when the facet joint gap is excessively opened, it will stimulate the sensory receptors located on the facet joints, which in turn leads to symptoms such as discomfort or pain in patients. Finally, it could potentially be attributed to the depth of cage placement. In this research, we identified that postoperative relative change in IDH has no correlation with postoperative FJD change. Because the height of intervertebral disc distraction lies in the longitudinal dimension, while the facet joint plane typically assumes a 45° angle and is influenced by both longitudinal and lateral dimensions. When the cage is placed relatively shallow, the effect on the facet joint space is relatively minor. On the contrary, when the cage is placed relatively deep, the influence on the facet joint space is relatively significant. Based on the above analysis, we hold the view that PAP is not related to IDH, but is associated with the change in FJD. These findings carry significant clinical implications for surgical strategies. To prevent the FJD increase from exceeding the 27.95% threshold, especially at high-risk segments such as C4/5 and C5/6, surgeons should optimize cage positioning rather than merely restricting cage height. Placing the cage at a relatively shallower (more anterior) position provides adequate anterior column support without excessively wedging open the posterior facet joints. Furthermore, regarding the balance between minimizing facet distraction and achieving indirect decompression, alleviating neural compression remains the absolute clinical priority. In cases involving severe posterior compression, such as ligamentum flavum buckling, sufficient distraction is necessary. However, performing a meticulous direct anterior decompression can reduce the surgical reliance on aggressive indirect distraction, thereby achieving optimal neurological recovery while minimizing the risk of PAP.

Our study found that among patients undergoing multi-level ACDF, a greater increase in postoperative C2 ~ 7 Cobb angle relative to preoperative values is associated with a higher likelihood of PAP. The greater correction of cervical curvature achieved by ACDF also increases the mechanical load on the cervical posterior column. When the physiological lordosis of the cervical spine decreases or disappears, the pressure on the intervertebral disc increases, the muscle strength of the neck is unbalanced, and fatigue injury is prone to occur. The nerve tissue may be subjected to traction and stimulation, causing discomfort such as upper limb pain, finger numbness, dizziness, etc. A recent study by Xu et al. [25] demonstrated that the spreader-assisted vertebral distraction technique significantly alleviates postoperative cervicalgia in patients undergoing ACDF, this effect may stem from the transient reduction of facet joint load distribution achieved through intraoperative vertebral distraction. Bilateral cervical facet joints contain rich sensory receptors capable of sensing the intensity of physiological stimulation [26], so when pressure on the posterior column increases, the bilateral cervical facet joints that bear most of the pressure will generate pain. Furthermore, the static sagittal balance of a healthy spine constitutes the physiological configuration of muscular structures achieved with maximal functional efficiency [27]. When there is sagittal imbalance in the cervical spine, the imbalance between flexor and extensor muscle strength causes PAP. With the continuation of the disease state for a period of time, the balance between flexor and extensor muscles gradually adapts, resulting in compensatory balance in the disease state, and the greater the correction of cervical curvature achieved by ACDF, the greater the disruption to this compensatory balance, leading to the reappearance of balance between flexor and extensor muscles, causing PAP in patients.

Interestingly, there was no statistically significant difference in the postoperative C2 ~ 7 Cobb change between the two groups after single-level ACDF. This may be related to the small correction ability of single-level ACDF for cervical curvature. The correction of cervical curvature by multi-level ACDF can be roughly considered as the accumulation of the effects of multiple single-level ACDF in correcting cervical curvature. Therefore, for multi-level ACDF, the postoperative C2 ~ 7 Cobb change is significant, leading to substantial alterations in cervical curvature and thus causing PAP. In contrast, single-level ACDF demonstrates limited cervical curvature correction. Furthermore, we observed no relationship between spinal cord neurofunctional recovery and PAP, consistent with the findings of other researchers [28–31].

This study has several limitations. Firstly, due to its retrospective nature, certain important radiographic parameters, such as the spinal canal diameter, were not quantitatively measured, which may limit our comprehensive evaluation of spinal stenosis and introduce selection bias. Secondly, subjective perceptions of PAP may vary among different patients in different environments. Thirdly, other postoperative complications that may affect PAP were not considered. Fourthly, for multi-level ACDF, local segmental parameters were excluded from the multivariable analysis due to the methodological challenges of construct-level aggregation, restricting our analysis to global alignment. Fifthly, because the predictive performance (AUC) was evaluated within the same cohort used for model development, the reported ROC thresholds may be subject to optimism bias and statistical overfitting, lacking internal or external validation. Future research should conduct multi-center, large-sample prospective studies with broader radiographic evaluations and independent validation cohorts to control for these factors and reliably determine risk factors associated with PAP.

Conclusion

In this study, we evaluated single-level and multi-level ACDF cohorts separately to identify risk factors for PAP. For single-level ACDF, a postoperative relative increase in FJD exceeding 27.95% was an independent risk factor. For multi-level ACDF, a postoperative C2 ~ 7 Cobb angle increase of more than 3° was the primary risk factor. Therefore, meticulous control of FJD in single-level procedures and avoiding overcorrection of the C2 ~ 7 Cobb angle in multi-level procedures may reduce the prevalence of PAP.
