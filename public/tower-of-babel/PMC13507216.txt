Two telomere-to-telomere Nelumbo genome assemblies reveal domestication history and empower precision breeding
Authors: Heng Sun, Jia Xin, Yuye Yu, Gangqiang Dong, Juan Liu, Xianbao Deng, Yanyan Su, Heyun Song, Ruirui Li, Dong Yang, Qiqi Liang, Shuangxia Jin, Mei Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lotus (Nelumbo) is an ancient aquatic plant of major ecological, economic, and cultural importance, yet its genomic architecture and domestication history remain incompletely resolved. Here, we generate telomere-to-telomere reference genomes for the two extant species, Asian lotus (N. nucifera) and American lotus (N. lutea). Comparative genomics analysis reveals divergence in centromeric regions and chromosomal structural variation between the two species. Population analysis of 832 globally distributed lotus accessions supports tropical Asia as the primary dispersal cradle of Asian lotus. We detect historical introgression events contributing to modern cultivated gene pools, and identify key loci regulating flower color variation and rhizome enlargement. To support research and breeding, we develop the Nelumbo Multi-omics Genome Platform (NMGP; http://182.92.235.125:18888/lotus/home/), integrating multi-omics data with a Pearson correlation-weighted Fusion of machine learning Models for Genomic Prediction (PFMGP) framework. These resources provide foundation for studying lotus evolution and genome-informed breeding.

Introduction

The genus Nelumbo (family Nelumbonaceae), commonly known as lotus, represents an ancient lineage of perennial aquatic plants that has survived since the Ice Age and is highly valued for its ornamental, edible, and medicinal properties1,2. Despite its long evolutionary history, the genus comprises only two extant species, Asian lotus (N. nucifera Gaertn.) and American lotus (N. lutea Willd.), which exhibit notable divergence in morphology and geographic distribution but share the same chromosome number (2n = 2x = 16, AA) and lack interspecific reproductive barriers3. Over the past decade, several genome assemblies for Nelumbo species have been produced and iteratively improved, providing an increasingly useful resource for lotus biology1,4,5. However, substantial gaps and unresolved regions remain, particularly within highly repetitive sequences, such as centromeres, telomeres, transposable elements (TEs), and segmental duplication (SD) regions that are critical but difficult to assemble accurately. These genomic components play important roles in genome evolution by influencing gene expression and shaping chromosomal architecture6–8. Thus, achieving the complete gap-free telomere-to-telomere (T2T) assemblies for Nelumbo genomes is essential for constructing fully resolved chromosome maps and enabling deeper evolutionary and functional insight.

American and Asian lotus diverged following the extinction of their ancestral intermediate during the Ice Age, a process that led to their separation into two distinct species9. Despite considerable research, the precise domestication center of Asian lotus remains a topic of debate. Fossils and phylogenetic evidence traces the early ancestor of N. nucifera back to approximately 20 million years ago (Mya) in eastern and southern Asia10, while historical and archaeological data indicate that its modern distribution and early cultivation were predominantly centred in Asia such as China, India, and Thailand2,3. Two major ecotypes, temperate and tropical ecotypes, arose in Asian lotus through prolonged adaptation to distinct geographic and climatic conditions, and these forms display significant genetic differentiation, particularly in traits related to rhizome enlargement11,12. Long-term extensive cultivation has further given rise to three major cultivation types, i.e., seed-, flower-, and rhizome-lotus13. Genetic diversity analysis shows relatively high diversity in flower- and seed-lotus, whereas rhizome-lotus exhibits comparatively lower diversity14. Domestication history is marked by multiple genetic origins and an uneven spatial distribution of diversity, with the highest levels in eastern China and unique haplotypes preserved in Yunnan (China), Indonesia, and Thailand15. Nevertheless, the evolutionary history of Asian lotus remains incompletely resolved, and whole-genome variation across ecotypes and cultivated types remains poorly understood.

Asian lotus has been cultivated for thousands of years across Asia, resulting in over 4000 lotus varieties derived through natural differentiation, spontaneous variation, and traditional hybrid breeding16. While this extensive genetic diversity provides a rich reservoir for improvement, it also complicates the selection of superior varieties due to widespread genetic admixture. In China, systematic lotus breeding only began in the mid-to-late 20th century. Consequently, only a limited number of dominant cultivars dominate current agriculture production, constraining efforts to elucidate genotype-phenotype linkages16,17. Additionally, the lack of interspecific population-level analyses limits our understanding of evolutionary divergence and gene flow, thereby hindering the development of improved lotus cultivars. In other major crops such as cotton18, rice19, and chrysanthemum20, comprehensive and user-friendly databases have been established to integrate high-quality omics resources and facilitate trait discovery and breeding. Similarly, a centralized genomic platform for lotus would enable the rapid identification of candidate genes associated with key agronomic traits and support genome-informed breeding programs.

In this work, we address these gaps by delivering two completely gap-free T2T assemblies, for Asian lotus and American lotus, constructed using PacBio HiFi, Oxford Nanopore ultralong reads, and Hi-C scaffolding. These assemblies resolve centromeric architecture, telomeric structure, and repetitive genomic regions, overcoming the limitations of earlier references. To characterize genetic diversity across the genus, re-sequencing data of 832 geographically and morphologically diverse accessions are analyzed, revealing patterns of population structure, divergence history, domestication bottlenecks, and loci associated with key agronomic traits. All genomic, variant, and functional annotation data are integrated into the Nelumbo Multi-omics Genome Platform (NMGP; http://182.92.235.125:18888/lotus/home/), an open-access database designed to facilitate functional genomics and genome-informed breeding of this important aquatic crop.

Results

De novo T2T assembly and annotation of Nelumbo genomes

High-quality, gap-free T2T genome assemblies of Nelumbo were generated by integrating 23.29 Gb and 25.00 Gb of PacBio HiFi reads (28.57×, 29.82×), 112.17 Gb and 77.36 Gb of ONT ultralong reads (137.59×, 92.26×), 43.5 Gb and 42.1 Gb of DNBSEQ-T7 PE150 reads (53.36×, 50.21×), and 234.25 Gb and 99.05 Gb of Hi-C data (287.35×, 118.13×) for N. nucifera (NNU) and N. lutea (NLU), respectively (Fig. 1a, b, Supplementary Data 1). Initial genome assembly was performed by integrating HiFi and ONT reads, generating draft genomes of 820.59 Mb for NNU and 852.85 Mb for NLU. Remaining gaps were filled by aligning ONT reads to flanking or poorly mapped regions, followed by local reassembly. The contig-level assemblies were further polished with short reads and corrected using HiFi-based local refinement, ultimately producing gap-free T2T genome assemblies of 815.22 Mb and 838.46 Mb for NNU and NLU, respectively (Table 1, Supplementary Fig. 1-3). Both genomes show substantial gains over previous versions: the contig N50 values increased from 484.3 kb to 111.39 Mb in NNU and from 1.34 Mb to 117.41 Mb in NLU, representing ~230-fold (‘China Antique v2.0’, ASM1431973v1)5 and ~90-fold (N. lutea, ASM2282859v1)1 improvements over previous assemblies (Table 1). Benchmarking against previous non-T2T references highlighted the recovery of complete centromeric chromosomes and intact telomeres across both species (Fig. 1c, d). Our genome assemblies eliminated all gaps present in previously published genome versions. For example, four gaps spanning 0.76 kb in NNU (original genomic coordinates: 66,103,940-66,246,625 bp; updated to 70,725,128-70,944,239 bp) were fully resolved, incorporating additional gene fragments (Nn4g25384-Nn4g25386) now mapped on Chr04.g17057-Chr04.g17058 (Fig. 1e). In NLU, a ~ 0.32 Mb gap region was similarly completed, and the region mapped by NL1g_01054 harbors three genes: chr1.g07200, chr1.g07201, and chr1.g07202 (Fig. 1e). Similarly, comparable improvements were observed throughout the genomes (Fig. 1c, d, Supplementary Data 2, 3). Each genome contained 16 telomeres and 8 centromeres, all of which were recovered with their characteristic repeat signatures (Fig. 1c, d, Supplementary Data 4). Telomeric regions in both species contained hundreds of copies of the canonical 7 bp repeat (CCCTAAA)n or (TTTAGGG)n (Fig. 1c, d, Supplementary Fig. 4, Supplementary Data 4). Consensus accuracy was high (quality values, QV = 54.04 and 45.45, respectively), and Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologues (BUSCO) assessments indicated 99.1% and 98.9% completeness for NNU and NLU, respectively (Table 1, Supplementary Data 5). Genome assembly quality, sequence composition, and gene content

Repetitive sequences accounted for 55.19% of the NNU genome and 66.41% of the NLU genome (Supplementary Data 8). NNU contained 334,514 long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons (293.53 Mb, 36.01% of the total assembly), whereas LTR-RTs comprised 38.32% (321.31 Mb) of the NLU assembly (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Data 8). Additionally, ribosomal (rRNAs) arrays were abundant, with 9468 and 6851 rRNA loci detected in NNU and NLU genomes, respectively (Supplementary Data 9). Major 5S rDNA sequences with lengths of ~0.71 Mb (5929 repeats) and ~0.40 Mb (3345 repeats) were located on chromosomes 4 in NNU and NLU, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 5, Supplementary Data 10). In NNU, major 45S rDNA clusters ( ~ 0.45 Mb, 292 repeats; ~0.31 Mb, 210 repeats, and ~0.23 Mb, 144 repeats) were found on chromosomes 3, 6, and 5, whereas NLU harbored two large clusters on chromosome 3 ( ~ 1.93 Mb, 1125 repeats) and 2 ( ~ 0.67 Mb, 412 repeats). Besides the major 45S rDNA, several minor 45S rDNA loci with low copy number were detected in both genomes (Supplementary Fig. 5, Supplementary Data 10). Collectively, these assemblies meet and exceed the benchmarks for complete T2T plant genomes, providing a comprehensive and accurate genomic foundation for future research and breeding.

Genomic divergence and structural variants in the Nelumbo genomes

Genomic variation is a major driver of evolution, species diversification, and trait determination21. To elucidate the evolutionary and orthologous relationships between N. nucifera and N. lutea, a maximum-likelihood phylogeny was constructed, indicating that the two species are diverged approximately 15.1 Mya (Fig. 1f). This time coincides with the Middle Miocene Climatic Optimum (MMCO), a period characterized by elevated global temperatures, which likely facilitated long-term ecological isolation and lineage divergence22. Structural variants (SVs) are pervasive in plant genomes and profoundly influence genome architecture, gene regulation, and complex trait formation23. Using the gap-free T2T assemblies, a comparative genomic analysis revealed strong overall chromosomal collinearity between the two species, with ~500.4 Mb of syntenic sequence (Fig. 2a). A total of 145,739 SVs were identified between the two genomes, including 62,571 insertions, 4421 duplications, 504 inversions, 69,916 deletions, and 8327 translocations, unevenly distributed across all chromosomes (Supplementary Fig. 6, Supplementary Data 11). Correlation analysis further indicated that SVs were largely associated with TEs (Supplementary Fig. 7). In NNU, most SVs were located in the intergenic regions (47.03%, 68,542), followed by intronic (24.14%, 35,179), upstream (5.03%, 7332), and exons (4.67%, 6,799) (Supplementary Data 12). A total of 23 large inversions ( > 1 Mb) were detected in both species, the largest spanning 11.76 Mb and 11.02 Mb, respectively (Supplementary Data 13). Validation of four small SVs (0.15–9.4 kb) and seven large SVs (0.5–11.7 Mb) using PacBio long reads and Hi-C contacts confirmed the accuracy of SV boundaries (Supplementary Fig. 8, Supplementary Data 14). To investigate the genomic expansion of NLU, insertion variants were examined and found to exceed deletions by 14.47 Mb. Insertions were highly enriched for TEs (Fig. 2b), particularly LTR retrotransposons, which accounted for 70.24% of TE containing insertions, followed by DNA transposons (7.07%) and LINEs (4.47%) (Fig. 2b, c). These findings suggest that TE insertion proliferation is the major contributor to the larger NLU genome. SV-associated gene changes further highlight functional divergence between the two species. Compared with NLU, NNU harbors 387 novel genes derived from deletions in the NLU genome, while NLU contains 476 novel genes derived from insertions in its own genome relative to NNU (Supplementary Data 15, 16). Functional enrichment analysis showed that novel NLU genes are primarily enriched in plant hormone signal transduction, plant-pathogen interaction, and negative regulation of seed germination, while NNU specific genes are mainly enriched in hormone metabolic process, starch and sucrose metabolism, and plant-pathogen interaction (Supplementary Figs. 14–17). Two NNU SV regions contained one and five SV-associated genes, respectively, all involved in auxin response pathways (Fig. 2d, e). These include homologs of AtSAUR64 (Chr06.g23281, Chr06.g23282, and Chr06.g23285) and AtSAUR66 (Chr06.g23283), which encode SAUR-like auxin-responsive proteins that act as key upstream regulators of auxin signaling24. This suggests that these SVs likely modulate auxin biosynthesis and may contribute to phenotypic differences in plant height and organ size development between Asian lotus and American lotus.

Centromere characteristics of the Nelumbo genomes

Centromeres are essential chromosomal loci whose functions are conserved across eukaryotes, yet their DNA sequences vary widely in size, structure, and organization25. However, all previous assemblies of N. nucifera and N. lutea contained thousands of gaps ( ~ 2810 and 1400, respectively), preventing accurate reconstruction of centromeric and telomeric regions1,5. Using gap-free T2T genomes, centromeres were identified through chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) with an anti-CenH3 antibody, followed by localization of CenH3-enriched regions. CenH3-binding regions were dominated by LTR retrotransposon fragments, but their compositions differed markedly between species (Fig. 2f). In NNU, LTRs accounted for 88.7% of centromeric sequence, whereas NLU showed a lower proportion of LTRs but a substantially higher proportion of satellite repeats and miscellaneous sequence types (Fig. 2f, Supplementary Figs. 18–35). For instance, cen1 spans 47.71–48.77 Mb in NNU and 52.97–55.30 Mb in NLU, and both regions exhibit dense ChIP-seq peaks and abundant LTR retrotransposons (Fig. 2g, h). NLU cen1 also showed significant satellite repeat enrichment (Fig. 2h, Supplementary Fig. 19). Centromere boundaries were predicted using a combination of centromere prediction software and ChIP-seq read density. Core centromeres ranged from 1.06 to 1.52 Mb in NNU and 0.97 to 2.33 Mb in NLU, with no significant correlation between centromere size and chromosomal length (Supplementary Figs. 18–35, Supplementary Data 4). Sequence composition analysis revealed strong enrichment of LTR elements across all centromeres (Supplementary Figs. 36, 37, 39, 40). These patterns indicate that rapid expansion of LTR retrotransposons has been a key force shaping lotus centromere evolution (Fig. 2i, j). In addition, we observed a relatively low gene density in the centromeric regions of lotus (Supplementary Figs. 38, 41).

Domain-based classification of proteins26 showed that centromeric LTRs are dominated by Ale and Angela subfamilies (Fig. 2k, l), distinct from rice, maize, and cotton, whose centromeres are dominated by the CRM or Tekay elements27–29. The historical dynamics analysis showed that centromeric LTRs are relatively younger than those outside centromeres, with NNU harboring younger centromeric LTRs than NLU (Fig. 2k). Gypsy elements are younger than Copia elements within centromeric regions in NLU, whereas the opposite pattern was observed in NNU. For key centromeric constituent elements, the age hierarchy in NNU follows the order Athila, followed by Ale, and then Angela, whereas in NLU, Ale and Angela are earlier than Athila (Fig. 2l). In addition to transposable elements, 53 genes in NNU and 70 genes in NLU were identified within centromeric regions, and these genes encode proteins with predicted core biological functions (Supplementary Data 17).

Genetic diversity and population structure in the Nelumbo genus

To investigate the phylogenetic relationships and population structure within the Nelumbo genus, 832 accessions were analyzed, generating 7.87 Tb of sequence data with an average depth of 11.61× per accession. Samples were collected primarily from seven countries of Asia and North America (China, Japan, Thailand, India, Singapore, Cambodia, and the United States), representing geographically and genetically diverse wild and cultivated populations across distinct climatic regions (Fig. 3a). Using 4.42 M high-quality single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), phylogenetic analysis resolved the accessions into five distinct groups: three Asian lotus ecotypes (A1: Asian 1, A2: Asian 2, A3: Asian 3), American×Asian hybrid lotus (A4), and American lotus (A5) (Supplementary Data 18). Population structure analyses clearly separated American and Asian lotus, revealing a well-defined pattern of geographic distribution and distinct ancestral components (Fig. 3b). Genetic subdivision within Asian lotus populations was detectable, reflecting complex evolutionary histories. The A4 group exhibited biparental ancestry derived from artificial crosses between Asian lotus and American lotus (Fig. 3b). Principal component analysis (PCA) further confirmed the consistency of the results (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. 42). Phylogenetic analysis showed that A1 diverged earliest, followed by A2 and A3. Furthermore, the Asian lotus ecotypes displayed a strong latitudinal gradient. From A1 to A3, the proportion of tropical lotus individuals decreased from 15.7% to 0.5%, and that of subtropical lotus individuals dropped from 71.1% to 15.9%. By contrast, the proportion of temperate lotus individuals increased from 7.2% to 79.8% (Fig. 3b). These patterns suggest that tropical regions are the dispersal center for Asian lotus, followed by northward expansion. To quantify genetic diversity among various lotus populations, 31 accessions were randomly selected from each group (to match the smallest group size to minimize sampling bias), and nucleotide diversity (π), population differentiation (FST), linkage disequilibrium (LD), heterozygosity, inbreeding coefficients (F), and runs of homozygosity (ROH) were calculated within and between groups. Nucleotide diversity was lowest in American lotus A5 (π = 9.62 × 10−5), whereas Asian lotus groups (A1-A3) exhibited substantially higher diversity (Average π = 1.79 × 10−3). Pairwise FST values revealed strong genetic diversity between American lotus and Asian lotus, with an average of 0.55 between A5 and A1-A3 (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Data 19). Within Asian lotus, FST values ranged from 0.02 to 0.39, indicating a gradient of population differentiation (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Data 19). LD decay analysis (r²) showed lower LD in the A4 and A5 compared with Asian lotus groups (Fig. 3e). American lotus (A5) exhibited a low level of heterozygosity, elevated F value, and an excess of short ROHs segments, collectively indicating a pronounced historical genetic bottleneck (Fig. 3f-h). In contrast, the hybrid A4 group displayed the highest heterozygosity, fewer short ROHs segments, and lower F value, consistent with recent artificial introgression between Asian lotus and American lotus. Within Asian lotus, genomic heterozygosity gradually declined from A1 to A3, accompanied increasing F values and longer ROH segments. These patterns suggest prolonged domestication-associated inbreeding interspersed with introgression, resulting in distinct demographic histories among Asian lotus ecotypes (Fig. 3f-h). Together, these results highlight deep genetic divergence between Asian lotus and American lotus, and reveal substantial, structured genetic diversity within the Asian lotus populations, shaped by domestication history, geographic expansion, and interspecific gene flow.

Retracing the demographic history and genetic introgression of Nelumbo

To reconstruct the demographic history of Nelumbo genus, historical effective population sizes (Ne) were estimated for major lineages. Over the past 10,000 years, temperate Asian lotus exhibited significant fluctuations in Ne, characterized by rapid declines followed by partial recoveries (Fig. 4a). In contrast, both tropical Asian lotus and American lotus experienced sharp Ne reductions approximately 1000 years ago, a pattern distinct from that of temperate populations. Extending the analysis to deeper timescales over 104 years, revealed a persistent reduction in Ne in American lotus, which remained consistently lower than that of Asian lotus (Fig. 4b). Asian lotus experienced multiple population bottlenecks coinciding with glacial periods, likely reflecting severe environmental constraints during these intervals. Tropical and subtropical Asian lotus appear to have been less affected by the Last Glacial Maximum (LGM), indicating the existence of distinct glacial refugia in mid‑ and low‑latitude regions that supported the survival of Asian lotus during glacial events. Gene introgression, a major driver of evolutionary diversification and environmental adaptation30, was then examined. Patterson’s D statistic detected extensive sharing of derived alleles among wild lotus, rhizome lotus, and flower lotus, providing strong evidence of widespread introgression (Fig. 4c). Additionally, f-branch (fb) and D statistics analysis revealed substantial gene flow between wild lotus and rhizome lotus (high Z-score of 58.23, D value of 0.86, and fb value of 1), indicating that rhizome lotus cultivars retain a strong genetic continuity with wild populations. Inter-specific hybridization between Asian lotus and American lotus has been widely exploited in flower lotus breeding. The red-flowered variety ‘Qiusanse’ (QSS) and the white-yellowish variety ‘Qiumudan’ (QMD) originated from selfed progeny of an interspecific hybrid (Fig. 4d). Following hybridization, introgressed genomic segments are progressively fragmented by recombination and shaped by selection31. Genome-wide introgression analysis across seven accessions from the breeding pedigree of elite flower lotus cultivars ‘QSS’ and ‘QMD’ identified a total of 213,980 pairwise identical-by-descent (IBD) segments, with an average length of 63.38 kb (Fig. 4e, Supplementary Data 20). In ‘QMD’ and ‘QSS’, 16.36% and 14.32% of the IBD segments, respectively, were inherited from the ancestral cultivar ‘DFH’, with additional contributions from ‘DH’, ‘JH’, and ‘NLU’. To trace the genetic origins of key horticultural traits, we estimated locus-specific ancestry contributions across genomic regions. Parental contribution analysis revealed that ‘JH’ was the predominant contributor in both cultivars (30.08% in ‘QSS’ and 26.97% in ‘QMD’). While in ‘QSS’ followed by ‘Ti’ (21.74%), ‘NLU’ (18.09%), and ‘DH’ (17.56%), in ‘QMD’ followed by ‘Ti’ (21.82%), ‘NLU’ (19.91%), and ‘DFH’ (16.36%) (Fig. 4e, Supplementary Data 21). To focus on the determinants of flower color, we examined shared IBD regions associated with this trait. The key regulatory gene NnMYB532, which controls lotus flower color, was located within a shared IBD segment on chromosome 4. Sequence variations at this locus are strongly associated with petal color divergence (Fig. 4f). Red-flowered cultivars shared conserved nucleotide and amino acid sequences, whereas a single-nucleotide mutation in non-red-flowered ‘NLU’ and ‘QMD’ introduced a premature stop codon, resulting in truncated protein products (Fig. 4f). This association between NnMYB5 coding mutations and petal color variation aligns with previously published functional evidence confirming its pivotal role in petal color regulation.

Identification of key genes involved in rhizome enlargement in lotus

A defining phenotypic distinction between tropical and temperate Asian lotus is the absence of winter underground rhizome enlargement in tropical types, whereas temperate lotus develops enlarged storage rhizomes (Fig. 5a). To investigate the genetic basis of this differentiation, a genome-wide association study (GWAS) identified 245 significant quantitative trait loci (QTLs), harboring 744 genes (Fig. 5b, Supplementary Data 22). Several previously reported QTLs associated with rhizome enlargement overlapped with these GWAS results (Fig. 5c). In addition, a genome-wide scan based on FST values and nucleotide diversity ratios (π ratios) identified 1875 and 1887 candidate genes, respectively (Fig. 5b, Supplementary Data 22, Supplementary Fig. 43). Subsequent cross-population composite likelihood ratio (XPCLR) analysis revealed an additional 2776 genes, yielding a total of 5762 candidate genes supported by at least one of the four complementary approaches (Fig. 5b, Supplementary Data 22). GO and KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that these genes were significantly enriched (P < 0.05) in processes such as cellular anion homeostasis, protein export, and oxidative phosphorylation (Supplementary Fig. 44, 45, Supplementary Data 23, 24). Using complementary approaches, NnIDD14 was pinpointed as a candidate gene putatively involved in rhizome enlargement regulation, and showed elevated expression specifically detected during the rhizome enlargement stage of lotus (Supplementary Fig. 46). The genomic region harboring NnIDD14 was delimited via complementary sliding-window population genomic analyses integrating GWAS, FST, and XPCLR signals (Fig. 5d, e, Supplementary Data 25). Additionally, natural variants within the NnIDD14 locus showed significant statistical associated with rhizome enlargement traits (Supplementary Data 26), and allelic variation analysis identified 13 polymorphic sites spanning this gene that define two predominant haplotypes associated with either enlarged or non-enlarged rhizome populations, respectively (Fig. 5f). Functional validation using a transient expression system in lotus rhizomes revealed that NnIDD14 overexpression significantly promoted cell expansion and starch biosynthesis (Fig. 5g-k). Collectively, these converging lines of evidence indicate that NnIDD14 as a plausible candidate gene associated with rhizome enlargement and lotus domestication, and suggest that sequence variation at the NnIDD14 locus may have been subject to adaptive selection during domestication, which could contribute to rhizome phenotypic divergence between tropical and temperate lotus ecotypes. PFMGP enables genomic breeding

Compared with traditional breeding, intelligent genomic breeding can significantly shorten breeding cycles and improve selection accuracy by algorithmically simulating optimal combinations of genomic fragments. To this end, we first developed an intelligent breeding model, Pearson correlation-weighted Fusion of machine learning Models for Genomic Prediction (PFMGP), which integrates genotype data with predictive algorithms to predict offspring phenotypes. To evaluate predictive performance, PFMGP was compared with five widely used genomic selection (GS) models: the classical GS model (GBLUP)33, two machine learning (ML)-based models (LightGBM34 and XGBoost35), and two deep learning (DL)-based models (DNNGP36 and DeepCCR37). Validation was performed across multiple plant datasets, including one trait from 167 lotus accessions, six traits from 2000 Iranian bread wheat accessions, four traits from 1245 cotton accessions, 13 important agronomic traits from 2,110 rice accessions, and three traits from 6210 maize accessions38. Across all species and traits, PFMGP consistently achieved the highest predictive accuracy (Fig. 6a, Supplementary Figs. 47, 48, Supplementary Data 27–31). For example, using 11,093,016 markers to predict the petal count (PC) in 167 lotus accessions, PFMGP achieved an accuracy of 0.63, outperforming all other methods. Relative to GBLUP, machine-learning models, and deep-learning approaches, PFMGP improved prediction accuracy by 29.39%, 6.38%, and 11.13%, respectively (Fig. 6a, Supplementary Data 27). In addition, PFMGP was used to predict the accuracy of different traits in other species, exhibiting the highest predictive accuracy compared to other models (Fig. 6a, Supplementary Figs. 47, 48, Supplementary Data 28–31). Prediction accuracy increased and root mean square error (RMSE) decreased as SNP set size increased, indicating improved model performance with greater genomic resolution. Construction of a comprehensive Nelumbo database

Recent advances in lotus genomics, including improvements in sequencing, assembly, gene annotation, and the accumulation of extensive multi-omics data, have created unprecedented opportunities for integrative analysis of lotus biology3. To support the lotus research community and genome scientists, we developed the first integrated and multifunctional lotus genomic database, the Nelumbo Multi-omics Genome Platform (NMGP; http://182.92.235.125:18888/lotus/home/). This platform provides a centralized repository of multi-omics data and analytical tools to facilitate investigations into the population genetics, gene functions, and key biological traits of lotus. The database integrates five major omics datasets, including genomics, transcriptomes, metabolomes, epigenomes, and variomics generated both in this study and public repositories. The platform is organized into nine core functional modules: Resource, Genome, Populations, Transcriptome, Metabolome, Epigenome, PFMGP, Tools, and Download, enabling comprehensive exploration of lotus genomic and phenotypic diversity (Fig. 6b).

Designed with accessibility and usability in mind, NMGP offers several distinctive features. First, it hosts a comprehensive collection of 563 lotus germplasms, accompanied by high-resolution images and detailed phenotypic annotations, enabling efficient germplasm exploitation and rapid identification of accessions with desirable traits. Second, a dedicated population genetics module allows streamlined and accurate dissection of population structure and genetic relationships. Third, NMGP incorporates the PFMGP intelligent breeding model, providing users with a practical framework for genome-informed breeding applications. The platform is freely accessible worldwide, allowing registered users to download datasets and utilize all functional modules, with technical support available as needed. A built-in quick-search engine facilitates rapid retrieval of relevant information. Beyond conventional FASTA-based visualization, NMGP offers a simplified workflow that enables single-click visualization by selecting a genome and specifying an identifier, substantially improving operational efficiency. All visual outputs can be exported as high-resolution images suitable for research purposes. The database is regularly updated with newly generated omics datasets and ongoing functional enhancements to meet evolving research needs. Collectively, NMGP is designed to serve as a one-stop resource for global lotus research, supporting robust gene function analysis and providing a solid data support for genome-assisted breeding efforts and variety improvement.

Discussion

Uncovering genomic “dark matter” and resolving the structure and function of highly repetitive genomic regions represent major frontiers in contemporary genomics research39. Since the release of the first draft genome of N. nucifera ‘China Antique’ in 20134, several additional lotus genomes, including those of ‘Chinese Tai-zi’40,41, ‘Taikonglian NO.3’10, and Nelumbo lutea1, have been reported, establishing a foundational framework for trait dissection and functional genomics. Nevertheless, limitations in sequencing depth, assembly contiguity, and unresolved repetitive regions have constrained the utility of these references for comprehensive evolutionary and structural analyses. In particular, the incomplete representation of centromeres, telomeres, and other repeat-rich regions in previous assemblies has hindered efforts to fully interrogate genome organization and divergence in Nelumbo. This limitation has also restricted the precision of gene mapping, functional element identification, and molecular breeding applications. In light of this, we generated de novo T2T reference genomes for Nelumbo (Fig. 1). These assemblies resolved complex genomic regions including centromeres and telomeres, thereby providing a high-fidelity resource that markedly improves the accuracy of gene annotation, structural variant detection, QTL fine-mapping, and molecular marker development for lotus genetic improvement. Comparisons of chromosome lengths between the prior non-T2T assembly and the newly generated NNU T2T assembly show that several T2T chromosomes (e.g., Chr01, Chr05, and Chr06) are shorter than their non-T2T counterparts (Fig. 1c). These modest length differences likely stem from distinct assembly pipelines and inherent artifacts to the non-T2T assembly, such as incompletely resolved repeats, ambiguous gap padding, and chimeric redundant contigs. Here, we identified marked differences in centromere regions between Asian lotus and American lotus, likely due to their evolutionary divergence approximately 15.1 Mya (Fig. 1f). In NNU, centromeres are predominantly composed of LTRs, whereas NLU exhibits reduced LTR content with a significantly higher proportion of satellite repeats and miscellaneous sequence types (Fig. 2g, h). Furthermore, LTRs in both NNU and NLU are primarily dominated by the Ale and Angela subfamilies (Fig. 2l), in contrast to rice, maize and cotton27–29, suggesting that LTR retrotransposon dynamics have mediated centromeric structural remodeling during Nelumbo evolution, thereby suggesting a key role in centromeric structural diversity.

Leveraging these high-quality assemblies, we constructed a comprehensive landscape of SVs between Asian lotus and American lotus. Insertions and deletions constitute the dominant classes of SVs and account for much of the observed genome size divergence between the two species, with TEs emerging as the primary drivers of these differences (Fig. 2a-c, Supplementary Figs. 6, 7). This finding is consistent with accumulating evidence that TE proliferation, counterbalanced by TE removal and purifying selection, plays a central role in genome size evolution across plant lineages42,43. In Nelumbo, TE-driven expansion of genomic regions and chromosomal rearrangements have contributed to genome structural divergence between species. Moreover, TE insertions proximal to genes have introduced functional variation in loci controlling key traits, thereby increasing the pool of genic variation available for selection and domestication of lotus42. The SV-associated genes identified here are primarily involved in growth regulation and developmental responsiveness, highlighting the importance of SVs in shaping phenotypic diversification in Nelumbo (Supplementary Figs. 14–17). For example, two large SVs specific to Asian lotus encompass several auxin-related genes, suggesting a potential mechanistic link between structural variation and auxin-related pathways (Fig. 2d, e). Given the central role of auxin in regulating plant stature and organ development, these SVs may contribute to the greater plant height and morphological differences observed between Asian lotus and American lotus. Together, these findings highlight the functional significance of SVs in the evolution and domestication of lotus, establishing a robust genomic foundation for future molecular breeding efforts and targeted functional characterization of key loci within the Nelumbo genome.

Significant genetic differentiation between Asian lotus and American lotus was observed in this study (Fig. 3), consistent with their geographic separation1. Specifically, a pronounced genetic boundary shaped by geographic isolation and latitudinal differentiation exists between American and Asian lotus. The average population differentiation coefficient (FST = 0.55) indicates strong genetic separation between the American lotus and the Asian lotus groups. These two groups also exhibited significant differences in π and heterozygosity (Fig. 3d, f). Asian lotus demonstrates a wide distribution and high genetic diversity, likely reflecting frequent genetic exchange and adaptive responses to heterogeneous environments across latitudinal, geographic, and climatic gradients (Fig. 3). This is evident in the observed latitudinal gradient, where the proportion of tropical lotus decreases with increasing latitude, accompanied by an increase in temperate ecotypes (Fig. 3b). Furthermore, the latitudinal shift in genetic composition indicates that tropical regions served as a dispersal center, followed by subsequent northward expansion of Asian lotus (Fig. 3b). Our PCA results reveal a continuous genetic cline across Asian lotus accessions along the main axis of variation, and this genetic differentiation pattern is characteristic of gradual, directional range expansion, and cannot be fully explained by multiple independent origins or long-term in situ isolation of discrete populations (Fig. 3c). Instead, it supports a gradual directional expansion shaped by climatic and geographic gradients. Demographic stability of tropical and subtropical populations during glacial periods further underpins this unidirectional northward expansion pattern (Fig. 4b). In contrast, the low π of American lotus aligns with its restricted geographic range and limited gene flow, with low polymorphism further reinforcing this constrained distribution (Fig. 3d). We observed partial incongruence between our phylogenetic topology and the ADMIXTURE ancestry-clustering results for several Asian lotus subgroups (Fig. 3b). Such discrepancies are frequently reported in diverse plant taxa such as walnut44, Chinese fir45, and rice46, which may stem from shared ancestral polymorphism, asymmetric introgression, or differences in the underlying algorithmic assumptions of the employed methods. Despite the geographical separation and distinct phenotypic variations, no reproductive isolation exists between Asian lotus and American lotus47. Modern breeding techniques have hybridized American lotus with Asian lotus, yielding numerous novel ornamental varieties. These hybrids exhibited higher genetic diversity and more rapid linkage disequilibrium decay (Fig. 3e-h), underscoring the value of wild germplasm as a key resource for breeding and trait improvement in cultivated lotus. This study also reveals that the NnMYB5 gene, associated with petal color, exhibits sequence variation within a shared IBD region on chromosome 4 (Fig. 4d-f). Previous studies demonstrated that the yellow coloration of American lotus results from a single-point mutation in the coding region of NlMYB5, leading to premature termination of the encoded protein. In contrast, flower color variation in Asian lotus is attributed to the presence or absence of NnMYB5, which regulates anthocyanin accumulation32. Here, we discovered that NnMYB5 is frequently retained as a heterozygous locus during flower lotus breeding. Haplotype variation at this locus can be transmitted across generations, producing progeny whose flower colors segregate in accordance with parental phenotypes (Fig. 4f).

With the rapid advances in sequencing and genome assembly technologies, genomics, proteomics, and metabolomics studies of Nelumbo have generated extensive datasets, providing valuable resources for comparative genomic and evolutionary research1,4,5. Despite this progress, existing online platforms remain limited in scope and lack a comprehensive, user-friendly, and integrated database that consolidates high-quality genomic resources across Nelumbo species48. To address this gap, the NMGP was constructed as integrated resource that unifies genome assemblies, transcriptome profiles, metabolome datasets, and epigenome information from diverse Nelumbo accessions (Fig. 6b). NMGP incorporates detailed population-level germplasm information for hundreds of accessions and introduces the PFMGP intelligent breeding framework. By integrating phenotypic data with genome-wide markers, this model enables accurate prediction of optimal breeding combinations, overcoming key limitations of traditional breeding methods (Fig. 6a, Supplementary Figs. 47, 48). Its application improves selection efficiency and supports a transition toward data-driven precision breeding strategies in lotus. In addition, NMGP provides a platform built-in bioinformatic tools that facilitate analysis of molecular genetics, population structure, and species evolution, thereby lowering technical barriers to multi-omics data utilization. Designed according to open-access principles, the NMGP database prioritizes usability and accessibility, is managed by a dedicated professional team that oversees routine system maintenance, server stability and data security, and relies on regular systematic data updates as well as sustained funding to guarantee data accuracy, relevance and continuous platform improvement. Collectively, NMGP serves as both a foundational resource for basic lotus research and a practical framework for intelligent breeding, with promising potential to accelerate elite cultivar development and promote the sustainable advancement of the lotus industry. Future efforts may extend this framework to incorporate multi-environment breeding optimization and cross-species comparative analyses with other crop databases.

In conclusion, the availability of T2T reference genomes for N. nucifera and N. lutea provides an unprecedented view of the complete lotus genome architecture. These findings provide new insights into Nelumbo genome evolution, population genetics, and future crop breeding directions. Moreover, our newly developed database and intelligent breeding model have the potential to advance lotus molecular biology, optimize germplasm utilization, and revolutionize future breeding efforts.

Methods

Genome sequencing and assembly of NNU (N. nucifera) and NLU (N. lutea)

Fresh young leaves of N. nucifera (‘Chinese Antique’, hereafter referred to as NNU) and N. lutea (hereafter referred to as NLU) were collected from the Wuhan Botanical Garden (Wuhan, China) for genome sequencing. For SMRT sequencing, SMRTbell libraries with 40 kb insert fragments were prepared using the SMRTbell Express Template Prep Kit 2.0, according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Pacific Biosciences, CA, USA) and sequenced on the PacBio Sequel II platform. For ONT Ultra-long sequencing, libraries were prepared using the SQK-LSK109 ligation sequencing kit (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK) according to the manufacturer’s protocol and sequenced on the Oxford Nanopore PromethION.

The genomes of NNU and NLU were assembled using an integrative strategy combining ultra-long ONT reads ( ≥ 30 kb) and highly accurate PacBio HiFi reads ( ≥ 99.99%). Initial contig-level assemblies were generated using Hifiasm (v0.19.9-r616)49–51 with parameters “-t 100 -ul ont.fastq.gz hifi.fasta.gz”, producing highly contiguous draft genomes. Chromosome-scale scaffolding was subsequently performed with HapHiC (v1.0.7) using Hi-C data52 with parameters “pipeline genome.fasta HiC.bam 8 --RE “GATC” --correct_nrounds 2 --remove_allelic_links 4 --threads 70 --processes 70 --max_inflation 10”, yielding chromosome-level assemblies. At this stage, 19 and 6 gaps remained in the NLU and NNU, respectively. To further improve assembly accuracy and completeness, a three-step gap-closing and polishing strategy was applied. First, Medaka (v2.0.1) (https://github.com/nanoporetech/medaka) was used to gap-close and polish the chromosome-level assemblies with ONT reads using the parameter “inference” and “sequence”. This step reduced the number of gaps in the NLU genome to 12 and in the NNU genome to 3. Second, the medaka-polished assemblies were further refined using Racon (v1.5.0) (https://github.com/isovic/racon) with PacBio HiFi reads using default parameter. After this round of gap-closing and polishing, the NLU genome still contained 12 gaps, while the NNU genome remained gap-free. Finally, TGS-GapCloser (v1.2.1)53 was applied using ONT reads to close residual gaps with parameters “--scaff genome.fasta --reads ont_reads.fasta --output xx --ne --thread 70”, resulting in complete, gap-free T2T genome assemblies. Both genomes were completely gap-free, each containing zero unresolved gaps. The completeness of the NNU and NLU T2T genomes was assessed using BUSCO (v6.0.0)54 against the eudicotyledons_odb12 database. To evaluate consensus accuracy, a k-mer library was constructed from PacBio HiFi reads using Meryl (v1.3), with the optimal k-mer size determined by the “best_k.sh” script in Merqury (v1.3)55. Subsequently, QV per base and switch errors were calculated for the assemblies using Merqury (v1.3) with the default parameter.

Telomere patching

Telomeric regions were identified using the TIDK pipeline (v0.2.65) with default parameters56. This pipeline detects canonical plant telomeric repeat motifs (CCCTAAA at the 5’-terminus and TTTAGGG at the 3’-terminus). Telomeric repeat abundances were visualized using R to confirm intact telomere structures.

Genome annotation

MITE-Hunter (v11-2011)57 was utilized to identify MITEs (Miniature Inverted-repeat Transposable Elements). LTRharvest (v1.6.5)58 and LTR_Finder (v1.07)59 were utilized to identify LTR retrotransposons, which comprise the largest proportion of the genome. LTR_retriever (v2.9.0)60 was employed to integrate the prediction results, yielding high-quality LTR sequences. RepeatModeler (v2.0.3)61 was employed for de novo annotation of the transposable elements. Finally, RepeatMasker (v4.0.9) was utilized to mask the identified repeat fragments by searching against the Repbase library. The tRNAscan-SE (v2.0.12)62 was utilized for the de novo annotation of tRNAs, while Rfam (v15.0)63 was used to annotate rRNA and other non-coding RNAs.

For homology-based prediction, protein-coding gene sequences from Arabidopsis thaliana, Nymphaea colorata, Oryza sativa, and Zea mays were aligned to the lotus genome sequences using Diamond (v2.1.8.162) with parameter “tblastn” by ProtHint (v2.6.0) (https://github.com/gatech-genemark/ProtHint#about). GeneMark (v4.71) with parameter “--EP”64 was then used to predict the precise gene structure of the corresponding genomic region for each BLAST hit. For transcript-based prediction, RNA-seq reads were aligned to the genome using Hisat2 (v2.2.1)65 and assembled into transcripts with StringTie (v2.2.3)66. The assembled transcripts were subsequently aligned to the lotus genome using the Program to Assemble Spliced Alignments (PASA) (v2.5.2)67 to support genome annotation. For ab initio gene prediction, three tools: AUGUSTUS (v3.5.0)68, GlimmerHMM (v3.0.4c)69, and GeneMark (v4.71) with parameter “--ET”70, were employed and trained using PASA-H-set gene models. Results from these three approaches, all gene models were integrated using EvidenceModeler (v2.1.0)71. Functional annotation of predicted proteins was assigned through Diamond (v2.1.8.162) with parameter “blastp” against the NCBI non-redundant protein (NR), Swiss-Prot, and eggNOG (v4.5). Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG annotations were performed using KOBAS 3.072.

Phylogenetic tree construction and divergence time estimation

O. sativa was selected as the outgroup for phylogenetic inference. Single-copy orthologous genes were identified using Orthofinder (v2.3.8)73 with parameters “-og”. In total, 23,129 orthologous groups were identified across the 7 genomes. Protein sequences of 1671 single-copy orthologous genes were aligned using MUSCLE (v3.7)74 and the resulting alignments were concatenated into a supermatrix. A phylogenetic tree of the 7 species was constructed using the maximum likelihood method implemented in RAxML (v8.2.12)75 with parameter “-m GTRGAMMA -# 100 -f ad”. Divergence times were estimated using the MCMCtree from the PAML package (v4.9j)76. Two calibration points (O. sativa versus N. nucifera: 142.1-163.5 Mya; N. nucifera versus V. vinifera: 121.5-130.2 Mya)77 were used to constrain the divergence time.

Identification of genetic variants and syntenic analysis

Pairwise genome alignments between NNU and NLU were performed using nucmer from the MUMmer package (v4.0.0rc1) with parameters “-c 100 -l 100”78. The resulting alignments were filtered to retain one-to-one alignment blocks using delta-filter, with parameters ‘-1 -i 90 -l 100’. Structural variants (SVs) were identified using SyRI (v1.6.3)79 with default settings. SVs including insertions, deletions, duplications, inversions, and translocations were retained ( < 1 Mb in size and > 50 bp in length for insertions, deletions, and duplications; > 50 bp for inversions and translocations). MCScanX (v1.0.0)80 was employed to identify collinear orthologs between NNU and NLU genomes, requiring a minimum of ten homologous genes to define a collinear block.

Whole-genome resequencing and variant calling

High molecular weight genomic DNA was extracted from leaf tissue using the CTAB method and fragmented randomly. DNA sequencing libraries were constructed following standard Illumina library preparation protocols, generating paired-end libraries with an insert size of 150 bp. All libraries were sequenced using the DNBSEQ-T7 platform.

Paired-end reads were aligned to the NNU T2T reference genome using BWA-MEM2 (v2.2.1) with the default parameters81. SAMtools (v1.17)82 was then used to convert SAM files into BAM format and to filter out unmapped or non-uniquely mapped reads. After sorting, PCR duplicates were removed using the ‘markdup’ function in Sambamba (v1.0.1) software83. For reads with identical mapping coordinates, only the read pair with the highest mapping quality was retained.

Variant calling was performed following the best practice workflow recommended by GATK (v4.5.0.0) software84. In brief, SNPs/InDels were identified for each sample using the HaplotypeCaller module and the GVCF model within the GATK software. A joint genotyping across all samples was performed with a combined GVCF file. Variant filtering was applied using the following criteria: QD < 2.0 | | MQ < 40.0 | | FS > 60.0 | | SOR > 3.0 || MQRankSum <12.5 || ReadPosRankSum <8.0. SNPs with a missing rate > 0.1, a read depth of <3, a minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.05, or GQ < 5 were excluded. The remaining high-quality SNPs were used for subsequent analysis. SNP annotation was conducted using the ANNOVAR package (Version: 2013-05-20) in accordance with the NNU T2T reference genome85.

Identification of rDNA arrays and centromere

Ribosomal DNA arrays were initially identified by BLAST searches using known repetitive sequences as queries, including: the 5S rRNA monomer (GenBank ID: U32085.1), 18S rRNA (GenBank ID: U42827.1), ITS1-5.8S rRNA-ITS2 region (GenBank ID: KC404827.1), and the 28S rRNA (GenBank ID: XR_005924313.1) against both genomes. The genomic distributions of 45S and 5S rDNA loci were then visualized using custom R scripts.

Candidate centromeric regions were predicted using quarTeT (v1.2.5) with parameters “CentroMiner --TE --gene”, using genome assemblies in FASTA format and transposable element (TE) annotations (GFF3)86. Active centromeres were identified using CenH3 ChIP-seq experiments following a published protocol87, with input DNA serving as a control. ChIP and input libraries were constructed using Illumina’s standard protocol and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq Xplus platform. Raw reads from ChIP and input samples were trimmed using fastp (v0.23.1)88 and aligned to the assembled NNU or NLU genome via BWA-MEM2 (v2.2.1)81 with default parameters. SAMtools (v1.17)82 was used to filter alignments (retaining reads with mapQ ≥ 30) and count aligned reads in 10-kb bins; read depth across all chromosomal positions was further calculated using the bedtools (v2.31.1). Signal normalization was performed relative to total mapped reads for each sample. Mean fold enrichment was calculated in 10-kb non-overlapping windows across each chromosome using custom scripts. CENH3 peaks were identified using MACS3 (v3.0.3)89 with parameters “-g NNU/NLU size -n chip --broad --broad-cutoff 0.1”. Final centromere positions and repeat compositions were visualized using custom R script.

ChIP-seq

Based on the amino acid sequence of the CenH3 protein (Chr03.g12637 for NNU, chr3.g14784 for NLU), corresponding peptides conjugated to a cysteine residue were synthesized. The peptides were further conjugated to keyhole limpet hemocyanin and used to generate polyclonal antibodies (dilution 1 mg/mL) at Nantong Yeastog Biotechnology (https://www.yeastog.com). The resulting antisera were affinity purified and used in subsequent ChIP experiments.

ChIP experiments were performed following a protocol provided by Nantong Yeastog Biotechnology. Fresh leaf tissue was harvested, cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde, and ground into a fine powder. Nuclei were isolated using NIB buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 9.5, 80 mM KCl, 10 mM EDTA pH 8.0, 0.5 M sucrose, 1 mM spermidine, 200 μL 1% Proteinase inhibitor). The isolated nuclei were sonicated to generate chromatin fragments of ~100–300 bp. Following sonication, chromatin solution was purified by centrifugation and equally divided into two fractions: one incubated with the antibody for ChIP and the other processed without antibody were used as the input control. DNAs from both ChIP and input samples were used for library construction according to Illumina protocols and sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq platform (San Diego, CA, USA).

Population structure and phylogenetic analysis

A total of 832 lotus accessions were collected from seven countries across Asia and North America: China, Japan, Thailand, India, Singapore, Cambodia, and the United States. These accessions represent geographically and genetically distinct populations distributed across diverse climatic zones. The country-specific sample sizes are as follows: 742 from China, 31 from the United States, 31 from Thailand, 20 from Japan, 6 from India, 1 from Singapore, and 1 from Cambodia. The three ecological types of Asian lotus were classified according to their latitudinal distribution and climatic regionalization of East Asia. Specifically, tropical lotus refers to accessions distributed below 23.5 °N within tropical zones; subtropical lotus includes materials ranging from 23.5 °N to 34 °N in continental East Asia, as well as germplasm from the warmer, ocean‑influenced regions of Japan where the subtropical monsoon climate prevails; temperate lotus is defined as germplasm collected from the temperate climatic areas north of the Qinling-Huaihe boundary (approximately 34 °N) in eastern China, which is characterized by a cold continental winter climate. Additionally, among the 832 accessions, 491 were classified as wild, 252 as cultivated, 78 as interspecific hybrids, and 11 as unknown, based on historical cultivation and introduction records (Supplementary Data 18).

To eliminate potential confounding effects arising from clonal or duplicated samples, we calculated pairwise identity-by-state (IBS) values using PLINK (v1.90b6.10)90 with the parameter “--distance square ibs”. To ensure sample independence, one individual was removed from each pair exhibiting an IBS = 1, and the remaining independent genotypes were retained for subsequent population structure analyses. High-quality SNPs (MAF ≥ 0.05, missing rate ≤ 0.1, sequencing depth ≥ 3) were selected for population structure analysis using VCFtools (v0.1.17)91. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) pruning was performed using PLINK (v1.90b6.10)90 with parameters “--indep-pairwise 40 kb 20 0.5”. A neighbor-joining tree was constructed based on pairwise genetic distances calculated from genome-wide SNPs using TreeBest (v1.9.2)92 with parameters “nj -b 1000”. The resulting tree was visualized using iTOL (https://itol.embl.de)93, and tree topology was assessed using 1000 bootstraps. PCA was performed using GCTA (v1.24.2)94 with parameters “--make-grm --thread-num 4 -autosome --autosome-num 8” and “--pca 3 --thread-num 4”, and the statistical significance of eigenvectors was evaluated using a Tracy-Widom test. Population structure was further inferred using the ADMIXTURE (v1.3.0)95, with number of ancestral populations (K) ranging from 2 to 10. Nucleotide diversity (π), FST, and ROH were calculated using VCFtools (v0.1.17)91. Heterozygosity and the inbreeding coefficients (F) value were estimated using PLINK (v1.90b6.10)90 with the parameter “--het”. LD decay was assessed using PopLDdecay (v3.41)96, with parameters “-MaxDist 500 -MAF 0.05 -Miss 0.1”. r² values were calculated pairwise between markers within a 500-kb window, averaged across the genome.

Demographic history reconstruction

Demographic histories of N. nucifera and N. lutea were inferred using the pairwise sequentially Markovian coalescent (PSMC) model97, covering a timescale of 10 million to 10,000 years ago. PSMC (v0.6.5-r67) analysis was conducted with parameters: “-N30 -t15 -r5 -p ‘4 + 25 * 2 + 4 + 6”. To further resolve recent demographic changes within the past 10,000 years, SMC + + (v1.15.2)98 was applied using default parameters. Effective population size estimates assumed a generation time (g) of 1 year and a mutation rate (μ) of 1.43×10-9 bases/generation10.

Gene flow analysis

To investigate the genetic basis of cultivar formation in ‘Qiumudan’ (QMD) and ‘Qiusanse’ (QSS), we conducted an identity-by-descent (IBD) analysis using SNP data from the following accessions: ‘Dongfanghong’ (DFH), ‘Donghe’ (DH), Thai accession (Ti), ‘Jinghong’ (JH), American lotus (NLU), ‘QMD’, and ‘QSS’. Genotypes were first imputed and phased using BEAGLE (v05May22.33a) to impute and phase the genotypes99. IBD segments were then identified using Refined IBD (v17Jan20.102) with parameters “window = 0.1, length = 0.02, lod = 3, trim = 0.001”100. The Patterson’s D and fb values were calculated using Dtrios and Fbranch implemented in Dsuite (v.0.5 r58)101 with parameters “Dtrios -c -t tree.nwk”. The “tree.nwk” file contained the following topology: (Outgroup,(wild,(ou,(hua,zi)))).

Selective sweep analysis

To detect genomic regions exhibiting significant signatures of selective sweep, both FST and nucleotide diversity ratio (log2π ratio (πnon_enlarged/πenlarged)) values were calculated using a 40-kb sliding window and shift of 20-kb across the genome, using VCFtools (v0.1.17)91,102 with parameters “--weir-fst-pop pop1.list --weir-fst-pop pop2.list --fst-window-size 40000 --fst-window-step 20000” and “--window-pi 40000 --window-pi-step 20000”. Considering that genomic regions under selection are expected to exhibit reduced levels of genetic variation, regions demonstrating either the top 5% of extreme differentiation in allele frequency or the top 5% of the highest differences in nucleotide diversity were identified as candidate selective sweeps. The genes located within or near these selective regions were classified as candidate genes. The XPCLR score were calculated using the XPCLR (v1.0) software103 with parameters “-xpclr pop1.ped pop2.ped -w1 0.005 200 2000 chr -p0 0.95” using 40 kb sliding windows that had a 20 kb overlap between adjacent windows. Regions within the top 5% of XPCLR scores were considered candidate selective regions, and genes within these regions were retained for further analysis.

Genome-wide association study analysis

GWAS was conducted using a mixed linear model (MLM) implemented in Genome-wide Efficient Mixed Model Association (GEMMA, v0.98.3)104 with parameters “-k xx -c xx -lmm 4 -miss 0.1 -maf 0.05”. High-quality SNPs (MAF ≥ 0.05, missing rate ≤ 0.1, and sequencing depth ≥ 3) were used for GWAS analysis. The statistical model ‘Y = Xα + Sβ + Kµ + e’ was applied, where Y denotes the phenotype, X denotes the genotype, S denotes the structural matrix, K denotes the relative kinship matrix, α and β denote fixed effects, Kµ denotes the random effect, and e denotes the residual error. The first three PCA were used to construct the S matrix using GCTA (v1.24.2)94 with parameters “--make-grm --thread-num 4 -autosome --autosome-num 8” and “--pca 3 --thread-num 4”, while the simple matching coefficient matrix was used to generate the K matrix with parameters “-gk 1 -miss 0.1 -maf 0.05”. Manhattan and quantile-quantile (Q-Q) plots of our GWAS results were visualized using the qqman and ggplot2 packages in R (v4.4.3). A genome-wide significance threshold of P < 1×10-6 (-log10(P)> 6) was used to identify significant associations105,106. For each significant SNP, a 40-kb region upstream and downstream was extracted to define the candidate genomic interval for gene annotation and downstream analyses.

Transcriptome data analysis

Public transcriptome datasets corresponding to rhizome development were retrieved from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive with accession number SRA271278. Low-quality reads and adapters were trimmed using fastp (v0.23.1)88. Retained reads were mapped to the reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.2.1) with default parameters65. Only uniquely mapped reads were retained for gene expression quantification, which was performed using StringTie (v2.2.3)66 with the parameter “-e”.

Boundary determination and functional characterization of NnIDD14 gene

Combining the precisely defined chromosomal physical boundaries of the NnIDD14 gene (Chr04: 39,877,825–39,880,589) and the results of three complementary analyses (GWAS, FST, and XPCLR), we preliminarily inferred that NnIDD14 acts as a candidate gene involved in rhizome enlargement (Supplementary Data 25).

Transient expression assays were performed by agroinfiltration of lotus rhizome sections as described below. The full-length CDS of NnIDD14 was inserted into the pMDC43 vector1 using Gateway system to obtain the pMDC43-NnIDD14, and the resultant construct was introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 via electroporation. Lotus rhizomes at the early enlargement stage from the ‘Xiangfen’ variety were harvested for agroinfiltration. Rhizomes were rinsed three times with sterile water, cut into 0.5 cm thick sections under sterile conditions, and immediately immersed in sterile water. A. tumefaciens cultures harboring the empty pMDC43 vector (control) or the pMDC43-NnIDD14 construct were resuspended in MES liquid medium supplemented with acetosyringone, and the OD600 was adjusted to 0.6. After removal of surface moisture, rhizome slices were immersed in the bacterial suspension for 30 min. Treated slices were cultured on MS solid medium supplemented with 3% glucose and sterile filter paper and incubated at 23 ± 1 °C. After 48–72 h of dark incubation, rhizome samples were collected for paraffin section preparation and total starch content determination. Total RNA was extracted using the Plant Total RNA Isolation Kit (ZP411C-1, Beijing Zoman Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) and cDNA was synthesized using the TransScript One-Step gDNA Removal and cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Lot#M31212, Beijing Trans-Gen Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The StepOnePlus Real-time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA) was used for qRT-PCR analysis. The NnACTIN (Gene ID Chr01.g02496) gene was used as the internal reference2. Primers for gene cloning and qRT-PCR were designed using Primer Premier 5.0 software and listed in Supplementary Data 32 (synthesized by AuGCT Biotech Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China). Total starch content was determined using the Starch Content Assay Kit (#BC0705, Solarbio, Beijing, China). Paraffin section images were captured using CaseViewer, and cell number as well as cell area were quantified via ImageJ software. All experiments were performed with three biological replicates. For cell area quantification, each biological replicate corresponded to the mean value from randomly measuring 30 individual cells. The pMDC43-NnIDD14 construct reported in this study is available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

PCR validation of small-scale inversions

To experimentally verify the reliability of the identified SVs, PCR-based validation was conducted on five small-scale inversions. Sense and anti-sense primers were designed across the flanking regions of SV breakpoints using Primer Premier 5.0 (Supplementary Data 32). Genomic DNA from NNU and NLU was used as templates for PCR amplification. The PCR thermal cycling protocol was set as follows: initial denaturation at 98 °C for 6 min, 35 cycles of 98 °C for 15 s, 56 °C for 15 s, and 72 °C for 6 s, followed by a terminal extension at 72 °C for 6 min. All PCR amplicons were further subjected to Sanger sequencing at AuGCT Biotech Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Subsequent sequence alignment and visualization were conducted using DNAMAN software.

Construction of the Nelumbo NMGP database

The NMGP database was developed using a robust and modular technological framework. The frontend was implemented using React 18, with React Router 6 enabling seamless navigation within the single-page application. Ant Design 5 served as the primary UI component library, with styling enhanced through Styled-components. Data visualization was implemented using ECharts, while Axios was used for efficient HTTP request management. The platform adopts a modular architecture to ensure both scalability and maintainability, with reusable components organized in the components directory, functional modules in the views directory, and static resources in the assets directory.

The database provides a comprehensive suite of tools tailored for lotus research, including user authentication, germplasm resource visualization, genome assembly and annotation, gene indexing, expression profiling, metabolomic and epigenomic exploitation, genomic selection modeling, JBrowse integration, BLAST searches, GO/KEGG enrichment analysis, primer design, heatmap visualization, and transcription factor analysis.

For backend-frontend communication, the platform employs http-proxy-middleware to securely proxy API requests. Axios interceptors are used for centralized response handling, error management, and response transformations, thereby improving reliability and maintainability. The interface adopts a cohesive green color scheme (#BDF7C0), providing a professional and scientifically oriented visual identity. The backend infrastructure is primarily built on the Django framework, extended with the Django REST Framework (DRF). MySQL serves as the database management system supported by optimized indexing strategies that improve query performance, particularly for large-scale genomic data, transcription factor analysis, and primer design. In addition, uWSGI enables asynchronous application execution, improving response speed and supporting high-concurrency access. Through a stable API interface, the platform integrates complex data processing with frontend visualization, enabling real-time data queries, dynamic updates, and interactive exploration of multi-omics datasets.
