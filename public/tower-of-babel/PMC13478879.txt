Nickel‐Based Anode Materials in Potassium‐Ion Batteries: A Review and Perspectives
Authors: Ziyi Liu, Jiachang Zhao, Yanqiu Lyu, Jianfeng Mao, Xigui Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Potassium‐ion batteries (PIBs) have emerged as one of the most promising alternatives to lithium‐ion batteries (LIBs) for energy storage, owing to their advantages such as abundant potassium resources and low cost. The design and optimization of anode materials are paramount, as they largely determine the overall electrochemical performance of PIBs. In recent years, nickel‐based compounds have attracted growing interest because of their high theoretical capacities and potentially favorable rate capability. However, the practical application of these materials as anode materials for PIBs faces major obstacles, stemming from pronounced volume expansion during the potassiation/depotassiation process and insufficient electronic conductivity during the cycling process. The latest progress of nickel‐based anode materials for PIBs (nickel oxides, nickel sulfides, nickel selenides, bimetallic selenides, nickel tellurides, nickel nitrides, and nickel phosphides) was systematically reviewed in this paper. The methods of enhancing the electrochemical performance of nickel‐based materials were summarized, with a focus on strategies such as compositing with carbon materials, designing nanostructures, and designing microstructures. Then, the application of these materials as anode materials in PIBs was discussed. Finally, the key challenges and future research directions for nickel‐based anode materials were presented.

Introduction

From the exploration, selection, and utilization of primary energy sources, to the conversion, transmission, and end‐use of secondary energy sources, and then to the iterative development of new energy systems dominated by renewable energy, the demand for high‐performance, high‐stability, and scalable energy storage systems is becoming increasingly urgent. As the core component of the electrochemical energy storage system, lithium‐ion batteries (LIBs) have been extensively used in numerous fields. However, the scarcity of lithium resources and their uneven geographical distribution pose challenges to its continuous ability to meet the demands for energy storage devices for lower costs, higher energy density, longer cycle life, and faster charging/discharging rates [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. Potassium‐ion batteries (PIBs) have emerged as one of the most promising alternatives to LIBs, owing to their advantages such as abundant potassium resources and low cost. The core mechanism of PIBs is as follows: During the charging and discharging cycles, potassium ions can reversibly intercalate and de‐intercalate between the cathode and anode materials, while electrons complete the directional migration through the external circuit. Through this collaborative process, chemical energy is efficiently converted into electrical energy. The kinetic characteristics of this process directly affect the quality of the electrochemical performance of PIBs. The solid‐state diffusion law of ions in the electrode materials can be summarized as the following formula: (1) τeq= L2/D

Based on the positive correlation between the mean diffusion time (τ eq) and the square of the diffusion path (L), and the negative correlation with the diffusion coefficient (D), the improvement of electrode kinetic performance can be achieved through the following two strategies: (1) introducing heteroatoms into the electrode material to enhance the potassium‐ion‐diffusion coefficient (D) and (2) nanostructuring the electrode material to construct efficient ion‐transport channels, thereby shortening the ion‐diffusion path (L).

The working principle and application potential of PIBs are presented (Figure 1). As one of the core alternative candidate systems in the post‐lithium‐ion battery era, the development of PIBs benefits from its outstanding economy and excellent intrinsic characteristics: First, the potassium resource reserves in the earth's crust are much higher than those of lithium, and the prices of potassium‐based raw materials such as K2CO3 and KCl are significantly lower than those of lithium‐based raw materials such as Li2CO3, laying a low‐cost material foundation for large‐scale application. Second, compared with the standard hydrogen electrode, the standard reduction potential of potassium (−2.936 V vs. SHE) is close to that of lithium (−3.04 V vs. SHE), which enables PIBs to have a higher energy density. Third, potassium has similar electrochemical performance to lithium, and the Lewis acidity of K+ is weaker, forming a smaller solvation structure in the electrolyte and presenting better ionic transport kinetics and rate capability. These advantages collectively highlight the significant application potential of PIBs in next‐generation large‐scale energy storage system [7]. It is worth noting that, due to the larger radius of potassium ions compared to lithium ions, the process of their intercalation/de‐intercalation is more likely to cause significant electrode volume expansion, which may lead to structural failure. At the same time, the larger ion radius will hinder diffusion within the solid phase, resulting in poor ion‐transport kinetics. For example, Jian et al. [8] found that the intercalation of potassium ions leads to a significantly greater expansion of the graphite lattice (about 61%) compared to the intercalation of lithium ions, with an expansion magnitude six times larger. The anode materials of PIBs can be classified into three types: intercalation‐type, alloy‐type, and conversion‐type. Intercalation‐type anode materials include graphite [9], porous carbon [10], and layered oxides [11]. Graphite stores K+ via reversible intercalation but suffers volume swelling and capacity decay; tuning interlayer spacing and oxygen groups can relieve this yet requires precise regulation [12]. Bi [13], Si [14], Sn [15], and Sb [16] are representative alloy‐type anode materials that form K–M alloys delivering high theoretical capacities yet undergoing severe volume strain exemplified by the 407% volume expansion of Sb during K3Sb generation, and carbon composite modification, surface coating, and compatible electrolyte selection are effective tactics to alleviate such strain [17, 18]. The currently verified improvement strategies include compositing with carbon, the formation of protective layer, and the selection of compatible electrolytes [19]. Conversion‐type anode materials contain metal‐based compounds (oxides, chalcogenides, phosphides, nitrides, etc.) [20, 21]. These metal‐based compounds store potassium via conversion into metallic nanoparticles and potassium species through multielectron transfer, delivering higher theoretical capacities than intercalation materials, yet suffer structural collapse and short cycle life due to recurrent formation of potassium compounds. Therefore, in the field of conversion‐type anode materials for PIBs, how to effectively mitigate the volume expansion during the cycling process while maintaining the structural integrity of the anode materials is a key issue that needs to be urgently addressed.

Energy density (E g, unit: Wh kg−1) is one of the key indicators for evaluating the overall performance of batteries. The theoretical gravimetric energy density contributed solely by cathode and anode active materials can be approximated by Equation (2):

(2) Eg=K⋅(Uc− Ua)⋅Qc⋅QaQc+Qa where U c and U a denote the average redox potentials of cathode and anode versus K/K+, respectively, U c − U a represents the average operating voltage of the batteries, and Q c and Q a are the gravimetric specific capacities (mAh g−1) of cathode and anode active materials. K is the unit conversion constant. The term Qc⋅Qa(Qc+Qa) describes the matched equivalent specific capacity of paired electrodes, which implies the total energy density is limited by the electrode with lower specific capacity. Therefore, capacity matching between cathode and anode is indispensable to maximize energy output, and thus pairing high‐voltage cathodes with robust low‐potential anodes stands as a core research focus. Simultaneously, strategies such as nanostructure design, composite modification, and interfacial engineering can address these issues like volume expansion, insufficient cycling stability. Electrode engineering that maximizes the utilization of potassium active material can efficiently translate the intrinsic properties of materials into practical battery performance, which is a critical step toward the commercialization of PIBs.

Nickel is a key member of the transition metals. Among numerous candidate anode materials, nickel‐based materials are widely regarded as one of the most promising alternative candidates in electrochemical energy storage devices due to their high electrochemical performance, abundant global reserves, and environmental friendliness. Nickel has a lower first‐ionization energy and higher electronegativity (737.1 kJ mol−1, 1.88) compared to Fe (762.5 kJ mol−1, 1.80) and Co (760.4 kJ mol−1, 1.84), which endows it with superior redox properties. Theoretical specific capacity serves as a crucial metric for evaluating anode materials. Using the formula C = nF/3.6M (where n is the number of electrons transferred, F is Faraday's constant, and M is the relative molecular mass), the theoretical specific capacities of several nickel‐based materials for PIBs, such as NiO, NiS, NiSe, and Ni2P, are calculated to be 717.48, 590.67, 389.41, and 541.95 mAh g−1, respectively. This advantage establishes a solid physical foundation, offering significant potential for advancing the application of nickel‐based electrodes in batteries. For example, Lai et al. [22] reported that porous spherical NiO/Ni particles (specific surface area: 125–240 m2 g−1) deliver exceptional electrochemical performance. The material maintained high capacity at a current density of 50 A g−1, with a capacity retention ratio of 97% after 10,000 cycles. This is attributed to two main factors: First, the high Ni content enhances conductivity by 4–5 orders of magnitude compared to pure NiO, thereby lowering charge‐transfer resistance, reducing polarization, and promoting electron transport; second, the embedded Ni nanoparticles help to maintain the structural integrity of the electrode during cycling. Furthermore, high reversible capacity is a key advantage of nickel‐based anode materials. Although conversion‐type nickel‐based anode materials may exhibit reversible electrochemical redox behavior, this does not necessarily imply complete reconstruction of the pristine crystalline phase. After the first potassiation, the electrode is likely to evolve into nanoscale Ni domains embedded in amorphous or poorly crystalline potassium‐containing products. During depotassiation, Ni—X bonding may be partially re‐established, while the original particle size, phase purity, and long‐range order may remain unrecovered. Therefore, structural reversibility should be claimed only when directly supported by operando or carefully designed ex situ characterization. Nickel, which serves as the redox center in various batteries, maintains reversible capacity even when charged to high cutoff voltages, thereby directly advancing its development as high‐performance anode materials for batteries. Nickel‐based anode materials are commonly described using an idealized conversion model. For a compound with the general formula Ni x X y , the overall reaction can be expressed as

NixXy+ 2yK+ + 2ye−⇌xNi + yK2X (X = O, S, Se…)

For nickel phosphides, the corresponding idealized reaction is

NixPy+ 3yK+ + 3ye−⇌xNi + yK3P

These equations describe the overall stoichiometry of complete conversion; experimentally observed pathways may include intermediate phases, partial conversion, amorphization, and interfacial potassium storage, and it scientifically demonstrates the potential of nickel‐based materials as anode materials for PIBs, clarifies the origins of their superior performance advantages as well as the root causes of their inherent limitations, and further provides guidance for more specific material design strategies. However, nickel‐based materials still face inherent limitations in practical applications. Specifically, when using single nickel‐based compounds (such as oxides, sulfides, phosphides, etc.) as anode materials, they often exhibit poor conductivity and significant volume expansion during cycling, which leads to unstable electrode structures or even structural collapse, limiting their electrochemical performance and practical application potential. To overcome these limitations, researchers have explored various modification strategies. Based on current reports, primary modification methods can be categorized as follows: (1) Compositing nickel‐based compounds with carbon materials (e.g., graphene, carbon nanotubes) enables the formation of nanoparticles, which can mitigate volume expansion during charge/discharge cycles, suppress particle agglomeration, and enhance electronic conductivity [23]; (2) design of three‐dimensional nanostructures. Design a microstructure with porous, hollow, and internal channels to shorten the ion‐diffusion path, facilitate mass transport, and alleviate volume expansion; and (3) the construction of rational heterostructures. Utilizing the interfacial synergistic effects to enhance the potassium‐storage performance. Thanks to the comprehensive application of the aforementioned strategies, the electrochemical performance of nickel‐based anode materials, such as specific capacity, rate capability, and cycling stability, has been significantly improved. These modification strategies not only broaden the application horizons of nickel‐based materials in energy storage but also lay a solid theoretical and practical foundation for their future design and optimization [24, 25, 26].

Nickel‐based compounds (NiX, such as oxides, sulfides, and phosphides) typically participate in electrochemical reactions as conversion‐type anode materials. NiX exhibit a typical conversion‐type reaction mechanism (Table 2). They are regarded as key candidates for next‐generation high‐performance anode materials for PIBs. The potassium‐storage mechanism in nickel–sulfur anode materials is primarily conversion‐type anode materials. Li et al. [29] clearly demonstrated the two‐step insertion–transformation potassium‐storage reaction of the nickel‐based sulfide NiS2 using testing methods such as cyclic voltammetry (Figure 4a,c). The cyclic voltammograms (CVs) of NiS2@C and NiS2@C@C were tested at 0.1 mV s−1 in the voltage range of 0.01–2.5 V. For NiS2@C, the reduction peak at 0.56 V in the first cycle indicates the presence of some unavoidably irreversible processes and the recurrent formation of solid electrolyte interface (SEI) film, which is in good agreement with the phenomenon of a decrease in current intensity at the reduced peaks in subsequent cycles. The reduction peak appeared at 2.19 V in the second cycle, reflecting the formation of the intercalation product K x NiS2. The peak at 0.91 V represents the conversion reaction generating Ni and K2S, similarly, the two homologous oxidation peaks correspond to the extraction reaction from Ni and K2S back to K x NiS2 at 1.92 V. The two pairs of redox peaks in the second cycle are almost completely overlapped with the peak positions in the next cycle, evincing outstanding electrochemical reversibility during cycling. NiS2@C@C electrodes show similar CV profiles, slight peak shift might be attributed to the change of microstructure and composition caused by the outer carbon coating layer.

Analogous to nickel sulfide systems, nickel selenides also operate via a conversion‐type potassium‐storage mechanism. For instance, Ye et al. [31] designed S,P co‐doped NiSe2 hollow carbon nanospheres (S,P‐NiSe2/HC). The “intercalation‐conversion” hybrid potassium‐storage mechanism was revealed through ex situ X‐ray diffraction (XRD) measurements at different charge/discharge states (0.477, 0.01, 1.995, 3.0 V) (Figure 4f): When discharged to 0.477 V, the diffraction peaks of NiSe2 weakened and shifted, indicating the intercalation of K+ to form K x NiSe2. Upon further discharging to 0.01 V, characteristic peaks of K2Se appeared, corresponding to the further conversion of K x NiSe2 into K2Se and Ni. When charged back to 3.0 V, the K2Se peaks disappeared and the NiSe2 peaks recovered, revealing highly reversible electrochemical redox behavior. The corresponding reaction equations are NiSe2 + xK+ + xe− → K x NiSe2 and K x NiSe2 + (4 − x)K+ + (4 − x)e− → 2K2Se + Ni. When employed as anode material for PIBs, it retains a high reversible capacity of 131.6 mAh g−1 after 3000 cycles at the current density of 1 A g−1. This exceptional electrochemical performance stems from the unique hollow nanostructure of the material, whose high specific surface area effectively mitigates volume expansion and shortens ion‐diffusion pathways. More importantly, density functional theory (DFT) calculations further reveal that doping S and P atoms into NiSe2 nanoparticles significantly enhances the intrinsic electrochemical activity of the material and provides strong adsorption capacity for K+.

As with the previous two systems, Yan et al. [30] demonstrated that the mechanism of potassium storage in the nickel–phosphorus system is still a conversion‐type mechanism. The cathodic peak at 1.0 V in the initial CV curve of Ni2P@NPC can be ascribed to some irreversible reactions that happened to form solid electrolyte SEI layer, and it fades away in the subsequent scans. The peak at 0.02 V could be ascribed to the intercalation of potassium ion into porous carbon to form KC8. For the anodic scan, the oxidation peak at 1.3 V is attributed to the de‐insertion of potassium ions (Figure 4d). For comparison, the CV curves of commercial pure Ni2P are shown (Figure 4e). In the first cycle of pure Ni2P, two cathodic peaks appear at 0.9 and 0.45 V. The peak at 0.9 V disappears after the first cycle (SEI formation), while the 0.45 V peak corresponds to the generation of K3P. Only one anodic peak at 1.45 V belongs to potassium extraction from K3P. After the second cycle, the CV curves of Ni2P@NPC overlap well with those of pure NPC, demonstrating excellent electrochemical reversibility. Similarly, to investigate the reaction mechanisms and changes in the internal structure of Ni2P@SCC during cycling. Zhang et al. [32] employed imaging techniques including in situ transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and high‐resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM). Compared to its as‐synthesized state, Ni2P@SCC retained good morphology during both the fully discharged stage (Figure 4g) and the charging stage (Figure 4i). During the fully discharged stage, d‐spacings of 2.75, 1.98, and 1.75 Å corresponded to the K3P(203), K3P (103), and Ni (200) crystal planes, respectively (Figure 4h), indicating that Ni2P had transformed into K3P and Ni. The carbon framework preserves the particle‐level morphology and confines the conversion products. The reappearance of the Ni2P (111) lattice fringe after charging provides local evidence for partial reconstruction of Ni2P (Figure 4j).

It should be noted that the conversion reactions summarized in Figure 3 and Table 2 represent idealized overall stoichiometric processes. For Ni‐based conversion‐type anodes, electrochemical reversibility, as reflected by recurring redox peaks or reversible capacity, should be distinguished from complete crystallographic recovery of the pristine phase. After deep potassiation, the electrode may contain nanosized Ni domains embedded in amorphous or poorly crystalline potassium‐containing products. During depotassiation, K+ extraction and partial re‐establishment of Ni‐X coordination may occur, whereas the original phase purity, long‐range order, and particle‐level structure may not be fully restored. Therefore, unless directly supported by operando or carefully designed ex situ structural evidence, the term “reversible conversion reaction” in this review refers primarily to electrochemical reversibility rather than complete regeneration of the original crystalline phase.

Ni–O

Nickel oxide (NiO) has become an important candidate anode material for PIBs due to its remarkable theoretical capacity of up to 717.48 mAh g−1. The potassium‐storage process of NiO in PIBs is a reversible conversion reaction, the overall reaction equation of which is as follows [33, 34, 35]:

NiO+2K++2e−↔Ni+K2O

However, its practical application is limited by inherent issues, namely low intrinsic electronic conductivity, severe volume expansion during cycling, sluggish ion migration, and unstable electrode–electrolyte interfaces. These challenges make it difficult to meet the requirements for high‐performance anode materials for PIBs. The carbon/graphene composite structures prepared via directed synthesis can form continuous electronic transport pathways, with their hollow internal cavities acting as a mechanical buffer layer that prevents structural collapse during cycling; the increased interlayer spacing lowers the migration energy barrier for potassium ions and exposes more reactive sites, thereby enhancing interfacial adsorption and redox kinetics. In order to solve such problems, Peng et al. [34] used 1D hollow NiO/C rods as the anode materials for PIBs, which demonstrated excellent electrochemical performance. At a current density of 0.1 A g−1, the discharge specific capacity of the 1D hollow NiO/C rod electrode retained a discharge capacity as high as 525 mAh g−1 after 45 cycles, which was 1.6 times that of the 1D hollow carbon rod and 24.0 times that of the pure NiO anode material. Moreover, at a high current density of 1.0 A g−1, after 1000 cycles, the 1D hollow NiO/C rod could still maintain a stable specific capacity of 119 mAh g−1. Similarly, Qiao et al. [33] reported for the first time a simple in situ growth strategy to synthesize pillared graphene nanosheets with expanded interlayer spacing as free‐standing flexible electrodes for PIBs. The expanded interlayer spacing was confirmed to accelerate ion transmission by kinetics. The graphene nanosheet/NiO‐500 (GS/NiO‐500) composite could be assembled into a binder‐free flexible anode, and this anode had ultrahigh reversible specific capacity. At a current density of 0.1 A g−1, its reversible specific capacity could reach 416.1 mAh g−1. Meanwhile, the anode exhibited excellent rate performance. At a current density of 1 A g−1, its specific capacity reached 305.6 mAh g−1. In addition, the anode showed superior capacity retention. After 3000 cycles at a current density of 1 A g−1, its capacity retention ratio could still reach 82.6%.

Currently, NiO has great application potential as anode material for PIBs, but the research remains relatively limited and faces two major challenges: insufficient exploration of its application and insufficient understanding of its storage mechanism (such as reaction kinetics of transformation and volume changes). However, the methods for controlling NiO synthesis are relatively limited, there is a lack of research on the quantitative relationships between synthesis conditions, structural evolution, and the mechanisms of performance degradation, making it difficult to achieve targeted and precise material design. Future research should reveal its reaction mechanism and the structure–property relationship during structural evolution, explore the electrode failure mechanism, and provide theoretical support for material design and optimization.

Ni–S

PIBs still lack stable anode materials; however, transition metal chalcogenides have shown potential due to their abundant resources, low cost, environmental friendliness, and high theoretical capacity. Among them, NiS2, Ni3S2, and NiS have been regarded as promising anode materials for PIBs because of their high theoretical capacity (such as 873 mAh g−1 for NiS2 and 590 mAh g−1 for NiS), low cost, and good reversibility. In particular, NiS outperforms other nickel sulfides and metal sulfides in terms of charge transfer and reactivity. However, the poor electrical conductivity of such materials (for instance, the electrical conductivity of Ni3S2 is 1.2 × 10−4 Ω cm−1), structural expansion during cycling and degradation of the conductive network, etc., have restricted their commercialization. Consequently, the research mainly focuses on developing sulfide/carbon composites, nanostructures, and mesoporous structures to overcome these obstacles [36, 37, 38, 39, 40]. The modification of nickel sulfides primarily employs two types of strategies that can be synthesized on a large scale: (1) combining hydrothermal/in situ sulfidation with carbon coating (reduced graphene oxide [RGO], carbon nanotubes, porous carbon, and nitrogen‐doped carbon) to prepare core–shell, yolk–shell, and three‐dimensional hierarchical carbon–sulfide composite structures and (2) constructing bimetallic heterostructures through interfacial engineering. Synthesis parameters such as sulfidation temperature, surfactant dosage, and etching time can precisely control particle size, pore volume, carbon‐layer thickness, and heterojunction contact area. By adjusting the synthesis parameters to match the carbon pore size with the sulfide particle size, the utilization of active materials can be maximized; the construction of heterostructures serves as an efficient and complementary strategy for modifying carbon composites, enabling the simultaneous optimization of both electronic conductivity and ionic transport kinetics.

NiS2

Pure metal sulfides such as nickel disulfide (NiS2) have poor conductivity and significant volume expansion. By combining them with carbon materials such as carbon nanotubes and rGO, a three‐dimensional “electronic highway” can be constructed, enabling rapid electron transmission. At the same time, the buffering space can suppress volume expansion and ensure structural integrity. And the huge specific surface area also promotes efficient ionic transmission. This strategy solves the three major problems: conductivity, volume expansion, and ionic transmission at one stroke.

Li et al. [29] first fabricated nitrogen/carbon dual‐doped hierarchical NiS2(NiS2@C@C) as electrode material for PIBs, whose electrochemical performance was markedly superior to that of single‐carbon modified NiS2@C, with a reversible capacity of 302.7 mAh g−1 after 100 cycles at 0.05 A g−1 in contrast to 88.2 mAh g−1 for NiS2@C and a retained capacity of 116.9 mAh g−1 after 200 cycles at 0.5 A g−1 versus 32.1 mAh g−1 for NiS2@C. The dual carbon layer buffers volume expansion, suppresses structural collapse, and improves cycling stability, and the initial Coulombic efficiency (ICE) of NiS2@C@C (78.6%) also exceeds that of NiS2@C (77.8%). This pioneering work provides a strategy for boosting the performance of NiS2‐based material and drives further research on NiS2/carbon composite. In another similar work, Han et al. [41] prepared NiS2/3DGO anode via in situ vulcanization and self‐assembly, achieving both a high reversible capacity of 391 mAh g−1 and outstanding rate capability (stable cycling at 1 A g−1). Li et al. [42] prepared RGO‐supported nickel disulfide nanocrystals (denoted as c‐NiS2/rGO) with strong chemical Ni–O interactions via an interfacial engineering strategy and employed them as anode materials for PIBs. Electrochemical performance tests revealed that the c‐NiS2/rGO anode exhibited an excellent reversible capacity of 215.8 mAh g−1 even at a high current density of 2 A g−1. Zheng et al. [43] successfully synthesized nickel disulfide/rGO (NiS2/rGO) nanocomposites using polyethylene oxide–polypropylene oxide–polyethylene oxide (PEO–PPO–PEO) as a surfactant via a simple hydrothermal method. When employed as anode material in PIBs, it maintained an excellent reversible capacity of 320 mAh g−1 after 100 cycles at a current density of 0.05 A g−1. Chen et al. [44] synthesized a reinforced concrete‐structured nickel disulfide/carbon nanotube (NiS2/CNTs) hybrid material. When employed as anode material in PIBs, the NiS2/CNTs hybrid exhibited outstanding rate capability (reversible capacity of 469.4 mAh g−1 at 0.05 A g−1 and 250.8 mAh g−1 at 2 A g−1). It also demonstrated outstanding cycling stability (reversible capacity retention of 353.3 mAh g−1 after 200 cycles at 0.1 A g−1 current density, with a capacity retention rate of 81.2%), which was seven times that of pure NiS2. Gan et al. [45] employed an interfacial engineering strategy to encapsulate core–shell structured NiS2@C nanoparticles within MOF‐derived hollow carbon shells, constructing a yolk–shell structured NiS2@C@C composite for use as anode material for PIBs. This material integrates the synergistic advantages of internal buffer pores, an outer protective carbon shell and a highly conductive carbon layer, thereby exhibiting exceptional electrochemical performance, with a stable rate capacity of 306 mAh g−1 maintained even at an ultra‐high current density of 20 A g−1 and a capacity of 358 mAh g−1 retained after 1600 cycles at 1 A g−1. The aforementioned research focuses on NiS2/carbon composite anodes for PIBs. By adopting a carbon‐based composite strategy, the electrical conductivity and structural stability of NiS2 were enhanced, partially addressing the core challenges hindering the application of conversion‐type metal sulfides and providing a feasible solution for the development of high‐capacity long‐cycle anode materials. In the future, it is necessary to simultaneously optimize the three major indicators: capacity, rate capability and cycling stability to avoid the limitation of a single performance and carry out research on the compatibility of the cathode of the full battery to verify its practical application potential.

Ni3S2

Trinickel disulfide (Ni3S2) as electrode material with high theoretical capacity, exhibits a conversion reaction mechanism based on multielectron transfer, enabling a significantly higher theoretical capacity compared to conventional graphite anodes (~279 mAh g−1). After modification, its capacity can be increased to 300–765 mAh g−1. Furthermore, Ni3S2 exhibits a low electrical resistivity (approximately 1.2 × 10−4 Ω cm) demonstrating superior electronic conductivity compared to most transition metal sulfides (TMSs). The construction of heterostructures or hollow/mesoporous architectures can effectively mitigate volume expansion during K+ insertion/extraction, thereby enhancing structural stability. Moreover, the abundant reserves of Ni and S elements in the Earth's crust and their low raw material costs provide a solid material foundation for further modification research and practical applications. The potassium‐storage mechanism of Ni3S2 anode material for PIBs is dominated by conversion reactions, accompanied by significant pseudocapacitive behavior. The specific electrochemical process can be divided into the following stages: During the initial discharge stage, K+ ions first intercalate into the interstitial spaces of the Ni3S2 lattice to form an intermediate phase, effectively lowering the energy barrier for subsequent reactions. As K+ continues to insert, Ni3S2 undergoes a complete conversion reaction, decomposing into metallic Ni and K2S, accompanied by a multielectron transfer process. During charging, K+ extraction and oxidation of metallic Ni may partially re‐establish Ni–S coordination, producing an electrochemically reversible response. Complete crystallographic regeneration of Ni3S2 should not be assumed without direct structural evidence. In addition, the multielectron conversion between Ni–S species and metallic Ni/K2S underpins the high theoretical capacity. The fundamental conversion reaction can be expressed as follows [46, 47, 48, 49]:

Ni3S2+4K++4e−↔ 3Ni+2K2S

Research indicates that the construction of Ni3S2/carbon composites is an effective strategy for the enhancement of their electrochemical performance. Carbon materials (such as rGO and carbon nanotubes) can form continuous conductive networks, significantly reducing the charge‐transfer resistance of electrodes and compensating for the inherent electronic conductivity deficiency of Ni3S2. Simultaneously, the elastic framework and porous structure of the carbon layer which provide effective buffer space for volume changes in Ni3S2 during cycling suppress the agglomeration of active material and structural fragmentation of electrode, thereby comprehensively enhancing cycling stability. Sadan et al. [50] reported the application of mesoporous Ni3S2 as anode material for PIBs. The XRD results are displayed (Figure 5a). TEM images reveal the corresponding microstructure features (Figure 5b,c). Ni3S2 particles are uniformly dispersed within the carbon matrix, with particle sizes below 50 nm. Their lattice spacings are 0.23 and 0.28 nm, corresponding to the (021) and (110) crystal planes, respectively. When employed as anode material for PIBs, this mesoporous Ni3S2 exhibits outstanding rate capability and cycling stability. At current densities of 1 A g−1 and 100 A g−1, the anode delivers specific discharge capacities of 534 mAh g−1 and 38 mAh g−1, respectively, and it maintains an initial capacity of 411 mAh g−1 after 1000 cycles at 10 A g−1 with an ultra‐low capacity decay rate of only 0.047%, which reflects outstanding long‐cycle durability (Figure 5d,e). Sun et al. [52] employed a spatially confined“edge‐to‐edge”strategy to synthesize a heterogeneous Ni–Ni3S2@SC composite (comprising Ni–Ni3S2 nanoparticles and sulfur‐doped carbon matrix) for use as anode material for PIBs. This material exhibits exceptional rate capability, long‐term cycling stability, and volumetric capacity, which is attributed to the sulfur‐doped carbon matrix that provides efficient electron‐transport pathways and robust structural reinforcement. The corresponding PIB delivered a specific capacity of 197 mAh g−1 at a current density of 2 A g−1, retaining 86.2% of its initial capacity after 100 cycles and achieving a high volumetric capacity of 850 mAh cm−3 at 0.05 A g−1.

Beyond compositing with carbon materials, the construction of well‐designed Ni3S2‐based heterostructures represents another key strategy for the enhancement of their electrochemical performance. K+ first intercalates into interlayer or interstitial voids within the heterostructure, forming intermediate compound. As the potential decreases, the intercalated K+ drives redox reactions in the metal sulfides decomposing them into metallic elements (Ni) and alkali metal sulfides (K2S). For example, Yu et al. [51] synthesized a novel Ni3S2–Ni hybrid nanosphere structure with an internal cavity, encapsulated by a nitrogen‐doped carbon shell (denoted as Ni3S2‐Ni@NC‐AE), and it delivers outstanding specific capacity of 438 mAh g−1 after 150 cycles at 0.1 A g−1, favorable rate capability, and ultra‐stable long cycling performance with a residual capacity of 176.4 mAh g−1 after 5000 cycles at 1 A g−1 (Figure 5f,h).

NiS

When NiS is composited with carbon materials such as graphene, boron‐doped carbon, or biomass‐derived carbon, its potassium‐storage behavior is mainly governed by a conversion‐type reaction. During discharge, K+ participates in the conversion of NiS, accompanied by the reduction of Ni2+ to metallic Ni and the formation of K2S. During the subsequent charge process, K+ is extracted from potassium sulfide species and Ni–S coordination may be partially re‐established, giving rise to a reversible electrochemical response. In the absence of direct structural evidence, this process should be described as electrochemical reversibility of the conversion reaction, rather than complete regeneration of crystalline NiS after cycling. The reversible reaction can be expressed by the following idealized overall equation [53, 54]:

NiS+2K++2e−↔ Ni+K2S

Carbon materials, with their high electrical conductivity, large specific surface area, and excellent structural flexibility, mainly play an auxiliary role in the composite system and do not participate in the core potassium‐storage reaction. Their synergistic effects are manifested in three key aspects: First, a continuous electron transmission channel is constructed to significantly enhance the electrical conductivity of NiS, effectively addressing the bottleneck of its inherent low electrical conductivity. Second, through a porous structure or coating structure, the volume expansion generated by NiS during the conversion reaction is buffered, preventing the structural collapse and particle agglomeration of the electrode material. Finally, the abundant pore structure can shorten the diffusion path of K+, increase the ion migration rate, thereby ensuring the cycling stability and rate capability of the electrode material, and ultimately compensating for the inherent defects of NiS, such as poor conductivity and significant volume expansion during cycling.

To address the intrinsic drawbacks of pristine NiS anodes including inferior electronic conductivity and drastic volume fluctuation during repeated potassiation/depotassiation, researchers have extensively adopted carbon matrix confinement and rational nanostructure engineering strategies. A series of elaborate carbon‐modified NiS composites with diverse microarchitectures have been fabricated to synchronously construct continuous conductive networks, reserve buffer voids for volume variation, and expose abundant electroactive sites, whose structural design and electrochemical behaviors are elaborated as follows. Zhao et al. [55] successfully synthesized a unique box‐shaped NiS@C composite. The NiS particles were encapsulated within nitrogen‐doped carbon cages through Ni—N bonds exhibiting outstanding potassium ion storage performance (a stable specific capacity of 171 mAh g−1 was retained after 300 cycles at a current density of 1 A g−1). Yang et al. [56] developed a unique yolk–shell structured nickel–iron bimetallic sulfide nanosheets@carbon nanospheres (NFS@C) composite and employed it as a high‐performance anode material for PIBs. Electrochemical characterization reveals that the NFS@C yolk–shell nanospheres exhibit exceptional potassium‐storage performance, retaining a specific capacity of 297 mAh g−1 after 250 cycles at a current density of 0.1 A g−1 and delivering a capacity of 52 mAh g−1 even at an elevated current density of 7.0 A g−1, thus demonstrating superior cycling stability and rate capability. This meticulously designed structure offers multiple advantages, not only providing sufficient void space to accommodate the drastic volume expansion of nickel iron sulfide during cycling but also constructing abundant ion‐diffusion pathways to form a continuous, stable conductive network across the entire electrode. Xu et al. [57] prepared a composite material (h‐NiS@N‐C) with nickel sulfide nanosheets loaded onto the inner surface of the nitrogen‐doped carbon matrix channels. This structure can provide more exposed active sites for the electrochemical reactions in PIBs. Electrochemical test results show h‐NiS@N‐C achieves a reversible specific capacity of 500 mAh g−1 after 200 cycles at 0.1 A g−1 (Figure 6a,b). Even at a high current density of 1 A g−1, the composite demonstrated excellent long‐term cycling stability, maintaining a specific capacity of 260 mAh g−1 after 1000 cycles. Wei et al. [58] prepared an asphalt‐based activated carbon fiber composite (ACF/NiS) loaded with nickel sulfide nanoparticles. The flowchart and internal structure of ACF, CF/NiO, ACF/NiO, and ACF/NiS are displayed (Figure 6c). EDS mapping proves metal nanoparticles disperse evenly on the ACF/NiS surface without evident agglomeration (Figure 6e). Its hierarchical pore structure and abundant active sites enhance the Coulombic efficiency and capacity of PIBs. This material exhibits a specific surface area of 1552 m2 g−1, with mesopores accounting for 38% of the pore volume. At a current density of 0.5 A g−1, this electrode delivers an ICE of 84.22% and a specific capacity of 292.5 mAh g−1, with a capacity retention ratio of 95.7% maintained after 200 cycles (Figure 6d).

The above research work indicates that NiS‐based composites with box‐like structure, yolk–shell structure, and porous hollow carbon sphere composite structure all exhibit excellent potassium‐storage performance. This finding provides crucial insights for subsequent research: First, the core strategy should focus on “the morphology regulation of NiS and structure matching of carbon material.” For instance, precisely regulating the compatibility between the pore size of carbon materials and the particle size of NiS particles to improve the utilization of active sites. Second, when carbon materials doped with the elements such as nitrogen and phosphorus were combined with NiS, the conductive property of the electrode and the ion adsorption capacity can be simultaneously enhanced. These efforts are of great significance in promoting the application of NiS‐based materials in PIBs. However, the interfacial charge‐transfer mechanism between NiS and carbon materials still requires further clarification. Future work should combine in situ characterization techniques with DFT simulations to accurately analyze the ion‐transport pathways and the interaction rules at the interface. Furthermore, research on the assembly of full‐cell should be strengthened to optimize key parameters such as the formulation of electrolyte and the compaction density of electrode, thereby accelerating the translation of NiS/carbon composites from laboratory research into practical energy storage devices.

Ni–Se

In the anode materials of PIBs, transition metal selenides (TMSes) such as NiSe2, NiSe, and Ni3Se4 demonstrate significant advantages over transition metal oxides (TMOs) and TMSs. Compared to TMOs and TMSs, certain TMSes exhibit pronounced benefits when used as anode materials in PIBs. Selenium (with an electronegativity of 2.55 and a larger atomic radius) enhances the delocalized charge effect of Se2−, endowing TMSes with superior conductivity, tunable d‐orbital electron configurations (providing abundant adsorption sites for K+) and a high theoretical specific capacity dominated by conversion reactions. However, practical application of TMSes faces bottlenecks: The insertion/extraction of K+ (ionic radius 1.38 Å) readily causes lattice distortion and structural damage in single‐phase TMSes. Some nickel‐based selenides exhibit volume expansion ratios exceeding 200%, which aggravates particle agglomeration and pulverization as well as the damage and reconstruction of the SEI film, deteriorating the reaction kinetics and restricting the capacity release. The mainstream optimization strategies include (1) compositing with carbon materials (e.g., graphene) to enhance conductivity and antiexpansion properties. (2) Designing hierarchical porous structures to shorten the diffusion path of K+ and optimize kinetics. (3) Constructing heterostructures (e.g., Ni3Se4/CoSe2, NiSe2/CoSe2) to reduce charge‐transfer resistance and enhance stability via built‐in electric field [59]. The “carbon‐functionalized composite/heterointerface engineering” synthesis framework established for the Ni–Se system can be extended to the design of other transition metal chalcogenide anodes; subsequent synthesis optimization must be carried out in tandem with the selection of an appropriate electrolyte system to form a stable SEI layer, and full‐cell validation should be conducted to advance practical application.

NiSe2

Nickel selenide (NiSe2), as a highly promising anode material for PIBs, has a narrow bandgap energy (resistivity is less than 10−3 Ω cm) that endows the material with high electrical conductivity. Moreover, NiSe2 generally adopts a cubic pyrite‐type structure containing Se–Se dimers. As anode material for PIBs, NiSe2 achieves a theoretical specific capacity of up to 495 mAh g−1 through a multielectron conversion‐reaction mechanism [60]. Furthermore, NiSe2 can enhance conversion‐reaction kinetics due to weaker metal—selenium (M—Se) bonds and significantly higher intrinsic electrical conductivity compared to metal oxides. The aforementioned outstanding electrochemical performance is the result of the synergistic contribution of high conductivity, crystal structure stability, and pseudocapacitive behavior [31, 61, 62]. When NiSe2 is used as an anode material, K+ insertion at the early discharge stage may be followed by conversion reactions involving the reduction of Ni species and the formation of potassium selenides. During the charge process, K+ extraction from potassium–selenium species is accompanied by oxidation of metallic Ni and partial recovery of Ni–Se coordination. Such behavior supports the electrochemical reversibility of the NiSe2‐based electrode. However, unless the recovery of the pristine NiSe2 crystalline phase is directly verified by operando or carefully designed ex situ structural characterizations, reversible capacity or the reappearance of selected structural signals should not be taken as proof of complete phase regeneration after cycling. The overall charge–discharge reaction of NiSe2 can be described as follows, depending on the reported potassium–selenide products:

2NiSe2+4K++4e−↔ 2Ni+K2Se+K2Se3

To address the kinetic bottleneck of NiSe2 anode materials in PIBs, Chu et al. [62] designed and fabricated a coral‐like NiSe2/graphene composite electrode (NSG) and first applied it as anode material for PIBs. The graphene network not only enhanced the electronic conductivity but also physically constrained the volume expansion, thereby endowing the material with excellent potassium‐storage performance. The reversible specific capacity of NSG remained as high as 522 mAh g−1 after 50 cycles at 0.05 A g−1, significantly outperforming the previously reported selenide‐based anode materials. Furthermore, the construction of heterostructures also serves as an effective approach to address the problems of electron/ion kinetic retardation in the anode materials for PIBs. For instance, Gong et al. [63] designed a multiinterface regulated nickel–iron bimetallic selenide (NiSe2/FeSe2, abbreviated as NFS). The NFS heterostructure comprises two selenides with different band structures, enabling the formation of a built‐in electric field that not only accelerates the interfacial electron transport but also reduces the resistance of ionic diffusion. Thanks to the aforementioned structural advantages, the fabricated NFS@NC@C composite exhibits outstanding potassium‐storage performance, delivering an initial specific capacity of 349.6 mAh g−1 at a current density of 0.1 A g−1, along with excellent rate capability and maintaining a remarkably low capacity fading rate of 0.021% per cycle after 1200 cycles at a high current density of 5 A g−1. Similarly, Yang et al. [64] synthesized a CoSe2@NiSe2 cross‐stacked heterostructure material. Corresponding HRTEM characterizations are provided (Figure 7a,b). Its unique heterostructure synergizes with the carbon coating to afford more ion adsorption sites, reduce diffusion energy barriers, and boost rapid potassium‐ion‐diffusion kinetics, thus exerting the aforementioned positive effects. When employed as a potassium‐ion battery anode, this material exhibits exceptional cycling stability (Figure 7c,d), retaining a specific capacity of 160 mAh g−1 after 700 cycles at a current density of 1.0 A g−1. Heterogeneous bimetallic selenides have significant advantages over single‐metal selenides. The adjustable composition of the components can accelerate ion transport, and the synergistic effect can inhibit the agglomeration of active substances and alleviate volume changes. The rich interface can integrate multiple component characteristics and produce synergistic effects, which is an effective way to optimize electrode performance, it can accelerate charge‐transfer kinetics, reduce surface reaction energy barriers, and ion‐diffusion resistance [65, 66, 67].

NiSe

Nickel selenide (NiSe) is an important member of nickel chalcogenides. It possesses the advantages of high intrinsic conductivity (1 × 10−3 S m−1), low electronegativity (2.5 on the Pauling electronegativity scale), hexagonal NiAs‐type structure, high safety, and high active utilization rate, making it a highly promising anode material for PIBs. However, when large‐sized K+ ions are intercalated/de‐intercalated, its insufficient conductivity and volume expansion significantly degrade high‐rate capability and long‐term cycling stability. The carbon‐coated or carbon‐loaded NiSe nanostructures are a widely validated effective solution strategy, which can optimize the electrochemical performance by enhancing electron conduction, shortening ion‐diffusion paths, and inhibiting particle agglomeration. The potassium storage in NiSe follows a conversion reaction mechanism. Ex situ TEM confirmed that the potassiation process of it is stable. During discharge, Ni2+ is reduced to elemental Ni, and Se2− combines with K+ to form potassium–selenide compounds. During charging, K+ is extracted from potassium selenide species, accompanied by oxidation of metallic Ni and partial reconstruction of Ni–Se coordination. This reversible redox process contributes to the cycling performance of NiSe‐based electrodes, while complete regeneration of the pristine NiSe phase requires direct structural verification. Based on the reaction rules of nickel‐based selenides, the charge–discharge reaction equations can be categorized as follow [68, 69, 70]:

NiSe + 2K++2e−↔ Ni + K2Se

Hussain et al. [71] reported a novel nitrogen‐doped carbon‐coated nickel selenide uniform nanoparticles (denoted as “NiSe@NC”), which was used as anode material for PIBs. The synthetic route and characterization data of NiSe@NC are displayed (Figure 8a). This material exhibits exceptional rate capability and cycling stability (Figure 8b,c), NiSe@NC maintained a specific capacity of 334 mAh g−1 after 100 charge–discharge cycles with a nearly 100% Coulombic efficiency. When the current density was increased stepwise from 100 to 200, 500, 1000, 2000, and 5000 mAh g−1, the reversible specific capacities of NiSe@NC achieved were 510, 466, 403, 354, 314, and 267 mAh g−1, respectively. Notably, a specific capacity of 494 mAh g−1 was recovered when the current density was restored to 100 mAh g−1 after 72 cycles, corresponding to 97% of its initial specific capacity at 100 mAh g−1. Mao et al. [72] constructed a three‐dimensional nickel selenide/carbon/rGO composite system (NiSe@C/rGO), achieving efficient regulation of NiSe electrode performance: The nanoscale NiSe active units can significantly shorten the diffusion distance of K+ to optimize the kinetics, and the amorphous carbon‐coating layer can effectively buffer volume deformation and inhibit side reactions between the electrode and electrolyte, while the rGO network further enhances the electronic conductivity and maintains the integrity of the electrode structure. The multicomponent synergy mentioned above endows NiSe@C/rGO with exceptional potassium‐storage performance, as evidenced by its superior rate capability with a reversible specific capacity of 160 mAh g−1 retained even at a high current density of 10 A g−1 and excellent cycling stability with a specific capacity of 301 mAh g−1 maintained after 700 cycles at 0.2 A g−1.

In addition to the construction of carbon‐coated materials, component regulation has also become an effective strategy for improving the potassium‐storage performance of metal compounds electrodes. Among them, the development of heterostructured composites is particularly important [73]. Yan et al. [74] innovatively encapsulated ZnSe/NiSe heterostructure hollow microspheres uniformly within nitrogen‐doped carbon nanofibers (ZnSe/NiSe@NC) and applied them to the anode materials for PIBs. The ZnSe/NiSe@NC electrode exhibits remarkable advantages in both rate capability and cycling stability, retaining 456.3 mAh g−1 (with a retention ratio of 90%) after 100 cycles at 0.1 A g−1, delivering 425.9 mAh g−1 (with a retention ratio of 92%) after 500 cycles at 0.5 A g−1, and cycling stably for 1500 cycles at a high current density of 2.0 A g−1 to retain a capacity of 305.6 mAh g−1 with a negligible per‐cycle capacity decay rate of only 0.011%. Through precisely tuned chemical composition and microstructure, this material exhibits outstanding reversible capacity, exceptional rate capability, and long‐term cycling stability.

In the future, such research can be combined with the surface chemical properties of NiSe/carbon composites, and an appropriate electrolyte system can be designed (such as adding fluorinated carbonate additives and using high‐concentration electrolytes). Through the interaction between the electrolyte components and the electrode surface, a more stable SEI film can be constructed, improving the high‐temperature cycling stability and safety of the batteries.

Ni3Se4

As a promising anode material, the potassium‐storage performance of trinickel tetraselenide (Ni3Se4) was frequently improved by the fabrication of heterostructures (e.g., Ni3Se4/CoSe2) or carbon composites. For Ni3Se4, potassium storage is generally described by a conversion‐type mechanism. During discharge, K+ insertion and subsequent conversion reactions can break Ni—Se bonds, reduce Ni species to metallic Ni, and generate K2Se. During the reverse charge process, K+ extraction from K2Se and partial re‐establishment of Ni–Se coordination may occur, leading to a reversible electrochemical response. However, the currently available evidence should not be overinterpreted as complete recovery of the original Ni3Se4 crystal structure after cycling. The reaction equation should therefore be regarded as an idealized overall stoichiometric representation:

Ni3Se4+8K++8e−↔3Ni+4K2Se

The electrochemical performance of Ni3Se4 anode material for PIBs can be effectively enhanced through rational heterostructure design. For instance, Zhang et al. [75] prepared a carbon‐shell‐coated Ni3Se4/CoSe2 heterostructure nanosphere composite (Ni3Se4/CoSe2@C). The core mechanism for its outstanding cycling performance lies in that the carbon coating layer can maintain the structural stability of the material and enhance the electron conduction efficiency. More importantly the heterostructure can effectively promote the ion/electron transport. This Ni3Se4/CoSe2@C composite exhibited exceptional cycling performance, retaining a specific capacity of 330.1 mAh g−1 after 200 cycles at a current density of 0.1 A g−1 when employed as anode material for PIBs.

Bimetallic Selenides

Compositional coupling and heterointerface formation in bimetallic selenides can induce lattice strain, electronic redistribution, and additional active interfaces [76, 77]. Benefiting from the synergistic effects of bimetallic components and precise structural design, the prepared anode materials demonstrate comprehensive advantages over single‐metal selenides in core electrochemical performance indicators, including reversible specific capacity, rate capability, and long‐term cycling stability. This modification strategy has become an efficient route to optimize anode performance and is widely adopted in energy storage material research.

Considering how to address the volume expansion caused by the insertion/extraction of potassium ions, the design of a reasonable and precise hollow structure has been proven to be a feasible solution. Hussain et al. [78] employed a template‐free solvothermal strategy to synthesize hierarchical carbon‐composite porous doughnut‐shaped Fe3Ni2Se4@C (NFS@C) via the Kirkendall effect (Figure 9b). The conversion mechanism of the material is elaborated (Figure 9a). The reaction mechanism can be summarized as the following two steps: Fe3Ni2Se4 + xK+ + xe− ↔ K x Fe3Ni2Se4 and K x Fe3Ni2Se4 + (8 − x)K+ + (8 − x)e− ↔ 4K2Se + 3Fe + 2Ni. Its high specific surface area and short charge diffusion path can accelerate the redox kinetics and enhance the rate capability and cycling stability, effectively breaking through the performance limitations of traditional electrode materials. Furthermore, the material exhibited excellent electrochemical performance. It delivered a reversible capacity ranging from 405 to 133 mAh g−1 at current densities of 0.1–10 A g−1. Notably, when the current density was switched back to 5 and 0.1 A g−1, the capacity recovered to 185 and 411 mAh g−1, respectively, demonstrating its superior high‐rate stability (Figure 9c). This material provides an effective reference for the application of bimetallic selenides in PIBs and energy storage devices. Similarly, it can also be used to address the problems of slow potassium‐ion‐transport kinetics, poor charge‐transfer ability and short cycle life of the anode composed of transition metal nickel selenide. Huang et al. [79] synthesized a dice‐shaped nitrogen‐doped carbon‐coated nickel cobalt selenide (NiCo2Se4@NC) nanocomposites, achieving highly efficient and stable potassium storage. The nitrogen‐doped carbon shell constructs a highly conductive framework that accelerates K+ transfer and suppresses volume collapse, while the hetero‐metal components form dual hetero‐interfaces of Ni‐Se, Co‐Se, and carbon, enhancing the K+ migration rates, improving the interfacial reversibility and electrochemical kinetics and demonstrating that the dual‐metal synergistic effects can optimize the theoretical specific capacity. This material exhibits excellent electrochemical performance, delivering a capacity of 471.9 mAh g−1 at 2 A g−1 to demonstrate superior rate capability and retaining a capacity of 454.5 mAh g−1 after 1000 cycles at the same current density, thus showcasing excellent cycling stability.

Ni–P

The inexpensive and readily available nickel phosphides (transition metal phosphides) have been regarded as alternative anode materials for PIBs due to their low intercalation potential, high volumetric capacity, and excellent electrochemical performance. To date, various nickel phosphides such as Ni2P, Ni5P4 (metal‐rich phase), and NiP4 (phosphorus‐rich phase) have been extensively studied, and there are differences in the number of K+ reactions and electron transfer ability among the three [80, 81]. Although breakthroughs have been made in the development of anode materials for PIBs, nickel phosphides still face core challenges: (1) poor electronic conductivity, which reduces the utilization ratio of active materials and leads to rapid capacity degradation; (2) volume expansion during potassiation/depotassiation induces structural damage; and (3) repeated formation and rupture of SEI layers induced by volume changes, consuming electrolyte and reducing cycling stability and Coulomb efficiency. Various types of nickel phosphide undergo multielectron conversion reactions to form K3P; however, they generally suffer from poor intrinsic conductivity and significant volume expansion. Synthetically prepared confined carbon structures (pea‐pod‐shaped carbon tubes, multishell hollow microspheres, and three‐dimensional porous carbon foams) physically constrain the pulverization of nickel phosphide particles; cross‐linked carbon networks provide continuous electron‐transport pathways, while boron/nitrogen heteroatom doping modulates the electronic states at the interface, lowering the adsorption energy barrier for potassium ions and improving reaction kinetics [82, 83].

Ni2P

The composite of dinickel phosphide (Ni2P) with carbonaceous materials, which takes advantage of the conductive mechanism of carbon and the favorable conditions of high surface area and porosity to facilitate the rapid charge transfer and shorten the diffusion length of potassium ions, has been proven to be one of the effective methods for improving the electrochemical performance of the anode material of Ni2P. The inherent issues of low conductivity, slow kinetics, and substantial volume expansion in TMPs can be effectively addressed by forming hybrid composites with carbon matrices. They also provide buffer space for volume expansion during cycling, thereby substantially enhancing the structural integrity of the electrode [84, 85, 86]. The chemical reaction equation for potassium storage in Ni2P is as follows:

Ni2P+3K++3e−↔2Ni+K3P

Based on this, Tao et al. [87] developed an innovative pea‐pod‐like structure (M x P y @BNCNTs, where M = Fe, Co, Ni, and M x P y is confined within boron/nitrogen co‐doped carbon nanotube arrays) through a simplified method. The oriented and uniformly distributed carbon nanotube arrays can serve as ion/electron‐transport channel, shortening the charge‐transfer path and protecting the encapsulated Ni2P nanoparticles from structural deformation caused by K+ intercalation/deintercalation, thereby enhancing the long‐term cycling stability of the anode. A schematic diagram of the fabrication route for M x P y @BNCNTs is provided (Figure 10a). Ni2P nanoparticles are homogeneously distributed inside BNCNT arrays and form a distinctive pea‐pod architecture (Figure 10b). Most reported transition metal phosphide (TMP) anodes only deliver high specific capacities under low current densities (below 0.8 A g−1) (Figure 10c). However, significant volume expansion during charge–discharge cycles causes structural deformation, leading to deteriorated cycling performance when the current density increases to 1.0 A g−1 or higher. This research provides an innovative solution for the stable structural design to address the problem of high‐rate stability in TMP‐based anode materials. Similarly, Zhang et al. [32] encapsulated ultrafine Ni2P nanoparticles within tubular mesoporous carbon channels (Ni2P@SCC). The channel confinement effect mitigates volume expansion during charging and discharging, enhances charge‐transport kinetics, and improves structural stability and ion‐diffusion rates. Electrochemical tests revealed a reversible specific capacity of 875 mAh g−1 after 300 cycles, and the specific capacity at a current density of 0.05–6.0 A g−1 was 1160–410 mAh g−1. When the current density returned to 0.05 A g−1, the specific capacity (~1050 mAh g−1) was close to the initial value, demonstrating outstanding rate capability and electrochemical performance. The Ni2P nanoparticles confined within mesoporous carbon nanotubes can provide more active sites for K+ storage, maintain the stability of the electrode structure to improve cycling performance, and the microstructure adjustment can effectively alleviate the problems of volume expansion, slow charge‐storage kinetics, and poor conductivity. Yan et al. [30] used biomass gelatin as the raw material to prepare a Ni2P and nitrogen–phosphorus codoped porous carbon composite material (Ni2P@NPC). The cross‐linked porous structure of this material can significantly alleviate the volume changes and particle aggregation of the active substances during charging and discharging. The carbon matrix encapsulating Ni2P nanodots optimizes the charge‐transfer path, and the interface engineering design enables it to have excellent electrochemical kinetics at high rates. Electrochemical tests showed that the material with a large specific surface area provides abundant K+ reactive sites. After 100 cycles at 0.1 A g−1, the reversible capacity was 282 mAh g−1. After 5000 cycles at 1 A g−1, it still retained 212 mAh g−1, and the electrochemical performance was outstanding [30, 32].

Researchers have systematically investigated the structural design, interfacial regulation, and electrochemical performance optimization of Ni2P‐based materials. Their experimental results demonstrated the viability of achieving efficient potassium storage via a synergistic ‘conversion‐intercalation’ mechanism. The comprehensive advantages of Ni2P in potassium‐storage capacity, rate capability, and cycling stability confirm its potential as anode material for PIBs, providing crucial experimental evidence and theoretical framework for developing TMP‐based energy storage materials. Future studies could employ DFT calculations to screen optimal dopant elements and design stable heterointerfaces, thereby providing a rational guide for experimental synthesis.

NiP2

Nickel diphosphide (NiP2) is a typical TMP. Due to its relatively low working potential and high theoretical specific capacity, it is regarded as a promising candidate material for the anode. However, the inherent electrical conductivity of NiP2 anode material is insufficient, and the volume changes during electrochemical cycling cannot be ignored, which result in poor rate capability and rapid capacity degradation. Therefore, researchers have coated it with carbon and doped it with heteroatoms, combining the advantages of various modification strategies to achieve the “synergistic enhancement” effect. The potassium‐storage reaction in NiP2 can be summarized as

NiP2+6K++6e−↔Ni+2K3P

Among these, Li et al. [88] successfully synthesized NiP2 nanoparticles loaded onto multishell hollow nitrogen‐doped carbon microspheres (NiP2@MHNC). A schematic diagram showing the synthesis procedure of NiP2 is presented (Figure 11a). The C—N and N—Ni bonds formed by nitrogen doping enhance structural stability and electron transport. Furthermore, the combined features of a high surface area and a multishell hollow framework mitigate volume changes and facilitate rapid K+ transport during cycling. TEM images (Figure 11b) reveal a rough, spherical surface morphology. Electrochemical testing (Figure 11c) indicates a specific capacity of 173 mAh g−1 after 3000 cycles at 5 A g−1, significantly outperforming NiP2, NiP2@C, and NiP2@NC. The synergistic effect of N‐doped bonding and multishell hollow structure collectively enhances the cycling stability and rate capability of the material. This strategy provides an effective direction for further optimizing the electrochemical performance of NiP2 in PIBs.

Ni5P4

Pure Pentanickel tetraphosphide (Ni5P4) electrodes typically exhibit significant volume expansion and low electrical conductivity, and the enhancement of the electrochemical performance of Ni5P4 in PIBs through modification is a current research focus. The potassium‐storage reaction in Ni5P4 can be summarized as follows:

Ni5P4+12K++12e−↔5Ni+4K3P

Yang et al. [89] successfully synthesized a highly stable a‐MnO x P y /Ni5P4/CF hybrid anode material. This material exhibits exceptional rate capability, delivering reversible capacities of 437.6, 415.0, 345.3, 304.7, 269.5, and 223.1 mAh g−1 at current densities of 0.1–5 A g−1, respectively and retaining a capacity of 436.5 mAh g−1 when cycled back to 0.1 A g−1 after 70 consecutive cycles at varying current densities. Similarly, to address the large volume expansion and relatively low electrical conductivity of pure TMPs, rational structural design serves as one approach. In addition, compositing carbon matrices with metal phosphides represents another effective method to mitigate volume expansion and enhance electronic conductivity. The three‐dimensional porous carbon foam structures, characterized by high specific surface area and abundant pores, can effectively buffer the volume expansion problem of anode materials during the cycling process, and can also provide conductive channels for ion transport. Ye et al. [90] designed and synthesized ultrafine Ni5P4 nanoparticles embedded in three‐dimensional porous carbon foam and used them as the anode materials for PIBs. At current densities of 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, and 5 A g−1, the Ni5P4/C material exhibited reversible capacities of 227.5, 163.8, 144.6, 130.6, 123.6, and 114.1 mAh g−1, respectively, demonstrating outstanding rapid charging and discharging capability.

Other Nickel‐Based Anode Materials

Nickel Telluride (NiTe)

As a transition metal telluride (TMT), NiTe undergoes a reduction reaction with K+, and Ni2+ is reduced to metallic Ni. For example, Ye et al. [91] synthesized stable NiTe@MoS2@C heterojunctions via hydrothermal synthesis. CV testing confirms that NiTe undergoes a typical conversion‐type potassium‐storage reaction. CV curves of NiTe@C and NiTe@MoS2@C anodes are displayed (Figure 12a,c). For NiTe@C, the initial cathodic peak at 0.62 V originates from the NiTe‐K+ conversion reaction alongside irreversible SEI generation. In the second and third cycles, the cathodic peak shifts to a higher potential, indicating the formation of ultrafine NiTe nanocrystals after the initial cycle. Additionally, an intense oxidation peak at 1.88 V is observed, which can be attributed to the conversion of metallic Ni nanocrystals back to NiTe. The NiTe@MoS2@C electrode shows sharper redox peaks and well‐overlapped CV profiles in the following cycles, demonstrating heterostructure‐facilitated electrochemistry and superior reversibility of the NiTe conversion reaction for KIBs.

From this, we can conclude that mechanistic analysis of potassium (de)intercalation in NiTe and the synergistic effects arising from the heterojunction reveal that the core electrochemical reaction can be expressed as follows:

The conversion reaction of NiTe: NiTe+2K++2e−↔Ni+K2Te

Heterojunction cooperative reaction(overall reaction):NiTe+MoS2+6K++6e−↔Ni+Mo+K2Te+2K2S

The tight interfacial bonding achieved through precise hydrothermal synthesis suppresses relative slippage between the NiTe and MoS2 phases during cycling, thereby mitigating the severe volume expansion issue associated with pure NiTe; the carbon‐coated structure exhibits both excellent high‐rate capacity retention and an exceptionally long cycle life, demonstrating that synthetically controlling the interfacial bonding strength of multicomponent systems is key to addressing the core performance limitations of NiTe. The presence of the heterojunction does not modify the inherent reaction route but promotes both kinetic rates and reversibility by leveraging a built‐in electric field and interfacial cooperative effects. Meanwhile, the carbon layer does not participate in electrochemical active reactions serving solely as structural support and conducting medium.

Ni–N

However, it is worth noting that nickel‐based materials may also undergo adsorption‐type potassium‐storage reactions. For example, Wu et al. [92] systematically investigated the atomic configuration, structural stability, and electrochemical performance of two‐dimensional pentagonal nickel nitride (penta‐NiN2) monolayers. Through pure DFT theoretical calculations, they have fully elucidated the adsorption‐based sodium/potassium‐storage mechanisms of alkali metal ions. Electron‐localization function (ELF) results show obvious charge accumulation between Ni and N atoms, demonstrating stable covalent Ni—N bonds that maintain the whole pentagonal skeleton during alkali adsorption. However, almost no charge density accumulates between adsorbed K layers and the substrate, which means weak van der Waals—like interaction between adatoms and penta‐NiN2. As a result, various layers of alkali atoms prefer to disperse and adsorb on the monolayer surface rather than forming metal clusters (Figure 12d,e).

Challenges and Solutions of Nickel‐Based Anodes in Potassium Ion Batteries

PIBs are also inevitably confronted with a series of key challenges. (1) The larger ionic radius of K+ (vs. Li+) exacerbates volume strain during cycling, leading to electrode pulverization and loss of electrical contact. (2) The larger radius of K+ directly increases its diffusion resistance through the solid electrode matrix, which consequently yields low diffusion coefficients and sluggish kinetics. (3) The irreversible capacity decay observed during prolonged cycling results from two key factors: progressive structural degradation of the electrode and the repetitive growth/cracking of the SEI layer—a process that depletes electrolyte and active potassium ions. A key focus of current research is to engineer electrodes with superior electrochemical properties, specifically targeting high rate capability, long‐term cycling stability, and fast ion transport [11, 93, 94, 95].

Compositing With Carbon Materials

Compositing with carbon‐based materials (such as graphene, carbon nanotubes, and other carbon nanomaterials) is an effective strategy for optimizing the structure and electrochemical performance of anode materials. The carbon matrix acts as a conductive scaffold that confines the active material, inhibits particle agglomeration, and shortens transport paths. Moreover, its inherent flexibility and mechanical stability accommodate volume changes during potassiation/depotassiation, thereby preventing electrode pulverization and ensuring structural integrity [5, 6, 96]. Carbon materials such as graphene and carbon nanotubes inherently exhibit superior electrical conductivity. A significant reduction in electrode charge‐transfer resistance is achieved by forming 3D crosslinked conductive networks, which enable continuous electron transport. This synergistic effect thereby enhances the electronic conductivity, cycling stability, and rate capability of the electrode material [97, 98, 99].

Designing Nanostructures

Nanostructuring represents a prevalent strategy to boost battery electrochemical performance. Nanoscale engineering drastically amplifies the specific surface area, enriching accessible reactive sites and facilitating ion adsorption, charge storage, and interfacial redox kinetics between electrode and electrolyte. For nickel‐based electrode materials, precise regulation of synthetic parameters (calcination temperature, precursor concentration) tailors directional nanostructures including nanospheres and nanosheets in accordance with intrinsic crystallographic characteristics and crystal growth behaviors, laying a structural prerequisite for superior electrochemical performance [49, 80]. Three‐dimensional hierarchical architectures assembled from ultrathin nanosheets possess prominent structural superiority: Their highly open framework maximizes electrode–electrolyte contact area to expedite ion migration and charge transfer, while plentiful interior void spaces alleviate volume strain generated by recurrent K+ insertion/extraction and prevent structural pulverization and collapse. Moreover, the ultrathin nanosheet subunit drastically shortens bulk‐phase potassium‐ion‐diffusion pathways, lowering ionic diffusion resistance and further optimizing ion‐transport kinetics [100, 101]. For example, Zhang et al. [32] encapsulated ultrafine Ni2P nanoparticles within tubular mesoporous carbon channels to prepare Ni2P@SCC composites. In this material, Ni2P particles measure only approximately 5.4 nm in size, exhibiting a high specific surface area while maintaining excellent monodispersity even at 600 °C. Owing to its unique nanostructure, it not only enhances electronic conductivity but also effectively preserves the structural integrity of Ni2P nanoparticles during charge–discharge cycles.

Designing Heterostructures

Aiming at the inherent low conductivity and slow ion transport of nickel‐based sulfide, selenide and phosphide anodes reviewed above, heterostructure engineering has proven a powerful modification route. Heterointerfaces formed between nickel‐based active phases and foreign metal compounds or carbon frameworks can build interconnected conductive networks to cut charge‐transfer resistance greatly. A strategy of forming heterogeneous composites by introducing conductive carbons and metallic nanoparticles establishes a continuous conductive network spanning the electrode, thereby sharply reducing the charge‐transfer resistance. Concurrently, interfacial phenomena such as band bending and built‐in fields at heterojunctions serve to regulate ion diffusion and lower the associated energy barriers, resulting in a significant boost in the electrode's kinetic performance.

Looking forward, achieving precise control over the chemical composition, microstructure, and interfacial bonding of heterostructures will be pivotal. This control will allow us to transcend the current performance boundaries of batteries, offering crucial support for technological innovation and industrial applications in energy storage.

Performance Evaluation and Development Challenges of Nickel‐Based Anodes in PIBs

From Half‐Cells to Full Cells: Comprehensive Performance Evaluation of Nickel‐Based Anodes

From a practical application perspective, the high capacity measured in half‐cell tests cannot serve as the sole evaluation criterion; rather, it must be interpreted comprehensively across multiple dimensions in conjunction with active material loading, areal capacity, ICE, average operating potential, cycle life, long‐term Coulombic efficiency, rate capability, electrode packing density, active material content, electrolyte volume, pre‐potassiation requirements, and full‐cell test results. Currently, electrochemical characterization of nanostructured nickel‐based composite materials is mostly conducted under conditions of low active material loading, with the addition of large amounts of nonactive components such as conductive carbon in the electrode system. Although this testing approach can effectively enhance apparent performance metrics such as mass‐specific capacity, excessively low electrode loading, a disproportionately high proportion of inactive phases, and low electrode sheet compaction density significantly reduce electrode space utilization. This directly limits the electrode's volumetric capacity and the battery's overall energy density, while also failing to accurately reflect the material's cycling stability, Coulombic efficiency stability, and rate‐recovery performance under actual operating conditions. Furthermore, some electrodes in potassium‐based energy storage systems require pre‐potassiation, and the amount of electrolyte used significantly affects interfacial stability and long‐term service performance; these critical conditions are often overlooked in conventional half‐cell testing. Consequently, establishing a unified and standardized benchmark testing system is of paramount importance: The testing process must employ consistent voltage windows and current densities, and fully and transparently disclose all experimental conditions, including the proportion of active material, electrode sheet compaction parameters, electrolyte volume, and pre‐potassiation processes. In addition to evaluating basic half‐cell performance, the assessment system must also focus on long‐term cycle life, dynamic Coulombic efficiency, and rate‐recovery characteristics. Ultimately, comprehensive validation must be conducted using high‐loading electrodes and full‐cell systems to ensure that the test data are comparable across different systems and possesses engineering reference value [39, 102, 103, 104].

Based on the review of nickel‐based oxide, sulfide, selenide, telluride, nitride, and phosphide anode materials for conversion‐type PIBs presented in this paper, it is evident that different types of nickel‐based compounds exhibit significant differences in potassium‐storage capacity, intrinsic conductivity, rate capability, and cycling stability: Nickel‐based phosphides possess outstanding reversible specific capacity, while nickel‐based selenides and sulfides exhibit excellent electronic conductivity and high‐rate cycling characteristics. Nickel‐based oxides are inexpensive to produce but suffer from insufficient intrinsic conductivity and significant volume deformation issues. Nickel‐based nitrides, however, remain primarily the subject of theoretical calculations and currently lack systematic experimental validation. A comprehensive analysis of existing experimental conditions reveals that the high‐capacity and excellent cycling performance data reported in the literature are mostly obtained from idealized half‐cell test systems featuring low active material loading and a high proportion of conductive additives. Most studies have not standardized core testing parameters such as voltage windows and current density, nor have they fully disclosed key information such as electrolyte formulations, dosages, and pre‐potentiation processes. Furthermore, there is a widespread lack of evaluation of engineering metrics—such as high‐loading electrode performance, initial Coulombic efficiency, and rate recovery capability—as well as full‐cell validation. Only a few systems have demonstrated good performance under high areal loading conditions, resulting in insufficient cross‐comparability among the vast amount of existing electrochemical data and making it difficult to accurately reflect the material's application potential under actual operating conditions. Therefore, future research in this field needs to establish a standardized testing and evaluation system, conduct multidimensional performance characterization using high‐loading electrodes, and validate results through full‐cell testing to further bridge the gap between laboratory research and industrial applications.

Prospects for the Commercial Application of Nickel‐Based Anode Materials

The development of nickel‐based anode materials has gone through multiple stages, and new research directions keep emerging. In the early period, researchers mainly focused on nickel‐based composite materials. Elemental nickel does not participate in electrochemical reactions; it is primarily adopted to improve electrode conductivity, and its hollow and porous structures can alleviate volume expansion. This is the earliest researched system. The subsequent research shifted to nickel‐based alloys (Ni‐M), including nickel–antimony (Ni–Sb), nickel–tin (Ni–Sn), and nickel–silicon (Ni–Si). In such alloys, inert nickel atoms occupy lattice sites, which relieves the volume deformation generated during the insertion and extraction of active metal ions and optimizes electrical conductivity. Encapsulating these alloys in carbon matrices further improves their cycling stability. The third stage centers on single‐component NiX. As conversion‐type materials including oxides, sulfides, phosphides, and selenides, they store potassium via conversion reactions and deliver higher theoretical capacities. Related research mainly combines these materials with carbon and metals and adopts structural designs such as nanowires, hollow structures and core–shell architectures to solve the problems of poor intrinsic conductivity and severe volume expansion. Later, multicomponent nickel‐based compounds became the research focus. Nickel–cobalt, nickel–iron, nickel–manganese, and other multimetal composites, combined with hierarchical structural design, can effectively prevent electrode structural failure. These materials possess better rate performance and cycling stability than single‐component nickel compounds and have become a popular research hotspot in recent years. Furthermore, a series of novel material systems have been developed, such as heterogeneous nickel‐based composites, nickel‐polymer aerogels, and MOF‐derived materials, which continuously innovate the design ideas of nickel‐based anode materials [24, 104].

As nickel‐based anode materials progress toward practical application, they face the following key challenges: (1) inherent performance limitations. Pure nickel‐based compounds (such as oxides, sulfides, and phosphides) generally suffer from insufficient intrinsic conductivity, during charge–discharge cycles, they undergo significant volume changes, which can easily lead to structural collapse of the electrode and the shedding of active material, ultimately resulting in rapid capacity degradation. (2) Challenges in controlling composition and structure. In the case of metallic nickel and nickel‐based alloy systems, it is difficult to precisely control the nickel doping ratio, particle morphology, and size; if the nickel content is too high, it will displace the active material, reducing the electrode's specific capacity and ICE, while excessively large nickel particles will further diminish the material's electrochemical activity. (3) Insufficient interfacial and structural stability in composite systems. While the modification of nickel‐based materials using carbon and metallic materials can optimize electrochemical performance, carbon‐phase materials such as carbon nanotubes and graphene are prone to agglomeration and stacking, leading to a loss of structural integrity after prolonged cycling. Furthermore, conventional coating and composite processes struggle to ensure long‐term stability of the interfaces during cycling. (4) Constraints relating to preparation processes and industrialization costs. The synthesis of certain highly active nickel‐based materials (such as nickel phosphides) involves complex processes and offers limited process control. Besides, the large‐scale production of multicomponent composite systems and materials with specialized nanostructures presents significant challenges, which severely hampers their commercialization.

Conclusion and Perspectives

In this paper, the research progress and applications of nickel‐based anode materials including Ni–O, Ni–S, Ni–Se, Ni–Te, Ni–N, and Ni–P for PIBs were presented (Table 3). Most experimentally studied Ni–O, Ni–S, Ni–Se, Ni–Te, and Ni–P anodes are commonly described by conversion‐type reactions, whereas adsorption‐dominated storage has so far been proposed mainly for theoretical Ni–N structures, but they inherently share three common drawbacks: low intrinsic electronic conductivity, severe lattice volume expansion during repeated potassium ion insertion and extraction, and slow potassium‐ion‐diffusion kinetics. All modification strategies across material systems center on controlling synthesis, microstructure, and interfacial chemistry: Tuned via synthesis engineering, these properties modulate electron transport, K+ diffusion, volume buffering, and electroactive sites; microstructural variations further govern conversion reversibility, pseudocapacitance, charge‐transfer impedance, and expansion tolerance, ultimately defining reversible capacity, rate capability, and cycling stability. Three mainstream synthesis and modification methods: compositing with carbon materials, nanostructure design, and heterostructure design. These methods work synergistically to enhance structural performance, systematically address the inherent defects of pure nickel‐based anode materials, and establish a universal design paradigm for high‐performance conversion‐type anodes in PIBs. Therefore, future research should integrate experimental characterization with theoretical simulations to explain the microscopic action mechanisms underlying electrochemical behaviors from multiple perspectives, thereby advancing the practical application of such materials in PIBs and facilitating the screening of more suitable anode materials. According to the current research trends of nickel‐based anode materials, when designing nickel‐based anode materials in the future, the following aspects can be considered to solve such problems.

First, the performance of PIB anodes determines the full‐cell electrochemical performance. The selection of the anode materials is crucial to the comprehensive performance of energy storage devices. In the future, theoretical DFT calculations can be used to predict ideal anode materials with better reaction kinetics and to deeply explore their electrochemical mechanisms. The development of conversion‐type anode materials is advancing rapidly, achieving significant breakthroughs in specific capacity, rate capability, and cycling stability, and there is a possibility of competing with the anode materials for LIBs. Future research should focus more on exploring the relationship between the structure and electrochemical performance of nickel‐based anode materials and optimizing the design and engineering of electrode nanostructures to enhance the performance of PIBs and effectively alleviate the current limitations such as low cycling stability, insufficient safety, and slow kinetics.

Second, while the selection of nickel‐based anode materials is important, it is even more crucial to fully realize their application potential through continuous optimization. Therefore, future research on anode materials should thoroughly consider how to enhance energy density through meticulous electrode design. This holds critical significance for advancing PIBs from laboratory development to practical real‐world applications. Furthermore, it is essential to conduct in‐depth investigations into the structures and engineering strategies of various nickel‐based anode materials, aiming to develop novel nickel‐based materials with high electrochemical performance, high specific capacity, and extended cycle life.

Third, unraveling electrochemical mechanisms is essential for the practical application of nickel‐based PIB anodes. Conventional ex situ methods only acquire postcycling static data and fail to capture real‐time dynamic changes, limiting the understanding of material working mechanisms and failure modes. In situ characterization is irreplaceable for exploring the electrochemical mechanisms of battery anodes. Different from conventional ex situ tests, a variety of advanced in situ techniques can dynamically track the real‐time evolution of crystal structures, micromorphologies and interfacial states during charge and discharge. They effectively capture transient structural changes and volume variation behaviors that cannot be detected by traditional means. Meanwhile, these methods quantitatively analyze ion migration and charge‐transfer processes, clarifying electrochemical reaction kinetics and rate‐limiting steps. Most importantly, in situ investigations build reliable structure–performance correlations by linking microscopic features to macroscopic battery properties. Such studies reveal the intrinsic reasons for capacity decay and structural failure, providing solid experimental guidance for the rational design and performance optimization of high‐performance electrode materials. Compared with LIBs, in situ characterization is less widely applied to nickel‐based PIB anodes. Future studies should adopt various in situ techniques and their combined use for in‐depth mechanistic exploration. The integration of in situ monitoring, kinetic analysis and theoretical calculations will facilitate the rational design and performance improvement of high‐performance nickel‐based anodes. Finally, the crystal structure and electronic structure of nickel‐based materials can be regulated to maximize their active sites. And template methods can be used to synthesize composite materials with multilevel micro–nano structures, such as three‐dimensional networks, yolk–shell structures, and porous nanosheets. Further, carbon materials can be used for surface coating or compositing to construct three‐dimensional conductive network structures with buffer spaces. The combined use of multiple strategies aims to simultaneously address the key issues faced by nickel‐based materials when used as anode materials, such as significant volume expansion, poor cycling stability, and insufficient conductivity, ultimately achieving high specific capacity and long cycling stability for practical applications.
