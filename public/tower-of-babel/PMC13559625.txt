Association between primary tumor surgery and survival in patients with de novo stage IV triple-negative breast cancer with visceral metastases: a SEER-based propensity score-matched study
Authors: Ruizhe Yue, Zheng Wang, Hao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe role of primary tumor surgery in patients with de novo stage IV triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) with visceral metastases remains controversial. This study investigated the association between primary tumor surgery and survival outcomes in this high-risk population.MethodsFemale patients diagnosed with de novo stage IV TNBC with liver, lung, and/or brain metastases between 2010 and 2020 were identified from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database. Overall survival (OS) and breast cancer-specific survival (BCSS) were compared between patients who underwent primary tumor surgery and those who did not. Propensity score matching (PSM), Kaplan-Meier analysis, Cox regression, subgroup analysis, robust sensitivity analysis, and restricted mean survival time (RMST) analysis were performed.ResultsA total of 1,827 patients were included, of whom 543 underwent primary tumor surgery and 1,284 did not. After 1:1 PSM, 507 matched pairs were generated. After matching, the median OS was 15 months in the surgery group and 9 months in the non-surgery group, while the median BCSS was 15 months and 10 months, respectively. In multivariable Cox analysis, primary tumor surgery was independently associated with longer OS [hazard ratio (HR) =0.532, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.462–0.612, P<0.001] and BCSS (HR =0.544, 95% CI: 0.470–0.629, P<0.001). These associations remained stable in robust Cox sensitivity analyses. RMST analyses at 24 and 36 months further supported the robustness of the observed survival association.ConclusionsPrimary tumor surgery was associated with longer OS and BCSS in patients with de novo stage IV TNBC with visceral metastases. This observed association may partly reflect patient selection, underlying health status, metastatic burden, and response to systemic therapy. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating real-world evidence rather than definitive evidence of a causal treatment benefit. Individualized surgical decision-making should be based on careful patient selection and multidisciplinary evaluation.

Highlight box

Key findings

• In this SEER-based propensity score-matched study, primary tumor surgery was associated with longer overall survival and breast cancer-specific survival in patients with de novo stage IV triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) with liver, lung, and/or brain visceral metastases.

• The association remained stable in robust Cox models and restricted mean survival time analyses.

What is known and what is new?

• The role of primary tumor surgery in de novo stage IV breast cancer remains controversial, and evidence from randomized trials and retrospective studies is inconsistent. Previous studies mainly evaluated all metastatic breast cancer patients or broad metastatic TNBC populations.

• This study specifically focused on a high-risk subgroup of patients with de novo stage IV TNBC and visceral metastases.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Primary tumor surgery should not be considered routine treatment for all patients with visceral metastatic TNBC.

• In carefully selected patients, surgical decision-making may be considered within a multidisciplinary framework and in conjunction with systemic therapy.

• Prospective studies and external validation are needed to determine whether this observed survival association reflects a true causal treatment effect or is mainly driven by patient selection and unmeasured confounding.

Introduction

Breast cancer is currently the most commonly diagnosed malignancy among women worldwide and remains a major threat to women’s health (1). Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) accounts for approximately 15–20% of all breast cancers (2). Because it lacks expression of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), TNBC is characterized by aggressive biological behavior, early recurrence and metastasis, and poor prognosis. Previous studies have reported that larger tumor size, nodal involvement, higher T and N stage, and other adverse clinicopathological characteristics are associated with recurrence, distant metastasis, or poor prognosis in patients with TNBC (3,4). In patients with TNBC who present with distant metastases at initial diagnosis, tumor burden is high, disease progression is rapid, systemic treatment options are relatively limited, and survival remains unsatisfactory even with standard first-line systemic therapy (5). Previous studies have shown that molecular subtypes are associated with distinct metastatic patterns, and triple-negative tumors are more likely to develop visceral metastases, particularly to the lung, liver, and brain (6,7).

For patients with de novo stage IV TNBC and visceral metastases, cure is rarely achievable. Treatment mainly aims to delay tumor progression, improve quality of life, and prolong survival (8). Systemic therapy, especially chemotherapy, is therefore the cornerstone of treatment. However, whether primary tumor surgery should be performed in this setting remains controversial (9-13). Several retrospective studies have suggested that resection of the primary tumor may improve survival in selected patients with de novo stage IV breast cancer (9-12), whereas findings from prospective and randomized studies have been inconsistent (14-16). International consensus guidelines for advanced breast cancer emphasize individualized multidisciplinary decision-making based on systemic therapy rather than routine primary tumor surgery for all patients (8).

Compared with other molecular subtypes of breast cancer, TNBC more frequently metastasizes to visceral organs such as the lung, brain, and liver (6,7). Existing studies have mainly evaluated the prognostic impact of primary tumor surgery in overall metastatic breast cancer populations or in broad TNBC populations (11,12,17,18). Few studies have specifically focused on patients with de novo stage IV TNBC and liver, lung, or brain metastases, who represent a particularly high-risk subgroup with difficult clinical management.

Therefore, the present study used the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database to investigate the association between primary tumor surgery and overall survival (OS) and breast cancer-specific survival (BCSS) in patients with de novo stage IV TNBC and visceral metastases. We also explored prognostic factors and clinical subgroups in which primary tumor surgery may be associated with different survival outcomes, with the aim of providing real-world evidence for individualized surgical discussion in this high-risk population. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD and STROBE reporting checklists (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1163/rc).

Methods

Data source

Data were obtained from the SEER database of the US National Cancer Institute using SEER*Stat software version 9.0.4. Female patients diagnosed between 2010 and 2020 with de novo stage IV TNBC and liver, lung, and/or brain metastases were identified. Because the SEER database contains publicly available and de-identified data, institutional review board approval and informed consent were not required. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) female sex; (II) pathologically confirmed primary invasive breast cancer; (III) negative ER, PR, and HER2 status, consistent with TNBC; (IV) American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 7th edition stage IV disease at initial diagnosis; (V) presence of liver, lung, and/or brain metastasis at diagnosis; and (VI) available clinicopathological variables, primary tumor surgery information, survival status, and follow-up information. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) breast cancer not being the first primary malignancy; (II) metastasis limited to bone, soft tissue, pleura, or other non-visceral sites; (III) missing key information on primary tumor surgery or other core analytic variables; (IV) incomplete follow-up time, survival status, or cause of death; and (V) non-invasive breast cancer.

Variables and outcomes

The following variables were collected: demographic characteristics, including age at diagnosis, race, and marital status; tumor pathological characteristics, including histologic grade, AJCC 7th edition T stage and N stage, visceral metastatic sites, and number of visceral metastatic sites; treatment information, including primary tumor surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy; and survival outcomes, including survival time, vital status, and cause of death. The primary outcome was OS, defined as the time from breast cancer diagnosis to death from any cause or last follow-up. The secondary outcome was BCSS, defined as the time from breast cancer diagnosis to death attributable to breast cancer. Follow-up data were available through December 31, 2020.

Primary tumor surgery was defined according to the SEER cancer-directed surgery variable for the primary breast tumor. However, SEER does not reliably capture surgical intent, the exact indication for surgery, the timing of surgery relative to systemic therapy, the extent of axillary surgery, or whether the procedure was performed for local control, palliation, or attempted definitive locoregional management. Chemotherapy was categorized as “Yes” and “No/Unknown” according to SEER coding. Therefore, the “No/Unknown” category included both patients who did not receive chemotherapy and patients with unavailable chemotherapy information.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.3.1. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages. Between-group comparisons were performed using continuity-corrected chi-square tests for binary variables and Pearson chi-square tests for multicategory variables. To reduce baseline confounding, 1:1 nearest-neighbor propensity score matching (PSM) with a caliper width of 0.05 was performed. Matching variables included age, race, marital status, histologic grade, T stage, N stage, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, liver metastasis, lung metastasis, and brain metastasis. The number of visceral metastatic sites was not included in the propensity score model because it was derived from liver, lung, and brain metastasis variables; however, it was retained for subgroup analyses. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were used to assess balance after matching, and SMD <0.1 was considered indicative of acceptable balance. Kaplan-Meier curves were generated for OS and BCSS and compared using the log-rank test. Median follow-up was estimated using the reverse Kaplan-Meier method. Clinically relevant variables and variables with potential prognostic value were included in the multivariable Cox regression models. Because multiple visceral metastases were derived from liver, lung, and brain metastasis variables, they were not entered into the multivariable model simultaneously with organ-specific metastatic variables to avoid model overlap. Generalized variance inflation factors (GVIFs) were calculated to assess multicollinearity. Schoenfeld residuals were used to test the proportional hazards assumption. Because the proportional hazards assumption was not fully satisfied for several covariates, robust Cox models accounting for matched subclasses and restricted mean survival time (RMST) analyses at 24 and 36 months were performed as sensitivity analyses. Subgroup analyses were conducted according to age, liver metastasis, lung metastasis, brain metastasis, number of visceral metastatic sites, and chemotherapy status. Interaction tests were performed to compare effect differences across subgroups. Subgroup results were interpreted as exploratory.

Results

Baseline characteristics and PSM

A total of 1,827 eligible female patients with de novo stage IV TNBC and visceral metastases were included in the final analytic cohort. Among them, 543 patients underwent primary tumor surgery and 1,284 did not. The patient selection process is shown in Figure 1. The median follow-up time estimated by the reverse Kaplan-Meier method was 50 months. Before PSM, the two groups differed significantly in several clinicopathological and treatment variables, indicating baseline imbalance. After 1:1 nearest-neighbor PSM with a caliper width of 0.05, 507 matched pairs were generated. Baseline imbalances between the surgery and non-surgery groups were substantially reduced after matching, and most covariates achieved SMDs below 0.1. Only N3 stage showed a marginal SMD slightly above 0.1 (Table 1; Figure S1).

Survival analysis

Before PSM, the median OS was 15 months in the surgery group and 8 months in the non-surgery group. The median BCSS was 16 months in the surgery group and 9 months in the non-surgery group. After PSM, the median OS was 15 months in the surgery group and 9 months in the non-surgery group, while the median BCSS was 15 and 10 months, respectively. After matching, the 2-year OS rates were 28.6% and 13.7% in the surgery and non-surgery groups, respectively. The corresponding 2-year BCSS rates were 30.0% and 15.3%, respectively. Cox regression analysis of prognostic factors

In the multivariable Cox model after PSM, primary tumor surgery was independently associated with longer OS [hazard ratio (HR) =0.532, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.462–0.612, P<0.001] and BCSS (HR =0.544, 95% CI: 0.470–0.629, P<0.001). Chemotherapy was also independently associated with better OS and BCSS. Liver metastasis, lung metastasis, and brain metastasis were associated with worse prognosis for both OS and BCSS. The other/unknown race category was associated with better OS and BCSS compared with White race. Age, histologic grade, N stage, and radiotherapy were not statistically significant in the multivariable models, while T4 stage showed a borderline association with poorer OS (Tables 2,3; Figure 3). Multicollinearity diagnostics showed low adjusted GVIFs for all variables, indicating no substantial multicollinearity. Schoenfeld residual tests suggested that the proportional hazards assumption was not fully satisfied for several covariates, including surgery, chemotherapy, T stage, N stage, lung metastasis, and brain metastasis. Therefore, robust Cox models and RMST analyses were performed as sensitivity analyses.

Sensitivity analysis

In robust Cox models accounting for matched subclasses, primary tumor surgery remained significantly associated with longer OS (HR =0.532, 95% CI: 0.458–0.618, P<0.001) and BCSS (HR =0.544, 95% CI: 0.466–0.635, P<0.001), supporting the stability of the main findings. Because Schoenfeld residual tests suggested that the proportional hazards assumption was not fully satisfied for several covariates, RMST analyses were further performed. At 24 months, the RMST for OS was 14.689 months in the surgery group and 10.483 months in the non-surgery group, with a between-group difference of 4.206 months (95% CI: 3.189–5.224, P<0.001). At 36 months, the corresponding OS RMST values were 17.457 and 11.710 months, respectively, with a difference of 5.747 months (95% CI: 4.317–7.177, P<0.001). Similar results were observed for BCSS. At 24 months, the RMST for BCSS was 15.018 months in the surgery group and 11.071 months in the non-surgery group, with a difference of 3.947 months (95% CI: 2.913–4.980, P<0.001). At 36 months, the RMST for BCSS was 17.953 months in the surgery group and 12.473 months in the non-surgery group, with a difference of 5.480 months (95% CI: 4.001–6.959, P<0.001) (Table S1).

Subgroup analysis of the survival association with primary tumor surgery

To further explore the association between primary tumor surgery and OS across different clinical subgroups, patients were stratified by age, liver metastasis, lung metastasis, brain metastasis, number of visceral metastatic sites, and chemotherapy status. Primary tumor surgery was associated with longer OS across all evaluated subgroups. The association was observed in patients aged <65 years (HR =0.563, 95% CI: 0.474–0.669) and ≥65 years (HR =0.569, 95% CI: 0.451–0.718), as well as in patients with liver metastasis (HR =0.602, 95% CI: 0.487–0.744), lung metastasis (HR =0.589, 95% CI: 0.497–0.698), brain metastasis (HR =0.546, 95% CI: 0.371–0.803), and multiple visceral metastases (HR =0.708, 95% CI: 0.523–0.959). Interaction testing suggested that chemotherapy status might modify the association between surgery and OS (P for interaction =0.02). No significant interaction was observed for age, liver metastasis, lung metastasis, brain metastasis, or number of visceral metastatic sites (Figure 4).

Exploratory construction and internal validation of a prognostic nomogram

As an exploratory secondary analysis, a 2-year OS nomogram was constructed based on the multivariable Cox regression results using primary tumor surgery, marital status, T4 stage, chemotherapy, liver metastasis, lung metastasis, and brain metastasis (Figure 5A). The apparent Harrell’s C-index of the model was 0.689, and the bootstrap-corrected C-index was 0.685, indicating moderate discriminative ability. The calibration curve showed acceptable agreement between predicted and observed 2-year OS probabilities (Figure 5B).

Discussion

TNBC lacks effective endocrine and HER2-targeted treatment options and is characterized by aggressive behavior, early recurrence and metastasis, and poor prognosis. When liver, lung, or brain metastases are present, patients face a high risk of rapid deterioration and visceral organ failure. Traditionally, these patients have been regarded as having incurable systemic disease, with limited value for local intervention. In this large SEER-based cohort, we specifically excluded patients with only bone, soft tissue, pleural, or other non-visceral metastases and focused on a high-risk subgroup of patients with TNBC and visceral metastases. After adjustment for measured baseline differences by PSM, the median OS and BCSS in the surgery group remained longer than those in the non-surgery group, and multivariable Cox regression further showed that primary tumor surgery was associated with longer OS and BCSS. However, this association should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of a causal treatment benefit. Surgical candidacy in real-world practice is likely influenced by patient fitness, comorbidity burden, performance status, organ function, metastatic burden, disease tempo, systemic treatment response, and physician selection, many of which are not captured in SEER. Therefore, the observed survival association may partly reflect more favorable baseline characteristics and treatment responsiveness among patients selected for surgery. These findings provide additional real-world evidence for individualized discussion of local treatment in carefully selected patients with visceral metastatic TNBC and are broadly consistent with several previous population-based and real-world studies (9-12,17-21).

The role of primary tumor resection in de novo stage IV breast cancer remains controversial. The MF07-01 randomized clinical trial suggested that locoregional surgery may improve survival in selected patients, whereas the ECOG-ACRIN EA2108 trial did not demonstrate an OS benefit from early local therapy (14,15). More recently, the JCOG1017/PRIM-BC randomized trial also contributed important prospective evidence to this debate (16). A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized trials suggested that primary breast surgery should not be routinely performed for all patients with de novo metastatic breast cancer, although selected subgroups may still warrant further investigation (13). Current international consensus guidelines emphasize systemic therapy and individualized multidisciplinary decision-making rather than routine primary tumor surgery for all patients with advanced breast cancer (8). Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as supportive evidence for individualized surgical decision-making in carefully selected patients rather than as evidence supporting routine surgery for all patients with visceral metastatic TNBC.

Subgroup and interaction analyses were performed to evaluate whether the association between surgery and OS differed across clinical subgroups. Surgery was associated with longer OS across all evaluated subgroups. Chemotherapy status showed a statistically significant interaction with surgery, suggesting a possible interplay between local treatment and systemic therapy. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because chemotherapy response is not available in SEER. Patients who achieved disease control or favorable response to systemic therapy may have been more likely to undergo surgery, whereas patients with rapidly progressive disease may have remained in the non-surgery group. Thus, the apparent survival association in the surgical group may partly reflect response-based selection rather than the independent effect of surgery itself. In contrast, age, liver metastasis, lung metastasis, brain metastasis, and number of visceral metastatic sites did not show significant interactions. The subgroup findings should be considered exploratory and require validation in prospective studies.

An important methodological issue in the present study is that the proportional hazards assumption was not fully satisfied for several covariates. To address this issue, we performed both robust Cox models accounting for matched subclasses and RMST analyses at clinically relevant time points. The results of these sensitivity analyses were consistent with the main Cox models, supporting the stability of the observed association between primary tumor surgery and longer survival. Nevertheless, the HRs should be interpreted as overall associations during follow-up rather than strictly time-constant effects.

Several biological hypotheses may partly explain the observed survival association of primary tumor surgery. First, resection of the primary tumor may reduce the overall tumor burden and thereby theoretically improve the effectiveness of subsequent systemic therapy (22,23). Second, according to the self-seeding hypothesis, circulating tumor cells may not only disseminate from the primary tumor to distant organs but may also re-infiltrate the primary site, contributing to further tumor progression. Removal of the primary lesion could theoretically interrupt this dynamic process. Third, the primary tumor may promote systemic immunosuppression through the release of tumor-derived factors and the expansion of myeloid-derived suppressor cells, thereby impairing antitumor CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell responses. Surgical removal of the primary tumor might partially alleviate this immunosuppressive state (23,24). Nevertheless, these mechanisms remain speculative in the context of the present population-based analysis and require further biological and prospective clinical investigation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective database study, it cannot fully eliminate selection bias and residual confounding despite the use of PSM. Although PSM reduced imbalance in measured covariates, it could not adjust for unmeasured determinants of surgical candidacy. SEER lacks important prognostic and treatment-selection variables, including Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status, frailty, comorbidity burden, organ function, detailed metastatic burden, disease tempo, patient preference, and multidisciplinary assessment of operability. Patients who underwent primary tumor surgery may therefore have had better baseline health status, lower disease burden, more favorable disease biology, or better access to care than those who did not undergo surgery. These unmeasured differences may have overestimated the observed survival association of surgery. Second, immortal time bias should be considered. Survival time was calculated from diagnosis, but patients in the surgery group necessarily had to survive long enough to undergo surgery. Because SEER does not provide sufficiently detailed information on the exact timing of surgery relative to diagnosis and systemic therapy, surgery could not be modeled as a time-dependent exposure. Therefore, immortal time before surgery may have biased the survival estimates in favor of the surgical group. Third, SEER does not provide detailed treatment-related variables that may substantially influence treatment allocation and outcomes, including the timing of surgery, sequence of surgery and systemic therapy, response to preoperative systemic therapy, surgical intent, operative indication, extent of axillary surgery, surgical margin status (R0/R1/R2), specific chemotherapy regimens, dose intensity, immunotherapy, antibody-drug conjugates, PARP inhibitors, or subsequent lines of therapy. Therefore, we could not distinguish curative-intent locoregional surgery from palliative surgery or surgery performed for local symptom control. Our findings should not be interpreted as supporting a specific surgical procedure, surgical extent, or surgical intent. Fourth, the chemotherapy variable in SEER is coded as “Yes” versus “No/Unknown”. The “No/Unknown” group may include both patients who truly did not receive chemotherapy and those with unavailable chemotherapy information, which may introduce misclassification bias. In addition, response-based selection bias may exist because patients who responded well to chemotherapy or achieved better disease control were more likely to be considered surgical candidates, whereas patients with rapidly progressive disease may have remained in the non-surgery group. Thus, part of the observed survival difference may reflect better biological response to systemic therapy rather than the independent effect of primary tumor surgery. Fifth, SEER lacks information on germline BRCA1/2 mutation status, other actionable genomic alterations, programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) status, and molecular characteristics of metastatic lesions. These factors are clinically relevant in metastatic TNBC because they may influence the use of PARP inhibitors, immunotherapy, and other contemporary systemic treatments, thereby affecting survival outcomes (25). Furthermore, receptor conversion between primary and metastatic lesions could not be evaluated. Finally, the nomogram constructed in this study was internally validated only and should be regarded as an exploratory secondary analysis rather than a validated clinical decision-making tool. Marital status may act as a proxy for social support, access to care, or socioeconomic factors rather than a direct biological prognostic factor, which may limit the clinical interpretability of the nomogram. External validation and decision curve analysis are needed before the model can be considered for clinical use. Therefore, our results should be interpreted as evidence that primary tumor surgery was associated with better observed survival in a real-world population, rather than proof of a causal survival benefit.

Conclusions

In patients with de novo stage IV TNBC and visceral metastases, primary tumor surgery was associated with longer OS and BCSS after adjustment for measured confounders, and chemotherapy was also an important favorable prognostic factor. However, given the retrospective design, potential residual confounding, immortal time bias, treatment misclassification, and response-based selection bias, this association should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of a causal survival benefit. These findings suggest that visceral metastasis alone should not automatically preclude multidisciplinary discussion of local therapy in carefully selected patients. Surgical decision-making should incorporate the patient’s general condition, organ function, metastatic burden, feasibility of complete resection, systemic treatment response, treatment intent, and patient preference. Prospective studies are needed to determine whether the observed association reflects a true treatment effect in this high-risk population.
