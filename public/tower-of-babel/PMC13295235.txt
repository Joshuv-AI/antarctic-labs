Streptomyces corchorusii, sodium silicate, and potassium silicate as eco-friendly alternatives to control fungal rot of navel oranges during cold storage and shelf life
Authors: Mohamed I Elsayed, Najeeb M Almasoudi, Mohamed A Awad, Adel D Al-Qurashi, Kamal A M Abo-Elyousr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOranges undergo internal metabolic changes during cold storage and shelf life at ambient conditions, resulting in physiological and pathological disorders, mainly caused by Penicillium digitatum (green mold) and Penicillium italicum (blue mold), which decrease consumer preferences and marketability. To elucidate the effects of eco-friendly, safe treatments to control mold spoilage and maintain the quality of Navel oranges during cold storage and shelf life, freshly mature fruits were dipped for 15 min in 1% potassium silicate (PS), 0.5% sodium silicate (SS), or 10 6 CFU/ml Streptomyces corchorusii phytstc7 (STC), either alone or in combinations. The fruits were cold stored at (5 °C and 85–90% RH) for 50 days, followed by 3 days of shelf life (23 ± 1 °C and 60–70% RH). All treatments recorded no green mold incidence during 30 days of cold storage followed by 3 days of shelf life. However, after 40 and 50 days, the control treatment demonstrated a progressive increase in disease incidence, reaching 16.6 and 23.3%, respectively. Conversely, single treatments with PS, SS, and STC significantly suppressed disease development by 2.6%, 4.7%, and 2.0%, respectively. The combined treatment (PS + SS + STC) exhibited synergistic effects, and no visible disease was observed during 50 days of cold storage and 3 days of shelf life. This treatment also exhibited superior effects in maintaining regular physical and biochemical parameters of fruit quality. Other combinations, such as PS + STC and SS + STC, also performed exceptionally well, with a mean incidence of 1.3% and 0.7%, respectively. The severity of the disease followed a pattern similar to that of the disease incidence. Overall, postharvest dipping in 106 CFU/ml STC alone or combined with 1% PS and 0.5% SS could be an effective treatment to control green and blue mold and to retain the quality of ‘Navel oranges’ during 50 days of cold storage and shelf life.Keywords: Actinomycest, Acidity, Antioxidants, Blue and green mold, Orange, Penicillium, Phenolic compounds, Shelf life

Introduction

Oranges are commercially important fruits worldwide due to their health-promoting compounds and pleasing sensory attributes [1]. It is classified as a non-climacteric type, characterized by low respiration rates and ethylene production during cold storage and shelf life [2]. However, oranges undergo rapid internal metabolic changes during cold storage and during shelf life at ambient conditions, leading to physiological and pathological disorders. In addition, postharvest decay, mainly caused by Penicillium digitatum and P. italicum, further reduces consumer acceptance and marketability. Green and blue mold are the most important causes of citrus diseases, representing 80% of total rot decay. Therefore, it is necessary to search for safer alternatives to control mold [3]. Green mold disease, caused by the fungal pathogen P. digitatum, is currently the most significant postharvest disease. Controlling P. digitatum is a global challenge and typically requires integrated management throughout the pre- and postharvest chains. Leaf spraying of potassium silicate on young seedlings during propagation can suppress the growth of P. digitatum and up-regulate defense-related genes, therefore reducing disease severity of seedlings, for example, in citrus trees [4]. Biological control has been an effective tool for controlling important plant diseases, such as green mold on citrus fruits caused by P. digitatum [5]. Treatment with spore suspension of Streptomyces corchorusii showed potent in vitro and plant antifungal activities against phytopathogenic fungi e.g. F. oxysporum f. sp. niveum [6], El-Abyad et al. [7] against F. oxysporum f. sp. phaseoli (wilt disease in bean), and had two main mechanisms of action: production of non-volatile organic compounds and production of volatile organic compounds against different pathogens. Non-volatile organic compounds (NVOCs) produced by Streptomyces spp. can inhibit plant pathogenic fungi by secreting three classes of substances: diffusible antifungal metabolites, extracellular hydrolytic enzymes, and siderophores. Studies by Cuervo et al. [8] have confirmed that enzymes such as chitinase can degrade fungal cell walls, while siderophores enhance antagonistic activity by limiting iron availability. Volatile organic compounds produced by Streptomyces spp can inhibit pathogenic fungi through non-contact mechanisms such as suppressing spore germination. Some of these metabolites can also activate plant defenses, and the relevant conclusions are supported by the studies of Cuervo et al. [8]. When conducting pilot experiments using S. corchorusii as a biocontrol agent combined with low doses of synthetic fungicides, the combination controlled P. digitatum disease better than synthetic fungicides alone at high concentrations. Biological agents effectively control different pathogens and could be used in integrated treatments with synthetic fungicides [9]. The use of biocontrol agents for disease control, compared with synthetic fungicide applications, can reduce the doses and frequency of applications, thereby reducing the release of toxic residues into the environment. However, several problems arise in the commercialization of biocontrol agents, including production costs, shelf life, compatibility, and stability under different field conditions during commercial cultivation [10].

Many studies on biological agents of green mold on citrus and other crops have shown promising results [11–13]. The study of Tian et al. [14] investigated the beneficial effects of biocontrol yeasts Cryptococcus laurentii and Rhodotorula glutinis + Si on Alternaria alternata and Penicillium expansum molds during jujube fruit (Chinese date, Zizyphus jujuba) storage at 20 °C and 0°C. The control of the diseases caused by A. alternata and P. expansum on jujube fruit at 20 °C was most effective when C. laurentii and R. glutinis were applied as cell suspensions with Si (5 × 107 cells/ml) plus Si at a concentration of 2%.

Salts have been used as antimicrobial agents in postharvest treatment of citrus fruits. The antifungal effect of salts is mainly due to changes in the pH of the fruit peel surface and to the delay in the activity of cell wall-degrading enzymes (polygalacturonase, pectinase, and pectin methyl esterase) produced by the pathogen in the early stages of infection [15, 16]. In addition, sodium, potassium, or calcium silicate salts release sodium, potassium, and calcium ions that adjust the osmotic balance of the cell, transport ions, stimulate metabolism, and reduce toxicity by maintaining the Na + K +/Cl balance in the cytoplasm [17]. However, salt treatments are notorious for not protecting the fruit for long periods or from re-infection. Therefore, the application time is an important factor in achieving the best results in fruit protection [18]. The effects of sodium and potassium salts on fruits and the possibility of activating their defense response against fungal attack, stimulating the synthesis of phytoalexins, increasing the activity of phenylalanine ammonia-lyase, and enhancing the synthesis of peroxidases in tissues were studied, which positively affect fungal resistance [19, 20]. Silicon (Si) is one of the most beneficial mineral elements for plants [21]. Silicon is important and effective in improving plant resistance to biotic stresses. Several mechanisms exist through which silicon can increase plant resistance to biotic stresses. Silicon can be incorporated directly into the plant cell wall to fortify its durability, thereby providing a physical barrier against pathogen penetration [22]. Silicon may also elevate metabolism related to plant defense and increase the production of antimicrobial compounds [e.g., lignin and phenol] as well as enzymes associated with defense (i.e., peroxidase (POD)) [23]. Furthermore, silicon plays an important role in interacting with other hormones to enhance plant resistance to pathogens. In horticultural crops belonging to the genus citrus, potassium silicate reduces the severity of disease caused by Lasiodiplodia theobromae and Diplodia natalensis, as well as the resistance to Penicillium digitatum experimental infection, depending on the citrus species [24]. Sodium silicate has a wide range of applications in plant disease response, including enhancing resistance to pathogens, regulating plant growth, and activating a series of disease resistance-related genes [25]. Interactions between sodium silicate treatment and specific genes involved in disease responses activate the salicylic acid- or jasmonic acid-mediated signaling pathways. Most laboratories and field trials have confirmed that exogenous sodium silicate treatment can enhance plant defense responses against a range of diseases, including rice blast, downy mildew on cucumber, and numerous fungal pathogens [26, 27]. While some previous studies have shown the independent effects of silicate salts or biological control agents on postharvest citrus diseases, little is known about their potential synergistic effects when applied in combination under commercial cold storage and shelf-life conditions. Specifically, its integration with potassium and sodium silicate as less-harmful substitutes for synthetic fungicides has not been well studied. Thus, the novelty of this study is that it evaluated for the first time a combination of silicate salts and S. corchorusii as an integrated strategy to control green- and blue-mold infections while maintaining fruit quality during extended storage. Therefore, the present study aimed to assess the effectiveness of these treatments, individually and in combination, in reducing postharvest decay and maintaining fruit quality during extended cold storage. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of 1% potassium silicate (PS), 0.5% sodium silicate (SS), or 106 CFU/ml Streptomyces corchorusii phytstc7 (STC) either alone or in combinations to control postharvest mold spoilage and retain regular quality attributes of Navel oranges during cold storage and shelf life. Materials and methods

This study was conducted on Washington navel orange [Citrussinensis (L.) Osbeck] collected from a commercial orchard in Najran, Saudi Arabia. The experimental trees were 17 years old, grafted onto Rough lemon (Citrus jambhiri) rootstock, and growing in loamy sand soil with a pH of 8.2. Trees were planted at a spacing of 6X6 m with a plant density of approximately 278 trees ha⁻1. The orchard was managed according to the standard local commercial practices, including a drip irrigation system. Fruits were harvested at the commercial mature stage, when peel coloration reached approximately 70–100% yellow–orange. Healthy, uniform, and defect-free fruits were selected to minimize variability among treatments. Fruits were carefully selected to ensure uniformity in size and weight, with an average fruit diameter of 70–85 mm and an average fruit weight of 100–150 g, representing commercially marketable orange fruits. Fruits were transported to the Postharvest Technology Laboratory of King Abdulaziz University in Jeddah immediately after harvest. To maintain fruit quality and reduce physiological changes during transit, the samples were transported to the postharvest laboratory in an insulated vehicle under shaded conditions, avoiding direct exposure to sunlight and high ambient temperatures. Upon arrival at the laboratory, fruits were immediately inspected again for any handling injuries and then randomly allocated to the respective postharvest treatment groups prior to storage experiments. The experiment was arranged in a completely randomized design (CRD) with three replicates of 50 fruits each.

All fruit samples (810) were thoroughly surface-sterilized by immersion in 1% sodium hypochlorite solution for 2 min, then washed with distilled water for 1 min, and dried on sterile filter paper. Thirty fruits of each replication were immersed for 15 min in either distilled water (control) or 1% potassium silicate (PS), 0.5% sodium silicate (SS), and 106 CFU/ml Streptomyces corchorusii phytstc7 (STC), either alone or in combinations. The fruits were immersed in the treatment suspension until completely covered for 15 min. The mixing agent Tween 20 was added to all treatment formulations at a rate of 1 ml/L⁻1 [3]. After air drying for 1 h, the fruits were packed in perforated cardboard boxes and cold stored at (5 °C and 85–90% RH) for 50 days, followed by 3 days of shelf life at ambient (23 ± 1 °C and 60–70% RH). Samples were taken from three replicates for each treatment and examined at 10-day intervals after cold storage, plus 3 days of shelf life. This period was chosen to stimulate the critical early retail transition phase immediately following removal from cold storage, rather than to represent the full commercial marketing period (7 to 14 days), depending on logistics, temperature management, and market distribution practices.

Source of the bioagents and chemicals compounds

Streptomyces corchorusii phytstc7 (STC) used here was obtained from Plant Pathology lab at King Abdulaziz University, it was identification before and deposited in NCBI under accession number PX514792. sodium silicate (SS) and Potassium silicate (PS) were obtained from fisher Scientific U.K and BDH laboratory Sipplies, respectively.

Preparation of Streptomyces corchorusii inoculum

The Streptomyces corchorusii phytstc7 strain was inoculated on starch casein agar (SCA) medium and cultured at 28 ± 2 °C for 7 days to ensure sufficient growth and sporulation. Spores were collected by flooding the culture plates with sterile distilled water containing 0.1% Tween 80 and scraping them off via gentle agitation. The obtained suspension was filtered through sterile cheesecloth to eliminate mycelial residues. Suspensions of spore final concentration were prepared as 1 × 10⁶ CFU mL⁻1 in sterile distilled water. Serial dilution and plate count method on SCA medium after incubation at 28 °C for 3–5 days was performed to confirm the concentration. Fruit treatments were performed immediately after the suspension was prepared.

Disease incidence and severity (%)

Disease incidence (%) was calculated as the percentage of infected fruits out of the total number of assessed fruits (30 fruits per replicate per sampling time). Disease severity (%) was evaluated based on the infected surface area using a standard rating scale according to Elsayed et al. [3]. No artificial pathogen inoculation was performed; the study relied on natural infection under storage conditions.

Weight loss (%)

Fifteen fruits for each replicate were periodically weighed, and the percentage of weight loss was calculated by the difference between the initial weight and that recorded on sampling date. Rotten fruits were excluded from the same replicate and weighted again and the weight loss calculated according to ensure accuracy.

Total soluble solids (TSS%)

TSS was determined using a hand refractometer; a drop of orange juice was placed on the digital refractometer (Pocket Refractometer PAL 3, ATAGO, Japan), and the reading was recorded and briefly expressed in percentage according to Ahmed et al. [28]. A total of 9 fruits per treatment were used. Measurements were conducted on juice extracted from the edible pulp. For each fruit, two readings were taken and averaged.

Titratable acidity (TA%)

It was determined as citric acid and calculated as percentage according to Paul et al. [29]. Titratable acidity (TA) was determined using juice from 9 fruits per treatment, with measurements conducted in duplicate per sample. Fruit juice was diluted with distilled water at 1:2 ratio by titrating with 0.1 N sodium hydroxide up to pH 8.2, by an automatic titrator (HI 902, HANNA Instrument, USA) and the results were calculated as a percentage of citric acid.

TSS/acid ratio

TSS/acid ratio was recorded by dividing the TSS value by the TA value.

Fruits firmness (N)

Fruit firmness was measured using a digital basic force gauge (Mecmesin, Sterling, Virginia, USA), model BFG 50 N, with a constant penetration speed. Measurements were taken using a cylindrical stainless-steel probe (5 mm diameter). Measurements were performed on whole fruits with the peel intact at two equatorial opposite sides of each fruit after removing a small superficial area of the peel to ensure uniform contact with the pulp tissue. The probe was advanced perpendicularly into the fruit flesh to a penetration depth of 10 mm at a constant speed of 2 mm s⁻1. The maximum force required to penetrate the tissue was recorded and expressed in Newton (N). For each treatment, measurements were taken on a representative sample of fruits, and the mean value was used for statistical analysis [30].

Vitamin C (mg/100 ml juice ascorbic acid)

Vitamin C was measured by titrating fresh juice samples using the AOAC-recommended substrate of 2, 6 -dichlorophenolendophenol until the pink color [31], 9 fruits per treatment, with used to measurements. The results were expressed in percentage.

Fruit peel lightness (L*) and Hue angle (h°)

Peel lightness (L*.) was measured using a model colorimeter (color reader CR-410, Konica Minolta Inc., Japan), according to Labib et al. [32]. Peel Hue angle (h°) was measured using the same instrument outputs (h° = Arctan (b*/a*) according to Habibi et al. [33] 2.10. Enzymes measurements of peel and pulp.

Crude extract

A sample of 1 g peel tissue, randomly collected from 10 fruits per replicate, was homogenized with 20 mM Tris–HCl buffer (pH 7.2) using a laboratory homogenizer. The homogenate was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant obtained was used as the crude enzyme extract. The extract was stored at − 20 °C until analysis of peroxidase (POD) and polyphenol oxidase (PPO) enzyme activities.

Peroxidase determination (POD)

Peroxidase (POD) (EC 1.11.1.7) activity was assessed using the method reported in Miranda et al. [34]. The assay mixture (1 mL) contained 50 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.5), 40 mM guaiacol, 8 mM H₂O₂, and 0.1 mL of crude enzyme extract. Enzyme activity was determined by monitoring the increase in absorbance at 470 nm for 1 min due to the oxidation of guaiacol using a UV–Vis Spectrophotometer (PD-303UV, APEL Co., Ltd., Japan). One unit of POD activity was defined as the amount of enzyme causing an increase of 1.0 in optical density per minute under the assay conditions.

Polyphenoloxidase determination (PPO)

Polyphenoloxidase (PPO) (EC 1.14.18.1) activity was quantified according to Jiang et al. [35]. The reaction mixture, with a final volume of 1 mL, consisted of 50 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.5), 40 mM guaiacol, 8 mM H₂O₂, and 0.1 mL of crude extract. The enzymatic oxidation of guaiacol was monitored by recording the change in absorbance at 470 nm for 1 min UV–Vis Spectrophotometer (PD-303UV, APEL Co., Ltd., Japan). One unit of POD activity corresponded to the amount of enzyme that produced an increase of 1.0 absorbance unit per minute under the standard assay conditions.

Statistical analysis

The results were statistically analysed as a completely randomized design with three replicates by analysis of variance (ANOVA) using the statistical package software SAS (SAS Institute Inc., 2000, Cary, NC., USA). Comparisons between means were made by F-test and the least significant differences (LSD) at P = 5%.

Results

Effect of postharvest PS, SS and STC treatments on green mold incidence and severity

No green mold incidence was detected in any treatment, including the control, during the first 30 days of cold storage, followed by 3 days of shelf life (Table 1). This may be attributed to the combined effect of low-temperature storage and the natural resistance of intact fruit tissues. Disease development occurred only after prolonged storage (40–50 days), during which the control fruit showed a progressive increase in incidence, reaching 16.6% and 23.3%, respectively. This indicates that decay development in untreated fruit is closely associated with extended storage duration and physiological deterioration. Conversely, all treatments significantly reduced disease incidence. STC was the most effective individual treatment (6.7%), followed by PS (6.6%) and SS (13.3%). The combined treatments were more effective. No symptoms of disease were detected in PS + SS + STC. Moreover, PS + STC and SS + STC also maintained a very low incidence (3.3%), confirming that the combined treatments were not only effective in delaying disease appearance but were also protecting fruits from green mold during long-term storage.

Fruits firmness (N)

Fruit firmness decreased gradually with the increase in storage and shelf life period, reaching the lowest value in the control (45.36 N) at the end of the storage period consistent with natural cell wall degradation during storage (Table 4). However, there were no significant differences in fruit firmness among all treatments. PS and SS treatments tended to show slightly lower firmness compared with the control, while combination treatments maintained relatively better firmness retention.

Discussion

The current findings showed that potassium and sodium silicate, and the bioagent Streptomyces corchorusii PHYTSTC7, significantly decreased the incidence and severity of green mold disease in Navel oranges during cold storage and shelf life compared to the control. The mechanisms of action of potassium and sodium silicate (a silicic acid-based treatment) could include cell wall thickening due to silica adhesion, quinone oxidation in exocytosis pathways, and cell lysis induced by silica. Thus, silica exocytosis from fungal interior cells leads to lysis with little or no cytoplasmic content spillage, while the release of the toxin is prevented [36]. The bacteria produce acriflavin, an analog of actinomycin, and due to the production of these toxic metabolites, it can kill the phytopathological fungi [37].

The mode of action of potassium and sodium silicate is believed to create an unfavorable environment for pathogens, inhibit their enzyme activities, or produce bioactive molecules, but it also induces plants to develop stronger cell walls or species responses [24, 38].

The results suggest that a similar activation of the host defense mechanism occurs with Streptomyces corchorusii producing AHL-lactonase. Hence, this Streptomyces isolate can protect citrus against green mold through Streptomyces-mediated ISR in a soilless production system, including environmentally friendly container horticulture [39]. Interaction studies of silicate application with other preventive methods, including other biological or chemical control agents or active substances with a comparable mechanism of action, are of utmost importance as well because of possible activation, enhanced or decreased effectiveness, or feasibility resulting from unsuspected interactions [24].

According to this study, silicate applications are very informative under field conditions as well, as potassium and sodium silicates have been reported to increase decay resistance by improving fruit quality in citrus, making them a good tool for controlling postharvest pathogens. Their multi-mechanistic mode of action is responsible for this inhibition in disease severity while maintaining quality during storage. These compounds regulate the pathogen's growth and development, both directly and indirectly. They prevent spore germination, restrict mycelial development, and breach cell membrane integrity in fungi. In addition, improving fruit resistance may be closely linked to the induction of a series of host defense responses, including antioxidant and pathogenesis-related enzymes that enhance fruit tolerance to postharvest stress. Moreover, silicon deposition in peels likely strengthened cell wall integrity, forming a physical barrier that resists pathogen penetration and colonization [40]. Therefore, the promotion of induced biochemical defenses and enhanced structural resistance may have contributed to the improved storability and decreased decay observed in silicate-treated oranges in this study. These results also correlate well with earlier reports emphasizing silicate compounds as safe and green agents for the management of postharvest diseases [24, 41]. An application of potassium silicate, sodium silicate, or Streptomyces corchorusii can serve as a preventive treatment for green mold disease. This would typically be a nonsynthetic fungicide treatment that is traditionally used. All in all, preventing or controlling green mold disease successfully will reduce the incidence of fruit infestation and increase resistance to both P. digitatum and P. italicum for prolonged periods. Potassium silicate, sodium silicate and Streptomyces corchorusii (usable with certain restrictions) may be applied to citrus fruits without or have little quality impact. Thus, there is no doubt that it helps, and can help, in integrated disease management protocols, given its high impact and its adherence to fruit quality and safety standards, including phytosanitary specifications and mandatory standards [42].

Similar to findings of previous research, the results indicated that with prolonged duration of cold storage and shelf life, weight loss of 6.60 percent at day 50th for PS treatments as well (4.15%) followed by control, is within acceptable limits which suggests fruits were still consumable [43, 44]. Normally, water loss exceeding 10% of the original fruit weight will cause wilting and cytomembranous rupture in senescence and marketability of citrus fruits [45].

This study adopted a natural infection condition rather than an artificial inoculation protocol using spores at a specified concentration to evaluate the inhibitory effect on postharvest fruit diseases; this design aligns with real pathogen exposure scenarios in actual postharvest storage. While natural infection has issues of variation in infection timing and inoculum dose, it can generate real-world application performance data of silicates and biocontrol agents under commercial storage conditions. This study observed that both disease incidence and fruit severity decreased in the treatment groups, indicating that the tested treatments can limit pathogen colonization or slow disease progression. However, this study cannot distinguish whether the effect arises from inhibiting spore germination, restricting colonization, or delaying symptom onset. Therefore, the findings of this study provide only evidence of overall disease suppression and cannot be used to draw conclusions about a precise mechanistic assessment of antifungal activity. Follow-up research needs to conduct controlled artificial inoculation studies using a standardized Penicillium spore suspension to clarify the mechanism of action and improve experimental reproducibility.

Weight loss following harvest is generally attributed to both transpiration and respiration [46–48]. The reduction of water loss by Si-based treatments may be due to the modifications of the epidermal cell layer by extra Si depositions [32] or by covering fruit stomata with an a-Si layer, which had a positive effect on reducing fruit respiration and losses of water [29]. The reduction in titratable acidity levels during storage is attributed to increased membrane permeability, which facilitates the respiration of acids stored in cell vacuoles and their conversion into sugars through specific cellular processes [28, 49]. The results obtained for TSS are in accordance with those previously reported by [32, 49], where the applied silicon and potassium helped increase the synthesis of sugars in the fruit, thereby increasing TSS content. It has been suggested that titratable acidity decreases in fruits as a result of the conversion of acids to sugars during respiration [24, 41].

Firmness is mainly dependent on two factors, cell turgidity and peel thickness, which are influenced by (I) the activity of enzymes degrading cell wall polysaccharides, directly affecting fruit softening. Previous reports demonstrated that under alkaline conditions, pectin methyl esterase activity can be stimulated, resembling the breakdown of methyl esters at the boundary layer between the cell wall and the extracellular matrix to lower peel pH [50, 51]. The increased firmness seen in fruits treated with silicate is likely due to a reduction in the activities of enzymes that degrade cell walls and/or) membranes. Salts, such as potassium silicate or sodium silicate, are applied to change the pH to that of the fruit peel surface. Similar to these results, in line with the reports of PG, PL, and PME, is inhibited activity, respectively [24].

The content of antioxidant compounds such as vitamin C and carotenoids in fruit is maintained by silicon treatments [52, 53], possibly via the reduction of fruit respiration [41]. The ability of silicon to maintain antioxidant capacity and subsequently reduce the risk of abiotic stress has been reported in previous studies [29].

The current experiment revealed that all of the applied treatments resulted in the retention of fruit skin color, as reflected by retaining a higher hue angle and L* value compared to the untreated fruits. Silicate treatments effectively decreased fruit respiration and ethylene production, and noticeably reduced metabolic activity and senescence, thereby retaining fruit color. These results agree with those reported for Anna apples [32]. According to other studies [54, 55], the chromatic functions L*, C*, and h° correlate closely with color change. The delayed skin color darkening may be related to senescence inhibition by decreased fruit respiration [56, 57].

At the end of storage, polyphenol oxidase (PPO) and peroxidase (POD) enzyme activities had significantly decreased. This was observed in citrus fruit (long-term cold storage and shelf life. PPO and POD are also closely associated with stress response and postharvest defense metabolism in citrus fruits like sweet orange (Citrus sinensis) and mandarin (Citrus reticulata). In these conditions, loss of membrane integrity and cellular compartmentalization leads to disruption of enzyme–substrate interaction, which, in turn, results in a sharp decrease in enzymatic activity during prolonged storage. Navel orange fruit has also been shown to exhibit similar patterns during long-term storage, with postharvest treatments and storage duration strongly affecting oxidative metabolism [58].

Citrus fruit POD activity is generally associated with hydrogen peroxide detoxification and stress tolerance mechanisms. For instance, POD activity has either increased or decreased depending on stress intensity and storage duration in ‘Newhall’ navel orange [58], whereas it decreases significantly at late storage due to senescence and metabolic exhaustion. PPO activity in citrus is also low but can be induced under stress conditions, such as mechanical injury or chilling; however, prolonged storage decreases this activity due to enzyme instability and substrate depletion [59]. It has been reported that antioxidant enzyme systems also deteriorate significantly during extended storage in mandarins, which is closely associated with increased lipid peroxidation and loss of membrane integrity [60]. In the case of long storage, these physiological changes lead to decreased enzymatic efficiency and low PPO and POD activities observed in fruits stored over extreme lengths.

PS treatment most likely reduced the developing oxidative stress, thereby decreasing the inactivation rate of enzymes during the early to middle storage period. An improved antioxidant capacity [61] and slower senescence-associated changes in metabolism could lead to more stable enzyme activity profiles under SS treatment than in controls. The STC treatment, reported in postharvest systems for citrus fruit, improves chilling tolerance and maintains membrane stability, which may preserve antioxidant enzyme systems during storage [62]. Apart from assessing alternative postharvest treatments, the focus of this current study is on the possibility that silicate salts can be used in combination with Streptomyces-based biocontrol to increase the effectiveness of disease control under prolonged cold storage conditions. This approach contrasts with much previous work, which has focused on individual treatments; here we evaluated the integrated application of potassium silicate, sodium silicate, and S. corchorusii phytstc7 for combined disease suppression and fruit quality retention. These, respectively, provide applied evidence for the inclusion of mineral salts with biological control agents as an element of a more sustainable postharvest management system for citrus fruit.

Conclusions

During cold storage and shelf life, navel oranges may become infected by several postharvest fungal pathogens, particularly green and blue molds, which are commonly controlled using chemical fungicides. Therefore, there is increasing interest in developing safer alternative treatments. The results of the present study showed that all applied treatments involving potassium and silicon salts, as well as Streptomyces corchorusii phytstc7, reduced green mold incidence during storage compared with the untreated control. No disease symptoms were observed in treated fruits during the first 30 days of cold storage, followed by 3 days of shelf life, whereas the control treatment exhibited disease incidence of 16.6% and 23.3% after 40 and 50 days, respectively. Single treatments with PS, SS, and STC significantly reduced disease incidence to 2.6%, 4.7%, and 2.0%, respectively, at the end of storage. Moreover, the combined treatment (PS + SS + STC) provided the greatest reduction in disease development, with no visible symptoms observed under the conditions of this study, while also maintaining fruit quality attributes during storage. These findings suggest that postharvest dipping in 10⁶ CFU/ml STC alone or in combination with 1% PS and 0.5% SS has potential as an alternative strategy for reducing green and blue mold incidence and maintaining the quality of navel oranges during up to 50 days of cold storage, followed by shelf life.
