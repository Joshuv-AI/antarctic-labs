Mobility-informed metapopulation models predict the spatio-temporal spread of respiratory epidemics across scales
Authors: Aakash Pandey, Lu Zhong, Lior Rennert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPredicting where and when epidemics will spread is essential for guiding early public health responses, yet remains challenging when surveillance data are sparse. Mobility-based approaches offer a promising alternative by leveraging human movement patterns to predict spatial spread without requiring detailed epidemiological data.MethodsWe evaluated the ability of mobility-informed effective distance to predict the relative timing of epidemic onset for three respiratory pathogens: severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), seasonal influenza virus, and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV). We compared effective distances derived from county-level commuting data, airline traffic flows, and a theoretical radiation model within a metapopulation framework. Analyses were conducted at the county level across South Carolina for all three pathogens and at the state level across the United States for influenza and SARS-CoV-2.ResultsEffective distance reliably predicted the relative order of epidemic onset across pathogens and spatial scales. Across all analyses, epidemics arrived earlier in larger, more connected populations, and effective distance outperformed geographic distance as a predictor of onset timing. In metapopulation simulations of a hypothetical respiratory pathogen, radiation-model effective distance was highly correlated with simulated arrival times (Spearman’s ρ = 0.95, 95% CI [0.90 to 0.98]), substantially outperforming commuting-based effective distance (ρ = 0.54, 95% CI [0.24 to 0.75]). In empirical analyses at the county level in South Carolina, radiation-model effective distance consistently outperformed commuting-based measures for the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) first wave (ρ = 0.62 vs. 0.39), the 2022–2023 influenza season (ρ = 0.57 vs. 0.44), and the 2022–2023 RSV season (ρ = 0.70 vs. 0.47). At the state level, airline-based effective distance predicted arrival timing for COVID-19 (ρ = 0.46, 95% CI [0.19 to 0.66]) and onset timing for the 2024/2025 influenza season (ρ = 0.45, 95% CI [0.14 to 0.66]).ConclusionsMobility-informed models capture consistent spatiotemporal patterns of epidemic spread across pathogens and scales. These findings support their use as scalable, data-efficient tools for early outbreak preparedness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s44263-026-00293-8.

Background

Predicting when and where infectious diseases will spread is critical for effective public health response. During the early stages of an epidemic, surveillance data are sparse and uncertainty is high [1, 2], yet health authorities must make rapid decisions about deploying limited resources, including personnel, diagnostic tools, pharmaceutical and non-pharmaceutical interventions. Strategic deployment of these constrained resources is essential to reduce transmission, mitigate morbidity and mortality, and prevent waste [3, 4]. Mathematical models can support this process by providing early insight into transmission dynamics and guiding intervention strategies [5, 6].

Metapopulation models are a widely used framework for studying the spatiotemporal spread of infectious diseases [7–10]. Central to these models is the incorporation of human mobility, which governs the spatial spread and temporal progression of outbreaks [11–14]. When parameterized with empirical or theoretical mobility data, metapopulation models can forecast disease arrival times and transmission trajectories across regions. For instance, the Global Epidemic and Mobility (GLEaM) model integrated airline and commuting data to predict the international and subnational spread of the 2009 H1N1 pandemic [15–17]. Similar approaches have reconstructed the spatial diffusion of HIV and SARS [18, 19]. Meslé et al. [20] provide a broad review of airline-data-driven infectious disease models. More recently, mobility-informed models have been central to understanding coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) dynamics. Yigma [21] used US air traffic data to assess the COVID-19 importation risk at the county level. Similarly, Kraemer et al. [22] leveraged mobile phone-derived human mobility data to track and predict the spread of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) lineage B.1.1.7 in the UK, while Pei et al. [23] applied commuting data to model the spatial propagation and differential impacts of intervention timing on COVID-19 within the United States. In addition, Hamada et al. [24] found a strong correlation between mobility patterns and COVID-19 early case growth rates at the US county level. Together, these studies demonstrate that mobility-based models can produce reliable probabilistic forecasts of spatial spread, including the relative order of outbreak emergence across regions.

Respiratory infectious diseases, including COVID-19, seasonal influenza, and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) infection, remain a substantial public health burden within the United States and across South Carolina [25]. For example, the 2024/2025 influenza season recorded the highest cumulative hospitalization rate since 2010–2011 [26], with influenza-related infections accounting for approximately 13% of all emergency department visits at peak. RSV infection accounted for roughly 1.35% of emergency department (ED) visits at its peak in South Carolina [27], and COVID-19 alone resulted in over 360,000 hospitalizations statewide between March 2020 and December 2023 [28]. The ability to anticipate the geographic spread of these pathogens is particularly important in South Carolina, where pronounced disparities in healthcare infrastructure between rural and urban areas amplify the impact of delayed response on vulnerable populations [29].

In this study, we develop a mobility-informed metapopulation framework to examine the spatiotemporal spread of respiratory pathogens at the county level within South Carolina and at the state level across the United States. Using county-level commuting data, we estimate the relative timing of COVID-19 arrival during the first epidemic wave and the seasonal spread of influenza and RSV across multiple years. We ask whether mobility data, including commuting flows and airline traffic volumes, can predict the relative timing of epidemic onset at different spatial scales. We show that effective distance [7], a network-based metric derived from mobility data, reconstructs the geographic progression of disease spread with notable accuracy. We further demonstrate that the radiation model [30], a parameter-free theoretical mobility model, performs comparably to and in several cases outperforms empirical commuting data for spatial prediction. These findings highlight the potential of mobility-driven approaches as low-data, scalable tools for anticipating epidemic trajectories and supporting proactive public health planning.

Methods

Data sources

We assembled epidemiological, mobility, and demographic data from multiple public and administrative sources at both state and county levels.

State-level data

COVID-19 case counts by state were obtained from the New York Times cumulative case database, which provides daily cumulative confirmed cases at the state level across the United States [31]. State-level influenza surveillance data were obtained from the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) FluView platform, which reports weekly estimates of influenza-like illness activity [32]. State-level air traffic data were obtained from the U.S. Department of Transportation Consumer Airfare Report, which reports passenger volumes for the top 1,000 origin–destination airport pairs ranked by traffic, restricted to markets averaging at least 10 passengers per day [33]. These data capture true origin-to-destination passenger flows, including itineraries with intermediate connections, rather than individual nonstop flight segments. The data are reported quarterly; we aggregated them to annual totals. To construct state-level mobility networks, we mapped each airport to its corresponding state and summed passenger flows accordingly. We used 2019 air traffic data for analysis of COVID-19 spread and 2024 data for influenza.

County-level data

County population estimates and commuting flows were obtained from the U.S. Census Bureau [34]. Commuting data are drawn from the 2016–2020 five-year American Community Survey (ACS) and represent average county-to-county workplace commuting flows over that period. These estimates reflect long-term commuting patterns and do not capture seasonal or year-to-year variation. The key variables used were residence county (origin), workplace county (destination), and flow volume.

Hospitalization data

County-level inpatient hospitalization records for COVID-19, influenza, and RSV were obtained from the South Carolina Revenue and Fiscal Affairs Office [35] for 2019–2023. The dataset contains de-identified, line-level records from participating healthcare facilities across the state. Records were originally reported at daily resolution and ZIP Code Tabulation Area (ZCTA) spatial scale; we aggregated them to weekly intervals at the county level. Although the dataset spans 2019–2023, pandemic-era control measures (masking, social distancing) substantially suppressed influenza and RSV activity following the 2019–2020 season (Supplementary Material 1: Fig. S1). In the 2020/2021 influenza season, for instance, most counties reported no or very few hospitalizations. We therefore restricted our analysis to seasons that exhibited a typical epidemic curve for a seasonal respiratory pathogen. Specifically, we used data for the COVID-19 first wave (Dec 15, 2019 – Sept 10, 2020), the influenza 2019/2020 and 2022/2023 seasons, and the RSV 2019/2020 and 2022/2023 seasons.

Estimating epidemic onset time

Surveillance data are often incomplete, particularly at fine geographic scales and in rural areas with limited public health infrastructure. Case reporting near the epidemic peak, however, tends to be more reliable for inferring overall epidemic dynamics. We therefore used a retrospective approach to estimate the timing of epidemic onset in each county, based on the analytical solution of the susceptible–infectious–recovered (SIR) model [36]. This method infers the likely time of epidemic seeding from the observed peak infection time under an assumption of exponential early growth governed by the basic reproduction number (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}_{0}$$\end{document}).

We first smoothed weekly county-level hospitalization counts using a LOESS (locally estimated scatterplot smoothing) function with a fixed span of 0.3 across all counties and diseases. This span was chosen to suppress short-term reporting fluctuations while preserving seasonal-scale epidemic trends and enabling robust identification of peak timing. From each smoothed curve, we identified the date and magnitude of peak hospitalization for each county. To convert peak hospitalizations to estimated peak infections, we applied disease-specific case-to-hospitalization ratios. For example, although the case-to-hospitalization ratio varies during different waves of COVID-19 [37], early estimates before 2021 show that about 5% of estimated infections were hospitalized [38]. Accordingly, we scaled the peak weekly COVID-19 hospitalization counts by a factor of 20 to estimate the corresponding weekly incident infections. Influenza and RSV hospitalization counts were scaled similarly. The case-to-hospitalization ratio varies by age and season for both diseases [39, 40]. For example, data on influenza-related hospitalizations from 2010 to 2023 shows the case-to-hospitalization ratio varies from 58 to 93 [40]. We used the average (75) ratio to estimate peak infection. RSV hospitalization is strongly age-dependent; however, for simplicity, we assume the ratio to be around 20 for the general population [41]. Weekly incident infections estimated in this way were then multiplied by the average duration of infection to approximate the number of currently infectious individuals (prevalence) at peak. Mobility model and effective distance

Effective distance [7] is a dimensionless, network-based measure of connectivity between locations derived from mobility probabilities. We computed effective distances between South Carolina counties using two approaches: a theoretical radiation model based solely on population sizes and distances, and empirical data (commuting data and air traffic data). For the theoretical model, the effective distance was derived from the relative mobility flow grounded in the classical radiation model framework [30]. The radiation model assumes that individuals select destinations based on the availability of opportunities, with travel probability depending on the populations of the origin and destination and the intervening population representing competing opportunities closer to the origin. The full radiation model scales flows by the total number of commuters originating from each location, but this information may not be available in all settings. We therefore used the unscaled radiation model kernel, which requires only population sizes and spatial configuration, making it applicable in data-limited contexts where commuting volumes are unknown.

Estimating spatial spread from effective distance and epidemic timing

To assess whether effective distance predicts the spatial progression of epidemic spread, we quantified the association between effective distance from a putative epidemic origin and the relative timing of epidemic activity across counties. This analysis was conducted using two complementary timing metrics: retrospectively estimated epidemic onset times derived from the SIR-based approach described above, and observed hospitalization onset times defined as the first week in which the weekly hospitalization count exceeded one case. Retrospectively estimated onset times offer an advantage over the timing of the first few observed cases because they are anchored to the epidemic peak, which is less sensitive to sporadic early hospitalizations, variation in surveillance capacity across counties, and stochastic noise in early case detection. Observed hospitalization onset times, while more susceptible to these sources of variability, represent the type of signal most readily available during the early phase of an outbreak.

For each disease and season, we identified the county with the earliest estimated or observed onset time as the putative origin of spread. Effective distances were computed from the origin county to all other counties using the commuting data-derived effective distance metric and radiation model (Eq. 5), yielding distances \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{eff,j}$$\end{document}. The 95% confidence interval was obtained from the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution.

Metapopulation model

To evaluate the ability of the effective distance framework to predict spatial epidemic spread, we simulated transmission of a hypothetical acute respiratory infection across South Carolina counties using a discrete-time, stochastic metapopulation SIR model. In the primary analysis, the epidemic was seeded with a single infectious individual in Greenville County; all other counties began fully susceptible, and no additional importations were permitted after initialization. The model was simulated for 100 daily time steps across 1,000 independent stochastic realizations from identical initial conditions. For each realization, epidemic onset in a given county was defined as the first time step at which the number of infectious individuals exceeded zero. Onset times were averaged across realizations to obtain the mean first-arrival time for each county. Because Greenville County was the seeding location, effective distances from Greenville to all other counties were computed independently from the radiation model and commuting-based networks. The association between these effective distances and mean simulated arrival times was then assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation. To examine the sensitivity of this approach to misspecification of the epidemic origin, we conducted an additional set of simulations in which the epidemic was simultaneously seeded in both Greenville and Charleston counties. Effective distances were still computed assuming a single origin in Greenville County. Comparing the resulting correlations to those from the single-seeding scenario allowed us to assess the robustness of the effective distance framework when the true origin is not a single location or is only partially identified. The code for the metapopulation model and analyses is publicly available in a GitHub repository at: https://github.com/apandey101/mobility-metapop-manuscript.git [47].

Disease arrival and epidemic onset times

We distinguish between two temporal metrics used to characterize spatial spread: disease arrival time and epidemic onset time. Disease arrival time refers to the point at which the first infected individual appears in a location. Because transmission is stochastic, an imported case may fail to generate sustained local spread and therefore may not lead to an epidemic. Epidemic onset time, by contrast, refers to the point at which sustained local transmission becomes established. In this study, the epidemic onset time is estimated retrospectively from the epidemic curve using the SIR-based approach described above, in which the timing of peak incidence is used to infer an effective start time for local epidemic growth, rather than the precise moment of first introduction.

For empirical analyses based on hospitalization data, we also defined an observed hospitalization onset time as the first week in which the weekly count exceeded one case. Because influenza and RSV can produce sporadic hospitalizations outside the primary epidemic period, we applied a seasonal restriction to avoid misattributing isolated off-season cases to the start of a new epidemic wave. Examination of the data confirmed that the main influenza and RSV seasons consistently began after late July; we therefore defined hospitalization onset as the first week after July 30 in which the count exceeded one case. In stochastic simulations, the exact timing of each introduction is directly observable. We defined the simulated arrival time as the interval between seeding at the epidemic origin and the appearance of the first case in a given county.

Our county-level analyses employed two complementary measures of epidemic onset. We primarily focused on estimated epidemic onset times, calculated retrospectively from peak incidence using the SIR-based approach described above, as a proof of concept that effective distance can predict the relative order of epidemic onset. However, in practice, peak incidence data are only available after an epidemic has progressed. To test whether effective distance is also predictive using data available early in an epidemic, we additionally evaluated observed hospitalization onset times, defined as described above. These represent the type of surveillance signal most readily accessible during the early stages of an outbreak, when spatial forecasts would be most valuable for public health preparedness. For COVID-19 state-level surveillance data, we used the date of the first reported case, and in simulation experiments, we also report the simulated arrival time as defined above.

Results

Epidemics arrive earlier in larger populations, followed by gradual diffusion to smaller areas

Our retrospectively estimated epidemic onset times showed moderate to strong agreement with observed hospitalization onset dates, with Spearman’s ρ values of 0.64 (95% CI [0.47 to 0.77]) for COVID-19 (Fig. 1a), 0.61 (95% CI [0.32 to 0.82]) for the 2022–2023 influenza season (Fig. 1b), and 0.71 (95% CI [0.43 to 0.87]) for RSV (Supplementary Material 1: Fig. S2a). This concordance supports the use of the SIR-based retrospective approach as a reasonable estimator of epidemic onset timing.

Across all three pathogens, estimated epidemic onset dates were negatively correlated with county population size, indicating that epidemics consistently arrived earlier in larger, more connected counties before spreading to less populated areas (Fig. 1c and d). This pattern is consistent with the expectation that highly connected population centers serve as early hubs for epidemic seeding and amplification. The relationship was strongest for the COVID-19 first wave (ρ = −0.75, 95% CI [− 0.88 to − 0.56]; Fig. 1c) and the 2022–2023 RSV season (ρ = −0.73, 95% CI [− 0.86 to − 0.51]; Supplementary Material 1: Fig. S2c), and was also evident for the 2019–2020 influenza season (ρ = −0.45, 95% CI [− 0.66 to − 0.18]; Supplementary Material 1: Fig. S2d) and the 2022–2023 influenza season (ρ = −0.48, 95% CI [− 0.73 to − 0.15]; Fig. 1d). A similar relationship was observed at the state level for COVID-19 (ρ = −0.59, 95% CI [− 0.77 to − 0.35]) and the 2023–2024 influenza season (ρ = −0.48, 95% CI [− 0.22 to − 0.67]; Supplementary Material 1: Fig. S5). Notably, no such correlation was observed for peak hospitalization times (Supplementary Material 1: Fig. S4), suggesting that population size influences when epidemics establish but not necessarily when they peak.

Effective distance from radiation model predicts the spatial pattern of epidemic onset

In simulations using a stochastic metapopulation SIR model with disease introduction in Greenville County, the mean time to first infection in each county was highly correlated with effective distance from Greenville (Spearman’s ρ = 0.95, 95% CI [0.90 to 0.98]; Fig. 2a). To assess sensitivity to origin misspecification, we repeated the simulations with simultaneous seeding in both Greenville and Charleston counties but computed effective distances assuming Greenville as the sole origin. The correlation was reduced but remained moderate (ρ = 0.50, 95% CI [0.25 to 0.69]; Supplementary Material 1: Fig. S7), indicating that effective distance retains predictive value even when the true seeding pattern is only partially captured. Consistent patterns emerged when we compared radiation-model effective distances with retrospectively estimated onset times from hospitalization data. For the COVID-19 first wave, onset dates were positively correlated with effective distance from Greenville County (ρ = 0.62, 95% CI [0.35 to 0.80]; Fig. 2b). Similar relationships were found for the 2022–2023 influenza season (ρ = 0.57, 95% CI [0.32 to 0.74]; Fig. 2c) and the 2022–2023 RSV season (ρ = 0.70, 95% CI [0.50 to 0.83]; Fig. 2d), with Charleston County assumed as the initial seeding location for both.

Positive correlations were observed across all diseases and seasons (Supplementary Material 1: Fig. S6, Table S1). Commuting-based effective distance also predicts epidemic onset, though less strongly

To assess whether commuting data yield similar spatial predictions, we repeated the analyses using effective distances derived from county-level commuting flows. In simulations seeded in Greenville County, the correlation between commuting-based effective distance and mean time to first infection was moderate (Spearman’s ρ = 0.54, 95% CI [0.24 to 0.75]; Fig. 3a), notably weaker than the corresponding radiation model result (ρ = 0.95). Commuting-based effective distance was positively correlated with retrospectively estimated onset dates for all three pathogens, though correlations were consistently lower than those from the radiation model. For the COVID-19 first wave, the correlation with effective distance from Greenville County was ρ = 0.39 (95% CI [0.10 to 0.64]; Fig. 3b, Supplementary Material 1: Table S2). With Charleston County as the assumed origin, correlations were ρ = 0.44 (95% CI [0.12 to 0.68]) for the 2022–2023 influenza season and ρ = 0.47 (95% CI [0.17 to 0.70]) for the 2022–2023 RSV season. Positive correlations were also observed between commuting-based effective distance and observed hospitalization onset dates (Supplementary Material 1: Fig. S9, Table S1). Correlations were ρ = 0.37 (95% CI [0.10 to 0.62]) for the COVID-19 first wave, and for the 2022–2023 season, ρ = 0.44 (95% CI [0.19 to 0.63]) for influenza and ρ = 0.48 (95% CI [0.17 to 0.72]) for RSV.

Relative timing of epidemic onset correlates with effective distance across seasons

To assess whether the spatial pattern of epidemic spread is consistent across seasons, we examined the relative timing of epidemic onset across counties, defined as the difference between each county’s onset date and the earliest onset date observed in South Carolina during a given season. Estimated onset timing was positively associated with effective distance across all disease–season combinations (Fig. 4, Supplementary Material 1: Fig. S10, Table S2). For influenza, correlations were moderate in 2019–2020 (radiation model: ρ = 0.36, 95% CI [0.05 to 0.63]; commuting: ρ = 0.33, 95% CI [− 0.01 to 0.59]) and strengthened in 2022–2023 for both mobility metrics. RSV showed minimal association in 2019–2020 (radiation: ρ = 0.30, 95% CI [− 0.11 to 0.64]; commuting: ρ = 0.31, 95% CI [− 0.07 to 0.63]), but strong correlations emerged in 2022–2023, particularly under the radiation model. Across all settings, radiation-based effective distances yielded correlations comparable to or exceeding those from commuting data (Supplementary Material 1: Table S2).

Airline data-based effective distance predicts state-level epidemic timing

To assess whether the effective distance framework generalizes to a broader spatial scale and an alternative mobility data source, we evaluated airline traffic networks as predictors of state-level epidemic timing across the United States. Using 2019 air traffic data among major airports, we computed effective distances from Washington state, where the first U.S. COVID-19 case was reported, to all other states. Effective distance was positively correlated with the date of the first reported COVID-19 case in each state during the initial pandemic wave (Spearman’s ρ = 0.46, 95% CI [0.19 to 0.66]; Fig. 5a, Supplementary Material 1: Table S3), indicating that states with stronger air travel connectivity to Washington experienced earlier case detection. We applied the same approach to the 2024–2025 influenza season using 2024 air traffic data. Effective distance from Texas, identified as the earliest-onset state, was positively correlated with estimated epidemic onset dates across states (ρ = 0.45, 95% CI [0.14 to 0.66]; Fig. 5b).

Discussion

Respiratory infectious diseases, including COVID-19, seasonal influenza, and RSV, continue to impose substantial burdens on healthcare systems across the United States [26]. In this study, we developed a metapopulation framework that leverages empirical mobility data and a theoretical mobility model to estimate the relative timing of epidemic onset for these pathogens at multiple spatial scales. We demonstrated this framework by predicting county-level epidemic progression within South Carolina and state-level epidemic timing across the United States. The ability to estimate the relative order of epidemic arrival across locations without requiring detailed epidemiological parameters makes this approach particularly suited to early-phase response planning, when data are limited and time-sensitive decisions about resource allocation are critical.

Our findings highlight the potential utility of mobility-informed models as tools for strategic public health decision-making, particularly in forecasting the early geographic trajectory of infectious diseases [7, 11, 14, 15, 48]. Consistent with established patterns observed in previous pandemics and seasonal epidemics, we find that infections usually arrive first in regions with high population density and extensive connectivity, typically large urban counties or states with major transportation hubs, before diffusing outward to smaller, less connected areas [49–52]. This directional flow from population centers to peripheral regions was consistent across multiple respiratory pathogens and seasons, suggesting a generalized mechanism of spatial disease spread. Even when initial seeding events occur in sparsely populated counties, infections tend to propagate toward urban centers, which then act as secondary hubs that amplify transmission and facilitate further spatial diffusion. The robustness of these patterns across disease systems underscores the value of mobility-informed models for anticipating the spread of novel pathogens. In the absence of precise epidemiological parameters such as transmission rates or incubation periods, these frameworks can still provide actionable insight into the relative timing or ordering of an emerging outbreak, supporting early situational awareness and more informed allocation of public health resources [7, 48].

The effectiveness of simple analytical mobility models, such as gravity and radiation models, in capturing large-scale human movement patterns reflects a fundamental regularity and predictability in how people move [30, 53–56]. This predictability can be harnessed to model the spatial diffusion of infectious diseases, particularly during the early stages of an outbreak when real-time epidemiological data are sparse. Brockmann and Helbing demonstrated this by using global air traffic data and effective distance to reconstruct the spatial progression of the SARS outbreak and the 2009 H1N1 pandemic [7]. Our results extend this work by showing that effective distance enables accurate prediction of relative epidemic onset timing at both county and state levels across three respiratory pathogens. These findings also align with recent work by Babazadeh Maghsoodlo et al., who reported a strong linear relationship between effective distance and “overtaking time,” the point at which local transmission dynamics dominate over spatial seeding [48]. Their study, consistent with ours, suggests that effective distance is predictive not only of epidemic onset but potentially of other epidemic phases as well. Together, these findings support the broader applicability of effective distance as a unifying metric for spatiotemporal epidemic forecasting.

Variation in predictive accuracy across disease systems highlights the importance of epidemiological context and transmission dynamics. The presence of multiple seeding events violates the assumption of a single-source introduction, leading to spatial decoherence and reduced predictive accuracy. Our simulation results confirm this: when infections were seeded at multiple locations, the correlation between effective distance and arrival time weakened under the assumption of a single epidemic origin. Similar dynamics can occur with seasonal respiratory diseases such as influenza and RSV [57]. At the county level, concurrent epidemic onset across multiple densely populated counties, driven by local transmission or residual endemic circulation, can generate overlapping waves that complicate inference of directional spread. Seasonal resurgence may arise from amplification of existing low-level transmission rather than new external introductions, and repeated or temporally staggered introductions can further blur the relationship between effective distance and epidemic timing, particularly in endemic or multi-strain contexts. Nonetheless, when surveillance systems can identify and distinguish newly emergent strains, the framework is likely to perform more reliably, as strain-specific spread more closely resembles a classic invasion process with a well-defined geographic origin. A recent genomic analysis of influenza sequences collected during 2014–2023 supports this view, finding that commuting patterns combined with lineage competition were strong predictors of seasonal influenza epidemics in the United States [58].

While our framework offers a flexible and data-efficient approach to predicting spatial patterns of disease spread, several limitations should be noted. The county-level commuting flows and annual airline traffic data used here are static and do not capture temporal changes in movement. More dynamic and high-resolution mobility data, such as mobile phone-derived estimates or weekly transportation patterns, could improve predictions during rapidly evolving outbreaks. However, previous studies have shown that even high-resolution mobility proxies may not accurately capture epidemic dynamics if they do not reflect the relevant spatial and temporal scales of transmission [59–62]. The commuting flows used in our analysis are derived from the 2016–2020 American Community Survey five-year estimates and represent average workplace commuting patterns, capturing only one component of human mobility. National summaries from the 2022 ACS release indicate substantial changes in commuting patterns relative to pre-pandemic years [63], but county-level origin–destination data reflecting these changes are not yet publicly available. Our analysis therefore relies on pre-pandemic commuting patterns, which may introduce additional uncertainty when analyzing pandemic-period spread. The radiation model outperformed commuting data in our analysis, possibly because it captures broader movement patterns beyond workplace commuting alone. At the same time, theoretical mobility models are themselves statistical approximations of human movement and may not always accurately represent transmission-relevant contact patterns.

Our analyses rely on retrospectively estimated epidemic onset dates, which are useful for validating the modeling framework but do not directly reflect real-time decision-making contexts. Prospective forecasting would require robust near-real-time surveillance systems capable of promptly detecting local epidemic activity. To address this, we also examined observed hospitalization onset dates to assess whether effective distance could predict the relative timing of epidemic spread using signals available earlier in an outbreak. However, observed onset dates may be sensitive to differences in surveillance quality and healthcare access across counties, potentially leading to earlier detection in larger or better-resourced areas. This detection bias is an important limitation for prospective inference, particularly in rural counties with more limited surveillance capacity. Finally, while our framework is based on a simple SIR model, it could be extended to incorporate additional epidemiological complexities, including age-structured transmission, vaccination dynamics, and hospitalization burden. Such extensions would improve utility not only for spatial prediction but also for evaluating intervention strategies and estimating healthcare demand.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates that metapopulation models informed by human mobility, whether derived from empirical commuting and air traffic data or from theoretical frameworks such as the radiation model, can provide robust predictions of the relative timing of respiratory disease spread across spatial scales. Effective distance serves as a powerful, low-data metric for anticipating the geographic progression of pathogens such as COVID-19, influenza, and RSV, and its predictive value is consistent across pathogens and seasons. As global connectivity expands and novel pathogens continue to emerge, incorporating scalable, mobility-based models into public health infrastructure alongside enhanced surveillance systems can strengthen epidemic preparedness and support timely, informed response.
