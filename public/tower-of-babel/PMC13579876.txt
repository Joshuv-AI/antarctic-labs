Lifestyle habits, gender differences, and their association with sleep and mental health among Syrian medical residents
Authors: Mohammad Basheer Alameer, Jameel Soqia, Mohammed Al-shafie, Lujain Nahas, Jad Samaan, Laila Yakoub-Agha, Abdallah Hanbli, Nadim Zerez, Ahmad Almouselli, M. Eyad Chatty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMedical residency is a demanding phase that often compromises sleep quality and mental health. This study aimed to assess the association between lifestyle factors—specifically smoking, caffeinated beverage intake, alcohol consumption, and body mass index—with sleep quality, anxiety symptoms, and depressive symptoms among medical residents in Syria.MethodsThis manuscript reports a secondary analysis of the dataset collected and described by Soqia et al. This multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted across 22 hospitals in Syria, enrolling 2,047 medical residents. Validated instruments were used to assess outcomes: the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index for sleep quality, the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 for depressive symptoms, and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 scale for anxiety symptoms. Logistic regression analyses were conducted to investigate the relationships between lifestyle habits and mental health outcomes.ResultsSmoking was significantly associated with poor sleep quality (odds ratio 1.33), depressive symptoms (odds ratio 1.36), and anxiety symptoms (odds ratio 1.26). Among female residents, coffee and alcohol consumption were linked to increased anxiety symptoms, with odds ratios of 1.39 and 2.56, respectively. An underweight body mass index was associated with a lower likelihood of poor sleep quality (odds ratio 0.17), depressive symptoms (odds ratio 0.33), and anxiety symptoms (odds ratio 0.51) in female participants.ConclusionsLifestyle habits such as smoking, caffeine and alcohol intake, and body weight status were significantly associated with sleep quality and mental health among Syrian medical residents. Interventions promoting smoking cessation, sleep hygiene, and moderated consumption of stimulants and alcohol may improve well-being and professional performance in this population.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-026-08321-5.

Background

Medical residents are often confronted with numerous challenges that can significantly impact their mental well-being and quality of sleep, as medical training is an exceptionally demanding and high-stress field. These challenges include a heavy workload, academic demands that leave little time for relaxation, fatigue, overwhelming patient care responsibilities, and long overnight shifts [1].

Research has shown that medical residents often adopt various strategies to cope with stress or enhance productivity during prolonged work hours, such as consuming caffeine and energy drinks along with dietary changes and exercise [2]. Moreover, mental disorders such as Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD) and Major Depressive Disorder (MDD), along with poor sleep quality, can be affected by habits like smoking and certain drinks, namely coffee and tea [3–5]. Additionally, the demanding nature of their work often hinders a healthy lifestyle, which is associated with these disorders [2, 6].

While these challenges affect medical residents globally, the situation is exacerbated in developing countries like Syria, where medical residents may face even greater hardships. The destruction of healthcare facilities and the mass migration of healthcare workers seeking safety have resulted in an intensified workload and heightened mental strain for the remaining residents. These circumstances have further compromised their quality of sleep and mental health [7, 8]. Faced with these challenges, Syrian medical residents often adopt comparable coping strategies, which may lead to the development of unhealthy habits. These habits may exacerbate poor sleep quality and stress-related conditions.

Existing research lacks a focus on how specific coping habits are uniquely associated with Syrian medical residents’ mental health and sleep quality. This study aims to fill this gap by exploring this relationship, marking an important step towards understanding and addressing their unique challenges.

Methods

Study design, participants, and sampling

The present study is a secondary analysis of participant data originally collected by Soqia et al. in a multicenter cross-sectional survey of Syrian medical residents [8].

This multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted across 22 major hospitals in Syria. A total of 2,047 medical residents from diverse specialties and training years were enrolled. Data collection occurred between October 27, 2023, and April 4, 2024, utilizing face-to-face interviews with a structured, validated questionnaire to ensure completeness and minimize missing responses. Participation was open to all eligible residents.

The questionnaire was distributed to residents of all years of different specialties in the following hospitals: 1-National University Hospital (Damascus University), 2-Al-Mouwasat University Hospital (Damascus University), 3-Children University Hospital (Damascus University), 4-University Hospital of Obstetrics and Gynecology in Damascus University, 5-Al-Bairouni University Hospital (Damascus University), 6-Cardiosurgery University Center (Damascus University), 7-Damascus Hospital (Damascus general hospital), 8- Ibn Al-Nafees Hospital (Damascus general hospital), 9-Al-Zahrawi Hospital (Damascus general hospital), 10-Surgical Kidney Hospital (Damascus general hospital), 11-Lattakia University Hospital (Lattakia), 12-13-14-National Hospital of Latakia (Lattakia general hospital includes 3 major hospitals), 15-National Hospital of Hama (Hama general hospital), 16-Homs University Hospital (Homs), 17-Aleppo University Hospital (Aleppo University), 18-Hospital of Obstetrics and Gynecology (Aleppo University), 19-Aleppo University Hospital of Cardiovascular Surgery (Aleppo University), 20-Al-Razi Hospital (Aleppo general hospital), 21-Ibn Rushd Hospital (Aleppo general hospital), and 22- Aleppo’s Children’s Hospital (Aleppo general hospital).

This study sought to ensure comprehensive regional representation across Syria. Hospitals in the northern region were identified as Hospitals 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, and 22. The western region was represented by Hospitals 11, 12, 13, and 14. The eastern region was covered by Hospital 15, while the southern region included Hospitals 1 through 10. Finally, Hospital 16 represented the central region of Syria.

Sample calculation

Damascus University comprises six affiliated teaching hospitals. Notably, medical residents are institutionally affiliated with Damascus University rather than individual hospitals and routinely rotate across facilities as part of their training curricula. To maintain representativeness, we aggregated the sample at the institutional level rather than hospital-specific calculations. Measures

The survey was divided into four sections (Supplementary file 1).

Demographics

Age, gender, body mass index (BMI) calculated from self-reported height and weight, alcohol drinking, coffee and tea drinking, energy drinks consumption, and smoking habits. Participants were asked to choose which drinks they usually consume from multiple options. The Body Mass Index (BMI) has been categorized into seven distinct ranges as follows: severely underweight (below 16.0), underweight (less than 18.5), normal weight (18.5 to 24.9), overweight (25 to 29.9), obesity class I (30 to 34.9), obesity class II (35 to 39.9), and obesity class III (40 or greater). BMI was calculated as Kg/m2 [9].

Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI)

The PSQI is a self-report questionnaire that evaluates sleep quality over a one-month time interval and is validated in Arabic [8].

The instrument consists of 19 items, which are combined into seven components: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleeping medication, and daytime dysfunction. Each component is scored on a scale from 0 to 3, with 3 indicating severe difficulties. The sum of these components yields a global PSQI score ranging from 0 to 21, where higher scores denote poorer sleep quality. A conservative cut-off of 5 was used in this study to indicate poor sleep quality [10]. The present study obtained a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.93 for the PSQI, suggesting strong internal reliability.

Depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms among participants were assessed using the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9). Participants rated the frequency of depression-related symptoms experienced over the preceding two weeks on a 4-point Likert scale (0 = “not at all” to 4 = “nearly every day”). A score of ≥ 10, aligning with established clinical thresholds, was used to define clinically significant depressive symptoms. This cutoff is consistent with validated recommendations for PHQ-9 interpretation. The Arabic version was validated in a previous study and showed good psychometric propertie [11]. The present study obtained a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.89 for the PHQ-9, suggesting strong internal reliability.

Anxiety symptoms

Anxiety symptoms were assessed using the 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD-7) scale, a well-validated instrument with established reliability and validity in general population studies [12]. This tool has been used in previous research to measure generalized anxiety disorder in individuals 12. The GAD-7 items are scored on a 4-point Likert scale (0 = “not at all” to 3 = “nearly every day”), yielding a total score range of 0–21. To optimize diagnostic accuracy, a cutoff score of ≥ 10 was applied to identify clinically significant anxiety symptoms, as recommended in prior validation studies [12]. The Arabic version was validated in a previous study and showed good psychometric properties [11]. The present study obtained a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.93 for the GAD-7 scale, suggesting strong internal reliability.

The time needed for each interview was approximately 25 min.

Ethical statement

This study adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethical Committee of Damascus University (Approval ID: MD130623-115; Date: June 13, 2023). Written informed consent was secured from all participants. Before enrollment, participants were briefed on the study’s objectives and methodology. Participation was voluntary, anonymous, and conducted without coercion, with confidentiality ensured throughout the research process.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics involved using frequency and percentage for categorical variables, along with mean and standard deviation for continuous variables. A t-test was utilized to analyze differences regarding BMI between males and females, and a chi-square test was conducted to determine the difference in smoking and consumption of coffee, tea, alcohol, and energy drinks between males and females. Binary logistic regression was done to determine predictors of poor sleep quality, depressive symptoms, and anxiety symptoms among all participants, as well as separately for males and females, using the same reference categories for categorical variables. The assumptions of the binary logistic regression model were verified, where nominal and ordinal independent variables, which have mutually exclusive and exhaustive categories, were utilized. The absence of multicollinearity among the variables was confirmed through Spearman’s correlation test (absence of multicollinearity is indicated by the lack of a strong correlation between two variables, the strength of the correlations ranged between [-0.27–0.24]). The absence of multicollinearity was confirmed by linear regression testing, where: tolerance values ranged from 0.922 to 0.997, and VIF values ranged from 1.003 to 1.085. A p-value of < 0.05 was used to determine the statistical significance. The statistical analysis was done using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA).

Results

Demographic information

Data was collected from 2047 doctors in specialist training from 22 major university hospitals from different governorates. The percentage of females was 53.2%, and males were 46.8%, with an average age of 26.36 years, while the average body mass index was 23.74. Most participants were non-smokers (79.3%), and most did not drink alcohol (96.5%) or energy drinks (91.5%). In contrast, approximately two-thirds of participants drink coffee (71.8%) and tea (69.9%). Full details appear in Table 2.

Discussion

What we eat or drink is a collection of behaviors that result from the interplay between various internal (cognitive, biological, psychological…) and external factors (social environment, culture, economic variables…) [13]. In this study, we discussed the interplay of these behaviors and factors and their association with poor sleep quality and mental health disorders, represented by depressive symptoms and anxiety symptoms among Syrian medical residents.

Although previous studies from high-income countries report weight gain among residents due to stress-related eating and reduced physical activity [14, 15], our sample showed a mean BMI within the healthy range. This may reflect contextual factors unique to Syria, where long shifts, food scarcity, and high physical demands within understaffed hospitals reduce opportunities for regular meals. Meanwhile, almost 20% of participants in our sample were smokers, which is similar to results reported in a Syrian study [16] and a systematic review [17]. Chronic stress is known to influence smoking behavior. Individuals may start smoking, maintain, or relapse as a response to stress [18, 19]. Despite physicians’ knowledge about the health risks of smoking, some healthcare professionals choose to smoke to relieve stress [20]. A comprehensive explanation for these two behaviors is still elusive, but there is great importance in having doctors who are conscious about their health, as they are more likely to recommend a healthier lifestyle for their patients regarding weight gain, smoking, or other potentially harmful behavior [21].

Tea and coffee consumption was prominent in our study. The use of coffee as a stimulant to tackle fatigue is embedded in the medical culture around the world [22], while the use of tea is not as thoroughly documented. A previous study from Syria reported high use of coffee and tea among medical students to stay awake during exam preparations [23]. Moreover, tea and coffee consumption are a big part of the Syrian culture, explaining this result.

Almost every participant in our study didn’t drink alcohol or energy drinks. The religious background of the Syrian population explains the scarcity of alcohol use. In contrast, studies from neighboring countries like Jordan and Saudi Arabia reported high usage of energy drinks among medical residents [24, 25]. The lack of local energy drink manufacturers and retailers in Syria resulted in a lack of energy drink consumption among Syrians, including medical residents.

Females smoked and drank tea less than males in our sample. The cultural aspect is important to understand this result, as smoking is viewed as “unladylike” in the Arab culture [26]. Moreover, females tend to be more considerate of their health and more engaged in preventive measures [27]. Additionally, in other studies, females were more likely to drink tea than males [28]. Gender is a prominent biological factor that can shape habits, as it modulates aspects such as perception, consumption, and reaction to interventions [29]. The cultural aspect may have played a role in the result as well, as certain beverages could be stereotyped based on gender, therefore creating a difference in consumption intention that is statistically significant [30].

Smoking was associated with poor sleep quality, depressive symptoms, and anxiety symptoms. This connection was reported in multiple studies [3, 4], but whether smoking was a result or a reason for these disorders is debatable, as some authors suggest a bidirectional association, while others support one direction over the other [31]. Many theories explain this association, such as neural changes mediated by acetylcholine and catecholamine that make smoking initiation or relapse more probable among individuals diagnosed with these disorders, changes in brain structures directly linked to increased risk of psychiatric disorders, oxidative stress and inflammation induced by smoking, the stimulant effect of nicotine, and its nightly withdrawals [32–34]. Forming a clearer understanding of these mechanisms could be a cornerstone for future recommendations to reduce the prevalence of these disorders.

In addition, our results showed that Smoking in males was associated with higher odds of depressive symptoms, while smoking for the female gender did not exhibit the same importance. While our result might differ from literature that states that females are more affected by smoking on a biological level, despite the abundance of male over female smokers [35], other studies emphasized that both genders suffer from the repercussions of nicotine [36] and that this relationship is affected by many factors and is dose-dependent. As the size of smoking side effects is undoubtedly linked to the heaviness of smoking [32, 37]. This may be the reason why our results were only significant for males.

Coffee and alcohol consumption were associated with anxiety symptoms in our sample, among females in particular, but not with depressive symptoms or poor sleep quality. Although literature agrees that caffeinated drinks have a measurable effect on mood and sleep quality, which might result in depressive symptoms and low quality of sleep [38]. This relationship is dependent on dosage and frequency of consumption, which can be further emphasized with other research mentioning that moderate consumption has no negative effect on mental health, and so, our results are still coherent with the literature [39]. Furthermore, we found that the most common mental health disorder related to caffeine overconsumption was GAD and that the female gender was more affected by caffeine’s physiological effects, further solidifying our argument [40]. A meta-analysis concluded that caffeine intake, especially over 400 mg per day, increases anxiety by blocking adenosine receptors, reducing blood flow in the cerebrum, stimulating noradrenaline release, and modifying local release of dopamine, all of which led to anxiety [41].

On the other hand, alcohol use has been found to make its users less tolerant to distress and display higher levels of anxiety, especially among women, but the mechanism of this interaction is still unclear [42]. Although mood disorder is the most common mental health disorder associated with alcohol and can take on the form of MDD symptoms, this relationship is studied based on co-occurrence only [43], which might validate our findings, as alcohol was studied as a predictor. Similar literature explored the relationship between alcohol consumption and GAD, MDD, and poor sleep quality, signifying a measurable effect in terms of alcohol being indeed an important predictor for these disorders. However, this correlation is heavily moderated by dosage and frequency of intake [44]. Differences between males and females regarding coffee and alcohol effects on studied disorders can be explained by hormonal fluctuations, differences in metabolism, and the higher prevalence of anxiety among females [40, 42].

The finding that underweight female residents had lower odds of poor sleep quality, anxiety, and depressive symptoms contrasts with literature linking low BMI to psychological distress [45, 46]. Several contextual explanations may account for this pattern. In Syrian society, lower body weight in women is often socially reinforced, potentially reducing body-image-related stress. Additionally, underweight women who remain in demanding residency programs may represent a resilient subgroup with different coping strategies. Reverse causality is also possible: women with better mental health may maintain lower weight due to more stable routines. Finally, BMI may not accurately reflect nutritional status or health in this population, and reporting biases cannot be excluded. Our other results can also be traced in previous literature. Regarding depression, evidence seems to be inconsistent, with some studies depicting a relationship between higher BMIs and depression, while other studies found the contrast to be the case [47]. In terms of GAD, previous studies reported that extreme BMI values, whether high or low, were associated with increased anxiety [48, 49].

Still, we would like to emphasize that sleep quality, depression, and GAD are the sum of different factors and can be influenced by various extrinsic and intrinsic variables [50]. Some of which may even display synergistic effects and should be studied together [51]. However, the study of certain variables solely may suggest valid results despite differing from past studies, taking into consideration the particularity of each population and the different exposures they are subjected to. This further highlights the complexity of these disorders and the correlations they have with different predictors and urges for tailored interventions to help prevent the escalation of mental health disorders’ risk among healthcare providers.

Conclusions

The aforementioned results highlight the importance of adopting a healthy lifestyle, especially among medical residents. Understandably, residents use some coping strategies to tackle fatigue, but it’s important to spread awareness regarding the outcomes of these strategies. Procedures like smoking cessation, the improvement of sleep quality, and limiting caffeine and alcohol intake can help greatly in improving the mental health and overall life quality of medical residents, which would hopefully result in improved medical training outcomes and ultimately better health care for patients now and in the future.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The quantity and frequency of alcohol and caffeine intake were not collected, which restricts interpretation of dose-dependent effects. All lifestyle variables, height, and weight were self-reported, introducing the possibility of recall or reporting bias. In addition, some lifestyle exposures—particularly alcohol and energy drinks—had very small numbers in one or both sex groups, which limited statistical power for detecting sex-specific effects. For this reason, we did not include formal sex × lifestyle interaction terms and instead used sex-stratified models. Future studies with larger and more balanced samples should examine these interactions directly.
