Liftings of Sobolev maps into closed Riemannian manifolds via double coverings and minimal connections relative to planar sets, with an application to ferronematics
Giacomo Canevari, Federico Luigi Dipasquale, Bianca Stroffolini

Abstract
We consider Sobolev maps from a planar domain into a closed Riemannian manifold and their BV liftings via a double covering of the target. We establish a sharp lower bound on the jump length of the lifting, expressed in terms of a geometric quantity: the minimal connection, relative to the domain, of the non-orientable singularities. As an application, we analyse minimisers of a two-dimensional model of ferronematics under “mixed” boundary conditions — that is, Dirichlet conditions for the liquid crystal order parameter and Neumann conditions for the magnetisation vector. Keywords: Lifting problem, minimal connections, topological singularities, Ginzburg-Landau functional, Allen-Cahn equation. 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: 35Q56 ⋅ \cdot 76A15 ⋅ \cdot 49Q15 ⋅ \cdot 26B30



Let
\mathscr{N}
,
\mathscr{E}
be closed Riemannian manifolds and
\Pi\colon\mathscr{E}\to\mathscr{N}
a covering map. The lifting problem asks whether a given map

u=\Pi\circ v
for some lifting
v\colon\Omega\to\mathscr{E}
with comparable regularity. While the problem is classical for continuous maps on simply connected domains, its analysis becomes substantially more delicate in weaker regularity settings, such as Sobolev or BV spaces.
The most studied case is the covering map
\Pi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
given by
\Pi(\theta):=e^{i\theta}
, which arises naturally, e.g., in the Ginzburg–Landau theory of superconductors. The analysis of this case was initiated in
[
15

14
]

[
17

in which the lifting problem always has a positive answer (see also
[
19
]
for a comprehensive discussion).

Subsequent contributions extended these results to Besov spaces
[
39
]
and to more general target manifolds
[
13
,
8
,
42
,
40

For liftings through the exponential map

[
30

and later revisited by Davila and Ignat

Generalisations to a wider class of covering maps

In this paper, we restrict our attention to maps defined on planar domains,
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and focus on double coverings
\Pi\colon\mathscr{E}\to\mathscr{N}
(see Section
1.1

\Pi\colon\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}

\Pi(z):=z^{2}

Under suitable conditions ensuring in-plane molecular alignment, the state of a thin nematic layer can be represented by a map from a physical domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}

In this example, the set
\mathscr{N}
is a smooth, compact manifold, diffeomorphic to the unit circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}\subseteq\mathbb{C}

is a vector field

(1)

\mathbf{Q}(x)=\sqrt{2}\left(\mathbf{n}(x)\otimes\mathbf{n}(x)-\frac{\mathbf{I}}{2}\right)

\mathbf{Q}
corresponding to the positive eigenvalue at almost every point.
From a mathematical viewpoint, and up to composition with a diffeomorphism
\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathscr{N}

\mathbf{M}
of
\mathbf{Q}
is a selection of a square root of
\mathbf{Q}

In the next result, we denote by
\mathrm{J}_{v}
the jump set of a map
v\in\SBV(\Omega,\,\mathbb{R}^{2})
, and we write
\mathscr{H}^{1}
for the
1

A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we write
A=B\mod\mathscr{H}^{1}
if and only if
\mathscr{H}^{1}(A\setminus B)+\mathscr{H}^{1}(B\setminus A)=0
. Our statement applies to maps
u\colon\Omega\to\mathscr{N}
which are locally
W^{1,2}
away from a finite set of points singularities, which may be orientable — meaning, roughly speaking, that
u
admits a Sobolev lifting in a neighbourhood of that singularity — or not. For a more precise definition of orientable and non-orientable singularities, see Section
1.1
. However, only the non-orientable singularities play a rôle in the main estimate (
3
).

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathscr{N}
,
\mathscr{E}
be closed, smooth Riemannian manifolds and
\Pi\colon\mathscr{E}\to\mathscr{N}

a smooth double covering map.
Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded, simply connected domain
of class
C^{2}
and let
a_{1}
, …,
a_{p}
,

b_{1}
, …,
b_{r}
be distinct points in
\Omega
. Let

u\in W^{1,1}(\Omega,\,\mathscr{N})\cap W^{1,2}_{\loc}(\Omega\setminus\{a_{1},\,\ldots,a_{p},\,b_{1},\,\ldots,\,b_{r}\},\,\mathscr{N})

be a map with a non-orientable singularity at each
a_{j}

and an orientable singularity at each
b_{h}
.
If
v\in\SBV(\Omega,\,\mathscr{E})
is a lifting
for
\mathscr{E}
via
\Pi
, then

(3)

\mathscr{H}^{1}(\mathrm{J}_{v})\geq\mathbb{L}^{\Omega}(a_{1},\,\ldots,\,a_{p}).

The equality holds if and only if there exists a minimal
connection
\{L_{1},\,\ldots,\,L_{q}\}

for
\{a_{1},\,\ldots,a_{p}\}
relative to
\Omega
such that

\mathrm{J}_{v}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{q}L_{j}\mod\mathscr{H}^{1}
.

Theorem
1
generalises Proposition A.1 in
[
23
]
, which holds under the additional assumptions that
\Omega
is convex and
v
is continuous in a neighbourhood of
\partial\Omega
. Heuristically, Theorem
1
follows from the topological observation that boundary points of the jump set
\mathrm{J}_{v}
of a lifting can only be non-orientable singularities of
u
or points in
\partial\Omega
. However, defining precisely the “boundary of the jump set
\mathrm{J}_{v}
” requires care, as the standard notion of topological boundary is inadequate and rectifiable sets, without additional structure, do not come with a natural boundary operator. A more suitable notion in this context is the boundary modulo two in the sense of currents, as used in
[
23
]
. Here, we avoid mentioning explicitly the theory of currents and instead rely on a result by Ambrosio, Caselles, Masnou, and Morel
[
3
]
on the structure of planar sets of finite perimeter.
However, the arguments still rely implicitly on the homological structure of currents modulo two, as becomes most apparent in Lemma
1.7
.

Here, however, we focus on an application to
ferronematics
.
Ferronematics are composite materials obtained by
suspending magnetic nanoparticles in a nematic liquid crystal host
[
20
,
38
]
.
We adopt the modelling approach of
[
16
]
,
based on two order parameters.
The orientation of the liquid crystal molecules
is described by the Landau-de Gennes
\mathbf{Q}
-tensor,
which is a map from the physical domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}

to the space
\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}
of
2\times 2
, symmetric, real matrices
with trace equal to zero.
Nonzero values of
\mathbf{Q}
correspond to configurations
with a well-defined molecular alignment,
while
\mathbf{Q}=0
indicates an isotropic state,
where all the directions of molecular alignment are equally likely.
The distribution of magnetic nanoparticles is
described by the average magnetisation vector,

\mathbf{M}\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
The system is described by the free-energy functional

(4)

\mathscr{F}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M}):=\int_{G}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left|\nabla\mathbf{Q}\right|^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left|\nabla\mathbf{M}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{M})\right)\mathrm{d}x,

where
\varepsilon
is a non-dimensional parameter.
The interaction between the liquid crystal
host and the magnetic inclusions is encoded in the potential
f_{\varepsilon}
,
which takes the form

(5)

f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{M}):=\frac{1}{4}\left(1-\left|\mathbf{Q}\right|^{2}\right)^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\left(1-\left|\mathbf{M}\right|^{2}\right)^{2}-\varepsilon\beta\,\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{M}+\kappa_{\varepsilon}.

Here
\beta>0
is given and
\kappa_{\varepsilon}
is an additive
constant, depending only on
\varepsilon
and
\beta
,
chosen so that
\inf f_{\varepsilon}=0
.
Potential energies similar to (
5
)
arise from suitable homogenisation limits
[
21
]
.
For positive values of
\beta
, the potential
f_{\varepsilon}

favours alignment between the liquid crystal
molecules and the magnetisation vector.
Indeed, the potential
f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q},,\mathbf{M})
is minimised
by pairs
(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{pot}},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{pot}})

that satisfy the conditions

(6)

|\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{pot}}|=\lambda_{\varepsilon,\beta},\qquad\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{pot}}=\sqrt{2}s_{\varepsilon,\beta}\left(\frac{\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{pot}}\otimes\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{pot}}}{\lambda_{\varepsilon,\beta}^{2}}-\frac{\mathbf{I}}{2}\right)

where
\mathbf{I}
is the
2\times 2
identity matrix
and
\lambda_{\varepsilon,\beta}
,
s_{\varepsilon,\beta}
are
positive constants, uniquely determined by
\varepsilon
and
\beta
,
such that

\lambda_{\varepsilon,\beta}\to\left(\sqrt{2}\beta+1\right)^{1/2},\qquad s_{\varepsilon,\beta}\to 1

as
\varepsilon\to 0
(see
[
23
, Lemma B.2]
).
We study the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
, which is physically
relevant in the “large domain” regime
(i.e., the size of the domain is much larger than
the typical correlation length for the liquid crystal molecules).
The asymptotic analysis of Ginzburg-Landau-type functionals shows that the energy of the
\mathbf{Q}
-component concentrates at finitely many points,
which correspond to
non-orientable
singularities
of the limiting liquid crystal configuration.
In particular, the
\mathbf{Q}
-component of minimisers
converges to a matrix-valued map that does not
admit a continuous orthonormal eigenframe.
However, as the coupling promotes alignment between
\mathbf{M}

and the eigenvectors of
\mathbf{Q}
, the energy for the
\mathbf{M}
-component
is expected to concentrate along singular lines, corresponding to jumps in the eigenvector frame.

The asymptotic analysis of minimisers for
\mathscr{F}_{\varepsilon}
,
subject to Dirichlet boundary conditions for
both
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
and
\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}
,
has been carried out in the paper
[
23
]
.
We will consider here mixed boundary conditions,
i.e. Dirichlet boundary conditions for
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}

and homogeneous Neumann boundary
conditions for
\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}
:

(7)

\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}=\mathbf{Q}_{{\mathrm{bd}}},\quad\partial_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}=0\qquad\textrm{on }\partial\Omega,

where
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
is the exterior unit normal to
\partial\Omega
.
We then assume the boundary datum
\mathbf{Q}_{{\mathrm{bd}}}

(does not depend on
\varepsilon
and) takes the form

(8)

\mathbf{Q}_{{\mathrm{bd}}}=\sqrt{2}\left(\mathbf{n}_{{\mathrm{bd}}}\otimes\mathbf{n}_{{\mathrm{bd}}}-\frac{\mathbf{I}}{2}\right)\qquad\textrm{on }\partial\Omega,

for some map
\mathbf{n}_{{\mathrm{bd}}}\in C^{2}(\partial\Omega,\,\mathbb{R}^{2})

which, a priori, is completely independent of the
values of
\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}
on the boundary.
We assume that the domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
is bounded,
simply connected and of class
C^{2}
, so that

\mathbf{n}_{{\mathrm{bd}}}\colon\partial\Omega\to\mathbb{S}^{1}

has a well-defined topological degree, which we call

.

1
Liftings and relative minimal connections

1.1
Double coverings of Riemannian manifolds

First, let us recall the definition and basic properties
of (double) covering maps. We refer the reader to,
e.g.,
[
32
]
for more details.
Let
\mathscr{N}
,
\mathscr{E}
be two closed (i.e., compact and without boundary)
smooth Riemannian manifolds. A smooth map
\Pi\colon\mathscr{E}\to\mathscr{N}

is called a double covering of
\mathscr{N}

if and only if for any
w\in\mathscr{N}
there exists
an open neighbourhood
V
of
w
and two disjoint
open sets
U_{1}
,
U_{2}\subseteq\mathscr{E}

such that
\Pi^{-1}(V)=U_{1}\cup U_{2}
and the restrictions

\Pi_{|U_{1}}\colon U_{1}\to V
,
\Pi_{|U_{2}}\colon U_{2}\to V
are
isometric diffeomorphisms. It follows from this definition
that the inverse image
\Pi^{-1}(w)
of any point
w\in\mathscr{N}

contains exactly two point of
\mathscr{E}
.

Remark 1.1
.

Since
\mathscr{N}
is compact, it is possible
to choose
V
,
U_{1}
,
U_{2}
in the definition of covering
to be geodesic balls with uniform radius. More precisely,
let
B_{0}:=B_{\delta_{0}}^{\mathscr{N}}(w)
be the geodesic open ball in
\mathscr{N}

of centre
w
and radius
\delta_{0}
.
There exists
\delta_{0}>0
small enough that,
for any
w\in\mathscr{N}
, the inverse image
\Pi^{-1}(B_{0})

consists exactly of two disjoint geodesic balls in
\mathscr{E}
,
say
B_{1}=B_{\delta_{0}}^{\mathscr{E}}(z_{1})
and
B_{2}:=B_{\delta_{0}}^{\mathscr{E}}(z_{2})
,
and the restrictions
\Pi_{|B_{1}}\colon B_{1}\to B_{0}
,

\Pi_{|B_{2}}\colon B_{2}\to B_{0}
are isometric diffeomorphisms.
This follows from the definition of double covering,
reasoning e.g. by contradiction and using the compactness of
\mathscr{N}
.

The prototypical example of a double covering is the
natural quotient map
\Pi\colon\mathbb{S}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\mathrm{P}^{n}
,
whose restriction to any open set
U\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{n}
strictly
contained in a half-sphere is a one-to-one isometry.
When
n=1
, the real projective line
\mathbb{R}\mathrm{P}^{1}

is (isometrically) diffeomorphic to the unit circle and,
up to composition with this diffeomorphism,

\Pi
can be identified with the map
defined by
\Pi(z):=z^{2}
for
z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
.

For any double covering
\Pi\colon\mathscr{E}\to\mathscr{N}
, there exists a
unique isometric diffeomorphism
\Phi\colon\mathscr{E}\to\mathscr{E}
such that

(1.1)

\Pi(\Phi(z))=z,\qquad\Phi(z)\neq z\qquad\textrm{for all }z\in\mathscr{E}.

(For instance, in case
\Pi\colon\mathbb{S}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\mathrm{P}^{n}
,

\Phi\colon\mathbb{S}^{n}\to\mathbb{S}^{n}
is the antipodal map,
\Phi(z):=-z
.)
For each
z\in\mathscr{E}
,
\Phi(z)
is defined as the unique
point
y\in\mathscr{E}
such that
y\neq z
and
\Pi(y)=\Pi(z)
.
The definition of
\Pi
implies that
\Phi
is a local isometry
and that
\Phi(\Phi(z))=z
for each
z\in\mathscr{E}
, so

\Phi
is indeed a (isometric, involutive) diffeomorphism.
For technical reasons, at some points of the proof
we will also need an auxiliary function, which we introduce
in the following lemma.

Lemma 1.1
.

There exists a Lipschitz function

\Xi\colon\mathscr{E}\times\mathscr{E}\to\mathbb{R}
that satisfies
the following properties for any
z_{1}\in\mathscr{E}
,
z_{2}\in\mathscr{E}
:

(1.2)

\displaystyle\Xi(z_{1},\,z_{2})=1\qquad

\displaystyle\textrm{if and only if }z_{1}=z_{2}

(1.3)

\displaystyle\Xi(z_{1},\,z_{2})=-1\qquad

\displaystyle\textrm{if and only if }z_{1}=\Phi(z_{2})

(1.4)

\displaystyle\Xi(z_{1},\,z_{2})

\displaystyle=\Xi(\Phi(z_{1}),\,\Phi(z_{2})).

For instance, when
\Pi
is the quotient map
\mathbb{S}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\mathrm{P}^{n}
,
one such function
\Xi\colon\mathbb{S}^{n}\to\mathbb{S}^{n}
is simply given
by
\Xi(z_{1},\,z_{2}):=z_{1}\cdot z_{2}

Proof of Lemma
1.1
.

For a sufficiently small
\delta_{0}>0
,
the compact set
K:=\{(z_{1},\,z_{2})\in\mathscr{E}\times\mathscr{E}\colon\dist_{\mathscr{N}}(\Pi(z_{1}),\,\Pi(z_{2}))\leq\delta_{0}\}

has two connected components:
one is
K_{+}:=\{(z_{1},\,z_{2})\in\mathscr{E}\times\mathscr{E}\colon\dist_{\mathscr{E}}(z_{1},\,z_{2})\leq\delta_{0}\}
,
the other one is

K_{-}:=\{(z_{1},\,z_{2})\in\mathscr{E}\times\mathscr{E}\colon\dist_{\mathscr{E}}(\Phi(z_{1}),\,z_{2})\leq\delta_{0}\}
.
(This is a consequence of Remark

.)
Let

for

and
\sigma(z_{1},\,z_{2}):=-1
for
(z_{1},\,z_{2})\in K_{-}

We define

\Xi(z_{1},\,z_{2}):=\sigma(z_{1},\,z_{2})\,\left(1-\frac{1}{\delta_{0}}\dist_{\mathscr{E}}(\Pi(z_{1}),\,\Pi(z_{2}))\right)_{+}

for all

where

It is not difficult to check that this

Euclidean embeddings and function spaces.

By Nash isometric embedding, we can identify
\mathscr{E}
,

\mathscr{N}
as closed submanifolds of some Euclidean spaces,
say
\mathscr{E}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
and
\mathscr{N}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Since
\mathscr{N}
,
\mathscr{E}
are both compact,
the geodesic distance functions
\dist_{\mathscr{N}}
,
\dist_{\mathscr{E}}

induced by the Riemannian metrics
are equivalent to the Euclidean one.
In other words, there exists a constant
C
such that

(1.5)

\displaystyle\left|w_{1}-w_{2}\right|\leq\dist_{\mathscr{N}}(w_{1},\,w_{2})\leq C\left|w_{1}-w_{2}\right|,

(1.6)

\displaystyle\left|z_{1}-w_{2}\right|\leq\dist_{\mathscr{E}}(z_{1},\,z_{2})\leq C\left|z_{1}-z_{2}\right|,

for any
w_{1}\in\mathscr{N}
,
w_{2}\in\mathscr{N}
,
z_{1}\in\mathscr{E}
,
z_{2}\in\mathscr{E}
.
These inequalities can be proved, e.g., reasoning by contradiction,
using the smoothness and compactness of
\mathscr{N}
,
\mathscr{E}
.
Thanks to (
1.5
), (
1.6
), we can extend
\Pi
,
\Phi
,
\Xi
to Lipschitz
maps
\Pi\colon\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\Phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
,

\Xi\colon\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{R}

(denoted with the same symbols, by abuse of notation).
We define function spaces such as

\BV(\Omega,\,\mathscr{N}):=\left\{u\in\BV(\Omega,\,\mathbb{R}^{m})\colon u(x)\in\mathscr{N}\textrm{ for a.e. }x\in\Omega\right\}\!,

which is a metric space with the distance induced by
the norm in
\BV(\Omega,\,\mathscr{N})
.
We define other function spaces, such as

\BV(\Omega,\,\mathscr{E})
or
W^{1,p}(\Omega,\,\mathscr{N})

for instance, in a completely analogous way.
Choosing a different embedding, say,

\mathscr{N}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m^{\prime}}

results in a metric space
\BV(\Omega,\,\mathscr{N}^{\prime})

that is equivalent to
\BV(\Omega,\,\mathscr{N})
,
in the sense that there exists a Lipschitz map

\BV(\Omega,\,\mathscr{N})\to\BV(\Omega,\,\mathscr{N}^{\prime})

with Lipschitz inverse. Indeed, an isometric
diffeomorphism
\psi\colon\mathscr{N}\to\mathscr{N}^{\prime}

is also a Lipschitz map with Lipschitz inverse
with respect to the Euclidean distance in
the ambient spaces
\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\mathbb{R}^{m^{\prime}}
,
because of (
1.5
).
Therefore, composition with
\psi
induces
a Lipschitz map
\BV(\Omega,\,\mathscr{N})\to\BV(\Omega,\,\mathscr{N}^{\prime})

with Lipschitz inverse, by the chain rule
(see e.g.
[
4
, Theorem 3.96]
).
It would also be possible to
define
\BV(\Omega\,\mathscr{N})
,
\BV(\Omega,\,\mathscr{E})
in
a completely intrinsic way, without any reference to a
Euclidean embedding (see for instance the metric space
approach in
[
2
]
),
but we will not need such generality for our purposes.

Orientable and non-orientable singularities.

We recall the notion of orientable
and non-orientable singularities of a map
u\colon\Omega\to\mathscr{N}
.
Suppose, first, that
u
is continuous except for a finite number
of discontinuity points
p_{1},\,\ldots,\,p_{n}
.
Given a continuous map

\gamma\colon\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\Omega\setminus\{p_{1},\,\ldots,\,p_{n}\}
,
we will say that
u
is orientable on
\gamma(\mathbb{S}^{1})

if there exists a continuous map
v\colon\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\mathscr{E}

such that
u(\gamma(\omega))=\Pi(v(\omega))
for all
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
.
The homotopy lifting property (see
[
32
, Proposition 1.30]
)
guarantees that, for any closed annulus

\overline{B}_{R}(a)\setminus B_{r}(a)\subseteq\Omega\setminus\{p_{1},\,\ldots,\,p_{n}\}
,

u
is either orientable on both
\partial B_{R}(a)

and
\partial B_{r}(a)
, or it is non-orientable on both.
As a consequence, for any singular point
p_{i}
,

u
is either orientable on
\partial B_{\rho}(p_{i})

for all
\rho>0
small enough or it is non-orientable
on
\partial B_{\rho}(p_{i})
for all
\rho>0
small enough.
Accordingly, we will say that
p_{i}
is an orientable
singularity of
u
or a non-orientable one.
The homotopy lifting property also implies that orientability
is preserved by uniform convergence: if
\{u^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

is a sequence of smooth maps that converges uniformly on the image
of
\gamma\colon\mathbb{S}^{1}\to\Omega\setminus\{p_{1},\,\ldots,\,p_{n}\}
,
then for sufficiently large values of
k
,

u^{k}
is orientable on
\gamma(\mathbb{S}^{1})
if and only if
the limit map
u
is.

The case

u\in W^{1,2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega\setminus\{p_{1},\,\ldots,\,p_{n}\},\,\mathscr{N})

follows from the previous discussion,
thanks to the following density result
(see
[
44
, Proposition p. 267]
).
Given a closed set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we write
C^{\infty}(K,\,\mathscr{N})

for the set of functions
K\to\mathscr{N}
that admit a smooth
extension
U\to\mathscr{N}
on some open neighbourhood
U
of
K
.

Proposition 1.2

(
[
44
]
)
.

For any bounded, Lipschitz domain
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
the set
C^{\infty}(\overline{D},\,\mathscr{N})

is dense in
W^{1,2}(D,\,\mathscr{N})
.

Now, if

u\in W^{1,2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega\setminus\{p_{1},\,\ldots,\,p_{n}\},\,\mathscr{N})

then
u
is continuous on the circle
\partial B_{\rho}(p_{i})

for every
i
and almost every value of the radius
\rho>0
,
by Fubini theorem and Sobolev embedding

W^{1,2}(\partial B_{\rho}(p_{i}))\hookrightarrow C^{0}(\partial B_{\rho}(p_{i}))
.
By Proposition
1.2
and a diagonal argument,
there exists a sequence of smooth
functions
u^{k}\colon\Omega\setminus\{p_{1},\,\ldots,\,p_{n}\}\to\mathscr{N}

that converges to
u
in

W^{1,2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega\setminus\{p_{1},\,\ldots,\,p_{n}\})
.
Fubini theorem and Sobolev embeddings again give
uniform convergence
u^{k}\to u
on
\partial B_{\rho}(p_{i})

for all index
i
and a.e. radius
\rho
.
As a consequence,
u
is orientable on
\partial B_{\rho}(p_{i})

if and only if
u^{k}
, for sufficiently large
k
, is.
The homotopy lifting property shows that,
for each index
i
and almost every
\rho_{1}
,
\rho_{2}
small enough,

u
is orientable on
\partial B_{\rho_{1}}(p_{i})

if and only if it is on
\partial B_{\rho_{2}}(p_{i})
.
Therefore, it still makes sense to say whether
p_{i}
is an orientable
singularity of
u
or not.

Sobolev liftings.

As explained in the introduction, there are several results
available on existence (or non-existence) of liftings
for Sobolev maps. Nevertheless, we recall here some
of the existing arguments, because they will be useful to us later on.

Lemma 1.3
.

Let
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded, Lipschitz domain,

u\in W^{1,2}(D,\,\mathscr{N})
, and
u^{k}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{D},\,\mathscr{N})

be such that
u^{k}\rightharpoonup u
weakly in
W^{1,2}(D,\,\mathscr{N})
and pointwise a.e.
For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
v^{k}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{D},\,\mathscr{E})

be a lifting of
u^{k}
. Then, we can extract
a (non-relabelled) subsequence so that

v^{k}\rightharpoonup v
weakly in
W^{1,2}(D)
,
where
v
is a lifting of
u
.

Proof.

By definition, the covering map
\Pi

is a local isometry, i.e. its differential at any point
x\in\mathscr{E}

is an isometry between the tangent spaces
of
\mathscr{E}
and
\mathscr{N}
at
x
and
\Pi(x)
.
Since directional derivatives of a map
w\in W^{1,2}(D,\,\mathscr{E})

are tangent to
\mathscr{E}
at almost every point,
the chain rule implies

(1.7)

\left|\nabla w\right|=\left|\nabla(\Pi\circ w)\right|\qquad\textrm{a.e.\penalty\ in }\Omega\ \textrm{ for any }w\in W^{1,2}(D,\,\mathscr{E}).

In particular,
|\nabla u^{k}|=|\nabla v^{k}|

for all
k
. Therefore, the sequence
(v^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

is bounded in
W^{1,2}(D,\,\mathscr{E})
and we can extract a subsequence
that converges weakly in
W^{1,2}(D)
,
strongly in
L^{2}(D)
and almost everywhere to some
limit
v\in W^{1,2}(D,\,\mathbb{R}^{\ell})
. By taking the limit
pointwise a.e., we deduce that
v(x)\in\mathscr{E}
and
u(x)=\Pi(v(x))

for a.e.
x\in\Omega
.
∎

Combining Lemma
1.3
with Proposition
1.2

and classical existence results for smooth liftings
(see e.g.
[
32
, Proposition 1.33]
),
we obtain existence results for
W^{1,2}
-liftings.
For example, any map
u\in W^{1,2}(D,\,\mathscr{N})
in a bounded,
Lipschitz, simply connected domain
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
admits a
lifting
v\in W^{1,2}(D,\,\mathscr{E})
. This fact remains true
in higher-dimensional simply connected domains
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
but the proof is more delicate; see e.g.
[
13
]
.
We also have uniqueness of the lifting in
W^{1,2}
,
up to composition with
\Phi
.

Lemma 1.4
.

Let
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded, Lipschitz domain.
Let
v_{1}\in W^{1,2}(D,\,\mathscr{E})
,
v_{2}\in W^{1,2}(D,\,\mathscr{E})

be liftings of the same map

.
Then, either
v_{1}=v_{2}
a.e. in
\Omega
or
v_{1}=\Phi(v_{2})
a.e. in
\Omega
.

Proof.

Let
w:=\Xi(v_{1},\,v_{2})
, where
\Xi
is the function
given by Lemma
1.1
.
Since
\Xi
is Lipschitz-continuous, the chain rule implies
that
w\in W^{1,2}(\Omega,\,\mathbb{R})
. Moreover,
for almost every
x\in\Omega
we have
\Pi(v_{1}(x))=\Pi(v_{2}(x))
,
and hence

).
Since
\{1,\,-1\}
is a discrete set,
it follows that

must be constant.
The lemma follows, again by (
1.2
), (
1.3
).

1.3
Liftings and sets of finite perimeter

Given a set
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we will denote by
\partial^{*}E

the essential boundary of
E
, which is defined as

\partial^{*}E:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\colon\limsup_{\rho\to 0}\frac{\left|B_{\rho}(x)\cap E\right|}{\rho^{2}}>0,\ \limsup_{\rho\to 0}\frac{\left|B_{\rho}(x)\setminus E\right|}{\rho^{2}}>0\right\}\!.

Given a measurable subset
E\subseteq\Omega
,
we define its perimeter relative to
\Omega
as

P(E,\,\Omega):=\sup\left\{\int_{\Omega}\div\mathbf{V}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\colon\mathbf{V}\in C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{c}}(\Omega,\,\mathbb{R}^{2}),\ \left\|\mathbf{V}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq 1\right\}<+\infty.

For smooth sets,
\partial^{*}E=\partial E
, and the perimeter
P(E,\,\Omega)

coincides with the surface area of
\Omega\cap\partial E
,
by the Gauss-Green theorem.
More generally, a result of Federer
[
28
, 4.5.11]

implies that
P(E,\,\Omega)=\mathscr{H}^{1}(\Omega\cap\partial^{*}E)
.
If the set
\Omega
has smooth, or even just Lipschitz,
boundary and
E
has finite perimeter relative to
\Omega
,
then
E
has finite perimeter in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
(This follows from the definition of perimeter,
via a localisation argument based on cut-off functions
that approximate the indicator function of
\Omega
.)
Since in the sequel we will be only be interested
in subsets of a regular domain
\Omega
,
we will simply write that a set ‘has finite perimeter’
and will not specify whether the perimeter
is taken relative to
\Omega
or
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Given two sets
A
,
B
, we will denote by
A\triangle B:=(A\setminus B)\cup(B\setminus A)
their symmetric difference.
The symmetric difference is compatible with
the essential boundary, as made precise
by the following lemma.

Lemma 1.7
.

If
A
,
B
are sets of finite perimeter,
then
A\triangle B
has finite perimeter and

(1.8)

\partial^{*}(A\triangle B)=\partial^{*}A\triangle\partial^{*}B\mod\mathscr{H}^{1}.

In this proof and later on, we will denote
by
\mathrm{J}_{w}
the jump set of a function

and by

,

on either side of the jump set.
(See e.g.
[
4
, Chapter 3]
for more details.)
We extend

,
w^{-}
to functions

,
\widetilde{w}^{-}

defined

\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|
is uniformly bounded, in terms of
\beta
only.
∎

Notation on the Ginzburg-Landau functional.

For the
\mathbf{Q}
-component, we can lift results
from the Ginzburg-Landau literature, thanks to the isometric
isomorphism
\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
given by

(2.10)

\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}\ni\mathbf{Q}\mapsto\mathbf{q}:=\sqrt{2}\left(Q_{11},\,Q_{12}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

In particular, this allows us to borrow some terminology
from
[
12
]
. Let
\mathbf{Q}_{{\mathrm{bd}}}\colon\partial\Omega\to\mathscr{N}

be a boundary datum of class
C^{2}
, oriented by a

C^{2}
-map
\mathbf{n}_{\mathrm{bd}}\colon\partial\Omega\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
,
as in (
8
). A direct computation
shows that the vector field
\mathbf{q}_{{\mathrm{bd}}}=((q_{{\mathrm{bd}}})_{1},\,(q_{{\mathrm{bd}}})_{2})

associated with
\mathbf{Q}_{{\mathrm{bd}}}
as in (
2.10
)
satisfies
(q_{{\mathrm{bd}}})_{1}=(n_{{\mathrm{bd}}})_{1}^{2}-(n_{{\mathrm{bd}}})_{2}^{2}
,

(q_{{\mathrm{bd}}})_{2}=2(n_{{\mathrm{bd}}})_{1}(n_{{\mathrm{bd}}})_{2}

or equivalently, in complex notation,

\mathbf{q}_{\mathrm{bd}}=\mathbf{n}_{\mathrm{bd}}^{2}.

In particular, if
d
denotes the boundary degree of
\mathbf{n}_{\mathrm{bd}}
,
then
\mathbf{q}_{\mathrm{bd}}
has degree
2d
.
Let
a_{1},\,\ldots,\,a_{2\left|d\right|}
be distinct point in
\Omega
.
We say that
\mathbf{Q}^{\star}\colon\Omega\to\mathscr{N}
is a
canonical harmonic map
with singularities at
(a_{1},\,\ldots,\,a_{2\left|d\right|})
and boundary datum
\mathbf{Q}_{{\mathrm{bd}}}
if it is smooth in
\Omega\setminus\{a_{1},\,\ldots,\,a_{2\left|d\right|}\}
, continuous in
\overline{\Omega}\setminus\{a_{1},\,\ldots,\,a_{2\left|d\right|}\}
, has non-orientable singularities of degree
\sign{(d)}/2
at each point
a_{j}
, satisfies the boundary condition
\mathbf{Q}^{\star}=\mathbf{Q}_{{\mathrm{bd}}}
on
\partial\Omega
and the equation

\partial_{j}\left(Q^{\star}_{11}\,\partial_{j}Q^{\star}_{12}-Q^{\star}_{12}\,\partial_{j}Q^{\star}_{11}\right)=0

in the sense of distributions in
\Omega
.
Such a map exists and is unique, see
[
12
, Theorem I.5, Remark I.1]
. The canonical harmonic map belongs to
W^{1,2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega\setminus\{a_{1},\,\ldots,\,a_{2\left|d\right|}\},\,\mathscr{N})
and the limit

(2.11)

\mathbb{W}(a_{1},\,\ldots,\,a_{2\left|d\right|}):=\lim_{\sigma\to 0}\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega\setminus\bigcup_{j=1}^{2\left|d\right|}B_{\sigma}(a_{j})}\left|\nabla\mathbf{Q}^{\star}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x-2\pi\left|d\right|\left|\log\sigma\right|\right)

exists and is finite (see
[
12
, Theorem I.8]
).
Following the terminology in
[
12
]
, the function
\mathbb{W}

is called the
renormalised energy
.
We also define, for
\varepsilon>0
,

\gamma(\varepsilon):=\inf\left\{\int_{B_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{4\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\left|u\right|^{2}-1\right)^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}x\colon u\in W^{1,2}(B_{1},\,\mathbb{C}),\ u(x)=x\textrm{ for }x\in\partial B_{1}\right\}

and

(2.12)

\gamma_{*}:=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left(\gamma(\varepsilon)-\pi\left|\log\varepsilon\right|\right)

The limit
\gamma_{*}
exists, is finite and
strictly positive
[
12
, Lemma III.3]
.

Allen-Cahn structure for the
\mathbf{M}
-component.

In order to highlight an Allen-Cahn structure
on the variable
\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}
, as in
[
23
]
,
we rely on a change of variable.
Let
G\subseteq\Omega
be a simply connected domain
with smooth boundary
\partial G
,
and let
(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})\in W^{1,2}(\Omega,\,\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2})\times W^{1,2}(\Omega,\,\mathbb{R}^{2})
be a pair satisfying

(2.13)

\displaystyle\int_{G}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left|\nabla\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{4\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}-1\right)^{2}\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\leq\Lambda\left|\log\varepsilon\right|,

(2.14)

\displaystyle\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}(x)\right|\geq\frac{1}{2},\quad\left|\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}(x)\right|\leq\Lambda\qquad\mbox{for almost any }x\in G,

where
\Lambda
is some positive constant that does not depend on
\varepsilon
.
By the spectral theorem and lifting results
[
15
,
13
,
8
]
, we can write

(2.15)

\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}=\frac{\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}\otimes\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}-\mathbf{m}_{\varepsilon}\otimes\mathbf{m}_{\varepsilon}\right)\qquad\mbox{in }G,

where
(\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{m}_{\varepsilon})
is an orthonormal frame of eigenvectors
of
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
in
G
, with
\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}\in W^{1,2}(G,\,\mathbb{S}^{1})
and

\mathbf{m}_{\varepsilon}\in W^{1,2}(G,\,\mathbb{S}^{1})
.
Then, we define a map
\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\colon G\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
component-wise,
as
\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}:=((u_{\varepsilon})_{1},\,(u_{\varepsilon})_{2})
with

(2.16)

(u_{\varepsilon})_{1}:=\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon},\qquad(u_{\varepsilon})_{2}:=\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\mathbf{m}_{\varepsilon}.

This change of variable allows us to effectively
decouple the energy. Indeed, we can rewrite the
functional (
4
) (localised to
G
) as

(2.17)

\mathscr{F}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon};\,G)=\int_{G}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left|\nabla\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+g_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon})\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}x+\int_{G}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}h(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon})\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}x+R_{\varepsilon}

(see
[
23
, Proposition 3.2]
), where

(2.18)

\displaystyle g_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}):=\frac{1}{4\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\left|\mathbf{Q}\right|^{2}-1\right)^{2}-\frac{2\kappa_{*}}{\varepsilon}\left(\left|\mathbf{Q}\right|-1\right)+\kappa_{*}^{2},

(2.19)

\displaystyle h(\mathbf{u}):=\frac{1}{4}\left(\left|\mathbf{u}\right|^{2}-1\right)^{2}-\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{2}}\left(u_{1}^{2}-u_{2}^{2}\right)+\frac{\beta^{2}+\sqrt{2}\beta}{2},

and
R_{\varepsilon}
is a remainder term, such that

(2.20)

R_{\varepsilon}\to 0\qquad\textrm{as }\varepsilon\to 0

(cf.
[
23
, (3.9) and Lemma 4.10]
).
The functions
g_{\varepsilon}
,
h
are both nonnegative

[
23
, Lemma 3.3 and Lemma 3.4]
.
Moreover,
h
has two non-degenerate minima at

(2.21)

\mathbf{u}_{\pm}:=\left(\pm\left(\sqrt{2}\beta+1\right)^{1/2},0\right),

and the optimal cost for a transition
between
\mathbf{u}_{-}
and
\mathbf{u}_{+}
can be evaluated explicitly: we have

(2.22)

\begin{split}c_{\beta}&:=\inf\left\{\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{2h(\mathbf{u}(t))}\left|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)\right|\mathrm{d}t\colon\mathbf{u}\in W^{1,1}([0,\,1],\,\mathbb{R}^{2}),\ \mathbf{u}(0)=\mathbf{u}_{-},\ \mathbf{u}(1)=\mathbf{u}_{+}\right\}\\
&=\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3}\left(\sqrt{2}\beta+1\right)^{3/2}.\end{split}

The interplay between the Ginzburg-Landau
and the Allen-Cahn energies leads to the following
definition of a modified renormalised energy:
given distinct points
a_{1}
, …,
a_{2\left|d\right|}
in
\Omega
,
we define

(2.23)

\mathbb{W}_{\beta}^{\Omega}(a_{1},\,\ldots,\,a_{2\left|d\right|}):=\mathbb{W}(a_{1},\,\ldots,\,a_{2\left|d\right|})+c_{\beta}\,\mathbb{L}_{\beta}^{\Omega}(a_{1},\,\ldots,\,a_{2\left|d\right|}),

where
\mathbb{W}
is the Ginzburg-Landau reneormalised energy,
as in (
2.11
), and
\mathbb{L}_{\beta}^{\Omega}
is the length of a relative minimal
connection, as defined in (
2
).

2.2
Proof of Theorem
2

The next step towards the proof of Theorem
2

is the construction of admissible competitors for the minimisation
problem that satisfy sharp energy bounds from above.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
a_{1},\,\ldots,\,a_{2\left|d\right|}
be distinct points in
\Omega
.
Then, there exist maps
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}\in W^{1,2}\left(\Omega,\,\mathscr{S}_{0}^{2\times 2}\right)
,

\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}\in W^{1,2}\left(\Omega,\,\mathbb{R}^{2}\right)
that satisfy
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}=\mathbf{Q}_{{\mathrm{bd}}}

on
\partial\Omega
and

\mathscr{F}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon})\leq 2\pi\left|d\right|\left|\log\varepsilon\right|+\mathbb{W}_{\beta}^{\Omega}\left(a_{1},\cdots,a_{2\left|d\right|}\right)+2\left|d\right|\gamma_{*}+\mathrm{o}_{\varepsilon\to 0}(1),

where
\mathbb{W}_{\beta}
and
\gamma_{*}
are
as in (
2.23
), (
2.12
) respectively.

Proof.

This result follows along the lines
of
[
23
, Proposition 4.19]
.
First, we construct a suitable
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
with non-orientable
singularities at the points
a_{1}
, …,
a_{2\left|d\right|}
,
by following existing arguments in the Ginzburg-Landau literature
(see e.g.
[
12
,
1
]
).
Next, we define a vector field
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{\varepsilon}\in\SBV(\Omega,\,\mathbb{R}^{2})

of constant norm, such that
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{\varepsilon}(x)

is an eigenvector of
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}(x)
at almost every
x\in\Omega

and the jump set of
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{\varepsilon}

is a minimal connection
\{L_{1},\,\ldots,\,L_{q}\}

for
\{a_{1},\,\ldots,\,a_{2\left|d\right|}\}
relative to
\Omega
.
The existence of
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{\varepsilon}
follows from Lemma
1.8
.
Now, all that remains to do is to define
\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}

as a regularisation of
\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}_{\varepsilon}
along its jump set.
This can be achieved by solving an optimal profile problem ‘à la Modica-Mortola’
[
41
]
.
The change of variable (
2.16
) turns out to be useful
in this respect: we first construct suitable maps
\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}

near each line segment
L_{j}
, in such a way that
\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}

is an optimal transition profile for (
2.22
)
along the transversal direction to
L_{j}
;
then, we define the corresponding
\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}

as
\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}:=(u_{\varepsilon})_{1}\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon}+(u_{\varepsilon})_{2}\mathbf{m}_{\varepsilon}
,
where
(\mathbf{n}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{m}_{\varepsilon})
is an eigenframe for
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}
,
as in (
2.15
).
This is possible because Lemma
1.6

guarantees that the line segments
L_{j}
are pairwise disjoint and
contained in
\Omega
, except possibly at their endpoints.
The details of the construction are carried
out in
[
23
]
.
The only difference is that some of the
L_{j}
’s
may touch the boundary of
\Omega
at an endpoint.
In this case, the construction carries over
(Equation (4.95) in
[
23
]

still makes sense if
L_{1}
has an endpoint on
\partial\Omega
),
but there will be error terms in the estimates
(Equation (4.96) and (4.97)) due to boundary effects.
However, the regularisation only affects a neighbourhood
of
L_{j}
of thickness
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
, with
\sigma_{\varepsilon}\to 0

as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Since the boundary of
\Omega
is of class
C^{2}
,
the errors we introduce because of the boundary
tend to zero as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
∎

In the next lemma, we collect useful estimates
that follow from the Ginzburg-Landau literature.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{\star},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}^{\star})
be a minimiser of the functional
\mathscr{F}_{\varepsilon}
subject to the boundary condition
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{Q}_{{\mathrm{bd}}}
on
\partial\Omega
, where
\mathbf{Q}_{{\mathrm{bd}}}
satisfies (
8
).
Let
d
be the degree of
\mathbf{Q}_{{\mathrm{bd}}}
. Then,
(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{\star},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}^{\star})
satisfies

(2.24)

\mathscr{F}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{\star},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}^{\star})\leq 2\pi\left|d\right|\left|\log\varepsilon\right|+\mathbb{W}_{\beta}^{\Omega}(a_{1},\cdots,a_{2\left|d\right|})+2\left|d\right|\gamma_{*}+\mathrm{o}_{\varepsilon\to 0}(1)

for any distinct points
a_{1},\,\ldots,\,a_{2\left|d\right|}
in
\Omega
.
Moreover, there exists an
\varepsilon
-independent constant
C
such that

(2.25)

\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left|\nabla\mathbf{M}^{\star}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}^{\star}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}^{\star}_{\varepsilon})\right){\mathrm{d}}x\leq C.

Proof.

The estimate (
2.24
) is an immediate consequence of Proposition
2.2
.
To prove (
2.25
), we apply existing results
from the Ginzburg-Landau literature.
First, we identify
\mathbf{Q}^{\star}_{\varepsilon}

with a map
\mathbf{q}^{\star}_{\varepsilon}\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
as in (
2.10
).
From (
2.1
), (
2.4
),
and (
2.24
), we deduce

(2.26)

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left|\nabla\mathbf{q}^{\star}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{8\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\left|\mathbf{q}^{\star}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}-1\right)^{2}\right){\mathrm{d}}x&\leq\mathscr{F}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}^{\star}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}^{\star}_{\varepsilon})+\beta^{2}\int_{\Omega}\left|\mathbf{M}^{\star}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{4}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\\
&\leq 2\pi\left|d\right|\left|\log\varepsilon\right|+C,\end{split}

where
C
is a constant that does not depend on
\varepsilon
.
As a consequence
[
27
, Theorem 1.1]
,
there exists an
\varepsilon
-independent constant
C

such that

(2.27)

\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\left(\left|\mathbf{Q}^{\star}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}-1\right)^{2}{\mathrm{d}}x=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\left(\left|\mathbf{q}^{\star}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}-1\right)^{2}{\mathrm{d}}x\leq C.

Moreover,
we can apply estimates such as
[
43
, Theorem 2]

or
[
34
, Theorem 1.1]
to
\mathbf{q}^{\star}_{\varepsilon}
and,
keeping (
2.27
) into account, we deduce

(2.28)

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\mathbf{Q}^{\star}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}x=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\mathbf{q}^{\star}_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}x\geq 2\pi\left|d\right|\left|\log\varepsilon\right|-C,\end{split}

again for some
\varepsilon
-independent
C
.
By comparing (
2.24
) with (
2.28
),
we obtain the uniform bound (
2.25
).
∎

Proof of Theorem
2
.

The estimates in Lemma
2.3
, together with
classical results in the Ginzburg-Landau literature (see e.g
[
35
, Theorem 2.4]
,
[
36
, Proposition 1.1]
,
[
34
, Theorems 1.2 and 1.3]
,
[
43
, Theorem 1]
)
imply that (a non-relabelled subsequence of)
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{\star}
converges, strongly in
W^{1,p}(\Omega)
for
p<2
, to a map
\mathbf{Q}^{\star}
with
non-orientable singularities at some
points
a^{\star}_{1}
, …,
a^{\star}_{2\left|d\right|}
in
\Omega
.
The proof that
\mathbf{Q}^{\star}
is the canonical harmonic map
with singularities at
a^{\star}_{1}
, …,
a^{\star}_{2\left|d\right|}

is exactly as in
[
23
, Proposition 4.9]
.

In order to obtain compactness for
\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}^{\star}
,
we apply the change of variable described in (
2.16
).
Classical arguments in the Ginzburg-Landau theory
(along the lines of
[
12
, Theorem III.3]
)
imply that there exists a finite set
X^{\star}
,
with
\{a_{1}^{\star},\,\ldots,\,a_{2\left|d\right|}^{\star}\}\subseteq X^{\star}\subseteq\overline{\Omega}
,
such that
\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{\star}\right|\to 1

locally uniformly in
\Omega\setminus X^{\star}
.
(In fact, with a bit more work it is possible to show that

X^{\star}=\{a_{1}^{\star},\,\ldots,\,a_{2\left|d\right|}^{\star}\}

in our case, but we will not need this equality
to complete the proof.)
Let
G
be a smooth, simply connected domain
whose closure is contained in
\Omega\setminus X^{\star}
.
We have
\left|\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{\star}\right|\geq 1/2

in
G
for all
\varepsilon
small enough, so that we
may define
\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}^{\star}
as in (
2.16
).
From (
2.17
) and (
2.20
), we know that

\int_{G}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}^{\star}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}h(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}^{\star})\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{G}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left|\nabla\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}^{\star}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}f_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{\star},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}^{\star})\right)\mathrm{d}x+\mathrm{o}_{\varepsilon\to 0}(1).

The uniform bound (
2.25
) gives

\int_{G}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left|\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}^{\star}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}h(\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}^{\star})\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq C,

for some constant
C
that does not depend on
\varepsilon
,
G
.
We can now apply compactness results for the vectorial Modica-Mortola functional
(see e.g.
[
7
]
or
[
29
, Theorems 3.1 and 4.1]
)
to obtain convergence
\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}^{\star}\to\mathbf{u}^{\star}
in
L^{1}(G)
,
at least along a non-relabelled subsequence.
We define
\mathbf{M}^{\star}:=u_{1}^{\star}\mathbf{n}^{\star}+u_{2}\mathbf{m}^{\star}
,
where
(\mathbf{n}^{\star},\,\mathbf{m}^{\star})
is an eigenframe for
\mathbf{Q}^{\star}
.
The very same arguments as in
[
23
, Proposition 4.11]
show that
\mathbf{M}^{\star}

can be extended to a well-defined map
\SBV(\Omega,\,\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
that
\left|\mathbf{M}^{\star}\right|=\left(\sqrt{2}\beta+1\right)^{1/2}
a.e. in
\Omega

and that
\left(\sqrt{2}\beta+1\right)^{-1/2}\mathbf{M}^{\star}
is a lifting of
\mathbf{Q}^{\star}
.

We can prove a sharp lower bound
on the energy for the
\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{\star}
by applying

\Gamma
-convergence arguments in the Ginzburg-Landau literature
[
1
]
.
Given
\sigma>0
, let
D_{\sigma}:=\bigcup_{x\in X^{\star}}B_{\sigma}(x)
.
For any
\sigma
small enough, we have (see
[
23
, Lemma 4.18]
)

(2.29)

\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left(\mathscr{F}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{\star},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}^{\star};\,D_{\sigma})-2\pi\left|d\right|\left|\log\varepsilon\right|\right)\geq\pi\log\sigma+2\left|d\right|\gamma_{*}-C\sigma,

where
C
is a constant that does not depend on
\varepsilon
,
\sigma
.
On the other hand, we have

(2.30)

{\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathscr{F}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{\star},\,\mathbf{M}_{\varepsilon}^{\star};\,\Omega\setminus D_{\sigma})\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega\setminus D_{\sigma}}\left|\nabla\mathbf{Q}^{\star}\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+c_{\beta}\mathscr{H}^{1}\left(\mathrm{J}_{\mathbf{M}^{\star}}\cap(\Omega\setminus D_{\sigma})\right)}

exactly as in
[
23
, Equation (4.81)]
.
Combining (
2.29
) with (
2.30
),
recalling the definition (
2.11
)
of
\mathbb{W}(a^{\star}_{1},\,\ldots,\,a^{\star}_{2\left|d\right|})
,
and passing to the limit as
\sigma\to 0
, we obtain

(2.31)

\begin{split}\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\big(\mathscr{F}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{Q}^{\star}_{\varepsilon},\,\mathbf{M}^{\star}_{\varepsilon})-2\pi\left|d\right|\left|\log\varepsilon\right|\big)\geq\mathbb{W}(a^{\star}_{1},\,\ldots,\,a^{\star}_{2\left|d\right|})+c_{\beta}\mathscr{H}^{1}(\mathrm{J}_{\mathbf{M}^{\star}})+2\left|d\right|\gamma_{*}.\end{split}

By comparing (
2.31
) with the upper bound (
2.24
),
and keeping Theorem
1
into account,
we obtain

\mathbb{W}^{\Omega}_{\beta}\left(a^{\star}_{1},\,\ldots,\,a^{\star}_{2\left|d\right|}\right)\leq\mathbb{W}\left(a^{\star}_{1},\,\ldots,\,a^{\star}_{2\left|d\right|}\right)+c_{\beta}\mathscr{H}^{1}\left(\mathrm{J}_{\mathbf{M}^{\star}}\right)\leq\mathbb{W}^{\Omega}_{\beta}\left(a_{1},\,\ldots,\,a_{2\left|d\right|}\right)

for any distinct points
a_{1}
, …,
a_{2\left|d\right|}
in
\Omega
. This proves that

(a^{\star}_{1},\,\ldots,\,a^{\star}_{2\left|d\right|})

is a minimiser of
\mathbb{W}^{\Omega}_{\beta}
and, moreover,

\mathscr{H}^{1}(\mathrm{J}_{\mathbf{M}_{*}})=\mathbb{L}^{\Omega}(a_{1}^{\star},\,\ldots,\,a^{\star}_{2\left|d\right|})
.
Then, Theorem
1

implies that
\mathrm{J}_{\mathbf{M}_{*}}
is carried by a
minimal connection for
\{a_{1}^{\star},\,\ldots,\,a^{\star}_{2\left|d\right|}\}

relative to
\Omega
.
This completes the proof of the theorem.
