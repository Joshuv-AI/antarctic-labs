Reconfigurable assembly, disassembly, and self-propulsion of microparticles via optoelectronic tweezers
Authors: Ao Wang, Ziyi Wang, Wenyan Niu, Caiding Ni, Shunxiao Huang, Chutian Wang, Menglu Tan, Lingze Zhang, Lin Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractReconfigurability and autonomous mobility are critical for micro/nanorobot systems to perform complex tasks. Here, we propose a synergistic optoelectric control strategy based on an optoelectronic tweezers (OET) platform, which enables the controllable assembly and disassembly of heterogeneous microparticles through electric field induced dipole interactions and electrohydrodynamic (EHD) flows. We find that the assembled structures exhibit stable self-propulsion and can move directionally along the boundaries of optical patterns. This self-propulsion arises from the asymmetric shadow effect induced by top illumination, which generates a lateral dielectrophoretic (DEP) driving force. Notably, the self-propulsion velocity shows an anomalous non-monotonic dependence on the AC frequency. By incorporating DEP levitation and microlens focusing effects, we further elucidate the physical mechanism. At low frequencies, increased levitation height expands the focused light spot beneath the polystyrene (PS) microparticles, reducing the shadow asymmetry below the metal microparticles and consequently weakening the lateral DEP force. Furthermore, by combining dynamic optical patterns with electric field modulation, multilevel reconfiguration of multi-particle clusters is achieved. This strategy effectively couples reconfigurable assembly with autonomous propulsion, providing a versatile pathway for the development of intelligent and modular microrobotic systems. Subject terms: Applied optics, Optical physics, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Micro/nanorobots (MNRs) are miniature electromechanical systems that operate at micrometer or nanometer scales and perform specific tasks through external actuation or autonomous control1,2. An ideal MNR typically integrates multiple functional modules, including propulsion, sensing, navigation, and cargo-loading components, to execute more intelligent and complex tasks3–5. However, at small scales, conventional macroscale fabrication and assembly methods are often difficult to apply. Consequently, current research primarily focuses on single particles as the basic configuration for MNRs6–8, which restricts their versatility.

In contrast, heterogeneous composite structures assembled from units with different materials3,9 and sizes10–12 offer a practical route to integrate multiple functions. These assemblies can simultaneously provide self-propulsion, payload carrying, and environmental responsiveness, thereby demonstrating superior adaptability in fields such as targeted drug delivery13–15, biosensing16,17, and cargo transport18,19. However, conventional passive assembly strategies (e.g., evaporation induced assembly20 and vapor deposition21) typically depend on fixed boundaries and passive processes, which lack the controllability and flexibility for real-time adjustment. In recent years, although active assembly and actuation technologies driven by external fields such as magnetic22,23, acoustic24–27, and electric fields28–30 have achieved significant progress, the selective manipulation at the individual scale and reconfigurable assembly remain a persistent challenge.

Optical field manipulation, with its high spatial resolution and excellent programmability, enables precise control of microstructures31–33. Various light induced effects, including optoelectronic34,35 photothermal36–38, and photochemical39–41, have shown promise in microscale object manipulation, assembly, and actuation. Among these, optoelectronic tweezers (OET) represent a prominent optoelectronic coupling technique. By generating light induced DEP forces42,43, OET enables precise and parallel manipulation of microparticles44–46. Previous studies have shown that OET can achieve self-propelled steering47 and self-assembly48 of Janus particles, as well as the formation of topologically reconfigurable particle arrays with self-restoration properties49. However, integrating reconfigurable assembly with self-propulsion in a single OET system remains an unresolved challenge.

To address these challenges, we propose an OET-based strategy for the controlled assembly and self-propulsion of heterogeneous microparticles. First, we develop a general framework for particles assembly and disassembly, and validate its controllability and reversibility using chain and ring structures. Subsequently, the self-propulsion of polystyrene (PS)-metal dimers and the key factors governing their motion are systematically investigated. Numerical simulations are further employed to analyze the non-monotonic response of the propulsion velocity to variations in alternating current (AC) frequency. Finally, dynamic optical patterns are used to control dimer trajectories and propulsion directions, while enabling multilevel reconfiguration of multi-cluster assemblies during guided self-propulsion. Overall, this strategy couples reversible assembly with self-propulsion, paving the way for designing intelligent MNRs capable of complex tasks.

Results and Discussion

Controlled assembly and disassembly of chain and ring

Chain and ring structures serve as fundamental topological units, whose geometry directly governs a system’s structural stability and collective motion. Numerous biological systems form such topologies to adapt to complex environments50,51. At the micro/nanoscale, chains52–55 and rings48,56 composed of particles or functional modules demonstrate collective behaviors that surpass those of isolated particles. Motivated by this, we employ the OET system to achieve controlled assembly and disassembly of chain and ring structures, as well as their reversible transformation, providing a versatile experimental route for building reconfigurable microsystems.

The OET system and chip have been described in our previous work44. Briefly, the OET chip consists of two indium tin oxide (ITO)-coated glass substrates. The bottom substrate is deposited with a 1 μm hydrogenated amorphous silicon (a-Si:H). An 80 μm gap is formed between the two substrates, into which microparticles dispersed in aqueous solution are introduced using a syringe pump. An alternating current (AC) signal, supplied by a signal generator, is applied between the substrates to establish a vertical AC electric field. Under this field, micro-objects are subjected to various field induced effects, including electrohydrodynamic (EHD) flows, AC electroosmosis (ACEO), dielectrophoresis (DEP), and electrothermal flows57,58.

As illustrated in Fig. 1a, b, under a low frequency AC field (300 Hz, 3 Vpp), a PS bead and a metal bead generate oppositely directed EHD flows near the bottom of the chip (Movie S1). This contrast arises primarily from differences in their dielectric properties, where the sign of the dimensionless dipole coefficient C0 determines the rotation direction of the EHD flow59. The PS bead (C0<0) generates contractile vortices with inward flow, aggregating surrounding particles. Conversely, the metal bead (C0>0) produces expansional vortices and outward flow that push surrounding particles away (Supplementary Note 1). This different flow response enables the controlled assembly and disassembly of microstructures.

Scale bar: 50 μm

In addition to fluidic effects, significant DEP interactions occur between microparticles, which can be approximated as dipole-dipole interactions60. In the plane perpendicular to the AC field, homogeneous particles (same DEP polarity) repel each other, while heterogeneous pairs attract. Applying this principle, we assembled a stable chain structure by using light spots to arrange metal and PS beads alternately under a high frequency AC field (100 kHz, 10 Vpp). Then, switching to a low frequency field (300 Hz, 3 Vpp) disassembled the chains, as the expansional EHD flow from the metal beads disrupted the dipolar equilibrium (Fig. 1c, Movie S2). Furthermore, when metal beads were anchored by optical spots during the disassembly process, selective outward diffusion of PS microspheres was achieved (Fig. 1d, Movie S2). This demonstrates that selective disassembly of chain structures can be realized through the synergistic control of electric field frequency and optical confinement.

Figure 1e and Movie S3 further illustrate the controlled transition from chain to ring. This process is initiated by removing one terminal metal bead, creating a chain with one metal and one PS bead at its ends. Subsequently, a light spot guides the rotation of the leading bead, while another spot locks the terminal metal bead to prevent undesired displacement. Once the end-to-end distance falls within the effective range of dipolar attraction, the chain spontaneously closes to complete the topological transformation. The resulting ring structure can be controllably assembled and disassembled by switching between high and low frequency AC fields, both with and without optical confinement (Fig. 1f, g, Movie S4). This high degree of reconfigurability demonstrates the versatility and potential of our strategy for building sophisticated microsystems. It should be noted, however, that the assembled structures remain stable only in the presence of the applied AC field. Upon removal of the AC field, the dipolar interactions disappear, and the structures tend to disassemble under Brownian motion and flow disturbances. Enhancing post-assembly structural stability will be an important focus of our future work.

Self-propulsion mechanism of microparticle dimer

As shown in Fig. 2a, when a metal bead and a PS bead are in close proximity, they attract each other via dipolar interactions and spontaneously assemble into a dimer. Similar phenomena were reported in our previous work61. Notably, once formed, the dimer exhibits distinct self-propulsion, with its propulsion direction consistently oriented toward the metallic side (Movie S5). Experimental results indicate that the self-propulsion velocity of the dimer is influenced by both the AC electric field and illumination intensity (Fig. S1). Previous studies have shown that clusters composed of microspheres of different sizes62 or material types30,63 can achieve self-propulsion in AC fields due to asymmetric EHD flows. Similarly, metallo-dielectric Janus particles also exhibit spontaneous propulsion in AC fields, typically attributed to mechanisms such as induced-charge electrophoresis (ICEP)64,65 or self-dielectrophoresis (sDEP)66,67.

Our analysis indicates that EHD flow, ICEP, and sDEP effects are not the dominant mechanisms driving the self-propulsion of dimers in this system. First, both EHD and ICEP originate from the electrical double layer (EDL) formed at the solid-liquid interface. Their dynamical behavior is governed by the EDL charging time constant, resulting in a characteristic low-pass frequency response where the time-averaged flow velocity decreases inversely or quadratically with increasing frequency58. Typically, these effects are significant only at low frequencies (<10 kHz)68. In our system, the characteristic EDL charging frequency is estimated to be about 600 Hz (Supplementary Note 2). However, the dimer maintained stable self-propulsion up to 1000 kHz, which is inconsistent with the frequency response characteristics of EHD and ICEP. Second, prior studies have shown that ICEP-driven Janus particles typically move toward the nonmetallic side. In contrast, the dimer in our experiments consistently propels toward the metallic side, which directly contradicts the directional behavior characteristic of ICEP and further rules it out as the dominant mechanism. Finally, regarding sDEP, it usually arises from structural asymmetry of the particle itself, which induces a local electric field gradient and generates a DEP force leading to self-propulsion66. In conventional two electrode systems, there have been no reports of sDEP driving stable self-propulsion in heterogeneous particle clusters. Furthermore, numerical simulations were performed to evaluate the potential sDEP effects within the dimer (Fig. S2). The results indicate that, compared to Janus particles, the DEP forces generated by the PS-metal dimer itself are insufficient to produce significant thrust or overcome fluidic drag to initiate stable directional motion. Consequently, sDEP cannot account for the self-propulsion behavior observed in this work.

Recent studies suggest that the second mobility reversal in metallo-dielectric Janus particles stems from the dielectric hemisphere’s semi-transparency; it casts a weaker optical shadow than the metallic side, creating a non-uniform light induced electric field beneath the particle that alters its propulsion69. Inspired by this insight, we modified the illumination configuration in our OET system to examine its impact. Specifically, we implemented two experimental setups: top illumination (Fig. 2b) and bottom illumination (Fig. 2c). Under the same light power, we observed that the dimer exhibited pronounced self-propulsion under top illumination but remained nearly stationary under bottom illumination (Fig. 2d, e and Movie S6). This difference can be attributed to the asymmetric shadow region formed beneath the dimer under top illumination (Fig. 2f). The shadowed area exhibits lower photoconductivity than the illuminated region, creating a localized electric field gradient. The resulting DEP force is stronger on the metallic side, which enhances positive DEP attraction there and ultimately drives the dimer steadily toward the metal side. Further measurements indicate that the self-propulsion velocity of the dimer increases approximately linearly with top illumination intensity (Fig. S3), suggesting that higher light intensity enhances the shadow asymmetry beneath the metal bead, thereby generating a larger lateral DEP force.

Particle size also plays a crucial role in the propulsion performance of the dimers. As shown in Fig. S4, we measured the self-propulsion velocity of dimers composed of PS beads with different diameters and 20 μm metal beads. When the diameter ratio between the PS and metal beads was 1, the self-propulsion velocity reached its maximum. When the diameter ratio was smaller than 1, the asymmetric shadow formed beneath the metal bead was too small to generate a sufficient driving force. When the diameter ratio was larger than 1, although the shadow asymmetry was enhanced, the fluid resistance acting on the dimer increased significantly, which instead reduced the self-propulsion velocity. Therefore, the configuration with a diameter ratio of 1 achieved the best balance between the driving force and fluid resistance.

Influence of AC frequency on dimer self-propulsion velocity

To elucidate the impact of AC frequency on the self-propulsion behavior, we measured the propulsion velocities of the dimers over a range of frequencies. The results show a clear non-monotonic trend: the velocity first increases and then decreases with increasing frequency, reaching a peak at approximately 500 kHz (Fig. 3a, black curve). However, if only the asymmetric shadow induced by top illumination is considered, numerical simulations predict a monotonic decrease in propulsion velocity as frequency increases (Fig. 3a, cyan curve). The discrepancy between experiment and theory indicates that, besides the shadow effect, an additional competing mechanism must jointly regulate the dimer’s self-propulsion.

As the AC frequency increases, the dielectric levitation height of the PS bead decreases, causing the focused light spot to shrink. d Numerical simulation of the electric-field intensity distribution (blue: low intensity; red: high intensity). Black arrows indicate the direction of the Maxwell stress tensor acting on the bead surface, and white streamlines denote the electric-field lines (simulation conditions: 100 kHz, 10 Vpp, levitation height = 8.5 μm)

In the high frequency range (500–1000 kHz), the experimental data agree well with the theoretical prediction and the decline in velocity follows the typical frequency response of the OET system70. As frequency rises, the capacitive reactance of the photoconductive layer drops significantly, reducing the impedance difference between illuminated and shadowed regions. This diminishes the voltage drop and the local electric field gradients between these regions, leading to a monotonic reduction of the DEP force acting on the dimer. The major deviation is observed in the low frequency range (100–500 kHz). Experimentally, as the frequency decreases, the dimer gradually moves out of the initial focal plane and exhibits an upward displacement. Using the equilibrium position at 1000 kHz as a reference, we quantified the levitation height at different frequencies with a microscope focusing-based method71. The results reveal that the levitation height increases substantially as the AC frequency decreases (Fig. 3b), consistent with the characteristic behavior of DEP levitation72,73.

The variation in levitation height further modulates the self-propulsion performance through micro-optical effects. When collimated light illuminates the semi-transparent PS bead, a high intensity photonic nanojet can form on its shadow-side due to optical focusing74. Here, the PS bead can be approximated as a micro-convex lens with its focal point situated near the sphere surface. In the low frequency range, as the AC frequency increases, the levitation height of the dimer gradually decreases, and the focused light spot beneath the PS bead correspondingly shrinks (Fig. 3c). This change progressively enhances the electric field intensity below the metal bead (Fig. S5), thereby increasing the positive DEP force acting on the metallic side of the dimer and leading to a higher self-propulsion velocity. After incorporating both DEP levitation and the microlens effect into the simulation model (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Note 3), the simulated results (Fig. 3a, red curve) achieve a high degree of consistency with the experimental trends. This mechanism is further supported by additional experiments. Replacing the PS bead with a SiO2 bead leads to a reduction in propulsion velocity (Supplementary Note 4). We attribute this effect to the higher transparency of SiO2 relative to PS, which strengthens the microlens effect under top illumination, weakens the asymmetric shadow distribution, and consequently reduces the propulsion velocity.

In summary, the self-propulsion speed of the dimer is governed by two mechanisms with distinct frequency responses. In the low frequency range, the system is dominated by the microlens effect induced by AC dielectric levitation. Specifically, a decrease in frequency leads to an increase in levitation height, which expands the focused light spot beneath the PS bead. This reduces the shadow asymmetry with respect to the metal bead of the dimer, thereby suppressing the self-propulsion velocity. In the high frequency range, the reduction of the electric field gradient with increasing frequency becomes the dominant factor, leading to a gradual decline in propulsion speed.

Dynamic behavior and motion control of the dimer at optical boundaries

Self-propelled microparticles are often influenced by various boundaries, which can significantly alter their motility and dynamic behavior75. Experimentally, we observed that during self-propulsion, when a dimer reaches the boundary of a light pattern, it does not cross the bright-dark interface. Instead, it aligns with the edge at a certain angle and continues to propel persistently along the boundary (Fig. 4a, b and Movie S7). Measurements of the edge-following velocity under various electric field parameters (Fig. 4c) show that the self-propulsion velocity increases with the voltage amplitude. Meanwhile, the velocity exhibits a non-monotonic frequency response that peaks at 400 kHz. This trend is consistent with the frequency response measured when the dimer moves within the illuminated region (Fig. 3a, black curve), indicating that edge-guided motion is also regulated by the levitation-induced optical field enhancement. Notably, under the same electric field and illumination conditions (400 kHz, 10 Vpp, 0.8 W/cm2), the dimer moves significantly faster along the pattern edge (31.9 μm/s) than inside the illuminated area (11.8 μm/s). This enhancement can be attributed to the maximum electric field strength gradient occurring at the bright-dark interface47, which amplifies the DEP effect induced by asymmetric shadow.

Time-sequence images demonstrating reversal of the dimer’s propulsion direction induced by a localized light spot (d) and by the electric field (e). f Self-propulsion of dimers along complex optical boundaries (blue: start; red: end). The dimer propelling along the optical boundary can maintain stable circular motion without external disturbances. Its initial rotation direction (clockwise or counterclockwise) is determined by the configuration of the dimer when it first contacts the light pattern edge. To actively control the propulsion direction, we propose two strategies.

The first strategy is shown in Fig. 4d and Movie S8. Exploiting the characteristic that the self-propulsion direction always points toward the metallic side, we initially reduce the illumination intensity of the light pattern. Subsequently, a localized light spot is applied to guide the dimer to rotate, thereby achieving the reversal of the propulsion direction along the boundary.

The second strategy is shown in Fig. 4e and Movie S9. Given that the propulsion velocity decreases at lower frequencies, the AC frequency is first reduced to 80 kHz to decelerate the dimer. In this state, the metal bead is attracted toward the pattern edge while the PS bead is repelled. The resulting net torque overcomes viscous resistance and drives rotation of the dimer. Subsequently restoring the frequency to 400 kHz enables a reversal of the propulsion direction.

In addition to the circular optical pattern, we further designed a complex optical pattern consisting of straight segments, 90° corners, and 180° U-turns to demonstrate the motion capability of dimers. As shown in Fig. 4f and Movie S10, four dimers move clockwise along the boundary of the optical pattern and successfully complete turns along the predefined path. These results indicate that the system not only enables controlled motion along simple optical boundaries, but also supports stable guidance along predefined trajectories in complex optical patterns.

A significant advantage of the OET is the ability to dynamically and programmably reconfigure optical patterns. Utilizing this feature, we can not only confine the motion range of the dimers but also precisely regulate their transport modes. As shown in Fig. 5a, when the circular light pattern gradually shrinks inward, the dimers, initially moving along the edge of the light pattern, are continuously guided inward, forming a typical planar spiral trajectory (Movie S11). Depending on the initial orientation of the metal bead in the dimer, the spiral motion can exhibit either a clockwise (Fig. 5b) or counterclockwise (Fig. 5c) rotation. When the circular light pattern is translated at a constant speed (Fig. 5d), the dimer self-propels along the boundary while being carried by the overall pattern, resulting in a helical-like motion trajectory (Fig. 5e, f and Movie S12). It is important to distinguish this behavior from the kinematically similar motion of Janus particles47,48. The propulsion in our system is driven by shadow-induced DEP, while the motion mechanisms in existing Janus systems are usually based on ICEP or sDEP effects.

By tuning the light patterns and the AC frequency, we achieve precise control over both the direction and mode of dimer self-propulsion along light pattern boundaries. These results highlight the flexibility of the proposed system and its capability for accurate regulation of microscale locomotion behaviors.

Multilevel reconfigurable assembly of trimers

Figure 6a shows the self-propulsion, controllable assembly and disassembly, and multi-cluster reconfiguration of a trimer composed of one metal bead and two PS beads under the combined guidance of an optical pattern and an AC electric field. Similar to the dimer, the trimer also has the ability to propel itself. However, it should be noted that the geometric configuration of the trimer is crucial for its motion behavior: when the trimer forms a linear configuration (Fig. S6a), the shadow beneath it is symmetric and cannot generate an effective driving force, resulting in a stationary state. Conversely, when the trimer forms a triangular configuration (Fig. S6b), the shadow distribution becomes asymmetric, thereby enabling self-propulsion. Moreover, during propulsion, the two side PS beads sweep backward due to fluid drag, which helps maintain the stability of the triangular configuration. This allows the trimer to sustain its motion continuously once self-propulsion is initiated.

Dynamic behaviours and reconfiguration of trimers.

a Schematic of the self-propulsion, controlled assembly/disassembly, and multi-cluster reconfiguration of trimers. b Self-propulsion and interactions of trimers along a circular light pattern track: (i) Initial distribution of trimers on the track; (ii) Clockwise self-propulsion along the track; (iii) Trimer P1 approaches P2 and decelerates due to inter-particle interactions; (iv) P1 flips its configuration and reverses its direction. c Controlled disassembly and reassembly of trimers: (i) Stable self-propulsion; (ii) Disassembly driven by expansional EHD flow after switching to a low‑frequency field (300 Hz, 3 Vpp); (iii) Restoration of electric field parameters (400 kHz, 10 Vpp) to drive the reassembly of particles into trimers; (iv) Resumed self‑propulsion of the reassembled trimer. d Multi-cluster reconfiguration of trimers: (i) Three trimers approach each other; (ii) Assembled into a single nine-particle cluster and continued moving; (iii) Complete disassembly of the nine-particle cluster into individual particles; (iv) Reconfigured into three trimers

To achieve precise guidance of the trimer’s motion path, we project a narrow circular light pattern track, which serves as a microscale rail. By aligning the track with the geometric symmetry axis of the trimer, the two PS beads are placed on opposite sides of the track, thereby imposing a lateral constraint on the trimer’s direction of motion. As shown in Fig. 6b and Movie S13, when three sets of trimers are placed onto this circular track, they spontaneously initiate clockwise self-propulsion along the track. Owing to individual variations, the propulsion speeds differ among the trimers. The faster moving trimer (P1) gradually approaches the slower one (P2) at approximately 10.2 s. Influenced by interparticle dipolar interactions, the metal bead in P1 is first repelled by P2 and decelerates. Meanwhile, the two side PS beads continue to move forward due to inertia, eventually overtaking the metal bead and triggering a configurational flip of the trimer. This configuration flip changes the orientation of the asymmetric shadow, causing P1 to switch from clockwise to counterclockwise motion at 12.8 s.

During the trimer self-propulsion, the controllable disassembly and reassembly can also be achieved by combining the aforementioned electric field control method. As shown in Fig. 6c and Movie S14, the three trimers self-propel along the light track at 1.2 s. When the AC electric field is switched from a high frequency to low frequency at 5.2 s, the expansional EHD flow generated by the metal bead pushes the PS beads outward, disassembling the three trimers into nine individual particles. Subsequently, at 7.2 s, the AC electric field is restored to high frequency, and the particles reassemble into three trimers and continue to self-propel along the light track (8.0 s).

Besides the controllable disassembly and reassembly within individual trimers, reconfigurable assembly can also occur between different trimers. As shown in Fig. 6d and Movie S14, when multiple trimers approach each other (9.1 s), interparticle interactions drive their fusion into a larger nine-particle cluster (10.2 s), which remains capable of self-propulsion. Switching the AC field to a low frequency, the nine-particle cluster can be disassembled again (16.1 s). Subsequently, restoring the AC field to a high frequency induces the particles to reassemble into three trimers (20.1 s). The approach thus allows reversible transitions between individual particles, trimers, and larger clusters through periodic AC field tuning, offering a pathway to dynamically reconfigurable microscale systems. Overall, the proposed strategy enables multilevel and reconfigurable assembly across individual particles, trimers, and multi-particle clusters.

Conclusion

This study presents a versatile strategy for the assembly and autonomous propulsion of heterogeneous microparticles on an OET platform. By exploiting interparticle dipolar interactions and EHD flows, we achieved reversible transitions between chain-like and ring-like structures. We further elucidated a novel self-propulsion mechanism for PS-metal dimers: asymmetric shadowing under top illumination produces a non-uniform photoconductivity distribution, which generates a lateral DEP force that drives dimer propulsion. To explain the observed non-monotonic frequency dependence of propulsion speed, we incorporated AC DEP levitation and a microlens effect. In the low frequency range, increased levitation height expands the focused optical field, rendering the shadow distribution more symmetric and thereby weakening propulsion. In the high frequency range, the diminished electric field gradient weakens the driving force, resulting in a gradual decrease in propulsion speed. Moreover, using dynamic optical patterns, we realize reversible control of the dimer propulsion direction, and further demonstrate multilevel reconfiguration in trimer and multi-cluster systems. Overall, the proposed strategy shows promise for the precise microscale manipulation and assembly of MNRs. Since it enables the accurate assembly of heterogeneous microparticles at the individual level, the assembled structures could remain stable after removal of the electric field when combined with particle locking methods, such as polyvinylpyrrolidone76, DNA strands77, or electrostatic interactions78. On this basis, the PS beads could be replaced with drug-loaded particles or functional sensing particles, while the metal beads could also be magnetized to introduce additional responsiveness. Therefore, this system may provide new opportunities for precise delivery, controllable manipulation, and the construction of functional microrobotic systems in complex biological environments.

Materials and methods

Experimental setup

The OET platform used in this study was modified from a Light Operator S1 (Micro Nano Robot Co., Ltd) by integrating an additional bottom-illumination module. Both the top and bottom illumination employed 10× objectives (Olympus MPLFLN10X), with an illumination wavelength of 610 nm. The illumination power was measured using an optical power meter (Thorlabs PM121D). The maximum illumination intensities were 0.8 W/cm2 for top illumination and 0.9 W/cm2 for bottom illumination. The AC electrical signals were generated by a signal generator (UTG2062B) and amplified using a power amplifier (ATA-105B).

Sample preparation

The PS beads and metal beads (silver-coated PS beads), both with a diameter of 20 μm, were supplied as an aqueous suspension (MicroNano Intelligent Technology Co., Ltd.). To prevent adhesion and ensure long-term stability, the beads were resuspended in a deionized (DI) water solution containing 0.05% (w/v) Pluronic F-127 (Sigma-Aldrich). The final conductivity of the suspension was measured to be 5e−4 S/m using a conductivity meter. Prior to each experiment, the suspension was sonicated for 5 min.
