Development of a prediction model for infectious mononucleosis using machine learning algorithms based on blood cell analysis parameters
Authors: Yuhong Qin, Lianjie Zhang, Hongbin Yu, Mingzhuang Zhao, Sicheng Qian, Yanqing Mao, Qingzhen Han, Tao Sun, Lin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInfectious mononucleosis (IM) presents with nonspecific clinical manifestations, leading to frequent misdiagnosis or delayed diagnosis, and is associated with potentially severe complications. Existing etiological diagnostic methods are characterized by prolonged turnaround times. This study aims to establish an IM model using eight machine learning algorithms and select the optimal one, so as to further improve the laboratory diagnostic accuracy of IM.MethodsThe study included 234 patients diagnosed with IM from April 2024 to December 2025 as the case group. The control group comprised 478 non-IM subjects, consisting of 236 patients with other pathogenic infections who exhibited reactive lymphocytes on microscopic examination and 242 healthy individuals. Recursive feature elimination (RFE) in conjunction with cross-validation was employed to rank feature importance and select optimal feature variables. Eight machine learning algorithms were trained, and their predictive performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), F1 score, Precision-Recall (PR) curve and Confusion Matrix. The contribution of each feature to the model’s predictions was quantified using SHapley Additive exPlanation (SHAP) analysis. Subsequently, the model’s performance was rigorously externally validated using an independent validation cohort.ResultsThree features - Reactive lymphocyte percentage (Reactive lymph%), Lym-Y, and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) - were selected for constructing the IM predictive model. The model constructed using the Adaptive Boosting Classifier(AdaBoost) machine learning algorithm demonstrated the best performance in the test set. It achieved an AUC of 0.928, an accuracy of 0.853, a sensitivity of 0.898, a specificity of 0.832, and an F1 score of 0.800. SHAP consistent with decision feature importance rankings, indicated that Reactive lymph% was the most significant feature in the predictive model; It was associated with an elevated risk of IM. Validation cohort also confirmed the robust performance of the AdaBoost predictive model, with an AUC of 0.923 and an F1 score of 0.797.ConclusionThe IM predictive model constructed based on the AdaBoost machine learning algorithm combined with three blood cell analysis parameters exhibits satisfactory predictive efficacy. Based on the established model, the missed laboratory diagnosis rate in IM may potentially be reduced, pending prospective validation.Keywords: Infectious mononucleosis, Blood cell analysis, Machine learning algorithms, Reactive lymphocyte, Predictive model

Introduction

Infectious Mononucleosis (IM) is an infectious disease caused by Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection that predominantly affects children, adolescents, and young adults. Due to advances in healthcare and evolving lifestyle practices, the incidence of IM has been increasing progressively among adolescent and adult populations [1, 2]. Furthermore, IM accompanied by complications such as liver injury may present with more severe clinical manifestations, potentially necessitating hospitalization [3]. The classic clinical triad of IM comprises fever, pharyngitis, and cervical lymphadenopathy, potentially accompanied by hepatosplenomegaly. The characteristic peripheral blood profile reveals lymphocytosis and the presence of reactive lymphocytes. Due to its clinical similarity to other conditions—including cytomegalovirus infection, toxoplasmosis, and various hematological disorders—IM is prone to misdiagnosis or delayed diagnosis. Consequently, laboratory findings play a crucial role in clinical decision-making [4, 5]. Diagnosis of EBV infection generally depends on cytological evaluation (Reactive lymph% >10%, lymphocyte percentage > 50%, or lymphocyte count > 5 × 10⁹/L) and etiological testing (Viral Capsid Antigen - Immunoglobulin M, Early Antigen - Immunoglobulin G, EBV nucleic acid) [6, 7]. Cytological assessment is highly operator-dependent, whereas etiological methods are characterized by prolonged turnaround times and a potential risk of false-negative findings.

In recent years, automated hematology analyzers equipped with multi-parameter capabilities and high-resolution scatter plots have emerged as a novel approach for the rapid screening of IM. The complete blood count scattergram, which analyzes scattered light signals and fluorescence intensity of leukocytes, enables the identification of atypical lymphocyte populations and reveals their characteristic morphological patterns, thereby offering valuable guidance for the early detection of reactive lymphocytes [8, 9]. However, inter-operator variability in scattergram interpretation necessitates the integration of manual microscopic examination in laboratory practice to enhance diagnostic accuracy [10].

Machine learning integrates statistics and computer science, employing diverse mathematical functions to train datasets and optimize parameters, thereby extracting complex patterns from multifaceted data that may elude intuitive human detection. This approach has been extensively adopted across various disease contexts and demonstrates considerable promise in the development of predictive models [11–13]. Machine learning algorithms offer the potential to establish early diagnostic prediction models for IM, thereby reducing clinical misdiagnosis and assisting clinicians in developing appropriate treatment strategies. In our study, eight machine learning models incorporating hematological parameters were constructed and externally validated, from which the optimal model was identified to enhance the laboratory diagnostic accuracy of IM and facilitate its popularization and application in primary healthcare settings.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

This retrospective study included 234 patients with IM, 236 patients with other infections, and 242 healthy controls from the Fourth Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University between April 2024 and December 2025. Data collected between April 2024 and April 2025 were used for model development, while data from May 2025 to December 2025 were employed for model validation. Reactive lymphocytes were detected by manual microscopic examination of blood cell analysis in all IM and other infection cases.The diagnosis of IM was established according to the 2024 IDSA/ASM laboratory diagnostic guidelines [14]. Patients with indeterminate diagnoses or missing data were excluded from the study. This study received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Fourth Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University, with a waiver of informed consent, in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki (No. 20260001).

Methods

Data collection and feature selection

Patient baseline characteristics were collected, including age, gender and Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection status. The model incorporated a total of 155 feature parameters, comprising one peripheral blood smear parameter (Reactive lymph%) and 154 parameters derived from complete blood count reports and related research variables. These included neutrophil count (Neu#), lymphocyte count (Lym#), monocyte count (Mon#), platelet count (PLT), red blood cell count (RBC), neutrophil percentage (Neu%), lymphocyte percentage (Lym%), monocyte percentage (Mon%), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and various scattergram parameters (e.g., Lym-X, Lym-Y, Lym-Z), among others. All hematological scattergram parameters were measured using clinically validated Mindray BC-6800 PLUS and BC-7500 CRP analyzers with strict daily quality control, and intra-laboratory comparisons were performed twice per year. The Reactive lymph% was determined by two morphologists (one of whom was a senior technician) through microscopic examination following staining. Inter-observer concordance followed WS/T 806–2022, achieving 95% (20 slides), above the required ≥ 90%. The Wright-Giemsa staining solution used was manufactured by Zhuhai Baso Biotechnology Co., Ltd. All reagents used in conjunction with the analyzers in this study were original manufacturer reagents and remained within their designated expiration periods.

All included data were first preprocessed through data cleaning and normalization. Given the sensitivity of the subsequent machine learning algorithms to feature scales, StandardScaler was applied to scale the numerical features to have a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one. Feature selection was performed using RFE, a method well-suited for high-dimensional datasets, with logistic regression as the base estimator was used. Following best practices [15], Beginning with the full set of 155 features, the algorithm recursively eliminated the least important features based on their coefficient weights. In each iteration, the contribution of each feature to model performance was assessed using 5-fold cross-validation. The feature with the minimal contribution was subsequently removed, thereby iteratively reducing the number of features. Finally, the selected features were ranked based on their importance within the model to identify the key feature variables. we reduced 155 initial features to 3 key features, achieving a feature-to-case ratio of 1:78 and Events Per Variable (EPV) > 70, meeting recommended thresholds.

Model development and performance evaluation

The selected feature variables were used to develop the IM predictive diagnostic model. According to the TRIPOD guidelines [16], the dataset comprising 498 samples was initially partitioned into a training set (n = 348) and a test set (n = 150) using a 70:30 ratio for model development, This 70:30 split was selected based on methodological recommendations for moderate sample sizes [17–18]. After stratified sampling, the training set contained 33% positive cases (114/348) and the test set contained 33% positive cases (49/150), maintaining consistency with the original dataset (33%). Data splitting was implemented with scikit-learn (version 1.6.1), and stratified sampling was applied to maintain consistency in outcome proportions between the training and testing cohort. Eight machine learning algorithms were developed and validated, including Logistic Regression (LR), Multi-Layer Perceptron Classifier (MLP), Decision Tree Classifier (DT), K-nearest Neighbors Classifier (KN), Random Forest Classifier (RF), Adaptive Boosting Classifier (AdaBoost), Quadratic Discriminant Analysis (QDA), and Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA).The predictive performance of the models was evaluated by calculating the area under the curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and F1 score for each machine learning algorithm. The optimal model was further evaluated for diagnostic performance using PR curve and confusion matrix analyses. SHAP analysis was applied to elucidate the contribution of each feature to the model’s predictions. Subsequently, independent validation was performed on a separate dataset comprising 214 samples to assess the model’s robustness and generalizability (Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26.0). Continuous variables with a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s), and comparisons between two groups were conducted using the two independent samples t-test. Continuous variables with a non-normal distribution were expressed as median (Q1, Q3) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages), and comparisons between groups were performed using the chi-squared (χ²) test. All tests were two-tailed, and P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Feature selection for model development

Optimal feature variables for model development were selected using data cleaning and normalization, RFE, and feature importance ranking to construct the IM prediction model. the three features with the highest contribution to classification were selected as Reactive lymph%, Lym-Y, and PLR (Fig. 2A). The mean AUC reached its maximum when the number of features was three, indicating that this feature combination possessed the best discriminative ability (Fig. 2B).

Model development and assessment

In the training set, all eight machine learning models developed for IM prediction exhibited excellent fitting performance, with consistently high AUC values ranging from 0.931 to 0.959. Among these, the KN and RF algorithms achieved the highest performance (Fig. 3A). In the testing set, all models maintained strong generalization ability, with AUC values predominantly exceeding 0.890. Notably, the AdaBoost algorithm demonstrated superior performance, achieving an AUC of 0.928, which was significantly higher than that of the other models (Fig. 3B).

The PR curve demonstrated that the AdaBoost model achieved high recall rates in both the training and testing sets (0.965 and 0.898, respectively), indicating a low rate of missed diagnoses (Fig. 3C-D). The confusion matrix further revealed that the AdaBoost model misclassified only 8 positive samples in the training set and 5 positive samples in the testing set (Fig. 3E-F).

Model interpretability analysis

To gain insight into how the three selected features influence the model’s predictions, SHAP analysis was employed to elucidate the “black box” of IM screening. The calculated SHAP values revealed the relative importance of the three indicators, ranking in descending order as follows: Reactive lymph%, Lym-Y, and PLR (Fig. 4A). To visually represent the impact distribution of each feature on the model’s output, a SHAP summary plot was generated. This analysis identified Reactive lymph% as the most influential feature in the model. Notably, an increase in Reactive lymph% was associated with a higher predicted risk of infectious mononucleosis (Fig. 4B).

Model assessment in the validation cohort

To further evaluate the robustness of the model, an independent dataset comprising 214 samples was employed for external validation (Table 2). In the external validation cohort, the AdaBoost model demonstrated excellent robustness and overall performance. It achieved a high AUC of 0.923, along with strong sensitivity (0.900) and specificity (0.826). Its F1 score (0.797) was among the highest of all models (Fig. 5A).The confusion matrix further indicated that the AdaBoost model misclassified only 7 positive samples (Fig. 5B).

Discussion

This study represents the first effort to develop a machine learning-based diagnostic model for IM by integrating hematological parameters from automated analyzers with manual blood smear findings. Similar to the comparative approach adopted by Rafie et al. [19], which systematically evaluated XGBoost and CatBoost for Type 2 diabetes prediction, our study extended this methodological paradigm to eight machine learning algorithms for infectious disease prediction. Among the eight candidate models evaluated, the AdaBoost algorithm exhibited the best overall performance for IM screening, achieving an AUC of 0.928, accuracy of 0.853, sensitivity of 0.898, specificity of 0.832, and F1 score of 0.800. External validation further confirmed its robustness, yielding an AUC of 0.923 and an F1 score of 0.797.This model can serve as a stable and reliable automated decision-support tool for clinical diagnosis.Moreover, the short turnaround time associated with complete blood count analysis ensures timely results, underscoring its substantial clinical utility.Furthermore, we employed SHAP analysis to interpret the model and observed that elevated Reactive lymph% were associated with an increased predicted risk of infectious mononucleosis, a finding consistent with prior studies.

IM is caused by Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection. EBV primarily infects B lymphocytes, which triggers the activation and extensive clonal expansion of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (predominantly CD8 + T cells), resulting in a marked increase in peripheral blood lymphocyte count. Concurrently, virus-induced bone marrow suppression, accelerated neutrophil apoptosis, and the chemotactic migration of peripheral neutrophils to sites of inflammation collectively contribute to a relative or absolute neutropenia. These observations are consistent with the findings of the present study [20, 21].

Considerable research has been devoted to the diagnosis of IM. While reliance on clinical symptoms alone - specifically the classic triad of fever, pharyngitis, and cervical lymphadenopathy - offers high sensitivity, its specificity remains limited This limitation arises because a wide range of viral respiratory infections can manifest with comparable clinical features [22]. Although peripheral blood smear examination for Reactive lymph% (≥ 10%) is operationally simple, cost-effective, and offers considerable diagnostic value during the early disease course, its positivity rate is significantly influenced by operator expertise, timing of phlebotomy, and clinical stage of the disease. Consequently, the false-negative rate warrants careful consideration [23]. Molecular biology techniques, such as real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) for assessing peripheral blood EBV-DNA load, offer high sensitivity and specificity, and hold particular diagnostic value in immunocompromised individuals or clinically atypical cases where conventional testing is inconclusive.However, given that low-level EBV-DNA latency may also be detectable in healthy populations and that the test has a prolonged turnaround time, excessive reliance on PCR-based diagnosis may increase the risk of overdiagnosis [24–26]. Traditional heterophile antibody tests have reported sensitivities of 79–95% and specificities of 82–99% [27]. But it involves additional financial costs. In this study, an AdaBoost-based diagnostic model incorporating three routine laboratory indicators was developed for IM, demonstrating improved diagnostic performance. AdaBoost builds a team through a ‘relay of error correction’ and makes decisions via collective voting. Among ensemble learning approaches, AdaBoost is highly accurate, fast to train, works well with complex medical data, and automatically identifies which features matter most. These qualities make it popular for disease diagnosis (e.g., diabetes, cancer) and mental health prediction [28]. The intrinsic interrelationships among the three selected features—Reactive lymph%, Lym-Y, and PLR - exhibit fundamental differences between the target disease state and both healthy conditions and other disease entities.More specifically, this distinction likely reflects the coordinated and dynamic nature of the host immune response during viral infection. For example, the elevation of reactive lymphocytes—reflecting activation and proliferation of the lymphoid lineage—may exhibit a distinct proportional and synergistic relationship with thrombocytopenia, which potentially results from direct viral suppression or increased consumption [29–30]. This specific relationship is not observed in healthy individuals or in patients with other inflammatory conditions. The AdaBoost model offers advantages including high precision, automated feature selection, and robustness against overfitting, enabling it to effectively capture and leverage disease-specific, non-linear feature interactions.Rather than depending solely on the elevation or reduction of individual indicators—such as PLR exceeding 150 or reactive lymphocyte percentage greater than 10%—it comprehensively evaluates the intricate patterns of how these indicators co-vary, thereby enabling more accurate classification [31].

The present study focused on three key indicators: Reactive lymph%, Lym-Y, and PLR. Reactive lymphocyte represents a morphological correlate of lymphocyte activation and proliferation triggered by viral antigen stimulation, and serves as a critical screening indicator for conditions including infectious mononucleosis and viral hepatitis [6].Our model effectively integrated quantitative reactive lymphocyte data—comprising both percentage and scattergram parameters—with the strengths of machine learning algorithms, thereby improving its predictive performance. Furthermore, PLR is a marker of systemic inflammation that reflects the balance between platelet aggregation and adaptive immunity [32]. In EBV infection, the virus infects B lymphocytes and triggers a CD8 + T cell response, leading to lymphocyte subset imbalance and immunoglobulin abnormalities [33]. PLR integrates platelet and lymphocyte components, and its changes may capture EBV-induced immune dysregulation. Previous studies have shown that PLR levels are elevated in EBV DNA-positive children and correlate with EBV-specific antibodies [34]. In our study, PLR emerged as a key feature with high SHAP values, suggesting that beyond its predictive value for EBV-IM diagnosis, PLR may reflect the host’s antiviral immune status.

Furthermore, the scattergrams generated by contemporary hematology analyzers serve as a visual representation of cellular optical properties—including granularity, internal complexity, and nucleic acid content—and contain a wealth of information that extends considerably beyond conventional three-lineage cell counts [8]. A key innovation of this study lies in its effort to convert abstract scattergram information into quantifiable features suitable for machine learning model training.

This study has several limitations. First, this is a single-center retrospective study. This systematic review notes that current AI studies on polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) generally lack independent external validation. Most models are developed with single-center datasets, limiting generalizability. It emphasizes that multicenter and independent external validation is essential to improve clinical applicability [35]. Second, the standardization of scattergram feature extraction presents a challenge, as scattergram patterns vary across different instrument platforms. Although data cleaning and normalization were performed in the initial stage of this study, certain discrepancies may persist, potentially limiting the direct generalization and application of the current model across different platforms. Third, any viral infection capable of stimulating T lymphocyte activation may potentially interfere with the model, leading to false-positive results, as the symptoms of infectious mononucleosis are primarily attributed to T cell proliferation and activation [36, 37]. Following JEHP guidance [38], we plan to conduct prospective multicenter validation with standardized data collection protocols, pilot feasibility testing across 2–3 sites, and collaborative model monitoring through an open-source network. Future research could explore deep learning-based image recognition techniques to perform end-to-end learning directly from scattergrams, which may more fully exploit their information potential [39, 40].

Conclusion

this study successfully constructed and compared multiple machine learning models, ultimately identifying the AdaBoost model as the optimal predictive model based on blood cell analysis parameters (Reactive lymph%, Lym-Y, and PLR). Within this model, Reactive lymph% contributed the most to the prediction. Similar to Seyfi et al. [41], our model prioritizes accessibility. Using only routine blood parameters, it is low-cost and easy to implement. With Automated hematology analyzer with digital morphology in primary care, it can rapidly stratify risk, enabling early identification of EBV-IM and timely referral. It has the potential to improve screening efficiency for relevant diseases, assist clinicians in early decision-making.
