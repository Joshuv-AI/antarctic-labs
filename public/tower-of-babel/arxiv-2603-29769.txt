Exceptional Sets for Quasiconformal Mappings in General Metric Spaces II Thanks: 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification. Primary 30C65, 30L10; Secondary 28A80, 30L05 Thanks: P. K. has been supported by the Research Council of Finland, grant number 364210.
Behnam Esmayli, Pekka Koskela, Khanh Nguyen

Abstract
. A homemorphism between domains in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} , n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 is quasiconformal, with its intricate analytic and geometric consequences, if the (pointwise) linear dilatation – a purely metric quantity – is uniformly bounded. Gehring proved that it will suffice to verify the uniform bound up to a set of measure zero as long as we can show that the dilatation is finite outside a subset of finite Hausdorff– ( n − 1 ) (n-1) measure. In short, we say that we can allow an exceptional codimension 1 1 subset. In the metric setting, it has been proved, roughly speaking, that one can allow an exceptional codimension p p subset, p ≥ 1 p\geq 1 , if the source space satisfies a p p -Poincaré inequality. We prove, effectively, the sharpness of the latter claim.



1.
Introduction

Quasiconformal homeomorphisms between domains in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
, enjoy many regularity properties. They are absolutely continuous on almost every line, belong to Sobolev spaces of higher order than in their definition, are (locally) Hölder continuous, have the Lusin N property, etc. These properties are tied to and follow from the equivalence of the so-called
metric, analytic, and geometric
definitions of quasiconformality in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Fundamental works in the Euclidean setting are due to Ahlfors, Gehring, and Väisälä.

Study of quasiconformal mappings in non-Riemannian settings (e.g. in the Heisenberg groups and more general Carnot groups) was motivated by complex dynamics and rigidity questions in geometry. Important results were obtained by Mostow, Pansu, Korányi, Bourdon, Pajot, and many others. We refer to the introduction of
[
1
]
for references and a brief history of the major developments in the theory of quasiconformal mappings in non-Euclidean settings.

Heinonen and Koskela
[
9
]
found techniques that were metric and did not depend on the special structures (e.g. foliation) of the underlying Euclidean or Carnot groups. In
[
10
]
, they introduced the so-called “metric spaces with controlled geometry” which unified many of the existing results about quasiconformal mappings.

1.1.
Removability of sets in metric quasiconformality

Let
f\colon X\to Y
be a homeomorphism between two metric spaces (without any isolated points). For
r>0
, set

\displaystyle L_{f}(x,r)=

\displaystyle\sup\{d(f(y),f(x)):d(y,x)\leq r\},

\displaystyle l_{f}(x,r)=

\displaystyle\inf\{d(f(y),f(x)):d(y,x)\geq r\},

and let

H_{f}(x)=\limsup\limits_{r\to 0}\frac{L_{f}(x,r)}{l_{f}(x,r)}.

We say that a homeomorphism
f\colon X\to Y
is
metrically quasiconformal
if there exists some
1\leq H<\infty
such that
H_{f}(x)\leq H
for every
x\in X
.

The definition, requiring a distortion control at
every
point and
all
small scales, is quite inconvenient to verify in practice. In the Euclidean setting, already Gehring
[
3
,
4
]
proved that it suffices to verify the bound a priori only outside a set of
\sigma
-finite
{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}
-measure.

It was proved in Heinonen-Koskela
[
9
]
that, in the Euclidean setting, one can replace
\limsup
with
\liminf
, i.e. for quasiconformality it suffices to have a uniform bound on

h_{f}(x)=\liminf\limits_{r\to 0}\frac{L_{f}(x,r)}{l_{f}(x,r)},

i.e., a bound that is a priori on
some
sequence of small scales.

Finally, Kallunki-Koskela
[
13
,
12
]
proved that one can work with
h_{f}
and at the same time allow an exceptional set:

Theorem 1.1

(
[
12
, Corollary 1.2]
)
.

Let
f\colon\Omega\to\Omega^{\prime}
be a homeomorphism between domains in
\mathbb{R}^{n},n\geq 2
. Suppose that
E\subset\Omega
has
\sigma
-finite
{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}
-measure,
h_{f}(x)<\infty
at every
x\in\Omega\setminus E
, and for some
H<\infty
,
h_{f}(x)\leq H
almost everywhere on
\Omega
. Then
f
is quasiconformal, i.e. for some
H^{\prime}<\infty
, we have
H_{f}(x)\leq H^{\prime}
for every
x\in\Omega
.

Such reductions in assumptions were motivated by and found applications in complex dynamics, see e.g.
[
23
,
6
,
25
,
18
,
5
,
5
]
. Observe that the assumptions on
h_{f}
are stronger than simply requiring
h_{f}(x)\leq H
almost everywhere. In fact, the homeomorphism
f
defined in (
7.1
), when viewed as on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, shows the necessity of assuming more that
\operatorname*{ess\,sup}h_{f}<\infty
.

In metric setting, one is interested in minimal assumptions on
H_{f}
, or preferably on
h_{f}
, that still imply regularity properties for homeomorphisms
f\colon X\to Y
, for example, quasisymmetry, Sobolev regularity, etc. As in the Euclidean setting, study of such properties uses the relationships between
metric
,
analytic
, and
geometric
(modulus) definitions of quasiconformality. “A key point in connecting these definitions is absolute continuity on paths” (
[
17
]
).

The starting point of our investigation is the following result due to Balogh-Koskela-Rogovin:

Theorem 1.2

(
[
1
,
16
]
)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be proper metric measure spaces with
X
locally Ahlfors
Q
-regular,
Q>1
, and let
f\colon X\to Y
be a homeomorphism. Suppose that there exists a set

with
\sigma
-finite
{\mathcal{H}}^{Q-p}
-measure,

, such that

at every

. Finally, assume that

is

-regular off

. Then,

supports a

-Poincaré inequality, then

and, in particular,

Q
-a.e. path.

Recall that proper means that closed bounded sets are compact. Other terminology will be explained in Section
2
. The less standard notion of

being “locally Ahlfors

-regular off

2.1
. In our settings, being in the Newtonian-Sobolev space

is simply equivalent to having an

-integrable upper-gradient.

For more recent results of this nature, see
[
26
]
,
[
22
]
and
[

]
. The homeomorphism in Theorem

, under some additional natural assumptions on the spaces, enjoys other regularity properties. For example, if

satisfies a

show that

is analytically quasiconformal and it is locally quasisymmetric, hence geometrically quasiconformal. We will not pursue such details and focus only on Sobolev regularity in the rest of this paper.

1.2.
Main results

It is worth remarking that the
N^{1,p}
-regularity part of the conclusion of Theorem
1.2
does not require any Poincaré inequality. However, Koskela-Wildrick
[
16
]
showed that
N^{1,Q}
-regularity can fail in the absence of a
p
-Poincaré inequality. They prove this by constructing an example in the plane, which is the inspiration for the construction of our main object in the current paper.

Although their example cannot support a
p
-Poincaré inequality due to Theorem
1.2
, Koskela-Wildrick
[
16
]
conjecture that it may support some weaker Poincaré inequality, i.e. a
q
-Poincaré inequality for some
q>p
. Using our construction, we confirm their conjecture, as the two constructions are practically bi-Lipschitz equivalent (see Section
3.4
).

We prove not only the necessity of a
p
-Poincaré inequality on
X
, but also the sharpness of

in order to have the full Sobolev regularity in Theorem
1.2
.

Theorem 1.3
.

For every
p\in(1,2)
and every
\varepsilon>0
, there exist compact spaces
X
and
Y
, a closed set
E\subset X
, and a homeomorphism
f\colon X\to Y
such that

•

X
is compact, geodesic, and Ahlfors
2
-regular,

•

Y
is locally Ahlfors
2
-regular off
f(E)
,

•

0<{\mathcal{H}}^{2-p}(E)<\infty
,

•

H_{f}(x)=1
at every
x\in X\setminus E
,

•

X
supports a
(p+\varepsilon)
-Poincaré inequality,

but for every
q\geq p+\varepsilon
, there exists a family of paths with positive
q
-modulus such that
f
is not absolutely continuous on them. In particular,
f\notin N^{1,q}_{loc}(X,Y)
. Moreover,
X
and
Y
can be chosen to be compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, with the induced metric and measure.

In order to prove Theorem
1.3
we will construct a two-parameter family
\mathbf{X}:=\mathbf{X}(\lambda,\nu),\,\lambda\in(0,1/2),\,\nu\in\mathbb{N}
, of subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
that are compact and Ahlfors
2
-regular. Our second main result (Theorem

) proves that
\mathbf{X}
supports a

-Poincaré inequality if and only if

, where

is explicitly given by formula (

), and in particular depends only on

and

.

Given

, by choosing the parameters
\lambda
and
\nu
appropriately, we will have
{\swabfamily p}_{0}\in(p,p+\varepsilon)
, which completes the requirements on

in Theorem

. The homeomorphism

and
Y=f(X)
will be as in
[
16
]
, and

will be the Cartesian product of two Cantor sets (Section

).

The most involved part of proving Theorem

is confirming the Poincaré inequality on
\mathbf{X}
, i.e., Theorem

, which occupies most of the paper. Theorem

and its proof can be of independent interest. We prove the Poincaré inequality on

by constructing explicit Semmes’s pencils of curves.

Remark 1.4
.

Analogues of our spaces

can be constructed in higher dimensions and used to prove higher dimensional versions of Theorem

.

1.3.
Comparison to non-self-similar Sierpiński carpets

Our methods are somewhat similar to those employed in
[
20
]
, but here we carry the calculations until we obtain well-known pointwise estimates, which are sufficient for the validity of the Poincaré inequality. In
[
20
]
, the authors appeal to a characterization of Poincaré inequality via modulus bounds, due to Keith
[
15
]
, and choose to use their pencils of curves to obtain these modulus bounds rather than the pointwise inequalities.

However, our spaces
\mathbf{X}
and the Sierpiński carpets in
[
20
]
are constructed very differently. While the carpets arise from
deleting
a countable collection of squares, our spaces
\mathbf{X}
are constructed from
adding
(union of) countably many squares. As a result, while one obtains carpets as a Gromov-Hausdorff limit of its finite approximations, a fact that is utilized in the proofs in
[
20
]
, it is not obvious how one can approximate our
\mathbf{X}
with a sequence of, say, “nice” Lipschitz domains in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

2.
Preliminaries

Throughout,
(X,d,\mu)
will be a metric space, with no isolated points, equipped with a Borel measure
\mu
.

2.1.
Ahlfors regularity

We say
\mu
is
doubling
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\mu(2B)\leq C\mu(B),\quad\text{for every ball $B\subset X$}.

We further require that for one ball, hence for all balls,
0<\mu(B)<\infty
. We call
C

the doubling constant
of
\mu
.

A stronger condition is Ahlfors regularity. We say that
\mu
is
Ahlfors
Q
-regular
if for every
x\in X
and every
0<r<\operatorname{diam}X
we have

C^{-1}r^{Q}\leq\mu(B(x,r))\leq Cr^{Q},

for a constant
C>0
independent of
x
and
r
. We say that
X
is
locally Ahlfors
Q
-regular
if for every compact
A\subset X
there exist constants
C>0
and
r_{0}>0
such that

\forall\,x\in A,\;\forall\,r\in(0,r_{0}):\;C^{-1}r^{Q}\leq\mu(B(x,r))\leq Cr^{Q}.

Definition 2.1

(
[
16
]
)
.

Let
F\subset X
be non-empty. We say that
X
is
Ahlfors
Q
-regular off
F
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that for every
x\in X\setminus F
there exists
r_{x}>0
such that

\forall\,r\in(0,r_{x})\,:\quad C^{-1}r^{Q}\leq\mu(B(x,r))\leq Cr^{Q}.

2.2.
Absolute continuity and upper-gradients

For a rectifiable path in
X
we denote by
\gamma_{s}\colon[0,\ell(\gamma)]\to X
the arc-length parameterization of
\gamma
. Given a Borel function
g\colon X\to[0,\infty]
and a rectifiable path
\gamma
, we define

\int_{\gamma}g\,ds:=\int_{0}^{\ell(\gamma)}g(\gamma_{s}(t))\,dt.

Let
(Y,d)
be another metric space. We say that a function
u\colon X\to Y
is
absolutely continuous
on a rectifiable curve
\gamma
in
X
if the composition
u\circ\gamma_{s}\colon[0,\ell(\gamma)]\to Y
is absolutely continuous.

We say that a Borel function
g\colon X\to[0,\infty]
is an upper-gradient of a continuous mapping
u\colon X\to Y
if

(2.1)

d(u(y),u(x))\leq\int_{\gamma}g\,ds,

for every
x,y
in
X
and every rectifiable curve
\gamma
that joins
x
and

. We say that
g
is an upper-gradient for a continuous mapping
u
defined on (or restricted to)

if (
2.1
) holds for all rectifiable curves in
U
.

The following is easy to prove from the definitions and the so-called absolute continuity of integral.

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose
g
is an upper-gradient of a continuous mapping
u\colon X\to Y
and
\int_{\gamma}g\,ds<\infty
for a rectifiable curve
\gamma
. Then
u
is absolutely continuous on
\gamma
.

Let
\Gamma
be a collection of paths in
X
. For
1\leq p<\infty
,
the

-modulus
of

is defined as

\operatorname{Mod}_{p}(\Gamma):=\inf_{g}\int_{X}g^{p}\,d\mu,

where the infimum is taken over all Borel functions
g\colon X\to[0,\infty]
such that
\int_{\gamma}g\,ds\geq 1
for all rectifiable curves
\gamma\in\Gamma
.

We say that a certain property holds for
p
-almost every curve
in
X
, written

-a.e. curve
, if the collection of curves for which it fails has zero

-modulus. By Hölder’s inequality, if

then a property that holds for

; but, of course, the converse does not necessarily hold.

Lemma 2.3

(
[
8
, Lemma 5.2.8]
)
.

For every Borel function

,
\int_{\gamma}g\,ds<\infty
for
p
-a.e. curve

in

From Lemmas
2.2
and

Corollary 2.4

If

and

is absolutely continuous on

-almost every curve.

When

is locally compact and

is in

if it has an upper-gradient that belongs to

. We will not need further properties of the space

, known as the Newtonian-Sobolev spaces in literature (

is absolutely continuous on

2.7.
The outline of our proof of the
p
-Poincaré inequality

The key phenomena behind the example of the bow-tie space in the previous section, which will inspire the later constructions and analyses in our work, are as follows:

(1)

Due to the pinching in the space itself, the traces of our path families must lose measure at certain regions, which introduces unbounded coarea densities when comparing the double integrals to the area integrals;
1
1

1

The pinching at the endpoints
x
and
y
is not a problem due to (
2.6
).

(2)

We use Hölder’s inequality to separate these coarea densities from the
L^{p}
-integrals of upper-gradients;

(3)

The coarea densities are in
L^{p/(p-1)}
only for certain range of
p>{\swabfamily p}_{0}
;

(4)

If calculations work out, we obtain pointwise inequalities involving the truncated maximal function of
g^{p}
, hence, a
p
-Poincaré inequality follows by Lemma
2.6
.

Our proof of the Poincaré inequality for our space
\mathbf{X}
will be a more involved execution of the above. In Section
4
we construct pencils of curves between arbitrary regions of our space. In Section
5
we estimate the coarea densities associated with these path families. Using these, in Section
6
we prove the pointwise inequalities that are sufficient for the Poincaré inequality.

In our space
\mathbf{X}
there are two competing factors. The first factor is that our space is made up of “good parts” that are glued to each other along not just single points but along copies of a Cantor set
{\swabfamily C}
. So, the pinching is not as extreme as in the bow-tie space. This helps to get a better Poincaré inequality, i.e. with
p<2
.

Indeed, it is well-known that if we glue two unit squares along a Cantor set
{\swabfamily C}
, then the space supports a
p
-Poincaré inequality for all
p>2-\dim{\swabfamily C}
. This is proved in
[
10
, Section 6.14]
, but also follows easily from our construction of the path family
\Gamma(I)
later, for which we calculate the coarea density and prove that it is integrable exactly when
p>2-\dim{\swabfamily C}
.

However, the second factor works against the Poincaré inequality: our paths have to cross the Cantor set
{\swabfamily C}
not once or twice, but “un-countably many times” determined by a second Cantor set
{\swabfamily D}
. This intensifies the blowups of the coarea density for the path families, preventing a
p
-Poincaré inequality for
p
too close to
1
.

Therefore, we should expect better Poincaré inequalities if

{\swabfamily C}
has a larger Hausdorff dimension or
{\swabfamily D}
has a smaller Hausdorff dimension
(Remark
6.2
).

The main technical achievement of this paper is that in the case of self-similar Cantor sets, we explicitly calculate the sharp
{\swabfamily p}_{0}
such that our space satisfies a
p
-Poincaré inequality for all
p>{\swabfamily p}_{0}
, and for no
p\leq{\swabfamily p}_{0}
.

Lemma 3.6

Observe however that countably many points from

Proof of Lemma
3.6
.

We first prove (c).
Let
p_{j}\in Q_{j}
satisfy
\|p_{j}-p_{0}\|\to 0
.
For any cube
Q
, only finitely many
p_{j}
can satisfy
p_{j}\in Q
; otherwise a subsequence of
p_{j}
would lie within
Q
and since
Q
is closed, its limit
p_{0}
would belong to
Q
, hence to
\mathbf{X}^{\prime}
, which is a contradiction.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary.
Note that
there are only finitely many cubes
Q
with
\operatorname{diam}Q\geq\varepsilon
. By the preceding argument, there exists
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that if
j>N_{0}
, then
p_{j}
is in none of those cubes. Hence, for every
j>N_{0},
we have
\operatorname{diam}Q_{j}<\varepsilon
. This proves
\operatorname{diam}Q_{j}\to 0
as
j\to\infty
.

We next prove claim (a). Since
\mathbf{X}
is the closure of the union of all cubes, there is a sequence of cubes
Q_{j}
and points
p_{j}:=(x_{j},y_{j})\in Q_{j}
such that
\|p_{j}-p_{0}\|\to 0
. By claim (c),
\operatorname{diam}Q_{j}\to 0
. For each
j\in\mathbb{N}
, choose some
(x_{j}^{\prime},y_{j})\in Q_{j}
with
x_{j}^{\prime}\in{\swabfamily C}
(such points exist). It now follows that

\displaystyle|x_{j}^{\prime}-x_{0}|

\displaystyle\leq|x_{j}^{\prime}-x_{j}|+|x_{j}-x_{0}|\leq\operatorname{diam}Q_{j}+\|p_{j}-p_{0}\|.

Both terms on the right converge to zero, hence
x_{j}^{\prime}\to x_{0}
. Since
x_{j}^{\prime}\in{\swabfamily C}
for all
j
and since
{\swabfamily C}
is closed, we conclude that
x_{0}\in{\swabfamily C}
. Now, observe that if
y_{0}
were not in
{\swabfamily D}
, then
y_{0}
would be in some
J\in\mathcal{J}
, and so,
p_{0}=(x_{0},y_{0})
would belong to a cube on row
J
; which contradicts the assumption that
p_{0}\notin\mathbf{X}^{\prime}
. Thus,
y_{0}\in{\swabfamily D}
and we conclude that
p_{0}\in{\swabfamily C}\times{\swabfamily D}
.

Finally, we prove claim (b). By properties of the Cantor set
{\swabfamily D}
, there exists a sequence of
J_{j}\in\mathcal{J}
and
y_{j}\in J_{j}
, such that
y_{j}\to y
as
j\to\infty
. Clearly, each
p_{j}:=(x_{0},y_{j})
belongs to a cube on row
J
and
|y_{j}-y_{0}|=\|p_{j}-p_{0}\|\to 0
. The proof is complete.
∎

\mathbf{X}
contains the set

Proof.

Fix an arbitrary

with

Lemma 3.9
.

For every

there exists a path

Proof.

Indeed, by Lemma

and by the assumption (

We additionally assume two key conditions on

Definition 3.10

(

Let

(

For the self-similar Cantor sets both properties hold. The proof easily follows from the construction; see Remark

.

3.3.1.
Quasiconvexity

We define
the length metric
on
\mathbf{X}
by

d(p,q):=\inf\{\ell(\gamma):\text{$\gamma$ is a path in $(\mathbf{X},\|\cdot\|)$ that joins $p$ to $q$}\}.

Lemma
3.9
shows that indeed
(\mathbf{X},d)
is a metric space.

Clearly,
d(p,q)\geq\|p-q\|
for all points and
d(p,q)=\|p-q\|
if the two points belong to the same cube. But a quantitative reverse relation holds, i.e.
(\mathbf{X},\|\cdot\|)
is so-called
quasiconvex
(cf.
[
16
, Proposition 4.10]
).

Proposition 3.13
.

If in the construction of
\mathbf{X}
, corresponding to
{\swabfamily C}(\lambda)
and
{\swabfamily D}
, the removed intervals for
{\swabfamily D}
are
\rho
-separated and
\tau
-dense
for some
\lambda\in(0,1/2),\tau\geq 1,0<\rho<1
,
then the identity map
(\mathbf{X},d)\to(\mathbf{X},\|\cdot\|)
is bi-Lipschitz, with a constant that depends only on
\lambda
,
\rho
and
\tau
.

The idea is that to join a pair of points between cubes that do not align vertically, we first move “vertically”, from each one, until we reach a common large cube that allows to join horizontally. The separatedness and density conditions ensure that we can do this within a reasonable distance from the original points. In Section
4
we will construct much more: a family of curves between any two cubes. So, we do not provide a separate proof here.

It is a well-known fact that if we equip a proper quasiconvex space with the length metric, then it becomes geodesic; see e.g.
[
8
, Lemmas 8.3.11 and 8.3.12]
.

Corollary 3.14
.

The metric space
(\mathbf{X},d)
is geodesic, and the identity map
(\mathbf{X},\|\cdot\|)\to(\mathbf{X},d)
is bi-Lipschitz. Moreover, the class of rectifiable curves and the length of individual curves are the same with respect to either metric.

In what follows, the default metric on
\mathbf{X}
will be the intrinsic metric
d
, with respect to which
\mathbf{X}
is geodesic.

3.3.2.
Ahlfors
2
-regularity

Recall that
\mu
is the restriction of the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
to
\mathbf{X}
.

Proposition 3.15
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{J}
is
\tau
-dense,
\tau\geq 1
, in the sense of Definition
3.11
.
Then
(\mathbf{X},d,\mu)
is Ahlfors 2-regular, with constants that depend only on
\lambda
and
\tau
.

Proof.

Let
p=(x,y)\in X
and
0<r<\operatorname{diam}\mathbf{X}
. Since
\|p-q\|\leq d(p,q)
for all points
q\in X
, we have
B(p,r)\subset\{q:\|q-p\|<r\}
. Hence, the upper bound
\mu(B(p,r))\leq\pi r^{2}
is always true. So, it remains to show

(3.3)

\mu(B(p,r))\gtrsim r^{2},

with a constant that does not depend on
r,p
.

We choose the (not so significant) cutoff of
2+\tau
. By applying the density condition with
r=1
, we see that (
3.3
) holds for all
2+\tau\leq r<\operatorname{diam}\mathbf{X}
(compare to case (2) below). Hence, we shall restrict to the case of
r\leq 2+\tau
. First, let us assume that
p\in X^{\prime}
. Let
Q=I\times J
be the cube that contains
p=(x,y)
. We obtain (
3.3
) by considering two cases.

Case (1): When
r/(2+\tau)\leq|J|
, we have a uniform quantitative bound, in terms of
\lambda
and
\tau
, on the measure of the portion of
B(p,r)
that lies inside
Q=I\times J
. So, in this case,
\mu(B(p,r))\gtrsim r^{2}
holds with a constant that depends only on
\lambda
and
\tau
.

Case (2): When
|J|<r/(2+\tau)
, we find a lower bound on
\mu(B(p,r))
as follows. Write
r^{\prime}:=r/(2+\tau)
, which is less than
1
. We fix
t\in{\swabfamily C}\cap I
and consider
p_{1}=(t,y)
. By the density of
\mathcal{J}
, we find
J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}

such that
|J^{\prime}|\geq r^{\prime}
and
|y-y_{0}|\leq\tau r^{\prime}
for some
y_{0}\in J^{\prime}
. Let
p_{2}:=(t,y_{0})
.

Because
d(p,p_{1})\leq|I|<r^{\prime}
and
d(p_{2},p_{1})\leq\tau r^{\prime}
, from the triangle inequality we get

B(p_{2},r^{\prime})\subset B(p_{1},(\tau+1)r^{\prime})\subset B(p,r).

Since
r^{\prime}\leq|J^{\prime}|
, case (1) applied to the point
p_{2}
and the radius
r^{\prime}
yields

\mu(B(p,r))\geq\mu(B(p_{2},r^{\prime}))\gtrsim r^{2}.

We have proved (
3.3
) for
p\in\mathbf{X}^{\prime}
. To prove (
3.3
) for
p\in\mathbf{X}\setminus\mathbf{X}^{\prime}
, we apply Lemma
3.6
to choose a sequence
p_{j}\in\mathbf{X}^{\prime}
such that
d(p_{j},p)\to 0
. For every
\varepsilon\in(0,r/2)
there exists
j
such that
B(p_{j},r-\varepsilon)\subset B(p,r)
. Applying the previous analysis to
p_{j}\in\mathbf{X}^{\prime}
gives

\mu(B(p,r))\geq\mu(B(p_{j},r-\varepsilon))\gtrsim(r-\varepsilon)^{2},

with the constants independent of
\varepsilon
. By letting
\varepsilon\to 0
, we obtain the desired estimate
\mu(B(p,r))\gtrsim r^{2}
. The proof is complete.
∎

3.4.
Comparison to the construction in
[
16
]

First of all one must rotate the construction in
[
16
]
by
90
degrees for the comparisons below to apply. Let us denote the space constructed in
[
16
]
by
\widetilde{X}
. Under our notation,
\widetilde{X}
corresponds to our
X
with the choice of
{\swabfamily C}={\swabfamily C}(3^{-m})
and
{\swabfamily D}={\swabfamily C}(3^{-n})
, where
1\leq m<n
are integers such that
\frac{n}{m}\in\{2,3,\ldots\}
. Notice that
{\swabfamily D}
is not scaled here.

The surviving intervals
I
for
{\swabfamily C}
have lengths of the form
3^{-im}
and the removed intervals
\mathcal{J}
for
{\swabfamily D}
have lengths of the form
3^{-i^{\prime}n}(3^{n}-2)
; see Remark
3.1
. Therefore, very importantly,

(3.4)

the ratios
\frac{|I|}{|J|}
are bounded away from
1

by a quantity that depends only on
n
and
m
.

For fixed
J
, the following set will be in
\widetilde{X}
, except possibly for
the few largest
J
,

(3.5)

\{(x,y):\inf J+\operatorname{dist}(x,{\swabfamily C})\leq y\leq\sup J-\operatorname{dist}(x,{\swabfamily C})\}.

Observe that depending on
|J|
this consists of multiple connected components that are congruent to one another. They will be bounded by vertical rigid shifts of the graphs of
x\mapsto\pm\operatorname{dist}(x,{\swabfamily C})
(Figure
3.2
).

Figure 3.2.
The space in
[
16
]
is, practically, the union of the interiors of regions bounded between vertical shifts of the graphs of
x\to\pm\operatorname{dist}(x,{\swabfamily C})
.

The projection of each one of such pieces onto the
x
-axis is equal to

\Bigl[\inf I-\frac{|J|}{2},\sup I+\frac{|J|}{2}\Bigr],

where
I
is a surviving interval with a maximal length
|I|
subject to
|I|\leq|J|
.

Due to (
3.4
), each of these pieces is bi-Lipschitz equivalent, with constant depending only on
n
and
m
, to the rectangle
Q=I\times J
. (There is a quantitative lower bound on how close the top and bottom “teeth” can get to each other, after normalizing for scale.)

As in our case, one needs a large piece to ensure the connectivity of the space. We do so in this paper by scaling
{\swabfamily D}
such that at least one
J
has
|J|\geq 1
. Instead, in
[
16
]
they “manually” attach one large piece to one end of the union of the pieces above. Finally, like we do, they add
{\swabfamily C}\times{\swabfamily D}
to complete the space.

In
[
16
]
they prove that
\widetilde{X}
, equipped with the standard Euclidean distance and measure, is quasiconvex (
[
16
, Proposition 4.10]
) and Ahlfors
2
-regular (
[
16
, Proposition 4.11]
). They use
\widetilde{X}
to prove the necessity of a
p
-Poincaré inequality in the second claim of Theorem
1.2
. They conjecture that
\widetilde{X}
might support some weaker Poincaré inequality but still better than a
2
-Poincaré inequality.

Although the construction in
[
16
]
is the inspiration for our
\mathbf{X}
and very similar in nature, our construction is much less technical, which is why we are able to prove sharp Poincaré inequalities.

We just mentioned that the individual building blocks of
\widetilde{X}
are uniformly bi-Lipschitz to the building blocks (the cubes) in our construction of
\mathbf{X}
. The relative positioning of these blocks in the two spaces are also very similar (but one has to consider only “half of” our
\mathbf{X}
for this). Thus, by some more analysis one can show that the spaces are (globally) bi-Lipschitz. We do not carry out the details for the same reason that calculations become unruly.

Therefore, by proving a
p
-Poincaré inequality for
\mathbf{X}
, we indirectly confirm that the space
\widetilde{X}
in
[
16
]
satisfies a
p
-Poincaré inequality for the same range of

; thus confirming their conjecture.

\displaystyle\lesssim\,\mathchoice{\hbox{\hskip 2.08334pt\hskip-2.08334pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\displaystyle\phantom{\int}$}$}\hskip-2.08334pt\hskip-2.77774pt\raisebox{-2.15277pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\displaystyle-$}$}}\hskip-2.77774pt\hskip 2.08334pt}\kern-7.49994pt}{\hbox{\hskip 2.08334pt\hskip-2.08334pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\textstyle\phantom{\int}$}$}\hskip-2.08334pt\hskip-2.77774pt\raisebox{-2.15277pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\textstyle-$}$}}\hskip-2.77774pt\hskip 2.08334pt}\kern-7.49994pt}{\hbox{\hskip 1.72743pt\hskip-1.72743pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\scriptstyle\phantom{\int}$}$}\hskip-1.72743pt\hskip-2.45831pt\raisebox{-1.50694pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}$}}\hskip-2.45831pt\hskip 1.72743pt}\kern-6.78813pt}{\hbox{\hskip 1.57988pt\hskip-1.57988pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\phantom{\int}$}$}\hskip-1.57988pt\hskip-2.29166pt\raisebox{-1.07639pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}$}}\hskip-2.29166pt\hskip 1.57988pt}\kern-6.49301pt}\int_{\Gamma_{x,y}}\int_{\gamma}g\,dsd\gamma

\displaystyle\lesssim d(x,y)\Bigl(\mathcal{M}_{Cd(x,y)}g^{p}(x))^{1/p}+\mathcal{M}_{Cd(x,y)}g^{p}(y))^{1/p}\Bigr).

Since,
C
and the comparison constants are independent of
x
and
y
, we have confirmed (
6.12
). We have proved that if
p>{\swabfamily p}_{0}
, then
\mathbf{X}
supports a
p
-Poincaré inequality.

6.4.
Necessity of
p>{\swabfamily p}_{0}

Proposition 6.10
.

The space
\mathbf{X}
corresponding to the choice of
{\swabfamily C}={\swabfamily C}(\lambda)
and
{\swabfamily D}=\frac{1}{1-2\lambda^{\nu}}{\swabfamily C}(\lambda^{\nu}),
where
\lambda\in(0,1/2)
and
\nu\in\{2,3,\ldots\}
, does not support a
q
-Poincaré inequality for any
q\in[1,{\swabfamily p}_{0}]
.

Proof.

First, suppose that
q<{\swabfamily p}_{0}
. Denote by
J_{0}
the largest interval in
\mathcal{J}
, which will be of unit length with
\inf J_{0}>0
. Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
and set

\mathcal{J}_{k}=\left\{J\in\mathcal{J}:|J|=\lambda^{k\nu},J\subset\Bigl[{\inf J_{0}}/{2},\inf J_{0}\right]\Bigr\}.

Let

N_{k}:=\operatorname{Card}\mathcal{J}_{k}
. From the construction,
N_{k}\approx 2^{k}
for large
k
. Let
J^{1},\ldots,J^{N_{k}}
be the intervals in
\mathcal{J}_{k}
ordered such that
\sup J^{i}<\inf J^{i+1}
for
i=1,2,\ldots,(N_{k}-1)
. There exists a monotone function
\psi_{k}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

•

\psi_{k}
is piecewise-linear on
\bigcup_{i=1}^{N_{k}}J^{i}
,

•

\psi_{k}^{\prime}(y)=\frac{1}{N_{k}|J^{i}|}=\frac{1}{{N_{k}}\lambda^{k\nu}}
for every
i=1,2,\ldots,(N_{k}-1)
, every
y\in J^{i}
,

•

\psi_{k}^{\prime}\equiv 0
on the complement of
\bigcup_{i=1}^{N_{k}}J^{i}
,

•

\psi_{k}(y)\equiv 0
for all
y\leq\inf J^{1}
,

•

\psi_{k}(y)\equiv 1
for all
y\geq\sup J^{N_{k}}
.

We define
u_{k}\colon\mathbf{X}\to\mathbb{R}
by
u_{k}(x,y)=\psi_{k}(y)
. We have,

\mu(\left\{x\in\mathbf{X}:u_{k}(x)=1\right\})\approx\mu(\left\{x\in\mathbf{X}:u_{k}(x)=0\right\})\approx\mu(\mathbf{X}).

We fix a ball
B
such that
B=\mathbf{X}
and
\operatorname{diam}B\approx\operatorname{diam}\mathbf{X}
. Clearly,

\displaystyle\int_{B}|u_{k}-({u_{k}})_{B}|d\mu\gtrsim\min\Bigl\{\int_{\{u_{k}=1\}}|u_{k}-{(u_{k})}_{\mathbf{X}}|d\mu,\int_{\{u_{k}=0\}}|u_{k}-{(u_{k})}_{\mathbf{X}}|d\mu\Bigr\}.

For every
k
,
(u_{k})_{B}
is either less that
1/2
or more than it. Thus, from the estimates above,

(6.14)

\,\mathchoice{\hbox{\hskip 2.08334pt\hskip-2.08334pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\displaystyle\phantom{\int}$}$}\hskip-2.08334pt\hskip-2.77774pt\raisebox{-2.15277pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\displaystyle-$}$}}\hskip-2.77774pt\hskip 2.08334pt}\kern-7.49994pt}{\hbox{\hskip 2.08334pt\hskip-2.08334pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\textstyle\phantom{\int}$}$}\hskip-2.08334pt\hskip-2.77774pt\raisebox{-2.15277pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\textstyle-$}$}}\hskip-2.77774pt\hskip 2.08334pt}\kern-7.49994pt}{\hbox{\hskip 1.72743pt\hskip-1.72743pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\scriptstyle\phantom{\int}$}$}\hskip-1.72743pt\hskip-2.45831pt\raisebox{-1.50694pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}$}}\hskip-2.45831pt\hskip 1.72743pt}\kern-6.78813pt}{\hbox{\hskip 1.57988pt\hskip-1.57988pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\phantom{\int}$}$}\hskip-1.57988pt\hskip-2.29166pt\raisebox{-1.07639pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\rule{0.0pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}$}}\hskip-2.29166pt\hskip 1.57988pt}\kern-6.49301pt}\int_{B}|u_{k}-({u_{k}})_{B}|d\mu\gtrsim 1,

where the constant is independent of
k
.

On the other hand, one directly checks that for every
k
,

g_{k}(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\frac{1}{N_{k}\lambda^{k\nu}}\chi_{J^{i}}(y)

is an upper-gradient of
u_{k}
.
Recall that on row
J^{i}
, there are exactly
2^{k\nu}
many cubes of side length
\lambda^{k\nu}
. From these considerations and
N_{k}\approx 2^{k}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{X}}g^{q}d\mu=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{N_{k}\lambda^{k\nu}}\Bigr)^{q}\Bigl(2^{k\nu}(\lambda^{k\nu})^{2}\Bigr)

(6.15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N_{k}^{1-q}2^{k\nu}\lambda^{-k\nu q+2k\nu}\lesssim(2^{k})^{(1+\nu+2\nu\log_{2}\lambda)-q(1+\nu\log_{2}\lambda)}.

Condition
q<{\swabfamily p}_{0}
is exactly the sufficient condition for the last expression to converge to zero when
k\to\infty
. Thus, for
q<{\swabfamily p}_{0}
, the combination of inequalities (
6.15
) and (
6.14
) rule out the possibility of a
q
-Poincaré inequality on
\mathbf{X}
. So, the proposition is proved in the case of
q<{\swabfamily p}_{0}
.

From Keith and Zhong’s celebrated result
[
14
]
, if a complete metric space, equipped with a doubling measure, supports a
p
-Poincaré inequality with
1<p<\infty
, then there exists
\varepsilon>0
so that the space supports a
(p-\varepsilon)
-Poincaré inequality. Thus, from the previous case, we deduce that
\mathbf{X}
does not support a
{\swabfamily p}_{0}
-Poincaré inequality either. Proof of Proposition
6.10
is complete.
∎

7.
A Quasiconformal Map on
\mathbf{X}

As before, the space
\mathbf{X}
is built with respect to the Cantor sets
{\swabfamily C}={\swabfamily C}(\lambda)
and
{\swabfamily D}=\frac{1}{1-2\lambda^{\nu}}{\swabfamily C}(\lambda^{\nu})
.

Let
\phi
denote the Cantor-Vitali function associated to
{\swabfamily D}
. It is well-known that
\phi
is not absolutely continuous; indeed, its restriction to any surviving interval at any step of the the construction of
{\swabfamily D}
is not absolutely continuous. Define
f\colon\mathbf{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
by

(7.1)

f(x,y):=(x+\phi(y),y).

Notice that
f
is an isometry on each cube
I\times J
.

Set
Y:=f(\mathbf{X})
and
E:={\swabfamily C}\times{\swabfamily D}
. We have
\dim E={\dim{\swabfamily C}+\dim{\swabfamily D}}
and
0<{\mathcal{H}}^{\dim E}(E)<\infty
(see
[
21
]
). Moreover, the map
f
is a homeomorphism, and
Y
is compact and Ahlfors
2
-regular off
f(E)
.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
q>{\swabfamily p}_{0}
. Then there exists a family
\Gamma
of rectifiable paths in
\mathbf{X}
such that
\Gamma
has positive
q
-modulus and that the map
f
fails to be absolutely continuous on every path in
\Gamma
. In particular,
f\notin N^{1,q}(\mathbf{X};Y)
.

Proof.

Fix
\Gamma=\Gamma(Q_{1},Q_{2})
, the path family associated with some pair of cubes in
\mathbf{X}
where
Q_{2}
is the first large cube below/above
Q_{1}
. By Corollary
5.9
,
\Gamma(Q_{1},Q_{2})
has positive
q
-modulus. Since
\phi(y)
is the Cantor function, we can show that
f
is not absolutely continuous on any
\eta_{t}\in\Gamma(Q_{1},Q_{2})
. This is basically because absolute continuity of
\phi
fails exactly along
{\swabfamily D}
and each
\eta_{t}
crosses the cubes along
{\swabfamily D}
, therefore, (the first component of)
f\circ\eta_{t}
fails to be absolutely continuous (compare to the proof of
[
16
, Lemma 5.1]
).
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Let
p\in(1,2)
and
\varepsilon>0
be given. It is enough to prove the claim for small
\varepsilon
. We choose
\lambda\in(0,1/2)
and
\nu\in\{2,3,\ldots\}
such that

(7.2)

2-\dim{\swabfamily C}<{\swabfamily p}_{0}<2-\dim{\swabfamily C}+\varepsilon.

and

\dim{\swabfamily C}+\dim{\swabfamily D}=2-p.

Such choices are possible due to the asymptotic behavior of
{\swabfamily p}_{0}
(Remark
6.2
).

With choices made above for
{\swabfamily C}
,
D
, and
E
, we have
\dim E=2-p
and
{\swabfamily p}_{0}<p+\varepsilon
.

Let
f\colon\mathbf{X}\to Y
be the homeomorphism defined in (
7.1
). Then
H_{f}(x)=1
at every
x\notin E
, hence, a.e. on
\mathbf{X}
. The topological and Ahlfors regularity assumptions hold on
\mathbf{X}
and
Y
.

Moreover,
\mathbf{X}
supports a

(p+\varepsilon)
-Poincaré inequality by Theorem
6.1
, and the exceptional set
E
satisfies
0<{\mathcal{H}}^{2-p}(E)<\infty
. However, by Proposition
7.1
,
f
fails to be absolutely continuous on
q
-a.e. path, and hence
f\notin N^{1,q}(\mathbf{X};Y)
, for any
q\geq p+\varepsilon
. Proof of Theorem
1.3
is complete.
∎
