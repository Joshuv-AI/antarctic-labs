The public health impacts of drug-induced homicide laws: a community-engaged scoping review
Authors: Jennifer J Carroll, Dmitri Fisher, Bayla Ostrach
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDrug-induced homicide (DIH) laws are state laws that confer criminal liability equivalent to homicide for the sale or delivery of drugs subsequently associated with a fatal overdose.MethodsWe undertook a scoping review of literature on the public health impacts of DIH laws and held focus groups to seek guidance on the interpretation of review results from three directly-impacted community groups: public health and harm reduction professionals; people who use drugs (PWUD); and family members of adult victims of fatal overdose.ResultsEight publications met our inclusion criteria. Three of those publications presented the results of a systematic analysis of DIH laws and/or their effects; the remaining five publication presented exploratory results related to these topics. Six of the eight publications suggest that DIH laws actively deter people from calling 911 to report an overdose. Two publications suggest that DIH laws have small but disruptive impacts on drug market participation. One publication suggests that friends and family of overdose victims constitute about half of all people charged under DIH laws and the conclusion that DIH charges are overwhelmingly brought in response to the death of a white person. Public health and harm reduction professionals and PWUD reported, generally, that these conclusions align with their lived experience and prior understandings of DIH laws. Family members rejected the evidence that DIH laws deter calling 911, asserting that such a relationship makes no sense, often relying on stereotyped understandings of substance use to support that claim. Public health and harm reduction professionals advocated for additional research documenting the negative impacts of DIH laws, with the goal of informing more effective drug policy. Family members advocated for more research on friends and loved ones affected by overdose.ConclusionsCurrent evidence on the impacts of DIH laws remains limited but consistently indicates negative consequences for public health. Additional research is needed, especially research that addresses the expressed needs and priorities of directly impacted community groups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40352-026-00419-y.Keywords: Drug induced homicide, Criminalization, Overdose, Substance use, Scoping review

Introduction

Overdose deaths in the United States exceeded 100,000 per year in 2022 and 2023 and are projected to have exceeded 80,000 in 2024 (Ahmad et al., 2026). Increasing overdose deaths prompted a sharper policy focus on substance use as a public health problem (El-Sabawi, 2019b), exemplified in multiple legislative actions to expand access to substance use disorders treatment and services (Davis, 2019) and significant federal investment in public health infrastructure to understand overdose trends and reduce the number of these preventable deaths (U.S. Centers for Disease Control & Prevention, 2024). At the same time, many law enforcement and criminal legal professionals continue to advocate for punitive responses to drug use, contradicting current evidence (National Institute of Justice, 2016) by framing criminalization and coercion as deterrents to substance use behaviors (El-Sabawi, 2019a).

Many punitive responses to substance use, including the criminalization of drug possession, distribution, and manufacture, and coerced treatment through drug court and court diversion programs, have been mainstays of American drug policy for decades (Hoffman, 2000; Nadelmann, 1989). Drug-induced homicide (DIH) laws, however, have become a defining characteristic of drug criminalization in the contemporary overdose landscape, with the number of resulting criminal prosecutions increasing by 1000% between 2010 and 2020 (Health in Justice, 2026). DIH laws, broadly defined, create criminal liability equivalent to homicide for the sale or delivery of drugs subsequently implicated in a fatal overdose (Temple Univeristy Center for Public Health Law Research & The Action Lab, 2025).

Despite many efforts to coordinate public health and punitive responses to substance use (e.g. Wolff et al., 2022), a growing body of evidence suggests that these two approaches produce fundamentally discordant results. Public health interventions to reduce overdose, such as enhanced access to naloxone and expanded healthcare coverage through the U.S. Medicaid service, are all evidence-based interventions demonstrated to reduce overdose mortality (Abouk et al., 2019; Andersson et al., 2021; Carroll et al., 2018; Kravitz-Wirtz et al., 2020; McClellan et al., 2018). However, criminal drug law enforcement, including the seizure of illicit drug products, the arrest of drug suppliers, and incarceration for any reason, have been repeatedly associated with increased overdose mortality in the geographic areas immediately affected by these law enforcement actions (Carroll et al., 2020; Kral et al., 2025; Lowder et al., 2022; Mital et al., 2020; Ranapurwala et al., 2022; Ray et al., 2023).

Current evidence for the public health impacts of DIH laws is less robust, with several challenges to the systematic assessment of those effects. First among these, arguably, is the fact that DIH laws have come into meaningful use only in the last 10–15 years, despite being enacted in many states as early as the 1980 s (Beletsky, 2019). Thus, the opportunity to rigorously evaluate the impacts of these laws has emerged relatively recently. Other research challenges likely include the potentially complex relationships between DIH laws and various discrete health outcomes such as overdose (e.g. Carroll et al., 2021); the near impossibility of measuring changes in clandestine activity that DIH laws may effect such as drug use and drug distribution (Manning, 1981; Moore et al., 2022); and widely-held yet unsubstantiated beliefs about drug markets and drug market actors that serve to foreclose certain questions in public discourse about what DIH laws accomplish (El-Sabawi et al., 2023). Whatever the cause, current understanding of the public health impacts of DIH laws is poor.

Further, the intended effects of DIH laws, as described by their proponents, are often unclear or inconsistent. Generally speaking, five major theories have been developed that justify the use of criminal punishment, in general; those theories, each in their own turn, find utility in deterrence (i.e. dissuading others from preventing the same crime), incapacitation (i.e. removing dangerous individuals from society), rehabilitation (i.e. reducing the risk that an individual will re-offend through supportive or therapeutic services), reparation (i.e. taking action to correct the harm incurred by the crime), and punishment for its own sake (see, for example, United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime, 2019). In this vein, proponents seem to disagree about which of these effects DIH laws are poised to achieve. Some prosecutors have voiced strong agreement with claims that DIH laws deter substance use, deter drug distribution, and reduce overdose in their communities (Morrissey et al., 2024), while others cite fairness, justice, and closure for families as the desirable outcomes that DIH prosecutions are able to produce, with little attention to what population-wide impacts DIH laws may have, if any (Rothberg & Stith, 2018).

To address these gaps, we conducted a community-engaged scoping review of scientific and scholarly literature on DIH laws. A scoping review of existing literature seeks to examine the full breadth and range of existing evidence on a general topic, as opposed to a systematic review, which would typically restrict its scope to a narrowly defined research question and/or a narrowly defined outcome (Arksey & O’Malley, 2005). This review is community-engaged in that we sought direct consultation on the evaluation of our scoping review findings (Levac et al., 2010) from four affected community groups—(1) public health and harm reduction professionals, (2) people who use drugs (PWUD), (3) people who have lost an adult family member to fatal overdose, and (4) law enforcement and criminal legal professionals—and succeeded in obtaining that consultation from the first three. Similar to previous studies (Flam-Ross et al., 2022), we sought feedback from these “expert” groups in order to assist in the interpretation of review findings, discuss the impact and utility of these findings for their communities, and identify priority directions for future research.

Community engagement site

We sought community feedback on our scoping literature review in Haywood County, North Carolina, a county located in the western, Appalachian region of the state with disproportionately high rates of overdose and overdose death compared to the rest of the state (NC Department of Health & Human Services, 2025). Haywood County is a location where two members of the study team (BO and JC) have previously led ethnographic studies related to substance use and public health services for (PWUD). It is also the jurisdiction in which a criminal case against a local man prompted the development of North Carolina’s own DIH law (NC § 14–18.4), which created two new crimes in North Carolina state law: “death by distribution” and “aggravated death by distribution.” These crimes are classified as a Class C, B2, or B1 felony, which, according to North Carolina state sentencing guidelines (NC § 15A-1340.17), allow for maximum sentences of approximately 15 years, 33 years, and life without parole, depending on aggravating circumstances.

Haywood County’s 2018 second degree murder case, which resulted in a sentence of 25–31 years for the individual convicted, was covered extensively in local news outlets (Perrotti, 2018a, 2018b) and was widely followed by local residents. The case gained an even higher profile when state representative Dean Arp (then serving District 69, located about 160 miles east of Haywood County) collaborated with Ashley Welch, the District Attorney for the seven western-most North Carolina counties, including Haywood, to draft the bill proposing a statewide DIH law; that law was subsequently enacted in 2019 (Stone, 2019). Moreover, existing research suggests that PWUD in Haywood County are disproportionately subject to harassment and coercion by local police compared to PWUD in other North Carolina counties (Morrissey et al., 2022). Thus, if any local community has had the opportunity to learn about DIH laws and consider their impacts in a way that could inform this scoping review, the residents of Haywood County have.

Methods

We conducted a scoping review of the scholarly literature on the public health impacts of DIH laws, charges, and/or prosecutions. We then summarized those findings for presentation and discussion in separate focus groups composed of PWUD, public health and harm reduction professionals, and adults who have lost a family member to fatal drug overdose. This entire process followed the 6-step recommendations of Levac et al. (2010), which were produced as an extension of Arksey and O’Malley’s (2005) foundational approach for conducting scoping reviews.

Specifically, we first identified a primary research question: namely, what empirical evidence do we have about the real-world impact of DIH laws in the United States? Second, we identified relevant articles through a broad search of scholarly literature. Third, we systematically selected articles for inclusion in our scoping review. Fourth, we charted the information available in each of the included studies. Fifth, we used multiple methods—including tabulation, text description, and visual description—to summarize our review findings and report those findings to our community groups. Sixth, we consulted with these community groups to gain insight into their understanding and interpretation of the review findings, discuss the potential impacts of existing evidence on policy and practice, and identify important gaps in existing research (Levac et al., 2010). We describe each of these study elements in turn below.

Identifying relevant literature

As the public health impacts of DIH laws remain an emerging topic in peer reviewed literature, a scoping review that allows for the description of available evidence on a single topic was more appropriate for our purposes than a more traditional systematic review, which often requires the selection of specific analytical approaches or specific exposure and outcome measurements for inclusion (Munn et al., 2018). The goals of this scoping review were to identify and describe any empirical evidence that describes the impacts of DIH laws on individual actors (including case reports of or trends in charges under these laws, actual charges brought, convictions secured, and sentencings under these laws), on measured or reported human behavior (i.e. substance use-related activities, substance distribution-related activities, uptake or avoidance of available health and emergency services, etc.), or on measurable health outcomes (i.e. overdose, criminal justice involvement, etc.). We purposefully selected broad definitions for these concepts to maximize the scope of empirical evidence that could be given serious consideration for inclusion in this review to maximize the breadth and comprehensiveness of the findings included. For similar reasons, we focused our review on peer-reviewed sources but also included other forms of scholarly output that are not subject to peer review in the strictest sense in the collection of sources reviewed, including law review articles and theses.

Our initial search strategy was developed in consultation with research staff at the North Carolina State University library. Aiming for the broadest and most comprehensive initial search, we performed iterative literature searches using the following search string, which consists of the three different terms commonly used to define the crime that we refer to here as DIH:

("drug-induced homicide") OR ("death by distribution") OR ("delivery resulting in death")

This query was run on March 31, 2023 in the following databases: PubMed, an index of biomedical literature maintained by the National Institutes of Health; the Public Affairs Information Service (PAIS) Index, a database of publications on public policy maintained by ProQuest LLC; ProQuest Dissertation & Theses database; Nexus Uni, a database of news, business, and legal sources maintained by LexisNexis; and Google Scholar, a database of scholarly works maintained by Google.

The details of our review and exclusion process are illustrated in Fig. 1. Our initial searches of these five databases returned a total of 393 records. Of those, 123 were removed as duplicates or as records that were so incomplete that the indicated work could not be identified. Twenty-eight other records were also excluded at this stage due to several broad a priori criteria: commentaries with no novel empirical data (n = 16); data not collected in the United States (n = 8); conference papers (n = 3); and works written in a language other than English (n = 1). The abstracts of the remaining 242 records were screened for relevance to our question of interest. At this stage, 175 records were excluded for topical irrelevance or a clear lack of novel empirical data (such as meta-analyses, modeling studies, or legal briefs based on data already in the literature). The remaining 67 records were then subject to a full-text review. At this stage, 58 studies were excluded that did not present novel empirical data directly relevant to our question of interest. This included descriptions of policies, law review articles that discussed the legal framework of DIH laws but not their real-world impacts, studies that discussed the implications of their data in the context of DIH and similar laws but did not collect data directly relevant to these laws or their effects, and studies that assessed the impacts of drug criminalization broadly, lifting up DIH laws as a salient example of criminalization without any methodological focus on these laws per se.

Selection and elimination process for this scoping review

This full text review returned a final sample of nine records. One of those records, a longitudinal study of the impacts of DIH laws on county-level overdose rates (Lee et al., 2022), was subsequently found to be fatally flawed (Carroll et al., 2023a) and was voluntarily retracted by the authors (“Retraction Notice,” 2023). We included the remaining eight records in this review.

Charting and summarizing review findings

Two authors (JC and BO) discussed the selected articles to determine what data, details, or findings should be extracted from each. Collectively, these researchers decided that the items to be systematically charted included: (1) whether the article was peer reviewed (yes/no); (2) the article type (Research Article, Policy Analysis, Law Review Article); (3) the study design used (Cross-sectional qualitative study, Cross-sectional survey study, Cross-sectional mixed-methods study, and News/media analysis); (4) a description of the study population recruited for the study (i.e. “PWUD in Baltimore, Maryland”); (5) the sample size; (6) the primary aim of the study (i.e. “to assess the health and service needs of PWUD”); (7) whether the findings related to DIH laws were systematic or exploratory (i.e. was the study designed to investigate some characteristic or impact of DIH laws, or did the study focus elsewhere and generate data about DIH laws along the way?); and (8) the methodological limitations of the study with respect to their contribution to knowledge about the public impacts of DIH laws. In addition to these seven common criteria, the authors also chose to (9) summarize any data or findings from each article that were relevant to the public health impacts of DIH laws in an open text field. We adopted this approach to maximize the detail of data captured while accommodating the diversity of extractable findings identified. Two members of the research team (JC and BO) discussed the relevant findings extracted from the eight articles included in the systematic review and observed that those findings could be roughly categorized into three broad hypotheses, to which each article somehow contributed: (1) that DIH laws may deter civilians from calling 911 during an overdose emergency; (2) that DIH laws may have some deterrent effect on drug distribution behaviors; and (3) that DIH charges are filed against individuals in racially patterned ways that have the potential to produce racially inequitable health and welfare outcomes. Using box and arrow diagrams, we visually mapped the relationships that were supported, whether systematically or anecdotally. We then followed one of two processes (below) for concisely summarizing these findings through visualizations that could be presented to and discussed with community groups.

For the first two hypotheses, supported by multiple articles, we combined those diagrammed relationships into a single logic model. Importantly, all studies included in this systematic review were cross-sectional. Therefore, the direction of any hypothesized causal relationships depicted in those logic models could not be directly determined. However, where directionality is inherently implied by the logic model design, we chose that directionality after lengthy discussion in which all authors triangulated the qualitative data presented in these studies, the interpretations supported by the authors of these articles in their presentation or discussion of results, and the expertise of two of our study team members (JC and BO) who, collectively, have more than 4 decades of experience working and conducting research among PWUD, much of which was conducted in North Carolina, specifically. When all three of these elements aligned with a particular causal direction, that direction was considered a valid hypothesis emerging from the collective findings of this systematic review. For the third hypothesis, supported by only one study, we chose to visualize the findings of that paper (which compared reported race categories across two groups of people) with two pie charts, one for each group, side by side. The final visualizations presented in our focus groups are in Appendix 1. We also created a simplified version of Table 1 to present to focus group members, found in Appendix 2.

Sampling and recruitment for focus groups

Using a combination of key informant referrals and purposive sampling, we aimed to recruit participants from four populations in Haywood County, North Carolina: (1) public health and/or harm reduction professionals; (2) PWUD; (3) people who have lost an adult relative to fatal overdose and do not fall into either Group 1 or 2; and (4) law enforcement or criminal legal system professionals. As we describe below, we succeeded in holding focus groups with members from the first three of these populations. For each group, we engaged a combination of targeted recruitment strategies consisting of direct recruitment of existing professional contacts; referrals from project participants; and direct recruitment of relevant professionals with publicly available contact information.

For the recruitment of public health and harm reduction professionals (Group 1), two authors (BO and JC) leveraged pre-existing professional relationships to directly recruit current staff at a local syringe services program (SSP) as well as staff at a local resource center for homeless and other services where the SSP maintains an office. To recruit PWUD in Haywood County (Group 2) a team member (BO) asked SSP staff as well as staff from a local resource center providing services to PWUD to host and advertise a focus group among their participants and clients who identify as PWUD. To identify and recruit persons who had lost an adult family member to fatal overdose (Group 3), a team member (BO) contacted a community-based organization that runs a support group for residents of Haywood County who have lost an adult child to fatal overdose. Research team members approached local group facilitators and introduced them to this project. Those facilitators then agreed to invite members of the support group to a focus group discussion for this study.

Recruitment of law enforcement and criminal legal system professionals (Group 4) involved direct and indirect outreach (by JC, BO, and DF) to leadership at the county sheriff’s department, police departments in the county’s two largest municipalities, and the District Attorney’s office through publicly available contact information. We also directly contacted recently retired law enforcement professionals who had been serving jurisdictions in Haywood County. We began recruiting this group through referrals from local contacts who had worked directly with these persons in a professional capacity. One police chief replied to our inquiries to say that he and his officers were unable to participate. A second police chief offered to forward our request to investigators in his department; however, to our knowledge that handoff never occurred, and this chief did not respond to any follow-up communication. Repeated inquiries were made via phone and email to representatives from all other law enforcement and criminal legal agencies whose jurisdiction falls within the limits of Haywood County, including several to whom we were personally referred, over a period of nearly nine months. None offered any response to our communications.

Focus group data collection and analysis

Separate focus groups were conducted for each population recruited (Groups 1–3) between September and December 2023. Four (n = 4) public health and harm reduction professionals (Group 1) were consulted in two, non-overlapping focus groups to facilitate participation by professionals with conflicting availability. Seven (n = 7) PWUD (Group 2) were consulted in a single focus group, as were five (n = 5) people who had lost an adult family member to fatal overdose (Group 3).

Focus group leaders (BO and JC) developed a guide to facilitate focus group discussions (Appendix 3). The guide first probed participants’ familiarity with DIH laws, including North Carolina’s state law; their beliefs about the merits and impacts of DIH laws; and what evidence they believe exists to describe the implementation and impact of DIH laws, generally. At this point a member of the research team (JC) described the scoping literature review with focus group members. This description included a verbal overview of research methods as well as a verbal description of the empirical findings extracted from each study included in the review. We also presented focus group participants a simplified table detailing the studies included in the review and visualizations of review findings (Appendices 1 and 2). We encouraged focus group members to ask questions about the materials. Once a general discussion of the review findings had concluded, focus group leaders then returned to the focus group guide to probe participants’ reactions to the review findings; their thoughts on how the results were potentially important for or useful to their community; and what gaps in understanding or unanswered questions they would like researchers to pursue next.

All focus groups discussions lasted one to two hours in length and were audio-recorded and transcribed for analysis. Focus group participants received 50 USD for their participation. This protocol was administratively reviewed and determined to be exempt from further review by the Institutional Review Board at North Carolina State University.

Transcripts were deductively coded (by JC and BO) according to three overarching themes guiding this community-engaged literature review: (1) participants’ interpretation of and/or reactions to the review findings; (2) participants perceptions of the impact and/or importance of review findings; (3) and participant-identified priorities for future research. Initial findings from these deductive coding activities were discussed amongst all authors (JC, BO, and DF) and subsequently refined to ensure that findings were limited to participants’ commentary on the review findings (not rooted in more general discussions that constituted part of the focus group) and that disparate viewpoints among focus group members were adequately captured. The community-based findings presented below—organized according to each focus group’s interpretation of the evidence, assessment of the impact of that evidence, and recommendations for future research—emerged in this final stage of analysis.

Results

Literature included in the scoping review

Eight articles were included in this scoping review, all published between 2017 and 2022 (Beletsky, 2019; Carroll et al., 2021; Footer et al., 2022; Latimore & Bergstein, 2017; McLean, 2018; Ondocsin et al., 2020; Peterson et al., 2019; Rouhani et al., 2021). All presented the results of a cross-sectional analysis of a human subjects cohort in a peer-reviewed publication, with the exception of Beletsky (2019), a law review article (subject to significant editorial review but not peer-review in the traditional sense) that presents and analyzes novel data from a sample of DIH cases described in online news media. Beletsky’s (2019) analysis considers a nation-wide dataset, while the other seven articles collected data locally throughout the eastern seaboard of the United States: Haywood County, North Carolina (Carroll et al., 2021), Baltimore, Maryland (Footer et al., 2022; Latimore & Bergstein, 2017), McKeesport, Pennsylvania (McLean, 2018), southern West Virginia (Ondocsin et al., 2020), Rhode Island (Peterson et al., 2019), and Ann Arundel County, Maryland (Rouhani et al., uh 2021).

Findings from systematic studies of DIH laws

Three articles systematically investigated some aspect of DIH laws: Beletsky (2019), Peterson et al., (2019), and Rouhani et al., (2021). Beletsky (2019) analyzed data about unique DIH prosecutions extracted from online news media publications between 2000 and 2017. Each news media publication identified through a keyword search was manually reviewed to reduce Type I error and coded each entry according to the following variables, as available: the state in which charges were brought; the described relationship of the person charged to the overdose victim; the type of drug(s) implicated in the overdose; race data about the person charged and the overdose victim; and any resolution to the case that may have been specified in the news piece. Descriptive statistics provided by Beletsky (2019) show that, in their dataset, 50% of all persons charged with DIH were friends or romantic partners of the overdose victim, while 47% of cases involve what Beletsky describes as “drug distribution by a ‘traditional’ dealer” (2019, p. 874). Further, of those cases defined by a “traditional dealer” relationship, exactly half (n = 43) were characterized by a white defendant charged with DIH in connection to the death of another white person; the other half (n = 43) were characterized by a Black or Hispanic person charged with DIH in connection to the death of a white person. In other words, DIH prosecutions identified by this study that were only brought against “traditional” drug distributors following the death of a white person (Beletsky, 2019).

Peterson et al., (2019) presented an analysis of 40 qualitative interviews conducted with 40 people incarcerated in Rhode Island’s unified jail and prison system and who were receiving medication for opioid use disorder while incarcerated. The purpose of this study was to explore what insights this population could offer on how DIH laws might affect fentanyl distribution. Respondents generally described DIH laws as ineffective in reducing overdose and deterring drug distribution; many respondents listed demand for fentanyl and barriers to employment opportunities in the formal economy as drivers of drug distribution over which DIH laws had no effect. Some interviewees also described DIH laws as a clear deterrent to calling 911 to report an overdose emergency due to the perceived risk of arrest and jail time after alerting authorities to the overdose event (Peterson et al., 2019).

Rouhani et al., (2021) addressed a similar question through a cross-sectional survey administered to 173 PWUD in Ann Arundel County, Maryland, providing the only quantitative analysis based on human subjects data included in this scoping review. The survey measured perceived risk of arrest at the scene of an overdose by asking participants to indicate whether it was true or false that someone could be arrested simply for being at the scene of an overdose, that someone could be arrested because they experienced an overdose, and that they were personally concerned they could be charged with manslaughter or murder if they supplied drugs involved in an overdose. The survey also measured knowledge of DIH laws, finding that perceived risk of arrest at the scene of an overdose was positively associated with non-white race (aOR 2.2, 95% CI 1.8–2.6) and knowledge of someone who had been previously charged with DIH (aOR 2.6, 95% CI 1.7–4.0) (Rouhani et al., 2021).

Exploratory findings about DIH laws

The remaining five articles included in the scoping review offered exploratory findings related to the public health impacts of DIH laws based on qualitative research with PWUD and/or clients of SSPs (Carroll et al., 2021; Footer et al., 2022; Latimore & Bergstein, 2017; McLean, 2018; Ondocsin et al., 2020). Latimore and Bergstein (2017) conducted qualitative interviews with 22 participants at an SSP in Baltimore, Maryland about factors that shape their decision to call 911 when witnessing an overdose. Some, but not all, participants specifically mentioned perceived risk of being charged with homicide or involuntary manslaughter (to which DIH is often statutorily defined as equivalent in severity and/or punishment) as a deterrent to calling 911 during an overdose event. Similarly, Footer et al. (2022), conducted 20 interviews with PWUD in Baltimore, Maryland to explore their perspectives on and experiences with law enforcement professionals. Many participants voiced concern that they may be arrested if they call 911 to report an overdose. One of the 20 participants described a general reluctance among PWUD to participate in selling drugs to support their drug purchases out of concern that they would face homicide charges.

McLean (2018) conducted 50 surveys and 18 semi-structured interviews among participants at a substance use services organization in McKeesport, Pennsylvania to explore the broad impacts of punitive drug policies on overdose response. Nearly all of McLean’s interviewees expressed a perceived risk of arrest when calling 911 to report an overdose, and three participants directly quoted in the manuscript voiced concern that they would be at risk of homicide or manslaughter charges if 911 was called. In another exploratory study, Ondocsin et al. (2020) conducted 21 qualitative interviews with people who inject opioids in southern West Virginia to explore the risk environment characterizing that area following the closure of the region’s only SSP. When describing the risks they experienced, some participants stated that calling 911 to someone’s home to respond to an overdose would result in manslaughter charges for the homeowner where the overdose occurred.

Finally, Carroll et al. (2021) presented limited empirical data in a policy analysis on DIH laws. Carroll et al. interviewed three participants of a local SSP in Haywood County, North Carolina—identified as part of a larger, state-wide needs assessment for harm reduction services—who described wide-spread changes in the local opioid supply following the 2018 conviction and sentencing of a local man for second degree murder for supplying drugs later implicated in a fatal overdose. These interviewees observed that the potency of illicit opioid products declined in the months following the conviction and then renormalized several months later. Interviewees attributed this fluctuation to fear of similar criminal charges among local drug sellers that peaked and then waned following the 2018 case. In both instances, increased unpredictability of local drug products was cited as a potential source of overdose risk. The study also summarized observations from local harm reduction and overdose education professionals, who reported hearing wide-spread reluctance to call 911 among local PWUD, especially among those familiar with the 2018 murder case.

Synopsis of hypotheses supported by data extracted in the scoping review

The systematic and exploratory findings extracted from the eight studies described above offered evidence in support of three distinct hypotheses. The first hypothesis, that DIH laws deter PWUD from calling 911, is supported by Carroll et al. (2021), Rouhani et al (2021), Ondocsin et al. (2020), Latimore and Bergstein (2017), Peterson et al. (2019) and McLean (2018). Collectively, these studies suggest that both general and specific knowledge of DIH laws are causally connected to reduced willingness to call 911, often due to perceived risk of arrest.

The second hypothesis, that DIH laws may impact the behaviors of drug suppliers in sub-optimal ways or in ways other than intended, is supported by evidence in Footer et al. (2022) and Carroll et al. (2021). Finally, the third hypothesis, concerning patterns in the implementation of DIH laws in response to fatal overdose events, is supported by Beletsky’s (2019) finding that as many as half of all DIH charges are brought against friends and family members of overdose victims and, in the case of traditional dealer/buyer relationships, charges are brought almost exclusively in response to the overdose death of a white person.

We summarized and visually depicted these three hypotheses (as described above) for community members consulted for this review (see Appendices 1 and 2) to facilitate the presentation of findings and subsequent focus group discussions.

Community-engaged review: Public health and harm reduction professionals

When invited to comment on their interpretation of these scoping review findings, discussion with this group centered on how well the evidence presented aligned with their real-world observations and experiences working with PWUD. One described the results as “vindicating.” She noted, “I think, when I read stuff like this, I’m like, oh, yea, the things I’ve felt innately for a long time are true and are empirically shown. And that’s what I really love about this [information].”

Public health and harm reduction professionals found data suggesting that DIH laws deter PWUD from calling 911 unsurprising. One noted, when offering their initial reaction to the review findings, “Well, I think the number one thing is: we have talked about that [911 deterrence].” Data on the race of and defendant relationships with overdose decedents in DIH cases also aligned with this group’s previously held suspicions:

I wasn’t surprised to see that…half of the people who were prosecuted were…user-dealers, people who were sharing with their friends in order to afford their supply, and I wasn’t entirely expecting, but also wasn’t surprised to hear, the other half of the prosecutions are...racially motivated.

One suggested that the frequency with which friends and family of overdose victims are charged with DIH suggests that “the charges are not being used appropriately, not in any way that would help them [criminal legal system professionals] meet their stated goals.”

Public health and harm reduction professionals also offered explanations that expanded on the hypothesis that DIH laws may have a deterrent effect that is suboptimal or different than intended. In response to Carroll et al. (2021) finding that a DIH case may temporarily reduce the potency of local drug products—and thus increase the cost of obtaining a desired dose—noted:

When…people are using drugs, they will go to great lengths to get drugs. And so how forcing people to spend so much more money on these seriously stepped on [cut or diluted] drugs is causing any sort of positive change, I don’t understand.

The discussion also revealed general agreement among this group with the assertion that demand for illicit drugs will not be affected by DIH laws (“There’s this belief that if we make people who use drugs’ lives harder, they will stop wanting to use drugs, That…is just not true.”) and that a likely secondary effect of DIH prosecutions not observed in the studies is a higher incidence of drug-related petty crime, driven by the financial desperation of PWUD (“Making drugs less obtainable and more expensive is only gonna increase the crime rate.”).

When prompted to discuss the potential importance or wider impact of this data, this group found the scoping review data directly applicable to their professional work among PWUD, especially their efforts to educate PWUD on how to respond to an overdose event. One harm reduction professional had already been offering tailored advice based on the information previously available (“The anecdotes I hear are that cops are rushing to the scene [of an overdose], hence that section of my overdose curriculum about talking to first responders [when] cops are rushing to the scene.”). Some suggested directly educating people who use drugs with “practical guidelines” to reduce the risk of criminal prosecution, including “how to handle that call [to 911] if you need to make it.” One observed that the content of that advice should include very explicit instruction to remain silent, such as “you just go sit in there and you keep your mouth shut. You didn’t do nothing.”

Importantly, some members of this group struggled over how to advise local PWUD when DIH laws create a clear conflict of interest between overdose victims and bystanders, finding little clarity or guidance in the evidence presented to them. One observed, when reflecting on what advice to give those who risk arrest when calling 911, “I would advise anyone, especially if they were sharing [drugs], to clear the scene [of evidence] or clear the scene of themselves.” After a pause they continued, “Probably, but I don’t know if that’s the best [overdose response] advice to give.” The rest of this group acknowledged this conflict, except for one, who suggested that the priority should be training PWUD to “handle an overdose yourself” without calling 911 at all.

Nevertheless, this group felt that the findings of the scoping review could be very impactful if shared with the wider community, offering hope that it might inspire empathy for PWUD or change minds about the value of the DIH law. For example, one public health professional said, “I never voted until I started working in harm reduction.” Another group member who works primarily with unhoused PWUD noted that “the [general] community is clueless about the homeless population…we had [a direct services program for the unhoused] shut down a few years ago because of ignorant community, I think.” At the same time, public health and harm reduction professionals felt that the review findings had little to offer PWUD. One reasoned, “People would have access to [this] information, [but] I don’t know what they’re going to do with it.” Another elaborated on this thought, observing “The situation still the same. There is a risk if you call 911 of being charged with this crime.”

This group suggested several priority avenues for future research. First, several endorsed efforts to quantify the effect of DIH prosecutions on overdose outcomes. One noted:

The first couple of steps in the equation are mapped out there [see Appendix 1], that people are more concerned about the outcome if they call 911 and [are] less likely to call, but it doesn’t talk about how the actual person who overdoses fares.

One also advocated for deeper qualitative research among PWUD, especially the traumatic effects of torn social ties due to overdose death as well as the criminal legal consequences it might invite:

If we could know the impact on people’s drug use just due to the emotional toll on communities as a result of these laws…a caregiver or a family member, a close friend who played a big part in my life being arrested, along with the fact that someone died. What impact is that having on people who use drugs?

In general, these suggestions were shaped by broader questions like “I wonder how much of this stuff could tip public opinion?” and “Could you get cops to care about the impacts of their actions?” In other words, these stated research priorities were not motivated by a perceived need to understand the impacts of DIH laws, per se, but rather by the group’s perceived need to convince community leaders and policy makers that this evidently harmful law is, in fact, harmful.

Community-engaged review: People who use drugs

When prompted to discuss their interpretation of the evidence presented to them, PWUD largely expressed that the findings of our scoping review aligned with their prior experiences and beliefs. In the initial conversation following the presentation of review findings, focus group members expressed shared frustration with criminalized responses to substance use in a general sense. One comment that garnered verbal agreement from most of the focus group highlighted the perceived futility of punitive responses to drug use:

We spend so much money and time trying to put people that are associated with drugs behind bars when that money could be spent [other ways]. There is no rehabilitation in prison. That’s not what it is.

Several also spoke of the mismatch between the apparent goals and real-world outcomes of criminalization, generally speaking. One stated:

You should be getting help instead of 20-year prison sentences for being a drug addict. Just because, I mean, a lot of this is simple possession. You get a couple of charges and the enhancement law, habitual offender, they hit you with that bitch and you get caught two or three times with a $20 little sack of meth and you fixing to go down for seven or eight years for habitual offender. And the whole time you’re just a little teeny fish.

A few also spoke to the way criminal justice involvement served to perpetuate their substance use (“There’s more drugs in prison than there is right here”).

Some focus group members responded to the evidence presented with critiques of what they perceived to be widespread misalignment between the actions of criminal legal professionals and the purported public health goals of drug control efforts generally. For example, one focus group member attributed the enthusiastic policing of PWUD to a widespread misunderstanding of so-called “drug-related crime” among criminal legal professionals:

A lot of that is getting confused and there’s a lot of people going to jail for drug charges that, very little of the problems is behind drugs, it’s more behind mental [illness]. And it’s getting confused and they lockin’ them up thinking, there’s another sorry ass drug addict off the street, which, the whole time he’s got mental problems.

Similarly, another suggested that law enforcement officers making drug-related arrests aren’t thinking about the larger consequences of their actions, positive or negative, noting “They just want to clear the books. That’s all they want to do. They want to put somebody in prison and, ok…they got it off the books, they go home.” Indeed, it was striking how unanimously focus group members responded to the findings of this scoping review with critiques about drug criminalization, generally, often endorsing the belief that punitive responses to drug use were inherently futile and driven by a criminal legal professionals’ profound lack of understanding of the problems they ostensibly seek to tackle.

In contrast, nearly all focus group members expressed surprise at the findings of Beletsky’s media analysis (2019), especially the racial disparities in the kinds of overdose deaths that prosecutors pursue through DIH charges (“You hear about racism, shit like that, but to hear that, it’s kind of amazing” and “So, they don’t care about [this] killing Black folks?”). Several members of the group connected this finding to a recent overdose of a Haywood resident known to some in the room. One remarked:

The little Black girl [who overdosed]. Nobody investigated that whole lot about it. She was just as important as anybody around me. She was loved and she was missed. Seemed like it got swept under the rug. That’s fucked up.

Importantly, discussion of this death and the lack of institutional response the focus group members saw in its aftermath took place throughout and in between discussions of the inappropriateness of criminal or punitive responses to drug use, suggesting that the lack of investigation was perceived by focus group members as another indicator of the institutional abandonment of Black persons, not a missed opportunity to pursue a desirable form of “justice” through a police investigation.

When prompted to share their views on the utility of these scoping review findings, PWUD were broadly unenthusiastic. One summarized this sentiment, offering, “Knowing this, I don’t think it’s going to change the drug trade or the drug buying. I don’t think it’s going to matter…and I don’t see where it would.” However, several voiced the sentiment—shared by public health and harm reduction professionals—that a more focused training on the legal risks of calling 911 (“I would say [we need] increased awareness of the law itself”) and on the use of naloxone to manage overdose emergencies without calling 911 (“I think giving a class on how to administer [naloxone] might be helpful…[so] people know how to use that shit”) would be helpful to PWUD given the risks posed by DIH laws.

Importantly, PWUD struggled to offer suggestions for future research based on the findings of this scoping review. Our questions about this were met with long silences—silences which seemed all the more powerful given earlier comments that the data we presented offered nothing new; with the narrow exception of specific racial inequities in the filing of DIH charges, focus group members already knew everything we told them, because they had lived it.

Community-engaged review: People who have lost an adult family member to fatal overdose

This focus group consisted entirely of women who had lost an adult child to overdose sometime within the past few years. This demographic feature was likely due to our choice to recruit members of this community through a mutual aid group that had already developed a unique socio-demographic profile. Most, if not all, of these focus group members had direct interactions with law enforcement following the death of their child (either as the result of a formal police investigation or to apply pressure to police to pursue a formal investigation), and all had personal connections to peers who had experienced similar losses that resulted in DIH charges being filed against someone for the death of their child.

When prompted to offer their interpretation of the review findings, members of this focus group strongly rejected the evidence suggesting that DIH laws may deter PWUD from calling 911 to report an overdose. One summarized this feeling as follows:

Yea, I don’t see the connection in not calling 911. If I’m not the one that sold you that drug, I want you to get help. I don’t see how your drug dealer possibly facing charges–and I’m not your drug dealer, I’m just a fellow drug user, right? I don’t see the connection that that would stop somebody from calling 911.

This general disbelief was a recurring theme in the conversation, producing several explanatory claims followed by a rapid back and forth of agreement by other focus group members. For instance, some tied this disbelief to the fact that no DIH charges had been filed in Haywood County since the 2018 s degree murder conviction that inspired the state-wide DIH law (Stone, 2019): “Why would they be worried? No, the last time one was done [DIH charge filed] was five years ago. One time, one time.”

More often, though, the conversation about the perceived unbelievability of this finding shifted rapidly between assertions that calling 911 was a natural, human reaction to a medical emergency and assertions that PWUD, due to their substance use, lack the cognitive ability to be swayed by the risk of long-term consequences, such as being charged with DIH, with participants energetically echoing each other’s statements. The excerpt that follows is illustrative of this lively pattern in the discussion:

Speaker 1: I think they’re not thinking long term. They’re in a panic state. You know, do you want to go to jail that night, see, if you’re partying with them, your mind is not going to want to call 911, your mind is going to want to be, oh, I’m out of here dude. You know, do they want to go to jail that night?

Speaker 2: I think a lot of them will still call 911 and then take off.

Speaker 3: Exactly, that might be it. I wouldn’t hang out.

Speaker 2: And I know you guys [research team] are going by studies, but I am not convinced it would stop somebody from calling 911. I think we all, whether we’re on drugs, not on drugs, I think we all have the same human instinct to save another human.

Speaker 4: Yea morals. I know there’s not much of them now, but.

Speaker 2: Even if I was in the process of breaking the law myself, you can call 911, [give] the address, then hightail it out of there. So, I’m not sure that–I understand that the people that were part of that study that that’s what they said. But I still feel like, you know…if they’re high, I would love to think that even in his own drug state of mind that my son would call 911.

Speaker 5: Oh, I know my daughter would.

Speaker 2: My son would.

This single focus group provided insufficient opportunity to explore the themes, beliefs, and contradictions that make up these viewpoints. However, it bears mentioning that an explicit tension arose throughout the discussion between the deep love and grief that focus group members held for their deceased children and deep anger and frustration with the persons who use or sell drugs who were proximal to those preventable deaths. This tension may provide a bit—though certainly a woefully incomplete bit—of explanatory context for how this discussion about calling 911 took shape.

In contrast, members of this entirely white focus group generally expressed high confidence in Beletsky’s (2019) finding that nearly all DIH cases brought against defendants classified as “traditional dealers/suppliers” were brought in response to the death of a white person:

Speaker 1: I feel like our country, especially our media, we are inherently biased to, how do I want to say this? White people are more important. That’s not my opinion, obviously.

JC: It’s the discourse, as we anthropologists like to say.

Speaker 1: Right. And you know, you don’t hear about the white drug dealer selling drugs to the Black person and them overdosing. And I’m certain it happens.

Speaker 2: It happens in [another North Carolina county] all the time.

Speaker 1: But it’s more salacious, more headlines too, especially if it’s a middle class or higher white person that overdoses.

Several focus group members endorsed this conclusion while also recognizing that media coverage of overdose and DIH cases were useful—needed even—to keep the issue of overdose deaths “front of mind” for policy makers and the public.

Similar to PWUD, members of this focus group voiced low enthusiasm for the importance or impact of the scoping review findings, suggesting that the evidence held little value for themselves as individuals or for their wider community. Some members of this group expanded upon this view by noting that DIH charges had not been filed in Haywood County since the enactment of the state-wide DIH law, which, they asserted, artificially limited the ability to test the impacts of DIH prosecutions. For instance, in response to Carroll et al.’s (2021) exploratory study of the impacts of Haywood County’s 2018 case, focus group members offered a variety of comments to this end:

If no one’s going to be charged, then what does it matter?

How can you do a big study when it’s only been one time?

You’re never going to be able to test it again unless they convict somebody or charge somebody.

The law as it’s written is not being enforced, so it doesn’t matter.

In the same vein, scoping review findings related to DIH laws deterring 911 were not considered important, because they were not considered valid by focus group members, for reasons listed above. Findings related to racial inequities in DIH cases were also not lifted up by the group as important for their community; conversation on this topic concluded with the general acknowledgement that racism is systemic and pervasive, without discussion of how those challenges should be addressed.

Finally, members of this focus group shared many suggestions for future research. The first priority identified was to investigate law enforcement and prosecutors who are declining to file DIH charges: “What I would like to know is, investigate the people that are not enforcing it [the DIH law].” The second major suggestion was to “include more folks that it’s personally touched” in research. One focus group member clarified that this meant recruiting more parents affected by the loss of an adult child to overdose into research cohorts to better understand their experiences:

I would love to see a study expand with more people like us. I would love you all to be able to go to other counties and interview more moms and dads. And I’d love to have more dads. I wish we could get more dads involved.

Another said, “I feel like a lot of this focus…is ‘poor, pitiful drug dealer. Let’s see if we can get him another chance’…Well, there’s no focus on what they did. Our kids are not coming back.” Several others echoed this desire for policy-makers and researchers, alike, to make the stories and experiences of people who lost their life to overdose a central feature of their work.

Discussion

This scoping review of existing literature on the public health impacts of DIH laws identified only three studies that systematically investigated this relationship: one qualitative study (n = 40) conducted in Rhode Island prisons (Peterson et al., 2019), one survey study (n = 173) conducted in Anne Arundel County, Maryland (Rouhani et al., 2021), and one law review article presenting an analysis of DIH cases (n = 263) identified through media coverage (Beletsky, 2019). The remaining five articles included in this review offered limited, exploratory data that was encountered organically in the process of data collection for a different study on a different (if occasionally related) topic. In general, the quality of scientific evidence on the public health impacts of DIH laws is poor.

The most robust evidence from this scoping review suggests that DIH laws deter overdose witnesses from calling 911 to seek emergency medical help. The investigation of potential deterrence effects of DIH laws, inclusive of deterrence of criminal behavior and help-seeking behavior, dominated this literature. Other core theoretical justifications for criminal punishment (incapacitation, rehabilitation, punishment, and reparation) are effectively absent. Six of the eight articles included in this review, including two of the three articles describing systematic investigations, offer empirical evidence that align with the conclusion that DIH laws deter bystanders from calling 911 (Carroll et al., 2021; Latimore & Bergstein, 2017; McLean, 2018; Ondocsin et al., 2020; Peterson et al., 2019; Rouhani et al., 2021). Of note, the magnitude of that deterrent effect cannot be determined from these studies and is an important subject of future research.

This conclusion comports with the overwhelming evidence that willingness to call 911 to report an overdose is significantly increased by the establishment of Good Samaritan Laws (L. Hamilton et al., 2021; Jakubowski et al., 2018; Moallef & Hayashi, 2020; Nguyen & Parker, 2018; Rees et al., 2019). In this context, Good Samaritan Laws are state laws that, variably, extend limited immunity from criminal charges to persons who call 911 to report an overdose (Legislative Analysis & Public Poilcy Association, 2023). Scholars have long asserted, based on related evidence and expertise, that increasing the criminal punishment for drug law violations directly undermines Good Samaritan Laws and creates effective deterrents to calling 911 in the event of an overdose (Bergstein & Latimore, 2017; El-Sabawi et al., 2023; L. K. Hamilton et al., 2022; Ostrach et al., 2024; Ostrach & Hayes, 2019; Pamplin et al., 2023). This scoping review found evidence that supports this assertion; however, the piecemeal nature of this data, situated in various systematic and exploratory findings across several disconnected papers, limits our ability to draw robust conclusions about how, and how much, DIH laws shape bystanders’ choices. Future studies purposefully designed to investigate this relationship could offer much needed insight on the true impacts of DIH laws on community-level outcomes.

Focus group discussions about this and other findings from the scoping review revealed deep disconnects between different community groups that are directly impacted by DIH laws. The most striking example of this disconnect can be seen in the discordant reactions to evidence that DIH laws deter overdose bystanders from calling 911. Both the public health and harm reduction professionals and the PWUD who participated in focus groups voiced immediate recognition of this relationship. Many had long believed this to be the case, with some finding the data “vindicating” and others finding the data unremarkable because the relationship was otherwise self-evident. Our focus group of people who had lost adult family members to overdose, however, broadly rejected this conclusion on the basis that such a relationship simply didn’t make sense, appealing to a variety of assertions about the alleged thinking patterns and lived experiences of PWUD to support that assertion. This group also suggested that the impacts of DIH laws could not be reasonably assessed until a greater quantity of DIH cases were prosecuted. This claim is not inherently correct. Numerous analytical methods, such as the difference-in-difference approach (Columbia Public Health, 2019) and appropriately designed explanatory-sequential mixed methods protocols (Fetters et al., 2013), are capable of assessing the impacts of point-in-time events such as a single DIH prosecution. This does not undermine family members’ point that more (and more robust) research is badly needed, but it does point to both practical and ideological conflicts between the motivations of and, consequently, research needs expressed by PWUD (to avoid DIH cases to improve personal safety and overdose response) and family members (to pursue more DIH cases to improve understanding of their community impacts).

Unfortunately, we were unable to incorporate insights from law enforcement and criminal legal professionals into this analysis, because members of this group who were recruited for the study repeatedly declined to participate or even respond to our numerous invitations to discuss the study. This, alone, is an important finding of this study. While all persons are endowed with the right to refuse participation in any research effort, a blanket refusal by public servants working in law enforcement and criminal legal systems to interface with members of the public who are making scholarly inquiries severely hampers scientific investigations into the knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors of these professional groups. Limited survey data collected from North Carolina prosecutors suggests that many of these professionals endorse unsubstantiated claims about the public health effects of DIH laws—that they can deter drug distribution, deter fentanyl distribution, deter substance use, and reduce overdose deaths—in the absence of any evidence for these claims and in contradiction with existing data, such as that presented here, that the risk of an overdose event turning fatal is likely exacerbated by DIH laws and prosecutions (Morrissey et al., 2024). Thus, though we were unable to assess these relationships in this study, there is evidence to suggest that law enforcement and criminal legal professionals, like family members, hold views that are discordant with the lived experiences and insights of PWUD. It bears noting that family members’ common frustrations with the lack of DIH charges in Haywood County may suggest a major disconnect between the views of this group and those of criminal legal professionals as well.

Importantly, our focus group discussions highlight how poorly existing research on DIH laws serves the needs of the community groups directly affected by overdose. While some focus group participants found reason to advocate for better community education about DIH laws, individual rights, naloxone administration, and overdose response generally, most found little in the scoping review findings that was directly applicable to their lives. We interpret this as a powerful clarion call for public health and drug policy researchers. It points to the need for the intentional involvement of directly impacted community groups in the design and planning of research on DIH laws and similar criminalizing interventions. Many excellent models for community-based research among PWUD have been developed (Boilevin et al., 2019; Closson et al., 2016; Mathias et al., 2025; Morgan et al., 2023; Simon et al., 2021; Thulien et al., 2022). More researchers should prioritize these community-integrating approaches in their work.

Less attention has been paid in research to the recruitment and direct involvement of family members touched by overdose. Previous efforts have shown that the inclusion of relatives of adults who have experienced an overdose in scholarly investigations of drug policy and overdose can be challenging (e.g. Carroll et al., 2023b). We believe that the willingness to participate shown to us by family members in Haywood County is the result of long-term relationship-building between local research team members (BO) and directly impacted community members and leaders. It may be the case that a similar investment in trusting relationships is a necessary pre-requisite to the involvement of family members such as these. Though the challenges they face are different than those faced by PWUD, members of this group also have a great deal at stake and a great deal to lose of their participation in research is not carefully and respectfully managed. The community-based participatory research collaboration with families affected by overdose outlined by Matthias et al. (2025) may offer a productive model to follow.

Community members variably identified several priority areas for future research. Public health and harm reduction professionals advocated for research efforts to quantify the impact of DIH laws on overdose and other potentially negative health outcomes, likely motivated by their awareness of recent research quantifying the impact of law enforcement drug seizures on overdose deaths in U.S. cities (Kral et al., 2025; Ray et al., 2023). Focus group discussions among this group and among PWUD highlighted a perceived need for more informed strategies to respond to overdose and reduce the risk of arrest and prosecution when responding to an overdose event. Researchers could address this concern through the community-involved development and evaluation of adapted overdose response education, for example. Family members also advocated for research further exploring how DIH laws impact public health and how overdose events play out in the current policy environment. Indeed, no study identified in this scoping review investigated or even attempted to investigate the experiences of family members affected by overdose or by DIH prosecutions in response to that overdose. This is, indeed, a meaningful gap in current scholarship that should be robustly addressed in future research.

Limitations

Our findings are subject to limitations. The scoping review was intended to be expansive; however, very little scholarship that speaks directly to the public health impacts of DIH laws has been produced. Thus, judgement calls often had to be made about whether or not to include an article in the review based on a loosely related piece of data that emerged, often anecdotally, in its results. A different set of inclusion criteria may have produced slightly different results. However, given our dedication to erring on the side of inclusion rather than exclusion, we do not suspect these differences in results included would support meaningfully different conclusions from the data extracted. We were unsuccessful in our attempts to recruit law enforcement and criminal legal professionals to participate in this study; therefore, this key perspective is missing from our discussion about the scientific evidence presented here. Our focus group participants were recruited primarily through community organizations: public health professionals through local service organizations, PWUD through a local resource center, and people who have lost an adult relative to fatal overdose from a local support group. Further, our focus groups were conducted in a single county in western North Carolina. Thus, the viewpoints shared in those focus groups may not be representative of local community members who do not engage the services of these local organizations, communities in other regions, people of different demographic or socio-economic profiles, or people living in different policy environments. Much more research is needed on the lived experiences of and “common sense” beliefs held by the large and diverse population of people directly touched by overdose and DIH prosecution in the United States.

Conclusion

Though existing evidence on the public health impacts of DIH laws is generally very poor, relevant studies have repeatedly and consistently shown that DIH laws deter people who use drugs from calling 911 to report an overdose emergency. Little is known about any other deterrent effects (deterring drug selling, e.g.) that DIH laws might have, as available evidence is extremely limited and exploratory in nature. Further, the limited data available on trends in DIH prosecutions suggests extremely racialized motivations for pursuing these charges. Important questions raised by members of all community groups included in this community engaged review, remain unaddressed in current research. Robust qualitative and quantitative research on DIH laws and the ways these laws shape criminal legal involvement, overdose, and the lived experiences of people touched by overdose is urgently needed.
