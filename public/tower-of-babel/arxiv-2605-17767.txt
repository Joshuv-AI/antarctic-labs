Feature Learning in Linear-Width Two-Layer Networks: Two vs. One Step of Gradient Descent
Behrad Moniri, Hamed Hassani

Abstract
We study feature learning in two-layer neural networks within the linear-width regime, where the number of hidden neurons, sample size, and input dimension scale proportionally. While recent work has analyzed feature learning via a single step of gradient descent on the first layer weights in this regime, such one-step update schemes are fundamentally limited: the update to the weights is approximately rank-one, captures only a single direction, and requires the target function to have an information exponent of one. In this paper, we go beyond one-step updates to provide a full characterization of the features learned during the second step of gradient descent with step-sizes η 1 ≍ N α 1 \eta_{1}\asymp N^{\alpha_{1}} and η 2 ≍ N α 2 \eta_{2}\asymp N^{\alpha_{2}} for α 1 , α 2 ∈ [ 0 , 0.5 ) \alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in[0,0.5) , where N N is the number of hidden neurons. We derive a sharp spectral characterization of the updated weights, demonstrating they behave as a spiked random matrix with multiple outliers, each corresponding to a learned direction. We show that the number of these outliers is determined by the scaling parameters α 1 \alpha_{1} and α 2 \alpha_{2} through ⌊ α 2 1 / 2 − α 1 ⌋ \lfloor\frac{\alpha_{2}}{1/2-\alpha_{1}}\rfloor . Furthermore, by analyzing the alignment between these learned directions and the target function, we identify a qualitative gap between training with independent versus reused batches. While independent batches restrict learning to directions with an information exponent of one, batch reuse enables the second update to capture directions even when the information exponent exceeds one, provided that α 1 \alpha_{1} and α 2 \alpha_{2} are chosen properly. This shows that the benefits of batch reuse, previously observed in narrow-width regimes, persist in the high-dimensional linear-width limit as well. By characterizing these early-phase spectral evolutions, our work proposes a tractable framework for studying optimization and feature learning phenomenology in modern overparameterized networks.

While the one-step updated model has proven instrumental in exploring feature-learning-dependent aspects of modern machine learning, such as optimization design and studying emerging empirical phenomena

Moreover, for the model to have improvements over random features, the target function should have a non-zero linear component. In this paper, we extend this analysis to subsequent gradient descent update on the first-layer weights to uncover the fundamental structural changes it induces. In particular, we ask the following questions:

What features does the second step of gradient descent learn? How does the spectrum of the first-layer weight matrix evolve as a results of the second update? How do the learned features and the spectrum of the updated weights depend on the step-sizes? Does the second update enable learning beyond a single direction, and functions without a linear component?

1.1
Contributions

Toward answering the above questions, in this paper, we make the following contributions:

•

We study feature learning in two-layer linear width neural networks. Specifically, we extend the one-step training procedure introduced in
19
;
7
and update
\mathbf{W}
using two steps of update with step-sizes
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
. We consider a regime with
\eta_{1}\asymp N^{\alpha_{1}}
and
\eta_{2}\asymp N^{\alpha_{2}}
,
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in[0,1/2)
and examine how the learned features change with
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
.

•

We present a spectral analysis of the updated first-layer weight matrix. We show that after the second update, this matrix can be approximated in operator norm by the initialized weight
\mathbf{W}_{0}
plus a low-rank perturbation matrix, controlled by the scaling parameters
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
. The right singular vectors of the low-rank term lie in the span of a collection of
\Lambda=\lfloor\frac{\alpha_{2}}{1/2-\alpha_{1}}\rfloor
vectors
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2;k},
for
k\in\{1,\dots,\Lambda\}
. Figure
1
illustrates this finding. See Theorem
4
for details.

•

Next, we study how the learned directions
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2;k}
align with the relevant directions in the ground truth target function. We show that the answer changes significantly depending on whether the same or two independent sets of training data has been used to compute the gradients in the first and second iteration.

•

We show that when both steps use the same set of data to compute the gradient, the model can learn relevant directions even if the target function has no linear component. Whereas if a fresh independent set of samples is used for the second update, the model is still restricted to learning functions that have a linear part.

•

Finally, we provide numerical simulations and show that they empirically verify our theory.

To start, we first summarize the results from the prior work

Proposition 1
.

Under the conditions of Section
3.1

\displaystyle\Big\|\nabla_{\mathbf{W}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm Corr}(\mathbf{W}_{0},\mathbf{a}_{0};\mathbf{X}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{1})+c_{1}\mathbf{a}_{0}\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}^{\top}\Big\|_{\rm op}=\tilde{O}_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\right),

in which
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}:=\frac{1}{\xi_{1}{n}}\mathbf{X}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{1}
. Moreover, the updated first-layer weight matrix after the first step of (
5
) is given by
\mathbf{W}_{1}=\mathbf{W}_{0}+c_{1}\eta_{1}\mathbf{a}_{0}\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}^{\top}+\bm{\Delta}_{1}
with

Note that
\|\mathbf{W}_{1}\|_{\rm op}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(1)

This result fully characterizes the updated weight

Based on the proposition above, the vector

A one-step update is fundamentally limited in two ways. First, the update is approximately rank-one; thus, it can only learn a single direction
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}

M
distinct and orthogonal directions
\{\bm{\beta}_{\star,k}\}_{k\in[M]}
. Second, note that based on the conditions in Section
3.1
, the target function can written as

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{M}g_{k}(\mathbf{x}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{\star}^{\top})=\mathbf{x}^{\top}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{g_{k},1}\bm{\beta}_{\star,k}\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{M}g_{k,\perp}(\mathbf{x}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{\star,k})

with
\mathsf{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim{\sf N}(0,\mathbf{I}_{\mathrm{d}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{X}}})}\left[(\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{u})\,g_{k,\perp}(\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{u})\right]=0
,
\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}=1
. The vector
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}=({\xi_{1}n})^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{1}
is as a linear estimator and can only learn the linear part of the sum above; the nonlinear part effectively acts as an unlearnable noise for
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}
. Note that only directions
\bm{\beta}_{\star,k}
with
\tau(g_{k})=1
(i.e., those with
c_{g_{k},1}\neq 0
) contribute to the linear component, and as a result, a single step of gradient descent cannot learn directions
\bm{\beta}_{\star,k}
with
\tau(g_{k})>1
. We formalize this in the following theorem.

Proposition 2
.

Under the conditions of Section
3.1
, for any
p\in[M]
, the learned vector
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}
satisfies

\displaystyle\Big|\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{\star,p}-c_{g_{p},1}\Big|=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

This proposition shows that for any component
p\in[M]
of the target function in (
2
) with
\tau(g_{p})=1
, the learned direction
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}
has a non-trivial alignment with
\bm{\beta}_{\star,k}
. This alignment of weights with target directions is often referred to as weak recovery (see e.g.,
22
;
20
;
5
;
65
, etc.). Given a nontrivial alignment between the weights and a target direction, the second layer
\mathbf{a}
can be trained using regularized least squares to achieve good generalization performance (see e.g.,
(
7
, Theorem 11)
,
(
54
, Theorem 4.5)
, etc.).

In what follows, we analyze the second step of gradient descent and provide a precise characterization of the updated weight matrix
\mathbf{W}_{2}
and study the directions that the second update can learn, and the role of the step-size. First, we provide an approximation for the gradient of the loss function evaluated at
(\mathbf{W}_{1},\mathbf{a}_{0})
for a batch of training samples
(\mathbf{X}_{2},\mathbf{y}_{2})
.

Theorem 3
.

Under the conditions of Section
3.1
, the gradient matrix
\nabla_{\mathbf{W}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm Corr}(\mathbf{W}_{1},\mathbf{a}_{0};\mathbf{X}_{2},\mathbf{y}_{2})
, can be approximated with the matrix
\mathbf{G}
given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}:=-\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}(k+1){c}_{k+1}c_{1}^{k}\eta_{1}^{k}\;{\mathbf{a}_{0}^{\odot(k+1)}}\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2;k}^{\top},\quad\text{with}\quad\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2;k}:=\frac{1}{\xi_{2}n}\mathbf{X}_{2}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{y}_{2}\odot(\mathbf{X}_{2}\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1})^{\odot k}\right)

in which
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}
is defied in Proposition
1
, such that

\displaystyle\Big\|\nabla_{\mathbf{W}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\rm Corr}(\mathbf{W}_{1},\mathbf{a}_{0};\mathbf{X}_{2},\mathbf{y}_{2})-\mathbf{G}\Big\|_{\rm op}=\tilde{O}_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\right).

Notably, this theorem shows that the gradient used in the second iteration is no longer rank-one. Hence, unlike the initial update, the model is no longer restricted to learning a single direction. In particular, the gradient matrix aligns with a new set of directions,
\{\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2;k}\}_{k\in[L]}
. In the next theorem, we use this result to provide a full characterization of the weight matrix
\mathbf{W}_{2}
.

Theorem 4
.

Assume that the conditions of Section
3.1
hold with step-sizes
\eta_{1}\asymp N^{\alpha_{1}}
and
\eta_{2}\asymp N^{\alpha_{2}}
, and define the function

\Lambda:[0,1/2)\times[0,1/2)\to\mathbb{N}
as

\displaystyle\Lambda(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}):=\min\left(L-1,\left\lfloor\frac{\alpha_{2}}{1/2-\alpha_{1}}\right\rfloor\right),\quad\forall\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in[0,1/2).

The updated weight matrix after the second step of gradient descent update (
5
),
\mathbf{W}_{2}
, is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{2}=\mathbf{W}_{0}

\displaystyle+c_{1}\mathbf{a}_{0}\left(\eta_{1}\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}+\eta_{2}\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2;0}\right)^{\top}+\sum_{k=1}^{\Lambda(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}(k+1){c}_{k+1}c_{1}^{k}\;\eta_{2}\eta_{1}^{k}\;{\mathbf{a}_{0}^{\odot(k+1)}}{\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2;k}}^{\top}+\bm{\Delta}_{2}

where the vectors
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2;k}
are defined in Theorem
3
, and
\|\bm{\Delta}_{2}\|_{\rm op}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
.

In the above approximation expansion for
\mathbf{W}_{2}
, we have
\|\mathbf{W}_{0}\|_{\rm op}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
,
\|c_{1}\eta_{1}\mathbf{a}_{0}\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}\|_{\rm op}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(\eta_{1})=O_{\mathbb{P}}(N^{\alpha_{1}})
,
\|c_{1}\eta_{2}\mathbf{a}_{0}\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2;0}\|_{\rm op}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(\eta_{2})=O_{\mathbb{P}}(N^{\alpha_{2}})
. Also, the
k
-th term of the sum has an operator norm
O_{\mathbb{P}}(\eta_{2}\eta_{1}^{k}N^{-k/2})=O_{\mathbb{P}}(N^{\alpha_{2}+k(\alpha_{1}-1/2)})
. Thus, all the
\Lambda
terms in the sum have operator norm
\Omega_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
. Hence, the matrix
\bm{\Delta}_{2}
is negligible compared to the first terms.

This theorem shows that
\mathbf{W}_{2}
is a spiked random matrix, with a spectrum consisting of a bulk of singular-values that stick together resulting from the initialization
\mathbf{W}_{0}
and multiple outliers from the low-rank terms. The number of these outliers is controlled by
\Lambda(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\in\mathbb{N}
and depends on the scaling of the step-sizes. Figure
2
shows
\Lambda(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
for different values of
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in[0,0.5)
.

This theorem shows that after the second step of gradient descent update, the weights are in the
Bulk+Spikes
phase of training from
45
, and that the dynamics closely match the early phase training dynamics empirically characterized in prior work (see also
46
;
70
).

Using the theorem above, we immediately have the following corollary.

Corollary 5
.

Under the same conditions as Theorem
4
, the following statements hold:

•

If
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}<1/2
, we have
\Lambda(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})=0
and no new directions will be learned compared to the one-step update, and

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{2}=\mathbf{W}_{0}+c_{1}\mathbf{a}_{0}\left(\frac{\eta_{1}}{\xi_{1}n}\mathbf{X}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{1}+\frac{\eta_{2}}{\xi_{2}n}\mathbf{X}_{2}^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{2}\right)^{\top}+\bm{\Delta}_{3},\quad\text{with}\quad\|\bm{\Delta}_{3}\|_{\rm op}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)

In particular note that this is always the case, regardless of
\alpha_{2}
, whenever
\alpha_{1}=0
.

•

When
\alpha_{1}\to 0.5^{-}
, we have
\Lambda(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})=L-1
as long as
\alpha_{2}>0
.

Note that in particular, the first statement of the corollary shows that in the reused batch setting, if
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}<1/2
, the second update is exactly equivalent to a one-step of update with step-size
\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}
. This extends the finding in
7
;
70
that proved a similar result when
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}=0
.

Moreover, the second statement shows that if
\alpha_{1}\to 0.5^{-}
(as long as
\alpha_{2}>0
), the number of learned directions will proliferate. Note that in this paper, we do not cover the case with
\alpha_{1}
or
\alpha_{2}
equal to
0.5
. When
\alpha_{1}=0.5
, the error term
\bm{\Delta}_{1}
in Proposition
1
will have
\|\bm{\Delta}_{1}\|_{\rm op}=\Theta_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
. Although in that case
\|\bm{\Delta}_{1}\|_{\rm op}\asymp\|\mathbf{W}_{0}\|_{\rm op}
and
\bm{\Delta}_{1}
can no longer be neglected, it can be be shown that
\bm{\Delta}_{1}
essentially equivalent to an independent noise, with the effect of rescaling the variance of the untrained weight
\mathbf{W}_{0}
(see
(
17
, Section A.4)
). A similar phenomenon will also occur for the second gradient step when
\alpha_{2}=0.5
with
\bm{\Delta}_{2}
becoming comparable to
\mathbf{W}_{0}
in operator norm. We leave the analysis of the boundary cases as future work.

5
Analysis of the Learned Directions

Now let’s turn back to the learned directions
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2;k}
. Recall that the direction learned by the first step of gradient descent
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}=(\xi_{1}n)^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{1}
is a linear estimator, and thus
\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{2}:=\mathbf{X}_{2}\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}
is the prediction of this estimator for covariates
\mathbf{X}_{2}
. The vector
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2;k}=(\xi_{2}n)^{-1}\mathbf{X}_{2}^{\top}(\mathbf{y}_{2}\odot\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{2}^{\odot k})
is a nonlinear estimator for predicting the labels
\mathbf{y}_{2}
from
\mathbf{X}_{2}
. This formulation underscores the inherent difference between the reused and fresh batch settings of Section
3
: in the reused batch setting,
\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{2}
is the prediction for the same samples used to construct
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}
; conversely, in the fresh batch setting,
\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{2}
is the prediction for a fresh, independent set of samples that were not used in the construction of
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}
.
In what follows, we study the alignments of the learned directions
\{\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2;k}\}_{k=1}^{\Lambda(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}
with the target directions
\{\bm{\beta}_{\star,k}\}_{k=1}^{M}
. We use it to characterize the differences between the reused batch and the fresh batch settings.

\bm{\beta}_{\star,p}

Theorem 6
.

Assume that the conditions of Section
3.1
hold. In the
reused batch
setting, for any
p\in[M]
and
q\in[\Lambda(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})]
we have

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}_{\star,p}^{\top}\,\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2;q}\to_{\mathbb{P}}\mathsf{E}\left[z_{p}y\left(\phi y+\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{g_{k},1}z_{k}+G\right)^{q}\right]

where
z_{1},\dots,z_{M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}{\sf N}(0,1)
,
{\varepsilon}\sim{\sf N}(0,\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2})
are independent random variables, and
y={\varepsilon}+\sum_{k=1}^{M}g_{k}(z_{k})
. Moreover
G\sim{\sf N}(0,\phi\,\mathsf{E}[y^{2}])
independent of other randomness.

Note that when
q=0
, the above theorem simplifies to
\bm{\beta}_{\star,p}^{\top}\,\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2;0}\to\mathsf{E}[z_{p}y]=c_{g_{p},1}
which recovers Proposition
2
. The above theorem shows that
\bm{\beta}_{\star,p}^{\top}\,\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2;q}
can be non-zero even if
c_{g_{p},1}=0
. We demonstrate this in the following simple example.

Example 7
.

Consider the batch reuse setting and assume that
M=1
,
\sigma_{\varepsilon}=0
, and
g_{1}(x)=H_{3}(x)
. We have
\bm{\beta}_{\star,1}^{\top}\,\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2;q}\to\mathsf{E}[zH_{3}(z)\left(\phi H_{3}(z)+G\right)^{q}]
that is non-zero for
q=2
. This means that
\mathbf{W}_{2}
will have a non-trivial alignment with
\bm{\beta}_{\star,1}
if the step-sizes satisfy
\Lambda(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\geq 2
.

This example demonstrates that in the reused batch setting, with proper step-sizes, the second update does can in fact make
\mathbf{W}_{2}
aligned to directions corresponding to an information-exponent greater than one. This is in line with a similar finding in the narrow-width regime by
22
;
41
showing that gradient descent with reused batches can learn functions with information exponent exceeding one. Now, let’s move to the fresh batch setting.

Theorem 8
.

Assume that the conditions of Section
3.1
hold. In the
fresh batch
setting, for any
p\in[M]
and
q\in[\Lambda(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})]
we have

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}_{\star,p}^{\top}\,\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2;q}\to_{\mathbb{P}}\mathsf{E}\left[z_{p}y\left(\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{g_{k},1}z_{k}+G\right)^{q}\right]

where
z_{1},\dots,z_{M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}{\sf N}(0,1)
,
{\varepsilon}\sim{\sf N}(0,\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2})
are independent random variables, and
y={\varepsilon}+\sum_{k=1}^{M}g_{k}(z_{k})
. Moreover
G\sim{\sf N}(0,\phi\,\mathsf{E}[y^{2}]/\xi_{2})
independent of other randomness. Consequently, for any direction
\bm{\beta}_{\star,p}
with

we have
\bm{\beta}_{\star,p}^{\top}\,\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2;q}\to 0
for all
q
.

This limit is fundamentally different from the limit in Theorem
6
. In particular, it shows that in the fresh batch setting, the two-step updated weight matrix
\mathbf{W}_{2}
never aligns with a target direction that has information exponent greater than one.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we establish a sharp spectral characterization of the first-layer weight matrix in two-layer neural networks after two steps of gradient descent. We show that the spectrum of the updated weights consists of a bulk of eigenvalues from initialization and multiple outliers, which we fully characterize for different scaling regimes of the step-sizes. Our analysis demonstrates that the second update allows the model to capture multiple relevant directions, effectively expanding the model’s capacity to learn beyond single-index functions. These findings provide a rigorous mathematical framework for understanding the emergence of structured representations during the early stages of optimization in the linear-width regime.

Useful Lemmas

Lemma 9
.

For any
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}}
and
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}
and
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{u}\times d_{v}}
, we have

\displaystyle(\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^{\top})\odot\mathbf{A}=\diag(\mathbf{u})\mathbf{A}\diag(\mathbf{v}).

Proof.

For any indices
(i,j)
, the
(i,j)
-th entry of the left-hand side is

[(\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^{\top})\odot\mathbf{A}]_{ij}=(\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^{\top})_{ij}A_{ij}=u_{i}v_{j}A_{ij}.

Similarly, the
(i,j)
-th entry of the right-hand side is

[\text{diag}(\mathbf{u})\mathbf{A}\,\text{diag}(\mathbf{v})]_{ij}=\sum_{k,l}(\text{diag}(\mathbf{u}))_{ik}A_{kl}(\text{diag}(\mathbf{v}))_{lj}.

The sums collapse to
u_{i}A_{ij}v_{j}
, finishing the proof.
∎

Lemma 10

(Taylor expansion of Hermite Polynomials)
.

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle H_{k}(x+y)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}x^{j}H_{k-j}(y).

Proof.

Note that
\frac{d}{dx}H_{k}(x)=kH_{k-1}(x)

(
1
, Equation 22.8.8)
and thus
\frac{d^{j}}{dx^{j}}H_{k}(x)=\frac{k!}{(k-j)!}H_{k-j}(x)
. By Taylor expanding
H_{k}(x+y)
at
y
, we find

H_{k}(x+y)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{x^{j}}{j!}\frac{d^{j}}{dy^{j}}H_{k}(y)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}x^{j}H_{k-j}(y),

concluding the proof.
∎

Lemma 11
.

Each of the following statements hold with probability
1-o(1)
:

(a)

\|\mathbf{y}_{1}\|_{\infty}=\tilde{\Theta}(1)
(similarly for
\|\mathbf{y}_{2}\|_{\infty}
).

(b)

\|\mathbf{a}_{0}^{\odot p}\|_{\infty}=\tilde{\Theta}({N^{-\frac{p}{2}}})

(c)

\|\mathbf{a}_{0}^{\odot p}\|_{2}=\Theta(N^{\frac{1-p}{2}})

(d)

\|\mathbf{X}_{1}\|_{\rm op}=\Theta(\sqrt{N})
(similarly for
\|\mathbf{X}_{2}\|_{\rm op}
).

(e)

\Big\|\mathbf{y}_{2}\odot(\mathbf{X}_{2}\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1})^{\odot j}\Big\|_{\infty}=\tilde{\Theta}(1)

Proof.

Here, we prove each statement separately.

(a)

Note that
\mathbf{y}_{1}=\bm{{\varepsilon}}+\sum_{k=1}^{M}g_{k}(\mathbf{X}_{1}\bm{\beta}_{\star,k})
. Using the standard (sub-)gaussian maximal inequality
(
68
, Proposition 2.7.6)
, we have
\max_{i\in[n]}\left[{\varepsilon}_{i}\right]=\tilde{\Theta}(1)
with probability
1-o(1)
. For the signal term, recall that
\|\bm{\beta}_{\star,k}\|=1
, thus the entries of
\mathbf{X}_{1}\bm{\beta}_{\star,k}
are independent and distributed according to
{\sf N}(0,1)
. Thus, using standard Gaussian Maximal Inequalities, with probability
1-o(1)
we have

\max_{i\in[n]}\left[|\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{\star,k}|\right]=\tilde{\Theta}(1)
. Putting these together proves part (a).

(b)

Similar to part (a), this part follows from the standard gaussian maximal inequality, by recalling that each element
i\in[N]
of
[\mathbf{a}_{0}]_{i}\sim{\sf N}(0,1/N)
.

(c)

Let
a_{i}\sim{\sf N}(0,1/N)
for
i\in[N]
be the
i
-th entry of
\mathbf{a}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. We have

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{a}_{0}^{\odot p}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{2p}.

Thus, we have
\mathsf{E}\|\mathbf{a}_{0}^{\odot p}\|_{2}^{2}=(2p-1)!!\cdot N^{1-p}
. Part (c) follows by applying the sub-Weibull concentration inequality
69
to prove the concentration of
\|\mathbf{a}_{0}^{\odot p}\|_{2}^{2}
around
\mathsf{E}\|\mathbf{a}_{0}^{\odot p}\|_{2}^{2}
.

(d)

Part (d) is standard, see e.g.,
(
68
, Theorem 4.4.5)
.

(e)

To prove this part, first note that from part (a),
\|\mathbf{y}_{2}\|_{\infty}=\tilde{\Theta}(1)
. Also, using an expansion of
\mathbf{y}
similar to the proof of part (a), we have
\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}\|_{2}=\|\frac{1}{\xi_{1}n}\mathbf{X}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{1}\|_{2}=\Theta_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
. Putting these together, will conclude the proof.

∎

Lemma 12
.

Let
k\geq 1
and consider
H_{k}(\mathbf{W}_{0}\mathbf{X}_{1}^{\top})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times\xi_{1}n}
. With probability
1-o(1)
we have

\displaystyle\|H_{k}(\mathbf{W}_{0}\mathbf{X}_{1}^{\top})\|_{\rm op}=\tilde{O}(\sqrt{N}).

Similarly
\|H_{k}(\mathbf{W}_{0}\mathbf{X}_{2}^{\top})\|_{\rm op}=\tilde{O}(\sqrt{N}).

Proof.

First, note that by the orthogonality of Hermite polynomials and
(
58
, Chapter 11.2)
, we can write

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim{\sf N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{\mathrm{d}_{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{X}}})}\left[H_{k}(\mathbf{W}_{0}\mathbf{x})H_{k}(\mathbf{W}_{0}\mathbf{x})^{\top}\right]=k!(\mathbf{W}_{0}\mathbf{W}_{0}^{\top})^{\circ k}.

Next, using
(
67
, Theorem 5.39)
and
(
8
, Corollary A.21)
, we have

\|k!(\mathbf{W}_{0}\mathbf{W}_{0}^{\top})^{\circ k}\|_{\rm op}\leq Ck!

for some universal constant
C>0
. Now, to upper bound the operator norm of
H_{k}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_{0}^{\top})
, note that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\|H_{k}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_{0}^{\top})\|_{\rm op}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\|H_{k}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_{0})^{\top}H_{k}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_{0})\|_{\rm op}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{1}{n}\|H_{k}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_{0}^{\top})\|_{\rm op}^{2}\geq t^{2}/n\right]

67
, Theorem 5.8)
to upper bound the numerator as follows:

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}\left[\left\|\frac{1}{n}H_{k}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_{0})^{\top}H_{k}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_{0})-k!(\mathbf{W}_{0}\mathbf{W}_{0}^{\top})^{\odot k}\right\|_{\rm op}\right]\leq\max\left((Ck!)^{1/2}\delta,\delta^{2}\right)

Note that

\displaystyle\left\|{\frac{1}{\xi_{2}{n}}\mathbf{X}_{2}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{y}_{2}\odot(\mathbf{X}_{2}\mathbf{Q}_{1}^{-1}\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1})^{\odot k}\right)}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\xi_{2}n}\|\mathbf{X}_{2}\|_{\rm op}\cdot\left\|{\mathbf{y}_{2}\odot(\mathbf{X}_{2}\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1})^{\odot k}}\right\|_{2}.

\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}\|_{2}=\Theta_{\mathbb{P}}(1)

\mathbf{X}_{2}\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1}
is element-wise
\Theta_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
. The vector
\mathbf{y}_{2}
is also element-wise
\Theta_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
. Hence,

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathbf{y}_{2}\odot(\mathbf{X}_{2}\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1})^{\odot k}}\right\|_{2}=\Theta_{\mathbb{P}}(\sqrt{\xi_{2}n})=\Theta_{\mathbb{P}}(\sqrt{N})

Putting everything together, we proved that
\|\mathbf{O}_{k}\|_{\rm op}=\Theta_{\mathbb{P}}(\eta_{1}^{k}\eta_{2}N^{-k/2})
. Recall form Section
3.1
that
\eta_{1}\asymp N^{\alpha_{1}}
and
\eta_{2}\asymp N^{\alpha_{2}}
. Thus

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{O}_{k}\|_{\rm op}=\Theta_{\mathbb{P}}\left(N^{(\alpha_{1}-1/2)k+\alpha_{2}}\right)

which is
o(1)
for all
k
satisfying

\displaystyle k>\Lambda(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}):=\min\left(L-1,\left\lfloor\frac{\alpha_{2}}{\frac{1}{2}-\alpha_{1}}\right\rfloor\right).

Hence,
\mathbf{W}_{2}
can be approximated with
\bar{}\mathbf{W}_{2}
given by

\displaystyle\bar{}\mathbf{W}_{2}=\mathbf{W}_{0}

\displaystyle+c_{1}\mathbf{a}_{0}\left(\frac{\eta_{1}}{\xi_{1}n}(\mathbf{X}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{1})+\frac{\eta_{2}}{\xi_{2}n}(\mathbf{X}_{2}^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{2})\right)^{\top}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{\Lambda(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})}{(k+1){c}_{k+1}c_{1}^{k}\eta_{2}\eta_{1}^{k}\;\mathbf{a}_{0}^{\odot(k+1)}\,\left[\frac{1}{\xi_{2}{n}}\mathbf{X}_{2}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{y}_{2}\odot\left(\frac{1}{\xi_{1}n}\mathbf{X}_{2}\mathbf{X}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{1}\right)^{\odot k}\right)\right]}^{\top},

with
\|\mathbf{W}_{2}-\bar{}\mathbf{W}_{2}\|_{\rm op}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
, proving the theorem.

Proof of Lemma
13

Proof.

The squared norm of
\bm{\rho}_{-i}
is given by

\displaystyle\|\bm{\rho}_{-i}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\bar{\bm{\beta}}\|_{2}^{2}-2\bar{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\neq i}{\mathbf{x}_{j}}\,y_{j}\right)+\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\neq i}{\mathbf{x}_{j}}\,y_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}

(9)

In what follows, we compute the limit of each these terms.

First Term.

For this term in (
9
), we recall that the vectors
\{\bm{\beta}_{\star,k}\}_{k\in[M]}
are orthonormal and
\bar{\bm{\beta}}=\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{g_{k},1}\bm{\beta}_{\star,k}
. Hence, we readily have

\displaystyle\|\bar{\bm{\beta}}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{g_{k},1}^{2}.

Second Term.

The second term in (
9
) can be written as

\displaystyle-2\bar{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\neq i}{\mathbf{x}_{j}}\,y_{j}\right)=-\frac{2}{n}\sum_{j\neq i}\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{g_{k},1}({\mathbf{x}_{j}}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{\star,k})\,y_{j}.

Note that
z_{k}:=\mathbf{x}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{\star,k}\sim{\sf N}(0,1)
. Thus, using the weak law of large numbers, with probability
1-o(1)
we have

\displaystyle-2\bar{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\neq i}{\mathbf{x}_{j}}\,y_{j}\right)\to-2\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{g_{k},1}\mathsf{E}\left[z_{k}y\right]

where
y={\varepsilon}+\sum_{t=1}^{M}g_{t}(z_{t})
in which
z_{1},\dots,z_{M}\sim{\sf N}(0,1)
and
{\varepsilon}\sim{\sf N}(0,\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2})
are independent random variables, because the vectors
\bm{\beta}_{\star,1},\dots,\bm{\beta}_{\star,M}

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}[z_{k}y]=\mathsf{E}\left[z_{k}{\varepsilon}+\sum_{s=1}^{M}z_{k}g_{s}(z_{s})\right]=c_{g_{k},1}.

Hence, putting everything together, we have

\displaystyle-2\bar{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\neq i}{\mathbf{x}_{j}}\,y_{j}\right)=-2\sum_{k=1}^{M}c_{g_{k},1}^{2}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

Third Term.

The third term in (
9

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\neq i}{\mathbf{x}_{j}}\,y_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=i}^{n}{\mathbf{x}_{j}}\,y_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)=n^{-2}\left(\mathbf{y}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{y}\right)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

Recall that
\mathbf{y}=\bm{{\varepsilon}}+\sum_{k=1}^{M}g_{k}(\mathbf{X}\bm{\beta}_{\star,k})
. Hence, recalling the independence of
\bm{{\varepsilon}}
from all other randomness in the problem, using the weak law of large number we have

Case of
