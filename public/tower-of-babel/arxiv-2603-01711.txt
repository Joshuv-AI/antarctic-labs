Lower bounds for the large deviations and moments of the Riemann zeta function on the critical line
Louis-Pierre Arguin, Nathan Creighton

Abstract
. Building on work in [ 4 ] on the Riemann zeta function at height T T off the critical line, we prove an unconditional lower bound on the critical line for real large deviations of the order V ∼ α ​ log ⁡ log ​ T V\sim\alpha\log\log T for any α > 0 . \alpha>0. This gives another proof of the sharpest known unconditional lower bounds on the fractional moments of the Riemann zeta function, due to [ 10 ] . The lower bound on large deviations is of the same order of magnitude as the upper bound proved in [ 1 ] , for the range 0 < α < 2 . 0<\alpha<2.

1.
Introduction

Ever since Selberg’s Central Limit Theorem
[
18
]
for
\log\zeta\left(\frac{1}{2}+{\rm i}t\right)
, the distribution of values of the Riemann zeta function on the critical line
{\rm Re}\ s=1/2
has been a key topic in analytic number theory.
It is of particular interest to understand the extent to which the Gaussian behaviour persists beyond the standard deviation order,
\sqrt{\log\log T}
.
Theorem 2 in
[
16
]
states that the measures of the level sets obey the same asymptotic for deviations of
\log|\zeta(\frac{1}{2}+{\rm i}t)|
up to
\left(\log\log T\right)^{\frac{3}{5}}
.
However, it is the range of the distribution at the order of the variance which determines the growth of the moments of the Riemann zeta function.
This provides the motivation for studying the measure of level sets at this range.

1.1.
Main results

We prove a lower bound for the measure of level sets of the Riemann zeta function on the critical line at the order of magnitude
\sqrt{\log\log T}.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\alpha>0
and
V\sim\alpha\log\log T.
Then for
T
sufficiently large, we have

(1.1)

\frac{1}{T}\left|\left\{t\in[0,T]:\log\left|\zeta\left(\frac{1}{2}+{\rm i}t\right)\right|>V\right\}\right|\geq K_{\alpha}\int^{\infty}_{V}\frac{e^{-\frac{y^{2}}{\log\log T}}}{\sqrt{\log\log T}}{\rm d}y,

with the constant
K_{\alpha}
satisfying

(1.2)

K_{\alpha}\gg\left(C\alpha^{2}\log\alpha\right)^{-\alpha^{2}},

as
\alpha\to\infty,
for some absolute constant
C
.

A matching upper bound up to a constant was proved unconditionally in
[
1
]
in the range
0<\alpha<2
.
By using the method of decomposing moments into integrals over measures of level sets as pioneered by Soundararajan
[
19
]
, we recover the lower bound for all the fractional moments proved in
[
10
]
and
[
15
]
, with the same constant.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
\alpha>0
be any positive real number and
T
be sufficiently large.
Then for
K_{\alpha}
as above,

(1.3)

\int^{T}_{0}|\zeta(1/2+{\rm i}t)|^{2\alpha}{\rm d}t\gg K_{\alpha}T\left(\log T\right)^{\alpha^{2}}.

In
[
19
]
, Soundararajan mentions:
Our proof suggests that the dominant contribution to the
2k^{\text{th}}
moment comes from
t
such that
\zeta(1/2+{\rm i}t)
has size
(\log T)^{k},
and this set has measure about
T/(\log T)^{k^{2}}
.

Theorem
1.1
confirms the suggestion that the values of this range do occur with high enough measure to produce the moments.
More work is required to reverse the decomposition of the moments and attain results concerning the measure of level sets, even when the corresponding moments bounds are known. From the lower bound on the fractional moments, Soundararajan requires the Riemann Hypothesis to attain an upper bound as well as the following lower bound large deviation result in
[
19
, Corollary B]
:

(1.4)

\text{meas}\left\{t\in[0,T]:\left|\zeta\left(\frac{1}{2}+{\rm i}t\right)\right|\geq\left(\log T\right)^{k}\right\}\geq T(\log T)^{-k^{2}+o(1)}.

The main purpose of Theorem
1.1
is to remove the dependency on the Riemann Hypothesis and sharpen this to attain the unconditional Gaussian decay, but we go further and get constants necessary to retrieve the moments bound in Corollary
1.2
.

Note that in
[
4
]
, the authors produced a lower bound for large deviations shifted off the critical line to real part
\sigma_{0}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta(\alpha)}{\log T},
for some sufficiently large parameter
\delta(\alpha).
By a convexity argument, this was enough to recover the moment bounds on the critical line, albeit with a worse constant
\gg(C\alpha^{36}\log\alpha)^{-\alpha^{2}}
.
In contrast, Theorem
1.1
restores the results to large deviations on the critical line with the same constant as the sharpest known results for the corresponding lower bounds for the moments.
Conjecture 2 in
[
16
]
predicts that, in fact, the constant for the large deviations of the range in Theorem
1.1
matches that from the conjectures of Keating and Snaith
[
14
]
for the moments.
More precisely, the constant should be the product of an arithmetic factor
a_{\alpha}
and a geometric factor
g_{\alpha}
. As
\alpha\to\infty
, these factors behave like (
[
6
]
,
[
12
]
)

(1.5)

a_{\alpha}=(\log\alpha)^{-\alpha^{2}}\exp(O(\alpha^{2})),\qquad g_{\alpha}=\alpha^{-\alpha^{2}}\exp(O(\alpha^{2})).

The constant
K_{\alpha}
is approximately a square worse than this conjectured value.

1.2.
Results in the
q
-aspect

The method of proof generalises to many other families of
L
-functions where two distinct twisted moments can be bounded, with a lower bound for the lower order moment and an upper bound for the higher. For example, one can use it to give lower bounds on large deviations of central values of primitive Dirichlet
L
-functions. For this, in place of the twisted second and fourth moments of the Riemann zeta function, we deploy the twisted second
[
13
]
,
[
5
]
and fourth
[
20
]
moments of central values of
L
-functions with even, primitive characters with the same large modulus. This yields the following theorem.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\alpha>0,

q
be a large prime, and
W\sim\alpha\log\log q.
Then

(1.6)

\frac{1}{\varphi^{+}(q)}\left|\left\{\chi\text{ even primitive modulo }q:\log\left|L\left(\frac{1}{2},\chi\right)\right|\geq W\right\}\right|\geq K_{\alpha}\int^{\infty}_{W}\frac{e^{-\frac{y^{2}}{\log\log q}}}{\sqrt{\log\log q}}{\rm d}y,

where the constant
K_{\alpha}
satisfies the bounds in Equation (
1.2
).

This matches the upper bound proven in
[
3
, Theorem 1.1]
when
0<\alpha<1.

In
[
13
, Section 7]
, it is shown that, when ranging over the even primitive characters with a large modulus
q,
a positive proportion of central
L
-values are
\gg(\log q)^{-\frac{1}{2}}
(and hence don’t vanish). By using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to compare the first and second mollified moments of the central values, they conclude these results couldn’t simultaneously hold without a positive proportion of large central values. This theme runs throughout the proof of Theorem
1.1
, where we use the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality in the form of the Paley-Zygmund inequality (see
[
17
, Section 2.3.1]
), which states that for a non-negative random variable
Z
with finite second moment and a parameter
0<\delta<1,
one has

(1.7)

\mathbb{P}\left(Z\geq\delta\mathbb{E}\left[Z\right]\right)\geq(1-\delta)^{2}\frac{\mathbb{E}[Z]^{2}}{\mathbb{E}[Z^{2}]}.

Theorem
1.3
is consistent with the Central Limit Theorem for central
L
-values with a large fixed modulus, see for example
[
8
, Theorem 4.2.1]
. As in Corollary
1.2
, this yields lower bounds on all the positive fractional moments of the central Dirichlet
L
-values with fixed modulus.

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
q
be a large prime and
\alpha>0
be any positive real number. Then for
K_{\alpha}
as in Equation (
1.2
),

(1.8)

\frac{1}{\varphi^{+}(q)}\sum_{\chi\text{ even primitive mod }q}\left|L\left(\frac{1}{2},\chi\right)\right|^{2\alpha}\gg K_{\alpha}\left(\log q\right)^{\alpha^{2}}.

1.3.
Organisation of the proof

The proof of Theorem
1.1
relies on the recursive scheme of
[
2
]
, as in
[
1
]
and
[
4
]
.
The method approximates
\log|\zeta|
by the real parts of Dirichlet polynomials using an appropriate mollification on the critical line.
In order to compute large moments of such polynomials, we need to restrict the associated partial sums.
This is explained in detail in Section
2.1
.
To attain a sharp lower bound on the critical line, we use the Paley-Zygmund inequality on mollified
\zeta
.
The bound then follows by computing twisted second and fourth moments, cf. Proposition
2.2
.
This is the most technical part of the paper since to improve the constant it is necessary to work with a tilted probability measure.
Proposition
2.2
is proved in Section
3
.
One ingredient is Proposition
3.5
, which gives precise estimates for moments of
\zeta
twisted not by a Dirichlet polynomial, but rather a polynomial in the real part of a Dirichlet polynomial.
The proof of this auxiliary result is given in Appendix
A
. Appendix
B
gathers results on moments of the random Steinhaus model needed in the proof.

1.4.
Notation

Throughout this paper, we assume that
t
is uniformly distributed in the interval
[0,T]
and write
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{E}
for the corresponding expectation.

We make no effort to specify the absolute constant
C
in Theorem
1.1
, and so for positive functions
f
and
g
we write
f\ll_{\alpha}g,
to denote that for each fixed
\alpha
there is a constant
C(\alpha)
such that for all sufficiently large
T
, we have
f\leq C(\alpha)g,

and moreover there is an absolute constant
k,
such that for all sufficiently large
\alpha,
we have

(1.9)

C(\alpha)\leq\exp\left(k\alpha\right).

We then use
f\asymp_{\alpha}g
to denote
f\ll_{\alpha}g\ll_{\alpha}f.

2.
Method of Proof

2.1.
The partial sums and their restrictions

In this section, we explain the partial sums considered in
[
1
]
,
[
4
]
and
[
3
]
.
In order to control the logarithm of
\zeta
, we define for
\eta>0
the partial sums:

(2.1)

\displaystyle S^{(1)}(\eta,t)=\sum_{\log\log p<\eta}\frac{1}{p^{{\frac{1}{2}+it}}},\qquad S^{(2)}(\eta,t)=\sum_{\log\log p<\eta}\frac{1}{2p^{{1+2it}}}.

Then the combined approximation for
\log\zeta(\frac{1}{2}+it)
, discarding the bounded contribution of powers of primes above squares, is given by:

(2.2)

S(\eta,t)=S^{(1)}(\eta,t)+S^{(2)}(\eta,t).

We expect the size of
S(\eta,t)
to be largely influenced by
S^{(1)}(\eta,t).

The largest primes considered in the above sums will be up to

(2.3)

T^{\ast}=T^{\frac{1}{\mathscr{R}(\alpha^{2}+1)}},

for some large absolute constant
\mathscr{R}
. As will be explained in Section
2.2
, the choice of
T^{\ast}
is dictated by the largest primes whose distribution we can control using the optimal choice for the degrees of tilts
k_{j,\mathbf{u}}
in Equation (
3.8
). We cannot show that, if the growth up to
p\leq T^{\ast}
obeys the linear growth at the log-log scale, then there is a chance of this persisting up to
p\leq T
for a large deviation of size
V
, since we have no control of the primes beyond
T^{\ast}.
The proof instead shows that if the partial sum up to
p\leq T^{\ast}
is significantly larger than
V
, say

(2.4)

{V^{*}}=V+\mathscr{A}(\alpha+1)

for some large constant
\mathscr{A},
then there is a large probability of the ‘full sum’ for
\log\left|\zeta\left(\frac{1}{2}+{\rm i}t\right)\right|
not falling below
V
. Because we have to truncate at
T^{\ast}
rather than
T
, and aim for partial sums of size
{V^{*}}
rather than
V
, we do not expect this to give a sharp value for the constant
K_{\alpha}
.

We divide the intervals of primes
p\leq T^{*}
on the log-log scale at values
n_{j},
and study the partial sums on the intervals

(2.5)

\mathscr{P}_{j}=\{p\text{ prime: }\log\log p\in[n_{j-1},n_{j})\}.

The checkpoints
n_{j}
are defined as follows.
Let
\mathscr{J}
be the largest integer for which
\log_{\mathscr{J}+2}T>1000.

For the scaling factor,
\mathbf{s}=10^{6}
, we set for
1\leq j\leq\mathscr{J}

(2.6)

n_{j}=\log\log T^{\ast}-\mathbf{s}\left(\log_{j+2}T-\log_{\mathscr{J}+2}T\right),\quad T_{j}=e^{e^{n_{j}}}=T^{\frac{1}{\mathscr{R}(\alpha^{2}+1)}\frac{\log^{\mathbf{s}}_{\mathscr{J}+1}}{\log^{\mathbf{s}}_{j+1}}}.

Note that
T_{\mathscr{J}}=T^{*}
.
For the small primes, we let
\mathscr{P}_{0}
be the set of primes with
\log\log p
between

n_{-1}=-100,\quad\text{and}\quad n_{0}=\frac{\log_{4}T}{1000}.

For the steps
n_{j}
for
0\leq j\leq\mathscr{J},
we define

(2.7)

S_{j}(t)={\rm Re}(S(n_{j},t)),

to be the real parts of the partial sums defined in Equation (
2.2
) evaluated at the ends of the intervals.
These partial sums should behave like a random walk for
1\leq j\leq\mathscr{J}
. With this in mind, we will show that for most values of
t
with
\log|\zeta(1/2+{\rm i}t)|>V^{*}
, the partial sums
S_{j}(t)
should lie in a small window around a line in the log-log scale for primes, with approximate gradient

(2.8)

\kappa^{*}=\frac{V^{*}}{n_{\mathscr{J}}}.

More precisely, to control the small primes in
\mathscr{P}_{0}
, we take the mollifier

(2.9)

M_{0}(t)=\sum_{p\mid n\Rightarrow p\in\mathscr{P}_{0}}\frac{\mu(n)}{n^{\frac{1}{2}+{\rm i}t}},

and the restriction

(2.10)

G_{0}=\left\{\left|M_{0}(t)\right|^{2}\in[L_{0},U_{0}]\right\},

with

(2.11)

L_{0}=\exp\left(-2\kappa^{\ast}n_{0}\right)\text{ and }U_{0}=\exp\left(-2\kappa^{\ast}n_{0}+n_{0}^{\frac{2}{3}}\right).

These bounds represent an interval for
|M_{0}(t)|^{2}\approx\exp\left(-2S_{0}(t)\right)
which has large probability given that
S_{\mathscr{J}}(t)>{V^{*}}
.

To get precise estimates, the restrictions for
S_{j}(t)
,
j\geq 1
, must depend on the value of
\left|M_{0}(t)\right|^{2}
.
We thus define the approximate gradient of the remainder of the walk to be

(2.12)

\kappa[z]=\frac{{V^{*}}-z}{n_{\mathscr{J}}-n_{0}},\ z>0.

For
1\leq j\leq\mathscr{J}
, the barrier for the partial sum at
n_{j}
is then prescribed by

(2.13)

\displaystyle L_{j}(z)

\displaystyle=z+\kappa[z](n_{j}-n_{0})-10^{4}\log_{j+2}T,

\displaystyle U_{j}(z)

\displaystyle=z+\kappa[z](n_{j}-n_{0})+10^{4}\log_{j+2}T.

In order to get a discrete set of intervals to sum over, we divide the interval
[L_{0},U_{0}]
into intervals of length
\Delta_{0}^{-1}
where

(2.14)

\Delta_{0}=e^{100\max\{\alpha,1\}n_{0}}.

Given a value of
\left|M_{0}(t)\right|^{2}
lying in
[L_{0},U_{0}]
, we define the event that the partial sum is good all the way up to
T^{\ast}
as

(2.15)

G_{\mathscr{J}}=G_{0}\cap\left\{S_{j}(t)\in[L_{j}\left(\mathfrak{n}\Delta_{0}^{-1}\right),U_{j}\left(\mathfrak{n}\Delta_{0}^{-1}\right)]\quad\forall 1\leq j\leq\mathscr{J},\quad\text{where }\mathfrak{n}=\lfloor-\Delta_{0}\log\left|M_{0}(t)\right|\rfloor\right\}.

We will henceforth omit the implicit dependence of
L_{j}(z)
and
U_{j}(z)
on the starting point
z
, since it is only at
n_{1}
that there could be a significant deviation for large
T
.

For
1\leq j\leq\mathscr{J}
, we set the mollifiers to be:

(2.16)

M_{j}(t)=\sum_{n}\frac{e_{j}(n)}{n^{\frac{1}{2}+it}},

where
e_{j}(n)=\mu(n)
if
p|n\Rightarrow p\in\mathscr{P}_{j}
and
\Omega_{j}(n)\leq 5\alpha^{2}(n_{j}-n_{j-1})^{10^{5}}
, and zero otherwise.
The complete mollifier is then

(2.17)

M(t)=\prod_{j=0}^{\mathscr{J}}M_{j}(t).

The length of this Dirichlet polynomial is
\ll T^{\frac{1}{1000}}
for a suitably large choice
\mathscr{R}
in (
2.3
). It will transpire that for the small primes in
\mathscr{P}_{0},
we don’t have strong Gaussian behaviour and so the twists must be long and the mollifier
M_{0}
cannot be truncated. Conversely, for large
j
, the mollifier must be short to give a short twist, but this is permissible since the prime phases exhibit stronger Gaussian behaviour.
A tight control of the partial sums lying in the barrier corresponds to pointwise bounds on the mollifier.

Lemma 2.1
.

For
t\in G_{\mathscr{J}}
, we have
\left|M(t)\right|^{2}\asymp_{\alpha}e^{-2L_{\mathscr{J}}}.

Proof.

The proof follows from modifying the pointwise bounds on each section
M_{j}(t)
of the mollifier from
[
2
, Lemma 23]
conditional on the good event
G_{\mathscr{J}}.
Taking the product of these pointwise bounds over all the intervals
\mathscr{P}_{j}
for
0\leq j\leq\mathscr{J}
completes the proof.
∎

2.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

We assume
\alpha\geq 1
. An easy adaptation of the method yields the bounds with
K_{\alpha}\asymp 1
as
\alpha\to 0^{+}
.
From here on in, we remove the implicit dependence of
\zeta
and
M
on
t,
to ease the notation.
Restricting to the event
G_{\mathscr{J}}
, we have the following lower bound on
\mathbb{P}(\log|\zeta|>V)
.

(2.18)

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\log|\zeta|>V\right\}\cap G_{\mathscr{J}}\right)=\mathbb{P}(|\zeta|^{2}>e^{2V}|G_{\mathscr{J}})\cdot\mathbb{P}(G_{\mathscr{J}})\geq\mathbb{P}(|\zeta M|^{2}>e^{2(V-L_{\mathscr{J}})}|G_{\mathscr{J}})\cdot\mathbb{P}(G_{\mathscr{J}}).

A lower bound on the conditional probability can be attained by using the Paley-Zygmund inequality (
1.7
):

(2.19)

\mathbb{P}(|\zeta M|^{2}>e^{2(V-L_{\mathscr{J}})}|G_{\mathscr{J}})\geq(1-\delta)^{2}\frac{\mathbb{E}[|\zeta M|^{2}|G_{\mathscr{J}}]}{\mathbb{E}[|\zeta M|^{4}|G_{\mathscr{J}}]}^{2},

where

(2.20)

\delta=\frac{\exp({2V-2L_{\mathscr{J}}})}{\mathbb{E}[|\zeta M|^{2}|G_{\mathscr{J}}]}.

By the definition of
T_{\mathscr{J}}
and of the mollifiers, we expect that the event
G_{\mathscr{J}},
which only depends on the primes
p\leq T_{\mathscr{J}},
will decorrelate from the mollified value
\left|\zeta M\right|^{2}.
Thus we should have that the above is

\displaystyle\left(1-\delta\right)^{2}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\zeta M\right|^{2}\right]^{2}}{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\zeta M\right|^{4}\right]}\asymp_{\alpha}\frac{(\log T_{\mathscr{J}})^{2}}{(\log T)^{2}}\left(1-\delta\right)^{2}\gg_{\alpha}1.

To ensure
\delta<1
, since
L_{\mathscr{J}}={V^{*}}-O(1)
, we need to take
V^{*}
slightly larger than
V
.

It remains to evaluate
\mathbb{P}(G_{\mathscr{J}})
. Here, we can resort to Gaussian approximation results for the distribution of the sum of the random variables at each prime for all the intervals
\mathscr{P}_{j}
by approximating indicator functions by polynomials as in
[
4
]
. The resulting Gaussian approximation results apply asymptotically for the intervals
\mathscr{P}_{j},
for
j\geq 1,
but for the small primes in
\mathscr{P}_{0},
we also pick up the arithmetic factor
a_{\alpha}
from Equation (
1.5
). So we expect:

(2.21)

\mathbb{P}(G_{\mathscr{J}})\asymp_{\alpha}a_{\alpha}\frac{e^{-\frac{{V^{*}}^{2}}{\log\log T_{\mathscr{J}}}}}{\sqrt{\log\log T_{\mathscr{J}}}}\asymp_{\alpha}a_{\alpha}e^{-2\alpha^{2}\log\alpha+(2\mathscr{A}+4\gamma)\alpha^{2}}\cdot\frac{e^{-\frac{V^{2}}{\log\log T}}}{\sqrt{\log\log T}}.

However, we cannot pass directly to the random Steinhaus model using indicator functions
\mathbf{1}(G_{\mathscr{J}})
as twists, and we must instead convert the restrictions into Dirichlet polynomials. The event
G_{\mathscr{J}}
is very rare compared to a typical deviation of the size
\sqrt{\log\log T}
from Selberg’s Central Limit Theorem, thus the necessary Dirichlet polynomials required to detect a large deviation have very high degrees. This means we must truncate the partial sums at relatively small primes to have a permissible twist where mean value theorems apply. In
[
4
]
, this truncation occurred at
T^{1/\alpha^{m}}
for a power
m
much larger than
2
, which leads to a worse constant. However, because we tilt rather than relying solely on indicator functions uniformly close to
1
, we can use shorter polynomials, and so we are able to control primes all the way up to
T_{\mathscr{J}}=T^{\frac{1}{\mathscr{R}(\alpha^{2}+1)}}
.
The following proposition, proved in the next section, gives bounds on
\mathbb{P}(G_{\mathscr{J}})
and the necessary expectations.

Proposition 2.2
.

For the event
G_{\mathscr{J}}
in (
2.15
), we have

(2.22)

\mathbb{P}(G_{\mathscr{J}})\asymp_{\alpha}(e^{-\gamma}\alpha^{2}\log\alpha)^{-\alpha^{2}}\frac{e^{-\frac{V^{*2}}{\log\log T}}}{\sqrt{\log\log T}},

(2.23)

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\zeta M\right|^{2}\mathbf{1}\left(G_{\mathscr{J}}\right)\right]\asymp_{\alpha}(e^{-\gamma}\alpha^{2}\log\alpha)^{-\alpha^{2}}\frac{e^{-\frac{V^{*2}}{\log\log T}}}{\sqrt{\log\log T}}\text{ and }

(2.24)

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\zeta M\right|^{4}\mathbf{1}\left(G_{\mathscr{J}}\right)\right]\ll_{\alpha}(e^{-\gamma}\alpha^{2}\log\alpha)^{-\alpha^{2}}\frac{e^{-\frac{V^{*2}}{\log\log T}}}{\sqrt{\log\log T}}.

Substituting the expectations (
2.22
) and (
2.23
) into Equation (
2.20
), observing that
L_{\mathscr{J}}={V^{*}}-O(1)
, and recalling the convention (
1.9
), we see that

(2.25)

\delta\leq\exp\left(2V-2{V^{*}}+O\left(\alpha\right)\right).

Hence, it suffices to choose
{V^{*}}=V+O(\alpha)
as in (
2.4
) to ensure that
\delta\leq e^{-1}
.
This completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

3.
Proof of Proposition
2.2

The proof of Proposition
2.2
is based on approximating indicator functions for the good event
G_{\mathscr{J}}
by polynomials evaluated at real parts of Dirichlet polynomials, and on twisted moments of these quantities.
We review these tools in Sections
3.1
and
3.2
.

3.1.
Approximation of the events
G_{\mathscr{J}}

We partition the intervals
[L_{j},U_{j}]
into subintervals of length
\Delta_{j}^{-1}
, where

(3.1)

\Delta_{j}=\left\lfloor\log^{10}_{j+1}T\right\rfloor.

We will see from Equation (
3.19
) that
\Delta_{j}
cannot be taken much larger. The key observation is that
\Delta_{j}
does not grow with
\alpha
, enabling us to keep the Dirichlet polynomials for the indicator functions short.

For a lower bound, we need to split the region within the barriers into the union of intervals contained in
[L_{j},U_{j}]
for each
j
, whilst for an upper bound, we need to ensure the union covers
[L_{j},U_{j}]
. We thus define the sets
\mathfrak{I}^{+}
for the upper bound, and
\mathfrak{I}^{-}
for the lower bound. We take
\mathfrak{I}^{\pm}
to be the set of tuples
\left(u_{0},...,u_{\mathscr{J}}\right)
such that:

•

u_{j}\Delta_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
for each
j
,

•

u_{0}\in[L_{0}\mp\Delta_{0}^{-1},U_{0}\pm\Delta_{0}^{-1}]
and

•

-\frac{1}{2}\log u_{0}+\sum^{j}_{m=1}u_{m}\in[L_{j}\mp\Delta_{j}^{-1},U_{j}\pm\Delta_{j}^{-1}]\ \forall 1\leq j\leq\mathscr{J}.

We then need to understand the increments

(3.2)

Y_{j}=S_{j}-S_{j-1},\qquad 0\leq j\leq\mathscr{J}.

We partition on the values of the quantities

(3.3)

Z_{j}=\begin{cases}|M_{0}|^{2}&j=0,\\
Y_{j}&1\leq j\leq\mathscr{J}.\end{cases}

We relate the values
Z_{j}
to the partial sums
S_{j}
as follows:

(3.4)

\displaystyle\cup_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathfrak{I}^{-}}\{Z_{j}\in[u_{j},u_{j}+\Delta_{j}^{-1}]\forall j\}\subset\left\{|M_{0}|^{2}\in[L_{0},U_{0}+\Delta_{0}^{-1}],\ S_{j}\in[L_{j},U_{j}+\Delta_{j}^{-1}],\ 1\leq j\leq\mathscr{J}\right\},

and

(3.5)

\displaystyle\cup_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathfrak{I}^{+}}\{Z_{j}\in[u_{j},u_{j}+\Delta_{j}^{-1}]\forall j\}\supset\{|M_{0}|^{2}\in[L_{0}+\Delta_{0}^{-1},U_{0}],\ S_{j}\in[L_{j}+\Delta_{j}^{-1},U_{j}],\ 1\leq j\leq\mathscr{J}\}.

The events on the left-hand side are essentially disjoint for different tuples
\mathbf{u}\in\mathfrak{I}^{\pm}.
We hence observe that

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathfrak{I}^{-}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\zeta M\right|^{2}\mathbf{1}(Z_{j}\in[u_{j},u_{j}+\Delta_{j}^{-1}]\forall 0\leq j\leq\mathscr{J})\right]

(3.6)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\zeta M\right|^{2}\mathbf{1}(G_{\mathscr{J}})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathfrak{I}^{+}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\zeta M\right|^{2}\mathbf{1}(Z_{j}\in[u_{j},u_{j}+\Delta_{j}^{-1}]\forall 0\leq j\leq\mathscr{J})\right].

In order to bound each expectation it is necessary to convert the indicator functions into Dirichlet polynomials. First, we must tilt to reduce the necessary length of the Dirichlet polynomials to give a twist which can be handled by Proposition
3.5
.

A standard approach in large deviations is to introduce an exponential tilt for values to become typical in the tilted measure.
To improve the constant, we use a polynomial tilt, which is better suited to applying the Mean Value Theorem for Dirichlet polynomials.
The tilt effectively reduces the degree required for the Dirichlet polynomials mimicking the indicator functions.

We begin by calculating the lower bound for each
u\in\mathfrak{I}^{-}.
Instead of calculating

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\zeta M\right|^{2}\mathbf{1}(Z_{j}\in[u_{j},u_{j}+\Delta_{j}^{-1}]\forall 0\leq j\leq\mathscr{J})\right],
we multiply by the partial sums over the intervals
\mathscr{P}_{j},
each raised to a power depending on the values of:

(3.7)

z_{-1}=0,\quad z_{j}=-\frac{1}{2}\log u_{0}+\sum^{j}_{k=1}u_{k},\quad 0\leq j\leq\mathscr{J}.

We consider the tilted expectations
\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\zeta M\right|^{2}\prod^{\mathscr{J}}_{j=0}Y_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}\mathbf{1}(Z_{j}\in[u_{j}+\Delta_{j}^{-a},u_{j}+\Delta_{j}^{-1}])\right],

where

(3.8)

k_{j,\mathbf{u}}=\lfloor\kappa^{*}(z_{j}-z_{j-1})\rfloor,\ \text{ for $0\leq j\leq\mathscr{J}.$}

Thus,
k_{j,\mathbf{u}}=\lfloor\kappa^{*}u_{j}\rfloor
for
1\leq j\leq\mathcal{J}
, and
k_{0,\mathbf{u}}=\lfloor-\frac{\kappa^{*}}{2}\log u_{0}\rfloor
.
If we have values close to a given target interval
\mathfrak{u}\in\mathfrak{I}^{\pm}
then the following key lemma shows we can specify the value of the tilt up to
\asymp_{\alpha}
factors.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathbf{u}\in\mathfrak{I}^{\pm}
and
c_{-1},c_{0},...,c_{\mathscr{J}}
be real numbers satisfying

•

c_{-1}=0

•

\left|c_{j}-z_{j}\right|\leq 100
for each
0\leq j\leq\mathscr{J}.

Then
\prod^{\mathscr{J}}_{j=0}\left(c_{j}-c_{j-1}\right)^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}\asymp_{\alpha}\mathfrak{c}_{\mathbf{u}},
where

(3.9)

\mathfrak{c}_{\mathbf{u}}=\prod^{\mathscr{J}}_{j=0}\left(z_{j}-z_{j-1}\right)^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}.

Proof.

By Taylor expansion of the moments, we see

(3.10)

\prod^{\mathscr{J}}_{j=0}\left(\frac{c_{j}-c_{j-1}}{z_{j}-z_{j-1}}\right)^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}=\exp\left(\sum^{\mathscr{J}}_{j=0}2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}\left(\frac{c_{j}-z_{j}-(c_{j-1}-z_{j-1})}{z_{j}-z_{j-1}}+O\left(\frac{1}{\left(z_{j}-z_{j-1}\right)^{2}}\right)\right)\right).

By Equation (
3.8
), we see the total error term is

(3.11)

\exp\left(\sum^{\mathscr{J}}_{j=1}\frac{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}{(z_{j}-z_{j-1})^{2}}\right)=\exp(O(1)),

and hence we may bound the above quotient as

(3.12)

\exp\left(O(1)+\sum^{\mathscr{J}-1}_{j=0}(c_{j}-z_{j})\left(\frac{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}{z_{j}-z_{j-1}}-\frac{2k_{j+1,\mathbf{u}}}{z_{j+1}-z_{j}}\right)+\frac{2k_{\mathscr{J},\mathbf{u}}\left(c_{\mathscr{J}}-z_{\mathscr{J}}\right)}{z_{\mathscr{J}}-z_{\mathscr{J}-1}}\right).

By Equation (
3.8
) we have
k_{j,\mathbf{u}}=\kappa^{*}(z_{j}-z_{j-1})+O(1)
for
0\leq j\leq\mathscr{J}
and so this is

(3.13)

\exp\left(O(1)+2\kappa^{*}\left(c_{\mathscr{J}}-z_{\mathscr{J}}\right)+\sum^{\mathscr{J}-1}_{j=0}O\left(\frac{1}{z_{j}-z_{j-1}}+\frac{1}{z_{j+1}-z_{j}}\right)\right)=\exp(O(1+\kappa^{*})).

This is acceptable as an
\asymp_{\alpha}
factor, since
\kappa^{*}\to\alpha
as
T\to\infty.

∎

The indicator function for each interval can be approximated by a polynomial due to the following lemma from
[
4
]
.

Lemma 3.2

(Lemma 2 in
[
4
]
)
.

For all
X>100
,
\Delta
sufficiently large and

and

coefficient bounded by

\mathbf{1}\left(x\in\left[0,\Delta^{-1}\right]\right)(1-e^{-\Delta^{a-2}})\leq|\mathscr{D}^{+}(x)|^{2}\leq\mathbf{1}\left(x\in\left[-\Delta^{-a},\Delta^{-1}+\Delta^{-a}\right]\right)+e^{-\Delta^{a-2}}.

and

(3.15)

\mathbf{1}\left(x\in\left[\Delta^{-a},\Delta^{-1}-\Delta^{-a}\right]\right)(1-e^{-\Delta^{a-2}})\leq|\mathscr{D}^{-}(x)|^{2}\leq\mathbf{1}\left(x\in\left[0,\Delta^{-1}\right]\right)+e^{-\Delta^{a-2}}.

The choice of

, provided it is fixed, will not have any qualitative effect in the proof; at most it will effect the constant

in Equation (

throughout.

In
[
4
]
, an accurate upper bound on the twisted moments was not required to give a lower bound on the probability and so the bound from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality sufficed. This is no longer the case when tilting to recover a constant of the shape in Equation (
1.2
). Thus, it is necessary to control the contribution of points far away from the interval

, which translates into a bound on the growth of

when

is large. The following simple lemma gives a sufficient bound on

when

.

Lemma 3.3
.

If
X
and
\Delta
are sufficiently large and

then

(3.16)

\left|\mathscr{D}^{\pm}(y)\right|\leq\left|2y/X\right|^{100X\Delta^{3a}}.

Proof.

Substituting the bounds on the coefficients of

from Lemma

shows that

\displaystyle\left|\mathscr{D}^{\pm}(y)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum^{100X\Delta^{3a}}_{l=0}\frac{(2\pi)^{l}}{l!}\Delta^{2a(l+2)}|y|^{l}=\sum^{100X\Delta^{3a}}_{l=0}\frac{(2\pi)^{l}}{l!}\Delta^{2a(l+2)}|X|^{l}\left|\frac{y}{X}\right|^{l}.

Since

we can bound the last term to write the above as

(3.17)

\leq\left|\frac{y}{X}\right|^{100X\Delta^{3a}}\Delta^{4a}\sum^{100X\Delta^{3a}}_{l=0}\frac{(2\pi)^{l}}{l!}\Delta^{2al}X^{l}\leq\left|\frac{y}{X}\right|^{100X\Delta^{3a}}\Delta^{4a}\exp(2\pi\Delta^{2a}X),

which follows from truncating the exponential Taylor series.
For

∎

3.2.
Twisted moments for real partial sums

In this section, we follow
[
7
]
.
Motivated by the idea of showing that the behaviour of the partial sums over different intervals
\mathscr{P}_{j}
should decorrelate, we define
\alpha
-separable twists. The number of prime factors required in each interval grows with
\alpha
, and so we must truncate at smaller primes to keep the total length of the Dirichlet polynomial short.
The following definition will apply to the product of the polynomials
\mathscr{D}^{\pm}
evaluated at the real partial sums
Y_{j}
.

Definition 3.4
.

We say
Q
is
\alpha
-separable if it is of the form

(3.18)

Q(t)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p|mn\Rightarrow\log\log p\leq n_{\mathscr{J}}\\
\Omega_{0}(mn)\leq\log^{\frac{1}{100}}T\\
\Omega_{j}(mn)\leq\alpha^{2}(n_{j}-n_{j-1})^{10^{4}}\forall 1\leq j\leq\mathscr{J}\end{subarray}}\frac{a(n,m)}{n^{\frac{1}{2}+it}m^{\frac{1}{2}-it}},

where
a(n,m)=\overline{a(m,n)}
for all
n,m
. Here,
\Omega_{l}(n)
denotes the number of prime factors of
n
(with multiplicity) that lie in
\mathscr{P}_{l}
.

The condition
a(n,m)=\overline{a(m,n)}
ensures that
Q(t)
is real whenever
t
is real. Observe moreover that an
\alpha
-separable polynomial can have length at most:

(3.19)

e^{e^{n_{0}}\log^{\frac{1}{100}}T}\cdot\prod^{\mathscr{J}}_{j=1}T_{j}^{\alpha^{2}(n_{j}-n_{j-1})^{10^{4}}}=T^{\sum^{\mathscr{J}}_{j=1}\frac{\alpha^{2}(n_{j}-n_{j-1})^{10^{4}}\log_{\mathscr{J}+1}^{s}T}{\mathscr{R}(\alpha^{2}+1)\log_{j+1}^{s}T}+o(1)}\leq T^{\frac{1}{1000}},

for a suitably large absolute constant
\mathscr{R},
which means we can apply the Mean Value Theorem for Dirichlet Polynomials to evaluate
\mathbb{E}\left[Q^{2}\right].

In
[
7
]
, the distribution of quadratic twists of elliptic curves are considered; there may be much cancellation between terms with the same square-free parts, and so in order to preserve these in relating twisted mollified moments, these terms are grouped together. Inspired by this, we group together terms in
Q^{2}
, and using the relation
(rm)^{it}(rn)^{-it}=m^{it}n^{-it},
we may write

(3.20)

Q^{2}=\sum_{n,m}\frac{b(n,m)}{m^{\frac{1}{2}+it}n^{\frac{1}{2}-it}},

for some coefficients
b(n,m)
such that
b(n,m)=\overline{b(m,n)}
and
b(n,m)=0
if
(n,m)>1
.

Indeed, if we expand out
Q^{2}
, we get

(3.21)

Q^{2}=\sum_{u,v}\frac{P(u,v)}{u^{\frac{1}{2}+it}v^{\frac{1}{2}-it}},\qquad P(u,v)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}n_{2}=u\\
m_{1}m_{2}=v\end{subarray}}a(n_{1},m_{1})a(n_{2},m_{2}).

If we take out any common factors of
u
and
v
in Equation (
3.21
), we then have

(3.22)

b(n,m)=\sum_{r}\frac{P(rn,rm)}{r}\qquad\text{if $(n,m)=1$, and $0$ otherwise.}

Note that
|b(n,m)|\leq b(1,1)
for all
n,m
, thus the integral
\frac{1}{T}\int^{T}_{0}Q(t)^{2}{\rm d}t
is dominated by the diagonal term
b(1,1)
.
Indeed, when
(u,v)=1
we may set
\mathcal{C}(u,v)=\sum_{r}a(ru,rv),

so that

(3.23)

\displaystyle|b(n,m)|=\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(u_{1},v_{1})=(u_{2},v_{2})=1\\
nv_{1}v_{2}=mu_{1}u_{2}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{C}(u_{1},v_{1})\mathcal{C}(u_{2},v_{2})\Big|\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(u_{1},v_{1})=(u_{2},v_{2})=1\\
nv_{1}v_{2}=mu_{1}u_{2}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{2}\left(\left|\mathcal{C}(u_{1},v_{1})\right|^{2}+\left|\mathcal{C}(u_{2},v_{2})\right|^{2}\right).

Since the sum is supported on coprime integers, each pair
(u,v)
appears at most once as
(u_{1},v_{1})
and at most once as
(u_{2},v_{2})
, and so the above sum is at most
b(1,1).

With these considerations, the twisted moment formulae proved in Appendix
A
take the following form.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
Q
be
\alpha
-separable and
M
be as in Equation (
2.17

b(c,g)
as defined in Equation (

(3.24)

\displaystyle\int^{T}_{0}\Big|\zeta\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)M(t)\Big|^{2}Q(t)^{2}{\rm d}t\asymp T\frac{\log T}{\log T_{\mathscr{J}}}b(1,1),

\displaystyle\int^{T}_{0}\Big|\zeta\left(\frac{1}{2}+it\right)M(t)\Big|^{4}Q(t)^{2}{\rm d}t\ll T\left(\frac{\log T}{\log T_{\mathscr{J}}}\right)^{4}b(1,1),

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{Y}_{0}^{2k_{0,\mathbf{u}}}\left(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{-}(\left|\mathscr{M}_{0}\right|^{2}-u_{0})^{2}+\epsilon_{0}\right)\right]\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{Y}_{0}^{2k_{0,\mathbf{u}}}\mathbf{1}(\left|\mathscr{M}_{0}\right|^{2}\in[u_{0},u_{0}+\Delta_{0}^{-1}])\right]\exp\left(O\left(\Delta_{0}^{-\frac{1}{4}}\right)\right).

Proof of Proposition
3.6
.

We follow the proof in Section 3.2 of

Because

pointwise, the polynomials in Lemma

Equation (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{Y}_{0}^{2k_{0,\mathbf{u}}}\left(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{-}(\left|\mathscr{M}_{0}\right|^{2}-u_{0})^{2}+\epsilon_{0}\right)\right]\geq\left(1-e^{-\Delta_{0}^{a-2}}\right)\times

(3.39)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{Y}_{0}^{2k_{0,\mathbf{u}}}\left(\mathbf{1}(\left|\mathscr{M}_{0}\right|^{2}\in[u_{0}+\Delta_{0}^{-a},u_{0}+\Delta_{0}^{-1}-\Delta_{0}^{-a}])+\epsilon_{0}\right)\right],

upon readjusting the error term

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{-}(\left|\mathscr{M}_{0}\right|^{2}-u_{0})^{2}+\epsilon_{0}\right)\mathscr{Y}_{0}^{2k_{0,\mathbf{u}}}\right]=

\displaystyle\exp\left(O\left(\Delta_{0}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{Y}_{0}^{2k_{0,\mathbf{u}}}\right]\right)\right)\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{Y}_{0}^{2k_{0,\mathbf{u}}}\mathbf{1}(\left|\mathscr{M}_{0}\right|^{2}\in[u_{0}+\Delta_{0}^{-1},u_{0}+2\Delta_{0}^{-1}])\right].

we see that

(3.42)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{-}(\left|\mathscr{M}_{0}\right|^{2}-u_{0})^{2}+\epsilon_{0}\right)\mathscr{Y}_{0}^{2k_{0,\mathbf{u}}}\right]=\exp\left(O\left(\Delta_{0}^{-\frac{1}{4}}\right)\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{Y}_{0}^{2k_{0,\mathbf{u}}}\mathbf{1}(\left|\mathscr{M}_{0}\right|^{2}\in[u_{0},u_{0}+\Delta_{0}^{-1}])\right]).

The expectations in (
3.37
) associated to the approximations
\mathscr{D}_{j}^{\pm}
to the indicator functions for partial sums up to
p\leq T_{j}
for
j\geq 1
are considerably more involved, because the restriction on the length of twists for Proposition

means we cannot take long enough Dirichlet polynomials to approximate the indicator functions for all possible values of
\mathscr{Y}_{j}.
Clearly, the distribution of
\cos\left(\theta_{p}\right)
is symmetric about
0
, meaning that, without the restriction of the indicator function, a jump of approximately
\alpha\left(n_{j}-n_{j-1}\right)
for the walk to go from the interval
\left[L_{j-1},U_{j-1}\right]
at
n_{j-1}
to
\left[L_{j},U_{j}\right]
at
n_{j}
is about as likely as a fall of
-\alpha\left(n_{j}-n_{j-1}\right).

Thus the indicator function must be approximated significantly beyond this range, which supports the choice of
X_{j}
in Equation (
3.27
). Since the random variable
\mathscr{Y}_{j}
may not lie in the range
X_{j}
of the target interval, where
\mathscr{D}_{j}^{-}
resembles an indicator function, we must use Markov’s inequality to show these large values are unlikely. This leads to the following proposition.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
(\mathscr{N}_{j},1\leq j\leq\mathscr{J})
be independent Gaussian random variables with mean
0
and variance
\sigma_{j}^{2}
as given in (
3.32
).
We have

(3.43)

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{Y}_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}\left(\mathscr{D}_{j}^{-}(\mathscr{Y}_{j}-u_{j})^{2}+\epsilon_{j}\right)\frac{(\mathscr{Y}_{j}-u_{j})^{2X_{j}}}{X_{j}^{2X_{j}}}\right]\ll\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{N}_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}\mathbf{1}(\mathscr{N}_{j}\in[u_{j},u_{j}+\Delta_{j}^{-1}])\right]}{2^{X_{j}}}.

(3.44)

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{Y}_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}\left(\mathscr{D}_{j}^{-}(\mathscr{Y}_{j}-u_{j})^{2}+\epsilon_{j}\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{N}_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}\mathbf{1}(\mathscr{N}_{j}\in[u_{j},u_{j}+\Delta_{j}^{-1}])\right]\left(1+O\left(\Delta_{j}^{-\frac{1}{4}}\right)\right).

Proof.

We start with the inequality (
3.43
), which is the most involved. The expectation is dominated by the region where
\left|\mathscr{Y}_{j}-u_{j}\right|>X_{j}.
Here, there is little information about
\mathscr{D}_{j}^{-}
; it ceases to approximate the indicator function for an interval for these large values. In Section 7.3 of
[
2
]
these large values are handled via the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. However, this would disrupt the tilting and ultimately prevent us from achieving a good constant in Theorem
1.1
. Instead, we rely on the bounds on the coefficients and use Lemma
3.3
for such large inputs into the polynomials
\mathscr{D}^{\pm}.

The left-hand side of (
3.43
) is

(3.45)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{Y}_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}\left(\mathscr{D}_{j}^{-}(\mathscr{Y}_{j}-u_{j})^{2}+\epsilon_{j}\right)\frac{(\mathscr{Y}_{j}-u_{j})^{2X_{j}}}{X_{j}^{2X_{j}}}\mathbf{1}(|\mathscr{Y}_{j}-u_{j}|<X_{j})\right]+

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{Y}_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}\left(\mathscr{D}_{j}^{-}(\mathscr{Y}_{j}-u_{j})^{2}+\epsilon_{j}\right)\frac{(\mathscr{Y}_{j}-u_{j})^{2X_{j}}}{X_{j}^{2X_{j}}}\mathbf{1}(|\mathscr{Y}_{j}-u_{j}|\geq X_{j})\right].

The first expectation is negligible, and this can be verified when we turn to prove Equation (
3.44
). For the second expectation, we can use Lemma
3.3
to bound it as

(3.46)

\ll\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{Y}_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}\left(\frac{2(\mathscr{Y}_{j}-u_{j})}{X_{j}}\right)^{200X_{j}\Delta_{j}^{3a}}\frac{(\mathscr{Y}_{j}-u_{j})^{2X_{j}}}{X_{j}^{2X_{j}}}\right].

By adding a non-negative quantity, we may bound this as:

(3.47)

\leq\frac{1}{2^{2X_{j}}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{Y}_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}\left(\frac{2(\mathscr{Y}_{j}-u_{j})}{X_{j}}\right)^{2r_{j}}+\mathscr{Y}_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}\left(\frac{2(\mathscr{Y}_{j}+u_{j})}{X_{j}}\right)^{2r_{j}}\right],

where
r_{j}=X_{j}+100X_{j}\Delta_{j}^{3a}
. This expansion now has the benefit of just involving even moments of
\mathscr{Y}_{j}
with positive coefficients. Using Lemma
B.2
, as well as the symmetry in the distribution of
\mathscr{N}_{j}
, we see this is at most:

(3.48)

\frac{1}{2^{2X_{j}}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{N}_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}\left(\frac{2(\mathscr{N}_{j}-u_{j})}{X_{j}}\right)^{2r_{j}}+\mathscr{N}_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}\left(\frac{2(\mathscr{N}_{j}+u_{j})}{X_{j}}\right)^{2r_{j}}\right]=\frac{1}{2^{2X_{j}-1}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{N}_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}\left(\frac{2(\mathscr{N}_{j}+u_{j})}{X_{j}}\right)^{2r_{j}}\right].

We recall the following Gaussian moments:

(3.49)

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{N}_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}+2r_{j}}\right]=\sigma_{j}^{2(k_{j,\mathbf{u}}+r_{j})}\frac{(2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}+2r_{j})!}{2^{k_{j,\mathbf{u}}+2r_{j}}(k_{j,\mathbf{u}}+r_{j})!},\qquad\mathbb{E}[\mathscr{N}_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}]=\sigma_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}\frac{(2k_{j,\mathbf{u}})!}{2^{k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}k_{j,\mathbf{u}}!},

with the variance
\sigma_{j}^{2}
given in Equation (
3.32
).
Minkowski’s inequality for the shifted Gaussian moments now yields:

(3.50)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{2X_{j}-1}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{N}_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}\left(\frac{2(\mathscr{N}_{j}+u_{j})}{X_{j}}\right)^{2r_{j}}\right]\leq\sigma_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}\frac{(2k_{j,\mathbf{u}})!}{2^{k_{j,\mathbf{u}}+2X_{j}-1}k_{j,\mathbf{u}}!}\times\Big(\frac{2\left(\sqrt{2\sigma_{j}^{2}(k_{j,\mathbf{u}}+r_{j})}+u_{j}\right)}{X_{j}}\Big)^{2r_{j}}.

We must compare this to the upper bound in Equation (
3.43
), which is

(3.51)

\frac{\int^{u_{j}+\Delta_{j}^{-1}}_{u_{j}}z^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}e^{-\frac{z^{2}}{2\sigma_{j}^{2}}}{\rm d}z}{2^{X_{j}}\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{j}}.

Since
z\in[u_{j},u_{j}+\Delta_{j}^{-1}]
, the integrand is

(3.52)

\displaystyle u_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}e^{-\frac{u_{j}^{2}}{2\sigma_{j}^{2}}}

\displaystyle\exp\left(2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}\log(1-\frac{u_{j}-z}{u_{j}})+\frac{u_{j}^{2}-z^{2}}{2\sigma_{j}^{2}}\right)=

\displaystyle u_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}e^{-\frac{u_{j}^{2}}{2\sigma_{j}^{2}}}

\displaystyle\exp\left((u_{j}-z)(\frac{u_{j}+z}{2\sigma_{j}^{2}}-\frac{k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}{u_{j}})+O\left(\frac{k_{j,\mathbf{u}}\Delta_{j}^{-2}}{u_{j}^{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}u_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}e^{-\frac{u_{j}^{2}}{2\sigma_{j}^{2}}}.

For the last bound, we used that
\frac{u_{j}+z}{2\sigma_{j}^{2}}=\kappa^{*}+O(1)
by Equation (
2.13
) and the fact that
\frac{k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}{u_{j}}
is also
\kappa^{*}+O(1)
by Equation (
3.8
). Hence, the upper bound in Equation (
3.43
) is greater than:

(3.53)

\frac{u_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}e^{-\frac{u_{j}^{2}}{2\sigma_{j}^{2}}}}{2^{X_{j}+1}\Delta_{j}\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{j}}.

Using Stirling’s approximation, we have

(3.54)

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}\frac{(2k_{j,\mathbf{u}})!}{2^{k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}k_{j,\mathbf{u}}!}

\displaystyle\ll\left(\frac{\sigma_{j}^{2}k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}{e}\right)^{k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}

\displaystyle\ll e^{-\frac{u_{j}^{2}}{2\sigma_{j}^{2}}}u_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}e^{\frac{u_{j}^{2}}{2\sigma_{j}^{2}}-k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}\left(\frac{2\sigma_{j}^{2}k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}{u_{j}^{2}}\right)^{k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}

\displaystyle\ll e^{-\frac{u_{j}^{2}}{2\sigma_{j}^{2}}}u_{j}^{k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}e^{\frac{U_{j}-L_{j-1}}{2\sigma_{j}^{2}}(U_{j}-L_{j-1}-2\kappa^{*}\sigma_{j}^{2})}\left(\frac{u_{j}(L_{j}-U_{j-1})}{2\sigma_{j}^{2}k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}\right)^{-k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}.

Since by Equation (
3.27
),
X_{j}\gg\frac{k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}{\alpha},
we see

(3.55)

k_{j,\mathbf{u}}<\frac{r_{j}\kappa^{*}}{\exp(10^{4})},\quad U_{j}-L_{j-1}<\frac{r_{j}}{\exp(10^{4})},

and so the contribution in Equation (
3.50
) is

(3.56)

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta_{j}\sigma_{j}}{2^{X_{j}}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{N}_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}\mathbf{1}(\mathscr{N}_{j}\in[u_{j},u_{j}+\Delta_{j}^{-1}])\right]

\displaystyle\left(\left(\frac{u_{j}(L_{j}-U_{j-1})}{2\sigma_{j}^{2}k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}\right)^{-\frac{\kappa^{*}}{\exp(10^{4})}}e^{\frac{U_{j}-L_{j-1}-2\kappa^{*}\sigma_{j}^{2}}{\exp(10^{4})\sigma_{j}^{2}}}\Big(\frac{\sqrt{2\sigma_{j}^{2}(k_{j,\mathbf{u}}+r_{j})}+u_{j}}{X_{j}}\Big)^{2}\right)^{r_{j}}.

From Equations (
2.13
) and (
3.8
), we see

(3.57)

\left(\frac{u_{j}(L_{j}-U_{j-1})}{2\sigma_{j}^{2}k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}\right)^{-\frac{\kappa^{*}}{\exp(10^{4})}}\leq\left(1+\frac{10^{5}\log_{j+1}T}{\sigma_{j}^{2}\kappa^{*}}\right)^{\frac{\kappa^{*}}{\exp(10^{4})}}\leq 1.1,

whilst
e^{\frac{U_{j}-L_{j-1}-2\kappa^{*}\sigma_{j}^{2}}{\exp(10^{4})\sigma_{j}^{2}}}\leq 2.

Finally, from the choice of
X_{j}
in Equation (
3.27
), we see that, for
T
sufficiently large,

(3.58)

X_{j}>20\left(\sqrt{2\sigma_{j}^{2}(k_{j,\mathbf{u}}+r_{j})}+(U_{j}-L_{j-1}+1)\right),

so that the last term in (
3.50
) is
\leq 2^{-r_{j}}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{N}_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}\left(\frac{2(\mathscr{N}_{j}+u_{j})}{X_{j}}\right)^{2r_{j}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{N}_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}\mathbf{1}(\mathscr{N}_{j}\in[u_{j},u_{j}+\Delta_{j}^{-1}])\right]2^{-X_{j}-r_{j}},

for
T
sufficiently large, where we used that
\sqrt{n_{j}-n_{j-1}}\leq 2^{n_{j}-n_{j-1}-\frac{1}{2}}.
This completes the proof of (
3.43
).

We now prove Equation (
3.44

The expectation can be split into two cases with

\mathbf{1}(|\mathscr{Y}_{j}-u_{j}|>X_{j})

For the lower bound for

We bound it nevertheless, since it would be needed for an upper bound when dealing with

The first expectation
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{Y}_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}\left(\mathscr{D}_{j}^{-}(\mathscr{Y}_{j}-u_{j})^{2}+\epsilon_{j}\right)\mathbf{1}(|\mathscr{Y}_{j}-u_{j}|\leq X_{j})\right]

(3.59)

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{Y}_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}\mathbf{1}(\mathscr{Y}_{j}\in[u_{j},u_{j}+\Delta_{j}^{-1}])\right]\exp\left(O\left(\Delta_{j}^{-\frac{1}{4}}\right)\right).

The tight bounds on
\mathscr{Y}_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{N}_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}\mathbf{1}(\mathscr{N}_{j}\in[u_{j},u_{j}+\Delta_{j}^{-1}])\right]\left(1+O\left(\Delta_{j}^{-\frac{1}{4}}+\epsilon_{j}\Delta_{j}\log_{j+2}T\right)\right).

Finally, we may bound the expectation with
\mathbf{1}(|\mathscr{Y}_{j}-u_{j}|>X_{j})

(3.61)

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{Y}_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}\left(\mathscr{D}_{j}^{-}(\mathscr{Y}_{j}-u_{j})^{2}+\epsilon_{j}\right)\mathbf{1}(|\mathscr{Y}_{j}-u_{j}|>X_{j})\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{Y}_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}\left(\mathscr{D}_{j}^{-}(\mathscr{Y}_{j}-u_{j})^{2}+\epsilon_{j}\right)\frac{\left(\mathscr{Y}_{j}-u_{j}\right)^{2X_{j}}}{X_{j}^{2X_{j}}}\right],

for which we apply (
3.43
).

We can now bound all expectations in (
3.37
). Using Proposition
3.7
, the main term is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left[\left|\zeta M\right|^{2}Y_{0}^{2k_{0,\mathbf{u}}}\left(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{-}(Y_{0}-u_{0})^{2}+\epsilon_{0}\right)\prod_{1\leq j\leq\mathscr{J}}Y_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}\left(\mathscr{D}^{-}_{j}(Y_{j}-u_{j})^{2}+\epsilon_{j}\right)\mathbf{1}\left(|Y_{j}-u_{j}|\leq X_{j}\right)\right]

(3.62)

\displaystyle\gg\frac{\log T}{\log T_{\mathscr{J}}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{Y}_{0}^{2k_{0,\mathbf{u}}}\mathbf{1}(\left|\mathscr{M}_{0}\right|^{2}\in[u_{0},u_{0}+\Delta_{0}^{-1}])\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\prod^{\mathscr{J}}_{j=1}\mathscr{N}_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}\mathbf{1}(\mathscr{N}_{j}\in[u_{j},u_{j}+\Delta_{j}^{-1}])\right].

Using Lemma
3.1
, we see that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathfrak{c}_{\mathbf{u}}}\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left[\left|\zeta M\right|^{2}Y_{0}^{2k_{0,\mathbf{u}}}\left(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{-}(Y_{0}-u_{0})^{2}+\epsilon_{0}\right)\prod_{1\leq j\leq\mathscr{J}}Y_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}\left(\mathscr{D}^{-}_{j}(Y_{j}-u_{j})^{2}+\epsilon_{j}\right)\mathbf{1}\left(|Y_{j}-u_{j}|\leq X_{j}\right)\right]

(3.63)

\displaystyle\gg_{\alpha}\frac{\log T}{\log T_{\mathscr{J}}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{\mathscr{Y}_{0}}{z_{0}}\right)^{2k_{0,\mathbf{u}}}\mathbf{1}(\left|\mathscr{M}_{0}\right|^{2}\in[u_{0},u_{0}+\Delta_{0}^{-1}])\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\prod^{\mathscr{J}}_{j=1}\mathbf{1}(\mathscr{N}_{j}\in[u_{j},u_{j}+\Delta_{j}^{-1}])\right],

where for convenience we set
z_{0}=-\frac{1}{2}\log{u_{0}}.

This expectation is easiest to handle by performing the calculations over the Gaussian variables
\mathscr{N}_{j}
with
j\geq 1
first. For a fixed

we sum over all combinations
\mathbf{u}=(u_{0},...,u_{\mathscr{J}})
in
\mathfrak{I}^{-}

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathfrak{I}^{-};u_{0}=u^{*}_{0}}\frac{1}{\mathfrak{c}_{u}}\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left[\left|\zeta M\right|^{2}Y_{0}^{2k_{0,\mathbf{u}}}\left(\mathscr{D}_{0}^{-}(Y_{0}-u_{0}^{*})^{2}+\epsilon_{0}\right)\prod_{1\leq j\leq\mathscr{J}}Y_{j}^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}\left(\mathscr{D}^{-}_{j}(Y_{j}-u_{j})^{2}+\epsilon_{j}\right)\mathbf{1}\left(|Y_{j}-u_{j}|\leq X_{j}\right)\right]

(3.64)

\displaystyle\gg\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{\mathscr{Y}_{0}}{z^{*}_{0}}\right)^{2k_{0,\mathbf{u}}}\mathbf{1}(\left|\mathscr{M}_{0}\right|^{2}\in[u_{0}^{*},u_{0}^{*}+\Delta_{0}^{-1}])\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\prod^{\mathscr{J}}_{j=1}\mathbf{1}\left(z^{*}_{0}+\sum^{j}_{l=1}\mathscr{N}_{l}\in[L_{j},U_{j}]\right)\right],

where
z_{0}^{*}=-\frac{1}{2}\log u_{0}
and we have a slight abuse of notation justified since
k_{0,\mathbf{u}}=\left\lceil\kappa^{*}z_{0}^{*}\right\rceil
is independent of
u_{1},...,u_{\mathscr{J}}.

It is an easy exercise using the tilt and barrier to perform the expectation over the normal variables in the second expectation:

(3.65)

\asymp_{\alpha}\mathbb{P}\Big(z^{*}_{0}+\sum^{\mathscr{J}}_{l=1}\mathscr{N}_{l}\in[L_{\mathscr{J}},U_{\mathscr{J}}]\Big),

Since
\sum^{\mathscr{J}}_{l=1}\mathscr{N}_{l}
is a centred Gaussian with variance
\sigma^{2}=\sum_{p\in\cup^{\mathscr{J}}_{j=1}\mathscr{P}_{j}}\frac{1}{2p}+\frac{1}{4p^{2}}
, the above is

\asymp_{\alpha}\int^{\infty}_{L_{\mathscr{J}}}\frac{e^{-\frac{(z-z^{*}_{0})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}}{\sigma}{\rm d}z\asymp_{\alpha}\frac{e^{\frac{-\left(L_{\mathscr{J}}-z^{*}_{0}\right)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}}{\sigma}.

We must now reconcile the two random variables
\mathscr{Y}_{0}
and
\mathscr{M}_{0}
in (
3.64
) to evaluate the expectation.
Firstly, we need to convert the polynomial tilt
\mathscr{Y}_{0}^{2k_{0,\mathbf{u}}}
into an exponential tilt.
Observe that:

(3.66)

-\log\left|\mathscr{M}_{0}\right|=\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{p\in\mathscr{P}_{0}}\frac{\cos k\theta_{p}}{p^{\frac{k}{2}}}\quad\in[\mathscr{Y}_{0}-10,\mathscr{Y}_{0}+10],

by bounding the terms with
k\geq 3
in the sum pointwise. This range is sufficiently small that we may convert the tilt into an exponential by Taylor expansion:

(3.67)

\left(\frac{\mathscr{Y}_{0}}{z^{*}_{0}}\right)^{2k_{0,\mathbf{u}}}\asymp_{\alpha}\exp\left(2k_{0,\mathbf{u}}\left(\frac{\mathscr{Y}_{0}}{z^{*}_{0}}-1\right)\right).

Combining this with Equations (
3.65
) and (
3.3
) gives that the right-hand side of the bound in Equation (
3.64
) is

(3.68)

\frac{e^{\frac{-\left(L_{\mathscr{J}}-z^{*}_{0}\right)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}}{\sigma}\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp\Big(2k_{0,\mathbf{u}}\left(\frac{\mathscr{Y}_{0}}{z^{*}_{0}}-1\right)\Big)\mathbf{1}\left(\left|\mathscr{M}_{0}\right|^{2}\in\left[e^{-2z^{*}_{0}},e^{-2z^{*}_{0}}+\Delta_{0}^{-1}\right]\right)\Big].

Using this, Equation (
3.66
) and the definition of
L_{\mathscr{J}}
, as well as the restrictions of
|\mathscr{M}_{0}|^{2}
given by the indicator function, the above simplifies to

(3.69)

\displaystyle\asymp_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\exp\left(-2k_{0,\mathbf{u}}\right)\frac{e^{-\frac{\left(V^{*}-z^{*}_{0}\right)^{2}}{n_{\mathscr{J}}-n_{0}}}}{\sqrt{n_{\mathscr{J}}-n_{0}}}\cdot\mathbb{E}\big[\exp\left(-2\kappa^{\ast}\log\left|\mathscr{M}_{0}\right|\right)\mathbf{1}(\left|\mathscr{M}_{0}\right|^{2}\in[e^{-2z^{*}_{0}},e^{-2z^{*}_{0}}+\Delta_{0}^{-1}])\big],

To evaluate the expectation, note that the random variables
(\theta_{p},{p\text{ prime}})
remain independent in the tilted measure:

\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}\left[\bullet\right]=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\bullet\exp\left(-2\kappa^{\ast}\log\left|\mathscr{M}_{0}\right|\right)\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-2\kappa^{\ast}\log\left|\mathscr{M}_{0}\right|\right)\right]},

with the associated tilted probability of an event
A
defined as
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}(A)=\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbf{1}(A)\right].

It is then more convenient to work with the variable

(3.70)

R_{0}=\log\left|\mathscr{M}_{0}\right|+\kappa^{\ast}n_{0}.

The expectation becomes

(3.71)

e^{2\kappa^{\ast 2}n_{0}}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-2\kappa^{\ast}R_{0}\right)\right]\cdot\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}\left(R_{0}\in\left[\kappa^{\ast}n_{0}-z^{*}_{0},\kappa^{*}n_{0}-z^{*}_{0}+\delta_{0}\right]\right),

where the increments

\delta_{0}=\delta_{0}(z^{*}_{0},\Delta_{0})=\log\left(1+\Delta_{0}^{-1}e^{2z^{*}_{0}}\right)

vary mildly due to the mapping in Equation (
3.70
).
Using Lemma 12 from
[
4
]
, we have

(3.72)

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-2\kappa^{\ast}R_{0}\right)\right]\asymp_{\alpha}a_{\alpha}e^{\gamma\alpha^{2}}e^{-\kappa^{\ast 2}n_{0}}.

Since we have tilted to favour the range, it is easy to approximate the
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}
-probability in (
3.71
) at each
z^{*}_{0}
following
[

Remark
.

We believe that the truncation
T^{\ast}=T^{\frac{1}{\mathscr{R}(\alpha^{2}+1)}}
is the best possible with our techniques, and so the constant
K_{\alpha}
cannot be improved beyond the shape in Equation (
1.2
). To see this, write
P_{j}(S_{j}-S_{j-1})
for any possible Dirichlet polynomial representing a tilt and the restriction in the interval
\mathscr{P}_{j}
. In our proof, we have taken the tilt to depend on the interval
\mathbf{u}\in\mathfrak{I}^{\pm}.
Here,
P_{j}(S_{j}-S_{j-1})=(S_{j}-S_{j-1})^{2k_{j,\mathbf{u}}}D^{\pm}_{j}((S_{j}-S_{j-1})-u_{j})^{2},
which favours jumps consistent with the partial sum over the interval
\mathscr{P}_{j}
lying in the relevant boundaries.
We now argue that we must have

(3.74)

\deg P_{j}\gg\alpha^{2}(n_{j}-n_{j-1}).

We now consider the contribution to the expectation
\mathbb{E}\left[\left|P_{j}(S_{j}-S_{j-1})\right|\right]
from different ranges of values for
S_{j}-S_{j-1}
. To be a good approximation to the probability we remain within the barrier, a large contribution must come from when
S_{j}-S_{j-1}\approxeq u_{j}
. However, this is less likely than smaller values of
S_{j}-S_{j-1}
due to Gaussian decay, and so
P_{j}
must have a high degree to make the interval dominate the expectation.

More concretely, for
x\approx u_{j}
in the target increment for the interval
\mathscr{P}_{j}
and
y\approx\frac{\alpha(n_{j}-n_{j-1})}{2}
(say), we should have

(3.75)

\frac{e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{n_{j}-n_{j-1}}}}{\sqrt{n_{j}-n_{j-1}}}\int^{x+\Delta_{j}^{-1}}_{x}P_{j}(z){\rm d}z\gg\frac{e^{-\frac{y^{2}}{n_{j}-n_{j-1}}}}{\sqrt{n_{j}-n_{j-1}}}\int^{y+\Delta_{j}^{-1}}_{y}P_{j}(z){\rm d}z.

The ratio of the Gaussian factors is approximately
e^{-3\alpha^{2}(n_{j}-n_{j-1})/4}.

We use the basis of shifted Chebyshev polynomials
\left\{T_{n}\left(\frac{z^{\prime}-\alpha(n_{j}-n_{j-1})/2}{\alpha(n_{j}-n_{j-1})/100}\right)\right\}
for the polynomial
\mathfrak{F}_{j}(z^{\prime})=\int^{z^{\prime}+\Delta_{j}^{-1}}_{z^{\prime}}P_{j}(z){\rm d}z
and the (shifted) orthogonality relations of these Chebyshev polynomials to relate the coefficients in this basis to

\sup_{y\approx\frac{\alpha(n_{j}-n_{j-1})}{2}}\mathfrak{F}_{j}(y).

Using a pointwise bound for
\mathfrak{F}_{j}(x)
when
x
is close to the target jump, one can thus show that the minimum degree of any such polynomial used in the twist must obey (
3.74
).

Appendix B
Probabilities in the Steinhaus model

We first compare the moments of
A_{p,1}=\cos(\theta_{p})p^{-1/2}
and
A_{p,2}=\cos(2\theta_{p})p^{-1}/2
to those of the Gaussian variable
N_{p,i}=\nu_{i}p^{-\frac{i}{2}}Z_{p,i}
, where the variables
Z_{p,i}
are IID standard Gaussian variables for
i=1,2
with
\nu_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
and
\nu_{2}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}
.

Lemma B.1
.

For
i\in\{1,2\}
and any
k\in\mathbb{N},

(B.1)

\mathbb{E}[A_{p,i}^{2k-1}]=\mathbb{E}[N_{p,i}^{2k-1}]=0,

while for the even moments, we have the bound

(B.2)

\mathbb{E}[A_{p,i}^{2k}]\leq\mathbb{E}[N_{p,i}^{2k}].

Proof of Lemma
B.1
.

From the odd symmetries of the distributions, Equation (
B.1
) is immediate, while for the even moments in (
B.2
), we may assume
k\geq 2,
since we have constructed the moments to be equal for
k=1.
Then we have the
2k
-th moments:

(B.3)

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\nu_{i}^{2k}\cos^{2k}(i\theta_{p})}{p^{ik}}\right]=\frac{\nu_{i}^{2k}{2k\choose k}}{2^{k}p^{ik}}.

But we recall the Gaussian compute the moments of
N_{p}
precisely, to attain:

(B.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[N_{p,i}^{2k}]

\displaystyle=\frac{\nu_{i}^{2k}(2k)!}{2^{k}p^{ik}k!}.

Comparing Equations (
B.3
) and (
B.4
) completes the proof of inequality (
B.2
).∎

These bounds translate into bounds on the sum
\mathscr{Y}_{j}
from (
3.2
).

Lemma B.2
.

Let
1\leq j\leq\mathscr{J},

i=1,2,

\mathscr{S}_{j,i}=\sum_{p\in\mathscr{P_{j}}}A_{p,i}
and
\mathfrak{N}_{j,i}=\sum_{p\in\mathscr{P}_{j}}N_{p,i}
.
Then for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

(B.5)

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{S}_{j,i}^{2k-1}\right]=\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{N}_{j,i}^{2k-1}]=0,

while for the even moments, we have

(B.6)

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{S}_{j,i}^{2k}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{N}_{j,i}^{2k}].

Hence, if
k\ll(n_{j}-n_{j-1})T_{j-1}^{\frac{1}{100}},
then

(B.7)

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{Y}_{j}^{2k}\right]\ll\mathbb{E}[\mathscr{N}_{j}^{2k}]

for the centred Gaussian
\mathscr{N}_{j}
with variance
\sigma_{j}^{2}
defined in Equation (
3.32
)

Proof of Lemma
B.2
.

Again by the odd symmetries of the distributions we see Equation (
B.5
) is immediate, while for (
B.6
), we expand out the power to write for
\mathbf{n}=(n_{p},p\in\mathscr{P}_{j})

(B.8)

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathscr{S}_{j,i}^{2k}\right]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{p}\geq 0,\forall p\in\mathscr{P}_{j}\\
\sum_{p\in\mathscr{P}_{j}}n_{p}=2k\end{subarray}}{2k\choose\mathbf{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{p\in\mathscr{P}_{j}}A_{p,i}^{n_{p}}\right].

The expectation splits by independence, and we use Equation (
B.1
) to show the expectation vanishes unless all the
n_{p}
are even.
Hence we may write the sum as

(B.9)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}2|n_{p},\forall p\in\mathscr{P}_{j}\\
\sum_{p\in\mathscr{P}_{j}}n_{p}=2k\end{subarray}}{2k\choose\mathbf{n}}\prod_{p\in\mathscr{P}_{j}}\mathbb{E}\left[A_{p,i}^{n_{p}}\right].

Using Equation (
B.2

(B.10)

\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}2|n_{p},\forall p\in\mathscr{P}_{j}\\

Finally, we use Equation (
B.1
) again to show the other choices with some odd values of
n_{p}
don’t contribute to the sum, so this is

(B.11)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{p}\geq 0,\forall p\in\mathscr{P}_{j}\\
\sum_{p\in\mathscr{P}_{j}}n_{p}=2k\end{subarray}}{2k\choose\mathbf{n}}\prod_{p\in\mathscr{P}_{j}}\mathbb{E}\left[N_{p,i}^{n_{p}}\right]=\mathbb{E}[\mathfrak{N}_{j,i}^{2k}].

∎
