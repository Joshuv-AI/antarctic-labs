Machine learning-powered audio-omics processing method as an auxiliary diagnostic approach for advanced nasopharyngeal carcinoma
Authors: Ting You, Fangna Huan, Zhenzhen Luo, Yifan Ma, Zhihua Liu, Songjiang Wu, Dan Zhang, Liuyun Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is located in the nasopharyngeal mucosa and exhibits a strong correlation with Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) [1]. Serological detection of EBV antibodies is often used in NPC screening [2–4]. Notably, NPC is common in China and Southeast Asia. However, even in these NPC-endemic areas, the positive predictive value of EBV-based screening strategies remains suboptimal [2]. Although screening with EBV-specific antibodies or EBV DNA can increase diagnosis rate of NPC about 50%, more than 95% of EBV positive people were need to unnecessary clinical examinations and follow-ups, which led to long-term anxiety and low adherence of the participants [5–8]. Moreover, among the patients clinically diagnosed with NPC, only about half of them test positive for the EBV-specific antibodies, highlighting the limitations of current serological screening approaches [2].

NPC is a common head and neck tumor with distinct clinical management compared to other types of cancer [9]. Radiotherapy can precisely target the tumor while minimizing damage to adjacent healthy tissues, making it the preferred approach for treating NPC [10]. Tailored radiotherapy target is crucial to therapeutic effect. So artificial intelligence, including radiomics and deep learning, has exhibited considerable efficacy in whole clinical management for NPC [11]. Leveraging clinical information, imaging data, and gene expression profiles of NPC patients, ML models construct sophisticated prediction models to accurately assess their responses to treatment and the risks of disease recurrence and metastasis. Especially radiomics, a technique that extracts and analyzes quantitative features from tumor imaging, has been a pivotal approach within the ML framework [12]. By mining imaging-based features associated with prognosis, radiomics provides robust scientific evidence for clinicians to formulate subsequent treatment strategies, thereby facilitating precision and personalized medicine in NPC management [13]. Long et al. developed and assessed prognostic models utilizing a traditional Cox proportional hazards model alongside six ML algorithms [14]. Similarly, Zuo et al. reported an ML-derived prognostic signature for progression-free survival in non-metastatic NPC, highlighting the potential of integrated clinical variables for individualised risk evaluation [15]. In addition, Zeng et al. established a multicentre prognostic nomogram for synchronous metastatic NPC, providing complementary evidence that data-driven prognostic modelling may improve clinical stratification [16]. Collectively, these studies support the clinical relevance of ML in NPC management. Existing work has primarily focused on prognostic prediction and treatment decision support. Audio-omics remains an untouched frontier in NPC. No existing literature has delved into using acoustic features or voice analyses for NPC diagnosis, prognosis, or treatment assessment rather. This void is striking, given that vocal alterations might mirror physiological changes caused by NPC. As audio-omics gains traction in other medical domains, exploring its potential in NPC could pioneer non-invasive diagnostic methods and reshape patient management strategies.

Approximately 70% of NPC patients having intermediate to advanced disease at the time of initial diagnosis [17]. Because the anatomical location of NPC, which is adjacent to crucial structures involved in vocal function, often leads to significant voice changes [18]. As the tumor grows and compresses surrounding tissues, including the pharyngeal muscles and nerves regulating vocal production, patients may experience symptoms such as hoarseness, vocal fatigue, and altered pitch. These voice alterations, which are early and often overlooked manifestations of NPC, present a unique opportunity for developing novel screening strategies. By leveraging advanced machine learning-based audio-omics technologies to analyze these acoustic changes, a non-invasive, accessible, and cost-effective screening method could potentially be established.

Herein, we developed a machine learning-powered audio-omics processing method for screening advanced NPC [19]. We collected the audio files of 359 advanced NPC patients and 304 normal controls in our hospital from 2022 to 2025. Then, we used machine-learning powered Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma Screening (ML-NPCS) system contained three steps: speech acquisition, acoustic features extraction, and classification decision-making for screening NPC diagnosis. The results showed that the ML-NPCS system screening accuracy of NPC reaches 84.2%, which paves a practical and efficient way for NPC screening.

Materials and methods

Acquisition of NPC patients and healthy subjects

We collected audio data from 359 advanced NPC patients and 304 healthy controls at our hospital between 2022 and 2025. All participants provided written informed consent after being fully informed of the study objectives and procedures. For NPC patients, inclusion criteria were: histologically confirmed advanced NPC (AJCC 8th edition stage III-IV), age between 18 and 70 years, and ability to provide clear speech samples. Exclusion criteria included history of prior head and neck surgery/radiation, cognitive impairment affecting speech, and coexisting severe respiratory diseases. For healthy controls, inclusion criteria were: no history of cancer, age-matched to patients (± 5 years), and no history of major speech or hearing disorders.

A total of 663 participants were included, comprising 359 patients with advanced nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) and 304 age- and gender-matched healthy controls. Pearson’s Chi-square test was applied for between-group comparisons of categorical confounding variables (gender, hypertension, smoking history), while independent samples t-test was used for the continuous variable (age), with a two-sided α level of 0.05 set for statistical significance. Using a stratified random allocation, 530 participants (287 patients with advanced NPC and 243 healthy controls) were allocated to model training, and 133 participants (72 patients with advanced NPC and 61 healthy controls) were reserved for independent testing. ML-NPCS system for auxiliary discrimination of advanced NPC

The ML-NPCS system is composed of three steps: speech acquisition, acoustic features extraction, and classification decision-making for auxiliary discrimination of advanced NPC.

Speech acquisition

The initial stage of voice signal acquisition involves the deployment of portable recording devices to collect samples of the voice recordings from participants. In order to optimize the voice signal collection process and maintain consistency across data samples, subjects are required to sequentially rephrase the integers one through ten in Mandarin. The available analytical recordings were mono WAV files sampled at 16 kHz with 16-bit resolution.

Acoustic features extraction

Subsequent to the signal acquisition, the process of feature extraction is undertaken for each individual speech [20]. In the time-domain analysis, the root mean square (RMS) energy of each frame signal is calculated as a quantitative measure of the variation in laryngeal airflow drive. Concurrently, a statistical analysis of the zero-crossing rate (ZCR) is conducted to characterize alterations in the alternating pattern of voiced and unvoiced sounds. A shift in the ZCR distribution can be interpreted as a reduction in the proportion of voiced sound segments, potentially attributable to vocal cord vibration disorders. Utilizing a Mel frequency-domain analytical paradigm, a series of frequency-domain features are extracted. This process entails the implementation of a nonlinear transformation, which is applied to the output of a fast Fourier transform (FFT) and further processed via a 40-channel Mel filter bank. The extracted spectral features include the spectral centroid, which exhibits a potential correlation with the dimensions of the nasal resonance cavity, manifesting as a shift toward lower frequencies in the perceived timbre. Furthermore, the spectral roll-off offers a quantitative measure of high-frequency harmonic absorption attributable to space-occupying lesions. Spectral bandwidth provides a characterization of formant dispersion. Subsequently, thirteen Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCCs) are derived through the application of a discrete cosine transform to the log-frequency cepstral spectrum. The lower-order Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCCs 1–4) are primarily representative of the gross morphological characteristics of the vocal tract. Conversely, the higher-order coefficients (MFCCs 5–13) exhibit a greater sensitivity to subtle variations in vocal tract configuration, potentially reflecting anatomical structural deformations such as incomplete closure of the soft palate. The spectral contrast characteristics are determined by quantifying the energy differential between the harmonic peaks within each subband and the ambient noise substrate. Analysis of this metric provides a means of characterizing the enhancement of glottic turbulent noise attributable to the presence of a tumor. The quantification of beat rate (Beats Per Minute, BPM) characteristics, serving as an indicator of aberrant synchronization in pronunciation rhythms, is accomplished through the application of spectral flux analysis and dynamic programming optimization techniques. A frame-by-frame analysis is conducted to determine the fundamental frequency and the associated distribution of harmonic energy. Subsequently, the average pitch trajectory characteristics are calculated based on these measurements, thereby providing a quantitative assessment of instantaneous disturbances in vocal cord vibration frequency. Through the collaborative application of multi-scale signal characterization techniques, a foundational framework of acoustic biomarkers, specifically tailored for the diagnosis of nasopharyngeal carcinoma, is established, thereby providing the essential underpinning for the development of the ML-NPCS system.

Classification decision-making

In consideration of the constraints imposed by the limited sample size, a Support Vector Machine (SVM) algorithm is selected for the implementation of the ML-NPCS system. In order to mitigate the potential confounding effects of disparate feature dimensions on the performance of the classifier, the extracted multi-scale signal features are subjected to Z-score normalization and standardization preprocessing. The inherent nonlinear separability of the high-dimensional feature space necessitates the application of a kernel function to facilitate the classification process. A radial basis kernel function is therefore employed to map the data into a higher-dimensional space, within which an optimal hyperplane is determined by maximizing the classification margin, thereby achieving discrimination between nasopharyngeal carcinoma patients and healthy controls. In the verified implementation, standardisation parameters were fitted using the training data only and subsequently applied to the independent test set.

Statistical analysis

Test-set performance was summarised using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1-score, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC AUC). 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for binomial proportions were calculated using the Wilson method. ROC AUC was calculated from the continuous SVM decision scores.

Results

Positive rate of EBV in the enrolled advanced NPC patients

The result showed that only 50% cases were positive for EBV in the enrolled advanced NPC patients in our hospital from 2022 to 2025. This proportion is descriptive and is not a measure of diagnostic accuracy.

NPC screening accuracy of ML-NPCS system

This study developed a machine learning powered auxiliary diagnostic model for NPC patients. The dataset comprised acoustic features from 663 participants, including 359 patients with advanced NPC and 304 healthy controls. Pearson’s Chi-square test was applied for between-group comparisons of categorical confounding variables (gender, hypertension, smoking history), while independent samples t-test was used for the continuous variable (age), with a two-sided α level of 0.05 set for statistical significance. All pre-specified confounding variables showed no statistically significant between-group differences (all P > 0.05), indicating excellent baseline balance and high comparability between the advanced NPC group and healthy controls, which effectively controlled the risk of confounding bias for subsequent etiological and prognostic analyses. The stratified random allocation assigned 530 participants (287 patients with advanced NPC and 243 healthy controls) to model training and 133 participants (72 patients with advanced NPC and 61 healthy controls) to independent testing. The final model achieved an overall accuracy of 84.2% (95% CI, 77.1%-89.4%) in the independent test set. Analysis of the confusion matrix, as shown in Fig. 1, revealed that the model yielded 64 true-positive, 8 false-negative, 48 true-negative, and 13 false-positive classifications, corresponding to a sensitivity of 88.9% (95% CI, 79.6%-94.3%), a specificity of 78.7% (95% CI, 66.9%-87.1%), a precision of 83.1% (95% CI, 73.2%-89.9%), an F1-score of 85.9%, and a ROC AUC of 88.4%.

Confusion matrix for the ML-NPCS model in the independent test set (n = 133; 72 patients with advanced NPC and 61 healthy controls): true positive = 64, false negative = 8, true negative = 48, and false positive = 13

To elucidate the model’s decision-making mechanism, as illuminated as Fig. 2, the distribution of samples in a two-dimensional feature space was visualized by analyzing the classification boundaries of the RBF kernel function. Sensitivity analysis of hyperparameters revealed that higher gamma values (gamma) refined decision boundaries but risked overfitting, while increasing the regularization coefficient (C) intensified penalties for misclassified samples. Based on visualization results, the optimal parameter combination (C = 50, gamma = 0.1) was selected to balance high sensitivity with improved generalization performance.

Two-dimensional feature visualization based on RBF kernel function with different parameters

To further elucidate the model’s decision-making process, Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) method was employed to quantify feature importance, with results visualized in Fig. 3. Mean absolute SHAP values quantify feature contributions on the decision-function scale. Larger values indicate greater relative influence on model prediction, but they do not represent physiological effect sizes or causal mechanisms. The analysis revealed systematic differences in acoustic features between NPC patients and healthy individuals. Spectral_contrast (SHAP value: 0.036) and MFCC_11 (0.034) emerged as the most discriminative features, the contributions of MFCC_5 (0.033) and BPM (0.032) suggest abnormalities in formant structures and slowed speech rhythm in patients. Spectral_bandwidth (0.031) and Spectral_centroid (0.031) demonstrated meaningful contributions, indicating deviations in the spectral energy distribution range (bandwidth) and its central frequency (centroid) in patient speech. In contrast, lower-order MFCC features (e.g., MFCC_1, MFCC_2; 0.031 − 0.029) exhibited slightly lower contributions than anticipated.

Discussion

The utility of EBV as a screening tool for NPC is inherently limited by the biological complexity of viral infection and disease progression [21]. Not all NPC patients test positive for EBV infection, highlighting that the virus is not a universal precursor to the malignancy [22]. Conversely, EBV infection is far from exclusive to NPC, it’s a common pathogen associated with a spectrum of health conditions, including infectious mononucleosis, lymphoproliferative disorders, and other epithelial malignancies [23–25]. While EBV markers may contribute to risk assessment in certain contexts, their inability to distinguish between infected individuals who will develop NPC and those who will remain unaffected or develop other diseases limits their utility as a standalone screening modality. Therefore, complementary non-invasive diagnostic approaches warrant investigation.

This study developed a ML-driven screening model for NPC patients based on acoustic features, demonstrating interpretability in both classification performance and feature contribution. A support vector machine (SVM) method was employed for feature analysis and classification [26]. Analysis of the confusion matrix, as shown in Fig. 1, revealed that the model exhibited sensitivity of 88.9% (95% CI, 79.6%-94.3%), suggesting potential discrimination of disease-associated acoustic patterns. However, the specificity for healthy samples was 78.7% (95% CI, 66.9%-87.1%), and should be interpreted in light of the corresponding uncertainty estimate. For instance, factors such as articulatory habits, age-related variations, or environmental noise in healthy individuals may introduce spectral diversity, leading to model confusion in distinguishing healthy controls from patients. Future work should expand the sample size, incorporate demographically diverse healthy samples, and integrate adversarial training or attention mechanisms to mitigate interference from non-pathological features, thereby enhancing classification robustness.

To elucidate the model’s decision-making mechanism, the distribution of samples in a two-dimensional feature space was visualized by analyzing the classification boundaries of the RBF kernel function, highlights the potential of acoustic feature-based ML models in NPC screening while underscoring the need for larger, diversified datasets and advanced algorithmic strategies to address specificity challenges. To further elucidate the model’s decision-making process, SHAP method was employed to quantify feature importance, as showed in Fig. 3. Spectral_contrast (SHAP value: 0.036) and MFCC_11 (0.034) emerged as the most discriminative features, highlighting the significance of energy contrast between spectral subbands (e.g., high- vs. low-frequency distribution disparities) and higher-order Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCCs, reflecting vocal tract resonance characteristics) in NPC detection. Notably, the elevated contributions of MFCC_5 (0.033) and BPM (0.032) suggest abnormalities in formant structures and slowed speech rhythm in patients, potentially linked to pathophysiological mechanisms. For instance, the prominence of BPM (beats per minute) may reflect irregular speech pacing caused by respiratory dysfunction in cancer patients. Additionally, Spectral_bandwidth (0.031) and Spectral_centroid (0.031) demonstrated meaningful contributions, indicating deviations in the spectral energy distribution range (bandwidth) and its central frequency (centroid) in patient speech. These differences likely originate from altered vocal fold vibration patterns or airflow control impairments. In contrast, lower-order MFCC features (e.g., MFCC_1, MFCC_2; 0.031 − 0.029) exhibited slightly lower contributions than anticipated, implying that higher-order spectral details (e.g., MFCC_11) are more critical for distinguishing pathological speech. Finally, the significance of Pitch_mean (0.030) suggests that shifts in fundamental frequency may serve as potential biomarkers, potentially associated with abnormal vocal cord tension.

These findings validate the clinical relevance of acoustic features in NPC detection and provide quantitative insights into underlying pathological mechanisms. Future studies should integrate laryngoscopy or respiratory function tests to correlate these acoustic markers with physiological indicators, thereby enhancing the clinical credibility of the model. This multi-modal validation could bridge computational findings with clinical observations, fostering more reliable ML-driven diagnostic tools.

This study is also limited by the advanced-stage-only cohort, single-center internal test set, and use of a Mandarin recitation task. Although the use of a fixed Mandarin recitation task reduced variability in spoken content, residual differences in recording duration, loudness, and speaking rate may have influenced the extracted acoustic features. These factors may co-vary with disease status or individual speaking habits and therefore cannot be excluded as potential confounders in the present study. Future studies should apply standardized recording duration, calibrated loudness acquisition, amplitude normalization, speaking-rate adjustment or covariate modelling, and sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of acoustic biomarkers after controlling for these variables. Prospective multicenter validation including early-stage disease, symptomatic controls, standardized recording, comparator models, and continuous-score ROC analysis is required. Furthermore, the SVM classifier demonstrated promising discrimination in the independent test set, but the present study evaluated only one machine learning modelling approach. Therefore, the reported performance should be interpreted as evidence of the potential discriminative value of voice-derived acoustic features rather than evidence that SVM is superior to alternative classifiers. Future studies should perform prespecified head-to-head comparisons of SVM with other machine learning models using identical preprocessing, data partitioning, hyperparameter optimization, and external validation procedures.

Conclusion

In this study, a ML-based acoustic model was developed for preliminary auxiliary discrimination of advanced NPC from healthy controls. In the independent internal test set, ML-NPCS achieved an accuracy of 84.2%, a sensitivity of 88.9%, and a specificity of 78.7%. Further prospective external validation is required before any population-level screening application can be considered.
