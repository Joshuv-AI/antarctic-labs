Digital exclusion, functional limitations transitions, and life expectancy: a multinational longitudinal study
Authors: Qian Liu, Fan Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study examined associations between digital exclusion and functional limitations transitions, and quantified impacts on life expectancy across diverse international contexts.MethodsWe analyzed 124,332 adults aged ≥50 years across 23 countries using five harmonized longitudinal aging cohorts. Functional limitations were classified into functional independence, mild and severe functional limitations, with death as an absorbing state. Continuous-time multi-state Markov models examined associations between digital exclusion and transition patterns, while life expectancy was calculated through numerical integration of model parameters.ResultsDigital exclusion demonstrated significant associations with adverse functional limitations transitions. Progression to mild limitations in three countries and Europe showed hazard ratios (HRs) of 1.47 (95% CI: 1.23-1.75) to 2.56 (95% CI: 1.38-4.75), and to severe limitations in four countries (excluding Europe) showed HRs of 1.24 (95% CI: 1.06-1.45) to 1.81 (95% CI: 1.15-2.86), while recovery from severe limitations was impaired in England, the United States and Europe (HR: 0.55 [95% CI: 0.48-0.62] to 0.74 [95% CI: 0.62-0.88]). Digital exclusion was associated with reductions in both functional life expectancy (3.72 years in Mexico to 14.27 years in China at age 50) and total life expectancy, with reductions largest at younger ages yet persisting through age 90; conversely, functional limitations duration increased in some countries.ConclusionsDigital exclusion is associated with functional limitations and reduced life expectancy, with associations varying across national contexts and most pronounced among younger age groups. Findings underscore the need to integrate digital inclusion into healthy aging policies while maintaining non-digital healthcare pathways.Keywords: Digital exclusion, Functional limitations, Multi-state Markov model, Life expectancy, Healthy aging

Introduction

Digital technology's rapid expansion has fundamentally transformed healthcare delivery and daily living activities as digital platforms become increasingly integrated into routine health management. Given accelerating global demographic transitions toward aging populations, examining the relationship between digital exclusion and functional limitations transitions has become a critical research priority. Digital exclusion refers to the inequity in both access to and the ability to utilize information and communications technologies, including the internet (Seifert, 2020), disproportionately affects older adults who constitute the largest proportion of internet non-users globally (Wang et al., 2025). It represents a multidimensional phenomenon encompassing access to digital health services, information acquisition, communication channels, social participation opportunities, all of which can influence health trajectories.

Contemporary evidence reveals profound health implications of digital exclusion among aging populations. Digital inclusion demonstrates health-beneficial impacts through facilitating healthcare assistance, information access, and patient monitoring (Hanlon et al., 2017; Hou et al., 2022), including 33% reduced depression likelihood (Cotten et al., 2014) and improved successful aging outcomes, operationalized as preserved intrinsic capacity across cognitive, sensory, physical, and functional domains alongside active life engagement (Chen et al., 2024; Jiang & Yang, 2022). Conversely, digital exclusion operates through direct routes involving inaccessible digitally-delivered health services and indirect routes affecting broader health determinants such as social participation and health behaviors (Middle & Welch, 2022). Empirical evidence demonstrates substantial adverse implications across these same aging domains, revealing a consistent pattern of health deterioration associated with digital exclusion. From a functional health perspective, multinational research examining 108,621 participants showed that digital exclusion significantly increases functional dependency risk, with incidence rate ratios ranging from 1.15 in Mexico to 2.59 in China (Lu et al., 2022), while parallel multi-national research indicates significant associations with elevated frailty risks (Li, 2024). Turning to cognitive health, digital exclusion shows consistent associations with cognitive impairment across 62,413 participants, with odds ratios spanning 1.92 to 2.81 (Wang et al., 2024), while in mental health, it is consistently associated with elevated depressive symptoms across 23 countries (Wang et al., 2025). However, this evidence base suffers from three critical limitations that constrain theoretical and practical advancement.

First, existing research predominantly examines unidirectional associations between digital exclusion and adverse health outcomes, treating functional disability as a static endpoint rather than a dynamic process characterized by bidirectional transitions. Functional disabilities follow complex trajectories involving transitions across multiple states (i.e., functional independence, mild disability, severe disability, and death) (Goodarzi et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2024), with evidence demonstrating bidirectional movement between all non-absorbing states and individuals with mild disability showing greater probability of improvement than deterioration (Guo et al., 2024). This dynamic nature challenges the unidirectional approach dominating current digital health equity research. While previous longitudinal investigations established associations between digital exclusion and increased functional limitations risk (Lu et al., 2022), the field lacks multi-state transition analyses examining how digital exclusion influences the risk of transitions between different functional limitations states over time. This methodological gap obscures whether digital exclusion primarily promotes functional deterioration, impedes recovery, or simultaneously affects multiple transition pathways, which has implications for intervention design and timing.

Second, despite establishing associations between digital exclusion and functional outcomes, research has not examined how these dynamic functional limitations transitions translate into differential longevity and healthy life expectancy patterns, the ultimate population health metrics. Understanding these relationships carries critical implications for healthy aging trajectories and health equity. Substantial international variation exists in disability-life expectancy relationships, with country-specific evidence revealing marked differences in survival patterns: Mexican data demonstrate approximately 11-year survival advantages for functionally independent individuals at age 60 (Cabrero Castro et al., 2021), while US analyses show 2.8-year total life expectancy and 5.9-year disability-free life expectancy benefits among those without limitations at age 65 (Jia & Lubetkin, 2020). However, the pathways linking digital exclusion to these ultimate health outcomes remain unexplored. This represents a critical knowledge gap, as digital exclusion may influence not only whether individuals develop functional limitations but also the duration spent in various functional limitations states and the compression or expansion of morbidity in later life. Without understanding these relationships, we cannot assess whether digital exclusion alters aging processes or merely produces transient functional impairments.

Third, dramatic global variation in digital exclusion prevalence, ranging from 21.69% in Denmark to 97.15% in China (Wang et al., 2024), raises critical questions about contextual moderation of health effects. Complementing these disparities, functional limitations prevalence also varies considerably across countries. According to multinational comparative data (Lu et al., 2022), the proportion of older adults with functional dependency was 25.6% in China, 23.9% in England, 20.2% in the United States, 40.9% in Mexico, and 26.1% across European countries. These figures reveal substantial cross-national heterogeneity in functional limitations prevalence, underscoring the importance of assessing whether the health impacts of digital exclusion operate through universal or context-specific mechanisms. As healthcare systems worldwide increasingly integrate digital technologies into service delivery, understanding whether digital exclusion operates through universal mechanisms or is moderated by diverse international contexts has become essential for designing effective interventions. Yet existing multinational research has not examined cross-national variations in digital exclusion's associations with functional limitations transitions and longevity outcomes, limiting evidence for context-specific versus universal intervention strategies. A key axis of contextual variation may be the degree to which healthcare access has become coupled to digital platforms. Where medical resources, appointment registration, and care coordination are predominantly delivered through digital channels, individuals lacking internet access face compounded barriers to timely and appropriate care. Conversely, where robust non-digital pathways such as walk-in consultations, community-based services, or family caregiving networks remain widely accessible, the marginal health impact of digital exclusion may be attenuated. This digital-healthcare coupling framework generates directional expectations about cross-national heterogeneity that the present study is designed to explore.

This study addresses these gaps by examining longitudinal associations between digital exclusion and bidirectional functional limitations transitions among adults aged ≥50 years across multiple national contexts. We investigate whether digital exclusion increases risks of functional deterioration or impedes functional recovery, assess its differential impacts on total life expectancy and life expectancy across different functional limitations states, and examine heterogeneity across age groups, socioeconomic strata, and regional settings. Findings will inform evidence-based digital inclusion strategies for aging populations globally.

Methods

Study design and population

This investigation utilized longitudinal data from five established cohort studies: China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) waves 1-4 (2011-2018), Mexican Health and Aging Study (MHAS) waves 3-5 (2012-2018), Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE) waves 5, 6, 8 (2013, 2015, 2019), English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA) waves 1-6 (2002-2012), and Health and Retirement Study (HRS) waves 10-15 (2010-2020), incorporating 124,332 participants aged 50+ years from 23 countries. In the analyses, England, the United States, China, and Mexico were examined as individual countries, whereas European estimates were derived from pooled SHARE data across multiple European countries. Wave selection was based on concurrent availability of digital exclusion measures and functional limitations evaluations. Comprehensive methodological details for each study, including the list of European countries included in SHARE, are documented in Supplementary Methods S1. This research was conducted following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Participant selection

Consistent with prior longitudinal aging research (Cabrero Castro et al., 2021), and following established methodological approaches in multistate transition modeling (Luo et al., 2024; Marroig, 2023), participants were excluded if they had missing data on key variables or lacked sufficient longitudinal observations for functional limitations transition modeling, with no imputation procedures applied. Exclusion criteria included: (1) incomplete functional limitations information across assessment waves; and (2) single-wave functional limitations data availability only. This methodology aligns with established multi-state modeling practices requiring participants to have multiple observed functional limitations states during follow-up periods to enable transition analyses (Marroig, 2023). Furthermore, to account for the potential bias of sample exclusions, we compared baseline characteristics between included and excluded participants for each cohort. Detailed participant flow diagrams and final sample distributions by cohort are shown in Supplementary Figs. S1–S5, along with wave-specific sample selection tables for each cohort in Supplementary Tables S1–S5.

Assessment of digital exclusion

Digital technology use data were obtained via self-administered questionnaires across all cohort studies (Table S6). MHAS participants indicated whether they could access internet services at their residence. For ELSA, participants in waves 1-5 were asked whether they use the internet and/or email, while wave 6 assessed internet usage frequency with responses ranging from 1 = “Every day, or almost every day” to 6 = “Never.” SHARE constructed the internet use variable from the inquiry: “In the last 7 days, have you used the Internet at least once for e-mailing, searching for information, making purchases, or for any other purpose?” CHARLS participants responded to questions regarding their internet usage within the preceding month. HRS respondents were queried: “Do you regularly use the Internet (or the World Wide Web) for sending and receiving e-mail or for any other purpose, such as making purchases, searching for information, or making travel reservations?” For MHAS, digital exclusion on an individual level was not available, so the alternative question was asked: “Do you have Internet access at home?“. To ensure cross-study comparability, we created a dichotomous digital exclusion variable following established methodology (Lu et al., 2022). Internet use was defined as affirmative responses in HRS, CHARLS, SHARE, MHAS, and ELSA waves 1-5, with weekly or more frequent usage constituting use in ELSA wave 6. Digital exclusion was characterized by negative responses or usage frequency below weekly thresholds.

Functional limitations assessment

Functional capacity was evaluated through standardized basic and instrumental activities of daily living (ADL and IADL) assessments administered across all study waves. Six ADL domains were examined (dressing, walking across a room, bathing, eating, getting in or out of bed, toileting) alongside six IADL areas (meal preparation, shopping, telephone use, medication management, household work or gardening, money management). We operationalized functional states by integrating ADL and IADL performance, establishing three discrete capacity levels consistent with established methodological frameworks (Hou et al., 2019; Marroig, 2023): functional independence (independent in all ADL and IADL), mild functional limitations (difficulty with ≥1 IADL while maintaining ADL independence), and severe functional limitations (difficulty with ≥1 ADL, regardless of IADL status). This classification acknowledges that IADL impairments typically precede ADL limitations in the functional limitations process (Marroig, 2023). Death was incorporated as an absorbing state in transition models. Due to rare occurrences of direct transitions from functional independence to death (transition intensities: 0.000003 to 0.004 across the four countries and Europe) causing model convergence issues, this pathway was excluded from adjusted models, consistent with prior multi-state modeling literature (van Houwelingen et al., 2014).

Covariate assessment

Sociodemographic variables included sex (female vs. male), age (50-59, 60-69, and ≥70 years), educational attainment (less than upper secondary education, upper secondary and vocational training, and tertiary education), marital status (not married/partnered vs. married/partnered), residential location (rural vs. urban), and living arrangements (living with others vs. living alone). Behavioral factors encompassed smoking history (no vs. yes) and alcohol consumption history (no vs. yes). Health status incorporated chronic disease presence (no vs. yes). Cognitive impairment (no vs. yes) was additionally assessed in MHAS and ELSA, given substantial missing data in the remaining cohorts. Household income tertiles (low, middle, and high) based on within-cohort distributions were included for all cohorts except CHARLS. In all models, the first-mentioned category served as the reference group. Additional details are provided in Supplementary Table S7.

Statistical methodology

Multi-state Markov modeling

Continuous-time multi-state Markov models were applied to examine transitions between functional limitations states and identify determinants of functional limitations progression using the msm package in R (Marroig, 2023). Four states were specified: functional independence, mild functional limitations, severe functional limitations, and death. Models assumed unobserved exact transition times, potential multiple transitions between observations, and precisely recorded death dates (Mendonça et al., 2020). Transition pathways were specified based on observed patterns, excluding rare transitions to maintain model stability following established practices (van Houwelingen et al., 2014). The framework generated hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) representing instantaneous transition rates conditional on covariates. Transition probabilities were estimated as competing event probabilities rather than conditional on survival (Marroig, 2023). Mean sojourn times quantified average duration in each state before transition (Luo et al., 2024).

Life expectancy estimation

Life expectancy measures were computed using the elect package in R (Marroig, 2023), fitting multinomial regression models to estimate total and marginal life expectancy from multi-state parameters (van den Hout et al., 2019). Estimates were computed at nine specific exact ages (50, 55, 60, 65, 70, 75, 80, 85, and 90 years), where total life expectancy represented expected remaining lifespan at each specified age and marginal life expectancy calculated expected years in each living state regardless of initial condition. Stratified analyses by sex, residence, and chronic disease status evaluated differential digital exclusion impacts across population subgroups.

Sensitivity analyses

Multiple sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of our findings across different model specifications. First, we examined models incorporating additional covariates, including cognitive impairment measures in China, United States, and Europe where available, along with household income variables in China, to assess potential residual confounding effects. Second, we implemented simplified three-state models (functional independence, any functional limitations, death) to examine consistency across different functional limitations classification schemes and determine whether the distinction between mild and severe functional limitations states was essential for detecting digital exclusion associations. Third, we applied alternative functional limitations definitions using ADL-only criteria, where mild functional limitations was defined as difficulty with a single ADL and severe functional limitations as difficulty with multiple ADLs, evaluating whether findings were dependent on IADL inclusion in the primary functional limitations classification. These sensitivity analyses confirmed the stability of our results under varying methodological assumptions, providing greater confidence in the validity and robustness of the observed digital exclusion associations.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software, with significance levels set at p < 0.05 for hypothesis testing.

Results

Participant characteristics

Table 1 showed that this study included 124,332 individuals aged 50 years and above from five international longitudinal surveys: Mexico (n = 13,856), England (n = 11,787), China (n = 16,674), United States (n = 25,030), and Europe (n = 56,985). Demographic profiles revealed distinct cross-national patterns. China exhibited the youngest cohort with 53.0% aged 50-59 years, while Europe contained the oldest population with 37.1% aged ≥70 years. Women represented the majority across the four countries and Europe (50.8%-57.3%). Geographic distribution showed rural predominance in China (60.1%) and Europe (67.9%), contrasting with urban concentration in the United States (73.0%) and Mexico (69.4%). Social characteristics indicated that most individuals cohabited with others (78.2%-94.1%) and maintained marital partnerships (62.9%-86.5%). Educational attainment varied substantially: Mexico and China demonstrated lower educational levels, with 86.5% and 87.7% respectively having less than upper secondary education, whereas the United States showed the highest tertiary education rates (22.4%). Lifestyle factors differed markedly, with tobacco use ranging from 37.5% in Mexico to 62.7% in England, and alcohol consumption from 23.2% in Mexico to 89.6% in England. Chronic disease burden was highest in the United States (70.7%) and lowest in China (45.2%). Cognitive impairment rates were documented in Mexico (15.9%) and England (14.5%). Digital exclusion prevalence varied dramatically from 48.8% in the United States to 96.9% in China. One-year transition probabilities revealed bidirectional patterns of functional limitation transitions, with individuals capable of both progressing to more severe states and recovering to better functional status (Supplementary Table S8).

Regarding functional limitations deterioration, digital exclusion was significantly associated with worsening from functional independence to mild functional limitations in England (HR: 1.47, 95% CI: 1.23-1.75), China (HR: 2.56, 95% CI: 1.38-4.75), the United States (HR: 1.98, 95% CI: 1.8-2.17), and Europe (HR: 1.83, 95% CI: 1.64-2.03). Similarly, deterioration from functional independence to severe functional limitations showed significant risk increases across four countries, with hazard ratios ranging from 1.24 (95% CI: 1.06-1.45) in Mexico to 1.81 (95% CI: 1.15-2.86) in China. Digital exclusion also increased risk of further deterioration from mild to severe functional limitations in the United States (HR: 1.22, 95% CI: 1.07-1.38) and Europe (HR: 1.34, 95% CI: 1.12-1.59).

Conversely, digital exclusion significantly impaired recovery from severe functional limitations to functional independence in England (HR: 0.74, 95% CI: 0.62-0.88), the United States (HR: 0.55, 95% CI: 0.48-0.62), and Europe (HR: 0.72, 95% CI: 0.60-0.86). Digital exclusion additionally reduced recovery from mild functional limitations in the United States (HR: 0.84, 95% CI: 0.76-0.92).

Regarding mortality, digital exclusion was associated with increased death risk among those with severe functional limitations in England (HR: 1.59, 95% CI: 1.13-2.25), the United States (HR: 1.36, 95% CI: 1.24-1.50), and Europe (HR: 1.36, 95% CI: 1.13-1.65). These findings indicate that digital exclusion consistently increases functional limitations deterioration risk, impairs recovery likelihood, and elevates mortality risk specifically among severely disabled individuals.

Life expectancy

Life expectancy estimates at ages 50, 55, 60, 65, 70, 75, 80, 85, and 90 years revealed that digital exclusion was consistently associated with substantial reductions in both functional and total life expectancy (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table S9).

For functional life expectancy, digital exclusion was associated with decreases ranging from the smallest reductions in Mexico (3.72 years at age 50 to 0.88 years at age 90) to the largest in China (14.27 years at age 50 to 3.34 years at age 90), maintaining statistical significance across the entire age spectrum (all p < 0.001).

Total life expectancy showed similarly consistent patterns, with reductions ranging from the smallest decreases in Mexico (2.77 years at age 50 to 0.53 years at age 90) to the largest in the United States (8.15 years at age 50 to 1.25 years at age 90, all p < 0.001). In contrast, associations between digital exclusion and functional limitations duration showed heterogeneous patterns across the four countries and Europe. For mild functional limitations life expectancy, digital exclusion was associated with increased duration in China (2.53 years at age 50 to 1.19 years at age 70, all p < 0.05) and the United States (1.58 years at age 50 to 0.29 years at age 90, all p < 0.001), with modest increases in England at younger ages (0.38 years at age 50 to 0.33 years at age 55, all p < 0.05), while Mexico and Europe showed non-significant associations.

For severe functional limitations life expectancy, increases were observed in China (4.10 years at age 50 to 1.20 years at age 90, all p < 0.01) and the United States (1.39 years at age 50 to 0.28 years at age 90, all p < 0.001), with significant increases in Mexico at younger ages (0.87 years at age 50 to 0.52 years at age 70, all p < 0.05) and England at ages 50-55 (0.77 to 0.62 years, all p < 0.05), while Europe demonstrated non-significant associations.

Stratified analyses

To explore population heterogeneity in the impacts of digital exclusion, we conducted stratified analyses (Supplementary Fig. S6–S9). Chronic disease stratification showed that individuals without pre-existing chronic diseases demonstrated larger functional life expectancy losses and greater increases in functional limitations duration compared to those with chronic diseases in both China and the United States. Sex stratification revealed distinct patterns between the two countries: in China, males showed greater total life expectancy reductions whereas females experienced greater increases in functional limitations duration; in the United States, females demonstrated both greater functional life expectancy losses and greater increases in functional limitations duration compared to males. Notably, the stratified analyses indicated that differences across chronic disease status and sex were less pronounced in other cohort comparisons. Furthermore, the impact of digital exclusion was highly consistent between urban and rural areas across the four countries and Europe.

Sensitivity analyses

Models incorporating additional covariates maintained the associations between digital exclusion and functional limitations transitions, with one exception of reduced mortality risk among those with mild functional limitations in the United States (Supplementary Table S10). The simplified three-state model showed increased risk of functional independence to functional limitations across the four countries and Europe, with impaired recovery and elevated functional limitations-related mortality in England, United States, and Europe (Supplementary Table S11). ADL-only definitions revealed consistent patterns: digital exclusion was associated with increased risk of progression to mild functional limitations (excluding Mexico) and to severe functional limitations (excluding China), while impairing recovery from mild to functional independence (England) and from severe to mild functional limitations (United States) (Supplementary Table S12). These analyses confirm that digital exclusion's associations with functional limitations transitions remain robust across different model specifications.

Discussion

This study provides the most comprehensive examination to date of digital exclusion's associations with functional limitations transitions and life expectancy among adults aged 50 and older across the four countries and Europe. Our findings yield three principal conclusions. First, digital exclusion was consistently associated with increased risks of progressing to more severe functional limitation states, while impaired recovery to better functional status was observed in several countries. Second, digital exclusion was associated with reductions in both functional and total life expectancy, with the magnitude of these associations varying across national contexts. Third, stratified analyses revealed important heterogeneity: individuals without chronic diseases, women (in China and the United States), and younger age groups experienced larger effects of digital exclusion on life expectancy outcomes.

The observed patterns align with mounting evidence that digital connectivity serves as a protective factor for health functioning in adults aged 50 and older. Our transition-based analysis demonstrates that digitally excluded adults aged 50 and older face significantly higher risks of functional deterioration, suggesting that internet access provides pathways that actively prevent functional decline rather than merely maintaining existing health status. The mechanisms underlying these associations likely operate through multiple interconnected pathways. Digital exclusion limits opportunities for health information seeking, prevents utilization of telehealth services including timely consultation with healthcare professionals, real-time monitoring, and purchasing medications and health devices (Green et al., 2008; McManus et al., 2021; Shim et al., 2018), and reduces social engagement and communication opportunities. Studies show that digitally excluded individuals with lower levels of social communication were at higher odds of functional dependency. This aligns with evidence that internet users participate in more social activities (Kai & Junjie, 2021), alleviate social isolation through physical activities (Duplaga & Szulc, 2019; Ingen et al., 2017), and engage in beneficial health behaviors (Duplaga & Szulc, 2019; Xavier et al., 2014). Digital inclusion has emerged as an alternative pathway for alleviating social isolation and improving quality of life for older adults (Kamin et al., 2021; The Lancet Healthy, 2021).

Cross-national variations revealed that digital exclusion significantly impaired recovery from severe functional limitations exclusively in high-income countries (HICs), including England, the United States, and Europe, while showing no significant associations in China and Mexico. These differential patterns likely reflect fundamental differences in healthcare system organization and digitalization during our study periods. In HICs, healthcare systems have increasingly integrated digital health technologies into rehabilitation service delivery, with literature documenting development of telerehabilitation approaches for various conditions (Fonda et al., 2023; Nizeyimana et al., 2022), meaning digitally excluded individuals face barriers to accessing rehabilitation-related information and services. In contrast, China's telemedicine infrastructure, despite rapid growth with “Internet Plus Healthcare” initiatives (2015-2018), primarily emphasized diagnosis and treatment through business-to-business hospital networks rather than patient-centered rehabilitation (Cui et al., 2020; Ye et al., 2023), while both China and Mexico maintained predominantly facility-based rehabilitation services and family caregiving rooted in cultural traditions of filial piety and familismo (Min & Barrio, 2009; Trujillo et al., 2012; Wu et al., 2024). In contexts where digital health infrastructure for rehabilitation remains limited and family-based care predominates, the incremental impact of digital exclusion on severe functional limitations recovery appears less pronounced, underscoring that digital exclusion's effects are context-dependent and shaped by existing healthcare delivery structures.

These findings should also be interpreted in light of contextual differences across countries. The mechanisms which digital exclusion affects health may vary depending on national institutional environments. Differences in healthcare system digitalization, availability of non-digital service channels, social welfare systems, and patterns of family caregiving may shape how strongly digital exclusion translates into functional health risks (Robinson et al., 2015). Our cross-national design therefore does not assume identical mechanisms across settings but instead allows examination of whether associations between digital exclusion and functional health transitions emerge across diverse institutional and technological environments.

The pronounced cross-national variations in life expectancy losses reveal how the coupling of medical resources with digital access differentially affects digitally excluded individuals. China exhibited the most substantial reductions, likely attributable to healthcare resources becoming predominantly accessible through digital platforms. During our study period, tertiary hospitals, which concentrate superior medical expertise and advanced equipment, required electronic health cards integrated with mobile payment platforms for registration, consultation, payment, and prescription services (Tao et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2022), thereby relegating digitally excluded individuals to resource-poor primary care facilities with limited specialist availability and diagnostic capabilities (Gao et al., 2022). In stark contrast, Mexico demonstrated the smallest reductions, reflecting substantial compensatory effects of non-digital access channels. Widespread pharmacy-adjacent clinics operated on walk-in basis without appointments, providing low-cost consultations through direct encounters. Additionally, 12-60% of Mexicans, particularly in rural areas with limited modern clinical access, depended on traditional medicine practices and herbal medicines for primary healthcare needs. These resources were accessible through direct community contact without digital intermediation (Cabada-Aguirre et al., 2023; Geck et al., 2020). Such non-digital, cash-based pathways enabled healthcare access independent of internet connectivity, attenuating digital exclusion's marginal health impact.

The strong age gradient suggests that digital exclusion is most damaging when it occurs in midlife and “younger-old” ages. A loss of 14.27 functional life-years at age 50 but only 3.34 years at age 90 implies that lacking digital access during earlier stages removes more opportunities to accumulate health capital (better information, social connection, and care use) over a longer remaining lifespan. This pattern is consistent with Chinese studies showing that Internet or mobile Internet use yields larger health gains among middle-aged and younger older adults than among those ≥60 years (Wang & Chen, 2024). Life-course research likewise indicates that midlife is a critical window when modifying behavioral and social risk factors produces disproportionate benefits for healthy ageing, whereas at very old ages multimorbidity, frailty, and selective survival constrain the marginal impact of new protective resources such as digital inclusion (The Lancet Healthy, 2025). Moreover, consistent with the WHO Healthy Ageing Framework, functional ability in old age depends increasingly on supportive physical, social, and technological environments rather than individual-level risk factors alone, which may further contribute to the weaker associations observed at very old ages (Beard et al., 2016).

In China and the United States, the greater prolongation of functional limitations-adjusted life expectancy among women likely reflects their higher reliance on the internet for health information access, disease management, and fulfilling caregiving responsibilities (Twenge & Martin, 2020). Digital exclusion weakens their ability to access disease management information and caregiving resources, thereby increasing functional limitations risks and leading to a greater extent of functional limitations-adjusted life expectancy prolongation compared to men. Chinese men experience greater total life expectancy loss due to digital exclusion, possibly because of their stronger dependence on the internet for economic opportunities, social participation, and psychological support (Li et al., 2025). In countries like Mexico, Europe, and the UK, while sex differences exist, the impact of digital exclusion is more balanced, likely due to more egalitarian sex role divisions, social support systems, and patterns of internet use.

The urban–rural analysis shows that differences in life expectancy between urban and rural adults aged 50 and older are small across countries. In China, rural patients often bypass primary care and seek care directly in urban hospitals due to concerns about primary care quality (Duckett et al., 2016), resulting in similar digitalized healthcare access barriers for both urban and rural adults aged 50 and older. In the UK and the United States, primary care systems provide comparable service capacity across urban and rural areas, and these adults exhibit minimal differences in primary care utilization (Fraze et al., 2022). Although primary care is becoming increasingly digitalized, traditional channels such as telephone appointments and face-to-face consultations remain available in both settings (Litchfield et al., 2024). These factors ensure accessible non-digital alternatives for digitally excluded populations in both urban and rural areas, contributing to the relatively balanced impact of digital exclusion. These patterns underscore how digital exclusion interacts with country-specific sex inequalities and urbanization dynamics.

Our analysis revealed a counterintuitive finding: when comparing the marginal effects of digital exclusion across groups, individuals without chronic diseases experienced significantly larger reductions in life expectancy than those with chronic diseases. For healthy individuals, internet access is a key source of preventive health resources, including information on maintaining healthy lifestyles, early health screenings, and monitoring tools that promote long-term well-being (Chen et al., 2023; Weaver et al., 2010). Digital exclusion deprives them of these important preventive measures, especially during critical health periods. In contrast, individuals with chronic diseases may benefit from more regular and structured engagement with formal healthcare systems through scheduled clinical visits and ongoing professional monitoring (Glasziou et al., 2005). Such institutional care routines could potentially sustain healthcare contact through non-digital channels, offering one plausible explanation for why the life expectancy gap associated with digital exclusion was attenuated in this group. Directly evaluating this interpretation would require information on healthcare utilization patterns alongside digital access, future research designed to examine these pathways would help clarify how chronic disease status modifies the life expectancy implications of digital exclusion.

Several methodological limitations should be acknowledged. First, differences in the operationalization of digital exclusion across the four countries and Europe may affect cross-dataset comparability. MHAS assessed internet access at the household level, whereas the remaining cohorts used individual-level assessments; moreover, some cohorts incorporated time-bound or frequency-based usage items. Although our coding aligned with established practices in prior digital exclusion research, these variations introduce measurement heterogeneity that should be considered when interpreting results. Second, our measure captures internet access as the foundational barrier to digital participation rather than the full multidimensional construct including digital literacy and usage competency. Future research incorporating multidimensional assessments would provide more nuanced understanding. Third, our measures of functional limitations captured self-reported difficulties in performing activities rather than actual care dependency, as data on help-seeking for daily activities were unavailable. This limits findings to impairment-based transitions without reflecting true dependency states. Fourth, although this study utilizes the most comprehensive internationally harmonized longitudinal aging data, the heterogeneity in digital infrastructure, socioeconomic development, and healthcare systems across included countries may limit direct comparability. Fifth, the over 40% item non-response rate in CHARLS income data reflects the structural complexity of the income module, which requires simultaneous recall and aggregation of income across multiple heterogeneous sources from all household members, representing a level of detail that is atypical of standard household surveys (Zhao et al., 2014). Missing cases are likely concentrated among respondents with the most complex or informal income arrangements, such that complete-case analyses incorporating income would themselves introduce selection bias. Self-reported household income in Chinese surveys is additionally subject to systematic mean-reverting misreporting relative to diary-based benchmarks (Li & Wang, 2024), further limiting its validity as a socioeconomic indicator. Because digital exclusion is more prevalent among lower-income individuals who also experience worse functional outcomes, exclusion of income likely produces upward bias in the CHARLS estimates; educational attainment and residential location, already included as covariates, are expected to absorb a substantial share of this confounding, though some residual overestimation cannot be ruled out. Finally, although we compared baseline characteristics between included and excluded participants (Supplementary Table S13–S17) and conducted sensitivity analyses to assess robustness, the sample exclusion may introduce potential bias.

Conclusion

This multinational study demonstrates that digital exclusion is associated with functional limitations and reduced life expectancy among adults aged 50 and older across the four countries and Europe. Digital exclusion increased risks of transitioning to more severe functional limitations states while impairing recovery from functional limitations, particularly in high-income countries where healthcare delivery has become increasingly digitalized. The magnitude of life expectancy reductions varied substantially across national contexts, with larger effects observed where healthcare access is tightly coupled with digital platforms. The pronounced associations among younger-old adults and individuals without chronic diseases suggest that digital exclusion exerts its greatest impact during life stages when preventive health behaviors and health capital accumulation yield the longest returns. These findings underscore the importance of targeting digital inclusion interventions toward midlife populations and maintaining non-digital healthcare access channels as health systems undergo digital transformation. Addressing digital exclusion should be integrated into healthy aging policies as a modifiable determinant of functional trajectories in aging populations.
