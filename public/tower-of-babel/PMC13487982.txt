Utidelone in patients with advanced soft tissue sarcoma refractory to anthracycline and antiangiogenic therapy (UTISARC): a single-arm phase II study
Authors: Jie Liu, Yang Fu, Bin Wang, Yun-Feng Tang, Yao-Tiao Deng, Yu Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTreatment options remain limited for patients with advanced or metastatic soft tissue sarcoma after failure of both anthracycline-based chemotherapy and antiangiogenic therapy. Utidelone, an epothilone analog, has demonstrated antitumor activity across a broad spectrum of preclinical models and clinical trials. This single-arm, phase II trial was designed to assess the efficacy and safety of utidelone in a heavily pretreated population.MethodsThis study enrolled patients aged 18 years or older with locally advanced or metastatic soft tissue sarcoma who had previously been treated with anthracycline-based chemotherapy and antiangiogenic tyrosine kinase inhibitors. Participants received utidelone via a 90-minute intravenous infusion at a dose of 30 mg/m² on days 1–5 of each 21-day treatment cycle, continuing until disease progression or the occurrence of unacceptable toxicity. The primary endpoint was median progression-free survival. The secondary endpoints included the objective response rate, overall survival and safety.ResultsBetween August 2022 and March 2025, a total of 27 patients were included in the study. The median age was 55 years (range, 30–68). There were 15 patients with leiomyosarcoma, 3 with dedifferentiated liposarcoma, 3 with epithelioid sarcoma, 2 with angiosarcoma, and 4 with other types of sarcoma. The median number of prior systemic therapy regimens was 2 (range, 1–4). The median treatment exposure was 5 cycles (range, 1–10). Two patients achieved a partial response, resulting in an objective response rate of 7%. The median progression-free survival was 4.6 months (95% confidence interval, 3.6–5.6). Overall survival data were immature, with only 9 death events (33%) observed. The estimated 12-month overall survival rate was 80%. Most adverse events were grade 1 or 2 in severity. The most frequently observed grade ≥ 3 adverse event was peripheral neuropathy (11%).ConclusionsIn this exploratory trial, utidelone demonstrated preliminary antitumor activity and a manageable safety profile in patients with refractory soft tissue sarcoma. Further exploration of novel combination therapies involving utidelone is warranted to enhance its clinical utility.Trial registrationChiCTR2200062161, registered on July 26, 2022.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-026-04990-x.

Background

Soft tissue sarcoma (STS) is a rare and highly heterogeneous group of malignancies arising from mesenchymal tissue [1]. Localized disease is traditionally managed with wide local excision, often supplemented with radiotherapy [1]. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy may increase the likelihood of complete surgical resection, especially in patients with large, high-grade tumors, and the degree of pathological response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy has also been shown to correlate, to some extent, with the efficacy of subsequent palliative treatment [2]. However, the benefit of perioperative chemotherapy on overall survival (OS) remains uncertain; consequently, its use is generally restricted to high-risk patients [3]. Prognostic tools such as the Sarculator nomogram, which is designed to estimate the 10-year OS rate, can support decision-making regarding adjuvant chemotherapy [2]. The management of advanced STS represents a substantial challenge in contemporary oncology. While anthracycline-based chemotherapy remains the cornerstone of first-line treatment [4–7] and multitargeted antiangiogenic tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKI) are well established in the second-line setting [8–10], most patients inevitably experience disease progression. In this context, treatment options have become increasingly fragmented and are guided primarily by specific pathological subtypes [11–20]. Real-world evidence from the large-scale METASARC observational study demonstrated the limited clinical benefit of third-line and later systemic therapies, with median OS ranging from 5.4 to 8.5 months across the majority of histological subtypes [21]. This poor prognosis underscores the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies capable of overcoming acquired drug resistance in heavily pretreated patients.

Epothilones and their analogs have emerged as a promising class of antimicrotubule agents [22]. While these compounds share a similar mechanism of action with taxanes, they exhibit greater antiproliferative potency, particularly in tumor cells that overexpress P-glycoprotein, a transmembrane transporter encoded by the ABCB1 gene. This efflux pump actively expels structurally diverse chemotherapeutic agents, including anthracyclines and taxanes, thereby reducing intracellular drug accumulation and compromising therapeutic efficacy [22–24]. Utidelone, a genetically engineered epothilone analog, binds to the β-subunit of tubulin to stabilize microtubule assembly and prevent depolymerization, thus resulting in G2/M phase arrest and subsequent apoptosis [25, 26]. Notably, utidelone retains activity against tumors overexpressing the ABCB1 gene, highlighting its ability to evade this common mechanism of multidrug resistance [25, 27]. Moreover, utidelone in combination with gemcitabine induces immunogenic cell death, promotes intratumoral infiltration of T lymphocytes, and thereby enhances antitumor immunity in mouse models [28]. This preclinical promise translated into clinical benefit in a phase II trial involving patients with heavily pretreated, anthracycline- and taxane-refractory metastatic breast cancer (MBC), in which utidelone monotherapy achieved an objective response rate (ORR) of 29% and a median progression-free survival (PFS) of 5.4 months. The addition of capecitabine further improved efficacy, yielding an ORR of 42% and a median PFS of 7.9 months [26]. These findings were confirmed in a pivotal phase III trial, which demonstrated statistically significant improvements in both PFS (8.4 vs. 4.3 months; hazard ratio [HR] = 0.46, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.36–0.59) and OS (19.8 vs. 16.0 months; HR = 0.75, 95% CI:0.59–0.94) with the utidelone–capecitabine combination versus capecitabine alone in this refractory MBC population [29, 30], leading to regulatory approval in China. Ongoing research explores its potential in populations with high unmet medical needs, such as a phase II trial of utidelone plus bevacizumab in ERBB2-negative MBC with active brain metastases, reporting a clinically meaningful central nervous system ORR of 43% [31].

On the basis of this rationale, we hypothesized that utidelone may provide clinical benefit to patients with STS who have progressed after both anthracycline chemotherapy and antiangiogenic TKI therapy, leading to the initiation of the UTISARC trial—a single-arm, phase II study to assess the efficacy and safety of utidelone in this treatment-refractory population.

Methods

Study design, setting and participants

This investigator-initiated, single-arm, phase II study received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee on Biomedical Research, West China Hospital of Sichuan University (reference number: 2022 − 642). The study registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (https://www.chictr.org.cn/) on July 26, 2022 (registration number: ChiCTR2200062161). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. Patients were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: a confirmed pathological diagnosis of locally advanced or metastatic STS; age ≥ 18 years; documented disease recurrence, metastasis, or progression after prior systemic therapies, which must have included one regimen of anthracycline-based chemotherapy (including neoadjuvant and adjuvant treatment) and at least one line of multitargeted antiangiogenic TKI therapy, with first-line combination therapy involving an anthracycline and a multitargeted antiangiogenic TKI permitted; at least one measurable lesion according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1; an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0 or 1; and adequate hematological, cardiac, hepatic, and renal function. The main exclusion criteria included the presence of other concurrent malignant tumors; symptomatic brain metastases; active infections; bleeding or coagulation disorders; gastrointestinal ulcers; known hypersensitivity to the investigational drug or its excipients; a history of grade ≥ 2 peripheral neuropathy; a history of psychiatric illness; and specific histological subtypes—including gastrointestinal stromal tumor, embryonal or alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma, dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans, Ewing sarcoma, undifferentiated small round cell sarcoma, inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor, clear cell sarcoma, alveolar soft-part sarcoma, malignant mesothelioma, and primary bone sarcoma.

Treatment

Utidelone was administered as a 90-minute intravenous infusion at a dose of 30 mg/m² on days 1–5 of each 21-day cycle and continued until disease progression or the occurrence of unacceptable toxicity. Premedication to prevent hypersensitivity reactions was administered 30–60 min prior to the first utidelone infusion in each treatment cycle (day 1) and consisted of intramuscular diphenhydramine (40 mg), intravenous dexamethasone (10 mg), and intravenous cimetidine (300 mg). For subsequent infusions on days 2–5 within the same cycle, the doses of dexamethasone and diphenhydramine could be reduced by 50%, whereas the cimetidine dose remained unchanged. In addition, antiemetic prophylaxis with 5 mg of intravenous tropisetron was initiated one hour before each utidelone infusion and continued for six consecutive days. Starting from the 13th patient, frozen gloves and socks were applied 30 min prior to each utidelone infusion to reduce the risk and severity of peripheral neuropathy. Depending on the type and severity of adverse events, utidelone treatment could be delayed, discontinued, or administered at a reduced dose to manage toxicity. Patients had two opportunities for stepwise dose reduction: initially to 24 mg/m² and subsequently to 19 mg/m².

Endpoints

The primary endpoint of the study was PFS, which was defined as the time from treatment initiation to disease progression or death from any cause. The secondary endpoints included ORR, defined as the proportion of patients who achieved a complete response or partial response (PR) as the best overall response, confirmed by repeat imaging at least 4 weeks apart; the disease control rate (DCR), defined as the proportion of patients with complete response, PR, or stable disease (SD); OS, defined as the time from treatment initiation to death from any cause; and treatment-related toxicity. Tumor assessments were conducted via computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging at baseline and every two treatment cycles during the first 24 weeks of therapy, in accordance with RECIST version 1.1 criteria, and subsequently every 9–12 weeks. Survival status was updated every 12 weeks at each scheduled follow-up visit. Treatment-related toxicities were monitored throughout the treatment period and for 30 days following the last dose, based on the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0.

Statistical analysis

Based on historical data, the median PFS with placebo in patients with advanced STS previously treated with anthracycline-based therapy ranges from 1.0 to 1.6 months [8–10, 32]. Sample size calculations were predicated on the assumption that utidelone treatment would achieve a median PFS of 3.6 months in patients who had progressed after both anthracycline-based chemotherapy and antiangiogenic TKI therapy, compared with a null hypothesis of 1.6 months. Assuming a two-sided significance level (α) of 0.05, a statistical power (1–β) of 0.90, and an estimated 20% dropout rate, a total sample size of 27 patients was required. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient characteristics, treatment response, and safety outcomes. Time-to-event endpoints, including PFS and OS, were estimated using the Kaplan‒Meier method with 95% CI. All statistical analyses were conducted by IBM SPSS Statistics (version 25.0), and graphical representations were generated with GraphPad Prism (version 10.1.2).

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 28 patients were enrolled between August 2022 and March 2025. Following the exclusion of one patient with a pathologically confirmed malignant phyllodes tumor of the breast, the remaining 27 patients received at least one cycle of utidelone therapy and constituted the efficacy and safety analysis set (Fig. 1). The median age was 55 years (range, 30–68), with 12 male patients (44%) and 15 female patients (56%). Leiomyosarcoma was the most common pathological subtype (n = 15, 56%), and the vast majority of patients (n = 25, 93%) presented with metastatic disease. All patients had previously received anthracycline-based chemotherapy and antiangiogenic TKI therapy with anlotinib. A substantial proportion (n = 23, 85%) had undergone two or more lines of previous systemic treatment, highlighting the heavily pretreated and refractory nature of this patient population. Efficacy

As of the data cutoff date of September 15, 2025, the median follow-up duration was 19.0 months (95% CI: 13.4–24.6). The best percentage change in target lesions from baseline for each patient is presented in the waterfall plot (Fig. 2A). One patient with leiomyosarcoma and one with dedifferentiated liposarcoma achieved a confirmed PR, yielding an ORR of 7% (2/27). No complete responses were documented. SD was achieved in 19 patients, yielding a DCR of 78% (21/27). Progressive disease (PD) was the first documented outcome in six patients (22%), with PD driven by new lesions observed in two of these patients (7%). Among the 15 patients with leiomyosarcoma, the treatment outcomes were as follows: one patient achieved a PR, 11 patients achieved SD, and three patients experienced PD, resulting in an ORR of 7% and a DCR of 80%. The time to response and time to progression, stratified by sarcoma subtype, are displayed in the swimmer plot (Fig. 2B). Durable clinical benefit was observed in 9 of 27 patients (33%), with PFS duration exceeding 6 months, including eight patients who maintained SD and one patient who achieved a sustained PR. The Kaplan‒Meier curves for PFS and OS are shown in Fig. 3. The median PFS was 4.6 months (95% CI: 3.6–5.6) for the overall population and 4.3 months (95% CI: 2.4–6.2) for the leiomyosarcoma subgroup. The PFS durations for the two patients with uterine leiomyosarcoma were 3.1 and 7.1 months, respectively; neither achieved an objective response. The PFS durations for the three patients with dedifferentiated liposarcoma were 1.6, 9.2, and 34.0 months, respectively. At the time of analysis, the OS data were immature, with only 9 death events (33%) observed. The estimated 12-month OS rate was 80%.

Three (11%) patients had previously received gemcitabine combined with a taxane—two were treated with docetaxel and one with albumin-bound paclitaxel—while the remaining 24 (89%) were taxane-naïve. Among patients with prior taxane exposure, PFS durations were 1.5, 5.6, and 8.3 months; by comparison, taxane-naïve patients had a median PFS of 4.5 months (95% CI: 2.6–6.4). Objective responses were observed in one of the three taxane-pretreated patients (33%) and in one of the 24 taxane-naïve patients (4%).

Safety

All 27 patients were evaluated for safety. Treatment-related adverse events were generally manageable, and most were grade 1 or 2 in severity (Table 2). The most common adverse events (all grades) were peripheral neuropathy (89%), decreased neutrophil count (44%), decreased white blood cell count (37%), anemia (37%), alopecia (37%), and anorexia (37%). Grade ≥ 3 adverse events were primarily peripheral neuropathy (11%) and diarrhea (7%). Other grade 3–4 events, each reported in 4% of patients, included decreased neutrophil count (one grade 4 event), decreased white blood cell count, increased alanine aminotransferase, increased gamma-glutamyl transferase, and increased alkaline phosphatase. Dose reductions of utidelone to 24 mg/m² were required in 4 patients (15%) due to neutropenia, diarrhea, liver function impairment, or peripheral neuropathy. Treatment was discontinued in 7 patients (26%), primarily due to peripheral neuropathy (5 patients, 19%). No treatment-related deaths occurred during the study.

Discussion

The UTISARC trial represents the first prospective study to evaluate the efficacy and safety of utidelone, an epothilone analog, in patients with advanced STS who have progressed following both anthracycline-based chemotherapy and antiangiogenic TKI therapy. The results demonstrate that utidelone provides a clinically meaningful PFS benefit, with a median PFS of 4.6 months and an ORR of 7%, along with a manageable safety profile. The observed therapeutic activity in this heavily pretreated patient population supports further investigation of utidelone as a potential treatment option, addressing an important unmet clinical need in the current treatment landscape for advanced STS.

Both taxanes and epothilones are microtubule-stabilizing agents. Paclitaxel monotherapy has shown clinically meaningful efficacy in advanced angiosarcoma, yielding a median PFS of 4.0–6.6 months [20, 33], but the antitumor activity of taxanes in unselected subtypes of advanced STS is notably limited, with median PFS ranging from only 1.6 to 2.4 months for either paclitaxel or docetaxel [34–36]. Docetaxel is more frequently administered in combination with gemcitabine for advanced STS, resulting in an improved median PFS of 4.1–6.2 months [18, 19, 37]. These data indicate that taxanes exhibit modest therapeutic utility as monotherapy in unselected STS populations. Early studies of epothilones in advanced STS also yielded suboptimal results. Neurotoxicity and neutropenia are common dose-limiting toxicities among epothilone analogs [22]. Ixabepilone has demonstrated limited antitumor efficacy at clinically tolerated doses in patients with advanced STS [38, 39]. BMS-247550, another epothilone B analog, was evaluated in chemotherapy-naïve patients with metastatic STS and demonstrated moderate antitumor activity—with a response rate of 6% and a median PFS of 4.5 months—accompanied by considerable toxicity, including grade 3–4 neutropenia in 48% of patients and grade 3–4 neuropathy in 26% [40]. In contrast, utidelone has demonstrated a more favorable hematologic safety profile compared to other epothilone analogs [25]. Although grade 3 peripheral neuropathy occurs in 11% of patients treated with utidelone (Table 2), it was generally manageable through proactive interventions, such as prophylactic cryotherapy using frozen gloves and socks during infusion, as well as dose modifications and supportive care. The ORR of 7% and median PFS of 4.6 months observed with utidelone treatment reflect preliminary evidence of antitumor activity in patients with refractory STS who have progressed following both anthracycline-based chemotherapy and antiangiogenic TKI therapy.

In a comparable patient population, the fifth cohort of the REGOSARC trial evaluated regorafenib in non-adipocytic STS patients who were intolerant to or had progressed following both doxorubicin-based chemotherapy and pazopanib [32]. The study demonstrated a statistically significant PFS benefit over placebo (median PFS: 2.1 vs. 1.1 months; HR = 0.33, 95% CI: 0.15–0.74), indicating that the sequential use of another multitargeted antiangiogenic TKI can still provide clinical benefit through sustained vascular suppression and the inhibition of distinct molecular targets [32]. In this context, utidelone treatment achieves a competitive median PFS of 4.6 months, positioning it as a mechanistically distinct cytotoxic alternative.

Objective responses to utidelone were observed in both leiomyosarcoma and dedifferentiated liposarcoma. As a later-line therapy for advanced leiomyosarcoma and liposarcoma, the clinical activity of utidelone aligns with that of other established cytotoxic agents, such as eribulin (a microtubule dynamics inhibitor; ORR, 4% [16]) and trabectedin (a marine-derived DNA minor groove binder; ORR, 10% [17]). In advanced leiomyosarcoma, trabectedin has advanced to the first-line setting based on robust clinical evidence. The combination of doxorubicin plus trabectedin followed by trabectedin maintenance significantly improved both median PFS (12.0 vs. 6.0 months; HR = 0.37, 95% CI: 0.26–0.53) and OS (33.0 vs. 24.0 months; HR = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.44–0.95) compared with doxorubicin monotherapy [5, 6]. Lurbinectedin, a trabectedin analog, in combination with doxorubicin for first-line treatment of metastatic leiomyosarcoma, is currently being evaluated in the ongoing phase IIb/III SaLuDo trial [41]. As a selective exportin-1 inhibitor, selinexor demonstrated clinical activity in dedifferentiated liposarcoma [42]. The phase II/III SEAL trial reported that selinexor improved PFS (medians 2.8 vs. 2.1 months; HR = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.52–0.95) and time to next treatment (medians 5.8 vs. 3.2 months; HR = 0.50, 95% CI: 0.37–0.66) compared with placebo among heavily pretreated patients with advanced dedifferentiated liposarcoma [43]. Further supporting its utility, a recent phase I study of gemcitabine plus selinexor in advanced STS showed promising antitumor activity, with an ORR of 31% and a median PFS of 5.6 months [44]. Collectively, these findings suggest that utidelone may contribute to enhanced antitumor efficacy when used in combination regimens or in earlier treatment settings.

In the UTISARC study, PFS was prespecified as the primary efficacy endpoint, consistent with the clinical trial standards for evaluating antitumor activity in pretreated sarcoma populations [8, 9, 32, 37]. A durable clinical benefit was observed, with 9 of 27 patients (33%) achieving PFS exceeding 6 months, providing preliminary evidence of antitumor activity in this advanced, treatment-refractory sarcoma setting. In contrast, OS—designated as the secondary endpoint—remains immature at the time of analysis. Moreover, OS interpretation is confounded by the heterogeneity in post-progression therapies. Definitive assessment of utidelone’s effect on OS will require prospective, randomized controlled trials.

Several limitations of this study should be considered when interpreting the results. The single-arm, nonrandomized design and small sample size limit the robustness of the conclusions and preclude definitive efficacy comparisons with other treatments or best supportive care. Second, the inherent histological heterogeneity of STS raises the possibility that utidelone activity may not be uniform across all subtypes. This limitation is particularly relevant given the predominance of leiomyosarcoma in our study, despite the observation that the leiomyosarcoma subgroup exhibited numerically lower efficacy than the overall population. Third, only three patients in our study had prior taxane exposure, which limited our ability to draw conclusions about whether prior taxane treatment influences response, although one patient achieved a PR. Finally, the absence of biomarker analysis—including tubulin isotype expression, relevant gene mutations, and P-glycoprotein levels—hampers efforts to identify patients most likely to derive clinical benefit.

Conclusions

In the UTISARC trial, utidelone demonstrated preliminary antitumor activity and a manageable safety profile in patients with advanced STS previously treated with both anthracycline-based chemotherapy and antiangiogenic agents. Objective responses were observed in leiomyosarcoma and dedifferentiated liposarcoma. To definitively establish its efficacy, future randomized trials comparing utidelone with the investigator’s choice of therapy in treatment-refractory STS are warranted. Additionally, the evaluation of utidelone in earlier lines of therapy or in rational combination regimens with agents such as doxorubicin may enhance clinical outcomes for patients with this challenging disease.
