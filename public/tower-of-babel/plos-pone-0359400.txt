A prototype-aligned multi-scale attention network for few-shot industrial surface defect detection

Rubing Huang, Lianyi Zhao

ABSTRACT
Few-shot industrial surface defect detection remains challenging because annotated defect images are scarce, defect scales vary considerably, and textured backgrounds can cause false responses. This study proposes a Prototype-Aligned Multi-Scale Attention Network (PA-MSAN) that integrates multi-receptive-field feature extraction, spatial-channel attention, and region of interest (RoI)-level prototype matching within a two-stage detector. Experiments are conducted on NEU-DET under 1-shot, 3-shot, and 5-shot episode settings, with 5-shot cross-domain evaluation on GC10-DET using target-domain support prototypes. Multi-scale feature-pyramid fusion represents local textures and broader defect morphology, while attention weighting enhances defect-sensitive regions and channels before region proposal network (RPN) proposal generation and RoI Align. Few-shot classification is performed by cosine similarity between RoI features and class prototypes, and training jointly optimizes detection classification, bounding-box regression, region proposal, and Prototype Alignment Loss (PAL). Under the 5-shot NEU-DET setting, PA-MSAN achieves 92.2 ± 0.4% mean average precision (mAP)@0.5, 88.7 ± 0.4% small-object average precision (APsmall), 0.89 ± 0.01 average intersection over union (IoU), and a 4.1 ± 0.2% background false response rate, with 23.4 ms detection time and 18.7 × 10⁶ parameters. Compared with Faster R-CNN, PA-MSAN improves mAP@0.5 by 2.9 percentage points and APsmall by 3.9 percentage points while reducing the background false response rate by 1.7 percentage points. Under the 5-shot target-domain support setting, PA-MSAN achieves 86.4 ± 0.4% mAP@0.5 on GC10-DET, 5.8 percentage points (6.3%) below the corresponding NEU-DET result. The results support region-level prototype alignment for limited-label metallic surface inspection under the evaluated datasets and two-stage configuration.

1. Introduction Surface defect detection is an important part of ensuring product quality and production efficiency in industrial production. With the improvement of intelligent manufacturing and automation level, the demand for high-precision and real-time defect detection is increasing day by day. In complex industrial scenarios, there are numerous types of defects with significant differences in size and shape. At the same time, the background environment is complex, and traditional manual detection is not only inefficient, but also subject to subjective judgment errors [ 1 ]. Therefore, building intelligent systems that can adapt to diverse defect features and achieve reliable classification and detection under few-shot conditions has important academic value and application significance for improving the level of industrial automation [ 2 ]. In industrial production, surface defect detection is challenged by three closely related factors: limited annotated defect samples, large defect-scale variation, and strong background texture interference [ 3 , 4 ]. Scarce samples restrict deep networks from learning stable category-level representations, while scale variation requires multi-scale perception to capture both fine linear defects and larger defect regions [ 5 , 6 ]. In addition, small and irregular defect shapes together with mottled material textures can reduce localization accuracy if the detector relies only on single-level features or shallow feature representations [ 7 , 8 ]. Existing studies address these challenges from complementary directions. Convolutional neural network (CNN)-based detectors provide a basic framework for automated defect localization but remain sensitive to scarce samples and small defects [ 9 , 10 ]. Multi-scale feature fusion and feature-pyramid designs improve sensitivity to defects of different sizes [ 11 , 12 ], while recent few-shot industrial detectors use bidirectional feature fusion and multi-stage proposal refinement to improve localization under limited supervision [ 13 ]. Attention-based methods strengthen defect-related responses and suppress background textures [ 14 , 15 ], and attention-guided few-shot frameworks further use channel-spatial weighting and support-query semantic alignment [ 16 ]. Prototype learning aggregates scarce class features into compact representations and improves inter-class separability [ 17 , 18 ]. Representative few-shot detectors adopt different transfer mechanisms: TFA performs classifier-level fine-tuning [ 19 ], FSRW reweights query features using support information [ 20 ], and Meta R-CNN introduces RoI-level meta features for low-shot detection [ 21 ]. PA-MSAN differs by constructing explicit class prototypes from attention-enhanced support RoI features, comparing RoI embeddings with those prototypes through cosine similarity, and regularizing the shared RoI embedding with Prototype Alignment Loss. This study formulates few-shot industrial surface defect detection as a region-level prototype-alignment problem. PA-MSAN first constructs multi-receptive-field pyramid features to preserve defect information across scales and then applies spatial-channel attention to the fused representation before proposal generation. Support-region features obtained after RoI Align are aggregated into class prototypes, and RoI features are matched to those prototypes in the same cosine-similarity space. RPN proposal learning and bounding-box regression optimize localization, while prototype matching and Prototype Alignment Loss optimize few-shot class discrimination on the shared RoI representation. The resulting architecture links multi-scale representation, attention refinement, region localization, and metric-based classification within one training objective. The main contributions of this paper are as follows: (1) PA-MSAN is proposed to formulate few-shot industrial surface defect detection as an RoI-level prototype-alignment task. Support-set prototypes are constructed from attention-enhanced region features and directly guide RoI classification. Localization is optimized through the shared RoI representation together with RPN and bounding-box regression objectives, thereby coupling region-level metric learning with two-stage detection. (2) A hierarchical feature representation module is developed for the defect detection task by combining feature-pyramid fusion with convolutions of multiple receptive fields. With this design, the detector can describe subtle local textures, medium-scale defect structures, and higher-level semantic information even when only a few support samples are available. (3) A defect-sensitive prototype matching strategy is introduced at the RoI level by using spatial-channel attention together with RoI Align. The attention mechanism helps filter out irrelevant background textures, and the cosine similarity between region features and class prototypes allows the detector to distinguish defect categories more effectively when only a few labeled examples are available. (4) A joint training objective is designed by optimizing detection classification loss, bounding-box regression loss, RPN loss, and Prototype Alignment Loss together. Through this objective, the model learns more accurate region localization and more discriminative feature distributions, with samples from the same class becoming closer and different classes remaining better separated. This helps improve the stability of few-shot detection and supports cross-domain adaptation with limited target-domain supervision. 2. Network design and method implementation This paper proposes PA-MSAN for surface defect detection with scarce labeled samples and substantial scale variation. The network contains three interacting components: multi-scale feature extraction, attention enhancement, and prototype-based region classification, which are optimized jointly with the two-stage detection objectives. Multi-scale feature extraction module To represent industrial defects with substantial scale variation, PA-MSAN combines multi-receptive-field convolution with a feature-pyramid pathway containing top-down fusion and lateral connections [ 28 – 30 ]. The module produces hierarchical feature maps that preserve fine texture details, intermediate defect structures, and broader semantic context [ 31 , 32 ]. Lateral projections align channel dimensions before feature addition, and the resulting pyramid features provide multi-scale inputs for attention refinement, proposal generation, prototype matching, classification, and box regression. The convolution operation can be expressed as: F ( l ) = δ ( W ( l ) * F ( l − 1 ) + b ( l ) ) (3) In Equation (3) , F (l) denotes the feature map of the l -th convolutional layer, F (l−1) denotes the input feature map from the previous layer, W (l) is the convolution kernel, b (l) is the bias term, * denotes convolution, and δ(·) denotes the rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation function. Convolution kernels of 3 × 3, 5 × 5, and 7 × 7 are used at different levels to balance the responses to small and large defects [ 33 , 34 ]. Multi-scale features are integrated through the feature-pyramid fusion mechanism. To make element-wise fusion dimensionally valid when adjacent feature levels have different channel numbers, the lateral feature is first projected to the target channel dimension: P l = ϕ l ( F l ) + Up ( P l + 1 ) (4) In Equation (4) , P l denotes the fused pyramid feature at level l, F l denotes the lateral feature at level l, ϕ l (·) denotes a lateral projection, typically implemented by a 1 × 1 convolution, and Up (·) denotes the upsampling operation used to align spatial resolution. This projection avoids channel-dimensional mismatch before feature addition. Attention mechanism and feature enhancement The hierarchical feature map generated by the multi-scale feature extraction module contains local and global information for defects of different sizes. The attention module applies channel weighting first and spatial weighting second to the fused pyramid feature. The input F p is used to generate channel weights M c ( F p ) ; channel-weighted features F c are then used to generate spatial weights M s ( F c ) . The resulting attention-enhanced feature F a is shared by the RPN, RoI Align, prototype branch, classification head, and box-regression branch [ 37 , 38 ]. Channel attention uses global average pooling and global maximum pooling to summarize the fused pyramid feature and maps the resulting descriptors through a two-layer fully connected network: M c ( F p ) = σ ( W 2 δ ( W 1 GA P ( F p ) ) + W 2 δ ( W 1 GMP ( F p ) ) ) (6) In Equation (6) , M c ( F p ) denotes the channel attention weight generated from the pyramid feature F p . Channel attention strengthens defect-related channels and suppresses background-noise channels. Prototype learning and few-shot classification Under few-shot conditions, a detector must distinguish defect categories from a small number of labeled support images. PA-MSAN constructs class representations from fixed-size RoI features at the region level. Candidate regions are aligned through RoI Align, projected to the prototype embedding space, and aggregated by class. For each class k, the prototype p k is the arithmetic mean of its support-region features and is defined as: p k = 1 | S k | ∑ f i ∈ S k f i (9) In Equation (9) , p k denotes the prototype vector of category k , S k denotes the support feature set of category k , |Sk| is the number of support samples in that category, and f i denotes the region feature vector extracted by RoI Align. RoI Align reduces boundary quantization errors and improves prototype consistency for small defects by producing fixed-size region representations before projection into the embedding space. The matching between features and prototypes is measured using cosine similarity, defined as: s i , k = f i T p k ∥ f i ∥ 2 ∥ p k ∥ 2 (10) In Equation (10) , s i,k ∈ [−1, 1] denotes the cosine similarity between the input region feature f i and the class prototype p k , ‖·‖ 2 denotes the L2 norm, and f i T p k denotes the inner product. The similarity distribution over all class prototypes is used for few-shot defect classification. The cosine-similarity scores are converted to class probabilities by a softmax over the K defect prototypes: P ( y i = k ∣ f i ) = exp ( s i , k ) ∑ j = 1 K exp ( s i , j ) (11) In Equation (11) , the cosine-similarity scores are normalized across the K defect prototypes to obtain the prototype-based class probability for each RoI feature. The resulting probability distribution is used for few-shot class prediction and in the Prototype Alignment Loss. During optimization, PAL increases the similarity of each labeled training RoI embedding derived from the K-shot support images and their augmented views to its ground-truth class prototype while suppressing similarities to competing prototypes [ 41 , 42 ]. Related few-shot metal-defect work has explored adaptive support-feature correlation and hierarchical multi-scale fusion for cross-domain generalization [ 43 ]. L PAL = − 1 N ∑ i = 1 N log P ( y i ∣ f i ) (12) In Equation (12) , L PAL denotes the Prototype Alignment Loss, N is the number of labeled training RoI embeddings included in the loss, and y i is the ground-truth class of embedding f i . The loss is computed from RoIs derived from the K -shot support images and their augmented training views. In PA-MSAN, the prototypes are constructed from RoI-Aligned support-region features, so the metric representation is defined at the same region level used by the detection head. 2.5. Joint optimization and training strategy PA-MSAN uses a joint objective to optimize region classification, box regression, proposal generation, and prototype-space structure within the same training procedure. The total loss is defined as: L joint = λ d L det + λ r L reg + λ RPN L RPN + λ p L PAL (13) In Equation (13) , L joint denotes the total joint loss, L det is the detection classification loss, L reg is the bounding-box regression loss, L RPN is the region proposal loss, and L PAL is the Prototype Alignment Loss. The coefficients λ d , λ r , λ RPN , and λ p weight the four objectives. The detection classification loss L det is cross-entropy on the linear classification logits, L reg is Smooth L1 loss for bounding-box regression, and L RPN combines RPN objectness and proposal-regression losses. L PAL structures the prototype embedding while sharing the upstream RoI representation with the auxiliary linear classifier. Table 1 separates the complete prototype-learning branch from PAL: removing the full prototype-learning branch lowers mAP@0.5 by 7.9 percentage points, while removing PAL with the prototype classifier retained lowers mAP@0.5 by 3.3 points. During inference, the prototype metric head provides the few-shot class decision and the linear classification head remains an auxiliary training branch. The detection classification head computes linear classification logits from the RoI feature as: z i = W f i + b (14) In Equation (14) , f i denotes the RoI feature vector for candidate region i , W and b are the learnable parameters of the auxiliary linear classification head, and z i contains the logits supervised by L d e t . The prototype metric head uses Equations (10) and (11) to compute cosine similarities and normalized prototype class probabilities. The network parameters are updated using Adam optimizer, and the optimization formula is as follows: θ t + 1 = θ t − η m ^ t v ^ t + ϵ (15) In Equation (15) , θ t denotes the network parameters at iteration t, η is the learning rate, m ^ t and v ^ t are the bias-corrected first- and second-moment estimates, and ε is the numerical-stability constant used by Adam. During the training process, the epoch-wise step learning-rate decay strategy is used to control the parameter update amplitude, consistent with the setting that the learning rate decays to 0.95 times its previous value every 10 epochs: η e = η 0 γ n e (16) In Equation (16) , η e denotes the learning rate at epoch e , η 0 denotes the initial learning rate, γ is the decay coefficient, and n e is the number of completed 10-epoch decay intervals. The experiments use γ = 0.95. During the training process, the input features extracted through data augmentation and multi-scale attention modules are simultaneously subjected to detection classification loss, bounding box regression loss, RPN candidate region loss, and PAL constraints. Joint optimization makes the features close to the category prototype in high-dimensional space and ensures consistency among the samples. Experimental datasets and acquisition This experiment used two publicly available industrial surface defect detection datasets, NEU-DET [ 22 , 23 ] and GC10-DET [ 27 ], to train and evaluate the proposed model. NEU-DET is a steel surface defect detection dataset released by Northeastern University and contains six typical defect categories, including Crazing, Scratch, Pitted Surface, Patch, Rolled-in Scale, and Inclusion, with bounding-box annotations. GC10-DET contains ten defect categories (punching, weld line, crescent gap, water spot, oil spot, silk spot, inclusion, rolled pit, crease, and waist folding) with bounding-box annotations and is used to evaluate cross-domain adaptation with limited target-domain supervision in more complex industrial scenarios. NEU-DET images are grayscale steel surface images with a resolution of 200 × 200, whereas GC10-DET contains grayscale metallic surface-defect images with variable resolutions in commonly used releases. For a consistent input setting, all images from both datasets are read or converted into a single-channel grayscale format, resized to 416 × 416, and normalized to the 0–1 interval. Random augmentation, including rotation, horizontal/vertical flipping, scaling, illumination gain and offset, and Gaussian noise injection, is applied only to training and support-set images; validation and test images undergo deterministic resizing and normalization only. Each dataset is divided once into 70% training, 15% validation, and 15% test subsets. For a given run, the 70% training subset is the candidate pool from which exactly K annotated support images per class are sampled. Parameter updates use only these support images and their augmented views. The validation and test subsets remain held out from parameter learning. p i = N i N total (17) In Equation (17) , p i denotes the proportion of samples from class i , N i denotes the number of samples in that class, and N t o t a l is the total sample count. This quantity is used to check category balance before K-shot support sampling. Image normalization and bounding-box transformations are applied consistently with the geometric augmentation operations so that defect annotations remain aligned with the transformed images. Experimental configuration and training settings Experiments are conducted on Ubuntu 20.04 using an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 graphics processing unit (GPU) with 24 GB memory and an Intel Xeon Gold 6338 central processing unit (CPU). CUDA acceleration is used for training and inference. All images are resized to 416 × 416 and normalized to the 0–1 interval before entering the network. Inference-time measurements use batch size 1 with 416 × 416 inputs on the RTX 3090. Detection time includes network forward inference and detection post-processing/NMS and excludes disk input/output (I/O) and input preprocessing. PA-MSAN consists of a multi-scale convolutional backbone with feature-pyramid fusion, spatial and channel attention, an RoI-level prototype branch, RPN proposal generation, and a detection head. The attention-enhanced pyramid representation is used by the RPN and RoI Align stages. The resulting RoI features are processed by the prototype metric head for few-shot class prediction and by the box-regression branch for localization; the auxiliary linear classification head provides the Ldet supervision during training. Training uses mini-batch stochastic optimization with batch size 16. Adam is initialized with a learning rate of 1 × 10 −3 , which is multiplied by 0.95 every 10 epochs. The optimizer uses adaptive first- and second-moment estimates as specified in Table 5 . During inference, the prototype class probability from Equation (11) is combined with the RPN objectness probability to form the final detection confidence: s conf = P ( obj ) · P ( cls ∣ obj ) (18) In Equation (18) , s conf denotes the final detection confidence score, P ( obj ) is the candidate-region objectness probability, and P ( cls ∣ obj ) is the prototype-based conditional class probability. During NMS, IoU is computed between predicted boxes; evaluation IoU is computed between matched predicted and ground-truth boxes. During validation and testing, detections are evaluated at an IoU threshold of 0.5, with an NMS threshold of 0.5 and a confidence threshold of 0.25. Each training run uses a 200-epoch budget, and the checkpoint with the highest validation mAP is used for final test evaluation. A Dropout rate of 0.3 is applied to the fully connected layer during training. Discussion The results characterize PA-MSAN under three coupled conditions in few-shot industrial surface inspection: limited labeled samples, substantial defect-scale variation, and background texture interference. The complete architecture combines multi-scale feature extraction, spatial-channel attention, and RoI-level prototype matching, and the ablations quantify the contribution of each component under the shared 5-shot protocol. The multi-scale feature extractor combines feature-pyramid fusion with 3 × 3, 5 × 5, and 7 × 7 kernels to represent fine textures, intermediate defect structures, and broader spatial context. PA-MSAN reaches 88.7 ± 0.4% APsmall in the 5-shot NEU-DET setting, and removing MSFE lowers mAP@0.5 by 10.7 percentage points. The stacked-3 × 3 comparison in Table 4 further shows that the multi-kernel configuration yields higher mAP@0.5 and APsmall with comparable feature-extractor parameters and lower reported FLOPs. Spatial-channel attention refines the fused feature representation before proposal generation. The complete model reaches 0.89 ± 0.01 average IoU and a 4.1 ± 0.2% background false response rate. Removing spatial attention lowers mAP@0.5 by 5.0 percentage points, and removing channel attention lowers it by 3.8 percentage points. These ablations are consistent with complementary roles for spatial localization and channel-wise feature selection in textured industrial backgrounds. Prototype learning provides the few-shot class representation used by the final metric classifier. Support-region features are averaged into class prototypes and evaluation RoIs are compared with these prototypes by cosine similarity, while PAL regularizes the shared embedding. Removing the complete prototype-learning branch lowers mAP@0.5 by 7.9 percentage points, and removing PAL alone lowers it by 3.3 points. PA-MSAN also records higher 5-shot mAP@0.5 than TFA, Meta R-CNN, and FSRW under the shared evaluation protocol, indicating that the proposed RoI-level prototype formulation is competitive with the evaluated few-shot detectors on NEU-DET. The general-purpose and few-shot-specific baselines provide complementary reference points. Under the 5-shot NEU-DET setting, PA-MSAN reaches 92.2 ± 0.4% mAP@0.5 with a reported detection time of 23.4 ms, compared with 87.6 ± 0.3% for YOLOv5, 89.3 ± 0.4% for Faster R-CNN, 88.4 ± 0.4% for TFA, 86.8 ± 0.5% for Meta R-CNN, and 85.9 ± 0.7% for FSRW. These results describe the accuracy-computation trade-off under the tested implementation and evaluation conditions. Cross-domain evaluation assesses PA-MSAN under a change in dataset and target-class composition. With 5-shot GC10-DET support prototypes, the model reaches 86.4 ± 0.4% mAP@0.5, compared with 92.2 ± 0.4% on NEU-DET. The 5.8-point (6.3% relative) difference reflects the combined change in class semantics, class count, object scale, image distribution, and target-domain adaptation under limited labeled support. The two-stage RPN/RoI Align configuration requires 23.4 ms per image and 18.7 × 10⁶ parameters on the reported platform. Structured pruning [ 46 ], knowledge distillation [ 47 ], and lightweight backbones such as MobileNetV3 [ 48 ] provide direct routes toward lower deployment cost. Evaluation on NEU-DET and GC10-DET covers steel and metallic surface defects; broader validation can extend the analysis to composite surfaces, semiconductor wafers, highly reflective components, and other production environments. Each class is represented by a single arithmetic-mean prototype, while adaptive support-feature correlation [ 43 ] and multi-prototype modeling [ 49 ] provide relevant directions for representing stronger intra-class morphological variation. Recent industrial defect research has highlighted information-density imbalance and heterogeneous local defect characteristics in practical surface inspection scenarios [ 50 ]. Self-supervised pre-training and parameter-efficient adaptation provide additional directions for improving data efficiency [ 51 ]. 6. Conclusion This study evaluates PA-MSAN for few-shot industrial surface defect detection with limited labeled samples, large defect-scale variation, and textured backgrounds. PA-MSAN combines multi-receptive-field feature-pyramid fusion, spatial-channel attention, RoI-level prototype matching, RPN proposal generation, and joint detection/prototype optimization. The evaluation covers controlled 1-shot, 3-shot, and 5-shot NEU-DET experiments together with limited-supervision cross-domain adaptation on GC10-DET. The method constructs class prototypes from attention-enhanced support RoI features and uses cosine similarity for final few-shot classification. RPN and bounding-box regression optimize localization through the same RoI representation, while the auxiliary linear classification head and Prototype Alignment Loss provide classifier-space and metric-space supervision during training. The ablations separately evaluate multi-scale feature extraction, prototype learning, PAL, attention components, attention placement, and the explicit multi-kernel design. Under the 5-shot NEU-DET protocol, PA-MSAN reaches 92.2 ± 0.4% mAP@0.5, 88.7 ± 0.4% APsmall, 0.89 ± 0.01 average IoU, and a 4.1 ± 0.2% background false response rate. Under the same evaluation protocol, it records higher mAP@0.5 than CNN Detector, Proto CNN, Attention CNN, YOLOv5, Faster R-CNN, TFA, Meta R-CNN, and FSRW. The controlled ablations show the largest mAP decrease after removing MSFE, followed by removal of the complete prototype-learning branch, spatial attention, channel attention, and PAL. With 5-shot target-domain GC10-DET support, PA-MSAN reaches 86.4 ± 0.4% mAP@0.5, compared with 92.2 ± 0.4% on NEU-DET. The 5.8-percentage-point (6.3% relative) difference summarizes the cross-dataset result under limited target-domain supervision. The evaluation covers NEU-DET and GC10-DET with the two-stage PA-MSAN configuration. Future work will examine adaptive and multi-prototype aggregation, lighter deployment architectures, and broader validation across industrial materials and imaging conditions.
