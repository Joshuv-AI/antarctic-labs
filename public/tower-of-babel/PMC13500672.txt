Synergy of postural adaptation and exteroception for robust CPG-driven quadrupedal locomotion
Authors: Irfan Tito Kurniawan, Wei Zhu, Dai Owaki, Mitsuhiro Hayashibe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Reinforcement learning (RL)-based controllers for quadrupedal locomotion often learn unnatural, uncoordinated gaits that are unsuitable for real-world deployment, necessitating laborious reward shaping. Central pattern generator (CPG)-RL approaches alleviate these issues by utilizing biologically inspired CPGs to generate rhythmic foot trajectories modulated by learned policies. While previous CPG-RL works have employed postural adaptation and exteroception to modify gait, their exact functional roles and distinct adjustment strategies remain largely unexplored. To systematically investigate the roles of these mechanisms, we augment CPG-RL with exteroception via terrain elevation samples and postural adaptation through direct joint angle corrections. Ablation experiments reveal that while exteroception facilitates energy-efficient anticipatory planning and accelerates balance recovery, its benefits are constrained by the rigidity of CPG modulation. Postural adaptation resolves this limitation, enabling the flexible limb motions required to translate terrain awareness into robust, stable, and accurate locomotion. We observe distinct emergent gait strategies: exteroception drives precise, shorter steps to minimize collisions, whereas postural adaptation reduces step clearance for efficiency, relying on rapid reactions to maintain stability. The resulting synergy yields superior robustness compared to an end-to-end RL baseline, achieves a natural trot without gait-shaping rewards, and generalizes well to out-of-distribution conditions, establishing a foundation for configurable and reliable quadrupedal locomotion.

Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become the dominant approach to quadrupedal locomotion, enabling real-world robots to traverse and maneuver through challenging terrains with remarkable robustness1–4. Controllers using this approach rely on RL policies trained to directly predict target angles for each of the robot’s joints. However, the numerous degrees of freedom (DOF) make them prone to converging on uncoordinated and unnatural gaits that are inefficient and unsuitable for real-world deployment. This issue necessitates complex gait-shaping reward functions, such as those promoting longer swings or smoother actions1,2, which require significant effort to design and tune.

Animal locomotion is driven by central pattern generators (CPG), spinal neural circuits that generate rhythmic patterns of neural activity independently of rhythmic inputs5. CPGs, modeled as differential equations, are often employed in quadrupedal locomotion controllers to generate stable, rhythmic foot trajectories that can be modulated via low-dimensional control inputs, thereby simplifying the control problem5. Conventional controllers typically use handcrafted strategies to coordinate CPG trajectories based on physical interactions or sensory feedback6–11, enabling natural gaits capable of handling slopes and small steps. However, their reliance on extensive manual parameter tuning or specific morphological dynamics limits their generalizability to diverse, unseen complex terrains. To overcome these limitations, RL policies have been employed to modulate CPG trajectories in a framework known as CPG-RL12–17. Compared to end-to-end RL approaches, CPG-RL facilitates learning of natural, transferable, and user-configurable gaits via simpler and easier-to-tune reward functions12,14. Recent works continue to expand the capabilities of this framework, enabling feats such as dynamic transitions across diverse animal gaits18 and robust locomotion under hardware faults19.

Animals recover from unforeseen disturbances and negotiate uneven terrains by modifying their gait through two complementary pathways. While spinal CPGs drive the foundational rhythmic patterns for locomotion20, precise gait modifications require descending supraspinal signals acting in parallel with these spinal circuits21,22. This supraspinal control executes postural adaptation, utilizing muscle activations via descending pathways like the reticulospinal and vestibulospinal tracts, to maintain balance in response to perturbations or terrain constraints23. Crucially, these adaptations rely on integrating distinct sensory modalities, namely proprioception for immediate body state estimation, and exteroception to anticipate terrain variations and minimize energy expenditure22,24,25.

CPG-based controllers have emulated postural adaptation by adjusting their trajectories through joint torque10,26, angle1,17,19,27, or foot position offsets14,15. Concurrently, they have adopted exteroception by utilizing inputs such as optical flow28, terrain elevation samples13,16, or distance to terrain features14,15. By combining postural adaptation and exteroception, CPG-RL approaches have demonstrated success in obstacle avoidance13, gap crossing14,15, and uneven terrain traversal16. However, the exact contributions of these mechanisms to locomotion behavior and performance require further investigation. Furthermore, while postural adaptation and exteroception offer the capacity to handle unexpected disturbances, the actual performance of CPG-RL approaches under such conditions remains largely unevaluated12–17.

This work adopts the CPG-RL paradigm to systematically investigate the roles of postural adaptation and exteroception in CPG-driven locomotion. Our implementation emulates biological motor control, where supraspinal centers coordinate movement by simultaneously tuning spinal CPGs and providing descending motor commands. By using a single RL policy to represent these higher-level centers, our controller captures this natural coordination, with the policy utilizing exteroception to modulate CPG rhythms while concurrently issuing direct joint angle corrections for postural adaptation. We test our controller and its ablated variants in velocity tracking on uneven terrains under unexpected disturbances, scenarios requiring both proactive and reactive gait adaptations. We analyze the quantitative results to clarify the individual and combined contributions of both mechanisms to locomotion in terms of robustness, energy efficiency, stability, balance recovery, and tracking accuracy. Moreover, we characterize the emergent gait strategies driven by each mechanism, revealing how they adapt to complex terrains and external perturbations. Finally, we assess the generalizability of the framework through sim-to-sim stress tests with out-of-distribution friction and masses, and validate the contributions of the CPG backbone through comparison with a standard end-to-end RL baseline.

While recent end-to-end RL work has analyzed sensory importance and shown that minimal proprioception is sufficient for robust locomotion29, their analysis omitted anticipatory exteroception, such as vision or terrain elevation samples. We complement these findings by systematically characterizing the functional roles of anticipatory exteroception and postural adaptation in a CPG-driven locomotion framework. Mirroring the unrestricted nature of biological descending commands, our postural adaptation adjusts foot trajectories in all directions, expanding upon the one or two-dimensional offsets employed in prior works14–16. Rather than low-level, hard-wired reflexes, this postural adaptation represents learned, high-level corrective descending commands jointly optimized with the rhythmic gait, emerging naturally without specialized reward shaping.

Results

Our experiments followed an ablation study methodology, comparing four variants of the control architecture: Base (CPG-RL only), Perceptive (CPG-RL with exteroception), Postural (CPG-RL with postural adaptation), and Full (CPG-RL with both mechanisms). Exteroception is implemented by including terrain elevation samples in the RL policy’s observation space (Fig. 1), while postural adaptation is realized through additional joint angle-space offset actions in its action space. Unless otherwise stated, all policy variants were trained using eight fixed, independent random seeds, with results reported as the pooled mean and standard deviation. For evaluation, we aggregated 1024 runs per seed, yielding a total of 8192 runs per variant. Robust locomotion on uneven terrains

Each ablated variant was evaluated by having it track a forward velocity command of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v^*_{b, x} = 0.6$$\end{document} \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\textrm{m} / \textrm{s}}$$\end{document} on four terrains: a flat terrain and three uneven terrains depicted in Fig. 1. Test terrains were newly and randomly sampled from the training distribution’s highest difficulty level, ensuring they were unseen but in-distribution. Tests were conducted with and without unexpected disturbances, implemented as lateral pushes of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v_{b, y} = 1.0$$\end{document} \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\textrm{m} / \textrm{s}}$$\end{document} applied to the robot’s base every five seconds. We opted for instantaneous velocity changes to ensure a consistent and reproducible post-impact state, as force-based disturbances are highly sensitive to the robot’s instantaneous pose, contact configuration, and effective inertia at the moment of impact. A run was deemed successful if the robot did not fall for 30 seconds. With this experiment, we aim to study the roles of exteroception and postural adaptation in locomotion, rather than out-of-distribution generalization. Results are detailed in Table 1.

In the absence of disturbances, the Base variant achieved over 81% success rate on all terrains, aligning with recent evidence that minimal proprioception enables robust locomotion29. However, Base exhibited comparatively poor energy efficiency and body stability, along with intense limb motions, as indicated by its high cost of transports (COT), mean absolute body angular velocities, and joint accelerations. The Postural variant demonstrated the benefits of postural adaptation, which allows for flexible and rapidly adaptable foot trajectories. It outperformed Base in nearly all metrics across all terrains, achieving superior robustness, energy efficiency, and body stability with reduced limb motion intensity (p < 0.0006). The sole exception was the success rate on Discrete terrain, where the improvement was not statistically significant (p > 0.4). The Discrete terrain features distinct vertical steps that demand precise and strategic foot placements to avoid stumbling and entrapment, a challenge that postural adaptation alone cannot fully address.

Contrary to expectation, the Perceptive variant achieved success rates similar to Base on most uneven terrains (p > 0.75 for Discrete and Wave), despite having access to terrain structure information. While exteroception did improve overall energy efficiency (p < 0.002) and body stability on most terrains (p < 0.006, except Wave), the policy’s capacity to exploit this information was constrained by the rigid nature of CPG modulation. This limitation is evident when comparing Perceptive to Full, which achieved over 98% success rates across all terrains by combining exteroception and postural adaptation (p < 0.009 for all uneven terrains). This implies that while exteroception provides terrain awareness, postural adaptation is essential to translate this awareness into successful actions. Comparing Postural to Full, both equipped with postural adaptation, further isolates the benefits of exteroception. With the addition of terrain awareness, the Full variant exhibited improved body stability across all terrains (p < 0.002), albeit with increased limb motion intensity (p < 0.01). This advantage became more pronounced on challenging terrains, where it significantly enhanced energy efficiency (p < 0.04 on Rough and Discrete) and success rates (p < 0.001 on Discrete).

While disturbances did not impact the Base variant’s success on flat terrain, they significantly degraded its robustness, energy efficiency, and body stability on uneven terrains. Disturbance rejection is inherently more challenging on uneven terrains, as the robot must account for terrain geometry while executing recovery steps. This explains why the Perceptive variant generally maintained its performance better than Base under disturbance, achieving significantly higher success rates (p < 0.03) on Rough and Discrete terrains. Comparing performance degradation under disturbances confirms that postural adaptation significantly aids disturbance rejection. With its improved body stability and rapidly adjustable foot trajectories, the Postural variant maintained robust performance on Rough and Wave terrains, experiencing only 11% and 8% drops in success rates, respectively, contrasting the 32% and 44% drops observed in Base. However, Postural still suffered a substantial 21% drop on Discrete terrain. This indicates that while postural adaptation effectively mitigates continuous terrain variations, it struggles to reject disturbances on terrains requiring foothold precision.

The Full variant leverages the synergy between exteroception and postural adaptation for superior disturbance rejection. Specifically, exteroception identifies safe footholds during recovery, while postural adaptation allows the policy to reach them precisely and rapidly. As a result, Full maintained over 93% success rate on all terrains, limiting the performance degradation to only 5% compared to the disturbance-free case. Ultimately, the Full variant achieved the highest success rates, energy efficiency, and body stability across all test scenarios, confirming that robust locomotion requires both proactive planning and flexible execution.

Gait characterization and locomotion behavior

To evaluate nominal gait characteristics, we commanded each ablated variant to track a forward velocity command of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v^*_{b, x} = 0.6$$\end{document} \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\textrm{m} / \textrm{s}}$$\end{document} on flat terrain. Front foot trajectories were recorded over 5-second windows across all runs, yielding more than 200,000 gait cycles per variant. The resulting mean foot trajectory cycles and their standard deviations are plotted in Fig. 2, while the corresponding quantitative metrics are detailed in Table 2. Step frequency is omitted from Table 2 because all variants exhibited a similar trot gait with statistically indistinguishable frequencies.

Comparing blind variants (Base and Postural) to their sighted counterparts (Perceptive and Full) reveals that exteroception alters the gait strategy from conservative, reactive probing to precise, proactive targeting. Access to exteroception significantly reduced step lengths (p < 0.002) and increased swing trajectory squareness (p < 0.001), yielding shorter strides with steeper ascent and descent. Blind variants adopt longer strides to probe for ground contact and maximize the likelihood of finding a foothold. They use rounded swing trajectories that graze over potential obstacles rather than stomping down, which would increase the risk of destabilizing impacts. In contrast, sighted variants exhibited shorter strides to enable precise foot placement on perceived safe regions, thereby improving stability by minimizing moment arms of ground reaction forces. Their swing trajectories have steeper ascent and descent to clear obstacle edges, pass over them, and land precisely onto the target, reducing the risk of stumbling. Contrary to intuition, the shorter, steeper nominal gait of the sighted variants is more energy-efficient than that of their blind counterparts, as indicated by the lower COT in the flat-terrain, disturbance-free scenario in Table 1.

Comparison of variants with and without postural adaptation (Postural against Base, Full against Perceptive) demonstrates that this mechanism allows for more efficient, stable locomotion. Access to postural adaptation significantly reduced step height (p < 0.002) and increased duty factor (p < 0.0008), indicating a longer stance phase. By design, variants without postural adaptation were constrained to the user-configured step clearance of 0.12 m. In contrast, variants equipped with postural adaptation exhibited up to 27% lower step clearances, trading height for energy efficiency by reducing leg-lift costs, albeit at a higher risk of stumbling. However, their ability to rapidly adapt foot trajectories proved sufficient to justify this risk, as evidenced by their superior success rates and body stability on uneven terrains (Table 1). In the sighted Full variant, postural adaptation significantly rounded the swing trajectory (p < 0.002), further improving energy efficiency.

These behavioral differences are clearly visible on Discrete terrain (Fig. 3). Lacking terrain awareness, blind variants often stumbled or became entrapped, whereas sighted variants proactively modified their gait to target safe footholds. To quantify this behavior, we analyzed horizontal foot impact forces during the same Discrete terrain traversals evaluated in Table 1, with metrics detailed in Table 3. Since blind policies must make physical contact with vertical step faces to detect them, they are expected to experience higher collision forces. Consistent with this hypothesis, sighted variants experienced significantly lower collision forces than their blind counterparts across both disturbance conditions (p łe 0.05), confirming that exteroception effectively minimizes impacts through proactive adaptation. We further found that the effect of postural adaptation on impact forces depended on exteroception. The Postural variant experienced higher forces than Base due to its reduced clearance, whereas Full achieved the lowest forces among all variants. This confirms that Full leverages postural adaptation for terrain-aware trajectory adjustments that mitigate collision risks despite the lower clearance.

Response to unexpected disturbances

To isolate disturbance response characteristics from terrain constraints, we applied lateral pushes of varying magnitudes while the robot tracked a forward velocity command of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v^*_{b, x} = 0.6$$\end{document} \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\textrm{m} / \textrm{s}}$$\end{document} on flat terrain. We evaluated responses to disturbance magnitudes both within the training distribution (łe 1.0 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\textrm{m} / \textrm{s}}$$\end{document}) and outside of it (1.25 and 1.5 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\textrm{m} / \textrm{s}}$$\end{document}). Mean disturbance response metrics and their standard deviations for successful recoveries are shown in Fig. 4. In this experiment, all ablated variants maintained over 98% success rates across all push magnitudes, save for Postural, which saw a reduced success rate of 76 ± 31% at the strongest magnitude. Notably, the specific disturbance rejection strategies we observed emerged without explicit reward incentives for minimizing recovery time. Instead, policies were driven solely by penalties on joint energy consumption and body velocities in the roll, pitch, and vertical axes.

Comparison of recovery times reveals that exteroception accelerates recovery, with sighted variants exhibiting consistently faster recovery times than their blind counterparts. This advantage stems from the more robust nominal gait patterns that emerge when policies have access to exteroception. Interestingly, postural adaptation alone does not guarantee faster recovery, as evidenced by the slower response of Postural compared to Base, likely due to the time required to stabilize its larger limb motions. However, combining it with exteroception eliminated this drawback, enabling the Full variant to achieve the fastest recoveries across all push magnitudes.

Variants with postural adaptation consistently exhibited higher joint motion range increase than their non-adaptable counterparts, confirming that this mechanism enables more aggressive recovery actions. These actions translated directly to improved stability, evidenced by lower peak body roll deviations under in-distribution pushes. However, the performance of Postural degraded on out-of-distribution pushes, exhibiting reduced success rates and higher peak body roll deviations than Perceptive. Its nominal gait, characterized by reduced clearance and extended stance phases, is optimized for energy-efficient recovery from in-distribution pushes but lacks the clearance margin to handle stronger, unseen disturbances. Conversely, Full achieved the best stability across all magnitudes while using less joint motion than Postural. The higher clearance and superior inherent robustness of its nominal gait, enabled by exteroception, allowed for corrections that were more precise than reactive adaptation alone.

In our setup, exteroception provides the vertical displacement of terrain points relative to the robot’s base. While these experiments were conducted on flat terrain, exteroception still yields uniform relative body height information that is unavailable through proprioception alone. To verify whether policies actively rely on this feedback during recovery, we additionally ablated the Perceptive variant by freezing its terrain elevation inputs to a constant nominal value (Supplementary Table S1). Compared to the original Perceptive variant, this ablated version exhibited no statistically significant changes in recovery time, joint motion range increase, or peak body roll deviation across push magnitudes (all p > 0.1). As the only differences between sighted and blind variants are this information and their emergent gait strategies, the improved disturbance rejection capacity of sighted variants must be driven primarily by the superior baseline robustness of their nominal gait.

Postural adaptation mechanism design

Prior work typically applies offsets in foot position-space, modifying CPG trajectories before inverse kinematics. In contrast, we implemented postural adaptation as direct joint angle-space offsets to prioritize implementation simplicity, replacing the need for foot position reachability validation with straightforward joint limit clamping. To validate this design, we compared both approaches on forward velocity tracking on flat terrain (baseline) and Discrete terrain with unexpected disturbances (hardest scenario), mirroring the setup in Table 1. We used the Full variant and conducted 4096 evaluation runs across four seeds, with results detailed in Table 5. Both approaches demonstrated similar performance across most metrics in both test scenarios, with paired tests showing no statistically significant differences (p > 0.1), except for body stability on Discrete terrain where foot position offsets were marginally superior (p < 0.02). We therefore adopt joint angle offsets for their implementation advantages while delivering comparable performance.

Sim-to-sim robustness

To assess generalization beyond the training distribution, we stress-tested the Full variant on the hardest scenario (Discrete terrain with unexpected disturbances) with out-of-distribution friction coefficients and payloads. We reduced the static and dynamic friction coefficients to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _s / \mu _d = 0.4/0.3$$\end{document} (half the training minimum) and applied 6 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{kg}$$\end{document} payloads (double the training maximum), equivalent to half the robot’s nominal mass. As detailed in Table 6, our policy demonstrated robust generalization, maintaining success rates above 87% and preserving body stability under out-of-distribution payloads, albeit with an expected decline in energy efficiency and tracking accuracy. Interestingly, slippery conditions led to marginally improved success rates and energy efficiency even under increased mass, while only slightly degrading body stability. This implies that high friction is not a prerequisite for robustness. Overall, these results indicate that the Full variant generalizes robustly to physical parameters well beyond its training distribution, establishing a strong foundation for successful sim-to-real transfer.

Contributions of the CPG backbone

To isolate the contribution of the rhythmic CPG backbone, we compared the Full variant against a standard end-to-end RL baseline, namely Isaac Lab’s default Unitree A1 locomotion controller30. This baseline shares the same sensory inputs but operates via direct joint angle control without CPGs, functionally analogous to relying solely on postural adaptation. For a fair comparison, the baseline was trained with identical terrains, curricula, domain randomization, command ranges, observation noise, and exteroception specifications. We evaluate forward velocity tracking on flat terrain and on Discrete terrain with and without unexpected disturbances, following the setup in Table 1. Table 7 summarizes performance metrics for the baseline and our framework, configured at both the highest (Full-High) and lowest (Full-Low) step clearance settings, evaluated over 4096 runs across four seeds.

Full-High achieved significantly higher success rates than the baseline on uneven terrain across disturbance conditions (p < 0.03 for both), while the baseline achieved significantly superior energy efficiency (p < 0.05) and marginally better tracking (p łe 0.05). While the baseline’s low-clearance, low-frequency, long-strided gait enabled this superior efficiency, it compromised stability due to the increased risk of collision. This inherent trade-off between efficiency and robustness is further evidenced by Full-Low, which yielded improved flat-terrain COT but reduced success rates on uneven terrain compared to Full-High. However, even in this low-clearance configuration, Full-Low maintained a significantly higher success rate than the baseline in the most challenging scenario (p < 0.03). Additionally, our framework learned a near-perfect trot (50.8% duty factor) without gait-shaping rewards, whereas the baseline converged to an imperfect, stance-dominant trot (60.9% duty factor) despite extensive reward shaping (Fig. 6). By offloading the fundamental gait cycle, the CPG backbone allows the policy to focus exteroception and postural adaptation on gait modulation and corrections for robust, configurable, and natural locomotion.

Extension to stairs traversal

To assess the framework’s adaptability to complex geometries, we evaluated its capacity to traverse stairs. As this task inherently requires the synergy of both exteroception and postural adaptation, ablated variants lacking either mechanism are incapable of robust traversal and were consequently excluded. Therefore, we trained only the Full variant to traverse stairs without altering the reward function, limiting architectural changes to an expanded exteroceptive range of 1.6 × 1.0 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{m}$$\end{document} and an increased long short-term memory (LSTM) layer hidden size of 512. Discussion

This work systematically investigated the functional contributions of postural adaptation and exteroception to CPG-driven locomotion. Consistent with the cortical control of visually guided walking22,24,25, our results demonstrate that exteroception facilitates energy-efficient anticipatory planning. Conversely, the role of postural adaptation in improving body stability through adaptable and intense limb motions is consistent with theories on the integration of spinal circuits with descending supraspinal signals for reactive gait adaptations21,31. Interestingly, we found that the synergy between these two mechanisms is essential for handling terrain variations and rejecting unexpected disturbances. While exteroception provides terrain awareness, its benefits are constrained by the rigid nature of standard CPG modulation. Postural adaptation resolves this limitation, enabling the flexible foot trajectories required to translate this awareness into robust, energy-efficient, and stable locomotion.

These mechanisms also dictate the emergent gait strategies adopted by the policy. We observed that sighted variants adopt shorter steps with steeper swing trajectories to precisely target safe regions, leading to improved stability and reduced collisions, whereas blind variants rely on conservative, long-strided probing gaits to find footholds. Furthermore, variants equipped with postural adaptation naturally converged to lower step clearances for improved energy efficiency, relying on rapid reactions to mitigate the increased collision risks.

Under disturbances, exteroception facilitates rapid and efficient recovery by identifying safe footholds on uneven terrain and by enabling the emergence of an inherently robust nominal gait. Conversely, postural adaptation enables the aggressive limb motions necessary to maintain stability during high-magnitude pushes. While postural adaptation alone can prolong recovery times due to these large limb motions, exteroception mitigates this latency, resulting in the fastest and most stable responses to both in-distribution and out-of-distribution disturbances.

Comparison against a standard end-to-end RL baseline reveals that the rhythmic priors provided by CPGs are crucial for focusing exteroception and postural adaptation on gait modulation and adjustments, resulting in superior robustness. These priors also naturally yielded a near-perfect diagonal trot, whereas the baseline converged to an imperfect, stance-dominant trot despite extensive reward shaping. Building on this foundation, the Full variant exhibited robust sim-to-sim generalization, maintaining stability despite drastically increased mass and reduced friction, establishing a strong foundation for sim-to-real deployment. This versatility is further confirmed by the framework’s capacity to learn complex traversal tasks without reward alterations, achieving robust performance on stairs under disturbances. Although our experiments focused on the trot gait, the benefits of exteroception for anticipatory planning and postural adaptation for dynamic stability are gait-agnostic and thus would extend to other locomotion patterns.

By isolating the roles of postural adaptation and exteroception in CPG-driven locomotion, this work lays the foundation for further developments on configurable and reliable quadrupedal locomotion. Looking forward, future research could explore the integration of neuromorphic computation to enable real-time, energy-efficient control inspired by biological neural architectures. Prior work has shown that combining spiking neural networks with deep RL can generate energy-efficient gait patterns32, suggesting new possibilities for neuromorphic implementations in combination with deep RL.

Methods

Our control architecture, which incorporates both exteroception and postural adaptation, is depicted in Fig. 8a . The core architecture is based on CPG-RL12, where an RL policy modulates CPG parameters based on proprioception, body velocity commands, past actions, and CPG states. To enable rapid and precise gait modifications, the policy performs direct joint angle corrections, emulating the postural adaptation in animals. These corrections are subsequently smoothed and summed with the CPG-generated joint angle targets. Furthermore, to facilitate terrain structure-aware decision-making, the policy is provided with exteroceptive information in the form of sparse terrain elevation samples. In the real world, this information would be accessible from onboard lidars, depth cameras, or by querying prebuilt elevation maps.

Postural adaptation

As shown in Eqs. (4–6), the CPG-generated foot positions depend on the oscillation phase θ. Since θ cannot change instantaneously, with its rate of change limited to ω, the robot’s ability to rapidly adapt its CPG-generated foot trajectories is limited. Additionally, the z-axis position follows a constant pattern, with the policy capable of modulating only its rate of change and not its amplitude. To address these design consequences, the CPG-generated joint angle targets are summed with joint angle corrections from the postural adaptation.

The postural adaptation provides joint angle-space corrections \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q_{\text {cor}_{i,j}}$$\end{document} for each joint j of each limb i following:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \ddot{q}_{\text {cor}_{i,j}} = a \left( \frac{a}{4} \left( q^*_{\text {cor}_{i,j}} - q_{\text {cor}_{i,j}} \right) - \dot{q}_{\text {cor}_{i,j}} \right) , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where a = 150 is a positive constant convergence factor. Similar to the CPG oscillation equations, this equation drives the value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q_{\text {cor}_{i,j}}$$\end{document} to match the desired joint angle corrections \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q^*_{\text {cor}_{i,j}}$$\end{document} provided by the policy. This equation smooths out the joint angle corrections and prevents erratic foot trajectories, eliminating the need for dedicated reward functions during policy training. Empirically, we observed that policies trained without this equation tend to learn unnatural, suboptimal gaits. Unlike prior studies that employ CPG trajectory corrections in foot position-space14–16, we opted for direct corrections in the joint configuration space. As validated in the Results section, this design applies corrections after inverse kinematics, allowing us to enforce kinematic validity through simple joint limit clamping while avoiding complex foot position reachability checks.

Other details

We conducted simulations using the NVIDIA Isaac Sim simulator with the Isaac Lab robot learning framework33 and the Unitree A1 quadruped. The physics simulation was updated at 1 kHz while the RL policy operated at 100 Hz. To ensure trajectory smoothness and numerical stability, the CPG and postural adaptation internal states were numerically integrated for 10 iterations during each policy step. The policy was implemented as an LSTM34 layer with 256 hidden units, followed by two fully connected layers with 256 and 128 hidden units each, with exponential linear unit (ELU) activations applied after each layer. It was trained using the proximal policy optimization (PPO) algorithm35, with the same hyperparameters as in12 except for the lower entropy coefficient of 0.002, chosen to facilitate convergence. We used the reward functions shown in Table 8 during training, penalizing command tracking errors, velocities in undesired directions, and joint efforts. We penalized joint power rather than torque or acceleration to directly minimize energy consumption while allowing high-torque or high-acceleration actions when they are justified, such as during stance phases or disturbance recovery. No dedicated reward terms were used to train the joint angle corrections.

We used fixed initial phase differences between the CPGs of each limb to encourage a trot-like gait. Although this study focuses on trotting, the framework extends to other gaits such as walk, pace, or gallop, by simply modifying these phase offsets or introducing coupling between the CPGs. These CPG-related randomizations were performed to train a policy capable of smoothly transitioning between different, user-defined CPG gait parameters. New velocity commands were sampled every 8 seconds, and pushes were randomly applied to the robot’s base every 8 to 12 s. These pushes were implemented by instantaneously offsetting the body velocity in a random direction, with the magnitude linearly increasing from 0.0 to 1.0 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\textrm{m} / \textrm{s}}$$\end{document} over the course of training. The environment resets if the robot’s base makes contact with the terrain or if the robot survives for 20 s.

Evaluation metrics

We used the cost of transport (COT) as a measure of energy efficiency. It relates energy consumption to the distance traveled by the robot, which can be expressed as8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {COT} = \frac{\sum ^{T}_{t = 0} \sum ^{J}_{j = 0} |\tau _{t, j} \dot{q}_{t, j} |}{mg \sum ^{T}_{t = 0} |v_{b,*,t} |}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where m is the robot’s mass, g is the gravity acceleration, T and J are the number of time steps and joints, τ and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\dot{q}$$\end{document} are the torque and angular velocity of the robot’s joints, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v_{b, *}$$\end{document} is the body velocity in the desired direction. A lower COT corresponds to higher energy efficiency. To assess locomotion quality, we measured body stability using the mean absolute body angular velocity in the roll and pitch axes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline{|\omega _{b,xy} |}$$\end{document} and limb motion intensity using the mean absolute joint acceleration \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline{|\ddot{q} |}$$\end{document}. Command tracking performance was evaluated using the mean absolute error (MAE) between the desired and actual body velocities.

We characterized gait strategies using standard step geometry (height \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_{\text {step}}$$\end{document}, length \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$l_{\text {step}}$$\end{document}, and frequency \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_{\text {step}}$$\end{document}), alongside two specialized metrics. To quantify trajectory squareness, we calculated the percentage of the gait cycle where the foot remains above 80% of maximum clearance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_{z>0.8h}$$\end{document}. This temporal metric serves as a proxy for the steepness of vertical ascent and descent, as a trajectory with steep vertical movements reaches its peak height earlier and maintains it longer. Consequently, this metric allows us to evaluate the policy’s strategy to minimize the risk of foot stumbling or entrapment on terrain features. We also measured the mean norm of horizontal foot impact forces \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline{F_{xy}}$$\end{document} against vertical terrain features as a proxy for stumbling or foot entrapment.

Finally, we defined three metrics to quantify the response to unexpected disturbances. Recovery time measures the duration required for the robot to resume velocity tracking in the direction of the push. Joint motion range increase is defined as the increase in mean peak-to-peak joint angles during the 1-second window after a push relative to nominal walking, representing the aggressiveness of the recovery motion. Similarly, peak body roll deviation is the maximum absolute body roll during the same 1-second window minus the root-mean-square roll of normal walking, isolating the instability caused by the push.
