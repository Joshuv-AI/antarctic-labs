 Task 1.6 – Three-Dimensional Printing of All-Solid-State Lithium Batteries
 (Jianchao Ye, Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory)

Objectives. The project has two primary objectives: (1) down select three-dimensional (3D) printing and
post-processing approaches for solid-state electrolyte (SSE) / cathode integration, and (2) understand battery
failure mechanisms via ex situ and in situ characterization.
Impact. The adoption of a thin separator layer, thick cathode structure, and metallic lithium anode will lead to
electric vehicle batteries with > 350 Wh/kg energy density for increased mileage per charge. The higher ionic
conductivity with suppression of lithium dendrite growth will allow high critical current densities for
fast-charging applications. The improved electrode/electrolyte contact will increase battery cycle life for
long-term service.
Approach. The technical approaches include advanced manufacturing based on 3D printing and related
techniques, ex situ / in situ characterizations, and battery testing. Direct-ink writing and projection micro
stereolithography (PµSL) 3D-printing techniques will be employed to fabricate thin-film SSEs (< 100 µm),
gradient SSEs, and 3D interfaces for battery performance evaluation. Three approaches, including sintering-
free, hybrid, and co-sintering, will be investigated. The knowledge obtained from studying these approaches is
transferable and complementary to each technique.
Out-Year Goals. The long-term vision of the team is to 3D-print all components of the all-solid-state lithium
battery (ASSLB) to facilitate the scale-up of ASSLB manufacturing. In this project, the team will tackle the
issues emerging from integrating solid electrolyte with electrodes. The project goal is to demonstrate a
successful 3D-printing approach to integrate SSE into electrodes and show reasonable capacity retention
(> 80%) after 300 cycles at current density ≥ 1 mA/cm2.
Collaborations. The team will work closely with a computational partner (Task 3.6 led by B. Wood) to better
understand battery failure mechanisms and design new battery architectures and chemistries for
performance improvement.

 Milestones

1. Successful printing of a 3D porous separator for 250 Wh/kg gravimetric energy density design, with
 satisfying porosity and mechanical/thermal stabilities. (Q1, FY 2025; Completed)
2. Achieving conductivity > 10-4 S/cm at room temperature along with good stability with lithium anode and
 LiFePO4 or Ni-Mn-Co cathode for the polymer electrolyte (PE). (Q2, FY 2025; Completed)
3. Successful printing of a 3D porous separator to further increase the gravimetric energy density to
 300 Wh/kg, with improved chemical/mechanical/thermal stabilities. (Q3, FY 2025; Completed)
4. Integrate the PE into the printed porous separator while maintaining > 10-4 S/cm at room-temperature
 conductivity. (Q4, FY 2025; On schedule)

 Task 1.6 – Ye, LLNL

 Progress Report

In FY25 Q3, the team continued to develop and characterize three-dimensional porous HDDA membranes
(3DMs) for interdigitated solid-state batteries. Figure 1 shows optical and X-ray tomography images of the
resulting 3DMs. A 1-mm-wide edge was added in design for easier sample handling. Defects were minimized
by using fresh photoresist and implementing post-UV and thermal curing. Based on measurements of the dried
samples’ actual geometry, the energy densities of single-stack pouch cells could reach 321, 365, and 387 mAh/g
for 210, 420, and 630 µm fin length designs, respectively. Multi-stack pouch cells can further improve energy
densities by another 10-30%. A summary is presented in Table 1. Due to the ultra-thick interdigitated electrode
design, the projected areal capacity can reach 7, 12, and 17 mAh/cm2. However, the structured 3DM area is 2.3,
3.6, and 4.9 times larger than the projected area, resulting in a true areal capacity of about 3.1-3.5 mAh/cm2.
The actual performance of the three designs will be tested once cathode and anode infilling is complete.

 Task 1.6 – Ye, LLNL
As shown in Q2 report, the chemical and thermal stabilities of the HDDA membranes are satisfactory. Further
improving the mechanical properties of porous HDDA 3DMs can help prevent stress-related defects. The team
measured the mechanical properties and behaviors of the porous HDDA printed under different light intensities.
Using light intensities from 60 to 220 (out of a maximum of 255), the porous HDDA density increased from
0.53 to 0.74 g/cm3, attributed to the improved degree of polymerization (Figure 2A). Correspondingly, Young’s
modulus increased from 24 to 159 MPa, and yield strength from 1.2 to 5.4 MPa (Figure 2A). Currently, to
maximize the ionic conductivity of the solid polymer electrolyte (SPE)/HDDA composite, the lowest intensity
of 60 is chosen for 3DM printing. Post-UV and thermal curing were found to be effective in improving
mechanical properties by increasing cross-link density while maintaining porosity. With post-UV curing,
samples can be dried without external constraints, providing clear evidence of increased mechanical robustness.

Figure 2. Mechanical testing. (a) Compression testing results on porous HDDA cubes printed using light
intensities from 60 to 220 (out of a maximum of 255). (b) Cylindrical punch compression on the printed 3DMs.
(c) Optical images of the 3DM after 30% compression.

210 µm-fin-length 3DMs were compressed using a 7.8 mm-in-diameter cylinder punch. Load-displacement
curves are shown in Figure 2B. A smooth transition from elastic to plastic deformation is observed at an
approximate load of 20 N. After compressing to 30% strain (60 µm), the 3DM still maintains its shape, with no
apparent damage observed by optical imaging (Figure 2C). This demonstrates promising processibility for
electrode integration.
