Regulatory role of IRF2 in proliferation and invasion of nasopharyngeal carcinoma and its underlying mechanism
Authors: Chaofen Zhao, Jiayu Song, Xunyan Luo, Yue Chen, Feng Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryIRF2 is an interferon regulatory factor with context-dependent roles in cancer. We examined IRF2 expression and function in nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) using immunohistochemistry, immunofluorescence, western blot, and functional assays in cell lines and a xenograft model. IRF2 was upregulated and predominantly nuclear in NPC, correlating with advanced T stage and higher EBV DNA load. ROC analysis indicated diagnostic value (AUC = 0.837). IRF2 overexpression promoted proliferation, migration, invasion, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and suppressed apoptosis, whereas knockdown inhibited these phenotypes and reduced tumor growth in vivo. Mechanistically, IRF2 activated the Wnt/β-catenin pathway by upregulating β-catenin and facilitating its nuclear translocation. These findings establish IRF2 as an oncogenic driver in NPC and suggest the IRF2/Wnt/β-catenin axis as a potential therapeutic target.

Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is an epithelial carcinoma predominantly originating from the pharyngeal recess of the nasopharynx, with high incidence in Southeast Asia and Southern China. According to the international agency for research on cancer, approximately 120,000 new NPC cases were reported globally in 2022.1 Studies have reported that 20%–30% of NPC patients develop distant metastases within 5 years after treatment,2 while 10%–20% experience local recurrence.3,4 The 5-year survival rate for patients receiving re-irradiation after recurrence is less than 40%.5 Local recurrence and progression remain major challenges in NPC management. Therefore, in-depth investigation of the molecular mechanisms underlying tumor cell proliferation and invasion, and the identification of potential therapeutic targets based on these mechanisms, have become crucial research directions in NPC treatment.

Tumor development involves dysregulation of cellular signaling pathways, microenvironment reprogramming, immune surveillance escape, uncontrolled cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis, which are regulated by a series of genes and signaling molecules.6 Interferon regulatory factors (IRFs) are a family of pleiotropic transcriptional regulatory proteins comprising nine members (IRF1-IRF9), playing significant roles in cell growth regulation and tumorigenesis.7 For instance, IRF1 inhibits tumor formation by binding to p300 and stimulating p53-dependent transcription to enhance p21 gene expression.8 IRF3 suppresses colorectal cancer development by specifically antagonizing β-catenin nuclear translocation.9 Members such as IRF1, IRF3, IRF5, and IRF8 exert tumor-suppressive effects by regulating the cell cycle, inducing apoptosis, and inhibiting tumor angiogenesis.10,11

Interferon regulatory factor 2 (IRF2) is a key transcription factor within the IRF family, acting as a hub in various signaling pathways. It regulates interferon (IFN) and immune-related gene expression, and participates in innate immune responses, cell cycle checkpoint control, and the coordination of programmed cell death signaling pathways.12,13,14 Unlike the tumor-suppressive IRF1, IRF2 exhibits tissue-specific dual regulatory roles in solid tumors. Currently, Qi et al.15 have confirmed IRF2 overexpression in NPC via immunohistochemistry (IHC) and western blot (WB), identifying it as an oncogene, but its downstream effectors and signaling network remain incompletely elucidated.

Tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and migration are core biological processes in cancer progression and metastasis, involving aberrant activation of multiple signaling pathways and dynamic regulation of the cellular microenvironment.16,17 Tumor development is complex, with epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) being a critical initial step. EMT is a cellular process driven by inducing signals, transcriptional regulators, and downstream effectors under pathological and physiological conditions, wherein epithelial cells lose epithelial characteristics and acquire a mesenchymal phenotype and behavior, leading to enhanced cell migration and invasion. This process plays key roles in embryonic development, tissue repair, and cancer metastasis.18 During EMT, tumor cells gain invasive and metastatic capabilities, accompanied by loss of cell polarity, disruption of intercellular junctions, and cytoskeletal reorganization.19 Molecularly, EMT is characterized by reduced expression of the epithelial marker E-cadherin and increased expression of mesenchymal markers such as ZEB1, ZEB2, N-cadherin, vimentin, snail, and twist.20 EMT enhances tumor cell invasiveness, stemness, chemotherapy resistance, and distant metastasis.21,22 Studies report that increased IRF2 expression can inhibit tumor growth and improve patient prognosis in gastric cancer by directly regulating AMER1 transcription and suppressing the Wnt/β-catenin pathway.23 Additionally, IRF2 regulates EMT in tumors. In glioma, IRF2 enhances EMT-mediated migration and invasion.24 While IRF2 is established as an oncogene in NPC, its role in regulating EMT remains unclear.

The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway plays crucial roles in embryonic development, tissue homeostasis, and various diseases. It regulates gene expression, influencing cell proliferation, differentiation, and migration. Key molecules in this pathway include Wnt proteins, frizzled receptors, LRP5/6 co-receptors, and β-catenin.25 β-catenin, a multifunctional protein, acts as a key signaling hub in the Wnt pathway. Its nucleocytoplasmic translocation is closely associated with tumorigenesis, making it a potential therapeutic target.26 The stability of β-catenin, a key downstream effector of Wnt signaling, is tightly regulated. Upon binding of extracellular Wnt ligands to frizzled receptors, disheveled (Dvl) protein is activated, inhibiting glycogen synthase kinase-3 beta (GSK-3β) activity, thereby preventing the phosphorylation-dependent degradation of β-catenin and promoting its accumulation in the cytoplasm. When cytoplasmic β-catenin concentration exceeds a threshold, it translocates to the nucleus, forms complexes with other proteins, and initiates target gene expression. In tumor cells, β-catenin accumulation promotes nuclear translocation, where it interacts with T cell factor (TCF)/lymphoid enhancer factor (LEF) transcription factors, upregulating genes like snail and slug, promoting EMT, and driving metastasis.27 The Wnt/β-catenin pathway is a key regulator of EMT, and its aberrant activation is closely linked to invasion and metastasis in various cancers.28,29,30,31 In gastric cancer cells, IRF2 inhibits EMT by regulating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway.23 In liver cancer, IRF2 increases lenvatinib mesylate resistance by regulating β-catenin expression.32 IRF2-mediated regulation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway exhibits differential effects on EMT across different tumors. However, whether IRF2 influences EMT in NPC via the Wnt/β-catenin pathway remains unreported.

Current evidence suggests that IRF2 can co-regulate EMT and Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation. Nevertheless, the role of the “IRF2-Wnt-EMT” regulatory network in NPC pathogenesis remains a research gap. This study aimed to investigate the clinical significance of IRF2, its impact on the malignant behaviors of NPC cells, and the potential underlying signaling pathways, with the goal of offering new theoretical insights that may inform targeted therapy for NPC.

Results

Subcellular localization of IRF2 in NPC and its prognostic potential

Comparative proteomic analysis revealed that the expression level of IRF2 in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma is significantly higher than in most solid tumors. IRF2 has been demonstrated to play roles in various cancers (Figure 1A). However, its precise subcellular localization in NPC cells remains unclear. To determine the subcellular localization of IRF2, we first queried the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database. It was found that IRF2 is primarily localized to the nucleus, with minor expression observed at cell-cell junction structures and within the cytoplasmic matrix (Figure 1B). To preliminarily assess the prognostic value of IRF2, we performed a Kaplan-Meier survival analysis using the TCGA head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC) dataset via the GEPIA2 platform. As shown in Figure 1C, no significant association was observed between IRF2 expression level and overall survival of HNSC patients (log rank p = 0.83; HR for high expression = 0.97, p(HR) = 0.82). This result suggests that the prognostic significance of IRF2 may be limited or context-dependent, warranting further investigation in dedicated NPC cohorts (Figure 1C). Subsequently, we experimentally validated the localization of IRF2 in NPC cell lines (CNE1, HONE1, 58F and 6-10B) using immunofluorescence (IF) staining. Predictive potential of IRF2 for NPC diagnosis

To preliminarily explore the diagnostic potential of IRF2 for NPC, we performed ROC curve analysis based on IHC scores from chronic inflammatory tissues and NPC tissues. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) for IRF2 in discriminating between chronic inflammation and NPC was 0.837 (95% CI: 0.760–0.915). At a cutoff value of 168.17, the sensitivity was 94.44% and the specificity was 58.73% (Figure 4). These findings suggest that IRF2 may serve as a candidate biomarker for NPC; however, its diagnostic utility requires further validation in larger, independent cohorts. Establishment of stable IRF2 overexpression and knockdown NPC cell lines

To further investigate whether IRF2 regulates the malignant biological behavior of NPC, we established IRF2 overexpression and knockdown cell models by selecting cell lines with high endogenous IRF2 expression for knockdown and those with low expression for overexpression, in order to study its effects on the invasion, migration, and proliferation of NPC. Based on the findings from the first part of this study, we selected the NPC cell lines 58F and 6-10B for IRF2 overexpression, and the cell lines CNE1 and HONE1 for IRF2 knockdown. The results showed that in the 58F and 6-10B cell lines, IRF2 levels were significantly higher in the overexpression groups compared to the empty vector control groups (p < 0.05), indicating the successful construction of stable IRF2-overexpressing NPC cell lines. Similarly, in the CNE1 and HONE1 cell lines, IRF2 expression was significantly lower in the knockdown groups compared to the control groups (p < 0.05), with the sh2-IRF2 and sh3-IRF2 constructs showing the most pronounced reduction (Figure 5). Effect of IRF2 on the migration and invasion abilities of NPC cells

To further investigate the regulatory role of IRF2 in the migration and invasion of NPC cells, this study utilized successfully established NPC cell models with IRF2 overexpression and knockdown. The effects of IRF2 on the migratory and invasive abilities of NPC cells were systematically evaluated through wound healing assays, transwell migration assays and Matrigel invasion assays. The experimental data revealed that IRF2 overexpression significantly enhanced the migration and invasion capabilities of NPC cells as assessed by transwell assays (p < 0.05, Figure 7). In wound healing assays, IRF2 overexpression accelerated wound closure. However, wound closure can be influenced by both cell proliferation and apoptosis, which are also modulated by IRF2. Therefore, the wound healing results should be interpreted with caution and considered together with the transwell migration data. Effect of IRF2 on the subcutaneous tumorigenicity of NPC cells

Based on the aforementioned in vitro experimental results, we found that IRF2 overexpression significantly promoted the proliferation of NPC cells, while its knockdown inhibited proliferative capacity (p < 0.01). To establish correlative evidence linking in vitro and in vivo experiments, this study constructed a subcutaneous xenograft tumor model in nude mice to evaluate the regulatory role of IRF2 on NPC proliferation within the in vivo microenvironment. The experimental results demonstrated that IRF2 knockdown significantly inhibited the in vivo proliferative ability of NPC cells (p < 0.05). Further pathological analysis of the subcutaneous xenograft tumor tissues via hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining confirmed the successful establishment of the xenograft model. IHC staining results revealed that compared to the control group, the expression levels of both IRF2 and the cell proliferation marker Ki-67 were significantly reduced in the IRF2 knockdown group (p < 0.05, Figure 9). These findings indicate that IRF2 plays an important role in regulating the in vivo proliferation of NPC cells. However, it should be noted that the subcutaneous xenograft model in this study was used primarily to assess tumor growth. Effect of IRF2 on epithelial-mesenchymal transition in NPC cells

Based on the aforementioned findings, the expression level of IRF2 was significantly positively correlated with the invasion, migration, and proliferation abilities of NPC, suggesting that IRF2 may function as an oncogene involved in regulating malignant progression. To further elucidate its molecular mechanism, and considering the crucial role of EMT in tumor metastasis, we hypothesized whether IRF2 might drive the malignant phenotype of NPC by regulating the EMT process. We examined the levels of key EMT-related molecules following IRF2 overexpression and knockdown via WB analysis. The results demonstrated that after IRF2 overexpression, the expression of the epithelial marker E-cadherin was significantly downregulated, while the expression levels of the mesenchymal markers N-cadherin and vimentin, as well as the EMT transcriptional regulators snail1, snail2 (slug) and ZEB1 were markedly upregulated (p < 0.05). Conversely, in the IRF2 knockdown group, E-cadherin expression significantly increased (p < 0.05), whereas the expression levels of the aforementioned mesenchymal markers and regulatory factors were significantly reduced (Figure 10). IRF2 regulates EMT in NPC cells in association with the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway

Previous studies have demonstrated that IRF2 overexpression significantly promotes EMT in NPC cells, while its knockdown effectively inhibits this process. However, the specific molecular mechanism by which IRF2 regulates EMT remains unclear. This section aims to explore the potential signaling pathways and regulatory networks that may be involved in IRF2-mediated EMT in NPC, while acknowledging that the current data support associative rather than definitive causal relationships. Given the limited availability of public genomic databases specifically for NPC and its shared biological characteristics with HNSC, we initially utilized the GEPIA database (http://GEPIA.cancer-pku.cn/) to analyze the correlation between IRF2 and β-catenin gene expression within the HNSC dataset. The analysis yielded a p value of 0 and a correlation coefficient (R) of 0.41, suggesting a significant correlation between IRF2 and β-catenin (Figure 11). This prompted the investigation of whether IRF2 potentially regulates β-catenin in NPC. Subsequent validation via WB analysis revealed that upregulating IRF2 led to decreased expression of GSK3β, a key protein in the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, and increased expression of β-catenin (p < 0.05). Conversely, IRF2 knockdown produced the opposite effect (p < 0.05, Figure 11). Discussion

Expression of IRF2 in NPC and its clinical significance

NPC is a malignant tumor with a distinct geographical distribution, showing a particularly high incidence in Southeast Asia and southern China.33 Current treatment for NPC primarily relies on a comprehensive approach involving radiotherapy, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted therapy. Recent clinical studies and meta-analyses indicate that induction chemotherapy followed by concurrent chemoradiotherapy significantly improves the overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), and distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS) of NPC patients.34,35 However, despite advancements in therapeutic strategies, some patients still face challenges such as treatment resistance, recurrence, and metastasis. Therefore, exploring effective therapeutic targets, establishing personalized precision treatment models and optimizing therapeutic strategies have become urgent needs to overcome current treatment bottlenecks and improve long-term patient prognosis. Previous studies have confirmed that IRF2 plays diverse roles in various cancers and acts as an oncogene in NPC tissues.36 Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms of IRF2 in NPC development and signaling transduction remain incompletely elucidated. Based on this, our study aims to further investigate the intrinsic molecular mechanisms of IRF2 in NPC pathogenesis, intending to provide new theoretical foundations and potential targets for the precision treatment of NPC.

Firstly, this study revealed the expression profile of IRF2 in NPC and its potential clinical significance through multi-level experimental analyses. Preliminary immunohistochemistry results demonstrated that the expression of IRF2 was significantly higher in NPC tissues and cell lines compared to chronic nasopharyngeal inflammatory tissues and the normal nasopharyngeal epithelial cell line NP69, with statistical significance. This finding is consistent with previous reports,36 further supporting the role of IRF2 as an oncogene in NPC. The clinical significance of IRF2 varies across different cancer types. For instance, high IRF2 expression is associated with poorer overall survival in pancreatic cancer37,38; in liver cancer, IRF2 affects patient prognosis by regulating the SOCS family39; in cervical cancer, IRF2 is considered a potential diagnostic marker40; in childhood acute myeloid leukemia, IRF2 can predict disease prognosis and relapse41; in glioma, high IRF2 levels correlate with more advanced tumor grades and worse outcomes, potentially serving as a prognostic biomarker and indicator of immune status42; IRF2 has been used to construct clinical prognostic models for predicting outcomes in breast cancer.43 ROC curve analysis suggested that IRF2 might have potential utility in distinguishing chronic nasopharyngeal inflammation from NPC, with an AUC of 0.837 (95% CI: 0.760–0.915). At a cutoff value of 168.17, the sensitivity was 94.44% and the specificity was 58.73%. Although the high sensitivity hints at a possible role for preliminary screening, the relatively low specificity and the lack of an independent validation cohort indicate that IRF2 should be considered a candidate biomarker at this stage. Its clinical applicability requires confirmation through larger, multi-center prospective studies with long-term follow-up. Our results support further investigation of IRF2 as a potential auxiliary diagnostic marker for NPC, rather than a definitive clinical tool. Furthermore, based on the IRF2 expression levels from 64 NPC patients categorized into high and low expression groups according to the cut-off value, we found that IRF2 expression was significantly correlated with patients’ T stage and plasma EBV DNA load, but not with smoking history, N stage or M stage. This suggests that IRF2 may play an important role in the local invasiveness of NPC. EBV is closely associated with NPC, and elevated viral DNA levels typically indicate active viral replication. IRF2 may promote NPC development by facilitating EBV replication.44,45

Although this study preliminarily confirmed that IRF2 is highly expressed in NPC and correlated with T stage and EBV DNA load, the sample size is limited, and the study is a single-center retrospective analysis. The oncogenic mechanisms of IRF2 require further in-depth investigation. Future studies should expand the cohort and conduct functional experiments to validate the causal role of IRF2. Additionally, does IRF2 influence tumor progression by regulating the EBV life cycle or interferon signaling pathways? In summary, IRF2 holds promise as a potential target for individualized NPC therapy, but its clinical translation necessitates further exploration of its molecular mechanisms and optimization of diagnostic models.

Investigation into the regulatory role and mechanism of IRF2 in NPC proliferation and invasion

The interferon regulatory factor family, a class of evolutionarily conserved transcriptional regulatory proteins, participates in various pathophysiological regulations by coordinating processes such as innate immune responses and cell fate determination. Among them, IRF2 has attracted significant attention due to its unique “double-edged sword” characteristic. IRF2 can function as either an oncogene or a tumor suppressor in human tumorigenesis. For example, IRF2 acts as an oncogene in liver cancer32 and rectal cancer,46 while serving as a tumor suppressor in gastric cancer,23 lung cancer,47 and osteosarcoma.36 This functional heterogeneity suggests that the oncogenic tendency of IRF2 may be finely regulated by the tissue microenvironment, co-regulatory factor networks, or epigenetic modifications. This study focuses on the role of IRF2 in NPC and, for the first time, reveals its clear pro-malignant phenotype in this context. A series of functional experiments confirmed that overexpression of IRF2 significantly enhanced cell proliferation activity and maintained a survival advantage by inhibiting apoptotic pathways. Furthermore, transwell assays demonstrated that IRF2 overexpression increased the number of migrated and invasive cells, while IRF2 knockdown decreased these capabilities. In wound healing assays, IRF2 overexpression also accelerated wound closure. However, the observed changes in wound closure could be partially attributable to these processes rather than solely to enhanced cell migration, because IRF2 simultaneously promotes proliferation and suppresses apoptosis. Thus, the wound healing results should be interpreted as supporting evidence rather than definitive proof of altered migratory capacity. Nevertheless, the consistent trends observed across multiple functional assays (including transwell migration, which directly measures cell movement) support a role for IRF2 in regulating NPC cell migration. This study further discovered through subcutaneous tumor formation experiments in nude mice that knocking down IRF2 significantly inhibited tumor growth, suggesting that the pro-metastatic property of IRF2 might be related to its ability to remodel the microenvironment. Does IRF2 promote the invasion and migration of NPC cells by regulating the EMT process? We further explored the underlying molecular mechanisms via WB experiments. Further studies indicated that IRF2 overexpression significantly induced downregulation of E-cadherin and upregulation of N-cadherin, snail1, snail2, and ZEB1, while knockdown produced the opposite effect. These findings demonstrate the oncogenic role of IRF2 in NPC from both functional phenotypic and molecular mechanistic perspectives.

Existing literature suggests that β-catenin, the core effector molecule of the Wnt signaling pathway, can directly drive the EMT process by activating transcription factors such as snail and twist upon its nuclear accumulation.27 In cancer, nuclear localization of β-catenin is closely associated with tumor invasion, metastasis, and chemoresistance. Given the limited public genomic database resources for NPC and its shared biological characteristics with HNSC, we performed a correlation analysis between IRF2 and β-catenin gene expression in the HNSC dataset using the GEPIA database. The results indicated a high correlation between IRF2 and β-catenin. Therefore, we asked whether IRF2 regulates EMT in NPC cells in association with the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. To explore this possibility, we first validated changes in the expression levels of key Wnt/β-catenin pathway proteins, GSK-3β and β-catenin, following IRF2 knockdown or overexpression using WB. Subsequently, nuclear and cytoplasmic protein fractionation experiments were conducted to extract cytoplasmic and nuclear proteins. WB analysis detected changes in β-catenin localization and IF staining assessed β-catenin distribution in the cytoplasm and nucleus upon IRF2 overexpression. The results indicated that IRF2 overexpression downregulated GSK-3β, increased β-catenin expression levels, and promoted its nuclear translocation. Conversely, IRF2 knockdown yielded opposite results. This discovery expands the understanding of the IRF family proteins’ functions in tumor invasion and proliferation, providing a new perspective on the molecular mechanisms of EMT in NPC. This study suggests that IRF2 may be an upstream driver of this process. The positive correlation between IRF2 expression levels and β-catenin nuclear localization indicates that IRF2 overexpression may promote malignant progression by activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. We consider several potential mechanisms underpinning this interaction: (1) IRF2 may influence EMT by regulating the subcellular distribution of β-catenin. As a transcription factor, the nuclear localization signal (NLS) of IRF2 might assist β-catenin in crossing the nuclear membrane. We plan to further investigate potential direct interaction between IRF2 and β-catenin through co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) or fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) experiments. (2) IRF2 may affect the stability or transcriptional activity of β-catenin by transcriptionally regulating genes such as Axin, APC, or TCF4. For instance, the APC protein can inhibit β-catenin nuclear accumulation by promoting its degradation.48 If IRF2 downregulates APC expression, it might indirectly lead to β-catenin accumulation. (3) β-catenin is typically anchored to the cell membrane by binding to E-cadherin, and the loss of E-cadherin during EMT may release β-catenin for nuclear translocation.49 In this study, IRF2 overexpression led to E-cadherin downregulation, suggesting that IRF2 might disrupt the E-cadherin/β-catenin complex, promoting the migration of free β-catenin into the nucleus.

In summary, IRF2 is predominantly localized in the nucleus of NPC cells and may function as an oncogene potentially involved in local invasion and EBV replication. IRF2 appears to drive the progression of NPC by promoting cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and EMT, while simultaneously inhibiting apoptosis. These effects are potentially associated with the activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, though the causal relationship requires further investigation. Additionally, the in vivo validation of this proposed mechanism was not performed in the current xenograft model, representing an important direction for future research. Finally, the potential clinical utility of IRF2 as a diagnostic or prognostic biomarker in NPC warrants further evaluation in larger, independent cohorts.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, although our data demonstrate a strong correlation between IRF2 expression, Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation, and EMT phenotypes, direct causal evidence linking IRF2-mediated Wnt/β-catenin activation to these functional outcomes is lacking. Rescue experiments (e.g., simultaneous β-catenin knockdown or Wnt pathway inhibition in IRF2-overexpressing cells, and Wnt activation in IRF2-knockdown cells) are needed to establish causality. Second, the precise molecular mechanism by which IRF2 promotes β-catenin nuclear translocation remains unexplored—whether through direct protein-protein interaction, transcriptional regulation of β-catenin or its regulators (e.g., APC, Axin), or disruption of the E-cadherin/β-catenin complex. Third, the clinical sample size is relatively modest (n = 64) and derived from a single center, with no external validation cohort or long-term follow-up survival data; the diagnostic specificity was also limited (58.73%). Therefore, larger, multi-center prospective studies with comprehensive follow-up are required to validate the diagnostic and prognostic value of IRF2 in NPC. Fourth, although the subcutaneous xenograft experiments demonstrated that IRF2 knockdown significantly inhibited tumor growth, we did not perform IHC or WB analyses to assess Wnt/β-catenin pathway activity or EMT marker expression within the harvested tumor tissues. Consequently, whether the proposed IRF2-Wnt/β-catenin-EMT regulatory axis operates in the in vivo tumor microenvironment remains to be directly validated in future studies. Additionally, combining clinical NPC samples to systematically examine the correlation between IRF2 protein expression and β-catenin nuclear translocation, along with the spatial co-localization of EMT markers, is necessary. Finally, based on clinical follow-up data, assessing the prognostic and predictive value of the co-expression patterns of these molecular features for patient outcomes will be crucial.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human subjects

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue samples were obtained from patients with nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC; diagnosed between February 2018 and June 2020) and chronic nasopharyngitis (diagnosed between January 2013 and April 2014) at the Department of Pathology, Affiliated Cancer Hospital of Guizhou Medical University (Guiyang, China). A total of 63 NPC patients (47 males, 16 females; age range not specified) and 63 chronic nasopharyngitis patients were included. The sex distribution and age information are reported in Table 1. The influence of sex on IRF2 expression was analyzed (p = 0.412, no significant association). Age of subjects is reported as ≥60 vs. <60 years. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou Medical University (approval number: 2023-575). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to sample collection and the informed consent process strictly followed the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Cell lines

The immortalized human nasopharyngeal epithelial cell line NP69 (gifted by the Li Ka Shing Faculty of Medicine, The University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong, China) and NPC cell lines including CNE1, HONE1, HK-1, 58F and 6-10B were used. All NPC cell lines were gifted by the Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center (Guangzhou, China). The sex of these cell lines is not specified by the original providers. Cells were maintained under standard culture conditions (37°C, 5% CO2) as described in method details. All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and routinely tested negative for mycoplasma contamination.

Animal models

Immunodeficient male nude mice (4–5 weeks old, weighing 14–15 g) were purchased from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology (Beijing, China). Only male mice were used. Therefore, the influence of sex on the results could not be assessed. Mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with controlled temperature (22°C–25°C) and humidity (40–50%), a 12-h light/dark cycle, and free access to food and water. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care Welfare Committee of Guizhou Medical University (approval number: 2304694).

Method details

Cell culture

Cells were cultured following standard protocols. Cryopreserved cells were rapidly thawed in a 37°C water bath, resuspended in complete medium (RPMI-1640 or DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin), and centrifuged. The pellet was seeded into T25 flasks and maintained in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. For subculturing, cells at 70–80% confluence were washed with PBS, detached using trypsin, and neutralized with complete medium. After centrifugation, the cell pellet was resuspended and passaged at a 1:3 to 1:4 split ratio. Cell concentration was determined using an automated cell counter. For cryopreservation, harvested cells were resuspended in serum-free cryopreservation medium, stored at −80°C, and subsequently transferred to liquid nitrogen.

Vector construction and stable transfection

Lentiviral vectors for IRF2 overexpression (GV492) and knockdown (GV493) were constructed and packaged by GeneChem (Shanghai, China). Viral titers were 5×108 TU/mL (overexpression) and 1–3×109 TU/mL (knockdown). For transfection, cells were seeded in 6-well plates to 70–80% confluence. Medium was replaced with 1 mL fresh medium containing 40 μL enhancer solution, and lentivirus was added at appropriate MOI. After 16 h, viral supernatant was replaced with complete medium. GFP expression was monitored at 48 h; when transfection efficiency exceeded 90%, cells were selected with puromycin for 14 days to establish stable lines. IRF2 expression was confirmed by WB.

Nuclear and cytoplasmic protein extraction

Nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins were extracted using a commercial subcellular fractionation kit (according to manufacturer’s instructions). Cells in logarithmic growth phase were harvested, washed with PBS, and resuspended in pre-cooled cytoplasmic extraction reagent A containing 1 mM PMSF. After vortexing and ice incubation, reagent B was added. The lysate was centrifuged (12,000–16,000 × g, 5 min, 4°C) to obtain supernatant (cytoplasmic fraction). The nuclear pellet was washed and lysed in nuclear extraction buffer with 1 mM PMSF, incubated on ice for 30 min with intermittent vortexing, and centrifuged (12,000–16,000 × g, 10 min, 4°C) to obtain nuclear fraction. Both fractions were mixed with 5× loading buffer, boiled, and stored at −80°C.

WB

Proteins were extracted using ice-cold RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors, followed by ultrasonication and centrifugation. Protein concentration was determined by BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto methanol-activated PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk, incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies (see key resources table), then with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) and analyzed with ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells seeded on coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% BSA. Primary antibodies (anti-IRF2, anti-β-catenin) were applied overnight at 4°C, followed by fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (in the dark). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Coverslips were mounted with 90% glycerol and sealed before imaging.

Wound healing assay

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates pre-marked with parallel reference lines. At >90% confluence, cells were serum-starved for 12 h, then a uniform scratch wound was created using a sterile pipette tip. Detached cells were washed away with PBS, and serum-free medium was added. Images were taken at 0 h and 24 h under an inverted phase-contrast microscope. Wound closure was quantified by measuring the remaining cell-free area using ImageJ. Experiments were performed in triplicate and repeated three times.

Colony formation assay

Cells (500–1000 per well) were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured for 10–14 days with periodic medium changes. Visible colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (30 min) and stained with crystal violet (10 min). Colonies were imaged and counted using ImageJ.

Transwell migration and invasion assay

Transwell chambers (Corning) were used. For invasion assays, the upper chamber was pre-coated with Matrigel (1:8 dilution in serum-free medium) and polymerized overnight. Serum-starved cells (3–7 × 104 in 200 μL serum-free medium) were seeded into the upper chamber; the lower chamber contained 700 μL complete medium with 20% FBS as chemoattractant. After 24–48 h, non-migrated/invaded cells on the upper membrane surface were removed with a cotton swab. Cells that traversed the membrane were fixed, stained with crystal violet, and imaged. The number of migrated/invaded cells was quantified from multiple random fields using ImageJ.

CCK-8 assay

Cells (1–3 × 103 per well in 200 μL) were seeded in 96-well plates. At 0, 24, 48 and 72 h, medium was replaced with 100 μL fresh medium containing 10% CCK-8 reagent, incubated at 37°C for 2 h in the dark. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. All experiments had at least three replicate wells. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism.

Apoptosis detection by flow cytometry

Apoptosis was assessed using an Annexin V-APC/7-AAD staining kit. Cells (1–5 × 105) were resuspended in 500 μL 1× binding buffer, stained with 5 μL Annexin V-APC and 10 μL 7-AAD, and incubated for 5 min at room temperature in the dark. Flow cytometry was performed within 1 h. Data were analyzed with FlowJo 10.0 and SPSS 27.0.

HE staining

Tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, cleared, and paraffin-embedded. Sections (3–5 μm) were stained with hematoxylin (5 min), differentiated in 1% acid alcohol, counterstained with eosin (3 min), dehydrated, cleared, and mounted with neutral resin.

IHC

IHC was performed on 3–5 μm FFPE sections. After baking, dewaxing, and rehydration, antigen retrieval was done by microwave heating. Endogenous peroxidase was blocked with 3% H2O2, followed by goat serum blocking. Sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-IRF2 primary antibody at optimal dilution. After washing, secondary antibody was applied and signal amplified. Visualization used DAB substrate, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. Staining intensity was scored 0–3 by two independent pathologists. Immunoreactivity score (0–300) was calculated as: (1 × % weak) + (2 × % moderate) + (3 × % strong).

Xenograft animal model

Male nude mice (4–5 weeks old, 14–15 g) were randomly allocated to shControl (n = 5) or shIRF2 (n = 5) groups. Exponentially growing CNE1 cells (stable shControl or shIRF2) were harvested, resuspended in pre-cooled PBS at 1×107 cells/mL. Two hundred microliters (2×106 cells) were injected subcutaneously into the right shoulder. Tumor size was measured every two days with a digital caliper; volume = (length × width2)/2. After three weeks, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, tumors excised, weighed, and either fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde or snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0 and GraphPad Prism 8.2. Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± SD. For normally distributed data, independent Student’s t test was used. Otherwise, Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared by Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis evaluated diagnostic performance. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Exact n values, what n represents (e.g., number of independent experiments, number of animals, number of cells), and the statistical test used for each experiment are indicated in the figure legends. For bar graphs, error bars represent SEM unless otherwise specified. Asterisks in figures denote significance levels (∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001) as defined in each legend.
