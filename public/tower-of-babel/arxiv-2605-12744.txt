Bousfield Localizations on the Nonmodular Lattice N 5 N_{5}
Sofía Martínez Alberga, Constanze Roitzheim

Abstract
. We provide a complete description of the model category structures on the nonmodular lattice N 5 N_{5} . Furthermore we explain how these model category structures are related to each other via Bousfield localization. This work heavily relies on the use of combinatorical objects from equivariant homotopy theory known as transfer systems , and it results in a wealth of interesting interactions between combinatorial and homotopical methods.



1.
Introduction

Model category structures supply us with a fundamental language for homotopy theory. Since their original inception in the 1960’s, they have become invaluable for formalizing phenomena in rational, stable, and equivariant homotopy, which classically requires extensive background knowledge about the specific underlying categories of e.g. spaces or spectra. If instead one considers model category structures on a finite lattice, one strips model categories from those elaborate technical prerequisites and brings the model category techniques themselves into the foreground.

To discuss model structures on a finite lattice
\mathcal{P}
,
transfer systems
play an important role. A transfer system is a subcategory of a lattice that contains the all objects of
\mathcal{P}
and is closed under pullbacks.
These special subcategories prove to be a central tool for the study of equivariant homotopy commutativity
[
BH15
]
, and also link to other rich combinatorial structures
[
BBR21
]

[
BOOR23
]
.
A model category structure on
\mathcal{P}
determines and is determined by a pair
\mathsf{W}\supseteq\mathcal{T}
, where
\mathsf{W}
is a wide decomposable subcategory and
\mathcal{T}
is a transfer system, subject to further restrictions specified in
[
MOR+25a
]
.
In this case,
\mathsf{W}
plays the role of the weak equivalences and
\mathcal{T}=\mathsf{AF}
the role of the acyclic fibrations of the model structure.
Therefore, not only do transfer systems on finite lattices provide bridges between seemingly unrelated concepts, they also shift the focus away from abstract set theory to more manageable, “hands-on” constructions.

One such construction on model categories is
Bousfield localization
, which provides a formal framework for modifying an existing model structure into a new model structure by incrementing the class of weak equivalences.
A common occurrence of Bousfield localization in topology is localizing a category of topological spaces or spectra by changing the weak equivalences from weak homotopy equivalences to
E_{*}
-isomorphisms for a suitable homology theory
E_{*}
.
In the context of finite lattices, the effect of left and right localization on a pair
(\mathsf{W},\mathsf{AF})
has been determined in
[
CBGMA+25
]
. A part of this work shows that while left Bousfield localization of course leaves
\mathsf{AF}
unchanged, right Bousfield localizations on
\mathcal{P}
are classified by a minimal generating arrows called
Golden Arrows
which depend only on
\mathsf{W}
. The present article exemplifies another instance of Golden Arrows on a lattice that is not rectangular or even modular.

The specific lattice
N_{5}
considered in this paper is relatively small, but is
nonmodular
, meaning that it fails to satisfy crucial distributive properties.
While nonmodular lattices are harder to work with in practice than their nicely-behaved modular counterparts, they do make a frequent appearance in homotopy theory.
To start, in homotopy-coherent structures, the lattice
N_{5}
appears as the
3
-
Tamari lattice
.
Recall that the
n
-Tamari lattice this is the poset consisting of sequences of
(n+1)
-letters, where the order is induced by applications of the associative law
((xy)z)\rightarrow(x(yz))
.
Furthermore the
n
-Tamari lattice is isomorphic to the poset of transfer systems on a total order
[n]
, see
[
BBR21
]
. As a direct consequence, if a finite lattice
\mathcal{P}
contains
[2]
, its poset of transfer systems
\mathrm{Tr}(\mathcal{P})
will always be nonmodular.
Additionally it was exhibited by
[
KLM+25
]
that the subgroup lattice Sub(
G
) is nonmodular for numerous interesting classes of nonabelian groups
G
such as dihedral groups and alternating groups.
Therefore, in order to understand
G
-equivariant stable homotopy theory for these groups, it is vital to understand transfer systems on
N_{5}
and their homotopy-theoretic interactions.

Our main result is the following, which appears as Theorem
5.4
.

Theorem
.

Every model category structure on
N_{5}
can be obtained as a sequence of left and right Bousfield localizations of the trivial model structure.

This new understanding of the behaviour of Bousfield localizations on
N_{5}
will add a useful puzzle piece to the behaviour of Bousfield localizations on finite lattices in general.

Organization

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we introduce
N_{5}
and recall some basic lattice terminology. Section
3
develops the lattice of transfer systems on
N_{5}
, as well as the dual notion known as cotransfer systems. In this section we will see that the fact that pushouts of indecomposable arrows are not necessarily indecomposable again is the first major practical obstacle of working with a nonmodular lattice. Section
4
recalls the relationship between transfer systems and model structures, which allows us to systematically determine all model structures on
N_{5}
as well as draw some first conclusions from this. Finally, in Section
5
we explore the effect of Bousfield localizations on our previous calculations, which also demonstrates the application of the Golden Arrows in a non-modular context.

An equivalent definition of modularity is that a lattice is modular if and only if it satisfies the

In practice, many proofs and situations assume lattices to be modular, and therefore nonmodular lattice often require special treatment. Before our next remark on modularity, let us introduce the following convenient terminology.

Definition 2.3
.

We say that an arrow

Remark 2.4
.

A lattice is modular if and only if the pushout of a short arrow is again short. We see that this is clearly not the case for
N_{5}
. Conversely, assume that we have a lattice where the arrow
x\rightarrow y
has a pushout
p\rightarrow q
that factors as
p\rightarrow z\rightarrow q

y
and
z
are not comparable, because otherwise the pushout would not have target
q

Then, even though
p\leq z
, we have

p\vee(y\wedge z)=p\neq z=(p\vee y)\wedge z,

which violates the distributive law.

Definition 3.1
.

A

on a category

If
\mathcal{C}

the poset of all transfer systems on
\mathcal{C}

4.
Transfer systems and model structures

We assume that the reader is familiar with the basic definitions regarding model categories, but we recall some notions for convenience.

Definition 4.1
.

A
model structure
on a category
\mathcal{C}
consists of three subcategories
\mathsf{W}
,
\mathsf{C}
and
\mathsf{F}
called
weak equivalences
,
cofibrations
and
fibrations
, respectively, satisfying the axioms below.
The subcategories
\mathsf{AC}=\mathsf{C}\cap\mathsf{W}
and
\mathsf{AF}=\mathsf{F}\cap\mathsf{W}
are called
acyclic cofbrations
and
acyclic fibrations.

(1)

\mathcal{C}
has all finite limit s and colimits.

(2)

(2-out-of-3) If two out of the three morphisms
f
,
g
and
g\circ f
are in
\mathsf{W}
, then so is the third.

(3)

(Retracts)
\mathsf{W}
,
\mathsf{C}
, and
\mathsf{F}
are closed under retracts.

(4)

(Lifts)
\mathsf{AC}={}^{\boxslash}\mathsf{F}
and
\mathsf{C}={}^{\boxslash}\mathsf{AF}

(5)

(Factorization)
\mathsf{W}=\mathsf{AF}\circ\mathsf{C}=\mathsf{F}\circ\mathsf{AC}

We will denote fibrations with a double-tipped arrow.

We make the following observations.

•

In the case of the category
\mathcal{C}
being a finite lattice, the first two axioms become trivial.

•

The data a model structure is completely determined by the classes
\mathsf{W}
and
\mathsf{AF}
only. The same holds for other choices of pairs of classes, for example
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathsf{F}
only, but we choose the presentation of
\mathsf{W}
and
\mathsf{AF}
as it is the most suitable for our methods.

•

The acyclic fibrations
\mathsf{AF}
are always closed under retracts and contain all identity morphisms
[
DS95
, Proposition 3.14]
, which means that they form a transfer system. This is also true for
\mathsf{F}
.

Furthermore, when
\mathcal{C}
is a lattice, the weak equivalences of a model structure satisfy something stronger than the 2-out-of-3 axiom, see
[
DZ21
, Proposition 1.8]
.

Lemma 4.2
.

The weak equivalences of a model structure on a lattice
\mathcal{C}
are decomposable, i.e. if
g\circ f
is in
\mathsf{W}
, then so are
g
and
f
.

Therefore, on a finite lattice the data of a model structure gives us a wide decomposable subcategory
\mathsf{W}
and a transfer system
\mathcal{T}=\mathsf{AF}\subseteq\mathsf{W}
. However, not every wide decomposable subcategory is the weak equivalence set of a model structure.

Example 4.3
.

On the lattice
[2]\times[1]
, consider the wide decomposable subcategory with only nontrivial arrow
(1,0)\rightarrow(1,1)
. There is no model structure with this as its weak equivalence set: the factorization axiom tells us that the map
(1,0)\rightarrow(1,1)
has to be either an acyclic fibration or an acyclic cofibration. Therefore, in particular, either its pullback or its pushout must be a weak equivalence, which is not the case.

In this spirit, we say that a wide decomposable subcategory
\mathsf{W}
is a
weak equivalence set
if there is a model structure with
\mathsf{W}
as its weak equivalences.
There is in fact a necessary and sufficient condition for a wide decomposable subcategory to be a weak equivalence set, see
[
MOR+25a
, Theorem 5.8]
.

Theorem 4.4
.

A wide decomposable subcategory
\mathsf{W}
of a finite lattice
\mathcal{C}
is a weak equivalence set if and only if following holds:

For every
f\in\mathsf{W}
there is a factorization into short arrows

f=\sigma_{n}\circ\sigma_{n-1}\circ\cdots\circ\sigma_{2}\circ\sigma_{1}

such that there is a
k
with

•

for
i\leq k
, all pushouts of
\sigma_{i}
are in
\mathsf{W}
,

•

for
i>k
, all pullouts of
\sigma_{i}
are in
\mathsf{W}
.

Let us apply this result to the pentagon
N_{5}
.

Proposition 4.5
.

On the pentagon lattice
N_{5}
, every wide decomposable subcategory is a weak equivalence set.

Proof.

The claim is proven using the condition from Theorem
4.4
by breaking the work up into the possible lengths of a morphism
f\in\mathsf{W}
. (By “length” we mean the number of short arrows a morphism can be factored into.)
Table
1
of pushouts and pullbacks in
N_{5}
tells us that every morphism in
N_{5}
of length 1 has either no nontrivial pushouts or no nontrivial pullbacks, so if
f\in\mathsf{W}
is of length 1 then the condition of Theorem
4.4
is satisfied automatically.

There are two morphisms in
N_{5}
of length 2, namely
A\rightarrow 1
and
0\rightarrow C
. We see that if we factor
A\rightarrow 1
into short arrows
A\rightarrow C\rightarrow 1
, the two short arrows
A\rightarrow C
and
C\rightarrow 1
have no nontrivial pushouts, so the condition of Theorem
4.4
. A similar argument holds for
0\rightarrow C
.

The only morphism in
N_{5}
of length 3 is
0\rightarrow 1
. If
0\rightarrow 1\in\mathsf{W}
, then by decomposability of
\mathsf{W}
, we automatically have
\mathsf{W}
is the set of all morphisms in
N_{5}
and there is nothing to check.
∎

Corollary 4.6
.

There are 22 weak equivalence sets on
N_{5}
.
∎

Fixing a weak equivalence set
\mathsf{W}
, not every transfer system
\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathsf{W}
arises as the acyclic fibrations of a model category.

Example 4.7
.

Consider the lattice
\mathcal{C}=[1]\times[1]
and the wide decomposable subcategory
\mathsf{W}
with only nontrivial arrow
(0,0)\rightarrow(0,1)
. Then there is a model structure on
\mathcal{C}
with
\mathsf{AF}=\mathsf{W}
, but there is no model structure with this
\mathsf{W}
and the trivial transfer system as its acyclic fibrations. This can be verified directly from the model category axioms, see also
[
BOOR23
, Remark 3.15]
and
[
MOR+25a
, Example 4.16]
for details.

The above example illustrates that the model category axioms always force a certain minimal set of acyclic fibrations to be present. A complete characterization is given by the following result from
[
MOR+25a
, Theorem 4.20]
.

Theorem 4.8
.

For every weak equivalence set
\mathsf{W}
there is a transfer system
\mathcal{T}_{min}\subset\mathsf{W}
such that the following are equivalent:

•

There is a model structure with weak equivalences
\mathsf{W}
and
\mathsf{AF}=\mathcal{T}
.

•

\mathcal{T}_{min}\subseteq\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathsf{W}
.

Fix a weak equivalence set
\mathsf{W}
.
We denote by
\mathsf{AF}(\mathsf{W})\subseteq\mathrm{Tr}(\mathcal{C})
the set of all of transfer systems
\mathcal{T}
form a model structure on
\mathcal{C}
with weak equivalences
\mathsf{W}
and acyclic fibrations
\mathcal{T}
.
Theorem 4.20 of
[
MOR+25a
]
shows that
\mathsf{AF}(\mathsf{W})
is a sublattice of
\mathrm{Tr}(\mathcal{C})
and moreover is an interval. Recall that an interval in a lattice
\mathcal{P}
with minimal element
A
and maximal element
B
is the set

[A,B]=\{X\in\mathcal{P}\,\,|\,\,A\leq X\leq B\}.

(Despite its name, note that an interval in a lattice is not necessarily isomorphic to
[n]
.)
More precisely, one has

\mathsf{AF}(\mathsf{W})=[\mathcal{T}_{min},\mathcal{T}_{max}]=\{\mathcal{T}\in\mathrm{Tr}(\mathcal{C})\,\,|\,\,\mathcal{T}_{min}\subseteq\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{max}\},

where
\mathcal{T}_{max}
is the (unique) largest transfer system contained in
\mathsf{W}
, and
\mathcal{T}_{min}
is the transfer system from Theorem

5.
Bousfield localization

Working with Bousfield localization on a finite lattice of course means that we do not need to pay attention to any of the set-theoretic existence questions that usually arise. We refer to
[
BR20
, Chapter 7.1]
and
[
Hir03
, Chapter 3]
for the general definitions and only summarize what is required for our purposes.

Left Bousfield localization
L_{S}
at a set
S
changes an existing model structure
\mathsf{W},\mathsf{C},\mathsf{F}
in the following way:

•

L_{S}(\mathsf{W})
is the smallest weak equivalence set containing
S
, the pushouts of
S
, and
\mathsf{W}
,

•

the cofibrations remain the same, i.e.
L_{S}(\mathsf{C})=\mathsf{C}
.

As a consequence, by the lifting axiom of model structures,
L_{S}(\mathsf{AF})=\mathsf{AF}
,
L_{S}(\mathsf{F})\subseteq F
and
L_{S}(\mathsf{AC})\supseteq\mathsf{AC}
. We will specify what we mean by the “smallest” weak equivalence set further along the way.

Dually,
right Bousfield localization
R_{S}
at a set
S
changes an existing model structure
\mathsf{W},\mathsf{C},\mathsf{F}
in the following way:

•

R_{S}(\mathsf{W})
is the smallest weak equivalence set containing
S
, the pullbacks of
S
and
\mathsf{W}
,

•

the fibrations remain the same, i.e.
R_{S}(\mathsf{F})=\mathsf{F}
.

Consequently,
\mathsf{AC}=R_{S}(\mathsf{AC})
,
\mathsf{AF}\subseteq R_{S}(\mathsf{AF})
and
\mathsf{C}\supseteq R_{S}(\mathsf{C})
.

Let us specify how the weak equivalences on an underlying lattice change under left and right localization. We concentrate on the case where the set
S
consists of a single morphism
f
. For both left (and right) localization, we start by adding
f
to
\mathsf{W}
and then keep adding the necessary morphisms until we reach a set containing
\mathsf{W}
,
f
, and the pushouts (resp. pullbacks of
f
), and which satisfies the conditions of Theorem
4.4
. We only describe
R_{f}(\mathsf{W})
;
L_{f}(\mathsf{W})
is constructed dually using
\mathsf{AC}
instead of
\mathsf{AF}
and pushouts instead of pullbacks. More details for both constructions are given in
[
CBGMA+25
, Section 2]
.

Construction 5.1
.

From
\mathsf{W}
to
R_{f}(\mathsf{W})
:

(1)

Establish
\mathsf{AF}_{(0)}:=\mathsf{AF},\,\,\,S_{(0)}:=\{f\}
.

(2)

Define
\mathsf{AF}_{(n)}:=\langle\mathsf{AF}_{(n-1)}\cup S_{(n-1)}\rangle_{TS}
.

(3)

Take
\mathsf{W}_{(n)}
to be the closure of
\mathsf{AF}_{(n)}\circ\mathsf{AC}
under the two-out-of-three property.

(4)

Set
S_{(n)}
to be the set of arrows in
\mathsf{W}_{(n)}\backslash\mathsf{W}_{(n-1)}
and continue with Step (2).

This process either terminates or stabilizes as we are in a finite lattice, and the resulting set is the weak equivalence set
\mathsf{W}_{N}=R_{f}(\mathsf{W})
for some index
N
.

Since we present the data of a model category in terms of its weak equivalences and acyclic fibrations, with this construction, we know how the weak equivalences change for both left and right localization. For left localization, we also know that the acyclic fibrations remain unchanged.
For right localization, the acyclic fibrations are changed by the addition of the following set, see
[
CBGMA+25
, Theorem 2.16]

Theorem 5.2
.

For right Bousfield localization on a lattice at a short arrow
f
, the acyclic fibrations become
R_{f}(\mathsf{AF})=\left<\mathsf{AF}\cup\Gamma_{f}\right>_{TS}

where

\Gamma_{f}=\{s\rightarrow t\,|\,s\in S(\sigma),t\in T(\sigma)\,\,\mbox{for some short arrow $\sigma\in R_{f}(\mathsf{W})\setminus\mathsf{W}$}\}

with

\displaystyle T(\sigma)=\{y\,\,|\,\,\mbox{$y$ is weakly equivalent to the target of $\sigma$ before localization},

\displaystyle\mbox{and $y$ is maximal with this property}\}

and

\displaystyle S(\sigma)=\{y\,\,|\,\,\mbox{$y$ is weakly equivalent to the source of $\sigma$ before localization},\,\,y\leq t\,\,

\displaystyle\mbox{for some $t\in T(\sigma)$, and $y$ is maximal with these properties}\}.

The set
\Gamma_{f}
is called the
Golden Arrows
.

In other words, we look at the weak equivalence components before localization, observe which of those are joined after localization and add arrows from the maximal elements of the source components to the maximal elements of the target components with respect to the old weak equivalences.

One key question about all Bousfield localizations on a fixed underlying lattice is if the poset of all model structures together with the left and right localizations is connected. In other words, can any model structure be obtained from the trivial model structure by a sequence of left and right localizations?

For the 23 model structures on the square

Every model category structure on
