Long non-coding RNA IL6-AS1 promotes severe community-acquired pneumonia in children through the miR-149-5p/MAPK6 axis
Authors: Chang Liu, Fanghua Jian, Jing Li, Hui Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSevere community-acquired pneumonia (SCAP) seriously endangers children’s lives. This study aimed to explore the potential regulatory mechanisms of long non-coding RNA IL6-AS1 (lncRNA IL6-AS1) in SCAP.MethodsThis study included 160 children with SCAP and 160 healthy controls. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) was used to induce human embryonic lung fibroblasts (MRC-5) to construct an SCAP cell model. Functional experiments were conducted by combining real-time quantitative PCR, the cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay, flow cytometry, and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). The targeted regulatory relationship between different genes was verified through RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) experiment and dual luciferase assay.ResultsIn children with SCAP, IL6-AS1 expression showed significant upregulation. IL6-AS1 has a strong diagnostic value for SCAP, and its expression was significantly positively correlated with indicators of inflammatory response intensity (C-reactive protein (CRP), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), procalcitonin levels (PCT)) in affected children. It may emerge as an independent risk factor influencing the prognosis of SCAP patients, with significantly reduced cumulative survival rates observed in children exhibiting high IL6-AS1 expression. In vitro experimental results showed that IL6-AS1 could be dose-dependently up-regulated by LPS. Inhibiting IL6-AS1 expression could effectively alleviate the decrease in cell activity and increase in apoptosis levels induced by LPS, thereby reducing cell damage and inflammation imbalance. Moreover, IL6-AS1 targeted microRNA-149-5p (miR-149-5p), while miR-149-5p targeted mitogen-activated protein kinase 6 (MAPK6). Further experiments confirmed that concurrent inhibition of miR-149-5p alongside suppression of IL6-AS1 reversed the protective effects mediated by the former. Conversely, simultaneous knockdown of MAPK6 mitigated the cellular damage and inflammatory response induced by miR-149-5p inhibition.ConclusionsIL6-AS1 aggravated SCAP inflammatory response and cell damage through the miR-149-5p/MAPK6 axis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-07038-4.Keywords: IL6-AS1, MAPK6, SCAP, miR-149-5p

Background

Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is among the most common causes of infectious mortality worldwide [1], while severe CAP (SCAP) is a primary contributor to patient mortality [2]. Due to their weakened immune systems and underdeveloped respiratory systems, children are more susceptible to SCAP. This disease progresses rapidly, and relying solely on clinical symptoms, physical signs, and routine auxiliary tests for diagnosis can easily delay the optimal treatment window, potentially leading to fatal outcomes in pediatric patients [3, 4]. Thus, it is of paramount importance to identify highly sensitive and specific diagnostic markers for early-stage SCAP to enable timely targeted diagnosis and treatment in clinical practice, thereby improving patient outcomes.

Long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) does not have a protein-coding function [5, 6]. It participates in key biological processes such as immune response and inflammatory response of the body through epigenetic regulation, transcriptional regulation, and competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) [7, 8]. Research indicates that in the pathophysiology of SCAP, an overactivated inflammatory response serves as a core driver of lung tissue injury and disease progression [9]. Studies have shown that lncRNA MALAT1 and lncRNA MANCR can predict the prognosis of SCAP [10, 11]. Moreover, lncRNAs may exert key regulatory roles in this process by mediating the activation of inflammatory signaling pathways through ceRNA networks [12, 13]. For instance, lncRNA MANCR participates in SCAP regulation via the microRNA (miRNA)-20a-5p/ mitogen-activated protein kinase 1 (MAPK1) axis [11], while lncRNA OIP5-AS1 modulates inflammatory responses as a ceRNA through the miR-150-5p/PDCD4 axis [12]. It is worth noting that long non-coding RNA IL6-AS1 (lncRNA IL6-AS1) is highly expressed in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [14]. IL6-AS1 promotes the inflammatory effect of lung macrophages through the S100 calcium-binding protein A9 (S100A9) / Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) pathway in the progression of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [15]. However, the regulatory role of IL6-AS1 in SCAP is still unknown. Database analysis confirms that IL6-AS1 targets and regulates miR-149-5p. Previous studies have demonstrated that miR-149-5p participates in multiple inflammation-related diseases: for instance, overexpression of miR-149-5p mitigates cerebral ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury [16]. In airway epithelial cells primarily responding to poly(I: C)-mimic viral infection, miR-149-5p functions by negatively regulating interleukin-6 (IL-6) production in BEAS-2B cells [17]. In TLR-mediated airway epithelial cell inflammation, miR-149-5p can be downregulated via the tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α)/nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) pathway after TLR2/TLR3 activation and directly regulates CHI3L1 mRNA expression, making it a potential therapeutic target in this inflammatory process [18]. Nevertheless, the role of miR-149-5p in SCAP remains unknown. The database shows that miR-149-5p targets and regulates mitogen-activated protein kinase 6 (MAPK6, also known as ERK3). MAPK6 is an atypical member of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) family, involved in cell proliferation, differentiation, inflammatory response, and the regulation of disease pathological processes [19, 20]. Research indicates that MAPK6 influences high-glucose-induced inflammatory responses, fibrosis, and oxidative stress in HK-2 cells [21]. Circ_0000285 promotes podocyte injury in diabetic nephropathy by sponging miR-654-3p and activating MAPK6 [22]. let7f-5p alleviates inflammatory damage in a pneumonia model by targeting MAPK6 [23]. Based on the above evidence, we hypothesize that the IL6-AS1/miR-149-5p/MAPK6 axis may be involved in the progression of SCAP.

To validate our hypothesis, this study combined clinical cohort analysis with basic molecular experiments to systematically investigate the expression characteristics, clinical value, and potential molecular regulatory mechanisms of IL6-AS1 in pediatric SCAP patients. For the first time, we revealed that IL6-AS1 exacerbated inflammatory responses and cellular damage through the IL6-AS1/miR-149-5p/MAPK6 ceRNA regulatory axis, potentially providing new theoretical support for SCAP diagnosis and prognostic assessment.

Methods

Study subjects

The clinical data of 160 children with SCAP hospitalized in the intensive care unit (ICU) of Affiliated Hospital of Yangzhou University were collected. Inclusion criteria: Age 1–6 years old; refer to the diagnostic criteria for SCAP, meet the definition of SCAP, and have no history of invasive procedures. Exclusion criteria: non-infectious lung diseases (such as acute asthma exacerbations, tuberculosis, interstitial pulmonary disease, etc.); those with severe underlying diseases; those with incomplete clinical data, unable to cooperate with follow-up, or those whose guardians refuse to participate in the study. All children with SCAP completed 28 days of follow-up [12], and death events were recorded.

A total of 160 volunteers from the health examination center during the same period were enrolled as healthy controls. They had no history of respiratory infections or pulmonary diseases within the past 6 months and no other medical conditions. Physical examinations revealed normal vital signs and no abnormalities on pulmonary auscultation. All guardians signed informed consent forms and cooperated with sample collection.

A total of 10 mL venous blood was collected from each participant using sterile disposable needles, and transferred into anticoagulant‑free vacuum blood collection tubes (Vacutainer system, Becton, Dickinson, NJ, USA). The serum was allowed to stand at room temperature for 10 min to complete coagulation, followed by centrifugation at 1000 x g for 15 min. The resulting supernatant was immediately aliquoted and stored in cryotubes at − 80 °C. For children aged 1–6 years, a 10 mL blood collection volume is well within the safe limit (less than 1% of total blood volume, given an average total blood volume of 80–90 mL/kg and body weight of 15–20 kg, i.e., approximately 1200–1800 mL total blood volume). This sampling volume sufficiently supports the full panel of experimental analyses, including serum isolation, RNA extraction, and reverse transcription quantitative real‑time polymerase chain reaction (RT‑qPCR), alongside the detection of multiple biomarkers: C‑reactive protein (CRP), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and procalcitonin (PCT). Accordingly, it effectively avoids the need for repeated venipuncture in pediatric participants. Meanwhile, to strictly control individual variations and standardized sampling bias, an identical 10 mL venous blood volume was collected from healthy control children. This ensured complete consistency in blood collection volume, sample processing, and detection conditions between the case group and the control group, adhering to rigorous principles for controlled comparative research.

This study has been approved by the Affiliated Hospital of Yangzhou University Ethics Committee (ethics approval number: 2022-YKL5-10-002). As this was a retrospective study without any prospective interventional procedures, relevant clinical trial registration was not applicable to the present research. Informed consent forms were signed by the legal guardians of all research subjects.

Cell culture and transfection

Human embryonic lung fibroblast cell line MRC-5 (from American Type Culture Collection, ATCC) was cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) medium (HyClone) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (HyClone). Cultures were maintained in a 37 °C, 5% CO₂ humidified incubator with medium changes every 2–3 days. Passaging occurred when confluence reached 80–90%. To establish a pneumonia inflammatory cell model, MRC-5 cells were stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) at final concentrations of 0 (blank control), 5, 10, 15, and 20 µg/mL for 12 h. Subsequent experiments employed the LPS concentration at which the very significant inflammatory phenotype first appeared.

Following LPS induction, transfection was performed when the cell confluence reached 70–80%. The transfection procedure strictly followed the Lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagent manual: small interfering RNA (siRNA, si)-IL6-AS1, si-MAPK6, miR-149-5p inhibitor (5’-GGGAGUGAAGACACGGAGCCAGA-3’), miR-149-5p mimic (5’-UCUGGCUCCGUGUCUUCACUCCC-3’), and their corresponding negative controls (inhibitor-NC: 5’-CGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3’; mimics-NC, 5’-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3’) were diluted in DMEM medium. An appropriate amount of Lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagent was then added. Cultured for 48 h before subsequent functional experiments. The siRNA, miRNA inhibitors/mimics, and corresponding negative control sequences used in this experiment were designed and synthesized by Shanghai Jima Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using the miRNeasy Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. All RNA samples exhibited A260/A280 ratios between 1.8 and 2.0, and A260/A230 ratios above 2.0, indicating no significant contamination by proteins, phenols, or salts, and adequate RNA integrity. Complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis was performed using the miScript II RT Kit (Qiagen). RT-qPCR amplification was conducted on a StepOnePlus Real-Time Fluorescent Quantitative PCR System (Applied Biosystems) using the SYBR Green qPCR SuperMix-UDG Kit (Invitrogen) for mRNA detection and miScript SYBR Green PCR Kit (Qiagen) for miRNA detection. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) served as the mRNA reference, while U6 small nuclear RNA (U6 snRNA) was used for miRNA. The primer sequences were as follows: IL-6-AS1 forward, 5’-CGTCGAGGATGTACCGAATT-3’; and reverse, 5’-GCAGCACAAGGCAAACCTCT–3’; miR-149-5p forward, 5’-CCCTCATTCTGTGCCACACTCCAGCTGGG-3’ and reverse, 5’-TGGTGTCGTGGAGTCG-3’; MAPK6 forward, 5’-TAAAGCCATTGACATGTGGG-3’ and reverse: 5’-TCGTGCACAACAGGGATAGA-3’; GAPDH forward, 5’-CTTTGGTATCGTGGAAGGACTC-3’ and reverse, 5’-GTAGAGGCAGGGATGATGTTCT-3’; U6 forward, 5’-GCTTCGGCAGCACATATACTAAAAT-3’ and reverse, 5’-CGCTTCACGAATTTGCGTGTCAT-3’.

Proliferation

Logarithmic growth phase MRC-5 cells were trypsinized, counted, and seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well. Cell counting kit‑8 (CCK‑8) assays were performed at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h post-incubation: 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each cell well and blank control well. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h in a humidified 5% CO₂ incubator. The optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA).

Apoptosis

Apoptosis levels were detected using the Annexin V-fluorescein isothiocyanate (Annexin V-FITC)/propidium iodide (PI) double staining method. The processed cells were collected. Adherent cells were digested with 0.25% trypsin, the reaction was terminated, and the cells were collected. The cells were washed 2–3 times with pre-chilled PBS and centrifuged to enrich the cells. Resuspended cells were gently pipetted in an appropriate volume of kit-provided binding buffer to form a single-cell suspension. Annexin V-FITC and PI staining solutions were added strictly, mixed thoroughly, and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 15 min. Within 1 h after incubation, use a flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) to analyze the samples. The fluorescence signals were analysed using FlowJo software (version 10.3, Tree Star, USA). Early apoptotic cells (Annexin V‑FITC⁺/PI⁻) and late apoptotic/necrotic cells (Annexin V‑FITC⁺/PI⁺) were combined to calculate the total apoptosis rate. This chart presented the results of five independent experiments (mean±standard deviation), which were obtained through three repeated measurements. The figure shown was derived from a single representative technical replicate.

ELISA

Protein levels of cytokines interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), and interleukin-10 (IL-10) were quantified using the ELISA kit (Invitrogen).

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) experiments

RIP experiments were performed according to the Magna RIP™ RNA-Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation Kit (Millipore Sigma) protocol. Cells were lysed on ice using the kit’s provided lysis buffer. The Protein A/G magnetic bead suspension and continued incubation at 4 °C for 2 h were performed to capture the antibody-target protein-RNA complex. Subsequently, purify the sample by washing to thoroughly remove non-specifically bound proteins and RNA contaminants. Recover and purify the released RNA using an RNA purification kit. After assessing RNA purity and concentration via Nanodrop, amplify the target RNA fragment using RT-qPCR technology to analyze the binding type and relative abundance of the RNA to the target protein.

Dual luciferase assay

Potential binding sites of miR-149-5p on IL6-AS1 and MAPK6 mRNA were screened using the ENCORI database. Based on the prediction results, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification was performed on fragments of IL6-AS1 and MAPK6 containing the binding sites (wild-type, WT), while fragments with mutations in the core sequence of the binding sites were designed and amplified (mutant-type, MUT). These fragments were subcloned into the pGL4.10 to construct recombinant vectors pGL4.10-IL6-AS1-WT/MUT and pGL4.10-MAPK6-WT/MUT. Human embryonic kidney 293T cells (HEK293T cells) cells were seeded in 24-well plates. And upon reaching 70–80% confluence, co-transfected with miR-149-5p-mimic/ mimic-NC or miR-149-5p-inhibitor/ inhibitor-NC using Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent. After 48 h of transfection, cells were washed once with PBS and lysed with 100 µL of passive lysis buffer (Promega) for 15 min at room temperature. Luciferase activity was measured using a dual‑luciferase reporter assay system (Promega) on a multifunctional microplate reader (Thermo Fisher). Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity for each well.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 21.0. Intergroup comparisons employed independent samples t-tests, nonparametric tests, and chi-square analysis. The diagnostic efficacy of serum IL6-AS1 for SCAP was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. Multivariate logistic regression models were employed to identify independent risk factors for SCAP onset, while the prognosis assessment was conducted using multivariate Cox regression and Kaplan-Meier methods. Pearson correlation analysis was applied to assess correlations between IL6-AS1 expression and clinical indicators. A P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

General clinical characteristics

This study compared the general clinical data of 160 healthy controls and 160 children with SCAP. The white blood cell (WBC), C-reactive protein (CRP), neutrophil count, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and procalcitonin (PCT) levels were significantly higher in SCAP, suggesting that children with SCAP may have a significant inflammatory response. Children with SCAP were further divided into survival subgroup (n = 112) and death subgroup (n = 48) according to prognosis for intra-group comparison. The results showed that the LDH in the death subgroup (P = 0.016) and the level of PCT (P = 0.015) were significantly higher than those in the survival subgroup (Table 1).

Expression and clinical value of IL6-AS1 in SCAP

IL6-AS1 expression was significantly upregulated in SCAP patients (Fig. 1A). ROC diagnostic performance analysis showed that IL6-AS1 has good diagnostic value for SCAP, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.8987 (95% confidence interval (CI) = 0.8462–0.9333), corresponding to a diagnostic sensitivity of 85.00% and a specificity of 86.88% (Fig. 1B). 160 SCAP patients were divided into low expression group (n = 72) and high expression group (n = 88) according to IL6-AS1 expression level (average value). The results showed that the IL6-AS1 level in the high expression group was significantly higher than that in the low expression group (Fig. 1C). Comparison of clinical characteristics between groups showed (Table 2): The levels of CRP, LDH, and PCT in the high expression group were significantly higher than those in the low expression group. Pearson correlation analysis results showed that the IL6-AS1 expression in SCAP patients was significantly positively correlated with CRP (r = 0.616), LDH (r = 0.728), and PCT (r = 0.617). This indicated that IL6-AS1 expression was closely related to the intensity of the patient’s inflammatory response and the degree of tissue damage (Table 3). The results of multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that LDH level, PCT level, and IL6-AS1 expression were all independent risk factors affecting the SCAP (Supplementary Table 1). Further subgroup analysis showed that IL6-AS1 expression in the SCAP death patient subgroup was significantly higher than that in the survival subgroup (Fig. 1D), suggesting that high IL6-AS1 expression may be associated with poor patient prognosis. Kaplan-Meier survival curve analysis further confirmed that the cumulative survival rate of SCAP patients in the IL6-AS1 high expression group was significantly lower than that in the low expression group (Fig. 1E). Multifactor Cox risk regression found that L6AS1 expression, LDH, and PCT were all independent significant risk factors for poor patient prognosis (Supplementary Table 2).

IL6-AS1 exacerbated LPS-induced cellular damage and inflammatory response

LPS exhibited a dose-dependent reduction in cell viability (Fig. 2A), promoted apoptosis (Fig. 2B), and upregulated IL6-AS1 levels (Fig. 2C). Given that significant differences in cell viability, apoptosis levels, and IL6-AS1 expression were observed following 10 µg/mL LPS treatment, this concentration was used for subsequent interventions. Additionally, LPS induced the release of proinflammatory factors (IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β) while suppressing the expression of the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 (Fig. 2D). Silencing efficiency validation revealed that IL6-AS1 expression in the si-IL6-AS1 group was significantly lower than in the si-NC group (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, IL6-AS1 expression in the LPS + si-IL6-AS1 group was markedly lower than in the LPS + si-NC group, indicating reliable silencing efficacy of IL6-AS1 (Fig. 2F). Further functional experiments demonstrated that IL6-AS1 knockdown partially reversed LPS-induced cellular damage and inflammatory imbalance. Specifically, it resulted in partial recovery of cell viability (Fig. 2G), reduced apoptosis levels (Fig. 2H), and attenuated inflammatory responses (decreased levels of pro-inflammatory factors IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β, and increased levels of anti-inflammatory factor IL-10, Fig. 2I). These findings suggested that IL6-AS1 may be a key promoter of LPS-mediated cellular damage and inflammatory responses.

Regulatory relationships between IL6-AS1, miR-149-5p, and MAPK6

Prediction using the ENCORI database revealed a potential binding site between IL6-AS1 and miR-149-5p (Fig. 3A). RIP results showed that IL6-AS1 and miR-149-5p were significantly enriched in the Argonaute 2 (Ago2)-precipitated fraction compared to the immunoglobulin G (IgG) control, suggesting both participate in RNA-induced silencing complex assembly (Fig. 3B). Dual luciferase assays further validated that: Co-transfection with miR-149-5p mimic significantly reduced luciferase activity in IL6-AS1-WT but had no significant effect on the IL6-AS1-MUT, confirming the targeted binding relationship between IL6-AS1 and miR-149-5p (Fig. 3C). Clinical sample analysis revealed significantly lower miR-149-5p expression levels in patients with SCAP (Fig. 3D). ENCORI database prediction indicated a potential binding site between MAPK6 and miR-149-5p (Fig. 3E). RIP and dual luciferase reporter assay results showed that MAPK6 is a direct target gene of miR-149-5p (Fig. 3F-G). Clinical sample analysis further revealed significantly higher MAPK6 expression levels in SCAP patients compared to healthy controls (Fig. 3H). Correlation analysis revealed that in SCAP patient samples, IL6-AS1 expression was significantly negatively correlated with miR-149-5p expression (r = -0.7414, Fig. 3I); IL6-AS1 and MAPK6 expression showed a significant positive correlation (r = 0.7216, Fig. 3J). miR-149-5p showed a significant negative correlation with MAPK6 expression (r = -0.7689, Fig. 3K). These findings suggested that IL6-AS1 may act as a ceRNA, relieving post-transcriptional repression of its target gene, MAPK6, by binding to miR-149-5p. These three factors may participate in the molecular pathological process of SCAP through the IL6-AS1/miR-149-5p/MAPK6 regulatory axis.

IL6-AS1 exacerbated LPS-induced cellular injury and inflammatory response via the miR-149-5p/MAPK6 axis

LPS dose-dependently downregulated miR-149-5p expression while upregulating MAPK6 levels (Fig. 4A-B). miR-149-5p inhibitor significantly reduced miR-149-5p expression (Fig. 4C). Knockdown of IL6-AS1 promoted miR-149-5p expression, while co-transfection with miR-149-5p inhibitor effectively reversed this upregulation (Fig. 4D). si-MAPK6 effectively silenced the MAPK6 gene (Fig. 4E). Further experiments demonstrated that si-IL6-AS1 and miR-149-5p inhibitor synergistically upregulated MAPK6 expression, while si-MAPK6 effectively reversed this upregulation (Fig. 4F). Functional recovery experiment demonstrated that in this model, inhibiting miR-149-5p partially reversed the trend of restored cell activity, reduced apoptosis, and weakened inflammation. Specifically, it manifested as decreased cell proliferation capacity, increased apoptosis levels, and exacerbated inflammatory responses. Furthermore, knocking down MAPK6 on this basis mitigated cellular damage and inflammatory imbalance (Fig. 4G-I).

Discussion

The pathogenesis of SCAP is complex, and an uncontrolled inflammatory response is the core pathological link that induces lung tissue damage, multiple organ dysfunction, and even death [24]. LncRNA has been confirmed to participate in the inflammatory imbalance process of disease through various regulatory mechanisms such as ceRNA [25, 26]. This study combined clinical sample analysis and in vitro functional experiments to confirm that IL6-AS1 was highly expressed in children with SCAP, and that this molecule has potential clinical value in disease diagnosis and prognosis assessment. Mechanistic studies further showed that IL6-AS1 could target and adsorb miR-149-5p through the ceRNA mechanism, thereby releasing its transcriptional inhibitory effect on the target gene MAPK6, up-regulating the MAPK6 level, and ultimately exacerbating the imbalance of cell damage and inflammatory response induced by LPS.

Previous studies have shown that IL6-AS1 expression tends to be up-regulated in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and its expression level is closely related to the degree of airway inflammation [14]. IL6-AS1 has also been confirmed to be involved in the regulation of the pathological process of inflammatory diseases such as ulcerative colitis [27]. Through the analysis of clinical samples, we found that IL6-AS1 showed significantly high expression in the serum of children with SCAP, and this molecule has good diagnostic and differential performance for SCAP. Further correlation analysis showed that the IL6-AS1 level was significantly positively correlated with classic inflammatory markers (CRP, PCT) and tissue damage markers (LDH), suggesting that it was closely related to the degree of inflammatory response and tissue damage process in SCAP. Prognostic analysis confirmed that high expression of IL6-AS1 was an independent risk factor for poor prognosis in children with SCAP. In summary, our in vitro data suggested that IL6-AS1 binds miR-149-5p via a ceRNA mechanism, relieving its post-transcriptional suppression of target gene MAPK6, which in turn upregulated MAPK6 expression and may exacerbate LPS-induced cellular hypofunction, increased apoptosis, and inflammatory dysregulation in lung fibroblasts. These findings identify the IL6-AS1/miR-149-5p/MAPK6 axis as a potential contributor to SCAP pathogenesis, warranting further in vivo and tissue-level investigation.

The ceRNA mechanism is a classic way for lncRNA to regulate gene expression [28, 29]. This study provides preliminary evidence suggesting that IL6-AS1 may regulate the miR-149-5p/MAPK6 axis through the ceRNA mechanism and thereby potentially participate in the imbalance process of lung tissue damage and inflammation in SCAP. However, these findings were currently limited to an LPS-stimulated MRC-5 cell model and require further validation in animal models, primary human airway epithelial cells, and clinical tissue specimens. Verification of clinical samples and the LPS-induced MRC-5 cell inflammation model showed that the expression patterns of IL6-AS1 and MAPK6 were consistent, and functional experiments confirmed that silencing IL6-AS1 could partially reverse the inhibition of cell activity, increased apoptosis, and imbalance of inflammatory factor secretion caused by LPS, indicating that IL6-AS1 was a pro-inflammatory molecule that mediated pathological damage in SCAP. This was also consistent with previous studies, which have shown that IL6-AS1 may be a key regulator during severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS)-CoV infection and severe acute respiratory syndrome [30]. In chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), upregulation of IL6-AS1 exacerbates lung inflammation [15].

This study confirmed through bioinformatics prediction combined with RIP and dual-luciferase reporter gene experiments: IL6-AS1 could target the adsorption of miR-149-5p. Its targeting relationship has also been reported in the past [14]. Analysis of clinical samples showed that IL6-AS1 and MAPK6 were highly expressed in children with SCAP, while miR-149-5p was low-expressed. The expression correlation between the three was fully consistent with the typical characteristics of the ceRNA regulatory network. Further functional experiments confirmed that miR-149-5p played an anti-inflammatory role in SCAP. Inhibiting the expression of miR-149-5p could partly reverse the cytoprotective effect mediated by silencing IL6-AS1, confirming that it was a key mediator for IL6-AS1 to regulate downstream inflammatory signals. This was also consistent with previous studies, which have shown that in respiratory virus infection models, overexpression of miR-149-5p can inhibit the mRNA levels of IL-6 and tumor protein p63 [17], miR-149-5p inhibits the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines in synovial fibroblasts in osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis [31], and increasing miR-149-5p can improve sepsis-induced acute lung injury [32].

MAPK6 is an atypical mitogen-activated protein kinase [33], and MAPK6 expression is significantly increased in the LPS-induced pneumonia model [23]. miR-128-3p suppresses inflammation by targeting penicillin-induced MAPK6 in astrocytes [34]. Our study found that the MAPK6 expression was up-regulated in SCAP, and the recovery experiment further verified that inhibiting IL6-AS1 and inhibiting miR-149-5p at the same time could reverse the protective effects of restoring cell activity, reducing apoptosis, and attenuating inflammation mediated by the former, while knocking down MAPK6 at the same time could again partly alleviate the cell damage and inflammatory response caused by inhibiting miR-149-5p. In addition, this study also revealed the positive feedback inflammation amplification loop mediated by this regulatory axis: inflammatory stimuli such as LPS could induce high expression of IL6-AS1, which released its post-transcriptional inhibition of MAPK6 by adsorbing miR-149-5p, leading to up-regulation of MAPK6 expression and further exacerbating inflammatory responses and cell damage, ultimately forming a positive feedback loop in which inflammation continues to worsen. This regulatory logic was fully verified in the recovery experiment.

This study still has certain limitations. First, this was a single‑center study with a relatively limited sample size. The absence of an external validation cohort may limit the generalizability of the diagnostic and prognostic conclusions. Larger, multicenter prospective studies with independent validation cohorts are needed to confirm the clinical utility of IL6-AS1. Second, this study relies solely on an LPS-stimulated MRC-5 lung fibroblast cell line for in vitro functional experiments. The role of the IL6-AS1/miR-149-5p/MAPK6 regulatory axis has not been verified in animal models, primary human airway epithelial cells, or lung tissue specimens from SCAP patients. Therefore, the current findings should be interpreted as preliminary, and the proposed regulatory axis requires further validation in more physiologically relevant systems. In addition, the specific downstream signaling pathways through which MAPK6 mediates lung tissue damage and inflammation imbalance in the pathological process of SCAP remain to be further explored. Future studies will address these limitations by conducting multicenter collaborations to increase sample size, incorporating external validation cohorts, and establishing SCAP animal models to further investigate the underlying mechanisms.

Conclusions

IL6-AS1 was significantly highly expressed in children with SCAP, and this molecule may have reliable disease diagnostic performance and prognostic evaluation value. Moreover, IL6-AS1 can target and adsorb miR-149-5p through the ceRNA mechanism, relieve its transcriptional inhibitory effect on the downstream target gene MAPK6, and thereby upregulate the MAPK6 level. This regulatory process could significantly aggravate the imbalance of cell damage and inflammatory response induced by LPS.
