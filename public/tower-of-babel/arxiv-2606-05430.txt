Abstract
. This work establishes a bipartite generalization of the Harer-Zagier formula using non-Hermitian Random Matrix Theory. More specifically, we use a decomposition of powers of Ginibre eigenvalues as a superposition of independent point processes to identify all coefficients of the generating function of the genus of a surface obtained by a random bipartite pairing of the sides of one polygon with k ​ M kM sides and k k polygons with M M sides.



1.
Introduction

In the celebrated work
[
10
]
, Harer and Zagier endeavored to study the Euler characteristic of moduli spaces of curves; this boils down to evaluating the numbers
\epsilon_{g}(n)
, which are defined as the number of ways a surface of genus
g\geq 0
can be obtained by a pairing of the sides of one polygonal face with
2n
sides (as represented on Figure
1
). Using techniques from hermitian Random Matrix Theory, especially a remarkable three-term recurrence on the moments of a GUE matrix, they obtain several equivalent characterizations of these numbers
\epsilon_{g}(n)
. For instance, the generating series of these coefficients is given by the following formula:

Theorem 1.1

(Harer-Zagier generating function
[
10
]
)
.

Let
n
be a non-negative integer; then the following equality holds:

\sum_{g=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\epsilon_{g}(n)\cdot N^{n+1-2g}=(2n-1)!!\sum_{l=0}^{n}2^{l}\binom{n}{l}\binom{N}{l+1}

for all integers
N
.

Figure 1.
A pairing between the sides of a
12
-gon (
n=6
).

Equivalently, the coefficients
\epsilon_{g}(n)
can be expressed with a closed form:

Theorem 1.2

(Harer-Zagier coefficients)
.

For
n,g\geq 1
we have the following equality

\epsilon_{g}(n)=(2n-1)!!\cdot 2^{n-2g}\sum_{p=0}^{2g}\frac{(-2)^{p}}{(n+1+p-2g)!}\binom{n}{n+p-2g}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n+1+p-2g}{n+1-2},

where
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}
is the unsigned Stirling number of the first kind.

The present work deals with a bipartite version of the above formulas. Let
k
and
M
be positive integers. Given a
kM
-gon and
k
distinct
M
-gons, we consider all bipartite edge pairings between the
kM
-gon and the
k
distinct
M
-gons; by bipartite, we mean that no pairing is allowed within the
kM
-gon, or between the smaller
M
-gons; this is represented on Figure
2
. With the same interpretation of edge pairings as before, we identify them as one edge and connect the faces without any twists to form an orientable surface. The question is then to study the genus of a surface obtained in this way. We will denote the number of such pairings leading to a surface of genus
g
as
\epsilon_{g}(k,M)
.

Figure 2.
Example of a bipartite pairing between the edges of a dodecagon and those of four triangles, i.e.
k=4
,
M=3
.

We establish the following formula for the generating function of these coefficients:

Theorem 1.3

(Bipartite generating function)
.

Let
k
and
M
be positive integers. Then we have the following equality

(1)

\sum_{g=0}^{\lfloor k(M-1)/2\rfloor}\epsilon_{g}(k,M)\cdot N^{k(M-1)+1-2g}=\frac{1}{kM+1}\sum_{l=0}^{k}(-1)^{l}\binom{k}{l}F_{kM+1}^{+}(N-lM).

for all integers
N
, where
F_{n}^{+}
denotes the rising factorial (Def.
1.7
).

We also derive an explicit closed-form expression for the number of pairings in Section
4.2
:

Theorem 1.4

(Bipartite coefficients)
.

For all integers
k,M>0
,
g\geq 0
, we have the following identity

\epsilon_{g}(k,M)=\frac{(-1)^{k}k!}{kM+1}\sum_{p=0}^{2g}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{kM+1}{p+kM+1-2g}\binom{p+kM+1-2g}{p+k}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{p+k}{k}(-M)^{p+k},

where
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}
and
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}
are, respectively, Stirling numbers of the first and second kind.

In Section
5
we recover the Harer-Zagier formula as a particular case of the above bipartite setting, noticing the relation:

\epsilon_{g}(k,2)=k!\cdot 2^{k}\cdot\epsilon_{g}(k).

between the original Harer-Zagier coefficients
\epsilon_{g}(k)
and the bipartite coefficients
\epsilon_{g}(k,M)
with
M=2
.
These results were derived from Random Matrix techniques as part of the recent bachelor’s thesis work
[
15
]
. Counting bipartite pairings is equivalent to counting the number of cycles in a commutator between random permutations with prescribed cycle structure, or the number of Grothendieck’s
dessins d’enfants
with prescribed degrees, which has also been achieved by other means – especially combinatorial and representation-theoretic methods. Most importantly, Jackson’s works
[
12
,
13
]
allow to enumerate products of permutations with given number of cycles, and Chen
[
5
]
recently recovered and extended this result using representation theory, while generalizing the Harer-Zagier formula by characterizing the number of products of cycles with specified conjugacy classes. The purpose of this work is to rederive equivalent expressions directly from RMT tools, and especially to illustrate the use of the complex Ginibre block-decomposition (Theorem
2.3
) for such a computation. One may hope to go further in this direction: indeed, the systematic use of non-hermitian RMT to tackle combinatorial problems seems particularly promising, due to the great progress made in recent years in the study of spectral moments of complex, real and quaternionic Ginibre ensembles
[
1
,
3
]
.

The structure of the paper is as follows:

•

Section
2
presents the statements and provides references for the random matrix results that are required in the proofs of our main theorems.

•

In Section
3
, we analyze the initial cases in detail in order to illustrate the method.

•

In Section
4
, we establish the bipartite version of the Harer–Zagier formulas for arbitrary parameters
k
and
M
, with the exact computation of the generating function (Theorem
1.3
), and the derivation of the explicit closed-form expression of the coefficients
\epsilon_{g}(k,M)
(Theorem
1.4
).

•

Finally, in Section
5
, we show that our result indeed constitutes a genuine generalization, in the sense that the classical Harer–Zagier formula can be recovered as a special case.

1.1.
Notations

We recall a few important definitions and properties that we rely on throughout the paper.

Definition 1.5
.

Let
\alpha
be a positive real number. The Gamma distribution with parameter
\alpha
is defined by the density

\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}t^{\alpha-1}e^{-t}

with respect to Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
where
\Gamma
is the Gamma function, defined as

\Gamma(\alpha)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}t^{\alpha-1}e^{-t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

Lemma 1.6
.

Let
\gamma_{\alpha}
be a gamma variable with parameter
\alpha
, then the
M
-th moment of
\gamma_{\alpha}
is given by

\mathbb{E}\left[{(\gamma_{\alpha})^{M}}\right]=\frac{\Gamma(M+\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha)}.

Proof.

By direct computation,

\mathbb{E}\left[{\gamma_{\alpha}^{M}}\right]=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}t^{M}t^{\alpha-1}e^{-t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}t^{(M+\alpha)-1}e^{-t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\frac{\Gamma(M+\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha)}.

∎

We also introduce the following notations for rising and falling factorials.

Definition 1.7
.

Let
n
be a non-negative integer. We call
F_{n}^{+}
and
F_{n}^{-}
the rising and falling factorial, defined as

\displaystyle F_{n}^{+}(X)

\displaystyle=X(X+1)\cdots(X+n-1)

\displaystyle F_{n}^{-}(X)

\displaystyle=X(X-1)\cdots(X-n+1)=(-1)^{n}F_{n}^{+}(-X).

By convention we denote
F_{0}^{+}=F_{0}^{-}=1
. Note that by definition
F_{n}^{+}
and
F_{n}^{-}
are polynomials of degree
n
.

The following lemma will be used multiple times.

Lemma 1.8
.

Let
n
and
k
be positive integers, then

F_{n}^{+}(k)=n\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\Gamma(n+j-1)}{\Gamma(j)}.

Proof.

The derivation comes from the discrete differential operator:

\partial:P(X)\mapsto P(X)-P(X-1).

Note that

\partial F_{n}^{+}=X\cdots(X+n-1)-(X-1)\cdots(X+n-2)

=X\cdots(X+n-2)\cdot[(X+n-1)-(X-1)]=nF_{n-1}^{+}.

Thus

F_{n}^{+}(k)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\partial F_{n}^{+}(j)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}nF_{n-1}^{+}(j)=n\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\Gamma(n+j-1)}{\Gamma(j)}.

∎

Definition 1.9
.

Let
n
be a positive integer. The signed Stirling numbers of the first kind
s(n,k)
are given by the coefficients of the falling factorial:

F_{n}^{-}(X)=X(X-1)\cdots(X-n+1)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}s(n,k)X^{k}.

The unsigned Stirling numbers of the first kind
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}
are given by the coefficients of the rising factorial:

F_{n}^{+}(X)=X(X+1)\cdots(X+n-1)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}X^{k}.

Definition 1.10
.

Let
n
and
k
be non-negative integers. The Stirling number of the second kind
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}
is the number of ways to partition a set of
n
labeled objects into
k
non-empty subsets.

We notice a similar link between Stirling numbers of the second kind and the falling factorials.

Proposition 1.11
.

Let
r
be a non-negative integer, we have the relation:

\sum_{m=0}^{r}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{r}{m}F_{m}^{-}(X)=X^{r}.

We present two useful properties of Stirling numbers of the second kind:

Proposition 1.12
.

Let
n
and
k
be non-negative integers, then

\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}=\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{k-j}\binom{k}{j}j^{n}.

Lemma 1.13
.

Let
r
be a positive integer, then

\sum_{m=0}^{r}(-1)^{m}\cdot m!\cdot\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{r}{m}=(-1)^{r}.

Proof.

We will use the Proposition
1.11
with
X=-1
:

(-1)^{r}=\sum_{m=0}^{r}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{r}{m}F_{m}^{-}(-1)=\sum_{m=0}^{r}(-1)^{m}m!\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{r}{m}.

∎

2.
Preliminaries: Ginibre eigenvalues and genus expansion

The present work relies on the properties of the complex Ginibre ensemble of random matrices. For a positive integer
N
, let
G
be an (unscaled)
N\times N
Complex Ginibre matrix, i.e. a random matrix with i.i.d. Gaussian entries

G_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(0,1)

for all
1\leq i,j\leq N
. The fact that
G
is a non-Hermitian matrix with complex coefficients is relevant to the purpose of modeling bipartite pairings with orientation-preserving identifications of edges. We build upon foundations that were laid especially in the works
[
6
,
8
,
9
,
11
,
14
]
among other sources, and recall here the theorems that will be needed in the next sections.

2.1.
Radii and powers of complex Ginibre eigenvalues

Eigenvalues of Ginibre Ensembles have been studied extensively since the seminal 1965 paper
[
9
]
, and we make use of some of their well-known properties. The first property we need is the independence of eigenvalue radii, a result known as Kostlan’s theorem:

Theorem 2.1

(Kostlan,
[
14
]
)
.

Let
\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N}\}
be the set of eigenvalues of Ginibre
G
, the following equality in distribution holds:

\{|\lambda_{1}|^{2},\ldots,|\lambda_{N}|^{2}\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\{\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{N}\},

where
(\gamma_{k})
are independent gamma variables with parameters
1,\ldots,N
respectively.

Another phenomenon is that high powers (i.e. with exponent
M\geq N
) are distributed as a set of independent variables with explicit distributions:

Theorem 2.2

(Hough, Krishnapur, Peres, Virág
[
11
]
)
.

Let
\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N}\}
be the set of eigenvalues of a complex Ginibre matrix
G
. For any
M\geq N
, the following equality in distribution holds:

\{\lambda_{1}^{M},\ldots,\lambda_{N}^{M}\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\{\gamma_{1}^{M/2}e^{i\theta_{1}},\ldots,\gamma_{N}^{M/2}e^{i\theta_{N}}\},

where
(\gamma_{k}),(\theta_{k})
are independent and
(\theta_{k})
are i.i.d. real variables uniform in
[0,2\pi]
and
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{N}
are gamma variables with corresponding parameters
1,\ldots,N
.

More generally, it was proved in
[
7
]
that for any power
M
, the powers of the eigenvalues decorrelate to some extent: in the case of intermediate power, when
M<N
, one still finds a description of the spectrum as the superposition of
M
independent point processes:

Theorem 2.3

(Block Decomposition
[
7
]
)
.

Let
\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N}\}
be the set of eigenvalues of a complex Ginibre matrix
G
. For
M\geq 1
, the following equality in distribution holds:

\{\lambda_{1}^{M},\ldots,\lambda_{N}^{M}\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{N}\},

where by denoting
I_{p}=\{i\in[N]\mid i\equiv p\pmod{M}\}
for
1\leq p\leq M
,
(z_{i})_{i\in I_{p}}
form independent blocks for different values of
p
, with joint density

\frac{1}{Z_{N,M,p}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i<j\\
i,j\in I_{p}\end{subarray}}|z_{i}-z_{j}|^{2}\prod_{i\in I_{p}}|z_{i}|^{\frac{2(p-M)}{M}}e^{-|z_{i}|^{2/M}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m(z_{i}),

where

Z_{N,M,p}=\pi^{c_{p}}c_{p}!M^{c_{p}}\prod_{j\in I_{p}}(j-1)!,~\text{and}~~c_{p}=|I_{p}|.

2.2.
Genus expansion

The computations presented here relies on the technique called genus expansion; this is a well-known technique in Random Matrix Theory, going back to the pioneering work of Wigner
[
18
,
19
]
, used in the context of several-matrix models
[
4
]
, in free probability
[
16
,
17
]
, as well as in enumeration of maps
[
20
,
2
]
. An exposition of this method is given in
[
6
]
, for words in complex Ginibre matrices specifically. Let
k
and
M
be positive integers. For the current purpose, we define

(2)

T_{k,M}(N):=\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{tr}(G^{kM})\overline{\mathrm{tr}(G^{M})^{k}}}\right],

where
N
is the size of the Complex Ginibre Matrix
G
. Using genus expansion, one can see that
T_{k,M}(N)
is a polynomial expression in
N
, and that it matches exactly the generating function of the coefficients
\epsilon_{g}(k,M)
:

Proposition 2.4
.

For any positive integers
k
and
M
,

(i)

T_{k,M}
is a polynomial in
N
and it can be written as

\displaystyle T_{{k},{M}}\left(N\right)=\sum_{g=0}^{\lfloor k(M-1)/2\rfloor}\epsilon_{g}(k,M)\cdot N^{k(M-1)+1-2g},

(ii)

T_{{k},{M}}\left(0\right)=0
,

(iii)

T_{k,M}
is a polynomial in
N
and
\deg(T_{k,M})=k(M-1)+1
,

(iv)

T_{k,M}
is odd if
2\mid k(M-1)
, and even otherwise.

Proof.

This is a particular case of genus expansion for words in complex Ginibre matrices, as explained in the paper
[
6
]
. In this case the words are simply powers of one Ginibre matrix of size
N
. Thus, (i) is given directly by the genus expansion formula, the proof of which can be described in the following way: one writes down the expansion of the terms
\mathrm{tr}G^{kM}
and
(\mathrm{tr}G^{M})^{k}
and then apply Wick’s formula, transforming the expectation in (
2
) into a sum over pairings that has exactly the same structure as a bipartite pairing counted by the numbers
\epsilon_{g}(k,M)
.
Properties (ii) and (iii) are then immediate consequences of this construction, as the exponent
k(M-1)+1-2g
is the number of vertices on the resulting graph (after identifications), and this number is clearly an integer between
1
and
k(M-1)+1
. We also notice that
\epsilon_{0}(k,M)\neq 0
to prove the third item, as by inspection there is always at least one way to form a sphere from one
kM
-gon and
k
separate
M
-gon (in fact these numbers are the Fuss-Catalan numbers (
9
)). For property (iv), we notice that all powers of
N
are of the same parity as
k(M-1)+1
, thus the polynomial
T_{k,M}
is either even or odd, depending on the values of
k
and
M
.
∎

3.
Bipartite formula: the first cases

We first provide a detailed computation of the first cases (
k=1,2,3,4
) of our main result. The common strategy to all cases is to evaluate the values
T_{k,M}(N)
at more than
\deg(T_{k,M})=k(M-1)+1
values, in order to fully characterize the polynomial
T_{k,M}
; this requires a detailed analysis as
k
grows larger, but for small values of
k
the computation is simplified. The case
k=1
was solved in
[
8
]
, which was the starting point of this project and of the bachelor thesis
[
15
]
. It turns out that the second case (
k=2
) can be solved with very little extra work. The next cases
k=3
and
k=4
can also be solved by a similar computation, with an extra ingredient, namely the block decomposition
2.3
, thus opening the way to the general proof presented in section
4
.

3.1.
Case
k=1

We consider bipartite pairings of two polygons of size
M
, as represented on Figure
3
.

Figure 3.
Example of a bipartite pairing of the sides of two hexagons i.e.
k=1
,
M=6
.

It was noticed in
[
8
]
(although with a different goal and specific notations) that one can evaluate the polynomial
T_{1,M}
directly from the distribution of high powers (Theorem
2.2
) and square radii (Theorem
2.1
). We give the details of this computation for the convenience of the reader.

Proposition 3.1
.

For
0\leq N\leq M
, we have the following equality

(3)

T_{1,M}(N)=\frac{1}{M+1}F_{M+1}^{+}(N).

Proof.

Going back to the definition (
2
), we find

T_{1,M}(N)=\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{tr}(G^{M})\overline{\mathrm{tr}(G^{M})}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[{\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}^{M}\overline{\lambda_{j}^{M}}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[{\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}z_{i}\overline{z_{j}}}\right],

where
(z_{j})_{j=1}^{N}
are the variables from Theorem
2.2
corresponding to the high powers
\lambda_{j}^{M}
with
M\geq N
. Using the independence of these variables, we find that the contribution is non-zero only if
j=i
; therefore by Kostlan’s theorem
2.1
, Lemma
1.6
and finally Lemma
1.8
, we find that

T_{1,M}(N)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[{|z_{i}|^{2}}\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\Gamma(M+i)}{\Gamma(i)}=\frac{1}{M+1}F_{M+1}^{+}(N),

which was the claim.
∎

Proposition
3.1
allows us to identify the polynomial
T_{1,M}
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
M
be a positive integer. Then we have that for every integer
N
,

(4)

T_{{1},{M}}\left(N\right)=\frac{1}{M+1}[F_{M+1}^{+}(N)-F_{M+1}^{-}(N)]=\frac{1}{M+1}\left[F_{M+1}^{+}(N)-F_{M+1}^{+}(N-M)\right].

Proof.

From propositions
3.1
and
2.4
we have that the polynomial

\frac{1}{M+1}\cdot F_{M+1}^{+}(N)-T_{{1},{M}}\left(N\right)

has
M+1
zeros
\{0,1,\ldots,M\}
, and it is of degree
M+1
, hence

\frac{1}{M+1}\cdot F_{M+1}^{+}(N)-T_{{1},{M}}\left(N\right)=C\cdot N(N-1)\cdots(N-M)=C\cdot F_{M+1}^{-}(N).

By inspection of the leading coefficient, we find the constant
C=\frac{1}{M+1}
and conclude that

T_{{1},{M}}\left(N\right)=\frac{1}{M+1}[F_{M+1}^{+}(N)-F_{M+1}^{-}(N)].

The second part of the claim comes from the correspondence between rising and falling factorial:

F_{M+1}^{-}(X)=F_{M+1}^{+}(X-M).

∎

This settles the case
k=1
. At that point, let us notice that we can generalize Proposition
3.1
to every
k
and find that the values of
T_{k,M}
in the integer interval
[\![1,M]\!]
are exactly given by those of a rising factorial. However, this fact alone is not sufficient to characterize the polynomial
T_{k,M}
in general.

Proposition 3.3
.

For any
k\geq 1
and
0\leq N\leq M
, we have the following equality

T_{k,M}(N)=\frac{1}{kM+1}F_{kM+1}^{+}(N).

Proof.

Similarly to the proof of Proposition
3.1
, we notice

T_{k,M}(N)=\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{tr}(G^{kM})\overline{\mathrm{tr}(G^{M})^{k}}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[{\sum_{i,j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}^{kM}\overline{\lambda_{j_{1}}^{M}}\cdots\overline{\lambda_{j_{k}}^{M}}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[{\sum_{i,j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}=1}^{N}z_{i}^{k}\overline{z_{j_{1}}}\ldots\overline{z_{j_{k}}}}\right],

where
(z_{n})_{n}
are the independent variables from Theorem
2.2
. It follows from the fact that these variables are independent with uniform argument that the only possible non-zero contributions to the sum are when
j_{1}=\cdots=j_{k}=i
. Therefore by Kostlan’s theorem
2.1
, Lemma
1.6
and Lemma
1.8
, we conclude that

T_{k,M}(N)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[{|z_{i}|^{2k}}\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\Gamma(kM+i)}{\Gamma(i)}=\frac{1}{kM+1}F_{kM+1}^{+}(N).

∎

3.2.
Case
k=2

We now consider bipartite pairings of the edges of a
2M
-gon with two separate
M
-gons, as represented on Figure
4
. We observe that in this case the polynomial
T_{2,M}
can be determined directly from proposition
3.3
, together with a parity argument.

Figure 4.
Example of a bipartite pairing of the sides of a hexagon and two triangles i.e.
k=2
,
M=3
.

Proposition 3.4
.

For
-M\leq N\leq M
we have the following equality

T_{2,M}(N)=\frac{1}{2M+1}\left[F_{2M+1}^{+}(N)+F_{2M+1}^{-}(N)\right]=\frac{1}{2M+1}\left[F_{2M+1}^{+}(N)+F_{2M+1}^{+}(N-2M)\right].

Proof.

The values
T_{2,M}(N)
for
N\in[\![0,M]\!]
are given by Proposition
3.3
and match with the claim.
As
\deg(T_{2,M})=2M-1
and
T_{2,M}
is odd from Proposition
2.4
, then for
-M\leq N\leq-1
:

(-1)^{2M-1}T_{2,M}(N)=T_{2,M}(-N)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Prop. \ref{prop:kM_1M}}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2M+1}F_{2M+1}^{+}(-N)=\frac{-1}{2M+1}F_{2M+1}^{-}(N).

Hence
T_{2,M}(N)=\frac{1}{2M+1}F_{2M+1}^{-}(N)
for
-M\leq N\leq-1
. We have thus verified that

T_{2,M}(N)=\frac{1}{2M+1}\left[F_{2M+1}^{+}(N)+F_{2M+1}^{-}(N)\right]

holds for
-M\leq N\leq M
.
∎

Note that the polynomial
T_{2,M}
has degree
2M-1
and that by Proposition
3.4
we can compute
2M+1
of its values. Thus, we can identify
T_{2,M}
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
M
be a positive integer. Then, for every
N
,

\displaystyle T_{{2},{M}}\left(N\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2M+1}\left[F_{2M+1}^{+}(N)+F_{2M+1}^{-}(N)-2F_{2M+1}^{+}(N-M)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2M+1}\left[F_{2M+1}^{+}(N)-2F_{2M+1}^{+}(N-M)+F_{2M+1}^{+}(N-2M)\right].

Proof.

From Proposition
3.4
we know that the polynomial

\frac{1}{2M+1}[F_{2M+1}^{+}(N)+F_{2M+1}^{-}(N)]-T_{{2},{M}}\left(N\right),

has degree
2M+1
with zeros at
\{-M,-M+1,\ldots,-1,0,1,\ldots,M\}
. Thus it is equal to

C\cdot(N+M)\cdots(N+1)N(N-1)\cdots(N-M)=CF_{2M+1}^{+}(N-M).

By inspection of the leading coefficient, we deduce
C=\frac{2}{2M+1}
and conclude.
∎

Note that the parity argument generalizes to all values of
k
, yielding the values of
T_{k,M}
in the integer interval
[\![-M,M]\!]
.

Proposition 3.6
.

For any
k\geq 1
and
-M\leq N\leq M
, we have the following equality

\displaystyle T_{k,M}(N)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{kM+1}\left[F_{kM+1}^{+}(N)+(-1)^{k}F_{kM+1}^{-}(N)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{kM+1}\left[F_{kM+1}^{+}(N)+(-1)^{k}F_{kM+1}^{+}(N-kM)\right]

Proof.

As
\deg(T_{k,M})=k(M-1)+1
and
T_{k,M}
is either even or odd depending on
k,M
, then for
-M\leq N\leq-1
:

(-1)^{k(M-1)+1}T_{k,M}(N)=T_{k,M}(-N)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Prop. \ref{prop:kM_1M}}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{kM+1}F_{kM+1}^{+}(-N)=\frac{(-1)^{kM+1}}{kM+1}F_{kM+1}^{-}(N).

Hence
T_{k,M}(N)=\frac{(-1)^{k}}{kM+1}F_{kM+1}^{-}(N)
for
-M\leq N\leq-1
. Then we notice that

T_{k,M}(N)=\frac{1}{kM+1}\left[F_{kM+1}^{+}(N)+(-1)^{k}F_{kM+1}^{-}(N)\right]

holds for
-M\leq N\leq M
by proposition
3.3
.
∎

3.3.
Case
k=3

We now consider bipartite pairings of the edges of a
3M
-gon and three smaller
M
-gons, as represented on Figure
5
.

Figure 5.
Example of a bipartite pairing of the sides of a 12-gon and three squares i.e.
k=3
,
M=4
.

The main motivation of presenting the cases
k=3,4
in detail is to showcase the core idea of the proof of the main result, which relies on Theorem
2.3
on the distribution of low powers of Ginibre eigenvalues. Using this theorem, we have the following.

Proposition 3.7
.

For
M<N\leq 2M
we have the following equality

T_{3,M}(N)=\frac{1}{3M+1}F_{3M+1}^{+}(N)-\frac{3}{3M+1}F_{3M+1}^{+}(N-M)

Proof.

For
M<N\leq 2M
, from Theorem
2.3
we have
|I_{p}|\in\{1,2\}
for all
1\leq p\leq M
. We also have

T_{3,M}(N)=\mathbb{E}\left[{\sum_{i,j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}^{kM}\overline{\lambda_{j_{1}}^{M}}\overline{\lambda_{j_{2}}^{M}}\overline{\lambda_{j_{3}}^{M}}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[{\sum_{i,j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}=1}^{N}z_{i}^{3}\overline{z_{j_{1}}}\overline{z_{j_{2}}}\overline{z_{j_{3}}}}\right],

still with the notations of Theorem
2.3
. Now we consider two possible cases: either the index
i
is in a block of size one, or it is in a block of size two.

First case:
i\in I_{p}
where
|I_{p}|=1
, i.e.
I_{p}=\{p\}
.
Then
z_{i}
is independent from other
z_{j}
’s, thus we only need to consider expressions of the type

\mathbb{E}\left[{z_{i}^{k}\overline{z_{i}}^{m}}\right]=\frac{1}{Z_{N,M,p}}\int_{\mathbb{C}}z_{i}^{k}\overline{z_{i}}^{m}|z_{i}|^{2(p-M)/M}e^{-|z_{i}|^{2/M}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m(z_{i}).

It is non-zero only if
k=m
, because
|z_{i}|^{2(p-M)/M}e^{-|z_{i}|^{2/M}}
is rotational invariant. Therefore the non-zero contribution we have in this case is when
i=j_{1}=j_{2}=j_{3}
with

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{|z_{i}|^{6}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{N,M,p}}\int_{\mathbb{C}}|z_{i}|^{6+2(p-M)/M}e^{-|z_{i}|^{2/M}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m(z_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{\pi\cdot 1!\cdot M\cdot(p-1)!}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{6+1+2(p-M)/M}e^{-r^{2/M}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{M(p-1)!}\cdot\frac{M}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{3M+p-1}e^{-u}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(3M+p)}{\Gamma(p)}.

Second case:
i\in I_{p}
where
|I_{p}|=2
, i.e.
I_{p}=\{p,p+M\}
.
Let
j
be the second element in
I_{p}
different from
i
. Then again we find that other
z_{r}
’s are independent, thus we also need to consider expressions such as
\mathbb{E}\left[{z_{i}^{k}\overline{z_{i}}^{m}\overline{z_{j}}^{l}}\right]
. More generally, we consider

\mathbb{E}\left[{z_{i}^{a}\overline{z_{i}}^{b}z_{j}^{c}\overline{z_{j}}^{d}}\right]=\frac{1}{Z_{N,M,k}}\int\int z_{i}^{a}\overline{z_{i}}^{b}z_{j}^{c}\overline{z_{j}}^{d}|z_{i}-z_{j}|^{2}|z_{i}z_{j}|^{2(p-M)/M}e^{-|z_{i}|^{2/M}-|z_{j}|^{2/M}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m(z_{i})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m(z_{j})

Note that integration is taken with respect to the density of the block
I_{p}
, given in Theorem
2.3
. This becomes:

\frac{1}{Z_{N,M,k}}\int\int z_{i}^{a}\overline{z_{i}}^{b}z_{j}^{c}\overline{z_{j}}^{d}(|z_{i}|^{2}+|z_{j}|^{2}-z_{i}\overline{z_{j}}-\overline{z_{i}}z_{j})|z_{i}z_{j}|^{2(p-M)/M}e^{-|z_{i}|^{2/M}-|z_{j}|^{2/M}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m(z_{i})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m(z_{j}).

Hence, by inspection we get a non-zero contribution in the following cases:

•

a=b
and
c=d
(contributions associated to
|z_{i}|^{2}
and
|z_{j}|^{2}
)

•

a+1=b
and
c=d+1
(contribution associated to
z_{i}\overline{z_{j}}
)

•

a=b+1
and
c+1=d
(contribution associated to
\overline{z_{i}}z_{j}
)

This once again due to the rotational invariance of the remaining part. In our context, this boils down to two possibilities for
\mathbb{E}\left[{z_{i}^{k}\overline{z_{i}}^{m}\overline{z_{j}}^{l}}\right]
, which we refer to as diagonal and off-diagonal contributions.

– Diagonal contributions;
the case
a=b
,
c=d
occurs if
m=k
and
l=0
. This corresponds to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{|z_{i}|^{6}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{N,M,p}}\int_{z_{i}}\int_{z_{j}}|z_{i}|^{6}(|z_{i}|^{2}+|z_{j}|^{2})|z_{i}z_{j}|^{2(p-M)/M}e^{-|z_{i}|^{2/M}-|z_{j}|^{2/M}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m(z_{i})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m(z_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z_{N,M,p}}\int_{z_{i}}\int_{z_{j}}|z_{i}|^{6+2+2(p-M)/M}|z_{j}|^{2(p-M)/M}e^{-|z_{i}|^{2/M}-|z_{j}|^{2/M}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m(z_{i})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m(z_{j})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{Z_{N,M,p}}\int_{z_{i}}\int_{z_{j}}|z_{i}|^{6+2(p-M)/M}|z_{j}|^{2+2(p-M)/M}e^{-|z_{i}|^{2/M}-|z_{j}|^{2/M}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m(z_{i})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m(z_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi^{2}M^{2}}{Z_{N,M,p}}\left[\Gamma(4M+p)\Gamma(p)+\Gamma(3M+p)\Gamma(M+p)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{\Gamma(3M+p+M)}{\Gamma(p+M)}+\frac{\Gamma(3M+p)}{\Gamma(p)}\right],

which is also the same as
\mathbb{E}\left[{|z_{j}|^{6}}\right]
.

– Off-diagonal contributions;
the
a=b+1
and
c+1=d
occurs if
m=k-1
and
l=1
. This can only correspond to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{|z_{i}|^{4}z_{i}\overline{z_{j}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{Z_{N,M,p}}\int_{z_{i}}\int_{z_{j}}|z_{i}|^{4}z_{i}\overline{z_{j}}\cdot(\overline{z_{i}}z_{j})\cdot|z_{i}z_{j}|^{2(p-M)/M}e^{-|z_{i}|^{2/M}-|z_{j}|^{2/M}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m(z_{i})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m(z_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{Z_{N,M,p}}\int_{z_{i}}\int_{z_{j}}|z_{i}|^{4+2(k-M)/M}|z_{j}|^{2+2(p-M)/M}e^{-|z_{i}|^{2/M}-|z_{j}|^{2/M}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m(z_{i})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m(z_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{Z_{N,M,p}}\int_{z_{i}}|z_{i}|^{4+2(p-M)/M}e^{-|z_{i}|^{2/M}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m(z_{i})\int_{z_{j}}|z_{j}|^{2+2(p-M)/M}e^{-|z_{j}|^{2/M}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m(z_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{-\pi^{2}M^{2}}{\pi^{2}\cdot 2!\cdot M^{2}\Gamma(p)\Gamma(p+M)}\cdot\Gamma(3M+p)\Gamma(M+p)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\Gamma(3M+p)}{\Gamma(p)}

To finish the calculation for the second case, we also need to count the number of occurrences of the expression
\mathbb{E}\left[{|z_{i}|^{4}z_{i}\overline{z_{j}}}\right]
can be found in the sum of
\mathbb{E}\left[{z_{i}^{3}\overline{z_{j_{1}}z_{j_{2}}z_{j_{3}}}}\right]
over all
i,j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}
. We notice that from
\{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}\}
two of them must equal to
i
and the other to
j
. Thus we have
3
occurrences of a term of type
\mathbb{E}\left[{|z_{i}|^{4}z_{i}\overline{z_{j}}}\right]
. Moreover observe that
p
will range from
1
to
N-M
, as there are
N-M
sets
I_{p}
of size
2
. Hence, combining both cases we achieve:

\displaystyle T_{3,M}(N)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I_{p}\\
|I_{p}|=1\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\left[{|z_{i}|^{6}}\right]+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I_{p}\\
|I_{p}|=2\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\left[{|z_{i}|^{6}}\right]-3\sum_{p=1}^{N-M}\frac{\Gamma(3M+p)}{\Gamma(p)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{N}\frac{\Gamma(3M+p)}{\Gamma(p)}-3\sum_{p=1}^{N-M}\frac{\Gamma(3M+p)}{\Gamma(p)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3M+1}F_{3M+1}^{+}(N)-\frac{3}{3M+1}F_{3M+1}^{+}(N-M),

(by Lemma
1.8
)

which was the claim.
∎

Now we can attain the following:

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
M
be a positive integer. Then we have the following

T_{3,M}(N)=\frac{1}{3M+1}\left[F_{3M+1}^{+}(N)-3F_{3M+1}^{+}(N-M)+3F_{3M+1}^{+}(N-2M)-F_{3M+1}^{+}(N-3M)\right]

for every integer
N
.

Proof.

From proposition
3.6
we have

T_{{3},{M}}\left(N\right)=\frac{1}{3M+1}\left[F_{3M+1}^{+}(N)-F_{3M+1}^{-}(N)\right]~~\text{for}~-M\leq N\leq M.

By proposition
3.7
we get

T_{3,M}(N)=\frac{1}{3M+1}\left[F_{3M+1}^{+}(N)-3F_{3M+1}^{+}(N-M)\right]~~\text{for}~~M<N\leq 2M.

Therefore by considering the polynomial

\frac{1}{3M+1}\left[F_{3M+1}^{+}(N)-3F_{3M+1}^{+}(N-M)-F_{3M+1}^{+}(N-3M)\right]-T_{3,M}(N)

we get
3M+1
zeros:
\{-M,-M+1\ldots,2M\}
. As this polynomial is of degree
3M+1
we explicitly write it as

C(N+M)(N+M-1)\ldots(N-2M)=C\cdot F_{3M+1}^{+}(N-2M).

To determine the constant
C
, we note that
\deg(T_{3,M})=3M-2
, thus by inspection of the leading coefficient (of degree
{3M+1}
), we find
C=-3/(3M+1)
.
∎

Yet again, we are able to specialize the above proposition to calculate exactly the values of
T_{k,M}
in the integer interval
[\![M,2M]\!]
, for any value of
k
.

Proposition 3.9
.

For any
k\geq 1
and
M<N\leq 2M
we have the following equality

T_{k,M}(N)=\frac{1}{kM+1}F_{kM+1}^{+}(N)-\frac{k}{kM+1}F_{kM+1}^{+}(N-M)

The proof is entirely analogous to the one of Proposition
3.7
. We do not repeat it for the sake of brevity. Note that it is also a special case of Theorem
1.3
, which will be proved in Section
4
.

The last case for which we give a specific computation is

, which means that we consider bipartite pairings between a

-gon and four

-gons, as represented on Figure

. Based on the moments computations of the previous case

, and the same symmetry trick used for case

, we establish the following identity:

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
M
be a positive integer. Then for every

,

T_{4,M}(N)=\frac{1}{4M+1}\sum_{l=0}^{4}(-1)^{l}\binom{4}{l}F_{4M+1}^{+}(N-lM).

,

.

Proof.

Using Proposition
3.6
we get:

T_{{4},{M}}\left(N\right)=\frac{1}{4M+1}\left[F_{4M+1}^{+}(N)+F_{4M+1}^{-}(N)\right]~~\text{for}~-M\leq N\leq M

and by Proposition

) for

computations, identifying the polynomial

, by computing

at some other specific values.

4.
Bipartite formula: the general case

4.1.
The proof of the formula

Let us now restate our main theorem and provide a proof of it.

Theorem 4.1

(Bipartite generating function)
.

Let
k
and
M
be positive integers. Then, for every
N\geq 0
,

T_{k,M}(N)=\frac{1}{kM+1}\sum_{l=0}^{k}(-1)^{l}\binom{k}{l}F_{kM+1}^{+}(N-lM).

Proof.

We first note that both sides of the equality are polynomials in
N
, therefore it is enough to prove for
N=qM
for
q>k
– which is an infinite set of values on which the block decomposition from Theorem
2.3
will be particularly tractable. Let us recall this decomposition: we have that

\{\lambda_{1}^{M},\ldots,\lambda_{N}^{M}\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{N}\},

with
M
independent blocks of size
q
for parameters
p\in[\![1,M]\!]
with joint density

\frac{1}{Z_{N,M,p}}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq q}|z_{i}-z_{j}|^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{q}|z_{i}|^{\frac{2(p-M)}{M}}e^{-|z_{i}|^{2/M}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m(z_{i}),

where

Z_{N,M,p}=\pi^{q}q!M^{q}\prod_{j=0}^{q-1}(jM+p-1)!.

We want to calculate, for
N=qM
specifically,

\displaystyle T_{k,M}(N)=\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{tr}(G^{kM})\overline{\mathrm{tr}(G^{M})}^{k}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[{\lambda_{i}^{kM}\overline{\lambda_{j_{1}}}^{M}\ldots\overline{\lambda_{j_{k}}}^{M}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}=1}^{q}\mathbb{E}\left[{z_{i}^{k}\overline{z_{j_{1}}}\overline{z_{j_{2}}}\cdots\overline{z_{j_{k}}}}\right].

We note that, by inspection, non-zero contributions can occur only when all
z_{j}
variables are in the same block as
z_{i}
. Let us compute the contribution for the block with index
p
. We assume that
I_{p}=\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{q}\}
(without loss of generality, as all blocks have same size
q
, and by exchangeability the indices do not matter to the computation).

\sum_{i,j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}=1}^{q}\mathbb{E}\left[{z_{i}^{k}\overline{z_{j_{1}}}\overline{z_{j_{2}}}\cdots\overline{z_{j_{k}}}}\right]=q\cdot\sum_{\alpha_{1}+\ldots+\alpha_{q}=k}\frac{k!}{\alpha_{1}!\cdots\alpha_{q}!}\mathbb{E}\left[{z_{1}^{k}\overline{z_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\overline{z_{q}}^{\alpha_{q}}}\right]

This comes from considering how many times (
\alpha_{\ell}
) the index
\ell
appears among
j_{1},\dots,j_{k}
. The factor
q
comes from fixing
i=1
instead of summing over
i
, and the factor
k!/(\alpha_{1}!\cdots\alpha_{q}!)
is the number of ways in which the weights
\alpha_{\ell}
can be realized. We then write down the expectation corresponding to the block
I_{p}
, which yields

\frac{q}{Z_{N,M,p}}\sum_{\alpha_{1}+\ldots+\alpha_{q}=k}\frac{k!}{\alpha_{1}!\cdots\alpha_{q}!}\int_{\mathbb{C}^{q}}z_{1}^{k}\overline{z_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\overline{z_{q}}^{\alpha_{q}}\prod_{i<j}|z_{i}-z_{j}|^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{q}|z_{i}|^{\frac{2(p-M)}{M}}e^{-|z_{i}|^{2/M}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m(z_{i}).

Observe that
\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{q}|z_{i}|^{\frac{2(p-M)}{M}}e^{-|z_{i}|^{2/M}}
is rotational invariant, hence we focus on the quantity

(5)

z_{1}^{k}\overline{z_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\overline{z_{q}}^{\alpha_{q}}\prod_{i<j}|z_{i}-z_{j}|^{2}=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{q}}\sum_{\tau\in S_{q}}\textrm{sgn}(\sigma)\textrm{sgn}(\tau)z_{1}^{k}\overline{z_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\overline{z_{q}}^{\alpha_{q}}\prod_{i=1}^{q}z_{i}^{\sigma(i)-1}\overline{z_{i}}^{\tau(i)-1}.

When integrating the above expression against a rotationally invariant weight, a non-zero contribution can only occur if the following equalities hold:

\begin{cases}k+\sigma(1)=\alpha_{1}+\tau(1),\\
\sigma(i)=\alpha_{i}+\tau(i)&\text{for}~~i\geq 2.\end{cases}

Note that the only index
i
for which
\sigma(i)-\tau(i)
could be negative is for
i=1
, elsewhere it has to be non-negative, as
\alpha_{i}\geq 0
. This imposes a strong constraint on the structure of
\sigma
and
\tau
, which we will exploit; in the following argument, we consider the permutation
\pi=\tau\circ\sigma^{-1}
and denote
\sigma(1)=a
and
\tau(1)=a+r
, where
r=k-\alpha_{1}\geq 0
.

First case:
r=0
.
We first consider the case when
r=0
, which forces
\alpha_{1}=k
and
\alpha_{i}=0
for all
i\geq 2
, as
k=\alpha_{1}+\cdots\alpha_{q}
with
\alpha_{\ell}\geq 0
for every
\ell
.
Thus, for a nonzero contribution we must have
\sigma=\tau
, and we are left with integrating

\frac{q}{Z_{N,M,p}}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{q}}|z_{1}|^{2(k+\sigma(1)-1)}\prod_{i=2}^{q}|z_{i}|^{2(\sigma(i)-1)}\prod_{i=1}^{q}|z_{i}|^{\frac{2(p-M)}{M}}e^{-|z_{i}|^{2/M}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m(z_{i}).

By a direct change of variable, we have that

(6)

\int_{\mathbb{C}}|z|^{a+2(p-M)/M}e^{-|z|^{2/M}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m(z)=\pi M\Gamma(aM/2+p)

Substituting the above identity, we obtain the following expression for these contributions:

\frac{qM^{q}\pi^{q}}{Z_{N,M,p}}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{q}}\frac{\Gamma((k+\sigma(1)-1)M+p)}{\Gamma((\sigma(1)-1)M+p)}\prod_{j=0}^{q-1}\Gamma(jM+p)=\frac{1}{(q-1)!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{q}}\frac{\Gamma((k+\sigma(1)-1)M+p)}{\Gamma((\sigma(1)-1)M+p)}.

Now we notice that for any
j\in[n]
, we have exactly
(q-1)!
permutations such that
\sigma(1)=j
. Therefore the sum of non zero contributions corresponding to
r=0
simplifies to

(7)

\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\frac{\Gamma((j+k)M+p)}{\Gamma(jM+p)}.

Second case:
r\geq 1
.
Now assume that
r=k-\alpha_{1}\geq 1
. The key remark is then that for the contribution to be nonzero,
\pi=\tau\circ\sigma^{-1}
must have a specific structure, namely, it has only one cycle that is not a fixed point. Let us explore the structure of
\pi
in some detail before carrying on with the computation.

\begin{matrix}1&2&\ldots&a&\ldots&a+r&\ldots&q-1&q\\
1&2&\ldots&a+r&-&-&\ldots&q-1&q\\
\end{matrix}

Recall that
a=\sigma(1)
. We first notice that, as

\forall i\neq 1,\quad\sigma(i)-\tau(i)\geq 0,

we must have, by setting
j=\sigma(i)
and using the fact that
\sigma
is a bijection,

\forall j\neq a,\quad j-\pi(i)\geq 0.

As for
j=a
, we have that

a-\pi(a)=\sigma(a)-\tau(1)=-r<0.

These constraints mean that for
\pi
we are restricted to only one
right
jump at
i=a
and the rest must be
left
jumps, which is only possible in one cycle as
\pi
must also be a bijection. By inspection, this cycle must also have
a
and
a+r
as extremal indices. More precisely, we must have some sequence
0=\beta_{0}<\beta_{1}<\ldots<\beta_{m}<\beta_{m+1}=r
, such that

\pi(a+\beta_{i+1})=a+\beta_{i},

as represented on Figure
7
. All other indices are fixed points of
\pi
.

Figure 7.
A visualization of the structure of
\pi=\tau\circ\sigma^{-1}
, with a unique right jump from
a
to
a+r
and
m+1
left jumps from
a+\beta_{i+1}
to
a+\beta_{i}
.

We notice that if we set
j=\sigma^{-1}(a+\beta_{i+1})
, then we have

\sigma(j)-\tau(j)=a+\beta_{i+1}-(a+\beta_{i})=\beta_{i+1}-\beta_{i}

So we have that
\alpha_{j}
is either
0
or some
\beta_{i+1}-\beta_{i}
for some
i
. And

\alpha_{1}=\sigma(1)-\tau(1)+k=k-r.

With this construction, the datum of a permutation
\sigma
and of the tuple
(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{m})
uniquely determines
\tau
, as we then have

\tau=(a+r,a+\beta_{m},\ldots,a+\beta_{1},a)\circ\sigma.

In particular, note that
\textrm{sgn}(\tau)=(-1)^{m+1}\cdot\textrm{sgn}(\sigma)
.
\sigma
can be any permutation, with the only requirement that
\sigma(1)\leq q-r
to accommodate the jump, and
r
would range from
1
to
k
, as this is the largest jump we can make from
\sigma(1)
. Therefore we can rewrite the sum over permutations (
5
) as follows, for a given parameter
k\geq r\geq 1
:

(8)

\sum_{m=0}^{r-1}\ \sum_{0<\beta_{1}<\ldots<\beta_{m}<r}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in S_{q}\\
\sigma(1)\leq q-r\end{subarray}}\frac{k!(-1)^{m+1}}{\prod_{i=0}^{m}(\beta_{i+1}-\beta_{i})!\cdot(k-r)!}|z_{1}|^{2(k+\sigma(1)-1)}\prod_{i=2}^{q}|z_{1}|^{2(\sigma(i)-1)}.

We first focus on the combinatorial expression

\sum_{0<\beta_{1}<\ldots<\beta_{m}<r}\frac{1}{\prod_{i=0}^{m}(\beta_{i+1}-\beta_{i})!},

which we simplify in the following way. By rewriting
a_{i}=\beta_{i+1}-\beta_{i}
with convention
\beta_{0}~=~0
and
\beta_{m+1}=r
, we have
a_{0}+\ldots+a_{m}=r
and all
a_{i}\geq 1
. Therefore, we have

\sum_{0<\beta_{1}<\ldots<\beta_{m}<r}\frac{1}{\prod_{i=0}^{m}(\beta_{i+1}-\beta_{i})!}=\frac{1}{r!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{0}+\ldots+a_{m}=r\\
a_{i}\geq 1\end{subarray}}\binom{r}{a_{0},\ldots,a_{m}}.

The sum represent exactly the number of ways to partition
r
objects into
m+1
non-empty sets, together with a labeling of these sets from
1
to
m+1
. Therefore this sum is equal to
(m+1)!\cdot\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{r}{m+1}
by definition of Stirling numbers, and hence

\sum_{0<\beta_{1}<\ldots<\beta_{m}<r}\frac{1}{\prod_{i=0}^{m}(\beta_{i+1}-\beta_{i})!}=\frac{(m+1)!}{r!}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{r}{m+1}.

We now perform the sum over
m
, and find the following quantity

\sum_{m=0}^{r-1}(-1)^{m+1}(m+1)!\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{r}{m+1},

which we can simplify by using Lemma
1.13
; moreover, as
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{r}{0}=0
, we find that the sum is exactly
(-1)^{r}
. Hence

\sum_{m=0}^{r-1}(-1)^{m+1}\sum_{0<\beta_{1}<\ldots<\beta_{m}<r}\frac{1}{\prod_{i=0}^{m}(\beta_{i+1}-\beta_{i})!}=\frac{(-1)^{r}}{r!}.

Therefore, the quantity (
8
) is equal to

\displaystyle\frac{q}{Z_{N,M,p}}\sum_{r=1}^{k}(-1)^{r}\binom{k}{r}

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in S_{q}\\
\sigma(1)\leq q-r\end{subarray}}\int_{\mathbb{C}^{q}}|z_{1}|^{2(k+\sigma(1)-1)}\prod_{i=2}^{q}|z_{1}|^{2(\sigma(i)-1)}\prod_{i=1}^{q}|z_{i}|^{\frac{2(p-M)}{M}}e^{-|z_{i}|^{2/M}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m(z_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{qM^{q}\pi^{q}}{Z_{N,M,p}}\sum_{r=1}^{k}(-1)^{r}\binom{k}{r}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in S_{q}\\
\sigma(1)\leq q-r\end{subarray}}\frac{\Gamma((k+\sigma(1)-1)M+p)}{\Gamma((\sigma(1)-1)M+p)}\prod_{j=0}^{q-1}\Gamma(jM+p).

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{k}(-1)^{r}\binom{k}{r}\sum_{j=0}^{q-r-1}\frac{\Gamma((j+k)M+p)}{\Gamma(jM+p)}.

Therefore, by summing over all possible block indices
p=1,\ldots,M
and all values of
r=0,\dots,k
including the contribution (
7
), we find the following expression for the value
T_{k,M}(N)
when
N=qM
:

\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{M}\sum_{r=0}^{k}(-1)^{r}\binom{k}{r}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{q-r-1}\frac{\Gamma((j+k)M+p)}{\Gamma(jM+p)}=\sum_{r=0}^{k}(-1)^{r}\binom{k}{r}\sum_{j=0}^{q-r-1}\sum_{p=1}^{M}\frac{\Gamma((j+k)M+p)}{\Gamma(jM+p)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{kM+1}\sum_{r=0}^{k}(-1)^{r}\binom{k}{r}\sum_{j=0}^{q-r-1}F_{kM+1}^{+}((j+1)M)-F_{kM+1}^{+}(jM)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{kM+1}\sum_{r=0}^{k}(-1)^{r}\binom{k}{r}F_{kM+1}^{+}((q-r)M)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{kM+1}\sum_{r=0}^{k}(-1)^{r}\binom{k}{r}F_{kM+1}^{+}(N-rM).

which concludes the proof, as these values fully characterize the polynomial.
∎

4.2.
Recovering the coefficient
\epsilon_{g}(k,M)

We noticed that we are able to extract the exact coefficient from the equality

\sum_{g=0}^{\lfloor k(M-1)/2\rfloor}\epsilon_{g}(k,M)N^{k(M-1)+1-2g}=\frac{1}{kM+1}\sum_{l=0}^{k}(-1)^{l}\binom{k}{l}F_{kM+1}^{+}(N-lM).

We expand the rising factorial in terms of Stirling numbers of the first kind as follows

F_{kM+1}^{+}(N-lM)=\sum_{p=0}^{kM+1}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{kM+1}{p}(N-lM)^{p}.

Hence

\displaystyle\frac{1}{kM+1}\sum_{l=0}^{k}(-1)^{l}\binom{k}{l}F_{kM+1}^{+}

\displaystyle(N-lM)=\frac{1}{kM+1}\sum_{l=0}^{k}\sum_{p=0}^{kM+1}(-1)^{l}\binom{k}{l}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{kM+1}{p}(N-lM)^{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{kM+1}\sum_{l=0}^{k}\sum_{p=0}^{kM+1}(-1)^{l}\binom{k}{l}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{kM+1}{p}\sum_{r=0}^{p}\binom{p}{r}N^{r}(-lM)^{p-r}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{kM+1}\sum_{p=0}^{kM+1}\sum_{r=0}^{p}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{kM+1}{p}\binom{p}{r}N^{r}(-M)^{p-r}\sum_{l=0}^{k}(-1)^{l}\binom{k}{l}l^{p-r}

We use the closed form for the Stirling number of the second kind, Proposition
1.12

\sum_{l=0}^{k}(-1)^{l}\binom{k}{l}l^{p-r}=(-1)^{k}k!\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{p-r}{k}

Hence

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{kM+1}\sum_{p=0}^{kM+1}\sum_{r=0}^{p}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{kM+1}{p}\binom{p}{r}N^{r}(-M)^{p-r}(-1)^{k}k!\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{p-r}{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{kM+1}\sum_{r=0}^{kM+1}\sum_{p=r}^{kM+1}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{kM+1}{p}\binom{p}{r}N^{r}(-M)^{p-r}(-1)^{k}k!\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{p-r}{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{k}k!}{kM+1}\sum_{r=0}^{k(M-1)+1}\left[\sum_{p=k}^{kM+1-r}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{kM+1}{p+r}\binom{p+r}{r}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{p}{k}(-M)^{p}\right]N^{r}

Finally we get

\epsilon_{g}(k,M)=\frac{(-1)^{k}k!}{kM+1}\sum_{p=k}^{k+2g}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{kM+1}{p+k(M-1)+1-2g}\binom{p+k(M-1)+1-2g}{p}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{p}{k}(-M)^{p}

Reindexing the sum, we get:

Proposition 4.2
.

For all integers
k,M>0
,
g\geq 0
, we have the following identity

\epsilon_{g}(k,M)=\frac{(-1)^{k}k!}{kM+1}\sum_{p=0}^{2g}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{kM+1}{p+kM+1-2g}\binom{p+kM+1-2g}{p+k}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{p+k}{k}(-M)^{p+k}.

In particular, we notice that the leading coefficient:
\epsilon_{0}(k,M)
simplifies to

(9)

\epsilon_{0}(k,M)=\frac{(-1)^{k}k!}{kM+1}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{kM+1}{kM+1}\binom{kM+1}{k}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{k}(-M)^{k}=\frac{k!\cdot M^{k}}{kM+1}\binom{kM+1}{k},

which one can identify as a Fuss-Catalan number, as expected.

5.
Epilogue: recovering the original Harer-Zagier Formula

In this last section, we explain why our bipartite theorem actually generalizes the original Harer-Zagier formula: indeed, one can recover Theorem
1.1
directly from Theorem
1.3
. For this, the key is to notice the following link between the original Harer-Zagier coefficients
\epsilon_{g}(n)
and the bipartite coefficients
\epsilon_{g}(k,M)
, at the specific values
M=2
and
k=n
.

Proposition 5.1
.

\epsilon_{g}(k,2)=2^{k}\cdot k!\cdot\epsilon_{g}(k).

Proof.

By definition,
\epsilon_{g}(k,2)
counts bipartite pairings between one
2k
-gon and
k
separate
2
-gons, as illustrated on Figure
8
. Notice, then, that when two edges of the
2k
-gon are joined to the same
2
-gon, the surface obtained is the same as with a direct edge pairing of the two edges of the
2k
-gon, with no
2
-gon involved. Therefore, every bipartite pairing of a
2k
-gon and
k
distinct
2
-gons can be naturally associated to a pairing of edges of a single
2k
-gon, yielding the same surface – which is precisely one of the pairings counted by the coefficients
\epsilon_{g}(k)
. This, however, is not a one-to-one correspondence, as a given pairing of the edges of the
2k
-gon can be obtained in exactly
k!\cdot 2^{k}
ways, as for each pairing of two edges, we can choose 1) which
2
-gons we use, out of
k
possible choices, which gives a factor
k!
and 2) in which direction it is used, with two possible choices for each
2
-gon, giving the factor
2^{k}
.
∎

Figure 8.
Example of a bipartite pairing of the sides of a hexagon with those of three
2
-gons, i.e.
k=3
,
M=2
, represented in orange. This pairing yields the same surface as a pairing of the edges of the hexagon, represented in blue. This could have been achieved in
3!\cdot 2^{3}=48
ways.

Starting with Theorem
1.3
, the bipartite generating function with
M=2
,
k=n
is given by the formula:

(10)

\sum_{g=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\epsilon_{g}(n,2)N^{n+1-2g}=\frac{1}{2n+1}\sum_{l=0}^{n}(-1)^{l}\binom{n}{l}F_{2n+1}^{+}(N-2l)

Replacing the bipartite coefficients by the Harer-Zagier coeffients according to Proposition
5.1
, we find

(11)

\sum_{g=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\epsilon_{g}(n)N^{n+1-2g}=\frac{1}{n!\ 2^{n}\ (2n+1)}\sum_{l=0}^{n}(-1)^{l}\binom{n}{l}F_{2n+1}^{+}(N-2l)

where, by definition,

(12)

F_{2n+1}^{+}(N-2l)=(2n+1)!\binom{N+2n-2l}{2n+1},

so that

(13)

\sum_{g=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\epsilon_{g}(n)N^{n+1-2g}=\frac{(2n)!}{n!\cdot 2^{n}}\sum_{l=0}^{n}(-1)^{l}\binom{n}{l}\binom{N+2n-2l}{2n+1}.

This is a closed expression for the Harer-Zagier generating function, but is not yet obviously the same as the one cited in Theorem
1.1
. We transform this expression using the coefficient extractor operator, denoted by
[x^{m}]
, simply noticing that

\binom{N+2n-2l}{2n+1}=[x^{2n+1}](1+x)^{N+2n-2l},

and thus

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{n}(-1)^{l}\binom{n}{l}\binom{N+2n-2l}{2n+1}

\displaystyle=[x^{2n+1}]\left((1+x)^{N}\sum_{l=0}^{n}(-1)^{l}\binom{n}{l}(1+x)^{2(n-l)}\right)

\displaystyle=[x^{2n+1}]\left((1+x)^{N}(-1+(1+x)^{2})^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=[x^{2n+1}]\left((1+x)^{N}(2+x)^{n}x^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=[x^{n+1}]\left((1+x)^{N}(2+x)^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{n}2^{l}\binom{N}{l+1}\binom{n}{n-l}.

We conclude that

(14)

\sum_{g=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\epsilon_{g}(n)N^{n+1-2g}=(2n-1)!!\ \sum_{l=0}^{n}2^{l}\binom{N}{l+1}\binom{n}{l}

which is the same expression as Theorem
1.1
. Thus, we have recovered the original, non bipartite, Harer-Zagier formula.
