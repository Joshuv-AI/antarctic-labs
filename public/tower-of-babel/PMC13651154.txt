pH-modulating binary hydrogen-bonded organic frameworks accelerate diabetic wound healing
Authors: Junpeng Sun, Xiang Sun, Mei Xia, Qianjun Xiao, Kaikai Ma, Zike Liao, Guanglai Mo, Xiaoying Li, Luying Lai, Haihao Huang, Chunhui Cui, Peng Li, Xiaopin Duan, Jisheng Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diabetic foot ulcers have become an increasingly serious clinical problem, in which wound pH dysregulation and infection lead to impaired healing. To improve this situation, we propose a fabric coated with a binary hydrogen-bonded organic framework (SMU-8@W) to enhance wound healing efficiency. Specifically, the hydrogen-bonded organic framework (SMU-8) not only suppresses wound infection through its high photodynamic activity but also guides a “near-neutral-weakly acidic-near-neutral” pH transition at the wound site, thereby modulating the wound microenvironment. Moreover, SMU-8@W exhibits favorable hemostatic performance (BCI = 21.19 ± 2.52%), moisture permeability (5874.20 ± 196.03 g·m⁻²·d⁻¹), biosafety, and long-term stability, effectively managing wound exudate and alleviating inflammation, thereby accelerating wound healing. In summary, SMU-8@W offers a pH-modulating, infection-suppressing, angiogenesis-promoting, and collagen-enhancing therapeutic strategy for diabetic foot ulcers.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04701-x.

Introduction

Diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs), a severe complication of diabetes, are characterized by a persistently alkaline wound microenvironment (pH 7.5–8.9) resulting from hyperglycemia, microvascular leakage, and microbial colonization [1–4]. This aberrant alkalinity perpetuates inflammation and severely impairs healing. Restoration of an acidic pH early in the healing process can suppress bacterial growth and promote angiogenesis [5]. Conversely, a slightly alkaline environment (∼pH 8.3) is more favorable for the proliferation and migration of keratinocytes and fibroblasts in later stages [6]. Thus, an ideal pH-regulation strategy must dynamically adapt to the wound’s evolving biology-establishing an acidic milieu initially to combat infection and shifting to an alkaline one subsequently to facilitate tissue repair [7]. However, achieving such dynamic pH modulation remains a critical challenge in chronic wound care.

Charge-assisted binary Hydrogen-bonded organic frameworks (HOFs), which are crystalline porous materials assembled via hydrogen bonding, π-π stacking, and electrostatic interactions, have garnered broad interest in biomedicine and catalysis [8]. A distinctive advantage of such materials lies in the precise incorporation of inherent acid-base functional groups into their framework, enabling the synergistic reinforcement of the hydrogen-bonding network at the molecular scale. According to the Brønsted-Lowry acid-base theory, proton transfer behavior in an acid-base system is determined by the relative acid dissociation constants (pKa) of the participating species, as quantitatively described by the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation: pH = pKa + log₁₀([A⁻]/[HA]), where [A⁻] and [HA] represent the concentrations of the conjugate base and the protonated acid, respectively [9, 10]. When the pKa of the acidic ligands (pKa(al)) is lower than that of the conjugate acid of the basic ligand (pKa(bl)), a weakly acidic microenvironment can be maintained during the early stage of wound healing, thereby inhibiting bacterial infection [11]. As H⁺ is gradually consumed, the environment can further transition to a stable weakly alkaline condition [12]. This alkaline shift promotes the proliferation and migration of keratinocytes and fibroblasts, supporting subsequent healing phases. Additionally, the inherent π-π stacking and push-pull electronic effects within these frameworks facilitate electron transfer, thereby enhancing their potential for photocatalytic antibacterial applications [13, 14]. However, the direct application of HOFs to wounds is limited by the risk of chemical irritation or toxicity associated with their aromatic building blocks [15, 16]. A promising strategy involves anchoring binary HOFs onto fabric fibers via recrystallization, which minimizes direct tissue contact while introducing multiple therapeutic benefits [17, 18]. The resulting composite not only imparts pH regulation and antibacterial activity to the fabric but also, through hydrophilic nanochannels interwoven with fiber interstices, forms a porous network that enhances moisture management [19]. This structure facilitates exudate transport via capillary action and promotes hemostasis by accelerating red blood cell and platelet aggregation [20, 21]. Together, these attributes position HOF-based fabrics as safe, effective pH-modulating and antibacterial dressings for diabetic wound care.

In this work, we constructed binary hydrogen-bonded organic frameworks (HOFs), termed SMU-7 and SMU-8, from weakly basic photosensitizer (tris[4-(pyridin-4-yl) phenyl] aniline, TP-monomer, pKa(bl) ≈ 6.05, calculated by ACD/Labs Software) and carboxylic acids (terephthalic acid, TA, pKa2 = 4.34; pyromellitic acid, PA, pKa₁ = 1.92, pKa₂ = 2.87, pKa₃ = 4.49, pKa₄ = 5.63). These HOFs were uniformly coated onto textiles via liquid-phase spray deposition (LPSD) to produce programmable pH-modulating and antibacterial composites (SMU-7@W and SMU-8@W) for diabetic wound therapy (Scheme 1). The fabric substrate minimizes direct HOF-tissue contact, reducing the cytotoxicity of SMU-8 by 2-3-fold. More importantly, the asymmetric acid-base nature of SMU-8-where carboxylic acids readily release H+ while weakly basic pyridine groups poorly retain them-confers a net weakly acidic character. This allows SMU-8@W to progressively acidify alkaline wound exudate, demonstrating programmable pH regulation. Furthermore, the hydrogen-bonded structure between the two ligands enhances light-induced charge separation, significantly promoting reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation under visible light. SMU-8 increases the yield of antibacterial singlet oxygen (¹O₂) 12-fold, endowing SMU-8@W with potent self-cleaning and antibacterial properties. The composite also improves fabric hydrophilicity 90-fold, enhances hemostatic performance 2.3-fold via accelerated blood clotting, and exhibits 65-fold higher moisture permeability than commercial wound dressings. Benefiting from these integrated functions, SMU-8@W first establishes a mildly acidic wound microenvironment and then transitions it to a mildly alkaline one. It demonstrates stronger antibacterial efficacy than the commercial product 3 M™ Silvercel™ Non-Adherent Antimicrobial Alginate Dressing (3 M-Ag), promotes angiogenesis, suppresses inflammation, and enhances collagen deposition, collectively accelerating the healing of infected diabetic wounds. By combining programmable pH modulation (i.e., guiding a “near‑neutral - weakly acidic - near‑neutral” pH transition that initially lowers the wound pH to inhibit bacterial infection and subsequently raises it to promote cell proliferation and migration), potent bactericidal activity, rapid hemostasis, and high breathability, SMU-8@W represents a novel and effective strategy for treating chronic diabetic wounds.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of SMU-7 and SMU-8

The binary HOFs were synthesized according to the strategy shown in Scheme 1. Briefly, the methanol solution of tris[4-(pyridin-4-yl) phenyl] aniline (TP-monomer) was mixed with the methanol solution of terephthalic acid (TA) or pyromellitic acid (PA) and left to stand at room temperature, successfully yielding bulk single crystals of SMU-7 (CCDC number: 2481232) and SMU-8 (CCDC number: 2481233). Single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SXRD) analysis indicates that SMU-7 crystallizes in the monoclinic space group C2/c (Table. S1), exhibiting a compact three-dimensional packing structure (Fig. 1A1, Table. S2). The Oak Ridge Thermal Ellipsoid Plot (ORTEP) diagram (50% probability ellipsoids) confirms the molecular structure within a single unit cell (Fig. S1). In the crystal, each TP-monomer is connected to two adjacent TA molecules via O-H···N hydrogen bonds (2.587 Å and 2.585 Å) and C-H···O hydrogen bonds (ranging from 3.218 Å to 3.360 Å), forming a hydrogen-bonded organic framework (Fig. 1A2, Table S3). SMU-8 crystallizes as transparent single crystals in the monoclinic space group P2₁/n (Table S4), adopting a compact three-dimensional packing structure (Fig. 1B1, Table S5). The molecular structure, illustrated by the ORTEP diagram with 50% probability ellipsoids (Fig. S2), reveals that each TP-monomer connects to adjacent PA molecules via multiple hydrogen bonds. These include N-H···O bonds (2.633–3.132 Å), O-H···O bonds (2.387–2.423 Å), and C-H···O bonds (ranging from 2.654 Å to 3.482 Å), collectively forming an extensive hydrogen-bonded network within the plane (Fig. 1B2, Table S6). The Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns of SMU-7 and SMU-8 closely match the simulated patterns (Fig. 1A3, B3), confirming the successful synthesis of the target HOFs. Additionally, π-π stacking interactions are present in both SMU-7 and SMU-8. In SMU-7, these include interactions between TP-monomer and TA molecules (distances: 3.550 Å and 3.759 Å), between adjacent TP-monomer molecules (3.649 Å), and between neighboring TA molecules (3.878 Å) (Fig. 1C). In SMU-8, the main π-π interaction occurs between adjacent PA molecules (3.629 Å) (Fig. 1D). These stacking interactions may provide efficient electron transport channels, promoting charge separation and transfer during photocatalysis, elevating electron density at reactive sites, and ultimately enhancing photocatalytic activity [22].

The microstructure of bulk SMU-7 and SMU-8 single crystals was characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). SMU-7 crystals exhibit a blocky morphology, while SMU-8 displays a rod-like structure, both with smooth surfaces and well-defined shapes. The particles show a uniform size distribution, with average dimensions of 132.00 ± 39.22 nm for SMU-7 and 66.04 ± 19.64 nm for SMU-8 (Fig. 1E-H). TEM images further reveal the smooth surface morphology of both materials (Fig. 1I and K). Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis (Fig. S3) indicates strong signals for C, N, and O, with no detectable impurity elements, confirming the high purity of SMU-7 and SMU-8. Elemental mapping (Fig. 1J and L) demonstrates a homogeneous and ordered distribution of C, N, and O throughout the crystals, corroborating the successful integration of the TP-monomer with the hydrogen-bonding units (TA and PA). These findings are further supported by XPS spectra and organic elemental analysis (Fig. S4, Table S7).

Subsequently, the synthesized SMU-7 and SMU-8 were characterized by ¹H NMR, FT-IR, as well as Raman spectroscopy, and thermogravimetric analysis (TGA). The ¹H NMR spectra provide direct evidence for the formation of HOFs by detecting changes in the chemical environment of protons in the molecules. In the ¹H NMR spectra of SMU-7 and SMU-8, the characteristic proton signals of the TP-monomer building unit were clearly observed (α-H, N = CH-, 7.68–7.81 ppm; β-H, C = CH-C, 8.60–8.62 ppm; Ar-H, 7.80–7.92 ppm), along with the characteristic peaks of ligands TA and PA (Ar-H, 8.10–8.25 ppm). All signals are highly consistent with the target structure, confirming the successful construction of hydrogen-bonded organic frameworks SMU-7 and SMU-8 (Fig. S5-6).

FT-IR spectroscopy further confirmed the successful construction of the SMU-7 and SMU-8 frameworks through shifts in bond vibration frequencies and changes in peak shapes. The infrared spectrum of SMU-7 clearly shows O-H and C-O stretching vibration absorption peaks at 3438 cm⁻¹ and 1274 cm⁻¹ attributed to TA, while the C-N stretching vibration characteristic of the TP-monomer is retained at 1323 cm⁻¹. Most importantly, the C = O stretching vibration absorption peak of the TA carboxyl group shifted significantly from 1683 cm⁻¹ to 1700 cm⁻¹, resulting in a blue shift phenomenon. Similarly, in the SMU-8 spectrum (Fig. S7), the characteristic peaks corresponding to the O-H and C-O stretching vibrations of pyromellitic acid are clearly observed at 3439 cm⁻¹ and 1277 cm⁻¹, along with the C-N vibration peak of the TP-monomer at 1330 cm⁻¹. Moreover, the C = O stretching vibration of the carboxyl group in PA shows a red shift from 1713 cm⁻¹ to 1700 cm⁻¹. The fundamental cause of both the red shift and the blue shift observed in FT-IR spectra is closely related to the formation of intermolecular hydrogen bonds and their impact on electron distribution. Within the SMU-7 framework, hydrogen bonding may weaken the conjugation effect or electron delocalization of the carboxyl group, leading to redistribution of electron density at the carboxyl site and thereby enhancing the double bond character of the C = O bond. Conversely, in the SMU-8 framework, this is attributed to the carboxyl oxygen atom participating as a hydrogen bond acceptor in forming stronger hydrogen bonds. Its electron-withdrawing effect reduces the electron density on the C = O bond, weakening bond strength and consequently lowering the vibrational frequency [23]. The red and blue shift phenomena strongly confirm the interactions between the building units and the TP-monomer, effectively demonstrating that intermolecular hydrogen bonds drive framework assembly.

Raman spectroscopy provided key evidence for the formation of SMU-7 and SMU-8 frameworks by revealing vibrational modes of their conjugated skeletons [24]. In SMU-7, a hydrogen-bonded O-H stretch appears at 3582 cm⁻¹ alongside a free O-H peak at 2561 cm⁻¹. The C = O stretching vibration of the carboxyl group splits into a quadruplet (1864, 1813, 1777, 1720 cm⁻¹), indicating multiple hydrogen-bonding configurations. Characteristic vibrations from the TP-monomer are also observed, including the C = C/C = N conjugated backbone at 1603 cm⁻¹, aromatic C-N stretching at 1268 cm⁻¹, and aromatic C-N deformation at 630 cm⁻¹. The SMU-8 spectrum shows similar features: a hydrogen-bonded O-H stretch at 3578 cm⁻¹, a free O-H peak at 2672 cm⁻¹, and a triplet splitting of the C = O stretch (1821, 1782, 1661 cm⁻¹). Notably, the C = C/C = N vibration is red-shifted to 1598 cm⁻¹, while aromatic C-N stretching and deformation occur at 1269 cm⁻¹ and 690/633 cm⁻¹, respectively (Fig. S8). This red shift indicates enhanced electron delocalization in the π-conjugated system of SMU-8, which may improve photophysical properties, suppress non-radiative decay, and promotes efficient energy transfer-all advantageous for ROS generation [25, 26].

The TGA curve of SMU-7 exhibits two distinct decomposition steps. The first occurs at 319.6 °C with a mass loss of 27.66%, corresponding to the thermal decomposition of the structural unit TA. The second step begins at 415.9 °C with a mass loss of 65.66%, indicating decomposition of the TP-monomer. In contrast, SMU-8 decomposes in two stages at 342.6 °C and 526.2 °C, with mass losses of 43.31% and 41.03%, respectively (Fig. S9-10 and Table S8-9). Compared to the TP-monomer, both SMU-7 and SMU-8 show decomposition at higher temperatures, primarily due to intermolecular hydrogen bonding [27, 28]. These results align well with the interactions observed in their single-crystal structures. Moreover, neither SMU-7 nor SMU-8 shows significant mass loss below 250 °C, and no polymorphic transitions occur at high temperatures, confirming their excellent thermodynamic stability.

The chemical stability of HOFs in physiological environments is also an important consideration for biological applications. PXRD analysis under different pH conditions (5.5 and 7.4, 37 °C) indicated that both HOFs maintained excellent crystallinity (Fig. 1M-P). Specifically, SMU-7 retained its characteristic diffraction peaks at 13.96°, 14.82°, 16.04°, 20.82°, 23.22°, 25.54°, and 27.96°, with no observable changes. Similarly, the characteristic diffraction peaks of SMU-8 (7.66°, 10.26°, 11.04°, 20.83°, 25.24°, and 26.06°) exhibited significant peak consistency across different incubation times, confirming their excellent structural integrity under physiologically relevant conditions. The crystallographic structure of the TP-monomer was also examined by PXRD. The experimental pattern exhibits well-defined diffraction peaks, confirming its high purity and crystalline nature (Fig. S11).

BET specific surface area analysis based on nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms further revealed the pore structure characteristics of the two HOF crystals, with pore sizes predominantly distributed around 2–10 nm, classifying them as mesoporous materials (typically defined as pore sizes between 2 and 50 nm) (Fig. S12-13).

Electrochemical and photoelectric properties of SMU-7 and SMU-8

TP-monomer is a triphenylamine-based photosensitizer. Given that hydrogen bonding between the ligands in binary HOFs may facilitate electron transfer and thereby enhance photocatalytic activity, we characterized and compared the photocatalytic properties of TP-monomer, SMU-7, and SMU-8 [29]. As shown in Fig. 2A, TP-monomer, SMU-7, and SMU-8 all exhibit strong light absorption in the 200–650 nm range, with TP-monomer limited to 200–450 nm. The increase in the absorption range may be attributed to the formation of an intermolecular hydrogen-bonded structure that extends the conjugated system, not only reducing the energy gap but also expanding the delocalization range of electrons, thereby making π electrons more readily excited.

The Tauc plots derived from (αhc/λ) versus hc/λ yielded optical bandgaps of 2.82 eV for TP-monomer, 2.59 eV for SMU-7, and 2.24 eV for SMU-8 (Fig. 2B-D), confirming that the hydrogen-bonded organic framework significantly narrows the bandgap and facilitates visible-light activation.

Fluorescence spectra under 360 nm and 488 nm excitation show broad emission around 520–550 nm for all three materials. However, under 561 nm excitation, only SMU-7 and SMU-8 exhibit strong emission, while TP-monomer shows no detectable signal (Fig. S14). This difference arises because TP-monomer, as a discrete molecule, has a large energy gap that prevents efficient excitation at lower energies [30]. Additionally, the fluorescent properties of SMU-7 and SMU-8 can also serve as detection criteria in subsequent stability experiments.

Time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) measurements revealed average exciton lifetimes of 16.32 ns for TP-monomer, 6.78 ns for SMU-7, and 1.54 ns for SMU-8. The lifetimes of SMU-7 and SMU-8 were found to be 2.41-fold and 10.60-fold shorter than that of TP-monomer, respectively, confirming that hydrogen bonding significantly accelerates charge transfer (Fig. S15). DFT calculations performed at the B3LYP/6-31G level reveal that the electronic bandgaps of TP-monomer, SMU-7 and SMU-8 decrease gradually, with values of 3.48 eV, 3.24 eV and 2.98 eV, respectively. (Fig. 2E). Although these theoretical values differ from the optical bandgaps-due to the exclusion of excited-state effects in DFT-the calculated narrowing trend agrees with the Tauc plot results [31].

High photo-response efficiency is a key indicator of a photocatalyst’s ability to achieve electron-hole separation and generate ROS [32]. As shown in Fig. 2F, the semicircular radii of SMU-7 and SMU-8 in the EIS Nyquist plots are significantly smaller than those of the monomer, with SMU-8 exhibiting the smallest radius, indicating the lowest charge-transfer resistance. The intermolecular hydrogen bonds present in SMU-8 and PA molecules facilitate more efficient light-induced carrier separation.

Transient photocurrent responses under intermittent visible light irradiation (Fig. 2G) further reveal that SMU-7 and SMU-8 generate photocurrent across multiple wavelengths, whereas the TP-monomer shows a strong response only under 380 nm UV light. This demonstrates that visible light can drive electron-hole separation in SMU-7 and SMU-8, with SMU-8 exhibiting superior carrier generation efficiency-a critical step in ROS production. Moreover, both SMU-7 and SMU-8 display stable and reversible photocurrent over at least eight on-off cycles.

These findings confirm that the binary HOFs, mediated by intermolecular hydrogen bonds in SMU-8, significantly enhance photogenerated carrier separation, highlighting the strong potential of SMU-8 as an efficient photocatalyst. Based on these results, we propose a photocatalytic mechanism for SMU-8 (Fig. 2H). Upon irradiation with appropriate wavelengths, SMU-8 absorbs light and undergoes electronic excitation, generating reactive species that disrupt the bacterial lipid membrane, proteins, and DNA, ultimately leading to bacterial death [33].

ROS-generating properties of SMU-7 and SMU-8

ROS yield is a key criterion for screening and optimizing novel photosensitizers. The performance of TP-monomer, SMU-7, and SMU-8 was evaluated using 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein (DCFH), 9,10-anthracenediyl-bis(methylene)dimalonic acid (ABDA), and electron spin resonance (ESR). First, total ROS production was assessed with DCFH, which is irreversibly oxidized to fluorescent dichlorofluorescein (DCF, Em = 525 nm) upon ROS exposure (Fig. 3A). Under visible light irradiation at doses ranging from 0 to 18.0 J/cm², all samples showed a dose-dependent increase in green fluorescence, significantly exceeding that of the PBS control. SMU-8 exhibited the most rapid fluorescence enhancement, in clear contrast to the negligible change observed with DCFH alone (Fig. 3B-E). Comparative analysis confirmed that at an irradiation dose of 18.0 J/cm², the mean fluorescence intensities (MFI) of SMU-8 and SMU-7 reached 87,449.00 ± 5,156.82 and 61,601.33 ± 2,109.52, respectively, which were 5.83-fold and 4.12-fold higher than that of the TP-monomer (14,975.00 ± 867.24). This result indicates that hydrogen bonding interactions with PA significantly enhance ROS generation within the TP-monomer framework (Fig. 3F). This enhancement is likely attributable to the PA unit in SMU-8, which effectively modulates the electronic structure of the photosensitizer via intermolecular hydrogen bonding. Such modulation facilitates the separation of photogenerated electron-hole pairs, enhances light absorption, and improves intersystem crossing (ISC) efficiency, collectively leading to a substantial increase in ROS production [34].

Among reactive oxygen species (ROS), singlet oxygen (¹O₂) is highly reactive and critically involved in antibacterial treatments. ABDA was used as a selective probe to quantify ¹O₂ production (Fig. 3G) [35]. Under light irradiation, all compounds (TP-monomer, SMU-7, SMU-8) decomposed ABDA more effectively than PBS (Fig. 3H-K). Notably, the kinetic degradation rate constants of SMU-7 (0.16 ± 0.01 min⁻¹) and SMU-8 (0.30 ± 0.01 min⁻¹) were 7-fold and 12-fold faster, respectively, than that of TP-monomer (0.02 ± 0.01 min⁻¹) (Fig. 3L), demonstrating the superior singlet oxygen generation efficiency, particularly that of SMU-8. This pronounced difference underscores the critical role of the hydrogen-bonded framework in enhancing ¹O₂ generation. We propose that the hydrogen-bond network in SMU-8 significantly increases the triplet yield and extends the triplet exciton lifetime, thereby creating more favorable conditions for energy transfer to ground-state oxygen (³O₂), resulting in efficient ¹O₂ production [36, 37]. The generation of ¹O₂ was further validated by ESR spectroscopy. No signals were detected in the dark. After visible light irradiation (18.0 J/cm²), the SMU-8 mixture showed the strongest TEMPO triplet signal, confirming its superior ¹O₂ production efficiency compared to TP-monomer and SMU-7 (Fig. 3M-O). The enhanced ROS generation of SMU-8 likely stems from the four carboxyl groups in PA, which form a denser and more stable intermolecular hydrogen-bond network compared to terephthalic acid (which contains only two carboxyl groups). This high-density binary hydrogen-bonded framework improves charge-transfer efficiency, thereby more effectively modulating the electronic structure of the photosensitizer unit.

These results demonstrate that the synergistic interplay of electron push-pull interactions mediated by hydrogen bonds significantly enhances the photodynamic performance of the small-molecule photosensitizer [14]. This architecture not only improves visible-light absorption but also establishes an efficient electron-transfer pathway. Upon photoexcitation, excited-state electrons from the donor rapidly migrate to the acceptor via hydrogen-bond-bridged interfaces. This process promotes efficient separation of photogenerated electron-hole pairs, substantially suppresses charge recombination, and ultimately leads to a higher quantum yield of ROS.

Characterization of textile substrates with HOF coatings

In the field of biomedicine, particularly in wound care, conventional textiles serve only as passive physical barriers and exudate absorbers, which limits their ability to exert effective antimicrobial effects. Textile surface functionalization represents an effective approach for endowing fabrics with enhanced properties including UV protection, antibacterial activity, and self-cleaning capabilities [38]. To achieve this, we deposited SMU-7 and SMU-8 onto three textile substrates - polyester fibers (P), woven cotton cloth (W), and knitted cotton cloth (K) - thereby preparing functionalized antibacterial composite coatings designated as SMU-7@P, SMU-8@P, SMU-7@W, SMU-8@W, SMU-7@K, and SMU-8@K respectively. The coating morphology and crystalline structure were systematically characterized using optical microscopy, SEM, and XRD. Visible color transitions from white to yellow (SMU-7) and orange (SMU-8) across all substrates confirmed successful coating application (Fig. 4A). Microscopic examination revealed a uniform color distribution with no significant agglomeration or uncoated areas, indicating that the HOFs were deposited on the fiber surface with high uniformity (Fig. 4B). XRD analysis demonstrated preserved crystallinity, with polyester fibers (17.9°, 22.9°, 26.2°) showing distinct SMU-7 (13.96°, 14.82°, 16.04°) and SMU-8 (10.26°, 11.10°, 25.20°) peaks in SMU-7@P/SMU-8@P composites. Similarly, cotton substrates (14.9°, 16.7°, 23.0°) maintained characteristic HOF peaks in SMU-7@W/SMU-8@W and SMU-7@K/SMU-8@K coatings (Fig. 4C-E). No crystalline phase transformation or amorphization occurred in the HOFs across all composite materials, indicating that this deposition method not only enables surface modification but also preserves the critical functional crystalline state of the HOFs. This allows for the sustained and stable generation of ROS during practical applications. SEM analysis of SMU-8 revealed complete textile coverage by micrometer-scale HOF crystals, though dense crystal aggregation precluded precise size determination (Fig. 4F, G). Notably, these smaller crystal structures, possessing a larger specific surface area, can penetrate the interstitial spaces between fibers, thereby achieving closer contact with the fiber surfaces. This may enhance the wash resistance of functionalized textiles [39]. This deposition technique effectively coats HOFs onto both synthetic and natural fibers, demonstrating excellent compatibility with textile substrates. As one of the most widely used textile materials in clinical wound care, woven cotton offers good hydrophilicity, which facilitates effective wound exudate management, ensures uniform contact between bacteria and the antimicrobial dressing, and thereby promotes superior antibacterial performance [40]. For these reasons, woven cotton was chosen as the primary substrate for subsequent biological experiments. The incorporation of HOFs into textiles presents a multifaceted advantage for wound management, primarily by reducing direct contact with wound tissues to mitigate potential cytotoxicity through limited cellular uptake. Furthermore, the demonstrated efficacy of HOFs in generating free radicals endows the textiles with enhanced antibacterial properties, aiding in the prevention of wound infection. Additionally, the inherent hydrophilic groups and porous structure of HOFs are expected to improve textile hydrophilicity and exudate absorption capacity, thereby promoting hemostasis. Of particular importance are binary framework materials composed of asymmetric acid-base pairs, in which acidic ligands release H+ and basic ligands show weak H+ adsorption, thereby exhibiting an overall weakly acidic character that enables sustained pH modulation of the wound microenvironment when deposited on textiles.

Multifunctional performance and biosafety evaluation of SMU-8@W in vitro

As a novel wound dressing, SMU-8@W was systematically evaluated for its multifunctional properties through pH measurements in simulated wound fluid (SWF) and in wounds, air permeability, moisture permeability, static contact angle measurements, blood coagulation index (BCI) assessment, hemolysis tests and cytotoxicity assays. The pH modulation capacity of the composite textiles was evaluated by monitoring changes in the pH of SWF following exposure to SMU-7@W and SMU-8@W (1 cm × 1 cm). The initial pH of the SWF was 7.32 ± 0.02. After treatment with SMU-7@W and SMU-8@W, the pH decreased to 5.65 ± 0.10 and 4.48 ± 0.18, respectively, but returned to near-neutral levels after 7 days (Fig. 5A). These results confirm that both materials initially shift the SWF pH from neutral to weakly acidic, followed by a gradual restoration to the normal range over time. A similar pH trend was observed in vivo when SMU-7@W and SMU-8@W were applied to diabetic wounds. The wound pH decreased from 7.57 ± 0.13 to 5.35 ± 0.12 during the initial days, and by day 7, it increased back to 7.10 ± 0.12 (Fig. 5B). The early mildly acidic microenvironment is beneficial for wound healing, as it helps inhibit bacterial growth while preserving the activity of key pro-healing cells-such as macrophages and fibroblasts-as well as related biological factors. Furthermore, as H+ release subsides and the pH approaches neutrality, conditions become favorable for the migration of skin cells, including keratinocytes and fibroblasts, thereby promoting wound closure [41, 42].

In the biodistribution study, the concentrations of PA in plasma, wound, and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were quantified over time using high-performance liquid chromatography (Fig. S16). After treatment with SMU‑8 and SMU‑8@W, the plasma concentrations of PA peaked at 2 h, respectively, with corresponding Cmax values of 69.70 ± 4.80 µg/mL and 26.00 ± 3.23 µg/mL, and AUC0−t values of 692.72 ± 95.76 µg·h/mL and 315.61 ± 67.71 µg·h/mL, respectively. This difference may be attributed to the reduced contact of SMU-8 with wound tissue caused by the woven cotton substrate, which benefits SMU‑8@W in maintaining prolonged antibacterial activity. The tissue distribution of PA in the wound, heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys further confirms this observation. At 1 and 4 h, the PA concentrations in wounds and organs were higher in the SMU-8 group than in the SMU-8@W group. In contrast, at 12 h, the wound concentration in the SMU-8 group was lower than that in the SMU-8@W group. This phenomenon not only provides SMU-8@W with better biosafety but also facilitates sustained modulation of wound pH.

The moisture permeability of functional dressings is a critical physical property that directly regulates humidity levels in the wound microenvironment [43]. Effective moisture permeability ensures timely removal of excess exudate, helping to prevent tissue maceration and reduce bacterial overgrowth associated with fluid accumulation [44]. Compared to woven cotton (4172.44 ± 311.71 g·m⁻²·d⁻¹), SMU-7@W (5280.37 ± 133.10 g·m⁻²·d⁻¹) and SMU-8@W (5874.20 ± 196.03 g·m⁻²·d⁻¹) exhibit enhanced moisture permeability by 25% and 42%, respectively (Fig. 5C). This phenomenon can be attributed to the abundance of carboxyl groups (-COOH) in the constituent units (TA and PA), which readily adsorb water molecules. Water is rapidly captured by these hydrophilic groups on the HOF surface, followed by efficient diffusion through both internal channels and the material surface, thereby accelerating the overall adsorption and transport of water [45]. The deposition of SMU-7 and SMU-8 reduced the breathability of the woven cotton substrate, as evidenced by the decrease from 782.83 ± 18.44 mm/s to 561.97 ± 10.82 mm/s for SMU-7@W and 488.07 ± 8.52 mm/s for SMU-8@W. This reduction is attributed to the partial obstruction of interstitial spaces between fibers by the deposited HOF crystals. Despite this decrease, the breathability of both SMU-7@W and SMU-8@W remained exceptionally high, exceeding that of the commercial 3 M dressing (3 M-D, 7.25 ± 3.87 mm/s) by more than 65-fold (Fig. 5D). This superior breathability helps maintain a dry wound environment, inhibits anaerobic bacterial growth, and thus promotes tissue regeneration.

The water and blood wettability of the textile substrate and a commercial gelatin hemostatic sponge (MHS) are shown in Fig. 5E and F. Compared to cotton fabric (45 s), water permeates SMU-7@W and SMU-8@W within 500 ms, representing a 90-fold faster wetting speed and demonstrating exceptional transient absorption capability (Fig. 5E, S17). This performance is attributed to the mesoporous structures of SMU-7 and SMU-8, which form abundant capillary networks on the fabric surfaces (Fig. S12, S13). Similarly, blood spread rapidly across SMU-7@W and SMU-8@W within seconds, demonstrating excellent blood wettability. In contrast, despite its porous structure, MHS showed limited blood penetration, with droplets failing to be fully absorbed even after 540 s (Fig. 5F, Supplementary Videos S1–S4)). This is likely due to the thicker gelatin layer in MHS, which requires prolonged expansion and softening for fluid permeation. The superior wettability of SMU-7@W and SMU-8@W facilitates rapid absorption of blood fluid absorption, leading to hemoconcentration and subsequent acceleration of red blood cell and platelet aggregation - crucial factors for efficient clot formation. In vitro hemostatic performance evaluation (Fig. 5G) revealed distinct differences among materials: the woven cotton group showed red washing solutions indicating minimal clotting, while SMU-8@W and SMU-7@W groups demonstrated significantly less solution coloration, comparable to MHS and indicative of excellent hemostatic capability. Quantitative assessment using the Blood Clotting Index (BCI) - a standard metric for evaluating hemostatic materials based on absorbance of unclotted blood at 540 nm - provided further evidence. A lower BCI value indicates better coagulation performance. After 20 min incubation, SMU-7@W and SMU-8@W showed significantly enhanced hemostatic performance, achieving BCI values of 20.58 ± 2.51% and 21.19 ± 2.52%, respectively. This represents an approximately 2.3-fold improvement over woven cotton (BCI = 48.63 ± 1.54%), which itself performed only slightly better than the TCP control (74.60 ± 2.21%) (Fig. 5H). This performance enhancement is attributed to the unique dynamic wetting capability of SMU-7@W and SMU-8@W. When blood meets the composite surface, water molecules rapidly permeate into the material’s interior. This process promotes the efficient accumulation of red blood cells and platelets on the surface, thereby accelerating clot formation to achieve rapid hemostasis.

The biocompatibility of SMU-7@W and SMU-8@W was assessed using hemolysis assays and CCK-8 assays. SMU-7@W and SMU-8@W groups revealed hemolysis rates below 5%, with supernatant nearly as transparent as that of the PBS negative control, indicating no hemolysis occurred. SEM imaging results also demonstrated that red blood cells in the woven cotton, SMU-7@W, and SMU-8@W treatment groups exhibited intact morphology, in contrast to the positive control (deionized water), where cell membrane rupture and cellular degeneration were clearly observed (Fig. S18). The cytotoxicity of SMU-7@W and SMU-8@W against three key cell types involved in wound healing, including human dermal fibroblasts (HDF-as, responsible for collagen and matrix synthesis), human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs, involved in angiogenesis), and Human adult low calcium high temperature keratinocytes (HaCaTs, critical for epithelial regeneration), was assessed using the CCK-8 assay [46]. Both composite textiles, loaded with 1 mg/mL of HOFs, demonstrated excellent biocompatibility, with cell viabilities remaining around 90% across all three cell lines. These values were significantly higher than those observed for SMU-7 alone (82.52% ± 0.71%, 78.39% ± 6.09%, and 78.64% ± 5.34% in HDF-as, HUVECs, and HaCaTs, respectively) and SMU-8 alone (62.80% ± 1.22%, 59.22% ± 2.26%, and 56.93% ± 3.30%, respectively) (Fig. 5I). Notably, woven cotton demonstrates good biosafety across various cell types (Fig. S19). Therefore, the enhanced cytocompatibility of SMU‑7@W and SMU‑8@W is likely attributable to the reduced release of HOFs and the intrinsically favorable cytocompatibility of the cotton substrate.

The IC₅₀ results were consistent with the viability data. The IC₅₀ values of SMU-7@W for HDF-as, HUVECs, and HaCaTs were 5.908 mg/mL, 6.202 mg/mL, and 5.435 mg/mL, respectively, representing 3.09-fold, 2.85-fold, and 2.15-fold increases compared to those of SMU-7 (1.912 mg/mL, 2.175 mg/mL, and 2.533 mg/mL, respectively). Similarly, SMU-8@W showed IC₅₀ values of 4.063 mg/mL, 5.780 mg/mL, and 5.313 mg/mL for HDF-as, HUVECs, and HaCaTs, which were 2.30-fold, 3.35-fold, and 2.59-fold higher than those of SMU-8 (1.764 mg/mL, 1.723 mg/mL, and 2.053 mg/mL, respectively) (Fig. 5J).

The significantly reduced cytotoxicity of SMU-7@W and SMU-8@W can be attributed to the effective anchoring of the HOFs onto the textile fibers, which minimizes direct contact between the crystalline materials and the cells. Together with their previously demonstrated anti-hemolytic properties, these findings underscore the potential of SMU-7@W and SMU-8@W as promising hemostatic wound dressings that support the healing process.

In vitro antibacterial efficacy and mechanism

Building upon the unique capability of textile-deposited SMU-8 to generate singlet oxygen (¹O₂) under visible light, we systematically evaluated the antibacterial performance of SMU-8@W against both Gram-positive (S. aureus) and Gram-negative (E. coli) bacteria. Our comprehensive assessment employed multiple analytical approaches: plate counting for viability assessment, live/dead staining for membrane integrity evaluation, SEM for morphological analysis, and biochemical assays for metabolic activity monitoring.

The light dose-dependent antibacterial studies revealed that visible light exposure significantly reduced bacterial survival across all treatment groups (TP@W, SMU-7@W, and SMU-8@W). Remarkably, SMU-8@W achieved near-complete bacterial eradication at a light dose of 18 J/cm² (30 mW/cm², 10 min), with inactivation rates of 99.67 ± 0.16% (E. coli) and 98.66 ± 2.00% (S. aureus), significantly outperforming both TP@W (52.29 ± 12.25% and 22.67 ± 8.68%) and SMU-7@W (86.07 ± 3.11% and 71.08 ± 6.06%) (Fig. 6A, B, E). Notably, SMU-8@W achieved higher antibacterial efficiency than the commercial positive control 3 M™ Silvercel™ Non-Adherent Antimicrobial Alginate Dressing (3 M-Ag), with the latter attaining 91.00 ± 1.35% against E. coli and 84.64 ± 4.52% against S. aureus at 10 min of treatment (Fig. S20). The incomplete bacterial eradication by 3 M-Ag may be attributed to the time-dependent nature of its antibacterial activity, as it requires a longer swelling time to fully release its active components.

Live/dead fluorescence staining further confirmed these results, showing intense red fluorescence (indicating membrane damage) in SMU-8@W-treated samples with survival rates of only 6.60 ± 2.65% (E. coli) and 3.78 ± 2.35% (S. aureus), consistent with plate counting data (Fig. 6C, D, F).

Biochemical analysis indicated that the SMU-8@W experimental group exhibited significantly higher levels of protein leakage for both S. aureus and E. coli, at 0.32 ± 0.02 mg/mL and 0.28 ± 0.04 mg/mL, respectively, compared to the TP@W group (0.22 ± 0.01 mg/mL and 0.24 ± 0.01 mg/mL) and the SMU-7@W group (0.17 ± 0.02 mg/mL and 0.13 ± 0.01 mg/mL). Simultaneously, SMU-8@W treatment significantly inhibited ATP synthesis in S. aureus and E. coli, reducing ATP levels by 57.79% and 73.13%, respectively, with reductions exceeding those observed in the TP@W group (21.68% and 27.13%) and SMU-7@W group (40.08% and 51.71%). Reducing sugars are a key indicator of bacterial cell membrane integrity. Compared to the TP@W and SMU-7@W groups, treatment with SMU-8@W resulted in a 2.19-fold and 1.35-fold increase in reducing sugar leakage from S. aureus, and a 1.89-fold and 1.28-fold increase from E. coli, respectively (Fig. S21-S23). The extensive leakage of cellular components-such as reducing sugars, proteins, and ATP-suggests severe damage to the bacterial cell membrane. The SMU-8@W group induced the highest level of leakage in both bacteria, which may be attributed to its efficient generation of ¹O₂ under light irradiation. As a highly reactive oxygen species, ¹O₂ rapidly oxidizes unsaturated lipids in the cell membrane, leading to increased membrane permeability [47].

Morphological changes in bacteria after SMU-8@W treatment were further examined using SEM. Untreated bacteria displayed smooth, intact surfaces with clear edges and maintained structural integrity. In contrast, bacteria treated with SMU-8@W or SMU-7@W under light irradiation exhibited severe membrane disruption, characterized by collapse, wrinkling, and extensive damage (Fig. 6G-H). The antibacterial action of SMU-8@W and SMU-7@W stems from their dense micron-scale HOF coating. This coating provides a large bacteria–material contact area, mitigating the diffusion-limited short lifetime of ¹O₂ and promoting efficient oxidation of bacterial membrane components such as polysaccharides, proteins, and nucleic acids [48], ultimately leading to effective membrane destruction and bacterial death.

Therapeutic efficacy of SMU-8@W against S. aureus-infected wounds

To systematically evaluate the wound healing efficacy of the SMU‑8@W dressing, a 7 mm full‑thickness circular wound was created on the dorsal skin of type II diabetic C57BL/6 mice, given the limited available skin area. To minimize wound contraction, a silicone splint ring (outer diameter 18 mm, inner diameter 10 mm) was placed around each wound and further secured using cyanoacrylate glue and sutures. Previous studies have shown that S. aureus is the most common pathogen in diabetic foot infections. Owing to impaired immune responses and infection promotion under hyperglycemic conditions, even low‑virulence staphylococci can cause persistent infections in the soft tissues of diabetic patients and mice. Therefore, the mouse infection model was established by inoculating the wounds with S. aureus for 2 h. It should be noted, however, that epidermal hyperproliferation, which is observed in human chronic wounds (e.g., diabetic foot ulcers and venous leg ulcers), is not prominent in diabetic mouse wounds [49, 50]. The mice were divided into four groups and treated for 14 days as follows: (1) Control (physiological saline), (2) woven cotton (1 × 1 cm), (3) SMU-8@W/L− (1 × 1 cm, dark condition), and (4) SMU-8@W/L+ (1 × 1 cm, exposed to 18 J/cm²) (Fig. 7A). Quantification of serum insulin (INS) and HbA1c levels in mice (Fig. S24) showed that, compared with normal mice, HbA1c values exceeded 6.5% in all diabetic mice, whereas INS levels were all below 5 µIU/mL. H&E staining of mouse pancreatic tissues further revealed that in the normal group, the islets exhibited normal morphology with clear boundaries, round or oval shapes, and regularly arranged islet cells. In contrast, mice in the disease model group showed vacuolation, degeneration, and necrosis of pancreatic islet tissues, along with irregular arrangement of islet cells. These results collectively confirm the successful establishment of a type II diabetic mouse model.

Wound healing was evaluated based on dynamic closure rates (measured on days 0, 3, 7, 10, and 14) and histopathological examination (H&E and Masson staining). As depicted in Figs. 7B-D, the saline-treated group showed markedly delayed healing, with closure rates of only 46.91 ± 4.20% on day 10 and 61.29 ± 3.10% on day 14. In contrast, the SMU-8@W/L+ group exhibited significantly accelerated wound closure, reaching 78.28 ± 4.06% by day 10 and nearly complete repair (93.61 ± 3.92%) by day 14. The percentages of unhealed wound area on day 10 were 43.54 ± 4.56% and 33.06 ± 5.52% for the woven cotton and SMU-8@W/L− groups, respectively. Notably, SMU-8@W/L− demonstrated better performance than woven cotton by day 7, with closure rates of 54.46 ± 5.23% versus 41.46 ± 8.50%. The rapid healing of infected wounds can be attributed to the synergistic mechanism involving wettability, pH, and ROS. However, SMU-8@W intrinsically integrates both moisture-regulating and pH-modulating properties, it is difficult to verify the individual contributions of these two factors separately. Instead, we calculated the separate effects of pH modulation combined with wettability from that of ROS. The combined contribution of pH modulation and wettability is 63.87%-69.90%, while ROS accounts for approximately 30.19%-36.13%.

Bacterial loads at the wound sites were further assessed on day 4 via colony-forming unit (CFU) counts (Fig. 7E-F). The SMU-8@W/L+ group exhibited a substantial decrease in bacterial survival, with the rate reduced to 29.53%, demonstrating potent photodynamic antibacterial activity. In contrast, the woven-cotton and SMU-8@W/L− groups showed bacterial survival rates of 85.14% and 69.59%, respectively, while the control group still displayed severe infection. These results confirm that SMU-8@W significantly inhibits bacterial growth on the wounds under light irradiation. Since the HOF is present only on the outer and inner surfaces of the fabric without penetrating the tissue, only the surface-bound HOF needs to generate ROS, eliminating the need for light to penetrate the tissue to achieve wound infection control. Under white light irradiation, the surface HOF produces sufficient ROS. On one hand, these ROS prevent external bacterial invasion and subsequent wound infection. On the other hand, once the fabric absorbs wound exudate, the generated ROS can enter the wound along with the exudate, killing bacteria both on the wound surface and within the wound. One of the key advantages of this strategy is that it requires no additional equipment, simply placing the fabric dressing on the wound and exposing it to ambient sunlight enables effective infection control. SMU-8@W/L− also exhibited notable bacteriostatic effects. This can be attributed to the weakly acidic microenvironment created by SMU-8@W, which restricts microbial adhesion and proliferation at the wound site.

Histological evaluations further elucidated the repair mechanisms. H&E staining showed complete epithelialization and well-organized skin layers in the SMU-8@W/L+ group by day 14, whereas other groups exhibited irregular epidermal and dermal structures. Quantitative analyses confirmed that SMU-8@W/L+ resulted in the shortest epithelial gap and the greatest granulation tissue thickness (0.70 ± 0.13 μm) compared to the Control (0.33 ± 0.04 μm), woven cotton (0.59 ± 0.13 μm), and SMU-8@W/L− groups (0.64 ± 0.10 μm) (Fig. 7G, S25). Masson staining revealed that, in contrast to the sparse and disorganized collagen fibers (17.24 ± 2.20% deposition) in the Control group, the SMU-8@W/L+ group exhibited dense, well-aligned mature collagen with a significantly higher deposition rate of 84.32 ± 4.60% (Fig. 7H, S24). Angiogenesis, assessed by CD31 immunofluorescence, was 2.23-fold and 1.61-fold higher in the SMU-8@W/L+ group than in the woven cotton and SMU-8@W/L− groups, respectively (Fig. 7I-J). Meanwhile, CD68 staining indicated that SMU-8@W/L+ reduced macrophage infiltration by 2.73-fold and 1.71-fold compared to woven cotton and SMU-8@W, demonstrating effective control of inflammation (Fig. 7K-L). In addition, the level of inflammatory infiltration in the healed skin was further evaluated by immunofluorescence and ELISA for TNF-α and IL-6 (Fig. S26-27). These results indicate that the SMU8@W/L+ treatment group effectively reduced the expression of TNF‑α and IL‑6 in skin tissue by suppressing the bacterial infection status of the wound, thereby facilitating accelerated wound healing. The ability of SMU-8@W to promote angiogenesis can be attributed to its excellent breathability, which maintains a well-oxygenated, dry wound microenvironment. This environment inhibits anaerobic bacteria and supports tissue regeneration. Enhanced angiogenesis, in turn, improves the delivery of oxygen and nutrients to the wound site, facilitating collagen deposition and accelerating healing. Furthermore, by effectively eliminating bacteria such as S. aureus, SMU-8@W mitigates persistent inflammatory responses and prevents pathogen-secreted enzymes (e.g., hyaluronidase) from degrading the nascent extracellular matrix and collagen fibers [51]. SMU-8@W effectively eliminates bacteria at the wound site, alleviates inflammatory conditions, and reduces collagen degradation, thereby promoting DFUs wound healing through multiple mechanisms.

Biosafety evaluation demonstrated excellent biocompatibility, with no significant differences in hematological parameters or histopathological changes in major organs in diabetic mice after 14 days of exposure. All measured parameters remained within normal ranges, confirming the material’s safety for biomedical applications (Fig. S28-29). The blood biochemical analyses also confirmed no obvious acute and long-term toxicity of SMU‑8@W (Fig. S30). These findings collectively demonstrate that SMU-8@W enables efficient, broad-spectrum antibacterial activity through visible light-driven ROS generation, while maintaining exceptional biosafety. This makes SMU-8@W a promising candidate for diabetic wound dressings, capable of combating infection while promoting DFUs healing.

Transcriptomic analysis of diabetic foot ulcer wound tissues

To characterize the molecular signaling changes induced by inflammation inhibition and antibacterial effects, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed on wound tissues from SMU-8@W-treated mice and control mice. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed high reproducibility and correlation among samples (Fig. 8A). Compared with the control group, the SMU-8@W/L+ group exhibited 685 significantly upregulated genes and 1,333 significantly downregulated genes, indicating that the combined effects of SMU-8@W/L+, including its antibacterial activity, play an important role in regulating infected wound repair (Fig. 8B-D). GO enrichment analysis showed that the differentially expressed genes were enriched in molecular functions such as cytokine receptor binding, growth factor activity, and extracellular matrix structural components. They were also enriched in cellular components such as extracellular matrix, collagen-containing extracellular matrix, and adherens junctions. In addition, they were enriched in biological processes such as wound healing, positive regulation of cell migration, and inflammatory response (Fig. 8E-G). To elucidate the cellular signaling pathways regulated by the above-mentioned differentially expressed genes, a Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was performed. Representative signaling pathways are presented in Fig. 8H. During the early stage of wound healing, the NF-κB signaling pathway may play a core regulatory role, responding to signals from external pathogens or tissue injury and initiating the transcription of a series of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) showed downregulation of the inflammatory response, which effectively promoted epidermal cell differentiation and development, thereby accelerating wound healing (Fig. 8I). The molecular mechanisms implicated by the transcriptomic data were further validated by qPCR (Fig. 8J). Compared with the control group, SMU-8@W treatment significantly reduced the mRNA expression levels of the inflammation-related genes IL-1β, IL-6, NFKBIA, and RELA (a core subunit of NF-κB), and this downregulation was more pronounced in the treatment group (SMU-8@W/L+) (Fig. 8J). Total RELA transcripts were decreased, and combined with the overall inhibition of the NF-κB signaling pathway observed in KEGG analysis, this suggests that SMU-8@W may simultaneously inhibit the activation of NF-κB and its downstream inflammatory cascade. Furthermore, the qPCR results of the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway, and IL-7 signaling pathway were highly consistent with the downregulation of the inflammatory response observed in RNA-seq (Fig. S31), further validating the reliability of the transcriptomic data. Collectively, SMU-8@W promotes extracellular matrix remodeling, cell migration, and epidermal differentiation associated with wound healing by suppressing the overactivation of inflammation-related signaling pathways.

Long-term stability of SMU-8@W in physiological environments

Although SMU-8@W demonstrated potent antibacterial, hemostatic, and wound-healing properties in diabetic wound models, its chemical stability and structural integrity under physiological (pH 7.4) and wound (pH 5.5) conditions required systematic evaluation to ensure reliability in clinical applications. To assess stability, SMU-7@W and SMU-8@W were immersed in buffered solutions at both pH values, and changes in fluorescence intensity were monitored over 168 h (7 days) at predetermined intervals.

Fluorescence monitoring revealed excellent retention for both materials. SMU-7@W (green fluorescence) showed an initial rapid release within the first 6 h (8.67 ± 6.00% at pH 7.4; 8.08 ± 6.10% at pH 5.5), likely due to loosely bound HOF crystals on the fabric surface. Subsequently, the framework stabilized considerably, retaining 87.24 ± 3.81% (pH 7.4) and 85.25 ± 6.33% (pH 5.5) of the original load after 7 days (Fig. S32). Similarly, SMU-8@W (red fluorescence) exhibited minimal cumulative release-only 14.02 ± 5.27% at pH 7.4 and 13.04 ± 1.31% at pH 5.5 after the same period (Fig. 9A-C). Total material loss remained below 15% under all conditions, indicating robust stability attributable to strong non-covalent interactions between HOFs and cotton fibers. The slight initial release did not affect overall performance, confirming structural integrity across different pH environments. Notably, the variation in release between pH conditions was less than 3%, underscoring the material’s broad adaptability to diverse wound microenvironments.

The functional performance of the material remained well preserved after 7 days of immersion in pH 7.4 buffer. First, hemostatic function was maintained, as the blood clotting index (BCI) stayed stable at around 21%, showing no significant difference from the initial value of 20.66% (Fig. 9D). Meanwhile, the wettability was also retained, with water penetrating SMU-8@W within 3000 ms, confirming a consistent hydration capacity (Fig. 9E). Furthermore, SMU-8@W exhibited sustained photodynamic antibacterial efficacy, maintaining approximately 90% inhibition rates against both E. coli and S. aureus (Fig. 9F, G). SMU-7@W exhibited comparable wettability and hemostatic performance, with rapid water penetration within 140 ms and stable BCI values (Fig. S33-34). Thus, SMU-8@W exhibits outstanding structural and functional stability under physiologically relevant conditions, retaining core antimicrobial, wetting, and hemostatic properties throughout prolonged exposure. These findings highlight the potential of SMU-8@W as an advanced functional dressing capable of reliable long-term performance across varying wound environments.

Conclusion

In summary, we present SMU-8@W-a dynamically pH-modulating HOF-textile composite that accelerates diabetic wound healing. By programmatically lowering wound pH to suppress infection and then raising it to aid cell proliferation, the material creates a regenerative microenvironment. A 12-fold increase in singlet oxygen (¹O₂) production, enabled by hydrogen-bond-mediated electron transfer, ensures strong antibacterial efficacy. Additional advantages include a 90-fold increase in wettability, 2.3-fold better hemostasis, high moisture permeability, and 2-3-fold higher biocompatibility from minimized HOFs exposure. This synergistic combination offers a translatable dressing technology for programmable management of chronic diabetic wounds.

Experimental section

Materials and cells

Pyromellitic acid (≥ 98.0% purity), terephthalic acid (≥ 98.0% purity), and tris[4-(pyridin-4-yl) phenyl] aniline (TP-monomer, C33H24N4, 95% purity) were purchased from Bide Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd. Methanol and N, N-dimethylformamide were purchased from Guangzhou Chemical Reagent Factory. All chemicals were used without further purification. Human dermal fibroblasts (HDF-as) and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and human immortalized keratinocytes cells (HaCaTs) were purchased from Shanghai Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences. TNF-α, IL-6, CD31 and CD68 antibodies were purchased from Affinity Antibody Company. Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), bovine serum albumin, and DAPI dye were obtained from Boster (Wuhan, China). The ATP content detection kit was purchased from Servicebio Co., Ltd., and the enhanced BCA protein detection kit and reducing sugar content detection kit were purchased from Solarbio Co., Ltd. Calcein-AM/PI cell viability/cytotoxicity assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Textile substrate (knitted cotton cloth, woven cotton cloth and polyester fibers cloth) was purchased from commercial sources.

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and human immortalized keratinocytes (HaCaTs) were maintained in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin). Human dermal fibroblasts (HDF-as) were cultured in a commercially available fibroblast-specific medium (Xiamen Yimo Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). All cell lines were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere, with medium changes performed every 48–72 h.

Methodologies and measurements

X-ray powder diffraction (XRD) data were collected on a Bruker D8 Venture diffractometer (Germany) at 298 K using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å). ¹H NMR spectral data were measured on a Bruker Avance NEO 400 MHz instrument (Billerica, MA, USA) using deuterated dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO‑d₆) as the solvent at 298 K. Chemical shifts (δ) were calibrated relative to the solvent residual proton signal (DMSO‑d₆: δ 2.50 ppm). Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) data were collected using a Thermo Fisher Scientific Nicolet iN10 spectrometer (Waltham, MA, USA). Samples were prepared using the KBr pellet method, and spectra were recorded in the range 4000 –400 cm⁻¹ with a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹ and 32 cumulative scans. Morphological images were collected using a ZEISS GeminiSEM 300 field emission scanning electron microscope (SEM; Carl Zeiss Microscopy Ltd., UK) with an acceleration voltage of 3 kV. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) data were collected using a JEOL JEM‑F200 field emission transmission electron microscope (Tokyo, Japan) at a working voltage of 200 kV. Surface elemental composition data of the samples were obtained using a Thermo Scientific K‑Alpha X‑ray photoelectron spectrometer (Waltham, MA, USA). Quantitative analysis of organic elements was performed using the Elementar Vario EL cube system (Langenselbold, Germany) based on dynamic combustion‑adsorption column separation technology, enabling simultaneous detection of C/N/O content. Ultraviolet-visible diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (UV–vis DRS) was performed using a Shimadzu UV‑3600i Plus spectrophotometer (Kyoto, Japan) equipped with a 60 mm integrating sphere accessory, with spectrally pure BaSO₄ as the reference standard.

Synthesis of SMU-7 single crystals

Terephthalic acid (1 mg/mL in methanol, 6.7 mL) was combined with tris[4-(pyridin-4-yl) phenyl] aniline (1 mg/mL in a methanol/N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF) mixture (v/v 96:4), 9.7 mL) in a crystallization vial. The mixture was allowed to stand at room temperature for solvent evaporation, yielding transparent SMU-7 single crystals, which were then used for TEM observation and single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SC-XRD) analysis.

Synthesis of SMU-8 single crystals

Pyromellitic acid (10.25 mg/mL in methanol, 1.0 mL) was combined with tris[4-(pyridin-4-yl) phenyl] aniline (1 mg/mL in a methanol/N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF) mixture (v/v 96:4), 9.7 mL) in a borosilicate glass crystallization vial. The mixture was allowed to stand at room temperature for solvent evaporation, yielding transparent SMU-8 single crystals, which were then used for TEM observation and single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SC-XRD) analysis.

Synthesis of SMU-7 and SMU-8 powders

SMU-7 and SMU-8 were synthesized via liquid‑phase self‑assembly. For SMU-7, a solution of terephthalic acid (56.25 mg/mL in DMF, 1.44 mL) was mixed with tris[4-(pyridin-4-yl) phenyl] aniline (25.6 mg/mL in DMF, 4.5 mL), followed by the addition of 6 mL methanol. The mixture was stirred at 500 rpm for 2 h to facilitate complete formation of SMU-7. Similarly, SMU-8 was prepared by combining pyromellitic acid (123 mg/mL in DMF, 1 mL) with tris[4-(pyridin-4-yl) phenyl] aniline (25.6 mg/mL in DMF, 4.5 mL), adding 6 mL methanol, and stirring at 500 rpm for 2 h to form an SMU-8 suspension. Both suspensions were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min. The precipitates were collected and washed three times with methanol (10 mL each) to eliminate unreacted precursors and residual DMF. Finally, the products were dried under ambient conditions to yield SMU-7 and SMU-8 as powders, which were then used for SEM observation.

Liquid-phase spray deposition (LPSD) of SMU-7, SMU-8 and TP-monomer coatings on textile substrates

The coating process was performed as follows: DMF solutions containing SMU-7, SMU-8, or TP-monomer (50 mg in 5 mL) were spray‑deposited onto various textile substrates (including knitted cotton, woven cotton, and polyester fiber fabrics, 5 × 5 cm²) using 10‑second spray cycles. After each deposition, samples were dried at 100 °C for 1 h, and the process was repeated until the solution was completely consumed. The coated textiles were washed with methanol (3 × 3 mL aliquots) to remove residual DMF and unreacted precursors, followed by final drying at 100 °C for 1 h.

Density functional theory calculations for SMU-7, SMU-8 and TP-monomer

Quantum chemical calculations were systematically performed using Gaussian 16 at the density functional theory (DFT) level. Initial geometry optimizations were conducted employing the B3LYP hybrid functional with the 6‑31G(d, p) basis set until the default convergence thresholds were reached. Using the optimized ground‑state geometries, we subsequently performed electronic structure analysis at the same theoretical level (B3LYP/6‑31G(d, p)), with particular emphasis on characterizing the frontier molecular orbitals (FMOs)—specifically the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO)—along with their associated electronic properties including orbital energies and HOMO–LUMO gap. For deeper electronic structure characterization, we employed Multiwfn to conduct comprehensive electron density difference analyses, examining molecular electrostatic potentials (MEPs), Fukui functions, and orbital composition. All computational results, including optimized molecular structures, molecular orbital isosurfaces, and electron density difference maps, were visualized using VMD (Version 1.9.4).

Photoelectrochemical characterization of SMU-7, SMU-8, and TP-monomer

The photoelectrochemical properties were evaluated using a CHI 760 electrochemical workstation (CH Instruments, USA) configured with a standard three‑electrode system. The system comprised (1) a working electrode of catalyst‑modified ITO glass (1 cm²), (2) a platinum sheet counter electrode (10 mm × 10 mm × 0.1 mm), and (3) a saturated Ag/AgCl reference electrode, immersed in 0.1 M Na₂SO₄ electrolyte. Photoresponse measurements including electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and transient photocurrent response (TPR) were conducted under illumination from a 300 W xenon lamp with a monochromator, testing multiple wavelengths: 380 nm (violet), 450 nm (blue), 520 nm (green), 600 nm (orange), 700 nm (red), and 6500 K cool white light. For working electrode preparation, 10 mg of each sample (SMU-7, SMU-8, or TP-monomer) was dispersed in 1 mL ultrapure water with 50 µL Nafion solution (5 wt%, DuPont), followed by 30 min sonication to achieve a homogeneous suspension. A 100 µL aliquot was then drop‑cast onto pretreated ITO substrates and dried at 60 °C for 4 h, yielding electrodes with a controlled active material loading of 0.50 ± 0.05 mg/cm².

Electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy analysis

The spin‑trapping experiments were conducted using 2,2,6,6‑tetramethylpiperidine (TEMP) as the radical capture agent. A 20 mM TEMP solution was prepared by dissolving 20 µL of TEMP in 1 mL of anhydrous methanol. For each sample (SMU-7, SMU-8, and TP-monomer), 1 mL of the material suspension was mixed with 1 mL of the prepared TEMP solution. Subsequently, 300–400 µL aliquots of the reaction mixtures were transferred to quartz ESR tubes for analysis. The measurements were performed on a Bruker EMXplus‑6/1 spectrometer equipped with a 300 W xenon lamp illumination system, enabling in situ light irradiation. The formation of TEMPO adducts was monitored in real time through continuous signal acquisition during the photoirradiation process.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation assessment

ROS generation was evaluated using two complementary fluorescent probes. Total ROS was detected with 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein (DCFH). Test solutions of SMU-7, SMU-8, or TP-monomer (1 mg/mL in PBS) were mixed with 50 µM DCFH; a DCFH/PBS solution served as the control. Under white light irradiation at doses of 0 J/cm² (30 mW/cm², 0 min), 5.4 J/cm² (30 mW/cm², 3 min), 9 J/cm² (30 mW/cm², 5 min), and 18 J/cm² (30 mW/cm², 10 min), the oxidation product DCF was monitored by recording fluorescence spectra (500–650 nm, λex = 480 nm), and the emission intensity at 525 nm was used to quantify total ROS generation.

Simultaneously, singlet oxygen (¹O₂) production was quantified using the 9,10-anthracenediyl-bis(methylene)dimalonic acid (ABDA) probe. Samples (1 mg/mL in PBS) containing 25 µM ABDA were irradiated under identical conditions, with ABDA/PBS as control. The temporal decay of ABDA absorption (300–550 nm) was tracked, with the logarithmic transformation of absorbance ratios (Ln(A₀/A) at 380 nm) providing a quantitative measure of ¹O₂ generation kinetics, where A₀ represents the initial absorbance before irradiation.

Assessment of pH regulation activity

We first evaluated the pH regulation of fabrics coated with HOFs in vitro using simulated wound fluid (SWF). SWF was prepared by dissolving 0.95 g of maximum recovery diluent (MRD) in 100 mL of sterilized Milli-Q water, followed by the addition of 100 mL of fetal bovine serum (FBS). Fabric samples (1 cm × 1 cm) of woven cotton, TP@W, SMU-7@W, and SMU-8@W were immersed in 0.2 mL of SWF, and the SWF was replaced at predetermined intervals (0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7 days). The pH was measured at various time points using a calibrated pH meter (LabSen241-3 A micro electrode; detection volume of 15 µL). Subsequently, the same fabrics were applied to skin wounds in diabetic mice. At predetermined time points, when the wound exudate reached a sufficient volume for measurement, the wound surface pH was monitored using the same pH meter without tissue penetration. The experimental groups included: saline, woven cotton group, TP@W group, SMU-7@W group, and SMU-8@W group. Each wound site was tested three times to ensure consistency and accuracy [52, 53].

Pharmacokinetics

To investigate the in vivo bioavailability of SMU‑8@W, C57 mice were randomly divided into two groups (n = 3), and received wound administration of either SMU‑8@W or an equivalent dose of SMU‑8. Blood samples were collected at multiple time points post‑administration. Plasma proteins were precipitated using acetonitrile: plasma (80 µL) was mixed with acetonitrile (250 µL), vortexed for 1 min, and centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 10 min. Then, the supernatant (100 µL) was taken and centrifuged again for 5 min, and the final supernatant (20 µL) was injected for HPLC analysis. Separation was performed on a Diamonsil C18 column (serial no. 8034287, 5 μm, 250 × 4.6 mm) with a C18 guard column (mobile phase: 0.1% phosphoric acid in water/methanol = 9:1; flow rate: 0.6 mL/min; temperature: 25 ℃). For tissue distribution analysis, organ homogenates were prepared and processed using the same method.

Wettability, air permeability, and moisture permeability of SMU-7@W and SMU-8@W

The air permeability of the fabric was evaluated in strict compliance with the Chinese national standard GB/T 5453 − 1997 (Textiles – Determination of Breathability), while moisture permeability was assessed following GB/T 12704.1–2009 (Textiles – Test Method for Moisture Permeability of Fabrics). Both tests were performed using a Ningfang Instruments YG825G Fabric Breathability Tester (manufactured by China Textile Instruments Co., Ltd.).

For wettability analysis, the contact angle and wetting time of SMU-7@W and SMU-8@W in pure water were measured using a LAUDA Scientific LSA100S-T contact angle tester (Germany). Each sample (5 × 5 cm) was placed on the testing stage, and 0.2 mL of pure water was deposited onto its surface. The spreading behavior of the water droplets was recorded at 0 s, 5 s, 30 s, 60 s, and 600 s. The same methodology was applied to compare blood spreading performance across different fiber composite surfaces.

Antimicrobial activity assessment under simulated daylight conditions

The antimicrobial efficacy of fiber composites was evaluated using the following standardized procedure: Sample materials (SMU-7@W, SMU-8@W, TP@T, and woven cotton) were cut into 0.5 × 0.5 cm² squares, sterilized with 75% ethanol, and oven-dried at 60 °C for 12 h. Bacterial suspensions were prepared by inoculating 10 µL of bacterial stock into 5 mL of culture medium, followed by 16 h incubation in a shaking incubator (37 °C, 250 rpm). Under sterile laminar flow conditions, 2 µL aliquots of bacterial suspension were uniformly applied onto fiber samples (n = 3 per group) including: (1) woven cotton, (2) TP@W, (3) SMU-7@W, (4) SMU-8@W, and 3 M-Ag groups.

Then secure the light source 20 cm above the tabletop and preheat for 10 min. After treatment with different doses of 0 J/cm² (30 mW/cm², 0 min), 5.4 J/cm² (30 mW/cm², 3 min), 9 J/cm² (30 mW/cm², 5 min), and 18 J/cm² (30 mW/cm², 10 min), the samples were transferred to 2 mL centrifuge tubes containing 0.5 mL of sterile water and vortexed for 10 s to detach the bacteria. The resulting suspensions were serially diluted (25 µL into 975 µL sterile water), and 10 µL aliquots were plated onto culture agar. After 12 h incubation at 37 °C, colony-forming units (CFUs) were enumerated from digital images of the culture plates to determine time-dependent antibacterial rates for each material group.

Bacterial viability and membrane integrity assays

The antibacterial efficacy of composite fiber materials was assessed through a combination of fluorescence-based viability staining and protein leakage measurements. Fiber samples (SMU-7@W, SMU-8@W, TP@W, and woven cotton control) were prepared as 1 × 1 cm² squares, sterilized with 75% ethanol, and dried at 60 °C for 12 h. Bacterial cultures were grown by inoculating 100 µL of bacterial suspension into 5 mL of culture medium, followed by 16 h incubation at 37 °C with 250 rpm shaking. Under aseptic laminar flow conditions, 100 µL aliquots of bacterial suspension were evenly distributed onto fiber samples (n = 3), including: (i) woven cotton, (ii) TP@W, (iii) SMU-7@W, and (iv) SMU-8@W groups.

Bacterial viability was determined using a dual-fluorescence staining protocol: propidium iodide (PI, 10 min incubation) selectively stained membrane-compromised (nonviable) bacteria, while 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, 15 min incubation) labeled all bacterial cells. Fluorescence microscopy was employed to visualize and quantify live/dead bacterial populations.

For protein leakage analysis, treated bacterial suspensions were subjected to sonication and centrifugation (12,000 g, 10 min). The resulting supernatants were analyzed using an enhanced BCA protein assay kit, with absorbance measurements at 560 nm serving as an indicator of membrane integrity loss and cellular content release.

Assessment of wound healing in a diabetic mouse model

The diabetic mouse model was established through a combination of high-fat/high-sugar diet and streptozotocin (STZ) administration. Four-to-six-week-old C57BL/6 mice were fed a high-fat/high-sugar diet for one month, followed by a 12–16 h fasting period before receiving an intraperitoneal injection of STZ (100 mg/kg). After an additional 2 h fasting period, blood glucose levels were monitored using a Sinocare GA-3 glucometer via tail vein sampling two days post-injection. Diabetes induction was confirmed when non-fasting blood glucose levels exceeded 22.0 mM on three consecutive measurements [4, 54].

For wound healing experiments, anesthetized mice (1.5% isoflurane) received dorsal 7 mm circular full-thickness wounds inoculated with 40 µL S. aureus suspension. Infected mice were randomly assigned to four treatment groups: (1) saline control, (2) woven cotton (1 × 1 cm), (3) non-irradiated SMU-8@W (1 × 1 cm), and (4) irradiated SMU-8@W (1 × 1 cm, 18 J/cm²). The wound areas were photographed and measured using ImageJ software on days 0, 4, 7, 10 and 14 after treatment. The wound closure percentage was calculated as: [(H₀-Hₙ)/H₀] × 100%, where H₀ represents initial wound area (day 0) and Hₙ denotes the area at measurement time points (n = 4, 7, 10, 14 days). The contribution rate of pH and wettability was calculated as [(An − A1) / (An − A0)] × 100%, and the contribution rate of ROS was calculated as [(A1 − A0) / (An − A0)] × 100%, where An represents the wound healing rate of the SMU-8@W/L+ group, A0 represents that of the control group, and A1 represents that of the SMU-8@W/L− group.

On day 14, skin samples surrounding the wound and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) were collected for histological evaluation. Tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (pH 7.4, 4 °C, 24 h), processed through graded dehydration, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned for H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining to assess epithelial regeneration, granulation tissue formation, and collagen deposition. Additionally, immunofluorescence microscopy (Nikon, Japan) was performed using antibodies against CD68, CD31, IL‑6, and TNF‑α to assess inflammatory macrophage infiltration, cytokine levels, and angiogenesis at the wound sites.

Measurement of serum TNF‑α and IL‑6 levels

Serum levels of TNF‑α and IL‑6 were measured using commercial ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, diluted serum samples and standards were added to the pre‑coated plates and incubated. After washing, detection antibodies and enzyme conjugates were sequentially added with intervening wash steps. The colorimetric reaction was developed with a TMB substrate, stopped with stop solution, and the absorbance was read at 450 nm. Cytokine concentrations were calculated from the standard curve.

In vivo antibacterial efficacy of SMU-8@W

On day 4 of the wound healing experiment in mice, bacteria were collected from the wound site, dispersed in 1 mL of sterile PBS, and 100 µL of the diluted suspension was plated onto agar. After incubation at 37 °C for 12 h, antibacterial activity was evaluated based on colony counts [55].

Biosafety assessment

To evaluate the potential organ toxicity of SMU-8@W, blood samples were collected through cardiac puncture on postoperative day 14. After allowing the samples to clot at room temperature for 30 min, they were centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 15 min to obtain serum for comprehensive biochemical analysis. Using specialized kits, we quantified multiple organ function markers: (1) liver function parameters including alanine aminotransferase (ALT, reference range: 10.06–96.47 U/L), aspartate transaminase (AST, 36.31-235.48 U/L), direct bilirubin (DBIL, 0.45–33.89 µmol/L), albumin (ALB, 21.22–39.15 g/L), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP, 22.52-474.35 U/L); (2) kidney function indicators comprising urea (UA, 44.42-224.77 µmol/L) and creatinine (CRE, 10.91–85.09 µmol/L); and (3) cardiac markers including creatine kinase (CK, 0-2070.55 U/L), creatine kinase MB isoenzyme (CK-MB, 0-1500 U/L), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH, 157.41-899.72 U/L).

Transcriptomic analysis of regenerated wound tissues

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent. After assessing RNA purity and integrity, poly(A)-containing mRNA was selectively captured, fragmented, and reverse‑transcribed into cDNA. Second‑strand synthesis was then carried out, and the RNA-DNA hybrid was converted into double‑stranded DNA. The ends were blunted with dUTP, and an A base was added to each end for T‑base ligation. The DNA was purified using magnetic beads. The second strand was digested with UDG enzyme, and PCR was performed to construct a library with a fragment size of 300 ± 50 bp. Sequencing was finally conducted on an Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform. Differential gene expression, Gene Ontology terms, and signaling pathway terms were analyzed.

q-PCR analysis of mouse skin tissues

Mouse wounds or skin tissues (approximately 30–50 mg) were collected, and total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol method. After determining the concentration and purity, total RNA (1 µg) was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit. The cDNA was then used as a template for q-PCR amplification with the SYBR Green method, and the relative expression levels of target genes were calculated. Long-term stability assessment

To evaluate the reusability potential of SMU-8@W and SMU-7@W, samples were subjected to immersion in PBS buffers at varying pH levels (5.5 and 7.4) for predetermined time intervals (3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 72, 96, 120, 144, and 168 h). Following each incubation period, the samples were retrieved, dried at 60 °C, and subsequently analyzed using fluorescence microscopy to assess their structural integrity and functional stability under different physiological conditions.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was determined using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with post hoc Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. The threshold for statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, with the following significance levels indicated: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001, **** P < 0.0001.
