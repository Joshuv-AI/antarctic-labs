1. Executive Summary

Today’s transportation vehicle customers demand manufacturers to produce engines with
lower weight and improved efficiency to reduce fuel consumption, emissions, and costs
associated with vehicle use, while maintaining or even increasing engine performance.
Developing the next generation of high-efficiency, very-low-emission internal-combustion
engines requires a combination of new combustion strategies, advanced materials, and new
materials-processing techniques to further increase engine efficiency and reduce weight. The
expected national economic, environmental, and energy-security benefits of these next-
generation engines and materials would be significant because most vehicles sold over the next
two decades will still include an internal combustion engine. The availability of advanced light-
weight engine designs will provide a significant positive impact on the fuel efficiency,
environmental impact, and competitiveness of the U.S. auto industry [1].

This collaborative multi-year advanced engine technology project summarized in the report is
to develop low mass and high efficiency medium-duty truck engine. The project was proposed
as a large-scale engine design and demonstration enabled by an advanced materials and
manufacturing development program, with a comprehensive plan spanning a period of four
years (two phases). The project began with engine architecture design and analysis activity,
advanced materials and manufacturing process development and down selection. It
culminated in an engine test cell evaluation with optimal materials and manufacturing solutions
supporting final vehicle simulation. The final engine tests verified engine weight reduction and
performance to the project objectives. [1] Figure 1 outlines the overall project strategy and
work streams.

Several combustion concepts/technologies were investigated and down selected, including
enhanced mixture motion and rapid combustion, increased compression ratio, combustion
charge dilution through cooled exhaust gas recirculation (EGR), and ultra-high pressure fuel
injection. High-strength and heat-resistant materials were developed and incorporated with
novel metal casting and additive manufacturing (AM) processes to produce highly durable
engine structures to maximize performance of the materials and systems with minimum mass
and cost. The opportunity to include advanced materials and manufacturing in conjunction with

high-potential combustion system and engine technologies also created the chance to revisit
the most appropriate engine specification to realize the desired efficiencies while reducing
engine weight cost-effectively. A point of focus was the most cost-effective solution to promote

Integrated computational materials engineering (ICME), [2][3] and computer-aided engineering

A new low-mass and high-efficiency (LMHE) medium-duty truck engine was developed in this
project to address the technological and societal need by offering an unprecedented
combination of lightweight and performance attributes that are superior to all known medium-
and heavy-duty engines. The LMHE V8 medium-duty truck engine, Figure 2, is powered with a
unique combination of several key features including 2-spark plugs per cylinder, ultra-high

combustion charge EGR dilution tolerance and mixture motion and rapid combustion, increased
compression ratio, and improved fuel economy by 10.44% compared with the L96 baseline

The advanced materials and manufacturing solutions have not only reduced engine weight but
also enhanced the engine performance and durability. The high temperature cast aluminum
alloys (ORNL’s ACMZ and GM’s High Q) have improved the head and block high temperature

2. Project Objective

The objective of this collaborative project between General Motors (GM), Oak Ridge National
Lab (ORNL), The Ohio State University (OSU), Michigan Technological University (MTU), and ECK
Industries Inc (ECK) is to develop a low mass and high efficiency (LMHE) medium duty truck
engine equipped with advanced materials and combustion technologies capable of ≥10% fuel
efficiency improvement and ≥15% engine weight reduction when compared to the 2015MY GM
L96 VORTEC 6.0L V8 engine compliant with applicable EPA (Environmental Protection Agency)
emission standards with performance demonstrated via simulation coupled with engine
dynamometer test.

3. Project Technical Scope Summary

This collaborative project was proposed as a large-scale engine design and demonstration enabled
by advanced combustion and materials technologies. The project was planned for four budget
periods (two phases) separated by distinct go/no-go decision points. The proposed categories are:
PHASE 1 – Technology Research and Development
Budget Period 1: Potential engine architecture designs and initial verification coupled with
advanced technologies of combustion system and materials and manufacturing concepts for weight-
saving and performance improvement through simulations and development
Budget Period 2: Evaluation of proposed individual technology concepts using current equivalent
architecture GM development engine(s) in conjunction with advanced materials and manufacturing
process development of high value weight reduction and performance improvement strategies for
components and sub-systems
PHASE 2 – Technology Validation and Demonstration
Budget Period 3: Design, procurement and manufacturing, assembly, and initial development
testing of proposed medium-duty truck engine capable of meeting requirements
Budget Period 4: Demonstration of performance and weight reduction to requirements using
engine test cell and vehicle simulation.
A total of three key go/no-go program decision points were dispersed throughout the four-year
duration. The first decision point (end of Budget Period 1) was achieved by using simulation and
evaluation of potential engine architecture and combustion and materials technology to determine
the primary engine architecture, and to select potential technologies for further work. Details about
the design concepts and computational simulation and evaluation are discussed in Section 4 –
Overview of LMHE Engine Combustion and Materials Development Concepts and Section 5 -
Advanced LMHE Engine Technology Development, respectively.
The second decision point (end of Budget Period 2) was achieved by assessing the outcome of proof-
of-concept testing of individual technologies and various technology combinations in hardware with
existing GM development engines. This enabled good correlation of simulation results with test

This decision point marked a key gate in determining the capability and confidence level of the
identified engine architecture and advanced technologies of combustion, materials, and
manufacturing solutions to meet the project objectives. The project technologies had demonstrated
the capability of meeting the objectives with an appropriate confidence level. As a result, the work
continued in the design, manufacture, procurement, and assembly of a new multi-cylinder engine to
be built and installed in dynamometer facilities for testing. Details about the proof-of-concept
testing and evaluation are discussed in Section 5 – Advanced LMHE Engine Technology
Development.
The third critical go/no-go decision point (end of Budget Period 3) was achieved with the promising
results of preliminary engine development testing incorporating the new materials and

4. Overview of LMHE Engine Combustion and Materials Development Concepts

In today’s medium and heavy-duty truck engines, application of advanced combustion and
materials technologies is lagging light duty engines. Some proven light duty engine
technologies have not been combined strategically and developed to meet the cost and
durability requirements of the medium duty truck market. Examples of legacy technology in
medium duty truck engines that could be greatly improved include PFI (port fuel injection), OHV
(overhead valve) pushrod 2 valve/cylinder combustion system, iron block, aluminum head, and
VVT (variable valve timing).

This collaborative project symbiotically integrated advanced combustion technologies with
innovative material and manufacturing process developments to assure that the higher thermal
loads and cylinder pressures associated with efficiency gains can be practically implemented
and leveraged by mass reduction. High temperature aluminum alloys and high strength cast
ferrous alloys, multi-materials design, advanced metal casting, overcasting, and additive
manufacturing technologies were developed and evaluated to produce highly durable engine
structures to maximize performance with minimum mass and cost.

4.1 LMHE Engine Advanced Combustion Development Concepts

Several stoichiometric combustion related technology categories were selected for
investigation and down-selection within the project including 1) basic combustion system
specification and layout, 2) cylinder deactivation strategies, 3) advanced fuel systems, 4)
advanced ignition systems, 5) advanced dilution systems, 6) advanced Atkinson or Miller cycle
valve event strategies, and 7) advanced variable induction system strategies. These candidate
technologies are collectively referred to as “advanced combustion technologies” in later
sections.

A large displacement, normally aspirated concept, and a small displacement with boosted
concepts were initially developed and evaluated. This evaluation was concluded with the
selection of the engine architecture to be optimized in support of meeting the project
objectives. The initial combustion system layout was driven by the performance and fuel
economy requirements of the selected engine architecture. These requirements had evolved
to specification of compression ratio, desired surface to volume ratio, dilution tolerance,
desired charge motion, spark plug location, DI fuel injector location and proximity to spark plug,
and intake port locations for advanced Port Fuel Injection (PFI) investigations. Cylinder
deactivation strategies are very effective at increasing fuel economy. In this project, the
benefits of specifying such a system were assessed relative to the desired medium duty truck
engine high usage operating region.

 Page 6

Advanced direct cylinder fuel injection (DI) systems interacted with the spark plug to create
turbulence and favorable mixture conditions at ignition. This project focused on “ultra” high-
pressure DI gasoline fuel injectors (>750 Bar) and the capability to execute late fuel injection
events creating extensive turbulence just prior to ignition. This strategy had developed the
potential to increase compression ratio by mitigating knock and stochastic pre-ignition
tendencies. The potential of high-pressure intake port fuel injection (PFI) was also investigated
with open valve injection to reduce particulate.

Use of advanced ignition systems to increase dilution tolerance in stoichiometric combustion
systems has been explored. Historically, only conventional inductive ignition systems have
achieved the desired average ignition energy, but this was neither efficient nor durable. In this
project, an advanced ignition system that allows the target ignition energy to be delivered with
a secondary current that resolves the historical issues was selected. This system allows proper
evaluation of the proposed advanced combustion technologies.

Intake charge dilution with EGR (exhaust gas recirculation) is a key enabler of improved
stoichiometric combustion system performance. However, this approach can cause ignition
delay and extended combustion durations which reduce efficiency and may cause poor
combustion stability. As noted, advanced ignition systems have shown to resolve some of the
ignitability challenges, including the presence of high charge motion. In addition, combustion
performance with EGR dilution can be improved by supplying EGR from a dedicated cylinder
operating with a rich air-fuel mixture. The exhaust constituents in this recirculated exhaust
have shown to significantly accelerate combustion speed as demonstrated by GM in the DOE
funded project “The Application of High Energy Ignition and Boosting/Mixing Technology to
Increase Fuel Economy in Spark Ignition Gasoline Engines by Increasing EGR Dilution Capability”
(DE-EE0005654).

Advanced Atkinson or Miller cycle valve event strategies were evaluated and applied in this
project to improve extended expansion ratio and engine efficiency. Traditionally, the expansion
ratio has been limited because it is geometrically linked to an engine’s knock limited
compression ratio. Late and early intake valve closing (LIVC and EIVC respectively) event
strategies have shown to effectively reduce the geometric compression ratio while maintaining
the efficiency benefits of the high geometric expansion ratio. Advanced LIVC or EIVC valve
event strategy was investigated and enhanced by the investigation of variable intake and
exhaust valve lift and duration capabilities. The use of intake and exhaust cam phasing in
conjunction with variable intake and exhaust valve lift and duration enables a large scale,
independent variation of the effective compression and exhaust events to support optimal
engine efficiency over a wide variety of engine operating points.

Advanced variable induction system strategies were applied to develop an appropriate
combustion speed for best efficiency and performance while simultaneously achieving
acceptable engine noise, vibration, and harshness (NVH). Often, it’s possible to achieve an
acceptable combustion speed at all operating points by careful detail design of the intake port
and combustion system to optimize charge motion. However, certain technology combinations

 Page 7

require a variable charge motion device. The induction system is also crucial to full load engine
operating performance. Some of the proposed technology solutions may have a negative
impact on full load performance. In this instance, using a variable induction system to alter the
effective length of the intake runners might be required to meet performance objectives. An
appropriate solution would be developed and implemented if such devices are required to
meet objectives.

4.2 LMHE Engine Advanced Materials and Manufacturing Development Concepts
Increasing engine power and efficiency requires more aggressive thermal cycles which push
cylinder pressure and temperature to the engine materials’ operational limits. Reducing engine
weight demands thinner wall design and lighter materials. These conflicting demands exceed
the current material properties of drivetrain parts such as the cylinder head and block, pistons,
and crankshaft. For cylinder heads, the more severe operating conditions require significant
improvements in high temperature fatigue and creep resistance, especially in thin-walled areas.
Simply improving high temperature tensile strength is not enough. Improvements in casting
defect populations as well as high cycle, low cycle, and thermomechanical fatigue resistance are
required. In aluminum castings, the fatigue strength can be increased 50~100% when the
defect sizes are reduced below a critical size (~50µm). [4-6] The size and population of
multiscale defects depend upon not only alloy composition and melt quality, but also upon the
casting process. Recently, there has been significant progress in developing high temperature
cast aluminum alloys, however, despite improved high temperature tensile strength, the
fatigue properties of the new alloys remain limited by the defects created by the traditional
casting processes. [7] Therefore, one of the key objectives in this project is to develop novel
casting processes to reduce and eliminate casting defects.

A significant mass reduction opportunity would be replacing the iron block with cast aluminum
(-113 lbs including the use of cast iron liners, -19% for the engine and -2% for the vehicle
assuming no changes to other parts). The key barriers to this are the lower stiffness of the cast
aluminum, and the inferior high temperature properties of common aluminum casting alloys
such as 319T7. These barriers can be overcome by local materials reinforcement strategies
such as adding local chills and overcasting a cast iron insert or metal matrix composite. Another
potential solution is to adapt the high temperature cast Al alloys and defect reduction
strategies developed for cylinder heads to the block. Hollow crankshafts offer another
opportunity to reduce mass. The crankshaft for the baseline engine was made of medium
grade nodular iron. To compensate for the reduced modulus in the single Al material block, high
stiffness crankshaft materials like cast steel or high-grade nodular iron can improve engine
rigidity and reduce NVH (noise, vibration, and harshness).

Cast Al alloys to meet the high temperature and high strength requirements associated with the
improved combustion efficiency need to be developed and applied for cylinder head, engine
block and piston in this project. ORNL team recently developed a new aluminum Al-Cu alloy
family with microalloying element additions of Manganese and Zirconium, termed ACMZ
(AlCuMnZr) alloys through previous DOE project. ACMZ alloys were developed to address the

 Page 8

challenging fabrication and performance requirements of an automotive cylinder head in
present and future higher efficiency engines. GM has also developed a cast aluminum Q alloy
through DOE project (DE-EE0006082) for cylinder head applications. Both alloys provide a
nearly 50-100°C advantage in tensile properties compared to existing cast automotive
aluminum alloys. In addition, the compositions of ACMZ variants and GM Q (Al-Si-Cu-Mg)
based alloys are further optimized and applied in cast aluminum engine block applications.

High strength and high modulus cast iron and steel would be evaluated for the crankshaft which
is the second heaviest component in the V8 engine. A new nodular iron crankshaft with 10-15%
higher Young’s modulus and 30-50% increase in UTS (ultimate tensile strength) over the
baseline is envisioned. These improvements would result from optimization of nodular size
(type 6~7), nodular count (>200/mm2), and matrix strength (>95% pearlite). GM has developed
a new cast steel alloy for the crankshaft which would be further optimized and tested in this
project. [8] The outcome of the work would be an optimum engineering solution for the
crankshaft based on both performance and cost.

Advanced manufacturing processes are developed and applied in the project to produce high
quality key engine components such as head, block, piston, and crankshaft with minimum
defects. Metal casting is one of the most cost-effective manufacturing processes for complex
engine components. In this project, novel casting processes like pressure-assisted precision
sand casting processes (PAPSC [9]) are implemented for producing high quality Al blocks and
heads with maximized mechanical properties. Advanced additive manufacturing processes are
developed to produce special high-performance parts like pistons made from aluminum or high
entropy alloys. Additionally, other new manufacturing technologies are explored including 3D
printing to produce intricate sand cores, overcast cylinder head valve seat. A focus is on the
most cost-effective solution to promote wide market acceptance. Furthermore, integrated
computational materials engineering (ICME) and advanced structural durability analysis tools
are used throughout the program to accelerate both the new alloy and manufacturing process
development and optimization, as well as the complete engine design and manufacturing plan.

A study of raising the camshaft bore on durability of the cast-aluminum block was also conducted. It
was found that the safety factors of the block were improved by 10%, particularly for hot-spot areas

Developing a high-strength, high-modulus, castable, cost-effective quality ductile iron for a
lightweight crankshaft application in a medium-duty combustion engine began at MTU. A
comprehensive literature search was conducted to understand the influencing factors on the elastic
modulus of ductile iron. Empirical data in the literature show that the elastic modulus decreases
significantly with increasing graphite fraction and with decreasing nodularity. Therefore, one
potential strategy for increasing the elastic modulus is to reduce the amount of graphite in the
microstructure by reducing carbon; however, castability (i.e., fluidity/mold filling, shrinkage
tendency, and hot tearing susceptibility) has been shown to degrade with decreasing carbon
content. Because the matrix phase—usually pearlite, ferrite, or some combination of both—makes
up much of the microstructure by volume, it is desirable to find an alloying scheme to increase the
matrix elastic modulus, thereby reducing the extent to which carbon content must be reduced to
achieve the target elastic modulus. The data available in the literature also indicate that cobalt is the
most-promising element for increasing the elastic modulus of ferrite when practicality (e.g., cost) is
considered. Silicon has been shown to significantly decrease the elastic modulus of ferrite, so, given
its relatively high concentration in ductile iron, it is desirable to evaluate the viability of reducing its
concentration. The available published data are for binary iron-based solid solutions, however, so
the combined effect of cobalt and silicon additions on the elastic modulus of iron needs to be
studied to isolate the solid solution effects from graphite effects, as well as in ductile iron alloys to
understand their overall effects. To search for additional potential alloying schemes to increase the
ductile-iron-matrix elastic modulus, first-principles calculations (i.e., density functional theory) was
employed to simulate the combined effects of various unlike solute elements on the elastic modulus
of iron.

7. LMHE Engine Technology Demonstration

7.1 Test Cell Fuel Performance Evaluation of Proposed Medium Duty Truck Solution
The combustion and materials teams worked in parallel to build PHASE 2 engines that could
evaluate the fuel economy improvement performance and the durability performance of the mass
reduction activities. These were referred to internally as the “Fuel Economy” (FE) engine, built and
tested by the combustion team, and the “Durability” (DE) engine, built and tested by the materials
engineering team; FE and DE respectively. This allowed each team to focus their test plans on their
specific aspect of the project deliverables, and not lose program timing troubleshooting test cell or
part performance issues that directly relate to their deliverables. The PHASE 2 engine shown in
Figure 65 was designed and built to demonstrate fuel economy performance. The PHASE 2 FE
engine contains the following key features: (1) two spark plugs/cylinder with supporting ignition
system; (2) ultra-high-pressure direct inject fuel system design integrated with cylinder head; (3)
active charge motion control valve integrated into intake assembly; (4) high-pressure exhaust gas
recirculation system; (5) cast aluminum (Al) block; (6) fabricated tubular exhaust manifolds; (7)
plastic rocker covers and valley tray; and (8) split cooling with valve bridge drilling, see Figure 66.
The PHASE 2 FE performance engine was tested with the procedure like PHASE 1 process.
