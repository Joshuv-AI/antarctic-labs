In Vitro Bactericidal Activity of Propamidine 0.1% and Other Ophthalmic Antiseptics on Conjunctivitis and Blepharitis Strains
Authors: Adrien Paschier, Laure Chauchat, Hayette Rebika, Marwan Sahyoun, Camille Guerin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe aim of this study was to assess the in vitro antimicrobial activity of propamidine isethionate 0.1% ophthalmic solution and other topical antiseptics, including hexamidine di-isethionate 0.1%, cethexonium bromide 0.025%, and picloxydine hydrochloride 0.05%.MethodsAntibacterial activity was tested in vitro on five Gram-positive bacterial strains commonly found in conjunctivitis and/or blepharitis. Test products were applied at a concentration of 80% at 20 °C (in accordance with NF EN 1040) for contact periods of 2, 5, 10, and 15 min. After neutralization, surviving bacteria were cultured, and colony forming units (CFU) were counted. Antimicrobial efficacy was expressed as log reduction in CFU. Bactericidal activity was defined as a reduction > 5 log.ResultsAll tested antiseptics demonstrated antibacterial activity with variability in magnitude of their effects depending on the bacterial strain and contact time. Propamidine 0.1% showed rapid activity, achieving maximal log reduction within 2 min against Staphylococcus epidermidis, Streptococcus pneumoniae, and Propionibacterium acnes. Cethexonium bromide 0.025% exhibited the highest antibacterial activity, with bactericidal effects observed against Corynebacterium striatum, S. pneumoniae, and P. acnes; hexamidine 0.1% and picloxydine 0.05% showed more limited or inconsistent bactericidal activity.ConclusionAll tested antiseptics demonstrated measurable in vitro antibacterial activity against common pathogens associated with conjunctivitis and blepharitis. The observed variability in antimicrobial magnitude highlight the influence of bacterial strain and exposure conditions on antiseptic performance. These results support the potential clinical interest of ocular antiseptics, including propamidine 0.1% in the management of mild bacterial conjunctivitis and blepharitis, although confirmation under clinical conditions remains necessary.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40123-026-01416-y.

Introduction

Bacterial conjunctivitis is a common ocular infectious disease in adults and children, generally mild but with increasing direct and indirect costs [2–4]. The clinical presentation is often nonspecific, making it difficult to distinguish bacterial from viral or allergic conjunctivitis based solely on clinical signs and symptoms [5, 6]. Because the tests are not performed systematically and culture results are typically delayed for several days, treatment is often initiated empirically [7]. Clinically, bacterial conjunctivitis present with redness, purulent discharge, and glued eyelids [8].

Blepharitis has a poorly understood, often multifactorial etiology [9]. Although Demodex infestation is recognized as a potential factor, affecting roughly 30% of chronic cases, most cases are associated with bacterial colonization and infection [4].

In adults, the most common bacterial pathogens causing conjunctivitis are Staphylococcus epidermidis (39%), Staphylococcus aureus (22%), and Streptococcus pneumoniae (6%). In blepharitis, the most common pathogens include Corynebacterium sp., S. epidermidis, S. aureus, and Propionibacterium acnes [9, 10]. Bacterial conjunctivitis and blepharitis are self-limited in most of cases and typically resolve without antibiotic treatment within 1–2 weeks [2, 11]. However, clinicians often prescribe broad-spectrum topical antibiotics despite uncertain etiology. This approach includes faster recovery, prevents severe disease, reduces re-infection and bacterial spread, and allows an earlier return to work or school, benefiting healthcare resources [12, 13]. On the other hand, the overuse of topical antibiotics can lead to multiple adverse consequences, including side effects, the emergence of multidrug-resistant bacterial strains, and disruption of the normal protective microbiota of the ocular surface [14–18].

Given the growing public health concern over bacterial resistance [17], topical antiseptics represent a valuable alternative between no active treatment (such as saline irrigation) and antibiotic use. Antiseptics are generally well tolerated and exhibit minimal risk of resistance. This approach allows antibiotics to be reserved for severe cases or immunosuppressed patients, thereby helping to limit the development of bacterial resistance [19].

Various ophthalmic antiseptics are available and currently recommended for the management of bacterial conjunctivitis or blepharitis in the absence of severity criteria [20]. Propamidine isethionate 0.1% (Brolène®) is a diamidine antiseptic that has been registered and marketed in the UK and in other countries such as Ireland, New Zealand, and Australia for several decades. It is a hydrosoluble cationic compound exhibiting broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against bacteria, fungi, yeast, and free-living amoebae [21]. It is indicated for the treatment of local infections of the superficial structures of the eye caused by susceptible microorganisms and is well known in France for its compassionate use in the management of acanthamoeba keratitis. Ocular antiseptics are generally assessed according to pharmacopeial standards on a broad panel of microorganism strains that may not always reflect the specific pathogens involved in ocular surface infections. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate in vitro the antibacterial activity of propamidine 0.1% and other ophthalmic antiseptic solutions (hexamidine 0.1%, cethexonium bromide 0.025%, and picloxydine 0.05%).

Methods

The entire methodology was performed in accordance with the European Standards (NF EN 1040, 2006 [1]) and is provided in supplementary materials. This standard is designed to assess the intrinsic bactericidal activity of antiseptic products under controlled in vitro conditions. It provides a harmonized experimental framework, allowing for the evaluation of antimicrobial activity under defined parameters such as contact time and inoculum.

Test Products

The antiseptic formulations tested were propamidine isethionate 0.1% (Kaogram®, a generic of Brolène®, Horus Pharma), hexamidine di-isethionate 0.1% (Désomédine®, Bausch and Lomb), cethexonium bromide 0.025% (Monosept®, Horus Pharma), and picloxydine hydrochloride 0.05% (Vitabact®, Théa). The concentration of the tested products during the in vitro assay was 80% (in accordance with European standards NF EN 1040, 2006 [1]), diluted with bacterial suspension (10%) and sterile purified water (10%). These solutions were prepared extemporaneously and used within 2 h.

Bacterial Strains

The bactericidal activity of each product was tested on five gram-positive bacterial strains (Institut Pasteur and LMG Bacteria collection). S. aureus (ATCC 6538, CIP 4.83), S. epidermidis (ATCC 12228, CIP 68.21), Corynebacterium striatum (ATCC BAA-1293), S. pneumoniae (ATCC 33400, CIP 102911T), and P. acnes (ATCC 6919, CYP 53.117T).

S. aureus, S. epidermidis, and C. striatum were maintained in tryptocasein soy agar (TSA), S. pneumoniae in Columbia agar + 5% sheep blood, and P. acnes in trypsocasein soy agar. For each test organism, two different suspensions were prepared: a “test suspension” (between 1.5 × 108 and 5 × 108 colony forming units [CFU]/mL) and a “validation suspension” (between 3.0 × 102 and 1.6 × 103 CFU/mL) to perform the controls and method validation.

This study does not involve any new experiments with human participants or animals.

In Vitro Bactericidal Activity Test

The bactericidal/bacteriostatic activity of antiseptics was determined following the European standard method [1], and the validity of the results was ensured through appropriate control performance (Laboratoire Icare, Saint-Bauzire, France). Neutralization and/or removal of bactericidal activity was verified and validated for each antiseptic, bacterial strain, and experimental condition (temperature and contact time). All control and validation procedures were performed in parallel with the tests, using the same neutralizing agents and conditions.

Bactericidal activity was defined as a ≥ 5 log reduction in bacterial count, in accordance with NF EN 1040 [1]. Reductions below this threshold were considered indicative of antibacterial activity but not bactericidal activity. Results reported as below the limit of quantification or above the upper limit of quantification were not considered fully quantifiable.

Bacterial suspensions were mixed with the test product and incubated at 20 °C (In accordance with European standards NF EN 1040, 2006 [1]) for 2, 5, 10, and 15 min. After incubation, samples were neutralized or filtered through membranes and plated on agar. Three controls validated the experimental conditions (control A), the neutralizer (control B), and the dilution-neutralization process (control C) (details provided in the supplementary material).

Plates were incubated for 48 h, and colony counts were converted to log CFU/mL to calculate log reductions. Only counts within defined ranges were considered valid, ensuring reliable measurement of antimicrobial activity. Calculations were performed only on plates containing 14–330 colonies, or 14–165 colonies for the membrane filtration method, in accordance with accepted counting ranges. Counts exceeding these ranges were considered non-quantifiable and were reported as greater than the highest countable value (e.g., > X.X × 10y), corresponding to a log reduction lower than X. Conversely, counts below 14 colonies were considered below the limit of quantification and were reported as less than the lowest countable value (e.g., < X.X × 10y), corresponding to a log reduction greater than X.

Propamidine 0.1%

Propamidine 0.1% exhibited antibacterial activity against all tested microorganisms (Fig. 1).

Log reductions ranged from 4.2 to > 5.2 depending on the strain and contact time. Reductions exceeding 5 log were observed for S. pneumoniae after 2 min. For conditions where counts exceeded the limit of quantification (e.g., > 5.2 against S. pneumoniae after 2 min), the exact log reductions could not be precisely quantified (Table S1).

Hexamidine 0.1%

Hexamidine 0.1% showed limited antibacterial activity across all tested strains (Fig. 1), with log reductions ranging from < 4.0 to < 4.3 at all contact times. No reductions exceeding 5 log were observed. For the values below the limit of quantification, the reductions could not be precisely evaluated (Table S2).

Cethexonium Bromide 0.025%

Cethexonium bromide 0.025% showed antibacterial activity against all tested microorganisms (Fig. 1), with log reductions ranging from 4.8 to > 5.4 depending on the strain and contact time. Reductions exceeding 5 log were observed for C. striatum, S. pneumoniae, and P. acnes at various contact times. For several conditions, reductions exceeded the quantification limit and were therefore not precisely quantified (Table S3).

Picloxydine 0.05%

Picloxydine 0.05% exhibited variable antibacterial activity depending on the strain (Fig. 1). Log reductions ranged from < 3.9 to 5.0. A reduction of 5.0 log was observed for S. epidermidis after 2 min. For several conditions, values were below the limit of quantification preventing precise evaluation of the reductions (Table S4).

Discussion

The ophthalmic antiseptics tested in this study are commercially available formulations indicated worldwide for the treatment of minor eye infections in adults and children, including mild bacterial conjunctivitis of unknown etiology. Currently, there is no specific preference or recommendation favoring one antiseptic over another, as they exhibit different mechanisms of action [21]. Propamidine 0.1% has been used for several decades in the UK for the treatment of mild acute conjunctivitis and blepharitis [22]. However, no previous published studies have evaluated its basic antimicrobial activity against common ocular bacterial strains in comparison with other antiseptics, including biguanides (picloxydine), quaternary ammoniums compounds (cethexonium bromide), and diamidines (hexamidine).

This study highlights variability in antibacterial activity between antiseptics, depending on the bacterial strain. Nevertheless, propamidine and all tested products demonstrated measurable in vitro antibacterial activity against the five bacterial strains commonly associated with conjunctivitis and blepharitis after contact times ranging from 2 to 15 min. These findings suggest that antimicrobial performance is influenced by exposure conditions and bacterial susceptibility, highlighting the importance of considering these parameters when interpreting in vitro efficacy.

Propamidine 0.1% exhibits an antimicrobial effect against all tested microorganisms, with maximal log reductions ranging from 4.4 to > 5.2. Bactericidal activity was achieved against S. pneumoniae with a reduction exceeding 5 log units. A log reduction of approximately 3.9 was observed after only 2 min, indicating a reduction of 99.99% of the microorganisms. This contact time aligns with the expected pharmacokinetics of ophthalmic solutions, as most topical agents are cleared from the ocular surface within 2–3 min of instillation [23]. Cethexonium bromide achieved reductions exceeding 5 log units for several strains (C. striatum, S. pneumoniae, and P. acnes), confirming its bactericidal activity under the tested conditions. In contrast, hexamidine 0.1% did not achieve reductions ≥ 5 log under the tested conditions, suggesting the absence of bactericidal activity according to NF EN 1040. On another side, picloxydine reached a 5-log reduction against S. epidermidis, indicating bactericidal activity under specific conditions, although this effect was not consistent across all tested strains. In clinical practice, ocular antiseptics are a viable alternative to antibiotics, because of their nonselective mechanism of action, and relatively limited bacterial resistance. They have been proposed for use in the treatment of superficial corneal-conjunctival infections and for preoperative and postoperative eye infection prophylaxis [24–26]. According to the recommendations of the French National Agency for Medicines and Health Products Safety [19] and the French Ophthalmology Society [27], the use of antiseptics in primary care is encouraged in the management of superficial ocular infections such as conjunctivitis or blepharitis. Therefore, antiseptics such as propamidine 0.1% eyedrops represent a valuable therapeutic option, either as monotherapy or as an adjunct, to ensure clinical recovery while reducing the risk of antimicrobial resistance [28]. Avoiding antibiotic use for mild ocular infections is consistent with antibiotic stewardship principles, addressing the global health challenge of bacterial resistance [29]. Overuse and misuse of topical antibiotics in ophthalmology contribute not only to resistance but also to unnecessary healthcare costs. Moreover, a recent meta-analysis of randomized controlled clinical trials confirmed that topical antibiotics had a modest clinical benefit for the resolution of bacterial conjunctivitis compared to a placebo [30].

Many mild ocular infections are self-limiting and can resolve spontaneously [31]. During this period, antiseptics can play a crucial role in reducing microbial load and preventing infection progression or transmission. Broad-spectrum antiseptics offer an additional advantage by targeting a wide range of pathogens, including bacteria, viruses, and fungi, reinforcing their relevance in ocular surface infection management. However, antiseptics may exhibit concentration-dependent toxicity, particularly at higher concentrations. In clinical practice, antiseptics are generally well tolerated owing to their low-dose formulations and short exposure times [32]. Indeed, corneal toxicity has been reported with higher concentrations of chlorhexidine, whereas diamidines show only limited toxicity under short-term exposure conditions [33]. In contrast, topical antibiotics are also associated with ocular adverse effects and may promote the emergence of bacterial resistance, reinforcing the need for alternative approaches [34].

The current study has several limitations. Gram-negative bacteria such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Moraxella catarrhalis, and Haemophilus influenzae were not evaluated. P. aeruginosa and M. catarrhalis are rarely isolated in conjunctival infections [35, 36] and are more commonly associated with contact lens-related bacterial keratitis [37]. H. influenzae predominantly affects the pediatric population, which explains its exclusion from the study. Furthermore, this species is notoriously difficult to culture and requires one to perform certain laboratory assays [38].

In addition, experiments were performed under standardized conditions in accordance with NF EN 1040, a recognized European standard for evaluating the intrinsic antibacterial activity of antiseptics. This method is designed as a controlled, single-condition testing rather than on repeated independent experiments or statistical analysis. While duplicate plating was used to ensure the reliability of bacterial enumeration, the absence of repeated independent experiments may limit the assessment of variability and reproducibility.

However, this approach is consistent with the design of the standard, which ensures reproducibility through strict methodological standardization. Within this framework, antibacterial activity was assessed at multiple contact times, providing an in vitro overview of antibacterial potential under defined conditions. The results should be interpreted as reflecting intrinsic antibacterial activity rather than clinical performance. Nevertheless, this approach is based on in vitro conditions that do not fully replicate the complexity of clinical use. In particular, as the method does not rely on repeated independent experiments, it does not allow for a detailed assessment of variability or statistical robustness. Accordingly, this study is intended to characterize intrinsic antibacterial activity under standardized conditions rather than to establish precise comparative performance between products.

Moreover, the products were tested at a concentration of 80%, as defined by the standardized methodology. Although this predefined dilution ensures methodological consistency and facilitates comparison across studies, it may not fully reflect real-life clinical conditions, where antiseptics are generally used in their undiluted form.

According to NF EN 1040 standard, a ≥ 5-log reduction is typically required to demonstrate bactericidal activity. However, in several cases, reductions were reported as exceeding the upper limit of quantification, preventing precise determination of the actual log reduction. Conversely, results expressed as “< values” indicate that reductions could not be accurately quantified. These methodological limitations should be considered when interpreting the antibacterial performance of the tested antiseptics.

Despite these limitations, the bacterial species included in this study represent the predominant pathogens involved in adult bacterial conjunctivitis and blepharitis, ensuring the relevance and applicability of our findings to routine clinical practice.

Another limitation is the use of collection of bacterial strains rather than clinical isolates. Further investigations are needed to confirm the efficacy of propamidine 0.1% against recent bacterial isolates, including both susceptible and resistant strains. Previous studies have demonstrated that diamidines such as hexamidine di-isethionate possess antibacterial activity against resistant clinical isolates [17, 36, 39]. Similarly Budzinskaya [40] reported that in vitro incubation with picloxydine for 15 min resulted in complete eradication of both susceptible and antibiotic-resistant Staphylococcus spp. and P. aeruginosa isolates. However, to date, no studies have specifically evaluated the antimicrobial activity of propamidine against the most common bacterial species responsible for conjunctivitis and blepharitis. This study therefore provides a valuable foundation for future investigations exploring other antiseptic agents or additional bacterial strains and expanded antimicrobial testing conditions.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the in vitro antibacterial activity of four different commercially available antiseptics (including propamidine 0.1%) after contact times ranging from 2 to 15 min against five bacterial strains commonly associated with conjunctivitis and blepharitis. The results highlight variability in the magnitude of antibacterial activity depending on the bacterial strain and contact time, indicating that antimicrobial performance is influenced by exposure conditions and bacterial susceptibility.

Within the standardized framework of this study, the objective was to provide an overall in vitro assessment of intrinsic antibacterial activity rather than a direct head-to-head comparison of clinical efficacy between products. While cethexonium bromide appeared to exhibit a greater bactericidal activity under certain conditions, all tested antiseptics demonstrated measurable antibacterial effects. These results support the potential clinical interest in using antiseptics such as propamidine 0.1% for the management of mild ocular surface infections, particularly in the current context of antibiotic stewardship, where limiting unnecessary antibiotic use and promoting antiseptic alternatives are strongly encouraged. Further research involving replicates and a broader range of bacterial strains is warranted to better characterize the full antimicrobial spectrum and confirm these findings under conditions more closely reflecting clinical use. Moreover, assessing antimicrobial activity using undiluted formulations (100% concentration) would help better approximate clinical exposure and address the limitations associated with dilution in the current experimental design.
