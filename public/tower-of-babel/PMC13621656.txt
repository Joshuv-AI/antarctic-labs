Argon plasma coagulation for endoscopic treatment for congenital pyriform sinus fistula in children
Authors: Yanyan Wang, Youtao Chen, Hailin Wu, Hong Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of a novel endoscopic approach using argon plasma coagulation (APC) for the treatment of congenital pyriform sinus fistulas (CPSF) in children.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 30 pediatric patients with PSF who were treated at Fujian Children’s Hospital (Fujian Branch of Shanghai Children’s Medical Center) between 2021 and 2025. All patients underwent neck color Doppler ultrasound and electronic laryngoscopy, with systematically analyzed of the results. Postoperative follow-up was performed at multiple time points.ResultsAll 30 procedures were successfully completed, with no cases of pharyngeal fistula, dysphagia, or infection around or beyond the fistula tract. During the follow-up period, ranging from 2 weeks to 3 years, no recurrence was observed.ConclusionEndoscopy-assisted argon plasma coagulation is a promising treatment for CPSF in children, offering advantages including minimal trauma, reduced bleeding, and clear visualization. This method appears safe and effective for infants and children with CPSF and warrants wider clinical adoption.Keywords: Argon plasma coagulation, pyriform sinus fistula, Endoscopic treatment, children

Congenital pyriform sinus fistula (CPSF) is a rare branchial arch-derived malformation of the neck, encompassing both third and fourth branchial cleft anomalies, and accounts for approximately 1%–10% of all branchiogenic malformations [1]. Approximately 80% of patients with CPSF present during childhood [2]. Unlike pediatric patients, who often experience recurrent neck abscesses and thyroiditis, neonatal CPSF typically manifests as a congenital cystic neck mass. This mass may compress the airway, leading to dyspnea, respiratory failure, and even life-threatening conditions [3, 4]. Therefore, when CPSF first appears in infancy, it is frequently misdiagnosed as lymphadenitis or other disorders, which can delay appropriate treatment.

Currently, there is no unified standard for the surgical management of CPSF in China. Treatment options primarily include open neck surgery and endoscopic surgery. In recent years, with advances in minimally invasive techniques, endoscopic cauterization of the internal fistula opening in the pyriform sinus has gained attention due to its minimal trauma and high safety profile, and its therapeutic efficacy has been widely recognized [5–7]. Between November 2021 and June 2025, a total of 30 patients with CPSF were treated using endoscopic argon plasma coagulation at Fujian Children’s Hospital (Fujian Branch of Shanghai Children’s Medical Center), with satisfactory outcomes. This article presents a retrospective analysis of the diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis of these cases, aiming to provide a reference for clinical practice.

Materials and methods

General information

In this study, we retrospectively reviewed the medical records of patients with CPSF who underwent endoscopic coblation between January 2021 and December 2025. Data on demographics, clinical features, diagnostic methods, treatment details, and outcomes were collected and evaluated. Inclusion criteria were : (1) Patients younger than 16 years old; (2) Clinically diagnosed with CPSF, without obvious local infectious symptoms; and (3) Routine preoperative examinations showing no contraindications to surgery. Exclusion criteria were: (1) patients with incomplete basic clinical data; (2) children in whom the fistula opening could not be identified; and (3) patients with CPSF presenting with an external cervical fistula requiring open surgical resection, as such cases are not suitable for simple endoscopic ablation of the internal opening under suspension laryngoscopy.

In the present study, clinical presentations were classified into three categories based on physical examination and imaging findings. A neck abscess was defined as a fluctuant mass with signs of inflammation, confirmed by imaging (ultrasound or CT) demonstrating a fluid collection without thyroid involvement. Acute suppurative thyroiditis (AST) or thyroid abscess was defined as acute suppurative thyroiditis with thyroid gland involvement on imaging, often accompanied by elevated inflammatory markers. A neck mass was defined as a palpable neck swelling without acute inflammatory signs, confirmed by imaging as a non‑suppurative lesion (e.g., congenital cyst or solid mass).

Surgical methods

Patients were placed in the supine position under general anesthesia, with tracheal intubation maintained throughout the procedure. A gastroscope was introduced orally to visualize the glottis and vocal cords. The fistula opening in the affected pyriform sinus, the Betz mucosal fold (a longitudinal mucosal fold between the medial wall of the pyriform sinus and the esophageal inlet), and the esophageal inlet were clearly identified under endoscopic view.

An assistant applied external pressure to the corresponding thyroid cartilage plate to fully expose the pyriform sinus fistula opening. Upon neck compression, a small amount of secretion was typically observed emanating from the fistula. An argon plasma coagulation (APC) probe was then advanced through the working channel of the endoscope. The tip of the probe was maintained approximately 3–5 mm from the mucosal surface. Coagulation was performed using a footswitch, with each application lasting 1–3 s. The APC settings were standardized at an argon gas flow rate of 0.8–1.0 L/min and a power output of 30 W.

The number of coagulation applications was adjusted according to the size and consistency of the lesion. During treatment, the targeted tissue underwent progressive desiccation and necrosis, resulting in uniform superficial coagulation and whitening of the surface. Repeat neck compression after ablation confirmed the absence of significant fluid leakage from the sealed fistula.

During the procedure, careful attention was paid to the direction of the APC probe. The tip was gently angled toward the anterolateral aspect of the thyroid cartilage plate to minimize risk to the medial and inferior recurrent laryngeal nerves, as well as the superior laryngeal nerve. Hemostasis was achieved using the APC probe, and the wound was inspected for any residual oozing.

Postoperatively, a nasogastric tube was routinely placed and maintained for one week. Prophylactic antibiotics were administered to prevent infection.

Follow-up time after surgery

All patients underwent scheduled follow-up assessments at 2 weeks, 1 month, 2 months, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 2 years, and 3 years postoperatively. Each follow-up included a review of medical history, evaluation of growth and development, neck color Doppler ultrasound, and endoscopic examination.

Criteria for efficacy evaluation

Cure was defined by the fulfillment of both following criteria: (1) absence of symptoms including odynophagia or pain/swelling in the ipsilateral anterior lower neck region throughout the postoperative follow-up period; and (2) confirmation via follow-up examinations (ultrasound, CT, MRI, or endoscopy) at 3 months postoperatively, demonstrating scar tissue formation and complete closure of the pyriform sinus fistula opening.

Recurrence was defined as the reappearance of any aforementioned clinical symptoms during follow-up, or the identification of a persistent fistula opening upon re-examination.

Results

Demographics and clinical features

A total of 30 pediatric patients with surgically confirmed CPSF were included in this study. The cohort consisted of 16 males (53.3%) and 14 females (46.7%) (Table 1). The median age at disease onset was 3 years (range: 1 day to 12 years), and the median disease duration of 1 month (range: 1 day to 4 months).

Patients’ characteristics of total population

The majority of fistulas were left-sided (28/30, 93.3%), with one right-sided case (3.3%) and one bilateral (3.3%). According to the clinical classification, the initial presentations were as follows: neck abscess in 17 patients (56.7%), AST/thyroid abscess in 2 patients (6.6%), and neck mass in 11 patients (36.7%). Among these, one patient experienced respiratory difficulty due to a large neck abscess. Additionally, one child presented initially with fever and cough, without specific neck symptoms.

Prior to definitive surgery, 11 patients (36.7%) underwent incision and drainage for neck abscesses. One of these patients required the procedure three times. All incision sites had healed with scar formation by the time of the endoscopic procedure.

Diagnostic methods

All children presented with varying degrees of lateral neck swelling at initial diagnosis (Fig. 1a), necessitating differentiation from conditions such as infected congenital dermoid cysts, second branchial cleft fistulas, thyroglossal duct cysts, cervical lymph node tuberculosis, and infected congenital vascular malformations.

When clinically feasible, preoperative workup was completed as comprehensively as possible and included neck color Doppler ultrasound, CT, and MRI. If the diagnosis remained uncertain, a contrast study was performed. Among the 30 patients, MRI confirmed CPSF in 12 cases, CT in 11 cases, and neck color Doppler ultrasound in 6 cases; one patient underwent a contrast study for diagnosis.

For patients in the typical inflammatory phase, neck color Doppler ultrasound (Fig. 1b) typically demonstrated a fistula tract (appearing as a mixed-echogenicity area) extending superiorly toward the pyriform sinus and inferiorly involving the thyroid gland. Endoscopic examination during inflammation (Fig. 1c) revealed mucosal edema in the pyriform sinus region, occasionally with a visible small fistula opening; compression of the neck mass often elicited discharge of milky-white secretions from the opening. Cervical MRI during the inflammatory phase (Fig. 1d) showed a cystic-solid neck mass with associated distortion of the pyriform sinus.

Definitive diagnosis of pyriform sinus fistula was established by direct visualization of the fistula opening, located at the apex or base of the pyriform sinus, during endoscopic examination (such as esophagoscopy or suspension laryngoscopy) under general anesthesia.

Treatments and outcomes

All 30 pediatric patients received anti-infective therapy during the acute inflammatory phase. Among them, 11 underwent abscess incision and drainage, with antibiotic therapy subsequently adjusted according to bacterial culture results or continued empirically. Following approximately one week of systemic anti-infective treatment and local wound management, inflammation was effectively controlled in all cases. Clinical signs, including neck swelling and tenderness, resolved, and incision sites healed without exudation.

Subsequently, endoscopic argon plasma coagulation (APC) was performed. The procedure was successfully completed in a single session for 25 patients, while 5 required a second intervention. Postoperative recovery was uneventful in all cases, with no major bleeding, neck swelling, or pain reported. No complications such as pharyngeal fistula, dysphagia, or infection along the fistula tract were observed.

During a follow-up period ranging from 1 to 36 months (including 6 patients > 2 years, 2 patients 1 year, 6 patients 6 months, 9 patients 3 months, and 7 patients 1 month), none of the 30 patients experienced recurrence. Follow-up assessments revealed no neck pain, abscess formation, or dysphagia. Discussion

Congenital pyriform sinus fistula (CPSF) is a rare anomaly resulting from the failed involution of the third and fourth brachial clefts. Studies reporting large patient series remain scarce in the literature [1, 8]. The condition shows no significant gender predilection, with approximately 90% of cases occurring on the left side [1]. This left-sided predominance is hypothesized to be related to asymmetric development of the branchial apparatus [9]. In our cohort of 30 patients, 28 (93.3%) had left-sided CPSF, and the male-to-female ratio was 1:1.14, consistent with previous reports.

CPSF can be anatomically classified into sinus tracts, fistulas, and cysts, based on the presence of internal and/or external openings. The sinus tract type, characterized by an internal opening at the base of the pyriform sinus, is the most common. The fistula type, often secondary to infection, abscess rupture, or iatrogenic procedures, typically presents with an external opening along the anterior border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle. The cyst type is more frequently observed in fetuses or newborns [10]. All 30 cases in this series were of the sinus tract type. The primary clinical manifestation was neck abscess formation, which in severe cases could lead to dyspnea or asphyxia. Systemic symptoms such as high fever or bacteremia were occasionally observed. Given the wide spectrum of clinical presentations—from localized neck pain to life-threatening conditions—accurate diagnosis and prompt management are crucial. In children presenting with high fever or respiratory distress, immediate incision and drainage of the neck abscess is indicated, followed by culture-directed antibiotic therapy.

The non-specific clinical presentation of CPSF necessitates differentiation from other congenital neck lesions. Initial diagnostic workup typically involves neck imaging, such as ultrasound, CT, or MRI. In typical cases, CT may demonstrate a cord-like or tubular lesion extending from the pyriform sinus to the thyroid, potentially showing rim enhancement. CT provides detailed anatomical information regarding the fistula’s course and its relation to adjacent structures and can guide surgical planning via 3D reconstruction, making it a valuable tool [11, 12]. However, its diagnostic yield varies widely (11%–85.7%) and is operator-dependent [13]. Limitations include radiation exposure and the frequent need for sedation in children. Ultrasound offers a non-invasive, real-time, and reproducible alternative and is recommended as a first-line imaging modality for suspected CPSF [14]. It can identify hypoechoic tubular structures connecting to the subcutaneous tissues, with reported diagnostic sensitivity ranging from 75% to 87.5% [15]. Similar to CT, its accuracy is highly experience-dependent. MRI, while avoiding radiation, is limited by longer scan times, higher cost, and the need for patient cooperation or sedation. Although hypopharyngeal contrast radiography can directly visualize the fistula tract and pyriform sinus morphology, its utility in uncooperative infants and young children is limited.

In our series, only one patient underwent contrast radiography. Endoscopic examination is more feasible in this age group. During the inflammatory phase, significant mucosal edema of the affected pyriform sinus is often observed, and occasionally, the internal fistula opening may be visualized. Compression of the neck abscess may elicit purulent discharge from the opening, confirming the diagnosis. However, diagnostic yield can be low when the opening is obscured by edema. Given that cervical ultrasound findings can be non-specific and mimic other inflammatory conditions, an experienced clinician must integrate findings from multiple modalities for a definitive diagnosis. In our cohort, diagnosis was established via MRI in 12 patients, CT in 11, cervical ultrasound in 6, and contrast radiography in 1. For cases with high clinical suspicion but inconclusive non-invasive imaging, direct laryngoscopy or esophagoscopy under general anesthesia provides a definitive diagnostic approach.

All 30 patients in this study were treated with endoscopic APC. The lack of a control group is a major limitation of this study. However, based on a review of the literature and comparisons with conventional surgery and other endoscopic treatments, we selected this method for the following reasons: First, the mean age at surgery in this cohort was only 2.3 years. Open surgery is associated with greater trauma, slower recovery, and risks including injury to the recurrent laryngeal nerve and superior laryngeal nerve, formation of pharyngocutaneous fistula, and wound infection. These risks are less acceptable for young children and their families. Moreover, incomplete ligation of the internal opening during open surgery carries a high risk of recurrence [16]. A single-center study demonstrated a recurrence rate of 33% for open resection [17]. Second, APC serves as a simpler and safer non‑contact thermal ablation technique. It delivers energy via ionized argon gas to precisely induce coagulative necrosis of the target mucosa [18]. Compared with other endoscopic treatment, APC offers distinct advantages. A retrospective analysis of 195 patients reported an endoscopic surgery success rate of 82% and a complication rate of 5.6% [19]. CO₂ laser ablation tends to be superficial, potentially leading to incomplete fistula closure and recurrence [20]; monopolar electrocautery, although effective, causes greater thermal damage and carries a higher risk of nerve injury [21]. In contrast, APC achieves precise mucosal ablation with controllable penetration depth, thereby reducing risks such as bleeding, perforation, and thermal nerve injury [22, 23]. Finally, the narrow pediatric laryngeal cavity and limited working space under suspension laryngoscopy make direct visual surgery challenging. The endoscopic‑assisted approach magnifies the operative field on a monitor, providing a clearer view that is crucial for precise ablation and avoidance of iatrogenic nerve injury. Based on our experience, APC is an effective first‑line treatment option that may eliminate the need for more invasive surgical procedures.

All patients underwent regular postoperative follow-up for 1 to 36 months. No recurrence was observed during the follow-up period. However, the follow-up duration for some cases remains insufficient, and longer follow-up is needed to confirm long-term cure. Postoperative management included nasogastric tube feeding for 3–7 days, followed by a semi-liquid diet. Patients were typically discharged 5–7 days postoperatively. Surveillance laryngoscopy at 2 weeks usually revealed mild mucosal edema with eschar separation, and at 12 weeks confirmed scar formation and fistula closure. Fistula closure was assessed at 12 weeks postoperatively; however, some patients had a shorter follow‑up, so the reported cure rate reflects outcomes within the available timeframe. All surgeries were performed after achieving infection control. Perioperative antibiotics were administered to mitigate the risk of recurrence from residual infection in the distal tract. The absence of recurrence in our series may be attributed to the selection of patients with the sinus tract type, which is particularly amenable to endoscopic ablation. Nevertheless, our sample size is relatively small, and follow‑up duration for some patients remains limited. Thus, larger series and longer follow‑up are needed to confirm the sustained efficacy of this approach.

In conclusion, endoscopic APC for the treatment of pyriform sinus fistula offers direct visualization, minimal invasiveness, technical simplicity, high efficacy, and a low recurrence rate. It is emerging as a mainstream surgical approach for CPSF in infants and young children and merits wider clinical application.
