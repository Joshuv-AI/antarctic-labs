Feasibility of a contactless, self-directed, home-based dim-light melatonin onset protocol: the Circadia pilot study
Authors: Gregory Bormes, Jessica J Love, Angus C Burns, Oluwaseun Akeju, Jakob M Cherry, Nisha Kantesaria, Lovemore Kunorozva, Salim Qadri, Shadab A Rahman, Frank A J L Scheer, Brandon Westover, John W Winkelman, Richa Saxena, Jacqueline M Lane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy ObjectivesTo test the feasibility of a self-directed, in-home, contactless salivary dim light melatonin onset (DLMO) assessment protocol to remotely measure endogenous circadian phase in participants with circadian rhythm disorders and healthy controls.Materials and MethodsThe 4–6-week protocol involved sleep, activity, and light tracking, sleep diaries, questionnaires, and two self-directed at-home salivary sample collections for DLMO one week apart with objective compliance measures. Individuals participated remotely using an online portal and received a mailed kit. The study population included five cases with Circadian-Rhythm Sleep–Wake Disorders and five controls ages 27-63.ResultsSalivary DLMO times could be calculated for 14/20 assessments, resulting in 8/10 participants with at least 1 valid DLMO and 6/10 having valid DLMOs for both. Failures in DLMOs resulted from compliance errors (eg, bright light levels) or consistently below/above threshold melatonin levels. After completing the DLMO collections, 70% of participants felt confident after completing DLMO 1, while 90% felt confident after DLMO 2. DLMO occurred on average at 24.08 hours (SEM = 1.10 hours) for four participants with Delayed Sleep Wake Phase Disorder, and at 21.37 hours (SEM = 0.71 hours) for five controls. The first and second DLMOs were strongly correlated (r = 0.98, p < .001).ConclusionsOur results indicate that contactless, self-directed, at-home DLMO assessments are feasible, reliable, and capable of detecting clinically relevant differences in circadian timing. The current protocol may serve as a framework to reliably and scalably assess circadian phase in both clinical and general populations. Statement of SignificanceHere, we report results of a pilot study regarding feasibility and accuracy of the first contactless, self-directed dim-light melatonin assessments, requiring no in-lab visitations. Our results indicate that contactless, self-directed, at-home dim light melatonin onset assessments are feasible, reliable and capable of detecting clinically relevant differences in circadian timing. This pilot study addresses the needs of a diverse patient population by increasing accessibility and simplifying diagnostic tools.

Introduction

Circadian rhythm sleep–wake disorders (CRSWDs) consist of a group of disorders characterized by sleep and wakefulness disturbances due to mistiming of the sleep/wake cycle relative to its desired timing that can stem from a variety of social, environmental, and genetic factors [1–3]. Two CRSWDs in particular, Advanced Sleep–Wake Phase Disorder (ASWPD) and Delayed Sleep–Wake Phase Disorder (DSWPD) have been shown to have a strong genetic component [4, 5]. Due to shifted physiological processes relative to the external environment, CRSWDs can lead to altered circadian timing which is frequently associated with neurological [6] and cardiometabolic comorbidities [7], as a risk factor for psychiatric disorders [8], pharmaco-resistance [9, 10] or as a preclinical sign of Alzheimer’s or Parkinson’s disease [11, 12]. By the International Classification of Sleep Disorders criteria, ASWPD is prevalent in up to 0.21% of the population, and DSWPD in about 3% [13–15], although CRSWDs are often undiagnosed or misdiagnosed, making the true prevalence difficult to estimate [16].

Diagnosis of a CRSWD typically involves establishing persistent sleep disturbance together with waketime excessive sleepiness related to sleep timing (ie, symptoms improve when timing can be self-selected) while ruling out confounding factors. Most often, symptoms are captured by self-report in a sleep log and/or in concert with objectively measured sleep timing through actigraphy. These methods capture symptoms, but do not assess underlying physiology. The current gold standard method for estimating circadian phase is a dim light melatonin onset (DLMO) determination, which can act as a supportive physiologic measure that can aid in both phenotyping and treatment plans for patients [17]. This often requires an in-person visit to a sleep or circadian clinic, which presents geographic, financial, and temporal barriers [18]. Scheduling visits, traveling to clinics, and staying at a clinic overnight all add to participant burden. Compounding these difficulties, waiting times for such diagnosis at circadian disorders sleep clinics are extremely long ranging between 5 and 7 months. DLMO assessments are not typically covered by insurance, which further complicates the use of DLMO testing for patients’ CRSWD diagnosis [15]. More accessible, less expensive methods of circadian phase assessment have been identified as a top priority in CRSWD research. With these barriers in mind, we designed the Circadia Study to be as accessible as possible.

Previous studies have demonstrated that DLMOs measured at home and supervised by a trained technician correlate highly with DLMOs measured in a laboratory in both control and DSWPD populations, indicating home-based DLMOs as a feasible diagnostic tool for measuring circadian phase [19, 20]. More recently, home-based DLMO assessment among adults with obesity was found to be feasible and correlated with sleep onset time [21]. In the Circadia Pilot study, we extend these findings by developing a novel self-directed, contact-free method for measuring DLMO at home that can be completed by an individual independently in the native home environment and without the requirement of attending a sleep lab, as in prior studies. This remote, self-directed assessment method is less expensive than traditional DLMOs and not limited by geographic barriers, thus making circadian phase data much easier to obtain and its assessment easier to complete for patients. Additionally, using our methods, circadian phase data can more easily be incorporated into research on myriad conditions where circadian rhythms may play a role, making this an incremental, but practical step toward scalable circadian phenotyping [4, 22].

In this pilot study, we aimed to test the feasibility of a novel at-home, self-directed, contact-free DLMO assessment protocol. We collected circadian phase and daily sleep data in 10 participants over a 4–6-week span. Each participant completed two DLMOs remotely, approximately 1 week apart, which we used to calculate DLMO two times for each participant. We objectively measured compliance with study conditions for the at-home DLMOs, assessed congruence between repeated measures, and identified areas for improvement.

Materials and Methods

Recruitment

The study was advertised on the MassGeneralBrigham (MGB) Rally recruitment site and through online CRSWD patient advocacy groups (Supplementary Figure 1). Participants were recruited from September 2021 to May 2022. After individuals expressed interest in the study, we contacted them via email with detailed information and a link to sign up on our web portal. After obtaining informed consent, we enrolled five individuals with a diagnosed CRSWD (n = 1 with ASWPD and n = 4 with DSWPD), and five controls with no diagnosed CRSWD (Table 1). Final follow-up occurred in July 2022. The study was approved by MGB Institutional Review Board (IRB Protocol Number: 2021P003666).

Online study portal

We created a Circadia Study online study portal through collaboration with the Broad Institute Data Science Platform using their Pepper Platform. Interested individuals were directed to the study portal where they could find more information about our study, including informational videos describing the study procedure. In the portal, they completed a web questionnaire screener (11 questions) to automatically determine their eligibility (Supplementary Table 1). Eligible participants then consented and enrolled directly through the study portal [19]. Participants used the study portal to complete each online task throughout the study, without need to contact with the study team.

Eligibility criteria

Eligible participants included anyone over the age of 23 [23] who could read and write at an 8th-grade English reading level. Participants were excluded based on the following criteria: individuals under the age of 23; enrollment in school (full-time and part-time); current pregnancy or delivery within a year of study participation [24]; current medications that may influence sleep; history of traumatic brain injury, stroke, or seizure disorder [25]; gingivitis, xerostomia, or periodontitis and blindness. Due to the longer-term study aim of determining the genetic causes of circadian rhythm disorders, participants were excluded if they were under the age of 23, as previous studies have demonstrated rapid age-related/developmental changes in chronotype prior to this [26–28]. Enrollment was limited to five controls with no diagnosed CRSWD and five individuals with either ASWPD or DSWPD and symptoms present for longer than three months.

Sleep logging and DLMO scheduling

Once a participant had enrolled, they landed on an introductory video that described the next steps of the study and directed them to begin sleep logging using a direct and secure connection of our custom portal to a sleep diary designed by researchers at Brigham and Women’s Hospital (the BWH Sleep Log) [29, 30] and self-schedule their two DLMO collection dates using Microsoft Bookings. We required the DLMOs to be a minimum one week apart (± 1 day). Participants logged their sleep online each day via the BWH Sleep Log using an online questionnaire (17 items) for the duration of their participation in the study (4-6 weeks; Supplementary Table 2). DLMO assessments were scheduled to begin 6 hours before their average bedtime estimated by study staff based on their online sleep diary data [19].

Circadia study at-home kit

To ensure at-home kits were mailed to engage participants only, participants were required to log their sleep for five days and scheduled their DLMO collections before receiving a Circadia Study kit complete with everything needed to complete their at-home DLMO. These kits included a personalized instruction manual (see Appendix), an Actiwatch Spectrum Plus to record light and activity data, a light meter, blue light-blocking glasses, two sets of nine salivettes in a closed plastic bottle, a time tracker lid (MEMS Cap) for the bottles to record the exact time each sample was taken, two freezer bags and ice packs in which to store collected samples, and a prepaid shipping label to return the kit and samples. The kit contained miscellaneous extra materials that might be needed during the DLMO collections including toothbrushes, tea lights, labels, markers, extra salivettes and aluminum foil, and contractor-grade trash bags to darken any windows in the collection space (Supplementary Table 3). Further details on each kit’s content are found in Supplementary Table 3. Participants were instructed to press and hold the small button on the left side of the Actiwatch for 3 seconds each time they went to sleep and each time they woke up. Participants wore these Actiwatches to track their light and activity data, and sleep/wake times for the duration of the study until they returned the kit.

Salivary DLMO collection

Before the collection, participants had access to written instructions, visual guides, and video tutorials describing how to set up their collection space and take saliva samples (see Appendix). All study kit materials were labeled with color and symbol coded stickers to ensure participants could easily set up their necessary collection components. Twenty-four hours prior to the start of their salivary DLMO collection, participants refrained from consuming alcohol, caffeine, or any recreational drugs [31]. On the day of the collection, participants refrained from consuming foods containing Red 40 dye or high concentrations of citric acid. Starting 6 hours before their collection, participants refrained from using nicotine or tobacco products [31]. To ease setup of the dim-light environment participants were provided with painter’s tape, contractor-grade garbage bags, aluminum foil, a light meter, and 18 tea lights which together emit less than 10 lux. Prior to starting their DLMO, participants verified through the study portal that they had adhered to each of these DLMO preparation steps and reported the lux level of their testing environment at least 30 minutes prior to their first sample using a BENETCH Digital Light Lux Meter (Shenzhen Jumaoyuan Science & Technology, Shenzhen, Guangdong, China) to confirm environment was <10 lux at the level of the cornea in the direction of gaze at the beginning of the DLMO assessment.

To increase compliance with the DLMO collection instructions, participants completed a Pre-Collection Attestation directly before and a Post-Collection Attestation directly after their DLMOs. The Pre-Collection Attestation asked whether participants had abstained from substances that would interfere with melatonin production such as melatonin, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), nicotine, medications, caffeine, and alcohol and whether they had arranged their collection space according to the instructions. Participants were provided with single-use acetaminophen in place of NSAIDs if needed. The Post-Collection Attestation asked whether the participant had collected each sample according to the provided instructions and had stored them correctly in the freezer. After they had completed their second DLMO, participants completed a post-DLMO Questionnaire (23 items) about their experiences in the study (Supplementary Table 4).

During their scheduled DLMO collection, participants collected nine samples in dim-light (<10 lux) hourly, starting 6 hours before habitual bedtime and ending 2 hours after habitual bedtime. Participants received automated reminder texts 45 minutes prior to the start of their DLMO and 5 minutes before each sample collection. During the DLMO participants were instructed to not consume food or liquids 30 minutes prior to each saliva sample. Participants were advised to place one of the frozen ice packs from the kit at the bottom of the freezer bag. After each sample was collected, participants were instructed to place the samples into a biohazard bag, and to the place biohazard bag into the freezer bag to keep the samples cold. Participants were instructed to ensure their Actiwatch was uncovered throughout the DLMO assessment to allow secondary, continuous assessment of DLMO validity, which allowed for continuous light-exposure monitoring for compliance. A member of the study team was available at all times by text, phone call, or email to answer any questions from the participants. Participants could use electronic devices like smartphones or laptops during the collection as long as the devices were on the lowest brightness setting, set to night mode to ensure low levels of blue light emission, and at least 24 inches from their face. After participants completed their DLMO salivary sample collection, they stored their samples in their home freezers using provided freezer bags. Study data collection ended once the participant collected two home DLMO sample collection days and wore their Actiwatch for at least 4 weeks. Participants shipped their kits back to the study team, including both the Actiwatch and the frozen samples from each of the DLMO collections in the provided insulated cooler box with frozen saliva samples in an additional insulated mailer package with cooler packs via expedited shipping (Supplementary Table 3).

Questionnaires

Participants completed four questionnaires through the study website: a Health and Lifestyle Questionnaire (23 items) [32], a Morningness-Eveningness Questionnaire [33] (MEQ; 19 items), attestations of compliance for each DLMO, and a post-study feedback questionnaire on participant experience and difficulties with compliance.

Data analysis

Upon receipt of the kit by the research team, each DLMO sample was inspected, and qualitative temperature data was recorded (ie, “completely frozen,” “partially frozen,” or “cool to the touch.”) Data from the Actiwatch and time-tracker bottle caps were accessed using Philips Actiware 6 Program and MEMS Adherence Software, respectively. We froze saliva samples immediately upon receipt at −80°C before shipping them to SolidPhase, Inc. (Portland, ME, USA) for melatonin assay using the Novolytix Direct Saliva Melatonin RIA kit (ALPCO Diagnostics, Windham, NH). Intra- and inter-assay CV were 7.9% and 9.8%, respectively. DLMO time was calculated by the study team using the Hockey-Stick method and a 3 pg/mL threshold method [34]. The Hockey-Stick DLMO time is calculated to be the time point where the melatonin profile switches from a straight line to the branch of a parabola, while the threshold time corresponds to the linearly-interpolated time where the melatonin concentration is calculated to cross the 3 pg/mL threshold [34]. In collections where both 3 pg/mL threshold and Hockey-Stick DLMO times were calculated, the values were strongly correlated (r = 0.996; Supplementary Table 5). The 3 pg/mL results are used in the main analyses for all but one participant for whom this method was unsuccessful and their Hockey-Stick DLMO time is used (see Supplementary Table 5). At the conclusion of their participation, each participant received an easy-to-read custom report that outlined their DLMO results, MEQ scores, and sleep logging data (Supplementary Fig. 2).

A series of compliance measures were used to ensure that participants stayed on track during the study and collected valid samples: completion of both Pre- and Post-Collection Attestations, consistency of light levels below 10 lux during the collection periods determined using the Actiwatch, and sample collection times within 5 minutes of the scheduled collection which was confirmed using the time-stamped MEMs Cap lid for accuracy. DLMO collection samples were considered valid only if a participant met all compliance measurements (Figure 1).

Results

The study sample included 10 individuals. Participants ranged in age from 27 to 63 years old, and the average age of the sample was 38.2 ± 11.7 (mean ± SD) years. Participants were predominantly female (9/10) and identified as women (n = 7) men (n = 1), and transgender/non-binary (n = 2). Of the 10 participants, seven identified as “White,” and three identified as “Asian” (Table 1).

Of the 10 participants comprising the DLMO sample, one participant had diagnosed ASWPD, four participants had diagnosed DSWPD, and five participants had no CRSWD diagnosis. Habitual bedtimes across the full study were calculated at a decimal clock time of 4.27 (SEM = 1.6 hours) in DSWPD, and 1.58 (SEM = 0.39 hours) in controls. Controls were included to test study protocols in participants who likely had no familiarity with CRSWDs and DLMO collections. The average duration of study involvement, from consent to study completion, was 42.4 days. Participants sleep logged for an average of 40.2 days and missed only 2.2 days of sleep logging on average. Five participants (50% of participants) never missed a day of sleep logging.

An advertisement on MGB’s Rally site had 86 candidates express interest in our study, and 30 completed the online screener questionnaire. Twenty candidates created an account, consented but failed to complete any study procedures, resulting in an attrition rate of 67%. Typically, those dropped candidates would sign up and either not begin sleep logging or only sleep log for only a few days before withdrawing. Of the 20 candidates who enrolled and then dropped out, they logged their sleep for an average of 2.4 days before dropping out, and 13 never began sleep logging at all. For this reason, we waited until candidates had logged their sleep online for at least five days and scheduled their first DLMO with us before sending a kit.

All 10 participants completed two DLMOs, collecting a total of 180 saliva samples across the study. Participants used our provided written and video instructions to set up their collection spaces with no study team oversight; only one participant contacted the study team with a question during their DLMO. The participants self-reported 100% compliance with pre- and post-collection protocols, as measured by the pre- and post-collection attestations.

The Actiwatch recorded light data in 30-second epochs, and this data allowed us to track whether light levels were sufficiently low during ambulatory DLMO collections. One participant didn’t wear their Actiwatch during one of their DLMO collections due to a chronic pain condition, so we had complete light data for 19/20 DLMOs. The light levels during the DLMO collections were below 10 lux (mean = 5.37 lux, median = 0.11 lux). Two participants had brief exposures to bright light on one or two of their DLMO collections, leading to high 95th percentiles of the light exposure distribution (>15 lux) while all other participants’ DLMOs had 95th percentiles <2 lux. For one of these participants, these periods of bright light levels (>50 lux) occurred during both of their DLMO collections. This participant reported these exposures each occurred from daylight through a window while using the restroom and attested to wearing blue light-blocking glasses during these periods. Despite this, a rise in melatonin was not detected in either DLMO from this participant, indicating potential melatonin suppression.

To objectively validate the timing of sample collection, salivettes for each DLMO sample collection were retrieved from a bottle with a time stamped lid (MEMs device) which recorded the time the lid was opened. Of 180 total samples collected across all DLMO collections throughout the study, there were seven instances (3.9% of all samples) where the timing of a saliva sample was not recorded, likely due to incomplete closure of a MEMs cap. One participant did not use the correct cap for their second DLMO, so none of the nine sample times were recorded. Overall, 147 (82%) samples were collected within 5 minutes of the scheduled collection time. The MEMs cap timing allowed us to objectively verify sample timing and utilize accurate collection time information for DLMO calculations (Figure 1, Table 2; Supplementary Table 6).

Using salivary melatonin data, we calculated DLMO times across our 20 total collections using the 3 pg/ml threshold (n = 13/20) and Hockey-Stick method (n = 12/20; Figure 2, Supplementary Figure 3). This resulted in an overall success rate of 70% (14/20) with one participant’s DLMO only detectable via the Hockey-Stick method. Overall, for 8/10 participants we were able to calculate a DLMO for at least one collection by one of these two methods. For 6/10 participants we were able to calculate a DLMO time for both DLMO collections. The inability to calculate DLMO for the other assessments was due to either sample invalidity based on objective non-compliance of light, consistently high (above threshold), or consistently low (below threshold) melatonin levels. For one participant, we were also unable to calculate one of their DLMOs due to a labeling issue with their samples. This participant was otherwise compliant, and we successfully calculated their other DLMO. In participants with a successfully calculated DLMO, self-reported bedtime in the 5 days leading up to the DLMO collection in decimal clock hours was found to be 4.22 hours (SEM = 0.90 hours) in DSWPD, and 1.67 hours (SEM = 0.53 hours) in controls. The average DLMO time in decimal clock hours for DSWPD participants was found to be 0.08 hours (SEM = 1.10 hours), while in controls it was found to be 21.37 hours (SEM = 0.71 hours). DLMO times were on average 4.15 hours (SEM = 0.37 hours) earlier than their self-reported bedtime in DSWPD participants, and 4.37 hours earlier (SEM = 0.74 hours) than self-reported bedtime in controls. Among the six participants for whom we calculated two separate DLMO times, DLMOs 1 and 2 were found to be strongly correlated (r = 0.98, p < 0.001; Figure 3). For 5/6 participants the residual between the two DLMOs was <1 hour, while one participant had a residual of 1.59 hours and the day of testing appeared unrelated to these residuals (Supplementary Table 7).

To assess participant confidence using the provided study materials (portal based, printed, and video content) and DLMO collection procedures, participants answered a post-DLMO Questionnaire (n = 23 Questions; Supplementary Table 4). Based on responses to the post-DLMO Questionnaire, 70% of participants felt confident completing both DLMOs with just the written and video instructions, 100% of participants felt confident completing both DLMOs with instructions and access to a member of the study team to answer questions during the collections, although only one participant asked any questions during either collection. Participant confidence in performing a DLMO collection rose from DLMO 1 collection (70% feeling confident) to DLMO 2 collection (90% feeling confident; Table 3).

Summary of post-DLMO questionnaire results

In the Post-DLMO Questionnaire, we identified challenges that individual participants faced during the study to inform a future longitudinal Circadia study. When asked about the most difficult part of the study, participants cited the length of DLMOs and/or difficulty staying awake under dim light conditions (n = 5), difficulty remembering to click the event marker button on their Actiwatch at sleep and wake (n = 3), difficulty of setting up the DLMO space (n = 2), and difficulty staying awake past their typical bedtime (n = 1), suggesting future points of study protocol modification.

Discussion

To our knowledge, the Circadia Pilot study is the first to measure endogenous circadian phase using a completely contact-free, home-based, and self-directed DLMO assessment. This protocol is unique because there are absolutely no in-person visits required and only communication with the study team if the individual needs it [21, 35]. It is also one of the first home studies to perform repeat DLMOs in healthy individuals and patients with CRSWD in the field [20]. While previous studies have required in-person instruction from study staff, participants in this study were able to comply with study protocols using instructional videos and written guides, with optional access to study staff via text messaging service. This protocol makes endogenous circadian phase assessment more accessible by making the DLMO assessment completely home-based and self-directed with a low-cost kit containing all necessary components to complete the assessment. This study, with both CRSWDs patients (n = 5) and controls (n = 5), found the at-home kits to be easy to use for most participants. We implemented objective measures of sample timing, activity, and light exposure to improve the validity of the remote assessment. With these measures, the kit materials, instructional videos, and study staff available to help participants during sample collections, our protocol mimics the procedures of a supervised, in-hospital and/or laboratory study without the barriers of cost, wait time, and travel restrictions.

While overseeing DLMOs remotely, we are able to objectively measure participant compliance to best practices for each individual saliva sample using Actiwatch and MEMs data to capture activity, light, and sample collection time data and ensure robust measurement of endogenous circadian phase. The circadian phase estimates from the kits were shown to be reliable within individuals, such that repeat DLMOs were very strongly correlated with one another. Furthermore, DLMO estimates for control and DSWPD individuals agreed with prior studies, with DSWPD participants exhibiting significant phase delay while showing no differences in phase angle of entrainment [22, 36]. Most instances of non-compliance with light level measures occurred prior to participants’ first DLMO sample collection, indicating a difficulty remaining in low light while participants were still setting up their collection space prior to their first sample collection. This should be remedied in future studies by instructing participants to set up and enter their study area 30 minutes prior to first sample collection. Additionally, while we used a handheld photometer and wrist-worn devices to measure light, we will aim in future to improve the validity of this measurement by including light sensors worn at the chest or eye level to more accurately capture ocular light exposure.

There were two cases of individuals for which we could not calculate a DLMO time using the data acquired in either DLMO. The challenge with the remote protocol is to distinguish errors in sample collection or compliance from a true melatonin profile. In the first case, the participant was the only individual diagnosed with ASWPD. This individual’s salivary melatonin levels consistently measured low (<2.5 pg/mL) throughout the collection period rendering calculation of a DLMO time using any published methods impossible. In rare cases of low salivary melatonin producers or mistimed assessment due to highly variable sleep–wake schedules, it may not be feasible to use salivary melatonin data for DLMO time [37]. Actiwatch data collected during this participant’s DLMO collections, however, showed four instances of non-compliant (>50 lux) light levels during DLMO 1 and three instances during DLMO 2, potentially resulting in melatonin suppression. While we cannot rule out biological low production of melatonin, our objective compliance measures point toward a need for repeat DLMO testing and increasing the sample size in order to increase the power for determining accurate DLMO time.

In the second case, a participant who had been diagnosed with DSWPD displayed highly inconsistent sleep times and a history of DLMO assays that failed, as was the case here. The rise of melatonin in this participant during both DLMO collections occurred before the calculated sampling window, resulting in the melatonin being above the threshold. In cases like these, a regular DLMO sampling window can be mistimed and therefore fail to capture circadian phase, and a longer saliva sampling window many hours beyond the habitual bedtime may be needed to capture a later DLMO occurrence. This participant’s DLMOs were scheduled on the weekend. In order to mitigate the risk of mistiming in such cases, it may be beneficial to schedule collections on weekday evenings, as to avoid the potential complications from weekend sleep variability, or otherwise use actigraphy-derived habitual bedtime. Alternatively, a 24-hour urine test may be considered, although this has a lower precision of assessment of DLMO given the much lower sampling resolution [38]. As we were unable to identify a DLMO in either collection from this participant, we suspect our DLMO was mistimed and referred the participant for 24-hour urinary melatonin collection [37, 38].

A key feature of the study design as a direct-to-participant study was its ease of enrollment. Consistent with a direct-to-participant approach, our recruitment and enrollment procedure was designed so that participants could enroll online with as few barriers to entry as possible. As a byproduct of this ease of enrollment, we anticipated a high attrition rate early in study participation, and we found this to be true among participants within the first few days after enrollment. This study design enabled participants to familiarize themselves with the study procedures and get a realistic idea of study burden before deciding to fully commit. Participants who lost interest or decided they could not commit were able to easily unenroll without significant wasted time or resources spent either by participants or the study team.

Our results suggest that autonomous, self-directed, at-home DLMOs are accurate and feasible. One current limitation of the study is the lack of objective temperature data for samples in transit. While salivary melatonin has been shown to be stable at temperatures below 4°C, this is not the case at room temperatures [39]. As such, since higher temperatures can affect the stability of salivary melatonin [40], future studies will utilize temperature sensors in the at-home study kit. Another limitation of the study was the small sample size of this pilot study and the scarcity of individuals with an ASWPD diagnosis (n = 1). We were unsuccessful in obtaining DLMO times for this individual and therefore, as our only ASWPD participant, we were unsuccessful at drawing conclusions regarding our kit for this patient population. Finally, while participants reported their diagnosis, diagnosing doctor, and diagnosing clinic, future studies may utilize formal clinical interviews. Importantly, our methods are generalizable, accessible, and relatively inexpensive. Through the use of a self-directed, at-home DLMO, it might be possible to lessen the number of clinician visits, and open up the possibility for telehealth appointments for circadian medicine. With this said, it is also important to note that while this would lessen many logistic burdens to circadian phenotyping for patients, both the cost of the assay and the limited insurance reimbursement still remain as substantial barriers for wider clinical uptake. As circadian rhythms have been implicated in a range of health conditions [6], circadian phase data can be a useful metric for studies beyond CRSWDs. We hope our at-home self-directed DLMO design can serve as a framework to improve accessibility in implementing circadian phase assessment in many other contexts.
