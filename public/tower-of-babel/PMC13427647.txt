Genomic signatures of host adaptation in Pasteurella multocida from poultry, cattle, swine, and cats: insights from interpretable machine learning
Authors: Qianlong Li, Yan Fan, Mingshu Wang, Shun Chen, Mafeng Liu, Dekang Zhu, Xinxin Zhao, Ying Wu, Qiao Yang, Juan Huang, Xumin Ou, Di Sun, Bin Tian, Yu He, Zhen Wu, Anchun Cheng, Shaqiu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pasteurella multocida is a major zoonotic pathogen causing fowl cholera and other animal-derived infectious diseases, posing significant threats to the poultry industry and public health. Rapidly identifying its host preference is critical for disease control and understanding cross-species transmission. This study collected genomic data of P. multocida from four key hosts (chicken, cat, pig, cattle) in the NCBI Pathogen Database, yielding 814 qualified strains. Phylogenetic analysis showed host-correlated branching: cat strains formed a distinct clade, while cattle, pig, and chicken strains clustered with host-specific sub-clades. Multi-locus sequence typing (MLST) revealed strong host-ST associations (cattle with ST1, pig with ST11/10/3) and weaker links (cat with ST30, chicken with ST124). This single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) matrix, alongside the host origin data, served as the feature set for training and evaluating five machine learning models: Support Vector Machine (SVM), Logistic Regression (LR), Random Forest (RF), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost). Models performed excellently on test sets (receiver operating characteristic area under the curve, ROC-AUC: 0.962–0.973; accuracy: 0.8528–0.865), with lower precision for chicken strains. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis identified high-impact SNPs: XGBoost relied on key SNPs, while RF showed distributed contributions. Genes with these SNPs enriched in metabolic processes, quorum sensing, and beta-lactam resistance, indicating common and host-specific adaptation strategies. Misclassifications (cat-chicken, cattle-pig) suggested cross-host transmission risks. This study integrates phylogenetics, machine learning, and functional genomics to decipher the genetic basis of P. multocida host adaptation, providing insights for fowl cholera control and cross-species transmission early warning with implications for poultry health management.

Introduction

Pasteurella multocida belongs to the family Pasteurellaceae and the genus Haemophilus. It is a commensal bacterium in the respiratory tracts of various mammals and birds, but it is also pathogenic to a wide range of animals including rabbits, chickens, cattle, sheep, and pigs, as well as humans. Clinical manifestations vary significantly among different infected hosts, making rapid diagnosis based on symptoms challenging (Dewhirst, et al., 2015; Wilson and Ho, 2013). P. multocida carries multiple virulence-associated genes and causes pathogenic infections through diverse mechanisms (Peng, et al., 2019). It exists as part of the normal oral flora in dogs and cats and is often transmitted via licking or biting (Lloret, et al., 2013). In pigs, it commonly colonizes the respiratory tract and can lead to atrophic rhinitis and respiratory diseases in both swine and cattle (Alhamami, et al., 2023; Niemann, et al., 2019). P. multocida is also responsible for severe septicemia and high mortality in chickens, often manifesting as explosive outbreaks known as fowl cholera (Allen, et al., 2024). As an important veterinary microbiological pathogen, P. multocida exhibits extensive animal transmission chains, and animal-derived strains represent a significant route for human acquisition. A study identified 330 human P. multocida infections directly associated with animal contact. Besides dogs and cats, pig-to-human transmission has also been documented, highlighting the serious public health threat posed by its cross-host transmission (Peng, et al., 2022). In livestock production and human clinical practice, antibiotics remain the primary method for prevention and treatment of P. multocida, and their frequent use has further exacerbated the spread of antimicrobial resistance.

It is well established that bacteria can undergo adaptive evolution to achieve host-specific commensalism or pathogenicity, most commonly through the acquisition or loss of resistance and virulence genes (Zhou, et al., 2023). Furthermore, pan-genome-wide association studies have revealed significant gene presence/absence variations among Proteus mirabilis strains from different hosts, and even among avian-origin mcr-1-positive Escherichia coli (E. coli) strains from different geographic regions (Li, et al., 2024; Zhang, et al., 2025). Phylogenetic analysis based on core genome single nucleotide polymorphisms has indicated a degree of host specificity in cattle P. multocida strains, suggesting that core genomic differences can distinguish P. multocida isolates from different hosts (Smith, et al., 2021).

The question of how to extract characteristic features from bacterial genomes and use them for strain characterization seems to be answered by machine learning (ML). With the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence, the availability of large genomic datasets is transforming the landscape of microbiological research, and ML is increasingly being applied in bacteriological studies (Sheppard, et al., 2025). For instance, ML has been used to predict bacterial metabolic system evolution (Konno and Iwasaki, 2023). By capturing whole-genome SNP differences and k-mer features and correlating them with corresponding resistance profiles, high-accuracy ML models have been constructed to predict susceptibility to various drugs in Acinetobacter baumannii and E. coli based on whole-genome data(Gao, et al., 2023; Ren, et al., 2022). By extracting diverse genomic features such as the presence of bla family resistance genes, insertion sequence length, type and count, and repeat sequence length, ML models have successfully predicted heteroresistance to piperacillin-tazobactam in E. coli, significantly accelerating the process of heteroresistance detection(Guliaev, et al., 2025). Additionally, researchers have developed ML models to predict the host origin (human, cattle, piscine, porcine) of Streptococcus agalactiae isolates based on whole-genome SNP differences (Ren, et al., 2024). ML models based on various principles offer multiple options for bacteriological applications, while SHAP interpretation of predictive results provides new avenues for mechanistic exploration. In summary, integrating bioinformatics and artificial intelligence into bacteriological research represents a developing trend in interdisciplinary studies. This study aims to utilize ML to construct high-accuracy models for predicting the host origin of P. multocida strains. By combining SHAP-based interpretation, we seek to elucidate the adaptive evolutionary differences among P. multocida from different hosts and interpret the preferences for cross-host transmission. The findings are expected to provide novel insights into the cross-host transmission and host adaptation evolutionary trends of P. multocida.

Methods

Screening and data collection of P. multocida genomes

As of September 28, 2025, P. multocida genomes from the four most common host origins (cat, chicken, pig, and cattle) were collected from the NCBI Pathogen Database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pathogens/) as study subjects. Genomic quality control was performed using CheckM v1.2.2 (https://github.com/Ecogenomics/CheckM), and only genomes with completeness ≥95% and contamination ≤5% were retained for subsequent analysis. A final set of 814 high-quality genomes with definitive host origin information was obtained.

Construction of a predictor feature matrix and phylogenetic analysis of P. multocida

The P. multocida reference genome ASM207325v2 was selected as the reference. Using Snippy v4.6.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/snippy), the aforementioned 814 P. multocida genomes were aligned against the reference sequence to generate a SNP difference matrix. This matrix was subsequently optimized: sites identical to the reference were encoded as 0, while mutated sites were encoded as 1, 2, 3, or 4 representing bases A, T, C, and G, respectively. SNP loci where the unmutated reference allele was present in half or more of the isolates were considered uninformative or detrimental for model optimization and were removed as predictors (Ren, et al., 2024).This process yielded a final matrix of 7,823 SNP loci, which served as predictors for subsequent machine learning (Table S1).

Phylogenetic analysis was performed based on the core genome alignment of the 815 P. multocida strains (including the reference). Gubbins v3.4.3 (https://github.com/nickjcroucher/gubbins) was employed to remove recombination events from the core genome. A phylogenetic tree was then constructed using FastTreeMP v2.2.0 under the GTRGAMMA model (Price, et al., 2010). The resulting tree was visualized and refined using the Chiplot online platform (Xie, et al., 2023). MLST was conducted using mlst v2.23.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/mlst) with the pmultocida scheme as the reference.

Construction and evaluation of machine learning models

In this study, multi-class machine learning methods including SVM, LR, RF, LightGBM, and XGBoost were employed to develop host origin prediction models based on the previously described whole-genome SNP feature matrix. The genomic data and host labels were rigorously aligned and subjected to quality control, with textual categories converted to numerical values using label encoding to ensure accurate strain-to-label correspondence and sufficient sample size per category. The dataset was stratified and randomly split into training and testing sets at an 8:2 ratio for model development.

To address potential class imbalance, we explored both oversampling (SMOTE) and undersampling (random undersampling) strategies during model training. Preliminary experiments indicated that oversampling led to overfitting on minority classes, while undersampling resulted in a significant loss of informative majority-class samples. Consequently, no resampling technique was ultimately adopted, and all models were trained on the original class distribution to preserve biological authenticity and avoid artificial sample synthesis.

We initially constructed RF, LR, and SVM. For the latter two algorithms, a One-vs-Rest strategy was implemented to handle the multi-class classification task. LightGBM and XGBoost models were trained using their native multi-class objective functions. The LightGBM model was conFig.d with specific parameters including a maximum depth of 6, 32 leaves, and a learning rate of 0.05, incorporating an early stopping mechanism to prevent overfitting. The XGBoost model was conFig.d with a maximum depth of 6 and a learning rate of 0.05 (Rajaperumal, et al., 2024).

All models were trained using 5-fold stratified cross-validation to evaluate stability, with final prediction models built on the complete training set. Model performance was comprehensively assessed using multiple metrics: accuracy, Kappa coefficient, Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), macro/micro-averaged AUC, and precision, recall, and F1 scores. Multiclass ROC and precision-recall (PR) curves were generated for additional visualization and evaluation. All code is publicly accessible and can be obtained at https://github.com/longge-longge-cell/ML-project1.

Application of SHAP for model interpretation

To further elucidate the decision-making mechanism of the optimal model, the SHAP framework was employed for feature importance analysis. Using the Tree Explainer algorithm, we generated top 20 important SNP loci profiles for each host category and the overall dataset, revealing key genetic markers influencing host prediction. Additionally, force plots were generated for randomly selected predictions from each model and host category to interpret the impact of individual SNP mutations at the isolate level.

Genetic basis of host tropism in P. multocida underlying SNP variations

Based on the top 20 important SNP loci identified by the SHAP algorithm, snpEFF v5.3a was utilized for SNP annotation (Cingolani, et al., 2012). First, a custom database was constructed using the protein sequences of the reference strain with the parameters "-noCheckCds -noCheckProtein". The previously generated snp.vcf file was then analyzed against this database to obtain SNP annotation results.

The genes affected by the screened SNPs were systematically collated, followed by functional enrichment analysis for this non-model organism. Briefly, eggnog-mapper v2.1.13 was employed to perform functional annotation of the reference strain ASM207325v2, yielding the eggnog.emapper.annotations results (Cantalapiedra, et al., 2021). Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were subsequently carried out using the R package clusterProfiler v4.16.0 (Wu, et al., 2021).

Cross-host transmission of misclassified strains

The misclassified strains from the optimal model were identified using Python. Their predicted hosts were subsequently analyzed against their actual hosts to assess correlations. Additionally, a phylogenetic tree was constructed for these misclassified strains following the methodology described in Section 2.2.

Results

Phylogenetics of P. multocida from four distinct host origins

This study collected genomic data of P. multocida from the four most prevalent host species available in the NCBI Pathogen Database. The cattle-derived P. multocida strains (n = 396) represented the largest dataset, followed by cat-derived (n = 174), pig-derived (n = 146), and chicken-derived (n = 98) strains. Phylogenetic analysis based on the recombination-free core genome (Fig. 1A) revealed a correlation between the population structure of P. multocida and host clustering. Specifically, the phylogenetic tree was divided into two major clades. Clade I predominantly consisted of cat-derived strains, with a small number of isolates also detected in cattle and chicken hosts; however, no pig isolates were identified within this clade. Clade II primarily comprised cattle-, pig-, and chicken-derived strains, each forming distinct phylogenetic clusters according to host origin. MLST further confirmed that certain STs were significantly associated with specific host origins (Fig. 1B). The results indicated a strong association between cattle-derived P. multocida and ST1, followed by ST59 and ST3. Pig-derived strains showed significant associations with ST11, ST10, and ST3, the latter representing a shared sequence type between cattle and pig isolates. Chicken-derived strains were weakly associated with ST124, and cat-derived strains with ST30; a considerable proportion of isolates from these two hosts possessed uncharacterized STs. In summary, this study demonstrates that the evolutionary lineages of P. multocida exhibit host-specific patterns. These findings support the feasibility of employing machine learning approaches to capture finer genetic distinctions for improved host origin prediction and differentiation.

Machine learning effectively distinguished P. multocida from different host origins using whole-genome SNP features

Leveraging efficient data processing and analytical capabilities, ML can precisely capture subtle SNP variations among P. multocida strains from different host origins and utilize these patterns for accurate prediction. This study selected five ML models known for their strong performance in predicting bacterial antimicrobial resistance. After training and testing, all models demonstrated robust discriminatory power in classifying the four host categories, achieving mean test set ROC-AUC values between 0.962 and 0.973 (Fig. 2A-E) with low coefficients of variation, indicating a highly consistent and accurate ability to distinguish different host classes. The mean Precision-Recall Area Under the Curve (PR-AUC) values ranged from 0.893 to 0.912, confirming that the ML models can accurately identify the true host origin (Fig. S1A-E). However, the PR-AUC results highlighted specific challenges for certain host categories. Interpretation of the optimal model's SNP-based predictions using SHAP

Both XGBoost and RF models were interpretable via SHAP. To investigate the relationship between SNP variations and the host preference of P. multocida, we performed further explanatory analysis. As described in the Methods section, we extracted the top 20 SNP loci with the highest contribution scores for predicting each of the four host origins from the two ML models (Fig. S2&3). The results showed that certain SNP loci, such as SNP2233 and SNP518144, exhibited high contribution in both XGBoost and RF for predicting the same host category. Feature importance analysis of the SNP loci revealed distinctly different patterns between XGBoost and RF. XGBoost demonstrated highly concentrated feature contributions. For predicting the four host categories, the top four SNPs had mean absolute SHAP values ranging from a minimum of approximately 0.4 to a maximum exceeding 1.4, with several other top-ranked SNPs also maintaining mean absolute values above 0.2. Notably, for pig and cattle source predictions, the top-contributing SNPs (SNP507148 and SNP518144, respectively) had mean absolute SHAP values 1.8 to 2.3 times higher than the second-ranked SNPs (SNP22333 and SNP507118), suggesting these specific loci serve as key markers for pig and cattle P. multocida. In contrast, RF exhibited a relatively uniform distribution of SNP feature contributions, with the highest mean absolute SHAP values ranging approximately between 0.01 and 0.025, and smaller differences in importance among features. In summary, while the majority of SNP loci captured by the two models for host origin prediction differed, a subset of SNPs demonstrated high contributions in both XGBoost and RF for predicting the same host category. The feature importance analysis of SNP loci revealed distinctly divergent patterns between the two models. XGBoost exhibited highly concentrated feature contributions, whereas RF showed a relatively uniform distribution of SNP feature importance. These findings suggest that both key individual SNPs and collective SNP variations may play roles in host adaptation.

Furthermore, we utilized violin plots to characterize the positive or negative relationship between SNP loci and host prediction. As shown in Fig. 5, the violin plots displayed the same ranking and pattern as the mean absolute SHAP values. In the RF model, for predicting cat-source P. multocida, only SNP508906 emerged as a high-feature-value SNP concentrated in the positive SHAP value region, indicating its significant positive contribution to the prediction. Cattle and pig-source predictions involved more high-feature-value SNPs with positive SHAP values, whereas chicken-source strains had almost no SNPs that significantly promoted prediction, correlating directly with the high prediction rates for cattle/pig sources and the low rate for chicken sources. Compared to RF, XGBoost identified more feature SNPs with larger SHAP values for cat and chicken-source predictions, while cattle and pig sources were characterized by feature SNPs with even higher SHAP values.

As described in the Methods, we employed random sampling to generate SHAP force plots, visualizing the contribution of individual SNPs to the prediction of specific samples (Fig. S4&5). In most randomly selected samples, the direction (positive/negative push) of individual SNP contributions aligned with the patterns observed in the Top 20 SNPs. Among the sampled predictions, XGBoost exhibited more SNPs exerting a positive push, with predictions generally exceeding the base value, whereas RF more frequently involved negative contributions, suppressing the prediction below the base value. This discrepancy suggests differences in the baseline prediction levels learned by the models during training, with XGBoost leaning towards a relatively optimistic base prediction and Random Forest maintaining a more conservative one.

Decoding host-specific evolution of P. multocida by SNP characterization

Based on the SHAP interpretation described above, high-impact SNP loci associated with P. multocida from different host origins were identified. ML models can accurately distinguish the host origins of P. multocida by leveraging variations in these loci, indicating that changes in high-impact SNPs likely reflect long-term adaptive evolution of the pathogen with its host. Combining results from XGBoost and RF, a total of 103 SNPs were selected for further annotation using snpEFF, yielding 2,023 annotation entries (Table S2). These annotations were categorized as follows: upstream gene variants (43.25%, 875/2,023), downstream gene variants (47.40%, 959/2,023), synonymous variants (7.32%, 148/2,023), missense variants (1.73%, 35/2,023), and intergenic region variants (0.30%, 6/2,023). In terms of functional impact, the majority of variants were classified as modifier impact (90.95%, 1,840/2,023), followed by low impact (7.32%, 148/2,023), with a small proportion exhibiting moderate impact (1.73%, 35/2,023). Furthermore, the analysis of base substitutions revealed that transitions (Ti) were more frequent than transversions (Tv). The most common transitions were A>G (n = 34) and G>A (n = 44), followed by C>T (n = 25) and T>C (n = 29) (Fig. S6A). Among synonymous and missense variants, transitions accounted for the majority (72.13%, 132/183), while transversions represented 27.87% (51/183), resulting in a Ti/Tv ratio approximating 3:1 (Fig. S6B).

First, to assess the impact of variant gene sets on biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions, we performed GO enrichment analysis (Fig. S7&8). In the RF model, cat-origin high-impact SNPs were mainly enriched in processes such as primary metabolic process, cellular biosynthetic process, organic substance biosynthetic process, and biosynthetic process. Chicken-origin SNPs were enriched in organic cyclic compound metabolic process, cellular aromatic compound metabolic process, and heterocycle metabolic process. Pig-origin SNPs were associated with cellular aromatic compound metabolic process, organic cyclic compound metabolic process, heterocycle metabolic process, and nucleobase-containing compound metabolic process. Cattle-origin SNPs were enriched in primary metabolic process, organic substance metabolic process, and nitrogen compound metabolic process. Most of the top-enriched GO terms across the four host origins belonged to biological processes and showed considerable overlap. Notably, the high-impact SNPs from different hosts exhibited identical p.adjust values for their respective GO terms, indicating a uniform statistical significance level of enrichment. Since statistical significance alone could not differentiate these terms, we used Gene Ratio to evaluate their biological relevance. A higher Gene Ratio implies a greater proportion of differentially expressed genes associated with the term, suggesting stronger biological impact. We further analyzed GO enrichment based on XGBoost-identified high-impact SNPs. Cat-origin SNPs were enriched in establishment of localization, location, and transport. Chicken-origin terms, such as nitrogen compound metabolic process and organic cyclic compound metabolic process, resembled those from the RF model. Pig-origin terms matched those of cat-origin but differed in Gene Ratio, while cattle-origin results were largely consistent with the RF model. Interestingly, in the XGBoost model, the main GO terms for chicken- and pig-origin strains were associated with both biological processes and cellular components and diverged more significantly from the RF results, whereas chicken- and cattle-origin results remained relatively consistent.

The GO enrichment analysis provided a macro-level overview, indicating that high-impact SNP variations in P. multocida were primarily enriched in biological processes and cellular components. To pinpoint specific pathways, we conducted a more focused KEGG analysis (Fig. 6). None of the KEGG pathways enriched in strains from the four host origins, under either model, showed conventional statistical significance (p ≤ 0.05). Therefore, we adopted a lenient threshold for exploratory interpretation. In the RF model, cat-origin high-impact SNPs were concentrated in seven pathways with relatively low gene counts but notable p.adjust values, including porphyrin metabolism, beta-lactam resistance, and arginine biosynthesis. Chicken-origin SNPs were enriched in beta-lactam resistance and quorum sensing, pig-origin in quorum sensing and histidine metabolism, and cattle-origin only in beta-lactam resistance. While the first three host origins showed relatively distinct pathways, all cattle-origin terms had identical p.adjust values, with only beta-Lactam resistance having a higher gene count, suggesting broader gene involvement in this pathway. These RF-based results indicated that high-impact SNPs in cat-, chicken-, and cattle-origin strains were associated with beta-lactam resistance genes — particularly prominent in chicken-origin strains. Additionally, SNPs from chicken-, pig-, and cat-origin strains were linked to quorum sensing and porphyrin metabolism, implying adaptive evolution in these regulatory mechanisms. However, the RF model failed to reveal a clear adaptive trend for cattle-origin strains. Subsequent XGBoost-based KEGG analysis (Fig. 6E&F) yielded more distinct insights: cat-origin strains were enriched in taurine and hypotaurine metabolism and ABC transporters; chicken-origin in histidine metabolism and metabolic pathways; pig-origin in two-component system (TCS) and sulfur cycle; and cattle-origin in ribosome. The two models captured complementary evolutionary signals: the RF model emphasized common adaptive pathways across hosts (e.g., beta-lactam resistance, quorum sensing), reflecting "shared selection pressures" such as antibiotic exposure or iron limitation. In contrast, the XGBoost model, with its centralized feature importance weighting, uncovered host-specific pathways like ribosome biogenesis in cattle and taurine metabolism in cats, revealing "specialized selection pressures" tailored to distinct host niches—such as cattle’s robust immune environment or cat-specific metabolic traits. Together, these pathways illustrate distinct evolutionary patterns and collectively elucidate the host-specific adaptive strategies of P. multocida.

Cross-host transmission route of P. multocida inferred from misassigned strains

Based on the misclassified strains identified from the test set predictions of both the RF and XGBoost models, 22 strains were consistently misclassified by both algorithms. One isolate (GCA_049364735.1_ASM4936473v1_genomic) was predicted as chicken-derived by RF and cattle-derived by XGBoost, while the remaining strains showed consistent misclassification patterns between models (Table S3). We posit that the recorded isolation source reflects the original host at the time of sampling, whereas the ML-predicted host origin may indicate the strain’s underlying host adaptation tendency. Such discrepancies suggest potential cross-host transmission events. Phylogenetic analysis of the 22 misclassified strains revealed three major clades (Fig. 7A). Two clades predominantly contained strains whose recorded and predicted hosts aligned closely with feline and chicken origins. The third clade primarily consisted of isolates originally obtained from cats, pigs, and cattle, but were consistently predicted as pig- or cattle-adapted. This host-associated clustering was corroborated by the ML predictions, with Clades 1 and 2 largely classified as cat- or chicken-associated, and Clade 3 mainly as pig- or cattle-associated. Furthermore, we identified a non-random host-misclassification pattern and performed an association analysis to quantify pairwise misclassification frequencies (Fig. 7B). From a computational perspective, these results should be interpreted as preliminary hypotheses rather than definitive conclusions. The results indicated that cat- and chicken-origin strains were most frequently misclassified as each other, followed by pig–cattle and cat–cattle misclassifications. Thus, we propose as a working hypothesis that cross-host transmission between cats and chickens may represent a common route for P. multocida in this dataset, with additional, though less frequent, transmission potential between pig and cattle, cat and cattle, pig and cat, and pig and chicken hosts. These hypotheses warrant further validation through independent methods such as phylogenetic reconstruction or epidemiological investigation.

Discussion

P. multocida is a significant public health pathogen, causing substantial economic losses in the livestock industry through diseases such as cattle pneumonic pasteurellosis and fowl cholera. Current prevention and control strategies for P. multocida are multi-faceted. Vaccination remains the primary approach for preventing infection, with several studies having developed vaccines for mice, pigs, and cattle(He, et al., 2024a; Islam, et al., 2023; Wu, et al., 2023). Antibiotic therapy constitutes another critical line of defense; however, treatment options are increasingly limited due to the emergence of multi-factorial mediated acquired resistance(Serna, et al., 2025). In addition to protecting susceptible animals and controlling infection sources, monitoring transmission routes is essential for effective P. multocida management. This study demonstrates that whole-genome SNP profiles can reliably predict the host origin (cat, chicken, pig, or cattle) of P. multocida strains.

Our investigation began with a phylogenetic analysis based on core-genome SNPs and MLST, which revealed discernible genetic divergence among strains from the four different host species. We subsequently constructed ML models utilizing the whole-genome SNP dataset. All five models tested exhibited high mean AUC-ROC values. However, the AUC-PR for chicken-origin strains was consistently lower (0.743-0.854). This performance discrepancy is likely attributable to the imbalanced dataset, where the number of cattle-origin strains was nearly four times greater than that of chicken-origin strains. A smaller sample size for chicken strains can impede the model’s ability to learn sufficiently discriminative features. Furthermore, the core-genome phylogeny indicated higher specific heterogeneity among chicken-origin isolates, which displayed considerable genetic similarity to cat, cattle, and pig strains. This likely resulted in fewer unique SNP features to distinctly identify chicken origins and introduced more background noise into the feature matrix. Consistent with this, Fig. 4 shows that all five models achieved lower precision, sensitivity, and F1-scores specifically for chicken-origin strains. Despite these challenges, our models demonstrated high overall accuracy and specificity in predicting host origins across all four categories, confirming their general effectiveness. Previous research comparing different genomic data layers (MLST, cgMLST, k-mer) for predicting Campylobacter host origins concluded that a cgMLST+XGBoost combination yielded optimal performance (Arning, et al., 2021). Although k-mer features theoretically encompass more comprehensive genomic information, the specific methodology of selecting k-mers of length 21 and subsequently applying Chi-squared test-based feature reduction may have inadvertently discarded critical discriminatory information, leading to its inferior performance compared to cgMLST. Conversely, and in alignment with our approach, a study on Streptococcus agalactiae host prediction achieved excellent performance using a whole-genome SNP difference matrix (Ren, et al., 2024).These findings, alongside our own, underscore the importance of feature selection/filtering and comparative model evaluation in ML-based bacterial studies. Notably, the top-performing models in the two cited studies were both ensemble tree-based algorithms. The interpretable algorithm SHAP is crucial for understanding model decision processes and the contribution of individual features, providing powerful insights into the relationship between predictions and feature factors (Wang, et al., 2025). We applied SHAP analysis to our top-performing RF and XGBoost models. While a minority of high-importance SNPs were identified consistently by both models, the majority of the top 20 SNP loci for different hosts were model-specific. Furthermore, the distributions of feature importance differed markedly: XGBoost exhibited highly concentrated feature contributions, whereas RF showed a more uniform distribution of importance across numerous SNPs without a dominant set of core loci. The distribution of host-specific high-contribution SNPs correlated directly with model predictive performance. Cattle- and pig-origin strains possessed a greater number of high-SHAP-value SNPs that positively drove predictions, corresponding to their higher prediction accuracy. In contrast, chicken-origin strains had almost no significantly predictive SNPs, aligning with their suboptimal performance. This disparity stems from the models' inherent architectures. XGBoost's gradient boosting mechanism and regularization constraints predispose it to identify and amplify the contribution of a few critical features. Its greedy splitting strategy prioritizes SNPs offering the highest information gain, resulting in highly concentrated feature importance. Conversely, RF, through bagging and feature randomness, constructs a diverse ensemble of decision trees. This democratic decision-making mechanism distributes feature contributions more evenly across multiple SNPs, potentially reflecting the biological reality of polygenic adaptation. From a microbial evolution perspective, the distinct SNP contribution patterns of the two models appear to map onto different genetic architectures of host adaptation. The concentrated, high-impact features in XGBoost support a "deterministic SNPs" hypothesis, where a few key loci are sufficient to determine host association. In contrast, RF's diffuse pattern aligns with an "infinitesimal/polygenic" model, emphasizing that host adaptation arises from the cumulative, small effects of numerous SNPs, where fewer impactful SNPs may lead to greater uncertainty in host assignment, as seen with chicken-origin strains. In summary, different machine learning models exhibit varying feature identification patterns, which consequently lead to divergent predictive performance and biological interpretations.

SNP variations can lead to functional changes in bacteria, which, through long-term co-evolution with hosts, can result in adaptive SNPs favoring a more specialized symbiosis (Chen, et al., 2021; Zou, et al., 2020). We hypothesized that the top 20 SNPs identified by the two ML models represent outcomes of adaptive evolution in P. multocida. To understand the functional implications of adaptation across different hosts, we performed SNP variant annotation followed by functional enrichment analysis using databases for non-model organisms. Based on the GO enrichment results (Figs. S7&8), the genomic evolution of P. multocida during adaptation to different hosts is characterized both by a shared reliance on core biological processes like primary metabolism and by host-specific SNP variations leading to divergent strategies in pathways such as nutrient utilization and substance transport. Subsequent KEGG pathway analysis, while not reaching conventional statistical significance thresholds, revealed suggestive patterns of adaptive divergence through an exploratory, lenient interpretation strategy (Fig. 7). Therefore, these results should only be used as clues for subsequent mechanistic studies and not as definitive conclusions. Results from the RF model indicated that cat-, chicken-, and cattle-origin strains all showed enrichment related to the evolution of beta-lactam resistance pathways. Beta-lactam antibiotics are commonly used in livestock farming to treat bacterial infections, exerting strong selective pressure on bacteria(Elsayed, et al., 2021; He, et al., 2024b). P. multocida potentially modulates resistance-related genes in this pathway via high-feature SNP variations, potentially reducing antibiotic efficacy and enhancing survival in drug-exposed environments. Quorum sensing and porphyrin metabolism were common adaptive directions for chicken-, pig-, and cat-origin strains. Quorum sensing is a core mechanism allowing bacteria to sense population density and coordinate group behaviors, regulating critical phenotypes like virulence factor production, biofilm formation, and colonization (Mukherjee and Bassler, 2019; Zeng, et al., 2023). Distinct host environments and immune mechanisms likely drive divergent evolution in the quorum sensing system. Porphyrins are precursors to heme, a key iron-acquisition vehicle for bacteria, and porphyrin metabolism is closely linked to oxidative stress resistance (Choby and Skaar, 2016). These enrichments suggest different host-origin strains face distinct host-imposed iron limitation and oxidative stress pressures. The XGBoost model further enriched this understanding by uncovering more diverse host-specific adaptive strategies. Cat-origin P. multocida showed adaptive evolution in taurine metabolism and ABC transporters. Taurine is an essential amino acid for cats, with notably higher concentrations in their bodies compared to other hosts. Variations in the taurine metabolism pathway may allow the bacterium to convert abundant host taurine into energy, enhancing its colonization competitiveness (Choby and Skaar, 2016). ABC transporters efficiently mediate the transmembrane transport of key substances, including taurine and iron ions, ensuring substrate supply for taurine metabolism and potentially expelling toxic metabolites under host immune pressure, forming a coordinated "metabolic specialization + environmental adaptation" strategy (Jia, et al., 2023). Chicken-origin P. multocida exhibited SNP variations in histidine metabolism, potentially optimizing histidine synthesis and utilization to meet protein demands, regulating intracellular pH to counteract intestinal acidity, and modulating virulence factor expression to enhance gut colonization. Concurrent enrichment in central metabolic pathways suggests a coordinated optimization of glycolysis and amino acid metabolism, improving energy utilization efficiency to adapt to the highly competitive and fluctuating nutrient environment of the chicken gut. We acknowledge that these enrichment findings are entirely computational and require experimental validation. To practically test the proposed metabolic adaptations, we suggest future in vivo studies using mouse immunization models: for example, measuring antibody titers and cytokine responses against predicted protective antigens expressed by strains from different host origins. Pig-origin strains were enriched for the TCS and sulfur cycle pathways. The TCS is a regulatory hub for virulence, physiological adaptation, and immunogenic phenotypes in P. multocida; for instance, deletion of qseC, a core gene in the QseBC TCS, significantly attenuates virulence (Yang, et al., 2021);Enrichment of the sulfur cycle pathway relates to the characteristics of the porcine gut microbiome, which is rich in anaerobes producing substantial sulfides (Kerr, et al., 2011); Variations in this pathway may enable P. multocida to utilize sulfides as a sulfur source for synthesizing essential compounds like cysteine and methionine, while expression of genes like sulfide oxidase could help mitigate sulfide toxicity, providing a niche advantage within the complex gut microbial community (Deissaly and Stoppani, 1963).

In contrast, variations in cattle-origin strains were significantly enriched in the ribosome. As the cell's protein synthesis machinery, enrichment here suggests a global optimization of gene expression regulation in cattle-adapted strains. The distinct pathway sets highlighted by RF and XGBoost likely reflect their intrinsic algorithmic characteristics. XGBoost excels at detecting complex epistatic interactions, thus its enriched pathways may represent core regulatory networks for the trait. RF is more sensitive to individual large-effect loci, and its enriched pathways might reflect fundamental supporting architectures. The minimal gene overlap between the two model's gene sets further supports that their findings are complementary rather than contradictory. Current research indicates that pathogen evolution involves not only virulence factor acquisition but also metabolic specialization through the loss of dispensable pathways and reinforcement of host-adapted mechanisms during niche adaptation (Wheeler, et al., 2018). In conclusion, our analysis indicates that the host preference of P. multocida is determined by multiple synergistic biological processes. Employing a combination of ML approaches provides a more comprehensive unveiling of the complex genetic mechanisms underlying its adaptation.

To understand the practical connection between host-differentiating SNPs and protective antigens, we examined the genomic locations of the high-impact SNPs identified by our models. SNP annotation (Table S2) revealed that the vast majority (∼90%) of these SNPs are located in non-coding regions (upstream, downstream, or intergenic), with only 1.73% being missense and 7.32% synonymous. Importantly, none of the top host-differentiating missense SNPs mapped to the coding sequences of well-characterized protective antigens, including OmpH, OmpA, PlpE, Oma87, and TonB-dependent receptors. However, a few SNPs were found in the putative promoter regions of these genes, suggesting potential effects on transcriptional regulation rather than on epitope structure. These findings imply that the core amino acid sequences of major protective antigens are largely conserved across host-origin strains, and a subunit vaccine designed based on these conserved sequences might theoretically confer cross-host protection. Nevertheless, regulatory SNPs could lead to variable antigen expression levels across different host origins (e.g., in chicken-origin strains, which showed lower predictive performance), which may affect actual vaccine efficacy. Therefore, we recommend that future studies quantify the expression of key protective antigens using targeted proteomics and validate cross-host protective efficacy through challenge experiments in respective animal models. Such work is essential to bridge the gap between evolutionary genomic insights and practical vaccine development.

Finally, through comparative analysis of the recorded versus predicted hosts for ML-misclassified samples, we inferred a higher likelihood of cross-host transmission between cat- and chicken-origin P. multocida, followed by transmission between cattle- and pig-origin strains. Consistent with our findings and previous studies, phylogenetic analyses have demonstrated close genetic relationships between cat- and chicken-origin strains, as well as between cattle- and pig-origin strains, suggesting these lineages may possess a broad host range or enhanced cross-host transmission capability (Peng, et al., 2018; Smith, et al., 2021). The application of ML for source attribution has been investigated in various bacterial species. The integration of ML with large-scale genomic resources provides a novel technological approach for bacterial source tracking(Pascoe, et al., 2024; Wheeler, et al., 2018; Zhang, et al., 2019).

We acknowledge several limitations in this study. First, although P. multocida is a zoonotic pathogen, human‑derived strains were excluded from this study due to their limited availability and inconsistent sequencing quality in public databases, which could compromise the overall reliability of the modeling dataset. As a result, the current models can only accurately distinguish host tropism among livestock and poultry but cannot provide early warning of cross‑species spillover from animals to humans, limiting their application in the prevention and control of zoonotic diseases. Future studies should incorporate high‑quality clinical strains of human origin to improve the models’ capacity for assessing zoonotic risk. Second, the imbalanced sample sizes across the four host origin led to suboptimal predictive performance for chicken-origin strains compared to the other three hosts. Third, our study was constrained by the selection of feature factors and ML models. We only compared five common algorithmic models and utilized a SNP difference matrix, a widely adopted feature set in bacterial ML studies, as the primary input. The choice of feature factors and ML models is critical for prediction performance. With the ongoing development of more sophisticated feature extractors and learning algorithms, ML-based approaches for bacterial source attribution hold promising potential for future applications, offering deeper insights and improved accuracy. Fourth, the inference of cross‑host transmission based on misclassified samples should be considered exploratory. The imbalanced sample sizes and the suboptimal predictive performance for chicken‑origin strains may bias the observed misclassification patterns, potentially over‑ or underestimating certain transmission routes (e.g., cat‑chicken). Additionally, misclassifications can arise from model‑specific feature selection rather than genuine biological relatedness. Therefore, these inferred transmission pathways are hypothesis‑generating and require validation through independent methods such as phylogenetic reconstruction or epidemiological investigation.

In summary, this study constructed and compared, for the first time, two high-performance ML models: RF and XGBoost, using genomic data of the four most common host-origin P. multocida strains from public databases. These models demonstrated strong capability in predicting the host origins (cat, chicken, pig, and cattle) of P. multocida, achieving high scores in Accuracy, F1-Score, and AUC-ROC. Through SHAP-based interpretation, host-adaptive genetic features underlying different host origins were identified. Furthermore, analysis of model misclassifications revealed potential high-frequency cross-host transmission pathways of P. multocida. By adopting a ML perspective, this study provides a novel approach for source-tracking animal-derived P. multocida, offers new insights into its host preference mechanisms, and contributes to future efforts in monitoring and blocking its transmission, thereby helping to mitigate its public health threat.
