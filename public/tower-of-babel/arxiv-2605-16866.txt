Abstract
We study the asymptotic behavior of widely used tests for evaluating and comparing predictive accuracy when forecast errors exhibit heavy tails. In particular, when loss differentials have infinite variance, the Diebold–Mariano test statistic converges to a nonstandard limit involving non-Gaussian stable random variables. As a consequence, conventional critical values can yield severely distorted inference: a nominal 5% test may reject a true null as often as 70% of the time. To establish these results, we develop a new stable limit theorem for strongly mixing, infinite-variance time series processes. Building on this theory, we consider subsampling-based inference that remains valid irrespective of tail-heaviness and requires no estimation of long-run variances or tail indices. An application to risk forecasts for emerging-market exchange rates shows that accounting for heavy tails can substantially alter conclusions about predictive performance relative to standard procedures.



Keywords:
Predictive ability, hypothesis testing, heavy tails, stable limit theory, subsampling.

1
Introduction

The Diebold-Mariano (DM) test of
Diebold and
Mariano 1995
and the superior
predictive ability (SPA) test of
Hansen 2005
are workhorse tools for evaluating and comparing
forecasts in economics and finance. They are routinely used to assess
volatility and risk forecasts, to compare macroeconomic forecasts across models and institutions, and to select among asset-pricing and term-structure models. The asymptotic justification for both tests rests
on a central limit theorem (CLT) that requires loss
differentials to have finite variance.

This justification is at odds with a basic stylized fact about the data.
Many of the underlying variables – asset returns, exchange rate
fluctuations, and measures of financial volatility – have power-law
(heavy) tails with index
\kappa>0
, and the empirically relevant range
routinely includes
\kappa<2
, corresponding to infinite (or undefined)
variance; see, e.g.,
Gabaix 2009
and

Ibragimov
et al. 2015
. If extreme tail realizations
are not fully captured by a forecast series, the corresponding forecast
errors and loss differentials inherit such tails.

The mismatch has substantial consequences for inference. In a simulation experiment, we find that a standard DM test at the 5% nominal level can reject a true null as often
as 70% of the time, irrespective of the sample size, under empirically
plausible heavy-tailed processes. In our empirical application to risk
forecasts for emerging-market foreign exchange rates – where the loss
differentials appear to have infinite variance – the standard DM test
would lead a researcher to conclude that a sophisticated GARCH-based risk
model statistically dominates each of nine competing forecasts. A
heavy-tail-robust alternative reaches a substantively different
conclusion: simple non-parametric rolling-window forecasts cannot be
rejected as inferior to any of the more sophisticated GARCH-based or
score-driven competitors.

A central problem in the forecast evaluation literature is to conduct inference about the
mean of a time series
(X_{t})_{t=1,\dots,n}
, where
X_{t}
is a
forecast error, a forecast loss, a loss differential, or a function of these. We focus first on
testing

\mathsf{H}_{0}:\mathbb{E}[X_{t}]=0,

(1.1)

against
\mathsf{H}_{\mathrm{A}}:\mathbb{E}[X_{t}]\neq 0
with

\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}|]<\infty
. Depending on how
X_{t}
is constructed,
(
1.1
) covers hypotheses such as forecast unbiasedness,
forecast optimality, forecast encompassing, and, most prominently, the
equal predictive ability (EPA).
Standard practice is to base inference on

T_{n}=\dfrac{\overline{X}_{n}}{\hat{\sigma}_{n}/\sqrt{n}},

(1.2)

where
\overline{X}_{n}
is the sample average and
\hat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}
is
typically a heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation consistent (HAC)
estimator of the long-run variance of
\overline{X}_{n}
, and to compare

T_{n}
against standard normal critical values; e.g.,
Diebold and
Mariano 1995
. The test is justified by
the CLT of
Ibragimov 1962
under the
assumption that
(X_{t})_{t=1,\dots,n}
is stationary, strongly mixing, and
has
finite
variance. These conditions underlie a large body of
work on forecast evaluation, including
White 2000
,

Hansen 2005
,
Hansen and
Lunde 2005
,

Hansen
et al. 2011
,
Patton and
Timmermann 2012
,

Barendse and
Patton 2022
, and
Odendahl
et al. 2023
.

This paper makes three main contributions.
First
, we derive the
limiting distribution of
T_{n}
under
\mathsf{H}_{0}
when
(X_{t})_{t=1,\dots,n}

is potentially heavy-tailed. When
X_{t}
has tail index
\kappa<2
,
T_{n}

converges in distribution to a ratio given in terms of two dependent non-Gaussian stable
random variables whose joint distribution is fully characterized by

\kappa
and the extremal dependence structure of
(X_{t})_{t=1,\dots,n}
.
The resulting ratio may be skewed, and its quantiles can differ
substantially from those of the standard normal.

Second
, to establish this result, we develop a novel stable limit
theorem for strongly mixing processes under infinite (or potentially
undefined) variance, providing a natural extension of the aforementioned
CLT to heavy-tailed processes. Our theorem
(Theorem
2.6
) only imposes regular variation, a
mild anti-clustering condition on extremes, and weak rates on mixing
coefficients. These conditions hold for a range of well-known linear and
non-linear processes – including autoregressive, GARCH, and stochastic
volatility models – and the theorem should be of independent interest to
researchers working with heavy-tailed time series more broadly.

Third
, we consider an inferential procedure that is valid under

both
finite- and infinite-variance regimes. We construct critical
values by subsampling
(
Politis
et al. 1999
)
and establish their
asymptotic validity using our new limit theory. The procedure is fully
data-driven: it does not require knowledge of
\kappa
, the convergence
rate of the sample mean, or consistent estimation of long-run variances.
The same approach yields confidence intervals for
\mathbb{E}[X_{t}]
, which
may itself be of interest in forecast evaluation.

Beyond these three contributions, we develop two further results. We extend our analysis to the SPA test of
Hansen 2005
, which addresses the more demanding problem
of deciding whether a benchmark forecast is dominated by any one of
m

competing forecasts. We provide a multivariate version of our limit theory and use it to derive the limiting distribution of the SPA test statistic under heavy tails. The result lays the groundwork for a heavy-tail-robust SPA test based on subsampling. This complements the conditional SPA test of
Li
et al. 2021
, which, like the original, assumes finite
higher-order moments.

Finally, we consider alternatives under which
\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}|]

is infinite. Such alternatives have not, to our knowledge, been addressed in the existing literature on inference about the mean of heavy-tailed time series. We show that
T_{n}
does not diverge under
\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}|]=\infty
,
so that any test based on it (whether using standard normal or subsampled critical values) is inconsistent. We propose a modified
statistic and subsampling procedure that deliver non-trivial rejection frequencies.

Our work is most closely related to the working paper by

Kim
et al. 2021
, who also consider the DM test under heavy tails.
Kim
et al. 2021
obtain a non-Gaussian stable limit for the DM statistic by direct application of
Davis 1983
and
Davis and
Hsing 1995
and propose subsampling for inference, invoking the validity result of
Kokoszka and
Wolf 2004
. They additionally analyze how the tail index of the loss differential depends on the tail indices of the target variable, the forecasts, and the loss function, and illustrate empirically the relevance of subsampling for realized volatility forecast comparisons. Our analysis differs in three respects: (i) building upon

Matsui
et al. 2025b
, we provide a new stable limit theorem for strongly
mixing processes under assumptions strictly more general than those used
in
Kim
et al. 2021
,
McElroy and
Politis 2002
, and

Kokoszka and
Wolf 2004
, and we verify these assumptions for a range of standard linear and
nonlinear time series processes; (ii) our analysis extends beyond the EPA setup to
forecast encompassing, optimality, and SPA testing, as well as
alternatives under which the mean may fail to exist; and (iii) we provide
rigorous arguments for the asymptotic validity of the subsampling-based
tests.

2
Set-up and limit theory

This section develops the limit theory underlying our inferential procedure. Section
2.1
provides some motivating examples and previews the main result. Sections
2.2
–
2.3
state the partial-sum limit theory under finite variance and heavy tails, respectively. Section
2.4
combines these into the limiting distribution of
T_{n}
in (
1.2
), and Section
2.5
illustrates the theory in the context of linear autoregressive processes.

2.1
Set-up and preview of main result

Given a random sample
(X_{t})_{t=1,\dots,n}
with a time-invariant mean, we consider testing the hypothesis
\mathsf{H}_{0}
in (
1.1
) against
\mathsf{H}_{\textrm{A}}:\mathbb{E}[X_{t}]\neq 0
with
\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}|]<\infty
. Following much of the existing literature on forecast evaluation and comparisons, we throughout take
X_{t}
as a
primitive
. Depending on the construction of
X_{t}
, the hypothesis test resembles those often encountered in the forecasting literature. We begin with three motivating examples.

Example 2.1
(Forecast Optimality)

Let
(f_{t})_{t=1,\dots,n}
denote a forecast series of a time series
(Y_{t})_{t=1,\dots,n}
, and let
e_{t}=Y_{t}-f_{t}
denote the forecast error. The forecast is said to be unbiased, if
\mathbb{E}[e_{t}]=0
, and it is said to be efficient if
\mathbb{E}[e_{t}f_{t}]=0
; see, e.g.,
Odendahl
et al. 2023
. Hence, forecast unbiasedness or forecast efficiency corresponds to the hypothesis
\mathsf{H}_{0}
in (
1.1
) when
X_{t}
is given by
e_{t}
or
e_{t}f_{t}
, respectively. In both cases,
X_{t}
may be heavy-tailed if the forecast errors are heavy-tailed.
\blacklozenge

Example 2.2
(Forecast Encompassing)

Let
(f_{1,t})_{t=1,\dots,n}
and
(f_{2,t})_{t=1,\dots,n}
denote two forecast series of a time series
(Y_{t})_{t=1,\dots,n}
, and let
e_{i,t}=Y_{t}-f_{i,t}
denote the forecast error of series
i=1,2
. Following
Harvey
et al. 1998
, forecast series 1 is said to encompass forecast series 2 if
\mathbb{E}[(e_{1,t}-e_{2,t})\,e_{1,t}]=0
. Hence, forecast encompassing means that
\mathsf{H}_{0}
holds with
X_{t}=(e_{1,t}-e_{2,t})\,e_{1,t}
,
which may be heavy-tailed whenever at least one of the forecast errors are.
\blacklozenge

Example 2.3
(Equal Predictive Ability (EPA))

Consider two forecast series
(f_{1,t})_{t=1,\dots,n}
and
(f_{2,t})_{t=1,\dots,n}
of a time series
(Y_{t})_{t=1,\dots,n}
. For a given loss function
\mathcal{L}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, the two forecast series are said to have equal predictive ability (EPA) if

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(Y_{t},f_{1,t})-\mathcal{L}(Y_{t},f_{2,t})]=0;

see
Diebold and
Mariano 1995
. Hence, the EPA hypothesis corresponds to the hypothesis
\mathsf{H}_{0}
in (
1.1
) with
X_{t}=\mathcal{L}(Y_{t},f_{1,t})-\mathcal{L}(Y_{t},f_{2,t})
.

A widely used loss function is the squared error,
\mathcal{L}(Y_{t},f_{1,t})=(Y_{t}-f_{1,t})^{2}=e_{1,t}^{2}
. Here, if the forecast error
e_{1,t}
has tail index
\kappa
, then – by construction – the squared error
e_{1,t}^{2}
has tail index
\kappa/2
, so that
X_{t}
may have infinite variance even when
e_{1,t}
or
e_{2,t}
themselves have finite variance.

Heavy-tailed losses may also occur when evaluating quantile forecasts as considered in the empirical application in Section
6
. Let
f_{1,t}
denote a
\tau
-level (conditional) quantile forecast of
Y_{t}
. The associated tick loss
(
Giacomini and
Komunjer 2005
)
is

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tick},\tau}(Y_{t},f_{1,t})=(\tau-\mathbf{1}\{Y_{t}-f_{1,t}<0\})(Y_{t}-f_{1,t})=(\tau-\mathbf{1}\{e_{1,t}<0\})e_{1,t}.

A common way to construct quantile forecasts for financial returns (or losses) is by means of GARCH-type models. Under mild conditions, if
Y_{t}
follows a GARCH process, then
Y_{t}
has a tail index
\kappa>0
and the tick losses inherit this tail index; see Section
6
for further details and an empirical illustration with emerging-market foreign exchange rates, for which the range
\kappa\in(1,2)
is empirically relevant.
\blacklozenge

In order to test
\mathsf{H}_{0}
, we consider the statistic
T_{n}
in (
1.2
) with
\hat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}=n^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{t}^{2}
. In contrast to
Diebold and
Mariano 1995
, we do not use a HAC estimator. This is deliberate: under heavy tails the sample mean has infinite (or undefined) long-run variance, so consistent estimation of this quantity is neither possible nor needed for our purposes. The test statistic
T_{n}
is, hence, given by the studentized sum

T_{n}=\dfrac{S_{n}}{\gamma_{n}},

(2.1)

with

S_{n}:=\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{t}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\gamma_{n}:=\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{t}^{2}}\;.

(2.2)

The normalization of
S_{n}
by
\gamma_{n}
is important, since these quantities are of the same stochastic order irrespective of the tail-heaviness of
X_{t}
; in particular, the statistic does not require knowledge about the rate of convergence of
S_{n}/n
.

To preview the main results of this section, we briefly summarize the limiting behavior of
T_{n}
under
\mathsf{H}_{0}
. Given appropriate regularity conditions,

•

as predominantly exploited in the existing body of literature on forecast evaluation and comparisons, if
X_{t}
has
finite
variance, then
T_{n}
has a
Gaussian
limit;

•

if
X_{t}
is heavy-tailed with tail index
\kappa\in(1,2)
, so that
X_{t}
has infinite variance, then
T_{n}
converges in distribution to the ratio
\xi_{\kappa}/\zeta_{\kappa/2}
, where
\xi_{\kappa}
is a
non-Gaussian

\kappa
-stable random variable and
\zeta_{\kappa/2}^{2}
is a
(\kappa/2)
-stable random variable.

The formal statement is given in Proposition
2.7
in Section
2.4
, which is a direct consequence of the partial-sum limit theorems in Sections
2.2
–
2.3
. Complementing this result, in Proposition
2.10
we show – in the context of heavy-tailed linear autoregressive processes – that the same non-Gaussian limit governs the HAC-based variant of
T_{n}
that is routinely used in applied forecast comparisons, so that standard normal critical values are potentially unreliable also for this statistic.

Remark 2.4

Throughout, we focus on testing
\mathsf{H}_{0}
in (
1.1
), but our results also enable the construction of confidence intervals for
\mathbb{E}[X_{t}]
when
X_{t}
may have infinite variance. This is useful, e.g., for assessing the accuracy of a forecast series: setting
X_{t}=\mathcal{L}(Y_{t},f_{t})
, the quantity
\mathbb{E}[X_{t}]
represents the expected loss, such as the mean squared forecast error. See Remark
3.6
for details.

2.2
Limit theory for partial sums under finite variance

We assume throughout that the data-generating process (DGP) for
X_{t}
is
stationary
, and we write
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
to signify that the process is indexed over all integers.

We start by considering the finite variance case. Before stating our assumptions, we recall the definition of strong mixing coefficients, characterizing the time-dependence of a real-valued stationary process
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
. Let
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
be real-valued, bounded functions acting on
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
and define

\alpha_{k}:=\sup_{|g_{1}|,|g_{2}|\leqslant 1}\left|\mathrm{cov}\left[g_{1}(\dots,X_{-1},X_{0}),g_{2}(X_{k},X_{k+1},\dots)\right]\right|,\qquad k\geqslant 0.

(2.3)

Following
Doukhan 1994
the numbers
(\alpha_{k})_{k\geqslant 0}
are the strong mixing coefficients of
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
, and the process is said to be strongly mixing (or
\alpha
-mixing) if
\alpha_{k}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
.
In order to derive the asymptotic properties of
T_{n}
in (
2.1
) under finite variance of
X_{t}
, we consider the following assumption.

Assumption 1

There exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}|^{2+\varepsilon}]<\infty
and the
\alpha
-mixing coefficients of
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
satisfy

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}^{\varepsilon/(2+\varepsilon)}<\infty.

(2.4)

Assumption
1
is standard in the literature on forecast evaluations. The summability condition in (
2.4
) holds for any
\varepsilon>0
, if the mixing coefficients
(\alpha_{k})_{k\geqslant 0}
decay at a geometric rate. Therefore, a wide range of well-known time series processes, including causal stationary solutions to linear autoregressive, GARCH-type and stochastic volatility processes, satisfy the summability condition; see Section
F
in the Supplemental Appendix for details. On the other hand, the moment condition
\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}|^{2+\varepsilon}]<\infty
typically imposes additional restrictions on the process, particularly that
X_{t}
has finite variance. With
\overline{\varepsilon}:=\inf\{\varepsilon\in(0,\infty]:\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}|^{2+\varepsilon}]=\infty\}
, we note that a smaller
\overline{\varepsilon}
imposes a faster decay on
\alpha_{k}
for (
2.4
) to hold. Consequently, Assumption
1
balances the order of finite moments and the rate of decay of the mixing coefficients. The assumption implies that a CLT and the ergodic theorem apply to
(X_{t}-\mathbb{E}[X_{t}])
and
(X_{t}-\mathbb{E}[X_{t}])^{2}
, respectively:

Theorem 2.5
(
Ibragimov 1962
)

Let
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a stationary process satisfying Assumption
1
. Then

\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\sum\nolimits_{t=1}^{n}(X_{t}-\mathbb{E}[X_{t}]),\sqrt{\sum\nolimits_{t=1}^{n}{(X_{t}-\mathbb{E}[X_{t}])}^{2}}\right)\xrightarrow{d}\left(Z,\sqrt{\mathrm{var}(X_{t})}\right),\qquad n\rightarrow\infty,

where
Z
is a
\mathrm{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
-distributed random variable with

\sigma^{2}:=\mathrm{var}(X_{t})+2\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\;\mathrm{cov}(X_{0},X_{t}),\qquad 0<\sigma^{2}<\infty.

(2.5)

2.3
Limit theory for partial sums under heavy tails

We now turn to the case where
X_{t}
has infinite second moment. To formalize what we mean by heavy tails, we recall the notion of regular variation: a real-valued random variable
X
is said to be regularly varying with tail index
\kappa>0
if

\mathbb{P}(X>x)\sim p_{+}\dfrac{L(x)}{x^{\kappa}}\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad\mathbb{P}(X<-x)\sim p_{-}\dfrac{L(x)}{x^{\kappa}},\quad\quad x\to\infty,

(2.6)

where
L
is a slowly varying function and the tail-balance coefficients
p_{+},p_{-}\geqslant 0
satisfy
p_{+}+p_{-}=1
. Importantly, under (
2.6
),
\mathbb{E}[{|X|}^{p}]=\infty

(<\infty)
for every
p>\kappa

(p<\kappa)
, and, hence, a smaller
\kappa
implies that
X
has heavier tails.

Following
Basrak and
Segers 2009
, a stationary real-valued process
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
is said to be regularly varying with index
\kappa>0
, if
X_{0}
is regularly varying with index
\kappa>0
in the sense of (
2.6
) and there exists a real-valued stochastic process
(\mathit{\Theta}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
such that for all integers
h\geqslant 0
,

\mathbb{P}(|X_{0}|^{-1}(X_{-h},\dots,X_{h})\in\cdot\;|\;|X_{0}|>x)\xrightarrow{w}\mathbb{P}((\mathit{\Theta}_{-h},\dots,\mathit{\Theta}_{h})\in\cdot),\quad\quad x\to\infty,

(2.7)

where
\overset{w}{\to}
denotes weak convergence of probability measures. The process
(\mathit{\Theta}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
is called the
spectral tail process
of
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
and characterizes the extremal dependence structure of
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
. Informally, conditional on a very large excursion at time
0
,
(\mathit{\Theta}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
records the shape of the surrounding cluster of large values, normalized by
|X_{0}|
. An iid process has
\mathit{\Theta}_{t}=0
almost surely for all
t\neq 0
, while persistent processes give rise to non-degenerate
\mathit{\Theta}_{t}
. By construction,
\mathbb{P}(|\mathit{\Theta}_{0}|=1)=1
with
\mathbb{P}(\mathit{\Theta}_{0}=\pm 1)=p_{\pm}\geqslant 0
. For more details on regularly varying time series we refer to
Mikosch and
Wintenberger 2024
.

With this notion in hand, we impose the following assumption.

Assumption 2

The process
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
is regularly varying with index
\kappa\in(0,1)\cup(1,2)
in the sense of (
2.7
).

We note that for
\kappa\in(1,2)
,
X_{t}
has infinite variance, whereas the variance is not defined for
\kappa\in(0,1)
.
1
1

1

As is standard in the literature on stable limit theory for dependent processes, we rule out the case
\kappa=1
. The case
\kappa\in(0,1)
is covered both for completeness and in view of the undefined-mean alternatives studied in Section
4
.
Under Assumption
2
there exists a deterministic sequence
(a_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
satisfying
a_{n}\to\infty
and

n\mathbb{P}(|X_{t}|>a_{n})\rightarrow 1,\qquad n\rightarrow\infty.

(2.8)

In particular, by regular variation of
X_{t}
, it holds that
a_{n}\sim L(n)n^{1/\kappa}
as
n\to\infty
for some slowly varying function
L
.
Given Assumption
2
, we make the following assumption about the dependence structure of
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
.

Assumption 3

Let
(a_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
satisfy (
2.8
). There exists a deterministic sequence
(r_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
with
r_{n}\to\infty
and
r_{n}=o(n)
as
n\to\infty
such that

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}n\sum_{t=k}^{r_{n}}\mathbb{E}[\min\{|a_{n}^{-1}X_{t}|,1\}\;\min\{|a_{n}^{-1}X_{0}|,1\}]=0.

(2.9)

Furthermore, the strong mixing coefficients
(\alpha_{k})_{k\geqslant 0}
of
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
satisfy

\dfrac{n}{r_{n}}\alpha_{\ell_{n}}\rightarrow 0,\qquad n\to\infty,

(2.10)

for some deterministic sequence
(\ell_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
with
\ell_{n}\rightarrow\infty
and
\ell_{n}=o(r_{n})
as
n\to\infty
.

The condition in (
2.9
) is a so-called anti-clustering condition; see e.g.
Bartkiewicz et al. 2011
. To provide some intuition for this condition, note that (
2.9
) implies

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}n\sum_{t=k}^{r_{n}}\mathbb{P}\left(|X_{t}|>a_{n}\ ,\ |X_{0}|>a_{n}\right)=0.

(2.11)

Hence, informally, if we observe a very large
|X_{0}|>a_{n}
, then
(X_{t})_{t\geqslant 1}
cannot stay above this threshold
a_{n}
for too long with non-negligible probability; the extremes may cluster, but long clusters become increasingly rare.

The two parts of Assumption
3
play complementary roles in the limit theory, and it is useful to separate them conceptually. Regular variation (Assumption
2
) together with the anti-clustering condition (
2.9
) delivers a stable limit for the sum of
k_{n}=n/r_{n}
independent block sums of
X_{t}
with each block having length
r_{n}
. The mixing condition (
2.10
) then ensures that this sum of independent block sums is a good approximation of the full partial sum
S_{n}=\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{t}
; we refer to Appendix
A
for additional details about this approximation argument.

In addition to playing complementary roles, note that the anti-clustering condition (
2.9
) and the mixing condition (
2.10
) depend on the same deterministic sequence
(r_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
. Similar to the role of the constant
\varepsilon>0
in Assumption
1
, balancing the tail-heaviness and the rate of decay of the mixing coefficients in the finite variance case, the sequence
(r_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
balances the clustering of extremes against the mixing coefficients: if
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
is likely to exhibit long clusters of extreme observations such that
\mathbb{P}\left(|X_{t}|>a_{n}\ ,\ |X_{0}|>a_{n}\right)>0
for large
a_{n}
and
t
, the anti-clustering condition requires that
r_{n}
grows at a sufficiently slow rate. This slow rate must be compensated for by a faster decay of
\alpha_{k}
in order for the mixing condition in (
2.10
) to hold.

Assumption
3
can be shown to hold under mild conditions for a wide range of well-known time series processes, including linear autoregressive processes (as considered in Section
2.5
), GARCH processes, and other processes given by affine stochastic recurrence equations, as well as stochastic volatility processes; see Section
F
of the Supplemental Appendix for additional details and technical conditions.

Assumptions
2
–
3
ensure that
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
obeys the following stable limit theorem.

Theorem 2.6

Let
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
satisfy Assumptions
2
–
3
, and define
\mu:=\mathbb{E}[X_{t}]\mathbf{1}(\kappa>1)
. With
(a_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
defined by (
2.8
), it holds that

a_{n}^{-1}\left(\sum\nolimits_{t=1}^{n}(X_{t}-\mu),\sqrt{\sum\nolimits_{t=1}^{n}(X_{t}-\mu)^{2}}\right)\overset{d}{\to}(\xi_{\kappa},\;\zeta_{\kappa/2}),\qquad n\to\infty,

(2.12)

where
\xi_{\kappa}
is a
\kappa
-stable random variable,
\mathbb{P}(\zeta_{\kappa/2}>0)=1
, and
\zeta_{\kappa/2}^{2}
is a
\kappa/2
-stable random variable.

To the best of our knowledge, the above result is new. Whereas many existing stable limit results rely on point process convergence (e.g.,
Davis and
Hsing 1995
), our result builds upon recent theoretical results of
Matsui
et al. 2025b
relying on convergence of so-called hybrid characteristic function–Laplace transforms. For a real-valued random variable
V
and a non-negative random variable
W
, the hybrid characteristic function–Laplace transform is the map

(u,\lambda)\mapsto\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{e}^{iuV-\lambda W}\big],\qquad u\in\mathbb{R},\ \lambda\geq 0,

which fully characterizes the joint distribution of
(V,W)
and reduces to the characteristic function of
V
when
\lambda=0
and to the Laplace transform of
W
when
u=0
. This approach typically applies under milder conditions than those imposed to establish point process convergence, and we carefully adapt the results of
Matsui
et al. 2025b
to strongly mixing processes and allow for
\mu\neq 0
. For further details about hybrid characteristic function-Laplace transforms, we refer to
Mikosch and
Wintenberger 2024
, and we refer to the textbook by
Samorodnitsky and
Taqqu 1994
for a treatment of non-Gaussian stable distributions.

The distribution of the limiting vector in (
2.12
) is fully characterized by the tail index and the extremal dependence structure of
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
. To see this, recall that by Assumption
2
,
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
is regularly varying and hence, by the definition in (
2.7
), has a spectral tail process
(\mathit{\Theta_{t}})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
. Define the
extremal cluster process
as

Q_{t}=\mathit{\Theta}_{t}/(\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}|\mathit{\Theta}_{j}|^{\kappa})^{1/\kappa},\qquad t\in\mathbb{Z},

(2.13)

which is the spectral tail process re-normalized so that
\sum_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}|Q_{t}|^{\kappa}=1
.
Then it holds that
(\xi_{\kappa},\zeta_{\kappa/2}^{2})
has hybrid characteristic function–Laplace transform given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{e}^{iu\xi_{\kappa}-\lambda\zeta_{\kappa/2}^{2}}\big]

\displaystyle=\exp\Big(\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathrm{e}^{iyu\sum_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}Q_{t}-y^{2}\lambda\sum_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}|Q_{t}|^{2}}-1-iyu\sum_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}Q_{t}\bm{1}\big\{\kappa\in(1,2)\big\}\Big]d(-y^{-\kappa})\Big),

(2.14)

with
u\in\mathbb{R}
and
\lambda\geq 0
. In particular, it holds that
\xi_{\kappa}
and
\zeta_{\kappa/2}
are dependent. Setting
\lambda=0
yields the characteristic function of the
\kappa
-stable random variable
\xi_{\kappa}
,

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{e}^{iu\xi_{\kappa}}]=\exp\big(-|u|^{\kappa}\tilde{\sigma}^{\kappa}(1-i\;\beta\;\textrm{sign}(u)\tan(\kappa\pi/2))\big),\qquad u\in\mathbb{R},

(2.15)

with
scale
and
skewness
parameters given, respectively, by

\tilde{\sigma}=\left(c_{\kappa}\;\mathbb{E}\big[\big|\sum\nolimits_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}Q_{t}\big|^{\kappa}\big]\right)^{1/\kappa}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\beta=\dfrac{\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\sum\nolimits_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}Q_{t}\big)_{+}^{\kappa}-\big(\sum\nolimits_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}Q_{t}\big)_{-}^{\kappa}\big]}{\mathbb{E}\big[\big|\sum\nolimits_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}Q_{t}\big|^{\kappa}\big]},

(2.16)

where

c_{\kappa}:=\frac{\Gamma(2-\kappa)\cos(\kappa\pi/2)}{1-\kappa},

(2.17)

and
\Gamma(\cdot)
the gamma function; see
Mikosch and
Wintenberger 2024
.
The distribution of
\xi_{\kappa}
is asymmetric whenever the imaginary part of its characteristic function is non-zero, that is, if
\beta\neq 0
. Consequently, whether or not the distribution is symmetric depends on the spectral tail process
(\mathit{\Theta}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
via (
2.13
). For certain processes, such as linear autoregressive processes considered in Section
2.5
below,
\beta
is determined entirely by the tail-balance coefficients
p_{\pm}=\mathbb{P}(\mathit{\Theta}_{0}=\pm 1)
. Moreover, note that the imaginary part of the characteristic function tends to zero as
\kappa\uparrow 2
. Intuitively, this property is due to the fact that a stable distribution with index of stability
\kappa=2
and a location parameter of zero is a normal distribution with zero mean and hence symmetric.

Let

(2)

Suppose that

2
–

3
Critical value construction

In this section we consider subsampling-based techniques for testing the hypothesis
\mathsf{H}_{0}
in (
1.1
) based on the test statistic
T_{n}
. Subsampling-based inference about the mean of a heavy-tailed time series has been considered in, e.g.,
McElroy and
Politis 2002
,
Kokoszka and
Wolf 2004
and
Bai
et al. 2016
. While these papers consider the construction of confidence intervals for
\mathbb{E}[X_{t}]
, our emphasis is on hypothesis testing. In particular, we extend the setting of
Politis
et al. 1999
to hypothesis testing for heavy-tailed time series with unknown rate of convergence of the sample mean, and we construct critical values based on subsample versions of the original test statistic. Consider the following algorithm.

Algorithm 3.1

Given a sample
(X_{t})_{t=1,\dots,n}
of size
n>1
, choose some integer
b_{n}\in(0,n)
and some nominal level
\eta\in(0,1)
.

1.

Compute the statistic given in (
2.1
),

T_{n}=\dfrac{\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{t}}{(\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{t}^{2})^{1/2}}.

2.

With
q_{n}:=n-b_{n}+1
, compute the subsample statistics

T_{i,b_{n}}:=\dfrac{\sum_{t=i}^{i+b_{n}-1}X_{t}}{(\sum_{t=i}^{i+b_{n}-1}X_{t}^{2})^{1/2}},\qquad i=1,\dots,q_{n}.

(3.1)

3.

Define

L_{n,b_{n}}(x):=q_{n}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{q_{n}}\mathbf{1}\big(T_{i,b_{n}}\leqslant x\big),

(3.2)

and compute the
\eta/2
and
(1-\eta/2)
empirical quantiles of
(T_{i,b_{n}})_{i=1,\dots,q_{n}}
, i.e.,

C_{n,b_{n}}(\eta/2)=\inf\{x:L_{n,b_{n}}(x)\geqslant\eta/2\}

(3.3)

and

C_{n,b_{n}}(1-\eta/2)=\inf\{x:L_{n,b_{n}}(x)\geqslant 1-\eta/2\}.

(3.4)

Then a two-sided equal tailed test of level
\eta
rejects
\mathsf{H}_{0}
if
T_{n}\notin[C_{n,b_{n}}(\eta/2),C_{n,b_{n}}(1-\eta/2)]
.

The algorithm is tailored to work irrespective of

having finite variance (Assumption
1
) or being regularly varying with
\kappa\in(1,2)
(Assumptions
2
–
3
). In particular, the algorithm does not rely on estimation of the long-run variance

in Theorem
2.5

in (

is ensured under the strong mixing conditions in Assumptions

and

In this section we consider extensions of the limit theory and related subsampling-based inference considered in Sections

3

4.1
Allowing for alternatives with
\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}|]=\infty

To construct a test that delivers non-trivial rejection frequencies under alternatives where
\kappa\in(0,1)
, we consider a modification of the original test statistic
T_{n}
. Define

\overline{\gamma}_{n}=\sum_{t=1}^{n}|X_{t}|.

(4.2)

\overline{\gamma}_{n}/n

\kappa\in(0,1)

\widetilde{T}_{n}=(\overline{\gamma}_{n}/n)T_{n}.

(4.3)

We have the following result.

Let the statistic

be given by (

and

\widetilde{T}_{n}\overset{d}{\to}\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}|]Z,\qquad n\to\infty,

with

.

2.

Under Assumptions
2

\kappa\in(1,2)

\widetilde{T}_{n}\overset{d}{\to}\mathbb{E}[|X_{t}|]\frac{\xi_{\kappa}}{\zeta_{\kappa/2}},\qquad n\to\infty,

with

.

3.

Under Assumption
1

2

\mathbb{E}[X_{t}]\neq 0

|\widetilde{T}_{n}|\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}\infty,\qquad n\to\infty.

(4.4)

Critical values for a test based on

can be computed by the subsampling Algorithm

in the Supplemental Appendix. Importantly, under

4.2
Testing for superior predictive ability

In Appendix
B
we provide a multivariate version of Theorem
2.6
. The result, Theorem
B.2
, may be used for testing a hypothesis about the mean vector of a multivariate heavy-tailed stationary time series. We illustrate this with an application to comparing multiple forecast series. Motivated by the work of
Hansen 2005
, see also
White 2000
, suppose that we have
m+1\geqslant 2
forecast series
(f_{j,t})_{t=1,\dots,n}
,
j=0,1\dots,m
, of a target variable
(Y_{t})_{t=1,\dots,n}
with
m
fixed. Given a loss function
\mathcal{L}(\cdot,\cdot)
, let
\mathbf{X}_{t}=(X_{t,1},\dots,X_{t,m})^{\prime}
be defined by

X_{t,j}:=\mathcal{L}(Y_{t},f_{0,t})-\mathcal{L}(Y_{t},f_{j,t}),\qquad j=1,\dots,m,

(4.6)

that is
X_{t,j}
is the loss differential between forecast series
0
and
j
at time
t
. Given that
\mathbf{X}_{t}
has time-invariant mean, the forecast series
0
is said to have
superior predictive ability
(SPA) over the other
m
competing forecast series if

\mathsf{H}_{\mathrm{SPA}}:\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}_{t}]\leqslant\mathbf{0}

(4.7)

is true, where the inequality holds entry-wise.
Hansen 2005
proposes a test for
\mathsf{H}_{\mathrm{SPA}}
against

\mathsf{H}_{\mathrm{non-SPA}}:\mathbb{E}[X_{t,j}]>0\ \text{for some}\ j\in\{1,\dots,m\}

(4.8)

under the assumptions of
\|\mathbf{X}_{t}\|
having bounded variance and suitable strong mixing coefficients of
(\mathbf{X}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
, see Assumption
B1
in Section
B
of the Appendix. Here
\|\mathbf{x}\|=(\sum_{j=1}^{m}x_{j}^{2})^{1/2}
for a vector
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. In order to account for the possibility of
\|\mathbf{X}_{t}\|
having infinite variance, that is, being regularly varying with index
\kappa\in(1,2)
(c.f., Assumption
B2
in the Appendix), we consider a modification of the test statistic proposed by
Hansen 2005
. Define

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{n}:=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbf{X}_{t},\quad\tilde{\gamma}_{n}:=\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{X}_{t}\|^{2}},\quad\mathbf{T}_{n}:=(T_{n,1},\dots,T_{n,m})^{\prime}=\tilde{\gamma}_{n}^{-1}\mathbf{S}_{n},

(4.9)

and the test statistic

V_{n}^{\mathrm{SPA}}:=\max\left[\max_{j=1,\dots,m}T_{n,j},0\right].

(4.10)

We have the following result.

Theorem 4.2

Suppose that
(\mathbf{X}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
is stationary. Let
V_{n}^{\mathrm{SPA}}
be given by (
4.10
).

1.

Suppose that
\mathsf{H}_{\mathrm{SPA}}
in (
4.7
) holds.

a.

Under Assumption
B1
,

V_{n}^{\mathrm{SPA}}\overset{d}{\to}\frac{\max\left[\max_{j=1,\dots,m}\tilde{Z}_{j},0\right]}{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{X}_{0}\|^{2}]}},\qquad n\to\infty.

where

\tilde{Z_{j}}=Z_{j}\mathbf{1}_{(\mathbb{E}[X_{0,j}]=0)},\qquad j=1,\dots,m,

(4.11)

with
\mathbf{Z}=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{m})^{\prime}
a Gaussian vector provided by Theorem
B.1
in the Appendix.

b.

Under Assumptions
B2
–
B3
with tail index
\kappa\in(1,2)
,

V_{n}^{\mathrm{SPA}}\overset{d}{\to}\frac{\max\left[\max_{j=1,\dots,m}\tilde{\xi}_{\kappa,j},0\right]}{\tilde{\zeta}_{\kappa,2}},\qquad n\to\infty.

where

\tilde{\xi}_{\kappa,j}=\xi_{\kappa,j}\mathbf{1}_{(\mathbb{E}[X_{0,j}]=0)},\qquad j=1,\dots,m,

(4.12)

with
\bm{\xi}_{\kappa}=(\xi_{\kappa,1},\dots,\xi_{\kappa,m})^{\prime}
a
\kappa
-stable random vector and
\tilde{\zeta}_{\kappa,2}^{2}
a strictly positive
\kappa/2
-stable random variable both provided by Theorem
B.2
in the Appendix.

2.

Suppose that
\mathsf{H}_{\mathrm{non-SPA}}
in (
4.8
) holds. Then under either Assumption
B1
or Assumptions
B2
–
B3
with tail index
\kappa\in(1,2)
,

V_{n}^{\mathrm{SPA}}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}\infty,\qquad n\to\infty.

As earlier, critical values for a test based on
V_{n}^{\mathrm{SPA}}
can be computed by a straightforward multivariate extension of the subsampling procedure in Algorithm
3.1
.

Remark 4.3

Under Assumption
B2
the entries of
\mathbf{X}_{t}
may have different tail heaviness. In this case, the entries of
\mathbf{S}_{n}
are of different stochastic orders whereas the normalizing quantity
\tilde{\gamma}_{n}
has stochastic order determined by the smallest of the tail indices, namely the index of regular variation of
\|\mathbf{X}_{t}\|
,
\kappa>0
. Consequently, the limiting distribution of
\mathbf{T}_{n}
in (
4.9
) may be singular with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
when some entries of
\mathbf{X}_{t}
have zero mean. This may affect the power properties of the test based on the statistic
V_{n}^{\mathrm{SPA}}
. An alternative test statistic, similar to the one considered by
Hansen 2005
, could be based on
\mathbf{S}_{n}\oslash\bm{\gamma}_{n}
with
\bm{\gamma}_{n}:=(\gamma_{n,1},\dots,\gamma_{n,m})^{\prime}
,
\gamma_{n,j}=\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{t,j}^{2}}
, and
\oslash
denoting entry-wise division. It remains an open task to derive stable limit results for
(\mathbf{S}_{n},\bm{\gamma}_{n})
under potentially heterogeneous tail heaviness across the entries of
\mathbf{X}_{t}
and, consequently, with entry-wise different rates of convergence.

6
Empirical illustration: Comparisons of risk forecasts for emerging-market foreign exchange rates

Quantification and hedging of foreign currency risk are important for international portfolio management
9
9

9

See, e.g.,
Christensen and
Varneskov 2021
and the references therein.
, and we consider in this section an application of Algorithm
3.1
to comparing risk forecasts for foreign exchange (FX) rate returns. FX rates are typically volatile, and their returns are heavy-tailed. For instance,
Ibragimov
et al. 2013
find that certain emerging-market FX rate returns are regularly varying, and standard confidence intervals for their tail index include values below two; that is, returns may have infinite variance. Among such FX rates is the South Korean won against U.S. dollars (KRWUSD), which we focus on here.
Let
Y_{t}
denote the FX rate log return on day
t
. Given some information set
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
available at time
t-1
denote by
Q_{t-1,\tau}
the
\tau
-quantile of the conditional distribution of
Y_{t}
given
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y_{t}\leqslant Q_{t-1,\tau}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})=\tau,\quad Q_{t-1,\tau}\in\mathcal{F}_{t-1},\quad\tau\in(0,1).

(6.1)

In the context of financial risk management, this quantile is the Value-at-Risk (VaR) at risk level
\tau
. Following Example
2.3
one may evaluate the time
t
VaR forecast using the tick loss function,

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tick},\tau}(Y_{t},Q_{t-1,\tau})=(\tau-\mathbf{1}\{Y_{t}-Q_{t-1,\tau}<0\})(Y_{t}-Q_{t-1,\tau}).

(6.2)

A common approach to quantifying VaR is by means of a GARCH-type model: suppose that
Y_{t}=\sigma_{t}Z_{t}
, with
\sigma_{t}>0
a function of past returns and
Z_{t}
a mean-zero variable independent of
\sigma_{t}\in\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
. Then the VaR of
Y_{t}
is given by
Q_{t-1,\tau}=c_{\tau}\sigma_{t}
, where the constant
c_{\tau}
is the
\tau
-quantile of the distribution of
Z_{t}
. Consequently, the tick loss is

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tick},\tau}(Y_{t},Q_{t-1,\tau})=(\tau-\mathbf{1}\{Z_{t}<c_{\tau}\})(Z_{t}-c_{\tau})\sigma_{t}.

For standard GARCH-type processes,
\sigma_{t}^{2}
can be written as a stochastic recurrence equation (see, e.g., Section
F.1
in the Supplemental Appendix), and under mild conditions,
\sigma_{t}
is regularly varying. By Breiman’s Lemma, the tick loss
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tick},t}(Y_{t},Q_{t-1,\tau})
is also regularly varying with index
\kappa
, and, likewise, loss differentials may be regularly varying.
Similar to
Patton
et al. 2019
, we compare the VaR forecasts from ten different methods using the tick loss function. The methods can be divided into three groups: rolling window-based estimation, GARCH models, and score-driven models. The rolling window methods, labeled ”RW-
H
”, compute the risk forecasts using a simple rolling window of
H
days with

7
Conclusion

Many key variables in economics and finance exhibit heavy-tailed behavior, and the forecast errors and loss differentials derived from them can inherit this feature. We show that standard tools for forecast evaluation, including the widely used Diebold–Mariano test, can be severely unreliable in such settings: under empirically plausible heavy-tailed data-generating processes, a test at the 5% nominal level can reject a true null of equal predictive ability as often as 70% of the time. The root cause is that the standard normal critical values are justified by a finite-variance central limit theorem that breaks down when the loss differentials have infinite variance.

To address this, we develop a novel stable limit theorem for strongly mixing time series processes under infinite (or potentially undefined) variance, extending the classical central limit theorem to the heavy-tailed setting. Building on this result, we derive the limiting distribution of the test statistic, which is a ratio of dependent non-Gaussian random variables. The quantiles of this ratio can differ markedly from those of a standard normal distribution. We then consider a subsampling-based inferential procedure that is valid regardless of tail-heaviness and requires no prior knowledge of the tail index, the convergence rate of the sample mean, or estimation of long-run variances.

An empirical application to risk forecasts for emerging-market foreign exchange rates, where the loss differentials appear to have infinite variance, illustrates the practical relevance: the standard Diebold–Mariano test and the subsampling-based test yield substantively different conclusions about equal predictive ability. In particular, simple rolling-window risk forecasts are not found to be inferior to forecasts based on more sophisticated GARCH and score-driven models once the heavy-tailed nature of the loss differentials is properly accounted for.

Lemma A.1

Let
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a real-valued stationary process. For
n\geqslant 1
and
(u,\lambda)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
, define for
\overline{\ell}\geqslant\ell\geqslant 1

\alpha_{n,\ell,\overline{\ell}}(u,\lambda):=\sup_{A\subset\left\{-n,\dots,-1,0\right\}}\left|\mathrm{cov}\left(\mathrm{e}^{ia_{n}^{-1}u\sum_{t\in A}X_{t}-a_{n}^{-2}\lambda\sum_{t\in A}X_{t}^{2}},\mathrm{e}^{ia_{n}^{-1}u\sum_{t=\ell}^{\overline{\ell}}X_{t}-a_{n}^{-2}\lambda\sum_{t=\ell}^{\overline{\ell}}X_{t}^{2}}\right)\right|.

It holds that for any
(n,u,\lambda)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
\alpha_{n,\ell,\overline{\ell}}(u,\lambda)\leqslant 4\alpha_{\ell}
,
where
(\alpha_{k})_{k\geqslant 1}
are the strong mixing coefficients of
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
defined in (
2.3
).

Proof of Lemma
A.1
:

For the ease of notation, with

\tilde{Y}_{0}:=a_{n}^{-2}\lambda\sum_{t\in A}X_{t}^{2}
,
Y_{\ell}:=a_{n}^{-1}u\sum_{t=\ell}^{\overline{\ell}}X_{t}
, and
\tilde{Y}_{\ell}:=a_{n}^{-2}\lambda\sum_{t=\ell}^{\overline{\ell}}X_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle\mathrm{cov}\left(\mathrm{e}^{ia_{n}^{-1}u\sum_{t\in A}X_{t}-a_{n}^{-2}\lambda\sum_{t\in A}X_{t}^{2}},\mathrm{e}^{ia_{n}^{-1}u\sum_{t=\ell}^{\overline{\ell}}X_{t}-a_{n}^{-2}\lambda\sum_{t=\ell}^{\overline{\ell}}X_{t}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{cov}\left(\cos(Y_{0})\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{Y}_{0}}+i\sin(Y_{0})\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{Y}_{0}},\cos(Y_{\ell})\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{Y}_{\ell}}+i\sin(Y_{\ell})\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{Y}_{\ell}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{cov}\left(\cos(Y_{0})\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{Y}_{0}},\cos(Y_{\ell})\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{Y}_{\ell}}\right)+i\mathrm{cov}\left(\sin(Y_{0})\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{Y}_{0}},\cos(Y_{\ell})\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{Y}_{\ell}}\right)

\displaystyle\left|\mathrm{cov}\left(\mathrm{e}^{ia_{n}^{-1}u\sum_{t\in A}X_{t}-a_{n}^{-2}\lambda\sum_{t\in A}X_{t}^{2}},\mathrm{e}^{ia_{n}^{-1}u\sum_{t=\ell}^{\overline{\ell}}X_{t}-a_{n}^{-2}\lambda\sum_{t=\ell}^{\overline{\ell}}X_{t}^{2}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|\mathrm{cov}\left(\cos(Y_{0})\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{Y}_{0}},\cos(Y_{\ell})\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{Y}_{\ell}}\right)\right|+\left|\mathrm{cov}\left(\sin(Y_{0})\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{Y}_{0}},\cos(Y_{\ell})\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{Y}_{\ell}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\quad+\left|\mathrm{cov}\left(\cos(Y_{0})\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{Y}_{0}},\sin(Y_{\ell})\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{Y}_{\ell}}\right)\right|+\left|\mathrm{cov}\left(\sin(Y_{0})\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{Y}_{0}},\sin(Y_{\ell})\mathrm{e}^{-\tilde{Y}_{\ell}}\right)\right|.

Lemma A.2

Let

\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{m}Z_{i}\right]-(\mathbb{E}[Z_{1}])^{m}\right|\leqslant\sum_{i=2}^{m}\left|\mathrm{cov}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}Z_{j},Z_{i}\right)\right|.

Proof of Lemma

:

For complex-valued

Lemma A.3

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
a_{n},b_{n},c_{n},d_{n}
be real-valued random variables with
\mathbb{P}(c_{n}\geqslant 0)=\mathbb{P}(d_{n}\geqslant 0)=1

\forall n
, and let
(k_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be an integer-valued deterministic sequence satisfying

as

. Define

X_{n}=\mathrm{e}^{ia_{n}-c_{n}},\quad x_{n}=\mathbb{E}[X_{n}],\quad Y_{n}=\mathrm{e}^{ib_{n}-d_{n}},\quad y_{n}=\mathbb{E}[Y_{n}],\quad z_{n}=\mathbb{E}[X_{n}Y_{n}],

,
y_{n}-1=o(1/k_{n})
, and

.
Then
z_{n}^{k_{n}}-x_{n}^{k_{n}}=o(1)
as

.

Proof of Lemma
A.3
:
From the identity in (
A.16
), it holds that

z_{n}^{k_{n}}-x_{n}^{k_{n}}=(z_{n}-x_{n})\left(x_{n}^{k_{n}-1}+\sum_{s=2}^{k_{n}}z_{n}^{s-1}x_{n}^{k_{n}-s}\right),

which gives the bound

|z_{n}^{k_{n}}-x_{n}^{k_{n}}|\leqslant k_{n}|z_{n}-x_{n}|\max(|z_{n}|,|x_{n}|)^{k_{n}-1}.

(A.17)

By assumption, there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
n
sufficiently large
\max(|z_{n}|,|x_{n}|)\leqslant(1+C/k_{n})
,
where we note that
\lim_{n\to\infty}(1+C/k_{n})^{k_{n}-1}=\mathrm{e}^{C}
. Consequently,
\max(|z_{n}|,|x_{n}|)^{k_{n}-1}=O(1)
, and given the bound in (
A.17
), it remains to show that

|z_{n}-x_{n}|=o(1/k_{n}).

(A.18)

We have that
z_{n}-x_{n}=\mathbb{E}[X_{n}(Y_{n}-1)]=\mathrm{cov}(X_{n},Y_{n})+x_{n}(y_{n}-1)
, where the second term is
x_{n}(y_{n}-1)=o(1/k_{n})
by assumption. Hence, (
A.18
) holds provided that

\displaystyle|\mathrm{cov}(X_{n},Y_{n})|=o(1/k_{n}).

(A.19)

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

|\mathrm{cov}(X_{n},Y_{n})|\leqslant\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[|X_{n}-x_{n}|^{2}]\mathbb{E}[|Y_{n}-y_{n}|^{2}]}.

(A.20)

With
{x}^{\ast}
denoting the complex conjugate of complex-valued

, we have that

|X_{n}-x_{n}|^{2}=|X_{n}-1|^{2}+|x_{n}-1|^{2}-2\Re\left((X_{n}-1){(x_{n}-1)}^{\ast}\right),

,

(A.21)

Likewise, by identical arguments,

(A.22)

Combining the bounds in (

)-(

) holds.
\square

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\gamma_{n}}{n^{1/2}\hat{\sigma}_{n}}\dfrac{S_{n}}{\gamma_{n}}=\left(c_{q}+\dfrac{(R_{1}+R_{2})(n/a_{n}^{2})}{a_{n}^{-2}\gamma_{n}^{2}}\right)^{-1/2}\;T_{n},

where by Proposition
2.9
,
T_{n}\overset{d}{\to}\xi_{\kappa}/\zeta_{\kappa/2}
. By Theorem
2.6
,
a_{n}^{-2}\gamma_{n}^{2}
has a limit that is strictly positive almost surely, and it suffices to show that
(R_{1}+R_{2})(n/a_{n}^{2})=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
, where we note that
n/a_{n}^{2}\to 0
. Clearly
R_{1}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
as
n\to\infty
. If
\kappa\in(1,2)
then
R_{2}
consists of
q
averages of zero mean ergodic summands, and, hence, by the ergodic theorem
R_{2}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
as
n\to\infty
. It remains to show that
R_{2}(n/a_{n}^{2})=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
for
\kappa\in(0,1)
. Observe that for any
\varepsilon>0

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(a_{n}^{-2}\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{t}Z_{t+j}>\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n}|X_{t}|\;|Z_{t+j}|>\varepsilon a_{n}^{2}\right)\leqslant\mathbb{P}\left(n\;\max_{t=1,\dots,n}|X_{t}|\;|Z_{t+j}|>\varepsilon a_{n}^{2}\right).

Since
|X_{t}|
and
|Z_{t+j}|
are independent and both are regularly varying with index
\kappa
, an application of Breiman’s lemma yields that their product is also regularly varying with index
\kappa<1
as well. Consequently, by regular variation of
|X_{t}|\;|Z_{t+j}|
and the fact that
a_{n}/n\to\infty
when
\kappa\in(0,1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(a_{n}^{-2}\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{t}Z_{t+j}>\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{t=1}^{n}\{|X_{t}|\;|Z_{t+j}|>\varepsilon\dfrac{a_{n}^{2}}{n}\}\right)\leq n\mathbb{P}\left(|X_{t}|\;|Z_{t+j}|>\varepsilon\dfrac{a_{n}^{2}}{n}\right)

\displaystyle\sim\varepsilon^{-\kappa}n^{1+\kappa}a_{n}^{-2\kappa}L(n^{-1}a_{n}^{2})\to 0,\qquad n\to\infty.

\square

D.1
Auxiliary results for subsampling

Throughout this section, we let
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
denote a regularly varying stationary process with tail index
\kappa\in(1,2)
. Furthermore, let
(a_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
be a sequence of reals chosen such that
n\mathbb{P}(|X_{t}|>a_{n})\to 1
as
n\to\infty
. Define

\gamma_{n,2}^{2}=a_{n}^{-2}\sum_{t=1}^{n}(X_{t}-\mu)^{2}\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad R_{n}=a_{n}^{-1}\dfrac{\sum_{t=1}^{n}(X_{t}-\mu)}{\gamma_{n,2}},\quad\quad n\geq 1,

(D.1)

with
\mu:=\mathbb{E}[X_{t}]
. Assuming that the stationary process
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
satisfies Assumption
3
, it holds by Theorem
2.6
that

\gamma_{n,2}\xrightarrow{d}\zeta_{\kappa/2}\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad R_{n}\xrightarrow{d}\xi_{\kappa}/\zeta_{\kappa/2},\quad\quad n\to\infty,

(D.2)

respectively, where
\zeta_{\kappa/2}
and
\xi_{\kappa}
are defined as in the theorem.

In this section we prove the validity of the subsampling Algorithm
3.1
. We begin with two auxiliary lemmas – Lemmas
D.1
and
D.2
– showing that subsampling techniques can approximate the limiting distributions in (
D.2
).
The proofs of these lemmas follow the same structure as
Politis
et al. 1999
adapted to strongly mixing, regularly varying processes with tail index
\kappa\in(1,2)
. For a block length
b_{n}\geqslant 1
, the subsample versions of (
D.1
) are

\tilde{\gamma}_{i,b_{n}}^{2}=a_{b_{n}}^{-2}\sum_{t=i}^{i+b_{n}-1}(X_{t}-\overline{X}_{n})^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{R}_{i,b_{n}}=\dfrac{a_{b_{n}}^{-1}\sum_{t=i}^{i+b_{n}-1}(X_{t}-\overline{X}_{n})}{\;\tilde{\gamma}_{i,b_{n}}},\quad i=1,\dots,q_{n},

(D.3)

where
q_{n}=n-b_{n}+1
,
\overline{X}_{n}=n^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{t}
, and
(a_{b_{n}})_{n\geqslant 1}
satisfies
b_{n}\,\mathbb{P}(|X_{t}|>a_{b_{n}})\to 1
as
b_{n}\to\infty
. We then show (in Lemmas
D.1
and
D.2
) that the limiting distributions of
\gamma_{n,2}
and
R_{n}
, respectively, can be approximated by

L_{\gamma,b_{n}}(x)=q_{n}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{q_{n}}\mathbf{1}(\tilde{\gamma}_{i,b_{n}}\leqslant x)\quad\text{and}\quad L_{R,b_{n}}(x)=q_{n}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{q_{n}}\mathbf{1}(\tilde{R}_{i,b_{n}}\leqslant x).

(D.4)

The validity of Algorithm
3.1
under the null hypothesis follows by replacing
\overline{X}_{n}
and
\mu
by zeros, whereas the validity of the Algorithm under the alternative hypothesis is demonstrated in the proof of Proposition
D.3
).

Lemma D.1

Let
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
satisfy Assumptions
2
–
3
with tail index
\kappa\in(1,2)
. Furthermore, let
L_{\gamma,b_{n}}(x)
be defined as in (
D.4
) with
(b_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
chosen such that
b_{n}\to\infty
and
b_{n}/n\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Then the following holds.

(1)

For every
x\in\mathbb{R}
, it holds that
L_{\gamma,b_{n}}(x)\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\mathbb{P}(\zeta_{\kappa/2}\leqslant x)
as
n\to\infty
,

(2)

For
\beta\in(0,1)
, define the corresponding quantiles
c_{b_{n}}(1-\beta)=\inf\{x\in\mathbb{R}:L_{\gamma,b_{n}}(x)\geqslant 1-\beta\}
. It holds that
\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{n,2}\leq c_{b_{n}}(1-\beta))\rightarrow 1-\beta
, as

Proof of Lemma
D.1
:

Proof of (1):
Note initially from its definition that
\tilde{\gamma}_{i,b_{n}}
is a.s.  strictly positive. Consequently, it holds that
L_{\gamma,b_{n}}(x)=\mathbb{P}(\zeta_{\kappa/2}\leqslant x)=0
a.s.  for every
x\leqslant 0
. It remains to show that the conclusion of (1) holds for every
x>0
. For this, observe that

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}_{i,b_{n}}^{2}

\displaystyle=a_{b_{n}}^{-2}\sum_{t=i}^{i+b_{n}-1}(X_{t}-\overline{X}_{n})^{2}

\displaystyle=a_{b_{n}}^{-2}\left[\sum_{t=i}^{i+b_{n}-1}(X_{t}-\mu)^{2}+b_{n}(\overline{X}_{n}-\mu)^{2}-2(\overline{X}_{n}-\mu)\sum_{t=i}^{i+b_{n}-1}(X_{t}-\mu)\right],

(D.5)

and introduce the following notation:
\gamma_{i,b_{n}}^{2}=a_{b_{n}}^{-2}\sum_{t=i}^{i+b_{n}-1}(X_{t}-\mu)^{2}
,
S_{i,b_{n}}=\sum_{t=i}^{i+b_{n}-1}(X_{t}-\mu)
and
E_{i,\gamma}=2a_{b_{n}}^{-2}(\overline{X}_{n}-\mu)S_{i,b_{n}}-b_{n}\;a_{b_{n}}^{-2}(\overline{X}_{n}-\mu)^{2}
. Now, inserting (
D.1
) into
L_{\gamma,b_{n}}(x)
in (
D.4
), and using that the events
\{\tilde{\gamma}_{i,b_{n}}\leqslant x\}
and
\{\tilde{\gamma}^{2}_{i,b_{n}}\leqslant x^{2}\}
are equivalent for every
x>0
yields,

\displaystyle L_{\gamma,b_{n}}(x)=q_{n}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{q_{n}}\mathbf{1}\left(\gamma_{i,b_{n}}^{2}\leqslant x^{2}+E_{i,\gamma}\right),\qquad x>0.

We seek to establish upper and lower bounds for
L_{\gamma,b_{n}}(x)
for every
x>0
. To obtain an upper bound, observe that we have the following set relation for every
d>0
,

\big\{\gamma_{i,b_{n}}^{2}\leqslant x^{2}+E_{i,\gamma}\big\}\subset\big\{\gamma_{i,b_{n}}^{2}\leqslant x^{2}+d\big\}\cup\big\{E_{i,\gamma}>d\big\}.

Using this relation and the fact that
(x^{2}+d)^{1/2}\leqslant x+d^{1/2}
for all
x,d>0
, we obtain

L_{\gamma,b_{n}}(x)\leqslant q_{n}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{q_{n}}\mathbf{1}\big(\gamma_{i,b_{n}}\leqslant x+d^{1/2}\big)+q_{n}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{q_{n}}\mathbf{1}\big(E_{i,\gamma}>d\big).

(D.6)

On the other hand, to obtain a lower bound for
L_{\gamma,b_{n}}(x)
for every
x>0
, we initially consider the following set relation

\big\{\gamma_{i,b_{n}}^{2}\leqslant x^{2}+E_{i,\gamma}\big\}\supset\big\{\gamma_{i,b_{n}}^{2}\leqslant x^{2}-d\big\}\cap\big\{E_{i,\gamma}\geqslant-d\big\}.

Next, observe that the function
x\mapsto(x^{2}-d)^{1/2}
is well-defined for every
x\in(d^{1/2},\infty)
and furthermore it holds that
(x^{2}-d)^{1/2}\geqslant x-d^{1/2}
on this interval. Applying these observations, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}\big(\gamma_{i,b_{n}}^{2}\leqslant x^{2}+E_{i,\gamma}\big)

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbf{1}\big(\gamma_{i,b_{n}}^{2}\leqslant x^{2}-d\big)-\mathbf{1}(E_{i,\gamma}<-d)

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbf{1}(\gamma_{i,b_{n}}\leqslant x-d^{1/2})-\mathbf{1}(E_{i,\gamma}<-d),\quad\quad d>0,\;x\in[d^{1/2},\infty).

Since we can choose
d>0
arbitrarily small, we thus obtain the following lower bound for
L_{\gamma,b_{n}}(x)
, which holds for every
x>0
,

L_{\gamma,b_{n}}(x)\geqslant q_{n}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{q_{n}}\mathbf{1}\big(\gamma_{i,b_{n}}\leqslant x-d^{1/2}\big)-q_{n}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{q_{n}}\mathbf{1}\big(E_{i,\gamma}<-d\big).

(D.7)

Next, we seek to show that the term involving
E_{i,\gamma}
is negligible in both relations (
D.6
) and (
D.7
). Since
d>0
in both relations, it suffices to show that
q_{n}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{q_{n}}\mathbf{1}(|E_{i,\gamma}|>d)=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
as
n\to\infty
. By the triangle inequality we have

|E_{i,\gamma}|=|2a_{b_{n}}^{-2}(\overline{X}_{n}-\mu)S_{i,b_{n}}-b_{n}\;a_{b_{n}}^{-2}(\overline{X}_{n}-\mu)^{2}|\leqslant 2a_{b_{n}}^{-2}|\overline{X}_{n}-\mu|\;|S_{i,b_{n}}|+b_{n}\;a_{b_{n}}^{-2}(\overline{X}_{n}-\mu)^{2}.

Therefore, it follows that we have for every
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle q_{n}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{q_{n}}\mathbf{1}\left(|E_{i,\gamma}|>d\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant q_{n}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{q_{n}}\mathbf{1}\left(2a_{b_{n}}^{-2}|\overline{X}_{n}-\mu|\;|S_{i,b_{n}}|+b_{n}\;a_{b_{n}}^{-2}(\overline{X}_{n}-\mu)^{2}>d\right)

\displaystyle=q_{n}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{q_{n}}\mathbf{1}\left(|a_{b_{n}}^{-1}S_{i,b_{n}}|>\dfrac{a_{b_{n}}\;d}{2|\overline{X}_{n}-\mu|}-\dfrac{b_{n}|\overline{X}_{n}-\mu|}{2a_{b_{n}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant q_{n}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{q_{n}}\mathbf{1}\left(|a_{b_{n}}^{-1}S_{i,b_{n}}|>\dfrac{d\,b_{n}/4-\delta^{2}}{\delta}\right)+q_{n}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{q_{n}}\mathbf{1}\left(\dfrac{b_{n}|\overline{X}_{n}-\mu|}{2a_{b_{n}}}>\delta\right).

Now, recall that
a_{n}\sim n^{1/\kappa}L(n)
and
a_{b_{n}}\sim b_{n}^{1/\kappa}L(b_{n})
as
n\to\infty
. An application of Potter’s bounds
(
Bingham
et al. 1989
, Theorem 1.5.6)
yields that
L(n)/L(b_{n})\leqslant c(n/b_{n})^{\epsilon}
for any
c>1,\;\epsilon>0
with
n
sufficiently large. Choosing
\epsilon
such that
1-1/\kappa-\epsilon>0
(which is possible since
\kappa\in(1,2)
),

\lim_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{b_{n}\;a_{n}}{n\;a_{b_{n}}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\dfrac{b_{n}}{n}\right)^{1-1/\kappa}\dfrac{L(n)}{L(b_{n})}\leqslant\lim_{n\to\infty}c\left(\dfrac{b_{n}}{n}\right)^{1-1/\kappa-\epsilon}=0.

(D.8)

Using this together with Theorem
2.6
we have that

\dfrac{b_{n}(\overline{X}_{n}-\mu)}{a_{b_{n}}}=\dfrac{b_{n}\;a_{n}}{n\;a_{b_{n}}}a_{n}^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{n}(X_{t}-\mu)\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0,\quad\quad n\to\infty.

(D.9)

Consequently,
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}(b_{n}|\overline{X}_{n}-\mu|/(2a_{b_{n}})>\delta)]=\mathbb{P}(b_{n}|\overline{X}_{n}-\mu|/(2a_{b_{n}})>\delta)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, for every
\delta>0
, and an application of Lemma
D.5
yields that,

q_{n}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{q_{n}}\mathbf{1}\left(\dfrac{b_{n}|\overline{X}_{n}-\mu|}{2a_{b_{n}}}>\delta\right)\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0,\qquad n\to\infty.

Similarly, noting
|a_{b_{n}}^{-1}S_{i,b_{n}}|=O_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
from Theorem
2.6
, and that
b_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
gives us that
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}(|a_{b_{n}}^{-1}S_{i,b_{n}}|>(d\,b_{n}/4-\delta^{2})/\delta)]\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, and by Lemma
D.5
,

q_{n}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{q_{n}}\mathbf{1}\left(|a_{b_{n}}^{-1}S_{i,b_{n}}|>\dfrac{d\,b_{n}/4-\delta^{2}}{\delta}\right)\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0,\quad\quad n\to\infty.

(D.10)

This proves that for every
\varepsilon>0
it holds that
q_{n}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{q_{n}}\mathbf{1}(|E_{i,\gamma}|>d)<\varepsilon
with probability tending to one.

Using this result with
d>0
sufficiently small together with the relations (
D.6
) and (
D.7
), we have for arbitrarily small

and every

q_{n}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{q_{n}}\mathbf{1}\big(\gamma_{i,b_{n}}\leqslant x-d^{1/2}\big)-\varepsilon\leqslant L_{\gamma,b_{n}}(x)\leqslant q_{n}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{q_{n}}\mathbf{1}\big(\gamma_{i,b_{n}}\leqslant x+d^{1/2}\big)+\varepsilon,

(D.11)

holds, with probability tending to one. Letting

and

go to zero, we note that (1) of the lemma follows by (

) if we can show that

q_{n}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{q_{n}}\mathbf{1}\big(\gamma_{i,b_{n}}\leqslant x\pm d^{1/2}\big)\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\mathbb{P}(\zeta_{\kappa/2}\leq x\pm d^{1/2}),\quad\quad n\to\infty.

(D.12)

The convergence in (

) holds by applications of Chebyshev’s inequality and Portmanteau’s theorem, provided that

Proof of (2):
From (1), we have that
L_{\gamma,b_{n}}(x)\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\mathbb{P}(\zeta_{\kappa/2}\leqslant x)
, and since

has a continuous distribution,

.
Hence, with probability tending to one, it holds for
\varepsilon>0
that
c(1-\beta)-\varepsilon\leqslant c_{b_{n}}(1-\beta)\leqslant c(1-\beta)+\varepsilon
.
Using this result, we have for sufficiently large
n
,

\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{n,2}\leqslant c(1-\beta)-\varepsilon)\leqslant\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{n,2}\leqslant c_{b_{n}}(1-\beta))\leqslant\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{n,2}\leqslant c(1-\beta)+\varepsilon).

Observe for any choice of
\varepsilon>0
, we have that
c(1-\beta)\pm\varepsilon
are continuity points of
\mathbb{P}(\zeta_{\kappa/2}\leqslant x)
. Then since
\gamma_{n,2}\xrightarrow{d}\zeta_{\kappa/2}
, we have by first letting

and then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{n,2}\leqslant c_{b_{n}}(1-\beta))

\displaystyle\geqslant\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{n,2}\leqslant c(1-\beta)-\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\mathbb{P}(\zeta_{\kappa/2}\leqslant c(1-\beta)-\varepsilon)=\mathbb{P}(\zeta_{\kappa/2}\leqslant c(1-\beta))=1-\beta,

\displaystyle\leqslant\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\gamma_{n,2}\leqslant c(1-\beta)+\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\mathbb{P}(\zeta_{\kappa/2}\leqslant c(1-\beta)+\varepsilon)=\mathbb{P}(\zeta_{\kappa/2}\leqslant c(1-\beta))=1-\beta.

\displaystyle=q_{n}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{q_{n}}\mathbf{1}\left(\dfrac{a_{b_{n}}^{-1}S_{i,b_{n}}}{\gamma_{i,b_{n}}}\leqslant(x+y)\Big(\dfrac{\tilde{\gamma}_{i,b_{n}}}{\gamma_{i,b_{n}}}-1\Big)+(x+y)\right)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)

F.1
Affine stochastic recurrence equations

Consider the process for
X_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
given by

X_{t}=A_{t}X_{t-1}+B_{t},\quad t\in\mathbb{Z},

(F.1)

where
((A_{t},B_{t}))_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}

A_{t},B_{t}\in\mathbb{R}

Buraczewski
et al. 2016
and
Mikosch and
Wintenberger 2024

With
(A,B)
denoting generic elements of
((A_{t},B_{t}))_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
, we have the following result

F.2
Stochastic volatility processes

For
X_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
the class of stochastic volatility (SV) processes is given by

X_{t}=\sigma_{t}z_{t},\quad t\in\mathbb{Z},

(F.2)

where, typically,
((\sigma_{t},z_{t}))_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
is stationary and
(\sigma_{t})_{t\leqslant 0}
and
(z_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
are independent. A special case is the stochastic autoregressive volatility process with
X_{t}
given by (
F.2
) and

\log(\sigma_{t})=\omega+\phi\log(\sigma_{t-1})+(\gamma+\beta\log(\sigma_{t-1}))u_{t},

(F.3)

with
\omega,\phi,\gamma,\beta\in\mathbb{R}

\gamma\neq 0
and

(z_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}\ \text{and}\ (u_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}\ \text{are mutually independent iid processes},

(F.4)

see, e.g.,
Carrasco and
Chen 2002
. Note here that the log-volatility process
\log(\sigma_{t})
in (
F.3
) obeys an affine SRE of the form (
F.1
) with

A_{t}=(\phi+\beta u_{t})\quad\text{and}\quad B_{t}=\omega+\gamma u_{t}.

(F.5)

We have the following result.
