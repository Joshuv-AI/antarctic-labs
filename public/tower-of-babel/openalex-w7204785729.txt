UV upconversion luminescence of highly Pr3+-doped silica-based
glasses prepared by gas levitation melting
Justian Chandra1,2, Yuuki Kitagawa1, Mario Affatigato3 and Kenji Shinozaki1,³

Pr 3+-doped aluminosilicate glasses were prepared by gas levitation melting, enabling high rare-earth incorporation beyond the conventional solubility limit of silica-based systems. Under 455 nm excitation, a broad ultraviolet
emission band peaking at ³291 nm was observed and assigned to the 4f 15d1 ¼ 4f 2 transition of Pr 3+, demonstrating UV upconversion in a silica-based glass matrix. The upconversion intensity showed a strong dependence
on Pr 3+ concentration, with a maximum at ³0.1 mol %, while higher concentrations led to signiﬁcant quenching.
Raman spectra revealed that increasing Pr 3+ content induces local structural ordering, indicating limited
solubility and its impact on luminescence performance. The addition of Al2O3 signiﬁcantly enhanced both
upconversion and photoluminescence intensities, with an optimum at intermediate Al content. This enhancement
is rationalized by improved spatial distribution of Pr 3+ ions and suppression of concentration quenching, while
excessive Al results in reduced emission due to structural over-modiﬁcation. These results demonstrate that UV
upconversion in silica-based glasses is governed by the interplay between local structure, dopant concentration,
and host-dependent electronic states. Gas levitation melting combined with compositional control provides an
eﬀective route to expand the accessible composition range and optimize luminescence properties.
Keywords: UV upconversion, Silica-based glass, Gas levitation melting, Pr 3+ ions, 5d–4f transition

1. Introduction
Rare-earth-ion-doped optical materials have been widely
used in solid-state lasers, optical ﬁbers, and radiation detectors.1) Among them, the praseodymium ion (Pr 3+) is
distinguished by its versatile electronic structure, which
enables both intraconﬁgurational 4f 2–4f 2 transitions and
interconﬁgurational 4f 15d1–4f 2 transitions. The latter are
parity allowed and give rise to intense, broadband emission
extending into the ultraviolet (UV) region, depending on
the host environment.1–3) This feature makes Pr 3+ particularly attractive for UV-emitting applications. Pr 3+-doped
materials have also been widely studied for blue-to-UV
upconversion (UC), in which visible excitation populates
higher-energy states.4,5) The emission wavelength and eﬃciency of this process strongly depend on the host matrix,
especially on the relative position of the 4f 15d1 levels with
respect to the 4f 2 manifold. This determines whether the
system favors UV emission or non-radiative relaxation via
lower-lying states such as 1S0.2,3) The generation of UVC
radiation (200–280 nm) through such mechanisms is of
particular interest for mercury-free sterilization technologies. UV upconversion of Pr 3+ has been mainly studied in

Glasses oﬀer several advantages over crystalline materials, including high formability, high transparency, optical
homogeneity, and compatibility with ﬁber and waveguide
geometries. In particular, silica glass enables long optical
interaction lengths, eﬃcient coupling with laser sources,
and direct integration into existing photonic platforms.
Despite these advantages, achieving eﬃcient UV emission
in silica-based glasses remains challenging. The rigid SiO2
network provides limited solubility for rare-earth ions
(RE), which often leads to clustering through RE–O–RE
linkages and results in concentration quenching. The incorporation of Al2O3 into SiO2 glass is known to signiﬁcantly modify both the glass network structure and the
incorporation behavior of rare-earth ions. In pure silica, the
structure consists of a highly polymerized network of
corner-sharing SiO4 tetrahedra, whereas Al3+ ions predominantly enter the network as tetrahedral AlO4¹ units,
providing charge compensation and altering network connectivity and local oxygen coordination.10,11) These structural changes lead to a more homogeneous glass matrix.

structural control is essential for optimizing luminescence

reactions with the container, contamination, and temperature constraints, especially for high melting compositions.
In contrast, gas levitation melting is a containerless technique.12,13) The sample is levitated and heated without
contact, which reduces contamination and enables melting
at very high temperatures followed by rapid cooling, typically on the order of 102–103 K/s. This non-equilibrium
process makes it possible to explore compositions and
dopant concentrations that are diﬃcult to achieve by conventional methods.
In this study, Pr 3+-doped aluminosilicate glasses were
prepared using gas levitation melting. UC and photoluminescence (PL) properties were investigated. Special attention was given to the eﬀects of Pr concentration and Al
content. By correlating the glass structure with the energetic position of the 4f 15d1 state, this work aims to clarify
the factors governing UV emission in silica-based glass
systems.

2. Experimental
SiO2, Al2O3, and Pr6O11 powders were weighed according to the nominal composition [xPrO1.5–yAlO1.5–
(100–x–y)SiO2]. Hereafter, samples with y = 0 and varying x are referred to as xPr, whereas samples with x = 2
and varying y are referred to as yAl. The total batch weight
was 2 g, and the powders were mixed thoroughly. The
mixture was partially melted by CO2 laser irradiation to
prepare precursor pieces. These precursors were then used
for glass preparation by a gas levitation melting method.
Each precursor was placed on a gas nozzle and levitated by
an argon gas ﬂow. While ﬂoating, the sample was heated
and melted by CO2 laser irradiation. The temperature was
monitored using an optical pyrometer and controlled by
adjusting the laser power. The melt was rapidly heated
to about 2000 °C within a few seconds and held at that
temperature for approximately 60 s. After that, the laser
irradiation was stopped, and the melt was cooled to obtain
glass. Afterward, the laser was switched oﬀ, and the melt
was allowed to cool naturally under levitation, resulting in
spherical glass beads with typical diameters of ³1 mm.
Details of the setup are described elsewhere.14,15)
Raman scattering spectra were measured at room temperature using a Raman spectrometer (RAMANtouch VISNIR-OUN, Nanophoton) with a 785 nm excitation laser.
The laser power at the sample surface was 20 mW. The
laser beam was focused on the sample surface using a 20X
objective lens. The spectral resolution was approximately

3. Result and discussion
Raman scattering spectra are shown in Fig. 1. At Pr
concentrations of x = 0–1 mol % (y = 0), a broad band is
observed around 440–460 cm¹1, which is attributed to the
Si–O–Si bending vibration.16) In addition, peaks around
490 and 605 cm¹1 correspond to the D1 and D2 bands characteristic of silica glass.17) A broad feature around 800
cm¹1 is also observed. These spectral features are consistent with a three-dimensional SiO4 network and indicate
that the basic silica structure is preserved in this concentration range.16) When the Pr concentration is increased,
sharp peaks appear around 738 and 950–1000 cm¹1. These
peaks are clearly diﬀerent from the broad bands typical of
glass and indicate increased local structural ordering in the
silicate network. For comparison, ¡-quartz exhibits a
strong peak at ³464 cm¹1, while ¡-cristobalite shows
characteristic peaks at 410–420 cm¹1.18) The absence of
these characteristic bands suggests that the structure deviates from a fully polymerized Q4 SiO2 network. Instead,
the observed Raman features are consistent with locally
modiﬁed SiO4 tetrahedral units, as in pyrosilicate-type
structures, where vibrational modes in the 600–800 cm¹1
region are associated with Si–O–Si bending and symmetric stretching, and those in the 900–1000 cm¹1 region correspond to higher-frequency Si–O stretching modes of
Si2O7 units.16) This indicates that local structural rearrangement occurs at higher Pr concentrations, accompanied by the formation of modiﬁed silicate units containing
Pr. However, as shown later, the introduction of Al2O3
suppresses these structural features.
It is important to consider the concentration scale. In
silica glass, the solubility of rare-earth ions is generally
limited, and uniform dispersion is diﬃcult above about 0.1
mol %. Even 1 mol % (³5 wt %) of rare earth content is
already regarded as a high concentration.19) It should ﬁrst
be noted that the Pr 3+ concentrations investigated in this
study extend to the mol % level, indicating a relatively
wide glass-forming and rare-earth incorporation range in
the present system. This structural evolution at high con653

At low Pr 3+ concentrations (0.02 and 0.1 mol %), both
Stokes PL and UCPL are clearly observed, indicating that
cross-relaxation is not dominant in this regime. The emission intensity reaches a maximum at 0.1 mol %, where a
balance between excitation eﬃciency and non-radiative
losses is achieved. At higher concentrations (²0.2 mol %),
the emission intensity decreases signiﬁcantly due to concentration quenching. Increasing the Pr 3+ concentration
enhances the absorption and population of intermediate
states such as 3P0 and 1D2 but also promotes phononassisted cross-relaxation processes. These processes, involving the intermediate 1D2 and 3Pj ( j = 0, 1, 2) levels,
compete with the upconversion pathway and reduce the
population of higher-energy states responsible for UV
emission. The optimal Pr 3+ concentration for upconversion luminescence in the present system is therefore determined to be approximately 0.1 mol %. This value lies
within the range typically reported for silica-based glasses
(0.05–0.5 mol %), where concentration quenching due to
cross-relaxation becomes signiﬁcant at higher concentrations.20,27,28) However, it should be emphasized that the
present system allows glass formation and luminescence
characterization at much higher Pr 3+ concentrations, extending to ³1 mol %, which is far beyond the conventional
optimal range. In silica-based systems, such high concen656

trations are generally associated with reduced interionic
distances, leading to enhanced cross-relaxation and concentration quenching, and are often linked to the onset of
rare-earth clustering.11,29) Therefore, the observed decrease
in emission intensity is consistent with concentration
quenching associated with Pr 3+–Pr 3+ interactions, often
attributed to clustering of rare earth ions in previous
studies.11,19) Rare-earth silicate systems are known to
exhibit wide liquid immiscibility regions, with monotectic
temperatures on the order of ³1700 °C even at relatively
low rare-earth concentrations.30) Under equilibrium conditions, such immiscibility can lead to liquid–liquid phase
separation, which may subsequently inﬂuence crystallization behavior. However, in the present study, samples were
prepared using a containerless levitation melting technique, which enables melting at very high temperatures
followed by rapid cooling. Such non-equilibrium processing conditions may allow the system to bypass liquid–
liquid phase separation and be frozen as a metastable glass.
These structural modiﬁcations are consistent with the formation of Pr-containing silicate or pyrosilicate-like units,
as suggested by the Raman analysis.
Figure 4(a) shows the UC spectra of the samples with a
ﬁxed Pr 3+ concentration of 2 mol % and varying Al3+ content under 455 nm excitation. In the absence of Al (0Al),
the UC emission is almost completely quenched. It should
be noted that the sample without Al2O3 exhibits a distinct
structural feature in Raman spectra compared to the Alcontaining compositions. With increasing Al content, the
emission intensity increases signiﬁcantly and reaches a
maximum at 0.33 mol % Al, followed by a decrease at
higher Al concentrations (0.67 mol %), and then almost
quenched at 1.33 mol % Al. This enhancement can be
rationalized by structural modiﬁcation of the glass network
by Al addition.19,29) As suggested by the Raman results, Al
incorporation leads to the formation of AlO4¹ units and
promotes a more homogeneous distribution of Pr 3+ ions.
As a result, the average distance between Pr 3+ ions increases, suppressing cross-relaxation processes and improving emission eﬃciency.19) However, at higher Al contents (e.g., 1.33 mol %), the emission intensity decreases
again. This behavior deviates from previous reports on
sol–gel-derived silica systems19) and suggests that excessive Al modiﬁes the local coordination environment and
site symmetry of Pr 3+ in a way that increases non-radiative
decay or alters transition probabilities. Figure 4(b) shows
the corresponding Stokes PL spectra. The PL intensity

sensitive to concentration-dependent interactions than

Pr 3+ ions, Al suppresses interionic interactions and enhances the eﬃciency of excitation to the 4f 15d1 state. At
excessive Al concentrations, however, changes in local
coordination and site symmetry may alter the crystal ﬁeld
and increase non-radiative decay rates, resulting in reduced
emission intensity. Therefore, the luminescence behavior
in the present system is governed by the interplay between
host-dependent positioning of the 4f 15d1 level, concentration-dependent non-radiative processes, and structural
modiﬁcation induced by Al addition. This uniﬁed interpretation consistently explains the observed spectral features and their dependence on composition.

4. Conclusion
3+

Pr -doped aluminosilicate glasses were successfully
prepared by gas levitation melting, enabling high rareearth incorporation beyond the conventional solubility
limit of silica-based systems. A broad ultraviolet emission
band peaking at ³291 nm was observed under 455 nm
excitation and assigned to the 4f 15d1 ¼ 4f 2 transition of
Pr 3+, conﬁrming that UV upconversion can be realized in
silica-based glasses. The upconversion intensity showed a
strong dependence on Pr 3+ concentration, with an optimum at ³0.1 mol %, while higher concentrations resulted
in signiﬁcant quenching. Raman spectra indicated that
high Pr 3+ concentrations induce local structural ordering
or segregation, which is consistent with reduced luminescence eﬃciency. Levitation melting enables Pr 3+ incorporation beyond the conventional concentration range in
silica-based glasses. Without Al2O3, structural changes
emerge above ³1 mol % Pr, whereas Al2O3 addition
allows retention of glassy spectral features even at higher
concentrations (e.g., 2 mol %). The emission intensity
reached a maximum at ³0.33 mol % Al, reﬂecting improved spatial distribution of Pr 3+ ions and suppression of
concentration quenching. In contrast, excessive Al addition reduced emission intensity, suggesting that overmodiﬁcation of the local structure increases non-radiative
processes. These results demonstrate that UV upconversion in silica-based glasses is governed by the interplay
between host-dependent 4f 15d1 level positioning, concentration-dependent non-radiative interactions, and structural
modiﬁcation of the glass network. Gas levitation melting
combined with compositional control provides an eﬀective approach to expand the accessible composition range
and optimize luminescence performance in silica-based
systems.
