Cylindrical Projections of Occupied Diffusions
Valentin Tissot-Daguette and Xin Zhang

Abstract
Occupied diffusions offer a Markovian framework for path-dependent dynamics by lifting the state space with a flow of occupation measures. Because this additional feature is infinite-dimensional, the simulation of these processes remains computationally intractable. We address this by introducing cylindrical projections , which approximate the occupation flow via a finite-dimensional system. We establish the strong convergence of this approximation to the initial process and derive corresponding convergence rates. The method is validated through Euler–Maruyama simulations of self-interacting diffusions and an application to the Local Occupied Volatility (LOV) model in finance. Finally, we provide a weak error analysis and explore its consequences for Monte Carlo methods and derivatives pricing.



Keywords.
Occupation measure, cylindrical projections, error analysis, stochastic simulation, self-interacting diffusions, path-dependent volatility

Introduction

Many stochastic dynamics arising in finance, engineering, and biology are

path-dependent
: the present evolution depends not only on the current state, but also on the past trajectory. They appear naturally across applications: in finance for volatility models with history-dependent dynamics
[
14
,
18
,
19
,
17
]
; in engineering and biology for delayed dynamics and systems
[
13
,
28
]
. To capture path-dependent phenomena, a natural approach is to work on the path space directly
[
10
]
, enabling the analysis of such systems via path-dependent partial differential equations (PPDEs)
[
11
,
12
]
. While this framework offers remarkable generality, it entails considerable computational complexity and analytical challenges around the regularity of path functionals, such as solutions of PPDEs.

An alternative, proposed in
[
26
,
25
]
(see also
[
5
,
24
]
), consists of enlarging the state space by a
measure-valued variable, the
occupation flow
, which records the time spent by the process
in arbitrary regions. One then recovers a Markovian framework that captures a specific form of path dependence arising from occupation times.
In this work, we shall focus on

occupied diffusions
, solving stochastic differential equations (SDEs) of the form

\begin{cases}d\calO_{t}=\delta_{X_{t}}\,\lambda(\calO_{t},X_{t})\,dt,\\[3.00003pt]
dX_{t}=b(\calO_{t},X_{t})\,dt+\sigma(\calO_{t},X_{t})\,dW_{t},\end{cases}

(1.1)

where
X_{t}\in\R^{d}
and
\calO_{t}
is a finite measure on
\R^{d}

that encodes the spatial distribution of the process’s history. With
\calO_{0}=0
,
\lambda\equiv 1
, and
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\calO_{t}(A)=|\{s\in[0,t]\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\,X_{s}\in A\}|
is the amount of time
X
has spent in
A
up to time
t
. The well-posedness of
(
1.1
)
was established in
[
26
]
, alongside several financial applications.
As argued in
[
27
]
, the path dependence of the diffusion coefficient
\sigma
(through
\calO
) gives rise to a subclass of path-dependent volatility models
[
14
,
18
,
19
,
17
]
. See also
Section

4.3
.

While (
1.1
) is Markov on
\calM(\R^{d})\times\R^{d}
, with
\calM(\R^{d})
denoting the set of finite measures on
\R^{d}
, direct simulation is difficult since

\calO_{t}
is infinite-dimensional.
A practical numerical method must therefore replace
\calO_{t}
by a finite-dimensional representation,
while controlling the induced error. This is the central objective of the present work. We here equip
\calM(\R^{d})
with a
cylindrical
structure, i.e., taking a countable family of test functions
(f_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, we identify any finite measure
\mathfrak{o}\in\calM(\R^{d})
with a sequence
(\mathfrak{o}(f_{k}))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, where
\mathfrak{o}(f)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\int f(x)\,\mathfrak{o}(dx)
denotes the integration of
f
with respect to
\mathfrak{o}
. For any truncation level
K
, we then heuristically approximate
\calO_{t}
in (
1.1
) by a finite dimensional process
Z_{t}^{K}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(\calO_{t}(f_{1}),\dotso,\calO_{t}(f_{K}))
. In Section 3, we introduce approximations
(X^{K}_{t},Z_{t}^{K})
as solutions to finite dimensional stochastic differential equations (
2.3
), which we call cylindrical projections of the occupied diffusion (
1.1
). In Theorem
3.1
, under standard Lipschitz assumptions on
(\lambda,b,\sigma)
, we prove that for any fixed horizon
T\in(0,\infty)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|X_{t}-X^{K}_{t}|^{2}\right]^{1/2}\leq\frac{C}{K},\quad\text{for some positive constant $C$.}

By embedding
(Z_{t}^{K})
to the space of positive measures, we obtain the same convergence rate from
(Z_{t}^{K})
to
(\calO_{t})
with respect to a cylindrical norm similar to
[
24
]
.

In Section 4, we implement an Euler–Maruyama scheme for the cylindrical projections and investigate several examples:
Cranston–Le Jan and Raimond self-attracting diffusions
[
8
,
23
]
, as well as a variant of the
local occupied volatility (LOV) model
[
27
]
.
These experiments illustrate the quantitative convergence of cylindrical projections. Motivated by Monte Carlo methods and derivatives pricing, we include in Section 5 a weak error analysis of the proposed projection of OSDEs.

Notations.

Let
d\geq 1

\calM=\calM(\R^{d})
be the space of finite Borel measures in
\R^{d}
. Introduce the dual pairing

\mathfrak{o}(\phi)=\int_{\R^{d}}\phi(x)\mathfrak{o}(dx),\quad\mathfrak{o}\in\calM,\quad\phi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\R^{d}\to\R,

whenever the above is well-defined.
Write

for the open ball of radius

Definition 2.1
.

Let
(g_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C_{b}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a separating family such that
g_{0}
is a constant function and

\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\lVert g_{j}\rVert^{2}_{\calC^{1}}\leq 1.
We then
define the
cylindrical norm
associated to

by

\lVert\mathfrak{o}\rVert\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\Big(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}|\mathfrak{o}(g_{j})|^{2}\Big)^{1/2},\quad\mathfrak{o}\in\calM(\R^{d}).

(2.1)

Given
(\mathfrak{o},x)\in\calM\times\R^{d}
, we also write
\lVert(\mathfrak{o},x)\rVert\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(\lVert\mathfrak{o}\rVert^{2}+|x|^{2})^{1/2}
.

Fix a finite horizon
T>0
and a stochastic basis
(\Omega,\calF,\F,\Q)
, where
\F=(\calF_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
satisfies the usual conditions.
Introduce the occupied stochastic differential equations (OSDE),

\displaystyle\begin{cases}d\calO_{t}=\delta_{X_{t}}\lambda(\calO_{t},X_{t})\,dt,&\quad\calO_{0}=\mathfrak{o}\in\calM,\\[2.5pt]
\,dX_{t}=b(\calO_{t},X_{t})\,dt+\sigma(\calO_{t},X_{t})\,dW_{t},&\quad X_{0}=x\in\R^{d},\end{cases}

(2.2)

where
b\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\calM\times\R^{d}\to\R^{d}
,
\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\calM\times\R^{d}\to\R^{d\times d}
, and
W
is standard Brownian motion in
\R^{d}
. The map
\lambda\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\calM\times\R^{d}\to\R_{+}
describes the rate of the stochastic clock
\Lambda_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\int_{0}^{t}\lambda(\calO_{t},X_{t})dt
. In particular, setting
\lambda\equiv 1
(i.e.,
\Lambda_{t}=t
) leads to the
calendar time occupation flow
,

\calO_{t}(A)=\mathfrak{o}(A)+\int_{0}^{t}\delta_{X_{s}}(A)ds=\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{1}_{A}(X_{s})ds,\quad A\in\calB(\R^{d}),\quad t\in[0,T].

If
\lambda=\text{tr}(\sigma\sigma^{\top})
, then
\Lambda_{t}=\text{tr}(\langle X\rangle_{t})
is the (aggregate) quadratic variation clock. Under the following assumption, (
2.2
) admits a unique strong solution
(\calO,X)
; see
[
24
, Lemma 3.2]
.

Assumption 2.1
.

The coefficients
\lambda,b,\sigma
satisfy the following linear growth and Lipschitz conditions: there exists
L>0
such that for all
\phi\in\{\lambda,b,\sigma\}
,

\displaystyle|\phi(\mathfrak{o},x)|

\displaystyle\leq L(1+\lVert(\mathfrak{o},x)\rVert),

\displaystyle|\phi(\mathfrak{o},x)-\phi(\mathfrak{o}^{\prime},x^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq L\lVert(\mathfrak{o}-\mathfrak{o}^{\prime},x-x^{\prime})\rVert.

When
\phi=\sigma
, then
|\sigma(\mathfrak{o},x)|
denotes the Frobenius norm of
\sigma(\mathfrak{o},x)\in\R^{d\times d}
.

2.1
Cylindrical Projection

Similar to
7
, for any
K\in\mathbb{N}
, take an open cover
(U_{k}^{K})_{\{k=1,\dotso,N_{K}\}}
of
B_{R+1}
such that for every
k\leq N_{K}
,
U_{k}^{K}\subset B_{1/K}(x_{k}^{K})
for some point
x_{k}^{K}\in B_{R+1}
. Set also
U_{0}^{K}=\R^{d}\setminus B_{R+1}
,
x_{0}^{K}\in\partial B_{R+1}
,
and choose a partition of unity
(f^{K}_{k})
subordinate to
(U_{k}^{K})_{\{k=0,\dotso,N_{K}\}}
, i.e.,

f_{k}^{K}\geq 0,\quad\;\sum_{k=0}^{N_{K}}f^{K}_{k}\equiv 1,\;\ \ \ \text{supp}(f^{K}_{k})\subset U_{k}^{K},\ \ k=0,\dotso,N_{K},

with
\text{supp}(f)=\overline{\{x\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\,f(x)>0\}}
.
Introduce also the vector-valued function
\bm{f}^{K}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\R^{d}\to\R^{N_{K}+1}
,
\bm{f}^{K}(x)=(f^{K}_{0}(x),\dotso,f^{K}_{N_{K}}(x))
.
The partition of unity is then used to project the occupation measure
\calO_{t}\in\calM
onto the finite-dimensional vector

Z_{t}^{K}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\calO_{t}(\bm{f}^{K})=(\calO_{t}(f_{0}),\ldots,\calO_{t}(f_{N_{K}}))\in\R^{N_{K}+1}.

Conversely, given
z\in\R^{N_{K}+1}
, an occupation measure is reconstructed through the lift

\displaystyle\mathfrak{o}^{K}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\R^{N_{K}+1}\to\calM,\quad z\mapsto\mathfrak{o}^{K}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{N_{K}}z_{k}^{K}\delta_{x_{k}^{K}}\in\calM.

For
\phi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\calM\times\R^{d}\to\R
,
define the projected map
\phi^{K}(z,x)=\phi(\mathfrak{o}^{K}(z),x)
,
(z,x)\in\mathbb{\R}^{N_{K}+1}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We then approximate OSDE (
2.2
) by the
projected OSDE
,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}dZ_{t}^{K}=\bm{f}^{K}(X_{t}^{K})\lambda^{K}(Z_{t}^{K},X_{t}^{K})\,dt,&\quad z_{0}=\mathfrak{o}(\bm{f}^{K}),\\[5.0pt]
dX_{t}^{K}=b^{K}(Z_{t}^{K},X_{t}^{K})\,dt+\sigma^{K}(Z_{t}^{K},X_{t}^{K})\,dW_{t},&\quad X^{K}_{0}=x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\end{cases}

(2.3)

with initial conditions
x,\mathfrak{o}
as in (
2.2
). Since
(\calO,X)
, solution to (
2.2
), is Markov, the system (
2.3
) is Markov as well.

2.2
Simulation Scheme

The simulation of trajectories from the projected SDE (
2.3
) is straightforward and summarized in
Algorithm

1
.
The Euler-Maruyama scheme is used in Step III. to update the process. Alternatively, exact or higher order schemes could be used depending on
\lambda,b,\sigma
; see
[
21
, Chapter 10]
.

In Step III 1. of
Algorithm

1
, the occupation vector
Z^{K}_{\text{\tiny tmp}}
is a temporary variable reused at each iteration. Indeed, as the joint process
(Z^{K},X^{K})
is Markov, updating
X
only requires the current occupation times rather than the entire history. This provides a significant computational and storage advantage over general path-dependent SDEs, where the state-space dimension typically grows with each time step.

Algorithm 1

Euler-Maruyama Scheme, Projected OSDEs (one sample)

Given:

x_{0}\in\R^{d}
,
T>0
,
N\in\N
,
\{f_{k}\}_{k\in\N}
(separating family),
K\in\N
(truncation level).

I.

Initialize
X_{0}^{K}=x_{0}
and temporary variable
Z^{K}_{\text{\tiny tmp}}=0\in\R^{N_{K}+1}
.

II.

Generate
independent Brownian increments
\delta W_{n}=W_{t_{n+1}}-W_{t_{n}}\in\R^{d}
,
n<N
.

III.

For

n=0,\ldots,N-1
:

1.

Z^{K}_{\text{\tiny tmp}}\ \longleftarrow\ Z^{K}_{\text{\tiny tmp}}+\lambda^{K}(Z^{K}_{\text{\tiny tmp}},X_{t_{n}}^{K})\bm{f}^{K}(X_{t_{n}}^{K})\delta t
,

2.

X_{t_{n+1}}^{K}\ =\ X_{t_{n}}^{K}+b^{K}(Z^{K}_{\text{\tiny tmp}},X_{t_{n}}^{K})\delta t+\sigma^{K}(Z^{K}_{\text{\tiny tmp}},X_{t_{n}}^{K})\delta W_{n}
.

IV.

Return

X^{K}=(X_{t_{0}}^{K},\ldots,X_{t_{N}}^{K})
and
Z^{K}_{\text{\tiny tmp}}=\calO_{T}(\bm{f}^{K})
.

3.1
Lenglart’s Inequality Revisited

Toward the proof of
Proposition

3.1

defined in (

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
Y_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sqrt{1+\lVert(\calO_{t},X_{t})\rVert^{2}}
, and
\tilde{\tau}_{R}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\inf\{t\geq 0\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\,Y_{t}\geq R\}

is the strong solution to (

Then for any

\displaystyle\Q(\tau_{R}\leq T)\leq\Q(\tilde{\tau}_{R+1}\leq T)\leq\frac{e^{\iota\gamma T}(2-\gamma)\E^{\Q}[|Y_{0}|^{3\gamma}]}{(R+1)^{3\gamma}(1-\gamma)}\leq\frac{4e^{\iota\gamma T}(2-\gamma)\E^{\Q}[|Y_{0}|^{3\gamma}]}{R^{3\gamma}(1-\gamma)},

(3.5)

where

Proof.

The first and third inequalities of (
3.5
) are straightforward. So we only prove the second inequality. Let us consider
Z_{t}=e^{-\iota t}Y_{t}^{3}
with some positive
\iota
to be specified later. According to Itô’s formula,

\displaystyle dY_{t}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2Y_{t}}\left(2X_{t}\,dX_{t}+2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\calO_{t}(g_{j})g_{j}(X_{t})\lambda(\calO_{t},X_{t})\,dt+\sigma(\calO_{t},X_{t})^{2}\,dt\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2Y_{t}^{3}}\left(X_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}(\calO_{t},X_{t})\,dt\right),

and also that

\displaystyle dZ_{t}=

\displaystyle-\iota Z_{t}\,dt+3e^{-\iota t}Y_{t}^{2}\,dY_{t}+3e^{-\iota t}Y_{t}d\langle Y\rangle_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-\iota t}\left(-\iota Y_{t}^{3}+3Y_{t}^{2}\left(\frac{2X_{t}b(\calO_{t},X_{t})+2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\calO_{t}(g_{j})g_{j}(X_{t})\iota(\calO_{t},X_{t})+\sigma(\calO_{t},X_{t})^{2}}{2Y_{t}}\right)\right)dt

\displaystyle+e^{-\iota t}\left(-\frac{X_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}(\calO_{t},X_{t})}{2Y_{t}^{3}}+\frac{3X_{t}^{2}\sigma(\calO_{t},X_{t})^{2}}{Y_{t}}\right)\,dt+\text{local martingale}.

Note that
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\calO_{t}(g_{j})g_{j}(X_{t})\leq\lVert\calO_{t}\rVert

\displaystyle\E^{\Q}[Z_{\tau}\mathbbm{1}_{A}]\leq\E^{\Q}[Z_{0}\mathbbm{1}_{A}].

Applying

We then conclude that

\displaystyle\Q(\tilde{\tau}_{R}\leq T)

\displaystyle=\Q\left(\sup_{t\leq T}|Y_{t}|\geq R^{3\gamma}\right)\leq\frac{\E^{\Q}\left[\sup_{t\leq T}Y_{t}^{3\gamma}\right]}{R^{3\gamma}}\leq\frac{e^{\iota\gamma T}(2-\gamma)\E^{\Q}[Y_{0}^{3\gamma}]}{(1-\gamma)R^{3\gamma}}.

Proof of
Proposition

3.1
.

Let
\bar{X}=X-X^{\tau_{R}}
and
\bar{\calO}=\calO-\calO^{\tau_{R}}
. In view of the definitions of
(\calO^{\tau_{R}},X^{\tau_{R}})
and
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\Q}
, we have

\displaystyle\lVert(\calO,X)-(\calO^{\tau_{R}},X^{\tau_{R}})\rVert^{2}_{\Q}=\E^{\Q}\left[\sup_{\tau_{R}\leq t\leq T}\lVert(\bar{\calO}_{t},\bar{X}_{t})\rVert^{2}\right].

Using the strong Markov property of (
2.2
) and conditioning on
\tau_{R}=t_{0}
,
(\calO_{t_{0}},X_{t_{0}})=(x,\mathfrak{o})
with
\lVert(x,\mathfrak{o})\rVert^{2}=R^{2}-1

(\calO_{t},X_{t})_{t\geq t_{0}}
solves the OSDE

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\calO_{t}=\mathfrak{o}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\delta_{X_{s}}\lambda(\calO_{s},X_{s})\,ds,\\[2.5pt]
X_{t}=x+\int^{t}_{t_{0}}b(\calO_{s},X_{s})\,dt+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\sigma(\calO_{s},X_{s})\,dW_{s}.\end{cases}

It is then straightforward to show that

\displaystyle|\bar{X}_{t}|^{2}\leq 2(t-t_{0})\int_{t_{0}}^{t}b^{2}(\bar{\calO}_{s}+\mathfrak{o},\bar{X}_{s}+x)\,ds+2|M_{t}|^{2},

with the martingale
M_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\sigma(\bar{\mathfrak{o}}+\calO_{s},x+\bar{X}_{s})\,dW_{s}
. From the linear growth of

\displaystyle|\bar{X}_{t}|^{2}\leq 2(t-t_{0})L^{2}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\left(R^{2}+\sup_{t_{0}\leq u\leq s}\lVert(\bar{\calO}_{u},\bar{X}_{u})\rVert^{2}\right)\,ds+2|M_{t}|^{2}.

Taking supremum over
t
on both sides and applying Doob’s martingale inequality,

\displaystyle\E^{\Q}\left[\sup_{t_{0}\leq s\leq t}|\bar{X}_{s}|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq[2(t-t_{0})+8]L^{2}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\left(R^{2}+\E^{\Q}\left[\sup_{t_{0}\leq u\leq s}\lVert(\bar{\calO}_{u},\bar{X}_{u})\rVert^{2}\right]\right)ds.

Using the definition of cylindrical norm,

\displaystyle\lVert\bar{\calO}_{s}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{t_{0}}^{s}g_{j}(X_{u})\lambda(\calO_{u},X_{u})\,du\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{t_{0}}^{s}|g_{j}(X_{u})|^{2}\,du\right)\left(\int_{t_{0}}^{s}\lambda(\calO_{u},X_{u})^{2}\,du\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{t_{0}}^{s}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}|g_{j}(X_{u})|^{2}\,du\right)\left(\int_{t_{0}}^{s}\lambda(\calO_{u},X_{u})^{2}\,du\right)

\displaystyle=(s-t_{0})\int_{t_{0}}^{s}\lambda(\calO_{u},X_{u})^{2}\,du,

recalling that
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\lVert g_{j}\rVert^{2}_{\calC^{1}}\leq 1
,
and hence

\displaystyle\E^{\Q}\left[\sup_{t_{0}\leq s\leq t}\lVert\bar{\calO}_{s}\rVert^{2}\right]\leq(t-t_{0})L^{2}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\left(R^{2}+\E^{\Q}\left[\sup_{t_{0}\leq u\leq s}\lVert(\bar{\calO}_{u},\bar{X}_{u})\rVert^{2}\right]\right)ds.

We therefore obtain the inequality

\displaystyle\E^{\Q}\left[\sup_{t_{0}\leq s\leq t}\left(|\bar{X}_{s}|^{2}+\lVert\bar{\calO}_{s}\rVert^{2}\right)\right]\leq L^{2}(3(t-t_{0})+8)\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\left(R^{2}+\E^{\Q}\left[\sup_{t_{0}\leq u\leq s}\lVert(\bar{\calO}_{u},\bar{X}_{u})\rVert^{2}\right]\right)ds.

Hence by Grönwall inequality, with some positive constant
C
depending on
L
and
T

\displaystyle\E^{\Q}\left[\sup_{t_{0}\leq s\leq t}\left(|\bar{X}_{s}|^{2}+\lVert\bar{\calO}_{s}\rVert^{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq 8L^{2}\left((t-t_{0})^{2}+(t-t_{0})\right)R^{2}\exp\left(8L^{2}\left((t-t_{0})^{2}+(t-t_{0})\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\left((t-t_{0})^{2}+(t-t_{0})\right)R^{2}.

Thus, we obtain that

\displaystyle\lVert(X,\calO)-(\calO^{\tau_{R}},X^{\tau_{R}})\rVert_{\Q}^{2}\leq CR^{2}\E^{\Q}\left[((T-\tau_{R})^{+})^{2}+(T-\tau_{R})^{+}\right].

By Lemma
3.1
,

\displaystyle\E^{\Q}[((T-\tau_{R})^{+})^{2}+(T-\tau_{R})^{+}]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}(2u+1)\Q[\tau_{R}\leq T-u]\,du

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(T^{2}+1)e^{\iota\gamma T}(2-\gamma)(1+|X_{0}|^{2}+\lVert\calO_{0}\rVert^{2})^{1.5\gamma}}{R^{3\gamma}(1-\gamma)},

and setting
\gamma=0.9
finally leads to

\lVert(X,\calO)-(\calO^{\tau_{R}},X^{\tau_{R}})\rVert_{\Q}^{2}\leq\frac{C(1+|x|^{2}+\lVert\mathfrak{o}\rVert^{2})^{1.35}}{R^{0.7}}.

∎

Proof of Theorem
3.1

We now prove our main result, which we decompose into the following steps.

Proof.

Step 1.
Recall that
Z^{K}_{0}=\mathfrak{o}(\bm{f}^{K})
. Let us estimate the difference of initial positions

\displaystyle\lVert\mathfrak{o}-\mathfrak{o}^{K}(Z^{K}_{0})\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}|\mathfrak{o}(g_{j})-\mathfrak{o}^{K}(Z_{0}^{K})(g_{j})|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left|\mathfrak{o}(g_{j})-\sum_{k=1}^{N_{K}}g_{j}(x^{K}_{k})\mathfrak{o}(f^{K}_{k})\right|^{2},

where we used that
\text{Supp}(\mathfrak{o})\subset B_{R}
and thus
\mathfrak{o}(f^{K}_{0})=0
. Notice that

\displaystyle\Big|\mathfrak{o}(g_{j})-\sum_{k=1}^{N_{K}}g_{j}(x^{K}_{k})\mathfrak{o}(f^{K}_{k})\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\Big|\sum_{k=1}^{N_{K}}\left(\int|g_{j}(x)-g_{j}(x^{K}_{k})|f^{K}_{k}(x)\,d\mathfrak{o}(x)\right)\Big|\leq\frac{1}{K}||g_{j}^{\prime}||_{\infty}.

Since
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}||g_{j}||_{\calC^{1}}^{2}\leq 1
, we obtain that
\lVert\mathfrak{o}-\mathfrak{o}^{K}(Z^{K}_{0})\rVert^{2}\leq 1/K^{2}
.

Step 2.
For the simplicity of notation, in the rest we omit the stopping time
\tau_{R},\tau_{R}^{K}
in the superscript of
X^{\tau_{R}},\calO^{\tau_{R}},X^{K,\tau^{K}_{R}},Z^{K,\tau^{K}_{R}}
.
Let us denote
\theta_{t}=\calO_{t}-\mathfrak{o}^{K}(Z_{t}^{K})
,
\varepsilon_{t}=X_{t}-X^{K}_{t}
, and then

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\lVert\theta_{t}\rVert^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\theta_{t}(g_{j})\int g_{j}(x)\,\frac{\theta_{t}(dx)}{dt}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\theta_{t}(g_{j})g_{j}(X_{t})\lambda(\calO_{t},X_{t})

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\theta_{t}(g_{j})\left(\sum_{k=0}^{N_{K}}g_{j}(x_{k}^{K}){f}_{k}^{K}(X_{t}^{K})\right)\lambda(\mathfrak{o}^{K}(Z_{t}^{K}),X_{t}^{K})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\theta_{t}(g_{j})(g_{j}(X_{t})-g_{j}(X_{t}^{K}))\lambda(\calO_{t},X_{t})

(I)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\theta_{t}(g_{j})\left(g_{j}(X_{t}^{K})-\sum_{k=0}^{N_{K}}g_{j}(x_{k}^{K}){f}_{k}^{K}(X_{t}^{K})\right)\lambda(\calO_{t},X_{t})

(II)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\theta_{t}(g_{j})\left(\sum_{k=0}^{N_{K}}g_{j}(x_{k}^{K}){f}_{k}^{K}(X_{t}^{K})\right)(\lambda(\calO_{t},X_{k})-\lambda(\mathfrak{o}^{K}(Z_{t}^{K}),X_{t}^{K})).

(III)

The term (

) is bounded from above by

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left|\theta_{t}(g_{j})\right|\lVert\nabla g_{j}\rVert_{\infty}|\varepsilon_{t}||\lambda(\calO_{t},X_{t})|

\displaystyle\leq|\varepsilon_{t}||\lambda(\calO_{t},X_{t})|\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left|\theta_{t}(g_{j})\right|\lVert\nabla g_{j}\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq|\varepsilon_{t}||\lambda(\calO_{t},X_{t})|\lVert\theta_{t}\rVert.

For any
x\in B_{R}
, according to our construction of
f_{k}^{K}
, for any
x\in B_{R}
,

\left|g_{j}(x)-\sum_{k=0}^{N_{K}}g_{j}(x_{k}^{K})\bm{f}_{k}^{K}(x)\right|\leq\frac{\lVert\nabla g_{j}\rVert_{\infty}}{K}.

Keeping in mind that both
X_{t}
and
X^{K}
are stopped within the ball

, the term of (

) is bounded from above by

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}|\theta_{t}(g_{j})|\frac{\lVert\nabla g_{j}\rVert_{\infty}}{K}|\lambda(\calO_{t},X_{t})|\leq\frac{1}{K}|\lambda(\calO_{t},X_{t})|\lVert\theta_{t}\rVert.

Using the Lipschitz property of
\lambda
, the term (
III
) is bounded by

\displaystyle L\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}|\theta_{t}(g_{j})|\lVert g_{j}\rVert_{\infty}(\lVert\theta_{t}\rVert+|\varepsilon_{t}|)\leq L(\lVert\theta_{t}\rVert+|\varepsilon_{t}|)\lVert\theta_{t}\rVert.

Together, we have the estimate

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\lVert\theta_{t}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2|\lambda(\calO_{t},X_{t})|\left(\frac{1}{K}+|\varepsilon_{t}|\right)\lVert\theta_{t}\rVert+2L(\lVert\theta_{t}\rVert+|\varepsilon_{t}|)\lVert\theta_{t}\rVert

\displaystyle\leq 2LR\left(\frac{1}{K}+|\varepsilon_{t}|\right)\lVert\theta_{t}\rVert+2L(\lVert\theta_{t}\rVert+|\varepsilon_{t}|)\lVert\theta_{t}\rVert,

where we used the linear growth property of

and
\sqrt{1+|X_{t}|^{2}+||\calO||^{2}}\leq R
. From
Step 1
, we have
||\theta_{0}||^{2}\leq 1/K^{2}
. In view of
\frac{2LR||\theta_{t}||}{K}\leq LR||\theta_{t}||^{2}+\frac{LR}{K^{2}}
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\lVert\theta_{t}\rVert^{2}\leq\frac{CR}{K^{2}}+CR\int_{0}^{t}\lVert\theta_{s}\rVert^{2}+|\varepsilon_{s}|^{2}\,ds,

and thus

\displaystyle\E^{\Q}\left[\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\lVert\theta_{s}\rVert^{2}\right]\leq

\displaystyle\frac{CR}{K^{2}}+CR\E^{\Q}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\sup_{0\leq u\leq s}(\lVert\theta_{u}\rVert^{2}+|\varepsilon_{u}|^{2})\,ds\right],

(3.6)

where
C
is a positive constant only depending on the Lipschitz constants and
T
that is allowed to change from line to line.

Step 3.
By definition of
\varepsilon_{t}
,

\displaystyle\frac{|\varepsilon_{t}|^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle\leq t\int_{0}^{t}\left(b(\calO_{s},X_{s})-b(\mathfrak{o}^{K}(Z^{K}_{s}),X^{K}_{s})\right)^{2}dt+\left|\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(\calO_{s},X_{s})-\sigma(\mathfrak{o}^{K}(Z^{K}_{s}),X^{K}_{s})\,dW_{s}\right|^{2}.

Taking supremum over time, using Doob’s martingale inequality and Lipschitz property of

\displaystyle\E^{\Q}\left[\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}|\varepsilon_{s}|^{2}\right]\leq C\E^{\Q}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\sup_{0\leq u\leq s}(\lVert\theta_{u}\rVert^{2}+|\varepsilon_{u}|^{2})\,ds\right],

Together with (
3.6

\displaystyle\E^{\Q}\left[\sup_{0\leq s\leq T}(\varepsilon_{s}^{2}+\lVert\theta_{s}\rVert_{s}^{2})\right]\leq\frac{CR}{K^{2}}\exp(CR).

(3.7)

∎

Weak Error Analysis and Monte Carlo Methods

Our main results in
Section

3
provide strong error estimates arising from cylindrical projections. These theorems naturally lead to weak convergence rates as outlined next.

Corollary 5.1
.

Suppose
\varphi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is Lipschitz with
\mathcal{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
equipped with the product of cylindrical norm and Euclidean norm. Then we obtain the weak convergence rate,

\displaystyle\left|\E^{\Q}[\varphi(\calO_{T}^{\tau_{R}},X_{T}^{\tau_{R}})]-\E^{\Q}[\varphi(\mathfrak{o}^{K}(Z_{T}^{K,\tau^{K}_{R}}),X_{T}^{K,\tau^{K}_{R}})]\right|\leq\frac{C\textnormal{Lip}(\varphi)}{K},

where
\textnormal{Lip}(\varphi)
denotes the Lipschitz coefficient of
\varphi
, and
C
is the constant of Theorem
3.1
. Moreover, similar to Remark
3.1
,
(\mathfrak{o}^{K}(Z_{T}^{K,\tau^{K}_{R}}),X_{T}^{K,\tau^{K}_{R}})
weakly converges to
(\calO_{T},X_{T})
at the rate
1/K
when
\lambda
is bounded, and at the rate
1/(\log K)^{0.35}
when
\lambda
is Lipschitz.

Proof.

Write
\varphi_{T}^{R}=\varphi(\calO_{T}^{\tau_{R}},X_{T}^{\tau_{R}})
,
\varphi_{T}^{R,K}=\varphi(\mathfrak{o}^{K}(Z_{T}^{K,\tau^{K}_{R}}),X_{T}^{K,\tau^{K}_{R}})
for brevity.
Thanks to the Lipschitz property of
\varphi
,

\displaystyle\left|\E^{\Q}\big[\varphi_{T}^{R}\big]-\E^{\Q}\big[\varphi_{T}^{R,K}\big]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\E^{\Q}\left[|\varphi_{T}^{R}-\varphi_{T}^{R,K}|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{Lip}(\varphi)\E^{\Q}\left[\lVert(\calO^{\tau_{R}}_{T},X^{\tau_{R}}_{T})-(\mathfrak{o}^{K}(Z^{K,\tau^{K}_{R}}_{T}),X^{K,\tau^{K}_{R}}_{T})\rVert\right]

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{Lip}(\varphi)\lVert(\calO^{\tau_{R}},X^{\tau_{R}})-(\mathfrak{o}^{K}(Z^{K,\tau^{K}_{R}}),X^{K,\tau^{K}_{R}})\rVert_{\Q}.

The result then follows from Theorem
3.1
.
∎

Applications to Monte Carlo Methods.

Given a function
\varphi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\calM\times\R^{d}\to\R
and a finite horizon
T>0
, suppose we are interested in estimating the value

p=\E^{\Q}[\varphi(\calO_{T},X_{T})],

where
X
evolves according to (
2.2
). Dropping the dependence on the exit time
\tau_{R}
for brevity, then combining Monte Carlo simulations with cylindrical projection leads to the estimator

p^{K,J}=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\varphi(\mathfrak{o}^{K}(Z^{K,j}_{T}),X_{T}^{K,j}).

Introduce
p^{K}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\E^{\Q}[\varphi(\mathfrak{o}^{K}(Z^{K}_{T}),X_{T}^{K})]
. Classically,
|p^{K}-p^{K,J}|\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{J}}
, so together with
Corollary

5.1
, there exist finite constants
C_{1},C_{2}
such that

|p-p^{K,J}|\leq|p-p^{K}|+|p^{K}-p^{K,J}|\leq\frac{C_{1}}{K}+\frac{C_{2}}{\sqrt{J}}.

In fact, a third error arises from time discretization which we shall omit here.

Example 5.1
.

Consider a floating strike Asian call option in the local occupied volatility (LOV) model from
Section

4.3
with
\kappa=0
,
x_{0}=100
. That is
\varphi(\calO_{T},X_{T})=(X_{T}-\overline{\calO_{T}})^{+}
, where
\overline{\calO_{T}}=\frac{1}{T}\int_{\R}x\calO_{T}(dx)
is the barycenter of
\calO_{T}
. Since
(\mathfrak{o},x)\mapsto x-\frac{1}{T}\int_{\R}y\mathfrak{o}(dy)
is linear in
\mathfrak{o}
,
x
, and
x\to x^{+}
is
1-
Lipschitz continuous, then
\varphi
is Lipschitz as well.
The Monte Carlo prices
p^{K,J}
and weak errors
|p-p^{K,J}|

12(b)
as function of
K
, with
J=2^{14}
many simulations. The exact price
p
is estimated using
K=100
occupation bins. The right panel of
Fig.

12(b)
confirms the linear rate of convergence with respect to
K
established in
Corollary

5.1
.
