Background

Healthcare-associated infections remain a major challenge for healthcare systems globally. These infections increase morbidity and mortality rates and impose a significant financial burden on healthcare systems (Abramson et al., 2025). According to the World Health Organization (2020), every healthcare facility should establish an infection prevention and control team. However, relying solely on dedicated infection control professionals may limit the effective implementation of infection prevention strategies across clinical departments. In most hospitals, the number of infection control professionals is not sufficient to provide continuous on-site oversight across all units, and a study showed that these professionals often work excessive hours (Doran et al., 2025). In addition, without staff embedded in each department, it is difficult to deliver infection control knowledge in a timely manner and to reinforce evidence-based practices at the bedside (Sopirala et al., 2014). To address these gaps, infection control link nurses have been introduced to support the implementation of infection prevention strategies within clinical departments (Dekker et al., 2022).

Infection control link nurses are frontline nurses with basic training in infection prevention and control. They take on these responsibilities in addition to their routine clinical duties (World Health Organization, 2020). Their roles include translating infection control policies into clinical actions, providing peer support, and assisting in the early identification of infection risks (Bai et al., 2024). The effective performance of these responsibilities requires a defined set of professional competencies (Storr et al., 2017).

Current international competency standards in infection prevention and control are primarily developed for dedicated infection control professionals, including those established by the World Health Organization (2020), the Certification Board of Infection Control and Epidemiology (2012), and the Association for Professionals in Infection Control and Epidemiology (2019). These standards cover key domains of infection prevention and control practice, such as leadership and program management, microbiology and infection surveillance, and quality improvement. They have been widely used as important references for competency assessment in this field (Abramson et al., 2025; Tomczyk et al., 2022).

Although infection control link nurses play a key role in hospital infection prevention and control (Dekker et al., 2019), there remains a lack of a role-specific core competency framework. This gap matters because infection control link nurses differ from dedicated infection control professionals in role positioning and scope of practice. Dedicated infection control professionals work full-time in infection control departments, focusing on infection surveillance, outbreak investigation, and supervision of control practices (Joint Prevention and Control Mechanism of the State Council of China, 2021). Infection control link nurses are clinical nurses who perform infection control duties alongside routine work. Their main role is to advocate for infection control practices within hospital wards and improve healthcare worker behavior (Dekker et al., 2019). Directly applying competency standards for infection control professionals to link nurses may lead to a mismatch between competency requirements and actual clinical practice.

This issue is particularly important in general hospitals in China. These institutions experience high bed turnover and manage substantial patient volumes, increasing the risk of healthcare-associated infections . Infection risks vary across departments. Intensive care units address multidrug-resistant organisms, surgical departments focus on preventing surgical site infections, and medical departments emphasize the implementation of basic infection prevention and control strategies (Shen et al., 2020). These differences suggest that infection control link nurses may require both general competencies shared across departments and additional competencies shaped by specific clinical contexts. However, before exploring specialty-specific competencies, a fundamental question must first be addressed: what core competencies are required for all infection control link nurses, regardless of their departmental affiliation? Thus, this study aimed to develop a core competency framework for infection control link nurses in general hospitals in China, clarify the core competencies required for this role, and inform future training and evaluation.

Methods

Study design

This study was conducted in two phases. First, a literature review was performed to identify preliminary core competency indicators. Second, a modified Delphi study was conducted to collect expert opinions and refine the content of the core competencies. The Delphi study was reported in accordance with the DELPHISTAR guideline: Delphi studies in social and health sciences—recommendations for an interdisciplinary standardized reporting (Niederberger et al., 2024). The DELPHISTAR checklist is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Literature review

Through a literature review, we identified existing competency frameworks and related competency descriptions. These materials served as a reference for developing a preliminary core competency framework for infection control link nurses.

Search strategy

We conducted a comprehensive search of nine databases: PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Scopus, CINAHL, CNKI, Wanfang Data, VIP, and SinoMed. The search covered all records from the inception of each database up to August 31, 2024. To improve search efficiency, we used both controlled vocabulary terms, including Medical Subject Headings where applicable, and free-text terms. The main search terms included "infection control link nurse," "infection control professionals," "infection control nurse," and "competency," along with their synonyms and related expressions. Boolean operators were used to combine search terms to improve search precision. In addition, we reviewed authoritative materials from the World Health Organization and professional infection prevention and control organizations to enrich the evidence base.

The general search structure followed this pattern: ((("infection control link nurse" OR "infection control liaison nurse" OR "ICLN" OR "infection prevention link nurse" OR "infection control nurse" OR "ICN" OR "infection prevention and control nurse" OR "infection prevention and control professional" OR "infection control professional" OR "ICP" OR ("nurse" AND "infection control")) AND ("competenc*" OR "capabilit*" OR "abilit*" OR "skill" OR "knowledge" OR "performance" OR "standard" OR "framework")). The search syntax was tailored to each database's specific requirements (Supplementary Table S2).

Inclusion criteria and study selection

Because there were few studies specifically on infection control link nurses, this study also included competencies related to other infection control positions. Only competencies applicable to the infection control link nurse role were included in the analysis.

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) the study described or evaluated competency frameworks, standards, or requirements related to infection prevention and control; (2) the study focused on infection control professionals, infection control nurses, infection control link nurses, or clinical nurses involved in infection control work; and (3) the study was written in English or Chinese. Studies were excluded if they were editorials, commentaries, or narrative reviews that did not include specific competency data, or if they were conference abstracts.

Two reviewers (BG, ZY) independently performed study selection, including screening titles and abstracts and conducting full-text assessments. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer (LR).

Data extraction

Two reviewers (BG, LR) independently extracted competency indicators. Before formal data extraction, we conducted a pilot test using three studies to ensure consistency in the extraction process. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion. The final dataset was confirmed by all team members.

Data integration

The extracted competency indicators were organized and then refined through repeated comparison and adjustment. Based on the responsibilities of infection control link nurses in Chinese general hospitals identified in a previous study (Bai et al., 2024), which included monitoring infection prevention and control indicators, reporting infection cases, supervising policy implementation and continuous quality improvement, providing infection control training and education, and managing the environment and processes, the competency indicators were further reviewed for applicability and relevance.

Four approaches were used to refine the competency indicators: (1) retaining directly applicable indicators; (2) merging overlapping indicators; (3) excluding indicators that were irrelevant or not aligned with the role responsibilities of infection control link nurses; and (4) rewording indicators to improve clarity and better reflect the clinical context. This process aimed to enhance the applicability of the indicators to general hospitals in China and support the subsequent Delphi study.

Modified Delphi method

Research team

The research team consisted of five members, including two infection prevention and control experts, two nursing management experts, and one doctoral student. All team members had at least five years of experience in infection prevention and control or nursing management. The team was responsible for designing the expert consultation questionnaire, determining the number of experts needed, distributing and collecting the questionnaires, and summarizing the experts’ feedback.

Development of questionnaires

After reviewing the relevant literature, we developed a preliminary competency framework, which served as the basis for the first round of the Delphi questionnaire. The questionnaire comprised four sections: (1) the introduction explained the study purpose, confidentiality assurance, informed consent process, and instructions for completion; (2) the demographic section collected information on experts’ gender, age, education, years of work experience, professional title, and affiliated institution; (3) the core competency framework included six domains, 33 subdomains, and 141 indicators. All domains, subdomains, and indicators were rated using a five-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 = not important at all to 5 = very important, to assess perceived importance. Open-ended fields were included for additional comments; and (4) the expert authority section assessed self-rated familiarity (Cs) and judgment basis (Ca) using structured rating scales, as proposed by Zeng (1994). Familiarity was rated on a five-level scale: very unfamiliar (0.2), unfamiliar (0.4), moderately familiar (0.6), familiar (0.8), and very familiar (1.0). Judgment basis included four aspects: theoretical analysis, practical experience, reference to domestic and international literature, and subjective intuition. The influence of each aspect on expert judgment was rated at three levels, with corresponding scores of 0.5 for high, 0.3 for medium, and 0.1 for low.

Expert consultation process

Experts were purposively selected from professionals in nursing, public health, and infection prevention and control to ensure diversity in professional roles. They were also drawn from southern, central, and northern regions of China to ensure geographic diversity. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) at least five years of relevant experience in infection prevention and control; (2) a bachelor's degree or higher; (3) an intermediate or senior professional title; and (4) willingness to participate in both Delphi rounds. According to Delphi methodology guidelines, a panel size of 15 to 50 experts was considered appropriate (Hsu, 2007).

Two Delphi rounds were administered via email from November 2024 to January 2025. Each round remained open for two weeks, with a reminder sent one day before the deadline. Based on the expert feedback obtained in each round, the questionnaire was iteratively revised. Indicator retention criteria were established a priori by the research team, incorporating predefined statistical thresholds for consensus. For the first round, indicators were retained if the mean importance score was 3.5 or higher, the coefficient of variation (CV) was ≤ 0.25, and percent agreement, defined as the percentage of ratings of 4 or 5, was ≥ 70%. More stringent criteria were applied in the second round: mean importance score ≥ 4.0, CV ≤ 0.25, and percent agreement ≥ 80% (Diamond et al., 2014), to retain only indicators with high expert agreement. Because the second round met the predefined consensus criteria and produced no substantial new revisions, the Delphi process was concluded.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 27.0 (IBM Corp.). Continuous and categorical data were presented as mean ± standard deviation and frequencies and percentages, respectively. Expert engagement was evaluated using response rates, with a response rate of ≥ 70% considered acceptable (Hsu, 2007). Indicator importance was assessed using mean importance scores. Expert authority (Cr) was computed as the mean of Cs and Ca, with Cr ≥ 0.70 indicating acceptable authority (Schmidt, 1997). Coordination of expert opinions was evaluated using the coefficient of variation (CV) and Kendall's coefficient of concordance (Kendall's W) (Gracht, 2012). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the authors’ institution (approval no 2022,070). All participants were informed of the research purpose and provided informed consent. Participation was voluntary, and participants could withdraw at any time. All data were anonymized and kept confidential.

Results

Literature review

A total of 4394 records were retrieved. After duplicates were removed and titles and abstracts were screened, 52 documents related to the research topic were included. After full-text review, 18 documents met the inclusion criteria (Supplementary Table S3). The publication years of the documents ranged from 2008 to 2023. Of these, 8 were peer-reviewed articles, 7 were documents issued by infection prevention and control associations or academic institutions, and 3 were master's or doctoral theses. The documents originated from China (n = 10), the United States (n = 2), the United Kingdom (n = 2), the World Health Organization (n = 1), Europe (n = 1), Canada (n = 1), and Brazil (n = 1). According to study design or document type, these documents included 5 qualitative studies, 10 mixed-methods studies, and 3 standards formulated by professional associations or academic institutions. A total of 158 competency indicators were extracted. After duplicates were removed and wording was refined, 141 indicators were retained.

Delphi study

Expert demographics

A total of 25 experts were invited to participate in the Delphi study. During the first round of consultation, two experts were excluded due to nonresponse. The remaining 23 experts from 8 provinces and municipalities in China (Beijing, Shanghai, Guangdong, Jilin, Hainan, Hubei, Inner Mongolia, and Liaoning) completed both rounds of consultation. Expert participation and authority coefficient

Expert participation remained high throughout the study, with response rates of 92% in the first round and 100% in the second round. In Round 1, 14 experts (60.9%) provided suggestions for revising the indicators, while two experts (8.7%) offered additional comments in Round 2. The Cr values were high in both rounds, with values of 0.874 in Round 1 (Cs = 0.787, Ca = 0.961) and 0.879 in Round 2 (Cs = 0.800, Ca = 0.957).

Coordination of expert opinions

As shown in Table 2, Kendall’s W values at the domain, subdomain, and indicator levels in the first Delphi round were 0.536, 0.335, and 0.345, respectively. In the second round, the corresponding values were 0.156, 0.172, and 0.175, respectively. All Kendall’s W coefficients were statistically significant (p < 0.001). Kendall’s W values decreased from the first to the second round. This change should be interpreted in light of revisions between rounds, including the modification, merging, and deletion of indicators, as well as changes in the number of items evaluated at each level. Since Kendall’s W depends on the number and structure of the items evaluated, lower values in the second round do not necessarily indicate weaker consensus (Legendre, 2022).

The Delphi consulting results

The initial competency framework comprised 6 domains, 33 subdomains, and 141 indicators. Following two rounds of Delphi consultation, the framework underwent substantial revision, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

In the first round, one domain (research capability), 11 subdomains, and 25 indicators were removed for failing to meet the predefined retention criteria. Two subdomains and 8 indicators were added based on expert recommendations to address gaps in the initial framework. A total of 75 indicators were restructured, either by reassigning them to more appropriate domains or subdomains or by consolidating overlapping or redundant indicators. An additional 63 indicators were reworded to improve clarity without altering their substantive content (Supplementary Table S4).

Expert feedback in the first round also prompted revisions to better align the indicators with the specific role of infection control link nurses. Six experts suggested removing the research capability domain because it set expectations more appropriate for specialized infection control professionals than for link nurses. Accordingly, this domain was removed, and its clinically relevant elements, such as evidence-based practice and access to infection control guidelines, were incorporated into the professionalism domain. Experts also suggested revising or removing indicators in the surveillance and reporting subdomain (B1), the outbreak recognition subdomain (B6), and antimicrobial stewardship indicators (A4.2 and A4.4) to better reflect the supportive and collaborative role of link nurses. In addition, indicators related to patient and family infection control education were added based on expert recommendations.

In the second round, consensus was reached on the overall structure and content of the framework. Two experts suggested minor wording revisions to improve clarity, and two indicators were revised accordingly. All experts agreed with the proposed changes. The final competency framework comprised 5 domains, 19 subdomains, and 75 indicators.

In the second round of Delphi consultation, all retained indicators had mean importance scores of 4.04 to 4.96, CV values of 0.04 to 0.20, and percent agreement values of 82% to 100% (Table 3).

Discussion

This study developed a core competency framework for infection control link nurses in general hospitals in China using a literature review and the modified Delphi method. The high expert authority coefficients and statistically significant Kendall’s W values supported the credibility of the Delphi results. The final framework included 75 competency indicators organized into five domains: infection prevention and control knowledge, infection prevention and control skills, communication and collaboration, education and training, and professionalism. These domains are interrelated and together form the foundation for effective role performance among infection control link nurses.

During the extraction of competency indicators, some overlap was found in the competencies required for different types of infection control professionals, such as communication, education, and evidence-based practice (World Health Organization, 2020). This overlap reflects the collaborative nature of infection prevention and control work (Isahak et al., 2022). However, overlap in competency content does not mean that these competencies serve the same function across roles.

In this study, overlapping competencies were not simply retained. Instead, their applicability to infection control link nurses was judged through the Delphi process, with reference to their role responsibilities, work settings, and practice needs. Competencies related mainly to hospital-level management or strategy were excluded, whereas those directly relevant to clinical practice were retained. Expert feedback in the first round prompted several role-specific refinements to the framework. The research capability domain generated the most discussion. Experts noted that infection control link nurses typically carry out infection control responsibilities alongside their regular clinical nursing duties, which limits the time and resources available for this role (Ward, 2016). Requiring independent research skills as a core competency was therefore seen as inconsistent with their role definition and potentially discouraging to practitioners (Wu et al., 2024). Accordingly, this domain was removed, and its most clinically applicable elements, such as accessing evidence-based guidelines and applying best practices, were embedded within the professionalism domain. This decision helped ensure that the framework prioritizes practice-oriented competencies over academic research skills.

In the first round, experts also identified role boundary issues across several areas. Regarding infection surveillance (B1) and outbreak recognition (B6), multiple experts indicated that tasks such as independently identifying outbreak trends and making epidemic control decisions fell primarily within physicians’ scope of practice rather than the role of infection control link nurses. These indicators were revised to reflect a supportive and coordinating function, consistent with the liaison nature of the role (Dekker et al., 2019). Similarly, indicators related to antimicrobial stewardship (A4.2 and A4.4) were revised to remove expectations around pharmacological mechanisms and prescription oversight, which experts considered more appropriate for physicians or pharmacists. In contrast, experts recommended adding indicators addressing patient and family infection control education, recognizing this as a frontline responsibility well suited to link nurses embedded within clinical departments (Dekker et al., 2022). These revisions collectively helped ensure that the final framework reflects the actual scope and boundaries of the infection control link nurse role, rather than duplicating the expectations of specialist infection control professionals.

The results indicated that the core competencies of infection control link nurses differed in their perceived importance based on expert ratings. Among the domains, infection prevention and control knowledge and skills received the highest mean importance scores, suggesting that experts viewed technical competence as a core component of the competency framework. Dekker et al. (2024) proposed that infection control link nurses help their colleagues implement prevention measures by adapting and operationalizing infection prevention policies to fit increasingly complex clinical conditions, while also providing practical guidance and feedback. Fulfilling these responsibilities requires a solid foundation of professional knowledge and skills. Shi et al. (2024) further showed in their empirical study that infection control link nurses with strong professional skills effectively enhanced the awareness and clinical practice of medical staff regarding infection prevention. These findings are consistent with the experts’ ratings in the present study, which highlight the central role of technical competence in infection control practice.

At the subdomain level, higher mean importance scores were assigned to competencies related to high-risk clinical practices, such as the prevention and control of catheter-associated infections and the implementation and supervision of standard and transmission-based precautions. Sopirala et al. (2014) found that implementing an infection control link nurse program, combined with ongoing reeducation, was associated with a 28% reduction in hospital-acquired methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus infections and a 41% reduction in bloodstream infections. In addition, Donati et al. (2020) showed, in a randomized controlled trial, that assigning infection control link nurses, along with systematic audit and feedback, significantly improved nurses' compliance with standard precautions. These two studies suggest that infection control link nurses can play an important role in high-risk clinical practice. Training for infection control may therefore focus on high-risk procedures that are closely tied to daily clinical work.

In addition to technical competencies, non-technical competencies such as communication and coordination, education and training, and professionalism were also rated as important by experts, although their mean importance scores were comparatively lower. These competencies nonetheless play a key role in supporting the effective implementation of infection prevention and control measures (Dekker et al., 2019). Effective communication and training can improve healthcare workers’ compliance with infection control practices (Li et al., 2025), while professionalism supports consistent and sustained implementation (Yu et al., 2022).

Compared with existing competency frameworks, the current framework provides more practice-oriented guidance for the development of infection control link nurses. The core competencies proposed by the World Health Organization (2020) and the Association for Professionals in Infection Control and Epidemiology (2019) are mainly designed for dedicated infection control professionals. These frameworks include areas such as policy development and complex epidemiological analysis. Such content may not fully align with the role of infection control link nurses, who are part-time clinical nurses. The competency framework for infection control link nurses published by the Royal College of Nursing (2021) is closer to this role. However, it mainly provides general descriptions and offers limited guidance on how competencies can be applied in clinical practice. In this study, the framework was developed based on the responsibilities of infection control link nurses. Each indicator was described in a clear and practice-oriented way. This helps ensure that every competency has a direct link to clinical practice.

Although the competency framework developed in this study is comprehensive, it may not be feasible for infection control link nurses to master all 75 competency indicators simultaneously. The expert-rated importance scores suggest that these competencies vary in perceived importance, indicating that a uniform approach to competency development may not be optimal. Therefore, a staged implementation strategy may be more practical and effective. Benner’s model (1982), which describes the development of clinical competence from novice to expert, provides a useful reference for this process. At the initial stage, infection control link nurses may need to focus on core knowledge and basic skills related to infection prevention and control, especially those competencies rated as more important by experts. Clear standards and guidance can support their practice at this stage (Dekker et al., 2022). With increasing clinical experience, infection control link nurses may gradually develop more comprehensive competencies, including interdisciplinary collaboration, effective communication, and clinical teaching and guidance (Kim et al., 2015). This staged approach helps transform the 75 competency indicators into a progressive development pathway rather than a simple checklist. It may also provide a practical basis for future empirical testing and for considering whether scale reduction is needed to improve usability in practice.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, most included documents and Delphi experts were from China, and the study focused on infection control link nurses in general hospitals in China, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, qualitative interviews were not conducted to explore the practical experiences and competency needs of infection control link nurses, which may have limited the depth of the competency content. Third, the competency framework has not been empirically validated in clinical settings, so its applicability and effectiveness in guiding practice remain uncertain.

Conclusion

This study developed a core competency framework for infection control link nurses in general hospitals in China based on a literature review and the modified Delphi method. The final framework included 5 domains, 19 subdomains, and 75 indicators. This structure provides a comprehensive representation of the competency requirements for infection control link nurses in clinical practice. The findings suggest that knowledge and skills in infection prevention and control are central to the competency framework. Communication, education, and professionalism play supportive and facilitating roles. This framework may serve as a reference for the selection, training, and evaluation of infection control link nurses. It also provides theoretical support for improving hospital infection prevention and control systems.
