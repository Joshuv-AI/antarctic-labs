Proteomic health archetypes identified in disease-free adults enable risk assessment for diverse chronic diseases
Authors: Xiangyang Zhang, Weizhen Yan, Xianliang Fan, Peng Zhang, Yan Gao, Yuanzhe Wang, Hong Shen, Changjing Cai, Shan Zeng, Jiang Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe prevention of common chronic diseases is hampered by the lack of tools to identify at-risk individuals. The plasma proteome could enable a systems-level assessment of health, but its clinical translation is limited by platform-specific biases and a focus on single diseases.MethodsWe aimed to develop and validate a clinically deployable proteomic classifier for multi-system risk prediction. Using data from 11,900 disease-free adults, we defined five ProteoHealth Archetypes (PHAs) via unsupervised learning. Critically, we trained a classifier using paired protein ratios to ensure robustness across measurement platforms.ResultsThe ratio-based classifier successfully transferred PHA signatures in an internal validation cohort (n = 3,570) and, importantly, in three external cohorts profiled by different technologies (SomaScan and mass spectrometry). Each PHA exhibited distinct, highly reproducible risks for developing cardiometabolic, inflammatory/immune, neurovascular, and psychiatric diseases. Individuals in high-risk archetypes experienced significantly steeper declines in survival. Genome-wide association analyses identified loci associated with PHA liability, and two-sample Mendelian randomisation produced effect directions consistent with the observational disease association. The differential proteomes and enriched pathways aligned with the specific disease profiles of each archetype.ConclusionsPHAs provide an externally transferable, mechanistically interpretable map of baseline proteomic health that forecasts multisystem disease and survival, offering a scalable substrate for prevention, risk communication, and biomarker-guided stratification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13073-026-01696-w.

Background

The human plasma proteome encodes a high-dimensional, organ-spanning readout of physiology that integrates genetic predisposition, environmental exposure, immune tone, and incipient tissue injury [1, 2]. Large-scale assays now quantify thousands of circulating proteins in population cohorts, revealing robust links between individual proteins and future diseases [3, 4]. Across large prospective settings, sparse multi-plasma protein signatures improve prediction for diverse incident diseases and mortality beyond conventional risk factors [5–7]. Recent atlases now connect thousands of circulating proteins to hundreds of prevalent and incident endpoints and to nearly a thousand health-related traits, indicating broad, biologically coherent protein–disease architecture [8–11].

Prior proteomic studies have identified proteins associated with various disease outcomes, but three gaps persist. First, associations are commonly analyzed in isolation [6, 8, 12], obscuring coordinated axes that span organ systems and co-morbidity clusters (e.g., cardiometabolic–renal, immune–fibrotic, neurovascular–cognitive). Second, relative protein concentrations are susceptible to batch and platform effects [5, 13], complicating cross-study validation and clinical translation. Third, mechanistic anchoring is often limited, and few frameworks connect germline variation, proteomic states and clinical outcomes to strengthen causal claims [6, 14].

These limitations motivate a population-level framework that discovers proteome-defined health states in disease-free adults and demonstrates that these states prospectively map to broad spectra of incident diseases and survival, and this framework can be applied to external cohorts. Unsupervised subtyping provides a natural route to recover latent, system-level biology without prespecifying endpoints [15–19]. However, no studies have yet used unsupervised clustering to define subtypes based on plasma proteomic data from large, prospective cohort studies.

In this study, we leveraged phenotyped population proteomics with prospective follow-up to build a scalable analytic framework (Fig. 1). At baseline, we defined ProteoHealth Archetypes (PHAs) by applying Leiden community [20] detection to a sample–sample similarity graph derived from plasma proteomes. We then relate PHAs to incident, multisystem clinical outcomes and to survival using multivariable Cox models, and we probe causality with Mendelian randomization. To support external validity, we transport the archetypes across independent cohorts and assay platforms via a ratio-based classifier, ensuring portability and calibration. Finally, we anchor the proteomic archetypes and their disease links by linking PHAs to germline genetic instruments and by pathway-level signals. Methods

Study design, cohort assembly, and ethics

We used the UK Biobank proteomics resource as our discovery substrate [10]. From 53,014 participants with baseline plasma proteomics (2,924 proteins), we excluded individuals without outcome follow-up and then removed all participants with any ICD-10–defined baseline disease. Quality control (QC) removed proteins undetected in ≥ 20% of participants and samples with ≥ 20% proteins undetected. Baseline disease status was ascertained using UK Biobank linked hospital in-patient records (HES/SMR/PEDW; Field IDs: 41,270 and 41,280) and national death registries (Field IDs: 40,000/40001/40002). Participants were classified as disease-free if no ICD-10 code corresponding to any predefined endpoint category was recorded prior to or at the blood draw date (Field ID: 3166). All available historical diagnosis records up to the blood draw date were used, with no minimum year restriction. Participants with kinship coefficient > 0.0884 (third-degree relatives or closer) were retained for phenotype analyses but excluded from GWAS to avoid relatedness confounding. The final analytic set comprised 11,900 disease-free participants with 2,913 proteins. We performed a random 7/3 split into a discovery cohort and a validation cohort prior to any modeling. UK Biobank operates under national research ethics approval with written informed consent from all participants. Data were retrieved from UK Biobank on 28 November 2024, and application ID is 93,044.

In external validation, we used three independent plasma proteomics datasets. The first external cohort was a pan-cancer plasma mass-spectrometry study reported by Bai et al. [21], comprising 2,251 plasma samples from patients with 18 solid-tumour types and from healthy controls, recruited at hospitals in mainland China. Proteins were profiled by data-independent-acquisition mass spectrometry on a Thermo Orbitrap instrument; processed expression data were obtained from the iProX partner repository (Project ID: IPX0003227000, https://www.iprox.cn/).

The second external cohort, accessed from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (accession GSE251855) [22], comprised 57 serum samples from patients with biopsy-confirmed metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH, n = 37) or metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD, n = 20), originally enrolled at a single clinical centre as part of a serum protein risk-stratification study. Samples were profiled using the SomaScan v4.1 aptamer array (7,596 aptamers) and corresponding processed expression values were used here.

The third external cohort, accessed from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (accession GSE95368) [23], comprised 48 plasma samples spanning five clinical groups: normal controls (n = 6), acute myocardial infarction (AMI, n = 12), acute stress cardiomyopathy (Takotsubo, n = 15), follow-up AMI (n = 4) and follow-up SCM (n = 11). Samples were originally collected at presentation and during the recovery phase, and were profiled using a multiplex aptamer-based assay.

Proteomic data handling and unsupervised subtyping

Protein abundances were analyzed on the provider-normalized scale [10, 11]. QC procedures included per-sample and per-protein missingness filters as above. Analyses were conducted on the post-QC matrix without imputation; models that operate per protein used all available observations for that protein. We benchmarked five unsupervised algorithms, Consensus clustering [24], K-means [25], Leiden [20], Gaussian mixture–based clustering [26], and Spectral clustering [27], on the discovery cohort’s 2,913-protein matrix. Internal validity was assessed using Silhouette [28], Dunn index [29], and Calinski–Harabasz (CH) indices [30]. For Leiden, we scanned the resolution parameter and quantified stability under resampling and re-initialization using adjusted Rand index (ARI) [31] and normalized mutual information (NMI). Leiden at resolution 0.2 maximized stability and was selected as the primary solution, yielding five subtypes, hereafter ProteoHealth Archetypes (PHAs). t-SNE was used only for visualization and had no role in model fitting. To assess the quality of our clustering solution, we calculated the modularity Q score, which quantifies the strength of division of a network into communities.

Sensitivity analysis for demographic confounding

To assess whether the identified proteomic clusters were driven by demographic or anthropometric factors rather than biological protein signatures, we performed a sensitivity analysis. We re-clustered the proteome data after regressing out the effects of age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, alcohol intake, education level, ethnicity, and Townsend deprivation index from each protein's abundance. Cluster agreement between the original and covariate-adjusted clustering was quantified using the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) and Normalized Mutual Information (NMI). Cluster purity was calculated as the proportion of samples in each original cluster that remained in the same cluster after adjustment.

Analysis of disease risks

Incident diseases were defined using linked hospital records and death registries (ICD-10), excluding prevalent cases at baseline by design. In outcome processing, we grouped incident diagnoses from hospital and registry data into predefined categories using ICD-10 codes: chronic lower respiratory disease (J40–J47), diabetes (E10–E14), heart failure (I50), malignant neoplasms (C00–C97), ischaemic heart disease (I20–I25), dementia (F00–F03, G30), stroke/cerebrovascular disease (I60–I69), chronic kidney disease (N18), hypertension (I10–I15), rheumatic disease (M00–M25), chronic urinary disease (N30–N39), psychotic disorders (F20–F29), and anxiety/depression (F40–F49). Time-to-event was from assessment to first event or censoring (death, loss to follow-up, or end of follow-up). Unless otherwise specified, multivariable models adjusted for age, sex, smoking status, BMI, alcohol intake frequency, Townsend deprivation index, education and race, with calendar time implicitly handled by the Cox baseline hazard. Proportional hazards assumptions were examined using Schoenfeld residuals; sensitivity analyses included removal of early events to mitigate reverse causation.

Externalizable classifier based on paired protein ratios

To enable cross-platform deployment, we trained a subtype classifier using paired protein-ratio features inspired by singleCellNet [32] approaches. Ratios were computed between selected protein pairs to attenuate platform- and scale-specific effects while accentuating relative biological programs. The classifier was trained in the discovery cohort with cross-validation and applied to the internal validation cohort and to external plasma-proteome datasets measured by mass spectrometry and SomaScan. We reported subtype distributions, separability in t-SNE space, and endpoint alignments. An “uncertain” class was assigned when posterior probabilities failed to exceed a prespecified threshold (30%). For an external cancer dataset, we evaluated discrimination using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and area under the curve (AUC).

Cross-validation of clustering stability

To assess the robustness and generalizability of the identified proteomic subtypes, we repeated tenfold cross-validation. The discovery cohort was randomly partitioned into 10 equal folds. For each iteration, nine folds served as the training set to build a protein-pair-based classifier for cluster prediction, and the remaining fold served as the validation set. The trained classifier was applied to the validation fold to assign cluster membership, and Cox proportional hazards models (adjusted for age, sex, BMI, smoking status, alcohol intake, and Townsend deprivation index) were then fitted for all pre-specified disease endpoints. This process was repeated 10 times with different random seeds to ensure reproducibility. Cluster stability was quantified by the coefficient of variation (CV) of hazard ratios across folds.

Differential proteome analysis

For each PHA, we performed one-versus-rest differential analysis across all proteins using two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, reporting log₂ fold-change and false-discovery-rate (FDR)-adjusted P values (Benjamini–Hochberg). These Wilcoxon-defined subtype markers were subsequently used for pathway enrichment.

Protein–disease association analysis

We fitted one-versus-rest multivariable Cox proportional-hazards models for each protein and each endpoint within the discovery cohort. Effect sizes are reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Multiple testing was controlled per endpoint using FDR (Benjamini–Hochberg). Volcano plots of log (HR) versus–log₁₀ FDR-adjusted P summarize these associations.

Subtype–disease associations and prognosis

For each PHA, we estimated hazards for incident diseases using one-versus-rest multivariable Cox models (each subtype contrasted with the remaining four, no universal reference), applying the same covariate set and FDR control as above. Kaplan–Meier curves compared overall survival across PHAs with log-rank tests.

Genome-wide association studies for subtype liability

We conducted genome-wide association studies (GWAS) for subtype liability using a one-versus-rest design in the discovery cohort. GWAS analyses were carried out with PLINK 2 [33]. Analyses adjusted for population structure. Manhattan plots illustrate association landscapes; full summary statistics have been deposited in the GWAS Catalog. To minimize confounding by demographic, anthropometric, and socioeconomic factors, all analyses were restricted to disease-free participants at baseline (N = 8,283). Each model adjusted for age, sex, BMI, smoking status, alcohol intake frequency, Townsend deprivation index, educational qualifications, self-reported race, and the first 10 genetic principal components to control for population stratification. Lead single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were defined as independent variants reaching P < 5 × 10⁻⁶ after linkage-disequilibrium (LD)-based clumping (r2 < 0.1, 1 Mb window). We systematically annotated all lead loci using NCBI dbSNP gene annotations and cross-referenced them against the NHGRI-EBI GWAS Catalog to identify previously reported trait associations. To assess whether PHA-associated genetic variants influence protein abundance, we performed protein quantitative trait locus (pQTL) overlap analysis using the comprehensive UKB-PPP resource [10], which catalogues sentinel pQTLs for 2,923 proteins. Overlap was defined as ± 1 Mb for cis-pQTLs (protein-coding gene within 1 Mb of the variant) and genome-wide for trans-pQTLs. For selected GWAS–pQTL pairs with available summary statistics, we performed Bayesian colocalization analysis using the coloc R package to test whether GWAS and pQTL signals share a common causal variant. Posterior probability PP.H4 > 0.8 was considered evidence for colocalization, while PP.H2 > 0.8 indicates distinct causal variants at the same locus (allelic heterogeneity).

Mendelian randomization

We performed two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) to test putative causal effects of PHA liability on major disease endpoints. Primary inference used inverse-variance weighted (IVW) estimates; sensitivity analyses included MR-Egger, weighted median, and weighted mode. We assessed heterogeneity (Cochran’s Q), horizontal pleiotropy (Egger intercept), and robustness (leave-one-out). Concordance in directionality with observational Cox estimates was pre-specified as a replication criterion.

Pathway enrichment

We performed KEGG pathway enrichment on Wilcoxon-defined differential protein sets (one-versus-rest per PHA) using an over-representation (hypergeometric) framework with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction. The background universe comprised all quantified proteins passing QC. Enrichment is summarized as GeneRatio, hit count, and adjusted P; representative results are visualized with bubble plots and detailed in the enrichment tables.

Statistical software

Analyses were conducted in R (v4.4.1). The packages broom (v1.0.10), mclust (v6.1.1), kernlab (v0.9–33), and ConsensusClusterPlus (v1.70.0) were used for k-means, Gaussian mixture–based clustering, spectral clustering, and consensus clustering, respectively. survival (v3.8–3) was used for Cox regression; CMplot (v4.6.0) for Manhattan plots; and clusterProfiler (v4.17.0) for pathway enrichment. Random seeds were set for all resampling procedures. All tests were two-sided, and false discovery rate (FDR) was controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure.

Baseline characteristics by ProteoHealth Archetypes (PHA) in the discovery cohort

Disease risks and prognosis across proteomic subtypes

We explored the relationship between PHA and disease risk using multivariable Cox proportional-hazards models under a one-vs-rest design (each cluster vs the remaining four), with FDR control applied. The subtypes exhibited distinct disease spectra (Fig. 2B–G and Additional file 2: Table S1): cluster 2 (cardiometabolic-dominant) showed higher hazards for hypertension (HR = 1.20, 95% CI 1.05–1.37; q = 1.7 × 10⁻4), ischaemic heart disease (HR = 1.21, 95% CI 1.08–1.39; q = 2.8 × 10⁻4), diabetes (HR = 1.21, 95% CI 1.05–1.34; q = 3.6 × 10⁻3), and heart failure (HR = 1.15, 95% CI 1.03–1.25; q = 8.2 × 10⁻3), alongside lower risk for cancer (HR = 0.82, 95% CI 0.72–0.92; q = 3.6 × 10⁻3) and anxiety/depression (HR = 0.78, 95% CI 0.62–0.97; q = 0.024); cluster 3 (inflammatory/immune-leaning) was associated with increased risks of chronic lower respiratory diseases (HR = 1.12, 95% CI 1.06–1.20; q = 9.2 × 10⁻4), rheumatic diseases (HR = 1.18, 95% CI 1.05–1.33; q = 0.0024), anxiety/depression (HR = 1.13, 95% CI 1.04–1.24; q = 0.0043), and a signal for cancer (q < 0.001), while showing lower risk for hypertension (HR = 0.83, 95% CI 0.71–0.94; q = 8.6 × 10⁻4); cluster 4 (neurovascular-dominant) displayed higher hazards for stroke (HR = 1.31, 95% CI 1.14–1.48; q = 0.0059) and dementia (HR = 1.29, 95% CI 1.01–1.42; q = 0.0106); cluster 5 (mental health-leaning) showed elevated risks of dementia (HR = 1.38, 95% CI 1.07–1.68; q = 0.011) and anxiety/depression (HR = 1.47, 95% CI 1.16–1.76; q = 0.041); other endpoints were less consistent after FDR; cluster 1 (balanced) did not show FDR-significant tendencies for the surveyed endpoints, consistent with a risk-neutral proteomic profile at baseline. Kaplan–Meier curves demonstrated clear separation of overall prognosis across the five subtypes (Fig. 2H). Clusters bearing cardiometabolic and neurovascular signals in the Cox models (Clusters 2 and 4) exhibited earlier and steeper declines in survival probability, whereas the risk-neutral subtype (Cluster 1) maintained the most favorable trajectory over follow-up. Sensitivity analyses at resolution = 0.15 (4 clusters) and resolution = 0.25 (6 clusters) replicated the direction of all primary disease associations, with log (HR) values highly correlated with the main solution (r = 0.959 and r = 0.839, respectively; Additional file 1: Fig. S2F-G and Additional file 3: Table S2), confirming that the five-archetype solution is not sensitive to resolution choice. The baseline demographic characteristics of the five clusters were shown in Additional file 1: Fig. S3. These results indicated that PHA at a disease-free baseline captures heterogeneous future disease liabilities and prognosis.

To further and specifically examine the extent to which the clustering itself is driven by demographic and anthropometric structure, we performed the additional sensitivity analysis precisely as the reviewer suggested. We regressed out the effects of age, sex, BMI, smoking, alcohol intake, Townsend deprivation index, education, and race (8 covariates in total) from each protein's levels using linear models and performed the identical unsupervised clustering pipeline on the resulting residuals. The concordance between the new clusters derived from this adjusted proteomic data and the original clusters was evaluated, and Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) was 0.834 (Additional file 1: Fig. S4A). This ARI value is substantially and significantly higher than the random expectation (Additional file 1: Fig. S4B), indicating that the core proteomic population structure we identified is highly stable and not merely an artifact of these demographic and socioeconomic factors. The high cluster purity for each original PHA (ranging from 0.898 to 0.934) further confirms that the vast majority of samples within each cluster remained together after covariate adjustment (Additional file 1: Fig. S4C).

Externalizable classification of ProteoHealth Archetypes using paired protein ratios

To enable subtype assignment in external datasets while minimizing batch/platform effects from absolute protein quantification, we trained a classifier on the discovery cohort using paired plasma-protein expression ratios as features (Fig. 3A). This design follows a label-transfer approach originally developed for single-cell data (singleCellNet) and permits robust scaling across different proteomic assays. Applied to the validation cohort, the model successfully assigned PHA labels across platforms with only a small fraction labeled as uncertain (cluster sizes: cluster 1 n = 1,497; cluster 2 n = 1,110; cluster 3 n = 659; cluster 4 n = 137; cluster 5 n = 127; uncertain n = 40). Validation of disease spectra and prognosis in the validation cohort

Using multivariable Cox models, we observed disease-risk patterns that mirrored the discovery cohort (Fig. 3D and Additional file 1: Fig. S5), and PHAs preserved their distinct disease spectra in validation. Representative hazard ratios and 95% CIs for each cluster and endpoint are provided in Additional file 4: Table S3 and reproduced graphically in Additional file 1: Fig. S5. To quantify agreement between discovery and validation, we compared per-HRs across cohorts. Overall, HRs were highly concordant (Pearson r = 0.76, P < 0.0001; Additional file 1: Fig. S5A). Cluster-wise correlations further supported cross-cohort reproducibility (Fig. 3E; cluster 1: r = 0.83, P = 0.03; cluster 2: r = 0.73, P = 0.016; cluster 3: r = 0.71, P = 0.020; cluster 4: r = 0.55, P = 0.10; cluster 5: r = 0.85, P = 0.002). Finally, Kaplan–Meier curves in the validation cohort (Fig. 3F) showed durable, subtype-specific separation of overall survival, with subtypes bearing stronger cardiometabolic or neurovascular signals (cluster 2 and cluster 4) exhibiting earlier and steeper declines, and cluster 1 maintaining the best prognosis among PHAs.

To quantify the predictive performance of PHA assignments, we computed cumulative disease incidence and predictive metrics for four major diseases (Additional file 5: Table S4). As shown in Additional file 1: Fig. S6A, C4 (Neurovascular) and C5 (Psychiatric) showed the highest dementia incidence (3.4% and 3.9%, respectively, vs. 2.0% in C1), and C5 showed markedly elevated stroke incidence (7.5% vs. 3.7–5.4% in other clusters). Using high-risk PHA membership as a binary classifier, negative predictive values (NPV) were consistently high across all endpoints (95.0–97.9%), confirming that individuals in low-risk PHAs have a very low probability of developing these diseases (Additional file 1: Fig. S6B). As expected for an unsupervised approach in a general population, positive predictive values were modest (3.1–3.7%), reflecting the low overall disease prevalence rather than a limitation of the clustering.

Cross-validation confirmed exceptional stability of proteomic subtypes and their disease associations. Across all 10 folds, the mean hazard ratio was 1.033 ± 0.155, with a coefficient of variation of only 0.22%. The mean HR across folds ranged from 1.029 to 1.036 (SD = 0.002). At the cluster-disease level, the median CV was 1.52% (range: 0.15%–5.48%), demonstrating that the identified proteomic subtypes are highly robust to different data partitions. The direction of all statistically significant associations was preserved across all folds (Additional file 1: Fig. S7 and Additional file 6: Table S5).

We further evaluated the classifier in three external datasets profiled by orthogonal proteomics platforms (Fig. 4A–D). The first cohort comprised 2,251 plasma samples from a pan-cancer mass spectrometry study [21], profiled using data-independent acquisition (DIA) mass spectrometry; this cohort included both cancer cases and non-cancer controls collected across multiple clinical centres. The second cohort comprised 57 serum samples from patients with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH/MASLD), profiled using the SomaScan v4.1 platform (7,596 aptamers) [22]. The third cohort comprised plasma samples from patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) or acute stress cardiomyopathy (Takotsubo syndrome) collected at acute and recovery phases, also profiled using SomaScan [23]. The predicted cluster distributions showed clear enrichment of cluster 3 (Inflammatory/immune archetype) among cancer cases versus controls, and enrichment of cluster 2 (Cardiometabolic archetype) in both the MASH/MASLD and AMI/cardiomyopathy cohorts (Fig. 4A, C and D). Using cluster 3 as the enriched cluster in the cancer dataset, the classifier achieved high sensitivity (0.929), specificity (0.966), and balanced accuracy (0.947) (Fig. 4B). Genome-wide signals of PHA liability and Mendelian randomization to incident diseases

To investigate germline architecture underlying the ProteoHealth Archetypes (PHAs) and their links to downstream disease, we first performed genome-wide association study (GWAS) for each of the five clusters (one-vs-rest design). The full summary statistics have been deposited in the GWAS Catalog [34]. Manhattan plots visualize genome-wide signal distributions for the PHAs in the discovery cohort (Fig. 4E, Additional file 1: Fig. S8A–D). These analyses establish polygenic components associated with subtype assignment and provide instruments for causal inference. Of 32 lead loci identified across five PHA GWAS (P < 5 × 10–6), 68.8% overlapped with known cis-pQTLs and trans-pQTLs from the UKB-PPP resource [10] (Additional file 7: Table S6 and Additional file 1: Fig. S9), supporting proteomic mediation of PHA genetic architecture. Genomic annotation revealed that several loci map to genes with established roles consistent with the respective archetype biology (e.g., CLEC16A at the C3 inflammatory locus; ADARB1 and RBFOX1 at C5 psychiatric loci), with none mapping to known demographic or anthropometric GWAS signals.

To formally test whether GWAS and pQTL signals share causal variants, we performed Bayesian colocalization analysis on three representative GWAS–pQTL pairs: PHA2–DNER (chr2:230 Mb), PHA4–GDNF (chr5:38 Mb), and PHA4–LIFR (chr5:38 Mb). None exceeded the colocalization threshold (PP.H4 > 0.8). Instead, the dominant posterior probabilities favored PP.H2 (distinct causal variants) (Additional file 8: Table S7). This pattern of allelic heterogeneity, where both GWAS and pQTL signals are present at the same locus but driven by different variants, is common at complex-trait loci and suggests that PHA-associated genetic variants influence proteomic state through regulatory mechanisms beyond simple modulation of single-protein abundance.

Using these instruments, we conducted two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) to test the putative causal relationships between PHAs and major disease endpoints. Across MR estimators (inverse-variance weighted, MR-Egger, weighted median/mode), the direction and magnitude of effects converged with the observational Cox results (Additional file 4: Table S3). For example, the cluster 2 showed causal links to cardiometabolic outcomes (diabetes and heart failure), whereas the cluster 4 aligned with neurovascular endpoints (stroke and dementia), and patterns for cluster 3 were consistent with inflammatory/immune phenotypes and oncologic signals. Panel-level MR summaries are shown in Fig. 4F–G and Additional file 1: Fig. S8E–H, with sensitivity analyses across MR methods supporting robustness. Together, these findings indicate that baseline proteomic subtypes have a detectable polygenic component and that MR estimates of PHA liability show effect directions consistent with the observational Cox associations.

Discussion

We report a population-level proteomic stratification of disease-free adults into five ProteoHealth Archetypes (PHAs) that are externally transferable across platforms and cohorts, align with multi-system incident disease risks and overall survival. Using UK Biobank plasma proteomes, unsupervised clustering yielded five stable subtypes. A paired protein-ratio classifier, inspired by single-cell label transfer [32], reproduced PHAs in an internal validation cohort and in external datasets with disease alignments preserved. Genome-wide association studies provided instruments for Mendelian randomization, which produced effect directions consistent with the cardiometabolic, inflammatory/immune, neurovascular and psychiatric outcomes identified in the Cox analyses, complementing the observational evidence.

We demonstrate that baseline plasma proteomes in healthy populations resolve into reproducible, biologically coherent health states that prospectively forecast risk across multiple disease classes, in line with recent large-scale studies showing multivariate protein signatures predict diverse future morbidities [6]. To make subtyping transferable across cohorts and platforms, we train on paired protein log-ratios—an approach motivated by compositional data principles and by known cross-platform/batch heterogeneity between Olink and SomaScan—thereby reducing assay-level shifts while preserving biological contrast [35]. Finally, we connect germline variation to proteomic subtypes and clinical outcomes: subtype-liability GWAS and two-sample Mendelian randomization leverage the rich pQTL atlas and proteogenomic literature to provide supportive evidence that these subtypes reflect upstream biology in addition to environmental and lifestyle influences.

Our choice of a five-cluster solution was guided by multiple considerations. First, the modularity score (Q = 0.425) indicates meaningful community structure in the proteomic network. Second, this resolution demonstrated high stability across bootstrap resampling and strong consensus across multiple clustering algorithms (ARI > 0.8). Third, and most importantly, the five clusters showed distinct and interpretable disease risk profiles across 13 major disease endpoints. While higher-resolution clustering could identify finer subdivisions, our bootstrap stability analysis (Additional file 1: Fig. S2D) showed diminishing returns beyond resolution 0.2, suggesting that additional subdivisions may represent noise rather than biologically meaningful subgroups. We acknowledge that with larger sample sizes or integration with additional omics layers, hierarchical stratification within these major clusters may reveal clinically relevant sub-phenotypes, which represents an important direction for future research.

Our validation strategy employed a classifier-based approach to test whether discovery-defined proteomic signatures could be reliably detected across independent cohorts and platforms. We acknowledge that this approach tests signature transferability rather than de novo cluster emergence. However, PHAs are fundamentally defined by shared proteomic patterns, and the classifier leverages these same features for prediction. Future studies with larger external cohorts could perform independent clustering to test whether analogous archetypes emerge de novo, which would provide complementary evidence for the universality of these proteomic states.

The cardiometabolic archetype (Cluster 2) coherently links endothelial/cardiac-load markers and lipid-handling proteins (NT-proBNP, ANGPT2, EDN1, APOM/PON/LPL) with pathway enrichment in platelet/ErbB–MAPK/cytoskeletal programs and with higher hazards for hypertension, ischaemic heart disease, heart failure, and diabetes—consistent with literature on NT-proBNP as a population prognostic marker [36, 37], ANGPT2 as an endothelial-activation/cardiovascular risk signal [38], endothelin-1 in hypertension/IHD [39], and APOM/PON/LPL axes in cardiometabolic disease [40, 41]. The inflammatory/immune archetype (Cluster 3) highlights matrix–immune remodeling (MMPs, TNF-axis) with Fcγ-receptor–mediated phagocytosis/adaptive-immune signaling, aligning with chronic airway [42, 43] and rheumatic phenotypes [44] and with inflammatory milieus implicated in tumor-promoting niches [45, 46]. The neurovascular archetype (Cluster 4) features proteins and pathways related to vascular–neuronal integrity consistent with stroke/dementia biology, which is supported by evidence for BBB/EGFR signaling [47, 48], cystatin-C in cognitive decline [49, 50], brain insulin signaling in neurodegeneration [51, 52], and endocytosis in Alzheimer’s pathogenesis [53, 54]. The psychiatric archetype (Cluster 5) concentrates neuronal ECM/synaptic programs with links to innate antiviral signaling and peroxisomal metabolism, in line with evidence for ECM/perineuronal-net abnormalities in psychiatric disorders [55, 56], interferon/ISG activity in CNS disease and mood effects [57, 58], and peroxisomal dysfunction in brain pathology [59].

Our GWAS analysis provides genetic evidence supporting the biological validity of PHAs. The 32 identified loci show no overlap with established demographic or anthropometric trait associations. This dissociation indicates that our covariate-adjusted GWAS successfully isolated PHA-specific genetic signals from demographic confounders. The biological coherence of annotated loci further supports the interpretation that PHAs represent distinct biological states rather than statistical artifacts.

The substantial pQTL overlap (68.8% of loci) confirms that genetic variants influencing PHA membership also regulate protein abundance, providing a mechanistic link between genotype, proteome, and disease risk. However, the lack of strong colocalization (PP.H4 < 0.8) for tested loci suggests that PHA-associated variants do not simply modulate individual protein levels, but rather influence broader regulatory networks. This is consistent with the composite, multi-protein nature of PHA phenotypes and highlights the value of proteomic archetypes as integrative readouts of complex biologiclal processes that cannot be captured by single-protein biomarkers. Although our GWAS identified subtype-associated loci and MR estimates aligned with the observational risk patterns, the modest colocalisation of PHA-GWAS hits with cis-pQTL signals indicates that most of the cluster-defining proteins are not directly explained by the lead PHA-associated variants. The PHAs are therefore best interpreted as composite proteomic states shaped by the integration of genetic, environmental, lifestyle, demographic and physiological influences, rather than as primarily genetically determined entities.

Our findings have several clinical and translational implications. In preventive care, proteome-based labels can complement standard risk scores by summarizing thousands of circulating proteins into interpretable health states that track long-term hazards and survival. For target and biomarker work, the intersection of differential proteins, per-protein hazards and pathway readouts naturally nominates monitoring candidates and mechanistic pathways, in line with FDA/NIH frameworks that specify evidentiary expectations for biomarker qualification and use [60]. Finally, because relative protein abundances vary across laboratories and technologies, our paired-ratio classifier offers a practical route to cross-platform deployment by attenuating platform/batch effects.

We benchmarked multiple clustering algorithms and selected Leiden based on internal indices and stability. Disease associations were estimated using multivariable Cox models with prespecified covariates and multiplicity control and survival differences were consistent with risk profiles. The label-transfer classifier reproduced PHAs in an internal cohort and in external datasets spanning distinct platforms and clinical contexts, with clear subtype separability. Taken together, these measures helped ensure the reproducibility of our classification and enhanced its translational potential.

Several caveats merit emphasis. Generalizability may be constrained by biobank ancestry composition and ascertainment, and multi-ancestry replication is needed. Additionally, residual confounding cannot be excluded in observational associations despite adjustment. MR reduces but does not eliminate pleiotropy concerns, and instruments for some PHAs may be modest. Moreover, proteomic coverage is incomplete and platform specific, and some biological pathways remain unmeasured. Prospective and interventional studies are needed to test whether subtype-guided prevention or therapy selection improves outcomes and to quantify temporal stability and transitions of proteome-defined health states under lifestyle or pharmacologic perturbation. Cross-platform and cross-cohort validation are required, given concordance between Olink, SomaScan and mass spectrometry and ancestry-stratified proteogenomic architecture in large cohorts. Moreover, the three external validation cohorts used to assess cross-platform transferability of the PHA classifier are disease-enriched rather than population-based and the MASH cohort is small (n = 57) and restricted to a specific hepatic disease context. Genuine population-based external validation in large prospective cohorts with baseline proteomics and incident disease follow-up remains an important future priority. Finally, the current study is based on a single proteomics time point per participant, and we are therefore unable to assess the longitudinal stability of PHA assignments or quantify rates of inter-archetype transition. Quantifying PHA transition rates and their clinical implications will require repeat proteomics data, such as those being generated by the ongoing UK Biobank repeat-sampling initiative, and represents an important direction for future research.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we identified five ProteoHealth Archetypes (PHAs) in disease-free adults that capture reproducible, biologically coherent baseline proteomic health states and prospectively stratify risks across multiple chronic disease domains. By combining unsupervised proteomic subtyping with a paired protein-ratio classifier, our framework enables transfer of these archetypes across independent cohorts and proteomics platforms. The convergence of observational associations, genetic evidence, and pathway-level biological signals further supports the mechanistic relevance of these subtype-specific health states. Collectively, these findings suggest that plasma proteome-based archetyping may provide a scalable and interpretable approach for early risk stratification, prevention-oriented population screening, and biomarker-guided precision medicine, while future prospective multi-ancestry and longitudinal studies will be important to confirm generalizability and define the stability of these proteomic states over time.
