Whole genome copy number variation analysis to detect genomic regions for resistance to Haemonchus contortus in Sheep
Authors: Hamza Jawad, Olufemi Osonowo, John S. Gilleard, Sean Thompson, Younes Miar, Ghader Manafiazar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRising drug resistance necessitates genomic selection to enhance parasite resistance in sheep. Copy number variations (CNVs) constitute structural genomic alterations that influence livestock traits by modifying gene dosage. This study aimed to identify candidate CNVs that differentiate between Haemonchus contortus-resistant and susceptible Rideau Arcott ewe lambs through comprehensive whole genome sequencing (WGS) analysis.ResultsFecal egg count (FEC) analysis of 32 inoculated animals across five time points (days 21, 28, 35, 42, and 56 post-infection) identified 16 individuals with consistent extreme parasite response phenotypes. The resistant group (n = 9) showed a 10.5-fold lower FEC at day 21 post-infection and maintained 3- to 14-fold lower FEC (p < 0.05) across multiple time points than the susceptible group (n = 7). Consensus calling across CNVpytor, DELLY, and Manta algorithms yielded 22,184 high-confidence CNVs from WGS data at 6.75X genome coverage. These CNVs were consolidated into 3,751 CNV regions (CNVRs) encompassing 65.0 Mb (2.63%) of the sheep autosomal genome. Group-wise comparison identified 329 resistant-only and 133 susceptible-only differential CNVRs. The resistant group harboured 3.5-fold more gain category CNVRs and 13-fold more associated duplicated genes than susceptible animals. A 2.3 Mb duplication containing CLCA1 appeared exclusively in resistant sheep, which is associated with mucus production. Functional enrichment analysis also confirmed that calcium-gated chloride channel activity was exclusively in the resistant group, which is associated with regulating mucus production. Additional resistance-associated gain CNVRs encompassed LTC4S, ROBO1, RASGRP1, PRKCQ, and MBL2. Conversely, susceptible animals exhibited metabolic dysfunction potentially due to PIK3CA and AGXT duplications, with an enrichment of pathways associated with diabetic cardiomyopathy and prion disease. The study suggests associations between resistance-linked CNVRs and genes involved in mucosal barriers, immune cell trafficking, and T-cell responses. In contrast, susceptible group-linked CNVRs are associated with metabolic disruption, suggesting that metabolic disturbance is a key factor in susceptibility.ConclusionsThe first WGS-based CNV analysis successfully identified novel structural variants distinguishing resistant from susceptible Rideau Arcott ewe lambs. The discovery of resistance- and susceptibility-specific gene duplications and metabolic pathways offers new targets for genetic selection. However, the candidate CNVRs warrant validation in larger populations for implementation in breeding programs aimed at improving parasite resistance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-13076-0.

Background

Gastrointestinal nematodes (GINs), especially the blood-feeding Haemonchus contortus, pose a significant challenge to sheep production, as GINs reduce meat and milk production, increase abortion rates, and lead to mortality in infected animals [1–3]. Parasite-infected lambs show 10% higher greenhouse gas emissions per kg of weight gain due to prolonged finishing periods, resulting in increased production costs and environmental footprint [4]. Traditional anthelmintic treatments face declining effectiveness from increasing drug resistance, while organic farming mandates further limit chemical dewormer use [5]. Therefore, selective breeding programs for developing parasite-resistant sheep to control GIN infections should offer a sustainable alternative [6–8].

Fecal egg count (FEC), measured as worms’ eggs per gram (EPG) of hosts’ feces, quantifies parasite burden and serves as a primary indicator of parasite resistance in sheep. Selective breeding programs utilizing FEC as a resistance proxy have successfully enhanced parasite resistance in many sheep breeds, such as Merinos [9, 10]. Indeed, FEC heritability in sheep varies widely (h2 = 0.01 to 0.65) across ages and breeds of the population under study, but it is generally considered a moderately heritable trait [11–13]. However, FEC-based selection approach faces several constraints, including seasonal variation, the need for repeated fecal sampling, and either adequate technical training of farm staff or labour-intensive laboratory analyses. Therefore, the FEC-based approach makes traditional breeding programs costly and time-consuming, as it relies on phenotypes that can only be measured after the animals have been exposed to GINs, as well as requires accurate pedigree documentation.

To overcome traditional breeding constraints, genomic marker-assisted selection has been introduced, which is more suitable for such an expensive and difficult trait to measure. Host resistance to parasites involves multiple immune and physiological defence mechanisms, indicating complex genetic control [14]. Recent advancements in genomic technologies utilize single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and copy number variations (CNVs) for genomic prediction, where these variants are incorporated into prediction models to enhance livestock production accuracy for complex traits [15, 16]. While SNPs involve single nucleotide changes (1 bp), CNVs encompass larger DNA segments ranging from 50 base pairs (bp) to several megabases (Mb) [17, 18].

Copy number variations are genomic phenomena defined by variations in the number of copies of specific DNA segments, where DNA segments are duplicated or deleted relative to a reference genome [18–20]. Although less frequent than SNPs, CNVs affect larger genomic regions and consequently exert greater phenotypic effects on economically important traits [21]. These CNVs impact phenotypes through changing gene structure and dosage alteration, coding sequence disruption, expression modification and exposing recessive alleles [18, 21, 22]. When CNVs occur in overlapping regions across multiple individuals, they form CNV regions (CNVRs), which are particularly important for trait variation within a population [23, 24]. Examples of CNV-trait relationships in animals include duplication of the agouti signalling protein (ASIP) gene linked to white coat colour in sheep [25, 26]. Similarly, the duplication of the CIITA gene confers GIN resistance in cattle [26] while the STX17 gene duplication increases melanoma susceptibility in horses [27]. Hence, CNVs serve as valuable components in genomic prediction models for trait analysis and selection, thereby helping to better understand the genomic background of traits.

Identification of CNVs can be achieved by either high-throughput SNP genotyping array or by whole genome sequence (WGS) technologies. New genomic platforms and datasets for detecting SVs and CNVs, along with functional genes associated with parasite resistance, have been recently compiled for sheep [28, 29]. SNP array-based CNV detection is less costly and has been more widely applied in sheep CNVs studies [24, 26, 30–32]. For example, using the Illumina OvineSNP50 BeadChip array, 14 CNVs were shown to be associated with FEC and packed cell volume, which overlap with parasite resistance quantitative trait loci (QTL) [8].

Despite its low cost and widespread use, SNP array-based technology has several inherent limitations. SNP array-based approaches can only detect pre-selected variants at specific loci, potentially missing other crucial structural variants that may play an essential role in disease resistance [33, 34]. In contrast, WGS technique provides comprehensive genome-wide detection of all variation types (CNVs, indels, and novel mutations), offering complete coverage for identification of resistance-linked genomic markers [24, 35].

The WGS approach provides superior detection of structural variants through multiple techniques, including read-depth, paired-end mapping, and split-read methods [17]. Despite the advantages of WGS and CNV-based genomic markers, no previous study has investigated the structural variants underlying H. contortus resistance in well-defined resistant and susceptible sheep groups using WGS-based CNV analysis. This gap primarily exists due to the previously high cost of WGS, as well as challenges in establishing well-characterized groups of experimental sheep that are both resistant and susceptible to H. contortus infection. However, with decreasing WGS costs, mapping CNVs with high resolution and accuracy has become feasible.

Therefore, this study aimed to identify CNVRs differentially present in H. contortus resistant versus susceptible Rideau Arcott ewe lambs using WGS data from a controlled longitudinal trial. The identified candidate genes and associated pathways could be incorporated into genomic prediction models after validation. Thus, candidate genes and pathways could serve as valuable genetic tools for improving selection accuracy in selective breeding programs and to better understand the genomic background of the traits [36].

Methods

Experimental design and animals

Animals were managed in accordance with the Canadian Council on Animal Care guidelines [37], and the experiment was approved (Protocol #064) by the institutional animal care committee of Olds College of Agriculture and Technology, Olds, AB, Canada. The current study reporting follows the animal research: reporting of in vivo experiments guidelines (ARRIVE) for transparent documentation of animal research. Details of the experimental design, animal sourcing, parasite inoculation, deworming, FEC measurement, and feeding protocols are reported in other studies [38].

In short, the study involved 78 Rideau Arcott ewe lambs (7–8 months old; 44.4 ± 6.1 kg BW) considered in a 57-day, longitudinal, controlled trial with a 2 × 2 factorial design. The experimental cohort was randomly selected from a single commercial source (commercial farm near Olds, AB, Canada) to maintain genetic homogeneity and minimize management-related variation. Animals received ad libitum access to water, wheat straw, and a 4:1 whole barley-protein supplement ration (16% crude protein, 4 Mcal/kg digestible energy).

Parasite challenge and treatment groups

This experiment was part of a larger study in which the animals were randomly assigned to two groups: the inoculated (IN) group and the control (CT) group. The health status of all animals was assessed one day before parasite inoculation (day − 1), confirming they were strongyle-free and had complete blood cell counts within the normal range. The following day (day 0), the IN group (n = 39) received 5,000 third-stage H. contortus larvae (L3) from a characterized drug-susceptible strain obtained from Louisiana State University, USA. Louisiana State University has been a recognized source of characterized H. contortus strains for parasitology research [39]. The pathogenicity of our used strain was confirmed through typical infection dynamics with eggs first detected at 21 days post infection (dpi), peak FEC at 28 dpi, reduced growth performance, and development of anemia in the IN group as compared to the CT group [38, 40].

On day zero, H. contortus L3 inoculation in the IN group was achieved by a standardized protocol. As per protocol, the L3 containing solution was continuously agitated on site and delivered via a 5 mL BD syringe by administering 2 mL parasite L3 inoculum followed by 2 mL distilled water ingestion per animal. In contrast, the CT group received a sham inoculum in the form of distilled water. The CT group animals acted as negative controls to confirm that no natural infection occurred during the experimental period.

On day 28, half of each group was drenched (D) with anthelmintic drugs [ivermectin (Ivomec®) and fenbendazole (Safe-Guard®)] as per manufacturer guidelines. In vivo efficacy of this combined drench against the H. contortus strain was confirmed by complete FEC clearance (0.0 ± 0.0 EPG) in the IN-D group by 35 dpi, maintained through 56 dpi [38]. In contrast, the other half of each group did not receive any drug (ND) and received only distilled water. This resulted in the creation of four treatment groups: CT-D (control-drenched), CT-ND (control non-drenched), IN-D (inoculated-drenched), and IN-ND (inoculated non-drenched). This 2 × 2 factorial experimental design and group allocations are illustrated in detail in Fig. 1. Animals were housed in four separate outdoor pens to prevent inter-group transmission of infection.

Sample collection and analysis

Fecal samples (~ 10 g) from the rectum of individual animals (n = 78) were collected on days − 1, 7, 21, 28, 35, 42, and 56 and stored at 4 °C. The FEC analysis was performed within 7 days after collection using the modified McMaster technique [41]. Briefly, 2 g of fecal material was mixed in 28 mL saturated magnesium sulphate flotation solution (in-house prepared), followed by counting eggs in the two chambers of the McMaster slide. The egg counts were converted to EPG through multiplication by a factor of 50 [41]. On the last day of the trial (day 57), ear tissue samples were collected to be used for WGS.

To monitor subclinical infection progression and ensure animal welfare compliance during the 57-day trial period, several other physiological parameters were measured as reported in our other studies [40]. These include sequential body weight measurements, complete blood count profiles, daily feed intake records, and clinical health assessments [40]. All samples were collected from individual animals using manual restraint procedures without the use of anesthesia, in accordance with approved animal welfare protocols. At trial completion (day 57), all animals were confirmed healthy with no clinical signs of disease and were maintained under standard management practices until further processing.

Resistance phenotype classification

FEC was measured at seven time points (days − 1, 7, 21, 28, 35, 42, and 56 post-infection), of which five yielded detectable FEC values for IN-ND animals (days 21, 28, 35, 42, and 56) and two for IN-D animals (days 21 and 28). All time points with detectable FEC were used to classify animals into resistant and susceptible groups. All IN animals were classified into three resistance categories based on FEC thresholds relative to the group mean (µ) and standard deviation (SD). Specifically, resistant animals (high resistance, H) were defined as those with FEC < µ − 0.5 SD. Resilient animals (medium resistance, M) were defined as those with FEC within µ ± 0.5 SD (i.e., µ − 0.5 SD ≤ FEC ≤ µ + 0.5 SD). Susceptible animals (low resistance, L) were defined as those with FEC > µ + 0.5 SD.

However, further steps (a two-step approach) have been taken to confirm and define the extreme phenotypes. First, animals (n = 32) with complete FEC records from both IN-D and IN-ND groups were combined for days 21 and 28 (the common time points for both groups) to establish the overall ranking. Second, each group was analyzed separately: IN-D animals (n = 14) were ranked only on days 21 and 28 (as these were their only measurement points before drenching), while IN-ND animals (n = 18) were ranked across all time points with positive FEC values (21, 28, 35, 42, and 56 dpi). The combined groups analysis for days 21 and 28 served to validate that animals maintained their phenotypic classification (H, M, and L) whether analyzed within their original group or in the combined dataset, thus increasing the robustness of the phenotype selection criteria.

To ensure selection stability and repeatability, strictly individuals with consistent extreme phenotypes (R or S) or those showing only adjacent category shifts (M→R, R→M, M→S, or S→M) across time points were selected. Animals with extreme transitions (R→S or S→R) were excluded as inconsistent. Furthermore, only animals maintaining classification consistency in both combined and individual group analyses were selected for WGS.

FEC data are commonly zero-inflated and over dispersed. Therefore, non-parametric tests were considered appropriate for group comparisons. Statistical comparisons between resistant and susceptible groups were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test (p < 0.05), which is rank-based and does not assume normality. For repeatability analysis, FEC values were log-transformed to stabilize variance before calculating the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC), which is sensitive to measurement scale and variance structure. Spearman rank correlations were also calculated across measurement days to assess the consistency in FEC ranking among the selected animals.

Whole genome sequencing and quality control

Genomic DNA was extracted from ear tissues at Neogen Canada’s Edmonton facility, AB, Canada, where WGS was subsequently performed. The library preparation was performed using Illumina Nextera, and sequencing was conducted on the NovaSeq 6000 platform with 150 bp paired-end read technology. The sequencing data files were received via the SFTP protocol and included quality metrics with Q30 scores embedded in FASTQ files.

Bioinformatics analysis was performed by assessing sequence quality with FastQC v0.11.9, then filtering low-quality reads and adapter sequences using fastp v0.23.2 [42]. The filtering parameters included removing reads with ≥ 3 unidentified bases, average Phred quality score ≤ 20, and lengths below 50 bases. Additionally, adapter sequences and possible contaminants were also removed [23]. Filtered high-quality reads were aligned to the sheep reference genome ARS-UI_Ramb_v2.0 [43] using BWA-MEM2 v2.2.1 [44] with read groups added during alignment. This assembly is widely used in sheep genomic studies [45–47], which has 99.99% autosomal sequence identity with ARS-UI_Ramb_v3.0, with v3.0 only adding the Y chromosome that is absent in our ewe lambs samples.

The aligned sequences were finally converted to binary alignment map (BAM) format and sorted using SAMtools v1.15.1 [48]. Duplicate sequences were removed using Picard MarkDuplicates v2.27.5. The final BAM files were indexed using SAMtools, and a comprehensive quality assessment was performed using SAMtools flagstat, Picard alignment metrics, and QualiMap v2.3 to evaluate mapping quality and coverage depth. Individual sorted and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) duplicate-removed BAM files were used for further CNV analysis. Only samples meeting minimum WGS coverage thresholds were retained for downstream genomic analyses.

CNVs detection

CNVs detection utilized three distinct but complementary algorithms: CNVpytor v1.3.1 [49], DELLY v1.1.6 [50], and Manta v1.6.0 [51]. The CNVpytor software utilized a read-depth approach, and both DELLY and Manta apply a combination of paired-end and split-read mapping approaches [18].

CNVpytor v1.3.1 [49] is a computationally efficient implementation of CNVnator [52]. A custom configuration was generated for the sheep reference genome, specifying all 26 autosomes with species-specific guanine-cytosine content files for accurate bias correction [49]. After testing multiple bin sizes (100 bp, 500 bp, 1000 bp, and 10,000 bp), 1000 bp was selected as optimal for low WGS coverage, consistent with low-coverage livestock recommendations [53]. Stringent quality filters (p < 0.05, size ≥ 1 kb, Q0: 0-0.5, pN: 0-0.05, dG ≥ 100 kb) were applied to ensure reliable CNV calls. The 1 kb size threshold minimized noise, as CNV detection algorithms show reduced accuracy for smaller variants [54]. CNVpytor-based approach successfully detected both deletions and duplications across all autosomal chromosomes.

DELLY v1.1.6 [50] and Manta v1.6.0 [51] were used with their default parameters. Calls were filtered based on three main criteria to ensure quality and relevance [18]. First, quality filters excluded calls flagged as IMPRECISE by DELLY. Second, only calls with PASS flags from both DELLY and Manta were retained. Third, size filters removed CNVs smaller than 1 kb or larger than 5 Mb. To maintain consistency with CNVpytor and enable consensus calling, the DELLY and Manta analysis was restricted to deletions and duplications only. This excluded other variant types, such as translocations and inversions, that DELLY and Manta can detect [18]. Aligning with previous studies [34, 55], CNVs greater than 5 Mb were excluded to minimize the likelihood of false-positive detections arising from repetitive sequences or assembly ambiguities.

High-confidence CNVs were identified using SURVIVOR v1.0.7 [56] with default parameters to merge CNVs from all three detection algorithms. This included the individual VCF files from CNVpytor, DELLY, and Manta (48 files; 16 samples × 3 tools), which were merged with the following parameters: 1 kb maximum breakpoint distance, ≥ 2/3 caller support, consideration of CNV type and strand orientation, and 50% maximum size difference. This conservative approach reduced false positives while maintaining sensitivity [56].

CNVRs identification

CNVRs were determined using a custom Python implementation of the reciprocal overlap algorithm as described in CNVRuler v1.2 [57]. We identified CNVRs using all 16 samples collectively to ensure unbiased detection and consistent boundaries across this extreme phenotype dataset. This minimizes technical artifacts and captures CNVs present in both resistant and susceptible groups, regardless of frequency. Individual CNVs from all 16 samples were merged into CNVRs using ≥ 50% reciprocal overlap. For two CNVs (CNV1 spanning positions X to Y and CNV2 spanning Z to W, where X < Z < Y < W), reciprocal overlap was calculated as the minimum of two ratios: the overlap length divided by the length of each CNV. The CNVs with ≥ 0.5 overlap merged into a single CNVR (X to W) [58]. To further reduce spurious calls, only CNVRs in ≥ 2 samples were retained [59]. The resulting CNVRs were classified as “loss” (deletions), “gain” (duplications), or “mixed” (overlapping loss/gain regions). Linear regression analysis was performed to quantify the relationship between chromosome length and CNVR count across all autosomes.

Differential CNVR analysis for candidate marker discovery

To identify differential CNVRs, we used all collectively defined CNVRs from 16 samples and compared their frequencies between resistant and susceptible groups. CNVRs were classified into three biologically distinct categories based on their distribution patterns: (1) resistant-only (present exclusively in resistant animals), (2) susceptible-only (present exclusively in susceptible animals), and (3) common (present in both groups). Among all common CNVRs, we identified a subset (common-significant) that showed significantly (p < 0.05) different frequencies between resistant and susceptible groups. These common-significant CNVRs frequency differences were tested for statistical significance (p < 0.05) using Fisher’s exact test. For each common-significant CNVR, a 2 × 2 contingency table was constructed comparing CNVR presence between resistant and susceptible groups. Fisher’s exact test was chosen because it provides accurate p-values for small sample sizes without relying on asymptotic approximations [60]. Finally, the candidate (differential) CNVRs comprised all resistant-only, susceptible-only, and common-significant CNVRs.

For each group-specific CNVRs category, we calculated their descriptive statistics, including total CNVRs counts, structural types (gain/loss/mixed), size distributions, gene content, and chromosomal coverage. To compare structural type distributions (proportions of gains, losses, and mixed CNVRs) among the three categories, we used chi-square tests on 3 × 3 contingency tables. Similarly, size category distributions (1–5 kb, 5–50 kb, > 50 kb) were compared using chi-square tests. CNVR sizes were compared among groups using the Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for post-hoc comparisons. Mean sizes were additionally compared using ANOVA on log-transformed data to address the skewed distributions. The proportion of CNVRs containing genes was compared using chi-square tests on 3 × 2 contingency tables (genes present/absent × three categories).

To examine the chromosomal distribution of candidate CNVRs, we calculated the number and percentage of each CNVR category (resistant-only, susceptible-only, and common-significant) per chromosome. CNVR density was calculated as the total number of CNVRs per Mb of chromosome length, using the sheep reference genome. Chromosomes were evaluated for potential hotspots based on CNVR density exceeding twice the median density across all autosomes.

To quantify the contribution of CNVRs to FEC variation, Principal Component Regression (PC-Reg) was applied to the presence/absence matrix of differential CNVRs identified from the group-wise comparison. PC-Reg was necessary to address the high-dimensional challenge, where the number of predictors exceeded the number of observations. The first five principal components were extracted based on cumulative variance explained, and used as predictor variables in linear regression models with log-transformed FEC values (log(FEC + 1)) of 21 and 28 dpi as response variables. The proportion of phenotypic variance explained (R²) and adjusted R² were calculated to quantify CNVR contribution to parasite resistance phenotype.

Gene annotation and functional enrichment

We first performed gene annotation and enrichment analysis on CNVRs from all 16 samples. Functional enrichment was then carried out separately for genes within resistant-only, susceptible-only, and common-significant CNVRs to identify CNVR type-specific pathways and functions. Total gene counts among groups were compared using chi-square goodness-of-fit tests. The number of genes per CNVR was analyzed using ANOVA with category (resistant-only, susceptible-only, and common-significant) as a fixed factor.

Genes overlapping with CNVRs were identified using the NCBI RefSeq annotation for the sheep reference genome. The BEDTools v2.30.0 intersect function [61] was used to catalogue genes with ≥ 1 bp CNVR overlap. The Gene Ontology (GO), functional annotation and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses were carried out using g: Profiler [62]. Analyses were performed using R packages, including gprofiler2 version 0.2.3 [63]. The analysis included three GO categories: biological process, molecular function, and cellular component, with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction (p < 0.05). All statistical analyses were performed in R v4.4.3, with significance set at p < 0.05, unless stated otherwise. Identified genes and metabolic pathways were evaluated for biological relevance through a comprehensive literature review.

Results

Phenotypic classification of resistant and susceptible groups

The CT animals (CT-D and CT-ND groups) maintained zero FEC values throughout the 57-day experimental period. These animals were therefore excluded from genomic analysis as they would not contribute to phenotype-based classification. This group was used to ensure that there was no natural contamination. The IN animals (IN-D and IN-ND groups) showed no detectable eggs at 7 days post-infection (dpi). Initial egg detection occurred at 21 dpi only in IN animals.

From a total of 39 IN animals, those with missing FEC at critical time points (days 21 and 28) were excluded (n = 7) from phenotypic classification analysis (Fig. 1). The two-step classification process was then applied, which yielded 17 animals with extreme phenotypes (nine resistant, eight susceptible), which subsequently underwent WGS analysis (Fig. 1). The extreme phenotypes were distributed as follows: IN-D yielded four resistant and four susceptible animals, while IN-ND yielded five resistant and four susceptible animals. One IN-ND susceptible animal was subsequently excluded due to inadequate WGS coverage (< 5X).

The Mann-Whitney U test revealed the most significant (p < 0.001) phenotypic divergence at 21 dpi, where resistant sheep showed 10.5-fold lower FEC than susceptible sheep (Additional file 1: Table S1A). The peak infection was observed on 28 dpi when the susceptible animals reached the highest mean FEC (10771.0 ± 2528.0 EPG) compared to resistant animals (1489.0 ± 303.0 EPG; p < 0.01), as shown in Additional file 1: Fig. S1. The largest fold difference (13.9-fold difference, p < 0.05) occurred at 35 dpi, while significant (p < 0.05) differences between groups also appeared on 56 dpi (Additional file 1: Table S1A).

Repeatability analysis revealed ICC values of 0.52 for all 16 selected IN animals (combined IN-D and IN-ND groups) when analyzed at the critical classification time points (days 21 and 28). The IN-ND group (n = 9) showed higher repeatability when tracked longitudinally across five time points (days 21 to 56), achieving an ICC of 0.73. Pairwise Spearman correlations between days 21 to 56 timepoints for the IN-ND group ranged from 0.59 (days 21 vs. 42) to 0.97 (days 28 vs. 35), indicating strong rank-order consistency despite individual FEC variations (Additional file 1: Table S1B).

Whole genome sequencing quality assessment

Quality control of WGS data revealed one sample (17D) with inadequate coverage (4.6X), significantly below the mean coverage of 6.75X achieved in the other samples. Following the exclusion of one low-quality sample, the final dataset comprised 16 animals, consisting of 9 resistant and 7 susceptible individuals (Fig. 1). These 16 samples yielded 170.4 million paired-end raw reads per sample (Additional file 1: Table S2). After quality control and adapter trimming, an average of 72.3% of raw reads (123.2 million reads per sample) were retained for downstream analysis. The coverage depth of each individual ranged from 6.0X to 7.4X (Additional file 1: Table S2).

Detection of CNVs

The three complementary algorithms (CNVpytor, DELLY, and Manta) identified varying numbers of CNVs across the 16 sheep samples (Additional file 1: Table S3). After merging the results of these three algorithms, we retrieved 22,184 high-confidence CNV events (Table 1) with an average of 1,386.5 per animal. The distribution of CNVs across 26 autosomes was uneven (Fig. 2A), with chromosome 1 containing the highest number of CNVs (2,608) and chromosome 24 containing the lowest (129) (Additional file 1: Table S4).

Number and distribution of CNVR

Merging of 22,184 high-confidence CNVs yielded 3,751 CNVRs. These CNVRs comprised 3,659 loss regions, 67 gain regions, and 25 mixed regions (Fig. 2B). The 3,751 CNVRs covered 65.0 Mb of the sheep autosomal genome, representing 2.63% of the total autosomal sequence (2.47 Gb), with detailed coverage statistics provided in Additional file 1: Table S5. A comparison of CNV and CNVR counts, genome coverage, and detection platforms across published sheep CNV studies is provided in Additional file 1: Table S6. CNVRs were distributed unevenly across the 26 autosomes, with chromosome 1 harbouring the highest number of CNVRs and chromosome 24 the fewest (Table 2). Regression analysis revealed a highly significant positive correlation between chromosome length and CNVR count (R²=0.93, p < 0.001).

Functional annotation and gene enrichment analyses

Analysis of CNVR gene content from collectively analyzed 16 samples revealed 1,542 unique genes within or partially overlapping with 1,351 (36.01%) of the detected CNVRs (Additional file 1: Table S8). Of these genes, 875 (56.7%) were well-annotated protein-coding genes, while 667 (43.3%) were predicted genes (LOC identifiers). The results of GO analysis revealed 158 significantly enriched terms (FDR-adjusted p < 0.05), comprising 79 biological processes, 38 cellular components, and 41 molecular function terms (Additional file 1: Table S9). The top ten enriched terms from each GO category are shown in Additional file 1: Fig. S2.

Group-wise comparison of CNVRs

Of the 3,751 total CNVRs, 503 differential/candidate CNVRs were identified through group-wise comparison, while the remaining 3,248 showed no significant frequency differences between the two extreme phenotypic groups. The candidate CNVRs consisted of CNVRs exclusive to either resistant or susceptible animals, as well as those showing significant (p < 0.05) frequency differences between groups (Table 3). Resistant-only CNVRs were most abundant (65.4%), followed by susceptible-only (26.4%) and common-significant CNVRs (8.2%). These differential CNVRs showed varying chromosomal distribution patterns across the genome, with detailed chromosomal statistics provided in Additional file 1: Table S10 and significance levels illustrated in Additional file 1: Fig. S3. The distribution of CNVR types (gains, losses, and mixed) differed significantly (p = 0.01) among the three categories (Table 3). Both resistant-only and susceptible-only groups exhibited identical gain-to-loss ratios (0.03), and losses accounted for 97% of CNVRs in both groups. The single mixed CNVR was found only in the common-significant category (Table 3).

The common-significant CNVRs were significantly larger (median 2.2 kb) than both resistant-only (p < 0.05) and susceptible-only CNVRs (p < 0.05). This size difference was driven by the presence of large CNVRs in the common-significant category. The largest common-significant CNVR spans 5.03 Mb on chromosome 7. There was a greater size range of CNVRs in the resistant-only group. The most common-significant CNVR frequency differences were observed between CNVR_2659 and CNVR_3161 (both p = 0.003), which were present in 22% of resistant animals but in 100% of susceptible animals. Conversely, the most common-significant CNVR_2482 showed the strongest frequency difference between groups (p = 0.005), being present in 100% of resistant animals but only 29% of susceptible animals.

CNVR density across chromosomes ranged from 0.49 to 2.25 CNVRs per Mb, with a median density of 1.55 CNVRs/Mb (Additional file 1: Table S10). Chromosome 6 exhibited the highest overall CNVR density (2.25 CNVRs/Mb), while chromosome 24 had the lowest CNVR density (0.49 CNVRs/Mb). Notably, no chromosome exceeded the threshold for statistical hotspots (> 3.09 CNVRs/Mb, representing twice the median density). Chromosome 22 harboured CNVR_3421, the only mixed 4.4 Mb common-significant CNVR (p = 0.02), with 12 genes (7 well-annotated, 5 LOC) (Additional file 1: Table S10). The proportion of candidate CNVRs relative to total CNVRs varied by chromosome, ranging from 4.8% (chromosome 24) to 15.8% (chromosome 13). Specifically, the resistant-only CNVRs consistently represented 4.3 to 15.8% of total CNVRs per chromosome (Additional file 1: Table S10). PC-Reg results revealed that the 503 differential CNVRs explained 87.60% of phenotypic variation in FEC at 21 dpi (Adjusted R² = 81.40%, p < 0.001) and 69.43% at 28 dpi (Adjusted R² = 54.15%, p < 0.01).

Gene content and functional analysis of candidate CNVRs

Of the 503 candidate CNVRs, 173 (34.4%) contained annotated genes while 330 (65.6%) were in intergenic regions. These 173 gene-containing CNVRs encompassed 224 unique genes, distributed as 158 in resistant-only CNVRs, 51 in susceptible-only CNVRs, and 22 in common-significant CNVRs. Detailed gene listings are provided in Additional file 1: Table S11. Resistant-only CNVRs were distributed across all 26 chromosomes, whereas susceptible-only CNVRs spanned 24 chromosomes. Notably, resistant animals exhibited more gain CNVRs (7 vs. 2; 3.5-fold) and associated genes (26 vs. 2; 13-fold) than susceptible animals, with key resistance-associated gain genes including CLCA1, CASQ2, PDE11A, DPYS, SCEL, WWOX, and ARNTL (Table 4). In contrast, susceptible animals harboured only two gain genes: PIK3CA and AGXT. The common-significant CNVRs showed the highest gene density per CNVR compared with the resistant-only and susceptible-only groups (Table 3).

Discussion

Genomic selection for parasite resistance is considered an effective, cumulative, and sustainable solution to control GIN infections [7, 64, 65]. Previous genome-wide association studies have identified genomic markers for parasite resistance in sheep [66, 67]; however, CNVs, despite their greater phenotypic impact, remain understudied. This study represents the first CNV characterization in the Rideau Arcott sheep breed, which is a prominent Canadian composite breed developed for prolificacy and maternal traits [68]. Furthermore, we overcome two major limitations of previous sheep parasite CNV studies [8, 69], which relied on SNP arrays with either natural GIN infections or presumed breed differences.

Our factorial design, though implemented for the broader multi-objective studies [38, 40], provided specific advantages for CNV analysis by enabling the evaluation of multiple experimental factors at once and uncovering interactions between CNV status and time points. Control groups maintained zero FEC throughout the time points, which confirmed the absence of natural infection. The design was originally implemented to study deworming effects on animal recovery, but for this CNV analysis, it provided additional phenotyping time points: IN-ND animals were monitored until day 56, yielding five FEC measurements compared to two for IN-D animals. Given natural FEC variation and our sample size, these extended time points increased the reliability of resistance and susceptibility rankings before genomic analysis.

Initial FEC detection in the IN group at 21 dpi confirmed successful H. contortus subclinical infection establishment, consistent with its 18 to 21 day prepatent period [70]. The pathogenicity of the H. contortus strain was comprehensively documented in our companion studies, showing reduced growth performance and altered blood parameters in the IN group as compared to the CT group [38, 40]. Nemabiome sequencing confirmed a monospecific H. contortus infection, ensuring that resistance phenotypes reflect single-parasite responses rather than mixed infections [38], thereby strengthening the validity of our genomic findings. The striking phenotypic divergence between resistant and susceptible groups, with resistant animals maintaining up to 13.9-fold lower FEC throughout the infection period, confirmed successful selection of extreme phenotypes for genomic comparison.

Our extreme phenotype sampling (16 from 32 IN animals) enhances CNV detection power, with sample sizes comparable to previous successful CNV studies using 5 [71], 20 [22], 28 [72], 32 [73], and 36 samples [24]. Controlled longitudinal challenge trials and WGS analyses are inherently resource-intensive, which constrains the feasible sample size. To address this, we applied a multi-stage selection pipeline. We initially enrolled 78 animals housed under controlled conditions to prevent cross-contamination. From these, 39 animals were inoculated, and 32 had complete phenotypic records at the critical classification time points. Finally, only 16 animals with consistent extreme phenotypes were retained for WGS. This stepwise approach ensured that the selected cohort represented the most phenotypically stable and biologically distinct individuals, rather than a simple percentile cutoff. The FEC repeatability of 16 animals (from IN-D and IN-ND) at days 21–28 was moderate (ICC = 0.52), reflecting limited sampling points. However, a subset (n = 9) of the animals from the IN-ND group tracked across five time points (days 21–56) showed strong repeatability (ICC = 0.73), demonstrating that longitudinal sampling improves measurement reliability.

The ± 0.5 SD FEC thresholds, with longitudinal phenotypic consistency across multiple time points (21–56 dpi), further strengthened and made robust the phenotypic identification of resistant and susceptible groups. Future studies with larger cohorts and multiple time points could explore more stringent FEC thresholds (± 2 SD) to improve phenotype ranking and identify higher-confidence CNVRs.

Our three-algorithm consensus approach [18, 74] yielded 22,184 CNVs, consistent with other WGS-based sheep studies, which reported 18,152 to 39,145 CNVs [75, 76]. In line with this, the high number of CNVs further shows the superior detection power of WGS CNV studies (9,789 to 1,747,604 CNVs [24, 73]) compared with SNP array-based CNV studies (173 to 8,124 CNVs [8, 30, 31, 77, 78]). Our 3,751 CNVRs covering 2.6% of the sheep autosomal genome align with similar coverage in other studies reporting 2.3% [30], 2.7% [72], 2.7% [24], 3.2% [69] in sheep, and cattle with 2.5% [79] CNVRs coverage. However, CNVRs coverage varies widely across other sheep studies, from as low as 0.60% [71] to as high as 10% [75]. These variations in CNVs and CNVRs reflect factors such as different detection algorithms, filtering stringency, population size, genetic background, sequencing technology, and genome assembly quality [18, 77]. A detailed comparison of CNV and CNVR statistics across published sheep studies is provided in Additional file 1: Table S6.

The strong correlation between CNVR count and chromosome length confirms that structural variants distribute proportionally genome-wide, with longer chromosomes harbouring more CNVRs [77, 80]. The predominance of deleted CNVs in our study is consistent with other sheep WGS-based CNV studies examining traits such as wool quality [22], which is attributed to technical limitations in insertion detection and biological constraints on duplication formation [18, 81]. Biologically, non-allelic homologous recombination favours deletion formation, while technically, deletions under stronger purifying selection are more readily detected [82]. Moreover, the currently available end-mapping methods have a limited ability to detect insertions, and they only identify duplications when mapped reads are shorter than the fragment lengths [18, 22].

We identified 503 differential CNVRs spanning 224 unique genes between resistant and susceptible groups through a controlled H. contortus challenge, representing the first WGS-based comparison of CNVs for parasite resistance in sheep. The resistant group had more CNVRs (329 resistant-only vs. 133 susceptible-only), indicating resistance involves more extensive structural variation. The only similar approach was reported in cattle by Hou et al. [21], who used SNP arrays rather than the WGS approach. While Hou et al. [21] also found higher CNVRs in parasite-resistant cattle compared to susceptible cattle, although the difference was minimal (297 vs. 282), likely due to the SNP-based detection method used compared to our WGS approach and the sample size difference.

The high phenotypic variance explained by CNVRs’ contribution to FEC variation should be interpreted with caution. This likely reflects our extreme-phenotype sampling design rather than the proportion of FEC variance expected in an unselected field population. While structural variants typically explain 2–10% in random populations [83, 84], our approach is enriched for large-effect variants through multiple selection steps. We selected 16 animals with consistent extreme phenotypes from 32 infected animals and focused on 503 differential CNVRs from 3,751 total CNVRs. The 10.5-fold FEC difference between the resistant and susceptible groups further enriched for large-effect variants in our analysis. Furthermore, our controlled design minimized other sources of FEC variation, as animals were the same breed and age, housed under identical conditions, and received a standardized H. contortus challenge dose. Therefore, these findings should be considered exploratory and require validation in larger, independent populations under more diverse environmental conditions.

The large-sized CNVRs in our study were exclusively observed in resistant animals or showed significant frequency differences between the resistant and susceptible groups. The largest CNVR of 5.03 Mb (common-significant category) and another large CNVR of 2.3 Mb (resistant-only category) align with the CNVRs reported in other sheep studies, where CNVs up to 5.02 Mb [73] and 2.3 Mb [77] have been documented for wool-related traits. In contrast, susceptible-only CNVRs remained smaller in size. These size differences suggest that large CNVRs may confer resistance advantages through amplified gene dosage across extended genomic regions, enabling coordinated pleiotropic effects on multiple immune pathways [85].

The resistant-only CNVRs appeared across all 26 chromosomes, while susceptible-only CNVRs were absent from chromosomes 24 and 26, confirming that parasite resistance involves genome-wide structural adaptations. Although previous studies identified immune genes RIOK3 (at chromosome 24) and TLR3 (at chromosome 26) as resistance candidates [36], our CNVRs revealed different genes on chromosome 26 (e.g. TENM3 and PRAG1), suggesting multiple independent resistance mechanisms per chromosome. Furthermore, the absence of statistical hotspots (i.e., no chromosomes exceeded twice the median CNVR density) supports a polygenic architecture for H. contortus resistance [13, 86]. The 4.4 Mb common-significant mixed type CNVR on chromosome 22 represents a major structural variant in a chromosome previously implicated in parasite resistance through TLR9 and other immune genes [16, 36, 85, 87–89]. The 12 genes (including MBL2, PCDH15, PRKG1, DKK1, and A1CF) reported in our study within this 4.4 Mb CNVR region may represent additional resistance mechanisms or exert long-range regulatory effects on the known immune loci.

The ovine Major Histocompatibility Complex on chromosome 20 is divided into class IIa (~ 25.35–25.81 Mb) and class IIb (~ 7.16–7.43 Mb) subregions separated by an ~ 18.5 Mb non-MHC insertion [90, 91]. Our analysis identified 55 CNVRs on chromosome 20; however, none overlapped with the classical MHC regions, and no classical MHC genes were found within any CNVRs genome-wide. Similar to a cattle CNV study, where alternative immune genes were associated with GINs resistance [21], our findings highlight CLCA1 and MBL2 as contributors to H. contortus resistance, suggesting multiple complementary immune pathways.

We identified gain-CNVRs revealing both beneficial immune gene duplications and harmful metabolic gene amplifications that warrant opposite selection strategies. The resistant animals harboured significantly more gain CNVRs (7 vs. 2) and associated genes (26 vs. 2) than the susceptible animals. These dosage-sensitive duplications are likely associated with the resistance phenotype through threshold effects on immune responsiveness, particularly relevant for immune gene regulation [92, 93]. These findings suggest gain category CNVRs as potential selection targets that require validation.

The duplication of PIK3CA and AGXT observed in the susceptible sheep group may represent candidate markers associated with susceptibility and metabolic disruption. PIK3CA plays a central role in PI3K/AKT signalling, which regulates cellular metabolism, survival, immune-cell function, and inflammatory responses [94, 95]. Increased PIK3CA activity has been associated with metabolic reprogramming, altered mitochondrial homeostasis, and disease susceptibility across species [96, 97]. Parasites may also interact with host PI3K/AKT signalling pathways to influence immune-cell apoptosis, macrophage activation, and regulatory T-cell function [98], providing a plausible mechanism through which altered PIK3CA copy number could affect host response to H. contortus infection.

Similarly, AGXT is involved in hepatic glyoxylate metabolism, and disruption of this pathway can affect oxalate metabolism, mitochondrial function, oxidative stress, and inflammatory signalling [99–101]. Therefore, AGXT copy number gain may plausibly reflect altered metabolic status that could influence innate immune function during parasitic infection. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as the observed duplications may also reflect linkage with nearby causal variants or population-specific effects. Further functional validation and replication in larger sheep populations are required before these genes can be used in genomic selection or negative-screening programs.

The enrichment of diabetic cardiomyopathy and prion disease pathways exclusively in susceptible animals suggests that metabolic dysfunction may underlie susceptibility to H. contortus infection. These pathways share common features of mitochondrial impairment and cellular stress, indicating that animals unable to maintain metabolic homeostasis during parasitic challenge become overwhelmed by the physiological burden of infection. In sheep, susceptibility has likewise been associated with heightened stress reactivity and prolonged inflammation after GIN challenge [102], suggesting that impaired energy metabolism and elevated cellular stress may fundamentally compromise anti-parasitic defences. The association between metabolic disruption and parasite susceptibility extends beyond sheep, as susceptible cattle also showed CNV-mediated enrichment of metabolic disease networks [21].

The functional enrichment analysis of the resistant group further confirmed the parasite resistance as a polygenic trait involving multiple functional groups that create a multi-layered defence strategy [13, 86]. Here, we examine the specific genes underlying these mechanisms. Among the 158 resistance-specific genes identified, the seven gain CNVRs, which contain 26 genes, warrant particular attention as prime candidates for positive selection. The duplication of CLCA1 in our resistant animals aligns with previous studies identifying it as the most up-regulated gene in sheep resistant to GINs such as Teladorsagia circumcincta, as it regulates innate immunity [103, 104]. CLCA1 is implicated in regulating mucus production, with abomasal mucus containing mucin as its primary component, serving as a crucial innate immune barrier against GINs [105]. The coordination between calcium-gated chloride channels and ion-binding functions suggests immediate barrier responses through mucus regulation [105, 106].

The 2.3 Mb CNVR duplication on chromosome 1 also contains SELENOF, HS2ST1, SH3GLB1, and LMO4, suggesting coordinated amplification of defence pathways. Additional resistance-specific gain CNVRs include genes such as DPYS, CASQ2, PDE11A, SCEL, WWOX, and ARNTL. Notably, DPYS appears in a gain CNVR despite exhibiting significant downregulation in the zebra finch spleen following lipopolysaccharide exposure, suggesting its involvement in immune-related metabolic reprogramming during acute-phase responses to bacterial infection [107].

Beyond gain category CNVRs, resistance-associated genes within loss CNVRs provide additional insights, including several genes with established immune functions. The NELL1 gene has been associated with GINs resistance in Akkaraman sheep through its involvement in cell adhesion and signal transduction pathways essential for immune responses [88]. The CPQ gene, previously identified under positive selection in Iranian sheep breeds adapted to water-deficient environments, suggests a potential link between environmental stress resilience and disease resistance mechanisms [108]. The CNTNAP2 gene, associated with serum aspartate aminotransferase levels in Akkaraman sheep and implicated in human neurological disorders and immune dysregulation, highlights cross-species relevance to disease resistance [109].

The LTC4S gene appears within a loss CNVR exclusive to resistant sheep, and it encodes leukotriene C4 synthase, which drives anti-helminth immunity in mice [110] and being upregulated in sheep [111] and goat [112] tuft cells during H. contortus infection. This also suggests that optimal gene dosage rather than simple overexpression may determine the resistance mechanism. Similarly, ROBO1 appears in a loss CNVR category exclusive to resistant sheep, and it is associated with leukocyte chemotaxis and immune cell trafficking to infection sites [113]. ROBO1 has been reported for Newcastle disease resistance in chickens [114]. RASGRP1 is associated with disease resistance markers through its role in T-cell development, homeostasis, and differentiation [115]. Additional immune-related genes in resistance-specific loss CNVRs include PRKCQ, which encodes protein kinase C-theta. PRKCQ is essential for protective T-cell responses against Toxoplasma gondii, with its absence resulting in impaired T-cell activation and fatal encephalitis in mice [116].

The common-significant 4.4 Mb mixed CNVR harboured a gene MBL2, which encodes mannose-binding lectin for innate host defence, which showed association with FAMACHA scores in sheep and overlapped with QTLs for immunoglobulin A levels [16, 89], supporting its role in parasite resistance mechanisms. This mixed CNVR also contains PCDH15, PRKG1, DKK1, and A1CF, representing a complex structural variant with potential pleiotropic effects.

Future studies should validate the candidate 503 CNVRs, especially those in the gain category, both within larger Rideau Arcott populations and across other sheep breeds, to determine breed-specific versus universal resistance mechanisms. Validation within Rideau Arcott populations across different farms would confirm the consistency of these associations, while cross-breed validation would establish the broader applicability of identified candidate CNVRs. While our extreme phenotype approach enhanced the power of CNV detection, larger sample sizes would enable statistical validation and effect size estimation. The functional interpretation of candidate genes identified in this study is based on enrichment analyses and previous literature; therefore, experimental validation is required to confirm the causative roles of candidate CNVRs in parasite resistance. The identified candidate CNVRs also provide specific targets for future validation using targeted sequencing or PCR-based approaches before implementation in breeding programs. Future studies should address these limitations by utilizing larger sample sizes, increasing sequencing coverage, conducting functional validation experiments, and testing across diverse populations, including different breeds, environmental conditions, and H. contortus strains.

Conclusion

This study provides the first WGS-based CNV map specifically distinguishing H. contortus-resistant and susceptible sheep, identifying candidate regions for future validation. The identification of CLCA1 duplication exclusively in resistant animals, coupled with enrichment of mucus regulation pathways, reveals targetable mechanisms for genetic selection. The contrasting metabolic disruptions in susceptible animals (PIK3CA, AGXT duplications) suggest screening for metabolic integrity could complement resistance selection. Following validation in larger, independent cohorts across multiple breeds and environments, integrating these CNVRs into genomic selection indices could accelerate genetic gains for parasite resistance while reducing reliance on anthelmintics.
