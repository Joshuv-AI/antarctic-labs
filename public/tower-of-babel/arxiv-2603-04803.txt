Guiding Diffusion-based Reconstruction with Contrastive Signals for Balanced Visual Representation
Boyu Han Qianqian Xu Shilong Bao Zhiyong Yang, Ruochen Cui Xilin Zhao Qingming Huang

Abstract
The limited understanding capacity of the visual encoder in Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training (CLIP) has become a key bottleneck for downstream performance. This capacity includes both Discriminative Ability (D-Ability), which reflects class separability, and Detail Perceptual Ability (P-Ability), which focuses on fine-grained visual cues. Recent solutions use diffusion models to enhance representations by conditioning image reconstruction on CLIP visual tokens. We argue that such paradigms may compromise D-Ability and therefore fail to effectively address CLIP’s representation limitations. To address this, we integrate contrastive signals into diffusion-based reconstruction to pursue more comprehensive visual representations. We begin with a straightforward design that augments the diffusion process with contrastive learning on input images. However, empirical results show that the naive combination suffers from gradient conflict and yields suboptimal performance. To balance the optimization, we introduce the Diffusion Contrastive Reconstruction (DCR) , which unifies the learning objective. The key idea is to inject contrastive signals derived from each reconstructed image, rather than from the original input, into the diffusion process. Our theoretical analysis shows that the DCR loss can jointly optimize D-Ability and P-Ability. Extensive experiments across various benchmarks and multi-modal large language models validate the effectiveness of our method. The code is available at https://github.com/boyuh/DCR .

Preliminaries

Notation.
Let
\epsilon_{\theta}
denote the generative model,
f_{\phi}
denote the CLIP visual encoder, and
h_{\omega}
denote the projection module that maps CLIP features into the generative space. The training dataset is defined as
\mathcal{D}=\{\mathbf{x}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W\times 3}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, where
H
and
W
denote the image height and width. A
variational autoencoder (VAE)
encodes each image
\mathbf{x}
into a latent
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
. We denote the visual feature as
\mathbf{z}=f_{\phi}(\mathbf{x})
, the condition as
c=h_{\omega}(\mathbf{z})
, and the predicted noise as
\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{\theta}=\epsilon_{\theta}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{t},c,t)
at diffusion step
t
, with the ground-truth noise
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}^{\text{gt}}
. For clarity, we include a table of symbol definitions in the Appendix.

3.1
Diffusion Models

Diffusion models
23
generate data by learning to reverse a gradual noising process. In the forward process, clean samples are progressively corrupted with Gaussian noise over
T
steps until they become nearly random noise. The reverse process trains a denoising network to predict and remove noise step by step, reconstructing data from noise. The denoising process is formulated as

p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_{t})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1};\mu_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t),\sigma_{t}^{2}\mathbf{I}),

(1)

where
\mu_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}(\mathbf{x}_{t}-\frac{\beta_{t}}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}}\epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t))
is parameterized by the noise prediction network
\epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t)
,
\beta_{t}=1-\alpha_{t}
, and
\sigma_{t}^{2}
is typically chosen as either
\sigma_{t}^{2}=\beta_{t}
or
\sigma_{t}^{2}=\frac{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\beta_{t}
.

Stable Diffusion
66
extends this framework to conditional generation by adding additional information
c
into the denoising process. The conditional reverse step is expressed as

p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_{t},c)=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1};\mu_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},c,t),\sigma_{t}^{2}\mathbf{I}).

(2)

By operating in a latent space through an autoencoder, Stable Diffusion achieves efficient and high-quality text-to-image synthesis, establishing diffusion models as a foundation of modern generative modeling.

3.2
The Task of Visual Representation Learning

Visual representation learning aims to learn an encoder
f_{\phi}:\mathcal{X}\!\to\!\mathcal{Z}
that maps an image
\mathbf{x}
into a visual representation
\mathbf{z}
. The goal is to obtain a representation with rich understanding capacity, typically characterized by two key properties: strong
Discriminative Ability (D-Ability)
and
Detail Perceptual Ability (P-Ability)

30
;
60
.

Property 1 (D-Ability):
Discriminative ability describes a model’s ability to accurately and robustly distinguish between different visual categories. A representation with strong discriminative power can effectively separate distinct classes in the feature space, keeping samples from the same class tightly clustered while pushing samples from different classes farther apart
38
;
65
. Mathematically, for a mini-batch with class set
\mathcal{Y}
and feature set
\mathcal{Z}_{y}=\{\mathbf{z}_{i}|y_{i}=y\}
, we define the class center as
\mu_{y}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Z}_{y}|}\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_{y}}\mathbf{z}
. It is formulated as

\min S_{\text{inner}},\hskip 10.00002pt\max S_{\text{inter}},

(3)

S_{\text{inner}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Y}|}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Z}_{y}|}\sum_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_{y}}|\mathbf{z}-\mu_{y}|_{2}^{2},

(4)

S_{\text{inter}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Y}|(|\mathcal{Y}|-1)}\sum_{y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y},y\neq y^{\prime}}|\mu_{y}-\mu_{y^{\prime}}|_{2}^{2}.

(5)

Property 2 (P-Ability):
Detail perceptual ability reflects the model’s capacity to capture fine-grained visual details, enabling a comprehensive understanding of object attributes, spatial relations, and subtle semantic cues within an image. Following previous studies
76
;
77
;
57
;
42
, this perception is usually evaluated through reconstruction consistency. When a diffusion model can accurately reconstruct the original image from its learned representation, the representation is considered sufficiently rich. It is formulated as

\min\mathbb{E}_{t}\Big[\|\epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\ h_{\omega}(f_{\phi}(\mathbf{x})),t)-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}^{\text{gt}}\|_{2}^{2}\Big],

(6)

where
t
denotes the diffusion timestep,
f_{\phi}
denotes the CLIP visual encoder, and
h_{\omega}
denotes the projector.

4
Methodology

In this section, we first propose a naive optimization method that aims to pursue the two properties in
Section

3.2
. Due to gradient conflicts, we further introduce a joint optimization approach called
Diffusion Contrastive Reconstruction (DCR)
. A brief overview is provided in
Fig.

3
. DCR enhances CLIP’s visual representations through a pretrained conditional diffusion model with contrastive signals. We also provide a theoretical analysis showing how DCR jointly strengthens and balances the D-Ability and P-Ability of the learned representations.

4.1
A Naive Optimization Method

How to improve
D-Ability
?
Mainstream approaches commonly use a contrastive loss that pulls same-class features closer and pushes different-class features apart
65
. Specifically, for a mini-batch
\mathcal{B}
with cosine similarity
\mathrm{sim}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j})=\frac{\mathbf{z}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{z}_{j}}{\|\mathbf{z}_{i}\|\,\|\mathbf{z}_{j}\|}
, temperature
\tau>0
, and the positive set
\mathcal{P}(i)=\{j\in\mathcal{B}\setminus\{i\}\mid y_{j}=y_{i}\}
, a InfoNCE-style loss
61
is

\mathcal{L}_{\text{con}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}-\log\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{P}(i)}\exp\big(\mathrm{sim}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j})/\tau\big)}{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{B}\setminus\{i\}}\exp\big(\mathrm{sim}(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{k})/\tau\big)},

(7)

which decreases
S_{\text{inner}}
and increases
S_{\text{inter}}
in
Eq.

3
.

How to improve
P-Ability
?
The objective is typically realized by enforcing consistency between the reconstructed image and the original one
77
, namely

\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}}=\mathbb{E}_{t}\Big[\big\|\epsilon_{\theta}\big(\mathbf{x}_{t},\ h_{\omega}(f_{\phi}(\mathbf{x})),\ t\big)-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}^{\text{gt}}\big\|_{2}^{2}\Big],

(8)

which is exactly
Eq.

6
.

To obtain richer understanding representations without altering architectures or data pipelines, we consider a naive method that combines the two losses described above:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{joint}}=\lambda_{\text{con}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{con}}+\lambda_{\text{rec}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}},

(9)

where
\lambda_{\text{con}}
and
\lambda_{\text{rec}}
are hyperparameters that balance the two objectives.

Limitation
. Although this approach appears promising, we find in practice that it leads to a suboptimal solution, as shown in
Fig.

1
(d). As suggested in multi-task optimization
94
;
67
;
45
, we suspect that
learning competition
may occur. The contrastive objective encourages class separation, while the reconstruction objective maintains image-level consistency. The mismatch between these two objectives causes the easier one to dominate the other. These conflicting forces often lead to oscillation or degeneration during training, resulting in unstable convergence or even feature collapse.

Specifically, let
\mathbf{z}
denote the shared visual representation. At training step
t
, the update rule for
\mathbf{z}
is:

\mathbf{z}^{t+1}=\mathbf{z}^{t}-\eta\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{joint}}=\mathbf{z}^{t}-\eta\left(\lambda_{\text{con}}\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{con}}+\lambda_{\text{rec}}\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}}\right),

(10)

where
\eta
is the learning rate. Denoting
\mathbf{g}_{\text{con}}=\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{con}}
and
\mathbf{g}_{\text{rec}}=\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}}
, the overall optimization direction depends on the geometric relation between these two gradients. If their directions are inconsistent, the optimization will exhibit
competition
between the two objectives.

Gradient consistency and conflict.
To quantify this interaction, we define the cosine similarity between the two gradient directions:

\cos(\mathbf{g}_{\text{con}},\mathbf{g}_{\text{rec}})=\frac{\mathbf{g}_{\text{con}}^{\top}\mathbf{g}_{\text{rec}}}{\|\mathbf{g}_{\text{con}}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{g}_{\text{rec}}\|_{2}}.

(11)

When
\cos(\mathbf{g}_{\text{con}},\mathbf{g}_{\text{rec}})>0
, the two objectives cooperate and reinforce each other. However, when
\cos(\mathbf{g}_{\text{con}},\mathbf{g}_{\text{rec}})<0
, they conflict, leading to destructive interference in the gradient updates.

We conduct experiments on OpenAI CLIP ViT-L/14@224
65
and optimize the model using the joint loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{joint}}
. As shown in
Fig.

2
(a)(b),
\mathcal{L}_{\text{con}}
dominates the optimization process, while
\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}}
is suppressed and fails to converge.

Fig.

2
(c) further shows that during training,
86.3\%
of the steps have a negative cosine similarity
\cos(\mathbf{g}_{\text{con}},\mathbf{g}_{\text{rec}})
between the gradients of the two losses, indicating that
gradient conflict is pervasive throughout the process
. Moreover, as training progresses, this value becomes almost entirely negative, suggesting that gradient conflicts intensify in the later stages of optimization.

Remark 1
.

The above analysis reveals that naive weighted summation cannot effectively reconcile the conflicting learning objectives. Therefore, we argue that the problem should be addressed at its root by optimizing a single objective.

4.2
Diffusion Contrastive Reconstruction

We propose a new optimization framework, named
Diffusion Contrastive Reconstruction (DCR)
, that jointly optimizes the two optimization objectives (
Eq.

3
and
Eq.

6
) within a unified formulation. With this single loss, the gradient conflict is naturally resolved.

(1) Standard Diffusion-based Reconstruction.
As shown on the right side of
Fig.

3
, for an image
\mathbf{x}
, we sample a timestep
t
, obtain the forward noised latent
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
, and condition the denoiser with the image-driven embedding
c=h_{\omega}(f_{\phi}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}))
. The denoiser
\epsilon_{\theta}
predicts the noise
\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{\theta}=\epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},c,t)
(abbreviated as
\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}
), which determines the next latent
\mathbf{x}_{t-1}
.

(2) Injecting Contrastive Signals.
We construct a contrastive triplet in the denoising images, as illustrated on the left side of
Fig.

3
. Given an image
\mathbf{x}
, the
anchor
is the predicted noise when the image itself provides the condition,
i.e.
,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}=\epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\,h_{\omega}(f_{\phi}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})),\,t)
. We then apply an augmentation
\mathbf{x}^{+}=a(\mathbf{x})
(
e.g.
, random crop or color jitter
5
;
6
), compute
c^{+}=h_{\omega}(f_{\phi}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{+}))
, and define the
positive
as the noise predicted under this augmented condition,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{+}=\epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\,c^{+},\,t)
. All other images
\mathbf{x}^{j}
(
j\neq i
) within the same mini-batch serve as
negatives
. For each
\mathbf{x}^{j}
we set
c^{j}=h_{\omega}(f_{\phi}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{\,j}))
and obtain
\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{j}_{-}=\epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\,c^{j},\,t)
. Additionally, we include the ground-truth diffusion noise
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}^{\text{gt}}
(abbreviated as
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{gt}}
) as an auxiliary target to provide richer supervision.

(3) DCR Loss.
Let
d(u,v)=\exp(\mathrm{sim}(u,v)/\tau)
, where
\mathrm{sim}(u,v)=\frac{\langle u,v\rangle}{|u|,|v|}
denote cosine similarity and
\tau>0
a temperature. Define set
P=\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{+},\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{gt}}\}
, set
N=\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{-}^{k}\}_{k=1}^{N-1}
, and set
C=P\cup N
. Then the loss is

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{dcr}}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p\in P}\log\frac{d(\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},p)}{\sum_{c\in C}d(\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},c)}.

(12)

Intuitively, the core of diffusion models lies in predicting noise. Performing contrastive learning on the predicting noise allows the model to sense fine-grained differences in the original visual representations used as conditions. This helps the vision encoder capture detailed information and enhance its discriminative ability, ultimately achieving a natural balance between
D-Ability
and
P-Ability
.

\epsilon_{\theta}
remains frozen. As shown in
Fig.

3
, we use a two-stage schedule to transfer diffusion knowledge into the vision encoder gradually.

We freeze the visual encoder

Stage-2 (encoder enhancement).
We then freeze the projector
h_{\omega}
and train the visual encoder
f_{\phi}
. Since
h_{\omega}
has been aligned to the denoiser in Stage-1, gradients from

12
now directly refine the feature structure produced by
f_{\phi}
. Features from the same class are guided to produce conditions that yield similar predicted noises, while features from different classes are guided to produce conditions with dissimilar predicted noises. After this stage, we obtain a CLIP visual encoder with richer visual representations.

4.3
Theoretical Statements

Taking a step further, we conduct a theoretical analysis. The results show that the DCR loss is designed to satisfy the two objectives discussed in
Section

3.2
. We present these insights in the following two theorems. Due to space limitations, we provide a concise version here, and the proofs are deferred to the Appendix.

Theorem 1
.

Fix a diffusion step
t
and define
T(\mathbf{z})=\epsilon_{\theta}\bigl(x_{t},\,h_{\omega}(\mathbf{z}),\,t\bigr)
. Under a mild assumption, the intra-class scatter and inter-class scatter in the
feature
space (
S_{\text{inner}},S_{\text{inter}}
) can be bounded by those in the
noise
space (
S_{\text{inner}}^{(\epsilon)}(t),S_{\text{inter}}^{(\epsilon)}(t)
) as

S_{\text{inner}}\leq\frac{1}{m^{2}}\,S_{\text{inner}}^{(\epsilon)}(t),

(13)

S_{\text{inter}}\geq\kappa S_{\text{inter}}^{(\epsilon)}(t)-\eta S_{\text{inner}}^{(\epsilon)}(t),

(14)

where
m
,
\kappa
, and
\eta
are positive constants depending on the Lipschitz continuity of
T(\cdot)
.

Theorem 2
.

If negatives in the mini-batch are well separated from the anchor (i.e.,
\mathrm{sim}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{j}_{-})\ll\mathrm{sim}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{gt}})
for all
j\neq i
), and predicted-noise norms are bounded away from
0
and
\infty
, then the DCR loss reduces to a scaled reconstruction loss up to an additive constant:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{dcr}}=\lambda\big\|\epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\ h_{\omega}(f_{\phi}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})),t)-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}^{\text{gt}}\big\|_{2}^{2}+c,

(15)

where
\lambda>0
, and
c
is a constant.

Remark 2
.

According to Theorem
1
, as
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{dcr}}
decreases, we obtain
S_{\text{inner}}\downarrow
and
S_{\text{inter}}\uparrow
in the CLIP feature space, which fulfills the discriminative objective in
Eq.

3
. According to Theorem
2
, minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{dcr}}
becomes equivalent to minimizing the detail perceptual objective in
Eq.

6

6
Conclusion

This paper presents
Diffusion Contrastive Reconstruction (DCR)
, a general optimization framework that guides diffusion-based reconstruction with contrastive signals to balance discriminative and detail perceptual abilities. DCR effectively removes gradient conflicts and achieves consistent improvements in both aspects. Theoretical analysis and empirical results demonstrate that DCR not only enhances CLIP visual representations but also strengthens the visual reasoning capability of multimodal large language models.

Appendix C
Proof of Theorem 1

Definition 1

(Bi-Lipschitz Mapping
9
)
.

Let
T:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
be a mapping between two metric spaces
(\mathcal{X},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{X}})
and
(\mathcal{Y},\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{Y}})
. We say that
T
is bi-Lipschitz on a subset
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
if there exist constants
0<m\leq L<\infty
such that, for all
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathcal{M}
,

m\|z_{1}-z_{2}\|_{\mathcal{X}}\leq\bigl\|T(z_{1})-T(z_{2})\bigr\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq L\|z_{1}-z_{2}\|_{\mathcal{X}}.

(16)

In particular, a bi-Lipschitz map preserves pairwise distances up to constant factors and is injective on
\mathcal{M}
.

Assumption 1
.

Fix a diffusion step
t
and define
T(\mathbf{z})=\epsilon_{\theta}\bigl(x_{t},\,h_{\omega}(\mathbf{z}),\,t\bigr)
. According to Definition
1
, we assume that
T(\mathbf{z})
satisfies the bi-Lipschitz property.

Lemma 1

(Equivalent Variance Identity
99
)
.

Let
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be arbitrary vectors and denote their mean by
\bar{e}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}
. Then the empirical variance can be written as the average of all pairwise squared distances:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e_{i}-\bar{e}\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|e_{i}-e_{j}\|_{2}^{2}.

(17)

Proof.

For the left-hand side
, using
\bar{e}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}e_{k}
, we have:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e_{i}-\bar{e}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle e_{i}-\bar{e},e_{i}-\bar{e}\rangle

(18)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(\|e_{i}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\bar{e}\|_{2}^{2}-2\langle e_{i},\bar{e}\rangle\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e_{i}\|_{2}^{2}+n\|\bar{e}\|_{2}^{2}-2\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i},\bar{e}\right\rangle.

Since
\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}=n\bar{e}
, it follows that

\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i},\bar{e}\right\rangle=\langle n\bar{e},\bar{e}\rangle=n\|\bar{e}\|_{2}^{2}.

(19)

Therefore,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e_{i}-\bar{e}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-n\|\bar{e}\|_{2}^{2}.

(20)

For the right-hand side
, using
\|e_{i}-e_{j}\|_{2}^{2}=\|e_{i}\|_{2}^{2}+\|e_{j}\|_{2}^{2}-2\langle e_{i},e_{j}\rangle
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|e_{i}-e_{j}\|_{2}^{2}

(21)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\|e_{i}\|_{2}^{2}+\sum_{i,j}\|e_{j}\|_{2}^{2}-2\sum_{i,j}\langle e_{i},e_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e_{i}\|_{2}^{2}+n\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|e_{j}\|_{2}^{2}-2\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i},\ \sum_{j=1}^{n}e_{j}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-2\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

Again using
\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}=n\bar{e}
, we obtain

\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\|n\bar{e}\|_{2}^{2}=n^{2}\|\bar{e}\|_{2}^{2},

(22)

so

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|e_{i}-e_{j}\|_{2}^{2}=2n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-2n^{2}\|\bar{e}\|_{2}^{2}.

(23)

Dividing
Eq.

23
by
2n
yields

\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|e_{i}-e_{j}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-n\|\bar{e}\|_{2}^{2}.

(24)

Therefore, by combining
Eq.

20
and
Eq.

24
, we can obtain:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|e_{i}-\bar{e}\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|e_{i}-e_{j}\|_{2}^{2}.

(25)

This completed the proof.
∎

Restate of Theorem 1
.

Fix a diffusion step
t
and define
T(\mathbf{z})=\epsilon_{\theta}\bigl(x_{t},\,h_{\omega}(\mathbf{z}),\,t\bigr)
. Under a mild assumption, the intra-class scatter and inter-class scatter in the feature space (
S_{\text{inner}},S_{\text{inter}}
) can be bounded by those in the noise space (
S_{\text{inner}}^{(\epsilon)}(t),S_{\text{inter}}^{(\epsilon)}(t)
) as

S_{\text{inner}}\leq\frac{1}{m^{2}}\,S_{\text{inner}}^{(\epsilon)}(t),

S_{\text{inter}}\geq\kappa S_{\text{inter}}^{(\epsilon)}(t)-\eta S_{\text{inner}}^{(\epsilon)}(t),

where
m
,
\kappa
, and
\eta
are positive constants depending on the Lipschitz continuity of
T(\cdot)
.

Proof.

We proceed in three steps. Throughout, fix a diffusion step
t
and a mini-batch with class set
\mathcal{Y}
.

For class
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, let the feature set be
\mathcal{Z}_{y}=\{\mathbf{z}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{y}}
with
\mathbf{z}_{i}=f_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
and the noise set be
\mathcal{E}_{y,t}=\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{y}}
with
\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{i}=\epsilon_{\theta}(x_{t},h_{\omega}(\mathbf{z}_{i}),t)
. Denote the class means by
\mu_{y}=\frac{1}{n_{y}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{y}}\mathbf{z}_{i}
and
\nu_{y,t}=\frac{1}{n_{y}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{y}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{i}
. As shown in Eq. (4) and Eq. (5) in the main text, we define the intra-/inter-class scatters in the CLIP feature space by

S_{\text{inner}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Y}|}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\frac{1}{n_{y}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{y}}\|\mathbf{z}_{i}-\mu_{y}\|_{2}^{2},

(26)

S_{\text{inter}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Y}|(|\mathcal{Y}|-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}\\
y\neq y^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\|\mu_{y}-\mu_{y^{\prime}}\|_{2}^{2}.

(27)

Similarly, we define the intra-/inter-class scatters in the diffusion noise space by

S_{\text{inner}}^{(\epsilon)}(t)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Y}|}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\frac{1}{n_{y}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{y}}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{i}-\nu_{y,t}\|_{2}^{2},

(28)

S_{\text{inter}}^{(\epsilon)}(t)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Y}|(|\mathcal{Y}|-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}\\
y\neq y^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\|\nu_{y,t}-\nu_{y^{\prime},t}\|_{2}^{2}.

(29)

Step 1: Minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DCR}}
improves noise-space geometry.

Recall the DCR loss

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DCR}}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p\in P}\log\frac{d(\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},p)}{\sum_{c\in C}d(\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},c)},

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}=\epsilon_{\theta}(x_{t},\,h_{\omega}(\mathbf{z}),\,t)
is the anchor,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{+}
is the positive sample,
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\mathrm{gt}}
is the ground-truth, and
N=\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{-}^{(j)}\}
are negatives from other images in the mini-batch.
P=\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{+},\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}^{\mathrm{gt}}\}
,
C=P\cup N
.
d(u,v)=\exp(\mathrm{sim}(u,v)/\tau)
with
\mathrm{sim}
the cosine similarity and
\tau>0
. Let

p(q\mid\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}})=\frac{\exp(\mathrm{sim}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},q)/\tau)}{\sum_{c\in C}\exp(\mathrm{sim}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},c)/\tau)}.

(30)

Differentiating the loss with respect to the similarity term gives the following gradient expression:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DCR}}}{\partial\,\mathrm{sim}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},q)}=\begin{cases}-\tfrac{1}{2\tau}\bigl(1-2p(q\mid\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}})\bigr),&q\in P,\\
\tfrac{1}{\tau}\,p(q\mid\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}),&q\in N.\end{cases}

(31)

Thus, gradient descent on
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DCR}}

increases
the anchor’s cosine similarity with the two positives (
\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{+}
and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\mathrm{gt}}
) and
decreases
it with all negatives. Since

\mathrm{sim}(u,v)=\langle\bar{u},\bar{v}\rangle,\quad\text{where}\ \bar{u}=\frac{u}{\|u\|},\ \bar{v}=\frac{v}{\|v\|},

(32)

we have

\|\bar{u}-\bar{v}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\bar{u}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\bar{v}\|_{2}^{2}-2\langle\bar{u},\bar{v}\rangle=2(1-\mathrm{sim}(\bar{u},\bar{v})).

(33)

Therefore, minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DCR}}
is equivalent to

\min_{\phi,\omega}\bigl\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{+}\bigr\|_{2}^{2}+\bigl\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\mathrm{gt}}\bigr\|_{2}^{2},

(34)

\max_{\phi,\omega}\bigl\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{-}^{(j)}\bigr\|_{2}^{2},\ \forall j.

(35)

Then, we pass from pairwise distances to scatter. Specifically, for each class
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, the key component in
Eq.

28
,

\frac{1}{n_{y}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{y}}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{i}-\nu_{y,t}\|_{2}^{2}

(36)

is, by Lemma
17
, exactly the average of all pairwise squared distances
\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{i}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}
within that class (up to the constant factor
1/(2n_{y}^{2})
). Minimizing the pairwise distances between the anchor and its positive/ground-truth terms in
Eq.

34
therefore decreases the average intra-class pairwise distance, and thus decreases
\frac{1}{n_{y}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{y}}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{i}-\nu_{y,t}\|_{2}^{2}
for each
y
. Averaging over all classes yields

\min_{\phi,\omega}\;S_{\text{inner}}^{(\epsilon)}(t).

(37)

In contrast, the negative terms
\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{-}^{(j)}\}
are drawn from other images in the mini-batch, which typically belong to different classes. Maximizing the anchor–negative distances
\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{-}^{(j)}\|_{2}^{2}
increases the average pairwise distances between samples of different classes. This increase, when propagated to the distances between the corresponding class means
\{\nu_{y,t}\}_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}
, leads to an increase of the inter-class scatter
S_{\text{inter}}^{(\epsilon)}(t)
:

\max_{\phi,\omega}\;S_{\text{inter}}^{(\epsilon)}(t).

(38)

Combining
Eq.

37
and
Eq.

38
, we conclude that minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DCR}}
improves the geometry in the noise space by decreasing intra-class scatter and increasing inter-class scatter.

Step 2: Bounding feature-space intra-class scatter.

Fix a class
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, and write its feature set as
\mathcal{Z}_{y}=\{\mathbf{z}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{y}}
and its noise set as
\mathcal{E}_{y,t}=\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{y}}
with
\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{i}=T(\mathbf{z}_{i})
.

By Lemma
17
, the intra-class variance in the feature space can be written as

\frac{1}{n_{y}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{y}}\|\mathbf{z}_{i}-\mu_{y}\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{2n_{y}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{y}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{y}}\|\mathbf{z}_{i}-\mathbf{z}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}.

(39)

Similarly, the intra-class variance in the noise space is

\frac{1}{n_{y}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{y}}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{i}-\nu_{y,t}\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{2n_{y}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{y}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{y}}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{i}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}.

(40)

By Assumption
1
, there exists
m>0
such that, for all
\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2}\in\mathcal{M}
,

m\|\mathbf{z}_{1}-\mathbf{z}_{2}\|\leq\|T(\mathbf{z}_{1})-T(\mathbf{z}_{2})\|.

(41)

Equivalently,

\|\mathbf{z}_{1}-\mathbf{z}_{2}\|\leq\frac{1}{m}\|T(\mathbf{z}_{1})-T(\mathbf{z}_{2})\|.

(42)

Applying this inequality to each pair
(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{j})
gives

\|\mathbf{z}_{i}-\mathbf{z}_{j}\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{m^{2}}\|T(\mathbf{z}_{i})-T(\mathbf{z}_{j})\|^{2}=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{i}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{j}\|^{2}.

(43)

Substituting
Eq.

43
into
Eq.

39
and comparing with
Eq.

40
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{y}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{y}}\|\mathbf{z}_{i}-\mu_{y}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2n_{y}^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n_{y}}\frac{1}{m^{2}}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{i}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}

(44)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{n_{y}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{y}}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{i}-\nu_{y,t}\|_{2}^{2}.

Averaging over all classes
y\in\mathcal{Y}
yields

\displaystyle S_{\text{inner}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Y}|}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\frac{1}{n_{y}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{y}}\|\mathbf{z}_{i}-\mu_{y}\|_{2}^{2}

(45)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{m^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Y}|}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\frac{1}{n_{y}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{y}}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{i}-\nu_{y,t}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{2}}S_{\text{inner}}^{(\epsilon)}(t),

which proves the first inequality in Theorem 1.

Step 3: Bounding feature-space inter-class scatter.

By Assumption
1
, there exists
L>0
such that, for all
\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2}\in\mathcal{M}
,

\|T(\mathbf{z}_{1})-T(\mathbf{z}_{2})\|\leq L\|\mathbf{z}_{1}-\mathbf{z}_{2}\|.

(46)

Equivalently,

\|\mathbf{z}_{1}-\mathbf{z}_{2}\|\geq\frac{1}{L}\|T(\mathbf{z}_{1})-T(\mathbf{z}_{2})\|.

(47)

Fix two distinct classes
y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}
. Applying
Eq.

47
with
\mathbf{z}_{1}=\mu_{y}
,
\mathbf{z}_{2}=\mu_{y^{\prime}}
yields

\|\mu_{y}-\mu_{y^{\prime}}\|\geq\frac{1}{L}\|T(\mu_{y})-T(\mu_{y^{\prime}})\|.

(48)

We next relate
T(\mu_{y})
to
\nu_{y,t}
. We have

\nu_{y,t}=\frac{1}{n_{y}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{y}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{i}=\frac{1}{n_{y}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{y}}T(\mathbf{z}_{i}).

(49)

Then

\displaystyle\|T(\mu_{y})-\nu_{y,t}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|T(\mu_{y})-\frac{1}{n_{y}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{y}}T(\mathbf{z}_{i})\right\|_{2}

(50)

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{1}{n_{y}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{y}}\bigl(T(\mu_{y})-T(\mathbf{z}_{i})\bigr)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n_{y}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{y}}\|T(\mu_{y})-T(\mathbf{z}_{i})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L}{n_{y}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{y}}\|\mu_{y}-\mathbf{z}_{i}\|_{2},

where we used the triangle inequality and then the Lipschitz property of
T
. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\sum_{i=1}^{n_{y}}\|\mu_{y}-\mathbf{z}_{i}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{n_{y}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{y}}\|\mu_{y}-\mathbf{z}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

(51)

Hence,

\|T(\mu_{y})-\nu_{y,t}\|_{2}\leq\frac{L}{\sqrt{n_{y}}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{y}}\|\mu_{y}-\mathbf{z}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

(52)

Similarly, for class
y^{\prime}
, we have:

\|T(\mu_{y^{\prime}})-\nu_{y^{\prime},t}\|_{2}\leq\frac{L}{\sqrt{n_{y^{\prime}}}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{y^{\prime}}}\|\mu_{y^{\prime}}-\mathbf{z}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

(53)

Using the triangle inequality, we get

\displaystyle\|T(\mu_{y})-T(\mu_{y^{\prime}})\|_{2}

(54)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\|\nu_{y,t}-\nu_{y^{\prime},t}\|_{2}-\|T(\mu_{y})-\nu_{y,t}\|_{2}

\displaystyle-\|T(\mu_{y^{\prime}})-\nu_{y^{\prime},t}\|_{2}.

Combining this with
Eqs.

52
and
53
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|T(\mu_{y})-T(\mu_{y^{\prime}})\|_{2}

(55)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\|\nu_{y,t}-\nu_{y^{\prime},t}\|_{2}-\frac{L}{\sqrt{n_{y}}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{y}}\|\mu_{y}-\mathbf{z}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle-\frac{L}{\sqrt{n_{y^{\prime}}}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{y^{\prime}}}\|\mu_{y^{\prime}}-\mathbf{z}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Substituting
Eq.

55
into
Eq.

48
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\mu_{y}-\mu_{y^{\prime}}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{L}\|\nu_{y,t}-\nu_{y^{\prime},t}\|_{2}

(56)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{y}}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{y}}\|\mu_{y}-\mathbf{z}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{y^{\prime}}}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{y^{\prime}}}\|\mu_{y^{\prime}}-\mathbf{z}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Squaring both sides and using the inequality
(a-b-c)^{2}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}a^{2}-2(b^{2}+c^{2})
for any
a,b,c\in\mathbb{R}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\mu_{y}-\mu_{y^{\prime}}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2L^{2}}\|\nu_{y,t}-\nu_{y^{\prime},t}\|_{2}^{2}

(57)

\displaystyle-2\left(\frac{1}{n_{y}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{y}}\|\mu_{y}-\mathbf{z}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle-2\left(\frac{1}{n_{y^{\prime}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{y^{\prime}}}\|\mu_{y^{\prime}}-\mathbf{z}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\right).

Averaging the above inequality over all ordered pairs
(y,y^{\prime})
with
y\neq y^{\prime}
,
and recalling the definition of
S_{\text{inter}}
and
S_{\text{inter}}^{(\epsilon)}(t)
, we obtain

\displaystyle S_{\text{inter}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Y}|(|\mathcal{Y}|-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}\\
y\neq y^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\|\mu_{y}-\mu_{y^{\prime}}\|_{2}^{2}

(58)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Y}|(|\mathcal{Y}|-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}\\
y\neq y^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\left[\frac{1}{2L^{2}}\|\nu_{y,t}-\nu_{y^{\prime},t}\|_{2}^{2}-2B_{y,y^{\prime}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2L^{2}}S_{\text{inter}}^{(\epsilon)}(t)-\frac{2}{|\mathcal{Y}|(|\mathcal{Y}|-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}\\
y\neq y^{\prime}\end{subarray}}B_{y,y^{\prime}},

where
B_{y,y^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{n_{y}}\sum_{i\in I_{y}}\|\mu_{y}-\mathbf{z}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{n_{y^{\prime}}}\sum_{i\in I_{y^{\prime}}}\|\mu_{y^{\prime}}-\mathbf{z}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}
.

For the second term, note that for each fixed
y
, the quantity
A_{y}=\frac{1}{n_{y}}\sum_{i\in I_{y}}\|\mu_{y}-\mathbf{z}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}
appears exactly
(|\mathcal{Y}|-1)
times as the first index and
(|\mathcal{Y}|-1)
times as the second index when summing over all ordered pairs
(y,y^{\prime})
with
y\neq y^{\prime}
. Hence

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}\\
y\neq y^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\left(A_{y}+A_{y^{\prime}}\right)=2(|\mathcal{Y}|-1)\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}A_{y}.

(59)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{2}{|\mathcal{Y}|(|\mathcal{Y}|-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}\\
y\neq y^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\left(A_{y}+A_{y^{\prime}}\right)

(60)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{|\mathcal{Y}|(|\mathcal{Y}|-1)}\,2(|\mathcal{Y}|-1)\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}A_{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4}{|\mathcal{Y}|}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}A_{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4S_{\text{inner}}.

Substituting this back, we obtain the explicit bound

S_{\text{inter}}\;\geq\;\frac{1}{2L^{2}}S_{\text{inter}}^{(\epsilon)}(t)\;-\;4S_{\text{inner}}.

(61)

Finally, using the bound from Step 2,
S_{\text{inner}}\;\leq\;\frac{1}{m^{2}}S_{\text{inner}}^{(\epsilon)}(t)
, we arrive at

S_{\text{inter}}\geq\frac{1}{2L^{2}}S_{\text{inter}}^{(\epsilon)}(t)-\frac{4}{m^{2}}S_{\text{inner}}^{(\epsilon)}(t).

(62)

Thus, we have:

S_{\text{inter}}\geq\kappa S_{\text{inter}}^{(\epsilon)}(t)-\eta S_{\text{inner}}^{(\epsilon)}(t),

(63)

where
\kappa=\frac{1}{2L^{2}}
and
\eta=\frac{4}{m^{2}}
. It proves the second inequality in Theorem 1.

This completed the proof.
∎

Appendix D
Proof of Theorem 2

Restate of Theorem 2
.

If negatives in the mini-batch are well separated from the anchor (
i.e.
,
\mathrm{sim}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{j}_{-})\ll\mathrm{sim}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{gt}})
for all
j\neq i
), and predicted-noise norms are bounded away from
0
and
\infty
, then the DCR loss reduces to a scaled reconstruction loss up to an additive constant:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{dcr}}=\lambda\big\|\epsilon_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},\ h_{\omega}(f_{\phi}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})),t)-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}^{\text{gt}}\big\|_{2}^{2}+c,

where
\lambda>0
, and
c
is a constant.

Proof.

We consider a single anchor sample and drop the sample index for brevity.

Recall that the anchor is
\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}=\epsilon_{\theta}(x_{t},\,h_{\omega}(f_{\phi}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})),\,t)
, the two positives are
\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{+}
and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{gt}}
, and
N=\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{j}_{-}\}_{j}
denotes the set of negatives in the mini-batch. Let
P=\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{+},\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{gt}}\}
,
C=P\cup N
, and
\mathrm{sim}(u,v)=\frac{\langle u,v\rangle}{\|u\|_{2}\,\|v\|_{2}}
,
d(u,v)=\exp\bigl(\mathrm{sim}(u,v)/\tau\bigr)
, with
\tau>0
the temperature. The DCR loss for this anchor is

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DCR}}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p\in P}\log\frac{d(\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},p)}{\sum_{c\in C}d(\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},c)}.

(64)

Denote
u=\mathrm{sim}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{gt}})
,
v=\mathrm{sim}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{+})
, and write

Z_{\text{pos}}=\sum_{p\in P}\exp\bigl(\mathrm{sim}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},p)/\tau\bigr)=e^{u/\tau}+e^{v/\tau},

(65)

Z_{\text{neg}}=\sum_{j}\exp\bigl(\mathrm{sim}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{j}_{-})/\tau\bigr).

(66)

Then we can expand

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DCR}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p\in P}\left(\frac{\mathrm{sim}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},p)}{\tau}-\log\bigl(Z_{\text{pos}}+Z_{\text{neg}}\bigr)\right)

(67)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\tau}(u+v)+\log\bigl(Z_{\text{pos}}+Z_{\text{neg}}\bigr).

Using the notation in
Eq.

67
, we now show that
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DCR}}
is (up to scaling and an additive constant) equivalent to the reconstruction loss.

First, by the assumption that negatives are well separated from the anchor, there exist constants
\Delta>0
and
B\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all anchors in the mini-batch,

\mathrm{sim}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{j}_{-})\leq u-\Delta,\quad\forall j,\qquad|N|\leq B,

(68)

where
u=\mathrm{sim}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{gt}})
. Hence

Z_{\text{neg}}=\sum_{j}\exp\bigl(\mathrm{sim}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{j}_{-})/\tau\bigr)\leq B\exp\bigl((u-\Delta)/\tau\bigr).

(69)

Since
Z_{\text{pos}}\geq e^{u/\tau}
, we get

\frac{Z_{\text{neg}}}{Z_{\text{pos}}}\leq Be^{-\Delta/\tau}=\delta,

(70)

where
\delta>0
is a constant independent of the trainable parameters. Therefore

\displaystyle\log\bigl(Z_{\text{pos}}+Z_{\text{neg}}\bigr)

(71)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\Bigl(Z_{\text{pos}}\bigl(1+Z_{\text{neg}}/Z_{\text{pos}}\bigr)\Bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log Z_{\text{pos}}+\log\bigl(1+Z_{\text{neg}}/Z_{\text{pos}}\bigr),

and, using
0\leq Z_{\text{neg}}/Z_{\text{pos}}\leq\delta
,

0\leq\log\bigl(1+Z_{\text{neg}}/Z_{\text{pos}}\bigr)\leq\log(1+\delta)=C_{\text{neg}}.

(72)

Thus, from
Eq.

67
,

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DCR}}=-\frac{1}{2\tau}(u+v)+\log Z_{\text{pos}}+\Delta_{\text{neg}},

(73)

where
0\leq\Delta_{\text{neg}}\leq C_{\text{neg}}
. It remains to analyze the positive part:

\ell_{\text{pos}}(u,v)=-\frac{1}{2\tau}(u+v)+\log\bigl(e^{u/\tau}+e^{v/\tau}\bigr),

(74)

since

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DCR}}=\ell_{\text{pos}}(u,v)+\Delta_{\text{neg}}.

(75)

Next, we express
u
and
v
in terms of squared Euclidean distances. Define the normalized vectors

\hat{e}=\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}}{\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\|_{2}},\qquad g=\frac{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{gt}}}{\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{gt}}\|_{2}},\qquad p=\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{+}}{\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{+}\|_{2}}.

(76)

Then
u=\langle\hat{e},g\rangle
and
v=\langle\hat{e},p\rangle
. For unit vectors
a,b
we have

\|a-b\|_{2}^{2}=2(1-\langle a,b\rangle),

(77)

so, defining

d_{\text{gt}}^{2}=\|\hat{e}-g\|_{2}^{2},\qquad d_{+}^{2}=\|\hat{e}-p\|_{2}^{2},

(78)

we obtain

u=1-\frac{1}{2}d_{\text{gt}}^{2},\qquad v=1-\frac{1}{2}d_{+}^{2}.

(79)

Substituting into
\ell_{\text{pos}}
gives

\ell_{\text{pos}}=\frac{1}{4\tau}\bigl(d_{\text{gt}}^{2}+d_{+}^{2}\bigr)+\log\left(e^{-d_{\text{gt}}^{2}/(2\tau)}+e^{-d_{+}^{2}/(2\tau)}\right).

(80)

Since
\hat{e},g,p
are unit vectors, both squared distances are bounded:

0\leq d_{\text{gt}}^{2}\leq 4,\qquad 0\leq d_{+}^{2}\leq 4.

(81)

The logarithmic term in
Eq.

80
satisfies a bound. Let

a=-\frac{d_{\text{gt}}^{2}}{2\tau},\qquad b=-\frac{d_{+}^{2}}{2\tau},

(82)

so that

\log\left(e^{-d_{\text{gt}}^{2}/(2\tau)}+e^{-d_{+}^{2}/(2\tau)}\right)=\log(e^{a}+e^{b}).

(83)

For any real
a,b
, we have

\max\{a,b\}\leq\log(e^{a}+e^{b})\leq\max\{a,b\}+\log 2.

(84)

Using
0\leq d_{\text{gt}}^{2},d_{+}^{2}\leq 4
and
\tau>0
, we obtain constants
C_{\min},C_{\max}\in\mathbb{R}
depending only on
\tau
such that

C_{\min}\leq\log\left(e^{-d_{\text{gt}}^{2}/(2\tau)}+e^{-d_{+}^{2}/(2\tau)}\right)\leq C_{\max}

(85)

for all possible
d_{\text{gt}}^{2},d_{+}^{2}
. Moreover, the term
\frac{1}{4\tau}d_{+}^{2}
in
Eq.

80
is also bounded using
Eq.

81
, so it can be absorbed into the constants. Consequently, there exist finite constants
\tilde{C}_{\min},\tilde{C}_{\max}
(depending only on
\tau
) such that

\frac{1}{4\tau}d_{\text{gt}}^{2}+\tilde{C}_{\min}\leq\ell_{\text{pos}}\leq\frac{1}{4\tau}d_{\text{gt}}^{2}+\tilde{C}_{\max}.

(86)

We now relate
d_{\text{gt}}^{2}
to the true reconstruction error
\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{gt}}\|_{2}^{2}
. For any nonzero vectors
u,v
, the law of cosines gives

\|u-v\|_{2}^{2}=\|u\|_{2}^{2}+\|v\|_{2}^{2}-2\|u\|_{2}\|v\|_{2}\,\mathrm{sim}(u,v).

(87)

With
u=\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}
,
v=\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{gt}}
and
\mathrm{sim}(u,v)=\langle\hat{e},g\rangle=1-d_{\text{gt}}^{2}/2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\bigl\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{gt}}\bigr\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{gt}}\|_{2}^{2}-2\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\|_{2}\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{gt}}\|_{2}\left(1-\frac{d_{\text{gt}}^{2}}{2}\right)

(88)

\displaystyle=\bigl(\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\|_{2}-\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{gt}}\|_{2}\bigr)^{2}+\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\|_{2}\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{gt}}\|_{2}\,d_{\text{gt}}^{2}.

By assumption, predicted-noise norms are bounded away from
0
and
\infty
. Thus, there exist constants
0<\alpha\leq\beta<\infty
such that

\alpha\leq\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\|_{2},\ \|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{gt}}\|_{2}\leq\beta\quad\text{for all anchors.}

(89)

Using
Eq.

88
and the fact that
\bigl(\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\|_{2}-\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{gt}}\|_{2}\bigr)^{2}\geq 0
, we have

\bigl\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{gt}}\bigr\|_{2}^{2}\geq\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\|_{2}\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{gt}}\|_{2}\,d_{\text{gt}}^{2}\geq\alpha^{2}d_{\text{gt}}^{2}.

(90)

On the other hand, using
\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{gt}}\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\beta^{2}
and
\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\|_{2}\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{gt}}\|_{2}\leq\beta^{2}
, we obtain

\bigl\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{gt}}\bigr\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\beta^{2}+\beta^{2}d_{\text{gt}}^{2}.

(91)

Rearranging these inequalities yields

\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\left(\bigl\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{gt}}\bigr\|_{2}^{2}-2\beta^{2}\right)\leq d_{\text{gt}}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\bigl\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{gt}}\bigr\|_{2}^{2}.

(92)

Combining
Eq.

86
and
Eq.

92
, we get affine
bounds on
\ell_{\text{pos}}
in terms of the reconstruction error.
For the lower bound,

\displaystyle\ell_{\text{pos}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4\tau}d_{\text{gt}}^{2}+\tilde{C}_{\min}

(93)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4\tau\beta^{2}}\left(\bigl\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{gt}}\bigr\|_{2}^{2}-2\beta^{2}\right)+\tilde{C}_{\min}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\tau\beta^{2}}\bigl\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{gt}}\bigr\|_{2}^{2}+\left(\tilde{C}_{\min}-\frac{1}{2\tau}\right).

For the upper bound,

\displaystyle\ell_{\text{pos}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4\tau}d_{\text{gt}}^{2}+\tilde{C}_{\max}

(94)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4\tau\alpha^{2}}\bigl\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{gt}}\bigr\|_{2}^{2}+\tilde{C}_{\max}.

Define

\lambda_{\min}=\frac{1}{4\tau\beta^{2}},\qquad\lambda_{\max}=\frac{1}{4\tau\alpha^{2}},

(95)

and constants

c_{\min}=\tilde{C}_{\min}-\frac{1}{2\tau},\qquad c_{\max}=\tilde{C}_{\max}.

(96)

Then

\lambda_{\min}\bigl\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{gt}}\bigr\|_{2}^{2}+c_{\min}\leq\ell_{\text{pos}}\leq\lambda_{\max}\bigl\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{gt}}\bigr\|_{2}^{2}+c_{\max}.

(97)

Finally, recalling
Eq.

75
and the bound
0\leq\Delta_{\text{neg}}\leq C_{\text{neg}}
, we conclude that

\lambda_{\min}\bigl\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{gt}}\bigr\|_{2}^{2}+c_{\min}\leq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DCR}}\leq\lambda_{\max}\bigl\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{\text{gt}}\bigr\|_{2}^{2}+c_{\max}+C_{\text{neg}}.

(98)

Thus,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DCR}}
is sandwiched between two affine functions of the reconstruction loss
\bigl\|\epsilon_{\theta}(x_{t},h_{\omega}(f_{\phi}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})),t)-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}^{\text{gt}}\bigr\|_{2}^{2}
with strictly positive slopes. In other words, minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DCR}}
is equivalent (up to a positive scaling factor and an additive constant) to minimizing the standard reconstruction loss. We can summarize this equivalence in the form

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DCR}}=\lambda\bigl\|\epsilon_{\theta}(x_{t},h_{\omega}(f_{\phi}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})),t)-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t}^{\text{gt}}\bigr\|_{2}^{2}+c,

(99)

for some
\lambda\in[\lambda_{\min},\lambda_{\max}]
and constant
c
.

This completed the proof.
∎

E.1
CLIP Backbones

Here, we provide a detailed summary of the
6

3

•

OpenAI CLIP ViT-L@224 and OpenAI CLIP ViT-L@336

65
establish the canonical formulation of contrastive language-image pretraining, defining the feature space that later CLIP variants build upon. Their large-scale but noisy training data gives them strong open-world generalization, while their ability to perceive fine-grained visual details remains limited. The 336-resolution variant uniquely extends CLIP’s receptive field without altering the architecture, making it a standard choice for studying high-resolution alignment.

•

MetaCLIP ViT-L@224 and MetaCLIP ViT-H@224

87
distinguishes itself by reconstructing a high-quality, well-aligned training corpus using a principled filtering pipeline rather than relying on raw web data. This dataset-centric redesign leads to representations that are more stable, less noisy, and better calibrated than those of OpenAI CLIP. The ViT-H model further pushes scaling laws within the CLIP paradigm.

•

SigLIP ViT-SO@224 and SigLIP ViT-SO@384

95
departs from the classic softmax contrastive loss by introducing a sigmoid-based objective, fundamentally altering how positive and negative pairs influence training. This probabilistic formulation enables more fine-grained pairwise alignment and reduces overconfidence artifacts commonly observed in CLIP-like models. Its higher-resolution variant leverages the smoother objective to maintain stable training.

Appendix G
Future Works

In the future, we plan to extend our diffusion contrastive reconstruction beyond diffusion models to VAR-based generators, enabling a broader family of generative priors to contribute fine-grained visual supervision. In parallel, we aim to investigate how our framework can be applied to strengthen visual encoders from diverse architectures, thereby providing a unified and adaptable enhancement strategy for a wide range of vision systems. Moreover, we believe that a systematic study of reconstruction-based methods is essential for uncovering the underlying principles of representation enhancement and for establishing rigorous theoretical guarantees.
