Harnessing probiotics against candidiasis: a systematic review of experimental and mechanistic evidence

Taparia Saloni, Nagaraju Chaithra, Shivaswamy Umamaheshwari

Abstract

BackgroundCandida species are among the most important opportunistic fungal pathogens responsible for superficial mucosal infections and life-threatening invasive candidiasis, particularly in immunocompromised individuals. The increasing prevalence of antifungal resistance, biofilm-associated tolerance, and the emergence of multidrug-resistant species such as Candida auris have highlighted the need for alternative or adjunctive therapeutic strategies. Probiotics have emerged as promising microbiome-based interventions owing to their ability to inhibit Candida growth, suppress virulence traits, restore microbial homeostasis, and modulate host immune responses.MethodsThis systematic review comprehensively evaluated the experimental and mechanistic evidence regarding the anti-Candida activity of probiotics and probiotic-derived products. A systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science for studies published between 1 January 2015 and 5 January 2026 in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines. Studies meeting the predefined eligibility criteria were included in the qualitative synthesis.ResultsEighteen studies met the predefined eligibility criteria. Most studies investigated Lactobacillus species, followed by Bifidobacterium, Saccharomyces, and probiotic-derived metabolites or postbiotics, using predominantly in vitro models with limited in vivo evidence. Across studies, probiotics consistently demonstrated anti-Candida activity through inhibition of fungal growth, adhesion, yeast-to-hyphal transition, biofilm formation, and virulence-associated gene expression. Additional mechanisms included the production of antimicrobial metabolites, competitive exclusion, immune modulation, enhancement of epithelial barrier function, and restoration of microbial homeostasis. Emerging evidence also supports the antifungal potential of postbiotics and activity against non-albicans Candida species and Candida auris, although available evidence remains limited. Considerable heterogeneity in probiotic strains, experimental models, methodologies, and outcome measures precluded quantitative meta-analysis.ConclusionsCurrent evidence supports probiotics and postbiotics as promising microbiome-based strategies for reducing Candida pathogenicity; however, the available evidence remains predominantly preclinical. Well-designed clinical studies are required to establish strain-specific efficacy, optimal formulations, dosing strategies, safety, and therapeutic applicability before routine clinical implementation.Systematic review registrationhttps://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/view/CRD420261278363, identifier CRD420261278363.

Introduction

Candida species represent a major group of opportunistic fungal pathogens associated with a broad spectrum of clinical manifestations, ranging from superficial mucocutaneous infections to life-threatening invasive candidiasis, with increased prevalence, morbidity and mortality among immunocompromised individuals (). The global burden of candidiasis continues to increase owing to widespread use of invasive medical procedures, broad-spectrum antibiotics, immunosuppressive therapies, and the expanding population of susceptible hosts. Invasive candidiasis represents one of the most common healthcare-associated fungal infections worldwide and is associated with mortality rates ranging from 30% to 60%, depending on the patient population and healthcare setting (). Looking into this growing threat, the World Health Organization (WHO) has introduced the Fungal Priority Pathogens List (FPPL), which categorizes them into critical, high, and medium priority groups, with Candida species represented across all categories, highlighting their global clinical and public health significance ().

Under normal conditions, Candida species exist as commensal yeasts colonizing mucosal and epithelial surfaces, including the oral cavity, gastrointestinal tract, urogenital tract, skin, and respiratory tract (; ). However, under predisposing conditions such as immunosuppression, malignancy, diabetes mellitus, HIV/AIDS, prolonged antibiotic exposure, and disruption of the host microbiota they undergo a phenotypic transition from commensalism to pathogenicity through a yeast-to-hyphal morphological switch (; ). This transition facilitates tissue invasion, immune evasion, and biofilm formation, thereby contributing to increased pathogenicity and the development of both localized and systemic infections.

The emergence of non-albicans Candida (NAC), including Candida tropicalis, Candida glabrata, Candida parapsilosis, Candida krusei and Candida lusitaniae has further complicated clinical management due to their intrinsic and acquired antifungal resistance profiles (; ). In addition, Candida auris has emerged as a multidrug-resistant pathogen associated with healthcare-associated outbreaks, high transmissibility, and significant mortality, posing a significant global health challenge.

A key determinant of Candida pathogenicity is its ability to form biofilms on mucosal surfaces and medical devices. These structured microbial communities are embedded in an extracellular matrix that limits antifungal penetration and enhances resistance to host defenses, contributing to persistent and recurrent infections. Disruption of normal microbiota further exacerbates disease progression by reducing colonization resistance and impairing immune homeostasis.

The increasing prevalence of antifungal resistance has further complicated candidiasis management. The prevalence of azole resistance has increased greatly recently in NAC and in Candida auris. This has reduced the effectiveness of commonly employed antifungals, such as fluconazole. Echinocandins, which form the basis for treatment of invasive candidiasis, have also shown increasing resistance too, especially when dealing with C. glabrata infections. Another important factor contributing to antifungal failure is biofilm tolerance. It is characterized by antifungal tolerance shown by sessile cells of Candida present in biofilms.

Although antifungal agents remain the mainstay of therapy, their clinical effectiveness is increasingly limited by toxicity, emerging resistance, and poor activity against biofilm-associated cells. These challenges underscore the urgent need for alternative or adjunctive therapeutic strategies that can target Candida virulence while restoring microbial homeostasis.

Recent advances in microbiome research have highlighted the critical role of commensal microorganisms in maintaining mucosal homeostasis, providing colonization resistance against opportunistic pathogens and regulating the host immune responses. Disruption of the host microbiota has been recognized as an important predisposing factor for Candida overgrowth and infection. Consequently, probiotic-based interventions have attracted increasing attention as potential microbiome-based strategies capable of inhibiting Candida growth, suppressing virulence-associated traits, restoring microbial balance, and enhancing host defense mechanisms. Unlike conventional antifungal agents, probiotics may exert their effects through multiple complementary mechanisms, including direct inhibition of Candida growth, suppression of virulence traits, restoration of microbial homeostasis, and modulation of host immune responses (; ).

In this context, probiotics have emerged as potential biotherapeutic agents with antifungal, antibiofilm, and immunomodulatory properties (; ; ). Defined as live microorganisms that confer health benefits to the host when administered in adequate amounts, probiotics, particularly species belonging to Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, and Saccharomyces have demonstrated inhibitory effects against Candida through multiple mechanisms. These include competitive exclusion, production of antimicrobial metabolites, inhibition of adhesion and biofilm formation, enhancement of epithelial barrier function, and modulation of host immune responses ().

Although multiple studies have evaluated the effectiveness of probiotics for their anti-Candida activity, the available evidence remains fragmented due to substantial heterogeneity in probiotic strains, experimental models, methodologies and outcome measures. Furthermore, there has been considerable research on C. albicans in the past; however, Comparatively relatively few investigations into NAC species, newly emerging species like C. auris mechanisms related to pathogenicity (such as biofilm production and phenotypic switching), and even the use of metabolites and postbiotics from probiotics. To our knowledge, no previous systematic review has comprehensively integrated the experimental and mechanistic evidence regarding probiotic-mediated anti-Candida activity across diverse Candida species, virulence mechanisms, and probiotic-derived products.

Therefore, this systematic review aims to comprehensively evaluate mechanistic and experimental evidence on the role of probiotics in the prevention and management of candidiasis, with emphasis on anti-Candida activity, biofilm inhibition, virulence suppression, and host immune modulation.

Methods

Protocol and study registration

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidelines. The review protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under registration number CRD420261278363 before completion of data extraction and analysis. A predefined methodological framework was established before study selection to ensure transparency, reproducibility and methodological rigor throughout the review process while minimizing the risk of selection and reporting bias at each stage.

Eligibility criteria

A thorough literature search was done to identify relevant studies published between 1 January 2015 and 5 January 2026 representing the most recent 10-year evidence base available on probiotic interventions against Candida species. Eligibility was restricted to peer-reviewed original research articles published in English, owing to practical considerations related to language accessibility during screening and data extraction.

Studies were considered eligible if they investigated the anti-Candida, antibiofilm, anti-virulence or immunomodulatory effects of probiotics, probiotic-derived metabolites against Candida species using experimental in vitro and/or in vivo models or clinical study designs.

Eligible studies were required to report at least one predefined antifungal-related outcome, including inhibition of fungal growth, adhesion, germ tube formation, yeast-to-hyphal transition, virulence factors, biofilm development or modulation of host immune responses. Studies were excluded if they were review articles, editorials, book chapters, conference abstracts, or case reports. Additionally, studies not involving probiotics as an intervention, studies with incomplete or irrelevant data, non-English publications, and studies published before 1 January 2015 were excluded from the review. A summary of the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria is presented in .

Although clinical studies were considered eligible during screening, no clinical studies meeting the predefined inclusion criteria were ultimately identified for inclusion in the final qualitative synthesis.

A quantitative meta-analysis could not be performed due to high heterogeneity among the included studies. The heterogeneity arose from differences among the studies due to the use of different probiotic strains such as Lactobacillus paracasei, Lactobacillus rhamnosus, Lactobacillus fermentum, Lactobacillus acidophilus, Bacillus subtilis., etc. differences in the use of Candida species such as Candida albicans, Candida krusei, Candida tropicalis, Candida glabrata., etc, experimental models (in vitro, in vivo), different in the dosages and treatment durations, methods adapted for evaluating the anti-Candida activity, and outcomes measures. These differences limited the feasibility of performing a meta-analysis.

Search strategy

A systematic literature search of PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus was conducted to identify relevant studies published between 1 January 2015 and 5 January 2026, with the final search completed on 5 January 2026. These databases were selected because they provide comprehensive coverage of biomedical, microbiological and interdisciplinary scientific literature, thereby maximizing the identification of relevant studies investigating probiotic interventions against Candida species.

The search strategy was developed through preliminary scoping searches, including Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terminology and examination of keywords used in previously published literature. Both controlled vocabulary terms and free-text keywords were combined using Boolean operators (AND, OR, and NOT) to ensure comprehensive yet targeted retrieval of relevant studies.

A broad search strategy combining probiotic-related and Candida-related terms was used to maximize sensitivity and minimize the risk of missing potentially relevant studies. A highly sensitive search strategy was adopted because probiotic-Candida research encompasses diverse probiotic strains, Candida species, probiotic derived products and outcome measures. Although this results in a large number of records, it ensured comprehensive retrieval of all the available evidence and minimizes the risk of excluding potentially relevant studies. Outcome-specific keywords such as antibiofilm activity, virulence inhibition, and immunomodulation were intentionally not included to avoid introducing selection bias. The core search string used was:

(“probiotic” OR “Lactobacillus” OR “Bifidobacterium” OR “Saccharomyces”) AND (“Candida” OR “candidiasis”).

The complete database-specific search strategies are provided in .

PICO framework

The review was structured according to the PICO framework:

- Population (P): Experimental in vitro and in vivo models involving Candida species, including C. albicans, NAC species, and C. auris.
- Intervention (I): Administration of probiotic strains, probiotic-derived metabolites, or formulations via any route.
- Comparison (C): Untreated controls or standard antifungal therapies.
- Outcomes (O): Antifungal activity, reduction in fungal growth or colony-forming units (CFU), inhibition of adhesion and biofilm formation, suppression of virulence factors and yeast-to-hyphal transition, modulation of host immune responses, and restoration of microbial balance.

Literature screening process

All retrieved records were imported into Microsoft Excel for organization and management. Duplicate records were identified and removed prior to screening. A two-stage screening process was conducted independently, beginning with title and abstract screening to exclude irrelevant studies, followed by full-text assessment based on predefined eligibility criteria.

Disagreements between reviewers during screening and eligibility assessment were resolved through discussion and consensus. Where necessary, a third reviewer was consulted to resolve discrepancies. Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage were documented and incorporated into the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram.

Data extraction

Data were collected using a predefined extraction sheet created in Microsoft Excel before the review commenced. Two reviewers (ST and CN) independently extracted the relevant study information. Any disagreements identified during the extraction process were discussed and resolved with the involvement of a third reviewer (US).

The following information was extracted from each study:

- first author and year of publication
- country of origin,
- study design,
- probiotic strain(s) or postbiotic intervention,
- target Candida species,
- experimental model and methodology,
- Key Outcomes,

Quality assessment

Methodological quality assessment of the included studies was conducted using a predefined methodological appraisal checklist developed by the authors and adapted from previously reported quality assessment approaches used for experimental in vitro and in vivo studies. Since no validated appraisal tool exists for heterogeneous mechanistic probiotic-Candida studies, the checklist was developed by adapting methodological domains commonly reported in previous systematic reviews.

The appraisal checklist evaluated eight methodological domains:

- clarity of study objective
- appropriateness of study design,
- identification of probiotic strain or intervention,
- identification of Candida species,
- adequacy and reproducibility of experimental methods,
- clarity of outcome reporting,
- inclusion of appropriate control or comparator groups.
- quantitative outcome assessment and statistical reporting,

Each item was scored as 1 if the information was clearly provided and 0 if no information, vague description, or insufficient detail was provided. Hence, the maximum number of scores was 8 points per study.

Based on total score, studies were classified as:

- 0-2 = low quality
- 3-5 = moderate quality
- 6-8 = high quality

Quality assessment was performed independently by two reviewers, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion. The complete methodological appraisal criteria and the quality results of included studies are summarized in . The use of a tailored methodological appraisal checklist was adapted as it enabled evaluation of study-specific methodological features relevant to experimental probiotic-Candida research, including probiotic characterization, Candida species identification and mechanistic outcome measurement which are not completely addressed by conventional clinical risk-of-bias tools. However, this approach has certain limitations as the use of a customised appraisal framework may limit the direct comparison with quality assessments reported in other systematic reviews employing standardized assessment tools.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias assessment was assessed using a domain-based methodological quality assessment approach adapted from Cochrane risk-of-bias principles to evaluate the included in vitro and in vivo studies. Four domains were evaluated: selection bias, performance bias, detection bias, and reporting bias. Selection bias was assessed based on the adequacy of experimental group selection and the identification of probiotic interventions and Candida species. Performance bias evaluated the experimental procedures, treatment conditions and intervention protocols. Detection bias assessed the objectivity, reproducibility and appropriateness of outcome measurements. Reporting bias evaluated the transparency and consistency of outcomes reported. Each domain was categorized as low risk, some concerns, or high risk according to the methodology adopted and the outcomes reported. A complete risk of bias judgment was determined for each study using a conservative approach, whereby the final rating reflected the highest level of concern identified across the assessed domains. As the included evidence primarily consisted of experimental laboratory studies rather than randomized clinical trials, the assessment framework was modified to better align with the methodological characteristics of probiotic research. Standardized risk-of-bias tools such as SYRCLE and Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklists were not fully applicable due to the heterogeneous nature of in vitro and in vivo experimental designs. Therefore, a modified domain-based assessment framework was employed to better capture the methodological characteristics of probiotic-Candida experimental studies.

For graphical representation, the risk-of-bias assessments were presented using the robvis tool, which was employed to generate traffic-light and summary bar plots for better interpretation of methodological quality across the included studies.

Results

Study selection

A total of 36,955 records were identified through database searching, including Scopus (29,992), Web of Science (6,218), and PubMed (745). Prior to screening, 11,703 records were removed as non-research articles and 2,519 duplicates were excluded, leaving 22,733 records for title and abstract screening.

During title and abstract screening, 22,609 records were excluded because they were unrelated to the review objective or did not meet the predefined eligibility criteria. A total of 124 full-text articles were subsequently assessed for eligibility.

Following full-text review, 106 articles were excluded due to insufficient methodological detail, absence of probiotic-based interventions, or failure to report predefined anti-Candida outcomes, inappropriate study designs or failure to meet predefined inclusion criteria. The substantial reduction from 36,955 initially identified records to 18 included studies reflects the use of a highly sensitive search strategy designed to maximize the retrieval of potentially relevant literature. Most records were excluded during title and abstract screening because they were unrelated to probiotic-mediated anti-Candida activity, whereas full-text exclusions primarily resulted from the absence of probiotic interventions, failure to report predefined anti-Candida outcomes, insufficient methodological detail, or failure to satisfy the predefined eligibility criteria. Finally, 18 studies met the predefined inclusion criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis. No eligible clinical studies meeting the predefined inclusion criteria were identified.

The study selection process involved a sequential screening of records identified through all the databases followed by removal of duplicates. Later it was screened based on title and abstracts for the relevance of study including a full-text evaluation as per the predefined eligibility criteria. The complete study selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram ().

Characteristics of included studies

The characteristics of the included studies are summarized in . The 18 included studies were published between 2015 and 2026 and were conducted across diverse geographical regions, including Thailand, China, Brazil, Iran, India, Egypt, Malaysia, Italy, Bulgaria, and Japan,

Most studies employed in vitro experimental designs, while several also incorporated in vivo models such as murine models and Galleria mellonella larvae and Caenorhabditis elegans, to evaluate antifungal efficacy and host survival outcomes.

The probiotic strains investigated predominantly belonged to the genus Lactobacillus, particularly L. rhamnosus, L. paracasei, L. fermentum, and L. crispatus. Additional genera included Bifidobacterium, Streptococcus, and Enterococcus. The predominance of Lactobacillus species among the included studies suggest that they represent the most extensively investigated probiotic against Candida infections owing to their well-established antifungal properties. Several studies also evaluated probiotic-derived cell-free supernatants and postbiotic formulations. The primary target pathogen studied was Candida albicans, although several studies also evaluated NAC including Candida glabrata, Candida tropicalis, Candida krusei, Candida parapsilosis, and Candida famata. Though current probiotic research remains largely focused on Candida albicans, whereas evidence regarding NAC species and emerging multidrug-resistant pathogens such as Candida auris remains comparatively limited, highlighting an important gap in the existing literature.

Across studies, probiotics consistently demonstrated antifungal and antibiofilm activity through multiple mechanisms, including inhibition of fungal growth, suppression of germ tube and hyphal formation, disruption of biofilm development, inhibition of adhesion, and downregulation of virulence-associated genes as shown in .

Anti-Candida mechanisms of probiotics

The included studies consistently demonstrated that probiotics exert anti-Candida effects through multiple complementary mechanisms targeting fungal growth, virulence, biofilm formation, and host-pathogen interactions. The principal mechanisms identified across the included studies were.

- inhibition of Candida growth and adhesion
- suppression of yeast-to-hyphal transition
- disruption and prevention of biofilm formation
- downregulation of virulence-associated genes (including ALS3, HWP1, CPH1, EFG1, and ECE1)
- inhibition of hydrolytic enzyme activity such as proteases and phospholipases
- production of antimicrobial metabolites including organic acids and hydrogen peroxide
- modulation of host immune responses enhancement of antifungal defense and restoration of microbial homeostasis .

Several studies additionally reported immunomodulatory effects, including enhancement of host antifungal defense responses and restoration of microbial homeostasis. These mechanisms were observed against both C. albicans and NAC species across in vitro and in vivo experimental systems.

Quality assessment of included studies

The methodological quality assessment of the included studies is summarized in . Overall, the included studies demonstrated high methodological quality, with total quality scores ranging from 7 to 8 out of a maximum possible score of 8. All studies clearly reported their objectives, study design, probiotic intervention, target Candida species, experimental methodology, and primary outcomes. Minor methodological limitations were primarily related to incomplete quantitative outcome assessment and statistical reporting or insufficient description of control/comparator groups in a small number of studies. Despite the overall high methodological quality, considerable heterogeneity remained with respect to probiotic strains, experimental models, treatment conditions, outcome measures, and methodological approaches, which precluded quantitative meta-analysis. No study was classified as moderate or low quality according to the predefined methodological appraisal criteria.

Risk of bias assessment

Most included studies demonstrated a low to moderate risk of bias within each assessed methodological domain. Most studies clearly described the aim of experiments, interventions, outcomes, and methodology properly.

However, several studies demonstrated unclear risk of bias within domains such as comparator standardization, blinding and outcome assessment, and methodological reporting. Differences in experimental design and reporting led to heterogeneity of methodology quality.

Discussion

The incidence of Candida species infections has increased significantly over recent decades, with these organisms shifting from commensal microorganisms to opportunistic pathogens capable of causing both mucosal and systemic infections, particularly among immunocompromised populations. This increase is largely driven by widespread antibiotic use, immunosuppressive therapies, invasive medical procedures, and a growing population of susceptible individuals. In parallel, the emergence of antifungal resistance, particularly among Candida albicans and non-albicans Candida (NAC) species, has further complicated clinical management, highlighting the urgent need for alternative therapeutic strategies (; ).

The findings of this systematic review demonstrate substantial preclinical evidence supporting the antifungal and antibiofilm potential of probiotics against Candida species. Across the included studies, probiotic strains, predominantly belonging to Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, and Saccharomyces species, demonstrated broad antifungal activity through multiple complementary mechanisms (; ; ). These effects included inhibition of fungal proliferation, suppression of yeast-to-hyphal transition, disruption of biofilm formation, and modulation of host immune responses, collectively contributing to reduced Candida colonization and pathogenicity. These observations are consistent with emerging evidence highlighting probiotics as microbiome-based therapeutic Candidates capable of modulating fungal colonization, restoring microbial homeostasis, and enhancing host defense responses against opportunistic fungal pathogens (; ; ).

One of the most important findings of this review was the consistent inhibition of Candida biofilm formation across multiple experimental models. Biofilms represent major virulence determinants associated with reduced antifungal susceptibility, chronic infection, and persistence on mucosal surfaces and medical devices. Several included studies demonstrated that probiotics inhibited biofilm biomass, reduced biofilm maturation, and modulated expression of virulence-associated genes, including ALS3, HWP1, CPH1, EFG1, and ECE1. These findings are particularly significant because biofilm-associated Candida cells exhibit reduced susceptibility to conventional antifungal agents (; ). Therefore, targeting biofilm development represents an important strategy for reducing Candida pathogenicity and overcoming antifungal resistance (; ).

Despite consistent evidence supporting probiotic-mediated anti-Candida activity, the magnitude of antifungal effects varied considerably among studies, highlighting the importance of strain-specific characteristics. Although Lactobacillus species represent the most extensively investigated probiotic group against Candida infections, antifungal activity is not uniform among individual strains. Wang et al. demonstrated strong inhibition of C. albicans growth and yeast-to-hyphal transition by Lactobacillus crispatus, associated with suppression of virulence-related gene expression (). In contrast, Hashem et al. reported pronounced antibiofilm activity with Lactobacillus acidophilus and Lactobacillus plantarum cell-free supernatants against clinical Candida isolates (). Similarly, Rossoni et al. demonstrated that Lactobacillus paracasei strains reduced biofilm formation and modulated Candida virulence-associated gene expression (). These differences suggest that probiotic efficacy depends not only on probiotic genus but also on strain-specific properties, including metabolite production, adhesion capacity, ecological origin, surface characteristics, and interaction with Candida cells (; ). Moreover, variations in experimental design, including differences in Candida isolates, growth conditions, probiotic concentration, exposure duration, and outcome assessment methods, may contribute to the variability observed among studies and should be considered when interpreting comparative antifungal efficacy.

Differences in susceptibility between C. albicans and NAC species further indicate that probiotic activity may be species dependent. Although several studies demonstrated activity against NAC species, including C. glabrata, C. tropicalis, C. krusei, and C. parapsilosis, most mechanistic investigations continue to focus on C. albicans. Inturri et al. and Salari et al. reported inhibitory effects of probiotic-derived products against multiple Candida species, suggesting broader antifungal potential beyond C. albicans (; ). However, limited information is available regarding whether probiotic-mediated mechanisms are conserved across Candida species or whether species-specific strategies are required. This limitation is particularly relevant for emerging multidrug-resistant pathogens such as Candida auris. Rossoni et al. demonstrated that Lactobacillus paracasei-derived postbiotics exhibited activity against C. auris by disrupting biofilms, reducing persister cells, and improving survival in an experimental infection model (). Nevertheless, additional studies are required to determine whether these promising findings can be translated into clinically effective interventions.

The antifungal effects of probiotics appear to result from the combined action of multiple complementary mechanisms rather than a single inhibitory pathway. Organic acids, hydrogen peroxide, bacteriocin-like compounds, and other bioactive metabolites modify the local microenvironment, thereby reducing fungal adhesion, inhibiting yeast-to-hyphal transition, and preventing biofilm maturation (; ). In addition, probiotics may enhance colonization resistance by restoring microbial homeostasis, strengthening epithelial barrier integrity, and modulating innate and adaptive immune responses. The simultaneous targeting of multiple pathogenic pathways may explain the broad anti-Candida activity observed across different experimental models and probiotic strains (; ).

An important distinction among the included studies was the use of live probiotic organisms compared with probiotic-derived metabolites and postbiotic formulations. Studies evaluating live probiotic strains primarily emphasized mechanisms such as competitive exclusion, adhesion inhibition, and restoration of microbial balance. In contrast, studies investigating cell-free supernatants and postbiotics demonstrated direct antifungal activity mediated by secreted metabolites. Rossoni et al. showed that Lactobacillus paracasei-derived postbiotics exhibited potent activity against C. auris, including biofilm disruption, reduction of persister cells, and enhancement of host protection in an experimental infection model (). Similarly, several studies demonstrated antifungal effects of probiotic-derived supernatants through organic acids and antimicrobial compounds (; ). These findings suggest that postbiotics may provide advantages related to formulation stability and defined antimicrobial activity; however, the active compounds responsible for these effects remain incompletely characterized.

Previous studies have demonstrated the potential role of probiotics in fungal infections, particularly against C. albicans, emphasizing their antimicrobial and immunomodulatory properties. However, most earlier reviews focused on selected probiotic strains, clinical applications, or specific aspects of Candida pathogenesis (). For example, previous systematic reviews evaluated the role of probiotics in reducing oral Candida colonization and prevention of candidiasis but provided limited discussion regarding strain-level differences, biofilm inhibition, virulence regulation, and activity against emerging Candida species (; ). In contrast, the present systematic review provides a broader synthesis of experimental and mechanistic evidence by integrating findings involving C. albicans, NAC species, and the emerging multidrug-resistant pathogen C. auris. Furthermore, this review highlights multiple anti-Candida mechanisms, including fungal growth inhibition, virulence suppression, biofilm disruption, immune modulation, and the therapeutic potential of probiotic-derived metabolites and postbiotics.

Emerging evidence further suggests that probiotics may influence Candida pathogenesis through additional mechanisms, including quorum-sensing interference, production of bacteriocins and antimicrobial peptides, release of extracellular vesicles, enhancement of epithelial barrier integrity, and restoration of microbiota homeostasis (; ; ; ; ; ). Probiotic-derived effector molecules and secreted vesicles may facilitate host–microbe communication and contribute to antifungal activity by modulating fungal growth and host responses. Furthermore, probiotics can influence innate and adaptive immune pathways, including Toll-like receptor (TLR)-mediated signalling, NF-κB activation, and IL-17-dependent antifungal immunity, which are critical for mucosal defense against Candida albicans (; ). These complementary mechanisms further support the concept that probiotics exert multifactorial rather than single-target antifungal effects.

Despite these promising findings, several limitations should be considered. Most available evidence was derived from in vitro experiments and small-scale in vivo models, which may not fully reproduce the complex interactions between probiotics, Candida, host immunity, and resident microbiota occurring during human infection. Considerable heterogeneity was observed among studies regarding probiotic strains, dosages, formulations, experimental models, treatment duration, and outcome assessment methods, limiting direct comparison between studies. Due to this methodological heterogeneity, quantitative meta-analysis was not considered appropriate. In addition, variability in outcome reporting, limited statistical analysis, and incomplete characterization of probiotic-derived compounds in some studies contributed to uncertainty regarding the strength of available evidence.

Translation of probiotic-based strategies into clinical practice requires consideration of several practical and regulatory challenges. The effectiveness of probiotic interventions may depend on formulation stability, microbial viability, dosage optimization, and delivery approaches. Furthermore, regulatory frameworks for probiotic-based therapeutics vary between countries, creating challenges for clinical approval and implementation. Patient safety must also be carefully considered, particularly among immunocompromised populations, where rare cases of probiotic-associated infections have been reported. Therefore, standardized formulations, rigorous safety evaluations, and adequately powered clinical trials are essential before probiotics can be routinely incorporated into candidiasis management.

Nevertheless, the consistency of antifungal and antibiofilm effects observed across diverse experimental systems provides a strong rationale for further investigation. Future research should focus on standardized clinical trials evaluating strain-specific efficacy, optimal dosing strategies, safety profiles, and long-term outcomes. In addition, mechanistic studies investigating probiotic–host–fungal interactions and metabolite profiling may provide deeper insights into antifungal pathways. Emerging therapeutic approaches involving synbiotics, postbiotics, and targeted microbiome modulation may further improve the stability, specificity, and therapeutic applicability of probiotic-based strategies for candidiasis prevention and management.

Conclusion

This systematic review highlights substantial preclinical evidence supporting the antifungal, antibiofilm, and immunomodulatory effects of probiotics against Candida species, including Candida albicans, non-albicans Candida species, and Candida auris. Probiotic strains, especially Lactobacillus, consistently demonstrated inhibitory effects on fungal growth, adhesion, yeast-to-hyphal transition, biofilm formation, and virulence-associated pathways across diverse experimental models.

However, these findings are derived predominantly from heterogeneous preclinical studies, and their direct translation to clinical practice remains uncertain. Variations in probiotic strains, experimental models, methodologies, and outcome measures limit the generalizability of the current evidence. Therefore, although probiotics represent promising microbiome-based therapeutic candidates, their clinical efficacy, safety, optimal dosing strategies, and long-term outcomes remain to be established through well-designed clinical studies.

Future research should prioritize standardized mechanistic investigations and well-designed clinical trials to determine strain-specific efficacy, optimal therapeutic formulations, dosing strategies, long-term safety, and clinical applicability of probiotics and postbiotics in candidiasis prevention and management.
