Rethinking remimazolam: reconciling regulatory positioning with clinical reality
Authors: Theodora Wingert, J Robert Sneyd
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryGoyal and colleagues share a large United States health system’s experience of 9431 cases of real-world remimazolam use, offering insight into current practice patterns, recovery times, and accurate adverse event rates. The authors report rapid recovery after remimazolam, including shorter time to post-anaesthesia care unit discharge readiness and faster return to baseline on the Richmond Agitation–Sedation Scale following flumazenil administration, with a critical event rate of 0.7%. Interesting practice patterns were observed, including frequent co-administration with other anaesthetics rather than as a sole agent. Although only licensed for procedural sedation in the United States, the authors reported extensive use of remimazolam to induce anaesthesia, especially for patients with an American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status of 3–5. This editorial highlights the disconnect between the development of remimazolam and its practical clinical use, as well as the work still needed to clarify the agent’s optimal role.Keywords: benzodiazepines, drug hypersensitivity, general anaesthesia, pharmacovigilance, procedural sedation, remimazolam

Development of remimazolam besylate has been grindingly slow, from discovery in the late 1990s to United States (US) approval in 2020 (and Europe in 2021). A combination of sustained corporate disinterest and underinvestment meant that a narrow and protracted development programme was inevitable.1 Ownership of remimazolam passed from Glaxo-Wellcome (Research Triangle Park, North Carolina, USA) through TheraSci Ltd (Cambridge, UK) and CeNeS (Cambridge, UK) to Paion (Aachen, Germany), bankrupting the latter in the process.2 Shortfalls in the patenting process left Chinese competitors free to develop the tosylate salt.1 Remimazolam is broadly pharmacodynamically equivalent to midazolam, meaning it produces similar GABAA-mediated effects, with differences primarily in pharmacokinetics.3,4 So, early clinical development focused on procedural sedation5 with the prospect of straightforward trials and easily demonstrated swifter onset and offset. This offered prompt licensing for procedural sedation and some return on earlier investment.

Against this historical backdrop, Goyal and colleagues have shared a three-centre US experience in their retrospective audit of 9431 cases of remimazolam use. In their study, Goyal and colleagues report current practice patterns, recovery times, and accurate adverse event rates. With its use of real-world data, the study provides insight into both practical clinical use and true adverse event rates. By using electronic health record data, both the capture of critical events and, importantly, the denominator (the number of cases in which events could have occurred) are expected to be highly accurate. In contrast, most post-marketing pharmacovigilance studies rely on spontaneous reporting systems and thus are inherently limited by underreporting, reporting bias, and the absence of a true denominator. We consider these data in the context of remimazolam’s regulatory history and developmental trajectory.

General anaesthesia

With its regulatory and developmental origins firmly in sedation, attention then shifted to positioning remimazolam for ‘induction and maintenance of general anaesthesia’, without clearly defining its role as an induction agent.

Induction of anaesthesia by bolus i.v. injection is deceptively simple. ‘Push’ the syringe and the patient loses consciousness, whereas the underpinning complex interaction of pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics is largely unobserved. However, the dose required to achieve unconsciousness is critically dependent on the rate of injection (Table 1).6,7 Neuromuscular block also introduces the possibility that a motionless patient may not be unconscious.8 Consequently, the delayed introduction of sufficient (volatile) maintenance agent due to airway difficulties, obesity, underdosing, or patient transfer all risk accidental awareness.8

These nuances translate into a smoothly integrated performance with, for example, consecutive doses of fentanyl, propofol, rocuronium, and/or sevoflurane, each given in the right dose, at the right time, and at the right rate. Real-time feedback from physical signs and electronic monitoring guides the clinician, thereby closing the loop. Unsurprisingly, therefore, safe induction of anaesthesia in a heterogeneous patient population is a cornerstone of anaesthesia training. Consequently, proposing that anaesthesiologists accustomed to inhaled maintenance deconstruct their learning and switch to an unknown drug (remimazolam), delivered by a pump, is a big ‘ask’. This choice effectively closed the door on those not already routinely practising total i.v. anaesthesia. It also ignored historical reports that benzodiazepines offer stable induction of anaesthesia for high-risk patients (albeit with slow recovery).9,10

So it is that, years later, remimazolam is still not licensed for general anaesthesia in the US. In Europe, remimazolam maintenance is licensed, but is barely used given the high cost of the drug and negligible benefit in comparison with propofol.11 There things might have rested, were it not for anaesthesiologists being naturally curious and anxious to provide the best and safest care for their patients.

Fast-forward to today. Goyal and colleagues observed that remimazolam was mainly used off-licence, principally as an induction agent (53% of operating room administrations). Indeed, it was rarely used for anaesthetic maintenance. Most patients receiving remimazolam were elderly (median age 67 yr) and had higher American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status score (57.8% ASA physical status grade 3, 27.4% grade 4 or 5), suggesting that anaesthesiologists were selecting the agent for older patients with greater illness severity. Goyal and colleagues have illustrated an important and problematic gap in the regulatory approval underpinning remimazolam. Briefly, there is a paucity of data illustrating the optimum induction sequence with remimazolam transitioning to volatile maintenance, such as sevoflurane.

Mind the gap

In the British railway system, staff shout “mind the gap” to warn travellers to beware slipping between the train and the platform. Similarly, anaesthetists must carefully ensure adequate drug dosing during the transition from i.v. induction to inhalational maintenance of anaesthesia. The United Kingdom (UK) 5th National Audit Project (NAP) reported that 72 of 141 cases of anaesthetic awareness occurred during the induction phase of anaesthesia, with 62 (86%) receiving bolus induction agents and volatile maintenance.12 The report observed: ‘As the brain concentration of the intravenous induction agent declines, the brain concentration of the maintenance agent gradually increases. Thus, there may be a period where the overall concentration of anaesthetic agents is lower than desirable…’ Given this background of risk, the use of a new rapidly metabolised i.v. anaesthetic for induction of anaesthesia deserves detailed specification based on structured clinical investigations. Such evidence is currently scant.

Remimazolam and recovery

Goyal and colleagues’ primary aim was to examine times to return to baseline Richmond Agitation–Sedation Scale (RASS) score and post-anaesthesia care unit (PACU) discharge readiness. Median time to baseline RASS was 13 min, and median PACU discharge readiness time was 30 min, demonstrating rapid recovery. The authors also examined recovery times in relation to total remimazolam dose, showing significantly slower recovery at doses over 18 mg. However, in most cases (85%), remimazolam was co-administered with other hypnotics or opioids. Although the authors attempted a dichotomous adjustment for co-administration, this approach is not able to reliably account for such confounding. There are emerging, primarily retrospective, studies examining the co-administration of remimazolam with propofol13 and sevoflurane.14 However, taken together, these findings underscore the need for prospective studies with standardised protocols that account for co-administration and better define the independent effects of remimazolam when used alongside other agents.

Reversal with flumazenil

The availability of a reversal agent15 makes remimazolam a compelling option for clinicians, particularly in challenging non-OR locations, scenarios without anaesthesiologists, or procedures and patients where rapid emergence is desirable. In the study by Goyal and colleagues, flumazenil was used in 13% of all cases and was associated with a modest reduction in recovery time. Sixty-five percent of patients receiving remimazolam during cardioversion were given flumazenil, and this was associated with a reduction of median recovery time from 15 to 9 min. Reversal of remiimazolam's hypnotic effect by flumazenil requires clarification in future studies, so that the proper timing and doses can be established, with consideration of re-sedation. Flumazenil may provoke anxiety and agitation, as well as haemodynamic disturbance, and thus deserves careful consideration. Goyal and colleagues were not able to provide helpful clinical information in this regard.

Remimazolam and procedural sedation

Procedural sedation is not the private hegemony of physician anaesthesiologists, nor even of non-physician anaesthesia providers.16 Other clinicians who already make safe use of midazolam can reasonably be introduced to remimazolam. Indeed, it may offer safety beyond existing agents, given its pharmacologic properties and the availability of a reversal agent. Remimazolam now offers most of what non-anaesthesia sedationists like about propofol, that is, rapid onset, titratability and swift recovery—while reducing cardiorespiratory compromise. Indeed, it now seems hard to justify the use of propofol by non-anaesthetists when a safer, reversible alternative is available.

Accurate accounting of adverse events

The observed rate for critical events was 0.7% in Goyal and colleagues’ study, presumably reflecting a comorbid case-mix. Nineteen allergic reactions were reported. Although the absolute rate was low (0.2%), this significantly exceeds the incidence inferred from published pre-approval clinical trials and post-marketing case reports to date, estimated by the authors at approximately 0.01%–0.02%. The absolute event rates from Goyal and colleagues’ study are of critical interest, as in typical pharmacovigilance studies, while the number of adverse events is available through reporting mechanisms, the denominator (the number of cases in which events could have occurred) is difficult to obtain or unknown. We cannot calculate a rate without both a numerator (number of events) and a denominator. In contrast, the UK’s 6th National Audit Project17 reported one anaphylactic reaction to propofol from an estimated 2 290 723 exposures and none to midazolam from 297 076.

In this context, the inclusion with each 20 mg of remimazolam besylate of 79.13 mg of dextran4018 as a potentially allergenic excipient raises some unanswered questions about potential causation. In contrast, the official Chinese package insert (说明书) for remimazolam tosylate (Ribenin®)19 specifies the excipients as glycine, calcium disodium edetate, and hydrochloric acid. No dextran-containing excipient is listed, indicating that dextran is not part of the Chinese formulation. Although the two formulations appear to be broadly equivalent, head-to-head comparative data are negligible20 and cannot support any conclusions about relative safety.

Remimazolam in the intensive care unit

Remimazolam is not licensed anywhere for intensive care unit (ICU) sedation, although some relevant clinical studies exist or are underway.21 Unsurprisingly, the data of Goyal and colleagues shed no light on this application and we shall have to wait to evaluate such data as it becomes available. While we wait, we should be mindful that OR pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics do not automatically translate to the critical care unit. In particular, extended infusions of both remifentanil and remimazolam generate very high systemic concentrations of their principal metabolites.22,23 Parent drug accumulation of remimazolam has been reported by Japanese investigators and was cited as a reason for discontinuing development of remimazolam as an ICU sedative.24 Further, the high quantity of drug used in such settings means that ICU sedation with remimazolam may be implausibly expensive.

Looking ahead

From a practical standpoint, this audit of over 9000 cases of real-world remimazolam use offers important insight into the true adverse event rate and a helpful window into clinical utility. It also offers a template for future work with data using multi-institution electronic health record systems. Goyal and colleagues have shown us two important insights. Firstly, remimazolam is generally safe and effective. Secondly, there is substantial dissonance between the goals of the clinical programme and the realities of where remimazolam is useful. Several shortfalls of remimazolam’s development stand out (Table 1).

The history of remimazolam’s commercial development highlights corporate and developmental pitfalls that have influenced its trajectory. Despite these early challenges, its favourable pharmacologic profile and the availability of a reversal agent have prompted anaesthesiologists to adopt and explore its use. Further work is needed to clarify its optimal role within the anaesthetic pharmacopoea.
