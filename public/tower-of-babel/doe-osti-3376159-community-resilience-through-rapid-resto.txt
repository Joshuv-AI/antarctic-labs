 Executive Summary
Project Summary
Equitable and automated bottoms-up power restoration following an extreme event will
be demonstrated at a site in Puerto Rico. To do so, the team will develop enhanced grid
situational awareness techniques integrating behind-the-meter (BTM) distributed energy
resources (DER) discovery, impedance sweeping based outage boundary detection, and
feasible restoration path identification algorithms. Resilience metric will be developed and
incorporated along with situational awareness information in a distributed Model
Predictive Control (MPC)-based restoration optimization algorithm to control and mobilize
grid assets. These algorithms will be validated through power hardware-in-the-loop
experiments and ultimately, a site demonstration to show that outage recovery time and
total recovered load could be improved by >20% over the baseline.

Project impact

Extreme weather events, such as a hurricane, can result in significantly longer power
outages, impacting disadvantaged communities inequitably. This project will demonstrate
the value of a bottoms-up approach of power restoration in improving equitability of
access to electric power. Leveraging local DER assets and enhanced grid situational
awareness, this project will incorporate a resilience metric in the restoration optimization
algorithm to shorten outage recovery time and increase the recovered load by >20%
compared to a baseline.

Section I. Background
Following an extreme event, the power and energy disruptions experienced by
communities can have devastating societal and economic impacts. Restoration of
disadvantaged communities is often complicated by geographic remoteness from bulk
power system, which greatly increases the time to restoration, especially in a top-down,
human-driven restoration approach. For instance, the island-wide blackout after
Hurricane Maria left some regions of Puerto Rico without power for months, with only 49%
of the island power restored 60 days after the hurricane [1]. This problem could be mostly
mitigated through a bottom-up approach by forming microgrids and utilizing distributed
energy resources to recover vital loads within minutes. However, automatic restoration
and operation of a damaged power grid require new technologies. To address these
challenges, the goal of this project is to improve community resilience to extreme weather
events by employing smart and automated operational measures [2], leveraging low-cost
sensors, as well as distributed solar photo-voltaic (PV) and battery resources. In the event
of a complete outage of transmission sources, microgrids (MGs) will be formed to serve
local communities. This will improve restoration time, community resilience, and outage
costs. Since outage restoration is a complex problem due to the need to integrate
innovations in various domains (e.g., power electronics, telecommunications, DERs,
energy-storage, and MGs), the team has strategically prioritized the project objectives
according to key challenges and feasibility, as shown below.

Section II. Project Objectives
The project objectives have been identified that will span over three budget periods (BPs).
The goal of this project is to improve community resilience to extreme weather events by
employing smart and automated operational measures, leveraging low-cost and reliable
sensors as well as distributed solar PV and battery resources. Figure 1 shows the project
overview and the high-level focus area of each BPs. In the event of a complete outage of
transmission sources, microgrids will be formed to serve local communities. The
proposed solution will greatly improve restoration time, community resilience and outage
costs compared to the baseline performance.

 Figure 1 Three BPs focusing on development, validation, and field demonstration.

The project is separated into three phases: Community partner and demonstration site
selection, and engagement plan development (BP1), software and hardware
development and system validation (BP2), and field demonstration (BP3). The utility
partner will be engaged throughout the project to prioritize stakeholder needs. The
description of work scope for each BP is given as follows:
BP1 (9 months): The team will work to 1) identify a demonstration site and associated
community partner and 2) develop a community engagement plan. Meetings with the
community partner will be organized to receive input, which will inform technology R&D
in the subsequent budget periods.
BP2 (27 months): The objectives of BP2 are: 1) development of proposed hardware,
software, and resilience metrics, 2) integration and validation of proposed technologies in
a grid simulator. The team will work to develop resilience plan for the identified community
and develop early prototypes of proposed technology. Through continuous community
engagement (detailed in BP1), baseline technology capabilities (e.g., list of available
DERs, feeder configurations, current restoration practices) and restoration constraints will
be obtained. In addition, the team will design system GEVARhitecture and develop
technical specifications for the proposed hardware and software. With the specifications
identified, the team will develop advisory solutions for improving situational awareness
and operational solutions for restoration automation. The sensor development will focus
on development of core device capability and addition of outdoor weather packaging. The
team will also work on development and integration of proposed software. The outputs of
situational awareness algorithms will be provided as inputs to the restoration optimizer.
The restoration optimizer output will be integrated with GFM controllers and controllable
loads. Using representative feeders, developed together with the utility partner, the team
will run simulations to confirm integration. In addition to integration tests, the team will

 Page 9 of 45

validate the proposed solution using the power hardware-in-the-loop (PHIL) simulator in
the ARIES facility. The team will work with local universities in Puerto Rico to educate
graduate students and collaborate with faculty on the proposed technologies.
BP3 (9 months): The objectives of BP3 are installation and demonstration of the
proposed technologies in a location in Puerto Rico (PR) that serves a disadvantaged
community. The work scope in this BP includes installation of hardware and software in
Puerto Rico, at a location serving a disadvantaged community, and demonstration of the
applicable technologies. Community outreach activities will continue to inform
demonstration schedule and scope. Prior to installation, the team will develop a test plan
that will reflect the most relevant use cases and tests conducted in the PHIL environment.
The team will conduct site acceptance tests before the final field demonstration. A test
report will be prepared, including test processes, steps, and results, as well as lessons
learned and recommendations.

The team then came up with a scoring strategy to convert the categorical responses to
many of these questions to quantitative data and a weighing strategy to assign relative
importance to these questions for site selection such that probability of a successful
demonstration can be increased. For example, Table 4 shows the scoring scheme being
applied to three questions (a main and two sub questions) from the suitability criteria. A
scale of 1 to 5 is selected, where the presence of a microgrid is assigned a base score.
However, sites with a greater number of microgrids get a higher score in the Q# 1.2 and
sites with available information on generation capacity in Q# 1.3 get a higher score. Thus,
this results in Site 5 having the highest score, Site 4 to have the same score as Sites 1
and 2 owing to having more microgrids but less information, and Site 3 to be scored the
least due to lack of information. Although, the scoring scale is arbitrary, assigning a
scoring strategy and applying it uniformly across all sites allow us to evaluate the sites in
a quantitative manner. The weight (on a scale from 1 – 10) assigned to each question is
to reflect their relative importance. For example, the question “Does the site have
microgrids?” is assigned a weight of 10 because having microgrids will allow to provide
restoration using local resources. However, another question “Does the site have feeders
that are reconfigurable?” is assigned a weight of 7. This is because while reconfigurable
feeders could allow multiple paths for power restoration, it is not an essential feature as
power restoration can be demonstrated in a fixed network as well.

Task 3.0: Develop low-cost sensor
To improve the situational awareness, the sensor needs to be hardened for extreme
weather to generate data needed for estimating PV and storage availability. Sensors
based on passive non-intrusive platforms have shown promise in prior work at Sandia
National Laboratories (SNL), which was based on magneto-mechanical resonator for
ultra-low-cost current sensing. Building on the work in DE-EE-0009632, the team planned
to expand sensor capabilities for outdoor installation on utility-owned, medium voltage
connected assets on distribution grid.
Subtask 3.1: Develop low-cost sensor design specifications and prototype
Status – IN-PROGRESS: (SNL)

SNL and LUMA have collaborated to identify the requirements for the proposed low-cost
sensor considering LUMA electrical network. Several reports on microgrid design in
Vieques and Culebra have been reviewed by SNL towards finalizing the design
specifications for the sensor.
We have completed the first version of the sensor design specification documents
describing the different current, voltage and power range utilizing input for GE Vernova,
LUMA as well as information from NOAA and USGS websites for typical environmental
conditions.

In the demonstration of a prototype of the current sensor some practical challenges
emerged with regards to detecting AC current. We made good progress towards that.
Earlier using a vector network analyzer (VNA) which also provides drive signal for the
resonator antenna DC current (0-20A) was successfully detected which showed a near-
linear correspondence in the resonance frequency about the 0 current frequency value of
58.18kHz (Figure 4Error! Reference source not found.). When tested with AC current
the resonance frequency is expected to oscillate about the center frequency (zero current)
with a frequency shift that is a function of the current amplitude like a single tone
frequency modulated signal. A phase locked loop (PLL) circuit was used to demodulate
the signal which is implemented on a bread board. PLL response was tested and
calibrated using a signal generator exhibiting a linear rang of 51-61kHz with a sensitivity
of ~~0.1V/kHz. However, the VNA which was used to drive the resonator antenna had a
limited sweep rate (~10Hz) preventing detection of 50Hz AC signal due to aliasing effects.
Nevertheless, preliminary measurement with VNA showed oscillation of the reflection dip
(S11 signal) when driven with VNA for 1A – 10Hz signal. To detect 50Hz AC a voltage-
controlled oscillator (VCO) circuit capable of a sweep rate of > 500Hz is required. The
exciter circuit with voltage controlled adjustable sweep rate > 500KHz has been
developed and tested showing that it can successfully excite the resonator. The sweep
circuit provided sufficient current to the antenna to excite the resonator (Figure 5). One
additional challenge remains to be addressed which relates to the low-quality factor of
resonator signal, that is, the resonance peaks decays relatively slowly compared to the
AC signal. This requires that only the signal close to the resonance peak is input to the
PLL.

Task 4.0: Develop software for grid situational awareness
Subtask 4.1: Develop situational awareness software specifications and use cases
Status – COMPLETED: (GEVAR + NLR)
The system architecture (Figure 7) was designed to integrate the proposed algorithms.
Following a severe event, external inputs such as weather forecasts and customer outage
calls are fed into a sensing & analytics layer that incorporates power flow modeling,
customer-level outage estimation, outage boundary detection using impedance sweeps,
and DER discovery algorithm to determine their availability, with these data streams
integrated into a comprehensive grid status assessment module. The sensing layer can
also utilize field measurements, and emerging low-cost sensors to further enhance
situational awareness. The resulting outputs, including feeder status, DER forecasts, and
resource availability, are then provided to a restoration optimization layer, where the

                                              Page 20 of 45

rolling-horizon optimization with equity-aware resilience metrics will determine the most
effective recovery actions. These optimized decisions are subsequently implemented
through an asset-level control module that coordinates controllable loads, grid-forming
inverters, and feeder sectionalization, enabling actions such as distributed solar inverter
control, strategic load shedding and pickup, and distributed energy storage dispatch.
Overall, the framework provides an integrated, data-driven architecture for resilient
outage detection, restoration prioritization, and adaptive service recovery.

Outage Boundary Detection (NLR)
The algorithm developed was based on injection of low voltage by grid forming (GFM)
battery energy storage system (BESS) inverter into the grid in black out condition (Figure
8). Measured voltage and current waveforms were used to calculate the impedance of
the network and then compare it with results obtained either from power flow or PSCAD
model of the system. The same waveforms were used to determine the imbalances in the
system as well as indicating possible existing faults that need to be repaired or isolated
before the black start process.

 Figure 8. Outage Boundary Detection Algorithm
Using this algorithm, the team:
 • Conducted testing and validation of outage boundary detection method using NLR
 ARIES testing capabilities
 • Conducted studies using PSCAD model of the system under study (Vieques &
 Culebra (V&C) Islands) for outage boundary and faulted status detection
The outage detection algorithm used instantaneous voltage and current waveforms
(inputs) measured on GFM BESS low voltage or medium voltage terminals. Inverter sets
low voltage on its terminals when the system is in black-out state. Currents will start
flowing to the parts of the system (lines, transformers and loads) within the boundaries
that are electrically interconnected to GFM BESS.

                                                 Page 22 of 45

First, the algorithm will perform the imbalance check. It can be done by simply comparing
the RMS currents measured in each phase of the GFM inverter. Another way is to check
for presence of negative sequence currents in the measured current waveforms. For
negative sequence detection we have implemented an algorithm based on the second
order generalized integrator (SOGI) which is known for robust performance under
distorted waveform conditions. General diagram of SOGI algorithm is show in Figure 9.
The implementation in PSCAD is shown in Figure 10.

 Figure 9. SOGI block diagram and PSCAD block
If SOGI algorithm detects the presence of negative sequence currents above a certain
pre-determined level then it will signal fault condition in the system (unbalanced low or
high impedance fault). In such case, the black start process needs to be halted until the
fault in the system is repaired.
If no faults are present, the impedance detection control will start evaluating the
impedance of the network that inverter is connected to. First, FFT analysis is performed
on both voltage and current waveforms to extract fundamental components. Then, the
impedances (both magnitude and phase angle) for each phase are calculated based on
basic circuit equations. The output of the algorithm will include impedance magnitudes,
angles, active and reactive resistances. These values can be used to compare with
known values from power flow studies or real measurements.

The team designed the framework for a multi-step method to identify feasible restoration
paths after an outage and during recovery. Details regarding the algorithm is provided in

Subtask 4.4: Develop feasible restoration path identification algorithm
Status – IN-PROGRESS: (GEVAR)
The planned activity in this task is to develop a custom optimal power flow module to
identify feasible restoration paths after an outage. The team has worked on path
seGEVARhing for a single power source-multi critical load combination in an IEEE 123
node distribution network. Candidate path graphs were generated for single source
scenarios in Python. Preliminary simulations were performed to assess the feasibility of
generated paths, utilizing Grid-Forming Mode (GFM) storage as the power source for
each islanding system. Hourly loads profiles were tested for sequential power flow
simulation.
The framework for determining a feasible restoration path for microgrid-critical load
groups involves a multi-stage process as shown in Figure 27. The method includes three

                                       Page 33 of 45

stages to get the feasible restoration path solutions. In the first stage, potential restoration
paths are generated based on graph theory and evaluated using time-sequential power
flow simulations, considering uncertainties in generation resources and loads. Paths that
violate operational constraints are discarded, retaining only those with feasible power flow
results. The second stage involves forming load groups of critical loads that can be
restored together by a microgrid. These groups are assessed through power flow
simulations to eliminate infeasible options, resulting in a table of feasible restoration paths
detailing critical loads, required switches, and restoration reliability. The final stage
involves solving an optimization problem to identify the optimal restoration path based on
defined objectives and constraints. The framework in Figure 28 shows the process of
updating the feasible restoration path every four hours with the latest information,
ensuring consistent power supply to restored critical loads by applying constraints to
prevent changes to previously calculated paths. The feasible restoration path process
considers system information, load and generation forecasts for the next four hours to
seGEVARh for feasible paths and perform time-series power flow calculations while
detecting operational constraint violations. The output is the restored system topology,
including the status of system switches. Additionally, it can provide details on the restored
power of generation and critical loads for ACOPF dispatch calculations.

During the generation of candidate paths, it is possible to encounter identical switch status
results, even when the algorithm aims to restore different critical loads. To address this,
a repeat path reduction module was developed to optimize power flow simulations by
selecting paths that restore a greater number of critical loads. When repeated switch
statuses are detected, the module calculates the sum weight—representing the amount
of critical load within the network—using the graph network of IEEE123 system built in
Python. This calculation determines which paths should be retained. Only paths with a
higher sum weight are preserved for power flow assessment.
Given a candidate path as an input, the power flow assessment was tested, and voltage
and power of lines can be output and saved for further analysis. The storage model
operating under Grid-Forming Mode (GFM) control is used to manage the islanding power
flow. Each island within the IEEE 123-bus system is equipped with a GFM unit functioning
as a swing bus to facilitate islanding power flow simulations.

Restoration Optimizer Software Specification
The Restoration Optimizer (RO) consists of two MPC loops – inner and outer loop as
shown in Figure 30. The outer MPC loop will use the topology provided by the feasible
restoration algorithm, optimal power flow (OPF) based long term recovery plan dispatch,
distribution grid measurement feedback and disturbance forecast to compute the DER
setpoints for a 1–2-hour horizon (a receding horizon) approximately every 5 minutes. The
inner MPC loop will take these setpoints from the slower outer loop, and compute finer
setpoint variations for the DERs over a 1-minute horizon approximately every 2-5
seconds. In the network, it is envisioned that there will be multiple microgrids (MG) and
multiple dynamic zones that will be energized sequentially within each MG after an
outage. Each MG will have its local MPC that coordinates the resources/switching within
the zones.

as long as 30 minutes to run. To speed up the computation process, the full system linear
model was developed analytically by combining the analytical DER linear models and the
network linear model.

Section IV: Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions
During the project execution period from August 1, 2023, to April 30, 2025 (spending at
risk for May 1, 2024- April 30, 2025), the team completed BP1 tasks successfully, with a
portion of BP2 tasks was executed. The significant accomplishments and conclusions
achieved by the team are as follows:
 1. Established a robust technical suitability evaluation framework with incorporating
 more than 40 weighted criteria across multi-fields (infrastructure, community, demo
 requirements) and identified the islands of Vieques & Culebra as the demo site
 with evaluation on 5 candidate sites.
 2. Completed the situational awareness software specifications and definition of
 critical use case.
 3. The preliminary version of sensor prototype was completed and ready for initial
 testing.
 4. Developed the prototype of the restoration optimizer, near completion of the
 preliminary resilience metrics, substantial progress on outage boundary detection
 in EMT simulation through integration of Vieques and Culebra network models into
 NLR PHIL set-up, and meaningful advancements in restoration path identification,
 DER discovery, and restoration optimizer validation.
These achievements are significant and impactful because they directly support the
project’s core objective – demonstrating how a bottoms-up restoration approach that
leverages local DERs, enhanced situational awareness, and resilience-informed
optimization can reduce outage duration and improve equitable access to power during
extreme weather events.
Section V: Others
 A. Path Forward
The project team plans to continue exploring future funding opportunities to advance the
proposed technical approach, such as DOE laboratory calls and GE Vernova internal
R&D investments. Near-term efforts should prioritize the completion and validation of core
technical components, including the restoration optimizer, resilience metric, sensor
development, and situational awareness functionalities, along with additional integrated
testing in representative grid conditions.
Besides, the technology also presents future opportunities for transferring into resilience
and restoration applications for distribution utilities, such as pilot deployments and
potential integration into commercial-grade grid software solutions. However, immediate
commercialization is limited by some remaining technical risks, including the need for
further system validation, and proof of performance with varying grid conditions, as well
as barriers from funding, regulatory and utility adoption requirements, and interoperability
considerations.
