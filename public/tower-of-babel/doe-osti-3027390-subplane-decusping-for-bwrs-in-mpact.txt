 ABSTRACT
Control blade cusping can introduce significant error in boiling water reactor (BWR) calculations with
MPACT when blade tips fall partway within an axial method of characteristics (MOC) plane, requiring
homogenization of controlled and uncontrolled regions. This work implements subplane decusping for
BWRs in MPACT by enabling BWR-compatible subplane coarse mesh finite difference (CMFD) and
extending the existing decusping framework to represent between-assembly control blades that insert
from the bottom of the core. The method resolves axial heterogeneity on a refined subplane mesh in the
low-order solve and uses the resulting subplane flux shape to form flux-volume homogenized transport
cross sections for the partially rodded MOC plane. The capability is evaluated using a single physics
General Electric (GE)-14 assembly and a multiphysics Peach Bottom Unit 2 Type 1 assembly (PB2T1A)
with thermal hydraulic feedback. In both cases, coarse axial meshes with and without subplane decusping
are compared against fine mesh reference solutions over the full range of blade withdrawal positions.
Subplane decusping reduces maximum/average eigenvalue errors from 3,369/220 pcm to 303/36 pcm
for GE-14 and from 6,689/585 pcm to 1,582/109 pcm for PB2T1A. Additionally, it reduces pin power
root mean square errors from 5.2%/0.8% to 1.8%/0.2% for GE-14 and from 6.9%/0.6% to 2.3%/0.1%
for PB2T1A. These results demonstrate an effective, practical correction for BWR blade cusping in
VERA-MPACT.

 1. INTRODUCTION
1.1 MOTIVATION
Michigan PArallel Characteristics Transport (MPACT) (Collins et al. 2016) is the primary reactor physics
and neutron transport solver for the Virtual Environment for Reactor Applications (VERA) code (Turner
et al. 2016). MPACT utilizes the 2D/1D method to take advantage of the extruded nature of the geometry
in many nuclear reactor designs, particularly light-water reactors (LWRs) such as boiling water reactors
(BWRs) and pressurized water reactors (PWRs).
The 2D/1D method for neutron transport involves solving radial method of characteristics (MOC)
calculations in discrete axial slices of varying heights taken along the axial direction of the core. These
slices are then coupled via a lower-order nodal calculation of homogenized “pin” regions. This nodal
calculation utilizes radial transverse leakage boundary conditions for each pin computed by the 2D MOC
calculation and provides axial transverse leakage boundary conditions for each pin region for use by the
2D MOC solver. These separate but coupled solvers then iterate with each other to provide a 3D transport
solution with significantly lower memory and runtime costs than full 3D MOC methods while maintaining
similar levels of fidelity and accuracy. The primary motivation for the 2D/1D method is the observation
that reactors tend to have very smooth and gradual changes in neutron flux in the axial direction due
to their extruded designs, whereas much sharper changes occur in the radial directions as a result of
transitions between fuel, cladding, and moderator regions.
Because the 2D/1D method requires axial discretization into finite slices, slice boundaries are often
placed at material boundaries. Otherwise, axial homogenization of disparate materials would be required,
which may reduce fidelity and introduce errors. For the base reactor model, this is generally acceptable
because axial material regions tend to be long and relatively few axial material changes occur within the
reactor. Additionally, these changes are typically static during reactor operation and may only occur during
refueling between cycles.
However, control elements such as control rods in PWRs and control blades in BWRs are often moved
during reactor operation and can have tens or even hundreds of potential insertion positions within the

 1

core. It is not necessarily viable to add slice boundaries at all these potential insertion positions because
doing so would be computationally prohibitive due to the large number of slices required. It would also
introduce instability into the simulation, as 2D/1D methods often struggle with optically thin axial regions.
As a result, control elements must often be inserted only partway into one or more axial slices (as there
are often multiple insertion positions for different control banks). This partial insertion introduces errors
known as “cusping” effects, which often result in a significant underprediction of the eigenvalue.
For BWRs, this has historically not been a major issue for VERA because BWRs traditionally have
only 48 control blade steps and typically use only even steps during normal operation, resulting in just
24 potential insertion locations. As such, adding axial discretization at all 24 potential insertion locations
may not be prohibitive.
However, this assumption is beginning to reach its limits for three reasons. First, although odd insertion
levels are discouraged and rarely used in traditional BWRs, they are occasionally utilized. Second, there
has been recent interest in using VERA to simulate BWR transients. In a BWR transient, a scram often
occurs in which the control blades are continuously inserted at varying speeds until they are fully inserted.
In such situations, the limitation of control blade insertion locations to even steps is no longer applicable,
nor is the limitation to discrete step positions, since the blades may be between steps during the scram.
Third, modern and future BWR designs such as the Advanced Boiling Water Reactor (ABWR) (Final
Safety Evaluation Report: Related to the Certification of the Advanced Boiling Water Reactor Design
2020) are removing restrictions on control blade step locations, potentially moving toward fine motion
drives with continuous positioning rather than the traditional step-based paradigm.
As such, BWR control blade cusping is expected to be a significant source of error and an important
consideration for future VERA simulations of BWRs.

1.2 GOALS
Control rod cusping in PWRs has already received significant attention with respect to correction
and mitigation in VERA simulations (Graham et al. 2018). Of the decusping approaches that have
been implemented, subplane-based decusping methods have proven to be the most robust and broadly
applicable (Graham, Collins, and Downar 2017b). These methods provide a substantial reduction in
cusping-induced errors across a wide range of modeling conditions.
Previous analysis of available BWR decusping methods indicated that, initially, no decusping capability
was available in MPACT for BWR simulations. When polynomial decusping was later introduced, the
resulting improvements were relatively modest and varied significantly across different models and
problem configurations (Herring, Kim, and Jessee 2025).
Implementing subplane decusping for BWRs presents two primary challenges. First, the coarse mesh
finite difference (CMFD) subplane solve in MPACT has historically not supported BWR configurations.
As a result, several incompatibilities between the CMFD subplane implementation and the existing BWR
modeling framework in MPACT must be addressed in order to enable the subplane calculations required
for subplane-based decusping.
Second, although the overall framework for subplane decusping in MPACT is relatively general, the
specific capabilities needed to support BWR control blade geometry are currently absent. In PWRs,
control rods are located within fuel assemblies and insert from the top of the core. By contrast, BWR
control blades are located between fuel assemblies and insert from the bottom of the core. This difference
in geometry and insertion behavior requires additional modifications to the existing subplane decusping
implementation to correctly model the neutronic effects associated with BWR control blade motion.

 2

The objective of this activity was to implement and evaluate subplane decusping capabilities for BWR
simulations in MPACT. This effort included resolving the existing CMFD subplane compatibility issues
for BWR models and extending the current decusping framework to properly support between-assembly
control blade insertion. Following implementation, an initial set of analyses was performed using
representative BWR problems to assess the effectiveness of the subplane decusping approach and to
quantify the reduction in cusping-related errors achievable for BWR reactor simulations.

 2. SUBPLANE DECUSPING METHODS
2.1 SUBPLANE CMFD
Subplane CMFD in MPACT provides a mechanism for increasing axial resolution in the low-order solver
without increasing the number of radial planes solved using the higher-order MOC (Graham, Collins,
and Downar 2017a). In the standard CMFD formulation, each axial slice corresponds directly to a single
MOC plane, and each pin cell within that slice is homogenized into a single coarse mesh CMFD cell using
flux-weighted cross sections derived from the transport solution. Although this approach is efficient, it
ties the axial resolution of the CMFD system to the number of MOC planes. Increasing axial resolution
therefore requires adding more transport planes, which increases computational cost and can negatively
impact the stability of the 2D/1D iteration.
To alleviate this limitation, the subplane CMFD scheme subdivides each MOC plane—referred to as a
macroplane—into multiple smaller axial regions called subplanes for the CMFD and axial calculations.
The high-order MOC transport solve still operates on the macroplane mesh, but the CMFD equations and
axial transport solver operate on the refined subplane mesh. This allows the low-order system to represent
axial flux variations within a macroplane while maintaining a relatively small number of MOC planes.
As a result, the method captures intraplane axial heterogeneity more accurately without incurring the
computational expense associated with refining the transport mesh.
In this formulation, each homogenized pin cell within a macroplane is divided axially into a stack of
CMFD cells corresponding to the defined subplanes. Fluxes and cross sections are therefore represented
at the subplane level in the CMFD system. Radial coupling coefficients are obtained in the same manner
as in conventional CMFD, using currents at pin cell interfaces computed by the transport sweeper. Axial
coupling, however, is defined between adjacent subplanes using currents obtained from the axial solver,
allowing the CMFD equations to model leakage and flux variation across the refined axial mesh.
Because the transport solver operates on macroplanes while CMFD uses subplanes, a projection procedure
is used to exchange information between the two levels. After the CMFD solve, the resulting subplane flux
distribution is used to reconstruct the axial flux shape within each macroplane and update the fine mesh
flux or source used by the transport calculation. Conversely, fluxes and currents from the transport sweeps
are used to regenerate the homogenized quantities and coupling coefficients required by the CMFD system.
This two-level coupling preserves consistency between the high-order transport solution and the refined
low-order CMFD representation while enabling improved resolution of axial heterogeneity.

2.2 SUBPLANE DECUSPING FOR CONTROL ELEMENTS
Control element cusping is a well-known issue in the 2D/1D method when the tip of a control element
falls within an MOC plane rather than aligning with its axial boundaries. Because the MOC transport
calculation is 2D within each plane, the controlled and uncontrolled portions of the cell must be axially
homogenized for the transport solve. A simple volume homogenization of the materials preserves the
element volume but artificially smears the strong absorber across the entire plane, producing an incorrect
local flux depression and an associated error in reactivity and power distribution. This error, referred to as
control element cusping, can be eliminated by refining the axial mesh so that element tips align with plane
boundaries, but doing so significantly increases the number of MOC planes and the computational cost of
the calculation.
To address this problem without refining the transport mesh, MPACT implements a subplane decusping
method that leverages the subplane CMFD framework (Graham, Collins, and Downar 2017b). In this

 4

approach, the MOC plane containing the partially inserted control element is divided into multiple axial
subplanes for the CMFD and axial nodal calculations, while the MOC solver still treats the plane as a
single homogenized region. Instead of assigning a single homogenized material to all subplanes, the axial
heterogeneity introduced by the control element is represented explicitly: Subplanes located above the
element tip (for BWRs; below the tip for PWRs) use uncontrolled cross sections, whereas subplanes below
the tip (for BWRs; above the tip for PWRs) use controlled cross sections. This allows the CMFD and axial
calculations to resolve the sharp axial flux gradient near the element boundary rather than the smoothed
profile produced by a single homogenized region.
After the CMFD and axial calculations determine the subplane flux distribution, this information is
used to construct corrected cross sections for the MOC transport solve. Rather than performing a simple
volume homogenization of controlled and uncontrolled materials within the plane, the method performs
a flux-volume homogenization using the subplane fluxes predicted by the CMFD calculation. In this
procedure, the controlled and uncontrolled cross sections are weighted by the axial flux shape and subplane
heights when forming the effective cross sections used by the MOC calculation. This weighting reflects
the true importance of each axial region to the neutron balance and therefore compensates for the incorrect
flux shape that would otherwise result from volume averaging (Graham et al. 2018).
Through this two-level procedure, the subplane decusping method preserves the computational efficiency
of the original 2D/1D transport mesh while significantly reducing the errors caused by partially inserted
control elements. The CMFD and axial solvers capture the axial flux shape associated with the element
tip, and the resulting flux-weighted homogenization produces transport cross sections that better represent
the underlying heterogeneous configuration. Consequently, the method improves the accuracy of both
reactivity and power distributions without requiring additional MOC planes.

 3. TEST MODELS
Two different models were tested and results analyzed to demonstrate the functionality and effectiveness
of the subplane decusping. The first was a single physics General Electric (GE)-14 assembly that served
as the initial model for scoping MPACT decusping capabilities for BWRs. The second was a multiphysics
Peach Bottom Unit 2 Type 1 assembly (PB2T1A) from the first Peach Bottom Unit 2 (PB2) cycle.

3.1 SINGLE PHYSICS GE-14 ASSEMBLY
The GE-14 assembly (shown in Figure 1) is one of the controlled 3D single-bundle cases in the VERA
BWR progression problems, a benchmark suite developed to extend VERA assessment to BWR models
ranging from simple lattice calculations to more realistic bundle and mini-core configurations (Lawing,
Palmtag, and Asgari 2021). The GE-14 assembly itself is a representative, nonproprietary 10 × 10 GE
design compiled from public sources and engineering assumptions, making it a useful intermediate
benchmark for both general BWR neutronics verification and control blade modeling studies in MPACT.

 (a) GE-14 radial layout. (b) GE-14 axial part-length rods.

 Figure 1. GE-14 model.

The case is posed as a fixed-state, single-assembly BWR calculation with prescribed conditions rather
than coupled thermal hydraulic feedback. The fluid temperature is set to 547 K at 1,040 psia with no void
present, and the fuel and structural material temperatures are set to 600 K. This makes the model a useful
scoping problem for isolating neutronic cusping behavior without the additional complexity of coupled
feedback.
Radially, the model remains explicitly heterogeneous. The geometry uses the GE-14 lattice with a
15.24 cm assembly pitch, a 1.3 cm pin pitch, a channel box, two large water rods, and an original

 6

equipment manufacturer (OEM) control blade. This provides a detailed single-bundle representation while
retaining the characteristic controlled BWR geometry needed for the present study.
Axially, the model retains the full progression problem structure, including part-length rods, eight distinct
fuel lattice regions, upper and lower plug regions, top and bottom nozzles, a bottom reflector, and eight
spacer grids. Reactivity control is represented by the OEM control blade, which has 48 withdrawal steps
and therefore 49 possible blade tip positions when the fully inserted step 0 position is included.
On the solution side, the case is treated as a single physics MPACT problem without thermal hydraulic
coupling. Radial meshing is the same for all models, with four azimuthal regions for each pin, two
radial regions in each fuel region, and linear-isotropic flat-anisotropic (LIFA) (Choi et al. 2024) source
enabled. Two axial meshes are used—a fine mesh and a coarse mesh—and both capture the exact material
boundaries within the bundle.

3.2 MULTIPHYSICS PEACH BOTTOM UNIT 2 ASSEMBLY
The multiphysics PB2 assembly (shown in Figure 2) is a detailed single-bundle VERA representation of
the PB2T1A (Larsen 1978). PB2T1A is one of the 7 × 7 assembly designs used in cycle 1, and PB2 has
served as a major validation problem for VERA in both cycle depletion calculations (Choi et al. 2022)
and later turbine trip work (Herring, Salko, and Asgari 2025). The model considered here isolates one
complete fuel bundle and its associated control blade environment so that coupled bundle physics can be
examined at the assembly level with explicit geometry and feedback.

 (a) PB2 assembly radial layout. (b) PB2 assembly axial grids.

 Figure 2. PB2 assembly model.

The operating conditions are taken directly from the beginning of PB2 cycle 1. The case uses the MPACT
simplified thermal hydraulics (STH) solver to provide thermal hydraulic feedback. It is solved at 55.7%

 7

power, 973 psia pressure, 103.3% flow, 14.2 Btu/lb inlet subcooling, and 12.3% bypass. To represent a
single assembly, the rated power and flow are scaled to 4.3 MWth and 0.13 Mlb/h, respectively.
Radially, the model remains explicitly heterogeneous. The geometry uses a 15.24 cm assembly pitch
and a 7 × 7 lattice with 1.87452 cm pin pitch, wide and narrow water gaps, and a Zircaloy channel
box. The fuel loading contains two enrichments: 1.33 wt % and 0.71 wt % 235 U. Standard pins are
defined at 10.34 g/cm3 , and one special low-enrichment pin is reduced to 9.3347 g/cm3 through void
smearing. In lattice terms, this produces a Type 1 arrangement of 31 higher-enrichment pins, 17 standard
lower-enrichment pins, and one special reduced-density central pin. All pins are modeled with explicit
pellet, gap, and Zircaloy cladding regions.
Axially, the model is also described in detail. Above an 8.0163 cm lower reflector and a 5.3467 cm
lower plate, the assembly stack includes a 16.637 cm lower nozzle, a 365.76 cm active fuel region, a
40.64 cm plenum region, and a 10.1854 cm upper nozzle. Seven spacer grid locations are represented
explicitly at their individual elevations. Reactivity control is modeled with an explicit GE OEM control
blade containing 21 absorber tubes with B4 C absorber material. The blade has 48 withdrawal steps,
giving 49 possible blade positions when the fully inserted step 0 position is included. Because this is a
single-assembly problem, reflecting radial boundaries and vacuum top and bottom boundaries are used.
On the solution side, the model uses MPACT with transport-corrected P0 (TCP0) scattering, the source
expansion nodal method (P3 ) (SENMP3) (Choi and Kochunas 2022) for the axial solution, homogenized
grid treatment, and STH coupling. This choice is consistent with the broader PB2 steady-state and
depletion methodology, which relied on STH for feedback, and it contrasts with the later end-to-end Peach
Bottom turbine trip (PBTT) transient model, where VERA coupled neutronics to COBRA-TF (CTF)
to resolve subchannel behavior during the turbine trip event. The present case therefore preserves the
essential bundle geometry, the explicit blade environment, and thermal hydraulic feedback while avoiding
the much larger burden of a full-core simulation.
Two axial meshes were used: a coarse mesh and a fine mesh. Both capture all assembly material changes
exactly and therefore represent the same unsimplified PB2 assembly. Radial meshing was the same for all
models, with four azimuthal regions for each pin, two radial regions in each fuel region, and LIFA source
enabled.

 4. SIMULATION RESULTS
Both the GE-14 assembly and PB2T1A models (described in Section 3.1 and 3.2, respectively) were
simulated for three distinct models. The first was for the fine axial mesh, where all material boundaries
are captured as plane boundaries as well as all potential withdrawal step locations for a total of 76 axial
MOC planes. This model had subplane CMFD enabled to keep differences consistent, but decusping was
never used because cusping effects were entirely avoided by this fine axial mesh. The second and third
models both utilized a coarser axial mesh where every material boundary was captured but only every
4th withdrawal step location was captured by the plane boundaries for a total of 40 axial MOC planes.
The difference between the second and third models was then purely in the use of subplane decusping.
The second model utilized no decusping techniques, and the third model enabled subplane decusping
for the control blade, but both models had subplane CMFD enabled to isolate the improvement due to
decusping. These models will hereafter be occasionally referred to as “reference,” “none,” and “subplane,”
respectively.
Because the coarser axial mesh captures every 4th withdrawal step exactly, it is expected that the error
between the reference model and the coarse mesh models will be small regardless of decusping treatment
since cusping effects will be absent. Therefore, the sole source of error will be in the coarsening of the
axial mesh, which should be small with CMFD subplane enabled. It is instead on the steps in between the
4th steps that the error will be most pronounced and where the impact of decusping can be observed.

 5. CONCLUSIONS
The objective of this work was to implement and assess subplane decusping capabilities for BWR
calculations in MPACT. To achieve this objective, two related developments were required. First, the
CMFD subplane capability had to be made compatible with BWR modeling so that refined axial resolution
could be introduced in the low-order solution without increasing the number of MOC planes. Second, the
existing subplane decusping framework had to be extended to support the specific geometric and insertion
characteristics of BWR control blades, which are located between assemblies and insert from the bottom of
the core rather than from the top (as in PWR control rod systems). As discussed in Section 2, the resulting
approach represents the controlled and uncontrolled regions explicitly at the subplane level and uses the
subplane flux distribution to form flux-volume homogenized transport cross sections for the partially
rodded MOC plane.
The implemented capability was tested using two representative BWR problems. The first was a steady-state,
single physics GE-14 assembly model used as an initial scoping case. The second was a more realistic
multiphysics PB2T1A model with explicit bundle geometry and STH feedback. In both cases, a fine axial
mesh that captured all withdrawal locations served as the reference solution, and coarse mesh models with
and without subplane decusping were compared against that reference. This setup isolated the effect of
control blade cusping and allowed the impact of the new treatment to be evaluated over the full range of
blade withdrawal positions considered in the study.
For both test problems, the coarse mesh calculations without decusping exhibited the expected cusping
behavior, with the largest errors generally occurring between withdrawal locations that were exactly
represented by the coarse axial mesh. In the GE-14 case, subplane decusping reduced the maximum
eigenvalue error from 3,369 pcm to 303 pcm and the average eigenvalue error from 220 pcm to 36 pcm.
Over the same set of cases, the maximum pin power RMS decreased from 5.2% to 1.8%, and the
average pin power RMS decreased from 0.8% to 0.2%. Similar behavior was observed for the PB2T1A
multiphysics case. There, subplane decusping reduced the maximum eigenvalue error from 6,689 pcm to
1,582 pcm and the average eigenvalue error from 585 pcm to 109 pcm, while the maximum and average
pin power RMS were reduced from 6.9% to 2.3% and from 0.6% to 0.1%, respectively.
Overall, the results show that subplane decusping is an effective correction for BWR control blade cusping
in MPACT and that the required supporting subplane CMFD capability can be successfully applied to
BWR models. The method preserves the computational advantages of a coarser axial transport mesh
while recovering much of the accuracy otherwise lost when control blade tips fall within an MOC plane.
These findings support the use of subplane decusping as a practical path forward for BWR applications
in VERA, particularly for problems in which control blades occupy many possible insertion locations
and direct alignment of the transport mesh with all blade positions is not feasible. Future work should
extend the assessment to additional BWR configurations, compare directly against other available BWR
decusping approaches, and evaluate performance for transient scenarios involving continuously changing
blade position.
