A Theory on Flow Matching with Neural Networks
Yihan He, Qishuo Yin, Yuan Cao, Jianqing Fan, Han Liu

Abstract
In this work, we develop theoretical foundation for flow matching with neural-network–parameterized conditional velocity fields. We establish convergence guarantees for gradient descent in the over-parameterized 2-layered ReLU neural network regime. We derive generalization bounds for the conditional velocity-field matching objective. Building on these results, we provide Wasserstein-distance guarantees for the samples generated by the induced flow. Our analysis is based on generalization bound for multi-task representation learning with unbounded losses, which may be of independent interest beyond flow-based generative modeling. These theoretical results are validated through extensive experiments on both synthetic and real-world image benchmarks.

Introduction

Flow matching
(
Lipman et al. 2022
;
Albergo and Vanden-Eijnden 2022
)
has emerged as a state-of-the-art approach within diffusion-based generative modeling for learning and sampling high-dimensional data, including images
(
Esser et al. 2024
)
, and has been applied broadly across a range of other domains
(
Lipman et al. 2024b
)
.

Flow matching is attractive in part because it cleanly separates three design choices: (i) the
path
of intermediate distributions (e.g., linear interpolation in data space / straight-line couplings in rectified flow
(
Liu et al. 2022
)
, variance-preserving/variance-exploding diffusion schedules
(
Ho et al. 2020
;
Song et al. 2021
)
, or transport maps motivated by optimal transport
(
Lipman et al. 2023
;
Tong et al. 2024
)
), (ii) the
target velocity
associated with that path
(
Lipman et al. 2023
;
Tong et al. 2024
)
, and (iii) the
numerical solver
used at sampling time
(
Chen et al. 2018
;
Lipman et al. 2023
;
Song et al. 2021
)
. This modularity has led to a growing ecosystem of variants, including conditional flow matching constructions that leverage conditional distributions and couplings to define tractable target velocities
(
Tong et al. 2024
)
. Empirically, these methods have been applied to high-dimensional generation tasks such as images, audio, and video
(
Lipman et al. 2023
;
Lipman et al. 2024a
;
Guan et al. 2024
;
Jin et al. 2025
)
, and have also been used for conditional generation and editing, where one integrates an ODE while enforcing constraints (e.g., classifier guidance, inverse problems, or conditioning variables)
(
Dhariwal and Nichol 2021
;
Ho and Salimans 2022
;
Song et al. 2022
;
Chung et al. 2023
)
. Beyond generation, learned continuous-time transports are increasingly used as primitives for data interpolation, domain translation, and simulation of complex dynamical systems
(
Liu et al. 2022
;
Tong et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2018
)
.

Despite this empirical success, a theoretical understanding of flow matching remains incomplete. There are three intertwined sources of error that ultimately determine the quality of generated samples: (1)
optimization error
from training neural networks with nonconvex objectives using first-order methods, (2)
statistical error
from learning the velocity field from finitely many samples, and (3)
sampling error
from approximating the continuous-time dynamics with a neural network learned velocity field.

Hence, the questions that we address in this work can be summarized as follows:

1.

What is the convergence guarantee for the neural network training with gradient descent ?

2.

What is the generalization guarantee for the velocity field matching ?

3.

What is the distributional guarantee for samples generated by neural-network-based flow matching?

Contributions.

To address the three questions above, we summarize our main contributions as follows.

1.

We establish convergence guarantees for approximating high-dimensional conditional velocity fields using gradient-descent-trained two-layer neural networks. Our analysis connects the training dynamics to the neural tangent kernel (NTK) regime
(
Du et al. 2018
;
Jacot et al. 2018
)
and addresses the challenges posed by high dimensionality, including regimes where the data dimension
d
is of the same order as the sample size
n
.

2.

We derive
L_{2}
statistical error bounds for the velocity field learning problem by introducing a general high-dimensional multi-task learning generalization bound for unbounded losses. This result complements existing representation learning theory
(
Maurer 2016
;
Maurer et al. 2016
)
and may be of independent interest.

3.

We provide sampling error guarantees for flow matching in Wasserstein distance. The proof combines a geometric lemma that upgrades
L_{1}
control to
L_{\infty}
control on a compact high-dimensional subset with a refined analysis of the induced ODE dynamics. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first sampling error guarantee for gradient-descent-trained, neural-network-based flow matching algorithms.

4.

Our theoretical results are complemented by numerical experiments on both the synthetic and real-world images that validate the convergence and sampling error bounds. These experiments provide insights into the practical implications of our theoretical findings and highlight potential avenues for future research.

Organization.

The remainder of this work is organized as follows. Section
2
reviews related work and positions our contributions relative to existing literature. Section
3
introduces the main definitions and summarizes our results. Section
4
reports numerical experiments that complement the theoretical analysis. Finally, Section
5
discusses limitations and directions for future work.

Related Works

Our work is closely related to two branches of recent theoretical research on generative modeling and overparameterized neural networks. In the following, we first review recent theoretical developments for flow matching models, and then discuss the techniques most directly related to those used in this work.

Theoretical works on generative modeling algorithms.

Recent works
(
Fukumizu et al. 2024
;
Su et al. 2025a
;
Su et al. 2025b
)
study statistical convergence rates for variants of flow matching. In particular,
Fukumizu et al. 2024
shows that flow matching achieves near-optimal rates in the
p
-Wasserstein distance, comparable to diffusion models. However, their estimator is not neural-network-based, whereas neural networks are the primary object of study in our work.
Su et al. 2025a
;
Su et al. 2025b
investigate the learning error of variants of neural-network-based flow matching, but their analysis focuses on the theoretical limits of certain neural network classes and does not address achievability by a concrete optimization procedure. In contrast, the neural network solution we study is achievable via gradient descent with polynomial-time complexity.

Han et al. 2024
analyzes optimization and generalization for score matching in diffusion models using shallow neural networks. Their work does not provide distributional guarantees for samples generated from the learned score. Moreover, their two-layer network treats only the first layer weights
\{\mathbf{w}_{r}\}_{r\in[n]}
as trainable parameters, whereas our analysis allows weights from both the first and second layers (a.k.a.
\{\mathbf{a}_{r}\}_{r\in[n]}
and
\{\mathbf{w}_{r}\}_{r\in[n]}
) to be trainable. This distinction substantially complicates the analysis and renders several arguments in
Han et al. 2024
inapplicable in our setting.

Overall, much of the existing theory for flow matching is largely algorithmically agnostic, leaving a gap between statistical guarantees and practical learning procedures with polynomial-time complexity. In comparison, our analysis provides performance guarantees for gradient descent and bridges this gap by establishing algorithmic achievability.

Convergence and generalization guarantees for overparameterized neural networks.

A substantial body of work studies training dynamics and generalization in the overparameterized regime. Part of our analysis is motivated by recent advances connecting trained overparameterized neural networks to the neural tangent kernel (NTK)
(
Jacot et al. 2018
)
. In particular, a series of works establish convergence guarantees for gradient descent when training neural networks in the NTK regime
(
Li and Liang 2018
;
Du et al. 2019b
;
Allen-Zhu et al. 2019b
;
Du et al. 2019a
;
Chizat et al. 2019
;
Zou et al. 2019
;
Zou and Gu 2019
;
Lee et al. 2019
)
. Closely related studies also develop NTK-based generalization bounds for overparameterized neural networks
(
Allen-Zhu et al. 2019a
;
Arora et al. 2019a
;
Arora et al. 2019b
;
Cao and Gu 2019
;
Ji and Telgarsky 2020
;
Chen et al. 2021
)
.
Another line of work leverages mean-field techniques to analyze the training of overparameterized shallow networks
(
Mei et al. 2018
;
Chizat and Bach 2018
;
Mei et al. 2019
;
Wei et al. 2019
)
, deep networks
(
Lu et al. 2020
;
Fang et al. 2021
;
Ding et al. 2021
;
Ding et al. 2022
)
, and transformers
(
Gao et al. 2024
)
. While these studies offer valuable insights and theoretical tools for deep learning, they are largely developed for standard supervised learning settings. In comparison, our work studied the generative modeling problem.

3.1
Preliminaries, Definitions, and Assumptions

In this section we begin by introducing the problem setup and some preliminaries. Then we introduce the assumptions made in this work.

3.1.1
Problem Setup.

In the generative modeling problem, we are given a dataset
\{\mathbf{x}_{1,i}\}_{i\in[n]}
consisting of
n
data points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, assumed to be drawn from a high-dimensional distribution
\mathbb{P}_{1}
. The goal is to generate additional samples
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{1}
whose distribution is close to
\mathbb{P}_{1}
using
\{\mathbf{x}_{1,i}\}_{i\in[n]}
. We assume that the
\mathbf{x}_{1,i}
are i.i.d.

Flow Matching.

Flow matching is a state-of-the-art approach to generative modeling. It enables sampling without explicitly estimating the target distribution
\mathbb{P}_{1}
. Instead, it specifies a path of intermediate distributions that connects a tractable, easy-to-sample base distribution
\mathbb{P}_{0}
(e.g., a Gaussian) to the target data distribution
\mathbb{P}_{1}
. Concretely, one draws a collection of i.i.d. samples
\{\mathbf{x}_{0,i}\}_{i\in[n]}
from
\mathbb{P}_{0}
as source data. The objective of flow matching is to learn a mapping
\mathbf{u}
that satisfies
\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\sim\mathbb{P}_{1}
given
\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim\mathbb{P}_{0}

through interpolating the two datasets
\{\mathbf{x}_{0,i}\}_{i\in[n]}
and
\{\mathbf{x}_{1,i}\}_{i\in[n]}
as

\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}_{0,i})=\mathbf{x}_{1,i}
,
\forall i\in[n]
.

The flow matching algorithm learns a transport path between
\mathbf{x}_{0,i}
and
\mathbf{x}_{1,i}
,
i\in[n]
by constructing a conditional velocity field
\mathbf{v}_{\tau}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that, for all
\tau\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathbf{x}_{\tau}}{d\tau}=\mathbf{v}_{\tau}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau}|\mathbf{x}_{0}):=\sigma_{\tau}^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{0}+\frac{\mu_{\tau}^{\prime}}{\mu_{\tau}}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau}-\sigma_{\tau}\mathbf{x}_{0}),

(1)

where
\sigma
and
\mu
satisfying
\sigma_{0}=\mu_{1}=1
,
\sigma_{1}=\mu_{0}=0
are scalar functions with derivatives
\sigma^{\prime}
and
\mu^{\prime}
, respectively. We emphasize that the conditional velocity field
\mathbf{v}
also depends on
\mathbf{x}_{0}
and
\tau
.
Under this parametrization, the target mapping
\mathbf{u}
can be obtained by solving the ODE in (
1
) to produce
\mathbf{x}_{1}
from the initial condition
\mathbf{x}_{0}
. In practice
(
Lipman et al. 2022
;
Lipman et al. 2024a
)
, the velocity field
\mathbf{v}_{\tau}
is frequently represented by a neural network. In this work, we consider the following overparameterized two-layer neural network:

\displaystyle\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau},\tau,\mathbf{x}_{0})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{r=1}^{m}\mathbf{a}_{r}\sigma\left(\mathbf{w}_{r}^{\top}[\mathbf{x}_{\tau}^{\top},\tau,\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\top}]^{\top}\right)

where
m
denotes the width of the neural network. For
r\in[m]
, the neural network parameters
\mathbf{a}_{r}
and
\mathbf{w}_{r}
lie in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbb{R}^{2d+1}
respectively. The nonlinearity
\sigma
is taken to be the ReLU activation. We collect all network parameters into
\bm{\theta}=[\mathbf{a}_{1}^{\top},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{m}^{\top},\mathbf{w}_{1}^{\top},\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{m}^{\top}]^{\top}
.
We emphasize that this architecture is typically studied in the
overparameterized
regime, where
m
can be much larger than
n
.
To train the neural network on the samples
\{\mathbf{x}_{0,i}\}_{i\in[n]}
, we minimize the following empirical loss after drawing
\tau_{i}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathrm{Uniform}([0,1])
:

\displaystyle L(\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\|\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{i},i},\tau_{i},\mathbf{x}_{0,i})-\mathbf{v}_{\tau_{i}}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{i},i}|\mathbf{x}_{0,i})\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(2)

In this work, we consider the standard
gradient descent
algorithm as the optimization procedure, which is given by

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}^{(t+1)}=\bm{\theta}^{(t)}-\eta\cdot\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L(\bm{\theta}^{(t)}),

where
\eta
is the step size.
The network parameters
\bm{\theta}^{(0)}
are randomly initialized. In particular, we set

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{r}^{(0)}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}N(0,\kappa^{2}I_{d}),\qquad\mathbf{a}_{r}^{(0)}\overset{i.i.d.}{\sim}N(0,I_{d}).

where
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
controls the magnitude of the network parameters at initialization.
Given the gradient-descent-trained neural network model
\bm{f}_{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}}
with parameters
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}
, we generate a new random variable
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{1}
by first sampling
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\sim\mathbb{P}_{0}
and then solving the following ODE using standard numerical solvers
(
Butcher 2016
)

\displaystyle\frac{d\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{\tau}}{d\tau}=\bm{f}_{\widehat{\bm{\theta}}}\left(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{r},\tau,\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\right).

(3)

Remark 1
.

The flow matching pipeline considered in this work comprises two components: Algorithm
1
specifies the data generation procedure, and Algorithm
2
specifies the corresponding learning and sampling procedure. A key methodological choice in Algorithm
1
is that we draw the source sample
\mathbf{x}_{0}
from the conditional distribution
\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{x}_{0}\mid\mathbf{x}_{1})
, rather than from its marginal
\mathbb{P}_{0}
as is customary in standard flow matching formulations
(
Lipman et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2022
)
. This conditional coupling is adopted for theoretical reasons: it renders the population-level objective identifiable, in the sense that the target conditional velocity field
\mathbf{v}_{\tau}
is the unique minimizer of the expected loss. By contrast, the prevailing practice of sampling
\{\mathbf{x}_{0,i}\}_{i\in[n]}
independently from
\mathbb{P}_{0}
and pairing them with
\{\mathbf{x}_{1,i}\}_{i\in[n]}
via an arbitrary coupling admits a continuum of population minimizers, which precludes meaningful approximation guarantees for the learned velocity field.

Neural Tangent Kernel.

The neural tangent kernel (NTK)
(
Jacot et al. 2018
)
is a widely used framework that connects overparameterized neural networks to reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHSs). We define the following Gram matrix for
\mathbf{w}_{r}\sim N(0,\kappa^{2}I_{d})
:

\displaystyle\bm{H}^{(\infty)}_{ij}

\displaystyle:=I_{d}\mathbb{E}\Big[[\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{j}}^{\top},\tau_{j},\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\top}](\mathbf{w}_{r}\mathbf{w}_{r}^{\top}+I_{2d+1})[\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{i}}^{\top},\tau_{i},\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\top}]^{\top}

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}\{[\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{i}}^{\top},\tau_{i},\mathbf{x}_{0,j}^{\top}]\mathbf{w}_{r}>0,[\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{j}}^{\top},\tau_{j},\mathbf{x}_{0,j}^{\top}]\mathbf{w}_{r}>0\}\Big],

\displaystyle\bm{H}^{(\infty)}

\displaystyle:=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{H}^{(\infty)}_{i,j}\end{bmatrix}_{i,j\in[n]}.

We note that each block
\bm{H}^{(\infty)}_{ij}
of the Gram matrix can be decomposed as the sum of two matrices,
\bm{B}^{(\infty)}
and
\bm{C}^{(\infty)}
, where

\displaystyle\bm{B}^{(\infty)}_{ij}

\displaystyle:=I_{d}\mathbb{E}\Big[[\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{j}}^{\top},\tau_{j},\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\top}]\mathbf{w}_{r}\mathbf{w}_{r}^{\top}[\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{i}}^{\top},\tau_{i},\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\top}]^{\top}\mathbbm{1}\{[\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{i}}^{\top},\tau_{i},\mathbf{x}_{0,j}^{\top}]\mathbf{w}_{r}>0,[\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{j}}^{\top},\tau_{j},\mathbf{x}_{0,j}^{\top}]\mathbf{w}_{r}>0\}\Big],

\displaystyle\bm{C}^{(\infty)}_{ij}

\displaystyle:=I_{d}\mathbb{E}\Big[[\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{j}}^{\top},\tau_{j},\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\top}][\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{i}}^{\top},\tau_{i},\mathbf{x}_{0}^{\top}]^{\top}\mathbbm{1}\{[\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{i}}^{\top},\tau_{i},\mathbf{x}_{0,j}^{\top}]\mathbf{w}_{r}>0,[\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{j}}^{\top},\tau_{j},\mathbf{x}_{0,j}^{\top}]\mathbf{w}_{r}>0\}\Big].

We can similarly define
\bm{B}^{(\infty)}
and
\bm{C}^{(\infty)}
. The Gram matrix plays a central role in characterizing the optimization landscape of gradient descent. In particular, in the parameter-limit regime
m\to\infty
, it can be interpreted as an effective Hessian for the neural network. The matrices
\bm{B}^{(\infty)}
and
\bm{C}^{(\infty)}
correspond to the respective contributions from the trainable parameters
\mathbf{w}_{r}
and
\mathbf{a}_{r}
. We also establish the following result on the minimum eigenvalue of the Gram matrix.

Lemma 3.1

(Property of the Gram Matrix)
.

The Gram Matrix satisfies
\lambda_{\min}(\bm{H}^{(\infty)})>0
.

Remark 2
.

We note that this is a standard assumption in the NTK literature, and can be proved based on various assumptions on the training data
Jacot et al. 2018
;
Allen-Zhu et al. 2019a
;
Arora et al. 2019a
. In our problem setup, we can also establish this property rigorously.

We introduce a few assumptions considered in this work.

(Weak)

We assume that the distribution of

\mathbb{E}[\exp(\bm{t}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{1,i})]\leq\exp\left({Cd\|\bm{t}\|_{2}^{2}}/{2}\right).

We note that the above assumption provides tail guarantees for
\|\mathbf{x}_{1}\|_{2}
and will be used in the proofs of our generalization and gradient descent upper bounds. This sub-Gaussian assumption is standard in high-dimensional statistics
(
Wainwright 2019
)
. For the sampling bound, we will require a stronger condition, given by

Assumption 2

(Strong)
.

We assume that the distribution of
\mathbb{P}_{1}
has bounded support with
\|\mathbf{x}_{1}\|_{2}\leq B_{1}\sqrt{d}
for some large constant
B_{1}
.

And for the distribution of
\mathbf{x}_{1}
we need an anti-concentration bound given as follows.

Assumption 3

(Anti-Concentration)
.

We assume that the random vector
\mathbf{x}_{1}\sim\mathbb{P}_{1}
satisfies

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{x}_{1}\|_{2}}\right]\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}
and
\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{x}_{1}\|_{2}^{2}}\Big]\lesssim\frac{1}{d}
.

Intuitively, the anti-concentration bound ensures that the training samples do not collapse onto a single point as the sample size grows.

and

, this work requires the following upper bounds:

.

We assume that for all
\tau\in[0,1]
,

The above assumption ensures that the function

3.2
Main Theorems

This section presents the main results of this work. We state three theorems addressing (1) approximation guarantees for gradient descent, (2) generalization bounds for conditional velocity field learning, and (3) sampling error bounds. These results answer the three questions posed in the
Introduction
, respectively.

The Approximation Bound.

We introduce the following new notations

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{i}^{(t)}:=\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}^{(t)}}\left(\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{i}},\tau_{i},\mathbf{x}_{0,i}\right),\quad\mathbf{v}_{i}:=\mathbf{v}_{\tau_{i}}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{i}}|\mathbf{x}_{1,i}),\quad\Delta^{(t)}:=\bm{U}^{(t)}-\bm{V},

\displaystyle\bm{U}^{(t)}:=[\bm{u}_{1}^{(t),\top},\ldots,\bm{u}_{n}^{(t),\top}]^{\top},\quad\bm{V}:=[\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\top},\ldots\mathbf{v}_{n}^{\top}]^{\top}.

Then, we establish the following theorem characterizing the convergence rate of gradient descent.

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose that assumptions
1
,
3
, and
4
hold. Given
m\gtrsim\frac{n^{5}\|\bm{U}^{(0)}-\bm{V}\|_{2}^{2}}{\kappa^{2}\lambda_{\min}(\bm{H}^{(\infty)})\delta^{3}}d\log nd
,
\eta<\frac{2\lambda_{\min}(\bm{H}^{(\infty)})}{n^{2}d^{2}}
, and
\kappa=o\left(\frac{\lambda_{\min}(\bm{H}^{(\infty)})\delta}{n\sqrt{d}}\right)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, the prediction error at the
t
-th iteration satisfies

\|\Delta^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\Big(1-\frac{\eta\lambda_{\min}(\bm{H}^{(\infty)})}{2}\Big)^{t-1}\|\Delta^{(t-1)}\|_{2}^{2}

for some
C\in(0,1)
.
Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\Delta^{(t)}

\displaystyle=-(I-\eta\bm{H}^{(\infty)})^{t}\bm{V}+\bm{E}^{(t)},

\displaystyle\|\bm{E}^{(t)}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\frac{n\sqrt{d}\kappa}{\lambda_{\min}(\bm{H}^{(\infty)})\delta}+\frac{n^{3}d^{3}}{\sqrt{m}\delta}\log^{3}\frac{m}{\delta}+\frac{\eta n^{3}\log(n/\delta)}{\sqrt{m}\kappa\lambda_{\min}(\bm{H}^{(\infty)})\delta^{3/2}}\Big)

with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Remark 3
.

We establish a linear convergence rate for gradient descent. Intuitively, we show that gradient descent on a multidimensional neural network exhibits a convergence behavior comparable to that of minimizing a quadratic objective of the form
\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{H}^{(\infty)}\bm{x}
. The second result provides a more fine-grained characterization of the training dynamics by expressing the loss
\Delta^{(t)}
via an explicit matrix multiplication recursion along the optimization trajectory. A proof sketch is provided in Section
A
in the appendix.

The Generalization Bound.

Our main result on generalization guarantees for the velocity field learning loss is summarized in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose that assumptions
1
,
3
, and
4
hold.
Let
\bm{\theta}^{(t)}
being the learned parameters of the neural network with
t\geq\frac{C}{\eta\lambda_{\min}(\bm{H}^{(\infty)})}\log\frac{n}{\delta}
iterations of training. Let the choice of paramter
\kappa=O(\frac{\lambda_{0}\delta}{n})
and
m\geq\kappa^{-2}\text{poly}(n,\lambda_{\min}(\bm{H}^{(\infty)}),\delta^{-1},d)
. Let
\tau\sim\text{Uniform}([0,1])
. With probability at least
1-\delta
over the random initialization of neural network and the training set, the following holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\|\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}^{(t)}}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau},\tau,\mathbf{x}_{0})-\mathbf{x}_{\tau}(\mathbf{v}_{\tau}|\mathbf{x}_{0})\|_{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq C\sqrt{\bm{V}\bm{H}^{(\infty),-1}\bm{V}}\sqrt{\frac{(d+B_{0}^{2})\log^{2}(n/\delta)}{n}}

\displaystyle+C\sqrt{\frac{d\log(n/\lambda_{\min}(\bm{H}^{(\infty)})\delta)}{n}}.

Moreover,
\sqrt{\bm{V}\bm{H}^{(\infty),-1}\bm{V}}
is of order
B_{0}
, which implies

\mathbb{E}\Big[\|\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}^{(t)}}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau},\tau,\mathbf{x}_{0})-\mathbf{x}_{\tau}(\mathbf{v}_{\tau}|\mathbf{x}_{0})\|_{2}\Big]\leq C\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}\log(n/\delta)}
.

Remark 4
.

Our generalization bound implies that, with high probability, there exists a neural network produced by gradient descent whose generalization loss scales as
\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}\log(n/\delta)}
. This rate is analogous to the minimax rates that arise in many high-dimensional regression problems
(
Wainwright 2019
)
. We introduce the
\text{poly}(\cdot)
notation to streamline the presentation of higher-order factors.

A key technical difficulty is that our generalization error is measured in an
L_{2}
norm and the associated loss is unbounded, which falls outside the scope of many standard learning-theoretic tools developed for bounded losses. To address this issue, we develop a new Rademacher-complexity-based generalization bound tailored to unbounded loss functions. These techniques and the corresponding proof machinery are extensively discussed in Section
B
.

To facilitate the generalization bound given by Theorem
3.3
, we introduce a new generalization bound for multi-task learning with unbounded losses.

The following empirical multi-task Rademacher complexity is introduced by
Maurer et al. 2016

\displaystyle\pazocal{R}_{\mathbf{x}}(\pazocal{F})=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sup_{f\in\pazocal{F}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\epsilon_{ij}f_{j}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i})\bigg|\{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}\bigg],

where
\epsilon_{ij}
s are i.i.d. random sign. This complexity has been widely used in multi-task learning settings, where the output of the learning algorithm is multivariate. We also note that in related work
Han et al. 2024
, the authors construct a coupling between kernel-based regression and neural networks, and derive generalization bounds by leveraging existing results for kernel-based regression. In contrast, we adopt a different approach by directly upper bounding the empirical Rademacher complexity of the neural network class.

We establish the following lemma, which provides generalization bounds for unbounded losses and complements the bounded-loss results in
(
Maurer 2016
)
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose the loss function
\ell(\cdot,\cdot)
satisfies
\mathbb{E}[\sup_{\bm{f}\in\pazocal{F}}\cosh(\lambda\ell(f(\mathbf{x}),y))]\leq\exp(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2})
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. And
\ell(f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y})
is
\rho
-Lipschitz in the first argument in Euclidean norm. Then with probability at least
1-\delta
over the samples
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in\pazocal{F}}\Big\{\mathbb{E}[\ell(f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y})]

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(f(\mathbf{x}_{i}),\mathbf{y}_{i})\Big\}\leq 2\rho{\pazocal{R}}_{\mathbf{x}}(\pazocal{F})+\sigma\sqrt{\frac{2\log(1/\delta)}{n}}.

Remark 5
.

We note that the condition of
\mathbb{E}[\sup_{\bm{f}\in\pazocal{F}}\cosh(\lambda\ell(f(\mathbf{x}),y))]\leq\exp(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\lambda^{2})
entails a strong tail control for the supremum of the stochastic process
\ell(f(\mathbf{x}),y)
. A closely related condition appears in
Meir and Zhang 2003
, where the authors study the standard univariate setting. Our bound extends their analysis to the multi-task learning regime.

The Sampling Bound.

We next provide an upper bound on the Wasserstein-1 distance for the generated samples
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{1}
. We first recall the definition of the Wasserstein-1 distance.

Definition 1

(Wasserstein–1 distance)
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with finite first moments.
The
Wasserstein–1 distance
between
\mu
and
\nu
is defined as

W_{1}(\mu,\nu)\;:=\;\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\leq 1}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\mathbf{x})\,d\mu(x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\mathbf{x})\,d\nu(x)\right|
, where the supremum is taken over all real-valued functions

f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying the Lipschitz condition

|f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{y})|\leq\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|\quad\text{for all }\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

And our formal results are given as follows.

Theorem 3.5
.

Under the same condition as that in theorem
3.3
with an additional assumption
2
, we can show that with probability at least
1-\delta
over the random initialization of neural networks, the training samples and the test samples,

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{x}_{1}},\mathbb{P}_{\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{1}})\leq C(d)\Big(\frac{\log(n/\delta)}{n}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2(d+2)}}B_{1}^{-\frac{d+1}{d+2}},

where
C(d)
is a constant depending on
d
.

Remark 6
.

The theorem above implies that, provided
B_{1}=o\left(\frac{n}{\log n}\right)^{\frac{1}{2(d+1)}}
, the Wasserstein-1 error is
o(1)
. Nevertheless, consistency in the
W_{1}
metric requires the dimension
d
to remain bounded, which is also reflected in recent work such as
Fukumizu et al. 2024
;
Su et al. 2025a
. This pronounced dependence on
d
reflects the well-known
curse of dimensionality
in nonparametric distribution estimation
(
Wasserman 2006
)
. Section
C
in the appendix provides a more detailed discussion of our proof strategy, including the geometric argument that upgrades the
L_{2}
bound in Theorem
3.3
to an
L_{\infty}
bound.

5
Discussion

We discuss the limitations and a few open questions for future investigation. The first question we did not manage to answer is the sharpness of the bound given by the theorems in Section 3. To address this issue, one may need to construct a few example distributions that are hard to learn with flow matching algorithms. Another direction is that we only consider a two-layer neural network, and it is worth investigating whether deeper neural networks might exhibit different convergence behavior and distribution-learning properties. Finally, we only consider the simple gradient descent algorithm. In the real world applications, researchers normally utilize stochastic gradient descent methods or the Adam optimizer for optimizing deep neural networks. Whether these methods can yield similar results, or even provide better convergence guarantees for the learning procedure, is also worth exploring.

Appendix A
Gradient Descent Analysis

This section provides a comprehensive overview of the roadmap toward Theorem
3.2
. Our proof strategy follows
Du et al. 2018
in that we employ an induction argument based on the following condition:

Condition 1
.

Given
m\gtrsim\frac{n^{5}\|\bm{U}^{(0)}-\bm{V}\|_{2}^{2}}{\kappa^{2}\lambda_{\min}(\bm{H}^{(\infty)})\delta^{3}}d\log nd
,
\eta<\frac{2\lambda_{\min}(\bm{H}^{(\infty)})}{n^{2}d^{2}}
, and
\kappa=o\left(\frac{\lambda_{\min}(\bm{H}^{(\infty)})\delta}{n\sqrt{d}}\right)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, the prediction error at the
t-1
-th iteration satisfies

\displaystyle\|\Delta^{(t-1)}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\Big(1-\frac{\eta\lambda_{\min}(\bm{H}^{(\infty)})}{2}\Big)^{t-2}\|\Delta^{(0)}\|_{2}^{2}.

However, since the
\mathbf{a}_{r}
parameters are trainable in our setup, the induction argument becomes more intricate compared with
Du et al. 2018
. A key step, in the spirit of
Du et al. 2018
, is to introduce the following set:

\displaystyle\pazocal{A}_{i,r}(R)

\displaystyle=\{\exists\mathbf{w}_{r}:\|\mathbf{w}_{r}-\mathbf{w}_{r}^{(0)}\|_{2}\leq R,\mathbbm{1}\{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{r}^{(0)}\geq 0\}\neq\mathbbm{1}\{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{r}\geq 0\}\},

where we use the short hand notation
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}:=[\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{i},i},\tau_{i},\mathbf{x}_{0,i}^{\top}]^{\top}
.
We further define the index sets
S_{i}:=\{r\in[m]:\mathbbm{1}\{\pazocal{A}_{i,r}(R)\}=0\}
and its complement
S_{i}^{\perp}:=\{r\in[m]:r\notin S_{i}\}
. These sets facilitate the analysis of the ReLU activation, since for all
r\in S_{i}
we have

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}\{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{r}^{(t)}>0\}=\ldots=\mathbbm{1}\{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{r}^{(0)}>0\}.

Hence, the induction can be carried out more conveniently on the index set
S_{i}
. We further show that the complement set
S_{i}^{\perp}
satisfies the following properties.

Lemma A.1
.

Under assumptions
1
,
3
, and
4
, with probability at least
1-\delta
over the initialization, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{n}|S_{i}^{\perp}|\leq\frac{CmnR}{\delta}.

Building on the above preparation, we carry out the induction as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\bm{u}_{i}^{(t)}-\mathbf{v}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\|\bm{u}_{i}^{(t)}-\bm{u}_{i}^{(t-1)}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\bm{u}_{i}^{(t)}-\mathbf{v}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle+2\langle\bm{u}_{i}^{(t)}-\bm{u}_{i}^{(t-1)},\bm{u}_{i}^{(t)}-\bm{u}_{i}^{(t-1)},\bm{u}_{i}^{(t-1)}-\mathbf{v}_{i}\rangle\Big).

The difference between
\bm{u}^{(t)}
and
\bm{u}^{(t-1)}
can then be analyzed using the device of the set
S_{i}
as follows:

\displaystyle\sqrt{m}(\bm{u}_{i}^{(t)}-\bm{u}_{i}^{(t-1)})

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\in S_{i}}\Big(\mathbf{a}_{r}^{(t)}\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{r}^{(t)})-\mathbf{a}_{r}^{(t-1)}\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{r}^{(t-1)})\Big)

\displaystyle+\sum_{r\in S_{i}^{\perp}}\Big(\mathbf{a}_{r}^{(t)}\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{r}^{(t)})-\mathbf{a}_{r}^{(t-1)}\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{r}^{(t-1)})\Big).

And for the first term involving
\sum_{i\in S_{i}}
we further analyze
\mathbf{a}_{r}^{(t)}-\mathbf{a}_{r}^{(t-1)}
and
\mathbf{w}_{r}^{(t)}-\mathbf{w}_{r}^{(t-1)}
by the gradient descent formula. Note that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L(\bm{\theta}^{(t)})}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{r}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\bm{u}_{i}^{(t)}-\mathbf{v}_{i})^{\top}\mathbf{a}_{r}^{(t)}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\mathbbm{1}\{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{r}^{(t)}>0\},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L(\bm{\theta}^{(t)})}{\partial\mathbf{a}_{r}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\bm{u}_{i}^{(t)}-\mathbf{v}_{i})^{\top}\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{r}^{(t)}).

To upper bound the two terms above, we require separate bounds on
\|\bm{u}_{i}^{(t)}-\mathbf{v}_{i}\|_{2}
,
\|\mathbf{a}_{r}^{(t)}\|_{2}
, and
\|\mathbf{w}_{r}^{(t)}\|_{2}
. The first quantity is controlled by Condition
1
. The remaining two quantities can be bounded in terms of
\|\mathbf{a}_{r}^{(t)}-\mathbf{a}_{r}^{(0)}\|_{2}
and
\|\mathbf{w}_{r}^{(t)}-\mathbf{w}_{r}^{(0)}\|_{2}
via the following lemma.

Lemma A.2
.

Under condition
1
, we can show that for all
r\in[m]
,

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{a}^{(t)}_{r}-\mathbf{a}^{(0)}_{r}\|_{2}\leq C\frac{\eta\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{m}}\|\Delta^{(0)}\|_{2}\max_{i\in[n],j\in[t]}|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{w}_{r}^{(j)}|,

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{w}_{r}^{(t)}-\mathbf{w}_{r}^{(0)}\|_{2}\leq C\frac{\eta\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{m}}\|\Delta^{(0)}\|_{2}\max_{i\in[n],j\in[t]}\|\mathbf{a}_{r}^{(t)}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\|_{2}.

Using Lemma
A.2
, we can bound
\mathbf{a}_{r}^{(t)}
and
\mathbf{w}_{r}^{(t)}
, which in turn yields bounds on
\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{r}}
and
\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf{a}_{r}}
. This allows us to complete the induction for Condition
1
.

Appendix C
Sampling Analysis

This section provides a proof sketch of Theorem
3.5
and discusses some important intermediate results.
The proof for the Wasserstein-1 distance relies on an upper bound on
\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{x}_{1}-\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{1}\|_{2}]
. However, such a bound is hard to obtain because we only have
L_{1}
convergence for the error in the velocity field estimation,
\|\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}^{(t)}}-\mathbf{v}_{\tau}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau}|\mathbf{x}_{0})\|_{2}
. To upper bound the difference between two solutions to the ODE (
3
), we need a stronger result of the form

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{\tau},\tau}\|\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}^{(t)}}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau},\tau,\mathbf{x}_{0})-\mathbf{v}_{\tau}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau}|\mathbf{x}_{0})\|_{2}=o(1).

Such a result does not hold in general, as one can easily find counterexamples. To resolve this challenge, we prove the following lemma.

Lemma C.1
.

Assume that a compact domain
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is compact with nonempty interior. Assume that
X\in K
has density bounded below, with
p(X)\geq p_{\min}>0
. Assume that
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is Lipschitz in Euclidean norm with
|f(x)-f(y)|\leq L\|x-y\|_{2}
. Assume that there exists
\beta>0
such that for every
x\in K
and every
r\in(0,diam(K)]
we have
Vol(B(x,r)\cap K)\geq\beta r^{d}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\sup_{X\in K}|f(X)|\leq\left(\frac{2^{d+1}L^{d}}{\beta p_{\min}}\mathbb{E}[|f(X)|]\right)^{\frac{1}{d+1}}.

Remark 10
.

The idea behind Lemma
C.1
is that for a bounded, sufficiently well-shaped region with probability density bounded away from
0
, we can show that
L_{\infty}
convergence holds within this restricted region given
L_{1}
convergence.

The above lemma further gives rise to the following result.

Lemma C.2
.

Under the same conditions as in Theorem
3.3
, we define the compact set
K:={(\mathbf{x}_{0},\tau,\mathbf{x}_{\tau}):\|\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}\leq\zeta,\tau\in[0,1]}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\in K}\|\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle-\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau}|\mathbf{x}_{0})\|_{2}\leq C\Big(\frac{(4B_{0})^{d}}{\exp(-\zeta^{2}/2d)}\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}\log(n/\delta)}\Big)^{\frac{1}{d+1}}.

Specifically, if we let
\zeta=C\sqrt{d\log n}
for some constant
C
, and assume that
d\vee B_{0}=o(\log n)
, we can show that with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\sup_{(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{\tau},\tau)\in K}\|\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}^{(t)}}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau},\tau,\mathbf{x}_{0})-\mathbf{v}_{\tau}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau}|\mathbf{x}_{0})\|_{2}=o(\log({1}/{\delta})).

Remark 11
.

The above lemma implies that we can divide the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{2d}\times[0,1]
into two sets,
K^{c}
and
K
, and we can show that uniform convergence holds on
K
. However, the constraint on the dimension is significantly stricter here, as we can only allow
d=o(\log n)
rather than
d
being of order
o(n/\log n)
in Theorem
3.3
.

Then, we utilize a Gronwall-type inequality to bound the difference between the solutions
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}1
and
\mathbf{x}_{1}
on the set
K
. For the complement set
K^{c}
, we use Assumption
4
and the truncation step in Algorithm
2
to obtain the following upper bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{x}_{1}-\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{1}\|_{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{x}_{1}-\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{1}\|_{2}\mid\mathbf{x}_{0}\in K],\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{x}_{0}\in K)+3B_{0},\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{x}_{0}\in K^{c}).

We further utilize the fact that
W_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{1}},\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{x}_{1}})\leq\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{x}_{1}-\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{1}\|_{2}\big]
to obtain Theorem
3.5
.

And finally we show that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

Appendix F
Proof of Theorem
3.5

This section provides the complete proof of theorem
3.5
. For completeness we first prove all the lemmas in Section
C
and then move on to the final proof of theorem
3.5
.

Lemma F.1
.

Assume that a compact domain
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is compact with nonempty interior. Assume that
X\in K
has density bounded below with
p(X)\geq p_{\min}>0
. Assume that
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is Lipschitz in Euclidean norm with
|f(x)-f(y)|\leq L\|x-y\|_{2}
. Assume that there exists
\beta>0
such that for every
x\in K
and every
r\in(0,diam(K))]
we have
Vol(B(x,r)\cap K)\geq\beta r^{d}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\sup_{X\in K}|f(X)|\leq\left(\frac{2^{d+1}L^{d}}{\beta p_{\min}}\mathbb{E}[|f(X)|]\right)^{\frac{1}{d+1}}.

Proof.

Let

M:=\sup_{x\in K}|f(x)|.

Since
K
is compact and
f
is Lipschitz (hence continuous), the supremum is attained: there exists
x_{0}\in K
such that

|f(x_{0})|=M.

By the
L
-Lipschitz property, for any
x\in K
,

|f(x)|\geq|f(x_{0})|-|f(x)-f(x_{0})|\geq M-L\|x-x_{0}\|_{2}.

Define

r:=\frac{M}{2L}.

Then for all
x\in K\cap B(x_{0},r)
,

|f(x)|\geq M-Lr=\frac{M}{2}.

Using the lower bound on the density
p(x)\geq p_{\min}
and restricting the integral to this ball, we obtain

\mathbb{E}|f(X)|=\int_{K}|f(x)|p(x)\,dx\geq\int_{K\cap B(x_{0},r)}|f(x)|p(x)\,dx\geq\frac{M}{2}\,p_{\min}\,\operatorname{Vol}(K\cap B(x_{0},r)).

By the geometric condition on
K
, there exists
\beta>0
such that

\operatorname{Vol}(K\cap B(x_{0},r))\geq\beta r^{d}.

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}|f(X)|\geq\frac{M}{2}\,p_{\min}\,\beta r^{d}=\frac{M}{2}\,p_{\min}\,\beta\left(\frac{M}{2L}\right)^{d}=\frac{\beta p_{\min}}{2^{d+1}L^{d}}M^{d+1}.

Rearranging yields

M^{d+1}\leq\frac{2^{d+1}L^{d}}{\beta p_{\min}}\,\mathbb{E}|f(X)|.

Taking
(d+1)
-th roots gives

\|f\|_{\infty}\leq\left(\frac{2^{d+1}L^{d}}{\beta p_{\min}}\,\mathbb{E}|f(X)|\right)^{\frac{1}{d+1}},

which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma F.2
.

Under the same condition as in theorem
3.3
we define compact set
K:=\{(\mathbf{x}_{0},\tau,\mathbf{x}_{\tau}):\|\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}\leq\zeta,\tau\in[0,1]\}
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\in K}\|\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})-\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau}|\mathbf{x}_{0})\|_{2}\leq C\Big(\frac{(4B_{0})^{d}}{\exp(-\zeta^{2}/2d)}\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}\log(n/\delta)}\Big)^{\frac{1}{d+1}}.

Specifically, if we let
\zeta=C\sqrt{d\log n},\tau\in[0,1]\}
for some constant
C
. Assume that
d\vee B_{0}=o(\log n)
, we can show that with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\sup_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\in K}\|\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})-\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau}|\mathbf{x}_{0})\|_{2}=o\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right).

Proof.

We first consider the following region for
\mathbf{x}_{0}
,
\tau
,
\mathbf{x}_{\tau}
:

\displaystyle K=\{\mathbf{x}_{0},\tau,\mathbf{x}_{\tau}:\|\mathbf{x}_{0}\|_{2}\leq\zeta,\tau\in[0,1]\}.

Then we can show that the density of the joint distribution is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\tau)\geq\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\zeta^{2}}{2d}\right)=p_{\min}

where we note that
\mathbf{x}_{\tau}
can be obtained deterministically through solving ODE with
\mathbf{x}_{0}
and
\tau
.
And we also consider the Lipschitzness of the function
\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}
, note that similar to the analysis given by (
E
) we can show that for all
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1}=(\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{1}},\tau,\mathbf{x}_{0}^{(1)})
and
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{2}=(\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{2}},\tau,\mathbf{x}_{0}^{(2)})
we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{v}_{\tau_{2}}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{2}}|\mathbf{x}_{0}^{(1)})-\mathbf{v}_{\tau_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{1}}|\mathbf{x}_{0}^{(2)})\|_{2}\leq B_{0}\left\|[\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{1}}-\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{2}},\tau_{1}-\tau_{2},\mathbf{x}_{0}^{(1)}-\mathbf{x}_{0}^{(2)}]\right\|_{2}.

And for the Lipschitzness of neural networks, defining

\widehat{\bm{W}}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{w}_{1}(\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbf{w}_{1}^{\top}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{2})>0}-\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbf{w}_{1}^{\top}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{2})<0}),\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{r}(\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbf{w}_{r}^{\top}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{2})>0}-\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbf{w}_{r}^{\top}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{2})<0})\end{bmatrix},

then we can use similar arguments as in (

) to show that for

we can show that

\displaystyle\|\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1})-\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{2})\|

\displaystyle=\Big\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{r=1}^{m}\mathbf{a}_{r}\left(\sigma\left(\mathbf{w}_{r}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1}\right)-\sigma(\mathbf{w}_{r}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{2})\right)\Big\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\Big\|\sum_{r=1}^{m}\mathbf{a}_{r}\left|\mathbf{w}_{r}^{\top}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{2})\right|\Big\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\Big\|\sum_{r=1}^{m}\tilde{\bm{A}}\widehat{\bm{W}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{2})\Big\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\sqrt{\bm{V}\bm{H}^{(\infty),-1}\bm{V}^{\top}}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{2}\|_{2}\sqrt{\log(m/\delta)}

\displaystyle\leq CB_{0}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{2}\|_{2}.

(14)

Hence, the Lipschitz constant can be estimated by

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1})-\mathbf{v}_{\tau_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{1}}|\mathbf{x}_{0})-\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{2})+\mathbf{v}_{\tau_{2}}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau_{2}}|\mathbf{x}_{0})\right\|_{2}\leq CB_{0}\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{2}\|_{2}.

We also note that by the fact that

is a standard Isotropic Gaussian,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(K^{c})\leq C\exp\left(-\frac{\zeta^{2}}{2d}\right).

Hence we can show by the property of conditional expectation

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau},\tau,\mathbf{x}_{0})-\mathbf{v}_{\tau}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau}|\mathbf{x}_{0})\|_{2}\right]\leq\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(K)}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau},\tau,\mathbf{x}_{0})-\mathbf{v}_{\tau}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau}|\mathbf{x}_{0})\|_{2}|K\right].

Therefore, using lemma

we can show that

\displaystyle\sup_{(\mathbf{x}_{0},\tau)\in K}\|\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau},\tau,\mathbf{x}_{0})-\mathbf{v}_{\tau}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau}|\mathbf{x}_{0})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\frac{C2^{d}B_{0}^{d}}{p_{\min}}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau},\tau,\mathbf{x}_{0})-\mathbf{v}_{\tau}(\mathbf{x}_{\tau}|\mathbf{x}_{0})\|_{2}|\mathbf{x}\in K\right]\Big)^{\frac{1}{d+1}}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\frac{C(2B_{0})^{d}}{p_{\min}}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{f}_{\bm{\theta}}-\mathbf{v}_{\tau}(\mathbf{x})\|_{2}\right]\Big)^{\frac{1}{d+1}}

Hence, given that

for some constant

. Assuming that

∎

.
Note that for the approximate ODE, we have

And it is noted that

We then introduce

Hence, we conclude that with probability at least

We further can show that the
