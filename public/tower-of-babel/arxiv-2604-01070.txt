Stability, Contraction, and Controllers for Affine Systems
L. P. Wieringa, A. Padoan, F. Dörfler, J. Eising

Abstract
Recent developments in data-driven control have revived interest in the behavioral approach to systems theory, where systems are defined as sets of trajectories rather than being described by a specific model or representation. However, most available results remain confined to linear systems, limiting the applicability of recent methods to complex behaviors. Affine systems form a natural intermediate class: they arise from linearization, capture essential nonlinear effects, and retain sufficient structure for analysis and design. This paper derives necessary and sufficient conditions independent of any particular representation for three fundamental stability problems for affine behaviors: (i) converse Lyapunov theorems for contraction of input-output systems; (ii) implementability and existence of prescribed contractive references; and (iii) whether these references can be implemented with linear or affine controllers. For the latter, we show that linear controllers suffice for implementing a contractive closed-loop, and and affine controllers are needed for equilibrium placement.

For nonlinear systems in the behavioral setting, we cannot check for stability in a tractable manner. In the case of linear systems with a state-space representation, stability analysis is often done by calculating a quadratic Lyapunov function of the state. Once conditions for the analysis are in place, we are interested in the design of controllers for non-autonomous systems. In the behavioral setting, this problem is approached in terms of two related problems. On the one hand, the question of whether a given reference behavior can be

Contributions.
In this paper, we investigate stability for affine systems. Unlike linear systems, affine systems need not contain the origin, making classical stability ill-defined; contraction (see e.g.
[
20
]
,
[
21
]
) sidesteps this issue by comparing pairs of trajectories. We prove a number of fundamental results regarding stability and contraction:

1.

We prove, akin to converse Lyapunov results, that an affine system is contractive if and only if there exists a quadratic difference form as a contraction metric.

2.

We derive necessary and sufficient conditions for a given reference behavior to be implementable using a controller, and for the existence of an affine, implementable, and contractive reference behavior.

3.

We prove the fact that for the implementation of a contractive reference for an affine system, it is sufficient to consider linear controllers, and that using affine controllers allows for the placement of the resulting equilibrium.

In order to be equally suited to physics-derived affine models and data-based representations, we will work representation free, in the behavioral setting.

II
Behavioral preliminaries

In the behavioral approach, a
(dynamical) system
is not defined by a specific representation, but identified with a subset of a space of trajectories called its
behavior
. In this paper, we focus on the particular case where
\mathcal{B}\subseteq(\mathbb{R}^{q})^{\mathbb{Z}}
, that is, discrete-time systems taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{q}
. Moreover, we consider systems which are
time-invariant
, that is, if
w\in\mathcal{B}
then
\sigma^{t}w\in\mathcal{B}
for all
t\in\mathbb{Z}
. Lastly, we make the (unrestrictive) assumptions that all behaviors under consideration are nonempty and
complete
, i.e., there exists an
L\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
such that for every
w\in(\mathbb{R}^{q})^{\mathbb{Z}}
we have

\text{$w_{[t,t+L]}\in\mathcal{B}_{[t,t+L]}$ for all $t\in\mathbb{Z}$}\ \implies\ w\in\mathcal{B}.

If the integer
L
is required in context, we say that the system is
L
-complete
.

This paper focuses on the differences between linear and affine systems. For this, we will often need to refer to sets of behaviors. These are referred to as
model classes
. We focus on two model classes of time-invariant and complete behaviors, namely

•

\mathcal{L}^{q}
, the set of behaviors which are
linear
, i.e.,

w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathcal{B},\ \ \alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}\ \implies\ \alpha w_{1}+\beta w_{2}\in\mathcal{B};

•

\mathcal{A}^{q}
, the set of behaviors which are
affine
, i.e.,

w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathcal{B},\ \ \alpha\in\mathbb{R}\ \implies\ \alpha w_{1}+(1-\alpha)w_{2}\in\mathcal{B}.

Observe that
\mathcal{L}^{q}\subsetneq\mathcal{A}^{q}
, as every linear behavior is affine, but the converse does not hold in general.

Like systems in
\mathcal{L}^{q}
, any system
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{A}^{q}
can be represented in a number of ways (cf.
[
10
]
). Examples include affine state-space equations, representations in terms of measured data, or offset kernel representations, i.e.,

\mathcal{B}=\ker_{c}R(\sigma):=\left\{w\in(\mathbb{R}^{q})^{\mathbb{Z}}:R(\sigma)w=c\right\},

where
R\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}[\xi]
and
c\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. To make our results apply to each of these cases and avoid relying on a specific representation, we will work
representation-free
, that is, without relying on a representation, whenever possible.

Although linear and affine systems are closely related, there are some important differences between them that make studying the stability of affine systems notably more involved. For instance, affine systems in state-space form need not admit an equilibrium point, whereas for linear systems the origin is always an equilibrium. To study the stability of affine systems, we will thus introduce a number of notions inspired by the analysis of nonlinear systems to the behavioral setting. We say that a system
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{A}^{q}
is

•

autonomous
if for all
w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathcal{B}
,
w_{1}(t)=w_{2}(t)
for
t\leqslant 0
implies
w_{1}=w_{2}
;

•

contractive
if
\lim_{t\to\infty}(w_{1}(t)-w_{2}(t))=0
for all
w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathcal{B}
;

•

offset stable
if there exists a constant trajectory
\overline{w}\in\mathcal{B}
and
\lim_{t\to\infty}w(t)=\overline{w}
for all
w\in\mathcal{B}
.

Intuitively, a system is contractive if any two trajectories become indistinguishable, regardless of initial conditions.

Note that offset stable behaviors admit a
unique
constant trajectory. We may therefore say that an offset stable behavior
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{A}^{q}
with constant trajectory
\overline{w}\in\mathcal{B}
is
\overline{w}
-stable
. In this notation,
0
-stability coincides with the definition of stability for linear behaviors used in e.g.
[
11
,
12
]
.

We will revisit the following, simplest nontrivial example illustrating the results throughout this paper.

Example II.1
.

Consider the system
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}}
consisting of all scalar trajectories
w:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
such that for all
t\in\mathbb{Z}
,

10w(t+2)+18w(t+1)+9w(t)=20.

It is straightforward to see that
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{A}^{1}
and that
\mathcal{B}
is autonomous. By inspecting a number of trajectories of the system, we can see that each converges to the same constant trajectory. Indeed, rewriting the system centered at this fixed point, and deducing a linear state-space representation, we can verify offset stability using standard techniques.
\Box

However, the methodology of this example has a few downsides: it requires the existence and determination of the constant trajectory and it depends on a specific, non-unique, state realization. These are not immediate in more complex cases, illustrating the limitations of representation-dependent approaches. As such, we address the following problem:

1.

Given an autonomous system
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{A}^{q}
, provide representation-free necessary and sufficient tests to guarantee offset stability and contractivity.

Having examined autonomous affine systems, we then study control for
non-autonomous
affine systems. Such a system
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{A}^{q+k}
consists of trajectories which can be partitioned into
q
-dimensional
to-be-controlled
variables and
k
-dimensional
control variables
. In this setting, we investigate our last three problems:

2.

Given a system
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{A}^{q+k}
and an affine reference
\mathcal{R}\in\mathcal{A}^{q}
, provide tests for whether we can realize
\mathcal{R}
by interconnecting
\mathcal{B}
with an affine controller
\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{A}^{k}
.

3.

Given a system
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{A}^{q+k}
, provide necessary and sufficient conditions under which a contractive, implementable reference exists.

4.

Given a system
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{A}^{q+k}
, provide necessary and sufficient conditions under which the above can be achieved with a linear or affine controller.

We address these problems sequentially: the solution to problem 1) informs the tools needed for problems 3) and 4), which are in turn based on problem 2).

III
Stability theory for affine behaviors

In this section we study autonomous affine behaviors with the aim of resolving problem 1). A crucial insight enabling our analysis is the following result from
[
10
]
, which states that every affine system can be decomposed as the sum of a linear system and an
offset
trajectory.

Lemma III.1

(
[
10
, Theorem 3]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{A}^{q}
. Then

(i)

\dif(\mathcal{B})\in\mathcal{L}^{q}
, where
\dif(\mathcal{B}):=\mathcal{B}-\mathcal{B}
;

(ii)

\mathcal{B}=w_{0}+\dif(\mathcal{B})
for all
w_{0}\in\mathcal{B}
.

We refer to
\dif(\mathcal{B})
as the
difference behavior (of
\mathcal{B}
)
. Our first main result characterizes contractivity of affine systems on the basis of the difference behavior.

Lemma III.2
.

For
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{A}^{q}
, the following are equivalent:

(i)

\mathcal{B}
is contractive;

(ii)

\mathcal{B}
is offset stable;

(iii)

\dif(\mathcal{B})
is
0
-stable.

The proof of Lemma
III.2
hinges on proving that (i) implies (ii), that is, showing that for a contractive system all trajectories converge to a certain limit, and moreover that this limit must be a constant trajectory. The full details of the proof, as well as all other proofs for results in this paper, can be found in the Appendix.

Lemma
III.2
provides a number of avenues towards a test for these properties. Intuitively, testing for contractivity requires us to perform a test for all
pairs of trajectories
of
\mathcal{B}
, whereas testing for offset stability requires us to find a constant trajectory and then perform a test for each trajectory. Lastly, item (iii) in Lemma
III.2
allows us to convert results about the
0
-stability of linear behaviors into results about the contractivity of affine behaviors through the difference behavior. Often, when models are derived directly from data, determining the difference behavior can be numerically unstable (cf.
[
22
]
). However, given an offset kernel representation or a state-space realization, this yields:

Corollary III.3

(Consequences for representations)
.

Let
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{A}^{q}
. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\mathcal{B}
is contractive;

(ii)

\mathcal{B}
admits an offset kernel representation
\mathcal{B}=\ker_{c}R(\sigma)
where
c\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
and
R\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}[\xi]
is Schur, that is, the roots of
\det R
lie in the unit disk;

(iii)

\mathcal{B}
admits a state-space representation
1
1

1

That is,
\mathcal{B}
is the collection of all
y\in(\mathbb{R}^{q})^{\mathbb{Z}}
such that there is an
x\in(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\mathbb{Z}}
such that

x(t+1)=Ax(t)+E
and
y(t)=Cx(t)+F
for all
t\in\mathbb{Z}
, cf.
[
10
]
.
with matrices
(A,C,E,F)
where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is Schur, that is, the eigenvalues of

Note that every
minimal
offset kernel or state-space representation of a contractive system is guaranteed to have the property described in (ii) and (iii) respectively.

Example III.4

(ctd.)
.

Consider the system from Example
II.1
. Given that
\mathcal{B}
is already in the form of an offset kernel representation, we can prove that the system is contractive using Corollary

is Schur and hence the system is contractive. By Lemma

, it is therefore also offset stable. Moreover, the proof of Lemma

elucidates how to find the unique constant trajectory contained in

. Indeed, we see that
\overline{w}:=\frac{20}{37}\in\mathcal{B}
, and that

is
\overline{w}
-stable.
\Box

III-A

Quadratic difference forms

It is well-known that a linear system in state-space form is asymptotically stable if and only if it admits a quadratic Lyapunov function. In this subsection, we review the analogue of this result for linear behaviors, and then show how it generalizes to affine behaviors. To do so, we need the notion of a
quadratic difference form (QDF)
. For convenience, we denote by
\mathbb{S}^{k}
the set of real symmetric matrices of size
k
.

Let
\Phi\in\mathbb{S}^{q(L+1)}
for some
L\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. The QDF (induced by
\Phi
) is defined as

Q_{\Phi}(w)(t):=w_{[t,t+L]}^{\top}\Phi w_{[t,t+L]},

where
w\in(\mathbb{R}^{q})^{\mathbb{Z}}
. Note that the QDF
Q_{\Phi}
is actually not uniquely determined by
\Phi
. By adding zero blocks to
\Phi
, we can obtain a larger matrix which leads to the same QDF. We thus define the
degree
of
Q_{\Phi}
as the smallest
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
such that there exists a
\overline{\Phi}\in\mathbb{S}^{q(\ell+1)}
for which
Q_{\Phi}=Q_{\overline{\Phi}}
. This means that a QDF of degree
L
can be represented by a matrix in
\mathbb{S}^{q(L+1)}
, and that if
\Phi\in\mathbb{S}^{q(L+1)}
is given, then
Q_{\Phi}
has degree at most
L
. Finally, we define the
increment
of
Q_{\Phi}
by

\nabla Q_{\Phi}(w)(t):=Q_{\Phi}(w)(t+1)-Q_{\Phi}(w)(t),

where, as usual,
w\in(\mathbb{R}^{q})^{\mathbb{Z}}
. Note that
\nabla Q_{\Phi}
is also a QDF: indeed, by taking

\nabla\Phi:=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
I_{q(L+1)}\end{bmatrix}\Phi\begin{bmatrix}0\\
I_{q(L+1)}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}-\begin{bmatrix}I_{q(L+1)}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\Phi\begin{bmatrix}I_{q(L+1)}\\
0\end{bmatrix}^{\top}

we have
\nabla Q_{\Phi}=Q_{\nabla\Phi}
. From this we see that if
Q_{\Phi}
has degree
L
, then
\nabla Q_{\Phi}
has degree
L+1
.

The following result characterizes
0
-stability of a behavior
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{L}^{q}
in terms of the existence of a QDF satisfying certain (non)negativity conditions.

Theorem III.5

(
[
12
, Theorem 1]
)
.

For
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{L}^{q}
, the following are equivalent:

(i)

\mathcal{B}
is
0
-stable;

(ii)

There exists
L\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and
\Phi\in\mathbb{S}^{q(L+1)}
such that for all
w\in\mathcal{B}
and
t\in\mathbb{Z}
,

Q_{\Phi}(w)(t)\geqslant 0,\qquad\nabla Q_{\Phi}(w)(t)\leqslant 0,

and
\nabla Q_{\Phi}(w)=0
if and only if
w=0
.

Given the clear links to Lyapunov functions for linear systems in state-space representation, we refer to a QDF
Q_{\Phi}
which satisfies the conditions in (ii) as a
Lyapunov function (for
\mathcal{B}
)
. In order to make it possible to test for this condition, we note that for
L
-complete systems, the degree of a Lyapunov function can be bounded by
L-1

[
11
, Proposition 4.9]
. This and Lemma
III.2
lead to the following consequence for affine behaviors.

Lemma III.6
.

Let
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{A}^{q}
be
L
-complete. Then the following are equivalent.

(i)

\mathcal{B}
is contractive;

(ii)

there exists a positive semidefinite
\Phi\in\mathbb{S}^{qL}
such that for all
w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathcal{B}
and
t\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\nabla Q_{\Phi}(w_{1}-w_{2})(t)\leqslant 0\quad\textrm{and}

\nabla Q_{\Phi}(w_{1}-w_{2})=0\ \iff w_{1}=w_{2};

(iii)

there exists
\Psi\in\mathbb{S}^{q(L+1)+1}
such that
\Psi
can be partitioned with
\Psi_{11}=\nabla\Phi
for some positive semidefinite
\Phi\in\mathbb{S}^{qL}
and

\begin{bmatrix}w_{[t,t+L]}\\
1\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\Psi\begin{bmatrix}w_{[t,t+L]}\\
1\end{bmatrix}\leqslant 0,

for all
w\in\mathcal{B}
and
t\in\mathbb{Z}
, and there is precisely one trajectory
\overline{w}\in\mathcal{B}
for which equality holds for all
t\in\mathbb{Z}
;

(iv)

there exists a positive semidefinite
\Phi\in\mathbb{S}^{qL}
such that
Q_{\Phi}
is a Lyapunov function for
\dif(\mathcal{B})
.

Analogously to the definition of Lyapunov functions, we refer to
Q_{\Phi}
as in Corollary
III.6
.(ii) as a
contraction form
.

Remark III.7

(Computational issues)
.

If we have access to a kernel representation of
\dif(\mathcal{B})
, we can test for the condition (iii) using
linear matrix inequalities (LMIs)

[
23
]
. In the absence of a representation for
\dif(\mathcal{B})
and/or knowledge of the resulting equilibrium, testing for either of the other conditions can prove more convenient. In general, all conditions in Corollary
III.6
are finite-dimensional and convex, allowing efficient solutions.

Example III.8

(ctd.)
.

Consider the system from Example
II.1
. To test for contractivity, a standard approach is to attempt to find a
contraction metric
, i.e., a norm
\|\cdot\|
and scalar
0\leqslant\gamma<1
such that for all trajectories
w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathcal{B}
and all
t\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\|w_{1}(t+1)-w_{2}(t+1)\|\leqslant\gamma\|w_{1}(t)-w_{2}(t)\|.

Even though the system
\mathcal{B}
was shown to be contractive, it can be shown that for
any
norm and
0\leqslant\gamma<1
, there is a trajectory of
\mathcal{B}
violating the above condition. In contrast, Corollary
III.6
shows that there must exist a
contraction form
. Routine calculation reveals that

\Phi=\begin{bmatrix}9&9\\
9&10\end{bmatrix}

indeed induces a contraction form.
\Box

This demonstrates a fundamental advantage of the QDF-based approach: it can certify contraction in cases where no norm-based metric exists.

IV
Implementability

Having established tools for analyzing autonomous affine behaviors, we now turn to the design question: given a non-autonomous system with control variables, can we shape its behavior to achieve desirable properties such as contraction? To investigate this in further detail, we first need to formalize the notions of ‘interconnection’ and ‘control’ in the behavioral framework. Let
q,k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{A}^{q+k}
. The trajectories in
\mathcal{B}
can be thought of as pairs
(w,c)
where
w\in(\mathbb{R}^{q})^{\mathbb{Z}}
and
c\in(\mathbb{R}^{k})^{\mathbb{Z}}
. In this interpretation,
w
represents the
to-be-controlled variables
, whereas
c
represents the
control variables
. For instance, if
\mathcal{B}
is given in state-space form,
w
could denote the state variables, whereas
c
contains its input and output.

Consider a system
\mathcal{C}\subseteq(\mathbb{R}^{k})^{\mathbb{Z}}
. To interconnect
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{C}
, we define

\mathcal{B}\Join\mathcal{C}:=\{(w,c)\in\mathcal{B}:c\in\mathcal{C}\},

which represents the subbehavior of
\mathcal{B}
where the control variables are constrained by
\mathcal{C}
. Hence, we refer to
\mathcal{C}
as a
controller
. The
interconnection of
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{C}
is then defined as the projection of this subbehavior onto the to-be-controlled variables, i.e.

\mathcal{B}\lVert\mathcal{C}:=\{w\in(\mathbb{R}^{q})^{\mathbb{Z}}:(w,c)\in\mathcal{B}\Join\mathcal{C}\},

or equivalently,
\mathcal{B}\lVert\mathcal{C}=\pi_{w}(\mathcal{B}\Join\mathcal{C})
, where
\pi_{w}
is the projection onto
(\mathbb{R}^{q})^{\mathbb{Z}}
. In the case where
\mathcal{C}=(\mathbb{R}^{k})^{\mathbb{Z}}
, the controller imposes no restrictions on the trajectories of
\mathcal{B}
, and we see that
\mathcal{B}\lVert\mathcal{C}=\pi_{w}(\mathcal{B})
. Finally, when
\mathcal{C}=\{c_{0}\}
, we denote
\mathcal{B}\lVert c_{0}:=\mathcal{B}\lVert\{c_{0}\}
.

In some particular cases, all variables of a system are available for control (e.g. when stabilizing a system via state feedback). This corresponds to the case where the trajectories of
\mathcal{B}
have no to-be-controlled variables or, equivalently,
q=0
. To avoid minor technicalities and ensure that our definitions above are consistent with this context, we identify
\mathcal{B}\Join\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{B}\lVert\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{B}\cap\mathcal{C}
and
\pi_{w}(\mathcal{B})=\mathcal{B}
in this case. We refer to this special case as
full interconnection
, and refer to the case
q>0
as
partial interconnection
.

Next, we consider the notion of
implementability
. Let
\mathcal{M}
be a model class (i.e., a collection of behaviors) and let
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{A}^{q+k}
. We say that a reference behavior
\mathcal{R}\in\mathcal{A}^{q}
is
\mathcal{M}
-implementable
if there exists a
\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{M}
such that
\mathcal{B}\lVert\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{R}
. In this case, we say that
\mathcal{C}
implements
\mathcal{R}
. If useful for further clarification, we say that
\mathcal{R}
is
(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{B})
-implementable
. Our next result is a direct generalization of
[
18
, Proposition 1]
to the affine case, and provides necessary and sufficient conditions for
\mathcal{A}^{k}
-implementability of an affine behavior.

Theorem IV.1

(Affine implementability conditions)
.

Let
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{A}^{q+k}
and
\mathcal{R}\in\mathcal{A}^{q}
. Fix any
w_{0}\in\mathcal{R}
. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\mathcal{R}
is
\mathcal{A}^{k}
-implementable;

(ii)

\mathcal{R}\subseteq\pi_{w}(\mathcal{B})
and for some
c_{0}\in(\mathbb{R}^{k})^{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
(w_{0},c_{0})\in\mathcal{B}
, we have
\mathcal{B}\lVert c_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
.

(iii)

w_{0}+\dif(\mathcal{B})\lVert 0\subseteq\mathcal{R}\subseteq\pi_{w}(\mathcal{B})
.

In fact, it can be shown that if condition (ii) holds for some
c_{0}\in(\mathbb{R}^{k})^{\mathbb{Z}}
, it holds for any
c_{0}\in(\mathbb{R}^{k})^{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
(w_{0},c_{0})\in\mathcal{B}
.

Remark IV.2
.

Using Theorem
IV.1
to test for implementability involves checking twice whether all trajectories of an affine system are also trajectories of another affine system. For kernel representations this can be resolved using polynomial algebra. In the case where systems are given in a data-based representation, we can use linear algebra (cf.
[
9
, Corollary 1]
for the linear case).

V
Existence of offset stable references

Often, we are not interested in implementing a specific reference behavior, but rather in achieving a more general control objective. For instance, given
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{L}^{q+k}
, we may want to find a controller
\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{L}^{k}
such that the resulting interconnection
\mathcal{B}\lVert\mathcal{C}
is
0
-stable. In the language of implementability, this means we want to find a
0
-stable,
(\mathcal{L}^{k},\mathcal{B})
-implementable reference
\mathcal{R}\in\mathcal{L}^{q}
. In the full interconnection case, this particular problem is trivial.

Example V.1
.

We consider a linear behavior in state-space form to illustrate this issue. Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, and consider the behavior
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{L}^{n+m}
consisting of all state-input pairs
(x,u)
satisfying

x(t+1)=Ax(t)+Bu(t)

for all
t\in\mathbb{Z}
. In the classical problem of stabilization by state feedback, we have access to both the state and the input as control variables. Note that
\mathcal{R}=\{0\}
is then a
0
-stable,
(\mathcal{L}^{n+m},\mathcal{B})
-implementable reference. Indeed, we can simply take
\mathcal{C}=\{0\}
as the controller.
\Box

This may seem rather strange at first glance, as it gives the impression that
every
system
\mathcal{B}
of the form given in Example
V.1
is ‘stabilizable’ in some sense. This is however not the case. To see why, we introduce the notion of
regular
implementability.

V-A

Regular implementability

We first define what we mean by a
regular
interconnection. The intuitive idea is that an interconnection between a system and controller is
regular
if the controller does not over-constrain the to-be-controlled system by providing it with too many input signals. To make this more formal, we need to discuss the notion of inputs and outputs from a behavioral point of view.

Let
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{A}^{q}
and let
m\in[0,q]
. Let
p:=q-m
, and decompose
\mathbb{R}^{q}
as
\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Let
\pi_{u}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be the projection map. We say that
(\mathbb{R}^{m})^{\mathbb{Z}}
is
free in
\mathcal{B}
if there exists a permutation matrix
\Pi\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}
such that
\pi_{u}(\Pi\mathcal{B})=(\mathbb{R}^{m})^{\mathbb{Z}}
.
2
2

2

Here, we interpret
\Pi
and
\pi_{u}
as maps
(\mathbb{R}^{q})^{\mathbb{Z}}\to(\mathbb{R}^{q})^{\mathbb{Z}}
and
(\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{p})^{\mathbb{Z}}\to(\mathbb{R}^{m})^{\mathbb{Z}}
respectively.
We denote

\displaystyle\bm{m}(\mathcal{B})

\displaystyle:=\max\{m\in[0,q]:\text{$(\mathbb{R}^{m})^{\mathbb{Z}}$ is free in $\mathcal{B}$}\},

\displaystyle\bm{p}(\mathcal{B})

\displaystyle:=q-\bm{m}(\mathcal{B}),

and refer to
\bm{m}(\mathcal{B})
and
\bm{p}(\mathcal{B})
as the
input
and
output cardinality of
\mathcal{B}
respectively. An important fact is that
\bm{m}(\mathcal{B})=\bm{m}(\dif(\mathcal{B}))
and
\bm{p}(\mathcal{B})=\bm{p}(\dif(\mathcal{B}))
, see
[
10
, p. 13]
.

Now, given
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{A}^{q+k}
and
\mathcal{C}\subseteq(\mathbb{R}^{k})^{\mathbb{Z}}
, we say that the interconnection of
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{C}
is
regular
if
\mathcal{B}\Join\mathcal{C}
is nonempty and

\bm{m}(\mathcal{B}\Join\mathcal{C})=\bm{m}(\mathcal{B})-\bm{p}(\mathcal{C}),

or put less formally, the number of inputs of the interconnected system has to equal the original number of inputs minus the number of outputs supplied by the controller. The terminology for regular implementability is analogous to that of implementability, interjecting the word ‘regular(ly)’ where needed.

Notice that in Example
V.1
we have
\bm{p}(\mathcal{C})=n+m
, whereas
\bm{m}(\mathcal{B})=m
. In less mathematical terms, the controller
\mathcal{C}
supplies more outputs than the to-be-controlled system has inputs. To make the stabilization problem well-posed, we therefore restrict our attention to
regularly
implementable references.

Remark V.2
.

Given
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{A}^{q+k}
and
\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{A}^{k}
, one can show that the interconnection of
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{C}
is regular if and only if the interconnection of
\dif(\mathcal{B})
and
\dif(\mathcal{C})
is regular. This allows for the use of the linear conditions (cf.
[
19
]
) in testing the regularity of an affine interconnection.

V-B

Sufficiency of linear and affine controllers

Another well-known result about linear systems in state-space form is that a system can be stabilized by linear dynamic output feedback if and only if it is detectable and stabilizable. There is an analogous result in the linear case for behaviors (cf.
[
19
, Theorem 6]
), which we now generalize to the affine setting, resolving problem 3). To do so, we first need a few definitions.

Let
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{A}^{q}
. Given a constant trajectory
\overline{w}\in(\mathbb{R}^{q})^{\mathbb{Z}}
, we say that
\mathcal{B}
is
\overline{w}
-stabilizable
if for every
w\in\mathcal{B}
, there is a
\tilde{w}\in\mathcal{B}
such that
w(t)=\tilde{w}(t)
for
t\leqslant 0
and
\lim_{t\to\infty}\tilde{w}(t)=\overline{w}
. We say
\mathcal{B}
is
offset stabilizable
if there exists a constant trajectory
\overline{w}\in\mathcal{B}
such that
\mathcal{B}
is
\overline{w}
-stabilizable.

Similarly to Lemma
III.2
, we can now make an observation:

Lemma V.3
.

Let
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{A}^{q}
. Then
\mathcal{B}
is offset stabilizable if and only if
\dif(\mathcal{B})
is
0
-stabilizable.

Finally, for
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{A}^{q+k}
, we say that
\mathcal{B}
is
detectable
if

(w_{1},c),(w_{2},c)\in\mathcal{B}\ \ \implies\ \ \lim_{t\to\infty}(w_{1}(t)-w_{2}(t))=0.

That is, if two trajectories of
\mathcal{B}
have the same values in the control variables, then the trajectories contract. Detectability of an affine system turns out to be equivalent to the existence of an offset stable, implementable affine reference.

Lemma V.4

(Equivalent conditions for detectability)
.

For
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{A}^{q+k}
, the following are equivalent:

(i)

\mathcal{B}
is detectable;

(ii)

\dif(\mathcal{B})
is detectable;

(iii)

there exists an offset stable
\mathcal{A}^{k}
-implementable
\mathcal{R}\in\mathcal{A}^{q}
.

Note that this reference need not be
regularly
implementable however. As illustrated in Example
V.1
, this may yield trivial results. In order to obtain a contractive,
regularly
implementable affine reference, we need an additional stabilizability condition. Before presenting the main result of this section, we provide representation-based tests for detectability and offset stabilizability.

Lemma V.5
.

Let
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{A}^{q+k}
. The following are equivalent:

(i)

\mathcal{B}
is detectable and
\pi_{w}(\mathcal{B})
is offset stabilizable;

(ii)

\mathcal{B}
admits an offset kernel representation of the form

\mathcal{B}=\ker_{\footnotesize\begin{bmatrix}\eta_{1}\\
\eta_{2}\end{bmatrix}}\begin{bmatrix}R_{11}(\sigma)&R_{12}(\sigma)\\
R_{21}(\sigma)&0\end{bmatrix}

where
\rank R_{12}(\lambda)=k
and
\rank R_{21}(\lambda)
is constant for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
with
|\lambda|\geqslant 1
;

(iii)

\mathcal{B}
admits a state-space representation with matrices
(A,B,C,D,E,F)
where
(C,A)
is detectable and
(A,B)
is stabilizable.

With these conditions in place, we can state the main result of this section.

Theorem V.6
.

For
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{A}^{q+k}
, the following are equivalent:

(i)

\mathcal{B}
is detectable and
\pi_{w}(\mathcal{B})
is offset stabilizable;

(ii)

there exists a regularly
\mathcal{L}^{k}
-implementable, offset stable reference
\mathcal{R}\in\mathcal{A}^{q}
.

Moreover, if (i) holds and
(\overline{w},\overline{c})\in\mathcal{B}
is a constant trajectory, then there exists a regularly
\mathcal{A}^{k}
-implementable,
\overline{w}
-stable
\mathcal{R}\in\mathcal{A}^{q}
.

An interesting detail of Theorem
V.6
can be found in (
ii
): if
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{A}^{q+k}
is detectable and
\pi_{w}(\mathcal{B})
is offset stabilizable, we can always make the system offset stable (or equivalently, contractive (cf. Lemma
III.2
)) using a
linear
controller. On the other hand, using affine controllers allows us to choose the equilibrium to which resulting closed-loop system converges.

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we investigated stability, contraction, stabilizability, and controllers for affine systems. To be precise, we investigated foundational, representation-free existence results. After defining contraction and offset stability for autonomous affine systems, we showed their equivalence, giving rise to tests in terms of the difference behavior. After this, we generalized the use of quadratic difference forms from their use in stability analysis of linear systems towards playing a role as quadratic contraction forms. Moving our attention to control, we provided implementability conditions for the affine case, and derived a characterization for the existence of a regularly implementable offset stable reference. Interestingly, for this linear controllers are sufficient, and we require affine controllers only to place the fixed point of the closed-loop system. Our results show that the behavioral framework is a natural home for stability analysis of affine systems, with the difference behavior serving as the bridge to linear theory.
