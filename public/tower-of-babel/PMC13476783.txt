Effect of Adjuvant Melatonin in Preventing Preeclampsia in Women with Moderate to High-Risk Factor: A Randomised Clinical Trial
Authors: Mojgan Alishahi, Marzieh Talebian, Masoumeh Abedzadeh-Kalahroudi, Mojtaba Sehat, Javad Amini Mahabadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Preeclampsia is a serious disorder characterised by elevated blood pressure and damage to various maternal organs; it is recognised as one of the leading causes of maternal and foetal mortality. This study aims to evaluate the adjunctive effect of melatonin in preventing preeclampsia in women with moderate to high-risk for this disorder.Materials and Methods:This randomised, triple-blind, controlled clinical trial enrolled pregnant women with moderate to high-risk factor for preeclampsia who visited the clinics at Kashan University of Medical Sciences, Kashan, Iran. Convenience sampling and randomised block allocation provided a sample size of 70 participants per group. The intervention group received 80 mg of aspirin along with 10 mg of melatonin, while the control group received aspirin with a placebo. Data on demographic, obstetric characteristics, and patient outcomes were recorded in relevant checklists. The data were analysed using SPSS version 17.Results: There was no significant difference between the intervention and control groups in mean systolic and diastolic blood pressures at admission, hospitalisation, or delivery (P>0.05). Aspirin plus melatonin compared to aspirin plus placebo did not result in a statistically significant difference in delivery outcomes (P=0.453). The incidence of preeclampsia also demonstrated no significant difference between the two groups (P=0.357). Additionally, the mean gestational age was not significantly different between the groups (P=0.572). Regarding neonatal outcomes, no significant difference was observed in Apgar scores at one and five minutes (P>0.05). Similarly, there was no significant difference in mean head circumference, length, and birth weight of the newborns between the groups (P>0.05).Conclusion:Although melatonin has shown promising results in laboratory studies, these effects were not significantly observed in pregnant women. Further studies are needed to confirm the effects of melatonin in preventing preeclampsia (registration number: IRCT20230930059563N1).

Introduction

Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy encompass a spectrum of conditions, including chronic hypertension, gestational hypertension, preeclampsia, and chronic hypertension complicated by superimposed preeclampsia (1, 2). Among these, preeclampsia represents a multisystem disorder unique to pregnancy, characterised by abnormal placental perfusion and the subsequent release of soluble factors into the maternal circulation (3). These circulating factors induce endothelial dysfunction, systemic hypertension, and progressive damage to maternal organs (4). Preeclampsia is particularly prevalent among primigravid women and remains one of the leading obstetric complications, affecting approximately 3% of pregnancies, while hypertensive disorders as a whole occur in about 5-10% of all gestations (5, 6). Its pathophysiology is largely driven by endothelial injury and platelet activation, accompanied by heightened inflammation, oxidative stress, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and defective angiogenesis (7, 8). The clinical manifestations of preeclampsia vary according to the severity of the endothelial and organ involvement (9). Major risk factors include maternal comorbidities such as chronic kidney disease, hypertension, obesity and autoimmune disorders, as well as nulliparity, multiple gestations, a family or personal history of preeclampsia, and intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) (10, 11).

Preeclampsia is clinically categorised based on the timing of its onset, as early-onset (before 34 weeks of gestation) or late-onset (after 34 weeks), and by its severity, as severe or non-severe. Traditionally, the diagnosis has relied on the concurrent presence of new-onset hypertension and proteinuria after 20 weeks of gestation. However, contemporary definitions have broadened the diagnostic criteria to include evidence of maternal organ dysfunction, such as renal insufficiency, hepatic impairment, neurological or hematologic disturbances, uteroplacental dysfunction, and foetal growth restriction (12, 13). Routine clinical screening typically involves serial blood pressure monitoring and urine analysis for protein detection. Notably, approximately 15-25% of women with gestational hypertension eventually develop preeclampsia (14).

A range of diagnostic modalities is currently employed to evaluate placental structure and function, with Doppler ultrasonography being the most widely used and clinically informative tool (15). Numerous investigations have explored strategies for the prevention of preeclampsia in the general obstetric population, including non-pharmacological interventions such as bed rest, activity modification, regular physical exercise, dietary regulation (particularly sodium restriction), and antioxidant supplementation with agents like vitamins C and E, garlic extract, or fish oil. In contrast, studies targeting high-risk women have primarily focused on secondary prevention, examining the potential benefits of pharmacologic agents such as diuretics, progesterone, nitric oxide (NO) donors, calcium supplementation, and low-dose aspirin (16, 17).

Melatonin, a methoxyindole hormone, is predominantly synthesised and secreted by the pineal gland at night under physiological light-dark conditions. Its circadian rhythm is governed by the suprachiasmatic nucleus, which synchronises secretion with environmental light exposure; illumination can suppress or phase-shift melatonin release depending on the timing and intensity of the light stimuli (18). Beyond its chronobiological role, melatonin functions as a potent mitochondrial protector, exerting antioxidant capacities that equal or surpass those of conventional antioxidant agents (19). Activation of melatonin receptors (MT1 and MT2) initiates multiple intracellular signalling cascades that attenuate oxidative stress and modulate inflammatory responses. The identification of a complete melatonergic system within placental tissue is a major advancement in understanding its physiological role during gestation. Melatonin is recognised as a pivotal regulator of trophoblastic growth, differentiation, and placental function, particularly at critical stages of pregnancy (20). By maintaining uterine homeostasis and coordinating pathways linked to uterine receptivity and implantation, optimal maternal melatonin levels appear essential for successful reproductive outcomes (21). Alterations in melatonin synthesis, receptor expression, and enzymatic activity have been implicated in the pathophysiology of preeclampsia, suggesting that melatonin may serve both as a biomarker and as a therapeutic candidate for disorders associated with placental dysfunction and heightened oxidative stress. Notably, decreased melatonin levels have been reported in women with abnormal placentas, including those with preeclampsia or IUGR (22). Clinical and experimental data further indicate that short-term melatonin supplementation is safe and may reduce pregnan cy-related and perinatal complications. Given its strong safety profile for both mother and foetus, melatonin represents a promising adjunctive therapeutic and neuroprotective agent, particularly in conditions such as perinatal asphyxia (23, 24). Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms that underlie its antihypertensive and endothelial protective effects remain incompletely elucidated; proposed mechanisms include central modulation of vascular tone, systemic anti-inflammatory and lipid-lowering actions, enhancement of mitochondrial antioxidant defences, and direct effects on myocardial and endothelial function (25).

As explained, melatonin is an endogenous antioxidant hormone that is considered a safe and potentially effective drug for improving oxidative stress responses in the placenta or endothelium during preeclampsia. When used as a preventive treatment before the onset of preeclampsia, it may provide greater benefits compared to its use as an adjunctive therapy in individuals with established disease. The promising results from laboratory and earlyphase clinical trials indicate that a randomised controlled trial would be an appropriate study design to address this research question. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the adjunctive effect of melatonin in preventing preeclampsia in women with moderate to high-risk factors for this disorder.

Materials and Methods

Participants and study design

This study was a randomised, triple-blind, controlled clinical trial. Pregnant women receiving prenatal care at Kashan University of Medical Sciences Shahid Beheshti Hospital Clinic, Shabih Khani Maternity Clinic, and Imam Reza Clinic were enrolled. Primary outcome was the incidence of preeclampsia during pregnancy. The secondary outcomes included changes in systolic and diastolic blood pressure throughout pregnancy, gestational age at delivery, mode of delivery, and neonatal outcomes including birth weight, birth length, head circumference, and Apgar scores at 1 and 5 minutes. Participants were classified by an obstetrician as having a moderate to high-risk of developing preeclampsia. The sample size was determined based on prior studies that assessed the preventive effects of melatonin on preeclampsia, assuming a preeclampsia incidence of 20% in the control group and 45% in the intervention group, which corresponded to an anticipated effect size of 25% (22). Using a two-sided test with a significance level of 0.05 and a power of 80% (β=0.20), the calculated sample size was 54 participants per group. However, to account for an expected 10% dropout rate, the final sample size was adjusted to 70 women per group. The calculation was performed using the standard formula for comparing two proportions:

n = ((Z1-α/2 + Z1-β)² × (P1(1-P1) + P2(1-P2)))/(P1-P2)²

Where P1 and P2 represent the expected proportions of preeclampsia in the control and intervention groups, re spectively.

This research was conducted at Kashan University of Medical Sciences (IR.KAUMS.MEDNT.REC.1402.138) and thesis number (402027). The IRCT number was IRCT20230930059563N1. Data supporting the findings of this study are available upon reasonable request from the corresponding author.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligibility criteria included maternal age ≥16 years, gestational age between 12 and 28 weeks at enrolment, presence of moderate to high-risk for preeclampsia according to the United States Preventive Services Task Force guidelines, and provision of written informed consent. Exclusion criteria were multiple gestation, current or recent melatonin use within the past six weeks, known allergy to melatonin or aspirin, use of medications that could affect melatonin metabolism or clearance, inability to attend follow-up visits, refusal to participate, and the presence of foetal anomalies.

Study implementation

High-risk factors included a history of preeclampsia, particularly if associated with complications, multiple gestation, chronic hypertension, pre-existing type 1 or type 2 diabetes, kidney disease, autoimmune disorders, or a combination of moderate-risk factors. Moderate risk factors encompassed first pregnancy, obesity (body mass index (BMI>30)), a family history of preeclamp sia, low socioeconomic status, maternal age ≥35 years, maternal history such as low birth weight, small-for gestational-age infants, a ≥10-year interval since the last pregnancy, previous placental abruption or stillbirth, and use of assisted reproductive technology. Eligible partici pants received detailed counselling and they provided written informed consent after the study procedures were explained. Upon enrolment, participants were randomly assigned to either the intervention or control group. The intervention group received 80 mg of aspirin plus 10 mg of melatonin daily, whereas the control group received 80 mg of aspirin plus a matched placebo tablet (provided by VanaDarouGostar Company) once daily. Treatment commenced between 12 and 28 weeks of gestation, as prescribed by the obstetrician, and continued until de livery. At each visit, medication adherence was assessed and participants were reminded to maintain consistent use. All participants were followed until delivery. During the follow-up visits, blood pressure measurements were recorded, along with any hospitalisations. At delivery, data were collected on delivery mode, neonatal birth weight, length, head circumference, Apgar scores, and gestational age at birth.

Participants who missed scheduled follow-up visits were contacted by phone as reminders. Those who opted to discontinue the study or declined to take the assigned medica tion despite receiving thorough scientific explanations were withdrawn from the trial. Randomisation was conducted using block randomisation with blocks of four. Both participants and study personnel were blinded to group assignments to ensure a triple-blind design. To maintain blinding, both the intervention and control medications were provided in identical packaging, size, and labelling. The allocation codes were held by a research collaborator and were revealed upon completion of the study. Consequently, neither the participants nor the study personnel were aware of group assignments until after data analysis, ensuring a triple-blind design. Collected data included maternal age, BMI, blood pressure, parity, nationality, educational level, intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), gestational age at delivery, treatment allocation, delivery mode, and neonatal birth weight, all of which were systematically recorded in a standardised checklist.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS software version 17 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics including mean ± standard deviation (SD), frequency, and percentage were used to summarise participants’ demographic and clinical characteristics. The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Between-group comparisons for baseline characteristics were conducted using the independent t-test for continuous variables and the chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. A repeated measures analysis of variance was applied to evaluate changes in systolic and diastolic blood pressure across different time points (admission, hospitalisation, and delivery). Mauchly’s test of sphericity was performed to test the assumption of sphericity, and the Greenhouse-Geisser correction was used when this assumption was violated. Interaction effects between time and group (treatment vs. control) were also tested. Additionally, trend analyses (linear and quadratic) were performed to examine the pattern of changes over time. Between-subject effects were assessed for group and age factors. Logistic regression analysis adjusted for maternal age was used to evaluate the association between treatment group and the occurrence of preeclampsia. A two-tailed P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

In this study, 120 pregnant women with moderate to high-risk factor for preeclampsia were divided into two groups of 60 per group. The intervention group received aspirin and melatonin while the control group received aspirin and a placebo. Figure 1 illustrates the patient recruitment process in this study.

Table 1 shows the distribution of demographic characteristics of the patients. The results of this table indicate that the two groups were homogeneous of demographic characteristics, with no statistically significant differences observed in terms of age (P=0.065), BMI (P=0.350), age group (P=0.256), education (0.074), and nationality (0.673). According to the data in Table 1, the medical characteristics of the patients indicated no statistically significant differences between the two groups in terms of history of eclampsia (P=0.891), diabetes (P=0.855), kidney disease (P=0.243), hypertension (P=0.142), and autoimmune diseases (P=0.697).

The distribution of maternal and neonatal outcomes among participants with moderate and high-risk factors for preeclampsia is summarised in Table 1. The results indicated that the combination of aspirin and melatonin did not produce significant differences compared with aspirin alone in terms of delivery mode or neonatal sex (P=0.453). Similarly, gestational age at delivery did not differ significantly between the two groups (P=0.885). Regarding neonatal outcomes, no significant differences were observed with the one- or five-minute Apgar scores (P>0.05); the mean neonatal head circumference, length, and birth weight were also comparable between the groups (P>0.05, Table 1).

A repeated measures ANOVA was performed to evaluate changes in systolic blood pressure over three time points. Mauchly’s test indicated a violation of sphericity (results not shown), and Greenhouse-Geisser corrections were applied where appropriate. The main effect of time was statistically significant (F(1.78, df_adj)=4.35, P=0.018), which indicated a significant change in systolic blood pressure across the measured time points. No significant interactions were observed between time and age (F(1.78, df_adj)=0.73, P=0.468) or between time and group (F=0.018, P=0.974), suggesting that these factors did not significantly influence the within-subject changes (Table 2, Fig .2A). Trend analysis further revealed a significant linear trend across time levels (F(1,117)=5.411, P=0.022), confirming that changes in the dependent variable followed a consistent upward or downward pattern. The quadratic trend was non-significant (F=1.944, P=0.166), suggesting no curvature in the pattern of change. Interactions between the linear or quadratic trends and age or group were all non-significant (P>0.05), reinforcing that the observed linear pattern was not influenced by these variables.

Discussion

This study evaluated the effect of adjunctive melatonin on the prevention of preeclampsia in women with moderate to high-risk factors. Previous studies have indicated that melatonin is safe for use during pregnancy, with no adverse effects reported at doses up to 30 mg per day (26-30). Our findings demonstrated that combining aspirin with melatonin, compared with aspirin alone, did not significantly alter delivery mode or gestational age at delivery. Importantly, the incidence of preeclampsia, the primary outcome, did not differ significantly between the two groups. This observation indicated that melatonin supplementation had no measurable effect on pregnancy outcomes. Similarly, neonatal outcomes of one- and five-minute Apgar scores as well as head circumference, length, and birth weight were comparable between the groups, suggesting that melatonin did not influence neonatal health. To date, research on the effects of melatonin on preeclampsia has largely been limited to experimental and preclinical studies, with a primary focus on trophoblast and endothelial cell function in vitro or in animal models.

Experimental studies have provided several insights into the potential protective mechanisms of melatonin in preeclampsia. Hobson et al. (26) demonstrated that melatonin prevents excessive activation of endothelial cells and protects endothelial monolayers from damage induced by trophoblastic debris in preeclampsia serum. Similarly, Chen et al. (31) reported that melatonin inhibits the upregulation of intracellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1), which may contribute to improved vascular function. Hannan et al. (32) showed that melatonin significantly reduces the secretion of sFlt-1, an anti-angiogenic factor implicated in preeclampsia, in isolated trophoblast cells, suggesting a role in mitigating anti-angiogenic effects and supporting vascular health.

Hobson et al. reported that melatonin mitigates oxidative stress by reducing markers generated by superoxideproducing xanthine/xanthine oxidase and by activating the Nrf2 pathway along with downstream antioxidant enzymes, including heme oxygenase-1, thereby preventing cellular damage (26). Reiter et al. (33) and di Blasio et al. (34) reported that melatonin localises specifically within the mitochondria of placental trophoblast cells where it protects the electron transport chain from ischemia-hypoxia-induced injury. This mitochondrial action reduces reactive oxygen species production and prevents severe oxidative stress in placentas affected by preeclampsia.

Melatonin, through its antioxidant properties and regulation of endothelial function, has been shown to lower blood pressure in hypertensive animal models. Some studies suggest that melatonin may reduce blood pressure in pregnant women with preeclampsia by potentially improving endothelial function and enhancing NO production (34-36). In the present study, however, the combination of melatonin and aspirin did not significantly affect delivery mode or the incidence of preeclampsia compared with aspirin alone. These findings contradict prior experimental evidence that demonstrated the protective effects of melatonin on preeclampsia. This discrepancy may be attributed to the dosage of melatonin administered; higher doses may be required to achieve effective placental penetration and elicit measurable physiological effects, as indicated in some preclinical studies (32).

In addition, the duration of melatonin administration and the timing of its use during pregnancy may influence its effectiveness. The findings of the present study differ from previous reports, in which melatonin was administered multiple times each day in some protocols (28, 32). Variations in response to melatonin, including genetic and environmental factors, may also contribute to the observed outcomes. Most investigations were conducted in vitro or in animal models; therefore, clinical evidence in humans is limited. These considerations underscore the need for further well-designed clinical studies to more accurately assess the effects of melatonin during pregnancy and its potential role in preventing preeclampsia. Collectively, while preclinical data suggest promising benefits of melatonin, larger and more comprehensive clinical trials are necessary to validate its efficacy in improving pregnancy outcomes in real-world settings.

In this study, no significant differences were observed between the intervention and control groups at the one- and five-minute Apgar scores, a primary indicator of neonatal well-being that evaluates respiratory effort, heart rate, muscle tone, and skin coloration. This finding suggests that melatonin supplementation did not significantly affect the immediate health status of the newborns. Similarly, there were no significant differences in mean neonatal head circumference, length, or birth weight between the two groups. These measures, which reflect foetal growth, are typically influenced by maternal health and nutrition during pregnancy, indicating that melatonin had no detectable impact on these growth parameters when administered along with aspirin. Preclinical studies have shown that melatonin may protect against oxidative stress and inflammatory processes, potentially supporting foetal growth and mitigating complications related to placental insufficiency (37, 38). However, the present findings suggest that, in this clinical context, melatonin did not confer measurable benefits on neonatal outcomes.

Regular monitoring of blood pressure during pregnancy enables timely detection of abnormal changes and the implementation of interventions to prevent serious complications. In this study, blood pressure was assessed at enrolment, during prenatal visits, and at delivery. No significant interaction was observed between the study group and time, although a significant change in systolic blood pressure over time was noted. Overall, the combination of aspirin and melatonin did not demonstrate superiority over aspirin alone. Preclinical studies have suggested that melatonin may lower blood pressure by enhancing endothelial function and increasing NO levels (34, 39, 40); however, few clinical studies have evaluated this effect. In the present study, while the mean systolic and diastolic blood pressure in the aspirin plus melatonin group was slightly lower than in the control group, the difference was not statistically significant. These findings indicate that melatonin, either alone or at higher doses, may potentially lower blood pressure, but under the conditions of this study, its combination with aspirin did not yield significant changes. Further clinical research is warranted to clarify the impact of melatonin on blood pressure regulation and preeclampsia prevention in pregnant women.

This study has several strengths that may be used for future research. A key strength was the use of block randomisation with blocks of four within the triple-blind design, which minimised potential biases in group allocation and outcome assessment, and enhanced the internal validity of this study. Our comprehensive evaluation of multiple maternal and neonatal outcomes, including delivery mode, blood pressure, and neonatal Apgar scores, birth weight, length, and head circumference, provided a rich dataset for multivariate analyses. Furthermore, the application of rigorous inclusion and exclusion criteria, along with careful participant selection based on preeclampsia risk assessment and the absence of foetal anomalies, enhanced the validity and accuracy of the study population, preventing the enrolment of ineligible participants and ensuring robust study results.

One limitation of this study was the melatonin dosing regimen, as only 10 mg was administered once daily, whereas preclinical studies have used higher or multiple daily doses. Future research should explore various dosage and administration schedules to more accurately assess the effects of melatonin on preeclampsia and neonatal outcomes. Additionally, the lack of long-term followup limits understanding of maternal and infant outcomes beyond delivery; subsequent studies should track infant growth and neurodevelopment to clarify lasting effects. This study primarily assessed short-term outcomes, such as delivery mode and neonatal measures, without evaluating the impact on endothelial function or related biochemical markers. Future investigations should examine its influence on sFlt-1, ICAM-1, and other endothelial markers to elucidate mechanistic pathways. Finally, as the study was conducted in a limited number of centres within a single geographic region, larger, multi-centre trials across diverse populations are needed to enhance generalisability.

Future research could involve a direct comparison of melatonin with other effective drugs for preventing and treating preeclampsia to determine which medications offer the best outcomes. Although many studies in animal models have demonstrated the effects of melatonin on blood pressure reduction, more clinical trials are needed to accurately assess the effects of melatonin on blood pressure control in pregnant women. Future studies could explore the impact of combining melatonin with other antioxidants or medications to evaluate the potential enhancement of protective and therapeutic effects.

Conclusion

The addition of melatonin to aspirin did not significantly reduce the incidence of preeclampsia or improve maternal and neonatal outcomes. Neonatal measures, including Apgar scores, birth weight, length, and head circumference, were comparable between the aspirin and aspirin plus melatonin groups. Although preclinical studies have highlighted the antioxidant properties of melatonin and its capacity to enhance endothelial function under oxidative stress, these protective effects were not evident in this clinical cohort. The lack of observed benefits may be related to factors such as melatonin dosage, duration of administration, and individual variations in response. Further clinical trials with larger sample sizes and varied dosing regimens are warranted to more accurately assess the ability of melatonin to prevent preeclampsia and improve pregnancy outcomes. Overall, although melatonin demonstrates promising therapeutic potential, robust clinical evidence is required to substantiate its efficacy in pregnant women.
