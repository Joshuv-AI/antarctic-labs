To the Editor,

Aspirin‐Exacerbated Respiratory Disease (AERD) is a chronic condition that impairs quality of life and may pose life‐threatening risks [1]. Patients typically suffer from severe asthma and chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (CRSwNP). Accurate and timely diagnosis is essential to ensure patient safety through education and safe medication use, and to enable the initiation of disease‐tailored therapies for uncontrolled asthma and recurrent nasal polyps. Yet, diagnosis delay remains a defining feature of AERD [1, 2]. The treatment of AERD is well‐established, safe, and effective. Consequently, the main challenge highlighted in the literature is the difficulty in diagnosing this disease [1, 2, 3].

Despite its clinical importance, the timing of symptom onset and the progression of AERD remain poorly characterized [4]. Clarifying these temporal dynamics is necessary, particularly given recent evidence that AERD follows a significantly more severe clinical course than aspirin‐tolerant asthma [5]. Understanding the progression and pace of disease development is crucial for facilitating earlier recognition and enabling timely, tailored treatment [1], especially since early biologic intervention, prior to irreversible epithelial damage, holds the potential to fundamentally alter the disease course [6].

Advances in digital health data offer new opportunities to improve the identification of AERD patients through the coexistence of three diagnostic codes: asthma, nasal polyps, and NSAID sensitivity [3, 4, 5, 7, 8]. Hence, the objective of this study is to investigate the diagnostic sequence and temporal intervals between AERD‐related conditions through analysis of EMR data.

Our cohort included 181 MHS patients diagnosed with asthma, nasal polyps, and NSAID sensitivity between 2002 and 2022, of whom 101 were females (56%) and 80 were males (44%). Diagnoses were identified based on ICD codes for asthma (493) and nasal polyps (471). Most patients had repeated spirometry testing (median 4 tests per patient), with similar patterns observed across all sequence groups (p = 0.889). This pattern suggests consistent diagnostic practices and ongoing clinical follow‐up across the cohort. NSAID hypersensitivity was based on clinician‐documented patient history, typically requiring at least two episodes. The study was approved by MHS Institutional Review Board.

Patients were classified into six groups based on the chronological order of symptom diagnosis. Diagnostic intervals, biomarkers, and healthcare utilization were analysed across groups (see Table 1). Consistent with previous studies, AERD was more prevalent in women [3, 4, 5, 7, 9], and symptom onset typically occurs after age 40. In half of the cohort, asthma was the initial symptom, while 26% presented first with nasal polyps and the remainder (24%) with NSAID sensitivity.

The mean age at onset of each symptom was relatively similar. However, when examined separately by sequence group (see Table 1), significant differences in age at onset were observed for all three symptoms. For example, in the ANP group, mean asthma onset was 42 years, whereas in the NPA group it was 56 years. Similarly, the interval between first and last symptoms differed significantly: the overall mean was 8 years, ranging from 7 years (APN) to 11 years (NPA).

Characterizing these diagnostic intervals is of clinical importance, as it may help identify critical windows for earlier recognition and therapeutic intervention [5]. A shorter interval may reflect a more aggressive disease course, whereas a longer one may be associated with variations in clinical awareness, diagnostic practices, or access to specialized care [1, 9].

Differences in diagnostic practices may partly explain these temporal variations [2]. In most groups, asthma diagnosis was more frequently made by specialists. However, in the two groups in which asthma was the last symptom of the AERD triad to be diagnosed, fewer patients were referred to a specialist (NPA), or referral rates were similar (PNA). This observation suggests that earlier referral of patients with nasal polyp to a pulmonologist or allergy specialist may facilitate more timely recognition of asthma [7, 9].

Out of the six groups, two groups demonstrated a substantially delayed onset of NSAID sensitivity. In the APN group, mean age at NSAID sensitivity onset was 53 years, and in the PAN group, 51 years. Although the recorded timing of NSAID sensitivity in EMR data may not always reflect the true biological onset, this delay may reflect limited patient awareness of NSAID reactions [2]. Given the potential severity of such reactions, it may be advisable for health providers to explicitly inquire about NSAID use in patients with asthma and nasal polyposis, or to consider proactive sensitivity testing [2].

The six‐group classification enabled the identification of patterns that might have otherwise remained undetected, including both the age‐related differences in NSAID sensitivity and the distinct clinical trends observed in the PNA group. This group, the smallest (N = 14), showed a unique clinical profile. Although no statistically significant differences were observed in healthcare utilization, patients in the PNA group had the highest mean rates of physician visits, emergency department use, and hospitalizations, with large variability.

This trend is particularly interesting, as one might expect asthma, a condition that can be life‐threatening, to lead to greater healthcare utilization. In contrast, nasal polyps, which are not life‐threatening but substantially impair quality of life, appear to have a stronger impact on healthcare service use in this cohort. While the small sample size likely limited the ability to detect statistical power, these findings are nonetheless meaningful and may reflect a subgroup of patients with more severe or persistent disease courses.

The PNA group demonstrated the lowest mean number of FESS procedures, despite nasal polyps being the initial symptom in this group. In contrast, the PAN group, where nasal polyps also presented first, had the highest mean number of FESS procedures. This difference may reflect variations in disease management, referral patterns, or timing of intervention, and may partly explain the higher healthcare utilization observed in the PNA group. It is also possible that a delay in asthma diagnosis in the PNA group played a role, resulting in extended periods of uncontrolled inflammation and symptom progression [3]. Consequently, patients presenting with nasal polyps and NSAID sensitivity may represent a higher‐risk subgroup, in whom earlier evaluation for asthma and consideration of biologic therapies could be considered.

In conclusion, characterizing the diagnostic patterns and progression of AERD in real‐world settings may help reduce diagnostic delays. Patients presenting with nasal polyps and NSAID sensitivity prior to asthma may represent a distinct clinical subgroup. Recognition of such sequences may support earlier specialist referral and more timely consideration of targeted biologic therapy, with the potential to mitigate long‐term clinical and healthcare burden. While the six‐sequence classification provides a more detailed view of AERD progression, the relatively small subgroup sizes require cautious interpretation. Nevertheless, the sequence‐based analysis enabled identification of patterns that were not apparent when considering only the first manifestation of disease. Future studies may be conducted using broader, clinically‐oriented groupings based on the first manifestation of disease and on larger datasets. We hope this study may serve as a foundation for future EMR‐based investigations of diagnostic delay and subgroup patterns within heterogeneous AERD populations.
