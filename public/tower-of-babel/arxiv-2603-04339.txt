Quantum error mitigation by hierarchy-informed sampling: chiral dynamics in the Schwinger model
Theo Saporiti, Oleg Kaikov, Vasily Sazonov, Mohamed Tamaazousti

Abstract
Quantum simulations on current NISQ hardware are limited by its noisy nature, making efficient quantum error mitigation methods highly demanded. In this paper we introduce a novel mitigation scheme, applicable to arbitrary quantum simulations of time-dependent Hamiltonian dynamics on NISQ devices. The scheme uses a polynomial subset of extended qubit Bogoliubov-Born-Green-Kirkwood-Yvon (BBGKY) hierarchy equations as a sampling criterion of possible mitigated candidates for the quantum observables. We show that for favorable Hamiltonians the polynomial subset of BBGKY hierarchy equations leads to a polynomial overhead in both classical and quantum resources. We employ the method to mitigate simulations of the chiral magnetic effect (CME), a chiral feature of the Schwinger model. We empirically show the effectiveness of our scheme at recovering the real-time dynamics of the CME from noisy quantum simulations of the Schwinger model, for a range of different parameter values of the model. We numerically demonstrate a systematic reduction of quantum noise, together with an increasing noise reduction capability as the amount of BBGKY constraints grows.

III
Mitigation technique

In this Section we propose our novel mitigation technique, based on a BBGKY physics-informed sampling of possible mitigation candidates. We begin with a reminder on Trotterization, to both introduce notations and to formally state the mitigation goal. We then explain and build the mathematical justification behind our scheme, namely the possibility to compute exact real-time dynamics as classical expectation values, stemming from a probability distribution encoding the BBGKY hierarchy. Next, we build over this framework our novel mitigation technique. By generalizing the probability distribution to account for the noisy quantum simulation measurements, the once exact probabilistic method becomes a mitigation procedure, informed by supplemented BBGKY constraints. Finally, we explain how to numerically implement the technique as a simulated annealing algorithm.

where

. The BBGKY action is given by the summation of
\absolutevalue{\mathcal{Q}_r}(N_{\text{T}}+1)

III.3
Our mitigation scheme

Equation (
16
) provides an approximation of the exact real-time dynamics as the
\lambda\to\infty
limit of a classical expectation value, stemming from the distribution
\mathcal{D}(\lambda)
. Since
\mathcal{D}(\lambda)
is completely determined by a choice of an action in the PDF (
17
),
S(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}})
has the capability to encode the exact dynamics of the spin chain. As a result, real-time dynamics can be converted into an action form. It is in this spirit that we propose to gather all quantum simulation measurements
\bar{x}_{qs}
within the quantum action

S_{\text{Q}}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}):=\frac{\Delta t}{2}\sum_{q=1}^{\absolutevalue{\mathcal{Q}_{r+1}}}\sum_{s=0}^{N_{\text{T}}}\quantity(\frac{x_{qs} - \bar{x}_{qs}}{y_{qs}})^{2},

(19)

where

y_{qs}:=1-\begin{cases}\bar{x}^{2}_{qs},&\text{if $\absolutevalue{\bar{x}_{qs}}<1$}\\
\displaystyle\quantity(\frac{N_\text{S} \bar{x}_{qs} - \text{sgn}(\bar{x}_{qs})}{N_\text{S}+1})^{2},&\text{if $\absolutevalue{\bar{x}_{qs}}=1$}\end{cases}.

(20)

The above is the (modified) standard deviation associated to the set of single-shot measurements composing
\bar{x}_{qs}
, whose definition and use we justify in Appendix
F
. Once exponentiated, the quantum action (
19
) functions as a collection of Gaussian penalty terms, so that if one sets
S(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}})=S_{\text{Q}}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}})
then one trivially recovers
\chi_{qs}=\bar{x}_{qs}
. Indeed, (
19
) effectively substitutes all
E^{a}_{A}(s,\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}})
of (
18
) with constraints of the form
x_{qs}-\bar{x}_{qs}=0
.

If all quantum measurements
\bar{x}_{qs}
were noiseless, then (
19
) would encode the same physical information as the exponentially large sum in (
18
), evaluated at
r=R
. However,
\bar{x}_{qs}
measurements are obtained from noisy quantum devices, undermining the capability of (
19
) to grasp the exact dynamics. To tackle the issue, we propose to generalize the action as

S(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}):=\quantity(1 - z)S_{\text{Q}}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}})+zS_{\text{B}}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}),

(21)

where
0\leq z\leq 1
, which has yet to be defined, interpolates between the two actions,
z=0
corresponding to
S(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}})=S_{\text{Q}}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}})
and
z=1
to
S(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}})=S_{\text{B}}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}})
. Importantly, we evaluate
S_{\text{B}}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}})
at a radius
r
much smaller than
R
. With this new action,
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}
binds to the noisy measurements in
S_{\text{Q}}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}})
, while at the same time
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}
tries to satisfy the selected
\mathcal{Q}_{r}
BBGKY equations introduced in
S_{\text{B}}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}})
. This makes the obtained
\chi_{qs}
more physical than
\bar{x}_{qs}
, hence a more genuine representation of
\expectationvalue{\sigma_q (t_s)}
, because
x_{qs}
is additionally constrained by the considered BBGKY equations. We therefore propose
\chi_{qs}
as the mitigation of
\bar{x}_{qs}
, where
z\in[0,1]
quantifies the proportion of included physics informing the mitigation.

Two possible choices for the definition of
z
are considered in this paper. The natural
z:=\absolutevalue{\mathcal{Q}_{r}}/\absolutevalue{\mathcal{Q}_R}
definition requires the determination of
\mathcal{Q}_{R}
, which is an exponentially large subset whose computation undermines its practical use. To avoid this issue, we opt for the alternative definition
z:=\absolutevalue{\mathcal{Q}_{r}}/\absolutevalue{\mathcal{Q}_{r+1}}
which, as seen in Sec.
II.3
, can be computed with a
\text{poly}(N_{\text{Q}})
amount of classical resources. Hence, because of this last suitable property, we employ this second definition of
z
for all numerical results in the upcoming Sec.
V
.

Finally, from (
12
), the relationship between these two choices for
z
is (the inequalities are no longer strict because we allow
r\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
)

0<\frac{\absolutevalue{\mathcal{Q}_{r}}}{\absolutevalue{\mathcal{Q}_R}}\leq\frac{\absolutevalue{\mathcal{Q}_{r}}}{\absolutevalue{\mathcal{Q}_{r+1}}}\leq 1.

(22)

From the above, we see that the relative difference between the two ratios shrinks as
r
increases, until they become equal at
r=R-1
, and that both ratios become equal to unity at
r=R
.

III.4
Sampling of the mitigations

The evaluation of the mitigated configuration (
16
) implies calculating the
\lambda\to\infty
limit (task 1) of a
N_{\text{T}}\absolutevalue{\mathcal{Q}_{r+1}}
-dimensional integral (task 2). Task 2 cannot be tackled with quadrature formulas, as it would require the sampling of
\order{N_\text{S}^{N_\text{T} \abs{\mathcal{Q}_{r+1}}}}
configurations. To overcome this (classical) computational limitation, one can rely on the Metropolis-Hastings (MH) sampling procedure
55
;
31
, which we now briefly present.

From an initial configuration
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}_{0}
, the MH algorithm produces a Markovian chain of configurations
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\to\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}_{1}\to\dots\to\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}_{m}\to\dots\to\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}_{M}
whose coordinates are denoted by
x^{m}_{qs}
. These configurations are used in the estimation

\overrightarrow{\mathbf{\chi}}_{\text{MH}}:=\frac{1}{M_{\text{S}}}\sum_{m=1}^{M_{\text{S}}}\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}_{M_{\text{T}}+m\frac{M-M_{\text{T}}}{M_{\text{S}}}},

(23)

where
M_{\text{S}}
is the amount of (equispaced) selected samples after
M_{\text{T}}
thermalization sweeps
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}_{m}\to\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}_{m+1}
. In turn, a sweep is composed of
N_{\text{T}}\absolutevalue{\mathcal{Q}_{r+1}}
proposals
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}_{m}\to\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}_{m}
, where
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}_{m}
differs from
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}_{m}
by one randomly shifted chosen coordinate. The proposal is accepted with probability
\min(1,\omega(\lambda,\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})/\omega(\lambda,\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}))=\min(1,e^{-\lambda\Delta S(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime},\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}})})
, where
\Delta S(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime},\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}):=S(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})-S(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}})
. We optimize the evaluation of
\Delta S(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime},\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}})
by only computing the local variation of the action, given by the difference of the terms in
S(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})
and
S(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}})
involved in the proposal.

Thermalization ensures
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}_{M_{\text{T}}}
is sufficiently distant from the arbitrary and unphysical
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}}_{0}
, while the amount of selected samples reduces autocorrelation between selected configurations, improving the statistics
51
. The configurations of the Markov chain past the thermalization point approximate random draws of
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{X}}\sim\mathcal{D}(\lambda)
. In practice, these configurations will mostly be physical (in the BBGKY sense) oscillations around the quantum measurements.

Task 1 can be simultaneously solved with task 2 if incorporated in the MH sampling algorithm, which then needs to be generalized. We heuristically achieve this by slowly increasing
\lambda
at each sweep by a small variation
\Delta\lambda
. That way, by identifying
\lambda
with an inverse temperature, the MH algorithm becomes a simulated annealing procedure
46
, and the Markov chain gets progressively trapped within the peaking
\mathcal{D}(\lambda)
distribution as
\lambda
increases, reproducing the limit process. Algorithm

IV
Chiral dynamics in the Schwinger model

In this Section, we describe the CME showcased in QCD and its implications in current QCD phenomenology research. We then introduce the Schwinger model and its corresponding CME. Finally, we provide its spin-
\frac{1}{2}
chain Hamiltonian, together with the encoding of the induced electric current.

IV.1
The chiral magnetic effect

Consider approximately massless chiral fermions in a strong external magnetic field. Under these conditions, a chiral imbalance between right- and left-handed fermions, quantified by the chemical potential
\mu_{5}
, generates an electric current (or correspondingly an electric field) along the magnetic field lines: this phenomenon is called the CME
24
;
25
;
42
;
43
;
41
;
74
.

On a phenomenological level, following
41
, the strong magnetic field polarizes the spins of the positively-charged (negatively-charged) particles along (opposite to) its direction, forcing a spin-momentum lock of the fermions dictated by their chirality, which coincides with their helicity in the massless limit
74
. That way, assuming approximately parallel spins and momenta, right- (left-) handed fermions are bound to move along (opposite to) the magnetic field lines. At the same time, right-handed positive and left-handed negative (left-handed positive and right-handed negative) fermions collectively generate an electric current along (opposite to) the magnetic field axis. If the density of right-handed fermions matches the density of left-handed fermions,
\mu_{5}=0
, no charge separation is induced. However, in the event of a positive (negative) chiral imbalance
\mu_{5}\neq 0
, a positive (negative) macroscopic current is generated along (opposite to) the external strong magnetic field.

Due to its macroscopic nature, the CME can be experimentally observed
52
. As a result, the CME can be used to probe quantum phenomena otherwise inaccessible, such as topological transitions in color-confined QCD matter
43
. Indeed, these transitions come with chiral flips, which induce (at least locally) chiral imbalances
44
. For instance, taking advantage of the strong magnetic fields generated in relativistic heavy-ion collisions, the CME could be observed in the resulting quark-gluon plasma
43
. As the QCD topological transitions violate baryon number conservation, their observation via the CME in the rapidly expanding quark-gluon plasma (mimicking the early Universe) could provide a possible explanation to the baryon asymmetry problem
60
.

IV.2
The CME in the Schwinger model

The (massive
m>0
) Schwinger model is a
(1+1)
-dimensional theory describing quantum electrodynamics
63
. Despite its simplicity relative to
(3+1)
-dimensional QCD, it shares similar features such as confinement
13
and the sign problem
27
, making it a suitable toy model to, respectively, probe QCD and test quantum simulations of field theories. In particular, the Schwinger model also showcases a phenomenon similar to the CME, upon a chiral transformation of the fermionic fields by a time-dependent angle
\theta
and in the vanishing coupling constant limit
39
. While the former induces the chiral imbalance via the chiral chemical potential
\mu_{5}=-\dot{\theta}/2
, the latter decouples the theory into free fermions of chirally-rotated masses
39
. In this particular regime of the Schwinger model, a chiral imbalance induces an electric current as in the CME. Choosing the particular time-dependence for the sudden chiral quench

\dot{\theta}(t)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if $t\leq 0$}\\
-2\mu_{5},&\text{if $t>0$}\end{cases},

(24)

with
\theta(0)=0
and
\mu_{5}
from now on considered constant, it can be shown that the
t\ll 1
behaviour of the (spatially averaged) induced electric current is
39

\expectationvalue{j}_{\text{avg}}=4\mu_{5}mt^{2}\expectationvalue{\bar{\psi}\psi}_{\text{avg}}+\order{t^3},

(25)

where
\expectationvalue{\bar{\psi}\psi}_{\text{avg}}\sim-m
is the (spatially averaged) chiral condensate.

The discretized lattice formulation of the Schwinger model, in the above particular form, is obtained using Kogut-Susskind fermions
49
. Then, with an application of the Jordan-Wigner transformation
36
, the lattice is mapped to a quantum spin-
\frac{1}{2}
chain. By forcing periodic boundary conditions, the
N_{\text{Q}}\in 2\mathbb{N}_{1}
qubits Hamiltonian of the model brought to the spin chain reads
39

\begin{split}H&=\sum_{k=0}^{\frac{N_{\text{Q}}}{2}-1}\Bigl[a_{k}\quantity(\sigma^1_{2k+1} \sigma^1_{2k+2} + \sigma^2_{2k+1} \sigma^2_{2k+2})\\
&-\frac{\dot{\theta}}{8}\quantity(\sigma^1_{2k+1} \sigma^2_{2k+2} - \sigma^2_{2k+1} \sigma^1_{2k+2})\Bigl]\\
&+\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{N_{\text{Q}}}{2}-1}\Bigl[a_{k}\quantity(\sigma^1_{2k} \sigma^1_{2k+1} + \sigma^2_{2k} \sigma^2_{2k+1})\\
&-\frac{\dot{\theta}}{8}\quantity(\sigma^1_{2k} \sigma^2_{2k+1} - \sigma^2_{2k} \sigma^1_{2k+1})\Bigl]\\
&+(-1)^{\frac{N_{\text{Q}}}{2}}a_{N_{\text{Q}}-1}\quantity(\sigma^1_1 \sigma^1_{N_\text{Q}} + \sigma^2_1 \sigma^2_{N_\text{Q}})\prod_{k=2}^{N_{\text{Q}}-1}\sigma^{3}_{k}\\
&-(-1)^{\frac{N_{\text{Q}}}{2}}\frac{\dot{\theta}}{8}\quantity(\sigma^2_1 \sigma^1_{N_\text{Q}} - \sigma^1_1 \sigma^2_{N_\text{Q}})\prod_{k=2}^{N_{\text{Q}}-1}\sigma^{3}_{k}\\
&-\frac{m}{2}\cos(\theta)\sum_{k=1}^{N_{\text{Q}}}(-1)^{k}\sigma^{3}_{k}\\
&=:\sum_{(B,b)\in\mathcal{H}}h^{b}_{B}(t)\sigma^{b}_{B},\end{split}

(26)

where
a_{k}:=\frac{1}{2}\quantity[\omega- (-1)^k \frac{m}{2} \sin(\theta)]
and
\omega
is half of the inverse lattice spacing. Notice that
L_{\mathcal{H}}=N_{\text{Q}}
, but
\absolutevalue{\mathcal{H}}\sim\text{poly}(N_{\text{Q}})
. Likewise, the quantum observable corresponding to the (spatially averaged) electric current (
25
) brought to the spin chain reads
39

\begin{split}J&:=\frac{\omega}{2N_{\text{Q}}}\sum_{k=0}^{\frac{N_{\text{Q}}}{2}-1}\quantity(\sigma^1_{2k+1} \sigma^2_{2k+2} - \sigma^2_{2k+1} \sigma^1_{2k+2})\\
&+\frac{\omega}{2N_{\text{Q}}}\sum_{k=1}^{\frac{N_{\text{Q}}}{2}-1}\quantity(\sigma^1_{2k} \sigma^2_{2k+1} - \sigma^2_{2k} \sigma^1_{2k+1})\\
&+(-1)^{\frac{N_{\text{Q}}}{2}}\frac{\omega}{2N_{\text{Q}}}\quantity(\sigma^2_1 \sigma^1_{N_\text{Q}} - \sigma^1_1 \sigma^2_{N_\text{Q}})\prod_{k=2}^{N_{\text{Q}}-1}\sigma^{3}_{k}\\
&=:\sum_{q=1}^{\absolutevalue{\mathcal{Q}_0}}J_{q}\sigma_{q},\end{split}

(27)

where we take
\mathcal{Q}_{0}
to be the set of all Pauli strings
\sigma_{q}
appearing in
J
, and where by
J_{q}
we denote the real coefficient associated to
\sigma_{q}
. Again, we verify that
\absolutevalue{\mathcal{Q}_0}\sim\text{poly}(N_{\text{Q}})
. The above is the main object of our study, namely (
27

VI
Conclusions

In this paper, we introduced an extended qubit BBGKY hierarchy, employed it to formulate a novel quantum error mitigations scheme, and applied the latter on noisy quantum simulations of the Schwinger model CME. More precisely, we computed qubit BBGKY equations which are able to accommodate for arbitrary-many qubits time-dependent Hamiltonians. This in turn allowed us to get a deeper understanding of the corresponding BBGKY hierarchy structure.

We showed that, provided the Hamiltonian is built with a
\text{poly}(N_{\text{Q}})
amount of Pauli strings and that the initial state is a linear combination of a polynomially-large amount of computational quantum basis states, that the classical and quantum resources required to evaluate the BBGKY equations and to determine their immediate connections throughout the hierarchy is polynomial in
N_{\text{Q}}
, motivating the applicability of the BBGKY hierarchy for quantum error mitigation purposes. This led to the formulation of our method, where configurations representing possible mitigations are sampled by a simulated annealing algorithm, under the guidance of noisy quantum measurements and of physical constraints provided by a selected portion of the BBGKY hierarchy.

To assess the effectiveness of our scheme, we compared the physics-informed mitigations with the exact dynamics of the Schwinger model CME. We empirically demonstrated a systematic improvement of all performed CME quantum simulations, regardless of the radius
r
, both in terms of global noise reduction and of recovery of short-time dynamical behaviour. Moreover, we numerically showed that the effectiveness of our method at reducing quantum noise in both cases scales with
r
. Importantly, this implies that our method is readily applicable even when only a small polynomial portion of the exponentially large subhierarchy is implemented. In the particular case of the Schwinger model Hamiltonian, the theoretically predicted behaviour of the immediate connections among BBGKY equations was verified.

Possible extensions of this work include the application of our method for variational imaginary-time evolution as a post-processing procedure. Finally, our scheme could also mitigate the execution of arbitrary quantum circuits, if translated into Hamiltonian formulations.

Appendix B
Computation of the BBGKY equations

Start by denoting
\commutator{A}{B}_{\pm}:=AB\pm BA
for any quantum operators
A
and
B
where, for generality purposes, we also consider the anticommutator case throughout the text. Remember also that same-site Pauli operators satisfy the product relation
2

\sigma_{k}^{\mu}\sigma_{k}^{\nu}=\delta_{\mu\nu}+i\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\lambda}\sigma_{k}^{\lambda},

(33)

where Einstein’s summation convention applies to
\lambda\in\quantity{1,2,3}
. In particular, this implies that same-site Pauli operators of different directions
\mu\neq\nu
anticommute
\sigma_{k}^{\mu}\sigma_{k}^{\nu}=-\sigma_{k}^{\nu}\sigma_{k}^{\mu}
. Finally, define the number

\delta_{\pm}:=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if $+$}\\
1,&\text{if $-$}\end{cases}.

(34)

Ehrenfest’s theorem states that
19

i\derivative{t}\expectationvalue{\sigma^a_A}=\expectationvalue{\comm{\sigma^a_A}{H}_-}

(35)

so, using (
2
) and the bilinearity of the commutator, one is interested in evaluating

\begin{split}&\commutator{\sigma^a_A}{\sigma^b_B}_{\pm}=\\
&=\quantity(\prod_{i \in A \setminus B} \sigma^{a_i}_i)\commutator{\prod_{i \in A \cap B} \sigma^{a_i}_i}{\prod_{j \in A \cap B} \sigma^{b_j}_j}_{\pm}\quantity(\prod_{j \in B \setminus A} \sigma^{b_j}_j)\\
&=\quantity(\prod_{i \in A \setminus B} \sigma^{a_i}_i)\quantity(\prod_{j \in B \setminus A} \sigma^{b_j}_j)\\
&\quad\cdot\quantity(\prod_{k \in A \cap B} \sigma^{a_k}_k \sigma^{b_k}_k \pm\prod_{k \in A \cap B} \sigma^{b_k}_k \sigma^{a_k}_k)\\
&=\quantity(1 \pm(-1)^{d^{ab}_{AB}})\\
&\quad\cdot\quantity(\prod_{i \in A \setminus B} \sigma^{a_i}_i)\quantity(\prod_{j \in B \setminus A} \sigma^{b_j}_j)\quantity(\prod_{k \in A \cap B} \sigma^{a_k}_k \sigma^{b_k}_k)\\
&=i^{d^{ab}_{AB}}\quantity(1 \pm(-1)^{d^{ab}_{AB}})\\
&\quad\cdot\quantity(\prod_{i \in A \setminus B} \sigma^{a_i}_i)\quantity(\prod_{j \in B \setminus A} \sigma^{b_j}_j)\prod_{k\in A\cap B}\quantity(\delta_{a_k b_k} + \eps_{a_k b_k c} \sigma_k^c)\\
&=i^{\delta_{\pm}}f^{\pm ab}_{AB}\\
&\quad\cdot\quantity(\prod_{i \in A \setminus B} \sigma^{a_i}_i)\quantity(\prod_{j \in B \setminus A} \sigma^{b_j}_j)\prod_{k\in A\cap B}\quantity(\delta_{a_k b_k} + \eps_{a_k b_k c} \sigma_k^c).\end{split}

(36)

In the first equality, we partition
A\cup B=(A\setminus B)\cup(B\setminus A)\cup(A\cap B)
and extract the
A\setminus B
and
B\setminus A
portions of the Pauli strings from the commutator. In the third equality, the anticommutation of same-site Paulis picks up a
(-1)
factor if their directions differ, leading to the definition of the number of different directions
d^{ab}_{AB}
in (
8
). In the fourth equality, all imaginary units
i
are extracted because the sum in the last product is exclusive, meaning that for every element of
A\cap B
either the Kronecker delta or the Levi-Civita symbol part is picked. Finally, in the last equality, we compute

\begin{split}&i^{d^{ab}_{AB}}\quantity(1 \pm(-1)^{d^{ab}_{AB}})=\\
&=i^{\delta_{\pm}}i^{d^{ab}_{AB}-\delta_{\pm}}\cdot\begin{cases}2,&\text{if $(d_{AB}^{ab}-\delta_{\pm})\in 2\mathbb{N}_{0}$}\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\\
&=i^{\delta_{\pm}}\underbrace{2(-1)^{(d_{AB}^{ab}-\delta_{\pm})/2}\cdot\begin{cases}1,&\text{if $(d_{AB}^{ab}-\delta_{\pm})\in 2\mathbb{N}_{0}$}\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}}_{=:f^{\pm ab}_{AB}},\end{split}

(37)

leading to the definition of the generalized factor
f^{\pm ab}_{AB}
, so that
f_{AB}^{ab}:=f_{AB}^{-ab}
in (
7
). The generalized factor fully controls the result of the commutation/anticommutation

f_{AB}^{\pm ab}=0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\commutator{\sigma^a_A}{\sigma^b_B}_{\pm}=0.

(38)

Hence, for the
\delta_{-}=1
commutation choice, to obtain
\commutator{\sigma^a_A}{\sigma^b_B}_{-}\neq 0
it must be
d_{AB}^{ab}\geq 1
, implying
A\cap B\neq\varnothing
. In such cases, the
i^{\delta_{-}}=i
factor cancels the imaginary unit present in Ehrenfest’s theorem, in accordance with the fact that expectation values are real-valued quantities.

Appendix C
Connections of the BBGKY hierarchy

Let
\sigma_{A}^{a}
and
\sigma_{C}^{c}
be two Pauli strings. We define

\begin{split}&\sigma^{a}_{A}\to\sigma^{c}_{C}\quad\xLeftrightarrow{\text{def}}\\
&\exists((B,b),\gamma)\in\mathcal{H}\times(\mathbb{C}\setminus\quantity{0})\colon\commutator{\sigma_A^a}{\sigma_B^b}_{\pm}=\gamma\sigma_{C}^{c}.\end{split}

(39)

It is always the case that
\commutator{\sigma_A^a}{\sigma_B^b}_{\pm}
is proportional to a single Pauli string because of the previous Appendix
B
computation. Moreover, the proportionality constant
\gamma\neq 0
simultaneously ensures that
f^{\pm ab}_{AB}\neq 0
and that there exists a
t\in[0,T]
such that
h_{B}^{b}(t)\neq 0
.

We now show that
\sigma^{a}_{A}\to\sigma^{c}_{C}
implies
\sigma^{c}_{C}\to\sigma^{a}_{A}
. Assuming
\sigma^{a}_{A}\to\sigma^{c}_{C}
, multiply
\commutator{\sigma_A^a}{\sigma_B^b}_{\pm}=\gamma\sigma_{C}^{c}
respectively from the left and from the right by the (fixed)
\sigma_{B}^{b}
to, respectively, obtain

\begin{split}\sigma_{B}^{b}\sigma_{A}^{a}\sigma_{B}^{b}\pm\sigma_{A}^{a}&=\gamma\sigma_{B}^{b}\sigma_{C}^{c},\\
\sigma_{A}^{a}\pm\sigma_{B}^{b}\sigma_{A}^{a}\sigma_{B}^{b}&=\gamma\sigma_{C}^{c}\sigma_{B}^{b},\end{split}

(40)

where we used
(\sigma_{A}^{a})^{2}=(\sigma_{B}^{b})^{2}=1
. Combining the above two equations gives

\gamma\commutator{\sigma_C^c}{\sigma_B^b}_{\pm}=2\sigma_{A}^{a}\pm 2\sigma_{B}^{b}\sigma_{A}^{a}\sigma_{B}^{b}.

(41)

We now show that the above is not null. Assume by contradiction that
\sigma_{A}^{a}\pm\sigma_{B}^{b}\sigma_{A}^{a}\sigma_{B}^{b}=0
, then by multiplying both sides from the right by
\sigma_{B}^{b}
we obtain
\commutator{\sigma_A^a}{\sigma_B^b}_{\pm}=0
, in contradiction with the initial
\gamma\neq 0
assumption. By the previous Appendix
B
computation,
\gamma\commutator{\sigma_C^c}{\sigma_B^b}_{\pm}\neq 0
must be proportional to a single Pauli string as well. Finally, since
\sigma_{A}^{a}\neq\sigma_{B}^{b}\sigma_{A}^{a}\sigma_{B}^{b}
, then
\sigma_{B}^{b}\sigma_{A}^{a}\sigma_{B}^{b}
must be proportional to
\sigma_{A}^{a}
by a non-unit proportionality constant, leading to
\commutator{\sigma_C^c}{\sigma_B^b}_{\pm}
being proportional to
\sigma_{A}^{a}
, which is the desired result.

Appendix D
Bounds on correlator lengths

Let
L^{\pm ab}_{AB}
denote the length of the
\bigl\langle\commutator{\sigma^a_A}{\sigma^b_B}_{\pm}\bigr\rangle
correlator, meaning
L^{ab}_{AB}:=L^{-ab}_{AB}
in Sec.
II.3
. We assume
f_{AB}^{\pm ab}\neq 0
so, from Appendix
B
, in the
\delta_{-}=1
case it must be

A\cap B\neq\varnothing
. This leads to the lower bound

\delta_{\pm}\leq\absolutevalue{A \cap B}\leq\min(\absolutevalue{A},\absolutevalue{B}),

(42)

the upper bound being a trivial inequality.

If all directions in
A\cap B
differ, then
\commutator{\sigma^a_A}{\sigma^b_B}_{\pm}
is composed of at most
\absolutevalue{A \cup B}
Pauli operators. Likewise, if all directions in
A\cap B
match, then
\commutator{\sigma^a_A}{\sigma^b_B}_{\pm}
is composed of at least
\absolutevalue{A \Delta B}+\delta_{\pm}
Pauli operators, where the addition of
\delta_{\pm}
stems directly from the
d^{ab}_{AB}\geq 1
requirement in the
\delta_{-}=1
case, and where
A\Delta B:=(A\setminus B)\cup(B\setminus A)=(A\cup B)\setminus(A\cap B)
is the symmetric set difference. Therefore,
L^{\pm ab}_{AB}
is bounded by

\absolutevalue{A}+\absolutevalue{B}-2\absolutevalue{A \cap B}+\delta_{\pm}\leq L^{\pm ab}_{AB}\leq\absolutevalue{A}+\absolutevalue{B}-\absolutevalue{A \cap B}.

(43)

By using (
42
) and the identity
\absolutevalue{\abs{A} - \abs{B}}=\absolutevalue{A}+\absolutevalue{B}-2\min(\absolutevalue{A},\absolutevalue{B})

66
, the tightest bounds for
L^{\pm ab}_{AB}
become

\absolutevalue{\abs{A} - \abs{B}}+\delta_{\pm}\leq L^{\pm ab}_{AB}\leq\absolutevalue{A}+\absolutevalue{B}-\delta_{\pm}.

(44)

As a check, notice how the lower bound is always non-negative. Also, we check that in both
\delta_{\pm}\in\quantity{0,1}
cases the upper bound is always bigger than or equal to the lower one. Indeed, in the
\delta_{+}=0
case we recover the triangle inequality, while in the
\delta_{-}=1
case either
\absolutevalue{A}\geq\absolutevalue{B}
, implying
\absolutevalue{B}\geq 1
, or
\absolutevalue{B}\geq\absolutevalue{A}
, implying
\absolutevalue{A}\geq 1
.

Appendix E
Derivation of the BBGKY action

The equilibrium configuration
\overrightarrow{\mathbf{\chi}}
is such that
\forall(A,a)\in\mathcal{Q}_{R}:\forall s\in\quantity{0,\dots,N_\text{T}}:E_{A}^{a}(s,\overrightarrow{\mathbf{\chi}})=0
, with fixed boundary conditions
\forall q\in\quantity{1, \dots, \abs{\mathcal{Q}_R}}:\chi_{q0}=\bar{x}_{q0}
. Then, for any
q
and

\begin{split}&\chi_{qs}=\int\differential{\va{x}}x_{qs}\prod_{(A,a)\in\mathcal{Q}_{R}}\quantity(\prod_{s'=0}^{N_\text{T}} \delta(E^{a}_A (s', \va{x})))\\
&=\quantity(\prod_{s'=0}^{N_\text{T}} \lim_{\lambda_{Aa}^{s'} \to\infty})\int\differential{\va{x}}x_{qs}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}(A,a)\in\mathcal{Q}_{R}\\

&=\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\sqrt{\frac{\lambda T}{\pi N_{\text{T}}}}^{\absolutevalue{\mathcal{Q}_R}(N_{\text{T}}+1)}\\
&\qquad\quad\cdot\int\differential{\va{x}}x_{qs}\exp(-\lambda\Delta t \sum_{(A, a) \in\mathcal{Q}_R} \sum_{s'=0}^{N_\text{T}} E_A^{a}(s', \va{x})^2)\\
&=\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\int\differential{\va{x}}(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}})_{qs}w(\lambda)e^{-\lambda S(\overrightarrow{\mathbf{x}})}.\end{split}

(45)

In the second equality, we represent the Dirac delta distributions with
2

\delta_{\lambda}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{\pi}}e^{-\lambda x^{2}},

(46)

where
\lambda

(A,a)

\lambda

Appendix F
Variance of Z-measurements

Let
B_{1},\dots,B_{N_{\text{S}}}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal{B}(p)
be
N_{\text{S}}
discrete Bernoulli random variables of identical parameter
p\in[0,1]
. Then, for any
1\leq i\leq N_{\text{S}}
, the discrete
X_{i}:=2B_{i}-1
random variable represents a Z-measurement (or one-shot measurement) of a quantum observable, whose explicit definition fixes the value of
p
. The expectation value
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=2p-1\in[-1,1]
of
X_{i}
corresponds to the expectation value of the quantum observable, and one can find that

\text{Var}[X_{i}]=4p(1-p)=1-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]^{2}\in[0,1].

(47)

Given the realization
\bar{x}_{qs}
of
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]
, we pick the realization
y^{2}_{qs}=1-\bar{x}^{2}_{qs}
of
\text{Var}[X_{i}]
as the standard deviation
y_{qs}
of the Gaussian penalty term in (
19
) because we want
x_{qs}
to be a realization of a normally distributed
\mathcal{N}(\bar{x}_{qs},y^{2}_{qs})
continuous random variable. Indeed, we do not want
x_{qs}
to be a realization of the average
A:=\sum^{N_{\text{S}}}_{j=1}X_{j}/N_{\text{S}}
discrete random variable, because in that case
\text{Var}[A]=\text{Var}[X_{i}]/N_{\text{S}}
, and as a result
y_{qs}
would tend to zero in the
N_{\text{S}}\to\infty
limit. This allows
x_{qs}
to probe a fixed range around the noisy
\bar{x}_{qs}
, whose quantum error is independent of shot noise.

Divisions by zero in (
19
) can still occur if
\bar{x}_{qs}\in\quantity{\pm 1}
, leading to
y_{qs}=0
, which can happen with an unlikely (but still possible) sequence of identical Z-measurements. To avoid that, in the
\bar{x}_{qs}\in\quantity{\pm 1}
cases we slightly modify the estimation of the average used in (
20
), so that the former is forced to include an additional different Z-measurement

\bar{x}_{qs}\to\frac{N_{\text{S}}\bar{x}_{qs}-\text{sgn}(\bar{x}_{qs})}{N_{\text{S}}+1}.

(48)

One readily checks that the above expression tends to
\bar{x}_{qs}
in the
N_{\text{S}}\to\infty
limit.
