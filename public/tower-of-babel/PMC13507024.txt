TMEM16E-mediated macropinocytosis promotes cell survival under acidic stress
Authors: Jung-Eun Kim, Byoung-Cheol Lee, Byung-Chang Suh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
TMEM16E is a transmembrane protein that functions both as a phospholipid scramblase and a non-selective ion channel, playing a critical role in cellular ion transport and membrane dynamics. Recent studies have shown that the TMEM16E scramblase also facilitates membrane internalization through macropinocytosis. This study investigates the effects of extracellular protons on TMEM16E's scrambling activity and subsequent macropinocytosis under acidic conditions, which are particularly relevant in pathophysiological contexts such as muscular dystrophies and cancers. Our results indicate that TMEM16E-induced macropinocytosis, as evidenced by the internalization of annexin V, is significantly enhanced in acidic environments (pH 5.5). However, the overall number of macropinosomes, assessed using 70 kDa dextran, remained unchanged despite variations in extracellular pH. This suggests that TMEM16E-mediated macropinocytosis operates independently of extracellular proton concentrations. Upon extracellular acidification, both TMEM16E scrambling activity and macropinocytosis were rapidly inhibited, leading to a swift decrease in intracellular Ca2+ levels compared to physiological conditions. Notably, intracellular Ca2+ was cleared more quickly in acidic environments, indicating a regulatory role for the proton-dependent Ca2+ clearance pathways. Using wound healing and MTS assays, we demonstrated that TMEM16E expression significantly enhances cell proliferation and survival under acidic conditions. Our findings underscore the importance of TMEM16E-mediated macropinocytosis in maintaining plasma membrane integrity and promoting cell survival, highlighting its role as a crucial signaling pathway in both physiological and pathological contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-026-06291-1.

Introduction

TMEM16 proteins play a crucial role in various physiological processes, including ion transport, phospholipid scrambling, ionic conductance, and the regulation of membrane proteins [1]. Among these, TMEM16E is notable for its dual function as both a phospholipid scramblase and a non-selective ion channel, with its activity modulated by Ca2+ levels. It is predominantly localized in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) but is also expressed at the plasma membrane [2]. In humans, TMEM16E exhibits high expression levels in tissues such as bone, skeletal and cardiac muscles, cerebellar granule cells, and the testis [3]. Notably, the knockdown of TMEM16E has been linked to defects in nuclear positioning and compromised intracellular Ca2+ signaling [4]. Furthermore, mutations in TMEM16E have been associated with muscular dystrophies, including Miyoshi muscular dystrophy 3 (MMD3) and limb-girdle muscular dystrophy R12 (LGMDR12), and a bone disorder known as gnathodiaphyseal dysplasia (GDD) [2, 4]. The activation of phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) by TMEM16E and its direct interaction with growth factor receptors also suggests a functional role in promoting cancer cell development and proliferation [5–8].

Maintaining acid–base homeostasis is vital for sustaining life, as H+ ions are essential for regulating protein and membrane functions [9]. Consequently, extracellular pH (pHe) is a key determinant of the cellular environment, requiring precise regulation to support normal cellular activities [10, 11]. pHe imbalances can arise from various factors, including metabolic and respiratory acidosis/alkalosis, particularly in pathological conditions such as cancer, muscular dystrophies, stress responses, aging, and aberrant protein phase separation [12–16]. Additionally, intense physical exercise can lead to proton accumulation in skeletal muscle through ATP hydrolysis [17], highlighting the importance of understanding the molecular mechanisms by which protons influence protein function in physiological conditions. Recent studies have shown that Ca2+-activated chloride channels (CaCC) are modulated by both intracellular and extracellular protons [18, 19]. Specifically, TMEM16 family members, including TMEM16A and TMEM16F, are regulated by protons [20–22]. Intracellular protons inhibit the activity of TMEM16A and TMEM16F by competing with Ca2+ for binding at the Ca2+-binding site [21, 22], while extracellular protons enhance the ion channel activity of TMEM16A by increasing its open probability [20].

Our recent research indicated that TMEM16E-dependent scramblase activity facilitates macropinocytosis through the activation of growth factor receptors (GFRs) in a ligand-independent manner, which is crucial for restoring membrane asymmetry [5]. Macropinocytosis is a clathrin-independent endocytic pathway driven by actin polymerization, leading to the formation of large vesicles known as macropinosomes that engulf substantial volumes of extracellular fluid and membrane components [23]. This process plays a pivotal role in various cellular functions, including nutrient scavenging to support cancer cell growth, antigen presentation in immune cells, and the regulation of plasma membrane tension [23, 24]. Importantly, macropinocytosis is intimately linked to pH regulation. The formation of membrane ruffles, a prerequisite for macropinosome closure, is highly dependent on the activity of Na+/H+ exchangers (NHEs), which regulate submembranous pH [25]. Studies have shown that submembrane pH microdomains modulate the actin dynamics essential for ruffling and macropinosome formation [25]. Furthermore, extracellular acidosis, often found in tumor microenvironments, has been reported to influence endocytic traffic and efficiency, suggesting that macropinocytosis is highly responsive to the proton environment [26].

In this study, we explore the effects of extracellular protons on TMEM16E by assessing its scrambling and ion channel activities across varying extracellular proton concentrations. The muscular dystrophies, bone disorders, and tumor growth associated with TMEM16E can lead to systemic acid–base imbalances due to the accumulation of protons in tissues where energy metabolism is disrupted [12]. Macropinocytosis supports cell survival and growth by enabling nutrient uptake and activating growth signaling pathways such as GFRs, mTORC1, and PI3K [27]. In this study, we found that membrane-targeted annexin V (AV), a marker for phosphatidylserine (PS) binding, is rapidly and completely internalized under acidic extracellular pH conditions. Our data also reveal that intracellular Ca2+ levels are swiftly cleared in acidic environments. Based on these findings, we propose that the scramblase activity of TMEM16E diminishes rapidly at acidic pH due to the activation of a fast Ca2+ clearance pathway, while the non-selective macropinocytosis of fluid and membranes remains unchanged. Together, these results demonstrate that the preservation of macropinocytic activity and the termination of scrambling activity at lower pH critically contribute to maintaining normal membrane asymmetry and cell viability in acidic environments, such as those found in cancer and muscular dystrophies.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and transfection

HEK293T cells, which are large T-antigen transformed HEK293 cells, PAC knockout (KO) cells, and TMEM16F KO derived from HEK293T were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; HyClone, Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (HyClone, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 0.2% penicillin/streptomycin (HyClone, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The cultures were maintained under standard growth conditions at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Transient transfection of HEK293T WT, PAC KO, and TMEM16F KO cells was performed using the TransIT-X2 Dynamic Delivery System (MIRUS) following the manufacturer's protocol. The PAC gene was deleted in HEK293T cells using the CRISPR/Cas9 method [28, 29]. For transfection, cells were plated in a 35-mm culture dish at 50% to 60% confluency and were transfected with the following constructs: 50 ng of green fluorescent protein (GFP)-N1, 1000 ng of human TMEM16E-GFP and TMEM16E-mCherry, 1000 ng of M1R, 50 ng of mCherry-N1, 100 ng of Lyn-mCherry (Lyn-mCh), and 300 ng of pleckstrin homology domain of phospholipase C-δ1 (PLCδ1) labeled with mCherry (PH-PLCδ1-mCh). Forty-eight hours post-transfection, the cells were seeded onto poly-L-lysine-coated coverslip chips for subsequent live-cell imaging and voltage-clamp recordings.

Plasmids and chemicals

Human cDNA clones of TMEM16E (GenBank accession no. NM_001410964.1) were kindly provided by Anna Boccaccio from the Institute of Biophysics, Rome, Italy. The mouse M1 receptor (M1R) (GenBank accession no. NM_001112697) was obtained from Neil M. Nathanson at the University of Washington, Seattle, WA. The PH-PLCδ1-mCh construct was provided by Bertil Hille at the University of Washington School of Medicine, Seattle, WA, USA. The mCherry-N1 vectors were sourced from Clontech. The following chemicals were utilized in the study: Amiloride hydrochloride (Tocris), 5-(N-Ethyl-N-isopropyl) amiloride (EIPA, TargetMol), Alexa Fluor 568 and Alexa Fluor 680 (Invitrogen), Fluo-4 AM (Invitrogen), Calbryte-590 (AAT Bioquest), tetramethylrhodamine (TRITC)-Dextran 70,000 MW (Sigma-Aldrich), afatinib (BIBW 2992, Selleckchem), imatinib (STI571, Selleckchem), wortmannin (Enzo), oxotremorine-M (Oxo-M; Research Biochemicals), 5(6)-Carboxyeosin (CE; Abcam), SEA0400 (TargetMol), and additional chemicals from Sigma-Aldrich.

Reverse transcription (RT)-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from various organs of Sprague–Dawley rats aged postnatal days 5 to 6 (P5-P6) using QIAzol Lysis Reagent (QIAGEN) in conjunction with 2 M sodium acetate. Reverse transcription reactions were conducted utilizing the TOPscript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Enzynomics) following the manufacturer's instructions. The endogenous expression levels of TMEM16E were normalized to the housekeeping gene β-actin. Image analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

Confocal microscopy

All images were acquired using a Carl Zeiss LSM 700 confocal microscope (ZEISS) at room temperature (22–25 °C). Cell images were scanned with a 40X (water) objective lens at a resolution of 1024 × 1024 pixels, utilizing digital zoom. For time-course analyses, scramblase assay images were captured every 30 s using Zen Black imaging software at a resolution of 512 × 512 pixels, also with digital zoom. Cells were maintained in a standard external solution composed of 140 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM CaCl₂, and 10 mM HEPES, adjusted to pH 7.4 with NaOH. External solutions with varying pH levels were prepared using 10 mM MES (pH 5.5 and pH 6.5) and 10 mM Bicine (pH 8.5). To evaluate scrambling activity, regions of interest (ROIs) encompassing the whole cell, plasma membrane, or cytoplasm were selected in Zen software (Black Edition, ZEISS). The number of macropinosomes and macropinocytosis activity were assessed by calculating the fluorescent intensity of TRITC-Dextran puncta using ImageJ software. All images were converted to JPEG format, and raw data from the time-course experiments were processed using Microsoft Office Excel 365 and Igor Pro (WaveMetrics).

Scramblase assay

Scramblase activity was evaluated through live-cell imaging of annexin V (AV)-conjugated to Alexa Fluor-568, diluted 1:200 (Invitrogen). The scramblase activity of TMEM16E was stimulated by elevating intracellular Ca2+ levels using either 1 μM A23187 (Sigma-Aldrich), a Ca2+ ionophore, or 50 μM ATP. In most experiments, cells were incubated for 15 min with 1 μM A23187 or 50 μM ATP, along with AV (diluted 1:200) and an extracellular Ca2+ concentration of 2.5 mM. In specific experiments involving M1R-transfected cells, scramblase activity was triggered by treatment with 10 μM oxotremorine-M (Oxo-M). The scrambling activity was recorded via time-lapse imaging over 15 min, with a 30-s acquisition interval.

Calcium imaging

To measure intracellular Ca2+ levels, TMEM16E-mCherry (mCh) transfected cells on coverslips were pre-incubated with 2.5 mM Ca2+ in extracellular solutions adjusted to pH 7.4 with 10 mM HEPES, containing 4 μM Fluo-4 AM (Invitrogen) for 10 min at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere. Prior to applying chemical reagents, Fluo-4 AM was loaded to the cells. Time-lapse imaging of cytoplasmic fluorescent intensity was conducted at a resolution of 512 × 512 pixels, capturing images every 5, 10, or 15 s. For the measurement of intracellular Ca2+ influx, regions of interest (ROIs) were selected in the cytoplasmic region of the cells using Zen software (Black Edition, ZEISS).

Cell lysis and co-immunoprecipitation

HEK293T cells were seeded in a 100-mm cell culture dish (Falcon) at 50% to 60% confluency in a standard cell culture medium. The following day, cells were starved for 4 h in DMEM and then co-transfected with 10 μg of TMEM16E-GFP and 4 μg of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-HAx3 using the TransIT-X2 Dynamic Delivery System (MIRUS). After 6 h, the DMEM was replaced with fresh cell culture medium. Two days post-transfection, cells were treated with 2.5 mM Ca2+ in extracellular solutions adjusted to pH 7.4 (10 mM HEPES) or pH 5.5 (10 mM MES) for 1 min before cell lysis. Cell lysates were prepared using co-immunoprecipitation lysis buffer, consisting of 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0) (Duchefa Biochemie), 1 mM EDTA (Calbiochem), 50 mM NaCl (Sigma-Aldrich), 1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich), and 10% glycerol (Duchefa Biochemie), supplemented with 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF, Sigma-Aldrich) and a 1 × Halt protease & phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cell lysates were vortexed and incubated three times for 10 min at 4 °C, followed by centrifugation at 13,200 rpm for 15 min. Protein quantification was performed using a Pierce BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). To prepare immune complexes, 2 mg of protein lysates were mixed with 2 μg of rabbit anti-HA or control rabbit IgG (R&D Systems) antibody and rotated overnight at 4 °C. Immune complexes were collected by incubation with Protein A/G-PLUS Agarose beads (Santa Cruz) at 4 °C with agitation for 6 h. The immune complexes bound to agarose beads were centrifuged and washed three times with wash buffer containing 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 1 mM EDTA, 250 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, and 10% glycerol. The protein complexes were then mixed with 40 μL of co-immunoprecipitation lysis buffer and 10 μL of sample buffer (Biostem MENTOS) containing 250 mM Tris–HCl (pH 6.8), 0.5 M DTT, 10% SDS, 50% glycerol, and 0.2% bromophenol blue, and denatured at 37 °C for 30 min using a heating shaker incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Western blotting

The PVDF membrane was then incubated in a blocking buffer containing 5% skim milk powder (Sigma-Aldrich) and 1 × TBS-T at room temperature for 1 h. Next, the membrane was washed three times with 10 mL of 1 × TBS-T. The membrane was treated with primary antibodies: mouse anti-GFP (1:100, Santa Cruz sc-9996), mouse anti-HA (1:200, Santa Cruz sc-7392), and rabbit anti-GAPDH (1:25,000, Cell Signaling Technology #2955) supplemented with 3% BSA (MP Biomedicals) in 1 × TBS-T overnight at 4 °C. Afterward, the membrane was washed three times with 10 mL of 1 × TBS-T. Following the removal of the primary antibodies, the membrane was incubated with secondary antibodies: anti-mouse IgG (1:10,000, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and anti-rabbit IgG (1:10,000, Thermo Fisher Scientific) in 5% blocking buffer at room temperature for 1 h and washed three times. Western blots were developed using electrochemiluminescence solutions (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and signals were collected with X-ray film. Images were analyzed using ImageJ software.

Electrophysiological recordings

Whole-cell voltage-clamp recordings of TMEM16E currents were performed at room temperature (22 to 25 °C) in PAC KO HEK293T cells using a HEKA EPC-10 amplifier with Pulse software (HEKA Elektronik). Electrode pipettes, pulled from glass micropipette capillaries (Sutter Instrument), had resistances ranging from 2 to 4 MΩ. Series-resistance errors were compensated by 60%. TMEM16E currents were recorded with a membrane holding potential of − 60 mV, applying a 500 ms test pulse. All current data were analyzed using Igor Pro 6.0 (WaveMetrics). The external Ringer’s solution contained 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgCl2, and 10 mM glucose, with the pH adjusted to 7.4 using 10 mM HEPES or to 5.5 using 10 mM MES, adjusted with NaOH. The internal pipette solution consisted of 130 mM CsCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 10 mM EGTA, 3 mM Na2ATP, and 10 mM HEPES, with the pH adjusted to 7.35 using CsOH. For the pipette solution containing 3 μM free Ca2+, 4.7 mM CaCl2 was added, and the Ca2+ chelator was replaced with 10 mM HEDTA-Na3. The free Ca2+ concentration was calculated using the Ca2+/Mg2+/ATP/EGTA/HEDTA calculator, version 2.2b, available at WebMaxC.

Wound healing assay

To measure cell migration and proliferation, HEK293T cells were transiently transfected with either the GFP N1 vector or TMEM16E-GFP using the TransIT-X2 Dynamic Delivery System (MIRUS). Forty-eight hours post-transfection, GFP-positive cells were sorted using fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) on a BD FACSAria III. Cells were then plated at a density of 80,000 cells per well in a 24-well plate. Cell monolayers were scratched using a 200 μL yellow tip and washed twice with DPBS to remove cell debris. Subsequently, the cells were treated with solutions at pH 7.4 or pH 5.5, with or without 1 μM A23187, for durations of 0, 6, 24, and 48 h in a CO2 incubator to measure the closure of the scratched area.

MTS assay

For the cell viability assay, HEK293T cells were transiently transfected with the GFP N1 vector of TMEM16E-GFP using the TransIT-X2 Dynamic Delivery System (MIRUS). Forty-eight hours post-transfection, GFP-positive cells were sorted using FACS on a BD FACSAria III. Cell proliferation and viability were assessed using a 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−5(3-carboxymethoxyphenyl)−2-(4-sulfophenyl)−2H-tetrazolium (MTS) assay (CellTiter 96 AQueous One Solution, Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For this assay, cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 8,000 cells per well. Once the cells reached approximately 50% to 60% confluency, they were exposed to external solutions at pH 7.4 or pH 5.5, with or without 1 μM A23187, for durations of 0, 6, 24, and 48 h.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Microsoft Office Excel 365 (Microsoft), Igor Pro 6.0 (WaveMetrics), Zen software (black edition, ZEISS), ImageJ-Fiji software (NIH), GraphPad Prism 7.02 (GraphPad Software), or MATLAB R2024b. Statistical data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test. The significance of observations across multiple groups was assessed using one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. Differences were considered significant at the following levels: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001. Each experiment was repeated independently at least three times. “n” represents the total number of cells analyzed, collected from at least three independent biological replicates.

Results

pH-dependent regulation of TMEM16E scrambling activity and annexin V (AV) internalization

First, we investigated TMEM16E scrambling activity modulated by extracellular protons in TMEM16E-expressing HEK293T cells (Fig. 1). Treatment with 1 μM A23187 induced scrambling activity and triggered AV internalization. Notably, AV was partially internalized at pH 7.4 but completely internalized at pH 5.5, leading to a loss of plasma membrane signal (Fig. 1A-E). For quantitative analysis, AV fluorescence intensity was acquired using Zen software (Black Edition). We defined regions of interest (ROIs) to distinguish the plasma membrane from the cytosol, based on images where AV was targeted to the plasma membrane. Interestingly, cytoplasmic AV intensity significantly decreased at pH 6.5, whereas the intensity at pH 5.5 was comparable to pH 7.4 (Fig. 1D). Consistent with previous reports [2, 3], RT-PCR analysis of rat tissues confirmed high endogenous expression of TMEM16E in skeletal muscle (Fig. S1A, B). We also assessed basal activity; in the absence of A23187, no scrambling was induced across all pH solutions, confirming that the activity is strictly dependent on TMEM16E and elevated intracellular Ca2+ (Fig. S1C-E). Finally, treatment with 3 mM amiloride dramatically inhibited AV internalization at all pH levels (Fig. 1F-H and Fig. S2), indicating that AV is internalized via TMEM16E-mediated macropinocytosis even under acidic conditions.

Since HEK293T cells endogenously express the proton-activated chloride (PAC) channel [32], we utilized PAC knockout (KO) HEK293T cells to assess TMEM16E activity under varying extracellular proton levels [28]. We found no significant difference in ion channel activity between pH 7.4 and pH 5.5 in TMEM16E-expressing PAC KO cells, a stability not observed in non-transfected controls (Fig. S3A-B). Additionally, the scrambling activity in PAC KO cells mirrored the pattern seen in wild-type HEK293T cells across different pH conditions (Fig. S3C-E). This suggests that the PAC channel does not affect TMEM16E scrambling activity or AV internalization patterns at low extracellular pH.

It is reported that HEK293T cells endogenously express TMEM16F, a major Ca2+-dependent phospholipid scramblase [33]. To verify that the observed phenomena were not influenced by endogenous TMEM16F, we examined scrambling activity and AV internalization in TMEM16F KO HEK293T cells [29]. These KO cells exhibit constitutive GFP expression due to the selection marker integration. Consistent with WT cells, TMEM16F KO cells showed partial AV internalization at pH 7.4 and complete internalization at pH 5.5 upon A23187 stimulation. This internalization was completely abolished by amiloride (Fig. S4A, C-D). Additionally, we monitored the percentage of AV-positive cells and membrane blebs. While the percentage of AV-positive cells showed no significant difference between control and amiloride-treated groups, the percentage of membrane blebs significantly increased with amiloride treatment regardless of pH (Fig. S4B). These findings confirm that the pH-dependent AV internalization is mediated by TMEM16E, independent of TMEM16F.

Extracellular acidosis induces the enlargement of TMEM16E-mediated macropinosomes without influencing macropinosome intensity

Extracellular pH is known to directly affect the functional properties of membrane proteins such as CaCC, TMEM16A, and TMEM16F [18–22]. Our observation that membrane-targeted AV is completely internalized under acidic conditions (Fig. 1) prompted us to investigate whether this results from altered macropinocytosis or inhibition of TMEM16E scramblase activity. First, we assessed macropinocytosis activity under varying pH conditions. To quantify only the cytosolic AV fluorescence, we first induced TMEM16E scrambling with 1 μM A23187 for 15 min, and then removed surface-bound AV by washing with a Ca2+-free solution containing 10 mM EGTA for 2 min. The results showed that cytosolic AV intensity at pH 5.5 was comparable to that at pH 7.4, whereas it was significantly reduced at pH 6.5 (Fig. 2A and B). However, considering the increased net charge and binding affinity of AV at pH 5.5, the high intensity observed at this pH likely reflects an artifactual enhancement of fluorescence rather than increased macropinocytic uptake. Consequently, we concluded that assessing macropinocytosis solely based on AV internalization is unreliable under these conditions. To address the limitations of the AV assay, we monitored macropinocytosis using 70 kDa fluorescent dextran. Unlike smaller variants (e.g., 10 kDa), high-molecular-weight dextran (70 kDa) is preferentially internalized via amiloride-sensitive macropinocytosis, making it a specific marker for this pathway [34]. We assessed macropinocytosis in TMEM16E-expressing cells treated with 1 μM A23187 under varying extracellular pH conditions (Fig. 2C-F). Macropinosomes were categorized by diameter into small (0.2 ≤ d < 0.5 μm), medium (0.5 ≤ d < 1.0 μm), and large (d ≥ 1.0 μm) groups. Distribution analysis revealed a significant increase in the proportion of large macropinosomes under acidic conditions compared to physiological pH (Fig. 2D and F). Interestingly, despite the increase in size, the fluorescence intensity of cytosolic TRITC-Dextran puncta remained unchanged across all pH levels (Fig. 2E). Since macropinosomes typically undergo rapid shrinkage and cargo concentration during maturation [35, 36], the presence of enlarged vesicles with stable fluorescence intensity suggests that extracellular acidosis delays this shrinkage process rather than altering the uptake mechanism itself. These findings indicate that while extracellular protons influence macropinosome maturation dynamics, the fundamental efficiency of TMEM16E-driven macropinocytosis remains robust.

To definitively confirm that internalized AV enters via macropinocytosis, we examined the co-localization of TRITC-Dextran (70 kDa) and Annexin V (Alexa Fluor 680) in TMEM16E-GFP expressing cells. Treatment with 1 μM A23187 induced the internalization of AV 680, which clearly co-localized with TRITC-Dextran in the cytoplasm at both pH 7.4 and pH 5.5 (Fig. S5A, top). Conversely, amiloride treatment completely abolished both dextran uptake and AV internalization (Fig. S5A, bottom). Quantitative analysis showed that the number of AV puncta co-localizing with TRITC-Dextran was significantly higher at pH 5.5 compared to pH 7.4 (Fig. S5B, C). Given that macropinocytosis activity itself is independent of extracellular pH (Fig. 2C-F), this increase is likely attributable to the enhanced PS-binding affinity of AV caused by its increased net charge at acidic pH, rather than an upregulation of macropinocytosis. These results confirm that AV is internalized via TMEM16E-mediated macropinocytosis, a pathway that effectively sequesters membrane-bound AV even under acidic conditions.

The mechanism by which extracellular protons influence AV internalization patterns without altering TMEM16E-mediated macropinocytosis remains unclear. Since TMEM16E-mediated macropinocytosis is driven by the activation of growth factor receptors (GFRs), particularly through direct interaction with EGFR [5], we investigated whether extracellular protons modulate this interaction. First, we performed co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays after inducing TMEM16E scrambling with 1 μM A23187 for 1 min. The results showed no difference in TMEM16E-EGFR interaction between pH 7.4 and pH 5.5 (Fig. 3A and B). Next, we examined the kinetics of this interaction at pH 7.4 over a time course (0, 1, 7, and 15 min). The interaction significantly peaked at 7 min but returned to basal levels by 15 min (Fig. S6A, B). We hypothesize that this transient increase reflects the incorporation of the TMEM16E-EGFR complex into forming macropinosomes, followed by the recycling of TMEM16E to the plasma membrane, which restores the interaction to baseline. Finally, comparing the interaction at 15 min revealed no significant difference between pH 7.4 and pH 5.5 (Fig. S6C, D). Subsequently, we investigated whether the downstream signaling pathways of macropinocytosis remain functional under acidic conditions. Specifically, we examined the involvement of GFRs (EGFR and PDGFR) and phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) in TMEM16E-mediated macropinocytosis at pH 5.5. We treated TMEM16E-GFP expressing cells with specific inhibitors, afatinib (EGFR inhibitor) and imatinib (PDGFR inhibitor) [37–40], in an AV-binding buffer (pH 5.5) containing 1 μM A23187. Afatinib dramatically inhibited AV internalization, whereas imatinib resulted in only partial inhibition (Fig. 3C and D). Furthermore, treatment with wortmannin, a specific PI3K inhibitor, strongly suppressed AV internalization at pH 5.5 (Fig. 3E and F). These findings confirm that EGFR and PI3K are critical for TMEM16E-mediated macropinocytosis, even in acidic environments. Taken together, our results demonstrate that while extracellular protons may alter the apparent dynamics of AV uptake due to binding affinity changes, the core TMEM16E scramblase-driven macropinocytosis machinery remains fully functional and unperturbed.

Extracellular protons accelerate calcium clearance via proton-dependent mechanisms

Although TMEM16E-mediated macropinocytosis remains robust under acidic conditions, the pattern of AV internalization is markedly influenced by extracellular pH. Given that TMEM16E scramblase activity is strictly Ca2+-dependent, it is crucial to investigate how extracellular protons affect intracellular Ca2+ dynamics. We monitored intracellular Ca2+ levels using Fluo-4 AM. Upon stimulation with 1 μM A23187, intracellular Ca2+ levels surged and then decayed back to baseline much more rapidly at acidic pH (pH 5.5, τ = 100 ± 7 s; pH 6.5, τ = 109 ± 2 s) compared to pH 7.4 (Fig. 4A-C). This accelerated clearance was consistently observed over a 15-min observation period (Fig. S9A-C). To rule out the possibility that this rapid decay was an artifact of pH-dependent Fluo-4 quenching, we repeated the experiments using Calbryte-590 AM, a pH-insensitive Ca2+ indicator. Consistent with the Fluo-4 results, confocal imaging confirmed that intracellular Ca2+ levels returned to baseline significantly faster under acidic conditions (pH 5.5 and 6.5) than at pH 7.4 (Fig. S7A, B). To elucidate the mechanism underlying this rapid Ca2+ decay under acidic conditions, we investigated potential clearance pathways. Extracellular acidosis is known to modulate intracellular Ca2+ via store-operated Ca2+ channels [41] and various clearance transporters, including the Na+/Ca2+ exchanger (NCX) [42] and plasma membrane Ca2+-ATPase (PMCA) [43–45]. First, we examined the role of NCX using the specific inhibitor SEA0400. We observed no significant difference in intracellular Ca2+ clearance or TMEM16E-mediated activities (scrambling and AV internalization) compared to controls, suggesting NCX is not the primary driver (Fig. 4D-I). Next, we investigated PMCA using the inhibitor 5(6)-carboxyeosin (CE) (Fig. S8). While CE treatment elevated basal intracellular Ca2+ levels, thereby reducing the driving force for A23187-mediated Ca2+ influx, the characteristic rapid clearance pattern under acidic conditions persisted (Fig. S8A-C). This indicates that PMCA is also not responsible for the pH-dependent acceleration of Ca2+ decay. Given that NCX and PMCA are excluded, the rapid clearance is likely mediated by other complex mechanisms, such as store-operated Ca2+ entry (SOCE) [46], mitochondrial buffering, or direct Ca2+/H+ exchange activity [49, 50]. Collectively, our findings suggest that intracellular Ca2+ levels are tightly regulated by proton-sensitive clearance pathways distinct from canonical NCX or PMCA activity.

Finally, we investigated whether endogenous proton-sensing GPCRs contribute to the observed Ca2+ dynamics, as their activation is known to elevate intracellular Ca2+ via PI(4,5)P₂ depletion [51, 52]. To test this, we monitored cells co-expressing TMEM16E-Dark and the PI(4,5)P2 sensor PH-PLCδ1-mCh [53]. In the absence of A23187, switching to pH 5.5 caused no changes in membrane PI(4,5)P2 localization or intracellular Ca2+ levels (Fig. S9D-F). This confirms that proton-sensing GPCRs are not activated by extracellular protons in this context.

We also examined the effects of alkaline conditions (pH 8.5). Upon stimulation with 1 μM A23187, both TMEM16E scramblase activity and intracellular Ca2+ levels were substantially higher at pH 8.5 compared to pH 7.4 (Fig. 5A-E). However, AV internalization levels remained unchanged (Fig. 5B). This result reinforces that TMEM16E-mediated macropinocytosis operates independently of extracellular pH, even when Ca2+ and scrambling levels fluctuate. Collectively, our findings suggest that extracellular protons modulate TMEM16E scrambling activity by regulating intracellular Ca2+ via proton-dependent clearance pathways. TMEM16E-mediated macropinocytosis maintains membrane integrity under acidic stress

Our previous results showed that acidic conditions promote rapid intracellular Ca2+ clearance and the complete internalization of AV accumulated on the plasma membrane. We hypothesized that these mechanisms facilitate the repair of plasma membrane damage induced by Ca2+ overload (A23187). To test this, we monitored the percentage of AV-binding positive cells and changes in cell morphology through bright field (BF) imaging in the presence of 1 μM A23187 at various pH levels. Membrane blebs are defined as spherical protrusions of the plasma membrane indicative of cellular injury. We analyzed membrane blebs depending on TMEM16E-mediated macropinocytosis depending on extracellular protons using BF images (Fig. 7A). In control cells, the percentage of AV-positive cells decreased at lower pH (23% at pH 5.5 vs. 38% at pH 7.4), consistent with the pH-dependent AV binding affinity described earlier. Strikingly, membrane blebbing was minimal under acidic conditions (8 ± 2% at pH 5.5; 3 ± 2% at pH 6.5) compared to physiological pH (57 ± 3% at pH 7.4), suggesting enhanced cellular protection at low pH. Crucially, when macropinocytosis was blocked by amiloride, this protective effect was completely abolished. Amiloride treatment resulted in severe membrane blebbing across all pH levels (approx. 87–88%) (Fig. 7B). This phenotype was consistently observed in TMEM16F KO cells as well (Fig. S4B). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that TMEM16E-mediated macropinocytosis is essential for preventing membrane damage. Extracellular acidosis is generally known to impair cell viability by increasing cellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, up-regulating protein carbonylation, or activating the GRP4/CHOP pathways [10, 55, 56]. This condition is associated with pathological states such as cellular injury and dysfunction [57]. However, our experimental data indicate that membrane integrity is highly regulated by TMEM16E-mediated macropinocytosis under acidic conditions. To investigate this further, we measured cell migration, proliferation, and viability using wound healing assays and MTS assays in cells expressing GFP or TMEM16E (Fig. 7C-E and Fig. S11). In the wound healing assay, the percentage of wound closure did not differ between GFP- and TMEM16E-expressing cells under standard cell culture media (Fig. S11A, B). However, in pH 5.5 medium (MES-buffered), TMEM16E-expressing cells exhibited significantly enhanced wound closure compared to GFP-expressing cells at both 24 h and 48 h (Fig. S11C, D). Upon treatment with 1 μM A23187, the percentage of wound closure was significantly increased in TMEM16E-expressing cells in media adjusted to pH 7.4 with 10 mM HEPES at 24 h and 48 h. No significant difference was observed in the pH 5.5 media adjusted with 10 mM MES (Fig. 7C and D).

Since wound healing assays reflect a combination of cell migration and proliferation, we performed single-cell tracking to isolate the contribution of migration. To exclude cell division effects, cells were monitored for 4 h in Ringer’s solution (pH 7.4 or 5.5) lacking growth factors but supplemented with 10 mM glucose for ATP support. Quantitative analysis of total accumulated distance and average velocity revealed no significant differences in motility between GFP- and TMEM16E-expressing cells at either pH (Fig. S12D-F, J-L). This suggests that TMEM16E does not directly enhance cell migration machinery. However, a striking difference was observed in cellular morphology. While GFP-expressing cells exhibited extensive membrane blebbing—a sign of stress—TMEM16E-expressing cells maintained membrane integrity and displayed membrane ruffles characteristic of macropinocytosis (Fig. S12A-C, G-I). Collectively, these results indicate that the accelerated wound closure observed previously is not due to faster migration. Instead, TMEM16E-mediated suppression of membrane damage preserves cellular integrity, thereby creating a more favorable environment for cell proliferation.

To confirm that the observed benefits were driven by macropinocytosis, we employed EIPA, a specific inhibitor. We first determined the optimal dosage, as concentrations above 30 μM showed significant cytotoxicity (Fig. 7F). Consequently, we used 20 μM EIPA to achieve inhibition without compromising cell health. Under this condition, the enhanced wound closure previously seen in TMEM16E-expressing cells at pH 7.4 was effectively abolished (Fig. 7G, H). Furthermore, MTS assays revealed that TMEM16E expression significantly boosted cell viability at pH 7.4 (6 h) and, more notably, at pH 5.5 (24 h) (Fig. 7E). However, this survival advantage was significantly diminished by 20 μM EIPA (Fig. 7I). The incomplete suppression of the protective effect is likely attributable to the mild concentration of EIPA used to avoid toxicity. These results indicate a dual role for TMEM16E-mediated macropinocytosis: it supports cell proliferation under physiological conditions and becomes critical for cell survival under acidic stress. In conclusion, our findings highlight that TMEM16E-mediated macropinocytosis is a pivotal mechanism for maintaining plasma membrane integrity, thereby ensuring membrane repair, survival, and proliferation during lipid scrambling.

Discussion

TMEM16E is highly expressed in skeletal muscle, and muscle damage related to muscular diseases, as well as intense physical activity, can alter systemic or local pH balance. However, the molecular mechanisms by which extracellular protons modulate TMEM16E activity remain poorly understood. In this study, we reveal that TMEM16E scrambling and intracellular Ca2+ dynamics are tightly controlled by proton-dependent Ca2+ clearance (Fig. 8). Under physiological conditions, the activation of TMEM16E scramblase by increased intracellular Ca2+ initiates GFR activation in a ligand-independent manner, triggering PI3K activation and resulting in the formation of macropinosomes within approximately 7 min. During this process, intracellular Ca2+ homeostasis is maintained by various factors, including ER-localized TMEM16E, PMCA, Na+/Ca2+ exchanger (Fig. 8, physiological condition) [44, 58, 59]. It has been reported that the ER-localized CaCC plays a critical role in regulating intracellular Ca2+ homeostasis by serving as a counterion channel for Ca2+ release and reuptake, transporting Cl⁻ ions across the ER membrane [60]. Extracellular pH significantly modulates intracellular calcium via pH-dependent clearance mechanisms [46–50], suggesting that TMEM16E scrambling activity may be rapidly inhibited by decreasing intracellular Ca2+ levels. However, TMEM16E-mediated macropinocytosis continues to occur through ligand-independent EGFR activation by the TMEM16E scramblase (Fig. 8, acidic conditions). pH homeostasis is fundamental to cellular physiology, governing enzyme kinetics, protein stability, and ion channel function [61]. Conversely, acidosis—a hallmark of pathological states—compromises nutrient uptake and cell cycle progression, leading to reduced viability [62]. In this context, our study highlights the adaptive role of TMEM16E. While single-cell tracking ruled out migration as the driver of enhanced wound closure, TMEM16E-expressing cells exhibited significantly faster closure under physiological pH (7.4) upon Ca2+ stimulation. Given the lack of migratory difference, this acceleration is attributable to enhanced cell proliferation. Strikingly, the functional contribution of TMEM16E shifts under pathological conditions. At pH 5.5, where acidic stress stalls general wound closure, TMEM16E conferred a significant survival advantage, as evidenced by higher viability in MTS assays compared to controls. Collectively, these findings suggest a dual function for TMEM16E-mediated macropinocytosis: it facilitates proliferation under homeostatic conditions while serving as a critical survival mechanism to preserve cellular integrity under acidic stress.

An intriguing observation in our study was the specific reduction in AV fluorescence at extracellular pH 6.5, despite intracellular Ca2+ dynamics remaining unaltered (Fig. 4). This discrepancy suggests that the reduced signal is a biochemical artifact reflecting the pH-dependent modulation of AV-PS binding affinity, rather than a physiological decrease in TMEM16E scrambling activity. AV binding to PS is mediated by electrostatic interactions, typically requiring Ca2+ bridges at neutral pH [30]. However, previous studies indicate that at lower pH (≤ 5.0), protons can substitute for Ca2+, facilitating strong AV-PS binding even in the absence of calcium [31]. Our data supports a biphasic binding model: robust fluorescence is maintained at pH 7.4 via Ca2+ bridges and at pH 5.5 via protonation. In contrast, pH 6.5 likely represents a transition zone where the environment is too acidic for optimal Ca2+ bridging yet insufficiently acidic to fully engage the proton-dependent binding mechanism. Consequently, the AV probe exhibits its lowest binding affinity in this range. Importantly, the fact that macropinosome formation assessed by pH-stable dextran remained unaffected across all pH conditions (Fig. 2) confirms that TMEM16E functional activity persists, and the dip in fluorescence at pH 6.5 is attributable solely to the biophysical limitations of the AV probe.

Regulating intracellular Ca2+ homeostasis is essential for cellular function, as increased Ca2+ concentrations can disrupt cell homeostasis and lead to cell death and cytotoxicity [63]. Under physiological conditions, various factors contribute to the increase of intracellular Ca2+, including inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate (IP3) signaling, store-operated Ca2+ entry (SOCE), and purinergic signaling [54, 64, 65]. During this process, PS exposed by TMEM16E-dependent scramblase binds to AV, subsequently activating GFRs, which triggers macropinocytosis. This raises the question: how is intracellular Ca2+ regulated during TMEM16E-dependent scramblase activation upon ATP application? (Fig. 6E, F). We hypothesize that increased intracellular Ca2+ levels are restored to basal levels more rapidly in cells expressing TMEM16E. Previous studies have reported that TMEM16E, located on the ER membrane, plays a role in regulating intracellular Ca2+ [59]. It is known that ER-localized Ca2+-activated chloride channels function as counterion channels, counteracting the diffusion potential for Ca2+ release and reuptake by SERCA pumps in the ER membrane [60, 66]. Based on this information, we propose that TMEM16E significantly preserves membrane integrity and promotes cell survival by mediating macropinocytosis in the plasma membrane while effectively regulating intracellular Ca2+ levels within the ER under physiological pH conditions.

To elucidate the mechanism of rapid Ca2+ clearance under acidic conditions, we investigated the role of major extrusion pathways. While PMCA and NCX are known to contribute significantly to Ca2+ homeostasis [42–45], our inhibitor studies revealed that neither blockade was sufficient to abolish the rapid Ca2+ decay observed at low pH (Fig. 4; Fig. S8). This suggests that the mechanism extends beyond simple extrusion across the plasma membrane. Indeed, intracellular Ca2+ dynamics are heavily influenced by pH-sensitive processes, including the inhibition of influx channels (e.g., SOCE, L-type channels) [46, 47] and enhanced sequestration into organelles like the ER and mitochondria (e.g., SERCA, Ca2+/H+ exchanger) [48–50]. Specifically, the proton gradient may energetically favor Ca2+ uptake into these organelles, providing a highly efficient clearance route. Collectively, our findings indicate that the rapid reduction of intracellular Ca2+ in acidic environments is mediated by a multifaceted, proton-dependent clearance system rather than a single transporter. This mechanism likely serves a protective function, preventing prolonged Ca2+ signaling and excessive scrambling, thereby complementing the repair function of TMEM16E-mediated macropinocytosis.

Our data indicate that the rate of increase in intracellular Ca2+ is faster at low pH compared to neutral pH (7.4) (Fig. 4A-C and Fig. S9A-C). Proton-sensing G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), such as ovarian cancer G-protein-coupled receptor 1 (OGR1) and proton-activated GPCRs (GPR4, GPR65), are known to elevate intracellular Ca2+ levels by depleting PI(4,5)P2 [51, 52]. However, we did not detect any significant effects from these proton-sensing GPCRs in our experiments (Fig. S9D-F). This raises the question: why does Ca2+ increase more rapidly at acidic pH? Notably, this phenomenon was not observed in purinergic signaling induced by ATP treatment (Fig. 6E-G), but rather specifically in Ca2+ elevation induced by A23187. Previous reports suggest that an acidic extracellular environment relative to the intracellular space can accelerate Ca2+ uptake by a factor of 10 to 40 times. This occurs because intracellular H+ ions are exchanged for extracellular Ca2+, and the ionophore binds Ca2+ more effectively at lower pH [67]. Therefore, we hypothesize that the rapid increase in intracellular Ca2+ at acidic pH is specifically mediated by the action of A23187.

In previous studies, we reported that TMEM16E scramblase induces macropinocytosis by activating GFRs, specifically EGFR and PDGFR, in a ligand-independent manner [5]. In this study, we propose a molecular mechanism by which extracellular protons regulate TMEM16E scramblase and macropinocytosis. Acidosis has been shown to inhibit cell viability and reduce cell survival [10, 11, 68]. Our findings elucidate the physiological and pathological significance of TMEM16E scramblase-driven macropinocytosis. We discovered that TMEM16E-mediated macropinocytosis acts synergistically with pH-dependent Ca2+ clearance pathways in acidic environments. This interaction plays a crucial role in tightly regulating plasma membrane integrity and cell survival during scramblase activity. This study suggests that TMEM16E-mediated macropinocytosis serves as a critical signal transduction pathway, even in pathological conditions.
