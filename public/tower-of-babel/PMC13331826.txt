KIF7 promotes the proliferation of clear cell renal cell carcinoma by activating the WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway
Authors: Yao Wang, Xiao-Ming Wang, Da Huo, Shi-Bo Wang, Qian-Yi Liu, Jing Pang, Tao Shen, Di Cui, Wen-Juan Zhao, Qing-Feng Luo, Nan Li, Ai-Qun Chen, Ju Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAberrant activation of SHH signaling contributes to the progression of multiple malignancies, with KIF7 functioning as a critical mediator that regulates signal transduction from SMO to GLI transcription factors. However, the specific role of KIF7 in ccRCC has not been fully elucidated. In this study, we investigated the expression pattern, prognostic significance, functional role, and potential mechanisms of KIF7 in ccRCC. Analysis of TCGA-KIRC data demonstrated that KIF7 expression was significantly elevated in tumor tissues compared with adjacent normal tissues. High KIF7 expression was strongly associated with advanced pathological stage and poor overall survival. IHC performed on commercial tissue microarrays further confirmed increased KIF7 protein levels in ccRCC samples. Functional experiments in the 786-O cell line revealed that KIF7 overexpression markedly enhanced cell proliferation. Mechanistically, KIF7 overexpression activated the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway and upregulated downstream cell cycle regulators, including c-Myc and c-Jun. KIF7 expression was positively correlated with infiltration of CD4 + T cells, macrophages, and neutrophils. Moreover, it showed significant positive associations with immune checkpoint molecules PDCD1 and CD160. GSCA/GDSC-based drug sensitivity prediction suggested that high KIF7 expression was associated with increased predicted sensitivity to docetaxel and bleomycin, whereas no significant association was observed for I-BET-762. In conclusion, KIF7 plays a tumor-promoting role in ccRCC by enhancing proliferation, activating Wnt/β-catenin signaling. Additionally, it was associated with shaping the immune microenvironment. These findings highlight KIF7 as a potential prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target in ccRCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10238-026-02203-6.Keywords: KIF7, ccRCC, Wnt/β-catenin, Cell proliferation

Introduction

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is one of the most common malignant tumors worldwide. According to data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study, the global mortality rate associated with RCC has been increasing year by year [1]. Among its subtypes, clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) is the most prevalent histological type, accounting for approximately 75%–82% of all RCC cases [2, 3]. Although early-stage ccRCC has a relatively high cure rate, with a 5-year survival rate exceeding 90%, this rate drops sharply to around 10% once distant metastasis occurs [4, 5]. Therefore, it is crucial to further investigate the mechanisms underlying the initiation and progression of ccRCC and to identify novel diagnostic and therapeutic targets.

Recent advances have substantially expanded our understanding of RCC biology, particularly in ccRCC. Beyond the classical VHL/HIF axis, ccRCC is increasingly recognized as a molecularly heterogeneous malignancy characterized by metabolic reprogramming, aberrant signal transduction, metastatic plasticity, and variable therapeutic responses. Metabolic dysregulation represents a central feature of RCC pathogenesis and treatment resistance, involving alterations in glucose metabolism, lipid metabolism, mitochondrial function, and amino acid utilization [4]. Recent studies have further identified several metabolism-associated regulators, including TKT, FBP1, FDX1, and NCOA7, which have been implicated in ccRCC metabolism, tumor growth, metastasis, or metabolic vulnerability [3, 6–8]. In parallel, non-coding RNA-mediated regulatory networks, such as DCST1-AS1-related signaling and exosomal miR-222-3p-mediated regulation, have been reported to contribute to RCC progression and metastatic dissemination [9, 10]. Other recent studies have highlighted the involvement of PI3K/AKT-related signaling pathways in ccRCC progression, as well as emerging therapeutic concepts involving metabolic intervention, cuproptosis-associated targeting, and combinatorial immuno-radiotherapeutic strategies [11–14]. Collectively, these findings indicate that RCC progression is regulated by multiple interconnected molecular programs, thereby underscoring the need to identify novel regulators that may link developmental signaling, cytoskeletal dynamics, and oncogenic pathway activation.

The Hedgehog (Hh) signaling pathway was first discovered in Drosophila and plays a crucial role in vertebrate embryonic development, organogenesis, and cell proliferation [15, 16]. This pathway is activated by three ligands—Sonic Hedgehog (SHH), Indian Hedgehog (IHH), and Desert Hedgehog (DHH)—and transmits signals through transmembrane proteins such as Patched (PTCH) and Smoothened (SMO) to GLI family transcription factors, thereby regulating downstream gene expression [17–20]. Recent studies have demonstrated that the Sonic Hedgehog (SHH) signaling pathway is also involved in the tumorigenesis and progression of ccRCC. The mRNA and protein levels of SHH pathway-related genes, including SHH, SMO, and GLI1, are generally elevated in ccRCC tissues, with particularly high expression observed in low-grade tumors [21, 22]. Moreover, high levels of SMO and GLI expression are associated with the upregulation of oncogenic factors such as VEGFA and CCND1, suggesting that SHH signaling may promote early ccRCC progression by regulating angiogenesis and cell cycle pathways [23]. SHH pathway inhibitors, such as cyclopamine and RU-SKI43, significantly suppress ccRCC cell proliferation and migration, and reduce the expression of target genes including BCL2 and MYC [23]. Further research has revealed that SPOP, an adaptor of the E3 ubiquitin ligase complex, regulates SHH and WNT signaling activity through SUFU, thereby modulating the malignant behavior of ccRCC cells [24].

Kinesins are a family of motor proteins that move along microtubules and are essential for mitosis, intracellular transport, and cilia formation [25]. Multiple studies have shown that various kinesin family members are aberrantly expressed in cancers and are involved in tumorigenesis, proliferation, and metastasis, suggesting their potential as diagnostic and therapeutic targets [26]. In ccRCC, oncogenic potential has been reported for kinesins such as Kinesin-2 (KIF3A [27]); Kinesin-4 (KIF4A [28], KIF14 [27]); Kinesin-5 (KIF11 [29]); Kinesin-6 (KIF20A/B [29–31],KIF23 [32, 33]); Kinesin-8 (KIF18A/B [34, 35]); Kinesin-13 (KIF2C [36]). KIF7, a member of the kinesin-4 family, plays a critical role in embryonic primary cilia formation and Hedgehog signal transduction by modulating the signaling from SMO to GLI and cooperating with SUFU to maintain the balance of GLI activity [37–41]. In recent decades, KIF7 has been shown to contribute to the progression of multiple tumor types, including medulloblastoma, bladder cancer, gastric cancer, prostate cancer, pancreatic cancer, and breast cancer [42–47]. However, the expression pattern and functional role of KIF7 in ccRCC remain largely unknown.

The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is a complex network of protein interactions with key roles in embryonic development and tumorigenesis [48]. Recent studies have revealed functional crosstalk between the Hh and Wnt/β-catenin pathways, particularly during cancer development and progression [49]. This crosstalk has been implicated in the pathogenesis of basal cell carcinoma [50], with the signal transduction stage from SMO to GLI in the Hh pathway identified as a critical node of interaction [51]. Previous studies have shown that KIF7 is an important regulatory factor during this stage, involved in modulating the transmission of signals from activated SMO to downstream GLI factors [38]. Therefore, KIF7 may play a potential role in integrating Hh signaling and regulating the Wnt pathway.

This study systematically evaluates KIF7 expression in ccRCC and examines its correlation with patient prognosis. We further explore its role in tumor progression and the molecular mechanisms involved. The results aim to provide insights into ccRCC pathogenesis and inform potential targeted therapies.

Methods

Cell culture

ccRCC cell line 786-O was purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (SCSP-5059). The cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, Cat. No. 11875093) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (EXCELL) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S), and maintained in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator.

Western blot

Proteins were extracted from cells using an appropriate amount of RIPA lysis buffer (100 µL per 1 × 10⁶ cells). After extraction, protein concentrations were measured by nanodrop. 5× loading buffer was added, mixed well, and heated at 100 °C for 5 min. Pre-stained molecular weight markers were used to track protein migration. During electrophoresis, the voltage was set at 80 V for 15 min and 150 V for 45 min. After electrophoresis, the gel was immersed in transfer buffer for 15 min. PVDF membranes were equilibrated in balance buffer for 5 min. Filter paper, membrane, and gel were stacked in sequence, ensuring no air bubbles, and an appropriate amount of buffer was added. Semi-dry transfer was performed for 16 min. After transfer, membranes were washed sequentially for 5 min each in H2O, TBS, and TBST. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h and incubated with the primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Membranes were washed three times for 5 min each in TBST and incubated for 1 h with secondary antibodies, followed by washing three times for 5 min each in TBST. The chemiluminescence reagent was prepared by mixing Solution A and B in a 1:1 ratio. The membranes were developed in the chemiluminescence solution, and imaging was performed to capture and save the images. The molecular weight was determined with YEASEN Tri-Color Pre-stained Protein Marker (20352ES). The antibodies against c-Myc (67447-1-lg), GAPDH (60004-1-Ig), HRP-Mouse (SA00001-1), HRP-Rabbit (SA00001-2) were purchased from Proteintech, antibody against DYKDDDDK (14793 S) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology, antibody against β-catenin (610154) was purchased from BD Biosciences, antibody against c-Jun (T55290S) was purchased from Abmart. The antibodies were diluted according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell transfection

Cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 1.5 × 10⁵ cells per well. The plasmid backbone used was pCMV-EGFP-3×Flag-MCS-Neo, which contains an ampicillin resistance gene for bacterial selection. The full-length coding sequence (CDS) of human KIF7 (NM_198525.3) was cloned into the plasmid vector within the multiple cloning site (MCS), between the EcoRI and BamHI restriction sites. The plasmid was amplified in E. coli and purified using a plasmid extraction kit (TIANGEN, DP123-02) for subsequent transfection experiments. Cell transfection was carried out using Lipo3000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. At 24 h post-transfection, upon detection of fluorescence under a fluorescence microscope, cells were lysed using RIPA buffer and collected for subsequent analysis.

Immunohistochemistry

Tissue microarrays (HKidE030PG02, Shanghai XinChao) were deparaffinized three times in xylene, rehydrated in a series of decreasing alcohol concentrations, and then subjected to antigen retrieval in sodium citrate buffer (pH 6). The samples were washed three times for 5 min each in ddH2O, followed by washing three times for 5 min each in PBS. The sections were incubated with 3% H2O2 at room temperature for 10 min to block endogenous peroxidase activity, followed by a 20-minute wash in 1% PBST at room temperature and additional PBS washes. Normal goat serum was added and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. The KIF7 antibody (Santa sc-517550) was diluted 1:100 and incubated on the tissue microarray in the dark at 4 °C overnight. After washing with PBST for 5 min, three PBS washes were performed. The secondary antibody was added and incubated at room temperature for 30 min, followed by washing three times for 5 min each in PBS. The DAB chromogen was applied, and the tissue microarray was immediately washed with ddH2O for 5 min after color development. Hematoxylin counterstaining was applied for 30–40 s, followed by washing with tap water for 10 min and ddH2O for 5 min. Finally, tissue sections were dehydrated through increasing concentrations of alcohol and mounted in xylene.

Cell cycle

After digestion with trypsin, 786-O cells were collected by centrifugation and resuspended in pre-chilled PBS. After another centrifugation step, cells were resuspended in 75% ethanol and stored at -20 °C overnight for fixation. The fixed cells were centrifuged to remove ethanol and washed once with cold PBS. Propidium iodide (PI) staining solution was prepared and added to the cells, which were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 15–30 min for DNA staining. After staining, cells were analyzed using flow cytometry. The flow cytometer detected PI fluorescence with an excitation wavelength of 535 nm and emission wavelength of 615 nm, analyzing the DNA content based on light scattering.

EdU staining

786-O cells were seeded at a density of 1.5 × 10⁵ cells per well in a 12-well plate and cultured until they reached 70%-80% confluence. The plasmid containing the KIF7 sequence was transfected into 786-O cells. EdU labeling was performed according to the YF® Click-iT EdU Imaging Kit (C6016M) protocol. Finally, cells were imaged using a fluorescence microscope to observe the proportion of EdU-labeled cells.

Bioinformatics analysis

Data acquisition and preprocessing

Gene expression data of ccRCC was obtained from public databases. The TCGA (The Cancer Genome Atlas) dataset was used as the primary source, and related gene expression data and clinical information of TCGA-KIRC were downloaded from the GDC Data Portal. All data were preprocessed and normalized using R software (version 4.0.2).

Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

To assess the overall differences in gene expression levels between the ccRCC tumor group and non-tumor group, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed on the data. The “prcomp” R package was used for PCA analysis, and PCA scatter plots were generated.

Differentially Expressed Genes Analysis (DEGs)

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were analyzed by DESeq2 and edgeR packages. The thresholds of identify significantly differentially expressed genes was set to |log₂Fold Change| > 1 and P-value < 0.05 to.

Unpaired and paired analysis

To further explore the expression changes of genes in SHH signaling pathway in ccRCC, both unpaired and paired analyses were performed and visualized with log₂Fold Change bar plots (using the R package “ggplot2”). Venn diagrams (using the R package “VennDiagram”) were then used to analyze the intersection of the results from both unpaired and paired analyses, identifying genes that were significantly upregulated or downregulated in both analyses.

Gene expression heatmap

To show the expression patterns of differentially expressed genes in various samples, a heatmap was generated using the “pheatmap” R package.

Cox regression and survival analysis

Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis (using the R package “survival”) was performed to evaluate the relationship between different genes, clinical variables (e.g., age, gender, clinical stage), and patient overall survival (OS). Subsequently, multivariate Cox regression analysis was conducted to adjust for potential confounding factors, further validating the independent association of these factors with ccRCC prognosis.

Survival curves were plotted using the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences between groups were tested using the log-rank test. The survival curves were drawn using the R package “survminer.”

Nomogram and calibration plot

To further validate the predictive performance of prognostic factors, a nomogram was constructed (using the R package “rm”) to predict the 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year survival rates of ccRCC patients based on genes like KIF7 and clinical features. Meanwhile, a calibration plot (using the R package “rms”) was used to assess the predictive accuracy of the nomogram by checking the deviation between the model’s predictions and actual observations, thus validating the model’s effectiveness.

Tumor-infiltrating immune cells analysis

TIMER (https://cistrome.shinyapps.io/timer/) was exploited to examine the relationship between the expression of the KIF7 gene and tumor-infiltrating immune cells.

Immunoinhibitor analysis

TISIDB (http://cis.hku.hk/TISIDB/index.php), an open-access website for investigations on tumor and immune system interactions, was exerted to explore the correlations between the KIF7 gene and known immunoinhibitors.

Drug sensitivity analysis

Drug sensitivity analysis was performed using the GSCA/GSCALite platform. Drug response data were obtained from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database. Spearman correlation analysis was used to evaluate the association between KIF7 mRNA expression and predicted drug sensitivity. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Analysis of differential expression of genes in SHH signaling pathway in ccRCC

To assess the overall differences in gene expression between the tumor group and normal group, we first performed Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on TCGA data (Fig. 1A). The results showed significant separation between the tumor group (red) and non-tumor group (gray) in terms of major gene expression patterns, indicating clear differences between the two groups.

(B) The volcano plot displays differentially expressed genes, with upregulated genes in red and downregulated genes in blue (|log₂FC| > 1, P < 0.05), where the x-axis represents log₂Fold Change and the y-axis represents -log₁₀P-value. Next, to identify the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the tumor and non-tumor groups, we conducted a volcano plot visualization analysis (Fig. 1B). The x-axis of the volcano plot (log₂Fold Change) represents the fold change in gene expression, and the y-axis (-log₁₀P-value) indicates statistical significance. Using the selection criteria |log₂Fold Change| > 1 and P-value < 0.05, we identified 5908 significantly upregulated genes (red) and 3262 significantly downregulated genes (blue).

To further explore the expression changes of genes in the SHH signaling pathway, we performed unpaired and paired analyses and visualized the results with a log₂Fold Change bar plot (Fig. 1C ~ D). Subsequently, we conducted an intersection analysis using a Venn diagram (Fig. 1E) to compare the results from both the unpaired and paired analyses. We found that 17 genes were significantly upregulated and 12 genes were significantly downregulated in both analyses, suggesting that these genes may related to tumor progression.

Among differentially expressed genes, CCND1, CDON, ARRB2, PTCH2, CSNK1E, CCND2, MGRN1, GLI1, BCL2, KIF7, GRK2, GPR161, GRK3, CSNK1G2, SMURF1, CUL1, and SUFU were significantly upregulated in unpaired and paired analyses. Additionally, GAS1, EVC, PTCH1, DHH, CUL3, BTRC, CSNK1A1L, PRKACB, SHH, HHAT, FBXW11, and DISP1 showed significant downregulation in the paired analysis, suggesting that they may be involved in tumor suppression and negative regulation of signaling (Fig. 1F).

In summary, the heatmap, generated using both unpaired and paired analyses, revealed changes in gene expression in the SHH signaling pathway in ccRCC, highlighting potential candidate genes for further investigation.

The Potential role of KIF7 as a prognostic predictor for ccRCC

Using ccRCC data from TCGA, the expression of SHH pathway–related genes across different clinical stages (I, II, III, IV) was systematically analyzed. The results revealed significant differences in gene expression among the stages. Specifically, the expression levels of ARRB2, CSNK1E, CSNK1G2, GLI1, GRK2, and KIF7 increased with advancing stage. In contrast, the expression levels of BTRC, CUL3, EVC, FBXW11, HHAT, and PTCH1 decreased with advancing pathologic stage (Fig. 2A). Subsequently, univariate Cox regression analysis was performed on 17 factors, including the 12 differentially expressed SHH-related genes mentioned above, along with age, sex, and clinical stage. The analysis showed that 15 of these factors were significantly associated with overall survival (OS) in ccRCC patients (Fig. 2B). Among them, nine factors with a hazard ratio (HR) > 1 were identified as high-risk factors, while six factors with HR < 1 were considered protective factors. Multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis revealed that in ccRCC patients, five factors with HR > 1 were identified as high-risk, and one factor with HR < 1 was identified as protective (Fig. 2C). In both univariate and multivariate analyses, in addition to sex, age, and clinical stage, high expression of KIF7, ARRB2, and CSNK1E were all identified as high-risk factors for ccRCC. Further survival analysis indicated that high expression of KIF7, ARRB2, and CSNK1E was significantly associated with shorter OS (Fig. 3A). Previous studies have shown that ARBB2 [52] and CSNK1E [53] promote ccRCC cell proliferation. Therefore, in our study, we focused on the role and mechanisms of KIF7. By constructing a nomogram, we found that high expression of KIF7 indicated poor 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year survival probabilities in ccRCC (Fig. 3B). We also constructed a calibration plot to test the prediction accuracy of the nomogram, and the results showed that the calibration lines were very close to the ideal line, indicating good consistency between the predicted and observed outcomes (Fig. 3C). These results further confirm the prognostic value of KIF7 in ccRCC.

The Expression of KIF7 protein in ccRCC and its correlation with prognosis

To validate the results of the bioinformatics analysis of KIF7 mRNA levels in ccRCC, we performed immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining of the KIF7 protein on a tissue microarray (Shanghai Outdo Biotech, HKidE180Su03) (Fig. 4A). Quantitative analysis of the immunohistochemical staining images with ImageJ indicated a significantly higher level of KIF7 protein in cancer tissues than in the paired normal tissues (Fig. 4B). Next, we further analyzed the correlation between KIF7 protein expression and tumor prognosis using clinical data from the microarray. The results showed a significant negative correlation between KIF7 protein expression and overall survival (OS), which was consistent with the findings from bioinformatics analysis (Fig. 4C). Additionally, univariate analysis of the clinical information revealed a significant positive correlation between KIF7 protein expression and both pathologic staging and AJCC score (Table 1). These results further support a positive association between KIF7 protein expression and the malignancy of ccRCC.

Analysis of the Correlation Between KIF7 Expression Levels and Clinical Pathological Features of ccRCC

KIF7 promote tumor cell proliferation by activating the WNT signaling pathway

To further investigate the role of KIF7 in ccRCC, we established a KIF7 overexpression model in the 786-O cell line. In this experiment, an empty vector plasmid (CT-eGFP) was used as the vector control, while an Overexpression-KIF7-eGFP plasmid was used as the experimental group. The plasmids were transfected into 786-O cells using Lipo3000. Cells transfected with either construct exhibited green fluorescence, confirming successful plasmid transfection (Fig. 5A). EdU assay results demonstrated that KIF7 overexpression significantly enhanced the proliferative capacity of 786-O cells (Fig. 5B), suggesting its involvement in cell cycle regulation. Flow cytometry further revealed that KIF7 overexpression accelerated progression of the cell cycle into the S phase, resulting in an increased proportion of cells in the S phase. (Fig. 5C). To further explore the potential mechanism by which KIF7 promotes ccRCC cell proliferation, we examined the expression of downstream molecules related to the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. Western blot results showed that KIF7 overexpression upregulated several key downstream proteins, including β-catenin, c-Jun, and c-Myc (Fig. 5D), further supporting the hypothesis that KIF7 exerts its biological function through activation of the Wnt signaling pathway.

KIF7 expression correlates with immune infiltration and chemosensitivity in ccRCC

ccRCC is a kind of highly immune-infiltrated cancer [54]. To explore the potential association between KIF7 expression and immune infiltration, we analyzed the correlation between KIF7 mRNA expression and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes in ccRCC using TIMER. The results showed that the expression level of KIF7 was significantly positively correlated with the infiltration levels of CD4 + T cells, macrophages, and neutrophils (Fig. 6A). Given the importance of immune checkpoint molecules in anti-tumor immunity, we analyzed the associations between the expression of SHH pathway genes and known immune checkpoint-related markers using TISIDB. KIF7 mRNA expression showed significant associations with multiple immune checkpoint genes. It was positively correlated with CD160, LAG3, LGALS9, PDCD1, PVRL2, TGFB1, and VTCN1, but negatively correlated with CD274, HAVCR2, IDO1, and IL10RB (Fig. 6B; Supplementary Fig. 1; Supplementary Table 1). These findings suggest a potential association between KIF7 expression and the immune microenvironment of ccRCC, including immune cell infiltration and immune checkpoint-related gene expression.

KIF7 Expression Correlates with Immune Infiltration and Chemosensitivity in ccRCC (A) KIF7 expression positively correlated with the infiltration of CD4 + T cells, macrophages, and neutrophils; (B) Correlations of KIF7 protein expression with the expression level of immune inhibitors in ccRCC. Correlation was analyzed using Spearman Correlation Test; (C) Drug sensitivity analysis was performed using GSCALite

Considering that therapeutic response is an important determinant of cancer prognosis, we further explored the association between KIF7 expression and predicted drug sensitivity using the GSCA platform. Drug sensitivity data were obtained from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database, and the correlation between KIF7 mRNA expression and drug response was evaluated using Spearman correlation analysis. In this analysis, KIF7 expression was significantly associated with the predicted sensitivity to docetaxel and bleomycin, whereas no significant association was observed for I-BET-762 (Fig. 6C). These findings suggest a potential association between KIF7 expression and predicted drug response in ccRCC.

Discussion

KIF7 is a mammalian homolog of Drosophila Costal2 (Cos2) and plays a key role as a ciliary-associated protein in the regulation of the Smoothened (Smo) to GLI transcription factor signaling pathway [38]. In recent years, studies have reported that KIF7 is aberrantly expressed in various malignancies and is closely associated with the progression of medulloblastoma, bladder cancer, gastric cancer, prostate cancer, pancreatic cancer, and breast cancer [42–47]. However, the role of KIF7 in ccRCC remains unclear.

Through transcriptome data analysis, this study found that KIF7 mRNA is significantly overexpressed in ccRCC tissues and positively associated with poor prognosis. Immunohistochemical results further confirmed the high expression of KIF7 in tumor tissues, which showed significant positive correlations with pathological stages and AJCC stages.

Cellular experiments further confirmed that overexpression of KIF7 significantly enhanced the proliferative activity of 786-O cells, accompanied by an increased proportion of cells in the S phase, suggesting that KIF7 may facilitate tumor progression by accelerating the G1-to-S phase transition. Mechanistically, high expression of KIF7 was shown to upregulate β-catenin protein levels and key downstream genes of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, including the transcription factors c-Jun and c-Myc, both of which are essential regulators of the G1/S phase transition [55].

C-Jun is an oncogene encoding an essential component of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)-activated protein-1 (AP-1) complex, and is considered a positive regulator of the G1/S transition in the cell cycle [56]. C-Jun is highly expressed in various types of lymphoma and synergistically promotes tumor cell growth and survival by regulating cell proliferation, inhibiting apoptosis, and enhancing invasiveness [57]. Additionally, some studies have shown that ectopic overexpression of c-Jun in breast cancer cells can significantly increase the proportion of cells in the S-phase and enhance their migration and invasion abilities [58].

C-Myc is a classical oncogene, and its encoded transcription factor is widely involved in regulating cell growth, differentiation, cell cycle progression, cell size, genomic stability, angiogenesis, and apoptosis [59]. Overexpression of c-Myc promotes the transition of cells from the G1 phase to the S phase, initiating DNA synthesis [60]. C-Myc has been shown to be associated with accelerated cell cycle progression in various cancers, such as breast cancer and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), where c-Myc promotes tumor progression by driving S-phase progression and immune escape [61, 62].

In this study, we observed that high expression of KIF7 significantly upregulated the expression levels of c-Jun and c-Myc, which are transcription factors that regulate the transcription of genes involved in the cell cycle. Combining with cell cycle analysis, we speculate that KIF7 may activate the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, thereby upregulating key cell cycle regulators such as c-Jun and c-Myc, ultimately promoting the G1/S transition and proliferation of ccRCC cells, contributing to tumor progression.

In addition to its potential role in cell-cycle progression, KIF7 may also be relevant to migration- and invasion-associated phenotypes in ccRCC. Tumor invasion and metastasis are complex biological processes involving cytoskeletal remodeling, extracellular matrix degradation, EMT-associated phenotypic changes, invasion-related structures, and dynamic interactions with the tumor microenvironment [63, 64]. Although MMP1, MMP2, and TGF-β signaling were not directly examined in the present study, these molecules provide a biologically relevant framework for interpreting the association between KIF7 expression and advanced clinicopathological features in ccRCC. MMP1 has been linked to metastatic potential, EMT-related activity, TNFα/NF-κB signaling, and tumor–immune cell interactions, while MMP1-related signaling axes have also been implicated in cell migration and invasion [64, 65]. MMP2 is another matrix-remodeling molecule involved in tumor invasion, migration, and metastatic dissemination, with recent studies highlighting MMP2-related transcriptional or signaling regulation in tumor metastasis [66, 67]. In addition, TGF-β signaling is closely associated with EMT, stromal remodeling, immune regulation, and metastatic microenvironment formation [64, 68]. Given the known role of KIF7 in Hedgehog signaling and the reported crosstalk between Hedgehog and Wnt/β-catenin pathways, MMP1, MMP2, and TGF-β-related signaling may serve as candidate directions for future studies investigating KIF7-associated invasive phenotypes [38, 49–51]. However, because MMP1, MMP2, TGF-β signaling, extracellular matrix degradation, and invasion-related structures were not directly assessed in this study, this hypothesis remains speculative and requires further mechanistic validation.

Previous studies have demonstrated that Hedgehog (Hh) signaling can modulate cytotoxic T cells and regulatory T cells, thereby promoting immunosuppression and shaping the immune infiltration landscape in pancreatic cancer [69]. As a key regulatory component of the Sonic Hedgehog (SHH) pathway, KIF7 regulates the activity of GLI transcription factors, primarily by modulating their stability and subcellular localization, thus influencing downstream signaling. However, whether KIF7 also contributes to remodeling the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) remains unclear due to a lack of relevant research. Given that ccRCC is a malignancy characterized by high levels of immune infiltration, we further explored the relationship between KIF7 expression and immune-related factors to assess its potential immunoregulatory role. Our findings revealed a significant positive correlation between KIF7 mRNA expression and the infiltration of CD4⁺ T cells, macrophages, and neutrophils, all of which are recognized as components of tumor-associated immune cells. Tumor-infiltrating immune cells can generally be divided into two categories: tumor-promoting immune cells (e.g., regulatory T cells and myeloid-derived suppressor cells, MDSCs) and tumor-antagonizing immune cells (e.g., effector T cells, NK cells, dendritic cells, M1-polarized macrophages, and N1-polarized neutrophils) [70]. Notably, the elevated expression of KIF7 in ccRCC was positively associated with CD4⁺ T cell infiltration. Previous research has demonstrated that CD4⁺ T-cell infiltration can promote tumor cell proliferation in ccRCC via the TGF-β1/YBX1/HIF2A axis [71]. These findings suggest that KIF7 expression may be associated with CD4⁺ T cell infiltration and may reflect a potential link between KIF7 and the immune microenvironment of ccRCC. However, whether KIF7 directly regulates CD4⁺ T cell recruitment or function remains to be experimentally determined. We also investigated the correlation between KIF7 expression and immune regulatory markers. Using TISIDB, we identified that KIF7 expression was positively correlated with CD160, LAG3, LGALS9, PDCD1, PVRL2, TGFB1, and VTCN1, while being negatively correlated with CD274 (PD-L1), HAVCR2, IDO1, and IL10RB. In recent years, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have emerged as a novel immunotherapeutic strategy and have shown remarkable efficacy across multiple cancer types, including melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer, renal cell carcinoma, and lymphoma [72–75]. PD-1, encoded by PDCD1, is expressed on activated T cells, B cells, and myeloid cells, while PD-L1, encoded by CD274, interacts with PD-1 to inhibit T-cell activity and mediate immune escape [76]. The PD-1/PD-L1 axis remains a principal target for ICIs [77]. KIF7 expression showed a positive correlation with PDCD1, which encodes the inhibitory checkpoint receptor PD-1, but a negative correlation with CD274, which encodes PD-L1. This paradoxical correlation pattern suggests that KIF7 expression may be associated with a complex immune checkpoint landscape in ccRCC, rather than a simple unidirectional immunosuppressive phenotype. These results provide a rationale for further exploring whether KIF7 expression is associated with ICI responsiveness in ccRCC. However, the potential immunoregulatory significance of KIF7 requires validation in immunotherapy-treated clinical cohorts and experimental models. Considering the potential role of KIF7 in ccRCC progression, we further employed the GSCA platform to investigate its potential association with predicted anticancer drug sensitivity. Our GSCA/GDSC-based prediction analysis suggested that high KIF7 mRNA expression was associated with increased predicted sensitivity to docetaxel and bleomycin. Interestingly, docetaxel has been reported to induce apoptosis and G2/M cell-cycle arrest in ccRCC by inhibiting the MAPK signaling pathway [78]. Given that KIF7 overexpression was associated with altered cell-cycle distribution in our experimental model, the predicted association between KIF7 expression and docetaxel sensitivity may be worthy of further investigation. I-BET-762 has been reported to inhibit cell proliferation by suppressing c-Myc and cell-cycle-related pathways [79, 80]. However, no significant predicted association between KIF7 expression and I-BET-762 sensitivity was observed in our analysis. Because these drug sensitivity results were derived from GSCA/GDSC-based computational prediction rather than direct drug-response assays, they should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating findings. Further in vitro dose–response assays and clinical validation are required to determine whether KIF7 expression has predictive value for docetaxel, bleomycin, or I-BET-762 response in ccRCC.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study is the first to systematically reveal the high expression characteristics of KIF7 in ccRCC and its tumor-promoting mechanisms. It suggests that KIF7 may promote cell proliferation and tumor progression by activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and downstream transcription factors c-Jun and c-Myc. We also found that KIF7 expression was associated with immune infiltration and immune checkpoint-related gene expression in ccRCC. Our study provides evidence for the first time that KIF7 protein may be promising therapeutic targets and novel biomarkers for ccRCC. Future research should further explore the cross-regulation between KIF7 and Wnt/SHH signaling pathways, utilize animal models to clarify its role in tumor growth and metastasis, and assess its stability and generalizability as a prognostic biomarker in multi-center clinical samples, with the aim of promoting its potential translational application in the precise diagnosis and treatment of ccRCC.
