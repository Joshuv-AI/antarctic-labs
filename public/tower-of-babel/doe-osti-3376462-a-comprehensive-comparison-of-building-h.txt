 ABSTRACT
The built environment continues to expand due to increasing human populations with some projections
indicating additional growth of 2.5 billion persons by 2050. Buildings are a key component of the built
environment and information on building height and urban morphology in general can help to better
understand and address a host of development and urban planning issues including energy consumption,
urbanization, population dynamics, urban heat islands, environmental degradation, and others. To date,
several approaches have been developed to address the need for mapping building height at scale. These
approaches can be grouped into two main categories: 1) directly measure height from remote sensing
products or 2) indirectly estimate height from footprint morphology features at the building-by-building
level. Due to a number of these global height datasets now being available for consumption and use across
domains, there is a critical need to understand the differences displayed between these products and how
the use of these may influence the outcome when applied. In this analysis, we compare and evaluate
5 global building height datasets: Global Human Settlement, World Settlement Footprint 3D, Global
Building Morphology dataset, three-dimensional Global Building Footprints, and an approach developed
at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL). Our analysis shows that, across 5 cities at a global scale with
high-quality building height reference data, the approach developed by ORNL of inferring height at the
building-by-building level is more accurate and consistent. This was evidenced by the ORNL product
improving upon the baseline in all 5 cities, the only product to do so. These findings will aid researchers
in choosing a global building height product that best meets their needs and provides global availability,
consistency, and accuracy.

 1. INTRODUCTION
With population projections pointing to upwards of 10 billion people over the next 50 years, the built
environment is expanding, both horizontally and also vertically [37, 47]. If this expansion happens in an
uncontrolled and unplanned manner it can lead to various maladies such as water scarcity [27], public
health related issues [7, 23] and environmental degradation [4]. For example, rapid expansion of the
built environment between 2000-2017 led to human-related pressures on natural resources and extreme
loss of biodiversity in Sub-Saharan Africa [27]. Population growth and urbanization in South America
has led to an increase in wildfire risk, with fires there growing more extreme in duration and intensity
[24]. Sustainable development is within the realm of possibility yet characterization (i.e. age, use type,
height) of the built environment is necessary to assist various stakeholders to ensure cites are developed in
a resilient and sustainable manner [32].
Buildings are a core component within the built environment and are foundational pieces to better
understand Urban Heat Islands (UHI) [9], population distribution [43], economics [16], public transportation
[12], among others [14, 34, 53]. Knowledge of building morphology, and building height in particular, has
become an integral feature for a number of domains including emergency response [17] and sustainable
development [52]. For example, research by Perez et al. [39] in Japan highlights the changes observed
in population dynamics based on differing periods of construction, of which there has recently been an
increase in high-rise, narrow buildings within urban centers. Alexander [1], when investigating heat
effects on cities in Denmark found that building height had a large influence on the severity of the high
temperature events. Therefore, while knowledge of building locations is an important facet, contextual
information such as building height can provide important insights for planning and management of the
built environment.

specific locales, public knowledge on building height at broader scales is limited, with less than 3% of

As an alternative to remote sensing approaches, research has shown that it is possible to infer building
height at the building-by-building level using morphology features generated from the building footprints
themselves [5, 33, 36, 47]. Biljecki et al. [5] first discovered that it was possible to infer building height
by using morphology features at the building level in the Netherlands. Milojevic-Dupont et al. [33]
built upon Biljecki’s [5] work and inferred heights for buildings across Europe by leveraging city-level
information in cohesion with morphology features, but did so at a contextual level. This was an important
development and allowed the machine learning model to learn from multiple different buffers (50𝑚, 100𝑚,
etc.), potentially aiding in separating buildings from rural / urban settings. Nachtigall et al. [36], inferred
building age across Europe using morphology features and also tested their approach on other built
environment targets, namely height. Stipek et al. in [47] found that it is possible to infer building height
using morphology features alone. Furthermore, they displayed the ability to infer height on footprints
derived from satellite imagery, the first time to the authors knowledge that this had been achieved. Another

work of note which leveraged both morphology features and SAR data is that of Che et al. [8]. The authors
gathered building height information from sources such as Microsoft and OpenStreetMap (OSM) and then
integrated their training process with information on population and socioeconomic activities from the
satellite imagery to generate a building height layer at the building-by-building level.
Only a select few studies have shown the ability to generate a building height product at scale [19, 30, 32,
40, 47]. Pesaresi et al. [40] produced Global Human Settlement Layer (GHSL) BUILT-H, in which they
generated multiple products for the built environment (average gross building height, average net building
height) at a 100𝑚 resolution. To accomplish this, they leveraged five open-access Digital Elevation Model
(DEM) at multiple resolutions to extract information on the vertical component of built-up areas at a global
scale. They then applied a uni-variate and multivariate linear regression model to infer their target at a
100𝑚 resolution and validated their approach in 6 globally-dispersed cities (Hong Kong, London, New
York, San Francisco, Sao Paolo, Toronto).

Esch et al. [19] utilized a higher resolution elevation model with direct measurement to produce a 90𝑚
gridded building height layer called the World Settlement Footprint (WSF3D). This dataset was created by
first masking settlement areas around the globe. Within those settlement areas, the authors used a 12𝑚
DEM produced from the TanDEM-X synthetic aperture radar mission to identify and measure vertical
edges. These edges are attributed to outline an individual building of which they then measure its height.
Data were then aggregated to the 90𝑚 pixel resolution. This effort’s validation process included two
separate parts. One part utilized high resolution imagery and performed a traditional accuracy assessment
of the final product by comparing the results to the imagery, at select sites. The second part was less
traditional and included crowd-sourcing information from 75,724 street-view imges collected in 15 cities
across the globe. In total, over 130,000 buildings were used in the validation process.
Li et al. [30] recently produced another gridded height product called the Global Building Morphology
Dataset for Urban Hydroclimate Modelling (GLAMOUR) at 100𝑚 grid cell resolution, hereafter referred
to by the acronym GLAM for simplicity. The authors of this study use a multi-task deep learning model
along with publicly available datasets, namely Sentinel 1 and 2 satellite imagery and two elevation models

 3

(NASADEM and Copernicus DEM) to generate a building height estimate globally. To validate their
approach, they compared their result to the WSF3D product produced by Esch et al. [19] and investigated
19 geospatially distinct regions. They found that both products were comparable in certain cities, but they
outperformed Esch et al. [19] in other areas, such as East Asia (Korea, China). Ma et al. [32] recently
released a Global Building Height Layer (GHB2020) derived from the spaceborne instrument GEDI, along
with Landsat 8, Sentinel 1 and 2 data and a high-resolution DEM. The authors state that their product is
comparable to GHSL based on evaluating RMSE, but is at a coarser resolution of 150𝑚.
In research completed by Stipek et al. [47], the authors demonstrated the ability to infer heights at a
building-by-building level across the U.S. (ORNL). To do so, they generated 65 unique morphology
features [25] at the building level and then inferred height from those features using an XGBoost model.
They then leveraged footprints derived from both lidar and satellite data to infer heights across the entirety
of the U.S., totaling over 100 million buildings. Their results showed an error of 3𝑚 or less for 97% of all
buildings mapped. The ability to infer height from footprints derived from satellite imagery allows this
flexible approach to be quickly applied to areas where height values are currently unavailable. Recently,
there has been a dramatic increase in the amount of open-source building footprint data from companies
such as Microsoft1 and Google2 . Therefore, the approach presented by Stipek et al. [47] can be leveraged
to infer heights for buildings at a global scale.
Another study of note that has produced a global estimate of building height is by Che et al. [8]. The
authors in this study create a global product called 3DGLO and utilize a multisource approach to leverage
multiple sources of earth observation data (SAR, optical, DEM/Digital Surface Model (DSM) and VIIRS
nighttime lights), along with morphology features extracted from building footprints to compile a set of
input features. They then subdivide the globe into 33 distinct subregions and develop a building height
model for each region using the XGBoost algorithm. The models are trained with reference data provided
from multiple sources. Once the results are compiled, they validate their model by comparing their product
to manually calculated building heights from Google Street View as well as independent reference data
from 17 globally distributed cities.
While each of the aforementioned studies (GHSL [40], WSF3D [19], GLAM [30], GBH2020 [32], ORNL
[47], 3DGLO [8]) have produced a building height product that covers the globe, there is no consensus
or formal study on the differences between the varying products. While Li et al. [30] (GLAM) compared
their product with those of Esch et al. [19] (WSF3D), they simply compared their results in relation to
building height and investigated the error at different breaks within the built environment and did not
undergo a formal analysis. Furthermore, with the recent development by Stipek et al. [47] and Che et
al. [8] in which they inferred height at a building-by-building level, there is now a need for an analysis
between gridded and building-level products, something which has yet to be done within the current
literature. It must be noted that Che et al. [8] did compare their product (3DGLO) with GHSL and
other products, yet it is unclear which method they leveraged to downscale the raster products, such as a
weighted mean or average for the building height. Furthermore, several of the aforementioned authors have
claimed to have the most accurate building height layer without conducting formal analysis against the
other datasets. Based on the varying resolutions (90𝑚, 100𝑚, building-by-building) and the importance of
building height across domains (UHI’s, emergency response, economics, population dynamics), a formal
analysis is necessary.

resolutions (< = 100𝑚, building-by-building). To accomplish this, we compare each product to a reference
dataset comprised of lidar-derived building heights using Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Root Mean

 2. METHODS
Here, we describe the overall workflow associated with generating a comprehensive analysis of multiple
building height products at two resolutions (100𝑚 and building-by-building). We scale all products to
a 100𝑚 resolution, which involved scaling down products such as GHSL, from 250𝑚 to 100𝑚 and also
scaling up ORNL, from building-by-building to 100𝑚. To compare how each of the products did at both
the 100𝑚 and building-by-building level, we evaluate each product in relation to the lidar building height
data using a simple median comparison, over which any model would be an improvement. Next, we
leverage the JS divergence to measure how each product’s height histogram diverged from the histogram
which was displayed by the lidar datum. For both the 100𝑚 grid and building-by-building comparison, we
investigated five unique and spatially diverse cities in which there is ground truth lidar datum for building
height: Berlin, New York, San Francisco, Takamatsu, and Tokyo. Furthermore, we also compare products
at a 100𝑚 resolution in fifteen cities in which there were no lidar datum for building height. Figure 2
highlights the spatial extent of this research.

DATA
For our analysis we leveraged 3.51 million buildings and 1.79 million 100𝑚 grid cells across 5 cities
(Berlin, New York, San Francisco, Takamatsu, Tokyo) and 3 continents. These cities display unique and
diverse patterns of development as well as variability in building footprint shapes, sizes, and heights.
Collectively, they provide a robust platform by which to compare differing global building height
products. The data and code we leveraged in this study is all open-source and freely available through
publicly-accessible websites. The individual global building height datasets used in this comparison are
described first, followed by the reference lidar datasets.

GHSL
GHSL3 is a publicly available suite of products and data portal hosted by the Joint Research Centre of
the European Commission. Some of the available datasets include GHS-BUILT-S, which depicts the
distribution of built-up surfaces (essentially buildings) across the globe, GHS-POP, a global population
raster layer, and GHS-BUILT-C, which delineates morphological settlement zones. Data layers are
available at multiple spatial resolutions, time periods and in multiple coordinate systems. For this research,
we utilized the GHS-BUILT-H product, which represents building height at a 100𝑚 grid size. A full
description of the methods used to create the BUILT-H product are available in Pesaresi et al [40]. As a
very concise overview, linear regression was applied across sixty features derived by applying a series of
convolutional, morphological and textural filters to a set of global elevation models including SRTM30,
SRTM90, ASTER, AW3D30 and MERIT. It is important to note that BUILT-H is produced in two options:
Average Net Building Height (ANBH) and Average Gross Building Height (ANGH). We utilized ANBH,
which is represented as the built area volume divided by the surface area.

WSF3D
WSF3D4 is a global raster dataset that includes information on building area, volume and height at
90m resolution. The authors leverage a fine scale human settlement mask (World Settlement Footprint
2019) to limit the geographic extent of their analysis. Only areas identified by the settlement mask are

GLAMOUR
GLAM5 is a global building height dataset produced at a 100𝑚 resolution. The authors first generate
a grid of human settlement activity in which they keep the cell if the population exceeds 300 persons /
km2 . Next, they acquire open-source satellite imagery in the form of Sentinel-1 SAR imagery, Sentinel-2
optical imagery, a DSM (NAADEM) for low and mid latitudes (-60.0 < latitude < 60.0) and DEM
data from Copernicus for the remaining areas. To ensure their imagery was cloud free, they utilize an
aggregation approach to create a high-quality mosaic from multi-temporal Sentinel-2 imagery in an
automated workflow. The authors then estimate the number of footprints and average height using the earth
observation data in a multi-task deep learning model. They restrict their estimates to areas they deemed as
urbanized based on human population layers. They find their approach improved upon WSF3D in specific
areas.

3DGLO
The authors of the 3DGLO dataset6 [8] also utilize a multi-source approach that incorporates earth
observation data and building footprint information. Similar to the ORNL approach, the authors of this
study extract building morphology features from building footprints and develop a set of 114 features
based on morphology and earth observation data including SAR imagery, optical imagery, a digital surface
model and nighttime lights. They then subdivide the globe into 33 subregions and develop a building
height model for each region using the XGBoost algorithm. The models are trained with reference data
provided from 2 different sources and cover most of the globe. They validate their model by comparing to
manually calculated building heights from Google Street View as well as independent reference data from
17 globally-distributed cities.

ORNL
The ORNL building height product takes a different approach from the others described above. Whereas
those products use primarily satellite-derived products like elevation models and satellite imagery, the
ORNL approach uses machine learning to identify patterns in planar building footprints. To accomplish
this, the authors first generate 65 distinct morphology features from each building footprint. The features
generated fall into three main categories: geometric, such as area and perimeter; engineered, such as the
complexity ratio which compares traits like the ratio of the area to the perimeter, and contextual, which
includes information about neighboring features as various distances (50𝑚, 100𝑚, 250𝑚, 500𝑚, 1000𝑚).
Once these features are calculated, the authors then leverage a gradient boosting algorithm to learn what
patterns may exist between the features and building height. The structure of this workflow allows for it to
be applied to any building footprint dataset at scale, thus allowing for this product to be generated globally
on any footprint layer. For this research we followed the steps provided by the authors in the publicly
available repository7 and generated a building height layer for each of the cities selected for review without
any local training data.

Reference Data
To compare each of the products, we leveraged lidar-derived reference building footprint and height
datasets which have been used in previous research [33, 36, 46, 47]. The footprints and lidar data used
for Takamatsu and Tokyo were provided by the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism8 .
For Berlin, Germany, we leveraged the dataset provided by the Business Location Center9 which provide
building footprints and height. New York and San Francisco footprints and height data are provided by
ORNL, Federal Emergency Management Agency Geospatial Response Office, and the Department of
Homeland Security Science and Technology Directorate10 . For further details on the datasets for San
Francisco, New York, and Berlin, please see [33, 47]. Further information associated with the dataset for
Takamatsu and Tokyo please see [46].

EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN
We conducted two distinct analyses on the building height products to understand how the products
compare in various cities across the globe. One experiment uses the data at a spatial scale of 100m and
the other uses the data at a spatial scale of the individual building. For the building level analysis, we omit
GHSL in the comparison, since the authors of this product specifically state that it should not be used at
that granularity.
The two aforementioned experiments are conducted in 5 cities across the globe where high quality building
height reference data are available. We also evaluate the height products in 15 globally-dispersed cities
where no reference data is available, with the purpose of comparing building height distributions in areas
that have to-date been left out of the literature. Because no reference data are available, we do not attempt
to statistically evaluate the datasets, but we provide figures of the height distributions for the reader to
visually compare and interpret (Figure 3).

Analysis I - 100m Spatial Scale
To prepare the data for the 100𝑚 spatial scale analysis, we first downloaded GHSL, GLAMOUR, and
WSF3D from their source locations. The ORNL product was created by first cloning the code and
following the procedures outlined in Stipek et al. [47]. The same building dataset, minus the cities of
interest in this paper was leveraged to train the model. For example, when testing on Tokyo or Takamatsu,
there was no local data used in the training of the model and the city was out-of-sample data. We
downloaded the 3DGLO building height layer from the openly-accessible zenodo link11 .
A gridded vector layer, or fishnet, with a cell size of 100𝑚 was created and the raster values for each of
the three gridded global height products (GHSL, WSF3D, GLAMOUR) were extracted using a weighted
mean for each cell in the fishnet. We structured our methodology to involve the least amount of pixel
manipulation and to ensure the highest degree of comparability between datasets. This approach eliminates
the need for data resampling, leading to no pixel averaging or alteration of values. The datasets are aligned
to the same coordinate system, but the pixels maintain their original values. We feel that this provides the
most accurate and direct comparison of the datasets. It is more computationally expensive than resampling
the rasters and analyzing them through pixel-to-pixel comparison, but it maintains the original data fidelity.
For the ORNL and 3DGLO product, as well as the reference data, we calculated a mean building height
for all buildings and portions of buildings located within each 100𝑚 fishnet cell. The weighted mean is

Jenson-Shannon Divergence
We leveraged the JS divergence metric as a measure of the difference in height distributions between
datasets [31]. The JS differs from other divergence algorithms, such as the Kullback-Leibler (KL)
divergence in that the JS treats both distributions equally and is bounded between 0, meaning a high
similarity, and 1, signifying minimal similarity. We compared the JS distribution for each of the 4 global
products with the lidar height distribution in each of the 5 cities for which we had reference data.

Analysis II - Building-by-Building Approach
To compare each of the products at the building-by-building level, we proceeded with the following steps
for the two raster products, GLAM and WSF3D products.

Exact Extract - Raster Products
To generate a building level height value from the raster datasets, we leveraged the exact extract algorithm12 .
This algorithm works by summarizing the values of an overlaying raster dataset within the bounds of a
given polygon. It is important to note that due to certain buildings overlapping multiple 100𝑚 grid cells,
we deployed a weighted mean to ensure a fair comparison (Figure 4). For example, if 90% of a building
was within a grid cell with a height of 10𝑚 and 10% was within a grid cell with a height of 0𝑚, the
building would be classified as 9𝑚 tall. While previous work [8] has down-scaled raster products to the
building level for comparison, it is not clear the method they leveraged to generate heights. This algorithm
was run for all 5 of the cities in which we had lidar reference data. We did not run this algorithm for the
ORNL and 3DGLO product which were natively generated at a building level.

 3. RESULTS
To ensure a fair and robust comparison between the various products, we evaluated building heights from
5 different global products across 2 spatial scales and in multiple cities across the globe (5 with reference
and 15 with no reference data). The 2 spatial scales analyzed include evaluating the data at a 100𝑚 x 100𝑚
grid cell level and at the individual building level.
We then evaluate the building height values of the different products to known lidar ground truth dataset at
the same spatial scales. Next, we generate 4 different metrics to statistically compare the height values.
These are the median, MAE, RMSE, and the JS divergence. The median, which assigns all buildings
within the dataset the median building height of the entire sample serves as a baseline and is a commonly
used approach in previous research in relation to building height [33, 47]. MAE and RMSE are both
measures of error between a reference dataset and a model prediction, the latter of which is the standard
accuracy method as defined by the American Society for Photogrammetry and Remote Sensing [2] when
evaluating height values in non-vegetated areas. Finally, we calculate the JS divergence, which measures
the degree to which one distribution diverges from another [31]. The JS differs from other divergence
algorithms, such as the KL divergence in that the JS treats both distributions equally and is bounded
between 0, meaning a high similarity, and 1, signifying minimal similarity.
To further our analysis, we also compared each of the products across 15 globally distributed cities in
which there is no lidar reference data (Figure 3). We do this to test the differences between global height
products in cities and countries that to-date have been unstudied due to a lack of publicly-available
reference height data.

 4. DISCUSSION
With the rapid urban growth being displayed across cities globally, characterization of the built environment
is of utmost importance. There are a number of products which have been developed to measure [19, 28,
30, 40, 51] or infer [5, 33, 36, 47] building height yet only 5 products (GHSL, GLAMOUR, WSF3D,
ORNL, 3DGLO) at present have the potential to produce building height maps at scale at a resolution
of 100𝑚 or finer. We demonstrate that a machine learning approach using morphology features achieves
the highest accuracy for mapping building height at the individual building level. This was evidenced
by ORNL displaying the lowest JS divergence score for 4 of the 5 cities at the 100𝑚 and 3 of the 5 for
the building-by-building level. Furthermore, ORNL had the lowest RMSE for all of the cities at the
building-by-building level and the 100𝑚 resolution level.
One major caveat with this research is in the different methodologies and temporality associated with the
building height products. For example, WSF3D [19] was developed explicitly from the Tandem-X radar
mission which was conducted between 2011-2013. GHSL [40], GLAM [30], and 3DGLO [8] all leverage
multiple earth observation products, including optical imagery, synthetic aperture radar and DEMs, but
use slightly different inputs and methods. Stipek et al. [47] built upon the approaches by Biljecki et al. [5]
and Milojevic-Dupont et al. [33] and inferred height from morphology features derived at the building
level using a gradient boosted tree algorithm. Not only do these approaches use different input data, but the
pre-processing and modeling steps also vary, leading to unique and distinct outputs. Therefore, to ensure a
fair and robust comparison we compared the MAE and RMSE at both a gridded and building-by-building
level.
Another caveat of this study is that 2 significant open-source building height products, one developed by
Microsoft and the other by Google, are not included in the analysis. The primary driver for their absence is
that we only included products which have global coverage of building height. Microsoft footprints contain
building heights in the U.S. and Australia while the Google product only has coverage in the southern
hemisphere. Furthermore, Stipek et al. compared their product to the Microsoft product and found that
the ORNL product more closely resembled the true building height distribution across select cities in the
U.S. [47]. Nonetheless, these products provide high value and should be leveraged when appropriate.
Another limitation of this research is the geographic extent of our study area which only includes 5 cities.
These cities were chosen due to their diversity of building age and morphology as well as high quality
reference height information. For example, Berlin, Germany displays a bi-modal height distribution
highlighted by Milojevic-Dupont et al. [33] which none of the products were able to map with high
accuracy, evidenced by a bi-modal error distribution (Figure 6). Tokyo, with its high concentration of
high-rise buildings, evidenced by the high median MAE and RMSE, has a high diversity of heights while
Takamatsu, also in Japan, had a much higher concentration of low-rise building. This building height
distribution in Japan was captured well by the 3DGLO product, but was unable to maintain this level of
accuracy for other cities in Europe (Berlin) and the United States (New York, San Francisco).
As the built environment expands, understanding building height is a crucial parameter across a broad
swath of domains such as population modeling[43, 45, 55], UHI studies[9], urban energy use[41],
infectious diseases and hazards[18, 49], among others. Work by Schug et al.[43] documented that
including building height information into a model for developing population estimates in New York
greatly improved the results, compared with not having height. Similarly, research by Huang et al. [26]
found that the most difficult areas in which to estimate population were those in dense urban areas where
building height information was unavailable. Another area of research is UHIs, which are becoming
more common and intense at a global scale and models which focus on urban heat impacts rely heavily on

 17

building height [3, 10, 11]. Finally, in another case for building height data, as populations have expanded
across sub-saharan Africa, the expansion of the built environment at both the horizontal and vertical scale
has put stressors on the regions natural resources to sustain the growing population [27].
Here, we demonstrate that while there are various products estimating building height at a global scale,
these products have large differences as evidenced through rigorous statistical comparisons against
reference data, as well as visual interpretation of height distributions in areas with no available reference
data. When comparing against known reference data, the ORNL building height approach consistently
outperforms the other products (GHSL, WSF3D, GLAM, 3DGLO) at both the gridded (100𝑚) and
building-by-building level. ORNL produced the lowest RMSE value in all 5 cities at both the 100𝑚 vector
grid level and at the building-by-building level. Moreover, the ORNL product had the lowest JS metric,
signifying it more closely resembled the lidar derived building height distribution in 4 of the 5 cities at
the 100𝑚 grid level and 3 of the 5 for the building-by-building level. In areas with no available reference
data, we found stark differences in height distributions, indicating that the selection of the building height
product may dramatically impact the analysis being performed. As more reference data becomes available
further work is needed to fully understand if these trends hold in other cities across the globe, especially
in regions such as Africa, South America and Southeast Asia. Nevertheless, this work represents the first
known comprehensive comparison of multiple global building height datasets and provides a look at how
these products differ in various cities.
