When remission is not restoration: catastrophic survival after hematopoietic stem cell transplantation
Authors: Rong Wei
Published: 2026

The bone marrow report was on my desk when I returned from the ward. It said what we had been waiting to see: complete remission, full donor chimerism. For a patient with refractory acute myeloid leukemia after haploidentical hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, there are few words more reassuring.

By our usual standards, this was success. The graft had taken. The leukemia was no longer detectable. Months of risk, expense, and effort had led to the result we had hoped for.

And yet I could not feel relieved.

Before transplantation, she and her family had sat across from me in the clinic. Her options were limited, and she knew it. She was a middle-aged woman with young children. “If there is still a chance,” she told us, “I want to try. My children are still too young.” Her family had already spent much of what they had. The transplant would ask even more. Still, after repeated discussions, she chose to proceed.

It was not a decision made in denial. She understood that treatment might fail. She understood that infection, graft-versus-host disease, organ injury, relapse, and death were possible. But the alternative was also clear. For her, the chance of life, however uncertain, was worth taking.

At first, the course seemed to justify that choice. Her neutrophils rose. Donor chimerism became complete. We allowed ourselves to believe that she had crossed the hardest part of the journey.

Then, during immune reconstitution, she developed severe viral encephalitis.

The change was sudden. She became confused, then unresponsive. Soon she was in a deep coma, complicated by septic shock. For days, our focus narrowed to survival: antivirals, infection control, hemodynamic support, organ support. Eventually, the virus was cleared. Her circulation stabilized. She began to breathe without mechanical support.

Then one day, she opened her eyes.

But she did not come back as the person we had known.

The encephalitis had left her with severe, irreversible cortical injury. When I stood beside her bed with the marrow report in my hand, she looked at me without recognition. I had imagined telling her that the leukemia was in remission. I had imagined tears, perhaps a cautious smile after months of fear. Instead, the words had nowhere to go.

Her memory, judgment, and independence were profoundly impaired. The woman who had spoken so clearly about her children and her hopes had survived in body. But much of the mind that had formed those hopes had been taken away.

The consequences did not end with the neurologic injury.

For a time, her husband tried to live with this bewildering reality. I do not know, and cannot fairly judge, everything he carried: grief, exhaustion, financial pressure, the pain of facing a wife who was the most familiar stranger. Eventually, he left with their children.

Her parents became her caregivers.

They were both in their seventies. In clinic, her mother guided her gently by the arm, speaking in a slow, patient voice. Her father carried the bags: medications, water, old test reports. During admissions, her mother washed her face and combed her hair. Her father fed her slowly, spoon by spoon. On late-night rounds, I would pass her door and see her asleep, her face calm. Beside the bed, her mother sat half-awake, one hand resting on the blanket. Her father sometimes stood by the window, staring into the dark.

I began to feel uneasy about the word “success.”

In hematology, we need measurable outcomes. Complete remission matters. Donor chimerism matters. Progression-free survival, overall survival, non-relapse mortality—these endpoints allow us to evaluate treatment and improve care.

But this patient made me confront what those endpoints cannot contain.

Her leukemia was gone, but she could not return to the life for which she had endured treatment. The transplant had achieved its biological goal, yet the person who had chosen that goal could no longer participate in the life it preserved. We had avoided one tragedy and arrived at another.

I do not believe this outcome could have been predicted exactly. Severe infection is a known risk after transplantation, and neurologic complications are possible. At the time, recommending transplantation was medically reasonable. Her disease was aggressive, her options were limited, and her wish to pursue a chance of cure was clear.

Still, I have wondered whether our conversations before transplant were broad enough.

We discussed death, relapse, infection, graft-versus-host disease, and organ failure. But did we spend enough time on the possibility that treatment might work and still leave behind a life the patient would not have chosen? We are accustomed to discussing catastrophic death. We are less prepared to discuss catastrophic survival: a state in which life is biologically preserved, but cognition, independence, relationships, and personhood are profoundly altered.

Patients often ask, “What is the chance that I will live?” Another question is just as important and harder to answer: “If I live, what kind of life might I be living?”

Choice requires more than disclosure of mortality rates. It requires a conversation about values: What does the patient most hope for? What does she most fear? What level of dependence would be acceptable? What outcome might feel worse than death? Who would carry the burden if survival came without independence?

These questions may not change the decision. But they can make the decision more honest.

Since caring for her, I have changed the way I speak with families before high-risk treatment. I still talk about cure. I still believe in fighting for remission. But before the consent form is signed, I pause longer. I ask what survival means to the person sitting in front of me. I try to explain that the hardest outcomes are not always divided neatly into life and death.

The marrow report said we had cured her leukemia. It was true, but it was not the whole truth. Medicine can win the visible battle and still leave a family standing in ruins. Sometimes cure restores a life. Sometimes it leaves only its echo.
