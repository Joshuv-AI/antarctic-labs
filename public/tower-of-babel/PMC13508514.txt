Triangle-like lumen in fibrosing mediastinitis-induced pulmonary vein stenosis: a case report
Authors: Chan Zhou, Bo Li, Yichen Wang, Zhipeng Yang, Jing Li, Xin Wang, Pan Wu, Sheng Zhang, Jing Zeng, Xinghui Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFibrosing mediastinitis (FM) is a rare chronic fibrotic disease of the mediastinum, characterized by diffuse fibrous tissue proliferation in the mediastinal space that can compress adjacent pulmonary vessels bilaterally. This fibrous proliferation is the core pathological basis for simultaneous stenosis of pulmonary arteries (PAs) and pulmonary veins (PVs) in affected patients, with adhesion as a secondary pathological manifestation of FM rather than the primary cause of vascular stenosis. Fibrous tissue proliferation compresses the pulmonary veins, causing stenosis, obstructing pulmonary circulation reflux, leading to pulmonary congestion and pulmonary hypertension, with symptoms such as dyspnea and hemoptysis. Diagnosis relies on contrast-enhanced chest CT and pulmonary angiography, and multimodal endovascular imaging including intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) and optical coherence tomography (OCT) can provide refined morphological details of stenotic lesions.Case presentationIVUS and OCT revealed a rare triangle-like lumen in FM-induced PV stenosis, a specific imaging feature that supplements the morphological data of FM-related PV stenosis and provides a new intravascular evaluation reference for such rare lesions. The combined use of IVUS and OCT helps differentiate extrinsic compression from intrinsic vascular pathology, which is critical for treatment selection. Treatment is mainly based on drugs to inhibit fibrosis, interventional procedures or surgery to relieve obstruction.ConclusionsThis case suggests that a triangle-like lumen on intravascular imaging may represent a useful clue to FM-induced pulmonary vein stenosis. However, this is a single observation; the specificity of the triangle-like lumen for FM remains unknown. Combined IVUS and OCT is resource-intensive and not routinely recommended. We report this morphology solely as a potential diagnostic clue when clinical suspicion of FM exists.

Introduction

Here, we report a case of FM-induced combined PA and PV stenosis in which combined IVUS and OCT identified a triangle-like luminal configuration, and we discuss its potential diagnostic and interventional implications [1].

Case presentation

A 69-year-old female patient presented with exertional dyspnea for 2 years. Echocardiography showed enlarged right heart, mild tricuspid regurgitation, and elevated systolic pulmonary artery pressure. Contrast-enhanced chest CT indicated bilateral hilar soft tissue density shadow with calcified nodules, bilateral inferior PAs and superior PVs stenosis. Accordingly, FM-caused pulmonary vessel stenosis was diagnosed. The diffuse fibrous proliferative tissue in the mediastinum of this patient compressed the bilateral hilar PAs and PVs at the same time, which was the direct cause of the combined stenosis of pulmonary arteries and veins.

After PAs stenting, we targeted superior PVs lesions. Selected PV angiography showed severe stenosis of the first drainage branch of left superior PV (Arrow in Fig. 1A) and the second drainage branch of right superior PV. In order to obtain high-resolution intraluminal morphological details that cannot be visualized by conventional angiography or computed tomography, we performed IVUS and OCT examinations.

Rationale for IVUS and OCT

Conventional angiography and CT cannot provide high‑resolution intraluminal morphological details. IVUS and OCT were used to precisely evaluate stenosis pattern, lumen shape, vessel wall characteristics, extramural compression, and stent expansion [2].

Advantages

Disadvantages

Limited tissue penetration; inability to assess deep extramural structures; requirement for blood clearance during imaging [3].

These examinations showed a triangle‑like lumen with extravascular fibrous tissue and minimal organized thrombus, without significant intimal hyperplasia (Fig. 1B and C). This morphological appearance is consistent with PV stenosis caused by FM and aids in differentiation from PV stenosis due to other etiologies.

After stenting, selected PV angiography showed no visual stenosis (Arrow in Fig. 1D); however, IVUS and OCT indicated slightly under-expanded stent (Fig. 1E and F). No further post-dilation was performed as no pressure gradient across the stent was detected on catheter measurement. The patient was treated with rivaroxaban 20 mg/day and aspirin 100 mg/day after the procedure.

Follow‑up

At the 3-month follow-up, the patient’s exertional dyspnea was significantly relieved. Repeated imaging examinations confirmed that the stents were patent without obvious restenosis.

Indications for intervention

Interventional therapy is indicated in patients with: (1) clinically significant symptoms including dyspnea, hemoptysis, or right heart failure; (2) hemodynamically significant stenosis; (3) elevated pulmonary artery pressure and right heart enlargement; (4) severe focal vascular obstruction; and (5) unsatisfactory response to optimal medical therapy.

In the present case, stenting was selected because of severe extrinsic compression and focal stenosis, where simple balloon dilation is often insufficient to maintain long-term patency.

Treatment strategies

Treatment strategies for FM-induced pulmonary vascular stenosis include medical therapy, endovascular intervention, and surgical treatment.

Medical therapy

Antifibrotic agents to slow mediastinal fibrosis progression; anticoagulation and antiplatelet therapy to prevent thrombosis; and symptomatic treatment for pulmonary hypertension and congestion.

Endovascular intervention

Balloon angioplasty and stent implantation to relieve vascular compression and restore blood flow. IVUS and OCT are used to guide stent selection and assess expansion.

Surgical treatment

Resection or decortication of mediastinal fibrous tissue, reserved for diffuse or refractory lesions with high surgical risk.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this case describes a triangle-like lumen observed by IVUS and OCT in fibrosing mediastinitis-induced pulmonary vein stenosis. This morphology may reflect extramural fibrotic compression rather than intrinsic intimal hyperplasia and may help differentiate FM-related pulmonary vein stenosis from other causes in selected cases [4]. Combined IVUS and OCT may provide complementary information for lesion characterization and interventional planning, but its routine use should be balanced against cost, availability, and operator expertise. Further cases and longer follow-up are needed to confirm the reproducibility and clinical significance of this finding.

Discussion

Pathophysiological mechanism of FM-induced pulmonary vascular stenosis

In this case, IVUS and OCT were not employed redundantly. Instead, they offered complementary mechanistic insights essential for characterizing this rare stenosis. IVUS provided a macroscopic assessment of vessel caliber and the longitudinal extent of extramural compressive forces, while OCT delivered microscopic visualization of the luminal architecture, specifically excluding significant intimal hyperplasia and confirming minimal organized thrombus and optimal stent apposition. Therefore, the rationale for combining these modalities was to elucidate the pathophysiological mechanism of an ambiguous lesion, rather than advocating for their routine use in all pulmonary vein interventions. Importantly, we do not advocate dual-modality intravascular imaging for all pulmonary vein stenosis cases. Newer hybrid IVUS-OCT devices or high-resolution IVUS alone may suffice in most routine scenarios. The rationale for using both in this patient was diagnostic uncertainty and the need to distinguish extrinsic compression from intimal pathology – a distinction with direct therapeutic implications. We acknowledge that this approach increases cost, procedure time, and requires specialized expertise, which limits its generalizability.

Unique contribution and clinical added value of this case

The added value of this case lies not simply in the use of intravascular imaging, but in the identification of a triangle-like luminal configuration in fibrosing mediastinitis-induced pulmonary vein stenosis. This morphology was associated with extramural fibrotic compression and the absence of significant intimal hyperplasia on IVUS and OCT. This finding is clinically relevant because idiopathic pulmonary vein stenosis is usually characterized by intrinsic intimal proliferation, whereas FM-related stenosis is mainly caused by external compression from mediastinal fibrotic tissue. Therefore, the triangle-like lumen observed in this case may serve as a potential morphological clue to prioritize FM in the differential diagnosis and to support a stent-based interventional strategy in selected patients with severe focal compression.In complex cases such as FM‑induced pulmonary vein stenosis, multimodality imaging (CT, angiography, IVUS, OCT) may be mandatory to fully characterize the lesion and guide therapy, as each modality provides complementary information that cannot be obtained from a single technique.

A brief literature review supports this concept. Li et al. reported a case of FM with pulmonary hypertension secondary to PV stenosis, emphasizing the role of CT and hemodynamic assessment [5]. Wang et al. used OCT to document rapidly progressive PV stenosis in FM, highlighting OCT’s ability to detect early intimal changes [6]. Alagha et al. described the challenges of differentiating extrinsic compression from intrinsic pathology in FM using conventional imaging alone [7]. Our case complements these reports by demonstrating that the combination of IVUS and OCT can identify a triangle‑like lumen as a potential morphological clue for FM‑induced PV stenosis, a finding not previously described.

This case also refines current practice by illustrating a practical multimodal assessment pathway. CT and angiography identified the anatomical distribution and severity of stenosis, IVUS assessed vessel size and extramural compression, and OCT provided high-resolution assessment of luminal morphology, intimal structure, organized thrombus, and stent apposition. The combined approach was used to clarify the mechanism of an unusual pulmonary vein stenosis rather than to advocate routine dual-modality imaging in all pulmonary vein interventions.

Limitations

We must acknowledge several limitations of this study. First, as a single-case report, the generalizability of the “triangle-like lumen” as a specific diagnostic sign for FM-induced pulmonary vein stenosis remains to be establishedthrough larger-scale studies. Second, the follow-up period was relatively short; therefore, long-term stent patency and clinical outcomes could not be determined.

Regarding the imaging modality, while the combined use of IVUS and OCT provided complementary information, this dual-modality approach is resource-intensive, costly, and requires dedicated expertise, which may limit its routine application in many centers. Crucially, this report cannot definitively conclude that combined imaging is superior to contemporary IVUS or OCT alone; rather, it suggests that dual-modality imaging may be selectively advantageous in anatomically complex cases requiring refined evaluation.

Finally, our case does not demonstrate superiority of combined imaging over either modality alone. The triangle-like lumen was visible on both IVUS and OCT; therefore, if only one modality had been used, the finding would still have been observed.
