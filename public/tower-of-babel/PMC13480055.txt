Moral resilience in the face of ethical conflicts in hospital nursing practice: a qualitative study
Authors: Gabrielle dos Santos Feijó, Jamila Geri Tomaschewski Barlem, Simoní Saraiva Bordignon, Graziele de Lima Dalmolin, Laurelize Pereira Rocha, Romario Daniel Jantara, Edison Luiz Devos Barlem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMoral resilience has become an increasingly relevant capacity in nursing, particularly in hospital settings, where recurring ethical dilemmas and organizational constraints may compromise nurses’ moral judgment and professional well-being. In this context, the present study aimed to understand moral resilience among nurses working in hospital practice.MethodsThis qualitative, exploratory, and descriptive study included 30 clinical nurses working in adult and pediatric medical and surgical units of a university hospital. Data were collected between July and August 2025 through semi-structured interviews. The textual corpus was processed using IRAMUTEQ software, applying the Descending Hierarchical Classification (DHC) method, and analyzed according to the principles of Discursive Textual Analysis.ResultsThe textual corpus generated five lexical classes, organized into three emergent categories: “Ethical dilemmas and learning processes in the construction of moral resilience,” “Interpersonal relationships, communication, and institutional limits in the exercise of moral resilience,” and “Processes of moral transformation and strengthening.” The findings revealed that nurses experience recurrent ethical conflicts in everyday hospital practice, which challenge their moral integrity, particularly when reconciling personal values, professional principles, and institutional demands. Moral resilience emerged as a dynamic and relational process, developed through experience, ethical reflection, interpersonal relationships, spirituality, and the confrontation of situations involving suffering, finitude, and organizational constraints.ConclusionsThe findings indicate that strengthening moral resilience depends not only on individual attributes but is deeply influenced by workplace relationships, institutional ethical climate, and organizational conditions. These results underscore the importance of educational and institutional strategies that promote dialogue, ethical reflection, interprofessional collaboration, and professional support in daily clinical practice, contributing to quality of care, patient safety, and nurses’ ethical well-being.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12910-026-01518-z.

Introduction

The hospital environment constitutes a setting of high ethical, technical, and emotional complexity, in which nurses play a central role in mediating clinical decision-making and comprehensive human care. Daily clinical practice is permeated by situations that extend beyond technical competence, requiring moral discernment in contexts marked by vulnerability, suffering, and finitude. In such settings, ethical dilemmas frequently emerge, demanding not only scientific competence but also strong moral values and the capacity to sustain moral integrity in the face of adversity [1, 2].

Nursing, as a profession grounded in care, faces challenges that span ethical and emotional dimensions. High-complexity hospital units expose nurses to urgent situations, patient suffering, and decision-making under pressure, requiring sensitivity and critical judgment. In these contexts, nurses must reconcile personal values, institutional demands, and patients’ individual needs, making ethical care a central axis of professional practice and an indispensable element for maintaining moral integrity [3–5]. Within this framework, moral resilience emerges as an essential construct for understanding how nurses respond to ethical adversity in clinical settings. It has been defined as the capacity to sustain or restore moral integrity in the face of moral complexity, distress, or uncertainty [2, 6, 7]. Rather than representing a fixed individual trait, moral resilience is increasingly understood as a dynamic and developmental process, involving the recognition of moral tensions, engagement in critical reflection, and the transformation of challenging experiences into opportunities for ethical learning and professional growth. The development of this capacity is influenced by both individual and contextual factors, including effective communication, interprofessional support, and continuous ethics education [8, 9]. In addition, institutional elements such as ethical climate, organizational support, and a culture of safety play a central role in shaping nurses’ ability to sustain ethical positions in adverse situations [10, 11].

Among the most significant ethical challenges that mobilize moral resilience is moral distress, experienced when professionals recognize the ethically appropriate course of action but are constrained from acting due to institutional, hierarchical, or circumstantial barriers. Moral distress represents one of the most complex and impactful phenomena in nursing, as it reflects a conflict between moral judgment and practical constraints, generating feelings of powerlessness, guilt, and emotional exhaustion [12, 13]. When persistent, it can undermine professional meaning, compromise motivation, and negatively affect both nurses’ well-being and patient safety [14, 15].

The relevance of moral resilience became even more evident during the COVID-19 pandemic, when nursing professionals were exposed to extreme vulnerability, resource scarcity, and the need for rapid decision-making under intense emotional strain [16, 17]. This context highlighted that ethical competence extends beyond normative knowledge, encompassing the capacity to cope with moral distress and to sustain commitment to fundamental values despite exhaustion and uncertainty [18, 19].

Authors such as Rushton and Schaik et al. [6, 20] emphasize that moral resilience should not be understood solely as an individual attribute, but rather as a relational and collective process sustained through peer support and institutional environments that promote ethical dialogue. This perspective broadens the understanding of nursing care, recognizing ethical practice as a form of resistance to the dehumanizing conditions that may affect healthcare work [21, 22].

Despite advances in the international literature, there remains a scarcity of studies addressing moral resilience from the perspective of nurses within the Brazilian context. This gap is particularly relevant given that national healthcare settings are shaped by specific structural, organizational, and sociocultural conditions that directly influence working environments, power relations, and the management of ethical conflicts. Moreover, existing studies have predominantly emphasized conceptual or quantitative approaches, with limited exploration of how moral resilience is experienced and constructed in everyday clinical practice. Qualitative investigations are therefore essential to access the meanings attributed by professionals to their moral experiences and to identify strategies emerging from practice that support the strengthening of ethical integrity [23, 24].

Therefore, this study aims to understand moral resilience among nurses working in hospital practice. Specifically, it seeks to explore how nurses experience ethically challenging situations, the strategies they use to cope with such situations, and the individual, relational, and institutional factors involved in the development of moral resilience. The study is delimited to clinical nurses working in a hospital setting, focusing on their lived experiences within this specific context.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study adopted a qualitative, exploratory, and descriptive design, grounded in the understanding that ethical phenomena are constructed through participants’ subjective interpretations and through the interaction between researcher and field. The study was conducted in different clinical units of a university hospital located in southern Brazil.

The university hospital is a public teaching and healthcare institution affiliated with the Brazilian Hospital Services Company (EBSERH) network and serves as a regional reference center within its coverage area. It has approximately 210 beds distributed across medical, surgical, maternal–child, and intensive care units, representing a medium- to high-complexity healthcare setting. Owing to its academic nature and the diversity of clinical contexts, the institution provided a suitable environment for investigating nurses’ ethical and moral experiences.

Participants and sampling

The hospital employs 103 clinical nurses, of whom 30 participated in this study. Participants were intentionally recruited from different clinical sectors to ensure heterogeneity of experiences and variability in the contexts analyzed.

Inclusion criteria were being a clinical nurse working in hospital care units for at least one year and agreeing to participate in the study. The minimum of one year of professional experience was established to ensure sufficient exposure to morally challenging situations, thereby enhancing depth and consistency in the narratives. Exclusion criteria included being on vacation or on any type of leave during the data collection period.

Purposive sampling was used to select nurses capable of providing rich and relevant information regarding morally challenging experiences in the hospital context. Sample size was guided by theoretical saturation, understood as the point at which additional interviews no longer contributed substantively new insights into the phenomenon under investigation. Saturation was monitored concurrently with data collection and analysis, through continuous comparison of emerging codes and categories. It was considered achieved when redundancy of themes was observed and no new relevant dimensions emerged from subsequent interviews, in accordance with established qualitative research criteria [25, 26].

Data collection

Data collection took place between July and August 2025 through semi-structured interviews organized into three sections: (1) sociodemographic and professional information; (2) a conceptual presentation of moral resilience, accompanied by three illustrative scenarios of recurring ethical dilemmas in clinical practice to facilitate understanding of the phenomenon; and (3) open-ended questions addressing moral resilience.

The semi-structured interview guide was developed by the research team specifically for this study based on the literature on moral resilience and ethical conflicts in nursing practice. The interview guide is available as Supplementary Material (Supplementary File 1).

The final section of the interview guide explored nurses’ perceptions of ethically challenging experiences, coping strategies, and elements considered necessary for the development of moral resilience, encompassing individual, relational, and institutional dimensions.

To ensure methodological quality, the study followed the principles of the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [27]. Prior to data collection, a pilot test was conducted with three doctoral nursing students from the Graduate Program in Nursing who had clinical experience in the study hospital. The pilot aimed to assess clarity and comprehensibility of the questions. No modifications were required, and the instrument was maintained in full for participant interviews.

Participants were invited in person through visits to each clinical unit across all work shifts to schedule interviews according to availability. Recruitment included morning, afternoon, and night shifts, ensuring participation from nurses with diverse routines and ethical experiences. There were no refusals or losses during recruitment and data collection.

Interviews were conducted in a private setting to ensure confidentiality and comfort. When in-person participation was not feasible, interviews were conducted remotely via Google Meet. Only the interviewer and participant were present during the interviews, which lasted approximately 30 to 45 min, depending on narrative depth.

At the beginning of each interview, participants received detailed explanations regarding the study’s objectives and procedures to foster a trusting environment conducive to open expression. Participants were informed that data would be used exclusively for research purposes and that anonymity would be ensured. After clarification, written informed consent was obtained prior to recording.

Face-to-face interviews were audio-recorded, while remote interviews were video-recorded. All interviews were fully transcribed by the researcher and returned electronically to participants for review, correction, and validation. Only after participant confirmation were the transcripts included in the analysis.

Researcher characteristics and reflexivity

The interviews were conducted by the principal researcher, a female nurse and Master’s student in Health Sciences at the time of data collection, with prior experience in qualitative research and academic training focused on ethical phenomena in clinical practice. The interviewer had no hierarchical relationship or prior personal relationship with the participants, which supported independent data collection. During interviews, she adopted a reflexive and non-directive stance to minimize potential personal influence on participants’ accounts.

Data analysis was initially conducted by one author and subsequently discussed and reviewed collectively with the remaining authors to enhance interpretive consistency. Participants confirmed the accuracy of transcripts, with full agreement regarding their content.

Potential sources of bias were considered throughout the research process. As the primary researcher had academic training and prior interest in ethical phenomena, there was a possibility of interpretive influence during data collection and analysis. To mitigate this, a reflexive stance was maintained, supported by systematic documentation of analytical decisions and discussions among the research team. In addition, participant validation of transcripts and collective review of analytical categories contributed to reducing subjective bias and enhancing the credibility of findings.

Data analysis

Data were processed using IRAMUTEQ (Interface de R pour les Analyses Multidimensionnelles de Textes et de Questionnaires), a free software widely used in qualitative research for textual analyses, ranging from word frequency calculations to Descending Hierarchical Classification (DHC) and similarity analysis. The software facilitated systematic organization and processing of textual data, contributing to analytical rigor and transparency [28, 29].

The Reinert method, also known as Descending Hierarchical Classification (DHC), was applied to identify and group vocabulary into stable and representative lexical classes derived from interview content.

IRAMUTEQ was used as a tool to assist in the initial organization and processing of the textual corpus, enabling the identification of lexical patterns and structural relationships present in the data. The results generated by the software, based on Descending Hierarchical Classification, guided but did not determine the analytical process. These results were subsequently interpreted using Discursive Textual Analysis (DTA), which provided the theoretical and methodological framework for in-depth analysis and the construction of meaning. Thus, IRAMUTEQ contributed to the systematization of the data, while DTA guided the interpretive and analytical stages, ensuring coherence between the computational support and the qualitative analysis.

Data analysis followed the principles of Discursive Textual Analysis as proposed by Moraes and Galiazzi [30]. The first stage, unitarization, involved fragmentation and deconstruction of texts to identify meaning units. The second stage, categorization, consisted of articulating and integrating these units to construct broader meanings and develop analytical categories. Finally, in the stage of capturing the emergent new, interpretive synthesis was conducted, reorganizing meanings into a metatext expressing comprehensive understanding of the studied phenomenon. Three categories emerged from this analytical process: (I) “Ethical dilemmas and learning processes in the construction of moral resilience”; (II) “Interpersonal relationships, communication, and institutional limits in the exercise of moral resilience”; and (III) “Processes of moral transformation and strengthening.”

Rigor and trustworthiness

Methodological rigor was ensured through criteria of credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability. Credibility was strengthened through participant validation of transcripts; dependability through systematic documentation of data collection and analysis procedures; and confirmability through the maintenance of an audit trail consisting of analytical notes, decision records, and successive versions of category development, enabling traceability of interpretive processes. Transferability was supported by providing a detailed description of the institutional context and study setting [31].

Ethical considerations

All ethical principles established by Resolution No. 510 of April 7, 2016, of the Brazilian National Health Council were observed, ensuring compliance with ethical standards for research in Human and Social Sciences [32]. The study received approval from the Research Ethics Committee (Approval No. 6.034.053) and formal authorization from the university hospital’s Office of Teaching and Research.

Based on the lexical structure generated by IRAMUTEQ, the text segments were interpreted using Discursive Textual Analysis, enabling the transformation of these classes into analytically meaningful categories that express a deeper understanding of the phenomenon, in line with the objective of understanding moral resilience in ethically challenging situations.

The dendrogram illustrates the organization of the textual corpus into lexical classes based on similarities in vocabulary usage. Classes positioned closer together indicate greater thematic proximity, while more distant classes reflect semantic differentiation, revealing the diversity of participants’ discourses and the coexistence of distinct perspectives on moral resilience [28, 29].

Analysis of Fig. 1 shows that the five classes are distributed across branches reflecting varying degrees of similarity and opposition in content. Class 5 appears more independent, suggesting a distinct dimension of moral resilience related to processes of transformation and strengthening. In contrast, Classes 1 and 4 are situated within the same broader analytical structure, indicating related aspects of ethical dilemmas and learning processes. Classes 2 and 3 exhibit strong proximity, reflecting shared meanings associated with interpersonal relationships and institutional dynamics. Together, these patterns highlight the multidimensional and relational nature of moral resilience in hospital nursing practice.

In light of Discursive Textual Analysis and considering the similarities in content identified during the analytical process, the results were organized into three categories that reflect key dimensions of moral resilience in hospital nursing practice. Category I, titled “Ethical dilemmas and learning processes in the construction of moral resilience,” comprises Classes 1 and 4 and concerns how nurses interpret and learn from ethically challenging situations. Category II, titled “Interpersonal relationships, communication, and institutional boundaries in the exercise of moral resilience,” encompasses Classes 2 and 3 and highlights the influence of relational and organizational factors. Finally, Category III, titled “Processes of moral transformation and strengthening,” corresponds to Class 5 and reflects deeper processes of ethical development.

Ethical dilemmas and learning processes in the construction of moral resilience

This category contributes to understanding moral resilience among nurses working in hospital practice by highlighting its gradual and processual development, influenced by ethical training, gaps between theory and practice, and learning accumulated throughout professional experience. Participants’ accounts reveal that although ethics is addressed during undergraduate education, preparation for managing complex ethical dilemmas and for developing moral resilience is perceived as insufficient, overly theoretical, and poorly connected to the realities of clinical practice.

In general, participants reported that moral resilience was not explicitly or comprehensively addressed during academic training. Rather, its development was primarily associated with time, practical experience, and accumulated professional learning.

“I think this issue of resilience, of course you already know what is right and what is wrong, and that is related to ethics […] but you refine that over time. It’s not during your undergraduate education; it comes with time, in practice, through learning and life experiences.” (E23).

“I think this issue of resilience, of course you already know what is right and what is wrong, and that is related to ethics […] but you refine that over time. It’s not during your undergraduate education; it comes with time, in practice, through learning and life experiences.” (E23).

“Resilience wasn’t really discussed. I only came across it during my master’s degree […] During undergraduate training, I practically never heard about it. We had an ethics course, which may have touched on something, but it was brief and focused more on research ethics than on clinical practice […] Some professors didn’t have much practical experience […] You really only learn by living it.” (E16).

“Resilience wasn’t really discussed. I only came across it during my master’s degree […] During undergraduate training, I practically never heard about it. We had an ethics course, which may have touched on something, but it was brief and focused more on research ethics than on clinical practice […] Some professors didn’t have much practical experience […] You really only learn by living it.” (E16).

Participants emphasized that undergraduate education provides conceptual foundations but remains insufficiently connected to real-world practice, limiting the development of applied ethical competencies. Ethical learning was described as fragmented and often disconnected from everyday professional demands.

“What’s really missing is learning how to deal with people. Being resilient means having a broader perspective, not seeing things in a narrow way. Undergraduate programs need to work more on helping students think outside the box […] Many programs focus on technical and practical aspects but don’t address the moral or relational dimensions […] This type of training is still lacking.” (E30).

“I received ethical education, but it was very abstract. I couldn’t really connect it to practice. Over time, through professional development, I sought knowledge that helped me face ethical dilemmas, but there wasn’t much connection between theory and practice.” (E5).

“I received ethical education, but it was very abstract. I couldn’t really connect it to practice. Over time, through professional development, I sought knowledge that helped me face ethical dilemmas, but there wasn’t much connection between theory and practice.” (E5).

“I developed my learning through reading legislation, especially when preparing for public exams […] Since I didn’t have much emphasis on this during undergraduate education, I had to seek it on my own.” (E27).

The transition to professional practice was described as particularly challenging, marked by insecurity and moral distress due to insufficient preparation for complex clinical situations.

“In nursing school, everything was very rigid. I remember practical exams with strict time limits […] But when you face a real patient, each situation is different […] I felt that nursing school didn’t prepare me for that. My first years in practice were difficult because of that gap.” (E3).

“It’s not that ethics wasn’t discussed, but it stayed very theoretical. In practice, it’s different […] When someone has hospital experience, they can better connect theory and practice. Theory is important, but it has to be adapted to real situations.” (E5).

With increasing professional experience, clinical practice emerged as the primary space for ethical learning and the progressive development of moral resilience.

Moral resilience was also related to technical-scientific competence, professional confidence, and ethical positioning.

Finally, participants emphasized that the work environment may either facilitate or hinder the development of moral resilience, particularly in contexts marked by workload and institutional fragilities.

Overall, these findings highlight moral resilience as a dynamic process constructed over time through the interplay between ethical education, clinical experience, scientific knowledge, and professional maturation.

Interpersonal relationships, communication, and institutional limits in the exercise of moral resilience

This category contributes to understanding moral resilience among nurses working in hospital practice by highlighting how communication, interpersonal relationships, and institutional dynamics both support and challenge its development. The accounts illustrate how moral resilience is expressed and negotiated within the hospital context, particularly in situations involving ethical conflicts, professional hierarchies, and interprofessional interactions.

Participants emphasized communication and dialogue as central strategies for managing ethical conflicts and strengthening moral resilience, particularly through collective discussion and team-based decision-making.

“We usually gather the team […] we talk together, discuss what needs to be discussed, and report it to the unit management, either formally or verbally […] so we can address the situation together with leadership.” (E1).

“We usually gather the team […] we talk together, discuss what needs to be discussed, and report it to the unit management, either formally or verbally […] so we can address the situation together with leadership.” (E1).

“When it’s a nursing technician under my supervision, I intervene immediately […] I call the person and talk. If it’s a colleague, we usually bring it to the unit manager. If it’s a physician, we take it to the unit manager and they address it.” (E4).

“When it’s a nursing technician under my supervision, I intervene immediately […] I call the person and talk. If it’s a colleague, we usually bring it to the unit manager. If it’s a physician, we take it to the unit manager and they address it.” (E4).

Communication spaces were also described as sources of emotional support and ethical learning, enabling shared reflection and collective coping with morally challenging situations.

“I lived and suffered, and by talking with colleagues about my suffering […] that’s how I matured in decision-making.” (E16).

“I lived and suffered, and by talking with colleagues about my suffering […] that’s how I matured in decision-making.” (E16).

Processes of moral transformation and strengthening

This category contributes to understanding moral resilience among nurses working in hospital practice by highlighting processes of ethical transformation and strengthening developed in response to morally challenging situations. Participants’ accounts describe experiences of conflict, suffering, and decision-making in which nurses are required to balance personal values, ethical principles, and institutional constraints. Within this process, moral resilience is progressively strengthened, becoming evident in the ability to sustain professional integrity in contexts marked by work overload, interprofessional tensions, and situations of human vulnerability.

Ethical dilemmas emerged in complex and deeply human situations, requiring nurses to go beyond technical protocols and engage in moral judgment, sensitivity, and ethical responsibility in decision-making.

“I had a disagreement with a physician about whether a child, around six or seven years old, should visit her mother, who was terminally ill and in a coma. In my view, the child did not need to see that situation, as there would be no benefit for the mother. The physician believed the child should see her to understand the situation. I disagreed and argued that the child should first be evaluated by a psychologist, and only then decide.” (E5).

Situations involving perceived insensitivity toward life generated feelings of indignation and moral conflict, affecting both professional practice and personal values.

“We had a complication, and the child died […] The physician looked at the resident and said, ‘Let’s practice intubation on this body.’ I found that extremely insensitive […] it felt like a violation of the body. That was one of the most disturbing experiences for me as a professional.” (E1).

Overall, these findings highlight moral resilience as a dynamic process developed through experience, critical reflection, spirituality, and moral courage. Although suffering is not eliminated, nurses progressively develop the capacity to sustain professional integrity in the face of adversity, reinforcing ethical practice as a fundamental dimension of nursing care.

Discussion

Nursing practice in hospital settings constitutes a field permeated by decisions that extend beyond the technical domain, requiring continuous engagement with ethical and moral conflicts. In this study, which aimed to understand moral resilience among nurses working in hospital practice, the findings indicate that nurses experience dilemmas that challenge their ethical education, professional autonomy, and capacity to sustain moral integrity under institutional pressures. The results demonstrate that moral resilience is not a strictly individual attribute, but a dynamic competence constructed through the interplay between professional experience, critical reflection, and dialogue in the face of adversities emerging from daily care practice. The discussion of the findings is organized according to the three main analytical categories identified in this study.

The narratives indicate that moral resilience manifests in a routine marked by decisions that challenge personal values and organizational demands [13]. Moral distress emerges when professionals recognize what would be morally appropriate but perceive themselves as unable to act according to their principles due to rigid hierarchies, insufficient institutional support, and intra-team conflicts [12]. This experience, described in the literature as a dissonance between ethical duty and contextual constraints, represents one of the most expressive features of nursing work [12, 14]. Furthermore, persistent moral distress may result in emotional exhaustion, diminished sense of purpose, and, in more severe cases, withdrawal from work, directly affecting quality and safety of care [15, 19]. In this scenario, moral resilience consolidates as an essential competence to sustain ethical integrity and preserve the meaning of caring amid institutional constraints that trigger moral distress [6, 12].

In the narratives, moral resilience appears as a competence continuously challenged in contexts where human dignity is perceived as threatened or when fundamental principles of care seem violated. In such circumstances, moral distress emerges as an expression of the dissonance between what professionals recognize as ethically appropriate and institutional restrictions that limit their action. Reports involving disrespect toward the body, futile prolongation of treatments, and neglect in the face of patient suffering were described as experiences of high emotional and ethical impact. Beyond signaling structural fragilities within the hospital environment, these situations also reflect difficulties in dealing with finitude and insufficient preparation of teams to conduct end-of-life processes centered on dignity and humanization. In convergence, the literature indicates that moral distress intensifies when the duty to relieve suffering conflicts with institutional impositions [1, 22, 34]. In this context, moral resilience consolidates as a competence that enables nurses to sustain coherence between values and practice, reaffirming commitment to human dignity even under institutional pressures [6, 10]. However, unlike studies that predominantly emphasize individual coping strategies, the present findings underscore the central role of relational and organizational factors in shaping moral resilience.

Confronting finitude emerges as a sensitive axis of work, particularly in pediatric contexts, where ethical suffering intensifies due to recognition of therapeutic limits and the need to accept that care, at certain moments, must translate into comfort, respect, and presence. This sensitivity toward end of life suggests moral maturation constructed through daily coexistence with pain and human contradictions. Studies by Ko and Bae [35] and Yu et al. [10] reinforce this understanding by indicating that ethical care in terminality requires emotional balance and moral resilience to discern the boundary between life prolongation and therapeutic obstinacy. Thus, terminality represents one of the most expressive contexts in which moral resilience is required, demanding ethical discernment, professional integrity, and the capacity to take a position amid care-related tensions [6, 10, 34].

The findings indicate that moral resilience develops through a process of ethical maturation, nourished by reflection on lived experiences. Moral distress, often described as powerlessness resulting from inability to act according to one’s values, appears associated with hierarchical structures, lack of opportunities to be heard, and organizational impositions that restrict autonomy [12, 14]. When combined with workload and resource scarcity, this scenario affects both nurses’ emotional health and quality and safety of care [15, 19]. In this sense, moral distress does not constitute the core phenomenon analyzed, but rather the context that both challenges and calls forth moral resilience. At the same time, narratives show that many professionals seek to transform suffering into learning and ethical growth [6, 12]. Critical reflection enables reinterpretation of painful experiences and strengthens moral resistance, which constitutes a central element of moral resilience. According to Schaik et al. [20], this competence develops when professionals find meaning in adversity, recognize limits, and reaffirm values. Moral resilience therefore does not merely signify enduring pressure, but sustaining integrity in the face of what threatens the meaning of caring.

Another relevant finding concerns perceived fragility in ethical preparation during academic training and its effects on practice. Many participants reported insecurity in assuming moral leadership positions and managing complex ethical dilemmas, suggesting that training still fails to provide sufficient tools for morally difficult decisions in real contexts. The literature converges in indicating that theoretical knowledge, when disconnected from reflective practices and meaningful formative experiences, is insufficient to sustain moral decision-making and the development of moral resilience [5, 11, 21]. Strengthening this competence therefore requires articulation between technical-scientific knowledge, ethical discernment, and the subjective capacity to bear the consequences of decisions in daily care practice. However, while previous studies often emphasize curricular inclusion of ethics as sufficient, the present findings suggest that such approaches may remain limited when not integrated with experiential and reflective learning in real clinical contexts.

The experiences analyzed also demonstrate that strengthening moral resilience occurs largely through interpersonal relationships and communication within teams. Dialogue, mutual respect, and recognition of others’ expertise emerge as protective elements, fostering ethical deliberation, collective support, and reduction of moral distress. These findings are supported by studies emphasizing active listening, ethical deliberation, and peer support as relevant strategies for developing moral resilience [6, 10, 23]. Conversely, environments marked by authoritarian hierarchies and ineffective communication tend to intensify feelings of powerlessness and frustration, hindering ethical leadership and compromising nurses’ autonomy [4, 8]. However, unlike studies that primarily frame communication as an individual competency, the present findings highlight its collective and organizational nature, emphasizing the role of team dynamics and institutional culture in shaping moral resilience.

Reported interprofessional conflicts highlight the complexity of hospital teamwork and the impact of power asymmetries on nursing moral agency. In many cases, nurses are placed in subordinate positions, particularly in relation to physicians, generating insecurity in confronting questionable conduct. However, narratives indicate that over time and with experience, greater firmness develops in sustaining decisions grounded in technical-scientific knowledge and ethical support. The literature recognizes this maturation as a component of moral resilience, associated with moral courage and fidelity to principles even within hierarchical structures [3, 18].

In this context, healthy interpersonal relationships consolidate as one of the most consistent pillars for moral strengthening. Open communication, empathy, and mutual respect sustain coping with adversities and favor collective strategies for overcoming challenges. Peer support and ethical deliberation reduce the impact of moral distress and strengthen belonging and psychological safety in the workplace. This context aligns with the concept of moral safety, understood as confidence that ethical concerns can be expressed without fear of judgment or punishment [36]. Environments that cultivate such protection tend to promote coherence, autonomy, and greater consistency between values and care practices.

Additionally, narratives highlight that the organizational environment directly influences expression of moral resilience. Work overload, insufficient human and material resources, and lack of managerial support were identified as barriers to ethical practice and emotional balance. Recent evidence indicates that institutional ethical climate and organizational support are associated with the ability to maintain moral integrity amid daily challenges [7, 16, 18]. Strengthening an ethical culture therefore extends beyond individual responsibility and requires institutional structures that value dialogue, emotional safety, and shared responsibility for care.

Another aspect concerns spirituality as a source of strength and coping. Faith, empathy, and respect for others’ beliefs were described as resources for sustaining emotional balance in the face of pain and suffering. The literature suggests that spirituality may operate as moral support, helping professionals reinterpret experiences and maintain purpose in care [2, 10]. In this sense, spirituality is not merely a private resource, but may represent an expression of humanity that connects professionals to the deeper meaning of caring.

Thus, moral resilience emerges as a capacity sustained by reflection on experiences and the ability to transform moral pain into learning. This reconstruction requires sensitivity to recognize vulnerabilities and courage to reaffirm principles amid adversity. According to Schaik et al. [20], moral resilience does not mean avoiding suffering, but confronting it with ethical awareness and converting it into moral strength and professional growth.

The findings also indicate that although nurses are recognized as essential members of multiprofessional teams, both technically and ethically, difficulties persist in affirming moral leadership. This barrier relates to historical devaluation of the profession and persistence of hierarchical cultures that subordinate nursing knowledge to medical knowledge, weakening professionals’ sense of belonging and confidence, and sometimes leading them to question the legitimacy of their decisions. Strengthening the ethical identity of the profession is therefore fundamental, recognizing that nurses’ autonomy is not restricted to technical execution but constitutes a moral commitment to care and defense of life in its human dimension [37, 38].

Finally, the evidence indicates that confronting moral distress and challenges in hospital environments cannot be attributed solely to individual effort. Institutions must assume responsibility in promoting ethical and emotional well-being through listening spaces, reflective meetings, active ethics committees, support groups, and continuing education programs. Such initiatives strengthen collective bonds, expand dialogue, and provide support amid daily adversities [9, 10, 39]. Institutions that value ethical reflection and acknowledge emotional impact of care tend to foster more cohesive and secure teams, with positive effects on quality of care and humanization of the hospital environment [7, 40].

Understanding moral resilience in hospital nursing requires recognizing care as a complex practice that integrates technique, sensitivity, and ethics. Addressing moral dilemmas depends not only on knowledge, but on the capacity to sustain integrity in the face of what threatens the meaning of caring. By reaffirming its ethical essence, nursing reaffirms its commitment to human dignity, justice, and preservation of life in its multiple expressions.

This study offers important contributions to the understanding of moral resilience among nurses working in hospital practice. From a theoretical perspective, it reinforces the conception of moral resilience as a relational competence rather than an individual attribute. From a methodological standpoint, the use of qualitative analysis enabled in-depth exploration of nurses’ lived experiences, capturing the complexity of ethical challenges in real-world contexts. Additionally, this study contributes contextually by providing evidence from the Brazilian healthcare setting, highlighting specific institutional and cultural factors that influence moral resilience.

The findings also have important implications for practice. In nursing education, they highlight the need to strengthen the integration between ethical theory and clinical practice, promoting reflective and experiential learning approaches. In healthcare institutions, the results underscore the importance of fostering ethical climates that support open communication, interprofessional collaboration, and moral deliberation. Strategies such as ethics rounds, reflective spaces, and continuing education programs may contribute to strengthening moral resilience among nurses and improving the quality of care.

As it was conducted in a single university hospital, the findings reflect a specific institutional context, which may limit their transferability to other healthcare settings with different organizational structures and cultures, particularly those that differ from the Brazilian healthcare system. Furthermore, the qualitative nature of the study, based on self-reported experiences, may be influenced by subjective interpretation and social desirability bias. These factors may affect the scope of the findings, particularly regarding their generalization. However, they do not compromise the depth of understanding achieved, which is consistent with the aims of qualitative research.

Conclusions

This qualitative study expands understanding of moral resilience as a dynamic and relational ethical process within hospital nursing practice. The findings indicate that moral distress is a recurrent experience among nurses, particularly when they recognize ethically appropriate conduct but are constrained by institutional hierarchies, organizational pressures, or practices that undermine human dignity in care.

Moral resilience emerged not as an individual trait, but as a competence developed over time through lived experience, sustained by ethical reflection and interpersonal relationships in the workplace. Situations such as end-of-life care, interprofessional conflicts, and structural limitations were identified as key contexts in which nurses’ moral integrity is challenged, requiring ethical discernment, moral courage, and sensitivity to human vulnerability.

The results highlight the importance of relational and contextual factors in sustaining moral resilience. Dialogue, mutual respect, peer support, and open communication function as protective elements that mitigate moral distress and strengthen nurses’ ethical agency. Spirituality also emerged as a relevant coping resource, contributing to the reinterpretation of experiences and the preservation of moral meaning in situations involving suffering and therapeutic limits. At the same time, structural and educational fragilities persist, including insufficient preparation to manage complex ethical dilemmas, work overload, resource scarcity, and limited institutional support.

From a practical perspective, these findings underscore the need to strengthen ethical climates in healthcare institutions by promoting open communication, interprofessional collaboration, and spaces for reflection and moral deliberation. In nursing education, integrating ethical theory with experiential and reflective learning is essential to better prepare professionals for ethically challenging situations.

This study contributes by positioning moral resilience as a relational and context-dependent competence, shaped by the interaction between moral distress, power relations, institutional context, and nurses’ ethical agency. Future research should explore moral resilience in different healthcare and cultural contexts, as well as investigate interventions aimed at strengthening ethical environments and supporting nurses’ moral agency and well-being.
