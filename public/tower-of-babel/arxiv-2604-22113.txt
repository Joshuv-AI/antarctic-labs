Dynamic solutions of next generation neural field models with delays Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Oleh E. Omel’chenko, Carlo R. Laing

Abstract
We study networks of theta neurons arranged on a ring with delayed interactions. In the continuum limit the systems are described by next generation neural field models with delays. We consider distributed delays with both finite and infinite support, and conduction delays. The stability of spatially uniform and localized bump states is determined, and we find that they undergo Hopf bifurcations as parameters related to the delays are varied. These bifurcations create traveling waves and “breathing” bump solutions. These dynamic solutions satisfy self-consistency equations and we show how to efficiently solve these equations. Following traveling waves and periodic solutions as parameters are varied provides a global picture of the influence of different delays on pattern formation processes in spatially extended networks of theta neurons.



keywords

neural field model, delays, theta neuron, Riccati equation.

Introduction

Time delays are ubiquitous in neural systems. Action potentials travel at finite speeds along axons and
dendrites, and synaptic processing is not instantaneous
[
13
,
67
,
18
]
.
Many authors have studied the effects of delays in neural
models
[
29
,
73
,
22
,
56
,
2
]
,
which often take the form of delay differential
equations (DDEs). Often, the only analytical progress that can be made in the study of DDEs
is the determination of the stability of a steady state, although periodic solutions
can be constructed in special cases
[
18
,
41
]
.
The accurate numerical solution of DDEs requires dedicated software such as the
Matlab solver
dde23

[
65
]
, and numerical bifurcation analysis of DDEs can be performed
using software such as DDE-BIFTOOL
[
23
]
.

One well-studied type of neural model is the neural field model, describing activity at a spatial
scale much greater than that of a single neuron
[
14
,
17
,
3
,
10
,
74
]
. Such models
typically take the form of an integro-differential equation, involving an integral over space to model
long range connections between neurons. Many authors have studied the effects of delays
in these classical neural field
models
[
59
,
7
,
6
,
72
,
28
,
70
,
60
,
55
]
.

However, the neural field models just mentioned are essentially phenomenological, not rigorously
derived from networks of spiking neurons. In contrast, the last decade has seen the introduction and study of
next generation
neural field models, derived from networks of theta
neurons
[
38
,
40
,
43
,
16
,
11
,
27
,
62
,
15
]
. The derivation relies on
the Ott/Antonsen ansatz
[
51
,
52
]
, a result showing the existence of a low-dimensional
attracting manifold on which the dynamics of infinite heterogeneous networks of phase oscillators
can be described by differential equations governing a small number of macroscopic variables.
One of the first applications of the Ott/Antonsen ansatz was to the study of chimera
states in networks of sinusoidally coupled Kuramoto phase oscillators
[
1
,
37
]
, but theta
neurons have the same mathematical form as Kuramoto oscillators and the ansatz is equally
applicable to infinite networks of them
[
44
,
38
]
. It is of interest to compare
the effects of delays in classical neural field models with the effects in next generation models.

Next generation neural field models take the form of a Riccati equation, i.e., the time derivative
of a complex-valued variable is given by a quadratic function of that variable, with
possibly time-dependent coefficients
[
43
]
.
Thus periodic solutions of these models are periodic solutions of periodically-forced Riccati equations.
If the periodic forcing is prescribed, the periodic solution of interest can be found in a computationally
efficient manner using a small number of numerical integrations of Riccati equations
[
49
,
48
,
43
]
.
In previous work we used this technique to study periodic solutions in
next generation neural field models
[
43
]
. One of the models studied there had a
single discrete delay
and we followed periodic solution of the model as the delay was varied.

In this paper we greatly extend the results in
[
43
]
to models in which there is a continuum
of delays, either distributed delays or conduction delays. For each of three models, we semi-analytically
determine the stability of spatially uniform and bump states. We show how to derive equations,
the solutions of which self-consistently describe
traveling waves and breathing bump solutions, and how to efficiently solve these equations. Following
solutions of these equations as parameters are varied we find that traveling waves and breathing bumps are created in Hopf bifurcations
from spatially uniform and bump states, respectively. In many cases we determine the stability of solution
branches by direct numerical simulation of the corresponding neural field model.
Note that the use of self-consistency arguments to study the existence of solutions in infinite networks
of phase oscillators goes back to at least Kuramoto
[
35
]
.

Our basic model is a system
of
N
theta neurons described by the equations

\frac{d\theta_{j}}{dt}=1-\cos{\theta_{j}}+(1+\cos{\theta_{j}})(\eta_{j}+\kappa I_{j}(t)),\qquad j=1,2\dots,N.

(1)

The heterogeneity of neurons results
from their excitability parameters
\eta_{j}
being
chosen from a Lorentzian distribution

\Lambda(\eta)=\frac{\Delta}{\pi}\frac{1}{(\eta-\eta_{0})^{2}+\Delta^{2}}

with center
\eta_{0}
and half-width at half maximum
\Delta
. In the absence of coupling
(
\kappa=0
) a single theta neuron is the normal form of the saddle-node-on-invariant-circle
(SNIC) bifurcation
[
24
,
26
]
. The neuron is excitable if its intrinsic drive
\eta_{j}
is negative,
and fires periodically with frequency
\sqrt{\eta_{j}}/\pi
if
\eta_{j}>0
.
The theta neuron is equivalent to a quadratic integrate-and-fire (QIF) neuron
under the assumption of infinite firing threshold and reset values
[
45
]
.

Neurons interact synaptically by sensing input currents
I_{j}(t)
,
which consist of impulses coming
from all neurons in the system.
In addition, the coupling strength
\kappa

characterizes the sensitivity of neurons
to these currents. (Coupling with gap junctions could also be
included
[
40
,
50
]
, but for simplicity we do not do so.)
To mimic the spatial organization
of the network, we assume that neurons
are arranged on a ring, i.e., a one-dimensional array
with periodic boundary conditions,
and that their interaction depends
on the distance between them.
A simple example of such a model was proposed in
[
38
]
,
where the input currents in (
1
) had the form

I_{j}(t)=\frac{2\pi}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}G_{jk}P(\theta_{k}(t)),

(2)

with a pulsatile function

P(\theta)=(2/3)(1-\cos{\theta})^{2}

representing the pulse of current emitted
by a neuron when
\theta
increases through
\pi
.
(Note that, as explained in
[
44
]
,
the exponent of
2
in
P(\theta)

can be varied to make the function more narrow.)
The coupling weights in (
2
)
are defined by
G_{jk}=G(2\pi(j-k)/N)

for some
2\pi
-periodic function
G(\cdot)
.
Thus, the resulting model (
1
), (
2
) represents
N
theta neurons
equally-spaced around a ring of circumference
2\pi
.
Studying a ring of neurons is appropriate when modeling behavior related to an angular
variable such as head direction
[
78
]
or angular distance in working memory
tasks
[
75
]
. Other studies of ring networks include
[
36
,
27
,
40
,
60
]
.

The spatio-temporal patterns
observed in model (
1
), (
2
)
were analyzed in detail in
[
47
,
43
]
.
Roughly speaking, they include spatially uniform
quiescent and active states as well as bump states,
i.e., localized patches of activity,
which can be stationary, moving, or “breathing” (periodically varying width).
In the limit
N\gg 1
, the properties
of all the above states can be described
using a neural field equation
following from application of the Ott/Antonsen theory
[
51
,
44
]
.
In this paper, we perform a similar analysis,
but in a much more complex setup,
where the interaction currents
I_{j}(t)

contain physically motivated time-delays.
More specifically, we consider two types of delays.

(i) When impulses from other neurons arrive
at the
j
th neuron, it may exhibit some delay
before responding to them. Also,
the effect of the impulses is not necessarily instantaneous,
but may continue over time.
Both properties can be modeled by an input current with distributed delays

I_{j}(t)=\frac{2\pi}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}G_{jk}\int_{0}^{\infty}S(t^{\prime})P(\theta_{k}(t-t^{\prime}))\>dt^{\prime}=\frac{2\pi}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}G_{jk}\int_{-\infty}^{t}S(t-s)P(\theta_{k}(s))\>ds,

(3)

where the delay kernel
S(t)
is zero up until
a fixed time
\tau>0

and is a non-increasing function for
t\geq\tau

such that
\int_{0}^{\infty}S(t)dt=1
.
Such kernels have been studied in
[
42
]
,
for example.

For model (
1
), (
3
),
we distinguish two qualitatively different situations:
The case of a monotonically decreasing kernel
S(t)

with infinite support, an example of which is

S(t)=\begin{cases}0,&t<\tau,\\
a^{-2}(t-\tau)e^{-(t-\tau)/a},&t\geq\tau\end{cases}

(4)

with
a>0
,
and the case of a constant finite-support kernel

S(t)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{a}&\tau<t<\tau+a,\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases}

(5)

with
a>0
, as considered in, for example
[
56
]
. Note that in both cases
we have
\lim_{a\to 0}S(t)=\delta(t-\tau)
, i.e., just a discrete delay of
\tau
.

(ii) Another type of delay can occur in a neural network due to the finite speed of impulse transmission.
We call such delays conduction (or propagation) delays and represent them using input currents of the form

I_{j}(t)=\frac{2\pi}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}G_{jk}P\left(\theta_{k}\left(t-\tau-\frac{2\pi|j-k|}{Nc}\right)\right),

(6)

where
\tau
is a fixed delay
and
c
is the conduction speed.
Note that the expression
|j-k|
here means

\min(|j-k|,N-|j-k|)
and thus
takes into account periodic boundary conditions.
Eqs. (
1
), (
6
)
model a system in which impulses arrive
after a time equal to a fixed delay
\tau

(which may be zero) plus a time proportional
to the distance they have traveled.
Similar types of delays were studied in
[
64
,
34
,
77
]
, and
specifically in the context of neural models in
[
20
,
72
,
71
,
69
,
61
]
.

2
Methods

In the continuum limit case
N\to\infty
,
the dynamics of the discrete system (
1
) admits a probabilistic description.
Assuming that
x_{j}=2\pi j/N
denotes
the position of the
j
th neuron in the interval
[0,2\pi]
,
we can describe the state of system (
1
)
with the probability density
\rho(\theta,x,\eta,t)

such that
\rho(\theta,x,\eta,t)d\theta\>dx\>d\eta

is the probability of finding neurons
with
\theta_{j}\in(\theta,\theta+d\theta)
,

x_{j}\in(x,x+dx)

and
\eta_{j}\in(\eta,\eta+d\eta)
at time
t
.
Using the Ott/Antonsen theory
[
51
,
52
]
,
it can be shown that the long-term dynamics
of the density
\rho(\theta,x,\eta,t)

is attracted to an invariant manifold
parametrized by the complex-valued variable

z(x,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{i\theta}\rho(\theta,x,\eta,t)d\theta\>d\eta

known as the local order parameter
[
47
,
38
]
,
which by definition satisfies
|z(x,t)|\leq 1
.
Moreover, the dynamics on this manifold
is described by the neural field equation

\frac{\displaystyle\partial z}{\displaystyle\partial t}=\frac{\displaystyle[i(\eta_{0}+\kappa I(x,t))-\Delta](1+z)^{2}}{\displaystyle 2}-i\frac{\displaystyle(1-z)^{2}}{\displaystyle 2},

(7)

where
\eta_{0}
and
\Delta
are parameters of the Lorentzian distribution
\Lambda(\eta)

and
I(x,t)
is an integral term corresponding
to the mean-field synaptic coupling in the discrete system (
1
).
In particular, for model (
1
), (
3
) we have

I(x,t)=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}G(x-y)\int_{-\infty}^{t}S(t-s)H\left(z\left(y,s\right)\right)ds\>dy

(8)

where (for the particular function
P(\theta)
we use)

H(z)=1-\frac{\displaystyle 2}{\displaystyle 3}(z+\overline{z})+\frac{\displaystyle 1}{\displaystyle 6}(z^{2}+\overline{z}^{2})\equiv 1+2\>\>\mathrm{Re}\>{[D(z)]}

(9)

and
D(z)=-2z/3+z^{2}/6
.
On the other hand, for model (
1
),
(
6
) we have

I(x,t)=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}G(x-y)H\left(z\left(y,t-\tau-\frac{\displaystyle|x-y|}{\displaystyle c}\right)\right)dy

(10)

where
H(z)
is again given by (
9
).
Note that due to periodic boundary conditions
the expression
|x-y|
in (
10
)
means
\min(|x-y|,2\pi-|x-y|)
,
or equivalently
\arccos(\cos(x-y))
.

Remark 1
.

In the case of the delay kernel (
4
),
we can use the linear chain trick
[
4
,
33
]

to rewrite the system (
7
), (
8
) as one with only a discrete delay,
rather than a continuum of delays.
Indeed, for

S^{\prime}(t)=a^{-2}e^{-(t-\tau)/a}-a^{-3}(t-\tau)e^{-(t-\tau)/a}=a^{-2}e^{-(t-\tau)/a}-S(t)/a.

Thus

\frac{\displaystyle\partial I(x,t)}{\displaystyle\partial t}=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}G(x-y)\int_{-\infty}^{t}S^{\prime}(t-s)H\left(z\left(y,s\right)\right)ds\>dy=[Y(x,t)-I(x,t)]/a

Y(x,t)\equiv\frac{1}{a}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}G(x-y)\int_{-\infty}^{t-\tau}e^{-(t-\tau-s)/a}H\left(z\left(y,s\right)\right)ds\>dy.

Differentiating

\frac{\displaystyle\partial Y(x,t)}{\displaystyle\partial t}=\frac{1}{a}\left[\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}G(x-y)H\left(z\left(y,t-\tau\right)\right)dy-Y(x,t)\right].

(12)

Thus we have written system (
7
), (
8
) with the delay kernel (
4
)
as two non-delayed equations, (

and one with a discrete delay, (

like

, still reducing the original equation to a finite set of non-delayed equations
and one with a discrete delay

, but for the purpose of explaining our
technique we use the kernel (

).

In the case of the finite-support

We consider each of the above continuum limit systems

Using our previous results from
[

]
,
we choose the parameters

,

,

We observe that Hopf bifurcations of a spatially uniform steady state typically give rise to traveling waves that represent moving bumps
in the corresponding discrete theta neuron systems.
(Note that in the degenerate case,
a spatially uniform periodic solution
may appear instead of a traveling wave.)
On the other hand, Hopf bifurcations of stationary bump states typically give rise to periodic solutions
of neural field equations
that represent breathing bumps.
To calculate entire branches of traveling waves
and periodic spatially nonuniform solutions,
we derive self-consistency equations
that significantly speed up these calculations.
As a result, we obtain a global picture of the influence
of different delays on pattern formation processes
in spatially extended networks of theta neurons.

Note that since the values of stationary states do not depend on the delay parameters that
we vary, we do not expect to observe stationary bifurcations such as a saddle-node bifurcation
as delay parameters are varied. While all of our analysis involves the continuum limit equation (

we show some solutions of the discrete network (
1

System without delays

The neural field equation (
7
)
with a non-delayed input current (

)
was analyzed in

\displaystyle\hat{w}_{1}

where angle brackets indicate an average over

4
Distributed delays — infinite support

In this section, we consider the neural field equation (

) with an input current term
containing distributed delays (
8

More specifically, we analyze the case
of a delay kernel (

We perform the following steps.
First, we conduct a linear stability analysis

4.1
Stability of stationary states

We start by considering the stability of stationary states,
generalizing the results in
[
47
]
.
Suppose that Eqs. (
7
), (
8
)
have a time-independent solution

Letting

\displaystyle\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}

\displaystyle\mu(x)v+i\kappa\zeta(x)

\mu(x)=\left[i\left(\eta_{0}+\kappa\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}G(x-y)H(Z(y))\>dy\right)-\Delta\right](1+Z(x))+i(1-Z(x))

\displaystyle i\kappa\zeta(x)\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}G(x-y)\left[D^{\prime}(Z(y))\left\{v_{+}(y)\frac{e^{\lambda(t-\tau)}}{(1+a\lambda)^{2}}+\overline{v}_{-}(y)\frac{e^{\overline{\lambda}(t-\tau)}}{(1+a\overline{\lambda})^{2}}\right\}\right.

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left.\overline{D^{\prime}(Z(y))}\left\{\overline{v}_{+}(y)\frac{e^{\overline{\lambda}(t-\tau)}}{(1+a\overline{\lambda})^{2}}+v_{-}(y)\frac{e^{\lambda(t-\tau)}}{(1+a\lambda)^{2}}\right\}\right]dy.

\lambda\begin{pmatrix}v_{+}\\
v_{-}\end{pmatrix}=\mathcal{L}(\lambda)\begin{pmatrix}v_{+}\\
v_{-}\end{pmatrix},

(24)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\lambda)\begin{pmatrix}v_{+}\\
v_{-}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\mu(x)&0\\
0&\overline{\mu(x)}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}v_{+}\\
v_{-}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{i\kappa e^{-\lambda\tau}}{(1+a\lambda)^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\zeta(x)&\zeta(x)\\
-\overline{\zeta(x)}&-\overline{\zeta(x)}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}G(x-y)D^{\prime}(Z(y))v_{+}(y)\>dy\\
\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}G(x-y)\overline{D^{\prime}(Z(y))}v_{-}(y)\>dy\end{pmatrix}.

Here, as usual, we say that
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}

is the spectral value of Eq. (
24
) if the operator
\lambda\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{L}(\lambda)
,
where
\mathcal{I}
is the identity operator,
is not invertible. If, in addition,
\lambda\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{L}(\lambda)
is a Fredholm operator
of index zero, we say that
\lambda
belongs
to the discrete spectrum
\sigma_{\mathrm{discr}}
,
while otherwise
\lambda
belongs to the essential spectrum
\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}
.

The essential spectrum corresponding to
Z(x)

is determined by the first term
in the definition of
\mathcal{L}(\lambda)

and has the form

\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}=\{\mu(x):x\in[0,2\pi]\}\cup\{\mathrm{c.c.}\},

where
\{\mathrm{c.c.}\}
is the complex conjugate of the previous term. As for the discrete spectrum,
it can be found as follows.
Because of the form of
G(x)
, see (
14
), we can write

\begin{pmatrix}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}G(x-y)D^{\prime}(Z(y))v_{+}(y)\>dy\\
\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}G(x-y)\overline{D^{\prime}(Z(y))}v_{-}(y)\>dy\end{pmatrix}=V_{1}+V_{2}\cos{x}+V_{3}\sin{x}

(25)

where
V_{1,2,3}\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Substituting (
25
) into (
24
) we have

\begin{pmatrix}\lambda-\mu(x)&0\\
0&\lambda-\overline{\mu(x)}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}v_{+}\\
v_{-}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{i\kappa e^{-\lambda\tau}}{(1+a\lambda)^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\zeta(x)&\zeta(x)\\
-\overline{\zeta(x)}&-\overline{\zeta(x)}\end{pmatrix}\left[V_{1}+V_{2}\cos{x}+V_{3}\sin{x}\right]

and thus, assuming
\lambda\notin\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}
, solving this equation for
v_{+}
and
v_{-}
and multiplying on the
left by a diagonal matrix containing the derivatives of
D
we have

\begin{pmatrix}D^{\prime}(Z(x))v_{+}\\
\overline{D^{\prime}(Z(x))}v_{-}\end{pmatrix}=L(x,\lambda)\left[V_{1}+V_{2}\cos{x}+V_{3}\sin{x}\right]

(26)

where

L(x,\lambda)=\begin{pmatrix}D^{\prime}(Z(x))&0\\
0&\overline{D^{\prime}(Z(x))}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\lambda-\mu(x)&0\\
0&\lambda-\overline{\mu(x)}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\frac{i\kappa e^{-\lambda\tau}}{(1+a\lambda)^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\zeta(x)&\zeta(x)\\
-\overline{\zeta(x)}&-\overline{\zeta(x)}\end{pmatrix}

is a
2\times 2
matrix. Writing

both sides by
G(x-y)
and integrating

\pi

\displaystyle V_{1}+V_{2}\cos{x}+V_{3}\sin{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}G(x-y)L(y,\lambda)\left[V_{1}+V_{2}\cos{y}+V_{3}\sin{y}\right]dy

\displaystyle=A\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}L(y,\lambda)\left[V_{1}+V_{2}\cos{y}+V_{3}\sin{y}\right]dy

\displaystyle+B\cos{x}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}L(y,\lambda)\cos{y}\left[V_{1}+V_{2}\cos{y}+V_{3}\sin{y}\right]dy

\displaystyle+B\sin{x}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}L(y,\lambda)\sin{y}\left[V_{1}+V_{2}\cos{y}+V_{3}\sin{y}\right]dy.

Matching coefficients of the constant,
\cos{x}
and
\sin{x}

this gives a set of three simultaneous homogeneous linear equations for

that will have a non-zero solution if the determinant of

B\langle L(x,\lambda)\cos{x}\rangle&B\langle L(x,\lambda)\cos^{2}{x}\rangle&B\langle L(x,\lambda)\cos{x}\sin{x}\rangle\\
B\langle L(x,\lambda)\sin{x}\rangle&B\langle L(x,\lambda)\sin{x}\cos{x}\rangle&B\langle L(x,\lambda)\sin^{2}{x}\rangle\end{pmatrix}

and angle brackets indicate an average over
[-\pi,\pi]
, applied to each component
of a matrix separately. Solutions of the equation

|I_{6}-C(\lambda)|=0

(27)

determine the discrete spectrum

associated with the stability of the stationary state

.

4.2
Traveling waves

Each Hopf bifurcation shown in Fig.
1
corresponds
to the emergence of a traveling wave solution
of Eqs. (
7
), (
8
)
from the same spatially uniform state.
To show this and to find the entire branch
of traveling waves, we use a special semi-analytical approach.

First, we recall that according to Remark
1
,
system (
7
), (
8
)
with delay kernel (
4
)
is equivalent to the three equations (
7
), (
11
) and (
12
).
For a traveling wave moving to the left with a fixed speed
v
,
the profile of the wave in the comoving frame
\xi=x+vt

is given by a
2\pi
-periodic solution of

v\frac{\displaystyle dz}{\displaystyle d\xi}=\frac{\displaystyle[i(\eta_{0}+\kappa I(\xi))-\Delta](1+z)^{2}}{\displaystyle 2}-i\frac{\displaystyle(1-z)^{2}}{\displaystyle 2}

(30)

where

v\frac{\displaystyle dI}{\displaystyle d\xi}=(Y-I)/a

(31)

and

v\frac{\displaystyle dY}{\displaystyle d\xi}=\frac{1}{a}\left[\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}G(\xi-y)H\left(z\left(y-v\tau\right)\right)dy-Y\right].

(32)

Using the form of
G(x)
, see (
14
), we can write (
32
) as

\displaystyle v\frac{\displaystyle dY}{\displaystyle d\xi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{a}\left[A\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}H(z(y-v\tau))dy+B\cos{\xi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}H(z(y-v\tau))\cos{y}\>dy\right.

(33)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left.B\sin{\xi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}H(z(y-v\tau))\sin{y}\>dy-Y\right],

the
2\pi
-periodic solution of which is

Y(\xi)=\alpha+\beta\cos{\xi}+\gamma\sin{\xi}

for some constants
\alpha,\beta
and
\gamma

that depend in a simple explicit way on the values of the integrals
in (
33
). Given this form of
Y
, the
2\pi
-periodic solution
of (
31
) is
I(\xi)=\delta+\epsilon\cos{\xi}+\phi\sin{\xi}

for some constants
\delta,\epsilon
and
\phi
that depend in a simple and explicit way
on the coefficients in
Y
.

An important ingredient of the following analysis
is a special property of Eq. (
30
), described in
[
49
]
:
for any
v,\Delta>0
, any
2\pi
-periodic coefficient
I(\xi)
,
and any values of the coefficients
\eta_{0}
and
\kappa
,
Eq. (
30
) has one
2\pi
-periodic solution
z(\xi)

such that
|z(\xi)|<1
. This solution is not explicitly known,
but it can be easily computed using the fact
that (
30
) is a periodically-forced Riccati equation.
Put briefly, one numerically integrates (
30
) for
0\leq\xi\leq 2\pi

three times with three different initial conditions.
The three final states are enough to determine the initial condition
of (
30
) for which the solution is
2\pi
-periodic
(and less than one in magnitude), and this
equation is then integrated a fourth time with this initial condition
to construct the periodic solution,
z(\xi)
.

From the above explanation, it is clear that if we are only interested
in
2\pi
-periodic solutions of (
30
)–(
32
)
such that
|z(\xi)|<1
, it is sufficient to consider only
I(\xi)
as the unknown function,
while the other unknowns
z(\xi)
and
Y(\xi)
can be reconstructed from it.
Moreover, for the coupling function
G(x)
given by (
14
),
the unknown function
I(\xi)
is specified by three scalars,
which significantly reduces the complexity of the problem.
Putting everything together, we have an algorithm
for describing traveling wave solutions of (
7
), (
8
)
with delay kernel (
4
) in a self-consistent way:

1.

Given values of
\delta,\epsilon
and
\phi
describing the profile of
I(\cdot)
in a snapshot
of a traveling wave solution of (
7
) for some set of parameter values,
construct
I(\xi)
and
find the
2\pi
-periodic solution
z(\xi)
of (
30
) with
|z(\xi)|<1
.

2.

Given this
z(\xi)
, calculate
H(z(\xi))
and write it as

H(z(\xi))=\Omega+\Phi\cos{(\xi)}+\Psi\sin{(\xi)}+\mbox{higher harmonics}

for some known constants
\Omega,\Phi
and
\Psi
, and perform the integrals
in (
33
).

3.

Once the integrals
in (
33
) are known, calculate the constants describing
Y(\xi)
:

\alpha,\beta
and
\gamma
.

4.

Since
Y(\xi)
is now known, substitute it into (
31
) and solve
for
I(\xi)
, which will be of the
form
I(\xi)=\tilde{\delta}+\tilde{\epsilon}\cos{\xi}+\tilde{\phi}\sin{\xi}
.
If
\tilde{\delta}=\delta,\tilde{\epsilon}=\epsilon
and
\tilde{\phi}=\phi
we are done.

Note that due to the translational invariance of the system we can set
\epsilon=0
.
In practice we solve

\delta-\tilde{\delta}=0\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad\phi-\tilde{\phi}=0

along with the pinning condition
\tilde{\epsilon}=0
,
giving three equations in three unknowns (
\delta,\phi
and
v
).
These equations are solved numerically using Newton’s method, and solutions can be followed
as parameters are varied using pseudo-arclength continuation
[
39
,
30
]
.
The algorithm is extremely
quick to run, as most of the computational effort goes into the four numerical integrations
of (
30
). Note that our method does not involve discretization of the spatial domain,
and numerical integration of (
30
) can be performed to whatever accuracy is required simply
by decreasing tolerances in the numerical integrator used. (See below for more
discussion of efficiency.)

Following solutions of the equations that self-consistently describe
a traveling wave as parameters are varied,
we find that they are created in the previously found Hopf bifurcations
of the spatially uniform state
Z_{3}
, the locations of which are shown in blue in Fig.
1
.
Sometimes the bifurcations are supercritical and sometimes subcritical.
This is shown in Fig.
3
where we follow a traveling wave as

is varied
for two different values of
\tau
.
The stability of the traveling waves was checked empirically.
We used the computed triplet
(z(\xi),I(\xi),Y(\xi))
as an initial condition
in Eqs. (
7
), (
11
) and (
12
).
By adding a small perturbation to it, we checked
whether the dynamics of (
7
), (
11
), (
12
)
converges to the predicted traveling wave.
If so, we added a symbol (triangle or circle) to Fig.
3
.
In this way we found that for
\tau=1.5
, the spatially uniform state
loses stability supercritically with decreasing

and gives rise to a traveling wave of arbitrarily small amplitude
(dashed line in Fig.
3
).
But for
\tau=3
the bifurcation is subcritical
(solid line in Fig.
3
),
giving a region of bistability between the red and blue curves
(for this value of
\tau
) in Fig.
1

where the spatially uniform state and a traveling wave are both stable.
Saddle-node bifurcations of traveling waves were also found for other values of
\tau

and are shown by the red curves in Fig.
1
.

4.3
Periodic solutions with spatial structure

We now show that each Hopf bifurcation
in Fig.
2
corresponds
to the emergence of a periodic solution
of Eqs. (
7
), (
8
)
from a stationary bump state.
For this, we develop another version
of the self-consistency argument.

Suppose that
z(x,t)
is a solution of (
7
), (
8
)
such that
z(x,t+T)=z(x,t)
with some
T>0
.
Then in the case of delay kernel (
4
),
the triplet
(z(x,t),I(x,t),Y(x,t))
is a solution
of (
7
), (
11
), (
12
) with the same period
T
.
Write (
12
) as

\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\partial Y(x,t)}{\displaystyle\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{a}\left[A\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}H(z(y,t-\tau))dy+B\cos{x}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}H(z(y,t-\tau))\cos{y}\>dy\right.

(34)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left.B\sin{x}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}H(z(y,t-\tau))\sin{y}\>dy-Y(x,t)\right].

Next, writing
Y(x,t)
as a Fourier series in space with time-dependent coefficients we see that all
coefficients decay to zero except those multiplying the spatially-constant and
\cos{x}
and

\sin{x}
terms. We shift our solution in space so that the coefficient of the
\sin{x}
term is
zero and thus write

\displaystyle Y(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{0}+\sum_{m=1}^{M}[a_{m}\cos{(m\gamma t)}+b_{m}\sin{(m\gamma t)}]

(35)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left[c_{0}+\sum_{m=1}^{M}[c_{m}\cos{(m\gamma t)}+d_{m}\sin{(m\gamma t)}]\right]\cos{x}

where
\gamma=2\pi/T
. The input current
I(x,t)
will have a similar expansion:

\displaystyle I(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}+\sum_{m=1}^{M}[\alpha_{m}\cos{(m\gamma t)}+\beta_{m}\sin{(m\gamma t)}]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left[\gamma_{0}+\sum_{m=1}^{M}[\gamma_{m}\cos{(m\gamma t)}+\delta_{m}\sin{(m\gamma t)}]\right]\cos{x}.

Note that, strictly speaking, the Fourier expansions of
Y(x,t)
and
I(x,t)

contain coefficients with all positive integer indices
m
,
but since we are only looking for an approximate solution
of (
7
), (
11
), (
12
),
we truncate these series at a finite number
M
.

Substituting the above expansions into (
11
),
we find
\alpha_{0}=a_{0},\gamma_{0}=c_{0}
and

\displaystyle-m\gamma\alpha_{m}

\displaystyle=(b_{m}-\beta_{m})/a,

(36)

\displaystyle m\gamma\beta_{m}

\displaystyle=(a_{m}-\alpha_{m})/a,

(37)

\displaystyle-m\gamma\gamma_{m}

\displaystyle=(d_{m}-\delta_{m})/a,

(38)

\displaystyle m\gamma\delta_{m}

\displaystyle=(c_{m}-\gamma_{m})/a

(39)

for
m=1,2,\dots,M
.
These are linear simultaneous equations, so given the coefficients of
Y
,

\{a_{0},\dots,d_{M}\}
, we can easily find
the coefficients of
I
,
\{\alpha_{0},\dots,\delta_{M}\}
.

Similarly, write the
T
-periodic functions

A\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}H\left(z\left(y,t\right)\right)dy=e_{0}+\sum_{m=1}^{M}[e_{m}\cos{(m\gamma t)}+f_{m}\sin{(m\gamma t)}]

(40)

and

B\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}H\left(z\left(y,t\right)\right)\cos{y}\>dy=g_{0}+\sum_{m=1}^{M}[g_{m}\cos{(m\gamma t)}+h_{m}\sin{(m\gamma t)}].

(41)

Then the delayed versions of these are

\displaystyle A\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}H\left(z\left(y,t-\tau\right)\right)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e_{0}+\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left\{[e_{m}\cos{(m\gamma\tau)}-f_{m}\sin{(m\gamma\tau)}]\cos{(m\gamma t)}\vphantom{\sum}\right.

(42)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left.\vphantom{\sum}[e_{m}\sin{(m\gamma\tau)}+f_{m}\cos{(m\gamma\tau)}]\sin{(m\gamma t)}\right\}

and

\displaystyle B\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}H\left(z\left(y,t-\tau\right)\right)\cos{y}\>dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g_{0}+\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left\{[g_{m}\cos{(m\gamma\tau)}-h_{m}\sin{(m\gamma\tau)}]\cos{(m\gamma t)}\vphantom{\sum}\right.

(43)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left.\vphantom{\sum}[g_{m}\sin{(m\gamma\tau)}+h_{m}\cos{(m\gamma\tau)}]\sin{(m\gamma t)}\right\}.

Substituting (
42
)–(
43
) and (
35
) into (
34
),
we find
a_{0}=e_{0},c_{0}=g_{0}
and

\displaystyle-m\gamma a_{m}

\displaystyle=\left[e_{m}\sin{(m\gamma\tau)}+f_{m}\cos{(m\gamma\tau)}-b_{m}\right]/a,

(44)

\displaystyle m\gamma b_{m}

\displaystyle=\left[e_{m}\cos{(m\gamma\tau)}-f_{m}\sin{(m\gamma\tau)}-a_{m}\right]/a,

(45)

\displaystyle-m\gamma c_{m}

\displaystyle=\left[g_{m}\sin{(m\gamma\tau)}+h_{m}\cos{(m\gamma\tau)}-d_{m}\right]/a,

(46)

\displaystyle m\gamma d_{m}

\displaystyle=\left[g_{m}\cos{(m\gamma\tau)}-h_{m}\sin{(m\gamma\tau)}-c_{m}\right]/a

(47)

for
m=1,2,\dots,M
.
These are also simultaneous linear equations so given the coefficients
\{e_{0},\dots,f_{M}\}

and
\{g_{0},\dots,h_{M}\}
we can easily solve them to find
the coefficients of
Y
,
\{a_{0},\dots,d_{M}\}
.

Thus we have an algorithm for finding approximate periodic solutions
of (
7
), (
11
), (
12
) in a self-consistent way:

1.

From a simulation of (
7
)–(
8
)
for which there is a stable periodic solution, extract one period of

.
From this, fit the coefficients
\{\alpha_{0},\dots,\delta_{M}\}
.
Given these coefficients, use them to construct
I(x,t)

and find the
T

5.2
Traveling waves

A traveling wave solution of (
7
), (
13
)
moving to the left with speed

will have a profile that is a solution of

v\frac{\displaystyle dz}{\displaystyle d\xi}=\frac{\displaystyle[i(\eta_{0}+\kappa I(\xi))-\Delta](1+z)^{2}}{\displaystyle 2}-i\frac{\displaystyle(1-z)^{2}}{\displaystyle 2}

(48)

where

I(\xi)=\frac{1}{a}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}G(\xi-y)\int_{\tau}^{\tau+a}H\left(z\left(y-vs\right)\right)ds\>dy.

(49)

From the form of
G(x)
, see (
14
), we can write
I(\xi)=\delta+\epsilon\cos{\xi}+\phi\sin{\xi}

for some constants
\delta,\epsilon
and
\phi
. Given these constants, we
can find the
2\pi
-periodic
solution of (
48
),
z(\xi)
, with
|z(\xi)|<1
,
as explained above or in
[
43
,
49
]
.
We then calculate
H(z(y))
. Writing

H(z(y))=\alpha+\beta\cos{y}+\gamma\sin{y}+\mbox{higher harmonics}

we have

\displaystyle H(z(y-vs))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha+\beta[\cos{y}\cos{(vs)}+\sin{y}\sin{(vs)}]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\gamma[\sin{y}\cos{(vs)}-\cos{y}\sin{(vs)}]+\mbox{higher harmonics}

and thus

\displaystyle\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}G(\xi-y)H(z(y-vs))dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\pi A\alpha+B\pi\left\{\cos{\xi}[\beta\cos{(vs)}-\gamma\sin{(vs)}]\vphantom{\sum}\right.

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left.\vphantom{\sum}\sin{\xi}[\beta\sin{(vs)}+\gamma\cos{(vs)}]\right\}.

Then, evaluating the integral over
s
in (
49
) we obtain

\displaystyle I(\xi)

\displaystyle=2\pi A\alpha+\frac{B\pi}{va}\cos{\xi}\{\beta[\sin{(v(\tau+a))}-\sin{(v\tau)}]+\gamma[\cos{(v(\tau+a))}-\cos{(v\tau)}]\}

\displaystyle+\frac{B\pi}{va}\sin{\xi}\{-\beta[\cos{(v(\tau+a))}-\cos{(v\tau)}]+\gamma[\sin{(v(\tau+a))}-\sin{(v\tau)}]\}

\displaystyle\equiv\tilde{\delta}+\tilde{\epsilon}\cos{\xi}+\tilde{\phi}\sin{\xi}.

If these tilded coefficients match the un-tilded ones we are done.
As above we can use the translational invariance of the system to
set
\epsilon=0
, so in practice we solve

\delta-\tilde{\delta}=0\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad\phi-\tilde{\phi}=0

along with the pinning condition
\tilde{\epsilon}=0
,
giving three equations in three unknowns (
\delta,\phi
and
v
).
Note that for some other delay kernels with compact support, e.g., piecewise linear, it is
also possible to analytically evaluate
the integral over

in (

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle B\pi\cos{x}\left(c_{0}+\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left\{\cos{(m\gamma t)}[c_{m}\cos{(m\gamma s)}-d_{m}\sin{(m\gamma s)}]\right.\right.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{a}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}G(x-y)\int_{\tau}^{\tau+a}H\left(z\left(y,t-s\right)\right)ds\>dy=\tilde{\alpha}_{0}+\sum_{m=1}^{M}[\tilde{\alpha}_{m}\cos{(m\gamma t)}+\tilde{\beta}_{m}\sin{(m\gamma t)}]

\displaystyle\left.\overline{D^{\prime}(Z(y))}\overline{v}_{+}(y)e^{\overline{\lambda}t}e^{-\overline{\lambda}\tau}e^{-\overline{\lambda}|x-y|/c}+\overline{D^{\prime}(Z(y))}v_{-}(y)e^{\lambda t}e^{-\lambda\tau}e^{-\lambda|x-y|/c}\right]dy.

Then, separately equating the terms proportional to
e^{\lambda t}

and the terms proportional to
e^{\overline{\lambda}t}
,
we get two equations that can be written in the matrix form

\displaystyle\lambda\left(\begin{array}[]{c}v_{+}\\[5.69054pt]
v_{-}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mu(x)&0\\[5.69054pt]
0&\overline{\mu}(x)\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}v_{+}\\[5.69054pt]
v_{-}\end{array}\right)+i\kappa e^{-\lambda\tau}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\zeta(x)&\zeta(x)\\[5.69054pt]
-\overline{\zeta(x)}&-\overline{\zeta(x)}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}G(x-y)e^{-\lambda|x-y|/c}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}D^{\prime}(Z(y))v_{+}(y)\\[5.69054pt]
\overline{D^{\prime}(Z(y))}v_{-}(y)\end{array}\right)dy.

As in previous sections, the
nonlinear eigenvalue problem (
6.1.2
) defines two types of spectra. The essential spectrum is given explicitly

\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}=\{\mu(x)\>:\>x\in[-\pi,\pi]\}\cup\{\mathrm{c.c}\}.

The remaining discrete spectrum
\sigma_{\mathrm{discr}}
consists
of isolated eigenvalues
\lambda\notin\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}
,
for which Eq. (
6.1.2
)
has nontrivial bounded solutions.
This part of the spectrum can be found
only approximately by assuming

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}v_{+}(x)\\[5.69054pt]
v_{-}(x)\end{array}\right)\approx\sum\limits_{k=-M}^{M}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}v_{k,+}\\[5.69054pt]
v_{k,-}\end{array}\right)e^{ikx}

with some positive integer
M
,
inserting this into the equation

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}v_{+}(x)\\[5.69054pt]
v_{-}(x)\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\kappa e^{-\lambda\tau}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda-\mu(x)&0\\[5.69054pt]
0&\lambda-\overline{\mu}(x)\end{array}\right)^{-1}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\zeta(x)&\zeta(x)\\[5.69054pt]
-\overline{\zeta(x)}&-\overline{\zeta(x)}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}G(x-y)e^{-\lambda|x-y|/c}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}D^{\prime}(Z(y))v_{+}(y)\\[5.69054pt]
\overline{D^{\prime}(Z(y))}v_{-}(y)\end{array}\right)dy,

and then projecting the resulting equation
onto the subspace spanned
by the Fourier modes
e^{ikx}
with
|k|\leq M
.
As a result, one obtains a nonlinear eigenvalue
problem in the
2(2M+1)
-dimensional space. Essentially one forms a

2(2M+1)\times 2(2M+1)
matrix that depends on
\lambda
. Values of
\lambda
for
which the determinant of this matrix is zero are eigenvalues associated with the
stability of the solution
Z(x)
. Using
M=10
we obtain the results shown
in Fig.
11
for the stability of a stationary bump,
for two different values of
\eta_{0}
.

7
Discussion

We studied next generation neural field models derived from infinite
networks of heterogeneous synaptically coupled theta neurons on a ring. The interactions
between neurons are delayed,
and we considered two forms of delays: distributed in time (two different examples),
and conduction delays proportional
to the distance between neurons. Very little work has been done on such
models
[
12
,
43
]
. In each model we found the stability of spatially uniform
and bump states in terms of parameters describing the delays. When varying
delay parameters these states
undergo Hopf bifurcations leading to the creation of traveling waves and “breathing” bumps,
respectively. We showed how such time-dependent states can be described in a self-consistent
way, and how to efficiently solve the corresponding self-consistency equations.
We did not find Turing bifurcations leading to stationary patterns, for reasons explained
in Appendix
A
.
Our results rely on choosing a small number of harmonics for the coupling function

G(x)
, and the use of an efficient algorithm to find periodic solutions
of a periodically forced Riccati equation.

We now briefly discuss the relationships between our results and previous work.
Many authors have studied the effects of delays in classical neural field models,
often linearizing them around a spatially uniform
state and determining the types of patterns that arise when it becomes
unstable
[
6
,
7
,
72
,
70
,
60
]
.
Typically, the patterns seen include stationary
Turing patterns, spatially uniform periodic states, and standing and traveling waves.
We also found spatially uniform periodic states and traveling waves that arise
from bifurcations of a spatially uniform state.
In addition, we found periodic solutions (breathing bumps) that arise from Hopf bifurcations
of stationary bumps, not often studied in systems with delays (but see
[
59
]
).

The recent paper
[
19
]
considered a model similar to the one in
Sec.
6
but with discrete nodes and Wilson–Cowan dynamics at each node.

We now discuss a number of options for future work.

While we have considered networks of theta neurons, they are equivalent to quadratic
integrate-and-fire (QIF) neurons, so our methods could be used to study networks of QIF neurons,
such as
[

]
, but with delays
[

Winfree oscillators

are another type of
model neuron and the Ott/Antonsen ansatz can be used to study infinite networks of

[

Winfree oscillators.

We considered several types of delays: distributed and conduction.
Other types of delays that could be considered include activity-dependent

[

53

One point of interest is that we assumed that the conduction velocity has a single value;

approach will also occur in periodically forced systems

We studied a one-dimensional domain with periodic boundary conditions, but a more
