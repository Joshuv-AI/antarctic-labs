Optimal energy decay rates for Klein–Gordon equations with Kelvin–Voigt damping
Filippo Dell’Oro, Lassi Paunonen and David Seifert

Abstract
. We study the long-time behaviour of solutions to a one-dimensional linear Klein–Gordon equation with Kelvin–Voigt damping. One of the interesting features of the equation is that the generator of the associated C 0 C_{0} -semigroup has multiple spectral points on the imaginary axis. As our main result, we show that the energy of every possible solution converges to zero as time goes to infinity and, moreover, we provide an optimal polynomial energy decay rate for a certain class of solutions.

1.
Introduction

In this paper we study the long-time behaviour of solutions to the following one-dimensional Klein–Gordon equation with Kelvin–Voigt damping:

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} u_{tt}(x,t)&-u_{xx}(x,t)+mu(x,t)-u_{txx}(x,t)=0,&&\,\,\,x\in\mathbb{R},\ t>0,\\
u(x,0)&=u_{0}(x),&&\,\,\,x\in\mathbb{R},\\
u_{t}(x,0)&=v_{0}(x),&&\,\,\,x\in\mathbb{R},\end{aligned}\end{cases}

where
m>0
is a fixed constant.
The initial data satisfy
u_{0}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and
v_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Setting
z_{0}=(u_{0},v_{0})
,
we define the energy of the corresponding solution as

(1.2)

E_{z_{0}}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}m|u(x,t)|^{2}+|u_{x}(x,t)|^{2}+|u_{t}(x,t)|^{2}dx,\quad\,t\geq 0.

For sufficiently regular solutions, a straightforward calculation yields the energy identity

\dot{E}_{z_{0}}(t)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}|u_{tx}(x,t)|^{2}dx,\quad\,t\geq 0,

so that
E_{z_{0}}
is non-increasing. The main aim of this article is to show that
E_{z_{0}}(t)
actually
decays to zero as
t\to\infty
for all possible solutions and, in addition, to find optimal
estimates for the rate of decay for certain classes of solutions.

When considering Klein–Gordon equations with Kelvin–Voigt damping posed on a finite interval (or more generally
on a bounded multi-dimensional domain) with Dirichlet boundary conditions, it is well known that
the energy decays exponentially to zero for all possible solutions, that is,
the associated contraction
C_{0}
-semigroup is exponentially stable. Indeed, in this situation,
one can take advantage of the Poincaré inequality and achieve exponential stability
by following the same strategy employed for the viscous global damping, namely,
by constructing a perturbed energy functional which satisfies a convenient differential inequality
and then applying the Grönwall lemma.
Several results on the stability (and regularity) properties of linear wave equations with
Kelvin–Voigt damping on bounded domains may be found for instance in
[
1
,
9
,
8
,
10
,
11
,
16
,
24
,
28
]
.

When studying problem (
1.1
), or more generally
Klein–Gordon equations with Kelvin–Voigt damping
on unbounded multi-dimensional domains whose geometries are incompatible with the validity of the Poincaré inequality, the picture changes drastically.
Indeed, as we will show in the present article,
problem (
1.1
) is associated with a contraction
C_{0}
-semigroup
(T(t))_{t\geq 0}
on the Hilbert space

X=H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\times L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
whose infinitesimal generator
A
possesses multiple (but a finite number of) spectral points on the imaginary axis.
More precisely, the intersection of the spectrum of
A
with the imaginary axis consists exactly of the two points

\pm i\sqrt{m}
.
This feature prevents
(T(t))_{t\geq 0}
from being exponentially stable or even semi-uniformly
stable in the sense of
[
5
,
6
]
. However,
since these spectral points are not eigenvalues of
A
, we may exploit
the classical Arendt–Batty–Lyubich–Vũ theorem
[
2
]
and deduce that the energy

E_{z_{0}}(t)
converges to zero as
t\to\infty
for all initial data
z_{0}\in X
. On the other hand, as already noted, with a view to finding estimates for the rate of decay of certain classes of solutions,
the presence of two imaginary spectral points does not allow us to exploit the classical methods of semi-uniform stability
[
5
,
6
]
or their generalisation
[
4
,
33
]
which crucially
depend on the spectrum of
A
touching the imaginary axis at most at zero. The abstract criteria from
[
27
]

accommodate finite spectral points on the imaginary axis but, although they apply in the more general setting of Banach spaces, they provide only sub-optimal decay rates in Hilbert spaces.
Instead, our strategy consists in combining the
results of
[
4
]
with those of
[
30
]
, finding
in our main Theorem
4.1
the optimal decay estimate

E_{z_{0}}(t)=O(t^{-2}),\quad\,\,\text{as }t\to\infty,

for a certain class of solutions to problem (
1.1
). These solutions are precisely those that originate
from initial data
z_{0}
lying in the intersection of the ranges of the operators
i\sqrt{m}-A
and
-i\sqrt{m}-A
.

Notation.

The notation used is standard throughout.
If
A
is a closed linear operator on a (complex) Hilbert space, we denote its
domain by
D(A)
, its range by
\Ran(A)
, its spectrum by
\sigma(A)
, its point spectrum by
\sigma_{p}(A)
and its resolvent set by
\rho(A)
.
For
\lambda\in\rho(A)
we write
R(\lambda,A)
for the resolvent operator
(\lambda-A)^{-1}
.
The symbol
\mathcal{B}(X)
stands for the space of bounded linear operators on a Banach space
X
.
We denote by
\mathbb{C}_{-}
and
\mathbb{C}_{+}
the open left and right complex half-plane, respectively,
and we set
\mathbb{R}_{+}=[0,\infty)
.
Given
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, we define the square root
\sqrt{\lambda}
by taking the branch cut along the
negative real axis. In particular,

\re\sqrt{\lambda}\geq 0
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, with strict inequality for
\lambda\not\in(-\infty,0]
.
For
p,q\in\mathbb{R}

we write
p\lesssim q
to indicate that
p\leq Cq

for some constant
C>0
.
Finally, we use conventional asymptotic notation, including ‘big O’ and ‘little o’.

2.
The Semigroup Generator and Its Boundary Spectrum

We consider the complex Hilbert space

X=H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\times L^{2}(\mathbb{R}),

endowed with the norm

\|z\|_{X}^{2}=m\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}+\|u^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}+\|v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}

for
z=(u,v)\in X
.
Then, we rewrite (
1.1
) as an abstract Cauchy problem on
X
, that is

(2.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{z}(t)&=Az(t),\quad t\geq 0,\\
z(0)&=(u_{0},v_{0})\in X,\end{aligned}\right.

where
A:D(A)\subseteq X\to X
is the linear operator
Az=(v,(u+v)^{\prime\prime}-mu)

with domain

D(A)=\big\{(u,v)\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\times H^{1}(\mathbb{R}):u+v\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R})\big\}.

The following result summarises the main properties of
A
, including a description of
its spectrum on the imaginary axis
i\mathbb{R}
.

Theorem 2.1
.

The following hold:

(a)

A
is closed;

(b)

A
is dissipative;

(c)

\sigma(A)\cap i\mathbb{R}=\{i\sqrt{m},-i\sqrt{m}\}
;

(d)

\sigma_{p}(A)\cap i\mathbb{R}=\emptyset
.

Proof.

(a) Closedness of
A
may be verified by means of standard methods, for instance by adapting the argument given in the proof of
[
29
, Lemma 2.1]
.

(b) For every
z=(u,v)\in D(A)
, a straightforward calculation yields

\re\langle Az,z\rangle_{X}=-\|v^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\leq 0,

so
A
is dissipative.

(c) We begin by proving that
i\mathbb{R}\setminus\{i\sqrt{m},-i\sqrt{m}\}\subseteq\rho(A)
.
To this end, let us fix
s\in\mathbb{R}
with
|s|\neq\sqrt{m}
. Since
A
is closed,
it is enough to show that for every
\hat{z}=(\hat{u},\hat{v})\in X
the resolvent equation

(2.2)

(is-A)z=\hat{z}

has a unique solution
z=(u,v)\in D(A)
.
The equation is equivalent to

\displaystyle isu(x)-v(x)=\hat{u}(x),

\displaystyle isv(x)-(u+v)^{\prime\prime}(x)+mu(x)=\hat{v}(x).

Setting
w=u+v

we may rewrite the system above in the form

(2.3a)

\displaystyle(1+is)u(x)-w(x)=\hat{u}(x),

(2.3b)

\displaystyle(m-s^{2})u(x)-w^{\prime\prime}(x)=is\hat{u}(x)+\hat{v}(x).

Substituting (
2.3a
) into (
2.3b
), we obtain

(2.4)

-w^{\prime\prime}(x)+\lambda_{s}\hskip 0.85358ptw(x)=\hat{v}(x)+(is-\lambda_{s})\hat{u}(x),

where

(2.5)

\lambda_{s}=\frac{m-s^{2}}{1+is}\not\in(-\infty,0].

To solve (
2.4
), we introduce the
Green’s function

(2.6)

G_{s}(x)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\lambda_{s}}}\,{\rm e}^{-\sqrt{\lambda_{s}}|x|}.

Since
{\rm Re}\sqrt{\lambda_{s}}>0
,
it is readily seen that

G_{s}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.
Denoting by
*
the convolution product on
\mathbb{R}
,
the solution to (
2.4
)
may be written as

w(x)=(G_{s}\,*\,\hat{v})(x)+(is-\lambda_{s})(G_{s}\,*\,\hat{u})(x).

Indeed, by means of a simple calculation, we obtain

w^{\prime}(x)=(Q_{s}\,*\,\hat{v})(x)+(is-\lambda_{s})(Q_{s}\,*\,\hat{u})(x),

where

(2.7)

Q_{s}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\big[{\rm e}^{\sqrt{\lambda_{s}}x}H(-x)-{\rm e}^{-\sqrt{\lambda_{s}}x}H(x)\big]

with
H
being the Heaviside step function.
Note that
w,w^{\prime}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
by
Young’s inequality for convolutions and the fact that
G_{s},Q_{s}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. A further simple calculation
now shows that
w
solves (
2.4
) and so in particular
w\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
Once
w=u+v
has been found, it is immediate to check that

(2.8)

\begin{cases}u(x)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+is}\big[(G_{s}\,*\,\hat{v})(x)+(is-\lambda_{s})(G_{s}\,*\,\hat{u})(x)+\hat{u}(x)\big]\\
\vskip 6.54413pt\cr v(x)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+is}\big[is(G_{s}\,*\,\hat{v})(x)-(s^{2}+is\lambda_{s})(G_{s}\,*\,\hat{u})(x)-\hat{u}(x)\big]\end{cases}

is the desired unique solution to (
2.2
).

It remains to show that
\pm i\sqrt{m}\in\sigma(A)
.
We will work with
i\sqrt{m}
only; an analogous argument applies to

-i\sqrt{m}
. For
k\geq 1
, let

u_{k}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\Phi\Big(\frac{x}{k}\Big),

where
\Phi:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
is a smooth bump function
with
\Phi(x)=1
for
|x|\leq 1/2
and
\Phi(x)=0
for
|x|\geq 1
. Since
u_{k}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R})

it follows immediately that
z_{k}=(u_{k},i\sqrt{m}u_{k})\in D(A)
for every
k\geq 1
. Moreover,

\|z_{k}\|_{X}^{2}\geq 2m\|u_{k}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\frac{2m}{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Big|\Phi\Big(\frac{x}{k}\Big)\Big|^{2}dx\geq 2m.

On the other hand, it is straightforward to check that

\|(i\sqrt{m}-A)z_{k}\|_{X}^{2}=(1+m)\|u_{k}^{\prime\prime}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\frac{2(1+m)}{k^{4}}\|\Phi^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}\to 0

as
k\to\infty
.
Hence
i\sqrt{m}
is an approximate eigenvalue of
A
.

(d) If
(i\sqrt{m}-A)z=0
for some
z=(u,v)\in D(A)
, reasoning
as above in the passage from (
2.2
)
to (
2.4
)
we see that
w=u+v
satisfies the equation
w^{\prime\prime}(x)=0
.
Since
w\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, this forces
w=0
and therefore
z=0
as well.
Hence
i\sqrt{m}\notin\sigma_{p}(A)
.
An analogous argument applies to

-i\sqrt{m}
.
∎

Note that Theorem
2.1
tells us in particular that
0\in\rho(A)
.
Since the resolvent set is open, this implies that
\lambda-A
is surjective for some

\lambda>0
.
Since
A
is also dissipative, exploiting the Lumer-Phillips theorem we obtain the following result;
see e.g.
[
14
, Corollary II.3.20]
.

Theorem 2.2
.

The operator
A
is densely defined and generates a contraction
C_{0}
-semigroup
(T(t))_{t\geq 0}
on the Hilbert space
X
.

3.
Resolvent Estimates

In this section, we obtain upper bounds on the growth rate of
\|R(is,A)\|
as
|s|

tends to
\sqrt{m}
and to infinity. Our main result here is the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

The following hold:

(a)

\|R(is,A)\|=O(1)
as
|s|\to\infty
;

(b)

\|R(is,A)\|=O\big(|s-\sqrt{m}|^{-1}\big)
as
s\to\sqrt{m}
;

(c)

\|R(is,A)\|=O\big(|s+\sqrt{m}|^{-1}\big)
as
s\to-\sqrt{m}
.

In what follows, for
s\in\mathbb{R}
with
|s|\neq\sqrt{m}
, the
number
\lambda_{s}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0]
is defined in (
2.5
).
We begin with an elementary technical lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

There exists a constant
m_{0}>0
depending only on
the (fixed) parameter
m>0
such that

\cos\Big(\frac{\arg\lambda_{s}}{2}\Big)\geq m_{0}

for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
with
|s|\neq\sqrt{m}
.

Proof.

For
|s|<\sqrt{m}
we have
\arg\lambda_{s}=-\arctan s
,
yielding
|\arg\lambda_{s}|<\frac{\pi}{2}
. Therefore

\cos\Big(\frac{\arg\lambda_{s}}{2}\Big)\geq\frac{1}{{\sqrt{2}}}.

For
s>\sqrt{m}
we have
\arg\lambda_{s}=-\arctan s+\pi
, and thus

\frac{\pi}{2}<\arg\lambda_{s}<-\arctan\sqrt{m}+\pi
. As a consequence

\cos\Big(\frac{\arg\lambda_{s}}{2}\Big)\geq\cos\Big(-\frac{\arctan\sqrt{m}}{2}+\frac{\pi}{2}\Big)=\sin\Big(\frac{\arctan\sqrt{m}}{2}\Big)>0.

Finally, for
s<-\sqrt{m}
we have
\arg\lambda_{s}=-\arctan s-\pi
, which implies that

\arctan\sqrt{m}-\pi<\arg\lambda_{s}<-\frac{\pi}{2}

and the conclusion follows as before.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{R}
with
|s|\neq\sqrt{m}
and
\hat{z}=(\hat{u},\hat{v})\in X
be fixed.
From Theorem
2.1
and its proof, we know that the resolvent
equation
(is-A)z=\hat{z}
has a unique solution
z=(u,v)=R(is,A)\hat{z}\in D(A)

given by (
2.8
). Recall also that the Green’s function

G_{s}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
is defined in (
2.6
).

We first estimate
\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}
. To this end,
using Young’s inequality for convolutions, we compute

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\frac{1}{{1+|s|}}\|G_{s}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\big[\|\hat{v}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}+|is-\lambda_{s}|\|\hat{u}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\big]+\frac{1}{{1+|s|}}\|\hat{u}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})},

where, as in the remainder of the proof, the implicit constant depends on
the (fixed) parameter
m>0
but is independent
of both
s
and
\hat{z}
. Noting that
|is-\lambda_{s}|\lesssim 1+|s|
, we find

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|G_{s}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\big[\|\hat{v}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}+\|\hat{u}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\big]+\frac{1}{{1+|s|}}\|\hat{u}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|G_{s}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\|\hat{z}\|_{X}+\frac{1}{{1+|s|}}\|\hat{z}\|_{X}.

At this point, a straightforward calculation combined with Lemma
3.2
shows that

\|G_{s}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}=\frac{1}{|\lambda_{s}|}\Big[\cos\Big(\frac{\arg\lambda_{s}}{2}\Big)\Big]^{-1}\lesssim\frac{1}{|\lambda_{s}|}\lesssim\frac{1+|s|}{|s-\sqrt{m}||s+\sqrt{m}|},

yielding

\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\frac{1+|s|}{|s-\sqrt{m}||s+\sqrt{m}|}\|\hat{z}\|_{X}.

Since
v=isu-\hat{u}
, from the bound above we easily get

\|v\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\frac{1+s^{2}}{|s-\sqrt{m}||s+\sqrt{m}|}\|\hat{z}\|_{X}.

Next, by means of an elementary calculation, we see that

u^{\prime}(x)=\frac{1}{1+is}\big[(Q_{s}\,*\,\hat{v})(x)+(is-\lambda_{s})(Q_{s}\,*\,\hat{u})(x)+\hat{u}^{\prime}(x)\big],

where we recall that the function

Q_{s}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
is defined in (
2.7
).
As before, using Young’s inequality for convolutions and recalling that

|is-\lambda_{s}|\lesssim 1+|s|
, we obtain

\|u^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\|Q_{s}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\|\hat{z}\|_{X}+\frac{1}{{1+|s|}}\|\hat{z}\|_{X}.

An application of Lemma
3.2
combined with a straightforward calculation gives

\|Q_{s}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}=\frac{1}{|\lambda_{s}|^{1/2}}\Big[\cos\Big(\frac{\arg\lambda_{s}}{2}\Big)\Big]^{-1}\lesssim\frac{1}{|\lambda_{s}|^{1/2}}\lesssim\frac{1+|s|^{1/2}}{|s-\sqrt{m}|^{1/2}|s+\sqrt{m}|^{1/2}},

and thus

\|u^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\frac{1+|s|^{1/2}}{|s-\sqrt{m}|^{1/2}|s+\sqrt{m}|^{1/2}}\|\hat{z}\|_{X}.

Collecting the bounds obtained so far, we see that

\|z\|_{X}\lesssim\frac{1+s^{2}}{|s-\sqrt{m}||s+\sqrt{m}|}\|\hat{z}\|_{X},

which implies that

\|R(is,A)\|\lesssim\frac{1+s^{2}}{|s-\sqrt{m}||s+\sqrt{m}|}.

The result follows.
∎

4.
Asymptotic Stability and Quantified Decay Rates

The following is the main result of the paper.

Theorem 4.1
.

The energy (
1.2
) satisfies
E_{z_{0}}(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
for every
z_{0}\in X
.
Moreover,

E_{z_{0}}(t)=O(t^{-2}),\quad\,\,\text{as }t\to\infty,

for every
z_{0}\in\Ran(i\sqrt{m}-A)\cap\Ran(-i\sqrt{m}-A)
. This decay rate is
optimal in the sense that given any function
r:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to(0,\infty)
satisfying
r(t)=o(t^{-2})
as
t\to\infty
, there exists
z_{0}\in\Ran(i\sqrt{m}-A)\cap\Ran(-i\sqrt{m}-A)
such that
E_{z_{0}}(t)\neq O(r(t))
as
t\to\infty
.

Proof.

Since
\sigma_{p}(A)\cap i\mathbb{R}=\emptyset
and
\sigma(A)\cap i\mathbb{R}=\{i\sqrt{m},-i\sqrt{m}\}
is finite by Theorem
2.1
, it follows from the
classical Arendt–Batty–Lyubich–Vũ stability theorem
[
2
]
that
the contraction
C_{0}
-semigroup
(T(t))_{t\geq 0}
generated by
A
is strongly asymptotically stable,
i.e.

E_{z_{0}}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\|T(t)z_{0}\|_{X}^{2}\to 0,\quad\,\,\text{as }t\to\infty,

for
all
z_{0}\in X
.
Next, let
B_{1}=(i\sqrt{m}-A)R(1,A)\in\mathcal{B}(X)
and
B_{2}=(-i\sqrt{m}-A)R(1,A)\in\mathcal{B}(X)
.
Note that
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
commute, and that
B_{1}B_{2}
commutes with
A
.
By Theorems
2.1
and
3.1
we have

\displaystyle|s-\sqrt{m}|\|R(is,A)\|\lesssim 1,\qquad

\displaystyle s\in[0,\sqrt{m})\cup(\sqrt{m},2\sqrt{m}],

\displaystyle|s+\sqrt{m}|\|R(is,A)\|\lesssim 1,\qquad

\displaystyle s\in[-2\sqrt{m},-\sqrt{m})\cup(-\sqrt{m},0],

\displaystyle\|R(is,A)\|\lesssim 1,\qquad

\displaystyle s\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[-2\sqrt{m},2\sqrt{m}],

and hence Assumption 3 in
[
30
]
is satisfied.
As a consequence, if we define

\Omega_{\pm}=\{\lambda\in\overline{\mathbb{C}_{+}}\,:0<|\lambda\mp i\sqrt{m}|\leq\min\{1,\sqrt{m}/2\}\}

then
[
30
, Lemma 13]
implies that

\displaystyle\sup_{\lambda\in\Omega_{\pm}}\|R(\lambda,A)(\pm i\sqrt{m}-A)R(1\pm i\sqrt{m},A)\|<\infty.

Boundedness of
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
combined with the resolvent identity then yield

\sup_{\lambda\in\Omega_{+}\cup\Omega_{-}}\|R(\lambda,A)B_{1}B_{2}\|<\infty
.
Since
[
30
, Lemma 15]
guaranteees that

\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}_{+}\setminus(\Omega_{+}\cup\Omega_{-})}\|R(\lambda,A)\|<\infty,

we obtain

\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}_{+}}\|R(\lambda,A)B_{1}B_{2}\|<\infty.

The latter bound and
[
4
, Theorem 4.7]

lead to the estimate

(4.1)

\|T(t)B_{1}B_{2}\|^{2}=O(t^{-2}),\quad\,\,\text{as }t\to\infty.

We now show that
\Ran(B_{1}B_{2})=\Ran(i\sqrt{m}-A)\cap\Ran(-i\sqrt{m}-A)
.
Clearly
\Ran(B_{1})=\Ran(i\sqrt{m}-A)
and
\Ran(B_{2})=\Ran(-i\sqrt{m}-A)
. It is also clear that

\displaystyle\Ran(B_{1}B_{2})\subseteq\Ran(B_{1})\cap\Ran(B_{2})=\Ran(i\sqrt{m}-A)\cap\Ran(-i\sqrt{m}-A).

On the other hand, if

w\in\Ran(B_{1})\cap\Ran(B_{2})
,
then
w=B_{1}z
for some
z\in X
, and so

w=B_{1}z=2i\sqrt{m}R(1,A)z+B_{2}z.

Recalling that
w\in\Ran(B_{2})
, the identity above yields
R(1,A)z\in\Ran(B_{2})
,
which is to say that
R(1,A)z=B_{2}y
for some
y\in X
. Since
B_{2}=(-i\sqrt{m}-1)R(1,A)+I
, we have
y\in D(A)
and
z=B_{2}(I-A)y
, which finally implies that

w=B_{1}B_{2}(I-A)y\in\Ran(B_{1}B_{2}).

This proves that
\Ran(B_{1}B_{2})=\Ran(i\sqrt{m}-A)\cap\Ran(-i\sqrt{m}-A)
.
In light of (
4.1
) and the equality
E_{z_{0}}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\|T(t)z_{0}\|_{X}^{2}
for
z_{0}\in X
and
t\geq 0
, the proof
of the first part of Theorem
4.1
is complete.

For the optimality part, let
r:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to(0,\infty)
be such that
r(t)=o(t^{-2})
as
t\to\infty
and suppose, for the sake of obtaining a contradiction, that

E_{z_{0}}(t)=O(r(t))
as
t\to\infty
for all
z_{0}\in\Ran(i\sqrt{m}-A)\cap\Ran(-i\sqrt{m}-A)
. Since
\Ran(i\sqrt{m}-A)\cap\Ran(-i\sqrt{m}-A)=\Ran(B_{1}B_{2})
, an application of the uniform boundedness principle yields
\|T(t)B_{1}B_{2}\|^{2}=O(r(t))
as
t\to\infty
, and hence

t\|T(t)B_{1}B_{2}\|\to 0
as
t\to\infty
.
We now adapt the argument given in the proof of
[
4
, Theorem 6.9]
. Suppose first that
i\sqrt{m}
is a limit point of
\sigma(A)
. Then there exists a sequence
(\lambda_{k})_{k\geq 1}
in
\sigma(A)\cap\mathbb{C}_{-}
such that
\lambda_{k}\to i\sqrt{m}
as
k\to\infty
. Let
t_{k}=-1/\re\lambda_{k}
for
k\geq 1
, noting that
t_{k}\to\infty
as
k\to\infty
.
Recalling that the spectral radius of a bounded linear
operator is dominated by the norm of the operator, it
follows from the spectral inclusion theorem for the Hille–Phillips functional calculus
[
17
, Theorem 16.3.5]
that

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to\infty}\,t\|T(t)B_{1}B_{2}\|

\displaystyle\geq\limsup_{k\to\infty}\,t_{k}\left|e^{t_{k}\lambda_{k}}\frac{(i\sqrt{m}-\lambda_{k})(-i\sqrt{m}-\lambda_{k})}{(1-\lambda_{k})^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{e}\lim_{k\to\infty}\left|\frac{i\sqrt{m}+\lambda_{k}}{(1-\lambda_{k})^{2}}\right|=\frac{2\sqrt{m}}{e(1+m)}>0,

which contradicts our earlier observation that
t\|T(t)B_{1}B_{2}\|\to 0
as
t\to\infty
.
So
i\sqrt{m}
must be an isolated point of
\sigma(A)
. From the theory of spectral decompositions coming out of the Riesz–Dunford functional calculus, we therefore obtain a decomposition
X=X_{0}\oplus X_{1}
of our space
X
into two non-trivial closed subspaces
X_{0},X_{1}
which are invariant under
(T(t))_{t\geq 0}
and such that, furthermore, the restrictions
A_{k}
of
A
to
X_{k}
,
k=0,1
, satisfy
A_{1}\in\mathcal{B}(X_{1})
,
\sigma(A_{1})=\{i\sqrt{m}\}
and
\sigma(A_{0})=\sigma(A)\setminus\{i\sqrt{m}\}
;
see e.g.
[
14
, Chapter IV]
.
Let
(T_{1}(t))_{t\geq 0}
denote the semigroup on
X_{1}
generated by
A_{1}-i\sqrt{m}
. Then

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to\infty}\,t\|(i\sqrt{m}-A_{1})T_{1}(t)\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to\infty}t\|T_{1}(t)(i\sqrt{m}-A_{1})(-i\sqrt{m}-A_{1})R(1,A_{1})^{2}\|\|R(-i\sqrt{m},A_{1})(I-A_{1})^{2}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to\infty}t\|T(t)B_{1}B_{2}\|\|R(-i\sqrt{m},A_{1})(I-A_{1})^{2}\|=0,

and it follows from
[
23
, Theorem 2.1]
that
i\sqrt{m}-A_{1}=0
. This contradicts the fact that
i\sqrt{m}\not\in\sigma_{p}(A)
, so the argument is complete.
∎

We conclude the section by providing a “concrete” characterisation of
\Ran(i\sqrt{m}-A)\cap\Ran(-i\sqrt{m}-A)
in order to better understand
the class of solutions for which we have our decay estimate.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
z_{0}=(u_{0},v_{0})\in X
. Then
z_{0}\in\Ran(i\sqrt{m}-A)\cap\Ran(-i\sqrt{m}-A)
if and only
if all of the following conditions hold:

(a)

x\mapsto\displaystyle\lim_{a\to-\infty}\int_{a}^{x}[i\sqrt{m}u_{0}(r)+v_{0}(r)]dr\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
;

(b)

x\mapsto\displaystyle\lim_{a\to-\infty}\int_{a}^{x}[-i\sqrt{m}u_{0}(r)+v_{0}(r)]dr\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
;

(c)

x\mapsto\displaystyle\lim_{b\to-\infty}\int_{b}^{x}\Big(\lim_{a\to-\infty}\int_{a}^{y}[i\sqrt{m}u_{0}(r)+v_{0}(r)]dr\Big)dy\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
;

(d)

x\mapsto\displaystyle\lim_{b\to-\infty}\int_{b}^{x}\Big(\lim_{a\to-\infty}\int_{a}^{y}[-i\sqrt{m}u_{0}(r)+v_{0}(r)]dr\Big)dy\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

We omit the proof, which can be carried out by
adapting the arguments contained in the proof of
[
29
, Proposition 4.3]
.

5.
The Half-Line Case

As mentioned in the
introduction, our techniques can be
modified straightforwardly to deal with the Klein–Gordon equation on the half-line
with Kelvin–Voigt damping and Dirichlet boundary condition:

(5.1)

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} u_{tt}(x,t)&-u_{xx}(x,t)+mu(x,t)-u_{txx}(x,t)=0,&&&x\in(0,\infty),\ t>0,\\
u(0,t)&=0,&&&t>0,\\
u(x,0)&=u_{0}(x),&&&x\in(0,\infty),\\
u_{t}(x,0)&=v_{0}(x),&&&x\in(0,\infty).\end{aligned}\end{cases}

Again
m>0
is a fixed constant,
while this time the initial data satisfy
u_{0}\in H^{1}(0,\infty)

and
v_{0}\in L^{2}(0,\infty)
with
u_{0}(0)=0
.
In this situation,
we work with the Hilbert space

Y=\big\{(u,v)\in H^{1}(0,\infty)\times L^{2}(0,\infty):u(0)=0\big\}

endowed with the same norm as in Section
2
, and we consider the operator
A

with the new domain

D(A)=\big\{(u,v)\in Y:v\in H^{1}(0,\infty),\,\,v(0)=0,\,\,u+v\in H^{2}(0,\infty)\big\}.

Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
remain valid.
In particular, in order to show that

\pm i\sqrt{m}\in\sigma(A)
,
we may use the same argument as in the proof of Theorem
2.1
,
but this time with

u_{k}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\Phi\Big(\frac{x}{k}-1\Big),\quad x\in[0,\infty),

to accommodate the Dirichlet boundary condition. Likewise,
for every fixed
s\in\mathbb{R}
with
|s|\neq\sqrt{m}
and
\hat{z}=(\hat{u},\hat{v})\in Y
,
the resolvent equation
(is-A)z=\hat{z}

has a unique solution
z=(u,v)=R(is,A)\hat{z}\in D(A)
. Extending

\hat{u}(x)
and
\hat{v}(x)
by zero for
x\leq 0
, and considering the same
complex number
\lambda_{s}
in (
2.5
) and the same
Green’s function
G_{s}
in (
2.6
), the solution becomes

\displaystyle u(x)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+is}\big[(G_{s}\,*\,\hat{v})(x)+(is-\lambda_{s})(G_{s}\,*\,\hat{u})(x)+\hat{u}(x)-\hat{\eta}_{s}\hskip 0.56905ptG_{s}(x)\big]

\displaystyle v(x)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+is}\big[is(G_{s}\,*\,\hat{v})(x)-(s^{2}+is\lambda_{s})(G_{s}\,*\,\hat{u})(x)-\hat{u}(x)-is\hskip 0.56905pt\hat{\eta}_{s}\hskip 0.56905ptG_{s}(x)\big]

for
x\in[0,\infty)
, where
\hat{\eta}_{s}\in\mathbb{C}
is given by

\hat{\eta}_{s}=2\sqrt{\lambda_{s}}\big[(G_{s}\,*\,\hat{v})(0)+(is-\lambda_{s})(G_{s}\,*\,\hat{u})(0)\big].

The additional term containing
\hat{\eta}_{s}
is needed on account of the Dirichlet boundary condition.
Arguments completely analogous to those presented in
Section
3
lead to the same resolvent bounds of Theorem
3.1
, while
Theorem
4.1
and its proof are unchanged.
