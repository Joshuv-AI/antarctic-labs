First server’s effect on the expected number of games in Tennis
Ali Mohammadi

Abstract
. We show that information on the first server influences the expected total number of games and margin in a tennis match under the standard assumption that each player’s serve point win probability remains constant, and identify the exact regions, in terms of these probabilities, in which this effect is non-negligible. We confirm numerically that this effect is bounded by at most 0.4 0.4 games at both the set and match level. This translates, for example, to roughly a 9 9 percent shift in the probability that a match exceeds 19.5 19.5 games when the players’ serve point win probabilities differ by 10 10 percent. We complement the analysis with an empirical comparison on professional match data, illustrating the adequacy of the constant-probability assumption for modelling the total number of games.

2.
Order of serve effect at set level

Proposition 2.1
.

Assume
{\mathcal{G}}_{A}+{\mathcal{G}}_{B}>1

for the expected number of games in a set, where

Let
\pi_{n}^{A}

A

n

\pi_{n}^{B}

\displaystyle\pi_{7}^{A}-\pi_{7}^{B}

\displaystyle=({\mathcal{S}}_{61}^{A}+{\mathcal{S}}_{16}^{A})-({\mathcal{S}}_{61}^{B}+{\mathcal{S}}_{16}^{B})

\displaystyle=-({\mathcal{S}}_{62}^{A}+{\mathcal{S}}_{26}^{A})+({\mathcal{S}}_{62}^{B}+{\mathcal{S}}_{26}^{B})

Similarly,

.

, and

3.
Order of serve effect at match level

The following claim provides a reduction of the match-level dependence on the order of serve to the initial server of the first set. In particular, it encodes the evolution of serve order across sets in terms of first set quantities, allowing totals and margin comparisons at the match level to be expressed through first set asymmetries.

Claim 3.1
.

Define the one-set transition probabilities

\alpha_{\ell}={\mathbb{P}}(W=A,\ L=\ \ell\mid S=A),\quad\beta_{\ell}={\mathbb{P}}(W=B,\ L=\ \ell\mid S=A),

\gamma_{\ell}={\mathbb{P}}(W=A,\ L=\ \ell\mid S=B),\quad\delta_{\ell}={\mathbb{P}}(W=B,\ L=\ \ell\mid S=B),\quad\ell\in\{\mathrm{e},\mathrm{o}\}.

where
L
is the set length parity, writing
e
for even and
o
for odd,
S
is the initial server and
W
the set winner. Write
p
for the probability that
A
wins a set. Then

\alpha_{e}+\alpha_{o}=\gamma_{e}+\gamma_{o}=p,\qquad\beta_{e}+\beta_{o}=\delta_{e}+\delta_{o}=1-p.

Let
S_{j}\in\{A,B\}
denote the first server of set
j
, let
N\in\{2,3\}
be the
number of sets. The following quantities determine the transition structure:

q_{A}={\mathbb{P}}(S_{2}=A\mid S_{1}=A)=\alpha_{e}+\beta_{e},\qquad q_{B}={\mathbb{P}}(S_{2}=A\mid S_{1}=B)=\gamma_{o}+\delta_{o},

and

\rho_{A}={\mathbb{P}}(S_{3}=A,\ N=3\mid S_{1}=A)=2\alpha_{e}\beta_{e}+\alpha_{o}\delta_{o}+\beta_{o}\gamma_{o},

\rho_{B}={\mathbb{P}}(S_{3}=A,\ N=3\mid S_{1}=B)=\gamma_{e}\delta_{o}+\gamma_{o}\beta_{e}+\delta_{e}\gamma_{o}+\delta_{o}\alpha_{e}.

Setting
x=\alpha_{e}-\gamma_{o}
, and
y=\beta_{e}-\delta_{o}
, we have

q_{A}-q_{B}=x+y,\qquad\rho_{A}-\rho_{B}=2xy.

Proof.

A third set is played exactly when the first two sets are split. Under

S_{1}=A
, the only ways that set
3
is played and started by
A
are

(A\text{ wins even},\,B\text{ wins even}),\quad(B\text{ wins even},\,A\text{ wins even}),

(A\text{ wins odd},\,B\text{ wins odd}),\quad(B\text{ wins odd},\,A\text{ wins odd}),

giving the equation for
\rho_{A}
. We similarly derive
\rho_{B}
. Also,

\displaystyle\rho_{A}-\rho_{B}

\displaystyle=2\alpha_{e}\beta_{e}+\alpha_{o}\delta_{o}+\beta_{o}\gamma_{o}-\gamma_{e}\delta_{o}-\gamma_{o}\beta_{e}-\delta_{e}\gamma_{o}-\delta_{o}\alpha_{e}

\displaystyle=2\alpha_{e}\beta_{e}+(p-\alpha_{e})\delta_{o}+(1-p-\beta_{e})\gamma_{o}

\displaystyle\qquad-(p-\gamma_{o})\delta_{o}-\gamma_{o}\beta_{e}-(1-p-\delta_{o})\gamma_{o}-\delta_{o}\alpha_{e}

\displaystyle=2\alpha_{e}\beta_{e}-2\alpha_{e}\delta_{o}-2\beta_{e}\gamma_{o}+2\gamma_{o}\delta_{o}

\displaystyle=2(\alpha_{e}-\gamma_{o})(\beta_{e}-\delta_{o})=2xy.

∎

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
T
denote the total number of games in a best-of-three sets match between
A
and
B
. Assume
{\mathcal{G}}_{A}+{\mathcal{G}}_{B}>1
. Then, writing
T^{\text{match}}_{A}={\mathbb{E}}(T\mid S_{1}=A)
and
T^{\text{match}}_{B}={\mathbb{E}}(T\mid S_{1}=B)
, we have
T^{\text{match}}_{A}<T^{\text{match}}_{B}
if
{\mathcal{G}}_{A}>{\mathcal{G}}_{B}
.

Proof.

Write
N\in\{2,3\}
for the number of sets played and
N_{A}
for those started by
A
. Let
T_{1}
denote the total number of games won by
A
in the first set,
T^{\text{set}}_{A}={\mathbb{E}}(T_{1}\mid S_{1}=A)
,
and similarly define
T^{\text{set}}_{B}
. Then

{\mathbb{E}}(T\mid S_{1}=\sigma)=T^{\text{set}}_{B}\cdot\,{\mathbb{E}}(N\mid S_{1}=\sigma)+(T^{\text{set}}_{A}-T^{\text{set}}_{B})\,{\mathbb{E}}(N_{A}\mid S_{1}=\sigma),

for
\sigma\in\{A,B\}
. By
[
5
, Section 3.3]
, probability of winning a set is independent of the first server, and so
{\mathbb{E}}(N\mid S_{1}=A)={\mathbb{E}}(N\mid S_{1}=B)
, giving

T^{\text{match}}_{A}-T^{\text{match}}_{B}=(T^{\text{set}}_{A}-T^{\text{set}}_{B})(M_{A}-M_{B}),

where

M_{A}={\mathbb{E}}(N_{A}\mid S_{1}=A),\qquad M_{B}={\mathbb{E}}(N_{A}\mid S_{1}=B).

We compute
M_{A}-M_{B}
. Using Claim
3.1
, and
M_{A}=1+q_{A}+\rho_{A}
,

M_{B}=q_{B}+\rho_{B}
.

Hence

M_{A}-M_{B}=1+(q_{A}-q_{B})+(\rho_{A}-\rho_{B}).

Writing
x=\alpha_{e}-\gamma_{o}
, and
y=\beta_{e}-\delta_{o}
, Claim
3.1
gives

M_{A}-M_{B}=1+x+y+2xy.

Since
F(x,y)=1+x+y+2xy
is affine in each variable separately, its minimum over the rectangle
[-p,p]\times[-(1-p),1-p]
is attained at a corner. At the four corners,
F
takes the values
2p(1-p)
,
2(1-p)^{2}
,
2p^{2}
, and
2+2p(1-p)
,
all of which are strictly positive for
0<p<1
. Hence
M_{A}-M_{B}>0
.
Since, by Proposition
2.1
,
T^{\text{set}}_{A}-T^{\text{set}}_{B}<0
, it follows that
T^{\text{match}}_{A}<T^{\text{match}}_{B}

as required.
∎

Proposition 3.3
.

Assume that
{\mathcal{G}}_{A}+{\mathcal{G}}_{B}>1
. Let
H^{\text{match}}_{A}={\mathbb{E}}(H\mid S_{1}=A)
, and
H^{\text{match}}_{B}={\mathbb{E}}(H\mid S_{1}=B)
, where
H
is the difference between number of games won by
A
and
B
.
Then
H^{\text{match}}_{A}>H^{\text{match}}_{B}
.

Proof.

Let
H_{1}
denote the difference between number of games won by
A
and
B
in the first set and write

H^{\text{set}}_{A}={\mathbb{E}}(H_{1}\mid S=A),\qquad H^{\text{set}}_{B}={\mathbb{E}}(H_{1}\mid S=B).

As in Proposition
3.2
, writing
N
for the number of sets played and
N_{A}
for those started by
A
,

{\mathbb{E}}(H\mid S_{1}=\sigma)=H^{\text{set}}_{B}\,{\mathbb{E}}(N\mid S_{1}=\sigma)+(H^{\text{set}}_{A}-H^{\text{set}}_{B})\,{\mathbb{E}}(N_{A}\mid S_{1}=\sigma).

Since
{\mathbb{E}}(N\mid S_{1}=A)={\mathbb{E}}(N\mid S_{1}=B)
, we obtain

H^{\text{match}}_{A}-H^{\text{match}}_{B}=(H^{\text{set}}_{A}-H^{\text{set}}_{B})(M_{A}-M_{B}),

where
M_{A}={\mathbb{E}}(N_{A}\mid S_{1}=A)
, and
M_{B}={\mathbb{E}}(N_{A}\mid S_{1}=B)
.
By Proposition
3.2
,
M_{A}-M_{B}>0
. Hence, since by Proposition
2.2
,
H^{\text{set}}_{A}-H^{\text{set}}_{B}>0
, it follows
H^{\text{match}}_{A}-H^{\text{match}}_{B}>0
,
as required.
∎

from the model. This behaviour is
consistent with the sigmoid shape of the underlying probability functions, where the gradient is

, and hence small perturbations in the inputs induce
disproportionately large changes in outcomes. See, for example,

This phenomenon is reasonably consistent across both ATP and WTA data, indicating that the breakdown is

4.4.

The dataset used in this paper
[
6
]
does not record the identity of the first server. We infer the order of serve from the reported scores and the number of breaks of serve. (The dataset records the number of service games for each player, although since there is a large number of missing values for the WTA dataset, we resort to using break statistics.)

Let
G_{W}
and
G_{L}
denote the total numbers of games won by the match winner and loser respectively, deduced from the score line, and let
SG_{W}
and
SG_{L}
denote their corresponding numbers of service games. Then

Remark 4.1
.

Write
M(x,y)
for the function
[
5
, equation 48]
, such that the probability of
A
winning a match against
B
is
{\mathcal{M}}_{AB}=M(\mathcal{P}_{A},\mathcal{P}_{B})
. A useful observation in
[
3
]
is that
M(\mathcal{P}_{A},\mathcal{P}_{B})\approx M(\mathcal{P}_{A}+\varepsilon,\mathcal{P}_{B}+\varepsilon)
for sufficiently small values of
\varepsilon>0
(see
[
3
, Figure 1]
). This justifies the authors’ use of the approximation

{\mathcal{M}}_{AB}\approx\frac{M(0.6+\Delta_{AB},0.6)+M(0.6,0.6-\Delta_{AB})}{2},

as long as
\Delta_{AB}=\mathcal{P}_{A}-\mathcal{P}_{B}
is sufficiently small. (The value
0.6
appears in the formula as it is roughly the average value for
\mathcal{P}_{A}
across the professional tours.)

This relation is one-to-one, implying that given a value of
{\mathcal{M}}_{AB}
, it may, up to some small error, be mapped to expected total number of games or margin for that match. In particular, this suggests that
{\mathcal{M}}_{AB}
may serve as a strong covariate for these quantities. This observation suggests the possibility of modelling odds for total games and various other markets using odds for the win market. Consequently, after the coin toss is observed, one should in principle be able to update
the odds for totals and handicap markets by conditioning on the realised initial server. Noting that bookmaker odds for various relevant markets are never updated after the coin toss, this suggests a small but systematic inefficiency.
