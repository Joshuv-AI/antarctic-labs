Diagnostic Yield and Clinical Impact of Comprehensive WES/WGS Testing Beyond Common Genetic Causes in Hereditary Optic Atrophy
Authors: Katrine M Johannesen, Karen Grønskov, Line Kessel, Sarah Linea von Holstein, Lisbeth Birk Møller, Mette Kjøbæk Gundestrup Andersen, Marianne Søndergaard Khinchi, Steffen Hamann, Marianne Wegener, Mette Bertelsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHereditary optic atrophy is characterized by degeneration of retinal ganglion cells and may result from a wide range of genetic etiologies. While pathogenic variants in OPA1 and primary mitochondrial variants causing Leber hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON) account for a substantial proportion of cases, many patients remain genetically unsolved. We evaluated the diagnostic yield and clinical impact of comprehensive whole exome/genome sequencing (WES/WGS)‐based virtual panel testing in 62 partially pre‐screened individuals with suspected hereditary optic atrophy. A total of 51 genes associated with optic atrophy and mitochondrial DNA variants were analyzed. Clinical data were systematically retrieved from medical records, including information on extraocular manifestations. A genetic diagnosis was established in 21 patients (33.9%). Pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in OPA1 accounted for 57.1% of solved cases, whereas 42.9% involved other genes, including WFS1, ACO2, NR2F1, UCHL1, CACNA1F, and COQ2. In the majority of patients with non‐OPA1 findings, the genetic diagnosis prompted additional clinical evaluation, surveillance, or therapeutic intervention. Our findings demonstrate that broad WES/WGS‐based testing increases diagnostic yield and expands the genetic spectrum beyond OPA1 and LHON, frequently revealing syndromic conditions with direct clinical implications. Comprehensive genomic testing with broader gene panels should therefore be considered part of the diagnostic workup when hereditary optic atrophy is suspected.Keywords: genetics, ophthalmogenetics, optic atrophy

Introduction

Hereditary optic atrophy is characterized by degeneration of retinal ganglion cells and progressive visual loss, typically with onset in childhood or early adulthood. Patients commonly present with reduced visual acuity, visual field defects, and color vision impairment. Fundoscopy reveals optic disc pallor, and optical coherence tomography (OCT) may demonstrate thinning of the macular ganglion cells and peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL). Although no curative treatment currently exists, establishing a genetic diagnosis is important for prognostic evaluation, identification of syndromic features, and genetic counseling, as well as for distinction between hereditary optic atrophy and optic atrophy with non‐genetic causes such as inflammation, infection, or compressive lesions [1]. As part of the diagnostic work‐up neuroimaging is often performed to exclude structural pathology, whereas genetic testing may help establish a hereditary diagnosis and guide patient management.

Pathogenic variants in OPA1 are the most common cause of autosomal dominant optic atrophy (ADOA), also known as Kjer optic atrophy. OPA1 encodes a GTPase protein expressed in the inner mitochondrial membrane, essential for mitochondrial dynamics and maintenance of retinal ganglion cells. The condition is characterized by bilateral optic nerve degeneration, usually presenting within the first decade of life, with variable severity and incomplete penetrance [2]. In addition to isolated optic atrophy, some individuals develop extraocular manifestations including sensorineural hearing loss, peripheral neuropathy, myopathy, and ataxia [3] Another major cause of hereditary optic atrophy is Leber hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON), most commonly resulting from one of three recurrent mitochondrial DNA variants (m.3460G>A, m.11778G>A, m.14484 T>C). LHON typically presents in young adulthood with subacute unilateral central vision loss, followed shortly by involvement of the contralateral eye. Fundoscopy in the acute phase reveals hyperemia of the optic disc, peripapillary telangiectasis, vascular tortuosity, and swelling of the retinal fiber layer followed in later phases by optic disc pallor [3]. Penetrance is incomplete and influenced by sex and additional genetic or environmental factors [3].

Despite the predominance of OPA1 and LHON, a substantial proportion of patients remain genetically unsolved. In patients clinically suspected of Kjer optic atrophy, OPA1 variants account for approximately 60%–90% of cases, leaving a significant subset unsolved [1, 3]. Recent studies applying next‐generation sequencing (NGS) approaches, including gene panels, whole exome sequencing (WES), and whole genome sequencing (WGS), have reported diagnostic yields ranging from approximately 22% to 37% in cohorts with hereditary optic neuropathy [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. Some of the studies have demonstrated genetic heterogeneity beyond OPA1, with recurrent involvement of genes such as WFS1, ACO2, and NR2F1. WFS1 encodes the transmembrane glycoprotein wolframin, which is crucial for calcium homeostasis, protein‐folding and export, as well as stress regulation, all within the ER of the cells. WFS1 is highly expressed in brain, pancreas, and heart correlating with the clinical phenotype of Wolfram syndrome, classically characterized by childhood‐onset diabetes mellitus, optic atrophy, deafness, and progressive neurodegeneration [10]. ACO2 encodes a mitochondrial enzyme essential for the Krebs cycle, and pathogenic variants in ACO2 are associated with infantile‐cerebellar‐retinal degeneration and optic atrophy [11]. NR2F1 encodes a transcription factor regulating early embryonic brain development, and the cause of Bosch‐Boonstra‐Schaaf optic atrophy syndrome (BBSOAS), characterized by developmental delay, optic atrophy, and epilepsy [12].

In the present study, we aimed to evaluate the diagnostic yield of comprehensive WES/WGS‐based virtual panel testing in a cohort of children and adults with suspected hereditary optic atrophy. In addition to determining genetic diagnostic yield, we systematically assessed the clinical consequences of the molecular diagnoses, including the identification of syndromic features and changes in clinical management, an aspect that has not been systematically addressed in previous studies.

Materials and Methods

Patients were identified through the clinical database of the Department of Clinical Genetics, Copenhagen University Hospital (Rigshospitalet, Denmark). Individuals referred for genetic testing between January 1, 2022, and January 24, 2025 due to clinical suspicion of hereditary optic atrophy were eligible for inclusion. As this was a clinically referred cohort, some patients had undergone prior targeted genetic testing based on suspected phenotype. Patients with a clinical suspicion of LHON were often tested for the three common LHON‐associated mtDNA variants, whereas patients with a phenotype and family history suggestive of dominant optic atrophy could have undergone prior OPA1 single gene analysis.

Genetic Analyses and Variant Classification

Genetic analyses were performed on DNA extracted from whole blood samples using standard methods.

Whole exome sequencing (WES) was performed using the Twist Human Core Exome Kit (Twist Bioscience, South San Francisco, CA, USA) followed by short‐read sequencing on an Illumina platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), with a median target coverage ≥ 80×.

Whole genome sequencing (WGS) was performed using PCR‐free library preparation and short‐read sequencing on an Illumina platform with an average coverage of 30× (National Genome Center Denmark).

Sequence reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg38) using BWA, and variants were called using the GATK pipeline. Structural variants and copy number variants (CNVs) were detected using CNVkit, CNVnator, Manta, Lumpy, and Delly. Variant annotation, filtering, and prioritization were performed using VarSeq (Golden Helix, Bozeman, MT, USA).

Multiplex Ligation‐dependent Probe Amplification (MLPA) for deletion and duplication analysis of OPA1 was performed using kit P229‐B5 (MRC‐Holland, Amsterdam, Netherlands).

Data were analyzed using a virtual gene‐panel of 51 genes associated with optic atrophy and related disorders including nuclear genes and mtDNA variants (ACO2, AFG3L2, ALPK1, AP3B2, ATAD3A, ATP1A3, AUH, CACNA1F, C19orf12, CISD2, COQ2, DNAJC30, DNM1L, EPRS1, FDXR, ISCA2, MCAT, MECR, MFF, MFN2, MIEF1, MT‐ATP6, MT‐ND1, MT‐ND4, MT‐ND6, MTPAP, MTRFR, NBAS, NDUFA12, NDUFAF5, NDUFS1, NDUFS2, NR2F1, OPA1, OPA3, PMPCA, POLG, RTN4IP1, SLC25A46, SLC44A1, SLC52A2, SPG7, SSBP1, TFG, TIMM8A, TMEM126A, TSFM, UCHL1, VCAN, WFS1, YME1L1). Segregation analysis was performed where parental samples or other relevant family samples were available.

Variants were classified according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines [13]; and additional general recommendations from the ClinGen working group (https://clinicalgenome.org/working‐groups/sequence‐variant‐interpretation).

Patients with non‐OPA1 pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were included for detailed clinical description. In selected cases, variants of uncertain significance (VUS) were included when clinical, segregation, or functional data supported a likely contribution to the phenotype.

Data Collection

For all patients, demographic data, sequencing method, and genetic results were recorded. For individuals with non‐OPA1 and non‐LHON findings, detailed clinical data were systematically retrieved from electronic medical records, including ophthalmological findings (best‐corrected visual acuity, color vision, visual fields, fundus examination, and optical coherence tomography (OCT)), neurological features, systemic manifestations, clinical follow‐up, therapeutic interventions, and pedigree information. The clinical diagnosis of optic atrophy was established by the referring ophthalmologist or neuro‐ophthalmologist based on the overall clinical assessment, including fundoscopic findings, OCT, visual function testing.

Ethics Statement

Written informed consent was obtained from all individuals or their legal guardians in cases when clinical data were presented. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Danish data protection authorities. The study was approved by Science Ethics and Research support, Center for Disease, Capital Region (R‐24061677).

Results

Sixty‐two patients were included (43.5% female, 56.5% male). The mean age at genetic evaluation was 38 years (range 2–74 years). Forty‐nine individuals underwent whole exome sequencing (WES), and 13 underwent whole genome sequencing (WGS). In 12 patients, exome/genome sequencing was supplemented with MLPA analysis of OPA1.

A molecular diagnosis was established in 21 unrelated patients, corresponding to a diagnostic yield of 33.9%. Pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in OPA1 accounted for 12 of 21 genetically solved cases (57.1%). The remaining nine genetic diagnoses (42.9%) involved other genes: WFS1 (n = 3), ACO2 (n = 2), and NR2F1, UCHL1, CACNA1F, and COQ2 (n = 1 each). WFS1 (n = 3)

Three patients harbored clinically relevant variants in WFS1.

Patient 1 (female, age 43) presented with bilateral optic atrophy and was compound heterozygous for one likely pathogenic variant and one VUS in WFS1 (Table 1) confirmed in trans. Previous OPA1 and LHON frequent mitochondrial variant testing was negative. Following the molecular diagnosis, endocrinological assessment revealed prediabetes requiring treatment with metformin. Hearing was normal at latest follow‐up. Ophthalmological findings are shown in Figure 1.

Patient 2 (female, age 5) carried a heterozygous likely pathogenic missense variant (Table 1) and presented with mild bilateral optic atrophy with mild pallor of the optic nerves and slight thinning of the ganglion cell layer on OCT. Subsequent audiological evaluation demonstrated mild bilateral low‐frequency sensorineural hearing loss that currently at the age of 7 years does not require hearing aids. Her vision loss is mild and stationary at latest follow‐up (7 years of age).

Patient 3 (male, age 45) was compound heterozygous for two variants of uncertain significance (VUS), Table 1. The variant p.(Ile349_Phe350delinsValIle) was however interpreted as likely pathogenic since p.Phe350Ile alone is classified accordingly. Previous genetic testing including only OPA1 and LHON frequent mitochondrial variants was negative. He had a history of decreased vision from early childhood; however, he was not diagnosed with optic atrophy until the age of 27 years. Following the genetic diagnosis, audiological and endocrinological evaluations showed no clinically significant abnormalities at last follow‐up. Brain MRI, as part of the diagnostic work‐up, showed mild cerebellar and pontine atrophy on MRI.

ACO2 (n = 2)

Two patients harbored biallelic pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in ACO2.

Patient 4 (female, age 24) carried two pathogenic variants (Table 1) and presented with optic atrophy, retinitis pigmentosa, ataxia, developmental delay, and progressive neurological involvement including dystonia and loss of speech. Ophthalmological examination demonstrated bilateral optic nerve pallor and indicated retinal dystrophy (Figure 1).

Patient 5 (female, age 14) was compound heterozygous for two likely pathogenic variants (Table 1) and presented with bilateral optic atrophy, myopia, and retinal thinning. Brain MRI revealed small optic nerves and unilateral cerebellar hypoplasia without corresponding clinical neurological symptoms.

NR2F1 (n = 1)

One patient (male, age 7) harbored a de novo pathogenic deletion of exon 2 in NR2F1 (Table 1). He presented with developmental delay, bilateral optic atrophy, hypermetropia, and latent nystagmus. Brain MRI demonstrated partial agenesis of the corpus callosum. One focal seizure had occurred at last follow‐up, with EEG showing focal epilepsy with left centroparietal–post‐temporal activity.

UCHL1 (n = 1)

One patient (male, age 26) carried a splice‐site variant in UCHL1 (Table 1), initially classified as a VUS but reclassified as likely pathogenic following functional analysis demonstrating aberrant splicing. Previous OPA1 testing was negative. He presented with progressive bilateral optic atrophy and central visual field defects with childhood onset. Affected family members carrying the same variant exhibited ataxia without optic atrophy.

CACNA1F (n = 1)

One male patient (age 10) carried a likely pathogenic splice‐site variant in CACNA1F (Table 1). He presented at the age of 5 years with reduced visual acuity and myopia. Ophthalmological findings included thinning of the ganglion cell layer on OCT, and full‐field electroretinography showed an electronegative pattern with reduced photopic and scotopic responses. Initial targeted panel testing was negative, and the diagnosis was established later by WGS.

COQ2 (n = 1)

One patient (male, age 36) carried a homozygous missense variant in COQ2, classified as a VUS (Table 1). Given the variant assessment, the specific clinical presentation and absence of alternative genetic findings, the variant was considered a potential contributor to the phenotype. He presented with progressive visual impairment, optic atrophy, and retinitis pigmentosa. Based on the molecular findings and clinical presentation, coenzyme Q10 supplementation was initiated. Clinical response has not yet been determined.

Discussion

In this cohort of 62 individuals with suspected hereditary optic atrophy, comprehensive WES/WGS‐based testing yielded a molecular diagnosis in 33.9% of patients. This diagnostic rate is consistent with previous next‐generation sequencing (NGS) studies reporting yields between 22% and 37% in hereditary optic neuropathies [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. As in prior reports [4], OPA1 remained the most frequently implicated gene; however, 42.9% of genetically solved cases involved non‐OPA1 genes, highlighting the genetic heterogeneity of hereditary optic atrophy.

Our relative frequency of OPA1 variants was lower than the historically reported detection rate of up to 70% in clinically suspected autosomal dominant optic atrophy (ADOA) [1, 14]. This likely reflects partial pre‐screening of patients for OPA1 and common LHON variants prior to referral for WES/WGS, resulting in a higher number of OPA1‐negative cases, as has also been the case in other pre‐screened cohorts [4]. In addition, more recent studies have included broader and more heterogeneous patient cohorts compared with earlier family‐based ADOA studies, which may contribute to a relative decrease in the proportion of OPA1‐associated disease [6, 7].

Here, we uncovered genetic causes of hereditary optic atrophy in six non‐OPA1 genes. Other cohorts have had similar diagnostic yield, but a larger number of non‐OPA1 genes [4, 5, 6]. This could be attributed to the larger patient cohorts in other studies that would have allowed them to uncover some of the ultra‐rare genetic variants, which we could not with our limited patient cohort.

No patients with primary LHON variants were identified in this cohort. This likely reflects past clinical practice, in which patients suspected of LHON have frequently been tested directly for the three common mitochondrial DNA variants prior to referral for broader genomic testing. Consequently, individuals with confirmed LHON may not have proceeded to WES/WGS‐based analysis. Additionally, for many patients with LHON, the disease‐causing variant is already known in the family and therefore new affected family members are tested for the specific variant only.

Notably, six of nine patients with non‐OPA1 findings exhibited extraocular manifestations, including developmental delay, movement disorders, endocrine dysfunction, or hearing loss. In several cases, these features were recognized or systematically evaluated only after the genetic diagnosis was established, highlighting the clinical utility of comprehensive genetic testing. Compared to other studies that have also used WES/WGS, many of the non‐OPA1 findings are recurrent across studies. This is especially true for the genes WFS1, ACO2, NR2F1, which are all among the top 10 genes in studies of hereditary optic atrophy using NGS techniques [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9].

In our cohort, three patients harbored clinically relevant variants in WFS1, illustrating the phenotypic spectrum ranging from classical Wolfram syndrome—an autosomal recessive disorder characterized by optic atrophy, diabetes mellitus or insipidus, sensorineural hearing loss, and neurological or urological involvement—to milder autosomal dominant presentations referred to as non‐classic Wolfram syndrome [3, 10, 15]. None of the patients had been suspected of syndromic disease prior to genetic testing. Following the genetic diagnosis, one patient was found to have prediabetes sufficient to prompt treatment, and another was diagnosed with mild low‐frequency sensorineural hearing loss. Genotype–phenotype correlations in WFS1‐related disorders suggest that missense variants are more frequently associated with milder phenotypes, whereas truncating variants are often linked to more severe disease [10, 15]. All three patients in our cohort carried at least one missense variant, which may partly explain the relatively mild or late‐onset clinical presentations. Patient 1 was genetically consistent with autosomal recessive Wolfram syndrome based on compound heterozygosity for two variants (one likely pathogenic, one VUS). However, her phenotype was mild, consisting of optic atrophy and prediabetes at last follow‐up. The c.1597C>T variant was previously classified as likely pathogenic at initial evaluation. However, following recent updates to the GnomAD database, the variant has now been identified in two homozygous individuals, leading to its reclassification as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS) based on current criteria. Nevertheless, it remains plausible that these individuals may exhibit very mild or subclinical phenotypes that have not been recognized or reported as disease. Patient 2 carried a previously reported missense variant (p.Thr699Met) associated with non‐syndromic low‐frequency sensorineural hearing loss [16]. In this patient that also was found to have mild hearing loss, mild optic atrophy was identified; however, visual function has remained stable to date. Given her young age, continued surveillance is warranted. Patient 3 carried two variants classified as VUS in WFS1; however, variant p.(Ile349_Phe350delinsValIle) involves a change of two consecutive amino acids of which p.Phe350Ile alone is classified as likely pathogenic with 8 points (PS4_Su, PM2_Su, PM3_M, PM5_M, PP3_M). Thus, classification as classical or non‐classic Wolfram syndrome remains uncertain as he lacked defining syndromic features at last evaluation.

Two patients carried biallelic variants in ACO2, demonstrating the broad phenotypic spectrum associated with this gene. ACO2 variants were initially described in association with infantile cerebellar‐retinal degeneration, including optic atrophy, retinitis pigmentosa, ataxia, and developmental delay [11]. Subsequent studies have expanded the phenotype to include milder or isolated optic atrophy presentations [11, 17]. In our cohort, one patient exhibited a classic syndromic phenotype, whereas the other presented with predominantly ophthalmological involvement, further supporting phenotypic variability in ACO2‐related disease.

One patient harbored a de novo exon deletion in NR2F1, consistent with Bosch‐Boonstra‐Schaaf optic atrophy syndrome (BBSOAS) [18]. The patient displayed a typical phenotype with developmental delay, optic atrophy, and structural brain abnormalities. The deletion was identified by WGS, illustrating the advantage of genome‐wide approaches in detecting structural variants that may be missed by exome‐based analyses. Emerging evidence suggests potential therapeutic implications for epilepsy in BBSOAS, including response to perampanel [19].

A splice‐site variant in UCHL1 was identified in one patient and reclassified as likely pathogenic following functional validation. Heterozygous UCHL1 variants are associated with variable autosomal dominant phenotypes including optic atrophy, late‐onset ataxia, and neuropathy [20, 21]. In our cohort, optic atrophy was the predominant feature in the index patient, whereas other affected family members primarily exhibited neurological manifestations. This intrafamilial variability complicates prognostic counselling and underscores the importance of genetic diagnosis in guiding follow‐up.

One patient carried a homozygous missense variant in COQ2, classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS) according to ACMG criteria (Table 1). The variant, however, fulfilled multiple pathogenicity criteria and was close to the threshold for likely pathogenic classification. The clinical presentation, including optic atrophy in combination with retinitis pigmentosa, was highly suggestive of a mitochondrial disorder compatible with COQ2‐related disease [22]. In the absence of alternative genetic findings, the variant was considered a strong candidate to contribute to the patient's phenotype, and coenzyme Q10 supplementation was initiated, as this has previously been shown to counteract the symptoms of primary Q10 deficiency, including kidney and muscular symptoms [23]. Longer follow‐up and functional validation will be required to further clarify the pathogenicity of the variant and potential treatment response.

In this partially pre‐screened cohort, comprehensive WES/WGS‐based virtual panel testing provided a genetic diagnosis in approximately one‐third of patients and revealed clinically actionable findings in a substantial proportion of non‐OPA1 cases. In six of nine patients with non‐OPA1 diagnoses, the genetic result prompted additional clinical evaluation or surveillance. Establishing a genetic diagnosis extends beyond confirmation of the underlying cause and has important implications for patient management, surveillance of syndromic features, and genetic counselling regarding prognosis, reproductive planning, and identification of at‐risk family members.

In our current clinical practice, testing has transitioned to a WGS‐based approach, which allows improved detection of structural variants, such as the NR2F1 deletion identified in patient 6. Broader genome‐wide analyses may therefore further increase diagnostic yield compared with exome‐based strategies. Emerging technologies, including long‐read sequencing, are also expected to potentially further enhance variant detection.

The study is limited by its retrospective design and partial prior genetic testing, which may influence diagnostic yield estimates. The partially pre‐screening for OPA1 and LHON, may underestimate the true proportion of these conditions in an unselected population. Furthermore, interpretation of variants of uncertain significance remains challenging, particularly in genes with evolving phenotype definitions. We also acknowledge that distinguishing primary optic atrophy from retinal disease with secondary optic nerve involvement may be challenging in some cases and this may potentially have contributed to the proportion of unresolved cases in our cohort. Most patients will have had an MRI performed, as this is only omitted if there is a strong suspicion of a genetic cause, for example If there is a convincing family history of optic atrophy. Consequently, alternative causes of optic nerve disease cannot be completely excluded in all unresolved cases. Finally, not all patients with negative WES results routinely underwent WGS when this became available. Therefore, deep intronic variants and other variant classes detectable by WGS cannot be excluded in all unresolved cases.

In conclusion, our findings confirm the considerable genetic heterogeneity underlying hereditary optic atrophy and support the use of comprehensive genomic testing in patients clinically suspected of this condition. Beyond establishing a genetic diagnosis, the genetic characterization facilitates identification of syndromic features, enables initiation of relevant surveillance strategies, and prepares patients for future gene‐specific therapies.
