Metabolomic insights into functional protein mediated salt stress adaptation in the osmophilic yeast Zygosaccharomyces rouxii
Authors: Dingkang Wang, Lerong Liu, Sha Liu, Ting Shi, Yihe Bu, Yanqin Rong, Shiyu Liu, Longzhan Gan, Yiting Kang, Yongjie Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHigh salinity inhibits microbial growth by disturbing osmotic balance, redox equilibrium, and membrane integrity. Zygosaccharomyces rouxii, an osmophilic yeast widely used in high-salt fermented foods, has evolved adaptive responses to such stress, but the global metabolic mechanisms underlying these responses, especially under functional protein supplementation, remain poorly understood.Methods and resultsIn this study, an untargeted UPLC-HRMS-based metabolomics approach was used to investigate the metabolic responses of Z. rouxii to high-salt stress and functional protein supplementation. Multivariate analysis revealed significant metabolic reprogramming at 120 g/L NaCl, while functional protein supplementation induced a distinct metabolic state beyond that caused by salt stress alone. Pathway enrichment analysis showed that protein supplementation markedly enhanced amino acid metabolism, antioxidant-related pathways, and nutrient transport, while preferentially modulating lipid metabolism associated with membrane remodeling. In particular, pathways related to unsaturated fatty acids and glycerolipids were finely regulated, suggesting improved membrane flexibility under hyperosmotic conditions.ConclusionsCollectively, these findings indicate that functional protein supplementation enhances coordinated metabolic regulation in Z. rouxii, thereby improving metabolic flexibility and salt stress tolerance. This study provides mechanistic insights into osmotic stress adaptation and proposes a feasible strategy to enhance yeast robustness under high-salt conditions.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

High salinity is a significant environmental stressor for microbial growth, metabolism, and survival [1]. Elevated NaCl concentrations generate osmotic and ionic stress, disrupting cellular water balance, inducing oxidative damage, and impairing membrane integrity, requiring substantial physiological and metabolic responses [2, 3]. Osmophilic and halotolerant yeasts, such as Zygosaccharomyces rouxii, have evolved sophisticated stress response strategies that include the accumulation of compatible solutes, membrane lipid remodeling, and activation of redox defense systems, allowing them to thrive in high-salt environments, such as those found in soy sauce and other fermented foods [4, 5]. Despite these adaptations, severe salinity continues to impose a significant metabolic load, and enhancing stress tolerance remains a critical problem for both basic research and industrial applications.

Metabolomics has evolved in recent years as a potent tool for characterizing cellular responses to environmental stress at the metabolic level [6–8]. Untargeted metabolomic investigations have shown that salt stress significantly reconfigures amino acid, lipid, and energy metabolism in yeasts and other microorganisms [9]. In parallel, nutritional or functional supplementation treatments have gained popularity as an effective way to improve microbial robustness [10, 11]. Functional proteins and bioactive protein complexes have been shown to improve stress tolerance by influencing antioxidant activity, nitrogen metabolism, and membrane-associated activities [12, 13]. Protein supplementation was therefore introduced in this study because it may act not only as a nutrient source, but also as a functional regulator that supports amino acid metabolism, antioxidant defense, and membrane stability under salt stress. However, how functional protein supplementation alters the global metabolic network of osmophilic yeasts under high-salt conditions remains poorly understood, particularly at the intracellular metabolite level.

To systematically examine Z. rouxii’s metabolic responses to high salinity and functional protein supplementation, we used an untargeted UPLC–HRMS-based metabolomics method. Our goal was to clarify how functional protein supplementation affects amino acid, redox, transport, and lipid metabolism under hyperosmotic stress by combining multivariate statistical analysis with pathway enrichment. Our findings shed new light on the metabolic processes underlying Z. rouxii’s increased salt tolerance and show that functional protein supplementation is a valuable strategy for fostering metabolic adaptability and stress tolerance in osmophilic yeasts.

Materials and methods

Strain and stress experiment

The strain Z. rouxii CGMCC 3791, preserved in the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center (CGMCC), was originally isolated from horsebean chili paste and identified by physiological, biochemical, and 26 S rDNA sequence analyses [14]. Z. rouxii was grown at 30 °C on yeast extract-peptone-dextrose (YPD) medium. For functional protein supplementation tests, cells were fed casein containing astaxanthin at a final concentration of 1 mg/mL. The seed cultures were then transferred to fresh YPD medium and cultured for an additional 24 h as pre-cultures [4]. The cells were cultivated in YPD medium (10 g/L yeast extract, 20 g/L peptone, and 20 g/L glucose) containing the functional protein (1 mg/mL) at 0 g/L and 120 g/L NaCl, for a metabolic study. For each experimental condition, Z. rouxii was cultivated independently, and cells from these independent cultures were collected separately for metabolomics analysis. Based on our long-term growth-curve determination for Z. rouxii, the mid-exponential growth phase was reached at 24 h of cultivation. Therefore, all experimental groups were harvested simultaneously after 24 h, centrifuged for 5 min at 8000 g, and twice washed with distilled water in preparation for metabolomics analysis. The washed cell pellets were then resuspended in distilled water, and 600 µL of the suspension was used for intracellular metabolite extraction.

Extraction of intracellular metabolites

Frozen specimens were thawed to room temperature before processing. Aliquots (600 µL) were transferred to 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes with 200 µL of methanolic L-2-chlorophenylalanine solution (0.06 mg/mL) as an internal standard, followed by a brief vortex mixing (10 s). Protein precipitation was commenced by adding 200 µL of cold methanol: acetonitrile (2 : 1) solution, followed by vortexing (1 min) and ultrasonic extraction (10 min, ice-water bath). After incubating at -20 °C for 30 min, samples were centrifuged (13,000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C). The supernatant (400 µL) was dried using a centrifugal vacuum concentrator. The residue was reconstituted in 300 µL of methanol: water (1:4), vortexed for 30 s, then sonicated for 3 min in an ice-water bath. The cleared supernatant (150 µL) was filtered through 0.22 μm membrane filters into LC vials for storage at -80 °C until analysis after a two-hour incubation at -20 °C and another centrifugation (13,000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C). Equal aliquots from each experimental sample were combined to create quality control samples [15].

Liquid chromatography mass spectrometry/mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis

Ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography combined with high-resolution mass spectrometry (UPLC-HRMS) was used for metabolomic analysis. Thermo Scientific Q-Exactive Orbitrap mass spectrometer with a HESI-II ion source was integrated with a Waters ACQUITY UPLC I-Class Plus system to construct the analytical platform. Using an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) maintained at 45 °C and an autosampler temperature of 10 °C, chromatographic separation was accomplished. The mobile phase contained (A) 0.1% aqueous formic acid and (B) 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile, supplied at 0.35 mL/min with the following gradient: initial 5% B (0.01–2 min), raised to 30% B (4 min), 50% B (8 min), 80% B (10 min), and 100% B (14–15 min), followed by re-equilibration. Mass spectrometric detection was performed in both positive and negative ionization modes with the following parameters: scan range m/z 100–1000; resolution 70,000 (full scan) and 17,500 (MS/MS); stepped normalized collision energies (10, 20, 40 eV); spray voltages 3.8 kV (positive) and 3.2 kV (negative); sheath gas 35 arb; auxiliary gas eight arb; capillary temperature 320 °C; and vaporizer temperature 350 °C [16].

Metabolomics data processing and analysis

The collected LC-MS raw data were thoroughly processed using Progenesis QI (v2.3, Nonlinear Dynamics) and a rigorous analytical methodology. The initial processing involved spectral deconvolution, peak selection with retention-time alignment (≤ 0.1 min window), and intensity normalization across datasets. High-accuracy criteria were used to extract mass-spectral characteristics, with precursor ions having a mass error ≤ 5 ppm and fragments having a mass error ≤ 10 ppm. Product ions had to have at least 5% relative abundance.

Statistical analysis

The normalized metabolomics dataset was evaluated using multivariate statistical methods in RStudio. Each experimental group included three biological replicates. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to assess global metabolic variance and analytical reproducibility. To optimize group separation and identify discriminatory characteristics, supervised modelling was conducted using orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) and partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) [17].

Results

PCA-based discrimination and differential metabolite identification

Before metabolomic analysis, the effect of functional casein supplementation on the survival of Z. rouxii under salt stress was evaluated by plate counting. Under 120 g/L NaCl, exogenous supplementation with functional casein increased the viable cell count from 2.1 × 108 CFU/mL to 5.66 × 108 CFU/mL, indicating that protein supplementation improved yeast survival under high-salt conditions. Based on this result, an untargeted metabolomic analysis was performed to explore the intracellular metabolic mechanisms underlying enhanced stress adaptation. Untargeted metabolomic analysis was used to identify both global metabolic trends and particular metabolite changes in Z. rouxii after salt stress and functional protein supplementation. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear separations among the control (0 g/L NaCl), salt-stressed (120 g/L NaCl), and protein-supplemented salt-stressed groups (Fig. 1A), suggesting significant intracellular metabolic changes. The first two principal components accounted for 56.8% and 15.8% of total variance, respectively, while quality control samples clustered tightly, demonstrating the analytical platform’s resilience and reproducibility. Notably, functional protein supplementation induced a distinct metabolic shift compared to salt stress alone, as evidenced by the separation of the 120 g/L NaCl and 120 g/L NaCl + Protein groups. In addition, Fig. 1B summarizes the number of significantly differential metabolites in the two comparison groups. Compared with the control, the 120 g/L NaCl + Protein group contained 498 up-regulated and 717 down-regulated metabolites. In comparison with the 120 g/L NaCl group, the 120 g/L NaCl + Protein group showed 111 up-regulated and 599 down-regulated metabolites. These results indicate that functional protein supplementation induced substantial metabolic reprogramming, with down-regulated metabolites accounting for the majority of the differential features in both comparisons.

Volcano plot analysis further illustrated the extent of metabolite variation in the two comparison groups, consistent with the global PCA patterns (Fig. 2). In Fig. 2A (120 g/L NaCl + Protein vs. None), 498 metabolites were significantly up-regulated and 717 were significantly down-regulated (p < 0.05, |log₂FC| > 1), indicating extensive metabolic reprogramming in the protein-supplemented cells under high-salt conditions relative to the untreated control. In Fig. 2B (120 g/L NaCl + Protein vs. 120 g/L NaCl), 111 metabolites were significantly up-regulated, and 599 were significantly down-regulated, demonstrating that functional protein supplementation further altered the intracellular metabolic profile beyond the effect of salt stress alone. Taken together, these results show that the combined treatment induced broad metabolic remodeling. At the same time, the comparison between Fig. 2B groups highlights the specific contribution of functional protein supplementation under high-salt stress.

Functional protein supplementation reshapes amino acid and lipid metabolism of Z. rouxii under high-salt stress

To clarify the metabolic reprogramming of Z. rouxii in response to functional protein supplementation under salt stress, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed (Fig. 3). The top enriched pathways in the comparison of 120 g/L NaCl + Protein and the untreated control (Fig. 3A) were primarily associated with amino acid metabolism, including arginine and proline metabolism, arginine biosynthesis, D-amino acid metabolism, histidine metabolism, and alanine/aspartate/glutamate metabolism. These pathways are closely linked to intracellular pH buffering, nitrogen consumption, and osmotic adjustment, suggesting that functional protein supplementation enhances salt tolerance by strengthening amino acid-based osmoprotective and stress-adaptive processes.

Furthermore, folate significantly enriches the one-carbon pool and the metabolism of thiamine and glutathione, indicating improved cofactor availability and redox equilibrium at high salinity. Significantly, aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis and ABC transporters were similarly enriched, suggesting increased capability for protein synthesis and nutrient transport in protein-supplemented cells. Further comparison of 120 g/L NaCl + Protein and 120 g/L NaCl alone (Fig. 3B) showed a clear enrichment of metabolic pathways associated with lipids, such as production of unsaturated fatty acids, α-linolenic acid metabolism, and arachidonic acid metabolism. Functional protein supplementation increases adaptive lipid metabolism beyond the effects of salt stress alone, as these pathways are essential for membrane remodeling and maintenance of membrane fluidity under osmotic stress. All of these findings show that functional protein supplementation increases the metabolic robustness and salt stress tolerance of Z. rouxii by inducing coordinated modulation of amino acid, redox, transport, and lipid metabolic pathways.

Functional protein supplementation modulates lipid-associated metabolite profiles under salt stress

A heatmap analysis of the top 50 substantially divergent metabolites revealed diverse metabolic patterns in the non-stressed control, 120 g/L NaCl, and 120 g/L NaCl + protein groups (Fig. 4). A clear clustering separation was observed, demonstrating significant metabolic reprogramming in response to salt stress and functional protein supplementation. Numerous lipid-related metabolites, including unsaturated fatty acids and acylcarnitine derivatives, accumulated significantly more under salt stress than under control conditions, indicating an increased lipid metabolic response to high salinity. Functional protein supplementation at 120 g/L NaCl, on the other hand, significantly altered this profile, reducing the abundance of several salt-induced lipid metabolites and selectively modulating specific molecules. These results show that functional protein supplementation can effectively modify intracellular metabolite expression under high-salt circumstances, particularly via modulating lipid-associated metabolic reactions.

Lollipop map analysis indicated considerable differences in intracellular metabolites of Z. rouxii under various treatment settings (Fig. 5). Compared to the untreated control, cells exposed to 120 g/L NaCl with functional protein supplementation showed significant metabolic reprogramming (Fig. 5A), as evidenced by marked changes in lipid-related metabolites, organic acids, and amino acid derivatives, with both up- and down-regulated compounds exhibiting high VIP values. These findings suggest a robust metabolic response, including membrane remodeling and stress adaptation, under high-salinity conditions. When 120 g/L NaCl + Protein was compared to 120 g/L NaCl alone (Fig. 5B), more metabolite changes were seen, especially in glycerolipid derivatives and bioactive lipid compounds. The upregulation of numerous signaling and membrane-associated metabolites suggests that functional protein supplementation alters intracellular metabolism, thereby improving Z. rouxii’s stress tolerance under hyperosmotic conditions.

Discussion

Microorganisms under extreme osmotic and ionic stress from high salinity must undergo substantial metabolic reprogramming to maintain cellular equilibrium [18]. Untargeted metabolomics in this investigation demonstrated that Z. rouxii experiences significant global metabolic changes under 120 g/L NaCl stress, as shown by several differentially expressed metabolites and distinct PCA separation. These results are in line with earlier studies demonstrating that osmophilic and halotolerant yeasts respond to salt stress by broadly altering central carbon, amino acid, and lipid metabolism to prevent oxidative damage, water loss, and membrane disruption [19, 20].

Crucially, our findings show that functional protein supplementation creates a unique metabolic state that can be distinguished from that induced by salt stress alone, underscoring its role as an active modulator rather than a passive nutrient supply. One of the main conclusions of this study is that, in high-salt environments, functional protein supplementation significantly improved amino acid-related metabolic pathways, such as arginine and proline metabolism, glutamate-related pathways, and aminoacyl-tRNA production [21, 22]. Proline, arginine, and glutamate are well-known compatible solutes and stress protectants that help maintain osmotic balance, regulate redox, and stabilize proteins and macromolecular structures [23, 24]. Improved antioxidant capacity, crucial under salt-induced oxidative stress, is further supported by enrichment in glutathione metabolism [25, 26].

Similar metabolic patterns have been observed in Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Debaryomyces hansenii, in which increased amino acid turnover and redox buffering are strongly associated with greater salt tolerance [27, 28]. Thus, our findings suggest that functional protein supplementation enhances Z. rouxii’s innate osmoprotective and stress-adaptive systems at the metabolic level. This difference between Fig. 3A and B likely reflects the different biological meanings of the two comparisons. Figure 3A represents the overall response to both salt stress and protein supplementation, so amino acid metabolism is more prominent. By contrast, Fig. 3B highlights the specific effect of protein supplementation under salt stress, in which lipid remodeling becomes the primary additional adaptive response to maintain membrane fluidity and integrity. In addition to amino acid metabolism, lipid-associated pathways have emerged as a key target for functional protein regulation under salt stress. Protein supplementation preferentially modulates lipid metabolism, including unsaturated fatty acid production, glycerolipid metabolism, and arachidonic/α-linolenic acid pathways, as evidenced by KEGG enrichment, heatmap clustering, and lollipop map analysis. Membrane lipid remodeling is a key adaptation to maintain membrane fluidity and integrity under hyperosmotic conditions [29]. The observed reduction in excessive salt-induced lipid accumulation, together with the upregulation of specific bioactive and signaling lipids, points to a more regulated and efficient membrane adaptation approach in protein-supplemented cells [30]. Similar regulatory processes have recently been described in stress-adapted yeasts and fungi, where fine-tuning rather than straightforward upregulation of lipid synthesis was linked to increased stress resilience [31, 32]. Overall, our findings indicate that functional protein supplementation enhances coordinated regulation of amino acid, redox, transport, and lipid metabolism in Z. rouxii, thereby improving metabolic flexibility and salt stress tolerance. Future studies could further compare the metabolic responses of Z. rouxii with those of Saccharomyces cerevisiae under separate non-ionic (e.g., glycerol) and ionic (e.g., NaCl) osmotic stress conditions, thereby helping distinguish the relative contributions of osmotic and ionic components to yeast stress adaptation.

Conclusion

Finally, this study presents a comprehensive metabolomic viewpoint on Z. rouxii’s adaptive responses to high-salt stress and functional protein supplementation. High salinity caused broad metabolic disturbances, whereas functional protein supplementation resulted in a discrete and coordinated remodeling of intracellular metabolism, primarily affecting amino acid, redox, transport, and lipid pathways. The increased modulation of compatible solute metabolism, antioxidant capacity, and membrane lipid remodelling suggests that functional protein supplementation enhances metabolic flexibility while reinforcing natural stress-adaptive mechanisms under hyperosmotic conditions. These findings not only add to our understanding of salt tolerance mechanisms in osmophilic yeasts but also highlight functional protein supplementation as a potential approach to improve microbial robustness in high-salt fermentation processes and related commercial applications.
