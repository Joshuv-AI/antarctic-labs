Kinesiophobia in older patients after stroke: a mixed-methods study using a life-course perspective
Authors: Yujing Gu, Honggan Wang, Mengmeng Zhong, Zhaodi Liao, Jianfen Ma, Jun Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo examine the prevalence of kinesiophobia and factors associated with it in older patients after stroke, and to explore how historical context, social relationships, physical condition, and psychological factors collectively shape the experience of kinesiophobia in later life.DesignA mixed-methods study incorporating a cross-sectional quantitative survey and qualitative semi-structured interviews.MethodsBetween January 2024 and June 2025, 307 patients aged ≥ 60 years in the subacute or chronic phase after stroke were recruited from a tertiary hospital in Wuxi, China. Patients with severe cognitive impairment, psychiatric disorders, or non-stroke-related mobility limitations were excluded. Participants completed structured questionnaires assessing physical function, psychological status, and illness adaptation. Quantitative data were analysed using descriptive statistics, correlation analyses, and binary logistic regression to identify factors associated with kinesiophobia. Following the survey, semi-structured interviews were conducted from July to September 2025 with 18 participants to explore their experiences from a life-course perspective.ResultsAmong the 307 participants, the prevalence of kinesiophobia was 64.8% (199/307). Quantitative analysis identified muscle weakness, fear of falling, frailty, and anxiety as factors associated with a higher likelihood of kinesiophobia, while age ≥ 81 years, exercise self-efficacy, and illness acceptance were statistically associated with a lower likelihood of kinesiophobia. Qualitative analysis revealed four core themes: historical time and place, linked lives, timing in lives, and human agency, highlighting the interplay of early-life experiences, cultural values, family interactions, environmental barriers, and cognitive appraisal in the development of kinesiophobia.ConclusionKinesiophobia in older patients after stroke is influenced by a combination of physical, psychological, social, and experiential factors. These findings may inform comprehensive nursing assessments to identify patients who may be at risk of kinesiophobia and to guide the development of individualised rehabilitation and home-care strategies that support safe exercise participation.ImpactKinesiophobia should be integrated into routine nursing assessments for older patients after stroke. Applying assessment findings to care planning and home-care guidance may help identify activity avoidance and inform strategies to support rehabilitation engagement.Patient contributionPatients participated in surveys and semi-structured interviews but were not involved in study design or data analysis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07809-9.

Introduction

Stroke is a major global public health concern, and its disease burden continues to rise with population ageing [1–3]. In China, stroke-related mortality is concentrated predominantly among older adults [4]. Owing to the combined effects of physiological ageing, multimorbidity, and frailty, the rehabilitation trajectory of older patients after stroke is often more complex than that of younger and middle-aged patients [5].

Clinically, stroke recovery is typically divided into acute (< 1 week), subacute (1 week to 6 months), and chronic (> 6 months) phases [6]. The subacute phase represents a critical window for functional recovery and neuroplasticity, whereas rehabilitation in the chronic phase increasingly focuses on long-term functional maintenance and management of sequelae [6]. Recent systematic reviews have confirmed the significant benefits of exercise interventions in improving balance, enhancing muscle strength, and increasing mobility [7–9]. Although clinical guidelines and empirical studies consistently emphasise the importance of maintaining appropriate physical activity after stroke [10, 11], a substantial proportion of people who have had a stroke fail to establish regular physical activity or exercise habits after stroke onset [12, 13]. Reduced activity among people after stroke is influenced by multiple factors, including physical limitations, such as walking ability, balance, and postural control; sociodemographic factors, such as age and pre-stroke activity levels; and psychological variables, such as kinesiophobia [14, 15].

Among these factors, kinesiophobia has been recognised as an important psychological determinant of rehabilitation engagement. It refers to an excessive and persistent fear of physical activity, fundamentally rooted in an individual’s subjective perception of the risk of reinjury [16]. Importantly, kinesiophobia is not a specific phobia in the psychopathological sense; rather, it is more commonly conceptualised as a psychological construct used to explain fear-driven patterns of avoidance behaviour, which may restrict participation in exercise and physical activity [17]. Previous studies have reported a high prevalence of kinesiophobia among people after stroke, with rates ranging from 47.9% to 78% [18, 19]. Kinesiophobia rarely occurs in isolation; instead, through the Fear-Avoidance Model, it may reduce rehabilitation participation and contribute to disuse-related functional decline, thereby creating a vicious cycle [17]. However, one question remains insufficiently explained: when rehabilitation training is repeatedly emphasised and the benefits of exercise are widely recognised, why do some older patients after stroke continue to perceive exercise as a potential threat and engage in avoidance behaviours?

Previous quantitative studies have mainly examined individual-level physical symptoms and psychological adaptation variables, such as pain experience, frailty, and illness acceptance, to explore their statistical associations with kinesiophobia [19, 20]. Although these studies provide important evidence for identifying factors associated with kinesiophobia, they pay limited attention to broader temporal and contextual influences, including sociocultural relations, life stage, and previous experiences. Moreover, evidence among older Chinese patients after stroke remains relatively limited, particularly regarding how kinesiophobia is understood within patients’ family and life contexts.

The life-course perspective offers an appropriate theoretical framework for addressing these gaps [21]. This perspective emphasises that health status, illness experience, and adaptive responses are shaped over time by the interplay of personal experiences, social relationships, and structural conditions, which accumulate and interact throughout the lifespan. It has also been used to understand how individuals develop self-care practices, negotiate caregiving relationships, and adapt to changing care needs across different life stages [22]. In this study, this perspective is particularly useful for understanding why older patients’ perceptions of exercise-related risk may not arise solely from post-stroke functional impairment, but may also be intertwined with earlier life experiences, bodily experiences, family roles, and previous illness events.

Accordingly, this study adopts an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design. In the quantitative phase, a cross-sectional survey was conducted to identify the prevalence and key factors associated with kinesiophobia among older patients in the non-acute phase after stroke. In the qualitative phase, guided by the life-course perspective, in-depth interviews were used to trace participants’ life trajectories, explore the meanings and contextual mechanisms underlying kinesiophobia, and explain how the quantitative findings were embedded in participants’ life experiences. By integrating quantitative and qualitative evidence, this study seeks to move beyond the identification of isolated risk factors and develop a more holistic and contextualised understanding of kinesiophobia among older patients after stroke, thereby providing a theoretical basis for nursing assessment and rehabilitation interventions.

Methods

Data collection and analysis were conducted in two sequential phases. The first phase involved quantitative research, using questionnaires to collect data and perform statistical analyses to identify factors independently associated with kinesiophobia. The second phase consisted of qualitative research, conducted after completing the quantitative analysis. The quantitative component employed a cross-sectional observational design and was reported following the STROBE guidelines [23], while the qualitative component adhered strictly to the COREQ reporting standards [24].

Quantitative research

Sample selection

Convenience sampling was used to recruit older patients in the non-acute phase after stroke who attended the outpatient neurology centre of a tertiary hospital in Jiangsu Province between January 2024 and June 2025. Based on previous studies, the prevalence of kinesiophobia among older patients after stroke was estimated to be 78% [19]. Substituting these values yielded a base sample size of 264. Accounting for an anticipated 10% rate of invalid questionnaires or attrition, the minimum required sample size was set at 294. To ensure statistical stability and power, the study aimed to recruit at least 300 participants.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) a confirmed diagnosis of stroke by imaging (CT/MRI); (2) patients in the subacute or chronic phase, with stable condition, clear consciousness, and cognitive and communicative ability to complete the questionnaire independently or with assistance; (3) age ≥ 60 years; and (4) voluntary participation with written informed consent. Exclusion criteria were: (1) significant psychiatric disorders or severe cognitive or intellectual impairment; and (2) conditions unrelated to stroke that severely limited mobility, such as paralysis due to fractures or spinal cord injury.

Data collection

The study was conducted following approval from the institutional ethics committee. Trained researchers distributed questionnaires, provided participants with information about the study, emphasised voluntary participation and withdrawal rights, and obtained written informed consent. Researchers were available on-site to clarify questions and assist participants in completing the questionnaires, which took approximately 20–30 min each. Completed questionnaires were reviewed immediately to ensure completeness. For participants with lower educational levels, researchers assisted in questionnaire completion through face-to-face interviews, with the cooperation of caregivers when necessary.

Assessment tools

Demographic and clinical information was collected using a structured questionnaire (full details in Supplementary Table S1). Participants also completed validated questionnaires assessing physical function, psychological status, and illness adaptation, including the Tampa Scale of Kinesiophobia (TSK, > 37 indicating presence of kinesiophobia) [16], Tilburg Frailty Indicator (TFI) [25], Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) [26], Self-Efficacy for Exercise (SEE) [27], and Acceptance of Illness Scale (AIS) [28]. Detailed scoring rules and psychometric properties of all questionnaires are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0. Descriptive statistics were conducted as follows: the Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess normality. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed variables were expressed as median and interquartile range. Categorical variables were summarised using counts (n) and percentages (%). Group differences between participants with and without kinesiophobia were examined using appropriate tests. Normally distributed continuous variables with homogenous variances were compared using independent-samples t-tests, whereas variables with non-normal distributions or unequal variances were analysed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was applied to explore associations between kinesiophobia levels and other scale scores. To identify factors independently associated with kinesiophobia, variables were first assessed for multicollinearity using variance inflation factors (VIF), and then a binary logistic regression model was constructed. All tests were two-sided, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Qualitative research

Sample selection

To obtain a comprehensive understanding of kinesiophobia among older non-acute stroke patients, participants were purposively selected between July and September 2025 following the principle of maximum variation. Eligible participants had completed the quantitative phase and scored > 37 on the TSK. The sample aimed to explore experiences and influencing factors rather than support causal inferences. After the fourteenth interview, no substantially new codes were identified. Following discussion within the research team, thematic saturation for participants with kinesiophobia was confirmed after sixteen interviews, as no new core concepts or themes emerged.

After thematic saturation had been reached, preliminary quantitative analysis indicated that age ≥ 81 years was associated with a lower likelihood of kinesiophobia. To explore this unexpected finding, two additional participants aged ≥ 81 years with TSK ≤ 37 were purposively recruited as contrastive explanatory cases. These participants were not included to generate new themes but to help interpret why some oldest-old patients did not report kinesiophobia. Their interviews were analysed using the same coding procedures as the initial sixteen participants and compared with existing codes and themes to identify convergent and divergent patterns.

Data collection

All interviews were conducted independently by the first author, a graduate student trained in qualitative research methods and interview techniques, who had no direct clinical or professional relationship with the participants, ensuring consistency and minimising potential bias. The research team developed a semi-structured interview guide based on the study objectives and relevant literature. Its feasibility was tested through pilot interviews with three participants, and multiple revisions were made according to feedback, resulting in the final version (Supplementary Table S3). During interviews, the researcher used probing and clarifying questions, such as asking whether fear was persistent or situation-specific, to gain a deeper understanding of participants’ experiences. Questions were open-ended and broad, allowing participants to narrate freely while the researcher guided the conversation when necessary to encourage full and meaningful responses. Interviews took place in quiet outpatient offices and were scheduled according to participants’ convenience. Each interview lasted approximately 30 min. After obtaining informed consent, all interviews were audio-recorded and supplemented with detailed field notes capturing nonverbal behaviours and emotional responses to provide richer contextual information. To quantify the impact of specific factors contributing to kinesiophobia, such as muscle weakness or prior falls, participants were asked to rate each factor on a scale from 1 to 10, with higher scores indicating a greater influence.

Data analysis

Qualitative data were analysed using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase thematic analysis with a theory-informed hybrid inductive–deductive approach [29]. All audio recordings were transcribed verbatim, checked against the original recordings for accuracy, and anonymised before analysis. The first author repeatedly read the transcripts to become familiar with the data, recorded preliminary analytic notes, and conducted the initial coding of all transcripts. Initial coding was primarily inductive, focusing on participants’ descriptions of kinesiophobia, perceived exercise-related risk, rehabilitation experiences, family interactions, bodily changes, and avoidance behaviours.

To enhance analytic credibility, a second researcher independently reviewed the transcripts and initial coding results. Coding discrepancies and alternative interpretations were discussed by the two researchers and further reviewed in research team meetings until consensus was reached. Similar codes were grouped into candidate subthemes through repeated comparison within and across transcripts. Life-course theory [21] was used as a sensitising and organising framework during theme development and interpretation rather than as a rigid coding matrix. Specifically, concepts including historical time and place, linked lives, timing in lives, and human agency informed the organisation of empirically derived subthemes into higher-order themes when supported by participants’ accounts. The research team refined theme boundaries, clarified the relationships between themes and subthemes, and selected representative quotations to support the final interpretation.

Integration

Joint displays are a commonly used data integration strategy in mixed-methods research, enabling the alignment or integration of different types of findings within a single visual format to connect quantitative and qualitative results. This approach facilitates the development of novel interpretive perspectives and offers a more comprehensive understanding than analyses based on a single method alone [30]. In this study, quantitative and qualitative data and their findings were presented simultaneously in graphical (Fig. 2) and tabular (Table 5) form to support integrative analysis.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Wuxi People’s Hospital (Approval No. KY23122). Wuxi People’s Hospital served as the study site and was responsible for ethical review, participant recruitment, informed consent procedures, data collection, and data management. The Affiliated Children’s Hospital of Jiangnan University contributed to study design, research supervision, data analysis and interpretation, and manuscript preparation. All participants provided written informed consent before enrolment. Participation was entirely voluntary, and participants could withdraw at any time without any adverse consequences. Participant privacy and anonymity were strictly protected, and all collected data were kept confidential and not disclosed to unauthorized third parties.

Results

Quantitative results

Characteristics of older patients after stroke

A total of 319 questionnaires were distributed. Twelve questionnaires were excluded because they showed patterned responses, suggesting poor response quality. Finally, 307 valid questionnaires were included in the analysis. Before analysis, all questionnaires were checked for completeness and response quality. No missing values were identified in the final dataset of 307 valid questionnaires; therefore, complete-case analysis was performed using the final valid dataset, and no imputation was conducted. The prevalence of kinesiophobia was 64.8% (199/307). There were 194 male participants (63.2%) and 113 female participants (36.8%), with ages ranging from 60 to 85 years (mean ± SD: 71.30 ± 7.40 years). Regarding education, 243 participants (79.2%) had completed junior high school or below, and 64 participants (20.8%) had completed senior high school or higher. Approximately 56.4% of participants were cared for by their spouse. The majority of patients had ischemic stroke (91.5%). Muscle weakness was present in 60.3% of participants, and 56.7% reported fear of falling. Additional demographic and clinical characteristics are detailed in Table 1.

Binary logistic regression analysis of kinesiophobia

Kinesiophobia status was used as the dependent variable, and variables that were statistically significant (p < 0.05) in univariate and correlation analyses were included as independent variables in a binary logistic regression model. All candidate variables were entered simultaneously into the model using the enter method. No stepwise procedure was applied, and no post hoc variable removal was performed. Scale scores were entered as continuous variables, and categorical variables were coded as follows: age 60–70 years served as the reference category, with 71–80 years and ≥ 81 years coded as dummy variables; monthly household income < 5,000 RMB was the reference; absence of pre-stroke exercise habit was the reference; normal muscle strength was the reference category for muscle strength; and absence of fear of falling was the reference category for fear of falling. Multicollinearity was assessed among all independent variables entered into the logistic regression model using variance inflation factors. The VIF values ranged from 1.078 to 4.108, suggesting no evidence of serious multicollinearity. The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test yielded a χ² of 3.915 and p = 0.865, indicating an adequate model fit. The logistic regression results indicated that abnormal muscle strength, fear of falling, frailty, and anxiety were associated with a higher likelihood of kinesiophobia, whereas age ≥ 81 years, higher exercise self-efficacy, and greater illness acceptance were associated with a lower likelihood of kinesiophobia, as shown in Table 3.

Qualitative results

General information on patients interviewed

In total, eighteen participants were interviewed. Two contrastive cases were included as exploratory and hypothesis-generating cases to help contextualize the theme “Timing in Lives,” particularly regarding possible age-related adaptation, recalibrated rehabilitation expectations, acceptance of functional limitations, care dependency, and potential differences in reporting kinesiophobia. Demographic details are provided in Supplementary Table S4. Four major themes and nine subthemes were identified: historical time and place, linked lives, timing in lives, and human agency (Table 4). Participants were coded P1–P18. Major themes and sub-themes

Historical time and place

Within the theme of historical time and place, three subthemes emerged, suggesting that kinesiophobia among older patients after stroke may be influenced by the social contexts and health beliefs formed early in life. Nearly all participants grew up in environments marked by material scarcity and limited educational opportunities, where physical activity was primarily labour-oriented and tied to daily survival. Exercise was equated with practical, functional tasks. One participant recalled, “Exercise? We never had such a concept as children, we just went to the fields to work” (P5). Encountering modern rehabilitation exercises focused on functional recovery often elicited feelings of unfamiliarity or resistance. Another participant said, “Why should I exercise deliberately? Sweeping the floor at home is no different” (P10). Traditional cultural beliefs emphasizing rest and preservation of energy further shaped attitudes toward exercise. Some participants viewed physical activity as appropriate mainly for younger people and regarded later life as a stage for conserving physical and mental energy. Exercise was perceived as a potential depletion of already limited physiological capacity rather than a necessary component of rehabilitation. One participant explained, “Young people need to exercise. I am grateful my condition is stable now; I dare not push my body any further” (P1). Environmental barriers also contributed to kinesiophobia. High-density housing, ageing buildings, lack of elevators, limited community accessibility, weather conditions, and uneven rural roads all undermined participants’ sense of safety during movement or exercise. One participant noted, “We live in an old building on the third floor without an elevator. I used to manage the stairs, but now my spouse has to help me down. It feels very dangerous” (P4).

Linked lives

Kinesiophobia was also shaped by social and interpersonal factors, divided into three subthemes. First, older patients often considered intergenerational responsibilities when making decisions about exercise, reflecting altruistic tendencies. Several participants linked their own health to their children’s quality of life, expressing concern that reinjury could lead to rehospitalisation and increase caregiving burdens. P15 said, “I hardly go out to exercise because I fear injury, hospital visits cost money, and my children would have to accompany me. It would disrupt their work.” Family-centred values further influenced caregiving behaviours in Chinese culture. Younger family members typically regard elder care as an unquestionable duty, and a second injury, such as a fall or overexertion, could conflict with cultural expectations of filial piety. As P5 recalled, “After I fell ill, my daughter-in-law would not let me do any housework. She only allowed me to walk around indoors. She said if I fell, people would say they did not take proper care of me, that they were unfilial.” Broader social networks, including neighbors, fellow patients, and online communities, also transmitted risk signals. Misleading information online often heightened concerns about physiological vulnerability. One participant explained, “The internet says strokes are caused by fragile blood vessels and that exertion can trigger bleeding again. I dare not move much. I am afraid even a little effort could rupture my vessels” (P1).

Timing in lives

Kinesiophobia may also be influenced by the life stage at which the stroke occurs. In this exploratory comparison, patients under 81 years old appeared to describe stronger kinesiophobia-related concerns and greater challenges in regaining daily functioning. Two illustrative cases are presented to contextualize this possible pattern, one aged 61 and the other 83. P11 (61 years old) said, “I had just retired and planned to help take care of my grandchildren. Then I suffered a cerebral hemorrhage. I heard that regular exercise would benefit my leg, and I was willing to do rehabilitation. I still want to live a few more years. But sometimes it is not that I do not want to exercise; my leg just feels unsteady when I stand.” In contrast, P18 (83 years old) reflected, “At my age, I take life day by day. I do not need to exercise. If I do not move, I do not feel afraid. My children provide for my daily needs; all I have to do is enjoy life.” These exploratory accounts suggest that role expectations and future orientation shaped by different life stages may influence how patients weigh rehabilitation benefits against exercise risks; however, this interpretation should be considered hypothesis-generating given the small number of contrastive cases.

Human agency

After a stroke, patients reported engaging in defensive avoidance strategies. They described their bodies as uncontrollable. P6 explained, “My body does not respond the way it used to.” Chronic pain and sleep disturbances further drain physical and psychological resources, leading patients to view exercise as high effort with low return: “My leg already hurts, walking a few more steps makes it worse, and I did not sleep well all night because of the pain” (P7). Concerns about exercise safety are also prominent. Patients who experienced falls leading to rehospitalisation tend to maintain heightened vigilance, reducing activity to preserve safety: “I fell at home once and went to the emergency room at night. Luckily, I did not bleed again. I feel safer if I do not exercise” (P2).

Some participants described using maladaptive coping strategies and cognitive appraisal processes closely linked to kinesiophobia. Patients reported feeling internalised anxiety and were highly sensitive to minor bodily changes, which increased avoidance behaviour: “I was walking outside and my heart started beating fast. I immediately went home, afraid something might go wrong if I continued” (P3). Loss of function can trigger shame and self-deprecation. P7 reflected, “For me, exercise is no longer rehabilitation; it is the way others look at me, as if saying ‘you are disabled.’” Negative cognitive processing also plays a role. Even when patients recognise that others may be worse off, some interpret this not as reassurance but as evidence of their own fragility. P13 described, “During a follow-up at the hospital, I saw others who were sicker than me. I did not feel lucky; it made me more afraid. I thought, I can still stand now, but what if I fall while exercising and end up like them?”

Data integration

Figure 2 and Table 5 present the integration of quantitative findings with qualitative life-course themes. The qualitative findings helped interpret statistically significant factors associated with kinesiophobia: the observed association between age ≥ 81 years and lower likelihood of kinesiophobia was explored through the theme of timing in lives; fear of falling was interpreted across historical time and place, linked lives, and human agency; muscle weakness and frailty mainly through human agency; anxiety through linked lives and human agency; and exercise self-efficacy and illness acceptance through human agency. In addition, qualitative data extended the quantitative results by revealing contextual and life-course influences not directly captured by the surveys, such as labour-oriented understandings of activity, traditional ageing beliefs, intergenerational responsibility, environmental barriers, social risk information, life-stage expectations, and internalised stigma. Overall, kinesiophobia was shaped by interactions among physical function, frailty, psychological appraisal, family relationships, environmental context, and life-stage adaptation.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is among the few mixed-methods investigations to examine kinesiophobia among older patients after stroke from a life-course perspective. By integrating quantitative and qualitative evidence, we found that kinesiophobia in this population is not merely a reaction to post-stroke physical impairments, but is embedded within long-standing health beliefs, interpersonal and family relationships, role expectations across life stages, and the coping strategies adopted under physical limitations. In other words, kinesiophobia reflects both a response to current bodily status and the cumulative influence of past experiences and present circumstances. This holistic understanding suggests that fully appreciating its formation and its impact on rehabilitation behaviour requires situating kinesiophobia within broader life and social contexts.

Historical time and place

The life courses of older Chinese adults have been shaped by distinct historical contexts. Many experienced war, famine, and severe educational scarcity during childhood and youth. Specific events in the twentieth century profoundly altered their trajectories; for example, the “sent-down” movement relocated large numbers of urban youth to rural areas for agricultural work [31]. Previous research indicates that childhood participation in physical activity strongly predicts health behaviours in adulthood, and resource deprivation often limited opportunities for organised sports among disadvantaged children [32]. This may have fostered a perception that physical labour is equivalent to exercise, thereby reducing the acceptability of rehabilitation interventions among older patients after stroke, a finding consistent with recent evidence [33].

Cultural beliefs, including values, health perceptions, and lifestyle, further shape engagement in physical activity among older adults with chronic conditions [34]. Individuals’ understanding of health and ageing may influence their attitudes toward exercise and its incorporation into daily life [34]. In the context of culturally emphasised age-related frailty and the principle of “rest to preserve energy,” older adults may more readily perceive exercise as appropriate for younger individuals while framing late-life health maintenance as minimising activity and conserving energy.

Environmental factors also play a critical role. Unlike in many Western settings, higher residential density in China often correlates negatively with physical activity [35]. While Western studies suggest that dense neighborhoods with good street connectivity promote activity, China’s large population, rapid urban expansion, and limited land resources have led to urban planning that emphasises compact living, often at the expense of open spaces and exercise facilities, thereby constraining opportunities for physical activity [35]. Social participation and outdoor activity have been shown to enhance well-being and the sense of normalcy in stroke survivors [36]. However, environmental barriers may trigger avoidance even before activity is initiated. For example, older patients living in older buildings without elevators may perceive leaving home as inherently risky because of fall-related concerns, a finding consistent with our quantitative results. Additionally, although empirical research on local sports facility accessibility is limited, evidence from other Chinese cities indicates that cultural facilities are primarily publicly funded, whereas sports facilities are largely market-driven and require payment [37]. Considering economic constraints, older people after stroke may favour cultural public spaces over sports facilities; however, as participants noted, truly accessible and free community facilities remain limited.

Linked lives

Another culturally salient factor associated with kinesiophobia among older patients after stroke is concern about becoming a burden to others. Chinese culture is grounded in collectivist values that prioritise others’ needs, while excessive attention to one’s own needs is often viewed as inappropriate [38]. Our findings align with recent studies showing that patients may place family well-being above their own health [39]. Related research indicates that patients are highly attuned to caregivers’ behavioural responses, emotional expressions, and interaction patterns, through which they perceive caregiving burden. This perception is frequently accompanied by anxiety, consistent with our quantitative findings, as well as feelings of guilt, which may undermine psychological adjustment and negatively affect rehabilitation outcomes [38].

At the same time, the Confucian value system, particularly norms of filial piety, frames caring for older family members as a nonnegotiable moral obligation [38]. Events that could lead to secondary injury, such as falls or excessive activity, may be interpreted as failures in family caregiving and threaten moral evaluation and social reputation. This concern may prompt family members to adopt overly cautious or restrictive care strategies to avoid being labelled unfilial. Family harmony is often prioritised, and patients tend to defer to family judgments in health-related decisions while avoiding choices that could provoke conflict [38]. Such risk-averse approaches may conflict with contemporary rehabilitation principles that emphasise early mobilisation and gradual functional training. This underscores that education directed at family members may remain insufficient.

Broader social networks also appear to play an important role in the development of kinesiophobia. The internet has become a primary source of health information for the public. Although online platforms, including websites, blogs, and social media, facilitate the dissemination of health knowledge, the quality of information varies widely, with a substantial presence of inaccurate or misleading content. Compared with other health domains, topics related to physical activity are particularly vulnerable to misinformation, especially regarding safety and health benefits [40]. When patients encounter risk messages through social interactions or online sources that directly link physical activity to recurrent cerebral hemorrhage, fear is likely to emerge naturally.

Timing in lives

Previous studies have suggested that advanced age may be significantly associated with the presence of kinesiophobia (OR = 1.051, p = 0.036) [41]. Interestingly, in our quantitative analysis, adults aged 81 years or older may be less likely to report kinesiophobia compared with those aged 60–70 years (OR = 0.115, p = 0.005). Exploratory observations from two contrastive cases suggest that some patients aged 81 years and older may recalibrate rehabilitation expectations, accept age-related functional decline, rely more on family care, place less emphasis on full functional recovery, or report kinesiophobia differently. This pattern aligns with response shift theory, which proposes that individuals adapt psychologically to health changes by adjusting internal standards, values, and their conceptualisation of quality of life [42]. For example, patients aged 81 years and older may shift priorities from independence toward acceptance of care and redefine rehabilitation success from full functional restoration to maintaining quality of life. Notably, these interpretations are tentative and based on limited data, and the evidence is insufficient to establish causal relationships. Thus, rather than suggesting that advanced age itself causally protects against kinesiophobia, the observed association of age ≥ 81 years with lower reported kinesiophobia may reflect adaptive expectations, acceptance of functional decline, care dependency, or possible differences in reporting kinesiophobia. This exploratory interpretation requires confirmation in larger and preferably longitudinal studies. For adults aged 60–70 years with kinesiophobia, future assessment-informed strategies could consider supporting adaptive response shifts to help align functional expectations with current capabilities. For patients aged 81 years and older, it may be appropriate to encourage safe and appropriately graded participation in rehabilitation while supporting psychological well-being, without suggesting that chronological age itself confers protection.

Human agency

Physical symptoms appear to be a central driver of kinesiophobia. We found that patients with impaired muscle strength were 2.793 times more likely to experience kinesiophobia than those without such impairment (p = 0.045). Participants frequently described their bodies as “uncontrollable,” and when individuals are unable to predict the outcomes of movement, they may reduce activity to avoid perceived risks, consistent with previous findings [43]. Pain and sleep disturbances further deplete psychological and physical resources over time. According to cognitive behavioural models of kinesiophobia or fear of reinjury, concern about pain arises from anticipatory beliefs that physical activity may exacerbate pain or trigger recurrent injury [44]. Patients tend to adopt one of two coping patterns. Some confront pain, viewing it as manageable and insufficient to substantially restrict daily function, while others form a direct association between movement and pain, leading to avoidance and persistent kinesiophobia [44]. Patients often remain unaware of their own catastrophic interpretations of pain, overestimating its severity and risk, which fosters kinesiophobia. Our findings further suggest that adverse experiences during activity, such as pain exacerbation, may reinforce negative expectations about exercise by creating a perception of disproportionate effort relative to benefit.

Fear of falling emerged repeatedly in participants’ narratives. A multicentre cross-sectional survey reported that the risk of injurious falls among older patients after stroke reached 74.76% [45]. Falls can result in fractures, traumatic brain injury, and soft tissue damage, undermining rehabilitation progress. Saika Aihara et al. [46] proposed that fall experiences prompt patients to reconstruct their perceptions of bodily function and environmental safety while adapting to post-stroke changes. Conversely, some individuals develop anticipatory anxiety about falling again. Our results suggest that fear of falling was strongly associated with kinesiophobia, with an odds ratio of 5.056 (p = 0.01). Consistent with findings by Ewelina Bąk et al. [19], frailty symptoms in older patients after stroke, including self-reported fatigue and reduced physical capacity, may interact with stroke-related impairments to promote the development of kinesiophobia. From a complementary perspective, Fatma Zehra Genç et al. [47] showed that when frailty was treated as the dependent variable, each one-point increase in TSK score was associated with a 1.128-fold higher risk of frailty (p < 0.001). These findings suggest a potential reciprocal cycle among older patients after stroke, in which frailty may intensify kinesiophobia, while kinesiophobia may restrict activity and rehabilitation participation, thereby further exacerbating frailty.

Regarding psychological coping patterns and cognitive appraisal, several key aspects warrant attention. For some patients, confronting the inevitable consequences of illness is itself a formidable challenge, which leaves them dominated by illness experiences during attempts to coexist with the condition and limits their capacity to move forward. In this study, greater illness acceptance was associated with a lower likelihood of kinesiophobia (OR = 0.879, p = 0.009). This finding suggests that healthcare professionals should support patients during rehabilitation in reconstructing self-perception, encouraging acceptance of objective functional limitations, adjustment of existing cognitive frameworks, and the gradual development of a more stable and positive sense of self. Ewelina Bąk et al. reported a strong association between kinesiophobia and anxiety, noting that without effective intervention, heightened anxiety may adversely affect rehabilitation decisions and adherence [19]. We concur with this view and further emphasise that fostering hope and optimism constitutes a vital psychological resource for coping with severe illness, helping to buffer the long-term burden imposed by disease. Consistent with previous research [18], exercise self-efficacy was closely associated with kinesiophobia. Exercise self-efficacy reflects an individual’s belief in their capacity to perform physical activity and achieve exercise goals, serving as a core psychological driver of exercise initiation and maintenance. Patients with low self-efficacy often lack confidence in exercise outcomes and safety, which may increase vulnerability to kinesiophobia. Stigmatisation is also common among older people after stroke [48]. Some participants acknowledged that concern about being viewed differently led them to avoid exercise or limit outdoor activities. It is crucial for individuals to recognise themselves as whole persons who are not defined solely by bodily condition or diagnostic labels, as self-awareness plays a pivotal role in buffering and alleviating the negative effects of internalised stigma in stroke survivors [48]. Sharing personal experiences with others may further facilitate open and authentic self-expression. For example, Zhou et al. [43] found that among stroke patients with motor impairment, appropriate downward comparison, such as recognising one’s own survival and attending to peers with more severe conditions, could alleviate negative emotions and strengthen confidence in coping with adversity. We would add, however, that downward comparison does not invariably yield positive effects and may instead position others as references for potential deterioration, thereby reinforcing perceptions of bodily vulnerability and exercise-related risk.

Implications for clinical practice

The findings of this study may provide practical implications for rehabilitation nursing in older patients after stroke and underscore the need to systematically identify and manage kinesiophobia across nursing assessment, intervention, communication, and environmental support [15, 18]. During the assessment phase, nursing evaluations should extend beyond isolated measures of physical function and conceptualize kinesiophobia as a multidimensional phenomenon. In addition to routine assessments of motor function, balance, and fall risk, attention may be given to frailty status, anxiety levels, exercise self-efficacy, illness acceptance, prior life experiences, patients’ understanding of physical activity, and perceptions of exercise-related risk and avoidance behaviours, particularly fear of falling, to enable early identification of individuals who may be at risk [15, 49, 50].

In future nursing management, individualised and stratified strategies could be considered based on age, functional capacity, and adaptive level. For younger older adults, exercise intensity could be gradually increased under appropriate safety supervision, with emphasis on strengthening exercise self-efficacy [51]. For patients aged 81 years and older or those with functional limitations, low-risk and progressive activities could be prioritised, combined with psychological support and cognitive behavioural strategies to potentially facilitate realistic adjustment of functional expectations and support safe rehabilitation engagement. This recommendation should be interpreted cautiously, given the exploratory nature of the observed association between age ≥ 81 years and lower reported kinesiophobia. In nursing communication and health education, it is essential to address the influence of social comparison, family caregiving patterns, and cultural beliefs on exercise-related decision making. Scientific education and individualised guidance may be used to reinforce patients’ confidence in physical activity, while family members could be educated to provide appropriate support and avoid excessive protection that may restrict rehabilitation participation. From the perspective of nursing management and environmental support, creating a safe, controllable, and supportive rehabilitation environment is critical. During hospitalisation and at discharge, consistent and clear exercise guidance may be provided alongside individualised rehabilitation plans [52]. During follow-up, activity engagement and psychological status could be monitored through telephone contact, community outreach, or home-based follow-up, with timely adjustment of rehabilitation strategies. At the community level, integration of community rehabilitation resources and social activities may support patients in gradually adapting to daily physical activity and has the potential to inform sustained rehabilitation engagement and quality-of-life support.

Limitations

This study has several unavoidable limitations. First, the quantitative component employed a cross-sectional design, which limits the ability to establish causal relationships between factors associated with kinesiophobia and outcome variables. Second, participants were recruited from a single medical institution, resulting in a relatively homogeneous sample that may restrict the generalisability of the findings. Future studies should incorporate more heterogeneous populations across multiple centres to enhance external validity. Third, due to study design considerations, patients with significant cognitive or communication impairments were excluded, which may limit the applicability of the results to the broader stroke population. Fourth, although a mixed-methods approach was used to deepen understanding of the research questions, the quantitative component relied primarily on self-reported measures and may therefore be subject to common method bias. Finally, several potentially important factors, such as community environmental accessibility and individuals’ prior life experiences, were not systematically included in the quantitative analysis. Future research using longitudinal or multicentre designs, together with more comprehensive and integrated measurement tools, is warranted to further clarify how multilevel factors influence kinesiophobia among older patients after stroke.

Conclusion

By integrating quantitative and qualitative data within a life-course analytical framework, this study examined factors associated with kinesiophobia among older patients after stroke. Early-life socioeconomic conditions, culturally embedded beliefs developed over time, and critical life transitions may shape attitudes toward physical activity and exercise behaviour in later life. At the same time, limited family support, restricted access to social resources, and insufficient policy protection may intensify kinesiophobia. These findings may provide the necessary contextual basis for developing future assessment strategies and interventions for kinesiophobia among older patients after stroke.
