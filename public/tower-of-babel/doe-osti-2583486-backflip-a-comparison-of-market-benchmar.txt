Preface
As the photovoltaic (PV) industry has rapidly expanded across the world, there has been a
growing interest in increasing the lifespan and reliability of PV modules. Relative to that, recent
concern has emerged regarding the recyclability of modules and their component materials,
including fluoropolymer-based backsheets. Laminated polyethylene terephthalate (PET) core
backsheets have traditionally been used in the PV industry, but new, coextruded polyolefin
backsheets show promise as an improved alternative. To better understand their performance in
the field, mini-module (MiMo) and coupon samples of seven different backsheets—including
contemporary PET, novel polyolefin, and other backsheets, were run through hygrometric- and
photolytic-accelerated weathering to identify, quantify, and better understand each material’s
degradation modes. This report shares the key findings from the first five completed experiments
of the cumulative 4000-hour study. After 85°C/85% relative humidity (“Damp Heat”)
accelerated testing, degradation of the PV module’s electrical interconnects was observed
through electroluminescence imaging and current-voltage (I-V) characterization of the MiMos
containing PET core layers. Micro-cracking was observed on the APO and AAA backsheets on
the MiMos used in IEC TS 62788-7-2 UV weathering (method A3) as well as on the PO-2 and
PPE backsheets on the MiMos used in Damp Heat (85°C/85% relative humidity) testing. Micro-
cracking is of concern because it can lead to critical failures such as macro-cracking and
delamination, compromising the electrical insulation. Changes in surface roughness (deduced
from gloss measurements), mechanical characteristics (tensile test), electrical insulation
(breakdown voltage), and polymer chemical structure at the surface are also described in this
report.

1 Introduction
Solar photovoltaic (PV) devices are the fastest-growing energy sources in the world, and they are
critical to the world energy economy’s greater incorporation of renewable energy sources. Due to
solar cells’ accessibility, cost effectiveness, and flexible use, solar panel installations reached a
record capacity of greater than 120 GW in 2020, with that number projected to grow
exponentially in subsequent years [8], [9], [10]. The rapid installation of new solar energy
systems has created a growing interest in increasing the lifetime and reliability of solar power
systems relative to initial cost effectiveness and efficiency. Relative to that, there is currently no
widespread system, such as recycling or dismantling, to address the end of life of a solar panel
[11].

PV modules are designed as multilayered composites consisting of glass, semiconductors,
polymers (encapsulant and backsheet), and metal components. Polymeric backsheets play a
critical role in the overall performance and lifespan of current PV module designs. Backsheets
serve as a packaging material, protecting the electrical components of the module and the
attending personnel against electrical shock and fire ignition. Thus, the backsheet is designed to
be resilient in a variety of meteorological climates. Polyethylene-based backsheet materials,
including a polyethylene-terephthalate (PET) core, have been widely used in the PV industry for
decades; such backsheets, however, have been shown to have some vulnerability to
embrittlement and delamination, compromising essential external Relied Upon Insulation in the
PV module [12]. This exposure presents an immediate safety concern to workers maintaining
these systems, as well as causing losses in performance (due to tripped inverters, corrosion, and
other degradation) [13]. Recent research into polyolefin-based backsheets has shown promising
robustness relative to present polyethylene-based backsheet materials, suggesting that they may
be used to achieve sufficient lifespan and PV module reliability [14]. Furthermore, polyolefins
are more recyclable [15] and can be formulated to be environmentally robust, such that no
fluoropolymer outer layer (which is difficult to recycle) is needed.

This study seeks to investigate these new polyolefin-based backsheets while comparing their
long-term durability to existing industry benchmark backsheets. This comprehensive study
examines coupons and mini-module (MiMo) specimens through 4000 cumulative hours of
accelerated testing. Photolytic and hygrometric accelerated testing were used to identify,
quantify, and better understand the different degradation modes of polymeric backsheets.
Ultraviolet (UV) accelerated testing, including the IEC TS 62788-7-2 (methods A2 and A3), as
well as custom methods (including with and without water spray), are examined in this study.
Hygrometric weathering, including the conditions of 45°C/85% relative humidity (RH),
65°C/85% RH, and 85°C/85% RH (the popular “Damp Heat” test [16]), are also examined in this
study. UV testing examines the UV resistance/response of exposed materials, whereas
hygrometric testing indicates how the backsheet responds to degradation driven by heat,
humidity, and even water ingress [17]. It is hoped that the decoupling of photo- and hygro-
degradation will allow for greater insight into the benefits and shortcomings of each backsheet
while also providing insights into the mechanical, electrical, and chemical behavior of the
backsheets relative to a PV module.

2 Background
Polymers degrade via various physical and chemical pathways. Common degradation modes for
backsheet materials include UV photodegradation and hygrometric aging. The typical terrestrial
UV solar spectrum (280–400 nm) contains photons that exist at a greater energy than the bonds
that are found in hydrocarbon polymers, particularly C-C bonds [18], [19]. This means that UV
light can interact with these chemical bonds, contributing energy that breaks the bonds. This
leads to an evolution of the overall structure of the polymer over time through cleavage, cross-
linking, and the formation of new compounds/functional groups. Photochemical degradation is a
common degradation mode in polymers that can be mitigated by adding UV absorbers and UV
light stabilizers to the material. Humidity and heat can accelerate photodegradation, yet these
parameters often separately degrade backsheet materials in the dark. The PET core materials
traditionally used in backsheet manufacturing are especially sensitive to hydrolysis via de-
esterification [20], [21], where water reacts with the hydrolysable backbones of the polymer to
cause chemical and mechanical degradation (embrittlement and cracking) through random
scission [22]. Early studies of polyolefin backsheets have observed that they are more resistant to
UV degradation and hydrolysis than current polyethylene backsheets [14].

3 Methodology
3.1 Samples and Sample Manufacturing
217 polymer sheet coupons and 161 MiMos were manufactured using seven different solar
backsheets (Table 1). The backsheets for the study included: three industry benchmarks (TPT,
PPE, and KPf); one known-bad backsheet (AAA); and three novel polyolefin backsheets (PO-1,
PO-2, and APO). The novel backsheets included materials manufactured by DSM (now
Endurans Solar Solutions) [12]. The thickness of the backsheets is given in Table 1, including
the average and the variation for two standard deviations (S.D.), based on six micrometer
measurements (MG8206, General Tools Manufacturing Co.) of a representative piece of each
backsheet. The MiMos were manufactured using a non-tempered float glass (Planibel
Clearvision, AGC Inc.) with no antireflective coating. A single 156 mm, Si-Cz, p-PERC solar
cell (previously diced into four equal pieces) was connected with ribbon to an edge-mounted

 3.4 Verification of Specimen Temperature
 The specimen temperature was verified in situ because the Xe lamp results in radiative heating to
 temperatures greater than the chamber temperature during UV weathering. Based on the method
 developed for a round-robin experiment for the IEC TS 62788-7-2 standard, the key equipment
 and configuration for temperature verification are represented in Figure 3. One coupon and one
 MiMo were equipped with a 30 AWG T-type thermocouple (5SRTC-TT-T-30-12, Omega
 Engineering Inc.); the temperature was monitored using a wireless logger (MWTC-D-T-915,
 Omega Engineering Inc., Figure 3(a)) located inside a custom protective plastic housing (Figure
 3(c)). Data were acquired from a receiver unit (MWTC-REC1-915, Omega Engineering Inc.,

 6

Figure 3(a)). As shown in Figure 3(b), the thermocouple was dabbed with a thermal joint
compound, TIM-517 (Timtronics LLC), and attached to the chamber-wall-facing surface of each
specimen using Kapton polyimide tape (DuPont de Nemours Inc). Most of the latter experiments
were performed with a thermocouple in the specimen center and a separate thermocouple in the
specimen corner. The chamber was run for 90 minutes to allow temperatures to completely
stabilize. Thermocouple data was acquired at 1 Hz in °F then converted to °C to give better
temperature resolution. Chamber operating conditions—acquired every 1 minute—were taken
from the chamber’s electronic operating log. These included: irradiance (at 340 nm and 300 nm
≤ λ ≤ 400 nm); temperature (chamber, black panel, and white panel); and humidity (chamber).
The average of the final 1000 seconds was used for temperature assessment.

Regarding Figure 4, the temperature history indicates that the chamber and specimen stabilized
prior to the analysis of temperature, i.e., during the final 1000 seconds. The higher temperature
and greater variation for the coupon likely result from its geometry and ability to interact with its
surroundings, i.e., its lesser heat capacity and perhaps a greater convective heat transfer
coefficient. The magnitude of variation is greater for the BPT (greater absorptance and resulting
temperature rise from the ChT) than the WPT. The periodic variation in temperature likely
results from the data sampling rate relative to the rate of rotation of the carousel. The profile
observed for the BPT resembles aliasing sampling error for the chamber measurements (recorded
every minute) relative to the specimen temperature (measured every second). Physical vibration
or deformation of the specimens in the chamber (greatest for the compliant coupons) may have
also contributed to specimen temperature fluctuation. Increasing the carousel speed from 1 rpm
to 10 rpm eliminated the specimen temperature oscillations. It is unclear whether the periodic
temperature variation associated with slow carousel rotation could contribute to a mechanical
fatigue or degradation mechanism for polymer specimens, e.g., if specimens are aged at a
temperature close to a phase transition temperature.

4 Characterization Methods
4.1 I-V (“Flash”) Testing
A 5600 Solar Simulator (EternalSun Spire) was used to flash test MiMo samples for electrical
characterization. The precision of the measurements (2 S.D.) for the modules was on the order of
±2.5% for short-circuit current (Isc), ±0.5% for open-circuit voltage (Voc), ±2.5% for maximum
power (Pmax), ±1.0% for fill factor (FF), and unspecified for aperture efficiency (which was not
precisely defined based on the actual size of the quarter-cells and the accuracy of their placement
within the MiMo). Tests were conducted on specimens maintained at 25°C ± 0.5°C at a light
intensity of 1000 W·m-2 across a spectrum of 300–1300 nm. Data profiles were corrected to the
representative measured quantum efficiency of the cells. Dark I-V measurements were obtained
for the unaged samples and at the final read point (4000 h) for all experiments. In addition, all
MiMos were measured with a hard mask at 1000 W·m-2 at 4000 h to evaluate the effect of the
packaging degradation (including any discoloration of the backsheet). For this measurement, the
additional area of the MiMo was reduced to just beyond the periphery of the cell by the mask
aperture. Two unaged MiMos of each type of backsheet (except BS-7, for which there was only
one) were also measured using the hard mask, so that change in performance could be
determined for the aged MiMos for each backsheet. A black high-density polyethylene mask was
made for measurements after initial cursory examination with a soft mask (black packing
material, used to separate the coupons during shipping). A 13% loss of power was initially
observed at NREL for the soft mask on an unaged quarter-cell MiMo, while a 3% loss of power
was initially observed at SNL for a hard mask on an unaged four-cell MiMo. It was decided to
perform I-V read points through the experiments at NREL using unmasked MiMos to emphasize
any change in the backsheet in addition to any change in cell performance. The loss of power of
19.5% (average for all seven backsheets) was observed using the hard mask for the backsheets
from the unaged quarter-cell MiMos at NREL.

4.2 Electroluminescent (EL) Imagining
EL imaging was conducted on all MiMos using a Proline IR Camera (Finger Lakes
Instrumentation) and an N5768A DS power supply (Agilent Technologies Inc). A Si detector
with a 950 nm high-pass filter (Digital High Definition Brands Co.) filter captured images from
~950 nm to 1150 nm. An external current of 0.6 A [~60% of the short-circuit current (Isc) for
unaged MiMos] was used at a voltage of 5V [to avoid damage in the event of inadvertent
specimen failure]. The EL images were analyzed using the Imageio and NumPy Python libraries
to extract an average image grayness value. The grayness corresponds to the grayscale—a single
numeric value that is not obtained from and not necessarily correlated to the color scale (red,
green, blue) or laboratory color (L, a*, and b*). Grayness was benchmarked and found to be
comparable (±<1%) for test 1 (85°C/85% RH) between Python and an automated grayness
extraction algorithm in ImageJ (National Institutes of Health) at all read points. A subset of
MiMos was compared between the entire MiMo (including the backsheet) and just the cell (after
manual cropping of the image).

4.3 Mechanical Tensile Testing
Tensile tests were conducted on 1 cm-wide strips cut from the coupon samples (Figure 2), five of
which were aligned with the length of the specimen in the direction transverse to the extrusion of

 10

the backsheet and one of which was in the machine extrusion direction. Tests were conducted
similar to IEC TS 62788-2 [31] using a 1122/5500R Tensile Tester (Instron Corp.) with the
Automatic Video Extensometer (Instron Corp.) attachment and a 5 kN load cell (Instron Corp.).
After the original tester broke, tests were conducted using a 5966 Tensile Tester (Instron Corp.)
with the Automatic Video Extensometer (Instron Corp.) attachment and a 10 kN load cell
(Instron Corp.). Wedge (manual) grips were used in the first five experiments (1, 2, 3, a, and d)
up to the 2000 h read point. Pneumatic side action grips with rubber coated faces were then used
to help better hold samples in place during testing. A specimen holding fixture (P/N 2701-222,
Instron Corp.) was used to load and unload specimens without inadvertently pinching the
operator. The minimum load, 0.06 N, which limited the measurement of extremely brittle
specimens, could be resolved when loading and unloading unaged TPT backsheet. The 0.5 µm
displacement was the resolution of the video extensometer (manufacturer’s datasheet), which
also limited the measurement of extremely brittle specimens. A universal swivel joint was used
above the top grip to aid machine and specimen alignment during testing. Specimens were
marked for the extensometer using a custom stencil with an uncured black silicone adhesive
(Fortasun PV-804, DuPont de Nemours, Inc.) and then loaded to have a 50 mm gage (for BS-3, -
5, and -7) or a 25 mm gage (for BS-1, -2, -4, and -6) between the specimen grips. As shown in
Figure 2, five replicates that were aligned to the tensile direction and one replicate that was
aligned to the machine direction were examined per coupon at each read point. Specimens were
preloaded from 1 N to 6 N (depending on the BS examined) to reduce the initial toe in the data
profile. Tests were performed at a crosshead displacement rate of 50 mm⋅min-1 and a data
acquisition rate of 500 Hz. The load and displacement data channels were analyzed using a
custom worksheet to extract the following parameters: elongation to break (EtB), ultimate tensile
strength (UTS), ultimate toughness (UT, obtained by numerical integration of the
load/displacement curve up to the EtB, according to the trapezoid rule), yield stress (σy, either
where the first zero slope occurs in a local maximum, or else at the intersection of the
stress/strain curve and the 2% offset of the initial slope), and elastic modulus (E, obtained by
averaging the slope between 30% and 60% of the yield strength). The fracture parameters were
then subjected to a Weibull analysis using the Survival and Reliability Life Distribution Tool in
the JMP software (SAS Institute) to identify and censor outliers (exceeding 95% probability
bounds). EtB, UTS, and UT were taken from the Weibull analysis. That is, α - the Weibull scale
parameter characterizing the magnitude at the 63rd percentile and β - the Weibull shape
parameter characterizing the variation; σy and E were taken as the average and standard
deviation of the replicate specimens, respectively. One limitation of the tensile testing was that
the laboratory humidity was not controlled at NREL, despite the fact that the backsheets may be
sensitive to water, which can plasticize many polymers. The outdoor relative humidity in the dry
Steppe climate at NREL typically ranges from 11% to 48%, whereas the elastic modulus of
polyamide may decrease by 5x from 50% to 100% (water soaked) [54].

4.4 Dielectric Breakdown Testing of the Electrical Insulation
The DC breakdown voltage (VBD) was characterized at the laboratory ambient temperature of
23°C, according to IEC TS 62788-2 [31], [32], using a 4TCE100-10/D149 tester (Phenix
Technologies Inc.) [33] in Univolt N 61 B transformer oil (Exxon Mobil Corp., consistent with
ASTM D 3487 [34]). The tester used 25 mm-diameter brass electrodes. The test duration was at
least 10 seconds, as controlled by the rate of the DC voltage ramp (specified rates included 0.1,
0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, and 5 kV⋅s-1). The ramp rate of 5 kV⋅s-1 was most often applied in this study.

 11

Positive polarity was applied, with the high potential connected to the air side of the backsheet
and the negative terminal connected to the sun side. The current during testing and breakdown
was limited to 10 mA by the tester. Six 50 mm × 45 mm replicates were examined. Specimens
were conditioned for one week prior to testing in a sealed chamber with a saturated Mg(NO3)2
solution, i.e., the ISO 291 class 2 atmosphere [35]. Similar to the fracture-related tensile test
data, the VBD data was subject to a Weibull analysis to identify and censor outliers and then
obtain the final α and β parameters. Because the tester had to be repaired numerous times during
the study, emphasis was placed on first verifying the breakdown voltages of the coupons:
unaged, 4000 h (all tests), and the c test (which gave the greatest number of measurable results).

4.5 Camera Imaging of Visual Appearance
RAW-format images were taken on each side of all samples, first using a 24.6 Mpix Alpha-900
(Sony Corp.) and then later, when the original camera broke, using a 42.4 Mpix Alpha-99 II

(5500K) fluorescent lights. The images were then color balanced using Photoshop (Adobe Inc.).
A color card (ColorChecker Classic, X-Rite Inc.) was imaged for each photographic session to
document the color balancing.

4.6 Optical Microscopy
Microscope images were taken on a VHX 5000 Digital Microscope (Keyence Corp.) [30]. Cross-
polarized images were taken using Keyence VH-Z100 filters at 200x magnification to achieve
color saturation and reduce glare. The “Full Coaxial” (bright field) illumination at maximum
intensity was used. Before taking images, the microscope was white balanced using the white
standard provided with the microscope. High-resolution high-dynamic-range (HR-HDR, 1.92
Mpix) images were taken to increase the image contrast for feature identification, except for the
corner location on the MiMo sun side. On that location, special care was taken to ensure that the
backsheet-encapsulant interface was captured in the image; standard HDR images were taken in
order to reduce the noise in the image (based on visual observation of the general image quality).
In additional to the standard images, additional images were taken at other magnifications (e.g.,
1000x) in select cases.

4.8 Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) of the Chemical
 Structure
An Alpha-II FTIR spectrometer equipped with a “Pt” diamond attenuated total reflectance
(ATR) fixture (Bruker Corp.) was used for polymer chemical structure characterization. The
sampling depth of the measurements was estimated to be on the order of 2 µm. Scans were taken
from 4000 cm-1 to 400 cm-1 using the instrument’s finest 2 cm-1 increment to give the maximum
resolution for spectral features. The result was taken as the average of 50 measurements, based
on a convergence study for BS-6 (expected to have the greatest surface roughness [36]), from
2800 cm-1 to 1800 cm-1 (a region devoid of peak features). An atmospheric correction for H2O
and CO2 was automatically applied using the instrument software. A background correction was
applied to the measured spectra using the instrument software; the background was updated
every 14 measurements. To remove any contamination, the 2 mm x 2 mm ATR crystal was
wiped with a paper optical tissue and isopropyl alcohol solvent prior to each use. Two spectra
measurements were taken for each coupon or MiMo specimen. Scans were conducted on the air
side and sun side of each coupon specimen. A strong signal was not readily obtained from the
original MiMo specimens, where mechanical compliance of the multiple layers was suspected.
Therefore, ~1 cm x ~3 cm samples were hand cut from the MiMo specimens after their 4000 h

all backsheets, with limited backsheet-specific differences. Similar results (loss of Pmax for Damp
Heat testing beyond 1000 h) have been observed in previous studies [43], [44], [45], [46], [47].
The diffusion of water, with subsequent damage to the cell, is thought to dominate here [6], [7].
However, the greater change in the laminated PET-based backsheets after 2000 h may come
from hydrolysis of the core layer. For PET, hydrolysis can generate acid species (e.g.,
terephthalic acid), which can directly affect metallic interconnects and/or catalyze the
degradation of EVA—which can also generate acidic species (e.g., acetic acid). Acidic species
can corrode the metals present in the interconnects; they may also catalyze the degradation of
backsheet layers [48], [49]. The different backsheets have different rates of acid transmission,
which may also affect the extent of corrosion. The loss in Isc in Figure 7 is consistent with
increased series resistance from corrosion, suggested by Rseries in Figure 9. Separately, the
formation of intermetallic compounds has been observed in modules used in hot and humid
locations [50], which may be limited in this study because the MiMos were aged in an open-
circuit electrical bias. The loss in Voc and increase in Rshunt may result from damage to the cell.
Figure 7, Figure 8, Figure 9, and Figure 10 confirm that backsheets are able to protect the front
of the cell, presumably based on the diffusivity and mass transport characteristics of the different
backsheet materials as well as their electrical insulation. The figures further confirm that the
quarter-cell MiMo construction is helpful in distinguishing module components, which is
particularly beneficial for UV weathering (where chamber space is expensive).

6.3 Macro-Cracking of Coupon Specimens
As shown in Figure 13, macro-cracking (at the mm scale, and observed in camera imaging) of
the sun side was observed on BS-5 coupons after UV weathering. Cracking was first observed
after 1000 h of a (A3) weathering (Figure 13). A similar appearance was observed on the BS-5
coupon after 2000 h of d (A2) and b (dry) weathering, and cracking was observed on the BS-5
coupon after 1000 h of c (water spray) weathering. Macro-cracking of the sun side was also
observed for the Type 5 coupons (laminated to include an additional EVA layer) after 2000 h of
a (A3) and c (water spray) weathering. In Figure 13, the cracks evolve to smaller feature sizes
after 2000 h of A3 weathering. The crack geometry initially differed from equiaxed mud
cracking, with the cracks elongated along the MD. The cracks became equiaxed by 4000 h in
most UV weathering tests. No cracks were observed in the BS-5 MiMos after 4000 h of
weathering, including proximate or distant from the edges of the MiMos. The depth of the cracks
was not quantified; however, the cracks did not extend through the air side of the coupons.
Cracks were also observed by the end of study for the Albuquerque, NM and Cocoa, FL outdoor
coupon samples, where the sun side was shaded (facing down onto the supporting fixture, out of
direct sunlight).

 24

 Figure 13. Macro-cracking observed on the sun side of the coupons for BS-5 throughout test
 condition a (IEC TS 62788-7-2 method A3).
The specificity to the sun side in Figure 13 suggests that the cracking occurred in the e-layer,
which is composed of PE for BS-5 [4]. The directionality of the cracking (which in some cases
had not evolved from a series of linear cracks to mud cracks) may have resulted from material
anisotropy and/or sample mounting. The layers in some backsheets have been characterized as
being mechanically anisotropic, including size change (or other strain). The transfer of strain
between anisotropic layers may also give an anisotropic effect, even if the e-layer is itself not
anisotropic. The stress/strain profiles in the mechanical tensile test suggest that biaxially oriented

 25

PET was used for the core layer. That material, however, may not have perfect in-plane isotropy.
In contrast, the coupons were mounted by clipping their top corners to the rack in the Weather-
ometer chambers, constraining the TD relative to the MD. The absence of cracks in the MiMo
specimens may have resulted from the combined mechanical response of the layers in the MiMo
or the transfer of additives (e.g., cross-linking initiator) during lamination. Macro-cracking of the
encapsulant layer on the sun side of sheet specimens in previous weathering studies did not
always also occur in laminated MiMo specimens [59], [60] or was confined to the edges of
MiMo specimens [60]. The cracking of the Type-5 coupons, where the back encapsulant was
present, suggests that the mechanical response dominated for BS-5 in this study. Interestingly,
the cracking of BS-5 occurred in the UV experiments (mud cracking in both A3 and c (water
spray)) for the Type-5 coupons through two layers of UV blocking encapsulant. The cracking of
the sun side of BS-5 in UV weathering may be compared to Figure 26 and Figure 29, where the
results suggest that cracking does not greatly compromise the mechanical or electrical insulation
performance. Macro-cracking of BS-5 from outdoor weathering was mostly examined in
microscope imaging, therefore it is compared in Figure 17, below.

Although not shown, in addition to Figure 13, macro-cracking was observed for the air side of
the BS-2 MiMos for 85°C/85% RH. In addition, localized delamination of the air side was
observed for the BS-2 coupons and MiMos for 85°C/85% RH. Edge cracking with localized
delamination of the air side was observed for BS-2, -5, and -7 MiMos for 85°C/85% RH. After
the c test, the backsheet was delaminated on BS-2 and BS-6 MiMos at edges or including
blisters. BS-6 evidenced a chalking (e.g., on coupons and MiMos by 4000 h in all tests), where
the air surface eroded, leaving a residue on contact. The chalking of BS-6 was most overt after
the c test, which gave a visibly larger, powdery residue. The air side surfaces of the coupons and
MiMos showed discolored streaks (brown amidst white) after the c test; these were particularly
noticeable after 2000 h. Closer examination of the air side surfaces of the coupons and MiMos
after the c test revealed brightness contrast (both global and localized) for BS-5, BS-6, and BS-7.
The local appearance varied between glossy and matte for these samples. Test c gave the greatest
degradation of the NREL bar code stickers (used to identify the samples in addition to the etched

but did not have to be replaced.

Regarding the additional visual (camera) observations, the cracking and delamination suggest

removal of larger volumes of surface material. The irregular discolored appearance after the c

studies. The heterogeneous surface degradation for the c experiment implies localized erosion
and/or thinning of the surface layer. In the event of surface damage, the water spray may
transport damaged material from the surface, accelerating the erosion of the surface layer.
Interaction between the water and embrittled or delaminated layers could accelerate the erosion
process. Scratches on any of the backsheets may contribute to cracking, which underscores the
need for proper handling before and after lamination. Scratches were often observed on the sun
side surfaces of the coupon specimens, which may be softer than the air side surfaces. In general,
much of the most ubiquitous damage observed via camera or microscope imaging resulted from
UV weathering, indicating the importance of photolytic aging in backsheet material selection and
module design type qualification. Macro-cracks have been shown to initiate from micro-cracks,
propagating between layers and through the thickness of a backsheet with aging [62].

laminated within a module, were not further investigated. While not always evident, minerals
were observed to more often stick to the elastomeric E-layer on the sun side of the Albuquerque
specimens, as previously identified in soiling studies, including polymeric surfaces [63]. On the
air side, BS-4 and BS-6 (PA) were more often biocontaminated, whereas the low surface energy
fluoropolymers materials in BS-3 and BS-7 had the least bio-soiling. The sun side of BS-5
became cracked for the Albuquerque, NM and Cocoa, FL specimens, which was only observed
indoors for UV weathering. Only chamber reflected light would reach the sun side of BS-5 as the
indoor Xe lamp instead faced the air side. For the outdoor samples, fluttering of the samples
(leaking light, at the mechanical mounting), piping of light at the periphery, and incomplete
opacity may have allowed UV irradiation. Light is also suspected to have reached the sun side
based on the photosynthetic species observed there. No micro-cracking was observed for the
outdoor BS-6 (PA) specimens, which was observed after A3 and A2 indoor weathering as well
as 85°C/85% RH. It is unclear if surface erosion (from outdoor precipitation and/or dew
condensation) and/or the accelerated nature of the indoor tests distinguishes those specimens
from outdoor weathering.

The results in Table 5 quantify the distinctions observed in Figure 18. The PET backsheets stand
out as the stiffest (greatest slope in Figure 18; greatest Eeff in Table 5) and strongest (greatest σ
values in Figure 18; greatest σy,eff and UTS in Table 5). The Eeff, σy,eff, and UTS may therefore
give a more acute assessment of the degradation of these backsheets. The PO- and APO-based
backsheets stand out as the most extensible (greatest ε in Figure 18; greatest EtB in Table 5). The
EtB may therefore give a more acute assessment of the degradation of these backsheets. The EtB
for all backsheets in Table 5 exceeds the representative maximum strain for a PV module in
Figure 18, i.e., the strain resulting from a backsheet conforming to the outside of a 0.8 mm solder
wire. The representative maximum strain for a PV module in Figure 18 exceeds the εy, eff for all
the backsheets in Table 5 by an order of magnitude, suggesting that localized inelastic
deformation of the backsheet material layers might occur at topographic conformities in PV
modules. In practice, the conformity of backsheets to underlying layers and features is not
perfect (i.e., it is more rounded and occurs over a more limited distance). Thus, the value (ε =
30%) gives an upper limit for the local strain in a PV module that is seldom realized because of
the limited fidelity of conformity. Although some manufacturers say that backsheets may be
highly anisotropic, the similar performance of each backsheet in the TD and MD indicates that
the backsheets examined in this study were approximately isotropic in the in-plane direction. The
use of BO-PET (typical for PV) would avoid anisotropy in BS-3, BS-5, and BS-7; the use of
randomly oriented glass fibers in the core layer would avoid anisotropy in BS-6. The results for
Eeff are sometimes statistically different between TD and MD, whereas σy,eff, εy, eff and other
characteristics are statistically the same. The modulus measurements do challenge the tensile test
(constituting a small portion of the data even for the elevated data acquisition rate used in this
study). σy,eff, εy, eff and other characteristics suggest the backsheets have limited anisotropy − it is
reminded that biaxially oriented PET is used ubiquitously in the PV industry, which would
reduce directional sensitivity of the mechanical characteristics.

A gloss of 100 is comparable to the polished glass reference; a gloss of 0 indicates a matte
surface. A decrease in gloss may therefore indicate roughening of the surface, including
texturing, spalling, or cracking. Gloss (shininess) of the surface is reduced with surface
roughening, with a corresponding scattering of light. A different magnitude of effect may be
observed with different incident angles, which favor specular (20°) through diffuse scattered
(85°) light. Backsheets with a low gloss value tend to remain low (seemingly less affected)
through the experiments. While changes in roughness were qualitatively suggested in microscope
imaging (Figure 14 and Figure 15), the surface was quantitatively characterized in the gloss
measurements (Figure 34, Figure 35, Figure 36, and Figure 37). A change in gloss corresponding

BS-6 (all tests) and BS-5 (hygrometric tests). Gloss therefore might be an alternate method to
identify micro-scale cracking. No overt change in gloss, however, was observed for BS-4, which
was cracked in microscope observations. It remains to be determined whether the results for BS-
4 could have been better correlated if the operating wavelength and angle of acceptance for the
gloss meter were optimized for PV backsheets. As a reminder, the surface roughness can be
quantified directly, e.g., using a mechanical profilometer or optical interferometer.

A change in gloss may also indicate a change in the spectral reflectance of the specimen through
bandpass of measurement; however, the modest change in b* for the air side of BS-5 through
UV weathering in Figure 38 and Figure 39 instead suggests that surface roughening dominates in
the BACKFLIP study. A change in spectral reflectance would be expected to give a correlated
change in power during I-V measurements. Roughening of the surface may be accompanied by
erosion, where chalking might be expected for the backsheets containing TiO2.

The similar change in gloss between the MiMo center and corner likely follows from the
relatively uniform temperature of the specimens during weathering. Although a cell is present at
the center, with only backsheet at the corner, the temperature was separately verified in Table 3
to be similar (comparable to the variation of measurement) between the locations. The similar
temperature during UV weathering likely results from the specimen orientation—the backsheet
was facing the lamp and the darker (more absorbing) cell was facing the chamber walls.
Repeated measurements of the same surface therefore do not add greatly to the characterization,
other than to confirm the result, but could be used to establish a statistical significance of
measurement. The similar magnitude and change in gloss between coupons and MiMos suggests
that samples with different geometries are affected similarly, i.e., the gloss characteristic captures
surface-based performance and degradation that changes similarly between coupons and MiMos,
consistent with degradation resulting from a similar specimen temperature during aging. The
similar degradation observed between coupons and MiMo specimens suggests the same
degradation mechanism for each backsheet type. For example, mechanisms of UV

where a similar PA formulation was used. Relative to the air side, however, different materials
were used on the sun side of BS-1 and BS-5, which Figure 34 and Figure 36 suggest are affected
by both UV and hygrometric aging. The increased variability for both the air side and the sun

of the MiMos in light storage (using a thin-film T-type thermocouple adhered to the air side of

of the hottest summer days.

Figure 47 compares the chemistry of BS-6 between experiments and relative to an unaged

and peak formation at 1102 cm-1. Although the other spectra changes for UV weathering were
observed, the new peak at 1102 cm-1 was not observed for the c test (water spray). The changes
from UV weathering in Figure 47 were observed on the air side of the coupons (facing the lamp)
but were not typically observed on the sun side of coupons (facing the chamber walls). For the

for the a (A3), b (dry), and d (A2) tests, with the peak at 1102 cm-1 typically being greater in
magnitude and sometimes exceeding the normalized scale of the comparative figures. While the
profile shape is self-similar, the intensity varied between the experiments below 800 cm-1.
Features similar to BS-6 in Figure 47 were also observed for BS-4.

Figure 47. FTIR spectra for BS-6 (AAA) coupons. The profiles for the air side after 4000 h of aging

Peak enhancement and peak formation indicate substantive molecular changes, which for BS-6
only result from UV photodegradation, as hygrometric weathering was performed in a dark
chamber. Peak broadening about ~3200 cm-1 was previously attributed to the formation of
alcohols and carboxylic acids [36], and the peak at 1710 cm-1 was previously attributed to
carbonyl group products (i.e., carboxylic acid) resulting from moisture-induced breakdown of
imide groups from a photooxidation process [2]. The broad peak at 1102 cm-1 corresponds to the
C-O stretch mode [65] and was previously observed to result from both artificial and natural UV
weathering [53]. This peak is attributed to oxidation, i.e., incorporation of C-O moieties into the
surface.

The UV damage in Figure 47 occurs with the micro-scale mud cracking observed in Figure 15.
However, the sampling depth for the measurement in Figure 47 is estimated to be on the order of
2 µm because an attenuated total reflectance (ATR) crystal attachment was used in the
measurement. A second degradation mechanism (e.g., chain scission, possibly occurring with the

 73

evolution of the crystallinity) is implied from the spectra for 85°C/85% RH, where no unique
features were observed. Thermal degradation could also explain the cracking of the AAA
backsheet during aging at elevated temperatures or while conducting relative thermal index
testing in the dark [61]. The effects of UV weathering on the known-bad BS-6 are also identified
in Refs. [48], [49], [53]. In contrast to previous studies examining accelerated testing and
outdoor weathering, the occurrence of a separate mechanism for thermal degradation is now
directly demonstrated by comparing between Figure 14, Figure 15, and Figure 47. Roughening
of the surface, which facilitates crack formation, may also contribute to the incipient features
shown in Figure 14 and Figure 15 for 85°C/85% RH. The peaks below 800 cm-1 may result from
the presence of TiO2 in the formulation of BS-6, i.e., from fortuitous sampling between
measurements or from increased exposure via surface erosion. Practically speaking, the FTIR
characterization method may become limited below 600 cm-1. The similar changes observed for
BS-4 may result from the polyamide 12 resin also used in the surface layer for that backsheet [4].

The lack of a new peak at 1102 cm-1 for the AAA coupons and MiMos may result from erosion
and/or neutralization occurring during the c test (water spray). The erosion of BS-6—suggested
by the gloss measurements—may be elevated for the c test such that surface-specific degradation
is removed throughout aging, mitigating the peak. The peak at 1102 cm-1 may be unique to the
material used in the air surface layer of AAA; the glass fibers observed during fractography
imaging for the c test indicate that the air surface had been completely removed (e.g., from
erosion during aging or fracture during tensile testing). The elevated humidity at the surface may
neutralize the chemistry, enabling the peak at 1102 cm-1. In either case, the peak may result from
a compositional difference present only in the surface layer of the air side of the backsheet.
Microscopy observation of the glass fibers (present only in the core layer of AAA) in specimens
after 4000 h suggests that the c test causes erosion sufficient to completely remove the air surface
layer. In addition to Figure 47, the spectra for the backsheets with changes indicating UV
photodegradation (including BS-2 air side, BS-4 air side, BS-5 air and sun sides, and BS-6 air
side) were rounded and/or attenuated for the c test relative to other UV weathering tests,
suggesting removal of the degraded surface material—even for the sun side of BS-7, which did
not evidence strong UV degradation.

6.12 Activation Energy Analysis of the Characteristics Examined
Table 7 summarizes the analysis of the effective activation energy (Ea) for the characteristics
examined in this study. The method of analysis in Ref. [66] was applied—namely, (1) the change
(“∆”) in each characteristic was first determined relative to the unaged value for each backsheet;
(2) a linear slope was applied to the ∆ data, where the best linear fit for the slope was not fixed to
intercept zero; (3) data were censored beyond a perceived inflection in the slope to limit the
analysis to the initial degradation (e.g., a single degradation regime) of the backsheets; (4)
characteristics, specimen geometries, and aging that did not evidence possible thermal activation
(e.g., where degradation was increased with temperature) were excluded from analysis. The
uncertainty of the Arrhenius analysis was determined from the method of propagation of
uncertainty. Uncertainty was first determined for the variation of each characteristic with respect
to the variation in duration of aging, and then for the variation of the slope (e.g., ∆P/∆t the
change in power with respect to time) with respect to the variation in temperature.

 76

Table 7 includes: the backsheet (both the index and name designation); the frequency factor, c1,
where the dimension of measurement is defined separately by each characteristic; and the Ea.
The unit of measurement is given for c1 in each section heading. The Ea was determined for
power (from I-V performance), ultimate toughness (from mechanical tensile testing), elongation
to break (from mechanical tensile testing), ultimate tensile strength (from mechanical tensile
testing), yield strength (from mechanical tensile testing), gloss, and color (b* for yellow/blue).
The result for gloss is given as an average for all angles examined (20°, 60°, and 85°). The Ea
was lowest (on the order of 0 kJ⋅mol-1 – 10 kJ⋅mol-1) for gloss. The Ea was moderate for EtB,
UTS, Pmax, UT, b*, and εy (on the order of 30 kJ⋅mol-1 – 50 kJ⋅mol-1, except for EtB from
hygrometric aging, where it was ~20 kJ⋅mol-1). A greater thermal activation was determined for
the UTS and UT of the PET-based backsheets (on the order of 110 kJ⋅mol-1 – 140 kJ⋅mol-1). A
thermal activation of 34 ± 16 kJ⋅mol-1 (average ± 1 S.D.) was observed for Pmax. A greater Ea was
determined from the ∆[gloss] of MiMos (18 ± 7 kJ⋅mol-1) relative to coupons (6 ± 5 kJ⋅mol-1). A
similar thermal activation was determined from the ∆[b*] of MiMos (43 ± 16 kJ⋅mol-1) relative
to coupons (47 ± 16 kJ⋅mol-1). The uncertainty of measurement in Table 7 is of a similar order of
magnitude to the Ea.

Regarding Table 7, only a limited number of characteristics could be analyzed, which was a
surprise relative to the original intent of the BACKFLIP project. However, BACKLFIP is
expected to be representative, in which case Ea may not be determined for a variety of
characteristics and backsheets used in the PV industry. More specifically, the Ea for Pmax is
comparable to 18 kJ⋅mol-1, the Ea for the transport of water [67]. The Ea for Pmax is believed to
reflect the processes that facilitate the corrosion of the MiMo interconnects rather than the
degradation of the backsheets. Deviation from the ideal value of 18 kJ⋅mol-1 may reflect
backsheet-specific differences in the transport and/or accumulation of water. The relatively low
Ea for gloss suggests a thermally insensitive rate-limiting degradation mechanism. Provided that
the degradation of gloss is indeed thermally activated, a wider range of evaluation temperatures
(greater than 45°C – 85°C) is recommended to improve the evaluation of Ea and the certainty of
measurement. The greater Ea from the ∆[gloss] of MiMos relative to coupons may reflect the
more complicated structure of the MiMos, where the misfit between multiple layers of separate
packaging materials facilitates mechanical damage and subsequent erosion of the backsheet
surface. The Ea for the UTS and UT of the PET-based backsheets is comparable to 129 kJ⋅mol-1,
the Ea for the hydrolysis of PET [21]. This is reminiscent of the highly accelerated nature of the
85°C/85% RH test condition, where the moisture level exceeds the use conditions for terrestrial
PV by at least two orders of magnitude. A narrower range of evaluation temperatures (between
65°C and 85°C) and a shorter duration of aging is recommended to improve the evaluation of Ea
and the certainty of measurement. The moderate Ea for EtB, UTS, Pmax, UT, b*, and εy is similar
in magnitude to the discoloration of PV encapsulants [66]. While the Ea for b* is similar to other
characteristics, different rate limiting mechanisms may facilitate changes in EtB, UTS, UT, and
εy. This is because the mechanisms facilitating discoloration (e.g., decomposition of an additive)
are not automatically expected to affect the mechanical characteristics (which typically result
from processes such as cross-linking or chain scission). The similar thermal activation for b* and
the mechanical characteristics is more likely to be a coincidence. The similar thermal activation
was determined from the ∆[b*] of MiMos relative to the mechanically simpler coupons, further
suggesting that color and mechanical characteristics are not coupled. Like discoloration of
encapsulants, thermal activation on the order of 30 kJ⋅mol-1 – 50 kJ⋅mol-1 for b* is expected to
result from defect-mediated degradation (e.g., from impurities and/or additives). The
performance of BS-6 was not distinguished by temperature in Figure 24 and Figure 30; therefore,
no Ea could be determined. In the case of mechanical performance, BS-6 may be limited by the
adhesion of the PA matrix to the glass fibers; in the case of electrical insulation, the use of
steady-state aging in this study may not result in the development of defects that aid electrical
breakdown. As a reminder, the Ea in Table 7 represents the effective or rate-limiting reaction
through the range of examination in this study for degradation that may occur as the result of a
series of separate chemical processes. The uncertainty of measurement is unfortunately similar to
the Ea in Table 7, which is a limitation of the range and number of temperatures examined in this
study. Despite the large uncertainty, Table 6 identifies the order of magnitude for Ea for some
characteristics not previously examined in the literature.

Although not directly identified, connections between some of the results can be inferred by
reviewing Table 8. For example, the final and most prominent degradation of I-V performance,
EL contrast, and mechanical characteristics for BS-3, -5, and -7 may result from degradation of
the PET core (including cracking and acid formation) during hygrometric aging, enabling
interconnection corrosion and cell damage. The 85°C/85% RH test condition, however, well
exceeds the terrestrial operating condition, including within PV modules, both instantaneously
and beyond 2000 h. Other observations (micro-scale cracking, gloss, color, and FTIR) suggest
damage to the surface layer(s). In some cases, several observations occur for the same backsheets
for UV weathering, suggesting photodegradation that might have been more readily observed if a
wider range of chamber temperatures was used. For example, in Table 8, a minimal change in
the average value with a distinguishable increase in variation is observed but not indicated for
the gloss and color of all backsheets from UV weathering. Many of the associated degradation
modes do not seem to occur with a corresponding reduction in I-V performance, including
micro-cracking, surface roughening, and change in the polymer chemical structure.

More specific observations also stand out from Table 8. Some results in the table are not relevant
to PV modules. These include cracking of the sun sides of BS-5 coupons during UV weathering
(which was observed in both Type-3 and Type-5 coupons, but not in MiMos) and discoloration
of the sun sides of MiMos in hygrometric aging (the sun would bleach the chromophore species
during PV operation). The blooming of additives in FTIR was specific to the PO-based
backsheets (which all use the same sun side material layer), but was not observed for MiMo
samples. Test c stands out in: microscopy (morphology), gloss, color, and polymer structure
(FTIR). The surface erosion alone (which may be compared between microscopy and gloss) is
not consistent with previous examples in the PV literature, suggesting that the water spray is
overly accelerated relative to traditional use of PV modules. Backsheet erosion may, however,
become more prominent if vertically mounted modules find greater use (e.g., to make use of
bifacial cells and reduce the need for cleaning) or if cleaning of the back of the module finds
greater use (e.g., to reduce soiling in bifacial modules). Some anticipated results are absent from
Table 8. Macrocracking was seldom observed in this study for most backsheets, although it has
been observed in PV installations for the known-bad BS-6. Microscope observation of micro-
cracking, however, may be consistent with the eventual formation of larger cracks, e.g., for BS-
6. Camera observation of cracking and/or delamination of BS-2, BS-5, and BS-7 is also
consistent with hygrometric embrittlement. Lastly, Table 8 can be used to identify observations
for characteristics of interest—some of the observations and their implications are explored
further in the results above, Sections 6.1 through 6.12.

This study sought to establish a connection between surface and bulk characteristics. For
example, surface cracking of BS-6 has previously been suggested to facilitate through-thickness
cracking with continued aging [64]. This study gives direct evidence of the simultaneous UV
weathering and thermal (possibly hygrometric) degradation of BS-6, which is hypothesized to
lose attachment between the PA matrix and the mechanically reinforcing glass fibers. More
often, however, the connections between the characterizations believed to most examine bulk
(tensile test and VBD) and surface (microscopy, gloss, color, and FTIR spectroscopy) remain
tenuous. For example, the tensile characteristics of BS-5 are critically affected by hygrometric
aging; hygrometric aging also causes discoloration and micro-cracking. In addition, UV
weathering causes roughening and evolution of the BS-5 polymer structure. However, the change
in VBD cannot be determined with aging. The combined results for BS-5 are consistent with

 82

degradation from hygrometric aging, with subsequent lesser UV degradation occurring. In
contrast, the tensile characteristics of BS-2 are more notably affected for UV weathering. BS-2
also shows roughening as well as evolution of its polymer structure from UV weathering. The
VBD of BS-2 is significantly affected by hygrometric aging, and hygrometric weathering also
causes micro-cracking and discoloration. This suggests that the degradation of BS-2 in a PV
module may occur in a complex manner that depends on the complex environmental history of
an outdoor location. BS-1, BS-3, BS-4, and BS-7 give a mixture of results that, like BS-2, serve
as a reminder that while a particular degradation mechanism (e.g., UV photodegradation,
hydrolysis, thermal degradation) often dominates, multiple mechanisms may occur and even
interact.

Several of the characteristics in this study illustrate that steady-state aging alone insufficiently
represents the effects of the outdoor use of PV modules. First, the known-bad BS-6 did not
exhibit macro-cracking in the MiMo specimens, which is frequently observed for modules used
in a variety of world locations (climates). The use of an accelerated test sequence (e.g., Damp
Heat then UV weathering then Thermal Cycling as in module accelerated sequential testing
(MAST)) or combined-accelerated stress testing have both been demonstrated to result in
cracking of AAA backsheets. Another example from this study was the discoloration of BS-2
and BS-4 during 85°C/85% RH, which was found to be unrepresentative because it could be
subsequently photobleached. The disparate surface morphology of some backsheets in the
fractography analysis between 85°C/85% RH, A3, and the c test (water spray) also suggests that
degradation can have a synergy when applied simultaneously. The specimen geometry was also
found to be critical in this study, e.g., the sun sides of BS-5 coupons were cracked in the UV
weathering and field aging experiments, which was not observed in the MiMo specimens (where
the additional layers presumably prevent cracking). Although single-material specimens may
sometimes be sufficient to verify certain performance characteristics, multiple-layer specimens
are necessarily to verify material compatibility. This study provides further motivation for the
use of more complex aging methods (relative to steady state) with appropriate specimen
geometries for the safety and design type qualification of PV materials and components.

The abundance of electrical insulation results that greatly exceed 8 kV (the present safety
requirement for 1500 V DC systems) raises the concern that the PV industry presently requires
PV backsheets to be excessively thick. It is cautioned that: (1) the specimens were tested in this
study at laboratory ambient temperature rather than at the elevated temperature that may be
observed for active PV systems (expected to reduce VBD); (2) the effects of aging may be greater
in active PV systems than in the BACKFLIP study because only steady-state aging was used in
this study; (3) the effects of material loss through product life (e.g., erosion from UV, blowing
sand, and/or cleaning) have not been considered; and (4) the effects of inverter switching noise
(AC ripple in addition to the DC system voltage) have not been considered. The DC breakdown
voltage test has limited industry experience and may not be the optimal method for PV
backsheets (e.g., relative to a withstand voltage or another method). More work to verify the
appropriateness of the backsheet thickness as well as the electrical insulation method is
recommended.

Limited correspondence was observed between many of the characteristics examined in the
BACKFLIP study. The variety of activation energy values obtained for different characteristics
from the same backsheets in this study suggests that at least some of the characteristics are

 83

limited by different degradation mechanisms. For example, the lack of correspondence in VBD
(where BS-2 was affected in hygrometric tests) and the tensile test (where BS-2 was not affected
in hygrometric tests) may reflect differences in the sample volume (where VBD may favor the
interior and the tensile test may favor the surface) as well as the initial performance in each
characteristic. Confirming the critical the critical defects (e.g., for tensile test and VBD) could
help, but may require parametric study (parametric variation of the surface area, volume, or edge
length) or additional methods of defect visualization. Sequential or combined aging may give
additional degradation, also providing better correspondence between surface and bulk damage.
Lastly, not all material degradation may be detected by the limited set of characterizations used
in BACKFLIP.

The BACKFLIP study can be used to inform which test conditions should be applied in future
studies, including the examination of the activation energy. For example, precise examination of
the hydrolysis of PET-based backsheets might be more rigorously examined with additional test
conditions between 85°C/85% RH and 65°C/85% RH. Also, the gloss was found to be affected
in all UV tests; however, that characteristic might benefit from a wider range of test temperatures
to obviate its temperature dependence in addition to the surface damage itself. A broader range
of specimen temperatures would be helpful in future studies, if valid test conditions can be
identified (e.g., A4 or A1 test conditions from IEC 62788-7-2). It was hoped that the set of test
conditions applied in BACKFLIP would enable the determination of the activation energy for all
characteristics and backsheets examined. In practice, additional test conditions are needed to
determine the activation energy. The results of this study may be used to help assign test
conditions in future studies.

While the c test (water spray) was found to provide the greatest degradation, its validity remains
to be verified. The degradation from the c test (including the magnitude of damage and/or the
number of backsheets affected) stands out relative to the other experiments for its effect on
surface morphology (camera and optical microscopy), color, gloss, VBD, tensile test (UTS), and
polymer structure (FTIR). To date, the spalling or removal of the surface layer observed via
microscopy in BACKFLIP has not been readily identified in field-degraded PV specimens, so
the c test is not expected to give field-representative results. Generally speaking, material
degraded at the surface can mask subsequent degradation of the underlying material, preventing
subsequent damage. In some applications, the removal of the degraded surface material can
therefore give overly accelerated or even unrepresentative results. Consider that in traditional PV
installations, the backsheet is protected from rain by the sun side of the module, as the backsheet
faces the ground. The use of PV may change, however, with bifacial modules—if cleaning of the
back surface became necessary (to reduce soiling contamination) or if vertical mounting found
greater use (to facilitate irradiation of the module back), then more aggressive accelerated testing
(as in the c test) might be appropriate. Although it was applied in BACKFLIP to facilitate
Arrhenius analysis, the appropriateness of the 85°C/85% RH test condition for PV backsheets
has been questioned within the industry, including for 1000 h duration.

Because a wealth of data was obtained during the BACKFLIP study, some recommendations can
be made regarding characteristics to examine. Recommendations can be given regarding the
characteristics that gave the strongest or most distinct signal. For example, b* and YI had greater
changes in color relative to a* (modestly correlated) and L (anticorrelated). However, the choice
of b* and YI over other characteristics may seem obvious because the color yellow has overtly

 84

negative aesthetic implications. Regarding gloss, the incident angles of 20° and 60° often gave
distinct degradation for specific backsheets relative to others, suggesting that 85° may not be the
preferred measurement angle (or that the range of test temperatures must be increased for 85° to
work well). FTIR was found to give incomplete detection of degradation—consider the micro-
cracking of BS-6 observed in microscopy after 85°C/85% RH with no overt change in FTIR
spectra; or consider the unique morphology of the air sides of BS-2 and BS-4 observed in
fractographic examination between the A3, 85°C/85% RH, and c (water spray) tests. Unlike
changes in the population of side groups and cross-linking chemistry, FTIR has a particularly
limited ability to diagnose polymer chain scission. Regarding analysis, the toughness
characteristic was verified in this study to identify backsheets prone to changes in UTS and EtB,
which may be helpful if a single characteristic is desired for mechanical evaluation of backsheets
(e.g., to reduce the effort required for analysis). Because it examines the energy to failure, the
toughness is more similar in principle to a fracture mechanics test and might be used if the
“trouser” tear test (ISO 6383-1) cannot be performed. Lastly, Weibull analysis is recommended
for mechanical tensile and VBD testing—the method is intrinsically expected to give a better fit to
the measured data because the defect-driven characteristics have a non-Gaussian cumulative
distribution function. A more accurate result may be achieved from the Weibull fitting of a small
sample size (5 specimens –10 specimens), and standardized protocols already exist (including
ISO 20501, ASTM C1239, and IEC 62539) that might be used to censor outliers and achieve
more objective results. Lastly a slope analysis can be used to asses if multiple populations of
defect co-exist or a source of error in measurement may be occuring.

Regarding the characteristics to examine, the usefulness or application-specific requirements
might also be considered. For example, while the PV literature has in recent decades given
attention to discoloration of backsheets, b* and YI can be limited outside of aesthetic-specific
applications (e.g., building adapted photovoltaics (BAPV) and building integrated photovoltaics
(BIPV)). This is because the discoloration of polymers may reflect the chemical degradation of
polymer additives rather than the polymer bulk; therefore, color does not necessarily correlate to
other characteristics. Conversely, the gloss characteristic (which can correlate with surface
erosion) is not as frequently reported in the PV literature. The gloss of the air sides of the
backsheets was affected for all UV weathering experiments (A3, b (dry), c (water spray), and
A2), suggesting photodegradation-facilitated surface erosion and/or roughening. The erosion of
backsheets, however, is not well examined in the PV literature, which may be of concern, given
the historic use of air-side surface layers of limited thickness relative to the core layer as well as
the recent exploration of thin-film coatings on the air-side surfaces of backsheets.

8 Summary
Improvements in the materials and manufacturing of PV backsheets are key to increasing the
reliability, lifespan, and recyclability of PV modules. This report contains the results from the
experiments in the BACKFLIP study through 4000 h of accelerated aging. The backsheets for
the study included three industry benchmark backsheets (TPT, PPE, and KPf); one known-bad
backsheet (AAA); and three novel polyolefin backsheets (PP, PO, and APO).

Cracking of the backsheet, which was observed several times in this study, is often cited as a
critical safety concern. No major change in the breakdown voltage was detected, however,
including for the micro-cracking observed in this study. This may reflect the limited damage
resulting from the steady-state aging conditions applied. It has also not been proven that micro-
scale cracking of the air-side surface layer will automatically compromise relied-upon insulation
or result in macro-scale cracking that adversely affects a PV module. In the case of UV
weathering of the sun side of BS-5, the macro-scale surface cracking did not give a significant
change in breakdown voltage, nor is it valid, as it was not observed in the laminated MiMo
specimens. Based on FTIR measurements, cracking may be enabled by damage to the polymer
surface structure during UV weathering. Based on the gloss measurements, UV weathering is
suspected to enable surface erosion and surface roughening, which may provide nucleation sites
for crack formation.

Aside from electrical performance verification, the mechanical tensile test is frequently used to
screen for backsheet degradation, including mechanical embrittlement. Although traditional
mechanical tensile testing does not examine the characteristic of direct interest (fracture
toughness, MPa⋅m1/2), it is used in multiple industries as a proxy for embrittlement. Tensile
testing did readily identify the known-bad BS-6 (AAA), which was found to readily degrade in
all experiments. Perhaps disturbingly, the performance of BS-6 was also affected for many other
characteristics, in many of the seven BACKFLIP experiments. Tensile testing, however, did not
give great correspondence to electrical insulation (VBD) and even gave false negative results
(hygrometric weathering PET-based backsheets). Further refinement of the accelerated aging and
further understanding of the connections between essential characteristics (electrical insulation
mechanical performance) is needed.

Less overt changes in performance include loss of electrical performance for the PET-based
MiMos through hygrometric weathering and discoloration (which indicates chromophore
formation). The former is corroborated by interconnect corrosion in EL images and series
resistance measurements. Further examination, including shunt resistance, suggests cell
degradation. The loss of MiMo power therefore likely results from the diffusion of water through
the backsheets (with only limited dependence on the backsheet materials) rather than the direct
durability of specific backsheets. The interpretation of backsheet discoloration is unclear because
system owners may have different requirements for appearance. Furthermore, discoloration is
not expected to have a significant effect on module electrical performance. Photobleaching is
observed in some cases, whereas light is ordinarily present for PV during operation.

The BACKFLIP study provides an in-depth example of the present state of PV backsheet
technology. Several concerns arose from the study (see the discussion section for further detail).
These include the following:

 86

• Several of the characteristics in this study provide examples that steady-state aging alone
 and simple specimen geometries insufficiently represent the effects of the outdoor use of
 PV modules.

• The abundance of VBD electrical insulation results greatly exceeding 8 kV raises the
 concern that the PV industry presently requires PV backsheets to be excessively thick.

• Limited correspondence was observed between many of the characteristics examined in
 the BACKFLIP study. This lack of correlation may result from insufficient accelerated
 aging and/or the nature of the degradation (e.g., surface vs. volume damage).

• The BACKFLIP study can be used to inform which test conditions should be applied in
 future studies, including examination of the activation energy. The optimal test
 temperatures were found to vary according to the characteristic of interest.

• The c test (water spray) was found to provide the greatest degradation, but its validity
 remains to be verified. The validity of UV weathering with water spray is expected to be
 doubtful for traditional PV, but remains to be determined for bifacial PV (e.g., back
 surface cleaning and/or vertical mounting).

• Some recommendations can be made regarding the characteristics to examine, including
 the characteristics with the strongest or most distinct signal (85° gloss measurements
 were not well distinguished) in addition to application-specific requirements (e.g., gloss
 and the possible erosion of surface layer(s)).

• Some methods of analysis may facilitate future examination. For example, toughness
 may allow an efficient single-characteristic analysis of the mechanical tensile test.
 Weibull analysis may improve the fidelity of the tensile and VBD tests, while also
 allowing objective automation of analysis.
