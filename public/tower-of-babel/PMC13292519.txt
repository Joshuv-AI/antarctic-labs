“We learn while doing”: informal training experiences of critical care nurses managing dialysis technologies in intensive care units
Authors: Mohammed Yousef Almulhim, Samah Faisal Fakhry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute kidney injury (AKI) is among the most prevalent life-threatening complications in intensive care units (ICUs), with continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) being the primary mode of renal support for haemodynamically unstable patients. Despite the technical complexity of CRRT, formal training programmes for ICU nurses remain inconsistent across healthcare settings. This study explored the informal training experiences of critical care nurses managing dialysis-dependent patients in Al-Ahsa, Saudi Arabia.MethodsA qualitative descriptive design was employed, guided by reflexive thematic analysis as described by Braun and Clarke. Semi-structured in-depth interviews were conducted with 20 purposively sampled ICU nurses with direct CRRT management responsibilities across healthcare facilities affiliated with King Faisal University, Al-Ahsa. Data were analysed through six iterative phases of thematic analysis. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of King Faisal University.ResultsFour overarching themes were generated: (1) “Figuring It Out on the Floor”, nurses described learning CRRT management primarily through observation and immediate bedside practice; (2) “The Colleague Is the Textbook”, experienced peers served as informal educators, transmitting practical knowledge through demonstration and narration; (3) “Anxiety Behind the Machine”, nurses articulated persistent fear of error that gradually transformed into competence through repeated exposure; and (4) “Gaps the System Does Not See”, participants identified a systemic absence of structured CRRT training and called for formalised competency frameworks.ConclusionsCritical care nurses in this setting rely heavily on informal, peer-mediated learning to acquire CRRT competencies in the absence of standardised institutional training. These findings highlight an urgent need for structured simulation-based CRRT education, written protocols, and competency assessment frameworks. Nursing leaders and ICU educators must recognise and address the informal learning burden carried by frontline nurses to safeguard patient safety and support workforce development.Clinical trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04914-3.Keywords: Continuous renal replacement therapy, Informal learning, Intensive care nursing, Qualitative research, Dialysis technology, ICU nurses, Saudi Arabia, Workplace-based learning

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) represents one of the most prevalent and clinically consequential complications affecting critically ill patients in intensive care units (ICUs) worldwide. Studies estimate that AKI occurs in 20–50% of ICU patients, with mortality rates ranging from 30% to over 60% in those requiring renal replacement therapy [1, 2]. Among the available modalities, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) has emerged as the preferred approach for haemodynamically unstable patients, offering precise fluid management, gradual electrolyte correction, and superior tolerance compared with intermittent haemodialysis [3]. The global adoption of CRRT in ICU settings has expanded considerably over the past two decades, and the therapy is now considered an indispensable component of critical care nephrology [4, 5].

Critical care nurses are the primary operators and monitors of CRRT in most ICU environments. Their responsibilities encompass circuit initiation and termination, fluid balance management, troubleshooting alarms, anticoagulation monitoring, and the rapid identification of circuit clotting or haemodynamic compromise [6]. These tasks demand a synthesis of theoretical knowledge, technical proficiency, and real-time clinical reasoning. Yet, the nursing literature consistently documents significant gaps in CRRT-specific knowledge, competency, and confidence among ICU nurses, particularly those in the early stages of their careers [7]. Inadequate preparation has been linked to increased circuit downtime, avoidable complications, and sub-optimal delivery of prescribed therapy doses [8].

Despite the centrality of nurses to CRRT delivery, the evidence base regarding how nurses actually acquire CRRT competence in clinical practice remains limited. Quantitative studies have documented deficits in knowledge and identified training needs [9, 10], but the experiential, qualitative dimension, specifically, how nurses learn to manage these technologies when formal training is absent or insufficient, has received far less scholarly attention. A 2024 phenomenological study by Demet and colleagues [11] explored Turkish ICU nurses’ experiences of CRRT and found that nurses faced challenges including information deficits and heightened workload. This work, while illuminating, was conducted in a single country context and did not specifically examine informal learning pathways. There remains an important qualitative gap in understanding the lived, situated learning processes through which nurses acquire CRRT competency in everyday ICU practice [12, 13].

Saudi Arabia’s healthcare landscape presents a particularly salient context for this inquiry. The Kingdom’s ICUs serve a rapidly expanding critically ill population amid an ongoing nursing workforce transformation shaped by Vision 2030, which places a premium on Saudisation of the nursing profession and the advancement of nursing education and competency standards [14]. Studies have documented that CRRT is widely used in Saudi ICUs, yet training structures remain heterogeneous and largely dependent on individual unit culture rather than standardised institutional programmes [15, 16]. Informal learning, knowledge acquired outside formal educational programmes through peer interaction, observation, and experiential trial, constitutes a significant and underexplored mechanism of professional development in this environment [17]. Understanding how nurses in Al-Ahsa-affiliated ICUs navigate CRRT learning in the absence of formal support is therefore both contextually pertinent and clinically significant.

This study aimed to explore the informal training experiences of critical care nurses managing dialysis technologies in ICUs affiliated with healthcare facilities in Al-Ahsa, Saudi Arabia. The guiding research question was: “What are the experiences of critical care nurses in learning to manage continuous renal replacement therapy in the context of informal and workplace-based training?”

Methods

Study design

A qualitative descriptive design was employed, underpinned by a constructivist epistemological perspective that acknowledges the subjective and socially situated nature of knowledge [18]. Reflexive thematic analysis, as elaborated by Braun and Clarke [19, 20], was selected as the analytical approach because of its accessibility, flexibility, and suitability for generating rich thematic accounts of experiential phenomena in health professional contexts. This design was chosen in preference to survey-based approaches because the aim was to capture the depth, nuance, and contextual complexity of nurses’ informal learning processes, dimensions that quantitative methods are ill-equipped to address.

Study setting and recruitment

The study was conducted across ICUs within healthcare facilities affiliated with King Faisal University in the Al-Ahsa region of the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia. Al-Ahsa is a governorate with a population exceeding one million, served by a network of tertiary and secondary care hospitals. The ICUs involved included medical, surgical, and mixed critical care units with bed capacities ranging from 10 to 24 beds per unit. CRRT services were operational in all participating units. Purposive sampling was employed to recruit nurses with direct experience of CRRT management, ensuring maximum variation in terms of years of experience, unit type, gender, and nationality. Recruitment was facilitated through ward charge nurses and nursing supervisors following institutional approval. A total of 28 nurses with eligible CRRT management experience were identified and approached for participation through direct personal invitation by ward charge nurses and nursing supervisors, supplemented by written information sheets distributed at unit briefings. Of these, 8 nurses declined participation, primarily citing time constraints and shift scheduling conflicts, resulting in a final sample of 20 participants (response rate: 71.4%).

Sample size and adequacy

A total of 20 ICU nurses participated in the study. Sample size was guided by the principle of information power as described by Malterud and colleagues [21], which posits that the adequacy of a qualitative sample is determined not by a fixed numerical threshold but by the density and specificity of the information generated. Given the focused research question, the homogeneous practice setting, and the detailed semi-structured interview format, a sample of 20 participants was deemed to provide sufficient informational richness to support rigorous thematic analysis. Recruitment ceased when the research team determined that no substantively new codes or thematic dimensions were being generated from additional interviews.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria required participants to be: (1) registered nurses currently employed in a participating ICU; (2) directly involved in the management of CRRT or intermittent dialysis for critically ill patients; (3) with a minimum of 12 months of ICU experience; and (4) willing to provide written informed consent. Nurses who had completed a formal, nationally accredited CRRT training programme within the previous six months were excluded, as such recent structured training was considered likely to substantially alter the experience of informal learning that was the focus of inquiry.

Data collection

Data were collected between October and December 2025 through individual, face-to-face, semi-structured interviews conducted in the participants’ clinical workplace or in a private adjacent room, according to participant preference. Interviews were conducted in Arabic, the first language of Saudi participants, and in English for non-Saudi expatriate nurses who expressed a preference for English-language interviews. Each interview lasted between 45 and 75 min. All interviews were conducted by the first author (M.Y.A.), who holds both clinical and academic expertise in critical care, and who was not in a supervisory relationship with any participant. A reflective journal was maintained throughout data collection to document the researcher’s positioning and evolving analytical thinking.

Interview structure and conduct

The interview guide was developed through a systematic iterative process informed by existing literature on informal learning in clinical nursing and CRRT nursing competency [10]. The guide was piloted with two ICU nurses who did not subsequently participate in the main study; piloting confirmed the comprehensibility and exploratory adequacy of the questions. The guide covered five domains: (1) the participant’s introduction to CRRT management; (2) the nature and sources of learning in everyday practice; (3) challenges and facilitators of CRRT competency development; (4) the role of colleagues and peers in learning; and (5) participants’ views on training needs and systemic gaps. The full interview guide is provided in Additional File 1.

Recording and transcription

All interviews were audio-recorded with participants’ consent using a password-protected digital recorder. Recordings were transcribed verbatim by the research team. Arabic transcripts were translated into English by a bilingual nursing research professional and checked for fidelity against the original recordings by a second bilingual member of the team. Any discrepancies in meaning were resolved through consensus discussion. Transcripts were not returned to participants for member-checking given practical constraints, but analytical summaries were shared with four purposively selected participants, all of whom confirmed that the emerging themes accurately reflected their experiences.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using the six-phase reflexive thematic analysis framework of Braun and Clarke. In Phase 1, the research team familiarised themselves with the data through repeated reading and noting of initial impressions. In Phase 2, systematic line-by-line coding was applied across the full dataset. In Phase 3, codes were collated and sorted into candidate themes. In Phase 4, themes were reviewed against both the coded extracts and the entire dataset to assess their coherence and scope. In Phase 5, themes were refined, named, and defined. In Phase 6, the final report was produced, integrating rich participant quotations with analytical commentary. Analysis was conducted by two researchers (M.Y.A. and S.F.F.) independently before consensus meetings to ensure rigour. NVivo 14 software was used to manage data organisation and coding.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Institutional Review Board of King Faisal University, Al-Ahsa, Saudi Arabia (IRB Approval No.: KFU-ETHICS4343). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection, following provision of an information sheet explaining the study purpose, procedures, voluntary nature of participation, and right to withdraw at any time without any adverse consequences. Confidentiality, anonymity, and data protection were maintained throughout the study. Audio recordings and transcripts were stored securely on password-protected devices accessible only to the research team; identifying details were removed during transcription; and participants were assigned alphanumeric codes (N01-N20) for analysis and reporting. No names, employment identifiers, or identifiable institutional details were included in the transcripts, analysis files, or manuscript quotations. The study was conducted in accordance with relevant ethical guidelines and regulations, including the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Rigour and reflexivity

Trustworthiness was addressed through attention to credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability [22]. Credibility was enhanced through prolonged engagement with the data, triangulation of analytical perspectives between two independent researchers, and analytical summary sharing with participants. Transferability is supported by thick description of the setting, sample, and data collection process. Dependability was promoted through maintenance of an audit trail documenting analytical decisions at each phase. The first author maintained a reflexive journal, acknowledging a clinical background in internal medicine that may have shaped the framing of questions and initial coding, a potential bias that was continuously interrogated during analysis. The first author (M.Y.A.) is an Associate Professor in Internal Medicine with extensive ICU clinical experience; this background facilitated rapport with participants but carried the risk of normalising informal training as an inevitable feature of critical care environments, potentially underestimating institutional accountability. The second author (S.F.F.) is a Professor of Nursing Administration with expertise in nursing education policy, contributing an institutional and pedagogical lens that counterbalanced the clinical perspective. Both researchers entered the study with the assumption that informal learning, while prevalent, reflects a systemic training deficit, an orientation that was continuously examined through peer debriefing sessions, reflexive memos, and iterative consensus meetings to mitigate interpretive bias and ensure that findings reflected participants’ constructions rather than researcher presuppositions.

Theme 1: “Figuring It out on the floor”

This theme captures the foundational learning dynamic experienced by the majority of participants: an immersion in CRRT practice that preceded, and in many cases entirely replaced, formal instruction. Nurses across all levels of experience described entering the CRRT environment with minimal preparatory training, and described learning as something that happened not through structured programmes but through the immediate, unforgiving reality of clinical work.

Learning by watching and doing

Most participants described their first encounter with CRRT as one of direct observational immersion, often occurring during their first days or weeks in the ICU. The absence of a formal induction programme meant that shadowing a more experienced colleague, itself an informal arrangement, was the primary gateway to competency.

I had no CRRT training when I was assigned to my first patient on dialysis. I watched another nurse set up the circuit and I tried to absorb everything. Nobody told me what to look for. I just watched and memorised. The next time, I did it myself.(N07)

This experience of watchful absorption was echoed across multiple interviews, with participants describing a tacit curriculum built from bedside proximity. The knowledge transmitted was predominantly procedural and contextual, when to intervene, how to respond to alarms, how to interpret haemodynamic cues, rather than the theoretical pharmacological and physiological knowledge typically covered in formal curricula.

You learn what the numbers on the screen mean when you see a patient’s blood pressure drop at the same time the machine alarms. That connection, you cannot learn it from a book. You learn it because it happened in front of you, and you had to act. (N14)

Senior nurses in the sample reflected on having been in the same position earlier in their careers, and described the watchful learning mode as so normalised within their unit culture that it had become invisible as a training structure.

Trial, error, and self-correction

Beyond observation, participants described an active process of trial and self-correction as a central mechanism through which CRRT competency was built and refined. Rather than accessing formal troubleshooting guidance, nurses reported developing personal repertoires of problem-solving strategies through iterative experience.

The first time the circuit clotted, I panicked. I called a senior nurse and she helped me. But the second and third time, I started to see the pattern, what the pressure readings were doing before the clot. I made my own checklist in my head. Nobody gave me that checklist. (N03)

This self-generated knowledge had a deeply personal and contextual character. Participants could articulate complex troubleshooting sequences that they had developed independently, but these were rarely formalised or shared in a standardised way. The knowledge resided in the individual rather than in the institutional system, rendering it vulnerable to loss through staff turnover.

Every nurse here has their own way of doing certain things with the machine. Things they figured out themselves. When that nurse leaves, the knowledge leaves too. There is nothing written down. (N19)

Theme 2: “The colleague is the textbook”

If Theme 1 captured nurses’ individual learning efforts, Theme 2 illuminated the social architecture through which much CRRT knowledge was transmitted: an informal collegial structure in which experienced nurses served as de facto educators. This theme encompasses both the generative dimensions of peer-to-peer learning and the social constraints that at times limited its effectiveness.

Peer-to-peer knowledge transfer

Participants consistently identified senior colleagues as their most valued and most accessed learning resource. The knowledge transmitted in these interactions was practical, situated, and often unarticulated until the moment of teaching, a form of pedagogical improvisation driven by immediate clinical need.

One colleague, she has been here twelve years. She knows every sound the machine makes. When I am confused, I go to her. She explains everything in a way that makes sense, not like a textbook, but like she is showing me what to see. She is my real training programme. (N11)

This peer knowledge transfer was bidirectional in some instances. Several junior nurses described situations in which they had shared new technical knowledge, gleaned from manufacturer updates, online forums, or postgraduate study, with more senior colleagues who lacked access to formal continuing education. This reversed the traditional hierarchy and highlighted the democratising potential of informal learning networks.

I did an online course on CRRT filters last year. I came back and told the senior nurses about the new anticoagulation protocols. They were interested. We learned from each other. That is not supposed to be how training works, but that is how it works here. (N05)

Hierarchy and the limits of asking

Despite the value attributed to peer learning, participants described a persistent social and hierarchical tension that constrained their willingness to seek help or acknowledge uncertainty. The ICU’s emphasis on competence and efficiency created an environment in which admitting a knowledge gap felt professionally risky.

You don’t want to ask too many questions. It looks like you don’t know what you are doing. So sometimes you try to figure it out yourself, even if you are not sure. You don’t want to seem weak in front of the team. (N02)

Several participants recounted instances in which this reluctance to seek help led them to proceed with a clinical task about which they were not fully confident, relying on their developing intuition rather than verified knowledge. While most such situations resolved without incident, the participants acknowledged the patient safety implications of this dynamic.

There was a time I was unsure whether to change the filter or wait. I didn’t ask because the consultant was in the unit and I did not want to interrupt. I made a decision myself. It worked out, but it could have been different. (N16)

Theme 3: “Anxiety behind the machine”

This theme addresses the affective dimension of nurses’ CRRT learning trajectories, the emotional experience of managing a complex, high-stakes therapy with insufficient preparation. Participants described a dominant emotional narrative that began in acute anxiety and, for many, evolved over time toward a more grounded sense of competence. This trajectory, however, was not universal, and some nurses described residual anxiety even after years of CRRT experience.

Fear of error and patient harm

The prospect of causing harm to a patient through CRRT mismanagement was a source of intense anxiety, particularly for newer nurses. The continuous, life-sustaining nature of the therapy, and its direct relationship to haemodynamic stability, amplified the perceived stakes of every clinical decision.

Every time the alarm goes off, my heart jumps. I am always afraid I will do something wrong and the patient will deteriorate because of me. That fear does not go away quickly. For me, it stayed for more than a year. (N08)

This fear was compounded by the perceived lack of a clear reference point for clinical decision-making. Without formal protocols or standardised guidance, nurses reported making decisions in an informational vacuum, relying on accumulated observational learning and peer advice rather than institutional frameworks.

When I first started, I would read the machine screen and not know what was dangerous and what was normal. Nobody had sat with me and told me what the alarm ranges meant clinically. I was guessing. That is a terrifying feeling when someone’s kidneys are depending on your decisions. (N18)

Emotional adaptation over time

Despite the intensity of early anxiety, most participants described a discernible trajectory of emotional adaptation. Repeated exposure to CRRT management, including the experience of managing complications and recovering from errors, generated a gradual sense of confidence and clinical authority.

After maybe one year, something changed. I stopped fearing the machine. I started to see it as a tool I understood. That moment when you know what to do before the alarm even sounds, that is when you feel like a CRRT nurse, not just a nurse beside a CRRT machine.(N01).

This emotional transformation was closely linked to a shift in the nature of nurses’ relationship with the machine, from an opaque, threatening technology to a familiar, interpretable instrument. Several participants described this shift in almost phenomenological terms, as a change in perceptual orientation.

“It is like the machine speaks to you after a while. You understand its language. But learning that language, no one teaches it to you formally. You learn it from the machine itself, from the errors, from the nights when things go wrong.” (N12).

Theme 4: “Gaps the system does not see”

The final theme situates individual and peer learning experiences within a broader structural critique. Across all 20 interviews, participants articulated, with varying degrees of explicitness, the systemic gap constituted by the absence of institutional, standardised CRRT training. This theme reflects nurses’ systemic consciousness, their capacity to name the structural conditions shaping their learning experience, and their calls for formalised support.

Structural absence of formal training

Every participant described the current CRRT learning environment as one in which formal training was either entirely absent or sporadic and unsystematic. Many reported that their onboarding to CRRT management had been solely peer-mediated, with no institutional curriculum, competency assessment, or mentorship structure.

When I was assigned to care for a CRRT patient for the first time, I received a thirty-minute briefing from the charge nurse. That was my training. Thirty minutes, and then I was alone with the patient. If anything had happened, it would have been because the system failed, not because I failed. (N09)

Participants reflected on the incongruity between the high complexity and clinical risk of CRRT management and the informality of the training structures available. Several drew explicit comparisons to other advanced nursing competencies, mechanical ventilation, vasoactive infusions, for which structured training pathways existed, highlighting the specific neglect of CRRT education.

We have training for ventilators. We have simulation for vasopressors. For CRRT, which is at least as complicated, there is nothing official. We just figure it out. That is not acceptable when a patient’s life depends on it. (N13)

Calls for structured competency support

Participants did not merely diagnose the structural gap but articulated specific and substantive proposals for how it should be addressed. Simulation-based training was the most frequently cited recommendation, with nurses describing a desire for hands-on, low-stakes environments in which to practise circuit management, troubleshooting, and emergency responses without patient risk.

I wish we had a simulation lab where we could practise everything, circuit priming, managing pressure drops, responding to a clotting alarm, without a real patient. I would have learned so much faster, and I would have been less afraid. (N06)

Additional recommendations included written, accessible clinical protocols for common CRRT scenarios, periodic competency assessments to formalise learning milestones, and dedicated CRRT mentorship roles within the unit staffing structure.

There should be a named nurse in every ICU who is the CRRT resource person. Someone who is trained properly, who updates knowledge, and who is responsible for teaching others. Right now, that role falls to whoever happens to be most experienced. That is not a system. That is an accident. (N17)

Thematic summary: the relationship across themes

The four themes together constitute an integrated account of informal CRRT learning as a system-produced necessity rather than a pedagogically intentional strategy. Nurses “figured it out on the floor” (Theme 1) because colleagues, rather than institutions, were “the textbook” (Theme 2). This informal learning occurred within an affective landscape of anxiety and gradual emotional adaptation (Theme 3), and was experienced by participants as symptomatic of structural “gaps the system does not see” (Theme 4). The themes are not sequential but mutually reinforcing: the structural absence of formal training intensifies both the reliance on peer learning and the emotional burden carried by individual nurses, while the emotional stakes of clinical responsibility sharpen the desire for systemic reform. Together, these findings reveal informal learning not as a neutral or chosen approach but as a response to institutional insufficiency with direct implications for patient safety and nursing professional development.

Discussion

This study generated rich qualitative data illuminating how critical care nurses in Al-Ahsa, Saudi Arabia, acquire and consolidate competence in CRRT management through informal, workplace-based learning processes in the absence of standardised formal training. The four themes, immersive floor-based learning, collegial knowledge transfer, affective adaptation, and systemic training gaps, together offer a coherent and clinically significant account of an underexplored phenomenon. These findings both affirm and extend the existing literature, while adding important regional and contextual specificity [23].

The first theme, describing learning through watching and trial-and-error, aligns closely with findings from the 2025 qualitative study by Kakyo and colleagues on informal mentoring in acute care nursing, which found that nurses and midwives develop critical competencies through peer-facilitated practical engagement rather than formal programmes [24, 25]. Our findings extend this work by showing that in the high-acuity, technology-intensive CRRT context, the absence of formal guidance amplifies the personal and patient-safety stakes of informal learning, rendering it not merely inadequate but potentially hazardous. The work of Kılıç and colleagues [26], who found that Turkish ICU nurses caring for CRRT patients experienced information deficits and felt insufficiently prepared, resonates strongly with our findings and underscores the cross-national generalisability of this concern.

The centrality of peer-to-peer knowledge transfer identified in Theme 2 reflects patterns documented in broader studies of ICU nurse learning. Practical knowledge in the ICU, as demonstrated in a phenomenological study of experienced ICU nurses by Carmona-Monge and colleagues, is fundamentally socially constructed and transmitted through situated interaction rather than formal instruction [27]. The informal educator role occupied by senior CRRT nurses in our study mirrors Benner’s foundational framework of knowledge acquisition in nursing, in which expert nurses transmit tacit clinical wisdom through demonstration and narration rather than articulation [28]. The hierarchical constraints on asking for help, however, represent an important complication of this dynamic, echoing findings that the ICU’s performance culture can inhibit the help-seeking behaviours essential to safe informal learning [29, 30].

Theme 3’s account of anxiety followed by emotional adaptation over time parallels the trajectory described by Fredholm and colleagues in their study of critical care nursing students learning during COVID-19, who described attachment, authenticity, and autonomy as evolving dimensions of professional formation in ICU contexts [31]. Our finding that emotional adaptation was closely linked to an accumulation of error-informed experience, rather than to formal skills acquisition, carries significant pedagogical implications: it suggests that the development of CRRT confidence is currently dependent on near-miss and error experiences rather than on safe simulated practice. This is a systemic vulnerability that simulation-based education is specifically designed to address [32].

The structural critique articulated in Theme 4 is consistent with international literature documenting the inadequacy of CRRT training across health systems. A 2024 survey of 66 hospitals across central and South China found that formal CRRT training was inconsistent and that nurses identified education deficits as a primary barrier to quality CRRT delivery [33]. An earlier review [34] highlighted that initial vendor-provided training, while widespread, rarely led to sustained competency maintenance in the absence of periodic institutional support. Our Saudi context adds the important dimension of Vision 2030’s nursing professionalisation agenda, which creates both a policy imperative and an institutional mechanism for formalising CRRT education within national competency frameworks [35].

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The study was conducted within a single regional healthcare context in Al-Ahsa, which may limit the transferability of findings to ICU settings with different training cultures, resource environments, or nursing workforce compositions. Participants were recruited through purposive and convenience sampling, introducing the possibility of volunteer bias, nurses with stronger views about training gaps may have been more willing to participate. All data are based on self-report, and recall of earlier learning experiences may be subject to retrospective reconstruction. The absence of observational data means that the actual practice behaviours underlying nurses’ accounts could not be directly verified.

Implications for nursing education and practice

These findings carry substantive implications for nursing education, ICU management, and health policy. At the institutional level, ICU nursing leads and hospital educators should urgently develop standardised CRRT competency frameworks that include simulation-based induction, written procedural protocols, and periodic competency reassessment. The “CRRT resource nurse” model proposed by participants represents a practical and cost-effective structural intervention that could formalise the informal educator roles that senior nurses already occupy. At the policy level, the Saudi Commission for Health Specialties and nursing regulatory bodies should consider developing nationally endorsed CRRT nursing competency standards, aligned with Vision 2030 professional development objectives, to reduce regional variation in training quality. At the educational level, nursing faculties offering critical care specialisation programmes should incorporate CRRT-specific simulation modules into postgraduate curricula, ensuring that future ICU nurses enter practice with greater preparatory competence than the current generation describes having received.

Conclusion

This study offers the first qualitative exploration of informal CRRT learning experiences among ICU nurses in an Al-Ahsa, Saudi Arabia context, generating four themes that collectively reveal a system reliant on peer mediation and personal resilience in the absence of structural training support. Critical care nurses are learning to manage life-sustaining dialysis technologies through observation, trial, error, and collegial transmission, processes that are generative but insufficient for consistent patient safety. The call for formalised, simulation-supported CRRT education, resource nurse models, and nationally endorsed competency standards is both urgent and well-grounded in the experiential evidence presented here. Addressing these structural gaps is not merely a matter of professional development, it is an obligation of institutional stewardship and patient protection.
