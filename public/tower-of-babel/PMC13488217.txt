Combined impact of inflammation score and depression on clinical outcomes in patients after percutaneous coronary intervention: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Junshuo Zhu, Yindu Liu, Qianqian Song, Haofeng Zhou, Bingqing Bai, Jinna Chang, Wenqi Zhao, Liming Lei, Huan Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSystemic inflammation and depressive symptoms are both associated with adverse cardiovascular prognosis, but their combined prognostic value after percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) remains uncertain. This study examined the individual and joint associations of inflammatory score (IS) and clinically relevant depressive symptoms with traditional major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE).MethodsA total of 487 patients with angiographically confirmed coronary artery disease were included. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), with PHQ-9 ≥ 10 used as the primary definition of clinically relevant depressive symptoms. IS was calculated from z-scores of C-reactive protein and white blood cell count and dichotomized using the original cohort-specific median cutoff (-0.78). The primary outcome was traditional MACE. Cox regression, restricted cubic spline, additive interaction, and exploratory discrimination analyses were performed.ResultsDuring follow-up, 134 patients (27.5%) experienced MACE. After extensive cardiovascular adjustment, PHQ-9 ≥ 10 remained independently associated with MACE (HR 1.91, 95% CI 1.08–3.39, P = 0.027), whereas high IS alone was not significant (HR 1.34, 95% CI 0.89-2.00, P = 0.159). Patients with both high IS and PHQ-9 ≥ 10 had the highest MACE risk compared with those with low IS and PHQ-9 < 10 (HR 3.50, 95% CI 1.58–7.76, P = 0.002). hs-CRP threshold analyses were less consistent, and additive interaction estimates were imprecise.ConclusionsPHQ-9 ≥ 10 was independently associated with traditional MACE. Coexistence of high IS and PHQ-9 ≥ 10 identified a high-risk subgroup, although IS and additive interaction findings require cautious interpretation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-06021-2.Keywords: Coronary artery disease, Percutaneous coronary intervention, Inflammation score, Depression, Major adverse cardiovascular events

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) continues to be a leading cause of death globally, presenting a growing public health challenge [1, 2]. For patients with significant coronary stenosis, percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) remains a cornerstone of revascularization therapy [3]. Despite substantial advances in interventional strategies and secondary prevention, many patients remain at risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) following PCI, highlighting the need to identify robust prognostic markers [4–6].

Systemic inflammation has been well-established as a central driver of atherosclerosis and thrombosis, and plays a pivotal role in the development of cardiovascular events [7–9]. Common inflammatory biomarkers such as high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) [10] and white blood cell count (WBC) [11], as well as composite indices like the hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio, have demonstrated predictive value in various populations [12, 13].

Concurrently, psychosocial factors, particularly depression, are increasingly recognized as independent risk contributors in cardiovascular disease [14–16]. Depression can lead to autonomic dysfunction, heightened inflammatory responses, and unhealthy lifestyle behaviors, which in turn exacerbate cardiovascular risk [17].

Although inflammation and depression have been individually linked to poor cardiovascular outcomes, few studies have examined their combined prognostic impact in patients undergoing PCI [18, 19]. Moreover, evidence remains limited in Chinese populations, where cultural and healthcare factors may influence both the prevalence and consequences of depression and inflammation. Integrating biological and psychosocial risk factors into a unified predictive framework may improve risk stratification and personalized management in post-PCI care.

Therefore, this retrospective cohort study aimed to investigate the individual and synergistic effects of systemic inflammation and depressive symptoms on long-term cardiovascular outcomes in patients with coronary heart disease undergoing PCI.

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital between October 2017 and January 2018. A total of 705 patients with angiographically confirmed CAD were initially screened. After application of the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, as well as exclusion of patients with missing baseline data or incomplete follow-up ascertainment, 487 patients were included in the final analysis, while 218 patients were excluded (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of study selection. A total of 705 hospitalized patients were initially screened. After confirmation of at least one coronary vessel with ≥ 50% stenosis on angiography, 573 patients met the preliminary eligibility criteria. Of these, 86 patients were excluded because of unavailable IS and PHQ-9 data (n = 31), unavailable covariates (n = 22), unavailable history of PCI (n = 5), or loss to follow-up (n = 28). Ultimately, 487 patients were included in the final analysis and categorized into four groups according to inflammatory score (IS) and depression status

The inclusion criteria were: (1) a primary discharge diagnosis of CAD; (2) a history of intracoronary stent implantation or at least one epicardial coronary artery stenosis (≥ 50%) confirmed by coronary angiography; and (3) the ability and willingness to complete the PHQ-9 questionnaire. The exclusion criteria included: (1) severe valvular heart disease, or cardiomyopathy not caused by coronary artery stenosis; (2) cognitive impairment precluding questionnaire completion.

In the analytic cohort, PCI status was further clarified using procedural records. Among the 487 included patients, 367 (75.4%) underwent PCI or balloon angioplasty during the index hospitalization, including 344 patients with stent implantation and 23 patients treated with PTCA/balloon angioplasty without new stent implantation. The remaining 120 patients did not receive PCI or stent implantation during the index hospitalization but met the inclusion criteria based on prior intracoronary stent implantation and/or angiographically confirmed coronary stenosis. A history of prior intracoronary stent implantation was present in 161 patients (33.1%).

All participants provided informed consent, and the study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital (No.GDREC2017203H). The study was conducted in strict accordance with the ethical principles laid down in the Helsinki Declaration. Clinical trial number: not applicable. To minimize selection bias, strict inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied throughout the recruitment process.

Assessment of depression

Depressive symptoms were evaluated using the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), a widely used and validated open-access screening tool. Each item is scored on a 4-point Likert scale (0–3), with total scores ranging from 0 to 27. Based on established thresholds, depression severity was categorized as follows: 0–4 (minimal), 5–9 (mild), 10–14 (moderate), 15–19 (moderately severe), and 20–27 (severe). In the primary analysis, depression was defined as a PHQ-9 score ≥ 10, consistent with the validated cutoff for clinically relevant depressive symptoms and probable major depression. PHQ-9 ≥ 5 was analyzed only in sensitivity analyses to capture broader depressive symptoms, because scores of 5–9 indicate mild symptoms rather than depression. The PHQ-9 was used as a screening instrument rather than a formal psychiatric diagnostic assessment.

Inflammatory score calculation

Fasting venous blood samples were collected at baseline. High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) was measured using an immunoturbidimetric assay, and white blood cell (WBC) counts were obtained using standard automated analyzers. The inflammatory score (IS) was derived as the sum of the individual z-scores for hs-CRP and WBC. For categorical analyses, patients were dichotomized according to the cohort-specific median value of the IS (-0.78). This cutoff was used for within-cohort comparison and exploratory risk stratification, rather than to define an externally validated clinical threshold.

To address the limited external reproducibility of the internally derived IS, parallel analyses were performed using clinically established hs-CRP categories: <1, 1–3, and > 3 mg/L. In these analyses, hs-CRP in mg/L was derived from CRP reported in mg/dL by multiplying by 10.

PHQ-9 assessment and baseline blood sampling for hs-CRP and WBC were performed during the baseline hospitalization as part of the clinical assessment. Patients undergoing PCI at the time of assessment were excluded, so these measurements were not obtained during the PCI procedure itself. Therefore, hs-CRP was interpreted as a baseline inflammatory marker rather than as a peri-procedural inflammatory response.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was traditional MACE, defined as the occurrence of all-cause death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke, cardiac rehospitalization, or repeat revascularization during follow-up. The broader composite outcome, defined as traditional MACE plus non-cardiac rehospitalization, was analyzed as a sensitivity outcome rather than the primary endpoint.

Patient follow-up was conducted through structured telephone interviews approximately five years after hospital discharge. Patients who could not be contacted during follow-up were excluded from the final analysis.

Covariates and baseline assessment

Baseline demographic and clinical information was collected through standardized interviews and medical record reviews. Variables included age, sex, education level, marital status, BMI, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, smoking status, creatinine, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), PCI indication, number of diseased vessels, left main disease, prior myocardial infarction, prior PCI or stent history, and NYHA/Killip functional class. Laboratory parameters included fasting blood glucose, total cholesterol, triglycerides, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, creatinine, glycated hemoglobin, hs-CRP, and WBC count.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Between-group comparisons were performed using analysis of variance, the Kruskal-Wallis test, or the chi-square test, as appropriate.

Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The crude model was unadjusted. Model 1 was adjusted for age and sex. Model 2 was additionally adjusted for marital status, education level, BMI, hypertension, diabetes, smoking status, and log-transformed creatinine. Model 3 was further adjusted for LVEF, PCI indication, number of diseased vessels, left main disease, prior myocardial infarction, prior PCI or stent history, and NYHA/Killip functional class. Restricted cubic spline analyses were used to evaluate nonlinear associations. Additive interaction was assessed using relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), attributable proportion (AP), and synergy index (SI), and results were interpreted as evidence for possible positive additive interaction rather than biological synergy. Proportional hazards assumptions were evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals. Receiver operating characteristic analyses were considered exploratory. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.0), and a two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Joint association of IS and PHQ-9 ≥ 10 with MACE

Compared with patients with low IS and PHQ-9 < 10, those with both high IS and PHQ-9 ≥ 10 had the highest risk of MACE. In Model 2, the HR for the high IS and PHQ-9 ≥ 10 group was 4.00 (95% CI 1.99–8.03, P < 0.001). After extensive cardiovascular adjustment, this association remained statistically significant (Model 3: HR 3.50, 95% CI 1.58–7.76, P = 0.002). By contrast, the high IS and PHQ-9 < 10 group (HR 1.27, 95% CI 0.82–1.95, P = 0.284) and the low IS and PHQ-9 ≥ 10 group (HR 1.40, 95% CI 0.59–3.31, P = 0.441) were not statistically significant after full adjustment (Table 2). Parallel hs-CRP threshold analyses

In parallel analyses using clinically established hs-CRP thresholds, hs-CRP categories were not independently associated with MACE after full cardiovascular adjustment. Compared with hs-CRP < 1 mg/L, the HR was 0.88 (95% CI 0.48–1.63, P = 0.693) for 1–3 mg/L and 1.02 (95% CI 0.59–1.75, P = 0.949) for > 3 mg/L. Using hs-CRP > 3 mg/L as a binary exposure also showed no significant association after full adjustment (HR 1.09, 95% CI 0.71–1.67, P = 0.692). The joint pattern based on hs-CRP and PHQ-9 ≥ 10 was less consistent than that observed for the composite IS (Supplementary Table S4 and Supplementary Figure S1).

Sensitivity, interaction, and exploratory analyses

Sensitivity analyses using the broader composite outcome yielded directionally similar results. In the fully adjusted composite outcome model, the high IS and PHQ-9 ≥ 10 group remained at increased risk (HR 3.47, 95% CI 1.76–6.81, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Table S5). Analyses using PHQ-9 ≥ 5 as a broader depressive-symptom definition also showed consistent associations; PHQ-9 ≥ 5 was associated with MACE after full cardiovascular adjustment (HR 3.28, 95% CI 2.20–4.89, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Table S6). Medication-related exploratory analyses are provided in Supplementary Tables S7A-S7C.

Additive interaction analyses suggested a possible positive additive interaction between high IS and PHQ-9 ≥ 10 for MACE, but estimates were imprecise: RERI 1.83 (95% CI -0.80 to 10.06), AP 0.52 (95% CI -0.44 to 0.91), and SI 3.74 (95% CI -32.35 to 52.37) (Table 3). Thus, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of significant biological synergy.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of patients after PCI, clinically relevant depressive symptoms, defined as PHQ-9 ≥ 10, were independently associated with a higher risk of traditional MACE during long-term follow-up. Elevated inflammatory score (IS) was associated with MACE in the basic clinical models, although this association was attenuated after more comprehensive cardiovascular adjustment. Importantly, patients with both high IS and PHQ-9 ≥ 10 had the highest risk of MACE, and this association remained significant after adjustment for cardiovascular risk factors, CAD severity, PCI indication, LVEF, renal function, smoking status, prior MI, prior PCI/stent history, left main disease, and functional class. These findings suggest that the coexistence of inflammatory burden and clinically relevant depressive symptoms may identify a subgroup of patients with increased long-term vulnerability after PCI.

Inflammatory score and MACE prognosis

Systemic inflammation, quantified through a composite inflammatory score based on CRP and WBC, was associated with an increased risk of MACE in the basic clinical adjustment models. This observation is consistent with previous studies showing that inflammatory markers are associated with cardiovascular risk, highlighting the clinical importance of subclinical inflammation in atherogenesis and thrombosis [20]. However, in the fully cardiovascular-adjusted model, the independent association between high IS and MACE was attenuated and no longer statistically significant. This suggests that part of the apparent prognostic effect of IS may be explained by underlying cardiovascular disease severity, renal function, prior cardiovascular history, or procedural characteristics.

Mechanistically, inflammation may promote endothelial dysfunction, facilitate monocyte recruitment, and accelerate plaque instability through pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, TNF-α [21], and CRP [22]. These mediators may increase oxidative stress, activate coagulation pathways, and impair nitric oxide bioavailability, thereby contributing to cardiovascular deterioration [23]. Nevertheless, the internally derived IS used in this study should be interpreted as an exploratory measure of inflammatory burden rather than a clinically validated threshold. In parallel analyses using clinically established hs-CRP categories (< 1, 1–3, and > 3 mg/L), hs-CRP alone was not independently associated with MACE after full cardiovascular adjustment. Therefore, the inflammatory findings should be interpreted cautiously and require validation in external cohorts [24].

Depressive symptoms and MACE prognosis

A nonlinear relationship between PHQ-9 scores and MACE was observed, suggesting that greater depressive symptom burden may be associated with worse cardiovascular prognosis. This finding is consistent with prior evidence indicating that depression is associated with recurrent events and mortality in patients with coronary heart disease [15]. In this analysis, PHQ-9 ≥ 10 remained significantly associated with MACE even after extensive cardiovascular adjustment, supporting the relevance of clinically meaningful depressive symptoms in post-PCI risk assessment.

Several biological and behavioral mechanisms may explain this association. Depression has been linked to autonomic dysregulation, including reduced heart rate variability and increased sympathetic activation, both of which may predispose patients to ischemia and arrhythmia [25]. In addition, depressive states may amplify systemic inflammation through upregulation of cytokines such as IL-6, CRP, and interferon-γ, thereby further aggravating vascular injury [26]. From a behavioral perspective, depressive symptoms are associated with smoking, physical inactivity, poor diet, and lower adherence to treatment, all of which may adversely affect cardiovascular outcomes [27]. In sensitivity analyses using PHQ-9 ≥ 5 to capture broader depressive symptoms, the associations remained directionally consistent. However, PHQ-9 ≥ 5 should be interpreted as mild or broader depressive symptoms rather than clinical depression.

In practical terms, the PHQ-9 may be a feasible screening tool in routine post-PCI care because it is brief, standardized, and easy to administer. In high-volume cardiology settings, it could potentially be incorporated into discharge assessment or follow-up visits as an initial screening step. However, positive screening results should not be interpreted as equivalent to a formal psychiatric diagnosis, and effective implementation would require staff awareness, workflow support, and referral pathways for further psychological or psychiatric evaluation when needed.

Joint association between inflammation and depressive symptoms

The most notable finding was that patients with both high IS and PHQ-9 ≥ 10 had the highest risk of MACE. In the fully adjusted model, this group remained at significantly increased risk compared with patients with low IS and PHQ-9 < 10, whereas high IS alone and PHQ-9 ≥ 10 alone were no longer statistically significant in the joint exposure model. This pattern suggests that the coexistence of inflammatory burden and clinically relevant depressive symptoms may represent a high-risk phenotype after PCI.

From a mechanistic perspective, this joint association may reflect a bidirectional relationship between inflammation and depressive symptoms. Systemic inflammation may influence mood and brain function through cytokine signaling and neuroendocrine disruption, whereas depressive symptoms may exacerbate inflammation through stress-related immune activation and adverse health behaviors [28]. Pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α may cross the blood-brain barrier, alter neurotransmitter metabolism, and dysregulate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, thereby contributing to depressive symptoms [29, 30]. Conversely, depression-related changes in glucocorticoid receptor sensitivity and cortisol rhythm may perpetuate immune dysregulation, creating a feedback loop that accelerates vascular damage [31, 32].

However, the interaction findings should be interpreted cautiously. Additive interaction analysis suggested a possible positive additive interaction between high IS and PHQ-9 ≥ 10, but the confidence intervals were wide and included the null. Therefore, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of biological synergy. Rather, simultaneous assessment of inflammatory burden and clinically relevant depressive symptoms may help identify patients with higher long-term MACE risk.

Sensitivity analyses using the broader composite outcome yielded directionally similar results, with the high IS and PHQ-9 ≥ 10 group remaining at the highest risk. However, because the composite outcome included non-cardiac rehospitalization, traditional MACE was used as the primary endpoint in the revised analysis to provide a more specific cardiovascular outcome. Exploratory discrimination analyses showed only modest AUC values, and therefore the present findings should be viewed primarily as evidence of association and risk stratification rather than as a definitive predictive model.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, inflammation and depressive symptoms were assessed only at baseline, so changes in inflammatory burden or psychological status during follow-up could not be evaluated. Second, depressive symptoms were measured using the PHQ-9, which is a validated screening tool rather than a structured psychiatric interview. Although PHQ-9 ≥ 10 was used to define clinically relevant depressive symptoms in the primary analysis, this cutoff should not be interpreted as a formal diagnosis of major depressive disorder. Third, the inflammatory score was internally derived from standardized CRP and WBC values, and its cutoff was based on the cohort-specific median value of -0.78. Therefore, the IS threshold is data-dependent and may not be directly generalizable. In parallel analyses using clinically established hs-CRP categories, hs-CRP alone was not independently associated with MACE after full cardiovascular adjustment, suggesting that the inflammation-related findings should be interpreted as exploratory. Fourth, although traditional MACE was used as the primary endpoint in the revised analysis, outcome ascertainment was based on retrospective follow-up data. The broader composite outcome, which additionally included non-cardiac rehospitalization, was retained only as a sensitivity analysis because of its clinical heterogeneity. Fifth, although the revised models included more comprehensive cardiovascular adjustment, residual confounding remains possible. Medication-related confounding also cannot be excluded, particularly because aspirin and statin use were nearly universal, whereas antidepressant use was rare, limiting stable medication-adjusted analyses. Some evidence of deviation from the proportional hazards assumption was observed for the joint exposure models; therefore, Cox estimates should be interpreted as average associations over follow-up. Finally, this was a single-center retrospective study, which limits causal inference and external validity. Future multicenter prospective studies with repeated biomarker assessment, structured psychiatric evaluation, standardized hs-CRP measurement, and more comprehensive treatment and follow-up data are needed to validate these findings.

Conclusions

Clinically relevant depressive symptoms defined by PHQ-9 ≥ 10 were independently associated with traditional MACE. The coexistence of high IS and PHQ-9 ≥ 10 identified a subgroup with the highest MACE risk, even after extensive cardiovascular adjustment. However, the independent association of high IS was attenuated after full adjustment, hs-CRP threshold analyses were less consistent, and additive interaction findings should be interpreted cautiously. Future multicenter prospective studies are needed to validate these findings and clarify whether integrated inflammatory and psychological assessment can improve post-PCI risk stratification.
