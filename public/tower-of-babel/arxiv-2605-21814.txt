Optical analogy for stellarators: Ridges as caustics and coils as singularities
Wrick Sengupta \aff 1 \corresp, Stefan Buller \aff 1, Rogerio Jorge \aff 2, John Kappel \aff 3, Andrew Brown \aff 1, Richard Nies \aff 4, Pedro F. Gil \aff 5, Nikita Nikulsin \aff 6, Per Helander \aff 6, Amitava Bhattacharjee \aff 1

Abstract
A common feature of most numerically optimized stellarator geometries is the presence of sharp ridges on outer flux surfaces, irrespective of the rotational transform. Despite their importance, an analytical theory for their existence has been lacking. In this work, we demonstrate that ridges are not artifacts but mathematical necessities. We develop such a theory for devices with quasisymmetry (QS). We demonstrate that QS exhibits close connections with the theory of geometrical optics, following Parker’s “optical analogy” (E.N. Parker, Geophys. Astrophys. Fluid Dyn , 1989). By mapping vacuum QS to the eikonal equation of geometrical optics, we derive the conditions for ridge formation, identified as field line caustics where magnetic field lines focus. Furthermore, we prove a geometric theorem for stellarator coil design: both ridges and filamentary coils must lie on the zero-determinant manifold of the magnetic gradient tensor. This topological constraint unifies the description of plasma ridges and external coils, providing a precise criterion for identifying valid coil locations and explaining the efficacy of the magnetic gradient lengthscale (J. Kappel et al., Plasma Phys. Control. Fusion , 2024) as a coil optimization parameter. We demonstrate that as the device becomes more compact, sharp ridges naturally form on the inboard side in quasiaxisymmetry. We support our analytical theory with extensive numerical evidence.

2
Geometry of flux surfaces, curvatures, ridges and coils

In this work, we focus on sharp ridges that arise naturally in most optimized stellarators and appear as creases or sharp flux surface corners. Due to their sharp features, they collimate field lines, which tend to align with them. Unlike X-points, they typically do not close on themselves.

Although ubiquitous in most 3D stellarator equilibria, especially when optimized for stability, a thorough study of these structures is complicated by several factors, including the nonlinearity of the underlying equilibrium equations, the enforcement of QS, proximity to current coils, and the 3D geometry of flux surfaces. In particular, there are no clear answers on what aspect of equilibrium physics, coils, geometry, or their combination, prompts these ridges to exist at certain locations in certain devices.

In this Section, we showcase a few typical examples to demonstrate how the geometry of a flux surface
\psi
, particularly the Gaussian curvature, can strongly influence the flux expansion
|\bm{\nabla}\psi|
, the locations of the ridges, and the deviations of coils from planarity. Later in the paper, we present analytical and numerical evidence to explain these observations.

2.2
Non-planarity of coils and curvature of outer flux-surface

Coil complexity is a major cost driver for stellarators. Planar coils are easier to build, but to reproduce magnetic fields that lie on a complex 3D flux-surface shape in a stellarator using only modular coils, non-planarity cannot be avoided. The inboard side of the stellarators is typically where the coils get crowded
(
49
)
. Surface geometry plays a fundamental role in determining the non-planarity as shown in recent works
90
;
77
. In particular, field lines bunch in regions where the
field is stronger, which happens typically close to a ridge and spread out in between because the ridges are of finite extent on generic irrational flux surfaces. As a result, the current coils, which must be transversal to the field lines due to constraints from Biot-Savart, develop a clear S-like excursion.

We show in Fig.
4
this typical S-shaped non-planarity observed in filamentary coils and winding surface-based coils in the inboard side, where the Gaussian curvature
K_{G}\leq 0
. The non-planarity is worse in winding-surface-based coils because, unlike 3D filaments, the winding-surface coils cannot move radially. In contrast, the
K_{G}>0
regions have almost planar filaments. Thus, the
K_{G}=0
curves, which are parabolic
(
50
;
82
)
, seem to separate the almost planar and highly non-planar sections of filaments. Ridges align quite well with parabolic lines and are regions where the magnetic field lines are pinned down.

In the rest of the paper, we focus on addressing the following questions. Why are ridges aligned with a) the magnetic field lines, b) the parabolic lines with
K_{G}=0
c) the minimum
|\bm{\nabla}\psi|
, or equivalently maximum flux expansion regions, and finally d) what
K_{G}\leq 0
implies for coil complexity and how it correlates with the Kappel-Landreman proxy
L_{\nabla B}

3
Quasisymmetry and the optical analogy

Equilibrium 3D magnetic fields, typically described by ideal magnetohydrostatics (MHS), with nested flux surfaces and nonzero rotational transform, are often imposed as a requirement for stellarator design. QS is imposed as a constraint on top of the ideal MHS equilibrium condition. Although currently no rigorous mathematical theory of 3D equilibrium magnetic fields with nested surfaces exists, imposing QS is known
(
85
;
96
)
to ameliorate some of the difficulties of maintaining nestedness associated with ideal MHS.

We further restrict ourselves to vacuum fields. Besides simplicity, vacuum fields are highly relevant to stellarator optimization, since plasma pressure is typically a small fraction of the magnetic pressure. Plasma pressure and currents can always be added as perturbations in our treatment.

3.1
Eikonal formulation for QS

We begin with curl-free or vacuum magnetic fields that are conveniently described using a magnetic scalar potential such that

\displaystyle\bm{B}=\bm{\nabla}\Phi,\quad\Phi=G_{0}\varphi_{B},

(3.1)

Here,
G_{0}
is a constant and
\varphi_{B}
is the Boozer toroidal angle. Since
\bm{B}
must be divergence-free,

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\Phi=0.

(3.2)

We then impose quasisymmetry (QS). QS in its various equivalent forms has been discussed in several references
(
40
;
17
;
91
;
86
;
23
;
95
)
, therefore, we shall keep our discussion to a minimum. QS ensures integrability of guiding center trajectories. However, for this work, the key feature of a quasisymmetric vacuum field is that it must satisfy a nonlinear overdetermined system of PDEs.

Following
23
;
88
, we use the
poloidal flux

\psi
to label the nested QA flux surfaces, which will be our convention for the rest of the paper. For QH, one can choose an analogous helical flux. From
\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\psi=0
, we have the orthogonality condition

\displaystyle\nabla\Phi\cdot\nabla\psi=0.

(3.3)

To make analytical progress while avoiding the nonlinear overdetermination problem of quasisymmetry, we now take a different approach. We now employ Boozer coordinates
(\psi,\vartheta_{B},\varphi_{B})
with poloidal flux
\psi
, rotational transform
\iota(\psi)
, Boozer toroidal angle
\varphi_{B}
and a poloidal or helical Boozer angle
\vartheta_{B}
depending on QA or QH
(
55
)
. The covariant and the contravariant forms of
\bm{B}
are then given by

\displaystyle\bm{B}=\bm{\nabla}\Phi,\quad\bm{B}=\bm{\nabla}\psi\times\bm{\nabla}\alpha,\quad\Phi=G_{0}\varphi_{B},\quad\alpha\equiv\frac{1}{\iota}\vartheta_{B}-\varphi_{B}.

(3.4)

From the two-term form of QS in a vacuum with vanishing toroidal current,

\displaystyle\bm{B}\times\bm{\nabla}\psi\cdot\bm{\nabla}B=G_{0}\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}B\quad\implies\quad\bm{\nabla}\psi\times\bm{\nabla}\vartheta_{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}B=0,

(3.5)

it follows that the hidden symmetry of QA is manifest in the Boozer coordinates with

\displaystyle B=B(\vartheta_{B},\psi).

(3.6)

Hence, a quasisymmetric vacuum field satisfies the following the eikonal equation
(
19
)

\displaystyle|\bm{\nabla}\Phi|^{2}=B^{2}(\vartheta_{B},\psi).

(3.7)

Although the exact form of
B
is not readily available, progress can be made due to the fact that in almost all numerically optimized designs with typical aspect ratios, the change of
B
from the magnetic axis to the outermost surface is a small fraction (
\lessapprox 1/10
) of its constant value on the axis. Thus, to build intuition, the exact knowledge of
B
is not as crucial as the knowledge of the type of QS. Our exact results in Section
5
do not need these approximations.

With the eikonal equation in hand, we are in a position to discuss the optical analogy, which will be our main tool to understand how
B
and geometry interact and possible singularities of the vacuum QA system.

The behavior of magnetic field lines and equilibrium quantities of interest such as the field strength and

being small as discussed in Section

In Section
2.1
, we pointed out several distinctive features of vacuum QA configurations near ridges. Building on the optical analogy, we now demonstrate how the magnetic fields near the maximum of
B
can lead to the formation of ridges in regions where the Gaussian curvature is non-positive. We also show why

4.1
Magnetic field lines and field strength near magnetic ridges

We now provide a simple proof that the magnetic field lines must be tangential to the magnetic ridges. Let the ridge (which acts locally as the boundary of the plasma) be defined by two smooth surfaces,
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
, which face the plasma on one side and intersect each other along a curve
\bm{R}
, the ridge. The angle between the normals of
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
is usually less than 180 degrees on the plasma-facing side. Since
\bm{B}
is tangent to
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
, its component normal to these surfaces vanishes. As a result,
\bm{B}
vanishes in two different directions orthogonal to
\bm{R}
. Unless
\bm{B}
also vanishes in the direction of the ridge, the latter coincides with a field line.

In other words, the field cannot cross
\bm{R}
unless its component parallel to
\bm{R}
vanishes. On the other hand, if the parallel component vanishes, then the field can cross
\bm{R}
. An example of such behavior is a tokamak with an X-point but no toroidal field.

It also follows from continuity alone that the gradient of any flux function
\psi
vanishes at a sharp ridge
\bm{R}
. The latter can again be thought of as an intersection between two surfaces,
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
. The vector
\bm{\nabla}\psi
is orthogonal to both surfaces and therefore points in different directions on
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
. It can only be continuous at
\bm{R}
by vanishing there.

A similar continuity based argument can be used to prove that a ridge in a field with QS is straight if it is not aligned with the symmetry direction. Starting from the two-term form of QS
|\bm{\nabla}\psi|\bm{B}\times\bm{\hat{n}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}B=G_{0}\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}B
, where
\bm{\hat{n}}
is a unit vector normal to the flux surface, we observe that
\bm{\nabla}\psi
vanishing on the ridge forces
\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}B
to vanish since
G_{0}
is nonzero. Thus, QS implies that
B=|\bm{B}|
is constant in two different directions: along the field and in the symmetry direction. At the ridge,
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}
thus vanishes in any direction tangential to
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
, i.e. in three independent directions, which implies that
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}=0
on
\bm{R}
. Since the curvature vector of the field line following
\bm{R}
is equal to
\bm{\nabla_{\perp}}(\ln|\bm{B}|)
, it must be straight.

These results hold for sharp ridges of non-zero length. If
\bm{R}
is only sharp in one point, then its curvature vanishes in that point, making
\bm{R}
locally straight. The differential geometric ramifications of these results are discussed in Appendix
B
.

4.2
Flux surface geometry near ridges

We have argued that continuity implies that a quasisymmetric
B
should be approximately constant
B_{0}
near a sharp ridge, which implies that the ridge must be locally straight. We elaborate in this Section the far-reaching impact on the behavior of local field lines and flux surfaces due to this simple observation.

Employing the standard Cartesian coordinates
(X,Y,Z)
with unit vectors
(\bm{\hat{x}},\bm{\hat{y}},\bm{\hat{z}})
, we now explore the implications of the eikonal equation with a constant
B
on the geometry. Let
\Phi=B_{0}\varphi
be the scalar potential such that
\bm{B}/B_{0}=\nabla\varphi
. The eikonal equation reads

\displaystyle\varphi_{,X}^{2}+\varphi_{,Y}^{2}+\varphi_{,Z}^{2}=1.

(4.1)

For constant
B
, the ray equation (
3.11
) implies that the rays are the constant momentum vector
\bm{p}\equiv\nabla\varphi
, which can be chosen to be

\displaystyle\bm{p}=p_{0}\bm{\hat{x}}+q_{0}\bm{\hat{y}}\pm\sqrt{1-p_{0}^{2}-q_{0}^{2}}\bm{\hat{z}}.

(4.2)

Here,
(p_{0},q_{0})
are two constant parameters that serve as initial conditions for the ray tracing. The wavefront
\varphi
is given by the 3 parameter
(\varphi_{0},p_{0},q_{0})
family of planes
\varphi(X,Y,Z)=u(X,Y,Z;p_{0},q_{0},\varphi_{0})
with

\displaystyle u(X,Y,Z;p_{0},q_{0},\varphi_{0})=\varphi_{0}+Xp_{0}+Yq_{0}\pm Z\sqrt{1-p_{0}^{2}-q_{0}^{2}}.

(4.3)

In the simplest form, as discussed in
(
25
)
, constant
B
implies that in the optical analogy picture, rays (
4.2
) are straight lines and the elementary wavefronts (
4.3
) of the complete integral are planes. We then utilize the concept of a
complete integral

(
25
;
30
;
27
)
and
general solution
through formation of envelopes. Treating (
4.3
) as a complete integral that depends on three arbitrary constant parameters
(p_{0},q_{0},\varphi_{0})
, we can obtain a general solution as envelopes of these elementary planes such that the rays are still straight lines, but the wavefronts themselves need not be planar, for example, they could be locally spherical.

To obtain less trivial solutions of the eikonal equation (
3.7
), we now allow small
\epsilon
deviations from a constant
B
. The constants
(p_{0},q_{0},\varphi_{0})
appearing in the elementary solution may then be promoted to functions of two ray labels
(\psi,\alpha)
, chosen to be constant along the rays. We identify these labels with Clebsch variables, so that
\bm{B}=\nabla\psi\times\nabla\alpha
is tangent to the ray/field-line direction. We then obtain the envelope by eliminating the three quantities
p_{0},q_{0},\varphi_{0}
from the three simultaneous equations

\displaystyle\varphi(X,Y,Z)=u(X,Y,Z;p_{0}(\psi,\alpha),q_{0}(\psi,\alpha),\varphi_{0}(\psi,\alpha)),\quad\partial_{\psi}u=0,\quad\partial_{\alpha}u=0

(4.4)

The envelope equations, in principle, allow the determination of
\psi,\alpha,\varphi
as functions of only
(X,Y,Z)
through the standard methods of ray tracing. For
\epsilon\neq 0
, an explicit expression for the complete integral exists only for a handful of special cases, but nonetheless, the envelope idea persists, and can be constructed perturbatively if needed.

The rays, which are tangential to the flux surfaces, are all straight lines as
\epsilon\to 0
given by (
4.2
). A stringent geometrical constraint on the flux surfaces arises from this fact alone. From differential geometry (e.g., Chapter IV from
42
), we know that any straight line on a surface must be an asymptotic curve since the normal curvature vanishes identically. Asymptotic curves can exist only on surfaces with non-positive
K_{G}
. A surface containing a one-parameter family of straight line segments is a
ruled surface
, which has
K_{G}\leq 0
. These line segments are asymptotic curves and acts as local rulings wherever the surface is regular. The important special case with zero
K_{G}
everywhere is called
developable
since it can be developed into a plane. Since a torus must have both positive and negative
K_{G}
, the straight field lines must be restricted to the hyperbolic
K_{G}\leq 0
part of the torus, which for QA is the inboard side. For
\epsilon\neq 0
, the field lines remain approximately asymptotic curves even though they are not strictly straight lines anymore. We discuss this more in Section
4.3
.

Developable ruled surfaces may possess singular space curves where neighboring rulings approach each other, such as
edges of regression
or
cuspidal edges
. Near such curves, the surface normal curvature can be arbitrarily large. Although a generic non-developable ruled surface does not possess such an edge of regression, it has a related regular curve, the
line of striction
, defined as the locus of closest approach of neighboring rulings. In a singular developable limit, the line of striction approaches the edge of regression.

These sharp edges can be obtained from the complete integral as envelopes of the rays and are aptly called
singular solutions
. We will show later that these singular solutions correspond to the sharp ridges on the flux surfaces. Thus, near a sharp ridge where the hyperbolic side of the flux surface is well approximated by a ruled surface, the ridge may be interpreted as a limiting striction/regression curve near
K_{G}\to 0
.

Choosing
\psi(X,Y,Z),\alpha(X,Y,Z)
as flux surface and field line labels, we can obtain the singular solutions as envelopes of
\alpha
curves on a fixed flux surface by solving

\displaystyle\varphi(X,Y,Z)=u,\quad\partial_{\psi}u=0,\quad\partial_{\alpha}u=0,\quad\partial_{\alpha}^{2}u=0.

(4.5)

The singular solution (
4.5
) does not necessarily exist for all geometries since
\partial_{\alpha}^{2}u
might not vanish anywhere on the surface. However, if a solution exists, it represents a space-curve along which different field line labels
\alpha
touch each other, forming an envelope. Clearly,
|\bm{\nabla}\alpha|\to\infty
near the envelope. Since
\bm{B}=\bm{\nabla}\psi\times\bm{\nabla}\alpha
must be finite, and
\nabla\psi
and
\nabla\alpha
are not parallel,
|\bm{\nabla}\psi|\to 0
near the singular solutions. In optics, such singular curves are identified as the envelope of rays forming ray caustics. Since magnetic field lines can not actually touch each other, the singular solution, if it exists, represents a limiting curve, beyond which smooth solutions can not exist.

4.3
Field line curvature near ridges

In the last Section, we showed that an important consequence of the optical analogy is the direct correlation between the field line curvature, gradients of
B
, and the Gaussian curvature of the flux surface. In QS, one can show that near the magnetic axis, where
B
is essentially a constant, the vanishing of the magnetic axis curvature signals the breakdown of QS
(
55
;
54
;
89
)
. Thus, the behavior of the critical points of
B
and field line curvature deserves special attention in the study of singularities of the vacuum QA system. In the following, we discuss the constraints on field line curvature imposed by QS.

The field line curvature
\bm{\kappa}=\bm{\hat{t}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\bm{\hat{t}},\;\bm{\hat{t}}\equiv\bm{B}/B,
is determined completely by the gradients of
B
since for vacuum fields
(
40
)

\displaystyle\bm{\kappa}=\frac{\bm{\nabla}B}{B}-\frac{\bm{\hat{t}}}{B^{2}}\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}B.

(4.6)

By definition,
\bm{\kappa}
has no component along
\bm{\hat{t}}
. The other two components,
\kappa_{n},\kappa_{g}
along
\bm{\hat{n}}=\bm{\nabla}\psi/|\bm{\nabla}\psi|
and
\bm{\hat{b}}\equiv\bm{\hat{t}}\times\bm{\hat{n}}
, called the normal and the geodesic components, are given by
(
40
)

\displaystyle\bm{\kappa}=\kappa_{n}\bm{\hat{n}}+\kappa_{g}\bm{\hat{b}},\quad\kappa_{n}=\frac{\bm{\nabla}\psi\cdot\bm{\nabla}B}{B|\bm{\nabla}\psi|},\quad\kappa_{g}=\frac{\bm{B}\times\bm{\nabla}\psi\cdot\bm{\nabla}B}{B^{2}|\bm{\nabla}\psi|}.

(4.7)

Using the two-term form of vacuum QS (
3.5
), we can rewrite the geodesic curvature, which is proportional to
\bm{B}\times\bm{\nabla}\psi\cdot\bm{\nabla}B
in terms of
\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}B
such that

\displaystyle\kappa_{g}=\frac{G_{0}\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}B}{B^{2}|\nabla\psi|}.

(4.8)

Since
|\nabla\psi|
becomes small as we approach a sharp magnetic ridge, continuity forces
\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}B
to be small. Thus,
B
is close to a critical point of
B
, either a maxima
B_{M}(\psi)
or minima
B_{m}(\psi)
or a saddle of
B
. Away from the sharp ridge but in its immediate neighborhood where
|\nabla\psi|
is no longer zero, continuity implies that
\kappa_{g}
is still zero due to the numerator. The field lines are then locally geodesic. The local geodesic nature is consistent with the variational form or Fermat’s principle for the optical analogy, which shows that rays will minimize distances along their path. Configurations where all field lines are geodesics are either isodynamic with
B=B(\psi)
(
72
;
14
)
or QP with
B=B(\varphi_{B},\psi)

(
100
;
60
)
. For QS, the critical field lines and the field lines neighboring them are approximately isodynamic. However, if the
|\nabla\psi|
term in the denominator simultaneously vanishes, the geodesic curvature can still be nonzero near critical points of
B
and a more careful limit must be taken.

Even if a critical field line has zero geodesic curvature, it is not necessarily a straight line since the normal curvature of the field line at that point is, in general, nonzero, and can only vanish under further stringent restrictions imposed on
B
. It can be shown
(
87
)
that for vacuum fields
B^{\prime}_{M}(\psi)\geq 0
, and
B
can only attain its maximum on the boundary. For QS, the minima and maxima are typically symmetric, hence,
B_{m}^{\prime}(\psi)\leq 0
. Therefore, for generic QS systems, the radial derivative
\partial_{\psi}B
, and hence the normal curvature
\kappa_{n}
, do not vanish, even if the geodesic curvature does near the critical points of
B
.

In the immediate vicinity of the sharp ridge, where
|\bm{\nabla}\psi|
is not exactly zero, which is also the case with all numerically optimized compact QA configurations, we must have
B_{M}^{\prime}(\psi)\to 0
to have
\kappa_{n}\to 0
. The vanishing of
B_{M}^{\prime}
is in sharp contrast to the near-axis behavior where
B_{M}(\psi)/B_{0}=1+\overline{\eta}\sqrt{2\psi}+O(\psi)
, where
\overline{\eta}
is a positive constant with
\overline{\eta}R_{a}\sim 1
. Near the magnetic axis, where
|\bm{\nabla}\psi|\sim\sqrt{2\psi}
,
\kappa_{n}\sim|\bm{\nabla}\psi|B_{M}^{\prime}(\psi)/B_{0}\sim\overline{\eta}>0
and
\kappa_{n}R_{a}\sim 1
. Although this behavior is expected near the axis, it holds quite well in the full volume
(
91
;
95
)
for 2-field period QA configurations such as the Landreman-Paul precise QA, which lacks a sharp 3D ridge. Thus, for sharp ridges to exist,
B_{M}
must deviate significantly from the near-axis prediction.

Indeed from Figure
15
we find that all configurations with
n_{fp}>2
show significant deviation from the near-axis behavior. On the inboard side with
B\approx B_{M}
we find
B_{M}^{\prime}(\psi)\to 0
. Thus, field lines must be approximately asymptotic curves for which
\kappa_{n}\to 0
. The fact that asymptotic lines must lie in the negative Gaussian curvature region of the flux surface, implies that the region of 3D ridges with
B\approx B_{M}
must be contained in the overall
K_{G}\leq 0
region of the flux surface. A systematic proof of these statements are given in Section
5.1
and in Appendix
B
.

We now contrast the 3D sharp ridges with the axisymmetric X-point. Axisymmetry constrains the X point to be a planar circle with zero torsion and normal curvature typically of the order of the inverse major radius
R_{a}
such that
\kappa_{n}R_{a}\sim 1
. Thus,
\kappa_{n}R_{a}
is non-zero along a sharp axisymmetric ridge, in general, and can only vanish if the ridge is exactly on the top and the bottom of the torus, where the Gaussian curvature is identically zero on the parabolic curve.

Finally, we point out that the appearance of these straight-line geodesic magnetic fields near the maximum
B
is a characteristic signature of the failure of QS. From the ray equation, we find that near points of vanishing field line curvature close to
B_{M}
,
d^{2}\bm{x}/d\mathcal{T}^{2}=0
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\bm{x}=\bm{x}_{0}+\bm{x}_{1}\mathcal{T},\quad\mathcal{T}=\ell/B_{M},

(4.9)

where
\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{1}
are constant vectors. Since
d\bm{x}/d\mathcal{T}=\bm{B}
,
\bm{x}_{1}
points in the direction of the local
\bm{B}
. The key reason for the failure of QS near these straight field lines is that as
|\bm{\nabla}\psi|\to 0,
the field line
\bm{B}=G_{0}\bm{\nabla}\varphi_{B}
and the QS vector
\bm{u}=\partial\bm{X}/\partial\varphi_{B}
must align since
\bm{B}\times\bm{u}=\bm{\nabla}\psi

(
23
;
86
)
. However, this alignment cannot work when the rotational transform is nonzero because
\bm{B}
must have both toroidal and poloidal components in Boozer coordinates, whereas
\bm{u}
is purely toroidal.

We have seen earlier in Figures
3
and
3
that there are points on the ridges, where simultaneously the principal curvature of the surface
\kappa_{2}
is a maximum, and
\kappa_{1}
is zero, such that the local Gaussian curvature is also zero. The ridges align quite well with the magnetic field lines and are local minima of
|\bm{\nabla}\psi|
. Furthermore, these points also align very well with the maxima of
B
. Near such points, the field lines are essentially straight lines, which we demonstrate numerically in Figure
11
. Essentially, the top view of any compact QA device should look like an
n_{fp}
-sided polygon on the inboard side, where
n_{fp}
is the number of field periods, and the sharp ridges define the sides of the polygon. Clear examples of the approximately polygonal shape in the inboard side of the flux surfaces can be seen in Figure 2 from
(
81
)
, Figure 1 from
(
93
)
, Figures 1 and 3 from
67
and Figure
11
in this paper.

5
Exact results on the Grad-B tensor and its eigenvalues: implications for ridges and coils

We present two exact results that show how ridges and coils are connected to the determinant of the
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}
tensor.
In Section
5.1
, we formalize the optical analogy into concrete statements connecting the
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}
tensor and the Hessian of the flux surface
\bm{\nabla}\bm{\nabla}\psi
. In Section
5.2
we prove that all filamentary coils must lie on zero level sets of the determinant of the
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}
tensor.

5.1
Quasisymmetric ridges must lie on zero-determinant manifold of the magnetic gradient tensor

We have so far relied on the optical analogy and the continuity to understand the structure of ridges. In this Section, we obtain exact results that show the deep connections and the parallels between ridges and current filaments.

The only assumption we make is that a quasisymmetric vacuum field with nested flux surfaces can be found. More explicitly, we assume that
\bm{B}=\bm{\nabla}\Phi
is a vacuum field with flux function
\psi
such that
\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\psi=0
and level sets of
\psi
foliate the toroidal volume. However, as discussed in Section
4.1
we must consider the possibility of
|\nabla\psi|
to vanish on a sharp ridge. The vanishing on
|\nabla\psi|
being a singular limit, care must be exercised in the description of sharp magnetic ridges. We avoid indeterminacy of the normal vector
\bm{\hat{n}}\equiv\nabla\psi/|\nabla\psi|
and the elements of the first and second fundamental forms of the flux surface by allowing
|\nabla\psi|
to be small but nonzero. The necessary mathematical details are provided in Appendix
C
.

From the gradient of the condition
\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\psi=0
or equivalently
\nabla\Phi\cdot\nabla\psi=0
we obtain

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\psi+\bm{\nabla}\bm{\nabla}\psi\cdot\bm{B}=0,

(5.1)

which connects components of the
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}
and the
\nabla\nabla\psi
tensors. It is convenient to use the orthonormal Darboux frame
(\bm{\hat{n}},\bm{\hat{b}},\bm{\hat{t}})
where

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{t}}\equiv\frac{\bm{B}}{B},\quad\bm{\hat{n}}\equiv\frac{\bm{\nabla}\psi}{|\bm{\nabla}\psi|},\quad\bm{\hat{b}}\equiv\bm{\hat{t}}\times\bm{\hat{n}},

(5.2)

to rewrite (
5.1
) as

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\hat{n}}\frac{|\bm{\nabla}\psi|}{B}+\bm{\nabla}\bm{\nabla}\psi\cdot\bm{\hat{t}}=0

(5.3)

We now dot (
5.3
) with
\bm{\hat{t}}
, use the fact that
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}
is symmetric for vacuum fields, and utilize the curvature identities given in (
4.7
). We find the fundamental relation

\displaystyle\kappa_{n}|\bm{\nabla}\psi|+\bm{\hat{t}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\bm{\nabla}\psi\cdot\bm{\hat{t}}=0,

(5.4)

between the field line normal curvature
\kappa_{n}
and the
\bm{\hat{t}}\bm{\hat{t}}
component of the Hessian
\bm{\nabla}\bm{\nabla}\psi
. Since
\kappa_{n}=\bm{\hat{n}}\cdot\nabla\ln{B}
for vacuum fields, (
5.4
) is a direct constraint on the radial gradient of
B
. Consequently, if
\kappa_{n}
or equivalently
\bm{\hat{n}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}B
vanishes,
\bm{\hat{t}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\bm{\nabla}\psi\cdot\bm{\hat{t}}
must also vanish, which means that, by definition, the field lines are asymptotic curves with
\bm{\hat{t}}
denoting the asymptotic direction on the flux surface. Note that
\bm{\hat{t}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\kappa_{n}\neq 0
in general. However, if
\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}B=0
and
\bm{\hat{n}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}B
vanish simultaneously, then
\kappa_{n}=0,\bm{\hat{t}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\kappa_{n}=0
.

Next we represent
\bm{\nabla}\bm{\nabla}\psi/|\nabla\psi|
and
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}/B
by
3\times 3
matrices of the form
a_{ij}
and
c_{ij}
respectively, where
i,j=1,2,3
correspond to the Darboux frame directions (
\bm{\hat{n}},\bm{\hat{b}},\bm{\hat{t}}
). The following components of the
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}/B
tensor can be deduced from the definitions of the field line curvature components given in (
4.7
),

\displaystyle c_{13}=\kappa_{n},\quad c_{23}=\kappa_{g},\quad c_{33}=\frac{\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}B}{B^{2}}.

(5.5)

Further,
c_{11}+c_{22}+c_{33}=0
from the divergence-free nature of the magnetic field.

The condition (
5.3
) connects
c_{1j}
to
a_{j3}
through

\displaystyle c_{1j}+a_{j3}=0,\quad j=1,2,3.

(5.6)

In particular,
a_{33}=-\kappa_{n}
and
c_{13}=\kappa_{n}
, both of which vanish in the limit of vanishing
\kappa_{n}
. The Gaussian curvature of the flux surface
K_{G}
is equal to
(a_{22}a_{33}-a_{23}^{2})
. The limit
\kappa_{n}\to 0
,
a_{33}\to 0
forces

\displaystyle K_{G}\to-\left(a_{23}\right)^{2},

(5.7)

which shows that
K_{G}
is locally negative definite. The parabolic curve
K_{G}=0
is then given by
a_{23}\to 0
. Further, in this limit,
c_{12}\to 0
from (
5.6
).

We now turn to
\mathscr{D}_{3}=\text{Det}\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}
. As discussed in Appendix (
B
), near a sharp ridge both
K_{G}
and
\kappa_{n}
go to zero simultaneously. Therefore, near a sharp ridge,

\displaystyle(\text{Det}\bm{\nabla}\bm{B})/B=-c_{11}((c_{11}+c_{33})c_{33}+c_{23}^{2}).

(5.8)

Thus,
\mathscr{D}_{3}\neq 0
in general near ridges. This is where QS plays a crucial role. QS connects the geodesic curvature
\kappa_{g}
to
\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}B
as shown in (
4.8
). Due to QS, the components
c_{23},c_{33}
are no longer independent but are both proportional to
\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}B
. Consequently, Det
(\bm{\nabla}\bm{B})
is also proportional to
\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}B
from (
5.5
) and (
5.8
)

\displaystyle\frac{\text{Det}\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}}{B}=-c_{11}\frac{\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}B}{B^{2}}\left(c_{11}+\frac{\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\nabla}B}{B^{2}}\left(1+\left(\frac{G_{0}}{|\bm{\nabla}\psi|}\right)^{2}\right)\right),

(5.9)

and vanishes near
B=B_{M}
on a ridge.

We note that several components of the
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}/B
tensor namely
c_{12},c_{13},c_{23},c_{33}
vanish near the ridge where
\kappa_{n}\to 0,\kappa_{g}\to 0
. However,
c_{11}=-c_{22}
does not necessarily vanish. Therefore, the Frobenius norm
|\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}|^{2}_{F}
expressed in terms of the metric elements as
B^{2}\sum_{ij}c_{ij}
, stays finite as long as
c_{11}\neq 0
. The subharmonic nature of
B

(
87
;
99
)
sheds light on the degeneracy of the
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}
tensor. From the Laplacian of
B^{2}
in Cartesian coordinates and
\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{B}=0
, we get

\displaystyle(1/2)\nabla^{2}B^{2}=\sum_{i}\bm{\nabla}B_{i}\cdot\bm{\nabla}B_{i}\geq 0,

(5.10)

which implies that
B^{2}
is subharmonic and that it can not only attain its maximum on the outer flux surface boundary and not in the interior. Near the magnetic ridge, where
B
approaches a constant both in radial and angular directions,
\partial_{X_{i}}B_{i}
is small. Here,
X_{i}
denotes local coordinates in the
(\bm{\hat{n}},\bm{\hat{b}},\bm{\hat{t}})
frame. Thus,
\bm{\nabla}B_{i}
and the Frobenius norm vanish if
\bm{\nabla}X_{i}
stays finite for all
i
. However, as we discussed in Section
4.2

\bm{\nabla}X_{i}
need not stay finite since
|\bm{\nabla}\psi|\to 0
imply divergence of
|\nabla\alpha|
for finite
B
near a ridge.

We now discuss the significance of the
K_{G}\leq 0
condition and limits on its applicability. As discussed in Section
4.1
and Appendix
B
, the vanishing of
K_{G}
related to the vanishing of
\kappa_{n}
holds only when the sharp ridge is not aligned with the symmetry direction. We thereby exclude axisymmetric or exactly helically symmetric ridges in exactly symmetric devices. Since a torus must have
K_{G}
of both signs, (
5.7
) cannot be satisfied on the whole surface, but is locally possible only near an extremum of
B
. Near a sharp magnetic ridge,
\kappa_{2}
blows up while
\kappa_{1}\approx\kappa_{n}
goes to zero. Thus,
K_{G}=\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}
is zero along the ridge but varies considerably across the ridge. Since field lines must cover the whole flux surface ergodically for generic irrational rotational transform the
K_{G}\leq 0
imposes a natural barrier for the field lines to cross the sharp ridge on the flux surface. We demonstrate this barrier for field lines clearly in Figure
12
for a
n_{fp}=4
QA device that has a sharp ridge. In contrast, the
n_{fp}=2
case shown in Figure
13
do not show a clear barrier since its ridge is not sharp. We quantify the sharpness of ridges through the eigenvalue of the
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}
tensor as discussed in Section
5.3
.

From the differential geometry point of view, the parabolic curve
K_{G}=0
is an envelope of the asymptotic curves
\kappa_{n}=0
. Therefore, the simultaneous vanishing of
K_{G}
and
\kappa_{n}
implies that neighboring field lines with
\kappa_{n}=0
must form a ray caustic or a singular envelope of rays near
K_{G}=0
. From the negative definiteness of
K_{G}
in (
5.7
), we can approximate the flux surface locally as a ruled surface. The field lines that are asymptotic curves on this surface provide the natural ruling. The rulings approach each other as
K_{G}\to 0
leading to a singular line of striction as anticipated from the optical analogy in Section
4.2
. Finally, we note that from the Beltrami-Enneper theorem,
\tau^{2}+K_{G}=0
for asymptotic curves. Thus, (
5.7
) implies that
a_{23}
must be equal in magnitude to the torsion
\tau
of the field line. Since
K_{G}
varies a lot across the ridge, the torsion of nearby field lines must be large.

5.2
Filamentary coils must lie on zero-determinant manifold of the magnetic gradient tensor

For vacuum fields, the eigenvalues of the
3\times 3
, real, traceless, symmetric
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}
tensor must satisfy a cubic equation of the form

\displaystyle\lambda^{3}+\mathcal{P}\lambda+\mathcal{Q}=0.

(5.11)

The coefficient of
\lambda^{2}
, given by
\mbox{Tr}(\bm{\nabla}\bm{B})
, vanishes identically thanks to the divergence-free property of
\bm{B}
. For vacuum configurations, the other two quantities
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
, which depend on the details of the specific type of equilibrium, are related to the determinant and the
L_{\nabla B}
metric
(
49
)

as

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}=-\mbox{Det}(\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}),\quad L_{\nabla B}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-\mathcal{P}/B^{2}}}.

(5.12)

The
L_{\nabla B}
metric is defined in terms of the Frobenius norm as

L_{\nabla\bm{B}}\equiv\frac{B\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{|\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}|_{F}^{2}}}.\quad\frac{\left|\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}\right|^{2}_{F}}{B^{2}}\equiv\sum_{ij}c_{ij}^{2}.

(5.13)

The discriminant of the cubic
\Delta_{d}=-(4\mathcal{P}^{3}+27\mathcal{Q}^{2})
must be positive for three real roots. In the limit of
\mathcal{Q}\to 0
, near the locus of vanishing 3D determinant
\mathscr{D}_{3}=0
, it follows from (
5.11
) that the eigenvalues are
(-\sqrt{-\mathcal{P}},0,\sqrt{-\mathcal{P}})
or equivalently
(-B/L_{\nabla B},0,B/L_{\nabla B})
. Therefore, if the current source is such that
\mathscr{D}_{3}=0
, which coincides with the zero eigenvalue, the magnitude of the non-zero eigenvalues is directly related to the
L_{\nabla B}
metric
(
49
)
.

We now show that indeed the singularities of vacuum fields originating from filamentary coils are always tangential to surfaces defined by
\mathscr{D}_{3}(x,y,z)=0
with necessary details in Appendix
E
.
The essential idea is to use a Frenet-Serret frame along the filament and approximate the Biot-Savart integral via
local induction approximation
(LIA), which is used extensively in the theory of vortex filaments
(
92
;
24
)
.

We represent the filamentary coil by a space curve
\bm{r}_{c}(\ell)
parameterized by its arclength
\ell
, and employ the standard orthogonal Mercier coordinate system
(\rho,\omega,\ell)

(
61
;
99
;
97
;
47
)
equipped with the orthonormal unit vectors
(\bm{\hat{t}}=\bm{r}_{c}^{\prime}(\ell),\bm{\hat{\rho}}=\bm{\nabla}\rho,\bm{\hat{\omega}}=\bm{\nabla}\omega)
. We use the following notation:
C=\mu_{0}I/2\pi
is the strength of the current filament,
\rho
is the distance from the filament,
\theta=\omega-\int\tau_{c}d\ell
is the angle between the normal
\bm{\hat{n}}
and the radial unit vector
\bm{\hat{\rho}}
, and
\kappa_{c}(\ell),\tau_{c}(\ell)
are the curvature and torsion of the filament. LIA in these coordinates is a formal expansion in
\kappa_{c}\rho
, identical to the near-axis expansion with the magnetic axis replaced by the coil.

Within the LIA, the field near the current filament is

\displaystyle\bm{B}=B_{\rho}\bm{\hat{\rho}}+B_{\omega}\bm{\hat{\omega}}+O((\kappa_{c}\rho)^{0}),

(5.14)

\displaystyle B_{\rho}=-\frac{C}{2\rho}\left(\kappa_{c}\rho\ln{\kappa_{c}\rho}\right)\sin\theta,\quad B_{\omega}=\frac{C}{\rho}\left(1+\frac{\kappa_{c}\rho}{2}\cos\theta\left(-\ln{\kappa_{c}\rho}+1\right)\right).

The
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}
tensor in the
(\bm{\hat{t}},\bm{\hat{\rho}},\bm{\hat{\omega}})
basis is of the form

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}-\kappa_{c}B_{n}\quad 0\;\\
\quad 0\qquad\mathscr{H}_{2}\;\end{pmatrix}\quad\mathscr{H}_{2}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{\rho}B_{\rho}\qquad\qquad\partial_{\rho}B_{\omega}\\
\frac{1}{\rho}(-B_{\omega}+\partial_{\omega}B_{\rho})\quad\frac{1}{\rho}(\partial_{\omega}B_{\omega}+B_{\rho})\end{pmatrix},

(5.15)

where the normal component in the Frenet frame,
B_{n}=\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\hat{n}}
, reads

\displaystyle B_{n}\equiv\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\hat{n}}=-C\frac{\sin\theta}{\rho}\left(1+\frac{\kappa_{c}\rho}{2}\cos\theta\right).

(5.16)

The traceless condition of
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}
implies

\displaystyle\partial_{\rho}B_{\rho}+\frac{1}{\rho}(\partial_{\omega}B_{\omega}+B_{\rho})-\kappa_{c}B_{n}=0.

(5.17)

The largest terms in the left side of (
5.17
) from (
5.14
) are
O(1/\rho\ln{\kappa_{c}\rho})
, which can be seen to cancel out, leaving only
O((\kappa_{c}\rho)^{0})
terms. Thus,
\bm{B}
is divergenceless to the order of accuracy of (
5.14
).

The determinant
\mathscr{D}_{3}=\mbox{Det}(\bm{\nabla}\bm{B})
obtained from (
5.15
) is

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{3}=-\kappa_{c}B_{n}\mathscr{D}_{2},\quad\mathscr{D}_{2}\equiv\mbox{Det}\mathscr{H}_{2}=\frac{1}{\rho}\left(\partial_{\rho}B_{\rho}\left(\partial_{\omega}B_{\omega}+B_{\rho}\right)-\partial_{\rho}B_{\omega}\left(-B_{\omega}+\partial_{\omega}B_{\rho}\right)\right).

(5.18)

The term represented by
\mathscr{D}_{2}
is the determinant of the 2D Hessian
\mathscr{H}_{2}
.

At a distance
\rho>0
, we observe that
\mathscr{D}_{3}
vanishes if
\kappa_{c}=0
or
B_{n}=0
.
\mathscr{D}_{2}
is negative definite due to the traceless condition and is
O(1/\rho^{2})
. Since
\kappa_{c}\neq 0
for a generic filament,
\mathscr{D}_{3}=0
and
B_{n}=0
are satisfied when
\sin\theta=0
. Thus, for
\theta=0
and
\theta=\pi
,
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}
is rank 2, with the null direction coinciding with the direction of the coil. In the limit
\rho\to 0^{+}
with
\sin\theta
fixed at zero, the null direction aligns everywhere with the direction of the tangent to the coil, implying that the coil itself must lie on a
\mathscr{D}_{3}=0
surface. We note here that as we approach the
\mathscr{D}_{3}\to 0
surface, the two non-zero eigenvalues of the
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}
tensor grow large as the inverse of
\rho
. For a rigorous
\rho\to 0
limit we show in Appendix
E
that a finite and normalized determinant,
\bar{\mathscr{D}_{3}}
, can be defined instead of
\mathscr{D}_{3}
, which does not alter any of our conclusions. Finally, we note that due to the large
1/\rho
terms in the expression for
\bm{B}
near a given filament, the LIA with a single coil is still an excellent approximation for multiple coils.

5.3
Ridges, coils and the
L_{\nabla B}
metric

The framework we have developed provides crucial insight not only for coils but also for flux surface geometry and properties of the magnetic field lines near the regions of vanishing
|\bm{\nabla}\psi|
. The key to understanding the geometry dependence is through the behavior of the
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}
tensor and its eigenvalues. For coils, the eigenvalues determine the
L_{\nabla B}
metric for the coil-plasma distance. For flux surfaces with sharp features like X points or ridges that can potentially serve as divertors, the eigenvalues control the local behavior of the magnetic field lines. In particular, we can quantify the sharpness of the ridge in terms of the eigenvalues of the
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}
tensor.

We now discuss the
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}
tensor and its eigenvalues in three cases of practical importance: the axisymmetric tokamak X point, the O and X points of an island divertor of a stellarator, and, finally, the 3D sharp ridges we have discussed thus far. The details are provided in Appendix
D
. In each of these cases, we restrict ourselves to regions near O or X points or near ridges, with a common feature that
|\bm{\nabla}\psi|
is locally a minimum and can tend to zero. We show that the eigenvalues of
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}
in these three cases can be markedly different.

Since we compare the eigenvalues from different geometries, it is convenient to normalize the equilibrium quantities with the major radius
R_{a}
and a reference magnetic field strength
B_{a}
. From here on, we assume normalized quantities, such that a regular eigenvalue corresponds to
\lambda_{0}B_{a}/R_{a}
, where
\lambda_{0}
is order unity constant.

We start with the axisymmetric case. As shown in Appendix
D.2
, the normalized eigenvalues are regular if the second derivatives of the flux surface label
\psi
are bounded. However, if the second derivatives of
\psi
are large, which can happen due to strong axisymmetric shaping, both
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
can be large, leading to large eigenvalues. Thus,
L_{\nabla B}
can be small near areas of strong flux surface shaping.

Next, we consider the case of a regular O or X point generated by a closed field line of an island chain. We show in Appendix
D.3
that in this case, the normalized eigenvalues are always regular. Locally, the flux surface is always a rotating ellipse or hyperbola whose rotation rate and elongation are determined by their values near the closed field line. Thus, the second-order derivatives of
\psi
can not be arbitrarily large. As a result, regular X points do not enforce any restrictions on
L_{\nabla B}
.

Finally, we consider the 3D sharp ridges. As shown in Appendix
D.4
, the normalized
\mathcal{Q}
goes to zero near the ridge, which is generally not true in either the axisymmetric or the closed field line cases. The normalized
\mathcal{P}
is related to the second-order derivatives of
\Phi
through its 2D Hessian. Thus,
L_{\nabla B}=1/\sqrt{|K_{G}|}
near the ridges. Just like the axisymmetric case, large second-order derivatives of
\Phi
lead to large values of
K_{G}
and hence small
L_{\nabla B}
. However, on the ridge where
K_{G}=0
,
L_{\nabla B}
can diverge. We can obtain the same result based on the subharmonic property of
B
discussed in Section
5.1
. It is indeed possible for the
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}
tensor to become degenerate in the vicinity of the ridge, when
B
approaches a constant on the outer flux surface in both angles and radial variables. In this degenerate case, the Frobenius norm is small, which implies that
L_{\nabla B}
diverges. Therefore, the
L_{\nabla B}
metric must be handled with care near the ridges.

The three cases discussed above illustrate the interesting role played by the second-order derivatives of the flux surface label and Gaussian curvature in the case of axisymmetry with strong axisymmetric flux surface shaping and 3D ridges, but not in the case of regular O and X points.

We have shown that
\mathcal{Q}=-\mbox{Det}(\bm{\nabla}\bm{B})
goes to zero both near the 3D ridges and coils. A quantity analogous to
\mathcal{Q}
has been recently investigated in the fluid dynamics context, generalizing the Okubo-Weiss criterion by investigating the
\mathsfbi{\bm{\nabla}\bm{v}}
tensor for a flow field
\bm{v}

(
4
)
. Associated with
\mathcal{Q}=0
is a null direction of the
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}
tensor, which aligns with ridges and coils. In both these cases, the nonzero eigenvalues are given by
\pm\sqrt{-\mathcal{P}}=\pm B/L_{\nabla B}
. Thus, while using the
L_{\nabla B}
metric to find nearby coils, one needs to be careful near regions with significant flux surface shaping.

We conclude by noting that the regular O and X points and the regular tokamak X points are periodic and are uninterrupted on the torus. We can also include the
n_{fp}=2
QA configurations in this group, characterized by a lack of strong flux surface shaping. Our analysis shows that the normalized eigenvalues of the
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}
are
O(1)
and so coils can be further out. Now we consider 3D sharp ridges that are highly localized, being restricted to mostly lie on the inboard
K_{G}\leq 0
region. Localized sharp ridges naturally imply localized flux surface shaping, which, from our analysis, would require complex coils in the vicinity of the strong shaping on the inboard side. This is also correlated with large normalized eigenvalues.

7
Conclusions

In conclusion, we have analyzed the behavior of magnetic field lines and the field strength of vacuum quasisymmetric fields on a surface far from the magnetic axis. Formulating quasisymmetry as an eikonal equation, we have shown that in compact QA, sharp ridges tend to form on the inboard side where the Gaussian curvature is typically negative. The magnetic field lines in the vicinity of the ridges are essentially straight, with far-reaching consequences, discussed below.

Firstly, the straightness of the magnetic field lines immediately puts constraints on the field strength. Since the field strength is dictated by vacuum QA, the implications of straight field lines have observable consequences, such as the radial derivative of the maximum
B
is approximately zero on the outer surface. Also, straight lines tangential to a surface can only exist on the negative Gaussian curvature side. We have obtained an exact relation between the 3D
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}
tensor and the 3D Hessian of the flux surface
\bm{\nabla}\bm{\nabla}\psi
and demonstrated how quasisymmetry plays a crucial role in ensuring Det
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}=0
near ridges.

Secondly, we have argued that 3D ridges are very different from regular X points associated with rational surfaces. We have quantified the difference in terms of the eigenvalues of the
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}
tensor normalized by the major radius. For regular X points, we have shown that the eigenvalues of
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}
normalized by the major radius are
O(1)
, whereas for sharp 3D ridges, they are either zero or much larger in magnitude than unity. The largeness of the eigenvalue corresponds to the variation of the Gaussian curvature across the ridge. We note here that the large change in curvature across a ridge is not the mathematically accurate definition of a ridge, since the ridge point is defined by an extremum of a principal curvature along the same principal direction. For our case, this translates to
\partial_{\ell}\kappa_{n}=0
since the principal direction and the principal curvature of the surface locally coincide with the field line direction and the normal curvature
\kappa_{n}
close to
B=B_{M}
. It happens that a large variation in the other principal curvature
\kappa_{2}
across the ridge is beneficial for a better alignment of the ridge with the magnetic field, but not strictly necessary. However, in terms of numerical computation, detecting the large
\kappa_{2}
might be easier for a ridge detection.

Thirdly, we have shown that filamentary coils must lie on
\mbox{Det}\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}=0
surfaces. The implication of this is that the direction of the coil filament must align with the null direction of the
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}
tensor. The other eigenvalues are large and of opposite signs. The similarity with the case of the 3D ridges is striking and indeed shows that ridges and coils are both associated with the null space of the
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}
tensor. Thus, it would be harder to generate coils for configurations with sharp 3D ridges due to the strong surface shaping. The coil-to-surface distance and coil non-planarity are both affected by surface shaping. We have defined a new coil-to-surface metric,
\mbox{Det}(\bm{\nabla}\bm{B})/\|\nabla{\mathbf{B}}\|_{F}^{3}
, which approximates where (but not to what extent) the coils are close to the plasma. While only slightly less accurate than the
L_{\nabla B}
metric, it significantly outperforms the null case.

Finally, we have shown that field line torsion varies rapidly near the ridges. The ridge itself is almost straight with zero torsion but the neighboring field lines have large torsion, essentially proportional to the square root of the negative Gaussian curvature. In the simple model where the winding surface is a flux surface
(
90
)
, the non-zero torsion or non-planarity of
\bm{B}
directly constrains the non-planarity of the coils since the current potential
\bm{K}=\bm{\hat{n}}\times\bm{B}
. Further, in the light of recent work on QI by
77
, the large variation in the torsion of the field lines due to the large variation of the Gaussian curvature near the ridge has a direct relevance for coil non-planarity. We showed that near the sharp ridges, the field lines locally align with the principal direction with the smaller principle curvature. Since the field lines must also lie on flux surfaces, the principal directions must twist sharply in the vicinity of the ridges. Thus, the the flux surface itself need to bend at the ridges and then twist sharply away from the ridge to accommodate the field lines with large torsion. The sharp twist can contribute to large non-planarity of coils in QS much like in QI. Further work along these lines is ongoing.

Appendix A
The formal mathematics underlying ridges

We now discuss the formal mathematical definition and several important geometrical properties of the ridges. We also emphasize its connection with caustics and envelopes that play a fundamental role in geometrical optics.

A.1
Brief summary of the relevant ideas from differential geometry of surfaces

We need a few essential definitions from the theory of surfaces in differential geometry
(
102
;
50
;
21
)
before we can proceed.

On a smooth surface (say of constant flux
\psi
), parametrized by local coordinates
\bm{r}=(u,v)
at each point
\bm{P}
, we can define a unit normal
\bm{N}
, which coincides with
\bm{\nabla}\psi/|\bm{\nabla}\psi|
at a non-critical point where
|\bm{\nabla}\psi|\neq 0
. The map from a point to its normal is the
Gauss map
. The first and second fundamental forms of the surface are defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}\equiv d\bm{r}\cdot d\bm{r}=g_{ij}dr^{i}dr^{j},\quad\mathrm{II}\equiv-d\bm{r}\cdot d\bm{N}.

(A.1)

The
shape operator
can be obtained through the
(u,v)
derivatives of
\bm{N}
as
S(\bm{r}_{,u})=-\bm{N}_{,u}
and
S(\bm{r}_{,v})=-\bm{N}_{,v}
. The eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the shape operator gives the
principal curvatures

(\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2})
and
principal directions

(\bm{e}_{1},\bm{e}_{2})
.
The
Gaussian curvature

K_{G}\equiv\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}
, and the
mean curvature

H=(\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2})/2
are the determinant and half the trace of the shape operator.

The
parabolic line
with

when either
\kappa_{1}=0
or
\kappa_{2}=0

K_{G}<0

K_{G}>0

The
asymptotic direction
consists of directions along which
\mathrm{II}
vanish identically.
Asymptotic curves
are curves with tangent in the asymptotic direction, for which the normal curvature is always zero. Asymptotic curves exist only in
K_{G}\leq 0
regions. For
K_{G}<0
, a pair of asymptotic curves exist, while on the parabolic line
K_{G}=0

is a point where the asymptotic curve has zero geodesic curvature. Since by definition, asymptotic curves have zero normal curvatures, at the inflection points, they are straight lines. Thus, any straight line on a surface must be an asymptotic curve with degenerate inflection points.

At points called

, the two principal curvatures are equal. The ratio of the two principal curvatures typically scales as the aspect ratio of the device. Since in a stellarator, the aspect ratio is always larger than one, even for the most compact designs, the principal curvatures are almost always unequal. Thus, umbilical points are rare and will be ignored henceforth.

A.2
Formal definition of ridges and ridge points

Formally, ridges are defined as a collection of ridge points at which one of the principal curvatures attains an extremum along the corresponding principal direction
(
82
;
50
)
. Thus, curves along which
\bm{e}_{1}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\kappa_{1}=0
or
\bm{e}_{2}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\kappa_{2}=0
defines ridges. Being an extrema of a principal curvature, ridge points are analogs of
vertices
of planar curves
(
21
)
, where the curvature has a local extremum.

Closely associated with planar vertices is the concept of an
evolute

(
2
)
, which is defined as the envelope to the normals to the curve. In the case of a planar curve, the evolute is obtained by joining all points that are located at normal distances from the curve equal to the radii of curvature. Evolutes have cusp singularities near the vertices. In other words, on a planar curve, the vertices are also points that are closest to the cusp singularity of the evolute to the curve.

The surface analog of an evolute is obtained from the loci of the two principal curvatures. Hence, there are typically two of these surfaces, which are also called caustic surfaces or focal surfaces
(
82
;
50
)
.

The caustic surfaces can have singularities such as cusp points or cuspidal edges called
ribs

(
82
)
. At these singularities, two or more normals meet. Ridge points are the surface analogs of planar curve vertices in the sense that they are the closest points on the surface to the cusp singularities of the caustic surface, the distance between them scaling inversely with the curvature.

A.3
Connection with caustics

Caustics play an important role in optics
(
13
;
11
)
, and are defined as surfaces where two or more rays meet such that the formal geometrical optics breakdown leads to formally infinite intensity of light. The meeting of rays in a caustic signals a characteristic degeneracy that play a crucial role in the theory of rainbows and ship wakes
(
10
)
.

We illustrate the underlying mechanism for the formation of caustics in optics with the example of asymptotic approximations of oscillatory integrals typical in WKB theory. The method of stationary phase allows one to expand the oscillatory integral near a critical point up to quadratic terms and evaluate the resulting Gaussian integral analytically. As a result, we obtain the square root of the second derivative of the phase in the denominator. However, if the critical point is degenerate, the second derivative of the phase goes to zero. This happens precisely near the turning points in WKB theory where incident, reflected and transmitted “rays” can coexist. Near such degenerate critical points, the amplitude of the Gaussian approximation formally blows up, which is the essence of a caustic formation. Near turning points, we need the third order terms in the phase to obtain a finite approximation of the integral in terms of Airy functions.

The mathematical machinery needed for treating caustics self-consistently is catastrophe theory
(
1
;
13
;
12
;
83
)
. Due to the association with caustics, the description of ridges requires similar tools
(
82
;
50
)
, which we discuss in further details in our forthcoming paper. In the following, we briefly summarize relevant known results from
8
.

The singularities of the Gauss map that happens on the parabolic curves have deep connection with ridges. Banchoff, Gaffney, and McCrory
8
shows that the generic singularities of the Gauss map are
folds and cusps
. Typically, lines of curvature
\bm{e}_{i}
cross asymptotic lines transversely at folds. However, at the cusp they are tangential. Gaussian cusps occur precisely where the ridge lines cross the parabolic curve with vanishing principal curvature. The inflection points on the asymptotic lines also accumulate near the cusps of Gauss, essentially requiring the asymptotic lines to be straight. Asymptotic lines approach the parabolic curve and are tangent to it at the cusp. The parabolic curves thus serves as a local envelope to all the asymptotic curves near the cusp of Gauss.

A.4
Implications for stellarators

We now emphasize certain key differences between regular X point of a tokamak or a resonant island and the 3D ridges in stellarators that arises due to caustics.

Magnetic field lines are essentially straight and therefore asymptotic lines in the vicinity of the ridges, as we have shown using the optical analogy. They approach the parabolic line but can not cross since
K_{G}
mus be negative and so stay tangential. Thus, the parabolic curves form an envelope along the ridge. As we saw in the last Section, this configuration is the signature of the cusp of Gauss. The essential point to note here is that the vanishing of the field line normal on the inboard side with

is highly nontrivial and does not happen in regular X points.

Next, we note that in both these cases,

\psi

\psi

\psi=B_{0}(\ell)(y^{2}-x^{2})/2

|\partial(\psi_{,x},\psi_{,y})/\partial(x,y)|=B_{0}^{2}\neq 0
. However, the form
\psi=B_{0}(y^{2}-x^{3})
that can occur near cusps
(
20

is degenerate.

D.1
Eigenvalues of the Grad-B tensor

The
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}
tensor is a
3\times 3
matrix with real coefficients. It is traceless owing to divergence-free nature of
\bm{B}
. Since current
\mu_{0}\bm{J}=\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{B}
is related to the antisymmetric components of the
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}

Using Cayley-Hamilton theorem
(
101
)
, the characteristic equation of
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}
is

\displaystyle\mbox{Det}(\lambda\mathbb{I}-\bm{\nabla}\bm{B})=\lambda^{3}-\mbox{Tr}(\bm{\nabla}\bm{B})\lambda^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(\mbox{Tr}(\bm{\nabla}\bm{B})\right)^{2}-\mbox{Tr}((\bm{\nabla}\bm{B})^{2})\right)\lambda-\mbox{Det}(\bm{\nabla}\bm{B})=0.

(D.1)

Since
\mbox{Tr}(\bm{\nabla}\bm{B})=\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{B}=0
, we have

\displaystyle\mbox{Det}(\lambda\mathbb{I}-\bm{\nabla}\bm{B})=\lambda^{3}+\mathcal{P}\lambda+\mathcal{Q}=0,\quad\mathcal{P}=-\frac{1}{2}\mbox{Tr}\left((\bm{\nabla}\bm{B})^{2}\right),\quad\mathcal{Q}=-\mbox{Det}(\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}).

(D.2)

The eigenvalues
\lambda_{i},i=1,2,3
of the
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}
tensor satisfy
\prod_{i}(\lambda-\lambda_{i})=0
such that

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}+\lambda_{3}=0,\quad\mathcal{P}=\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}+\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}+\lambda_{3}\lambda_{1},\quad\mathcal{Q}=-\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}.

(D.3)

It follows from the traceless condition that
2\sum_{i\neq j}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}+\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}=0
for
i,j=1,2,3
. Thus,
\mathcal{P}
can also be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}=-\frac{1}{2}(\lambda_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{3}^{2}+\lambda_{3}^{2}).

(D.4)

Now, from the definition of the Frobenius norm,
|\mathsfbi{M}|_{F}^{2}=\mbox{Tr}(\mathsfbi{M}\mathsfbi{M}^{\top})
and
L_{\nabla B}
of an arbitrary symmetric tensor
\mathsfbi{M}
we find that

\displaystyle|\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}|_{F}^{2}=(\lambda_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{3}^{2}+\lambda_{3}^{2})=-2\mathcal{P}\quad\Rightarrow\quad L_{\nabla B}=\frac{\sqrt{2}B}{\sqrt{|\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}|_{F}^{2}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{P}|/B^{2}}}.

(D.5)

D.2
Grad-B tensor in axisymmetry

We first analyze the axisymmetric case relevant to tokamak-like divertors. We employ the standard cylindrical coordinates
(R,\zeta,z)
with orthonormal vectors
(\bm{\hat{R}}=\bm{\nabla}R,\bm{\hat{\zeta}}=R\bm{\nabla}\zeta,\bm{\hat{z}}=\bm{\nabla}z)

(

(D.6)

Here,

is the current,
\psi(R,z)

\displaystyle\psi_{,zz}+\psi_{,RR}-\frac{1}{R}\psi_{,R}+R^{2}p^{\prime}(\psi)+II^{\prime}(\psi)=0.

(D.7)

The gradient of
\bm{B}
from (
D.6

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\bm{\hat{R}}=\frac{\bm{\hat{\zeta}}\bm{\hat{\zeta}}}{R},\quad\bm{\nabla}\bm{\hat{\zeta}}=-\frac{\bm{\hat{\zeta}}\bm{\hat{R}}}{R},

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{R}\left(\bm{\hat{R}}\bm{\nabla}\psi_{,z}-\bm{\hat{z}}\bm{\nabla}\psi_{,R}\right)-\frac{I}{R^{2}}\left(\bm{\hat{\zeta}}\bm{\hat{R}}+\bm{\hat{R}}\bm{\hat{\zeta}}\right)

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{I^{\prime}(\psi)}{R}\bm{\nabla}\psi\bm{\hat{\zeta}}-\frac{\bm{\hat{R}}}{R}\frac{\bm{\hat{\zeta}}\times\bm{\nabla}\psi}{R}+\frac{\psi_{,Z}}{R}\frac{\bm{\hat{\zeta}}\bm{\hat{\zeta}}}{R}\right).

(D.9)

Near O and X points, where

D.9

\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}
takes the simple form

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}=\frac{1}{R}\left(\psi_{,Rz}\left(\bm{\hat{R}}\bm{\hat{R}}-\bm{\hat{z}}\bm{\hat{z}}\right)+\left(\bm{\hat{z}}\bm{\hat{R}}\;\psi_{,zz}-\bm{\hat{R}}\bm{\hat{z}}\;\psi_{,RR}\right)\right)-\frac{I}{R^{2}}\left(\bm{\hat{\zeta}}\bm{\hat{R}}+\bm{\hat{R}}\bm{\hat{\zeta}}\right).

(D.10)

In the alternative matrix form in the
(\bm{\hat{\zeta}},\bm{\hat{z}},\bm{\hat{R}})
basis

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}=\frac{1}{R}\begin{pmatrix}\;\;0\qquad\quad\;0\qquad-I/R\\
\quad 0\qquad-\psi_{,Rz}\qquad\;\psi_{,zz}\\
-I/R\quad-\psi_{,RR}\qquad\psi_{,Rz}\end{pmatrix},

(D.11)

with trace and determinant given by

\displaystyle\mbox{Tr}{\left(\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}\right)}=0,\quad\mbox{Det}{\left(\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}\right)}=\left(\frac{I}{R^{2}}\right)^{2}\psi_{,Rz}.

(D.12)

Normalizing the axisymmetric quantities with major radius
R_{a}
and field strength
B_{a}

\displaystyle\bar{R}=\frac{R}{R_{a}},\quad\overline{z}=\frac{z}{R_{a}},\quad\overline{I}=\frac{I}{B_{a}R_{a}},\quad\overline{\psi}=\frac{\psi}{B_{a}R_{a}^{2}},

(D.13)

we find that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{P}}{(B_{a}/R_{a})^{2}}=\frac{1}{\overline{R}^{2}}\left(\overline{\mathscr{H}}_{2}[\overline{\psi}]-\left(\frac{\overline{I}}{\overline{R}}\right)^{2}\right),\quad\frac{\mathcal{Q}}{(B_{a}/R_{a})^{3}}=-\left(\frac{\overline{I}}{\overline{R}^{2}}\right)^{2}\overline{\psi}_{,\overline{R}\overline{z}},

(D.14)

The normalized eigenvalues

\overline{\mathcal{P}},\overline{\mathcal{Q}}
are both
O(1)

are
O(\rho)
smaller compared to terms such as
\partial_{\rho}B_{\rho},(1/\rho)\partial_{\omega}B_{\rho}
. Thus, we naturally end up with

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}=\centering\begin{pmatrix}-\kappa_{c}B_{n}\qquad\qquad 0\qquad\qquad\qquad 0\qquad\\
0\qquad\qquad\partial_{\rho}B_{\rho}\qquad\qquad\partial_{\rho}B_{\omega}\\
\qquad\quad 0\qquad\frac{1}{\rho}(-B_{\omega}+\partial_{\omega}B_{\rho})\quad\frac{1}{\rho}(B_{\rho}+\partial_{\omega}B_{\omega})\end{pmatrix},\@add@centering

(E.3)

where

\displaystyle B_{n}\equiv\bm{B}\cdot\bm{\hat{n}}=B_{\rho}\cos\theta-B_{\omega}\sin\theta.

(E.4)

We now define a reduced determinant surface
\mathscr{D}=0
corresponding to
B_{n}=0
. This surface can be given a natural interpretation of an effective flux surface such that the normal component of
\bm{B}
given by
B_{n}
vanishes on the surface. Thus, it is natural to parametrize the determinant
\mathscr{D}=0
by

\displaystyle\bm{r}=\bm{r}_{0}(\ell)+\rho_{\mathscr{D}}\bm{\hat{n}}.

(E.5)

We have the following tangent vectors on the determinant surface

\displaystyle\partial_{\rho_{\mathscr{D}}}\bm{r}=\bm{\hat{n}},\quad\partial_{\ell}\bm{r}=h_{c}\bm{\hat{t}}+\rho_{\mathscr{D}}\tau_{c}\bm{\hat{b}},\quad h_{c}=1-\kappa_{c}\rho_{\mathscr{D}}\cos\theta.

(E.6)

In the limit of
\rho_{\mathscr{D}}\to 0
, the tangent vectors are
\bm{\hat{n}}
and
\bm{\hat{t}}

The unit normal vector to the determinant surface is

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{N}}=\frac{\partial_{\ell}\bm{r}\times\partial_{\rho_{\mathscr{D}}}\bm{r}}{|\partial_{\ell}\bm{r}\times\partial_{\rho_{\mathscr{D}}}\bm{r}|}=\bm{\hat{b}}

(E.7)

Thus, the binormal vector
\bm{\hat{b}}
is the normal to the surface
\hat{\bm{N}}
, which is a textbook
(
102
)
example of asymptotic curves on a ruled surface. For asymptotic curves Gaussian curvature is non-positive and can be related to the torsion through
K_{G\mathscr{D}}=-\tau_{c}^{2}
.

Finally, for the sake of rigor, we now define a normalized determinant
\bar{\mathscr{D}}_{3}
that stays finite as we approach the Det
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}
surface. The essential idea is to use the Cayley - Hamilton invariants for a traceless symmetric matrix such as
\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}
. As discussed in Appendix
D
, the characteristic equation Det
(\lambda\mathbb{I}-\bm{\nabla}\bm{B})
leads to the invariants
\mathcal{P}=-(1/2)\text{Tr}((\bm{\nabla}\bm{B})^{2})
and
\mathcal{Q}=-\text{Det}\bm{\nabla}\bm{B}
. In terms of the three real eigenvalues
(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{\pm})
,
\mathcal{P}=(-1/2)(\lambda_{0}^{2}+\lambda_{+}^{2}+\lambda_{-}^{2}),\mathcal{Q}=-\lambda_{0}\lambda_{+}\lambda_{-}
. As we approach the current filament,
\lambda_{0}\to 0
while
\lambda_{\pm}
blow up. In this limit
\lambda_{-}\to-\lambda_{+}
, such that
-\mathcal{P}\to\lambda_{+}^{2},\mathcal{Q}\to\lambda_{0}\lambda_{+}^{2}
. To isolate the null eigenvalue surface defined by
\lambda_{0}
, we now define
\bar{\mathscr{D}}_{3}{}
such that

\displaystyle\bar{\mathscr{D}}_{3}{}\equiv\frac{\mathcal{Q}}{(-\mathcal{P})^{3/2}}.

(E.8)

The
3/2
power ensures a smooth continuity
\bar{\mathscr{D}}_{3}{}\to 0
as we take the limit
\rho\to 0
. Other choices are also possible, but we do not dwell on that here. The normalized determinant is useful for numerical purposes as it is designed to be smooth near coils. However, it does not change the conclusions obtained from
\mathscr{D}_{3}\to 0
. The reason is that
\mathscr{D}_{3}\to 0
not because of any power of
\rho
but because of the
\sin\theta
factor that goes to zero near the coils.
