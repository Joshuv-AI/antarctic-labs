Active Testing of Large Language Models via Approximate Neyman Allocation
Zeli Liu, Jiancheng Zhang, Cong Liu, Yinglun Zhu

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) require reliable evaluation from pre-training to test-time scaling, making evaluation a recurring rather than one-off cost. As model scales grow and target tasks increasingly demand expert annotators, both the compute and labeling costs needed for each evaluation rise rapidly. Active testing aims to alleviate this bottleneck by approximating the evaluation result from a small but informative subset of the evaluation pool. However, existing approaches primarily target classification and break down on generative tasks. We introduce a novel active testing algorithm tailored to generative tasks. Our method leverages semantic entropy from surrogate models to stratify the evaluation pool and then conducts approximate Neyman allocation based on signals extracted from these surrogates. Across multiple language and multimodal benchmarks and a range of surrogate-target model pairs, our method significantly improves on baselines and closely tracks Oracle-Neyman, delivering up to 28% MSE reduction over Uniform Sampling and an average of 22.9% budget savings.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed across diverse reasoning, multimodal, and agentic tasks
(
Wei et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2023
;
Zhu et al. 2025
)
, often via API-based services. Reliable evaluation of these models is needed repeatedly throughout model development, including model selection during pre- and post-training, regression testing across successive releases, safety and quality monitoring after deployment, and coverage across the long tail of domains and sub-tasks each model is asked to handle. The per-evaluation cost is also growing rapidly along two axes. On the inference side, four layers of scaling—pre-training, post-training, test-time, and agentic paradigms
(
Hoffmann et al. 2022
;
Ouyang et al. 2022
;
Snell et al. 2024
;
Yao et al. 2022
;
Zuo and Zhu 2025
;
Zuo et al. 2026
)
—substantially increase the compute spent on every input. On the annotation side, many target tasks extend beyond the capabilities of standard annotators, since, in the absence of established benchmarks for niche or real-world inputs, grading frequently requires costly domain experts.

Active testing has been proposed as a principled approach to this regime, selecting a small but informative subset for labeling and using an estimator to recover full-pool performance
(
Kossen et al. 2021
;
Farquhar et al. 2021
;
Berrada et al. 2025
)
. Existing pipelines, however, fall short of the requirements of modern generative LLM evaluation: they are designed for classification rather than for multi-token generation, often require retraining a data selector, or depend on internal activations and logits that black-box API services generally do not provide. In this paper, we extend active testing to generative tasks and propose a training-free and theoretically unbiased active-testing method applicable to both large language and multimodal models. Our central insight is that uncertainty in generative outputs is fundamentally a property of meaning rather than tokens, and therefore cannot be adequately captured by classification-style entropy measures. Our method leverages

;

)
as a meaning-level uncertainty signal that reflects generative ambiguity in model outputs. Building on this signal, our method constructs a stratified estimator equipped with a surrogate-driven Neyman-style allocation

)

Related Work

Active testing.

Active testing was introduced by
Kossen et al. 2021
for label-efficient model evaluation, using a surrogate-driven acquisition function debiased by the LURE estimator
(
Kossen et al. 2022
;
Farquhar et al. 2021
)
, and later extended to LLMs by
Berrada et al. 2025
via a frozen in-context surrogate that removes the retraining loop. Both methods are tied to the classification setting, which mismatches the generative deployment of modern LLMs.

AcTracer
(
Huang et al. 2026
)
performs multi-stage sampling which relies on hidden states unavailable in black-box APIs, and its confidence-matching proxy does not yield a theoretically unbiased estimator. GAT
(
Ramakrishnan et al. 2026
)
retains the LURE estimator but rewrites each input into a True/False proxy task with first-token acquisition, again mismatched with multi-token generative evaluation.
Zhou et al. 2025
study efficient pairwise win-rate estimation between two LLMs via control variates, whereas we target single-model evaluation.

Sampling, stratification, and budget allocation.

Sampling theory provides classical tools for estimating population quantities from a small queried subset
(
Cochran 1977
;
Lohr 2021
)
. Variance-reducing designs include stratified sampling
(
Neyman 1992
;
Dalenius and Hodges Jr 1959
)
, unequal-probability sampling
(
Hansen and Hurwitz 1943
)
, systematic sampling
(
Madow and Madow 1944
)
, cluster and multi-stage sampling, and adaptive designs
(
Thompson 1990
)
. The companion question of budget allocation across strata determines how labels are distributed, ranging from equal allocation and proportional allocation to variance-optimal Neyman allocation under known stratum standard deviations
(
Neyman 1992
)
. Such designs admit unbiased finite-population estimation through inverse-probability weighting
(
Horvitz and Thompson 1952
)
.

Active learning.

Efficient utilization of labeling resources is also a central objective in model training, alongside broader efficiency considerations
(
Wang et al. 2024a
;
Wang et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2025
)
.
Active learning aims to select the most informative subset from an unlabeled pool for annotation, thereby reducing labeling costs
(
Settles 2009
)
. Traditional active learning methods are typically based on either diversity
(
Sener and Savarese 2017
;
Agarwal et al. 2020
)
or uncertainty
(
Tong and Koller 2001
;
Scheffer et al. 2001
)
. It has been shown to improve data efficiency from both theoretical perspectives
(
Krishnamurthy et al. 2019
;
Puchkin and Zhivotovskiy 2021
;
Zhu and Nowak 2022a
;
Zhu and Nowak 2022b
)
and empirical studies
(
Saran et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2023
)
. Recently, active learning has also emerged as a common strategy for improving data efficiency in large pretrained models
(
Bhatt et al. 2024
;
Yuan et al. 2024
;
Zhang and Zhu 2025
)
.

3
Problem Setting

Active testing.

We study active testing for large language and multimodal models.
We denote
f
as the target model and let
\mathcal{D}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
be a finite pool of unlabeled test inputs drawn from an evaluation distribution
p_{\mathrm{eval}}
.
We denote
\ell
as the loss function of predictive errors. Following
Berrada et al. 2025
, the ideal population risk of interest is:

R\;=\;{\mathbb{E}}_{(x,y)\sim p_{\mathrm{eval}}}\!\left[\ell(f(x),y)\right].

Since
p_{\mathrm{eval}}
and the labels of all possible test inputs are unknown, we condition on the fixed pool
\mathcal{D}
and instead estimate the finite-pool risk:

R_{\mathrm{D}}\;=\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell\big(f(x_{i}),y_{i}\big).

Active testing avoids labeling the full pool by selecting a subset
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathcal{D}
of size
M\ll N
and constructing an estimator
\widehat{R}
for
R_{\mathrm{D}}
from the labeled subset
(
Kossen et al. 2021
)
.
The objective is to minimize the estimation error of
\widehat{R}
under a fixed labeling budget while preserving unbiasedness with respect to the full-pool risk,
{\mathbb{E}}[\widehat{R}]=R_{\mathrm{D}}
.

Evaluation metrics.

Following
Berrada et al. 2025
, we evaluate a testing method by the squared error of its risk estimate against the ground-truth finite-pool risk
R_{\mathrm{D}}
, where
R_{\mathrm{D}}
is obtained by labeling the entire pool. For a fixed labeling budget
M
, let
\widehat{R}^{(t)}
denote the estimate produced by estimator
\widehat{R}
in Monte Carlo trial
t
, and let
T=3{,}000
be the number of trials. We compute the mean squared error (MSE)

\mathrm{MSE}(\widehat{R})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\widehat{R}^{(t)}-R_{\mathrm{D}}\right)^{2}.

To enable comparisons across different benchmarks, we also follow
Berrada et al. 2025
and report the error relative to the Uniform Sampling baseline. Let
\widehat{R}_{\mathrm{unif}}
denote the estimator induced by Uniform Sampling. We report the relative-MSE ratio
r(\widehat{R})=\frac{\mathrm{MSE}(\widehat{R})}{\mathrm{MSE}(\widehat{R}_{\mathrm{unif}})}
.

4.1
Motivation

A natural way to extend the method of
Berrada et al. 2025
, which provides an effective solution for active testing in classification tasks, to generative settings is to retain their LURE estimator and replace first-token predictive entropy (PE) with token-averaged PE computed over multi-token generations from a surrogate model. However, this straightforward PE-based adaptation does not outperform Uniform Sampling, as shown in
Fig.

2
.
We identify two factors underlying this failure.
First, token-level PE entangles semantic uncertainty with surface-form variation
: semantically equivalent generations with different phrasings induce different token-level logit sequences, causing PE to be inflated by paraphrastic diversity rather than genuine difficulty
(
Kuhn et al. 2023
;
Farquhar et al. 2024
)
.
Second, modern LLMs are systematically overconfident
, collapsing PE into a narrow low-entropy band and weakening the acquisition signal; surrogate and target PE remain only moderately correlated in our analysis, yielding a substantial mass of instances with systematic disagreement, where surrogate and target confidence sharply diverge
(
Farquhar et al. 2024
)
.

Based on the analysis of the failure factors, we further consider replacing PE with semantic entropy (SE), a meaning-level uncertainty signal that we introduce in
Section

4.2
. However, this SE-LURE estimator also fails across various datasets, as shown in
Fig.

2
. This suggests that the failure is not due to the uncertainty signal, but to the LURE estimator itself. In particular, LURE applies per-sample importance weights (
v_{m}\propto 1/a_{m}(i_{m})
)
(
Berrada et al. 2025
)
, which amplify surrogate–target mismatches. When the surrogate assigns low acquisition probability to instances that the target is actually uncertain about, these weights dominate the estimator. Since such mismatches are systematic rather than rare, improving the entropy signal alone does not reduce variance but instead exposes this instability.

This observation indicates that the core issue lies in the importance-reweighting mechanism, suggesting the need for an alternative estimation strategy that avoids such variance amplification. Motivated by this, we turn to a Neyman-style stratified estimator, which allocates the labeling budget in a variance-aware but unbiased manner.

The proposed estimator constructs SE-based strata from surrogate-derived uncertainty signals, performs a proxy Neyman-style allocation of the labeling budget across strata, and aggregates the per-stratum target losses with a Horvitz–Thompson estimator. We next explain the details of each of the four steps in
Algorithm

1
.

Signal generation.

This step generates uncertainty signals by running the surrogate model
g
on the evaluation pool
\mathcal{D}
to produce
k
generations per input (
k=10
throughout). The generations are used to calculate semantic entropy and self-consistency scores. This surrogate pass incurs only modest overhead, as
g
is substantially smaller than the target model
f
, and the
k
generations share a single prefill through KV-cache reuse supported by modern inference frameworks
(
Kwon et al. 2023
)
.

Semantic entropy addresses the fact that token-level predictive entropy in generative models can conflate uncertainty over
meaning
with uncertainty over surface form
(
Kuhn et al. 2023
;
Farquhar et al. 2024
)
. For each input
x_{i}
, it clusters generations into meaning-equivalence classes
\mathcal{C}_{i}
and computes entropy over the induced class probabilities, as in
Eq.

1
. When the answer space is closed, as in our benchmarks after parsing, the equivalence classes coincide with distinct parsed answers and
Eq.

1
reduces to the Shannon entropy over parsed-answer frequencies.

Based on the generated samples, we construct two training-free uncertainty signals: the per-sample semantic entropy
\mathrm{SE}(g(x_{i}))
and the self-consistency score
(
Wang et al. 2022
)
, defined as in
Eq.

2
. Here
g(x_{i})^{(j)}
is the
j
-th surrogate generation for input
x_{i}
. Per-sample semantic entropy captures the surrogate’s uncertainty on sample
x_{i}
, while the self-consistency score measures agreement among surrogate generations (higher values indicate stronger consensus and easier instances). It is used to construct the Bernoulli proxy for Neyman allocation described below.

Stratification on semantic entropy.

We partition
\mathcal{D}
into
H
strata
\mathcal{D}_{0},\ldots,\mathcal{D}_{H-1}
of sizes
N_{h}=|\mathcal{D}_{h}|
based on semantic entropy. In practice, a non-trivial fraction of samples satisfy
\mathrm{SE}(g(x_{i}))=0
, so we assign them to a base stratum
\mathcal{D}_{0}=\{x_{i}\in\mathcal{D}:\mathrm{SE}(g(x_{i}))=0\}
and apply equal-quantile binning to the remaining samples
\{x_{i}\in\mathcal{D}:\mathrm{SE}(g(x_{i}))>0\}
. We set
H=5
throughout and provide an ablation over
H
in
Section

5.3
.

Budget allocation.

Given a fixed stratification, classical Neyman allocation
m_{h}^{*}\propto N_{h}\sigma_{h}
provides a theoretical optimum that minimizes the estimation variance
(
Neyman 1992
)
. However, this optimum depends on the within-stratum standard deviation
\sigma_{h}
of the target loss, which is not directly accessible without labeling the entire pool. To address this, we replace
\sigma_{h}
with a smoothed Bernoulli proxy
\sqrt{p_{h}(1-p_{h})}+\delta
, where
p_{h}=\frac{1}{N_{h}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{h}}\mathrm{SC}(g(x_{i}))
denotes the mean surrogate self-consistency within stratum
\mathcal{D}_{h}
. The resulting allocation is defined as in
Eq.

3
. The offset
\delta>0
prevents
m_{h}
from collapsing when
p_{h}
approaches
1
in the base stratum. We use
\delta=0.75
and report an ablation study in
Section

5.3
.

Estimation.

Within each stratum
\mathcal{D}_{h}
, we draw
m_{h}
samples uniformly without replacement and obtain their labels. Let
\mathcal{S}_{h}\subset\mathcal{D}_{h}
denote the selected subset, and define the within-stratum empirical loss mean

\bar{\ell}_{h}\;=\;\frac{1}{m_{h}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{h}}\ell(f(x_{i}),y_{i}).

Since each sample in stratum
\mathcal{D}_{h}
is selected with equal inclusion probability
\pi_{h}=m_{h}/N_{h}
, we can write the Horvitz–Thompson form of
\widehat{R}
over the globally selected set
\mathcal{S}=\bigcup_{h=0}^{H-1}\mathcal{S}_{h}
:

\widehat{R}\;=\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{\ell(f(x_{i}),y_{i})}{\pi_{i}}.

Under stratified simple random sampling, where
\pi_{i}=\pi_{h}
for all
x_{i}\in\mathcal{D}_{h}
, this expression simplifies to
Eq.

4
, which is a theoretically unbiased estimator for the finite-pool risk
R_{\mathrm{D}}
, as we discuss next.

4.3
Theoretical Analysis

We establish the following theorem to formalize the theoretical unbiasedness of our method.

Theorem 1
.

Our method yields an unbiased estimator of the finite-pool risk:

\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{R}\right]\;=\;R_{\mathrm{D}},

for any stratification
\{\mathcal{D}_{h}\}_{h=0}^{H-1}
of
\mathcal{D}
and any allocation
\{m_{h}\}_{h=0}^{H-1}
satisfying
1\leq m_{h}\leq N_{h}
and
\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}m_{h}=M
. The result follows from (i) unbiasedness of within-stratum uniform sampling without replacement, and (ii) linearity of expectation for the stratified Horvitz–Thompson estimator.

-means

Conclusion and Limitations

We studied active testing for advanced generative language and multimodal models, where each label usually requires costly expert annotation and classification-oriented LURE estimators fail to outperform Uniform Sampling once the target model is evaluated through multi-token generation. We introduced a training-free and theoretically unbiased active-testing method that lifts the difficulty signal from tokens to meaning via semantic entropy, partitions the evaluation pool according to this signal, and allocates the label budget using a surrogate-driven proxy for Neyman allocation. Across language and vision-language reasoning benchmarks with both open-source and frontier proprietary surrogate–target pairs, our method consistently reduces estimation error relative to Uniform Sampling, closely tracks the infeasible Oracle-Neyman reference, and reaches matched precision with substantially fewer expert labels.

Our study also has some scope limitations. First, our experiments focus on closed or parseable answer spaces where generations can be mapped to canonical answers; extending active testing to open-ended generation remains challenging because evaluation noise can obscure estimator error. Second, the method assumes access to a surrogate model that is substantially cheaper than the target while still correlated with target-side difficulty, which may constrain deployment in some API-only scenarios. Future work can extend this framework by developing modality-aware surrogate signals and allocation rules for richer vision–language, interactive, and open-ended evaluation tasks.

Proof of the unbiasedness guarantee for our method

Proof.

Write
\ell_{i}=\ell(f(x_{i}),y_{i})
for brevity.
Fix a stratification
\{\mathcal{D}_{h}\}_{h=0}^{H-1}
of
\mathcal{D}
and an allocation
\{m_{h}\}_{h=0}^{H-1}
with
1\leq m_{h}\leq N_{h}
and
\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}m_{h}=M
. Within each stratum
\mathcal{D}_{h}
, let
\mathcal{S}_{h}
be a uniform sample without replacement of size
m_{h}
drawn from
\mathcal{D}_{h}
, and let
\bar{\ell}_{h}=\tfrac{1}{m_{h}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}_{h}}\ell_{i}
.
Since
\mathcal{S}_{h}
is sampled uniformly from all size-
m_{h}
subsets of
\mathcal{D}_{h}
, each such subset has probability
1/\binom{N_{h}}{m_{h}}
. For any fixed example
i\in\mathcal{D}_{h}
, exactly
\binom{N_{h}-1}{m_{h}-1}
of these subsets contain
i
, so

\Pr\left[i\in\mathcal{S}_{h}\right]\;=\;\frac{\binom{N_{h}-1}{m_{h}-1}}{\binom{N_{h}}{m_{h}}}\;=\;\frac{m_{h}}{N_{h}},\qquad\text{for every }i\in\mathcal{D}_{h}.

Rewriting the sample mean as a sum over the full stratum,
m_{h}\bar{\ell}_{h}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{h}}\mathbb{I}[i\in\mathcal{S}_{h}]\,\ell_{i}
, and applying linearity of expectation yields

{\mathbb{E}}[\bar{\ell}_{h}]\;=\;\frac{1}{m_{h}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{h}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbb{I}[i\in\mathcal{S}_{h}]\right]\ell_{i}\;=\;\frac{1}{m_{h}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{h}}\frac{m_{h}}{N_{h}}\,\ell_{i}\;=\;\frac{1}{N_{h}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{h}}\ell_{i}\;=\;R_{h},

the within-stratum risk. Because
\{\mathcal{D}_{h}\}_{h=0}^{H-1}
partitions
\mathcal{D}
and
\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}N_{h}=N
, a second application of linearity gives

{\mathbb{E}}[\widehat{R}]\;=\;{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}N_{h}\bar{\ell}_{h}\right]\;=\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}N_{h}R_{h}\;=\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{h}}\ell_{i}\;=\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell_{i}\;=\;R_{\mathrm{D}},

which establishes the unbiasedness claim. This is the usual Horvitz–Thompson inclusion-probability argument specialized to uniform sampling within each stratum.
