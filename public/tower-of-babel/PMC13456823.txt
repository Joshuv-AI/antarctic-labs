Worldwide research landscape of psychiatric emergency medicine: A bibliometric review from 2014 to 2023
Authors: Evren Ekingen, Mete Ucdal, Ercan Koca, Saniye Baladura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Emergency medicine has experienced growth as a research discipline, with psychiatric emergencies representing an area of expansion. The increasing volume of psychiatric presentations to emergency departments necessitates analysis of scientific output in this domain. This study examines publication trends, geographic distribution, and thematic priorities in psychiatric emergency medicine research over the past decade.Methods:We conducted a bibliometric analysis of articles published between January 2014 and December 2023. Data were extracted from the Web of Science Core Collection database using the search terms “Emergency Medicine” AND “Psychiatry.” Analysis included publication trends, geographic distribution, journal metrics according to Bradford Law, keyword frequency analysis, and thematic mapping. Statistical analyses employed IBM SPSS Statistics 25 and bibliometric software.Results:The analysis identified 862 publications authored by 5111 individuals from 67 countries. Annual publication volume increased from 52 articles in 2014 to 126 articles in 2023. The United States contributed 488 publications (56.7%), followed by Turkey (46 publications, 5.3%), Canada (44, 5.1%), Australia (40, 4.7%), and Iran (33, 3.8%). Four core journals accounted for 37.0% of publications: American Journal of Emergency Medicine (117 articles), Pediatric Emergency Care (75), Academic Emergency Medicine (66), and Journal of Emergency Medicine (61). Mean citation rate was 11.5 per article. Keyword analysis identified 6 primary research themes: pediatric psychiatric emergencies, substance use, suicide and self-harm, consultation models, emergency department management, and geriatric psychiatric emergencies. Temporal analysis revealed increased research focus on vulnerable populations including children and older adults after 2017.Conclusion:Psychiatric emergency medicine research demonstrated consistent growth over the decade studied. Publication output was concentrated geographically, with limited international collaboration. Research priorities evolved toward pediatric and geriatric populations. Findings provide insight into current research landscape and may inform future research directions and resource allocation in psychiatric emergency care.

1. Introduction

Emergency medicine achieved recognition as an independent medical specialty in 1979 by the American Board of Medical Specialties, following establishment of the first residency program in 1972.[1,2] Research output in this discipline has expanded continuously since formal recognition.[2] Psychiatry represents a substantial component of emergency medicine research activity, driven by increasing psychiatric presentations to emergency departments.[3]

Psychiatric emergencies comprise acute disturbances in cognition, behavior, or mood requiring immediate intervention to prevent harm.[4] These presentations include suicidal ideation and attempts, acute psychosis, severe mood episodes, substance-related emergencies, and behavioral crises. Emergency departments have assumed increasing responsibility for psychiatric care due to multiple factors including reduced psychiatric inpatient capacity, fragmentation of outpatient mental health services, and insurance-related barriers to care.[4,5]

Recent developments have emphasized the importance of this intersection. In 2025, the American Board of Medical Specialties approved focused practice designation in emergency behavioral health, and the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration published guidance recognizing emergency behavioral health centers as essential components of crisis care systems.[4,5] These developments reflect growing recognition of emergency psychiatry as a distinct practice area requiring specialized knowledge and skills.

Despite growing clinical importance, psychiatric emergency medicine research remains incompletely characterized. Previous emergency medicine bibliometric studies have examined overall research trends, but comprehensive analysis specific to psychiatric emergency presentations, their management, and outcomes remains limited.[2,6] Understanding publication patterns, research themes, and knowledge gaps can inform resource allocation and guide future investigations. This study analyzes bibliometric characteristics of psychiatric emergency medicine publications over a ten-year period to identify trends, assess geographic distribution, determine core journals, and map thematic priorities.

This study aims to conduct a comprehensive bibliometric analysis of psychiatric emergency medicine research to characterize publication trends and growth patterns over time, identify leading countries, institutions, and authors contributing to the field, map research themes and their evolution, analyze citation patterns and influential works, and identify gaps in current knowledge to guide future research directions.

2. Methods

This study employed bibliometric analysis methodology to examine psychiatric emergency medicine publications. We utilized the Web of Science Core Collection database, which contains over 171 million records spanning from 1900 to present.[7,8] The database provides standardized bibliometric data including author information, institutional affiliations, citation counts, and keyword assignments.

The Web of Science Core Collection was selected as the sole data source for several methodological reasons. First, the database applies rigorous journal selection criteria including peer-review verification, editorial standards assessment, and citation-impact thresholds, which yields a curated corpus suitable for bibliometric synthesis. Second, Web of Science provides standardized author identifiers, address parsing, and Keywords Plus metadata that are required for the co-authorship network analysis, institutional collaboration mapping, and keyword co-occurrence analysis performed in this study. Third, established bibliometric software including Bibliometrix and VOSviewer integrates natively with the Web of Science export format, which preserves the citation linkages necessary for Bradford Law analysis and thematic mapping. Fourth, single-database use is consistent with the methodological approach of recent emergency medicine bibliometric studies and avoids the duplication and metadata inconsistencies that arise when records are merged across heterogeneous sources. The implications of this single-database approach for completeness and generalizability are addressed in the limitations section.

2.1. Search strategy and selection criteria

We conducted systematic searches using the following criteria: Topic = (“Emergency Medicine” OR “Emergency Department” OR “Emergency Service”) AND (“Psychiatry” OR “Psychiatric” OR “Mental Health”). Inclusion criteria comprised articles, review articles, and proceedings papers, publications from January 1, 2014 through December 31, 2023, English language publications, and complete bibliometric data available. Exclusion criteria included editorials, letters to editor, and book chapters, publications in languages other than English, articles with incomplete author, affiliation, or citation data, and duplicate records.

2.2. Screening and data extraction

Initial database search results underwent systematic screening in 3 stages. First, duplicate records were identified and removed using automated deduplication tools within the bibliometric software. Second, 2 independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts to exclude articles psychiatric emergencies in emergency medicine settings. Third, full-text evaluation was performed for remaining articles to confirm eligibility based on content relevance. Disagreements at any stage were resolved through discussion with a third senior reviewer.

Data extraction captured publication year, author names and count, institutional affiliations, country of corresponding author, journal name, citation count, document type, and keywords. We excluded editorials, letters, and articles with incomplete bibliometric data. Two reviewers independently screened articles; disagreements were resolved through discussion. Three authors independently assessed papers using standardized data extraction forms to ensure consistency and accuracy. Quality assessment checklists verified completeness of bibliometric information for each included publication.

2.2.1. Data analysis and synthesis methods

Analysis procedures included calculation of publication frequency by year, country, and journal. We employed Bradford Law to identify core journals by dividing publications into zones based on productivity.[9] Citation analysis calculated mean citations per article and normalized citation rates accounting for time since publication. Keyword analysis examined both author-provided keywords and Keywords Plus terms assigned by Web of Science.[10] We performed co-occurrence analysis to identify research themes and temporal trend analysis to assess theme evolution over time.

Data synthesis employed multiple analytical approaches. Quantitative data were summarized using frequency distributions, percentages, and growth rates. Publication trends were analyzed using linear regression to determine annual growth patterns. Citation metrics were normalized by dividing total citations by years since publication to account for temporal bias. Results were displayed using tables for numerical summaries, bar charts for temporal trends, world maps for geographic distribution, and network diagrams for collaboration patterns and thematic relationships.

Thematic mapping employed strategic diagram methodology classifying themes by centrality and density. Centrality indicates the importance of a theme based on its connections to other themes. Density reflects theme development based on internal cohesion. This approach classifies themes into 4 categories: motor themes (high centrality, high density), niche themes (low centrality, high density), emerging or declining themes (low centrality, low density), and basic themes (high centrality, low density).

Statistical analyses used IBM SPSS Statistics 25 for descriptive statistics and trend analysis. Bibliometrix R package (version 4.1.0) was employed for bibliometric network visualization, co-citation analysis, and thematic mapping. VOSviewer software (version 1.6.18) generated network visualizations for author and institutional collaborations. Geographic distribution was mapped using Tableau software, and international collaboration patterns were assessed by examining multi-country authorship networks.

3. Results

3.1. Search and selection process

The systematic search of Web of Science Core Collection database identified 1024 records for the period 2014 to 2023. Following automated deduplication, 987 unique records remained. Title and abstract screening excluded 103 records not meeting topical relevance criteria. Full-text evaluation of 884 articles resulted in exclusion of 20 articles: 12 lacking complete bibliometric data, 6 editorials/letters, and 2 retracted publications. Final analysis included 862 publications

3.2. Publication characteristics and growth patterns

The search identified 862 publications meeting inclusion criteria. These comprised 825 original articles (95.7%), 16 review articles (1.9%), and 19 proceedings papers (2.2%). Two retracted articles were excluded from analysis. Publications involved 5111 authors from 372 institutions across 67 countries. Single-author publications numbered 16 (1.9%); multi-author collaborations constituted 98.1% of publications. Mean author count per publication was 5.9 (standard deviation [SD] = 3.2). International collaborations represented 24.1% of publications.

3.3. Historical perspective

The earliest publication in our dataset appeared in January 2014, authored by Larkin GL et al., titled “Mental Health and Emergency Medicine: A Research Agenda” published in Academic Emergency Medicine. This foundational work established key research priorities including: crisis intervention models, boarding reduction strategies, substance use screening protocols, and suicide risk assessment tools. Between 2014–2016, early research concentrated primarily on epidemiological descriptions of psychiatric emergency department utilization patterns and demographic characterization of patient populations.

Publication volume demonstrated consistent growth across the study period (Fig. 1). Annual output increased from 52 publications in 2014 to 126 publications in 2023, representing 142% growth (annual growth rate 9.8%, R2 = 0.94, P < .001). Growth slowed markedly in 2020 (98 publications, an increase of only 3 over the 95 recorded in 2019, against a mean year-on-year gain of approximately 8 publications across the period), coinciding with the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, before accelerating again thereafter. The growth pattern suggested an acceleration in publication rate after 2017, with mean annual increase of 7.4 publications (95% CI: 5.8–9.0).

3.4. Geographic distribution and international collaboration

Geographic distribution exhibited substantial concentration (Fig. 2). The United States produced 488 publications (56.7% of total), establishing clear dominance in the field. Turkey ranked second with 46 publications (5.3%), followed by Canada (44 publications, 5.1%), Australia (40, 4.7%), and Iran (33, 3.8%). The top ten countries accounted for 79.2% of total publications. High-income countries produced 92.1% of publications, upper-middle-income countries contributed 6.8%, and lower-middle-income countries accounted for 1.1%. No publications originated from low-income countries, highlighting significant geographic inequity in research output.

International collaboration rates varied substantially across countries. Australia demonstrated the highest collaboration rate at 42.7%, while Iran showed the lowest at 8.2%. The overall international collaboration rate was 24.1%. Network analysis revealed 3 major collaboration clusters: North American (USA-Canada-UK, 156 collaborative publications), European (Germany-France-Netherlands-Switzerland, 78 collaborative publications), and Asia-Pacific (Australia-Japan-South Korea, 44 collaborative publications).

Zone 1 contained 4 core journals that accounted for 319 publications (37.0% of total output): the American Journal of Emergency Medicine (117 articles), Pediatric Emergency Care (75 articles), Academic Emergency Medicine (66 articles), and the Journal of Emergency Medicine (61 articles). Zone 2 contained 18 journals contributing 318 publications, and Zone 3 contained 221 journals contributing 223 publications. The top 20 journals by publication volume and citation metrics are summarized in Table 1. The concentration of more than one third of the corpus in 4 core journals confirms that psychiatric emergency medicine research is published predominantly in general emergency medicine journals rather than in psychiatry-specific outlets.

Citation analysis demonstrated a mean of 11.5 citations per article (SD 18.7, range 0–284). Normalized citation rates accounting for years since publication averaged 2.1 citations per year, and the h-index of the entire corpus was 47, indicating that 47 publications received at least 47 citations each. 3.6. Most productive and impactful authors

Analysis of authorship patterns identified 5111 unique authors contributing to the 862 publications. Betz ME (University of Colorado) led with 18 publications focusing on firearm safety and suicide prevention in emergency settings, accumulating 312 total citations. Ranney ML (Brown University) contributed 16 publications with emphasis on violence prevention and behavioral health screening, receiving 289 citations. Horowitz LM (National Institute of Mental Health) published 14 articles concentrating on pediatric suicide screening instrument development and validation, with 356 citations.

Analysis of most impactful authors by h-index within this dataset revealed: Betz ME (h-index = 12), indicating 12 publications with at least 12 citations each; Ranney ML (h-index = 11); and Goldstein AB (h-index = 10). The mean number of publications per productive author (defined as ≥ 5 publications) was 8.2 (SD = 3.4, range 5–18). Among the top 20 authors, 14 (70%) were affiliated with US institutions, 4 (20%) with Canadian institutions, and 2 (10%) with Australian institutions. Gender analysis revealed 11 female authors (55%) and 9 male authors (45%) among top contributors. The top 20 authors collectively produced 234 publications (27.1% of total output) and received 4156 citations (42.1% of total citations), demonstrating substantial concentration of productivity and impact.

3.7. Top institutions

Institutional analysis identified 372 unique institutions contributing to psychiatric emergency medicine research. The University of Colorado led with 34 publications (3.9% of total), followed by Harvard Medical School (28 publications, 3.2%), Brown University (26 publications, 3.0%), and University of California San Francisco (24 publications, 2.8%). The top 20 institutions collectively produced 378 publications (43.9% of total output).

Canadian institutions showed strong presence with University of Toronto ranking fifth (22 publications, 2.6%) and University of British Columbia eighth (19 publications, 2.2%) among top contributors. Other notable institutions included Yale University (21 publications), Johns Hopkins University (18 publications), and University of Michigan (17 publications). Among international institutions outside North America, Hacettepe University in Turkey (12 publications), University of Sydney in Australia (11 publications), and Tehran University of Medical Sciences in Iran (10 publications) demonstrated substantial contributions.

Institutional collaboration network analysis revealed a strong US-centered hub with international satellite connections primarily to Canadian, Australian, and UK institutions (Fig. 4). The University of Colorado showed highest collaboration degree (connections with 28 different institutions), followed by Harvard Medical School (24 institutions) and Brown University (21 institutions). Mean institutional collaboration degree was 6.8 (SD = 4.2) for top 20 institutions. International institutional collaborations most frequently occurred between US and Canadian institutions (89 collaborative publications), US and UK institutions (47 collaborative publications), and US and Australian institutions (34 collaborative publications). Within-country institutional networks were most developed in the United States (156 inter-institutional collaborations) and Canada (34 inter-institutional collaborations).

The second most-cited work was Horowitz et al (2012) “Ask Suicide-Screening Questions: A brief instrument for the pediatric emergency department” published in Archives of Pediatric and Adolescent Medicine (267 citations within 2014–2023 analysis period, mean 22.3 citations per year). Though published before the study period, its sustained citation impact qualified it for inclusion. This brief 4-item screening tool demonstrated feasibility and acceptability in busy emergency department settings with 96.9% sensitivity for suicide risk.

Third was Cappelli et al (2016) “Evaluating telepsychiatry for crisis intervention in pediatric emergency” published in Crisis (198 citations, mean 22.0 citations per year), demonstrating non-inferiority of telepsychiatry consultation compared to in-person assessment for pediatric psychiatric emergencies, with implications for expanding access to specialized psychiatric services.

Other highly cited articles included: Boudreaux et al on emergency department screening for suicide risk (186 citations); Newton et al examining pediatric mental health emergency department utilization trends (174 citations); and Doupnik et al on psychiatric boarding in pediatric emergency departments (168 citations). Among the top 50 most-cited articles, 32 (64%) addressed pediatric populations, 41 (82%) originated from US institutions, and 38 (76%) were published in emergency medicine or pediatric journals rather than psychiatric journals. The mean publication year of top 50 articles was 2016.2 (SD = 2.4), with 38 articles (76%) published between 2014–2018, indicating relatively early establishment of foundational literature during the study period.

Thematically, the top 50 most-cited articles concentrated on: suicide screening and risk assessment (18 articles, 36%), pediatric psychiatric emergency patterns and outcomes (14 articles, 28%), substance use and intoxication management (6 articles, 12%), telepsychiatry and consultation models (5 articles, 10%), psychiatric boarding and emergency department operations (4 articles, 8%), and geriatric psychiatric emergencies (3 articles, 6%). This distribution reflects field priorities and indicates suicide screening research achieved greatest impact and uptake within psychiatric emergency medicine practice.

3.9. Keyword analysis and research themes

Keyword analysis examined 4892 unique terms. Most frequent keywords were: care (111 occurrences, 12.9%), children/pediatric (64, 7.4%), visits/presentations (62, 7.2%), risk/risk assessment (58, 6.7%), management (52, 6.0%), suicide (48, 5.6%), prevalence (46, 5.3%), depression (43, 5.0%), anxiety (39, 4.5%), and substance use (37, 4.3%).

Cluster 1 – Pediatric Psychiatric Emergencies (186 articles, 21.6%): This cluster encompassed research on children, adolescents, and youth mental health crises. Key themes included developmental considerations in psychiatric assessment, parental involvement in treatment decisions, school-based prevention programs, and pediatric-specific screening instruments. Representative highly-cited articles addressed validation of suicide screening tools in pediatric populations and characterization of increasing pediatric mental health emergency department utilization.

Cluster 2 – Substance Use and Intoxication (174 articles, 20.2%): Research focused on alcohol intoxication, opioid overdose, stimulant-related emergencies, and polysubstance use. Key themes included naloxone administration protocols, medication-assisted treatment initiation in emergency settings, screening and brief intervention approaches, and toxicological considerations. Studies addressed both acute management and linkage to ongoing addiction treatment.

Cluster 3 – Suicide and Self-Harm (158 articles, 18.3%): This cluster examined suicide attempts, suicidal ideation, nonsuicidal self-injury, and prevention strategies. Major themes included risk stratification instruments, safety planning interventions, means restriction counseling, and follow-up care coordination. Research emphasized evidence-based screening approaches and disposition decision-making.

Cluster 4 – Psychiatric Consultation Models (142 articles, 16.5%): Studies addressed emergency psychiatric consultation services, telepsychiatry applications, crisis intervention team approaches, and integrated behavioral health models. Research examined consultation response times, disposition outcomes, and patient satisfaction. Telepsychiatry emerged as rapidly growing subtheme, particularly after 2020.

Cluster 5 – Emergency Department Operations (127 articles, 14.7%): This operational research cluster addressed psychiatric boarding, length of stay, resource utilization, and capacity management. Studies examined factors contributing to prolonged emergency department stays for psychiatric patients, interventions to reduce boarding, and economic impact of psychiatric emergency visits.

Cluster 6 – Geriatric Psychiatric Emergencies (75 articles, 8.7%): Research focused on delirium assessment and management, behavioral disturbances in dementia, late-life depression, and cognitive impairment evaluation. Studies addressed challenges in distinguishing psychiatric from medical causes of behavioral changes in older adults and appropriate disposition for geriatric psychiatric patients.

3.11. Temporal trend analysis

Temporal trend analysis revealed significant evolution in research themes over the study period. Pediatric psychiatric emergencies demonstrated a marked increasing trend with 12.4% annual growth post-2017 (P < .01), driven by rising pediatric mental health emergency department utilization rates and heightened national attention to the youth mental health crisis. This growth pattern reflected broader societal concerns about adolescent mental health, particularly regarding depression, anxiety, and suicidal behavior among young people.

Geriatric psychiatric emergencies similarly showed substantial growth with 15.2% annual increase (P < .01), reflecting aging population demographics and increased recognition of unique geriatric psychiatric emergency needs. This trend corresponded with growing awareness that older adults presenting with behavioral disturbances require specialized assessment approaches to distinguish psychiatric from medical etiologies, particularly regarding delirium, dementia-related behavioral symptoms, and late-life mood disorders.

Telepsychiatry research increased substantially, particularly after 2020, demonstrating 31.3% annual growth from 2020–2023 (P < .001). This acceleration was directly attributable to the COVID-19 pandemic and associated regulatory changes facilitating telemedicine implementation. Research during this period shifted from feasibility studies to implementation science, comparative effectiveness analyses, and patient satisfaction assessments, reflecting rapid real-world adoption of telepsychiatry consultation models in emergency settings.

Substance use research showed modest decline at -3.2% annually (P = .08), not reaching statistical significance but suggesting potential topic saturation or methodological shift. Analysis indicated this decline reflected transition from general substance use studies toward more focused research on specific substances, particularly opioid-related emergencies, synthetic cannabinoids, and novel psychoactive substances. The overall volume of addiction-related research remained substantial but became more specialized rather than broadly categorized under substance use.

Suicide and self-harm research remained remarkably stable throughout the period with only 0.8% annual change (P = .72), maintaining consistent representation at approximately 18% of publications annually. This stability suggested sustained research priority and continuous knowledge development rather than episodic attention. The consistent focus reflected ongoing clinical importance of suicide risk assessment and prevention as core components of psychiatric emergency medicine practice.

3.12. Thematic mapping

Strategic diagram analysis classified research domains into 4 distinct categories based on centrality and density measures (Fig. 6), providing insight into theme maturity, interconnectedness, and field positioning. Motor themes, characterized by high centrality and high density, included emergency department visits and utilization patterns, suicide risk assessment and screening, length of stay and boarding, and pediatric psychiatric crises. These represented well-developed, important research areas with strong internal structure and extensive external connections to other themes. Motor themes served as drivers of field advancement, connecting multiple research streams and demonstrating both theoretical development and practical relevance. Their position indicated central importance to psychiatric emergency medicine scholarship and substantial influence on related research areas.

Niche themes, demonstrating low centrality but high density, encompassed specific pharmacological interventions including ketamine for agitation and rapid tranquilization protocols with antipsychotics, telepsychiatry applications and associated technologies, physical restraint and seclusion management practices, and cultural competency in psychiatric emergency care delivery. These represented specialized areas with well-developed internal research programs and dedicated expert communities but limited connection to the broader field. Niche themes indicated focused expertise addressing specific clinical questions or populations, characterized by depth rather than breadth of influence. Their isolation suggested opportunities for integration with motor themes to enhance field-wide impact.

Emerging or declining themes, characterized by low centrality and low density, included artificial intelligence and machine learning applications for risk prediction and decision support, precision psychiatry approaches tailoring interventions to individual patient characteristics, social media impacts on mental health crises particularly among adolescents, and long-term outcomes following psychiatric emergency department visits. These themes showed weak development and marginal current importance but represented potential future growth areas warranting strategic investment. Their underdeveloped status reflected either nascent research programs not yet achieving critical mass or declining interest in previously explored topics. The emerging versus declining distinction required longitudinal monitoring to differentiate growth trajectories from fading research interests.

Basic themes, demonstrating high centrality but low density, comprised healthcare policy and insurance coverage affecting psychiatric emergency care access, general access to care and mental health service availability, stigma and discrimination experiences among psychiatric emergency patients, and mental health workforce issues including emergency psychiatry staffing and training. These were recognized as important transversal themes connecting to multiple research areas across the field but lacking cohesive, focused research development within psychiatric emergency medicine literature specifically. Basic themes represented foundational issues acknowledged as significant by researchers but not yet consolidated into structured research programs. Their high centrality indicated widespread relevance, while low density suggested fragmented rather than coordinated investigation, presenting opportunities for systematic research agenda development.

3.13. Subgroup analysis by economic development

Subgroup analysis stratified by World Bank economic classification revealed substantial differences in research methodologies, collaboration patterns, and thematic focus between high-income and middle-income countries. Regarding research design rigor, high-income countries published significantly greater proportions of methodologically rigorous prospective studies at 31.2% compared to middle-income countries at 18.5% (P < .001). Conversely, retrospective database studies were more common in middle-income countries, comprising 64.8% of publications versus 51.3% in high-income countries. This disparity reflected differences in research infrastructure, funding availability, and capacity for conducting resource-intensive prospective investigations. The predominance of retrospective designs in middle-income settings suggested reliance on existing administrative data and medical records rather than prospectively designed studies with standardized data collection protocols.

Collaboration patterns demonstrated marked differences, with high-income countries showing substantially higher rates of international collaboration at 26.8% compared to 14.3% for middle-income countries (P < .001). Furthermore, publications from high-income countries involved mean 1.8 collaborating countries per international publication compared to 1.3 for middle-income countries, indicating more extensive multinational research networks. These patterns suggested that high-income institutions served as collaboration hubs while middle-income country researchers faced greater barriers to international partnership formation, potentially related to funding disparities, language considerations, and established academic networks favoring high-income country connections.

Citation impact analysis revealed significantly higher mean citation rates for publications from high-income countries at 12.1 citations per article (95% confidence interval [CI]: 11.4–12.8) compared to middle-income countries at 8.4 citations per article (95% CI: 6.9–9.9, P < .01). This 44% difference in citation impact persisted even after controlling for publication year and journal impact factor, suggesting factors beyond simple visibility contributed to differential citation patterns. Possible explanations included greater focus on universally relevant research questions in high-income country studies, stronger methodological rigor influencing citation likelihood, language advantages for English-language publications from predominantly English-speaking high-income countries, and citation bias favoring work from prestigious institutions concentrated in high-income nations.

Research focus diverged substantially between economic groups. High-income countries published more frequently on service delivery models, comprising 23.4% of their publications, and quality improvement initiatives at 18.7%. These topics reflected mature healthcare systems with capacity to systematically evaluate and refine service delivery approaches. In contrast, middle-income countries concentrated more heavily on epidemiological descriptions at 41.2% of publications and clinical case series at 28.6%. This pattern suggested middle-income country research remained in earlier developmental stages, establishing foundational epidemiological understanding and clinical characterization before progressing to intervention evaluation and system optimization studies typical of high-income country research portfolios.

3.14. Synthesis of findings and knowledge gaps

This comprehensive bibliometric analysis reveals psychiatric emergency medicine as a rapidly expanding field characterized by strong geographic concentration in high-income countries, particularly the United States, alongside emerging but still limited international collaboration networks. The identification of 6 major research clusters demonstrates both substantial breadth of psychiatric emergency medicine scholarship and concerning potential fragmentation, with relatively isolated research communities addressing distinct clinical populations and practice domains. Several critical knowledge gaps emerged from systematic analysis of publication patterns, thematic evolution, and methodological approaches.

Geographic inequity represents perhaps the most striking finding, with complete absence of research contributions from low-income countries and minimal representation from middle-income countries. This pattern suggests substantial global knowledge gaps in psychiatric emergency care delivery systems, appropriate assessment and management approaches for resource-limited settings, and cultural adaptations of screening instruments and interventions developed primarily in North American contexts. The concentration of research in wealthy Western nations limits generalizability of findings and potentially perpetuates care delivery models inappropriate for global majority populations.

Methodological limitations pervade the literature, with predominance of retrospective observational designs comprising 58.4% of total publications. While such studies provide valuable descriptive epidemiology and generate hypotheses, the relative scarcity of prospective cohort studies and randomized controlled trials evaluating interventions limits causal inference and evidence-based practice guideline development. This methodological distribution suggests the field remains in relatively early developmental stages regarding intervention research, with substantial need for rigorous comparative effectiveness studies and implementation trials.

Underdeveloped themes including artificial intelligence applications for risk prediction and clinical decision support, long-term outcomes following psychiatric emergency encounters, and precision psychiatry approaches tailoring interventions to individual patient characteristics remain substantially underexplored despite considerable potential impact on future clinical practice. The marginal positioning of these topics in current thematic mapping suggests missed opportunities for innovation and modernization of psychiatric emergency care delivery. Strategic research investment in these emerging areas could substantially advance the field beyond current practice paradigms.

Integration gaps characterize basic themes including healthcare policy, access to care barriers, and mental health workforce development. Despite recognition of these issues as fundamentally important to psychiatric emergency care delivery, research addressing these topics remains fragmented rather than consolidated into coordinated research programs. The high centrality but low density of these themes indicates widespread acknowledgment of significance without corresponding systematic investigation, suggesting need for deliberate agenda-setting and multi-institutional collaborative efforts to develop cohesive knowledge bases addressing systemic challenges.

Pediatric-adult population imbalance constitutes another significant gap, with substantial research attention devoted to pediatric psychiatric emergencies comprising 21.6% of publications while geriatric populations receive limited focus at only 8.7% of research output. This disparity appears increasingly problematic given rapidly aging global demographics and unique complexities of geriatric psychiatric emergency assessment, including challenges distinguishing medical from psychiatric etiologies of behavioral disturbances, medication considerations in older adults, and appropriate disposition decisions for geriatric patients. The relative neglect of geriatric psychiatric emergency research represents a critical gap requiring urgent attention.

Implementation science deficiency represents a final major limitation, with relatively little research addressing implementation of evidence-based practices, dissemination strategies for validated screening instruments and interventions, or real-world effectiveness of approaches shown efficacious in controlled studies. The gap between research evidence and routine clinical practice implementation suggests need for greater emphasis on pragmatic trials, implementation frameworks, and evaluation of strategies for promoting uptake of evidence-based psychiatric emergency care practices across diverse emergency department settings.

3.15. Proposed research framework

Based on systematically identified knowledge gaps and considering current field strengths, we propose an integrated research framework comprising 4 priority domains designed to address critical limitations while building on established research foundations. This framework emphasizes methodological rigor, international collaboration, practical relevance, and explicit attention to implementation and dissemination alongside traditional efficacy research.

Domain 1 focuses on Global Psychiatric Emergency Care, establishing international research networks to characterize psychiatric emergency care delivery systems in diverse economic and cultural contexts. This domain prioritizes descriptive epidemiology in currently underrepresented regions, cross-cultural validation of screening instruments and risk assessment tools, development of culturally-adapted interventions appropriate for diverse populations, and evaluation of scalable, resource-appropriate approaches for settings with limited psychiatric specialty availability. Research under this domain should explicitly include investigators from low- and middle-income countries as equal partners rather than simply study sites, building sustainable research capacity while addressing critical knowledge gaps regarding global psychiatric emergency care delivery.

Domain 2 advocates for a Life-Course Approach, implementing age-specific research programs addressing developmentally appropriate psychiatric emergency assessment, acute treatment, and disposition decision-making across pediatric, adult, and geriatric populations. Particular emphasis should be placed on geriatric psychiatric emergency research given current underrepresentation and demographic trends. This domain includes comparative studies examining how assessment approaches, screening instrument performance, intervention effectiveness, and outcomes vary across developmental stages. Research should address transitions between pediatric and adult emergency psychiatric care systems and unique considerations for older adults including medical-psychiatric differentiation, medication safety, and appropriate disposition options for geriatric patients with psychiatric emergencies.

Domain 3 addresses Technology Integration, systematically evaluating artificial intelligence and machine learning applications for suicide risk stratification, clinical decision support systems, and emergency department resource allocation optimization. This domain extends beyond simple feasibility studies to rigorous comparative effectiveness research evaluating AI-augmented versus traditional clinical approaches, implementation science examining barriers and facilitators to technology adoption, and ethical considerations regarding algorithmic decision-making in psychiatric emergency contexts. Simultaneously, this domain expands telepsychiatry research beyond current feasibility focus toward comparative effectiveness studies, cost-effectiveness analyses, implementation outcomes research, and evaluation of hybrid models combining telepsychiatry with in-person assessment for optimal resource utilization.

Domain 4 emphasizes System-Level Innovation, conducting health services research addressing persistent challenges including psychiatric boarding and emergency department crowding, alternative disposition models beyond traditional inpatient psychiatric admission, integration of emergency departments within broader crisis care systems including mobile crisis teams and crisis stabilization units, and mental health workforce development including emergency psychiatry fellowship training and emergency physician behavioral health competency enhancement. This domain prioritizes policy-relevant outcomes, pragmatic trial designs evaluating real-world effectiveness, and explicit implementation strategies facilitating translation of research findings into routine practice improvements across diverse emergency care settings.

This proposed framework deliberately addresses current limitations including geographic concentration, methodological constraints, underdeveloped themes, and implementation gaps while building strategically on established research strengths in suicide screening instrument development and validation, pediatric psychiatric emergency epidemiology and care delivery, and psychiatric consultation models. Successful implementation requires sustained funding commitment, deliberate international collaboration fostering equitable partnerships, methodological sophistication including rigorous prospective designs and implementation science approaches, and explicit attention to practical relevance ensuring research findings directly inform clinical practice and health system improvements in psychiatric emergency care delivery.

4. Discussion

This bibliometric analysis examined 862 publications in psychiatric emergency medicine over a ten-year period (2014–2023). Results demonstrate consistent growth in research output, geographic concentration of productivity, evolution toward focus on vulnerable populations, and emergence of novel research domains.

4.1. Summary of main findings

This analysis revealed substantial growth in psychiatric emergency medicine research, with publication volume more than doubling from 52 articles in 2014 to 126 articles in 2023. Research remained heavily concentrated in high-income countries, particularly the United States, with limited representation from middle-income countries and complete absence from low-income nations. Six major research themes emerged: pediatric psychiatric emergencies, substance use and intoxication, suicide and self-harm, psychiatric consultation models, emergency department operations, and geriatric psychiatric emergencies. Suicide screening and risk assessment tools garnered the highest citation impact, indicating substantial influence on clinical practice. International collaboration occurred in approximately one-quarter of publications, primarily connecting North American, European, and Asia-Pacific research networks. The field demonstrated evolution from descriptive epidemiology toward intervention studies and service delivery research, with emerging interest in telepsychiatry, artificial intelligence applications, and implementation science approaches.

4.2. Interpretation of findings

Publication growth averaged 8.3% annually, with acceleration after 2017 to 12.4% annually. This growth rate exceeds that reported for emergency medicine broadly (6.7%), suggesting psychiatric emergency medicine as a particularly dynamic subdomain.[2] The 2017 inflection point may reflect increased recognition of emergency departments as mental health safety nets, policy initiatives addressing psychiatric boarding, and growing awareness of mental health crisis care needs. The 2020 slowdown in growth aligns with documented disruptions across medical research due to the COVID-19 pandemic, followed by rapid recovery as pandemic-related mental health research increased.[5,11]

Recent developments in 2025 further emphasize field growth. The American Board of Medical Specialties approved focused practice designation in emergency behavioral health in February 2025, representing formal recognition of this practice domain.[12] The Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration published guidance in January 2025 defining emergency behavioral health centers as essential crisis care components.[13] These developments establish emergency psychiatry as a distinct practice area requiring specialized competencies and suggest continued research expansion.

Geographic distribution revealed marked concentration. The United States produced 56.7% of publications, consistent with patterns observed across medical specialties but particularly pronounced in psychiatric emergency research. This concentration may reflect historical factors, as emergency medicine and emergency psychiatry as organized specialties originated in the United States.[1,2]However, this concentration raises questions regarding generalizability of findings to diverse healthcare systems and patient populations.

Turkey position as second-highest producer (5.3% of publications) represents notable achievement potentially reflecting governmental research incentives and emphasis on indexed publications for academic advancement.[14] However, Turkey low international collaboration rate (12.3%) suggests limited knowledge exchange with international research networks. Similar patterns in Iran and other middle-income countries indicate structural barriers to international partnership requiring investigation.

Australia demonstrated highest international collaboration rate (42.7%) despite moderate publication volume, suggesting efficient integration into international research networks. This pattern may provide a model for countries seeking to maximize research impact with limited resources. The near-absence of publications from low-income countries reflects global research inequities, with countries experiencing greatest psychiatric emergency burden producing least research.

Citation analysis revealed mean rate of 11.5 citations per article, consistent with emergency medicine norms. The concentration of 42.8% of citations among top 50 articles demonstrates typical academic citation patterns but suggests potential over-reliance on limited foundational studies. Academic Emergency Medicine demonstrated highest normalized citation impact despite lower publication volume than American Journal of Emergency Medicine, indicating selective publication strategy emphasizing methodological rigor.

Keyword analysis and thematic mapping identified 6 major research clusters.[9,10] Suicide and self-harm research demonstrated highest centrality in the network, confirming this as a central concern appropriately reflecting clinical importance and emergency department role in prevention. Pediatric psychiatric emergencies showed strong increasing trend, aligning with documented increases in child and adolescent mental health presentations to emergency departments.[15] Recent data indicate emergency department visits for mental health among youth continue increasing, with presentations in 2025 showing sustained growth from pre-pandemic levels.

Geriatric psychiatric emergencies demonstrated similarly strong growth trajectory, addressing an aging population and unique presentations at interfaces of delirium, dementia, and depression. This growth represents an appropriate response to demographic shifts. Research in this area remains relatively underdeveloped despite increasing volume, suggesting opportunities for further investigation.

Substance use research showed modest declining trend despite ongoing public health challenges including opioid epidemic. This decline may indicate topic migration to specialized addiction medicine and toxicology journals rather than decreased clinical relevance.[14] Alternatively, it may suggest research saturation in descriptive epidemiology with need for innovative intervention studies.

Thematic mapping revealed important patterns.[9] Motor themes (emergency department visits, risk assessment, boarding) represent mature, influential research areas but may benefit from innovative methodological approaches to avoid repetitive investigation. Healthcare policy and access characterization as basic themes (high centrality, low density) reveals critical gap. Despite recognized importance, this area lacks cohesive research development. Given emergency departments’ role in mental health safety net and ongoing policy debates regarding crisis care financing and psychiatric boarding, this underdevelopment warrants attention.

Emerging themes including artificial intelligence applications, precision psychiatry, and social media impacts represent promising directions but require substantial investment given current low development. Recent 2025 publications address implementation of simulation-based training for psychiatric emergency management and qualitative studies examining healthcare provider experiences with psychiatric emergencies, suggesting evolution toward implementation science and quality improvement research.

Methodological considerations include single-database approach limiting capture of nonindexed publications, English-language restriction potentially underrepresenting research from non-English-speaking countries, and temporal bias affecting citation accumulation for recent publications. Bibliometric analysis examines publication patterns and impact but cannot assess clinical significance or methodological quality of individual studies,.[1,14–17]

Specifically, reliance on the Web of Science Core Collection as the sole bibliographic source carries known trade-offs that warrant explicit acknowledgement. Web of Science emphasizes journals indexed in the Science Citation Index Expanded and Social Sciences Citation Index, which yields broad biomedical coverage relative to Embase or PsycINFO but narrower journal coverage relative to Scopus. PubMed-indexed publications appearing in journals outside the Web of Science Core Collection were therefore not captured, and this exclusion may particularly affect smaller regional psychiatry journals and recent open-access outlets. Cross-database comparative bibliometric studies in adjacent fields have reported overlap of approximately 75–90% between Web of Science and Scopus for biomedical research, suggesting that the principal trends reported here regarding publication growth, geographic concentration, and thematic clustering are robust, while absolute publication counts and citation totals should be interpreted as conservative lower-bound estimates. Future bibliometric work in psychiatric emergency medicine would benefit from triangulation across Web of Science, Scopus, and PubMed to refine these estimates and to capture publications indexed in only one source.

Clinical implications include recognition that evidence base emphasizes pediatric and suicide-related research, potentially overshadowing other conditions requiring investigation. Research supporting geriatric psychiatric emergency care remains limited despite growth. Policy research underdevelopment limits evidence-based policy formation. Research priorities should address underrepresented populations, advance methodological rigor through increased prospective designs, develop health services and policy research, conduct implementation science studies, and support capacity building in low- and middle-income countries.[18,19]

Future research should examine global epidemiology through multinational studies, focus investigations on vulnerable and marginalized populations, conduct longitudinal outcome studies, perform comparative effectiveness trials, implement and evaluate evidence-based interventions, address health equity explicitly, validate novel technologies including artificial intelligence applications, optimize systems integration, and develop workforce training and support structures.

5. Conclusion

Psychiatric emergency medicine research demonstrated consistent growth over the decade examined, with evolution toward focus on vulnerable populations including children and older adults. Publication output remained geographically concentrated with limited international collaboration. Research priorities evolved but gaps persist in health services research, global representation, and long-term outcomes. Recent developments in 2025 including focused practice designation and formal recognition of emergency behavioral health centers indicate continued field maturation. Addressing identified gaps through international partnerships, health equity initiatives, and innovative methodological approaches can advance evidence generation to improve care for individuals experiencing mental health crises in emergency settings.
