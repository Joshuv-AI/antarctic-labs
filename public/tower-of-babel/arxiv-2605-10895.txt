FPT Approximation Schemes for Min-Sum Radii and Min-Sum Diameters Clustering
Fabrizio Grandoni, Anupam Gupta, Jatin Yadav

Abstract
In the classical Min-Sum Radii problem ( MSR ) we are given a set X X of n n points in a metric space and a positive integer k ∈ [ n ] k\in[n] . Our goal is to partition X X into k k subsets (the clusters ) so as to minimize the sum of the radii of these clusters. The Min-Sum Diameters problem ( MSD ) is defined analogously, where instead of the radii of the clusters we consider their diameters. For both problems we present FPT approximation schemes for the natural parameter k k . Specifically, given ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 , we show how to compute ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) -approximations for both MSD and MSR in time ( 1 / ε ) k ​ n O ⁡ ( 1 ) (1/\varepsilon)^{k}n^{O(1)} and ( 1 / ε ) O ⁡ ( k / ε ​ log ⁡ 1 / ε ) ​ n poly ⁡ ( 1 / ε ) (1/\varepsilon)^{O(k/\varepsilon\log 1/\varepsilon)}n^{\poly(1/\varepsilon)} respectively. The previous best FPT approximation algorithms for these problems have approximation factors 4 + ε 4+\varepsilon and 2 + ε 2+\varepsilon , respectively, and finding an FPT approximation scheme for both these problems had been outstanding open problems.



1
Introduction

We consider two classical clustering objectives closely related to
each other:
min-sum radii
and
min-sum diameters
, which
seek to partition the points of a given metric space into
k

clusters, such that the sum of their radii or diameters is
minimized. Both these problems have long been known to be NP-hard, but
their approximability has been a source of much interest, as we detail
below. In this work, we study these questions from the perspective of

parameterized complexity
, and ask:
what can we do in time

f(k)\poly(n)
, where
k
is the number of clusters and
n
the
size of the metric space?

Formally, both the Min-Sum Radii and Min-Sum Diameters problems are
defined on a (symmetric) metric space
\mathcal{M}=(X,d)
with
X
being a
collection of
n

points
. Given an integer
k\in[n]
denoting
the target number of clusters, the goal in both problems is to
partition the point set
X
into
k
sets (also called

clusters
)
S_{1},S_{2},\ldots,S_{k}
. The difference between the
two problems is in their objective functions:

•

The diameter of a cluster
S
is the maximum distance between
any pair of points in
S
—i.e.,

\operatorname{diam}(S)=\max_{p,q\in S}d(p,q).

In the Min-Sum Diameters (
MSD
)
problem, the goal is to minimize
\sum_{i\in[k]}\operatorname{diam}(S_{i})
.

•

The
center
(or the
1
-center) of a cluster
S
is a
point
p\in X
minimizing
\max_{q\in S}d(p,q)
; the latter
quantity is called the
radius
of the
cluster.In other words,

\operatorname{rad}(S):=\min_{p\in X}\max_{q\in S}d(p,q).

In the
Min-Sum Radii (
MSR
) problem, the goal is to minimize

\sum_{i\in[k]}\operatorname{rad}(S_{i})
.

Both these problems are
NP-hard
[
11
,
14
]
,
and hence have been studied using the lens of approximation
algorithms. Over a sequence of works giving polynomial-time approximation algorithms
[
11
,
8
,
13
,
6
]
,
the approximation ratio for
MSR
has been improved to
3+\varepsilon
;
since the two objectives are separated by
a factor of at most
2
, this implies a
(6+\varepsilon)
-approximation for

MSD
as well, which is currently the best approximation factor
known. The
MSD
problem does not admit a polynomial time algorithm
with an approximation factor of better than
2
, unless

P=NP

[
11
]
; however,
MSR
admits a
quasi-polynomial time approximation scheme
(QPTAS)
[
14
]
, and hence a polynomial
time approximation scheme (PTAS) is likely to exist.

MSR
and
MSD
are also well-studied in
terms of parameterized approximation algorithms, where we allow the
running time to depend super-polynomially in some parameter. For the
natural parameter
k
, the best known results are a
2+\varepsilon
(resp.,

4+\varepsilon
) approximation for
MSR
(resp.,
MSD
) in FPT time

f(k,\varepsilon)\cdot n^{g(\varepsilon)}
for some computable functions
f,g

[
2
,
9
]
.
In this work, our focus is on
FPT approximation schemes
—i.e.,

(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithms running in time

f(k,\varepsilon)\cdot n^{\poly(1/\varepsilon)}
for some computable function

f
. The following natural question
remains unresolved:
do these two problems admit FPT
Approximation Schemes?

1.1
Our Results

The main results of our work answer the above question in the
affirmative. For the
MSR
problem, which is known to be
W[1]
-hard
[
4
]
, we substantially improve upon the mentioned
(2+\varepsilon)
-approximation in FPT time

[
2
,
9
]
.

Theorem 1.1

(Min-Sum Radii)
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, one can compute a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation for

MSR
in deterministic time

(1/\varepsilon)^{O(\nicefrac{{k}}{{\varepsilon}}\ln\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}})}n^{O(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{3}}}\ln\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}})}
.

We then turn our attention to the
MSD
problem. It is remarkable that
essentially all previous approximation algorithms for this problem
were obtained by losing a factor of
2
over the results for
MSR
. In
other words, the best approximation factors were
6+\varepsilon
and
4+\varepsilon

in polynomial and FPT time respectively. We manage to break past this
issue, and give a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation in FPT time:

Theorem 1.2

(Min-Sum Diameters)
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, one can compute a
1+\varepsilon
approximation for
MSD
in randomized time
(O(1/\varepsilon))^{k}n^{O(1)}
and in deterministic
time
(O(k/\varepsilon))^{k}n^{O(1)}
.

Note that such a result is not possible in polynomial time: an
approximation of better than a factor of
2
would imply that

P=NP

[
11
]
.
Our approach also gives an exact algorithm
with runtime
n^{k+O(1)}
(see
Appendix

C
), thereby
improving the previous best runtime of
n^{5k+O(1)}
given
by
[
4
]
.

The same algorithm can also be utilized to give an FPT approximation scheme for the
MSD
problem (and thus a FPT
(2+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm for the
MSR
problem) with
mergeable constraints
. Here, we are given an upper bound
k
on the number of clusters and each cluster is required to satisfy some constraint. The constraint is such that, if two disjoint subsets of points satisfy the constraint, then so does their union.
Carta et al.
[
7
]
show that fairness constraints like
exact fairness
and
ratio balance
, as well as non-fairness constraints such as lower bounds on cluster sizes, are mergeable.
The previous best known result for mergeable constraints is an FPT
(4+\varepsilon)
-approximation for
MSR
(and an implied FPT
(8+\varepsilon)
-approximation for
MSD
)
[
1
]
.

Theorem 1.3

(Mergeable clustering)
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, one can compute a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation for
MSD
(and thus a
(2+\varepsilon)
-approximation for
MSR
) under any mergeable constraint (that is verifiable in polynomial time)
in randomized time
(O(1/\varepsilon))^{k}n^{O(1)}
and in deterministic
time
(O(k/\varepsilon))^{k}n^{O(1)}
.

1.2
Our Techniques

1.2.1
The Algorithm for Min-Sum Diameters

Our FPT approximation scheme for
MSD
is surprisingly clean, once
viewed from the right perspective. To explain the guiding
intuition, let us present a slight variant of our algorithm. We
claim that the following algorithm has probability at least

(\varepsilon/k)^{O(k)}
of returning a
1+O(\varepsilon)
approximate solution;
therefore, an FPT randomized approximation scheme can be obtained by
repeating it an FPT number of times. Our
recursive algorithm gets in input a subset of points
S\subseteq X

and a parameter
k^{\prime}\in[k]
, and returns a clustering of
S
with at
most
k^{\prime}
clusters. With probability
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
, the algorithm returns

\{S\}
itself as a clustering (this happens also when

k^{\prime}=1
). Otherwise, let
x,y\in S
be a diameter witness pair, i.e.,

d(x,y)=\operatorname{diam}(S)
. The algorithm chooses
R\in[0,\operatorname{diam}(S)]
uniformly
at random and
k_{1}\in[k^{\prime}-1]
uniformly at random. Then it returns the
union of the two solutions obtained recursively for the subproblems

(S_{1},k_{1})
and
(S\setminus S_{1},k^{\prime}-k_{1})
, where

S_{1}=S\cap\Ball(x,R)
and
\Ball(x,R)
is a ball of radius
R

centered at
x
(containing all the points at distance at most
R

from
x
). The desired clustering is obtained from the root call

(X,k)
.

To see the main idea of the analysis, consider the special case of the
root call. Let
X^{\star}_{1},\ldots,X^{\star}_{k}
be the optimal clustering, so that
the optimal cost is
\opt=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\operatorname{diam}(X^{\star}_{j})
. Consider the
diameter witness pair
x,y\in X
chosen for
X
. Let us project (see

Figure

1
) the points of
X
along the interval

I=[0,\operatorname{diam}(X)]
such that each
p\in X
is placed in position

d(x,p)
along
I
. In particular,
x
and
y
are projected to the
endpoints of
I
. Each optimal cluster
X^{\star}_{j}
induces an interval

I^{\star}_{j}
of width at most
\operatorname{diam}(X^{\star}_{j})
, where these intervals might
overlap. Crucially, the portion of
I
spanned by such intervals has
length at most
\opt
. Then, if
\operatorname{diam}(X)\leq\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\opt
, with probability
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
the
above algorithm returns
\{X\}
, which is a feasible
1+O(\varepsilon)

approximation.

Otherwise, the region of
I

not
spanned by the intervals

I^{\star}_{j}
has length at least
\varepsilon\cdot\operatorname{diam}(X)
. In this case, with
probability
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
the algorithm chooses to pick a random pair

(R,k_{1})
. Now, with probability at least
\varepsilon
,
R
falls in the
region of
I
not spanned by the intervals
I^{\star}_{j}
. Assuming that this
happens, each optimal cluster
X^{\star}_{j}
is either entirely at distance
at most
R
from
x
(hence it is fully contained in
S_{1}
for this
recursive call), or it is entirely at distance more than
R
from
x

(hence it is entirely contained in
X\setminus S_{1}
). Furthermore,
with probability
\frac{1}{k-1}
the chosen value
k_{1}
for this
recursive call is exactly the number of optimal clusters fully
contained in
S_{1}
. Therefore, the two subproblems
(S_{1},k_{1})
and

(X\setminus S_{1},k-k_{1})
partition the problem in two subproblems that
can be solved independently.

The actual algorithm in §
3
is an
optimized and slightly crisper version of the above description. Moreover, we can derandomize it at a small additional cost: the
details are given in
Appendix

B
.

1.2.2
The Algorithm for Min-Sum Radii

Our FPT
approximation scheme for
MSR
is substantially more involved. It is
instructive to first see how to achieve a
2(1+\varepsilon)
approximation in
FPT time (as done by

[
2
,
9
]
). Consider an optimal solution, which can be equivalently described as a set of balls
B_{1}^{\star},B_{2}^{\star},\ldots,B_{k}^{\star}
. We compute a set of balls that together cover all the points. Until there exists a yet-uncovered point
c_{j}
, we construct a new ball

B_{j}:=\Ball(c_{j},2r^{\star}_{\kappa(j)})
, where
r^{\star}_{\kappa(j)}
is the
radius of the optimal cluster
B^{\star}_{\kappa(j)}
containing

c_{j}
. Notice that
B_{j}
must entirely contain

B^{\star}_{\kappa(j)}
. Repeating this process ensures that we create at
most
k
balls covering all the points, whose total radius is at most

2
times the optimal cost
\opt
. The running time is affected by the
guessing of the values
r^{\star}_{\kappa(j)}
: this can be made FPT by a
simple preprocessing step (losing another factor
1+\varepsilon
in the
approximation).

Our improved FPT algorithm is based on the following three main ideas:

(a)

If we can define a ball
D:=\Ball(c,r)
such that the total
radius of the optimal clusters completely contained within
D
is at
least
r/(1+\varepsilon)
, we can output the ball
D
as part of the final solution, and charge the
radius
r
to the optimal clusters completely handled by it.

(b)

If there are a constant number
M
of optimal balls whose total
radius is an
\Omega(\varepsilon)
fraction of the optimal cost, we could guess
the centers and radii of these
M
balls in polynomial time,
and then recurse on the remaining points. Crucially, the optimal
cost of this residual instance is now smaller by a constant
factor. This can only happen for a constant number of recursion levels before the
residual cost is only an
\varepsilon
factor of
\opt
—whereupon we
can just use any
O(1)
-approximation algorithm.

(c)

Suppose that we find a ball

E:=\Ball(c,r)
and a collection
{\cal B}
of
boundary
balls around
E
(that intersect
E
without being contained in it) such that the total radius of
\cal B
is tiny, say
O(\varepsilon^{2}r)
. Then we can use a
O(1)
-approximation to cover the points inside
{\cal B}
, as
long as inside
E
we are able to compute balls of total radius
\Omega(\varepsilon r)
that are
close
to optimal.

Let us describe in more detail how to combine the above ideas. Our
starting step is the construction of an alternative set of

defensive
balls

D_{1},D_{2},\ldots,D_{\ell}
, with each ball of the form

D_{j}=\Ball(c_{j},r_{j})
, which together cover all the points
X

2+\varepsilon

described above and with a similar notation, we pick an arbitrary

c_{j}

D_{j}:=\Ball(c_{j},2r^{\star}_{\kappa(j)})
(later we call
B^{\star}_{\kappa(j)}

the
core
of
D_{j}
). If
D_{j}
includes the center of some other

B^{\star}_{h}
still containing uncovered points, we increase
the radius of
D_{j}
by the radius

of the largest such

B^{\star}_{j}
is fully covered by

D_{j}

D_{j}

The (
defensive
) clustering induced by the defensive balls has
already some useful properties:

1.

For each optimal cluster
B^{\star}_{i}
, there exists at least one
defensive ball
D_{j}
fully containing it. We say that
B^{\star}_{i}
is
assigned to the first
D_{j}
that fully covers
B^{\star}_{i}
, and let

I(j)
be the indexes of the optimal clusters assigned to
D_{j}

(including necessarily
\kappa(j)
).

2.

Observe that
r_{j}\leq r^{\star}_{\kappa(j)}+\sum_{i\in I(j)}r^{\star}_{i}
. If
we are lucky and
r_{j}\leq(1+O(\varepsilon))\sum_{i\in I(j)}r^{\star}_{i}
, then
we can use Idea
(a)
to directly include
D_{j}
in our
solution; we say that such a
D_{j}
is
good
.

However, the defensive balls may not satisfy the condition

r_{j}\leq(1+O(\varepsilon))\sum_{i\in I(j)}r^{\star}_{i}
. In that case, we call
such a defensive ball
bad
. As an extreme case, it might happen
that
r_{j}=2r^{\star}_{\kappa(j)}
for all the defensive balls
D_{j}
—we
need further ideas for this case, if we are to get a better-than-
2

approximation.

The second part of our algorithm is designed to address the problem
due to the presence of bad defensive balls. In this second part, we
pick some (bad) defensive ball
D
, and
expand
it to
incorporate other defensive balls, according to the following
construction (that has some similarities with the construction of
D

itself, but with some critical differences). Let
E=\Ball(c,r^{\prime})
be
the current expanded ball (initially
E=D
), and consider the

boundary balls

{\cal B}
of
E
, i.e., the defensive balls
that intersect with
E
without being fully covered by
E
. We
iteratively increase the radius of
E
as long as one of the following
conditions holds:

•

(
cheap expansion
) We increase the radius of
E
by the minimal amount
S
such that

E
fully contain
{\cal B}
, under the assumption that the total radius of the latter balls is at least
\rho S
for a carefully chosen constant
\rho\geq 2
. Notice that this expansion is
cheap
since by paying an extra
S
we incorporate defensive balls of total radius at least
2S
, and therefore optimal clusters of total radius at least
S
.

•

(
large expansion
) If there exists a boundary ball
B\in{\cal B}
of sufficiently large radius
r
w.r.t. to the radius
r^{\prime}
of
E
, namely
r\geq\Omega(\varepsilon^{2})r^{\prime}
, we increase the radius of
E
by
2r
for the largest such
r
. Notice that after this expansion
E
fully includes
B
.

We show that, for a constant
\rho
large enough, a constant fraction of the final radius
r^{\prime}
of
E
is due to large expansions (where we consider the initial
D
as a large expansion). Even more, a constant fraction of
r^{\prime}
is only due to the radius of a
constant
number
M
of defensive balls that
caused
such large expansions. Since the latter
M
defensive balls are bad, this means that their respective cores have total radius
\Omega(\varepsilon)r^{\prime}
. This way we can apply Idea
(b)
: we can guess the mentioned cores and capture a constant fraction of the profit of all the optimal clusters fully contained in
E
.

Furthermore, we show that the total radius of the final boundary balls
{\cal B}
is at most
O(\varepsilon^{2})r^{\prime}
. Hence we can apply Idea
(c)
: we include such balls in the solution under construction and charge their cost to the cores that we guessed before. After removing the points covered by
{\cal B}
, the algorithm then just continues recursively on the remaining points
X^{\prime}
inside
E
and in their complement
X^{\prime\prime}
(modulo guessing the number of optimal clusters that still need to be fully covered on the two sides).

We remark that, by the first property of the defensive balls, after removing the boundary balls, the remaining part of each optimal cluster is fully contained in
X^{\prime}
or in
X^{\prime\prime}
. In particular, it does not happen that some optimal cluster has to be covered in part in the subproblem associated with
X^{\prime}
and in part in the one associated with
X^{\prime\prime}
(which would cause at least a factor
2
in the approximation ratio). This would not be true if we used, instead of the defensive balls, the balls as in the
2+\varepsilon
approximation that we described before.

The final algorithm is actually slightly different for subtle
technical reasons, in particular, due to certain defensive balls covering the centers of some optimal balls that they do not defend; however, the above description captures the essential
ideas.

1.3
Related Work

Polynomial-time Approximate Algorithms.
Both exact and
approximation algorithms for
MSR
and
MSD
problems have been
extensively studied. (Any
\alpha
-approximation for
MSR
immediately
implies a
2\alpha
-approximation for
MSD
, so we do not explicitly
mention all the implied results for
MSD
henceforth.) For
approximation algorithms running in polynomial time, a sequence of
improvements
[
11
,
8
,
13
]

culminated in a
(3+\varepsilon)
-approximation for
MSR
by Buchem et
al.
[
6
]
; they also give a

(3+\varepsilon)
-approximation for
MSR

with outliers
, and a

(3.5+\varepsilon)
-approximation for
MSR

with outliers and
generalized lower bounds
, where there is a lower bound for each
point specifying the number of points that must be assigned to it, if
it is made a cluster center.

Exact Algorithms.
A remarkable result of
Gibson et al.
[
14
]
showed that the
MSR
problem can be solved exactly in
n^{O(\log n\log\Delta)}
time,
where
\Delta
is the aspect ratio of the metric space; they used this
algorithm to also give a QPTAS. In another
work
[
15
]
, they showed that
MSR
can be solved
in polynomial time for Euclidean spaces of fixed dimension. The
runtime of the exact algorithm for
MSR
in general metric spaces was
improved to
n^{O(\log n\log\log n+\log\Delta)}
by Banerjee et
al.
[
3
]
. While there is a trivial polynomial time exact
algorithm for
MSR
when
k
is a constant (one can choose the centers
and the radii of all the clusters in
n^{O(k)}
time), an exact
polynomial time algorithm for
MSD
for a constant
k
was first given
by Behsaz and Salavatipour
[
5
]
, with a runtime
of
n^{O(k^{2})}
. This was recently improved to
n^{5k+O(1)}
by
[
4
]
, which we further improve to

n^{k+O(1)}
in
Appendix

C
.

-approximation

,

]
;

]
.

, the works

[

,

,

,

]

[

]
. For

Preliminaries and Notation

For a metric space
\mathcal{M}=(X,d)
, a
k
-clustering
(or a

k
-partition
) is a collection of subsets

S_{1},\ldots,S_{k^{\prime}}
, where
k^{\prime}\leq k
, with

X=\cup_{i\in[k^{\prime}]}S_{i}
. Observe that we allow a
k
-clustering
to have fewer than
k
clusters. For the objective functions that we
consider, this is without loss of generality: one can transform any
clustering with
k^{\prime}<k
clusters into one having exactly
k
clusters
and no larger cost, by simply removing
k-k^{\prime}
points from some of the
clusters and forming singleton clusters using these points.

Given
c\in X
and a value
r\geq 0
, we let
\Ball(c,r)
denote the

ball
of radius
r
around the center
c
: i.e.,

\Ball(c,r):=\{x\in X\mid d(c,x)\leq r\}
. Given a ball
B
, we
use
c(B)
and
r(B)
to denote its
center
and
radius

respectively. Recall that the
diameter
of set
S\subseteq X
is defined as

\operatorname{diam}(S):=\max_{p,q\in S}d(p,q)
;
any vertex pair
p,q\in S
with

d(p,q)=\operatorname{diam}(S)
is called a
diameter witness
pair.

When considering the
MSR
problem, we define the cost for a set
\mathcal{B}

of balls to be
\cost(\mathcal{B}):=\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}r(B)
.
Analogously, when considering the
MSD
problem, the cost of a set

\mathcal{C}
of clusters is
\cost(\mathcal{C}):=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\operatorname{diam}(C)
.

Slightly abusing notation, we use the term
cluster
and

ball
interchangeably for
MSR
. In our algorithms for
MSR
, we
will consider subproblems identified by subsets
X^{\prime}\subseteq X
of
points. Having fixed some optimal solution
\OPT
for the
MSR
problem, given
X^{\prime}\subset X
, we define

\OPT(X^{\prime}):=\{\OPT_{i}\in\OPT\mid\OPT_{i}\cap X^{\prime}\neq\varnothing\}
to be
the set of optimal balls that contain at least one point in
X^{\prime}
. We
denote the corresponding cost by

\opt(X^{\prime}):=\sum_{\OPT_{i}\in\OPT(X^{\prime})}r(\OPT_{i})
.

and

Appendix

Let

The Procedure
\mathtt{recMSD}

The main technical lemma, which we prove next, shows that the output
clusterings have near-optimal cost with sufficiently high probability:

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\opt_{i}(S)
denote the cost of the optimal
i
-clustering of

S
and
(\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2},\ldots\mathcal{C}_{t})
be the output of

\mathtt{recMSD}(S,t)
. Then, for any
\varepsilon\in[0,1)
and any

r\in\{1,2,\ldots t\}

\Pr\left[\cost(\mathcal{C}_{r})\leq\frac{\opt_{r}(S)}{1-\varepsilon}\right]\geq\varepsilon^{r-1}.

Proof.

We prove this via induction on
t
. In the base case, namely
t=1
, the statement is trivially true since there is only one

1
-clustering. Otherwise, if
\opt_{r}(S)\geq\operatorname{diam}(S)(1-\varepsilon)
,
then again the statement is true, since

\text{cost}(\mathcal{C}_{r})\leq\operatorname{diam}(S)
. Otherwise, consider the clusters

X_{1},X_{2},\ldots X_{r^{\prime}}
in the optimal
r
-clustering of
S
,
where
r^{\prime}\leq r
. For each cluster
X_{h}
, define

to be the
smallest distance from
x
to some point in
X_{h}
and
R_{h}
to be
the largest distance from
x
to some point in
X_{h}
, and define

I_{h}
to be the interval
[L_{h},R_{h}]
. Observe that the length of

is at most
\operatorname{diam}(X_{h})

by
the triangle inequality. Hence, the sum of the lengths of the
r^{\prime}

intervals corresponding to the optimal clusters is at most

\opt_{r}(S)
. Now, since
\opt_{r}(S)<(1-\varepsilon)\operatorname{diam}(S)
, the
threshold

does not lie inside any of these intervals with
probability at least
\varepsilon
. In this event, we get a split
S_{1}
,

S_{2}
where
S_{1}
is precisely a union of some
r_{1}
clusters of the
optimal
r
-clustering of
S
and
S_{2}
is the union of the
remaining

r_{2}=r-r_{1}\geq r_{2}^{\prime}
. The algorithm computes
\mathcal{A}
as

\mathtt{recMSD}(S_{1},t-1)
and
\mathcal{B}
as the output
of
\mathtt{recMSD}(S_{2},t-1)

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\cost(\mathcal{A}_{r_{1}})\leq\frac{\opt_{r_{1}}(S_{1})}{1-\varepsilon}\right]\geq\varepsilon^{r_{1}-1}\quad\text{and}\quad\Pr\left[\cost(\mathcal{B}_{r_{2}})\leq\frac{\opt_{r_{2}}(S_{2})}{1-\varepsilon}\right]\geq\varepsilon^{r_{2}-1}.

If both these events happen, then after the
(\min,+)
convolution, we must have:

\displaystyle\text{cost}(\mathcal{C}_{r})\leq\text{cost}(\mathcal{A}_{r_{1}})+\text{cost}(\mathcal{B}_{r_{2}})\leq\frac{\opt_{r_{1}}(S_{1})+\opt_{r_{2}}(S_{2})}{1-\varepsilon}\leq\frac{\opt_{r_{1}}(S_{1})+\opt_{r^{\prime}_{2}}(S_{2})}{1-\varepsilon}=\frac{\opt_{r}(S)}{1-\varepsilon}.

where the second inequality holds since

and every

\Pr\left[\cost(\mathcal{C}_{r})\leq\frac{\opt_{r}(S)}{1-\varepsilon}\right]\geq\varepsilon\cdot\varepsilon^{r-2}=\varepsilon^{r-1}.\qed

Lemma 3.2
.

The procedure

4
An FPT Approximation Scheme for
MSR

In this section we describe our FPT approximation scheme for

MSR
. Using the preprocessing lemma (
Section

2.1
), we
can assume that the distances are integers and the optimum is bounded
by
\frac{8k}{\varepsilon}
. We fix a total ordering
\prec
over
X
, and
assume that
\prec
is globally available throughout the run of our
algorithm. Let
\OPT
denote the optimal solution (interpreted as a set of balls). We will assume that
no optimal ball in
\OPT
contains the center of another optimal ball
(since otherwise, we could expand the first optimal ball by the radius
of the second optimal ball to include the second optimal ball inside
it, without adding to the cost).

4.1
The General Approach

Our algorithm is recursive in nature. Suppose that we need to cluster
a set
S\subseteq X
of points using at most
t\leq k

clusters. Recall from
Section

2
that
\OPT(S)
denotes the
balls of the optimal solution
\OPT
(for the original problem on

(X,d)
with up to
k
clusters) which contain at least one point from

S
. Suppose that
\OPT(S)
consists of balls

B_{1}^{\star}=\Ball(c_{1}^{\star},r_{1}^{\star}),\ldots,B_{h}^{\star}=\Ball(c_{h}^{\star},r_{h}^{\star})
, where
c_{i}^{\star}
and

r_{i}^{\star}
denote the center and radius of the optimal ball

B_{i}^{\star}
. We will assume that
h\leq t
and
c_{i}^{\star}\in S
for all
i\in[h]
. We call pairs
(S,t)
that satisfy this condition as
valid pairs
.

Definition 4.1

(Valid Pair)
.

For a subset
S\subseteq X
and a parameter
t\leq k
, the pair

(S,t)
is said to be
valid

|\OPT(S)|\leq t
and the center of
each ball in
\OPT(S)
belongs to
S
.

Although not all subproblems we encounter will form a valid pair, our analysis will recursively maintain the invariant that the input to each recursive call is a valid pair. In particular, observe that the original input
(X,k)
forms a valid pair. Our algorithm consists of three phases:

1.

Computing a “defensive clustering”:
In this phase, we cluster
the subset
S
of points into “defensive balls”, such that each
optimal ball in
\OPT(S)
is contained in at least one defensive
ball. We ensure that these defensive balls themselves do not have
high cost, and satisfy some other good properties. (See

Section

4.2
for the definition of the defensive
clustering, and
Section

4.3
for the algorithm
to construct them.)

2.

Defining the subproblems:
In this phase, we extend a ball

E
(initialized as an arbitrary defensive ball), until we can
divide the problem into three parts: the points in the defensive
balls on the boundary of
E
, and then the non-boundary points
decompose into those falling inside
E
and those outside
E
. At
the end of this extension, we want that the defensive balls on the
boundary have a negligible cost compared to the radius of
E
, and
that a constant number of optimal balls inside
E
account for a
constant fraction of the radius of
E
. This is done in
Section

4.4.1
.

3.

Recursively Solving the Subproblems:
Since the defensive
balls on the boundary in our solution are inexpensive, we can just
include them in our solution (for technical reasons, we might need to expand these balls before including them, while charging the cost of expansion to the additional covered optimal balls). We now cover the points not covered by
these balls (the “non-boundary” points) by recursively solving the
problems inside and outside
E
independently. However, for the
inside problem, we need to first guess a constant number
M
of
optimal balls before the recursive call, thereby reducing the
optimal value on the sub-instance, and ensuring rapid progress.

The high level idea of the procedure is to choose a set
\mathcal{B}
of balls
to include in our solution, and cover the points not covered by
\mathcal{B}

recursively by dividing the remaining problem into subproblems. The
challenge is to ensure that the subproblems do not interact

badly
with one another, while also making progress at every
level of the recursion tree. Let us first describe a key property that we would like the set of our chosen
balls to have:

Definition 4.3

(Central property)
.

A set
\mathcal{B}
of balls is said to satisfy the

if for each optimal ball
B^{\star}_{i}
whose center
c_{i}^{\star}
is covered by
\mathcal{B}
,
\mathcal{B}
must cover all of
B_{i}^{\star}
, that is

c_{i}^{\star}\in\cup_{B\in\mathcal{B}}B\implies B_{i}^{\star}\subseteq\cup_{B\in\mathcal{B}}B.

Observe that, if a set
\mathcal{B}
of balls satisfying the central property is removed from
S
(where
(S,t)
forms a valid pair), then the remaining set
S^{\prime}
of uncovered points satisfies one of the two requirements of a valid pair: for each optimal ball
B_{i}^{\star}
that intersects
S^{\prime}
, its center
c_{i}^{\star}
must be in
S^{\prime}
. To understand this requirement, suppose this property was not satisfied. Then, there might be an optimal ball, say

B_{i}^{\star}
whose center
c_{i}^{\star}
is covered by
\mathcal{B}

B_{i}^{\star}
are covered. Hence, in the recursive subproblems obtained on removing the set of points covered by
\mathcal{B}

Now, note that

R^{\prime}\geq\frac{2R_{\text{large}}}{5}\geq\frac{r(E)}{5}

2.

M^{\prime\prime}\leq M

Phase 3: Identifying and Solving the Subproblems

Our algorithm sets

4.6
Time complexity of
\mathtt{recMSR}

To complete the proof of
Theorem

1.1
, we now bound the runtime
of the algorithm. Consider the recursion tree of
\mathtt{recMSR}
and let

f(\ell,t)
denote the maximum possible number of nodes in the
subtree rooted at a node corresponding to a call

\mathtt{recMSR}(S,t,\ell)
.

All the for loops in
\mathtt{recMSR}
, have at most
(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})^{O(k)}

iterations since
|\mathcal{C}|\leq(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})^{O(k)}
, and the number
of
t
-tuples of non negative integers with sum
O(\frac{k}{\varepsilon})
,
for any
t\leq k
is also
(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})^{O(k)}
. Also, the for
loop in
{\tt guessAndSolveMSR}
has at most
n^{2M}

iterations. Thus, there exists
b=(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})^{O(k)}
, such
that
\mathtt{recMSR}(S,t,\ell)
makes at most
b
calls to subproblems at
level
\ell
, and at most
b\cdot n^{2M}
calls to subproblems at level

\ell+1
. Hence, we have that for all
t\geq 1,\ell\in\{0,1,\ldots,L-1\}
:

f(\ell,t)\leq bf(\ell,t-1)+bn^{2M}f(\ell+1,t-1)+1.

The following lemma proves an FPT upper bound on the size of the recursion tree.

Lemma 4.11
.

f(\ell,t)\leq b^{2(t+L-\ell)}n^{2M(L-\ell)}
for all
t\in\{0,1,\ldots,k\},\ell\in\{0,1,\ldots,L\}

Proof.

We prove this via induction on
L-\ell+t
. When
\ell=L
or
t=0
, there are no recursive calls and hence the statement is trivially true. We have:

\displaystyle f(\ell,t)

\displaystyle\leq bf(\ell,t-1)+bn^{2M}f(\ell+1,t-1)+1

\displaystyle\leq b\cdot b^{2(t+L-\ell-1)}n^{2M(L-\ell)}+bn^{2M}\cdot b^{2(t+L-\ell-2)}n^{2M(L-\ell-1)}+1

\displaystyle\leq 2b^{2(t+L-\ell)-1}n^{2M(L-\ell)}+1

\displaystyle\leq b^{2(t+L-\ell)}n^{2M(L-\ell)}.\qed

In each node of the recursion tree, we spend
(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})^{O(k)}\cdot n^{2M}
time. Thus, substituting
b=(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})^{O(k)}
,
L=\left\lceil\frac{6}{\varepsilon}\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil
and
M=\left\lceil\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right\rceil
, we get the following result:

Corollary 4.12
.

The overall time complexity of
\mathtt{recMSR}
is

\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)^{O\left(k^{2}+\nicefrac{{k}}{{\varepsilon}}\log\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}\right)}n^{O\left(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{3}}}\log\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}}\right)}.

4.5
and

our discussion of the first

-approximation for

5
Concluding Remarks

We consider the min-sum diameters and min-sum radii problems, where we
partition a set of points into
k
parts, to minimize the sum of the
diameters or radii of the parts. For both problems, we present the
first FPT approximation schemes, with respect to the natural parameter

k
; the previous best FPT approximation algorithms for these problems
have approximation factors
4+\varepsilon
and
2+\varepsilon
,
respectively. Specifically, given
\varepsilon>0
, we show how to compute

(1+\varepsilon)
-approximations for both
MSD
and
MSR
in time

(1/\varepsilon)^{k}n^{O(1)}
and

(1/\varepsilon)^{O(k/\varepsilon\log 1/\varepsilon)}n^{\poly(1/\varepsilon)}
respectively.

Our work suggests several open problems. The question of whether we can get an
exact
algorithm for
MSD
in time parameterized by
k
remains a tantalizing open problem: no
W[1]
-hardness is known for this problem. Improving the approximation ratio of the
MSR
problem under mergeable constraints to get an FPT PTAS remains open; we only give a
(2+\varepsilon)
-approximation. It would also be interesting to improve the runtimes of our algorithms further, and remove the dependence on
\varepsilon
in the exponent of
n
for the
MSR
problem.

Appendix C
Simple and Fast Exact Algorithm for
MSD

In this section, we describe a simple exact algorithm for
MSD
that
runs in
n^{k+O(1)}
, improving the
n^{5k+O(1)}
bound
from
[
4
]
.

To solve the problem on some subset
S\subseteq X
, again consider a
diameter witness pair
x,y
; i.e., such that
d(x,y)=\operatorname{diam}(S)
. Order the points of
S
on a line as

z_{1}=x,z_{2},\ldots z_{|S|}=y
in increasing order of distance
from
x
, breaking ties arbitrarily. As a thought experiment, project
each cluster
X_{h}
in the optimal solution onto an interval

I_{h}=[L_{h},R_{h}]
where
L_{h}:=\min\{i\in[|S|]:z_{i}\in X_{h}\}

and
R_{h}:=\max\{i\in[|S|]:z_{i}\in X_{h}\}
. If there exists some

q\in[|S|-1]
such that
q
and
q+1
are never a part of the same
interval, then we can split the problem into subproblems

\{z_{1},\ldots z_{q}\}
and
\{z_{q+1},\ldots z_{|S|}\}
. On the other
hand, when no such
q
exists, it is optimal to have all the points in
the single cluster. This is because, the cost of the optimal
clustering equals the sum of

\operatorname{diam}(X_{h})\geq d(x,z_{R_{h}})-d(x,z_{L_{h}})
over all indices
h

of the optimal clusters, and each
q\in[|S|-1]
contributes at least

d(x,z_{q+1})-d(x,z_{q})
to at least one term of this sum. Hence, the optimal cost is at
least
d(x,z_{|S|})-d(x,z_{1})=\operatorname{diam}(S)
. Since our algorithm does not
know the projected intervals, it tries all these options and chooses
the best one.

To bound the runtime, observe that the number of nodes in the
recursion tree is given by
T(n,1)=1
and for all

t\geq 2,n\geq 1
:

T(n,t)=1+2\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}T(m,t-1).

It can be proved via
induction on
t

T(n,t)\leq n^{t}
for all
n,t
. This is
clearly true for

if either of these
holds true. Otherwise, when

\displaystyle T(n,t)

\displaystyle=1+2\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}T(m,t-1)

\displaystyle=1+2\sum_{m=1}^{n-2}T(m,t-1)+2T(n-1,t-1)

\displaystyle=T(n-1,t)+2T(n-1,t-1)\leq(n-1)^{t}+2(n-1)^{t-1}

\displaystyle=(n-1)^{t-1}(n+1)=n^{t}\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{t-1}\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq n^{t}\left(1-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)<n^{t}.

Since we spend
n^{O(1)}

overall time complexity of our exact algorithm for the original input
with parameter

is
n^{k+O(1)}
.

E.1
Approximation factor analysis

We will prove the following upper bound on the approximation factor:

Lemma E.3
.

For each
\ell\in\{0,1,\ldots,L\}
and each valid pair
(S,t)
, the cost of the solution returned by
{\tt recMSR2}(S,t,\ell)
is at most
\rho(\ell)\cdot\opt(S)
where
\rho(\ell)=1+25\varepsilon+(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{6})^{L-\ell}.

Proof.

Our proof will use induction on
|S|+L-\ell
(analogous to
Section

4.5
). The lemma statement trivially holds if
|S|=0
since we return an optimal solution in this case, and also if
\ell=L
since each defensive clustering is a
2
-approximation and
\rho(L)\geq 2
.

Let us now consider
\ell<L,|S|>0
. In this case, we obtain a set of defensive balls
\mathcal{B}
and a set of temporary subproblems
(S_{1},k_{1}),(S_{2},k_{2})\ldots,(S_{m},k_{m})
, corresponding to expanded balls
E_{1},E_{2},\ldots,E_{m}
. Here, the set
\mathcal{B}
corresponds to indices
J=G\cup J^{\prime}_{1}\cup J^{\prime}_{2}\ldots\cup J^{\prime}_{m}
, where
G
denotes the set of good defensive balls and
J^{\prime}_{p}
denotes the set of boundary defensive balls of
E_{p}
(that were available at the time of creation of
E_{p}
, that is, outside all of
E_{1},E_{2},\ldots,E_{p-1}
). We then expand the balls in
\mathcal{B}
as in
Section

4.4
to get a set
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
of balls that satisfy the central property. Then, we update each
S_{p}
by removing the points covered by
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
. Recall from
Appendix

E
that
\OPT(S_{p_{1}})
and
\OPT(S_{p_{2}})
are disjoint for any
p_{1}\neq p_{2}\in[m]
, and
|\OPT(S_{p})|\leq k^{\prime}_{p}
,
\opt(S_{p})\leq r(E_{p})
for all
p\in[m]
. Thus, each pair
(S_{p},k^{\prime}_{p})
forms a valid pair since
|\OPT(S_{p})|\leq k^{\prime}_{p}
and
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
satisfies the central property.

To solve the subproblem
S_{p}
, we consider each tuple
\mathcal{B}^{\prime\prime}=(B^{\prime\prime}_{1},\ldots,B^{\prime\prime}_{M^{\prime}_{p}})
where
M^{\prime}_{p}=\min(k^{\prime}_{p},M)
and compute
{\tt recMSR2}(S_{p}\setminus\bigcup_{i\in[M^{\prime}_{p}]}B^{\prime\prime}_{i},t-M^{\prime}_{p},\ell+1)

10

\min(M^{\prime}_{p},|\OPT(S_{p})|)

\OPT(S_{p})

\mathcal{B}^{\prime\prime}
that consists of all the optimal balls having indices in

M^{\prime}_{p}-|I^{\prime}_{p}|

and centered at the center of one of the optimal balls in
I^{\prime}_{p}
. Since

is a valid pair, so is
(S_{p}\setminus\bigcup_{i\in I^{\prime}_{p}}B_{i}^{\star},k^{\prime}_{p}-M^{\prime}_{p})
as optimal balls do not contain the centers of other optimal balls and
\OPT(S_{p}\setminus\bigcup_{i\in I^{\prime}_{p}}B_{i}^{\star})\subseteq\OPT(S_{p})\setminus\{B_{i}^{\star}:i\in I^{\prime}_{p}\}
. Thus, by the induction hypothesis the cost of the output

\cost(\mathcal{B}_{p}^{\star})\leq\rho(\ell+1)\opt(S_{p}\setminus\bigcup_{i\in I^{\prime}_{p}}B_{i}^{\star})+\sum_{i\in I^{\prime}_{p}}r_{i}^{\star}.

E.2
Time complexity of
recMSR2

In any call
{\tt recMSR2}(S,t,\ell)
, we consider
(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})^{O(k)}
candidate clusterings in the set
\mathcal{C}
. For each of these clusterings, we iterate over
O(2^{k})
choices for the subset of
good
defensive balls, and form a set of up to
t
temporary subproblems. Then we iterate over all
(1/\varepsilon)^{O(k)}
possible expansions of the chosen balls, and then make a call for each subproblem to
guessAndSolveMSR2
at level
\ell+1
. Each invocation of
guessAndSolveMSR2
makes
O(n^{2M})
calls to
recMSR2
at level
\ell+1
corresponding to the guessed optimal balls. Thus, any level
\ell
call to
{\tt recMSR2}
makes
(1/\varepsilon)^{O(k)}\cdot 2^{k}\cdot(1/\varepsilon)^{O(k)}\cdot t\cdot n^{2M}=(1/\varepsilon)^{O(k)}n^{2M}
calls to
recMSR2
at level
\ell+1
. Hence, the recursion tree of
recMSR2
has a branching factor of
(1/\varepsilon)^{O(k)}n^{2M}
and a depth of
L
as there are
L
levels. Substituting
L=\left\lceil\frac{6}{\varepsilon}\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil
and
M=\left\lceil\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right\rceil
, we get that the number of nodes in the recursion tree is bounded by

\left(\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)^{O(k)}n^{2M}\right)^{L}=\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)^{O(\nicefrac{{k}}{{\varepsilon}}\log\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}})}n^{O(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{3}}}\log\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}})}.

Since we take
(1/\varepsilon)^{O(k)}\cdot n^{2M}
time per node of the recursion tree, the overall running time of
recMSR2
is also
(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})^{O(\nicefrac{{k}}{{\varepsilon}}\log\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}})}\cdot n^{O(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon^{3}}}\log\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}})}
, thus proving
Theorem

1.1
.
