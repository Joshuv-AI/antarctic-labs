 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This report documents the design, structural validation, and performance analysis of an ultra-high
performance concrete (UHPC) reinforced International Organization for Standardization (ISO) shipping
container intended for the secure transport of compact, factory-fueled microreactors. The container
integrates 0.75-inch layers of UHPC on both the interior and exterior surfaces of a standard 40-foot ISO
steel container, creating a high-strength, impact-resistant, and ballistically protective composite structure.
The use of UHPC not only enhances structural resilience but also leverages its relatively lightweight
nature compared to traditional protective materials, ensuring that the overall container weight remains
within practical limits for transport and handling requirements. The engineered configuration includes
four longitudinal skid rails and six welded C-channel cross members, optimized to support concentrated
loads from reactor modules during both stationary deployment and crane lifting operations. All structural
elements were analyzed under code-based load cases using transformed section analysis and verified
against design limits prescribed by American Society of Mechanical Engineering (ASME) Boiler and
Pressure Vessel Code (BPVC), American Concrete Institute (ACI) 318/349, American Institute of Steel
Construction (AISC) 360, and ISO 1496. The composite UHPC–steel floor system demonstrated
significant increases in bending stiffness and strength while maintaining steel stresses below yield and
concrete stresses within allowable tension and compression limits. Structural analyses revealed that steel
cross member bending stresses were maintained at less than 1.85 ksi, representing only 6.2% of the 30 ksi
allowable, and shear stresses were limited to 0.24 ksi, or just 1.2% of the 20 ksi AISC 360 code limit.
UHPC compression stresses remained at 0.41 ksi, amounting to only 2.9% of the 14.3 ksi allowable based
on ACI 318, and tension stresses were capped at 0.35 ksi, equating to 70% of the 0.5 ksi cracking limit
specified by Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) standards. Similarly, bending stresses in the
sidewalls and axial stresses in the corner posts were maintained at 1.85 ksi (6.2% of the 30 ksi limit) and
2.86 ksi (9.5% of the 30 ksi limit), respectively. These analyses confirm robust structural performance
while retaining large margins relative to design limits.

To evaluate threat resistance, a comprehensive ballistic test campaign was conducted using six types of
projectiles, including armor-piercing rounds. UHPC slabs between 2.5 and 4.0 inches thick successfully
resisted full penetration across all threat classes. This empirical data was used to calibrate two advanced
modeling frameworks: the Lattice Discrete Particle Model with fiber reinforcement (LDPM-F) and the
CTH continuum hydrocode. LDPM-F resolved mesoscale fracture and fiber-bridging behavior, while
CTH simulated high-strain-rate projectile interaction and penetration dynamics, providing full-field
validation against observed damage and velocity decay. The container’s utility was further evaluated
through a reactor-specific case study, demonstrating compatibility with the operational, structural, and
regulatory requirements of microreactor deployment. Load path modeling and structural stress evaluation
confirmed adequate support conditions during crane handling, over-the-road transport, and stationary
positioning.

This work aligns with the mission of the U.S. Department of Energy’s (DOE) Office of International
Nuclear Security (INS) and its Engineering and Technology Solutions (ETS) Pillar. It directly advances
the transport security topical area under the Nuclear Security of Advanced Reactors (INSTAR) technical
line. By embedding security and safety-by-design (2S) principles into the container structure itself, the
project provides a hardened, certifiable solution for microreactor logistics. Overall, the UHPC-reinforced
ISO container offers a validated, lightweight, and threat-tolerant alternative to conventional Type
B(U)/B(M) casks. It supports flexible, scalable deployment of advanced nuclear systems while enhancing
physical protection, transport resilience, and design defensibility under international nuclear safety and
security standards.

 1. INTRODUCTION

The global nuclear energy sector is entering a transformative phase marked by the emergence of compact,
factory-fueled microreactors and small modular reactors (SMRs). These advanced systems are engineered
for rapid deployment in remote, austere, or decentralized locations, offering unprecedented flexibility,
operational simplicity, and energy resilience. However, these advantages also introduce complex
challenges, particularly in the secure and regulatory-compliant transport of these systems. Unlike
traditional approaches where nuclear fuel and reactor components are shipped separately, microreactors
may be designed to be transported as fully fueled, self-contained units. This introduces new risks and
heightens the need for robust, threat-informed containment strategies. This project responds to this
challenge through the development of a next-generation transport solution: an ultra-high performance
concrete (UHPC) reinforced International Organization for Standardization (ISO) container capable of
safeguarding a 32,000-pound microreactor core against a broad spectrum of hazards during intermodal
transport. The design integrates UHPC onto the interior and exterior surfaces of a standard corrugated
steel ISO container. This combination results in a high-strength, radiation-shielding, and impact-resistant
container that adheres to stringent nuclear safety standards. Shear connectors, composite interaction
principles, and code-based stress checks ensure that the container maintains structural integrity under
lifting, transport, and extreme event conditions.

The project exemplifies the integration of security- and safety-by-design (2S), providing a hardened,
certifiable transport container that can support international deployment of microreactors. This project is
driven by the convergence of technological innovation in reactor design, evolving security threats, and
logistical limitations in nuclear material transport. Microreactors and SMRs are increasingly viewed as
viable solutions for power generation in off-grid or critical infrastructure settings. Their modularity and
compact form factors are expected to allow for delivery in most standard freight containers, based on
design principles and preliminary assessments of leading microreactor systems. However, transporting a
complete, fueled reactor introduces heightened concerns around sabotage, theft, and accident
survivability. The use of traditional cask systems, often massive and incompatible with containerized
logistics, does not scale well with the anticipated growth of microreactor deployment. The need for a
lighter, more versatile, yet equally secure alternative is clear. UHPC offers a material platform that
combines high compressive and tensile strength with impact resistance, fire durability, and radiation
shielding. Its successful application in civil and nuclear infrastructure further underscores its suitability,
such as in the reinforcement of bridge decks and highway structures, where UHPC improves durability
and load capacity. In the nuclear sector, UHPC has been investigated for use in containment structures to
enhance strength and durability, shield walls for radiation attenuation, and even reactor support pads
where its seismic resistance provides critical stability during dynamic loading. By embedding UHPC into
the surfaces of an ISO container, the transport shell itself becomes a protective and structural asset,
eliminating the need for separate shielding and reinforcement layers. This innovation enables not only
safe handling and transportation but also broadens the operational utility of the container to include on-
site shielding and protection.

The report begins by introducing the fundamental properties and performance characteristics of UHPC. It
explains how UHPC differs from conventional concrete in strength, toughness, permeability, and
radiation shielding, with comparisons against traditional cask materials. This section establishes the
rationale for selecting UHPC as the base material for the container system. Following this, the document
presents a detailed account of ballistic impact testing conducted on UHPC slabs. The testing section
outlines ammunition types, testing conditions, and key performance outcomes such as penetration depth
and damage morphology. The results confirm UHPC’s effectiveness in resisting both small arms and
armor-piercing rounds, supporting its application in nuclear transport. The report then turns to a practical
case study that evaluates the compatibility of the container with specific microreactor systems. The case

 2. ULTRA-HIGH PERFORMANCE CONCRETE (UHPC) OVERVIEW

2.1 MECHANICAL AND STRUCTURAL PERFORMANCE OF UHPC

UHPC is a cementitious composite known for extremely high strength and ductility. It typically achieves
compressive strengths above 150 MPa (i.e., over four times that of conventional structural concrete) [1–
8]. This remarkable strength is enabled by a dense binder matrix and steel fiber reinforcement. UHPC
mixes often include 1–2% steel micro-fibers by volume, which bridge cracks and impart tensile strengths
on the order of 8–15 MPa, along with strain-hardening post-cracking behavior [1,8]. The combination of
high compressive and tensile capacity, plus energy absorption in the post-cracking regime, gives UHPC
exceptional toughness and impact resistance [1,9–16]. Unlike brittle normal concrete, UHPC can sustain
multiple fine cracks prior to fracture, exhibiting high energy absorption without sudden failure [1,16].

Beyond strength, UHPC’s refined particle packing and low water content yield an ultra-low porosity
microstructure, translating to very low permeability. As a result, UHPC shows outstanding durability
under aggressive environments, such as coastal or marine conditions with high chloride exposure, regions
subject to extreme freeze–thaw cycles, and industrial settings exposed to chemical or acidic attack [1,17].
It resists chloride penetration (critical for corrosion prevention) and sulfate attack far better than
conventional concrete, which protects not only the integrity of the UHPC itself but also the reinforcing
materials it may contain, such as embedded steel rebar or metal components [1]. UHPC’s dense
microstructure prevents the ingress of chlorides and other aggressive agents, minimizing the risk of
corrosion of embedded steel. This makes UHPC particularly advantageous for infrastructure where
protecting reinforcement from exposure to de-icing salts, seawater, or other corrosive environments is
critical, such as bridge decks and transport containers. Studies have found UHPC has negligible chloride
ingress and suffers virtually no damage even after hundreds of freeze–thaw cycles, retaining durability
factors near ~100 in severe cold-region testing with repeated freezing and thawing [17]. For instance,
transportation scenarios such as shipping spent fuel via ports or railways in harsh winter climates directly
benefit from UHPC’s resilience, as it prevents cracking or scaling due to salt, water ingress, or
temperature fluctuations during transit. The impermeable matrix also greatly mitigates reinforcement
corrosion and chemical leaching, ensuring the long-term integrity of UHPC materials even when exposed
to chemical contaminants or acidic environments [1,17]. These superior material properties make UHPC
an attractive candidate for nuclear applications where long-term safety and longevity are paramount [1–
8]. Table 1 compares key properties of UHPC against traditional materials for cask construction,
highlighting UHPC’s advantages in strength-to-weight and inherent shielding capacity.

2.2 BLAST AND BALLISTIC RESISTANCE

One of UHPC’s most pronounced advantages is its performance under extreme impact, blast, and
projectile loads. The high strength and fiber toughness of UHPC enable it to absorb and dissipate large
amounts of energy without catastrophic failure [16]. In blast tests, UHPC elements sustain significantly
less damage than conventional concrete. For example, Cavili and Rebentrost (2006) subjected 100 mm-
thick panels to identical explosions: a UHPC (RPC/Ductal) panel showed almost no visible damage,
whereas a standard 50 MPa reinforced concrete panel suffered heavy scabbing, exposed rebar, and deep
cratering [19]. The UHPC panel remained structurally intact with only minor cracking, while the normal
concrete panel lost large chunks of material [19]. UHPC’s ductile behavior under high-rate loading allows
the development of many micro-cracks instead of brittle fragmentation [16]. This prevents the violent
ejection of concrete shards, a critical safety benefit, since flying debris from a cask could spread
contamination. Furthermore, this containment capability offers added safety advantages by preventing
flier plates, such as heavy or high-velocity fragments from the microreactor or its internal components,
from escaping the container during extreme events. UHPC’s capacity to localize debris within the
container reduces the risk of unintended release of hazardous materials. This ensures that the containment
system serves as both a protective shield and a structural fail-safe in situations where internal reactor
integrity might be compromised. Indeed, large-scale blast experiments have confirmed that UHPC panels
can be much thinner than conventional concrete yet resist the same blast, and the risk of injury or damage
from fragments is “virtually eliminated” due to UHPC’s no-spall1 performance [19].

Under ballistic impact, UHPC likewise outperforms traditional materials. Tests on UHPC slabs under
military rifle fire (i.e., 7.62 mm full metal jacket (FMJ) at ~850 m/s) showed no perforation and no back-
face fragmentation; the UHPC slabs achieved a ballistic resistance rating (i.e., R2) with zero fragments
dislodged upon bullet impact [19]. In contrast, ordinary concrete would spall severely on the rear face
when perforated. Research indicates UHPC targets can withstand impact energies several times higher
than normal reinforced concrete before failure, thanks to fiber-bridging and energy absorption. In one
study, UHPC panels were shown to survive impulses ~4 times greater than conventional reinforced

concrete panels before breaching [16]. These attributes approach the performance of metallic armor in
some respects, making UHPC an ideal material for protecting nuclear contents against both accidental
explosions and deliberate attacks [16,19]. UHPC’s proven use in military fortifications and security
structures can be directly leveraged to harden transport casks or storage modules, ensuring they maintain
integrity under extreme shock and impact loads.

2.3 THERMAL PERFORMANCE AND FIRE RESISTANCE

Nuclear transport and storage containers must endure not only mechanical insults but also thermal
extremes (e.g. the regulatory fire of 800 °C for 30 minutes in Type B transport tests [20]. UHPC offers
generally robust performance at high temperatures, with some important design considerations for fire. Its
inherent strength is retained to a significant extent up to moderately elevated temperatures, outperforming
conventional concrete in slow-heating scenarios [8,21–23]. Studies on UHPC dry storage cask designs
have shown that UHPC can reduce thermal stresses and associated damage when exposed to elevated
temperatures compared to typical concrete or steel-composite designs [8,21–24]. In one design study, a
UHPC-based waste container exhibited significantly lower stress and damage after a severe heat exposure
than a traditional steel–concrete cask, particularly at the critical lid-to-body interface [8,21–24]. This
suggests UHPC can better preserve structural integrity in accident-fire conditions, an important safety
margin for containment.

However, due to UHPC’s dense microstructure, there is a known risk of explosive spalling under rapid
heating (e.g., in a fire). Trapped pore moisture can generate high vapor pressures since the tight UHPC
matrix impedes venting. Without mitigation, this can cause pieces of concrete to burst off when heated
quickly, which is a serious concern for fire safety. To address this, standard practice is to integrate small
amounts of polypropylene fibers into UHPC mixes that may experience high heat [25]. Polypropylene
fibers melt at ~170 °C, well below the critical 200 °C+ range where concrete’s pore pressure peaks [25].
As they melt, the polypropylene fibers leave behind capillary channels that relieve steam pressure,
dramatically reducing the risk of spalling [25]. Empirical studies confirm that even a modest
polypropylene fiber dosage is highly effective; concretes with 0.1% polypropylene fibers show little to no
spalling in fire tests, whereas identical mixes without polypropylene explosively spall [25]. Additional
measures can further improve performance; for instance, hybrid fiber systems (e.g., combining
polypropylene with steel fibers) have shown synergistic benefits, as steel fibers enhance residual strength
while polypropylene fibers protect against spalling. In fact, using a hybrid fiber mix completely prevented
explosive spalling in UHPC at even low fiber dosages, by increasing permeability through dual
mechanisms (i.e., fiber channels and microcracking) [26].

With these design interventions, UHPC elements can achieve robust fire endurance, matching or
exceeding the fire performance of conventional concrete. UHPC test specimens have endured the standard
30-minute, ~800 °C fire exposure without loss of load-bearing capacity when polypropylene fibers and
other provisions were in place [25]. Importantly, UHPC’s lower thermal conductivity compared to steel
also helps insulate nuclear contents during a fire. In summary, a properly engineered UHPC container can
safely endure both prolonged moderate heating (e.g., decay heat from spent fuel) and short-term fire
extremes without compromising containment. The inclusion of polypropylene fibers and other thermal
safeguards is now a proven approach to ensure UHPC transport casks meet fire safety requirements (e.g.,
preventing spalling and maintaining integrity throughout a regulatory fire).

2.4 RADIATION SHIELDING CAPABILITIES

Beyond mechanical and thermal demands, a cask must also provide radiation attenuation for both gamma
rays and neutrons. Traditional cask designs achieve this by using either thick concrete overpacks
(typically 0.6–0.9 m walls for spent fuel storage) or heavy steel shells supplemented by lead and neutron-

 5

moderating polymers [8,18,21,22]. UHPC presents a compelling alternative by combining structural
strength and radiation shielding in a single material.

Gamma ray shielding in concrete is primarily a function of material density and atomic composition.
UHPC’s densely packed matrix gives it a slight inherent edge in shielding efficiency over ordinary
concrete of similar composition. For example, studies have found that for the same density, a UHPC mix
can achieve a given dose attenuation with about 40% thinner thickness than a conventional normal-
strength concrete [18]. This improvement is attributed to UHPC’s minimal air voids and higher solid
content per volume, which increase the effective density and electron density for photon attenuation [18].
In practical terms, a slimmer UHPC shield can provide equivalent gamma protection, enabling lighter and
more compact cask designs. Even standard UHPC (without special additives) has a higher linear
attenuation coefficient for gammas (~0.14–0.15 cm−1 for Co-60) compared to conventional concrete
(~0.10 cm−1), translating to a half-value layer of roughly 5 cm for UHPC vs 7–8 cm for normal concrete
(see Table 1) [18]. Compared to traditional cask designs used for spent fuel, which often employ either
thick concrete overpacks or heavy steel shells combined with lead shielding, UHPC offers a balance of
shielding efficiency and structural functionality. While steel and lead provide much thinner gamma
shields due to their higher attenuation coefficients (~0.5–1.1 cm⁻¹ for Co-60), they do so at the cost of
increased weight and do not contribute to neutron shielding. In contrast, UHPC, with its dense matrix and
moderate hydrogen content, can concurrently provide radiation shielding and structural integrity. This
allows UHPC designs to achieve comparable or even superior dose attenuation with a lighter overall
system when the shielding requirements for both gamma and neutron radiation are integrated.

UHPC can be purpose-formulated as radiation-shielding UHPC by incorporating high-density mineral
additives in the mix. Researchers have explored replacing standard sand or aggregates with materials like
barite (BaSO₄), magnetite (Fe₃O₄), hematite (Fe₂O₃), or ilmenite (FeTiO₃) to boost gamma attenuation
[27]. These heavy aggregates raise the concrete’s density and atomic number content, directly improving
gamma ray attenuation. Magnetite-rich UHPC, for instance, has been shown to achieve linear attenuation
coefficients on the order of 0.17–0.20 cm−1 for Cs-137 gamma (~0.66 MeV), compared to ~0.10–
0.15 cm−1 for typical normal mixes [18,27]. Such radiation-shielding UHPC mixes can provide the same
shielding with substantially less thickness. There is a trade-off; very high heavy aggregate content can
slightly reduce workability and even compressive strength [27]. However, studies show moderate
additions (e.g., ~20–50% heavy sand) achieve relatively large attenuation gains with minimal effect on
mechanical performance [18]. Among candidates, hematite has been recommended as a particularly
effective additive, improving gamma shielding with little adverse impact on strength, more so than lead or
magnetite which can cause mixing or strength issues [18]. In short, tailored UHPC mixes can serve as
structural gamma shields, eliminating the need for separate lead layers.

For neutron radiation, UHPC benefits from the inherent hydrogen in cement hydrate water and the ability
to integrate neutron absorbers. While UHPC has lower free water content than ordinary concrete, its
chemically bound water (i.e., in hydrates) still provides hydrogenous material for moderating neutrons
(i.e., slowing down fast neutrons). In fact, steel alone is a poor neutron shield as it has virtually no
hydrogen, whereas a hydrogen-bearing material like concrete/UHPC is far more effective at attenuating
neutrons by elastic scattering [18]. This leads to a striking comparison: a relatively thin UHPC shell can
often outperform a thick steel shell in neutron attenuation. In essence, replacing a thick steel cask body
with an optimized UHPC shell significantly enhances neutron shielding capacity without resorting to
toxic lead or bulky polymer neutron absorbers. If needed, UHPC mixes can further incorporate additives
like boron (e.g., boron carbide or borate aggregates) to capture thermal neutrons and suppress secondary
gamma emissions [18]. Researchers developing UHPC for SMR containments have explicitly targeted
improved radiation attenuation alongside strength. For example, UHPC mixes have been formulated that
leverage a dense microstructure plus high-Z additives to boost both gamma and neutron attenuation
relative to normal concrete [28]. By minimizing voids and including selected heavy or hydrogen-rich

 6

components, these UHPC mixes achieved measurably higher radiation attenuation while maintaining
ultra-high strength and toughness [28].

In summary, UHPC can serve as structure and shield simultaneously, unlike traditional designs that use
separate structural metal and shielding materials. Its high electron density, especially when loaded with
heavy minerals, attenuates gamma rays effectively, and its moderate hydrogen content, plus optional
neutron absorbers, attenuates neutrons. This dual functionality enables simpler and potentially lighter
container designs that still meet strict dose limits for transport and storage. UHPC containers eliminate
the need for lead liners or polymer neutron panels, removing those failure points and material
compatibility issues. The result is a unified material solution providing load-bearing support, gamma
shielding, and neutron moderation all in one, which is highly attractive for next-generation nuclear
container designs.

2.5 UHPC IN SECURITY APPLICATIONS

UHPC has gained recognition not only for its exceptional structural and mechanical properties but also
for its relevance in security-sensitive applications. Due to its resistance to extreme impact loads, forced
entry attempts, and deliberate sabotage, UHPC excels as a protective material for critical infrastructure
and transport systems. Its unique combination of material strength, ductility, and energy absorption makes
it an ideal candidate for security designs that safeguard personnel, sensitive equipment, and nuclear
contents against adversarial threats.

One of UHPC’s most pronounced security strengths lies in its resistance to explosive forces. Extensive
studies, such as those by Cavili and Rebentrost (2006), compare UHPC against conventional concrete
under identical blast tests and show that UHPC panels suffer minimal damage while ordinary concrete
undergoes heavy cratering, scabbing, and material fragmentation [19]. UHPC’s ductile and strain-
hardening behavior allows internal energy absorption, preventing catastrophic rupture and violent
fragmentation. This property significantly reduces the risk of flying debris, which is critical for
minimizing collateral damage in security contexts. Applications of UHPC in bomb-resistant structures,
such as embassies and sensitive government facilities, demonstrate its capacity to withstand high-energy
explosions without compromising safety [16].

UHPC’s high toughness and fiber reinforcement also enable it to excel in ballistic resistance, which is
crucial for protective barriers against projectile attacks. Research findings indicate UHPC slabs subjected
to military rifle fire show no perforation, no rear-face fragmentation, and localized impact containment,
outperforming normal concrete (see Section 3 of this report) [19]. Unlike ordinary concrete, which spalls
violently and loses material integrity under impact, UHPC exhibits fine microcracking that resists
penetration while maintaining the thickness of the barrier. This behavior is comparable to metallic armors
in terms of protective performance, making UHPC a cost-effective and multifunctional alternative for
security applications such as protective walls, secure enclosures, or critical equipment shielding [27].

UHPC has significant potential to enhance security in nuclear transport and long-term storage containers.
Its resistance to perforation, explosive breaches, and ballistic impacts makes it a robust protective shell in
scenarios where sabotage or theft is a concern. A UHPC transport cask, for instance, can shield nuclear
materials both physically and structurally, deterring forced entry while providing sufficient safeguards
against explosion or firearm attacks during transit. Security-sensitive applications for modular containers
include SMR reactor shipments and used fuel transport, where UHPC designs can serve as protective
vaults while balancing weight and mobility constraints.

Furthermore, advances in UHPC design have explored hybrid systems that integrate steel fiber
reinforcement for localized toughness while embedding sensor-enabled systems for intrusion monitoring.

 7

These smart UHPC panels provide dual-functionality: physical protection alongside real-time alerts of
security threats. Such innovations improve UHPC’s applicability to next-generation nuclear safeguarding,
where simultaneous monitoring and hardening are needed [28].

Overall, UHPC is emerging as a transformative material for security-critical applications, demonstrating
unparalleled performance in blast and ballistic resistance while providing scalable forms for nuclear
safeguarding infrastructure. Its adoption in transport casks, modular reactor containment, and high-
security facilities aligns with the growing need for security-by-design principles, ensuring robust
protection while maintaining operational performance.

 especially suitable for applications where weight and mechanical resilience are critical

 A UHPC component can be made thinner and lighter while meeting the same structural

 example, regulatory frameworks like the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) safety
 standards for transport of radioactive materials, as well as the American Concrete Institute (ACI)
 codes or the American Society for Testing and Materials (ASTM) specifications for concrete
 durability and impact resistance, require materials used in transport containers to demonstrate
 sufficient compressive strength, fracture resistance, shielding capacity, and resilience under
 extreme environmental conditions. UHPC can achieve these requirements with less material
 volume and weight due to its superior mechanical properties. This illustrates how UHPC can
 replace thick steel sections with much thinner composite sections without sacrificing
 performance.

 Compared to ordinary concrete, UHPC’s superior strength allows dramatic section reduction. For
 instance, a UHPC wall might be only one-third the thickness of a normal concrete wall for the
 same structural capacity, and it can often eliminate the need for heavy rebar cages since the fiber
 reinforcement in UHPC carries tensile loads. The net effect is a lighter overall container.
 Reducing container weight is crucial for transport logistics: lighter packages mean lower shipping

 8

 costs, easier handling, and the ability to use existing cranes/vehicles with higher safety margins.
 All else equal, thinner sections manufactured to meet regulatory thresholds for impact resistance
 and shielding, combined with UHPC’s tensile fiber reinforcement, could allow substantial weight
 reduction while complying fully with industry requirements for nuclear material containment.
 Replacing large portions of a steel cask with UHPC could cut total package weight by tens of
 percent, greatly easing handling and transport.

 By addressing the weaknesses of both steel (e.g., high weight, high cost, low neutron attenuation)
 and normal concrete (e.g., low strength, large required thicknesses, and susceptibility to material
 degradation), UHPC provides a balanced solution for next-generation nuclear containers.

 Cost Considerations: On a per-unit material basis, UHPC is significantly more expensive than
 traditional concrete due to the use of premium materials such as steel fibers, high-grade cement,
 silica fume, and advanced chemical admixtures. However, when compared to steel, the dominant
 material used in high-performance cask designs, UHPC presents a significantly lower-cost
 alternative. For example, fabricating a large stainless steel cask involves expensive materials,
 heavy welding and machining, and strict quality control measures such as non-destructive
 evaluation of welds, all of which drive up production costs. Lead shielding, if used, must be cast
 or poured and encapsulated, further adding to the complexity and expense. In contrast, UHPC can
 be cast in forms in a single pour, using relatively inexpensive raw materials like cement, sand,
 and fibers, which are far cheaper per unit volume than stainless steel.

 While UHPC mix costs are higher than ordinary concrete due to its high cement and fiber content
 as well as advanced admixtures, the net volume of material required for applications is
 significantly lower. UHPC’s exceptional compressive strength, durability, and resilience allow
 for thinner sections that can meet or exceed the structural and regulatory requirements of thicker
 traditional concrete sections. This results in reduced use of steel reinforcement (i.e., rebar) and
 less bulky overall designs. For example, a UHPC design may use only a fraction of the thickness
 required by a traditional concrete design. These system-level efficiencies further extend to
 transportation and handling, as lighter overall systems reduce costs, improve operational
 efficiency, and enhance safety when using existing equipment like cranes and lift vehicles.

 Moreover, UHPC production can potentially leverage existing precast concrete manufacturing
 techniques, which are readily scalable. Compared to the niche industry of thick forging and
 welding specialized steel casks, precast concrete production benefits from a widely established
 global supply chain. Concrete constituents are abundantly available worldwide, while the
 materials and processes for stainless steel casks are limited to specialized industries with higher
 procurement and fabrication costs. As the technology matures and economies of scale evolve,
 UHPC promises to significantly reduce both procurement and manufacturing costs for transport
 and storage packages.

 This dual comparison of higher per-unit material costs versus traditional concrete, and lower
 system-level and manufacturing costs compared to steel, underscores the importance of assessing
 UHPC's performance, production, and life-cycle costs in specific applications. For weight-critical
 and performance-intensive uses, such as nuclear transport casks, UHPC strikes an appealing
 balance of cost, strength, scalability, and durability. In contrast, in cost-sensitive environments
 with less demanding load or shielding requirements, fixed infrastructure applications using
 traditional concrete may remain more economical.

 Radiation Shielding Effectiveness: Traditional steel casks rely on high-density metal for gamma
 shielding and often embed separate neutron absorbers (e.g., borated polymers), whereas concrete

 9

 overpacks rely on bulk thickness and water content for neutrons. UHPC strikes a balance by
 providing both types of shielding inherently. Its base density (~2.4 g/cm3) is higher than ordinary
 concrete’s (~2.3 g/ cm3) due to reduced porosity, but much lower than steel’s (~7.8 g/ cm3).
 Nevertheless, because concrete is rich in light elements (e.g., H, O, Si, Ca), a UHPC shell can
 actually outperform an all-steel shell in neutron shielding, as previously discussed above, by
 efficiently slowing neutrons. For gamma shielding, steel and lead have an edge in attenuation per
 unit thickness; however, UHPC can approach the required attenuation by using slightly increased
 thickness, while still remaining considerably lighter overall. Moreover, UHPC allows the
 incorporation of high-Z aggregates to boost gamma attenuation to levels unachievable with
 normal concrete [18,27]. Specialized heavy metals (e.g., depleted uranium or tungsten) have been
 used in a few cask designs as compact gamma shielding materials. For example, depleted
 uranium has been incorporated into the inner shells of some high-capacity spent nuclear fuel
 casks to achieve dense, localized shielding. A notable example is the U.S.-based Nuclear
 Assurance Corporation’s Spent Fuel Transport Cask, which uses depleted uranium as an integral
 part of its gamma shielding assembly. Similarly, tungsten has been leveraged in certain compact
 research reactor casks or medical isotope transport containers where space constraints are critical.
 These materials are often chosen for their superior shielding effectiveness due to their high
 atomic numbers (Z), which make them highly effective at attenuating gamma radiation, but their
 inclusion significantly increases cask weight and cost, and they play no structural role in the cask
 body. In contrast, UHPC provides a single-material solution; it is load-bearing and shielding at
 the same time. This integration simplifies cask construction and eliminates interfaces between
 disparate layers.

  Durability and Longevity: Standard reinforced concrete casks can degrade over time; observed
 issues after 20–30 years include surface cracking, salt-induced rebar corrosion, freeze–thaw
 damage, alkali-silica reaction (ASR), and leaching of calcium hydroxide, which can reduce
 shielding performance [29]. These aging mechanisms necessitate inspections and sometimes
 repairs or license renewals for extended storage. UHPC, by virtue of its ultra-durable matrix,
 mitigates most common concrete degradation modes. Its low permeability prevents significant
 water ingress, chloride diffusion, or sulfate attack, greatly slowing any corrosion of internal
 reinforcement [1,8,17]. UHPC is almost immune to freeze–thaw deterioration; laboratory tests
 show no internal microcracking or scaling even after hundreds of severe cycles [17]. In older dry
 storage modules, freeze-thaw spalling and surface scaling have been noted as an aging concern; a
 UHPC module would essentially eliminate that concern [29]. Likewise, UHPC’s crack control via
 fiber reinforcement means it experiences far fewer and finer cracks from thermal gradients or
 shrinkage, cutting off pathways that could accelerate aging. Overall, a UHPC container could
 serve for extended service periods (e.g., 50+ years) with minimal maintenance, whereas a
 conventional concrete cask might require refurbishment or could fall below safety margins in that
 timeframe [8,21–24]. Additionally, unlike steel, UHPC does not rust and does not need protective
 paint or coatings. This avoids the ongoing costs of repainting, coating repair, and corrosion
 monitoring that steel casks demand. In life-cycle cost analysis, the initial material cost of UHPC
 may be higher than normal concrete due to premium constituents like fibers and admixtures, but
 the avoided maintenance and the extended life yield a lower total cost of ownership [8,21,22].
 UHPC capitalizes on the economy of concrete while overcoming its historical durability
 limitations.

As discussed above, UHPC outperforms conventional cask materials in strength-to-weight ratio and
multi-functionality, as outlined in the comparison of mechanical properties. It also provides comparable
or better radiation shielding with reduced bulk, as detailed in the analysis of gamma and neutron shielding
capabilities, and promises lower overall costs when considering its long-term durability and life-cycle
advantages.

2.7 LIMITATIONS OF UHPC

While UHPC offers several groundbreaking advantages, it is not without its limitations. These
constraints, coupled with industry-specific challenges, have prevented UHPC from becoming the standard
material in nuclear transport and storage. Understanding these issues is critical to assessing its suitability
for widespread adoption.

 1. High Initial Material and Production Costs
 One of the primary reasons UHPC has not been broadly adopted in the nuclear transport industry
 is its significantly higher initial cost compared to traditional concrete. UHPC formulations require
 premium materials such as specialized steel fibers (i.e., 1–2% by volume), finely ground silica
 fume, high-grade cement, and advanced admixtures, all of which contribute to substantially
 higher production costs on a per-unit basis. These additional costs are further magnified by the
 need for highly controlled mixing and curing conditions, specialized equipment, and rigorous
 quality assurance protocols. Conversely, traditional concrete relies on inexpensive, readily
 available materials and well-established production methods, making it an economical choice for
 applications with low mechanical requirements.

 However, while UHPC is more expensive than normal concrete, it is considerably less expensive
 than steel when assessing raw material costs per unit mass. For example, high-grade steel (e.g.,
 stainless steel 316) involves substantial material and fabrication costs, making it one of the most
 expensive options for nuclear containers. This cost profile is one reason UHPC is increasingly
 viewed as an intermediary, offering strength and durability closer to steel's performance while
 avoiding its extremely high cost.

 11

 Further, at the system level, UHPC’s higher material unit cost can be offset by its ability to
 dramatically reduce the required volume of material. Because of its superior strength and
 durability, thinner UHPC structures may achieve the same performance as much thicker concrete
 walls or heavy steel sections, enabling weight reductions that lower transportation costs, improve
 handling efficiency, and reduce the use of supplemental materials like rebar. As discussed earlier,
 regulatory frameworks emphasize compressive strength, durability, impact resistance, and
 shielding capacity – all of which UHPC can provide with less material volume.

 This dichotomy between higher per-unit material and production costs and potentially lower
 overall system costs underscores the importance of context. In applications requiring high
 performance and weight-critical designs, such as nuclear transport and storage casks, UHPC
 offers a cost-effective alternative to steel and a more efficient solution than traditional concrete.
 On the other hand, for applications with minimal performance or weight constraints, such as fixed
 infrastructure in less demanding environments, the higher material costs of UHPC may make
 traditional concrete the more practical choice.

2. Complexity in Manufacturing and Placement
 The production and placement of UHPC pose additional challenges that limit its adoption as an
 industry standard. UHPC requires sophisticated mixing equipment to handle its low water-to-
 cement ratios (typically below 0.2), as well as careful dosing and homogenization of steel fibers
 to prevent clumping. On-site applications can be particularly difficult due to the material's low
 workability, which complicates large-scale pours and requires specialized formwork.
 Furthermore, achieving the dense microstructure and ultra-high strength characteristics of UHPC
 demands strict curing regimens, often including sealed conditions or thermal curing for prolonged
 periods. These technical requirements, combined with the need for highly skilled labor, make
 UHPC less practical for widespread, field-based deployment compared to steel or conventional
 concrete.

3. Limited Track Record in Nuclear Applications
 Despite its obvious potential, UHPC has a relatively short operational history in nuclear transport
 and storage applications. The industry has traditionally favored materials like steel, lead, and
 conventional concrete due to their long-established performance benchmarks, existing standards,
 and regulatory acceptance. Nuclear transport packages must meet stringent safety requirements
 mandated by national regulatory agencies such as the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission
 (NRC), as well as international guidance provided by organizations like the IAEA. These
 standards require extensive real-world test data to validate material performance under extreme
 conditions, including radiation shielding, drop tests, thermal extremes, and long-term durability.
 However, only a limited body of data is available that directly demonstrates UHPC's ability to
 meet these criteria in nuclear applications. This lack of precedent introduces regulatory hurdles
 and reluctance to adopt UHPC over legacy materials with proven track records.

4. Fire-Induced Explosive Spalling Risks
 One of the inherent vulnerabilities of UHPC is its susceptibility to explosive spalling during rapid
 heating, such as during a potential transport fire. The dense matrix of UHPC traps pore moisture,
 which rapidly vaporizes under high temperatures, generating internal steam pressure that can
 cause chunks of material to burst off violently. For regulatory nuclear transport fire tests, such as
 the NRC requirements or international guidance from the IAEA, which specify exposure to
 800 °C for 30 minutes, explosive spalling could compromise both the integrity of the container
 and its shielding properties. While the inclusion of polypropylene fibers has been shown to
 mitigate this issue by creating vent channels for steam, such modifications increase both the

 12

 material cost and design complexity. As a result, additional engineering solutions must be
 implemented to meet fire safety standards, further delaying adoption.

5. Density and Weight Challenges
 Although UHPC achieves an excellent strength-to-weight ratio compared to conventional
 concrete, its density is still higher than that of non-structural materials commonly used solely for
 nuclear shielding, such as lead or borated polyethylene. For example, typical UHPC has a density
 of approximately 2.4 g/cm³, compared to approximately 11.3 g/cm³ for lead or 0.95 g/cm³ for
 borated polyethylene. These materials are effective at minimizing weight in shielding-focused
 designs, but they lack structural load-bearing capacity. UHPC, in contrast, integrates both
 shielding and structural performance into a single material solution.

 When compared to conventional concrete, which has a density of around 2.3 g/cm³, UHPC
 provides similar density but far greater compressive strength. This allows for thinner sections that
 can offset UHPC’s slightly higher weight per unit volume. However, shielding requirements,
 especially for effective neutron or gamma attenuation, may still require moderate wall
 thicknesses. This can reduce some of the weight-saving benefits of UHPC as a combined
 structural and shielding material.

 Steel, another common material used in nuclear transport and storage systems, introduces a
 different set of trade-offs. With a density of about 7.85 g/cm³ compared to UHPC's 2.4 g/cm³,
 steel is significantly heavier. It offers excellent durability and mechanical strength, making it
 advantageous for designs that require very thin walls or that must withstand high impact during
 transport. However, a comparable mechanical strength in UHPC can often be achieved with much
 thicker sections, which results in a lower overall material weight. Unlike steel systems, which
 often rely on heavy supplemental shielding materials such as lead, UHPC’s inherent radiation
 shielding properties can be tailored into its mix, providing additional design efficiencies.

 As a result, UHPC is less appealing for applications that prioritize ultra-light shielding materials
 like lead or borated polyethylene, or for systems that require thin-walled steel-based designs.
 However, for integrated systems that demand both structural resilience and effective radiation
 shielding, UHPC offers a compelling alternative. It provides a unique combination of mechanical
 and shielding properties with moderate weight efficiency when compared to conventional
 concrete, lead, or steel.

6. Resistance to Change and Industry Inertia
 The nuclear transport industry is highly risk-averse, and for good reason, as public safety and
 regulatory compliance are central concerns. This cautious approach naturally slows the adoption
 of novel materials like UHPC. Transitioning to UHPC would require significant investment to
 revise existing cask designs, conduct regulatory testing, and reform industry standards.
 Furthermore, stakeholders in the industry, including government agencies, nuclear utilities, and
 cask manufacturers, are accustomed to working with established materials such as steel, lead, and
 normal concrete. This institutional inertia is compounded by the already long lead times
 associated with nuclear projects, further delaying market adoption of UHPC.

7. Insufficient Standardization and Supply Chain Limitations
 Unlike steel or traditional concrete, UHPC lacks the widespread standardization needed to ensure
 consistent properties across suppliers and batches. Variability in formulations and differences in
 how UHPC is processed can significantly impact performance, particularly under extreme or
 atypical conditions. The global supply chain for UHPC-specific components, particularly steel
 fibers and silica fume, is limited compared to generic steel or cement, making large-scale

 13

 adoption challenging. Ensuring a reliable supply chain for UHPC materials would require
 significant investments by both manufacturers and suppliers to support scalability.

The combination of these limitations (e.g., high costs, manufacturing complexity, fire risks, and industry
inertia) explains why UHPC has not yet become an industry standard for nuclear transport containers.
While UHPC outperforms many traditional materials in durability, strength, and multifunctionality, these
advantages come at the expense of upfront economic and logistical feasibility. To achieve broader
adoption, further research and development, real-world case studies, and standardization efforts are
required to demonstrate UHPC's long-term safety and reliability while addressing cost and supply chain
constraints. Until then, UHPC is likely to remain a niche material for specialized applications rather than
a wholesale replacement for established materials in the nuclear transport industry.

2.8 DESIGN IMPLICATIONS FOR NUCLEAR TRANSPORT CONTAINERS

The superior properties of UHPC open up new possibilities in the design and operation of transport and
storage containers for advanced reactors and nuclear materials:

  Optimized Geometry and Integration: UHPC can be cast or sprayed into or onto complex
 shapes, enabling optimized geometries that improve performance and functionality. Unlike steel
 plates which are limited to uniform thickness and require welding, a UHPC container can be
 monolithically formed with variable wall thickness, internal ribs, embedded shielding layers, and
 other geometric features tailored to specific load or shielding requirements. Designers can thus
 put material exactly where needed for strength or radiation attenuation without incurring
 fabrication complexity. For example, regions near closure lids or impact points could be cast
 thicker or with steel fiber “rich” zones, while other areas remain thinner to save weight. UHPC
 also bonds well to steel inserts, so components like lifting lugs, trunnions, or bolt reinforcements
 can be cast integrally into the structure. This streamlines the container profile by eliminating
 many external attachments or brackets, which on steel casks must be welded on. Modularity is
 also enhanced: UHPC modules can be tailored in shape and size to different payloads relatively
 easily by adjusting formwork or spray layout.

 This is especially useful for the emerging variety of advanced reactor and SMR designs, which
 have diverse form factors. For instance, the Westinghouse eVinci Microreactor, with its compact
 cylindrical core and transportable design, requires geometric flexibility and tight shielding
 configurations that UHPC can provide. Similarly, the Antares R1, a small High-Assay Low-
 Enriched Uranium (HALEU) Tristructural-Isotropic (TRISO)-based reactor for military and
 space applications, is well-suited for ISO container-based deployment, benefiting from UHPC's
 ability to adapt to its cylindrical geometry while reducing overall mass. In contrast, reactors like
 the Kairos KP-FHR, which require large integrated salt handling systems, and the BWXT
 Advanced Nuclear Reactor, with its larger power output and shielding needs, present geometric
 and thermal challenges that make them less suitable for containerized UHPC transport. A family
 of UHPC container module designs could be developed to accommodate a range of reactor
 vessels or fuel canisters by simply modifying mold dimensions, rather than retooling an entire
 metallurgical fabrication process. Overall, UHPC enables compact, efficient cask geometries that
 still meet regulatory requirements for impact resistance, stacking, and drop tests, without the
 straight-jacket of uniform-thickness steel walls.

  Reusability and Modular Deployment: A notable advantage of UHPC for transport applications
 is the potential for container reusability. UHPC’s durability means a container could be used
 many times over a long service life with minimal degradation. Current transport casks (especially
 those using concrete overpacks) are sometimes treated as one-time use or require significant

 14

 refurbishment after each use due to cracking, spalling, or other damage. In contrast, a UHPC
 transport container could be designed with a removable lid system to allow retrieval and
 replacement of the contents (e.g. an SMR reactor module or spent fuel canister) at the destination,
 after which the same container can be used for another shipment. Concepts for modular UHPC
 shipping containers include robust bolted closures and sealing systems that maintain containment
 during transport but can be opened under controlled conditions at a plant site. This reusability is
 particularly attractive for SMRs and portable reactor systems, which might be moved multiple
 times (e.g., from factory to site, site to site, or site back to factory). The ability to reuse the
 transport container reduces the total number of casks needed and spreads the capital cost over
 multiple deployments. It also provides flexibility for onsite storage: the UHPC module that
 delivered the reactor or fuel can double as an interim storage or shielding structure at the site,
 then be used again for removal years later. This dual use maximizes asset utilization and can
 simplify regulatory approvals. In short, UHPC could enable a new paradigm of modular, reusable
 transport containers, in contrast to bespoke single-use shipping packages.

 Life-cycle and Long-Term Performance: UHPC’s long-term durability translates to excellent
 life-cycle performance for nuclear containers. Traditional concrete casks have exhibited aging
 after a few decades, raising concerns for extended storage beyond their initial license. By using
 UHPC, many of these issues (e.g., thermal cracking, freeze–thaw damage, moisture ingress, etc.)
 are significantly reduced. This means a UHPC container can maintain its safety margins longer
 without needing repairs or retrofits. Regulators are increasingly interested in 60+ year dry
 storage; UHPC appears well-suited to meet such durations. Some studies project that UHPC
 could achieve a service life >100 years with minimal maintenance in storage conditions, far
 exceeding typical assumptions for conventional concrete [17,30]. For operators, this means fewer
 inspections, less downtime, and more confidence in containment over time. The lack of corrosion
 in UHPC, except for any embedded steel components, which can be managed, is a major
 advantage in outdoor or marine environments where steel casks might suffer chloride-induced
 corrosion. The total life-cycle cost of a UHPC container is thus potentially much lower; while the
 initial fabrication might be comparable or slightly higher than a traditional concrete or steel unit,
 the avoidance of recoating, repairs, and early replacement yields savings. This aligns with broader
 industry goals to improve the sustainability and economics of spent fuel storage by extending the
 life of capital assets.

 Security and Safeguards: Transporting nuclear material also involves security concerns, such as
 mitigating risks of theft or sabotage. UHPC provides inherent benefits in this area due to its
 exceptional blast and impact resistance. A transport container made from UHPC would be
 extremely difficult to penetrate or breach with external explosive devices or high-velocity
 projectiles, effectively serving as a protective vault for the contents. This aligns with security-by-
 design (SeBD) principles, where the container itself provides delay and defense against
 adversarial attacks.

 An attacker trying to shoot at or detonate a UHPC container would be less likely to release
 radioactive material than with a thinner-walled steel cask, as UHPC resists perforation and
 prevents fragmentation that could disperse its contents. This feature is particularly relevant for the
 transport of advanced reactor designs, including SMRs and microreactors, which may be
 transported as complete reactor modules in certain deployment scenarios. A UHPC container
 could not only provide shielding but also serve as a physical defense barrier against sabotage
 attempts during transit.

 15

 By hardening the transport package, much like an armored convoy, UHPC adds an extra layer of
 security beyond armed escorts. This increased resilience could facilitate the movement of nuclear
 assets, including SMRs or microreactors, between facilities or countries with greater confidence,
 potentially addressing regulatory and security hurdles associated with these advanced
 technologies. In essence, UHPC enables the transport container to function both as a shielding
 medium and a security barrier, offering a unique advantage over conventional designs.

Overall, UHPC provides a multifunctional, high-performance material platform for nuclear transport and
storage containers. Its adoption could yield slimmer, lighter container designs that are easier to handle and
cheaper to transport, while at the same time offering equal or greater strength and radiation protection
compared to legacy steel or reinforced concrete systems. The ability to cast modular, reusable UHPC
containers tailored to various reactors or fuel types offers logistical flexibility for the emerging SMR
industry and other advanced reactor deployments. In the long term, UHPC’s durability and resilience
promise extended service life and lower life-cycle costs, addressing the critical need for safe long-
duration storage of nuclear materials beyond initial design lives. These advantages combined, position
UHPC as an enabling technology for next-generation nuclear material containment, effectively marrying
the best attributes of concrete and steel into a single composite that meets the extraordinary demands of
nuclear safety and security. The enhanced safety margins and performance of UHPC-based containers can
bolster confidence among regulators and the public, supporting the wider adoption of innovative reactor
systems with the assurance of a robust transport and containment infrastructure.

 3. BALLISTIC EXPERIMENTS AND RESULTS

To evaluate the performance of UHPC under ballistic impact, a series of live-fire experiments were
conducted using multiple ammunition types, including civilian-grade rounds and armor-piercing (AP)
projectiles commonly used in law enforcement and other specialized applications. These tests are critical
to informing the design of a UHPC-reinforced ISO shipping container intended to contain sensitive
equipment (e.g., reactor components) while offering high protection against ballistic threats during
transport or storage in uncontrolled environments.

Understanding UHPC’s ability to resist high-velocity projectiles enables the research team to establish
minimum slab thicknesses for ballistic protection, verify empirical safety factors, and directly calibrate
computational simulations presented later in this report. Ultimately, these results provide design assurance
that specific classes of ballistic threats will not compromise the structural or shielding integrity of the
container.

3.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP AND CONDITIONS

Ballistic testing was conducted on March 19, 2025, at an accredited testing facility. Slabs of UHPC
ranging from 2.5” to 4.0” in thickness were subjected to direct fire from six different types of ammunition
(see Table 3 below). Each slab was securely mounted to prevent motion during testing, and all impacts
were perpendicular to the slab surface to simplify interpretation and modeling.

To assess the material’s consistent resistance across multiple impacts, multiple rounds were fired at each
slab during testing. However, these rounds were not explicitly aimed at the same point on the slab to
brute-force penetration through cumulative damage. Instead, impacts were distributed across the surface
to evaluate the slab’s uniform ballistic resistance and to avoid invalidating subsequent measurements of
individual projectile behavior. This approach ensures the results reflect the inherent material performance
characteristics of UHPC under single high-energy impacts without introducing overlap-induced damage.

High-speed imaging, crater measurement tools, and 3D laser scanning were used to capture damage
morphology and quantify material removal. An example of the front-face of one of these damaged slabs,
and its corresponding 3D mesh generated with the laser scanning tool is shown in Figure 1. The depth of
penetration was measured using both optical and tactile depth gauges post-impact.

 3. Velocity clustering and projectile behavior:
 The data in Figure 2 reveal clear clustering of projectile types based on their striking velocities
 and penetration behavior. Projectiles fall into three general regimes: high-velocity armor-piercing
 rounds, intermediate-velocity rifle rounds, and low-velocity handgun rounds. The UHPC slabs
 display a consistent ability to arrest projectiles across these regimes, with thicker slabs offering
 progressively larger margins of safety.

 High-energy rounds such as the APM2 and .50 Cal M2 Ball, striking at approximately 2,850–
 3,000 ft/s require the highest level of ballistic resistance, making them the most demanding
 threats evaluated in these experiments. The APM2 rounds exhibit near-penetration effects at 2.5
 inches but are effectively stopped at greater thicknesses. The M855, while similarly fast, delivers
 slightly lower energy due to its smaller mass and is consistently arrested without full back-face
 damage, even at thinner slabs.

 Intermediate-energy rounds like the M80 also travel at high speed but are less aggressive in terms
 of sectional density and core hardness. They pose minimal challenge to slabs ≥2.5 inches. In
 contrast, low-velocity handgun threats such as the .44 Mag SWCGC and 9mm NIJ FMJ travel at
 just 1,300–1,400 ft/s and are easily defeated by all UHPC slabs tested, with minimal penetration
 and localized surface damage.

 These results confirm that UHPC resists penetration not simply by absorbing kinetic energy, but
 by responding to localized pressure and stress wave propagation, both of which are tightly
 coupled to projectile shape, material, and impact velocity. The data also affirm UHPC’s broad-
 spectrum effectiveness, allowing for standardized slab designs to defend against multiple threat
 categories.

significantly exceeds NIJ Level II and III standards for handgun protection.

3.3.2 3D Damage Morphology

Advanced 3D scanning technologies were employed to capture high-resolution, post-impact surface and
subsurface damage in UHPC slabs subjected to ballistic testing. These scans offer a non-destructive,
quantitative, and spatially resolved method for assessing the full-field damage response of UHPC under

 21

high-strain-rate loading conditions. Compared to traditional visual inspection or point-based measurement
techniques, 3D scanning enables a comprehensive mapping of deformation, crater geometry, spall
patterns, and surface cracking, providing a critical bridge between physical testing and numerical
simulation. An example of one of these scans is provided in Figure 1.

The scanning system used in the test campaign employed structured-light or laser triangulation to capture
surface topography with sub-millimeter accuracy. Markers or reference targets were applied to the slabs
prior to testing to facilitate spatial alignment and stitching of multiple scan passes. Typical scan resolution
ranged from 0.1–0.5 mm, depending on the size of the region of interest and the complexity of the
damage morphology. The resulting point cloud or mesh data was processed into digital elevation models,
triangulated surface meshes, and depth maps, which were then analyzed using specialized metrology or
modeling software.

3D scans provide precise measurements of impact crater dimensions, including depth (from the impact
surface to the crater floor), diameter (entry hole and overall spalled region), volume (material displaced or
pulverized), and wall slope (indicating localized shear or brittle failure). These metrics are critical for
characterizing penetration mechanics and energy dissipation near the impact zone. While surface cracking
is often visually apparent, 3D scanning allows for accurate mapping of crack length and propagation
paths, width and branching, in addition to intersections and coalescence points. This information can be
used to infer the stress wave patterns, material anisotropy, and the role of embedded fibers in arresting
crack growth. Furthermore, it provides validation benchmarks for simulations that attempt to resolve
crack initiation and progression.

Moreover, 3D scan data are indispensable for calibrating and validating simulations of ballistic impact.
Specifically, the scanned outputs can be used to compare predicted and observed crater shapes and
penetration depths, align simulated crack networks with actual crack paths, quantify error margins in
damage extent and surface deformation, and generate initial or boundary conditions for secondary
simulations (e.g., residual stress analysis).

3.3.3 Use of Experimental Data for Simulation Calibration

The data obtained from the experimental ballistic campaign provides a robust foundation for calibrating
numerical simulations of high-velocity impact into UHPC materials. These datasets (i.e., striking velocity
and penetration depth) capture the transition from subcritical to near-critical impact behavior, define
energy dissipation thresholds, and identify the conditions under which ballistic failure modes initiate or
are suppressed. They also support the development of accurate, predictive models that inform structural
design, threat assessment, and material optimization.

The observed variation in penetration depths across projectile types reflects not only differences in
velocity, mass, and geometry, but also the complex interplay between material strength, toughness, and
strain-rate sensitivity. For example, the consistent arrest of high-energy threats at slab thicknesses above
3.0 inches, and near-perforation at 2.5 inches, reveals the material’s ballistic resistance boundary. These
findings offer discrete calibration points for tuning simulation parameters, such as dynamic tensile and
compressive strength, fracture energy, post-cracking behavior, and rate-dependent stress-strain response.
In particular, cases involving near-perforation, such as modified .50 Cal rounds impacting 4-inch slabs or
APM2 rounds nearly breaching 2.5- and 3.0-inch panels, are critical for validating predictive fidelity.
Simulations must not only replicate the correct penetration depth, but also capture the underlying failure

 22

modes: tunneling, back-face scabbing, spall cone development, or residual material ligaments2. Accurate
representation of these localized phenomena ensures that the simulation framework reflects the true
inelastic energy dissipation mechanisms of UHPC under high-velocity loading.

Additionally, the velocity vs. thickness trends inform how impact energy is absorbed across different slab
geometries and projectile classes. This supports the calibration of projectile-target interaction models,
such as momentum transfer, penetration resistance, and interface failure behavior. It also enables
refinement of boundary conditions and contact algorithms to match observed behavior under varying
strike velocities and obliquity. The experimental data further allow simulations to be benchmarked against
physical damage indicators, such as rear-face dust, microcracking, or crack tunneling. These qualitative
markers provide constraints on simulated crack propagation and energy release rates. By matching both
the quantitative (e.g., penetration depth) and qualitative (e.g., morphology) outcomes, simulations can be
reliably extended to untested conditions, including intermediate slab thicknesses, non-standard threat
projectiles, or different impact angles.

Ultimately, these data-driven calibrations ensure that simulations are not only mathematically stable, but
also physically representative. They support high-confidence predictions of ballistic resistance, guide the
engineering of UHPC-based protective systems, and reduce the dependence on costly full-scale testing.
As UHPC is increasingly adopted in security-critical structural applications, such validated simulations
will play a key role in certifying design performance, defining safety margins, and informing regulatory
compliance across a range of threat environments.

 4. CASE STUDY SELECTION

This section evaluates the feasibility of encapsulating microreactors and SMRs within a UHPC-reinforced
ISO shipping container. The assessment includes physical compatibility, transportability, licensing
maturity, and thermal and shielding considerations. Each design is reviewed for geometric integration,
fuel and coolant type, power output, and technical readiness.

While UHPC-reinforced ISO containers provide a promising solution for the transport and deployment of
microreactors, scaling this approach to larger SMRs presents significant challenges due to the physical
dimensions, power output, and shielding requirements of most SMRs. These factors often exceed the
design limits of ISO container-based systems, which are optimized for smaller, low-power microreactors.
Therefore, the analysis herein is focused on assessing microreactor compatibility, while acknowledging
the limitations of UHPC-containerized transport for larger SMRs.

 1. Westinghouse eVinci Microreactor
 The eVinci microreactor by Westinghouse is a compact, transportable heat pipe-cooled reactor
 designed to deliver 5 MWe of electrical power. It uses HALEU TRISO fuel and is engineered for
 deployment in remote and off-grid locations.. Its geometry, cylindrical core, and mature status
 (e.g., ready for commercial demonstration) make it an ideal candidate for containerized
 deployment using UHPC reinforcement. eVinci is supported by the DOE’s Advanced Reactor
 Demonstration Program and maintains an active test site in Etna, Pennsylvania [36,37].

 2. BWXT Advanced Nuclear Reactor
 The BWXT Advanced Nuclear Reactor is a high-temperature gas reactor under development by
 BWXT Advanced Technologies. It outputs approximately 50 MWe and uses TRISO fuel.
 Although supported by the Advanced Reactor Demonstration Program, it is not yet ready for
 commercial testing. Its size and thermal output exceed the geometric and shielding limits of a
 standard ISO container, making it less suitable for containerized UHPC deployment [38].

 3. Kairos KP-FHR (Hermes)
 The KP-FHR is a pebble-bed TRISO reactor using FLiBe salt as coolant. The Hermes
 demonstration unit is under construction at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) and is
 intended to validate manufacturing, supply chain, and thermal handling techniques. While
 innovative, the reactor's integrated salt handling systems and volume requirements render it
 incompatible with ISO container-based transport [39].

 4. Antares R1
 The Antares R1 is a low-power HALEU TRISO reactor designed for space and military field
 applications. Its small thermal output (sub-MWe scale) and cylindrical design make it physically
 compatible with ISO container dimensions. However, the system is still under early testing and
 lacks full-scale deployment or licensing experience [40].

 5. GA-EMS Energy Multiplier Module (EM2)
 The EM2 is a helium-cooled fast reactor producing 265 MWe, using a sealed below-grade
 configuration. Its core geometry and thermal management requirements exceed containerized
 transport thresholds. It is not suited for ISO-scale containment [41].

 6. Other Systems
 Reactors such as X-Energy’s Xe-100, NuScale’s NPM, and ARC-100 are larger-scale SMRs
 producing between 77 MWe and 100+ MWe per module. Their pressure vessel dimensions,

 24

 shielding demands, and licensing strategies are designed for semi-permanent civil infrastructure
 and are incompatible with transport in ISO containers [42–44]. Larger SMRs, despite their
 modular design, pose significant challenges due to their size, power output, and shielding
 requirements, which generally preclude containerized configurations.

Among the reviewed systems, only the eVinci and Antares R1 reactors demonstrate general compatibility
with a UHPC-reinforced ISO shipping container. Of these, the eVinci reactor's modular design, compact
heat pipe system, low-pressure and low-power profile, and TRISO fuel safety characteristics make it
particularly well-suited for simplified thermal and shielding requirements. Furthermore, publicly available
discussions from its manufacturer have referenced ISO container-based transport as a potential design
consideration [36,37].

However, it is important to clarify that the analysis presented in this report is not specific to the eVinci
reactor or any other commercially available design. The case study employs a generic reactor model
designed to simulate a versatile and broadly applicable microreactor system, decoupled from any
proprietary or system-specific technical details. While certain design criteria in the case study, such as
portability, modularity, and compact geometry, reflect general characteristics found in advanced reactor
concepts like eVinci, these similarities are coincidental and unrelated to any specific design. The
framework and methodologies described are deliberately meant to remain agnostic to any single reactor
type, ensuring their applicability across a diverse spectrum of compact nuclear systems.

 5. DESIGN OF THE UHPC SHIPPING CONTAINER

Standard intermodal shipping containers are corrugated steel structures originally designed to carry
distributed cargo loads up to 79,000 lb within their volume [47]. In this project, a 40 ft × 8 ft × 8.5 ft ISO
container is repurposed to house a 32,000 lb microreactor core for transport and deployment. A 3-D
model of a typical ISO corrugated steel shipping container is shown in Figure 3. The microreactor case
study used herein has a cylindrical core and weighs ~32,000 lb. Placing such a heavy, concentrated load
inside a container pushes the container’s structural capacity beyond its conventional limits since contents
are typically assumed to be more uniformly distributed. Notably, the container floor and side frame must
support the reactor’s weight during stationary operation (i.e., when the container is placed on the ground
or platform) and also during lifting by crane (i.e., when the entire weight is supported at the corner
fittings, inducing global bending).

 Figure 3. Standard ISO shipping container.

To safely carry this load, the ISO shipping container is reinforced with UHPC layers sprayed or cast onto
its inside and outside surfaces. UHPC is an advanced fiber-reinforced cementitious composite known for
exceptional strength and durability, with compressive strength above 22 ksi and sustained post-cracking
tensile strength thanks to steel fiber reinforcement [48,49]. These properties have made UHPC a
promising material for nuclear structural applications, offering superior mechanical performance and
longevity in shielding and support structures [50]. By creating a composite steel–UHPC section, the stiff
concrete layers can share stresses with the steel container walls, greatly increasing bending rigidity, shear
resistance, and axial load capacity. By creating a UHPC–steel–UHPC sandwich section, the stiff UHPC
layers act as high-strength skins while the corrugated steel serves as a ductile shear core. This
configuration greatly amplifies structural performance: flexural rigidity can increase by an order of
magnitude relative to bare steel sheets, ultimate flexural capacity improves by ~25% [51], shear resistance
increases by 50% or more with shear cracking and ultimate capacities up to 90% and 60% higher than
conventional designs [52], and axial compressive capacity gains range from 5–25% in confined members
to as much as 57–94% in prefabricated UHPC composites [53,54]. Essentially, the thin corrugated steel
walls and floor of the container are converted into a “sandwich” structure with UHPC as outer skins
(inside and outside) and steel as the core. An example of this UHPC-steel sandwich is shown in Figure 4.

This section outlines a design procedure for the UHPC-reinforced container to meet structural integrity
requirements under the described loads. The geometry and material assumptions, including container
structural components and UHPC properties are described first. Next, analytical models are formulated
for critical elements (floor beams, side rails, corner posts) to calculate bending moments, shear forces,
and axial forces in both stationary and lifting scenarios. Design equations are derived to check stresses
against allowable values from relevant design codes, namely:

  American Society of Mechanical Engineers (ASME) – Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code (BPVC)
 Section III (Nuclear Facility Component Supports) for steel allowable stresses [55];
  American Concrete Institute (ACI) – ACI 318 and 349 for concrete strength design (modified for
        UHPC as needed) [56,57];
  NRC – regulatory guides for load combinations and safety factors appropriate to nuclear
 applications [58].

Code-prescribed safety factors and load combinations are incorporated. For example, ASME Section III,
Subsection NF limits primary stress in steel supports to the lesser of 0.6Fy (i.e., 60% of yield stress Fy) or
0.5Fy under normal conditions, providing a safety factor of ~1.67 on yield strength [59]. This means that
instead of designing components to carry loads right up to their yield limit (a factor of 1.0), the allowable
stresses are capped at roughly 60% of yield, ensuring substantial reserve capacity against plastic
deformation and providing robustness under uncertainties in load, material variability, and service
conditions. In concrete design, strength reduction factors φ are applied per ACI guidelines (φ≈0.9 for
flexure, 0.75–0.85 for shear, and 0.65 for compression members) [57]. Here, using φ-values less than 1.0
reduces the nominal strength to a lower design strength, introducing deliberate conservatism so that the
actual structural capacity exceeds the calculated design capacity, thereby ensuring safety despite
uncertainties in loading, construction quality, and material behavior. The NRC generally requires that
structural components remain within elastic limits (e.g., <50% of yield stress) for normal service loads
with additional margins for accident conditions. Herein, a conservative design approach is applied, using
reduced allowable stresses via φ-factors and considering dynamic load amplification for lifting, to ensure
ample safety margins.

Using these criteria, iterative calculations are performed to size the UHPC thickness. Configurations with
different numbers of support rails (i.e., 2, 4, or 6) and the presence or absence of cross-ties (lateral beams
connecting the rails) are evaluated to see how the load distribution changes and how much UHPC is
required in each case. Intermediate results demonstrate how increasing the UHPC layer thickness
progressively improves bending and shear capacity. This process leads to an optimal reinforcement
scheme that meets all strength requirements with minimum added material. Finally, the results are
presented in comparative tables and graphs, and recommendations are provided regarding the most

 27

efficient configuration. The section concludes with a summary of findings and a discussion of the
achieved safety margins and code compliance.

5.1 ASSUMPTIONS AND GEOMETRY

5.1.1 Container Structure

The container is a standard 40-foot ISO intermodal container with exterior dimensions 40′-0″ length × 8′-
0″ width × 8′-6″ height. The primary structural frame consists of four corner posts (i.e., vertical square
tubular columns at the corners of the container), connected by top and bottom side rails along the length
and by end rails at the front and rear ends. Steel cross-members serve as transverse floor beams and span
the width of the container, supporting the floor. The walls (sides and ends) and roof of the ISO container
are made of corrugated Corten steel panels (~0.060–0.079 in thick, ~14 gauge) welded to the frame [47].
The original floor is 1.125 in thick marine plywood placed on top of the steel cross-members [60]. For the
analysis presented herein, we assume the wood floor may be removed and replaced with a thin UHPC
slab or grout so that the interior UHPC layer can directly engage the steel cross-members (ensuring
composite action between UHPC and steel floor beams).

The side wall corrugations are assumed to be filled/covered by a UHPC layer that, when applied, create
an effectively flat composite wall. Each side wall panel is about 7′-9″ high × 40′ long (minus door
openings on one end). The bottom side rails (along each side at floor level) are structural members that
typically are a thin-walled “Z” or box section (e.g. ~6″ deep × 3–4″ wide, 0.177 in thick steel) [46]. The
floor cross-members are spaced at ~10–12″ intervals along the length. The cross-members herein are
modeled as steel square tubes 2.36″×2.36″, with a thickness of 0.118″. Each cross-member spans ~7.7 ft
(the interior width between side rails) and is welded to the bottom side rails at its ends [46].

5.1.2 Microreactor and Supports

The microreactor core is idealized as a rigid cylinder of weight W = 32,000 lb. For modeling, it is
assumed that the cylinder’s length is ~20 ft and the diameter is ~7ft so that it fits within the container
along with ancillary equipment. The reactor sits centered within the container, elevated slightly above the
floor on longitudinal support rails. These support rails are steel beams (e.g., W-sections or rectangular
tubing) running lengthwise (i.e., parallel to the container length) under the reactor, serving as a cradle that
distributes the load to the container floor. Three cases are considered herein for analytical purposes: 2
rails (one near each side of the reactor base, a minimal support), 4 rails, and 6 rails (multiple closely
spaced supports). All rails are assumed to have sufficient bending stiffness that the reactor’s weight is
shared approximately equally among them (i.e. each rail carries ~W/(# of rails). The rails are oriented
longitudinally and attached (or firmly bearing) at their intersections with each floor crossmember.
Without any additional framing, each rail will load the floor only at the discrete points directly under it,
meaning it will exert point loads on each cross-member it crosses. In some configurations, lateral cross-
ties (i.e., beams running transverse, beneath the rails) to interconnect the rails into a ladder-like frame are
considered. These cross-ties, if present, help spread the load; when a rail presses on one crossmember, the
tie beams can engage adjacent rails and adjacent cross-members, distributing the weight more evenly
through composite action of the rail/tie frame. Both extremes are analyzed herein: rails acting
independently (i.e., no ties) and rails tied together (i.e., full load spreading), to bound the behavior. An
example of a practical loading setup is shown in Figure 5; here there are four skid rails supporting the
cylindrical microreactor.

 28

 Figure 5. Practical loading setup with four skid rails.

Geometrically, the rail positions are chosen to support the reactor near its edges. For instance, with 2 rails,
they are placed under the outer quarter-points of the cylinder footprint, in a way that the reactor rests on
two “skids” near its edges. With 4 or 6 rails, they would be spaced roughly uniformly across the base. The
presence of more rails reduces the load per rail; using N rails cuts the force per rail to W/N. Similarly,
adding cross-ties between rails causes multiple cross-members to share the load of a given rail. The
effects of these supports on the required UHPC thickness will be quantified in the following sections.

5.1.3 UHPC Layers

UHPC is applied as a thin layer on the interior surface, exterior surface, or both, as shown in Figure 6.
The UHPC mix is assumed to have a compressive strength f’c ≈ 22 ksi and elastic modulus E ≈ 6.0×103
ksi. Compressive strength represents the maximum stress the material can withstand while being squeezed
or loaded in compression, which governs its ability to support heavy loads without crushing. The elastic
modulus is a measure of stiffness, indicating how much the material will deform under load; higher
values mean the layer contributes more strongly to the overall rigidity of the container. These values are
conservative relative to published UHPC capabilities; compressive strengths of 25–30 ksi are achievable
and elastic modulus of up to ~7,600 ksi [48]. Lower-bound strengths are deliberately employed herein to
ensure safety. Steel fibers in the UHPC provide it with notable post-cracking tensile capacity; first-crack
tensile strength can reach 0.7–1.3 ksi and peak (i.e., post-cracking) tensile strength 1.5–2.0 ksi in flexure
[49]. Tensile strength reflects the material’s resistance to cracking or tearing when being pulled apart or
bent, and in fiber-reinforced UHPC it determines how well the layer can bridge and control cracks.
However, for this container design, UHPC tensile stress is limited to a conservative value (i.e., 0.5 ksi
allowable) to account for the initiation of micro-cracks. In compression, the UHPC can be utilized to high
stress; thus, a strength reduction φ ~0.65–0.75 for compression is used herein, meaning allowable ~0.65f’c
≈ 14 ksi. However, in practice the thin UHPC layers are more likely to be governed by tensile cracking or
bond rather than crushing.

The UHPC is assumed to bond well to the prepared steel substrate. Composite action is assumed to be
maintained, meaning that the steel plate and UHPC layer act as a unified section with no slip. This is a
reasonable assumption given UHPC’s excellent bonding behavior and potential enhancements such as
sandblasting or using shear connectors. To further ensure and sustain full composite action under all
loading conditions, welded steel shear studs are employed at the interface between the steel and UHPC, as
shown in Figure 7. These studs, typically ½" to ¾" in diameter and welded directly to the steel surface
prior to UHPC application, serve as mechanical anchors that transfer longitudinal shear forces and
prevent relative slip between the materials. Stud height is selected to ensure adequate embedment within
the UHPC layer while maintaining necessary cover for durability. The spacing of the shear studs is
designed based on composite beam theory3, often on the order of 4–8 inches depending on the expected
interface shear demand, and they are laid out in patterns (e.g., rows or staggered grids) that optimize both
load transfer and constructability. The use of shear studs is especially critical in the floor system, where
high shear stresses are expected due to the reactor’s concentrated load and crane lifting forces. The studs
are also advantageous on vertical surfaces and ceilings, where gravitational forces and overhead spraying
complicate bond performance.

Thus, when calculating flexural capacity, the UHPC layer is effectively transformed into an equivalent

compatibility and strain continuity across the interface. This composite approach is similar to how a
concrete slab and steel beam are combined in composite floor design, except here the UHPC is very high
strength and thin. When UHPC is applied on both faces of a steel panel, it effectively creates a symmetric
sandwich panel which maximizes the section moment of inertia for bending. If applied only on one face,
the composite section will be asymmetric, and the UHPC will mainly contribute on the side where it is in
compression under bending since UHPC’s tensile contribution beyond cracking is limited. This will be
analyzed in detail in the bending checks in Section 5.4.1.4 of this report.

In the proposed design, the interior UHPC covers the inner walls, interior floor, and possibly the ceiling.
Practically, this means the wood flooring is removed and replaced by a UHPC overlay so that the UHPC
is in direct contact with the steel cross-members and rails. Shear studs are also welded to these cross-
members to ensure the composite action between the steel framing and the UHPC overlay, as shown in
Figure 7. The exterior UHPC covers the outside faces of the side walls, the entire underside of the floor
(i.e., encasing the bottom of cross-members and side rails), and potentially the roof exterior. For structural
capacity, the most important areas are the floor and lower sides because they experience the highest
stresses. It is assumed that the roof does not carry significant load except self-weight since the reactor is
not attached to the roof; thus, adding UHPC on the roof is more for physical protection (e.g., impact,
ballistic, and environmental durability). If in a future configuration the microreactor were supported from
or attached to the roof, the roof would then need to be analyzed as a load-bearing element; in that case,
composite action between the roof steel and UHPC would become critical, and the flexural and shear
checks for the roof would have to be carried out in the same manner as for the floor system. Overall, this
analysis focuses on the floor and sidewalls up to the neutral axis height.

5.2.1 Stationary (Gravity Load)

The container is supported at its base, typically at the four bottom corner fittings sitting on a foundation or
transport platform. The full weight of the microreactor acts downward on the floor via the support rails.
The container frame must transfer this load to the supports at the corners. In a standard container on
ground, often the bottom side rails near the corners and some portion of the base frame carry the load. In
this analysis, conservative support conditions are assumed where the container is supported only at the
four bottom corners. This is the worst case for bending of the floor and side rails, since the middle is
unsupported. Any additional midspan support from ground contact are neglected. Thus, the floor cross-
members span between the side rails, and the side rails span between the corner posts, under the reactor
load. The distribution of the 32 kip concentrated load among the cross-members is studied herein. For
example, with 2 rails, each rail carries ~16 kip which it delivers to each cross-member it crosses. With
~12″ cross-member spacing, the reactor might sit atop ~10–12 cross-members; the load on each cross-
member under a rail would then be on the order of 1.3–1.6 kip (i.e., 16 kip/12) if evenly distributed. In
reality, the distribution may be uneven with the middle cross-members taking a bit more load. The global
bending of the container’s side structure is also analyzed; effectively, the side rails and corner posts form
a longitudinal beam carrying the floor load to the corners. The maximum bending moment occurs at mid-
span of the container (i.e., under the reactor) and will put the bottom side rail in tension and the top side
rail in compression. The overall load path and resulting structural actions are illustrated in Figure 9: the

5.2.2 Lifting (Corner Lift Load)

The container is lifted from above by a spreader beam attaching to the top corner fittings, as is standard in
ISO container handling. In this scenario, the weight of the reactor and container is supported by the top
frame at the corners, putting the corner posts in compression, meaning that they act like columns hanging
the container. The container will experience a bending deformation because the heavy reactor in the
middle causes the bottom to sag downward relative to the lifted corners. This is essentially an inverted
loading compared to stationary support since now the top corners are load points that are in tension from
the spreader and the weight in the middle causes a downward deflection. However, from the container’s

 32

perspective, the internal forces are similar to the stationary case because it is still a beam spanning
between supports with a central load. The difference is that in lifting, the corner posts experience a large
compressive axial load since they must carry the weight to the top fittings, and the side rails experience
bending with potentially different boundary conditions depending on frame action at the corners. The
container frame is considered simply supported at the corner fittings for global bending calculations,
which is a reasonable approximation if the spreader clamps allow some rotation. The dynamic impact of
lifting is also considered since a crane lift can introduce inertial loads if there is any acceleration or sway.
A typical hoisting dynamic factor is ~1.25 (i.e., 25% increase in force to account for acceleration and
impact). A dynamic amplification factor is applied to the reactor weight for the lifting case to be
conservative. Thus, the design lift load might be ~1.25×32,000 = 40,000 lb effective. This ensures the
structure has extra capacity for any shock loading.

In the lifting case, each top corner fitting carries roughly one fourth of the total load, about 10 kip each, or
10 kip × 1.25 = 12.5 kip, accounting for impact. Since these forces go down the corner posts, it is
imperative to check the corner post compression stress and buckling stability. The floor cross-members in
lifting do not directly carry additional load since the reactor still sits on them with the same weight, but
now the supports moved from below to above. The load path is still from reactor to rails to cross-
members to side rails to corner posts, but the corner posts are now in compression rather than the bottom
corners in bearing. One new consideration in lifting is local lifting stresses at the corner fittings (e.g.,
distortions at the top frame). In a standard ISO container, the top rail and corner junction are designed to
handle a full payload in lift, per ISO 1496 testing [46].

5.2.3 Load Combinations

For this design, the main structural action is primarily the dead load of the reactor. In a nuclear context,
one would also consider environmental loads (seismic, wind) and accidental loads (e.g. transport shocks).
Here we assume the most critical load is the reactor weight itself; if needed, other loads can be
superimposed with appropriate factors. The NRC guidelines (e.g., General Design Criteria [61] and
Regulatory Guides [58] usually require combining loads with factors to ensure Service Level D (accident)
conditions are within ultimate capacity. In absence of specific additional loads, it can be assumed that
Service Level A (normal operation) stresses are well within allowable limits (typically <100% yield) and
even hypothetical accident factors (e.g., 1.5× load) would not push beyond ultimate capacity. It will be
shown herein that the UHPC shipping container keeps stresses mostly below ~50% of yield in normal
conditions, providing substantial reserve.

5.3 STRUCTURAL LIMIT STATES

5.3.1 Stationary Loading Scenario

When the container is supported on its base (e.g., on a foundation or rails along the bottom), the heavy
reactor weight is transferred through the floor into the bottom side rails and to the supports . In the worst-
case, it can be conservatively assumed that only the corner posts/rails carry the load (i.e., no continuous
support along the length), which forces the floor and side structure to carry the weight to the four corners.
The UHPC layers applied to the interior floor and exterior underside effectively create a sandwich
composite for the floor beams. Here, ‘UHPC–steel–UHPC’ denotes UHPC on both faces (interior floor
and exterior underside), forming a symmetric sandwich. This configuration increases the bending stiffness
and capacity of the floor beam significantly. If only one face is coated (interior-only or exterior-only), the
section is asymmetric and the UHPC primarily contributes on the compression side under bending,
yielding smaller gains than the full sandwich. The structural checks are verified step-by-step for these
configurations.

 33

1. Load Distribution to Floor Beams: The first step involves determining how the heavy load
 spreads to the floor crossmembers. Let W be the total weight of the reactor. If the reactor is
 supported by Nr longitudinal steel rails inside the container, each rail carries approximately W/Nr
 load. These rails in turn sit atop the transverse floor beams (cross-members). With cross-ties
 between rails (to spread load), it’s reasonable to assume the load is distributed evenly to the m
 crossmembers under the reactor’s footprint.
2. Floor Beam Bending: Each floor cross-member is modeled as a simply-supported beam
 spanning the container width. The rail sitting on it applies point loads (or a distributed line load)
 to the beam. A conservative case is two equal point loads P (from the rail) placed symmetrically
 on the beam. The maximum bending moment for two symmetric point loads is M = P·a, where a
 is the distance from each support to the load.

3. Bending Stress Check: Compute the bending stress in the steel beam σ = M/S, where S is the
 section modulus of the beam’s cross-section. Design criteria require no yielding in normal service
 (i.e., steel stresses must be limited to ~0.6Fy for Service Level A) . In the case of loading the
 container with a relatively heavy microreactor, UHPC reinforcement is needed to increase the
 beam’s flexural capacity.

4. Composite Section Capacity: With UHPC layers bonded to the steel (inside top and outside
 bottom), the floor beam acts as a composite section. The UHPC is transformed into an equivalent
 steel area using n = EUHPC/Esteel, where EUHPC and Esteel are the modulus of elasticity for the UHPC
 and steel, respectively and n is the modulus ratio. For a given UHPC thickness, t on each side,
 over an effective width b of floor slab engaged, the UHPC area per side is AUHPC = b·t. The
 transformed area is AUHPC' = n·AUHPC. The neutral axis yNA of the composite section is found by
 equating moments of area: yNA = (ΣEi·Ai·yi) / (ΣEi·Ai) from a reference (e.g., bottom of section) .
 Then the composite second moment of area, also known as the section’s moment of inertia Icomp
 must be calculated: Icomp = Σ(Ei·Ii + Ei·Ai·di2), where Ii is the moment of inertia of each part of the
 composite section (i.e., UHPC and steel parts) and di is the distance from the neutral axis of the
 composite section to the centroid of each part of the section. Next, the section modulus Scomp must
 be determined for the steel bottom fiber Sbottom since it is critical in bending if the bottom is in
 tension: Sbottom = Icomp / ybottom where ybottom is the distance from the neutral axis to the bottom steel
 fiber. If UHPC is applied inside and outside (both top and bottom of the beam), the section is
 roughly symmetric. The neutral axis ends up near the mid-thickness of the steel web (due to
 symmetric reinforcement). The composite moment of inertia increases dramatically. In reality the
 UHPC at the tension side will crack once its tensile strength is exceeded, so it cannot fully
 contribute tension resistance beyond first-crack. A conservative design assumes limited UHPC
 tension capacity in service (allowable tensile stress ~0.5 ksi to avoid microcracks). Nevertheless,
 the interior UHPC (on top of the beam) acts in compression and the exterior UHPC (bottom)
 offers stiffness (and some post-crack residual strength via fibers).

5. Floor Beam Shear: Check the shear in the cross-member at the supports. The reaction at each
 end of a loaded beam is R = P (for two symmetric loads P each). The beam’s web (i.e., two sides
 of the tube) provides the shear area Aw for a tube. The shear stress is τ = P/Aw. In practice, even
 with dynamic factors, shear stresses remain modest relative to bending stresses and the possibility
 of local web buckling. Thus, floor beam strength is governed by flexure rather than shear. Shear
 buckling of the thin steel web must also be considered, but the container’s corrugated wall
 geometry and the added UHPC effectively stiffen the web against buckling. The UHPC overlay
 increases the effective web thickness dramatically, raising the critical shear buckling stress of the
 panels to a very high level. Thus, web shear capacity with UHPC is not a concern.

 34

 6. Side Rail and Corner Post Action: In the stationary case, if the container’s base support is
 continuous (e.g., sitting on a platform along each side rail), the side rails mainly experience
 vertical reaction forces and little bending since the floor beams transfer load directly near where it
 is applied. In the worst-case assumption of only corner support, however, the container’s floor
 acts like a large span carrying the load to the corners via the side structures. This induces axial
 compression in the side rails and corner posts as they channel the weight to the supports. To be
 conservative, consider the scenario where each bottom side rail would see roughly half the reactor
 weight as axial compressive load if the floor beams push down on the rails, which are supported
 only at the ends. Spread over 40 ft, this axial force is relatively small, so pure axial compression
 in the rails is not critical. Corner posts at the four corners each take about W/4 compression loads.
 Each corner post is designed to handle up to ~116 kip in compression (for 8-high stacking),
 therefore, if the microreactor weighs W = 32 kip, W/4 = 8 kip would be a negligible stress (safety
 factor >14) [46]. Even accounting for dynamic impact or uneven load, the corner post
 compressive stress is extremely low. Buckling of the posts under this load is not a concern either;
 the posts are very robust and designed for much larger compression loads since their critical
 buckling load is orders of magnitude higher than 8 kip. If only the corners support the container,
 there will also be some global bending in the side frames, similar to the lifting case, described
 next. In truth, a container housing such a heavy object would likely be supported along its length
 to avoid this extreme bending. Nevertheless, for completeness, the side structure must be
 designed as a deep beam in this scenario; the side walls plus side rails form a deep composite
 girder spanning 40 ft with the load at mid-span. With UHPC layers on both sides of the wall, the
 web’s effective thickness and stiffness increase greatly, so shear and deflection checks are easily
 satisfied since the UHPC-steel sandwich wall can carry the shear with a large margin.
 Additionally, the UHPC improves out-of-plane rigidity of the wall, preventing any excessive
 bulging as the container sags.

In summary, for the stationary scenario, the critical checks are floor beam bending, which is governed by
steel yield without UHPC, and potentially global bending if supports are only at the corners. The UHPC
interior/exterior retrofit raises the floor capacity by ~5 times in bending stiffness, ensuring steel stresses
remain below 0.6Fy and UHPC compressive stresses below ~0.5f’c in this operating condition. Deflections
must also be kept within reasonable limits (e.g., generally a small fraction of span), which protects the
integrity of the container and any attachments.

5.3.2 Lifting Loading Scenario

When the container, with the reactor inside, is lifted by a crane at its top four corner fittings, the load path
and stresses change significantly. In the lifting scenario, the container behaves like a simply supported
beam of span 40 ft (corner to corner) with an internal load W at mid-span. This induces global bending in
the side frames and puts the corner posts in tension since the bottom wants to drop relative to the lifted
top. All relevant stresses under this condition, including dynamic effects, must be considered. An impact
factor of 15% has been applied to lifted loads herein to account for dynamic effects during crane hoisting,
based on the American Society of Civil Engineering (ASCE) 37-14 [62] and the American Institute of
Steel Construction (AISC) recommendations [63]. Although ISO 1496-1 [46] and ISO 3874 [64] specify
container test loads and handling configurations, they do not provide explicit dynamic amplification
factors; therefore, the impact allowance is taken from structural engineering best practices to ensure
safety and conservatism. The design goal is to keep stresses well below yield. The major structural checks
for lifting are as follows:

 1. Global Bending of Side Structure: Each side of the container spans ~40 ft between lifting
 points. With the heavy load W at mid-span, the side wall/rail structure sees a peak moment of
 M = (W·L)/8, considering a symmetric mid-span load in a simply-supported beam and this load

 35

 being split evenly amongst each of the two walls. Without UHPC, the container relies on the full
 8.5′ side wall acting as a deep plate-girder to resist this moment. The steel stresses in that case are
 within basic yield limits, since the container’s original design can handle being lifted with its
 rated load (~79 kip gross). However, since the distribution of stress in the unreinforced container
 is very non-uniform, the corrugated steel web would be highly stressed and potentially near
 buckling in some zones, and the deflection would be large. With UHPC layers on the side walls
 and rails, the side structure transforms into a composite beam of vastly higher stiffness (see
 below, where the effective stiffness is estimated to increase by a factor of 3–5). The UHPC
 effectively thickens the flanges and web: the side rails and corner posts are encased in UHPC, and
 the side wall panel becomes a sandwiched UHPC-steel-UHPC plate. The effective moment of
 inertia of the sidewall increases by an estimated factor of 3-5 with UHPC layers. Thus, the
 bending stress in the steel rails and posts is greatly reduced. In quantitative terms, if the
 composite sidewall/rail has section modulus Scomp,side, the bending stress is σ = M/Scomp,side. The
 UHPC on the exterior of the side wall (e.g., the tension side when the wall bends outward under
 sagging) carries part of the tensile force up to its cracking limit, after which the steel takes the
 remainder. The interior UHPC on the side wall is in compression, which is beneficial since
 UHPC excels in compression. It must be ensured that the steel stress stays below ~0.6Fy and the
 UHPC exterior layer’s tension stays below its cracking strength. Deflection under lift is also
 greatly reduced by the stiff composite action since the reinforced container behaves almost like a
 rigid tube under lift.

2. Floor Beam and Rail Loads: Even when lifted, the heavy reactor still bears on the floor cross-
 members and support rails inside the container. Thus, the local floor beam checks from the
 stationary case remain relevant under lifting. Each cross-member sees roughly the same reactions
 as the stationary case. The difference is that now the supports of those floor beams (i.e., the side
 rails) are not rigid since they are part of the globally bending frame. However, the side rails are
 much stiffer than the cross-members, therefore, it is a reasonable assumption to treat the floor
 beams as simply supported at the side rails, as usual. The floor beam bending check under lifting
 is conducted similarly as the stationary case. With UHPC reinforcement, the floor beams remain
 well within stress limits (for both steel and UHPC) under the lifting condition, as well. In fact, the
 worst-case bending in floor beams likely occurred in the stationary case when the base was fully
 rigid because then the beams had to carry the entire load with little global deflection to help. In
 the lifting case, the global flexure might actually relieve a tiny bit of floor beam moment since the
 frame sags, distributing load slightly. The shear in floor beams under lifting is similar to the
 stationary case and poses no issue, plus UHPC adds slight dowel action and clamping to further
 increase shear resistance. It is also worth noting that the internal support configuration (e.g., 2
 rails vs 4 or 6 rails) has minimal effect on global stresses during lifting. Fewer rails mean each
 rail carries more of the reactor weight, but regardless of how it is distributed internally, the total
 load is the same 32 kip at mid-span for the container’s structure. Thus, the global bending of the
 container is governed by the total weight and container span, not by the number of internal rails.
 The internal supports mainly affect the floor local behavior since more rails spread the load to
 more cross-members, reducing individual beam load. Thus, for designing UHPC thickness, the
 global reinforcement needs (e.g., side wall, rails, posts) are driven by the total load, whereas the
 local floor reinforcement needs are driven by how concentrated the load is on single beams.

3. Corner Posts: In top lifting, each corner post is essentially in tension, acting as a hanging rod
 supporting the bottom portion of the container, which carries the weight, from the top corner
 fitting that is attached to the crane. In this scenario, each of the 4 top corners supports roughly
 W/4 plus some container weight. The axial tensile stress in a corner post of area ~3.5 in² is
 extremely low. The corner posts also connect the top and bottom rails, so as the container bends,
 there may be a minor bending moment imposed at the connections (e.g., the posts may see slight

 36

 bending if the deflected shape causes rotation at the ends). However, these bending effects in the
 corner posts are small and the posts are very stout. The posts were originally designed to carry
 huge compressive loads; now in tension they are even less likely to fail since there is no buckling
 concern in tension. It must be ensured that the combined stress (i.e., axial + bending, if any)
 meets the code interaction criteria. Although container lifting primarily induces global axial stress
 in the corner posts and side frames, the local floor system must still carry vertical load through
 the cross members. In this study, it is conservatively assumed that floor cross members continue
 to experience concentrated loading during lifting, even though some redistribution may occur due
 to suspension geometry or container curvature. Overall, the corner posts under lift are not a
 limiting factor and they have large safety margins.

Under lifting conditions, the UHPC-reinforced container performs exceedingly well. All steel stresses
(i.e., side rails, corner posts, floor beams) stay well below yield, and UHPC stresses remain within
allowable limits (no crushing and minimal cracking) . The interaction of bending, shear, and axial loads in
each component satisfies code requirements with comfortable margins. The deflections and buckling
safety are greatly improved by the UHPC layers since the container essentially acts as a composite tube
with much higher rigidity. A design philosophy was adopted herein to ensure redundancy: even if the
UHPC were to crack or partially debond in an extreme event, the steel structure alone, perhaps at reduced
allowable stress, could still carry the load without collapse. This conservative approach meets nuclear
safety guidelines for heavy load lifts.

5.4.2 Parametric Study of Minimum UHPC Required

This section explains the setup, assumptions, methodology, and results of a detailed parametric study of
UHPC composite floor systems for an ISO shipping container used to support a 32,000 lb reactor. The
goal of the study is to determine the minimum required UHPC thickness to meet code-based structural
stress limits under different layout configurations.

 40

The study determines the minimum required UHPC thickness that satisfies all strength-based code
requirements for:

  Steel bending and shear in the cross members
  Compression and tension in the UHPC
  Global bending of the container sidewall
  Axial stress in corner posts (lifting case)

This study is important because it:

  Optimizes UHPC volume (minimizing material cost and weight)
  Enables sizing of cross members (e.g., C2×2.3 vs C4×4.14)
  Provides validated input for container design, structural safety, and licensing

5.4.2.1 Physical Configuration and Load Path

The reactor rests on steel skid rails placed longitudinally inside the shipping container. These rails bear
directly onto a structural floor system comprising:

  Sprayed UHPC top layer (variable thickness)
  C-channel cross members (e.g., C2×2.3) running transversely
  Corrugated steel ISO floor, onto which the C-channels are welded
  Optionally, a UHPC layer below the corrugated steel

The C-channels are welded to both the side rails and the corrugated floor, forming a continuous support
network. The UHPC is sprayed over and between the channels, forming a composite slab. Shear studs or
mechanical keys ensure composite action between the UHPC and the steel frame. Shear studs are
assumed to be spaced at 4–6″ on center, consistent with composite floor design standards [66]. This
spacing is sufficient to transfer horizontal shear and prevent interfacial slip under the expected load levels
from skid rails. Stud anchorage into UHPC is assumed to develop full shear transfer capacity, as shown in
FHWA-HRT-06-103 [48].

Considering the results outlined in Section 5.4.2.3, the recommended design for the UHPC ISO shipping

5.5 FINAL DESIGN: RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

This section justifies the selection of a structurally optimal configuration for a UHPC-reinforced shipping
container floor system intended to support a 32,000 lb reactor. After a comprehensive parametric study
and evaluation of numerous design permutations, the configuration that demonstrated superior
performance and resilience across all structural checks was identified as follows: four longitudinal skid
rails, six C2×2.3 C-channel cross members welded transversely to the container side rails and the
corrugated steel ISO floor, and UHPC applied symmetrically on both the interior and exterior surfaces of
the floor system.

5.5.1 System Configuration and Structural Load Path

The container floor system is modeled as a layered composite slab. The steel floor framing consists of
shallow but wide C2×2.3 channels acting as transverse cross members. These channels are welded to the
container’s longitudinal side rails and directly to the corrugated ISO floor beneath. A bottom layer of
UHPC is sprayed to fill the corrugations and form a continuous bond with the bottom flanges of the C-
channels. An additional UHPC layer is applied above and between the C-channel flanges, embedding
their tops and providing a continuous upper face. The corrugated ISO floor is assumed to participate
structurally as a composite core layer when fully bonded to the UHPC and cross members. Although it is
thin (typically 14 gauge), its corrugated geometry provides out-of-plane stiffness. Composite interaction
is enabled through shear studs or mechanical bonding. While not explicitly modeled in flexural
calculations, it plays a critical role in distributing shear and providing local diaphragm stability.

Longitudinal skid rails, structural steel members supporting the reactor, are positioned atop the exterior
UHPC layer of the floor section. The skid rails are modeled as rigid elements for load introduction. Their
bending flexibility is conservatively neglected in this study. In future designs where rail flexibility
becomes significant, a more detailed simulation may be required to assess load spreading to underlying
floor members.

When the reactor is placed inside the container, loads transfer from the reactor into the longitudinal skid
rails. These rails deliver the load as discrete lines onto the UHPC top layer, which disperses the forces
into the transverse cross members. The cross members transfer the vertical shear and bending moments
into the side rails, which serve as boundary supports. Global loading of the container also produces
sidewall bending and vertical axial loads in the corner posts, especially during lifting.

 44

5.5.2 Analytical Modeling and Simulation Approach

To model the structural response, a transformed-section composite analysis approach is used. This
method calculates a new equivalent moment of inertia and centroid for the composite cross section, which
includes interior UHPC (compression layer), steel C2×2.3 channel (primary flexural member), corrugated
steel, and exterior UHPC (tension layer).

The UHPC is transformed to an equivalent steel area using the modular ratio. Composite section
properties are calculated per unit width of floor system, typically with b = 1.0 inch. This assumption
simplifies analysis and provides normalized values for UHPC thickness and flexural capacity. The
composite moment of inertia includes transformed areas and distances from the neutral axis for UHPC
(top and bottom) and steel (C-channel), using modular ratio n = EUHPC / Esteel. Full composite action is
assumed, made possible by shear studs or mechanical bonding at the UHPC–steel interface, in accordance
with ACI 318-19 §16.2.4 [56] and AISC 360-16 Chapter I [66].

The calculations are conservative by design. Loads are applied as concentrated point loads at midspan of
cross members (i.e., worst-case condition). No load distribution or arching effects from the UHPC shell
are assumed. The container is treated as simply supported with no continuous moment resistance at the
ends. Despite these conservative assumptions, the chosen configuration exceeds all performance criteria.

5.5.6    Summary

The parametric study has demonstrated that a configuration with four skid rails, six C2×2.3 cross

application provides a highly effective structural solution. For context, several lower-performing
configurations were evaluated and rejected due to either higher required UHPC thickness or smaller

required substantially thicker overlays to remain uncracked (e.g., up to ~1.875 in with 2 rails × 2 cross
members), while sparse framing with 2 rails × 2 cross members drove steel demands near the allowable
limits even with interior-only UHPC (σsteel ≈ 28.15 ksi vs 30 ksi allowable). Symmetric layouts with fewer
cross members (e.g., 2 rails × 6 cross members) also required thicker UHPC (e.g., ~1.75 in) to control
tension, offering less material efficiency than the selected 4-rail × 6-member configuration. These
comparisons, summarized in Table 6, motivated the choice of four rails, six C2×2.3 cross members, and
0.75 in UHPC per face as the best balance of thickness, demand-to-capacity ratios, and constructability.

Although the selected design uses relatively lightweight C2×2.3 channels, their use is justified by the
composite action developed with the UHPC layers. Transformed section analysis shows that the resulting
composite moment of inertia and section modulus are sufficient to maintain all stresses well below code-
defined limits. The inclusion of both interior and exterior UHPC helps stabilize the thin C-channels,
improving torsional rigidity and mitigating local buckling risk. This layout is not only structurally
conservative but operationally robust, constructible, and defensible under all applicable design codes. It
balances material efficiency with performance, while remaining flexible for future container adaptations
or licensing requirements.

5.6 IMPLICATIONS OF INCREASED PAYLOAD WEIGHT

An important consideration when evaluating the UHPC container design methodology is the potential
impact of substantially increased payload weight on the structural performance of the container. If the
reactor payload were to approach weights closer to 100,000 lbs, the design stress evaluations for key
structural elements, including the container’s floor system and sidewalls, must be reexamined [68]. Such
an increased payload would necessitate modifications to internal crossmembers, additional skid rails, and
adjustments to the applied UHPC thicknesses. These measures would ensure that all critical load
scenarios, including crane-lifting and stationary deployment, remain within allowable design margins.

Furthermore, a higher payload weight would require validating compatibility with intermodal transport
logistics and assessing compliance with ISO weight requirements for standard containers. Special
handling arrangements, such as overweight permits or trailers with reinforced axles, would likely be
needed to ensure safe transport. These considerations highlight the importance of integrating payload
weight sensitivity analyses into the structural optimization process throughout the design and testing
phases.

 6. MODELING AND SIMULATION

6.1 MESOSCALE MODELING

6.1.1 Lattice Discrete Particle Model (LDPM) Overview

The Lattice Discrete Particle Model (LDPM) is a discrete mesoscale computational framework developed
to simulate the nonlinear mechanical behavior of concrete and other quasi-brittle materials. Unlike
conventional continuum-based finite element approaches, LDPM explicitly models the granular
heterogeneity of concrete by representing coarse aggregate particles as interacting rigid bodies. These
particles are embedded within a polyhedral lattice formed by a 3D Delaunay tessellation, and their
interactions occur across the triangular facets of the tetrahedral cells. Each interaction facet serves as a
potential failure plane governed by a local vectorial constitutive law that captures normal and shear
stresses as functions of relative displacements and rotations between neighboring particles [69].

LDPM resolves critical quasi-brittle damage mechanisms at the mesoscale, including tensile cracking,
frictional sliding, shear dilatancy, and inelastic compaction under compression. When tensile strains
exceed a critical threshold, a cohesive fracture process initiates at the facet, characterized by strain-
softening and progressive crack opening. In shear, LDPM accounts for aggregate interlock and frictional
resistance, with the shear stress coupled to the normal pressure acting on the crack face. Under high
compressive confinement, the model simulates pore collapse and rehardening, capturing the ductile
behavior seen in confined concrete [69,70].

LDPM has been validated extensively against laboratory experiments involving uniaxial and multiaxial
stress states. It reproduces size-dependent strength and fracture patterns, stiffness degradation, tension–
shear coupling, and strain localization with high fidelity [70]. Figure 10 illustrates the LDPM
mesostructure and its core computational entities.

Figure 10. LDPM mesostructure and lattice architecture. (a) Randomly packed spherical aggregates. (b) 3D
Delaunay tessellation forming tetrahedral cells. (c) Facets between particles serve as potential failure planes.

To extend LDPM to reinforced concrete, rebar elements can be integrated into the lattice using beam
elements with embedded bond-slip links. These connections transfer stress between rebar and surrounding
lattice nodes, enabling simulation of anchorage, dowel action, and rebar fracture. This LDPM–rebar
coupling has been successfully applied in flexural testing simulations, impact resistance studies, and
failure analysis of reinforced concrete columns and panels [72].

6.1.2 LDPM-F: Modeling Fiber-Reinforced UHPC

The Lattice Discrete Particle Model with fiber reinforcement (LDPM-F) framework is an extension of
LDPM that incorporates discrete steel or synthetic fibers into the concrete mesostructure. It was
developed to capture the behavior of fiber-reinforced concretes (FRC), particularly ultra-high-
performance fiber-reinforced concrete (UHPFRC), where fibers play a critical role in enhancing post-
cracking ductility, toughness, and energy absorption [73].

In LDPM-F, fibers are modeled as linear segments randomly distributed in the concrete domain, each
with a defined orientation, length, and diameter. A fiber–facet interaction is established whenever a fiber
crosses an LDPM facet. The model calculates an additional traction vector associated with the fiber’s
bridging force, which is superposed on the facet’s concrete stress vector. This bridging traction becomes
active once the facet enters a tension or shear-dominated cracking regime. It resists crack opening and
sliding, reproducing fiber pull-out, debonding, and rupture behaviors [73,74].

LDPM-F integrates several key micromechanical phenomena:

 1. Debonding and pull-out: Controlled by bond strength, slip-hardening, and fracture energy.
 2. Snubbing effect: Accounts for increased resistance from fiber curvature during pull-out.
 3. Matrix spalling: Models fiber-induced microcracking at the exit point.
 4. Fiber rupture: Limits traction if fiber tensile strength is exceeded.
 5. Plastic yielding: Simulates inelastic stretching of ductile fibers [74,75].

These mechanisms are parameterized based on experimental data such as single-fiber pull-out tests and
fiber tensile tests. Recent calibration efforts for both steel and polypropylene FRC have demonstrated
how parameter sensitivity and mesh resolution affect LDPM-F’s predictive accuracy [76,77]. Figure 11
illustrates how LDPM-F simulates fiber–matrix interactions.

6.1.3 Model Validation and Structural-Scale Applications

LDPM-F has been validated in various material and structural tests, including:

  Uniaxial and direct tension of dogbone and notched specimens [73,74]
  Notched three-point bending [73,74]
  Confined and unconfined compression [73]
  Rebar pull-out [74]
  Reinforced UHPC beam flexure and shear tests [74]
  Projectile impact and blast resistance [75,76]

In these studies, LDPM-F was shown to accurately reproduce the post-cracking ductility, multiple-
cracking behavior, and failure modes observed in FRC and UHPFRC. For instance, Bhaduri and Alnaggar
(2019) used LDPM-F to demonstrate the transition from brittle shear failure to ductile flexural failure in
reactive UHPC beams by varying fiber content and rebar ratio [74]. Similarly, Smith et al. (2014) applied
LDPM-F to simulate projectile penetration into UHPC panels, predicting depth of penetration and damage
morphology in agreement with full-scale ballistic tests [75]. LDPM-F has also been applied to simulate
blast loading scenarios, reproducing crater dimensions and slab damage under contact detonations [76].
These capabilities highlight LDPM-F’s strength in modeling complex loading conditions and strain-rate-
sensitive fracture behaviors.

6.1.4 Relevance to UHPC Shipping Container Design

The UHPC shipping container concept presents a structural system composed of fiber-reinforced UHPC
slabs integrated with steel stiffeners, shear connectors, and possible internal reinforcement. LDPM-F is
well suited for this application because it:

  Captures shear and flexural crack propagation across corrugated or planar UHPC slabs
  Resolves fiber-induced tension–shear interaction critical to blast or puncture resistance
  Tracks failure localization and cracking morphology with millimeter resolution
  Enables parametric studies of fiber volume, orientation, and rebar layout

Additionally, LDPM-F can be implemented using the Multiphysics Analyses of the Response of
Structures (MARS) simulation software, a scalable high-performance computing framework optimized
for LDPM simulations. MARS enables simulation of structural-scale models (e.g., entire container wall
assemblies) with millions of lattice nodes and fiber interactions [78]. The MARS simulation software is
discussed more in Section 6.2.

Overall, LDPM-F offers a powerful simulation capability for analyzing the mechanical performance and
failure behavior of fiber-reinforced UHPC structures. By capturing mesoscale cracking, fiber-bridging,
and rebar–matrix interaction in a unified framework, LDPM-F allows virtual prototyping of advanced
UHPC components like shipping containers. Its use can guide optimization of fiber content, layout of
reinforcement, and structural detailing to ensure performance under service and extreme conditions.

6.2 SIMULATIONS USING LDPM-F WITH MARS

The MARS software is a high-performance computational framework designed specifically to implement
and execute the LDPM and its fiber-reinforced extension (i.e., LDPM-F). Developed by ES3 Inc. in
collaboration with academic and national laboratory partners, MARS enables highly detailed simulations
of quasi-brittle materials, such as concrete and UHPFRC, at multiple scales (e.g., from laboratory
coupons to structural assemblies) [78].

6.2.1 Core Architecture and Computational Strategy

MARS is built around a robust explicit dynamic integration scheme, making it particularly effective for
solving large-scale, nonlinear fracture problems involving severe discontinuities, dynamic crack
propagation, strain localization, and impact or blast loading [78]. The solver treats each coarse aggregate
particle in the LDPM mesh as a rigid body with six degrees of freedom (i.e., three translational and three
rotational), connected via deformable interfaces defined on tetrahedral facets. The equations of motion for
these particles are integrated over time using a central difference method, which allows stable and
efficient time-stepping even in highly nonlinear regimes [69,78].

For LDPM-F applications, MARS includes additional modules that handle:

  Generation and random distribution of fibers with prescribed orientation distributions and volume
 fractions
  Automatic detection of fiber–facet intersections within the 3D lattice
  Computation of fiber bridging forces based on micromechanical laws accounting for debonding,
 frictional pullout, snubbing effects, and fiber rupture
  Superposition of fiber tractions on facet-level concrete stress calculations

The solver uses a data-parallel architecture with full support for Message Passing Interface, enabling
deployment on high-performance computing clusters. This parallelization allows simulations of
structural-scale UHPC components containing millions of particles and fiber-matrix interactions to be
executed with reasonable computational effort and memory footprint [78].

6.2.2 Modeling Capabilities

In LDPM-F mode, MARS can simulate a wide range of complex behaviors, including:

  Multiaxial stress states and confinement effects in UHPC
  Crack initiation, branching, and coalescence under tensile and shear loading
  Transition from distributed microcracking to localized fracture zones
  Post-cracking behavior in fiber-reinforced systems, including multiple cracking and crack width
 control
  Dynamic loading scenarios such as blast, impact, and drop tests
  Interactions between UHPC matrix, discrete fibers, steel rebars, and shear studs

MARS also provides facilities for coupling LDPM mesh with external structural elements such as rigid
bodies, boundary constraints, and embedded steel reinforcement modeled using Timoshenko beam
elements [72]. These rebar elements can be bonded to the LDPM mesh via penalty-based links that allow
simulation of dowel action, bond-slip degradation, and anchorage failure under load [72].

6.2.3 Input and Output Management

Users define simulations through structured input files that specify material parameters, mesh geometry,
fiber configurations, and loading protocols. LDPM material parameters (e.g., tensile strength, fracture
energy, elastic modulus, friction coefficients) can be calibrated using experimental data and sensitivity
studies. The software includes internal preprocessing utilities for mesh generation, fiber population, and
boundary condition assignment [78].

Post-processing output includes:

  Local variables such as facet stresses, crack opening displacements, damage indices, and fiber

In parallel, a complementary set of simulations is being conducted using CTH, a continuum-based

processes, fiber bridging, and aggregate-scale crack patterns, whereas CTH is more effective for modeling
bulk wave propagation, large-scale stress fields, and energy distribution across the target. As a result,
LDPM-F provides detailed insight into micromechanical damage mechanisms, while CTH offers a
broader systems-level perspective on overall structural response. By comparing LDPM-F and CTH results
side-by-side for identical test conditions, the following objectives will be addressed:

  Quantify differences between discrete and continuum modeling approaches.
  Evaluate scale-dependent mechanisms (e.g., crack localization vs. wave propagation).
  Develop multi-scale interpretations of ballistic impact in UHPC.

The ultimate aim of any extended simulation effort would be to establish a robust, validated, and
calibrated mesoscale model that can be confidently used for design, optimization, and performance
assessment of UHPC-based ballistic-resistant systems. The combination of high-fidelity discrete
modeling (LDPM-F), validated through experiment, and supplemented by continuum methods (CTH),
will form a comprehensive modeling toolkit for future design applications in security and critical
infrastructure protection.

6.3 SIMULATION OF UHPC PANELS USING CTH

6.3.1 Simulation Framework and Modeling Inputs

6.3.1.1 CTH Software Overview

The simulations in this section were conducted using CTH, an advanced Eulerian shock physics code
developed by Sandia National Laboratories. CTH is optimized for modeling complex, multi-material
interactions under high strain rates, including large deformations, shock propagation, penetration, and
material failure. The code supports one-, two-, and three-dimensional geometries, including axisymmetric
cylindrical models, and features capabilities such as:

  Adaptive Mesh Refinement to improve resolution in regions of interest.
  Support for elastic-plastic, viscoplastic, porous, and explosive material models.
  Second-order accurate numerical solvers that reduce numerical diffusion and dispersion.
  Parallel scalability, demonstrated on systems utilizing over 1.5 million processor cores.
  End-to-end simulation workflows including visualization and output tracking capabilities.

These features make CTH particularly well-suited for modeling ballistic impacts into UHPC under
dynamic loading conditions.

6.3.1.2 Geometry and Meshing Strategy

The preliminary simulations shown herein used a 2D axisymmetric cylindrical mesh, selected for
computational efficiency while accurately capturing radial and longitudinal effects of high-velocity
impacts. Mesh refinement was concentrated in the impact zone, encompassing both the projectile
trajectory and the deformation region within the UHPC slab. This approach allows for enhanced accuracy
in tracking fracture patterns and projectile degradation. The CTH program employs an adaptive mesh that
automatically refines resolution around the impact zone and in regions exhibiting significant changes in
density, pressure, or velocity. This approach improves accuracy while maintaining computational
efficiency.

Key Findings

 1. Projectile-Material Interaction Dynamics
 The 0.50 Cal M2 Ball projectile exhibits deep penetration behavior resulting from its high kinetic

  Intermediate Stages: As the 0.50 Cal round progresses through the slab, it induces

  Terminal Stages: The projectile emerges from the rear face of the UHPC slab after

 2. Material Response and Damage Evolution
 The UHPC slab exhibits multiple damage mechanisms during the penetration and perforation

  Crushing and Compaction Near the Impact Zone: The material directly beneath the

  Tensile Cracking and Spallation: Secondary tensile cracks radiate outward from the

 shock waves reach the free surfaces and tensile stresses exceed the material's strength.

  Material Ejection and Fracture Propagation: As the projectile exits, fragmented

 propagation and lateral energy dispersion as the projectile punches through the last layer

 3. Projectile Behavior
 The 0.50 Cal M2 Ball projectile maintains its structural integrity throughout the penetration and
 perforation process. Unlike smaller or less robust projectiles, the M2 Ball’s hardened core
 prevents significant deformation or erosion during its high-strain-rate interaction with the UHPC.
 This allows it to retain enough residual energy to perforate the slab and emerge on the exit side

 sustained energy transfer until perforation is complete. This significantly distinguishes the
 performance of the M2 Ball projectile from the M855, which lacks the energy required to achieve
 perforation in similar UHPC conditions.

 with smaller-caliber projectiles like the M855, the 0.50 Cal M2 Ball simulation highlights several

  Complete Perforation: The 0.50 Cal projectile’s energy is sufficient to cause through-

  Larger Damage Footprint: The energy delivered by the 0.50 Cal round results in a

  Higher Energy Transfer: While both projectiles induce localized crushing, the kinetic

Overall, the 0.50 Cal M2 Ball projectile successfully perforates the UHPC slab, demonstrating its ability

6.4 COMPARATIVE EVALUATION OF LDPM-F AND CTH BALLISTIC SIMULATIONS

The preceding sections have presented simulation results from two fundamentally different computational
frameworks: LDPM-F, a discrete mesoscale fracture model implemented in MARS, and CTH, a
continuum-based Eulerian shock physics code. Each method offers unique modeling capabilities and
perspectives on the ballistic response of UHPC slabs under impact loading. LDPM-F explicitly resolves
the granular heterogeneity and fracture processes at the mesoscale, capturing tensile cracking, fiber
bridging, fragmentation, and post-peak softening. CTH, by contrast, uses homogenized material models
with rate-dependent plasticity and damage laws, making it well suited for simulating high-strain-rate
phenomena like shock propagation and projectile failure over large domains.

Despite these foundational differences, both models produce broadly consistent trends when applied to
UHPC slabs impacted by ballistic projectiles. However, LDPM-F provides more detailed insight into
internal damage mechanisms, such as crack initiation, fiber pull-out, and spall formation, whereas CTH
excels at capturing continuum-level deformation, projectile erosion, and shock interaction effects. This
comparison is particularly important in the context of model calibration and validation. As described in
Section 3 of this report, a comprehensive set of experimental ballistic tests was conducted using a range
of projectile types and UHPC slab configurations. These physical tests provide key benchmark data, such
as penetration depths, spall cone geometries, and damage patterns, which are used to tune both LDPM-F
and CTH material parameters. LDPM-F is calibrated against experimental data by adjusting
micromechanical parameters such as fiber bond strength, fracture energy, and strain-rate sensitivity to
reproduce the observed damage modes and projectile arrest conditions. CTH, on the other hand, uses this
same data to calibrate its damage evolution laws, failure criteria, and equation-of-state parameters for
UHPC. Both models aim to replicate not only final penetration depths but also dynamic features such as
energy dissipation rates and crack propagation velocity.

A deliberate cross-validation strategy is being applied, in which the same projectile-slab configurations
are modeled using both LDPM-F and CTH. By comparing simulation outcomes across these two
modeling paradigms and aligning them with experimental results, it becomes possible to identify
strengths and limitations in each framework. For instance, if LDPM-F over-predicts crack width while
CTH underestimates penetration depth, this triangulation points to specific material parameters or
modeling assumptions that require refinement. The dual-model approach also ensures robustness and
improves confidence in the predictive accuracy of simulations used for UHPC protective system design.
LDPM-F offers detailed resolution of fracture processes critical for forensic interpretation and
microstructural design, while CTH provides efficient scalability and the ability to simulate full-system
responses under varied impact scenarios.

Ultimately, the integration of experimental validation, cross-platform simulation, and iterative calibration
forms a comprehensive methodology for advancing the ballistic design of UHPC systems. This approach
enables not only high-fidelity prediction of failure modes but also informed engineering decisions on
material selection, slab thickness, fiber volume fraction, and reinforcement detailing for both structural
integrity and ballistic resistance.

For the nuclear transport industry, the modeling methodology presented herein offers a reproducible,

7.1.1 Design and Construction

The standalone UHPC-reinforced container package consists of a corrugated steel ISO container shell
coated with ~0.75″ of high-strength fiber-reinforced concrete on both sides.. This essentially forms a
steel–UHPC composite sandwich, which significantly stiffens the container walls and improves impact
resistance. In simulations of drop impacts, UHPC-based waste containers have shown significantly less
damage and stress than traditional concrete/steel designs [21,22]. In one study, a UHPC container
suffered the least damage and stress concentration under 9 m drop scenarios, whereas a
conventional steel-lined concrete container showed high damage at the lid-body interface, and thus
risking lid-sealing failure [21,22]. The fiber reinforcement in UHPC helps control cracking and maintain
structural cohesion even when the concrete matrix is stressed or cracked, thus improving post-impact
integrity.

7.1.2 Accident Impact Resistance

Standard Type B packages are engineered to survive severe mechanical abuse. IAEA SSR-6 requires that
Type B packages withstand a 9 m free drop onto an unyielding surface and a 1 m puncture drop onto a
steel bar, sequentially, without loss of containment [20]. Traditional Type B casks achieve this typically
via thick forged steel shells or ductile cast iron bodies, shock-absorbing impact limiters (e.g. wood or
foam-filled end caps), and secure lid locking systems. These casks can deform plastically under impact
but are not breached. For instance, a spent fuel transport cask with impact limiters attached can weigh
~100 tons and is designed to maintain containment after such drops [23,79].

 72

By comparison, the standalone UHPC-reinforced ISO container package would rely on the composite
action of the steel shell and UHPC layers to absorb impact energy. The high strength of the UHPC and
the ductility of the steel could effectively distribute impact forces and prevent catastrophic tearing. The
thin (i.e., 0.75 in) exterior UHPC might serve as a sacrificial crush layer to protect the inner steel wall,
while the interior UHPC could shield and support the contents. However, the standard shipping container
geometry (large flat panels and corners) is less inherently impact-resistant than a cylindrical cask. Severe
drops could cause buckling of steel panels or cracking of the UHPC. The UHPC shell would need to be
carefully engineered (possibly with additional reinforcement ribs or bonded rebar) to ensure it does not
delaminate from the steel under shock. Notably, UHPC can provide higher specific energy absorption
than ordinary concrete; one analysis found a UHPC container design was 1.35–1.75 times stiffer than a
conventional steel–concrete container while reducing the weight by over 60%, indicating improved
impact performance for a given mass [21,22]. Still, to fully meet Type B structural criteria, the UHPC
container may require external impact limiters or crushable attachments on corners (much like cask
impact caps) to manage the 9 m drop deceleration forces.

7.1.3 Containment and Integrity

A critical aspect of structural integrity is maintaining containment of radioactive material. Type B
packages include a defined containment boundary (e.g., a sealed inner vessel). For the standalone UHPC
ISO package, the containment boundary must be explicitly established, either (i) the reactor’s qualified
primary vessel if demonstrated to meet containment requirements under 10 CFR 71/IAEA SSR-6, or (ii) a
package-provided inner liner/enclosure engineered and tested as the containment system. This boundary
must remain leak-tight under the regulatory normal and accident conditions. Under impact, that inner
containment must remain leak-tight. The UHPC and steel shell’s role is to prevent breach of this inner
vessel or to block any dispersal of contents even if some internal damage occurs. Regulators require that
after accident tests, any leakage from the package be extremely limited [20]. The composite UHPC
container must maintain its integrity (no large cracks or openings) to comply. Encouragingly, UHPC
designs have shown excellent cohesion under impact; even when cracked, the fiber bridging prevents
pieces from separating [21,22]. In drop simulations, the UHPC container did not experience the through-
wall breaches observed in some conventional concrete designs [21,22]. This suggests that, structurally, a
well-designed UHPC-reinforced container could survive moderate to severe mechanical shocks
comparable to Type B requirements. Nevertheless, extensive physical testing and computer simulations
would be needed to validate this for full-scale containers, given uncertainties in scaling up from small
waste containers to a 40 ft long structure. If fissile material is present, the standalone package must also
demonstrate criticality safety for the defined contents via geometry control and/or fixed neutron absorbers
under both normal and accident conditions.

7.2 RADIOLOGICAL SHIELDING CAPACITY

Type B packages must ensure that radiation levels outside the package stay within regulatory limits under
both normal and accident conditions. Under normal transport, the external dose rate must be low
(typically <2 mSv/h at the surface and <0.1 mSv/h at 2 m distance) for non-exclusive use [20,45]. Under
hypothetical accident conditions, a Type B package must retain enough shielding that the dose rate at 1 m
from the package does not exceed 10 mSv/h [20]. Standard B(U)/B(M) casks achieve this via materials
like lead, tungsten, or thick steel walls which attenuate gamma radiation, and hydrogenous materials
(polymers, water, or concrete) with neutron absorbers (e.g. boron) for neutron attenuation. For example,
used fuel transport casks are often constructed with a steel–lead–steel sandwich or monolithic heavy
metal: “Used fuel assemblies are shipped in Type B casks which are shielded with steel, or a combination
of steel and lead [81].” These traditional casks are extremely massive (e.g., >100 tons empty), reflecting
the large thickness of shielding required to safely contain highly radioactive contents [79].

 73

As a standalone Type B–equivalent package, the proposed ~1.5″ total UHPC thickness alone is not
intended for high-activity source terms; either (a) the certified contents must have a low radiation source
term/self-shielding adequate to meet dose limits, or (b) additional integrated shielding features must be
incorporated into the package design to satisfy SSR-6/10 CFR 71 dose-rate criteria under normal and
accident conditions.

UHPC is a form of high-density concrete that can be formulated with heavy aggregates or additives to
improve radiation attenuation. Concrete is widely used as a shielding material in storage and disposal
systems because it is inexpensive and effective in bulk [23]. However, the UHPC layer proposed here is
quite thin (i.e., 0.75″ inside, 0.75″ outside, and ~1.5″ total). By itself, a few inches of concrete provides
only limited gamma shielding, which would likely be insufficient for high-activity nuclear materials. For
perspective, spent fuel dry storage casks often use ~12–16 in of ordinary or heavy concrete (in concrete
overpacks) or an equivalent thickness of steel to reduce dose rates to acceptable levels [23]. In the
Canadian dry storage container example, a 21.65 in thick steel-lined concrete wall was needed for
shielding, whereas a 11.811 in UHPC wall gave comparable shielding performance with far less weight
[21–23].

Therefore, to meet Type B shielding standards, the UHPC container concept must incorporate additional
shielding measures or be limited to contents with relatively low radioactivity. If transporting a new (fresh)
microreactor or SMR that has fresh fuel, the radiation source term is relatively low since fresh low-
enriched fuel assemblies emit low levels of gamma (mainly from uranium decay and small impurities).
Thus, fresh uranium fuel assemblies often require no heavy shielding during transport; they are shipped in
specialized packages for mechanical protection and criticality safety, but “have a low radioactivity level
and radiation shielding is not necessary [79].” In such a scenario, a UHPC-lined container might be
sufficient, as the steel plus thin layer of concrete could attenuate what little radiation exists to very low
levels. The UHPC would primarily serve structural and minor shielding roles, with the fuel’s own
cladding and any built-in reactor shielding containing most radiation.

Another consideration is neutron radiation (e.g., from any plutonium or actinides in the fuel, or from a
recently shut down core). Traditional casks often include neutron shielding such as borated polyethylene
or resins in an annular layer. Concrete (including UHPC) contains hydrogen (as water of hydration) and
can moderate neutrons to some extent, and boron can be added to capture thermalized neutrons. UHPC’s
low porosity means it has less free water than ordinary concrete, which might reduce its hydrogen content
for neutron moderation. If neutron emitters are present, the design may require an inner thermal-neutron
absorber (like a borated polymer lining or boron additives in the UHPC mix) to ensure neutron dose rates
stay low.

In summary, for high-radiation payloads the UHPC container is at a disadvantage compared to purpose-
built casks, which are specifically optimized for shielding (using dense metals and dedicated neutron
shields). The UHPC container concept is more feasible for lower-activity shipments (fresh fuel or low-
level waste) or if the reactor has substantial self-shielding. It must be demonstrated that the container’s
shielding (plus any supplemental layers) can maintain dose rates <2 mSv/h at the surface during normal
transport and <10 mSv/h at 1 m after an accident, as required [20]. Otherwise, it would not receive Type
B certification for high-activity content.

7.3 THERMAL PERFORMANCE

7.3.1 Heat Dissipation Under Normal Conditions

During routine transport, packages may generate heat either from radioactive decay (e.g. decay heat in
spent fuel) or from ambient environmental conditions (e.g., sunlight, ambient temperature). Regulations
limit the temperature of accessible surfaces (i.e., no more than 50°C in still air 38°C ambient (or 85°C if
shipped under exclusive use with precautions) to prevent burns and material degradation [82,83]. A
package must be designed so that any internal heat generation does not compromise its integrity or cause
excessive external temperatures [83]. In a standard Type B cask, thermal management is often achieved
by metal construction (e.g., steel shells that conduct heat outwards) and external cooling fins or spacing to
allow convection. For example, a spent fuel cask with a 36 kW heat load uses a thick metal structure to
conduct heat to its surface, where fins dissipate it to air [79].

If transporting a fresh (i.e., unirradiated) microreactor, decay heat is minimal (fresh fuel produces
negligible heat). In that case, both the UHPC container and a standard cask would essentially just have to
handle ambient heat. The UHPC’s thermal conductivity is lower than a standard metal type B cask; low
thermal conductivity means the UHPC layer acts as insulation. However, during normal operation, this
could trap any heat inside. If a microreactor core were producing even a small amount of decay heat (or
residual heat from prior testing), the insulated container might get hotter internally than a metal cask
would. Careful thermal analysis would be needed; ventilation or heat sinks might be required if there is
significant heat. By contrast, metal casks readily conduct heat but can get hot to the touch; they rely on
large thermal mass and surface area to keep external temperatures within limits. A UHPC-coated
container may need design features like heat-conducting paths or forced-air cooling if the payload has a
notable heat output, to ensure compliance with the 50°C surface limit [83].

7.3.2 Fire Hazard

The IAEA and NRC accident standard includes a fully engulfing 800°C fire for 30 minutes around the
package [79]. This is a severe thermal shock that the package must survive without losing containment or
excessive shielding degradation. Standard Type B packages generally have thick steel (which has a high
heat capacity and can absorb heat for 30 minutes without the inner contents reaching failure temperature)
or they incorporate thermal barriers (e.g. insulation layers or intumescent coatings). Lead-shielded casks
must be designed so the lead does not melt and leak out; the lead is usually encased and the cask’s
thermal analysis ensures the lead temperature stays below ~327°C during the 30 min fire. Some casks use
concrete or resin as part of their structure; those materials can experience spalling or decomposition in
extreme heat, so they are protected by outer shells and sometimes sacrificial layers.

The UHPC-reinforced container has some inherent advantages in fire conditions. First, concrete has a
high heat capacity and low thermal conductivity, meaning the UHPC shell would heat up slowly and
transmit heat inward at a slow rate. Studies have shown that UHPC containers experience lower thermal
stresses compared to steel-lined concrete designs when exposed to 800°C fires [23]. In a simulation, a
11.81 in thick UHPC container maintained integrity better than a 21.65 in concrete-steel container under a
30 minute 800°C exposure, because the steel liner in the latter rapidly conducted heat into the concrete
causing high stress, whereas UHPC’s low conductivity kept the heat more localized [23]. This suggests
that the UHPC coating on a container could act as an effective thermal insulator, protecting the inner
contents (and the steel structure) from the peak fire temperatures. The steel container’s mechanical
strength will degrade at high temperatures (carbon steel loses much of its yield strength above ~500°C),
but the UHPC layer could keep the steel below critical temperature if it insulates well for 30 minutes. The
inside UHPC layer further protects the payload by delaying heat transfer.

 75

One challenge for concrete in fire is explosive spalling; this occurs when moisture inside the concrete
turns to steam and causes the concrete to crack or burst. Since UHPC is relatively dense, it is actually
more prone to spalling if not properly formulated, because its low permeability traps steam pressure. To
mitigate this, engineers incorporate polypropylene fibers in UHPC mixes for fire resistance [25]. At high
temperature (i.e., above 170°C), polypropylene fibers melt and leave tiny channels, releasing steam
pressure and thereby reducing spalling risk [84]. It is documented that adding a small fraction (e.g. 0.5–
1% by volume) of polypropylene fibers in UHPC can effectively prevent explosive spalling under fire
conditions [84]. With these measures, the UHPC layers are expected to remain largely intact through the
30 minute fire. The outermost surface might experience some cracking or charring, but the bulk of the
material should not crumble off.

Thus, in a regulatory fire, the UHPC-coated container might perform as well as or better than a metal cask
in terms of preserving shielding and containment. The concrete’s insulation means the inner reactor or
fuel would heat up more slowly. The container would need to be evaluated for thermal stress. Studies
have shown that thermal stresses were actually lower in the monolithic UHPC design than in the steel-
lined one under the same fire, because steel liners caused uneven heating [23]. This bodes well for the
UHPC container’s structural integrity in a fire. Importantly, any elastomer seals (e.g., rubber gaskets on
container doors) would not survive 800°C, so the design likely would require metal seals or welds for
containment if using container doors. A Type B cask usually has double metallic O-ring seals on the lid
that can withstand high temperatures. The UHPC container may need to weld the door shut or have a
bolted hatch with metal seals for certification, since a standard container door gasket would burn away
and potentially allow leakage.

In summary, the thermal performance of the UHPC-reinforced container can be robust if properly
designed. The UHPC provides a fireproof shield that protects the structure and contents, and the addition
of polypropylene fibers can mitigate spalling [25]. The standard Type B cask also survives the fire by
virtue of thermal mass and engineering. Overall, neither design would allow fuel to be damaged or
released.

For the proposed 1.5-inch UHPC thickness design in this study, differences in thermal performance must
be carefully evaluated. While the thicker UHPC layers discussed earlier (e.g., 11.81 inches tested in
previous simulations [23]) demonstrated robust fire performance compared to conventional concrete-steel
designs, a thinner UHPC layer will exhibit faster heat transfer and potentially higher thermal stresses due
to the reduced thermal mass and insulating capacity. Specifically, the UHPC layer in this study may heat
up at a faster rate under the 800°C 30-minute IAEA/NRC fire simulation, which could increase the risk of
spalling and elevate the inner contents' temperature. However, the inclusion of polypropylene fibers to
mitigate spalling and the UHPC's inherently low thermal conductivity remain beneficial in slowing heat
transmission compared to metallic casks.

The UHPC container must prove that after the fire (and preceding drop tests), it still meets containment
and shielding requirements (e.g., no gross cracking that compromises radiation shielding or sealing). It is
critical to validate this thinner UHPC design through fire testing and associated thermal stress simulations
to ensure it meets containment and shielding requirements after exposure to the regulatory fire. Moreover,
given the reduced thickness, additional design features, such as an inner thermal barrier or external
sacrificial layers, may be necessary to preserve the integrity of the payload during the fire. Design
considerations must also address the potential challenge of heat removal from internal sources (e.g., a hot
microreactor payload), which may be less efficient in a thinner UHPC container than in a metallic cask
due to UHPC’s insulating properties.

Given UHPC’s fire-resistance (e.g., no melting, gradual heat transfer), it could have an edge in keeping
internal temperatures low during the 30 minute blaze [23]. On the other hand, any heat generated inside

 76

(e.g., from a hot payload) would be harder to remove in the UHPC container than in a metallic cask; thus,
this aspect needs careful management in design.

7.4 WEIGHT AND PAYLOAD CAPACITY

A major practical difference between a UHPC-reinforced ISO container and a traditional Type B cask is
in weight and payload fraction. Conventional Type B casks are extremely heavy relative to their payload.
They often have a payload (fuel or contents) of only a few tons, enclosed by a cask weighing tens of tons.
For example, a large spent fuel cask might carry ~10–20 tons of fuel but weigh approximately 100 tons
empty and 110–125 tons loaded [79,80]. This approximately 5:1 weight-to-payload ratio is due to the
thick shielding and structural material needed to meet safety requirements. Smaller Type B packages
(e.g., those for medical or industrial sources) weigh less overall, but even those tend to heavily outweigh
their radioactive contents. This high mass impacts transportation: heavy casks require special trucks
(multi-axle trailers), railcars, or ships for movement, and cannot be handled by standard freight
equipment. As a result, Type B(U) casks for spent fuel and reactors are generally transported as
oversize/overweight loads, with route surveys and often police escorts (due to radiological security and
weight). The logistics are complex and costly.

Since the UHPC container concept is aimed at leveraging standard intermodal infrastructure, weight is a
critical factor. A standard 40 ft ISO container has a typical empty weight of approximately 8,600 lb and is
rated for a maximum gross weight of 79,000 lb under ISO standards [47]. If UHPC layers are added, the
container gets heavier. A rough estimate shows that a 1.5″ thickness of UHPC over the entire 40 ft
container could weigh approximately 27,000 lb by itself (assuming 150 lb/ft³ density). Thus, the modified
container may weigh 35,600 lb empty. This is considerably heavier than a standard ISO shipping
container but still an order of magnitude lighter than a full-size spent fuel cask. With an empty weight of
35,600 lb, the container could still accommodate a payload up to 43,400 lb while remaining near within
the ISO gross weight limit of 79,000 lb. In trucking, the U.S. federal gross vehicle weight limit is 80,000
lb for standard trucks without special permit [83]. Subtracting 35,000 lb for the truck and trailer, that
leaves 45,000 lb for the container and its payload, without a permit; thus, if the UHPC container with the
reactor inside can be kept around 45,000 lb, it can ship under normal regulations [83]. In practice, a
heavier load (e.g., 80,000 lb total) would require overweight permits and possibly additional axles on the
trailer, but this is a routine process for heavy industrial equipment. Many states allow permitted loads well
above 80k lb (e.g., 100k–150k+ lb) with proper arrangements [83]. The UHPC microreactor shipping
container, at worst, would fall into this heavy-haul category; this is still far easier to transport than a 100-
ton cask that might need a specialized transporter or rail.

If the UHPC container is kept at or under ~35 ton gross, it could be handled by standard container cranes,
reach-stackers, and container ships. Notably, the ISO corner fittings and frame would need to remain
intact and certified – adding heavy concrete might necessitate reinforcing the corner posts to handle the
extra static and dynamic loads, especially if stacked. For safety, it might be shipped as a single unit (i.e.,
not stacked under or over other containers). On a container ship, overweight containers are sometimes
accepted but with restrictions (e.g. stowage on bottom tiers and limited stacking above). The advantage of
being an ISO-format package is significant: it can be secured on standard chassis, rail well-cars, or
container vessel slots without bespoke handling gear. In contrast, a Type B cask shipment typically
requires a flatbed or heavy-haul trailer with a cradle, and transfer between modes (e.g., truck to rail) often
needs cranes or gantry systems. The UHPC ISO shipping container would simplify intermodal transfer
and could potentially go directly onto a train or ship, reducing handling steps; this translates to logistical
flexibility and cost savings.

Overall, the UHPC container offers a much better payload-to-tare ratio than massive casks; more of the
weight is payload rather than packaging. This is beneficial up to the limits of infrastructure. As an

 77

example, a 60,000 lb microreactor may have a total package weight of 100,000 lb; this exceeds standard
road limits but is within typical oversize haul range (permits would be required, but not extraordinary
ones) [83]. It would not be trivial to get a 100k lb container onto a regular container ship due to exceeding
the ISO weight; such a move might require a break-bulk shipment or special arrangement. However, if the
reactor is at the low end (20,000–40,000 lb), then the container with the reactor inside could likely remain
within standard limits (i.e., ≤ 79,000 lb gross), enabling fully normal containerized shipment. In contrast,
Type B casks for reactors or fuel invariably require special handling as oversized cargo. For example, a
110-ton cask would necessitate heavy-lift cranes and possibly chartered transport vessels. The difference
in logistics footprint is considerable.

7.5 CERTIFICATION AND REGULATORY COMPLIANCE

Transport of nuclear materials is governed by international regulations, most fundamentally the United
Nations “Model Regulations on the Transport of Dangerous Goods” [85]5, from which modal and sector-
specific frameworks are derived, including IAEA SSR-6, “Regulations for the Safe Transport of
Radioactive Material” [20], and compatible national regulations (e.g. U.S. 10 CFR Part 71 [45], Canadian
Packaging and Transport of Nuclear Substances Regulations [86], European Agreement concerning the
International Carriage of Dangerous Goods by Road [87], European Regulations concerning the
International Carriage of Dangerous Goods by Rail [88], etc.). The UHPC ISO container is evaluated here
as a candidate standalone Type B(U)/B(M) package, not as an overpack, and therefore must
independently satisfy the full performance requirements (e.g., structural, thermal, shielding, containment,
and, where applicable, criticality). Within IAEA documentation, Type B(U) vs Type B(M) designations
refer to the approval requirements for Type B packages [20]. In general, Type B packages are specialized
containers designed for the safe transportation of radioactive materials. These packages are designed to
withstand severe accident conditions, ensuring containment of radioactive contents, even under extreme
hazards like impact, fire, or water immersion. Type B packages are typically used for materials with
relatively high radioactivity (e.g., spent nuclear fuel, high-level radioactive waste, and medical radio
nuclides). More specifically, a Type B(U) package requires unilateral approval by the competent authority
in the country of origin, whereas Type B(M) requires multilateral approval (i.e., every country through or
into which the package is transported must authorize it) [82]. In practice, a package is labeled B(M) if it
has certain characteristics outside the bounds for unilateral approval, such as a maximum normal
operating pressure greater than 100 kPa (gauge) or features that necessitate special stipulations for
shipment [82]. Many modern cask designs are Type B(U), meaning that they meet all standard criteria
and only need home country approval. However, if a package has unusual aspects (e.g., requiring
operational controls or very high activity), multilateral (i.e., Type B(M)) approval may be needed.

For the UHPC container concept discussed herein, certification would entail demonstrating compliance to
the same rigorous standards that Type B packages must meet. This includes structural (e.g., the 9 m drop,
1 m puncture, etc.), thermal (e.g., 800°C fire, 30 minutes), immersion (e.g., immersed under 15 m of
water for 8 hours, and for high activity contents, a 200 m deep immersion test to ensure no leakage) tests,
plus evaluations of containment, shielding, and if fissile material is present, criticality safety [20,79]. The
package design must prevent criticality under both normal and accident scenarios; this is usually achieved
by geometry control and/or neutron absorbers [20]. Also, under normal conditions of transport, it must
maintain shielding and containment; to satisfy this requirement, there is a series of lesser tests for “
Normal Conditions” such as a 0.3 m drop, stacking test, rain spray, etc., which any Type B also must pass
[20]. For fissile contents, the standalone package must demonstrate subcriticality with appropriate
margins under normal and hypothetical accident conditions per the applicable regulations.

5 The IAEA SSR-6 transport regulations are developed in alignment with, and as a specialized application of, the United Nations

“Model Regulations on the Transport of Dangerous Goods,” which establish the international framework for all classes of
hazardous material transport.

The UHPC shipping container would need a complete Safety Analysis Report for Packaging or similar
documentation showing, through analysis and testing, that it meets these criteria. This is a non-trivial
hurdle since every material and design feature (e.g., the composite steel-UHPC behavior, the door sealing
method, etc.) must be justified. Regulatory bodies like the U.S. NRC would evaluate whether the
container can contain the radioactive contents under all conditions. Containment assurance is particularly
crucial; presumably the microreactor’s sealed primary system could serve as the containment boundary,
but the package might still need a secondary containment barrier. The container’s door likely requires a
redesign, perhaps welded closure or a bolted lid, to qualify as a leak-tight containment system. Traditional
casks use robust flanges and multiple seal rings machined to high precision; an intermodal container door
was never intended for such tight sealing [89]. Without modification, the UHPC shipping container would
leak in immersion or release vapor in a fire. Thus, an engineering solution like a welded inner liner or a
clamp-down lid in place of the doors would be implemented to satisfy containment leakage rate criteria.

While not strictly mandated by the IAEA, many Type B cask designers follow ASME BPVC Section III,
Division 3 (“NUPACK”) for the containment system [55]. This provides a vetted framework for
designing the cask containment to pressure, fatigue, etc., analogous to nuclear pressure vessels. For a
UHPC container, the outer container itself, if considered part of containment, does not fit the typical mold
of a pressure vessel, but it still must be built under a quality program and have structural analysis per
recognized standards (e.g. ASME BPVC or ACI codes for concrete). Because UHPC is relatively new in
nuclear packaging, there are not established code rules specifically for UHPC transport packages; thus,
the certification will lean on full-scale testing and detailed finite element analysis to validate performance.
The container also should meet ISO 1496 standards for freight containers if it is to be used intermodally,
which implies certain structural tests (e.g., stacking, lifting, racking) not conflicting with its NRC/IAEA
design [46]. Balancing compliance with both ISO container standards and nuclear packaging
requirements would be part of the engineering challenge.

A standard Type B(U) package design undergoes review by the competent authority (e.g., NRC in the
U.S.) which issues a Certificate of Compliance if all criteria are met. For international shipments, that
certificate is recognized in other countries or validated by them. If the UHPC shipping container has
unusual features, for instance, it relied on administrative controls or had a pressure >100 kPa due to
possible hydrogen from heat, it might be classified as B(M) needing each country’s approval. Assuming it
is designed to fully comply, it could be a B(U). The timeline and cost to certify a new package design can
be significant (e.g., several years and millions of dollars) because of analysis, physical drop/fire testing,
and iterative reviews. Standard cask designs have many decades of precedent and often borrow from
earlier proven designs, whereas a UHPC container is novel. Regulators may require full-scale drop tests
to be convinced. For example, waste containers in some programs have undergone actual drop and fire
tests to validate calculations [21,22].

Since IAEA regulations are performance-based, they are not prescriptive about materials. Thus, a UHPC-
steel container can be certified if it passes the performance tests and criteria. For example, the Raddecom
UHPC waste containers developed in the United Kingdom were evaluated in an “expert view” for
disposability and presumably for transport classification; they concluded such containers could meet Type
B requirements for certain intermediate-level wastes [90]. This indicates regulatory openness to UHPC as
a material, provided it is demonstrated to work. The UHPC container would be certified to carry a
specific content (e.g. a particular reactor model with defined radionuclide inventory and decay heat). As
such, the certificate will list the allowable contents and any operational controls, such as tie-down
procedures and cooldown time post-shutdown.

In summary, both the UHPC container and standard casks ultimately have to satisfy the same safety
standards (i.e., IAEA SSR-6 [20] and NRC 10 CFR 71 [45]). The conventional casks come with a

 79

pedigree of meeting those standards through conservative, well-tested design features. The UHPC
container would be a new approach, and compliance would hinge on rigorous engineering and perhaps
innovative design features. It would also have to comply with any relevant U.S. Department of
Transportation requirements for freight (e.g., labeling and structural integrity in normal transport).

Overall, there is no fundamental regulatory barrier to using a UHPC-reinforced container as a standalone
Type B package, provided it passes the required performance tests and obtains regulatory approval. It
should be emphasized that this assessment focuses primarily on structural, shielding, and thermal aspects;
additional considerations such as containment assurance and criticality safety for fissile nuclear materials
would require dedicated analysis and potentially supplementary design features. As with any novel
package concept, the certification process would be extensive, and collaboration with advanced nuclear
materials packaging experts (e.g., those investigating next-generation concepts under 10 CFR 71) could
help identify appropriate testing pathways for UHPC-based systems.. Once certified, it would carry a
unique identification marking and must be fabricated under an approved quality assurance program. In
operation, each shipment would require compliance with IAEA marking and labeling (e.g., the package
would be marked “Type B(U)”, it would bear the trefoil labels and “Radioactive II/III” placards, as
appropriate [20]). The same security and oversight measures (e.g., drivers with radiation and emergency
response training) apply as they do to regular cask shipments.

7.6 COST CONSIDERATIONS

One motivation for exploring UHPC reinforcement is cost reduction. Traditional Type B casks are very
expensive; a single spent fuel transport cask can cost on the order of several million USD to manufacture,
owing to thick forged steel sections, precision machining, exotic materials (e.g., neutron absorbers and
multi-layer seals), and a limited number of suppliers. The design and licensing costs are also high, but
spread over multiple units if a design is produced in quantity. In contrast, a UHPC-reinforced container
could leverage much cheaper materials and fabrication methods. UHPC is essentially concrete with steel
fiber reinforcement; while UHPC mix is more expensive than normal concrete per cubic yard, it is still far
cheaper per volume than stainless steel or lead. The standard corrugated steel ISO container is a mass-
produced commodity item and a new 40 ft container is only a few thousand dollars. The process of
spraying or casting UHPC onto a container might be done in a simple manufacturing facility (or even
onsite at a reactor factory) without specialized foundry or forge. The Raddecom UHPC container was
explicitly noted as a “low cost, scalable and flexible package” for radioactive waste [90]. By reducing the
reliance on heavy forgings and using mostly concrete technology, they achieved notable cost savings for
waste packages.

While exact cost figures are not publicly available, one can infer that concrete shielding-based packages
have historically been cheaper than metal casks. As a magnitude check, UK analyses often assume a
concrete shielded box costs about one-quarter of an equivalent ductile cast iron container, while
individual dry cask systems used for spent fuel storage and transport can cost ≥$1 million per cask
[91,92]. For example, many intermediate-level waste containers are just concrete boxes (with steel rebar
and maybe a steel liner) because it is far more economical for one-time use than a thick lead cask. UHPC
containers could be similarly cost-efficient – even if each container costs tens of thousands of dollars to
produce, that is still a small fraction of a multi-million-dollar cask. The fibers and admixtures in UHPC
add cost, but given the thin sections, the total material volume is relatively small (e.g., approximately 150
ft³ of UHPC in the proposed design), which might cost a few thousand dollars per ft³ at most. If the
concrete cost for the proposed design is under $20k, the ISO container cost is ~$5k, and labor/QA may
add another tens of thousands, the total cost would likely be well under $0.5M per unit in manufacturing
costs, potentially even <$0.1M, depending on features.

 80

Standard Type B casks are built to be reusable assets over decades. Their relatively high upfront cost is
mitigated by the ability to perform dozens or hundreds of shipments. They are robust to repeated
handling, although they need maintenance of seals and periodic recertification. A UHPC-coated container
could potentially be reused as well, but one might also design it as a semi-disposable package (especially
if the cost per unit is low). For example, if a microreactor is shipped to a site and then remains there for
decades, the container might not need to be recovered and it could serve as a storage or shielding structure
on site or be discarded. If it does need to come back, UHPC’s durability means it should survive storage
and another trip, barring an accident. Operational cost differences include that a cask requires specialized
equipment to load/unload fuel, whereas a UHPC container might allow easier loading (e.g., drive the
reactor in, or crane it in through an open side). The UHPC container could reduce labor costs if it
streamlines the integration of the reactor as cargo.

Transportation cost must also be considered. Heavier and oversize shipments incur higher fees due to
obtaining permits, in addition to requiring escort vehicles and slower modes of transportation. If the
UHPC container allows use of standard container logistics (e.g., designed for trains and ships), it could
sharply cut transport costs. As an illustration, one study noted that transporting a single spent fuel cask by
specialized means can cost on the order of $100k+ per shipment (including security and handling);
whereas sending an equivalent load as ordinary freight in a container could be much cheaper [93]. If the
UHPC container can be handled like regular freight, it might avoid expensive chartered shipments. That
said, given the hazardous nature, many of the transport cost components (e.g., security, insurance,
regulatory compliance) will still apply. It is unlikely one would put a loaded nuclear reactor container on
a public container ship without special arrangements. In practice, even if physically possible, the nuclear
industry tends to use dedicated transport or at least dedicated sections, for example, ships adhering to the
INF Code for irradiated nuclear fuel [79]. Therefore, the cost savings in transport may not be as large as
using a regular container ship, but there are savings in flexibility and time.

An often overlooked cost is disposal of the packaging at end of life. A steel cask may become radioactive
due to either neutron activation of steel or contamination and then itself becomes radwaste to be disposed
of or decontaminated at a high cost. A concrete container might also become slightly activated or
contaminated. If the UHPC container is treated as disposable, one could entomb it with the reactor,
especially if is going to a repository. UHPC is not as valuable to recover as a multi-million dollar cask, so
one could avoid costly decontamination operations. Alternatively, if reused, it might need periodic
refurbishment (e.g., checking for cracks, reapplying any shielding compound if it chips, etc.). For
localized damage, refurbishment is straightforward and field-executable with a small crew using standard
concrete-repair methods (e.g., inspect, prep, patch with UHPC repair mix, cure, verify bond)no heavy
fabrication is required. Repairs that affect containment, shielding, or lifting points must follow the
package quality assurance and re-inspection criteria.

Overall, the UHPC-reinforced container promises lower unit cost by using inexpensive materials and
existing container infrastructure [90]. It does shift some costs to analysis and testing (i.e., one-time
engineering costs to certify the new design). If that cost is justified by multiple uses or multiple units, it
can be worthwhile. Standard casks have high upfront and manufacturing costs but known performance
and longevity. If microreactors are to be widely deployed, investing in a certified UHPC container design
could yield a fleet of affordable packages. On the other hand, if only a handful of shipments will ever
occur, it might be more economical to use or adapt an existing certified cask design (if one fits the reactor
dimensions) despite the high cost per cask, rather than develop a brand-new package. In the microreactor
context, especially for defense applications, cost and deployability are key, so a simpler UHPC container
solution is attractive.

7.7 MATERIAL COMPOSITION AND DESIGN CONSIDERATIONS

The UHPC-reinforced shipping container is essentially a hybrid material system consisting of thin
corrugated Corten steel walls (typical container steel) combined with UHPC layers containing steel fibers
(and possibly rebar mesh or other reinforcement). The material composition of UHPC usually includes
Portland cement, silica fume, fine sand, high-range water reducer, and steel micro-fibers (~2% by
volume). This yields compressive strengths of 150–200 MPa and notable post-cracking tensile capacity
due to fibers. Compared to conventional concrete, UHPC has higher density and virtually no coarse
aggregate; the mix can be tailored for nuclear use by adding heavy metal aggregates or boron as needed.
The container’s structural frame (e.g., corner posts, rails) is steel, which carries the primary loads during
lifting and stacking. Encasing that steel in UHPC can protect it from corrosion and distribute loads. In
fact, the UHPC container design by Othman and Sabrah (2022) was partly motivated by eliminating the
steel liner plates used in older designs (which added weight and corrosion issues) by using solid UHPC
walls instead [23].

One interesting aspect is radiation effects on materials. Both steel and concrete are both known to tolerate
radiation relatively well at the levels expected in transport (much lower than inside reactors). The UHPC’
s cement matrix could undergo some neutron and gamma exposure without significant loss of strength,
especially for short transport durations. Concrete used in reactor biological shields and spent fuel storage
has performed adequately over decades, though very high neutron fluences can cause damage. For
transport, this is negligible. The steel fibers in UHPC might get activated if exposed to high neutron flux,
but again minor for transport. The composite nature also means that thermal expansion differences must
be managed since steel and concrete expand at different rates; normally this can cause internal stresses,
but UHPC’s bond to steel (if applied correctly) and fiber content might accommodate some differential
strain. The design likely would account for that with expansion gaps or choosing a container steel that has
a compatible expansion coefficient.

Standard Type B(U) casks often use austenitic stainless steel, for corrosion resistance and toughness, or
ductile cast iron. Lead is a common shielding material in older casks, though modern designs sometimes
avoid lead in favor of solid steel or tungsten, especially in smaller packages, to handle higher decay heats
without melting concerns. Neutron shielding material in casks is often provided by a polymer or resin,
which can degrade under high temperatures, so sometimes it is placed outside the containment boundary.
There are also casks that use concrete as a material. Overall, the palette of materials in standard casks can
be quite varied, including metals (e.g., steel, lead, aluminum for fins), polymers, wood, and sometimes
concrete. Furthermore, each material is placed optimally for its role (e.g. wood in impact limiter crushes
to absorb energy, lead for gamma shielding, etc.). These casks are the result of many design iterations to
satisfy conflicting requirements of strength, shielding, weight, and heat removal.

By contrast, the UHPC container tries to achieve a more uniform solution: using UHPC for both structural
reinforcement and radiation shielding simultaneously. This simplification means each material is
performing double duty because the concrete provides structure and shielding while the steel provides
structure and some minor shielding. The composition is thus simpler, which could reduce points of
failure. It consists of all inorganic, fireproof materials except for maybe rubber door seals. This inherent
fireproof nature is an advantage of the material set.

UHPC is known for exceptional durability in civil applications because of its low permeability and high
strength. It resists water ingress, chloride penetration, and should do well against weathering. If a
container is stored outside or transported by sea, the UHPC cover would protect the underlying steel from
rust, especially if the exterior UHPC is properly cured and perhaps coated/sealed to prevent cracking.
Standard ISO containers are painted and can corrode over time; here the concrete could serve as a
permanent protective layer. One must ensure the bond between UHPC and steel does not allow moisture

 82

creep and hidden interfaces to corrode. For the interior, UHPC is compatible with high temperatures and
radiation, so it could handle any residual heat or radiation from the reactor better than many polymers or
paints.

The material choices also affect how the reactor is integrated. A steel cask typically bolts onto a reactor
vessel or has a basket structure; in a UHPC container, one might include a structural steel cradle or frame
inside to secure the reactor. That cradle would transmit forces to the container walls, rather than relying
on the thin corrugated walls. The UHPC coating on walls increases stiffness, but the primary load-bearing
might still be through the steel frame of the container

Material composition will matter when the package is decommissioned. The UHPC can be difficult to cut
or melt, so disposing of a contaminated UHPC container might involve size-reducing the concrete (e.g.,
using a concrete saw, which will generate dust needing radiological controls). Steel casks can be melted
down (if low contamination) or stored as waste. Neither situation is trivial, but since concrete is generally
acceptable in radwaste disposal and provides radiation shielding even as waste, one could directly entomb
a contaminated UHPC container in a repository without processing; it effectively serves as its own waste
package. This aligns well if the reactor itself is going to a repository; one might leave it inside the UHPC
container as an overpack.

On a practical note, UHPC materials are commercially available and several vendors provide premixed
UHPC or the components to mix it. The fiber reinforcement is commodity steel fiber. Thus,
manufacturing can be done in many locations and there is no need for a specialized forge or foundry. This
decentralization can reduce lead times. For example, if the military needed to deploy a microreactor, they
could manufacture the UHPC container in a standard precast concrete plant or construction yard with the
right molds, rather than waiting for a large cask to be built by a limited supplier. This could be a strategic
advantage.

7.8 OPERATIONAL AND LOGISTICAL CONSIDERATIONS

Beyond the technical criteria, there are practical operational factors in using a UHPC-reinforced container
versus a standard Type B package, as described in the following sections.

7.8.1 Handling and Infrastructure

As mentioned, the UHPC container (if certified as a standalone Type B package and within weight limits)
can utilize conventional handling equipment (e.g., container cranes, reach stackers, etc.) if within weight
limits. This could allow loading onto ships or trains that are not exclusively for nuclear cargo. However,
in reality, high-activity material transport often uses dedicated vehicles or vessels for security and safety
[79]. Even if the container fits on a normal ship, regulators and carriers may insist on special
arrangements (e.g. a ship for sea transport of a reactor). For road transport, a container can be carried on a
standard container chassis trailer. A heavy cask, in contrast, often needs a low-boy trailer with a custom
cradle and sometimes the trailer has to be specially designed for weight distribution. Using a container
could simplify last-mile delivery (e.g., a container can be offloaded at a port and put on a train or truck
easily).

7.8.2 Deployment and Unloading

With a UHPC-reinforced ISO shipping container, the reactor can potentially be installed or removed more
easily once the package is certified and operated per its approved procedures. The large side doors (or an
openable side panel) could allow rolling or lifting the reactor module out at the destination. A cask
usually requires heavy cranes at both load and unload ends to vertically lift the payload out. If the

 83

microreactor is skidded into a container horizontally, it might be deployed without a giant crane, perhaps
using ramps or on-board winches, which suits austere environments. On the flip side, a container might
not have the same level of internal support structure to guide and secure the reactor compared to a cask’s
engineered basket. Operational procedures to secure the reactor in the container (e.g., welding tie-downs,
adding temporary support cribbing) will be needed.

7.8.3 Security

Both types of packages are subject to strict security measures when carrying nuclear material. Although
required placards/labels will identify Class 7 radioactive material per IAEA SSR-6, an ISO-format UHPC
container reduces conspicuity relative to an exposed cask and integrates with standard intermodal flows;
these are logistical benefits rather than true “security through obscurity.” [20]

Recognizing regulatory and operational sensitivities, a shipment at roughly Category II significance
would employ layered, defense-in-depth measures emphasizing deterrence, detection, delay, and
response. Typical elements include pre-approved routing with restricted layovers; continuous tracking
with geofencing and exception alerts; redundant two-way communications and duress signaling; tamper-
indicating devices and seal accountability; two-person integrity for access; controlled information
handling (need-to-know, late-release routing); procedures to avoid unscheduled stops; and pre-
coordinated law-enforcement notification or escort, as applicable. These practices align with established
transport security approaches for Class 7 shipments [76] and would apply to a UHPC ISO package once
certified [45].

Beyond logistics and procedures, the package can contribute to forced-entry and ballistic delay. The
UHPC shell hardens door skins and hinge/lock regions, protects structural members from tool access, and
limits spall/penetration, offering meaningful delay relative to a thin-walled ISO container. Design options
(e.g., recessed/armored locking mechanisms, welded or bolted closures with tamper space, internal shear-
resistant tie-downs, and protected lift points) can increase time-to-defeat while remaining compatible with
handling and inspection. Final delay credits would be established through qualification testing and the
security plan at certification [20,45]. See also Section 6 of this report for ballistic/forced-entry
performance insights from simulations.

While both a conventional Type B cask and the UHPC ISO concept must meet the same Class 7 security
requirements (including placarding) and employ defense-in-depth, their form factors and logistics
footprints drive differences in how deterrence, detection, delay, and response are implemented. Key
security-relevant differences include:

  Signature and conspicuity
 Conventional casks are visually distinctive (large cylindrical body, impact limiters, lift trunnions)
 and typically moved on dedicated equipment; they draw attention but align with mature
 escort/response doctrines. The UHPC ISO package blends into freight streams (reduced
 conspicuity), but placarding/marking still declares Class 7 radioactive material. The benefit is
 primarily logistical (e.g., intermodal compatibility), not concealment [20,76].

  Closures and access points
 Type B casks use a small number of engineered closures (e.g., bolted, gasketed metallic lids with
 tamper-evident seals), simplifying access control and inspection. An ISO container has large
 doors and more seams; the UHPC ISO concept therefore requires hardened, purpose-built
 closures (e.g., armored/recessed locks, welded or bolted hatches with metallic seals, internal
 cross-bars) and defined tamper-evidence provisions to achieve comparable assurance [20,45].

 84

  Physical delay characteristics
 Casks rely on thick metal sections and impact limiters that inherently resist cutting, prying, and
 ballistic attack. The UHPC shell provides robust ballistic/forced-entry delay relative to a thin ISO
 wall, but total delay depends on UHPC thickness, fiber content, and any embedded hard-facing
 (e.g., steel/ceramic strike plates), especially around hinges, locks, and lift points. Security-critical
 zones should be locally hardened and verified by time-to-defeat testing (See Section 6 of this
 report).

  Chain-of-custody and tamper indication
 Casks have established lid-seal accountability, torque verification, and inspection regimes. The
 UHPC ISO package should adopt equivalent controls: high-security tamper-indicating
 devices/bolt seals on closures, instrumented or fiber-optic seals where feasible, defined inspection
 points accessible without breaching the package, and clear acceptance criteria for seal integrity
 during hand-offs [20,45].

  Routing, stops, and laydown
 Cask movements often use dedicated routings, restricted layovers, and purpose-designed secure
 laydown areas. Because the UHPC ISO package can traverse intermodal nodes, the security plan
 must explicitly control pause points (e.g., no unsecured yard storage), define secure stowage
 locations and durations, and specify contingencies for unscheduled stops to preserve delay and
 response posture [76].

  Monitoring and telemetry
 Both forms benefit from continuous tracking and exception-based alerting. The ISO format
 allows straightforward integration of discreet sensors (door status, vibration/shock,
 GPS/geofence, ambient radiation trend) within structural cavities; these should be specified as
 part of the certified monitoring suite and tied to communications redundancy and escalation
 procedures [76].

  Escort and response alignment
 Casks align to long-standing convoy/escort practices. For the UHPC ISO package, equivalent
 deterrence-and-response postures should be defined (consistent with Category II shipments),
 including pre-coordination with jurisdictional law enforcement, communications protocols, and
 arrival/site security interfaces appropriate to intermodal transfers [76].

  Security testing and crediting
 Cask designs have an empirical pedigree for forced-entry resistance and time-to-defeat. The
 UHPC ISO package should plan qualification tests (e.g., ballistic, forced-entry, and tool-attack on
 closures and structural members) to establish security credits and to inform any required local
 hardening or procedural compensatory measures [20,45].

7.8.4 Regulatory Acceptance

Innovative packages can face extra scrutiny from regulators not only for safety but also operational
aspects. For example, authorities will consider tie-down methods and how the package is secured on the
transport vehicle (per 10 CFR 71 [45], the package must be tied down such that forces in an accident do
not compromise it [20]). A container has standard corner castings for tiedown, which are proven in
freight. The NRC also reviews how easy it is to conduct inspections and emergency response. A standard
cask can be rapidly surveyed for radiation or leaks; a container could hide issues (e.g. if something leaked
inside, first responders might not see it until opening). Thus, emergency procedures might differ as
responders are familiar with casks and their closure systems; with a UHPC container, responders might

 85

need training on how to safely open or vent it if needed. However, since the container would be certified,
detailed operating procedures (e.g., loading, unloading, routine tests of seals, etc.) would be part of the
safety documentation and must be followed.

7.8.5 Multimodal Transfers

If shipping internationally, the package may go through ports and customs. A UHPC container could
potentially simplify customs clearance since it fits into intermodal processes. Because the package
remains in a standard ISO 40-ft container with an ISO 6346 identifier, it can be manifested, pre-cleared,
and tracked through the same electronic data interchange and risk-assessment pipelines used for
containerized cargo at container terminals (rather than bespoke break-bulk workflows used for out-of-
gauge casks). The form factor also aligns with established International Maritime Dangerous Goods
Code/Class 7 documentation and container-oriented screening/handling at ports, which reduces
scheduling friction, notwithstanding that Class 7 shipments still require special authorizations and
inspections. But nuclear shipments often bypass typical customs anyway and have special clearances. For
rail transport, heavy casks may require special freight cars (with cushioning devices, etc.), whereas a
container could ride on a normal flatcar if within weight limits. An example: Russia’s TUK transport
casks are sometimes carried inside standard cargo containers or frames for ease of handling, which shows
the appeal of containerization even for casks [79].

7.8.6 Environmental Conditions

Shipping containers must endure a range of environments (e.g., vibration, salt spray, etc.). UHPC is
robust against weather, but extreme cold might potentially make the concrete matrix a bit less tough. The
fiber reinforcement helps maintain toughness at low temperatures, which is beneficial if the package is
transported in winter or stored outside. Steel casks at low temperatures have to ensure materials are above
their ductile-brittle transition; austenitic steel is sometimes employed because it remains tough at low
temperatures. UHPC’s performance at low temperatures would need to be validated, but likely it remains
stable. A concern is if any free water is present in pores; however, UHPC’s low porosity means there is
little water to freeze. The container would also have to handle vibrations and shocks from transport.
Concrete can crack under repetitive vibration if not well-supported, so design would include padding or
supports to limit how much the reactor or internals rattle against the walls.

7.8.7 Use Case Specifics

The question context includes shipping whole microreactors or SMRs. For microreactors (typically 1–20
MW thermal outputs, very compact cores), the UHPC container approach seems quite feasible. Some
designs (e.g. Westinghouse’s eVinci) are explicitly designed to be “factory-fabricated and transportable in
shipping containers via rail, barge, and truck [37].” This suggests that industry is already considering
using ISO containers to ship these reactors. It is possible that those designs envision a container mainly as
a convenient enclosure and assume the core is fresh (no significant radiation). If an SMR is larger (say a
NuScale power module or similar, which is much heavier and bigger than a 40 ft container), then this
concept would not apply; those would use custom heavy transport equipment. For moderately sized
SMRs that do fit, the considerations mirror those for microreactors.

7.8.8 Regulatory and Stakeholder Acceptance

The nuclear transport community is conservative. Gaining trust in a UHPC ISO container-based solution
would require transparent demonstration that it is as safe as the traditional methods. Assuming the
technical case is made, one could see regulators approving it, especially as there is precedent for using
concrete packages for waste transport in some contexts. The operational flexibility it offers (e.g., it is

 86

cheaper and potentially quicker to deploy) aligns with goals for advanced reactor deployment. It might
even allow having multiple such containers ready to go, rather than a limited number of casks that have to
be booked and shared.

 8. CONCLUSIONS

The UHPC-reinforced ISO shipping container project represents a significant engineering advance in
nuclear transport technology. The initiative combines material innovation, advanced structural design, and
the flexibility of standardized shipping container infrastructure to address evolving challenges in securely
and safely deploying compact nuclear systems such as microreactors and low-power SMRs. The final
deliverable of this project is a design methodology that systematically enables the development of high-
performance, ballistically rated, UHPC-integrated ISO shipping containers. This methodology, which is
adaptable to diverse payloads and operational contexts, is agnostic to specific reactor systems and serves
as a foundational tool for future optimization and deployment across the nuclear industry.

8.1 STRATEGIC OBJECTIVES AND INDUSTRY ALIGNMENT

The project aligns closely with the mission of the Office of INS within the US DOE, which prioritizes the
prevention of theft and sabotage of nuclear materials and facilities. This work incorporates 2S principles
and directly contributes to INS’s ETS Pillar and INSTAR framework. By integrating structural safety and
physical protection features into a modular and certifiable system, the UHPC shipping container concept
addresses critical transport vulnerabilities for mobile reactor systems and factory-fueled cores.

The design enhances INS’s ability to engage bilateral and multilateral partners by providing tangible
reference models, high-fidelity simulation frameworks, and validated technical artifacts for nuclear
transport security workshops, scenario-based risk analyses, and international guidance development. It
also establishes best practices that can facilitate regulatory harmonization and improve industry adoption
of threat-informed transport architectures. Specifically, this work supports industry stakeholders by
delivering a scalable pathway for integrating secure transport systems into reactor design and licensing
strategies. Modular UHPC container designs, validated for both physical protection and operational
performance, offer commercial vendors critical licensing data, reduce regulatory uncertainty, and provide
transferable solutions that align with export and international safety standards.

8.2 TECHNICAL INNOVATION AND DELIVERABLE DESIGN METHODOLOGY

The core innovation of this project lies in the development and validation of a methodological design
framework for UHPC-enhanced shipping containers capable of addressing high-consequence transport
scenarios and achieving performance-based compliance with international nuclear regulations. This
framework leverages the superior properties of UHPC, such as high compressive strength, tensile capacity
due to fiber reinforcement, and thermally stable, low-permeability characteristics, to engineer a hardened
shell compatible with ISO container dimensions and handling systems. Key technical achievements of the
design methodology include:

 1. Structural Integration
 Thin UHPC layers are bonded to corrugated steel ISO container walls using mechanical shear
 studs and optimized surface preparation. This configuration creates a steel-UHPC sandwich
 composite that significantly improves flexural rigidity, impact resistance, and multi-threat
 resilience.

 2. Ballistic Resistance Validation
 Iterative live-fire testing using armor-piercing and high-energy projectiles confirmed the ability
 of UHPC slabs to resist full penetration across varied thicknesses (2.5–4.0 inches). Combined
 mesoscale (LDPM-F) and continuum (CTH) simulation models were calibrated to experimental
 results, enabling accurate predictions of penetration depth, damage morphology, and energy
 dissipation for untested threat scenarios.

 90

 3. Load-Bearing Capacity
 Analytical tools rooted in ASME BPVC, ACI 318 and 349, AISC 360, and ISO 1496 were
 employed to assess and optimize the container’s structural performance under bending, shear,
 compression, and dynamic loads induced by lifting or stationary deployment. A parametric design
 study identified the minimum UHPC thickness required to maintain code-defined safety margins
 with various rail and cross-member configurations.

 4. Design Flexibility
 The framework integrates a parametric analysis of UHPC layer thickness, support geometries,
 and allowable stresses under normal and accident conditions, enabling customized solutions
 based on mission-specific payload constraints, threat resistance requirements, and operational
 environments.

 5. Security Integration
 The container introduces an innovative concept of a security-rated enclosure within the ISO
 shipping container class, integrating ballistic resistance and forced-entry delay features. This
 design operationalizes physical protection measures for nuclear systems during transport and
 stationary site phases.

 6. Scalability
 The design process emphasizes modular construction, using spray-applied UHPC methods that
 are compatible with standard container manufacturing facilities and scalable for both small and
 large production runs. This supports rapid deployment while lowering production costs compared
 to bespoke cask designs.

8.3 DEPLOYMENT RELEVANCE

The UHPC container design framework addresses a critical unmet need in nuclear logistics by
systematically enabling the secure transport of compact nuclear reactor systems under realistic conditions.
The methodology supports multiple operational milestones:

 1. End-to-End Transport
 The framework facilitates reliable intermodal logistics (truck, rail, maritime) for reactor
 deployments to remote or austere locations. It directly supports transport scenarios for modular,
 factory-fueled microreactor for off-grid power generation.

 2. Physical Protection During Transit
 The UHPC container reduces vulnerabilities associated with shipping pre-fueled reactors by
 providing a tamper-resistant, impact-tolerant platform that minimizes exposure to theft or
 sabotage risks. For advanced reactor vendors seeking secure transport solutions, the methodology
 provides validated design data that can simplify licensing and compliance pathways.

 3. Onsite Versatility
 A deployed UHPC container functions not only as a protective transport enclosure but also as
 shielding or an overpack structure during stationary site operation. It can provide ballistic delay
 and containment assurance against unauthorized access or sabotage scenarios.

 91

 4. Global Regulatory Alignment
 The container design satisfies international transport regulations for radiation shielding, structural
 resilience, and accident survivability as outlined in IAEA SSR-6 and U.S. 10 CFR Part 71. Its
 adherence to ISO weight and dimensional constraints further ensures compatibility with global
 intermodal shipping infrastructure.

 5. Technological Exchange
 INS can leverage the design framework to inform joint scenario planning, collaborative system
 evaluations, and regulatory technical assistance efforts, ensuring international stakeholders adopt
 high standards for nuclear transport security.

8.4 HIGHLIGHTS OF STRUCTURAL AND BALLISTIC PERFORMANCE

The validated UHPC container prototype emphasizes structural integrity across multiple scenarios,
ensuring safe deployment even under extreme loading conditions. The container mitigates key
vulnerabilities through high-performance material configurations and innovative structural reinforcement
strategies:

 1. Structural Robustness
 The container’s composite steel-UHPC structure was tested for geometric integration, load-path
 distribution, and code compliance under stress conditions. Live-fire experiments and validated
 simulations reveal strong ballistic resistance, sustained post-cracking toughness, and energy
 absorption mechanisms using fiber-reinforced UHPC slabs.

 2. Ballistic Resistance
 High-fidelity ballistic simulations demonstrated the container’s ability to resist penetration from
 armor-piercing projectiles, preserving containment and shielding integrity. Practical performance
 thresholds were defined for UHPC thickness and configuration, ensuring compatibility with ISO
 weight limits while maintaining passive security features.

 3. Fire and Environmental Resilience
 UHPC’s inherent fire resistance and low permeability provide an integrated solution for
 protecting nuclear contents under regulatory thermal scenarios, including the 800°C, 30-minute
 fire endurance required for Type B packages.

8.5 FUTURE EXTENSIONS

Several future enhancements and research directions are recommended to expand the design framework’s
applicability and performance envelope. These include:

  Extreme and Compound Hazard Analysis
 Expanded simulations for blast loading, vibration-induced fatigue during rail or maritime transit,
 extreme temperature gradients, and accident scenarios such as multi-directional drops or
 submersion.

  Safeguards Integration
 Incorporating tamper-evident seals, embedded sensors, and active/passive monitoring
 technologies into the container architecture to enhance transport security and nuclear material
 accountancy.

 92

  Prototype Scaling and Certification
 Development of a full-scale prototype, followed by robust drop, fire, and immersion tests to
 achieve regulatory certification and maturity for real-world transport applications.

  Dynamic Licensing Case Study
 Refinements to the UHPC design for integration within novel microreactor concepts or advanced
 SMRs with heightened operational demands.

8.6 SUMMARY

The UHPC-reinforced ISO container project establishes a pioneering structural and security solution for
transporting nuclear materials and modular reactor systems. It delivers a comprehensive design
methodology capable of producing high-performance, adaptable shipping containers for diverse nuclear
deployment scenarios. Anchored in physical testing, validated simulations, and parametric optimization,
the methodology ensures robust structural performance, ballistic resistance, and regulatory compliance.

The final deliverable is a reproducible framework for tailoring UHPC-based container systems to specific
missions while preserving versatility, cost efficiency, and scalability for industry-wide adoption. This
design process provides stakeholders across the nuclear enterprise, including regulators, vendors, and
international partners, with the tools and confidence required to achieve next-generation transport
security, advancing the safe, secure, and efficient deployment of emerging reactor technologies.
