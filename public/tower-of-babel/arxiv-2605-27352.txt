From Scores to Gibbs Correctors: Accelerating Uniform-Rate Discrete Diffusion Models
Yuchen Liang, Ness Shroff, Yingbin Liang

Abstract
Discrete diffusion models have achieved strong empirical performance in text and other symbolic domains, but, especially for uniform-rate models, they often require many steps to generate a single sample. Existing acceleration methods either rely on training additional quantities or suffer from slow mixing. In this work, we propose a novel Gibbs-based corrector for discrete diffusion models, termed Gibbs-Accelerated Discrete Diffusion (GADD). GADD leverages the structure of the concrete score function to construct Gibbs posterior likelihoods directly, without requiring any additional training beyond standard score estimation. We show that GADD achieves an overall sampling complexity of 𝒪 ⁡ ( polylog ⁡ ( ε − 1 ) ) \mathcal{O}(\mathrm{polylog}(\varepsilon^{-1})) , yielding the first such rate for diffusion-based samplers for uniform-rate discrete diffusion models. We also conduct numerical experiments demonstrating the practical advantages of GADD across synthetic data, zero-shot text sampling, and zero-shot conditional music generation. These results corroborate the theory and show that GADD consistently improves sample quality and wall-clock efficiency over standard baselines, including vanilla Euler methods and CTMC correctors. Beyond this, our theoretical analysis introduces a novel framework for analyzing predictor–corrector methods in discrete diffusion models, which may be of independent interest. Unlike existing approaches that rely on the Girsanov change-of-measure technique, our method is based on an induction argument that tracks error propagation across predictor iterations while accounting for inaccuracies in the corrector updates.

Introduction

Generative modeling is a central problem in deep learning, aiming to learn distributions that closely match observed data and enable high-quality sample generation. In recent years, diffusion models
[
39
,
17
,
4

have emerged as a powerful and flexible generative framework, achieving state-of-the-art performance across a wide range of applications.
Among them, discrete diffusion models operate directly over discrete sample spaces and have proven highly effective for discrete-data domains. They have achieved strong empirical performance in natural language processing
[

, graph generation
[

,
29

[

Despite their empirical success, diffusion models face a major bottleneck in generation efficiency. Unlike classical generative models such as GANs, diffusion models typically require a large number of iterative steps to generate a single sample. A substantial body of work has explored accelerated sampling methods for
continuous
diffusion models, including predictor-corrector schemes
[

[

[

Among the two canonical classes of

diffusion models,

diffusion models (MDMs) exhibit unique structure induced by the masking dynamics: once a token reaches the absorbing masked state it remains fixed thereafter, which facilitates the development of faster samplers through structured unmasking steps. This built-in structure can be exploited to design efficient samplers. For example,
[
46

showed that MDMs admit an analytic characterization of sampling times, requiring at most

[

In contrast, despite the strong language modeling performance of masked diffusion models (MDMs),

showed that uniform-rate models augmented with predictor-corrector methods can outperform both autoregressive (AR) and mask diffusion models in few-step text generation.

Despite promise, accelerating uniform-rate models is challenging and comparatively underexplored, since they lack the salient structure exploited by masking-based designs.
From a theoretical perspective, random-step samplers (e.g., uniformization) can simulate the reverse process exactly up to score-estimation error, but their realized step counts can be unbounded. For deterministic-step samplers, existing total-variation complexity bounds scale only polynomially in

1.1
Our Contributions

In this paper, we propose the
first
uniform-rate discrete-diffusion-based sampler that achieves
\mathcal{O}(\polylog(\varepsilon^{-1}))
convergence rate. Our detailed contribution are as follows.

Algorithmic Design:
Our key contribution is to propose a novel Gibbs-based corrector, the Gibbs-Accelerated Discrete Diffusion (GADD) algorithm, for sampling from uniform-rate discrete diffusion models. Notably, leveraging properties of the existing concrete score function, our GADD algorithm
does not need extra-training
beyond the score functions. Instead, the posterior likelihood required for the Gibbs update can be directly obtained from the score estimator.

Theoretical Analysis:
We theoretically show that GADD achieves an overall sampling complexity of
\mathcal{O}(\polylog(\varepsilon^{-1}))
, improving upon existing diffusion-based sampling methods for uniform-rate discrete diffusion models.
In comparison, all previous samplers only achieve the convergence rate of
\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{poly}(\varepsilon^{-1}))
.
In particular, we show that the diffusion process naturally provides a
warm-start
to apply Gibbs correctors; such a warm-start simultaneously reduces both the Gibbs convergence error (under perfect per-step update) and the error from inaccurate score estimation.

Numerical Experiments:
We further conduct numerical experiments demonstrating the practical advantages of GADD across synthetic data, zero-shot unconditional text sampling, and zero-shot conditional music generation. These results corroborate our theoretical findings and show that GADD achieves superior efficiency and robustness compared to previous approaches. In particular, GADD consistently improves sample quality at the same NFE budget, performs well on challenging spiky target distributions, and yields favorable wall-clock efficiency in realistic discrete-generation settings.

General Framework for Predictor-Corrector Sampling:
Overall, we develop a general framework for analyzing predictor-corrector methods in discrete diffusion models, based on an induction argument that tracks error propagation across iterations while accounting for inaccurate corrector updates. By decomposing the error into initialization, mixing, and estimation components, the framework provides explicit conditions for controlling global error without stronger score accuracy requirements than in vanilla diffusion models. Beyond GADD, it also applies to the classical CTMC corrector
[
6
]
, offering a unified view of its convergence under approximate updates.

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we review both discrete diffusion models and Gibbs samplers, the two major components in the work.

2.1
Discrete Diffusion Sampling

Discrete diffusion models are defined by a forward noising process and a corresponding reverse denoising process involving
discrete
data. The forward process is formulated as a continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC) on the discrete state space
\mathcal{X}=[S]^{d}
, where
d
denotes the number of tokens and each token takes values from a vocabulary of size
S
. Let
x_{0}\in[S]^{d}
denote the initial data sample, distributed according to the probability mass function (p.m.f.)
q_{0}
.
Let
R_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{S^{d}\times S^{d}}
be the (time-dependent) rate matrix of the CTMC. For any two states
x,y\in[S]^{d}
, the entry
R_{t}(x,y)
specifies the instantaneous transition rate from
x
to
y
at time
t
. The conditional distribution of the state at time
t+\Delta t
given the state at time
t
satisfies

q_{t+\Delta t\mid t}(y\mid x)=\mathds{1}\left\{y=x\right\}+R_{t}(x,y)\,\Delta t+o(\Delta t),

(1)

where
\mathds{1}\left\{\cdot\right\}
denotes the indicator function. Validity of the CTMC requires that
R_{t}(x,y)\geq 0
for all
x\neq y
and
\sum_{y}R_{t}(x,y)=0
for all
x
. Among the many choices of
R_{t}
, one typical choice is the “uniform-rate” matrix
[
30
]
. Specifically, the “uniform-rate” is defined as

R_{t}(x,y)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{S}&\text{if}\penalty\ \mathrm{Ham}\left(x,y\right)=1\\
0&\text{if}\penalty\ \mathrm{Ham}\left(x,y\right)\geq 2\end{cases},

where
\mathrm{Ham}\left(x,y\right)
denotes the Hamming distance between
x
and
y
.
With such an
R_{t}
, the tokens would evolve independently and homogeneously for each token
[
6
,
30
]
. Also, one can show that
q_{T}\approx\mathrm{Uniform}([S]^{d})
and the mixing speed is exponentially fast
[
44
]
.

To sample from the diffusion model, one typical approach is to walk through the reverse process, which is defined as the exact time reversal of the forward CTMC with initial distribution equal to
q_{T}

[
6
,
20
]
. By
[
6
]
, the reverse process is itself a CTMC whose transition rate is
\mathchoice{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\displaystyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\displaystyle R\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\textstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\textstyle R\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptstyle R\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle R\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}_{t}(x,y):=R_{t}(y,x)q_{t}(y)/q_{t}(x)
, which depends on the density ratio for all Hamming-distance-1 pairs
(x,y)
, a quantity commonly referred to as the
(concrete) score
. Since this quantity is generally intractable, we estimate it using a neural network, with one popular choice of loss function: the score-entropy loss
[
30
]
.
After we have obtained an approximate score, for practical sampling, we can discretize the continuous-time reverse process and employ approximate sampling methods for CTMC, such as the Euler method
[
30
]
.
More specifically, define the estimated reverse rate as
\hat{R}_{t}(x,y):=R_{t}(y,x)s_{t}(y,x)
.
Upon initializing
x_{t_{N}}\sim\mathrm{Uniform}([S]^{d})
(with
t_{N}=T
), the Euler method is given by, for each
k=N-1,\dots,0
,

x^{i}_{t_{k}}=\begin{cases}a,&\text{w.p.}\penalty\ \hat{R}_{k+1}^{i}(x_{t_{k+1}}^{i},a)(t_{k+1}-t_{k}),\penalty\ \forall a\neq x^{i}_{t_{k+1}}\\
x^{i}_{t_{k+1}},&\text{w.p.}\penalty\ 1+\hat{R}_{k+1}^{i}(x_{t_{k+1}}^{i},x_{t_{k+1}}^{i})(t_{k+1}-t_{k})\end{cases},

(2)

where

\hat{R}_{k+1}^{i}(x^{i},a):=\hat{R}_{t_{k+1}}(x,x^{-i}\oplus_{i}a),\penalty\ \forall a\neq x^{i},\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{and}\penalty\ \hat{R}_{k+1}^{i}(x^{i},x^{i}):=-\sum_{a\neq x^{i}}\hat{R}_{k+1}^{i}(x^{i},a).

2.2
Random-Scan Gibbs Samplers

In the sampling literature, Gibbs sampling is among the most popular methods due to its simplicity, exactness of conditional updates, and ease of implementation. One typical Gibbs samplers is the (single-site) random-scan Gibbs sampler, where an update index is drawn randomly at each step. For a target distribution
\pi
supported on
\mathcal{X}
, define the one-step Markov transition kernel for random-scan Gibbs sampler as

P(x,y):=\begin{cases}w_{i}\pi^{i}(y^{i}|x^{-i})&\text{if}\penalty\ y^{-i}=x^{-i}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

(3)

Here
w_{i}
is some weight function over the indices that sums up to 1.
For random-scan Gibbs samplers, such a kernel enjoys many regularity conditions of Markov operators. First, given its probabilistic nature,
P
is aperiodic. Also, if
\pi(x)>0
over the support,
P
is irreducible. Given these two properties, running such a Markov chain would yield a unique stationary distribution, which can be shown as
\pi
itself.
For a full review of Gibbs samplers (Glauber dynamics), we refer readers to
[
21
]
.

One way to characterize the convergence of such a Gibbs operator is through the Wasserstein-1 distance (associated with the Hamming metric), defined as:

\mathrm{W}_{1}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2}):=\inf_{\Gamma\in\Pi(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})}\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\Gamma}\ \mathrm{Ham}\left(x,y\right),

where
\Gamma(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})
is the set of couplings of
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}
.
Under certain classical assumptions (cf. Theorem 14.6 in
[
21
]
), one can show that

\mathrm{W}_{1}(\nu_{1}P,\nu_{2}P)\leq(1-\rho)\mathrm{W}_{1}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2}).

(4)

Here
\rho
is also called the
spectral gap
, which depends on the structure of the underlying distribution.
The Wasserstein distance is closely related to the total-variation distance, which is a typical metric to characterize convergence in diffusion models (e.g.,
[
27
,
35
]
), as follows (see
[
21
, Proposition 4.7]
):

\mathrm{TV}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})\leq\mathrm{W}_{1}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})\leq d\cdot\mathrm{TV}(\nu_{1},\nu_{2}).

(5)

3.2
Constructing Posterior Likelihood from Score

To utilize the Gibbs corrector for target
q_{t}
(where
t\in[\delta,T]
), one needs to have access to
q_{t}^{i}(\cdot|x^{-i})
for all
t\in[\delta,T]
and
i\in[d]
. Our key observation is that such information is readily available in the score function, which is the density ratio between all Hamming-distance 1 pairs.
Notice that

\textstyle q_{t}^{i}(x^{i}|x^{-i})=\left(\sum_{y^{i}\in[S]}\frac{q_{t}^{i}(y^{i}|x^{-i})}{q_{t}^{i}(x^{i}|x^{-i})}\right)^{-1}=\left(\sum_{y^{i}\in[S]}\frac{q_{t}(x^{-i}\oplus_{i}y^{i})}{q_{t}(x)}\right)^{-1}.

Thus, one example is to use the following estimator:

\textstyle\hat{q}_{t}^{i}(x^{i}|x^{-i})=\left(\sum_{y^{i}\in[S]}s_{t}(x^{-i}\oplus_{i}y^{i},x)\right)^{-1}.

(6)

With a slight abuse of notation, we define
s_{t}(x,x)\equiv 1
(which does not require any estimation).
We have provided more estimator candidates in
Appendix

B
.

To clarify, although the posterior estimator in (
6
) (and also those in
Appendix

B
) requires a summation over
[S]
, this does not necessarily require
S
forward calls to the score model. For example, with the implementation of SEDD, a
single
forward output of the score function is of size
(B,d,S)
(where
B
is the batch size), which contains all the corresponding scores (i.e., density ratios) used to aggregate. Thus, per Gibbs-step, we only need to call the score function
once
to get an estimate of the posterior.

Such a construction enables direct evaluation of the posterior probabilities required by the Gibbs sampler,
which does not require any extra-training and can be directly used for acceleration
. In contrast, previous correctors that employ Metropolis-Hastings algorithms
[
18
]
require an additional estimator for the likelihood ratio between all pairs of
(x,y)
, which is both computation- and memory-intensive given that
x,y\in[S]^{d}
is high dimensional. With the estimator in (
6
), our Gibbs corrector avoids such auxiliary estimation and remains scalable to high-dimensional domains.

3.3
Convergence Analysis of GADD

We first define a few useful notations. Let
p_{T-t_{k},\ell}
denote the sampling distribution at outer-loop step
k=N-1,\dots,0
(with target
q_{t_{k}}
) and at inner-loop step
\ell=1,\dots,L_{k}
, without estimation error.
The following assumptions are needed for the score estimate.

Assumption 1
.

Fix
t_{k}\in[\delta,T]
,
i\in[d]
and
\ell\in[L_{k}]
. Suppose that score estimate satisfies that

\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x^{-i}\sim p^{-i}_{T-t_{k},\ell}\\
x^{i}\sim q_{t_{k}}(\cdot|x^{-i})\end{subarray}}\sum_{y:\mathrm{Ham}\left(x,y\right)=1}R_{t_{k}}(y,x)\left|s_{t_{k}}(y,x)-\frac{q_{t_{k}}(y)}{q_{t_{k}}(x)}\right|\leq\varepsilon_{\text{est}}.

Here we need a
L_{1}
-type score estimation error, which is different from the score-entropy error typically employed in the literature
[
44
,
35
]
. This estimation error arises because our analysis directly controls the total variation distance (see
Lemma

2
), a choice that has also been adopted in prior works
[
6
,
36
]
. Additionally, for technical reasons, we require the expectation to be taken with respect to
p^{-i}_{T-t_{k},\ell}
, which is a slightly perturbed version of the true target distribution
q_{t_{k}}^{-i}
. This condition is, however, likely to be satisfied when using a warmed Gibbs corrector, as is the case for GADD.

Assumption 2
.

Fix
t\in[\delta,T]
. Suppose that
s_{t}(y,x)\in[M^{-1},M]
for all
x
and
y
.

The bound on
s_{t}
is quite typical in prior literature
[
44
,
27
]
. Also, our main convergence result in
Theorem

1
only depends logarithmically on

.

With these assumptions, we are ready for the convergence guarantees of GADD.

\rho_{*}=\Omega(\mathrm{poly}^{-1}(d))
, the total number of sampling steps admits an explicit dependence on the dimension:
N_{\mathrm{total}}=\mathcal{O}\big(\mathrm{poly}(d)\polylog(\varepsilon^{-1})\big)
. This is a substantial improvement over prior acceleration methods with theoretical guarantees
[
36
]
.

On a high level, the diffusion process and the Gibbs sampler benefit from each other in GADD. First, the Gibbs corrector, through its cascading updates, achieves an exponentially decaying error at each outer loop, reducing the total number of required steps from
\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{poly}(\varepsilon^{-1}))
to
\mathcal{O}(\polylog(\varepsilon^{-1}))
. On the other hand, the diffusion process naturally provides a
warm-start
condition that enables the Gibbs sampler to converge more effectively (see
Lemma

1
). This also mitigates estimation errors arising from an inaccurate corrector, as fewer corrector steps are required to reach convergence (see
Lemmas

2
and
3
).

3.4
Proof Sketch of
Theorem

1

Here we provide a proof sketch of
Theorem

1
to describe the idea of our analysis approach.
The full proof is provided in
Appendix

D
.
Overall, the main technical novelty is to involve inaccurate correctors in the analysis. Different from previous predictor-only approaches
[
25
,
35
,
12
]
, here the total convergence error cannot be easily decomposed via Girsanov’s change-of-measure theorem. Rather, the backbone of our approach is an induction argument over the predictor steps.
Such a predictor-corrector analysis can also be extended to other correctors, including the CTMC corrector as we show in
Section

4
(see
Theorem

2
).

Overall, the sources of error include the outer-loop and inner-loop initialization errors, as well as error propagation arising from imperfect score estimation during the inner-loop iterations. Among these, the outer-loop initialization error is straight-forward to derive, with
\mathrm{TV}(p_{\text{init}},q_{T})\lesssim\sqrt{d\log S}\cdot e^{-T/2}
(see (
9
), cf.
[
44
,
36
]
) .
As follows, we focus on the inner-loop errors.

Step 1: Bounding Inner-loop Initialization Error (
Lemma

1
).

Assuming that the previous outer-loop step ends with a small error, our goal is to show that this can be propagated to the start of the corrector steps, yielding a
warm-up
condition. Indeed, such an error can be decomposed as (see (
F.1
))

\mathrm{TV}(p_{T-t_{k},0},q_{t_{k}})\leq\underbrace{\mathrm{TV}(\hat{p}_{T-t_{k+1},L_{k+1}},q_{t_{k+1}})}_{\text{error inherited from previous step}}+\underbrace{\mathrm{TV}(q_{t_{k+1}},q_{t_{k}})}_{\text{error from moving target}}+\underbrace{\mathrm{TV}(\hat{p}_{T-t_{k+1},L_{k+1}},p_{T-t_{k},0})}_{\text{error from predictor step}}.

In the above, the first term is small by our assumption. As long as the step-size vanishes as
t_{k+1}-t_{k}=\kappa=o(1)
, the second and third terms are also small since the true and estimated scores have non-diverging upper-bounds. Here unlike vanilla discrete diffusion samplers, our choice of
\kappa
does not need to scale as
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
, which allows for significantly fewer outer-loop steps.

Step 2: Bounding Inner-loop Estimation Error (
Lemmas

2
,
3
and
4
).

We now turn to the second source of error, which arises from inaccurate correctors. This setting differs from standard analyses of Gibbs sampling, where the target posterior likelihood is typically known (see
Appendix

A
for a survey). To study this effect, we first analyze error propagation in general random-scan Gibbs samplers with target distribution
\pi
(
Lemma

2
); this result may be of independent interest. We then specialize to diffusion-path distributions, using the posterior likelihood estimators in (
6
) or (
8
) (
Lemmas

3
and
4
). In both cases, the error can be upper-bounded provided the estimated scores satisfy suitable lower-bound conditions. Notably, the required form of score estimation closely matches that used in vanilla diffusion models
[
6
,
28
]
.

Step 3: Combining all parts together.
We now combine the previous ingredients to establish the induction framework.
Suppose that at the previous step
t_{k+1}
, we have already shown

\mathrm{TV}(\hat{p}_{T-t_{k+1},L_{k+1}},q_{t_{k+1}})\lesssim L_{k+1}\varepsilon,

where
L_{k+1}
denotes the number of corrector steps in the preceding outer-loop iteration. Then,
Lemma

1
provides an upper bound on the initialization error for the next corrector loop.
We can further decompose the error accumulated within the corrector loop as

\mathrm{TV}(\hat{p}_{T-t_{k},L_{k}},q_{t_{k}})\leq\underbrace{\mathrm{TV}(p_{T-t_{k},L_{k}},q_{t_{k}})}_{\text{error from pure Gibbs sampling}}+\underbrace{\mathrm{TV}(\hat{p}_{T-t_{k},L_{k}},p_{T-t_{k},L_{k}})}_{\text{error from inaccurate Gibbs transitions}}.

Here the second term can be bounded using
Lemma

3
as
\mathrm{TV}(\hat{p}_{T-t_{k},L_{k}},p_{T-t_{k},L_{k}})\lesssim L_{k}M\varepsilon_{\text{est}}
.
Since
\varepsilon_{\text{est}}=O(\varepsilon/M)
, the inductive hypothesis is preserved.
Meanwhile, by standard Gibbs sampling convergence results (cf. (
4
)), in order to achieve
\mathrm{TV}(p_{T-t_{k},L_{k}},q_{t_{k}})\leq\varepsilon
, it suffices to take
L_{k}\lesssim\rho_{t_{k}}^{-1}\log\left(\frac{d^{2}}{\varepsilon}(M+S\delta^{-1})\right)
.
Summing over
k
yields the desired total number of steps. Notably, our analysis introduces no additional hidden factors beyond those arising from pure Gibbs sampling.

Analysis of CTMC Corrector

In this section, we further apply the framework developed in
Section

3.4
to other existing correction schemes for discrete diffusion models, thereby demonstrating the generality of our analysis.
A common class of correctors in the literature is based on CTMC dynamics. Specifically, at outer-loop time
t_{k}
, an additional CTMC with an appropriately chosen correction rate is simulated to target the distribution
q_{t_{k}}
. One example of such a correction rate is given by (cf.
[
6
, Section 4.4]
):

R^{c}_{k}(x,y):=R_{t_{k}}(x,y)+\hat{R}_{t_{k}}(x,y).

(7)

Indeed,
[
6
, Proposition 4]
shows that the stationary distribution of
R^{c}_{k}
is correct. As follows, we analyze the non-asymptotic performance of such a corrector using the framework of
Theorem

1
.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose perfect score estimation and that
2
holds. In order to achieve
\varepsilon
-TV error, the total number of steps needs to satisfy (ignoring log-dependencies)

N_{total}\lesssim\frac{d^{2}M^{2}}{\mu_{*}^{2}\varepsilon^{2}},

where
\mu_{*}:=\inf_{t_{k}\in[\delta,T]}\mu_{t}
is the minimum modified log-Sobolev constant of the rate matrix
R^{c}_{k}
.

The proof of
Theorem

2
is provided in
Appendix

E
. This result readily extends to the broader class of all CTMC correctors with the correct stationary distribution. In particular,
Theorem

2
shows that,
even in the absence of estimation error
, achieving
\varepsilon
-accuracy in TV requires
\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{poly}(\varepsilon^{-1}))
total steps.
Compared to
Theorem

1
, this highlights the advantage of our proposed GADD sampler, which employs Gibbs correctors instead of CTMC-based ones. The proof of
Theorem

2
closely parallels that of
Theorem

1
, relying on the same induction strategy to handle the evolving target distributions and to control the accumulation of corrector errors due to a finite number of corrector steps.

Our analysis also sheds light on why CTMC correctors are inferior to Gibbs-based ones. Although the continuous-time CTMC converges to the target at an exponential rate, practical implementations require
discretizing the continuous-time trajectory
(as in
[
6
,
14
]
). This discretization introduces a non-negligible error that significantly slows convergence, leading to an overall complexity of only
\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{poly}(\varepsilon^{-1}))
(see

5
).

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we have developed an accelerated sampling algorithm for uniform-rate discrete diffusion models with provable convergence guarantees. Remarkably, our GADD algorithm achieved
\mathcal{O}(\polylog(\varepsilon^{-1}))
convergence rate.
The accelerated rates were achieved by a novel Gibbs-based corrector that constructs posterior conditionals directly from the score function, thus without requiring additional training. We also demonstrated the superiority of GADD over previous CTMC correctors, along with its effectiveness on real-data experiments. An interesting direction for future work is to develop a theoretical understanding of predictor-corrector methods for masked diffusion models.

Appendix A
Additional Related Works

We present the most relevant related works in
Section

1.2
; additional references are now discussed below.

Theory of Acceleration for Continuous Diffusion Samplers.
A substantial body of theory has established accelerated convergence guarantees for continuous diffusion models, including both SDE and ODE solvers. Broadly, two main acceleration strategies have been explored. The first aims to more accurately approximate the continuous-time reverse process, for example by using higher-order numerical solvers such as Runge-Kutta methods or successive refinement (e.g.,
[
42
,
25
,
24
,
23
]
).
The second acceleration strategy augments the diffusion process with a corrector step that steers particles toward the target distribution. This predictor-corrector paradigm has been widely adopted in empirical studies of both continuous and discrete diffusion models
[
6
,
40
]
. From a theoretical perspective,
[
18
]
proposed the RTK-MALA and RTK-ULD algorithms, which incorporate Metropolis-Adjusted Langevin Algorithm (MALA) and Underdamped Langevin Dynamics (ULD) as correctors, respectively. Notably, RTK-MALA achieves a logarithmic convergence rate in
\varepsilon^{-1}
.

Theory on Gibbs Sampling.
Since the seminal work of
[
15
]
, Gibbs samplers have been shown to be effective across a wide range of applications, including sampling from graphical models, feasible sets of graph coloring problems
[
21
]
, determinantal point processes
[
37
]
, mixtures of densities
[
3
]
, and Bayesian hierarchical models
[
2
]
. An orthogonal line of work studies the parallelization of Gibbs samplers under specific graph structures
[
16
]
.
Notably, all of these works assume access to the exact posterior likelihood, whereas in diffusion-based settings the posterior must be estimated, introducing additional challenges that are not addressed by existing analyses.

Empirical Studies on the Acceleration of Masked Diffusion Models.
Beyond acceleration for uniform-rate models, several empirical studies have focused on masked diffusion models.
[
22
]
constructed a discrete MCMC transition path for correction and learns a separate corrector kernel. More recently,
[
45
]
proposed an informed correction strategy. Notably, these approaches are primarily empirical and do not provide theoretical convergence guarantees.

We now discuss
[
45
]
in more detail. That work employs an informed Gibbs corrector specifically tailored to
masked (i.e., absorbing-rate)
diffusion models, whereas our work focuses on the
uniform-rate
setting. While both of their and our approaches utilize Gibbs-type correctors, uniform-rate diffusion models pose distinct algorithmic challenges. In particular, the score functions in uniform-rate models need to be estimated differently from those in masked diffusion, where the masked score (i.e., a time-scaled clean-data distribution) naturally facilitates Gibbs updates. In contrast, our method relies on posterior likelihood estimators specifically designed for uniform-rate models (e.g., (
6
) and (
8
)), enabling principled Gibbs corrections in this setting.

From

\mathrm{KL}(\mathchoice{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\displaystyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\displaystyle q\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\textstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\textstyle q\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptstyle q\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle q\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}_{0}||p_{0})\lesssim(d\log S)e^{-T}.

\mathrm{TV}(p_{\text{init}},q_{T})\lesssim\sqrt{\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{init}}||q_{T})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{\lesssim}}\sqrt{d\log S}\cdot e^{-T/2}.

Intuitively, this error remains small due to the smoothness of the diffusion process, which in turn yields a warm-up condition.

Lemma 1
.

Fix adjacent steps
s<t

Suppose that
\mathrm{TV}(\hat{p}_{T-t,L},q_{t})\leq\varepsilon

D.3
Step 3: Combining all parts together

We are now ready to bring the pieces together. Suppose we choose
T\asymp\log(d(\log S)/\varepsilon^{2})
, by (
9
), the grand initialization error satisfies

\mathrm{TV}(p_{\text{init}},q_{T})\leq\varepsilon.

Also, when we choose
\kappa\asymp\left(\log\log(d/\varepsilon^{2})\right)^{-1}
, the total number of outer-loop steps satisfies that (cf.
[
7
]
)

N\asymp\frac{T+\log\delta^{-1}}{\kappa}\lesssim\left(\log\log(d/\varepsilon^{2})\right)(\log(d/\varepsilon^{2})+\log\delta^{-1}).

Next we use an inductive argument by inducting on
k=N-1,\dots,0
.
Suppose, for purpose of induction, that we already have

\mathrm{TV}(\hat{p}_{T-t_{k+1},L_{k+1}},q_{t_{k+1}})\lesssim L_{k+1}\varepsilon.

Then, at step
k
, by
Lemma

1
and under
2
, we have

\mathrm{TV}(p_{T-t_{k},0},q_{t_{k}})\lesssim\kappa d(M+S\max\{1,t_{k}^{-1}\})+L_{k+1}\varepsilon.

We now consider two sources of errors. The first is one that comes from the Gibbs sampling process. Following from standard analysis (cf. (
4
)),

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}(p_{T-t_{k},L_{k}},q_{t_{k}})

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{W}_{1}\left(p_{T-t_{k},L_{k}},q_{t_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq(1-\rho_{t_{k}})^{L_{k}}\mathrm{W}_{1}\left(p_{T-t_{k},0},q_{t_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq(1-\rho_{t_{k}})^{L_{k}}d\cdot\mathrm{TV}(p_{T-t_{k},0},q_{t_{k}}).

Thus, in order for
\mathrm{TV}(p_{T-t_{k},L_{k}},q_{t_{k}})\lesssim\varepsilon
, it suffices to have

\displaystyle L_{k}

\displaystyle\asymp\frac{\log\left(\frac{d}{\varepsilon}\cdot\mathrm{TV}(p_{T-t_{k},0},q_{t_{k}})\right)}{\rho_{t_{k}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\log\left(\frac{d}{\varepsilon}\cdot(\kappa d(M+S\max\{1,t_{k}^{-1}\})+L_{k+1}\varepsilon)\right)}{\rho_{t_{k}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\log\left(\frac{d^{2}}{\varepsilon}(M+S\delta^{-1})\right)}{\rho_{t_{k}}}.

The second error comes from score estimation. From
Lemma

3
and under
1
and
2
, we have

\mathrm{TV}(\hat{p}_{T-t_{k},L_{k}},p_{T-t_{k},L_{k}})\lesssim L_{k}M\varepsilon_{\text{est}}.

Thus, the total error at step
k
is

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}(\hat{p}_{T-t_{k},L_{k}},q_{t_{k}})

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{TV}(p_{T-t_{k},L_{k}},q_{t_{k}})+\mathrm{TV}(\hat{p}_{T-t_{k},L_{k}},p_{T-t_{k},L_{k}})

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon+L_{k}M\varepsilon_{\text{est}}.

As long as
\varepsilon_{\text{est}}=O(\varepsilon/M)
, this verifies the inductive hypothesis.

Therefore, when
\varepsilon_{\text{est}}=O\left(\frac{\rho_{t_{0}}}{M}\varepsilon\right)
, we have
\mathrm{TV}(\hat{p}_{T-\delta,L_{0}},q_{\delta})\lesssim\varepsilon
. Meanwhile, the total number of steps satisfy (with
\delta\asymp\frac{\varepsilon}{d}
):

\displaystyle N_{total}

\displaystyle=N+\sum_{k=N-1}^{0}L_{k}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{k=N-1}^{0}\frac{\log\left(\frac{d^{3}}{\varepsilon^{2}}S+\frac{d^{2}}{\varepsilon}M\right)}{\rho_{t_{k}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\log(d/\varepsilon^{2})\frac{\log\left(\frac{d^{3}}{\varepsilon^{2}}S+\frac{d^{2}}{\varepsilon}M\right)}{\rho_{*}}.

The proof is now complete.

Theorem

Fix the outer-loop step

T_{k}
, and let the corrector step-size be
\eta_{k}
. (Here we do not need to shrink step-sizes towards the end because the CTMC rate is regular.) Note that
L_{k}=T_{k}/\eta_{k}
. We write
\tilde{p}_{T-t_{k},\tau}

\tau

As before, the quantity of interest is
\mathrm{TV}(\hat{p}_{T-t_{k},L_{k}},q_{t_{k}})

\mathrm{TV}(\hat{p}_{T-t_{k},L_{k}},q_{t_{k}})\leq\mathrm{TV}(\tilde{p}_{T-t_{k},T_{k}},q_{t_{k}})+\mathrm{TV}(\hat{p}_{T-t_{k},L_{k}},\tilde{p}_{T-t_{k},T_{k}}).

Here the first term is the typical convergence error of a CTMC with time-homogeneous rate matrix. Classical results
[
5

have established that, for any initial distribution
p_{T-t_{k},0}

\mathrm{TV}(\tilde{p}_{T-t_{k},T_{k}},q_{t_{k}})^{2}\leq 2\log\frac{1}{\min_{x}q_{t_{k}}(x)}\cdot e^{-2\mu_{k}T_{k}}\lesssim d(\log S)e^{-2\mu_{k}T_{k}},

where
\mu_{k}
is the modified log-Sobolev constant of the rate matrix
R^{c}_{k}
. Since the reverse rate
\mathchoice{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\displaystyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\displaystyle R\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 3.0mu\reflectbox{$\textstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-3.0mu\textstyle R\mkern 3.0mu$}}$}\mkern-3.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptstyle R\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}{\mkern 2.0mu\reflectbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\vec{\reflectbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\scriptscriptstyle R\mkern 2.0mu$}}$}\mkern-2.0mu}_{t_{k}}
ultimately depends on the underlying target distribution
q_{t_{k}}
,
\mu_{k}
is also data-dependent, but it is independent of the target accuracy
\varepsilon
.

We now consider the second term in (
11
), which corresponds to the discretization error. The result is included in the following lemma. While we only consider the case without estimation error for simplicity, it can be easily adapted to the more general case with standard decomposition (e.g.,
[
27
]
).

Lemma 5
.

Suppose that there is no estimation error. Under
2
and using the
\tau
-leaping or the Euler method, we have

\mathrm{KL}(\tilde{p}_{T-t_{k},T_{k}}||p_{T-t_{k},L_{k}})\lesssim\eta_{k}T_{k}d^{2}M^{2}\log(MS).

Proof.

See
Section

F.5
.
∎

Therefore, continuing from (

), with perfect estimation, we have

\mathrm{TV}(p_{T-t_{k},L_{k}},q_{t_{k}})\lesssim\sqrt{d(\log S)}e^{-\mu_{k}T_{k}}+\sqrt{\eta_{k}T_{k}d^{2}M^{2}\log(MS)}.

which implies that

(i)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x^{i}\in[S]}\frac{1}{S}\sum_{y^{i}\in[S]}\left|\frac{s_{t}(x,x^{-i}\oplus_{i}y^{i})}{\sum_{x^{i}\in[S]}s_{t}(x,x^{-i}\oplus_{i}y^{i})}-\frac{\frac{q_{t}(x)}{q_{t}(x^{-i}\oplus_{i}y^{i})}}{\sum_{x^{i}\in[S]}\frac{q_{t}(x)}{q_{t}(x^{-i}\oplus_{i}y^{i})}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x^{i}\in[S]}\frac{1}{S}\sum_{y^{i}\in[S]}s_{t}(x,x^{-i}\oplus_{i}y^{i})\left|\frac{1}{\sum_{x^{i}\in[S]}s_{t}(x,x^{-i}\oplus_{i}y^{i})}-\frac{1}{\sum_{x^{i}\in[S]}\frac{q_{t}(x)}{q_{t}(x^{-i}\oplus_{i}y^{i})}}\right|

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x^{i}\in[S]}\frac{1}{S}\sum_{y^{i}\in[S]}\frac{1}{\sum_{x^{i}\in[S]}\frac{q_{t}(x)}{q_{t}(x^{-i}\oplus_{i}y^{i})}}\left|s_{t}(x,x^{-i}\oplus_{i}y^{i})-\frac{q_{t}(x)}{q_{t}(x^{-i}\oplus_{i}y^{i})}\right|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x^{i}\in[S]}\frac{1}{S}\sum_{y^{i}\in[S]}s_{t}(x,x^{-i}\oplus_{i}y^{i})\left|\frac{1}{\sum_{x^{i}\in[S]}s_{t}(x,x^{-i}\oplus_{i}y^{i})}-q_{t}^{i}(y^{i}|x^{-i})\right|

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x^{i}\in[S]}\frac{1}{S}\sum_{y^{i}\in[S]}q_{t}^{i}(y^{i}|x^{-i})\left|s_{t}(x,x^{-i}\oplus_{i}y^{i})-\frac{q_{t}(x)}{q_{t}(x^{-i}\oplus_{i}y^{i})}\right|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(ii)}}{{\leq}}\frac{1}{2}M\sum_{a\in[S]}\left|\left(\sum_{y^{i}\in[S]}s_{t}(x^{-i}\oplus_{i}y^{i},x^{-i}\oplus_{i}a)\right)^{-1}-q_{t}^{i}(a|x^{-i})\right|

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{S}\sum_{a\in[S]}q_{t}^{i}(a|x^{-i})\sum_{y^{i}\in[S]}\left|s_{t}(x^{-i}\oplus_{i}y^{i},x^{-i}\oplus_{i}a)-\frac{q_{t}(x^{-i}\oplus_{i}y^{i})}{q_{t}(x^{-i}\oplus_{i}a)}\right|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(iii)}}{{\leq}}M^{2}S^{-1}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a\in[S]}q_{t}^{i}(a|x^{-i})\sum_{y^{i}\in[S]}\left|\frac{q_{t}(x^{-i}\oplus_{i}y^{i})}{q_{t}(x^{-i}\oplus_{i}a)}-s_{t}(x^{-i}\oplus_{i}y^{i},x^{-i}\oplus_{i}a)\right|,

where
(i)
follows because

\frac{1}{\sum_{x^{i}\in[S]}\frac{q_{t}(x)}{q_{t}(x^{-i}\oplus_{i}y^{i})}}=\frac{1}{\sum_{x^{i}\in[S]}\frac{q_{t}^{i}(x^{i}|x^{-i})}{q_{t}^{i}(y^{i}|x^{-i})}}=q_{t}^{i}(y^{i}|x^{-i}),

(ii)
follows from
2
and by relabeling
(x^{i},y^{i})
as
(y^{i},a)
, and
(iii)
follows from the proof of
Lemma

3
(see (
F.3
)). Compared with (
F.3
), the only difference is in the extra
M
constant (that arises from normalization). The rest of the proof follows in the same manner as that of
Lemma

3
, and the proof is complete.

F.5
Proof of
Lemma

5

The proof follows similar to
[
27
, Theorem 2]
, but with several key differences. In particular,
[
27
, Theorem 2]
is specialized to the predictor error in the outer-loop CTMC. In such a case, the analyzed reverse CTMC has a corresponding forward process that provides nice geometric properties (e.g., dimension-wise factorization used in
[
27
, Lemma 2]
). This is different from our case with the corrector CTMC, which does not have such nice properties. On the other hand, different from the time-inhomogeneous reverse CTMC, the corrector CTMC has a time-homogeneous rate that helps the analysis.

We will omit all subscript
k
whenever obvious. Define
\hat{R}^{c}_{\tau}
as the rate corresponding to the discretized sampling process at time
\tau
. For
\tau\in[\eta_{k}\ell,\eta_{k}(\ell+1)]
,
given the current state

x_{\eta_{k}\ell}
, we have
\hat{R}_{\tau}^{c}(x_{\eta_{k}\ell},\cdot)=R_{\eta_{k}\ell}^{c}(x_{\eta_{k}\ell},\cdot)
. (Note that they are not equal for arbitrary state
x
within the time duration, cf.
[
27
]
.) Also note that
\tilde{p}_{T-t_{k},0}=p_{T-t_{k},0}
.

Recall the definition of
R^{c}
in (
7
). Under
2
, a useful upper bound for
R^{c}
is that,
\forall x\neq y
,

\displaystyle R^{c}(x,y)

\displaystyle=R_{t_{k}}(x,y)+\hat{R}_{t_{k}}(x,y)=R_{t_{k}}(x,y)+R_{t_{k}}(y,x)s_{t_{k}}(y,x)

\displaystyle\in\left[S^{-1}(1+M^{-1}),S^{-1}(1+M)\right].

(17)

Also note that
R^{c}(x,y)=0
whenever
\mathrm{Ham}\left(x,y\right)\geq 2
. Since this bound is uniform over
(x,y)
, from
[
6
, Appendix B.5]
, we also have the same uniform upper bound for
\hat{R}_{\tau}^{c}(x,y)
as

\hat{R}_{\tau}^{c}(x,y)\in\left[S^{-1}(1+M^{-1}),S^{-1}(1+M)\right].

Now,
[
27
, Theorem 1]
implies that

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(\tilde{p}_{T-t_{k},T_{k}}||p_{T-t_{k},L_{k}})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{L_{k}-1}\int_{\eta_{k}\ell}^{\eta_{k}(\ell+1)}\mathbb{E}_{x_{\tau}\sim\tilde{p}_{T-t_{k},\tau}}\underbrace{\left[\sum_{y:y\neq x_{\tau}}\hat{R}^{c}_{\tau}(x_{\tau},y)-R^{c}(x_{\tau},y)+R^{c}(x_{\tau},y)\log\frac{R^{c}(x_{\tau},y)}{\hat{R}_{\tau}^{c}(x_{\tau},y)}\right]}_{=:g_{\tau}(x_{\tau})}\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{=}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{L_{k}-1}\int_{\eta_{k}\ell}^{\eta_{k}(\ell+1)}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{\tau}\sim\tilde{p}_{T-t_{k},\tau}\\
x_{\eta_{k}\ell}\sim\tilde{p}_{T-t_{k},\eta_{k}\ell}\end{subarray}}\left[g_{\tau}(x_{\tau})-g_{\tau}(x_{\eta_{k}\ell})\right]\mathrm{d}\tau

where
(i)
follows because
g_{\tau}(x_{\eta_{k}\ell})=0
. Here
note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[g_{\tau}(x_{\tau})-g_{\tau}(x_{\eta_{k}\ell})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x_{\eta_{k}\ell}\sim\tilde{p}_{T-t_{k},\eta_{k}\ell}}\left[\sum_{x_{\tau}}\tilde{p}_{T-t_{k},\tau|\eta_{k}\ell}(x_{\tau}|x_{\eta_{k}\ell})g_{\tau}(x_{\tau})-g_{\tau}(x_{\eta_{k}\ell})\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}_{x_{\eta_{k}\ell}\sim\tilde{p}_{T-t_{k},\eta_{k}\ell}}\left[\sum_{x_{\tau}}\left(\mathds{1}\left\{x_{\tau}=x_{\eta_{k}\ell}\right\}+R^{c}(x_{\eta_{k}\ell},x_{\tau})(\tau-\eta_{k}\ell)\right)g_{\tau}(x_{\tau})-g_{\tau}(x_{\eta_{k}\ell})\right]

\displaystyle=(\tau-\eta_{k}\ell)\cdot\mathbb{E}_{x_{\eta_{k}\ell}\sim\tilde{p}_{T-t_{k},\eta_{k}\ell}}\left[\sum_{x_{\tau}:\mathrm{Ham}\left(x_{\tau},x_{\eta_{k}\ell}\right)=1}R^{c}(x_{\eta_{k}\ell},x_{\tau})g_{\tau}(x_{\tau})\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(ii)}}{{\lesssim}}(\tau-\eta_{k}\ell)\cdot(dS)\cdot\frac{M}{S}\cdot dM\log(SM),

where in
(ii)
we have used the uniform upper bound for
R^{c}
in (
F.5
) and that for
g_{\tau}(x)
as follows:

\displaystyle\left|g_{\tau}(x)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{y:y\neq x}\hat{R}_{\tau}^{c}(x,y)-R^{c}(x,y)+R^{c}(x,y)\log\frac{R^{c}(x,y)}{\hat{R}_{\tau}^{c}(x,y)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{y:\mathrm{Ham}\left(x,y\right)=1}\left|\hat{R}_{\tau}^{c}(x,y)\right|+\left|R^{c}(x,y)\right|+\left|R^{c}(x,y)\right|\cdot\left|\log\frac{R^{c}(x,y)}{\hat{R}_{\tau}^{c}(x,y)}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim d(S-1)\cdot\frac{M}{S}\log(SM).

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(\tilde{p}_{T-t_{k},T_{k}}||p_{T-t_{k},L_{k}})

\displaystyle\lesssim d^{2}SM\log(SM)\sum_{\ell=0}^{L_{k}-1}\int_{\eta_{k}\ell}^{\eta_{k}(\ell+1)}(\tau-\eta_{k}\ell)\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\lesssim d^{2}SM\log(SM)\sum_{\ell=0}^{L_{k}-1}\eta_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle=\eta_{k}T_{k}d^{2}M^{2}\log(SM).

The proof is now complete.
