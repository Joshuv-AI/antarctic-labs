Angle Between Two Vectors over Finite Fields and an Application to Projective Unique Decoding
Kamil OTAL

Abstract
. We introduce a Hamming-type angular function angle H ⁡ ( u , v ) := min c ∈ 𝔽 q ⋆ ⁡ d H ​ ( u , c ​ v ) \operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,v):=\min_{c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\!\star}}d_{H}(u,cv) on pairs of nonzero vectors in 𝔽 q n \mathbb{F}_{q}^{n} and show that it satisfies all three metric axioms up to scalar multiplication. The function angle H \operatorname{angle}_{H} is invariant under nonzero scalar multiplication in either argument and therefore descends to a genuine integer-valued metric on the projective space ℙ ⁡ ( 𝔽 q n ) \mathbb{P}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}) . As a concrete application, we prove an angular (or projective ) version of the unique-decoding theorem for linear codes: if angle H ⁡ ( u , C ∖ { 0 } ) < d / 2 \operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,C\setminus\{0\})<d/2 , where d d is the minimum distance of the linear code C C , then the closest direction in C C to u u is unique up to nonzero scalar multiplication. We then discuss how this angular viewpoint relates to the proximity-gap programme for Reed–Solomon codes. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first attempt to define an angle notion for vectors over finite fields and interpret it from several perspectives, including geometry, coding theory, and cryptography.

1.
Introduction

Distance and angle are the two basic geometric primitives that
underlie the metric viewpoint on a vector space. Over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, both
admit elegant definitions in terms of an inner product, and the

angular metric

\operatorname{angle}_{E}(u,v)=\arccos\!\left(\frac{\langle u,v\rangle}{\|u\|\,\|v\|}\right),\qquad u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\},

is the cornerstone of any quantitative notion of “parallelism”
between vectors.

Over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, the analog of the Euclidean inner product
is well known to be poorly behaved as a measurement of geometry: in
particular, one may have
u\cdot u=0
for a nonzero vector
u
, and
the relation
u\cdot v=0
does not faithfully capture
orthogonality in any geometric sense. Distance, on the other hand,
is well behaved: the Hamming distance
d_{H}
on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
is a genuine
metric, and it is the engine of virtually all coding theory.

In this note we observe that the Hamming distance can be used to

define
a satisfactory angular notion on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, without ever
invoking an inner product. Explicitly, for nonzero
u,v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
we
set

\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,v):=\min_{c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\!\star}}d_{H}(u,cv).

Our main theorem (Theorem
3.2
) asserts that
\operatorname{angle}_{H}
enjoys
the three identifying properties of a metric, up to scalar
multiplication. Equivalently,
\operatorname{angle}_{H}
descends to an
integer-valued metric on the projective space
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
.

The motivation for studying such an object is twofold.

Conceptually
, it furnishes a geometric vocabulary — a notion
of two vectors being “more or less parallel” — in a setting where
the inner-product picture fails.
Practically
, the quantity

\operatorname{angle}_{H}
is exactly the Hamming distance from a vector to a punctured
line through the origin, an object that arises naturally in proximity
testing for linear codes
[
1
,
2
,
4
]
and in the
list-decoding literature
[
3
]
.

Our contributions are as follows.

(1)

We give a self-contained proof that
\operatorname{angle}_{H}
satisfies the
metric axioms up to scalar equivalence (Theorem
3.2
).

(2)

We record the projective-space interpretation, give a linear-time
algorithm for computing
\operatorname{angle}_{H}
, and contrast it with the Euclidean
angular metric (Section
4
).

(3)

We define the Hamming angle from a vector to a set, carefully
relating
\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,S)
and
d_{H}(u,S)
when
S
is a linear code
(Section
5
, Proposition
5.2
).

(4)

As a concrete application, we prove an
angular unique
decoding theorem
: a linear code
C
of minimum distance
d
admits
unique projective decoding up to angular radius
d/2

(Theorem
5.4
). We then discuss the
relationship between this angular viewpoint and the proximity-gap
problem.

Notation.

Throughout,
q
is a prime power and
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is the finite field of
q

elements. We write
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\!\star}:=\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0\}
for the multiplicative
group. For
u\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
,
\operatorname{wt}_{H}(u):=|\{i\in[n]:u_{i}\neq 0\}|

denotes the Hamming weight, and

d_{H}(u,v):=\operatorname{wt}_{H}(u-v)=|\{i:u_{i}\neq v_{i}\}|

denotes the Hamming distance. Note the basic identity

\operatorname{wt}_{H}(\alpha u)=\operatorname{wt}_{H}(u)
for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\!\star}
, which we use
repeatedly without further comment.

2.
Angle in the Euclidean Setting

We briefly revisit the Euclidean situation to motivate what follows.
The signed (oriented) angle between two vectors, although familiar in
the plane, fails as a metric for the standard reasons: it is defined
only modulo
2\pi
, can be negative, and is antisymmetric in the
sense that
\theta(u,v)=-\theta(v,u)
. The triangle inequality
also fails in general. Worse, in dimensions
\geq 3
there is no
canonical orientation, and the signed angle is not even uniquely
defined without additional choices.

The remedy is to pass to the
unsigned
angle, which is the
length of the shortest arc on the unit sphere connecting the two
normalized vectors. Let
S^{n-1}=\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\|u\|=1\}
and

d_{\mathrm{geo}}(u,v):=\arccos\langle u,v\rangle
denote the
geodesic distance on
S^{n-1}
. Then
d_{\mathrm{geo}}
is a metric
on
S^{n-1}
(see, e.g.,
[
5
, Chapter 3]
). For arbitrary
nonzero
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, one defines

\operatorname{angle}_{E}(u,v):=d_{\mathrm{geo}}\!\left(\frac{u}{\|u\|},\frac{v}{\|v\|}\right)=\arccos\!\left(\frac{\langle u,v\rangle}{\|u\|\,\|v\|}\right),

which is invariant under positive scalar multiplication in either
argument. Concretely,
\operatorname{angle}_{E}
satisfies:

(1)

\operatorname{angle}_{E}(u,v)=0
iff
u=cv
for some positive
c
.

(2)

\operatorname{angle}_{E}(u,v)=\operatorname{angle}_{E}(v,u)
for all nonzero
u,v
.

(3)

\operatorname{angle}_{E}(u,w)\leq\operatorname{angle}_{E}(u,v)+\operatorname{angle}_{E}(v,w)
for all nonzero
u,v,w
.

In short, after normalization the Euclidean angle becomes a bona fide
metric, namely the spherical metric. The principal observation
behind our work is that an analogous mechanism is available over

\mathbb{F}_{q}
, with the role of the unit sphere played by the

projective space

\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
.

Remark 2.1
.

A useful geometric coincidence is the chain

\operatorname{angle}_{E}(u,v)\;\longleftrightarrow\;\bigl|\mathrm{arc}_{E}(u,v)\bigr|\;\longleftrightarrow\;\bigl|\mathrm{chord}(u,v)\bigr|\;\longleftrightarrow\;d_{E}(u,v),

which holds whenever
u,v\in S^{n-1}
. That is, the Euclidean
angle, the arc length, the chord length, and the Euclidean distance
all agree as ways to compare two unit vectors — up to the
appropriate transformation. In contrast, over
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
the analogous
chain breaks down (Section
4.4
), and one
genuinely needs the angular notion in addition to the distance.

3.
The Hamming Angle: Definition and Main Theorem

Definition 3.1
.

For
u,v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, the
Hamming angle
between
u
and
v
is

\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,v)\;:=\;\min_{c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\!\star}}\,d_{H}(u,cv).

Note that
\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,v)
is a nonnegative integer in
\{0,1,\dots,n\}
.
Equivalently,
\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,v)=n-\max_{c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\!\star}}\mathrm{agr}(u,cv)
,
where
\mathrm{agr}(x,y):=|\{i:x_{i}=y_{i}\}|
is the agreement
count.

The following is our main theorem.

Theorem 3.2
.

The Hamming angle function
\operatorname{angle}_{H}\colon\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\times\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{Z}

satisfies the following three properties:

(1)

(Scalar identifiability)

\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,v)=0
if and only
if
u=cv
for some
c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\!\star}
.

(2)

(Symmetry)

\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,v)=\operatorname{angle}_{H}(v,u)
for all

u,v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
.

(3)

(Triangle inequality)

\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,w)\leq\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,v)+\operatorname{angle}_{H}(v,w)

for all
u,v,w\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
.

Proof.

(1)
The Hamming distance is a metric, so

d_{H}(u,cv)=0
if and only if
u=cv
. Since
\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,v)
is a
minimum of nonnegative quantities, it equals
0
if and only if

d_{H}(u,cv)=0
for some
c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\!\star}
, i.e.,
u=cv
for some

c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\!\star}
.

(2)
Let
c_{0}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\!\star}
be an element attaining the minimum
in
\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,v)
, so that
\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,v)=d_{H}(u,c_{0}v)
. Then

d_{H}(u,c_{0}v)=\operatorname{wt}_{H}(u-c_{0}v)=\operatorname{wt}_{H}\!\bigl(c_{0}^{-1}(u-c_{0}v)\bigr)=\operatorname{wt}_{H}(c_{0}^{-1}u-v)=d_{H}(v,c_{0}^{-1}u),

where the second equality uses the invariance of
\operatorname{wt}_{H}
under
multiplication by the nonzero scalar
c_{0}^{-1}
. Since

c_{0}^{-1}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\!\star}
, we conclude

\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,v)=d_{H}(v,c_{0}^{-1}u)\;\geq\;\min_{c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\!\star}}d_{H}(v,cu)=\operatorname{angle}_{H}(v,u).

The reverse inequality follows by the same argument starting from

\operatorname{angle}_{H}(v,u)
. Hence
\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,v)=\operatorname{angle}_{H}(v,u)
.

(3)
By part (2) we may equivalently write

\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,v)=\min_{c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\!\star}}d_{H}(cu,v).

Pick
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\!\star}
attaining the minima for
\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,v)
and

\operatorname{angle}_{H}(v,w)
, respectively, so that

\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,v)=d_{H}(c_{1}u,v),\qquad\operatorname{angle}_{H}(v,w)=d_{H}(v,c_{2}w).

By the triangle inequality for
d_{H}
,

\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,v)+\operatorname{angle}_{H}(v,w)=d_{H}(c_{1}u,v)+d_{H}(v,c_{2}w)\;\geq\;d_{H}(c_{1}u,c_{2}w).

Now use again the scalar-invariance of
\operatorname{wt}_{H}
:

d_{H}(c_{1}u,c_{2}w)=\operatorname{wt}_{H}(c_{1}u-c_{2}w)=\operatorname{wt}_{H}\!\bigl(c_{1}^{-1}(c_{1}u-c_{2}w)\bigr)=\operatorname{wt}_{H}(u-c_{1}^{-1}c_{2}\,w)=d_{H}\!\bigl(u,c_{1}^{-1}c_{2}\,w\bigr).

Since
c_{1}^{-1}c_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\!\star}
,

d_{H}(c_{1}u,c_{2}w)\;\geq\;\min_{c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\!\star}}d_{H}(u,cw)=\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,w).

Combining the two displays gives the triangle inequality. ∎

4.
Properties of the Hamming Angle

4.1.
Projective-space interpretation

By definition,
\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,v)=\operatorname{angle}_{H}(\alpha u,\beta v)
for any

\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\!\star}
: the rescaling is absorbed into the choice
of
c
inside the minimum. Hence
\operatorname{angle}_{H}
depends only on the

projective points

[u],[v]\in\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
.

Proposition 4.1
.

The function
\overline{\operatorname{angle}_{H}}([u],[v]):=\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,v)
is a
well-defined integer-valued metric on
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
.

Thus, just as
\operatorname{angle}_{E}
is the geodesic metric on
S^{n-1}
,
\operatorname{angle}_{H}
is
a natural metric on the projective space
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
. In
particular,
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
plays the role over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
that the unit
sphere plays over
\mathbb{R}
.

Remark 4.2
.

The metric
\overline{\operatorname{angle}_{H}}
is (essentially) the
projective
Hamming metric
, and the underlying quantity
\min_{c}d_{H}(u,cv)
has
been studied in coding theory in the context of projective Reed–Solomon
codes and Grassmann codes (where one considers distances between

k
-dimensional subspaces). The novelty of the present viewpoint is
the explicit angular interpretation and the resulting metric axioms,
which appear not to have been recorded together in this form.

4.2.
Efficient computation of
\operatorname{angle}_{H}

Although
\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,v)
is a minimum over
q-1
scalars, it can be
computed in a single pass:

Proposition 4.3
.

Given
u,v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
,
\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,v)
can be computed in time
O(n)

(under the standard assumption that field operations cost
O(1)
).

Proof.

Partition
[n]
according to the joint values of
(u_{i},v_{i})
:

•

N_{0}:=|\{i:u_{i}=0\text{ and }v_{i}=0\}|

— positions where
u
and
cv
agree for
every

c
;

•

N_{1}:=|\{i:u_{i}\neq 0,\;v_{i}=0\}|

— positions where
u_{i}\neq(cv)_{i}=0
for every
c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\!\star}
;

•

N_{2}:=|\{i:u_{i}=0,\;v_{i}\neq 0\}|

— positions where
u_{i}=0\neq cv_{i}
for every
c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\!\star}
;

•

For
c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\!\star}
,
A_{c}:=|\{i:u_{i},v_{i}\neq 0,\;u_{i}=cv_{i}\}|

— positions where
u_{i}=(cv)_{i}
for that particular
c
.

A position contributes to
d_{H}(u,cv)
unless it is counted in

N_{0}
or in
A_{c}
. Therefore

d_{H}(u,cv)=n-N_{0}-A_{c},\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,v)=n-N_{0}-\max_{c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\!\star}}A_{c}.

To compute
\max_{c}A_{c}
, iterate over
i\in[n]
: whenever both

u_{i}
and
v_{i}
are nonzero, increment a counter indexed by the
ratio
u_{i}/v_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\!\star}
. The maximum counter value is

\max_{c}A_{c}
. The total work is
O(n)
.
∎

4.3.
Extremal values and small examples

The smallest value
\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,v)=0
is achieved exactly when
u,v

span the same projective point. At the other extreme:

Proposition 4.4
.

For
u,v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
,

\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,v)\leq n-1

unless
at every position exactly one
of
u_{i},v_{i}
vanishes, in which case
\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,v)=n
.

Proof.

If some position
i
has
u_{i}\neq 0
and
v_{i}\neq 0
, take

c=u_{i}v_{i}^{-1}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\!\star}
in Proposition
4.3
;
then
A_{c}\geq 1
, so
\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,v)\leq n-1
. Otherwise,

N_{0}+N_{1}+N_{2}=n
and
\max_{c}A_{c}=0
, so

\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,v)=n-N_{0}
. The latter equals
n
exactly when
N_{0}=0
,
i.e., exactly one of
u_{i},v_{i}
is nonzero at every position.
∎

Example 4.5

(The case
q=2
)
.

Since
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\!\star}=\{1\}
when
q=2
, the definition reduces to

\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,v)=d_{H}(u,v)
. In other words, over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
the angular
metric coincides with the Hamming metric on
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
.
The genuinely new content of
\operatorname{angle}_{H}
appears for
q\geq 3
.

Example 4.6

(A computation in
\mathbb{F}_{3}^{3}
)
.

Let
u=(1,2,0)
and
v=(1,1,2)
in
\mathbb{F}_{3}^{3}
. Compute:

•

For
c=1
:
cv=(1,1,2)
and
d_{H}(u,cv)=|\{2,3\}|=2
.

•

For
c=2
:
cv=(2,2,1)
and
d_{H}(u,cv)=|\{1,3\}|=2
.

Hence
\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,v)=2
. Note that

u\cdot v=1\cdot 1+2\cdot 1+0\cdot 2=3\equiv 0\pmod{3}
,
so
u
and
v
are “orthogonal” in the inner-product sense, yet

\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,v)=2<3=n
. This illustrates the next subsection.

4.4.
Differences from the Euclidean angle

We highlight two qualitative differences between
\operatorname{angle}_{H}
and
\operatorname{angle}_{E}
.

(A) Inner products are not faithful detectors of right angles.

Over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
u\cdot v=0

iff

\operatorname{angle}_{E}(u,v)=\pi/2
. The
corresponding equivalence fails over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
: as in
Example
4.6
, one can have
u\cdot v=0
while
\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,v)

is strictly less than
n
. In fact,
\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,v)=n

implies

u\cdot v=0
(since by Proposition
4.4
the
supports of
u
and
v
are disjoint), but the converse fails.
Consequently, there is no clean way to read off “trigonometric”
quantities from
\operatorname{angle}_{H}
, and the relation between the inner product
and the angle is one-way only.

(B)
\operatorname{angle}_{H}
is statistical-distance-like.

Recall that the statistical distance between two probability
distributions
P,Q
is
1
exactly when
P
and
Q
have disjoint
supports. By Proposition
4.4
, the analogous
statement holds for
\operatorname{angle}_{H}
: the maximum value
\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,v)=n
is
achieved precisely when
u,v
have “disjoint structure” in the
sense that at every coordinate at most one of them is nonzero. The
Euclidean
\operatorname{angle}_{E}
exhibits no such phenomenon.

5.
An Application: Projective Unique Decoding

We now apply
\operatorname{angle}_{H}
to a concrete problem in coding theory. Our
result is an angular (or
projective
) refinement of the
classical unique-decoding theorem.

5.1.
The Hamming angle to a set

Definition 5.1
.

For nonzero
u\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
and a subset
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
with

S\cap\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\neq\emptyset
, define

\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,S)\;:=\;\min_{v\in S\cap\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\setminus\{0\}}\,\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,v).

The next proposition records the exact relationship between

\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,S)
and the Hamming distance
d_{H}(u,S)
when
S
is a
linear subspace. We caution that the naive identity
\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,S)=d_{H}(u,S)
is
false
in general: the two quantities differ when
the zero vector is the closest point of
S
to
u
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
be a linear subspace with
C\neq\{0\}
, and
let
u\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
. Then

\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,C)\;=\;\min_{c\in C\setminus\{0\}}\,d_{H}(u,c)\;\geq\;d_{H}(u,C),

with equality
\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,C)=d_{H}(u,C)
if and only if the minimum

d_{H}(u,C)=\min_{c\in C}d_{H}(u,c)
is attained at some nonzero

c\in C
.

Proof.

Since
C
is a subspace, for every
v\in C\setminus\{0\}
and every

c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\!\star}
we have
cv\in C\setminus\{0\}
. Conversely, any

w\in C\setminus\{0\}
is of the form
1\cdot w
. Hence

\displaystyle\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,C)

\displaystyle=\min_{v\in C\setminus\{0\},\;c\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\!\star}}d_{H}(u,cv)=\min_{w\in C\setminus\{0\}}d_{H}(u,w).

Comparing this with

d_{H}(u,C)=\min_{w\in C}d_{H}(u,w)=\min\bigl(\operatorname{wt}_{H}(u),\,\min_{w\in C\setminus\{0\}}d_{H}(u,w)\bigr)

gives the claim.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

In the regimes of interest for proximity testing

[
1
,
2
]
, the input
u
is purported to be close to a
codeword
c\neq 0
, in particular closer to
c
than to
0
. There,

\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,C)=d_{H}(u,C)
and the two notions coincide. The careful
distinction in Proposition
5.2
is what one needs
in order to use
\operatorname{angle}_{H}
as a drop-in replacement for
d_{H}
in such
arguments.

5.2.
Angular unique decoding

Recall that the
minimum distance
of a linear code

C\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
is

d:=\min_{c\in C\setminus\{0\}}\operatorname{wt}_{H}(c)
,
which (by linearity) coincides with

\min_{c,c^{\prime}\in C,\,c\neq c^{\prime}}d_{H}(c,c^{\prime}).

The classical unique
decoding theorem states that, for any
u\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, there is at most
one codeword
c\in C
with
d_{H}(u,c)<d/2
.

We prove the angular analog.

Theorem 5.4

(Angular unique decoding)
.

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
be a linear code of minimum distance
d
, and
let
u\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
. If
\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,C)<d/2
, then the minimum

\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,C)=\min_{c\in C\setminus\{0\}}\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,c)

is attained at a codeword
c^{\star}\in C\setminus\{0\}
that is
unique up to multiplication by an element of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\!\star}
. Equivalently,
the projective point
[c^{\star}]\in\mathbb{P}(C)
is uniquely
determined by
u
.

Proof.

Suppose for contradiction that
c_{1},c_{2}\in C\setminus\{0\}
both
satisfy
\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,c_{i})<d/2
for
i=1,2
, and that

c_{1}\notin\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\!\star}\cdot c_{2}
, i.e.,
[c_{1}]\neq[c_{2}]
in

\mathbb{P}(C)
.

By the triangle inequality of
\operatorname{angle}_{H}

(Theorem
3.2
(3)),

\operatorname{angle}_{H}(c_{1},c_{2})\leq\operatorname{angle}_{H}(c_{1},u)+\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,c_{2})<\frac{d}{2}+\frac{d}{2}=d.

On the other hand,

\operatorname{angle}_{H}(c_{1},c_{2})=\min_{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\!\star}}d_{H}(c_{1},\alpha c_{2})=\min_{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\!\star}}\operatorname{wt}_{H}(c_{1}-\alpha c_{2}).

For each
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\!\star}
,
c_{1}-\alpha c_{2}\in C
by linearity,
and
c_{1}-\alpha c_{2}\neq 0
because
[c_{1}]\neq[c_{2}]
(otherwise

c_{1}=\alpha c_{2}
would put
c_{1}
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\!\star}\cdot c_{2}
). Therefore
each
c_{1}-\alpha c_{2}
is a
nonzero
codeword, and

\operatorname{wt}_{H}(c_{1}-\alpha c_{2})\geq d
. Taking the minimum over
\alpha

gives
\operatorname{angle}_{H}(c_{1},c_{2})\geq d
, contradicting the previous display.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

Theorem
5.4
is the projective analog
of classical unique decoding. The classical theorem fixes a
codeword
c^{\star}
, whereas the angular version fixes only the

direction

[c^{\star}]
. Both theorems share the same radius

d/2
, and indeed the proof reduces to applying the triangle
inequality of an appropriate metric —
d_{H}
in the classical case,

\operatorname{angle}_{H}
in the projective case. In this sense
Theorem
5.4
is not a strengthening of
the classical theorem but rather its natural projective sibling.

Corollary 5.6
.

Under the hypothesis of Theorem
5.4
, the
projective list-decoding list

L_{\mathrm{proj}}(u,\rho):=\bigl\{[c]\in\mathbb{P}(C):\operatorname{angle}_{H}(u,c)<\rho\bigr\}

has size at most
1
for any
\rho<d/2
.

5.3.
Relation to the proximity-gap problem

The proximity-gap phenomenon
[
1
,
2
]
concerns

affine
lines in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
: given
f,g\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, one studies the
line
L_{f,g}:=\{f+zg:z\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\}
and asks whether the points
on
L_{f,g}
are uniformly close to a code
C
or, on the contrary,
uniformly far from it. The relevant codewords come in correlated
pairs
(c_{0},c_{1})
, with
c_{0}+zc_{1}
tracking
f+zg
on a common
agreement set.

The function
\operatorname{angle}_{H}
does not directly capture this affine geometry;
it is by design invariant under scalar multiplication only in

each argument separately
, whereas a proximity-gap statement
needs the joint linear structure of
(f,g)
. However, the two
frameworks meet at the following points.

•

Both produce decoding radii of the form
d/2
or
J(\delta)
.

Theorem
5.4
corresponds to the
“trivial” unique-decoding-radius regime in which the proximity loss

\varepsilon^{\star}
is essentially
0
. Up to the Johnson radius

J(\delta)=1-\sqrt{1-\delta}
, recent work
[
1
]
achieves
the same
\varepsilon^{\star}=0
for affine lines, but at the price
of
O(n)
exceptional values of
z
.

•

Correlated agreement is a 2-vector projective statement.

A pair of vectors
(u_{0},u_{1})
has
\delta
-correlated agreement with
a code
C
if there exist
c_{0},c_{1}\in C
and
T\subseteq[n]
with

|T|\geq(1-\delta)n
such that
u_{i}
and
c_{i}
agree on
T
for
each
i
. This is precisely the statement that the

2
-dimensional
subspace
\langle u_{0},u_{1}\rangle\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, modulo
C\oplus C
, has a representative of small weight —
a higher-dimensional generalization of the
1
-dimensional projective
notion captured by
\operatorname{angle}_{H}
.

•

The triangle inequality of
\operatorname{angle}_{H}
provides a uniqueness
obstruction.
As in Theorem
5.4
, the
triangle inequality immediately forbids two distinct projective
codewords from being simultaneously
\rho
-close to the same input
when
2\rho<d
. The corresponding obstruction in the affine setting
underlies the unique-decoding regime of all known proximity-gap
proofs.

A natural direction for future work is to define a
projective

proximity-gap problem in the
\operatorname{angle}_{H}
framework — i.e., projective
lines in
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
rather than affine lines in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
— and
to determine whether the
\operatorname{angle}_{H}
triangle inequality and the
projective-space geometry yield improvements in the unique-decoding
regime analogous to those of
[
1
]
. We leave this to future
investigation.

6.
Concluding Remarks

We have introduced an angular metric
\operatorname{angle}_{H}
on nonzero vectors over

\mathbb{F}_{q}
, defined by minimizing the Hamming distance over scalar
multiples of one argument. The function
\operatorname{angle}_{H}
satisfies the
metric axioms up to scalar equivalence and descends to a genuine
integer-valued metric on the projective space
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n})
. As
an application, we showed that the triangle inequality of
\operatorname{angle}_{H}

gives a clean angular analog of classical unique decoding
(Theorem
5.4
).

Several directions invite further study.

(1)

Higher-dimensional angles.
The angle between a vector
and a
k
-dimensional projective subspace, or between two such
subspaces, is the natural Grassmannian counterpart of
\operatorname{angle}_{H}
. This
should connect
\operatorname{angle}_{H}
to existing work on Grassmann codes and
subspace designs
[
2
]
.

(2)

Affine
\operatorname{angle}_{H}
.
Adapting
\operatorname{angle}_{H}
to capture

affine
lines (in particular, sets of the form

\{f+zg:z\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\}
) would provide an angular language for the
proximity-gap problem itself, not just for its projective shadow.

(3)

Other metric vector spaces.
The construction of
\operatorname{angle}_{H}

from
d_{H}
uses only the scalar-invariance of
\operatorname{wt}_{H}
. Any
metric vector space whose norm is invariant under nonzero scalars
admits an analogous angular metric. It would be interesting to study
such constructions over rings or for non-Hamming metrics
(rank metric, Lee metric, etc.).
