Experimental investigation on the stable operating range of a two-stroke aviation kerosene engine at idle based on cyclic variation analysis
Authors: Hui Li, Zhiqiang Han, Jian Wang, Jiangeng Li, Buwei Deng, Lei Hou, Zinong Zuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe excess air ratio and intake temperature significantly influence the cyclic variation of a two-stroke aviation kerosene engine under idle conditions. Characterizing their effects on combustion stability is essential for optimizing idle control strategies. Aiming at the problem of insufficient description of idle combustion stability of aviation kerosene two-stroke engine, this study experimentally investigated a two-stroke engine at 2300 r/min mounted on a dynamometer test bench, with intake temperatures ranging from 0 to 40 °C and excess air ratios from 0.7 to 0.9. Cylinder pressure was measured to calculate the coefficient of variation of indicated mean effective pressure (COVIMEP) and maximum cylinder pressure (COVPmax). Results demonstrate that COVIMEP first decreases and then increases as the mixture enriches, reaching a minimum at an excess air ratio of 0.8, whereas COVPmax increases monotonically with enrichment. Higher intake temperature improves mean IMEP and Pmax while reducing their COV values. Notably, the misfire boundaries identified by COVIMEP and COVPmax differ. The region with COVIMEP exceeding 10% occupies only 6% of the entire test range, while COVPmax-based unstable combustion occurs under two scenarios: high intake temperature with rich mixture, and low intake temperature with lean mixture. Therefore, appropriate evaluation parameters should be selected according to specific control objectives for idle speed regulation.Keywords: Aviation kerosene, Idle condition, Two-stroke, Cyclic variationSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering

Introduction

In recent years, the market scale of Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) has experienced sustained annual growth. Small-power two-stroke piston engines, which are favored for their high power-to-weight ratio, simple structure, and low cost, have been widely adopted in small and medium-sized UAVs1–3. In terms of fuel selection, given the flammability and explosive hazards associated with gasoline, aviation kerosene has attracted considerable attention for its application in small aviation piston engines. This interest stems from its high flash point, energy density comparable to that of gasoline, and high utilization rate within the aviation fuel sector4–7.

Compared with gasoline, aviation kerosene exhibits higher viscosity and poorer atomization characteristics. In engines equipped with port fuel injection (PFI), fuel droplets with greater mass tend to condense into a liquid film upon contact with cold intake port walls. The poor evaporation performance of this heavy fuel film results in deviations between the actual and theoretical air-fuel ratios, which in turn leads to deteriorated combustion in two-stroke engines under idle conditions8. In response to the atomization and combustion challenges associated with aviation kerosene, Liu et al.9 reported that its combustion performance and heat release rate are inferior to those of gasoline under low-load conditions, and that the average indicated mean effective pressure (IMEP) when burning RP-3 fuel is significantly lower than that observed with gasoline. Liao et al.10 investigated the effects of injection pressure and secondary injection on the mixture formation and combustion characteristics of aviation kerosene and air. Their findings indicate that increasing injection pressure can accelerate fuel evaporation, yet it also leads to an increase in the mass of liquid film deposited on the combustion chamber wall. Moreover, excessively delaying the second injection timing tends to impair fuel evaporation, increase the mixture concentration gradient, and ultimately degrade combustion performance. Hooper et al.11 found that preheating the engine cylinder using glow plug technology serves as an effective method to enhance the evaporation and atomization of low-volatility fuels. The aforementioned studies have primarily focused on improving the atomization and evaporation of aviation kerosene in two-stroke engines as a means to investigate its combustion characteristics.

Under low-load operating conditions of two-stroke engines, particularly at idle when the throttle is nearly closed, the intake pressure is considerably lower than that under normal operating conditions. In this state, airflow and fuel diffusion are weak, in-cylinder charge fluctuations are significant, and the residual exhaust gas within the cylinder is excessive. Moreover, the low combustion temperature contributes to poor combustion performance and notable fluctuations in engine speed12. The use of aviation kerosene further exacerbates the combustion cyclic variation in PFI two-stroke engines under idle conditions.

Numerous studies have investigated cyclic variations in internal combustion engines. Most of the existing studies use the coefficient of variation parameter to characterize the dispersion of in-cylinder combustion characteristics in each cycle distribution. Wang et al.13 examined the characteristics of multi-cylinder combustion cyclic variations in a gasoline HCCI engine under different combustion modes. Their findings revealed that the in-cylinder cyclic variation for homogeneous charge compression ignition (HCCI) remained below 2%, and cyclic variations were primarily governed by the intake air quantity. Fang et al.14 investigated the cyclic variations associated with methanol-diesel dual-fuel combustion under plateau conditions. The results showed that as altitude increased, the COVPmax rose significantly. This was attributed to low in-cylinder temperatures under low-load conditions, combined with the cooling effect of methanol on intake temperature, which extended the ignition delay period. Consequently, a larger quantity of mixture was formed during this extended delay, leading to rapid combustion upon ignition and resulting in increased COVPmax.

In studies examining cyclic variations in two-stroke engines, Amin et al.15,16 improved combustion stability by employing exhaust gas recirculation (EGR). With an internal EGR rate of 30%, the COVPmax was 5.64%, whereas with an external EGR rate of 25%, COVPmax reached 6.67%. In the absence of EGR, the coefficient of variation of indicated mean effective pressure (COVIMEP) for a two-stroke engine could rise to 22.14%, and COVPmax could reach as high as 11.8%. Similarly, Ma et al.17 investigated the combustion characteristics and cyclic variation of an opposed-piston two-stroke gasoline direct injection engine and analyzed the effect of engine speed on cyclic variation. Their findings showed that COVIMEP increased with engine speed; at 2000 rpm, the minimum COVIMEP was 4.187%. Furthermore, at engine speeds of 2000 rpm, 3000 rpm, and 4000 rpm, the COVPmaxs were 7.406%, 7.919%, and 7.667%, respectively. Zhang et al.18 found that under different injection pressures, COVIMEP exhibited a “double valley” trend with variations in injection timing. Han et al.19 proposed a method for determining the stable combustion boundary of a multi-cylinder two-stroke engine based on cyclic fluctuations and inter-cylinder differences. Hassan et al.20 studied the combustion characteristics of hydrogen and nature gas blends in a two-stroke engine and found that as the hydrogen ratio increased to 30%, COVIMEP decreased by 23%, 10%, and 7% under 40%, 60%, and 70% load conditions, respectively. From the collective findings of these studies, it is evident that the cyclic variations for various combustion indicators in two-stroke engines are substantially higher than those observed in four-stroke engines.

Differences in mixture concentration and temperature across combustion cycles can contribute to the exacerbation of cyclic variations in two-stroke engines under idle conditions. Variations in intake temperature resulting from different altitudes can inevitably intensify combustion cyclic variations. Furthermore, in UAVs equipped with hybrid power systems, the electric motor provides power output during flight mode transitions. As the engine cannot be restarted in mid-air, it must be capable of sustained idle operation without stalling21. Regarding the influence of mixture temperature and concentration on combustion cyclic variations in two-stroke engines, several investigations have been conducted. Liu et al.9 employed a crankcase injection method to address challenges associated with uneven mixture formation, with the aim of improving engine performance under low-load conditions. Through comparative analysis of simulation and experimental results, Ji et al.22 demonstrated that cyclic variations in engine idle speed and IMEP were mitigated through hydrogen enrichment, suggesting that this approach serves as an effective method for improving engine idle performance under lean-burn conditions. Hu et al.23 investigated the effects of ignition advance angle, injection timing, and excess air ratio on the combustion characteristics of aviation kerosene and gasoline. Their findings indicated that under idle conditions, aviation kerosene requires a richer mixture for stable combustion, and due to its slower combustion rate, the crank angle corresponding to maximum cylinder pressure must be advanced relative to that of gasoline to achieve improved power output. Zhang et al.24 examined the effects of injection advance angle, ignition advance angle, and fuel injection quantity on cylinder pressure. The results demonstrated that with excess air ratios within the range of 0.6 to 0.8, the engine achieved rapid and smooth starting, accompanied by low COVPmax. Di Martino et al.25 suggested that combustion variability under idle conditions is inherently attributable to cyclic variations in mixture motion, the quantity of air and fuel delivered to the cylinder, and the quality of their mixing. Idle stability is also influenced by the air-fuel ratio, with richer mixtures contributing to reduced fluctuations in combustion state under idle conditions.

Therefore, mixture concentration and temperature exert a significant influence on combustion cyclic variations. However, existing studies have seldom focused on the combustion stability of aviation kerosene-fueled two-stroke engines under idle conditions across varying mixture concentrations and intake temperatures. Consequently, there remains a lack of theoretical and experimental data to support idle speed control and combustion optimization for two-stroke aviation kerosene engines. Therefore, this study focuses on addressing the insufficient description of idle combustion stability in aviation kerosene two-stroke engines. This study aims to investigate the idle stability characteristics of a dual-cylinder two-stroke aviation kerosene engine under different intake temperatures and excess air ratios, to analyze and elucidate the phenomena associated with combustion stability under idle conditions, and to provide a theoretical foundation for the control of combustion stabilities at idle.

Experimental setup and test conditions

Experimental setup

The experimental system employed in this study is comprised of an intake temperature control system, an engine measurement and control system, a data acquisition system, and a FQ170 opposed dual-cylinder two-stroke engine. In this study, the engine was mounted on a gantry frame and connected to the dynamometer via a high-elasticity carbon fiber shaft. The engine adopted PFI, with the injector mounted on the throttle body and a fuel injection pressure of 3 bar.

Test conditions and fuel

The test conditions are summarized in Table 3. The experimental parameters included a rotational speed of 2300 r/min and intake temperatures ranging from 0 ℃ to 40 ℃. The test fuel employed was RP-3 aviation kerosene, the physical and chemical properties of which are provided in Table 4. During the experiments, a 50:1 volume ratio blend of aviation kerosene and lubricating oil was used to satisfy the lubrication requirements of the engine. The mass of fuel delivered to the crankcase was regulated by controlling the injection pulse width at the intake. Since the scenario under investigation is the idle condition of a two-stroke engine, in order to obtain the overall change trend of combustion stability in this scenario, it is necessary to investigate the engine mixture concentration from the lean misfire state to the partial combustion state and then to the rich misfire state. Therefore, the excess air ratio in this study was maintained within a range of 0.7 to 0.9.

The coefficient of variation (COV) is a normalized measure that directly reflects the degree of dispersion of the probability distribution of statistical data. To investigate the combustion stability of the horizontally opposed dual-cylinder two-stroke engine under idle conditions, this parameter is widely regarded as the best parameter for evaluating combustion cyclic variations, and using the COV to quantitatively assess cyclic variation is commonly accepted in various studies26–28. A COV of more than 10% for key parameters would lead to poor vehicle drivability29,30. Therefore, in this study, COVi = 10% is adopted as the boundary threshold for evaluating combustion stability31.

The in-cylinder combustion parameters selected in this study were peak cylinder pressure (Pmax), the crank angle corresponding to Pmax, and IMEP, which were used to evaluate the idle combustion stability. IMEP represents the useful work output per unit of displaced volume, directly reflecting the engine’s work capacity under that operating condition. Peak pressure is an indicator used to evaluate the combustion state and mechanical load of an internal combustion engine. Existing research suggests that Pmax is highly sensitive to cyclic variations32. Hence, this study uses IMEP and Pmax as characterization parameters for two main aspects of combustion characteristics in two-stroke idle conditions: combustion state and work capacity.

Results and discussion

The influence of excess air ratio on cyclic variation

Figure 2 presents the distribution of IMEP over 50 consecutive combustion cycles at an intake temperature of 20 ℃ under varying excess air ratios. The obtained results indicate that as the excess air ratio decreases, the COVIMEP exhibits a trend of decreasing from an excess air ratio of 0.9 to 0.8 and increasing from an excess air ratio of 0.8 to 0.7. The variation of IMEP with excess air ratio is opposite to that of COVIMEP. A minimum COVIMEP of 5.58% was observed at an excess air ratio of 0.8. When the mixture was enriched from an excess air ratio of 0.9 to 0.8, the in-cylinder mixture transitioned from an excessively lean state, which was insufficient to maintain stable idle operation, to a comparatively more optimal concentration. This change resulted in a reduction in COVIMEP from 7.13% to 5.58%. At an excess air ratio of 0.7, however, COVIMEP reached 12.27%. At this time, the increase in mixture concentration deteriorated the combustion state in the cylinder and affected the combustion stability, resulting in a pronounced increase in combustion cyclic variation. Under this excess air ratio, the mixture was excessively rich, which adversely affected fuel atomization and vaporization, contributing to combustion instability. Unstable combustion led to deterioration of the overall engine performance under this condition. Furthermore, the mean IMEP decreased from 0.2520 MPa to 0.2361 MPa under this condition.

COVIMEP with different excess air ratios.

Figure 3 presents the Pmax distribution over 50 consecutive combustion cycles at an intake temperature of 20 ℃ under different excess air ratios. The results indicate that COVPmax increases as the excess air ratio decreases. Pmax increases when the excess air ratio decreases from 0.9 to 0.8, and decreases when the excess air ratio decreases from 0.8 to 0.7. At an excess air ratio of 0.9, the engine exhibits the lowest COVPmax, at 9.75%. As the mixture is enriched to an excess air ratio of 0.8, COVPmax increases to 9.89%, approaching the stability boundary. However, the overall difference within this operating range is small, with a Pmax difference of only 0.08 MPa. When the mixture concentration is increased further, combustion performance deteriorates sharply. Pmax decreases from 1.48 MPa at an excess air ratio of 0.8 to 1.27 MPa at an excess air ratio of 0.7, and COVPmax increases from 9.89 to 12.1%. Efficient combustion requires fuel accumulation over multiple cycles. At this stage, when the oil film accumulation rate in the crankcase exceeds the evaporation rate, the oil film continues to accumulate, and the evaporation rate decreases with the increase in oil film thickness. This prevents the fuel in the crankcase from reaching an ideal homogeneous state, causing the engine to tend towards misfire.

COVPmax with different excess air ratios.

Figure 4 presents the frequency distribution of the crank angles (CA) corresponding to Pmax at intake temperature of 20 ℃ under varying excess air ratios. As the mixture concentration increased, the average CA at which Pmax occurred shifted progressively closer to top dead center (TDC), the distribution of these CAs exhibited a progressively broader dispersion, and the peak frequency associated with the CA occurrence decreased. The primary reason for this observed phenomenon is that with the increase in aviation kerosene injection mass, the quantity of atomized and evaporated fuel increased correspondingly, thereby elevating the mixture concentration actively participating in the combustion reaction. Consequently, the mixture was able to transition more rapidly into the phase of fast combustion, resulting in the earlier occurrence of Pmax.

The influence of intake temperature on energy utilization efficiency

Figure 5 presents the distribution of IMEP over 50 consecutive combustion cycles at intake temperatures of 0 ℃, 20 ℃, and 40 ℃, with the engine operating at 2300 r/min. As the intake temperature increased, in-cylinder combustion performance improved, resulting in higher effective work output. COVIMEP decreased as the intake temperature increased from 0 to 40 ℃. The average IMEP decreased slightly from 0.2544 MPa to 0.252 MPa when the intake temperature increased from 0 to 20 ℃, and increased to 0.2671 MPa when the intake temperature increased from 20 to 40 ℃. With elevated intake temperature, the rate of kerosene evaporation within the crankcase increased. At a given fuel injection mass, a greater quantity of aviation kerosene participated in the combustion process through atomization and evaporation. Combustion that had previously required fuel accumulation over multiple cycles to achieve high efficiency could instead be achieved within a single cycle due to the formation of a more optimally mixed charge. Consequently, COVIMEP gradually decreased from 7.01% to 5.4%, indicating that the engine tended toward more stable combustion under these conditions. Compared with the intake temperature increase from 20 to 40 ℃, IMEP showed only slight changes when the intake temperature increased from 0 to 20 ℃. COVIMEP exhibited a slight change in the intake temperature range of 20 ℃ to 40 ℃. This shows that with the increase in intake temperature, the optimization of the combustion state exhibits two stages of change. In the range of 0 to 20 ℃, the change in the combustion state is mainly reflected in the convergence of the work output. In the range of 20 to 40 ℃, IMEP began to increase as the combustion state became less discrete.

COVIMEP at different intake temperatures.

Figure 6 presents the distribution of Pmax over 50 consecutive combustion cycles at intake temperatures of 0 ℃, 20 ℃, and 40 ℃, with the engine operating at 2300 r/min. Under the same injection pulse width, as the intake temperature increased, the average Pmax exhibited a generally increasing trend, rising from 1.31 to 1.48 MPa and then slightly decreasing to 1.44 MPa. In terms of cyclic variation, COVPmax increased from 9.61% at 0 ℃ to 10.02% at 20 ℃ and then decreased to 8.99% at 40 ℃. The changing trend of Pmax with the increase in intake temperature is different with that of IMEP. When the intake temperature increased from 0 to 20 ℃, the improvement in the combustion state led to an overall increase in combustion roughness, and the dispersion degree of Pmax showed a certain divergence. When the intake temperature was further increased to 40 ℃, the improvement in work output was reflected in the reduction of Pmax dispersion in terms of combustion roughness.

COVPmax at different intake temperatures.

At intake temperatures of 0 ℃, 20 ℃, and 40 ℃, the corresponding crankcase temperatures under idle conditions are approximately 55 ℃, 85 ℃, and 115 ℃, respectively. Figure 7 illustrates the evaporation rate of aviation kerosene within the crankcase at different engine speeds, along with the corresponding crankcase temperatures associated with intake temperatures of 0 ℃, 20 ℃, and 40 ℃. At low engine speeds, although the mixing time was longer, the fuel adhering to the crankcase walls could not evaporate effectively as a result of the low engine temperature. At approximately 2000 rpm, only about 90% of the injected fuel was able to participate in mixture formation. These findings corroborate the previously discussed trends in the average values and variation rates of Pmax and IMEP at different intake temperatures.

The influence of excess air ratio and intake temperature on the distribution of cyclic fluctuation

Figures 9 and 10 present the distribution of COVIMEP and the corresponding average IMEP values under varying intake temperatures and excess air ratios, with the engine operating at 2300 r/min. As shown in Fig. 9, the region corresponding to a COVIMEP of less than 10% progressively contracted toward an excess air ratio of 0.8 as the intake temperature decreased. The area characterized by a COVIMEP exceeding 10% accounted for only 6% of the entire test range, indicating that favorable idle stability can be achieved at this engine speed. Under these operating conditions, the IMEP remained above the misfire threshold, with values exceeding 0.24 MPa, suggesting a substantial margin for effective work output.

Figures 11 and 12 delineate the distribution of COVPmax and the corresponding average Pmax values under varying intake temperatures and excess air ratios, with the engine operating at 2300 r/min. Pmax serves as an indicator of the in-cylinder combustion state and its associated intensity, commonly referred to as combustion roughness. Owing to the pronounced influence of the scavenging process on combustion in two-stroke engines, the overall COVPmax observed in the tested engine remained closer to the 10% threshold compared to that typically observed in four-stroke engines. Across the entire range of intake temperatures and excess air ratios examined, the unstable combustion region identified on the basis of COVPmax was found to be more extensive than that defined by COVIMEP. Overall, as intake temperature decreased, the stable combustion region initially contracted and subsequently expanded. This observed phenomenon is attributable to the combined effect of low intake temperature and excessive cyclic fuel injection, which led to the accumulation of oil film within the crankcase under conditions of approximately 0 °C intake temperature and an excess air ratio of 0.7. Consequently, an increased number of cycles within the cylinder entered a state of partial misfire, thereby resulting in a concurrent decrease in both Pmax and COVPmax. The discrepancy in the boundary trends between COVPmax and COVIMEP within this region primarily arises from the differing sensitivity with which combustion state parameters, represented by Pmax, and performance parameters, represented by IMEP, reflect variations in environmental conditions. Under partial misfire conditions, the combustion of the locally formed mixture remains capable of generating a measurable quantity of useful work. As a result, IMEP exhibits comparatively lower sensitivity to unstable combustion than does Pmax, a parameter that directly and immediately reflects the combustion process.

Therefore, through a comprehensive analysis of the average values and variation rates of both Pmax and IMEP across the investigated operating range, it was determined that the tested two-stroke aviation kerosene piston engine operated in a partial combustion state at an intake temperature of 0 °C. When selecting an appropriate control strategy for idle conditions, it is necessary to avoid operating regions characterized by an excess air ratio below 0.75 and an intake temperature below 30 °C. Moreover, as discrepancies exist between the stability boundaries derived from Pmax and those derived from IMEP, it is essential to select suitable parameter constraints for analysis based on the specific control objectives.

Conclusions

In this study, experimental investigations and phenomenological analyses were conducted on the cyclic variation characteristics of a two-stroke aviation kerosene piston engine under idle conditions at varying intake temperatures and excess air ratios, employing Pmax and IMEP as the primary evaluation parameters. The main conclusions are drawn as follows:

Under the idle conditions of the two-stroke engine, the COVIMEP first decreased and then increased as the mixture transitioned from lean to rich conditions. An optimal excess air ratio existed at each intake temperature, thereby minimizing the cyclic variation of the engine during idle operation. As the intake temperature decreased, the misfire limit progressively contracted toward the optimal excess air ratio from both ends of the excess air ratio range.

Under the idle conditions of the two-stroke engine, the COVIMEP first decreased and then increased as the mixture transitioned from lean to rich conditions. An optimal excess air ratio existed at each intake temperature, thereby minimizing the cyclic variation of the engine during idle operation. As the intake temperature decreased, the misfire limit progressively contracted toward the optimal excess air ratio from both ends of the excess air ratio range.

Notable differences were observed in the misfire limits derived from analyses based on COVIMEP and COVPmax. This discrepancy arises because IMEP and Pmax characterize distinct aspects of engine performance and exhibit markedly different sensitivities to variations in both the excess air ratio and intake temperature. Therefore, appropriate evaluation parameters should be selected in accordance with the specific control objectives when implementing idle speed control strategies for the engine.

In contrast, the areas with a COVPmax of more than 10% were observed in two cases: a high excess air ratio (rich mixture) combined with a high intake temperature, and a low excess air ratio (lean mixture) combined with a low intake temperature. This is mainly because the optimal excess air ratio shifts toward the lean side with an increase in intake temperature.
