A Scoping Review on Strategies for Navigating Conflicting Rights Between Safety and Autonomy in Residential Long‐Term Care in the United States
Authors: Angela K Perone, Leyi Zhou, Ann Glusker, Jiwon Shin, Christina Xu, Poorvi Thairani, Yuka Iriguchi, Joseph George, Kati Khov, Ali Abud, Wendy Shu Peng, Manan Dua, Amy Jillian Wallrath, Anthony Chicotel, Lori Walsh, Meredith Chillemi, Jessica King
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundOlder adults in the United States increasingly require residential long‐term care, where staff must frequently navigate complex conflicts between residents' rights to safety and autonomy. Despite the prevalence of these ethical dilemmas and the potential for legal or professional harm, guidance for staff remains scarce. This scoping review identifies and synthesizes existing empirical strategies used to resolve these conflicts within U.S. residential long‐term care.MethodsFollowing the Arksey and O'Malley framework, a search was conducted across three academic databases (ProQuest Social Sciences, PubMed, and Scopus) for peer‐reviewed, empirical studies published in English between 1987 and 2024. Two independent reviewers screened articles based on their focus on safety and autonomy conflicts and the inclusion of resolution strategies. Data were extracted from 14 final articles to categorize topics and solutions.ResultsThe review identified 14 empirical studies covering topics such as fall prevention, dementia care, sexual expression, and daily routines. Strategies were synthesized into five categories: Innovation (new tools/policies), Compromise (balancing values), Advocacy (defending preferences), Reflection (team discussions), and Education (training, information). Innovation was the most frequently used strategy (8 of 14 studies), while education was the least utilized but most frequently suggested for future implementation.ConclusionsA significant research gap exists regarding evidence‐based strategies for navigating safety‐autonomy conflicts. These five categories provide guidance for clinical care and policymakers to understand the diversity of ways staff can address conflicting rights. More research is needed on how residential long‐term care staff understand and address conflicting rights on the ground and how to best disseminate these strategies more broadly.Keywords: assisted living, education, ethics, liability, nursing homes, regulations

Introduction

Older adults in the United States are increasingly needing long‐term care [1, 2]. Residential long‐term care provides long‐term care services and supports for physical, cognitive, or mental conditions in a residential setting, usually in assisted living or nursing homes [3]. While assisted living is predominantly regulated by states [3], nursing homes represent one of the most heavily federally regulated industries in the United States [4]. These regulations emerged from an era of significant abuse and neglect of care recipients and aim to enhance quality of care and outline resident rights [5]. However, the sheer volume of regulations and patient rights has created a new quandary for residential long‐term care: how do care practitioners respond when these rights and obligations conflict? Researchers and practitioners have long identified the harms associated with potential conflicts between residents' rights to safety and autonomy in nursing homes, including using monitoring technologies in long‐term dementia care [6, 7], caring for frail older adults [8] and older adults with fall risks [9], and caring for older adults with learning disabilities [10, 11]. In scholarly literature, including bioethical scholarship, safety tends to refer to regulations that require a safe environment for residents (and sometimes staff and visitors), which can be linked to resident comfort [12] or a professional obligation to protect residents from harm [13]. Autonomy tends to refer to resident choice or preferences [14], self‐determination [15], or independence to make decisions that assert one's commitments, values and life plans [16].

Bioethicists and philosophical literature have presented important questions around these concepts, including nuances in decision‐making around autonomy or safety in residential long‐term care [17, 18]. However, more research is needed that addresses the practical realities (and potential solutions) when these rights conflict. When regulatory safety and patient autonomy conflict, staff must resolve these tensions. While federal and state regulations require residential long‐term care communities to respect the safety and autonomy rights of residents, they lack guidance for how staff should respond when rights and regulations conflict. This gap in clarity has significant impacts on care quality and equity in these spaces, potentially resulting in harm to the resident, liability for the care provider or staff, and/or disciplinary actions (e.g., sanctions, penalties, loss of license, employment termination and loss of livelihood).

This scoping review emerged from practitioners, advocates, and scholars of long‐term care who reached out to the first author at conferences and informal meetings over the past 10 years asking—what should staff do when these conflicts arise? This scoping review is thus part of a larger empirical project examining conflicting rights in long‐term care that stems from research approaches using community‐derived questions on the state of the scholarly literature [17, 19]. This paper represents a smaller scoping review examining strategies for resolving conflicting rights between safety and autonomy in residential long‐term care in the United States.

A scoping review is appropriate, given that the state of the literature on this topic remains unknown, especially around how staff are navigating these conflicts, and general concepts and categories around potential resolutions remain ill‐defined [20]. While relevant academic databases were systematically searched, a scoping review was deemed appropriate to provide more focus to this area of study, to qualitatively assess the state of literature, to outline conceptual and methodological gaps in this area of research [21], and to pose conceptual categories to frame subsequent research and clarify conceptual boundaries on conflicting rights in long‐term care [20].

Methods

This scoping review followed Arksey and O'Malley's five‐step framework [22], as further refined by Levac, Colquhoun, and O'Brien [23], to ensure rigor in the study search, selection, and data analysis processes. The team of authors includes researchers with expertise in scoping reviews, including a trained academic librarian at a top research institution. The study was part of a larger project examining literature answering the following research questions: What conflicting rights/ethical issues in residential long‐term care have been identified in the scholarly literature? What strategies have residential long‐term care staff employed to address conflicting rights/ethical issues? This subproject specifically included literature examining conflicting rights/ethical issues between safety and autonomy in residential long‐term care in the United States.

Search Strategy and Eligibility Criteria

The study search in this scoping review included peer‐reviewed articles in English published from January 1, 1987 through October 1, 2024, with the background that 1987 was when the Nursing Home Reform Act was passed as part of the Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1987 (OBRA‐87). The search was conducted in three databases: ProQuest Social Sciences (covering PsycINFO, Sociological Abstracts, and Social Services Abstracts), PubMed, and Scopus, with search terms covering three main themes: (1) Residential long‐term care: nursing homes, assisted living, skilled nursing facilities; (2) Older adults: elder, aging, senior citizen; (3) Conflicting rights: ethical issues, ethical dilemmas, safety, autonomy, human rights. The team included an academic librarian (and co‐author) with expertise in systematic reviews who helped inform these criteria.

Article inclusion criteria were: (1) Study design: Empirical study design using qualitative, quantitative, or mixed methods. (2) Topic and setting: Focus on topics about conflicting rights or ethical dilemmas related to safety and autonomy in residential‐based long‐term care settings in the United States and must include solution strategies for resolving conflicting rights. (3) Target population: The study sample could include residents or workers, but the safety and autonomy conflicting rights must be related to older adult residents (age ≥ 50).

Studies were excluded if they were reviews, perspectives, commentaries, or study protocols (no outcomes reported); focused on residential long‐term care for youth or young adults; addressed only one ethical issue instead of opposing ethical issues or conflicts; were not about safety and autonomy; included no solutions for resolving conflicting rights; were published outside the study timeframe (January 1, 1987 through October 1, 2024); were not published in English; addressed care outside the United States, or had no full text available.

Study Selection

We used the Rayyan platform to conduct abstract and full‐text screening. We used Rayyan for duplicate removal, blinded title and abstract screening, and conflict resolution. While various software platforms (including Rayyan) have increasingly incorporated artificial intelligence (AI) tools, we did not use AI features for this study. Instead, we used Rayyan as a review management tool that allows independent reviewers to enter judgments manually to make it easier to compare decisions and resolve conflicts among the reviewers. Nine student reviewers assisted in this process (J.S., C.X., P.T., Y.I., J.G., K.K., A.A., W.S.P., A.J.W.), given the breadth of the larger study and that one of this project's goals was training students in this methodological tool for scholarly inquiry. All reviewers participated in extensive training, regular team meetings, and guidance by scholars experienced in this method, including an academic librarian. After downloading results and removing duplicates, 1126 items were included for title and abstract screening. The nine student research assistants were divided into three teams and worked together on screening; each study was reviewed independently by two student research assistants, and a third student in each team served as a reviewer to resolve conflicts. For the 270 articles included in full‐text screening, one student helped with downloading full‐text PDFs, and the other eight students worked in four pairs to conduct the reviews. The first author resolved conflicts and finalized 14 articles for data extraction.

Data Extraction and Synthesis

Data extraction was conducted by the student reviewers using Microsoft Excel and reviewed by first author A.K.P. and second author L.Z. Data collected included study location and setting, sample characteristics, definitions, and descriptions of safety and autonomy, and descriptions of solutions.

Results

Of the 1474 articles that were identified, 348 were removed before screening as duplicates, which left 1126 articles remaining. Of that number, 856 were excluded through initial screening as not meeting the inclusionary criteria. After the remaining articles (n = 270) were assessed through a full‐text screening, only 14 articles remained for the scoping review. Of the 270 articles, 80 were excluded as the wrong topic for not addressing solutions, 60 were excluded for the wrong topic for not addressing safety and autonomy, 43 were excluded for the wrong geographic location (outside the United States), 35 were excluded as the wrong article type (not an empirical study), 16 were excluded as articles in languages other than English, and 1 was excluded for wrong setting (in community hospital instead of nursing home). We were unable to obtain the full text of 21 articles, which were thus excluded. After this screening, 14 articles remained. See Figure 1: PRISMA Diagram.

Of the 14 articles included, 11 were qualitative [13, 14, 16, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31], including five case studies [13, 25, 28, 30, 31], two were quantitative [32, 33], and one was a mixed‐methods study [34].

Safety and Autonomy Topics

The 14 articles covered a range of topics under the umbrella of safety and autonomy. The earliest articles addressed the concept of safety and autonomy more generally in the context of ethics committees [32] and nursing home routines [16, 24]. Another earlier article addressed physical restraints used to prevent falls that could limit autonomy [25]. Another article characterized the core issue(s) relating to privacy and examined staff's focus on safety and efficiency over resident privacy and dignity to make decisions about personal care and activities [24]. One article addressed fire risks associated with resident smoking (safety) versus residents' autonomy to smoke [33]. Another article focused on transfer decisions and concerns about medical urgency based on safety that may conflict with a resident or family's desire to keep a resident at home [26]. The most recent article addressed conflicts with surveillance technology that can help prevent falls and ensure medication adherence but potentially at the expense of resident autonomy and overall personal decision‐making [27].

Several articles addressed safety and autonomy in the context of daily routines, especially around food [13, 14, 28]. One article addressed conflicts between residents' preferences or choices around daily routines (e.g., food choice, sleep time, bathing) that may conflict with what staff consider resident safety, including personal hygiene and safety associated with routine [14]. One article specifically focused on eating and concerns about dysphagia (difficulty swallowing foods or liquids) that foreground conflicts with resident choice around foods and their safety [13]. Another article addressed conflicts associated with residents refusing food as a form of death with dignity [28].

Concerns about safety and autonomy regarding dementia and cognitive impairment were foregrounded in four articles [29, 30, 31, 34]. Two articles addressed sexual expression and challenges arising when staff are worried about resident safety around sexual consent, particularly when cognitive impairment is a concern [29, 34]. One article addressed informed consent about medical care, surrogate selection, feeding, hydration, and end‐of‐life for residents with cognitive impairments [31]. Another article discussed concerns about protocols that ban anticholinergic drugs despite a resident's desire to use a particular medication for certain types of pain [30]. Types of Solutions

In this scoping review, we identified both solutions and suggestions for addressing conflicting rights. Solutions represented actual practices or policies that emerged on the ground and that were reported in the scholarly study. Suggestions were ideas proposed for the future that we wanted to capture in this scoping review. Five types of solutions emerged in the literature for navigating conflicting rights between safety and autonomy: innovation, compromise, advocacy, reflection, and education. As described more below, some articles used more than one solution, whereas others invoked only one of the five solutions. Based on the literature from this scoping review, we describe the five categories as follows: (1) Innovation: new policies, practices (e.g., giving frontline workers more time for oral care), or tools (e.g., new intake or assessment systems); (2) Compromise: taking action or making a decision that strikes a balance between two or more conflicting values or rights; (3) Advocacy: promoting or defending a resident's (or staff's) right to a particular choice or action; (4) Reflection: reflective practice or discussion that may lead to suggestions or solutions; (5) Education: information dissemination, training, or other informative activities for staff or family. Innovation

Eight of the 14 articles included some sort of new tool, practice, or policy to address conflicts between safety and autonomy [13, 14, 24, 25, 27, 31, 32, 34]. In the context of a more general article on safety and autonomy, one study found that resident autonomy was foregrounded when staff and residents acknowledged their personhood and established new practices of sharing personal life histories and milestones with each other [24].

Several articles discussed innovations around ethics committees, rounds, or experts [13, 31, 32]. In an article about a national study of 1278 nursing homes, 8% had established ethics committees (and 17% were considering forming one) to review conduct policies, provide advice on individual cases, and facilitate continued learning, including addressing issues relevant to safety and autonomy [32]. Another article discussed incorporating an “ethics rounds” as an alternative to an ethics committee, which comprised a medical director discussing one case per month with the care team, providing updates to the team, and incorporating subsequent commentary from an external ethicist [31]. One article discussed how staff resolved conflicts between safety and autonomy by incorporating new practices of bringing in ethics consultation and then developing new staff practices of allowing residents to eat preferred foods with risk‐reduction strategies (which also reflects compromise as noted below) [13].

Two articles addressed innovations in policy for specific safety and autonomy topics [25, 34]. In addressing concerns about physical restraints for fall prevention, one nursing home adopted a restraint‐free policy that incorporated case‐by‐case modifications (e.g., adjusting seating arrangements, guard rails) [25]. Several nursing homes concerned with sexual consent among dementia patients developed formal policies on intimacy, consent, and privacy in dementia care [34].

Two articles incorporated an innovative prototype or practice [14, 27]. The most recent article included a prototype for a new health monitoring system to help balance safety associated with falls and autonomy [27]. Another article described new practices that comprised culture change around resident decisions about their daily routines [14]. In this article, the researchers addressed concerns between resident choices around sleep times, food, and bathing that may conflict with what staff perceive as choices that enhance harm for the residents [14]. Nursing home staff conducted regular culture change meetings to discuss issues, educate staff, and encourage staff to support resident preferences around these daily routines [14].

Compromise

Six of the 14 articles included strategies for compromise when navigating conflicts between safety and autonomy [13, 14, 26, 27, 30, 33]. Compromise emerged when staff designated certain locations or colleagues to supervise residents who wanted to smoke [33], to help reach consensus among care staff about transfer decisions that posed conflicts around aging in place [26], to prioritize resident choice in using medication for pain relief that still incorporated some safety oversight [30], to incorporate a new health monitoring system that replaced direct surveillance with indirect, data‐driven information to prevent falls [27], to develop a diplomatic approach to balance resident choice around daily routines (e.g., sleep time, food, bathing) with minimizing resident harm [14], and to allow residents to eat preferred foods with risk‐reduction strategies (e.g., smaller bites, adaptive utensils) [13].

Advocacy

Four of the 14 articles discussed advocacy strategies [14, 16, 26, 29]. Advocacy arose when long‐term care ombudsmen advocated for the rights of residents with dementia to engage in sexual activity [29], when family members reached out to staff about resident preferences around daily routines, including sleep time [14], when residents were encouraged to self‐advocate about their autonomy [16], and when staff advocated for certain transfer decisions for residents to align with resident and family wishes and concerns about what was in the resident's best interest [26]. In the study about transfer decisions, advocacy occurred by enlisting a third party's support or targeted communication that tailored messages to obtain a desired outcome for a transfer [26].

Reflection

Five of the 14 articles explicitly discussed strategies of reflection [13, 26, 28, 30, 31]. Reflection occurred when staff engaged in processes to discuss and sometimes reach consensus about transfer decisions [26], decision‐making processes about medication for residents with dementia that involved reflections about the resident's functional ability and palliative context [30], multiple discussions about particular cases involving informed consent with residents who have dementia [31], team discussions on how to weigh various interventions that support autonomy and well‐being of a resident exploring death with dignity [28], and reflection with ethics consultation when a resident with medical restrictions wanted to eat preferred foods that may violate these restrictions [13].

Education

Four of the 14 articles involved strategies for education [13, 14, 27, 34]. Education included caregiver training on a new health monitoring device to prevent falls [27] staff training and sharing of information around sexual expression among residents with dementia [34], educating families around person‐centered care when engaging in culture change to support resident autonomy around daily routines [14], and education for staff and family around feeding tools that support residents' safely eating their preferred foods when concerns exist about their safety and eating [13].

Future Suggestions

Ten of the 14 articles included future suggestions on how to support staff, residents, and families when rights to safety and autonomy conflict [13, 14, 16, 26, 27, 29, 30, 32, 33, 34]. Future suggestions predominantly focused on education [13, 14, 27, 32, 34], and innovations in policy [33] and practice [13, 14, 16, 27, 29, 32]. One article encouraged more ethical reflection regarding medication management for residents with dementia [30]. Several articles also encouraged more research in particular methodologies (e.g., qualitative inquiry) [32] or topics [13, 26].

Discussion

Among the five strategies identified, the most frequently invoked solution category was innovation. Over half of the articles incorporated some sort of new tool, practice, or policy in its solution, which suggests that staff are regularly innovating on the ground. Some of the proposed innovations are less relevant, as state or federal policy has provided guidance on a particular topic. For example, in the context of physical restraints, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) updated its Guidance Manual for Long‐Term Care Facilities by clarifying that bed rails constitute an unlawful restraint [35]. This federal policy mostly aligns with the innovation on physical restraints in the scholarly article addressing this study that prohibited physical restraints but with some case‐by‐case modifications [25]. Innovation may also present avenues for supporting residents and staff around creatively foregrounding person‐centered approaches when one right may usurp another right (e.g., when autonomy may usurp safety).

Education was least invoked as a strategy for navigating conflicting rights, which was a surprising finding, given the emphasis on staff training in residential long‐term care [5] and research linking training to improved quality of care and resident quality of life [36, 37], staff retention [38], and greater awareness about or prevention of abuse and neglect [5, 39]. However, it was one of the most invoked suggestions for future implementation. This finding may also explain why sparse research exists on solutions regarding conflicting rights around safety and autonomy, despite it being a persistent issue in residential long‐term care. Staff may be addressing these issues in silos that could be hindering dissemination of innovative strategies within one or across multiple residential long‐term care settings in the United States. This scoping review underscores the need for more scholarly research that incorporates ways that staff are invoking education and training currently and how they could do so in the future to disseminate knowledge about strategies for addressing conflicts between safety and autonomy. More research could further explore how staff are using education to address concerns about potential harm and legal liability when autonomy is foregrounded over safety.

Four articles tended to focus on safety and autonomy conflicts in the context of dementia [29, 30, 31, 34], which underscores the centrality of these conflicts in dementia care. While we were surprised to not see dementia incorporated more in these fourteen articles, we observed that some articles expressly excluded participants with cognitive decline (thus possibly limiting discussion regarding dementia). It is also possible that dementia was less foregrounded, given the older publication dates of some of these articles, when dementia was less researched. As the overall number of people living with dementia continues to increase in the United States [40], more attention is needed to address complex nuances inherent to ensuring the safety and autonomy of residents with cognitive impairment. Given that people of color have higher rates of dementia [40], new protocols, policies, and trainings that address this issue should be mindful of how historical legacies and cultural norms have shaped how women and people of color have been perceived as needing to be protected (or not) and how choice has been historically contested (or not) with various groups.

This scoping review has several limitations. While the authors had hoped to use the CINAHL database, it was no longer available at the university libraries that the authors could access, which may have reduced access to otherwise relevant articles. At the methodological level, although this study employed paired screening and conflict resolution procedures, the screening and data extraction processes involved multiple student reviewers, which may have introduced variability in interpretation despite extensive training and supervision by senior authors. In addition, the analytic and synthesis approach inherent to scoping review methodology emphasizes breadth over depth, limiting the ability to examine specific implementation challenges, contextual factors, and unintended outcomes associated with the identified strategies. Another limitation is that this review only included articles from the United States, given that regulatory frameworks (and thus potential solutions) may be unique by country. However, this research team has another project comparing strategies among various countries that may provide additional guidance around conflicting rights for safety and autonomy.

Despite these limitations, this scoping review addresses an important and often overlooked area in residential long‐term care research: conflicting rights between safety and autonomy and the solutions that staff are identifying in navigating these conflicts. These conflicts are common on the ground, given the sheer volume of regulatory compliance for staff around safety and increasing concerns around liability from nursing homes in the United States [4] and a cultural shift in residential long‐term care for person‐centered care [5]. The solutions presented in this scoping review can also build on bioethical and philosophical literature around autonomy in residential long‐term care [17, 18] and serve as a foundation for subsequent empirical studies, practitioner guidance, and policy suggestions to help staff better address conflicting rights between safety and autonomy in residential long‐term care. This review also provides directions for future research, including more analysis of conflicting rights around safety and autonomy outside the United States, other areas of conflicting rights (e.g., privacy), and methodological approaches. Finally, this review has practical implications, including solutions that can be incorporated into existing infrastructure (e.g., team meetings, huddles, existing training) in the short‐term while also working to engage residents and care partners in more conversation and problem‐solving around these complex conflicts and ethical dilemmas.
