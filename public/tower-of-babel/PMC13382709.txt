Curative Oligometastatic Radiation Therapy: Why We Should Redefine Intent When Cure May Be Attainable
Authors: Jonathan B Wallach, Maria D Kelly, Ronald Shapiro, Mary McGunigal, Steve P Lee, Lori Hoffman-Hogg, Gabriela Wechsler, Ruchika Gutt, Veterans Health Administration Palliative Radiotherapy Task Force
Published: 2026

Hellman and Weichselbaum1 proposed the oligometastatic disease (OMD) state in their seminal 1995 editorial “Oligometastases,” reasoning that malignancy exists along a spectrum from localized to widely disseminated disease, with OMD existing in a restricted middle state in which cure or extended disease-free survival may still be attainable. Since the dual presentations of SABR-COMET and the oligometastatic non-small cell lung cancer phase 2 randomized controlled trials at the American Society for Radiation Oncology 2018 annual meeting, the paradigm of offering local radiation therapy (RT) for OMD has evolved rapidly, greenlighting numerous trials, including phase 3 trials.2,3 The Veterans Health Administration Palliative Radiotherapy Task Force recently surveyed 59 radiation oncologists and 47 medical oncologists working in Veterans Affairs facilities, assessing their perspectives regarding the treatment of OMD.4 As shown in Table 1, the 105 respondents to the question on whether high-dose RT is beneficial in appropriately selected cases responded affirmatively (100.0%). More surprisingly, most respondents (88.7%) indicated that RT for OMD contributes to cure (88.1% of radiation oncologists and 89.4% of medical oncologists; p = .84). Most radiation oncologists classified ≤5 lesions as OMD (as shown in Figure 1), whereas most medical oncologists classified ≤3 lesions as OMD (p = .006). Also, to our surprise, 52.9% of respondents (55.2% of radiation oncologists and 50.0% of medical oncologists; p = .60) indicated that local RT for OMD should not be limited by histology; the pancreatic adenocarcinoma EXTEND phase 2 trial provided some justification for a progression-free survival benefit and greenlighted the phase 3 EXPAND trial to test for an overall survival advantage, which is currently accruing patients.5,6

In total, 43.4% of physicians believed that a cure was possible, either by selecting choice 1 (n = 42) or choice 5, with their explanations in subsequent write-in comments (n = 4). This enthusiasm and optimism for treating with curative-intent in select OMD cases raises the question of whether OMD treatment should be subdivided into categories based on intent. In surgical oncology, treatment of OMD with clear curative-intent has been practiced for many decades, with the term “curative metastastectomy” appearing in the surgical literature at least as early as 1997, before becoming common thereafter.7 In patients with rectal adenocarcinoma with resectable liver metastases, a 2011 meta-analysis of 11 studies demonstrated a 10-year overall survival rate of 12% to 36%, with failure unlikely after 10 years, as reflected in the National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines’ recommendation for resection of liver- and/or lung-only metastases.8,9

The Palliative Radiotherapy Task Force would like to propose the new term “curative oligometastatic radiotherapy” (CORT) to reflect its inherent practical implications and to facilitate communication. CORT is distinct from metastasis-directed therapy in that the primary disease (if not previously eradicated) is addressed and there is necessarily a curative-intent; it is different from radical stereotactic ablative RT (SABR)/curative-intent SABR in referencing the oligometastatic state, and not all treatments need to be SABR. When CORT is administered, the radiation oncologist recognizes that the patient has OMD but believes the patient also has a nontrivial potential for cure, or at least very extended overall survival with durable local control. As a result, through shared decision-making with the patient, the cost-benefit analysis may shift toward administering aggressive treatments, with the radiation oncologist/patient agreeing to higher (“curative”) doses (in most situations, ablative/SABR) despite the potential toxicities. Other potential advantages of creating the CORT subclassification include reducing variability in OMD treatment across institutions, improving integration with definitive-intent systemic therapies, improving clinical trial grouping, enhancing data collection, standardizing terminology with “Surgical Oncology,” and perhaps even improving patient adherence to aggressive (but recommended) treatment protocols.

Patients who are candidates for CORT should meet most criteria consistent with long-term favorable outcomes, in which prior metastatic studies have demonstrated better oncologic outcomes. These criteria include:

The Palliative Radiotherapy Task Force will be applying this model in a point-based framework in future prospective trials. Through development and refinement of a CORT model, we can determine which patients have a high probability of cure or very long-term disease-free survival and thereby, merit the most aggressive CORT in a more algorithmic manner despite the inherent risk of toxicities. While we acknowledge that outcomes in OMD may appear unpredictable, we would like to attempt to bring more order to this treatment approach. In the future, the ongoing large-scale phase 3 studies SABR-COMET-3 (for 1-3 metastases), SABR-COMET-10 (for 4-10 metastases), and SABR-SYNC (for ≤10 metastases with an uncontrolled primary) will likely provide valuable data regarding which patients merit CORT.27, 28, 29 We recognize a limitation of this framework is that, at this time, it does not incorporate genomic signatures/genetic mutation profiles, which are an emerging area of prognostication for OMD.16,17

Shared decision-making with respective patients remains invaluable regarding balancing their potential for cure versus risks of toxicities, as OMD has varied presentations with difficult outcomes to predict. We hope that more precise terminology will better clarify treatment goals for patients and that the model will better enable tailoring treatment to an individual’s disease burden, prognosis, and resulting willingness to treat aggressively.
