The hidden needs of patients with diabetes in non-endocrinology departments for blood glucose management: a qualitative study
Authors: Wenshuai Song, Liping Xie, Huansheng Wang, Jingjing Teng, Xiaohe Ren, Hui Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with diabetes who are hospitalized in non‑endocrinology departments account for over 90% of all hospitalized patients with diabetes in China, yet their blood glucose management is often suboptimal and overshadowed by their primary diagnoses. The real‑life experiences and unmet needs of this population remain largely unexplored. This study aimed to explore the hidden needs of these patients regarding blood glucose management during hospitalization.MethodsThis descriptive qualitative study adhered to the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR). Using purposive sampling with maximum variation, we recruited 12 adult patients with diabetes from various non‑endocrinology departments (6 males, 6 females; age range 30–81 years; all type 2 except one with pregestational diabetes mellitus (PGDM)). The interview guide was informed by the COM‑B model (Capability, Opportunity, Motivation). Data were analyzed using inductive qualitative content analysis.ResultsFour main categories were identified: (1) Conflict between primary diagnosis and diabetes, (2) confusion regarding systematic blood glucose management, (3) barriers to self-management, and (4) the extra burdens. Collectively, these factors constitute hidden needs that remain inadequately identified and addressed within conventional specialty care pathways.ConclusionsPatients with diabetes in non-endocrinology departments experience profound hidden needs that are intertwined with their primary diagnoses and are routinely overlooked in current specialty focused care. Optimized management strategies must transcend mere blood glucose monitoring and medication adjustments, shifting toward patient-centered integrated care models. This requires systematic attention to response for patients’ economic and psychosocial needs, thereby effectively enhancing management outcomes and improving patient experience.Keywords: Diabetes, Non-endocrinology departments, Blood glucose management, Patient experience, Qualitative research

Introduction

Diabetes is a chronic disease that occurs either when the pancreas does not produce enough insulin or when the body cannot effectively use the insulin it produces [1]. Persistent hyperglycemia not only serves as a core trigger for multiple diabetes complications but also increases the risk of various adverse clinical events, including delayed wound healing, prolonged hospital stays, and elevated mortality rates by impairing physiological processes such as cardiovascular function and inflammatory responses [2, 3]. As a globally prevalent chronic metabolic disorder, diabetes has become a significant public health issue due to its high prevalence, high disability rate, and substantial healthcare burden [4]. According to the International Diabetes Federation, the global diabetes prevalence reached 589 million in 2024, resulting in 3.4 million deaths and incurring at least $1 trillion in healthcare expenditures [5]. Currently, China has the largest number of adult patients with diabetes and ranks second globally in total diabetes-related healthcare expenditures [6]. Research indicates that by 2024, China’s adult diabetes population accounted for one-quarter of the world’s total, reaching 148 million. This figure represents a more than sixfold increase from 22.6 million in 2000 over a span of two decades [6].

Hyperglycemia is prevalent among hospitalized patients and closely associated with adverse clinical outcomes. Statistics indicate that approximately 8.3% of hospitalized patients in China have diabetes, while the prevalence of hyperglycemia reaches as high as 29.6% [7]. However, over 90% of these patients are admitted to non-endocrinology departments, where glycemic management is significantly more challenging than in endocrinology departments, presenting unique difficulties [8, 9]. Research indicates that glycemic management standards for non-endocrinology patients are significantly lower than those in endocrinology departments, with poorer rates of glycemic management [10]. A survey by Sun et al. [11] further confirmed that in non-endocrinology departments, both the frequency of blood glucose monitoring and the overall level of glycemic management are suboptimal, highlighting an urgent need for improvement. For instance, postprandial glucose monitoring plays a crucial role in glycemic management. Patients achieving postprandial targets are more likely to reach HbA1c goals than those meeting only fasting targets [12]. Yet implementation rates remain low in Chinese non-endocrinology departments, with fewer than 40% of patients undergoing rigorous 2-hour postprandial glucose monitoring [11].

Suboptimal glycemic management significantly increases the risk of perioperative complications, average length of hospital stay, and adverse events in hospitalized surgical patients, as well as non-surgical inpatients [13, 14]. For surgical patients, the persistent hypoxic and hyperglycemic state in individuals with diabetes damages vascular smooth muscle function and integrity, creating a complex and adverse wound microenvironment, including the vascular microenvironment that impedes surgical wound healing [15]. For medical patients, elevated blood glucose levels also interfere with the management of other comorbidities, increasing the risk of infection and organ damage [16]. Evidently, the current state of blood glucose management among non-endocrinology inpatients is concerning. Its effectiveness not only directly impacts glycemic management itself but profoundly influences treatment outcomes, clinical prognosis, and healthcare efficiency.

From the perspective of management, most non-endocrinology inpatients with diabetes in China still use the combination of self-management of non-endocrinology department and consultation management of endocrine department as the main form of blood glucose management, and there are some defects [7, 10]. First, blood glucose management is merely one of many multidisciplinary challenges faced by non-endocrinologists, who often lack confidence and expertise in managing diabetes-related emergencies, determining appropriate medication dosage adjustments, and timing these interventions [17]. Second, widespread multidisciplinary collaboration issues persist, including delayed consultation response times, lack of shared and interoperable blood glucose data, insufficient human resources, and low management efficiency [18]. These factors collectively contribute to suboptimal blood glucose management among hospitalized patients with diabetes in non-endocrinology departments.

From the patient’s perspective, personal factors also pose significant challenges to blood glucose management. Studies indicate that most non-endocrinology patients lack sufficient understanding of diabetes and its complications, and generally lack basic knowledge and skills for self-management of blood glucose [11, 19]. Concurrently, psychological stress, anxiety, and potentially increased financial burdens stemming from the disease directly impact patients’ motivation for dietary management, medication taking, and lifestyle adjustments [20]. Thus, non-endocrinology inpatients with diabetes generally face greater difficulties in self-managing blood glucose than endocrinology patients, with multiple factors, physiological, psychological, and socioeconomic intertwining and interacting. The specific pathways and mechanisms through which these factors influence blood glucose management outcomes require further research to clarify.

Several qualitative studies have investigated the experiences of hospitalized patients with diabetes. For example, some studies have explored barriers to medication taking and dietary management in general inpatient populations, while others have focused on the challenges of blood glucose monitoring in endocrinology wards or have extended the scope to caregivers’ perspectives [20–22]. However, very few qualitative studies have specifically targeted patients with diabetes admitted to non‑endocrinology departments, where glucose management is often overshadowed by primary diagnoses, and where the experiences of hospitalized patients with diabetes differ significantly from those in endocrinology settings. Moreover, existing qualitative work has primarily concentrated on describing visible barriers—such as lack of knowledge, monitoring frequency, and financial burden—but how these factors are overlooked by patients, healthcare providers, and even the healthcare system remains largely unexplored. To fill this gap, the present study employs inductive qualitative content analysis to systematically explore the hidden needs of non‑endocrinology inpatients with diabetes regarding blood glucose management during hospitalization. By illuminating these unarticulated dimensions, this study contributes empirical evidence for developing a more patient‑centered, integrated care model tailored to this underserved population.

Objective

This descriptive qualitative study aimed to explore the hidden needs of hospitalized patients with diabetes in non‑endocrinology departments regarding blood glucose management during hospitalization, and to inform more integrated, patient‑centered inpatient glucose management strategies. The focus was on identifying the barriers, confusions, and psychosocial burdens that patients experience but rarely voice, and on understanding how current specialty‑centered care fails to address these unarticulated needs.

Method

Study design and participants

This is a qualitative study adhering to SRQR. The study employed a sampling principle combining purposive sampling with maximum variation to select patients with diabetes from different departments with diverse experiences and backgrounds, to ensure the diversity and complementarity of the research findings. Data collection utilized semi-structured interviews with participants from the Qingdao Campus of Qilu Hospital of Shandong University. Prior to formal interviews, interviewers provided patients with detailed explanations of the study’s objectives and methodology, and the informed consent forms were signed by the patients. Eligibility criteria included: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) hospitalization in non-endocrinology department; (3) confirmed diagnosis of diabetes; (4) Informed consent and voluntary participation in the study. Exclusion criteria: (1) Concurrent severe comorbidities; (2) Death during the study period; (3) Withdrawal from the study. Demographics of participants are summarized in Table 1 below. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Qingdao Campus of Qilu Hospital of Shandong University. (Approval No. KYLL-KS-2024269).

Theoretical framework

This study employs the COM-B Model as its theoretical framework, which forms the core of the Behaviour Change Wheel (BCW) [23] (Figs. 1 and 2). This model posits that for individuals to engage in target behaviors within specific contexts, a dynamic synergy among three elements is required: capacity, opportunity and motivation. Patients’ blood glucose management constitutes a critical health behavior influenced by multiple factors [24]. Designing interview guides based on this model (Table 2) ensures that qualitative data collection can closely focus on the three core dimensions that affect patient behavior, in order to comprehensively cover potential promoting factors and barriers to blood glucose management as much as possible. Importantly, the COM‑B model was used only for interview design, not as a coding framework during data analysis.

Interview questions

Sample size

In this study, saturation was conceptualized and operationalized as data saturation [25], defined as the point at which no new codes or categories emerge from two consecutive interviews. In line with guidance for qualitative content analysis, data collection continued until this criterion was met. This study employed a theory‑based interview approach. Francis [26] recommends conducting at least 10 interviews, with a maximum of 3 additional interviews permitted if data saturation is achieved after preliminary analysis. Initially, we planned to conduct at least 10 interviews and decided to add up to 3 more based on data saturation. However, during the actual interview process, we observed that data saturation had not been achieved. Consequently, after reaching 10 cases, no new categories emerged in the interview data. To further confirm saturation, we conducted two additional interviews, concluding the process when no new categories appeared. Thus, this study interviewed a total of 12 patients.

Data generation

Face‑to‑face semi‑structured interviews were conducted by two trained interviewers (SWS and RXH), both of whom were nurses with a Master’s degree in Nursing with prior training in qualitative research methods, and are currently conducting research on diabetes related topics as the project leader. Neither interviewer had a pre‑existing therapeutic relationship with any participant. The interviewer contacts the patient one day in advance and make an appointment for a suitable time. Interviews took place in private, quiet offices, consultation rooms, or single patient rooms within each department, scheduled at times when no medical procedures were planned. Before each interview, the purpose of the study was explained, and written informed consent was obtained. Participants were assured of confidentiality and that they could withdraw at any time. Interviews were audio‑recorded with permission, and non‑verbal cues were noted. Each interview lasted 15–45 min.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using the inductive qualitative content analysis method described by Graneheim and Lundman [27]. The analysis followed these steps:

Transcription and preparation. All interviews were transcribed verbatim within 24 h by SWS and RXH. Each transcript was checked against the original audio recording by XLP for accuracy. Transcripts were anonymized using participant IDs.

Meaning unit identification. Meaning units (words, phrases, sentences, or paragraphs relevant to blood glucose management) were identified.

Coding. SWS and RXH independently performed open coding using NVivo 12 software. No pre-existing coding framework (including the COM-B model) was imposed. Codes were derived directly from the participants’ statements.

Category development. Similar codes were grouped into subcategories, and subcategories were further aggregated into main categories based on patterns and relationships. Disagreements between SWS and RXH regarding coding or categorization were resolved through discussion with WHS and TJJ until consensus was reached.

Finalization. All authors reviewed the final category structure to ensure it accurately reflected the data. The resulting categories and subcategories are presented in Table 3 before the narrative results.

Results

Category 1: Conflict between primary diagnosis and diabetes

Subcategory 1.1: Patients prioritize primary diagnosis over diabetes

Outside endocrinology departments, patients subjectively rank their conditions based on perceived urgency and threat level. They place absolute priority on their primary diagnosis, viewing diabetes as a new issue that can be postponed or a background old problem. For patients diagnosed with diabetes during the current hospitalization, they have not yet had the opportunity to fully understand the disease, learn self‑management skills, or recognize its long‑term implications. Consequently, they naturally focus on the immediate surgical or medical issue that prompted admission, viewing diabetes as a secondary concern that can be addressed later. This cognitive prioritization, however, creates a “hidden” dynamic: patients rarely articulate their diabetes‑related questions or difficulties to their physicians. At the same time, non‑endocrinology clinicians, pressed by heavy caseloads and lacking diabetes expertise, seldom probe patients’ understanding or concerns about glucose management. As a result, the patient’s need for integrated guidance remains unvoiced and unasked.

“I was only diagnosed with diabetes during this hospitalization. I never felt anything before… Surgery is the top priority now. Just manage my blood sugar for me. We can address it later.” (N3, Spinal surgery Ward patient).

“From morning till night, it’s either intravenous infusion or endless checks. Sometimes I forget to ask the nurse for a blood sugar check, other times I’m out for tests and can’t get back… I just don’t have the energy to focus on anything else. Besides, diabetes is an old condition—I know how to manage it.” (N8, Hepatology Ward patient).

Meanwhile, for patients who proactively seek information on blood glucose management, their focus typically centers on how glycemic management impacts primary diagnosis, treatment, and recovery. Simply put, they view blood glucose management as an add-on condition, overlooking the fact that diabetes itself requires specialized management and carries lifelong implications. Non‑endocrinology physicians, untrained to address long‑term diabetes trajectories, rarely extend the conversation. Consequently, the patient’s need for sustained, future‑oriented diabetes education remains unexplored. This need stays hidden, masked by both the patient’s focus on the primary diagnoses and the clinician’s specialty‑bound scope.

“I’ve had diabetes for a long time. I previously lost a toe due to foot complications, which was quite devastating. Now I’m especially afraid of going blind from high blood sugar.” (Ophthalmology Ward patient, N10).

“During daily rounds, my doctor mainly discusses my baby’s development and delivery-related issues. When I occasionally ask about my diabetes, the doctor’s explanations are quite brief… Although an endocrinologist has already seen me, I’m still very worried that diabetes might affect my baby’s growth.” (Obstetrics Ward patient, N2).

Subcategory 1.2: Confusion over conflicts between primary diagnosis and diabetes management

For non-endocrinology inpatients with diabetes, blood glucose management inevitably conflicts with the management of their primary disease (e.g., medication, activity, diet) across multiple dimensions, causing confusion and helplessness. Regarding activity, for instance, some patients face conflicts between the immobilization or bed rest required by their primary diagnosis and the increased activity recommended for diabetes management.

“My knees hurt constantly, making it impossible to stand or move for long periods. I rarely exercise, so I can’t follow the exercise plan my doctor prescribed. I worry this affects my glycemic management.” (Joint Surgery Ward patient, N11).

“I usually do exercises at home following TV programs after meals. But staring at the screen too long makes my eyes uncomfortable, so I rarely exercise for long periods.” (Ophthalmology Ward patient, N10).

“I experienced hypoglycemia once. Because I was scheduled for surgery, I hadn’t eaten. By 3 PM, I felt dizzy and sweaty. My blood sugar was 4.2 mmol/L. The nurse quickly administered glucose. I’m actually confused about how long before surgery one should fast. Also, if the surgery is delayed, shouldn’t the doctor prescribe nutritional supplements or something to prevent hypoglycemia?” (Urology Ward patient, N9).

Despite these clear struggles and expressed confusion, patients rarely directly asked their physicians to resolve these conflicts. They either accepted the situation passively (“I had no choice”) or assumed that doctors would address it if important. Thus, the patient’s need for how to navigate concurrent demands remained hidden: neither explicitly voiced nor systematically elicited.

Regarding medication, drugs prescribed for the patient’s primary diagnosis may also conflict with diabetes medications. On one hand, essential medications for treating the primary condition, such as corticosteroids, can elevate blood glucose levels, making glycemic management more challenging. On the other hand, certain hypoglycemic agents or insulin use may pose potential risks to the treatment of the primary diagnosis. In these medication‑related conflicts, patients again demonstrated resignation rather than active questioning. Meanwhile, non‑endocrinology physicians rarely initiated discussions about trade‑offs or potential adjustments. Consequently, the patient’s need for integrated pharmacotherapy guidance buried under the assumption that each specialist’s plan would automatically harmonize.

“My doctor prescribed dexamethasone to promote fetal lung maturation. My blood sugar spiked during treatment, but I had no choice—it was for the baby’s better development.” (Obstetrics Ward patient, N2).

“After taking metformin orally for three years, my creatinine levels worsened, so I started insulin injections. It’s been many years now—I feel like I’ve reached the final stage of diabetes treatment.” (Nephrology Ward patient, N5).

Category 2: Confusion regarding systematic blood glucose management

Subcategory 2.1: Fragmented disease understanding

Most patients reported that blood glucose management information received during hospitalization was fragmented, passive, and confusing. While aware they needed to monitor blood glucose and administer insulin, they possessed little knowledge about the underlying reasons or treatment plans. This resulted in a lack of holistic understanding of their condition and management plan, revealing a strong desire for systematic, personalized diabetes education.

“I prick my finger seven times a day. The nurse comes, pricks me, records it, and leaves… When my blood sugar spikes, the doctor adjusts my insulin, but I don’t really understand why it’s high or what to do next.”(Ophthalmology Ward patient, N10).

“The doctor only said‘suboptimal glycemic management affects wound healing,’ but I don’t understand how exactly. It just makes me more anxious. I’m afraid to ask too many questions and bother them… I wish someone could explain the reasons—I’m afraid these minor issues might affect my surgery outcome.” (Spinal surgery Ward patient, N3).

“I don’t really understand how to manage my blood sugar. The information I’ve received during hospitalization feels fragmented—I just know I need to monitor, but I don’t know why or what the overall plan is. Some online sources are even pseudoscience. I hope the hospital can provide us with a scientific, authoritative, and systematic handbook explaining how to exercise and manage my diet.” (Cardiology Ward patient, N12).

What makes this need “hidden” is that despite their confusion and desire for explanation, patients rarely directly request systematic education from their providers. As one participant admitted, “I’m afraid to ask too many questions and bother them.” Non-endocrinology nurses and physicians, focused on recording glucose values and adjusting insulin per protocol, do not routinely assess patients’ understanding. Fragmented information is delivered passively, and patients’ unspoken demand for a coherent, personalized education plan remains unarticulated.

Subcategory 2.2: Inadequate consultation model

When non-endocrinology inpatients with diabetes experience abnormal blood glucose levels requiring professional intervention, they commonly face delayed responses and insufficient guidance from endocrinology consultations. This prevents timely and precise adjustments to abnormal glucose levels, exacerbating patients’ confusion about blood glucose management and diminishing their confidence in treatment. Patients’ need for coordinated, comprehensible cross‑specialty guidance, including clarification of contradictory recommendations remains hidden.

“My blood sugar hasn’t been well-managed since surgery. My attending physician said a consultation was needed, but never specified when it would happen or how the treatment plan would be adjusted afterward. I’ve just been waiting.” (Gynecology Ward patient, N4).

“The consulting doctor spoke very technically—I didn’t understand most of the terms. When I asked, ‘Can I keep taking my usual medications?’, he just said, ‘Follow our plan for now.’But his plan differed from the orthopedic surgeon’s, leaving me even more confused about what to do.” (Joint Surgery Ward patient, N11).

Subcategory 2.3: Lack of transition period management

Non-endocrinology inpatients with diabetes lack organized, continuous guidance and support during two critical transitions: from home (a familiar self-management environment) to hospitalization (an unfamiliar, institutionalized setting), and from hospitalization (a fully supported, structured environment) back to home (where complete self-responsibility is required). This results in inadequate continuity of blood glucose management throughout the patient journey.

“My blood sugar was well-managed at home, but after admission, the doctor abruptly stopped my long-term oral hypoglycemic medication and switched me directly to insulin. I honestly don’t understand why this adjustment was made.” (Respiratory Medicine Ward, N6).

“During hospitalization, I monitored my blood sugar four times daily under constant supervision by doctors and nurses. After discharge, I was instructed to monitor only once daily. What if my levels spike or drop between monitors and I don’t notice? It feels like no one is monitoring me anymore—it’s extremely unsettling.”(Gynecology Ward patient, N4).

These transition‑related needs remain hidden because patients rarely voice their confusion or anxiety about medication changes or post‑discharge monitoring. As one participant noted, “I honestly don’t understand why this adjustment was made”—yet she did not ask the physician for an explanation. Non‑endocrinology physicians, focused on discharge efficiency and primary diagnosis follow‑up, do not routinely ask patients whether they feel prepared for post‑discharge self‑management. Thus, patients’ need for coherent transition planning remains hidden.

Category 3: Barriers to self-management

Subcategory 3.1: Fear of pain associated with blood glucose monitoring

Methods of self-monitoring blood glucose include fingerstick capillary blood glucose monitoring and subcutaneous interstitial fluid glucose measurement [28]. Currently in China, the primary method for self-monitoring blood glucose is fingerstick monitoring, which requires patients to puncture their fingertips for each measurement. However, the pain itself is not “hidden”—both patients and nurses acknowledge its existence. What remains hidden are the consequences of pain that patients do not disclose and providers do not systematically address. Patients’ need for pain‑reducing monitoring options silently contributes to the documentation of “poor compliance” without its true cause ever being identified or addressed.

“When the nurse comes to check my blood sugar, I always close my eyes and grit my teeth—it hurts so much… If I had to prick myself, I just couldn’t bring myself to do it, so I rarely check at home.” (Gastroenterology Ward patient, N1).

“I want to monitor my blood sugar regularly, but pricking my finger hurts too much every time. If only there were a painless meter or one that didn’t require finger pricks.” (Breast Surgery Ward patient, N7).

Subcategory 3.2: Difficulty changing existing lifestyle

Some patients expressed difficulty altering their established lifestyle, primarily due to the strong willpower required to sustain the dietary and exercise routines necessary for diabetes self-management over the long term. Most patients are aware that a healthy lifestyle and dietary structure can achieve better blood glucose management, but not everyone can persist in it for the long term. What makes this need “hidden” is that patients rarely translate their struggles with long‑term engagement into a direct request for help. They may laugh off their lapses “I just can’t resist rewarding myself” but do not explicitly ask clinicians for strategies to sustain behavior change. Patients’ needs, hidden behind self-deprecating humor and clinical inattention, remain unexpressed and unaddressed.

“When the whole family eats together and everyone else is enjoying their food so much, sometimes I just can’t resist ‘rewarding’ myself with a few extra bites. Afterward, my blood sugar spikes… It’s just too hard to completely avoid these temptations.” (Joint Surgery Ward patient, N11).

“Diabetes runs in our family—I think it’s linked to genetics and eating habits. Sometimes I just can’t resist grabbing some skewers… I’ve been hospitalized twice because of it.” (Gastroenterology Ward patient, N1).

“Sticking to it for days or weeks? Anyone can do that. The real challenge is that this is a lifelong commitment requiring immense willpower… I have a friend with diabetes who exercises relentlessly through wind and rain and keeps her blood sugar well-managed. I just can’t match her discipline.” (Breast Surgery Ward patient, N7).

Category 4: The extra burden

Subcategory 4.1: Financial burden

Non-endocrinology inpatients with diabetes often have multiple underlying comorbidities, making medications, monitoring devices, and dietary adjustments for glycemic management an additional financial burden. The long-term economic burden accumulated outside the hospital further affects patients’ treatment choices during hospitalization and their self-management decisions after discharge [29]. Even when more comfortable, advanced monitoring devices are available, patients remain constrained by daily cost concerns and are reluctant to use them. For example, continuous glucose monitors (CGMs) can track blood glucose fluctuations in real time and record values; they are easy to insert and well-tolerated [30]. However, due to their high cost, most patients are less likely to choose them even when recommended by healthcare providers during hospitalization.

“My son bought me a CGM. It cost several hundred yuan, isn’t covered by insurance, and only lasts half a month… After using it, I still prick my finger to monitor my blood sugar—it’s more economical.” (Cardiology Ward patient, N12).

“I’ve had diabetes for years and worn out several glucose meters. Combined with glucose strips and insulin needles, the financial burden is significant, to be honest.” (Nephrology Ward patient, N5).

“Diabetes is a lifelong condition requiring strict dietary management. But after researching online, I found diet for people with diabetes are expensive, so I try to buy ingredients and prepare meals myself at home.” (Gynecology Ward patient, N4).

Subcategory 4.2: Social shame

While public awareness of diabetes has improved in China, a diabetes-friendly social environment remains lacking. For non-endocrinology hospitalized patients, the long-term social stigma and shame experienced in daily out-of-hospital social interactions profoundly affect their self-management, psychological status, and help-seeking behaviors throughout hospitalization, and continue to influence their glycemic management after discharge. Negative social evaluations and feelings of shame arising from managing diabetes in public settings outside the hospital extend into patients’ hospital experience and subsequent self-care practices at home [31, 32].

“Due to my job, I often have to attend social events and carry my insulin pen with me. Even though I try to adjust my mindset, I still feel embarrassed every time I inject insulin. I don’t want others to know, so I always make excuses to go to the restroom to do it. Sometimes, when I really can’t get away, I just skip the dose… Because injecting insulin in public always feels a bit stressful.” (Hepatology Ward patient, N8).

“I have many friends and used to go out for meals often. After being diagnosed with diabetes, I became very focused on glycemic management. Everyone was considerate, adjusting meals and drinks for me. But I didn’t want to dampen their spirits, so gradually I stopped going out.” (Urology Ward patient, N9).

Subcategory 4.3: Guilt toward family

For non‑endocrinology inpatients with diabetes, dietary restrictions, regular monitoring and limited mobility bring personal difficulties and increase reliance on family caregivers, triggering feelings of burden. To avoid worrying relatives, patients often hide such negative emotions as an unmet hidden need. For instance, one patient delayed reporting palpitations for a month so as not to disturb her son’s work. Concentrating on clinical indicators, healthcare providers rarely inquire about family‑related stress or caregiver burden. Consequently, patients’ demands for emotional support and family‑burden‑relieving strategies remain unspoken and unaddressed under a facade of self‑sufficiency.

“Last month I felt some palpitations, but I didn’t want to interrupt my son’s work, so I didn’t tell him. It dragged on for days without improvement, so I finally told him. He took time off to accompany me for tests, which revealed coronary heart disease and suboptimal glycemic management.” (Cardiology Ward patient, N12).

“Blood glucose strips are cheaper online, but I don’t know how to shop online… My kids have to constantly keep track of this. When supplies run low, they have to rush to restock.” (Respiratory Medicine Ward patient, N6).

Discussion

This study employed semi‑structured interviews to reveal a series of profound and intertwined challenges faced by patients with diabetes in non‑endocrinology departments. Patients commonly find themselves caught in direct conflict between primary disease treatment and diabetes management requirements, leading to fundamental confusion and feelings of helplessness. This finding is consistent with previous qualitative studies showing that patients with multiple chronic conditions often prioritize acute or symptomatic illnesses over asymptomatic ones [33, 34]. Simultaneously, diabetes information received during hospitalization is fragmented, and patients experience poor coordination among multidisciplinary consultations and an absence of transitional support. Such fragmentation has been recognized as a major barrier to effective chronic disease management in hospital settings [35].

From the patient’s perspective, the root cause of blood glucose management challenges for patients with diabetes hospitalized in non‑endocrinology departments often lies in a series of hidden needs overlooked in routine specialty care. These patients are admitted for primary diagnoses, which marginalizes their diabetes management requirements. Their needs may go unarticulated due to difficulty expressing them while focusing on primary illnesses, or be actively filtered out as medical attention centers on the primary diagnosis, ultimately becoming barriers to effective blood glucose management.

From the healthcare provider perspective, within China’s high‑intensity medical environment, non‑endocrinology staff face immense pressure. Complex diabetes management is frequently simplified to periodic monitoring and medication adjustments, becoming a numbers‑focused, not patient‑centered, practice. Non‑endocrinology clinicians rarely have the opportunity or sufficient expertise to delve into the behavioral, psychological, or social drivers behind blood glucose fluctuations. Consequently, patients’ genuine struggles—such as refusing monitoring due to fear of pain or self‑adjusting medications out of confusion—remain unseen and unaddressed within their primary treatment plans.

One of the most fundamental hidden needs stems from the conflict between the primary diagnosis and diabetes. Our findings show that patients prioritize their primary diagnosis over diabetes, viewing the latter as a secondary or future concern. This cognitive hierarchy leads to a “hidden” dynamic: patients rarely voice diabetes‑related questions, and non‑endocrinology clinicians, pressed by heavy caseloads and lacking diabetes expertise, seldom probe patients’ understanding. This pattern is not unique to China. In a UK study of hospitalized surgical patients with diabetes, over half had no clear plan for diabetes management during admission, and 1/6 were never seen by a diabetes team. Additionally, nearly 40% reported that their surgical team never discussed diabetes management during admission [36]. Similarly, a Germany qualitative study found that patients hospitalized in non- endocrine department perceived diabetes as a “background condition” that was actively deprioritized by both themselves and their physicians [37]. Thus, the conflict between the primary diagnosis and diabetes prioritization appears to be a universal challenge in specialty‑centric hospital care. However, the Chinese context adds a specific layer: the rapid throughput and high patient‑to‑clinician ratios in tertiary hospitals exacerbate this marginalization, making it even less likely that patients’ hidden needs will be elicited. For context, China’s number of registered nurses per 1,000 population is 4.32 (2025) [38], compared to the OECD average of 9.2 (2023) [39], indicating that China’s nursing workforce level remains significantly below the average of developed countries. Patients’ need for integrated guidance remains unexpressed and unasked, yet it is fundamental to safe and effective care.

Beyond this conflict, we found that patients experience confusion regarding systematic blood glucose management. They reported fragmented disease understanding, inadequate consultation models, and a lack of transition period management. The fragmented information they receive is delivered passively, and their desire for a coherent, personalized education plan is rarely articulated. All of the above will have a negative impact on disease management [40]. Non‑endocrinology inpatients with diabetes experienced delayed endocrinology consultations, and those who received consultations often could not reconcile contradictory recommendations are also well‑recognized problems [41]. Our findings extend this by showing that the absence of structured multidisciplinary protocols leaves patients confused and anxious, eroding their motivation. Furthermore, the lack of transition planning—abrupt medication switches without explanation and unclear post‑discharge monitoring plans—is a global safety concern. A study in the Netherlands [42] reported that information regarding medications such as medication changes, the expected effect, side effects, and instructions for use are frequently poorly communicated to patients during hospitalization and at discharge. Consideration of patients’ needs and their active engagement in decision-making during hospitalization regarding their medications are often lacking. Several studies have reported that discharge bundles including a written diabetes action plan and follow‑up appointment can significantly reduce the readmission rate of patient dysglycemia rates, effects, and even reduce readmission rates [43–45]. Yet in Chinese non‑endocrinology wards, such bundles are rarely implemented. The hidden need here is not simply for more information, but for systematic, coordinated, and transitional guidance that respects the patient’s cognitive and emotional state during hospitalization.

These system‑level confusions are compounded by individual barriers to self‑management. Fear of pain from fingerstick monitoring and difficulty changing long‑standing lifestyle habits were prominent barriers. Although pain is acknowledged by both patients and nurses, its consequences—avoidance of monitoring—remain hidden because patients do not disclose the extent of their distress, and providers do not systematically inquire. This is not a China‑specific issue. Research from multiple countries and regions found that fear of pain was one of the most commonly cited barriers to self‑monitoring of blood glucose [45–47]. Similarly, the difficulty of sustaining lifestyle changes—especially dietary restraint in social eating contexts—reflects the powerful influence of automatic motivation and social environment, as framed by the COM‑B model [23, 24]. What makes this need “hidden” is that patients often mask their struggles with self‑deprecating humor “I just can’t resist rewarding myself” and rarely translate them into explicit requests for behavioral support. Providers, in turn, label suboptimal adherence as “non‑compliance” without exploring the underlying behavioral drivers.

Beyond these medical and behavioral challenges, patients carry an extra burden that is often invisible in clinical encounters. Our findings reveal that non‑endocrinology inpatients with diabetes face multidimensional psychosocial burdens including financial strain, social shame, and family guilt. Financial constraints directly limit access to advanced monitoring technologies like CGMs. This is a major issue in low‑ and middle‑income countries, but even in high‑income settings, out‑of‑pocket costs for CGMs remain a barrier for many patients [48]. In China, where basic insurance does not cover CGMs in most regions, patients use them alongside fingerstick monitoring to save costs, which is a strategy that reflects both resourcefulness and unmet need. Social shame associated with insulin injection and dietary restrictions is another powerful hidden need. Patients avoid injections in public to conceal their condition, which is particularly prominent among young people [49]. However, the Chinese cultural emphasis on “saving face” and not burdening others may intensify this shame. Most strikingly, guilt toward family—particularly toward their independent adult children (i.e., those who are employed and have their own families)—emerged as a distinct category. Patients delayed seeking care or hid symptoms to avoid disturbing their adult children. This resonates with findings from filial piety‑based societies such as China and Korea, where older adults often prioritize family harmony over their own health [50, 51]. Yet in non‑endocrinology clinical practice, these emotional burdens are tacitly dismissed as private matters or signs of “good compliance” (i.e., not complaining). The hidden need is for psychosocial screening and support—something that is rarely provided in busy specialty wards. The core insight is that hidden needs arise whenever healthcare systems fail to create a safe, proactive environment for patients to voice their non‑biomedical concerns.

Looking across these four interrelated categories, a common thread emerges: patients’ hidden needs are not isolated problems but are deeply interconnected, stemming from a structural disconnect between specialty‑centric hospital care and the demands of chronic disease co‑management. When a patient is admitted for primary diagnosis, the entire system—scheduling, rounds, discharge planning—revolves around that primary diagnosis. Diabetes management becomes an “add‑on” task, reduced to episodic glucose checks and consultation orders. This structural marginalization systematically filters out patients’ questions, fears, and contextual struggles. Therefore, establishing a truly effective hospital‑based blood glucose management system requires looking beyond suboptimal glycemic management as a surface issue. The core starting point must be systematically identifying, understanding, and addressing these hidden needs. This demands a fundamental shift from isolated, specialty‑based management centered on biomedical indicators toward an integrated care model centered on the whole patient. Such a model must incorporate cross‑specialty education, contextualized psychosocial support, structured multidisciplinary collaboration, and continuity of care planning. Only by placing the patient and their entire disease burden at the center of care, actively listening to the struggles obscured by the noise of specialty‑focused treatment, and providing integrated, personalized support can we break the long‑standing impasse of management barriers.

Limitations and strengths

This study was conducted in a single tertiary hospital in China, and its contextual specificity should be considered when applying the findings to other settings. To support transferability, we have provided detailed participant demographics, extensive verbatim quotes, and a transparent description of the analytical process. Self-reported data are subject to recall bias. Despite efforts to minimize social desirability, participants might have underreported sensitive feelings such as shame or family guilt. We did not conduct participant triangulation, primarily due to difficulties in re-contacting participants after discharge and concerns about increasing their emotional burden given the sensitive nature of the topics discussed. The absence of prolonged engagement in the wards could have limited deeper contextual understanding. However, the richness of the data and the rigorous analytical process strengthen the credibility of the findings.

Conclusion

Patients with diabetes hospitalized in non‑endocrinology departments have multiple hidden needs that are deeply intertwined with their primary diagnoses and are routinely overlooked in current specialty‑focused care. This study identified four interconnected categories of hidden needs: conflict between the primary diagnosis and diabetes, confusion regarding systematic blood glucose management, barriers to self‑management, and multidimensional psychosocial burdens. Addressing these hidden demands requires a fundamental shift from an isolated, indicator‑driven model to an integrated, patient‑centered approach. Health systems should prioritize cross‑specialty education, structured multidisciplinary collaboration, and transitional care planning. Only by systematically eliciting and responding to patients’ unarticulated needs can we improve inpatient glucose management outcomes and enhance the overall care experience for this underserved population.
