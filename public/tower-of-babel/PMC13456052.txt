Transitional care interventions and outcome measures in stroke rehabilitation: a scoping review
Authors: Carla Tierney-Hendricks, Alyssa Garza, Liam Keefe, Rachel Cullen, Renee C. Noordzij, Jasmine J. Li, Jessica G. Bell, Janet Prvu Bettger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:Stroke survivors experience physical, emotional, and cognitive-communication impairments, requiring rehabilitation services across multiple care settings. Transitional care interventions (TCIs) are actions to ensure care coordination. This scoping review aimed to describe the intervention components and outcomes of TCIs in stroke rehabilitation.Materials and methods:A scoping review was conducted using 5 databases (PubMed, CINAHL, OVID MEDLINE, PsycINFO, Rehabilitation & Sports Medicine) for the years 2012–2026. After removing duplicates, 4,663 abstracts were screened, and the full text of articles was reviewed. Intervention characteristics, components, and outcomes were extracted from the full-text articles. Descriptive, numeric and narrative summaries were prepared; intervention components and outcomes were categorized.Results:The review included 49 articles, representing 31 TCIs with 23 different intervention components. Interventions predominantly employed individual-level components (education, self-management). System-level components (care coordination) were used less frequently. Clinical outcomes are predominantly focused on physical function. Cognitive-communication outcomes were evaluated less frequently; persons with aphasia and cognitive impairments were often excluded from studies. Implementation outcomes were assessed for 20 TCIs.Conclusion:Alignment of individual- and system-level intervention components with intended outcomes is necessary to advance TCI research. Further work is needed to tailor TCIs to the needs of persons with cognitive-communication disabilities.

Introduction

Stroke represents a major global health challenge, currently ranking as the third leading cause of disability worldwide among non-communicable diseases [1]. Approximately 94 million people are living with the effects of stroke globally, and an estimated 89% of the stroke burden occurs in low- and lower-middle-income countries, where access to rehabilitation and transitional care services are often limited [1]. Stroke survivors experience a range of long-term physical, emotional, cognitive, and communication impairments that impact social participation and quality of life [2]. Despite advances in acute stroke treatment, improved survival rates have not corresponded with increased post-acute support for physical, cognitive, and emotional recovery [3]. Stroke survivors are frequently discharged from the hospital into complex systems of care that are under-resourced for long-term rehabilitation [4]. One in four stroke survivors is discharged home from the hospital within four days of their stroke with no additional services despite residual deficits [5]. Many stroke survivors face persistent limitations without sufficient access to post-discharge support, leading to unmet needs in functional, social, and emotional recovery [6]. Specifically, stroke survivors report a high proportion of unmet needs related to cognition and communication, and these nonphysical needs are significantly associated with worse health-related quality of life [7]. The long-term sequelae post-stroke underscore the urgent need to better support stroke survivors beyond the acute care setting.

Recovery following stroke requires rehabilitation care delivered within multiple health settings across the continuum of care. This shift in care responsibility from one healthcare setting to another is commonly referred to as a care transition. Care transitions represent a vulnerable period for patients and their caregivers and are often characterized by fragmented care and avoidable complications, which ultimately lead to poor outcomes for both patients and their caregivers [4,8–10]. There is evidence that one in five patients may experience suboptimal or unsafe care around the time of discharge from a hospital, primarily due to poor continuity of care and the coordination challenges posed by multiple independent professionals and agencies [11]. Improving the quality and continuity of care during these transitions is essential to addressing the unmet needs and promoting improved outcomes in stroke recovery.

Over the past two decades, improving transitions between hospital and community care has gained increased attention as a strategy to optimize patient outcomes and control healthcare costs [12]. This priority aligns with policy initiatives for value-based care, which incentivizes hospitals and post-acute care facilities for successful community discharges without readmission. Additionally, provider billing codes were introduced in the United States in 2013 to support the uptake of transitional care management [13]. Transitional care interventions (TCIs) are a set of actions designed to ensure the coordination and continuity of care and often involve patient and family education, communication among healthcare professionals, and coordination of services [8]. TCIs have been shown to improve healthcare utilization (e.g. re-admission rates) and biomedical outcomes (e.g. blood pressure) for patients with chronic medical conditions discharging to the community from acute care settings [14–17]. Yet, the development and implementation of transitional care interventions focused on enhancing functional recovery and reducing disability in the rehabilitation populations are in its infancy [18–20].

Transitional care interventions are complex and often comprise multiple, inter-related components designed to address individual patient needs with consideration to the local healthcare and community context [21]. In 2022, the National Transitions of Care Coalition (NTOCC) outlined a set of essential, evidence-based intervention components critical to safe and effective care transitions in the management of chronic conditions [22]. Though not explicitly stated, the NTOCC components operate at both the individual and system levels. At the individual level, treatment components focus on enhancing the knowledge, behaviors, and beliefs of patients, caregivers, and healthcare providers to promote informed and engaged participation in care. At the system level, intervention components target organizational processes, including care coordination logistics, communication among care team members, and mechanisms for ensuring timely referrals and follow-up care. Complex interventions such as TCIs also measure various outcomes to evaluate the impact of multi-level intervention components. Characterizing TCI components and outcomes with a focus on individual and system levels is critical to unpacking the “black box” of TCIs in stroke rehabilitation. This work will guide future research for more intentional design, testing and optimization of TCIs.

Thus, this scoping review aimed to provide an in-depth examination and synthesis of the intervention characteristics (e.g. context and structure), components and outcomes of TCIs in stroke rehabilitation literature that will lay the foundation for the design and refinement of future interventions. Specifically, we intended to identify individual- and system-level intervention components employed, clinical and implementation outcomes evaluated, and if persons with aphasia and/or other cognitive-communication disabilities were included in study populations.

Methods

A scoping review methodology was selected as a systematic approach to synthesize the existing literature and identify research gaps for transitional care in stroke rehabilitation [23]. This review was guided by Arksey and O’Malley’s five-stage framework [24], with refinements proposed by Levac and colleagues [25]. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [26] checklist was used to guide research reporting. The study protocol was not registered.

Stage 1: Identifying the research question

To characterize transitional care interventions in the stroke rehabilitation literature, our research team developed the following research questions: 1) What are the individual- and system-level intervention components used in TCIs in the stroke rehabilitation literature? 2) What clinical and implementation outcomes are measured within these studies? and 3) Do TCI studies in the stroke rehabilitation literature include persons with aphasia and other cognitive-communication disabilities in their study population?

Stage 2: Identifying the relevant studies

Studies were included if they involved the adult stroke population, aged 18 years and older. Eligible studies described a TCI. Given the variability of terminology used in the transitional care literature, we defined TCIs as the actions and processes to ensure care continuity and coordination between two or more care settings [27]. This intervention and approach to care delivery is complementary but distinct from early supported discharge (excluded from this review), which aims to reduce the length of hospital stay using an intensive approach to home-based, specialized multi-disciplinary rehabilitation to restore functional independence [28]. Five electronic databases, PubMed, CINAHL, OVID MEDLINE, PsycINFO, and Rehabilitation & Sports Medicine were used to identify relevant articles for inclusion. The search terms and strategies for each database were developed in collaboration with an academic librarian (JGB). Search terms such as “transitional care” OR “discharge planning” OR “care coordination” AND “rehabilitation” AND “stroke” were used. Pilot searches were conducted to test and refine the search strategies. Database-specific subject headings, search terms and Boolean operators were refined through team discussion. Formal searches using the refined search strategies were first conducted on October 23, 2024. Given the time duration from the start of the project to data extraction, a second round of searches was conducted on January 20, 2026, across all databases. We applied filters to retrieve articles published after January 2012, to examine the literature published since the previous review by Bettger and colleagues [29]. Our final search strategies are reported in Supplementary Appendix 1.

Stage 3: Selecting studies

All search results were imported into Covidence [30] for screening and review. Duplicate articles were removed. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were developed by authors CTH and AG. The inclusion criteria were: (1) adults aged 18 years or older with a stroke diagnosis, including ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, transient ischemic attack, and/or mixed diagnoses including stroke, (2) studies focused on transitional care, defined as the actions and process to ensure care continuity across two or more care settings, (3) study designs including randomized controlled trials (RCTs), feasibility studies, quasi experimental designs, and methods papers, (4) studies that measure at least one rehabilitation-oriented outcome as defined by the WHO International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health [31], (5) studies published between January 2012 and January 2026, and (6) peer-reviewed articles available in full text. Exclusion criteria included: (1) studies not written in English, and (2) conference abstracts, editorials, reviews, case studies, unpublished theses or dissertations, and observational studies. Observational study designs included articles that characterized factors influencing transitional care in stroke rehabilitation (e.g. studies characterizing needs or factors influencing transitions between care settings) but did not describe or test a specific intervention. Though excluded, observational studies were tagged within Covidence to track the volume of descriptive studies in this topic.

Title and abstract screening were performed by two independent reviewers from a team of 6 researchers (AG, CTH, RC, RCN, LK, JJL). Due to the number of raters, we assessed inter-rater reliability at the screening level using percent agreement. A third reviewer from the research team resolved the conflict in the abstract screening phase. Full text screening was completed independently by two authors (AG, CTH, LK, JJL). Fleiss’ Kappa statistic was calculated to assess inter-rater reliability of full-text review for study inclusion. Discrepancies for full-text screening were resolved through research team discussion with input from a third reviewer.

Step 4: Charting the data

To characterize each transitional care intervention, primary authors (AG and CTH) generated a list of elements relevant to the research questions to capture the structure and context of the intervention, intervention components and outcomes, and study population parameters. An initial extraction template was developed using Microsoft Excel for pilot data charting. Primary authors (AG and CTH) piloted the initial template by independently extracting data elements from five randomly selected included articles. After pilot extractions, the template was refined through team discussion to collapse and refine intervention component categories. Additionally, the field for outcome measures was restructured as an open response field.

The finalized data extraction tool was inputted into Covidence and included the following characteristics: authors, intervention name, year(s) study was conducted, study design, inclusion/exclusion criteria for study participants, country where the intervention was developed and/or tested, transition type (i.e. acute care to home), profession of intervention facilitators, recipients of the intervention, intervention components, method of contact (i.e. in person, videoconference), timing and dosage of the intervention and outcome domains and measurement timepoint. The categories for intervention components were informed by the National Transitions of Care Coalition Essential Elements bundle [64] and categorized as system- or individual-level intervention components. System-level components were defined as actions or strategies targeting organizational processes, logistics and/or communication. System-level components include interprofessional and interinstitutional communication, referral processes, access to services, care coordination and mechanisms for progress monitoring. Individual-level components addressed behavior change in the knowledge, skills and beliefs of intervention recipients and include education, needs screening, distribution of resources, self-management training and tools, and goal setting. Definitions for all individual- and system-level intervention components are available in Supplementary Appendix 2. Using the final data extraction tool within Covidence, authors (AG, CTH, LK, JJL) independently extracted data from each article. Discrepancies were resolved through team discussion to reach a consensus on final coding for data analysis and synthesis.

Step 5: Collating, summarizing and reporting results

Data were exported from Covidence as a Microsoft Excel file. Discrete data fields included study characteristics and intervention structure, context and components were organized into tables and figures and summarized using frequency counts. Characteristics related to study population and outcome measures were extracted in an open response format, which was then organized and coded by AG and CTH. Study population information on the inclusion of persons with aphasia and other cognitive-communication impairments were coded based on patterns in the data. Outcome measurement domains were categorized as clinical, health services and implementation science measures. Clinical outcomes are measured at the level of the patient or care partner and included level of disability, physical, cognitive, and psychosocial functioning and quality of life. Health service outcomes evaluate healthcare delivery and quality and include re-admission rates, healthcare utilization, and patient and family satisfaction. Lastly, implementation outcomes measure the effects of implementing the intervention within real-world settings. Implementation outcomes were coded using Proctor et al. [32] taxonomy. The outcome measurement scales and instruments were extracted from each article by LK after exporting the data from Covidence. Following our procedures for two independent coders, CTH independently reviewed the outcome measures for each article and consensus on discrepancies were resolved through team discussion. Review findings were summarized using descriptive statistics, frequency counts and visualizations (i.e. tables and figures) and organized by research question.

Results

Final search results

A total of 7,583 articles were identified through database and citation searches of which 4663 were determined to be unique. Citation searching included articles cited by included studies that further described interventions. The percent agreement for title and abstract screening across nine pairs of reviewers was 93.7% and 119 full text articles were reviewed for inclusion with substantial agreement (KF = 0.67). A total of 49 articles were determined to be eligible for this scoping review (see Figure 1). Observational studies were not eligible for inclusion in the review but tagged within Covidence. There was a total of 170 observational studies specific to transitional care in stroke rehabilitation.

Intervention characteristics

The 49 articles included in the review represented a range of study designs: 19 RCTs, 9 feasibility studies, 11 methods papers, 5 quasi-experimental studies, 3 process evaluations, 1 proof-of-concept trial, and 1 cohort study. The 49 articles described 31 distinct transitional care interventions in the stroke population, as some interventions were described across multiple publications. The description of intervention characteristics, components and outcomes were synthesized at the intervention level rather than by individual articles. Table 1 presents the intervention characteristics, and Figure 2 summarizes the intervention components. The interventions were developed and/or tested across various countries, including Canada (n = 5), United States (n = 3), the Netherlands (n = 3), England (n = 3), Australia (n = 2), Denmark (n = 2), Sweden (n = 2), Thailand (n = 2) and one each from Norway, China, Japan, New Zealand, Iran, Uganda, Spain and Austria. Most of the interventions addressed the transition from an acute care facility to home (n = 13), followed by the inpatient rehabilitation to home transition (n = 7) and transitions across multiple care settings (n = 9). In examining study populations for the inclusion of persons with aphasia and/or other cognitive-communication impairments, 4 interventions explicitly included persons with aphasia and other cognitive-communication disorders in their study samples with 2 of these interventions designed to address the unique needs of this population (CHANT, PCSD) [36, 60]. For the remaining interventions, 17 had restrictive inclusion of persons with cognitive-communication impairments, 4 explicitly excluded persons with cognitive-communication impairments, and 4 did not include a description of the participant’s cognitive-communication status. Two interventions did not include patient participants in their evaluation of the intervention.

Individual vs system level intervention components

The interventions varied in complexity from 3 component interventions (Enhanced discharge link) [40] to multi-component interventions including as many as 21 intervention components (TCSI) [52–54]. Most of the interventions were multi-level and included both individual- and system-level intervention components. Yet, interventions such as Voluntary Training with Family Participation [37] included only individual-level intervention components, while the Enhanced Discharge Link intervention [40] used only system-level intervention components. Figure 2 displays a summary of intervention components for each intervention including type and frequency.

Individual level intervention components such as, patient education (n = 29), caregiver education (n = 21), patient needs screening (n = 19), and self-management training (n = 18), were the most frequently used strategies in TCIs for stroke rehabilitation. The most frequently used system-level strategies included processes to improve access to rehabilitation services (n = 19), ongoing patient progress monitoring through digital tools or follow up tracking (n = 18) and logistics and systems for transferring health and rehabilitation information between providers and patients (n = 18). Strategies involving care coordination with a patient navigator, improving access to healthcare providers (i.e. on-call lines) or use of a structured discharge planning tool were only used in 4/31 (13%), 5/31 (16%) and 6/31 (19%) interventions, respectively. Please see Supplementary Appendix 2 for definitions of intervention components.

Intervention outcomes

Outcomes for each intervention were characterized under three primary outcome domains, clinical, health service and implementation. A summary of outcomes and measures for each intervention is shown in Table 2. Almost all interventions (27/31) measured clinical outcomes, which aligns with the fact that most interventions including individual-level intervention components targeting behavior change at the level of the patient and/or care partner. Many interventions measured clinical outcomes related to activities of daily and community re-integration (n = 19) as well as physical function and mobility (n = 13). Few interventions evaluated effects of the TCI on cognitive functioning (n = 9) or language and communication ability (n = 3). As shown in Table 2, clinical outcomes were evaluated using a variety of measures and scales.

Regarding outcomes at the system level, 15 interventions evaluated health service outcomes, mostly related to hospital readmissions, length of stay and healthcare utilization. Implementation outcomes were evaluated in 20 interventions with many outcomes related to early-stage implementation such as acceptability, feasibility and fidelity. Later-stage implementation outcomes such as adoption and sustainment/maintenance were only evaluated in 2 studies.

Discussion

Our findings revealed that transitional care interventions in stroke rehabilitation range in complexity from simple, single-level to multi-component, multi-level interventions. TCIs in post-acute stroke care primarily rely on individual-level intervention components targeting patient and caregiver education, screening and self-management. System-level intervention components, such as care coordination with a patient navigator, improving access to healthcare providers and discharge planning tools, were employed less frequently. Consistent with the focus on individual-level intervention components, outcomes were predominately measured at the patient-level. Clinical outcomes related to physical function, mobility, activities of daily living and community re-integration were evaluated most frequently. Communication and cognition outcomes were infrequently evaluated across TCIs, as persons with aphasia and other cognitive-communication disorders were rarely fully included within study populations. Furthermore, approximately half of the studies evaluated health service or implementation outcomes with an emphasis on early-stage implementation indicators. The proposed characterization of individual- and system-level components based on NTOCC components can inform the alignment of TCI intervention components with intended outcome and measures in future intervention development and testing. This intentional and theory-driven approach to intervention design, adaptation and measurement has the potential to advance TCI research in rehabilitation. Additionally, our findings revealed gaps in addressing the needs of stroke survivors with cognitive-communication disabilities in transitional care.

Focus on individual-level support, despite increasing calls for system-level strategies

Transitional care interventions in stroke rehabilitation continue to prioritize individual-level support, despite growing recognition of the need for system-level strategies. Most interventions included in this review focused on patient education, caregiver training, and self-management strategies, placing the primary responsibility for managing recovery and navigating health systems on stroke survivors and their families. While such approaches are valuable for enhancing patient and caregiver engagement, they are insufficient in the absence of system-level strategies that facilitate organizational processes for continuity, accessibility, and equity. For example, in the COMPASS-TC trial, post-discharge follow-up care was impacted by system-level barriers such as inconsistent workflows, staffing turnover and lack of leadership support [18,81]. Creating organizational readiness prior to implementation and infrastructure for team collaboration were associated with improved reach and effectiveness of the transitional care intervention [81]. To advance the science and practice for care transitions in stroke rehabilitation, it is necessary to design and evaluate interventions that align intervention components with the needs of both individual patients and the healthcare system.

Clinical outcomes focused on physical function and disability in TCIs for stroke

Clinical outcome within the stroke TCI literature emphasizes the domains of physical function and activities of daily living, despite the evidence that stroke recovery encompasses more than physical rehabilitation [6]. This limited focus on cognitive-communication outcomes in TCI studies is consistent with the restricted inclusion of stroke survivors with aphasia and cognitive disorders in study populations. Cognitive-communication disorders affect approximately one-third of stroke survivors [82], yet few clinical guidelines comprehensively address their management [83]. Cognitive impairment and mood disturbances frequently coexist with physical deficits and can lead to long-term disability, even among those with mild stroke [84]. Unmet cognitive and communication needs post-stroke pose barriers to healthcare access [85], community re-integration [86–88] and life participation [89,90]. Coordinated, comprehensive care and improved support for community re-integration are important priorities for stroke survivors with aphasia and their care partners [91]. Future research focused on the design and adaptation of TCIs in rehabilitation should consider the cognitive and communication needs of stroke survivors.

Designing and implementing complex interventions for transitional care

Transitional care is a complex process involving the patient, their family and social supports, healthcare institutions and community resources [92]. As such, TCIs are also complex to address these various levels of influence. Complex intervention development requires specification of intervention components and explicit mapping of how these components are hypothesized to change desired outcomes. The Medical Research Council (MRC) offers guidance in the design and testing of complex interventions with an emphasis on not only the effects of the intervention, but also an understanding of how the intervention works to guide future testing, adaptation and implementation [93]. Incorporating frameworks and design methods, such as the MRC guidance, Intervention Mapping [94], Multiphase Optimization Strategy (MOST) [95], and Sequential Multiple Assignment Randomized Trials (SMART) [96] has potential to advance the science in post-stroke transitional care. These methods support explicit specification of the relationships between intervention components and outcomes and guide the evaluation of different combinations of intervention components to optimize efficacy.

Limitations

While this scoping review provides an in-depth analysis of the transitional care interventions studies in stroke rehabilitation, there are limitations that should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings. First, our inclusion criteria restricted the review to English-language, peer-reviewed studies. This decision excluded non-English sources and grey literature (such as technical reports and dissertations), potentially underrepresenting the breadth of transitional care interventions in stroke rehabilitation. Additionally, we included only studies published in 2012 or later to build upon Bettger and colleagues prior synthesis [29]. However, this approach may have excluded earlier foundational studies (i.e. [97,98]) that continue to inform transitional care practice. Second, although we searched multiple databases using a structured keyword strategy, it is possible that relevant studies were missed due to article indexing and organization of subject headers in the selected databases. Specifically, research grounded in public health, implementation science, or interdisciplinary fields (i.e. nursing, medicine) may not have been fully captured. Finally, due to the diversity of intervention designs, intervention dosage and outcome measures we were unable to draw conclusions across studies about efficacy. Lack of specificity of research reporting also limited our ability to evaluate the relationship of specific intervention components and measured outcomes. Future reviews employing more targeted inclusion criteria (i.e. specific outcome, RCT designs, etc.) may support the use of formal meta-analytic techniques to assess the impact of interventions.

Conclusions

This scoping review mapped the characteristics, intervention components and outcomes of transitional care interventions for individuals recovering from stroke. By coding individual- and system-level intervention components using the National Transitions of Care Coalition (NTOCC), we synthesized intervention components using a common language across studies within the stroke rehabilitation literature. We identified that stroke TCIs range in complexity and focus primarily on individual-level intervention components. Despite the literature suggesting the need for system-level change, system-level intervention components are underutilized in TCIs in stroke rehabilitation. Future research should consider system-level factors in the design, testing and implementation of TCIs in stroke rehabilitation care. This review also highlights gaps in addressing the cognitive-communication needs of stroke survivors within transitional care. Future work is needed to tailor transitional care resources and care models to be relevant and accessible to persons with cognitive-communication disabilities.
