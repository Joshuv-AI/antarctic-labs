Impact of Simultaneous Initiation of Finerenone and Empagliflozin on Albuminuria Irrespective of Baseline HbA1c Levels: A Participant‐Level Exploratory Analysis of the Randomised CONFIDENCE Trial
Authors: Janet B. McGill, Jennifer B. Green, Hiddo J. L. Heerspink, Johannes F. E. Mann, Amy K. Mottl, Julio Rosenstock, Peter Rossing, Muthiah Vaduganathan, Masaomi Nangaku, Meike Brinker, Charlie Scott, Katja Rohwedder, Li Li, Na Li, Margarida Seco, Rajiv Agarwal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsThis prespecified analysis evaluated whether the safety and efficacy of finerenone and a sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor (SGLT2i) combination vary by baseline glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c).Materials and MethodsAdults with type 2 diabetes and a urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (UACR) of 100 − < 5000 mg/g, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) 30–90 mL/min/1.73 m2 and HbA1c < 11% (< 97 mmol/mol) were randomised (1:1:1) to once‐daily finerenone, empagliflozin or combination. Primary outcome was UACR change from baseline at Day 180.ResultsBaseline HbA1c was available for 781/800 CONFIDENCE participants; baseline HbA1c and eGFR mean values (standard deviation) were 7.3% (1.2%) and 54.2 mL/min/1.73 m2 (17.1 mL/min/1.73 m2). At Day 180, HbA1c decreased with combination therapy and empagliflozin (both least‐squares mean change −0.1% [95% confidence interval −0.2, −0.0]), with no change with finerenone. In total, 212, 190, 188 and 191 participants were included in HbA1c Quartiles 1 (HbA1c ≤ 6.4% [≤ 46 mmol/mol]), 2 (HbA1c > 6.4 and ≤ 7.1% [> 46 and ≤ 54 mmol/mol]), 3 (HbA1c > 7.1 and ≤ 7.9% [> 54 and ≤ 63 mmol/mol]) and 4 (HbA1c > 7.9% [> 63 mmol/mol]), respectively. Change from baseline in HbA1c at Day 180 was greatest in Quartile 4. At Day 180, combination therapy reduced UACR from baseline in all HbA1c quartiles to a greater extent than either monotherapy (mean [95% CI] –53% [−63, −42], −47% [−57, −34], −63% [−70, −54] and −55% [−65, −42], in Quartiles 1–4, respectively).ConclusionSimultaneous initiation with finerenone and an SGLT2i reduced UACR compared with monotherapy and was well‐tolerated irrespective of HbA1c levels.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a common and devastating complication that affects 40%–60% of people with type 2 diabetes [1, 2]. Diabetes is the leading cause of end‐stage kidney disease (ESKD) in the United States and globally [3, 4], and declining kidney function, as well as elevated albuminuria, are associated with increased risk of ESKD, cardiovascular (CV) events and mortality [5, 6, 7, 8].

While the pathogenesis of CKD in type 2 diabetes is complex, hyperglycaemia is a well‐established pathogenic factor [9, 10]. Glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) is a predictor of estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) decline in people with or without diabetes, irrespective of stage of CKD [10]. A recent post hoc analysis of the ACCORD trial (NCT00000620) showed that among individuals with CKD and type 2 diabetes, higher baseline HbA1c was associated with more rapid decline in eGFR, with the strength of the association increasing with the extent of albuminuria [9]. Elevated HbA1c is also associated with increased risk of CV disease and all‐cause mortality [11, 12]. Conversely, lower HbA1c over time is associated with lower risk of developing CKD [13]. While the impact of HbA1c on the progression of established CKD is less well studied, the ADVANCE trial (NCT00949286) demonstrated that intensive glucose control in participants with type 2 diabetes was associated with a long‐term reduction in the rate of ESKD [14].

Several classes of drugs have shown efficacy in reducing eGFR decline and improving kidney outcomes in people with CKD and type 2 diabetes, including renin–angiotensin system inhibitors (angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors [ACEis] and angiotensin‐receptor blockers [ARBs]) [15], sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2is) [16], glucagon‐like peptide 1 (GLP‐1) receptor agonists (RAs) [17, 18] and the nonsteroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist finerenone [19, 20]. Albuminuria, as indicated by elevated urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (UACR), is a marker of kidney injury, and a predictor of CKD progression and of adverse clinical outcomes [21, 22, 23]. Meta‐analyses of clinical trials of therapies that slow CKD progression demonstrate that reduction in albuminuria is associated with improved kidney composite outcomes, making it an important surrogate outcome for clinical trials [21, 22].

A greater degree of UACR reduction with simultaneous initiation of finerenone and empagliflozin, versus either drug alone, in participants with type 2 diabetes and CKD with albuminuria was demonstrated in the CONFIDENCE clinical trial (NCT05254002) [24]. In the FIDELIO‐DKD (NCT02540993) and FIGARO‐DKD (NCT02545049) studies, baseline HbA1c level did not influence the effect of finerenone on kidney or CV outcomes, or reduction in UACR [25, 26]. In analyses of the CANVAS studies (NCT01032629 and NCT01989754) of canagliflozin in people with type 2 diabetes, HbA1c did not have a strong mediating effect on kidney outcomes [27]. In EMPA‐REG‐OUTCOME (NCT01131676), using a univariable Week 12 landmark analysis, HbA1c mediated 28.3% of the effect of empagliflozin on the composite kidney outcome in people with type 2 diabetes and established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease [28]. The objective of the current exploratory analysis was to determine whether baseline HbA1c modifies the effect on UACR reduction of the combination of finerenone and empagliflozin versus each drug as monotherapy in the CONFIDENCE trial.

Research Design and Methods

Trial Setting

The CONFIDENCE trial was a double‐blind, randomised, comparator trial of simultaneous initiation of empagliflozin and finerenone, finerenone alone or empagliflozin alone, in people with CKD and type 2 diabetes. The trial design, study protocol, statistical analysis plan and primary results have been published previously [24, 29, 30]. The trial was approved by the institutional review board at each participating site and all participants provided written informed consent. An independent data and safety monitoring committee was convened to oversee participant safety. The trial was first registered on clinicaltrials.gov on February 23, 2022 (NCT05254002).

Participants

Eligible adults had type 2 diabetes with HbA1c < 11% (97 mmol/mol), an eGFR between 30 and 90 mL/min/1.73 m2, and UACR between 100 and < 5000 mg/g. UACR was measured as the average of the first morning urine samples collected on 3 consecutive days. Participants were taking the maximally tolerated dose of an ACEi or ARB for more than 1 month prior to screening. Participants were excluded if they had a serum potassium level > 4.8 mmol/L, type 1 diabetes, or had used a potassium binder within 8 weeks before screening. Any diabetes therapy was permitted except for use of an SGLT2i or SGLT1/2i within 8 weeks prior to screening. GLP‐1 RA treatments were allowed, provided the dosage had remained stable for at least 4 weeks prior to the screening visit. Dose reductions (down‐titration of the GLP‐1 RA) were allowed during the study. Dose increases (up‐titration of the GLP‐1 RA) were not allowed.

Randomisation

Using an interactive web response system, participants were randomised 1:1:1 to receive either finerenone (10 or 20 mg [target dose] once daily, with a placebo matching empagliflozin), empagliflozin (10 mg once daily, with a placebo matching finerenone), or both drugs in combination. Participants receiving finerenone started at 20 mg daily if baseline eGFR was ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, and at 10 mg daily if baseline eGFR was < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, with up‐titration to 20 mg after 4 weeks if serum potassium was ≤ 4.8 mmol/L and the eGFR decrease was < 30% compared with the previous visit. The study was double‐blind, with treatment assignments concealed from investigators, treating physicians and participants.

Trial Visits and Assessments

There were seven prespecified trial visits. During the screening visit, urine samples for UACR assessment collected on 3 consecutive mornings at the participant's home were submitted. For subsequent visits, urine was collected on 2 consecutive mornings. Blood samples for measurement of serum potassium and for calculation of eGFR from serum creatinine were taken at every visit. HbA1c was measured at screening; Days 1, 14, 30, 90 and 180; and at 30 days after the last dose at both central and local laboratories. All UACR, serum potassium, and serum creatinine assessments were conducted at a central laboratory. Study medication was discontinued on Day 180 (±5 days). UACR, eGFR and serum potassium were reassessed 30 days later to evaluate the effects of discontinuation of study treatment.

Study Outcomes

The primary efficacy outcome was the change from baseline to Day 180 in the log‐transformed mean UACR. Changes in HbA1c and eGFR from baseline to Day 180 were analysed. Adverse events, adverse events leading to treatment discontinuations, serious adverse events and serious adverse events leading to treatment discontinuation were recorded. The incidence of hyperkalaemia was reported.

Statistical Analyses

In this analysis, participants were categorised into quartiles based on HbA1c at baseline. Efficacy analyses were performed using data from participants with HbA1c baseline measurements in the full analysis set (all randomised participants except those who did not receive at least one dose of study drug or had major Good Clinical Practice violations). The primary analysis used a mixed model for repeated measures with log‐transformed UACR as the dependent variable, as previously reported [24]. The model output was expressed as least‐squares mean baseline/treatment ratio, and the geometric mean ratios were converted to show percentage changes from baseline/between treatments.

A mixed model for repeated measures was used for estimating change in HbA1c from baseline by HbA1c quartile and included the factors treatment, time, treatment‐by‐time interaction, UACR category at screening, eGFR category at screening, HbA1c baseline value and HbA1c baseline value‐by‐time interaction. Separate unstructured covariance patterns were estimated for each treatment group.

Changes in eGFR from baseline to end of trial were analysed using a mixed model for repeated measures. eGFR was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation [31] with a modification for Japanese participants [32].

Subgroup analyses by HbA1c quartiles were prespecified prior to database lock. Given the exploratory nature of these analyses, models were not adjusted for multiple comparisons.

Treatment‐emergent adverse events were reported for participants who had received at least one dose of a trial drug and had an adverse event that had started or worsened after the first dose and up to 3 days after any temporary or permanent interruption of the trial treatment. Adverse events were summarised by treatment group and HbA1c quartile.

Results

A total of 818 participants were randomised across 14 countries, of whom 800 comprised the full analysis set. Baseline HbA1c was available for 781 participants, and 212, 190, 188 and 191 participants were included in HbA1c Quartiles 1, 2, 3 and 4, respectively (Table 1). HbA1c quartiles were defined as follows: Quartile 1 (HbA1c ≤ 6.4% [≤ 46 mmol/mol]), Quartile 2 (HbA1c > 6.4 and ≤ 7.1% [> 46 and ≤ 54 mmol/mol]), Quartile 3 (HbA1c > 7.1 and ≤ 7.9% [> 54 and ≤ 63 mmol/mol]), and Quartile 4 (HbA1c > 7.9% [> 63 mmol/mol]). Mean HbA1c (standard deviation [SD]) was 7.3 (1.2)% (56 [13.1] mmol/mol) overall, and 6.0 (0.4)% (42 [4.4] mmol/mol), 6.8 (0.2)% (51 [2.2] mmol/mol), 7.5 (0.2)% (58 [2.2] mmol/mol), and 9.0 (0.8)% (75 [8.7] mmol/mol), in Quartiles 1, 2, 3 and 4, respectively.

Overall, the mean (SD) eGFR was 54.2 (17.1) mL/min/1.73 m2 and median UACR (interquartile range) was 579 (292–1092) mg/g. Most baseline characteristics were similar across HbA1c quartiles, with some notable differences. Higher baseline HbA1c quartiles were associated with greater baseline body weight and body mass index. Participants in Quartiles 3 and 4 had a higher median UACR (628 and 649 mg/g, respectively) and greater proportions of participants with diabetic retinopathy (20.2% and 20.9%, respectively) compared with Quartiles 1 and 2 (520 and 504 mg/g, and 10.8% and 13.2%, respectively; Table 1). In addition, the proportion of participants using insulin increased between Quartile 1 (20.3%) and Quartile 4 (62.8%; Table 1). Within each HbA1c quartile, baseline characteristics were generally balanced across treatment arms (Table S1).

HbA1c Effect

Change from baseline in HbA1c at each time point up to Day 180 by treatment is shown in Figure 1A. At Day 90, HbA1c decreased by approximately 0.2% in the combination and empagliflozin alone groups and did not change in the group treated with finerenone alone. At Day 180, change in HbA1c from baseline was numerically greater in the combination and empagliflozin groups compared with finerenone alone, but differences between the monotherapy and combination groups were not statistically significant. At Day 180, changes in HbA1c were greatest in Quartile 4, but consistent across treatment arms in each quartile (Figure 1B; Table S2). Accordingly, there were no significant differences among treatment arms in change from baseline in HbA1c at Day 180 in any HbA1c quartile.

Adverse Events and Safety Assessments

Adverse events by HbA1c quartile are shown in Table S3. The rate of adverse events and serious adverse events was similar across all HbA1c subgroups. Investigator‐assessed hyperkalaemia occurred in 9.9%, 6.8%, 6.5%, and 9.9% of participants in Quartiles 1, 2, 3, and 4, respectively, for total participants. Within each quartile, the frequency of investigator‐assessed hyperkalaemia was greater with combination and finerenone alone compared with empagliflozin alone, occurring in < 15% of participants in any treatment group. Hypoglycaemia and hyperglycaemia events were uncommon, occurring in ten and five participants overall, respectively. No severe hypoglycaemia events were reported.

Conclusions

This prespecified subgroup analysis of the CONFIDENCE trial demonstrated consistent benefits of simultaneous initiation of finerenone and empagliflozin in participants with CKD with albuminuria and type 2 diabetes irrespective of baseline HbA1c. Consistent with the overall findings of the CONFIDENCE trial [24], combination therapy generally reduced UACR more than either monotherapy across HbA1c quartiles.

Blood glucose control, as indicated by HbA1c, is an important risk factor for the development of diabetic complications and their progression. People with type 2 diabetes and poorly controlled HbA1c are at increased risk of more rapid progression of CKD, CV events, and death [9, 10, 11, 12]. While maintaining good glycaemic control appears to offer protection from multiple diabetes complications, including microvascular complications such as retinopathy and nephropathy, and macrovascular complications such as CV disease events [33], a very intensive treatment strategy intended to achieve near‐normoglycaemia in the ACCORD study using available therapies at that time was associated with higher mortality and no reduction in the risk of nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke or CV death compared with standard treatment [34]. Higher baseline HbA1c is associated with the rate of decline of eGFR, although this association is stronger in people with poor glycaemic control with higher levels of albuminuria [9].

The findings of this analysis are consistent with previous outcome studies with finerenone. In a subgroup analysis of FIDELITY (pooled analysis of FIDELIO‐DKD and FIGARO‐DKD), the kidney benefits of finerenone in participants with CKD and type 2 diabetes were independent of baseline HbA1c [26]. Similarly, in the DAPA‐CKD and EMPA‐KIDNEY studies in patients with CKD, the effects of dapagliflozin and empagliflozin on the composite outcomes of eGFR decline, ESKD or kidney or CV death were consistent in people with or without diabetes [35, 36].

Participants in the CONFIDENCE trial had relatively low HbA1c levels, as evidenced by the mean (SD) baseline HbA1c of 7.3 (1.2)% (56 [13.2] mmol/mol). Unsurprisingly, patients in Quartile 4, with a mean baseline HbA1c of 9.0% (75 mmol/mol), had greater reductions in HbA1c than those in the other quartiles. HbA1c changed little from baseline at Day 180 in Quartiles 1–3, while in Quartile 4, HbA1c at Day 180 was reduced compared with baseline, and this reduction was observed in all three randomised treatment groups (Figure 2).

Although prespecified, this analysis and its findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis‐generating in nature, given the inherent limitations associated with secondary subgroup analyses. Baseline UACR values were imbalanced across treatment groups within individual HbA1c quartiles, which may have impacted comparison against the different HbA1c subgroups. Baseline HbA1c values were also not available for all study participants who were part of the full analysis set. The interpretation of HbA1c in individuals with CKD is subject to important limitations. Several factors commonly observed in CKD, including anaemia, iron deficiency and altered erythrocyte lifespan, are known to influence HbA1c levels and may result in under‐ or overestimation of long‐term glycaemic control [37, 38]. Moreover, the study population exhibited relatively well‐controlled glycaemia (overall mean baseline HbA1c: 7.3%), potentially limiting the generalisability of these findings to populations with more severe hyperglycaemia. Finally, the relatively short duration of treatment exposure (180 days) limits the ability to assess longer‐term outcomes.

In conclusion, this exploratory analysis showed that the benefit and tolerability of simultaneous combination therapy with finerenone and empagliflozin in participants with CKD and type 2 diabetes were observed irrespective of baseline HbA1c. Further prospective studies are needed to confirm this finding and explore potential mechanisms underlying the benefit of finerenone in individuals with poor glycaemic control.
