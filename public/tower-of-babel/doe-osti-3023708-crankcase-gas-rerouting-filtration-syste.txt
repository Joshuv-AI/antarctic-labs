Public Executive Summary

The U.S. has the opportunity to transform the energy sector by aggressively expanding the use of
abundant, domestic natural gas (NG) as a fuel. With the intrinsic composition of NG having a
higher H:C ratio (~3.6) than that of diesel (H:C ~ 1.8), lower levels of CO2, the preeminent
greenhouse gas (GHG), are generated during combustion. A major challenge is the emissions
abatement of the primary NG constituent methane (CH4). Its inherent stability makes it a potent
GHG. Lean-burn NG engines used to drive compressors in the NG supply chain, generate
electricity, and repower ships are substantial sources of methane emissions. For example,
combustion slip accounts for 24% of methane emissions from the U.S. NG gathering and
boosting sector, or 6% of methane emissions from NG Systems. Total hydrocarbon (THC)
emissions, or “fuel slip”, from 4-stroke lean-burn NG engines is composed primarily of methane
and is typically between 2-5% of fuel input when emission from the crankcase ventilation gas is
included. The primary objective of the ARPA-E AMPLIFY (Advanced Material Processes for
Leveraging Industrial Facility Yield) program (DE-FOA-0002404) is to develop enabling
technologies to achieve an overall methane conversion exceeding 99.5% (0.5% methane
remaining) for 4-stroke lean-burn NG engines.
In engines that use a pre-mixed air/fuel mixture, fuel is compressed into the piston ring area
during the compression stroke. During combustion, when pressure rise is even higher, some of
the ring belt mixture leaks past the rings and valve stem seals into the crankcase and is usually
vented from the crankcase to the atmosphere. While the recirculation of crankcase gases to the
intake of automotive engines is commonplace, in industrial engines closed crankcase ventilation
(CCV) systems are not standard. Removal of the oil mist from the crankcase gases is not a
simple matter. The long service life required of industrial engines makes oil contamination of
intake parts such as the turbocharger compressor and intercooler troublesome. Also, if the oil is
not removed with a high efficiency, some chemical species present in the oil can harm
aftertreatment systems. The water vapor in the crankcase presents an even bigger challenge. The
water vapor can condense or even freeze, causing contamination of oil, condensation in intake
system components, impact erosion of the turbocharger compressor wheel, and in extreme cases
can form enough ice to block flow in the crankcase ventilation system. Water condensation
challenges primarily exist in low ambient temperatures, which are not uncommon for gas
compression engines.
In this work a CCV system using a combination of high velocity impacting and filtration was
developed to remove oil from the crankcase gases with a high level of efficiency. The CCV
system includes one connection to the crankcase, a system to route crankcase gas (CCG), an oil
mist removal assembly, a crankcase pressure control device, integrated heaters, a drain back to
the crankcase, piping to route CCG into the intake, and instrumentation/controls. The crankcase
gases are re-ingested into the engine, allowing the methane in the crankcase gas mixture (∼1% of
fuel methane) to be burned with a co-benefit of increasing engine efficiency. The goal of this
project was to reduce methane emissions from crankcase gas from 4-stroke lean-burn engines by
greater than 75%.
A prototype CCV system was developed for laboratory testing. The system was evaluated based
on overall methane emissions reduction, engine efficiency improvement, and oil mist
removal/handling. The system reliability was then evaluated with a one-year field test. The field
test was performed at a compressor station in the Denver-Julesburg basin in Colorado. This site

 5

experienced a wide range of temperatures (subzero to >90°F) and weather extremes (snow, high
winds, etc.). During the field test the CCV system performance (methane reduction, oil mist
removal, etc.) and engine operation were monitored. Other reliability concerns such as water
condensation and intake system health were examined. The field test helped to define CCV
maintenance requirements such as replacement of consumables like filter cartridges.
As part of the AMPLIFY program a Cat® G3516J 1.0 MW gas compression was installed and
commissioned at the Colorado State University Powerhouse Energy Campus. The prototype
CCV was subsequently installed on the G3516J engine. Testing was then performed that showed
greater than 75% crankcase methane emission reduction, achieving the main project goal. The oil
mist removal efficiency was measured and demonstrated that the CCV oil removal system
reduced oil mist well below published original equipment manufacturer (OEM) limits. The CCV
system was installed in the field on a Cat® G3516ULB engine and initial performance testing
was carried out at low, medium, and high load conditions. Methane destruction and oil mist
removal efficiency were quantified. The crankcase methane emission destruction was measured
to be above 95% and oil mist removal efficiency exceeded the OEM requirement.
The performance of the CCV system on the field engine was closely studied during the one-year
field test to determine the robustness of the system related to oil droplet capture and the ability
for the system to sustain high methane reduction efficiency. Multiple filter elements on the CCV
system were evaluated over various replacement durations to refine maintenance requirements.
The engine was partially disassembled about nine months into the durability test to inspect the
turbocharger, intercooler, throttle valve, and other intake system components, which showed no
significant effects of the CCV system on intake system components. The engine operated
throughout the durability test with no detectable changes to engine controls (e.g. speed, torque,
lambda, or emissions), indicating the CCV system had no measurable impact on engine
operation. The CCV system heaters operated as intended and no water condensation was
observed. Crankcase pressure was also monitored and the CCV crankcase pressure control
system maintained crankcase pressure at a constant value, well below the OEM limit.
Methane destruction was calculated from emissions data collected monthly throughout the one-
year field test. Most data were collected at nominal load conditions (about 90%) for the
compressor station, which remained relatively constant. The crankcase methane destruction was
calculated to be above 95% throughout the duration of the field test. Total engine (exhaust +
crankcase) methane emission reduction ranged between 24-37%. To maintain oil mist reduction
below the OEM requirement the CCV system filter element should to be replaced every 1500
hours, which is a typical maintenance interval for other engine consumables. Caterpillar® Inc.
now offers CCV systems for new 3500 series engines and CCV retrofit packages for existing
engines.

Accomplishments and Objectives

Background and Project Overview

The U.S. could transform the energy sector by aggressively expanding the use of abundant,
domestic natural gas (NG) as a fuel. With the intrinsic composition of NG having a higher H:C
ratio (~3.6) than that of diesel (H:C.~ 1.8), lower levels of CO2, the preeminent greenhouse gas
(GHG), are generated during combustion. A major challenge is the abatement of the primary NG
constituent methane (CH4). Its inherent stability makes it a potent GHG, justifying emerging
regulations to curtail its emissions. Lean-burn NG engines used to drive compressors in the NG
supply chain, generate electricity, and repower ships are substantial sources of methane
emissions. For example, combustion slip accounts for 24% of methane emissions from the U.S.
NG gathering and boosting sector, or 6% of methane emissions from NG Systems. Total
hydrocarbon (THC) emissions, or “fuel slip”, from 4-stroke lean-burn NG engines is composed
primarily of methane and is typically between 3-5% when emission from the crankcase
ventilation gas is included. The primary objective of the ARPA-E AMPLIFY program (see DE-
FOA-0002404) is to develop enabling technologies to achieve an overall methane conversion
exceeding 99.5% (0.5% methane remaining) for 4-stroke lean-burn NG engines.

In engines that use a pre-mixed air/fuel mixture, fuel is compressed into the piston ring area
during the compression stroke. During combustion, when pressures rise is even higher, some of
the ring belt mixture leaks past the rings and valve stem seals into the crankcase and is usually
vented from the crankcase to the atmosphere. While the recirculation of crankcase gases to the
intake of automotive engines is commonplace, in industrial engines closed crankcase ventilation
(CCV) systems are not standard. Removal of the oil mist from the crankcase gases is not a
simple matter. The long service life required of industrial engines makes oil contamination of
intake parts such as the turbocharger compressor troublesome. Also, if the oil is not removed
with a high efficiency, some chemical species present in the oil can harm aftertreatment systems.
Crankcase ventilation for gas compression engines is especially challenging. The crankcase
fumes contain an oil mist that must be removed and ideally returned to the engine oil sump. If
the oil mist is not removed, the oil will impact and coat the turbocharger compressor, causing a
loss in efficiency. The water vapor in the crankcase presents an even bigger challenge. The water
vapor can condense or even freeze, causing contamination of oil, condensation in intake system
components, impact erosion of the turbocharger compressor wheel, and in extreme cases can
form enough ice to block flow in the crankcase ventilation system. This flow blockage results in
abnormally high crankcase pressures which can cause seal failures and excess oil consumption
past the piston rings and down the valve guides. These challenges primarily exist only in low
ambient temperatures. However, gas compression engines are commonly used in low ambient
temperatures, so the crankcase ventilation system will need to be tolerant of these conditions.
In the present work, a CCV system using a combination of high velocity impacting and filtration
will be developed to remove oil from the crankcase gases with a high level of efficiency (Figure
1). Selection of the location of the system connection to the crankcase is critical. A poor location
selection can result in high levels of lube oil droplet flow, increasing the oil loading on the
impinger and filter. This leads to increased maintenance and cost. The crankcase gases will then

 7

be re-ingested into the engine, allowing the methane in the crankcase gas mixture (∼1% of fuel
methane) to be burned with anticipated co-benefit of increasing engine efficiency.

The goal of this project was to reduce methane emissions from crankcase gas from 4-stroke lean-
burn engines by >75% over all engine operating conditions with a system that meets
AMPLIFY’s LCA and LCCA objectives, modified for the 75% target, herein referred to as the
“modified Stage 1 FOA objectives” and “modified Stage 2 FOA objectives”. The crankcase gas
routing/filtration system consists of one or more connections to the crankcase, a system to route
crankcase gas (CCG), a filter assembly, drain(s) back to the crankcase, piping to route CCG into
the intake, and instrumentation/controls. The filter assembly will uses a combination of high
velocity impacting and filtration to remove oil from the crankcase gas with a high level of
efficiency. The crankcase connection location(s) are critical in minimizing oil droplet flux into
the filter assembly. The goal of this project was to address the key operating requirements to
reliably route the CCG into the intake system to reduce methane emissions and increase engine
system efficiency. The first objective was to develop a prototype system for laboratory
demonstration. The system was evaluated based on overall methane emissions reduction, engine
efficiency improvement, and oil droplet removal/handling. The second objective was to evaluate
the system reliability with a one-year field test. The field test was performed at a site in the
Denver-Julesburg basin in Colorado. This location experienced a wide range of temperatures
(subzero to >90°F) and weather extremes (snow, high winds, etc.). During the field test the CCG
rerouting/filtration system performance (methane reduction, oil droplet removal, etc.) and engine
operation (fuel consumption, turbocharger efficiency, etc.) were monitored. Other reliability
concerns such as ice build-up due to water condensation were examined. The field test helped to
define maintenance requirements such as replacement of consumables like filter cartridges.

T2M study

Customer incentives, and thus customer interest in methane emissions reduction systems like
crankcase gas (CCG) filtration and routing has been widely variable. In the initial Tech to Market
program for CCG filtration and routing, there was little incentive for gas compression customers
to apply crankcase gas filtration to their G3500 gas compression engines, other than to use
otherwise vented methane to help power the engine (effectively increasing efficiency). Later,
incentives ebbed and flowed.

Marketing

During the first stages of this research, some market tailwinds to CCG filtration adoption appeared,
specifically with proposed fugitive methane taxation in the Inflation Reduction Act. Using the
Lifecycle Cost of Carbon Avoidance (LCCA) calculation, the estimated lifetime methane
reduction benefit at a modest methane tax rate and minimum durability of the CCG filtration
system indicated that the application can support a retail price of the system on the order of $20,550
(plus $1000 for installation). That retail price is far enough above the manufacturing cost to allow
for reasonable installer profit.

 14

The implementation of the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) methane fee for 2024 ($900/tonne
methane) is acting as a market adoption tailwind with Oil and Gas operators, many of which are
Cat® customers. Filtration and reingestion of crankcase gases is seen as low hanging fruit for
providing methane reductions for these customers. While mechanisms for fee assessment and
collection have not yet been formulated, fee accrual mechanisms are being applied.

The IRA methane fee ratchets up to $1200/tonne in 2025 and $1500/tonne in 2026+, increasing
fee risk and sentiment for adoption. Considering this fee escalation combined with anticipated
growth of crankcase gas rate over 20,000 hours of operation, the supportable price based on fee
avoidance becomes $31,340+$1000 in 2024, $42,080+$1000 in 2025, and $52,850+$1000 in
2026.

The value is significantly higher than the cost, such that CCG filtration technology is now offered
by Caterpillar® Inc. in a press release announcing the production availability for retrofit on G3600
engines and first fit on G3600 A4 gen 2 engines for gas compression,
https://www.cat.com/en_US/news/engine-press-releases/Caterpillar-oil-and-gas-closed-crank-
ventilation-system-reduces-engine-methane-emissions.html.

By the last quarter of the research, and with the cancellation of the methane fee in the IRA by the
current US Senate, the value of technology is reduced to the fuel savings. Using a value of methane
as $5 per GJ of heating value, that would represent $250 per ton methane. As such, the value of
the methane at risk (and thus of the technology) is about $5000 + $1000 installation. The business
case for commercializing the technology in the US is weaker, but many customers are interested

Intellectual Property (IP)

This task consists of document background IP, IP generation, and planned licensing arrangements
(if any) for the implementation of crankcase methane reduction systems.

The function of the CCV system is to route engine combustion blow-by from the crankcase,
remove nearly all the oil mist from the blow-by, route the cleaned blow-by gases to the charge air
system, and return the collected oil to the oil sump.

High efficiency oil mist separators have been in use for crankcase gases on diesel engines for many
years. The technologies for separating the oil droplets from the crankcase gases include coalescing
filtration (developed prior to 2000), tangential (centrifugal or cyclone) flow (applied ~2010),
labyrinth flow separation (also applied ~2010), passive mechanical variable geometry impaction

 15

(~2010), and multistage combinations thereof. Features can include pressure regulation and boost
assist to facilitate flow as blow-by rates can change during operation and with engine aging.

There are four large filtration suppliers that have provided such separators to diesel engine OEMs
(in alphabetical order, Donaldson, Hilliard, Racor (Parker Hannifin), Solberg, and Walker). The
purchase of finished (PF) designs considered for the G3516J at the CSU Powerhouse are shown in
Figure 2. All IP and design control for each separation technology is owned by the respective
supplier. The highlighted design was the one chosen for the lab engine.

The mist separation designs are no different for natural gas engines than for diesel engines, but
custom attachment to each engine family is necessary to minimize heat loss for avoiding ice
accumulation and oil-water emulsions in cold ambient. Opportunity for system level IP
(combinations of separation, flow control, plus other functions) may arise.

Entrained Oil concentration:
Blow-by gas vented from the crankcase contains oil that has the potential to foul the engine’s air
system when recirculated. Buildup of oil on turbocharger compressor vanes and intercooler heat
exchange surfaces can drastically reduce system performance, leading to shorter maintenance
intervals and unplanned downtime. The crankcase recirculation system must eliminate a large
portion of the entrained oil to ensure that the system performance and reliability are not affected,
and the final product remains attractive to the customer.

To this end, it was of interest to measure both the concentration and distribution of the entrained
oil in the crankcase gas in the field. Concentration and distribution data were taken at the same
engine/compressor operating conditions as the composition data reported above. Nominally, these
were low, normal, and high engine load conditions achieved through variations in compressor
suction/discharge pressures. Concentration measurements were performed across load, with 2-6
measurements per condition. Distribution measurements were only taken at the nominal load
condition with one replicate. Figure 5 features the processed results of these measurements.
Entrained oil concentration measurements were highly repeatable and remarkably consistent,
showing no significant correlation to engine load or crankcase flowrate. Concentration was also
quite high,resulting in fully saturated filters after just 3 minutes of runtime and peg the real-time
aerosol sampling instruments brought to the field (upper measurement limit: 150 mg/m3-std).
Distribution measurements also proved consistent, though only one replicate could be taken. The
majority of entrained droplets sampled were larger than 100 μm, meaning the specified filtration
technology will likely be sufficient to reach 99.7% dropout efficiency. Both concentration and
distribution data informed filtration/impaction equipment design and serveed as a baseline for final
field-tested performance.
Flow Rate:
The flow rate of the crankcase recirculation system is bounded by the expected minimum and
maximum blow-by flow rates. According to Caterpillar® guidelines for their other engines,
recirculation systems should be sized to accommodate twice the amount of blow-by gas that is
expected for a given application to guarantee that crankcase pressure buildup is minimized [1].
That is, for the anticipated recirculation flow rate, piping and filtration equipment must be sized
such that the crankcase backpressure resulting from doubling the flow rate will not exceed
prescribed system limits. The field measurements taken were between 283 L/min – 425 L/min.
Back Pressure:
Caterpillar® guidelines indicate that crankcase pressures should not vary more than 0.25 kPa from
the ambient barometric pressure for Cat® G3300, G3400 and G3500 engines [2]. In some
applications, pressure monitoring sensors are installed and will shut down the engine if crankcase
pressure exceeds the set limit. Others have sampled crankcase pressures for gas compression
engines operating in diverse locations and reported pressures ranging from -1.7 kPa or +1.2 kPa
[3].

Temperature:
Condensation of the water vapor in the crankcase and recirculating equipment must be avoided to
mitigate the potential of ice buildup for low temperature applications. In general, recirculated gases
must always be kept above the dew point temperature for the given concentration of water vapor.
Further, the mixing of water with oil could cause an emulsion that can clog the filtration equipment
and negatively impact the lubrication properties of the oil. Therefore, the CCV system must have
proper insulation and a possible heating element to maintain the gas above its dew point. For the
G3500 series engine we estimated a dew point limit of 25 °C using the saturation temperature of
the measured water vapor content. During our August field study, the crankcase gas temperature
ranged from 65-70 °C, but this was during a time of the year that the site ambient temperature was
between 35 and 40 °C. The preliminary design for the oil filtration device features embedded
heaters and their load is expected to closely correlate with ambient temperature.

Recirculation System Requirements and Preliminary Design:
Existing literature guidelines limit entrained oil carryover of a CCV system to 1.36-3.06 mg/kWh
across all applications [2]. The vent gas concentration and flowrate measured in the field resulted
in a mean oil carryover rate of 5.44 mg/kWh. Cascade impactor measurements showed that 82.4%
of the oil droplets had aerodynamic diameters greater than 0.3 µm, with the remaining 17.6%
composed of finer droplets and vaporous-state oil. The design of the CCV filtration drum (featured
in Figure 6) was expected to eliminate upwards of 99% of the particulates greater than 0.3 µm in
diameter, with unknown filtration efficiency at smaller sizes. The filtration drum applies to three
separation components. The first is a gas cyclone where particles larger than 10 µm will have
sufficient momentum to impact the walls of the drum and drop out. The second employs an inertial
separation impactor where the gas and entrained particles are forced to bend around flow
obstruction and the larger particles (2.5µm-10µm) will impact and drop out. The last stage is the
coalescing media filter where fine particles will be filtered by a coalescing filter element. The
coalescing filter is expected to require replacement at the regular oil change interval.

The performance of a variety of filtration equipment was reported on by Kissner, et. al. [2] and is
summarized in Figure 7. Our system employs a combination of the single cyclone, impactor with
fleece, and filter impactor technologies. In so doing, maximum oil dropouts can be achieved across
a wide range of droplet sizes. Back pressure on the crankcase resulting from this implementation
is kept to a minimum using a venturi-assist sourced from the turbocharger compressor outlet.

Gaseous Emission Tests
Emission tests were performed with the CCV system. The CSU METEC laboratory was contracted
to conduct all the emission tests. Figure 40 and 41 show an exhaust gas composition comparison
with the CCV system. The methane concentration measured during February (Month 11) and
March 2025 (Month 12) was higher (1155 vs 1623 ppm), with respect to average measured during
the previous 10 months. Further investigation into this behavior led to the discovery of a faulty
NOx sensor on the engine. Once the sensor was replaced the emissions returned to the average
values encountered during the first 10 months of the test.

Figure 42 illustrates the CH4 emission trendline, which has been updated monthly to quantify the
performance of the CCV system and confirm its reliability in reducing CH4 emissions. In this
figure, one can observe the reduction in engine CH4 emissions when operating with the CCV
system. The CH4 emissions without the CCV system correspond to the data points associated with
the Baseline (OCV) series, while the emissions with the CCV system correspond to the CCV
series. The emissions decreased from 2.86 g/bkWhr to 1.99-2.16 g/bkWhr, representing a CH4
emission reduction of 36.7% to 25.0%. The graph also shows an increase in methane concentration
during the months of February and March due to the NOx sensor malfunction, noted above. The
methane reduction even for the months when the engine operated out of the desired conditions was
kept constant around 30% of overall methane reduction with the implementation of the CCV
technology, regardless of the base OCV methane emission from the engine.

The CCG oil carryover and filtration efficiency were evaluated during the field demonstration.
Figure 43 shows the oil carryover as a function of CCV unit coalescing filter operating hours. A
polynomial curve was fit to the data points, and the OEM oil carryover limit (1.2 mg/kWhr) was
also plotted. The intersection of the OEM carryover limit line and the polynomial curve defines
the allowable operating hours for a CCV unit coalescing filter, which is approximately 1400
hours. Figure 44 compares the filtration efficiency results at different times during the test and
indicates the number of operating hours of the coalescing filter. In general, filtration efficiency
decreases as operating hours increase. When the coalescing filter is replaced, the filtration
efficiency of the system returns to a level similar to that observed in April 2024.

Throughout the field demonstration, filtration efficiency was measured for multiple filters with
different operating hours. The filter operating range was extended to slightly more than twice the
originally recommended replacement interval of 2000 hours to evaluate extreme use conditions.
The results suggest that a replacement interval of 1400 hours is advisable to ensure a safety
margin for oil droplet removal and avoid coating the intake components with oil.

Project Activities

The focus of this project was to develop a CCV system for reducing methane emissions from
vented CCG from 4-s lean-burn natural gas engines. Our approach was to condition the CCG and
recirculate it into the engine intake, thereby burning the crankcase methane. Effective
implementation of this approach required that the oil mist be removed from the CCG, the
crankcase pressure be controlled, and water condensation be avoided. Our project team was
successful in our implementation of the CCV system, resulting in a reliable system,
demonstrated with a one-year field test, that yielded a high level of crankcase methane
destruction (>95%).
