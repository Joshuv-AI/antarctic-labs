eHealth Literacy and Quality of Life in Patients: A Narrative Review of Mediating Effecting
Authors: Wenchen Zhao, Xinxia Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGuided by the World Health Organization’s Global Strategy on Digital Health 2020–2025, and with the widespread adoption of digital health concepts globally, public health management is undergoing a profound transformation toward online and intelligent models. In this context, leveraging digital intelligence technologies to empower full life-cycle health management—and thereby tangibly improve people’s quality of life—has become a core objective in the field of international health promotion. Meanwhile, through a review of the literature, we found that eHealth literacy can further influence quality of life through mediating factors. Therefore, this review summarizes specific mediating variables and explores their underlying mechanisms.MethodsBased on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, we conducted a literature search in the CNKI, PubMed and Web of science databases using keywords such as “eHealth literacy”, “quality of life”, and “mediating effect”. We employed a narrative review approach to summarize and synthesize the literature on mediating variables in the relationship between eHealth literacy and quality of life.ResultsA total of 7 studies were included after the screening process. All 7 articles included in the review were cross-sectional studies. Social support, self-efficacy, self-management ability, life satisfaction, and fear of disease progression were found to play significant mediating roles between eHealth literacy and quality of life.ConclusionExisting evidence indicates that the relationship between eHealth literacy and quality of life is influenced by mediating factors. Future eHealth interventions should target these mediating factors to effectively improve patients’ quality of life.

Introduction

With the continuous integration of “Internet Plus” into healthcare, the rapid development of tools such as electronic health records (EHRs), wearable devices, mobile health, and telemedicine has accelerated the advancement of smart healthcare.1 The World Health Organization also acknowledges the potential of eHealth to advance the Sustainable Development Goals, ultimately aiming to improve patients’ quality of life. 2 eHealth literacy is defined as the ability to seek, find, understand, and appraise health information from electronic sources and apply the knowledge gained to addressing or solving a health problem.3

Quality of Life (QoL) serves as an effective metric for assessing the physical health status of the population. Multiple studies have indicated4,5 a positive correlation between eHealth literacy and QoL; enhancing patients’ e-HL can facilitate effective self-management and improve their quality of life.

As a key channel for the timely dissemination of social guidelines and public prevention measures, digital platforms bear the significant responsibility of stabilizing societal expectations. However, driven by traffic-oriented logic, a massive amount of misinformation and disinformation spreads rapidly through social channels. This not only triggers social chaos and related anxiety but also poses a severe challenge to public health governance.6,7 Individuals with low eHealth literacy are unable to effectively process the overload of health information on the internet. The resulting misleading effects pose a significant challenge to the entire population and undermine their quality of life.8

Currently, although a substantial body of literature has explored the impact of eHealth literacy on QoL, few studies have delved into the underlying mediating effecting. Existing research is largely confined to a simple linear “literacy-outcome” association, overlooking potential complex transmission chains. This limitation makes it difficult to fully reveal the deep-seated psychological and behavioral pathways through which eHealth literacy translates into actual improvements in quality of life.

Therefore, this narrative review aims to conduct a systematic search across the CNKI, PubMed, and Web of Science databases and rigorously screen literature based on inclusion and exclusion criteria to provide an in-depth analysis of the included studies. Building on this foundation, the paper focuses on summarizing the mediating variables through which eHealth literacy affects quality of life, with the goal of providing empirical evidence for developing multi-dimensional social intervention strategies based on these mediating factors in future clinical practice.

Methods

A narrative literature review was conducted because it plays an important role in incorporating evidence into clinical practice, bringing together scientific knowledge on a specific topic.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The inclusion criteria of the review were: 1) the research content focuses on the mediating factors in the relationship between eHealth literacy and quality of life; 2) the type of study included observational and experimental studies, mixed method studies or qualitative studies; 3) the participants were adults; 4) The primary outcome measure is quality of life in adults.

The exclusion criteria:of the review were: 1)non-English or non-Chinese literature. 2) studies with incomplete data or where the full text was unavailable. 3) literature of low methodological quality. 4) duplicate publications.

Search Strategy

The search was carried out in several databases to obtain a more precise set of publications. The following keywords were used: (“eHealth literacy”; “QoL”; “mediating effect”) in the CNKI and PubMed databases.

Taking the PubMed database as an example, we developed the following search strategy:

#1: “eHealth literacy”[Title/Abstract] OR “electronic Health Literacy”[Title/Abstract] OR “Digital Health literacy”[Title/Abstract] OR “Media Health literacy”[Title/Abstract] OR “Mobile Health”[Title/Abstract] OR “technology health”[Title/Abstract] OR “internet”[Title/Abstract] OR “web”[Title/Abstract]

#2: “Quality of life”[Title/Abstract] OR “QoL”[Title/Abstract]

#3: “Mediating effect”[Title/Abstract]

#4: #1 AND #2 AND# 3

Study Selection and Data Extraction

After removing duplicates using EndNote software, two researchers independently screened the literature and extracted data in a double-blind manner. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer.

Characteristics of Included Studies

After summarizing the seven empirical studies that met the inclusion criteria, we found that existing evidence primarily points to five mediating factors: life satisfaction, fear of disease progression, self-efficacy, social support, and self-management behaviors. All seven articles represent high-quality empirical studies in the fields of nursing and public health published between 2024 and 2026. A core feature of these works is their rigorous application of mediating effect models to deeply analyze the complex underlying mechanisms between eHealth literacy and quality of life. Transcending the limitations of traditional simple correlations, these studies extensively employed Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) to precisely quantify and reveal mediating pathways involving life satisfaction, fear of disease progression, self-efficacy, social support, and self-management behaviors. This analytical paradigm not only validates the direct impact of eHealth literacy on quality of life but, more critically, elucidates the significant indirect effects exerted through the aforementioned mediating variables. Discussion

Current Status of Research at Home and Abroad

Research Status of e Health Literacy

The Proposal of the Concept of eHealth Literacy

In 2001, Eysenbach16 posits that eHealth is not merely the application of technology, but a new global mindset that leverages information and communication technologies to improve healthcare services In essence, it represents an intersection of medical informatics, public health, aiming to comprehensively enhance the quality and efficiency of healthcare through networked solutions. In 2005, according to the WHO, the essence of eHealth lies in the safe and cost-effective use of Information and Communication Technologies (ICT) to comprehensively support healthcare services, health surveillance, and medical education.; this definition laid the foundation for the concept of eHealth literacy.17

In 2006, Norman et al 3 further elaborated on the connotation of eHealth literacy, defining eHealth literacy as the ability to actively seek, find, understand, and evaluate health information from electronic sources, and then apply that knowledge to address or solve a health problem. It is a concept built upon traditional health literacy, further integrating information literacy and digital literacy. In 2014, Watkins18 pointed out that eHealth literacy is rooted in the concept of health literacy but places greater emphasis on the growing role of ICT in the realm of health information.

In 2015, Bautista19 posits that eHealth literacy is not merely an individual’s mastery of digital technology, but rather the result of the interplay between individual and social factors. It encompasses a comprehensive set of skills for using digital media to seek, understand, evaluate, and communicate health information, with the ultimate goal of maintaining or improving personal health and enhancing quality of life across the entire lifespan. In 2024, Ban20 argued that eHealth literacy is no longer just an individual skill in acquiring information, but rather the product of a dynamic interaction between the individual and the ever-evolving digital environment. It requires individuals to possess comprehensive adaptive capabilities to critically identify misinformation and make informed health decisions.

In summary, eHealth literacy is an evolving term; its concept continuously advances alongside technological progress, aiming to broaden the scope of eHealth literacy.

Theoretical Foundations Related to eHealth Literacy

Currently, the primary theoretical models for eHealth literacy include the Lily Model, the Integrated Model of eHealth Literacy (IMEHU), and the Transactional Model of eHealth Literacy (TMeHL).

The Lily Model was proposed by Norman et al3 within the context of the Web 1.0 environment. The Lily Model categorizes eHealth literacy into two main domains—analytical and professional skills—which specifically encompass six core literacies: traditional, information, media, health, computer, and scientific. It emphasizes that individuals must comprehensively integrate these abilities to effectively seek, understand, evaluate, and utilize health information in a digital environment to solve practical problems.

The Integrated Model of eHealth Literacy (IMEHU) was constructed by Bodie et al21 Building upon Norman et al’s Lily Model.The model posits that eHealth literacy is not only influenced by demographic variables, but is also jointly determined by motivational factors in seeking health information and efficacy factors such as individual self-efficacy.

The Transactional Model of eHealth Literacy (TMeHL) 22 views eHealth literacy as a dynamic, multi-layered comprehensive capability system, specifically encompassing four core dimensions: functional, communicative, critical, and transformative. It emphasizes that individuals must continuously apply these skills through ongoing interaction with the digital environment to effectively seek, evaluate, and utilize health information, ultimately leading to improved health outcomes.

With the advent of Web 2.0 and mobile health (mHealth), the concept—often referred to as digital health literacy—has shifted its focus toward bidirectional interaction and information co-creation. It is no longer confined to passive searching; instead, it demands advanced competencies from individuals to actively share, collaborate, critically evaluate, and protect privacy within dynamic digital environments like social media and wearable devices. This evolution marks a significant leap from merely receiving information to engaging in diversified shared decision-making regarding health.

To establish a model that incorporates the patient perspective, Kayser et al23 developed the eHL Extended User–Task–Context Matrix Model in 2015.The Model of eHealth literacy posits that eHealth literacy is not a singular individual ability, but rather a comprehensive outcome jointly determined by the dynamic interaction among user characteristics, specific tasks, and the surrounding context. The model emphasizes that only by considering these three elements together can we accurately assess an individual’s true level and needs in seeking and utilizing digital health information within a specific scenario.

Additionally,since the Lily model is not well-suited for Web 2.0 environments and fails to adequately reflect the influence of cognitive, social, and environmental factors,18 subsequent scholars have attempted to expand it to explain how contextual factors impact individuals’ eHealth literacy.

Although these models offer a more holistic perspective, they have not yet undergone sufficient evaluation and empirical research. Therefore, further exploration and validation are still required to establish a more robust and practical theoretical framework.

Scales Related to Electronic Health Literacy

Currently, the representative universal measurement tool for electronic health literacy is the first-generation Electronic Health Literacy Scale (eHEALS). With the development of internet interactivity and communication technologies, the connotation of electronic health literacy has continuously expanded. This evolution has given rise to second-generation universal measurement tools, including the Extended eHEALS (eHEALS-E), Electronic Health Literacy Scale (e-HLS), Digital Health Literacy Instrument (DHLI), eHealth Literacy Assessment Toolkit (eHLA), eHealth Literacy Questionnaire (eHLQ), and the Transactional eHealth Literacy Instrument (TeHLI).In existing research, the eHEALS remains the most widely used instrument for investigating the status quo of patients’ electronic health literacy. It has been translated into multiple languages and validated, demonstrating good reliability and validity.

Current Status of eHealth Literacy Levels

Multiple studies have indicated24–26 that the level of eHealth literacy among global patient populations is currently low, with significant variations observed across different populations and disease types. Although most patients possess basic capabilities in acquiring health information, they generally demonstrate deficiencies in core dimensions such as information discrimination, risk assessment, and the scientific application of information for disease self-management.

Populations such as the elderly, those with lower education levels, rural residents, and patients with multimorbidity are more significantly affected by the digital divide, exhibiting markedly lower levels of eHealth literacy. Despite the continuous improvement in the popularity of internet healthcare and health information, the growth rate of patients’ eHealth literacy still lags behind the development of information technology. Since this literacy level is closely related to treatment adherence and quality of life, it has become a critical factor influencing patient health outcomes and the effectiveness of chronic disease management.

Therefore, healthcare professionals need to strengthen individualized eHealth education for patients to enhance their knowledge reserves and improve their eHealth literacy levels. Furthermore, relevant authorities should improve the formulation and implementation of related policies to provide robust support for eHealth literacy research, thereby further enhancing the eHealth literacy of the entire population.27

Current Status of Research on Quality of Life

Concept and Connotation of Quality of Life

In 1993, the World Health Organization (WHO) defined quality of life as individuals’ perception of their position in life in the context of the culture and value systems in which they live, and in relation to their goals, expectations, standards, and concerns.28 Quality of life is a multifaceted construct rooted in an individual’s physical, psychological, and independent functioning, while also being shaped by social interactions and environmental contexts. This comprehensive metric spans multiple domains, including physical well-being, mental health, spiritual aspects, and overall functional capacity.29

Quality of life serves as a key outcome measure in clinical research. It effectively assesses patients’ health status across multiple dimensions—including physiological, psychological, and social aspects—and stands as a critical indicator reflecting treatment efficacy.30 Consequently, the quality of life of patients has garnered increasing attention from scholars.

Quality of Life Assessment Methods

For the assessment of health-related quality of life (HRQoL) in populations, various methods such as scale evaluation and subjective reporting can be employed, depending on the specific objectives and content of the assessment. Currently, the use of standardized scales has become the most common method for evaluating quality of life. This involves assessors using standardized instruments that have been proven to possess good reliability and validity to comprehensively evaluate the quality of life of the subjects.31 Guyatt et al 32 classified quality of life scales into two categories based on the target population: generic scales and specific scales.Generic scales possess strong comprehensiveness and can evaluate the quality of life across populations with different health statuses; therefore, they are frequently used for assessing the quality of life in the general population.Specific scales, on the other hand, offer higher sensitivity. They are capable of capturing subtle clinical changes affecting quality of life during the progression of a disease and are primarily used for evaluating the quality of life in patients with specific conditions.

Research on the Mediating Effect of eHealth Literacy on QoL

Scholars have currently conducted a series of preliminary explorations regarding the intrinsic relationship between eHealth literacy and quality of life. Through a review of relevant literature,5,33 it can be observed that eHealth literacy generally exerts a significant positive impact on patients’ quality of life. A high level of eHealth literacy not only effectively improves patients’ health outcomes and life experiences but also serves as a stable positive predictor for health-related quality of life. These findings provide important empirical evidence for understanding the mechanism of action between the two variables.

Social Support

Research has found a significant positive correlation between the level of eHealth literacy and quality of life in patients with chronic diseases; that is, as eHealth literacy improves, the health-related quality of life of patients also improves. It has also been confirmed that social support plays a multiple mediating role between eHealth literacy and quality of life.

A study by Tsai et al 34 showed that patients with higher eHealth literacy are more likely to obtain peer support and access greater social support through online channels. Meanwhile, these patients exhibit more positive health information proxy search behaviors. Health information proxy search refers to non-medical professionals passively or actively searching for health information on behalf of others;35 therefore, active proxy searching symbolizes positive social support.However, the applicability of health information surrogate searching across different cultural backgrounds and disease severities warrants further investigation.

Social support is defined as the assistance obtained through communication and connection between people. 36 As an external driving force, social support serves as a critical external resource for patients coping with disease stress. Through multidimensional synergistic effects, it effectively enhances patients’ quality of life, with its mechanism of action permeating psychological, physiological, and behavioral levels. When individuals face crises and difficulties, a robust social support network can mitigate stress-induced harm and facilitate physical and mental health development.37

Consequently, social support exerts a chain mediating effect between eHealth literacy and quality of life. In clinical practice, healthcare professionals can construct digital health communities and establish interactive platforms. They should guide patients’ families to utilize digital tools to enhance caregiving capabilities and access online health resources. By providing targeted assistance to patients with insufficient social support and perfecting their social support networks, clinicians can promote eHealth literacy to effectively improve individual quality of life by optimizing social support.

Self-Efficacy

Self-efficacy refers to an individual’s subjective judgment regarding their ability to successfully perform a specific behavior. 38 Cheng’s research indicates that the total effect of eHealth literacy on quality of life is 0.476. Social support and self-efficacy demonstrate a chain mediating effect in the process through which eHealth literacy influences quality of life, with a total mediating effect of 0.215. Notably, the pathway eHealth literacy → self-efficacy → quality of life exhibits the strongest effect.10

A study by Filabadi5 demonstrated a significant positive correlation between eHealth literacy and self-efficacy. Access to accurate health information can enhance individuals’ self-efficacy to utilize this information for lifestyle modifications and the adoption of healthy behaviors. Patients with higher eHealth literacy can efficiently screen, acquire, and rationally apply health-related information in online environments. This process enhances their evaluation and confidence in their ability to perform disease management behaviors, which further reinforces their self-efficacy.

Simultaneously, studies have shown39 a positive correlation between patients’ quality of life and self-efficacy; specifically, higher self-efficacy is associated with better quality of life. High self-efficacy enables patients to view treatment difficulties rationally, strengthens their confidence in recovery, enhances psychological resilience, and stimulates proactive intervention awareness, ultimately improving overall quality of life.

Therefore, self-efficacy plays a critical mediating role between eHealth literacy and patients’ quality of life. It functions by absorbing external positive influences and transforming them into internal individual motivation, thereby comprehensively driving the improvement of quality of life. In the future, healthcare professionals can provide training on health information acquisition, discrimination, and application to strengthen patients’ self-efficacy, ultimately leading to improved quality of life.

Life Satisfaction

Life satisfaction is a comprehensive cognitive evaluation of one’s overall life status based on self-defined criteria, serving as a key indicator for measuring quality of life and subjective well-being.40 Existing research has established a close relationship among eHealth literacy, life satisfaction, and quality of life. Specifically, life satisfaction plays a significant partial mediating role in the influence of eHealth literacy on quality of life.14

Improving patients’ eHealth literacy encourages them to actively explore and utilize accurate health information to enhance their living conditions. This process fulfills patients’ needs and aspirations for a better life, elevates their subjective well-being and life satisfaction, and further safeguards their quality of life.41 However, patients who are able to effectively enhance their life satisfaction through digital health resources often already possess a relatively strong socioeconomic foundation and positive psychological traits. For those at the bottom of the digital divide, lacking access to basic medical resources or deeply affected by structural poverty, this may remain nothing more than an unrealistic ideal.

The revelation of this mediating mechanism clarifies the core role of life satisfaction as a bridge, providing a new perspective for understanding the association between eHealth literacy and quality of life. This suggests that healthcare professionals should not only assist patients in better utilizing digital health resources but also focus on cultivating their life satisfaction. Through methods such as emotional counseling and value empowerment, clinicians can promote the effective transformation of eHealth literacy into improved quality of life.

Health Self-Management Ability

Health self-management ability is defined as the proactive decisions and actions individuals undertake to promote and sustain their well-being, drawing upon an integrated framework of management and behavioral science theories.12

Research indicates that for elderly patients with multimorbidity, eHealth literacy not only directly improves quality of life but also further influences it through the mediating role of self-management, as well as the chain mediating role of self-management and general self-efficacy.42 From the perspective of Bandura’s self-regulation theory,43 patients with higher eHealth literacy are able to access effective, personalized management advice online. This enables them to set specific goals, continuously monitor their progress, and constantly adjust their behaviors and strategies based on feedback, thereby maintaining life balance and achieving their ideal health outcomes.

Fear of Disease Progression

Fear of disease progression is characterized by a patient’s profound apprehension concerning the potential worsening, recurrence, or metastasis of their condition.44 Research by Haack et al 45 indicates that eHealth literacy is negatively correlated with fear of disease progression. Conversely, patients may harbor concerns about disease progression, worrying about worsening conditions, declining quality of life, and even perceiving their lives as being under threat. 15

The positive effect of eHealth literacy on quality of life, achieved by alleviating fear of disease, is particularly significant. However, exposure to negative online information may exacerbate patients’ fear of disease progression, trapping them in a vicious cycle of “the more they search, the more fearful they become, and vice versa.” Therefore, it is imperative to guide the public to recognize the limitations of online information and acknowledge that it cannot replace professional medical diagnosis, while advocating for moderate information consumption. Therefore, in future clinical practice, healthcare professionals should focus on strengthening patients’ eHealth literacy. This will assist patients in scientifically acquiring disease knowledge, discriminating against false information, and mastering their own condition status. Such measures can alleviate the fear of disease progression arising from information deficiency and cognitive bias. Simultaneously, psychological counseling should be provided to mitigate excessive fear, thereby reducing the detrimental impact of negative psychology on quality of life.

However,intervention models that target a single mediating factor are inadequate to address the complex mechanisms determining health. Effective health promotion strategies must transcend the limitations of focusing on a single mediator by comprehensively considering socioeconomic status and the accessibility of external resources, thereby adopting a multi-dimensional and synergistic approach to intervention.

Conclusion

Currently, driven by the dual forces of the WHO’s “people-centered” digital health strategy and the rapid advancement of technologies such as AI, the significance of eHealth literacy in enhancing patients’ quality of life (QoL) has become increasingly prominent. This review provides an in-depth analysis of the mediating factors influencing QoL.

However, few studies have specifically investigated the mediating effecting from the perspective of eHealth literacy; therefore, future research should expand the scope of mediating variables.All the literature included in this review was authored by Chinese scholars. Future research should incorporate empirical studies from a wider range of countries, thereby providing a scientific basis for developing more targeted global health intervention strategies. Furthermore, this review did not further integrate contemporary international literature related to resilience, emotional wellbeing, digital engagement, and technology-supported healthcare interventions. Future research could incorporate these areas to strengthen the interdisciplinary contextualization of the pathways between eHealth literacy and quality of life. Meanwhile, future research should develop comprehensive and multidimensional intervention strategies based on mediating factors to maximize the positive impact of eHealth literacy on quality of life.

In summary, social support, self-efficacy, self-management ability, life satisfaction, and fear of disease progression exhibit significant mediating effects between eHealth literacy and quality of life. eHealth literacy indirectly influences individual quality of life through these mediators.
