Dihydrotanshinone I as a novel signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 inhibitor for glioblastoma treatment
Authors: Kai-wen Cheng, Shao-jun Zhou, Jie Zeng, Yang-rui Zhang, Bing-qian Jin, Ping Wang, Ling-yan He, Xu-chen Qi, Xu-dong Liu, Lu-shan Yu, Yan-xing Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAmong tumors affecting the central nervous system (CNS), glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) is the most aggressive and lethal form. Given the complex mechanisms of the CNS and the blood-brain barrier (BBB), effective therapies for GBM remain limited. To identify potential therapeutic candidates for glioma, we developed a small-molecule library to screen for compounds with potent anti-proliferative effects and BBB permeability. The mechanisms underlying their anti-glioma activity were further elucidated to provide insights into new treatment strategies.MethodsA library of small molecules was screened to identify agents that significantly inhibit glioma cells activity. The effects of the lead compound (Dihydrotanshinone I, DHT) on glioma progression were evaluated through a series of experimental approaches, including cell counting kit-8 assays, cell cycle analysis, apoptosis detection, wound healing, transwell migration, reactive oxygen measurement, subcutaneous xenograft mouse models, and intracranial orthotopic tumor models. To elucidate the underlying mechanisms, network pharmacology and transcriptomic analyses were employed. The mechanism of DHT in glioma pathogenesis was further validated using bioinformatics analyses and clinical glioma tissue samples.ResultsBased on pharmacokinetic evaluation, DHT was found to traverse the BBB, indicating its capacity to reach the cerebral parench. In vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that DHT significantly suppresses glioma growth and progression. Mechanistic analyses using network pharmacology and RNA sequencing revealed that DHT induces glioma cell apoptosis by inhibiting the Janus kinase-signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) signaling pathway, increasing intracellular reactive oxygen species levels and triggering intrinsic apoptotic cascades. Furthermore, bioinformatic analyses of clinical cohorts coupled with validation in patient-derived glioma specimens confirmed that elevated STAT3 expression was correlated with unfavorable prognosis and was significantly increased in patients with high-grade glioma.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that, in addition to its role as a key contributor to glioma malignancy and a prognostic marker, STAT3 is also a target of DHT, highlighting its potential as a promising therapeutic candidate for treating GBM.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08385-7.Keywords: Glioblastoma multiforme, Blood-brain barrier, Dihydrotanshinone I, Apoptosis

Background

Cancer is a leading cause of global mortality, profoundly impacting both human health and the sustainability of healthcare systems, owing to its high treatment costs [1]. The National Cancer Center indicated that in 2022, China recorded 4,824,703 newly diagnosed cancer cases and 2,574,146 cancer-related deaths [1]. Globally, brain and CNS cancers ranked 19th in incidence (accounting for 1.7% of all cancers) but 12th in mortality (2.6%), highlighting their poor prognosis [2]. As the most aggressive and deadly form of primary CNS malignancy, GBM constitutes approximately 15% of gliomas, with an incidence estimated at 3.19 per 100,000 population [3]. Currently, the management of GBM relies on surgical resection combined with postoperative radiotherapy and chemotherapy. Temozolomide (TMZ), a U.S. Food and Drug Administration-approved alkylating agent capable of crossing the BBB, extends median survival by only 2 months. Moreover, its use is associated with severe neurotoxicity, gastrointestinal toxicity, and hematologic side effects [4]. Given the highly invasive and heterogeneous nature of GBM, its aggressive tumor microenvironment, and the protective role of the BBB, patient outcomes remain poor, with a median survival of 15-18 months and a 3-year survival rate of approximately 10% [5]. Although numerous compounds have demonstrated promising efficacy in preclinical studies, most have failed to achieve clinical approval, largely owing to insufficient BBB penetration [6]. Therefore, the development of novel agents that are safe, effective, and associated with minimal side effects that can cross the BBB represents an urgent unmet need in GBM treatment.

With more than 2,000 years of history, traditional chinese medicine (TCM) is among the earliest known medical traditions and has long served as a valuable source for drug discovery. Several anticancer agents that are widely clinically used, such as paclitaxel and etoposide [7], have originated from TCM-derived natural products [8]. Increasing evidence indicates that TCM may play a notable role in cancer prevention, recurrence, metastasis suppression, survival prolongation, and enhanced quality of life [9]. Salvia miltiorrhiza (Danshen), widely utilized in TCM for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular conditions, contains bioactive compounds with diverse pharmacological effects, such as anticancer, antioxidant, antibacterial, and anti-inflammatory activities [9, 10]. Among these, DHT, a major active diterpenoid quinone, exhibits antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, immunomodulatory, and cardioprotective effects [11]. Notably, DHT exhibits potent anticancer activity in breast [12], gastric [13], liver [7], prostate [14], ovarian [15, 16], thyroid [17], and colorectal cancers [18]. However, DHT exhibits inhibitory effects on glioma [19], the mechanisms through which DHT may exert therapeutic effects in GBM remain largely unclear.

In the present study, we initially screened a small-molecule compound library to identify compounds with inhibitory effects on glioma cells proliferation, leading to the identification of DHT, which can suppress glioma proliferation and traverse the BBB. Therefore, we aimed to elucidate the mechanism through which DHT exerts therapeutic effects on GBM.

Materials and methods

Drugs and reagents

TMZ (high-performance liquid chromatography [HPLC] purity > 98%) and Nocodazole (HPLC purity > 98%) were purchased from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). DHT (HPLC purity > 98%) was obtained from BiDe Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Antibodies against phospho-Stat3 (Tyr705, #9145S) and STAT3 (#4904S) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA). Antibodies against phospho-Stat3 (Ser727, #bsm-52210R) were purchased from Bioss (Beijing, China). Antibodies against B-cell lymphoma 2 (bcl-2, #12789-1-AP), Bcl-2-associated X protein (bax, #50599-2-1 g), caspase-9 (#10380-1-AP), β-actin (#66009-1-Ig), horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (#SA00001-2), and HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (#SA00001-1) were obtained from Proteintech (Rosemount, IL, USA).

Cell culture

Human glioma cell lines U251MG, U87MG (iCell Bioscience Inc., Shanghai, China) and T98G (Rosetta Biotechnology Inc., Shanxi, China) were authenticated with short tandem repeat profiling and confirmed to be negative for mycoplasma. The luciferase-expressing glioma cell lines, U87MG-LUC and U251MG-LUC, were procured from Servicebio (Wuhan, China) and iCell Bioscience Inc. (Shanghai, China), respectively. The cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. Specifically, U251MG; U251MG-LUC; U87MG and T98G cells were grown in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; #10-013-CV), whereas U87MG-LUC cells were cultured in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM; #G4551). All media were supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; #F8318) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (#G4003).Specifically, U251MG (PTEN-deficient, TP53 mutant) was used to investigate apoptosis and oncogenic signaling in malignant glioma; U87MG (PTEN wild-type, TP53 wild-type) was employed for studies of tumor proliferation, invasion and angiogenesis; and T98G (TP53 mutant, PTEN wild-type, high MGMT expression) served as a model for TMZ-resistant glioma subtypes. They exhibit complementary molecular and functional phenotypes that recapitulate the heterogeneity of gliomas.

Drug screening

A small-molecule library was constructed using the drugs currently available in our laboratory for screening compounds that can potentially inhibit the proliferation of glioma cells. The molecular library under consideration comprises a total of 100 species including TCM monomers, clinical drugs, and herb-derived compounds (Supplementary Material 2). Each compound was used at a concentration of 20 µM to treat 3 × 10³ cells for a period of 48 h. Subsequently, the CCK-8 reagent was added to evaluate the effect of candidate drugs on the viability of glioma cells. Drugs that at a concentration of 20 µM, exerted a significant inhibitory effect on the viability of both U251MG and U87MG cells were selected as candidate drugs, specifically those that resulted in the lowest cell viability in both cell lines.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8)

To evaluate the inhibitory effects of candidate compounds on glioma cell viability, U251MG and U87MG cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 103 cells /well. Following a 24 h incubation for cell adherence, the original medium was replaced with fresh culture medium containing candidate drugs (20 µM). After an additional 48 h of treatment, cell proliferation was quantified using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, #C6005). Briefly, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. The absorbance was subsequently measured at 450 nm using a Synergy H1 microplate spectrophotometer (BioTek Instruments, VT, USA). Compounds demonstrating significant anti-proliferative activity were selected for further investigation, specifically the compound that exhibited the lowest cell viability in both cell lines. TMZ was utilized as a positive control, representing the current clinical standard of care, to evaluate the potency of DHT in inhibiting glioma cells proliferation. Dose-response relationships were established by treating cells with TMZ (0-1000 µM), DHT (0- 20 µM), or nocodazole (0-20 µM).

Colony formation assay

The colony formation assay was performed to detect the long-term inhibitory effects of DHT on the proliferation and colony-forming ability of glioma cells. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates (1 × 10³ cells/well), incubated for 24 h, and treated with DHT (U251MG/U87MG: 0, 0.5, and 1 µM; T98G: 0, 1, and 2 µM). The medium was refreshed every 72 h for 12-14 d. After discarding the culture medium, colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, stained with 0.1% (w/v) crystal violet for 10 min, washed, air-dried, and photographed under a camera (Z50ii; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Wound healing and transwell assays

The wound healing assay and Transwell invasion assay were performed to investigate the effects of DHT on the migration and invasion abilities of glioma cells. For the wound healing assay, cells were seeded in 6-well plates until reaching approximately 95% confluence, after which a standardized gap was created using a sterile 200 µL pipette tip. Cells were cultured in DMEM containing 2% FBS and DHT (U251MG: 0, 1.25, and 2.5 µM; T98G: 0, 5, and 10 µM). Wound images were recorded at 0, 24, and 48 h under a microscope (Leica DMi1; Leica, Wetzlar, Germany), and closure areas were assessed using Image J software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

For the Transwell invasion assay, a total of 8 × 10⁵ cells per well were seeded into the upper chamber of Transwell inserts (8.0 μm pores; Corning Inc., Corning, NY, USA) in serum-free medium. The lower chamber was filled with complete medium containing 20% FBS and DHT at the indicated concentrations (U251MG: 0, 1.25, and 2.5 µM; U87MG: 0, 2.5, and 5 µM; T98G: 0, 5, and 10 µM). After 48 h of incubation, non-migrating cells were gently removed, and migrated cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and counted in five randomly selected fields under a microscope (Leica DMi1, Wetzlar, Germany).

Apoptosis assay

To investigate whether the drug can induce apoptosis in glioma cells, 2 × 106 cells per well were seeded in 6-well plates and treated with the indicated doses of DHT for 48 h (U251MG: 0, 1.25, and 2.5 µM; U87MG: 0, 2.5, and 5 µM; T98G: 0, 5, and 7.5 µM). Cells were then collected, washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), resuspended in binding buffer (#C1062L), and labeled with Annexin V-FITC and Propidium Iodide (#C1062L) for 15 min at 25 °C in the dark. Flow cytometric analysis (NovoCyte Advanteon; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was performed, with a minimum of 10,000 events acquired per sample. A quantitative analysis was executed with the utilization of Flow Jo v10.8.1 software.

Cell cycle analysis

To evaluate the impact of DHT on cell cycle progression, U251MG, U87MG, and T98G cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 106 cells/well. Following a 24-h period, the cells were subjected to 6-h serum starvation in serum-free medium to achieve synchronization. Subsequently, the medium was replaced with complete culture medium containing specified concentrations of DHT (U251MG: 0, 1.25, and 2.5 µM; U87MG: 0, 2.5, and 5 µM; T98G: 0, 5, and 7.5 µM) for a 48 h incubation. Serum-free medium and Nocodazole (a standard anti-mitotic agent) were used as positive controls for G0/G1 and G2/M phase arrest, respectively. Following the conclusion of the treatment, the cells were collected, rinsed with pre-chilled PBS, and fixed overnight in 70% ethanol (prepared with PBS and absolute ethanol) at 4 °C. Subsequently, cells were stained with PI/RNase solution (#C1502) for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. The cell cycle distribution was evaluated using flow cytometry (NovoCyte Advanteon, Agilent Technologies), and the results were analyzed with ModFit LT v5.0 software.

Immunofluorescence staining of γ-H2AX

To visualize DNA damage in cell nuclei under different drug concentrations, cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well. Following a 24-h adhesion period, the cells were subjected to 48-h drug treatment at the following concentrations: The U251MG cells were exposed to concentrations of 0, 1.25, and 2.5 µM, whereas the U87MG cells were exposed to 0, 2.5, and 5 µM. The T98G cells were exposed to 0, 5, and 7.5 µM. The DNA damage assay was performed via measurement of γ-H2AX immunofluorescence using the DNA Damage Assay Kit (#C2036S) in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The images were captured using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica Stellaris, Wetzlar, Germany).

EdU proliferation assay

To quantify individual proliferating cells, 5 × 104 cells were seeded into each well of 24-well plates. Following 24-h incubation to allow cell attachment, the cells were subjected to 48-h treatment with DHT (U251MG: 0, 1.25, and 2.5 µM; U87MG: 0, 2.5, and 5 µM; T98G: 0, 5, and 7.5 µM). Cell proliferation was assessed by employing the BeyoClic EdU‑594 Proliferation Detection Kit (#C0078S), in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Images were captured using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica Stellaris, Wetzlar, Germany).

Animals for LC-MS/MS analysis

Male Sprague-Dawley rats (6 weeks old; 200 g; Zhejiang Viton Lihua Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Hangzhou, China) were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions and acclimatized for one week before use. Specimens were obtained from nine rats post-gavage. Specifically, six rats were used for retro-orbital blood sampling; the other three were humanely euthanized to facilitate the collection of intact brain tissues. Following a 24 h fasting period, TMZ (MW = 194.15) was administered at a human-equivalent dose of 30.6 mg/kg (0.158 mmol/kg), calculated according to FDA-recommended body surface area normalization (human-to-rat ratio = 1:6.2; standard human: 70 kg, BSA = 1.73 m²; clinical dose = 200 mg/m²). DHT (MW = 278.3) was administered at a dose of 54.55 mg/kg, suspended in 0.5% sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC-Na), via oral gavage (5 mL/kg).

Blood sample collection and preparation for liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis

Blood samples were collected from the orbital venous plexus at predetermined time points (0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 24 h) after DHT administration. Blood samples were transferred into EDTA-coated tubes, gently mixed, and subjected to centrifugation at 3000 × g for 10 min at 25 °C. Plasma was separated, deproteinized with cold acetonitrile (1:3, v/v), and subjected to centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The sample was subsequently transferred into pre-cooled polypropylene tubes for the purpose of LC-MS/MS analysis.

Brain tissue collection and preparation for LC-MS/MS analysis

At 1 h post-dosing, animals were euthanized by CO₂ asphyxiation followed by decapitation. Brain parenchyma was collected simultaneously for tissue distribution studies. Brain tissue was homogenized in ice-cold saline (1:9, w/v) with an ultrasonic homogenizer. Homogenates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was treated with chilled acetonitrile (1:3, v/v). After centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, the resulting supernatant was collected for LC-MS/MS analysis. Following the completion of the designated treatment period, which lasted one hour, the animals were euthanized by cervical dislocation. Immediately thereafter, the brain parenchyma was collected for analysis of tissue distribution. The brain tissues were then homogenized with an ultrasonic homogenizer in ice-cold physiological saline (1:9, w/v). The homogenates were subsequently subjected to centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Thereafter, the resultant upper layer was mixed with ice-cold acetonitrile (1:3, v/v) to precipitate the proteins. Following a second centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, the upper layer was collected for LC-MS/MS analysis.

LC-MS/MS Analysis

To ascertain the plasma concentrations of the pharmaceutical agent at varying temporal points and its distribution in brain tissue at designated time points, LC-MS/MS was employed. The investigation will also encompass the characterization of its pharmacokinetic and tissue distribution properties in vivo. Quantitative analysis was carried out using an Agilent 6470 LC/TQ system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with a binary pump, autosampler (10 °C), and column oven (35 °C), coupled to a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source. Separation was achieved on an Agilent Eclipse Plus C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 3.5 μm). The mobile phase consisted of (A) 0.1% formic acid in water and (B) methanol, delivered at 0.25 mL/min. The optimized gradient was as follows: 10% B (0-0.3 min), linear increase to 90% B (0.31 min), hold at 90% B (1-3.5 min), and re-equilibrate to initial conditions (3.6-4.0 min). The injection volume was 20 µL, with a total run time of 4.0 min.

MRM mode was used to quantify DHT (m/z 279→261) and the internal standard loratadine (m/z 383→337) in positive ion mode. Source parameters were: gas temperature 300 °C, gas flow 6 L/min, nebulizer 45 psi, sheath gas temperature 400 °C, sheath gas flow 11 L/min, and capillary voltage 3500 V. For both analytes, the fragmentor voltage and collision energy were set at 135 V and 20 V, respectively, with a dwell time of 100 ms. Data acquisition and processing were performed using MassHunter software. Representative MRM chromatograms are shown in Supplementary Material 1: Fig. S2.

Pharmacokinetic analysis

Non-compartmental analysis (NCA) was performed using DAS software (v3.1.1; Mathematical Pharmacology Professional Committee, China). The following parameters were calculated: AUC₀–t and AUC₀–∞ (area under the plasma concentration–time curve); AUMC₀–t and AUMC₀–∞ (area under the first moment curve); MRT (mean residence time); λz (terminal elimination rate constant); t₁/₂ (elimination half-life); Tmax (time to maximum plasma concentration); Cmax (maximum plasma concentration), and CLz/F (apparent clearance).

Xenograft mouse model - subcutaneous tumor model

A subcutaneous xenograft tumor model of tumor cells was established to evaluate the inhibitory effect of the drug on tumor growth in vivo. Male BALB/c nude mice (4-week-old; 20 g; Zhejiang Viton Lihua Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Hangzhou, China) were acclimatized for 1 week under SPF conditions. Subcutaneous xenografts were established by injecting 5 × 106 glioma cells resuspended in a 200-µL mixture of culture medium and Matrigel (#354248, 1:1 ratio) into the right axillary region of each mouse. Once the tumors had reached an approximate size of 100 mm³, the mice were divided into four groups: a vehicle control group (0.5% sodium carboxymethyl cellulose), a TMZ group (30 mg/kg) [20], a DHT group (30 mg/kg) [21], and a DHT group (60 mg/kg). The treatments were administered orally at two-day intervals for a period of two weeks. The experimental treatments were administered via oral gavage five times per week. In order to rigorously evaluate the safety and therapeutic efficacy of the treatment, the body weight and tumor volume of each mouse were measured immediately prior to every administration throughout the study period. Upon attaining a tumor burden of 1000 mm³, terminal blood routine and biochemical panels were conducted using retro-orbital samples. Following euthanasia via cervical dislocation, tumor specimens were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, tunel staining, and immunohistochemistry evaluations. In addition, major visceral organs including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys were also collected for histological examination via H&E staining to screen for potential organ-specific lesions.

Xenograft mouse model - intracranial orthotopic model

An intracranial tumorigenesis model of glioma cells was constructed to investigate whether the drug can cross the BBB and further inhibit the occurrence and development of glioma in vivo. Male BALB/c nude mice (4-week-old, 20 g; Viton Lihua Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Hangzhou, China) were acclimatized for one week under SPF conditions. The establishment of the intracranial model was initiated with the inoculation of U251MG-LUC or U87MG-LUC glioma cells (5 × 104) into the subfrontal cortex. These cells were resuspended in 5 µL of Matrigel matrix (1:1 ratio) and stereotactically inoculated. The stereotactic coordinates were defined as ML (medial-lateral): The AP (anterior-posterior) direction was adjusted by + 1.5 mm, the DV (dorsoventral) direction was adjusted by − 2.4 mm, and the AP direction was adjusted by − 1.0 mm. These adjustments are consistent with previously established protocols [22]. Tumor-bearing mice were then divided into four experimental cohorts (vehicle control, 0.5% sodium carboxymethyl cellulose, 30 mg/kg of TMZ, and two doses of DHT, 30 or 60 mg/kg). The dosing protocol involved the administration of oral gavage five times per week. Immediately preceding each administration, the animal’s body weight was documented, and its clinical status was meticulously monitored on a daily basis. Two weeks post-implantation, the presence of a tumor was confirmed through the use of an Aniview 100X multi-modal in vivo imaging system (Guangzhou Biolight Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China) following the intraperitoneal administration of D-luciferin potassium salt (15 mg/mL, 150 µL per mouse). Tumor progression was longitudinally assessed every 7 days using the BLI system. Following the administration of euthanasia, the brain tissue as well as the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney tissues, were harvested. Tissue samples were prepared and subsequently subjected to H&E staining.

H&E staining

H&E staining method was employed to observe the pathological and morphological changes in tissues, thereby clarifying the morphological characteristics and pathological status of tissue cells. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded mouse tissues were sectioned, deparaffinized in xyelne, rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, stained with H&E (#C0105S ), dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared, and then mounted with a coverslip. Images were acquired using a Leica SCN400 slide scanner (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

TUNEL staining

TUNEL staining was used to evaluate the apoptotic effect of DHT treatment on glioma tissues. Tissues sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, followed by PBS washes. To enhance permeability, sections were treated with proteinase K (20 µg/mL) at 37° C for 15-20 min. TUNEL labeling was performed using an In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit (Roche) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with incubation at 37° C for 60 min in a humidified chamber protected from light. After washing with PBS, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and sections were mounted with an anti-fade medium. Slides were scanned using the Pannoramic DESK (3D HISTECH, Budapest, Hungary).

Blood biochemistry and hematology

Next, we evaluated the effects of the drug on blood biochemistry and hematology in mice, and to assess its safety and toxicity in vivo. Blood collection from mouse was performed from the orbital sinus, and the serum was separated via centrifugation (25 °C; 3000 rpm/min; 15 min). Liver-related parameters (alanine aminotransferase [23], aspartate aminotransferase [AST], alkaline phosphatase [24], gamma-glutamyl transferase [GGT], total bilirubin [TBIL], total bile acids [TBA], and urea), renal markers (creatinine [CREA] and uric acid [UA]), and cardiac enzymes (creatine kinase-myocardial band [CK-MB], creatine kinase [CK], and lactate dehydrogenase [LDH]) were quantified. Hematological profiles were determined using blood treated with ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid.

Target prediction of DHT

The potential macromolecular targets of DHT were predicted using the SwissTargetPrediction platform (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/) [25, 26], based on the principle of structural similarity. The chemical structure of DHT, represented by the SMILES string (CC1COC2 = C1C(= O)C(= O)C3 = C2C=CC4 = C(C = CC=C43)C), was submitted to the server with the species limited to “Homo sapiens”. After identification of candidate targets, these were retrieved for further analysis. The Systems Pharmacology Database and Analysis Platform (TCMSP, https://tcmspw.com/tcmsp.php) [27] is a unique systems pharmacology platform for Chinese herbal medicines that includes drug-target networks. “Dihydrotanshinone I” was input in the Herb name field and perform the search to obtain target information associated with DHT was performed. The Search Tool for Interactions of Chemicals (STITCH, http://stitch.embl.de/) is an online platform that utilizes artificial intelligence to predict the interactions of small molecules and proteins [28]. The chemical structure of DHT, represented by the SMILES string “(CC1COC2 = C1C(= O)C(= O)C3 = C2C=CC4 = C(C = CC=C43)C)”, was submitted to the server with the species limited to “Homo sapiens”. PharmMapper (https://lilab-ecust.cn/pharmmapper/), is a pharmacophore-based database that facilitate the prediction of potential drug targets [29, 30]. The relevant target information can be obtained by inputting the mol2 structural formula of DHT retrieved from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) into this database.

Target prediction of GBM

Genes associated with GBM were obtained from GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/) and MalaCards (https://www.malacards.org/). GeneCards automatically integrates gene-centric data from approximately 150 web sources [31]. MalaCards is an integrated, searchable database of human maladies and their annotations [32]. Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM, https://omim.org), is a comprehensive, authoritative compendium of human genes and genetic phenotypes [33, 34]. As indicated by the Therapeutic Target Database (TTD, https://ttd.idrblab.cn/), information regarding known and explored targets can be obtained [35]. After downloading GSE108476 from the GEO database, differential analysis was conducted using limma, and genes meeting |log₂FC| > 1, adj. p < 0.05 were defined as differentially expressed genes [36], and SymMap databases (http://www.symmap.org/) include many targets from the clinicals symptoms and diseases [37]. The shared objectives of DHT and GBM were subsequently identified through the utilization of the Bioinformatics online platform (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn).

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network, functional enrichment, and molecular docking

Common targets were analyzed to construct a PPI network using STRING v12.0 [38] and visualized using Cytoscape v3.10.0. Hub genes within the network were identified using CytoHubba. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were then conducted using Metascape (https://metascape.org/gp/index.html#/main/step1) [39]. The Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction was applied to adjust p-values, with a significance cutoff of p < 0.05. For molecular docking analysis, protein structures were collected from the Research Collaboratory for Structural Bioinformatics-Protein Data Bank, and DHT (CID: 114971) was retrieved from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). AutoDock Vina v1.5.7 was employed to perform molecular docking, and the docking results were visualized using PyMOL v2.6.0.

RNA sequencing analysis

Transcriptome analysis was performed to screen and identify the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in glioma cells following DHT intervention. This analysis was conducted to further explore the potential molecular mechanisms underlying the anti-glioma effect of DHT. RNA was extracted from DHT-treated (n = 3 per group) or control U251MG cells using RNAiso Plus (#9108). Sequencing libraries were prepared and processed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Novogene, Beijing, China). Reads were aligned to the GRCh38/hg38 genome using HISAT2 v 2.0.5. Differential expression analysis was conducted with DESeq2 (adjusted p < 0.05, |log₂ fold change| > 1), and subsequent GO and KEGG pathway enrichment of DEGs was carried out using cluster profiler.

Gene set enrichment analysis

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted to explore pathways and functional gene sets that were associated with the experimental condition. The analysis utilized GSEA v 4.3.2, and gene sets were obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/index.jsp). The enrichment was considered to be significant for gene sets with an FDR < 0.25 and a nominal p-value < 0.05.

ROS detection

Next, we aimed to detect the effect of the drug on intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in glioma cells, and to explore the molecular mechanism of apoptosis induction. After 48 h of treatment with DHT, the cells were incubated with 10 µM 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (#S0033S) for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. Intracellular ROS levels were then measured using flow cytometry (NovoCyte Advanteon, Agilent Technologies) and analyzed using FlowJo v10.8.1 software to determine relative ROS fluorescence intensity.

Western blotting

Western blotting was performed to detect the changes in the expression levels of target proteins. Following 48 h incubation with the indicated drugs, cells were harvested and lysed using 120 µL of lysis buffer (#P0038). For tumor tissue samples, protein extraction was performed by adding lysis buffer at a 1:1 (w/v) ratio, followed by homogenization and centrifugation. In order to ensure protein integrity, both protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (#P1045) were incorporated into the lysis buffer. Protein concentrations were subsequently quantified using the BCA assay, and aliquots of 20 µg protein were denatured at 100 °C for 10 min. Samples were separated on sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes, and blocked in 5% milk. The membranes were then subjected to an overnight incubation at 4 °C with specific primary antibodies. The following primary antibodies were utilized in this study: phospho-Stat3 (#9145S, 1:1000), STAT3 (#4904S, 1:1000), Bcl-2 (#12789-1-AP, 1:5000), Bax (#50599-2-1 g, 1:5000), caspase-9 (#10380-1-AP, 1:5000), β-actin (#66009-1-Ig, 1:10000). Following three 10 min washes in TBST to remove primary antibodies, the membranes were treated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (goat anti-rabbit IgG, #SA00001-2, 1:5000; or goat anti-mouse IgG, #SA00001-1, 1:20000) for 1 h at ambient temperature. The procedure was concluded with three further 10 min TBST rinses prior to chemiluminescent detection. Protein bands then subjected to chemiluminescent detection on a ChemiDoc MP system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA).

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was employed to assess the expression level of phosphorylated STAT3 (p-STAT3) in glioma samples. Tissue sections were subjected to deparaffinization and rehydration, followed by heat-induced epitope retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 20 min. Non-specific binding was blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin, and sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the indicated primary antibodies phospho-Stat3 (Ser727, #bsm-52210R, 1:500). After washing, HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (#PV-9001, Zhongshan Golden Bridge Biotechnology, Beijing, China) were applied, and signals were developed with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB, #ZLI-9019, Zhongshan Golden Bridge Biotechnology, Beijing, China). Finally, slides were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and mounted with a coverslip. Images were acquired using a Leica SCN400 slide scanner (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

Public database analysis

Gene expression and survival analyses were conducted using Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis 2 (GEPIA, http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn) and Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA, http://www.cgga.org.cn) with default parameters.

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was conducted in biological triplicate (n ≥ 3), and the results are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical significance was determined using a two-tailed Student’s t-test for two-group comparisons or one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple groups. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed to evaluate the association between protein expression and patient prognosis. A threshold of p < 0.05 was applied to indicate significance.

Drug screening identified DHT as an inhibitor of glioma cells proliferation

A small-molecule library was constructed from drugs present in our laboratory to screen for agents that suppress glioma cells proliferation. This library comprised TCM monomers, clinically used drugs, and compounds derived from herbs (Supplementary Material 2). We selected the human glioma cell lines U251MG and U87MG as in vitro screening models and assessed their viability using the CCK-8 assay (Fig. 1A). Among the 100 compounds evaluated at 20 µM, DHT demonstrated the most significant inhibitory effect on both U251MG and U87MG cells, exhibiting the lowest cell viability values of 0.8262 ± 0.08708 and 1.64 ± 0.6952, respectively (Fig. 1B-F, Supplementary Material 2–4).

DHT suppressed the tumorigenic activity of glioma cells

As mentioned above, DHT (C₁₈H₁₄O₅; 278.3 g/mol) is a major bioactive constituent of S. miltiorrhiza. Structurally, it is a phenanthrenequinone derivative composed of three aromatic rings and a dihydrofuran ring (Fig. 2A). To assess its antitumor effects against glioma cells, we conducted functional assays using U251MG, U87MG, and T98G cell lines. We used TMZ as a positive control. The CCK-8 assay results indicated that the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) of TMZ was 22.74 µM for U251MG, 16.71 µM for U87MG, and 49.5 µM for T98G cells, whereas that of DHT was 1.436, 3.084, and 5.013 µM, respectively (Fig. 2B-C). These results indicated that DHT exhibits stronger and dose-dependent cytotoxicity than TMZ. Consistent with this, colony formation assays demonstrated that DHT significantly suppressed the clonogenic survival of glioma cells in a concentration-dependent manner, as evidenced by reduced crystal violet staining (Fig. 2D).

Considering the highly invasive phenotype of glioma cells, we assessed the effects of DHT on cell migration and invasion. Transwell assays revealed that even at relatively low concentrations, DHT significantly inhibited invasion in a dose-dependent manner (Fig.2E; Supplementary Material 1: Fig. S1A). In wound healing assays performed with U251MG and T98G cells, U87MG cells were excluded owing to their excessive intercellular spacing. DHT treatment reduced wound closure rates in a concentration- and time-dependent manner (Fig. 2F-G).

We assessed the effect of DHT on apoptosis using Annexin V-FITC/PI staining and found that it resulted in a dose-dependent increase in the proportion of early and late apoptotic cells, supporting its role in inducing programmed cell death (Fig. 3A-B). Notably, DHT facilitated both early and late apoptosis in U251MG, U87MG and T98G cells in a concentration-dependent manner.

The cell cycle is a critical biological process that governs cell division and proliferation, encompassing the complete sequence from the completion of one division to the end of the next. Serum-free DMEM and nocodazole were used as a negative and positive controls, respectively, for cell cycle synchronization (Supplementary Material 1: Fig. S1B-C); the results of the flow cytometric analysis demonstrated that DHT treatment led to a substantial augmentation in the proportion of U251MG, U87MG, and T98G glioma cells in the S and G2/M phases, with a more pronounced accumulation in the S phase. The results suggested that DHT disrupts normal cell cycle progression and induced cell cycle arrest at the S and G2/M phases (Fig. 3 C-D, Supplementary Material 1: Fig. S1C). Immunofluorescence staining for γ-H2AX showed that DHT treatment markedly increased the number of γ-H2AX foci in a concentration-dependent manner, indicating that DHT induces DNA double-strand breaks and activates the DNA damage response pathway in glioma cells (Fig. 3E, Supplementary Material 1: Fig. S1D). Furthermore, EdU incorporation assays demonstrated that DHT treatment significantly reduced the number of EdU-positive proliferating cells (Fig. 3F, Supplementary Material 1: Fig. S1E), suggesting that activating of the DNA damage response suppresses DNA replication. Collectively, these findings indicated that DHT induces DNA double-strand breaks, activated the S-phase checkpoint, inhibits DNA replication, and ultimately leaded to cell cycle arrest in glioma cells.

These results indicated that DHT exerts a suppressive effect on the viability, proliferation, invasion, and migration of glioma cells, while concomitantly inducing cell cycle arrest and promoting cell apoptosis.

Concurrently, we compiled 8044 glioma-related genes from multiple databases, including GeneCards, OMIM, TTD, SymMap, MalaCards, HPIO, and GEO (Fig. 6B; Supplementary Material 1: Fig. S4B). Among these, we focused on seven primary genes: epidermal growth factor receptor, STAT3, SRC Proto-Oncogene, caspase 3, prostaglandin-endoperoxide synthase 2, heat shock protein 90 alpha family class A member 1, and Erb-B2 receptor tyrosine kinase 2.

Transcriptomic alterations in glioma cells following treatment with DHT

RNA-seq enables comprehensive profiling of transcriptomic alterations under specific conditions [40, 41]. Therefore, we conducted RNA-seq on U251MG cells treated with either dimethyl sulfoxide or DHT at the IC50 concentration to identify DEGs and enriched signaling pathways. RNA-seq analysis revealed substantial alterations in gene expression in cells treated with DHT compared to those in controls. Venn diagram analysis demonstrated that 12,413 genes were commonly expressed in both groups, with 509 and 829 uniquely expressed in the DHT and control groups, respectively (Fig. 7A). Using the cutoff criteria of |Log2fold change| > 1, p < 0.05), a total of 542 significantly upregulated and 221 downregulated genes were identified in DHT-treated U251MG cells (Fig. 7B). Hierarchical clustering confirmed distinct expression patterns between the two groups, indicating systemic transcriptomic modulation by DHT (Fig. 7C). GO enrichment analyses revealed that the DEGs were primarily enriched in ribosomal processes, receptor-ligand activity, and multiple cellular components (Fig. 7D-F). KEGG pathways analyses demonstrated significant enrichment in immune- and inflammation-related signaling pathways, including JAK-STAT, transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), tumor necrosis factor (TNF), and cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions (Fig. 7G). Consistently, gene set enrichment analysis confirmed the enrichment of these pathways, specifically the JAK-STAT signaling, apoptosis, cancer-related, and additional immune/inflammatory pathways (Fig. 7H-I).

DHT induced ROS generation and downregulated STAT3 phosphorylation

Excessive ROS accumulation disrupts intracellular redox homeostasis and contributes to glioma tumorigenesis and malignant progression [42]. Increased ROS levels also induce oxidative stress, causing glioma cells apoptosis. Our results indicated that treatment with DHT resulted in a pronounced, dose-dependent increase in intracellular ROS production in glioma cells (Fig. 8A-B). In parallel, network pharmacology and RNA-seq analyses revealed significant enrichment of the JAK-STAT3 signaling pathway, suggesting its central role in mediating the antitumor activity of DHT. Notably, STAT3 emerged as the primary hub gene, displaying the highest connectivity and the lowest predicted binding free energy. Therefore, we focused on STAT3 in subsequent experiments.

Western blotting revealed that treatment with DHT significantly suppressed STAT3 phosphorylation in glioma cells (Fig. 8C-E). Treatment with DHT also activated pro-apoptotic signaling, as evidenced by the upregulation of bax and cleaved caspase- 9 and the downregulation of bcl-2 (Fig. 8C-E). Consistent with the in vitro findings, immunoblotting analysis demonstrated that tumor tissues from DHT-treated mice exhibited significantly reduced levels of phosphorylated STAT3 (p-STAT3) compared with those from negative control mice (Fig. 8F-H). Immunohistochemical analysis further confirmed a significant reduction in p-STAT3-positive cells in tumor tissues from the DHT-treated group compared to those from the negative control group (Fig. 8I-J). These results demonstrated that DHT induces ROS production and suppresses STAT3 phosphorylation, thereby activating downstream apoptotic pathways and promoting glioma cells apoptosis.

Additionally, in patients with low-grade gliomas, increased STAT3 expression was associated with unfavorable overall and disease-free survival (Fig. 9B, C). In GBM (World Health Organization grade IV), STAT3 expression remained increased (Fig. 9D). In patients with GBM, no association was observed between STAT3 and overall survival, whereas its high expression was significantly correlated with decreased disease-free survival (Fig. 9E-F).

(G) STAT3 expression increased with tumor grade: WHO II (n=94), WHO III (n=50), WHO IV (n=85); **p<0.001 vs WHO II, ***p<0.0001; ##p<0.01 vs WHO III. (H) Overall survival was significantly shorter in patients with high STAT3 expression compared with those with low expression (p<0.0001; high group n=111, low group n=111). (I) In WHO grade II glioma, STAT3 expression was not associated with survival (high group n=45, low group n=45). (J) In WHO grade III glioma, high STAT3 expression was associated with shorter survival (high group n=24, low group n=23; p<0.05). (K) In WHO grade IV glioma, STAT3 expression was not associated with survival (high group n=43, low group n=42). (L) IHC results of clinical specimens revealed that p-STAT3 positive cell counts were significantly higher in high-grade versus low-grade glioma tissues

To confirm these findings, we analyzed the CGGA database and found that STAT3 expression increased with glioma grade, with maximal levels observed in grade IV tumors (Fig. 9G). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis indicated that patients with decreased STAT3 expression had significantly better survival than those with elevated expression. Although STAT3 expression did not significantly affect survival in patients with grade II gliomas (Fig. 9I), it was negatively associated with prognosis in those with grade III gliomas (Fig. 9J). In grade IV gliomas, STAT3 expression demonstrated no significant association with overall survival (Fig. 9K), potentially owing to the universally poor survival rate of patients with high-grade gliomas.

To further confirm the bioinformatic results, we collected tumor specimens from six patients with glioma (2023-2024; Approval No. 2025-90-Y-01, Supplementary Material 5) and performed immunohistochemistry for p-STAT3. Consistent with the database analyses, the proportion of p-STAT3-positive cells was notably higher in high-grade than in low-grade gliomas (Fig. 9L). These findings indicated that STAT3 expression increases with glioma grade and is associated with unfavorable clinical outcomes, suggesting its dual value as a prognostic marker and therapeutic candidate in glioma.

Discussion

Gliomas are highly aggressive CNS malignancies associated with poor prognosis [43]. Recently, TCM and natural bioactive compounds have attracted increasing interest as potential therapeutic strategies for glioma because of their multi-target mechanisms and relatively favorable safety profiles [44]. DHT, a major lipophilic constituent of Salvia miltiorrhiza , has been extensively reported to exhibit antitumor properties across multiple cancer types. In the present study, we demonstrated that DHT significantly inhibited malignant phenotypes of the glioma cells, including cell viability, proliferation, migration, and invasion. DHT induced S-phase cell cycle arrest and promoted both early and late apoptosis. Mechanistically, DHT exerted its anti-glioma activity through dual pathways: inhibition of STAT3 phosphorylation and induction of intracellular ROS accumulation. These upstream events were accompanied by increased expression of proapoptotic proteins (bax and caspase -9), decreased expression of the antiapoptotic protein bcl-2, and activation of the intrinsic mitochondrial apoptotic pathway (Fig. 10).

Proposed schematic diagram of the signaling pathway underlying the effects of DHT in glioma. DHT (red dots) suppresses glioma malignancy by inhibiting cell proliferation and division, inducing S-phase cell cycle arrest, promoting apoptosis, and reducing migration and invasion. Mechanistically, DHT downregulates STAT3 phosphorylation, elevates intracellular ROS, decreases bcl2 expression, increases bax expression, and subsequently activates caspase 9, ultimately leading to apoptotic cell death

TCM has a long-standing clinical history that has been developing for over millennia. It is characterized by a theoretical framework that has been thoroughly refined through extensive practical applications and is crucial in contemporary research on cancer prevention and treatment. Recently, natural products derived from the TCM have emerged as a crucial reservoir for anticancer drugs [8]. Indeed, several conventional chemotherapeutic agents, such as paclitaxel and etoposide, are derived from herbal extracts or structurally optimized derivatives, underscoring the translational potential of TCM [7]. Salvia miltiorrhiza has been widely used for treating cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases and possesses antioxidant, antibacterial, and anti-inflammatory properties [45]. Increasing evidence also supports its anticancer activity, including potential use as an adjuvant therapeutic agent for tumors [46]. Tanshinones, the primary lipophilic bioactive constituents of Salvia miltiorrhiza, belong to the phenanthraquinone family. These include tanshinone ⅡA, cryptotanshinone, DHT, and tanshinone ⅡB. In our preliminary screening process, DHT was identified as a compound of interest. Subsequent parallel comparative experiments with the other three monomers confirmed that DHT exerted the strongest inhibitory effect on glioma cells, as reflected by the lowest IC50 value. A substantial body of evidence supports the anti-glioma activity of compounds derived from Salvia miltiorrhiza. For example, cryptotanshinone has been shown to suppress GBM cell proliferation by targeting the STAT3 signaling pathway [47]. Furthermore, it induces autophagy and regulates mitochondrial mass through activation of the KEAP1/NRF2/HMOX1 axis in glioma cells [45]. Tanshinone ⅡA, another major constituent, exhibits significant cytotoxic activity against glioma cells [48], inhibiting proliferation and inducing apoptosis [49, 50]. Furthermore, salvianolic acid A and salvianolic acid B which are isolated from Salvia miltiorrhiza, also demonstrate strong anti-glioma potential. Salvianolic acid A enhances drug accumulation within brain tumor tissues, reduces cell viability [51], and suppresses proliferation, migration, and invasion while promoting apoptosis in glioma cells [52]. Conversely, salvianolic acid B has been reported to sensitize glioma cells to radiotherapy, thereby enhancing tumor cell death [53].

In addition, DHT has attracted increasing attention for its multifaceted anti-glioma activity. DHT inhibited GBM proliferation, enhanced the therapeutic efficacy of temozolomide, and attenuated its associated toxic effects [54, 55]. Furthermore, DHT has been shown to induce ferroptosis, a distinct form of regulated cell death, in glioma cells, thereby suppressing tumor growth [19]. In the present study, we employed an integrated approach combining network pharmacology and transcriptomic analysis to systematically identify potential molecular targets and molecular mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of DHT against glioma. This strategy enabled a more comprehensive characterization of its underlying anticancer properties.

The BBB, composed of endothelial cells, basement membrane, perivascular space, and glial cells, serves as a protective barrier that shields the brain from blood-borne pathogens and xenobiotics [56, 57]. However, this protective function also represents a major obstacle to the development of effective glioma therapeutics. Although many compounds demonstrate potent anti-glioma activity in vitro, numerous compounds exhibit limited BBB permeability, thereby failing to achieve therapeutic concentrations within the brain parenchyma [58]. This challenge substantially hinders clinical translation and contributes to the limited treatment options available for glioma patients [59]. To evaluate the in vivo translational potential of DHT, pharmacokinetic (PK) studies were conducted in a rat model. DHT contains a hydroquinone core structure [60], which confers chemical instability and susceptibility to redox reactions [61, 62]. During PK analysis, despite immediate centrifugation and protein precipitation following orbital blood collection, complete prevention of DHT oxidation in plasma proved difficult, leading to substantial inter-individual variability in plasma concentration-time profiles. These results demonstrated the ability of DHT to cross the BBB, achieving detectable concentrations in brain tissue ranging from 40 to 60 ng/mL.

However, the relatively low brain accumulation of DHT may be attributable to its susceptibility to oxidative degradation. Notably, DHT was nearly undetectable in brain tissue when samples were not immediately homogenized and processed with acetonitrile following dissection, suggesting that brain-specific factors may accelerate its oxidation or metabolic clearance. These findings underscore the need of exploring advanced formulation strategies to enhance DHT stability, such as nanodelivery systems, hydrogels, or liposomal encapsulation, thereby minimizing oxidative inactivation and improve brain delivery efficiency.

In tumor-bearing mice, in vivo safety assessments demonstrated no significant pathological damage to major organs including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney following 2–3 weeks of intragastric DHT administration at therapeutically relevant doses, as confirmed by serum biochemical analyses and histopathological examinations. However, at a higher dose of 60 mg/kg, a mild reduction in body weight was observed which is consistent with the findings reported in the literature [63]. A limitation of the present study is the absence of histopathological evaluation of gastrointestinal tissues, which warrants further investigation in future work. Routine hematological analyses revealed mild elevations in certain leukocyte and erythrocyte parameters, whereas platelet indices remained largely unchanged. These findings were consistent with those of previous reports [64]. The observed hematological alterations may be attributable to chronic stress associated with prolonged drug administration, as suggested by prior studies [65]. During dissection of orthotopic intracranial glioma models, tumor extension along the needle tract was occasionally observed, with adhesion to the meninges. This resulted in partial detachment of tumor tissue together with meningeal structures, leading to incomplete recovery of intact tumor specimens. To improve tissue collection in future experiments, refinement of the modeling protocol, such as increasing injection depth or sealing the needle tract with bone wax, may enhance specimen integrity and reproducibility.

STAT3 is a key cytoplasmic transcription factor that becomes activated cytokines and growth factors. Upon stimulation STAT3 undergoes a series of chemical modifications, including phosphorylation, activation, and subsequent nuclear translocation, where it modulates the transcription of downstream target genes. STAT plays a central role in multiple oncogenic processes, including cell proliferation, survival, angiogenesis, maintenance of stemness, invasion, metastasis, and chemoresistance [66, 67]. Aberrant hyperactivation of STAT3 has been reported in various malignant tumors [68] and is closely associated with tumor progression and an poor clinical outcomes [69]. Consequently, STAT3 is an attractive therapeutic target in cancer treatment [70]. DHT exerts antitumor effects in several cancer types, including hepatocellular carcinoma, melanoma, and esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, primarily through the suppression of the STAT3 signaling cascade [68, 71, 72]. In this study, we demonstrated that DHT also acts as a potential inhibitor of STAT3 in glioma cells. The phosphorylation status of STAT3 is closely associated with glioma onset, progression, and prognosis [73]. A notable finding is the significant increase in the phosphorylation levels of STAT3 during the transition from low-grade to high-grade glioma [74], consistent with our findings herein. Consequently, the targeted suppression of the STAT3 signaling pathway represents a promising therapeutic strategy in glioma management. For example, metformin has been shown to exert anti-glioma effects by modulating the phosphorylation of STAT3 in glioma stem cells [66]. Similarly, Calanquinone A has been demonstrated to reduce STAT3 expression, thereby suppressing proliferation, metastasis, and survival of GBM cells [67]. Tanshinone ⅡA has been reported to block STAT3 activity and trigger apoptosis in glioma cells [50]. By contrast, Cryptotanshinone selectively inhibits STAT3 phosphorylation, downregulates the expression of cell cycle-related proteins, and inhibits glioma proliferation [75]. Collectively, our findings provide further support for the role of STAT3 as a promising therapeutic target in glioma and offer novel insights into the development of natural products targeting STAT3 for glioma treatment.

Recent advances in clinical oncology highlight the growing translational interest in STAT3 inhibition. The STAT3 inhibitor WP1066 has demonstrated proapoptotic effects in glioblastoma cells and significant tumor suppression in xenograft models [76]. Two ongoing phase 1 clinical trials (NCT01904123 and NCT04334863) are currently evaluating the efficacy of this agent in the treatment of GBM [76]. Additionally, an active clinical trial (NCT05689619) is investigating the potential of the STAT3 inhibitor Silibinin in preventing recurrence of brain metastases. Another STAT3-targeting agent, TTI-103, is currently under clinical investigation for its effectiveness against various advanced malignancies, including metastatic breast cancer and hepatocellular carcinoma (NCT03195699, NCT05384119, and NCT05440708) [76]. Collectively, these ongoing clinical trials underscore the strong clinical translation potential of STAT3-targeted therapies in cancer treatment.

This study has limitations that should be acknowledged. First, although short-term safety was evaluated, the safe dosage range of DHT and its potential chronic toxicity require systematic investigation in animal models to provide stronger support for preclinical development and future clinical translation. Second, while our findings indicated that DHT suppresses STAT3 phosphorylation, direct molecular interaction studies, such as co-immunoprecipitation, pull-down assays, or biophysical binding analyses, are necessary to confirm whether DHT directly interacts with STAT3 and to further clarify the underlying mechanism. Finally, incomplete recovery of intact tumor tissues from orthotopic intracranial models limited our ability to comprehensively evaluate changes within the brain tumor microenvironment. Future studies employing optimized surgical protocols and advanced in vivo techniques will be essential to address this limitation.

Conclusion

We identified DHT as a potent candidate against glioma. DHT crossed the BBB, exhibited minimal systemic toxicity, and significantly inhibited glioma cells progression by suppressing proliferation, migration, and invasion while facilitating apoptosis and cell cycle arrest. Mechanistically, these effects were mediated via the inhibition of the JAK-STAT3 pathway and ROS-induced activation of the mitochondrial apoptotic cascade. Notably, STAT3 emerged as a target of DHT, suggesting DHT may act as a potential STAT3 inhibitor in gliomas. Collectively, our study suggested that DHT may serve as a valuable therapeutic candidate for glioma management.
