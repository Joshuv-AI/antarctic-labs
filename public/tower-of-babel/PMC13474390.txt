In vitro selection of VNAR antibodies using bioinformatics-based library design and cDNA display
Authors: Hiroki Anzai, Takuya Terai, Susumu Mitsuyama, Ryo Matsunaga, Kenwa Tamagawa, Issei Yamaguchi, DPN De Silva, Shigehiro Kuraku, Kouhei Tsumoto, Robert E Campbell, Shuichi Asakawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•A fully synthetic VNAR library was constructed using brownbanded bamboo shark genes and AlphaFold3 prediction for high-throughput cDNA display screening.•In vitro selection using EGFP and mNeonGreen selected VNAR clones VNAR-E1 and VNAR-N1, respectively.•BLI analysis confirmed that VNAR binders had KD values of 40 nM for mNeonGreen and 6.6 μM for EGFP.•This is the first report to demonstrate cDNA display as an effective method for VNAR selection.Keywords: VNAR, cDNA display, In vitro selection, Synthetic library, Fluorescent protein, Chiloscyllium punctatum

Introduction

Single-domain antibodies (sdAbs) are emerging as promising therapeutic modalities for drug development, complementing conventional antibodies and peptides [[1], [2], [3], [55]]. Conventional antibodies are composed of variable regions derived from both heavy and light chains, which can be engineered into single-chain variable fragments (scFvs) by connecting them using a linker peptide. In contrast, sdAbs consist of only the variable region of heavy-chain-only antibodies; therefore, they are single polypeptide chains from the beginning. Heavy-chain-only antibodies were originally discovered in camelids [4]. Subsequently, immunoglobulin new antigen receptors (IgNARs) were identified in cartilaginous fish such as sharks [5]. Variable domains of new antigen receptors (VNARs), the variable domain of IgNARs, possess a unique structure lacking the CDR2 loop and are considered the smallest naturally occurring binding domains in terms of molecular size, potentially enabling improved tissue penetration and therapeutic applications [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. Whereas conventional antibodies typically recognize flat or convex antigenic surfaces through their complementarity-determining regions (CDRs), VNAR feature an extended CDR3 loop that adopts a distinctive binding mode reminiscent of peptide aptamers, enabling access to deep-binding pockets, such as those found in receptors. This ability is not commonly observed in conventional antibody formats [[10], [11], [12]]. Owing to these unique properties, the development of therapeutics that utilize VNAR as a novel modality is garnering increasing attention [[13], [14], [15], [53]].

The most classical and widely employed approach for antibody isolation is antigen-driven immunization. In this method, experimental animals, such as mice, are immunized with a target antigen, and antigen-specific antibody-producing B cells elicited by the immune response are isolated and analyzed to obtain the monoclonal antibodies of interest. Shark-derived VNARs have attracted increasing attention as a unique antibody format that can be isolated using immunization-based approaches [16]. However, the limited availability of sharks and the challenges associated with their maintenance and handling remain major obstacles to establishing robust experimental platforms and acquiring high-affinity VNARs [17].

Recently, in vitro selection using large combinatorial libraries enabled by display technologies has become an essential approach for the discovery of novel antibodies. Among the various display methods, phage display remains the most widely adopted and has been extensively utilized as an animal-free system for developing monoclonal antibodies, including VNARs [[18], [19], [20], [54]]. Phage display systems utilizing immunized, naïve, and semi-synthetic libraries have been established and successfully applied for VNAR screening [[21], [22], [23], [24]]. However, phage display has inherent limitations, including a restricted library size and the need for Escherichia coli transformation in each round of biopanning, which limit the throughput of the screening process [25].

In vitro display technologies, such as messenger RNA (mRNA) and complementary DNA (cDNA) display, are promising approaches for addressing these issues [[26], [27], [28]]. cDNA display is an in vitro selection technology that enables the construction of a peptide or protein library in which each polypeptide is covalently linked to its corresponding cDNA via a puromycin-modified DNA linker. cDNA display molecules exhibit superior chemical and biological stability compared to RNA-based display systems owing to the use of cDNA instead of mRNA to store genetic information. Notably, even if the RNA portion is degraded, it does not affect the selection process [29]. Furthermore, the use of a cell-free translation system in cDNA display technology facilitates the efficient construction of libraries with high diversity (1012-13), enabling rapid molecular evolution experiments [30,31]. Although we and other researchers have previously reported the applications of cDNA display to screen peptide aptamers, profile the substrates of enzymes, and develop single-domain antibodies such as Variable Heavy chain domains (VHHs), no study to date has explored the use of this technology for VNAR antibody screening [[32], [33], [34]]. cDNA display has previously been reported to be effective for VHHs, and its underlying principle strongly suggests that this approach should also be applicable to VNARs [[34], [35], [36]]. In the present study, we therefore report on the feasibility of applying cDNA display to VNARs.

In previous studies, we focused on the brownbanded bamboo shark (Chiloscyllium punctatum), a species that is relatively easy to obtain and maintain in captivity. By analyzing cDNA obtained from peripheral blood lymphocytes (PBLs) of this species, we successfully identified the gene sequences encoding the constant region of IgNAR [37]. In this study, by analyzing VNAR sequences by NGS, we confirmed that there was no marked sequence divergence attributable to inter-individual variation in the inuzame shark; based on this confirmation, we determined an average framework region (FR) sequence and the CDR3 loop length, and attempted to design an artificial VNAR library using them as guidelines. In the present study, we aimed to design an artificial VNAR library by determining the framework regions and lengths of the CDR loops of IgNAR genes. VNARs exhibit structural diversity, primarily based on the number and position of cysteine residues and the resulting disulfide bond patterns. They are generally classified into four distinct types ([38,39]; Barelle et al., 2013). Notably, Type IV VNARs possess a single disulfide bond with cysteine residues located exclusively within the framework regions, resulting in a relatively simple structural configuration. To date, semi-synthetic VNAR libraries based on shark-derived germline sequences, where the cysteine residue in CDR1 is substituted with tyrosine, have been reported [40]. In this study, we identified VNAR sequences from brownbanded bamboo sharks and constructed a novel synthetic VNAR library by randomizing the CDR1 and CDR3 loops to introduce molecular diversity. This approach enables the simple and cost-effective generation of artificial VNAR libraries without the use of experimental animals. Furthermore, we conducted in vitro selection experiments using a model antigen as the target to validate the applicability of cDNA display for VNAR screening. We selected three fluorescent proteins, EGFP, mNeonGreen, and mCherry2, as model antigens [[41], [42], [43]]. Although their tertiary structures are similar, they exhibit low homology in amino acid sequences. The selected libraries were analyzed using next-generation sequencing (NGS), which enabled the identification of candidate VNAR sequences that bind to each fluorescent protein. Subsequently, to assess their affinity and specificity, we evaluated the interactions between selected candidate VNARs and target fluorescent proteins.

Materials and methods

Sampling and cDNA library synthesis

Five adult male brownbanded bamboo sharks (Chiloscyllium punctatum) reared at the Fisheries Laboratory of the University of Tokyo, Hamamatsu, Japan, were used in this study. All experimental procedures were performed in accordance with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Guidelines. Whole blood samples were harvested from the caudal vein after anesthesia with ethyl 3-aminobenzoate methanesulfonate [37]. PBLs were isolated by centrifugation and stored in RNAlater reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Total RNA was extracted using an RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA synthesis was performed using the PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara Bio, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA synthesis was performed at 37 °C for 15 min, followed by 85 °C for 5 s. Approximately 3 μg of total RNA, prepared by pooling the total RNA separately from four individuals, was used as the template.

Sequence analysis of natural VNAR

DNA samples were prepared for sequencing following the manufacturer’s instructions. Specifically, full-length DNA constructs for NGS were generated by performing a three-step polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using the synthesized cDNA library as a template and the primers listed in Table S1 to selectively amplify VNAR genes (Fig. 1A). In the first PCR, the synthesized cDNA library was used as a template, and VNAR gene-specific primers VNAR_Fw1 and VNAR-Rv1, which bind to the leader sequence and first constant domain sequence, respectively, were used [37]. The PCR cycle conditions consisted of 98 °C for 1 min, followed by 35 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 15 s, and 72 °C for 15 s, and then 72 °C for 1 min. The product of the first PCR was then used as a template for the second PCR, which was performed using a mixture of primers VNAR01-Fw2 to VNAR17-Fw2 and VNAR01-Rv2 to VNAR17-Rv2, each of which incorporated Illumina adaptor sequences. The PCR cycle conditions consisted of 98 °C for 1 min, followed by 30 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 65.5 °C for 15 s, and 72 °C for 15 s, and then 72 °C for 1 min. In the third PCR, the second PCR product was used as a template, and amplification was performed using the forward primer VNAR-Fw3, which included an Illumina barcode and adaptor sequence, along with the reverse primers VNAR01-Rv3 to VNAR12-Rv3, which included an Illumina barcode, index (TruSeq), and adaptor sequences. The PCR cycle conditions consisted of 98 °C for 1 min, followed by 30 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 65.5 °C for 15 s, and 72 °C for 15 s, and then 72 °C for 1 min. PCR clean-up was performed using the FavorPrep PCR Clean-Up Mini Kit (Favorgen Biotech, Taiwan). The resulting full-length DNA constructs were analyzed by deep sequencing using an Illumina MiSeq. The experiments were performed by Fasmac Co., Ltd., Japan. Sequence logo plots were generated from the obtained DNA sequence data using WebLogo.

Design and construction of the VNAR DNA library

The DNA library used for model selection was designed based on sequence logo plots, with ambiguous residues resolved using VNAR sequence data published by Zielonka et al., followed by structural predictions using AlphaFold2 and AlphaFold3 [40,44,45]. A DNA library (VNAR16 library) was constructed using a two-step primer extension PCR method with the primers listed in Table S1 (Fig. 2C). First, extension PCR was performed using two primer pairs, PrF1/PrR2 and PrF3/PrR4-3, to generate two DNA fragments. The PCR cycle conditions for PrF1/PrR2 consisted of 98 °C for 1 min, followed by 10 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 67 °C for 5 s, and 72 °C for 13 s, and then 72 °C for 1 min. For PrF3/PrR4-3, the conditions consisted of 98 °C for 1 min, followed by 8 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 64 °C for 5 s, and 72 °C for 8 s, and then 72 °C for 1 min. The randomized regions of PrF1 and PrR4-3 were synthesized by incorporating trinucleotide mixtures encoding 19 amino acids, excluding cysteine, into the corresponding oligonucleotides. The PCR products obtained in the first step were purified by preparative polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) and enzymatically assembled using EcoRI (Takara Bio, Japan) and T4 DNA ligase (Takara Bio, Japan), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Each PCR product was incubated with EcoRI at 37 °C for 3 h, purified using the FavorPrep PCR Clean Up Mini Kit, ligated with T4 DNA ligase at 16 °C overnight, and finally purified by preparative PAGE. The assembled DNA fragment was further amplified by PCR using primers PrF5 and PrR6 to obtain full-length DNA constructs, and then amplified again using Newleft and cnvK NewYtag as primers. The PCR cycle conditions for PrF5/PrR6 consisted of 98 °C for 1 min, followed by 12 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 65 °C for 5 s, and 72 °C for 30 s, and then 72 °C for 1 min. For Newleft/cnvK NewYtag, the conditions were 98 °C for 1 min, followed by 5 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 69 °C for 5 s, and 72 °C for 30 s, and then 72 °C for 1 min. PCR clean-up was performed with FavorPrep PCR Clean Up Mini Kit (Favorgen Biotech, Taiwan).

Preparation of cDNA display coding VNAR16 library

The DNA library was transcribed to mRNA using the T7 RiboMAX Large-Scale RNA Production System (Promega, USA) and photocrosslinked to a 3-cyanovinylcarbazole nucleoside (cnvK) puromycin linker (cnvK linker) [29]. cDNA display was performed as previously described [29]. The obtained mRNA–linker complex was translated in vitro using the PURE system (GeneFrontier, Japan) to form mRNA display molecules. The mRNA display library was immobilized on streptavidin-coated magnetic beads (SA beads; Dynabeads MyOne Streptavidin C1; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and reverse-transcribed using ReverTra Ace reverse transcriptase (Toyobo, Japan). Subsequently, RNase T1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was added to release the cDNA display molecules from the beads. The crude cDNA display library was purified using HisMag Sepharose Ni beads (Cytiva, USA).

Model screening of fluorescent proteins using the cDNA display system

The model antigens EGFP, mNeonGreen, and mCherry2 were expressed using the pBAD plasmid system [[41], [42], [43]]. E. coli DH10B cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) harboring each plasmid were cultured in Terrific Broth medium supplemented with ampicillin (100 mg/mL) until an optical density (OD)₆₀₀ of 0.8 was reached, at which point protein expression was induced by the addition of L-arabinose to a final concentration of 0.04%, followed by incubation at room temperature for 20 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation and lysed using a cell disruptor (Branson). The soluble fractions were collected, and fluorescent proteins were purified using Ni-NTA affinity chromatography (G-Biosciences, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Purified proteins were dialyzed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) using an Amicon Ultra-4 centrifugal filter device (MWCO 10 kDa, Millipore, Germany). The fluorescent proteins were biotinylated with a 10-fold molar excess of EZ-link NHS-SS-PEG4-Biotin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 25 °C for 30 min, followed by buffer exchange using Zeba Spin Desalting Columns (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). At the first round of selection, 800 pmol of each biotinylated fluorescent protein was immobilized on SA beads and incubated with the purified cDNA display library, which had been prepared from 192 pmol of mRNA–linker complex, in 800 μL of PBS with Tween 20 (PBS-T buffer) (500 mM NaCl, 8.1 mM Na₂HPO₄, 2.7 mM KCl, 1.5 mM KH₂PO₄, 0.05% Tween 20, pH 7.4) at 25 °C for 60 min. After washing with PBS-T, the bound cDNA display molecules were eluted using PBS-T containing 10 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP) by cleaving the disulfide bonds. The eluted cDNA was purified using the FavorPrep PCR Clean Up Mini Kit (Favorgen Biotech, Taiwan), amplified by PCR using T7PURE and cnvK NewYtag primers, and the product was used to prepare a cDNA display library for subsequent rounds of selection. The PCR cycle conditions consisted of 98 °C for 1 min, followed by 25 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 69 °C for 5 s, and 72 °C for 30 s, and then 72 °C for 1 min. The amount of cDNA in each fraction was quantified using quantitative PCR (qPCR) and normalized to the total input. Four rounds of in vitro selection were performed as described above. For the 2nd to 4th rounds of selection, small (18 pmol in 2nd, 6 pmol in 3rd, and 3 pmol in 4th) mRNA-linker complexes were used. Furthermore, the concentration of each fluorescent protein was adjusted to 1 µM in the 1st round, 100 nM in the 2nd round, 10 nM in the 3rd round, and 1 µM in the 4th round. Sequence analysis of the final selection libraries was performed by deep sequencing on an Illumina MiSeq. The experiments were performed at Fasmac Co., Ltd., Japan.

Pull-down assay

DNA constructs of promising clones were prepared using a 3-step primer-overlap extension PCR. The first extension was performed using the PrF1CL and PrR1CL primers. The PCR cycle conditions consisted of 98 °C for 1 min, followed by 10 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 68 °C for 5 s, and 72 °C for 10 s, and then 72 °C for 1 min. The second PCR was performed using the obtained DNA as a template and two primers encoding the VNARs of interest (E1F2 and E1R2, Table S1). The PCR cycle conditions consisted of 98 °C for 1 min, followed by 25 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 68 °C for 5 s, and 72 °C for 19 s, and then 72 °C for 1 min. The third round of PCR was performed using PrF3CL and PrR3CL as primers, yielding full-length DNA constructs of the VNAR clones. The PCR cycle conditions consisted of 98 °C for 1 min, followed by 25 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 69 °C for 5 s, and 72 °C for 30 s, and then 72 °C for 1 min. PCR clean-up was performed with FavorPrep PCR Clean Up Mini Kit (Favorgen Biotech, Taiwan). An mRNA–linker complex encoding the VNAR of interest was converted into a cDNA display as described above and subsequently purified using His Mag Sepharose Ni beads. The obtained cDNA display was incubated with Dynabeads MyOne Streptavidin C1 beads, on which each fluorescent protein was immobilized, at 25 °C for 60 min. The beads were then washed with PBS-T, and the bound cDNA display was eluted with PBS-T containing TCEP, followed by further purification. The amount of cDNA display bound to the fluorescent protein-immobilized beads was quantified by qPCR and normalized to the input amount.

Biolayer interferometry

The equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) was determined using the Octet RED384 system (Sartorius, Germany) based on biolayer interferometry (BLI). The mRNA–linker complex was prepared following the same protocol as described above, except that a special linear DNA linker termed an Octet linker (5′-BAAAAATFTCCAKGCCGCCCCCCGTCCTCZZCCP-3′, B: 5′-Biotin-TEG, F: FITC-dT, K: cnvK, Z: Spacer18, P: 3′-puromycin) was used herein. The mRNA–linker complex (12 pmol) encoding the VNAR of interest was converted into an mRNA display as described above and incubated with RNase T1 and RNase H (Takara Bio, Japan) at 37 °C for 15 min to obtain the VNAR–linker complex. Four volumes of His-tag binding buffer (20 mM sodium phosphate buffer, 0.5 M NaCl, 5 mM imidazole, pH 7.4) were added, and the VNAR–linker complex was incubated with His Mag Sepharose Ni beads overnight at 4 °C. The beads were then washed, and the bound VNAR–linker complex was eluted using His-tag elution buffer (PBS, pH 7.4, 10 mM EDTA, and 0.05% Tween 20). All assays were performed using streptavidin-coated SAX2 biosensors (Sartorius, Germany) in kinetics buffer (PBS, pH 7.4, 0.1% (w/v) BSA, and 0.05% Tween 20) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The VNAR–linker complex described above was immobilized on the biosensor tips as a ligand, and the corresponding fluorescent protein was used as the analyte. In BLI analysis, measurements at each analyte concentration are generally performed using separate sensors, and differences in ligand immobilization levels on each sensor can arise due to lot-to-lot variation and other factors, leading to differences in the absolute values of the maximum responses. Hence, the fitting in this analysis was performed using an Rmax unlinked model, in which Rmax was calculated independently for each analyte concentration. The binding data were fitted using a 1:1 Langmuir binding model.

Results

Construction of a randomized VNAR library and cDNA display formation

Putative VNAR-encoding regions were amplified by PCR from the cDNA library derived from non-immunized brownbanded bamboo sharks and analyzed by deep sequencing. VNARs are classified into four types based on the number and positions of cysteine residues, and classification of the 19,159 VNAR reads after deduplication according to cysteine residue patterns showed that Type I, Type II, and Type IV accounted for 1.6%, 92.2%, and 6.2%, respectively (Fig. 1B). The representative sequences for each VNAR type are shown in Fig. 1C. Although marked diversity was observed, particularly in CDR3 across these types, the present study focused on the design of the Type IV VNAR library; therefore, two sequences were provided for Type IV for comparison. Analysis of the identified VNAR nucleotide sequences revealed the presence of an EcoRI recognition site within the FR3b. This site was used as an anchoring point for the enzymatic assembly of DNA fragments during the construction of full-length DNA constructs.

In the library randomization strategy, CDR1 was set at 8 residues and CDR3 at the modal length of 16 residues, while random amino acids excluding cysteine were employed to prevent the formation of unintended disulfide bonds (Fig. S1). Next, to determine the sequences of the FRs and HVs, we used sequence logo plots generated from the NGS data (Fig. S2). For logo plot generation, we used deduplicated VNAR sequences of all types obtained from the NGS reads. Rather than relying on a single-clone-derived sequence, we sought to minimize bias attributable to specific clones and to enhance the versatility of the library by averaging expression-derived sequences from the captive population. Hence, for residue assignment in the FR and HV regions, a 50% frequency cutoff was applied. Positions that did not meet this criterion were resolved by referencing previously reported residues (Fig. S3). Using this approach, the VNAR16 library sequence shown in Fig. 2A was ultimately determined [40]. Structure prediction of the VNAR16 library using AlphaFold2 and AlphaFold3 indicated that a disulfide bond was formed within the VNAR domain and that CDR3, which interacts with the antigen, adopted a long hairpin loop structure, as shown in Fig. 2B [44,45]. We also examined whether sequence variations in CDR1 and CDR3 affect the three-dimensional structure of the VNAR16 library. Calculations were performed under conditions in which the amino acid composition of the CDRs was biased toward polar, nonpolar, aromatic, or proline-rich residues, and it was confirmed that the characteristic VNAR fold was maintained under all conditions (Fig. S4).

We constructed a full-length DNA construct by attaching the DNA moiety required for cDNA display synthesis. Elements such as the T7 promoter and linker hybridization region (LHR) were attached to both ends of the VNAR16 library sequence (Figs. 3A and S5), according to our previous study (Anzai et al., 2023). The formation efficiency of the cDNA display from this library (i.e., the ratio of successfully purified display molecules to input mRNA-linker) was estimated to be 0.64% (Fig. S6).

In vitro selection of antigen-binding synthetic VNARs

Considering the essential roles of fluorescent proteins in molecular and cell biology, as well as previous reports of their sdAbs being useful as experimental tools [46,47], three fluorescent proteins (EGFP, mNeonGreen, and mCherry2) were selected as the target antigens. In vitro selection of the VNAR16 library was performed using three different fluorescent protein antigens in four rounds of selection for each target (Table S2; Fig. 3B). First, the DNA of the VNAR16 library was transcribed into an mRNA library, and an mRNA-linker complex was formed by photocrosslinking the transcripts with a DNA linker containing puromycin (cnvK linker). The mRNA-linker complex was then subjected to cell-free translation. When puromycin (linked to the mRNA) enters the ribosome, it forms a covalent bond with the C-terminus of the translated VNAR, resulting in the formation of a protein-mRNA conjugate. Reverse transcription of the mRNA region yielded a cDNA display of the VNAR16 library. The cDNA display library was incubated with magnetic beads, on which the target antigen was immobilized. The cDNA display molecules bound to the target were recovered and amplified by PCR to construct a selected library. By repeating this cycle, VNARs with binding affinities for the target antigen were gradually enriched. The theoretical diversity of cDNA display molecules is calculated as the product of the molar amount of input mRNA–linker, cDNA display formation efficiency, and Avogadro’s constant. Based on this calculation, the diversity of cDNA display molecules used in the first round selection was estimated to be approximately 7.4 × 10¹¹, which is the highest value reported to date for VNARs when compared with phage display at 1.2 × 10¹⁰ and yeast display at 2 × 10⁸ [21,40]. Furthermore, whereas the large-scale preparation of phage display libraries reported in the literature requires a reaction volume of 2.5 L, our method enables easy preparation with a volume of only 0.8 mL, which corresponds to the capacity of a single microcentrifuge tube [21]. In all rounds, each fluorescent protein was immobilized onto streptavidin-coated magnetic beads using a disulfide-containing biotin linker. The cDNA display molecules were then incubated with the target, and those bound to the target were eluted using TCEP and 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate. From the second to the fourth rounds, only TCEP was used during the elution step to enhance the target specificity. Additionally, pre-selection was performed before affinity selection, where beads immobilized with non-target fluorescent proteins were used to remove non-specific binders. The resulting supernatant containing the unbound cDNA display molecules was incubated with beads coated with the target fluorescent protein, and the cDNA display bound to the target was collected via reductive cleavage with TCEP. Control selection using unmodified streptavidin-coated beads was performed under identical conditions. The amount of cDNA recovered from each round was quantified using qPCR to monitor the enrichment and selectivity of the antigen-binding VNARs (Fig. 3C). After the fourth round, VNARs with affinities for each fluorescent protein appeared to be enriched, as indicated by significantly higher cDNA recovery rates for the target beads than for the control beads.

Identification of antigen-binding VNAR candidates

The converged DNA libraries obtained for each target antigen through in vitro selection were analyzed using deep sequencing, and the resulting sequences were translated into amino acid sequences for further analysis. The high frequency of the top read sequences, along with the presence of identical CDR3 sequences, suggests that sufficient selection pressure was applied; however, no clear consensus motif was identified (Table 1). Next, we constructed a phylogenetic tree of the top-read sequences using MEGA12 software [48]. The VNAR sequences were classified into distinct groups corresponding to EGFP, mNeonGreen, and mCherry2, indicating the potential for antigen specificity (Fig. S7). Consequently, we selected 10 VNAR clones based on this analysis, either because of their high read frequency or their significant sequence differences. The selected sequences (VNAR-E1, E2, E4, N1, N3, N4, C1, C2, C4, and C6) were cloned and used to prepare cDNA display molecules. Pull-down assays were conducted, and the bound molecules were detected using qPCR (Fig. 4A). A standard curve was generated using a 10-fold serial dilution of the prepared cDNA display, and the input amount of the cDNA display diluted 100,000-fold was normalized to 1. The results showed that VNAR-E1, E2, and E4; VNAR-N1 and N3; and VNAR-C2, C4, and C6 exhibited binding affinities for their respective target fluorescent proteins (Fig. 4B). Based on the affinity analysis results, specificity assays were conducted for VNAR-E1, E4, N1, N3, C2, and C6, which showed relatively strong binding affinities for their target fluorescent proteins. Although non-specific binding to panels of irrelevant proteins was not clear, relatively weak non-specific interactions with other fluorescent proteins were observed, indicating high specificity. However, moderate cross-reactivity was observed for VNAR-C2 and VNAR-C6 (Fig. 4C). Accordingly, VNAR-E1, N1, and C6 were selected for detailed affinity characterization.

Determination of the affinity of VNARs by BLI

The binding properties of the selected VNARs (E1, N1, and C6) and their target fluorescent proteins were evaluated using biolayer interferometry (BLI). In our previous study, we established a simple and rapid system for measuring affinity using a peptide–linker conjugate and a target antigen [29]. In this study, we applied the same approach using a VNAR-linker conjugate for affinity analyses. VNAR–linker conjugates were obtained by RNase treatment of mRNA display clones and subsequent purification via their His-tags. These complexes were immobilized on streptavidin sensor chips, followed by the addition of the respective fluorescent proteins to measure binding responses (Fig. 5A). The results showed that the VNAR-E1–linker conjugate had a moderate affinity for EGFP (KD = 6.6 µM, R² = 0.8201, χ² = 0.2201), and VNAR-N1 showed a high affinity for mNeonGreen (KD = 41.4 nM, R² = 0.9025, χ² = 0.4075). However, no specific binding was observed between VNAR-C6 and mCherry2 (Fig. 5B, Table 2).

Discussion

VNARs possess uniquely long CDR3 loops, complex disulfide bond patterns, and a compact structure owing to the absence of CDR2, distinguishing them from VHHs and scFvs in terms of their binding mode. These structural characteristics enable VNARs to function as binders, even against antigenic sites that are typically inaccessible to conventional antibodies [49]. The isolation of VNARs using evolutionary molecular engineering approaches has been dominated by phage and yeast display, and no studies have reported the use of in vitro display technologies, such as mRNA or cDNA display [21,40]. In this study, we constructed a VNAR library with full randomization of both CDRs based on Type IV VNAR gene sequence information and demonstrated the feasibility of applying cDNA display to a high-throughput VNAR screening system. Type IV VNARs lack the disulfide bond between CDR1 and CDR3, which may confer greater structural flexibility on CDR3 and thereby contribute to interactions with multiple target antigens [40]. Based on this rationale, we adopted a type IV VNAR library design in which NGS data were used to define a representative VNAR scaffold sequence and CDR3 length that were not markedly biased by individual differences among sharks, and in which CDR1 and CDR3 were fully randomized without cysteine residues. Notably, the removal of cysteines from CDR1 and CDR3 is expected to suppress undesirable disulfide bond formation involving the CDRs, display molecules, or target antigens, thereby preventing aberrant cross-linking and structural fixation while preserving CDR3 flexibility and the functional diversity of the library. Although not a substitute for experimental validation, AlphaFold analysis provided supplementary supporting evidence that the designed sequences were not likely to be structurally compromised by differences in CDR sequence type (Fig. S4). Furthermore, by enzymatically assembling DNA fragments based on the EcoRI recognition site present in FR3b, we successfully constructed the library while limiting PCR amplification to two steps and minimizing PCR-associated variation in CDR diversity. Together, these design features enabled the construction of a highly diverse type IV VNAR library suitable for cDNA display-based screening. In addition to its high diversity, cDNA display also offers the advantage of allowing VNAR libraries to be prepared easily on a small scale (diversity: 7.4 × 1011, scale: 800 μL). Phage display requires preparation on a liter scale to achieve a diversity of 1.2 × 1010, whereas yeast display is limited to a diversity of 2 × 108. These comparisons suggest that cDNA display offers advantages in terms of library scalability and throughput. However, in any display technology, it is not possible to completely cover all sequences in the constructed VNAR library; therefore, the process of affinity maturation after the initial selection is considered useful [40].

In this study, as the objective was neither ultrasensitive protein detection nor therapeutic application, the affinity bias for in vitro selection was set in the range of 1 μM to 10 nM, and no bias was imposed at lower concentrations. Binding affinity assay using BLI showed that VNAR-E1 (KD = 6.6 µM) and VNAR-N1 (KD = 41.4 nM) were consistent with the selection stringency, indicating that the in vitro selection functioned as expected. However, while the χ² values were within an acceptable range, the R² values suggest that the fit for VNAR-N1 is borderline acceptable and that the fit for VNAR-E1 is comparatively poor. Accordingly, the derived kinetic and affinity parameters may be subject to increased uncertainty and should be interpreted cautiously. VNAR-N1 demonstrated 449-fold greater binding to mNeonGreen than to the structurally similar EGFP and 149-fold greater binding to mCherry2 in the pull-down assay, indicating its selectivity for the target antigen. Although the aggregation and oligomerization states of the fluorescent proteins and VNARs were not definitively characterized in this study, EGFP is known to exist as a weak dimer under physiological conditions, whereas the others are generally regarded as monomers. However, the possibility of partial aggregation or oligomerization cannot be excluded, as immobilization on beads increases the local concentration. For VNARs, the possibility of aggregation cannot be excluded, as these proteins do not necessarily exhibit high aqueous solubility. Nevertheless, our one-step screening approach confirmed that it was possible to obtain a VNAR with an affinity comparable to that achieved through affinity maturation using yeast display, suggesting the utility of the synthetic VNAR library and cDNA display-based screening system. Moreover, the construction of a secondary library for affinity maturation may provide a viable strategy for further improving the affinity of the VNARs obtained through this approach.

However, the current workflow has several technical issues that must be addressed. First, the cDNA display formation efficiency of the synthetic VNAR library was approximately 20-fold lower than that of previously reported cyclic peptide libraries [29]. In particular, a significant loss of cDNA display molecules was observed during the reverse transcription reaction and subsequent cleavage from the magnetic beads (Fig. S6). This is likely due to steric hindrance caused by the molecular size and structural complexity of the VNARs. The introduction of a flexible linker or hinge sequence between puromycin and the C-terminus of VNAR may improve the formation efficiency. Second, the BLI signals of the VNAR-linker conjugates were very weak. To address this issue, we extended the immobilization time of the VNAR-linker complex on the sensor chip. However, the amount of fluorescent protein bound as the analyte was approximately 25- to 50-fold lower than the amount of immobilized VNAR–linker conjugate used as the ligand, and the overall signal-to-noise ratio remained low, suggesting that the binding activity of the complex to the streptavidin-coated surface was limited. The cause of this decreased activity remains unclear, but the efficiency of complex formation or the fraction of correctly folded VNAR in the sample may be low, for example, due to incomplete disulfide bond formation during cell-free translation. Although the expression and purification of VNAR as an isolated protein is preferable, the present study employed the VNAR–linker conjugate format, which has been previously validated. Future efforts should focus on optimizing the oxidative folding conditions specific to the VNAR–linker conjugate and introducing additional purification steps to enhance its functional activity. Additionally, this issue can adversely affect the screening efficiency of cDNA display libraries, underscoring the need for further methodological improvements. Finally, the expression of VNARs in E. coli remains to be determined. Several VNARs reported to date have been shown to be expressible in E. coli [6,21,40,50,51]. However, whether the sequences obtained in the present study can be expressed in E. coli at high yields and in a soluble form requires separate validation. Because this issue was considered to fall within the application stage, periplasmic expression and optimization of expression strains were not addressed in the present study. Experimental evaluation of thermal stability (Tm) and solubility will therefore remain important in future studies.

The cDNA display system has been successfully applied to the in vitro selection of antibody-like molecules and various scaffold proteins, and has been established as a powerful screening technology comparable to phage display and mRNA display systems. Owing to several advantages, including rapid selection in a cell-free system, high stability and retrievability of genetic information via cDNA, and the capacity to handle libraries with extremely large diversity, cDNA display has attracted considerable attention as a powerful platform for in vitro selection [29,33,34,52]. The present study provides the first case study demonstrating the applicability of cDNA display to VNAR selection, suggesting that this approach could serve as a new screening strategy for future VNAR development. Nevertheless, the method remains insufficiently optimized, and further methodological improvements will be necessary to enable the robust and general acquisition of potent binders.

Finally, in the library design of this study, the consensus residues were adopted at positions within the framework regions. Consequently, there is a risk that the resulting library scaffold may not be representative of the majority of VNARs found in the expression repertoire. Furthermore, its biophysical properties may prove inferior to those of a validated single scaffold based on highly expressed, germline-encoded V and J genes. We note that our library itself has not yet been validated, and a more rigorous benchmarking comparison between VNAR cDNA display and phage display will be possible in future studies using a well-characterized immune VNAR library that has been validated and shown to contain high-affinity binders.
