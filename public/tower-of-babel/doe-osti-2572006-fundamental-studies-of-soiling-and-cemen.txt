ABSTRACT:
Accumulation of soils and other particulate matter on the front cover glass of solar photovoltaic
(PV) modules results in transmission losses that detrimentally affect the power output of PV
installations. The conventional solution to this problem is manual abrasive scrubbing of modules,
which entails significant water usage and manpower. To further combat this issue, there is a
concerted effort within the PV industry to develop anti-soiling (AS) coatings to mitigate power
losses due to soiling of PV modules, which can exceed 35% in some cases. However, relatively
little is known about the fundamental physical and chemical mechanisms that enable anti-soiling
behavior. In this project, a combination of advanced X-ray analysis tools and first principles
computational modeling has employed to understand the physical and chemical interactions
between environmental soils and PV cover glass materials. The specific objectives were: 1)
investigate soiling interactions and cementation behavior of relevant PV materials, including bare
glass, conventional antireflective (AR) coatings, and a commercial AS coating, using a library of
representative particulate soils, and 2) utilize first principles computational spectroscopy
modeling to interpret the experimental X-ray spectroscopy measurements and determine the
detailed surface chemistry changes that occur on these surfaces in the presence of soiling and
cementation.

1. Introduction
Transmittance losses due to soiling of the front cover glass of solar photovoltaic (PV) modules
result in reduced power generation and negatively impact the levelized cost of electricity (LCOE),
which is a critical parameter for establishing competitiveness between PV power generation and
fossil fuel sources. The attenuated solar transmittance that results from the accumulation of
particulate matter on a PV module effectively shades the cells, resulting in decreased power output.
In effect, soiling of PV modules can be thought of as a durability and reliability issue that needs to
be addressed. The nature of soiling of PV modules in the field is highly stochastic due to
regionally-dependent soil compositions, weather patterns, and local humidity and temperature.
Any amount of soiling represents a financial detriment to a solar installation, both through the loss
of power output and the operations and maintenance (O&M) costs associated with cleaning.

Furthermore, since modules in the field are continuously exposed to dew cycles, the front cover
glass is susceptible to chemical reactions that occur due to interactions between dust and the glass
surface in the presence of temperature, humidity, and pH. These reactions are thought to be the
root cause of a process called cementation, in which inorganic contaminants become chemically
bonded to the cover glass surface. As a result, there has been a growing interest within the industry
in developing anti-soiling coatings, which modify the way that particulate soils interact with the
coating surface [9]. While there are some locations and climates that are comparatively less
susceptible to soiling, there are no locations where soiling is not a factor in PV power production.
The regional variation in soil composition (both chemical and particle size) results in different
spectrally-dependent absorption and scattering characteristics for the accumulated soils.

Both soiling and soiling related damage (such as cementation) to the front cover glass can greatly
affect the performance, and therefore the economics, of PV installations. Over the lifetime of a PV
module, soiling represents an obstacle that decreases power output, negatively impacts the
operational integrity and reliability of the modules, and can vastly alter the economic impact of

that occur between realistic environmental soils and PV cover glass surfaces will help address the

2. Accelerated Soiling and Cementation Testing
2.1 Instrumented Soiling Chamber Development
An instrumented soiling chamber was developed to enable large-scale accelerated soiling and
cementation testing of glass coupons. The construction of the chamber is described in [1]. Specific
capabilities include temperature control of the sample plate from 0°C-100°C, humidification
control, and automated routines for simulated dew cycling. Standardized particulate dust
compositions were introduced to the chamber via compressed air, and allowed to uniformly settle
of the sample surfaces. To simulate cementation, the samples would then be subjected to
alternating cycles of low temperature/high humidity (to initiate condensation) and high
temperature/low humidity (to facilitate evaporation of condensed “dew”). Each dew cycle could
be completed in approximately 1 hour; PV modules in the field will typically experience a dew
cycle once per day, resulting in an effective 24´ speed-up using the artificial dew cycling.

2.2 Dust Deposition Characteristics
Accelerating cementation testing was performed first on bare low-iron glass coupons using 3
different dust formulations provided by Powder Technology Inc. (Arden Hills, MI): Arizona Test
Dust (ATD), Aramco Test Dust (ARM), China Test Dust (CDT). Each of these formulations are
standardized with respect to chemical composition and particular size distribution to mimic the
soil composition specific geographic areas: the American Southwest (ATD), the Arabian Gulf
(ARM), and Western China (CDT). Photographs demonstrating the dust deposition quality and
pigment are shown in Fig. 1 below.

2.3 Surface Chemistry Analysis of Deposited Dust in Response to Cementation
All 3 dust formulations described in Section 2.2 were subjected to up to accelerated cementation
testing for up to 30 dew cycles (effectively mimicking 1 month of outside exposure). Of particular
interest is the evolution of the dust surface chemistry in response to cementations. The results of
these experiments are described in [2]. Based on X-ray photo electron spectroscopy (XPS)
analysis and complementary water contact angle (WCA) measurements, it is hypothesized that
cementation in CTD is the result of carbonation of CaO, which is a slowly evolving chemical
reaction at atmospheric pressure due to the low partial pressure of atmospheric CO2. For ATD and
ARM, cementation may be driven by different chemical reactions due to differing
SiO2/Al2O3/CaO ratios and the formation of aluminosilicates. In addition, the ARAMCO dust is
composed of 90% ATD along with 10% milled salt, which is soluble during dew condensation and
can redeposit during evaporation. As a result, each soil potentially undergoes cementation due to
different interaction mechanisms, with can require a different mitigation strategy that depends on
geographic region.

functional silica-based coatings. These coatings were composed of silica nanoparticles, which
were nominally functionalized to be terminated with either hydroxyl or methyl groups, which
impacts the surface wettability. Since anti-soiling behavior is typically mediated by interactions
with water (including rain and intentional washing), the long-term stability of the surface
chemistry of these coatings is critical. In Fig. 2 below, the surface wettability of the solar

Due to the non-uniform deposition of dust on the methylated coatings due to surface energy
considerations, the optical losses that are associated with dust coverage are substantially reduced
as compared to the hydroxylated solar coating. Together, these measurements demonstrate a
complicated landscape of chemical interactions that occur between the deposited dust and surface
of a PV coating that influence the long-term stability and performance of these coatings in practical
settings. Even under the most controlled circumstances, chemical analysis is difficult to
quantitatively interpret. To address this, first principles X-ray absorption spectroscopy
calculations are a candidate to improve the interpretation of experimental results.

3. X-ray Absorption Spectroscopy Calculations from First Principles
3.1 Density Functional Theory Calculations and Model Development
To study the detailed surface chemistry of functionalized silica surfaces at the nanoscale, density
functional theory calculations were performed on model, undercoordinated amorphous silica
surfaces with a selection of relevant terminating groups: -OH, -CH3, and -CF3. These model the
solar (hydroxylated) and methylated surface coatings, along with a hypothetical coating
composition that includes simple fluorocarbons. Firstly, classical molecular dynamics (MD) is
used to build an atomistic model of a-SiO2, using melt-quench dynamics. A supercell of crystalline
a-quartz is modeled using a Si-O Tersoff potential [3], and equilibrated to 300 K under NVT
dynamics with a Nosè-Hoover thermostat. Next, the quartz is heated to 7000 K (well above the
melting temperature corresponding to the selected Tersoff potential), and then rapidly back to 300
K at a rate of 5 K/ps. The resulting bulk a-SiO2 supercell is then cleaved to expose an under-
coordinated a-SiO2 surface. The coordination number of each Si atom on the exposed surface is
calculated, and the neighborhood an isolated undercoordinated Si atom is selected to serve as a
sub-domain for density functional theory (DFT) calculations. This process is illustrated
schematically in Fig. 5.

4. Conclusions
Accelerated soiling and cementation testing has been performed on relevant PV cover glass
materials, including two functional anti-reflective coatings with several standardized soil
compositions. Chemical analysis of the particulate dusts and glass coupon surfaces after
cementation reveals substantive chemical changes that elucidate some of the relevant cementation
mechanisms. The surface energy of the functional coatings plays a substantial role in the optical
losses due to soiling, with lower energy coatings performing better due to soil agglomeration
behaviors. Complimentary DFT calculations provide a promising mechanism to better interpret
experimental NEXAFS (or other X-ray spectroscopy technique) results, potentially enabling the
assignment of specific peak shifts and absorption edge splitting to be associated with specific
functional groups and/or chemical reactions. The results of this study better inform the
mechanisms of cementation in PV materials, especially related to anti-soiling behavior, which will
support the development and validation of anti-soiling coatings in the rapidly growing US solar
industry and further facilitate the design of advanced materials for a transformative energy
technology.

5. Recommendations
COVID-related safety precautions and other operations disruptions at the Stanford Synchrotron
Radiation Lightsource impacted the availability of synchrotron X-ray analysis tools during the
project period. More data, especially O and C K-edge NEXAFS spectra, will help better validate
the DFT-based XAS calculations as a method to understand cementation reactions in PV cover
glass materials.
