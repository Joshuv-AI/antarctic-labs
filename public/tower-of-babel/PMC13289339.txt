Quantitative metabolomics and machine learning identify differential metabolomic biomarkers across normoglycaemia, pre-diabetes and diabetes in a Qinghai Plateau population
Authors: Tiemei Li, Bin Zhang, Qingxia Huang, Ruijie Xu, Haobo Gao, Lin Shi, Haijing Wang, Lei Zhao, Huiru Tang, Youfa Wang, Wen Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo identify biomarkers for pre-diabetes mellitus (PreDM) and diabetes mellitus (DM) in high-altitude Tibetan populations through integrated analysis of body fat composition (BFC), haematologic parameters, and metabolomics.Methods904 Tibetan participants were included. T-tests and Wilcoxon tests assessed differences in 21 blood parameters, 15 BFC measures, 7 anthropometrics, 332 metabolites among Control, PreDM and DM. Key biomarkers were identified using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), random forest (RF) and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) models. Diagnostic performance was evaluated using logistic regression, reporting precision, F1-score, sensitivity, specificity and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC).ResultsPreliminary analysis showed significant differences in 302 metabolites, 6 anthropometric, 14 biochemical and 15 BFC indicators between disease and control groups (p<0.05). Based on LASSO, RF and XGBoost methods, the biomarkers including body mass index, waist, visceral fat level (VFL), blood cholesterol, cholesteryl esters-to-total lipids ratio in low-density lipoprotein 6 (LDL6), phospholipid-to-total lipids ratio in intermediate-density lipoprotein (IDL) (IDPLp), polyunsaturated fatty acid/total fatty acid (PUFAp), triglycerides in LDL1, free cholesterol to total lipids ratio in LDL6, hypersensitive C reactive protein (hsCRP), red blood cell distribution width and LDL1 particle number showed differences between Control and PreDM; body mass index, waist, systolic blood pressure (BP), diastolic BP, hsCRP, VFL and particle number in IDL (IDPN) showed differences between Control and DM; IDPN, triglycerides-to-total lipids ratio in LDL3, cholesteryl esters-to-total lipids ratio in LDL1, and 3-hydroxybutyrate showed differences between PreDM and DM. Selected biomarkers demonstrated high diagnostic performance. AUROCs were 0.919 (0.883 to 0.954) for PreDM versus Control, 0.980 (0.943 to 1.000) for DM versus Control, 0.694 (0.597 to 0.783) for DM versus PreDM.ConclusionsThis study identified metabolic biomarkers (eg, IDPLp, PUFAp, hsCRP, VFL) that distinguish normoglycaemia from dysglycaemia in high-altitude Tibetans, but not preDM from DM. These findings provide insights into high-altitude metabolic dysregulation and lay a foundation for future altitude-specific screening.Keywords: Public Health, Diabetes Mellitus, Biometry

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

In 904 Tibetan adults, machine learning identified lipoprotein and metabolite biomarkers (eg, phospholipid-to-total lipids ratio in intermediate-density lipoprotein, polyunsaturated fatty acid percentage, hypersensitive C reactive protein) that accurately distinguish normoglycaemia from dysglycaemia, with area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) values of 0.919 and 0.980 for the two models. By contrast, the corresponding AUROC value was 0.694 for the differentiation between pre-diabetes and diabetes. These markers reveal hypoxia-specific lipid dysregulation.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM), a chronic metabolic disorder, predisposes individuals to severe complications including cardiovascular and renal diseases, substantially impairing quality of life and reducing life expectancy.1 China faces an acute challenge with its adult DM prevalence reaching 12.4%, significantly exceeding the global average.2 Pre-diabetes mellitus (PreDM), characterised by impaired glucose regulation, affects nearly 400 million adults globally.3 4 In China, a cross-sectional survey found a high PreDM prevalence of 35.7% among adults, much higher than in other regions around the world.4 Annually, 5%–10% of PreDM individuals progress to DM,5 with a lifetime conversion risk of 70%.6 7 Therefore, patients with pre-diabetes should take early control measures to prevent further development towards diabetes.

The Tibetan Plateau, averaging 4500 m above sea level,8 harbours a population with traditionally nomadic lifestyles and barley-based diets rich in dietary fibre, factors potentially contributing to historically low DM prevalence, approximately 4.3%.9 However, rapid modernisation is altering this trajectory, with rising DM incidence alongside persistently low disease awareness and treatment rates.9 10 Notably, the progression from PreDM to DM is highly heterogeneous, and a fraction of individuals with PreDM may regress to normoglycaemia without treatment,11 yet approximately 90% remain unaware of their dysglycaemic status.12 13 Furthermore, the precise characteristics defining the PreDM population, especially within the Chinese population, remain incompletely characterised.14 Therefore, clearly defining the characteristics of PreDM and DM populations, particularly elucidating differences in metabolic markers among individuals residing on the Tibetan Plateau, holds significant scientific and public health significance.

Serum metabolomics provides a comprehensive platform for low-molecule-weight metabolites profiling. Notably, detailed analysis of lipoprotein subfractions offers superior disease risk stratification compared with conventional lipid parameters.15,21 In recent years, machine learning (ML) algorithms, such as random forests (RF), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) and Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO), have demonstrated remarkable efficacy in integrating complex, multidimensional data to uncover intricate biomarker associations. This capability is exemplified by studies leveraging ML for metabolomics-based disease diagnostics. For instance, RF identified some high-density lipoprotein (HDL) particles enabling a DM diagnostic model with 0.931 sensitivity for early-stage patients.22 Similarly, LASSO selected significant variables for early recognition of coronary artery disease, with an XGBoost model achieving an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.988.23 Critically, the synergistic application of algorithms, particularly LASSO for feature selection coupled with XGBoost for modelling, has consistently yielded high diagnostic performance (AUC>0.9) across diverse disease contexts, underscoring the significant potential of ML in translating serum metabolomics findings into clinical applications.24,26 Although lipoproteins have received significant attention in the context of DM and PreDM, the predominant focus on individual lipid metrics has resulted in insufficient systematic analysis of lipoprotein particle subtypes, and comparative studies investigating the characteristic differences in lipoprotein particles between PreDM and DM specifically within plateau populations remain notably lacking.

Consequently, this study aimed to systematically compare quantitative profiles of body fat composition (BFC), blood parameters, and lipoprotein subfractions and small molecular weight metabolites among normoglycaemic (Control), PreDM and DM groups in a Tibetan plateau cohort, while employing advanced ML approaches to identify and validate biomarkers distinguishing PreDM from DM.

Methods and design

Study population and design

We conducted a cross-sectional study using data from a community-based open cohort,27 which included 1611 adults recruited from two settled Tibetan communities in the suburbs of Golmud City (2800 m above sea level) between December 2021 and May 2022. After exclusion of participants lacking physical examination data and biochemical indicator data (n=356), participants without DM or PreDM but have other chronic disease (n=351), a total of 904 Tibetan adults aged 18 to 85 years were included in the final analysis (figure 1). This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines.

Physical and biochemical measurements

Data on annual household income, education level, as well as the amount of smoking and alcohol consumption, were obtained through face-to-face interviews at study enrollment. Smokers were defined as individuals who currently smoked or had smoked within the past 5 years, while alcohol consumers were defined as participants who had used alcohol within the past 5 years. A whole-body composition analyser (Inbody 270, Korea) was used for BFC measurement in participants with a standard procedure. All participants refrained from exercise 2–3 days before the sample collection visit, maintained adequate sleep, and were on an empty stomach for 10–12 hours. Hospital nurses collected 5 mL of blood from the elbow vein, placed it in heparin-anticoagulant tubes, and mixed by inverting. The blood samples were sent to the laboratory of the Second People’s Hospital of Golmud City for processing and testing within 1 hour after collection. The test items included blood and urine routine tests, liver function indicators, such as alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alkaline phosphatase, renal function indicators, such as urea nitrogen, creatinine and uric acid.

H1-NMR profiling of serum metabolites

Metabolomics was detected on a 600 MHz AVANCE III Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) spectrometer equipped with a BBI probe (Bruker Biospin, Germany) following the protocol reported previously.28,30 Serum samples stored at −80◦C were thawed at 24°C within 30 min, and all subsequent operations were performed on the ice. Serum sample (320 µL) was mixed with phosphate buffer (0.085 M, pH 7.4, 320 µL) containing 10% D2O, and 600 µL of such mixture was transferred into a 5 mm diameter NMR tube. Samples were automatically handled by a SampleJet (Bruker Biospin) sample changer at 4°C before being inserted into an NMR spectrometer for metabolomic analysis. Two NMR spectra were recorded with a standard NOESYGPPR1D and LEDBPGPPR2S1D pulse sequence into 98 k data points at 310 K with 32 transients. Following the Fourier transformation, 112 lipoprotein parameters were quantified using the Bruker IVDr Lipoprotein Subclass Analysis (B.I.LISA). Besides, a total of 41 metabolites, including amino acids, ketone bodies, glucose, carboxylic acids and ethanol, were also quantified from the same spectra using the Bruker IVDr Quantification in Plasma/Serum (B.I.Quant-PS, Bruker Biospin). Detailed lipoprotein profiles consisted of quantitative concentrations for very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL), intermediate-density lipoprotein (IDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL) as well as HDL, 15 lipoprotein subfractions consisted of different components, and functional parameters were calculated from the above quantitative data. Other details of serum metabolites in this study have also been previously reported.15 Ultimately, 332 parameters were obtained to define the metabolomic phenotypes of each human serum sample (online supplemental table S1). NMR metabolomics was performed for all samples between April and May 2023. To evaluate analytical performance, the coefficient of variation (CV) of each metabolite was calculated across eight pooled serum QC sample aliquots analysed from 7 February to 10 October 2023. Metabolites with QC CV >30% or a missing rate >20% across all 20 samples were excluded. Missing values in metabolites with <20% missingness were imputed using the RF approach in the R package missForest.

Definition of DM and PreDM

Diabetes was diagnosed as fasting glucose levels ≥7.0 mmol/L or a glycated haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) level of ≥6.5% measured by a standardised test or a self-reported previous diagnosis of DM. PreDM was diagnosed as 6.1 mmol/L ≤fasting glucose <7.0 mmol/L or 5.7% ≤HbA1c level <6.5%, according to the 2024 American Diabetes Association’s Standards of Medical Care in Diabetes.31

Model development

To avoid data leakage and obtain unbiased performance estimates, we first stratified the participants by glycaemic status (Control, PreDM, DM) and randomly partitioned them into a training set (70%) and an independent test set (30%). All feature selection and model development were performed exclusively within the training set. The test set was kept completely separate and used only once at the final stage for performance evaluation. Importantly, fasting plasma glucose (FBG)and HbA1c, the diagnostic criteria used to define glycaemic status, were excluded from the predictor set to avoid circularity (online supplemental table S2). To address high dimensionality and collinearity, we applied three ML methods for feature selection. LASSO regression was performed with 10-fold cross-validation to determine the optimal regularisation parameter λ; variables with non-zero coefficients at λ₁ₛₑ were retained to ensure coefficient stability and mitigate overfitting risk.32 RF hyperparameters (number of trees, mtry) were optimised via cross-validation, selecting the configuration that minimised the out-of-bag error; feature importance was then quantified using the mean decrease in Gini impurity. XGBoost hyperparameters (including nrounds) were similarly optimised via cross-validation, with early stopping (100 rounds) applied to prevent overfitting; features with importance above the median were retained. Variables identified by at least two of the three ML models were designated as the final selected biomarkers. We further elucidated the interpretability of key biomarkers using Shapley Additive exPlanations.33 Logistic regression was chosen as the final classifier due to its interpretability and ease of clinical translation. Using the selected features, we trained a logistic regression model on the training set. To assess the internal stability of the model, we performed repeated stratified fivefold cross-validation (10 repeats) within the training set. The mean area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and its SD were calculated from all validation folds. As a sensitivity analysis, we compared the performance of full LASSO, RF and XGBoost classifiers with logistic regression. Finally, the locked model was evaluated once on the independent test set, reporting AUROC, precision, recall, F1-score, sensitivity and specificity with 95% CIs. The optimal probability threshold was determined by maximising the Youden index.

Statistical analyses

Participants were categorised into three groups (Control, PreDM, DM) coded as 0, 1 and 2, respectively. Baseline characteristics were presented as numbers (%) for categorical variables and median (P25; P75) for continuous variables (non-normal). Group differences were assessed for statistical differences using the Kruskal-Wallis H test for continuous variables, and the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact probability test for categorical variables. All statistical analyses were two-sided, and p<0.05 was considered statistically significant if no specific indication was stated. For ML-based biomarker discovery and prediction, we performed pairwise binary classification for each comparison separately: Control versus PreDM (0 vs 1), Control versus DM (0 vs 1), and PreDM versus DM (0 vs 1). All analyses were performed in R software (V.4.4.1), using the packages: ‘dplyr’, ‘caret’, ‘stats’, ‘multcomp’, ‘glmnet’, ‘randomForest’, ‘xgboost’, ‘pROC’, ‘ggplot2’ and ‘shapviz’.

Patient and public involvement

Participants were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this study.

BFC alterations across glycaemic states

Building on these demographic trends, comprehensive metabolic profiling revealed significant alterations in body composition and lipoprotein dynamics (Fig. 2, online supplemental table S2). Consistent with prior studies, indicators of central adiposity and overall body mass, such as visceral fat level (VFL), waist, and BMI, effectively distinguished the Control group from the PreDM group.34,36 Additionally, the PreDM group exhibited significant increases compared with Controls in blood markers (ALT), alongside decreases in AST/ALT ratio, albumin and HDL (figure 2A, online supplemental table S3). This pattern is well-aligned with the metabolic consequences of transitioning from a traditional, energy-intensive nomadic lifestyle to a more sedentary one. Excess visceral fat, as observed, releases free fatty acids; in synergy with hypoxia-induced lipolysis enzyme activity,37 38 this exacerbates hepatic gluconeogenesis and peripheral insulin resistance, providing a mechanistic link consistent with the literature.37 38 Additionally, in the comparison between Control and DM group, the changes in systolic blood pressure (SBP) were of great significance. Previous research has shown that the intricate dynamics, wherein a 10 mm Hg increase in SBP corresponds to an 8% increase in diabetes incidence, provide quantitative insights.39

Metabolic alterations across glycaemic states

In comparative analyses of lipoprotein subfractions (figure 2, online supplemental tables S3-S5), the PreDM versus Control group exhibited the most pronounced positive changes in VLDL-associated components—notably free cholesterol (FC), cholesterol (CH), triglycerides (TG), total lipids (LP), phospholipids (PL) and cholesterol esters (CE) within the VLDL 1 subclass (V1FC, V1CH, V1TG, V1LP, V1PL, V1CE), alongside elevated TG and LP in IDL (IDTG and IDLP), and ratios of IDL-CH/HDL-CH (ICHHCR) and VLDL-CH/HDL-CH (VCHHCR). Conversely, the greatest negative changes involved reductions in CH-to-TG ratio (CHTGR), CHTGR in HDL (H4CHTGR, H0CHTGR, H2CHTGR, H3CHTGR) and IDL (IDCHTGR), PL-to-LP ratio in IDL (IDPLp), apolipoprotein A1 (ApoA1) and FC in HDL 1 subclass (H1A1, H1FC), FC-to-LP in LDL 4 (L4FCp) (figure 2, online supplemental table S3). For DM versus Control, CE in IDL (IDCE) and VLDL 1 subclass (V1FC, V1CH, V1TG, V1LP, V1PL, V1CE) showed maximal increases, while CHTGR in LDL subclasses (L1CHTGR, L3CHTGR) and IDPLp decreased most significantly (figure 2, online supplemental table S4). When comparing PreDM and DM, TG-to-LP ratios in LDL subclasses (L3TGp, L4TGp), particle number (PN), apolipoprotein B (ApoB), CE, TG in IDL (IDPN, IDAB, IDCE, IDTG), H1TG, V4FC demonstrated the strongest positive shifts, whereas CHTGR and CHTGR in lipoprotein subtypes (L5CHTGR, L4CHTGR, L3CHTGR, L0CHTGR, L1CHTGR, H1CHTGR), and cholesterols in LDL subfractions (L3CE, L3CH) declined markedly (figure 2, online supplemental table S5). Among them, 27 lipoproteins were exclusively altered in PreDM versus Control, 4 lipoproteins in DM versus Control, and CE-to-LP ratio in VLDL 4 (V4CEp) solely in PreDM versus DM (figure 2).

Variables with non-zero coefficients in the model were chosen: LDL1 PN (L1PN), ApoB in LDL1 (L1AB), alanine, acetone, CE to LP ratio in LDL6 (L6CEp), TG to LP ratio in IDL (IDTGp), PL to LP ratio in VLDL (V0PLp), PL-to-LP ratio in IDL (IDPLp), aromatic amino acids (Phe+Tyr+Trp) (AAA), polyunsaturated fatty acid/total fatty acid (PUFAp), unsaturated fatty acid to polyunsaturated fatty acid ratio, waist, BMI, red blood cell distribution width (RDW), blood cholesterol (CH), VFL (online supplemental figure S1A-B). Complementary RF and XGBoost analyses identified the top 20 variables, respectively (online supplemental figure S1C-D). Among the three ML models (LASSO, RF, and XGBoost), with consensus biomarkers across ≥2 models comprising BMI, waist, VFL, CH, L6CEp, IDPLp, PUFAp, TG in LDL 1 (L1TG), FC to LP ratio in LDL6 (L6FCp), hypersensitive C reactive protein (hsCRP), L1PN, RDW (figure 3A-C).

Prediction of DM using biomarkers

To evaluate the predictive performance of the identified biomarkers, we first trained a logistic regression model on the training set using the consensus biomarkers selected by at least two ML methods. To assess internal stability, we performed repeated stratified fivefold cross-validation (10 repeats) on the training set. The mean AUROC and its SD were calculated from all validation folds. As shown in online supplemental figure S2, the internal cross-validation yielded a mean AUROC of 0.926 ± 0.029 for distinguishing PreDM from Control, 0.963 ± 0.030 for DM from Control, and 0.779 ± 0.065 for PreDM from DM. These results indicate that the logistic regression model based on the selected biomarkers has excellent and stable discriminative ability within the training data.

Subsequently, the locked logistic regression model was applied once to the independent test set (30% of the data, completely separated from all feature selection and training steps) to obtain unbiased performance estimates. As presented in figure 4, the model achieved high performance on the test set for all three comparisons. We found that the AUROCs were 0.919 (0.881 to 0.952) between PreDM and Control, 0.980 (0.940 to 1.000) between DM and Control, 0.694 (0.597 to 0.783) between DM and PreDM (figure 4).

As a sensitivity analysis, we directly compared the logistic regression model with LASSO, RF and XGBoost classifiers using the same selected biomarkers on the independent test set. There were no statistically significant differences (DeLong test, p>0.05; online supplemental figure S3). Given the comparable predictive accuracy, the simpler and more interpretable logistic regression was retained as the final model.

Discussion

This study establishes a comprehensive metabolic profile of Tibetan plateau populations across normoglycaemic, PreDM and DM states by integrating multidimensional data on BFC, blood parameters and metabolomics. Notably, ML identified distinct biomarker panels, including VFL, Waist, BMI, SBP, DBP, hsCRP, CH, RDW, L6CEp, 3_HBA, IDPLp, L1TG, PUFAp, L6FCp, L1PN, IDPN, L3TGp and L1CEp (selected by≥2 ML models), that effectively distinguish glycaemic states at high accuracy (Control vs PreDM (AUROC: 0.883–0.954); Control vs DM (AUROC: 0.943–1.000); However, the model performed poorly in distinguishing PreDM from DM (AUROC: 0.597–0.783)), suggesting that the selected biomarkers have limited utility for staging disease severity.

Before discussing the biological implications, we critically examine the validity of applying standard ADA HbA1c criteria in this high-altitude setting. Importantly, the optimal HbA1c cut-off for detecting diabetes varies substantially across high-altitude populations, ranging from 6.0% to 6.7%.40,42 This heterogeneity reflects the complex interplay of altitude, duration of residence, degree of erythrocytosis and genetic background (eg, EPAS1, EGLN1, PPARA haplotypes), indicating that a single ‘perfect cut-off’ applicable to all high-altitude populations is currently unavailable.43 Given this uncertainty, our study does not aim to redefine diagnostic thresholds. Instead, our goal is biomarker discovery using the ADA criteria, which capture broader metabolic information (long-term glucose exposure and postprandial excursions) than fasting glucose alone. This approach enables ML models to identify meaningful metabolomic signatures. To quantify this bias, we performed a sensitivity analysis using only FBG to define glycaemic status. Under the FBG-only definition, preDM cases dropped from 561 to 62 and DM cases from 108 to 77, and the same biomarkers performed poorly (AUROC 0.627–0.697; online supplemental figure S6). These results confirm that abandoning HbA1c would lead to severe information loss and underdiagnosis. Thus, while the ADA criteria may overestimate prevalence, they remain the most practical exploratory definition for biomarker discovery in this setting. Future studies should establish altitude-specific thresholds using Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (OGTT) as the gold standard.

These findings align with the paradigm shift toward nontraditional lipoprotein risk factors, which have gained increasing recognition in cardiovascular and metabolic disease research, surpassing traditional anthropometric and biochemical indicators such as BMI, ALT and HDL. Indeed, lipoprotein subfractions provide a more detailed and potentially earlier indication of metabolic disturbances, reflecting their growing importance in understanding the pathogenesis of cardiovascular and metabolic diseases. This shift in focus underscores their potential as valuable biomarkers for early risk detection.44 Consistent with this shift, our findings robustly demonstrate that alterations in specific lipoprotein particles and metabolites, such as IDPLp, PUFAp, L1PN, L6CEp, L6FCp, L1TG and RDW, are potent discriminators between normoglycaemia and dysglycaemia (Control vs PreDM and Control vs DM) within this high-altitude population. Notably, several of these markers—including CH, VFL, BMI, waist, hsCRP, SBP and DBP—were also selected, highlighting the multifaceted nature of metabolic dysregulation.

In alignment with these ML advantages, our study employed a rigorous tri-model framework (LASSO, RF, XGBoost) to identify biomarkers across all critical glycaemic comparisons: distinguishing Control from PreDM, Control from DM and PreDM from DM. This multimodel strategy enhanced robustness by mitigating selection bias inherent to single algorithms. Critically, this ML analysis was applied to a comprehensive metabolomic dataset encompassing quantitative profiles of lipoprotein subfractions and key small-molecule metabolites, basic anthropometric indicators, blood physiological indicators, biochemical indicators and BFC, among different groups. This approach directly addresses the recognised scarcity of studies using detailed metabolomics, particularly lipoprotein subfractions, for predicting transitions across early glycaemic stage,45 46 yielding robust biomarkers with high diagnostic accuracy.

Despite the high accuracy for detecting dysglycaemia, the model performed poorly in distinguishing PreDM from DM (AUROC=0.694, 95% CI 0.592 to 0.780). This limited discriminative ability is consistent with the clinical reality that Pre-DM and DM lie on a metabolic continuum rather than representing discrete categories. Many metabolic abnormalities—including altered lipoprotein metabolism, inflammation, and ketone body utilisation—already manifest during the prediabetic state and become further exacerbated only modestly in DM. Our biomarker selection was driven by contrasts between health and disease (Control vs PreDM and Control vs DM); consequently, the selected markers (eg, IDPLp, PUFAp, L1PN, hsCRP) show strong signal for detecting any dysglycaemia but have limited power to stage disease severity. Moreover, the small absolute sample size of the DM group (n=108) may have reduced statistical power for this pairwise comparison, and the potential overestimation of dysglycaemia by HbA1c at high altitude (as discussed in the limitations) could have introduced label noise that disproportionately affects the PreDM versus DM contrast. Nevertheless, the few markers that were selected for PreDM versus DM, IDPN, L3TGp, L1CEp, and 3_HBA may reflect later-stage metabolic deterioration. IDPN elevation suggests increased IDL particle flux, a known atherogenic feature; L3TGp increase indicates TG enrichment of small dense LDL, which is associated with enhanced atherosclerotic properties; L1CEp decrease implies impaired cholesterol esterification; and 3_HBA elevation is consistent with increased ketogenesis under insulin deficiency. These markers merit further investigation in larger, longitudinally designed studies.

Turning to mechanistic insights, interestingly, we observed significant changes in key lipoprotein alterations between Control and PreDM groups, with IDL particles, particularly IDPLp, emerging as key markers. The abnormal metabolism of IDL in the high-altitude Tibetan population appears closely linked to reduced hepatic metabolic efficiency under chronic hypoxia.47 Specifically, hypoxia upregulates hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF), which inhibits hepatic PL transfer protein activity, decreasing PL incorporation into IDL. As PL is critical for IDL structural stability, lower PL content reduces the IDPLp, impairing IDL stability and its conversion efficiency to LDL.48 From a metabolic pathway perspective, IDL conversion to LDL depends on hepatic lipase (HL) and lipoprotein lipase (LPL). Reduced IDPLp weakens IDL-HL binding, delaying IDL clearance and increasing vascular cholesterol deposition risk.49 High-altitude hypoxia further inhibits hepatic HL secretion, while insulin resistance in the PreDM group exacerbates LPL activity decline, collectively amplifying IDPLp-related IDL metabolic impairment.50 Furthermore, traditional Tibetan diets rich in barley (a source of β-glucan) promote lipid excretion, compensating for reduced IDPLp. However, diminished barley intake in modern PreDM diets weakens this protection, leading to more significant lipid clearance delays.27

Meanwhile, the increase in L1TG and L6FCp in the PreDM group may be related to abnormal cholesteryl ester transfer protein (CETP) activity.51 Enhanced CETP activity promotes the transfer of TG from VLDL to LDL, while reducing cholesteryl ester content in LDL, resulting in a lipid clearance disorder.52 This CETP-mediated dysregulation further compounds the lipid handling issues initiated by hypoxia and IDPLp reduction. Furthermore, in the DM group, L3TGp increased while L1CEp decreased, indicating remodelling of LDL subcomponents. Small, dense LDL3 (reflected by L3TGp) contains more TG and is associated with enhanced atherosclerotic properties.53 Under insulin resistance, excessive hepatic TG production and decreased LPL activity lead to TG increase, which exacerbates β-cell lipotoxicity.54 The concomitant decrease in L1CEp stems from reduced lecithin-cholesterol acyltransferase activity, impairing CE synthesis.55 This LDL remodelling represents a more advanced stage of dyslipidaemia.

Additionally, the ratio of PUFAp was significantly decreased in patients with PreDM and DM (online supplemental figure S4). PUFAs are key components of cell membranes and precursors for anti-inflammatory lipid mediators; their decline may impair membrane fluidity and reduce the synthesis of inflammation-resolving molecules such as lipoxins and resolvins.56 Epidemiologic studies have reported a close relationship between elevated biomarkers of inflammation and the worsening of DM and its complications.57

Beyond some specific lipoprotein change, our findings also highlight the role of inflammation and specific metabolites in disease progression. Previous research has indicated that elevated levels of hsCRP are strongly associated with advanced β-cell dysfunction and insulin resistance in patients with type 2 diabetes (T2D).58 Worsened low-grade inflammation (through increased levels of hsCRP) and poor glycaemic control were accompanied by reduced β-cell function in patients with T2D.59 Elevated RDW was also selected in PreDM versus Control, which may reflect chronic low-grade inflammation or erythropoietic stress under hypoxia (online supplemental figure S5).

This study offers significant strengths through its unique focus on Tibetan populations residing at high (2800 m) and ultrahigh (>4000 m) altitudes, encompassing comprehensive sociodemographic, clinical and lifestyle data from 904 adults, capturing distinct high-altitude adaptations. Quantitative profiling of 332 serum lipoprotein subfractions in this cohort revealed altitude-specific dysregulation of lipid metabolism across glycaemic states, providing novel mechanistic insights into lipid biology associated with PreDM and DM progression. Furthermore, employing a tri-model ML framework (LASSO, RF, XGBoost) significantly mitigated selection bias, enhancing confidence in identified biomarkers.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences about metabolic progression, necessitating longitudinal validation of predictive biomarkers. Second, the sample is limited to the Tibetan community in Qinghai; the low-oxygen and pressure environment limits the generalisability of our findings to other populations or regions. Third, although we included multiple indicators such as BFC and blood parameters, potential confounding factors—such as genetic background, detailed dietary patterns, quantitative smoking/alcohol consumption and medication history—were not addressed, which may affect the accuracy of the results. Fourth, we performed propensity score matching (1:1) on age, BMI, and waist to assess whether our biomarkers discriminate glycaemic status independently of these factors. After matching, the AUROCs decreased substantially, indicating that our metabolic signature shares considerable variance with age and adiposity. Given the exploratory nature of this study, we do not claim to have identified obesity-independent biomarkers. Rather, our findings provide a metabolic snapshot of dysglycaemia in a high-altitude Tibetan population, where age and body composition are integral to the metabolic state. External validation in cohorts with different demographic profiles is needed to test generalisability. Fifth, the application of standard ADA diagnostic criteria in this high-altitude cohort may have overestimated the true prevalence of PreDM and DM, as chronic hypoxia induces an upward bias in HbA1c independent of glycaemia. Future studies should establish altitude-specific diagnostic thresholds or develop metabolomics-based risk scores less susceptible to hypoxic interference, ideally using OGTT as the gold standard.

Conclusions

We identified quantitative biomarkers that distinguish normoglycaemia, Pre-DM and DM in high-altitude Tibetans, revealing altitude-specific lipoprotein dysregulation. However, these biomarkers poorly differentiate Pre-DM from DM, and their performance decreased after propensity score matching on age, BMI and waist. Despite these limitations, this study provides a metabolic snapshot of glucose dysregulation under chronic hypoxia and supports the future development of altitude-adapted screening tools.
