Response of three bell pepper cultivars to different nutrient solutions in NFT hydroponic system
Authors: Hamid Reza Roosta, Sajad Azizabadi, Seyed Hossein Mirdehghan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOptimizing hydroponic bell pepper (Capsicum annuum L.) production requires tailored nutrient solutions, as cultivar needs and climate conditions vary. This study therefore evaluated three globally-used nutrient solutions on three popular cultivars (‘Nirvin’, ‘Aranko’, and ‘Taranto’) under Iran specific climate, in a nutrient film technique (NFT) hydroponic system. The nutrient solutions tested were the standard Hoagland solution (control), the Netherlands nutrient solution, and the USDA-recommended solution. A factorial experiment based on a completely randomized design with three replications was conducted to assess growth, physiological traits, yield, and fruit quality parameters. Results showed that both cultivar type and nutrient solution significantly affected vegetative growth, reproductive traits, photosynthetic pigments, and fruit quality. ‘Aranko’ generally exhibited higher biomass accumulation (30.98% SDM) and leaf area (32.17%), especially when grown with the USDA solution, which also enhanced number of fruit (81.96%) and fruit mass (6.5%) compared to Hoagland solution. ‘Nirvin’ showed the highest leaf relative chlorophyll content (SPAD) and chlorophyll b content, particularly under the Hoagland solution. ‘Taranto’ demonstrated the highest photosynthetic efficiency across all nutrient solutions. The Netherlands and USDA solutions improved dry biomass and carotenoid content compared to the control. Photosynthetic parameters such as Fv/Fm and net photosynthesis were influenced by nutrient solution and cultivar interactions. Fruit quality traits, including firmness, vitamin C content, and color indices, were also enhanced by the USDA solution. Overall, the findings indicate that ‘Aranko’ showed the best results in yield, with superior biomass accumulation and fruit mass, particularly under the USDA solution.Subject terms: Plant sciences, Plant physiology

Introduction

Bell pepper (Capsicum annuum L.) is a horticultural crop of considerable interest in various countries, particularly Iran. The significance of this crop is not only due to its economic importance, but predominantly because it constitutes an exceptionally rich source of ascorbic acid. Furthermore, this pepper variety contains substantial amounts of insoluble dietary fiber, which can play a crucial role in the prevention and management of gastrointestinal disorders, such as constipation1. Additionally, bell peppers are a rich source of vitamins A and C, as well as the antioxidant lycopene, all of which may contribute to the prevention of various cancers. Today, soilless cultivation is increasingly recognized globally as a technology for producing plants that enhances the quality and quantity of horticultural products2. Hydroponic systems are expanding due to their numerous advantages, such as increased yield, production of healthy and uniform products, and reduced labor requirements3. The nutrient film technique (NFT) is a hydroponic method based on a shallow flow or thin layer of nutrient solution over the roots of growing plants to provide water, nutrients, and oxygen3. This efficient, cost-effective, and robust system enables precise regulation of the root-zone environment, facilitates uniform irrigation, and ensures consistent nutrient delivery to all plants4–8. Therefore, commercial systems use automated control of electrical conductivity (EC), pH, and nutrient solution analysis to inject a specific amount of supplemental solution into the system9. One of the key factors for successful production in these systems is providing the necessary nutrients for the plant, depending on the environmental condition during the growth cycle. In this cultivation system, the provision of an optimized nutrient supply is essential, serving as the primary determinant of plant growth, development, and productivity in the absence of soil10. Numerous hydroponic nutrient solution formulations, differing mainly in the concentration ratios of elements, have been developed and reported in the scientific literature to optimize plant growth and physiological responses across a range of crops11. Among these solutions are those developed by Hoagland and Schneider12, Ima and Angel11, and Novella et al.11 Additionally, leading countries in this field, such as the Netherlands and the United States, have proposed nutrient solutions based on their climatic conditions and the types of crops they cultivate. One of the basic needs of plants, in soilless cultivation, is nutrients, and if nutritional conditions are not balanced, production decreases. This nutritional requirement varies for each plant13. In general, a good nutrient formula depends on several variables, including plant species and cultivar, the type of system used (open or closed), the growth stage of the plant, the consumable part of the plant, planting season, and climatic conditions (temperature, light intensity, and day length)11. According to Steiner, the composition of the nutrient solution should maintain a specific balance capable of meeting the plant’s essential nutrient needs11. Excessively high levels of elements can cause osmotic stress, ionic toxicity, and ionic imbalance, while excessively low levels can lead to nutrient deficiencies14. Many efforts have been made to establish optimal total ionic concentrations in nutrient solutions for greenhouse crop production15–17. However, despite these efforts, there is still limited information regarding the optimal element concentrations for many crops.

Numerous nutrient solutions have been generally introduced for hydroponic cultivation, but all solutions are fundamentally based on six main elements: nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur, potassium, calcium, and magnesium. These generally include anions such as NO3−, H2PO4−, and SO42− and cations such as K+, Ca2+, and Mg2+18. Regarding the concentration range of the solution, it has been reported that reducing the solution concentration by up to 50% has no significant effect on the growth of crops such as anthurium (Anthurium andraeanum)19, gerbera (Gerbera jamesonii)20, and tomato (Lycopersicom esculentum Mill.)21. Even in closed hydroponic systems, while the concentrations may decrease in the root zone, no effects on plant growth have been reported22. On the other hand, increasing concentrations due to excessive absorption of elements can cause toxicity in plants. Although it has been reported that increasing the concentration in sage with a 200% Hoagland solution resulted in increased flowering, dry mass, and leaf area18. Furthermore, increasing potassium concentrations at 3.4 and 14.2 milliequivalents per liter has been shown to increase the dry mass of fruit, soluble solids, and lycopene in tomatoes23. Contrary to common belief, the balance of elements in a solution determines the optimal growth conditions24. The effects of different nutrient solution concentrations (full and ¾ strength Hoagland and Arnon25 nutrient solution, full and ¾ strength Rahman and Inden26 nutrient solution) on four varieties of capsicum (‘California Wonder’, ‘Wonder Bell’, ‘Capsicum F1a’ and ‘Capsicum F1b’) were evaluated. Using ¾ strength Rahman and Inden solution was suitable for California Wonder peppers in hydroponic systems in Bangladesh27. The effects of four nutrient solutions (Hoagland 1, Hoagland 2, England, and Knop) on physiological traits and fruit yield in two pepper cultivars (Dimaz and California Wonder) was investigated28. ‘Dimaz’ exhibited the highest total soluble solids and pH in the fruit. Ultimately, the England nutrient solution was identified as the most suitable for hydroponic pepper cultivation based on fruit quality parameters. A study found the optimal EC values of 2.0 dS m− 1 for tomato and 2.4 dS m− 1 for bell pepper in indoor vertical farms (IVF)29. Tomatoes and bell peppers grown in IVFs showed significant increases in total biomass compared to greenhouse seedlings, with noteworthy improvements at specific DLI (daily light integral) levels. Despite a dilution effect reducing nutrient concentration in plant, nutrient uptake was highest in IVF seedlings irrigated with the optimal nutrient solutions and DLI. In roses, nutrient solutions at 10 and 22 °C showed that the solution at 10 °C was absorbed less than at 22 °C but resulted in increased ammonia uptake and thicker root production, enhancing PSII photosynthesis activity30. Increased ammonia uptake provides a readily assimilated nitrogen source, which is rapidly incorporated into amino acids within the roots, stimulating meristematic activity and cortical cell expansion, thereby promoting the development of thicker root morphology with greater storage and metabolic capacity31. This enhanced NH₄⁺ assimilation within the thicker roots generates a substantial carbon-sink demand, consuming ATP and requiring carbon skeletons (2-oxoglutarate), which drives an increased phloem loading of sugars and alleviates end-product feedback inhibition on the source leaves. Consequently, the elevated sink strength upregulates the expression and activity of Rubisco and the electron transport chain, while the thicker root system improves hydraulic conductivity and nutrient translocation, collectively enhancing net photosynthetic rates to meet the heightened metabolic demand13.

Due to the fact that bell peppers fall into the category of fruit vegetables, they require more nutrients in hydroponic systems, particularly nitrogen, potassium, calcium, and phosphorus, compared to leafy vegetables11. In soilless cultivation, bell peppers experience two distinct growth phases, each with different nutritional needs. The initial phase post-transplantation is characterized by vegetative development aimed at establishing robust plant architecture over approximately six weeks, followed by the reproductive phase during which nutrient management is critical to maintain balanced nutrition and support sustained growth and fruit production. It has been reported that potassium plays a significant role in the second phase, following fruiting, and should be increased in the second solution, while nitrogen levels should be decreased. For bell peppers, nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium are the most critical nutrients, with their roles described as follows: Proper vegetative growth before fruiting is crucial for the plant’s ability to produce maximum fruit. Increasing nitrogen in the nutrient solution for bell peppers leads to an increase in the number of flowers and fruits. Phosphorus is essential for root development and reproductive stages such as flower, fruit, and seed production. High phosphorus in the nutrient solution contributes to plant establishment after seed sowing. Increased phosphorus has also been reported to accelerate fruiting in bell peppers32.

The issues in soilless cultivation of bell pepper include managing nutrient solution composition, electrical conductivity, and ensuring balanced nutrient uptake to optimize vegetative growth and fruit quality. Different cultivars vary in their physiological responses and nutrient requirements, making it essential to tailor nutrient formulations. Therefore, studying the interaction between nutrient solutions and cultivar types is necessary to enhance productivity and quality in hydroponic bell pepper cultivation. Considering that nutrient solution types depend on climate and the cultivar being grown, this study evaluated three well-known nutrient solutions used in different climatic conditions worldwide for bell pepper cultivation on three market-preferred cultivars under Iranian climatic conditions. The research hypothesis was that different nutrient solutions can affect bell pepper cultivars differently. Therefore, in this experiment, we aimed to investigate the effects of three solutions: the standard Hoagland solution as a control, the recommended solution for bell peppers in the Netherlands, and the solution recommended by the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) on the growth characteristics, physiological traits and fruit characteristics of the three commercial bell pepper cultivars: ‘Aranko’, ‘Nirvin’, and ‘Taranto’, and examined the strengths and weaknesses of each solution’s effect on the growth and yield of bell peppers, with emphasis on potassium, phosphorus, and nitrogen due to differing recommended concentrations, and discussing potential improvements.

Materials and methods

Experimental design and growth conditions

This experiment was conducted factorially based on a completely randomized design with three replications in the hydroponic greenhouse unit located in Rafsanjan, Iran (30°41″N, 55°99″) at an altitude of 1514 m, using the NFT system. The greenhouse maintained a temperature range of 25 ± 3 °C during the day and 20 ± 3 °C at night, with a photoperiod of 11 h of light (650 µmol m− 2 s− 1 PPFD) and 13 h of darkness, along with a relative humidity of 55–65%. For this purpose, seeds of three commercial bell pepper cultivars (F1) ‘Nirvin’, ‘Aranko’, and ‘Taranto’, produced by the Rijk Zwaan company, Netherlands, were obtained through Sepahan Royesh Isfahan, Iran, and sown in trays containing a perlite growing medium on October 15, 2022. Until the three-leaf stage, all cultivars were irrigated and fed only with Hoagland solution. After reaching the three-leaf stage, the seedlings were transferred as bare roots into mesh pots (5 cm diameter, filled with cocopeat: perlite substrates, 50:50 V/V) within the NFT system 34 days after seed sowing and systematically arranged in three separate NFT systems (3 m × 3 m, considering system limitation) to allow plant establishment and root access to the thin nutrient layer from above (Fig. 1). The distance of the system from the ground was 90 cm and the flow rate of nutrient solution was 2 L min− 1. Twenty-seven plants were growing in these hydroponic systems, with 3 plants for each treatment. In each separate NFT system, one of the solutions (USDA33, Netherlands33, and Hoagland11 was used (Table 1). The solution was completely replaced every week. EC of nutrient solutions was ~ 2.3 dS/m, reaching to 2.1 end of the week; and pH was maintained at 5.8–6.5 by using HNO3. The fruit harvest started on February 20, 2023. At the final harvest time on March 14, 2023, the plants were collected for parameter analysis.

Measurement of parameters

Vegetative and reproductive growth

The growth and reproductive parameters measured in this experiment included: number of leaves, number of fruits, leaf area, stem diameter, average fruit mass (yield), fresh mass of roots and shoots, and dry mass of roots and shoots. In all plants growing in the NFT system, stem diameter at the crown was measured with a caliper, and total leaf area of the plant was measured using a CI-202 Leaf Area Meter, CID Co., USA. For measuring fresh mass, the plant was divided into two parts: shoot and root. After washing the roots and allowing them to dry relative to the moisture from washing, each part was weighed separately. For dry mass measurement, plants remained one day in the greenhouse before transferring to the oven, during which free water was evaporated from the plants, then samples were placed in an oven at 72 °C for 48 h and then transferred to the laboratory for mass recording34.

Plant pigments

To measure chlorophyll a, b, total chlorophyll, and carotenoids at flowering and harvest, two mature leaves were sampled from each plant and extracted with acetone. Specifically, 0.25 g of fresh leaf sample was ground in a porcelain mortar with 10 mL of 80% acetone until a uniform solution was obtained. The samples were then placed in 20 mL Falcon tubes and centrifuged at 3500 RPM for 10 min. The absorbance of the resulting solution was measured using a spectrophotometer (UV/VIS Spectrometer, T80 Instruments PG Ltd model, made in Australia) at wavelengths of 480, 510, 645, 652, and 663 nanometers. SPAD index

To measure the SPAD index, three mature leaves were selected from each plant, and greenness was measured using the SPAD-502 Chlorophyll Meter, Minolta Co., Japan, at harvest.

Photosynthetic parameters

These parameters included the maximum quantum yield of PSII photochemistry (Fv/Fm), and photosynthetic performance index (PI). For measuring chlorophyll fluorescence and the photosynthetic performance index, the Hansatech LTD Pocket PEA Chlorophyll Fluorimeter, made in England, was used, which expresses chlorophyll fluorescence based on the variable Fv/Fm. At the harvest time, three leaves were selected from each plant, and the device clips were attached to them. After 20 min to create dark conditions, the Fv/Fm and PI indices were read.

For measuring photosynthetic characteristics, the CI-340 Photosynthesis Meter from CID Co., USA, was used. Measurements were conducted the day before harvesting, specifically around 9:00 AM and 12:00 Noon on fully expanded leaves35. The leaves were placed in the cuvette for 3 min, and then the measurement was done36. The air temperature and light intensity in cuvette during measurements were ambient. The CO2 concentration was 400 ppm and molar flow of air was 200 mol. s-1,37.

This device operates based on gas exchange through stomata and measures carbon dioxide and water vapor exchange, providing a non-destructive method for measuring parameters involved in photosynthesis. This measurement was conducted the day before harvesting (3 mature leaves/plant), and the resulting data included characteristics such as stomatal conductance, transpiration, gas exchange, net photosynthesis, water use efficiency (WUEi), and instantaneous carboxylation efficiency (A/C).

Fruit pigments

To measure lycopene, one gram of fresh bell pepper fully ripe fruit was ground in a porcelain mortar using liquid nitrogen. Then, 16 mL of an acetone-hexane solution (4 mL of acetone and 6 mL of hexane) was added to create a homogeneous solution. The samples were transferred to Falcon tubes, and after a few minutes, two phases were formed automatically. The upper phase was then removed, and absorbance was measured using a spectrophotometer (UV/VIS Spectrometer, T80 Instruments PG Ltd model, made in Australia) at wavelengths of 453, 505, 645, and 663 nanometers. The amount of lycopene was calculated using the following formula38:

Lycopene (mg/100 mL) = (− 0.0458 × A663) + (0.204 × A645) + (0.372 × A505) − (0.0806 × A453).

Vitamin C

To measure vitamin C, titration was used. First, juice was extracted (2 fully ripe fruits/plant) using a manual juicer, and 2 mL of each sample were set aside for separate titration. The iodine method with potassium iodide was chosen for titration. To prepare the iodine solution, 1.27 g of iodine were dissolved in a few milliliters of ethyl alcohol (Solution 1). Then, 16 g of potassium iodide were dissolved in distilled water and the volume was brought to 300 mL (Solution 2). Solutions 1 and 2 were combined and diluted to 1000 mL. To prepare the 1% starch indicator, one gram of starch was dissolved in distilled water and the volume was brought to 100 mL. The iodine solution was placed in a burette, and in an Erlenmeyer flask for each sample, 2 mL of juice, 2 mL of 1% starch, and 25 mL of water were added. The titration was continued until the solution changed to a light gray color. Each milliliter of the potassium iodide solution corresponded to 0.88 mg of vitamin C in 2 mL of juice, which was then calculated to determine the amount in 100 mL of juice34.

Fruit color

To assess the surface color of the fruit skin, a chroma meter (Konica Minolta CR 400, made in Japan) was used to measure the color of four different parts of the surface of five fruits from each replication. The chroma meter presents color as three indices: a*, b*, and L*. The L* index indicates the brightness (lightness) of the fruit skin color, ranging from zero (black) to 100 (white). The range of the a* index varies from + 60 (red) to − 60 (green), with green decreasing from − 60 and red decreasing from + 60 towards the center of the color diagram (zero). The b* index ranges from + 60 (yellow) to − 60 (blue), also decreasing towards the center of the color diagram (zero). The Chroma index indicates the intensity or purity of the color, with a value of zero at the center of the color diagram, increasing as the distance from the center increases. Nitrate in fruit juice

The nitrate level in fruit juice was measured using the portable NO3 device (LAQUATWIN, HORIBA Co., Japan). After extracting juice (2 fully ripe fruits/plant) with a manual juicer, the device was calibrated with distilled water for each sample. Then, 0.3 mL of fruit juice was placed in the device sensor to measure the nitrate level.

Titratable acidity

The titratable acidity of the fruit extract (2 fully ripe fruits/plant) was determined via titrating 0.01 N NaOH to the endpoint of pH 8.1. The results were expressed in mg equivalents of citric acid per 100 g of fresh mass40.

Total soluble solids (TSS) in fruit

Total soluble solids (°Brix) of 2 fully ripe fruits /plant were measured with a refractometer (PAL-1, Atago Co., Ltd; Japan). To read the Brix level of the fruit juice, a few drops of juice were placed on the prism surface of the refractometer, and the Brix level was measured.

Fruit firmness

To measure the firmness of the fruit in kilonewtons (kN), a Lutron FG5020 firmness tester, Taiwan, was used. For this purpose, three fruits were randomly selected from each plant and placed under the probe of the device. A constant pressure was applied to the fruits to measure their firmness. The average firmness of the three fruits for each replication was then calculated.

Data analysis

SAS software Version 9.3 was used for data analysis (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA, http://www.sas.com). The assumptions of normality and homogeneity were tested by Shapiro-Wilk and Levene, respectively. All data were statistically analyzed using two-way ANOVA model. By observing significant treatment effects in the analysis of variance (ANOVA), significant mean differences (P < 0.05) were calculated using the multiple range Duncan test as a post hoc at α = 0.05. The graphs were drawn using excel (2016). Multivariate analyses, including heatmap visualization, and Pearson correlation analysis between the different traits were performed using chiplot (https://www.chiplot.online/) software.

Results

Plant growth

According to the analysis of variance table, the main effects of nutrient solution and cultivar, and the interaction between nutrient solution and cultivar was significant for shoot fresh mass (SFM), root fresh mass (RFM), shoot dry mass (SDM), root dry mass (RDM), and leaf area of bell pepper plants (Tables 2 and 3). Regarding to stem diameter, only the simple effect of cultivar and the main effect of interaction between nutrient solution and cultivar had a significant effect on this trait (Table 3). Among the cultivars, ‘Aranko’ showed the highest shoot fresh mass, under the USDA nutrient solution, which was significantly greater than all other treatments (P < 0.05), as it was 23.40% higher than Hoagland nutrient solution (Table 2). The ‘Nirvin’ cultivar achieved its maximum shoot fresh mass when grown with the Netherlands nutrient solution, while ‘Taranto’ had the highest SFM of plant under the USDA solution. The lowest SFM was observed in ‘Nirvin’ under the Hoagland solution. Regarding root fresh mass, both ‘Aranko’ and ‘Taranto’ reached their highest values under the USDA solution. ‘Nirvin’ had the lowest root fresh mass under the Hoagland solution, but this increased with the Netherlands solution. For shoot dry mass, ‘Aranko’ exhibited the greatest values under both USDA and Netherlands nutrient solutions. ‘Nirvin’ and ‘Taranto’ had lower shoot dry mass under the Hoagland solution, with ‘Nirvin’ showing the lowest value per plant. In terms of root dry mass, ‘Aranko’ under the USDA solution had the highest value, followed by ‘Nirvin’ under the Netherlands solution. The lowest root dry mass was recorded in ‘Nirvin’ with the Hoagland solution. The largest leaf area was observed in the ‘Aranko’ cultivar treated with the USDA nutrient solution, which was significantly higher than all other treatments (P < 0.05). Both ‘Taranto’ and ‘Nirvin’ showed similar leaf areas under USDA and Netherlands solutions, with no significant difference between these two nutrient solutions. The smallest leaf area was recorded in ‘Taranto’ under the Hoagland solution. Stem diameter varied significantly among cultivars and nutrient solutions. The highest stem diameter was recorded in ‘Aranko’ under the Hoagland solution, followed closely by ‘Aranko’ under USDA and Netherlands solutions. ‘Nirvin’ and ‘Taranto’ had smaller stem diameters overall (Table 3).

According to the analysis of variance table, the simple effect of the nutrient solution, and the main effect of interaction between nutrient solution and cultivar had a significant effect on number of fruit and fruit mass of bell pepper plants (Table 3). The highest number of fruit per plant was observed in ‘Aranko’ under the USDA solution, significantly exceeding other treatments (24.34% higher than Hoagland solution). ‘Taranto’ and ‘Nirvin’ produced fewer fruits. The lowest number of fruit was recorded in ‘Aranko’ under Hoagland solution. Regarding fruit mass, ‘Taranto’ and ‘Nirvin’ showed the highest values under the USDA solution. These values were significantly greater than those obtained with the Netherlands and Hoagland solutions. ‘Aranko’ had lower fruit mass overall, with the highest fruit mass under the Netherlands solution and the lowest under Hoagland solution (Table 3).

Overall, the USDA nutrient solution generally promoted better vegetative growth across cultivars, especially for ‘Aranko’. The Netherlands solution also supported good growth, particularly for ‘Nirvin’. The USDA nutrient solution favored higher fruit mass in ‘Taranto’ and ‘Nirvin’, while ‘Aranko’ showed superior number of fruit under USDA. The Hoagland solution was less effective, resulting in the lowest growth parameters for most cultivars. Furthermore, the Hoagland solution generally resulted in reduced number of fruit, and fruit mass across cultivars. These results indicate that nutrient solution type and cultivar significantly influence vegetative and reproductive growth in bell pepper, and selecting an appropriate nutrient solution can optimize biomass production in hydroponic systems.

Physical and biochemical characteristics of fruit

The interaction between nutrient solution and cultivar had a significant effect on L index, hue angle, chroma, fruit firmness, vitamin C, total soluble solids, and nitrate concentration of bell pepper plants (Tables 6 and 7). Regarding titratable acidity, both the main effects of nutrient solution and cultivar had a significant effect on this trait, while only the main effect of nutrient solution had a significant effect on lycopene concentration of fruits. The L index values were generally highest in ‘Taranto’ across all nutrient solutions, with no significant differences among treatments (P > 0.05) (Table 6). ‘Nirvin’ also showed similar L index values, while ‘Aranko’ had significantly lower L index values, indicating darker fruit color compared to the other cultivars. The highest hue angle was observed in ‘Nirvin’ under the USDA and Netherlands solutions, indicating a more yellowish color. ‘Aranko’ showed intermediate hue values, while ‘Taranto’ had the lowest hue values, suggesting a shift towards red hues. Significant differences were observed among nutrient solutions within cultivars (P < 0.05), with USDA generally producing higher hue values (Table 6). Chroma (color saturation) values varied significantly among cultivars and nutrient solutions (Table 6). ‘Aranko’ under the Netherlands solution exhibited the highest chroma, indicating more vivid color saturation, followed by USDA and Hoagland. ‘Taranto’ had lower chroma values, with the Netherlands solution producing the highest chroma within this cultivar. ‘Nirvin’ showed moderate chroma values. Fruit firmness was highest in ‘Aranko’ under the USDA nutrient solution, significantly greater than all other treatments (P < 0.05) (Table 6). Both ‘Taranto’ and ‘Nirvin’ exhibited intermediate firmness values under USDA. The lowest firmness values were recorded in all cultivars under the Netherlands and Hoagland solutions. Overall, the USDA nutrient solution enhanced fruit firmness across all cultivars, particularly in ‘Aranko’. Fruit color parameters varied by cultivar and nutrient solution, with ‘Aranko’ producing darker, more saturated fruits, and ‘Nirvin’ showing higher hue angles. ‘Taranto’ fruits tended to be lighter with lower chroma. These results indicate that nutrient solution composition influences fruit color characteristics and firmness in bell pepper cultivars, which are important quality attributes for consumer acceptance and marketability.

Discussion

The present study evaluated the response of three bell pepper cultivars (‘Nirvin’, ‘Aranko’, and ‘Taranto’) to different nutrient solutions (USDA, Netherlands, and Hoagland) in an NFT hydroponic system. The results demonstrated significant interactions between cultivar and nutrient solution, influencing vegetative growth, physiological performance, and fruit quality. The results of this study are consistent with existing literature indicating that nutrient solution composition and cultivar specificity significantly influence growth and yield in hydroponic bell pepper production. The USDA nutrient solution promoted superior vegetative growth and biomass accumulation, particularly in the ‘Aranko’ cultivar, aligning with research by Oliveira et al.41 who demonstrated that balanced nutrient formulations enhance growth parameters in bell peppers. This suggests that the USDA formulation may provide a more balanced nutrient profile, enhancing biomass accumulation. Similarly, ‘Nirvin’s optimal response to the Netherlands solution corroborates findings from Pérez-Vázquez et al.42, emphasizing the importance of tailoring nutrient solutions to cultivar-specific needs to maximize growth and yield. The Hoagland solution was the least effective, resulting in reduced shoot and root biomass across most cultivars. This aligns with previous studies showing that nutrient solution composition significantly impacts hydroponic pepper growth43.

The Fv/Fm is the ratio of variable fluorescence (Fv) to maximum fluore scence (Fm) in a dark-adapted state, reflecting the maximum potential efficiency of PSII if all reaction centers are open. It is a key indicator of the intrinsic photosynthetic efficiency and plant health. The PI integrates multiple parameters including the density of active PSII reaction centers, energy trapping efficiency, and electron transport rate, providing a comprehensive measure of overall photosynthetic vitality and efficiency, especially under stress conditions44. Interestingly, while the USDA solution enhanced vegetative growth, the Netherlands and Hoagland solutions improved photosynthetic performance, as indicated by higher Fv/Fm and PI values. This suggests that although USDA promoted structural growth, the Netherlands and Hoagland formulations may better support photochemical efficiency. The higher net photosynthesis (PN) and carboxylation efficiency in ‘Aranko’ and ‘Taranto’ under USDA could be attributed to improved gs and water use efficiency (WUEi). ‘Nirvin’, however, exhibited lower PN despite higher gs, possibly due to metabolic limitations or cultivar-specific traits45. Photosynthetic parameters, including Fv/Fm and performance index, were elevated in plants receiving the Netherlands and Hoagland solutions, supporting observations by Rajaseger et al.4 that nutrient solution composition impacts photosynthetic efficiency and plant vitality. The relatively lower photosynthetic performance under the USDA solution despite higher biomass suggests a trade-off between growth rate and photochemical efficiency, a phenomenon reported in hydroponic systems46.

Fruit production was highly dependent on both cultivar and nutrient solution. The USDA solution serves as an effective catalyst for high fruit set in ‘Aranko’ and high fruit weight in ‘Taranto’ and ‘Nirvin’, but it does not universally optimize all yield parameters across genotypes. These findings highlight the necessity of implementing cultivar-specific nutrient management protocols to maximize both quantitative and qualitative yield components, paving the way for more sustainable and profitable soilless cultivation systems13. fruit production in bell pepper is highly dependent on the specific interaction between cultivar genetics and nutrient solution.

Biochemical quality traits, such as vitamin C and lycopene content, were also influenced by nutrient solution. The USDA solution maximized vitamin C in ‘Nirvin’, whereas the Hoagland solution increased lycopene, suggesting that antioxidant accumulation is nutrient-dependent. The elevated nitrate levels in ‘Nirvin’ and ‘Taranto’ under Hoagland raise food safety concerns, as excessive nitrate accumulation is undesirable in edible crops47. Fruit quality variation among solutions and cultivars is in agreement with the work of Felisberto et al.48, who noted nutrient modulation of antioxidant compounds and fruit firmness. The higher vitamin C content under the USDA solution and increased lycopene concentration with Hoagland treatment mirror similar biochemical shifts documented by Castoldi et al.49. However, elevated nitrate accumulation associated with Hoagland corroborates concerns raised by Zhang et al.50 regarding food safety risks of excessive nutrient loads in hydroponic produce.

The observed differences in growth and fruit quality among the nutrient solutions are likely driven by variations in their macronutrient compositions. For example, the USDA solution includes a balanced ratio of nitrogen (both nitrate and ammonium forms), phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium, and micronutrients designed to optimize overall plant development and yield in two developmental stages (vegetetive and reproductive growth stages). In contrast, the Netherlands solution has a different ratio of nitrogen forms (higher ammonium to nitrate ratio) and nutrient concentrations tailored for their climate (lower light intensity) and cropping systems, influencing nutrient uptake efficiencies. The Hoagland solution, originally developed for general plant nutrition, varies primarily in nitrogen sources (without ammonium form N) and nutrient concentrations and is sometimes less effective for crops like bell peppers grown in specific environments51.

Moreover, environmental conditions such as temperature, light quality, and humidity at the experimental site in Iran differ markedly from those in the Netherlands, affecting nutrient absorption, root metabolism, and overall plant physiology. This climatic and environmental factors, compounded by system-specific conditions such as substrate composition and hydroponic management, necessitate site-specific nutrient adjustments to accommodate plant needs51. Hence, while the nutrient solutions have standardized formulations, plant performance may vary due to these factors, explaining observed growth differences. Therefore, a combination of solution composition differences, form and concentration of nutrients, and site-specific environmental variations collectively influenced bell pepper growth and fruit quality in this study’s locale compared to others. This highlights the need for regionally optimized nutrient management strategies to maximize the efficiency and productivity of hydroponic systems.

The use of three cultivars in this experiment was intended to capture the genetic diversity and differential responses of bell pepper genotypes to nutrient solutions, recognizing that cultivar-specific physiological and metabolic traits significantly influence nutrient uptake, growth, and fruit quality52. Differences among cultivars often arise from genetic variation affecting root architecture, photosynthetic efficiency, and nutrient stress tolerance, which in turn affect how each cultivar responds to nutrient availability in hydroponic systems53. Including multiple cultivars allowed the assessment of nutrient solutions across a spectrum of growth habits and biochemical profiles, which is vital for recommending cultivar-specific nutrient management practices. This approach helps in optimizing production by identifying the nutrient formulations that best support the varying nutrient demands and physiological capacities of each cultivar under specific environmental conditions. Therefore, the inclusion of three cultivars provided a comprehensive evaluation framework to enhance the applicability and relevance of the findings for diverse hydroponic pepper production systems.

The findings highlight that cultivar selection is crucial, ‘Aranko’ performed best under USDA for vegetative growth and number of fruit, while ‘Nirvin’ benefited more from the Netherlands solution. Nutrient solution optimization is key, USDA supported overall growth and yield, but Netherlands and Hoagland improved photosynthetic efficiency and certain quality traits. Trade-offs exist between growth and quality, While USDA enhanced yield, Netherlands and Hoagland improved some biochemical attributes, suggesting that growers should tailor solutions based on production goals (flavor vs. yield).

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that both cultivar and nutrient solution significantly influence bell pepper performance in NFT hydroponics. The ‘Aranko’ cultivar showed the best results in yield, with superior biomass accumulation and fruit mass, particularly under the USDA solution. The ‘Nirvin’ cultivar excelled in fruit quality parameters, exhibiting the highest greenness index and chlorophyll b content when grown with the Hoagland solution. Since cultivar evaluation studies benefit from broader genetic representation, it is strongly recommended to include more cultivars in future studies to provide a clearer and more comprehensive assessment.
