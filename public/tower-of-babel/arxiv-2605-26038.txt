DRScaffold: Boosting Dense-Scene Reasoning in Lightweight Vision Language Models
Xinrui Shi, Kai Liu, Ziqing Zhang, Jianze Li, Anqi Li, Yulun Zhang

Abstract
Lightweight vision-language models perform competitively on standard benchmarks yet fail systematically in dense-scene reasoning, where multiple objects, attributes, and relations must be jointly grounded and resolved through multi-step inference. Such capability is critical for real-world applications where models must reliably interpret cluttered environments. Yet existing training signals provide no explicit grounding between reasoning steps and the underlying visual entities and relations, leaving lightweight models free to generate fluent but visually unanchored reasoning chains. To address this gap, we first introduce DRBench , a benchmark of 14,573 questions across 2,943 images, organized into five task categories spanning three progressive reasoning layers. Building on DRBench, we propose DRScaffold , a supervised fine-tuning framework that decomposes the supervision target into four causally ordered stages, enforcing grounded reasoning without architectural modification. Experiments on three lightweight VLMs demonstrate substantial gains on DRBench while preserving or improving performance on general-purpose benchmarks. Notably, Qwen2.5-VL-3B trained with DRScaffold surpasses the frozen Qwen2.5-VL-32B on DRBench, demonstrating that structured supervision can substitute for a significant portion of model scale in dense-scene reasoning. Our code and models are available at https://github.com/irene-shi/DRScaffold .

Introduction

While large-scale Vision-Language Models (VLMs), such as GPT-4o
2
and Gemini 2.5 Pro
8
, have shown impressive capabilities, high computational cost and latency limit their use in real-world deployment. Practical applications ranging from domestic robots to autonomous vehicles require lightweight VLMs that operate under strict resource and latency constraints. This has motivated a growing line of work on mobile-scale VLMs, including state-of-the-art architectures, such as Qwen2.5-VL
3
and Phi4-multimodal
1
. Beyond general-purpose models, specialized systems have emerged, such as LINGO-2
34
and EM-VLM4AD
12
, which are designed for autonomous driving tasks. Further efficiency is achieved by MobileVLM-V2
7
, with only 1.1B parameters.

However, these advances share a common blind spot: dense-scene reasoning.
In such settings, multiple objects, attributes, and relations are tightly coupled, requiring multi-step reasoning over visual dependencies, which are ubiquitous in autonomous driving, robotic manipulation, and surveillance. As shown in Figure
1
, lightweight models fail on such queries, exhibiting
relation misinterpretation, attribute misbinding, and hallucinated dependencies
. Empirically, Figure
2
shows that the gap between lightweight and large VLMs is modest on standard benchmarks, but widens on dense-scene reasoning, indicating that standard benchmarks underestimate performance disparities in complex scenes. Given the strong reasoning ability of large VLMs, a practical solution is to leverage their outputs as supervision drafts to train light models.

Yet current strategies remain insufficient due to a fundamental mismatch between the multi-stage reasoning process that dense-scene understanding requires and the undifferentiated form of existing training signals. Outcome-based supervision trains on final answers alone, discarding all intermediate reasoning
22
;
21
. Chain-of-Thought supervision improves on this by distilling the teacher’s reasoning process, but treats it as a flat token sequence without structural constraints
11
;
26
. Neither approach enforces alignment between reasoning steps and the underlying visual evidence.

We argue that dense-scene reasoning requires four distinct stages that must be explicitly modeled:
(1) entity grounding
to identify and locate relevant objects,
(2) relation modeling
to resolve the spatial or logical dependencies among them,
(3) stepwise reasoning
to perform multi-step inference based on the structured relations, and
(4) answer generation
. This decomposition is not reflected in any existing training. Existing methods entangle reasoning stages into a monolithic sequence, which allows lightweight models to minimize training loss by mimicking fluent language patterns without verifying which visual entity each step refers to. This leads to reasoning that sounds plausible but is factually ungrounded, a failure mode that flat supervision cannot diagnose or prevent.

To bridge this gap, we propose
DRScaffold
, a supervised fine-tuning framework that enhances
D
ense-scene
R
easoning through “
Scaffold
ing”, enabling the model to learn complex structure layer by layer. First, we reformulate the supervision target as a schema-constrained pipeline with fixed slot order: detected entities, scene graph, step-wise reasoning, and final answer, so that the answer token is causally conditioned on previously grounded entities and verified relations. Second, training proceeds via staged gradient masking: each of the four training phases unlocks one additional field for loss computation, compelling the student to internalize each stage before proceeding to the next.

Training and evaluating such a framework requires supervision that existing benchmarks do not provide. We introduce

Beyond final correct answers, each sample contains structured reasoning process annotations, including key entities, scene graphs, and reasoning chains. These serve as supervision targets for DRScaffold. DRBench includes a
False Premise Rejection

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We introduce DRBench, a dense-scene reasoning benchmark with five task categories across three progressive reasoning layers, providing structured intermediate reasoning annotations alongside a False Premise Rejection category for stricter hallucination evaluation.

•

We propose DRScaffold, a fine-tuning framework that decomposes the supervision target into a causally ordered four-stage schema and applies staged gradient masking to enforce structured reasoning in lightweight VLMs, requiring no architectural modifications.

Related Work

:

2.1
Supervised Fine-Tuning

Leveraging large models to generate supervision signals for training smaller models has become a scalable paradigm for reasoning-intensive tasks
31
;
28
;
22
. Unlike traditional knowledge distillation that aligns output distributions or intermediate feature representations
15
;
30
;
27
, recent approaches use large models to produce rich offline supervision, including instruction-following data
31
;
28
, reasoning traces
17
;
11
;
16
, and preference data
32
.

Among these, Chain-of-Thought (CoT) distillation has emerged as a particularly promising direction for reasoning-intensive tasks. Rather than training on final answers alone
22
;
21
, the student model is trained to reproduce the teacher’s step-by-step reasoning process
17
;
26
. DDCoT
38
separates multimodal reasoning into visual recognition and language reasoning, assigning each component to specialized models and integrating them into a joint CoT rationale. LLaVA-CoT
36
constructs a structured four-stage reasoning pipeline: summarization, visual interpretation, logical reasoning, and conclusion generation, demonstrating that structured stage tags significantly improve over untagged flat CoT.
However, both DDCoT and LLaVA-CoT treat reasoning as a sequence of language stages without grounding each step to specific visual entities or relations. In dense-scene settings, this is where models fail: a reasoning step that mentions “the object to the left” carries no guarantee that the model has correctly identified which object that refers to.
DRScaffold addresses this by explicitly conditioning each reasoning stage on a structured scene graph derived from the image.

2.2
Benchmarks for Visual Reasoning

Early visual question answering benchmarks such as VQAv2
13
and GQA
18
establish quantitative foundations for evaluating visual perception and reasoning, but have become insufficient as VLM capabilities rapidly advance. More recent work addresses this by expanding both scale and task diversity. MMMU
37
tests expert-level understanding across 30 disciplines, MMBench
23
systematically decomposes capability into a three-level taxonomy covering 20 ability dimensions and introduces the CircularEval strategy, and MMStar
5
filters for samples that genuinely require visual evidence to reduce language-only leakage. Specialized benchmarks, such as RealWorldQA
35
, MathVista
24
, and ScienceQA
25
, further target specific domains including spatial reasoning and scientific problem solving. DRBench addresses this gap by providing structured intermediate annotations alongside each sample, enabling evaluation at every stage of the reasoning pipeline.

Despite this progress, two gaps remain. First, every sample provides only a final answer label, treating the reasoning process as a black box and making it impossible to diagnose where a model’s reasoning breaks down. Second, no existing benchmark is specifically designed for dense-scene reasoning, where multiple objects, attributes, and relations must be jointly grounded and resolved.

2.3
Hallucination and Robustness Evaluation

A complementary line of work focuses on hallucination in VLMs. Benchmarks such as POPE
19
, AMBER
33
, HallusionBench
14
, and CDH-Bench
4
evaluate whether models hallucinate objects or attributes that are not present in the image, typically through direct binary existence queries.
However, these benchmarks mainly probe object-level hallucination in isolation. A model that correctly answers “No” to a direct existence query may still confabulate attributes or relations when the same absent entity is embedded within a more complex query. This represents a strictly harder failure mode that cannot be captured by binary polling alone. In DRBench, the False Premise Rejection category is specifically designed to test this.

3
DRBench

DRBench is built on a single principle: every sample must force a model to simultaneously ground multiple entities, resolve their relational dependencies, and derive an answer through a multi-step inference chain. Three properties differentiate DRBench from existing benchmarks. (1) Images are chosen for dense scenes that stress the three failure modes of relation misinterpretation, attribute misbinding, and hallucinated dependencies. (2) Every question is verified to require visual evidence. (3) Beyond the final answer, each sample carries structured annotations: key entities, a scene graph, and a staged reasoning chain, as shown in Figure
3
. DRBench comprises 14,573 questions across 2,943 images, organized into five categories spanning three progressive reasoning layers.

3.1
Image Sources and Scene Selection

We source images from two complementary datasets that together span static indoor and dynamic outdoor dense-scene distributions, preventing models from exploiting a single-scene prior. From Hypersim
29
, we select 440 photorealistic synthetic indoor scenes covering diverse room types, such as living rooms, kitchens, and offices, where objects appear in tight spatial proximity with ground-truth geometry available for answer verification. From Cityscapes
9
, we select ego-centric urban driving images from 27 out of the 50 German cities recorded under varying lighting and weather conditions, featuring densely annotated street scenes with pedestrians, vehicles, cyclists, and infrastructure. Across the full dataset, we annotate 5,674 entities and 2,444 unique relation types, reflecting the compositional complexity of dense scenes, as shown in Figure
4
.

3.2
Question Design and Taxonomy

Figure 5
:
Question taxonomy and category distribution in DRBench. Each category is illustrated with a representative sample question.

DRBench organizes its questions into five categories across three progressive layers. The layered design is motivated by a hierarchical decomposition of the cognitive demands required for embodied scene understanding: Layer 1 isolates structural grounding as the prerequisite for all higher-order reasoning; Layer 2 builds on this foundation by requiring models to apply grounded scene understanding toward deployment-relevant judgments; Layer 3 introduces an orthogonal challenge of logical rigor in the face of visually absent referents. Layer 1 and Layer 2 both concern entities that are present in the scene, progressing from structural perception to contextual application, while Layer 3 concerns entities that are entirely absent, testing whether a model resists fabricating what it cannot see. Figure
5
illustrates the distribution of question categories and representative examples.

Layer 1: Structural Grounding.

(1) Perception

questions anchor the target through multi-hop referring expressions, requiring the model to navigate a spatial chain to identify an entity’s attributes such as color, material, count, and state of motion.

(2) Spatial Reasoning

questions require the model to discover spatial relations from the scene. Some questions further require applying these relations as preconditions for physical prediction.

Layer 2: Contextual Application.

(3) Affordance Reasoning
questions probe whether a model can reason about the use and context of objects, rather than their properties alone.

(4) Anomaly Detection

questions ask whether the scene contains unsafe placement or anomalous conditions. Both categories presuppose successful structural grounding.

Layer 3: Logical Rigor.

(5) False Premise Rejection
questions embed a logically plausible but visually absent referent inside an attribute or relation query, rather than asking about its existence directly, so the model must first search the scene, explicitly verify that the referent is absent, and then reject the premise. This design makes hallucination strictly harder to avoid than in binary-polling benchmarks, as a model that correctly answers “No” to a direct existence query may still confabulate when the same absent entity is presupposed.

3.3
Quality Control

Figure 6
:
Construction and quality-control pipeline of DRBench. The pipeline includes two quality-control phases. Each check is illustrated with a representative failure case.

DRBench employs a multi-stage quality control pipeline combining automated filtering, LLM-assisted annotation and human verification. The full pipeline is illustrated in Figure
6
.

Visual Dependency Filtering.

A fundamental failure mode in multimodal benchmarks is that many questions can be answered correctly from language statistics alone, without consulting the image
5
. To eliminate such samples, we employ two text-only LLMs, DeepSeek
20
and Qwen-Max, to answer each question without access to the image. Any question that both models answer correctly is removed from the pool, yielding a retention rate of 80.2%.

Scene-Matching Filtering.

Dense scenes introduce source-specific failure modes that visual dependency filtering alone cannot catch. For Hypersim, a three-stage review is applied: Stage A uses Qwen-VL-Max to generate a scene summary and flag camera tilt; Stage B uses DeepSeek to identify questions implausible given the scene context; Stage C performs re-verification supported by annotations in Hypersim’s ground-truth geometry annotations, with pass, minor revision, or deletion as possible outcomes. For Cityscapes, Stages B and C are adapted to flag questions and cases where the target object is too small, ambiguous in color, or spatially unclear to support a reliable answer.

Annotation Generation and Consistency Verification.

Each sample’s structured annotations are generated in two phases. We first collect 300 human-annotated samples as exemplars. For the remaining questions, Qwen-VL-Max generates annotations conditioned on the image, question, options, and ground-truth answer. To facilitate consistency verification, the reasoning chain is structured into four ordered stages: Perception, Relation, Logic, and Conclusion.
DeepSeek then checks (1) mutual consistency between
the scene graph and key entities, and (2) that the reasoning chain’s conclusion entails the ground-truth answer. Failures trigger a feedback-revision loop back to Qwen-VL-Max, iterated up to 5 rounds, after which unresolved samples are discarded. A final human audit inspects the resulting annotations for correctness before inclusion.

4
DRScaffold

Figure 7
:
Overview of the DRScaffold schema design. The supervision target is decomposed into four ordered stages. Each stage is conditioned on different elements from previous stages.

DRScaffold transfers dense-scene reasoning to a lightweight student model via supervision over a fixed-order schema, requiring no architectural modifications. As illustrated in Figure
7
, it decomposes the supervision target into four causally ordered stages:
(1) Entity Grounding
,
(2) Relation Modeling
,
(3) Stepwise Reasoning
, and
(4) Answer Generation
.
The ordering of the four fields enforces a grounded reasoning pipeline: entities define the objects over which relations are formed, relations provide the scene graph used by subsequent reasoning, and the final answer is generated only after the intermediate evidence has been made explicit. Because the student decodes auto-regressively, denoting the four structured output fields as
F_{1},F_{2},F_{3},F_{4}
, the joint output distribution factorizes as:

p_{\theta}(y\mid\mathbf{x})=\prod_{k=1}^{4}p_{\theta}\!\left(F_{k}\mid F_{1},\ldots,F_{k-1},\mathbf{x}\right),

(1)

so that the answer token distribution
p_{\theta}(F_{4}\mid F_{1},F_{2},F_{3},\mathbf{x})
is explicitly conditioned on entities, relations, and reasoning, preventing the student from generating visually ungrounded reasoning.

Training Objective.

Let
\mathcal{F}=\{F_{1},F_{2},F_{3},F_{4}\}
denote the four structured
fields: key entities, scene graph, reasoning chain, and answer.
At phase
k
, we apply a binary gradient mask that includes only tokens
belonging to fields
F_{1},\ldots,F_{k}
, and the training objective is:

\mathcal{L}_{k}=-\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{\leq k}}\log p_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid y_{<t},\mathbf{x}),

(2)

where
\mathbf{x}
is the image-question input and
\mathcal{T}_{\leq k}

is the set of token positions belonging to fields
F_{1},\ldots,F_{k}
.
Tokens outside
\mathcal{T}_{\leq k}
are masked from gradient computation,
so the student cannot shortcut to the answer before correctly grounding earlier fields.
Training proceeds in four phases, each unlocking one additional field.
Phase 1 supervises
F_{1}
only, Phase 2 adds
F_{2}
, and so on through full supervision at Phase 4. All phases use identical training data, requiring no additional annotation.

Mixed Fine-Tuning.

To prevent catastrophic forgetting, Phase 4 mixes DRBench training samples with random 15,000 samples from LLaVA-Instruct-150K
21
, at a 1:1 ratio. LLaVA-Instruct-150K is constructed from COCO images and covers conversation, detailed description, and complex reasoning, a distribution sufficiently distinct from DRBench’s dense-scene reasoning tasks to serve as an effective replay buffer without interfering with domain-specific learning.

Quantitative Results.

Table
1
shows that DRScaffold yields substantial gains on DRBench across all three models, while preserving general-purpose benchmark performance. Most strikingly, Qwen2.5-VL-3B trained with DRScaffold surpasses the frozen Qwen2.5-VL-32B on DRBench, demonstrating that DRScaffold can substitute for a significant portion of model scale in dense-scene reasoning. Improvements on HallusionBench further indicate improved hallucination robustness via causal grounding and false premise rejection. The comparatively modest gain for Phi4-multimodal on DRBench is consistent with its stronger pre-trained baseline, indicating diminishing returns where the base model already handles dense-scene reasoning. Notably, Phi4-multimodal trained with DRScaffold achieves the highest average score, outperforming the frozen Qwen2.5-VL-32B.

Qualitative Results.

We provide visual comparisons in Figure
8
. Across all examples, different baseline models consistently fabricate or misidentify visual evidence in dense scenes, while the models enhanced by DRScaffold produce grounded and accurate responses. In both cases, DRScaffold eliminates the spurious predictions by anchoring each reasoning step to verified visual entities.

Comparison with Baselines.

Table
2
compares DRScaffold against three supervision strategies sharing the same base model, Qwen2.5-VL-3B, and training data.
All three baselines degrade performance on general-purpose benchmarks, indicating that naive supervision strategies improve dense-scene reasoning at the cost of general capability. DRScaffold is the only method that achieves the highest DRBench score while fully preserving general benchmark performance. CoT Supervision and LLaVA-CoT reach comparable DRBench scores to each other, suggesting that chain-of-thought reasoning alone, without causally grounded structure, hits a ceiling in dense-scene reasoning.

We also analyze the relationship between average output length and accuracy across methods, shown in Figure

On DRBench, DRScaffold produces the most concise outputs among all trained models while achieving the highest accuracy, ruling out length as a confounding factor.

5.3
Ablation Studies

Effect of Each Training Stage.

To diagnose how each stage contributes, we track two intermediate metrics alongside accuracy:
Scene Graph F1
, computed over relation triplets
\langle h,r,t\rangle
by soft-matching head, relation, and tail jointly, and
Reasoning Validity
, which measures whether the predicted reasoning chain entails the ground-truth answer as judged by DeepSeek.
As shown in Figure
10
, overall accuracy rises monotonically across stages and surpasses the frozen Qwen2.5-VL-32B after Stage 3, with False Premise and Anomaly categories benefiting most from early stages and Spatial Reasoning starting to improve after Stage 1. The largest jump in Scene Graph F1 occurs at Stage 2, and Reasoning Validity peaks at Stage 3. This demonstrates that the staged gradient masking progressively builds capabilities.
Figure
11
illustrates how each training stage refines model behavior. The base model fully hallucinates. After Stage 1, the model becomes correct when forced to produce chain of thought, yet still outputs the wrong final answer directly, revealing a disconnect between implicit perception and answer generation that entity grounding alone cannot bridge. Stage 2 resolves this by introducing scene graph supervision, after which the model consistently produces both correct reasoning and correct answers, confirming that relational structure helps bridge the gap.

Stage Removal and Stage Reordering.

Table
3
validates the necessity of both the complete stage set and the causal ordering, where all variants are trained for the same number of epochs. When stages are removed, we redistribute their training epochs proportionally across the remaining stages. “Random order” reports the average score over three random permutations, excluding the full causal order. Removing any subset of stages consistently degrades performance. Reversing the order or applying a random ordering degrades performance further, demonstrating that grounding coarser visual entities before finer relational inference is a necessary inductive bias for lightweight models.

6
Limitations and Discussions

DRBench currently covers indoor and urban driving scenes, but may not represent the full diversity of dense-scene environments encountered in deployment. While DRScaffold demonstrates consistent gains across three VLM architectures, the comparatively modest improvement on Phi4-multimodal suggests that models with stronger pretrained dense-scene priors benefit less, and the interaction between base model capacity and scaffold design warrants further study. Additionally, the staged training procedure increases total training time proportionally to the number of stages, which may limit applicability in resource-constrained settings. We expect that exploring adaptive training strategies for dynamic adaptation to different scenarios could further improve generalization.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we identify dense-scene reasoning as a critical yet underexplored bottleneck for lightweight VLMs. We introduce DRBench, a benchmark that provides structured intermediate annotations and a False Premise Rejection category that probes hallucination robustness. We further propose DRScaffold, a supervised fine-tuning framework that enforces grounded reasoning without architectural modification. Experiments demonstrate substantial gains on DRBench while preserving or improving performance on general-purpose benchmarks. Furthermore, structured supervision can substitute for a significant portion of model scale in dense-scene reasoning. Future work will focus on further diagnosing and improving complex reasoning in resource-constrained deployments.

A.1
Source Images

Our dataset is built on two complementary scene sources spanning both indoor and outdoor environments.

HyperSim.

HyperSim is a photorealistic synthetic indoor scene dataset rendered from artist-created 3D environments. Each scene comes with dense per-pixel annotations including semantic labels, instance masks, depth maps, and surface normals. We select high-quality preview frames and use the instance-level annotations to cross-validate model-generated structured labels in later pipeline stages. Figure

A.2
Step 1: Question Generation

For each image, Qwen-VL-Max is prompted to generate 15 for HyperSim or 8–10 for Cityscapes question–answer pairs. At this stage only the question text, answer, and answer options (for MCQ) are produced; structured fields (key objects, scene graph, reasoning chain) are generated later in Step 4 after quality filtering is complete.

Five reasoning categories.

Every question must belong to exactly one of:

•

Perception
: attribute identification anchored by multi-hop referring expressions.

•

Spatial Reasoning
: spatial layout and simple physics.

•

Affordance Reasoning
: object use within scene context.

•

Anomaly Detection
: unusual or unsafe placements grounded in visible evidence.

•

False Premise Rejection
: asks about an absent-but-plausible object; correct answer rejects the premise.

Key design constraints.

(a) Anchor only on large, unambiguous objects with sufficient pixel coverage.
(b) Answers uniquely supported by visible evidence, with no plausible alternatives.
(c) Multi-hop spatial chains in the question text.
(d) Minimum complexity:
\geq
3 task-relevant objects,
\geq
3 scene-graph relations.

Prompt 1: Question Generation (HyperSim indoor scenes)

Role:
You are an expert multimodal reasoning annotator for indoor scenes.
Generate exactly
15
high-quality reasoning questions about the given image.

Grounding & Answer Certainty (override all other rules):

1. Do NOT ask about objects that are too small, heavily occluded, or in extreme shadow.

2. Answers must be uniquely supportable by unambiguous visible evidence.

3. Prefer large, salient anchors (furniture, fixtures occupying enough pixels).

Question Design (5 Categories; at least 2 per category)

1. Perception -- e.g. ‘‘Which objects near the sink are blue?’’; format: multiple-choice or multi-select.

2. Spatial Reasoning -- e.g. ‘‘What is the direction of the plant relative to the stack of books?’’; must involve spatial relation, may involve simple physics.

3. Affordance Reasoning -- e.g. ‘‘Where is the best place to put a towel for easy access after washing hands?’’; must rely on object relationships.

4. Anomaly Detection -- e.g. ‘‘Is there any potentially unsafe or unreasonable object placement around the bathtub?’’; open-ended but MUST be grounded in visible evidence.

5. False Premise Rejection -- inquire about an object or spatial relationship completely absent from the image as if it were present; prefer objects not visible but logically plausible in the scene (e.g. accessories on a visible object); use referring expressions tied to what is visible so the model must search the image before concluding the part/object is absent. Example: only a car with an ordinary roof and
no
roof rack; ask ‘‘What color is the luggage rack on the roof of the car?’’ (no rack; answer must reject the premise from evidence). Do NOT use hypothetical language (‘‘If there were…’’, ‘‘Is there…’’). The reasoning chain and answer MUST explicitly state that the object or scene does not exist from visual evidence, thus rejecting the question’s premise.

Multi-hop spatial chains (preferred when it’s semantically smooth):

Bad: ‘‘What color is the lamp?’’

Better: ‘‘What color is the object on the floor to the left of the TV stand and in front of the window?’’

Difficulty constraints:

- Never ask questions answerable by common sense without reading the image.

- Avoid vague attributes; avoid precise metric estimation.

- MCQ distractors must be ruled out by visual evidence.

- At least 4 out of 15 questions must be open-ended.

Output format (JSON, question + answer only):

{"questions": [{"id","type","category","question","options","answer"}, ...]}

For Cityscapes, the structure is the same as Prompt 1 with some domain-specific adaptations.

A.3
Step 2: Text-Blind Answer Verification

A question that can be answered correctly without the image provides no visual reasoning signal. We apply a conservative dual-model text-blind check: both qwen-vl-max and deepseek-v3 independently attempt to answer each question without seeing the image (text-only mode). A question is flagged as bad only when both models produce the correct answer. The correctness is judged by a third deepseek-v3 model. This conservative criterion avoids false positives from lucky guesses and retains visually ambiguous questions for downstream visual review.

Prompt 2a: Text-Blind Answering

You are given a visual reasoning question but you CANNOT see the image.
Answer using only the question text and options (if any). Common-sense or linguistic cues may mislead; still pick the most likely option or give a brief text answer.

You MUST output JSON only with a single field "answer":

•

single_choice: exactly one of "A", "B", "C", "D".

•

multi_select: comma-separated letters, e.g. "A,C" or "B,D" (no spaces, letters sorted ascending).

•

open_ended: a short, precise English phrase or sentence.

Do not output markdown fences or any text outside the JSON object.

Prompt 2b: Judge Correctness

You judge whether two answers to the same visual QA question are semantically equivalent.
The model answered WITHOUT seeing the image.

Question:
question

Reference answer (ground truth, from annotators with image):
{ref_answer}

Model answer (text-only, no image):
{cand_answer}

Output JSON only: "similar": true if they mean the same or nearly the same; "similar": false otherwise.
Do not output anything outside JSON.

A.4
Step 3: Scene-Matching Filtering

Dense scenes introduce source-specific failure modes that visual-dependency filtering alone cannot catch. We apply a three-stage review pipeline:

Stage A (Qwen-VL-Max, vision; HyperSim only).

The model generates a concise scene/room-type summary and flags camera tilt. The summary is passed to Stage B as context. Cityscapes skips Stage A: all images are outdoor urban street scenes.

Stage B (DeepSeek-V3, text-only).

Given only the Stage A summary and the question/options/answer (no image), the text model identifies questions implausible given the scene context, e.g. a question presupposing a dishwasher in a bathroom or requiring precise floor-slope inference. Flagged questions are deleted.

Stage C (Qwen-VL-Max, vision).

Surviving questions are re-verified against the image. The model decides pass (keep unchanged), pass with minor revision (rewrite question/options/answer), or delete. For Cityscapes, Stage C also flags questions whose target object is too small, spatially ambiguous (e.g. “the car on the right” when several exist), or whose colour/identity is unclear due to lighting, shadow, or compression artefacts. False Premise Rejection questions do not go through this check.

A.5
Step 4: Structured Field Generation and Consistency Verification

After Steps 2–3 some questions have their answer corrected or question text revised. For every accepted question, whether modified or unchanged, we now generate the four structured supervision fields: key_objects, scene_graph, reasoning_chain using qwen-vl-max conditioned on the image and the verified question-answer pair. A text-only consistency verifier, deepseek-v3, then checks for internal contradictions; if it returns pass: false, its feedback is appended to the generation prompt and qwen-vl-max regenerates. This loop repeats for up to 5 rounds.

Prompt 4a: Structured Field Generation (Qwen-VL-Max)

You fill structured supervision fields for ONE QA item. The image is your perceptual ground.

Highest priority -- Answer Consistency:

The answer field is the ONLY ground truth.

key_objects, scene_graph, and reasoning_chain MUST all support EXACTLY that answer.

Do NOT describe distractor options as valid; only mention them if needed for elimination logic.

Output (strict JSON, no markdown, no extra keys):

{"key_objects": [...], "scene_graph": ["A --pred--> B", ...],

"reasoning_chain": ["[Perception]: ...", "[Relation]: ...", "[Logic]: ...", "[Conclusion]: ..."]}

Rules:

reasoning_chain: EXACTLY 4 strings in order [Perception] [Relation] [Logic] [Conclusion]. [Conclusion] must explicitly state the same ground-truth answer (same letter(s) or phrase).

scene_graph predicates: left_of, right_of, on, under, near, behind, in_front_of, attached_to, etc.

key_objects: task-relevant only; prefer minimal set needed to derive the answer. Better include all entities mentioned in scene_graph.

Prompt 4b: Consistency Verification (DeepSeek-V3, text-only)

You are a strict logic reviewer for multimodal QA labels. You do NOT see the image.
Check whether the proposed structured fields are logically consistent with each other
AND with the ground-truth answer. The given answer is always correct.

Flag: scene_graph vs key_entity contradiction; [Conclusion] vs answer mismatch;
distractor options treated as correct when answer excludes them.

Output STRICT JSON only:

{"pass": true}

or

{"pass": false, "feedback": "what is wrong", "hint": "how fields should change"}

C.1
Model Architecture and Base Models

We apply DRScaffold to three representative lightweight VLMs spanning different parameter counts and architecture families:

•

Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct
– 3B-parameter model with a native dynamic-resolution ViT; instruction-tuned baseline.

•

InternVL2.5-2B
– 2B-parameter InternVL2.5 family model; instruction-tuned baseline.

•

Phi4-multimodal
– Microsoft Phi-4 multimodal variant; strong general-purpose baseline.

In all experiments, only the
LLM decoder
(transformer layers) is fine-tuned via LoRA; the ViT encoder, aligner/connector, and token embeddings are frozen throughout training.

C.2
DRScaffold Training Phases

Training follows four phases P1, P2, P3, and P4 that progressively introduce more of the structured CoT supervision and then mixes in general instruction-following data to prevent catastrophic forgetting.

P1 – Entity Grounding.

Only the key_objects field of the CoT JSON is supervised; all subsequent fields are masked to weight 0.0. This phase bootstraps the LLM’s ability to ground visual entities.
Epochs:
0.5\times
; LR:
5e-5
; schedule: cosine with 10% warmup.

P2 – Relation Modeling.

Supervision extends to key_objects
+
scene_graph; reasoning_chain and answer remain masked. The model learns to produce structured spatial-relation triples.
Epochs:
1.0\times
; LR:
4e-5
; schedule: constant with 20-step warmup.

P3 – Stepwise Reasoning.

Full sequence supervision excluding answer. In this phase the model learns to complete the multi-step reasoning chain before committing to an answer.
Epochs:
1.0\times
; LR:
3e-5
; schedule: constant with 30-step warmup.

P4 – Anti-Forgetting Mix.

Full sequence supervision. The LLaVA portion is increased to
\sim
15,000 samples to more aggressively recover general benchmark performance. A very low learning rate is used to make only small corrections without undoing the CoT capabilities learned in P1–P3.
Epochs:
1.0\times
; LR:
3e-6
; schedule: cosine with 5% warmup.
