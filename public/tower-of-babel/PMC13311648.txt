Guided self-determination intervention versus an attention control group of people with type 2 diabetes in an outpatient clinic setting—the OVERCOME randomised clinical trial
Authors: Anne Sophie Mathiesen, Vibeke Zoffmann, Jane Lindschou, Janus Christian Jakobsen, Christian Gluud, Markus Harboe Olsen, Bodil Rasmussen, Emilie Haarslev Schröeder Marqvorsen, Mette Juel Rothmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAutonomy supportive interventions such as the guided self-determination method (GSD), may reduce diabetes distress in people with type 2 diabetes. The objective was to investigate the benefits and harms of GSD intervention versus attention control group intervention in adults (≥18 years old) with type 2 diabetes.Research designs and methodsThe design was a pragmatic, dual-centre, randomised, assessor-blinded, superiority clinical trial. People with type 2 diabetes were assigned by computer-generated randomisation to GSD or attention control, both interventions provided face-to-face, over telephone, or via video. The primary outcome was diabetes distress assessed with the Problem Areas in Diabetes questionnaire at 12 month follow-up. The secondary outcomes were depressive symptoms assessed with the Hospital Anxiety and Depression scale; quality of life assessed with the 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36); and non-serious adverse events assessed with the Negative Effects Questionnaire at 12 month follow-up. Statisticians were blinded to group allocation.ResultsThe trial was stopped early after a total of 150 of 224 planned participants were randomised (GSD n=74; attention control n=76). Linear regression showed beneficial effects of GSD on diabetes distress (mean difference (MD) −5.83; 98.75% CI −11.26 to −0.41) and depression (MD −2.77; 98.75% CI −5.24 to −0.30) but below our predefined minimal important differences. Quality of life (mental component) improved (MD 8.10; 98.75% CI 0.94 to 15.27), exceeding our predefined minimal important difference. We found no differences on the physical component of quality of life (MD 1.79; 98.75% CI −5.72 to 9.31), adverse events (MD 0.00; 98.75% Hodges-Lehmann CI 0.00 to 0.00) or the tertiary outcomes: HbA1c or motivation.ConclusionsThe GSD intervention versus attention control reduced diabetes distress and depression and improved quality of life (mental component). The effect sizes were below the predefined minimal important difference, except for quality of life (mental component) and affected by under-recruitment.Trial registration numberNCT04601311.Keywords: DIABETES & ENDOCRINOLOGY, Behavior, Clinical Trial, Person-Centered Care

Introduction

In Denmark, people with type 2 diabetes attending outpatient clinics have diabetes-related complications and often multiple comorbidities.1 In addition to these complications, they also have a higher prevalence of adverse psychosocial outcomes such as diabetes distress and depressive symptoms.2 3 Diabetes distress has been defined as the negative emotions of living with diabetes and affects up to 40% of individuals living with diabetes.4 A higher level of diabetes distress is associated with suboptimal diabetes self-management including inadequate medication taking,5 elevated HbA1c and impaired general emotional well-being that may fluctuate over time.6 Diabetes distress is important to address and treat as, if untreated, it may worsen into depression and deteriorate clinical outcomes.7

Evidence suggests that it is easier to achieve and maintain adequate diabetes self-management if the individual is autonomously motivated, that is, if they strive for goals they genuinely believe in.8 Thus, autonomy-supporting interventions and intrinsic motivation may be crucial factors in achieving real-life individual engagement and more long-term improvement through shared decision-making and collaborative goal setting.9 The guided self-determination (GSD) intervention is an empowerment-based method designed to promote autonomy and intrinsic motivation in people with diabetes. GSD may reduce diabetes distress10 and improve clinical outcomes such as HbA1c.10 11

Therefore, interventions supporting autonomy and intrinsic motivation might be vital in achieving long-term improvement and real-life individual engagement. The GSD intervention is an empowerment-based autonomy-supportive method designed to promote intrinsic motivation accomplished through goal setting and shared decision-making in collaboration between the person with diabetes and healthcare professionals. Subsequently, they may improve clinical outcomes10 11 through the following pathways:12,14 increased perceived autonomy support from the healthcare professionals, increased perceived competence in managing diabetes, reduced diabetes-related distress and ultimately improved glycaemic control.10 11

A systematic review and meta-analysis was previously conducted in order to investigate the evidence of psychosocial interventions on diabetes-related distress, HbA1c, depression and health-related quality of life (QOL) in individuals living with type 2 diabetes.15 We found small effects of psychosocial interventions on diabetes distress and depressive symptoms, but psychosocial interventions may not target specifically the autonomy-supportive constructs argued to enable the mechanisms behind a potential effect of GSD.11 Prior to initiating the OVERCOME trial, we conducted a new systematic review investigating specifically the effect of GSD or self-determination theory-based interventions versus standard care in people with diabetes.16 A total of 11 randomised clinical trials were identified, all of which were found to be at high risk of bias. The certainty of the evidence was consequently deemed to be low, which may indicate that previous trials potentially overestimated the beneficial effects of GSD.17 In addition, the included trials did not report any occurrence of adverse events or potential negative effects18 or applied an attention control group as a comparison. Which may also lead to overestimation of a potential effect of GSD interventions.19 Overall, we found that the existing evidence of an effect of GSD on diabetes distress in people with type 2 diabetes was deemed to be ambiguous. Furthermore, the prevailing evidence also appeared to be susceptible to both random and systematic errors.16 Thus, the launch of the OVERCOME trial was ethically justified.

Research design and methods

The trial was a pragmatic, investigator-initiated, dual-centre, randomised, parallel-group, assessor-blinded, superiority clinical trial of GSD intervention versus an attention control group in people living with type 2 diabetes. The trial was conducted according to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials extension for reporting non-pharmacological interventions 2010 (CONSORT)28 and was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04601311, 23rd October 2020) prior to including the first participant in November 2020. We published our design and protocol article on the OVERCOME trial in December 2021.29 Our detailed statistical analysis plan was published in November 2024 prior to publishing the present paper.30 online supplemental material 1:full protocol Participants were recruited from the Department of Endocrinology, the University Hospital of Copenhagen—Rigshospitalet, the Capital Region of Denmark and at Steno Diabetes Centre Odense, Odense University Hospital, the Southern Region of Denmark. The initial starting date was postponed due to the first wave of COVID-19 from June to November 2020. The last date of follow-up for the last participant was December 2023.

Patient and public involvement

The development of the GSD method is based on a comprehensive involvement of people with diabetes.13 14 Potential participants were also involved in revising the documents in the trial, for example, the participant information. The results of this trial are planned to be presented to a broad audience, including the participants and their relatives, as well as associations working with diabetes in Denmark and internationally. Nested in this trial, we conducted a qualitative study (n=10) to elaborate on the potential mechanisms of action concerning diabetes distress in participants that received the GSD intervention.31

Procedures

Initially, the potentially eligible participants were assessed against the inclusion and exclusion criteria during a routine visit at the outpatient clinics. A trial-specific information brochure was handed out and was followed up by oral information face-to-face or over the phone. Potential participants were given a minimum of 24 hours of consideration time prior to deciding on participation and the opportunity of bringing a third party (eg, a friend or relative). Consenting participants were booked for a baseline visit where informed consent was signed prior to conducting any trial-related tasks. The delivery and fidelity of the GSD intervention were supervised by the first (ASM), the second (VZ) and the last (MJR) author according to the fidelity tool developed for the GSD method.32

Trial interventions

The GSD intervention

The GSD is a theory-based problem-solving method to overcome barriers related to diabetes management and collaborative care.13 The method strives to strengthen autonomous motivation, empowerment and self-determination by focusing on life-illness integration, the relational potential for change and shared decision-making.14 These three essential areas are embedded in the reflection sheets for the participants to work with at home or online. Experienced nurses were certified in the method during the 32-hours GSD course, including two people with type 2 diabetes who volunteered to participate in sessions during the certification. The GSD course also included advanced communication techniques such as mirroring, active listening and value-clarifying questions to promote autonomous reflection. The certified nurses provided two to five individual need-based sessions every second week after randomisation. The sessions lasted approximately 1 hour and were conducted face-to-face, via video or over the telephone. The participant had the choice of an analogue GSD (in paper), a digital or a mixed version provided as a stepped-care intervention. The GSD intervention required participants to complete up to 13 reflection sheets in a predefined order as preparation for the individual sessions.29 For further information, we refer to our protocol.29

Control group intervention

The participants in the control group received two to five needs-based sessions with a psychology student or a communication-trained medical student, who were both certified counsellors. The needs-based sessions were based on a written sentence or problem area that the participant wished to work with in the sessions. The needs-defined two to five individual sessions were scheduled concurrently with the sessions in the experimental group every second week after randomisation. The sessions lasted up to 1 hour and were conducted face-to-face, via video or over the telephone.29

Randomisation and blinding

The Copenhagen Trial Unit, which is a Danish centre for clinical intervention research (htpps://ctu.dk), conducted the central randomisation. Participants were randomly assigned (1:1) to GSD versus an attention control group using a computer-generated allocation sequence with permuted blocks of various sizes. Randomisation was stratified by site and sex. The study’s steering committee, outcome assessors and statisticians were all unaware of the treatment allocation. The participants and intervention providers were not blinded. This was due to the difficulties of efficiently blinding participants in trials assessing psychological interventions.33 Blinding was broken on 19 September 2024 after the statistical report was finalised and the steering committee had agreed on the two possible abstracts.29

Statistical analyses

This randomised clinical trial was designed to evaluate superiority when comparing the intervention group versus the attention control group. The sample size calculation was based on our primary outcome and an estimated minimal clinically important difference from the mean of 6 points and SDs of 16 points from the systematic review conducted prior to initiating the OVERCOME trial.16 With a power of 80% (a beta at 20%) and a two-tailed α at 5%, a sample size of 112 participants was needed in each intervention group, corresponding to a total of 224 participants included in the trial. We did not adjust the sample size according to missing data, as we planned to have as close to 0% missing data as possible. For the secondary outcomes (depressive symptoms, QOL and adverse events), power calculations were conducted based on estimated minimal important differences derived from the two systematic reviews previously conducted by our research group,15 16 our feasibility trial34 and trials applying the predefined outcomes in a comparable population.18 25 The analyses followed the statistical analysis plan, which was published before unblinding of the data.30 In brief, all continuous outcomes were analysed using linear regression and dichotomous outcomes were analysed using logistic regression. The analyses were based on an intention-to-treat population and were adjusted for the baseline value of the outcome of interest. Missing data were handled according to the recommendations of Jakobsen et al.35 Treatment providers were not involved in the analyses.

Early stopping

Despite an extension of the study by 6 months, we were unable to recruit and randomise the full sample size of 224 participants. This was primarily due to the COVID-19 shutdowns and to a national nursing strike, which both impacted the recruitment rates. Thus, the statistical significance level was adjusted according to the Lan-DeMets sequential monitoring boundaries based on the O'Brien Fleming alpha-spending function.36 The methodology has the advantage of being able to include the adjustments for early stopping and produce adjusted CIs. The trial was terminated after inclusion of 150 participants, of which 138 participants had data on the primary outcome. This corresponds to 62% of the total planned participants of 224. Using Lan-DeMets O’Brien-Fleming approximation, the corrected threshold of significance (alpha) was 0.0125 and the corresponding CIs presented were 98.75%. Only the primary outcome was adjusted, while the other outcomes were exploratory. Alpha was calculated using the gsDesign-package, resulting in 0.0125, and using the PwrGSD-package, resulting in 0.0125.

Results

From November 2020 to December 2022, 430 persons with type 2 diabetes were screened for eligibility. Of them, 150 participants were included and randomly assigned to either GSD (n=74) versus the attention control group (n=76) (figure 1). In the GSD group, eight participants did not receive the allocated intervention, and an additional two participants were lost to follow-up. In the attention control group, three participants did not receive the allocated control intervention, and no additional participants were lost to follow-up. Of the provided intervention, 62 participants preferred the face-to-face sessions, and 12 participants chose the digital GSD. In the attention control group, 58 preferred face-to-face sessions, eight chose video sessions and 10 chose telephone. Number of sessions were, for the intention-to-treat population, a mean of 2.9 (95% CI 2.4 to 3.4) for the GSD intervention group versus 3.3 (95% CI 2.9 to 3.7) for the attention control group. Attendance data were missing for 44 participants.

Baseline characteristics

Overall, the two groups were balanced across clinical and demographic characteristics, with the following exceptions: of all included participants, almost 2/3 were male (66.2% in the GSD group versus 63.2% in the attention control group). Of participants allocated to the GSD intervention, 48.7% lived alone versus 37.8% in the attention control group. Regarding education level, more participants in the attention control group had a master’s (9.2% vs 4.1%) or a bachelor’s degree (27.6% vs 21.6%). More participants allocated to GSD intervention were treated with Glucagon-Like Peptide-1 (GLP-1) analogue (59.5% vs 39.5%) or insulin (66.2% vs 53.9%). Regarding comorbidities, participants allocated to the attention control group had more cardiovascular complications (46.1% vs 40.5%) and late complications of diabetes (67.1% vs 62.2%). Intention to treat

Diabetes distress assessed at 12 month follow-up was lower in the GSD group versus the attention control group (mean difference, MD −5.83; 98.75% CI −11.26 to −0.41, p=0.007). The adjustment for group imbalances at baseline (GLP-1 treatment, insulin treatment, employment status and comorbidities, categorised as ‘yes’ or ‘no’) demonstrates a marginally more pronounced reduction in diabetes-related distress (MD −6.69: 98.75% CI −11.00 to −2.38), p=0.003 (online supplemental file 1). Depressive symptoms assessed at 12 months follow-up were lower in the GSD group versus the attention control group (MD −2.77; 98.75% CI −5.24 to −0.30, p=0.005). QOL (mental component) was lower in the attention control group versus the GSD group (MD 8.10; 98.75% CI 0.94 to 15.27, p=0.005), but no difference was found on the physical component (MD 1.79; 98.75% CI −5.72 to 9.31) or on adverse events (MD 0.00; 98.75% Hodges-Lehmann CI 0.00 to 0.00, p=0.157) (table 2). We found no differences between the GSD intervention and the attention control group when assessing the exploratory outcomes: HbA1c (MD 3.02 mmol/mol; 95% CI −0.23 to 6.27, p=0.068); motivation assessed with TSRQ (autonomous) (MD 0.24; 95% CI −0.05 to 0.52, p=0.099); TSRQ (controlled) (MD −0.05; 95% CI −0.41 to 0.30, p=0.766); TSRQ (resigned) (MD −0.22, 95% CI −0.58 to 0.14, p=0.234) or serious adverse events.

Discussion

This is the first randomised clinical superiority trial assessing beneficial and harmful effects of a GSD intervention versus an attention control group in people with type 2 diabetes in an outpatient clinical setting. The primary analysis was an intention-to-treat analysis, which showed beneficial effects of GSD on diabetes distress, depression and QOL SF-36 (mental component), but no differences on the physical component of the SF-36 scale or adverse events. We found no differences when assessing the exploratory outcomes: HbA1c, TSRQ (autonomous), TSRQ (controlled), TSRQ (resigned) or serious adverse events.

At 12 month follow-up, we found a reduction in our primary outcome, diabetes distress. However, the effect on diabetes distress was below our predefined minimal clinically important difference of six points on the PAID scale. Despite the statistical significance, a minimal clinically important difference of less than 6 points on the PAID scale would nevertheless be considered to be of negligible clinical significance. Even though our subgroup analysis showed a non-statistically significant result and should be cautiously interpreted due to early stopping, the reduction in diabetes distress seemed to be driven by the effect in women (−10.6 in women compared with −3.2 in men). The latter has previously been reported in a population of young adults with type 1 diabetes, where a reduction was found in diabetes distress and improved glycaemic control in women compared with men.10 In the OVERCOME trial, the majority of participants were male, with 66.2% of the GSD group and 63.2% of the attention control group being male.

Thus, a new trial could potentially reach the minimal clinically important difference for diabetes distress if it only included females.

Our analysis showed a reduction in depressive symptoms, though still below our predefined minimal clinically important difference of three points on the HADS scale. Likewise, precision and transferability to a clinical context were affected due to low recruitment and thus underpowered sample size. A minimal clinically important difference below three points on the HADS scale should be considered as clinically trivial. However, given that access to psychologists specialised in diabetes distress is a challenge in many outpatient clinics providing diabetes care, a nurse-driven GSD intervention may potentially offer a desirable option for a brief and affordable stepped-care intervention for reducing diabetes distress and depressive symptoms. Furthermore, the relatively short GSD stepped-care intervention15 with a mean of approximately three sessions may increase the application of evidence-based care and support the treatment of diabetes distress and depressive symptoms of GSD in a clinical setting.

The OVERCOME trial found a beneficial effect of the GSD on QOL (mental component) larger than our minimal clinically relevant difference of six points, but no difference in the physical component or on adverse events. A cornerstone of the GSD is that the method fosters a collaborative relationship with healthcare providers, making patients feel understood and supported, which reduces the mental burden of living with type 2 diabetes.14 31 This aligns with the reductions in diabetes distress and depression and is supported in trials investigating GSD in people with long-term health conditions.37 38 The null findings on the QOL (physical component) may indicate that the disease trajectory in people with type 2 diabetes and multiple comorbidities is severe and despite improved mental health and reduced diabetes distress, the disease deteriorates over time.39

We found no effects when assessing the exploratory outcomes: HbA1c, TSRQ (autonomous), TSRQ (controlled), TSRQ (resigned) or serious adverse events. We hypothesised that the GSD method would promote autonomy and intrinsic motivation in people with type 2 diabetes leading to improved diabetes management, reduced diabetes distress and improved glycaemic control.29 The underlying reasons for the observed indication of effect of GSD on diabetes distress, without a concomitant increase in autonomy and decrease in control, may be attributable to the fact that GSD is more directly targeted at both diabetes management and diabetes-related distress, compared with interventions that are solely self-determination based. Nonetheless, earlier studies of GSD in young adults with type 1 diabetes have reported an enhancement in autonomy and decrease in control,10 11 but one trial reported a reduction in HbA1c in women only.11 We did not plan a subgroup analysis on HbA1c and a potential difference between men and women but given that we also in the OVERCOME trial found an effect on diabetes distress driven by the effect in women, it might follow the same pattern on HbA1c. Theoretically, these findings may also suggest potential disparities in the underlying mechanisms responsible for the observed effects between types 1 and 2 diabetes. A fidelity evaluation of the GSD intervention in a large trial examining long-term follow-up effects and the evaluation of the reflection sheets among women after treatment for breast cancer reports that the values clarifying reflection sheet (the unfinished sentences) is the most preferred reflection sheet.38 This finding was further substantiated by a qualitative evaluation embedded within the OVERCOME trial.31 In the OVERCOME trial, this reflection sheet focused on problem areas within diabetes, for example, perceived lack of support from family or friends, which is an item specifically addressed on the PAID questionnaire.

Due to the premature stopping of the trial, we included only 150 participants of the 224 planned. Accordingly, we adjusted the threshold for statistical significance; however, this adjustment does not address the trial’s underpowering due to early stopping and, in particular, increases the risk of type 2 errors, which should be taken into account when interpreting our results.35 The trial was stopped before the originally determined sample size was reached due to various factors. First, from March 2020 onwards, large parts of Danish society were shut down due to COVID-19, which was during the intervention and the follow-up period of the trial. Participants in the trial as well as potential participants in outpatient clinics are disproportionately vulnerable to COVID-19 due to multiple comorbidities and complications of diabetes.40 Thus, the COVID-19 shutdowns negatively impacted primarily recruitment, the continuity of the GSD trajectories and drop-out rates as a relatively high number of potentially eligible participants declined participation due to the shutdowns. Moreover, the societal and personal impact of the COVID-19 virus may also have affected the mental health of the participants, potentially including both the experience of QOL, depressive symptoms and diabetes distress.41 There is, however, no reason to believe that this impact may differ between the intervention groups of our trial. Second, during COVID-19, a considerable extra workload was required from the nursing staff in the outpatient clinics where the OVERCOME trial was hosted. Several nurses involved in the OVERCOME trial were relocated to COVID-19 departments to care for hospitalised people infected with the virus. Subsequently, some of these nurses resigned following the replacement, thus requiring education of new GSD certified nurses to provide the intervention. Overall, these challenges made recruitment challenging; they might have disrupted the GSD trajectories and reduced the potential effect of the GSD intervention. In contrast, this seemed not to be the case in the attention control intervention delivered by the psychology and medical students. These sessions were unaffected by the disturbance during COVID-19. Third, following the COVID-19 shutdown, there was a national nursing strike in Denmark, which lasted 2 months during the recruitment of participants. This also caused delays in participants’ recruitment, disturbed the continuity and potentially compromised the quality of the certified nurses’ delivery of the GSD intervention. Again, this was not the case for the providers of the attention control group intervention who were unaffected by the nurse strike. Likewise, the methodological choice of an attention control group may, however, in itself have led to lower estimated effects of the GSD intervention compared with when applying wait-list or no-treatment controls (often referred to as usual care).19 The trial was stopped before the originally determined sample size was reached due to the abovementioned organisational, logistical and thus overall funding issues, as well as the unforeseen consequences of the COVID-19 pandemic.

Strengths

We used a rigorous trial methodology designed to minimise systematic as well as random errors. Prior to initiating the trial, we conducted a systematic review,16 which enabled us to derive anticipated intervention effects for calculating the sample size and the estimated minimal clinically important differences for power calculations for the secondary outcomes.42 The risk of selective outcome reporting was reduced by predefining all outcomes in our trial protocol.29 To ensure transparency of our methodological choices, to enable reproducibility and not least to minimise selective reporting bias, a statistical analysis plan was published preceding the analysis and reporting of the results.43 We applied an attention control group to reduce the risk of the intervention having an effect due to the Hawthorne effect.44 Even without differences in effect in most predefined outcomes, the trial may still contribute to accumulating low risk of bias evidence in, for example, meta-analyses. Finally, the pragmatic design with few inclusion and exclusion criteria as well as the provision of the GSD intervention by the clinical personnel employed at the outpatient clinics may increase the generalisability of the results to a real-world clinical outpatient setting.

Limitations

The primary limitation is that we failed to include the full estimated sample size. This is reported as a common risk in trials including persons with multimorbidity.45 We did, however, explicitly describe a predefined plan for multiple imputations applied due to the premature stopping of the trial.30 Despite our effort to avoid missing data, we did experience a large proportion of missing outcome data, which should always warrant cautious interpretations of results. Another limitation of this trial was the superiority design in which firm conclusions can only be derived if statistically significant differences between groups are found on the primary outcome. The motivation for treatment and the therapeutic alliance might influence the efficiency of the interventions but we did not assess these variables. We did, however, assess both potential negative outcomes by the NEQ-20 and serious adverse events, which were not found to favour neither the intervention nor the attention-control group. A baseline assessment of the therapeutic and motivation for treatment would have allowed us to assess a potential effect modification on the interaction between the two variables and the effect of the intervention. At the design stage of this trial, we tried to account for the fact that it is not possible to blind participants and providers in psychological interventions. We argue that we considered the limitations regarding blinding of all psychological trials thoroughly,33 for example by blinding group allocation in the participants’ records, but acknowledge that any differences between groups may be due to bias, chance, as well as random errors, even in our set-up trying to reduce such risks. According to Cochranes risk of bias tool 2.0,46 for participant-reported outcomes, the outcome assessor should be considered as the study participant. Thus, the fact that several outcomes were assessed with self-reported questionnaires may increase the risk of bias of our reported results. We attempted to blind external statisticians by concealing groups as ‘A’ and ‘B’ but are aware that this procedure may not blind statisticians fully. The use of nurses to deliver the intervention in the treatment group and certified counsellors (psychology students and trained medical students) in the attention control group could introduce bias in outcomes that could potentially undermine direct comparisons between the treatment and control groups. Given the small sample and reduced power, subgroup analyses and interaction tests have limited interpretability, but caution should be exercised that null findings do not rule out potential differential effects.

Conclusion

We found that the GSD intervention versus attention control appeared to reduce diabetes distress and depression, but the effect sizes were minimal and below our predefined minimal important differences. QOL (mental component) improved, exceeding our predefined minimal important differences. The trial was stopped early after randomisation of 150 participants (GSD n=74 vs attention control n=76) and thresholds for statistical significance were adjusted accordingly. Linear regression showed no difference in QOL, the physical component. No evidence of an effect was found on any of our predefined exploratory outcomes: HbA1c, motivation, or serious adverse events.
