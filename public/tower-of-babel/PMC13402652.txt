Breaking the Debilitating Cycle: Pathophysiology, Assessment, and Multimodal Intervention of Secondary Debilitation After Hip Fracture in Older Adults—A Narrative Review
Authors: Wenyang Xu, Fan Xu, Maoqiang Fan, Yao Liu, Zhe Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHip fractures pose a serious threat to the quality of life among older adults and impose a heavy burden on both society and families. Although current surgical techniques for hip fractures have become increasingly refined, postoperative quality of life and overall function in older adult populations often steeply decline. This decline is marked by “secondary debilitation,” characterized by exacerbated sarcopenia, functional impairment, and physiological reserve depletion—a process that becomes a risk factor for recurrent fractures, creating a “vicious cycle” with hip fractures. This article provides a comprehensive overview of the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying “secondary debilitation,” discusses the clinical application of risk assessment tools, and presents a phased, stepwise intervention strategy aimed at interrupting the “vicious cycle.” The strategy includes early rapid rehabilitation, nutritional support, and prevention of complications; a mid‐phase multimodal approach involving multidisciplinary management, comanaged wards, fracture liaison services, and systematic rehabilitation; and, finally, late‐phase exploration of emerging pharmacotherapies and treatment methods. This review seeks to offer an evidence‐based foundation for optimizing clinical risk assessment and developing precise interventional strategies.Keywords: hip fragility fracture, multimodal intervention, older adults, sarcopenia

Introduction

The 1‐year mortality rate for older adults with hip fractures can reach as high as 25%, and approximately 50% of patients are unable to regain normal lower‐limb function. By 2019, the incidence, prevalence, and years lived with disability (YLDs) rate of hip fractures among individuals aged 55 years and older were 681.35 per 100,000, 1191.39 per 100,000, and 130.78 per 100,000, respectively, with higher rates observed in females compared to males [1]. The incidence of hip fractures in older adults is projected to rise to six million by 2050, and these numbers are expected to increase further with population aging [2]. The average cost of treating an older adult with a hip fracture is about US$62,327, which imposes a substantial burden on families and individuals and entails considerable losses to economic development and healthcare resources.

Although current surgical techniques have become relatively advanced, the field remains overly focused on surgical procedures, success rates, and fracture healing, while neglecting the precipitous decline in patients' postoperative quality of life and overall functional status. In particular, “secondary debilitation” refers to an acute exacerbation or a newly developed clinical condition triggered by the stress of hip fracture on a preexisting baseline. It is characterized by a rapid loss of muscle mass and function (i.e., secondary sarcopenia), depletion of physiological reserves, and functional deterioration. Unlike “primary debilitation,” the causes of secondary debilitation are clearly identifiable, progress rapidly, involve fewer aging‐related factors, and can be partially reversed through appropriate interventions.

During the recovery process from hip fracture, factors such as immobilization of the affected limb and malnutrition can lead to disuse atrophy of muscle and bone tissue, creating a “fracture–functional decline–debilitation–subsequent fall/fracture” vicious cycle (Figure 1). This cycle can cause long‐term disability, repeated hospitalizations, and elevated mortality. Therefore, this review aims to systematically examine the mechanisms underlying this “vicious cycle” and to explore how current best evidence may be used to disrupt it, thereby providing an evidence‐based foundation for optimizing clinical risk assessment systems and developing targeted intervention strategies.

“Fragile Cycle” Model: A vicious cycle of [fracture trauma → inflammatory stress/bed rest → malnutrition/muscle breakdown → decreased mobility → increased debilitation → recurrent falls/increased risk of death].

Pathophysiology of Secondary Debilitation

Secondary debilitation following hip fracture in older adults involves an inflammatory cascade and dysregulation of the endocrine‐metabolic axis, further modulated by exogenous factors such as mechanical loading and nutritional status. These multifactorial interactions amplify one another and collectively drive disease progression.

Systemic Inflammatory Cascade Reaction After Fracture Trauma

Hip fracture and subsequent surgery can elicit a pronounced systemic inflammatory response, encompassing various pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as interleukins (IL), tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF‐α), and C‐reactive protein (CRP). TNF‐α, which is predominantly secreted by macrophages, rapidly increases following tissue injury (Table 1). The signaling of TNF‐like weak inducer of apoptosis (TWEAK) and its receptor fibroblast growth factor inducible 14 (Fn14) has been shown to induce muscle atrophy in mice [3], and TNF‐α can reduce both muscle mass and regenerative capacity [4, 5].

Interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β) also plays a critical role in muscle atrophy. Under normal conditions, myogenic cells continuously express IL‐1β; however, IL‐1β released upon cell damage can induce apoptosis in these cells [6]. In addition, IL‐1β can stimulate IL‐6 production in muscle tissue, thereby further exacerbating the local pro‐inflammatory milieu [7]. IL‐6, IL‐8, and IL‐1β can activate the ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS), a ubiquitous proteolytic pathway; when these pro‐inflammatory factors are overexpressed, bone mass and muscle mass both decrease [8, 9].

Relevant analyses indicate that high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein (Hs‐CRP) is negatively correlated with forearm skeletal muscle protein synthesis and directly positively correlated with protein degradation. Notably, Hs‐CRP demonstrates a strongly negative correlation with net protein balance [10].

Targeting these pathways—through, for example, anti‐IL‐6 antibodies or senolytic agents—may help mitigate post‐fracture muscle loss. Systemic inflammatory response is a key pathological mechanism underlying secondary physical debilitation in elderly patients following hip fractures. This process is characterized by excessive secretion and interaction of pro‐inflammatory factors, which accelerate the reduction of skeletal muscle mass and the decline in muscle function. Furthermore, the close interplay between the inflammatory response and metabolic imbalance further exacerbates muscle atrophy and bone loss after fractures.

Metabolic Imbalance and Synthetic Resistance

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) serve as common progenitor cells for osteoblasts, myoblasts, and adipocytes; in older adults, MSCs tend to differentiate more into adipocytes [11]. The accumulation of adipose tissue leads to increased release of cytotoxic fatty acids, inducing higher levels of various inflammatory factors such as interferon, tumor necrosis factor‐α, and interleukins (IL‐6, IL‐8, etc.), thereby activating inflammatory pathways [12]. Animal models similarly indicate that chronic inflammation can disrupt the metabolic homeostasis of muscle [13].

Insulin resistance (IR) is more prevalent in older adults compared with younger individuals. Post‐fracture endocrine and metabolic disturbances—which lead to IR—result in diminished stimulation of protein synthesis pathways, heightened activation of protein degradation mechanisms (particularly the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway), and mitochondrial dysfunction. Collectively, these processes result in a decline in muscle mass, and the subsequent reduction in muscle mass further curtails the surface area for glucose transport, potentially exacerbating IR and creating a vicious cycle [14]. IR also dysregulates activation of the mTOR pathway within the intracellular insulin signaling cascade, culminating in decreased muscle mass [15]. Moreover, IR suppresses insulin or insulin‐like growth factor‐1 (IGF‐1) signaling, leading to downregulation of the phosphoinositide 3‐kinase/Akt pathway, reduced protein synthesis, and decreased phosphorylation levels of forkhead box O1 (FOXO1). Dephosphorylated FOXO1 promotes the activation of the ubiquitin–proteasome proteolytic pathway by upregulating E3 ligases—atrogin‐1 and muscle RING finger protein‐1—thereby accelerating muscle protein degradation. Notably, this mechanism differs from that observed in age‐related sarcopenia [16].

Studies have shown that older adults with limited postoperative physical activity exhibit lower‐than‐normal serum levels of growth hormone (GH), insulin‐like growth factor‐1 (IGF‐1), and mechano growth factor (MGF). Low levels of IGF‐1 and MGF correlate positively with reduced skeletal muscle mass [17], suggesting that the GH/IGF‐1 axis plays a pivotal role in maintaining skeletal muscle function [18].

Metabolic imbalance and anabolic resistance are key drivers of debilitation following fractures, with IR and chronic inflammation jointly affecting protein synthesis and degradation pathways, ultimately leading to loss of muscle mass and function. In addition to metabolic and inflammatory mechanisms, endocrine stress responses significantly impact post‐fracture recovery, further exacerbating debilitation.

Endocrine Stress Response

Hip fractures induce a severe traumatic stress response in the body, marked by heightened sympathetic nervous activity and stimulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, resulting in increased levels of glucocorticoids (GCs). Under the influence of GCs, the ubiquitin–proteasome system is activated, and two muscle‐specific ubiquitin E3 ligases—atrogin‐1 (muscle atrophy F‐box) and muscle RING finger protein‐1 (MuRF1)—are key determinants of muscle atrophy [19, 20]. GCs can also activate the autophagy–lysosome system, with autophagy playing a critical role in triggering mitochondrial fragmentation [21]. Furthermore, GCs promote the degradation of myofibrils while inhibiting protein synthesis, thereby contributing to muscle atrophy [22]. Concurrent catabolic processes reduce the nutritional support that muscle provides to bone [23]. Additionally, GCs suppress osteoblast differentiation and function, induce apoptosis of osteoblasts and osteocytes, and extend osteoclast lifespan, resulting in a negative bone metabolic balance [24].

Post‐fracture stress responses also affect the hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis, possibly causing transient elevations or reductions in thyroid hormone (TH) levels. TH influences the metabolism of multiple tissues throughout the body and is likewise a critical target in muscle. Muscle growth and repair primarily depend on a specialized population of mononuclear cells known as satellite cells (SCs), whose self‐renewal, proliferation, and differentiation are essential for maintaining healthy muscle regenerative capacity [25, 26]. Various stimuli, including muscle injury or disease, can induce SC activation. The activation, proliferation, and differentiation of SCs may be regulated at multiple steps by the active form of TH, triiodothyronine (T3) [27]. Notably, both hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism are associated with muscle disorders, indicating that normal muscle function depends on maintaining TH levels within an appropriate range [28]. Although TH exerts a significant influence on skeletal muscle, evidence regarding its role following hip fracture surgery remains limited, underscoring the need for further investigations involving multi‐center studies and diverse patient populations.

Fracture‐induced endocrine stress responses disrupt the regulatory mechanisms of endocrine axes, including GCs and THs, impairing the metabolic balance of bone and muscle and accelerating the progression of osteoporosis and muscle atrophy. Postoperative malnutrition, often associated with systemic metabolic and endocrine imbalances, is a significant external factor contributing to the acceleration of physical debilitation.

Malnutrition—A Duet of Insufficient Intake and Accelerated Decomposition

The incidence of malnutrition among older adults with hip fractures is relatively high, ranging from approximately 7% to 26% [29]. During hospitalization, factors such as moderate to severe pain, gastrointestinal reactions induced by perioperative nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs, preoperative fasting, prolonged surgical delays, postoperative intestinal obstruction and gastric retention, and anesthesia can lead to inadequate food intake, eventually resulting in malnutrition [30, 31]. In the first week following hip surgery, only 17.5% of patients meet their energy requirements [32]. Insufficient nutritional intake—particularly inadequate total energy and high‐quality protein—combined with deficits in essential micronutrients (e.g., minerals and vitamins) disrupts the anabolic processes and repair of bone and muscle, constituting a critical risk factor for debilitation after hip fracture [33, 34, 35]. The resulting energy deficit exacerbates postoperative catabolic responses, leading to the loss of muscle mass and function, and potentially impairing immune function and delaying healing [36, 37]. In patients with hip fractures, insufficient energy and nutrient intake significantly impairs anabolic processes, enhances catabolism, and delays the repair of bone and muscle tissues. During fracture recovery, the immobilization effect further accelerates the loss of muscle and bone function.

Neuromuscular Disuse and Immobilization Effects

The growth, development, and normal function of skeletal muscle rely on motor nerve innervation and regulation. Traumatic peripheral nerve injuries and various diseases can result in denervation of muscle, leading to neurogenic muscle atrophy, also known as disuse atrophy or dystrophic atrophy [38, 39]. In addition, disuse states caused by fracture‐related immobilization, surgery, and prolonged bed rest can similarly induce muscle atrophy [40]. Animal model studies using tail suspension in rodents—thereby inducing cranial fluid shifts and preventing weight‐bearing in the hind limbs—have demonstrated this approach to effectively simulate clinical sarcopenia [41, 42].

Under post‐fracture immobilization, MSCs exhibit increased differentiation into adipocytes. Adipose infiltration into skeletal muscle reduces muscle insulin sensitivity and leads to declines in muscle mass and strength [43, 44].

Prolonged immobilization after fractures not only leads to disuse muscle atrophy but also exacerbates fat infiltration, metabolic disorders, and debilitation through various mechanisms. The negative effects of immobilization on the skeletal system also contribute to the onset and progression of secondary osteoporosis following fractures.

Secondary Osteoporosis After Hip Fracture

Osteoporosis (defined as a T‐score ≤ −2.5) is characterized by decreased bone mineral density (BMD), which represents a nonspecific manifestation of aging and debilitation and is also the primary cause of hip fragility fractures [45]. Pathological conditions such as dysfunction of the osteoblast–osteoclast system, endocrine disturbances, prolonged postoperative immobilization, and inadequate nutritional intake can all contribute to the development of osteoporosis. Numerous studies have demonstrated a negative correlation between BMD and adverse clinical outcomes [46, 47], while others have suggested that osteoporosis further increases the risk of subsequent fractures and mortality in patients with fragility fractures [48]. Biomechanical studies confirm that in patients with femoral neck fractures undergoing joint replacement surgery, changes in stress distribution at the bone–prosthesis interface accelerate local bone resorption. Additionally, recovery from femoral bone resorption caused by mechanical stress and thermal damage during surgery can take up to 3 months, creating a high‐risk window for postoperative refracture [49]. High‐quality evidence indicates that standardized anti‐osteoporosis treatment can reduce refracture rates, lower the risk of implant failure, and significantly decrease mortality [50].

Secondary osteoporosis is a common comorbidity in older adults with hip fractures and represents a key component of the debilitation vicious cycle. Its management is crucial to reducing the risk of refractures and mortality. Multidimensional interventions targeting inflammation, metabolic imbalance, malnutrition, and osteoporosis are critical for improving the prognosis of older patients with hip fractures.

In conclusion, current knowledge gaps include insufficient longitudinal evidence on cross‐tissue causal pathways, a lack of systematic characterization of cell‐type‐specific regulatory mechanisms, and the absence of defined clinical thresholds and therapeutic windows for actionable biomarkers. Future studies should integrate multi‐omics, single‐cell/spatial transcriptomics, causal inference, and interventional research to establish a stratified, predictive, and actionable mechanistic framework. Multidimensional interventions targeting inflammation, metabolic imbalance, malnutrition, and osteoporosis are essential for improving outcomes in older patients with hip fractures.

Risk Assessment and Identification Tools

Risk assessment for older adults with hip fractures should be conducted throughout the preoperative, postoperative, and recovery phases, aiming to provide a comprehensive evaluation of debilitation and functional status. This approach forms the basis for individualized intervention strategies.

Preoperative Phase

Assessment of fragility fractures and sarcopenia involves identifying risk factors, conducting a comprehensive history and physical examination, and carrying out auxiliary tests. The Clinical Fragility Scale (CFS) is a judgment‐based tool frequently used in hospital settings for debilitation assessment. It evaluates specific domains (including comorbidities, function, and cognition) to yield a debilitation score ranging from 1 (very fit) to 9 (terminally ill). Outcomes significantly associated with CFS include mortality, comorbidity burden, functional decline, reduced mobility, and cognitive deterioration [51].

The Nottingham Hip Fracture Score (NHFS) includes age, sex, admission hemoglobin level, cognitive status, residence in a care facility, number of comorbidities, and history of malignancy. Because these variables are readily available early after admission, the NHFS is particularly suitable for early perioperative risk stratification and for identifying high‐risk patients who may require comprehensive geriatric management, nutritional optimization, delirium prevention, and individualized rehabilitation planning [52]. Several studies have demonstrated that the NHFS has excellent predictive ability for 30‐day mortality in patients with hip fracture, with good specificity [53, 54].

The American Society of Anesthesiologists Physical Status Classification System (ASA) is a commonly used tool for preoperative assessment of the overall burden of systemic disease. In older patients with hip fracture, a higher ASA class is associated with perioperative complications, increased mortality, prolonged hospital stay, and poorer postoperative recovery [55]. Although the ASA classification is not specific to hip fracture and does not directly assess debilitation or rehabilitation potential, it provides a rapid indication of physiological reserve and anesthetic risk at admission. Therefore, it should be used in combination with other assessment tools and laboratory investigations.

The concept of the debilitation Index (FI) posits that debilitation arises from the cumulative accumulation of health deficits over the lifespan, which may include symptoms, signs, diseases, disabilities, laboratory findings, imaging abnormalities, and social characteristics. The FI is calculated as the ratio of the number of deficits present to the total number of considered deficits. Greater numbers of health deficits increase the likelihood of becoming frail. Unlike classifying debilitation based merely on its three categories—robust, pre‐frail, and frail—FI can provide a more nuanced approach to determine the degree of debilitation and improve risk prediction [56].

The latest recommendations of the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older Adults (EWGSOP2) endorse the SARC‐F questionnaire as a screening tool. With a sensitivity of 60% to 95%, SARC‐F is effective for identifying sarcopenic individuals among older inpatients with hip fractures [57].

Preoperative assessment is a critical step in identifying debilitation, comorbidity burden, and rehabilitation potential, enabling effective stratification of perioperative risks and providing personalized decision support for comprehensive geriatric management. In the postoperative phase, comprehensive pain management and evaluation of basic functional abilities are equally essential for ensuring early recovery and facilitating rehabilitation progress.

Postoperative Stage to Discharge

Pain should be assessed during both the perioperative and rehabilitation phases after hip fracture. In older patients with hip fracture, self‐report tools such as the Emoji‐based Visual Analog Scale or the Numeric Rating Scale may be used. Although direct evidence regarding their accuracy for assessing postoperative pain after hip fracture is lacking, indirect evidence suggests a high level of agreement between these tools [58]. Both pain at rest and pain during movement should be assessed, as movement‐evoked pain directly affects early mobilization and rehabilitation training. For patients with dementia, delirium, or communication difficulties, observational tools such as the Pain Assessment in Advanced Dementia scale and the Abbey Pain Scale may be used [59, 60]. Multidimensional instruments, such as the Brief Pain Inventory, can further evaluate the impact of pain on mobility, sleep, mood, and daily function [61].

Activities of Daily Living (ADL) are a core measure of a patient's ability to perform basic self‐care and include daily physical activities such as eating, dressing, bathing, walking, toileting, and personal hygiene. Commonly used instruments include the Barthel Index, Katz ADL scale, Functional Independence Measure (FIM), and Lawton Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL) scale. ADL assessment is essential for determining functional independence and predicting postoperative recovery, long‐term prognosis, overall health status, and potential care needs after hip fracture [62]. The main advantage of ADL assessment is its close relevance to real‐life functioning; however, it cannot fully replace assessments of muscle strength, gait, balance, nutrition, cognition, and pain.

Postoperative pain management and ADL assessment are critical for monitoring early recovery and functional status, not only improving short‐term rehabilitation outcomes but also laying the foundation for post‐discharge recovery planning. Following discharge, rehabilitation assessments should focus on functional metrics (e.g., muscle strength and gait) and objective measurements of muscle and bone quality to optimize long‐term recovery outcomes.

Recovery Period After Discharge (3–6 Months/1 Year)

Grip‐strength measurement is both convenient and cost‐effective, and it is moderately correlated with strength in other areas of the body. Consequently, grip strength can serve as a reliable surrogate for more complex assessments of arm and leg strength. Low grip strength is a critical prognostic indicator for adverse outcomes, including prolonged hospitalization, increased functional limitations, declines in health‐related quality of life, and even mortality [63, 64].

Gait speed evaluation is a rapid, safe, and highly reliable method for assessing sarcopenia, and it is widely used in clinical practice. According to EWGSOP2, a single critical gait speed of ≤ 0.8 m/s is recommended as a threshold for severe sarcopenia [49]. The chair stand test measures the time required for a patient to rise from a seated position without using their arms, repeated five times, and is used to assess lower‐limb muscle strength. As it requires both strength and endurance, it is an effective and convenient method of strength measurement [65]. The Short Physical Performance Battery is a comprehensive test that consists of gait speed assessment, balance testing, and a chair stand test [66]. The maximum score on this battery is 12, and scores of ≤ 8 indicate poor physical performance [67, 68]. These assessments provide valuable prognostic information for older patients after hip fracture surgery.

Dual‐energy x‐ray absorptiometry (DXA) can accurately measure BMD and also provide data for calculating lean body mass, thereby enabling qualitative evaluations of osteoporosis and muscle mass. However, results may vary across DXA devices produced by different manufacturers [69, 70, 71]. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and computed tomography (CT) serve as the gold standards for noninvasive assessment of muscle mass and volume; however, clear consensus regarding the threshold for low muscle mass using these methods has yet to be established [68].

Compared with DXA, MRI, and CT, ultrasound has shown promising performance in estimating muscle mass and can be conducted at the bedside by a trained clinician. Nonetheless, further research is needed to confirm its applicability across various health conditions and functional states [72, 73]. Bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) devices are affordable, widely adopted, and portable and have been used to estimate total muscle mass or appendicular skeletal muscle mass. Nevertheless, discrepancies exist between BIA and DXA measurements, and variations in age and ethnicity among different populations must be taken into account [74, 75]. Additionally, BIA results may be influenced by the patient's fluid status.

Recent studies have shown that the D3‐dilution method—measuring the steady‐state isotopic concentration of D3‐creatinine in urine samples after administering labeled D3‐creatine—can provide a more accurate measure of skeletal muscle mass. This method exhibits considerable potential for diagnosing sarcopenia and monitoring treatment efficacy [76, 77]; however, further validation is needed in larger cohorts and across diverse regions [78].

The Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) is a weighted scoring tool based on the type and severity of pre‐existing comorbidities. It is primarily used to quantify comorbidity burden and to assess overall health status and prognostic risk. A higher CCI score generally indicates a greater comorbidity burden and higher disease complexity, with a corresponding increase in the risk of adverse outcomes, including mortality and poor functional recovery. Previous studies have shown that the CCI is associated with increased mortality in patients with fragility fractures [79]. Patients with higher CCI scores have significantly longer waiting times for surgery, longer hospital stays, and more frequent blood transfusions; in addition, the prevalence of osteoporosis is significantly higher among older patients with hip fracture [80]. Regarding functional mobility, patients with a CCI score ≥ 6 have lower absolute Pain and Mobility Score (PMS) values within 1 year after surgery, indicating poorer functional recovery. Multivariable analysis further demonstrated that a higher CCI score is an independent predictor of poor PMS‐based functional recovery at 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively [81].

Post‐discharge rehabilitation assessments encompass multidimensional measurements of overall muscle strength, functional performance, and musculoskeletal quality, providing a scientific basis for monitoring recovery, risk prediction, and dynamic treatment optimization.

In summary, the aforementioned risk assessment and diagnostic tools play predictive and evaluative roles at different stages of hip fracture progression. While complementary in predictive value, they remain independent in scope. By implementing a comprehensive assessment framework spanning the preoperative, postoperative, and follow‐up phases, holistic management of the recovery process in hip fracture patients can be achieved, thereby supporting improved prognosis and quality of life. However, it is important to note that nutritional status assessment and monitoring should be integrated throughout all stages of the disease course.

Breaking the “Cycle of Decline”: Multi‐Modal Intervention Strategies

Phase 1: Acute Phase in Hospital—Prevention and Mitigation

Fast Recovery

Enhanced Recovery After Surgery is a multimodal, interdisciplinary approach to perioperative care for surgical patients. Grounded in evidence‐based medical advances, the components of this modern care framework help reduce surgical stress, maintain anabolic balance, and lower complication rates by 30%–50%, with similar reductions in length of hospital stay [82, 83]. Studies have shown that regardless of when surgery is performed, if more than 3 days pass from admission to the initiation of mobilization, the likelihood of postoperative complications increases significantly. Moreover, patients who wait over 48 h for surgery and those who do not begin ambulation until more than 48 h postoperatively have a 6.3‐fold higher likelihood of postoperative complications compared with other patients [84]. Early postoperative mobilization—particularly weight bearing as tolerated within the first 48 h—contributes to improved functional recovery following hip fracture repair [85, 86]. Evidence indicates that older hip fracture patients receiving intramedullary fixation with satisfactory reduction can tolerate immediate full weight bearing postoperatively, without increasing the need for reoperation due to implant failure or prolonging overall recovery time [87]. The Canadian Orthopaedic Association clearly states in its practice guidelines that such protocols do not elevate the risk of mechanical complications related to surgical repair.

Nutritional Support

Muscle is the largest protein reservoir in the human body [88]. Insufficient protein intake increases the risk of sarcopenia and impairs quality of life [88, 89]. Low protein intake also elevates the risk of immune system deficiencies, thereby increasing susceptibility to infections. Consuming protein in excess of the amount required to avoid negative nitrogen balance may help prevent sarcopenia, maintain energy balance, improve skeletal health and cardiovascular function, and promote wound healing—ultimately enhancing older adults' capacity to recover from illness and injury [90]. The Food and Nutrition Board of the U.S. Institute of Medicine has established Dietary Reference Intakes, which include the Estimated Average Requirement (EAR), the Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA), and the Acceptable Macronutrient Distribution Range (AMDR) [90, 91]. The EAR for daily dietary protein is 0.66 g/kg/day, and the Board recommends an RDA of 0.8 g/kg/day for all adults over 18 years of age, including older adults over 65. However, experts in protein and aging recommend a protein intake of 1.2–2.0 g/kg/day or higher for older individuals [92, 93](Table 2). These recommendations, though, do not fully account for clinical circumstances such as trauma, hospitalization, or surgery—situations that are relatively common among older adults and have been demonstrated to require protein intake at levels higher than the standard recommendations [90].

Essential amino acids (EAAs) serve as potent stimulators of muscle protein synthesis, with whey protein—rich in EAAs—being included in various nutritional supplements [94]. Whey protein has been shown to promote improvements in muscle strength, muscle mass, and physical function in older adults [95]. Because whey is rapidly absorbed and has a relatively short duration of action, it quickly increases the availability of amino acids, resulting in a rapid and substantial acceleration of whole‐body protein synthesis [96, 97]. Furthermore, the effect of whey protein on whole‐body protein breakdown appears to be minimal or negligible [96], although not all studies support this position [98], indicating the need for further research.

Vitamin D is a pleiotropic hormone, synthesized primarily in the skin. Due to population aging and changes in lifestyle, most older adults experience insufficient sunlight exposure, which almost invariably leads to poor vitamin D status [99]. Inadequate vitamin D levels affect bone mineralization, potentially causing bone density loss in older adults and increasing their risk of refracture. Low vitamin D levels are also associated with decreased muscle performance and loss of type II fast‐twitch muscle fibers, further elevating the risk of falls [100]. A meta‐analysis found that supplementing vitamin D in older adults with serum 25‐hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] levels below 30 nmol/L can improve muscle strength [101]. Consistent with this finding, a randomized controlled trial (RCT) showed that daily supplementation of 1000 IU vitamin D3 over 9 months significantly reduced the incidence of falls [102]. However, there is currently no consensus regarding the optimal vitamin D supplementation level for potentially benefiting muscle function, balance, and fall risk reduction.

Calcium is an essential component of bone, supporting the restoration of bone density and strength—critical factors in the healing process. Older adults with hip fractures typically have low total calcium intake and increased physiological demands for calcium. Adequate calcium intake can expedite recovery, reduce the risk of subsequent fractures, and help maintain overall skeletal health [103]. According to the recommendations of the International Osteoporosis Foundation, the Institute of Medicine, and other authoritative bodies, older adults should consume around 1200 mg of calcium daily [104, 105]. One RCT reported that daily intake of 1142 mg calcium and 69 g protein, compared with 700 mg calcium and 58 g protein, reduced the risk of any type of fracture by 33%, hip fracture by 46%, and falls by 11% [102].

The mode of supplement administration and adherence pose considerable challenges among older adults with hip fractures. Patients in this population often do not tolerate nasogastric feeding and may struggle with oral intake due to palatability issues, which are particularly common among those with altered consciousness or severe debilitation. Malnutrition itself can contribute to feeding and digestive difficulties, further limiting supplemental intake [106]. Oral supplements commonly provide less than 405 kcal per day, whereas nasogastric feeding can deliver between 933 and 1500 kcal. Consequently, despite its higher complication risk, nasogastric feeding may be the better option for those requiring greater energy intake (e.g., patients undergoing hip fracture surgery) [106]. Measures aimed at boosting oral nutrition adherence and intake include high‐energy hospital meals and increased frequency of snack offerings between meals [107].

Multimodal Analgesia and Delirium Prevention

Hip fracture and postoperative pain are common and significant factors affecting rehabilitation outcomes. Pain associated with hip fracture may lead to prolonged bed rest, delayed mobilization, increased risk of deep vein thrombosis, muscle atrophy, functional impairment, missed opportunities for physical therapy, and functional disability lasting up to 6 months after surgery [108]. Inadequately treated pain is a major contributor to delirium, one of the most common hospital‐acquired complications among older adults admitted with hip fractures. Research indicates that the incidence of delirium following hip fracture can reach 41%, which is independently associated with worse functional recovery [109]. Opioid analgesics can induce delirium; thus, the American Geriatrics Society recommends using non‐opioid analgesics to optimize pain control [110]. In cases of long‐term opioid use, abruptly reducing or discontinuing the medication may lead to hyperalgesia, increasing the need for postoperative analgesics. Accordingly, sudden reduction of opioids during the perioperative period is not advised [111].

Local anesthesia techniques offer potential in mitigating opioid‐related side effects and can serve as effective alternatives for older adults experiencing fracture‐related pain [112]. Compared with orally administered or injected analgesics, perioperative nerve blocks have demonstrated favorable efficacy and safety in alleviating hip fracture pain. Although recent guidelines recommend the use of regional nerve blocks, many practicing anesthesiologists do not routinely employ this technique, preventing it from becoming a standard treatment for hip fracture pain management [113].

Four additional nonpharmacological interventions—acupressure, relaxation therapy, transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS), and physiotherapy—have also been found to be effective and exhibit a high degree of safety. However, their correct application and guidance often require dedicated healthcare personnel, additional time, and specialized training. Current evidence on these interventions remains limited, necessitating further research [113].

Phase 2: Transition Period and Medium‐ and Long‐Term Rehabilitation—Reversal and Reconstruction

Structured, Progressive Resistance Training

High‐quality evidence indicates that systematic physical therapy and resistance training (RT) can increase BMD, muscle mass, strength, and physical performance, and play an essential role in secondary prevention of falls and refractures [114]. According to reports, RT—via various modalities (e.g., elastic bands, free weights, and machines)—can improve both osteoporosis and sarcopenia [115]. Given that sarcopenia affects all skeletal muscles throughout the body, it is recommended to employ a whole‐body training approach targeting large muscle groups through resistance exercise. Previous studies have used differing training intensities expressed as percentages of one‐repetition maximum (1RM)—including 50% 1RM, 60% 1RM, and 85% 1RM [115, 116, 117]—concluding that a high‐intensity RT program (80% 1RM) is most effective for maximizing strength gains. For older adults with poorer physical condition, low‐intensity TE (≤ 50% 1RM) may be sufficient to promote strength improvements. Training parameters typically include two to three sessions per week, each consisting of one to four sets per exercise, with eight to fifteen repetitions per set [118].

Some evidence shows that certain elastic band training (EBT) programs can be performed at home with minimal equipment—for example, seated rows, biceps curls, lateral walks, and leg extensions. Following 12 weeks of EBT, older women, particularly those with age‐related osteoporosis and obesity, experienced a significant increase in skeletal muscle mass index and a substantial reduction in body fat percentage (BFP) [119]. Conversely, a 16‐month, high‐intensity, high‐frequency, heavy‐load, single‐set RT program (High‐Intensity Resistance Training) for older men with sarcopenia effectively prevented thigh muscle fat infiltration and improved muscle atrophy [120].

During postoperative rehabilitation following hip fracture, proper load bearing is crucial. Insufficient load bearing hinders bone healing, whereas excessive load bearing may cause fracture redisplacement and implant failure. Traditional assistive devices (e.g., crutches, walkers) typically do not help patients control load intensity. Currently, a burgeoning field involves an innovative robotic control system based on visualized weight bearing, which can sense lower‐limb load. This system provides intelligent, automated rehabilitation training and ambulation support to patients with lower‐limb dysfunction/balance issues or limb fractures and their sequelae, and has demonstrated positive clinical outcomes [121]. However, the application of robots using visual weight‐bearing technology remains relatively limited in postoperative hip fracture rehabilitation.

One study found that a 16‐week home‐based training program supplemented with TENS did not produce a statistically significant improvement in the ability to walk 300 m or more within 6 min among postoperative hip fracture patients [122]. At present, there is insufficient evidence confirming the efficacy of TENS in enhancing muscle strength, and further high‐quality trials are needed in this domain [123].

Continuous Nutritional Management

In the subsequent treatment of older adults with hip fractures, providing continuous nutritional support from the hospital setting to the community or home constitutes a practical, feasible, and safe cornerstone of postoperative care [114]. Indirect evidence suggests that, within 6 months following fracture, a daily intake of 40 g of protein and 600 kcal alongside weekly bisphosphonate therapy for 12 months leads to improved handgrip strength compared with monthly bisphosphonate alone over the same period [124]. Additionally, another study revealed that a two‐month synergistic multidisciplinary rehabilitation and nutritional intervention regimen for older adults with fragile hip fractures exerted a significantly positive impact on their functional status. In particular, essential amino acid supplementation markedly benefited patients with sarcopenia, indicating that such interventions may play a crucial role in the clinical management of this complex and challenging condition [125].

Secondary Prevention Pharmacotherapy for Osteoporosis

Anti‐resorptive therapy is a key strategy for the treatment of osteoporosis. Commonly used medications include bisphosphonates and denosumab, both of which increase BMD and reduce fracture risk, albeit via different mechanisms. Bisphosphonates bind to bone mineral surfaces and are internalized during bone matrix resorption by osteoclasts, thereby inhibiting osteoclast function or inducing their apoptosis [126]. Due to their prolonged retention in bone tissue, their anti‐resorptive effects can persist for months to years after discontinuation. Denosumab, a fully human IgG2 monoclonal antibody, binds specifically to the receptor activator of nuclear factor‐κB ligand (Receptor Activator of Nuclear factor‐κB Ligand, RANKL), inhibiting the formation, activation, and survival of osteoclasts [127]. Unlike bisphosphonates, the effects of denosumab are rapidly reversible upon discontinuation, often necessitating sequential use of other anti‐resorptive agents to prevent rapid bone loss and rebound fracture risk [128].

For patients with severe bone loss, current clinical guidelines recommend the use of anabolic agents as first‐line therapy [129]. Common anabolic agents include teriparatide, abaloparatide, and romosozumab. Teriparatide and abaloparatide stimulate the differentiation of osteoprogenitor cells into osteoblasts, enhance new bone formation by mature osteoblasts, and reduce osteoblast apoptosis [130]. Romosozumab, an anti‐sclerostin monoclonal antibody, has dual effects of promoting bone formation and inhibiting bone resorption [131].

For patients at high fracture risk, anti‐resorptive therapy is generally recommended. Such patients include those with a BMD T‐score ≤ −2.5, a history of vertebral or hip fractures, multiple prior fractures, or elevated 10‐year absolute fracture risk (e.g., ≥ 20%). For those at very high risk (e.g., recent vertebral fractures, hip fractures with a BMD T‐score ≤ −2.5, or multiple recent fractures), anabolic therapy should be prioritized, followed by sequential anti‐resorptive treatment to maintain the gains in bone mass and continue reducing fracture risk [50]. Evidence suggests that initiating treatment with abaloparatide for 18 months followed by sequential alendronate therapy for 2 years reduces fracture risk by 37% compared to 4 years of anti‐resorptive therapy alone [132]. Similarly, romosozumab treatment for 1 year followed by alendronate for another year reduces the risk of new non‐vertebral fractures by 19% compared to 2 years of alendronate alone [133].

Multidisciplinary Collaboration Model

Due to the complexity of the clinical conditions and social backgrounds of older adults with hip fractures, close collaboration among various professionals is necessary, requiring management strategies different from those used for younger patients in order to foster functional recovery and facilitate reintegration into everyday social environments [134]. A multidisciplinary team dedicated to older adults with hip fractures may include Emergency Department personnel, orthopedic surgeons, geriatricians, anesthesiologists, cardiologists, rehabilitation specialists, neurologists, osteoporosis treatment centers, post‐acute care referral teams, and nursing staff. Together, they devise a patient‐centered care plan aimed at reducing mortality and complications, improving functional outcomes and quality of life, and lowering the overall health care expenditure among older adults with hip fractures [135].

Upon admission, patients are transferred to a geriatric orthopedic ward managed jointly by orthopedic surgeons and geriatricians. The focus of care is early surgery (< 48 h), evaluation and management of comorbidities, secondary fracture prevention, physical therapy, nutritional management, and early discharge. Anesthesiologists and cardiologists conduct comprehensive preoperative assessments, including anesthesia and cardiovascular evaluations [135]. Geriatricians oversee comorbidity management, postoperative complication prevention, and secondary fracture prevention, whereas orthopedic surgeons are responsible for surgical planning and execution. Physical therapists and the nursing team provide standardized care and rehabilitation services, and integration of rehabilitation physicians and neurologists ensures early mobilization and recovery [135, 136, 137]. After discharge, patients receive follow‐up care in an integrated outpatient clinic staffed by orthopedic, rehabilitation, neurology, and geriatric specialists to coordinate various specialty treatments and medications [135]. This model has demonstrated effectiveness in reducing in‐hospital complications and mortality, shortening hospital stays, decreasing readmission rates, improving prognoses, and enhancing patient satisfaction with medical services [138].

Various models of orthogeriatric care, such as geriatric wards with orthopedic consultation services, orthopedic wards with geriatric consultation services, and co‐management approach, have been shown to improve patient outcomes [139, 140]. In the traditional model, orthopedic‐specialized wards are entirely managed by orthopedic healthcare professionals, who handle all medical issues, including comorbidities and non‐orthopedic complications. Treatment for most geriatric conditions is often initiated only after surgery, leading to prolonged preoperative preparation times and extended hospital stays. In contrast, rehabilitation hospital wards are limited in laboratory testing, medical technological support, and nursing surveillance, increasing the risk of readmission due to changes in the patient's condition.

The Orthogeriatric Care Model (OCM), also known as the Sheba Orthogeriatric Model, offers a distinct advantage by facilitating a faster and smoother transition of patients from specialized hospitals to rehabilitation hospitals. Under this model, patients do not need to wait for all medical issues to be fully resolved before being transferred. Geriatric specialists assess the suitability of transfer during routine ward rounds. For patients with medical issues that do not require intensive nursing surveillance, geriatricians at rehabilitation hospitals can manage their care directly, avoiding unnecessary transfers back to specialized orthopedic wards and effectively reducing readmission rates.

Some studies have shown that the OCM reduces adverse outcomes, such as mortality, disease progression, and quality of life decline, during follow‐ups within 1 year. However, the certainty of this evidence remains low [141]. Evidence indicates that, compared with conventional fracture care, the orthogeriatric co‐management model leads to a 28% reduction in in‐hospital mortality, a 14% reduction in one‐year mortality, and a 19% reduction in delirium incidence for elderly hip fracture patients. Hospital stays are shortened by 1–7 days, with the most significant benefits observed in older adults with multiple chronic conditions, sarcopenia, and debilitation [142].

This model provides critical advantages, including reducing healthcare expenditures, optimizing the use of medical resources, and alleviating the burden on families. It currently represents the most effective clinical management strategy for older patients with fragility hip fractures.

Fracture liaison services (FLS) bring together physicians, nurses, pharmacists, physical therapists, and other professionals to identify eligible patients, assess secondary fracture risks, and offer treatment protocols and follow‐up plans to prevent future fractures [143]. FLS comprise four steps: (1) emergency and orthopedic physicians identify high‐risk patients, using International Classification of Diseases (ICD‐10) fracture codes in patient records, and arrange for BMD measurement along with FRAX fracture risk assessments; (2) the patient undergoes DXA and FRAX evaluation in the hospital's DXA facility under the supervision of experienced nurses/physicians; (3) results of the DXA exam and clinical risk factors are reviewed by an experienced endocrinologist, who confirms the diagnosis and formulates treatment recommendations and follow‐up plans, subsequently forwarding this information to the patient's primary physician; (4) in consultation with the patient, the primary physician initiates appropriate osteoporosis medications aimed at reducing the risk of subsequent fractures [144]. FLS not only increase the frequency of BMD monitoring and the use of anti‐osteoporotic medications, but also enhance treatment adherence [145].

Phase 3: Emerging Drugs and Therapies

Anamorelin

Ghrelin is a peptide hormone mainly secreted by the stomach; elevated ghrelin levels promote appetite [146]. Anamorelin, a ghrelin receptor agonist that can be taken orally, enhances appetite in patients with cachexia and stimulates GH secretion, thereby increasing protein synthesis and muscle mass [147]. Recent studies have shown that after 12 weeks of once‐daily oral anamorelin, patients with cachectic sarcopenia experienced gains in body weight and lean body mass, although no improvement was observed in muscle strength as measured by handgrip testing [148]. Because anamorelin has not demonstrated benefits in terms of physical function and is still in Phase III clinical trials, it has not been approved for clinical use by the European Medicines Agency (EMA), although it has been approved in Japan [149]. On the basis of these recent observations and anamorelin's positive effects, it appears to hold promise as a future treatment for cachexia and sarcopenia. Further research is warranted to explore the potential of anamorelin in enhancing muscle strength and physical function.

Myostatin Antagonists

Myostatin is currently classified among the myokines and is primarily expressed in adult skeletal muscle [150]. It plays a crucial role as a negative regulator of muscle mass by inhibiting skeletal muscle differentiation and growth. Activation of the activin receptor IIB (ActRIIB) pathway by myostatin can lead to muscle atrophy [151], whereas blocking this pathway induces muscle hypertrophy and enhances muscle strength and physical performance [151]. In a mouse model of cachexia, antagonizing ActRIIB significantly prolonged survival, not only preventing muscle wasting but actually inducing net muscle growth, without notable effects on fat mass or tumor growth [152]. Bimagrumab is a monoclonal antibody directed against ActRII, thereby inhibiting myostatin and activin activity on skeletal muscle myoblasts and myotubes through ActRII blockade [153]. High‐level evidence demonstrates that higher‐dose bimagrumab (3 and 10 mg/kg) can increase muscle mass, with effects maintained for up to 52 weeks [154]. When administered as a single 30 mg/kg intravenous dose in patients with muscle‐related diseases, bimagrumab increased muscle mass at 8 weeks compared to controls, and showed a trend toward improvements in strength and function over the ensuing 24 weeks [155]. Further studies are needed to determine the optimal bimagrumab dosage and duration, as well as its safety, efficacy, and the spectrum of diseases that may benefit from this intervention.

SARMs

Androgen therapy has been shown to increase muscle mass and strength [156]. Enobosarm is a nonsteroidal selective androgen receptor modulator (SARM) that exerts its effect by directly activating muscular androgen receptors, as well as via an indirect mechanism mediated by muscle fibroblasts and non‐muscular androgen receptor pathways, effectively preventing cachexia‐related muscle atrophy [156, 157]. Two randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled Phase II studies concluded that enobosarm increased total muscle mass and improved the physical function of older men, postmenopausal women, and cancer patients with cachexia (muscle loss), while maintaining a good safety profile [158, 159]. Enobosarm potentially represents a novel and promising agent that targets signaling pathways involved in muscle growth and regeneration for the treatment of cachexia and sarcopenia; however, additional trials remain necessary.

In summary, based on cachexia models, emerging drugs show potential in the treatment of sarcopenia. Current research on these drugs focuses on regulating cellular signaling pathways and endocrine axes, though some underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Additionally, no direct evidence exists to confirm their effectiveness in elderly populations with hip fractures. Future studies should further investigate the pathophysiological network of sarcopenia to develop multidimensional precision interventions and evaluate these drugs in elderly hip fracture patients.

Current Challenges and Future Directions

Some Challenges Faced

Significant variation exists at the national level in the implementation of healthcare services, attributable to differences in the structures of healthcare systems across countries [160]. A large‐scale prospective study indicated that the total direct cost per hip fracture patient is estimated at US$62,327 or €46,029. Hospital care accounts for the largest share (48.5%) of total costs, followed by community care (40.9%) and care in residential facilities (8.1%) [161]. Owing to the high rates of hospital admission and subsequent transfers to rehabilitation units for hip fracture patients, the overall cost per patient is substantial, highlighting the need for innovative clinical solutions that shorten hospital stays and reduce associated inpatient expenses [161].

Given the variability in healthcare systems, diagnostic standards, insurance policies, and healthcare providers' understanding of the disease, integrating complex multimodal interventions into routine clinical practice remains a challenge. Although a few studies have investigated quality control in clinical and nursing pathways for hip fractures, systematically extending rehabilitation plans from the hospital to nursing homes and patients' homes has shown limited efficacy—particularly in ensuring adherence to rehabilitation and nutritional plans during transition phases and after discharge. It is crucial to find ways to involve relevant professionals in continuous patient follow‐up and develop comprehensive care pathways for older adults with hip fractures to improve their outcomes.

Considering the global demographic shift toward an aging population, the prevalence of the very old (over 80 years of age) is notably rising. This demographic often presents with: (1) limited or absent self‐care abilities; (2) multiple comorbidities, frequently accompanied by cognitive or consciousness impairments; (3) significant reliance on family or social services for medical care and daily living support; (4) a high likelihood of living alone. Currently, evidence on individualized treatment protocols, appropriate dosages, tolerability, and safety for very old hip fracture patients remains insufficient. Developing individualized interventions with a high safety margin for this population will be an important objective of future research.

Some Prospects for the Future

In chronic diseases that require long‐term follow‐up—such as neuromuscular disorders—home‐based remote monitoring and/or remote assistance can furnish accurate and reliable patient data, thereby potentially improving clinical outcomes. This approach holds considerable promise [162]. Telemedicine and telerehabilitation overcome temporal and spatial limitations, enabling physicians to monitor patients' rehabilitation status anytime and anywhere while providing treatment based on individual conditions. A study demonstrated that hip surgery patients undergoing telemedicine and telerehabilitation exhibited better hip function and significantly lower complication and anxiety levels compared to the telephone follow‐up group [163, 164]. During a study on postoperative rehabilitation in elderly women with hip fractures, the outbreak of COVID‐19 forced researchers to adapt follow‐up methods, replacing in‐person treatment and follow‐ups at rehabilitation centers with video technology and phone consultations. Despite this adjustment, the study achieved favorable outcomes and maintained fidelity [165]. Multiple studies have shown that, across various disease contexts, remote monitoring systems are efficient, reduce hospitalization rates, and exhibit good tolerability. While they can be challenging to operate, patient satisfaction with such services is generally high [166, 167, 168].

Telerehabilitation involves delivering a range of rehabilitation services—including physical therapy and specialized therapeutic interventions—through remote communication technologies to patients who must remain at home, obviating the need for in‐person therapist visits. Several RCTs have demonstrated that telerehabilitation can yield outcomes comparable to conventional therapies for restoring motor function [169]. Moreover, this strategy expands access to high‐quality care for individuals in remote areas and reduces costs for both public health systems and patients [170].

The judicious use of telemedicine may enhance monitoring and management of chronic and acute conditions in older populations by enabling early, prehospital intervention and driving transformation in healthcare and business models [171]. As our understanding of hip fractures in the elderly continues to evolve, medical approaches can no longer remain narrowly focused on surgery alone, neglecting postoperative quality of life, overall function, and health status. Instead, indicators such as improvements in handgrip strength and gait speed—both closely tied to daily activity—should serve as key efficacy measures in efforts to “reverse debilitation” after hip fracture.

With the continuous advancement of medical technologies and the global trend of population aging, alongside variations in ethnicity and lifestyle across countries and regions, the limitations of a one‐size‐fits‐all treatment approach have become increasingly evident. Geriatric hip fractures should no longer be regarded as isolated orthopedic injuries; instead, they should be recognized as a manifestation of systemic functional decline. Recent studies have shifted focus toward enhancing musculoskeletal reserves and delaying biological aging. However, early identification of debilitation in geriatric hip fracture patients by monitoring changes in certain biomarkers lacks guideline‐level evidence currently, particularly at the biomolecular and signaling pathway levels.

Artificial intelligence (AI) holds significant promise in assisting the diagnosis of hip fractures. Its diagnostic accuracy rivals that of radiologists and orthopedic surgeons. With AI support, the processing and interpretation of medical images can be expedited, effectively reducing preoperative preparation time, shortening hospital stays, and lowering mortality rates [172]. However, due to limitations in data volume and computational methods, AI's current capacity for predicting disease progression appears comparable to traditional statistical methods. Future research should focus on building large‐scale, accurate databases, developing advanced computational methods, and conducting clinical validation studies.

For the treatment of unstable intertrochanteric femoral fractures in older adults, Proximal Femoral Nail Antirotation (PFNA) is the preferred surgical method. Evidence suggests that robot‐assisted PFNA surgery offers distinct advantages over conventional PFNA surgery in terms of surgical quality, lower surgical trauma, and better postoperative functional recovery [173]. However, robot‐assisted surgeries are associated with longer preoperative preparation and anesthesia times compared to traditional approaches. Furthermore, the high costs associated with surgical robots limit their accessibility and widespread adoption. Policies regarding the integration of surgical robotic systems into current healthcare models require further exploration.
