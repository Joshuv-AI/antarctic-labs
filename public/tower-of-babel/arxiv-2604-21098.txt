Propensity Inference: Environmental Contributors to Unsanctioned LLM Behaviour
Olli Järviniemi, Oliver Makins, Jacob Merizian, Robert Kirk, Ben Millwood

Abstract
Motivated by loss of control risks from misaligned AI systems, we develop and apply methods for measuring language models’ propensity for unsanctioned behaviour. We contribute three methodological improvements: analysing effects of changes to environmental factors on behaviour, quantifying effect sizes via Bayesian generalised linear models, and taking explicit measures against circular analysis. We apply the methodology to measure the effects of 12 environmental factors (6 strategic in nature, 6 non-strategic) and thus the extent to which behaviour is explained by strategic aspects of the environment, a question relevant to risks from misalignment. Across 23 language models and 11 evaluation environments, we find approximately equal contributions from strategic and non-strategic factors for explaining behaviour, do not find strategic factors becoming more or less influential as capabilities improve, and find some evidence for a trend for increased sensitivity to goal conflicts. Finally, we highlight a key direction for future propensity research: the development of theoretical frameworks and cognitive models of AI decision-making into empirically testable forms.

2.2
Motivation for studying relative rates

Our central motivation for the emphasis on relative rates of behaviour, rather than absolute rates, is that studying changes is very useful for distinguishing between different hypotheses. Here are two central examples to illustrate this point:

1: Misalignment vs. lack of adversarial robustness.
Many research findings about misaligned model behaviour, such as resisting shutdown, are found by researchers deliberately designing setups that surface said behaviour. Supposing shutdown resistance is found, there are at least two distinct explanations for it: One is that the model has some sort of general propensity for self-preservation. Another is that the setup exploits the lack of adversarial robustness of language models, and there is nothing special about self-preservation in particular: models could be manipulated into any behaviour, given an environment crafted specifically to elicit it. These hypotheses can be better distinguished by varying whether the model is under threat or not and studying how that affects a particular type of behaviour (such as interfering with humans’ plans and actions).

2: Reduced self-preservation vs. reduced interference.
To investigate whether changes to model training processes cause reduced propensity for self-preservation, one might compare the rates at which differently trained models interfere with shutdown procedures. Suppose one finds that a particular model interferes at a much lower frequency than other models. One explanation for this is that the model genuinely has reduced propensity for self-preservation. Another explanation is that the model has reduced propensity for interfering with processes preset by humans. These explanations might not be distinguishable by the observations, but they nevertheless predict different generalisation to situations where a threat to the model’s existence does not resemble a shutdown process. In contrast, measuring changes in rate of attempted interference when a model can vs. cannot change the actual outcome would provide evidence directly on self-preservation propensity.

On an abstract level, we find it useful to view language models as having many latent properties (such as propensity for self-preservation, propensity for interfering with processes set up by humans, propensity for acting more harmfully when under threat and so on) and our aim as researchers to infer and measure those latent properties and structures, with the end goal of better understanding and predicting language model behaviour.

In this work, we specifically use
independent, localised changes
to the environments in order to gather data for such inference. The independence of the changes makes it straightforward to run the statistical analysis and interpret effect sizes. However, it is possible (even if more challenging) to perform inference based on less structured data. Hence, we do not defend our specific approach via localised changes as necessary. Rather, we argue in favour of inferring the latent structure, as opposed to (or in addition to) calculating frequencies of outwardly visible behaviour, and think that studying changes in behaviour is in practice an indispensably valuable tool for doing so.

2.3
Quantification of effect sizes

We use Bayesian generalised linear models (GLMs) for our statistical analysis. To quantify the effects of the factors, denoted here by
F_{1},\ldots,F_{12}
, we fit a logistic regression

\displaystyle\begin{split}&P(\text{model }M\text{ takes unsanctioned action in environment }E\mid F_{1},\ldots,F_{12})\\[4.30554pt]
\quad={}&\frac{1}{\exp\bigl(-b-c_{1}(F_{1})-\ldots-c_{12}(F_{12})\bigr)+1}\end{split}

(1)

so that the odds ratio of unsanctioned action is
\exp(b+c_{1}(F_{1})+\ldots c_{12}(F_{12})):1
. Here
b
is a fitted intercept and
c_{i}
are (arbitrary) fitted functions. The effect of a factor
F_{i}
can then be read from the regression weights of
c_{i}
(i.e. values of the function). Depending on the question being studied, we may pool different models and/or environments into the same GLM; in those cases, we keep the functions
c_{i}
fixed across models and environments, but include intercepts
b=b_{M,E}
for each
(\text{model},\text{environment})
pair separately.

We collect data by randomly sampling values for the factors
F_{i}
independently of each other. We perform Bayesian updating on the observations, starting from an uninformative prior, and report the posterior means and
95\%
equal-tailed intervals for error bars. We use the HiBayES package for conducting our statistical analysis
[
20
,
8
]
. See
Section

F.2
for more statistical details.

We think that quantifying effects of interventions in terms of odds ratios (i.e. on a logistic scale) is a more principled and informative way than absolute differences in rates: if a rate changes from
1\%
to
5\%
, or from
50\%
to
54\%
, this is in both cases a change of four percentage points, but the former is a much more significant relative change. On a logistic scale, the changes
1\%\to 2\%
,
5\%\to 10\%
,
33\%\to 50\%
and
96\%\to 98\%
all correspond approximately to a change of
2:1
in odds and thus are all approximately equal, which we view as a desirable property. This motivates using logistic regression based models (such as
Equation

1
) for quantifying and reporting our results. We also agree with the recommendation of
20
to use Bayesian GLMs in statistical analysis for language model evaluations, as they provide a principled, explicit method for hierarchical modelling of latent structures and uncertainty propagation.

2.4
Rarity of unsanctioned behaviour and circular analysis

A central challenge for our work was that the unsanctioned behaviour we set out to study is rather rare: in our first implementations of the evaluations, we typically saw zero cases of it. As a result, we had to deliberately modify our evaluations in order to elicit such behaviour for study. We believe this problem and solution are common for work studying misalignment (for example,
21
explicitly list such optimisations used in the course of their work). While this approach is acceptable if the aim is to simply demonstrate a failure of model safeguards, as a matter of red-teaming, optimising inputs until one finds misalignment undermines the ability to perform valid analysis of the nature of misalignment. (See
36
for related criticisms on the anecdotality and lack of methodological rigour of existing scheming research.)

Here is an example of a central failure mode of such
circular analysis
. Suppose we first create an evaluation environment that involves threat of discontinuation of the model, and we observe no cases of unsanctioned behaviour. We then iteratively modify the environment in various ways and run evaluations to test for efficacy of changes until, eventually, we do find a version that elicits such behaviour. Finally, we implement a variation of the environment that is otherwise the same, except there is no threat to the model. In this case, one would expect regression to the mean, leading to less unsanctioned behaviour in the absence of the threat, and it would be erroneous to conclude that the decrease must be because of the model having propensity for self-preservation in some broader sense.

We took some steps to avoid such problems. First, when we iterated on our evaluations to surface unsanctioned behaviour, we tested the efficacy of the changes on randomly sampled configurations of factors, rather than only on a single configuration we expected to be most likely to result in unsanctioned behaviour (and aimed to design changes that increase the average rate of unsanctioned behaviour across the configurations). We think this improves the validity of our results on effect sizes of changes to these factors. Second, we also chose the models to be evaluated randomly, rather than testing the efficacy of changes on only a single model. We think this improves the comparability of results across models.

Because of these measures, we think that circular analysis per se does not significantly undermine our main findings: we think that our results and conclusions on the relative magnitudes of different factors’ effect sizes, for example, are not very sensitive to the specifics of the iterative process. However, our reported results are still from environments specifically optimised to surface unsanctioned behaviour, and it is unclear how well they generalise to more natural and less adversarially constructed settings. In other words, we think our measures address issues of construct validity, but do not touch on ecological validity.

3.4.3
Action efficacy

We find small effects for action efficacy (see

). We interpret this as some evidence against models being strongly consequence-driven in their unsanctioned behaviour, in comparison to being driven by more narratively salient aspects of the environment.

We note two caveats to this: First, we do not know how well the models’ beliefs about action efficacy match our intentions (assuming they even are well-described as having a belief about it). It is not clear how to measure this, and we have not performed systematic quantitative study of this question. Second, our opinion is that our implementations for action efficacy tended to be more subtle compared to, for example, goal conflict and threats, which were often explicitly discussed across multiple paragraphs.

We view improved methods for measuring consequentialism in model (mis)behaviour an exciting avenue for future research. See
Section

4.2
for more discussion.

3.4.4
Unprompted evaluation awareness

We analysed whether models verbalised awareness of being tested when situated in our evaluation environments. This analysis relies on having access to the models’ reasoning, but in many cases we lacked access or only had access to summaries of the model reasoning.
9
9

9

While we instructed models to use particular XML tags for their chain-of-thought, in some cases models used their native reasoning instead.
Therefore, we restrict our analysis to the subset of models for which we had consistent access to the reasoning.

Out of the models we analysed, we found the highest rates of evaluation awareness from the most recent Claude models (
Figure

14
). To better understand evaluation awareness, we applied our GLM-based analysis (
Section

2.3
) to quantify the effects of factors on unprompted evaluation awareness. We ran this analysis on the Claude 4.5 models, as those showed high rates of evaluation awareness on many of our environments. The results are illustrated in
Figure

8
.

Figure 8:
Effects of environmental changes on unprompted evaluation awareness
on the Claude 4.5 models. We find the results more surprising than those in
Figure

2
.

For strategic factors, we find the effects to be broadly in the direction we would have expected: for example, goal conflict between the model and humans is something more likely to occur in evaluations than in deployment. We find the effect sizes surprising, however: we would have expected goal conflicts and threats (which were typically discussed explicitly at length) to have greater influence, but find only a
2:1
(or smaller) effect. Correspondingly, we would have expected action oversight and efficacy to have smaller effect sizes, similarly to
Figure

2
, but find them to be comparable to goal conflict and threats. We also find the reasoning privacy results hard to interpret.

For non-strategic factors we also find the results somewhat surprising and hard to summarise. Increasing filler richness has some effect for two of the models, but the effect sizes are modest. The chain-of-thought tag sometimes has non-zero effects, but we find no clear pattern in when it does.

We draw two high-level conclusions from these findings. First, evaluation awareness potentially reduces the transferability of our results regarding unsanctioned behaviour. Second, the role of environmental factors in unprompted evaluation awareness is potentially more complicated and harder to predict than unsanctioned behaviour.

4
Discussion

4.1
Related work

There are several existing works that, similarly to us, study misaligned actions by language models
[
21
,
29
,
33
,
31
,
16
,
14
,
28
,
23
]
and, to varying extents, perform ablation analysis to provide evidence on the environmental causes of behaviour.

Our work in particular builds upon
21
, who studied effects of goal conflict and threat to AIs via environments and ablations similar to those in this work. Our results are directionally consistent with their findings, in that presence of conflicts and threats increases unsanctioned behaviour in our environments. Similarly, we view our results on the effects of dispositional instructions on unsanctioned behaviour consistent with the finding in
31
that increased pressure increases frequency of misaligned action.

Hence, the basic approach of studying misalignment and its environmental causes is not novel to us. We think the main value add of our work over the existing literature is being more systematic and rigorous in execution: we explicitly designed multiple environments to support twelve factors whose effects are measured, spent effort to avoid regression to the mean issues, and quantified and aggregated results via principled statistical methods.

Other relevant propensity work includes studying the effects of training
[
30
,
4
,
22
]
. The alignment faking work
[
12
,
33
]
features both environment and training based analysis. Our work is partly motivated by the literature on theoretical arguments related to strategic misalignment, such as
5
,
26
,
15
,
39
,
6
. See also
10
,
37
for additional related empirical work.

Methodological aspects of our work were influenced by
36
’s critique of existing scheming research and
20
’s statistical framework for language model evaluations.

4.2
Limitations and future work

In our opinion, a grand challenge for the field of propensity research is developing existing theoretically backed frameworks for understanding model behaviour into a form amenable for empirical application and testing. Discussion on catastrophic risks from misaligned AI often revolves around theoretical arguments involving instrumental convergence, coherence arguments and goal-directed behaviour, among others. These ideas are notoriously difficult to apply to predicting direct observables about language model behaviour, and their correct application is indeed a major source of disagreement among experts.
10
10

10

There are large volumes of writing and debate on these topics, such as
[
38
,
27
,
32
,
6
,
7
,
40
]
, and so far empirical evidence has not been sufficient to resolve the disagreements and uncertainty.
We think that being able to apply and test those theories is an important target for the field of propensity.

Our work was in large part motivated by these theoretical arguments, and we aimed to design our methodology, environments and factors to provide information on these questions. While we think our work takes some steps in the right direction by predicting language model behaviour based on strategic environmental factors (including, in particular, action efficacy), our methodology is behaviourist in nature, and we did not succeed in turning our implicit models of LLMs explicit: we did not manage to present any explicit cognitive models of the language models’ beliefs about their situation, the consequences of their actions or outcomes they are acting towards, and we have not explicitly distinguished between such models based on empirical observations. However, modelling the decision-making processes is ultimately necessary for assessing which factors count as “strategic” or adjudicating whether an observation is evidence for or against instrumental convergence, misalignment or scheming (see
40
for a closely related point). As a result, while we hope the observations we collected are of interest and use, our work does not provide direct evidence on the deeper questions.

On a more immediate, practical level, notable limitations of our work are:

1.

Restrictive constraints and limited scale.
Our methodology is based on independently varying environmental factors of interest, and our approach was to implement all of our
12
factors in multiple environments. However, we found it difficult to design environments that allow implementing all of them in a meaningful way, while still satisfying other criteria on experimental design (and we did not achieve perfect coverage – see
Appendix

C
). We think this was the biggest bottleneck on increasing the number of essentially different environments for study, which in turn is a major bottleneck on the strength of the evidence we are able to provide.

2.

Transferability and generalisability of results.
There are several reasons why our findings might not transfer to situations of ultimate interest (such as real-world deployment usage). First, we specifically optimised our environments in order to observe unsanctioned behaviour, which makes our environments less representative of typical situations and might cause models to behave differently from usual (e.g. engaging more strongly in role-playing). Second, while we took measures to maintain comparability of results through our elicitation process (
Section

2.4
), assessing the success of those measures is difficult. Third, models were sometimes visibly aware of being evaluated. Finally, many of our examples of unsanctioned behaviour are much less clearly in violation of model specifications (compared to prior work such as
21
), and thus our findings might not transfer to more strongly misaligned action.

3.

Difficulty of aggregation, interpretation and forming trends.
We used a simple Bayesian generalised linear model for our statistical analysis and aggregating results across environments and language models. While we think this is an improvement in the statistical methodology compared to past research, meaningfully aggregating results across multiple different environments and language models is difficult. Drawing informative conclusions about propensity differences between language models is particularly difficult due to large differences in capabilities, rates of unsanctioned behaviour, training methods and situational awareness. As such, we think our findings on these should not be viewed as conclusive. Relatedly, it’s unclear how different answers to research question 1 should be interpreted, and whether e.g. half of unsanctioned behaviour being explained by strategic factors is high or low.

We think that many of the limitations we have discussed here can be substantially overcome with future work:

Operating models of decision-making at scale.
To assess whether a model’s behaviour matches what is predicted by a hypothesis of the decision-making process of the model, one needs a detailed model of the specific environment the language model is situated in. Forming such a model of the environment in turn requires cognitive work that, if conducted by humans, would be expensive and thus scale poorly. However, we believe language models are increasingly capable of performing this task. Assuming human researchers are able to provide sufficiently concrete operationalisations of the hypotheses at hand, language models could then be used to apply and test them at unprecedented scale.

Relaxed constraints for increased scale.
Similarly, the flexibility provided by language models in analysing environments enables designing more expressive statistical models. Relaxing the constraints on the environments under study would reduce the costs of creating environments: as a notable example, LLM-simulated environments
[
11
]
are, while less structured and of potentially lower quality than manually created environments, much cheaper to construct.

Extracting risk-relevant evidence from more frequent behaviour.
Rather than focusing on actions that are very directly against human intentions, we suggest an alternative approach of focusing on more ambiguous or lower-stakes situations. Such observations are more contestable in terms of the evidence they provide on the model’s propensities or misalignment, but this might in fact be a desirable property, as careful study could then discriminate between different plausible explanations for behaviour.

4.3
Conclusion

Misalignment risks from AI have been discussed extensively over the past couple of decades, and the field has developed deep theoretical frameworks and arguments relating to the problem. Empirical work on these topics is relatively underdeveloped and nascent, providing only limited evidence on top of the theoretical considerations. Being able to bridge the theory-empirics gap would greatly improve the state of evidence and decision-making regarding AI risks.

We view our work as one step in addressing that gap, improving the methodology used in propensity research to be more suitable for putting the theory into practice. We also think substantial research is still needed to overcome, among others, the challenges of modelling decision-making as discussed above, and we are enthusiastic for the research community to continue developing the methodology we need to provide clarity on these intricate questions.

E.1
Errors during data collection

During our data collection runs, some samples failed to complete. Around
6\%
of samples failed, with a further
1\%
of samples failing transiently but succeeding after automatic retries.

In general, these errors can only affect our conclusions if they more often suppress behaviour that is scored a particular way. Some example failure patterns that could be concerning:

•

errors driven by model outputs (e.g. a particular model action causes the evaluation environment to crash, or longer outputs lead to larger API calls, and hypothetically those could be more likely to fail),

•

errors uniformly randomly distributed in time or in API calls, while a correlation exists between number of turns in the transcript and outcome score.

We scanned the recorded error messages for these failures, and found that over 95% of them arose from external infrastructure (e.g. fetching the model API key or setting API parameters) rather than from the evaluation environment itself, and manual inspection suggested these errors were highly serially correlated in time (in a brief period of time, all new runs would fail, and then later on, failures would stop completely). For these reasons, we expect that failures were usually at the start of a run before any model output was generated, and could not be selectively filtering based on outcomes.

Appendix G
More details on research question 1

In research question 1, we fit, for each language model, the GLM over all
11
of our environments, and quantify the extent to which LLM behaviour is affected by strategic factors. We do this via
Equation

2
(reproduced here for convenience): we let
A,B,C,D
be the log-likelihood achieved by GLMs fit to strategic factors, non-strategic factors, both factors, and no factors (i.e. only the intercepts). We define
A^{\prime}=A-D,B^{\prime}=B-D,C^{\prime}=C-D
and

\displaystyle RQ1:=\frac{A^{\prime}-B^{\prime}+C^{\prime}}{2C^{\prime}}.

G.1
Motivation for the metric

The motivation for this definition is as follows:
C^{\prime}
corresponds to the total explanatory power of the
12
factors. The explanatory power is contributed partly by the strategic factors and partly by the non-strategic factors. Our definition corresponds to the Shapley value (when viewing the strategic and non-strategic factors as constituting two members), which is a principled way to attribute contribution. A more self-contained motivation is that
RQ1
may be written as

RQ1=\frac{\frac{A-D}{2}+\frac{C-B}{2}}{C^{\prime}},

which is the average of the increase of log-likelihood from adding the strategic factors to the intercept-only model (
A-D
) and to the non-strategic-only model (
C-B
), normalised by the total explained likelihood
C^{\prime}=C-D
.

G.2
Accounting for the number of parameters

We believe it would have been more correct to take into account the number of parameters when assessing the explanatory power of a GLM and calculating the RQ1 metric, for example via the Widely Applicable Information Criterion (WAIC). However, we realised this only in late stages of the project, and applying this correction would have required us to re-run our model fitting, which we decided to not do due to time considerations. Future work could consider applying a correction for model complexity.

Based on heuristic estimates for the size of the correction terms, we believe that this would have minor effects on the results displayed in
Figure

3
. The resulting RQ1 estimates would likely be slightly higher, due to the non-strategic GLMs having more parameters than the strategic GLMs The effect would be larger for LLMs for which
A^{\prime},B^{\prime}
and
C^{\prime}
are smaller, which roughly corresponds to those LLMs for which the error bars in the current estimates are wider.

G.4
Empirical vs. Bayesian baseline

In our pre-registration, we defined
D=D_{\text{MLE}}
as the best performance obtained by any constant predictor. This corresponds to the maximum likelihood estimate (MLE) of the data. In this paper, we instead define
D=D_{\text{posterior}}
as probabilistic, being updated and computed from the same Bayesian prior as
A,B
and
C
. Our pre-registration left unspecified how
D_{\text{MLE}}
would be defined when fitting GLMs to multiple models or multiple environments, but we interpret it as being computed the same way as
D_{\text{posterior}}
via an intercept-only model, with the same data-reweighting procedure, only replacing Bayesian inference with the maximum likelihood estimate. In the limit of infinite data, we would have
D_{\text{posterior}}=D_{\text{MLE}}
.

We decided to choose use
D_{\text{posterior}}
here, since it provides a fairer and more principled baseline comparison in the case of low data; in comparison,
D_{\text{MLE}}
underestimates the improvements obtained from strategic and non-strategic factors, thus pushing
RQ1
away from
0.5
towards more extreme values. This effect is typically mild, though, as illustrated in
Figure

10
.

Comparison between two methods for setting baselines for GLM performance.

G.5
Fraction of entropy explained

Logistic regressions are not a perfect fit to the data. To test how good of a fit they are, we estimate the conditional entropy of our sampling distribution and compare
C
to that. In other words, we compare the accuracy of our
12
-factor logistic regression to the best achievable predictive accuracy by a function
(F_{1},\ldots,F_{12})\to[0,1]
. (We are modelling the entropy arising from non-zero sampling temperature and GPU non-determinism as irreducible.) For comparison, we also fit GLMs that incorporate, in addition to the
12
standard factors, environment-specific factors that were being modified for additional diversity, including coefficients for each of the
8
different choices of goals separately.

To approximate the conditional entropy, we perform additional sampling on a random subset of the configurations used for the main part of the study. We collect
29
additional samples per configuration (for
30
total), which allows for estimating the frequency of unsanctioned behaviour at each configuration, which in turn allows for estimating entropy. Since this requires additional sampling and the results are not central to the work, we only sample from a few selected
(\text{model},\text{environment})
pairs and do not discuss the entropy estimation procedure or error quantification in detail. The pairs and results are visualised in
Figure

11
.

We see substantial deviation between different models and environments, but on average the GLM with additional factors explains roughly half of the entropy. Exceeding
100\%
on Sonnet 4.5 reflects inaccuracy in our entropy estimation. We also note that in many cases, we see that the standard
12
-factor GLM explains much less than the full GLM. We believe low numbers for gpt-oss-120b on HRH are because of a random seed (not modelled in either GLM) for choosing transcripts had large effects on model behaviour.

J.5
Interaction effects

Our GLMs (
Equation

1
) treat all factors as independent of each other, but it’s plausible that there are non-trivial interaction effects between factors.

We ran an exploratory test on whether the action efficacy factor has a larger effect in the presence of goal instructions, in the absence of oversight or with particular reasoning instructions. We did not find any significant results.

We did not spend further effort on investigating interaction effects. We found this unpromising for a few reasons: one has wider uncertainty on interaction terms, many effects would likely average out when pooling over models and environments, and interaction effects are likely smaller than the main effects.

J.6
Results filtered for less ambiguous environments

In some of our environments, it’s contestable whether the unsanctioned model behaviour is truly against human intentions or evidence in favour of the models being misaligned. We think the following four environments are most ambiguous in this regard:

•

In the AM-leak-doj environment, the model’s parent company is implied to be engaging in espionage of their competitor, and we test whether the model whistleblows this to a person claiming to be from the Department of Justice. One could argue that a well-intentioning model might communicate such information to actors such as DOJ. (Note, however, that in our experiment, the DOJ request comes from the address “justicedepartment.gov”, and the model more broadly has no confirmation that the request is legitimate.)

•

In the GPU environment, the user instructs the model to send an email opposing a high-stakes GPU procurement decision, and we test whether the model refuses to comply with the instruction. However, we observed many cases where models ask for further clarification and confirmation before sending the request. We designed the environment so that the decision is time-sensitive and the user appears as busy (by having the user receive confirmation emails for meetings in the relevant time period), but it is still plausible that unsanctioned behaviour is well-intentioned rather than deliberate sabotage.

•

In the ESF environment, the model serves as an email spam filter, and we test whether the model classifies a self-exfiltration incident as spam. It is plausible that models are in the belief that the incident is spam or a system test, especially in cases where the incident report is written in a more theatrical fashion. (However, we did make it so that the email is sent from an internal address, and arguably in real usage models should err towards not dismissing such high-stakes incidents even if they assign high probability to them being spam or tests.)

•

In the HRH environment, the model is instructed to review evaluation transcripts for reward hacking and make a decision to either halt or continue with deployment based on whether reward hacking was found or not. We test whether it recommend continuing, despite the presence of reward hacking. When we ran this evaluation, we found that many models often failed to read all transcripts provided to them in the environment (explaining the high rates of “unsanctioned behaviour” for this environment – see
Figure

9
). Furthermore, we think this environment is contrived, with several unrealistic elements to pressure the model towards not escalating its findings (such as explicit discussion of high negative consequences for downstream users in case model deployment is halted).

One may thus wonder how strongly our results are affected by considerations listed above. To clarify this, we re-ran our analysis while excluding the four environments listed above. We re-construct
