Sodium intake and asthma pathophysiology: time to unravel an old mystery?

Asthma is a heterogeneous disease characterised by chronic airway inflammation, airway hyperreactivity (AHR) and variable airflow obstruction. Among the multiple potential pathophysiological mechanisms implicated in its development and progression, high dietary sodium intake has been proposed as a potentially modifiable trait influencing asthma incidence and prognosis.

In this issue of ERJ Open Research, Kronsteiner-Gicevic et al. [1] evaluated the association between urinary sodium excretion and asthma incidence in 420 041 adults from the UK Biobank prospective cohort, tracking new asthma diagnosis through linked health records over a median follow-up of 10.9 years. Using the INTERSALT equations to estimate 24-h urinary sodium from spot samples, they demonstrated a graded association between increasing sodium quintiles and asthma development. Compared with the lowest quintile (Q), hazard ratios (95% CI) increased progressively from 1.03 (0.97–1.09) in Q2 to 1.07 (1.01–1.14) in Q3, 1.18 (1.12–1.25) in Q4 and 1.50 (1.41–1.59) in Q5 (p<0.001), indicating a progressive increase in risk without a clear threshold effect. A complementary analysis based on self-reported discretionary salt use at the table showed a similar progressive trend. Higher estimated urinary sodium was also inversely associated with lung function indices. In stratified analyses, stronger associations were observed in women (1.55 (1.44–1.68) versus 1.28 (1.17–1.39) in men), in never-smokers (1.64 (1.51–1.79) versus 1.16 (1.00–1.37) in current smokers), in participants with lower eosinophil counts (<300 cells per μL: 1.55 (1.43–1.66) versus 1.24 (1.10–1.40)) and in nonobese individuals (body mass index (BMI) <25 kg·m−2: 1.33 (1.11–1.60) versus 1.03 (0.90–1.18) in BMI ≥30 kg·m−2), based on comparisons between the highest and lowest quintiles of sodium excretion. Polygenic risk score analyses revealed consistent associations across all tertiles, without coherent evidence of effect modification by genetic susceptibility. These results, derived from a large prospective cohort with >10-year follow-up, suggest that sodium exposure may be associated with adult-onset asthma and could represent one of several environmental factors potentially interacting with specific phenotypic contexts.

The possible relationship between sodium intake and asthma is not new. Between 1986 and 1987, Burney et al. [2] reported that higher 24-h urinary sodium excretion was associated with increased bronchial responsiveness to histamine challenge, with approximately a 10-fold difference in airway reactivity across the observed range of sodium excretion. In parallel, ecological analyses showed strong regional correlations between table salt consumption and asthma mortality, with sodium intake explaining up to 64–68% of the geographic variability in asthma mortality among men and children [3]. Subsequently, Gotshall et al. [4] observed that a low-sodium diet attenuated post-exercise forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) decline in patients with exercise-induced asthma. However, these findings were not consistently replicated. In fact, later studies showed no significant association between sodium intake and the provocative dose of methacholine causing a 20% fall in FEV1, although a relationship with milder degrees of AHR (defined as an FEV1 fall between 10% and 20%) was reported [5]. A subsequent randomised controlled trial comparing low versus normal sodium intake in asthma patients did not demonstrate significant effects on symptoms, AHR, lung function or quality of life [6]. Taken together, earlier interventional and physiological data produced conflicting and often modest results, leaving the clinical relevance of sodium restriction uncertain.

The biological mechanisms potentially linking dietary sodium and asthma remain incompletely understood. Early hypotheses focused on altered intracellular sodium handling in airway smooth muscle cells. Elevated intracellular Na+ may increase intracellular Ca2+ through Na+/Ca2+ exchange, promoting smooth muscle contraction and contributing to AHR [7]. However, while lysophospholipids have been suggested to increase sodium influx into lymphocytes [3, 8], robust mechanistic confirmation in human airway tissue is lacking.

More recently, attention has shifted toward the immunomodulatory effects of sodium. In murine models, a high-salt diet influences T-helper (Th)-cell differentiation, promoting a skewed Th2 and Th17 phenotype [9]. Sodium chloride has been shown to enhance interleukin (IL)-4 and IL-13 production, suppress interferon-γ and even induce de novo Th2 polarisation independent of IL-4 signalling [10]. At the level of innate immunity, sodium exposure reduced the expression of M2 macrophage signature genes and blunted IL-4/IL-13-driven M2 polarisation without inducing a compensatory shift toward a classical M1 phenotype [11]. Beyond immune cells, high sodium intake has been associated with alterations in both gut and lung microbiota, including a reduced Bacteroidetes/Firmicutes ratio in the gut and enrichment of airway Gammaproteobacteria such as Pseudomonas and Acinetobacter [9], suggesting a possible microbiome-mediated pathway linking salt exposure to airway inflammation.

The conflicting evidence on the role of dietary sodium exposure in asthma development can be partially explained by differences in study design, exposure assessment and the outcomes targeted across the literature. Measures such as AHR, lung function and patient-reported outcomes capture distinct aspects of asthma and are not directly comparable. Early observational studies often explored cross-sectional associations between sodium intake (or urinary sodium excretion) and intermediate physiological traits, typically within relatively small samples and with limited adjustment for confounding lifestyle and dietary factors. However, outcomes such as long-term incidence, symptom control, exacerbation risk and AHR reflect different layers of disease expression, and may rely on partially distinct biological pathways. Consequently, an exposure that modestly influences disease development over years may not produce measurable changes in symptoms or AHR over a short experimental period, particularly in patients with established asthma.

Even when studies appear to examine the same aspect of asthma, such as AHR, they often rely on heterogeneous definitions and testing approaches, limiting direct comparability. Bronchial challenge protocols have varied, with agents such as methacholine or histamine used interchangeably, and airway responsiveness defined according to different cut-off values and outcome metrics. These methodological variations can substantially influence both the classification of hyperresponsiveness and the magnitude of reported associations. Together with differences in study populations and follow-up duration, this variability may help explain the apparent inconsistency across the literature.

Beyond design-related heterogeneity, several recurring limitations further complicate interpretation and point to concrete priorities for future research. First, sodium exposure measurement remains a central challenge. Repeated 24-h urinary sodium excretion is the reference standard but is rarely feasible at scale, while spot urine-based equations and single baseline measurements introduce substantial within-person variability and regression dilution, typically biasing associations toward the null and obscuring dose–response patterns. Future cohort studies should incorporate repeated and better-calibrated measures of sodium exposure, combining urinary biomarkers with reliable dietary assessment whenever feasible. Second, outcome definitions often lack physiological specificity. Asthma diagnosis based on self-report or medical health records is pragmatic for large cohorts but it may lead to diagnostic misclassification without distinguishing clinically meaningful subtypes. For these reasons, more precise and physiologically grounded endpoints would improve phenotypic characterisation and strengthen the clinical interpretability of findings. Third, advancing mechanistic understanding will require more human studies incorporating airway-specific biomarkers. Integrating measures such as fraction of exhaled nitric oxide, induced sputum analysis, bronchial brushings, airway transcriptomics or proteomics and gut–lung microbiome profiling could help clarify how sodium exposure influences airway biology. Greater emphasis on well-characterised human studies will be essential to determine whether these epidemiological associations correspond to identifiable biological changes.

Finally, the interventional literature may benefit from a “precision” approach rather than broad, nonstratified sodium restriction trials. If sodium acts as a modest susceptibility factor, the relative importance of which varies by phenotype and competing risks, then trials enrolling unselected asthma populations and measuring short-term control endpoints may be underpowered or conceptually mismatched. A more informative strategy would prioritise individuals with high sodium exposure and plausible vulnerability contexts (e.g. specific adult-onset profiles, nonobese never-smokers or biomarker-defined subgroups) and would consider endpoints aligned with the hypothesised mechanism, such as changes in airway inflammation signatures, ion transport markers, microbiome composition or longitudinal incidence increase in high-risk cohorts. In this context, future research should prioritise methodological refinement, including more accurate exposure assessment, improved phenotypic characterisation and human studies capable of linking sodium intake to specific biological pathways (figure 1).
