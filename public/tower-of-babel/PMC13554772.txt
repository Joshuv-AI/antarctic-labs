Prognosis by Renal Function With or Without Percutaneous Coronary Intervention in Non–ST-Segment Elevation Myocardial Infarction
Authors: Yong Hoon Kim, Ae-Young Her, Seung-Woon Rha, Cheol Ung Choi, Byoung Geol Choi, Jung Rae Cho, Min-Woong Kim, Ji Young Park, Eun-Seok Shin, Ju Han Kim, Youngkeun Ahn, Hyo-Soo Kim, Myung Ho Jeong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRenal dysfunction increases adverse outcomes in non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), but evidence comparing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) with non-PCI by renal function is limited.ObjectivesThe authors aimed to compare 3-year outcomes of PCI versus non-PCI by baseline renal function in NSTEMI.MethodsWe stratified 15,255 NSTEMI patients by estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR; ≥90, 60-89, 30-59, and <30 mL/min/1.73 m2). Primary outcome was 3-year major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events (MACCE; defined as all-cause death, recurrent myocardial infarction, or stroke). Multivariable-adjusted Cox regression was performed. Median follow-up was 36.0 months (IQR: 36.0-36.0 months; 40,097 person-years).ResultsWorsening renal function was associated with higher MACCE and mortality (all P < 0.001), except between eGFR ≥90 and 60-89. Compared with non-PCI, PCI was associated with lower adjusted risks in the eGFR 60-89, 30-59, and <30 strata, respectively, regarding MACCE (HR [95% CI]: 0.58 [0.47-0.71]; 0.66 [0.53-0.82]; 0.60 [0.48-0.75]), all-cause mortality (HR [95% CI]: 0.47 [0.37-0.60]; 0.57 [0.45-0.72]; 0.59 [0.47-0.76]), and cardiovascular mortality (HR [95% CI]: 0.48 [0.34-0.66]; 0.53 [0.39-0.70]; 0.56 [0.41-0.75]) (all P < 0.001), but not in eGFR ≥90 MACCE (HR: 0.83 [95% CI: 0.64-1.07]; P = 0.158). Significant interaction was observed only for all-cause mortality (P = 0.044).ConclusionsIn NSTEMI, worsening renal function was associated with higher long-term adverse outcomes. PCI was associated with lower risks across most renal function strata, but not in eGFR ≥90.Key words: glomerular filtration rate, myocardial infarction, non–ST-segment elevation, treatment outcome

Non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), defined as the absence of ST-segment elevation on electrocardiography, occurs more frequently than ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI).1 NSTEMI is associated with poor clinical profiles and long-term outcomes, making it a major subgroup within the acute myocardial infarction (AMI) population.2 The use of invasive strategies for treating NSTEMI has increased over time.3 In a French registry study,4 80% of patients were treated with an early invasive strategy—routine early coronary angiography followed by percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) when indicated—which was associated with significantly lower 3-year mortality compared with conservative management (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 0.44; 95% CI: 0.35-0.55). Recent guidelines1,5 recommend an early invasive strategy in patients with high-risk non–ST-segment elevation acute coronary syndrome (NSTE-ACS). Nevertheless, many patients with NSTEMI are still managed without revascularization, and contemporary registries consistently show that non-PCI or medically treated patients have substantially worse short- and long-term outcomes than those treated invasively.3 Higher serum creatinine levels at presentation are strongly associated with a lower likelihood of early angiography, and patients with NSTEMI and chronic kidney disease (CKD) have consistently worse clinical outcomes than those with preserved renal function.6 However, despite these data, contemporary evidence directly comparing PCI and non-PCI strategies across the spectrum of baseline renal function in patients with NSTEMI remains limited. Therefore, we evaluated the 3-year clinical outcomes of PCI versus nonrevascularization according to the baseline renal function in patients with NSTEMI.

Methods

Study protocols

We conducted an individual patient-level pooled analysis using 2 nationwide AMI registries in Korea: the Korea Acute Myocardial Infarction Registry–National Institutes of Health (KAMIR-NIH)7 and the Korea Acute Myocardial Infarction Registry–V (KAMIR-V).8 KAMIR-NIH enrolled patients with AMI from 20 tertiary centers between November 2011 and December 2015, whereas KAMIR-V enrolled patients from 33 cardiovascular centers between January 2016 and June 2020. Both registries are nationwide, prospective, multicenter cohorts that consecutively enrolled all-comer patients aged ≥18 years presenting with AMI at tertiary university hospitals equipped with primary PCI capability throughout Korea. The detailed methodologies of these registries have been published previously.7,8 The KAMIR-NIH and KAMIR-V protocols were reviewed and approved by the institutional ethics committees of all participating centers, including the Institutional Review Board of Korea University Guro Hospital (#KUGH-MD11024 and 2016GRO740, respectively). All patients provided written informed consent, and both registries complied with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study population and definitions

Of the 29,625 patients in the combined registries (13,104 from KAMIR-NIH and 16,521 from KAMIR-V), those with unstable angina (n = 90, 0.3%), STEMI (n = 14,206, 48.0%), or those who underwent CABG during the 3-year follow-up period (n = 74, 0.2%) were excluded, yielding a final cohort of 15,255 patients with NSTEMI (Figure 1). NSTEMI was defined according to the Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction.9 Patients were treated with guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) according to contemporary recommendations, and the mode of revascularization (PCI or CABG) was determined by the treating clinicians based on baseline clinical characteristics, coronary anatomical complexity, and procedural or surgical risk.10,11 Patients who underwent CABG during the 3-year follow-up period (n = 74) were excluded from the main comparative analysis because the small number of CABG cases precluded a meaningful comparison. Accordingly, treatment comparison was limited to the PCI and non-PCI groups. After discharge, the patients were followed up at 6 and 12 months in the clinic or by telephone, with additional visits as clinically indicated. Data were collected using a secure web-based case report form by independent coordinators, and all suspected events were adjudicated by an independent committee.7

Flowchart of Patient Selection and eGFR-Based Group Classification

Patient selection from the pooled KAMIR-NIH and KAMIR-V registries. After excluding patients with unstable angina, STEMI, or CABG, 15,255 patients with NSTEMI were analyzed and divided into a PCI cohort (group A; n = 12,982) and a non-PCI cohort (group B; n = 2,273). Each cohort was further stratified into 4 groups according to baseline eGFR (≥90, 60-89, 30-59, and <30 mL/min/1.73 m2). CABG = coronary artery bypass grafting; eGFR = estimated glomerular filtration rate; KAMIR = Korea Acute Myocardial Infarction Registry; NIH = National Institute of Health; NSTEMI = non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction; PCI = percutaneous coronary intervention; STEMI = ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction.

Clinical outcomes

The primary outcome was the occurrence of major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events (MACCE) during the 3-year follow-up period. MACCE included all-cause mortality, recurrent myocardial infarction (MI), and stroke. The secondary outcomes included each MACCE component. Deaths without identifiable noncardiovascular causes were classified as cardiovascular deaths.12 Recurrent MI after discharge was defined as ischemic symptoms with supportive electrocardiography or imaging findings together with an elevation in the creatine kinase–myocardial band (CK-MB) above the upper normal limit or troponin I levels exceeding the 99th percentile of the upper reference limit.13 Periprocedural MI was not considered a clinical outcome in this study. Stroke was adjudicated in cases of new embolic, thrombotic, or hemorrhagic events leading to neurological deficits lasting ≥24 hours, or when acute cerebral infarction was verified on imaging modalities.14 PCI was considered successful when TIMI flow grade 3 was achieved in the infarct-related artery with <30% residual stenosis. Follow-up time was calculated from the date of hospital discharge (index hospitalization) to the date of the first occurrence of any MACCE component (all-cause death, recurrent MI, or stroke), or to 3 years (1,095 days) after discharge, whichever came first. Patients who did not experience any MACCE component within 3 years were censored at the 3-year timepoint. Patients who were lost to follow-up before the end of the 3-year observation period were censored at the date of last known contact. Periprocedural MI occurring during the index hospitalization was not counted as a clinical event, consistent with the prespecified outcome definition. The median follow-up duration was 36.0 months (IQR: 36.0-36.0 months) for all included participants, reflecting the fact that 82.0% of patients (n = 12,509 of 15,255) completed the full 3-year follow-up period. Among those with follow-up shorter than 36 months (n = 2,746; 18.0%), the median follow-up was 9.2 months (280 days), primarily attributable to all-cause mortality occurring before the end of the observation period. Notably, among patients with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <30 mL/min/1.73 m2, 45.1% (n = 518 of 1,145) had follow-up shorter than 36 months, consistent with the highest all-cause mortality rate of 34.7% observed in this subgroup. The total cumulative follow-up was 40,097 person-years.

Statistical analysis

A detailed description of the statistical analysis is provided in the Supplemental Methods. Categorical variables were compared across renal function groups using the chi-square or Fisher's exact test, and continuous variables were compared using analysis of variance or appropriate nonparametric tests. Results are presented as mean ± SD, median with interquartile ranges, or n (%), as appropriate. Cumulative event rates were estimated using Kaplan-Meier methods and compared with the log-rank test. HRs and 95% CIs were obtained from Cox proportional hazards models. Because baseline imbalances and potential confounding could influence outcomes, multivariable Cox regression models were constructed separately within the PCI and non-PCI groups and for the overall PCI versus non-PCI comparison.

Covariates for multivariable Cox regression models were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and established prognostic importance in patients with NSTEMI, as supported by the 2023 European Society of Cardiology guidelines for the management of acute coronary syndromes,5 independent of their statistical significance in baseline comparisons. The selected candidate covariates included male sex, age, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), heart rate, body mass index, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), hypertension, diabetes mellitus, previous MI, previous PCI, previous CABG, previous stroke, current smoker, Killip class II/III, cardiogenic shock, cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) on admission, symptom-to-door time, door-to-balloon time, peak CK-MB, peak troponin I, blood hemoglobin, blood glucose, total cholesterol, triglyceride, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (Supplemental Table 1). The covariates for the non-PCI group model, which were also applied in the PCI versus non-PCI comparison analysis, are detailed in the Supplemental Methods (Supplemental Table 2). Multicollinearity diagnostics15,16 were subsequently applied to confirm the independence of the selected covariates using variance inflation factors (VIFs), tolerance statistics, and condition indices; covariates with VIF >5, tolerance <0.1, or condition index >10 were considered indicative of substantial multicollinearity. The results are reported in Supplemental Tables 1 and 2, and the final covariate set was determined accordingly. The proportional-hazards assumption was formally tested for all final Cox regression models using Schoenfeld residual analysis, and no meaningful violations were identified. Missing data patterns were examined across all covariates (Supplemental Table 3). Little's MCAR test yielded a statistically significant result (chi-square = 1003.850, df = 367, P < 0.001), indicating that the data were not missing completely at random (MCAR). A missing indicator analysis further demonstrated that patients with missing values were older, had lower eGFR, and experienced higher mortality rates compared with those with complete data (all P < 0.001), consistent with a missing-at-random (MAR) mechanism. Multiple imputation with chained equations was therefore performed under the MAR assumption, which is supported by these formal assessments.17, 18, 19 A 48-hour landmark analysis was performed to address potential immortal time bias, whereby patients in the PCI group necessarily survived long enough to undergo the procedure. Patients who experienced any component of MACCE within 48 hours of admission were excluded (n = 150; 113 PCI and 37 non-PCI), and the time origin was reset to 48 hours; multivariable-adjusted Cox regression models identical to those used in the primary analysis were applied to the remaining cohort (n = 15,105; Supplemental Table 5). The potential influence of non-PCI group heterogeneity on the primary findings was further examined through 3 prespecified sensitivity analyses, sequentially excluding patients with myocardial infarction with nonobstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA) (n = 1,022), CABG-indicated patients (n = 1,009), and both subgroups combined (n = 2,031) from the non-PCI group, yielding analytic cohorts of n = 14,233, n = 14,246, and n = 13,224, respectively (Supplemental Tables 6 and 7A).

To assess the potential influence of between-center and intercohort heterogeneity, 2 additional sensitivity analyses were performed: a center-clustered Cox proportional hazards model with robust sandwich standard errors (47 unique participating centers across the 2 registries (6 centers contributed to both) as the clustering unit) and a cohort-stratified Cox model with stratification by registry cohort (KAMIR-NIH vs KAMIR-V). The results of both analyses were highly consistent with those of the primary multivariable Cox regression model across all outcomes and eGFR strata (Supplemental Table 7B). Two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. To assess whether the association between PCI and clinical outcomes varied across eGFR strata, multiplicative interaction analyses were performed by incorporating a product term between treatment strategy (PCI vs non-PCI) and eGFR category into the multivariable Cox proportional hazards model. The significance of the interaction was evaluated using the likelihood ratio test, and the corresponding P values for interaction are presented in Figure 2. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 20 (IBM Corp) and R version 3.6.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Baseline characteristics of PCI-treated patients

Of the 15,255 patients with NSTEMI, 12,982 (85.1%) were assigned to the PCI group (group A) and 2,273 (14.9%) to the non-PCI group (group B). Each group was further stratified into 4 baseline eGFR categories (≥90, 60-89, 30-59, and <30 mL/min/1.73 m2). Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of patients treated with PCI according to eGFR. As eGFR decreased, patients were older, less likely to be male, and more frequently had cardiovascular comorbidities, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, prior MI, PCI, CABG, or stroke (all P values for trend <0.001). Worsening renal function was also associated with a more severe clinical presentation, including a higher Killip class, higher rates of cardiogenic shock and CPR on admission, and lower LVEF (all P values for trend <0.001). Patients with advanced CKD more often had multivessel disease and left main involvement, and underwent PCI with longer door-to-balloon time, less frequent use of intravascular ultrasound and fractional flow reserve, and a higher rate of balloon angioplasty (all P values for trend <0.001). In addition, lower eGFR was associated with less frequent prescriptions of intensive GDMT at discharge, particularly potent P2Y12 inhibitors, beta-blockers, renin-angiotensin system inhibitors, and statins.

Baseline clinical, laboratory, angiographic, and procedural characteristics in the medical treatment group

Table 2 summarizes the baseline characteristics of the patients treated with medical therapy according to eGFR. As the eGFR decreased, the patients were older and had a higher burden of cardiovascular comorbidities, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and prior atherothrombotic events (all P values for trend <0.001). Lower eGFR was also associated with more advanced clinical instability, as reflected by a higher Killip class, higher rates of cardiogenic shock and CPR on admission, and lower LVEF (all P values for trend <0.001). Worsening renal function was further associated with lower hemoglobin levels, higher myocardial injury markers (peak CK-MB and peak troponin I), and less frequent prescription of statins (P < 0.05). Complementary comparisons between the PCI and non-PCI groups across renal function categories are shown in Supplemental Tables 8 and 9. Across all eGFR strata, patients in the PCI group had higher peak CK-MB and/or troponin I levels, whereas those in the non-PCI group more often had prior MI, CABG, and CPR on admission and were less likely to receive GDMT at discharge.

Clinical outcomes by eGFR in the PCI cohort

During the 3-year follow-up, the MACCE incidence increased progressively with worsening renal function among patients treated with PCI (log-rank P < 0.001) (Table 3). At 3 years, MACCE occurred in 6.1% (95% CI: 5.6-6.7, n = 391 of 6,379), 11.1% (95% CI: 10.2-12.1, n = 450 of 4,053), 25.8% (95% CI: 23.7-27.9, n = 430 of 1,668), and 38.0% (95% CI: 34.9-41.3, n = 335 of 882) of the patients in groups A1-A4, respectively. Mortality rates increased stepwise from group A1 to A4 (all-cause mortality: 3.4% [95% CI: 3.0-3.8, n = 215 of 6,379], 7.3% [95% CI: 6.5-8.1, n = 295 of 4,053], 20.0% [95% CI: 18.1-22.0, n = 333 of 1,668], and 31.3% [95% CI: 28.3-34.5, n = 276 of 882]; cardiovascular mortality: 1.6% [95% CI: 1.4-2.0, n = 104 of 6,379], 4.2% [95% CI: 3.6-4.8, n = 167 of 4,053], 12.5% [95% CI: 10.9-14.2, n = 202 of 1,668], and 20.4% [95% CI: 17.7-23.2, n = 169 of 882]). Recurrent MI rates were 1.9% (95% CI: 1.5-2.2, n = 116 of 6,379), 2.8% (95% CI: 2.4-3.4, n = 110 of 4,053), 5.8% (95% CI: 4.7-7.1, n = 86 of 1,668), and 9.7% (95% CI: 7.7-12.2, n = 69 of 882), and stroke rates were 1.4% (95% CI: 1.2-1.8, n = 90 of 6,379), 2.1% (95% CI: 1.7-2.5, n = 80 of 4,053), 4.1% (95% CI: 3.2-5.3, n = 61 of 1,668), and 3.3% (95% CI: 2.2-4.9, n = 24 of 882) in groups A1-A4, respectively. After multivariable adjustment, the MACCE risk was similar between groups A1 and A2, whereas group A1 had a significantly lower risk than groups A3 and A4 (both P < 0.001). A similar trend was observed for all-cause and cardiovascular mortality rates. Mortality rates increased stepwise from group A1 to A4, with adjusted risks comparable between groups A1 and A2 but significantly lower in group A1 than in groups A3 and A4 (all P values < 0.001). Group A1 also had a lower adjusted risk of recurrent MI than groups A3 and A4 (both P < 0.001), with no significant differences between groups A1 and A2 or between groups A3 and A4. Stroke risk was not independently associated with renal function after adjustment.

Clinical outcomes by eGFR in the non-PCI cohort

At 3 years, the incidence of MACCE increased stepwise with worsening renal function in the non-PCI group (Table 4) (9.4% [95% CI: 7.7-11.4, n = 91 of 972], 20.4% [95% CI: 17.5-23.8, n = 130 of 637], 37.2% [95% CI: 32.6-42.1, n = 149 of 401], and 53.6% [95% CI: 47.7-59.7, n = 141 of 263] in groups B1 to B4, respectively; log-rank P < 0.001). Reasons for non-PCI according to baseline renal function

Supplemental Table 10 summarizes the reasons for non-PCI management across renal function strata. Independent predictors of MACCE

In the multivariate Cox analyses, the independent predictors of MACCE differed partially between the PCI and non-PCI cohorts (Supplemental Table 11). Among the PCI-treated patients, worsening renal function was associated with stepwise increases in MACCE risk, with groups A3 and A4 showing higher adjusted risks than group A1. Additional predictors included older age (≥65 years), LVEF (<50%), Killip class II/III, cardiogenic shock, CPR on admission, previous PCI, multivessel disease, and nonuse of beta-blockers, renin-angiotensin system inhibitors, and statins. In the non-PCI cohort, groups B3 and B4 had a higher adjusted risk of MACCE than group B1. Other independent predictors included older age, reduced LVEF, cardiogenic shock, hypertension, previous MI, diabetes mellitus, and nonuse of statins at discharge.

Discussion

The main findings of this nationwide contemporary cohort of patients with NSTEMI are summarized as follows.

First, long-term outcomes showed a graded worsening with declining renal function in both the PCI and non-PCI cohorts, particularly for MACCE and mortality, with the greatest differences at lower eGFR levels (Central Illustration). By contrast, the associations between recurrent MI and stroke were not uniform across the renal function strata. Second, when PCI was compared with non-PCI within each renal-function category, PCI was associated with more favorable survival outcomes across most eGFR strata (60-89, 30-59, and <30 mL/min/1.73 m2), whereas outcomes were comparable in patients with eGFR ≥90 mL/min/1.73 m2. After multivariable adjustment, differences in recurrent MI were largely attenuated, whereas PCI remained associated with a lower risk of stroke in the overall cohort and in patients with an eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m2. Lastly, across both treatment groups, older age, reduced LVEF, Killip class II/III, cardiogenic shock, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and nonuse of statins at discharge were common independent predictors of MACCE.

(Left) Among 15,255 patients with NSTEMI, a PCI strategy was compared with medical therapy alone (non-PCI) within 4 baseline eGFR strata (≥90, 60-89, 30-59, and <30 mL/min/1.73 m2). (Top Right) In both cohorts, 3-year adverse event rates rose progressively as renal function declined. (Bottom Right) PCI was associated with lower 3-year MACCE and all-cause mortality than non-PCI across the impaired-function strata, but not in patients with preserved renal function (eGFR ≥90 mL/min/1.73 m2). These findings suggest that the survival benefit of an invasive PCI strategy in NSTEMI is greatest among patients with worse renal function, including advanced chronic kidney disease. CKD = chronic kidney disease; eGFR = estimated glomerular filtration rate; MACCE = major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events; NSTEMI = non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction; PCI = percutaneous coronary intervention.

Previous studies have shown that patients with NSTE-ACS and CKD are less likely to undergo invasive management or GDMT and experience worse outcomes; however, the benefits of invasive strategies in advanced CKD remain uncertain.6,20 Our study extends these data by examining a large, nationwide NSTEMI cohort with 3-year follow-up, stratifying renal function into 4 clinically meaningful eGFR categories, and directly comparing PCI versus non-PCI within each stratum, with a significant interaction for all-cause mortality. Furthermore, by systematically characterizing the reasons for non-PCI across eGFR strata, our study offers a more nuanced understanding of how treatment decisions are made in clinical practice, which may provide additional insights into the real-world treatment patterns in patients with NSTEMI and CKD.

Patients with CKD exhibit diffuse atherosclerosis, vascular calcification, endothelial dysfunction, and a chronic proinflammatory, prothrombotic milieu that contributes to increased ischemic risk and excess cardiovascular morbidity and mortality.21, 22, 23 Consistent with these mechanisms, declining eGFR in our cohort was associated with a greater comorbidity burden and more frequent hemodynamic instability (eg, Killip II/III, shock, and CPR on admission), lower LVEF and anemia in both treatment groups, more complex coronary anatomy, and less frequent use of intravascular ultrasound/fractional flow reserve in the PCI cohort (Tables 1 and 2). In a large nationwide NSTEMI registry, patients with CKD (defined as having serum creatinine level >200 μmol/L) had significantly higher 1-year mortality than those without CKD (aHR: 1.47; 95% CI: 1.44-1.51; P < 0.001).6 In another NSTEMI registry involving patients treated with PCI, lower renal function (eGFR: <60 mL/min/1.73 m2) was associated with significantly higher MACCE and mortality compared with preserved renal function, with P < 0.001 for mortality differences.24 In medically managed acute coronary syndrome, worsening CKD was associated with higher long-term ischemic risk, with advanced CKD (eGFR: <30 mL/min/1.73 m2) showing significantly higher event rates than preserved renal function (creatinine clearance: ≥60 mL/min).25 However, these studies assessed each group separately, and the definitions and severity classifications of CKD differed across studies, making direct comparisons between PCI-only and non-PCI cohorts difficult. By contrast, our study evaluated PCI- and non-PCI-treated patients from the same NSTEMI cohort using a uniform renal function framework, allowing for a direct and internally consistent comparison of eGFR-related risk gradients according to the treatment strategy.

Although early invasive management has been associated with lower mortality in patients with NSTEMI, the benefits are attenuated in patients with severe CKD. PCI has also been associated with lower mortality than medical therapy across the CKD strata.26,27 Our findings align with and extend these observations, demonstrating progressively worse outcomes with declining renal function and an association between PCI and lower risks of adverse outcomes across most eGFR categories, except in patients with eGFR ≥90 mL/min/1.73 m2 (Table 5 and Figure 2). In patients with eGFR ≥90 mL/min/1.73 m2, the lack of a significant difference between PCI and non-PCI may reflect the overall lower ischemic risk and greater clinical stability in this subgroup, as well as the inclusion of MINOCA (Supplemental Table 10), for which the incremental benefit of revascularization is likely to be smaller. This interpretation is consistent with the contemporary evidence that early invasive strategies provide the greatest benefit in high-risk patients with NSTE-ACS, whereas the benefit in lower-risk patients is less certain.5 In addition, although the proportion of patients with potential CABG indications increased as renal function worsened, only few patients ultimately underwent surgical revascularization (74 patients in total; 57 in the PCI group and 17 in the non-PCI group) based on a separate retrospective review of the excluded cases, which may partly explain the poorer outcomes observed in the non-PCI cohort.

Unlike mortality and MACCE, the associations between recurrent MI and stroke were not fully consistent across the renal function strata. Several factors may explain these findings. The absolute number of recurrent ischemic events was relatively small in some subgroups, which may have limited the statistical power to detect modest differences between the treatment strategies.28 Additionally, recurrent MI and stroke are influenced by multiple competing mechanisms, including medication adherence, residual atherosclerotic burden, and noncardiovascular comorbidities, which may not be directly modified by the initial revascularization strategy.29

Our findings are concordant with previous observational data showing that non-PCI management is more frequently performed in patients with greater comorbidities and hemodynamic or ischemic complexity.30 In our cohort, patients managed without PCI more commonly exhibited markers of higher clinical risk, such as prior MI, previous CABG, and CPR at presentation, and were less frequently prescribed GDMT at discharge, which is consistent with this selection pattern. However, our study extends the prior work by systematically characterizing the specific reasons for non-PCI according to renal function. Earlier studies inferred treatment selection from risk profiles, whereas we directly quantified the clinical rationale, including CABG indications, MINOCA presentations, refusal of PCI, and cases without coronary angiography, which varied meaningfully across eGFR categories (Supplemental Table 10).

Treatment selection has important prognostic implications in patients with NSTEMI and CKD, and the magnitude of the association between PCI and lower adverse event risk may be greater in this high-risk population. Our results highlight the importance of individualized decision-making that integrates renal function and overall clinical risk, rather than deferring revascularization solely based on CKD. The strengths of this study include its large nationwide real-world cohort and the stratified comparison of PCI and non-PCI across 4 clinically meaningful eGFR categories.

Study limitations

First, this study was observational and nonrandomized; accordingly, all findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal, and unmeasured confounders may have influenced the results. Because treatment allocation was not randomized, residual confounding or selection bias may have persisted despite multivariable adjustment. Second, data on contrast-associated acute kidney injury and periprocedural complications were incomplete; thus, the procedural risk-benefit balance of PCI could not be directly assessed. Serial creatinine measurements at standardized postprocedural time points were not uniformly captured across participating centers, precluding a reliable assessment of contrast-associated nephropathy—a particularly important safety consideration in patients with preexisting CKD. Regarding bleeding outcomes, a direct PCI vs non-PCI comparison was not feasible because antiplatelet strategies differed substantially between the 2 groups, making a fair comparison of bleeding risk infeasible. Instead, we examined bleeding incidence separately within each treatment group by eGFR stratum. In the PCI group, multivariable-adjusted analysis demonstrated that bleeding risk increased progressively with worsening renal function (aHR 2.37 for eGFR 30-59 and aHR 4.98 for eGFR <30, both P < 0.001 vs eGFR ≥90; Supplemental Table 12A). A similar trend was observed in the non-PCI group based on crude event rates (Supplemental Table 12B). These findings highlight the need for individualized risk-benefit assessment—weighing the ischemic benefit of PCI against the heightened bleeding risk—particularly in patients with severely reduced renal function. Future prospective studies specifically designed to capture net clinical benefit, incorporating both ischemic and safety endpoints, are warranted. Third, granular data on renal disease severity (eg, proteinuria and duration of CKD) were not available, which may have limited the refined risk stratification. Finally, although propensity score–based methods, including inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW), were considered, the minimal overlap across eGFR strata led to a substantial loss of analyzable subjects and unstable estimates, rendering these approaches impractical. Overlap weighting was also attempted; however, it had similar limitations. The minimal overlap in propensity score distributions between the PCI and non-PCI groups across eGFR strata (Supplemental Figure 2) indicates that the 2 groups represent fundamentally distinct patient populations, reflecting strong confounding by indication. Accordingly, the primary analysis relied on multivariable-adjusted Cox regression. Standardized mean differences for key baseline characteristics are provided in Supplemental Tables 8 and 9 to allow readers to directly assess the degree of preadjustment imbalance. Accordingly, residual confounding factors may persist despite statistical adjustments, and our findings should be interpreted with caution and validated in future prospective studies. Conclusions

In this nationwide NSTEMI cohort, declining renal function was associated with progressively worse long-term outcomes in both the PCI and non-PCI groups. Across most eGFR strata, PCI was associated with a lower risk of MACCE and mortality compared with non-PCI, whereas no benefit was observed in patients with preserved renal function. Thus, renal dysfunction could meaningfully modify the outcome differences between treatment strategies. Given the observational study design, these findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal. Further prospective evaluation is needed to refine patient selection for invasive management in this high-risk population.
