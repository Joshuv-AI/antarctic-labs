Generalized Inverses of Matrix Products: From Fundamental Subspaces to Randomized Decompositions
Michał P. Karpowicz, Gilbert Strang

Abstract
We investigate the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse and generalized inverse of a matrix product A = C ​ R A=CR to establish a unifying framework for generalized and randomized matrix inverses. This analysis is rooted in first principles, focusing on the geometry of the four fundamental subspaces. We examine: 1. The reverse order law, A + = R + ​ C + A^{+}=R^{+}C^{+} , which holds when C C has independent columns and R R has independent rows. 2. The universally correct formula, A + = ( C + ​ C ​ R ) + ​ ( C ​ R ​ R + ) + A^{+}=(C^{+}CR)^{+}(CRR^{+})^{+} , providing a geometric interpretation of the mappings between the involved subspaces. 3. A new generalized randomized formula, A p + = ( P T ​ A ) + ​ P T ​ A ​ Q ​ ( A ​ Q ) + A^{+}_{p}=(P^{T}A)^{+}P^{T}AQ(AQ)^{+} , which gives A p + = A + A^{+}_{p}=A^{+} if and only if the sketching matrices P P and Q Q preserve the rank of A A , i.e., rank ⁡ ( P T ​ A ) = rank ⁡ ( A ​ Q ) = rank ⁡ ( A ) \mathrm{rank}(P^{T}A)=\mathrm{rank}(AQ)=\mathrm{rank}(A) . The framework is extended to generalized { 1 , 2 } \{1,2\} -inverses and specialized forms, revealing the underlying structure of established randomized linear algebra algorithms, including randomized SVD, the Nyström approximation, and CUR decomposition. We demonstrate applications in sparse sensor placement and effective resistance estimation. For the latter, we provide a rigorous quantitative analysis of an approximation scheme, establishing that it always underestimates the true resistance and deriving a worst-case spectral bound on the error of resistance differences.

4.1
General solution to
A^{+}\boldsymbol{b}=\boldsymbol{x}

As a full-rank decomposition,
A=CR
leads to the following useful formula
[

,
12
]

A^{+}=R^{+}C^{+}=R^{\mathrm{T}}(RR^{\mathrm{T}})^{-1}(C^{\mathrm{T}}C)^{-1}C^{\mathrm{T}}=R^{\mathrm{T}}(C^{\mathrm{T}}AR^{\mathrm{T}})^{-1}C^{\mathrm{T}}.

(6)

After a short inspection we can notice that the formula shows the ranks of

and

are equal. With equal ranks, we then have
\hbox{{C}}(A^{+}A)=\hbox{{C}}(A^{+})
by Lemma

. See also Theorem 1.4.2 in
[

or
[

.

Consider now the following equation

A^{+}\boldsymbol{b}=\boldsymbol{x}.

For any given
\boldsymbol{x}
and arbitrary
\boldsymbol{w}
, the general solution
\boldsymbol{b}
to that equation is equal to

\boldsymbol{b}=A\boldsymbol{x}+(I_{m}-AA^{+})\boldsymbol{w}.

That is easy to see, since

satisfies the first and the second Penrose identity. For any
\boldsymbol{b}
in
\hbox{{C}}(A)
we recover the corresponding
\boldsymbol{x}=A^{+}\boldsymbol{b}=A^{+}A\boldsymbol{x}+(A-A^{+}AA^{+})\boldsymbol{w}=A^{+}A\boldsymbol{x}
in the row space
\hbox{{C}}(A^{T})
. The map
A
from the row space
\hbox{{C}}(A^{T})
to the column space
\hbox{{C}}(A)
is inverted by the pseudoinverse
A^{+}
. That is a wonderful and powerful property that holds when both the first and the second Penrose identity hold.

To see what happens when that is not the case, consider the following rank-deficient

A=\left[\begin{array}[]{rrr}1&0\\
0&0\\
0&0\\
\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{rrr}1\\

\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{rrr}1&0\\
\end{array}\right]=C_{0}R_{0}.

When we complete

[C_{0},C_{1}]
and

\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{rrr}R_{0}\\
R_{1}\\
\end{array}\right].

Given that decomposition of

, the first Penrose identity holds for any matrix defined by the following
generalized formula

\displaystyle A^{g}=\left[\begin{array}[]{rrr}R_{0}\\
R_{1}\\
\end{array}\right]^{-1}\left[\begin{array}[]{rrr}I_{r}&Z_{12}\\
Z_{21}&Z_{22}\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{rrr}C_{0}&C_{1}\\
\end{array}\right]^{-1}.

Matrix
A^{g}
is a
generalized inverse
or
1-inverse
(since the first identity holds) of

.

The second Penrose identity introduces additional constraints on how
A^{g}
is constructed. We demand more, so additional constraints are what we should expect. Since

A^{g}AA^{g}=\left[\begin{array}[]{rrr}R_{0}\\
R_{1}\\
\end{array}\right]^{-1}\left[\begin{array}[]{rrr}I_{r}&Z_{12}\\
Z_{21}&Z_{21}Z_{12}\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{rrr}C_{0}&C_{1}\\
\end{array}\right]^{-1},

the equation

holds only when

. But then, matrix
A
and its
1
-inverse

have equal ranks. Therefore, any
1
-inverse
A^{g}
of
A
of the same rank is also
\{1,2\}
-inverse of
A
. The pseudoinverse
A^{+}
of

is a special example, as we have already seen.

4.2
Generalized inverse fails to invert

5
The general formula randomized

It follows that the first Penrose identity only is enough to characterize the general solution of

A\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{b}.

The second Penrose identity is not required.

Given

\boldsymbol{x}=G\boldsymbol{b}+(I_{n}-GA)\boldsymbol{z},

we have
A\boldsymbol{x}=AG\boldsymbol{b}+(A-AA^{g}A)\boldsymbol{z}=AG\boldsymbol{b}=\boldsymbol{b}
for arbitrary
\boldsymbol{z}
and
\boldsymbol{b}\in\hbox{{C}}(A)
. That is summarized by the following famous result due to Penrose
[
7
,
1
]
:

Theorem 3
.

Given
A
,
\bar{C}
and
\bar{R}
, a necessary and sufficient condition for the equation
\bar{C}X\bar{R}=A
to have a solution
X
is that

\displaystyle(\bar{C}G)A(H\bar{R})=A,

(12)

where
G
is a generalized 1-inverse of
\bar{C}
and
H
is a generalized 1-inverse of
\bar{R}
, i.e.,
\bar{C}G\bar{C}=\bar{C}
and
\bar{R}H\bar{R}=\bar{R}
. Then

\displaystyle X=GAH+Z-(G\bar{C})Z(\bar{R}H),

(13)

where
Z
is arbitrary.

Given
A
,
\bar{C}
and
\bar{R}
, we construct two projections,
\bar{C}G
for the column space
\hbox{{C}}(A)
and
H\bar{R}
for the row space
\hbox{{C}}(A^{T})
. They make the linear matrix equation work for
X
and guarantee
\bar{C}X\bar{R}
equals exactly
A
. That also provides an elegant characterization of generalized 1-inverse
A^{g}
(see e.g.
[
1
]
). Consider

\displaystyle X=A^{g}+Z-(A^{g}A)Z(AA^{g}),

(14)

where
Z
is a random matrix. By the first Penrose identity, we immediately have:

\displaystyle AXA=AA^{g}A+AZA-AZA=AA^{g}A=A.

Any
X
defined by the randomized formula above is a 1-inverse of
A
. But that randomization is not the one we should be looking for.

5.1
Pseudoinverse randomized

In practical engineering contexts, great outcomes are achieved using low-rank randomized approximations such as Nyström or CUR decompositions and sketch-and-precondition techniques
[

,
17
,

]
. We believe this justifies the formal integration of randomization within the pseudoinverse formula to explore its randomized framework further. To do that, we can use Theorem
3
again.

Let
P
and

denote random sampling matrices. We can use them to construct the following
1
-inverses of
\bar{C}
and
\bar{R}
,

\displaystyle G_{P}=(P^{T}\bar{C})^{+}P^{T}\quad\text{and}\quad H_{Q}=Q(\bar{R}Q)^{+}.

(15)

It is easy to see from the properties of pseudoinverse, that
\bar{C}G_{P}\bar{C}=\bar{C}
and
\bar{R}H_{Q}\bar{R}=\bar{R}
under proper rank conditions. As a result we introduce oblique projectors,
\bar{C}G_{P}
and

. The first one projects onto the column space
\hbox{{C}}(A)
along the nullspace
\hbox{{N}}(P^{T})
. Similarly, the oblique projector

projects onto the row space
\hbox{{C}}(A^{T})
along the nullspace
\hbox{{N}}(Q)

We now conclude our observations with the following new result in Theorem

.

Theorem 4
.

The pseudoinverse
A^{+}
of a product
A=CR
is equal to

A^{+}=(P^{T}CR)^{+}P^{T}CRQ(CRQ)^{+},

(16)

if and only if
P
and
Q
satisfy the following rank-preservation condition:

\mathrm{rank}(P^{T}A)=\mathrm{rank}(AQ)=\mathrm{rank}(A).

(17)

Proof.

If
A\boldsymbol{x}=0
, then
P^{T}A\boldsymbol{x}=0
, so
\hbox{{N}}(A)\subset\hbox{{N}}(P^{T}A)
. When
\mathrm{rank}(P^{T}A)=\mathrm{rank}(AQ)=\mathrm{rank}(A)
for some
P
and
Q
, then

\dim(\hbox{{N}}(A))=n-\mathrm{rank}(A)\ \text{and}\ \dim(\hbox{{N}}(P^{T}A))=n-\mathrm{rank}(P^{T}A).

It follows that
\dim(\hbox{{N}}(A))=\dim(\hbox{{N}}(P^{T}A))
and, therefore,
\hbox{{N}}(A)=\hbox{{N}}(P^{T}A)
. That implies the orthogonal complements must also be equal, i.e.,
\hbox{{C}}(A^{T})=\hbox{{C}}((P^{T}A)^{T}).

Since the subspaces of
A^{T}
and
P^{T}A
and
A^{+}
are identical, the orthogonal projectors must be equal:

(P^{T}A)^{+}(P^{T}A)=A^{+}A.

(18)

By the same argument, we establish that

\hbox{{C}}(AQ)=\hbox{{C}}(A).

And, since
AA^{+}
and
AQ(AQ)^{+}
are the unique orthogonal projectors onto
\hbox{{C}}(A)
and
\hbox{{C}}(AQ)
, with identical subspaces, we conclude that:

AQ(AQ)^{+}=AA^{+}.

(19)

Then, if the rank preservation condition holds, we have:

\displaystyle(P^{T}A)^{+}P^{T}AQ(AQ)^{+}

\displaystyle=[(P^{T}A)^{+}P^{T}A]Q(AQ)^{+}=A^{+}AQ(AQ)^{+}

\displaystyle=A^{+}[AQ(AQ)^{+}]=A^{+}AA^{+}=A^{+}.

Now we show that the rank preservation follows from the equality
A^{+}=(P^{T}A)^{+}P^{T}AQ(AQ)^{+}
. We have

\displaystyle\mathrm{rank}(A)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{rank}(A^{+})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{rank}\left((P^{T}A)^{+}P^{T}AQ(AQ)^{+}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{rank}((P^{T}A)^{+})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{rank}(P^{T}A).

Since it is always true that
\mathrm{rank}(P^{T}A)\leq\mathrm{rank}(A)
, we must have
\mathrm{rank}(P^{T}A)=\mathrm{rank}(A)
. Similarly, since
\mathrm{rank}(AQ)\leq\mathrm{rank}(A)
, we must have
\mathrm{rank}(AQ)=\mathrm{rank}(A)
. Thus, we have:

\mathrm{rank}(P^{T}A)=\mathrm{rank}(AQ)=\mathrm{rank}(A),

as desired.
∎

The formula

A^{+}=(P^{T}CR)^{+}\,P^{T}CRQ\,(CRQ)^{+}

(20)

admits a natural interpretation in terms of randomized sketching. It introduces two sketching matrices, an

by

as a row sketch and

\ell_{Q}
matrix

as a column sketch. For a given

, the sketched matrices

are of sizes

by

and
m
by
\ell_{Q}
, and can be substantially smaller than

by

.

Theorem
4
shows that

) is exact if and only if the sketches preserve the rank of

. Thus, the sketched formula is an exact characterization of the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse under the rank preservation conditions (

\end{array}\right]\cdot\dfrac{1}{5}\left[\begin{array}[]{rrr}-3&4\\

\end{array}\right]=\dfrac{1}{15}\left[\begin{array}[]{rrr}-8&9\\

\end{array}\right].

5.3
Generalized formula generalized

Observe that the formula in Theorem
4
employs oblique projectors determined using pseudoinverses to guarantee that all four Penrose identities are satisfied for the reconstructed
A^{+}
. But we can as well use projectors that are established through generalized inverses to obtain a generalized
\{1,2\}
-inverse
A^{g}
for
A
. Let us verify that
AA^{g}A=A
.

Theorem 5
.

Consider an
m
by
p
matrix
P
and
n
by
q
matrix
Q
satisfying the following condition:

\mathrm{rank}(P^{T}A)=\mathrm{rank}(AQ)=\mathrm{rank}(A).

(21)

Then, for any
\{1\}
-inverse
(P^{T}A)^{g}
and
\{1\}
-inverse
(AQ)^{g}
,

A^{g}=(P^{T}A)^{g}P^{T}AQ(AQ)^{g}

(22)

is a generalized
\{1,2\}
-inverse of
A
.

Proof.

By assumption,
P^{T}A(P^{T}A)^{g}P^{T}A=P^{T}A
and
AQ(AQ)^{g}AQ=AQ
. Therefore,

P^{T}A(I-(P^{T}A)^{g}P^{T}A)=0\quad\text{and}\quad(I-AQ(AQ)^{g})AQ=0.

Since
\mathrm{rank}(P^{T}A)=\mathrm{rank}(AQ)=\mathrm{rank}(A)
, that implies
\hbox{{C}}(I-(P^{T}A)^{g}P^{T}A)\subset\hbox{{N}}(P^{T}A)=\hbox{{N}}(A)
. And, therefore,

A(P^{T}A)^{g}P^{T}A=A.

since
A(I-(P^{T}A)^{g}P^{T}A)=A-A(P^{T}A)^{g}P^{T}A=0.

Similarly, we have
\hbox{{C}}(AQ)=\hbox{{C}}(A)
, which implies

AQ(AQ)^{g}A=A.

We can now verify that the first and the second Penrose identity follows from the established properties of
\{1\}
-inverses.

The first identity is easy to see,

A(P^{T}A)^{g}(P^{T}A)Q(AQ)^{g}A=AQ(AQ)^{g}A=A.

The second identity is somewhat more hidden, but it is also true. For clarity of presentation, let
G_{P}=(P^{T}A)^{g}
and
G_{Q}=(AQ)^{g}
. By associativity:

\displaystyle A^{g}AA^{g}

\displaystyle=G_{P}P^{T}(AQG_{Q}A)G_{P}P^{T}AQG_{Q}.

Since
AQG_{Q}A=A
:

\displaystyle A^{g}AA^{g}

\displaystyle=G_{P}P^{T}AG_{P}P^{T}AQG_{Q}

\displaystyle=G_{P}P^{T}(AG_{P}P^{T}A)QG_{Q}.

And since
AG_{P}P^{T}A=A
:

\displaystyle A^{g}AA^{g}

\displaystyle=G_{P}P^{T}AQG_{Q}=A^{g}.

Therefore,

A^{g}=(P^{T}CR)^{g}P^{T}CRQ(CRQ)^{g}

is a generalized
\{1,2\}
-inverse of
A=CR
.
∎

We can now transform the general formula in Theorem
5
to the following compact formula in Theorem
6
below (also, compare that with Theorem 1.6.5 in
[
1
]
).

Theorem 6
.

Consider an
m
by
p
matrix
P
and
n
by
q
matrix
Q
satisfying the rank preservation condition:

\mathrm{rank}(P^{T}A)=\mathrm{rank}(AQ)=\mathrm{rank}(A).

(23)

Let
P^{T}AQ=CR
be a full rank factorization of the sketched matrix. Then

A^{g}=Q(CR)^{+}P^{T}

(24)

is a generalized
{
1,2
}
-inverse for
A
.

Proof.

Let
A=C_{A}R_{A}
be a full rank factorization of the
m
by
n
matrix
A
, where the
m
by
r
matrix
C_{A}
has full column rank
r
and the
r
by
n
matrix
R_{A}
has full row rank
r
. Substituting this into the sketch
P^{T}AQ
, we obtain the factorization:

\displaystyle CR=(P^{T}C_{A})(R_{A}Q)=KL.

The rank preservation conditions imply that these factors retain full rank. Since
\mathrm{rank}(P^{T}A)=r
, we see that
\mathrm{rank}(P^{T}C_{A}R_{A})=\mathrm{rank}(KR_{A})=\mathrm{rank}(K)=r
. And, similarly, since
\mathrm{rank}(AQ)=r
, we have
\mathrm{rank}(C_{A}R_{A}Q)=\mathrm{rank}(C_{A}L)=\mathrm{rank}(L)=r
.

Since
K=P^{T}C_{A}
has full column rank and
L=R_{A}Q
has full row rank, the product
KL
is a full rank factorization of the sketch
CR=P^{T}AQ
. Then, by Theorem
1
(the Reverse Order Law), the pseudoinverse is:

\displaystyle(CR)^{+}=(KL)^{+}=L^{+}K^{+}.

We can now verify the first two Penrose identities for
A^{g}=Q(CR)^{+}P^{T}=QL^{+}K^{+}P^{T}
.
The first identity yields:

\displaystyle AA^{g}A

\displaystyle=(C_{A}R_{A})Q(L^{+}K^{+})P^{T}(C_{A}R_{A})

\displaystyle=C_{A}(R_{A}Q)L^{+}K^{+}(P^{T}C_{A})R_{A}

\displaystyle=C_{A}(LL^{+})(K^{+}K)R_{A}.

Since
L
has full row rank,
LL^{+}=I_{r}
. Since
K
has full column rank,
K^{+}K=I_{r}
. Thus:

\displaystyle AA^{g}A=C_{A}I_{r}I_{r}R_{A}=C_{A}R_{A}=A.

The second identity yields:

\displaystyle A^{g}AA^{g}

\displaystyle=Q(L^{+}K^{+})P^{T}(C_{A}R_{A})Q(L^{+}K^{+})P^{T}

\displaystyle=QL^{+}K^{+}(P^{T}C_{A})(R_{A}Q)L^{+}K^{+}P^{T}

\displaystyle=QL^{+}(K^{+}K)(LL^{+})K^{+}P^{T}

\displaystyle=QL^{+}I_{r}I_{r}K^{+}P^{T}

\displaystyle=Q(L^{+}K^{+})P^{T}=A^{g}.

That shows we have constructed the generalized
\{1,2\}
-inverse of
A
.
∎

Contrary to formula (
16
) in Theorem
4
, formula (
24
) does not satisfy the symmetry conditions without additional assumptions. That means
AA^{g}=C_{A}K^{+}P^{T}
is generally not symmetric. However, if
P
and
Q
represent a rotation of bases, symmetry is restored.

Theorem 7
.

If
P
and
Q
are square orthogonal matrices (i.e.,
P^{T}P=I_{m}
and
Q^{T}Q=I_{n}
), then

A^{+}=Q(P^{T}AQ)^{+}P^{T}.

(25)

Proof.

Since
P
and
Q
are square and orthogonal,
P^{T}=P^{-1}
and
Q^{T}=Q^{-1}
. We can invert the sketching relation
CR=P^{T}AQ
to recover
A
:

\displaystyle A=P(CR)Q^{T}.

It now follows from the properties of pseudoinverse that

\displaystyle A^{+}=(P(CR)Q^{T})^{+}=(Q^{T})^{T}(CR)^{+}P^{T}=Q(CR)^{+}P^{T}.

∎

6
Examples and applications

In this section we study how low-rank
A
comes from low-rank
A^{+}
. First, we present basic implementations of formulas derived earlier. Then, we use them in several interesting examples.

By Theorem
4
, rank-preserving matrices
P
(
m
by
p
) and
Q
(
n
by
q
) reconstruct the pseudoinverse
A^{+}
(
n
by
m
) of
A
(
m
by
n
) in formula (
16
). That formula describes the following simple (or naive) algorithm:

% Randomized pseudoinverse approximation of A
[m,n] = size(A); P = randn(m,p); Q = randn(n,q);
PTA = P’*A; AQ = A*Q;
Arplus = pinv(PTA)*PTA*Q*pinv(AQ);

An improved version comes directly from Theorem
7
. One approach to implement it is to calculate the SVD of the easier-to-calculate sketched matrix
P^{T}AQ
and then take its inverse, as the following code illustrates:

% Randomized pseudoinverse approximation of A
[m,n] = size(A); P = orth(randn(m,p)); Q = orth(randn(n,q));
Arplus = Q*pinv(P’*A*Q)*P’;

When only generalized inverse is required, we can use the algorithm given by Theorem
5
.

% Randomized generalized 12-inverse approximation of A by Theorem 6
[m,n] = size(A); P = randn(m,p); Q = randn(n,q);
[Qbar,Rbar] = qr(P’*A*Q,0);
Arplus = (Q/Rbar)*(P*Qbar)’;

Introduction of
P
and
Q
allows us to see the general design patterns behind matrix decompositions of randomized linear algebra and of the pseudoinverse approximations they require, either directly or indirectly. To be more specific, let us consider the full-rank factorization:

\displaystyle AQ=CR,

where
Q
is random. When
R^{+}=R^{T}(RR^{T})^{-1}
, then Theorem
4
reveals the following pattern:

\displaystyle A^{+}\approx A^{+}_{p}=(P^{T}A)^{+}P^{T}CR(CR)^{+}=(P^{T}A)^{+}P^{T}CC^{+}.

(26)

Good algorithms come from the good choice of
P
and
Q
, and
C
and
R
.

The
randomized SVD
algorithm (see Halko and Tropp
[
4
]
) makes an excellent choice of
C
to encode the basis for the column space of
A
. First, it finds an orthogonal basis by calculating the QR decomposition:

\displaystyle AQ=\bar{Q}\bar{R},\quad\text{where}\quad\bar{Q}^{T}\bar{Q}=I.

Then it sets
P=\bar{Q}
to obtain

\displaystyle A^{+}\approx A^{+}_{p}

\displaystyle=(P^{T}A)^{+}P^{T}AQ(AQ)^{+}

\displaystyle=(P^{T}A)^{+}P^{T}\bar{Q}\bar{R}(\bar{Q}\bar{R})^{+}

\displaystyle=(P^{T}A)^{+}P^{T}\bar{Q}\bar{Q}^{T}

\displaystyle=(\bar{Q}^{T}A)^{+}(\bar{Q}^{T}\bar{Q})\bar{Q}^{T}=(\bar{Q}^{T}A)^{+}\bar{Q}^{T}.

The oblique projector
A(P^{T}A)^{+}P^{T}
becomes an orthogonal one
A(\bar{Q}^{T}A)^{+}\bar{Q}^{T}
, projecting onto the column space
\hbox{{C}}(\bar{Q})
along the nullspace
\hbox{{N}}(\bar{Q}^{T})
, as in Figure
6
. The following Matlab code illustrates the idea:

% Pseudoinverse approximation in randomized SVD
[~,n] = size(A); Q = randn(n,q);
[Qbar,~] = qr(A*Q,0); P = Qbar;
Arplus = pinv(P’*A)*P’;
% Randomized SVD part calculates Ur, S, and V for a much smaller matrix P’*A:
% [Ur,S,V] = svd(P’*A,’econ’); Ar = P*Ur*S*V’

Now, let us consider the
CUR factorization
. It approximates:

\displaystyle A\approx\bar{C}U\bar{R},

where
C=A(:,\mathcal{J})=AI_{n}(:,\mathcal{J})=AQ
is a subset of columns of
A
indexed by
\mathcal{J}
,
R=A(\mathcal{I},:)=I_{m}^{T}(:,\mathcal{I})A=P^{T}A
is a subset of rows of
A
indexed by
\mathcal{I}
, and
U
is the mixing matrix. As demonstrated in
[
17
]
, the mixing matrix can be given the form of
U=A(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J})=I_{m}^{T}(\mathcal{I},:)AI_{n}(:,\mathcal{J})
. Then,

\displaystyle A^{+}\approx\bar{R}^{+}U\bar{C}^{+}=A(\mathcal{I},:)^{+}A(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J})A(:,\mathcal{J})^{+}.

That is formula in Theorems
4
and
7
with
P=I_{m}(:,\mathcal{I})
and
Q=I_{n}(:,\mathcal{J})
, namely,

\displaystyle A^{+}\approx A^{+}_{p}

\displaystyle=(AI_{m}(:,\mathcal{I}))^{+}I_{m}(:,\mathcal{I})AI_{n}(:,\mathcal{J})(AI_{n}(:,\mathcal{J}))^{+},

as the following Matlab code clearly illustrates:

% Randomized pseudoinverse approximation in CUR decomposition
[m,n] = size(A);
I = randperm(m,k);
J = randperm(n,k);
Arplus = pinv(A(I,:))*A(I,J)*pinv(A(:,J));
% or equivalently,
% In = eye(n); Im = eye(m);
% Q = In(:,J); P = Im(:,I);
% Arplus = pinv(P’*A)*(P’*A*Q)*pinv(A*Q);

Selecting rows and columns in
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
is crucial for ensuring approximation accuracy. Basic methods for this selection include pivoting, sampling, and randomized pivoting. For a comprehensive overview and analysis of accuracy in the CUR decomposition, refer to
[
19
,
20
]
. Notably, these results are frequently derived using oblique projectors, as illustrated in
[
9
]
.

6.1
Sparse sensor placement

We can apply matrix sampling strategies of Theorem
4
to address the sparse sensor placement problem
[
21
,
22
]
. Then, we revisit the generalized Nyström decomposition, illustrating the role of the pseudoinverse approximation involved.

The goal of sparse sensor placement is to construct a sparse measurement matrix
P_{p}
that selects a subset of
p
rows of a data (dictionary or basis) matrix
A
that allows for an accurate reconstruction of observation
\boldsymbol{y}
. We want to calculate a good estimate
\boldsymbol{y}_{e}
of
\boldsymbol{y}
in the column space
\hbox{{C}}(A)
based on selected samples
\boldsymbol{y_{s}}=P^{T}\boldsymbol{y}
(we can only observe
\boldsymbol{y}
at
p
locations in
P^{T}
). That translates to the following low-rank projection:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}_{e}=A(P_{p}^{T}A)^{+}P_{p}^{T}\boldsymbol{y}=AA_{p}^{+}\boldsymbol{y}.

(27)

The column space projection involves an approximation
A^{+}\approx(P_{p}^{T}A)^{+}P_{p}^{T}
. We want to find
P_{p}=I_{m}(:,\mathcal{I})
, where
\mathcal{I}
is the subset of
p<\mathrm{rank}(A)
rows.

Well-known solutions come from (deterministic) pivoting in QR and LU decompositions. The permutation matrix
P
of the full-rank column-pivoted QR decomposition is an excellent choice. The algorithm registers in
P
the indices of columns of
A
with the largest norm to improve numerical stability. To get rows, we calculate:

\displaystyle A^{T}P=\bar{Q}\bar{R}.

Then, applying (
16
) with
Q=\bar{Q}
we get:

\displaystyle A^{+}=(P^{T}A)^{+}P^{T}AQ(AQ)^{+}

\displaystyle=(P^{T}A)^{+}P^{T}P\bar{R}^{T}\bar{Q}^{T}\bar{Q}(A\bar{Q})^{+}

\displaystyle=(P^{T}A)^{+}\bar{R}^{T}(A\bar{Q})^{+}

\displaystyle=(\bar{R}^{T}\bar{Q}^{T})^{+}\bar{R}^{T}(P\bar{R}^{T}\bar{Q}^{T}\bar{Q})^{+}

\displaystyle=(P^{T}A)^{+}P^{T}.

By selecting
p
rows from
P
, we get the desired low-rank approximation:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}_{e}=A(P^{T}_{p}A)^{+}P^{T}_{p}\boldsymbol{y}.

(28)

When applied to
A
,

\displaystyle A=AA^{+}A\approx A(P^{T}_{p}A)^{+}P^{T}_{p}A=AA^{+}_{p}A.

(29)

The result is the following Matlab code (see also
[
23
]
):

[~,~,smx] = qr(A’,’vector’);
PT = Im(smx(1:p), :);
ys = PT*y;
ye = A*pinv(PT*A)*ys;
Arplus = pinv(PT*A)*PT;
Ar = A*Arplus*A;

Another solution with competitive properties is the LU decomposition with complete pivoting (row and column permutations):

\displaystyle BAD=LU.

In this case, by Theorem
6
we have:

\displaystyle A^{g}=D(BAD)^{+}B.

(30)

By selecting
p
rows from
B
and
q
columns from
D
, we obtain the estimation:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}_{e}=AA^{g}_{p}\boldsymbol{y}=AD_{q}(B_{p}AD_{q})^{+}B_{p}\boldsymbol{y}.

(31)

Also, the following reconstruction equation holds:

\displaystyle A=AA^{g}A\approx AD_{q}(B_{p}AD_{q})^{+}B_{p}A.

(32)

The Matlab code illustrates the idea:

[L,W,B,D] = lu(sparse(A));
Arplus = D(:,1:q)*pinv(full(B(1:p,:)*A*D(:,1:q)))*B(1:p,:);
Ar = A*Arplus*A;

That brings us to the generalized Nyström decomposition. As demonstrated in
[
16
,
9
]
, many randomized linear algebra algorithms can be written as two-sided projections that give rise to that decomposition. An
m
by
n
matrix
A
can be approximated with two sketching matrices, an
n
by
p
matrix
\Omega_{c}
for the column space, and
m
by
p
matrix
\Omega_{r}
for the row space:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} A\approx A\Omega_{c}(\Omega_{r}^{*}A\Omega_{c})^{+}\Omega_{r}^{*}A=AA^{+}_{p}A.\end{aligned}

(33)

The relationship with (
31
) and (
32
) is straightforward. By Theorem
4
, the generalized Nyström decomposition requires the low-rank pseudoinverse approximation:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} A^{+}\approx A^{+}_{p}=\Omega_{c}(\Omega_{r}^{*}A\Omega_{c})^{+}\Omega_{r}^{*}=Q(P^{T}AQ)^{+}P^{T}.\end{aligned}

(34)

As a result, in
[
16
]
we get the following implementation:

AQ = A*Q; PTA = P’*A;
[Qbar,Rbar] = qr(P’*A*Q,0);
Arplus = (Q/Rbar)*(P*Qbar)’;
Ar = A*Arplus*A;

In other words, low-rank
A
comes from low-rank
A^{+}
.

6.2
Effective resistance calculations

Consider a network consisting of
n
nodes connected by
m
edges. Each row of the
m
by
n
incidence matrix
A
representing that network shows which two nodes are connected. Edge
i
leaves node
j
when
A_{ij}=-1
, and goes to node
k
from
i
when
A_{ik}=1
. Then, given a diagonal
m
by
m
matrix
W
of edge conductance, the weighted Laplacian matrix describing the network is:

\displaystyle L=A^{T}WA.

(35)

When external flows
\boldsymbol{f}
are applied to the nodes, and
\boldsymbol{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{f}=0
, the equilibrium potentials
\boldsymbol{x}
satisfy the equation (see
[
24
]
for more details):

\displaystyle L\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{f}.

(36)

The Laplacian matrix is singular with rank
n-1
for a connected graph, so the potentials are determined only up to an additive constant
a\boldsymbol{1}
. By Theorem
3
, the general equilibrium solution is

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}=L^{+}\boldsymbol{f}+(I_{n}-L^{+}L)\boldsymbol{y}=L^{+}\boldsymbol{f}+a\boldsymbol{1}.

(37)

The effective resistance
R_{ij}
between nodes
i
and
j
measures how far apart (in terms of the overall energy or information flow) the nodes are in the network with edge conductance
W
. To determine that distance, we set
\boldsymbol{f}=\boldsymbol{e}_{i}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j}
to find equilibrium potentials:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}=L^{+}(\boldsymbol{e}_{i}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j})+a\boldsymbol{1}.

(38)

Then, we calculate the potential difference
x_{i}-x_{j}=(\boldsymbol{e}_{i}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j})^{T}\boldsymbol{x}
to obtain the desired effective resistance:

\displaystyle R_{ij}=x_{i}-x_{j}=(\boldsymbol{e}_{i}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j})^{T}\boldsymbol{x}=(\boldsymbol{e}_{i}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j})^{T}L^{+}(\boldsymbol{e}_{i}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j}).

(39)

The effective resistance matrix
R
is given by the formula:

\displaystyle R=\operatorname{diag}(L^{+})\,\mathbf{1}^{T}+\mathbf{1}\,\operatorname{diag}(L^{+})^{T}-2L^{+}.

(40)

This important matrix is computed from the pseudoinverse of the graph Laplacian matrix. For large networks, that can be computationally prohibitive. The formulas derived in this paper offer another perspective for efficient calculations, complementing the large body of work on effective resistance
[
25
,
26
,
27
,
28
]
.

One way is to calculate an approximation
L^{+}_{p}=Q(P^{T}LQ)^{+}P^{T}
by random sampling with
P
and
Q
. That immediately gives approximation:

\displaystyle R_{p}=\operatorname{diag}(L_{p}^{+})\,\mathbf{1}^{T}+\mathbf{1}\,\operatorname{diag}(L_{p}^{+})^{T}-2L_{p}^{+}.

(41)

However, we would also like to explore another way.

Let us partition the nodes into disjoint subsets
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
, where
\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{T}=\{1,...,n\}
. Consider
\mathcal{S}=\{i,j\}
selecting only two nodes in the network. We would like to estimate the effective resistance between these two nodes. Taking
P=Q=I_{n}(:,\mathcal{S})
and denoting
\boldsymbol{d}=[1,-1]^{T}
, we can consider the approximation:

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{ij}=(\boldsymbol{e}_{i}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j})^{T}L^{+}_{p}(\boldsymbol{e}_{i}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j})

\displaystyle=(Q\boldsymbol{d})^{T}Q(P^{T}LQ)^{+}P^{T}(P\boldsymbol{d})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{d}^{T}\begin{bmatrix}L_{ii}&L_{ij}\\
L_{ji}&L_{jj}\end{bmatrix}^{+}\boldsymbol{d}=\boldsymbol{d}^{T}L_{\mathcal{SS}}^{+}\boldsymbol{d}.

To examine how good an approximation that is in comparison to the exact value of effective resistance
R_{ij}
, it is good to start with the Kron-reduced form of
L
or its Schur complement:

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{\mathcal{S}}=L_{\mathcal{SS}}-L_{\mathcal{ST}}L^{+}_{\mathcal{TT}}L_{\mathcal{TS}}=L_{\mathcal{SS}}-E_{\mathcal{S}},

given the permuted block decomposition for
\mathcal{S}
, namely:

\displaystyle L=\begin{bmatrix}L_{\mathcal{SS}}&L_{\mathcal{ST}}\\
L_{\mathcal{TS}}&L_{\mathcal{TT}}\end{bmatrix}.

As demonstrated in
[
25
]
, the effective resistance
R_{ij}
is invariant with respect to that Kron reduction, i.e.,

\displaystyle R_{ij}=(\boldsymbol{e}_{i}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j})^{T}L^{+}(\boldsymbol{e}_{i}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j})=(\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}-\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j})^{T}\hat{L}^{+}_{\mathcal{S}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}-\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}).

That provides a convenient way for us to examine the relation between
R_{ij}
and
\tilde{R}_{ij}
for any
\mathcal{S}=\{i,j\}
. We now prove the following result.

Theorem 8
.

Consider a connected graph described by a weighted positive semidefinite Laplacian
L
and let
R_{ij}
denote the effective resistance between nodes
i
and
j
:

\displaystyle R_{ij}=(\boldsymbol{e}_{i}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j})^{\top}L^{+}(\boldsymbol{e}_{i}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j}).

(42)

Let
\tilde{R}_{ij}
be the approximation defined from the principal submatrix

L_{\mathcal{SS}}
with
\mathcal{S}=\{i,j\}
and
\boldsymbol{d}=[1,-1]^{\top}
by

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{ij}=\boldsymbol{d}^{\top}L_{\mathcal{SS}}^{+}\boldsymbol{d}.

(43)

Then,
\tilde{R}_{ij}\leq R_{ij}
for all
i,j
.

Moreover, for any nodes
i,j,k
the following inference rules hold,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \text{if}\ \tilde{R}_{ij}-\tilde{R}_{ik}<-\gamma,\ &\text{then}\ R_{ij}-R_{ik}<0,\\
\text{if}\ \tilde{R}_{ij}-\tilde{R}_{ik}>\phantom{-}\gamma,\ &\text{then}\ R_{ij}-R_{ik}>0,\end{aligned}

(44)

with
\gamma=2/\lambda_{2}(L)
.

Equivalently, the approximation error on the effective resistance differences satisfies the following inequality:

\displaystyle\bigl|(\tilde{R}_{ij}-\tilde{R}_{ik})-(R_{ij}-R_{ik})\bigr|\leq\frac{2}{\lambda_{2}(L)}\qquad\text{for all distinct }i,j,k.

(45)

Proof.

Let us first observe that for any symmetric
L\succeq 0
such that
LL^{+}\boldsymbol{d}=\boldsymbol{d}
, the properties of pseudoinverse imply that
\boldsymbol{d}^{T}(L^{+})^{T}L\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{u}^{T}LL^{+}\boldsymbol{d}=\boldsymbol{u}^{T}\boldsymbol{d}
. Thus,

\displaystyle 2\boldsymbol{d}^{T}\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}^{T}L\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=2\boldsymbol{d}^{T}\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}^{T}L\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{d}^{T}L^{+}\boldsymbol{d}+\boldsymbol{d}^{T}L^{+}\boldsymbol{d}

\displaystyle=-(\boldsymbol{u}^{T}-\boldsymbol{d}^{T}L^{+})L(\boldsymbol{u}-L^{+}\boldsymbol{d})+\boldsymbol{d}^{T}L^{+}\boldsymbol{d}

\displaystyle=-(\boldsymbol{u}-L^{+}\boldsymbol{d})^{T}L(\boldsymbol{u}-L^{+}\boldsymbol{d})+\boldsymbol{d}^{T}L^{+}\boldsymbol{d}

\displaystyle\leq\boldsymbol{d}^{T}L^{+}\boldsymbol{d}=\sup\{2\boldsymbol{d}^{T}\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}^{T}L\boldsymbol{u}|\ \boldsymbol{u}\perp\hbox{{N}}(L)\}.

Consider now a partition of
L
according to
\mathcal{S}
and its complement
\mathcal{T}
:

\displaystyle L=\begin{bmatrix}L_{\mathcal{SS}}&L_{\mathcal{ST}}\\
L_{\mathcal{TS}}&L_{\mathcal{TT}}\end{bmatrix}.

The Kron-reduced form of
L
is the Schur complement:

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{\mathcal{S}}=L_{\mathcal{SS}}-L_{\mathcal{ST}}L^{+}_{\mathcal{TT}}L_{\mathcal{TS}}=L_{\mathcal{SS}}-E_{\mathcal{S}}.

Recall that, as demonstrated in
[
25
]
, the effective resistance
R_{ij}
is invariant with respect to that Kron reduction.

It follows that
\hat{L}_{\mathcal{S}}\preceq L_{\mathcal{SS}}
, and so, for any
\boldsymbol{u}
we have:

\displaystyle-\boldsymbol{u}^{T}\hat{L}_{\mathcal{S}}\boldsymbol{u}\geq-\boldsymbol{u}^{T}{L}_{\mathcal{SS}}\boldsymbol{u}.

We are interested exclusively in the resistance distance for
\boldsymbol{e}_{i}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j}=I_{n}(:,\{i,j\})\boldsymbol{d}
, where
\boldsymbol{d}\in\hbox{{C}}(\hat{L}_{\mathcal{S}})
. In that case, adding
2\boldsymbol{d}^{T}\boldsymbol{u}
to both sides, we have:

\displaystyle 2\boldsymbol{d}^{T}\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}^{T}\hat{L}_{\mathcal{S}}\boldsymbol{u}\geq 2\boldsymbol{d}^{T}\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}^{T}{L}_{\mathcal{SS}}\boldsymbol{u}.

Taking the supremum over
\boldsymbol{u}
preserves the inequality, therefore

\displaystyle\sup\{2\boldsymbol{d}^{T}\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}^{T}\hat{L}_{\mathcal{S}}\boldsymbol{u}|\ \boldsymbol{u}\perp\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathcal{S}}\}=\boldsymbol{d}^{T}\hat{L}_{\mathcal{S}}^{+}\boldsymbol{d}\geq\boldsymbol{d}^{T}L_{\mathcal{SS}}^{+}\boldsymbol{d}=\sup\{2\boldsymbol{d}^{T}\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}^{T}L_{\mathcal{SS}}\boldsymbol{u}|\ \boldsymbol{u}\neq\boldsymbol{0}\},

and so:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{d}^{T}\hat{L}_{\mathcal{S}}^{+}\boldsymbol{d}\geq\boldsymbol{d}^{T}L_{\mathcal{SS}}^{+}\boldsymbol{d}

As a result,

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{ij}=\boldsymbol{d}^{T}L_{\mathcal{SS}}^{+}\boldsymbol{d}\leq\boldsymbol{d}^{T}\hat{L}_{\mathcal{S}}^{+}\boldsymbol{d}=R_{ij},

and we conclude the approximation always underestimates the true resistance,
0\leq R_{ij}-\tilde{R}_{ij}=\epsilon_{ij}
.

Since
L
is symmetric, there exists an orthonormal basis
V
of eigenvectors corresponding to real-valued eigenvalues
0=\lambda_{1}(L)\leq\lambda_{2}(L)\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{n}(L)
. Therefore, given
L^{+}=VS^{+}U^{T}
, we have

\displaystyle\|L^{+}\|_{2}\leq\max\{0,1/\lambda_{2}(L),\dots,1/\lambda_{n}(L)\}=1/\lambda_{2}(L).

For any pair
p,q
we have:

\displaystyle R_{pq}=(\boldsymbol{e}_{p}-\boldsymbol{e}_{q})^{T}L^{+}(\boldsymbol{e}_{p}-\boldsymbol{e}_{q})\leq\|\boldsymbol{e}_{p}-\boldsymbol{e}_{q}\|^{2}\,\|L^{+}\|_{2}=\frac{2}{\lambda_{2}(L)}.

We have already established, that
0\leq R_{ij}-\tilde{R}_{ij}=\epsilon_{ij}
. From that we conclude:

\displaystyle 0\leq\epsilon_{ij}\leq\frac{2}{\lambda_{2}(L)}.

Since

\displaystyle|\epsilon_{ij}-\epsilon_{ik}|\leq\max(\epsilon_{ij},\epsilon_{ik})\leq\frac{2}{\lambda_{2}(L)},

we can see that:

\displaystyle\bigl|(\tilde{R}_{ij}-\tilde{R}_{ik})-(R_{ij}-R_{ik})\bigr|\leq\frac{2}{\lambda_{2}(L)}.

This proves the stated additive error bound.

Finally, to establish the inference rules, set
\Delta=R_{ij}-R_{ik}
and
\tilde{\Delta}=\tilde{R}_{ij}-\tilde{R}_{ik}
.
If
\tilde{\Delta}<-\gamma
with
\gamma=2/\lambda_{2}(L)
, then

\Delta=\tilde{\Delta}-(\tilde{\Delta}-\Delta)\leq\tilde{\Delta}+|\tilde{\Delta}-\Delta|\leq\tilde{\Delta}+\gamma<-\gamma+\gamma=0.

Similarly, if
\tilde{\Delta}>\gamma
then

\Delta=\tilde{\Delta}-(\tilde{\Delta}-\Delta)\geq\tilde{\Delta}-|\tilde{\Delta}-\Delta|>\gamma-\gamma=0.

This establishes the inference rules (
44
).

∎

Consider a graph with
n=4
nodes, unit edge weights (conductances
W=I
), and the Laplacian matrix
L
:

\displaystyle L=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1&0&0\\
-1&2&-1&0\\
0&-1&2&-1\\
0&0&-1&1\end{pmatrix}.

The eigenvalues of
L
are
\{0,2-\sqrt{2},2,2+\sqrt{2}\}
, so the smallest non-zero eigenvalue is
\lambda_{2}(L)=2-\sqrt{2}
. By Theorem
8
, the upper bound on the error of the estimated resistance differences is:

\displaystyle\gamma=\frac{2}{\lambda_{2}(L)}=\frac{2}{2-\sqrt{2}}=2+\sqrt{2}\approx 3.4142.

The true effective resistance
R_{ij}
between nodes
i
and
j
can be calculated using the pseudoinverse:

\displaystyle L^{+}=\dfrac{1}{8}\begin{pmatrix}7&1&-3&-5\\
1&3&-1&-3\\
-3&-1&3&1\\
-5&-3&1&7\end{pmatrix}.

Let us verify the effective resistance between node
i=1
and nodes
j=2,3,4
. From

\displaystyle R_{ij}=(\boldsymbol{e}_{i}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j})^{\top}L^{+}(\boldsymbol{e}_{i}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j})

we obtain
R_{12}=1,R_{13}=2
and
R_{14}=3
.

Now, we can compare that with the estimate in Theorem
8
calculated for
\mathcal{S}=\{i,j\}
and
\boldsymbol{d}=[1,-1]^{\top}
. We have

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{ij}=\boldsymbol{d}^{T}L_{\mathcal{S}\mathcal{S}}^{+}\boldsymbol{d}=[1,-1]\begin{bmatrix}L_{ii}&L_{ij}\\
L_{ji}&L_{jj}\end{bmatrix}^{+}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
-1\end{bmatrix}.

The approximations are
\tilde{R}_{12}=1
,
\tilde{R}_{13}=1.5
and
\tilde{R}_{14}=2
.

Therefore, as expected, the property
R_{ij}\geq\tilde{R}_{ij}
holds:

\displaystyle R_{12}=1\geq 1=\tilde{R}_{12},\quad R_{13}=2\geq 1.5=\tilde{R}_{13},\quad R_{14}=3\geq 2=\tilde{R}_{14}.

The absolute errors
\epsilon_{ij}=R_{ij}-\tilde{R}_{ij}
are
\epsilon_{12}=0
,
\epsilon_{13}=0.5
and
\epsilon_{14}=1
, therefore

\displaystyle 0\leq\epsilon_{ij}\leq\gamma=2/\lambda_{2}(L)\approx 3.4142.

Finally, it is easy to see that whenever
\tilde{R}_{ij}-\tilde{R}_{ik}<-\gamma
, then
R_{ij}-R_{ik}<0
and the estimate preserves the ordering.

7
Related Work

The theory of generalized inverses has a rich history, with deep connections to modern randomized numerical linear algebra, as well as machine learning and modern AI models development. We survey the foundational results on pseudoinverses of matrix products and position our contributions within this landscape.

7.1
Classical theory of generalized inverses

The Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse was independently discovered by Moore using the concept of general reciprocal
[
29
]
, by Bjerhammar in
[
14
]
, and famously characterized by Penrose through the four defining identities
[
7
]
. The comprehensive treatment by Ben-Israel and Greville
[
1
]
remains the standard reference, consolidating decades of development in the field.

The reverse order law

The question of when
(AB)^{+}=B^{+}A^{+}
holds was definitively answered by Greville in
[
8
]
. He established that the reverse order law is valid if and only if

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}(BB^{*}A^{*})\subseteq\mathbf{C}(A^{*})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{C}(A^{*}AB)\subseteq\mathbf{C}(B),

(46)

or equivalently, when
A^{*}ABB^{*}=BB^{*}A^{*}A
. Erdelyi
[
30
]
provided an independent characterization via partial isometries the same year. Theorem
1
recovers a well-known sufficient condition: when
C
has full column rank and
R
has full row rank, the factors satisfy (
46
) trivially with
C^{+}C=I_{r}=RR^{+}
.

Full-rank factorization formula

The formula
A^{+}=R^{T}(C^{T}AR^{T})^{-1}C^{T}
for a full-rank factorization
A=CR
appears as Theorem 3.6.2 in
[
1
]
. Historical notes attribute the result to MacDuffee (1959, private communication) and subsequent refinements by Greville
[
13
]
. Our presentation follows this classical approach but emphasizes the geometric perspective of the four fundamental subspaces.

Generalized inverses of products

The extension to arbitrary products was developed by Cline
[
31
]
, who studied partitioned matrices and representations for
(AB)^{+}
without rank assumptions. Hartwig
[
32
]
extended these results to triple products. The corrected formula in Theorem
2
, namely
(CR)^{+}=(C^{+}CR)^{+}(CRR^{+})^{+}
, can be understood through this lens: the factors
(C^{+}CR)^{+}
and
(CRR^{+})^{+}
handle the projections between intermediate subspaces. While the underlying mathematics is established, our presentation directly connects it to the four fundamental subspaces framework.

The
\{1,2\}
-inverse characterization

Marsaglia and Styan
[
15
]
provided the complete characterization of
\{1\}
-inverses through formula (
4.1
). The constraint
Z_{22}=Z_{21}Z_{12}
for obtaining
\{1,2\}
-inverses, which implies equal ranks of
A
and
A^{g}
, is classical. Theorem
5
extends this by incorporating sketching matrices
P
and
Q
into the construction.

7.2
Randomized numerical linear algebra

The past two decades have witnessed remarkable developments in randomized algorithms for matrix computation. We focus on connections to pseudoinverse computation and low-rank approximation.

7.2.1
Randomized SVD and matrix approximation

The foundational work of Halko, Martinsson, and Tropp
[
4
]
established the randomized range-finder framework: given a target rank
k
and oversampling parameter
p
, one computes
Y=A\Omega
for a random
n\times(k+p)
matrix
\Omega
, then orthogonalizes to obtain
Q
with
\mathbf{C}(Q)\approx\mathbf{C}(A)
. The approximation
A\approx QQ^{T}A
leads to randomized SVD via the smaller matrix
Q^{T}A
. While pseudoinverse computation is implicit—one obtains
A^{+}\approx V\Sigma^{+}U^{T}
from the randomized SVD—explicit formulas for
A^{+}
in terms of sketching matrices are not developed in
[
4
]
.

The comprehensive survey by Martinsson and Tropp
[
6
]
provides extensive treatment of randomized algorithms but similarly focuses on approximation rather than pseudoinverse reconstruction.

Generalized Nyström approximation

Nakatsukasa
[
16
]
introduced the generalized Nyström approximation for arbitrary
m\times n
matrices:

\displaystyle\hat{A}=(A\Omega)(\Psi^{T}A\Omega)^{+}(\Psi^{T}A),

(47)

where
\Omega
and
\Psi
are sketching matrices. This formula involves the pseudoinverse of the core matrix
\Psi^{T}A\Omega
and produces a low-rank approximation
\hat{A}\approx A
. For symmetric positive semidefinite matrices with
\Psi=\Omega
, one recovers the classical Nyström approximation. Theorem
7
shows that when
P
and
Q
are orthogonal and
P^{T}AQ=CR
is a full-rank factorization, then
A^{+}=Q(CR)^{+}P^{T}
exactly—connecting the generalized Nyström structure to pseudoinverse reconstruction rather than matrix approximation.

CUR decomposition

The CUR decomposition approximates
A\approx CUR
where
C
contains selected columns,
R
selected rows, and
U
is a linking matrix. Mahoney and Drineas
[
33
]
established probabilistic guarantees, while Hamm and Huang
[
17
]
provided the definitive treatment of exactness conditions. Their result states that
A=CU^{+}R
and
A^{+}=R^{+}UC^{+}
hold if and only if
\mathrm{rank}(U)=\mathrm{rank}(A)
.

The connection to our framework is immediate: setting
P=I_{m}(:,\mathcal{I})
and
Q=I_{n}(:,\mathcal{J})
as column-selection matrices in Theorem
4
recovers the CUR pseudoinverse formula. Our contribution is the unified perspective showing that CUR, randomized SVD, and sparse sensor placement all emerge as special cases of the randomized formula (
16
) with different choices of
P
and
Q
.

Sketch-and-precondition methods

Iterative approaches such as Blendenpik
[
34
]
and LSRN
[
35
]
use sketching to construct preconditioners for iterative solvers rather than computing
A^{+}
directly. Recent work by Epperly, Meier, and Nakatsukasa
[
36
]
achieves stability through iterative refinement. These methods complement direct pseudoinverse formulas by providing practical algorithms for large-scale computation.

Effective resistance and graph Laplacians

Effective resistance, defined as
R_{ij}=(\boldsymbol{e}_{i}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j})^{T}L^{+}(\boldsymbol{e}_{i}-\boldsymbol{e}_{j})
for a graph Laplacian
L
, plays a fundamental role in spectral graph theory and network analysis
[
37
,
38
]
. The classical bound
R_{ij}\leq 2/\lambda_{2}(L)
, relating effective resistance to the algebraic connectivity (Fiedler eigenvalue), is well established
[
38
]
.

Dörfler and Bullo
[
25
]
proved that effective resistance is preserved exactly under Kron reduction (Schur complementation). Spielman and Srivastava
[
39
]
provided multiplicative
(1\pm\varepsilon)
approximation bounds for spectral sparsification. However, additive error bounds for effective resistance approximation in terms of algebraic connectivity appear to be new. Theorem
8
establishes that the principal submatrix approximation
\tilde{R}_{ij}=\boldsymbol{d}^{T}L_{\mathcal{SS}}^{+}\boldsymbol{d}
always underestimates the true resistance with error bounded by
R_{ij}-\tilde{R}_{ij}\leq 2/\lambda_{2}(L)
, and that resistance orderings are preserved when differences exceed this threshold.

7.3
Summary of contributions

We think the novelty of this paper lies in the integration of classical generalized inverse theory with randomized linear algebra. Theorem
4
provides a unifying lens through which established algorithms—randomized SVD, CUR decomposition, generalized Nyström, and sparse sensor placement—can be understood as instances of randomized pseudoinverse computation with specific choices of sketching matrices
P
and
Q
. The rank-preservation condition
\mathrm{rank}(P^{T}A)=\mathrm{rank}(AQ)=\mathrm{rank}(A)
is the criterion separating exact reconstruction from low-rank approximation. From that unification new insights may emerge. One we present here concerns the effective resistance estimation with sketching in Theorem
8
. There is a beautiful link between the rank-preserving generalized inverse and the effective resistance.

8
Conclusions

We proposed a unified framework for the analysis of generalized inverses of matrix products, integrating classical generalized inverse theory with randomized numerical linear algebra. By revisiting the pseudoinverse of
A=CR
from the perspective of the four fundamental subspaces, we have analyzed three central formulas, clarifying their geometric interpretations.

We believe that the principal contribution is the development of the generalized randomized framework presented in Theorems
5
and
4
. We have rigorously demonstrated that the exact reconstruction of the Moore-Penrose inverse
A^{+}
or a generalized
\{1,2\}
-inverse
A^{g}
using sketched matrices
P^{T}A
and
AQ
depends on the preservation of the rank of
A
. This framework provides a lens through which established algorithms, such as randomized SVD and CUR decomposition, can be better understood as specific instances of randomized generalized inversion.

Furthermore, the application of this framework to the approximation of effective resistance in Theorem
8
highlights its analytical utility. That approximation based on principal submatrices always underestimates the true resistance within a worst-case spectral bound while preserving the effective resistance ordering.

Exploration of randomized generalized inverses may still yield novel algorithms and deep theoretical insights into large-scale matrix computations, critical in development of modern AI models.
