The Efficacy of Left Atrial Appendage Occlusion During Thoracoscopic Epicardial AF Ablation for Improving Rhythm and Cardiac Function in Patients With Heart Failure
Authors: Seongjin Park, Dong Seop Jeong, Jiwon Kim, Myoung Jung Kim, Juwon Kim, Seung‐Jung Park, Kyoung‐Min Park, Young Keun On, Ju Youn Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe left atrial appendage represents the main source of thrombus formation in patients with atrial fibrillation (AF). The LAA plays an important role in LA reservoir function, especially in patients with heart failure (HF). Limited data are available regarding the efficacy of surgical left atrial appendage occlusion (LAAO) during thoracoscopic epicardial AF ablation for improving rhythm and cardiac function.MethodsPatients who underwent thoracoscopic epicardial AF ablation with or without LAAO for AF combined with HF from February 2012 to November 2023 were included. Recurrence of AF, major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), redo catheter ablation findings, and echocardiographic outcomes were compared between groups.ResultsAmong 127 patients, 13 (10.2%) did not receive LAAO according to their physicians' discretion. The atrial tachyarrhythmia recurrence tended to be higher in the non‐LAAO group (hazard ratio [HR] 1.38, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.68–2.78, p = 0.375). Of the 34 patients who underwent electrophysiology studies, the left pulmonary vein (PV) reconnection rate was significantly higher in the non‐LAAO group (odds ratio 19.5, 95% CI 1.61–236.61, p = 0.020). MACE was observed more frequently in the non‐LAAO group compared to the LAAO group (23.1% vs. 8.8%, HR 2.83, 95% CI 0.77–10.41, p = 0.117). Adjusted analyses showed lower postoperative E/e′ in the LAAO group than in the non‐LAAO group (p = 0.014).ConclusionsSurgical LAAO during thoracoscopic epicardial AF ablation was effective for maintaining durable PV isolation in patients with HF. Thoracoscopic epicardial AF ablation improves LV systolic function regardless of LAAO status, and LAAO was not associated with worsening postoperative diastolic function.

Introduction

The left atrial appendage (LAA) is a primary source of thrombus formation in patients with atrial fibrillation (AF), increasing the risk of embolic stroke [1]. Endocardial LAA isolation improves rhythm but may impair contractility and increase stroke risk [2, 3]. This concern has prompted interest in potentially safer epicardial approaches, such as thoracoscopic LAA occlusion (LAAO) [4].

Thoracoscopic epicardial AF ablation has emerged as a promising treatment option for AF patients, including those with left ventricular dysfunction. Rhythm control and cardiac function improve following thoracoscopic epicardial AF ablation, even in patients with refractory AF [5, 6]. Recent studies have demonstrated that thoracoscopic LAA resection can be performed safely without negatively affecting cardiac function [7]. However, the role of the LAA in heart failure (HF) patients remains unclear. The LAA may act as a reservoir or buffer for pressure and volume loads, which could be particularly relevant in patients with heart failure with reduced (HFrEF), mid‐range (HFmrEF), or preserved (HFpEF) ejection fraction [8, 9, 10]. While surgical LAAO is well established for stroke prevention, its role in rhythm durability and physiological remodeling in HF patients remains less explored.

In this study we address the clinical impacts of LAAO specifically within an HF cohort on rhythm outcomes and cardiac function undergoing thoracoscopic epicardial AF ablation, incorporating electrophysiological mapping to evaluate the mechanisms of recurrence.

Methods

Study Population

This was a single‐center, retrospective, observational study that included patients with AF combined with left ventricular systolic dysfunction who underwent thoracoscopic epicardial AF ablation between February 2012 and November 2023 (Figure 1). Inclusion was limited to individuals with drug‐resistant AF, meaning that their arrhythmia persisted despite antiarrhythmic drug therapy, and for whom thoracoscopic epicardial AF ablation was deemed appropriate based on clinical guidelines for rhythm control. HF was defined as HFrEF (left ventricular ejection fraction [LVEF] ≤ 40%), HFmrEF (LVEF 41%–49%), and HF with improved ejection fraction (previous HFrEF who now have LVEF > 40%) [11]. Accordingly, the study population was not restricted to patients with AF‐mediated cardiomyopathy alone. Patients who converted to open Cox–Maze surgery, underwent unilateral pulmonary vein (PV) isolation (PVI), or underwent thoracoscopic LAAO only without AF ablation were excluded.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Samsung Medical Center, South Korea (IRB No. 2020‐06‐159).

Surgical Techniques

Before surgery, all patients underwent transesophageal echocardiography to exclude LAA thrombus. Total thoracoscopic ablation procedures were performed under general anesthesia. No concomitant open cardiac surgery was performed in the analyzed cohort. All procedures were performed using standard techniques as described previously [12, 13].

Bilateral video‐assisted thoracoscopic access was obtained with three ports per side (two 5‐mm and one 10‐mm). The right side was approached first. After CO2 insufflation, ports were placed in the third, fourth, and sixth intercostal spaces. The pericardium was opened anterior to the phrenic nerve, and the right PV antrum was dissected. A bipolar clamp (AtriCure) was applied around the right PVs and fired 6–8 times. Linear ablation lines were added to create a box lesion, and SVC isolation was performed when right atrial enlargement was evident. Ganglionated plexi were targeted in early cases.

The left pericardium was opened posterior to the phrenic nerve. The ligament of Marshall was ablated. Bidirectional block was confirmed using an ORLab system. When LAA exclusion was performed, a stapler was used before 2018, whereas an epicardial clip has been used since 2018. Some patients did not undergo LAAO. The decision to perform or omit LAAO was made through a multidisciplinary team consultation comprising HF specialists, electrophysiologists, and the operating surgeon. LAAO was specifically deferred in patients where the exclusion of the LAA was clinically judged to potentially exacerbate severe HF. Successful LAAO was defined as complete exclusion of the trabeculated LAA. On postoperative CT, residual findings were classified as either a smooth‐walled basal stump after complete exclusion of the trabeculated portion or a residual trabeculated LAA remnant [14, 15]. Oral anticoagulation was resumed 1–2 days postoperatively and continued for at least 3 months. To ensure procedural consistency and minimize technical variability, all surgical procedures were performed by a single experienced surgeon.

Electrophysiology Studies

We performed electrophysiology for patients who experienced recurrence of arrhythmia and underwent redo procedures. Each electrophysiological study was performed under sedation or general anesthesia, and detailed electroanatomical data were obtained using the CARTO mapping system (Biosense Webster, Diamond Bar, CA, USA). Bidirectional block was confirmed for both PVs. We applied radiofrequency catheter ablation if a residual PV gap was present. Additional cavotricuspid isthmus ablation was performed after PVI. Other linear lesions were created at the discretion of the physician. All patients underwent catheter ablation with an open irrigated catheter (Thermocool, Biosense Webster). RF energy up to 25–35 W was used.

Data Collection and Follow‐Up

Baseline clinical information including patient demographics, CHA2DS2‐VASc scores, and HAS‐BLED scores, procedural details, echocardiographic parameters, N‐terminal pro b‐type natriuretic peptide (NT‐proBNP), and surgery‐related adverse events were collected from medical records. In addition to recurrence of atrial tachyarrhythmia, we evaluated major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), defined as a composite of embolic stroke, major bleeding, myocardial infarction, HF hospitalization, and all‐cause mortality. Major bleeding was defined as bleeding requiring transfusion, hemoglobin drop, or involving critical organs. Patients were routinely followed at 2 weeks and at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months after surgery, and every 6 months thereafter. Electrocardiography (ECG) was obtained at each visit. Rhythm surveillance included ambulatory Holter monitoring at scheduled follow‐up, typically at 3, 6, and 12 months and annually thereafter, using either 24‐h Holter monitoring or extended ambulatory monitoring (generally 3–8 days) according to clinical practice. In addition, when patients reported symptoms suggestive of tachyarrhythmia recurrence, further rhythm evaluation was performed using additional ECG, ambulatory monitoring, or wearable ECG devices as appropriate. We compared atrial tachyarrhythmia recurrence, echocardiographic parameters, MACE, and electrophysiological characteristics by LAAO status.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were compared using unpaired t‐test or Mann–Whitney U tests, based on normality (Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and Q–Q plots). Adjusted group differences were assessed using mean differences. Continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviations (SD) or median interquartile ranges, and categorical variables as frequencies (%), compared using chi‐square or Fisher's exact test. Logistic regression analysis was used to assess the risk of left side PV reconnection, with results reported as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Multivariable logistic regression models were built with clinically relevant variables identified through univariate analysis. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals for atrial tachyarrhythmia recurrence and MACE. Kaplan–Meier and log‐rank tests compared AF recurrence. Analyses were conducted using SPSS v26.0 and R v4.4.2. p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 127 patients were included in the analysis, of whom 13 (10.2%) did not undergo LAAO based on clinical discretion. Among the 114 patients who underwent LAA exclusion, an endoscopic stapler was used in 42 cases and an epicardial clip in 72 cases. Mean age was 55.7 years; 91.3% were male with predominantly persistent AF. Underlying structural heart disease was present in a subset of patients, including hypertrophic cardiomyopathy in 21 patients (16.5%). CHA2DS2‐VASc scores were similar between the LAAO and non‐LAAO groups (2.3 ± 1.3 vs. 2.4 ± 1.1), as were HAS‐BLED scores (1.1 ± 1.0 vs. 1.0 ± 0.9). There were no significant differences between the two groups in echocardiographic characteristics including mean LVEF, left ventricular diameter, left atrium size, or right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP) (Table 1). The mean baseline LVEF before surgery including improved EF was 50.5% ± 11.1%, and the overall mean LVEF at the time of diagnosis of AF was 37.9% ± 14.2%.

In terms of ablation procedures, four PVI, posterior box isolation, and ligament of Marshall ablation were performed in most patients, with no significant differences observed between the two groups (Figure S1). Among patients who underwent LAA exclusion, all trabeculated portions of the LAA were well excluded intraoperatively. On postoperative CT imaging, a residual smooth‐walled basal stump was observed in 5 cases, and no residual trabeculated LAA remnant was identified. Perioperative surgery‐related adverse events were infrequent and did not differ significantly by LAAO status (Table S1).

Recurrence and Clinical Outcomes

During follow‐up (mean follow‐up duration 60.5 ± 33.0 months), 69 patients (54.3%) experienced atrial tachyarrhythmia recurrence. There was a tendency for atrial tachyarrhythmia recurrence to increase in the non‐LAAO group, but the difference was not significant (HR 1.38, 95% CI 0.68–2.78, p = 0.375) (Figure 2A).

MACE was observed more frequently in the non‐LAAO group compared to the LAAO group, although the difference was not statistically significant (23.1% vs. 8.8%, HR 2.83, 95% CI 0.77–10.41, p = 0.117) (Figure 2B). Thrombotic embolic stroke occurred in 1 patient, major bleeding in 7, MI in 1, HF hospitalization in 3, and death in 2. One patient experienced both bleeding and death. At the last follow‐up, 34.8% of patients in the LAAO group and 53.8% of patients in the non‐LAAO group were maintaining oral anticoagulation therapy (p = 0.227).

Echocardiographic Parameters and Biomarkers

LVEF and left atrial volume index (LAVI) improved significantly overall among patients after the procedure, suggesting that thoracoscopic AF ablation itself contributed to enhanced cardiac function (Table S2). LVEF increased from 50.7% ± 11.2% to 57.3% ± 8.6% in the LAAO group and from 48.3% ± 11.0% to 52.8% ± 14.2% in the non‐LAAO group (Table 3, Figure 3A). LAVI decreased from 52.3 ± 18.0 mL/m2 to 47.8 ± 18.2 mL/m2 in the LAAO group and from 50.8 ± 13.3 mL/m2 to 43.4 ± 10.2 mL/m2 in the non‐LAAO group (Table 3). In adjusted analyses, postoperative E/e′ values were significantly lower in the LAAO group compared to the non‐LAAO group (p = 0.014) (Table 3, Figure 4). NT‐proBNP levels also showed decreasing trends in both groups. In the LAAO group, NT‐proBNP decreased from 602.5 ± 590.4 pg/mL to 489.8 ± 590.1 pg/mL, and in the non‐LAAO group, it decreased from 627.3 ± 723.9 pg/mL to 386.1 ± 487.6 pg/mL (Table 3, Figure 3B). Additional sensitivity analyses incorporating HF subtype, LV systolic function group, or postoperative atrial tachyarrhythmia recurrence did not materially alter the results for LVEF, E/e′, or NT‐proBNP (Tables S3–S5).

Discussion

This study provides real‐world evidence on the impact of thoracoscopic AF ablation with LAAO in high‐risk patients with both AF and HF, evaluating not only rhythm outcomes but also cardiac function. By including redo electrophysiological studies, we offer insight into the durability of PVI and mechanisms behind AF recurrence.

LAAO and Rhythm Outcomes

Previous research has consistently demonstrated the efficacy of thoracoscopic LAAO in reducing ischemic stroke and systemic embolism. The LAAOS III trial reported a significant reduction in ischemic stroke or systemic embolism (HR 0.67, 95% CI 0.53–0.85; p = 0.001) in patients undergoing surgical LAAO during cardiac surgery, even when most participants continued oral anticoagulation [16]. Similarly, a meta‐analysis by Tsai et al. reported that the surgical LAAO group had lower rates of ischemic stroke (OR 0.655, 95% CI 0.518–0.829; p = 0.0004) and mortality (OR 0.74, 95% CI 0.55–0.99; p = 0.0408) compared to the non‐LAAO group [17]. However, these studies did not observe differences in rhythm outcomes or cardiac functions [17, 18].

Notably, in our study we highlighted the potential role of LAAO in enhancing the durability of PVI. PV reconnection is a well‐established cause of AF recurrence after ablation procedures. Studies have demonstrated that early PV reconnection is an independent predictor of late arrhythmia recurrence, with a higher likelihood of AF recurrence when acute PV reconnection is observed during the procedure. Efremidis et al. reported that PV reconnection detected within 30 min of initial PVI was associated with a significantly increased risk of late recurrence [19]. Chee et al. demonstrated that connections through the LoM could be a significant cause of PVI failure, and that their targeted ablation significantly improved PVI success rates [20]. Additionally, ethanol infusion into the vein of Marshall has been shown to reduce acute PV reconnection and enhance ablation durability in persistent AF patients [21]. Consistent with these findings, the lower rate of left side PV reconnection observed in the LAAO group may be related to modification of epicardial connections around the LAA, left atrial ridge, and LoM region. Compared with open surgical ablation, thoracoscopic epicardial ablation is performed on the beating heart through limited access and may be more dependent on consistent device–tissue contact, local exposure, atrial wall thickness, and epicardial fat, all of which can affect lesion transmurality and durability. In this setting, concomitant LAAO may alter local tissue traction, exposure, or adjacent epicardial continuity around the left PV antrum, left atrial ridge, and LoM region in a way that could influence lesion durability. In our study, the lower rate of left side PV reconnection in the LAAO group should therefore be considered a hypothesis‐generating observation rather than direct proof of a causal electrophysiological mechanism [22].

In the present cohort, the residual postoperative finding was a smooth‐walled basal stump rather than a residual trabeculated LAA remnant [14, 15]. In adBCnodition, there was no overlap between the five patients with a residual smooth‐walled basal stump on postoperative CT and those with left side PV reconnection at redo. Therefore, the electrophysiological significance of a smooth‐walled basal stump remains uncertain in the present study.

LAAO and MACE

In addition to its role in rhythm outcomes, surgical LAAO may reduce MACE. Previous studies, including a meta‐analysis, demonstrated that surgical LAAO significantly reduced MACE rates compared to non‐LAAO [23]. Consistent with these findings, we observed fewer MACEs in the LAAO group, although this difference was not significant. Among the MACEs in our cohort, thrombotic embolic stroke occurred in one patient. This was a peri‐operative stroke, likely influenced by potential atrial stunning effects. Major bleeding events included gastrointestinal bleeding, hemopericardium, and hemoptysis.

At the last follow‐up, 34.8% of patients in the LAAO group and 53.8% of patients in the non‐LAAO group were maintaining oral anticoagulation therapy. Although this difference was not statistically significant, the lower anticoagulation rate in the LAAO group may reflect the effectiveness of LAAO for providing sufficient thromboembolic protection, reducing the reliance on anticoagulation therapy in this group. More research is needed to test this hypothesis.

Residual postoperative anatomy may also be relevant from a thromboembolic perspective [15]. However, the present HF cohort was not designed or powered to determine the independent stroke risk associated with a residual smooth‐walled basal stump.

LAAO with Thoracoscopic Ablation and Atrial Remodeling

Previous studies have shown that thoracoscopic AF ablation improves cardiac function. Van der Heijden et al. reported significant improvements in LAVI, left atrial contractility, and LVEF after thoracoscopic ablation in patients with persistent AF. These improvements were attributed to rhythm restoration and left atrial remodeling, highlighting the procedure's role in cardiac recovery [24]. Similarly, Kim et al. demonstrated that patients with left ventricular dysfunction undergoing thoracoscopic AF ablation experienced substantial and sustained improvements in LVEF during long‐term follow‐up. This finding underscores the effectiveness of thoracoscopic ablation in enhancing cardiac function in AF combined with HF populations [6]. The results of our study corroborate these findings by demonstrating postoperative improvements in LVEF, LAVI, and NT‐proBNP levels, regardless of LAAO status. Our findings suggest that while thoracoscopic ablation plays a pivotal role in restoring rhythm and improving cardiac function and atrial remodeling, the added benefits of LAAO warrant further exploration.

Prior studies have reported that endocardial LAA closure (LAAC) may negatively impact cardiac remodeling, including increases in LA dimension and LV filling pressure [25, 26]. The proposed mechanism for this increase involves the presence of an intra‐atrial device, which can reduce left atrial compliance and impair its reservoir function, thereby elevating filling pressures. Epicardial approaches to LAAO achieve complete external ligation of the LAA, inducing necrosis of the appendage tissue and eliminating both its electrical and physiological functions. This contrasts with endocardial approaches such as LAAC, which involve device placement within the appendage to prevent thrombus formation but may result in incomplete exclusion due to residual leaks or recanalization. These mechanistic differences may partly explain why epicardial LAAO and endocardial LAAC could have different physiological consequences [27]. The complete exclusion of the LAA with epicardial LAAO has been shown to sharply reduce levels of atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP) and brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) immediately post‐procedure, with long‐term downregulation of the renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system observed in subsequent follow‐up. This finding highlights the role of the LAA not only in natriuretic peptide secretion but also in broader neurohormonal modulation, including the regulation of blood pressure and fluid balance. In our cohort, lower postoperative E/e′ was observed in the LAAO group in adjusted analyses. Concomitant LAAO did not appear to worsen postoperative diastolic function in this HF cohort. In contrast, endocardial LAAC may leave portions of the LAA intact, allowing continued secretion of ANP and BNP, which could diminish its impact on neurohormonal pathways [28, 29].

In our cohort, thoracoscopic epicardial AF ablation was associated with overall improvement in cardiac remodeling regardless of LAAO status. Adjusted between‐group differences in NT‐proBNP were not statistically significant. Given the retrospective design, the small non‐LAAO group, and the potential for residual confounding by HF severity and treatment selection, the observed association with lower postoperative E/e′ should be interpreted cautiously.

Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design and small sample size—particularly in the non‐LAAO group—limit generalizability. The observational nature introduces potential selection bias, as LAAO decisions were based on clinical judgment. Slightly lower EF in the non‐LAAO group suggests possible inclusion of more severe HF cases. Residual confounding related to HF phenotype, severity, and postoperative atrial tachyarrhythmia recurrence, including the high proportion of patients with improved EF, cannot be fully excluded despite adjusted analyses. However, no patient was excluded from LAAO due to technical infeasibility, and cases with infeasible PVI were excluded, minimizing this bias. Second, the small sample size limited power to detect significant differences in MACE. Lastly, not all AF recurrence cases underwent redo procedures, potentially underestimating PV reconnection rates. Future studies should include large‐scale, prospective, randomized trials with extended follow‐up to assess long‐term effects of thoracoscopic LAAO on cardiac function and outcomes in high‐risk HF populations.

Conclusions

In conclusion, surgical LAAO during thoracoscopic ablation was associated with rhythm durability without adverse effects on overall cardiac function in patients with AF and HF. Thoracoscopic epicardial AF ablation was associated with improvement in LV systolic function regardless of LAAO status, and concomitant LAAO did not appear to worsen postoperative diastolic function. Further large‐scale prospective studies are warranted to confirm these findings and explore the long‐term effects of rhythm control with LAAO on cardiovascular outcomes.
