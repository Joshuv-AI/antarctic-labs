Public health impact of nuisance biting blackfly (Simulium damnosum s.l.) along the Asejire dam in Southwest, Nigeria
Authors: Dauda Ishola Azeez, Fatimah Oluwakemi Abdussalam, Enan Adamani, Kamilu Ayo Fasasi, Olabanji Ahmed Surakat, Joseph Kumbur, Juliana Amanyi-Enegela, Omoi Samuel, Kehinde Olutoyin Ademolu, Quadri Olusegun Adeshina, Monsuru Adebayo Adeleke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBlack flies remain a global public health concern, particularly through their biting behaviour which is a crucial risk factor in disease transmission. The present study seeks to investigate the public health implications of the biting behaviour of Simulium damnosum s.l along the Asejire dam, bordering Oyo and Osun State, Nigeria.MethodsSimulium damnosum s.l were collected using the standard human landing collection (HLC) between 0700 and 1800 across four study areas within first-line communities around the Asejire dam between December, 2024 and November, 2025. Collection was recorded hourly while the daily, monthly, and annual biting rate (ABR) were calculated using standard formulas.ResultA total of 9857 blackflies were collected with variations in abundance across the sites (P-value = 0.325). Osun had the higher flies collection 6685 (67.82%). The hourly catches varied in locations and bimodal biting peak was observed. Also, Simulium damnosum s.l abundance was high upstream of the dam than mid- and downstream. The monthly biting rate was more than 1000 bites/person/month in each of the study locations. The biting intensity (99,939.03) along the Oyo-Osun border is high, causing serious biting nuisance in the communities.ConclusionThe presence and persistence of black fly bites across the study communities along the border poses a great threat to disease transmission. Therefore, there is the need for an effective vector control measure targeted at preventing human-vector contact by the government of both states with a view to decimating fly abundance and reducing transmission risk.Keywords: Asejire dam, Osun, Oyo, Onchocerciasis, Biting rate, Breeding sites, Borderline

Background

Onchocerciasis and other skin diseases are important community health parasitic disease with over 125 million individuals estimated to be at risk of the infection globally [1]. It is characterized with blindness and skin depigmentation with over 90% of the population at risk of the disease in Africa [2]. More than 30 countries in tropical Africa; 6 countries of America and 1 country in the Arabian Peninsula are endemic to the disease [3]. West Africa is regarded as the seat of endemic cases of onchocerciasis in the World and Nigeria is the leading country with the largest number of infected persons in the whole of Africa [2]. The disease is ranked as the leading cause of blindness [4].

Southwest Nigeria, encompassing Oyo and Osun states, possesses ecological characteristics highly suitable for blackfly vector breeding, such as fast-flowing water bodies (Osun and Oba rivers, and Asejire dam) with suitable vegetational substrate and rocks along the border. The shared borders between these states create a continuum where human populations and blackfly vectors can move freely, potentially influencing disease transmission dynamics [5]. Mass drug administration has been ongoing in Oyo State since 1991, with disease interruption suspected in 2022 [2, 6], whereas in Osun State, active transmission persisted until 2015 [2].

Anthropophilic blackfly species from the genus Simulium are the vectors of Onchocerciasis. The S. damnosum Theobald complex is the most prevalent type that impacts African communities [7]. They are mostly attached to any rocky substrates underwater and to trailing vegetation in their immature stages. They reproduce in fast-moving, immensely oxygenated streams or rivers. The geographic distribution of onchocerciasis is influenced by their preference for these particular breeding habitats, which limits their presence mainly to communities near viable breeding sites [2]. The ecology and behavior of the blackfly, especially its appetitive flight range, is less than 15 km from breeding sites [2]. This eventually have a significant impact on the spread, intensity, and epidemic levels of onchocerciasis. Consequently, the most severely impacted communities are nearly 15-kilometer radius to the breeding site [2]. The economic burden in endemic regions of Nigeria has been considerable, underscoring the critical need for sustained public health initiatives and resource allocation to reduce the disease burden [8].

Adult Simulium damnosum are exclusively day biters, exhibiting notable hourly biting with identifiable peaks that are crucial for the eradication of onchocerciasis⁠ across West and East Africa. S. damnosum s.l. typically shows a bimodal diurnal biting pattern of a morning peak (usually between about 07:00–11:00) and an afternoon/evening peak (usually between about 14:00–18:00). Between these peaks, there is a “midday lull” of lower activity, often coinciding with the hottest hours of the day [9–11].

An annual biting rate of less than 1000 bites/person/year in savannah zone was considered acceptable in a previously abandoned areas [12] and any bite above 1000 per person per year is assumed to cause biting nuisance. However, according to Stapley [13] transmission can persist at a very low annual biting rate (ABR) between approximately 70–300bites/person/year, although depending on the residents’ size and exposure heterogeneity. Although it has been reported in 2021 that parasites can be efficiently established in a community even at low annual biting rates, implying a higher reinfection risk than previously assumed once mass drug administration (MDA) stops [14]. Therefore, the study seeks to investigate the dynamics of black fly distribution and biting nuisance across first line-communities along the Asejire dam with a view to understanding their public health implication.

Materials and methods

Study areas

This research was carried out in four first-line communities along the border of Oyo-Osun, southwestern Nigeria, which are Aro and Molosun (Osun State) and Olukeye and Jago (Oyo State). The locations were selected in the region with their peculiarities, as the river passes through each of the communities. In addition, Aro village (latitude 7.620553, longitude 4.155375) was selected along the Oba river system in Iwo Local Government Area, Molosun (latitude 7.526444, longitude 4.196735) was also selected along the Osun river system in Ayedire Local Government Area. The two water bodies originate differently and converge along the border between Osun and Oyo to form a dam. To the west of the Dam is Asejire community in Osun State while to the east of the dam is Olukeye in Oyo State. Olukeye (latitude 7.3475645, longitude 4.118329) was selected as another community where the dam is located in Oyo State. In addition, Jago (latitude 7.3107578, longitude 4.110348) located far downstream of the dam Oyo State. Jago was also selected as a community, because it receives water from the dam. Each of their peculiarities may enable them to have a different composition of flies and thus their vectoral capacity.

Ethical approval

Ethical clearance was obtained from the Ministry of Health of Oyo State and Osun State with Reference Number AD 13/479/624 and OSHREC/PRS/569T/740 respectively.

Community enlightenment, mobilization, and consent

The research team was led by each of the Local Neglected Tropical Disease (LNTD) coordinator to visit the village heads. The research was explained to them in their local language and were allowed to ask questions to avoid ambiguity. Informed consent was verbally obtained from the study communities. After a successful mobilization of the first-line communities, two human landing collectors (HLC) were recruited in each of the four communities (a total of eight collectors) through the community leaders and were trained on blackfly collection using the human landing method [15]. The study participants (both researchers and fly collectors) were treated with ivermectin before and after the research. This is necessary to ascertain that the participants are not unnecessarily exposed to the disease.

Collection of Adult Blackflies

A pair of dark-skinned Human Landing Collectors (HLC) exposed one leg at designated catching point at about 5 m close to the breeding site along the river system. Each of the collectors sat under a shaded tree for an hour and alternated. Collections were performed between 07:00 h to 18:00 for three consecutive days every month between December, 2024 and November, 2025 (comprising 6 months wet season and 6 months dry season). The collection was done using standardized HLC method [12]. The hourly collections were recorded and preserved in 80% ethanol in labelled 30 ml collection bottles. The samples were transferred to the Molecular Epidemiology and Vector Biology (MOVEB) laboratory (ISO 15189 certified) of the Department of Animal and Environmental Biology, Osun State University for morphological identification.

Morphological identification of collected samples

The collected flies were morphologically identified under a Dissecting microscope (Model: SZM-45) using the main key features including the swollen fore tarsus, white band of the hind leg (basitarsus), presence of setae on scutum/scutellum in the thorax and others as described by the World Health Organization (WHO) for adult black fly identification [12]. Only the morphologically identified black flies of the Simulium damnosum s.l were preserved in 80% ethanol until further analysis.

Determination of biting rates

The daily biting rate was determined by dividing the total number of blackflies caught in three days by the number of catching days and the number of catchers. The monthly biting rate was calculated by multiplying the number of blackflies caught by the number of days in the month and then dividing by the number of catching days in the month. The total number of blackflies caught in a year was multiplied by the number of days in a year, and the result was divided by the number of catching days to determine the annual bite rate.

Data analysis

The data obtained from the study were subjected to a one-way analysis of variance of blackfly sites along the dam. Statistical significance in variation was determined at a p-value of less than 0.05 (p < 0.05) at 95% confidence interval using IBM SPSS Statistics version 21. Descriptive statistics were done in Microsoft Excel version 21.

Discussion

The abundance of blackflies observed upstream, downstream and midstream in this study showed that the natural flow of water upstream support high population of the blackflies while at the midstream and downstream it is dependent on the release of water from the Asejire dam. This observation is not limited to Asejire dam alone as it has been reported in previous studies where abundance of blackflies is dependent on the release of water from dam [17] and has been reported to have a positive impact on onchocerciasis vectors in the Merowe dam in Sudan [18].

The public health and socio-economic impact of the blackfly-biting nuisance cannot be overemphasized. The observation of the morning peaks between 9:00am to 12:00pm aligns with previous report by [9]. Similarly, the 9:00am to 11:00am peaks observed is in consonance with report by [9] who reported similar pattern of biting behaviour in Osun State.

Furthermore, the evening peaks observed was 3:00pm to 5:00pm is in tandem with [9] who also reported the same evening peaks. The biting peak observed in wet season between 4:00pm to 6: 00pm coincides with the earlier report of [19, 20]. Overall, this shows the bimodal biting peaks of the flies and could indicate that sunlight and temperature play a crucial role in abundance and biting behaviour of blackflies. The higher evening peaks as compared to the morning peaks is a characteristic of savannah flies [10, 19] aligning with studies by [11, 21]. The observation of unimodal peaks in the wet season at Molosun and in dry season at Olukeye, conforms with the report of [10].

Biting activity rises from early morning as temperature increases in all four locations studied and reaches peaks before or near midday, falls when temperatures are very high at around 12:00 pm to 15:00pm, and rises again as late-afternoon temperatures moderate and decrease, this is termed bimodal biting patterns in previous studies [22]. Rainfall is a major determinant of reduced biting in the evening hours, as previously reported [23]. Very strong winds can also physically displace flies and blow them away, thereby causing a reduction in biting [23]. In several endemic communities, water collection, animal rearing, harvesting of farm produce, and other activities increase human-fly contact [11, 15] therefore, making human-fly contact easy. These peak hours correspond to the highest risk of Onchocerca volvulus transmission [19]. This implies that the fly collected may be parous or nulliparous [10].

In December and January (dry season), May to October (wet season) and even November, biting rates in Aro and Molosun exceeded the WHO threshold. It can be inferred that the abundance of blackflies in these communities during the wet season and the onset of dry season is higher than that of the late dry season, maybe as a result of its position to the dam (upstream). This corroborates the reports of [24, 25] which revealed that the abundance of black flies is higher in wet season than in the dry season.

The daily biting rate observed in each of the locations is more than the threshold of 30 bites/person/day reported as the WHO daily biting threshold [25]. This daily biting rate in each location is in tandem with the report of [26, 27] where they reported daily biting rate greater than 30 bites/person/day.

The monthly biting rates reported across West and Central Africa exhibit substantial geographical variation, reflecting differences in vector abundance, ecological conditions, and transmission intensity. Within Nigeria, documented bite rates include 3,160 bites/person/month along the Osun–Ekiti border in the southwest, 3,096–3,534 bites/person/month in the Imo River Basin, and 5,177–5,807 bites/person/month in Kachia, Kaduna State. Similar levels of 4,960 to 6,169 bites/person/month have been recorded in persistent onchocerciasis foci in Ghana. In contrast, hyperendemic areas such as the river valleys of Cameroon report markedly higher biting rates, often exceeding 30,000–50,000 bites/person/month. This gradient in biting pressure highlights the influence of local hydrology, vector species composition, and breeding site productivity on Simulium population dynamics and subsequent disease transmission risk [16, 26, 28–31].

The annual biting rate in this study exceeded the rate of 10,231.8 reported by for the border area between Osun and Ekiti states, but was lower than the rate of 530,322 reported by in Cameroon [16, 31]. Despite the dearth of result on these blackflies’ infectivity, a relatively low infectious fly population may jeopardize elimination of the disease [32] flag ABR > 1,000 bites/person/year or daily rates > 30 bites/person/day as thresholds for significant exposure and operational concern [16, 29].The World Health Organization’s Onchocerciasis Control Programme (WHO/OCP) cites an acceptable daily biting rate of approximately 30 bites per person per day as a nuisance threshold rate, DBR beyond this is regarded as excessive human exposure [27].

Exceeding the World Health Organization threshold of 1000bites/person/year, elevated blackfly biting rates constitute a significant public health burden that extends beyond vector-borne disease transmission to encompass substantial nuisance effects within affected communities. Each bite introduces salivary anticoagulants and inflammatory mediators that elicit severe dermatitis, pruritus, localized allergic responses, and secondary bacterial infections resulting from scratching. Chronic cutaneous lesions are frequently observed among residents, and persistent irritation contributes to sleep disturbance, fatigue, and diminished occupational productivity. At the community level, high biting intensity disrupts livelihoods by deterring outdoor agricultural, fishing, and water-collection activities during peak diurnal periods, thereby compromising food security and economic output. School attendance declines as children avoid exposure, while social and religious gatherings are curtailed. The sustained exposure also generates psychological stress, with affected populations reporting anxiety and a pervasive sense of vulnerability, which further erodes social cohesion and overall quality of life.

Economically, biting rates above the specified threshold are associated with reduced labor availability, increased household expenditure on symptomatic management of dermatological sequelae, and diminished external investment in endemic areas. Although, Simulium spp. are recognized vectors of Onchocerca volvulus, the nuisance associated with high biting densities persists as an independent public health concern even following interruption of parasite transmission, a phenomenon acknowledged by the WHO in its vector control guidelines. Consequently, effective interventions must integrate transmission control and nuisance mitigation strategies, including larviciding of breeding habitats, promotion of personal protective measures, community education on temporal biting patterns, and systematic entomological surveillance to monitor biting rates relative to established benchmarks. These findings underscore that when biting exceeds 1000 bites/person/year, nuisance control should be regarded as a legitimate and complementary objective to disease elimination within public health programming.

The second problem is the WHO’s eradication criteria for onchocerciasis, which emphasize infectivity rather than raw biting rate thus emphasizing the need for the infectivity status of the collected flies.

According to [30], ≤ 0.05% infective blackflies (5 infectious flies per 10,000) is an often-mentioned infectivity threshold that is consistent with stopping transmission. Although ABR “exceeding WHO threshold of 1,000 bites/person/year” is mentioned in some recent studies as a programmatic risk marker, this 1,000 ABR value is employed as a practical high-risk exposure cut-off in certain field work and is not the official exclusion criteria [29]. The intense biting activity of the flies predisposes residents to onchocerciasis in addition to its socio-economic burden and public health consequences. This is not limited to reduction in prevention in discharging their day-to-day activities, farming activities, affecting farm produce and invariably economic loss to the state and the country at large.

Conclusion

The present study reveals the biting activities upstream, midstream and downstream of the Asejire dam. It also detects an intense black fly biting activity across the Oyo-Osun State borders, a pertinent risk factor for disease transmission and nuisance, which may prevent residents from their daily livelihood activities, which could lead to impoverishment and economic loss. Therefore, the stringent need by the government of both states to intensify proactive vector surveillance and control with a view to dwindling fly bites and invariably mitigating disease transmission. The annual biting rate recorded along the border (99,939.03) is higher than the reported biting rates in Southwestern Nigeria, showing a remarkable biting nuisance in the studied communities, which may invariably affect productivity and socio-economic impact in the region. Effective elimination strategy along the borderline needs vector control, as the breeding sites are highly productive and produce more blackflies that can easily spread the disease in a jiffy as long as a few patients of onchocerciasis are in the community or have migrated to the community. Vector control interventions like larviciding of the breeding sites, personal protection with the use of repellent and wearing of protective clothing are highly essential in the region so as to curb the biting nuisance and its effects on the residents of the communities along the border.

Surveillance and vector control (e.g., human landing catches, timing of vector control or community education), sampling, and protection strategies must focus on these morning and late-afternoon biting peaks for easy control and elimination of the onchocerciasis vectors in the region.

Limitation of the study

A limitation of this study was that the Onchocerca volvulus infection status of the collected Simulium blackflies was not assessed. Molecular pool screening of the vectors to detect infective L3 larvae was not conducted, and breeding site evaluations to determine the cytospecies composition of the S. damnosum complex were not performed. These analyses were outside the scope and resources of the present investigation. Consequently, it remains unknown whether the blackflies captured during the survey period were actively transmitting onchocerciasis, and the specific sibling species responsible for biting in the study area were not identified. This limits direct inference about ongoing transmission intensity and vector-parasite compatibility in the sampled communities.
