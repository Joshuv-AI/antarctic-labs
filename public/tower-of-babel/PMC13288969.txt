Acceptance of an immediate postpartum intrauterine contraceptive device and associated factors among women who gave birth in public hospitals of Addis Ababa, Ethiopia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Wondossen Teklesilassie Geberemariam, Baize Mekonen, Hussen Mekonnen Asfaw
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDespite the postpartum intrauterine device being highly effective, inexpensive, non-hormonal and reversible, its acceptance is very low in developing countries including Ethiopia. Considering the importance of the use of immediate postpartum intrauterine contraceptives is a valuable means to keep women and children healthy.ObjectivesThis study aimed to assess the acceptance of an immediate postpartum contraceptive device and associated factors among women who gave birth in selected public hospitals in Addis Ababa.MethodA health institution-based cross-sectional survey was conducted among 336 women who gave birth in selected public health facilities in Addis Ababa from the beginning of February to March 2021. Information was gathered by using interviewer-administered questionnaires. Data were double-entered in Epidata V.4.6 statistical package, cleaned and exported to IBM SPSS statistics V.25 for analysis. Logistic regression models were used to predict the association of outcome variables and adjusted for possible confounders.ResultA total of 42 (12.5%) of women accepted to use the immediate use of postpartum intrauterine devices. Woman’s employment status such as (private employees (adjusted OR, AOR=4.269, 95% CI (1.280 to 14.234) (p<0.018), daily labourer (AOR=7.373, 95% CI (1.534 to 35.433) (p<0.013), student (AOR=11.004, 95% CI (1.602 to 75.572) (p<0.015)), and monthly income of the mother Birr greater than 7901.00 (AOR=6.589, 95% CI (1.736 to 25.009) (p<0.006) were found to be associated with acceptance of the immediate postpartum contraceptive device.ConclusionsThe acceptance of immediate postpartum intrauterine contraceptive device was low, that is 12.5%, maternal education, occupation, monthly income and attitude of women were associated with acceptance. Addressing women’s awareness by using target-oriented health education and training of healthcare workers was recommended.

Introduction

Family planning (FP) is a crucial basic human right for the well-being of women, families and communities.1 Contraception acceptance and use, as well as safer sexual habits, are improved by postpartum FP and empowerment programmes. Rapid and uncontrolled population growth is one of the most significant concerns facing rising countries.2 3

The immediate intrauterine contraceptive system is extremely effective during the postpartum period, immediately reversible after termination, non-hormonal, long-acting contraception that can be started within 10 min or up to 48 hours after the third stage of labour and is safe for the majority of postpartum women. While the majority of women prefer to delay their next pregnancy immediately after delivery, 70% of them are not using contraception; the majority of them were from developing countries.4 According to the report, women who do not use contraception are responsible for 86% of unintended postpartum pregnancies, and abortion is used to end 88% of pregnancies.5

In the first year following birth, the postpartum intrauterine contraceptive device (PPIUCD) is used to prevent unexpected and closely spaced pregnancies.4 During FP approaches, the mother and couples are strongly driven during the postpartum time, exposed to unplanned pregnancies and having an adverse consequence of pregnancy termination, early labour, postpartum haemorrhage (PPH), underweight infant, fetal death and maternal morbidity.4 6 In most healthcare institutions, intrauterine contraception is readily available. However, IUCD is poorly accepted by their spouse or family members due to denial, misinformation or fear of associated difficulties.68

Pregnancy-related maternal fatalities are disproportionately and unjustly rising. Globally, 295 000 women died during pregnancy and the postpartum period, including childbirth, in 2017 owing to pregnancy-related causes.9 In 2020, the world population is expected to grow at a rate of roughly 1.05% per year, with an average annual increase of 81 million people. High population growth rates put pressure on developing countries’ infrastructure, expansion, housing, healthcare, educational facilities, work prospects and efforts to reduce maternal and infant mortality.10 It accounts for 86% of global maternal deaths, including in countries in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) and South Asia, especially in developing countries.9

The ratio of maternal mortality in Ethiopia was also estimated at 412 per 100 000 live births in Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS) 2016, which is volatile during labour and delivery, and postpartum time. In Ethiopia, the highest Total Fertility Rate is 4.6%, given such unregulated population growth and its effect on society’s socioeconomic development, great emphasis has been put on FP, which plays a major role in reducing fertility worldwide. However, the unmet need for FP is 22%.11

The prevalence rate of contraceptives is 15%, that estimated approximately 16% of married couples in Nigeria have an unmet need for contraception, either for spacing births or to limit births, and are at risk of unintended pregnancy. Among users of contraception, intrauterine devices (IUDs) are commonly used for FP in Nigeria.12

Short pregnancy and birth periods are correlated with abortion and negative health complications, premature labour, PPH, low birth weight infants, neonatal death, and suggest growth and development and malnutrition.13 In comparison to developed countries, the rate of unintended pregnancy is higher in developing countries. Sexual, FP and reproductive healthcare are also required to prevent unplanned pregnancy.14

The study conducted in Ruanda indicated that health education of mothers and partners on PPIUCD, training of healthcare providers and availability of supplies to provide PPIUCD influenced the use of PPIUCD.15 However, the use of IUCD is still very low in SSA, including Ethiopia where the level of fertility and unmet need for FP is high.16 17

In general, it can be concluded from the above-mentioned discussion that, relative to other methods, the acceptance of PPIUCD is very low, and given the lack of understanding of the reasons for the stagnation or decrease in the use of IUCD in Ethiopia, the primary goal of this study was to identify the gap to address the rate of acceptance and the factors that affect the acceptance of PPIUCD during the immediate delivery of postpartum women for the use of PPIUCD and to fill the gap in the study area.

Methods

Study area

The study was carried out in selected public hospitals of Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia. According to the World Population Review 2024, the city population was over 4.8 million people and the growth rate was 4.4%. Addis Ababa has 13 government hospitals spread across 11 subcities (five federal hospitals, six under the Addis Ababa health bureau and two hospitals used by the uniformed community). Based on the lottery methods, four hospitals were picked. Therefore, Tikur Anbessa Specialised Hospital, ALERT Referral Hospital, St. Peter Specialized Hospital and Gandhi Memorial Hospital were selected. All public hospitals provide free FP and prenatal (antenatal, delivery and postnatal) services including IUDs.

Study design and sampling procedures

An institution-based cross-sectional survey was conducted in selected hospitals in Addis Ababa, from the beginning of February to the end of March 2021.

The sample size was determined using a single population formula with the assumption of factors associated with the uptake of the immediate contraceptive devices being 28.1%15 with a 5% level of significance, and a margin of error of 0.05, with the addition of a 10% non-response rate. With these assumptions, the minimum required sample size is 341. The sample size for different factors was calculated and the highest sample size of 341 was taken to minimise sampling errors.

Respondents were selected principally using lottery methods or simple random sampling. Initially, unique identification numbers were given to each public hospital. second, A total of four public hospitals was selected by lottery methods, then the proportion of sample size allocations was carried out according to the patient load in each public hospital. Systematic random sampling was used to select the study subjects from the selected sites, using the client’s admission cards as a sampling frame.

Data collection procedures

Data were collected using interviewer-administered structured questionnaires immediately after delivery. The data collection tools were adopted from previous similar studies3 15 18 and adapted to the local context. The data collectors and supervisors were recruited based on their previous experience in data collection, relevance of qualification (BSc in midwifery) and ability in the local language. Training was given for three consultative days to make them familiar with the tool and interview techniques. Emphasis was given to ethical issues, privacy and safety of the study subjects and the interviewers. The data collection tools were pretested in the randomly selected hospital out of the study site on 10% of the total sample size.

A fieldwork manual was developed by the principal investigator and used by all research teams. To ensure the quality of the data and minimise inter-interviewer variation, about 5% of the respondents were reinterviewed at random by the principal investigator and supervisors and checked for consistency. In addition, daily checks and follow-up were done by the supervisors and investigator.

Measurement

The primary outcome variable was self-reported acceptance to use immediate PIUCD. Accepted to use PPIUCD to delay or to avoid pregnancy immediately after delivery?” women were free to respond ‘yes’ or ‘No’. Variables known to be associated with acceptance were included in the analysis to provide an adjusted estimate of association.

Covariates included age, education, income, occupation and marital status. Obstetric characteristics such as antenatal care (ANC) visits, mode of delivery, use of FP, number of pregnancies, number of living children and desire to have children.

Postpartum intrauterine contraceptive device

Immediate postpartum IUCD insertion into the maternal uterus/womb after post-placental delivery, administered within 10 min to 48 hours by either SVD or CS.3

Knowledge of women about PPIUCD

The total number of responses to six information objects, with a minimum score of 0 and a maximum score of 6, was calculated. It was categorised as ‘high’ to quantify the information based on the percentage of people who knew 80% or more of the different qualities immediately PPIUCD, ‘moderate’ to those who knew 60%–79%, and ‘low’ to those who knew less than 60%.18 19

Data analysis

The completeness of the questionnaire was manually reviewed, coded and double-entered into EpiData V.4.6 software, to check its consistency then exported to SPSS for windows V.25; for statistical analysis used were percentage, frequency, bivariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis. Variables found to be (p<0.05 at the bivariate level) were selected and included in the multiple logistic regression analysis model to calculate the adjusted OR (AOR) with a 95% CI to estimate association and to control the potential confounding variables.

Factors associated with acceptance of immediate PPIUCDs

In the adjusted analysis maternal education, occupation and monthly income were found to be associated with acceptance of immediate PPIUCDs. Immediate postpartum women who were elementary (1–8) educated were less likely to accept to use immediate PPIUCD than women who were college and above educated (AOR=0.160, 95% CI (0.040 to 0.649) (p<0.010). On the other hand, women who were private employees (AOR=4.269, 95% CI (1.280 to 14.234) (p<0.018), daily labourers (AOR=7.373, 95% CI (1.534 to 35.433) (p<0.013) and students (AOR=11.004, 95% CI (1.602 to 75.572) (p<0.015) were more likely to accept than women who were housewives.

Women who had a monthly income of Birr 3201–5250, (AOR=4.731, 95% CI (1.497 to 14.949) (p<0.008), Birr 5251–7900, (AOR=5.712, 95% CI (1.489 to 21.910) (p<0.011); income greater than Birr 7901 (AOR=6.589, 95% CI (1.736 to 25.009) (p<0.006) were more likely to accept immediate PPIUCD than those who had monthly income less than Birr 3200 (table 5).

Discussion

Controlling and stabilising the population is essential for the country’s socioeconomic and social development. As a result, in a low-resource country like Ethiopia, the available contraceptive methods, especially long-acting ones like IUCD, are very feasible and cost-effective. This study aimed to find out how many women in public hospitals were accepted to use immediate PPIUCD and what factors influenced their decision.

The acceptance and its related factors of immediate PPIUCD were found to be low in this research in comparison to other previous studies that were conducted in the different study areas. The most common reasons for rejecting immediate PPIUCD were not being a woman’s preferred form 142 (42.3%), fear of side effects 132 (39.3%) and lack of knowledge about PPIUCD 118 (35.1%).

This result was backed up by a study conducted in the Ambo area, Oromia Region, which found that women preferred the method 32.5% of the time, were fearful of side effects 29.6% and lacked awareness about PPIUCD 24.5% of the time.19

The overall acceptance rate to use immediate PPIUCD was 12.5%. This finding was very low as compared with the study conducted in north India, primary health centre (39%),20 in the Marathwada region, India (25%),8 and in Rwanda, a mixed-method study (28.1%).15

This variation might be related to the study setting, emphasis on actual use (usage) rather than intended use (intention), healthcare education access, counselling at ANC regarding PPIUCD, level of understanding, availability of IUCD due to COVID-19 and myths about PPIUCD.

The acceptance of immediate PPIUCD was significantly associated among women who had primary (–8) education, attending 84% less likely to accept to use immediate PPIUCD than those women with education college and above (AOR=0.160, 95% CI (0.040 to 0.649) (p<0.010).

This was in line with the findings of the 2016 EDHS, which found that (49%) of currently married women were not using contraceptive methods and 2% were uncertain. Contraceptive use increased dramatically with the level of education, with at least 51% of women with higher education using FP, compared with 31% of women with no education.11 Inconsistent with Rwanda, those mothers who had attended tertiary education were three times more likely to have uptake to use immediate PPIUCD than mothers who had no education. In Bahir Dar, mothers who were college-educated and above were 21.24 times higher for IUCD relative to women who were unable to read and write, but mothers who were primary education were 8.08 times higher as compared with read and write and Ambo. Those mothers who were diploma and above were three times more likely to be involved than those with no education.15 19

This could be because education influences health behaviour; educated women are more empowered to decide on fertility control, need to discuss contraceptive issues openly, have a better understanding of how to use PPIUCD, and can speculate on risk and benefit issues; and the possible reason for variation could be due to the study setting, sample size differences or sociodemographic differences.

The finding of maternal occupation to immediately delivered women who worked for private employers and private workers, daily labourers and students was four times more likely to accept to use immediate PPIUCD than women who were housewives (AOR=4.269, 95% CI (1.280 to 14.234) (p<0.018), 7 times more likely to accept to use immediate PPIUCD than women who were housewives (AOR=7.373, 95% CI (1.534 to 35.433) (p<0.013) and 11 times more likely to accept to use immediate PPIUCD than women who were housewives (AOR=11.004, 95% CI (1.602 to 75.572) (p<0.015), respectively. This finding was supported by the finding that was done in Ambo town which was private employers.19 However, daily labourers and students were also significantly associated in this research, which may be attributed to sociodemographic differences and occupation status (in Addis Ababa many women work as daily labourers due to the large city but in Ambo few women can participate as a daily labourer and similarly many reproductive-age women have scholarship chances including the extension in Addis Ababa).

The participant’s monthly income contributes to immediate PPIUCD insertion. In this study, women who had a monthly income increase (higher monthly income) were more likely to use immediate PPIUCD than women who had a low monthly income. Participants with monthly incomes of 3201–5250, 5251–7900 and women with monthly incomes greater than 7901 were 5 times more often accepted to use immediate PPIUCD when compared with those who had monthly incomes less than 3200 (AOR=4.731, 95% CI (1.497 to 14.949) (p<0.008), 6 times more likely to accept to use immediate PPIUCD than women with monthly incomes less than 3200 (AOR=5.712, 95% CI (1.489 to 21.910) (p<0.011) and monthly incomes greater than 7901 were 7 times more likely to accept to use immediate PPIUCD than participants with a monthly income less than 3200 (AOR=6.589, 95% CI (1.736 to 25.009) (p<0.006) significantly associated to the acceptance rate and factors associated to PPIUCD, respectively.

This finding highly increased congruently to EDHS 2016, modern contraceptive usage increases income; this was 20% for mothers in the lowest wealth to 47% for mothers in the highest income11 and also congruent to that of the study done in Ambo town.19 The reason may be due to better-off women getting the best exposure to contraceptive methods due to access to IUCD everywhere including private clinics and a better understanding of PPIUCD.

In addition to the above, the current study also revealed that acceptance of the use of immediate PPIUCD increased significantly among women with favourable (positive) attitudes. This was an attitude (43.2%) that was favourable, but 56.8% were unfavourable attitudes due to this report. The majority of participants had a negative attitude about PPIUCD.

Participants who had favourable (positive) attitudes about PPIUCD were 14 times more likely to accept the use of PPIUCD (AOR=14.457, 95% CI (4.999 to 41.806) (p<0.000) than women with unfavourable (negative) attitudes. This finding was higher than the study done in Mekele town women who had a positive approach to long-acting reversible contraception.21 This disparity may be attributed to attitudes, perceptions of the advantages and complications of PPIUCD providing counselling, sociodemographic differences in a study subject, setting area and sample size.

Our study has various limitations; the cross-sectional nature of the study may make it difficult to determine the direction of the correlation between study variables, and the association can only be discussed in terms of plausible explanations. There was a danger of social desirability bias. The study was limited to the public facilities. Hence, it does not represent the proportion of acceptance to use immediate PPIUCD in private health facilities and governmental health centres and does not include the general community.

This study’s merits include the use of a random sampling technique with a high sample size. Again, precautions were taken when selecting experienced data collectors.

In conclusion, the acceptance of PPICD is low; the main reason is fear of side effects. Occupation, educational background and monthly income of women were found to be associated with acceptance of PPIUCDs among women in the study area.

Finally, addressing women’s awareness using target-oriented health education to increase knowledge and acceptance, and future research should investigate the potential influence of acceptance among women, including the private sector users. Further analytical studies are recommended to identify the acceptance and discontinuity with follow-up.
