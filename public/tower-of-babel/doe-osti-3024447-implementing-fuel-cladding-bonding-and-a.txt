 ABSTRACT

 Fuel rods irradiated in light-water reactors to burnup values above 45 MWd/kgU are subject to the
formation of a chemical and mechanical bond between the fuel and cladding upon gap closure. The
formation of the bond subsequently inhibits the ability of fission gases released from the fuel to flow freely
to the plenum of the rod. The flowing of gases within fuel rods is referred to as axial gas communication.
During transients, such as loss of coolant accidents, the bond may influence cladding deformation prior to
breaking. Upon bond breakage, gases are able to more freely communicate to the lower pressure regions
of the rod. The impact of bonding on gas communication and the ballooning behavior of the cladding
during a loss of coolant accident is of interest to the nuclear industry in support of burnup extension for
the existing light-water reactor fleet.
 In this report, a model to capture the effects of fuel cladding bonding in the BISON fuel performance
code is presented. The theory of the model along with implementation testing is provided. A summary of
a previously developed axial gas communication is given to set the stage for how the two models may be
coupled together. A full-length pressurized-water reactor fuel rod demonstration is highlighted to evaluate
the impact of including bonding on axial gas communication calculations using a preliminary coupling
methodology. An overview of the next steps regarding the modeling of bonding, axial gas communication,
and a more tightly coupled framework is also provided.

 1. INTRODUCTION

 As part of the life cycle for nuclear fuel rods, high pressures due to the continual creation and containment of
gases are an area of concern for safe operation as industry pursues higher discharge burnups. The performance
of high-burnup fuel rods containing restructured fuel and higher rod internal pressures during an accident
scenario, such as a loss of coolant accident (LOCA), remains an active area of research.
 Initially, the free volume of the fuel rod consists of a plenum, voids and cracks within the fuel, and the
fuel-to-cladding gap acts as a reservoir for the fill gases at an equilibrium pressure. As irradiation progresses,
fission gases are released into the free volume and impact the pressure along with dimensional changes that
consume some of the internal volume. At higher burnups above 45 MWd/kgU [1], a chemical and mechanical
bonding between the fuel and cladding can form. This bond changes the physical properties of the system,
limiting the free volume for gases by preventing separation of the fuel and cladding during power decreases.
Additional cracking within the fuel or the breaking of the bond due to tensile stresses may result in the
formation of new pathways for the contained gases to flow. The flow of the gas within the rod due to pressure
gradients that form is referred to as axial gas communication.
 Axial gas communication occurs throughout irradiation. Early in life, the fuel-to-cladding gap is open,
providing an almost instantaneous transport of released gases, which result in fast equilibration times. After
bond formation, the gas flow between different regions of the rod that have different pressures is severely
impacted as flow is restricted through the fueled region that has a relatively low permeability. The importance
of reduced gas communication is magnified during a LOCA. Strong pressure differentials may be present that
lead to localized ballooning of the cladding due to the impeded gas flow. As ballooning occurs, the pressure
gradient may increase causing further ballooning and potentially leading to cladding rupture. Coupling this
with large amounts of transient fission gas release potentially being released during the LOCA, the rod internal
pressure will continue to increase. If rupture occurs, the decay of the high plenum pressure to the system
pressure depends upon the severity of flow blockage. A strong pressure differential across severely fragmented
fuel may lead to dispersal of the fragments to the primary coolant system. On the other hand, the presence of
a fuel cladding bond may reduce ballooning and improve coolability of the rods. Therefore, the ability to
capture both phenomena in fuel performance simulations of high-burnup fuel subjected to a LOCA transient
is of interest.
 This report summarizes activities undertaken to begin the coupling of a fuel cladding bonding and axial
gas communication models in the BISON ¹ code [2] to assess high-burnup fuel rod performance. The report
is structured as follows: Section 2 presents an overview of the theory underpinning the implemented fuel
cladding bonding model, Section 3 summarizes past developments of the axial gas communication model
available in BISON, Section 4 describes the rudimentary coupling strategy adopted in this work as well as
provides details into the steps required to ensure a tight coupling between the two models, and Section 5
highlights the implementation testing of the bonding model and the LOCA demonstration problem to assess
impact of including bonding effects in axial gas communication studies. The report concludes with sections
summarizing the key findings of the work (see Section 6), a collection of developments necessary for future
work (see Section 7), and a list of publications related to this work from the current research year (see
Section 8).

 2. CONSTITUTIVE MODELS FOR FUEL CLADDING BONDING

 This section presents the finalized constitutive models for high-burnup fuel with bonding, which have
been incorporated into the main BISON code branch, building on the earlier implementation described in
Reference [3].
 Within a fuel rod due to the combined effect of irradiation and the external pressure imposed by the
coolant, the fuel experiences an outward expansion, while the cladding undergoes a inward reduction in radius.
The mechanisms causing these dimensional changes within the fuel include thermal expansion, gaseous and
solid fission product swelling, densification, cracking, and creep. Within the fuel, the mechanisms include
thermal expansion, irradiation growth, and creep. These dimensional changes results in a reduction of the
fuel cladding gap leading to the eventual contact between the two materials. After prolonged contact between
the fuel and cladding, a bond may form. The model for capture the effects of fuel cladding bonding is broken
into two parts: mechanical and thermal.
 The mechanical framework describing the fuel-cladding bonding is formulated by assuming bonding
exists and was developed to work in a 2D axisymmetric or Cartesian geometry. The transition from a bonded
to un-bonded state consists of a change from a solid state to a mixed gaseous/fragmented fuel state. The
gaseous mixture has no mechanical rigidity unless the fuel fragments are compressed against each other. As
such, the gas and solid mixture behaves similar to a mixture of gas and granular materials under hydrostatic
pressure confined by the surrounding media. The gas in this situation contributes to the overall pressure
exerted outward onto adjacent materials or structures.
 The implementation of the mechanical model for bonding in BISON is separated into two processes: (1)
the calculation of stresses and (2) the derivation of the tangent stiffness. The stress computation in the fuel
material depends on the fuel cracking state, which is described using a state variable α (1 for cracked fuel and
0 for solid). The model does not differentiate between the initial occurrence of cracking and the extensive
fragmentation that may occur in a LOCA. In either case, once cracked, the element is treated as a hydrostatic
entity. Three criteria, in the order of precedence, are used in the code to initiate the fuel cracking state:

 1. Prescribed Function: A prescribed function can be used as an input to describe the state of the fuel,
 either solid or cracked.

 2. Temperature-based Criterion: Describes fuel cracking by T > Tth , where T is the fuel temperature
 and Tth is a temperature threshold defined in the user input. A suitable value for Tth is provided by
 Walker et al. [4].

 3. Strength-based Criterion: Fuel cracking is determined by its maximum principal stress, and it is
 described as
                                         max(σ1 , σ2 , σ3 ) ≥ σ f ,
 where σ f is the fracture strength provided by the user and σi (i=1, 2, and 3) is the principal stress in
 direction i. A suitable fracture strength for UO2 can be found from a variety of references [5–8].

 3. AXIAL GAS COMMUNICATION

 Axial gas communication concerns the computation of local pressure as a function of axial position due
to a computed mole rate introduced due to pressure differentials. Gas no longer communicates in a given step
once pressure equilibrium is reached. Using Khvostov et al. [11] as a numerical foundation, an algorithm to
model gas flow within BISON under post-rupture conditions during a LOCA event was developed. Knowing
that information to determine the permeability or ability for gas to flow will need to come from the axial
fuel relocation model that already existed in BISON, which uses a Layered1D or Layered2D geometric
formulation, a Layered1D formulation was also adopted for the axial gas communication model. Utilizing the
same geometric representation enables the easier transfer of relevant quantities between the models. Notable
differences between the BISON and Khvostov implementations for gas communication are the discretization
of the gas flow rates and pressures as a function of height. The use of axial layers in Layered1D formulation
allows for other physical phenomena to be modeled in BISON, such as double ballooning events, bonded
regions, and varying fuel permeability due to cracking and fragmentation, which cannot be captured by the
Khvostov framework.
 The starting point of the model uses Poiseuille’s law [11], which assumes a laminar, stationary flow of
incompressible uniform fluid. This allows the conservation laws to be fulfilled while reducing the complexity
of the equations involved. The method followed by Reference [11] was primarily to find the flow rate of atoms,

 4. COUPLING THE MODELS TOGETHER

 The inclusion of a fuel cladding bonding model in fuel performance simulations is the most impactful
when considering its influence on axial gas communication phenomena both before and after cladding rupture.
From an experimental point of view, there is more data available for the post-rupture scenario, and this
has been the focus of the initial implementation of the axial gas communication model [12]. Therefore,
in the preliminary coupling strategy adopted in this work, the focus has been on the impact of the bond
on post-rupture depressurization compared to the standalone no-bonding model cases. The tight coupling
approach described below, which needs substantial further development to enable, requires the axial gas
communication model to be extended to include pre-rupture gas flow in scenarios where complete blockage is
not present. This will enable more accurate calculations of the ballooning process subjected to local pressure
effects and the fuel bond. Ultimately, with the industry’s shift in focusing on a no-rupture condition from the
minimization of fuel dispersal after a burst event, this is the required end goal for coupled gas communication
and bonding modeling. Nevertheless, with the wealth of data available in the post-rupture regime from a
wide variety of experimental programs, it is still useful to consider the post-rupture case when assessing the
impacts of the coupling strategies.

 4.1. Preliminary Coupling
 The preliminary coupling strategy is a one-way transfer of information from one model to the next in a
staggered execution of separate simulations. Looking at the models as described, the fuel cladding bonding
model provides a means of calculating the packing ratio for flow through the bonded region, which is different
from the packing ratio computed from the fuel relocation model. The first is computed from assuming a
mixture of fuel and gas upon rupture breakage, whereas the latter is computed from an estimation of fragment
sizes due to fuel pulverization. From a gas communication perspective, the flow through the corresponding
region is directly impacted by this packing ratio. Therefore, the preliminary coupling algorithm is to execute
the standalone bonding model and import as a function of axial location and the time that the packing ratio
computed into the axial gas communication simulation. One will note that the bonding model also contains a
constant user-defined gas flow rate, which indirectly affects the packing ratio predictions. Therefore, after
running the bonding informed axial gas communication simulation, it is prudent to compare the calculated
gas flow rates to the selected input for the bonding model. If there is a large discrepancy, the models should
be run again using more appropriate values for the gas flow rate.

 4.2. Tight Coupling
 In the tight coupling strategy planned for future work and required for enhanced LOCA behavior predictions
within BISON, the models are run concurrently in the same simulation passing necessary information such
as packing ratio and gas flow rates iteratively until a converged solution is achieved. To achieve this tight
coupling, several enhancements to the fuel cladding bonding and axial gas communication models are required.
These enhancements are outlined below:

 • Extend both the axial fuel relocation and axial gas communication models from Layered1D to 2D-RZ
 axisymmetric geometric representations

 • Extend the axial gas communication model to compute gas flow evolution prior to cladding rupture

 • Extend the bonding model to account for evolution of the gap before bond formation

 • Enable the ability to have radially varying permeability in axial gas communication.

full-length rod demonstration problem is presented that evaluates the effects of the bonding model on axial
gas communication predictions using the rudimentary coupling strategy.

 5.1. Implementation Testing
 Implementation testing refers to unit and regression tests included with a code submission to BISON
to ensure proper coding of the mathematical equations for the model of interest. These tests are designed
to capture changes in behavior due to future code modifications through a continuous integration process.
Through this system, adequate code coverage (i.e., tests exercising all available options) is ensured. BISON
utilizes the Continuous Integration, Verification, Enhancement, and Testing (CIVET) [14] platform for its
testing. This is the same platform utilized by Multiphysics Object Oriented Simulation Environment (MOOSE)
and all other MOOSE-based applications. Unit and regression tests for the entire code base are executed when
any change is proposed to the code.
 Here an overview of the implementation tests added for testing of the fuel cladding bonding model
described in Section 2. Details of the implementation testing for axial gas communication can be found
elsewhere [12]. The testing overview is broken down into two sections, one covering mechanical behavior
and the other thermal behavior.

5.1.1. Mechanical Implementation Tests

 The regression tests for mechanical behavior of the fuel cladding bond are designed to test the
ComputeFuelStress and ComputeFuelElasticityTensor models including under internal pressure load-
ing, tension, and shear. These tests are developed for both 2D Cartesian and 2D-RZ axisymmetric geometries.
Additional tests are added to verify that the different crack initiation (i.e., bond breakage) criteria (i.e., pre-
scribed function, temperature-based criterion, strength-based criterion) are working as expected. Most of the
regression tests are completed on a single 2D QUAD4 element in the Cartesian plane or assuming 2D-RZ ax-
isymmetry. A few cases explore the impact of additional mesh elements using 1x10 and 10x10 QUAD4 meshes
on the mechanical response. The tests above are included in the bison/test/tests/fuel_clad_bonding
directory within the repository. As an example, let’s consider the cracking function test included in the
repository to indicate bond breakage through a prescribed function. Here a 1-m by 1-m square is composed
of 10 elements in both the x and y directions. In both directions, the position from 0 to 0.8 consists of solid
fuel, 0.8 to 0.9 makes up the bonded region, and 0.9 to 1 indicates cladding. The temperature is ramped from
300 to 3000 K in 10 seconds. Cracking can only occur in the bonded region and is set to only occur after 2
seconds. The packing ratio is computed based upon the density of the region relative to its initial density.
Deformation is driven entirely by the increase in pressure due to the increasing temperature. Figure 3 presents
contour plots for the displacement magnitude, packing ratio, and fracture status at the end of the simulation.
As the bond breaks, the pressure can begin to exert load to the cladding, and therefore, the cladding expands.
The packing ratio decreases in the regions of highest deformation. Given the symmetric nature of the analysis,
the maximum displacement and stress in the x and y directions are 0.1056 m and 63.88 MPa, respectively.

 Three case studies are explored to evaluate the impact of axial gas flow on cladding ballooning during a
LOCA: standalone fuel cladding bonding simulation, standalone axial gas communication simulation, and a
simulation using the preliminary coupling strategy for the two models.
 For the standalone fuel cladding bonding simulation, the mechanical and thermal material models
described Section 2 are used for modeling a smeared fuel column and the plenum. The thin bonding layer
is not explicitly modeled; instead, the fuel and cladding meshes are directly connected at their common
interface nodes. The finite element grid is shown in Figure 8. The plenum is also meshed, and the gas material
properties are assigned to the elements in plenum.
 For high-burnup fuel, fission gas bubbles and porosities can become over-pressurized, stressing fuel grain
boundaries, causing the fuel to fragment when rapidly heated to high temperatures. In this modeling case
without precise knowledge when this can occur, fragmentation is assumed to occur at a prescribed time.
 Prior to the onset of LOCA, the gas volume in the fuel is assumed to be isolated from the plenum by
blocking the gas flow from the plenum to the fuel. In the analysis, the gas volume is assumed to be 1% of
the fuel column, which is equivalent to ∼1/5 of the plenum volume. At the beginning of the LOCA, the
fragmentation is assumed to have occurred instantaneously in the fuel column except for the top layer of fuel
elements that are used to separate fuel from the plenum.

 6. CONCLUSIONS

 This report has presented the theory behind a developed fuel cladding bonding model implemented in the
BISON fuel performance code to evaluate the impact of the fuel cladding bond on fuel rod behavior during
a LOCA. A summary of a previously developed axial gas communication framework was also presented.
Implementation testing of the bonding model as well as a full-length PWR rod LOCA demonstration problem
was highlighted. It was found that the implementation tests provided alongside the code verified proper
coding of the relevant correlations provided in Section 2. The results of cases studies on the full-length PWR
rod indicated that the modeling assumptions used in the bonding model can greatly influence the predicted
depressurization rates and cladding displacement profiles.

existing axial gas communication model. Several areas of future work have been identified to enhance the

predictability of the individual models and the coupling between them. These areas include:

 • Extend BISON’s axial gas communication and axial fuel relocation models to work in 2D-RZ axisym-
 metric and subsequently in three dimensions (3D). The extension to 2D-RZ will enable tight coupling
 with the presented bonding model. The 3D capabilities will allow for more detailed simulations of
 upcoming and past experiments to capture that have 3D effects.

 • Extend the axial gas communication model to pre-rupture evolution in cases where complete flow
 blockage has not yet occurred. Originally, the axial gas communication model was developed for post-
 rupture depressurization of the plenum to the system pressure due to wealth of available experimental
 data. Axial gas communication experiments focused solely on depressurization rates through a porous
 media can also be currently studies. More recent experiments include capturing gas flow rates in
 ballooning fuel rods that do not experience rupture. With the industry’s recent shift from minimizing
 fuel dispersal to a no rupture condition, this functionality will be essential going forward.

 • Extend the limits of the specific heat and viscosity correlations for the helium, argon, xenon, and
 krypton to higher temperatures and pressures for improved accuracy.

 • Incorporate radially varying permeability for axial gas communication. This is important if one has
 partially relocated fuel, which is expected in pre-rupture scenarios where the rod consists of intact fuel
 and relocation fragments. This would allow for more refined computations of the depressurization
 rates.

 • Account for local changes in transient fission gas release to more accurately compute the pressure
 gradients that may form within the rod.

 • Extend the bonding model to work at burnup prior to those when fuel cladding bonds traditionally
 form. This will be necessary to run full base irradiation prior to experiments focused on the impact of

 bonding on gas communication when performing a tightly coupled analysis.

 • Refine fuel cladding bonding model implementation within the code to remove code duplication related
 to the computation of thermal conductivities of the solid fuel and gaseous components within the bond
 as some correlations can be obtained from previously existing models. Re-evaluate the stress calculation
 of the bonded region to determine whether the support of evaluating at only a single quadrature point is
 needed compared to the standard approach of evaluating at all quadrature points in an element.

 • Couple the bonding model to existing models available in BISON to compute the thermal conductivity
 and specific heat of the solid and gaseous components to reduce code duplication.

 • Perform validation against experiments while incorporating the coupled fuel cladding bonding and
 axial gas communication framework.

 • Apply the coupled bonding and gas communication models to alternative high-burnup fuels for LWRs
 such as Cr2 O3 or Gd2 O3 doped UO2 .

 • Investigate turbulent flow, loss of mass, and rupture size effects on overall change in gas pressure over
 time.
