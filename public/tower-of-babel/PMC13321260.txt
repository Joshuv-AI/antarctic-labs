Molecular surveillance of Rickettsia and Bartonella in desert rodents and ticks from the southern margin of the Gurbantunggut desert, Xinjiang, China
Authors: Ercha Hu, Qiya Wu, Depeng Yang, Sen Yang, Tingxiang Luo, Yihan Wang, Zhen Ma, Wenxiu Hu, Ying Ma, Zhengxiang Hu, Jianlong Li, Bayinchahan Gailike, Qingyong Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDesert rodents and their parasitic ectoparasites act as reservoirs and vectors of zoonotic bacteria, while little research has characterized their community structure and pathogen pathogen infection profiles in the Gurbantunggut Desert remain insufficiently studied. A field investigation was conducted in the southern margin of the desert from October 2024 to June 2025. Gerbils and questing ticks were collected, identified via morphological and molecular methods, and tested for Rickettsia ompA gene and Bartonella gltA gene by PCR. Field sampling yielded 142 gerbil individuals and 361 tick specimens. Bartonella prevalence among these rodents reached 40.14%, whereas spotted fever group rickettsiae (SFGR). positivity was only 1.11% in tick samples. Gerbils are key Bartonella reservoirs, D. marginatus a specific Rickettsia conorii vector, highlighting zoonotic risks and providing baseline data for semi-arid desert pathogen transmission research.Keywords: Bartonella, Dermacentor marginatus, Hyalomma asiaticum, Rickettsia conorii, Rodent reservoir

Introduction

Rodents and ticks are key reservoirs and vectors of zoonotic pathogens, with their interaction mediating zoonosis transmission in natural ecosystems (Silaghi et al., 2016). Globally, extensive epidemiological investigations spanning America, Europe and Central Asia have consistently confirmed that rodent-tick systems sustain the endemic circulation of many pathogens (Esson et al., 2023; Foley et al., 2014; Silaghi et al., 2016), with regional climate shifts further reshaping host-vector community structures and pathogen prevalence profiles.

Previous studies have delineated distinct transmission dynamics for these two bacterial taxa: rodents primarily serve as persistent long-term reservoirs for Bartonella, shedding bacteria through skin abrasions to fuel intraspecific spread (Gutiérrez et al., 2015), whereas rickettsiae are largely maintained via transstadial and transovarial transmission within tick populations (Krawczak and Labruna, 2018), with rodents acting only as transient amplifying hosts after tick bites (Krawczak and Labruna, 2018). Co-circulation of both pathogens within the same rodent-tick assemblages further elevates the risk of mixed human infections once spillover occurs from wildlife hosts. While such cross-continental data clarify universal transmission patterns of tick-borne zoonoses, comparable systematic surveillance targeting desert rodent and tick populations remains limited in the arid borderlands of Northwest China.

The Gurbantunggut Desert, located in Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region of China, is a semi-arid desert with a certain amount of precipitation, and climate change has altered its environmental conditions, potentially affecting the assemblages of desert rodents and ticks. Rickettsia and Bartonella are important zoonotic pathogens associated with these hosts and vectors, causing severe human infections (Magdy et al., 2026). However, systematic investigations on gerbil and tick species composition, as well as the prevalence of these pathogens in the southern margin of the Gurbantunggut Desert particularly under changing climatic conditions remain limited. In particular, the southern margin of this desert features scenic landscapes that attract increasing outdoor human activities. In such settings, the risk of human infection through direct tick bites is considerably higher than through contact with gerbils. Screening questing adult ticks from these areas for Rickettsia and Bartonella is essential for assessing local zoonotic risk.

Morphological and molecular identification, combined with PCR-based pathogen detection, are reliable methods for such investigations (Volfová et al., 2026). The present study was conducted in the southern margin of the Gurbantunggut Desert from October 2024 to June 2025. Gerbils and questing ticks were collected, and their species were identified using morphological and molecular methods. Specifically, samples were identified to species, and detected the prevalence of spotted fever group rickettsiae (SFGR) and Bartonella using PCR, aiming to clarify the species composition of these hosts and vectors, determine the pathogen prevalence, and provide baseline scientific data for understanding zoonotic pathogen transmission in semi-arid desert ecosystems.

Material and methods

Study area and ethics approval

Sample collection was performed at the southern margin of the Gurbantunggut Desert, Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region, China, from October 2024 to June 2025 (Fig. 1). Sampling map was created with ArcGIS Pro (Esri, USA). Base map was from Standard Map Service System, Ministry of Natural Resources, China (http://bzdt.ch.mnr.gov.cn/). All animal procedures were performed in line with the guidelines in Document (2024)058, approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Xinjiang Agricultural University.

Sampling map of the southern margin of the Gurbantunggut Desert, Xinjiang, China. ArcGIS Pro (Esri, USA) was used for mapping, and Inkscape was applied to mark the three sampling locations. Full sampling information is listed in Table 1.

Sample collection

Gerbils were captured using snap traps placed near burrows and foraging paths based on their behavioral habits, specifically along rodent runways or burrow entrances (Wei et al., 2025). Traps were set and checked, with peanut kernels soaked in sesame oil as bait replenished, from sunrise to noon. Tick sampling was restricted to adult tick peak seasons (April–June and October) to avoid snow cover and extreme heat. Deceased gerbils were immediately placed in individual sealed bags first, then put into a portable vehicle-mounted refrigerator for cryopreservation, and subsequently transported to the desert protection station or laboratory.

Questing ticks were collected by flagging methods (D. marginatus) and manually from the field (H. asaticum)(Estrada-Peña et al., 2013). All collected ticks were placed in 5 ml EP tubes (5 ticks per tube) with pinholes, sealed tightly with Parafilm, and temporarily stored in empty plastic water bottles and transport to the laboratory.

Morphological identification

Ticks were identified under a Motic ST-39 stereomicroscope (Motic Corporation, Xiamen, China) using taxonomic keys (Apanaskevich and Horak, 2010; Estrada-Peña and Estrada-Peña, 1991; Li et al., 2017). Gerbils were identified to species level based on diagnostic external morphological characteristics, and taxonomic identification was verified by comparison with the ASM Mammal Diversity Database (https://mammaldiversity.org/) (Burgin et al., 2025).

DNA extraction

Whole individual ticks and approximately 50 mg of pooled heart, liver, spleen, lung and kidney gerbil tissues were separately homogenized in lysis buffer using a Servicebio SWE-FP automated tissue homogenizer. Genomic DNA from both samples was extracted using the TIANGEN Blood/Cell/Tissue Genomic DNA Extraction Kit following the manufacturer's instructions.

Molecular identification of ticks and gerbils

The mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI) gene was amplified by PCR using the same primer pairs for both ticks and gerbils: the forward primer COI-F (5′-GGTCAACAAAT CATAAAGATATTGG-3′) and reverse primer COI-R (5′-TAAACTTCAGGGTGACCAAAAAAT CA-3′) (Folmer et al., 1994). The PCR reaction was performed in a total volume of 50 μl under the following thermal cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 94°C for 5 min; 35 cycles of 94°C for 15 s, 56°C for 15 s, and 72°C for 1 min; followed by a final extension at 72°C for 10 min. Amplified products were visualized on 1.5% agarose gels. The PCR products were purified using TIANGEN Gel Extraction Kit (TIANGEN Biotech, Beijing, China), and the purified amplicons were sequenced by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). COI sequences were obtained from each gerbil species (n = 3), H. asiaticum (n = 6), and D. marginatus (n = 2). The sequences were aligned, and compared with reference sequences deposited in GenBank via BLAST (Altschul et al., 1990).

Pathogen detection and phylogenetic tree construction

Pathogens were detected by PCR targeting the ompA gene for spotted fever group rickettsiae (SFGR) and the gltA gene for Bartonella (Gutiérrez et al., 2014; Tan et al., 2025). For SFGR detection, a 532 bp fragment of the ompA gene was amplified using primers SFGR-F (5′-ATGGCGAATATTTCTCCCAAA-3′) and SFGR-R (5′-AGTGCAGCACTCGCTC CCCCT-3′). The PCR reaction (25 μl) was performed under the following conditions: 94°C for 3 min; 35 cycles of 94°C for 30 s, 52°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 30 s; and a final extension at 72°C for 5 min. For Bartonella detection, a 566 bp fragment of the gltA gene was amplified using primers BARTON-F (5′-GTATGTGCTTTTCTGTTCCTTGTG-3′) and BARTON-R (5′-GCTAATGCA AATAGAACAGTAAAC-3′). The PCR reaction (25 μl) was performed as follows: 94°C for 5 min; 35 cycles of 94°C for 30 s, 56°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 30 s; and a final extension at 72°C for 10 min.

Amplified products were visualized on 1.5% agarose gels, and positive amplicons were excised from the gels and purified using the same TIANGEN Gel Extraction Kit (TIANGEN Biotech, Beijing, China), followed by sequencing by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China).

The obtained sequences were edited and trimmed to remove low-quality regions and primer sequences using MEGA X. The trimmed sequences were then aligned with homologous reference sequences retrieved from GenBank using the ClustalW algorithm. Phylogenetic trees were constructed using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) method in MEGA X with 1000 bootstrap replicates, using Tamura-Nei model, with Gamma distribution (G) and invariant sites (I), and partial deletion of gaps with a 95% site coverage cutoff (Kumar et al., 2018).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R v.4.5.3 (R Core Team, 2025). Pathogen prevalence rates were calculated with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using the Wilson score method. Fisher's exact test was used to compare pathogen prevalence between male and female individuals within each gerbil species and within D. marginatus. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sample collection and identification

A total of 142 gerbils and 361 questing ticks were collected from the southern margin of the Gurbantunggut Desert, Xinjiang, in October 2024 to June 2025 (Fig. 1). Questing ticks were morphologically identified as Hyalomma asiaticum (n = 217) and Dermacentor marginatus (n = 144) (Table 1).

To confirm morphological identification, specimens were subjected to molecular analysis targeting the mitochondrial COI gene. For gerbils, one individual per species (R. opimus, M. meridianus, and M. libycus) was sequenced, yielding three sequences (GenBank accession nos. PZ326745, PZ326747, and PZ326746, respectively). All three sequences showed ≥99% identity with corresponding references in GenBank, confirming the morphological identifications.

For ticks, a total of eight individuals were sequenced, including six H. asiaticum (PZ278969–PZ278974) and two D. marginatus (PZ278967–PZ278968). All tick sequences also showed ≥99% identity with reference sequences, supporting the morphological assignments.

In addition, blood-feeding larvae and nymphs were observed on the eyelids, ears, and noses of some gerbils. COI sequencing of three nymphs confirmed they were H. asiaticum. Larvae could not be molecularly identified due to insufficient DNA concentration.

Pathogen detection

Pathogen detection results for 142 gerbils and 361 ticks are shown in Table 1. Among gerbils, 22 R. opimus, 27 M. meridianus and 8 M. libycus were Bartonella-positive, with total positive rates of 40.14% (57/142), and no SFGR-positive samples detected. Fisher's exact test showed no significant differences in Bartonella prevalence among gerbil species or between sexes (P > 0.05). For ticks, a total of 361 individuals were detected, with 4 SFGR-positive samples showing a SFGR positive rate of 1.11% (4/361), but no Bartonella-positive samples. SFGR prevalence was significantly higher in D. marginatus than in H. asiaticum (P < 0.0001), while the sex-based difference was not significant (P = 0.0915). Bartonella prevalence in gerbils was significantly higher than that in ticks (P < 2.2e-16) (Table 1; Fig. 2).

Discussion

This study provides a systematic investigation of gerbil and tick species composition and the prevalence of Rickettsia and Bartonella in the southern margin of the Gurbantunggut Desert, Northwest China. The main findings are as follows: gerbils showed a high Bartonella prevalence of 40.14% with no SFGR detected; among ticks, only D. marginatus carried R. conorii with a low positive rate, and no tick sample was positive for Bartonella. These results reveal a distinct pathogen–host–vector interaction pattern in this semi-arid desert ecosystem.

Although R. opimus showed a slightly higher Bartonella prevalence (45.8%) than M. meridianus (37.5%) and M. libycus (36.4%), these differences were not statistically significant, suggesting that Bartonella circulates broadly within the gerbil community rather than being restricted to a single host species. The high infection rate is closely associated with the long-term co-adaptation between Bartonella and native rodent hosts (Rodríguez-Pastor et al., 2024), as dominant small mammals in arid desert areas, R. opimus, M. meridianus and M. libycus maintain long-term relationships with local Bartonella strains (Wang et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2023). Frequent flea parasitism facilitates horizontal transmission, ultimately forming a stable endemic circulation of Bartonella in the sampling areas(Bown et al., 2004).

A key discrepancy is that despite a 40.14% Bartonella infection rate in gerbils, all 361 field-collected ticks tested negative. This can be explained by tick physiological status and the transmission biology of Bartonella (Telford and Wormser, 2010). All field-collected ticks in this study were overwintering adults; the blood meal obtained by nymphs had been completely digested and metabolized during several months of overwintering (Telford and Wormser, 2010). Interstadial transmission of Bartonella between tick developmental stages is inefficient and unstable (Bai et al., 2024). Moreover, conventional PCR has limited sensitivity for detecting extremely low pathogen loads. The possibility that starved overwintering adult ticks carry trace levels of Bartonella below the detection limit may not be ruled out, and more sensitive molecular assays are needed for further validation.

Only four SFGR-positive tick samples were detected, all identified as D. marginatus carrying R. conorii (Mediannikov et al., 2008), with an overall low positive rate of 1.11%. No SFGR infection was found in H. asiaticum or any gerbil specimen. These findings indicate that although SFGR-positive ticks co-occur with gerbils in the same habitat, gerbils do not participate in the SFGR transmission cycle. This may be attributed to multiple factors: D. marginatus may rarely feed on gerbils; gerbils may not be competent reservoirs for R. conorii; the low SFGR prevalence in ticks (1.11%) may result in insufficient exposure (Thu et al., 2019). In addition, H. asiaticum lacks effective carrying and transmission adaptability to local SFGR strains, and Bartonella may compete with Rickettsia for ecological niches within gerbil bodies (Obino and Duménil, 2019; Serafim de Castro et al., 2025), further suppressing the establishment of SFGR in the gerbil–tick system (Zeb et al., 2025).

Substantial seasonal and habitat-specific differentiation was also found in the species composition and distribution of questing ticks in these regions. In October, no adult ticks were collected from the ground, whereas larvae and nymphs were detected parasitizing on gerbils. In spring (April), overwintered adult ticks recovered and appeared in the wild, with few nymph parasitizing on gerbils. Field investigation verified that questing ticks were mainly distributed in ground crevices, sand gaps, leaf litter, rodent burrows, dry animal feces, and the root systems of Haloxylon ammodendron and Populus euphratica. These ticks exhibited obvious host-seeking initiative and sensory tropism (Makwatta et al., 2024). H. asiaticum adults could rapidly move toward humans and actively crawl onto exposed skin. Adult ticks usually fold their legs under the body in a static lurking state, and could immediately initiate directional crawling toward heat and breath stimuli (Chaisson and Hallem, 2012).

Phylogenetic analysis revealed considerable diversity among Bartonella genotypes in this study. The dominant Bartonella genotypes detected in the three gerbil species clustered closely with B. krasnovii, B. tribocorum, and an unclassified Bartonella sp. This phylogenetic diversity likely reflects long-term co-evolution between Bartonella strains and their gerbil hosts in the Gurbantunggut Desert ecosystem. Multiple genotypes co-circulating within the same rodent community have been reported in other arid regions (Gutiérrez et al., 2015; Rodríguez-Pastor et al., 2024), and may be attributed to repeated cross-species transmission events and local adaptation to different gerbil species. Notably, the B. krasnovii-like sequences were detected across all three gerbil species, while the B. tribocorum-like sequence was found only in R. opimus, suggesting differential host specificity among genotypes. Such phylogenetic diversity may enhance the persistence of Bartonella in the rodent community by providing genetic variation that allows evasion of host immune responses and adaptation to changing ecological conditions (Rodríguez-Pastor et al., 2025). Both B. krasnovii and B. tribocorum are recognized zoonotic Bartonella species confirmed to infect humans causing febrile illness, lymphadenopathy, and occasional systemic complications (Krügel et al., 2022). From a public health perspective, the presence of multiple zoonotic genotypes increases the risk of human exposure to genetically distinct Bartonella strains, which may differ in virulence or clinical presentation. Although clinical cases are not frequently reported in local surveillance, the high infection rate in rodent reservoirs and the wide distribution of competent flea vector may imply a hidden risk of sporadic human infection, especially among individuals with prolonged outdoor exposure or inadequate protective measures (Okaro et al., 2017).

The two R. conorii sequences obtained from D. marginatus, clustered into a well-supported monophyletic clade (bootstrap = 95%) with reference R. conorii sequences previously reported from Dermacentor ticks in Russia, Turkey, Poland, and other regions, while being genetically distinct from R. raoultii sequences from Kazakhstan. These two Xinjiang-derived sequences also grouped closely with other R. conorii sequences reported from different tick species in Shaanxi, Heilongjiang, and Jilin provinces of China, indicating a close phylogenetic relationship among R. conorii strains circulating in diverse tick vectors across geographically separated regions of China. Meanwhile, they formed a clearly separated lineage from other Rickettsia species such as R. sibirica and Candidatus R. tarasevichiae, further highlighting the potential public health risk of D. marginatus as a competent vector for R. conorii in Xinjiang, China.

This study used conventional PCR, which has limitations in sensitivity and detection scope. Unbalanced sample sizes among sites (Wusu n = 107; Fukang n = 5; Mulei n = 30), which limited statistical power for site and sex comparisons. Tick sampling was also restricted to adult peak seasons, potentially missing seasonal variations. Future studies should adopt more advanced methods as bacterial microbiome approaches can make an unbiased detection, improving low-abundance pathogen detection and identifying novel genotypes. Nested PCR and qPCR can complement this, with nested PCR enhancing sensitivity for low-copy pathogen gene fragments and qPCR enabling rapid quantitative detection. Such approaches will provide a more comprehensive understanding of the gerbil-tick-pathogen transmission network in semi-arid desert ecosystems.

Conclusion

This study investigated 142 gerbils and 361 ticks from three sites in Xinjiang. The overall infection rate of Bartonella in gerbils was 40.14%, with positive detections in R. opimus, M. meridianus, and M. libycus, while all ticks were negative for Bartonella. For SFGR, 1.11% of ticks were positive, all identified as D. marginatus from Wusu. No SFGR was found in gerbils or H. asiaticum. Gerbils serve as the main reservoir for Bartonella, whereas D. marginatus carries R. conorii. These results highlight the zoonotic risk posed by roden and tick borne pathogens to humans in desert-oasis regions.
