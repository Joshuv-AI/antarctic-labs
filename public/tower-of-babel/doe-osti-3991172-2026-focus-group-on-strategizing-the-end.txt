 Executive Summary
     Dates: June 22–26, 2026
 Location: Budapest, Hungary
 Co-hosts: U.S. Department of Energy Office of Radiological Security and the Hungarian
 Atomic Energy Authority
 Participants: Senior representatives from 15 countries, the International Atomic Energy
 Agency (IAEA), and partner laboratories and agencies.
 The 2026 Focus Group on Strategizing the End-of-Life Management of Radioactive
 Sources brought together senior regulators, radioactive waste management
 organizations, source users, technical experts, and international partners from Eastern
 Europe and the Black Sea region to examine safe, secure, and sustainable
 management of disused and vulnerable radioactive sources. Discussions treated end-
 of-life management (ELM) as a lifecycle problem rather than a single disposal activity,
 covering continued use, reuse, recycling, return to supplier, authorized storage or
 disposal, conditioning and consolidation, orphan-source recovery, and long-term
 disposition.
 Across sessions, participants emphasized that the most persistent barriers are often
 institutional, financial, logistical, and workforce-related rather than strictly technical.
 Resilient national programs therefore require clear authorities, reliable inventories,
 multiple disposition pathways, secure storage, realistic disposal planning, sustainable
 financing, capable personnel, strong transport and operational planning, and regional
 cooperation.

 1. Purpose and Scope
 The focus group examined how countries can manage radioactive sources throughout
 their lifecycle while preserving safe and secure end-of-life options. Participants
 compared national strategies and regulatory frameworks, inventory practices, return and
 recycling pathways, storage and disposal, source removal and packaging, transport
 security, orphan-source response, financing, workforce development, security culture,
 and opportunities for regional cooperation.

 2. Executive Findings
 • Clear legal and institutional frameworks are the foundation for effective ELM with
 defined responsibilities for regulators, users, waste-management organizations,
 suppliers, and other stakeholders.
 • National strategies should preserve multiple lifecycle pathways. Return to
 supplier is preferred when practical but cannot be the sole national strategy.
 • Accurate current and projected inventories are essential for infrastructure,
 staffing, funding, packaging, and disposal planning.
 • Interim and long-term storage remain essential because disposal capacity is
 limited or unavailable in parts of the region.
 • Transportation, customs, packaging, contractor availability, and coordination can
 create greater operational difficulty than source removal itself.
 • Legacy, orphan, damaged, and out-of-control sources require dedicated
 procedures, technical capability, and funding.
 • Financing and workforce development must be sustained over multidecade
 timescales, not limited to short project or budget cycles.
 • Regional cooperation and practical capacity building can help countries share
 specialized expertise, equipment, training, and response capability.
 • A novel scenario-based team game effectively demonstrated the value of
 practical, collaborative training. Broad interest in adapting the exercise for
 national use shows this game could be a tool for building regional capacity.

 3. Regional Context and Maturity
 The region includes substantially different levels of ELM infrastructure and institutional
 maturity. Some countries have established storage and disposal pathways, mature
 regulatory systems, and stable technical capability; others have centralized storage or

 5

 radioactive-waste facilities but limited disposal options; smaller programs may have few
 national facilities and depend more heavily on international assistance.
 These differences affect the feasibility of national and regional solutions. Countries with
 larger nuclear programs or source-related industries generally have more stable
 technical infrastructure, while smaller countries may struggle to maintain specialized
 staff, finance facilities, or justify national packaging and characterization capabilities.
 Regional planning should therefore accommodate different national capacities rather
 than assume a single model.

 4. National Strategy and Regulatory
 Control
 4.1 Key Insights
 Effective ELM depends on clear national policies, defined institutional responsibilities,
 source tracking, licensing, inspection, and enforcement. Strategies should cover the full
 lifecycle from acquisition through reuse, return, storage, disposal, or recovery.
 Participants also cautioned against automatically classifying every disused source as
 radioactive waste because early classification can foreclose reuse, recycling, export, or
 other future options (Table 1, appendix page 14; Table 2, appendix page 15).

 4.2 Best Practices
 • Maintain a comprehensive national source registry and link source, import/export,
 licensee, waste, and disposal records where practical.
 • Define when a source becomes disused, spent, or radioactive waste and require
 a credible end-of-life pathway before acquisition.
 • Use financial guarantees or other funding mechanisms proportionate to source
 category, activity, and anticipated liability.
 • Define responsibilities among regulators, users, suppliers, customs authorities,
 police, emergency responders, and waste-management organizations.
 • Establish specific procedures for orphan sources, sources out of regulatory
 control, and high-risk or conflict environments.

 4.3 Continuing Challenges
 National requirements for return, export, storage, and disposal vary. Other recurring
 problems include unclear ownership after license expiration or bankruptcy, restrictions
 on foreign-generated waste, limited experience with rare removals, and sources that
 were never authorized or never used. Return-to-supplier commitments can fail because

 6

 of bankruptcy, refusal, cost, legal uncertainty, or loss of documentation (Table 1,
 appendix page 14; Table 4, appendix page 16).

 5. Inventory Management and
 Legacy Sources
 5.1 Key Insights
 Accurate inventory information is the basis for most ELM decisions. Participants
 described separate or poorly integrated records for sources in use, imports and exports,
 and radioactive waste. Legacy sources may have paper-only records, duplicate or
 incorrect serial numbers, missing certificates, obsolete device designs, unclear shielding
 configurations, or no reliable transfer history (Table 5 appendix page 17; Table 6
 appendix page 17; Table 7, appendix page 18).

 5.2 Best Practices
 • Transition paper records to secure digital systems while preserving originals and
 use independent verification for critical entries.
 • Reconcile physical inventories against regulatory and facility databases and
 update records after transfer, reuse, recycling, storage, or disposal.
 • Record radionuclide, activity, category, serial number, device, location, owner,
 status, and available documentation.
 • Use national laboratories, hot cells, glove boxes, and other technical assets
 when source identification or characterization is difficult.
 • Preserve records needed for future repackaging and disposal. RAIS+,
 ARIS/xARIS, and national systems were discussed as possible tools.

 6. End-of-Life Pathways
 6.1 Reuse and Recycling
 Reuse, recycling, and transfer to another authorized user can extend source value and
 reduce disposal demand when safety, security, documentation, and regulatory
 requirements can be met. Recycling is generally more feasible for high-activity sources
 than for Category 3-5 sources because of transport and processing costs, and older or
 poorly documented sources may be unsuitable (Table 1 appendix page 14; Table 3
 appendix page 15).

 6.2 Return to Supplier

 7

 Return to supplier is widely regarded as a preferred pathway, particularly for Category 1
 and 2 sources, but a contractual commitment does not guarantee acceptance. Barriers
 include supplier bankruptcy or refusal, high shipping and acceptance costs, inflation,
 incomplete documentation, custom designs, limited experienced personnel, legal
 uncertainty, customs delays, and lack of accredited leak-testing capability. Possible
 approaches include binding take-back agreements, financial guarantees, shared
 insurance or bond mechanisms, government-to-government or government-to-supplier
 arrangements, and planned alternatives if return fails (Table 4, appendix page 16).

 6.3 Storage and Disposal
 Interim and long-term storage are essential where disposal is unavailable or still being
 developed. Planning must consider waste-acceptance criteria, radionuclide and half-life,
 facility capacity, retrievability, repackaging, structural degradation, physical protection,
 future relocation, and public acceptance. Several countries reported capacity pressure
 or limited pathways for particular materials. Regional disposal received support in
 principle, but national prohibitions on foreign-generated waste create major legal and
 policy barriers (Table 3, appendix page 15; Table 8, appendix page 19; Table 15,
 appendix page 22).

 7. Removal, Packaging, and
 Transportation
 7.1 Key Insights
 The technical removal of a source is often not the principal operational risk. Case
 studies showed that carriers, customs, contractors, weather, infrastructure damage,
 ownership changes, and communication failures can determine mission success.
 Strong local partnerships, clear decision-making authority, and early coordination can
 resolve failures that are not radiological in origin (Table 5, appendix page 17; Table 10,
 appendix page 22).

 7.2 Best Practices
 • Develop detailed operational plans with defined roles, dependencies, and
 contingencies before mobilization.
 • Identify transport, customs, packaging, and contractor constraints early; use
 experienced Class 7 customs brokers where appropriate.
 • Conduct site surveys, source characterization, leak testing, and package-fit
 assessments before removal.

 8

 • Maintain direct communication among regulators, customs, carriers, receiving
 facilities, owners, and national representatives.
 • Plan for weather, security incidents, infrastructure disruption, and changes in
 facility ownership.

 7.3 Packaging and Equipment
 National packaging capabilities vary widely. Some countries maintain package fleets;
 others rely on private industry, manufacturer packaging, or international assistance.
 Challenges include devices that do not fit standard packages, different certification
 requirements, limited access to certified packages, single-use containers, and unusual
 high-activity sources. Shared regional package resources may be especially useful for
 countries with small inventories (Table 9, appendix page 19).

 8. Transport Security and High-Risk
 Environments
 Sources are especially vulnerable in transit. Participants supported risk-based use of
 GPS tracking, centralized monitoring, backup communications, route and safe-haven
 planning, information protection, contingency procedures, and coordination of escorts
 and authorities. Routes may need to account for population centers, traffic, checkpoints,
 infrastructure damage, and conflict. In high-risk zones, source recovery must be
 coordinated with military, police, local authorities, and emergency-management
 organizations, with personnel safety and mission feasibility taking priority (Table 11,
 appendix page 20; Table 16, appendix page 23).

 9. Orphan Sources and Sources Out
 of Regulatory Control
 9.1 Effective Response Elements
 Participants agreed that orphan and out-of-control sources should be returned to
 regulatory control and managed through an appropriate disposition pathway. Effective
 response elements include archived records, risk-based search priorities, field surveys,
 characterization, secure recovery and transport, police involvement when criminal
 activity is suspected, licensed receiving facilities, state funding when no responsible
 owner exists, and cross-border notification and cooperation (Table 16, appendix page
 23; Table 17, appendix page 23).

 9.2 Categories Requiring Different Responses

 9

 Disused sources, orphan sources, sources never authorized or never used, conflict-
 damaged sources, and material associated with suspected criminal activity may require
 different regulatory, investigative, and operational responses. Participants also noted
 risks at scrapyards and transport hubs when incoming material is not adequately
 monitored (Table 16, appendix page 23; Table 17, appendix page 23).

 10. Resources and Capacity Building
 10.1 Financing
 No single funding model was identified as universally applicable. Countries use national
 budgets, user fees, dedicated waste funds, financial guarantees, supplier commitments,
 and international assistance. Recurring problems include short budget horizons, long-
 term liabilities, inflation, uncertain disposal costs, legacy sources without a responsible
 owner, and government assumption of costs after bankruptcy or supplier failure.
 Potential approaches include lifecycle-based fees, graded guarantees, inflation-adjusted
 pricing, polluter-pays mechanisms, state funding for legacy and orphan sources,
 regional cost-sharing, and periodic review of financial assumptions (Table 3, appendix
 page 15; Table 18, appendix page 24).

 10.2 Workforce
 Workforce sustainability is a major concern, especially where nuclear engineering
 programs or large nuclear sectors are absent. Challenges include aging staff, loss of
 institutional knowledge, small staffing pools, single-person expertise, weak career
 visibility, and difficulty maintaining skills that are needed only occasionally. Practical
 responses include mentoring, job-shadowing, succession planning, scenario-based
 training, cross-training, university and technical-school partnerships, scholarships tied to
 service, exchanges, and systematic preservation of procedures and lessons learned.
 Relationship development alone is insufficient unless knowledge is deliberately
 transferred (Table 12, appendix page 20; Table 18, appendix page 24).
 The novel scenario-based ELM training game emerged as a valuable training resource.
 It required multinational teams to develop responses to changing scenarios, reinforcing
 the need for national strategies that address problems as interconnected rather than
 sequential. The exercise demonstrated operational dependencies, promoted regional
 cooperation, and sparked innovative problem solving.

 11. Security Culture
 Security culture should be treated as an ongoing organizational capability rather than a
 one-time training topic. Good practices include self-assessment, trend analysis,
 leadership engagement, clear expectations for reporting abnormal conditions, scenario-
 based discussion, attention to informal workarounds, and training that connects

 10

 regulatory requirements to operational decisions. Participants also emphasized that
 safety and security measures can affect one another and should be considered together
 (Table 13, appendix page 21).

 12. Regional Cooperation Priorities
 Promising areas include shared storage or disposal concepts, regional package fleets,
 mutual support for characterization, cross-border orphan-source response, common
 customs and transport approaches, joint training and exercises, technical exchanges
 and mentoring, shared contractor and supplier information, legacy-source recovery, and
 more consistent documentation practices. Any regional arrangement would still need to
 address national law, liability, waste ownership, financing, acceptance criteria, transport
 permissions, and public communication.

 13. Priority Actions
 13.1 Near-Term Priorities
 • Develop or update national ELM strategies that preserve all feasible lifecycle
 pathways.
 • Modernize and reconcile source inventories and identify legacy, orphan, and
 vulnerable sources that require priority action.
 • Review return-to-supplier requirements and establish contingency pathways if
 return fails.
 • Map transportation, customs, packaging, and contractor dependencies; assess
 storage capacity and projected demand.
 • Document critical workforce capabilities and succession risks and expand
 practical training and regional knowledge exchange.
 • Strengthen security culture through self-assessment and scenario-based
 exercises.

 13.2 Medium- and Long-Term Priorities
 • Develop financing mechanisms that account for multidecade liabilities and
 changing costs.
 • Establish or expand secure storage; define waste acceptance and conditioning
 requirements; and evaluate realistic disposal options.
 • Develop national or shared packaging, leak-testing, and characterization
 capabilities.

 11

 • Establish cross-border procedures for transport, detection, and orphan-source
 response.
 • Preserve technical and institutional knowledge and shift international assistance
 toward sustained national capability development.

 14. Conclusion
 The Budapest Focus Group demonstrated broad regional interest in improving the safe,
 secure, and sustainable management of radioactive sources at the end of their useful
 lives. The discussions showed that ELM cannot depend on a single pathway, supplier,
 facility, or funding source. Resilient programs combine a clear legal and institutional
 framework, reliable inventories, multiple ELM pathways, secure storage and realistic
 disposal planning, sustainable financing, skilled personnel, strong operational and
 transport planning, effective orphan-source response, a mature security culture, and
 regional and international cooperation.
 Participants also favored continued capacity building and professional networking over
 isolated technical-assistance activities. Practical collaboration, repeated technical
 exchange, and transfer of operational knowledge provide a stronger foundation for
 durable regional solutions.
