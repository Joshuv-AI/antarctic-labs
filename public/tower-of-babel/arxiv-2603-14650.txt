Exact characterizations for quantum conditional mutual information and some other entropies
Zhou Gang

Abstract




Department of Mathematics and Statistics, Binghamton University, Binghamton, NY, 13902

Abstract

Lieb and Ruskai’s strong subadditivity theorem, which shows that the conditional mutual information must be nonnegative, is fundamental in quantum theory. It has numerous applications, such as in quantum error correction. When the mutual information is zero, the Petz recovery map can be used to reconstruct the quantum channel. When the mutual information is small, one seeks to define an optimal recovery channel. To this end, a mathematical characterization of the mutual information is desirable. We address this problem by providing an exact characterization of the mutual information, along with characterizations for other entropies. Our controls are sharp, leaving no room for improvement, in the sense that we provide equalities, regardless of whether the mutual information (or remainder) is small or large.
We transform the definitions of these entropies into a summation of explicitly constructed terms, and the definition of each term obviously demonstrates the desired positivity/convexity/concavity. The summation converges rapidly and absolutely in a chosen elementary norm.

The following well-known result links concavity/convexity of any function

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose that

is smooth function. For

(
\geq 0
), respectively.

Proof.

Suppose that (
3.3
) holds. It implies that

is concave (convex) if and only if
g^{\prime\prime}(t)\leq 0
(
\geq 0
), respectively.

What is left is to prove (

, define a function

by

\phi(b):=g(tb)-(1-t)g(0)-tg(b).

\phi
enjoys the following properties:

\phi(0)=\phi^{\prime}(0)=0.

Its first order derivative takes the form.

\phi^{\prime}(b)=t[g^{\prime}(tb)-g^{\prime}(b)]=-t\int_{tb}^{b}g^{\prime\prime}(\sigma)\,d\sigma.

Hence

\phi(b)=\int_{0}^{b}\phi^{\prime}(\lambda)\,d\lambda=-t\int_{0}^{b}\int_{t\lambda}^{\lambda}g^{\prime\prime}(\sigma)\,d\sigma\,d\lambda.

By setting
b=1
and using the definition of

3.3

5
Strong subadditivity of quantum entropy

Suppose that
\rho_{ABC}
is a positive-definite density matrix on the tensor product space
\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{C}
.
We are interested in studying
S(\rho_{ABC}\mid\rho_{AB}\otimes\rho_{C})-S(\rho_{BC}\mid\rho_{B}\otimes\rho_{C})
,
where
S(\rho_{1}|\rho_{2})
, for any density matrices
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
, is defined in (
4.1
),
\rho_{AB}
and
\rho_{B}
are defined as partial traces of
\rho_{ABC}:

\displaystyle\rho_{AB}

\displaystyle:=\text{Tr}_{C}\,\rho_{ABC},

\displaystyle\rho_{B}

\displaystyle:=\text{Tr}_{AB}\,\rho_{ABC},

and
\rho_{BC}
and
\rho_{B}
are defined similarly.

It was proved by Lieb and Ruskai in
[
26
]
that

\displaystyle I(A:C|B)_{\rho}=S(\rho_{ABC}\mid\rho_{AB}\otimes\rho_{C})-S(\rho_{BC}\mid\rho_{B}\otimes\rho_{C})\geq 0.

(5.1)

Recall the definition of quantum conditional mutual information
I(A:C|B)_{\rho}
in (
1.2
).

Before stating our result, we make some preparations to make Theorem
4.1
applicable.
We convert
\rho_{ABC}
into
\frac{1}{\dim(\mathcal{H}_{A})}I\otimes\rho_{BC}
by applying a trace-preserving, completely positive map. While various methods could achieve this, such as integration using an appropriate Haar measure, we opt for a discrete approach in this paper, prioritizing a rapid solution over computational efficiency.

Proposition 5.1
.

For any
N
, there exist finitely many unitary matrices

U_{1},\dots,U_{J}

s.t. for any
N\times N
Hermitian matrix
A,

\frac{1}{J}\sum_{k=1}^{J}U_{k}AU_{k}^{*}=\left(\frac{1}{N}\text{Tr}A\right)I_{N\times N}.

A\rightarrow\frac{1}{J}\sum_{k=1}^{J}U_{k}AU_{k}^{*}
is a Trace-preserving, completely positive map.

The proposition will be proved in subsection
5.1
.

We are ready to formulate our main result, which is Main Theorem
5.3
below.

Suppose that the subsystem
A
is
N
-dimensional. For any density matrix
\rho_{ABC}
, we apply the mapping
\frac{1}{J}\sum_{k=1}^{J}U_{k}\ (\cdot)\ U_{k}^{*}
on the subsystem
A
, to obtain

\frac{1}{J}\sum_{k=1}^{J}U_{k}\rho_{ABC}U_{k}^{*}=\frac{1}{N}I_{A}\otimes\rho_{BC}.

(5.2)

A useful observation is that

\displaystyle F_{J}:=S\left(\frac{1}{J}\sum_{k=1}^{J}U_{k}\rho_{ABC}U_{k}^{*}\ \bigg|\ \frac{1}{J}\sum_{k=1}^{J}U_{k}(\rho_{AB}\otimes\rho_{C})U_{k}^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=S\left(\rho_{BC}\ \bigg|\ \rho_{B}\otimes\rho_{C}\right).

(5.3)

Next, we derive an expression for
F(J)-S(\rho_{ABC}\big|\rho_{AB}\otimes\rho_{C})
by iteration.

To establish the iteration relation, we define, for any integer
K\in[2,J]
,

F_{K}:=S\Big(\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}U_{k}\rho_{ABC}U_{k}^{*}\ \Big|\ \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}U_{k}(\rho_{AB}\otimes\rho_{C})U_{k}^{*}\Big).

(5.4)

To make the application of Theorem
4.1
transparent, we adopt the same notations by defining objects corresponding to those in (
4.4

\displaystyle\begin{split}A_{1}:=&U_{K}\rho_{ABC}U_{K}^{*}\\
A_{2}:=&\frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}U_{k}\rho_{ABC}U_{k}^{*}\\
B_{1}:=&U_{K}\rho_{AB}\otimes\rho_{C}U_{K}^{*}\\
B_{2}:=&\frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}U_{k}\rho_{AB}\otimes\rho_{C}U_{k}^{*}.\end{split}

(5.5)

Define a function

F_{K}(t):=S\left(tA_{1}+(1-t)A_{2}\ \big|\ tB_{1}+(1-t)B_{2}\right).

(5.6)

When
t=\frac{1}{K}
,

F_{K}(\frac{1}{K})=F_{K}.

(5.7)

Recall the definition of
\Gamma
in (
4.14
). To display its dependence on
K
, we use the notation
\Gamma_{K}
. And we define

\tilde{\Gamma}_{K}(t):=2(\log e)\big\langle V_{I},\ \Gamma_{K}V_{I}\big\rangle.

(5.8)

By Theorem
4.1
, (
3.3
) and that
for any integer

S\big(U_{k}\rho_{ABC}U_{k}^{*}\ \big|\ U_{k}(\rho_{AB}\otimes\rho_{C})U_{k}^{*}\big)=S\big(\rho_{ABC}\ \big|\ \rho_{AB}\otimes\rho_{C}\big),

Lemma 5.2

For any integer

\displaystyle F_{K}=\frac{1}{K}S(\rho_{ABC}\,|\,\rho_{AB}\otimes\rho_{C})+(1-\frac{1}{K})F_{K-1}-\frac{1}{K}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\frac{\lambda}{K}}^{\lambda}\tilde{\Gamma}_{K}(\sigma)\,d\sigma\,d\lambda.

(5.9)

We are ready to state the main theorem:
For

(5.10)

Recall the identity in (
5.3

\displaystyle S(\rho_{BC}\ |\ \rho_{B}\otimes\rho_{C})=S(\rho_{ABC}\,|\,\rho_{AB}\otimes\rho_{C})-\frac{1}{J}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\frac{\lambda}{J}}^{\lambda}\tilde{\Gamma}_{J}(\sigma)\,d\sigma\,d\lambda-\sum_{K=2}^{J-1}\Delta_{K}.

|\mathcal{T}|=2^{N}
. Denote

\mathcal{T}=\{T_{1},\dots,T_{2^{N}}\}

(5.13)

and for each fixed
T_{l}
, define a diagonal matrix

K_{T_{l}}=\text{diag}[k_{1}(T_{l}),\ k_{2}(T_{l}),\ k_{N}(T_{l})]

with
k_{m}(T_{l})
,
m=1,\cdots,N
, defined as

k_{m}(T_{l})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }m\notin T_{l},\\
-1&\text{if }m\in T_{l}.\end{cases}

We will use this observation: for any
j\in T_{l}
,
K_{T_{l}}A
adds a minus-sign to the
j-
th column of
A
; and
AK_{T_{l}}
adds a minus-sign to the
j-
th row.

\frac{1}{2^{N}}\sum_{T_{l}\in\mathcal{T}}K_{T_{l}}AK_{T_{l}}
removes all the off-diagonal entries, but keeps the diagonal ones intact by the following reason: for any fixed
K_{T_{j}}
, apply it to the set
\big\{K_{T_{l}}\ |\ l=1,\cdots,2^{N}\big\}
will not change it:

K_{T_{j}}\big\{K_{T_{l}}\ |\ l=1,\cdots,2^{N}\big\}=\big\{K_{T_{l}}\ |\ l=1,\cdots,2^{N}\big\}.

and thus

K_{T_{j}}\Big(\frac{1}{2^{N}}\sum_{T_{l}\in\mathcal{T}}K_{T_{l}}AK_{T_{l}}\Big)K_{T_{j}}=\frac{1}{2^{N}}\sum_{T_{l}\in\mathcal{T}}K_{T_{l}}AK_{T_{l}}.

Consequently, we get a diagonal matrix:

\frac{1}{2^{N}}\sum_{T_{l}\in\mathcal{T}}K_{T_{l}}AK_{T_{l}}=\text{diag}[a_{11},a_{22},\dots,a_{nn}]=:\tilde{A}.

(5.14)

This mapping is trace-preserving and completely positive because
K_{T_{l}}=K^{*}_{T_{l}}
is unitary.

Next, we convert
\tilde{A}
into
\frac{\text{Tr}(\tilde{A})}{N}Id
by applying a trace-preserving completely positive map.

We consider the permutation group
G
on the set
\{1,2,\dots,N\}
, which has
N!
elements. For each
P\in G
, we define a unitary matrix
D_{P}
s.t.
D_{P}F
permutes the
N
rows of the matrix
F
accordingly.
Then

\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{P\in G}D_{P}\tilde{A}D^{*}_{P}=\frac{\text{Tr}(\tilde{A})}{N}I=\frac{Tr(A)}{N}I.

(5.15)

Obviously
A\rightarrow\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{P\in G}D_{P}AD^{*}_{P}
is a trace-preserving, completely positive map.

Combining (
5.14
) and (
5.15
), we find that, for any
N\times N
Hermitian matrix
A,

\frac{1}{2^{N}}\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{P\in G}\sum_{T_{l}\in\mathcal{T}}(D_{P}K_{T_{l}})A(K_{T_{l}}D^{*}_{P})=\frac{Tr(A)}{N}I.

Hence
A\rightarrow\frac{1}{2^{N}}\frac{1}{N!}\sum_{P\in G}\sum_{T_{l}\in\mathcal{T}}(D_{P}K_{T_{l}})A(K_{T_{l}}D^{*}_{P})
is the desired map in Proposition
5.1
.

Proof of Lemma

Recall that we are interested in the perturbation expansion of a Hermitian matrix

where
q\in[0,1]
is a scalar,
A>0
, and
V
is Hermitian,
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}
satisfies
|\epsilon|\ll 1
. It is well known that for some Hermitian matrices

and

,

(A+\epsilon V)^{q}=A^{q}+\epsilon V_{q}-\epsilon^{2}Q_{q}+O(\epsilon^{2}).

(A.1)

We find the desired

by using the standard theory, e.g. Löwner-Heinz theorem, see Lemma
B.1
below,

(A+\epsilon V)^{q}=\frac{\sin(\pi q)}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{q}\left(\frac{1}{t}-\frac{1}{t+A}\right)\,dt

to find that

Q_{q}=\frac{\sin(\pi q)}{\pi}\int_{0}^{+\infty}t^{q}(t+A)^{-1}V(t+A)^{-1}V(t+A)^{-1}\,dt\geq 0.

For
V_{q}
, one can also obtain an expression from (
A.2
). But we have to be careful with the cases
q=0,1
where the integrands are not integrable. For this reason, we derive an explicit expression for
V_{q}
in the following way.

For

(A+\epsilon V)^{1/2}=A^{1/2}+\epsilon V_{1/2}+O(\epsilon^{2}),

\big[A^{1/2}+\epsilon V_{1/2}+O(\epsilon^{2})\big]^{2}=\Big[(A+\epsilon V)^{1/2}\Big]^{2}=A+\epsilon V,

(A.3)

thus

A^{1/2}V_{1/2}+V_{1/2}A^{1/2}=V.

(A.4)

Similarly, for any
m\geq 2
,

is the solution to the equation

A^{1/2^{m}}V_{1/2^{m}}+V_{1/2^{m}}A^{1/2^{m}}=V_{1/2^{m-1}}.

(A.5)

The result is the following:

Lemma A.1
.

V_{1/2^{m}}
, in (
A.4
) and (

), is well defined for any integer

.

There exists a unique Hermitian matrix

such that

\lim_{m\to\infty}2^{m}V_{1/2^{m}}=\widetilde{V}.

(A.6)

For any

(A.7)

In particular, if

for some

and

,

V_{\frac{k}{2^{m}}}=\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}A^{\frac{l}{2^{m}}}V_{1/2^{m}}A^{\frac{k-1-l}{2^{m}}}=\sum_{l=0}^{2^{j}k-1}A^{\frac{l}{2^{m+j}}}V_{1/2^{m+j}}A^{\frac{2^{j}k-1-l}{2^{m+j}}}=\int_{0}^{\frac{k}{2^{m}}}A^{s}\widetilde{V}A^{\frac{k}{2^{m}}-s}ds.

(A.8)

where the second identity holds for any

Proof.

We begin by proving that
V_{1/2^{m}}
is well-defined.

We begin with setting up the problem.

By a unitary transformation, we can make
A
diagonal. Thus it suffices to consider an easier case, where
A>0
is diagonal: for some

k=1,\cdots,N,

We suppose that the matrix

is of the form

And we denote, for any nonnegative integer

\delta_{j}=2^{-j}.

Solve for

Inductively, we solve (

Next, we prove (

We observe that, as

, and hence,

d_{k}^{\delta_{j}}=1+\delta_{j}\ln d_{k}+O(\delta_{j}^{2}).

Consequently, the following limit exists: for some constant

Here

is the desired one in (

We are ready to prove (

The idea is simple,

is a product of

(A+\epsilon V)^{\frac{k}{2^{m}}}=\Big[(A+\epsilon V)^{\frac{1}{2^{m}}}\Big]^{k}=\Big[A^{\frac{1}{2^{m}}}+\epsilon V_{1/2^{m}}+O(\epsilon^{2})\Big]^{k}=A^{\frac{k}{2^{m}}}+\epsilon\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}A^{\frac{l}{2^{m}}}V_{1/2^{m}}A^{\frac{k-1-l}{2^{m}}}+O(\epsilon^{2}),

To prove (

Appendix B
Integral formula

We need the following integral formula, see e.g.
[
8
,
4
]
.

Lemma B.1
.

For any positive definite matrix
A
,
p\in(0,1),

A^{p}=\frac{\sin(p\pi)}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{p}\left(\frac{1}{t}-\frac{1}{t+A}\right)\,dt.

(B.1)

Proof.

Here, since
A
is positive definite, there exists a unitary matrix
U
s.t.

UAU^{*}=\text{diag}[a_{1},\dots,a_{n}]\hskip 11.74988pt\text{with }a_{k}>0.

To prove the desired formula (
B.1
), we only need to show that, for any scalar
a>0
,

\displaystyle a^{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin p\pi}{\pi}\int_{0}^{+\infty}t^{p}\left(\frac{1}{t}-\frac{1}{t+a}\right)\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin p\pi}{\pi}\int_{0}^{+\infty}t^{p-1}\frac{a}{t+a}\,dt

\displaystyle=a^{p}\frac{\sin p\pi}{\pi}D

where, in the last step, we changed variable
t\to at
and the constant
D\in\mathbb{R}
is defined as

D:=\int_{0}^{+\infty}t^{p-1}\frac{1}{1+t}\,dt.

Hence, what is left is to prove that

D=\frac{\pi}{\sin p\pi}.

(B.2)

Change variable
t=s^{2}
to find:

D=2\int_{0}^{+\infty}s^{2p-1}\frac{1}{1+s^{2}}\,ds.

Before applying complex analysis, we need to define
z^{p}
properly. Here we consider the following region:

\Omega:=\Big\{z=|z|e^{i\phi},\Big|\ \phi\in\left(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{3\pi}{2}\right)\Big\}

(B.3)

In this region we define
z^{q}
,
q\in(-1,1)
, as

z^{q}=|z|^{q}e^{iq\phi}.

Hence for
s=-|s|=e^{i\pi}|s|
:

\displaystyle s^{2p-1}

\displaystyle=|s|^{2p-1}\left(\cos(2p-1)\pi+i\sin(2p-1)\pi\right)

\displaystyle=-|s|^{2p-1}\left(\cos 2p\pi+i\sin 2p\pi\right).

Thus,

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{0}s^{2p-1}\frac{1}{1+s^{2}}\,ds=

\displaystyle-(\cos 2p\pi+i\sin 2p\pi)\int_{-\infty}^{0}|s|^{2p-1}\frac{1}{1+s^{2}}\,ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}(\cos 2p\pi+i\sin 2p\pi)D.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}s^{2p-1}\frac{1}{1+s^{2}}\,ds

\displaystyle=\frac{D}{2}\left[1-(\cos 2p\pi)-i\sin 2p\pi\right]

\displaystyle=D\sin p\pi\left[\sin p\pi-i\cos p\pi\right].

(B.4)

On the other hand, in the upper complex half plane,
s=i=e^{i\frac{\pi}{2}}
is the only singularity for the function
s^{2p-1}\frac{1}{1+s^{2}}
.

Hence, by the residue theorem:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}s^{2p-1}\frac{1}{1+s^{2}}\,ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2i}\left(\cos(p\pi-\frac{\pi}{2})+i\sin(p\pi-\frac{\pi}{2})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2i}\left(\sin(p\pi)-i\cos(p\pi)\right).

(B.5)

(
B.4
) and (
B.5
) imply that
D=\frac{\pi}{\sin p\pi}
as desired (
B.2
).
∎
