Multipartite device-independent quantum key distribution using W states Preprint: APS/123-QED
Makoto Ishihara, Wojciech Roga, Masahiro Takeoka

Abstract
Multipartite device-independent quantum key distribution (DI-QKD), also known as device-independent conference key agreement, enables more than two remote parties to share a common key with information-theoretic security even without trusting the devices. So far, several multipartite DI-QKD protocols have been proposed where Greenberger-Horne-Zeilinger (GHZ) states are used as multipartite entanglement. A natural question is then whether one can construct multipartite DI-QKD with the other type of multipartite entanglement. W state is of particular interest since it is intrinsically different from GHZ state and in some cases, easier to optically implement. In this paper, we show that multipartite DI-QKD is possible with W states. To this end, we construct Bell inequalities largely violated by W states, which can be used for the multipartite DI-QKD. Furthermore, we consider several different implementation scenarios. First, we analyze the minimum required detection efficiencies to extract finite amount of keys. Then we propose a long-distance multipartite DI-QKD protocol with single-photon interference and make detailed analyses with several physical implementation scenarios. We show that the protocol enables secret key distribution over longer distances than the existing multipartite DI-QKD protocols based on GHZ states. This study provides new insight about the relationship between multipartite entanglement and device-independent quantum information processing as well as opens an alternative path toward long-distance multipartite DI-QKD.

III
DI-QKD key rate

Here, we describe key rates of our protocol. We focus on asymptotic key rates and we assume that the devices behave independently and identically during implementations. A multipartite DI-QKD key rate of our protocol is expressed as follows
21

r=P_{\text{succ}}(H(A|x^{*},E)-\max_{i}H(A|B_{i},x^{*},y^{*}_{i})),

(2)

where
A
,
B_{i}
and
E
denote Alice’s,
\text{Bob}_{i}
’s and Eve’s system, respectively,
x^{*}
and
y^{*}_{i}
denote Alice’s and
\text{Bob}_{i}
’s measurement inputs for the key generation rounds, and
P_{\text{succ}}
is a probability that a
W
state is successfully distributed among the parties. The first term is the conditional von Neumann entropy between Alice and Eve, corresponding to Eve’s uncertainty about Alice’s measurement outcomes. The second term shows the error correction cost between Alice and Bobs.

What makes the implementation of multipartite DI-QKD protocols using
W
states challenging is the second term in the key rate formula (
2
). While the parties can extract perfectly correlated key bits from a noiseless GHZ state, they cannot do it from a
W
state even if the state is ideal. Therefore, error correction is inevitable for multipartite DI-QKD protocols using
W
states. Explicitly, when all the parties perform
\sigma_{X}
measurement on a
W
state, the error correction cost is

h\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{N}\right),

(3)

where
h
is the binary entropy and
N
is the number of parties
30
.

To realize multipartite DI-QKD protocols that use
W
states, the first term in (
2
) must be sufficiently large to compensate the error correction cost. The first term can be calculated by the violation of Bell inequalities
56
;
44
;
65
;
12
;
13
. To the best of our knowledge, however, there are no known Bell inequalities which make the first term high enough to realize DI-QKD protocols using
W
states. Thus, in this study, we construct Bell inequalities that
W

W
-based DI-QKD protocols possible.

We construct Bell inequalities which give sufficiently large values of the conditional von Neumann entropy in (

(4)

\displaystyle\bm{h}

\displaystyle=[h_{x\bm{y}}^{a\bm{b}}],

(5)

\displaystyle\bm{p}

\displaystyle=[p(a,\bm{b}|x,\bm{y})],

(6)

where
\bm{h},\bm{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{m^{N}d^{N}}

\begin{split}\bm{p}=[&p(0,\ldots,0|0,\ldots,0),\,\,~\ldots,p(d,\ldots,d|0,\ldots,0),\\

&p(0,\ldots,0|m,\ldots,m),\ldots,p(d,\ldots,d|m,\ldots,m)].\end{split}

(7)

(8)

We search a Bell expression

\begin{split}H(A|x^{*},E)&\geq H_{\text{min}}(A|x^{*},E)\\

(9)

Let

&\left[M_{a|x},N_{b_{i}|y_{i}}\right]=\left[M_{a|x},Z_{c}\right]=\left[N_{b_{i}|y_{i}},Z_{c}\right]=0\qquad\text{for all}~a,b_{i},c,x,y_{i},i\\
\end{split}

Calculating classical and quantum limits

The above method gives a Bell expression
\bm{h}

First, we explain how to calculate a classical limit

\bm{v}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m^{N}d^{N}}
where
(k\in\{1,2,\ldots,d^{Nm}\})

Then, a classical correlation

&M_{a|x}\geq 0,\quad N_{b_{i}|y_{i}}\geq 0\quad\text{for all}~a,b_{i},x,y_{i}\\
&\left[M_{a|x},N_{b_{i}|y_{i}}\right]=0\quad\text{for all}~a,b_{i},x,y_{i}\\
&M_{a|x},N_{b_{i}|y_{i}}\in B(\mathcal{H})\quad\text{for all}~a,b_{i},x,y_{i}\end{split}

where the maximum is taken over all quantum states

V
Analyzing required detection efficiency

In this section, we consider realistic detectors with imperfect detection efficiency and analyze the minimum required detection efficiency for multipartite DI-QKD protocols using
W
states.
The guessing probability approach used in the previous section works pretty well if there is no experimental imperfections.
However, we find that this approach is highly susceptible to imperfections including finite detection efficiency.
Therefore, in this section, we take another approach to calculate key rates, that is, the numerical optimization based on the quasi-relative entropy developed in Ref.
13
.
Here, we consider that an
N
-partite
W
state is distributed by the direct transmission where the
W
state is locally generated at some place and distributed among the parties (Fig.
1
(a)). We assume unit success probability,
P_{\text{succ}}=1
, for the
W
state generation and the state is shared by each party without any transmission losses and noises (i.e.
\eta=1
in Fig.
1
(a)).
We only consider the non-unit detection efficiency as the imperfection.

V.1
Measurement modeling

First, let us explain how to model detection efficiency. We consider scenarios where each party has two measurment settings with two outcomes, i.e.,
(N,2,2)
scenarios. For
i\in\{1,\ldots,N-1\}
, we define POVMs corresponding to Alice’s and
\text{Bob}_{i}
’s measurements as follows

\displaystyle M_{0|x}

\displaystyle=0.5\eta_{e}(I+\Pi(\theta_{x}^{A})),

\displaystyle\,M_{1|x}

\displaystyle=I-M_{0|x},

(21)

\displaystyle N_{0|y_{i}}

\displaystyle=0.5\eta_{e}(I+\Pi(\theta_{y_{i}}^{B_{i}})),

\displaystyle\,N_{1|y_{i}}

\displaystyle=I-N_{0|y_{i}},

(22)

\Pi(\theta)=\cos\theta\sigma_{Z}+\sin\theta\sigma_{X}

\theta_{x}^{A},\theta_{y_{1}}^{B_{1}},\ldots,\theta_{y_{N-1}}^{B_{N-1}}

In addition, we consider another measurement model to analyze experimental feasibility of multipartite DI-QKD protocols using
W

Therefore, we consider its approximate version where the measurement consists of optical displacement operation followed by a photon detection. This measurement is expressed as

\{\outerproduct{\alpha}{\alpha},I-\outerproduct{\alpha}{\alpha}\},

(23)

where
\ket{\alpha}
is a coherent state

\ket{\alpha}=e^{-\frac{|\alpha|^{2}}{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}\ket{n}.

(24)

Although Bell-inequality violation with this measurement and

states was analyzed in Ref.
8

\eta_{e}
as a pure-loss channel with transmissivity
\eta_{e}

In this method, we can obtain a lower bound on the conditional von Neumann entropy in (

13

\begin{split}c_{m}+\inf\quad&\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\frac{w_{i}}{t_{i}\ln 2}\sum_{a=0}^{1}\text{Tr}[\rho\hat{M}_{a|x^{*}}(Z_{a,i}+Z_{a,i}^{*}+(1-t_{i})Z_{a,i}^{*}Z_{a,i})+t_{i}Z_{a,i}Z_{a,i}^{*}]\\

(26)

where the infimum is taken over all quantum states
\rho

VIII
Conclusion

In this paper, we theoretically demonstrate that one can construct multipartite DI-QKD with
W
states.
We numerically construct Bell inequalities which
W
states violate largely. By using these Bell inequalities, we construct a multipartite DI-QKD protocol and show that one can extract device-independent secret keys from
W
states. We also discuss the realistic situations. First, we analyze the required detection efficiencies for
W
-based DI-QKD protocols when
W
states are ideally distributed. In addition, we take into account the channel losses for
W
state distribution. We propose to use the RIHT protocol for efficient distribution of
W
states over lossy channels and show that this protocol achieves longer distances than a multipartite DI-QKD protocol based on GHZ states even with experimentally feasible implementations. Our results open a potential of
W
states in DI-QKD.i

There are several interesting perspectives of this research.
In this paper, we numerically show that the quantum limits of our Bell inequalities are almost saturated by
W
states.
From the fundamental viewpoint, an important direction is to prove this analytically. It will give us better understanding of the relationship between
W
states and Bell inequalities. Improvement of the
W
state-based multipartite DI-QKD or application of the other multipartite entangled states such as Dicke states and
N00N
states are also interesting future work in practical side.

Appendix A
NPA hierarchy

Let us explain the NPA hierarchy. In this paper, we use the NPA hierarchy to calculate the conditional von Neumann entropy in (
2
) and quantum limits of Bell inequalities. Full explanation can be found in the original paper
48
.

First, we construct an optimization problem which is relaxed into SDP later by using the NPA hierarchy. Let
\mathcal{H}
denote a Hilbert space and
\rho
be a quantum state on
\mathcal{H}
. We define a set of operators on the same Hilbert space
\mathcal{O}
. Also, we consider expectation values of operators in this set
\expectationvalue{O_i^\dagger O_j}=\text{Tr}[\rho O_{i}^{\dagger}O_{j}]
where
O_{i}\in\mathcal{O}
. We consider an optimization problem where we minimize a linear combination of these expectation values over all quantum states
\rho
and operators
O_{i}
. The objective functions of the optimization problems in (
10
), (
20
), and (
26
) are expressed in this way. Since we optimize over all quantum states and operators without any constraints on the dimension of the Hilbert space, this optimization is not feasible. Then, by using the NPA hierarchy, we relax this optimization problem into SDP which is efficiently solved. In the
k
-th level NPA hierarchy, we choose
l
operators from a set of operators which is composed of Alice’s measurement operators, Bobs’ measurement operators, Eve’s measurement operators and the identity operator for
1\leq l\leq k
. Then, we consider a set of products of
l
those operators
\mathcal{O}_{k}
. We construct a
k
-th moment matrix
\Gamma^{(k)}
whose entries are
\Gamma_{ij}^{(k)}=\expectationvalue{(O_i^{(k)})^\dagger O_j^{(k)}}
where
O_{i}^{(k)}\in\mathcal{O}_{k}
. It is known that a moment matrix constructed in this way is positive semidefinite. Also, the objective function of the optimization problem is expressed as a linear combination of entries of a moment matrix. Therefore, the optimization problem is considered as SDP whose variable is a moment matrix. Here, note that a
k
-th level moment matrix does not necessarily correspond to feasible points of the original optimization problem and the range of optimization in the
k
-th level NPA hierarchy is wider than that of the original optimization problem. In the limit of
k\to\infty
, however, it is known that optimal values of these optimization problems coincide. We perform the NPA hierarchy by using NCPOL2SDPA
67
and solve SDPs by using MOSEK
2
.

Appendix B
Duality of semidefinite programming

We describe the duality of SDP
9
. We consider the following SDP

\begin{split}\min_{X}\quad&\text{Tr}[CX]\\
\text{s.t.}\quad&\text{Tr}[A_{i}X]=b_{i}\quad(i=1,\ldots,m)\\
&X\geq 0\end{split}

(33)

where
C
and
A_{i}
are
n\times n
matrices,
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a constraint vector, and we minimize over all positive semidefinite matrices
X
. We call this optimization a primal problem. We can construct a dual problem corresponding to the primal problem as follows

\begin{split}\max_{y,Z}\quad&\sum_{i=1}^{m}y_{i}b_{i}\\
\text{s.t.}\quad&C-\sum_{i}y_{i}A_{i}-Z=0\\
&Z\geq 0\end{split}

(34)

where we maximize over all vectors
y
and positive semidefinite matrices
Z
.

There is an important relationship between the primal and dual problems, that is, the following inequality holds

\text{Tr}[CX]\geq\sum_{i=1}^{m}y_{i}b_{i}.

(35)

In other words, an optimal value of the primal problem is not smaller than that of the dual problem. This inequality is derived in the following way.

\begin{split}\text{Tr}[CX]-\sum_{i}b_{i}y_{i}&=\text{Tr}[(\sum_{i}y_{i}A_{i}+Z)X]-\sum_{i}b_{i}y_{i}\\
&=\sum_{i}b_{i}y_{i}+\text{Tr}[ZX]-\sum_{i}b_{i}y_{i}\\
&=\text{Tr}[ZX]\\
&\geq 0\end{split}

(36)

Here, the inequality holds since
\text{Tr}[AB]\geq 0
when both
A
and
B
are positive semidefinite.

Appendix E
State calculation with Gaussian formalism

We calculate joint probability distribution of legitimate parties. Here, we give an argument for
N=3,4
.

First, denote
N
pairs of anihilation and creation operators
\{\hat{a}_{i},\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
which satisfy the following commutation relations

[\hat{a}_{i},\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j}]=\delta_{ij}.

(70)

By using these operators, we define the quadrature field operators
\{\hat{q}_{i},\hat{p}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
as follows

\displaystyle\hat{q}_{i}

\displaystyle=\hat{a}_{i}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i},

(71)

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{i}

\displaystyle=i(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i}-\hat{a}_{i}).

(72)

In the
N
-mode bosonic system, a quantum state
\rho
is characterized by its characteristic function

\chi(x)=\text{Tr}[\rho\mathcal{W}(x)],

(73)

where
\mathcal{W}
is a Weyl operator defined as

\mathcal{W}(x)=\exp[-ix^{T}r],

(74)

x\in\mathbb{R}^{2N}
is a real vector, and
r=[\hat{q}_{1},\hat{p}_{1},\ldots,\hat{q}_{N},\hat{p}_{N}]^{T}
is a vector that consists of the qudrature operators.

The covariance matrix of a two-mode squeezed state with mean photon number
\bar{n}
is as follows

\Gamma^{\text{TMSV}}(\bar{n})=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}vI_{2}&\sqrt{v^{2}-1}Z\\
\sqrt{v^{2}-1}Z&vI_{2}\end{array}\right],

(75)

where
I_{n}
is the
n\times n
identity matrix and

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&-1\end{array}\right].

The parameter
v
corresponds to squeezing level of the two-mode squeezed state, and it relates to the mean photon number of the state
\bar{n}
as
v=1+2\bar{n}
. Here, we omit an argument for a displacement vector since it is always a zero vector. Then, a coveriance matrix of an initial state is as follows

\Gamma^{\text{initial}}=\Gamma_{\bm{X}\bm{X^{\prime}}}^{\text{TMSV}}(\bar{n})\oplus I_{\bm{E}\bm{F}},

(78)

where

\begin{split}\Gamma_{\bm{X}\bm{X^{\prime}}}^{\text{TMSV}}(\bar{n})&=\Gamma_{AA^{\prime}}^{\text{TMSV}}(\bar{n})\oplus\Gamma_{B_{1}B_{1}^{\prime}}^{\text{TMSV}}(\bar{n})\\
&\quad\oplus\Gamma^{\text{TMSV}}_{B_{2}B_{2}^{\prime}}(\bar{n})\oplus\Gamma^{\text{TMSV}}_{B_{3}B_{3}^{\prime}}(\bar{n}),\end{split}

(79)

modes
A
and
B_{i}
are detected by Alice and
\text{Bob}_{i}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,N-1\}
, modes
A^{\prime}
and
B_{i}^{\prime}
are sent to the central station,
\bm{E}
denotes environmental modes for the pure-loss channels, and
\bm{F}
denotes environmental modes for the on-off detectors of the parties, respectively. We can model a pure-loss channel with transmissivity
\eta
as a beamsplitter with vacuum in one of the input modes. Also, we can effectively shift the detection efficiencies of the on-off detectors at each party’s sites prior to their displacement operations since any loss at the detectors can be compensated by changing the amplitude. The operation of the beamsplitter with transmissivity
\eta
can be expressed as follows

S^{\text{BS}}(\eta)=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\sqrt{\eta}\,I_{2}&\sqrt{1-\eta}\,I_{2}\\
-\sqrt{1-\eta}\,I_{2}&\sqrt{\eta}\,I_{2}\end{array}\right].

(80)

We can obtain a state after the channel transmission by applying this operation to the modes denoted by
\bm{X^{\prime}}
and
\bm{E}
.
Next, we consider the operation of the central node. The operation of a 50:50 beamsplitter is

S^{\text{50:50BS}}=S^{\text{BS}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{2}&I_{2}\\
-I_{2}&I_{2}\end{array}\right].

(81)

Then, we can calculate a quantum state after the interferometer
U
by appropriately applying this operation to the quantum state. We consider detection efficiencies of the on-off detectors at each party’s sites. These operations are expressed by beamsplitters with transmissivity
\eta_{e}
. Therefore, by applying these operations, we can get a quantum state before the single-photon interference, and let
\sigma
and

A^{\prime}
