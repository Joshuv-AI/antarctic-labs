The Optimal Rate Function in Covariant Quantum State Tomography
Arick Grootveld, Alexander Maloney, Jason Pollack, Peixue Wu

Abstract
The problem of quantum tomography is to estimate an unknown quantum state ρ \rho from a measurement of n n copies of ρ \rho . One can ask which tomography protocol, i.e. which choice of multi-copy measurement, gives the best possible estimate of ρ \rho . To do so, we characterize tomography protocols by their rate function , which governs the exponential rate at which a protocol assigns probability to a particular estimate σ \sigma of the true state ρ \rho . This rate function is a quantum mechanical generalization of the classical relative entropy between the true state and its estimate, and depends on the choice of protocol. It is bounded by the quantum relative entropy, and we show that this bound is sharp: for any ρ \rho and σ \sigma we construct a family of protocols whose rate functions converge to the quantum relative entropy D ( σ ∥ ρ ) D(\sigma\|\rho) . We consider the family of covariant tomography protocols; these are the basis independent state estimation schemes that assume no prior information about ρ \rho and σ \sigma . Keyl described a specific tomography protocol based on Schur sampling, and conjectured that among all covariant tomography protocols it has the largest possible rate function for all σ \sigma and ρ \rho . We prove this conjecture. The resulting rate function is an annealed version of quantum relative entropy, due to the cost of learning the eigenbasis in covariant quantum state tomography.

Introduction

1.1
Informal Overview

The basic task of quantum state tomography is the following: given
n
copies of an unknown quantum state
\rho
, obtain an accurate estimate for
\rho
. To do so, one must perform a measurement (or measurements) on these
n
copies.
Of course, in the limit
n\to\infty
one can determine
\rho
perfectly by, for example, measuring its matrix elements to any desired accuracy.
But in any realistic scenario – such as where
\rho
is generated by a physical process which we seek to characterize –
n
is finite, and we can obtain only an approximate description of
\rho
.
The natural question is whether there is an
optimal
state estimation scheme. Or more precisely: is there a sequence of measurements – and description of
\rho
which one obtains from these measurements – which gives the best possible approximation to
\rho
?

The goal of this paper is to answer this question in the affirmative, for a certain definition of “best possible approximation” of the unknown state
\rho
.
Specifically, we will characterize possible estimation schemes in terms of the asymptotic behaviour as
n\to\infty
of the probability that a scheme makes a large error in its determination of
\rho
. We will then show that an algorithm of Keyl
Key 06
provides the optimal approximation scheme, in the sense that it minimizes these errors.

To understand this, let us begin by stating the problem of quantum tomography a bit more precisely.
Consider an unknown density matrix
\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{d}
which we are trying to approximate, where
\mathcal{D}_{d}
is the state space of a
d

n

\rho^{\otimes n}

We wish to perform a measurement of
\rho^{\otimes n}
in order to obtain an approximate description of
\rho
. Of course, this approximate description will itself be a density matrix on the original
d
-dimensional quantum system; let us call this approximation
\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{d}
.
What this all means is that the measurement will be described by a positive operator-valued measure (POVM)
M_{\sigma}^{(n)}

\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{d}

The probability of obtaining a particular estimate

What we have sketched above is the basic mathematical formulation of quantum tomography: a tomography protocol is a POVM on the

P_{n}(\sigma|\rho)

\sigma=\rho

Much of the modern literature on quantum state tomography is formulated in terms of sample complexity, which is a measure of how rapidly the probability density
P_{n}(\sigma|\rho)

\sigma=\rho

\varepsilon>0

\Pr\{\|\sigma-\rho\|_{1}>\varepsilon\}\equiv\int_{\|\sigma-\rho\|_{1}>\varepsilon}P_{n}(\sigma|\rho)d\sigma\leq\eta.

This line of work has led to sharp bounds for mixed-state tomography, and to
efficient algorithms which achieve nearly optimal or optimal sample complexity

However, while
sample complexity is an essential benchmark, it gives only a coarse description of the error behavior of a tomography protocol.
For example, two protocols might have the same sample complexity

\sigma

\rho

, without taking into account the global structure of the probability distribution
P_{n}(\sigma|\rho)

In this paper we will characterize tomography protocols by studying the full probability distribution
P_{n}(\sigma|\rho)
in the large
n

I(\sigma\|\rho)\equiv-\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log P_{n}(\sigma|\rho),

(2)

when it exists. The function
I(\sigma\|\rho)
is known as the

for a tomography protocol, and captures the full asymptotic error landscape:
as
I(\sigma\|\rho)
increases, the error where we mistake
\rho
for
\sigma

becomes exponentially less likely at large

.
Thus, unlike the sample complexity, it allows for asymptotic comparisons of tomography protocols which take into account large deviations of the estimator
\sigma
. Implicitly in equation (
2

n
, as
P_{n}(\sigma|\rho)\approx e^{-nI(\sigma\|\rho)}
. As in classical statistics, this is often (but not always) the case; this is tantamount to the assumption that
P_{n}(\sigma|\rho)

The definition (
2
) is familiar from the classical theory of empirical measures: if one samples a classical probability distribution many times, then the Sanov theorem says that the probability of observing an empirical distribution decays with rate given by the classical relative entropy.

The quantum case is more subtle. The rate function
(
2
) depends on the choice of tomography protocol: different POVMs may suppress different erroneous estimates at different exponential rates. Nevertheless, as we review below, it is still the case that the rate is upper bounded by the quantum relative entropy
(
Key 06
, Theorem 3.3)
:

I(\sigma\|\rho)\leq D(\sigma\|\rho):={\rm Tr}\,\sigma\left(\log\sigma-\log\rho\right).

(3)

The bound (
3
) raises a natural question: is the relative entropy actually achievable as a rate function for quantum tomography? Our first result answers this question in a pointwise sense. We will define an appropriate set
\mathcal{E}
of ‘‘good’’ rate functions.
3
3

3

Specifically,
\mathcal{E}
is the set of rate functions for tomography protocols which are consistent with LDP, and which obey the unique-zero property that
I(\sigma|\rho)=0
only if
\sigma=\rho
; this means that errors become exponentially unlikely in the large
n
limit. See Section
4

Then the relative entropy upper bound is sharp at every pair of states:

Theorem 1.1
.

For all
\sigma,\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{d}
,

\sup_{I\in\mathcal{E}}I(\sigma\|\rho)=D(\sigma\|\rho).

This theorem resolves the pointwise relative entropy envelope predicted in
Key 06
.
It is important, however, to emphasize the pointwise nature of this result:
the protocol used to approach
D(\sigma\|\rho)
will depend on the pair
(\sigma,\rho)
.
This means that a protocol that performs well for one pair
(\sigma,\rho)
may perform poorly for other pairs
(\sigma^{\prime},\rho^{\prime})
.

Our goal is to understand whether there is a protocol which is optimal in a sense that is
independent
of the choice of
\rho
and
\sigma
.
In other words, the “best” protocol should maximize
I(\sigma\|\rho)
without assuming any information about the unknown state
\rho
. Such a protocol should be basis-independent. We will therefore focus our attention on tomography protocols which are
covariant
, in the sense that a change of basis for
\rho
will lead to the same change of basis for our estimate
\sigma
. More precisely, covariant protocols transform equivariantly with respect to the
\mathrm{U}(d)
unitary rotations of the original
d
-state quantum system: for any unitary
U
, we have

M^{(n)}_{U\sigma U^{\dagger}}=U^{\otimes n}M^{(n)}_{\sigma}(U^{\dagger})^{\otimes n}\,.

(4)

We now have a clear mathematical goal: optimize the rate function within the space of covariant tomography protocols.
In doing so, it is natural to decompose the POVMs with respect to both the
\mathrm{U}(d)
rotations of the
d
-state system, and the
S_{n}
symmetry that permutes the
n
samples.
The resulting representation theory is straightforward, due to Schur-Weyl duality
Sch 01
;
Wey 46
.

In studying this question, we will be able to answer a question first posed by Keyl
Key 06
, who initiated the large-deviation approach to quantum state tomography based on the rate function (
2
).
Keyl proposed a tomography protocol, using Schur–Weyl duality, in which
\rho
is estimated through a two-step measurement procedure. First, we estimate the spectrum of
\rho
by weak Schur sampling, i.e. by using the measurement that projects
\rho^{\otimes n}
onto an irreducible representation of the
S_{n}
symmetry that permutes the
n
samples. By Schur-Weyl duality, this is also an irreducible representation of
\mathrm{U}(d)
. So in the second step we estimate the eigenbasis through a covariant measurement built from the
highest-weight vector in this
\mathrm{U}(d)
representation. This protocol is covariant, in the sense described above.

Keyl computed the rate function of this protocol.
Writing

\sigma=U\operatorname{diag}(x)U^{\dagger},

(5)

where the spectrum
x
is ordered from largest to smallest, the rate function is
(
Key 06
, Theorem 3.2)
:

S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}(\sigma\|\rho)=-H(x)-\sum_{k=1}^{d}(x_{k}-x_{k+1})\log\Delta_{k}(U^{\dagger}\rho U),\quad x_{d+1}\equiv 0,

(6)

where
H(x)
is the Shannon entropy of
x
, and
\Delta_{k}(\cdot)
is the determinant of the leading
k\times k
principal minor.
This reduces to the relative entropy

D(\sigma\|\rho)
when
\sigma
and

\rho
commute, but can be smaller otherwise.

Our main result is that Keyl’s algorithm is optimal among all covariant quantum tomography protocols. Specifically, we prove

Theorem 1.2
.

If a covariant tomography protocol is consistent, i.e., if for any unknown state
\rho
and any
\varepsilon>0
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr\{\sigma\in N_{\varepsilon}(\rho)\}=1,\quad N_{\varepsilon}(\rho):=\{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{d}:\|\tau-\rho\|_{1}<\varepsilon\},

(7)

and if it satisfies a large deviation principle with rate function
I(\cdot\|\cdot)
, then its rate function is pointwise upper bounded by Keyl’s rate:

I(\sigma\|\rho)\leq S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}(\sigma\|\rho),

for all quantum states
\sigma,\rho
.

The only non-trivial assumption in this theorem is the consistency requirement, equation (
7
). This is simply the requirement that our tomography protocol “works,” in the sense that the probability measure for the estimator
\sigma
concentrates around
\rho
as
n\to\infty
. In other words, it is the requirement that the sample complexity is finite.
Since Keyl’s original protocol achieves the rate

S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}
, this theorem proves that Keyl’s rate is the optimal large-deviation exponent among consistent covariant tomography protocols.

This also gives a precise large-deviation formulation of the optimality suggested in recent work on debiased versions of Keyl’s algorithm
PSW 25
, where the authors ask in what sense Keyl’s entangled measurement should be regarded as optimal for full state tomography.

Before describing the proof of this theorem, let us comment on the physical intuition behind this result.
First, we note that
S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}(\sigma\|\rho)\leq D(\sigma\|\rho)
, and that this inequality is saturated only when
\sigma
and
\rho
commute (as we show in Proposition
4.6
).
Intuitively, this means that the problem of quantum state-estimation is more difficult than its classical counterpart. The reason is that in the quantum case one must determine not just the eigenvalues of
\rho
, but also its eigenbasis. It is the difficulty of finding this basis which slows down the rate at which any covariant tomography protocol can determine a state, from
D(\sigma\|\rho)
to
S^{\rm{Keyl}}(\sigma\|\rho)
.

In fact, this obstruction is already visible in our formula (
6
) for
S^{\rm{Keyl}}(\sigma\|\rho)
.
To see this, consider the basis where
\sigma
is diagonal, with eigevalues
x_{1}\geq x_{2}\geq\dots\geq x_{d}
and corresponding eigenstates
\left\{|i\rangle\right\}_{i=1}^{d}
. It is easy to see that

\sigma=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(x_{k}-x_{k+1}\right)\Pi_{k}

where
\Pi_{k}\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{k}|i\rangle\langle i|
is the projector on to the first
k
eigenstates.
We can then compare the usual relative entropy

D(\sigma\|\rho)=-H(x)-\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(x_{k}-x_{k+1}\right){\rm Tr}\,\left(\Pi_{k}\log\rho\,\Pi_{k}\right)

(8)

to Keyl’s rate function

S^{\rm{Keyl}}(\sigma\|\rho)=-H(x)-\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(x_{k}-x_{k+1}\right){\rm Tr}\,\log\left(\Pi_{k}\,\rho\,\Pi_{k}\right)

(9)

The only difference between the two is whether the projection
\Pi_{k}
is carried out inside or outside the logarithm.
This distinction can be interpreted as the additional cost of learning the eigenbasis of
\rho
.

This phenomenon is familiar from statistical physics, where one studies the thermodynamic properties of statistical averages. Quantities like (
8
), which involve the average of a logarithm, are known as
quenched
averages.
Quantities like (
9
) involve the logarithm of an average, and are known as
annealed
averages.
In the present case, the “averaging” under consideration is the projection onto the most likely
k
-dimensional eigenspace of
\sigma
.
4
4

4

To make the analogy even sharper, we can regard
H=-\log\rho
as a “Hamlitonian” which drives the learning process, and consider the Slater determinant state
|\Omega_{k}\rangle\equiv|1\rangle\wedge\dots\wedge|k\rangle\in\Lambda^{k}\mathbb{C}^{d}
in the
k
-fold exterior product of our Hilbert space. Then the traces appearing in (
8
) and (
9
) are the quenched and annealed free energies,
\langle\Omega_{k}|\Lambda^{k}\log\rho|\Omega_{k}\rangle
and
\log\langle\Omega_{k}|\Lambda^{k}\rho|\Omega_{k}\rangle
, respectively. Slater determinant states are labeled by
k
-dimensional subspaces (i.e. elements of the Grassmanian
\mathrm{Gr}_{k}({\mathbb{C}}^{d})
), so we may interpret these terms as the cost associated with determining successive rank
k
eigenspaces of
\rho
.

Quenched averages arise when the quantities being averaged over do not interact with the dynamics of the system being studied. Annealed averages arise when the quantities being averaged evolve along with the dynamics of the system. In our analogy with statistical mechanics, the “dynamics” is that of the learning process according to a topographic protocol. This perspective suggests why the optimal rate among covariant protocols is
S^{\rm{Keyl}}(\sigma\|\rho)
rather than
D(\sigma\|\rho)
: a covariant protocol requires one to learn the eigenspaces at the same time that one learns the spectrum, resulting in an annealed average rather than a quenched one.

Sketch of proof of Theorem

The converse part is a simple application of quantum Stein lemma

;

(\sigma,\rho)
and an exponent

\omega_{*}

Sketch of proof of Theorem

The proof is divided into three steps:

Step I: A normal form of a general covariant tomography protocol

Every tomography protocol is i.i.d.-statistically equivalent to one which is invariant under permutations of the
n
samples. This allows us to characterize the POVM in the Schur basis. A covariant protocol is a POVM indexed by a spectrum
x
and unitary
U
for our estimator
\sigma
, as in (
5
). In the Schur basis, the POVM takes the form given in Figure
2
, where
\lambda^{k}
labels different Young diagrams with
n
boxes and
d
rows. This characterization is given in Theorem
3.5
.

Figure 2:
Block diagonal form of a covariant POVM density in the Schur basis.

A general tomography protocol is the following:

Given
n
-copies of
\rho
:

1.

Apply the Schur transform to write
\rho^{\otimes n}
in the Schur basis.

2.

Perform the POVM of Figure
2
, indexed by spectrum
x
and unitary
U
.

3.

Collect the measurement outcomes
\widehat{x}
and
\widehat{U}
, and output

\sigma=\widehat{U}\operatorname{diag}(\widehat{x})\widehat{U}^{\dagger}.

A key point is that, since we are studying covariant protocols, the output gives separate estimates for the spectrum
\widehat{x}
and the eigenbasis
\widehat{U}
.

Step II: A quantum analogue of the correct empirical type.

The second step uses consistency. Classically, if the true distribution is
x
, then a consistent estimator must place almost all of its mass near the empirical type
x
. In the quantum setting, the Young diagram
\lambda
plays the role of the empirical type. Given a state
\operatorname{diag}(x)^{\otimes n}
, Schur sampling results in a measure concentrated on Young diagrams whose
normalized shape
\lambda/n
is close to
x
.

Therefore, if the tomography protocol is consistent, then when the true state is
\operatorname{diag}(x)
most of the probability
will come from Young diagrams satisfying
\lambda/n\approx x
. Since there are only
polynomially many Young diagrams, at least one such Schur block must contribute with the correct exponential order. This is the content of Lemma
6.2
.

Step III: Weight analysis inside the selected block.

The final step analyzes the selected Schur block more carefully. Each Schur
module decomposes into weight spaces for the diagonal torus
\mathrm{T}(d)\subseteq\mathrm{U}(d)
. Because the
localized neighborhood of
\operatorname{diag}(x)
is invariant under the torus, the
relevant localized operator can be assumed to be block diagonal in these weight
spaces.

Consistency forces a non-negligible contribution from weights whose normalized
weight is close to
x
. A principal-minor lower bound, Lemma
6.3
, then compares the value of the same block on an arbitrary state
\rho
with its value on
\operatorname{diag}(x)
. As shown in Lemma
6.4
, this comparison is exactly where Keyl’s expression—involving logarithms of principal minors—appears.

Combining these ingredients gives a lower bound on the probability of obtaining an estimate

\sigma
when the true state is
\rho
. Equivalently, it gives the upper bound

I(\sigma\|\rho)\leq S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}(\sigma\|\rho),

for the rate function of any consistent covariant protocol satisfying a Large Deviation Principle. Since Keyl’s protocol achieves
S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}
, this proves the optimality of Keyl’s rate among covariant tomography protocols.

1.3
Related work and discussion

The rate
S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}
has appeared in several other contexts, often
under a different name. It is the same quantity as the reverse quantum relative entropy
D_{R}(\sigma\|\rho):=\lim_{\alpha\to 1}D_{RS,\alpha}(\sigma\|\rho)
, where
D_{RS,\alpha}
is the reverse sandwiched Rényi divergence introduced in
(
AD 15
, equation (9))
. With this
notation,

S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}(\sigma\|\rho)=D_{R}(\sigma\|\rho).

Recent work of Hayashi and Fang
HF 26
adopts the name reverse quantum relative entropy for this limit and gives it an operational interpretation in composite quantum hypothesis testing. Fix a basis in which the null state
\rho
is diagonal, and let
G
be the diagonal torus on the corresponding basis. They consider the composite alternative

\mathscr{A}_{n}=\{\rho^{\otimes n}\},\quad\mathscr{B}_{n}=\left\{g^{\otimes n}\sigma^{\otimes n}(g^{\dagger})^{\otimes n}:g\in G\right\}.

In the Stein limit, Corollary 26 in
HF 26
shows that the optimal Type-II error exponent is given by
D_{R}(\rho\|\sigma)=S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}(\rho\|\sigma)
.

Keyl’s rate appears naturally through pinching methods in quantum hypothesis testing, including the test achieving the optimal rate in Hayashi and Fang’s work. Let

\mathcal{P}_{\rho^{\otimes n}}
denote the pinching map onto the spectral
projections of
\rho^{\otimes n}
. If one pinches the alternative state

\sigma^{\otimes n}
in the eigenbasis of the null state

\rho^{\otimes n}
, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}D\left(\rho^{\otimes n}\,\middle\|\mathcal{P}_{\rho^{\otimes n}}(\sigma^{\otimes n})\right)=D_{R}(\rho\|\sigma)=S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}(\rho\|\sigma).

This “right-pinched” or reverse-pinched relative entropy is the

\alpha\to 1
endpoint of the right-pinched Rényi divergences studied in
LBCR+ 24
. In contrast, the standard pinching used in proofs of quantum Stein’s lemma
HP 91
pinches the first argument in the eigenbasis of the second and recovers the usual Umegaki relative entropy
D(\rho\|\sigma)
. The same reverse-pinching mechanism appears in hypothesis testing with inconclusive outcomes. Ji and Regula
JR 25
show that, for three-outcome tests with an abstention outcome, reverse pinching yields a test with conclusive probability tending to one while achieving the Umegaki exponent in one type of error and the Keyl exponent in the other.

An additional interpretation of Keyl’s rate was given in
Nöt 15
as the asymptotic rate function for a proposed quantum method of types. Keyl’s rate also appeared, although implicitly in
Hay 25
. There, Hayashi proposed a quantum empirical distribution, achieving Keyl’s rate function in a version of Sanov’s theorem for hypothesis testing. Keyl’s tomography algorithm was generalized to arbitrary compact Lie groups via moment map estimation in
BCV 21
, resulting in a variation of Keyl’s rate function.

Open problems.
We end this subsection by recording several open directions suggested by the appearance of

S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}
in tomography and hypothesis testing.

First, our covariant optimality theorem leaves open a regularity version of Keyl’s original optimality question. Keyl conjectured that the lower semicontinuous envelope of admissible tomographic rate functions should be given by
S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}
. Theorem
1.1
shows that, without such regularity, the relative entropy
D(\sigma\|\rho)
is attainable pointwise by protocols tailored to
(\sigma,\rho)
. Moreover, Theorem
1.2
shows that covariance forces the smaller bound
S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}
. It remains open whether joint
lower semicontinuity alone, without assuming covariance of the protocol, is already enough to imply

I(\sigma\|\rho)\leq S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}(\sigma\|\rho)\qquad\forall\sigma,\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{d}.

Second, it would be interesting to understand whether Keyl’s rate admits a broader characterization through composite quantum hypothesis testing. In simple binary hypothesis testing between two fixed i.i.d. states, the optimal Stein exponent is the Umegaki relative entropy. However, composite hypothesis testing is substantially more subtle: the optimal exponent can be governed by regularized entropic quantities and can be
strictly smaller than the naive worst-pairwise relative entropy

MSW 22
;
BBH 21
;
FMVW 25
;
Lam 25
. This suggests the following problem: characterize the composite hypothesis testing problems whose optimal Stein exponent can achieve or be bounded by
S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}
.

Third, the relation between large-deviation rates and the usual sample complexity of tomography remains to be clarified. Sample complexity asks for
finite-
n
guarantees of the form

\Pr\{\|\sigma-\rho\|_{1}>\varepsilon\}\leq\delta,

whereas an LDP controls the exponential rate of this probability for fixed

\varepsilon
and fixed dimension at sufficiently large
n
:

\Pr\{\|\sigma-\rho\|_{1}>\varepsilon\}\approx\exp\left\{-nI(\sigma\|\rho)\right\}.

Thus, a pointwise larger rate function gives better asymptotic concentration, but this does not immediately imply better sample complexity uniformly in
d
, rank,
\varepsilon
, and
\delta
, because subexponential prefactors, and
local small-
\varepsilon
behavior can matter. Recent works have established essentially optimal sample-complexity bounds for mixed-state tomography
HHJ+ 17
;
OW 16
;
PSTW 25
;
HCMT+ 26
.
A natural problem is to determine how these finite-sample bounds are related to the rate functions. For example, does the
second-order expansion of
I(\sigma\|\rho)
near the diagonal

\sigma=\rho
recover the optimal
d
- and rank-dependence of tomography? Conversely, can one turn a globally stronger LDP rate into uniform non-asymptotic sample-complexity improvements?

the set of quantum states on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. We also denote by

\mathrm{U}(d)
the group of unitary operators on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, by
\mathrm{GL}(d)
the group of invertible linear operators on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
and by
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
the set of all linear operators.

•

Let

\Delta_{d}:=\left\{x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:x_{i}\geq 0,\ \sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}=1\right\}

be the probability simplex, and let

\Delta_{d}^{\downarrow}:=\left\{x\in\Delta_{d}:x_{1}\geq x_{2}\geq\cdots\geq x_{d}\right\}

be the set of probability vectors arranged in decreasing order. For any
vector
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we write
\alpha^{\downarrow}
for the vector
obtained by rearranging the entries of
\alpha
in decreasing order.

•

For
\alpha,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}
, we say that

\alpha
is majorized by
\lambda
, denoted by

\alpha\prec\lambda
, if

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}^{\downarrow}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}^{\downarrow},\qquad 1\leq k\leq d-1.

For any matrix
A

\Delta_{k}(A)

A_{k\times k}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}a_{ij}|i\rangle\langle j|

2.1
Schur–Weyl duality

Let
d,n\in\mathbb{N}
, and write
[d]=\{1,\ldots,d\}
.
We work with the
n
-partite Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}=(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes n},

equipped with its computational basis

\bigl\{|i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{n}\rangle:i_{k}\in[d]\bigr\}.

(11)

Thus the notation
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}
fixes both the tensor-product
structure and the computational bases. Let

\Lambda_{d,n}:=\{\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{d}:\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{d},\ \sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}=n\},

(12)

which is the set of Young diagrams with
n
boxes and at most
d
rows. We denote
\lambda\vdash_{d}n
as an element in
\Lambda_{d,n}
and
\ell(\lambda)
as the number of nonzero
elements of
\lambda
.

For each
\lambda\vdash_{d}n
, let

(\mathcal{P}_{\lambda},p_{\lambda})
denote the irreducible
S_{n}
-representation
indexed by
\lambda
, where
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}
is the Specht module. Let

(\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda},q_{\lambda})
denote the irreducible polynomial

\mathrm{GL}(d)
-representation
5
5

5

Alternatively,
U(d)
representation.
of highest weight
\lambda
, where

\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}
is the Schur module. Schur–Weyl duality decomposes the
tensor space as

\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{\lambda\vdash_{d}n}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}\otimes\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}.

After choosing orthonormal bases of
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}
, this
decomposition is implemented by a unitary change of coordinates, the

Schur transform
,

U_{\mathrm{Schur}}:(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{\lambda\vdash_{d}n}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}\otimes\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}.

(13)

The corresponding orthonormal basis of
\bigoplus_{\lambda\vdash_{d}n}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}\otimes\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}
is called a

Schur basis
.

The symmetric group
S_{n}
acts on
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}
by permuting tensor factors:

P(\pi)|i_{1}\cdots i_{n}\rangle:=|i_{\pi^{-1}(1)}\cdots i_{\pi^{-1}(n)}\rangle,\qquad\pi\in S_{n}.

(14)

For an
X\in\mathrm{GL}(d)
,
X^{\otimes n}
defines an action on on
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}
.
These two
actions commute. In the Schur basis, we have

U_{\mathrm{Schur}}\bigl(P(\pi)X^{\otimes n}\bigr)U_{\mathrm{Schur}}^{\dagger}=\bigoplus_{\lambda\vdash_{d}n}p_{\lambda}(\pi)\otimes q_{\lambda}(X).

(15)

If one represents the direct sum using an explicit block-label register, then
(
15
) may equivalently be written as

U_{\mathrm{Schur}}\bigl(P(\pi)X^{\otimes n}\bigr)U_{\mathrm{Schur}}^{\dagger}=\sum_{\lambda\vdash_{d}n}|\lambda\rangle\langle\lambda|\otimes p_{\lambda}(\pi)\otimes q_{\lambda}(X).

Since
q_{\lambda}
is a polynomial representation, it extends uniquely from

\mathrm{GL}(d)
to all
X\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
. Via Schur’s lemma, if
X^{n}\in\mathcal{L}((\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes n})
is permutation invariant, i.e.,

P(\pi)X^{n}P(\pi)^{\dagger}=X^{n},\quad\forall\pi\in S_{n},

then for any
\lambda\vdash_{d}n
, there exists
X^{n}_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda})
such that

U_{\mathrm{Schur}}X^{n}\,U_{\mathrm{Schur}}^{\dagger}=\sum_{\lambda\vdash_{d}n}|\lambda\rangle\langle\lambda|\otimes\textbf{1}_{\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}}\otimes X^{n}_{\lambda}.

(16)

2.2
Weights of representations of general linear groups

For
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{d}
with
|\alpha|=n
, we use the conventions
0^{0}=1
and
0^{m}=0
for
m>0
and denote the monomial

t^{\alpha}:=\prod_{k=1}^{d}t_{k}^{\alpha_{k}},\quad t\in\mathbb{C}^{d}.

Define the
\alpha
-weight space of
\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}
by

\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda,\alpha}:=\left\{v\in\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}:q_{\lambda}(\operatorname{diag}(t))v=t^{\alpha}v\ \text{for all }t\in(\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\})^{d}\right\}.

(17)

Equivalently, it is enough to test this identity on the compact diagonal torus

\mathrm{T}(d):=\{\operatorname{diag}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}):|z_{i}|=1\}.

The weight-space decomposition is

\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\mathrm{Wt}_{d}(\lambda)}\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda,\alpha},

where the weights of a Young diagram
\lambda
are given by

\mathrm{Wt}_{d}(\lambda):=\{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{d}:|\alpha|=n,\ \mathcal{Q}_{\lambda,\alpha}\neq 0\}=\{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{d}:|\alpha|=n,\ \alpha\prec\lambda\}.

The multiplicity of the weight
\alpha
is
\dim\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda,\alpha}
, and is known as a
Kostka number
.

Let
P_{\lambda,\alpha}
denote the orthogonal projection from

\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}
onto
\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda,\alpha}
. The following well-known facts will be used repeatedly:

1.

Diagonal action.

For
\operatorname{diag}(t)=\operatorname{diag}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{d})
with
t_{i}>0
,

q_{\lambda}(\operatorname{diag}(t))=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathrm{Wt}_{d}(\lambda)}t^{\alpha}P_{\lambda,\alpha}.

(18)

Since
q_{\lambda}
is polynomial, the same identity extends by continuity
to
t_{i}\geq 0
.

2.

Fourier formula for weight projections.

For
\alpha\in\mathrm{Wt}_{d}(\lambda)
, and
U(\theta)=\operatorname{diag}(e^{i\theta_{1}},\ldots,e^{i\theta_{d}})
,

P_{\lambda,\alpha}=\int_{[0,2\pi]^{d}}e^{-i\alpha\cdot\theta}q_{\lambda}(U(\theta))\frac{d\theta}{(2\pi)^{d}}.

(19)

If
\alpha\notin\mathrm{Wt}_{d}(\lambda)
, the same integral is
0
.

3.

Torus twirling kills off-diagonal weight blocks.

For
K_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda})
, and
U(\theta)=\operatorname{diag}(e^{i\theta_{1}},\ldots,e^{i\theta_{d}})
,

\int_{[0,2\pi]^{d}}q_{\lambda}(U(\theta))K_{\lambda}\,q_{\lambda}(U(\theta))^{\dagger}\frac{d\theta}{(2\pi)^{d}}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathrm{Wt}_{d}(\lambda)}P_{\lambda,\alpha}K_{\lambda}P_{\lambda,\alpha}.

(20)

4.

Upper triangular matrices can only increase weights.

Let
L
be an upper triangular matrix, then

q_{\lambda}(L)\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda,\alpha}\subseteq\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\in\mathrm{Wt}_{d}(\lambda)\\
\alpha\prec\beta\end{subarray}}\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda,\beta}.

(21)

To see the direction of the order, note that the elementary matrix
|i\rangle\langle j|,\ i<j
changes a weight
\alpha
to
\alpha+e_{i}-e_{j}
, which is larger in dominance order. Upper triangular matrices are generated by the diagonal torus and such raising operations.

We write
\mu_{\mathrm{Haar}}
for the normalized Haar probability measure on the unitary group
\mathrm{U}(d)
, namely the unique Borel probability measure satisfying
\mu_{\mathrm{Haar}}(VU)=\mu_{\mathrm{Haar}}(U)=\mu_{\mathrm{Haar}}(UV)
for fixed
U\in\mathrm{U}(d)
and all
V\in\mathrm{U}(d)
. The following property will be used repeatedly.

Lemma 2.1

(Schur orthogonality)
.

Fix
\lambda\vdash_{d}n
. For every
K_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda})
,

\int_{\mathcal{U}(d)}q_{\lambda}(U)\,K_{\lambda}\,q_{\lambda}(U)^{\dagger}\,d\mu_{\mathrm{Haar}}(U)=\frac{\Tr(K_\lambda)}{\dim\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}}\,\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}}.

(22)

2.3
Schur polynomials and concentration of Young diagrams

Since
q_{\lambda}
is a polynomial representation, the map

X\mapsto q_{\lambda}(X)
extends from
\mathrm{GL}(d)
to all

X\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
. We define the Schur polynomial, or Schur character,
by

s_{\lambda}(X):=\Tr q_{\lambda}(X).

(23)

When
X\geq 0
has eigenvalues
r=(r_{1},\cdots,r_{d})
with
r_{1}\geq r_{2}\geq\cdots\geq r_{d}\geq 0
, we also write

s_{\lambda}(\operatorname{diag}(r))=s_{\lambda}(X).

We will use the following standard estimates repeatedly.

Lemma 2.2

(Representation-theoretic estimates)
.

For
\lambda\vdash_{d}n
, let
\bar{\lambda}:=\lambda/n
. Then

\dim\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}\leq(n+1)^{d(d-1)/2},

(24)

and denote the entropy
H(p):=-\sum_{i=1}^{d}p_{i}\log p_{i},\ p\in\Delta_{d}
, we have

(n+d)^{-d(d+1)/2}e^{nH(\bar{\lambda})}\leq\dim\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}\leq e^{nH(\bar{\lambda})}.

(25)

Moreover, if
X\geq 0
has eigenvalues

r=(r_{1},\cdots,r_{d})
with
r_{1}\geq r_{2}\geq\cdots\geq r_{d}\geq 0
, then

r^{\lambda}\leq s_{\lambda}(X)\leq\dim\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}\,r^{\lambda}.

(26)

Let
\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{d}
. In Schur basis, we have

U_{\mathrm{Schur}}\rho^{\otimes n}\,U_{\mathrm{Schur}}^{\dagger}=\sum_{\lambda\vdash_{d}n}|\lambda\rangle\langle\lambda|\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}}\otimes q_{\lambda}(\rho).

Denote the projection onto the
\lambda
-block as

\Pi_{\lambda}=|\lambda\rangle\langle\lambda|\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}}.

Performing the measurements
\{\widetilde{\Pi}_{\lambda}=U_{\mathrm{Schur}}^{\dagger}\Pi_{\lambda}\,U_{\mathrm{Schur}}\}_{\lambda\vdash_{d}n}
defines a probability distribution on

\Lambda_{d,n}
:

\mathbb{P}_{n,\rho}(\lambda):=\Tr(\wt\Pi_\lambda\rho^{\otimes n})=\dim\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}\,s_{\lambda}(\rho).

(27)

For
p,x\in\Delta_{d}
, define the relative entropy

D(p\|x):=\sum_{i=1}^{d}p_{i}\log\frac{p_{i}}{x_{i}}
, with the convention that
D(p\|x)=+\infty
if

p_{i}>0
for some
i
with
x_{i}=0
. A well-known Keyl–Werner spectrum-estimation bound
KW 01
;
HM 02
, see also
(
CM 06
, Theorem 1 and Corollary 1)
, is given by

\mathbb{P}_{n,\rho}(\lambda)\leq(n+1)^{d(d-1)/2}\exp\!\left(-nD\!\left(\overline{\lambda}\|x\right)\right).

(28)

As an application, we have the following concentration property:

Lemma 2.3
.

For any state
\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{d}
with
x=\mathrm{spec}^{\downarrow}(\rho)
and
\delta>0
, denote

G_{n,\delta}(x):=\{\lambda:\|\lambda/n-x\|_{1}<\delta,\ \ell(\lambda)\leq\rank(x)\}.

(29)

Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{n,\rho}(G_{n,\delta}(x))=1.

(30)

3
General framework for quantum state tomography

Let
\mathcal{H}_{n}=(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}
be the
n
-fold tensor product Hilbert space, equipped with its computational basis. Given
n
copies of an unknown state
\rho
, a tomography protocol produces an estimator of
\rho
. Thus a tomography protocol is naturally a POVM whose outcome space is the state space
\mathcal{D}_{d}
.

To allow estimators with a continuum of possible values, we use Borel subsets of
\mathcal{D}_{d}
, denoted as
\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{D}_{d})
.

Definition 3.1
.

For any
n\geq 1
, a quantum state tomography protocol with output space
\mathcal{D}_{d}
, is a
\sigma
-additive positive-operator-valued measure

E_{n}:\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{D}_{d})\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{n})_{+},\quad E_{n}(\mathcal{D}_{d})=\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{H}_{n}},\quad E_{n}(\emptyset)=0.

(31)

Given a protocol
\{E_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
and
n
copies of an unknown state

\rho
, the distribution of the estimator is determined by the Born rule:

\widehat{\mu}^{\rho}_{n}(A):=\Tr\bigl(E_{n}(A)\rho^{\otimes n}\bigr).

(32)

It is often convenient to write the POVM
E_{n}
in a density form. Since
\mathcal{H}_{n}
is finite-dimensional, every such POVM admits a density with respect to some probability measure on
\mathcal{D}_{d}
. Indeed, one may
take

\mu_{n}(A):=\frac{1}{\dim\mathcal{H}_{n}}\Tr E_{n}(A).

(33)

Now, if
\mu_{n}(A)=0
then
E_{n}(A)=0
. Hence
E_{n}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mu_{n}
.
Applying the scalar Radon–Nikodym theorem to the matrix entries of
E_{n}

gives a measurable positive-operator-valued density

\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{d}\longmapsto M_{\sigma}^{(n)}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{n})_{+}

such that

E_{n}(A)=\int_{A}M_{\sigma}^{(n)}\,d\mu_{n}(\sigma),\qquad\int_{\mathcal{D}_{d}}M_{\sigma}^{(n)}\,d\mu_{n}(\sigma)=\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}.

We call
M_{\sigma}^{(n)}
a POVM
density
. With this representation, the probability density of obtaining the estimate
\sigma
, when the true state is
\rho
, is

P_{n}^{M}(\sigma|\rho):=\Tr\bigl(M_{\sigma}^{(n)}\rho^{\otimes n}\bigr),

and the probability that the estimator belongs to a subset
A\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{d}

is

\widehat{\mu}^{\rho}_{n}(A)=\int_{A}P_{n}^{M}(\sigma|\rho)\,d\mu_{n}(\sigma).

What this means is that, rather than starting from Definition
3.1
, we can alternatively characterize a general quantum state tomography protocol as a sequence of measurable families of positive operators

\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{d}\longmapsto M_{\sigma}^{(n)}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{n})_{+}

equipped with a sequence of probability measures
\mu_{n}
on
\mathcal{D}_{d}
, and subject to the usual constraint

\int_{\mathcal{D}_{d}}M_{\sigma}^{(n)}\,d\mu_{n}(\sigma)=\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{H}_{n}}
.
We denote such a tomography protocol as

\mathfrak{T}_{\mu,M}=\left(\{M_{\sigma}^{(n)}\}_{\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{d}},\mu_{n}\right)_{n\geq 1}.

(34)

This representation is not unique: different choices of
\mu_{n}
and
M_{\sigma}^{(n)}
may define the same POVM
E_{n}
. Moreover, different POVMs may induce the same distribution of estimators. We say two POVMs
\{E_{n}\}_{n\geq 1},\ \{E^{\prime}_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
are
i.i.d. statistically equivalent
if, for any
\rho
and
n\geq 1
, we have

\Tr(E_n(A) \rho^{\otimes n})=\Tr(E'_n(A) \rho^{\otimes n}),\quad\forall A\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{D}_{d}).

Covariant protocols

We will focus our attention on
covariant
protocols. Intuitively, a tomography protocol is covariant if rotating the true state by a unitary
U
simply rotates the output estimate
by the same unitary. This will provide substantially more structure on the space of tomography protocols.

Definition 3.2
.

We say that a quantum state tomography protocol
\{E_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is
covariant
, if for any
A\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{D}_{d})
,
U\in\mathrm{U}(d)
,

E_{n}(UAU^{\dagger})=U^{\otimes n}E_{n}(A)(U^{\dagger})^{\otimes n},\quad UAU^{\dagger}:=\{U\sigma U^{\dagger}:\sigma\in A\}.

If
E_{n}
has a density representation

E_{n}(A)=\int_{A}M_{\sigma}^{(n)}\,d\mu_{n}(\sigma),

then covariance is equivalently expressed as

\int_{UAU^{\dagger}}M_{\sigma}^{(n)}\,d\mu_{n}(\sigma)=\int_{A}U^{\otimes n}M_{\sigma}^{(n)}(U^{\dagger})^{\otimes n}\,d\mu_{n}(\sigma).

(35)

In general, the density
M_{\sigma}^{(n)}
itself need not satisfy a pointwise
covariance relation. The next lemma shows that, for covariant protocols, one
can choose a density realization for which such a relation holds.

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose the protocol
\{E_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is covariant, then there exist unitarily invariant probability measures
\mu_{n}
on
\mathcal{D}_{d}
and measurable familty
\{M_{\sigma}^{(n)}\}_{\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{d}}
with

E_{n}(A)=\int_{A}M_{\sigma}^{(n)}d\mu_{n}(\sigma),\quad\forall A\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{D}_{d}),

and
\mu_{n}
-almost surely,

M_{U\sigma U^{\dagger}}^{(n)}=U^{\otimes n}M_{\sigma}^{(n)}(U^{\dagger})^{\otimes n},\quad\forall U\in\mathrm{U}(d).

Proof.

Since
E_{n}
is covariant, we have that

\mu_{n}(\cdot):=\frac{1}{d^{n}}\Tr(E_n(\cdot))

is unitarily invariant. We denote
\widetilde{M}_{\sigma}^{(n)}
as the POVM density with respect to
\mu_{n}
, i.e.,

E_{n}(A)=\int_{A}\widetilde{M}_{\sigma}^{(n)}d\mu_{n}(\sigma).

Define

\displaystyle M_{\sigma}^{(n)}:=\int_{\mathrm{U}(d)}(W^{\dagger})^{\otimes n}\widetilde{M}_{W\sigma W^{\dagger}}^{(n)}W^{\otimes n}d\mu_{\mathrm{Haar}}(W).

Via the left invariance of the Haar measure, we have,
\mu_{n}
-almost surely, that
M_{U\sigma U^{\dagger}}^{(n)}=U^{\otimes n}M_{\sigma}^{(n)}(U^{\dagger})^{\otimes n}
for all
U\in\mathrm{U}(d)
and

\displaystyle\int_{A}M_{\sigma}^{(n)}d\mu_{n}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\int_{A}\int_{\mathrm{U}(d)}(W^{\dagger})^{\otimes n}\widetilde{M}_{W\sigma W^{\dagger}}^{(n)}W^{\otimes n}d\mu_{\mathrm{Haar}}(W)d\mu_{n}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathrm{U}(d)}(W^{\dagger})^{\otimes n}\int_{A}\widetilde{M}_{W\sigma W^{\dagger}}^{(n)}d\mu_{n}(\sigma)W^{\otimes n}d\mu_{\mathrm{Haar}}(W)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathrm{U}(d)}(W^{\dagger})^{\otimes n}E_{n}(WAW^{\dagger})W^{\otimes n}d\mu_{\mathrm{Haar}}(W)

\displaystyle=E_{n}(A),

where the first equality is the definition of
M_{\sigma}^{(n)}
; the second equality follows from exchanging the integral; the third equality uses unitary invariance of
\mu_{n}
and the last equality follows from the covariance of
E_{n}
. Thus
M_{\sigma}^{(n)}
is also a POVM density for
E_{n}
, concluding the proof.
∎

Recall that two protocols are i.i.d. statistically equivalent if
they induce the same distribution of the estimators for every input of the form
\rho^{\otimes n}
. A simple observation is that every protocol is statistically equivalent to a permutation invariant protocol. Thus we have the following characterization:

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose the protocol
\{E_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is covariant, then there exist unitarily invariant probability measures
\mu_{n}
on
\mathcal{D}_{d}
and measurable familty
\{M_{\sigma}^{(n)}\}_{\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{d}}
such that
\mu_{n}
-almost surely,

P_{\pi}M_{\sigma}^{(n)}=M_{\sigma}^{(n)}P_{\pi},\quad M_{U\sigma U^{\dagger}}^{(n)}=U^{\otimes n}M_{\sigma}^{(n)}(U^{\dagger})^{\otimes n},\quad\forall U\in\mathrm{U}(d),\ \pi\in S_{n},

and for any
A\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{D}_{d})
and
\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{d}
,

\Tr(E_n(A) \rho^{\otimes n})=\int_{A}\Tr(M_\sigma^{(n)} \rho^{\otimes n})d\mu_{n}(\sigma).

(36)

Proof.

Using Lemma
3.3
, there exist unitarily invariant
\mu_{n}
and
\{\widetilde{M}_{\sigma}^{(n)}\}_{\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{d}}
such that
\mu_{n}
-almost surely,

\widetilde{M}_{U\sigma U^{\dagger}}^{(n)}=U^{\otimes n}\widetilde{M}_{\sigma}^{(n)}(U^{\dagger})^{\otimes n},\quad\forall U\in\mathrm{U}(d).

Now define

M_{\sigma}^{(n)}=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}P_{\pi}\widetilde{M}_{\sigma}^{(n)}P_{\pi}^{\dagger}.

Using the fact that
P(\pi)
and
U^{\otimes n}
commute, we have
\mu_{n}
-almost surely,

P_{\pi}M_{\sigma}^{(n)}=M_{\sigma}^{(n)}P_{\pi},\quad M_{U\sigma U^{\dagger}}^{(n)}=U^{\otimes n}M_{\sigma}^{(n)}(U^{\dagger})^{\otimes n},\quad\forall U\in\mathrm{U}(d),\ \pi\in S_{n}.

Moreover, since
\rho^{\otimes n}
is permutation invariant, we have
\Tr(E_n(A) \rho^{\otimes n})=\int_{A}\Tr(M_\sigma^{(n)} \rho^{\otimes n})d\mu_{n}(\sigma)
, concluding the proof.
∎

We next record the form of a unitarily invariant probability measure
\mu_{n}
on
\mathcal{D}_{d}
. Let

s:\mathcal{D}_{d}\to\Delta_{d}^{\downarrow},\ s(\sigma):=\mathrm{spec}^{\downarrow}(\sigma)

be the spectrum map,
\nu_{n}
is defined as the probability measure on
\Delta_{d}^{\downarrow}
:

\nu_{n}(\Sigma)=\mu_{n}(s^{-1}(\Sigma)),\quad\forall\Sigma\subseteq\Delta_{d}^{\downarrow}.

(37)

Then
\mu_{n}
can be reconstructed by first sampling the spectrum

x\sim\nu_{n}
, then sampling
U\sim\mu_{\mathrm{Haar}}
, and finally setting

\sigma=U\operatorname{diag}(x)U^{\dagger}
.
Equivalently, for every measurable integrable function
F:\mathcal{D}_{d}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\int_{\mathcal{D}_{d}}F(\sigma)\,d\mu_{n}(\sigma)=\int_{\Delta^{\downarrow}_{d}}\int_{\mathrm{U}(d)}F(U\operatorname{diag}(x)U^{\dagger})\,d\mu_{\mathrm{Haar}}(U)\,d\nu_{n}(x).

(38)

Finally, we present a characterization of covariant protocols up to statistical equivalence:

Theorem 3.5
.

Suppose the protocol
\{E_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is covariant, then there exist unitarily invariant probability measures
\mu_{n}
on
\mathcal{D}_{d}
with decomposition (
38
) and POVM density
\{M_{\sigma}^{(n)}\}_{\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{d}}
of the form

M_{U\operatorname{diag}(x)U^{\dagger}}^{(n)}=U_{\mathrm{Schur}}^{\dagger}\left(\sum_{\lambda\vdash_{d}n}\dim\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}|\lambda\rangle\langle\lambda|\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}}\otimes q_{\lambda}(U)K_{\lambda,x}q_{\lambda}(U^{\dagger})\right)U_{\mathrm{Schur}},

(39)

such that for any
A\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{D}_{d})
and
\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{d}
, we have

\Tr(E_n(A) \rho^{\otimes n})=\int_{A}\Tr(M_\sigma^{(n)} \rho^{\otimes n})d\mu_{n}(\sigma).

For each
\lambda\vdash_{d}n
and
x\in\Delta_{d}^{\downarrow}
, we have
K_{\lambda,x}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda})_{+}
and

\int_{\Delta_{d}^{\downarrow}}\Tr K_{\lambda,x}d\nu_{n}(x)=1,\quad\forall\lambda\vdash_{d}n,

(40)

where
\nu_{n}
is defined by (
37
).

Proof.

By Lemma
3.4
, we may replace
E_{n}
,
without changing its statistics on i.i.d. inputs, by a protocol with a
density
M_{\sigma}^{(n)}
satisfying both permutation invariance and
unitary covariance:

P_{\pi}M_{\sigma}^{(n)}=M_{\sigma}^{(n)}P_{\pi},\quad M_{U\sigma U^{\dagger}}^{(n)}=U^{\otimes n}M_{\sigma}^{(n)}(U^{\dagger})^{\otimes n},\quad\forall U\in\mathrm{U}(d),\ \pi\in S_{n},

Recall that if
\{M_{\sigma}^{(n)}\}_{\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{d}}
is permutation invariant, it can be characterized as (
16
)

M_{\sigma}^{(n)}=U_{\mathrm{Schur}}^{\dagger}\left(\sum_{\lambda\vdash_{d}n}|\lambda\rangle\langle\lambda|\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}}\otimes K_{\lambda}(\sigma)\right)U_{\mathrm{Schur}},

where
\{K_{\lambda}(\sigma)\}_{\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{d}}
is a measurable family acting on
\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}
for each
\lambda\vdash_{d}n
.

The covariance of
M_{\sigma}^{(n)}
, together with the Schur–Weyl action
of
U^{\otimes n}
, gives

K_{\lambda}(U\sigma U^{\dagger})=q_{\lambda}(U)K_{\lambda}(\sigma)q_{\lambda}(U^{\dagger}).

(41)

Define

K_{\lambda,x}:=\frac{1}{\dim\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}}\,K_{\lambda}(\operatorname{diag}(x)),\quad\lambda\vdash_{d}n,\ x\in\Delta_{d}^{\downarrow}.

Then, for
\sigma=U\operatorname{diag}(x)U^{\dagger}
,

K_{\lambda}(\sigma)=\dim\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}q_{\lambda}(U)K_{\lambda,x}q_{\lambda}(U^{\dagger}).

(42)

Consequently,

\displaystyle M_{U\operatorname{diag}(x)U^{\dagger}}^{(n)}=U_{\mathrm{Schur}}^{\dagger}\left(\sum_{\lambda\vdash_{d}n}\dim\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}|\lambda\rangle\langle\lambda|\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}}\otimes q_{\lambda}(U)K_{\lambda,x}q_{\lambda}(U^{\dagger})\right)U_{\mathrm{Schur}}.

(43)

It remains to identify the normalization condition. Indeed, comparing the
\lambda
-blocks in Schur basis gives

\int_{\mathcal{D}_{d}}K_{\lambda}(\sigma)d\mu_{n}(\sigma)=\dim\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}\int_{\Delta_{d}^{\downarrow}}\int_{\mathrm{U}(d)}q_{\lambda}(U)K_{\lambda,x}\,q_{\lambda}(U^{\dagger})\,d\mu_{\mathrm{Haar}}(U)\,d\nu_{n}(x)=\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}},\qquad\forall\lambda\vdash_{d}n.

(44)

Equivalently, by Schur orthogonality, see Lemma
2.1
, we have

\int_{\Delta_{d}^{\downarrow}}\Tr K_{\lambda,x}d\nu_{n}(x)=1,\qquad\forall\lambda\vdash_{d}n.

(45)

∎

In summary, a general covariant tomography protocol is i.i.d. statistically equivalent to the following protocol:

Given
n
-copies of
\rho
:

1.

First apply the Schur transform to
\rho^{\otimes n}
.

2.

In Schur basis, perform the POVM of the form

\sum_{\lambda\vdash_{d}n}\dim\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}|\lambda\rangle\langle\lambda|\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}}\otimes q_{\lambda}(U)K_{\lambda,x}q_{\lambda}(U^{\dagger})

where
x\sim\nu_{n}
,
U\sim\mu_{\mathrm{Haar}}
and
\int_{\Delta_{d}^{\downarrow}}\Tr K_{\lambda,x}d\nu_{n}(x)=1
.

3.

Collect the measurement outcomes
\widehat{x}
and
\widehat{U}
, and output an estimator

\sigma=\widehat{U}\operatorname{diag}(\widehat{x})\widehat{U}^{\dagger}.

4
Large deviation principle and Keyl’s conjecture

In this section, we formally introduce the notion of large deviation principle, and review the conjecture proposed in
Key 06
. First, we introduce the notion of
consistency
. Recall that any quantum state tomography protocol induces the estimator distribution

\widehat{\mu}^{\rho}_{n}(A)=\Tr(E_n(A) \rho^{\otimes n})=\int_{A}\Tr(M_\sigma^{(n)} \rho^{\otimes n})d\mu_{n}(\sigma).

(46)

A good tomography protocol should concentrate its estimator near the true state. This motivates the following notion:

Definition 4.1
.

We say that the protocol
\left(\{M_{\sigma}^{(n)}\}_{\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{d}},\mu_{n}\right)_{n\geq 1}
is consistent, if for any
\varepsilon>0
and
\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{d}
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\widehat{\mu}^{\rho}_{n}\left(N_{\varepsilon}(\rho)\right)=1,\quad N_{\varepsilon}(\rho):=\{\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{d}:\|\tau-\rho\|_{1}<\varepsilon\},

(47)

where
\widehat{\mu}^{\rho}_{n}(\cdot)
is defined by (
46
).

In other words, consistency is the ordinary probabilistic requirement that the estimator converges in probability to the true density matrix, which is equivalent to finiteness of sample complexity.

The following formal definition from
(
Hol 00
, Definition III.6)
characterizes the exponential rate at which the protocol assigns probability to a particular estimate
\sigma
of the true state
\rho
, informally introduced in (
2
).

Definition 4.2
.

We say that the protocol
\left(\{M_{\sigma}^{(n)}\}_{\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{d}},\mu_{n}\right)_{n\geq 1}
satisfies a large deviation principle (LDP) with rate function
I(\cdot\|\rho):\mathcal{D}_{d}\to[0,\infty]
for any
\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{d}
, if
I(\cdot\|\rho)
satisfies

1.

I(\cdot\|\rho)\not\equiv\infty
;

2.

I(\cdot\|\rho)
is lower semicontinuous: for any sequence
\sigma_{n}\to\sigma

\liminf_{n\to\infty}I(\sigma_{n}\|\rho)\geq I(\sigma\|\rho)
;

3.

\{\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{d}:I(\sigma\|\rho)\in[0,c]\}
is compact for any
c\geq 0
,

and we have

•

for any open set
O\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{d}
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\widehat{\mu}^{\rho}_{n}(O)\leq\inf_{\sigma\in O}I(\sigma\|\rho).

•

For any closed set
F\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{d}
,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\widehat{\mu}^{\rho}_{n}(F)\geq\inf_{\sigma\in F}I(\sigma\|\rho).

We emphasize that our notion of consistency is purely probabilistic: the estimator distribution must converge to a point mass at the true state. This is weaker than requiring the LDP rate function to vanish only at the true state.
Indeed, in
(
Key 06
, Definition 2.2)
, Keyl includes the following unique-zero condition in the definition of
an LDP rate function for an estimation scheme:

I(\sigma\|\rho)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\sigma=\rho.

(48)

We show that this condition, together with an LDP, implies consistency:

Proposition 4.3
.

Suppose that the protocol satisfies an LDP with rate function

I(\cdot\|\rho)
. Assume that, for every
\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{d}
,

I(\sigma\|\rho)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\sigma=\rho.

Then the protocol is consistent.

Proof.

Fix
\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{d}
and
\varepsilon>0
. Let

F_{\varepsilon}(\rho):=\{\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{d}:\|\sigma-\rho\|_{1}\geq\varepsilon\}.

This set is compact and does not contain
\rho
. Since

I(\cdot\|\rho)
is lower semicontinuous, it attains its minimum on

F_{\varepsilon}(\rho)
. By the unique-zero assumption, this minimum cannot be
zero. Hence

\inf_{\sigma\in F_{\varepsilon}(\rho)}I(\sigma\|\rho)>0.

The closed-set LDP bound gives

\liminf_{n\to\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\widehat{\mu}_{n}^{\rho}\bigl(F_{\varepsilon}(\rho)\bigr)\geq\inf_{\sigma\in F_{\varepsilon}(\rho)}I(\sigma\|\rho)>0

Therefore, for sufficiently large
n
,

\widehat{\mu}_{n}^{\rho}\bigl(F_{\varepsilon}(\rho)\bigr)\leq\exp(-\frac{n}{2}\inf_{\sigma\in F_\varepsilon(\rho)}
I(\sigma\|\rho))\longrightarrow 0,

which is exactly consistency in Definition
4.1
.
∎

In fact, the consistency condition defined by Definition
4.1
is strictly weaker than the unique-zero property (
48
). To see this, suppose
E_{n}
is a protocol with rate function satisfying the unique-zero property. Then for a fixed
\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{d}
, define

\widetilde{E}_{n}(A):=\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)E_{n}(A)+\frac{1}{n}\,\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau\in A\}}\,\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{H}_{n}},\qquad A\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{D}_{d}).

One can verify that
\widetilde{E}_{n}
is consistent, but the rate function always admits
\tau
as a zero, breaking the unique-zero property. Indeed, the unique-zero condition rules out
subexponentially small probabilities of reporting wrong states, while ordinary consistency does not; nevertheless, our main theorem shows that Keyl’s covariant exponent remains optimal under this weaker consistency requirement.

Definition 4.4

(Tomographic rate functions)
.

A function

I:\mathcal{D}_{d}\times\mathcal{D}_{d}\to[0,\infty]

is called a tomographic rate function if there exists a consistent tomography
protocol such that, for every true state

\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{d}
, the estimator laws

(\widehat{\mu}_{n}^{\rho})_{n\geq 1}
satisfy a LDP with rate function

I(\cdot\|\rho)
.

We denote

•

\mathcal{E}
as the set of all tomographic rate functions.

•

\widehat{\mathcal{E}}
as the set of all tomographic rate functions satisfying unique-zero property (
48
).

•

\mathcal{E}^{0}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
as the subset consisting of those
I\in\mathcal{E}
such that, for every
\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{d}
, the map
\rho\mapsto I(\sigma\|\rho)
is lower semicontinuous.

•

\mathcal{E}^{c}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
as the subset consisting of those rate functions arising from covariant protocols.

Conjecture 4.5

(Keyl’s conjecture)
.

Fix any
\sigma,\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{d}
, define

\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{Id}}^{c}(\sigma\|\rho)=\sup_{I\in\mathcal{E}^{c}}I(\sigma\|\rho),\quad\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{Id}}^{0}(\sigma\|\rho)=\sup_{I\in\mathcal{E}^{0}}I(\sigma\|\rho),\quad\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{Id}}(\sigma\|\rho)=\sup_{I\in\widehat{\mathcal{E}}}I(\sigma\|\rho).

(49)

Then

S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}(\sigma\|\rho)=\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{Id}}^{c}(\sigma\|\rho)=\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{Id}}^{0}(\sigma\|\rho)\leq\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{Id}}(\sigma\|\rho)=D(\sigma\|\rho),

(50)

where
D(\sigma\|\rho)
is the relative entropy, and
S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}(\sigma\|\rho)
is defined in (
6
).

We explain more details about
S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}(\sigma\|\rho)
. For any
x\in\Delta_{d}^{\downarrow},\ \tau\in\mathcal{D}_{d}
, let
\Delta_{0}(\tau):=1
and let
\Delta_{k}(\tau)
be the determinant of the
k\times k
principal minor of
\tau
, defined in (
10
). Set

\Theta_{k}(\tau):=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\frac{\Delta_{k}(\tau+\varepsilon\textbf{1})}{\Delta_{k-1}(\tau+\varepsilon\textbf{1})},\quad 1\leq k\leq d,

(51)

We introduce the following function

L_{\mathrm{Keyl}}(x,\tau)=-\sum_{k=1}^{d}x_{k}\log\Theta_{k}(\tau),

(52)

with the convention
0\log 0=0
and
-u\log 0=\infty
for
u>0
.

Then by Abel’s summation formula, we also have

S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}(\sigma\|\rho)=-H(x)+L_{\mathrm{Keyl}}(x,U^{\dagger}\rho U),\quad\sigma=U\operatorname{diag}(x)U^{\dagger}.

Note that the definition of
S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}(\sigma\|\rho)
does not depend on the choice of
U
with
\sigma=U\operatorname{diag}(x)U^{\dagger},x\in\Delta_{d}^{\downarrow}
, see
(
Key 06
, Lemma 4.15)
.

For commuting states,
S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}(\sigma\|\rho)=D(\sigma\|\rho)
. Furthermore, it was shown that
S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}(\sigma\|\rho)<D(\sigma\|\rho)
for some noncommuting states
\sigma,\rho
, see the discussions after Theorem 3.3 in
Key 06
. Here we show a strengthened result:

Proposition 4.6

(Comparison with relative entropy)
.

For all
\sigma,\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{d}
,

S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}(\sigma\|\rho)\leq D(\sigma\|\rho)

as an inequality in
[0,\infty]
. Moreover,

1.

If
D(\sigma\|\rho)<\infty
, then

S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}(\sigma\|\rho)=D(\sigma\|\rho)\iff[\sigma,\rho]=0.

2.

If
D(\sigma\|\rho)=+\infty
, then

S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}(\sigma\|\rho)=D(\sigma\|\rho)=+\infty\iff\supp(\sigma)\cap\ker(\rho)\neq\{0\}.

A proof of this result, together with some additional properties of
S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}
are presented in Appendix
A
.

5
Non-covariant protocol achieving relative entropy as rate function

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.1
thus the pointwise relative entropy envelope predicted in the last equality in Conjecture
4.5
.

Our contribution is to show that for every pair

(\sigma,\rho)
and every
a<D(\sigma\|\rho)
, one can construct a non-covariant tomography protocol whose rate at
(\sigma,\rho)
is larger than
a
and has unique-zero property.

Recall that every tomographic LDP rate with unique-zero property (
48
) is bounded above by the quantum relative entropy, which is proved in
(
Key 06
, Theorem 3.3)
. We present a proof under the consistency condition only, slightly weakening the assumption:

Proposition 5.1

(Converse bound)
.

Let
I\in\mathcal{E}
be the rate function of any consistent tomography
protocol. Then for all
\sigma,\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{d}
,

I(\sigma\|\rho)\leq D(\sigma\|\rho).

Proof.

If
D(\sigma\|\rho)=+\infty
, the claim is trivial. Assume

D(\sigma\|\rho)<+\infty
. For
\varepsilon>0
, set

F_{\varepsilon}:=\{\tau:\|\tau-\sigma\|_{1}\leq\varepsilon\}.

By consistency under the true state
\sigma
,

\Tr(E_n(F_\varepsilon)\sigma^{\otimes n})\longrightarrow 1.

Thus
E_{n}(F_{\varepsilon})
is a sequence of tests whose type-I success
probability under
\sigma^{\otimes n}
tends to one. By the converse part of quantum Stein’s lemma
HP 91
;
ON 00
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\Tr(E_n(F_\varepsilon)\rho^{\otimes n})\leq D(\sigma\|\rho).

On the other hand, applying the closed-set LDP bound to
F_{\varepsilon}

under the true state
\rho
gives

\liminf_{n\to\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\Tr(E_n(F_\varepsilon)\rho^{\otimes n})\geq\inf_{\tau\in F_{\varepsilon}}I(\tau\|\rho).

Therefore
\inf_{\tau\in F_{\varepsilon}}I(\tau\|\rho)\leq D(\sigma\|\rho)
. Since
I(\cdot\|\rho)
is lower semicontinuous,

I(\sigma\|\rho)=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\inf_{\tau\in F_{\varepsilon}}I(\tau\|\rho)\leq D(\sigma\|\rho).

∎

We conclude the lower bound by proving a more general result, which embeds any Stein exponent into a rate function of a tomography protocol:

Theorem 5.2

(Embedding Stein exponents into tomography)
.

Let
\sigma_{0},\rho_{0}\in\mathcal{D}_{d}
. Suppose that

s\in[0,\infty]
is achievable by a sequence of binary tests
\{M_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
:

\Tr(M_n\sigma_0^{\otimes n})\longrightarrow 1,\quad\liminf_{n\to\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\Tr(M_n\rho_0^{\otimes n})\geq s.

(53)

Then, for every finite
a<s
, there exists a consistent tomography protocol satisfying an LDP with rate function
I_{a}
such that
I_{a}(\sigma\|\rho)=0\iff\sigma=\rho
and

I_{a}(\sigma_{0}\|\rho_{0})>a.

Proof.

Fix
a<s
. Choose
\gamma\in(0,1)
small enough such that

a/(1-\gamma)<s
. From the assumption (
53
), we can choose a block length

\ell
and an effect
M:=M_{\ell}
such that, with

\alpha=\Tr(M\sigma_0^{\otimes\ell}),\qquad\beta=\Tr(M\rho_0^{\otimes\ell}),

we have

\frac{1-\gamma}{\ell}d(\alpha\|\beta)>a,

where the binary relative entropy is defined by

d(\alpha\|\beta)=D((\alpha,1-\alpha)\|(\beta,1-\beta))=-\alpha\log\beta-(1-\alpha)\log(1-\beta)-h_{2}(\alpha).

To see this, note that
\alpha
is close to
1
and
-\log\beta
is greater than
a\ell
for large
\ell
.

By continuity of the binary relative entropy, we can choose
\eta
small enough so that

\frac{1-\gamma}{\ell}\inf_{|q-\alpha|\leq\eta}d(q\|\beta)>a.

Now split
n
copies into

m_{n}=\lfloor\gamma n\rfloor,\qquad k_{n}=\left\lfloor\frac{n-m_{n}}{\ell}\right\rfloor.

On the first
m_{n}
copies, run any baseline consistent tomography whose estimator

\widehat{\rho}_{n}
satisfies an LDP with rate function
K
and

K(\sigma\|\rho)=0\iff\sigma=\rho.

For example, one may choose Keyl’s protocol. On the remaining
k_{n}
blocks, measure
\{M,\mathbf{1}-M\}
, obtain

Z_{n,1},\ldots,Z_{n,k_{n}}
, and set

\bar{Z}_{n}=k_{n}^{-1}\sum_{i}Z_{n,i}.

Then

Y_{n}=(\widehat{\rho}_{n},\bar{Z}_{n})

satisfies an LDP with rate

R(\sigma,p\|\rho)=\gamma K(\sigma\|\rho)+\frac{1-\gamma}{\ell}d(p\|p_{\rho}).

(54)

Recall for any state
\tau
,
p_{\tau}:=\Tr(M \tau^{\otimes\ell})
. Define a post-processing map
\Phi:\mathcal{D}_{d}\times[0,1]\to\mathcal{D}_{d}

\Phi_{\omega_{*}}(\tau,p)=\begin{cases}\tau,&|p-p_{\tau}|\leq\eta,\\
\omega_{*},&|p-p_{\tau}|>\eta,\end{cases}

where we choose
\omega_{*}\neq\sigma_{0}
. The final estimator is given by

\widehat{\theta}_{n}=\Phi(\widehat{\rho}_{n},\bar{Z}_{n}).

(55)

Using the general contraction principle in Theorem
B.1
, and the details are covered in Appendix
B
, we can show that
\widehat{\theta}_{n}
satisfies a LDP with rate function

I_{a}(\sigma\|\rho)=\inf\left\{R(\sigma,q\|\rho):\ q\in[0,1],|q-p_{\sigma}|\leq\eta\right\},\quad\sigma\neq\omega_{*},

where
R(\sigma,q\|\rho)
is defined in (
54
). At
\sigma=\omega_{*}
, we have

I_{a}(\sigma\|\rho)=\min\bigg\{\inf\left\{R(\sigma,q\|\rho):\ q\in[0,1],|q-p_{\sigma}|\leq\eta\right\},\ \inf\left\{R(\tau,q\|\rho):\ q\in[0,1],\ \tau\in\mathcal{D}_{d},\ |q-p_{\tau}|\geq\eta\right\}\bigg\}.

Note that
I_{a}
has the unique-zero property. Away from
\omega_{*}
,
the term
K(\sigma\|\rho)
forces
\sigma=\rho
. At
\sigma=\omega_{*}
, because
R_{\rho}
has unique zero
(\rho,p_{\rho})
,
I_{a}(\omega_{*}\|\rho)=0
if and only if
\omega_{*}=\rho
.

At the target pair
(\sigma_{0},\rho_{0})
, with
\sigma_{0}\neq\omega_{*}
, we have

\displaystyle I_{a}(\sigma_{0}\|\rho_{0})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1-\gamma}{\ell}\inf_{|p-\alpha|\leq\eta}d(p\|\beta)>a,

which concludes the proof.
∎

6
Optimality of Keyl’s rate function for covariant protocols

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.2
thus the optimality of Keyl’s rate for covariant protocols predicted in the first equality in Conjecture
4.5
.

Namely, we show that if a covariant protocol is consistent and its estimator distributions satisfy an LDP, then its rate function cannot exceed Keyl’s rate at any point. Thus Keyl’s rate is a universal pointwise upper bound on the large-deviation rate function achievable by covariant tomography protocols.

Theorem 6.1

(Converse bound for covariant tomography)
.

Let
\{E_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be a covariant quantum state tomography
protocol. Assume that it is consistent in the sense of Definition
4.1
, and for each
\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{d}
, the estimator
distributions
\{\widehat{\mu}_{n}^{\rho}\}_{n\geq 1}
satisfy a large deviation
principle on
\mathcal{D}_{d}
with rate function

I(\cdot\|\rho)
. Then, for every

\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{d}
,

I(\sigma\|\rho)\leq S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}(\sigma\|\rho).

(56)

To prove the theorem, suppose we are given an arbitrary covariant quantum state tomography protocol. Following the general characterization in Theorem
3.5
, we can assume that the protocol is given by
\left(\{M_{\sigma}^{(n)}\}_{\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{d}},\mu_{n}\right)_{n\geq 1}
where
\mu_{n}
is unitarily invariant and
\{M_{\sigma}^{(n)}\}_{\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{d}}
has the form

M_{U\operatorname{diag}(x)U^{\dagger}}^{(n)}=U_{\mathrm{Schur}}^{\dagger}\left(\sum_{\lambda\vdash_{d}n}\dim\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}|\lambda\rangle\langle\lambda|\otimes\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}}\otimes q_{\lambda}(U)K_{\lambda,x}q_{\lambda}(U^{\dagger})\right)U_{\mathrm{Schur}}.

(57)

with
\int_{\Delta_{d}^{\downarrow}}\Tr K_{\lambda,x}d\nu_{n}(x)=1,\ \forall\lambda\vdash_{d}n
, where
\nu_{n}
is defined by (
38
). The corresponding estimator distribution is denoted by

\widehat{\mu}_{n}^{\rho,K}
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}^{\rho,K}_{n}(A)

\displaystyle:=\int_{A}\Tr(M_\sigma^{(n)} \rho^{\otimes n})d\mu_{n}(\sigma)

(58)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\vdash_{d}n}\dim\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}\dim\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}\Tr(q_\lambda(\rho)\iint_{U\diag(x)U^\dagger\in A} q_\lambda(U) K_{\lambda, x} q_\lambda(U^\dagger) d\nu_n(x) d\mu_{\mathrm{Haar}}(U)).

A local analysis of the probability of a neighborhood of an estimator is crucial. To this end, we introduce the localized seed operator

K_{\lambda,x}^{\varepsilon}:=\iint_{W\operatorname{diag}(y)W^{\dagger}\in N_{\varepsilon}(\operatorname{diag}(x))}q_{\lambda}(W)K_{\lambda,y}q_{\lambda}(W^{\dagger})d\nu_{n}(y)d\mu_{\mathrm{Haar}}(W).

(59)

One can check that
K_{\lambda,x}^{\varepsilon}
is positive,
torus-invariant, and satisfies

\Tr K_{\lambda,x}^{\varepsilon}\leq 1.

The following implication of consistency is crucial:

Lemma 6.2
.

If the protocol is consistent, i.e., for any
\rho
and
\varepsilon>0
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}\widehat{\mu}^{\rho,K}_{n}(N_{\varepsilon}(\rho))=1
. Then for any
x\in\Delta_{d}^{\downarrow}
and
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a Young sequence
\lambda^{(n)}
with
\lambda^{(n)}/n\to x
and
\ell(\lambda^{(n)})\leq\rank(x)
, such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\Tr(q_{\lambda^{(n)}}(\diag(x)) K_{\lambda^{(n)},x}^\varepsilon)=H(x).

(60)

Proof.

We extend the probability measures
\widehat{\mu}^{\rho,K}_{n}
on
\mathcal{D}_{d}
to probability measures on
\Delta_{d}^{\downarrow}\times\mathcal{D}_{d}
. For any
\lambda\vdash_{d}n
and
A\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{D}_{d})
, we define

\widehat{\mu}^{\rho,K}_{n}(\lambda,A):=\dim\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}\dim\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}\Tr(q_\lambda(\rho)\iint_{U\diag(x)U^\dagger\in A} q_\lambda(U) K_{\lambda, x} q_\lambda(U^\dagger) d\nu_n(x) d\mu_{\mathrm{Haar}}(U)).

Then by definition (
58
), we have
\widehat{\mu}_{n}^{\rho,K}(A)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash_{d}n}\widehat{\mu}^{\rho,K}_{n}(\lambda,A)
.

Note that the marginal distribution of
\lambda
is given by
\mathbb{P}_{n,\rho}
, defined in (
27
). Indeed, taking
A=\mathcal{D}_{d}
, we get

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{n}^{\rho,K}(\lambda,\mathcal{D}_{d})

\displaystyle=\dim\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}\dim\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}\Tr\left(q_{\lambda}(\rho)\int_{\Delta_{d}^{\downarrow}}\int_{\mathrm{U}(d)}q_{\lambda}(U)K_{\lambda,y}q_{\lambda}(U^{\dagger})d\mu_{\mathrm{Haar}}(U)d\nu_{n}(y)\right)

\displaystyle=\dim\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}\Tr(q_\lambda(\rho))=\mathbb{P}_{n,\rho}(\lambda),

where we used Lemma
2.1
and the normalization
\int_{\Delta_{d}^{\downarrow}}\Tr(K_{\lambda,y})d\nu_{n}(y)=1
. In particular,

\widehat{\mu}^{\rho,K}_{n}(\lambda,A)\leq P_{n,\rho}(\lambda),\quad A\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{D}_{d}).

(61)

For any
\delta>0
, recall that

G_{n,\delta}(x):=\{\lambda:\|\lambda/n-x\|_{1}<\delta,\ \ell(\lambda)\leq\rank(x)\}.

(62)

Via Lemma
2.3
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{n,\rho}(G^{c}_{n,\delta}(x))=0.

Therefore, for
A=N_{\varepsilon}(\operatorname{diag}(x))
, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}^{\operatorname{diag}(x),K}_{n}(N_{\varepsilon}(\operatorname{diag}(x)))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\vdash_{d}n}\widehat{\mu}^{\operatorname{diag}(x),K}_{n}(\lambda,N_{\varepsilon}(\operatorname{diag}(x)))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\in G_{n,\delta}(x)}\widehat{\mu}^{\operatorname{diag}(x),K}_{n}(\lambda,N_{\varepsilon}(\operatorname{diag}(x)))+\sum_{\lambda\in G^{c}_{n,\delta}(x)}\widehat{\mu}^{\operatorname{diag}(x),K}_{n}(\lambda,N_{\varepsilon}(\operatorname{diag}(x)))

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\lambda\in G_{n,\delta}(x)}\widehat{\mu}^{\operatorname{diag}(x),K}_{n}(\lambda,N_{\varepsilon}(\operatorname{diag}(x)))+\mathbb{P}_{n,\rho}(G^{c}_{n,\delta}(x))

where the last inequality follows from (
61
). Recall that
\widehat{\mu}^{\operatorname{diag}(x),K}_{n}(N_{\varepsilon}(\operatorname{diag}(x)))\to 1
, then for each
n>0
, we choose
\delta_{n}\to 0
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{\lambda\in G_{n,\delta_{n}}(x)}\widehat{\mu}^{\operatorname{diag}(x),K}_{n}(\lambda,N_{\varepsilon}(\operatorname{diag}(x)))=1.

Since

\widehat{\mu}^{\operatorname{diag}(x),K}_{n}(\lambda,N_{\varepsilon}(\operatorname{diag}(x)))=\dim\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}\dim\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}\Tr(q_\lambda(\diag(x)) K_{\lambda,x}^\varepsilon),

we have

\sum_{\lambda\in G_{n,\delta_{n}}(x)}\dim\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}\dim\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}\Tr(q_\lambda(\diag(x)) K_{\lambda,x}^\varepsilon)\geq 1-o(1).

Via Lemma
2.2
, for each
n\geq 1
, there exists
\lambda^{(n)}\in G_{n,\delta_{n}}(x)
, such that

\Tr(q_{\lambda^{(n)}}(\diag(x)) K_{{\lambda^{(n)}},x}^\varepsilon)\geq e^{-nH(\overline{{\lambda^{(n)}}})}\mathrm{poly}(n)^{-1}.

On the other hand, we always have

\dim\mathcal{P}_{\lambda^{(n)}}\dim\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda^{(n)}}\Tr(q_{\lambda^{(n)}}(\diag(x)) K_{{\lambda^{(n)}},x}^\varepsilon)\leq 1\implies\Tr(q_{\lambda^{(n)}}(\diag(x)) K_{{\lambda^{(n)}},x}^\varepsilon)\leq e^{-nH(\overline{{\lambda^{(n)}}})}\mathrm{poly}(n).

Taking
\lim_{n\to\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log
on both sides concludes the proof.
∎

The following lower bound is crucial for the weight analysis:

Lemma 6.3

(Principal minor lower bound)
.

Let
\tau\geq 0
, then for every
\alpha\in\mathrm{Wt}_{d}(\lambda)
,

P_{\lambda,\alpha}q_{\lambda}(\tau)P_{\lambda,\alpha}\geq\Theta(\tau)^{\alpha}P_{\lambda,\alpha},\qquad\Theta(\tau)^{\alpha}:=\prod_{k=1}^{d}\Theta_{k}(\tau)^{\alpha_{k}}.

Proof.

Assume
\tau>0
first. Using the Cholesky decomposition, there exists an upper triangular matrix
L
with diagonal entries equal to
1
, such that

\tau=L^{\dagger}\operatorname{diag}(\Theta(\tau))L.

Let
v\in\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda,\alpha}
. By the triangular weight property (
21
),

q_{\lambda}(L)v=v+\sum_{\beta\succ\alpha}v_{\beta},\qquad v_{\beta}\in\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda,\beta}.

Since
q_{\lambda}(\operatorname{diag}(\Theta(\tau)))
is diagonal in the weight-space decomposition and
different weight spaces are orthogonal,

\displaystyle\langle v,q_{\lambda}(\tau)v\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle q_{\lambda}(L)v,q_{\lambda}(\operatorname{diag}(\Theta(\tau)))q_{\lambda}(L)v\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\Theta(\tau)^{\alpha}\|v\|^{2}+\sum_{\beta\succ\alpha}\Theta(\tau)^{\beta}\|v_{\beta}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\Theta(\tau)^{\alpha}\|v\|^{2}.

This proves the operator inequality. When
\tau\geq 0
, replacing
\tau
by
\tau+\varepsilon\textbf{1}
and then sending
\varepsilon\to 0+
concludes the proof.
∎

The following universal upper bound is derived:

Lemma 6.4

(Comparison lemma)
.

Let
x\in\Delta_{d}^{\downarrow}
. Suppose
\lambda^{(n)}\vdash_{d}n

satisfies

\frac{\lambda^{(n)}}{n}\longrightarrow x,\quad\ell(\lambda^{(n)})\leq\rank(x)

and suppose
K_{\lambda^{(n)}}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda^{(n)}})_{+}
satisfying
\Tr K_{\lambda^{(n)}}\leq 1
and

q_{\lambda^{(n)}}(T)K_{\lambda^{(n)}}q_{\lambda^{(n)}}(T^{\dagger})=K_{\lambda^{(n)}},\ \forall T\in\mathrm{T}(d).

Assume that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\Tr\left(q_{\lambda^{(n)}}(\operatorname{diag}(x))K_{\lambda^{(n)}}\right)\leq H(x).

(63)

Then, for every
\tau\in\mathcal{D}_{d}
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\Tr\left(q_{\lambda^{(n)}}(\tau)K_{\lambda^{(n)}}\right)\leq L_{\mathrm{Keyl}}(x,\tau),

(64)

where
L_{\mathrm{Keyl}}(x,\tau)
is defined in (
52
).

Proof.

For brevity, we write
\lambda=\lambda^{(n)}
and

r:=\rank(x)=\#\{i:x_{i}>0\}.

We assume
\rank(\tau)\geq r
, otherwise
L_{\mathrm{Keyl}}(x,\tau)=+\infty
and (
64
) clearly holds. For any fixed
\delta>0
, we introduce

\omega_{x,\tau}(\delta):=\sup\left\{\left|\sum_{k=1}^{r}(y_{k}-x_{k})\log\Theta_{k}(\tau)\right|:\ y\in\Delta_{d},\ \rank(y)\leq r,\ \|y-x\|_{1}<\delta\right\}.

(65)

If
\Theta_{k}(\tau)=0
for some
k\leq r
, then

L_{\mathrm{Keyl}}(x,\tau)=+\infty
, and there is nothing to prove. Hence we
may assume that

\Theta_{k}(\tau)>0,\qquad k=1,\ldots,r.

Under this assumption the quantities
\log\Theta_{k}(\tau)
are finite, and

\lim_{\delta\to 0+}\omega_{x,\tau}(\delta)=0.

Denote

G_{n,\delta}^{\lambda}(x):=\left\{\alpha\prec\lambda:\left\|\frac{\alpha}{n}-x\right\|_{1}<\delta\right\}.

(66)

We claim

\limsup_{n\to\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\bigg( \sum_{\alpha\in G_{n,\delta}^\lambda(x)} \Tr(P_{\lambda,\alpha} K_\lambda)\bigg)\leq\omega_{x,\operatorname{diag}(x)}(\delta).

(67)

In fact, for any
\alpha\in G_{n,\delta}^{\lambda}(x)
,
\alpha/n
satisfies the constraint in (
65
), then by definition, we have

\left|\sum_{k=1}^{r}\left(\frac{\alpha_{k}}{n}-x_{k}\right)\log\Theta_{k}(\tau)\right|\leq\omega_{x,\tau}(\delta).

(68)

Choosing
\tau=\operatorname{diag}(x)
in (
68
), we have
\Theta_{k}(\tau)=x_{k}
thus taking the exponential we have

x^{\alpha}\leq\exp(-nH(x) + n\, \omega_{x,\diag(x)}(\delta)),\quad\alpha\in G_{n,\delta}^{\lambda}(x).

(69)

If
\alpha\notin G_{n,\delta}^{\lambda}(x)
, denote
\overline{\alpha}=\alpha/n
, we have
\|\alpha/n-x\|_{1}\geq\delta
. Note that such
\alpha
may not exist and in this case we set any quantity involving
\sum_{\alpha\notin G_{n,\delta}^{\lambda}(x)}
to be zero. Without loss of generality, we assume for all
n
, such
\alpha
exists. Then via Schur concavity of von Neumann entropy, we have
H(\overline{\alpha})\geq H(\overline{\lambda})
, and

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{r}\left(\frac{\alpha_{k}}{n}-x_{k}\right)\log x_{k}

\displaystyle=H(x)-H(\overline{\alpha})-D(\overline{\alpha}\|x)

\displaystyle\leq H(x)-H(\overline{\lambda})-D(\overline{\alpha}\|x).

Since
\overline{\lambda}\to x
, as
n
large enough, we can make
H(x)-H(\overline{\lambda})
arbitrarily close to zero. On the other hand, via Pinsker’s inequality,
D(\overline{\alpha}\|x)
is bounded away from zero. Thus whenever
n
is large enough, there exists
c_{\delta}>0
such that

\sum_{k=1}^{r}\left(\frac{\alpha_{k}}{n}-x_{k}\right)\log x_{k}\leq-c_{\delta},

which implies

x^{\alpha}=\exp(-nH(x) + n\sum_{k=1}^r \left(\frac{\alpha_k}{n} - x_k\right) \log x_k)\leq\exp(-nH(x) - n\, c_\delta),\quad\alpha\notin G_{n,\delta}^{\lambda}(x).

(70)

Then we have

\displaystyle\Tr(q_\lambda(\diag(x)) K_\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\prec\lambda}x^{\alpha}\Tr(P_{\lambda,\alpha} K_\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in G_{n,\delta}^{\lambda}(x)}x^{\alpha}\Tr(P_{\lambda,\alpha} K_\lambda)+\sum_{\alpha\notin G_{n,\delta}^{\lambda}(x)}x^{\alpha}\Tr(P_{\lambda,\alpha} K_\lambda)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-nH(x) + n\, \omega_{x,\diag(x)}(\delta))\sum_{\alpha\in G_{n,\delta}^{\lambda}(x)}\Tr(P_{\lambda,\alpha} K_\lambda)+\exp(-nH(x) - n\, c_\delta).

For the first equality, we used (
18
). The second equality follows from separating the weights into
G_{n,\delta}^{\lambda}(x)
and
G_{n,\delta}^{\lambda}(x)^{c}
. For the inequality, we used (
69
) and (
70
) respectively, and since
\Tr(K_\lambda)\leq 1
, we have
\sum_{\alpha\notin G_{n,\delta}^{\lambda}(x)}\Tr(K_\lambda P_{\lambda, \alpha})\leq\sum_{\alpha\prec\lambda}\Tr(K_\lambda P_{\lambda, \alpha})\leq 1
. On the other hand, since

\limsup_{n\to\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\Tr\left(q_{\lambda}(\operatorname{diag}(x))K_{\lambda}\right)\leq H(x),

there exists
\eta_{n}\to 0
, such that

\Tr(q_\lambda(\diag(x)) K_\lambda)\geq\exp(-nH(x) - n\, \eta_n).

Combining the two bounds, we get

\sum_{\alpha\in G_{n,\delta}^{\lambda}(x)}\Tr(P_{\lambda,\alpha} K_\lambda)\geq(e^{-n\,\eta_{n}}-e^{-n\,c_{\delta}})e^{-n\,\omega_{x,\operatorname{diag}(x)}(\delta)}.

Taking
-\frac{1}{n}\log
on both sides concludes the proof of (
67
).

Now we use the fact that
K_{\lambda}
is torus-invariant, thus by (
20
),
K_{\lambda}=\sum_{\alpha\prec\lambda}P_{\lambda,\alpha}K_{\lambda}P_{\lambda,\alpha}
. Then

\displaystyle\Tr(q_\lambda(\tau) K_\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in G_{n,\delta}^{\lambda}(x)}\Tr(P_{\lambda,\alpha} q_\lambda(\tau) P_{\lambda,\alpha} K_\lambda)+\sum_{\alpha\notin G_{n,\delta}^{\lambda}(x)}\Tr(P_{\lambda,\alpha} q_\lambda(\tau) P_{\lambda,\alpha} K_\lambda)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{\alpha\in G_{n,\delta}^{\lambda}(x)}\Tr(P_{\lambda,\alpha} q_\lambda(\tau) P_{\lambda,\alpha} K_\lambda)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{\alpha\in G_{n,\delta}^{\lambda}(x)}\Theta(\tau)^{\alpha}\Tr(P_{\lambda,\alpha} K_\lambda),

where the last inequality follows from
P_{\lambda,\alpha}q_{\lambda}(\tau)P_{\lambda,\alpha}\geq\Theta(\tau)^{\alpha}P_{\lambda,\alpha}
shown in Lemma
6.3
. Recall the definition
\omega_{x,\tau}(\delta)
in (
65
), and
\alpha\in G_{n,x}^{\lambda}(\delta)
satisfies
\|\alpha/n-x\|_{1}<\delta
, thus

\displaystyle\Theta(\tau)^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\exp(n\sum_{k=1}^r \frac{\alpha_k}{n} \log\Theta_k(\tau))

\displaystyle=\exp(n\sum_{k=1}^r x_k \log\Theta_k(\tau) + n\sum_{k=1}^r (\frac{\alpha_k}{n} - x_k) \log\Theta_k(\tau) )

\displaystyle\geq\exp(n\sum_{k=1}^r x_k \log\Theta_k(\tau) - n\,\omega_{x,\tau}(\delta)).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\Tr(q_\lambda(\tau) K_\lambda)

\displaystyle\leq-\sum_{k=1}^{r}x_{k}\log\Theta_{k}(\tau)+\omega_{x,\tau}(\delta)+\limsup_{n\to\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\bigg( \sum_{\alpha\in G_{n,\delta}^\lambda(x)} \Tr(P_{\lambda,\alpha} K_\lambda)\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq-\sum_{k=1}^{r}x_{k}\log\Theta_{k}(\tau)+\omega_{x,\tau}(\delta)+\omega_{x,\operatorname{diag}(x)}(\delta).

Finally, sending
\delta\to 0
, we conclude the proof.
∎

To transfer the derived local bound to pointwise rate function bounds, we need the following lemma:

Lemma 6.5

(From local bounds to pointwise rate bounds)
.

Let
\{\widehat{\mu}_{n}^{\rho,K}\}_{n\geq 1}
satisfy an LDP with rate
function
I_{K}(\cdot\|\rho)
. Fix
\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{d}
. Suppose that
for some
R\in[0,\infty]
, for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\widehat{\mu}_{n}^{\rho,K}(N_{\varepsilon}(\sigma))\leq R.

(71)

Then

I_{K}(\sigma\|\rho)\leq R.

(72)

Proof.

If
R=+\infty
, there is nothing to prove. Assume
R<+\infty
. Let

F_{\varepsilon}(\sigma):=\{\omega\in\mathcal{D}_{d}:\|\omega-\sigma\|_{1}\leq\varepsilon\}=\overline{N_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)}.

Then
F_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)
is closed and
N_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)\subseteq F_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)
. Hence

\widehat{\mu}_{n}^{\rho,K}(F_{\varepsilon}(\sigma))\geq\widehat{\mu}_{n}^{\rho,K}(N_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)),

Since
\widehat{\mu}_{n}^{\rho,K}
satisfies a LDP, we have

\inf_{\omega\in F_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)}I_{K}(\omega\|\rho)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\widehat{\mu}_{n}^{\rho,K}(F_{\varepsilon}(\sigma))\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\widehat{\mu}_{n}^{\rho,K}(N_{\varepsilon}(\sigma))\leq R.

We may choose
\omega_{m}\in F_{\varepsilon_{m}}(\sigma)
with
\varepsilon_{m}\to 0
, such that

I_{K}(\omega_{m}\|\rho)\leq R+\frac{1}{m}.

Since
\omega_{m}\to\sigma
and
I_{K}(\cdot\|\rho)
is lower semicontinuous,

I_{K}(\sigma\|\rho)\leq\liminf_{m\to\infty}I_{K}(\omega_{m}\|\rho)\leq R.

This proves the lemma.
∎

Finally, we are able to prove Theorem
6.1
:

Proof of Theorem
6.1
.

By Lemma
6.5
, we only need to show that for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\widehat{\mu}_{n}^{\rho,K}(N_{\varepsilon}(\sigma))\leq S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}(\sigma\|\rho).

Suppose
\sigma=U\operatorname{diag}(x)U^{\dagger}
, then

N_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)=\{\tau:\|\tau-\sigma\|_{1}<\varepsilon\}=\{\tau:\|U^{\dagger}\tau U-\operatorname{diag}(x)\|_{1}<\varepsilon\}=UN_{\varepsilon}(\operatorname{diag}(x))U^{\dagger}.

By definition of
\widehat{\mu}_{n}^{\rho,K}
, and left-invariance of Haar measure, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{n}^{\rho,K}(N_{\varepsilon}(\sigma))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\vdash_{d}n}\dim\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}\dim\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}\Tr(q_\lambda(\rho)\iint_{W\diag(y)W^\dagger\in N_\varepsilon(\sigma)} q_\lambda(W) K_{\lambda, y} q_\lambda(W^\dagger) d\nu_n(y) d\mu_{\mathrm{Haar}}(W))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\vdash_{d}n}\dim\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}\dim\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}\Tr(q_\lambda(\rho)\iint_{U^\dagger W\diag(y)W^\dagger U \in N_\varepsilon(\diag(x))} q_\lambda(W) K_{\lambda, y} q_\lambda(W^\dagger) d\nu_n(y) d\mu_{\mathrm{Haar}}(W))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\vdash_{d}n}\dim\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}\dim\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}\Tr(q_\lambda(\rho)\iint_{ W\diag(y)W^\dagger\in N_\varepsilon(\diag(x))}q_\lambda(U) q_\lambda(W) K_{\lambda, y} q_\lambda(W^\dagger) q_\lambda(U^\dagger) d\nu_n(y) d\mu_{\mathrm{Haar}}(W))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\vdash_{d}n}\dim\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}\dim\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}\Tr(q_\lambda(U^\dagger\rho U) K_{\lambda,x}^\varepsilon),

where
K_{\lambda,x}^{\varepsilon}
is introduced in (
59
) which is torus invariant. Since
\widehat{\mu}_{n}^{\rho,K}
is consistent, by Lemma
6.2
, there exists a Young sequence
\lambda^{(n)}
with

\lambda^{(n)}/n\to x,\quad\ell(\lambda^{(n)})\leq\rank(x),

such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\Tr(q_{\lambda^{(n)}}(\diag(x)) K_{\lambda^{(n)},x}^\varepsilon)=H(x).

Then by Lemma
6.4
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\Tr(q_{\lambda^{(n)}}(U^\dagger\rho U) K_{\lambda^{(n)},x}^\varepsilon)\leq L_{\mathrm{Keyl}}(x,U^{\dagger}\rho U).

Hence

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{n}^{\rho,K}(N_{\varepsilon}(\sigma))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\vdash_{d}n}\dim\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}\dim\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}\Tr(q_\lambda(U^\dagger\rho U) K_{\lambda,x}^\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\geq\dim\mathcal{P}_{\lambda^{(n)}}\dim\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda^{(n)}}\Tr(q_{\lambda^{(n)}} (U^\dagger\rho U) K_{\lambda^{(n)},x}^\varepsilon).

Finally, using Lemma
2.2
,
\dim\mathcal{P}_{\lambda^{(n)}}\approx\exp(n H(x))
and
\dim\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda^{(n)}}
is polynomial in

,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}-\frac{1}{n}\log\widehat{\mu}_{n}^{\rho,K}(N_{\varepsilon}(\sigma))\leq-H(x)+L_{\mathrm{Keyl}}(x,U^{\dagger}\rho U)=S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}(\sigma\|\rho).

∎

Appendix A
Properties of Keyl’s rate function

This appendix records some basic properties of Keyl’s rate function as described in (
6
). First we characterize when
S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}(\sigma\|\rho)
is infinite.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
\sigma,\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{d}
, and write

\sigma=U\operatorname{diag}(x)U^{\dagger},\quad x\in\Delta_{d}^{\downarrow},\quad x_{d+1}:=0.

Then the following are equivalent:

1.

S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}(\sigma\|\rho)=+\infty
.

2.

There exists
k
with
x_{k}>x_{k+1}
such that
\Delta_{k}(U^{\dagger}\rho U)=0
.

3.

\rank(\sigma)>\rank(\sigma\rho).

4.

\supp(\sigma)\cap\ker(\rho)\neq\{0\}
.

Proof.

From the formula in (
6
), we directly see that
S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}(\sigma\|\rho)=+\infty
if and only if Condition
2
holds. Note that Condition
3
being equivalent to Condition
4
follows from elementary linear algebra. Then we only need to show that Condition
2
is equivalent to Condition
4
. First, assume Condition
2
: there exists
k
with
x_{k}>x_{k+1}
such that
\Delta_{k}(U^{\dagger}\rho U)=0
, then there exists
z=(y,0,\cdots,0),y\in\mathbb{C}^{k}
such that
z^{*}(U^{\dagger}\rho U)z=0
implying
Uz\in\ker(\rho)
. Because
x_{k}>x_{k+1}\geq 0
, and
z
is supported on the first
k
components,
Uz\in\supp(\sigma)
implies
Uz\in\supp(\sigma)\cap\ker(\rho)
. Now assume Condition
4
, choose
0\neq w\in\supp(\sigma)\cap\ker(\rho)
and define
z:=U^{\dagger}w
, which has the form
z=(y,0,\cdots,0),y\in\mathbb{C}^{r}
where
r=\rank(\sigma)
. Since
w\in\ker(\rho)
, we have
U^{\dagger}\rho Uz=0
hence
z^{*}U^{\dagger}\rho Uz=0
implying
\Delta_{r}(U^{\dagger}\rho U)=0
, concluding Condition
2
.
∎

Notice that the finiteness condition for
S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}(\sigma\|\rho)
is weaker than the condition for
D(\sigma\|\rho)<+\infty
, in the sense that

\{(\sigma,\rho):S^{\rm{Keyl}}(\sigma\|\rho)=\infty\}\subsetneq\{(\sigma,\rho):D(\sigma\|\rho)=\infty\}.

For example, taking
\sigma=\ket{0}\bra{0}
and
\rho=\ket{+}\bra{+}
, we have
D(\sigma\|\rho)=\infty
, while
S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}(\sigma\|\rho)=-\log F(\sigma,\rho)<+\infty
.

Now we are able to prove Proposition
4.6
:

Proof of Proposition
4.6
.

If
D(\sigma\|\rho)=+\infty
, then the equality condition directly follows from Proposition
A.1
. Without loss of generality, we assume
D(\sigma\|\rho)<+\infty
.

Write
\sigma=\sum_{j=1}^{d}x_{j}|\psi_{j}\rangle\langle\psi_{j}|,\ x_{1}\geq\cdots\geq x_{d}
, and define

\Pi_{k}:=\sum_{j=1}^{k}|\psi_{j}\rangle\langle\psi_{j}|,\qquad x_{d+1}:=0.

Then
\sigma=\sum_{k=1}^{d}(x_{k}-x_{k+1})\Pi_{k}
. Moreover, for
U=\sum_{j=1}^{d}|\psi_{j}\rangle\langle j|
, we have
\sigma=U\operatorname{diag}(x)U^{\dagger}
and

\Delta_{k}(U^{\dagger}\rho U)=\Delta_{k}\left(\sum_{j,l=1}^{d}\langle\psi_{j}|\rho|\psi_{l}\rangle|j\rangle\langle l|\right)=\det(\Pi_k\rho\Pi_k),

where
\Pi_{k}\rho\Pi_{k}
is regarded as an operator fully supported on
\Pi_{k}\mathcal{H}
since
\supp(\sigma)\subseteq\supp(\rho)
. Hence

\log\Delta_{k}(U^{\dagger}\rho U)=\Tr\log(\Pi_k\rho\Pi_k).

Therefore

\displaystyle D(\sigma\|\rho)-S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}(\sigma\|\rho)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}(x_{k}-x_{k+1})\Bigl[\Tr\log(\Pi_k\rho\Pi_k)-\Tr(\Pi_k\log\rho\Pi_k)\Bigr].

Since
\log
is operator monotone, operator Jensen’s inequality
Han 79
gives

\log(\Pi_k\rho\Pi_k)\geq\Pi_{k}(\log\rho)\Pi_{k}.

Taking traces, we have
D(\sigma\|\rho)\geq S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}(\sigma\|\rho)
.

Now consider equality condition. The equality condition of Jensen’s inequality for the strictly concave function
\log
says that

\log(\Pi_k\rho\Pi_k)=\Pi_{k}(\log\rho)\Pi_{k}

holds if and only if
[\Pi_{k},\rho]=0
. Since every coefficient
x_{k}-x_{k+1}
is nonnegative, equality

D(\sigma\|\rho)=S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}(\sigma\|\rho)

holds if and only if

[\Pi_{k},\rho]=0\qquad\text{for every }k\text{ with }x_{k}>x_{k+1}.

Using
\sigma=\sum_{k=1}^{d}(x_{k}-x_{k+1})\Pi_{k}
, we have
[\sigma,\rho]=0
, concluding the necessity part. For the sufficiency part, if
[\sigma,\rho]=0
, it is direct to see
S^{\mathrm{Keyl}}(\sigma\|\rho)=D(\sigma\|\rho)
.
∎

Appendix B
General contraction principle of large deviations

In this section, we review a general contraction principle thus concluding the proof of LDP for the estimator defined in (
55
).

Let
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
be two Polish spaces, let
\{\mu_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of

\mathcal{X}
, and let
\Phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
be a measurable map. For each

\mathcal{Y}
. For any Borel set

A

A
).
The following result is a simpler version of Proposition 4.4 in
Mar 18
.

.

\{\mu_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
satisfies a LDP with rate function

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{n}\log\nu_{n}(O)\right)\leq\inf_{y\in O}\overline{J}(y),

y
with radius
\delta
and

\{\mu_{n}\}
is exponentially tight, i.e.,
\inf_{\text{compact}\ K\subseteq\mathcal{X}}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mu_{n}(K^{c})=-\infty
, then for any compact set
K\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{n}\log\nu_{n}(K)\right)\geq\inf_{y\in K}\underline{J}(y),

where

\underline{J}(y):=\inf\left\{I(x):\ x\in\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\mathrm{cl}\left(\Phi^{-1}\bigl(N_{\delta}(y)\bigr)\right)\right\}.

Using Theorem
B.1
, we can show that the estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{n}
defined in (
55
) satisfies a LDP. In fact, let
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{D}_{d}\times[0,1]
,
\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{D}_{d}
and recall that
p_{\tau}=\Tr(\tau^{\otimes\ell} M_\ell)
,

\Phi_{\omega_{*}}(\tau,p)=\begin{cases}\tau,&|p-p_{\tau}|\leq\eta,\\
\omega_{*},&|p-p_{\tau}|>\eta,\end{cases}

If
\sigma\neq\omega_{*}
, then for
\delta
very small such that
\omega_{*}\not\in N_{\delta}(\sigma)
, we have

\Phi_{\omega_{*}}^{-1}(N_{\delta}(\sigma))=\{(\tau,p):|p-p_{\tau}|<\eta,\ \tau\in N_{\delta}(\sigma)\},

which implies

\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\mathrm{int}\left(\Phi_{\omega_{*}}^{-1}\bigl(N_{\delta}(\sigma)\bigr)\right)=\{(\sigma,p):|p-p_{\sigma}|<\eta\},\quad\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\mathrm{cl}\left(\Phi_{\omega_{*}}^{-1}\bigl(N_{\delta}(\sigma)\bigr)\right)=\{(\sigma,p):|p-p_{\sigma}|\leq\eta\}

,

\Phi_{\omega_{*}}^{-1}(N_{\delta}(\omega_{*}))=\{(\tau,p):|p-p_{\tau}|<\eta,\ \tau\in N_{\delta}(\omega_{*})\}\bigcup\{(\tau,p):|p-p_{\tau}|\geq\eta,\ \tau\in\mathcal{D}_{d}\}.
