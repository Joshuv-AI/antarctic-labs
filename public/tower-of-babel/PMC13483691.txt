Background

Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) is becoming increasingly important in patient care, and ultrasound training presents a unique opportunity to increase undergraduate medical student competence in anatomy, imaging skills, and clinical pathology [1]. Unfortunately, most first-year residents feel limited in their confidence and skills to perform basic point-of-care ultrasound [1, 2]. To address this gap in knowledge, many medical schools now follow the recommendations of medical societies and integrate ultrasound training into their medical student curriculum [2, 3].

However, only 48 (27.7%) of the roughly 173 US medical schools have a formal ultrasound curriculum, most of which was only mandatory during the first (71.4%) and second (62.9%) preclinical years [4, 5]. This illustrates a significant disparity between the increasingly important role that POCUS plays in patient care and the lack of corresponding ultrasound training pathways. Even established American medical programs experience issues in the longitudinal development of basic and specialized ultrasonography skills [6].

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is a foundational tool in the vast majority of medical school ultrasound curricula due to its potential for diagnostic benefit with relatively minimal patient risk and resources needed for training [7, 8]. Conversely, transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) has historically been excluded from undergraduate ultrasound curricula due to concerns over patient risk, limited availability of teaching faculty, and resource constraints of performing the procedure in an operating room or echocardiography laboratory [9]. Despite these limitations, the arrival of high-fidelity cardiac ultrasound simulators, such as HeartWorks (Intelligent Ultrasound, Cardiff, UK), facilitates a significantly safer and more controlled learning environment for trainees. This calls for a re-evaluation of the risks and benefits of TEE instruction at all levels of medical training. Simulator technology, including HeartWorks, has been proven to improve real-life trainee performance and knowledge [9–12]. Nevertheless, there is still a limited amount of research applying TEE simulation training to medical student education [10, 13]. This project primarily aims to evaluate the confidence, skills, and TEE knowledge of medical students at various stages of their training before and after a simulator-based teaching intervention. This study’s secondary aim is to determine the association of prior ultrasound exposure to the effect of the intervention. The authors hypothesized that a simulator-based TEE training method would significantly improve the confidence, knowledge, and skills of medical students without prior TEE instruction, and that students with greater prior general ultrasound experience would show the highest levels of TEE competency. Ultimately, this study aims to help programs gauge the utility of TEE simulator-based training and evaluate the most valuable time to provide such interventions early in medical training.

Methods

The study was conducted in two phases. The first phase took a small cohort of medical students through the experimental curriculum to determine whether their knowledge, confidence, and skills would improve. The second phase took a larger cohort of students with less ultrasound training through the experimental curriculum to determine whether generalized implementation in medical schools would be feasible.

In the first phase of the study, second-year medical students with moderate ultrasound training (completed the Beginner Ultrasound curriculum at The Ohio State University College of Medicine (OSUCOM)) and fourth-year medical students with extensive ultrasound training (completed the Advanced Ultrasound curriculum at OSUCOM) were recruited. Further information on the Beginner Ultrasound curriculum and Advanced Ultrasound curriculum can be found in “Additional Files 1 and 2” respectively. All participants had no prior TEE training experience.

Before the training session, participants submitted an electronic informed consent form and recruitment survey to determine the level of their previous ultrasound and TEE experience. After their eligibility was confirmed, they submitted a 15-question electronic knowledge quiz and an electronic confidence survey (scale of 1 to 10) to assess their knowledge of and confidence with general ultrasound, cardiac ultrasound, cardiac anatomy, and TEE. All surveys and quizzes were newly created for this study by ultrasound-specialized clinical faculty (see attached supplemental files) and reviewed for content validity by ultrasound-specialized clinical faculty to ensure proper question quality, appropriate difficulty, and representativeness of the domain taught in the intervention. No pilot testing of the knowledge test was performed prior to the study.

Participants also watched an instructional video on basic TEE use prior to the training. At the training session, an ultrasound-trained physician used a HeartWorks TEE simulator to demonstrate how to obtain four views: mid-esophageal four-chamber, mid-esophageal long axis, transgastric short axis, and mid-esophageal bicaval. These views were chosen to reflect a focused TEE exam [14, 15].

Each student received 10 min of hands-on time with the simulator, which included 5 min of practice time with faculty input and 5 min to independently obtain the four views and identify the heart chambers and major vessels on the images. The same faculty examiners requested the identification of the same vessels in the same four views from all tested students to ensure internal validity. The students were judged to have successfully completed this skills assessment only if they were able to independently obtain the four requested views and correctly identify all visible heart chambers and major vessels. Then, participants took the knowledge quiz and confidence survey again to compare their scores pre- and post-training. Following the quiz and survey, students were given the opportunity to ask further questions regarding the HeartWorks machine and their performance.

In phase two of the study, 42 second-semester first-year medical students in their cardiopulmonary block with moderate ultrasound experience (at the end of the Beginner Ultrasound curriculum at OSUCOM), but no previous TEE experience, were recruited. These students underwent the same procedure as the phase one students, with the exception of no knowledge quiz being utilized due to time constraints during data collection sessions.

Data analysis was conducted using Microsoft Excel (Redmond, Washington) to compare the cohorts’ pre- and post-training scores and to identify whether the changes in the confidence, knowledge, and skills of participants differed between the cohorts with varying levels of ultrasound experience. The data was obtained from the confidence surveys, knowledge quizzes, and the ultrasound instructor’s assessment of the student’s ability to obtain the four views and identify anatomical structures. Paired t-tests were calculated to compare pre- and post-training data, with a p-value of less than 0.05 accepted for statistical significance. T-statistics were used to derive comparisons between year groups; otherwise, comparisons between year groups are descriptive and were not formally tested.

Results

Data for Phase 1 were collected from 10 second-year (M2) and 7 fourth-year (M4) medical students, and Phase 2 data were collected from 42 first-year (M1) students. The second-year students significantly improved their knowledge scores by 37.9% (p = 0.006, Table 1), averaging 13.1/15 correct responses post-intervention. The fourth-year students also significantly increased their test scores by 34.0% to 13.4/15 (p = 0.01). The t-statistics for knowledge scores were equal between M2s and M4s (-3.6), suggesting that although both cohorts had improved knowledge scores, there was not a substantial difference in the amount of improvement between the two cohorts (Table 1).

Discussion

The results from Phase 1 demonstrated that students with moderate and extensive ultrasound experience significantly improved their knowledge and confidence through the TEE simulator-based training. Fourth-year medical students increased their confidence more than second-year medical students, but both cohorts had comparable rises in knowledge. All students being able to successfully complete the TEE simulator skills test suggests that the training was effective. Therefore, although the tested intervention appears to be beneficial at multiple stages of medical training, those with more prior ultrasound experience may be able to better build on their existing foundation during higher skill level training. Comparing confidence score increases between the M2s and M4s from Phase 1 and the M1s in Phase 2, revealed relatively similar changes for the M1s and M2s. The M4s stood out as having much larger increases in their confidence scores than both other cohorts, but further investigation to determine the optimal timing of TEE training in medical education is needed. The results from Phase 2 demonstrated that this training method can be applied to a larger cohort, as evidenced by the improvements in all three categories in 42 first-year medical students with minimal ultrasound experience. Phase 2 supported the notion that simulator-based training can be successfully incorporated into medical training.

The findings are consistent with prior literature. For instance, Jelacic et al. identified an increase in anatomy knowledge in senior anesthesiology residents after HeartWorks TEE training. This study determined an increase in anatomy knowledge after HeartWorks TEE training as well, but in medical students with various levels of ultrasound experience. Furthermore, this study’s training session was three hours long and taught 20 TEE views [12]. Other studies, however, have reported that a barrier to incorporating TEE training in medical education is that there is not enough time in the curriculum [2]. To address this barrier, this study’s training session teaches 4 standard views and is, on average, less than 30 min long. Ang et al. conducted a study teaching the Extended Focused Assessment with Sonography in Trauma (E-FAST) examination to fourth- and fifth-year medical students in an Australian medical school. The results showed a significant increase in confidence, ultrasound knowledge, and ability to interpret images, which is in agreement with this study’s findings that ultrasound training can be beneficial in medical education [1].

One limitation of this study is the small sample size. Since the data collection sessions took place during the summer, many second-year students were out of town for summer break, and many fourth-year students were on time-consuming rotations or studying for the Step 2 exam. Due to these factors, participation in this study was limited by availability and/or interest. There is also potential for response bias since this was a volunteer research activity. Capacity was also limited by the availability of ultrasound-trained faculty to lead the training sessions. Studying the benefits of TEE training with greater sample sizes would, thus, be a meaningful next step. Another limitation is the limited generalizability of the training due to simulator availability. TEE is a growing technology that is expensive and not accessible at all schools. This study focused on short-term knowledge retention, but future studies could include a repeat assessment to understand if there is a decay in knowledge. Furthermore, the authors were not able to collect data regarding confidence and skills in Phase 2 for comparison with Phase 1 findings. Lastly, the authors acknowledge that there is no control group in this study and no formal external validation of the knowledge assessment took place, limiting the conclusions that can be drawn about this intervention’s efficacy compared to simply repeating the medical knowledge test.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the feasibility of the tested training method on a cohort of medical students with varied ultrasound experience. The data suggest that following this TEE training protocol with hands-on simulator practice, medical students’ knowledge, confidence, and skills in using TEE improved at various levels of training. The applied teaching protocol was more effective at building TEE confidence in the latter stages of medical training. The results of this study could assist educators in effectively implementing TEE training into the medical curricula. Further investigation with a larger sample size can help determine the best way to implement TEE training into cardiac ultrasound curricula in medical schools.
