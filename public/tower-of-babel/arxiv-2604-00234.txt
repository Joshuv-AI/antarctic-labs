Blockspace Under Pressure: An Analysis of Spam MEV on High-Throughput Blockchains
Wenhao Wang, Aditya Saraf, Lioba Heimbach, Kushal Babel, Fan Zhang

Abstract
On high-throughput, low-fee blockchains, a qualitatively new form of maximal extractable value (MEV) has emerged: searchers submit large volumes of speculative transactions, whose profitability is resolved only at execution time. We refer to this as spam MEV . On major rollups, it can at times consume more than half of block gas, even though only a small fraction of probes ultimately results in a trade. Despite growing awareness of this phenomenon, there is no principled framework for understanding how blockchain design parameters shape its prevalence and impact. We develop such a framework, modeling spam transactions competing for on-chain opportunities under a competitive equilibrium that drives their profits to zero, and deriving equilibrium spam volumes as a function of block capacity, minimum gas price, and the transaction fee mechanism. Empirical evidence from Base and Arbitrum supports the model: spam grew sharply as block capacity was scaled up and fell when minimum gas prices were introduced. Our analysis yields three main insights. First, spam is always costly: when block capacity is scarce, it displaces users and drives up gas prices; as block capacity grows, it increasingly consumes execution resources, raising network externality, i.e., the cost of provisioning and processing blocks. We show that spam takes an increasing share of each additional unit of block capacity, so capping it before all users are included creates a favorable trade-off: forgoing a small amount of user welfare eliminates disproportionate spam and externality. Second, we extend the analysis to priority fee ordering and show that ordering transactions by gas price helps reduce spam, as spammers must pay more to reach early block positions. Third, as user demand grows and blockspace is scaled accordingly, spam’s share of block capacity plateaus rather than growing indefinitely.



1
Introduction

Maximal extractable value (MEV) has been a central topic in blockchain research over the past several years
[
21
,
6
,
47
]
. Much of this work has focused on the Ethereum Layer 1, which, for a long time, was the primary home of decentralized finance (DeFi) and thus the main arena for MEV extraction. On the Ethereum Layer 1, the most widely studied MEV strategies, such as sandwich attacks, cyclic arbitrage, liquidations, and non-atomic arbitrage, are precise and targeted: a searcher identifies a specific on-chain opportunity through off-chain computation, constructs a transaction to capture it, and submits it with high confidence of success. These strategies rely on the ability to observe pending transactions in a mempool, i.e., the public waiting area for transactions awaiting inclusion, or to monitor state changes with sufficient time to react. Today, competition among searchers on Ethereum is mediated by block builder auctions
[
31
]
, in which searchers bid for the right to capture an opportunity. Only the winning transaction is included on-chain; losing bids are largely filtered out before execution, keeping most failed MEV attempts off the blockchain.

A qualitatively different form of MEV has recently emerged on high-throughput, low-fee blockchains. Rather than targeting a specific opportunity identified off-chain, searchers submit high volumes of speculative transactions whose profitability is resolved only at execution time. We refer to this class of strategies as
spam MEV
, a term that captures their speculative, high-volume nature; the phenomenon has also been called
optimistic MEV

[
48
]
or
probabilistic MEV

[
40
]
.

In spam MEV, both the detection and execution of opportunities reside largely in on-chain smart-contract logic
[
48
]
: a transaction probes whether a profitable opportunity exists at the moment of execution and, if so, captures it. When no opportunity is found, the transaction may revert or simply consume gas, i.e., the computational cost of executing transactions on the blockchain, searching without ever executing a trade.
This encompasses a range of speculative strategies, though cyclic arbitrage is the most prominently studied among them. To illustrate the contrast: as studied on the Ethereum Layer 1, a cyclic arbitrage bot precomputes a profitable path and executes a single atomic transaction, whereas on many high-throughput chains, the same type of bot speculatively submits transactions without knowing if an opportunity exists, repeatedly probing liquidity pools in the hope of capturing small gains and accepting a high failure rate in exchange for marginal profits.

First documented on Solana
[
53
]
, spam MEV has since been identified on Ethereum Layer 2 rollups, including Base, Optimism, and Arbitrum
[
48
,
30
]
. The scale of the phenomenon is striking: on Base and Optimism, spam MEV transactions consumed over 50% of block gas in Q1 2025
[
48
]
, yet only 6–12% of these speculative probes result in an actual trade. On top of that, despite consuming more than half of on-chain gas, spam MEV transactions pay less than a quarter of total fees
[
48
]
, as they carry low priority fees.

What makes spam MEV viable and prevalent? A combination of architectural and economic features common to modern high-throughput chains likely plays a role. Low transaction fees reduce the cost of failed speculative transactions, making repeated probing profitable even at low success rates. Fast block times, often below one second on rollups, can leave insufficient time for searchers to observe state changes and submit targeted transactions before the next block is produced, favoring continuous speculative submission over deliberate off-chain computation.
Similarly, most high-throughput chains lack a public mempool, e.g., rollups use centralized sequencers and Solana forwards transactions directly to the current block producer, limiting the information available for targeted extraction. Designs such as encrypted mempools
[
3
,
7
,
25
]
, when deployed, are likely to only increase spam MEV by further reducing the information available for targeted MEV strategies.

While the prevalence of spam MEV has been documented, there is no principled framework to study the interplay between design parameters and spam volumes, and the impact of spam on various stakeholders.
As a result, the response from various blockchain designers has been largely reactive and ad-hoc (c.f. Section
1.1
).

In this work, we fill this gap by developing a framework to determine the equilibrium volume of spam as a result of blockspace and fee design choices, and to analyze the impact of resulting spam on three fronts: (1) user welfare, (2) validator revenue, and (3) network externality, i.e., the cost of provisioning block capacity and processing transactions.We characterize the tradeoffs involved in design choices and provide guidance for blockchain designers to navigate these tradeoffs.

In the remainder of this section, we first highlight the industry’s response to the rise of spam MEV, describe the design levers and metrics we focus on in this work, and then summarize our contributions.

Newer chains have launched with spam in mind: Monad, for instance, sets a non-trivial minimum gas price and charges transactions based on the gas limit rather than gas consumed, making speculative transactions with large unused gas allocations more costly

These interventions share a common intuition: spam imposes costs on the network, and the costs of curtailing it are worth bearing. Yet the responses have been largely reactive and chain-specific, without formal analysis of how design parameters interact to determine the equilibrium level of spam. A key factor is that, unlike Ethereum L1 where congestion pricing naturally emerges, high-throughput chains can have slack capacity where the market-clearing gas price approaches zero, while the network still bears the cost of processing every transaction. A minimum gas price
g_{\text{min}}
ensures that each transaction pays at least a meaningful contribution toward its marginal cost and, as our analysis shows, serves as a key lever against spam in this regime.

1.2
Design Levers

We now describe the blockchain design choices that may influence spam transactions. These are the parameters that our framework analyzes.

Block Space Limit
B_{\text{max}}
.
The block space limit affects inclusion costs.
When the limit
B_{\text{max}}
is low relative to transaction demand, competition for inclusion in the block intensifies, increasing gas prices
g
and may therefore reduce the expected revenue of each spam transaction.
Conversely, when
B_{\text{max}}
is large enough to accommodate all transactions, gas prices
g
decrease, and therefore increase the expected profit of spam transactions.

Minimum Gas Price
g_{\text{min}}
.
A blockchain can impose a minimum gas price
g_{\text{min}}
per transaction, independent of block space availability. Higher values of
g_{\text{min}}
increase the cost when block capacity is slack, influencing the economic outcome for both spam and non-spam transactions. In the absence of a price floor, the equilibrium gas price
g
would approach zero when block capacity exceeds demand, resulting in a disproportionately high volume of spam transactions.

Transaction Fee Mechanism.
The design of the transaction fee mechanism (TFM) impacts transaction submission behavior.
Spam transactions often specify a large gas limit but typically consume only a fraction of it when executed without profitable arbitrage opportunities
[
48
]
.
A TFM that imposes higher costs for specifying large unused gas limits can alter transaction submission strategies.
Additionally, a TFM can dictate the ordering of transactions within a block, such as through priority fee ordering, which affects the resulting spam volumes.

1.3
Our Framework and Contributions

We develop a framework for analyzing spam MEV and the interplay with blockchain design choices.
We model spam bots as actors competing among themselves for opportunities on-chain, and study how the resulting equilibrium spam volume depends on the block space limit
B_{\text{max}}
, the minimum gas price
g_{\text{min}}
, and the transaction fee mechanism. We measure the impact of spam along three dimensions:
user welfare
(aggregate surplus of users),
validator revenue
(total fees collected from all transactions), and
network externality
(the cost borne by the network for provisioning block capacity and processing transactions). These metrics are formally defined in
Section

2
along with our model for user demand, TFM and users’ and spam bots’ utility. Our main results are:

1.

Closed-form equilibrium spam volume and gas prices
(
Section

3.1
): The equilibrium spam volume takes one of three forms: (1) no profitable entry for spam transactions, (2) an equilibrium determined entirely by
g_{\text{min}}
when slack blockspace is available and spam does not interfere with or crowd out other users, or (3) a congested equilibrium given by a closed-form expression. The three regimes are clearly characterized based on block capacity
B_{\text{max}}
. The equilibrium gas price remains at
g_{\text{min}}
in the second scenario, and is driven up by spam only in the third scenario.

2.

User welfare, revenue and externality characterization along with trade-offs
(
Section

3.2
): We analyze these metrics in equilibrium and characterize the trade-off regions between user welfare on one side, and revenue and externality on the other side. We study the loss in user welfare (and increase in revenue and externality) compared to a baseline world where no spam exists. The loss in welfare is maximized when block capacity exactly equals the quantity demanded by users at the floor price
g_{\text{min}}
, where spam causes maximum displacement.
We show that the design parameters where users are most harmed by spam is also where the network’s revenue gain from spam is largest.

3.

Parameter-setting guidance
(
Section

3.3
): We characterize the joint choice of
(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})
, showing that the effective ceiling on useful block capacity is
B_{\mathrm{plat}}
and that the optimal strategy depends on the tradeoff between user welfare and network externality. We show that as blockspace is scaled, spam consumes an increasing proportion of the marginal capacity added. Consequently, we give precise guidance on setting design parameters that significantly reduce spam at the cost of relatively small reduction in user welfare (due to low-value users).

4.

Priority fee ordering
(
Section

4
): We extend our theoretical analysis to an approximate priority fee ordering (PFO) TFM where blocks are divided into sub-blocks ordered by gas prices, with ordering in each sub-block being random. We show that priority ordering reduces spam by making early block placement expensive, and compute the equilibrium spam volume and outcome metrics.

5.

Demand-scaling analysis
(
Section

5
and
Section

7
): We tackle the question of whether and to what extent does spam pose a limit to scaling
[
41
]
. We first use real-world data from Base and Arbitrum to estimate the relationship between rise in MEV opportunity and increase in non-spam volume, finding it to be approximately linear.
We then show that when user demand grows and blockspace is scaled accordingly, spam does not vanish or explode: it remains a nontrivial share of included gas. Priority fee ordering lowers this share, but does not eliminate it.

6.

Mitigations
(
Section

6
): We draw insights from our analysis to propose practical mitigations for regimes where spam poses a concern. We divide our mitigations into two families: (1) incentive-based mechanisms (gas price floors, charging transactions based on the gas they reserve, i.e., gas-limit, rather than gas-used) and what we call cheap-path mechanisms that skip or cheaply execute the spam that does make it on-chain. The cheap path methods include filtering by the block producer if execution is not the bottleneck, and providing cancellation mechanisms in case execution is the bottleneck.

7.

Empirical evidence and case studies
(
Section

7
): Finally, we present empirical case studies of spam volumes on Base and Arbitrum over the period of January 2024–February 2026. This period is particularly interesting as these blockchains experimented with various parameter choices for minimum gas price and block capacity. We study the relationship between spam volumes (along with non-spam volumes) and the design levers in our framework.
We show that our empirical findings agree with several predictions from our theoretical model: (1) spam volume reduces when a minimum price floor is introduced or increased, with non-spam throughput remaining stable; (2) spam volume increases when block capacity is increased; (3) spam consumes a disproportionate share of the marginal blockspace as more blockspace is added; and (4) on Base spam is concentrated toward later positions in the block, matching the prediction of our PFO model.

Model

We present our model for analyzing spam MEV.
The design levers (block space limit
B_{\text{max}}
, minimum gas price
g_{\text{min}}
, and transaction fee mechanism) were introduced in
Section

1.2
.
For our transaction fee mechanism, we choose a mechanism that charges a transaction for its gas limit rather than gas used, and blockspace is counted in terms of transaction gas limits, similar to high-throughput blockchains such as Solana and Monad
[
16
]
.
This has the effect of gas charge being independent of actual execution, but our results can be straightforwardly adapted to a mechanism that charges only for the used gas (c.f. discussion in Section
6
).
For our initial analysis in Section
3
, the TFM orders transactions in a random order, mimicking the operations of a low-latency blockchain. We extend our analysis to priority-fee based ordering in Section
4
.

Now we formalize blockspace and spammers’ utility model and define the metrics that we use to evaluate the equilibrium outcomes.

2.1
Blockspace Demand and Utility Model

We analyze transaction dynamics within a single block.
Let
B\leq B_{\text{max}}
denote the total blockspace (in gas terms) in this block, and let
Q_{u}\leq B
represent the amount of included user gas, i.e., gas consumed by genuine (non-spam) transactions.
The demand from genuine users follows a demand function
D(g)
which specifies how much gas is demanded at any given gas price
g
.
Each spam transaction carries a gas limit of
s
, such that the number of spam transactions
S=(B-Q_{u})/s
.
The TFM gives a clearing price
g^{*}\geq g_{\text{min}}
that is paid by all the transactions included in the block.
A single arbitrage opportunity worth
r
is created by some user transaction and can be successfully claimed by a spam transaction if it is the first spam transaction to be sequenced after the user transaction.
We model the opportunity size
r
to be increasing in the amount of included user gas.
Looking ahead, the clearing price
g^{*}
and amount of spam
S
are endogenously determined in our analysis using a competitive equilibrium where the spammers’ net profits approach zero. The included user gas is then
Q_{u}=D(g^{*})
.

The utility of a spammer is the revenue it captures (
r
if successful and 0 otherwise) less the fees
s\cdot g^{*}
it pays.
The utility of a user transaction is defined as
u-f
(if included in the block, the utility is 0 otherwise), where
u
is its valuation of the transaction, and
f
is the gas fee it pays, such that
f<u
. Looking ahead,
u
is determined by the demand function, and
f
is determined by the clearing price
g^{*}
.

2.2
Metrics

We now describe the metrics used to quantify the impact of spam transactions on the blockchain.

Definition 2.1

(User Welfare)
.

The user welfare is defined as the aggregate utility of all users, i.e., the sum of
u_{i}-f_{i}
across all included users. As shown in
Fig.

3
for a linear demand curve, the user welfare is the area under the demand curve above the clearing price
g^{*}
.
A higher user welfare indicates a greater overall benefit to users of the blockchain.
We note that in our analysis in the following sections, user welfare is essentially the social welfare of all blockchain transaction senders, including the senders of the spam transactions, since in the equilibrium case the expected utility of each spam transaction is zero.

Definition 2.2

(Validator Revenue)
.

Validator revenue is the total gas fees collected from all included transactions (both users and spammers), written as

R=g^{*}\cdot B
,
where
g^{*}
is the equilibrium clearing gas price and
B
is total gas used. This quantity is
strictly positive
and represents the direct income to the network from transaction processing. For the purposes of this work, we do not consider burning of fees or block rewards, as they are merely a way to change allocation of revenue between token holders and validators, and do not affect the net economic incentives for spamming or the overall welfare analysis.

Definition 2.3

(Network Externality)
.

The network externality is the cost borne by the network for provisioning block capacity and processing transactions, written as

E(B_{\text{max}})=c_{1}B_{\text{max}}+c_{2}B,

where
c_{1}
is the per-unit cost of providing block capacity (e.g., bandwidth provisioning, data availability, node hardware), and
c_{2}
is the per-unit cost of computation (e.g., execution, state access). The capacity component
c_{1}B_{\text{max}}
depends on the provisioned capacity, not actual usage, while the compute component
c_{2}B
scales with actual gas consumed. Network externality also captures costs such as increased barriers to decentralization, which are not easily quantifiable but are important considerations in blockchain design, especially as it relates to spam transactions.

3
Analysis with Random Ordering

In this section, we study a simple random-ordering TFM, which is intentionally minimal.
There is a block of capacity
B_{\text{max}}
, a single opportunity of value
r
, and a volume of spam transactions that try to claim that opportunity.
Each spam transaction reserves
s
gas and is charged based on its gas
limit
, not on ex post gas used.
Unless specified otherwise, we use the simple linear demand function (
D(g)=D_{0}-\beta g
) for non-spam users’ gas consumption to expose the equilibrium characteristics.
Recall that
Q_{u}
denotes the amount of user gas included in the block.
In this section, we assume that the opportunity size
r
is linear in the included user gas, given by
r=\frac{Q_{u}}{D_{0}}\cdot r_{0}
.
Finally, we impose a floor
g_{\text{min}}
, which lets us study the design choice of a minimum inclusion price.

3.1
Spam Volume at Equilibrium

The first quantity we need is the probability that spam transactions claim the opportunity.
Suppose there are
S
spam transactions in the block.
Only the relative position of the opportunity among these
S
spam transactions matters.
For example, when
S=3
, we can visualize the situation by placing the three spam
transactions as triangles and considering the four possible slots where the opportunity
can appear:

The circles represent the possible positions where the opportunity can appear relative to the spam transactions.
In the first three positions, there is at least one spam transaction after the opportunity, so a spam transaction can claim it.
In the last position, the opportunity appears after all spam transactions, so it cannot be claimed.
More generally, there are
S+1

Therefore,

\Pr[\text{opportunity claimed by spam}]=\frac{S}{S+1}.

We next compute the clearing price at a fixed spam volume.
Recall that the user demand curve is characterized by
D=D_{0}-\beta\cdot g
where
g
is the gas price.
In the absence of spam, the market-clearing price implied by the demand curve and block size
B_{\text{max}}
is

g_{1}:=\frac{D_{0}-B_{\text{max}}}{\beta}
.
However, since the protocol enforces a price floor
g_{\text{min}}
, the actual clearing price without spam is
\max\{g_{\text{min}},g_{1}\}
.
If
S
spam transactions are included in the block and each reserves
s
gas, then the remaining space available to non-spam users is
B_{\text{max}}-Ss
.
The resulting clearing price is

g(S)=\max\left\{g_{\text{min}},g_{1}+\frac{s}{\beta}S\right\}.

The term
g_{1}
is the baseline price implied by demand when there is no spam, while the term
\frac{s}{\beta}S
captures the price increase caused by spam taking up block space.
The outer maximum applies the protocol’s price floor.

Given this price, the total expected spam utility at spam volume
S
is

u(S)=\frac{S}{S+1}r-Ssg(S).

The first term is the expected MEV revenue.
The second term is aggregate inclusion cost.
A competitive equilibrium is a spam level
S^{\ast}
at which further entry is not profitable.
Solving the zero-profit condition (
u(S)=0
) gives

S^{\ast}=\begin{cases}0,&\displaystyle r_{0}\frac{Q_{u}^{0}}{D_{0}}\leq sg^{0},\\[8.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{r_{0}D(g_{\text{min}})}{D_{0}sg_{\text{min}}}-1,&\displaystyle r_{0}\frac{Q_{u}^{0}}{D_{0}}>sg^{0}\text{ and }g^{0}=g_{\text{min}},\\[12.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\bigl(s-\Delta+\beta r_{0}/D_{0}\bigr)^{2}+4\beta r_{0}}-\bigl(s+\Delta+\beta r_{0}/D_{0}\bigr)}{2s},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
\Delta=D_{0}-B_{\text{max}}
,
g^{0}=\max\{g_{\text{min}},g_{1}\}
is the clearing price in the absence of spam, and

Q_{u}^{0}=\min\!\left\{B_{\text{max}},\;D(g_{\text{min}})\right\}

is the amount of user gas included in the absence of spam.

The first case says that spam does not enter when the effective opportunity value in the spam-free world is below the per-transaction inclusion cost.
The second case is the slack-at-the-floor regime, where the gas price remains
g_{\text{min}}
, and the block is not congested.
In this regime, spam depends on
r_{0}
,
g_{\text{min}}
, and the amount of non-spam demand that remains at the floor.
The third case is the congested regime, where spam raises the clearing price above the floor.
In this regime, spam depends on the baseline opportunity parameter
r_{0}
, the block size, and user demand.

(Connection to Mazorra et al.)
.

In the slack case,
S^{\ast}=r/(sg_{\text{min}})-1=1/c-1
where
c=(D_{0}sg_{\text{min}})/(r_{0}\cdot D(g_{\text{min}}))
is the cost-to-reward ratio.
This matches the lower bound on total spam from the timing game model of Mazorra et al.
[

, who show that equilibrium spam in an

[1/c-1,1/c]
and converges to

as

Our competitive equilibrium corresponds to this large-

[
40
]
.

For our welfare analysis later, we now derive the minimum blockspace, denoted by
B_{\mathrm{plat}}
, needed for the clearing gas price to remain at the floor
g_{\text{min}}
.
Looking ahead, the benefit of provisioning blockspace plateaus out beyond
B_{\mathrm{plat}}
.

B_{\mathrm{plat}}
can be written as

B_{\mathrm{plat}}=D(g_{\text{min}})+\left(\frac{r_{0}D(g_{\text{min}})}{D_{0}g_{\text{min}}}-s\right)_{+}.

In this equation, the first term is the amount of non-spam demand that remains at the floor price.
The second term is the equilibrium spam volume at the floor, converted into gas units.
Therefore,
B_{\mathrm{plat}}
is exactly the amount of block space needed to fit both user demand at
g_{\text{min}}
and the spam that is still profitable at that price.
For
B_{\text{max}}<B_{\mathrm{plat}}
, the block is still effectively scarce, so adding capacity lowers the clearing price and increases both user welfare and spam volume.
Once
B_{\text{max}}
reaches
B_{\mathrm{plat}}
, the gas price settles at the floor.
At that point, spam equilibrium volume reaches its peak level and no longer changes with further increases in block capacity
B_{\text{max}}
.

3.2
Welfare, Revenue, and Externality Analysis

We next study how the metrics in

vary with the design parameters.
To assess the impact of spam, we complement this analysis with a comparison between two worlds evaluated at the same parameter values: (1) the realized world with spam, and (2) a counterfactual world without spam.
Holding parameters fixed isolates the effect of spam on users, validators, and the cost of the blockchain to process blocks.
Our analysis here, especially on welfare and externality, provides the quantities needed later for choosing blockchain parameters wisely.
The comparison with the counterfactual spam-free world reveals which parameter regimes suffer the most from spam.

Spam world and spam-free world.
In the baseline world without spam, all block space is available to users. The gas price is therefore

g^{0}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})=\max\left\{g_{\text{min}},\frac{D_{0}-B_{\text{max}}}{\beta}\right\}
,
and the amount of user gas included in the block is
Q_{u}^{0}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}}):=\min\left\{B_{\text{max}},D(g_{\text{min}})\right\}
.

In the spam world, some of the block space may be taken up by spam.
Using the equilibrium spam volume
S^{\ast}
from
Section

3.1
, the amount of user gas that gets included is

Q_{u}^{\ast}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}}):=\min\left\{B_{\text{max}}-sS^{\ast}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}}),D(g_{\text{min}})\right\}.

Therefore, users get only the space that is left after spam.
We note that
Q_{u}^{\ast}
is always less or equal to
Q_{u}^{0}
, and the gap between
Q_{u}^{0}
and
Q_{u}^{\ast}
is the amount of useful block space that spam takes away from users.

User welfare.
Recall that the user welfare is the sum of the utility of the users.
Under the linear demand model, it depends only on how much user gas gets into the block.
Therefore, in the spam-free world,

W_{\text{user}}^{0}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})=\frac{(Q_{u}^{0}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}}))^{2}}{2\beta}
, and the user welfare in the spam world is
W_{\text{user}}^{\ast}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})=\frac{(Q_{u}^{\ast}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}}))^{2}}{2\beta}
.
The gap

\Delta W_{\text{user}}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})=W_{\text{user}}^{\ast}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})-W_{\text{user}}^{0}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})

measures the user-welfare loss caused by spam.
This delta is zero when spam does not enter, or when the block size is large enough that spam does not block user inclusion, and is negative otherwise. We also characterize when the loss is maximum in
Proposition

3.2
.

Validator revenue.
The validator revenue is how much gas is sold times the clearing price.
Without spam, they earn revenue only from users:

R^{0}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})=g^{0}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})Q_{u}^{0}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})
.
In the spam world, let

G^{\ast}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}}):=D(g^{\ast}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}}))+sS^{\ast}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})
denote the total gas sold in equilibrium.
Then, the validator revenue in the spam world is
R^{\ast}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})=g^{\ast}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})G^{\ast}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})
.
The revenue change caused by spam is

\Delta R_{\text{val}}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})=R^{\ast}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})-R^{0}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})
.
Note that this term is always positive, meaning revenue always increases with spam, even when users are worse off, since spam can raise the gas price, or fill the otherwise empty block space, or do both.

Externality.
We note that when choosing the parameters, we cannot ignore the cost that the parameter choices have on the overall blockchain ecosystem.
Along the lines of Buterin
[
12
]
, we model the ecosystem cost of processing blocks as

E(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})=c_{1}B_{\text{max}}+c_{2}G
,
where
c_{1}
is the cost of provisioning the block capacity and
c_{2}
is the per-gas execution cost.
In the baseline spam-free world,
E^{0}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})=c_{1}B_{\text{max}}+c_{2}Q_{u}^{0}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})
,
while the externality realized in the presence of spam is

E^{\ast}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})=c_{1}B_{\text{max}}+c_{2}G^{\ast}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})
.
The externality increase caused by spam is therefore

\Delta E(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})=E^{\ast}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})-E^{0}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})=c_{2}\bigl(G^{\ast}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})-Q_{u}^{0}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})\bigr).

The capacity term cancels because both worlds use the same
B_{\text{max}}
.
So
\Delta E
measures the extra execution workload created by spam.
Note that this additional burden is not only borne by the validators, but the overall ecosystem, such as higher hardware and bandwidth requirements for full nodes, which has second order externalities on decentralization.

3.3
Parameter-Setting Guidance

The analysis above gives the system designer two main parameters against spam, namely the block size
B_{\text{max}}
and the price floor
g_{\text{min}}
.
These two parameters affect user inclusion, validator revenue, and the system cost of supporting larger blocks.
A good choice should therefore increase user welfare, but should not keep increasing
B_{\text{max}}
once the extra capacity mostly creates spam or idle space. Figures
5
and
6
depict the tradeoff between welfare on one side, and revenue and externality on the other side.
We address each parameter in turn: here we first discuss choosing
B_{\text{max}}
for a fixed
g_{\text{min}}
, and we discuss choosing
g_{\text{min}}
for a fixed
B_{\text{max}}
in
Appendix

A
.

We present two rules for choosing
B_{\text{max}}
given a fixed gas price floor
g_{\text{min}}
: a simple baseline that scales to the point where user welfare plateaus, and a refined rule that stops earlier by requiring that marginal capacity primarily serves users.

A baseline rule: stop scaling at the plateau
B_{\mathrm{plat}}
.
Choose a gas price floor
g_{\text{min}}
.
For this fixed floor, user welfare increases with
B_{\text{max}}
until the system reaches the slack threshold
B_{\mathrm{plat}}(g_{\text{min}})
.
At this point, the block has enough space to fit both the non-spam demand that remains at the floor and the spam that is still profitable at that floor.
Once
B_{\text{max}}\geq B_{\mathrm{plat}}(g_{\text{min}})
, further increase in block size does not increase user welfare, because the user side is already capped by
D(g_{\text{min}})
.
The revenue also does not increase, and only the externality increases.
Therefore, if the designer fixes
g_{\text{min}}
and wants to maximize user welfare without creating unnecessary externality, the natural choice is

B_{\text{max}}^{\dagger}(g_{\text{min}})=B_{\mathrm{plat}}(g_{\text{min}}).

This gives a relatively simple benchmark rule: for a fixed gas price floor
g_{\text{min}}
, increase block size until the user welfare plateaus.
2
2

2

In practice, the designer may want to keep some slack in blockspace—as allowed by the bound on externality—to accommodate bursts in user demand, i.e., demand function steepening in our model. Some blockchains do this by choosing a block capacity larger than the demand at
g_{\text{min}}
, so that demand spikes during volatile periods can be absorbed without congestion.

A refined rule: require that marginal capacity does not admit disproportionate spam.
The benchmark rule
B_{\text{max}}^{\dagger}(g_{\text{min}})=B_{\mathrm{plat}}(g_{\text{min}})
is useful, but it is still too permissive in practice.
It allows block size to increase as long as user welfare has not yet completely flattened.
As we show in Figure
7(a)
, the proportion of spam to non-spam transactions added at the margin keeps increasing with increase in blockspace.
Therefore, in practice, the designer may want a stricter rule: if block size is increased by a small amount, a sufficient fraction of that extra capacity should go to useful user gas rather than to spam.
To make this precise, fix a step size
\Delta B>0
and define the marginal user share

m_{\text{user}}:=\frac{Q_{u}^{\ast}(B_{\text{max}}+\Delta B,g_{\text{min}})-Q_{u}^{\ast}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})}{\Delta B},

which measures what fraction of the next
\Delta B
units of capacity goes to useful user gas.
Once
B_{\text{max}}\geq B_{\mathrm{plat}}
,
m_{\text{user}}
becomes zero, because extra capacity is no longer used by either users or spam.

We now introduce a design parameter
\eta\in(0,1]
, which we call the
minimum marginal user share
(MMUS).
It means that when the designer increases
B_{\text{max}}
, at least an
\eta
fraction of the extra capacity should go to users.
For example,
\eta=0.8
means that at least
80\%
of the next unit of block capacity must go to useful user gas.
Given a fixed
g_{\text{min}}
, we choose
B_{\text{max}}
by
B_{\text{max}}^{\ast}(g_{\text{min}},\eta)\in\arg\max_{B_{\text{max}}}\;W_{\text{user}}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})

subject to

m_{\text{user}}\geq\eta
.
Because
W_{\text{user}}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})
is nondecreasing in
B_{\text{max}}
, this rule picks the largest block size for which the next unit of capacity still contributes at least an
\eta
fraction to users.
Beyond that point, further increases in
B_{\text{max}}
are not justified by this MMUS criterion.

This refined rule is a direct extension of the plateau rule.
Since user welfare is flat once
B_{\text{max}}\geq B_{\mathrm{plat}}
, we must have
B_{\text{max}}^{\ast}(g_{\text{min}},\eta)\leq B_{\mathrm{plat}}(g_{\text{min}})
whenever
\eta>0
.
If
\eta
is set very low, then
B_{\text{max}}^{\ast}(g_{\text{min}},\eta)
approaches the benchmark choice
B_{\mathrm{plat}}(g_{\text{min}})
.
If
\eta
is increased, the chosen block size moves leftward, because the rule now requires that the next unit of capacity mainly help users rather than spam.
This makes
\eta
an explicit conservativeness parameter.
Low
\eta
allows more aggressive scaling.
High
\eta
yields a stricter design that stops scaling earlier.

When
\Delta B\to 0
, the user gas share of marginal capacity becomes

m_{\text{user}}=\frac{\partial Q_{u}^{\ast}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})}{\partial B_{\text{max}}}.

Inside the entry-and-congested region, this derivative can be written as

m_{\text{user}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{B_{\text{max}}-D_{0}+s+\beta r_{0}/D_{0}}{\sqrt{\bigl(B_{\text{max}}-D_{0}+s+\beta r_{0}/D_{0}\bigr)^{2}+4\beta r_{0}}}\right).

This expression is strictly decreasing in
B_{\text{max}}
as shown in
Proposition

3.3
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Fix
g_{\text{min}}
and suppose the equilibrium is in the entry-and-congested region.
Let
g^{\ast}
denote the gas price at equilibrium.
Then, for any twice continuously differentiable and strictly decreasing demand curve
D(g)
, if
g^{\ast}D(g^{\ast})D^{\prime\prime}(g^{\ast})+2D(g^{\ast})D^{\prime}(g^{\ast})-2g^{\ast}\bigl(D^{\prime}(g^{\ast})\bigr)^{2}<0
(
D^{\prime}
is
\frac{\mathrm{d}D}{\mathrm{d}g}
), (which holds for the linear demand function), then
m_{\text{user}}
is decreasing in
B_{\text{max}}
.

The proof can be found in
Appendix

C
.
We note that the condition in
Proposition

3.3
holds for the linear demand function, and for the exponential demand function (i.e.,
D(g)=D_{0}\cdot e^{-\lambda g})
as well.

4
Analysis with (Approximate) Priority Fee Ordering

We now turn to a TFM with priority fee ordering (PFO), where transactions are executed in decreasing order of their bids rather than randomly.
This is promising for reducing spam, as in the random-ordering model, spam transactions can occupy early positions in the block without paying more than others, whereas under PFO, such placement becomes costly.

To capture the effects of PFO, we analyze a tractable approximation that extends the random-ordering model in
Section

3
.
Concretely, we assume that the block is divided into
n
sub-blocks of equal capacity.
These sub-blocks represent increasingly late execution positions in the block.
Transactions that choose to pay for priority are sorted into these sub-blocks by their priority bids, with higher bids placed earlier.
Within each sub-block, however, the order of transactions is still random.
Each transaction in a priority sub-block pays the minimum price required for inclusion in that sub-block.

For example, if
n=2
, then priority transactions with higher bids are placed in the first sub-block and are executed before priority transactions in the second sub-block.
Transactions that do not compete for priority are placed only after all priority transactions, in the lowest occupied part of the block.
They all pay the minimum fee needed to be included in the block, rather than the higher price needed to obtain an earlier execution slot.

In general, the block therefore consists of two regions.
The upper region is priority-ordered: transactions there are divided across sub-blocks according to their priority bids, and higher-priority sub-blocks execute first.
The lower region is inclusion-only: transactions there do not compete for earlier execution and instead pay the common minimum inclusion price for the block.
The rest of the model stays the same.
In particular, we still assume that the opportunity is randomly and independently located among the included user gas.

To capture the fact that not all users wish to compete for priority, we assume that a fraction
v\in[0,1]
of users opt into the priority market, while the remaining fraction
1-v
seek only inclusion.
Users always bid honestly relative to their chosen service class.
Priority-seeking users bid according to their willingness to pay for earlier execution, while inclusion-only users submit the minimum price needed to enter the block.

Let
\bar{g}
denote the minimum price needed for inclusion in the block, i.e., the clearing price of the last occupied sub-block.
Conditional on this
\bar{g}
, the demand of priority-seeking users is

D^{\mathrm{T}}(\bar{g})=vD(\bar{g}),

while the demand of inclusion-only users is

D^{\mathrm{L}}(\bar{g})=(1-v)D(\bar{g}).

Thus, only the
vD(\bar{g})
priority-seeking users contribute to the price ladder across earlier sub-blocks.
The remaining
(1-v)D(\bar{g})
users appear only in the lowest-price occupied part of the block, and all of them pay the common inclusion price
\bar{g}
.

Spam bots are modeled analogously.
A spam attempt can either compete for an earlier execution position or seek inclusion only.
In our approximate model, a spam transaction assigned to sub-block
i
should therefore be interpreted as a spam bid just high enough to obtain the execution priority of that sub-block.
This lets the equilibrium endogenously determine how much spam enters the expensive priority region and how much remains in the low-price inclusion region.

Finally, like in the previous section, we assume that the opportunity value scales linearly with total included user gas:

\bar{r}=r_{0}\cdot\frac{Q_{u}}{D_{0}},

where
Q_{u}
is the total user gas included in the block.
This captures the idea that more user activity creates more MEV opportunities, but that spam also reduces those opportunities when it displaces users.

4.1
Competitive spam equilibrium

In a competitive equilibrium, the utility of spam within each sub-block is zero, similar to
Section

3
.
This lets us compute equilibrium spam volume iteratively, from the top sub-block downward.
In the interest of space, we defer the detailed steps to derive the equilibrium to
Section

B.1
.

Figure
8
shows that PFO (with
n=500
sub-blocks) reduces spam volume relative to the fully random benchmark, but the size of the reduction depends critically on
v
, the fraction of users who bid for early execution rather than only for inclusion.
The effect of priority fee ordering on spam volume comes from the price separation it creates across sub-blocks.
When
v
is large, PFO reduces spam relative to the random-ordering benchmark, because early sub-blocks become expensive and spammers must pay more to place transactions near the top of the block.
As more users compete for priority, the price gap between early and late sub-blocks becomes larger, which makes it harder for spammers to spread attempts across the block and lowers the spam volume.

At the same time, the figure also shows that PFO does not always reduce spam.
In particular, when
v=0
, spam can be higher than under random ordering.
We note that this should be read mainly as an artifact of our approximate PFO model.
The reason is that sub-blocks give spammers more control over where to place their attempts. Even if early sub-blocks contain no spam, it is still profitable to place spam in the last sub-block, since those transactions can capture opportunities that survive from earlier parts of the block while paying only the lower inclusion price of the last sub-block.
By contrast, in the random-ordering case, first-unit entry may fail to be profitable altogether when the opportunity value is too small relative to the single clearing price.
Thus, PFO helps only when enough user demand is actually competing for priority.

Takeaway (Priority fee ordering).

Priority fee ordering is effective at reducing spam when many users pay for priority. When few do, most occupied sub-blocks clear at the common inclusion price
\bar{g}
, leaving spammers with relatively cheap access to early execution slots.

The main pattern is that a larger

At the same time, validator revenue rises in both the spam and no-spam worlds because a larger share of user demand is served through high-price sub-blocks.

The behavior of user welfare is different.
A higher

As a result, user welfare tends to be lower at higher

At the same time, for any fixed
v
, the gap between the spam and no-spam user-welfare curves shrinks as

The reason is that when blockspace becomes sufficiently abundant, spam no longer displaces users: the additional spam is absorbed by the extra capacity, so the user side converges to the no-spam benchmark.

Those curves are much closer across different values of

The reason is that changing

mainly reallocates surplus between users and the validator.
When
v
is high, users pay more for priority and validator revenue rises, but much of this increase comes from a transfer out of user surplus rather than from a change in the total value created by serving users.
Thus, once we add user welfare and validator revenue together, much of the variation across

In the spam world, the curves are not exactly identical, because

also affects equilibrium spam, and therefore changes both the amount of user gas served and the amount of spam fees collected.
But the remaining differences are much smaller than in the separate

and

5
Impact of Spam on Blockchain Scaling

One of the central concerns with spam (e.g., the one of Flashbots
[
41
]
) is that it may
limit scaling
, as additional block capacity could simply be consumed by spammers instead of benefiting users.
We now study whether and to what extent spam may limit the benefit of scaling to be passed down to a rise in user demand.

According to empirical data (see
Appendix

D
), on both Base and Arbitrum, the total size of MEV profit grows approximately linearly in non-MEV gas.
Accordingly, in this section, we assume that the opportunity value scales linearly with included user gas.

To formalize the problem further, let
\lambda\geq 1
denote a demand-scaling parameter.
We scale non-spam demand as

D_{\lambda}(g)=\lambda(D_{0}-\beta g)
,
so that the number of users at every price level grows proportionally with
\lambda
.
The realized opportunity value is then assumed to be

\bar{r}_{\lambda}=r_{0}\cdot\frac{Q_{u}}{D_{0}},

where
Q_{u}
is the equilibrium amount of included user gas under the scaled demand curve.
Thus, as more user gas is included, the MEV opportunity grows proportionally.
Additionally, let
\rho_{\mathrm{spam}}
denote the
spam share of included gas
, i.e., spam gas divided by total included gas.

5.1
Spam Volume Share under Demand Scaling with Random Ordering

We begin with the random-ordering benchmark and the two block-size rules from
Section

3.3
.
Under the baseline parameter choice, the designer sets
B_{\text{max}}
equal to the plateau threshold under the scaled demand curve.
Since all users willing to pay the floor are then included, the opportunity value at the plateau is

\bar{r}_{\lambda}^{\dagger}=r_{0}\cdot D_{\lambda}(g_{\text{min}})/D_{0},

and the corresponding plateau block size is

B_{\mathrm{plat},\lambda}=D_{\lambda}(g_{\text{min}})+\left(\frac{r_{0}D_{\lambda}(g_{\text{min}})}{D_{0}g_{\text{min}}}-s\right)_{+}.

Let
S_{\lambda}^{\dagger}
denote the equilibrium spam volume at
B_{\text{max}}=B_{\mathrm{plat},\lambda}
.
The spam share of included gas under the baseline rule is then

\rho_{\mathrm{spam}}^{\dagger}(\lambda)=\frac{s\,S_{\lambda}^{\dagger}}{B_{\mathrm{plat},\lambda}},

where the equality follows because the block is fully used at the plateau.

We also study how the refined block-size rule in
Section

3.3
behaves under the same scaling model.
Fix a floor
g_{\text{min}}
and a target
\eta\in(0,1]
for the minimum marginal user share.
Let
B_{\text{max}}^{\ast}(g_{\text{min}},\eta)
denote the resulting MMUS block size under the scaled demand curve, and let
S_{\lambda}^{\ast}
be the corresponding equilibrium spam volume.
The spam share of included gas is then

\rho_{\mathrm{spam}}^{\ast}(\lambda)=\frac{s\,S_{\lambda}^{\ast}}{B_{\text{max}}^{\ast}(g_{\text{min}},\eta)},

where, under the refined rule, the block is still in the congested region and so total included gas equals
B_{\text{max}}^{\ast}(g_{\text{min}},\eta)
.

Figure
11
plots
\rho_{\mathrm{spam}}^{\dagger}(\lambda)
and
\rho_{\mathrm{spam}}^{\ast}(\lambda)
for
\lambda\in[1,50]
, with
\eta=0.6
and a fixed price floor
g_{\text{min}}=20
.
The main pattern is that, under linear opportunity scaling, the spam share rises at first and then plateaus at a positive level.
The intuition is simple: both user demand at the floor and the induced MEV opportunity grow linearly with
\lambda
, so the amount of profitable spam also scales linearly.
As a result, scaling does not drive the spam share to zero.
Instead, the system approaches a regime in which spam remains a stable fraction of included gas.

We also observe that, for the same
\lambda

The reason is that the MMUS rule stops scaling earlier, namely at the point where the marginal benefit of extra capacity becomes too spam-heavy.
Thus, the refined rule does not eliminate spam under scaling, but it does keep the spam share systematically below the plateau-sizing benchmark.

Spam Volume Share under Demand Scaling with Approximate Priority Fee Ordering

We next ask whether priority fee ordering changes the scaling picture.
To isolate the effect of ordering, we keep the same block-size rule as above.

The case

6
Mitigations to Spam

Our theoretical analysis highlights that spam transactions consume block space yet produce essentially no state changes.
In the model, this appears as a reduction in useful block space, meaning less gas is left for non-spam users, which lowers user welfare.
This motivates mitigations that do not treat spam as a binary “attack” to be eliminated, but instead as a form of
resource abuse
: most spam transactions consume resources without producing useful output.

This view also matches what we see in practice.
Even when spam is not a consensus safety threat, sustained no-effect traffic can still create real operational pressure on RPC providers, indexers, and explorers, and some infrastructure operators have publicly downgraded support during spam on Base
[
36
,
35
,
24
]
.
At the protocol and client level, multiple ecosystems (including Base, Aptos, and Arbitrum) have responded by making low-value traffic more expensive via fee floors or minimum gas budgets, which can be interpreted as a simple incentive-based mechanism against high-volume probing
[
8
,
57
,
5
,
4
]
.

We organize mitigations into two families.
The first family makes probing more expensive at the margin so that competitive entry yields less spam volume, which we call
incentive-based mechanisms
.
The second family accepts that spam will exist, but tries to make failed probes cheaper to process than full on-chain execution, which we call
cheap-path mechanisms
.
We note that in practice, one can combine approaches in both families.

6.1
Incentive-based mechanisms

Incentive-based mechanisms reduce spam by increasing the expected cost of (failed) spam transactions, so that in the equilibrium, fewer spam attempts are sent to the blockchain.
These mechanisms do not require perfectly identifying spam, and they can be deployed as coarse policies that target the main source of externality, which is resource consumption.

Minimum gas prices.
A simple mechanism is to set a nontrivial floor gas price (i.e., increasing
g_{\text{min}}
).
As discussed in
Section

1.1
, several chains have adopted nontrivial fee floors, some from launch and others in response to observed spam
[
8
,
57
,
5
,
4
,
50
]
.
This is consistent with our analysis in
Section

3
: a sufficiently high floor gas price caps spam volume and preserves useful block space for users. We provide empirical evidence for this effect in
Section

7
.

Charging for reserved execution gas.
Another deterrence lever is to charge based on
gas limit
rather than ex post gas used.
This directly targets spam strategies that intentionally revert early or do minimal work when they fail.
In our model (
Section

3
), this changes the cost of each attempt from “pay only when you win” toward “pay for the capacity you force others to keep available”, which reduces equilibrium spam volume. Monad, for instance, adopted this approach
[
16
]
.

6.2
Cheap-path mechanisms

Cheap-path mechanisms aim to keep the cost borne by the system from failed spam transactions much smaller than the cost of regularly executing them on-chain.
The design choice depends on whether execution is the bottleneck for the block producer, i.e., the sequencer on a rollup or validators on Layer-1.
If execution is not the bottleneck, the block producer can afford to spend extra work on simulation and filtering.
If execution is the bottleneck, the design should instead avoid executing failed spam transactions altogether.

Filtering when execution is not the bottleneck.
When the block producer can simulate transactions off the critical path, it can filter out those that produce no state changes before including them in a block.
On Ethereum, builders and relays already simulate candidate transactions as part of the MEV-Boost pipeline
[
28
]
, and enshrined proposer-builder separation would make this role explicit at the protocol level
[
13
]
.
On rollups, the centralized sequencer can similarly simulate the ordered stream and drop no-effect transactions before posting data to Layer 1, saving data availability costs.
Unichain, for instance, implements revert protection: the sequencer simulates transactions and excludes those that would revert, so users do not pay gas for failed attempts
[
54
]
.
A practical concern is that these block producers themselves become targets for spam, as searchers flood them with candidate transactions to simulate. Reputation systems, rate limits, or even out-of-band transaction fees at the block producer level can mitigate this, similar to the operational protections that relays already implement today
[
28
]
.
A further design constraint is auditability: third parties must be able to verify that the block producer did not selectively drop valid transactions under the guise of filtering.

Cancellation when execution is the bottleneck.
When execution resources are scarce, the more direct approach is to allow a failed spam transaction to be
cancelled
without executing it fully, provided the cancellation is justified in a verifiable way.
One possible design is a proof-carrying cancellation rule, where the sender (or a third-party prover) provides evidence that the transaction would lead to no state changes, and the system charges a smaller but nonzero fee.
Because per-transaction verification (e.g., using ZKP) can be expensive when failed probes are short, a practical variant batches many “no-effect” proofs into a single aggregate proof per block or per sender, amortizing verification while still charging per cancelled attempt for its gas usage.

7
Empirical Analysis

Our previous analysis predicts that spam absorbs a disproportionate share of marginal block capacity (
B_{\text{max}}
) and that the gas price floor (
g_{\text{min}}
) directly gates spam profitability. We now examine how these two design levers affect spam in practice by analyzing daily spam gas on two major Ethereum Layer 2 rollups, Base and Arbitrum, which have undergone significant changes to both parameters during our observation period.

7.1
Data and methodology

We collect 790 daily observations per chain from January 2024 through February 2026 using Dune Analytics. To classify spam, we use an approach similar to the methodology employed by Flashbots
[
41
]
and consistent with the optimistic probing behavior described by Solmaz et al.
[
48
]
: we identify contracts that interact with decentralized exchanges (DEXes) but frequently fail to execute trades, i.e., they probe for arbitrage opportunities without producing ERC-20 token transfers. Using on-chain traces, we find all transactions where a contract makes an internal call to a known DEX pool or router address. For each such transaction, we check whether it produced at least one ERC-20 transfer event.
Contracts that interact with DEXes but produce no token transfer are probing but not trading. For each contract and month, we compute the fraction of DEX-interacting transactions that produced a transfer. Contracts with a transfer rate below 50% and at least 10 interactions are classified as spam candidates. We rank candidates by total gas consumed and conservatively retain only the top 100 per month. We then attribute all daily transactions from these contracts to spam transactions.
Non-spam gas is the total gas minus spam gas.

Remark 7.1

(A note on spam volume with PFO and random ordering.)
.

At first glance, the spam volume on Base and Arbitrum may look in tension with the theoretical analysis of priority fee ordering: one may expect a chain with priority-based ordering (i.e., Base) to exhibit less spam than a chain closer to FCFS (i.e., Arbitrum).
However, our data should not be interpreted that way.
The PFO analysis in
Section

4
is a comparative-static result that holds other design levers fixed.
Base and Arbitrum differ along several dimensions that also affect spam, including gas price floors, block capacity, user demand, MEV opportunities, etc. These differences are large enough that a raw cross-chain comparison does not isolate the effect of ordering alone. Indeed, in later parts of our sample, Arbitrum’s spam share is sometimes above Base’s, which is consistent with the view that ordering is only one lever among several. For this reason, we treat the empirical evidence in this section as evidence about how spam responds to within-chain changes in capacity and gas price floors.

Spam location within blocks on Base.

For each month from January 2024 through June 2025, we identify candidate spam contracts using the same DEX-probing heuristic as above.
We then collect all transactions sent to these contracts in the same month, rank them by transaction index within the block after excluding the system transaction, and for each

7.5
Summary

Spam scales super-linearly with block capacity (elasticity of 2.27) and disproportionately fills marginal capacity. Reducing the gas target reduces spam but also reduces non-spam throughput. The minimum gas price affects the two types of gas differently: on Base, the gas price floor steps that reduced spam did so while non-spam gas remained stable or grew. On Arbitrum, the fee doubling did not produce a lasting reduction, possibly because the increase was too small relative to spam profitability. These patterns are consistent with our theoretical analysis: when fees are low and capacity is abundant, increasing the gas price floor or reducing capacity can significantly gate spam without compromising non-spam throughput, corroborating the mitigation mechanisms discussed in
Section

6
.

Related Work

Empirics of spam MEV.
Spam MEV was first documented on Solana by Umbra Research
[
53
]
, who observed high failure rates among MEV transactions and provided early evidence that high-throughput, low-fee chains incentivize redundant submissions over priority-fee competition. Solmaz et al.
[
48
]
study optimistic cyclic arbitrage patterns on Ethereum Layer 2 rollups and find that cyclic arbitrage bots consumed over 50% of gas on Base and Optimism by early 2025, with only 6–12% of probes resulting in a trade. Gogol et al.
[
30
]
study reverted transactions on five rollups, finding that revert rates rose sharply after Dencun; by restricting to reverts, they capture one subtype of spam implementation and strategy, as not all unsuccessful probes revert. These works are primarily empirical. Our work develops a framework that characterizes how block capacity, gas price floors, and the transaction fee mechanism jointly determine spam volume, and analyzes the resulting impact on user welfare, validator revenue, and network externality. We complement the analysis with empirical evidence from Base and Arbitrum.

Modeling spam MEV.
Concurrent with our work, Mazorra et al.
[
40
]
model spam as a timing game among a finite number of searchers competing for an opportunity, showing that timing competition fully dissipates expected profits. Their equilibrium spam volume in the large-
n
limit corresponds exactly to ours, despite the models being derived independently. The two models are complementary: Mazorra et al. characterize when and how often each searcher submits in the timing game, while we focus on how protocol design parameters, i.e., block capacity, gas price floors, and transaction ordering, jointly shape spam volume and its welfare and externality implications.

In related work, Capponi and Zhu
[
15
]
model costly duplicate submissions as a latency race among traders competing for time-sensitive opportunities on blockchains, framing the problem as an observable analogue of HFT latency investment. They show that a time-priority auction (Timeboost) reduces redundant submissions and reallocates the waste into platform revenue, and validate this prediction on Arbitrum using a difference-in-differences design. Their focus is on a specific auction mechanism as mitigation, whereas we study how general design parameters, i.e., block capacity, gas price floors, and transaction ordering, jointly shape spam volume and welfare.

Targeted MEV.
Eskandari et al.
[
23
]
taxonomized front-running attacks on Ethereum, and Daian et al.
[
21

broadened the scope to MEV more generally, documenting priority gas auctions among searchers. Qin et al.
[
47

and Torres et al.
[

subsequently quantified sandwich attacks, arbitrage, and liquidations at scale. A large body of further work has measured specific strategies and infrastructure on the Ethereum Layer 1

9
Conclusions and Future Directions

We develop a framework for analyzing spam MEV under a competitive equilibrium, deriving equilibrium spam volumes and characterizing the impact on user welfare, validator revenue, and network externality. Empirical evidence from Base and Arbitrum validates our predictions.

Three main insights emerge. First, spam always imposes a cost: through reduced user welfare when block capacity is tight, through increased network externality when capacity is abundant, or both.
We show that spam claims a growing share of each marginal unit of block capacity; consequently, capping block capacity slightly below
B_{\mathrm{plat}}
yields a favorable trade-off: a modest welfare reduction removes a disproportionate amount of spam and substantially lowers externality.
Second, priority fee ordering helps curb spam by requiring spammers to bid more for earlier block positions. Third, under demand scaling, spam does not capture an ever-larger fraction of blockspace but instead settles at a bounded share.

An important direction for future work is the broader design of mechanisms that reduce the cost of spam without harming legitimate users.
This includes both making spam less attractive to send and making failed probes cheaper for the system to process. Understanding how these approaches interact and perform across different blockchain architectures remains an open question.

Appendix A
Choosing
g_{\text{min}}
for a fixed
B_{\text{max}}

The discussion above fixes
g_{\text{min}}
and chooses
B_{\text{max}}
.
In practice, the opposite situation also occurs: the designer may want to keep
B_{\text{max}}
fixed and choose the gas price floor instead.
This is natural when block size is costly to change, or when the system operator wants to adjust spam volume through the gas price floor rather than through throughput.

A baseline rule.
Fix a block size
B_{\text{max}}
.
The simplest rule is to choose the smallest
g_{\text{min}}
such that the current block size already equals the plateau threshold, that is,

g_{\text{min}}^{\dagger}(B_{\text{max}})\ \text{such that}\ B_{\mathrm{plat}}(g_{\text{min}}^{\dagger})=B_{\text{max}}.

Because
B_{\mathrm{plat}}(g_{\text{min}})
is strictly decreasing in
g_{\text{min}}
, this rule defines a unique gas price floor.
If the floor is already high enough that spam does not enter, then the baseline choice is simply

g_{\text{min}}^{\dagger}(B_{\text{max}})=\frac{D_{0}-B_{\text{max}}}{\beta}.

When the solution lies in the slack-with-spam region, we have

g_{\text{min}}^{\dagger}(B_{\text{max}})=\frac{-(B_{\text{max}}-D_{0}+s+\beta r_{0}/D_{0})+\sqrt{(B_{\text{max}}-D_{0}+s+\beta r_{0}/D_{0})^{2}+4\beta r_{0}}}{2\beta}.

If the gas price floor were lower than
g_{\text{min}}^{\dagger}(B_{\text{max}})
, then the current block size would be too small relative to the amount of spam and non-spam demand that wants to enter.
If the gas price floor were higher than
g_{\text{min}}^{\dagger}(B_{\text{max}})
, then the block would be slack, but the system would be using a stronger fee floor than needed to reach that point.

Choosing
g_{\text{min}}
with the refined rule.
The baseline rule makes the current block just sufficient, but it does not control how the newly admitted block usage is split between users and spam as the gas price floor is lowered.
Similar to the previous analysis, we define the user share of the newly admitted used capacity when the gas price floor is reduced from
g_{\text{min}}
to
g_{\text{min}}-\Delta g
(assume
\Delta g\to 0
):

\mu_{\text{user}}:=\frac{-\frac{\partial Q_{u}^{\ast}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})}{\partial g_{\text{min}}}}{-\frac{\partial Q_{u}^{\ast}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})}{\partial g_{\text{min}}}-\frac{\partial(s\,S^{\ast}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}}))}{\partial g_{\text{min}}}}.

The minus signs appear because we are studying the effect of lowering the gas price floor.

Proposition A.1
.

Fix
B_{\text{max}}
.
Then the local user share from lowering
g_{\text{min}}
has the following form with the linear demand function:

1.

If

g_{\text{min}}\geq\max\!\left\{\frac{D_{0}-B_{\text{max}}}{\beta},\;\frac{r_{0}D_{0}}{D_{0}s+\beta r_{0}}\right\},

then no spam enters and
\mu_{\text{user}}=1
.
In this region, lowering the gas price floor only admits additional users.

2.

If

g_{\text{min}}^{\dagger}(B_{\text{max}})\leq g_{\text{min}}<\frac{r_{0}D_{0}}{D_{0}s+\beta r_{0}},

then the block remains slack and spam enters.
In this region, lowering
g_{\text{min}}
strictly decreases the fraction of newly admitted used capacity that goes to users.

3.

If

\frac{D_{0}-B_{\text{max}}}{\beta}<g_{\text{min}}<g_{\text{min}}^{\dagger}(B_{\text{max}}),

then the spam world is congested.
In this region, the denominator in the definition of
\mu_{\text{user}}
is zero.

The proof can be found in
Appendix

C
.

Proposition

A.1
gives a simple refined rule for choosing
g_{\text{min}}
.
Fix a target
\eta\in(0,1]
and require that at least an
\eta
fraction of newly admitted used capacity go to users.
Inside the slack-with-spam region, the condition
\mu_{\text{user}}\geq\eta
becomes

\frac{\beta\,g_{\text{min}}^{2}}{\beta\,g_{\text{min}}^{2}+r_{0}}\geq\eta,

which is equivalent to

g_{\text{min}}\geq\sqrt{\frac{\eta r_{0}}{\beta(1-\eta)}}.

Therefore, if the block size
B_{\text{max}}
is fixed, a refined gas price floor choice is

\displaystyle g_{\text{min}}^{\star}(B_{\text{max}},\eta)=\max\!\left\{g_{\text{min}}^{\dagger}(B_{\text{max}}),\;\min\!\left\{\frac{r_{0}D_{0}}{D_{0}s+\beta r_{0}},\sqrt{\frac{\eta r_{0}}{\beta(1-\eta)}}\right\}\right\}

In this equation, the term
g_{\text{min}}^{\dagger}(B_{\text{max}})
is the baseline floor that makes the current block size just sufficient, and the second term is the user-share threshold.
Therefore, the designer lowers
g_{\text{min}}
only until either the current block size would cease to be sufficient, or the newly added capacity would become too spam-heavy.

Appendix B
Priority Fee Ordering

B.1
Equilibrium Analysis

Let
C=B_{\text{max}}/n
denote the capacity of each sub-block.
Let
S_{i}
denote the number of spam transactions in sub-block
i
, and let
s
denote the gas reserved by each spam transaction.

For a candidate spam profile
\mathbf{S}=(S_{1},\dots,S_{n})
and a candidate block-clearing price
\bar{g}
, we first define the gas of the priority-seeking users.
These are the users who opt into the priority market, and therefore fill the expensive sub-blocks from the top downward.
Let

Q_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{S};\bar{g})=\left(\min\!\left\{C-S_{i}s,\;vD(\bar{g})-\Gamma_{i-1}^{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{S};\bar{g})\right\}\right)^{+},

where
(x)^{+}=\max\{x,0\}
and

\Gamma_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{S};\bar{g}):=\sum_{j=1}^{i}Q_{j}^{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{S};\bar{g}),\qquad\Gamma_{0}^{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{S};\bar{g}):=0.

Thus, the priority-seeking users are exactly the users who occupy the highest-priced occupied sub-blocks.

Now define the residual user slots after spam and priority-seeking users in sub-block
i
by

L_{i}(\mathbf{S};\bar{g})=\bigl(C-S_{i}s-Q_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{S};\bar{g})\bigr)^{+}.

Let

m(\mathbf{S};\bar{g})=\min\{i:L_{i}(\mathbf{S};\bar{g})>0\},

with the convention
m(\mathbf{S};\bar{g})=n+1
if no such sub-block exists.
Thus, sub-blocks
1,\dots,m(\mathbf{S};\bar{g})-1
are fully occupied by priority-seeking users, while sub-block
m(\mathbf{S};\bar{g})
is the first sub-block in which inclusion-only users may appear.

Next define the total post-spam user capacity by

A(\mathbf{S})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(C-S_{j}s)^{+},

and the total included user gas at the candidate block-clearing price
\bar{g}
by

Q_{u}(\mathbf{S};\bar{g})=\min\!\bigl\{D(\bar{g}),\,A(\mathbf{S})\bigr\}.

The total amount of inclusion-only user gas that is admitted is therefore

Q_{u}^{\mathrm{L}}(\mathbf{S};\bar{g})=Q_{u}(\mathbf{S};\bar{g})-\Gamma_{n}^{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{S};\bar{g}).

Because all inclusion-only users bid the same price
\bar{g}
, we assume that they occupy the next available user slots contiguously, starting from sub-block
m(\mathbf{S};\bar{g})
and proceeding downward.
Their gas in sub-block
i
is therefore

Q_{i}^{\mathrm{L}}(\mathbf{S};\bar{g})=\begin{cases}0,&i<m(\mathbf{S};\bar{g}),\\[6.0pt]
\left(\min\!\left\{L_{i}(\mathbf{S};\bar{g}),\;Q_{u}^{\mathrm{L}}(\mathbf{S};\bar{g})-\displaystyle\sum_{j=m(\mathbf{S};\bar{g})}^{i-1}L_{j}(\mathbf{S};\bar{g})\right\}\right)^{+},&i\geq m(\mathbf{S};\bar{g}),\\[14.0pt]
0,&m(\mathbf{S};\bar{g})=n+1,\end{cases}

where an empty sum is understood to be zero.
The priority-seeking users determine the early, high-price part of the block.
The inclusion-only users then fill the next available
\bar{g}
-priced sub-blocks contiguously.
Any remaining lower sub-blocks are empty of user demand.

When
v=0
, we have
Q_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}=0
for all
i
, so all user-filled sub-blocks lie in the
\bar{g}
-priced region.
Users then occupy the available post-spam capacity contiguously from the top, and the remaining lower sub-blocks are empty.

Define the total user gas in sub-block
i
by

Q_{i}(\mathbf{S};\bar{g}):=Q_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{S};\bar{g})+Q_{i}^{\mathrm{L}}(\mathbf{S};\bar{g}),

and the cumulative user gas by

\Gamma_{i}(\mathbf{S};\bar{g}):=\sum_{j=1}^{i}Q_{j}(\mathbf{S};\bar{g}).

The sub-block price is then

g_{i}(\mathbf{S};\bar{g})=\begin{cases}P\!\left(\Gamma_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}(\mathbf{S};\bar{g})/v\right),&i<m(\mathbf{S};\bar{g})\text{ and }v>0,\\[6.0pt]
\bar{g},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Thus, the upper sub-blocks that are filled entirely by priority-seeking users clear above
\bar{g}
, while the first inclusion-only sub-block and all later user-occupied sub-blocks clear at
\bar{g}
.

Since the opportunity is randomly located among the included user gas, the probability that it originates in sub-block
i
is

\pi_{i}(\mathbf{S};\bar{g})=\frac{Q_{i}(\mathbf{S};\bar{g})}{Q_{u}(\mathbf{S};\bar{g})}
,

Q_{u}(\mathbf{S};\bar{g})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}Q_{j}(\mathbf{S};\bar{g})
.
Combining this with

\bar{r}=r_{0}\cdot\frac{Q_{u}(\mathbf{S};\bar{g})}{D_{0}}
,
the expected reward attributable to user gas in sub-block
i
is

\pi_{i}(\mathbf{S};\bar{g})\bar{r}=\frac{r_{0}}{D_{0}}Q_{i}(\mathbf{S};\bar{g})
.

Top and second top sub-blocks.
Given
S_{1}
spam transactions in the top sub-block, the opportunity is captured with probability
S_{1}/(S_{1}+1)
, by the same argument as in
Section

3.1
.
The expected utility of spam in the top sub-block is therefore

U_{1}(S_{1})=\frac{r_{0}}{D_{0}}Q_{1}(S_{1};\bar{g})\frac{S_{1}}{S_{1}+1}-S_{1}s\,g_{1}(S_{1};\bar{g}).

Setting
U_{1}(S_{1}^{\ast})=0
gives the equilibrium spam volume
S_{1}^{\ast}
in the first sub-block.

The success probability of spam in sub-block
2
again has two components.
First, the opportunity may originate in sub-block
2
and be captured there, contributing

\frac{r_{0}}{D_{0}}Q_{2}(S_{2};\bar{g})\frac{S_{2}}{S_{2}+1}.

Second, the opportunity may originate in sub-block
1
but not be captured there.
That occurs if it is placed after all
S_{1}^{\ast}
spam transactions in the first sub-block, which has probability
1/(S_{1}^{\ast}+1)
.
In that case, the opportunity remains live when sub-block
2
begins execution, and any positive spam in sub-block
2
captures it immediately.
This contributes

\frac{r_{0}}{D_{0}}\frac{Q_{1}^{\ast}}{S_{1}^{\ast}+1}
.
Therefore,

U_{2}(S_{2})=\frac{r_{0}}{D_{0}}\left(Q_{2}(S_{2};\bar{g})\frac{S_{2}}{S_{2}+1}+\frac{Q_{1}^{\ast}}{S_{1}^{\ast}+1}\right)-S_{2}s\,g_{2}(S_{2};\bar{g}).

Solving
U_{2}(S_{2}^{\ast})=0
gives the second sub-block equilibrium.

General sub-block.
For sub-block
i\geq 3
, suppose that
S_{1}^{\ast},\dots,S_{i-1}^{\ast}
have already been computed.
The success probability again has two components: the opportunity may originate in sub-block
i
and be captured there, or it may spill over from an earlier sub-block.

To describe spillover, let
h_{i}
be the index of the last earlier sub-block with positive spam, that is,

h_{i}=\max\{j<i:S_{j}^{\ast}>0\},

with the convention
h_{i}=0
if no earlier sub-block has positive spam.
Let

k_{i}=i-1-h_{i}

be the number of contiguous zero-spam sub-blocks immediately before sub-block
i
.
If the opportunity originates in any of those
k_{i}
zero-spam sub-blocks, then it reaches sub-block
i
alive and is captured there by any positive spam.
The contribution of those blocks is therefore

\frac{r_{0}}{D_{0}}\sum_{j=h_{i}+1}^{i-1}Q_{j}^{\ast}
.
If
h_{i}\geq 1
, then there is one additional spillover source: the opportunity may originate in sub-block
h_{i}
and fail to be captured there, which occurs with probability
1/(S_{h_{i}}^{\ast}+1)
.
Since all sub-blocks between
h_{i}
and
i
have zero spam, that opportunity then reaches sub-block
i
alive.
This contributes

\frac{r_{0}}{D_{0}}\frac{Q_{h_{i}}^{\ast}}{S_{h_{i}}^{\ast}+1}
.
Putting these terms together, the expected utility of spam in sub-block
i
is

\displaystyle\resizebox{30785863}{}{$U_{i}(S_{i})=\frac{r_{0}}{D_{0}}\left(Q_{i}(S_{i};\bar{g})\frac{S_{i}}{S_{i}+1}+\sum_{j=h_{i}+1}^{i-1}Q_{j}^{\ast}+\mathbf{1}[h_{i}\geq 1]\frac{Q_{h_{i}}^{\ast}}{S_{h_{i}}^{\ast}+1}\right)-S_{i}s\,g_{i}(S_{i};\bar{g})$}.

We solve
U_{i}(S_{i}^{\ast})=0
iteratively for
i=1,\dots,n
, with the convention that there is no spillover into the top sub-block.
If
U_{i}(S_{i})
remains nonnegative all the way up to the capacity boundary
S_{i}=C/s
, then the equilibrium for that sub-block is the corner solution
S_{i}^{\ast}=C/s
.

This gives a spam profile
S_{1}^{\ast},\dots,S_{n}^{\ast}
.
The equilibrium is then closed by the fixed-point condition

\bar{g}^{\ast}=\max\!\left\{g_{\text{min}},\;P\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(C-S_{i}^{\ast}s)^{+}\right)\right\}.

Equivalently,
\bar{g}^{\ast}
is the block-clearing price implied by the total post-spam user capacity.
At that price, user demand fills the block contiguously from the top until the included quantity

Q_{u}^{\ast}=\min\!\left\{D(\bar{g}^{\ast}),\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}(C-S_{i}^{\ast}s)^{+}\right\}

is exhausted, and any remaining lower sub-blocks are empty of user demand.
The total equilibrium spam volume is

S^{\ast}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}^{\ast}.

B.2
Derivation of Welfare Metrics

User welfare.
In PFO, users in different sub-blocks pay different prices.
To evaluate welfare, we separate the two user populations.

The priority-seeking users included in sub-block
i
are exactly those with ranks between
\Gamma_{i-1}^{\mathrm{T},\ast}
and
\Gamma_{i}^{\mathrm{T},\ast}
in the demand curve
vD(g)
, and each of them pays
g_{i}^{\ast}=g_{i}(S_{i}^{\ast};\bar{g}^{\ast})
.
Their consumer surplus is therefore

\int_{\Gamma_{i-1}^{\mathrm{T},\ast}}^{\Gamma_{i}^{\mathrm{T},\ast}}\left(P\!\left(\frac{q}{v}\right)-g_{i}^{\ast}\right)\,dq.

The inclusion-only users, by construction, all pay the same clearing price
\bar{g}^{\ast}
.
If their cumulative included gas through sub-block
i
is
\Gamma_{i}^{\mathrm{L},\ast}
, then their total consumer surplus is

\int_{\Gamma_{i-1}^{\mathrm{L},\ast}}^{\Gamma_{i}^{\mathrm{L},\ast}}\left(P\!\left(\frac{q}{1-v}\right)-\bar{g}^{\ast}\right)\,dq.

Summing over all sub-blocks gives total user welfare:

W_{\text{user}}(B_{\text{max}})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\Gamma_{i-1}^{\mathrm{T},\ast}}^{\Gamma_{i}^{\mathrm{T},\ast}}\left(P\!\left(\frac{q}{v}\right)-g_{i}^{\ast}\right)\,dq+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\Gamma_{i-1}^{\mathrm{L},\ast}}^{\Gamma_{i}^{\mathrm{L},\ast}}\left(P\!\left(\frac{q}{1-v}\right)-\bar{g}^{\ast}\right)\,dq.

This expression is the natural analogue of the single-price welfare formula from the random-ordering model, except that each sub-block clears at its own price and the lower occupied sub-blocks all clear at the common price
\bar{g}^{\ast}
.

Validator revenue.
Then we look into the validator revenue.
Each transaction in sub-block
i
pays
g_{i}^{\ast}
.
The total gas sold in sub-block
i
is
Q_{i}^{\ast}+S_{i}^{\ast}s
, which is the sum of user gas and spam gas.
Therefore, validator revenue is

R(B_{\text{max}})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}^{\ast}\cdot\bigl(Q_{i}^{\ast}+S_{i}^{\ast}s\bigr).

Under PFO, validator revenue can increase because priority-seeking users pay more in earlier sub-blocks, while inclusion-only users and spam are pushed into the low-price region that clears at
\bar{g}^{\ast}
.

g_{\text{min}}
gives

\frac{\partial Q_{u}^{\ast}}{\partial g_{\text{min}}}=-\beta\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{\partial(s\,S^{\ast})}{\partial g_{\text{min}}}=-\frac{r_{0}}{g_{\text{min}}^{2}}.

Substituting these derivatives into the definition of
\mu_{\text{user}}
yields

\mu_{\text{user}}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})=\frac{\beta}{\beta+r_{0}/g_{\text{min}}^{2}}=\frac{\beta\,g_{\text{min}}^{2}}{\beta\,g_{\text{min}}^{2}+r_{0}}.

Differentiating this expression gives

\frac{\partial\mu_{\text{user}}(B_{\text{max}},g_{\text{min}})}{\partial g_{\text{min}}}=\frac{2\beta r_{0}\,g_{\text{min}}}{(\beta g_{\text{min}}^{2}+r_{0})^{2}}>0.

Therefore
\mu_{\text{user}}
increases with
g_{\text{min}}
, or equivalently decreases as the gas price floor is lowered.

Finally, in the congested-with-spam region, the equilibrium price is already above the gas price floor.
A small change in
g_{\text{min}}
does not change the local equilibrium allocation, so both the user-gas increment and the spam-gas increment are zero.
Thus the denominator in the definition of
\mu_{\text{user}}
vanishes, and the local share is not informative in that region.
∎

Appendix D
MEV Revenue Scaling with User Volume

In this appendix, we estimate how the opportunity value
r
scales with user demand in deployed systems.
This matters for the scaling analysis in the main text.
If MEV opportunity size grows with user activity, then the amount of spam that can be sustained at equilibrium may also grow with adoption.

Methodology.

We study cyclic arbitrage on Ethereum Layer 2 rollups.
Our main case study is Base, and we also report corresponding estimates for Arbitrum.
We use daily observations and identify cyclic arbitrage transactions from decoded DEX swap data on Dune Analytics.
A transaction is classified as cyclic arbitrage if it contains at least three swaps that form a cycle, meaning that the first token sold is also the last token bought, and the transaction yields a positive net balance in exactly that token.
We exclude transactions routed through known DEX aggregator contracts, since these are more likely to reflect user-initiated routing rather than MEV.
For each arbitrage transaction, we compute gross profit from the net token balance and subtract the gas fee to obtain net profit.
We then aggregate these net profits by day and use the resulting daily total as a proxy for the available opportunity value
r
.
This proxy is imperfect, but it is natural in a competitive setting, since realized arbitrage profits should track the amount of extractable value available to searchers.

To measure user demand, we use daily non-MEV trading volume in USD and estimate the log-log regression

\log(P_{t})=\alpha+\gamma\log(V_{t}),

where
P_{t}
is daily net cyclic arbitrage profit and
V_{t}
is daily non-MEV trading volume.
The coefficient
\gamma
is the elasticity of realized cyclic arbitrage profit with respect to user trading volume.

Base.

We first analyze Base using 790 daily observations from January 2024 through February 2026.
The estimate is
\hat{\gamma}=1.08
with standard error
0.041
,
R^{2}=0.47
, and
p\approx 0
.
Thus, on Base, a
1\%
increase in user trading volume is associated with roughly a
1.08\%
increase in cyclic arbitrage profit, which is close to linear.

Arbitrum.

We next apply the same methodology to Arbitrum.
For Arbitrum, we estimate
\hat{\gamma}=0.82
with
R^{2}=0.13
.
The low
R^{2}
indicates that the relationship between user trading volume and cyclic arbitrage profit on Arbitrum is considerably weaker and less reliable than on Base, with most of the variation in arbitrage profit unexplained by trading volume alone.
