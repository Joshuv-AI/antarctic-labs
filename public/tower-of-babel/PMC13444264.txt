Recurrent episodes of hypoglycemia as an initial presentation of pituitary stalk interruption syndrome: A case report
Authors: Radhika Thagunna, Shivali Rao, Binit Bajracharya, Jayaj Adhikari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPituitary stalk interruption syndrome is a rare, congenital endocrine disorder of hypopituitarism. It has highly variable clinical presentation depending on the extent and type of hormonal deficiency and age at presentation, with growth hormone deficiency being the most common manifestation. Growth hormone plays an important role in maintaining normal blood glucose levels during stress or fasting, by stimulating hepatic gluconeogenesis and lipolysis. Therefore, hypoglycemia should alert us to a possibility of growth hormone deficiency necessitating imaging and facilitating early diagnosis of pituitary stalk interruption syndrome.Keywords: Isolated growth hormone deficiency, Short stature, Ectopic posterior pituitary, Congenital hypopituitarism

Introduction

PSIS is characterized by a triad of thin or interrupted pituitary stalk, small or absent anterior pituitary gland, and an absent or ectopic pituitary gland [1]. PSIS belongs to the holoprosencephaly spectrum with reported incidence of 0.5/100,000 live birth. It has first reported incidence in 1987 by Fujisawa et al. [2,3]. PSIS shows male predominance with mean age at presentation being 9.4-11.6 years [3]. Deficiency of anterior pituitary hormones may present as hypoglycemia, neonatal jaundice, short stature, cryptorchidism, delayed puberty and micropenis [1]. We present a case of 9 years old male who currently presented for the work up of short stature and had history of recurrent episodes of fainting attacks in the past. MRI brain was performed which revealed PSIS.

Case presentation

A 9 years old male was referred to our center for diagnostic work up for short stature. On detailed history taking, his parents gave a history of multiple episodes of loss of consciousness since he was 16 months of age. The episodes were usually 2-3 hours post prandial and were accompanied with excessive sweating. The hypoglycemic attacks were corrected by treatment with IV dextrose, providing symptomatic relief. The patient had no significant antenatal or family history. On physical examination, short stature (height for age being less than -3 standard deviations) and small external genitalia (penile length less than 3 cm) was noted. Otherwise, no other significant findings were noted on physical examination.

X-ray of left wrist (Fig. 1A and B) was ordered for bone age estimation, which according to Greulich and Pyle method of bone age estimation, corresponded to 3-4 years, which was less than this chronological age. Following which a suspicion for growth hormone deficiency was made and IGF-1, IGFBP- 3 levels, clonidine stimulation test and hormonal assay for anterior pituitary hormones were prescribed. Low IGF-1 levels of <15 ng/mL and IGFBP-3 levels of 0.77 mcg/mL was noted. Clonidine stimulation test was done showing no rise in growth hormone levels after clonidine stimulation, with all the values being <0.06 ng/mL. Prolactin levels were also raised (21.4 ng/mL). Other anterior pituitary hormone levels were within normal levels. A working diagnosis of isolated growth hormone deficiency was made and CE-MRI brain was performed with a focus on the hypothalamic- pituitary axis.

(A and B) Postero-anterior and lateral radiographs of left wrist): Showing the presence of 3 carpal bones: capitate, hamate, triquetrum.

MRI brain showed absence of pituitary stalk, hypoplastic anterior pituitary gland (measuring ∼2.3 mm in height in mid-sagittal plane) and presence of posterior pituitary bright spot in ectopic location at the median eminence following which a diagnosis of PSIS was made (Fig. 2). Additional finding of T2/FLAIR high signal intensity was noted in the right occipital and left parietal white matter probably representing the sequelae of hypoglycemic brain injury (Fig. 3, Fig. 4).

(A: T1W mid-sagittal image; B: T1W postcontrast mid sagittal image): Blue arrows showing reduced anterior height of anterior pituitary gland with absent pituitary stalk. Orange arrows showing posterior pituitary bright spot in ectopic location (near the median eminence).

(A: T1W coronal image; B: T1W postcontrast coronal image): Orange arrows showing posterior pituitary bright spot in ectopic location with absent infundibulum.

(A: T2W Axial, B: FLAIR Axial, C: DWI images): Blue arrows showing T2/FLAIR high signal intensity in periventricular white matter of right occipital lobe showing no restriction of diffusion.

The patient received subcutaneous growth hormone, 1 mg per day for 4 days in the hospital and was subsequently discharged with continuation injectable growth hormone supplement and 3 monthly follow up with growth, thyroid and adrenal hormone levels.

Discussion

PSIS is a rare congenital cause of hypopituitarism, presenting with isolated or combined anterior pituitary hormone defects [1,2]. The etio-pathogenesis of PSIS is not completely understood. Abnormal pituitary development during the embryonic period has been proposed, and mutations of the genes involved in early development of pituitary gland, namely the HESX1, LHX4, OTX2, SOX3, and PROKR2. However, these mutations are reported in less than 5% of cases of PSIS [4].

PSIS presents with anterior pituitary hormone deficiencies with deficiency of growth hormone in 100%, gonadotropin deficiency in 97.2%, corticotropin deficiency in 88.2%; and thyrotropin deficiency in 70.3% of cases. It is also shown to present as hyperprolactinemia (6.9%) due to lack of dopaminergic inhibition. Posterior pituitary function remains unaffected [1,5]. These findings corroborate with the findings in our case which showed isolated growth hormone deficiency and hyperprolactinemia. Sodero et al. [6] demonstrated that hypothalamic-pituitary abnormalities were detected on MRI in only 27.4% of cases with isolated growth hormone deficiency.

In 1 cohort study of 67 PSIS patients by Bar et al. [7], 10 patients (15%) were referred for neonatal hypoglycemia as the presenting problem which was in keeping with our case. The presence of recurrent hypoglycemic fainting attacks in our patient is clinically significant, as such episodes may precede overt growth failure and are frequently misattributed to other causes. This in turn contributes to delayed diagnosis, as observed in our case.

Conclusion

The rarity and heterogeneity of presentation of PSIS often results in delayed recognition and treatment. Early recognition of PSIS is crucial to prevent long term complications. This case report emphasizes the need for heightened clinical suspicion and pituitary imaging in children presenting with unexplained hypoglycemia and growth failure.
