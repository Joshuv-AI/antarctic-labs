1.
Foreword

Flat chains were introduced by Whitney; see
[
19
]
for an outline and
[
20
]
for the detailed account. Federer and Fleming interpreted flat chains with compact support as a special class of currents in the sense of de Rham; see
[
8
, 7.4]
and
[
9
, 4.1.18]
.
Metric currents, introduced in
[
3
]
by Ambrosio and Kirchheim, extend the notion of current to arbitrary complete metric spaces, though, these may lack a differentiable structure.

In Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, metric
m
-currents are conjectured
[
3
, §11]
to correspond to classical flat
m
-chains of finite mass; this is known as the
flat chain conjecture
. In section 3, we give a new proof of this conjecture in the case
m=1
.
Specifically, we prove:

Theorem 1.1
—

Let
T
be a metric
1
-current in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The associated de Rham current is a flat chain of finite mass.

The relevant notions are defined in the body of the paper. The one-dimensional case of the flat chain conjecture was first established by Schioppa in
[
17
]
. More recently, several alternative proofs have been obtained by Alberti, Bate, Marchese
[
1
]
, De Masi, Marchese
[
13
]
, Marchese, Merlo
[
12
]
, and Arroyo-Rabasa and Bouchitté
[
4
]
. Recently, Bate, Caputo, Takáč, Valentine and Wald proved a generalization of the one-dimensional flat chain conjecture in complete quasiconvex metric spaces in
[
5
]
.
The more difficult zero-codimensional case where
m=d
was proved by De Philippis and Rindler in
[
15
]
.

Let us also mention that a variant of the flat chain conjecture, in which metric currents are understood in the sense of Lang
[
11
]
and no finite mass assumption is imposed, was disproved by Takáč
[
18
]
in most cases (with the only exceptions occurring when
m=0
or
m=d=1
).

Our proof below fits on one page. It uses the validity of the conjecture for metric normal currents already observed in
[
3
]
, the identification of a space of 0-dimensional metric currents with a Lipschitz-free space, and the closed range theorem. Lipschitz-free spaces were introduced in Banach space theory to linearize metric spaces and their non-linear Lipschitz mappings; see
[
10
]
or
[
2
, Chapter 14]
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1
Lipschitz-free space —

Let
(E,d)
be a complete metric space with a distinguished base point
o\in E
.
We denote by
\rmLip_{0}(E)
the space of all Lipschitz functions
f\colon E\to\mathbb{R}
such that
f(o)=0
. Equipped with the norm
\|f\|_{\rmLip_{0}(E)}:=\rmLip(f)
, this is a Banach space.

We now recall how to construct a canonical predual of
\rmLip_{0}(E)
. For each
x\in E
, define the evaluation functional

\delta_{x}\colon\rmLip_{0}(E)\to\mathbb{R},\qquad f\mapsto f(x).

Each
\delta_{x}
is linear and continuous, and the map

\delta\colon E\to\rmLip_{0}(E)^{*},\qquad x\mapsto\delta_{x},

is an isometric embedding of
(E,d)
into
\rmLip_{0}(E)^{*}
.
The
Lipschitz-free space
over
E
is defined as the smallest closed linear subspace that contains the range of
\delta
:

\mathscr{F}(E):=\operatorname{cl}\operatorname{span}\,\delta(E)\subseteq\rmLip_{0}(E)^{*},

equipped with the norm inherited from
\rmLip_{0}(E)^{*}
. In a sense,
\mathscr{F}(E)
is a linearization of the metric space
E
.

We easily infer from the definition that
\mathscr{F}(E)
is separable whenever
E
is.
The following fundamental properties are also known to hold:

•

The map

\Upsilon\colon\mathscr{F}(E)^{*}\to\rmLip_{0}(E),\alpha\mapsto\alpha\circ\delta,

is an isometric isomorphism.

•

A sequence
(f_{n})
in
\rmLip_{0}(E)
converges weakly* to
f
(with respect to the duality with
\mathscr{F}(E)
) if and only if it is bounded in
\rmLip_{0}(E)
and
f_{n}\to f
pointwise.

For the reader’s convenience, we provide a proof of both claims.

Proof.

For any
\alpha\in\mathscr{F}(E)^{*}
and
x,y\in E
, we have

|\Upsilon(\alpha)(y)-\Upsilon(\alpha)(x)|=|\alpha(\delta_{y}-\delta_{x})|\leqslant\|\alpha\|_{\mathscr{F}(E)^{*}}\|\delta_{y}-\delta_{x}\|_{\mathscr{F}(E)}\leqslant\|\alpha\|_{\mathscr{F}(E)^{*}}d(x,y).

Hence
\Upsilon(\alpha)
is a Lipschitz map with constant
\rmLip\Upsilon(\alpha)\leqslant\|\alpha\|_{\mathscr{F}(E)^{*}}
. Clearly
\delta_{o}=0
, thus
\Upsilon(\alpha)\in\rmLip_{0}(E)
.

Now suppose that
\Upsilon(\alpha)=0
. Then
\alpha=0
on the dense subspace spanned by
\delta(E)
, and therefore
\alpha=0
everywhere. This establishes the injectivity of
\Upsilon
.

As for surjectivity, let us pick
f\in\rmLip_{0}(E)
. We define a linear map
\alpha\colon\operatorname{span}\delta(E)\to\mathbb{R}
by

\alpha\left(\sum_{x}\lambda_{x}\delta_{x}\right)=\sum_{x}\lambda_{x}f(x).

By definition of
\mathscr{F}(E)
, this map extends by density to an element of
\mathscr{F}(E)^{*}
of norm
\|\alpha\|_{\mathscr{F}(E)^{*}}\leqslant\rmLip(f)
. Clearly,
f=\Upsilon(\alpha)
and, therefore,
\|\alpha\|_{\mathscr{F}(E)^{*}}=\rmLip\Upsilon(\alpha)
.

Finally, the second claim is an easy consequence of the uniform boundedness principle, that guarantees the boundedness of weak* convergent sequences, and the duality between
\mathscr{F}(E)
and
\rmLip_{0}(E)
.
∎

2.2
Metric currents —

Let us denote by
\mathscr{B}^{\infty}(E)
the linear space of bounded Borel functions on
E
. A
metric
1
-current
in
E
is a bilinear map
T\colon\mathscr{B}^{\infty}(E)\times\rmLip(E)\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying the following properties:

•

Locality.
If
\pi
is constant on
\{f\neq 0\}
then
T(f,\pi)=0
.

•

Continuity.
For every
f\in\mathscr{B}^{\infty}(E)
and every sequence
(\pi_{n})
in
\rmLip(E)
such that
\pi_{n}\to\pi
pointwise and with uniformly bounded Lipschitz constants, one has

T(f,\pi)=\lim_{n\to\infty}T(f,\pi_{n}).

•

Finite mass.
There exists a finite Borel measure
\mu
on
E
such that for all
(f,\pi)\in\mathscr{B}^{\infty}(E)\times\rmLip(E)
,

(1)

|T(f,\pi)|\leqslant\rmLip\pi\int_{E}|f|\,d\mu.

The smallest measure
\mu
satisfying (
1
) is denoted
\|T\|
. The linear space of metric
1
-currents in
E
will be denoted
\mathscr{M}_{1}(E)
.

Additionally, we say that a metric
1
-current
T\in\mathscr{M}_{1}(E)
is
normal
whenever there is a finite Borel measure
\nu
on
E
such that

|T(1,f)|\leqslant\int_{E}|f|\,d\nu

for every bounded Lipschitz function
f
on
E
. The least such measure
\nu
is denoted
\|\partial T\|
. The linear subspace of normal
1
-currents in
E
is denoted
\mathscr{N}_{1}(E)
.

Proposition 2.3
—

Any integrable vector field
u\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
induces a metric
1
-current in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

\boldsymbol{[}\!\boldsymbol{[}u\boldsymbol{]}\!\boldsymbol{]}\colon(f,\pi)\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(uf)\cdot\nabla\pi.

Proof.

The map
\boldsymbol{[}\!\boldsymbol{[}u\boldsymbol{]}\!\boldsymbol{]}
is well-defined, according to Rademacher’s theorem. It is most obviously bilinear and satisfies the locality axiom. As for continuity, we fix
\varepsilon>0
and a sequence
(\pi_{n})
of Lipschitz functions that converge pointwise to
0
with
L:=\sup\rmLip\pi_{n}<\infty
. There is a map
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
\|uf-\varphi\|_{L^{1}}<\varepsilon
. By an integration by part, we have

|\boldsymbol{[}\!\boldsymbol{[}u\boldsymbol{]}\!\boldsymbol{]}(f,\pi_{n})|\leqslant L\|fu-\varphi\|_{L^{1}}+\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\pi_{n}\operatorname{div}\varphi\right|

By the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem, we infer that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}|\boldsymbol{[}\!\boldsymbol{[}u\boldsymbol{]}\!\boldsymbol{]}(f,\pi_{n})|\leqslant L\varepsilon.

We conclude with the arbitrariness of
\varepsilon
.
∎

2.4
Boundary of a metric
1
-current —

Suppose that
E
is separable, and fix an arbitrary point
o\in E
. The boundary of a metric
1
-current
T\in\mathscr{M}_{1}(E)
is usually defined as a metric
0
-current
\partial T
. Here, we take a slightly different approach and define
\partial T
as an element of the Lipschitz-free space
\mathscr{F}(E)
via

\partial T\colon f\in\rmLip_{0}(E)\mapsto T(1,f).

The finite mass assumption ensures that
\partial T\in\rmLip_{0}(E)^{*}
, while the continuity axiom guarantees that
\partial T
is sequentially weak* continuous. What we actually need, however, is full weak* continuity, so that
\partial T
indeed belongs to
\mathscr{F}(E)
. This stronger continuity follows from the separability of the predual
\mathscr{F}(E)
, thanks to classical results of Dieudonné on the bounded weak* topology in Banach spaces. For details, see
[
14
, Corollary 2.7.5]
.

Alternatively, when
E=\mathbb{R}^{d}
(the case of interest here), one may apply
[
6
, 4.1.3]
to identify
\mathscr{F}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with the space of linear forms
F:\rmLip_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
that are continuous with respect to the localized topology
\mathscr{P}_{\rmLip}
defined there, and then apply
[
7
, 3.1(A)]
to characterize
\mathscr{P}_{\rmLip}
-convergent sequences, and finally apply
[
7
, 3.2(B)]
to infer that the
\mathscr{P}_{\rmLip}
-continuity of
F
is equivalent to its sequential
\mathscr{P}_{\rmLip}
-continuity.
This, however, is merely another convenient point of view, since, in this case,
\mathscr{P}_{\rmLip}
is, indeed, a bounded weak* topology
[
7
, 7.4]
.

2.5
dR currents and flat chains —

For each integer
m\geqslant 0
, we denote by
\mathscr{D}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the linear space of compactly supported smooth
m
-forms
\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\bigwedge^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A
de Rham
m
-current
(shortened to dR
m
-current) is a continuous linear functional
\boldsymbol{T}\colon\mathscr{D}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
, where
\mathscr{D}^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is equipped with the locally convex topology described in
[
9
, 4.1.1]
. We adopt the convention that dR currents are denoted in boldface.

The
mass
of a dR current
\boldsymbol{T}
is defined by

\mathbf{M}(\boldsymbol{T})=\sup\left\{\boldsymbol{T}(\omega):\|\omega(x)\|\leqslant 1\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\right\}.

Here,
\|\cdot\|
denotes the comass norm on
\bigwedge^{m}\mathbb{R}^{d}
as in
[
9
, 1.8.1]
. Any other choice of norm would not affect the results presented here.

If
m\geqslant 1
, the
boundary
of
\boldsymbol{T}
is the de Rham
(m-1)
-current
\boldsymbol{\partial T}
defined by

\boldsymbol{\partial T}(\omega)=\boldsymbol{T}(d\omega)
.
We say that
\boldsymbol{T}
is a
Federer-Fleming normal
m
-current
(abbreviated as FF normal
m
-current) if

\mathbf{M}(\boldsymbol{T})+\mathbf{M}(\boldsymbol{\partial T})<\infty
.
When
m=0
, we say that
\boldsymbol{T}
is normal if it has finite mass.

Finally, the
flat norm
of a dR
m
-current
\boldsymbol{T}
is defined by

\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{T})=\sup\left\{\boldsymbol{T}(\omega):\|\omega(x)\|\leqslant 1\text{ and }\|d\omega(x)\|\leqslant 1\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\right\}.

We say that
\boldsymbol{T}
is a
flat
m
-chain
if there exists a sequence
(\boldsymbol{T}_{n})
of FF normal
m
-currents such that

\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{T}_{n}-\boldsymbol{T})\to 0
.

We stress that our definitions differ from Federer’s terminology in that we do not require normal currents or flat chains to have compact support.

2.6
—

To each metric
1
-current
T
, one can associate a dR current, namely the functional
\boldsymbol{T}\colon\mathscr{D}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\boldsymbol{T}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}f_{k}\,dx_{k}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{d}T(f_{k},x_{k}),

where
x_{k}
are the coordinate functions. The dR current
\boldsymbol{T}
has automatically finite mass: this comes from the finite mass assumption for the metric current
T
, as one easily proves
\mathbf{M}(\boldsymbol{T})\leqslant\sqrt{d}\|T\|(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

If, moreover, the metric current
T
belongs to
\mathscr{N}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then the dR current
\boldsymbol{T}
is FF normal. Indeed, for all
\omega\in\mathscr{D}^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
:

\boldsymbol{\partial T}(\omega)=\boldsymbol{T}(d\omega)=\sum_{k=1}^{d}T\left(\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial x_{k}},x_{k}\right)=T(1,\omega)\leqslant\|\omega\|_{\infty}\|\partial T\|(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

where the last equality is obtained by applying the chain rule for metric currents
[
3
, Theorem 3.5]
. This implies that
\mathbf{M}(\boldsymbol{\partial T})\leqslant\|\partial T\|(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

For
u\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the dR current associated to
\boldsymbol{[}\!\boldsymbol{[}u\boldsymbol{]}\!\boldsymbol{]}
is denoted
\mathscr{L}^{d}\wedge u
, and

\mathscr{L}^{d}\wedge u(\omega)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle u(x),\omega(x)\rangle\,dx

for all
\omega\in\mathscr{D}^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We claim that
\mathscr{L}^{d}\wedge u
is a flat
1
-chain. Indeed, if
(u_{n})
is a sequence in
\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
that converges to
u
in
L^{1}
, then we easily establish that
\mathscr{L}^{d}\wedge u_{n}\to\mathscr{L}^{d}\wedge u
in mass norm, and thus in flat norm. Since all
\mathscr{L}^{d}\wedge u_{n}
are normal
1
-currents, this proves the claim.

3.
Proof

The argument below is inspired by the second author’s work on the divergence equation; see
[
6
]
. Related arguments based on the closed range theorem have also appeared independently in the literature on the flat chain conjecture, notably in
[
18
]
and
[
5
]
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

The following commutative diagram illustrates our proof.
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{F}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\boldsymbol{[}\!\boldsymbol{[}\cdot\boldsymbol{]}\!\boldsymbol{]}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces-\operatorname{div}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\partial}

The map
-\operatorname{div}
is defined by
-\operatorname{div}=\partial\circ\boldsymbol{[}\!\boldsymbol{[}\cdot\boldsymbol{]}\!\boldsymbol{]}
. One easily checks that it is defined by

-\operatorname{div}(u)(f)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u\cdot\nabla f

and this ensures its continuity, as
\|-\operatorname{div}(u)\|_{\mathscr{F}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=\|-\operatorname{div}(u)\|_{\rmLip_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{*}}\leqslant\|u\|_{L^{1}}
. Its adjoint is the map

-\operatorname{div}^{*}\colon\rmLip_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d}),\qquad f\mapsto\nabla f

It is clear that
-\operatorname{div}^{*}
defines an isometric embedding. From this, we can deduce two properties of
-\operatorname{div}
:

•

Since
-\operatorname{div}^{*}
is injective,
-\operatorname{div}
has dense image, by a corollary of the Hahn-Banach theorem.

•

Since the image of
-\operatorname{div}^{*}
is closed, the image of
-\operatorname{div}
is also closed, by the closed range theorem (see
[
16
, 4.14]
).

Hence, combining density and closedness, we conclude that
-\operatorname{div}
is onto.

By surjectivity of
-\operatorname{div}
, there exists
u\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
\partial T=-\operatorname{div}(u)
. It follows that
\partial(T-\boldsymbol{[}\!\boldsymbol{[}u\boldsymbol{]}\!\boldsymbol{]})=0
, so that
T-\boldsymbol{[}\!\boldsymbol{[}u\boldsymbol{]}\!\boldsymbol{]}
belongs to
\mathscr{N}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Indeed, for every bounded Lipschitz map
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, one has

(T-\boldsymbol{[}\!\boldsymbol{[}u\boldsymbol{]}\!\boldsymbol{]})(1,f)=f(0)(T-\boldsymbol{[}\!\boldsymbol{[}u\boldsymbol{]}\!\boldsymbol{]})(1,1)+\partial(T-\boldsymbol{[}\!\boldsymbol{[}u\boldsymbol{]}\!\boldsymbol{]})(f-f(0))=0,

where the first term vanishes by locality.

The metric current
T
admits the following decomposition:

T=(T-\boldsymbol{[}\!\boldsymbol{[}u\boldsymbol{]}\!\boldsymbol{]})+\boldsymbol{[}\!\boldsymbol{[}u\boldsymbol{]}\!\boldsymbol{]}.

The first term is a current in
\mathscr{N}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and corresponds to a normal FF current, hence a flat chain of finite mass. The second term correspond to the FF current
\mathscr{L}^{d}\wedge u
, that is also a flat chain of finite mass. The conclusion follows.
∎
