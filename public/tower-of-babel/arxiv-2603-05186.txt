Introduction to holography
Nele Callebaut

Abstract




Introduction

These are course notes for the ‘Introduction to holography’ Master level course at University of Cologne.

The goal of the course is to give an introduction to holography.
Holography is a popular approach to quantum gravity, in which a theory of gravity can be described by a lower-dimensional boundary theory that itself has no gravity. The most concrete known example of a holographic model is the AdS/CFT correspondence, where the gravitational theory has a negative cosmological constant (the universe is asymptotically Anti-de Sitter) and the boundary theory is a conformal field theory. Symmetry plays a very important role in this duality. We therefore start the course with a review of Poincaré symmetry in quantum field theory, before moving on in the second chapter to conformal symmetry in conformally invariant quantum field theories or CFT’s. Then we move to the basics of AdS physics in chapters 3 and 4, which will already reveal hints to the existence of a duality with CFT.
After gathering the basic ingredients (CFT and AdS), in the second half of the course we are ready to formulate the AdS/CFT correspondence (chapter 5), including finite temperature AdS/CFT (chapter 6), which involves black holes and their thermodynamics in the gravitational theory (chapter 7). We end the course with an introduction to entanglement in AdS/CFT and the origin of statements that ‘gravity emerges from entanglement’ in holography.

1.2.2
Casimir and irreducible representations

Particularly useful operators to label and organize states in a QM system with symmetry
G
are the
casimir operators
. They have the properties that they 1) commute with all generators of the symmetry group
G
, and 2) give the same eigenvalue for all states in an irreducible representation of the group. This is best illustrated in the example of the SO(3) rotation group with Lie algebra (
1.42
) for the generators
J_{i}=(\vec{x}\times\vec{P})_{i}
. The operator
J^{2}=J_{i}J_{i}=J_{1}^{2}+J_{2}^{2}+J_{3}^{2}
is a casimir operator, typically denoted
c_{2}
(for ‘quadratic’ casimir, quadratic in the generators)

\displaystyle c_{2}^{rot}=J^{2}

(1.46)

because
[J^{2},J_{i}]=0
.
Since
J^{2}
and
J_{3}
commute, they can be diagonalized simultaneously with corresponding eigenvalues

and

\displaystyle J^{2}|\lambda,m\rangle

\displaystyle=\lambda|\lambda,m\rangle

(1.47)

\displaystyle J_{3}|\lambda,m\rangle

\displaystyle=m|\lambda,m\rangle.

(1.48)

We can regroup
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
into the operators

\displaystyle J_{\pm}=J_{1}\pm iJ_{2}.

(1.49)

The states obtained by acting with
J_{\pm}
on

are again eigenstates of
J^{2}
(with eigenvalue
\lambda
) and
J_{3}
(with eigenvalue
m\pm 1
). In particular,
J_{\pm}
act as ladder operators, raising or lowering the value of

:

\displaystyle J_{\pm}|\lambda,m\rangle

\displaystyle=c_{\pm}(\lambda,m)|\lambda,m\pm 1\rangle

(1.50)

where
c_{\pm}(\lambda,m)
is a normalization constant that can be determined. From
J^{2}-J_{3}^{2}=J_{1}^{2}+J_{2}^{2}\geq 0
, we know the value of
m^{2}
is bounded from above by

. Using the notation

for the maximal value of

, you can show (Exercise) that
\lambda=j(j+1)
and

runs from
-j,-j+1,\cdots,j-1,j
. It is then natural to use

, which is called

, instead of
\lambda
to label the eigenstates.

The states

\displaystyle|j,m\rangle,\qquad m=-j,-j+1,\cdots,j-1,j

(1.51)

form a subspace in the Hilbert space that is left invariant by the action of the group
G=SO(3)
, in the sense that acting on such a state by a unitary group operator

, will give back a linear combination of states in that subspace. The group does not take you outside of the subspace. This is true because the action of the group generators is

\displaystyle J_{3}|j,m\rangle

\displaystyle=m|j,m\rangle,\qquad J_{\pm}|j,m\rangle\sim|j,m\pm 1\rangle.

(1.52)

The states (
1.51
) form an
irreducible representation
of the group: symmetry transformations transform elements into other states in the irreducible representation (‘irreducible’ because it is a minimal such set of states, that cannot be reduced further). The eigenvalue of the casimir

is insensitive to those symmetry transformations. The casimir

therefore identifies the label

for the irreducible representation as a physical organization label for the states, that does not distinguish between states that by symmetry are equivalent.

Returning to the full Poincaré group, the quadratic casimirs are given by

\displaystyle c_{2}^{\text{Poincar\'{e}}}=P^{2}=P_{\mu}P^{\mu},\qquad\tilde{c}_{4}^{\text{Poincar\'{e}}}=W^{2}=W_{\mu}W^{\mu}

(1.53)

with

. The first one measures the mass

. For

and

with eigenvalue

(the details are not important here). The casimirs of the Poincaré group thus suggest to use the mass

\displaystyle\phi(x)\rightarrow\phi(x)+\omega_{a}(x)\mathcal{F}_{a}\phi(x),\qquad\text{(all $\omega_{a}(x)$)}.

(1.68)

This is not a symmetry of the system, hence the corresponding variation of the action does not vanish

\omega_{a}

(1.69)

On a solution

of the EOM, we do have that the variation of the action must vanish for all
\omega_{a}(x)

\displaystyle\delta_{all}S[\phi^{*},\partial\phi^{*}]=0,\quad\forall\omega_{a}.

(1.70)

This implies, making use of partial integration, that

\displaystyle-\int d^{d}x\,\partial_{\mu}j^{\mu}_{a}[\phi^{*},\partial\phi^{*}]\,\omega_{a}=0,\quad\forall\omega_{a}

(1.71)

and thus

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}j^{\mu}_{a}[\phi^{*},\partial\phi^{*}]=0.

(1.72)

This expresses that

is a conserved current and it is called the

.
Di Francesco in

]

) to be

\displaystyle j^{\mu}_{a}=\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\partial_{\mu}\phi}\partial_{\nu}\phi-\delta_{\mu}^{\nu}\mathcal{L}\right)(t_{a}x^{\nu})-\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\partial_{\mu}\phi}\mathcal{F}_{a}\phi(x).

(1.73)

The corresponding conserved charge is given by integrating the time component of the Noether current over all space-like directions at a given instance of time

\displaystyle Q_{a}=\int d^{d-1}xj_{a}^{0}.

(1.74)

The expected remaining time dependence
Q_{a}(t)
has been left out of the notation because the charge is time-independent, i.e. conserved:

\displaystyle\frac{dQ_{a}}{dt}=\int d^{d-1}x\,\partial_{0}j_{a}^{0}=-\int d^{d-1}x\,\partial_{i}j_{a}^{i}=-\int_{\infty}j_{a}^{i}d\sigma^{i}=0

by assuming the currents vanish at infinity.

Let us identify the Noether current of translations. For translations,
\omega_{a}=a_{\nu}
and the above argument defines the Noether current

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}T^{\mu\nu}=0,

(1.77)

Applying (

\displaystyle T^{\mu\nu}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\partial_{\mu}\phi}\partial^{\nu}\phi-\eta^{\mu\nu}\mathcal{L}.

(1.78)

The conserved charge

(1.79)

In particular,

, allowing us to identify the Noether current for translations with the energy momentum tensor
T_{\mu\nu}
.

. Then the expression (

(1.80)

with

The classical field theory of the previous section can be quantized to a quantum field theory. One can employ either the operator formalism for this, leading to field operators

An insertion
\phi(x^{\mu})

[

In the
quantum

path integral

\displaystyle Z=\int\mathcal{D}\phi\,e^{-S[\phi]}

\displaystyle\langle\phi(x_{1})\cdots\phi(x_{n})\rangle=\frac{1}{Z}\int\mathcal{D}\phi e^{-S[\phi]}\phi(x_{1})\cdots\phi(x_{n}).

(1.87)

The quantum field theory will be invariant under a Poincaré transformation of the fields

\displaystyle\phi(x)\rightarrow\phi^{\prime}(x)=\phi(x)-i\omega_{a}G_{a}\phi(x)

(1.88)

if the path integral is. Both the action and the path integral measure will transform

\displaystyle S[\phi]

(1.89)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\phi

For a classical field theory with Poincaré invariance,

or

To find the answer, we follow an analogue argument to the one in section

. That is, we start by considering the transformation with
x

\displaystyle\phi(x)\rightarrow\phi^{\prime}(x)=\phi(x)-i\omega_{a}(x)G_{a}\phi(x),

(1.91)

under which the action and path integral measure will
not
be invariant unless
\omega_{a}(x)\rightarrow\omega_{a}
. The transformed path integral
Z^{\prime}
will take the form

\displaystyle Z^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}\phi^{\prime}e^{-S[\phi^{\prime}]}=\int\mathcal{D}\phi e^{-S[\phi]-\int d^{d}x\,j^{\mu}_{a}\partial_{\mu}\omega_{a}}\approx\int\mathcal{D}\phi e^{-S[\phi]}\left(1+\int d^{d}x\,(\partial_{\mu}j^{\mu}_{a})\omega_{a}(x)\right),

(1.92)

with the current

\omega_{a}(x)

and realize that the fields
\phi^{\prime}
are just dummy variables that are integrated over, and can be replaced by another integration dummy
\phi

should be equal to
Z=\int\mathcal{D}\phi e^{-S[\phi]}
(for all
\omega_{a}(x)

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{\mu}j^{\mu}_{a}\rangle=0\qquad\text{(quantum Noether theorem)}.

(1.93)

Similarly, we can use the same dummy variable argument to demand that the correlator

\displaystyle\langle\phi(x_{1})\cdots\phi(x_{n})\rangle=\frac{1}{Z}\int\mathcal{D}\phi e^{-S[\phi]}\phi(x_{1})\cdots\phi(x_{n})

(1.94)

equals

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z}\int\mathcal{D}\phi^{\prime}e^{-S[\phi^{\prime}]}\phi^{\prime}(x_{1})\cdots\phi^{\prime}(x_{n}),

(1.95)

which can be further written out to first order in
\omega_{a}(x)
as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z}\int\mathcal{D}\phi e^{-S[\phi]}\left(1+\int d^{d}x\,(\partial_{\mu}j^{\mu}_{a})\omega_{a}(x)\right)\left(\phi(x_{1})\cdots\phi(x_{n})-i\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int d^{d}x\,\omega_{a}(x)\delta(x-x_{i})\phi(x_{1})\cdots G_{a}\phi(x_{i})\cdots\phi(x_{n})\right).

It follows that

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}\langle j^{\mu}_{a}(x)\phi(x_{1})\cdots\phi(x_{n})\rangle=i\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta(x-x_{i})\langle\phi(x_{1})\cdots G_{a}\phi(x_{i})\cdots\phi(x_{n})\rangle

(1.96)

.

When

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}j^{\mu}_{a}=0,

(1.97)

meaning it holds (implicitly) within an expectation value
\langle\partial_{\mu}j^{\mu}_{a}\cdots\rangle=0
with other possible operator insertions
\cdots
present (at different locations).

We will now use the Ward identity to study the action of the conserved Noether charge operator
Q_{a}
on the field operator
\phi

(1.100)

\displaystyle[M_{\mu\nu},\phi(x)]=\left(m_{\mu\nu}+S_{\mu\nu}\right)\phi(x).

(2.29)

(The
(-i)
factors were dropped, working in Euclidean signature here.)
Another way to arrive at the last expression, is the following. In a rotation or Lorentz invariant QFT, local operators at the origin should transform in irreducible representations of the rotation or Lorentz group. We therefore impose that such a QFT contains field operators
\phi
that satisfy

\displaystyle[M_{\mu\nu},\phi(0)]=S_{\mu\nu}\phi(0)

(2.30)

with
S_{\mu\nu}
matrices (not operators) that satisfy the same algebra as
M_{\mu\nu}
. One can then use the algebra to obtain the action in (
2.29
) of
M_{\mu\nu}
on the translated field
\phi(x)=e^{x\cdot P}\phi(0)e^{-x\cdot P}
.

In a scale invariant theory, similarly we impose that the theory contains field operators
\phi
that diagonalize the dilation operator
D

\displaystyle[D,\phi(0)]=\Delta\,\phi(0).

(2.31)

The eigenvalue
\Delta
is called the
conformal dimension
of the field
\phi(x)
. It follows from the conformal algebra that

\displaystyle[D,\phi(x)]=(\text{d}+\Delta)\phi(x)

(2.32)

with
\text{d}=x^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}
.

The special conformal transformations
K_{\mu}
can now be seen to act as
lowering operators
. For this, consider
[D,[K_{\mu},\phi(0)]]
. By letting this work on the vacuum state of the CFT, we can introduce a more readable notation for
[D,[K_{\mu},\phi(0)]]
. The vacuum state
|0\rangle
of the CFT is annihilated by all the conformal generators

\displaystyle P_{\mu}|0\rangle=M_{\mu\nu}|0\rangle=D|0\rangle=K_{\mu}|0\rangle=0\qquad\text{(vacuum state)}.

(2.33)

It follows that

\displaystyle[Q_{\epsilon},\phi]|0\rangle=Q_{\epsilon}\phi|0\rangle

(2.34)

so that we can use the notation “
[Q_{\epsilon},\phi]=Q_{\epsilon}\phi
” when we implicitly let the expressions work on the vacuum state. Now considering

\displaystyle[D,[K_{\mu},\phi]]|0\rangle=DK_{\mu}\phi|0\rangle,

(2.35)

it is easy to show that

\displaystyle D\,\,K_{\mu}\phi(0)|0\rangle=(\Delta-1)\,K_{\mu}\phi(0)|0\rangle

(2.36)

or “
DK_{\mu}\phi(0)=(\Delta-1)K_{\mu}\phi(0)
”. That is, if
\phi
is an eigenoperator of the dilation operator
D
with eigenvalue
\Delta
, then
K_{\mu}\phi
is an eigenoperator of
D
with eigenvalue
\Delta-1
. Similarly, the translations
P_{\mu}
are
raising operators
1
1

1

Indeed, this means that
K_{\mu}^{\dagger}=P_{\mu}
, shown in
[
11
]
eq. (112).

,
P_{\mu}\phi
being an eigenoperator of
D
with eigenvalue
\Delta+1

\displaystyle D\,\,P_{\mu}\phi(0)|0\rangle=(\Delta+1)\,P_{\mu}\phi(0)|0\rangle.

(2.37)

The eigenvalue of
D
should be bounded from below, so there must exist eigenoperators of
D
in the theory for which
\Delta
can not be lowered further:

\displaystyle[K_{\mu},\phi(0)]=0.

(2.38)

Such fields are called
primary fields
of the CFT. To summarize, a primary field
\phi(x)
satisfies

\displaystyle[D,\phi(0)]=\Delta\,\phi(0)\quad\text{and}\quad[K_{\mu},\phi(0)]=0\qquad\text{(primary field)}

(2.39)

as well as still
[M_{\mu\nu},\phi(0)]=S_{\mu\nu}\phi(0)
and
[P_{\mu},\phi(x)]=\partial_{\mu}\phi(x)
.
Given a primary field
\phi
, we can construct operators
\varphi
of higher conformal dimension by acting with momentum generators. Such operators are called
descendants
,

\displaystyle\varphi(0)=[P_{\mu_{1}},\cdots,[P_{\mu_{n}},\phi(0)]]\qquad\text{(descendant fields)}

(2.40)

\displaystyle[D,\varphi(0)]=(\Delta+n)\varphi(0).

(2.41)

Local operators in a CFT are divided into two types: primaries or descendants.
The descendants can be written as (linear combinations of) derivatives of other local operators, the primaries cannot.
It can be proven that any operator in a unitary CFT is a linear combination of primaries and descendants
[
11
]
.

The exponentiated charge

\displaystyle U=e^{Q_{\epsilon}}

(2.42)

implements a finite conformal transformation. More precisely, an infinitessimal conformal transformation
[Q_{\epsilon},\phi]
exponentiates to

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}=U\phi U^{-1}.

(2.43)

This follows from application of the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula

\displaystyle e^{A}Be^{-A}=e^{[A,\cdot]}B=B+[A,B]+\frac{1}{2!}[A,[A,B]]+\cdots.

(2.44)

The definition of a primary operator is often given in terms of its finite transformation: a primary operator
\phi(x)
in the CFT on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a field that under conformal transformations
x\rightarrow x^{\prime}
transforms as

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}(x^{\prime})=\left|\frac{\partial x^{\prime}}{\partial x}\right|^{-\Delta/d}\phi(x)\qquad\text{(primary field)}

(2.45)

with
\left|\frac{\partial x^{\prime}}{\partial x}\right|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det g^{\prime}}}=\Omega^{-d/2}
. For example, under a dilation
x^{\prime}=\lambda x
, the transformation is
\phi^{\prime}(x^{\prime})=\phi^{\prime}(\lambda x)=\lambda^{-\Delta}\phi(x)
and
\left|\frac{\partial x^{\prime}}{\partial x}\right|=\lambda^{d}
.

2.1.3
Radial quantization and state-operator correspondence

Let us now consider a Euclidean CFT, and for simplicity start with a two-dimensional theory
d=2
. The background geometry is the plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, which can be parametrized by Euclidean coordinates
(x,y)
, complex coordinates
(z,\bar{z})
or polar coordinates
(r,\theta)

\displaystyle ds^{2}_{plane}=dx^{2}+dy^{2}=dzd\bar{z}=dr^{2}+r^{2}d\theta^{2}

(2.46)

with

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=x+iy,\quad\bar{z}=x-iy

(2.47)

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=re^{i\theta},\quad\bar{z}=re^{-i\theta},\quad\theta\sim\theta+2\pi.

(2.48)

To quantize the theory, a choice of time is made. This choice should respect the symmetries of the theory, but otherwise is quite arbitrary in the Euclidean theory. The typical choice is to use the vertical direction

|\psi\rangle
defined on each timeslice (

In our scale invariant theory, it is also natural to use the radial direction as our choice of ‘time’

\displaystyle D

Backwards evolution is stopped at the origin

Under this coordinate transformation, the metric becomes

It is conformally equivalent to the metric of the cylinder of radius

where we also introduced the complex coordinates on the cylinder,

(and rescaled

and

\displaystyle z=e^{iw},\qquad\bar{z}=e^{-i\bar{w}}\qquad\text{(plane to cylinder)}.

The conformal theory is insensitive to the conformal factor

in (

or the cylinder

on the cylinder, with dilations

Consider the state

at a finite time

As we can associate this state with a point,

. It is an axiom of CFT that becomes especially intuitive in radial quantization.

Note that radial quantization (

) also explains why it is so natural in a CFT to work with eigenoperators of the scaling generator

2.1.4
Primary and descendant states, conformal multiplet and OPE

Making use of the state-operator correspondence, the discussion of the field content of a CFT in section
2.1.2
immediately applies to states of a CFT as well. Namely, we can define a
primary state

|\mathcal{O}\rangle
as an eigenstate of the dilation operator and rotation/boost operator that is annihilated by special conformal transformations

\displaystyle\begin{split}K_{\mu}|\mathcal{O}\rangle&=0\\
D|\mathcal{O}\rangle&=\Delta|\mathcal{O}\rangle\qquad\quad\text{(primary state)}\\
M_{\mu\nu}|\mathcal{O}\rangle&=S_{\mu\nu}|\mathcal{O}\rangle.\end{split}

(2.56)

This follows directly from letting equations (
2.39
) and (
2.30
) work on the vacuum, and using (
2.55
).

Descendant states
are obtained from successively working with momentum operators on a primary state. Such descendant states

\displaystyle|\mathcal{O}\rangle,\quad P_{\mu}|\mathcal{O}\rangle,\quad P_{\mu}P_{\nu}|\mathcal{O}\rangle,\cdots

(2.57)

form the basis for a
conformal multiplet
. The state
\mathcal{O}(x)|0\rangle
e.g. can be expanded in this basis and thus belongs to the conformal multiplet.

The states in the conformal multiplet transform into each other under conformal transformations, they form an irreducible representation of the conformal group (
2.27
). Each conformal multiplet is labeled by the quantum number
\Delta
, which is the conformal dimension of the operator
\mathcal{O}
that creates the lowest weight state
|\mathcal{O}\rangle
of the multiplet. Another notation of the basis of the multiplet makes this more clear:

\displaystyle|\Delta\rangle,\quad|\Delta+1\rangle,\quad|\Delta+2\rangle,\cdots\,.

(2.58)

The conformal (quadratic) casimir

\displaystyle c_{2}^{conf}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}J_{ab}J^{ab}

(2.59)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}M_{\mu\nu}M^{\mu\nu}+D(D-d)-P_{\mu}K^{\mu}

(2.60)

does not distinguish between states belonging to one conformal multiplet. Working on such a state it extracts an eigenvalue
\Delta(\Delta-d)
. This is for spinless fields. Including spin
l
, the casimir value is
\Delta(\Delta-d)+l(l+d-2)
.

Just as in section
1.2.2
, where the Poincaré casimir instructs us to label particles in a Poincaré invariant theory by their mass and spin, we see that the conformal casimir instructs us to label the field content of a conformal invariant theory by the conformal dimension
\Delta
and spin
l
. Note that indeed we required a replacement of the notion of a mass, because a conformally invariant theory cannot contain massive particles. Any scale in the theory explicitly breaks conformal invariance. For example, the action of a massless boson
S=\int d^{d}x(\partial_{\mu}\phi\partial^{\mu}\phi)
is conformal, but the one of a massive boson
S=\int d^{d}x(\partial_{\mu}\phi\partial^{\mu}\phi+\frac{1}{2}m^{2}\phi^{2})
is not.

We can consider the state
O_{i}(x)O_{j}(0)|0\rangle
. Because every state is a linear combination of primaries and descendants, it can be decomposed as

\displaystyle O_{i}(x)O_{j}(0)|0\rangle=\sum_{k\in\text{primaries}}c_{ijk}(x,P)O_{k}(0)|0\rangle

(2.61)

where
c_{ijk}
is an operator that packages together primaries and descendants in the
k
-th conformal multiplet. The state-operator correspondence allows to write an operator equation version of the above statement:

\displaystyle O_{i}(x_{1})O_{j}(x_{2})=\sum_{k\in\text{primaries}}c_{ijk}(x_{12},\partial_{2})O_{k}(x_{2})\qquad\text{(OPE)}

(2.62)

(valid inside any correlation function where the other operators
O_{n}(x_{n})
have
|x_{2n}|>|x_{12}|
). This is called the ‘operator product expansion’ or OPE.
It expresses that two operators that come close together can be expanded in a complete set of local operators. The expansion coefficients
c_{ijk}
as expected are singular. However, we will see later that this is perfectly fine, and the way in which they diverge encodes important information about the CFT.

2.2
2-dimensional conformal field theory

In two dimensions
d=2
, the structure of a conformal field theory is much richer than in higher dimensions. The results of the previous chapter all still apply, but only form part of the discussion. This is because the conformal Killing equation in two dimensions has infinitely more solutions than the ones we have discussed so far.

We consider a 2D Euclidean CFT,
d=2
and
g_{\mu\nu}=\delta_{\mu\nu}
or

\displaystyle ds^{2}=dx^{2}+dy^{2}=dzd\bar{z}.

(2.63)

Here we introduced complex coordinates

\displaystyle z=x+iy,\qquad\bar{z}=x-iy.

(2.64)

In a moment, we will also need the relation between the partial derivatives in both coordinates

\displaystyle\partial_{z}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}-i\partial_{y}),\qquad\partial_{\bar{z}}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}+i\partial_{y}).

(2.65)

The conformal Killing equation (
2.6
) takes the form

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\epsilon_{x}=\partial_{y}\epsilon_{y},\qquad\partial_{x}\epsilon_{y}=-\partial_{y}\epsilon_{x}

(2.66)

which can be recognized as the Cauchy-Riemann equations of complex analysis. Rewritten in complex coordinates,

\displaystyle\partial_{\bar{z}}\epsilon^{z}(z,\bar{z})=0\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{z}\epsilon^{\bar{z}}(z,\bar{z})=0,

they express that the complex functions
\epsilon^{z}=\epsilon_{x}+i\epsilon_{y}
and
\epsilon^{\bar{z}}=\epsilon_{x}-i\epsilon_{y}
are respectively holomorphic (no
\bar{z}
dependence) and antiholomorphic (no
z
dependence) functions:

\displaystyle\epsilon^{z}(z),\qquad\epsilon^{\bar{z}}(\bar{z}).

(2.68)

We have found that in two dimensions, not just the
\epsilon^{\mu}
in (
2.7
) solve the conformal Killing equation, but
all

\epsilon^{\mu}
of the form (
2.68
) do.
The 2-dimensional conformal transformations are

\displaystyle z\rightarrow z^{\prime}=z+\epsilon^{z}(z),\qquad\bar{z}\rightarrow\bar{z}^{\prime}=\bar{z}+\epsilon^{\bar{z}}(\bar{z}).

(2.69)

From this, we can read off the conformal generators
t_{a}
, using the notation introduced in (
1.8
). We assume the functions
\epsilon^{z}(z)
and
\epsilon^{\bar{z}}(\bar{z})
can be expanded in a Laurent series around the origin

\displaystyle\epsilon^{z}(z)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}c_{n}z^{n+1},\qquad\epsilon^{\bar{z}}(\bar{z})=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\bar{c}_{n}\bar{z}^{n+1}.

(2.70)

Then the infinitessimal constants
\omega_{a}
in (

and

, and the generators

as

and

\displaystyle l_{n}\equiv z^{n+1}\partial_{z},\qquad\bar{l}_{n}\equiv\bar{z}^{n+1}\partial_{\bar{z}}\qquad(n\in\mathbb{Z}).

(2.71)

The algebra that they satisfy is the
Witt algebra

\displaystyle[l_{n},l_{m}]=(n-m)l_{n+m},\qquad[\bar{l}_{n},\bar{l}_{m}]=(n-m)\bar{l}_{n+m},\qquad[l_{n},\bar{l}_{m}]=0\qquad(n,m\in\mathbb{Z}).

(2.72)

(l_{-1},l_{0},l_{1})
and

\displaystyle\begin{split}[l_{0},l_{1}]=-l_{1},\quad[l_{1},l_{-1}]=2l_{0},\quad[l_{-1},l_{0}]=-l_{-1}\\

and

(and

and

In summary,

\displaystyle p_{z}=l_{-1},\quad k_{z}=\bar{l}_{1},\quad\text{d}=l_{0}+\bar{l}_{0},\quad m_{xy}=i(l_{0}-\bar{l}_{0}).

(2.74)

What sets these generators apart from the other

2.2.1
Conformal charges and algebra in 2 dimensions

Now let us discuss the generators at the quantum level. For a given choice of time, the Noether charge is given by

\displaystyle Q_{\epsilon}=\int d^{d-1}x\,j^{0}_{conf}(x)\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{confcurrent}}}{{=}}\quad\int d^{d-1}x\;\epsilon_{\nu}(x)T^{0\nu}(x)

(2.81)

with the conformal current integrated over a full time slice. In radial quantization, the time slices are origin-centered circles in the plane, and so the conformal charge in complex coordinates takes the form

\displaystyle Q_{\epsilon}=\oint_{0}\left(\frac{dz}{2\pi i}T_{zz}(z)\epsilon^{z}(z)+\frac{d\bar{z}}{2\pi i}T_{\bar{z}\bar{z}}(\bar{z})\epsilon^{\bar{z}}(\bar{z})\right).

(2.82)

Here, we have written holomorphic and antiholomorphic stress tensor components. The (anti)holomorphicity follows from writing out stress tensor conservation and tracelessness of the stress tensor (
2.17
) in complex coordinates.

When writing a contour integral
\oint_{\mathcal{C}}
, an integration contour
\mathcal{C}
should be specified. In the case at hand, the contour is a radial time slice (see Figure
2.4
). As long as there are no other operator insertions around, we are free to move the contour, i.e. contract it to smaller or larger circles (or even deform it to another shape). This topological nature of
Q_{\epsilon}
gives a very nice visualization of the conservation, i.e. time-independence, of the charge (
1.75
). In practical terms, it means it is sufficient to specify the contour in (
2.82
) by its center
0
. We will soon encounter a situation where we are required to also specify the radius of the contour.

Figure 2.4:
Topological charge conservation.

Radial quantization is particularly useful in two dimensions because it unlocks the power of complex analysis, including the techniques of contour integration. For example, radial quantization allows us to ‘see’ charge conservation (Figure
2.4
).

We are interested in the conformal algebra
[Q_{\epsilon_{1}},Q_{\epsilon_{2}}]
. In previous instances (Poincaré invariant and global conformally invariant theories), we made use of the general QFT result (
1.100
) that the conserved charge acts as a generator of the symmetry on fields
\phi
,

\displaystyle\delta_{\epsilon}\phi=[Q_{\epsilon},\phi],

(2.83)

to argue that the charges satisfy the same algebra as the generators of the symmetry acting on functions. In two dimensions, there will be a correction to this claim.

Let us first understand what is meant by the above commutator
[Q_{\epsilon},\phi]
when
Q_{\epsilon}
is given by a contour integral. For this, we go back to how this commutator notation was introduced in the derivation of equation (
1.100
). Namely, this followed from integrating the Ward identity over a particular stretched volume such that

\displaystyle\langle\delta\phi(t,\vec{x})\cdots\rangle=\langle Q(t_{+})\phi\cdots\rangle-\langle Q(t_{-})\phi\cdots\rangle\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle t_{\pm}\rightarrow t}}{{=}}\,\langle 0|[Q(t),\phi(t,\vec{x})]\cdots|0\rangle

(2.84)

where the implicit time ordering in the correlators
\langle Q(t_{\pm})\phi\cdots\rangle=\langle 0|\mathcal{T}\left(Q(t_{\pm})\phi\cdots\right)|0\rangle
leads to the equal time commutator of
Q
and
\phi
after taking the limit
t_{\pm}\rightarrow t
of the integration volume becoming infinitely stretched.

Applying this to a 2D Euclidean CFT in radial quantization, with
Q_{\epsilon}
given in (
2.82
):

\displaystyle\langle\delta_{\epsilon}\phi(w,\bar{w})\cdots\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\left(\oint_{|z|>|w|}-\oint_{|z|<|w|}\right)\left(\frac{dz}{2\pi i}T_{zz}(z)\epsilon^{z}(z)+\frac{d\bar{z}}{2\pi i}T_{\bar{z}\bar{z}}(\bar{z})\epsilon^{\bar{z}}(\bar{z})\right)\phi(w,\bar{w})\cdots\rangle\,

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle|z|\rightarrow|w|}}{{=}}\,\langle 0|[Q_{\epsilon}(|w|),\phi(w,\bar{w})]\cdots|0\rangle.

(2.85)

This time we had to specify the radius of the origin-centered contour in the definition of the charge with respect to the other operator insertion at location
(w,\bar{w})
, because we can’t simply deform the contour through that obstruction. The combination of contours appearing in
\delta_{\epsilon}\phi
is illustrated in figure
2.5
. From the figure, we can see it can be deformed into a final contour that wraps the location of the operator tightly. It follows that

\displaystyle\delta_{\epsilon}\phi(w,\bar{w})

\displaystyle=\oint_{(w,\bar{w})}\left(\frac{dz}{2\pi i}\epsilon^{z}(z)T_{zz}(z)\phi(w,\bar{w})+\frac{d\bar{z}}{2\pi i}\epsilon^{\bar{z}}(\bar{z})T_{\bar{z}\bar{z}}(\bar{z})\phi(w,\bar{w})\right)=[Q_{\epsilon}(|w|),\phi(w,\bar{w})].

(2.86)

Figure 2.5:
Contour for
[Q_{\epsilon},\phi]
calculation, visualizing
\oint_{|z|>|w|}-\oint_{|z|>|w|}=\oint_{(w,\bar{w})}
.
See e.g.
[
15
]
.

To work out the contour integral in
\delta_{\epsilon}\phi
we need to make sense of the operator product
T_{zz}\phi
. This is done by the OPE (
2.62
), and the operator product expansion
T_{zz}(z)\phi(w,\bar{w})
is in short referred to as the ‘
T\phi
OPE’. Then the contour integrals can be worked out explicitly by making use of Cauchy’s integral formulas

\displaystyle\oint_{a}\frac{dz}{2\pi i}\,\frac{f(z)}{z-a}=f(a),\qquad\oint_{a}\frac{dz}{2\pi i}\,\frac{f(z)}{(z-a)^{n+1}}=\frac{\partial^{n}f(a)}{n!}\qquad(n\geq 0).

(2.87)

The first formula can be remembered from working out that
\oint_{0}\frac{dz}{z}
in coordinates
z=e^{i\theta}
gives
2\pi i
. The second can be remembered form the first Cauchy formula and partial integration.

To summarize, knowledge of the conformal transformation behavior
\delta_{\epsilon}\phi
of a field
\phi
is equivalent (through Cauchy’s integral formulas) with knowledge of the
T\phi
OPE.
Similarly, knowledge of the transformation behavior of the stress tensor
\delta_{\epsilon}T_{ww}(w)
is equivalent to knowing the
TT
OPE, short for the operator product expansion
T_{zz}(z)T_{ww}(w)
. We are specifically interested at this point in
\delta_{\epsilon}T_{ww}(w)=[Q_{\epsilon},T_{ww}(w)]
because it is an intermediate step in deriving the conformal algebra
[Q_{\epsilon_{1}},Q_{\epsilon_{2}}]
.

We already know to a large extent what the conformal transformation behavior of the stress tensor should be. Namely,
T_{\mu\nu}
is a 2-tensor (an object with 2 spacetime indices) and transforms in the same way as the metric tensor,

\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu}(x)\rightarrow T_{\mu\nu}^{\prime}(x)=T_{\rho\sigma}(x^{\prime})\frac{\partial x^{\prime\rho}}{\partial x^{\mu}}\frac{\partial x^{\prime\sigma}}{\partial x^{\nu}}\quad\text{(active)},\qquad T_{\mu\nu}(x)\rightarrow T_{\mu\nu}^{\prime}(x^{\prime})=T_{\rho\sigma}(x)\frac{\partial x^{\rho}}{\partial x^{\prime\mu}}\frac{\partial x^{\sigma}}{\partial x^{\prime\nu}}\quad\text{(passive)}

(2.88)

under finite, global conformal transformations, in respectively the active or passive picture. Written out in complex coordinates for the holomorphic stress tensor component (the discussion for the antiholomorphic component is completely analogous),

\displaystyle T_{zz}^{\prime}(z^{\prime})=\left(\frac{\partial z^{\prime}}{\partial z}\right)^{-2}T_{zz}(z).\qquad\text{(global)}

(2.89)

Indeed this gives the correct transformation behavior under Lorentz transformations of the stress tensor as a spin 2 field (2-tensor), and under scale transformations of the stress tensor as a
\Delta=2
field. The naive scaling dimension
\Delta=2
of the stress tensor follows from dimensional analysis:
\int d^{d-1}x\,T^{00}=E
or
T^{00}
is an energy density with dimension (energy/volume) or (1/length
2
).

The corresponding infinitessimal, global conformal transformation of
T_{zz}
for
\delta z=\epsilon^{z}(z)
is

\displaystyle\delta_{\epsilon}T_{zz}(z)\equiv T^{\prime}_{zz}(z)-T_{zz}(z)=\epsilon^{z}(z)\partial_{z}T_{zz}(z)+2\,T_{zz}(z)\partial_{z}\epsilon^{z}(z)

(2.90)

By
\delta_{\epsilon}T_{zz}(z)=[Q_{\epsilon},T_{zz}(z)]
, yet another way to write the global conformal transformation behavior of
T_{zz}
is as the
TT
OPE

\displaystyle T_{zz}(z)T_{ww}(w)=\frac{2T_{ww}(w)}{(z-w)^{2}}+\frac{\partial T_{ww}(w)}{z-w}+\cdots

(2.91)

where
\cdots
are higher order terms in
(z-w)
that don’t survive the Cauchy integral. It’s only the divergent terms in the OPE that contain info about the transformation behavior. The stress tensor components have dimension 1/length
2
so the left hand side of the
TT
OPE has mass dimension
4
. You can check that the terms on the right hand side have the same mass dimension, as they of course should. However, there is one possible divergent term missing on the right hand side that is also allowed on dimensional grounds, namely

\displaystyle\frac{c/2}{(z-w)^{4}}.

(2.92)

The numerator should just be a dimensionless number, and it is by convention called
c/2
, with
c
an important parameter in a 2D CFT called the
central charge
. Including this allowed term in the stress tensor OPE, we have

\displaystyle T_{zz}(z)T_{ww}(w)

\displaystyle=\frac{c/2}{(z-w)^{4}}+\frac{2T_{ww}(w)}{(z-w)^{2}}+\frac{\partial T_{ww}(w)}{z-w}+\cdots

(2.93)

\displaystyle T_{\bar{z}\bar{z}}(\bar{z})T_{\bar{w}\bar{w}}(\bar{w})

\displaystyle=\frac{\bar{c}/2}{(\bar{z}-\bar{w})^{4}}+\frac{2T_{\bar{w}\bar{w}}(\bar{w})}{(\bar{z}-\bar{w})^{2}}+\frac{\bar{\partial}T_{\bar{w}\bar{w}}(\bar{w})}{\bar{z}-\bar{w}}+\cdots

(2.94)

with central charges
c
and
\bar{c}
.

Working our way back, the central charge terms give contributions to the infinitessimal and finite transformation behavior

\displaystyle\delta_{\epsilon}T_{zz}(z)

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{z}(z)\partial_{z}T_{zz}(z)+2\,T_{zz}(z)\partial_{z}\epsilon^{z}(z)+\frac{c}{12}\partial_{z}^{3}\epsilon^{z}(z)

(2.95)

\displaystyle T_{z^{\prime}z^{\prime}}^{\prime}(z)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial z^{\prime}}{\partial z}\right)^{2}T_{zz}(z^{\prime})+\frac{c}{12}\{z^{\prime},z\}\quad\text{(active)},\qquad T_{z^{\prime}z^{\prime}}^{\prime}(z^{\prime})=\left(\frac{\partial z^{\prime}}{\partial z}\right)^{-2}\left(T_{zz}(z)-\frac{c}{12}\{z^{\prime},z\}\right)\quad\text{(passive)}

(2.96)

\displaystyle\hskip 230.46748pt=\left(\frac{\partial z^{\prime}}{\partial z}\right)^{-2}T_{zz}(z)+\frac{c}{12}\{z,z^{\prime}\}\quad\text{(passive)}

where the accolades are the standard notation for the
Schwarzian derivative

\displaystyle\{f(z),z\}\,:=\frac{\partial_{z}^{3}f}{\partial_{z}f}-\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{\partial_{z}^{2}f}{\partial_{z}f}\right)^{2}

(2.97)

which has the property
\{w,v\}=-\left(\frac{dw}{dv}\right)^{2}\{v,w\}
.
We have found the
general transformation behavior of the stress tensor under conformal transformations
. Indeed, specifically for global conformal transformations (
2.78
),
f(z)=(az+b)/(cz+d)
, the Schwarzian derivative vanishes
\{f(z),z\}=0
(check as an exercise), so that the general transformation behavior is still consistent with the transformation behavior under global conformal transformations we started the argument from.

We were interested in the transformation behavior of the stress tensor in order to derive the conformal algebra of the conserved charges
Q_{\epsilon}
. Similarly as in the
d>2
CFT case, where for generators
\epsilon=p,m,d,k
we denoted the associated basis conserved charge generators
Q_{\epsilon}=P,M,D,K
with capital letters, in the
d=2
CFT case under consideration we use the notation
L_{n},\bar{L}_{n}

(n\in\mathbb{Z})
for the basis conserved charges associated with the generators
\epsilon=l_{n},\bar{l}_{n}

(n\in\mathbb{Z})
. That is, we expand a general conserved charge as

\displaystyle Q_{\epsilon}=\sum_{n}c_{n}L_{n}+\sum_{n}\bar{c}_{n}\bar{L}_{n}.

(2.98)

Then from (
2.82
) with (
2.70
), we can read off

\displaystyle L_{n}=\oint_{0}\frac{dz}{2\pi i}\,z^{n+1}T_{zz}(z),\qquad\bar{L}_{n}=\oint_{0}\frac{d\bar{z}}{2\pi i}\,\bar{z}^{n+1}T_{\bar{z}\bar{z}}(\bar{z}).

(2.99)

These are the
Virasoro generators
.
Inverting the contour integral by using the Cauchy formulas, we see that the Virasoro generators are the Laurent expansion modes of the stress tensor:

\displaystyle T_{zz}(z)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{L_{n}}{z^{n+2}},\qquad T_{\bar{z}\bar{z}}(\bar{z})=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\bar{L}_{n}}{\bar{z}^{n+2}}.

(2.100)

(Note that in cylinder coordinates this is just a Fourier expansion.)

Using the
TT
OPE, you can work out

\displaystyle[L_{n},L_{m}]

\displaystyle=\left(\oint\frac{dz}{2\pi i}\oint\frac{dw}{2\pi i}-\oint\frac{dw}{2\pi i}\oint\frac{dz}{2\pi i}\right)z^{n+1}T_{zz}(z)w^{m+1}T_{ww}(w)

(2.101)

\displaystyle=(n-m)L_{n+m}+\frac{c}{12}(n^{3}-n)\delta_{n+m,0}.

(2.102)

In absence of the central charge, this would just be the classical Witt algebra (
2.72
), which would express that the conserved charges
Q_{\epsilon}
satisfy the same algebra as the conformal generators
\epsilon
working on functions,
[Q_{\epsilon_{1}},Q_{\epsilon_{2}}]=Q_{-[\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}]}
, as we encountered for Poincaré symmetry and for global conformal symmetry. The second term is a
central charge term
contribution to, or
central extension
of, the conformal algebra at the
quantum
level. The possibility of this occurring was first discussed in section
1.2
. The central charge term has the property that it commutes with all elements of the algebra.

We have found the famous
Virasoro algebra
of 2D CFT, which is the unique central extension of the Witt algebra:

\displaystyle\begin{split}[L_{n},L_{m}]&=(n-m)L_{n+m}+\frac{c}{12}(n^{3}-n)\delta_{n+m,0}\\
[\bar{L}_{n},\bar{L}_{m}]&=(n-m)\bar{L}_{n+m}+\frac{\bar{c}}{12}(n^{3}-n)\delta_{n+m,0}\qquad\text{(Virasoro algebra)}\\
[L_{n},\bar{L}_{m}]&=0\end{split}

(2.103)

for
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
.
It has as a subalgebra the global algebra

\displaystyle[L_{n},L_{m}]=(n-m)L_{n+m},\quad[\bar{L}_{n},\bar{L}_{m}]=(n-m)\bar{L}_{n+m},\quad[L_{n},\bar{L}_{m}]=0\qquad(n,m=-1,0,1)

(2.104)

or

\displaystyle[L_{0},L_{1}]=-L_{1},\qquad[L_{1},L_{-1}]=2L_{0},\qquad[L_{-1},L_{0}]=-L_{-1}

(2.105)

so that we recover the global conformal algebra of conserved charges of the previous chapter, (
2.20
)-(
2.24
).

What is the physical interpretation of the central charge
c
of the 2D CFT? (When we refer to
the
central charge
c
, we are implicitly assuming that
\bar{c}=c
.)
If you take as an example of a 2D CFT the theory of a free boson and calculate the
TT
OPE, you would find (
2.93
) with
c=1
. If you take as an example the theory of
n
free bosons, you would find (
2.93
) with
c=n
. This indicates that
c
is a
measure for the number of degrees of freedom
in the 2D CFT.
It has additional physical interpretations, some of which will appear in section
7.2
:
c
for casimir,
c
for Cardy, and others which will not be discussed in the course (Weyl anomaly,
c
-theorem, …). Reference:
[
5
]
section 4.4.

2.2.2
Field content: Virasoro primaries and Virasoro descendants

The conformal symmetry now allows to organize the field content of the CFT.
The strategy is completely analogous to the general
d(>2)
-dimensional case in section
2.1.2
. There, we considered an eigenoperator
\phi^{(\Delta)}
of the dilation operator
D
,

\displaystyle[D,\phi^{(\Delta)}(0)]=\Delta\,\phi^{(\Delta)}(0).

(2.106)

It is labeled by its eigenvalue
\Delta
, called the conformal dimension of
\phi^{(\Delta)}
or in radial quantization (
D=H_{\tau}
) the ‘energy’.
(Note that it is therefore just as natural to consider eigenstates of
D
in a CFT as it is to consider energy eigenstates in regular quantum mechanics.)

For a 2-dimensional CFT, we write the dilation operator in terms of the Virasoro operators as
D=L_{0}+\bar{L}_{0}
. Using this Virasoro operator notation, eigenoperators
\phi^{(h,\bar{h})}

\displaystyle[L_{0},\phi^{(h,\bar{h})}(0)]=h\,\phi^{(h,\bar{h})},\qquad[\bar{L}_{0},\phi^{(h,\bar{h})}(0)]=\bar{h}\,\phi^{(h,\bar{h})}

(2.107)

are labeled by the eigenvalues
h
and
\bar{h}
, called the
conformal weights
of
\phi
. The field is implicitly or explicitly labeled by these values, and their sum gives the conformal dimension
\Delta=h+\bar{h}
.

The remaining
L_{n}
Virasoro operators are raising and lowering operators:
L_{n},\bar{L}_{n}

(n>0)
lower the eigenvalue by
n
units

\displaystyle L_{0}L_{n}\phi(0)=(h-n)L_{n}\phi(0),\qquad\bar{L}_{0}\bar{L}_{n}\phi(0)=(\bar{h}-n)\bar{L}_{n}\phi(0)

(2.108)

and
L_{-n},\bar{L}_{-n}

(n>0)
raise the eigenvalue by
n
units

\displaystyle L_{0}L_{-n}\phi(0)=(h+n)L_{n}\phi(0),\qquad\bar{L}_{0}\bar{L}_{-n}\phi(0)=(\bar{h}+n)\bar{L}_{-n}\phi(0).

(2.109)

That is, the
(L_{n}\phi^{(h,\bar{h})})
operator has an ‘energy’ that is
n
units smaller than
h
. The notation in the lines above assumes there is an implicit action left and right on the vacuum state
|0\rangle
so that the action of
L_{0}
applied after the action of
L_{n}
, namely
[L_{0},[L_{n},\phi]]
, can be written as
L_{0}L_{n}\phi
. (See a bit further below for the definition of the vacuum state. At this point it is just a matter of shortening the notation.)

We use the lowering operator to define a
Virasoro primary
and the raising operators to define
Virasoro descendants
. The idea is that the Virasoro primary is a field whose ‘energy’ eigenvalue cannot be lowered furthered, hence it is annihilated by all the lowering operators

\displaystyle\text{Virasoro primary }\phi:\qquad[L_{n},\phi(0)]=0,\qquad[\bar{L}_{n},\phi(0)]=0\qquad(n>0).

(2.110)

In particular, these conditions include the conditions
[L_{1},\phi(0)]=0=[\bar{L}_{1},\phi(0)]
and thus
[K_{\mu},\phi(0)]=0
, which is the defining condition for a primary field in (
2.39
). That is, a
Virasoro primary
is also a primary. But a primary is not a Virasoro primary, because the number of conditions in (
2.110
) is much larger. The distinction between the
d=2
concept of Virasoro primary field and global conformal concept of primary field is sometimes made by referring to what we call the primary as ‘global primary field’ or ‘quasi-primary’.

Now we can build operators that have higher dimension by acting with raising operators. This constructs the descendants

\displaystyle\text{Virasoro descendants of }\phi:\qquad(L_{-1})^{n}\phi(0),\quad L_{-n}\phi(0)

(2.111)

having an additional ‘energy’
n
. For example, first descendants
L_{-1}\phi,\bar{L}_{-1}\phi
have conformal weights
h+1,\bar{h}+1
thus conformal dimension
\Delta+1
. Second descendants
(L_{-1})^{2}\phi,(\bar{L}_{-1})^{2}\phi,L_{-1}\bar{L}_{-1}\phi,L_{-2}\phi,\bar{L}_{-2}\phi
have
\Delta+2
, etc. The Virasoro descendants contain the global descendants as defined in (
2.40
) (constructed by the action of
L_{-1},\bar{L}_{-1}
or the momentum operator) but also new types of descendants (constructed by the action of other
L_{-n}
).

For completeness, we give some other (equivalent) definitions of the Virasoro primary. One is in terms of its finite conformal transformation behavior

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}(z^{\prime},\bar{z}^{\prime})=\left(\frac{\partial z^{\prime}}{\partial z}\right)^{-h}\left(\frac{\partial\bar{z}^{\prime}}{\partial\bar{z}}\right)^{-\bar{h}}\phi(z,\bar{z})

(2.112)

under all
z\rightarrow f(z),\bar{z}\rightarrow\bar{f}(\bar{z})
. This includes the global conformal transformations
z\rightarrow\frac{az+b}{cz+d},\bar{z}\rightarrow\frac{\bar{a}\bar{z}+\bar{b}}{\bar{c}\bar{z}+\bar{d}}
as a subset. The Virasoro primary has conformal weights
h=\frac{\Delta+s}{2}
,
\bar{h}=\frac{\Delta-s}{2}
, and conformal dimension or ‘energy’
\Delta=h+\bar{h}
(the eigenvalue of
D=L_{0}+\bar{L}_{0}
) and spin
s=h-\bar{h}
(the eigenvalue of
M_{xy}=i(L_{0}-\bar{L}_{0})
, for
z=x+iy
).

Finally, the Virasoro primary can be defined as a field
\phi^{(h,\bar{h})}
with the following
T\phi
OPE:

\displaystyle T_{zz}(z)\phi^{(h,\bar{h})}(w,\bar{w})=\frac{h\,\phi^{(h,\bar{h})}(w,\bar{w})}{(z-w)^{2}}+\frac{\partial_{w}\phi^{(h,\bar{h})}(w,\bar{w})}{z-w}+\cdots

(2.113)

\displaystyle T_{\bar{z}\bar{z}}(\bar{z})\phi^{(h,\bar{h})}(w,\bar{w})=\frac{\bar{h}\,\phi^{(h,\bar{h})}(w,\bar{w})}{(\bar{z}-\bar{w})^{2}}+\frac{\partial_{\bar{w}}\phi^{(h,\bar{h})}(w,\bar{w})}{\bar{z}-\bar{w}}+\cdots.

(2.114)

Indeed, the
T\phi
OPE by the Cauchy formulas contains the same information as the transformation properties of
\phi
under conformal transformations. Therefore
\delta_{\epsilon}\phi=[Q_{\epsilon},\phi]=\oint\cdots T\phi
all provide different ways of packaging the same info (infinitesimal transformation behavior).

2.2.3
Virasoro primary and descendant states, Virasoro multiplet

By the state-operator correspondence, which is CFT-specific, the organizational structure of the field operator content translates directly into a corresponding organizational structure of the states.

The
vacuum state

|0\rangle
is defined as

\displaystyle L_{n}|0\rangle=0=\bar{L}_{n}|0\rangle\qquad(n\geq 1).

(2.115)

This includes the global conformal vacuum condition
L_{\pm 1,0}|0\rangle=0
as well as other conditions that ensure zero energy
\langle T_{zz}\rangle=0=\langle T_{\bar{z}\bar{z}}\rangle
in the vacuum state, with
T_{zz}(z)|0\rangle=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{L_{n}}{z^{n+2}}|0\rangle
and
T_{\bar{z}\bar{z}}(\bar{z})|0\rangle=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\bar{L}_{n}}{\bar{z}^{n+2}}|0\rangle
well-defined, using that the definition also implies
\langle 0|L_{n}^{\dagger}=\langle 0|L_{-n}=0

(n\geq-1)
.

An
asymptotic state
is defined as the state associated with a Virasoro primary operator (state-operator correspondence) as

\displaystyle\phi^{(h,\bar{h})}(0)|0\rangle=|\phi^{(h,\bar{h})}\rangle.

(2.116)

This state is alternatively denoted as
|h,\bar{h}\rangle
, in a closer notation to
|\Delta\rangle
in section
2.1.4
before:

\displaystyle|h,\bar{h}\rangle=\lim_{z,\bar{z}\rightarrow 0}\phi^{(h,\bar{h})}(z,\bar{z})|0\rangle.

(2.117)

The asymptotic state is an eigenstate

\displaystyle L_{0}|h,\bar{h}\rangle=h|h,\bar{h}\rangle,\qquad\bar{L}_{0}|h,\bar{h}\rangle=\bar{h}|h,\bar{h}\rangle.

(2.118)

The definition of the Virasoro primary operator (
2.110
), acting on the vacuum state, together with the definition of an asymptotic state, naturally imposes the definition of a
Virasoro primary state

\displaystyle L_{n}|\psi\rangle=0=\bar{L}_{n}|\psi\rangle\qquad(n>0).

(2.119)

A given Virasoro primary state
|\psi\rangle
carries with it a collection of Virasoro descendant states, e.g.
L_{-1}|\psi\rangle=|\partial\psi\rangle
with
h+1
eigenvalue,
(L_{-1})^{2}|\psi\rangle,L_{-2}|\psi\rangle
with
h+2
, etc. This collection of states is called the Virasoro multiplet or Verma module of the state, labeled by
(h,\bar{h})
.

Anti-de Sitter geometry

We have only considered flat spaces so far,
g_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}
or
\delta_{\mu\nu}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In this chapter, we discuss more general, curved geometries and their symmetries. We start with the sphere
S^{2}

3.1
Sphere
S^{2}

The sphere
S^{2}
is a 2-dimensional curved geometry that can be defined as a particular surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Namely, the points in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
that satisfy

\displaystyle X^{2}+Y^{2}+Z^{2}=L^{2}

(3.1)

lie on a 2D surface embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, which is the sphere of radius
L
. We will refer to this as an ‘embedding definition’ of
S^{2}
, and
\mathbb{R}^{3}
as the embedding space.

The flat metric on embedding space is

\displaystyle ds^{2}_{emb}=dX^{2}+dY^{2}+dZ^{2}=g_{ab}^{emb}dX^{a}dX^{b}

(3.2)

in terms of flat space coordinates

\displaystyle X^{a}=(X,Y,Z)\qquad(a=1,2,3).

(3.3)

The scalar product or dot product in different notations is

\displaystyle X^{2}=X\cdot X=X_{a}X^{a}=g_{ab}^{emb}X^{a}X^{b}.

(3.4)

The dot product is per definition invariant under rotations:

X^{\prime}_{b}=R_{ba}X_{a}
, then
X^{\prime}_{b}X^{\prime b}=X_{a}X^{a}
if
R^{T}R=\mathbf{1}
(with transpose
(R^{T})_{ab}=R_{ba}
), i.e. if
R\in O(3)
is a
3\times 3
orthogonal matrix, element of the orthogonal group (
(\det R)^{2}=1
), and
R\in
rotation group
SO(3)
if
\det R=1
.

The embedding definition of the sphere can be written in shorter notation as

\displaystyle X_{a}X^{a}=L^{2}.

(3.5)

SO(3)
transformations leave the
\mathbb{R}^{3}
dot product and thus the
S^{2}
definition invariant. It follows that
SO(3)
is the symmetry group or isometry group of
S^{2}
, or said otherwise that the three
SO(3)
generators

\displaystyle m_{ab}=X_{b}\partial_{a}-X_{a}\partial_{b}\qquad(a,b=1,2,3)

(3.6)

are
Killing vectors
of the sphere.

We’d prefer to use only 2 intrinsic coordinates to describe the 2-dimensional sphere, rather than 3 embedding coordinates satisfying the additional constraint (
3.1
). To this end we find a parametrization for which (
3.1
) is automatically satisfied

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=L\sin\theta\cos\phi

(3.7)

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=L\sin\theta\sin\phi

(3.8)

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=L\cos\phi.

(3.9)

This defines the intrinsic coordinates

\displaystyle x^{\alpha}=(\theta,\phi)\qquad(\alpha=1,2).

(3.10)

These are of course just the polar and azimuthal angle. The parametrization
X^{a}(x^{\alpha})
, with
X^{2}+Y^{2}=L^{2}\sin^{2}\theta
, is such that
\theta
and
\phi
have the interpretation of describing orbits of radius
L\sin\theta
and angle
\phi
in the
XY
plane of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

The induced metric on the sphere is then

\displaystyle ds^{2}

\displaystyle=L^{2}(d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta\,d\phi^{2})=g_{\alpha\beta}(x)dx^{\alpha}dx^{\beta}

(3.11)

with range
\theta\in(0,\pi)
and
\phi\in(0,2\pi)
. The metric of the sphere
g_{\alpha\beta}(x)
depends on the coordinates, indicating it is curved. With this metric given, you can compute properties of the geometry such as its curvature.

To find the symmetry of a given geometry, we ask what are the coordinate transformations

3.2
Hyperboloid

The 2D surface defined by the embedding definition

\displaystyle X^{2}+Y^{2}-Z^{2}=L^{2}

(3.22)

is a 1-sheeted hyperboloid, and

\displaystyle X^{2}+Y^{2}-Z^{2}=-L^{2},\qquad Z>0

(3.23)

is one half
H_{2}
of a 2-sheeted hyperboloid.

This time we cannot rewrite the left hand side of these definitions as a dot product in the embedding space (
3.2
), which was very helpful in the case of the sphere to discuss the isometry group. Therefore, we can instead consider a Lorentzian embedding space

\displaystyle ds^{2}_{emb}=dX^{2}+dY^{2}-dT^{2}=g_{ab}^{emb}dX^{a}dX^{b}

(3.24)

and define the hyperboloids as

\displaystyle X^{2}+Y^{2}-T^{2}

\displaystyle=L^{2}\qquad(\text{1-sheeted hyperboloid})

(3.25)

\displaystyle X^{2}+Y^{2}-T^{2}

\displaystyle=-L^{2},\qquad T>0\qquad(H_{2})

(3.26)

which can be rewritten

\displaystyle X_{a}X^{a}

\displaystyle=L^{2}\qquad(\text{1-sheeted hyperboloid})

(3.27)

\displaystyle X_{a}X^{a}

\displaystyle=-L^{2},\qquad T>0\qquad(H_{2}).

(3.28)

It can be shown that the hyperboloid surfaces defined in this way are still Euclidean surfaces. Using the Lorentzian embedding space now allows to argue that the isometry group of the hyperboloid is the group that leaves the dot product of the embedding space (
3.24
) invariant: the isometry group of
H_{2}
is
SO(2,1)
, or of
H_{n}
is
SO(n,1)
.

The hyperboloids have constant negative curvature.

4.1
Classical massless and massive particles

Consider a single massless particle at the center of global AdS. Such a particle will travel on lightlike paths.
From the Penrose diagram we can read off that it takes a finite time

1
1

1

This statement is related to the fact that AdS is not globally hyperbolic, i.e. there are no Cauchy hypersurfaces. This led to statements in ’70s general relativity literature that the “Cauchy problem is not well-posed in AdS”. The Cauchy problem being: given initial conditions in a spacelike slice, determine evolution of the system. The resolution is that the evolution of an AdS system requires, because of the presence of the timelike boundary, boundary conditions to be imposed at the boundary. Including the boundary conditions, the evolution problem is perfectly well-defined, and there is no issue

Typically, reflective boundary conditions will be imposed. The photon will then bounce back at the boundary and follow a periodic path of period

Diffeomorphism invariance

This action is diffeomorphism invariant, in short diff invariant, or “general relativity is diff invariant”

\displaystyle S_{grav}[g_{{\alpha\beta}}(x),\psi(x)]\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle x\rightarrow f(x)}}{{=}}\quad S_{grav}[f_{*}g_{\alpha\beta}(x),f_{*}\psi(x)].

(4.15)

In GR (general relativity), we’re working with a manifold
M
with metric
g_{\alpha\beta}
and matter fields
\psi
, or a system
(M,g_{\alpha\beta},\psi)
. A mapping
f:M\rightarrow M
is a diff if
(M,g_{\alpha\beta},\psi)
and
(M,f_{*}g_{\alpha\beta},f_{*}\psi)
represent the same physical situation, with
f_{*}
the pullback defined below. Diffeomorphisms are just another name for active coordinate transformations (see e.g.
[
22
]
and
[
3
]
section 2.4). In the passive picture,
x\rightarrow x^{\prime}
, a field transforms
\phi(x)\rightarrow\phi^{\prime}(x^{\prime})
. In the active picture,
x\rightarrow x
and
\phi(x)\rightarrow\phi^{\prime}(x)
. The transformed field evaluated at the original position
x
is called the pullback. If
x^{\prime}=f(x)
in the passive picture, the pullback is denoted as
\phi^{\prime}(x)=f_{*}\phi(x)
. The pullback is also used to define the Lie derivative of
\phi
along

as
\mathcal{L}_{X}\phi=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{\phi^{\prime}(x)-\phi(x)}{\epsilon}
where
\phi^{\prime}(x)
is the pullback

for

a small displacement of the point
x
along the flow lines of the vector field
X

Because the metric is a dynamical variable, there is no prior geometry in GR and thus no

coordinate system for spacetime (in contradistinction to the discussions of field theory on a fixed background in the first chapters of the course). The diff invariance of GR is a gauge invariance: not a physical symmetry, but an expression of a redundancy in the description of the system. To describe the gauge invariant physics, the diff invariance should be fixed by a gauge choice, i.e. a choice of coordinates.

Variational problem

Let’s consider the variational problem. We vary the action while imposing boundary conditions that fix the metric and matter fields at the boundary of the manifold,
\delta g_{\alpha\beta}|_{\partial M}=0

\delta\psi|_{\partial M}=0

\displaystyle R_{\alpha\beta}-\frac{1}{2}R\,g_{\alpha\beta}+\Lambda g_{\alpha\beta}=8\pi G\,T_{\alpha\beta}^{M}(\psi),

(4.16)

and the Euler-Lagrange equations for the matter fields, e.g.

-functions as the gravitational stress tensor components, we see that the effect of the Brown-Henneaux diffs is

\displaystyle\begin{split}\delta_{\xi}T_{zz}^{BY}&=-\epsilon(z)\partial T_{zz}^{BY}-2T_{zz}^{BY}\partial\epsilon-\frac{\ell}{8G}\partial^{3}\epsilon(z)\\
\delta_{\xi}T_{\bar{z}\bar{z}}^{BY}&=-\bar{\epsilon}(\bar{z})\bar{\partial}T_{\bar{z}\bar{z}}^{BY}-2T_{\bar{z}\bar{z}}^{BY}\bar{\partial}\bar{\epsilon}-\frac{\ell}{8G}\bar{\partial}^{3}\bar{\epsilon}(\bar{z}).\end{split}

(4.33)

We recognize this as the transformation behavior of a 2D
CFT
stress tensor (
2.95
), in a CFT with a specific central charge given by

\displaystyle c=\frac{3\ell}{2G}\qquad\text{(Brown-Henneaux central charge)}.

(4.34)

Indeed, the Brown-Henneaux diffs can be seen to take the form of 2D conformal transformations of the boundary coordinates
z
and
\bar{z}
at the boundary
r\rightarrow\infty
. We see that symmetry of the asymptotic
geometry
of AdS
3
coincides with conformal symmetry of a 2D
CFT
at the conformal boundary of AdS
3
!

The more concrete statement is that the conserved charges associated with the asymptotic symmetry of an asymptotically AdS
3
geometry obey a Virasoro algebra with a central charge
c=\frac{3\ell}{2G}
. Namely, if we expand the Brown-York stress tensor components into modes

\displaystyle T_{zz}^{BY}(z)=\sum_{n}\frac{L_{n}^{BY}}{z^{n+2}},\qquad T_{\bar{z}\bar{z}}^{BY}(\bar{z})=\sum_{n}\frac{\bar{L}_{n}^{BY}}{\bar{z}^{n+2}}

(4.35)

then the conserved charges
Q^{BY}_{\xi}
associated with the Brown-Henneaux diffs
\xi
take the form
Q^{BY}_{\xi}=\sum_{n}c_{n}L_{n}^{BY}+\sum_{n}\bar{c}_{n}\bar{L}_{n}^{BY}
. They are classical charges, with a Poisson bracket algebra
\{Q^{BY}_{\xi_{1}},Q^{BY}_{\xi_{2}}\}_{PB}
. The resulting algebra (given here without derivation) is the Virasoro algebra

\displaystyle\begin{split}\{L_{n}^{BY},L_{m}^{BY}\}_{PB}&=(n-m)L^{BY}_{n+m}+\frac{\ell}{8G}(n^{3}-n)\delta_{n+m,0}\\
\{\bar{L}_{n}^{BY},\bar{L}_{m}^{BY}\}_{PB}&=(n-m)\bar{L}^{BY}_{n+m}+\frac{\ell}{8G}(n^{3}-n)\delta_{n+m,0}\\
\{L_{n}^{BY},\bar{L}_{m}^{BY}\}_{PB}&=0.\end{split}

(4.36)

As we have seen before, the algebra of conserved charges (
4.36
) contains the same information as the transformation behavior of the stress tensor (
4.33
). From both points of view a Virasoro symmetry with central charge (
4.34
) emerges. This result was obtained by Brown and Henneaux in 1986. It is a pre-AdS/CFT result that nowadays is interpreted as an AdS/CFT statement about the duality between AdS
3
gravity (with gravitational parameters
\ell
and
G
) and a 2D CFT (with central charge parameter
c
related to the dual parameters by (
4.34
)).

Emergent connection to 2D CFT

Before addressing some comments about the Brown-Henneaux result, let us first summarize the emergent connection to 2D CFT. We have seen that the asymptotic symmetry transformations of AdS
3
at the AdS boundary equal the (local) conformal transformations of a 2D CFT with
c=3\ell/2G
. In higher dimensions, the asymptotic isometry group of AdS
d+1
(
d>2
) is just the same as the isometry group
SO(d,2)
of AdS
d+1
. Combined with the boxed result of section
3.4
(which also applies to
d=2
but as a statement about the global conformal transformations of the CFT), we conclude that:

The asymptotic isometry transformations of AdS
d+1
geometry at the AdS conformal boundary
equal the conformal transformations of a CFT
d
.

Another consequence of the Brown-Henneaux result is that:

The (classical) Brown-York stress tensor of asymptotically AdS
3
gravity has the properties of (the expectation value of) a 2D CFT stress tensor.

Central charge appears at classical level?

In section
1.2
we first discussed the possibility of a central charge appearing in the algebra of conserved charges at the
quantum level
, reflecting the phase factor ambiguity of a quantum state. How does the physics of
classical
asymptotic AdS
3
gravity give rise to a central charge usually associated with quantum indeterminacy? The answer is in the presence of the boundary, which leads to an ambiguity of the classical charge and a corresponding central charge term in the algebra. For more details, including the relation to the comment discussed in the next paragraph, see
[
26
]
(section 3.1 and 2).

Physical diffs?

Another question concerns the interpretation of diffs in gravity. In the beginning of section
4.2
we paused at the interpretation of diff invariance in gravity as an unphysical or ‘gauge’ redundancy. But the Brown-Henneaux diffs give rise to the physics described by the Virasoro algebra. How is it that these diffs do have a physical interpretation? Again, it is the presence of the boundary of AdS that provides the answer. Namely, GR is locally diff invariant, but not invariant under diffs that reach the boundary. Some of the diffs that reach infinity are actual symmetries. This is best illustrated by an example. A time reparametrization with compact support
t\rightarrow t^{\prime}(t,x)
such that
t^{\prime}\rightarrow t
at
r\rightarrow\infty
is a local diff (no physics). In contrast, a global time shift
t\rightarrow t+1
acts at infinity, describing true time evolution (physics). Roughly, “local diffs are fake, global diffs are real”
[
23
]
.

4.2.2
Hints of AdS
3
/CFT
2
: Ryu-Takayanagi formula

As we will see later in more detail, there is an important relation between the classical gravitational concept of geodesics in AdS
3
and the quantum mechanical concept of entanglement entropy in a 2D CFT. It is called the Ryu-Takayanagi relation.

In AdS
3
,
ds^{2}=\frac{dZ^{2}-dt^{2}+dx^{2}}{Z^{2}}
, boundary-anchored geodesics are given by half-circles
x^{2}+Z^{2}=\left(\frac{L}{2}\right)^{2}
. Such a geodesic has a length

\displaystyle\int ds=2\ell\int_{\epsilon}^{L/2}\frac{dZ}{Z}\sqrt{x^{\prime}(Z)^{2}+1}=2\ell\log\frac{L}{\epsilon}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon).

(4.37)

The Ryu-Takayanagi formula identifies
\frac{1}{4G}
times the length of the boundary-anchored AdS
3
geodesic with the entanglement entropy of the interval at the conformal boundary that the geodesic anchors to. Indeed, using the Brown-Henneaux central charge we find

\displaystyle\frac{\text{length geodesic}}{4G}=\frac{c}{3}\log\frac{L}{\epsilon}.

(4.38)

The right hand side can be identified as the expression for the entanglement entropy for an interval of length
L
in a 2D CFT. (We have not derived this expression here. It can be derived using for example the replica trick
[
27
]
.)

4.3
Conformal matter on conformal boundary of AdS

In this chapter we have considered adding matter and gravity to the AdS geometry

\displaystyle ds^{2}=\frac{dZ^{2}-dt^{2}+d\vec{x}^{2}}{Z^{2}}

(4.39)

introduced in the previous chapter. The logical procedure is to add dynamical degrees of freedom in the bulk of the AdS spacetime. But it is also perfectly well-defined to take some conformal matter and place it on the AdS conformal boundary

\displaystyle ds^{2}=-dt^{2}+d\vec{x}^{2}

(4.40)

(while leaving the bulk of AdS untouched).
The result is a
d
-dimensional CFT living at the conformal boundary of AdS
d+1
.

AdS/CFT duality

The statement of AdS/CFT duality is that in fact adding conformal matter to the AdS conformal boundary is physically equivalent to adding gravity to the AdS bulk. Indeed we have already encountered some hints for this duality in the previous section when discussing gravity in AdS
3
. It was found that the same physics appear in the asymptotics of AdS
3
gravity and 2D CFT, more precisely in the Virasoro algebra and in the description of bipartitioning the system (across a geodesic in the bulk or across an interval in the CFT). In both instances, the physical effect (i.e. central charge extension of Virasoro algebra or appearance of
\frac{c}{3}\log\frac{L}{\epsilon}
in bipartioning of system) has a classical origin in the gravity theory but a quantum origin in the dual CFT.

Classical gravity in asymptotically AdS
d+1
geometry is dual to a quantum theory without gravity but with conformal symmetry at the
d
-dimensional conformal boundary.

This is a very bold statement, claiming that theories that we usually consider to be fundamentally different are in fact describing the same physics. The rest of the course is devoted to further investigating
this claimed duality.

AdS/CFT dictionary

5.1
Quantum gravity (QG)

Planck scale

In
D
-dimensional gravity, the Newton constant has the dimension of length to the power
D-2
,

\displaystyle[G]=L^{D-2}.

(5.1)

Usually gravity is weak, but at the Planck scale

\displaystyle\ell_{P}=(\hbar G)^{\frac{1}{D-2}}

(5.2)

QM interactions
(\hbar)
and gravity
(G)
become comparably strong, and a QM treatment of gravity forces itself. In
D=4
for example,
\ell_{P}=\sqrt{\hbar G}\approx 10^{-33}\text{cm}
. (We only sporadically reintroduce
\hbar
, using natural units
\hbar=1
through most of the course.)
The Planck mass is

\displaystyle M_{P}=\frac{1}{\ell_{P}}

(5.3)

and is a very high energy scale.

In a gravitational theory, we have an action
S_{grav}[g_{\alpha\beta},\psi]
for dynamical fields
\psi
and
g_{\alpha\beta}
.
In contrast to a field theory, the metric
g_{\alpha\beta}
is itself dynamical. Still, you could attempt to quantize gravity (
4.13
) using a background field method

\displaystyle g_{\alpha\beta}=\bar{g}_{\alpha\beta}+\sqrt{G}\,\,h_{\alpha\beta}=\bar{g}_{\alpha\beta}+\left(\frac{1}{M_{P}}\right)^{\frac{D-2}{2}}h_{\alpha\beta}

(5.4)

where only the metric fluctuations
h_{\alpha\beta}
are dynamical.
The
\sqrt{G}
prefactor is included to ensure a canonical kinetic term for the metric fluctuations
\int\partial h\partial h
in the
\frac{1}{M_{P}}
expansion of the gravitational action around
\bar{g}_{\alpha\beta}
. The expanded action will contain non-linear interactions to all orders in
h_{\alpha\beta}
, with a dimensionful coupling constant
G
(
5.1
).
This means the theory is
non-renormalizable
for
D>2
.

One can still make sense of the non-renormalizable theory in an
effective field theory
(EFT) sense.

Gravity as an EFT

References for more details:
[
23
]
p.8 and further, Donoghue gr-qc/9512024 and
http://www.scholarpedia.org/article/Quantum_gravity_as_a_low_energy_effective_field_theory
.

To treat gravity as an EFT, one must first write down the most general possible action consistent with the symmetries, and organize the terms in increasing order of derivatives. The starting assumption is that nature has a graviton, i.e. a massless spin-2 field. Consistency (diff invariance) imposes that the action takes the form of the EH action of GR plus an infinite number of terms which are higher order in curvatures
R,R_{\alpha\beta},\cdots
or derivatives (remember
R\sim\partial\partial g
)

\displaystyle S_{grav}[g_{\alpha\beta},\psi]=-\frac{1}{16\pi G}\int d^{D}x\sqrt{-g}\left(-2\Lambda+R+c_{1}R^{2}+c_{2}R_{\alpha\beta}R^{\alpha\beta}+c_{3}R_{{\alpha\beta}\gamma\delta}R^{{\alpha\beta}\gamma\delta}+\cdots\right)+S_{M}.

(5.5)

Choose a fixed order in derivatives up to which you will keep the terms in the action. You are allowed to do quantum field theory with this action, including loops, in a perturbative expansion (
5.4
). Quantizing the metric fluctuations
h_{\alpha\beta}
(which describe gravitational waves at the classical gravity level) into gravitons is similar in spirit to quantizing sound waves into phonons, i.e. quantized effective particles.
However, the answer you get can only be trusted if the neglected terms in the derivative expansion of the action are much smaller than the terms you kept. Because the theory is non-renormalizable, the perturbative expansion will break down at high energies. The point is that
at low energies

E<M_{P}
, the quantized EFT is a perfectly good quantum theory of gravity. It describes the low-energy phenomenology of a more fundamental theory, which is expected to involve new degrees of freedom at or above the Planck energy scale. The more fundamental UV theory is often referred to as the
UV-completion
of gravity.

We will not be concerned with the higher curvature corrections to the EH action in this course. We are allowed to neglect them when the AdS length
\ell
is large compared to the length scale
\ell_{s}
of new physics

\displaystyle\ell_{s}\ll\ell.

(5.6)

This is because the coefficients
c_{1,2,3,...}
scale with powers of
\ell_{s}
, by dimensional analysis and the rules of EFT. The length scale of new physics is smaller or equal than the Planck scale
l_{s}\leq l_{P}
.
The notation
\ell_{s}
refers to the fundamental scale of string theory, which is one proposed UV completion of quantized gravity. Another proposed UV completion is a lower-dimensional CFT, through (the strong version of) the AdS/CFT correspondence.

Gravitational path integral

In section (
1.4
), we reviewed how a field theory with action
S[\psi]
on a fixed spacetime manifold
M
can be quantized in the path integral formalism by integrating over fields defined on
M
. In quantum gravity, we must also integrate over the dynamical metric, giving rise to the gravitational path integral (in Euclidean signature)

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}g\mathcal{D}\psi\,e^{-S_{grav}^{E}[g_{\alpha\beta},\psi]}

(5.7)

with

\displaystyle S_{grav}^{E}[g_{\alpha\beta},\psi]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{16\pi G}\int_{M}d^{d+1}x\sqrt{g}(R-2\Lambda)+S_{M}[g_{\alpha\beta},\psi]-\frac{1}{8\pi G}\int_{\partial M}d^{d}x\sqrt{\gamma}\,K.

(5.8)

Boundary conditions are specified at infinity
\partial M
, for the matter fields as well as the geometry.

The gravitational path integral is in most cases only a formal expression that we don’t know how to actually evaluate. What we can do is approximate it by expanding it around a saddle point

\displaystyle Z\approx e^{-S_{grav}^{E}[g_{\alpha\beta}^{*},\psi^{*}]+S^{(1)}+\cdots}.

(5.9)

Here,
g_{\alpha\beta}^{*}
and
\psi^{*}
are solutions to the classical equations of motion forming a ‘classical saddlepoint’ for the path integral. It gives an order
1/G
contribution to the exponent.
The next term
S^{(1)}
is the one-loop term of order
G^{0}
, and the dots are higher-loop contributions.

Reinstating
\hbar
as the unit in which the action is measured in the path integral exponent
Z=\int\mathcal{D}g\mathcal{D}\psi

\,e^{-\frac{1}{\hbar}S_{grav}^{E}[g_{\alpha\beta},\psi]}
, it is clear that the expansion in
G
from the previous paragraph is in fact an expansion in
\hbar G
. The classical limit is

\displaystyle\hbar G\rightarrow 0\qquad\text{(classical)}

(5.10)

and quantum corrections are higher order in
\hbar G
.
The leading
Z
is often referred to as the
semi-classical path integral

\displaystyle Z\approx e^{-\frac{1}{\hbar}S_{grav}^{E}[g_{\alpha\beta}^{*},\psi^{*}]}

(5.11)

(and sometimes as the ‘classical path integral’).
It describes a
semi-classical approximation
of the theory, valid for small
\hbar G
.
Since
\hbar G
has dimensions, of length to the power
D-2
to be precise (when
\hbar=1
), the limit of small
\hbar G
more correctly is phrased as the limit where the Planck length is small compared to the curvature length scales

\displaystyle\ell_{P}\ll\ell\qquad\text{(semi-classical gravity)}.

(5.12)

5.2
String theory background

Not for exam. Only mentioning the take-away result for the progression to the next section.

The historical route to AdS/CFT proceeded through a string theory derivation involving different interpretations of string theoretic objects called D-branes. It lead Maldacena to formulate his AdS/CFT conjecture in 1997:

Moderate AdS/CFT

Classical supergravity in AdS
{}_{5}\times S^{5}

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{is dual to}}}{{\sim}}

\mathcal{N}=4
SYM theory with gauge group
U(N)
.

SYM is short for super Yang-Mills. There is supersymmetry on each side of the correspondence, hence the words ‘super’.
\mathcal{N}
is the number of corresponding supercharges. The field theory is a particular CFT that lives on the
(3+1)
-dimensional conformal boundary of AdS
5
. The classical supergravity limit on the left hand side corresponds on the right hand side to the strongly coupled limit of the CFT combined with the planar limit of large
N
. Since this large
N
CFT has a conjectured dual description that is higher-dimensional, it is called a
holographic CFT
.

The stronger statement is that:

Strong AdS/CFT

Super string theory (type IIB) in AdS
{}_{5}\times S^{5}

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{is dual to}}}{{\sim}}

\mathcal{N}=4
SYM theory with gauge group
U(N)
.

In this case there are no restrictions on the coupling constant and size of gauge fields of the CFT.

5.3
Statement of the AdS/CFT correspondence

The Maldacena conjecture can be formulated in a more general way as follows.

Strong AdS/CFT

A theory of QG in asymptotically AdS
{}_{d+1}\times Q

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{is dual to}}}{{\sim}}
a CFT on the cylinder
\mathbb{R}\times S^{d-1}

Here
Q
is some (possibly trivial) compact manifold. The gravity side of the duality is commonly referred to as the
bulk
, and the CFT side as the
boundary
.
In this formulation, the lower-dimensional CFT living on the conformal boundary of asymptotically AdS
d+1
is providing a
definition
of a theory of quantum gravity in an asymptotically AdS universe. In the modern viewpoint, AdS/CFT can be seen as a stand-alone approach to quantum gravity (without string theory as origin). The challenge is to unravel the dictionary between the two sides of the duality. The AdS spacetime is an emergent low-energy description of the bulk QG theory, and the UV-completion of the bulk is to be understood in terms of degrees of freedom of the dual CFT, which is a perfectly well-defined and UV-complete theory.

In practice, it is already very difficult to understand the duality between a (semi-)classical theory of gravity and a CFT, which is the more moderate version of AdS/CFT:

Moderate AdS/CFT

Classical gravity in asymptotically AdS
{}_{d+1}\times Q

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{is dual to}}}{{\sim}}
a
holographic
CFT on
\mathbb{R}\times S^{d-1}
.

The word
holographic
refers to conditions on the field theory side that make sure the dual theory is semi-classical. The main condition is that there is a parameter
N
in the CFT that is large. It can be the size of the gauge fields of the theory. In 2D CFT’s it is related to the central charge
c
of the theory,
N=c^{2}
.
We saw in section
4.2.1
that the central charge of a 2D CFT is related to the gravitational parameters of an AdS
3
gravity theory by (
4.34
). From that expression it indeed follows that large central charge
c
in a holographic CFT corresponds to small gravitational coupling
G\ll\ell
or
\ell_{P}\ll\ell
, consistent with the semi-classical limit (
5.12
) in the bulk.

Returning to the historical path of AdS/CFT,
we consider in the rest of this chapter the original AdS/CFT dictionary by Witten and Gubser Klebanov Polyakov ’98. It provides a practical recipe for computing large
N
CFT correlation functions from tree diagrams in AdS gravity (when the length scale of AdS is large), which was used to obtain many tests of the duality.
We will illustrate the dictionary by using it to calculate the CFT 2-point function from a gravitational bulk perspective.

5.4
GKPW dictionary and calculation of CFT 2-point function

Consider a
d
-dimensional CFT with action
S_{CFT}[\mathcal{O}]
for fields
\mathcal{O}
, and a (Euclidean signature) path integral
Z=\int\mathcal{D}\mathcal{O}\,e^{-S_{CFT}[\mathcal{O}]}
.
The main objects of study that contain physics are the correlation functions

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{O}(x_{1})\cdots\mathcal{O}(x_{n})\rangle:=\frac{1}{Z}\int\mathcal{D}\mathcal{O}\,\,\mathcal{O}(x_{1})\cdots\mathcal{O}(x_{n})e^{-S_{CFT}[\mathcal{O}]}.

(5.13)

The conformal invariance of the CFT imposes that these correlators obey the condition

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{O}\cdots\mathcal{O}\rangle=\langle\mathcal{O}^{\prime}\cdots\mathcal{O}^{\prime}\rangle

(5.14)

short for

\langle\mathcal{O}(x_{1}^{\prime})\cdots\mathcal{O}(x_{n}^{\prime})\rangle=\langle\mathcal{O}^{\prime}(x_{1}^{\prime})\cdots\mathcal{O}^{\prime}(x_{n}^{\prime})\rangle
.
(For the derivation, see the video lecture.)

A primary field
\mathcal{O}(x)
with conformal dimension
\Delta
transforms under a (global) conformal transformation to
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}(x^{\prime})
given in (
2.45
). Then the above condition on the 2-point correlator of primary fields imposes

\displaystyle\langle O(x_{1})O(x_{2})\rangle=\left|\frac{\partial x^{\prime}}{\partial x}\right|^{\Delta/d}_{x=x_{1}}\left|\frac{\partial x^{\prime}}{\partial x}\right|^{\Delta/d}_{x=x_{2}}\langle O(x^{\prime}_{1})O(x^{\prime}_{2})\rangle.

(5.15)

This has to be true for all
x\rightarrow x^{\prime}
conformal transformations (translations, rotations, scale transformations and special conformal transformations). This requirement is enough to fix the 2-point function to

\displaystyle\langle O(x_{1})O(x_{2})\rangle=\frac{1}{|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{2\Delta}}

(5.16)

independent of the details of the CFT and fixed entirely by conformal symmetry. The goal of this section is to rederive this result from a calculation in AdS gravity.

The definition of the correlation function can be rewritten in terms of a so-called generating functional

\displaystyle Z_{CFT}[\phi_{0}]\equiv\frac{1}{Z}\int\mathcal{D}\mathcal{O}\,e^{-S_{CFT}[\mathcal{O}]+\int d^{d}x\,\phi_{0}(x)\mathcal{O}(x)}\quad(=\langle e^{\int d^{d}x\,\phi_{0}(x)\mathcal{O}(x)}\rangle_{CFT})

(5.17)

as

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{O}(x_{1})\cdots\mathcal{O}(x_{n})\rangle:=\left.\frac{\delta^{n}Z_{CFT}}{\delta\phi_{0}(x_{1})\cdots\delta\phi_{0}(x_{n})}\right|_{\phi_{0}=0}.

(5.18)

Z_{CFT}[\phi_{0}]
is the generating functional of correlation functions of operators
\mathcal{O}
that couple to a source
\phi_{0}
. Writing

\displaystyle Z_{CFT}[\phi_{0}]\equiv e^{-W_{CFT}[\phi_{0}]}

(5.19)

defines
W_{CFT}[\phi_{0}]
as the generating functional of
connected
correlation functions of operators
\mathcal{O}
that couple to a source
\phi_{0}
. For the 2-point function:

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{O}(x_{1})\mathcal{O}(x_{2})\rangle_{c}:=-\left.\frac{\delta^{2}W_{CFT}}{\delta\phi_{0}(x_{1})\delta\phi_{0}(x_{2})}\right|_{\phi_{0}=0}=\langle\mathcal{O}(x_{1})\mathcal{O}(x_{2})\rangle-\langle\mathcal{O}(x_{1})\rangle\langle\mathcal{O}(x_{2})\rangle.

(5.20)

GKPW dictionary

Now we come to the original AdS/CFT dictionary known as GKPW dictionary [Gubser Klebanov Polyakov Witten ’98]. The bulk theory is a QG theory (e.g. string theory), or in a certain limit a classical gravity theory, with a field content that includes matter fields and gravitational fields (the metric).
We will denote a collection of these fields as
\phi^{i}(Z,x)
.
The boundary theory is a CFT with a field content that includes only matter fields. We use
\mathcal{O}^{i}(x)
to denote the collection of CFT fields that match the bulk fields
\phi^{i}
in the way prescribed below.

The GKPW dictionary says that the equivalence of the bulk and boundary theory can be expressed by basically equating the (Euclidean) path integrals of each side, in such a way that the boundary conditions on the bulk fields provide source values for corresponding CFT operators:

\displaystyle Z_{QG}[\phi^{i}\,\,|\,\,\phi^{i}_{\partial AdS}=\phi^{i}_{0}]\quad=\quad Z_{CFT}[\phi^{i}_{0}]

(5.21)

with
Z_{CFT}
the generating functional in the CFT that reduces to the CFT path integral
Z
when
\phi_{0}\rightarrow 0
.

The dictionary simplifies when the bulk is described by the gravitational sector and this gravitational sector can be described semi-classically through a saddle-point approximation,
Z_{QG}\rightarrow Z_{grav}\approx\exp{-S_{grav}[\phi^{i}_{*}]}
:

\displaystyle S_{grav}[\phi^{i}_{*}\,\,|\,\,\phi^{i}_{*,\partial AdS}=\phi^{i}_{0}]\quad=\quad W_{CFT}[\phi^{i}_{0}]

(5.22)

with
W_{CFT}
the generating functional of connected correlators of CFT operators
\mathcal{O}^{i}(x)
coupling to the boundary values
\phi^{i}_{0}(x)
of bulk fields
\phi^{i}_{*}(Z,x)
that solve the classical bulk EOM.
The action evaluated on the classical solutions
S_{grav}[\phi^{i}_{*}]
is called the on-shell action.

Some examples of
\int\phi_{0}^{i}\mathcal{O}^{i}
are:

•

Namely, we need to compute the classical action in AdS as a functional of the boundary conditions.

\displaystyle S_{M}[\phi_{*}]

UV/IR duality

The renormalization we had to include in the calculation is called
holographic renormalization
. It just reflects the renormalization on the CFT side of short-distance divergences
x_{1}\rightarrow x_{2}
in the correlators. Such short-distance divergences typically take the form of contact terms
\sim\delta(x_{1}-x_{2})
, and are called
UV divergences
. It is clear from the first term in (
5.48
) that the contact term of the CFT is tied to the
Z\rightarrow 0
divergence in the bulk. But from the bulk perspective, the divergence as
Z\rightarrow 0
is a large-distance or
IR divergence
, originating from the infinite size of the AdS spacetime. It is a feature of AdS/CFT that IR divergences in the bulk map to UV divergences in the boundary. This is sometimes referred to as
UV/IR duality
.

Relation between
m
and
\Delta
from casimir

There is a faster way to derive or remember the important relation (
5.39
) between the bulk field mass
m
and the boundary field conformal dimension
\Delta
.

We know the bulk theory is a QFT on a fixed AdS
d+1
background, since we have neglected backreaction of the light fields on the metric. Upon making this assumption, the full diff invariance of
S_{M}
in
S_{grav}
reduces to invariance under transformations that leave the background AdS
d+1
invariant. These are the isometries of AdS
d+1
, with group
SO(d+1,1)
.

Just as a QFT on a fixed Minkowski background has Poincaré symmetry with an associated casimir operator
c_{2}^{Poinc}
that indicates the field content should be labeled by casimir eigenvalues mass
m
and spin
s
(section
1.2.2
), our bulk QFT on fixed AdS
d+1
has a casimir operator
c_{2}^{AdS}
that indicates how to label the bulk field
\phi
. We expect that label to be the bulk mass
m
for a spinless field
\phi
, and we show this now.

The casimir is given by
c_{2}^{AdS}=\frac{1}{2}J_{ab}J^{ab}
with
J_{ab}
the generators of the AdS isometry. When working on a field
\phi
, it can be worked out that
[c_{2},\phi]=\Box\phi
with
\Box\equiv\Box_{AdS}
. This is also true when the bulk is a classical theory, since the casimir is a mathematical concept associated with a Lie algebra, e.g. given by the angular momentum operator squared
J^{2}
in QM, but also applicable in differential geometry, where the quadratic casimir is defined as the
G
-invariant second order differential operator on a manifold
M
with symmetry group
G
. This last definition immediately identifies the box operator
\Box
as the casimir. In any case, we have on the bulk side that the action of the casimir (associated with the isometry group of AdS) on the bulk field
\phi
gives
\Box\phi
which by the EOM we know has to equal
m^{2}\phi
. In conclusion

\displaystyle[c_{2}^{AdS},\phi]=m^{2}\phi.

(5.54)

From the point of view of the boundary CFT, the casimir associated with the isometry group of AdS
d+1
is the conformal casimir (
2.59
) (consistent with the conclusions of section
3.4
). It acts on scalar CFT operators with dimension
\Delta
as

\displaystyle[c_{2}^{conf},\mathcal{O}]=\Delta(\Delta-d)\mathcal{O}.

(5.55)

This shows the casimir gives us the relation between bulk and boundary labels

\displaystyle\Delta(\Delta-d)=m^{2}.

(5.56)

To fully derive this we would need the explicit relation between
\phi
and
\mathcal{O}
, which is known as the HKLL representation of
\phi
in the AdS/CFT program of
bulk reconstruction
. But we will not cover this in the course and the relation between
\Delta
and
m
is already apparent without going into the further details.

Witten diagrams

The theories of the standard model typically use S-matrices to model the physics of scattering experiments.
This is because these are QFT’s with massive particles. In CFT’s, massive particles are not allowed by symmetry, and in a sense the concept of a mass was replaced by the concept of a conformal dimension, as a labeling eigenvalue of the field content (as discussed around (
2.59
)). The physics of a CFT is then described in terms of correlators rather than scattering matrices.

We now see that the AdS/CFT duality allows to associate a mass to the CFT fields, by letting them couple to higher-dimensional, massive AdS fields!

Indeed, the duality also maps the physics content from correlators in the CFT to scattering diagrams in the bulk. The scattering diagrams that are used to calculate CFT correlation functions through the GKPW dictionary are called

]
. That section also introduces the concept of

, for interested students.)

6.1
Finite temperature QFT

A thermodynamic system in thermal contact with its environment at temperature
T
, and with volume
V
and number of particles
N
kept constant, is referred to as a canonical ensemble; its possible discrete quantum states as microstates. Denoting the energy of the system in microstate
s
as
E_{s}
, the canonical partition function is given by

Z=\sum_{s}e^{-\beta E_{s}},\quad\beta=\frac{1}{k_{B}T},

(6.1)

with
k_{B}
the Boltzmann constant which we will set equal to 1 from here on.

Consider a system at temperature
T
consisting of a field
\phi(\vec{x},t)
that is in the state
|\phi_{a}\rangle
at time
t
.
The partition function (
6.1
) can be rewritten for this system as

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=\int d\phi_{a}\langle\phi_{a}|e^{-\beta\hat{H}}|\phi_{a}\rangle=\text{Tr}\left(e^{-\beta\hat{H}}\right),\quad\beta=\frac{1}{T}.

(6.2)

In the
T=0
quantum field theory for
\phi(\vec{x},t)
, the transition amplitude for a transition from an initial state
\phi_{a}(\vec{x})
at time
t
to an end state
\phi_{b}(\vec{x})
at time
t^{\prime}
is given by the path integral

\langle\phi_{b}|e^{-i\hat{H}(t^{\prime}-t)}|\phi_{a}\rangle=\int_{\phi_{a}(\vec{x})=\phi(\vec{x},t)}^{\phi_{b}(\vec{x})=\phi(\vec{x},t^{\prime})}\mathcal{D}\phi\hskip 2.84526pte^{iS[\phi]}.

(6.3)

After performing a Wick rotation (
t\rightarrow-it_{E}
,
t^{\prime}-t\rightarrow-i\beta
,
iS\rightarrow-S_{E}
) and identifying initial and end state, we find for the Euclidian path integral along a closed path:

\int d\phi_{a}\langle\phi_{a}|e^{-\beta\hat{H}}|\phi_{a}\rangle=\int_{\phi(\vec{x},t_{E}+\beta)=\phi(\vec{x},t_{E})}\mathcal{D}\phi\hskip 2.84526pte^{-S_{E}[\phi]}.

(6.4)

From comparison with (
6.2
) we conclude that the Euclidean path integral along a closed path
\phi(\vec{x},t_{E}+\beta)=\phi(\vec{x},t_{E})
is equal to the canonical partition function at temperature
T=\frac{1}{\beta}
:

Z=\int_{\phi(\vec{x},t_{E}+\beta)=\phi(\vec{x},t_{E})}\mathcal{D}\phi\hskip 2.84526pte^{-S_{E}[\phi]}.

(6.5)

In other words:
turning on temperature in the field theory corresponds to compactification of Euclidean time

\displaystyle t_{E}\sim t_{E}+\beta,\qquad\beta=\frac{1}{T}.

(6.6)

6.2
Euclidean Schwarzschild black hole

Another instance where compactification of Euclidean time naturally appears is in the context of black holes. Let’s consider the most familiar one first, the flat space Schwarzschild solution. Wick-rotated to Euclidean signature, it is given by

\displaystyle ds^{2}=\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)dt_{E}^{2}+\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)^{-1}dr^{2}+r^{2}d\Omega^{2}

(6.7)

with the parameter
m
related to the mass
M
of the black hole by
2m\equiv 2GM
.
We can do a coordinate transformation to
\tilde{r}=r-2m
and expand in
\tilde{r}
to zoom in on the near-horizon region

\displaystyle ds^{2}=\frac{\tilde{r}}{2m}dt_{E}^{2}+\frac{2m}{\tilde{r}}d\tilde{r}^{2}+4m(m+\tilde{r})d\Omega^{2}\,+\mathcal{O}(\tilde{r}^{2})

(6.8)

or

\displaystyle ds^{2}=\left(\frac{\rho}{4m}\right)^{2}dt_{E}^{2}+d\rho^{2}+(4m^{2}+\frac{\rho^{2}}{2})d\Omega^{2}\,+\mathcal{O}(\rho^{4})

(6.9)

in terms of yet another radius

defined by
\rho^{2}=8m\tilde{r}
.

To avoid a conical singularity at
\rho=0
(

) in the

has period

. See figure

Schwarzschild is a spherically symmetric solution of the vacuum Einstein equations and there is no source at the location of the horizon that could justify an infinite curvature in the form of a conical singularity there. \displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{r^{2}}{\ell^{2}}-\frac{\mu}{r^{2}}\right)dt_{E}^{2}+\frac{dr^{2}}{1+\frac{r^{2}}{\ell^{2}}-\frac{\mu}{r^{2}}}+r^{2}d\Omega_{3}^{2}

\displaystyle C=\frac{\partial M}{\partial T}.

Chapter 3.

We discuss in this section the basics of RG (renormalization group) theory in a
d
-dimensional flat background. In RG theory, one considers the space of theories. The theories, for a field
\varphi
, can be described by actions

\displaystyle S[\varphi;g_{i}]

(6.28)

that also parametrically
depend on a set of coupling constants
\{g_{i}\}
(an alternative notation for
S[\varphi;g_{i}]
would be
S_{\{g_{i}\}}[\varphi]
), which you can think of as a set of coordinates spanning the space of theories. These parameters are ‘coupling constants’ in the general sense, including e.g. mass parameters.

Now the coupling constants
g_{i}
depend on the energy scale
\mu
at which you resolve your theory; they are running couplings
g_{i}(\mu)
.
The functions
g_{i}(\mu)
define the
RG flow
in the space of theories, which is conventionally thought of as a flow from UV to IR. Vanishing energy scale
\mu\rightarrow 0
corresponds to the IR limit,
\mu\rightarrow\infty
to the UV limit.

Fixed points
of the flow
g_{i}=g_{i}^{*}
have the property that

\displaystyle\left.\frac{dg_{i}}{d\mu}\right|_{g_{j}^{*}}=0.

(6.29)

The action at a particular energy scale is the Wilsonian Effective Action
S[\varphi;\mu,g_{i}]
. This effective field theory action (EFT) is taken to be the most general action that one is allowed to write down based on symmetry considerations, including all possible couplings
g_{i}
(infinitely many a priori) for allowed interaction terms of the fields e.g.
\varphi^{n},\varphi^{n}\partial_{\mu}\varphi\partial^{\mu}\varphi
, etc. Writing
\mathcal{O}_{i}(x)
for such linear combinations of the fields and their derivatives, the action takes the form of a kinetic term for
\varphi
plus ‘deformation terms’ (we’ll come back to this name later).

\displaystyle S[\varphi;g_{i}]=\int d^{d}x\,\left(\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\mu}\varphi\partial^{\mu}\varphi+\sum_{i}g_{i}\mathcal{O}_{i}(x)\right).

(6.30)

The
\mu
-dependence can be made explicit by extracting a factor
\mu^{d-\Delta_{i}}
from
g_{i}
for
\Delta_{i}
the classical scaling dimension of
\mathcal{O}_{i}(x)
, in order to make
g_{i}
dimensionless:
S[\varphi;\mu,g_{i}]=\int d^{d}x\,\left(\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\mu}\varphi\partial^{\mu}\varphi+\sum_{i}\mu^{d-\Delta_{i}}g_{i}\mathcal{O}_{i}(x)\right)
. But we will use the notation with the dimensionful coupling.

For e.g. a scalar field theory, examples of ‘deformation terms’ are
\frac{1}{2}m^{2}\varphi^{2}
and
\frac{1}{4!}\lambda\varphi^{4}
. That is,
g_{2}=m^{2}
and
g_{4}=\lambda
.
In the IR limit
\mu\rightarrow 0
, mass scales decouple as
m/\mu\rightarrow\infty
except for
m=0
: only massless particles remain, and moreover, more generally speaking, no dimensionful parameters are left.
This leaves a scale invariant theory (with no mass or other scales) described by a CFT. This is referred to as a
CFT fixed point
in the IR.
A curve
g_{i}(\mu)
forms a ‘renormalized trajectory’ if it connects the IR CFT fixed point to a UV CFT fixed point (called “asymptotic safety” by Weinberg), defining a theory on all length scales.

CFT’s, as fixed points, play a very special role in RG theory as the mileposts to and from which RG trajectories flow.

From the point of view of the CFT fixed points, the
\sum g_{i}\mathcal{O}_{i}
terms in the action reintroduce scales to the theory, deforming us away from scale invariance. Hence the name ‘deformation terms’.

In the neighborhood of a fixed point, the RG flow can be linearized
g_{i}(\mu)=g_{i}^{*}+\delta g_{i}
(for details see
[
32
]
), leading to a classification of couplings
g_{i}
into
relevant, marginal and irrelevant
, according to the scaling dimension
\Delta
of
\mathcal{O}_{i}
:

\displaystyle\Delta<d\qquad\text{relevant}

(6.31)

\displaystyle\Delta=d\qquad\text{marginal}

(6.32)

\displaystyle\Delta>d\qquad\text{irrelevant}.

(6.33)

A relevant coupling has the property that it grows, i.e. becomes relevant in the IR. An irrelevant coupling vanishes, i.e. becomes weaker or ‘irrelevant’ in the RG flow to the IR. Said otherwise, a relevant perturbation vanishes in the UV, while an irrelevant perturbation blows up in the UV. It is the sign of
d-\Delta
that is important for this classification.

It follows that the behavior of theories in the IR is determined by only a small number of couplings, namely the relevant couplings, instead of by the infinite initial set of couplings
g_{i}
. In the scalar field theory example,
g_{2}=m^{2}
is relevant in
d\geq 2
and
g_{4}=\lambda
is relevant in
d=2,3
but irrelevant in
d>4
. This justifies why we in practice only considered a few couplings
g_{2}
and
g_{4}
from the start.

Now let’s say we start from a CFT, e.g.
S[\varphi]=\frac{1}{2}\int d^{d}x\,\partial_{\mu}\varphi\partial^{\mu}\varphi
, and deform it with a term

\displaystyle\int\phi_{0}\,\mathcal{O}

(6.34)

with constant (i.e.

\phi_{0}
, and
\mathcal{O}
an operator of dimension
\Delta

Assuming the CFT is holographic, i.e. has a good semi-classical AdS dual,

represents the boundary value of a bulk field

\displaystyle\phi(Z,x^{\mu})\,\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle Z\rightarrow 0}}{{\sim}}\,\,Z^{d-\Delta}(\phi_{0}+\tilde{\phi}_{0}(x^{\mu}))+Z^{\Delta}\tilde{\phi}(x^{\mu}).

(6.35)

Correlators of
\mathcal{O}
in the deformed theory are calculated by setting

in (

The value of

\phi_{0}
with energy scale from the perspective of the field theory.

For

For

Consistent with our previous discussion of the interpretation of the

-direction in AdS as an RG direction in the boundary theory, it is the power of

in the holographic description of the deformation that contains the information about the type of deformation.
The holographic direction makes the RG direction of the boundary theory explicit in a geometric way.

7.1
Black hole thermodynamics

Black hole mechanics laws formally look like laws of thermodynamics. Including quantum effects at the semi-classical level (Hawking calculation in QFT on curved space), the black hole is no longer completely black but radiates at a Hawking
temperature
and has a Bekenstein-Hawking
entropy

S_{BH}
. This allows to interpret the black hole laws physically, not just formally, as black hole
thermodynamics
laws for the black hole as a thermal object:

\displaystyle\text{zeroth law}\qquad T\text{ constant}

(7.1)

\displaystyle\text{first law}\qquad\quad\delta M=T\delta S_{BH}

(7.2)

\displaystyle\text{second law}\qquad\delta S_{gen}\geq 0

(7.3)

where
M
is the mass of the black hole,
T
the Hawking temperature,
S_{BH}
the black hole entropy or Bekenstein-Hawking entropy, and
S_{gen}
the generalized entropy consisting of
S_{BH}
plus the entropy of matter on the outside-horizon region.

In section
6.1
, we saw that in a finite temperature QFT, the canonical partition function is given by the Euclidean path integral along a closed path of length
\beta=1/T

\displaystyle Z(\beta)=\sum_{s}e^{-\beta E_{s}}=\int_{\phi(\vec{x},t_{E}+\beta)=\phi(\vec{x},t_{E})}\mathcal{D}\phi\hskip 2.84526pte^{-S_{E}[\phi]},\qquad\beta=\frac{1}{T}.

(7.4)

Similarly, in a quantum theory of gravity, the canonical partition function for a system of gravitational and matter fields in thermal equilibrium at temperature
T
is at least formally

1

given by

In the semi-classical approximation,

Once the thermal partition function is identified with the Euclidean path integral, you can use the known formulas of statistical physics for obtaining the free energy, entropy and energy (with

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\beta}\log Z(\beta)\approx\frac{1}{\beta}S_{E,grav}[g_{*}]

\displaystyle=(1-\beta\partial_{\beta})\log Z(\beta)

\displaystyle S=\frac{A}{4}.

(7.12)

This is the famous

(7.13)

In this section we discuss 2D CFT at finite temperature, more specifically the torus partition function and Cardy formula. Only in the next section

will we go back to a bulk gravity perspective.

1)   CFT on cylinder (at
T=0
)

Figure 7.1:
Set-up of 2D CFT on cylinder

We consider a CFT on a cylinder, pictured in two different ways in figure
7.1
. The first representation is in terms of the
(t_{E},\phi)
coordinates with
\phi
the spacelike compactified coordinate, with identification
\phi\sim\phi+2\pi L
for
L
the radius of the cylinder, which will sometimes be set to 1 for simplicity of the formulas.
The second representation is in terms of the complex coordinate
w=\phi+it_{E}
, with identification
w\sim w+2\pi L
inherited of course directly from the
\phi
identification. In this rolled out picture of the cylinder, the strip between the bold lines
\phi=0
and
\phi=2\pi L
is the fundamental domain which corresponds to one wrapping of the cylinder upon identification of the bold lines, and all vertical lines in the picture
(\phi=n\pi L)
are to be identified.
The metric on the cylinder is
ds^{2}_{cyl}=d\phi^{2}+dt_{E}^{2}=dwd\bar{w}
but has to be supplemented by the identifications of the coordinates,
\phi\sim\phi+2\pi L
or
w\sim w+2\pi L
.

The compactification of the spacelike direction
\phi
leads to a Casimir contribution
\sim\frac{1}{L}
to the energy, more precisely the energy in the vacuum state is

\displaystyle E_{cyl}^{vac}=-\frac{c}{12}\frac{1}{L}.

(7.19)

To arrive at this result, use the transformation behavior of the CFT stress tensor under general conformal transformations
z\rightarrow z^{\prime}
in (
2.95
)
and apply it to the plane to cylinder conformal transformation
z\rightarrow w
discussed in (
2.53
),
z=e^{iw}
or
z=Le^{iw/L}
(where we see
w
automatically has the correct identifications because it appears in the exponent and
e^{2\pi i}=1
). It follows that

\displaystyle T_{cyl}(w)=-\frac{z^{2}}{L^{2}}T_{plane}(z)-\frac{c}{24}\frac{1}{L^{2}}

(7.20)

where the second term comes precisely from the anomalous (
=
non-tensorial) contribution to the transformation law of the stress tensor, namely the Schwarzian contribution in (
2.95
) proportional to the central charge
c
. The energy is defined as
H=\int d\phi\,T_{t_{E}t_{E}}=\int d\phi(T_{ww}+T_{\bar{w}\bar{w}})
and has to be evaluated in the vacuum state to find indeed
E_{cyl}^{vac}=\int_{0}^{2\pi L}d\phi\langle T_{t_{E}t_{E}}^{cyl}(t_{E}=0,\phi)\rangle_{vac}=(2\pi L)(-\frac{c}{12L^{2}})=-\frac{c}{12L}
.

Now, the goal will be to derive the partition function for the CFT on the cylinder at finite temperature
T
. In the canonical ensemble
(T,V,N)
, knowledge of the partition function allows to derive the entropy and free energy:

\displaystyle Z(T,V,N)\quad\leadsto\quad S(T,V,N),\,\,\,F(T,V,N)=E-TS

(7.21)

where
Z=\sum_{states}e^{-\beta E}
.
To turn on temperature, we periodically identify Euclidean time with period
\beta=1/T
. This procedure implies that we need to discuss the CFT on a torus.

2)   CFT on a torus

Figure 7.2:
Set-up of 2D CFT on torus

Compactification of Euclidean time direction
t_{E}
with periodicity
\beta
creates a torus, pictured in two ways in figure
7.2
with the additional coordinate identifications (compared to the cylinder) in green. The fundamental domain is a rectangle (rather than the strip) of size
2\pi\times\beta
. In fact, the more general fundamental domain for a torus is a parallellogram, specified by real numbers
\beta
and
\theta
as pictured in figure
7.3
. Alternatively, a torus is defined by the specification of two vectors
\vec{v}_{1}=(2\pi,0)
and
\vec{v}_{2}=(\theta,\beta)
. In figure
7.3
, the torus has already been ‘normalized’ by rotation of a general given
\vec{v}_{1}
to align with the
\phi
-axis, and rescaling to
|\vec{v}_{1}|=2\pi
. This is possible without loss of generality when the theory is rotational invariant and scale invariant, which is true for our 2D CFT.

Figure 7.3:
Torus definition

A torus is fully characterized by its
modulus

\tau

\displaystyle\tau=\frac{\theta+i\beta}{2\pi}.

(7.22)

Given the fundamental domain in figure
7.3
, the identifications required to cover the torus (once) are

\displaystyle(t_{E},\phi)\sim(t_{E}+\beta,\phi+\theta)\sim(t_{E},\phi+2\pi)

(7.23)

or

\displaystyle w\sim w+2\pi\sim w+2\pi\tau.

(7.24)

The full lattice pictured in figure
7.2
describes the multiple coverings of the torus as

\displaystyle w\sim w+(m+n\tau)2\pi,\qquad m,n\in\mathbb{Z}.

(7.25)

Now, to obtain the partition function of the finite temperature CFT on a cylinder, we need to write down the path integral on the torus.
In order to do this, there are two equally valid choices of the Euclidean coordinates spanning the torus, pictured in figure
7.4
. First, choosing
t_{E}
as Euclidean ‘time’ and
\phi
as ‘space’ direction, one writes the path integral by taking a state on the
t_{E}=0
surface
\phi:0\rightarrow 2\pi
, which is an element of the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{(2\pi,0)}
, and evolving it in the direction of
\vec{v}_{2}
, i.e. along the vector
\beta\partial_{t_{E}}+i\theta\partial_{\phi}
, to obtain

\displaystyle Z(\tau,\bar{\tau})=\tr_{\mathcal{H}_{(2\pi,0)}}e^{-\beta H+i\theta J}.

(7.26)

From now on, we will consider again the special case
\theta=0
(rectangular lattice cell for the torus) for simplicity (it can be included without problem).

Figure 7.4:
Two possible choices to label the torus directions

Second, one can instead choose
\phi
as Euclidean ‘time’ and
t_{E}
as ‘space’ direction, and write the path integral by taking a state on the
\phi=0
surface
t_{E}:0\rightarrow\beta
, which is an element of the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{(0,\beta)}
, and evolving it in the direction of
\vec{v}_{1}
, i.e. along the vector
2\pi\partial_{\phi}
, to obtain

\displaystyle Z(\tau,\bar{\tau})=\tr_{\mathcal{H}_{(0,\beta)}}e^{-2\pi J}.

(7.27)

Since both choices are valid, we require equality of

\displaystyle\tr_{\mathcal{H}_{(2\pi,0)}}e^{-\beta H}=\tr_{\mathcal{H}_{(0,\beta)}}e^{-2\pi J}.

(7.28)

In a general QFT, there is no relation between the Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{(2\pi,0)}
and
\mathcal{H}_{(0,\beta)}
, but in CFT there is! Namely, we can transform between the 2 choices of ‘time’ and ‘space’ by a conformal transformation: a rotation over 90 degrees plus a rescaling by
2\pi/\beta
. This is pictured in figure
7.5
. The rescaling is necessary for ‘normalization’ of the torus to
\vec{v}_{1}=(2\pi,0)
, i.e. imposing
\Delta\phi^{\prime}=2\pi
after the rotation. It follows that the other coordinate is rescaled with the same factor, leading to a periodicity
\Delta t_{E}^{\prime}=(2\pi)^{2}/\beta
. By conformal equivalence, it then follows that

\displaystyle\tr_{\mathcal{H}_{(0,\beta)}}e^{-2\pi J}=\tr_{\mathcal{H}_{(2\pi,0)}}e^{-\frac{(2\pi)^{2}}{\beta}H}.

(7.29)

Figure 7.5:
Required conformal transformation to connect
\mathcal{H}_{(2\pi,0)}
and
\mathcal{H}_{(0,\beta)}
Hilbert spaces.

Making use of (
7.29
), the condition (
7.28
) becomes

\displaystyle\tr_{\mathcal{H}_{(2\pi,0)}}e^{-\beta H}=\tr_{\mathcal{H}_{(2\pi,0)}}e^{-\frac{(2\pi)^{2}}{\beta}H}.

(7.30)

In this rewritten form, the traces left and right are over the same Hilbert space, so that the heavy notation can be replaced simply by a trace
\tr
. It is an equality condition between the path integral for traveling a distance
\beta
and
4\pi^{2}/\beta
in the (Euclidean) time direction

\displaystyle Z(\beta)=Z(\frac{4\pi^{2}}{\beta})\qquad\text{high $T$ - low $T$ \emph{duality}}.

(7.31)

It expresses a duality between high temperature and low temperature phases of the theory.

What the conformal transformation
(t_{E},\phi)\rightarrow\frac{2\pi}{\beta}(-\phi,t_{E})
seems to have done is to relate
Z
on the torus
\tau=\frac{i\beta}{2\pi}
with
Z
on the torus
\tau^{\prime}=\frac{i}{2\pi}\frac{4\pi^{2}}{\beta}=-\frac{1}{\tau}
. The transformation
\tau\rightarrow-\frac{1}{\tau}
between tori
is called an
S
-transformation. It leaves the definition of the torus invariant:

\displaystyle w\sim w+(m+n\tau)2\pi\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle S:\,\tau\rightarrow-1/\tau}}{{\longrightarrow}}\quad

\displaystyle w\sim w+(m-\frac{n}{\tau}2\pi

(7.32)

\displaystyle w\tau\sim w\tau+(m\tau-n)2\pi

(7.33)

\displaystyle w^{\prime}\sim w^{\prime}+(n^{\prime}\tau+m^{\prime})2\pi\quad;

(7.34)

that is, the
(m,n)
lattice is transformed into an
(m^{\prime},n^{\prime})=(-n,m)
lattice, which describes the
same
torus.
The only thing that has changed is how you count the lattice cells (with
m,n\in\mathbb{Z}
).

Similarly, the so-called
T
-transformation
\tau\rightarrow\tau+1
leaves the torus invariant, with
(m,n)\rightarrow(m+n,n)
. With repeated
T
- and
S
- transformations one can build a
PSL(2,\mathbb{Z})
transformation

\displaystyle\tau\rightarrow\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d},\qquad\bar{\tau}\rightarrow\frac{a\bar{\tau}+b}{c\bar{\tau}+d}

(7.35)

with
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{Z}
and
ad-bc=1
. We have arrived at the
PSL(2,\mathbb{Z})
or
modular invariance
of a 2D CFT on a torus, expressed by

\displaystyle Z(\tau,\bar{\tau})=Z\left(\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d},\frac{a\bar{\tau}+b}{c\bar{\tau}+d}\right)

(7.36)

of which the high
T
- low
T
duality (
7.31
) is one example.

3)   Cardy formula

Now we are ready to follow Cardy’s derivation of entropy of a 2D CFT on a cylinder. The torus path integral
Z(\beta)
is interpreted as the partition function for the system at temperature
1/\beta
. Let us discuss this partition function
Z(\beta)=\sum_{states}e^{-\beta E}
in two opposite temperature regimes.

At low temperatures,
\beta\rightarrow\infty
, the sum over states is dominated by the lowest energy or vacuum energy contribution:

\displaystyle\text{low $T$ or $\beta\rightarrow\infty$}\qquad Z(\beta)=\sum_{states}e^{-\beta E}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\beta\rightarrow\infty}}{{\approx}}e^{-\beta E_{vac}}=e^{\beta\frac{c}{12}\frac{1}{L}}

(7.37)

where we used (
7.19
).
Instead, at high temperatures
\beta\rightarrow 0
, the partition function
Z(\beta)=\sum_{states}e^{-\beta E}
is dominated by high energy states and is usually impossible to evaluate. In this case, however, we can make use of the powerful modular invariance (
7.31
) to write the partition function as

\displaystyle\text{high $T$ or $\beta\rightarrow 0$}\qquad Z(\beta)=\sum_{states}e^{-\frac{4\pi^{2}L^{2}}{\beta}E}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\beta\rightarrow 0}}{{\approx}}e^{-\frac{4\pi^{2}L^{2}}{\beta}E_{vac}}=e^{\frac{4\pi^{2}}{\beta}\frac{c}{12}L}

(7.38)

where the modular invariant rewritten form is dominated in the
\beta\rightarrow 0
limit by the vacuum energy contribution again. This only works because of the high temperature - low temperature duality.

The free energy defined by
Z(\beta)=e^{-\beta F}
can be written for high temperature as
F=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}cT^{2}\frac{L}{2\pi}
, proportional to the central charge
c
(a measure for the number of degrees of freedom).
The entropy
S(\beta)\equiv(1=\beta\partial_{\beta})\log Z=2\pi^{2}c/(3\beta)
and the energy
E(\beta)\equiv-\partial_{\beta}\log Z=\pi^{2}c/(3\beta^{2})
from which it follows that the entropy as a function of energy is given by

\displaystyle S(E)=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{c}{3}EL}\qquad\quad\text{(Cardy formula)}.

(7.39)

This is the famous Cardy formula for the entropy of high energy states of the 2D CFT on a cylinder of radius
L
.
Correspondingly, one can associate to it a density of states
\rho(E)
defined by
S=\log\rho
(called
\Omega
in a previous section),

\displaystyle\rho(E)=e^{2\pi\sqrt{\frac{c}{3}EL}}.

(7.40)

This is the Cardy formula for the density of states at high energy. It gives the number of CFT states with energy
E
, for
E
high, of said otherwise, the degeneracy of high-
E
CFT states.

The energy of a state on the cylinder is the conformal dimension
\Delta
because
H=L_{0}+\bar{L}_{0}=D
, so the Cardy formulas above can alternatively be written in terms of the dilation eigenvalue
\Delta
instead of the energy eigenvalue
E
: entropy
S(\Delta)=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{c}{3}\Delta}
and density of states
\rho(\Delta)=\exp{\{2\pi\sqrt{\frac{c}{3}\Delta}\}}
.

7.3
Holographic interpretation of Cardy formula

AdS
3
gravity is dual to a CFT
2
with
c=\frac{3l}{2G}
. The bulk theory is semi-classical for
\hbar G
small of
G\ll l
, with the dimension of
G
being a length to the power (bulk dimension
-2
). This regime corresponds to large central charge
c\gg 1
from the boundary perspective.
According to the Witten prescription, a large black hole in the bulk corresponds to high temperature in the dual CFT.
We consider in 3 bulk dimensions the BTZ black hole. It has a temperature
T
and entropy
S
given in (
7.17
) and (
7.18
), and a mass
M
given in terms of the (classical) bulk Virasoro generators in (
4.24
) as
M=\frac{L_{0}+\bar{L}_{0}}{l}
.

By AdS
3
/CFT
2
, the Virasoro generators of the asymptotic symmetry of the 3D bulk geometry (in the gravity theory with parameters the AdS radius
l
and gravitational constant
G
) map to Virasoro generators of the conformal symmetry of the 2D boundary CFT with a central charge given by the Brown-Henneaux result
c=3l/(2G)
. The mass of BTZ,
Ml=L_{0}+\bar{L}_{0}
, then has a dual interpretation as the eigenvalue of the
L_{0}+\bar{L}_{0}
operator in the CFT, which is
\Delta
or
El
:

\displaystyle\frac{3l}{2G}\rightarrow c,\qquad Ml\rightarrow\Delta.

(7.41)

Making these holographic replacements in the formula for the BTZ Bekenstein-Hawking entropy (
7.18
) gives

\displaystyle S_{BTZ}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{\ell^{2}M}{2G}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle AdS/CFT}}{{=}}2\pi\sqrt{\frac{c}{3}\Delta}=S_{Cardy}(\Delta)

(7.42)

meaning the black hole entropy has a dual interpretation as Cardy entropy of high-energy states of the dual CFT! This allows a statistical counting of black hole microstates as high-
\Delta
CFT states which describe quantum gravity holographically:

\displaystyle S_{BTZ}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle AdS/CFT}}{{=}}\log\rho_{Cardy}(\Delta).

(7.43)

This is a famous result by Strominger ’97 and one of the major accomplishments of the holographic approach to quantum gravity, providing a statistical interpretation of the black hole entropy. It is not satisfactory yet in the sense that the number of black hole microstates can be counted, but are not yet identified (in a bulk perspective).

The limits that go into the derivation of the result (semi-classical gravity for large mass black holes) can be summarized in the order of limits condition
\Delta\gg c\gg 1
.
The vacuum state of 2D CFT on the cylinder maps to empty AdS on the bulk side, separated with a gap from the BTZ solutions. This is illustrated in figure
7.6
.

Figure 7.6:

Before this holographic result, the highlight in the quest for a statistical interpretation of black hole entropy came from the string theory approach to quantum gravity. In a famous string theory result, Strominger and Vafa ’96 provided a microscopic state counting of a 5D extremal Reissner-Nordstrom black hole in supergravity, using the Cardy formula. In modern language, this has a similar AdS/CFT interpretation as the BTZ case discussed above.

Entropy

The
von Neumann entropy

\displaystyle S=-\text{tr }(\rho\log\rho)

(8.1)

gives a fundamental
definition for entropy as a measure for randomness/uncertainty of a statistical ensemble described by
\rho
, where
\rho
is the probability distribution (classically) or density matrix (QM).
S
gives an observer-dependent measure for the indeterminacy or lack of resolution of your system.

Let us check that this definition reduces to the notions of entropy we have used earlier in the course.
In the microcanonical ensemble, every microstate is equally probable, i.e. for an observer in a closed system
(E,V,N)
,
\rho=\frac{1}{\Omega(E)}
with
\Omega
the accessible phase space volume (classically)
or the number of available microstates (QM). Applying the von Neumann definition gives the entropy
S=k_{B}\log\Omega(E)
,
which depends on the energy.

S
quantifies the lack of information (not knowing which microstate is occupied) about your system.

In the canonical ensemble
(T,V,N)
for an open system observer, the Boltzmann probability distribution
\rho=\frac{e^{-\beta H}}{Z(\beta)}
gives a von Neumann entropy
S(T)=(1-\beta\partial_{\beta})\log Z(\beta)
, which depends on the temperature. In the thermodynamic limit
N\rightarrow\infty
, it becomes equal to the microcanonical entropy (for the average energy).

8.2
Geometric entropy or entanglement

The von Neumann entropy can be applied in the context of
d
-dimensional CFT to define the concept of ‘geometric entropy’ or entanglement.

Entanglement

Consider a
d
-dimensional CFT in a pure state
|\psi\rangle
described by the density matrix
\rho=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
. We consider the constant time slice pictured in figure
8.1
where we geometrically bipartition the system (assuming the Hilbert space can be factorized
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{A}\bigotimes\mathcal{H}_{\bar{A}}
) into a spatial region
A
and its complement
\bar{A}
. In
d>2
the region
A
is a ball, in
d=2
it is an interval.

Figure 8.1:
The picture shows the spacelike (compact) direction
\theta
of a
(1+1)
-dimensional CFT at some constant time
t
, bipartitioned into two intervals.

An observer that only has access to region
A
will measure a different density matrix, called the reduced density matrix

\displaystyle\rho_{A}=\tr_{\bar{A}}\rho.

(8.2)

It is obtained from
\rho
by tracing out degrees of freedom in
\bar{A}
.
The observer’s lack of information about the full system can be quantified by the von Neumann entropy of
\rho_{A}
:

\displaystyle S_{A}=-\tr(\rho_{A}\log\rho_{A})\qquad\text{(entanglement entropy)}.

(8.3)

This is by definition the
geometric entropy
or
entanglement entropy
associated with region
A
.

To understand its interpretation, we consider some limits in which
S_{A}
vanishes.
The von Neumann entropy will vanish when
\rho
is pure. You can see this for example by using that
\tr\rho^{2}=\tr\rho

for a pure state. Now the reduced density matrix
\rho_{A}
is in general no longer pure, but mixed,
\rho_{A}=\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}|\psi_{\alpha}\rangle\langle\psi_{\alpha}|
, with
\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}=1
or
\tr\rho_{A}=1
. It will be pure in the limit that the region
A
becomes the full time slice (
\bar{A}
vanishes), because then
\rho_{A}\rightarrow\rho
, which was pure by assumption. This shows that
S_{A}
is a measure for the
amount of missing information
from the point of view of the observer in
A
, namely
S_{A}\rightarrow 0
when this observer has access to the full system.

Another instance in which
\rho_{A}
will be pure, is when the pure state
\rho
of the CFT is separable,
|\psi\rangle=|\psi_{A}\rangle|\psi_{\bar{A}}\rangle
, meaning there is no quantum entanglement between degrees of freedom in
A
and degrees of freedom in
\bar{A}
. In that case
\rho_{A}=|\psi_{A}\rangle\langle\psi_{A}|
and
S_{A}=0
. This is consistent with the interpretation of
S_{A}
as a measure for the
amount of entanglement
between dof in
A
and dof in
\bar{A}
.

Properties:

S_{A}=S_{\bar{A}}
because
\rho
is pure

S_{A_{1}}+S_{A_{2}}\geq S_{A_{1}\cup\,A_{2}}
(subadditivity)

S_{ABC}+S_{B}\leq S_{AB}+S_{BC}
(strong subadditivity or SSA)

Modular Hamiltonian

Because
\rho_{A}
is a positive semi-definite matrix (
x^{\prime}\rho_{A}x\geq 0
for any vector
x\neq 0
), one can write it as

\displaystyle\rho_{A}=\frac{e^{-H_{mod}}}{\tr e^{-{H_{mod}}}}

(8.4)

for some Hermitian matrix
H_{mod}
in the exponent. This is reminiscent of the thermal density matrix taking the form
\rho_{thermal}=e^{-\beta H}/\tr e^{-\beta H}
in terms of the Hamiltonian
H
, and therefore in analogy
H_{mod}
is called the
modular Hamiltonian
or
entanglement Hamiltonian
associated with the region
A
:

\displaystyle H_{mod,A}=-\log\rho_{A}+c^{\prime}

(8.5)

where
c^{\prime}
is a normalization constant. It is in general a very non-local operator, but we will soon discuss a case where a closed expression for
H_{mod,A}
is known.

First law of entanglement

Under a change of the state
\rho_{A}^{\prime}=\rho_{A}+\delta\rho_{A}
, the entanglement changes to
S^{\prime}_{A}=S_{A}+\delta S_{A}
with
\delta S_{A}=-\tr(\delta\rho_{A}\log\rho_{A})+\mathcal{O}(\delta\rho_{A}^{2})
(using
\tr\delta\rho_{A}=0
). On the other hand, the change in the expectation value of the modular Hamiltonian
\delta\langle H_{mod,A}\rangle\equiv\langle H_{mod,A}\rangle_{\rho_{A}^{\prime}}-\langle H_{mod,A}\rangle_{\rho_{A}}
is also equal to
-\tr(\delta\rho_{A}\log\rho_{A})
. It follows that the entanglement fluctuations and modular Hamiltonian fluctuations are related by

\displaystyle\delta S_{A}=\delta\langle H_{mod,A}\rangle.

(8.6)

This is known as the
‘first law’ of entanglement
, again in analogy with the thermodynamic first law relating changes in entropy with changes in energy,
dE=TdS
, in the thermal state.

8.3
Ryu-Takayanagi

RT prescription

The interpretation of the CFT entanglement
S_{A}
in the dual AdS theory is provided by the Ryu-Takayanagi formula or
RT formula

\displaystyle S_{A}\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle AdS/CFT}}{{=}}\quad\frac{\mathcal{A}(\chi)}{4G}

(8.7)

where
\chi
is the RT surface
\equiv
co-dimension 2 surface of minimal area
\mathcal{A}
that is homologous to the boundary region
A
. In AdS
3
/CFT
2
that means the RT surface is a boundary-anchored geodesic (see figure
8.2
) and
\mathcal{A}(\chi)
is the length of that geodesic, see (
4.38
). The homology condition can be written as the condition
\partial\Sigma=\chi\cup A
, with
\Sigma
the area between the RT surface
\chi
and the boundary region
A
that is bounded by
\chi
and
A
. (If there was a hole in the spacetime region
\Sigma
for example, that hole would contribute to
\partial\Sigma
and the homology condition would not hold.)

According to RT, a
quantum
connection in the CFT has an interpretation as a
geometric
connection in the AdS bulk theory.

Figure 8.2:
The RT formula.

The RT formula resembles closely the Bekenstein-Hawking entropy formula (
7.13
). However, the first involves the RT surface
\chi
while the second involves a black hole horizon
h
. To understand the Bekenstein-Hawking entropy inspiration for the RT formula, we first consider a special choice of region
A
.

Special case of
A

Consider the special case of
A
, where
A=
half-line
x\geq 0
in CFT
2
. It is the region whose domain of dependence
\mathcal{D}(A)
, defined as the set of points that are causally connected to
A
, is the Rindler wedge
\mathcal{R}
. The Rindler wedge is the region of Minkowski space
x\geq 0,|t|<x
spanned by the Rindler time coordinate
t_{\mathcal{R}}
and Rindler space coordinate
R
:

\displaystyle ds^{2}_{\mathcal{R}}

\displaystyle=-dt^{2}+dx^{2},\qquad x\geq 0,|t|<x

(8.8)

\displaystyle=dR^{2}-R^{2}dt_{\mathcal{R}}^{2},\qquad R\geq 0,\text{any }t_{\mathcal{R}}

(8.9)

with

\displaystyle x=R\cosh t_{\mathcal{R}},\qquad t=R\sinh t_{\mathcal{R}}.

(8.10)

The Rindler metric has a horizon at
R=0
. This is the Rindler horizon, which is the edge of the Rindler wedge in the Minkowski spacetime. A Rindler observer, moving along a line of constant
R
as it evolves in Rindler time
t_{\mathcal{R}}
(see figure
8.3
), does not have access to the region of Minkowski space behind the Rindler horizon.

To avoid a conical singularity at the Rindler horizon in Euclidean signature, Euclidean Rindler time should be periodically identified with
\beta=2\pi
, such that a Rindler observer measure a temperature

\displaystyle T=\frac{1}{2\pi}.

(8.11)

The more precise statement is the following (reference:
[
23
]
(5.8) for a very nice derivation from path integral pictures). If a Minkowski observer, moving along a line of constant
x
as it evolves in time
t
, measures a vacuum state
\rho
, then an observer confined to
A
, i.e. a Rindler observer moving along a line of constant
R
as it evolves in time
t_{\mathcal{R}}
, measures a thermal state

\displaystyle\rho_{A}=\frac{e^{-2\pi H_{t_{\mathcal{R}}}}}{\tr e^{-2\pi H_{t_{\mathcal{R}}}}}.

(8.12)

Here
H_{t_{\mathcal{R}}}
is the Rindler time Hamiltonian. From the definition of the modular Hamiltonian in (
8.4
), we read off that

\displaystyle H_{mod,A}=2\pi\,H_{t_{\mathcal{R}}}.

(8.13)

That is, in the special case of
A
given by the half-line, the modular Hamiltonian is equal to
2\pi
times the generator of translations in
t_{\mathcal{R}}
(i.e. Minkowski boosts).
The entanglement
S_{A}
associated with the half-line becomes a thermal entropy.

Figure 8.3:
The Rindler wedge
\mathcal{R}
.

To the Rindler observer, its time
t_{\mathcal{R}}
is just regular time, and its full spacelike region it the Minkowski half-line
A
. Since it measures a temperature
T
, its bulk dual (called the AdS-Rindler spacetime) will contain a black hole (Witten prescription) with a black hole horizon. The Bekenstein-Hawking entropy of this black hole is holographically dual to the thermal entropy of the CFT state
\rho_{A}

\displaystyle S_{A}=\frac{\mathcal{A}(h)}{4G}

(8.14)

with
\mathcal{A}(h)
the area of the AdS-Rindler black hole horizon. It means that the RT surface
\chi
of the half-line
A
wraps the AdS-Rindler black hole horizon.
The above reasoning explains the
Bekenstein-Hawking form
of the RT formula, at least for the special case that
A
is the half-line. What about more general
A
?

There is a conformal transformation [Casini Huerta Myers ’11] that maps any interval
A
to the half-line
A^{\prime}
(in general dimensional language, any ball
A
to the half-plane
A^{\prime}
). Since the transformation is conformal, the reasoning of the previous paragraph still applies.

8.4
Gravity from entanglement

Figure 8.4:
The Bekenstein-Hawking inspiration for the RT formula.

Consider the first law of black hole thermodynamics (
7.2
) applied to the AdS-Rindler black hole

\displaystyle\delta M=\frac{1}{2\pi}\,\delta S_{BH}.

(8.15)

It expresses how a small change of mass of a black hole is related to its change in entropy. Said otherwise, it expresses how two black hole
solutions
with slightly different mass are related to each other, namely the black hole first law is an
on-shell
expression.
Relating two solutions of the Einstein equations (one with
M
and one with
M+\delta M
), it is natural that the first law is equivalent to the linearized Einstein equations. (This is made precise in the Iyer-Wald formalism.)

First consider the left hand side. The black hole mass is calculated from the Brown-York stress tensor, (
4.27
). Schematically,
\delta M=\int\delta T_{t_{\mathcal{R}}t_{\mathcal{R}}}^{BY}
. By AdS/CFT, the Brown-York stress tensor is identified with the expectation value of the CFT stress tensor (
5.58
), so that
\delta M=\int\delta\langle T_{t_{\mathcal{R}}t_{\mathcal{R}}}\rangle=\delta\langle H_{t_{\mathcal{R}}}\rangle
. Making use of (
8.13
), we arrive at
\delta M=\frac{1}{2\pi}\delta\langle H_{mod,A}\rangle
for
A
the half-line.

Now for the right hand side. The formula for the Bekenstein-Hawking entropy tells us that
\delta S_{BH}=\delta\mathcal{A}(h)/4G
. By AdS/CFT, this thermal entropy equals the entanglement entropy
S_{A}
for
A
the half-line (
8.14
). So we have
\delta S_{BH}=\delta S_{A}
.

Having used the AdS/CFT dictionary on each side of the black hole first law (
8.15
), it now reads

\displaystyle\delta\langle H_{mod,A}\rangle=\delta S_{A}.

(8.16)

In first instance for
A
the half-line, but since the half-line is mapped to any interval by a conformal transformation, to which the AdS/CFT physics is insensitive, it applies to any interval or ball
A
.

We recognize this dual CFT rewriting (
8.16
) of the black hole first law (
8.15
) as the first law of entanglement (
8.6
). The conclusion is that a gravitational first law in the bulk corresponds to an entanglement first law in the CFT. It follows that, from the CFT perspective, the entanglement first law imposes linearized Einstein equations in the dual bulk theory. This is a famous result by van Raamsdonk, and is often summarized in statements that
gravity emerges from entanglement
in AdS/CFT!
