CCR4-NOT is required for suppressing pervasive transcription and retrotransposable elements
Authors: Shardul D. Kulkarni, Alexis Morrissey, Aswathy Sebastian, Oluwasegun T. Akinniyi, Cheryl A. Keller, Istvan Albert, Shaun Mahony, Joseph C. Reese
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
CCR4-NOT regulates multiple steps in gene regulation and has been well studied in yeast. Although the human complex is known to play an essential role in cytoplasmic mRNA degradation, its nuclear functions are poorly characterized. Here, we used auxin-induced degradation to deplete the scaffold subunit CNOT1 and the E3 ligase CNOT4 to characterize the complex's regulation of transcription. Transient transcriptome profiling (TT-Seq) revealed that depleting either subunit led to widespread activation of RNA synthesis in genic and intergenic regions. Interestingly, fewer genes were down-regulated, including KRAB-zinc-finger protein (KZNF) genes. KZNFs repress genes and retrotransposable elements (rTEs), and consistent with decreased KZNF expression, rTEs, mainly long interspersed nuclear elements, were activated. Full-length active long interspersed nuclear elements and rTEs located outside of genes were activated too, suggesting that the increased transcription is not the direct result of transcription of the genes in which the rTEs are embedded. Most of the activated transcription events occurred near KZNF binding sites, suggesting that KZNF regulation plays a role in suppressing both genic and rTE transcription. Finally, we demonstrate that CCR4-NOT regulates the stability of rTE RNAs, indicating that the complex tightly controls transposon expression by repressing transcription and targeting their RNAs for degradation.

Pervasive transcription in humans refers to the extensive activity of RNA polymerase across the genome, expressing regions not normally transcribed including intergenic regions (IGRs), introns, antisense strands, and retrotransposable elements (rTEs). Although a subset of these transcripts may have regulatory roles, the majority are rapidly degraded to prevent interference with gene expression, transcriptional fidelity, and genome stability. Suppressing pervasive transcription is critical for maintaining transcriptional fidelity and preventing interference with regulatory networks. Transcription from nonfunctional regions or cryptic promoters results in aberrant RNA species, disrupts chromatin organization, and interferes with core nuclear processes such as DNA replication, transcription, and RNA processing. Strong regulatory mechanisms that limit pervasive transcription and retrotransposon activity are therefore required to ensure accurate gene expression, preserve genome stability, and reduce susceptibility to transcription-associated dysfunction diseases [reviewed in (1, 2, 3)].

Long interspersed nuclear elements (LINEs) are abundant retrotransposons that influence development, genome integrity, and genome evolution (4, 5, 6). LINE-1 (L1) sequences comprise nearly 20% of the human and mouse genomes (7). Because L1s are responsible for retro-transposition of nonautonomous retro-elements, such as SINEs, L1 activity may account for as much as 50% of mammalian DNA (8). Activation of LINE elements is associated with genome instability, cancer, neurological problems, and aging (6, 9, 10). LINEs and other repeat elements are silenced by Kruppel-associated box zinc-finger proteins (KZFPs), a large family of repressors (11, 12). KZFPs suppress rTEs by recruiting the transcriptional repressor KAP1/TRIM28, which in turn recruits chromatin corepressors such as the SETDB1 histone methyltransferase, heterochromatin protein 1, and the NuRD complex (11, 13, 14, 15, 16). KZFPs also bind near promoters of genes and endogenous retrovirus long terminal repeats (LTRs) to repress gene expression (11, 17, 18, 19). Furthermore, KZFPs play an important role in suppressing pervasive transcription from rTEs and other genic regions. Given the importance of KZFPs in shaping the transcriptional landscape of genomes and the dire consequences of activating LINE elements, identifying the mechanisms of LINE silencing and the pathways that regulate KZFP activity is crucial to understanding gene expression, development, and genome integrity.

CCR4-NOT is a highly conserved complex that regulates multiple steps in gene expression in the nucleus and cytoplasm. Subunits of the Ccr4-Not complex were first identified and characterized in yeast as regulators of the TATA-binding protein and transcription initiation, but it has since been shown to regulate multiple stages of gene expression (20, 21, 22). However, comparatively little is known about its function in metazoans, especially in the nucleus. In the cytoplasm, CCR4-NOT deadenylates and initiates mRNA decay, monitors the translatability of mRNAs, and directs the destruction of poorly translated messages, such as those enriched in nonoptimal codons (23, 24, 25, 26, 27). The scaffold subunit CNOT1 is critical for bridging RNA-binding proteins and the deadenylase subunits to target mRNAs for degradation (28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33). CNOT1 is required for the assembly of CCR4-NOT and its knockdown or depletion in cells destroys the complex and leads to reduced levels of many subunits (33, 34). CNOT1 depletion leads to caspase-dependent apoptosis and cell death and is considered essential in most cell types (33, 34, 35). CNOT1 knockout mice die during embryogenesis, indicating an essential role in mammalian development (36). CNOT4 is a RING domain-containing E3 ligase (22, 37, 38). CNOT4 is involved in the ubiquitination of the small ribosomal subunit protein eS7, which acts as a signal to pause translation during elongation as ribosomes decode nonoptimal codons (27, 39). Apart from its role in regulating cotranslational functions, CNOT4 was recently shown to mediate the effects of codon composition on transcription in the nucleus (40). Although involved in critical steps in gene regulation, CNOT4, unlike CNOT1, is not essential for viability (33). Our previous study revealed strikingly opposite phenotypes upon rapid and complete depletion of CNOT1 versus CNOT4. CNOT1 depletion led to disassembly of CCR4-NOT, cell cycle arrest, loss of cell viability and a genome-wide decrease in RNA decay. Acute depletion of CNOT4, on the other hand, did not affect CCR4-NOT integrity, cell viability or steady-state RNA levels. Interestingly, we found a global increase in RNA decay upon CNOT4 depletion (33).

Beyond its well-studied roles in mRNA deadenylation and decay, CCR4–NOT has been implicated as a critical transcriptional regulator in eukaryotic cells (from yeast to mammals) (21, 22). The complex interacts with general transcription factors, chromatin-associated proteins, and RNA Polymerase II to influence transcription initiation and elongation (41, 42, 43, 44, 45, 46). Yeast CCR4-NOT (yCcr4-Not) regulates TFIID and the SAGA (Spt–Ada–Gcn5 acetyltransferase), SRB/Mediator complex and PAF (RNA polymerase II-associated factor) complexes (20, 43, 44, 45, 46). Furthermore, yCcr4-Not affects transcription elongation by preventing RNA polymerase II arrest (42, 46, 47, 48, 49, 50).

Comparatively less is known about the transcriptional roles of human CCR4-NOT. CNOT1 represses ligand-dependent transcriptional activation function of estrogen receptor (ER) α, suggesting a negative role in transcriptional regulation (51). In addition, recruitment of CNOT2 and CNOT9 to the promotor of a reporter gene resulted in transcriptional repression in human U-2 OS cells (52). CCR4-NOT interacts with sequence-specific transcription factors, for example, c-Myc, p53, and nuclear receptors (53, 54). These interactions result in either repressing or coactivating their transcriptional output in a context-dependent manner. Overall, these data suggest that CCR4-NOT is a multifunctional transcriptional regulator that bridges upstream signaling cues and the core transcriptional machinery. Although there is ample evidence suggesting its involvement in regulating transcription, genome-wide studies uncovering its role in transcription in human cells are limited.

To probe the transcription functions of CCR4-NOT, we measured ongoing transcription using pulse-labeling after rapid depletion of CNOT1 and CNOT4. Depleting either subunit caused a rapid, widespread increase in transcription across the genome, including IGRs. A unique set of down-regulated genes was identified, including the Kruppel-associated box-containing zinc nuclear finger (KZNF) family of repressors. Consistent with the role of KZNFs in repressing rTEs, transcription of LINE and other rTEs increased. Thus, we have discovered a novel function for CCR4-NOT in regulating genome-wide transcription and maintaining genome integrity by suppressing pervasive transcription and transposable elements.

Results

Depletion of CNOT1 and CNOT4 leads to a widespread increase in nascent transcription

Yeast Ccr4- Not regulates transcription by promoting elongation and controlling the TATA-binding protein (21, 22, 42), but it is unclear whether human CCR4-NOT functions similarly. We performed transient transcriptome sequencing (TT-seq) using 4-thiouracil labeling to measure ongoing transcription (55). DLD-1 TIR 1 +, CNOT1 AID, and CNOT4 AID cells (33) were treated with auxin for 2 or 8 h to deplete the subunits, followed by a 15- minute labeling pulse (Fig. 1A). Immunoblotting for CNOT1 and CNOT4 confirmed rapid depletion of the proteins (Fig. 1B). TT-seq data were normalized to a spike-in control (Schizosaccharomyces pombe 4- thiouracil-labeled RNA). Two biological replicates were analyzed, a common practice that meets ENCODE standards. The replicates showed very high reproducibility (Pearson's r > 0.99, Fig. 1C). Interestingly, depleting CNOT1 or CNOT4 significantly increased nascent transcription genome-wide as early as 2 h postdepletion (Fig. 2, A and B). Protein depletion takes at least 1 h (33), so the increase in transcription was very rapid. Thousands of differentially transcribed genes (DTGs) were identified (Fig. S1, A-D), of which ≥ 80% showed enhanced transcription (≥1.5 FC, padj < 0. 01 cutoff, Fig. 2C) and were predominantly protein-coding mRNAs (∼80–90% of DTGs, Fig. 2D). Furthermore, there was a significant overlap between DTGs under CNOT1 and CNOT4 depletion conditions (Fig. S2, A and B). The TT-seq data were confirmed by RT-qPCR analysis of several candidate genes, with the majority showing increased nascent transcription when each subunit was depleted (Fig. S3, A−D. It is unlikely that altered mRNA decay is significantly affecting transcription, at least at the 2 h time point, because the response is rapid (2 h) and the median mRNA half-life in DLD-1 cells is 10.6 h (33). Furthermore, our previous work showed that depleting CNOT1 (increased) and CNOT4 (decreased) had opposite effects on mRNA half-life, and yet the transcriptional response was strikingly similar (33). However, the number of DTGs that met the 1.5-fold cutoff increased from 2 to 8 h of CNOT1 depletion (Fig. S1). CCR4-NOT regulates pervasive transcription in the IGRs

We next examined whether the increase in transcription is restricted to genes or also occurs in IGRs. The genome was divided into 10-kb windows, and TT-seq reads from genic regions within these windows were subtracted to quantify intergenic transcription. In some cases, segments overlapping with a gene resulted in windows shorter than 10 kb. We only considered IGRs > 8 kb in our analysis. The k-means clustering plot showed a marked change in transcription within IGRs. First, under both CNOT1 and CNOT4 depletion at 2 h, increases in transcription in IGRs were more prominent than decreases (clusters C2 and C4, versus clusters C3 and C5, Fig. 3A). Indeed, a similar pattern was observed when we specifically analyzed differentially expressed IGRs (Fig. 3A and 2 h data points). By contrast, depletion of CNOT1 for 8 h resulted in a marked decrease in transcription across several thousand IGRs (clusters C2, C3, and C5). This pattern was underscored by the number of differentially expressed IGRs (Fig. 3B, CNOT1 - 8 h). As noted above, one possible explanation is that, by 8 h of depletion, mRNAs encoding transcriptional repressors accumulate because of the loss of deadenylase activity in CNOT1-depleted cells. This would partially restore repression of pervasive transcription (see below). Finally, we examined several intergenic loci and found evidence of pervasive transcription in IGRs in depleted cells (Fig. 3, C–E). CNOT1 and CNOT4 regulate transcription of the KRAB-zinc finger repressor family genes

CNOT1-and CNOT4-depleted cells display widespread increases in genic transcription, except for genes in cluster 3, which were noticeably reduced (Fig. 2A, cluster 3). GO analysis of genes in cluster 3 revealed that “DNA binding transcription factor activity” was overrepresented (Fig. 4A, 143 genes, 2.1x enrichment, 2.3E-14 FDR). Furthermore, most transcription factors in that cluster were KZNFs, which function in gene repression (109/143, Fig. 4B). To gain further insight into this novel finding, we specifically examined ZNFs down-regulated under the depletion conditions and found that > 90% of the down-regulated ZNFs (≤0.67 FC, padj < 0.01) were KZNFs (Fig. S4A). KZFPs bind throughout the genome, and we speculated that the widespread increase in nascent transcription would correlate with the presence of a KZFP binding event. We used KZFP Chip-exo data (18) to determine whether the KZNFs in cluster 3 (Fig. 2A) bind near genes with increased transcription. We found that ∼90% of the DTGs had at least one KZFP binding peak within or ±1 KB of the gene (Fig. 4C). The binding peaks of the down-regulated KZFPs were more enriched at genes whose transcription increased than at those repressed by CCR4-NOT depletion (Fig. 4D). This strong association suggests that reduced KZFP expression contributes to the widespread increase in transcription in CCR4-NOT-depleted cells.

Depletion of CNOT1 and CNOT4 increases transcription of repeat elements, particularly LINEs

KZNFs cluster on chromosome 19, having expanded and diversified in response to the rise of novel endogenous retroelements (13, 18, 56, 57). Most KZNFs down-regulated by CCR4-NOT knockdown are on chr19, whereas KZNFs on other chromosomes typically show increased transcription, like the rest of the genome (Fig. S4B). Examination of Integrative Genome Viewer tracks over KZNF genes on Chr19 confirmed strong transcriptional down-regulation (Fig. S4C). KZFPs bind to and repress endogenous retroviral elements and protein-coding genes by targeting promoters and ancient LTRs adjacent to genes (5, 11, 18, 19, 58). To test whether depleting CNOT1 and CNOT4 derepresses rTEs, the TT-seq data were remapped using the Allo tool, which efficiently allocates multimapped reads to genomic and offers enhanced accuracy in multimapping read assignment to repeat elements (59). Because this is a new tool, we compared rTE expression estimates from Allo with those from the well-established, commonly used TETranscripts program (60). Since TETranscripts does not provide quantification of individual rTE expression as Allo does, we grouped rTEs within the same rTE family to enable comparison. The high correlation between reads for each rTE family between Allo and TETranscripts indicates that both approaches are equally effective at mapping rTEs (r = 0.98–0.99, Fig. S5).

Differential expression analysis of rTEs mapped with the Allo tool revealed increased expression of LINE retrotransposons (LINEs) and other repeat elements, including some SINEs and LTRs of endogenous retroviruses (Fig. 5, A and B). We then analyzed the expression of the mapped LINEs (N = 15,611) and found a striking increase in LINE transcription within 2 h of depleting CNOT1 or CNOT4 (Fig. 5C, clusters 1 and 2, ∼77% of all LINEs analyzed). Furthermore, the timing of LINE activation coincided with KZNF gene down-regulation (compare Figs. 2A and 5C). At 8 h after CNOT1 and CNOT4 depletion, global derepression of LINE expression was still observed (Fig. 5D, clusters 2 and 4, ∼79% of all LINEs analyzed), but a subset of LINEs was rerepressed in CNOT1-depleted cells (cluster 1). The recovery of LINE repression at 8 h could be explained by increased steady-state levels of certain KZNF mRNAs, due to reduced mRNA decay, thereby reestablishing repression by restoring KZFP levels. We examined the steady-state levels of KZNFs in depleted cells and found an initial, pronounced drop in abundance at 2 h in both CNOT1-and CNOT4-depleted cells, indicating that reduced transcription led to significant decreases in mRNA abundance by 2 h (Fig. S6, A and B). However, the steady state levels of these messages rebounded, and increased by 8 h due to enhanced stability in CNOT1-depleted cells (Fig. S6, A and B) (33). It is remarkable that compensatory effects of depletion were observed as soon as 8 h. This underscores the importance of examining short depletion times versus longer, days-long depletions of regulatory factors such as CCR4-NOT. In contrast, while the steady state levels dropped at 2 h in CNOT4-depleted cells, they returned to the starting level by 8 h (Fig. S6, C and D). This is consistent with our previous findings and those of others, which show CNOT1 depletion disrupts CCR4-NOT and abolishes deadenylase activity (33, 34, 61, 62). The majority of expressed repeat elements, including LINEs, are located within gene bodies (≈90%, Fig. S7A). To rule out the possibility that the increased reads mapping to rTEs were mainly due to increased transcription of the gene in which they are embedded, we analyzed the transcription of rTEs located outside any annotated gene. Indeed, several hundred rTEs outside genes were also activated upon CNOT1 or CNOT4 depletion (Figs. 6, A–H, S7, B–E). Functional full-length LINE-1 elements are ∼6 kb long, and contain a 5′-untranslated region harboring an internal promoter, two ORFs, and a 3′UTR (63). Since most LINE-1 elements are 5′ truncated and lack their promoter (64) we interrogated only those LINE-1s > 6 kb. 3% of the LINE-1s detected in our study were > 6 kb long (Fig. 7A), and several hundred of these full-length LINE-1s showed increased expression when CNOT1 or CNOT4 was depleted (Fig. 7, B and C). Finally, we analyzed the putatively active LINE-1s residing in the human genome as annotated by L1Base (v2) (65). Again, the majority of these active LINE-1s showed transcriptional activation under depletion conditions (Fig. 7D). LINEs influence the expression of adjacent genes by modulating chromatin, acting as enhancers, or providing alternative promoters and transcriptional start sites (5, 12, 18, 58). To investigate whether changes in gene transcription are linked to LINE expression, we correlated the change in transcription of KZFP-bound LINEs with that of the adjacent gene. We focused on KZFPs that are down-regulated in CCR4-NOT-depleted cells and have the highest number of binding peaks (the same set of KZFPs was analyzed in Fig. 4, C and D). The analysis revealed a highly significant positive correlation between changes in LINE transcription and those of their adjacent genes (Fig. S8, A and B). Similar correlations were observed with other transposable repeat elements, such as SINEs and LTRs (Spearman’s rho > 0.37, p-value < 8.42E-85, data not shown). Together, these findings suggest that CCR4-NOT’s ability to suppress pervasive transcription is related to its functions in regulating KZFPs.

CCR4-NOT regulates turnover of rTE transcripts

To our knowledge, there is no comprehensive data on the half-lives of transcripts derived from rTE elements. Processed rTEs are polyadenylated, and may be under the control of CCR4-NOT (66). To measure the half-lives of rTE transcripts and determine whether CCR4-NOT regulates their stability, we mapped our published dataset of RNA expression following transcriptional inhibition with triptolide (33) using Allo. We measured rTE RNA levels at different time points (0, 3, 6, and 12 h) after transcription shutdown in undepleted (DLD-1TIR1+) cells and in CNOT1-and CNOT4-depleted cells, and calculated their half-lives. We also separated rTEs by location (genic or intergenic) and by element type. The median half-life of rTE RNAs (8.9 h) was comparable to the median half-life of canonical RNAs (10.6 h) (33). We have shown that depleting CNOT1 or CNOT4 had opposite effects on RNA half-lives. Depleting CNOT1 increased, whereas depleting CNOT4 decreased, the half-lives of RNAs. Similar to all genes, depleting CNOT1 resulted in a significant global increase in the half-lives of rTE transcripts; the median half-life increased from 8.9 h to 14.4 h (Fig. 8A). By contrast, depleting CNOT4 accelerated RNA turnover, decreasing the median half-life to 6 h. When we examined changes in rTE stability within or outside a gene, the effects of subunit depletion were observed in both groups, suggesting that CCR4-NOT regulates the turnover of rTE transcripts irrespective of their location relative to the gene body (Fig. 8B). Finally, the effects of CNOT1 and CNOT4 depletion were observed across all rTE families (Fig. 8C). Taken together, our data show that CCR4-NOT plays a role in regulating the turnover of the rTE transcriptome-wide. Discussion

CCR4-NOT has been well studied in yeast, where it was initially defined as a transcription regulator, but was later found to carry out other posttranscriptional functions [reviewed in (20, 21, 22)]. However, much less is known about the transcription functions of human CCR4-NOT. Using TT-seq to directly measure nascent transcription, we show that acute depletion of either CNOT1 or CNOT4 results in a rapid, genome-wide increase in transcription, affecting protein-coding genes, IGRs, and repeat elements. We propose that this function is attributed, at least in part, to its regulation of KZNF expression. Furthermore, we provide the first comprehensive examination of CCR4-NOT’s function in regulating the decay of rTE RNAs. Taken together, our results suggest that, rather than acting solely as a posttranscriptional regulator, CCR4-NOT affects multiple layers of gene regulation, collectively shaping the transcriptional landscape.

Depletion of CNOT1 or CNOT4 leads to a widespread increase in nascent transcription within 2 h. This rapid response strongly argues that changes in mRNA stability alone cannot account for the upregulation of transcription. This conclusion is further supported by our data showing that depleting CNOT1 and CNOT4 has opposite effects on mRNA stability: CNOT1 depletion increases, whereas CNOT4 depletion decreases transcript stability [Fig. 8B, (33)]. However, we detected additional transcriptional changes after longer CNOT1 depletion (8 h), which may be augmented by changes in the steady-state levels of transcriptional regulators. For example, the enhanced transcription of a significant number of LINE elements observed at 2 h subsides by 8 h of CNOT1 depletion. This rerepression coincided with the recovery of KZNF steady-state mRNA levels (Fig. S6B ds). Likewise, other cumulative changes in genic transcription between 2 h and 8 h could result from compensatory changes in steady-state mRNA levels of regulators. On the other hand, depleting CNOT4 produces a temporally stable transcriptional response. In fact, the transcriptional state of CNOT1-and CNOT4-depleted cells is remarkably similar at 2 h, but begins to differ at 8 h when the effects of mRNA stability of regulators accumulate (Figs. 2A, 3A, and 5C).

The transcription of some genes correlates with the codon composition of the coding region, especially GC-containing codons (40). Recently, CNOT4 has been shown to regulate this phenomenon (37). Reporter gene assays showed that codon optimality affected nuclear transcription in a CNOT4-dependent manner. Coding regions comprise a small fraction of gene sequences, and the authors also found a correlation between the GC content of introns and 3′UTRs and the transcription of the affected genes, suggesting that this form of regulation is not restricted to the codon content of messenger RNAs. The mechanism by which codon composition and GC-content affect transcription is unknown, but it may involve a form of transcript buffering, a process in which changes in decay or translation lead to compensatory changes in transcription (67, 68, 69, 70). Our data indicate that transcription buffering occurs in CNOT4-depleted cells. Depleting CNOT4 accelerates mRNA turnover, yet steady-state mRNA levels remain unchanged because the increase in transcription offsets this effect (33).

One striking result is that, unlike the rest of the genome, transcription of KZNF genes was down-regulated in depleted cells. KZFPs are the largest family of transcriptional repressors in vertebrates, and the human genome has ∼350 KZNFs; ∼200 are located within six clusters on chromosome 19 (13). KZNFs bind ubiquitously throughout the genome, targeting promoters and binding sites within rTEs (11, 18, 71), and their activity would reshape the transcriptional landscape. KZFPs function in transcriptional silencing by repressing transposable elements, primarily retrotransposons (11), including LINEs. LINEs are a class of non-LTR retrotransposons with substantial potential to regulate chromatin structure and the transcription of adjacent genes (4, 16, 17, 58, 72). We observe robust derepression of repeat elements, most prominently LINE retrotransposons, in CCR4-NOT-depleted cells. TT-seq relies on short-read sequencing, raising the question of whether gene activation and transcription from genic promoters produce more reads over rTEs embedded in genes, or whether the loss of KZNF-mediated repression simultaneously activates both. We favor the latter. Transcription of repeat elements in IGRs and full-length LINE-1 elements, including those annotated as potentially active, was upregulated as well.

Since KZNFs suppress retrotransposon and gene expression, the increase in transcription in CCR4-NOT-depleted cells can result from reduced KZNF expression. However, the rapid activation of genes and rTEs occurs within 2 h of depleting CCR4-NOT subunits, which would require a reduction in the existing pool of KZNF mRNA and protein within this time frame. Remarkably, though, the steady-state level of KZNF mRNAs was significantly and strongly reduced within 2 h, indicating that the drop in KZNF transcription results in a significant decrease in KZNF mRNA and that meaningful changes in mRNA are achieved within this time frame (Fig. S6). KZNF protein levels would also need to be reduced substantially to relieve repression too. This suggests that the immediate increase in transcription of KZNF targets is more complex than just reducing KZNF levels. Instead, this argues for multiple mechanisms for how the complex regulates genic and rTE transcription. However, while the immediate effects may not be via KZNF regulation, the reduced KZNF levels in depleted cells are clear and would ultimately result in reduced KZNF levels that could impact normal physiological processes of the cell. In this regard, CCR4-NOT immediately represses rTE transcription, and a reinforcing silencing mechanism maintains repression by regulating KZNF levels. Such tight control is expected, given the consequences of rTE reactivation and pervasive transcription. Indeed, as discussed below, CCR4-NOT also targets rTE transcripts for degradation.

One mechanism for the immediate response is that CNOT4-dependent ubiquitination targets regulatory factors that control KZFP levels, potentially via ubiquitin-dependent proteolysis or nondestructive ubiquitin signaling. Such regulation can be very rapid. Another explanation for the rapid induction of genes and rTEs, which would not require full depletion of KZNF protein, is direct regulation by CCR4-NOT. CNOT3 and TRIM28 were identified as factors required for stem cell renewal and for preventing differentiation of mouse stem cells, and their promoter binding overlapped considerably (73). TRIM28/KAP1 is a corepressor that KZNFs use to silence rTE expression (11, 18). CCR4-NOT may regulate TRIM28 function or its corepressors, although we did not observe any changes in KAP1 protein levels during the depletion assay (data not shown).

Another significant contribution of our work is the demonstration that CCR4-NOT regulates the turnover of rTE-derived transcripts. We show that depleting CNOT1 markedly stabilizes rTE RNAs across all families and genomic contexts, whereas depleting CNOT4 accelerates their decay. These opposing effects mirror those observed for canonical mRNAs (33) and suggest that rTE transcripts are bona fide substrates of CCR4-NOT-mediated decay. Because rTE-derived transcripts are often polyadenylated (74, 75, 76), this 3′ end modification makes them ideal targets for CCR4-NOT-dependent RNA decay. We propose a model (Fig. 9) in which CCR4-NOT suppresses transcription of genes, IGRs, and repetitive elements, at least partly, by regulating KZNFs, while simultaneously promoting the decay of those transcripts to buffer fluctuations in transcriptional output. CCR4-NOT has been associated with the suppression of transcripts from heterochromatic telomeric regions in S. pombe through cotranscriptional gene silencing, which regulates chromatin structure, particularly H3K9me (77, 78, 79, 80). Likewise, Drosophila CCR4-NOT represses transcription of repeat elements and localizes to sites of repression (81). Interestingly, unlike the mechanism in fission yeast, no changes in chromatin modifications at the loci were observed in fly germ cells. This study found that genic transcription was not affected by CCR4-NOT mutations, except for genes located within 1.5 kb of transposable elements. Both mechanisms involve transcript degradation at the site of expression, which may require the nuclease activity of the Ccr4 and Caf1 subunits or the recruitment of the TRAMP complex and the exosome (82, 83, 84, 85). As described above, we propose that human CCR4-NOT’s regulation of KZNFs is at least a major contributor to the suppression of gene and rTEs transcription in humans. KZNFs are mammalian-specific. Thus, while the requirement for CCR4-NOT in suppressing repeat elements is conserved across eukaryotes, the mechanism employed to achieve this varies among species. This is akin to dosage compensation mechanisms. The overall regulatory logic and outcome are conserved, but the mechanism by which it is achieved is species-specific.

Yeast and human CCR4-NOT maintain genome integrity and DNA damage responses (20, 49, 50, 86). Spurious activation of rTE, especially LINEs, causes genome instability and contributes to disease (6, 9, 10). Recently, 5-ethynyl uridine incorporation and microscopy of stained cells showed that partial CNOT1 knockdown by siRNA transfection increased bulk transcription (86). This low-resolution method may have captured the increased genic and rTE transcription we demonstrated here. Interestingly, increased R-loop (RNA:DNA hybrids) formation and genome instability were also observed in the knockdown cells. R-loops are enriched in genomic regions with a higher abundance of L1 LINEs (87). Our discovery that CCR4-NOT suppresses global transcription and rTE activation suggests a mechanism by which CCR4-NOT contributes to genome stability and integrity.

Experimental procedures

Cell lines

DLD-1TIR1+ cells were a kind gift from Dr Mary Dasso at the National Institutes of Health. Cells were maintained in complete media [Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1X glutamax, and 1X penicillin and streptomycin] in a humidified incubator at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Generation of AID-tagged cell lines has been described previously (33).

RNA extraction

After treatment with ±1 mM auxin for the desired time, the media were decanted, and the cells were lysed directly in 1 ml Trizol. The cells were resuspended thoroughly by pipetting up and down several times and incubated at room temperature for 5 min 0.2 ml of chloroform was added per 1 ml of Trizol. The tubes were capped and shaken vigorously by hand for 15 s, then incubated at room temperature for 2 to 3 min. The mixture was then centrifuged at >12,000 x g for 15 min at 4 °C. The aqueous phase was collected in a separate tube, and an equal volume of 100% ethanol was added. The RNA was cleaned up using the Ambion RNA PureLink Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions and eluted in 100 μl RNase-free water.

Cell lysis and Western blot

Cells were washed with ice-cold wash buffer (PBS supplemented with 1 mM PMSF) and harvested by scraping in wash buffer, then briefly centrifuged at high speed for 10 s. The resulting cell pellet was lysed in Laemmli buffer, boiled at 95 °C for 5 to 7 min, and centrifuged at high speed for 10 min. The supernatant (lysate) was collected and stored at −20 °C until analysis by Western blotting with antibodies against CNOT1 (#44613S), CNOT4 (#PA5-82214), or DDX6 (raised in-house). Proteins were detected with horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibodies and enhanced chemiluminescence, and signals were visualized with a ChemiDoc MP imaging system (BioRad). The specificity of the antibodies was validated by shift in the mobility due to addition of C-terminal mAID tag and depletion upon auxin treatment (in case of CNOT1 and CNOT4) or by disappearance of the band upon siRNA treatment (in case of DDX6).

TT-seq

TT-seq libraries from two biological replicates were generated as described previously (55). Briefly, cells (parental, CNOT1AID, and CNOT4AID) were grown in 150-mm dishes to reach 70% confluence at the time of auxin treatment (2 h or 8 h). Cells were then labeled by adding 4-thiouridine (4TU, final concentration 500 μM) to the medium for 15 min. All work involving 4TU was performed under conditions of minimal direct light exposure. Medium was discarded, and cells were immediately lysed in Trizol. RNA extraction was carried out as described earlier. A total of 200 μg of total RNA was mixed with 5 μg of spike-in control (total RNA from S. pombe labeled with 4TU) in a 250-μl reaction. RNA fragmentation was carried out by adding 50 μl of NaOH (1M) to this reaction (40 min on ice). RNA fragmentation was stopped by adding 200 μl of Tris (1M, pH 6.8). The RNA was phenol/chloroform extracted and used for in vitro biotinylation using MTS-Biotin. RNA was heated at 65 °C for 10 min, quick-chilled on ice, and then 1X biotin buffer and MTS-biotin (Biotium) were added. Biotinylation was carried out in the dark at room temperature for 40 min on a rotator, followed by phenol/chloroform extraction to remove excess biotin. After heating at 65 °C and quick-chilling on ice, RNA was captured on Streptavidin beads (Dynabeads MyOne Streptavidin C1) at room temperature for 20 min. Beads were washed in wash buffer (100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 1 M NaCl, 10 mM EDTA, and 0.1% Tween 20; prewarmed at 55 °C) 6 times, followed by elution by incubating in 100 mM DTT (twice, 5 min each). The pooled eluates were precipitated in the presence of 0.5 M NaCl and 0.2 mg/ml glycogen. RNA was quantified using the Qubit RNA estimation kit.

Sequencing libraries were prepared using Illumina Stranded Total RNA Prep with Ribo-Zero Plus according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A total of 300 ng of RNA was heated at 65 °C and quickly chilled on ice, then EPH3 buffer (from the library preparation kit) was added to adjust buffer concentrations for cDNA synthesis, followed directly by first-strand synthesis. The human (GRCh38) and S. pombe (ASM294v2) genomes were concatenated, and reads were mapped to the combined genome using the STAR aligner (version 2.7.11a). Raw counts of all reads mapping to the annotated region for each gene (i.e., intron + exon) were obtained using featureCounts. Gene read counts were transformed using a variance-stabilizing transformation in the DESeq2 R package. Both normalization and differential expression analysis were performed with the DESeq2 package. We used the S. pombe spike-in read counts for normalization. Genes showing ≥ 1.5-fold change at p-adj < 0.01 were denoted as differentially expressed. Raw and processed DESeq2 data are found under accession number GSE308749.

Preparation of 4TU labeled S. pombe spike in RNA

S. pombe cells were gown to mid log phase in yeast peptone dextrose (YPD) broth followed by addition of 4-thiouracil at the final concentration of 50 μM. After 12 min, labeling was quenched by rapid mixing of the culture with dry ice-cold methanol. The mixture was centrifuged for 5 min at 3000 rpm at 4 °C. The pellet was resuspended in ice-cold YPD and centrifuged for 30 s at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded and the pellet (with OD600 = 5) was stored at −80 °C till RNA extraction.

Frozen pellets of labeled S. pombe cells were thawed in ice-cold YPD. Cells were centrifuged for 5 min at 3000 rpm at 4 °C. Supernatant was removed, and the pellet was resuspended in 450 μl AE buffer (10 mM Tris-Cl; 0.5 mM EDTA; pH 8.0), followed by addition of 50 μl of 10% SDS. The mixture was vortexed for 15 s followed by addition of 500 μl of AE-equilibrated phenol. After thoroughly mixing by vortexing, samples were incubated at 65 °C for 4 min and transferred to dry ice-ethanol bath for 60 s. The mixture was centrifuged for 4 min at 13,000 rpm at 4 °C and the aqueous phase was collected. A total of 300 μl AE phenol and 300 μl chloroform were added and the sample vortexed for 15 s followed by centrifugation for 2 min at 13,000 rpm at 4 °C. A total of 50 μl sodium acetate (3M, pH 5.3) and 1.25 ml of cold 100% ethanol were added to each sample, vortexed to mix and RNA was precipitated at −20 °C overnight. RNA was then pelleted by centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The pellet was washed in ice-cold 80% ethanol, airdried and resuspended in RNase free water. RNA was nanodrop estimated and its integrity was checked by running it on 1.2% agarose-Tris-Borate-EDTA gel electrophoresis. RNA was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C till use.

4TU labeling, cDNA synthesis, and RT-qPCR

4TU labeling and RNA extraction were carried out as described above. A total of 200 μg of total RNA was biotinylated with MTS-Biotin. Streptavidin pull-down was performed as described above. A total of 200 ng of purified RNA was used to synthesize cDNA with the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit, according to the manufacturer’s instructions, using a random hexamer primer (prediluted 1:3). The cDNA product was used directly in PCR applications or stored at −20 °C until use. RT-qPCR was performed using PerfeCTa SYBR Green SuperMix (Quantabio). For each condition, at least 2 experimental replicates were used. RT-qPCR was run on a CFX Opus 96 Real-Time PCR System (Biorad), and the data were analyzed using Biorad CFX Maestro 2.3 software (version 5.3.022.1030) and Microsoft Excel. Data was generated from at least 3 experimental determinations.

KZFP analysis

Raw sequencing files were downloaded from NCBI for 58 KZFPs identified in cluster 3 (Fig. 2A) of our TT-seq analysis, for which binding data were previously available (18). Reads were trimmed with fastp v0.23.4 using default settings. Bowtie2 v2.5.1 was then used to align the single-end reads to hg38 with the “-k 25” parameter to retain multimapped reads. Allo v1.1.1 (59) was used to allocate multimapped reads with default settings. Peaks were called with Macs2 using default settings. Gene locations were extracted from the GENCODE v29 gtf file for hg38. A gene’s location was considered the entire gene body, including introns. The resulting bed file was intersected with the KZFP peak calls ( ± 1000 bp) using BEDTools v2.31.1. The metadata for the KZFPs are included in Table S1.

Analysis of transcription in IGRs

To assess pervasive transcription in IGRs, the genome was divided into nonoverlapping 10 kb windows. Annotated coding regions overlapping the windows were subtracted to define IGRs. Reads mapping to intergenic windows were counted using featureCounts. Intergenic read counts were normalized using S. pombe intergenic spike-in read counts. Differential expression analysis was performed with DESeq2 to identify significantly differentially expressed intergenic windows. Raw and processed DESeq2 data are found under accession number GSE308749.

Analysis of repeat elements – TT-seq data

Reads were trimmed with fastp v0.23.4 and aligned with STAR v2.2.1 using the arguments “--outSAMmultNmax 25 --outFilterType BySJout” to retain multimapped reads. Allo v1.1.1 was used to assign multimapped reads with the arguments “--read-count --splice”. Following this, featureCounts was used to quantify transcript counts with the arguments “--countReadPairs -t exon -g gene_id -M”, which retains multimapped reads. The annotation file used in featureCounts was constructed from the GENCODE v29 gtf file and the annotated repeats from RepeatMasker v4.1.3 for hg38. After read summarization, DEseq2 was used to analyze differentially expressed transposable elements. In this analysis, the scaling factors calculated from the spike-in described above were used for normalization. In addition, canonical genes were removed from the table before DE analysis. We retained them during the featureCounts step to avoid transposable elements that overlapped gene exons. Raw and processed DESeq2 data are found under accession number GSE308749.

Calculation of half-lives of rTEs

To calculate RNA half-lives of rTEs, previously published sequencing data from our lab were interrogated [GEO: GSE277237, (33)]. Briefly, DLD-1TIR1+, CNOT1AID, and CNOT4AID cells were treated with auxin (1 mM), followed by triptolide (1 μM) for 0, 3, 6, and 12 h (in the presence of 1 mM auxin). Reads from the RNA-seq data were mapped using the Allo tool (59) and fold changes in expression relative to the 0 h treatment were calculated using DESeq2 analysis (with ERCC spike-in normalization). The decay rate constant (kdecay) was then calculated, followed by half-life determination [t1/2 = ln (2)/kdecay].
