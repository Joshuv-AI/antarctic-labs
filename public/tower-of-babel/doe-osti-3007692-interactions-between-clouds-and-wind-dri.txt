 Office of Science, Biological and Environmental Research (BER) program
 Atmospheric System Research (ASR) Program

Project Title: Interactions Between Clouds and Wind-Driven Surface Heat Exchanges over Land

 Principal Investigator: Ian N. Williams, Iowa State University
 Co-Investigator: William A. Gallus, Iowa State University

Executive Summary
Earth system model experiments show that increasing horizontal resolution fundamentally alters
the simulated soil-moisture-precipitation feedback. Kilometer-scale simulations often produce
weaker or even negative feedback compared to coarse-resolution models. A key difference of
kilometer-scale models is that they resolve mesoscale secondary circulations, including
boundary layer horizontal rolls and cellular structures, in addition to cold pools and downdrafts
associated with convective precipitation. However, because the relevant processes occur on
yet-smaller scales, these circulations are often poorly resolved. This project demonstrated that
boundary layer secondary circulations significantly affect surface heat exchanges and wind
gusts, and that current model parameterizations can misrepresent these processes at
kilometer-scale resolution. Using DOE Atmospheric Radiation Measurement (ARM)
observations and targeted experiments with the DOE Energy Exascale Earth System Model
(E3SM), we (1) identified physically unrealistic wind gust and surface flux responses to
secondary circulations, (2) diagnosed a systematic overestimation of wind shear in convective
cold pools, and (3) uncovered a multivariate relationship between land surface fluxes and the
scales of updrafts that form shallow cumulus clouds. These findings provide observation-based
recommendations for improving parameterizations of surface fluxes and wind gusts in
high-resolution Earth system models, thereby reducing uncertainty in convective storm
prediction and land-atmosphere feedbacks.

Summary of Research Findings
The first objective of this project was to understand how surface fluxes and wind gusts respond
to boundary layer secondary circulations. In Colston and Williams (2025), we simulated the
response of wind-driven fluxes to horizontal rolls and cellular structures in the boundary layer,
using the E3SM land model (ELM) at 3 km resolution. We found that rolls and cells generate
coherent spatial patterns in simulated land surface latent and sensible heat fluxes (~50 W m-2).
The surface energy balance shifts to accommodate wind-driven fluxes by reducing longwave
radiative cooling and ground heat conduction, demonstrating a connection between secondary
circulations and subsurface processes. ARM Southern Great Plains observations were used to
verify several aspects of the simulated surface response. The spatial patterns of radar-observed
boundary layer structures were consistent with ARM Doppler lidar vertical velocity retrievals.
Cross-correlation analyses revealed a quarter-cycle lag in surface wind gusts following the
passage of roll updrafts, meaning that boundary layer updrafts precede surface wind gusts. The
correlation pattern is vertically coherent, confirming that the observed wind gusts are driven by

mesoscale secondary circulations that fill the boundary layer (horizontal rolls and cellular
structures), rather than surface eddies.

We also evaluated E3SM’s convective wind gust parameterization in cases of rolls, cells, and
cold pools. Parameterized surface wind gusts increased surface heat fluxes within the quiescent
branches of secondary circulations (by 5 W/m2 in roll/cell cases and 50 W/m2 in cold pool
cases). Colston and Williams (2025) concluded that these results are physically unrealistic, and
recommended disabling the convective gust parameterization at resolutions that begin to
resolve secondary circulations. Additional ARM analyses (Colston and Williams, in prep.)
supported this recommendation. We found that periods of relatively calm winds are not
uncommon in unstable surface layers and within thunderstorm cold pools. Observed wind
speeds fall below 2 m/s for at least 10 minutes on 58% of summer days in the Southern Great
Plains. Near-calm surface wind gusts were best predicted without using a gust
parameterization. These results provide an observation-based recommendation for not using a
convective wind gust parameterization at kilometer scale resolutions such as those used in
DOE’s E3SM-SCREAM.

The second objective was to evaluate and recommend improvements in the representation of
wind-driven fluxes beneath convective clouds. In Castillo and Williams (2025), we used ARM
Doppler lidar and surface turbulence observations to evaluate the Monin-Obukhov similarity
theory used in E3SM’s surface turbulence parameterization. We found significant biases in
modeled wind profiles in the environments of thunderstorms. Few studies have examined this
problem, despite its importance for predicting surface wind gusts associated with convective
storms. We found that the theory overestimates wind shear in convective cold pools. Having
direct measurements, we were able to greatly reduce uncertainties in surface friction velocity
and Obukhov length in our study, providing a plausible explanation of differences between our
study and past work. Given our finding of overestimated shear by Monin-Obukhov similarity
theory, a likely source of the error is the missing representation of non-local transport of
momentum by organized downdrafts in cold pools. This conclusion was further supported by
evidence that the overestimation of wind shear was largest for cold pools having stable
stratification, meaning that the parameterized ‘small eddies’ are not effective enough at
transporting momentum in stably stratified layers.

The third objective of this project was to understand how land surface properties influence the
interactions between secondary circulations and convective clouds. We addressed the question
of how the types and scales of secondary circulations vary with land surface forcing. The widest
boundary layer updrafts are more likely to form the ‘roots’ of shallow cumulus clouds. In Williams
and Qiu (2022), we used ARM Doppler lidar and surface turbulence measurements to develop
potential predictors of the length scales of coherent updrafts associated with roll and cellular
structures. Past attempts using a stability parameter alone showed mixed success. We found
that the relationship between turbulence scale and surface fluxes is multivariate, which helps
explain why it has been difficult to predict the onset of rolls versus cells when using the stability
parameter alone. Radar clear-air returns were used to relate the lidar-derived turbulence scales
to horizontal rolls in the cloud-topped convective boundary layer, providing additional

observational evidence of the connection between boundary layer secondary circulations and
cumulus clouds.

PI Williams mentored several Iowa State University meteorology undergraduates who wrote
undergraduate senior theses related to this grant, with the following titles:
 ●​ Convective Triggering of Supercell Thunderstorms over Heterogeneous Land Surfaces
 ●​ Dual-Polarimetric Radar Predictors of Downburst Winds in Dry And Moist Environments
 ●​ Changes in Downdraft Convective Available Potential Energy Across the Contiguous
 United States
 ●​ The Role of Shear Instabilities in Mesoscale Convective Systems and Severe Wind
 Events
 ●​ Surface Friction and Cold Pool Shear Balance in Mesoscale Convective Systems: A
 Case Study and Experiment Using the WRF Model
 ●​ Influence of Elevated Terrain and Surface Properties on Convective Inhibition in the
 Central U.S.
 ●​ Effects of Storm Track Variability on Iowa’s 2020-2024 Drought
 ●​ Analysis of Precipitation Efficiency and Cloud Ice Generation Between MCS Types Using
 Dual-Polarization Radar Products

Four of these students went on to enroll in graduate meteorology programs, and one published
their work with the PI.

Williams and Fieweger (manuscript in press), led by undergraduate Fieweger, investigated how
environmental conditions and high-amplitude winter synoptic patterns interact to influence
downdraft convective available potential energy (DCAPE). We found that when changes in
environmental conditions are driven by temperature, simulated midlatitude cyclones advect
extreme downburst environments poleward, amplifying downburst potential beyond what
changes in local temperature alone would imply. This mechanism is a plausible explanation for a
recent high-impact December wind event in the U.S. Midwest.
