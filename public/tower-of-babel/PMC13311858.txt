Mechanical Revascularization of Cerebral Venous Sinuses in Extensive Cerebral Venous Sinus Thrombosis Using Pipeline-Pigging Technique and Intra-Sinus Tenecteplase: Technical Note and Review of Literature
Authors: Shakir Husain Hakim, Abdul Muqueet Shaikh, Vinod Singh Jatav, Anand Rammanohar Warrier, Abdusalam Kizhakkaniyakath, Pradeep Kumar V G, Presaad Pillai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnticoagulation therapy remains the cornerstone of treatment for cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (CVST). Endovascular therapy may be considered in selected patients with clinical deterioration despite optimal medical treatment. However, conventional mechanical thrombectomy using aspiration and/or stent-retrievers might be inadequate in extensive CVST because of large and organized thrombi and non-availability of venous thrombectomy devices. We present a technical modification using an angioplasty non-compliant (NC) balloon as an adjunct to suction thrombectomy for venous sinus mechanical thrombectomy in a case of extensive CVST. Conventional methods of thrombectomy failed to recanalize the sinuses; hence, an angioplasty NC balloon was used to decimate the clot with simultaneous suction thrombectomy to achieve near-complete recanalization of the affected sinuses with good clinical outcome. We name this the “pipeline-pigging” technique. The pipeline-pigging method of using a normal angioplasty NC balloon for venous sinus mechanical thrombectomy may serve as an effective and economical technical adjunct for extensive CVST refractory to conventional methods. This approach facilitates thrombus fragmentation, aspiration and improved delivery of thrombolytics and may expand the endovascular armamentarium for extensive CVST.Keywords: Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, Venous thrombectomy, Venous recanalization, Pipeline-pigging technique, Intra-sinus thrombolysis, Intra-sinus Tenecteplase

INTRODUCTION

Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (CVST) is an uncommon cause of stroke, accounting for 0.5% of all cases, which may present with intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) or venous infarction [1]. Anticoagulation with unfractionated heparin or low molecular weight heparin is the mainstay of treatment, even in the presence of hemorrhage [2,3]. Nevertheless, a subset of patients deteriorate clinically despite optimal anticoagulation, particularly those with extensive CVST or thrombus extending into the deep venous system [4]. Endovascular therapy (EVT) has emerged as a rescue strategy although robust evidence of clinical benefit is limited [5,6]. Technical challenges inherent to the venous system, including large-calibre sinuses and organised thrombi, may limit the effectiveness of arterial thrombectomy devices. We report a case of extensive CVST successfully treated using a “pipeline-pigging” technique in combination with intra-sinus thrombolysis after failure of conventional endovascular approaches.

CASE

A 58-year-old woman with type‑2 diabetes, dyslipidemia, and rheumatoid arthritis (on ayurvedic medications) presented with a 10-day history of headache and vomiting, followed by progressive bilateral visual blurring and diplopia over 4 days. Neurological examination revealed vision limited to light perception and grade IV papilledema in both eyes (Fig. 1G, H). Initial magnetic resonance imaging/magnetic resonance venography (MRI/MRV) (Fig. 1A–C) revealed extensive CVST involving the posterior half of superior sagittal sinus (SSS), torcula, right transverse sinus (TS) and sigmoid sinus (SS), without ICH, venous infarction, or congestive cerebral edema on available MRI sequences (Supplementary Fig. 1). An incidental right cerebellopontine angle mass consistent with an acoustic schwannoma was noted.

The patient was commenced on continuous intravenous unfractionated heparin infusion and titrated to maintain the therapeutic target of activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) twice the control level. Despite 5 days of optimal anticoagulation, her visual acuity continued to deteriorate, prompting endovascular treatment.

Procedure

Digital subtraction angiography (Fig. 2A–C) performed under general anesthesia confirmed the extensive thrombosis of posterior and middle third of SSS, torcula, right transverse and SSs, with cerebral venous congestion and venous drainage through collateral channels via superficial middle cerebral veins and cavernous sinus into facial veins. Endovascular treatment was performed through right femoral venous access using a 7F Shuttle sheath (Cook Medical). A 7F AXS Catalyst-7 (CAT-7) Distal Access Catheter (DAC) (Stryker Neurovascular) was navigated to the anterior most part of SSS and suction thrombectomy was performed with gentle pull of CAT-7; 2 such aspiration passes were performed using the Penumbra suction pump, however, there was no meaningful recanalization. To overcome this, mechanical maceration was performed using a 0.035-inch guidewire. Subsequently a 4×40 mm SolitaireX (Medtronic Neurovascular) stent (6×40 mm stent was not available at this point) was deployed in anterior third of SSS through a coaxial system using 7F AXS CAT-7 DAC and Excelsior XT-27 microcatheter (Stryker Neurovascular). The stent was slowly retrieved along with suction through CAT-7 DAC. Seven such passes of combined stent-retriever and aspiration were performed, but no significant clot retrieved. Then, local intra-sinus thrombolysis was performed using a total 6 mg of Tenecteplase administered slowly as small manual boluses over 10–15 minutes throughout the occluded SSS, right TS, and right SS via the microcatheter, starting from anterior most part of occluded SSS and then gradually pulling the microcatheter a few mm and injecting Tenecteplase to the proximal-most point of the SS. In addition, 1,000 units of heparin were administered intra-sinus. After Tenecteplase administration, we waited for about 15 to 20 minutes so that adequate time was given for its lytic action before undertaking aspiration starting from distal to proximal. Checking the angiography revealed partial recanalization of SSS and right SS.

At this time, a non-compliant (NC) angioplasty balloon (6×18 mm) was placed in the middle part of the SSS and was fully inflated (14 atm) before dragging the balloon all the way to the SS (like pipeline-pigging performed for cleaning oil and natural gas pipeline), along with a continuous pump (Penumbra) suction through the CAT-7 aspiration catheter, which was placed in the right jugular bulb. During balloon dragging, the CAT-7 was kept stationary in the right jugular bulb rather than advanced sequentially into the SSS, torcula, or transverse-SS to reduce repeated catheter manipulation across the thrombosed sinuses, maintain a stable aspiration route for fragments dragged from the SSS to the lateral sinus, and reduce distal clot migration. Moreover, the long length of free segment thus obtained while keeping the CAT-7 proximal may allow the retrieving inflated balloon to pass through different channels of the organised thrombus. No significant resistance was encountered while dragging the inflated balloon. The balloon was deflated just before entering the CAT-7 aspiration catheter with continuous suction maintained. Two such pipeline-pigging maneuvers were performed (Fig. 2G–J, Supplementary Video 1). This resulted in successful aspiration of small and large fragments of black clots.

Final angiography (Fig. 2D–F) demonstrated a nearly complete recanalization of the SSS and right transverse‑SS with restoration of antegrade flow and reduction in collateral venous drainage.

After procedure the patient was maintained on intravenous unfractionated heparin maintaining a target aPTT twice the control level, for 7 days. Subsequently, she was transitioned to oral warfarin with a target International Normalized Ratio of 2.5–3.5. Follow‑up MRI/MRV (Fig. 1D–F) showed a maintained recanalization of all sinuses. Clinically, she experienced a significant improvement in headache and partial recovery of her visual function (finger counting), fundus examination showed a significant improvement (Fig. 1I, J). She was discharged on oral anticoagulation.

DISCUSSION

Endovascular recanalization of cerebral venous sinuses is increasingly used to treat CVST that deteriorate neurologically despite adequate anticoagulation or in cases of extensive thrombus burden and poor neurological status [3,6]. To date, evidence supporting routine use of EVT remains limited, with only 1 randomised control trial (TO-ACT) and several uncontrolled studies evaluating its efficacy and safety [5,6]. In the TO-ACT trial, there was no difference in the proportion of patients achieving good functional outcome (modified Rankin Scale 0–1) at 1 year between EVT plus anticoagulation and anticoagulation alone (67% vs. 68%; relative risk 0.99, 95% confidence interval 0.71–1.38). The trial was halted for futility of clinical outcomes, likely reflecting technical challenges rather than definitive ineffectiveness of EVT in CVST [5].

Venous sinus anatomy and thrombus characteristics present unique technical challenges compared to arterial occlusions. Venous sinuses are large, non-cylindrical, triangular channels with maximal diameters of 13.0±2.6 mm for the SSS, 8.5±2.4 mm for the TS, and 8.6±2.0 mm for the SS [7]. Thrombi are often well-organized, particularly in delayed presentations, and clot burden is substantially higher than in intraarterial occlusions. Slow flow within the sinuses contributes to thrombus adherence and organisation, which, combined with the larger calibre and non-cylindrical geometry of dural sinuses, limits the effectiveness of devices designed for arterial thrombectomy. A similar evolution was also seen in ischemic stroke, in which studies that used early-generation thrombectomy devices did not demonstrate a benefit, highlighting the need for device and technique optimization [8].

Commonly employed endovascular techniques include local intra-sinus thrombolysis, stent-retriever thrombectomy, aspiration thrombectomy, or a combination of these approaches [9]. Despite widespread use, complete recanalization rates are modest. Combined stent-retriever and aspiration thrombectomy achieves complete recanalization only in 36% of reported cases [10]. This may be due to the smaller diameter of the stent-retrievers (maximum 6 mm) compared to the venous sinus calibre, insufficient deployment force, and the low efficiency of thrombus capture caused by the stent design [9]. Devices adapted from peripheral thrombectomy, such as AngioJet and Penumbra, have been applied intracranially. AngioJet is rigid and cumbersome, achieving complete recanalization in only 55% of patients [9], whereas Penumbra allows effective thrombus aspiration but faces challenges with large organized thrombi. These limitations highlight the need for adjunctive maneuvers to improve procedural success.

Various techniques using a balloon have been described as potential solutions to these challenges (Table 1), as inflated balloon provides circumferential wall contact, generating shear forces that can fragment adherent thrombus, facilitate aspiration, and enhance local thrombolytic penetration [11-17]. El Tecle et al. [11] reported “venoplasty” (Fig. 3A) with a Scepter XC (Terumo; compliant) balloon for sinus thrombosis followed by intra-sinus tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) infusion. Matsuda et al. [12] initially described the “dental floss technique” (Fig. 3B), in which venous thrombectomy performed by moving an inflated Transform (Stryker; compliant) balloon back and forth over a microwire across occluded sinus and under continuous aspiration through ACE64 catheter (Penumbra) kept close to the balloon, achieving satisfactory recanalization in 3 acute cases. However, moving forward an inflated balloon over a microwire is technically challenging due to the flexible shaft of the balloon catheter, as well as a high risk of trauma to the bridging vein orifice, which may lead to hemorrhage. In another case by Matsuda et al. [13], where the dental floss technique failed, the “balloon anchor with mobile aspiration technique” (Fig. 3C) achieved partial recanalization, in which a Shouryu HR (Kaneka; compliant) balloon was anchored to the most anterior part of the SSS, and the Penumbra ACE catheter was advanced to the anterior SSS under continuous aspiration. Yang et al. [14] reported 23 patients where venous thrombectomy was performed using a partially inflated NC balloon slowly drawn back across the thrombosed sinus to the SS. The procedure was combined with intra-sinus Urokinase thrombolysis, achieving complete and partial recanalization in 81% and 19%, respectively. Kojima et al. [15] performed venous thrombectomy with a Transform balloon, initially trying to move back and forth (dental floss technique) along the posterior SSS; however, recanalization was not achieved due to gliding of the compliant balloon and ineffective pushing because of the flexible shaft of the balloon catheter. Hence, they modified the technique by advancing an EMBOVAC (Cerenovus) aspiration catheter until the proximal marker of the balloon. Subsequently, the balloon and aspiration catheter was slowly pulled from the anterior SSS to posterior SSS with continuous aspiration. Dhok et al. [16] achieved effective recanalization by sliding an inflated Copernic RC (Balt; compliant) balloon backward across the occluded SSS under continuous aspiration through an ACE64 catheter. Zhang et al. [17] reported complete recanalization in 7 out of 10 patients using “sliding balloon-assisted thrombectomy” by sliding an inflated NC balloon repeatedly back and forth in the thrombus segment along with aspiration of clots using a 60 mL syringe through an ACE60 catheter (Penumbra), followed by intra-sinus thrombolysis using Urokinase.

In 7 published case reports and case series (Table 1), a total of 42 patients underwent mechanical thrombectomy using a balloon out of which 32 (76%) achieved successful recanalization whereas 8 (19%) patients achieved partial recanalization and 2 (5%) patients had technical failure. Results of balloon assisted thrombectomy techniques are comparably higher than that of conventional techniques (complete recanalization in 39.8%, partial in 55.5%, and no recanalization in 4.7%) [18]. Additionally, Yang et al. [14] and Zhang et al. [17] used intra-sinus Urokinase, and El Tecle et al. [11] used intra-sinus tPA infusion, while no one used Tenecteplase for intra-sinus thrombolysis.

In our case, a “pipeline-pigging technique” (Fig. 3D) involved controlled dragging of a fully inflated (14 atm) angioplasty NC balloon from the mid SSS to the right SS under continuous aspiration through a CAT-7 placed in right jugular bulb. A 6-mm balloon was selected because it is slightly smaller than the reported transverse-SS diameter to avoid injury to the sinus while still large enough to generate shear force within the SSS and prevent a snow-plowing effect in the bridging veins. Inflation to a balloon nominal pressure of 14 atm was used to fully expand and stiffen the NC balloon. Our technique is different from other described techniques in following ways. 1) Most other operators used a compliant balloon that exerts minimal or no shear force on an organised thrombus. We used a NC balloon inflated up to 14 atm nominal pressure to make it absolute rigid to cause higher shear force to decimate the organised thrombus. 2) Other authors used a to-and-fro movement of the balloon (dental floss technique) over a short segment. We dragged the balloon all the way from the SSS to the SS twice. In our view, this technique creates more shear force on an organized thrombus, thus leading to better fragmentation effective suction and higher chances of complete recanalization. This stepwise approach, applied after conventional methods had failed, emphasizes its role as an effective and economical technical adjunct.

To our knowledge, intra-sinus Tenecteplase has not been described previously, making this the first reported case of intra-sinus instillation of Tenecteplase. During venous sinus thrombectomy, intra-sinus administration of Tenecteplase may help lyse macerated clots and facilitate clot fragmentation and detachment, thereby easing aspiration through the suction catheter.

Potential risks of pulmonary embolism, venous sinus wall injury, endothelial denudation, bridging vein avulsion, clot displacement into the contralateral transverse-SS, thrombus impaction at cortical or bridging vein orifices, and hemorrhagic conversion are associated with these venous sinus thrombectomy approaches. The shaft of the balloon catheter may also reduce the effective aspiration lumen of the CAT-7; however, the balloon shaft outer diameter was much smaller than the inner diameter of the aspiration catheter, leaving sufficient space for suction. Larger-bore suction catheters may further improve aspiration efficiency. Internal jugular vein occlusion or filter protection were not used in this case. Instead, continuous pump aspiration was maintained through the CAT-7 positioned in the ipsilateral jugular bulb during balloon dragging, and high pressure pump suction was also performed through the 7F Infinity long sheath once the CAT-7 and balloon were removed, which captured relatively large clots.

While this technique demonstrates a feasible method to achieve recanalization in extensive CVST refractory to conventional EVT, further experience and systematic evaluation are required to define its role within the endovascular armamentarium.

CONCLUSION

Balloon catheters are used in venous thrombectomy in different ways. Our technique of “pipeline-pigging” using an angioplasty NC balloon with simultaneous aspiration can serve as a valuable technical adjunct in extensive CVST refractory to conventional endovascular approaches. Intra-sinus thrombolysis using Tenecteplase appeared effective in this case and may provide an additional adjunctive. While further validation is required, this approach may expand the endovascular toolkit for managing extensive or refractory CVST.
