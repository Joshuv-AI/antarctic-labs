1.0 Summary
 Biological aerosol – airborne particles of biological origin such as bacteria, fungi, viruses, pollen, and
 spores – play significant roles in cloud formation, disease transmission, and biogeochemical cycling
 (Fröhlich-Nowoisky et al. 2016, Bryan et al. 2019, Zawadowicz et al. 2019). Despite the critical
 importance of characterizing these aerosol types, significant limitations persist in characterizing their
 critical physical properties (e.g., number concentration, size, and shape), which can be quite irregular and
 strongly dependent on particle type and source. At the U.S. Department of Energy Atmospheric Radiation
 Measurement (ARM) User Facility’s Bankhead National Forest (BNF) site in Alabama, where biological
 aerosol processes and their coupling with land-atmosphere interactions are prominent (Kuang et al. 2026),
 super-micron aerosol are being sampled in the Aerosol Observing System (AOS) (Uin et al. 2019)
 through a high-flow-rate inlet with a 50% transmission efficiency at approximately 6 μm
 (Kuang et al. 2024). This limits our ability to detect ambient super-micron biological aerosol such as
 pollen, which can be as large as 20-40 μm in diameter. Additionally, the AOS instrument that is being
 used to measure super-micron aerosol, the aerodynamic particle sizer (APS; TSI 2004), does not provide
 information on particle shape, resulting in uncertainty in retrieved particle size.

 To address these science drivers and overcome these sampling limitations, we proposed the Holographic
 Aerosol Monitor for Biological Aerosol Experiment (HAMBAEX), the objective of which is to
 characterize the seasonality in super-micron biological particles (e.g., pollen, fungal spores) at the ARM
 BNF site using the Holographic Coarse Mode Aerosol Monitor (HoloCMA; Bravo-Frank et al. 2024)
 developed by Particle4X, Inc. The initial deployment of the HoloCMA was intended to span a full year of
 observations starting in June 2025, providing real-time, in situ measurements of coarse-mode aerosol
 properties (e.g., number, size, shape, type) from roughly 3 to 1000 μm. We proposed HAMBAEX to
 occur in two phases:
  • Phase 1 (this initial deployment) would focus on a surface deployment of HoloCMA in the M1 main
 instrument field to evaluate instrument operation, data collection protocols, and
 preventative/corrective maintenance procedures.
  • Phase 2 (contingent on a successful Phase 1) would focus on a deployment along the height of the
 main S10 walk-up tower to characterize the biological aerosol population below, within, and above
 the forest canopy.

 From June 2025 to November 2025, the HoloCMA was deployed on the roof of the AOS container
 (Figure 1). In its initial instrument configuration (hereby referred to as HoloCMA v1.0), HoloCMA v1.0
 had a passive sampling (e.g., no forced flow), where the aerosol would passively “drift” into the sensing
 volume along with the ambient airflow. During this period, HoloCMA v1.0 was found to under-count
 significantly with respect to the AOS APS (Figure 2), which was unexpected due to the sampling losses
 that were known to occur during transmission through the AOS inlet stack before reaching the APS
 (Kuang et al. 2024). Subsequent laboratory investigations by the instrument manufacturer Particle 4X,
 Inc. revealed that HoloCMA with an active sampling inlet and pump (hereby referred to as HoloCMA
 v2.0) was essential to mitigate this under-counting (Figure 3). Based on these tests, Particle 4X, Inc.
 proceeded o complete fabrication and testing of HoloCMA v2.0, which was then installed at the ARM
 BNF in February 2026. From February to May 2026, there were several operational delays due to:

 1

 • Changing HoloCMA v2.0 sampling location from the AOS roof to the adjacent guest AOS roof to
 mitigate the effects of pump vibrations from the mini-AOS that was also installed on the AOS roof
 (which led to some HoloCMA measurement artifacts) while still sampling “close enough” to the AOS
 APS for meaningful intercomparison.
 • Troubleshooting network connectivity issues after the instrument move to the guest AOS roof.
 • Instrument down time due to seasonal severe weather.

Due to the improved coarse-mode aerosol sampling with HoloCMA v2.0 and the sampling
interruptions associated with installation and adverse environmental conditions, we request an
extension of HAMBAEX through December 2026.

2.0 Results
 The general data processing workflow follows three steps:
 1. Acquire digital holographic recording of imaged objects (e.g., suspended bacteria, dust, pollen).
 2. Apply deep learning model and automated focusing/filtering algorithms to holographic images to
 detect particles and derive their morphology.
 3. Generate particle analysis report from detected particles and derived morphologies, resulting in a time
 series of the number size distribution from roughly 3 to 1000 μm and the associated particle
 type/class.

 3

Particle 4X, Inc. developed an instrument dashboard for HAMBAEX that displays a time series of the
total number concentration with tagged periods of normal conditions and instrument down time due to
either challenging environmental conditions or system interruption, and an associated “library” of particle
images and statistics at each reported time-step (Figure 4).

Figure 4. Screenshot of HoloCMA v2.0 dashboard with a time series of the total number concentration,
 with identified periods of normal conditions and instrument down time, as well as an image
 “library” of particles measured at each time step.

Further research opportunities include:
 • Continued deployment of the HoloCMA v2.0 at the ARM-BNF to gather sufficient ambient
 data over different aerosol loadings, aerosol types, and meteorological conditions.
 • A laboratory intercomparison of the HoloCMA v2.0 with the AOS APS in which both instruments are
 challenged with calibration aerosol of defined size in the super-micron size range, defined shape (e.g.,
 spherical), and defined composition (e.g., polystyrene latex, NaCl, oil).
 • A laboratory intercomparison of HoloCMA v2.0 with both a wideband integrated bioaerosol sensor
 (WIBS; Healy et al. 2012) and a filter-based sampling method followed by offline analysis (e.g., at
 the Environmental Molecular Sciences Laboratory – EMSL) using biological aerosol (e.g., pollen,
 bacteria).
 • Deployment of the HoloCMA v2.0 on mobile platforms (e.g., tethered balloon system, uncrewed
 aerial system) to characterize the atmospheric vertical distribution of biological aerosol up to cloud
 base.
