Golgi-Pattern PDGFRA Immunoreactivity Correlates With PDGFRA Mutation Status in Gastric Epithelioid and Mixed-Type Gastrointestinal Stromal Tumors
Authors: Rola H. Ali, Maram Alwazan, Smiley George, Ahmad R. Alsaber, Mohammad Alkandari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs) with PDGFRA mutations represent a distinct subset with characteristic clinicopathological features and important therapeutic implications. The diagnostic performance of PDGFRA immunohistochemistry (IHC) as a phenotypic correlate of PDGFRA mutation status requires further evaluation. This study aimed to assess the diagnostic performance and reproducibility of PDGFRA IHC against PDGFRA mutation status as the reference standard. A total of 117 tumors were analyzed (19 PDGFRA-mutant, 40 non-PDGFRA, 49 GISTs of undetermined genotype, 9 non-GIST mimics). IHC for KIT, DOG1, CD34, and PDGFRA was performed on full sections and tissue microarrays, and 3 pathologists independently scored intensity, extent, and Golgi-pattern accentuation. PDGFRA-mutant GISTs were predominantly gastric with epithelioid or mixed morphology. Sixteen carried D842V, whereas 3 had exon 18 deletion or exon 12 mutations. KIT expression was reduced (47.4%) compared with non-PDGFRA-mutant GISTs (85%), while DOG1 remained consistently positive. Overall, PDGFRA expression was observed in 94.7% of PDGFRA-mutants compared with 32.5% of non-PDGFRA-mutants (P<0.001), and Golgi-pattern accentuation was present in 89.5% (17/19) of PDGFRA-mutants versus 7.5% (3/40) of molecularly confirmed non-PDGFRA-mutants (P<0.001). Among non-GIST mimics, only a monophasic synovial sarcoma showed diffuse PDGFRA positivity. Interobserver agreement for PDGFRA was substantial (κ=0.691). PDGFRA IHC showed 95% sensitivity and 78% specificity but did not distinguish D842V from non-D842V variants. PDGFRA IHC correlates with PDGFRA-mutant genotype when diffuse Golgi-type staining is observed in a gastric epithelioid or mixed tumor showing reduced KIT expression. However, therapeutic selection—particularly for avapritinib—requires precise detection of D842V variants, which mandates molecular sequencing.

Gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs), the most common mesenchymal neoplasms of the gastrointestinal tract, have served as a paradigm of precision oncology for over 2 decades. GISTs represent one of the earliest examples in which tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), notably imatinib mesylate, demonstrated efficacy against metastatic and unresectable disease, paving the way for molecularly targeted therapies.1

The mutational profile of GISTs shapes their biological behavior and predicts TKI responsiveness.2 Approximately 75% to 80% harbor KIT mutations,3,4 most often involving exon 11, while 5% to 10% have PDGFRA mutations.5 The remaining 10% to 15% are KIT/PDGFRA wild-type,6,7 and may instead carry alterations in succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) subunit genes,8 NF1,9 BRAF, or KRAS.10,11

PDGFRA-mutant GISTs form a distinct subset characterized by a marked gastric predilection and predominantly epithelioid or mixed morphology.12,13 Among these, the exon 18 p.D842V substitution comprises about two-thirds of cases and confers primary resistance to imatinib.14 Until recently, treatment options were limited, but avapritinib—a selective inhibitor of this mutation—is now approved for advanced disease.15 Accordingly, reliable detection of PDGFRA-mutant tumors has important therapeutic implications.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) remains central to diagnosis. CD117 (c-KIT) immunoreactivity is seen in ∼95% of GISTs,16 while DOG1, a calcium-dependent chloride channel, is positive in ∼98% of cases and retains specificity even in CD117-negative tumors.17,18 However, the significant morphologic overlap of PDGFRA-mutant GISTs with SDH-deficient tumors, KIT-mutant epithelioid variants, and other mesenchymal mimics highlights the need for dependable immunophenotypic correlates.

Although PDGFRA IHC has shown good sensitivity, its specificity remains variable.19–21 In this study, we systematically evaluate the diagnostic performance of PDGFRA immunostaining in a genetically characterized GIST cohort,22 aiming to replicate existing findings within our laboratory and to clarify its potential role as an adjunctive phenotypic indicator in settings where access to comprehensive molecular testing may be limited or delayed.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Case Selection

A retrospective search of the pathology archives at Kuwait Cancer Center identified 24 molecularly confirmed GISTs with PDGFRA mutations diagnosed over the past decade, of which 19 had available paraffin blocks. For comparison, 3 preconstructed tissue microarrays (TMAs) comprising 89 GISTs lacking PDGFRA mutations were included. Tumor samples in the TMAs were arrayed as 1.5 mm cores in duplicate or triplicate, selected from viable tumor areas by a sarcoma pathologist to avoid necrosis or hemorrhage. The TMA cohort included 33 KIT-mutant GISTs, 2 NF1-mutant GISTs, 5 KIT/PDGFRA/RAS wild-type GISTs, and 49 GISTs with undetermined mutational status. Molecular characterization was performed using the Oncomine Comprehensive Assay v3 (OCAv3; Thermo Fisher Scientific), a targeted NGS panel covering 161 cancer-related genes. DNA was extracted from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue using the Ion Torrent RecoverAll Kit, quantified by Qubit fluorometry, and assessed for amplifiability before library preparation. Sequencing was carried out on the Ion S5 system, and variant calling followed standard OCAv3 pipelines with internal quality thresholds (eg, ≥500×coverage and ≥5% allele frequency for SNVs).22 Nine intra-abdominal mesenchymal tumors mimicking GIST were included as negative controls; all were confirmed to lack KIT and PDGFRA mutations. These consisted of one gastric leiomyoma, one inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor, one inflammatory fibroid polyp, one plexiform fibromyxoma, one disseminated low-grade endometrial stromal sarcoma with epithelioid morphology involving the omentum, one monophasic synovial sarcoma involving small bowel/mesentery, one histiocytic sarcoma involving small bowel, and 2 perivascular epithelioid cell tumors (PEComas).

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed on 4 μm FFPE sections using the Leica BOND-MAX autostainer and BOND Polymer Refine Detection Kit (Leica Biosystems, Newcastle, UK). Full-tissue sections were used for PDGFRA-mutant GISTs to assess staining heterogeneity, whereas TMA sections were used for non-PDGFRA GISTs and non-GIST controls. Antibodies, clones, dilutions, and retrieval conditions are summarized in Table 1. Staining interpretation incorporated intensity (0=negative, 1=weak, 2=moderate, 3=strong), extent of positivity, and the presence of perinuclear Golgi-type accentuation. Negative staining was defined as the complete absence of tumor cell reactivity (intensity 0), while equivocal staining was defined as weak cytoplasmic reactivity (intensity 1), regardless of extent. Positive staining required moderate or strong reactivity (intensity 2 or 3) in ≥10% of tumor cells, with ≥10% to <50% classified as low-level expression and ≥50% as high-level expression. Internal controls (endothelial and stromal cells within the tissue section) and external control slides processed in parallel were evaluated to ensure staining adequacy and specificity. All cases were independently reviewed by 3 pathologists, with discrepancies resolved by consensus.

PDGFRA Immunohistochemical Optimization

A comprehensive optimization protocol was undertaken to ensure high sensitivity and specificity (Supplementary Table S1 and Figure S1, Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/PAI/A4). Three heat-induced epitope retrieval conditions were evaluated: Leica ER2 buffer (pH 9) for 20 minutes, ER2 (pH 9) for 30 minutes, and ER1 buffer (pH 6) for 20 minutes, in combination with primary antibody dilutions ranging from 1:200 to 1:10,000. Optimal staining was defined as strong cytoplasmic labeling in known PDGFRA-mutant GISTs with minimal background and absence of nonspecific staining in negative controls. Normal gastric tissue demonstrated scattered PDGFRA-positive stromal cells in the lamina propria and submucosa, serving as expected physiological background staining.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages) were used for categorical variables, while comparisons were performed using the Pearson χ2 test (significance threshold P<0.05). Sensitivity and specificity of PDGFRA IHC were calculated using PDGFRA mutation status as the reference standard. Interobserver agreement among the 3 pathologists was assessed using the unweighted Cohen κ coefficient: slight agreement (κ=0.01 to 0.20), fair (κ=0.21 to 0.40), moderate (κ=0.41 to 0.60), substantial (κ=0.61 to 0.80), and almost perfect (κ=0.81 to 1.00). Analyses were performed using JAMOVI v2.5.7.0.

RESULTS

Overall Pathologic Characteristics

A total of 117 cases were included: 108 GISTs and 9 non-GIST intra-abdominal mesenchymal tumors. Most GISTs were gastric (57/108, 52.7%), followed by small bowel (41/108, 38%) and other sites, including colorectal and mesenteric locations (10/108, 9.3%). Histologically, 13 (12%) were epithelioid, 33 (30.6%) mixed, and 62 (57.4%) spindle-cell. Genotypically, 19 were PDGFRA-mutant and 40 were non-PDGFRA-mutant (33 KIT-mutant, 2 NF1-mutant, 5 KIT/PDGFRA wild-type), while 49 lacked mutation analysis. Among the gastric GISTs, 36/57 were molecularly characterized (PDGFRA=18, KIT=15, wild-type=3). As summarized in Supplementary Figure S2, Supplemental Digital Content 2, https://links.lww.com/PAI/A5, PDGFRA mutations involved exon 18 (n=17; including 16 p.D842V and one p.I843_D846del) and exon 12 (n=2; p.V561D). KIT mutations were diverse, affecting exon 11 (n=28), exon 9 (n=2), exon 13 (n=2), and exon 8 (n=1), including deletions, SNVs, insertions, and indels.

PDGFRA-mutant GISTs demonstrated a striking predilection for the stomach (18/19 cases, 94.7%), with a single mesenteric tumor and none arising in the small intestine or colorectum (P=0.001). In contrast, non-PDGFRA and uncharacterized GISTs were more evenly distributed between the stomach and the small bowel. Histologically, PDGFRA-mutant tumors were either mixed (10/19, 52.6%) or epithelioid (9/19, 47.4%), showing discohesive, sclerosing, or hypercellular patterns as described by Miettinen et al,12 with no pure spindle-cell examples (P<0.001). Figure 1 illustrates a PDGFRA-mutant GIST following treatment with avapritinib, highlighting residual epithelioid tumor and characteristic PDGFRA positivity. Conversely, spindle-cell morphology predominated among non-PDGFRA (29/40, 72.5%) and uncharacterized tumors (33/49, 67.3%). Risk group distribution did not differ significantly by genotype; PDGFRA-mutant GISTs included 42.1% low-risk and 26.3% high-risk tumors.

Among PDGFRA-mutant GISTs, 9/19 (47.4%) were KIT positive, whereas 10/19 (52.6%) were negative or equivocal. Accordingly, KIT expression was significantly less frequent in PDGFRA-mutants compared with non-PDGFRA GISTs (34/40, 85%) and uncharacterized tumors (35/49, 71.4%) (P=0.01). Among KIT-expressing PDGFRA-mutant tumors, only 3 (15.8%) demonstrated high-level expression. In contrast, DOG1 was consistently expressed across all groups; all PDGFRA-mutant tumors were positive, and 89.5% (17/19) showed high-level expression. Therefore, combined KIT/DOG1 positivity was observed in 47.4% of PDGFRA-mutants, 85% of non-PDGFRA, and 71.4% of uncharacterized tumors (P=0.01). CD34 expression showed no significant differences between groups: 84.2% (16/19) of PDGFRA-mutant tumors were positive, including 47.4% (9/19) with high-level expression (Table 2).

Diagnostic Performance of PDGFRA Immunohistochemistry

The optimized conditions for PDGFRA IHC using clone D13C6 consisted of HIER with ER2 buffer (pH 9) for 20 minutes, a 1:8000 primary antibody dilution, and a 15-minute incubation, achieving moderate to strong cytoplasmic staining with minimal background. Gastric mucosa and submucosal stromal cells showed expected physiological PDGFRA reactivity. PDGFRA IHC correlated strongly with mutation status: 18/19 (94.7%) PDGFRA-mutant tumors were positive, compared with 13/40 (32.5%) non-PDGFRA-mutant GISTs and 7/49 (14.3%) uncharacterized tumors (P <0.001). Specifically, high-level PDGFRA expression occurred in 84.2% (16/19) of PDGFRA-mutants but only 25% (10/40) of non-PDGFRA tumors (P <0.001). Golgi-type perinuclear accentuation was strikingly enriched in PDGFRA-mutant GISTs (17/19, 89.5%) and rare in other groups (P <0.001), as illustrated in Figures 2 and 3.

PDGFRA expression was significantly associated with histologic subtype (P<0.001). When stratified by morphology irrespective of genotype, PDGFRA positivity was most frequent in epithelioid tumors (10/13, 76.9%; all with Golgi-type staining), followed by mixed (18/33, 54.5%) and spindle-cell tumors (10/62, 16.1%). Golgi-pattern staining was distinctly uncommon in spindle-cell GISTs (4.8%). Conversely, dual KIT/DOG1 expression was present in only 15.4% of epithelioid tumors, compared with 66.7% of mixed and 87.1% of spindle-cell tumors, highlighting the complementary value of PDGFRA in KIT/DOG1-reduced epithelioid GISTs.

PDGFRA immunoreactivity was observed in 13/40 (32.5%) molecularly confirmed non-PDGFRA GISTs (ranging from low to high levels), comprising 9/33 (27.3%) KIT-mutant and 4/5 (80%) wild-type tumors. In contrast to PDGFRA-mutants, Golgi-type accentuation was infrequent, present in only 3/40 (7.5%) non-PDGFRA GISTs—1/33 KIT-mutant and 2/5 wild-type tumors—and these cases retained KIT and DOG1 expression. An additional 7/49 (14.3%) tumors with initially uncharacterized genotype demonstrated PDGFRA expression. Prospective molecular testing was subsequently performed in 6 of these cases, identifying PDGFRA mutations in 4 (66.7%), further supporting the role of PDGFRA IHC in raising suspicion for underlying PDGFRA mutations and prompting further molecular evaluation. Among non-GIST mesenchymal mimics, only 1/9 (11.1%)—a monophasic synovial sarcoma—showed diffuse PDGFRA positivity (Fig. 4).

Interobserver Variation

Interobserver concordance for IHC interpretation was mostly substantial. Kappa values were 0.741 for KIT, 0.505 for DOG1, 0.725 for CD34, and 0.691 for PDGFRA. Complete agreement was achieved in 79% (85/108) of KIT, 82% (89/108) of DOG1, 75% (81/108) of CD34, and 80% (86/108) of PDGFRA evaluations, supporting good reproducibility across observers. On the basis of consensus scoring, PDGFRA IHC demonstrated a sensitivity of 95% and a specificity of 78% relative to PDGFRA mutation status.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we comprehensively evaluated the immunophenotypic features of PDGFRA-mutant GISTs in comparison to non-PDGFRA-mutant tumors, leveraging both full-section and tissue microarray–based immunohistochemistry in a genetically characterized cohort. We found that PDGFRA IHC demonstrated high sensitivity (95%) and moderate specificity (78%) for PDGFRA-mutant GISTs, with the strongest diagnostic association observed when diffuse PDGFRA expression was accompanied by Golgi-type perinuclear accentuation in the appropriate pathologic context. Although molecular testing remains the gold standard for definitive mutation identification, these findings address a practical diagnostic challenge especially encountered in settings where access to next-generation sequencing may be limited, delayed, or constrained by tissue availability. Adding PDGFRA immunostaining to the GIST IHC panel may function as an adjunctive phenotypic indicator that raises suspicion for an underlying PDGFRA mutation and prompts timely molecular confirmation and clinical planning.

GISTs were among the first neoplasms in which treatment efficacy was closely linked to the underlying molecular alteration. Therapeutic decisions are guided not only by the mutated gene but also by the specific exon and, in some cases, the precise mutation involved.23,24 A prime example is the PDGFRA exon 18 p.D842V substitution, which carries distinct clinical implications. The D842V variant accounts for ∼75% of PDGFRA mutations in GISTs.25 It is well recognized for conferring primary resistance to imatinib, with patients showing low response rates and short progression-free survival.26 Avapritinib, a selective inhibitor targeting PDGFRA D842V, is now established as first-line therapy for unresectable or metastatic GISTs harboring this variant.27–29 The current cohort included a case of D842V-mutant gastric GIST that initially presented with advanced disease and progressed despite sequential treatment with imatinib followed by sunitinib. Subsequent therapy with avapritinib achieved a sustained partial response, ultimately allowing surgical resection of a tumor previously considered inoperable. Non-D842V PDGFRA mutations are generally imatinib-sensitive, though sensitivity varies among specific variants.14,30,31

As anticipated, PDGFRA-mutant GISTs demonstrated a marked gastric predilection (94.7%) and predominantly epithelioid or mixed morphology, with no pure spindle-cell examples.12,13 These tumors frequently showed reduced or absent KIT expression, consistent with the literature,32 while DOG1 remained uniformly positive; no KIT/DOG1 double-negative cases were observed, likely reflecting limited sample size. CD34 expression did not differ significantly by genotype. PDGFRA IHC correlated strongly with mutation status, with the highest diagnostic specificity observed when diffuse staining was accompanied by Golgi-type perinuclear accentuation in gastric epithelioid or mixed tumors with reduced KIT expression. Prior studies have reported high sensitivity of PDGFRA IHC using both polyclonal and monoclonal antibodies, most applying a ≥50% threshold for strong positivity,20,21,33 with several underscoring the diagnostic relevance of a dot-like/Golgi pattern.19,20,33,34 This pattern likely reflects retention of constitutively activated mutant receptors within the endoplasmic reticulum/Golgi apparatus, impairing membrane trafficking.35 In contrast, Golgi-type staining was rarely observed in non-PDGFRA-mutant GISTs in our series, which retained strong KIT and DOG1 expression, thereby reducing interpretive ambiguity. Interobserver reproducibility was substantial, supporting the reproducibility of the assessment. Collectively, these findings align with prior literature and support incorporation of PDGFRA staining into diagnostic workflows, provided that rigorous assay validation is performed and interpretive pitfalls are recognized (Fig. 5). This approach may be particularly helpful in the evaluation of KIT/DOG1 double-negative tumors and in cases with retained SDHB expression, thereby excluding SDH-deficient GIST, an important differential diagnosis in this morphologic setting.19,21 Importantly, PDGFRA IHC cannot substitute for genotyping and should be interpreted as a phenotypic correlate rather than a definitive indicator of mutation status, as it does not distinguish D842V from non-D842V variants.

Among 9 intra-abdominal non-GIST mimics, 8 were negative and only one—a monophasic synovial sarcoma—showed diffuse PDGFRA immunoreactivity, notably without Golgi-type staining. These observations are concordant with Papke et al,21 who documented PDGFRA expression in a subset of mesenchymal tumors, including synovial sarcoma, inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor, and plexiform fibromyxoma—entities typically distinguishable from PDGFRA-mutant GISTs based on morphology and broader immunophenotype. Earlier work by Peterson et al33 likewise identified PDGFRA positivity in a minority of non-GIST intra-abdominal mesenchymal tumors, generally with less extensive staining, and concluded that strong PDGFRA expression may be useful in this setting, albeit with limited sensitivity. These data underscore the importance of interpreting PDGFRA immunostaining within the full morphologic and immunophenotypic context, with molecular testing serving as the gold standard for definitive mutation identification and avoidance of diagnostic misclassification.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. The cohort size is modest and predominantly composed of GISTs, with limited representation of non-GIST mesenchymal mimics, and additional tumor types known to express PDGFRA were not comprehensively evaluated. Our findings emphasize that while PDGFRA IHC correlates with PDGFRA-mutant genotype within this cohort and may raise suspicion for mutation in appropriate morphologic settings, it must be interpreted in conjunction with histologic features and KIT/DOG1 staining. Molecular testing remains essential for definitive mutation identification and therapeutic decision-making. PDGFRA IHC may be particularly valuable in resource-limited settings, where access to NGS testing may be constrained.
