Tooth loss as a factor associated with deterioration of quality and increase in sleep duration: analysis of the 2016–2017 Chilean National Health Survey
Authors: Nicolás Portalier Dublé, Andrés Celis Sersen, Jorge Celis-Dooner, Dino Sepúlveda Viveros, Carlos Custodio Villanueva, Juan Pablo Vargas Buratovic, María Inés González Agurto, Nicolás Monsalves Morales, Mario Soto Marchant, Duniel Ortuño Borroto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTooth loss has been associated with various systemic diseases, but its relationship with sleep disturbances remains understudied. This study aimed to evaluate the association between tooth loss and sleep quantity and quality parameters in Chilean adults.MethodsThis cross-sectional analysis utilised data from the 2016–2017 Chilean National Health Survey (n = 5,009). Tooth loss was categorized as functional dentition (≥ 20 teeth), moderate tooth loss (10–19 teeth), severe tooth loss (1–9 teeth), and edentulism (0 teeth). Sleep quantity was classified as insufficient (< 7 h), adequate (7–8 h), or excessive (> 8 h). Sleep quality was assessed through four questions addressing snoring frequency, breathing pauses, daytime sleepiness, and morning tiredness. Multinomial logistic regression models estimated odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI), adjusted for sociodemographic, lifestyle, and health-related covariates.ResultsThe association between edentulism and excessive sleep duration was significant in unadjusted models (OR 1.94, 95% CI 1.48–2.56) but attenuated after full adjustment (OR 1.35, 95% CI 0.94–1.94). Edentulous individuals showed higher odds of snoring (OR 1.69, 95% CI 1.29–2.20) and daytime sleepiness (OR 1.58, 95% CI 1.15–2.17) compared to those with functional dentition. Also, severe tooth loss was associated with increased snoring (OR 1.39, 95% CI 1.10–1.75) and daytime difficulty staying awake (OR 1.28, 95% CI 1.02–1.61).ConclusionsThe findings reveal that individuals with severe tooth loss and edentulism are more likely to experience excessive sleep, snoring, and daytime sleepiness, while having a lower likelihood of deficient sleep.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-08776-8.Keywords: Tooth loss, Edentulous, Sleep, Oral health, Chile, NHS, Cross-sectional studies

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization, untreated dental caries and periodontitis are recognized as the most prevalent chronic non-communicable diseases worldwide, representing a major public health problem [1]. In 2021, approximately 3.5 billion people (42% of the global population) had some form of untreated oral condition [2].

Among the primary consequences of untreated oral diseases, tooth loss is particularly notable because it negatively impacts stomatognathic functions, such as swallowing and phonation [3, 4], as well as health-related quality of life, self-esteem, and mental health [5–7]. Additionally, numerous clinical and epidemiological studies have demonstrated that tooth loss is linked to various systemic diseases, including diabetes, hypertension, and rheumatoid arthritis, underscoring a complex interplay between oral health and overall health [8–10]. This association is biologically plausible, primarily through chronic periodontal inflammation, which induces a systemic inflammatory response implicated in these conditions. Furthermore, oral pathogens may disseminate and modulate the immune response, contributing to disease processes. However, tooth loss itself may act more as a marker of cumulative oral disease and shared risk factors rather than a direct causal factor [11]. In this context, an association between tooth loss and sleep disorders (SD) has also been described [12, 13]. SD is a group of conditions that affect sleep quality, duration, and continuity, including insomnia, hypersomnia, and obstructive sleep apnea-hypopnea syndrome (OSAHS), among others. These conditions have a significant impact on both physical and mental health [14, 15].

Like oral diseases, SD represent a public health problem due to their high prevalence. Although estimates vary depending on study design and population, it is estimated that around one billion adults suffer from OSAHS. This disorder is more common among older adults, in whom a prevalence of 46% has been reported, also associated with other problems such as poor sleep quality (40%), insomnia (29%), and excessive daytime sleepiness (19%) [16, 17].

Although the scientific literature has addressed different aspects of the relationship between oral health and sleep in various populations, a knowledge gap remains regarding this association in Chile. The Chilean National Health Survey (Chile NHS), conducted by the Ministry of Health (MINSAL) approximately every six years, is a representative tool of the Chilean population aged 15 years and older. This survey includes oral health and sleep modules, enabling an analysis of the relationships between these areas in the national population [18]. Therefore, this study aimed to analyze the association between tooth loss and sleep quantity and quality in the Chilean population., using a national representative dataset.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies. The analysis is based on data from the 2016–2017 Chilean National Health Survey (NHS) [19], an epidemiological surveillance instrument that evaluates 72 health problems and their determinants, with emphasis on non-communicable diseases. The methodological design of the NHS corresponds to an analytical cross-sectional study using secondary data, employing a random, stratified, multistage, household cluster sampling strategy, which ensures representativeness at the national, regional, and urban-rural levels in Chilean adults aged 15 years and older. The raw data from the original survey include 6,233 participants who completed the initial interview, of whom 5,520 received a home evaluation by nursing staff, 5,451 underwent laboratory tests, and 5,306 had an oral health assessment. Further details on the original survey can be found at: https://epi.minsal.cl/resultados-encuestas/.

For the purposes of this study, participants aged 15 years and older were included, and the final analytical sample was derived after applying the exclusion criteria detailed in Fig. 1.

Assessment of tooth loss

The evaluation of remaining teeth was conducted during NHS 2016–17 home visits, performed by trained nurses using a dental mirror, dental explorer, and standard operating light as part of an oral examination. Training was provided by nine dentists from the Chilean Ministry of Health and included theoretical components, practical demonstrations, clinical practice sessions, and a final 55-question assessment. Nurses achieved a mean score of 49.95 ± 2.74, with an inter-examiner kappa coefficient of 0.85 (p < 0.01) [20]. For analysis, the number of remaining teeth was categorized into functional dentition (20 or more teeth), moderate tooth loss (10–19 teeth), severe tooth loss (1–9 teeth), and edentulism (complete absence of teeth).

Assessment of sleep quantity and quality

The 2016–17 NHS sleep assessment considered two main aspects: the quantity and quality of sleep. This version of the NHS aimed to broaden sleep evaluation compared to previous editions. Sleep quality was measured using four specific questions included in the NHS 2016–17 questionnaire: “Have you been told that you snore every night or almost every night?”, “Have you been told that when you sleep, you stop breathing at times?”, “Do you find it difficult to stay awake during the day at least three days per week?”, and “Do you wake up feeling tired, or almost as tired as before sleeping, at least three days per week?” All questions offered “Yes” or “No” response options, with “No” used as the reference category in statistical analyses. These questions were selected from a total of 10 sleep-related items included in this version of the NHS.

Sleep quantity was evaluated based on the average daily sleep hours self-reported by participants, grouped into three categories according to current recommendations: insufficient sleep (< 7 h), adequate sleep (7–8 h, serving as the reference category), and excessive sleep (> 8 h). This data was collected via the standardized Chile NHS 2016–17 questionnaire, in which participants indicated their typical sleep duration over a 24-hour period.

Covariates

The theoretical framework describing the relationship between tooth loss and changes in both sleep quality and quantity was structured using a directed acyclic graph (DAGitty version 3.0), as illustrated in Fig. 2. The analysis included sociodemographic and health-related covariates. Sociodemographic variables encompassed: age (dichotomized into < 65 and ≥ 65 years), sex (male/female), and education level (measured as years of formal education, categorized as < 8 years, 8–12 years, and > 12 years). Lifestyle and behavioral variables included: smoking status (classified as daily smoker—one or more cigarettes per day, occasional smoker, less than one cigarette per day, former smoker, and never smoker) and alcohol consumption frequency over the past year (categorized as never, once or less per month, 2–4 times per month, 2–3 times per week, and four or more times per week). Health-related variables included: body mass index (BMI, categorized according to WHO cut-off points as underweight (< 18.5), normal weight (18.5–24.9), overweight (25–29.9), and obesity (≥ 30 kg/m²), self-reported history of diabetes (yes/no), and physician-diagnosed depression (yes/no). All these variables were gathered through the standardized Chile NHS 2016–17 questionnaire [18].

Statistical analysis

We performed descriptive analysis to summarize participant characteristics according to daily sleep hours using frequencies, given the categorical nature of the included variables. To analyze the association between tooth loss and sleep duration, multinomial logistic regression models were used, while logistic regression models were applied for sleep quality. In both cases, odds ratios and their 95% confidence intervals were estimated, adjusting by age, sex, educational level, smoking status, self-reported history of diabetes, self-reported history of depression, and alcohol consumption in the past year. Participants lacking information on the variables of interest were excluded (Fig. 1). All analyses were conducted using Stata MP version 17 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Ethics

The data used in this study comes from NHS 2016–17, which is publicly available and fully anonymized. All participants in the original study provided informed consent before their participation. The present analysis was approved by the Scientific Ethics Committee of Universidad de Los Andes under code CEC2025011. The database used and its analysis were conducted in strict compliance with the ethical principles established in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Table 1 shows the characteristics of the study participants. A total of 5,009 individuals were included, consisting of 63.1% women and 23.0% who were over 65 years old. Overall, 64.6% of individuals had at least 20 teeth, while 8.1% were edentulous. Among those with insufficient sleep, 5.1% were edentulous, whereas in the group with excessive sleep (> 8 h), edentulism prevalence was 13.3%. Regarding sleep quality, 55.4% reported snoring every night or almost every night. For the question “Have you been told that when you sleep, you stop breathing at times?”, 18.3% responded affirmatively. Additionally, 18.9% reported difficulty staying awake during the day at least three days a week. Finally, 34.7% of participants reported waking up feeling tired or nearly as exhausted as before sleeping, at least three days per week.

Table 2 shows the association between tooth loss and sleep duration. In edentulous individuals, compared to those with 20 or more teeth, the crude model showed an odds ratio (OR) of 1.94 (1.48–2.56) for excessive sleep. This association remained across all four models; however, in the fully adjusted model, it was not statistically significant, with an OR of 1.35 (0.94–1.94) for the total sample. Regarding insufficient sleep duration, all ORs across the models were below 1, suggesting no increased odds compared to those with adequate sleep duration (7–8 h per day. For individuals aged 65 or older, edentulous participants, compared to those with functional dentition, had an OR of 2.08 (1.25–3.46) for excessive sleep duration in the unadjusted model. This decreased to 1.28 (0.73–2.26) in the fully adjusted model, which was not statistically significant. Throughout both the total sample and the subgroup of those aged 65 or older, a gradient association was observed across the four models. Among those with 10–19 teeth, the OR for excessive sleep duration was 0.92 (0.69–1.23); among those with 1–9 teeth, the OR increased to 1.21 (0.87–1.67); and finally, among edentulous subjects, the OR was 1.35 (0.94–1.94).

In subjects aged 65 years or older, the OR for excessive sleep duration was 0.80 (0.45–1.43) for those with 10–19 teeth, 1.10 (0.63–1.92) for those with 1–9 teeth, and 1.28 (0.73–2.26) for those who were edentulous. None of these values was statistically significant.

Table 3 shows the association between the number of teeth and four sleep quality parameters. Individuals with edentulism or severe tooth loss were more likely to snore every night or almost every night, with ORs of 1.69 (1.29–2.20) and 1.39 (1.10–1.75), respectively. Additionally, edentulous individuals were 1.58 (1.15–2.17) times more likely to report difficulty staying awake during the day at least three times per week compared to those with at least 20 teeth. When comparing individuals with 1–9 teeth and those with 10–19 teeth to those with ≥ 20 teeth, the ORs were 1.25 (0.94–1.66) and 1.28 (1.02–1.61), respectively. Lastly, in the fully adjusted models for the questions “Have you been told that when you sleep you stop breathing at times?” and “Do you wake up feeling tired?”, no OR values above 1.0 were observed, and no statistically significant associations were found.

Discussion

This study revealed a significant association between tooth loss and alterations in sleep parameters among Chilean adults. In the general population, having between 1 and 9 teeth or presenting total edentulism was associated with a higher odd of excessive sleep in both crude models and those adjusted for some covariates; however, this association disappeared after controlling for all covariates. In contrast, edentulism was consistently associated to a lower odd of poor sleep. Among individuals under 65 years of age, tooth loss was associated with a higher odd of excessive sleep only in the model adjusted for age and sex. In contrast, edentulism showed a negative association with insufficient sleep hours. In those over 65 years, edentulism showed a higher odd of excessive sleep only in the crude model and a lower odd of poor sleep when adjusted for age and sex. In addition, tooth loss was associated with a higher odd of snoring and daytime sleepiness.

Excessive sleep is a major health concern due to its functional and clinical implications. Numerous studies have linked hypersomnia to a higher odd of metabolic disorders, such as obesity and type 2 diabetes, along with cardiovascular diseases and cognitive decline [21–23]. It is also strongly linked with psychiatric conditions like depression and anxiety, as well as neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson’s and Alzheimer’s [24, 25]. Importantly, long sleep duration has been identified as an independent odd factor for all-cause mortality, indicating that it may not only be a sign of underlying health problems but also a clinical marker of overall vulnerability, requiring evaluation and monitoring [26].

The observed association between tooth loss and sleep may be explained by two main mechanisms. First, the anatomical changes that occur following tooth loss, such as the decrease in vertical dimension, the upward rotation of the mandible, and the relative increase in tongue size, lead to a reduction in the retropharyngeal space, narrowing the upper airway. This may increase the likelihood of snoring and sleep apnea, decreases oxygenation during sleep, and causes micro-arousals, ultimately disrupting both sleep quality and structure [6]. Second, there is a strong connection between mental health and tooth loss. The latter has been linked to a higher odd of depression, which is also associated with altered sleep patterns [27, 28]. Additionally, tooth loss can negatively impact self-esteem and confidence, especially when it involves the front teeth, resulting in increased anxiety levels that can reduce sleep efficiency [27, 29].

The available literature to date suggests a relationship between the number of teeth and altered sleep patterns, although there is no consensus on the direction of this association. The work of Shihoko Koyama et al. highlighted this connection in a multivariable model involving patients over 65 years, describing a U-shaped relationship where having between 1 and 9 teeth was associated with a higher odd of sleeping less than 4 h (the lowest category), without representing a odd for a duration of 5 to 7 h, and with a persistent increase in odd when sleeping between 8 and more than 10 h [30]. A similar U-shaped association was described by Kyungdo Han and Jun-Beom Park in patients with fewer than 25 teeth in Korea [31]. Meanwhile, the results of Mohammad S. Al-Zahrani et al. show a higher odd of poor sleep (< 7 h) in people with fewer teeth, both in crude models and in models adjusted for covariates. They also describe a higher odd of excessive sleep (> 7 h), but only in the crude model, a relationship that disappears after adjusting for covariates, which contrasts in some cases with the findings of the present study. This group also provides innovative results by analyzing the relationship between sleep duration and tooth loss in anterior and posterior sectors, finding no significant differences between the two groups regarding poor or excessive sleep [32]. Finally, the group of Manyi Liu et al. focused on the association between periodontitis and sleep patterns measured in hours, once again describing a U-shaped pattern in which advanced stages of the disease were associated with altered sleep patterns, both in excess and in deficit. In contrast, early stages were linked to more preserved patterns [12].

Overall, these findings consistently support a non-linear, U-shaped association between tooth loss and sleep duration, where both short and long sleep are associated with poorer oral health outcomes. Short sleep duration has been associated with increased systemic inflammation and immune dysregulation, including elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and interleukin-1β (IL-1β), which may contribute to periodontal tissue breakdown and alveolar bone resorption [12, 30–32]. Additionally, sleep deprivation may impair host immune responses and disrupt the balance of the oral microbiota. In contrast, long sleep duration has been associated with underlying comorbidities, poorer general health status, and adverse socioeconomic conditions, which may indirectly influence oral health [30–32]. However, despite these proposed mechanisms, current evidence remains insufficient to explain why this relationship follows a U-shaped pattern rather than a linear one.

The differences observed across studies may be attributed to the characteristics of the populations examined and the variability in how variables are categorized. Nevertheless, the U-shaped relationship is consistently noted, where having fewer teeth is linked to irregular sleep patterns, including both excessive and insufficient sleep. It’s important to highlight that a common limitation of this type of research is that sleep duration is self-reported by participants rather than measured with polysomnography, which is considered the gold standard for evaluating sleep patterns. However, polysomnography is costly and challenging to implement in large epidemiological studies. Although this study shares that limitation, it provides an innovative perspective by not only examining the relationship between the number of teeth and sleep duration but also exploring sleep quality through questions about nocturnal breathing patterns and daytime sleepiness—areas with limited evidence in large-scale epidemiological research. The work by Anne E. Sanders et al. in the United States investigates this relationship, noting that for each tooth lost, the prevalence of high odd for OSAHS increases by 2%, opening avenues for further research [32]. While the questions about breathing patterns and sleepiness in this study carry the same potential bias as those measuring sleep duration—due to the absence of polysomnography, their findings represent a promising first step toward understanding the link between oral health and sleep hygiene, which deserves further investigation.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, its cross-sectional design precludes establishing causal relationships between tooth loss and sleep alterations. Second, key variables, including sleep duration, sleep quality, and some oral health measures, were self-reported, which may introduce recall and reporting biases. Additionally, although multiple covariates were included in the adjusted models, residual confounding from unmeasured factors cannot be ruled out.

Conclusion

This study identified a significant relationship between tooth loss and alterations in sleep patterns among the Chilean population, with notable differences based on age group and severity of tooth loss. The findings indicate that individuals with severe tooth loss and edentulism had higher odds of excessive sleep, snoring, and daytime sleepiness, while showing lower odds of poor sleep. These results highlight the need for an interdisciplinary approach that integrates oral health and sleep disorder management in clinical practice, promoting better collaboration among dentists, sleep doctors, and other healthcare professionals.
