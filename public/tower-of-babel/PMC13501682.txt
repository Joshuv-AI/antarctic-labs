HAM1 negatively regulates drought tolerance in Brassica juncea
Authors: Yiqing Meng, Zhuting Liu, Jianeng Che, Zhangping Li, Xiaoxiao Guan, Yimei Zhou, Yongming Bo, Xiaolong Lyu, Zhongyuan Hu, Jinghua Yang, Mingfang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDrought stress poses a severe and growing threat to global crop production due to climate change. Brassica juncea is an important vegetable and oil crop with high potential for cultivation in arid and semi-arid regions, yet its genetic mechanisms underlying drought resilience remain poorly understood. Here, we evaluated the phenotypes of geographically diverse B. juncea accessions under drought stress and identified a candidate gene BjuB.HAM1 via genome-wide association study (GWAS) approach, a plant-specific GRAS-family transcription factor, as a key negative regulator of drought tolerance. We discovered two structural variations (SVs) within BjuB.HAM1 that significantly impair drought tolerance in B. juncea. Using heterologous expression of BjuB.HAM1 in Arabidopsis thaliana, combined with downstream gene validation, we demonstrated that this gene inhibits the expression of AtUGT76C2, a cytokinin glycosyltransferase, and reduces drought tolerance by binding to its GT cis-elements in the promoter. Collectively, these findings uncover a new insight into the functional gene module of drought resistance, and provide valuable genetic targets for drought resistance breeding in Brassica juncea.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09267-2.Keywords: Drought tolerance, Brassica juncea, GWAS, HAM1, Negative regulator

Background

Anthropogenic activities are driving global climate change, leading to an increased frequency and severity of drought stress. This abiotic stress has become a major constraint on crop productivity and a significant threat to global food security [1]. Under drought conditions, plants suffer from physiological and molecular disturbances, including inhibition of photosynthesis, oxidative damage, and cellular dehydration, ultimately compromising growth and yield [2, 3]. To survive, plants have evolved intricate regulatory networks [4]. Among these, transcriptional reprogramming, orchestrated by transcription factors (TFs), serves as a central mechanism for adapting to abiotic stress [5]. TFs are DNA-binding proteins that modulate gene expression by interacting with pre-initiation complexes and binding in a sequence-specific manner to cis-regulatory elements within promoter regions. This interaction activates or represses RNA polymerase activity, thereby reprogramming gene expression profiles [5].

The plant-specific GRAS family of TFs, named after its founding members GAI, RGA, and SCR, plays diverse roles in development and stress responses [6]. Structurally, GRAS proteins feature a variable N-terminal region, which may contain homomeric repeats of specific amino acids and intrinsically disordered regions, along with a conserved C-terminal GRAS domain. The latter comprises five distinct motifs: leucine heptad repeat I (LHRI), leucine heptad repeat II (LHRII), VHIID, PFYRE, and SAW [6]. GRAS TFs participate in diverse physiological processes and contribute significantly to abiotic stress tolerance, including drought [6]. The HAIRY MERISTEM (HAM) subfamily is a conserved clade within the GRAS family, also referred to as the Lost MERISTEM (LOM) family, constitutes a subclade of GRAS TFs [7, 8]. HAM proteins exhibit functional conservation and redundancy, and their expansion in various plant lineages suggests the acquisition of novel roles or subfunctionalization [9]. This family often implicated in shoot meristem maintenance, chlorophyll biosynthesis and apical growth [10–12]. Additionally, HAM subfamily members are post-transcriptionally regulated by miRNAs and have been implicated in drought stress responses, branching architecture, heading time, leaf morphogenesis, and disease resistance [10, 11, 13–15]. However, the precise molecular function and regulatory targets of HAM TFs in modulating drought tolerance, especially in crops, remain largely unexplored, limiting their application in breeding.

Phytohormones are key integrators of drought stress signals. Cytokinins (CKs) exhibit complex, context-dependent roles in drought adaptation, often antagonizing abscisic acid (ABA) [16, 17]. Homeostasis of active CKs is partly maintained through glycosylation by enzymes such as UGT76C2 [18]. In A. thaliana, UGT76C2 expression positively correlates with drought tolerance, suggesting that manipulating CK metabolism via transcriptional regulation is a viable strategy for enhancing resilience [19].

Brassica juncea, an allotetraploid oilseed and vegetable crop derived from natural hybridization between B. rapa and B. nigra, is valued for its broad adaptability [20, 21]. Its cultivation in arid and semi-arid regions holds significant promise, yet its susceptibility to drought poses a major challenge [22–24] While natural variation for drought tolerance exists within its germplasm, the underlying genetic basis, particularly the role of key transcriptional regulators, is poorly understood. Identifying such regulators and their downstream targets is crucial for the molecular breeding of drought-resilient mustard varieties.

In this study, we aimed to dissect the genetic architecture of drought tolerance in B. juncea and characterize a key transcriptional regulator. We evaluated a diverse natural population under PEG-induced drought stress and employed genome-wide association study (GWAS) to identify candidate genes. Our findings highlight BjuB.HAM1, a gene encoding a HAM subfamily transcription factor, as a negative regulator of drought tolerance. Further investigation revealed that HAM1 represses the expression of UGT76C2, a cytokinin glycosyltransferase, by binding to GT cis-elements in its promoter. This work unveils a novel transcriptional regulatory module, BjuB.HAM1-UGT76C2, that fine-tunes cytokinin homeostasis to modulate drought response in B. juncea.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

A total of 135 B. juncea accessions were used in this study, the detailed information on all these accessions is listed in Table S1. All the above accessions were collected and self-maintained in our laboratory at Zhejiang University. Resequencing data for these accessions were obtained from a previously published study and have been deposited in the National Genomics Data Center under the accession number PRJNA696589 [25]. The T-DNA insertion mutant of A. thaliana (atham1 and atugt76c2) was obtained from the Arashare Technology Service Center.

To generate transgenic complementation and overexpression lines in (A) thaliana, the full-length coding sequence (CDS) of BjuB.HAM1 was amplified from the drought-sensitive accession R109 and cloned into the pMDC83 vector to create a C-terminal GFP fusion under the control of the CaMV 35 S promoter. The construct was introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 and subsequently transformed into the atham1 mutant (for complementation) and wild-type Col-0 (for overexpression) via the floral dip method.All plants were grown in a greenhouse at Zhejiang University, Hangzhou, China. The (B) juncea natural population was cultivated under a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod at 22 °C. A. thaliana lines (wild-type Col-0, atham1 mutant, complementation, and overexpression lines) were grown under a 10 h light/14 h dark photoperiod at 22 °C. For phenotypic assays, plants were arranged in pots (7 cm × 7 cm) in a completely randomized design. Pots were rearranged daily within trays to minimize microenvironmental effects.

Drought stress treatment and phenotypic evaluation

The drought tolerance assay was conducted in a greenhouse maintained at a constant temperature of 22 °C with a 14 h dark/10 h light photoperiod. Drought stress was applied to one-month-old plants by irrigating trays with a 10% (w/v) polyethylene glycol 6000 (PEG-6000) solution. Each B. juncea variety had at least 3 biological replicates. Prior to treatment, plants were well-watered and allowed to acclimatize for 48 h. The maximum quantum efficiency of photosystem II (Fv/Fm) of the second true leaf was measured as a baseline (0 h) using a Pocket PEA chlorophyll fluorimeter (Hansatech Instruments) after 30 min of dark adaptation. During the treatment, trays were irrigated with 2 L of 10% PEG-6000 every 2 days. Fv/Fm was remeasured at 3 days after treatment.

Leaf damage was assessed visually using a Damage Grade (DG) scale (1 to 6) based on the percentage of leaf area exhibiting chlorosis or desiccation as shown in Table 1 and Fig. S1, with higher grades indicating more severe leaf damage.

Data analysis

For the natural population, descriptive statistics and Pearson correlation analyses among drought indices (DG, DR, CDI) were performed using SPSS software (v26.0, IBM). For functional validation in A. thaliana, mortality rates (natural drought) or wilting rates (PEG-simulated drought) were averaged from at least three independent experiments. Statistical differences between genotypes were determined by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test (p < 0.05) using GraphPad Prism (version 10.0).

Genome-wide association study (GWAS)

GWAS was performed on the 135-accession panel using a high-density SNP dataset derived from our previously published study and a SV dataset. Methods of SNP calling, filtering and quality control were all based on this study [25]. The detailed genotyping and SNP calling pipeline is described as follows. Briefly, raw paired-end sequencing reads were aligned to the B. juncea reference genome (T84-66) using BWA (v0.7.17-r1188) with default parameters. SAMtools (v1.10) was subsequently utilized for sorting and indexing mapped reads. Initial SNP identification was implemented via VarScan (v2.4.3); the mpileup2snp function was executed with a minimum variant frequency of 0.01 and minimum sequencing coverage of 7. Raw single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were further filtered via self-compiled R scripts according to the following rigorous criteria: loci with a missing data rate exceeding 25% were discarded; only biallelic and simple SNPs were retained; monomorphic SNPs across all accessions were eliminated. Finally, SNPs distributed in non-genic regions were excluded to reduce analytical noise. The final high-quality panel contained 1,508,758 high-confidence SNPs. No additional variant calling or re-filtering was conducted in the present study. The SV dataset originated from the identical resequencing population. Corresponding SV identification and quality control were performed in-house following a unified bioinformatic workflow. This high-confidence SV dataset is currently unpublished and reserved internal data in our laboratory. Briefly, SV detection was performed based on read-depth variation and split-read signatures, and low-quality SVs with high missing rates and low allele frequency were removed to guarantee reliability for subsequent association analysis. Associations between SNPs, SVs and the three drought-tolerance indices were tested using a mixed linear model (MLM) in GEMMA. The first three principal components (to account for population structure) were included as fixed covariates, and a kinship matrix was fitted as a random effect. Significance was assessed using Wald’s test. The genome-wide significance thresholds were set at -log10(p) = 8 and -log10(p) = 5. Manhattan plots were generated using R (v4.1.0). Linkage disequilibrium (LD) heatmaps around significant peaks were constructed with LDBlockShow (v1.40).

Haplotype analysis

SVs in the BjuB.HAM1 gene were validated in accessions R109 and R44 by PCR using flanking primers (Table S3). 130 natural population was genotyped using the same primers. PCR products were resolved on 2% agarose gels. Phenotypic differences among haplotypes were assessed by one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test (p < 0.05).

Synteny, sequence analysis

Microsynteny analysis of the genomic region containing BjuB.HAM1 was conducted between the B. juncea reference genome (T84-66) and a genome assembly generated in our laboratory (AU201) using Multiple Collinearity Scan (MCScanX). Raw resequencing reads from R109 and R44 were aligned to the Varuna reference using BWA-MEM [26]. Processing, variant calling, and visualization were performed using SAMtools, GATK, and the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV), respectively.

Marker developing

Genotyping markers were designed by integrating information from the genome database, micro-synteny analyses, and BLAST results obtained from the Brassica Database (BRAD). Primers for DNA and cDNA cloning were based on gene sequences available in the relevant genome datasets. Semiquantitative and quantitative PCR primers were designed using the GenScript Real-time PCR (TaqMan) Primer and Probe Design Tool. All primers used for ectopic validation were designed via the SALK T-DNA Express platform. Detailed information on all primers is provided in Table S3.

Nucleic acid extraction gene expression analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from leaves using the CTAB method described by CIMMYT (2005). Total RNA was extracted from R109 and R44 leaves (sampled during a drought time-course) and from A. thaliana lines using TRIzol reagent (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, USA). cDNA was synthesized using the ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix with gDNA Remover (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan).

qPCR was performed using TOROGreen HRM qPCR Master Mix (TOROVID). For B. juncea, Bju.25 S was used as the internal reference; for A. thaliana, AtTIP41 was used. Expression levels were calculated using the 2^–ΔΔCt method.

Gene cloning and mutant identification

Gene cloning was carried out in two accessions, R109 (IN30) and R44 (03B0001), which exhibited extreme phenotypic responses under drought stress. For ectopic validation, all A. thaliana mutant lines were verified by PCR, with the wild-type Col-0 included as a negative control. All PCR amplifications were conducted using the KOD One™ PCR Master Mix-Blue (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan).

Transcriptome sequencing and analysis

Total RNA was extracted from Col-0, atham1 mutant, and BjuB.HAM1 complementation lines at the mid-stage of drought treatment. Libraries were prepared and sequenced on an Illumina platform. Raw reads were filtered for quality using fastp (v0.23.1). Clean reads were aligned to the A. thaliana (TAIR10) genome using HISAT2. Differential expression analysis was performed, and results were visualized via principal component analysis and volcano plots on the Sangerbox platform. Details of transcriptome sequencing data are provided in Table S4. And the raw sequence data have been deposited in the Genome Sequence Archive (Genomics, Proteomics & Bioinformatics 2025) in National Genomics Data Center (Nucleic Acids Res 2025), China National Center for Bioinformation / Beijing Institute of Genomics, Chinese Academy of Sciences (GSA: CRA043926) that are publicly accessible at https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa [27, 28].

Subcellular localization

The BjuB.HAM1-GFP fusion construct (in pMDC83) was transiently expressed in leaves of Nicotiana benthamiana plants expressing a nuclear RFP marker via Agrobacterium infiltration. After 60 h, fluorescence was observed using a Nikon A1 confocal laser scanning microscope (GFP: ex 488 nm, em 500–540 nm; RFP: ex 561 nm, em 580–620 nm).

Promoter cis-element analysis and target gene screening

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) showing consistent expression trends in both Col-0 and complementation lines versus the atham1 mutant were selected. The 2000-bp promoter regions upstream of these genes were retrieved from TAIR and scanned for the presence of the reported SCL27/HAM1 binding motif, the GT cis-element, whose sequence was G(A/G)(A/T)AA(A/T)GT, using appropriate software [11]. Genes containing the motif were prioritized for functional validation based on their annotation.

Yeast one-hybrid (Y1H) assay

To test the binding of HAM1 to the AtUGT76C2 promoter, a Y1H assay was performed. A 2000-bp fragment of the AtUGT76C2 promoter containing the putative GT cis-elements was amplified and cloned into the pBait-AbAi vector to generate the reporter construct (pAbAi-proUGT76C2). The full-length coding sequence of AtHAM1 and BjuB.HAM1 was amplified and cloned into the pGADT7 vector to generate the prey construct (pGADT7-AtHAM1 and pGADT7-BjuB.HAM1). The pAbAi-proUGT76C2 vector was linearized and transformed into the Y1HGold yeast strain (Coolaber, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, the pGADT7-AtHAM1 and pGADT7-BjuB.HAM1 vector was introduced into the resulting yeast strain. Transformants were plated on synthetic dropout (SD) medium lacking leucine (SD/-Leu) supplemented with 400 ng/mL Aureobasidin A (AbA) to suppress background growth and incubated at 30 ℃ for 3 days. The empty pGADT7 vector was used as a negative control. Protein-DNA interactions were evaluated based on the ability of yeast cells to grow on selective medium.

Dual luciferase (LUC) reporter assay

A dual-luciferase assay was performed to investigate the transcriptional regulatory effect of HAM1 on AtUGT76C2 expression. The 2000-bp promoter fragment of AtUGT76C2 was inserted into the pGreenII 0800-LUC vector, which contains both a firefly luciferase (LUC) reporter gene driven by the inserted promoter and a Renilla luciferase (REN) gene under the control of the constitutive 35 S promoter as an internal control. This construct (AtUGT76C2-LUC) served as the reporter. The coding sequence of AtHAM1 was cloned into the pGreenII 62-SK effector vector under the control of the 35 S promoter to generate the effector (CaMV35S-AtHAM1). The empty pGreenII 62-SK vector was used as a negative control. All constructs were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 (pSoup) chemically competent cells (Shanghai Weidi Biotechnology, China). Agrobacterial cultures were grown to an OD600 of approximately 0.8, harvested by centrifugation, and resuspended in infiltration buffer (10 mM MES, 10 mM MgCl₂, 150 µM acetosyringone, pH 5.6). The effector and reporter strains were mixed at a 9:1 ratio and co-infiltrated into fully expanded leaves of one-month-old Nicotiana benthamiana plants using a needleless syringe. After infiltration, plants were maintained in darkness for 24 h, followed by normal growth conditions for 48–60 h. Leaf discs were harvested and lysed with lysis buffer. Firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Yeasen, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions with a Feyond luminometer (AllSheng, China). Firefly LUC activity was normalized to Renilla REN activity to account for variations in transformation efficiency. At least three independent biological replicates were performed, each with three technical replicates. 0.15 mg/ml D-luciferin potassium salt solution was prepared. Using a soft brush, the solution was gently and evenly applied to the abaxial side of the tobacco leaves. After keeping in dark for 3 min, the leaves were placed into a chemiluminescence apparatus (Shenhua Science Technology, China).

Results

Phenotypic variation in B. juncea under simulated drought stress

Drought stress was simulated by adjusting osmotic pressure using polyethylene glycol (PEG-6000) aqueous solution, a well-established method for inducing water deficit under controlled conditions. Based on preliminary experiments, the response under this PEG-simulated drought differed from that under natural drought conditions. Leaf symptoms typically initiated from the margins as discoloration and wilting, then progressed inward until the entire leaf became fully wilted, desiccated, and drooped. Additionally, the phenotype developed acropetally, from older to younger leaves, over time. Although the rate of symptom development varied, all tested varieties—regardless of drought tolerance level—exhibited a similar progression of symptoms. Substantial variation in drought tolerance was observed among different B. juncea varieties (Fig. 1a). Drought-sensitive varieties displayed pronounced phenotypic changes within 3 days after treatment, whereas tolerant varieties showed no marked alterations during the same period. To consistently assess stress response, the second true leaf was evaluated at 3 days post-treatment, corresponding to a phase of progressive stress development.

Furthermore, the drop ratio of Fv/Fm (DR) was employed as an additional measure of plant stress status. Fv/Fm, representing the maximum photochemical efficiency of photosystem II (PSII), is a widely used indicator for assessing the impact of abiotic stress on plant photosynthetic performance. Measurements were taken at specific leaf locations using a handheld chlorophyll fluorescence meter. Preliminary experiments indicated that Fv/Fm values decreased significantly in leaves exhibiting visible damage symptoms under simulated drought. The point of maximum Fv/Fm reduction consistently corresponded to the most severely damaged area, which was typically located at the leaf margin. Therefore, the DR calculated as the relative decrease at this most affected point compared to the pre-treatment value was adopted as a diagnostic metric.

The drought tolerance of 135 natural B. juncea varieties was first evaluated using DG and DR at 3 days post-treatment. The phenotypic ranges were DR (-0.01–0.99), and DG (1.00–5.67) as shown in Fig. 1b and Table S5. The correlation between the two directly measured indices was 0.75, indicating a certain degree of relatedness while also suggesting that they capture distinct aspects of the trait (Fig. 1c). To integrate these two metrics into a comprehensive index, we combined them using the geometric mean to generate a new indicator, designated as Composite Damage Index (CDI). This composite index exhibited correlations of 0.95 with DR and 0.90 with DG. Its phenotypic ranges was 0.00 ~ 0.96 as shown in Fig. 1b, c and Table S2.

BjuB.HAM1 is a key candidate gene regulating drought tolerance

These three traits were subsequently used as phenotypic inputs for genome-wide association studies (GWAS) based on both SNPs and SVs datasets. In the SNP-based GWAS, we identified peaks on chromosomes A03 and B06 that exceeded or closely approached the significance threshold across all three traits (Fig. 2a and Fig. S2a and Table S5). The most significant locus on chromosome B06 was near the loci 55,264,361. In contrast, the SV-based GWAS revealed a suggestive association signal only on chromosome B06 at a position consistent with the SNP peak, near the position 55,263,308, although its −log10(p) values did not reach the threshold of 5 (Fig. S2b and Table S6). Given the co-localization with the SNP signal and the correlation among traits, this B06 locus was prioritized as a candidate region and named as qdro_B06.1. Using CDI as the GWAS phenotype, we performed linkage disequilibrium (LD) analysis in the vicinity of the B06 peak and delineated a candidate interval spanning from 55,239,707 to 55,280,189 bp, which harbored 6 genes (Table S7). Among these, 4 genes contained non-synonymous SNPs in their exons (Table S8). Expression profiling showed that one of the 6 genes, BjuB.HAM1 (BjuVB06G47090), was specifically induced under drought stress in the drought-sensitive variety (Fig. 2d, Fig. S3). To characterize the structural variation of BjuB.HAM1 across different B. juncea genomes, we first conducted a microsynteny analysis between the reference genome T84-66 and our in-house assembly AU201. The collinearity results revealed that the chromosomal region from 55.23 Mb to 55.27 Mb on chromosome B06 of T84-66 shared high homology with the 57.97–58.08 Mb interval on the corresponding chromosome of AU201. Within the syntenic block surrounding BjuB.HAM1, the AU201 assembly contained three additional annotated genes relative to T84-66, and the physical length of BjuB.HAM1 also varied considerably between the two assemblies (Fig. S4a). To further verify whether such SVs were associated with divergent drought tolerance, we compared genomic resequencing data between the drought-tolerant accession R44 and the drought-sensitive accession R109. Resequencing reads of the two accessions were initially mapped to the Varuna genome (an Indian oil-type consistent with the genetic background of R109), followed by visual genomic comparison. The results indicated that the BjuB.HAM1 target interval in R44 harbored a large-scale segmental deletion and displayed distinct multi-alignment patterns compared with R109 (Fig. S4b). We further validated sequence discrepancies via targeted gene cloning and sequence alignment between R44 and R109. Two 6.5 kb SVs were identified within the candidate interval, which existed as an insertion in R109 and a corresponding deletion in R44. The genotyping results in the population showed a significant correlation between the presence or absence of these two SVs and drought tolerance, with varieties lacking SVs showing significantly better drought tolerance than those with SVs (Fig. 2d). Specifically, the first SV was located upstream of the 5’-UTR region, whereas the second SV resided within the first exon of R44 (Fig. 2e). Collectively, these two SVs introduced two extra gene fragments and simultaneously shortened the full-length sequence of BjuB.HAM1 in R109. Based on the gene annotation and protein sequence alignment using the T84-66 and AU201 genomic resources, although the truncated BjuB.HAM1 protein in R109 lacked approximately 180 amino acids at the N-terminus and carried seven single-amino-acid substitutions relative to R44, this variant was consistently annotated as HAM1 (Fig. S5). Additionally, the truncated protein sequence could be perfectly matched to the canonical AtHAM1 sequence deposited in the TAIR database, indicating that this variant still represents a functional homologous HAM1 protein.

Overexpression of BjuB.HAM1 decreases plant drought tolerance

To evaluate the function of BjuB.HAM1 under drought stress, mutants of its homologous gene in A. thaliana were obtained, and complementary lines were generated by introducing BjuB.HAM1 into the mutant background, as well as overexpression lines in the wild-type (Col-0) background (Fig. 3c, Fig. S6). Under natural drought, the ham1 mutant exhibited the highest survival rate, with an average mortality of only 8.33% after rehydration. In contrast, Col-0 and the overexpression line showed significantly higher mortality (66.67%), while the complementation line was most sensitive, with 100% mortality (Fig. 3a, d). Consistent results were obtained under simulated drought. The ham1 mutant displayed the lowest wilting rate (36.80%), indicating superior drought tolerance. Col-0 wilted more readily (72.91%), and both the complementation and overexpression lines were highly susceptible, with wilting rates of 95.53% and 95.13%, respectively (Fig. 3b, e). Collectively, these data demonstrate that loss of HAM1 function enhances drought tolerance, while its restoration or overexpression severely compromises it. This establishes BjuB.HAM1 as a negative regulator of drought tolerance in plants.

HAM1 impacts drought tolerance by regulating AtUGT76C2 expression

Tobacco transient expression assays showed that the BjuB.HAM1 fusion protein was primarily localized in the nucleus (Fig. S7). To identify downstream target genes of HAM1, we performed transcriptome sequencing on Col-0, the ham1 mutant, and the complementation (restorer) line after two days of natural drought treatment. Comparative analysis revealed 1,488 significantly up-regulated and 1,661 significantly down-regulated genes in ham1 compared with Col-0 (Fig. S8a). When compared with the ham1-BjuB.HAM1 restorer line, the ham1 mutant showed 1,461 up-regulated and 1,442 down-regulated genes (Fig. S8b). Among these, 697 genes were consistently up-regulated and 558 were consistently down-regulated in ham1 relative to both Col-0 and the restorer line (Fig. S8c, d). To identify potential targets of BjuB.HAM1, we screened the promoters of core DEGs for its cognate GT cis-element (G(A/G)(A/T)AA(A/T)GT). This screening was based on the fact that BjuB.HAM1 belongs to the HAM subfamily of transcription factors known to bind this specific sequence [11]. This analysis highlighted AtUGT76C2, a cytokinin glycosyltransferase gene whose expression was up-regulated in ham1 and whose promoter contains three putative GT cis-elements (Fig. 4a). Yeast one-hybrid (Y1H) assays confirmed that AtHAM1, as well as BjuB.HAM1, bound to the promoter of AtUGT76C2 (Fig. 4b and S9a). Transient transcriptional activation assays in tobacco further demonstrated that AtHAM1 and BjuB.HAM1 could both bind to the AtUGT76C2 promoter and represses its expression (Fig. 4c and S9b). Consistent with the transcriptional repression by HAM1, qPCR analysis under simulated drought showed a gradient of AtUGT76C2 expression: highest in the ham1 mutant, intermediate in Col-0, and lowest in both the overexpression and complementation lines (Fig. 4d). To determine the biological function of AtUGT76C2, we evaluated the drought tolerance of an atugt76c2 mutant line. Under simulated drought, the mutant exhibited a significantly higher wilting rate compared to Col-0 (Fig. S9c), indicating that AtUGT76C2 positively contributes to drought tolerance. In summary, our integrated data support a regulatory module wherein the transcription factor BjuB.HAM1 localizes to the nucleus and AtHAM1, its homologous genes in A. thaliana, directly binds to the promoter of AtUGT76C2 to repress its expression. Since AtUGT76C2 functions as a positive regulator of drought tolerance, the repression exerted by HAM1 provides a mechanistic explanation for its negative role in the drought response pathway.

Discussion

With the continuous intensification of climate change, we will face a world that is hotter and more parched, therefore, utilizing genomic technologies to identify genes related to plant drought tolerance and improve plant performance under stress plays an important role in modern agriculture [29]. The integration of genome-wide association studies (GWAS) with phenotyping systems to identify methods for enhancing plant drought tolerance has emerged as a cutting-edge direction in current agricultural development [30]. The prerequisite for identifying precise genes is the accurate measurement of relevant traits. Assessing drought-induced damage in plants typically requires the integration of multi-dimensional phenotypic data. In this study, we combined a semi-quantitative visual damage score (DG) with a quantitative physiological parameter: the decline in the maximum quantum efficiency of photosystem II (Fv/Fm) measured at the most severely affected leaf site (DR). Both parameters have been previously employed to assess plant drought stress tolerance, for instance in barley [31]. In our simulated drought experiments with B. juncea, we observed a correlation between DG and DR; however, they were not equivalent. This discrepancy was consistent with subsequent GWAS results, which showed that the positions and significance levels of lead SNPs varied depending on the phenotypic index used. We hypothesize that overall leaf functional impairment results from the synergistic action of two related but distinct damage dimensions. Therefore, we introduced the Composite Damage Index (CDI), calculated as the geometric mean of DG and DR. The geometric mean inherently models this interaction by assigning higher weight to balanced values of DG and DR while penalizing large disparities between them. For example, a leaf with extensive but mild chlorosis (high DG, low DR) and a leaf with a small but severe necrotic lesion (low DG, high DR) would both yield a lower CDI compared to a leaf with uniformly moderate damage across a large area.

In this study, we employed a PEG-6000-based soil infiltration method to simulate drought stress; this approach reliably assessed tolerance levels among 135 accessions. Although the visible symptoms under this controlled assay differed from those in prolonged natural drought, this approach effectively captured the genetic basis of differential resilience, as confirmed by our subsequent analyses. In this study, we performed SNP-GWAS and SV-GWAS for various drought-related traits in B. juncea, identified BjuB.HAM1 as the candidate gene, and found two SVs which significantly associated with differences in drought tolerance in the gene promoter and sequence region. However, though we found SVs on BjuB.HAM1, the impact on gene function and expression still needs further investigation. BjuB.HAM1 encodes a transcription factor belonging to the HAM subfamily within the GAI-RGA-and-SCR (GRAS) family and a key regulator associated with drought tolerance variation. The GRAS family, including the ancient HAM lineage, plays fundamental roles in plant development and stress responses [32, 33]. HAM proteins exhibit functional conservation and redundancy, and their expansion in various plant lineages suggests the acquisition of novel roles or subfunctionalization [9]. This family is often implicated in shoot meristem maintenance, chlorophyll biosynthesis and apical growth [10–12]. Additionally, HAM subfamily members are post-transcriptionally regulated by miRNAs and have been implicated in drought stress responses, branching architecture, heading time, leaf morphogenesis, and disease resistance [10, 11, 13–15]. Previous studies have suggested that several HAM subfamily members act as target genes of miR171, and miR171 itself has been proven to participate in drought stress responses [11, 13, 34]. However, the reported drought-related functional investigations mainly focus on other HAM homologs, and the direct association of HAM1 with drought regulation remains ambiguous and lacks conclusive experimental support. Our functional characterization in (A) thaliana aligns with and extends this knowledge. The enhanced drought tolerance of the atham1 mutant, coupled with the severe drought-sensitive phenotype of BjuB.HAM1 overexpression lines, unequivocally demonstrates that HAM1 acts as a negative regulator of drought tolerance. This is consistent with the observed expression pattern in (B) juncea, where BjuB.HAM1 transcript accumulated progressively in the drought-sensitive accession R109 but remained nearly undetectable in the tolerant R44.

In the present study, functional validation of BjuB.HAM1 has been exclusively performed using a heterologous expression system in (A) thaliana. While this well-established model system enables rapid and preliminary characterization of gene functionality, it cannot faithfully recapitulate the intrinsic biological roles of target genes within the allotetraploid genetic background of (B) juncea. In-planta functional validation in B. juncea is therefore indispensable to further decipher the exact regulatory mechanism of BjuB.HAM1. Despite its necessity, direct functional characterization via genetic transformation and gene editing remains technically challenging in B. juncea, primarily due to its complex allotetraploid genome and widespread phenotypic variations across diverse horticultural ecotypes. The B. juncea genome consists of two distinct subgenomes, and the majority of protein-coding genes are present as multiple homologous copies, resulting in pervasive genetic redundancy across the genome. Such genomic characteristics necessitate the simultaneous knockout or transcriptional suppression of redundant homologous alleles to generate detectable and unambiguous phenotypic alterations [35, 36].Encouragingly, continuous advances in transgenic manipulation and clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)-based editing systems have rendered the simultaneous overexpression and multi-allelic knockout of redundant genes achievable in B. juncea, laying a solid technical foundation for follow-up in-planta gene validation.

We further elucidated a potential molecular mechanism underlying the role of BjuB.HAM1. Cytokinins (CKs) are known to fine-tune drought responses, often by antagonizing abscisic acid (ABA) signaling [16]. Their homeostasis is modulated by glycosyltransferases like UGT76C2, which inactivates CKs via glycosylation [37, 38]. Notably, overexpression of UGT76C2 enhances drought tolerance, while its loss-of-function increases sensitivity [19]. Previous studies have revealed that altered expression of AtUGT76C2 interferes with the expression of stress-responsive genes (including DREB2A, RD22, RD29B, LEA, COR47, and KIN1) and cytokinin signaling marker genes (including AHK2, AHK3, AHK4, ARR1, and ARR2). Accordingly, AtUGT76C2 modulates drought tolerance by disrupting cytokinin homeostasis and perturbing the cytokinin signal transduction cascade [19]. Our data integrate HAM1 into this regulatory module. Transcriptomic and qPCR analyses revealed that AtUGT76C2 expression was significantly higher in the atham1 mutant. Crucially, Y1H and LUC assays demonstrated that HAM1 protein can directly bind to GT cis-elements in the AtUGT76C2 promoter and repress its transcription. Therefore, we propose a model where HAM1 negatively regulates drought tolerance by directly suppressing UGT76C2, a positive regulator of cytokinin inactivation and drought adaptation.

Conclusion

In this study, we identified HAM1 as a potential candidate gene and demonstrated its role as a negative regulator of drought stress through GWAS. Specifically, HAM1 directly binds to the promoter of UGT76C2, which encodes a cytokinin glycosyltransferase, and effectively inhibits the expression of UGT76C2. This study not only confirms the functional involvement of the HAM subfamily in the plant response to drought stress but also provides a broader theoretical foundation for the genetic improvement of drought resistance in crops.
