Attitudes toward artificial intelligence: their association with creative self-efficacy and problem-solving ability in nursing interns
Authors: Mengjia Yi, Yanmei Gan, Tingting Liao, Lan Luo, Yunxu Huang, Gaoye Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo determine the levels of attitudes toward artificial intelligence(AI), creative self-efficacy, and problem-solving ability among nursing interns enrolled in diploma and bachelor’s nursing programs who were in the final stage of their clinical training in Guangxi, China, and to explore the relationships among these variables as well as the factors associated with attitudes toward artificial intelligence.MethodsA cross-sectional survey design was conducted among 259 nursing interns undertaking their final-year clinical internships across seven hospitals. Data were collected between July and August 2025 using an online questionnaire assessing sociodemographic characteristics, AI attitudes, creative self-efficacy, and problem-solving ability. Correlation and multiple linear regression analyses were performed. The study followed ethical principles and STROBE reporting guidelines.ResultsThe mean score for nursing interns’ attitudes toward artificial intelligence (AIA) was 47.70 ± 8.75, the mean score for creative self-efficacy (CSE) was 28.56 ± 6.20, and the mean score for problem-solving ability (PSA) was 79.83 ± 17.53. Spearman correlation analysis showed that AIA was positively correlated with PSA (p < 0.05) and CSE (p < 0.05), and that CSE was positively correlated with PSA (p < 0.05). Multiple linear regression analysis further indicated that gender, educational program, CSE, and PSA were independently associated with AIA after adjustment.ConclusionPositive correlations were observed among attitudes toward artificial intelligence, creative self-efficacy, and problem-solving ability in nursing interns. Educational program, gender, creative self-efficacy, and problem-solving ability were significantly associated with AI attitudes; however, these findings reflect associations rather than causal relationships.Keywords: Nursing interns, AI attitudes, Creative self-efficacy, Problem-solving ability

Introduction

Artificial Intelligence (AI) is increasingly being integrated into healthcare systems and has been shown in previous studies to be related to clinical decision-making, real-time patient monitoring, and nursing workflow outcomes [1–5]. The ongoing advancement of smart hospitals and intelligent nursing technologies has increasingly exposed nurses to artificial intelligence (AI) applications in clinical practice. This growing exposure is associated with considerations of technical proficiency, nurses’ comprehension of health-related information, their attitudes toward emerging technologies, and their capacity for AI-informed clinical decision-making.

Nursing interns represent a critical transitional group from academic education to clinical practice [6]. In China, students enrolled in both diploma and bachelor’s nursing programs are required to complete hospital-based clinical placements during the final stage of their training. At this stage, their professional identity and core competencies are still evolving, and their attitudes toward emerging technologies may be related to these ongoing developments [7]. While interns gradually engage with electronic health records, intelligent monitoring systems, and clinical decision support tools, differences in their attitudes toward artificial intelligence (AIA) may be associated with variations in learning engagement, willingness to adopt technology, and participation in clinical decision-making [8], which may be related to perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and subsequent adoption intentions.

Previous studies have shown that attitudes toward technology are important correlates of adoption and use behaviors [9, 10]. The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) further conceptualizes attitudes as a central link between technology perceptions and usage behavior [11]. Accordingly, examining nursing interns’ AIA is important for understanding technology adoption in clinical education and practice. In the context of nursing education, meaningful AI exposure extends beyond simple awareness of AI technologies and encompasses both educational and clinical experiences with AI. Educational exposure refers to structured learning opportunities, such as AI-related courses, workshops, seminars, and guided training activities that support understanding of AI concepts and applications. Clinical exposure refers to interaction with AI-enabled technologies during clinical training, including intelligent monitoring systems, electronic health records incorporating AI functionalities, and clinical decision-support tools. In the present study, participation in AI-related courses or training, familiarity with AI applications, and frequency of AI product use were included as descriptive indicators of nursing interns’ exposure to AI-related learning opportunities and practical interactions with AI technologies. In this study, AIA are defined as nursing interns’ overall positive or negative evaluative disposition toward the use of AI technologies in nursing practice. This construct reflects the extent to which interns view AI as favorable or unfavorable in the context of nursing care. AIA are likely intertwined with self-perceived competence. Social cognitive theory emphasizes that self-efficacy beliefs are relevant when individuals encounter complex or novel tasks [12]. Empirical studies employing visual narrative methods, such as Photovoice, further suggest that attitudes toward technology are associated with both external contexts and individuals’ self-efficacy [13]. Creative self-efficacy (CSE), which reflects confidence in generating novel ideas and solving problems in uncertain or complex situations, is particularly relevant as AI increasingly informs nursing decision-making [14]. Similarly, problem-solving ability (PSA) is a core nursing competency, reflecting the capacity to integrate knowledge, analyze clinical problems, and make informed decisions. Its development is associated with knowledge acquisition, practical experience, and psychological factors, including self-efficacy, motivation, and innovative awareness [15]. In clinical contexts where AI technologies are increasingly integrated into nursing practice, AIA, CSE, and PSA may be conceptually interrelated. Drawing on the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and social cognitive theory, this study proposes an integrated perspective in which attitudes toward AI are related not only to technology-related perceptions but also to individual self-efficacy beliefs and cognitive competence. Creative self-efficacy reflects confidence in dealing with novel and complex challenges, whereas problem-solving ability represents a core competence for analyzing information and making decisions in clinical contexts. Together, these factors may be associated with how nursing interns perceive, evaluate, and respond to emerging AI technologies in nursing practice. However, empirical evidence systematically examining these relationships among nursing interns remains limited. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the levels of AIA, CSE, and PSA among nursing interns and to examine the associations among these variables.

The study

Aim

To investigate the levels of attitudes toward artificial intelligence, creative self-efficacy, and problem-solving ability among nursing interns, and to examine the associations among these variables.

Design

A cross-sectional survey design was adopted.

Methods

Setting and sample

This cross-sectional study was conducted between July and August 2025. Using convenience sampling, 259 nursing students undertaking clinical internships (hereafter referred to as nursing interns) were recruited from diploma and bachelor’s programs.

In the Chinese nursing education system, nursing education is structured into diploma and bachelor’s tracks, each comprising sequential theoretical and clinical training. Diploma programs include 2 years of institution-based theoretical education followed by 8 months of clinical internship, whereas bachelor’s programs consist of 3 years of theoretical education followed by 10 months of clinical internship. The clinical internship is a mandatory component in the final stage of both programs and marks the transition from theoretical learning to clinical practice.

Clinical training was conducted in affiliated tertiary-level (Grade A) teaching hospitals under a joint training model involving nursing schools and hospital-based teaching departments. During the internship, nursing interns rotated across 10 clinical departments (e.g., internal medicine, surgery, intensive care units, emergency care) for 2–4 weeks per rotation, with joint supervision by clinical preceptors and academic instructors, providing opportunities for interns to gain experience in integrating theoretical knowledge and clinical competencies.

At the time of data collection, all participants were in the early stage of their internship and had completed 2–3 months of clinical placement.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) nursing interns currently undertaking clinical internships in diploma or bachelor’s nursing programs; and (2) ability to complete the questionnaire independently. The exclusion criteria were: (1) not participating in clinical internship during the study period (e.g., suspension, leave, or other absence); and (2) questionnaires identified as invalid during data screening, including those with inconsistent responses or extremely short completion times.

All participants provided informed consent prior to participation in accordance with ethical requirements.

The sample size was estimated using G*Power 3.1 for multiple linear regression analysis. Assuming a medium effect size (f² = 0.15), a significance level of α = 0.05, a statistical power of 0.95, and 3 predictor variables, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 119 participants. Considering a potential 10% invalid response rate, the target sample size was determined to be approximately 132 participants. Ultimately, 259 valid questionnaires were obtained.

Instruments

General information questionnaire

A self-designed general information questionnaire was developed based on a review of relevant literature on nursing students’ attitudes toward artificial intelligence and technology use [8, 9, 16] and aligned with the objectives of this study. The initial items were generated by the research team and iteratively refined to ensure clarity, relevance, and content validity. The questionnaire collected data on participants’ sociodemographic characteristics as well as their AI exposure, which was operationalized in this study to capture cumulative experience with artificial intelligence technologies in both educational and clinical contexts. Educational exposure was defined as participation in structured AI-related learning activities, such as formal courses, workshops, seminars, or guided training sessions integrated into the nursing curriculum, and was assessed through self-reported participation. Clinical exposure was defined as direct interaction with AI-enabled tools during hospital-based internships, including AI-assisted patient monitoring, clinical decision support systems, and electronic health records incorporating AI functionalities. Clinical exposure was measured via self-reported frequency of AI product use and subjective familiarity with AI applications. This operationalization provides a descriptive, quantifiable, and reproducible measure of AI exposure suitable for cross-sectional analysis, allowing differentiation between theoretical knowledge acquired in educational settings and practical experience gained in clinical practice.

Attitude toward the use of artificial intelligence in nursing

Attitude toward the use of artificial intelligence in nursing was assessed using the Attitude Scale Towards the Use of Artificial Intelligence Technologies in Nursing (ASUAITIN). The original scale was developed by Yilmaz et al. in 2024 [17] to assess clinical nurses’ use of artificial intelligence in nursing practice. The scale comprises 15 items across two dimensions (positive attitude and negative attitude) and is rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with total scores ranging from 15 to 75.

The Chinese version of the scale (ASUAITIN-C) was translated and validated by Hu et al. in 2025 [18], demonstrating a Cronbach’s α of 0.785 and a test–retest reliability of 0.91. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.862. Confirmatory factor analysis indicated acceptable structural validity (χ²/df = 3.10, GFI = 0.883, RMSEA = 0.090). Prior to the main survey, a pilot study was conducted with 15 nursing interns to assess item clarity and comprehensibility. Participants completed the scale and provided feedback on item clarity and comprehensibility. All items were considered understandable despite interns’ limited clinical experience, suggesting that the wording and content of the ASUAITIN-C were comprehensible for nursing interns. However, the pilot study primarily provided evidence of face validity and comprehensibility rather than full construct validation in this population.

Creative self-efficacy

Creative self-efficacy was measured using the Creative Self-Efficacy Scale developed by Gu et al. [19], which was originally developed in organizational employee populations. The Chinese version and its adaptation for nursing populations have been reported in previous studies [20], in which the scale was culturally adapted and applied in Chinese healthcare settings. The adaptation process ensured contextual relevance for clinical nursing environments while maintaining the original conceptual framework. The scale consists of eight items rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with total scores ranging from 8 to 40, where higher scores indicate greater creative self-efficacy. In prior studies, the scale demonstrated good reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.932). In the present study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.943, indicating excellent internal consistency in the nursing intern population.

Problem-solving ability

Problem-solving ability was assessed using the Chinese Version of the Social Problem-Solving Inventory (C-SPSI). The original scale was developed by Siu et al. in 2005 [21] and later translated and simplified by Wang in 2010 for use among nursing interns [22]. The scale consists of 25 items across five dimensions: Avoidance Style (AS), Impulsivity/Carelessness Style (ICS), Negative Problem Orientation (NPO), Positive Problem Orientation (PPO), and Rational Problem Solving (RPS).

All items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale. PPO and RPS are positively scored, whereas AS, ICS, and NPO are reverse-scored and were recoded during data analysis. Total scores range from 25 to 125, with higher scores indicating stronger problem-solving ability. The scale has demonstrated good reliability, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.871 and a test–retest reliability of 0.931. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.938.

Data collection

Before data collection, all members of the research team received standardized training on study procedures, ethical considerations, participant recruitment, and use of the online survey platform to ensure consistency in data collection.

Research team members disseminated study invitations through clinical departments and internship-related communication groups. To minimize potential recruitment bias and perceived coercion, invitations were distributed by researchers who were not involved in the participants’ academic assessment, clinical supervision, or internship evaluation. Participants were informed that participation was entirely voluntary and that declining or withdrawing would have no effect on their academic standing, internship evaluations, or relationships with instructors and clinical preceptors. The invitation included a QR code linking to the anonymous online survey platform (Wenjuanxing). After scanning the code, participants accessed a standardized instruction page describing the study purpose and providing an informed consent statement. Only those who provided electronic informed consent were able to proceed to the questionnaire.

To reduce potential common method bias, several procedural remedies were implemented. The survey was conducted anonymously, confidentiality was assured, and standardized instructions emphasized that there were no right or wrong answers and encouraged honest responses. In addition, the study used validated instruments with different item wording and scale formats.

All items were mandatory, and incomplete questionnaires could not be submitted. To prevent duplicate responses, each IP address and device was restricted to a single submission. Questionnaires displaying identical responses throughout, inconsistent answers, or a completion time of less than 180 s were excluded. A total of 261 questionnaires were collected, of which 259 were valid, resulting in an effective response rate of 99%.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 27.0. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and data with p > 0.05 were considered to be normally distributed. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using independent-samples t tests or one-way analysis of variance. Non-normally distributed data are presented as median and interquartile range and compared using non-parametric tests.

Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages and analyzed using chi-square tests. Pearson or Spearman correlation analyses were conducted to examine associations between continuous variables, according to data distribution. AIA in nursing was treated as the dependent variable. Variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analyses, along with theoretically relevant variables, were considered for inclusion in the multivariate model to avoid overlooking potentially important predictors. In addition, gender and other theoretically relevant variables were included as control variables in the regression model to account for potential confounding effects. A multiple linear regression analysis was performed to identify factors independently associated with attitudes. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), and all VIFs < 5 indicated acceptable collinearity. Regression assumptions—including linearity, normality of residuals, independence, and homoscedasticity—were checked and satisfied. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In addition, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted to assess the potential presence of common method bias. All measurement items were entered into an exploratory factor analysis. The results showed that multiple factors emerged and the first unrotated factor accounted for 30.43% of the total variance, indicating that no single factor dominated the covariance among the variables. This suggests that common method bias is unlikely to be a serious concern in this study, consistent with methodological recommendations [23].

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University (Approval No. 2025-E0681). All participants provided informed consent before participation.

Correlations among AI attitudes, creative self-efficacy, and problem-solving ability in nursing interns

Spearman correlation analysis revealed a moderate positive correlation between the positive dimension of AIA and CSE (P < 0.05), while the negative dimension of AIA was negatively correlated with CSE (P < 0.05). The positive dimension of AIA was significantly and positively correlated with avoidance style, impulsive problem-solving style, positive problem orientation, and rational problem solving within PSA (all P < 0.05), but showed no significant association with negative problem orientation (P > 0.05).

The negative dimension of AIA was negatively correlated with all five dimensions of PSA (all P < 0.05), indicating that stronger negative AIA was associated with greater difficulty in adopting effective problem-solving strategies. In addition, CSE was positively correlated with all PSA dimensions (all P < 0.05). Detailed results are presented in Table 3.

Multivariate regression analysis of factors associated with AI attitudes among nursing interns

The selection of sociodemographic variables (e.g., age, gender, educational program, and prior AI exposure) was informed by previous research on nursing students’ attitudes toward AI and technology acceptance, which indicates that individual characteristics are related to perceptions of AI’s usefulness and ease of use, and to overall attitudes and intentions regarding AI applications in healthcare settings. Systematic reviews and empirical studies have reported associations between demographic factors such as gender, academic level, and prior exposure to AI-related technologies and variations in AI-related perceptions and attitudes [24, 25]. Moreover, studies examining anxiety and attitudes toward AI have reported that individual background characteristics may be associated with affective and cognitive responses to AI [26]. Including these variables allows for a more complete description of factors associated with AIA and accounts for individual differences among nursing interns. The total AIA score among nursing interns was used as the dependent variable. Variables that were statistically significant in the univariate analysis (P < 0.05), including age, educational program, familiarity with AI-related products (e.g., ChatGPT, ERNIE Bot), frequency of using AI-related products, participation in AI-related courses or training, creative self-efficacy, and problem-solving ability, were entered as independent variables in the multivariate linear regression model. In addition, gender and duration of internship were included as control variables to adjust for potential confounding effects. Although significant differences in AI attitudes were observed across hospitals in the univariate analysis, hospital was not included in the multivariate regression model because participant distribution across hospitals was highly uneven, and several hospitals had relatively small subgroup sample sizes. Inclusion of hospital as a categorical predictor under these conditions may have resulted in sparse-category estimates and unstable regression coefficients. Consequently, the observed between-hospital differences should be interpreted cautiously, and future studies with more balanced multicenter samples are warranted to further examine potential institutional influences. Discussion

Levels of AI attitudes, creative self-efficacy, and problem-solving ability among nursing interns

The results of this study suggest that nursing interns’ attitudes toward artificial intelligence generally reflect a cautious yet receptive pattern, which may reflect perceptions of the potential value of AI in nursing while also indicating caution and some anxiety regarding technology adaptation. On the one hand, the observed pattern may reflect nursing interns’ positive perceptions of the potential value of AI applications in clinical nursing. On the other hand, interns’ limited clinical experience and the absence of systematically integrated AI content in the curriculum may be related to their level of understanding of and trust in AI functions, potentially reflecting a degree of technical anxiety and uncertainty. These findings align with those reported by Salameh et al. [9] among Palestinian nursing students. Previous research has also shown that although widespread AI adoption is associated with greater recognition of its clinical value [16], technology-related anxiety and trust issues remain prevalent. These findings may also be associated with the relatively limited exposure to AI-related content during routine clinical training.

In addition, the creative self-efficacy scores observed among nursing interns in this study were comparable to those reported by Ju et al. [20] among nurses in tertiary hospitals. This finding suggests that the level of creative self-efficacy among nursing interns was similar to that reported in previous studies of clinical nurses. Current nursing education emphasizes the integration of evidence-based practice and clinical decision-making [27], promoting scientific decision-making and patient safety through standardized, evidence-guided training. Although nursing education emphasizes standardized clinical training and evidence-based practice, the extent to which these educational approaches are associated with creative self-efficacy was not examined in the present study. From an educational perspective, these findings may suggest that nursing interns perceive greater emphasis on knowledge acquisition and procedural competence than on idea generation and solution exploration during training.

Furthermore, nursing interns’ PSA results suggest that their systematic thinking and rational analysis in complex clinical situations may vary across individuals. This finding aligns with Wang’s study [22], which highlighted that PSA may be associated not only with foundational nursing knowledge but also with the integration of theory with clinical context, multidimensional analysis, and the selection of optimal interventions.

Overall, this study provides a descriptive overview of nursing interns’ AI attitudes, creative self-efficacy, and problem-solving ability, suggesting potential associations among these constructs that warrant further investigation. However, these implications should be regarded as conceptual suggestions, as the effectiveness of specific educational strategies was not evaluated in the present study.

Associations of sociodemographic, educational level, creative self-efficacy, and problem-solving ability on AI attitudes

Multiple regression analysis indicated that, among the variables examined, educational program, creative self-efficacy (CSE), and problem-solving ability (PSA) were significantly associated with nursing interns’ attitudes toward AI, while sociodemographic variables were included to control for potential confounding effects, even if some of these variables were not statistically significant in the model. In addition, gender became statistically significant after adjustment for internship duration, educational program, and CSE, whereas it was not significant in the univariate analysis. This finding offers additional insight beyond the unadjusted results and suggests that the association between gender and AIA differed between the univariate and multivariate analyses, possibly reflecting the influence of confounding factors. One possible explanation is that male and female nursing interns may differ in factors related to their attitudes toward AI. However, because these factors were not directly measured in this study, the underlying reasons for the observed gender difference remain unclear. In addition, the relatively small proportion of male participants may limit the stability and generalizability of this finding. Therefore, the observed gender association should be interpreted cautiously, and future studies with more balanced samples are needed. Importantly, the inclusion of sociodemographic variables in the regression model allows for a more complete adjustment for individual differences and ensures that observed associations of educational and psychological factors with AI attitudes are not confounded by age, gender, or internship duration.

Among these factors, differences in AIA across educational programs were particularly apparent. Interns enrolled in bachelor’s programs demonstrated more positive AIA than those in diploma programs. This difference may be related to variations in educational exposure, including earlier engagement with evidence-based nursing, research methodology, and critical thinking training in bachelor’s curricula [28]. In contrast, diploma programs may place greater emphasis on technical skill development and provide less exposure to AI-related concepts. However, these interpretations remain speculative because specific educational experiences were not directly measured in this study.

Furthermore, CSE was positively associated with AIA, indicating that, in the context of emerging technologies, interns with higher creative self-efficacy may be more likely to exhibit positive AIA. This finding aligns with Bandura’s self-efficacy theory [29], which posits that efficacy beliefs are associated with attitudes and behavioral intentions, potentially corresponding to individual differences in AIA.

Similarly, PSA was also an important factor associated with AIA. Higher PSA was associated with more positive AIA, possibly reflecting differences in how nursing interns perceive and evaluate emerging technologies such as AI [30]. However, because this study assessed attitudes rather than actual technology use or AI-related competence, the underlying mechanisms of this association remain unclear.

Additionally, institutional context, such as the hospital in which an intern is placed, may be associated with differences in AI attitudes. Significant differences in AI attitudes were observed across the seven participating hospitals (Kruskal–Wallis test, P < 0.001), suggesting potential variation across institutional contexts. Hospital was not included in the main regression model because several hospitals had relatively small subgroup sample sizes, which may have resulted in unstable parameter estimation, but these observed differences highlight heterogeneity in AI attitudes across institutions. Because hospital-level characteristics were not directly measured, these interpretations should be considered exploratory.

Taken together, these findings suggest that nursing interns’ attitudes toward AI may be associated with multiple interrelated factors, including technology-related exposure, self-efficacy beliefs, and problem-solving ability. These results are broadly consistent with the Technology Acceptance Model and social cognitive theory in nursing education. Creative self-efficacy and problem-solving ability may represent complementary factors associated with nursing interns’ attitudes toward AI, providing a more integrated interpretation of the observed associations.

Associations between AI attitudes, creative self-efficacy, and problem-solving ability

In this study, AIA were positively correlated with both CSE and PSA, suggesting that more positive AI attitudes were associated with higher CSE and PSA scores. Specifically, positive AIA were positively correlated with the reverse-scored dimensions of avoidance style and impulsive problem-solving style, as well as with positive problem orientation and rational problem solving (r = 0.124–0.265, P < 0.05), whereas no significant association was observed with negative problem orientation (P > 0.05). In contrast, negative AIA were negatively correlated with all PSA dimensions (r = -0.247 to -0.307, P < 0.01). Among these, the correlations of positive AIA with positive problem orientation (r = 0.265) and avoidance style (r = 0.219) were relatively stronger. Because avoidance style, impulsivity/carelessness style, and negative problem orientation were reverse-scored, higher scores reflected more adaptive problem-solving tendencies rather than maladaptive characteristics. Although more adaptive problem-solving tendencies are generally considered beneficial, the present study assessed only attitudes toward AI and self-reported problem-solving ability. Therefore, the relationship between AI attitudes and actual clinical decision-making processes cannot be determined and warrants further investigation in future research. These observed associations may contribute to understanding how AIA are related to CSE and PSA among nursing interns.

In addition, positive AIA were positively correlated with CSE (r = 0.316, P < 0.01), whereas negative AIA showed a negative correlation. These findings may reflect differences in creative self-efficacy among nursing interns. Taken together, these findings suggest that more positive AI attitudes were associated with higher creative self-efficacy and higher problem-solving ability scores among nursing interns. In addition, because all variables were assessed using self-reported measures collected at the same time point, the observed correlations may have been influenced in part by common method variance and shared response tendencies. Although procedural remedies and Harman’s single-factor test suggested that common method bias was unlikely to be a serious concern, its potential influence cannot be completely excluded. Furthermore, while the observed associations were statistically significant, their magnitude was generally modest. Therefore, the relationships among AI attitudes, creative self-efficacy, and problem-solving ability should be interpreted cautiously. Because this study was cross-sectional and based on self-reported measures, the direction and underlying mechanisms of these associations cannot be determined. Future longitudinal and interventional studies using multiple data sources are needed to further examine these relationships.

Conclusion

AIA, CSE, and PSA demonstrated generally favorable score distributions among nursing interns, with significant positive correlations observed among the three variables. Educational program, gender, CSE, and PSA were significantly associated with AIA, indicating associations rather than causal relationships. These findings provide preliminary evidence regarding factors associated with nursing interns’ attitudes toward AI and may help inform future educational initiatives related to AI integration in nursing education and clinical training.

Limitation

This study employed a cross-sectional design, which limits the ability to infer causal relationships among variables and permits only the identification of associations. In addition, all data were collected via self-reported questionnaires, which may introduce common method variance (CMV) and response bias, potentially influencing the observed relationships among variables. Although procedural and statistical measures were applied to reduce and assess common method bias (e.g., anonymous data collection and Harman’s single-factor test), the possibility of residual bias cannot be completely excluded. Furthermore, convenience sampling was employed, and the sample was drawn from seven hospitals within a single geographic region (Guangxi Province), which may limit both the representativeness of the sample and the generalizability of the findings to nursing interns in other regions. In addition, the ASUAITIN-C was originally developed and validated among clinical nurses, whereas the present study applied the instrument to nursing interns. Although the items were found to be understandable among nursing interns, the pilot study mainly supported comprehensibility rather than full construct validation for this population. Therefore, the applicability of the instrument to this specific population should be interpreted with caution. Future studies are encouraged to further examine the psychometric properties of the ASUAITIN-C among nursing interns using larger samples and more comprehensive validation procedures. In addition, although differences in AI attitudes were observed across hospitals, hospital-level characteristics (e.g., digital infrastructure, AI-related training environment, and institutional support) were not directly measured or incorporated into the analysis, which may limit a more comprehensive understanding of contextual influences on AI attitudes. In addition, the predominance of female participants indicates a gender imbalance, which may influence overall findings and should be considered when interpreting results. Future studies are recommended to adopt longitudinal or experimental designs, utilize multiple data sources or objective measures to minimize common method bias, and conduct multicenter investigations with larger and more diverse samples while incorporating institutional-level variables to better examine contextual differences across clinical training settings, which may be associated with increased robustness and generalizability of the findings. Additionally, future research should aim for more gender-balanced samples to better explore potential gender-related patterns in attitudes, creative self-efficacy, and problem-solving ability.
