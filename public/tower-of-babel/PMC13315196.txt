Single-cell transcriptome profiling of primordial germ cells reveals differences between right and left gonads in chickens
Authors: Biyan Zhou, Jintang Luo, Zhuliang Yang, Zijun Mo, Yonghao Teng, Hongyu Zhou, Xiurong Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In female birds, the left ovary develops while the right gonad regresses. Whether and how PGCs differ between the two sides during early gonadal differentiation remains unclear. We performed single-cell RNA-seq on right and left gonads of female chicken embryos at HH29 and HH34. After quality control, cells were clustered, PGCs transcriptional programs compared, GO/KEGG enrichment and cell communication analyzed, and genes validated by qPCR. We found that at HH29, right-left PGCs differences were enriched for DNA damage response, p53-mediated cell-cycle arrest, ribosome, and translation-related terms. KEGG pathways included ribosome, ubiquitin-proteolysis, and oxidative phosphorylation. At HH34, divergence shifted to cellular senescence, cAMP signaling, and necroptosis. Genes like JUN, ID2, GADD45A, and GTSE1 were differentially expressed. Across developmental stages, right-side PGCs additionally showed enrichment in steroid hormone biosynthesis, estrogen signaling and ovarian steroidogenesis. Cell–cell communication analysis indicated that somatic–PGCs interactions within the NOTCH pathway were present only in the right gonad at both stages. Single‑cell profiling reveals a stage‑dependent shift of right–left PGCs differences from DNA damage/cell‑cycle programs at HH29 to senescence‑associated programs at HH34, together with right-specific NOTCH interactions. These data offer insights into microenvironmental regulation of PGCs fate during avian ovarian asymmetry.

Introduction

Primordial germ cells (PGCs) are pluripotent stem cells that are precursors of the germ cell lineage, which are responsible for transmitting genetic information to the next generation (Miyahara et al., 2014; Kinoshita et al., 2024). In chicken, they play a foundational role in germplasm conservation and represent a pivotal model system for genetic engineering research (Woodcock et al., 2019; Lázár et al., 2021; Idoko-Akoh et al., 2023). The unique migratory and developmental properties of avian PGCs (Tagami et al., 2017), setting them apart from PGCs of other animal species. PGCs were identified as originating from the central region of the hyaline zone on the ventral surface of the ectoderm by the Periodic Acid Schiff (PAS) staining (Eyal-Giladi et al., 1981; Jung et al., 2005; Nakamura et al., 2007), PGCs gradually transferred to the hypodermis as the development progressed (Ginsburg et al., 1987; Niu et al., 2024). By identifying the VASA homologue CVH as a surface marker for chicken PGCs (Tsunekawa et al., 2000), researchers found that the distribution of CVH-positive cell was similar to that of PGCs, which suggested that chicken PGCs originated from the central region of the hyaline zone at EG&K stage Ⅹ. As embryonic development proceeded, PGCs proliferated and migrated through the vascular system to the developing gonads, where they eventually colonized and settled.

The initial sign of gonadal development emerges on the third embryonic day as the ventral body cavity epithelium near the mesonephros thickens (Ebensperger et al., 1988). At stages Hamburger and Hamilton developmental stage 22-25 (HH22-25), the gonads are undifferentiated and morphologically identical in both sexes (Hamburger et al., 1951; Ayers et al., 2015; Estermann et al., 2020). Gonadal differentiation began after HH29-30, driven by key sex-determining genes and hormones (Guioli et al., 2007; Guioli et al., 2020; Clinton et al., 2023; Zhao D et al., 2022), leading to symmetrical testicular development in males and asymmetrical ovarian development in females (Ukeshima et al., 1991). This asymmetry in females became clearly apparent by stage HH34 (Guioli et al., 2014; Intarapat et al., 2014; Zou et al., 2020).

Transcending bulk population studies, single-cell sequencing captures cellular heterogeneity and discovers key embryonic differentiated genes through staged transcriptional mapping (Wagner et al., 2020; Jovic et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023). To date, several studies have employed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technology in avian research. For instance, scRNA-seq was employed to map high-resolution transcriptomic profiles of embryonic stem cell lineages, identifying novel surface markers of PGCs, and elucidating dynamic molecular pathways during chicken spermatogenesis (Mok et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025).

To elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying asymmetric gonadal development in females, quantitative proteomic analysis identified DMRT1, ZFPM2, and TSHZ3 as proteins potentially associated with degeneration of the right gonad in female embryos (Qin et al., 2022). Integrated single-cell and spatial transcriptome analyses of the asymmetric chicken ovaries revealed a distinct pro-follicular profile in the left ovary, contrasting with stromal/myogenic signatures on the right side (Wang et al., 2024), indicating that this asymmetry was regulated by multiple factors. Furthermore, detailed transcriptional and chromatin profiling of the chicken gonad identified LHX9 as a candidate transcription factor that may regulate the development of the left gonad into a functional ovary (Li et al., 2022). Collectively, these studies suggested that asymmetric development of the female gonads was a multifactorial process. It is governed by side-specific transcriptional programs and regulated by key proteins or genes that influence cell survival and differentiation.

Current comparative studies in PGCs have mostly focused on differences between male and female chicken embryos or comparisons across species. Soler et al. found that male PGCs and female PGCs have different abilities to absorb protein. A comparative analysis of migration, proliferation and apoptosis, combined with RNA-seq profiling, was conducted on female and male PGCs at E3.5, E4.5, and E5.5. The results revealed greater developmental differences than sex differences at the same stage, and E5.5 PGCs of both sexes exhibited a superior capacity for DNA damage repair, suggesting their enhanced suitability for long-term cryopreservation in vitro (Gong et al., 2024). Moreover, comparative studies revealed that the gonadal PGC proportion in chicken embryos is four times lower than zebra finch, with differences in both the signaling pathways and genes involved (Jung et al., 2023). However, the molecular differences between right and left gonadal PGCs during chicken embryonic development remain largely unexplored. To address this gap, we employed scRNA-seq to comprehensively compare their transcriptional profiles. We aim to uncover the cellular heterogeneity and identify the key signaling pathways and genetic regulators that underpin this asymmetric development. Our findings provide crucial insights into the mechanisms driving gonadal asymmetry in avian species.

Materials and methods

Study approval

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care Committee of Guangxi University (approval no. GXU2018-058) and conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Agricultural Animals in Research and Teaching (https://www.asas.org/services/ag-guide).

Embryonic gonad sample preparation

Fertilized eggs of Guangxi Partridge chicken were obtained from Guangxi Jinling Husbandry Group Co., Ltd. The eggs were incubated at 37.5 °C and 60% relative humidity with rotation every 2 h. The right and left gonads were individually dissected from embryos at embryonic stages E6 (HH29) and E8 (HH34). Embryonic stages were identified according to incubation time and developmental features (Hamburger and Hamilton, 1951). Subsequently, they were preserved in MACS Tissue Storage Solution (OE Biotech, Shanghai, China) at 2°C–8°C. Simultaneously, a small piece of limb bud was collected for molecular sexing. Genomic DNA was extracted and used for PCR amplification of the sex-linked CHD1 gene. Due to intron length differences between the Z and W chromosomes, females exhibited two bands (∼450 and 600 bp) and males a single band (∼600 bp), allowing sex determination by gel electrophoresis, as previously described (Clinton et al., 2001). After gender determination, a total of 15 pooled gonadal samples per side were used for scRNA-seq (n = 1).

Single-cell RNA library preparation and sequencing

Fresh gonad tissues were collected and stored in MACS Tissue Storage Solution (OE Biotech, Shanghai, China). Single-cell suspensions were prepared at a density of 1 × 105 cells/mL in PBS (HyClone, USA). The suspensions were loaded onto microfluidic chips, and single-cell libraries were generated using the 10 x Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3′ v3.1 protocol (OE Biotech, Shanghai, China). Final libraries were sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq 6000.

Single-cell RNA-seq data processing

Raw reads were aligned to the chicken genome sequence (GRCg6a) with Cell Ranger v8.0.1. Reads with identical cell barcodes, UMIs, and genes were grouped to compute the number of UMIs for each gene in each cell. The resulting UMI count tables for each cellular barcode were utilized in subsequent analyses. For all samples, quality control was performed based on the number of detected genes per cell (nGene) and the number of unique molecular identifiers (nUMI), and the percentage of mitochondrial gene-derived UMIs (percent.mito). The quantified read count matrix was subjected to statistical quality control using Seurat 4.0.0 (Hao et al., 2021) in R. In the initial quality control process, the following filtering criteria were applied to reject low quality cells and genes: nGene < 200, nUMI < 1000, log10GenesPerUMI < 0.7, and percent.mito >25. After quality control, 47,750 cells remained for further analysis. Cell type identification and clustering analysis were conducted using the Seurat program. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between samples or consecutive clusters were identified using the FindAllMarkers function. The criteria for DEGs were set as |log2FC| > 0.5 and p < 0.05.

Functional annotation

The clusterProfiler R package (version1.4.1) was then employed for Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) function enrichment analysis, aiming to identify biological functions or pathways associated with DEGs. Terms with an p-value < 0.05 and at least two genes were considered significantly enriched.

Cell-cell communication inference

To comprehend global patterns of cell-to-cell communication in the gonad and unveil signaling events associated with successive cell lineages, the R package CellChat 1.4.1 was employed. Ligand-receptor interactions were utilized for visualizing intercellular communications derived from scRNA-seq datasets (Jin et al., 2021). Cell groups containing fewer than 10 cells were excluded, and only ligand–receptor interactions with p < 0.05 were considered significant.

Quantitative PCR

Total RNA from left and right gonadal PGCs was extracted with Trizol reagent (Life Technologies, USA). Total RNA samples were then reverse-transcribed into cDNA (Complementary DNA) using HiScript® III RT SuperMix (Vazyme, China), qPCR was performed in 20 μL reactions containing 2 μL cDNA, 0.5 μL primers, and 10 μL ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) on a real-time PCR system. The qPCR reaction was conducted as follows: 95°C for 30 s; 95°C for 5 s, annealing temperature for the 30 s for 35 cycles; finally, melting curve collection at 60 to 95°C. All reactions were run with three biological replicates (each with two technical replicates), using β‑actin as the reference gene. Gene expression levels were normalized to an the reference gene and calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method (Livak et al., 2001). And primer sequences are listed in Supplementary table S1 (synthesized by Sangon Biotech, China).

Statistical analysis

The relative gene expression data of qPCR were expressed as the mean ± SEM (Standard error of the mean) from three independent technical and and biological replicates. Differences between groups were assessed by a two-tailed Student’s t-test using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software V8.0), with a p-value < 0.05 deemed statistically significant (* p < 0.05, ​**​ p < 0.01).

Results

Single-cell atlas and annotation of gonadal cell populations

We profiled whole right and left gonads from female embryos at HH29 and HH34 using 10x Genomics Chromium Single Cell 3′v3.1. After quality control, 24,462 cells (89.4%) were retained from right side gonads and 23,288 cells (91.4%) were retained from left side gonads for downstream analyses (Fig. 1A). Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) separated the cells into 13 transcriptionally distinct clusters (c1–c13; Fig. 1B). Cluster identities were then assigned using established markers (Supplementary table S2). The germ-cell markers DAZL and DDX4 (VASA) were highly expressed in cluster 13, which was therefore designated as the germ-cell/PGCs cluster in chickens (Fig. 1C and D). Cluster 1 co-expressed interstitial markers (COL1A2, COL3A1, POSTIN, DCN) and epithelial markers (PITX2, KRT7, KRT18), consistent with an interstitial–epithelial lineage state. c2 expressed CYP19A1, OSR1 and HSD17B1 (pre-granulosa cells); c3 expressed SOX18 and CDH5 (endothelial cells); c4 expressed SOX9, HEMGN and DMRT1 (supporting cells). Additional clusters represented erythrocytes (c5: HBE1, HBAD, HBA1), mesothelial cells (c6: SMOC2, MID1, GATA2), mesonephroi (c8: EYA1, PAX2, FGFR3), glial/neuronal-like cells (c10: MASP1, TAGLN3, MOXD1), podocytes (c11: NPHS2, LMX1B) and kidney tubules (c12: MIOX, HNF1B), while c7 and c9 comprised extra-gonadal cell types. Gene expression levels of marker genes for each cell cluster were shown in supplementary figure S1. These annotations delineated the major somatic compartments that constitute the PGCs niche within the embryonic gonad. Interstitial-epithelial cells and pre-granulosa cells represented the dominant populations in gonad. Right–left PGCs differences at HH29 highlight DNA damage and cell‑cycle programs

To explore molecular differences between right and left gonadal PGCs, we performed differential expression analysis within the germ cell cluster, and subsequently subjected stage-specific DEGs to GO and KEGG enrichment analyses (adjusted p < 0.05). At HH29, 332 DEGs were identified between right and left PGCs (Fig. 2A and B). These DEGs were predominantly enriched in GO categories related to DNA damage response, signal transduction by p53 class mediator resulting in cell cycle arrest, structural constituent of ribosome, translation and translation elongation factor activity (Fig. 2C). Taken together, these GO enrichments indicated that right- and left-sided PGCs at HH29 differ mainly in genes associated with cell-cycle regulation, DNA damage response and protein synthesis. To further characterize these differences, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed for DEGs. At HH29, KEGG pathway analysis showed significant enrichment of ribosome, ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis, non-homologous end-joining, cell cycle and oxidative phosphorylation (Fig. 2D), with representative DEGs contributing to these pathways including G2 and S-phase expressed 1 (GTSE1), inorganic pyrophosphatase 1 (PPA1), ribosomal protein lateral stalk subunit P1 (RPLP1) and ribosomal protein S15a (RPS15A). These KEGG pathway enrichments indicated that right–left PGCs differences extend to genes assigned to protein synthesis, genome maintenance, cell-cycle control and mitochondrial energy metabolism. Right–left PGC differences at HH34 shift toward senescence‑associated programs

At HH34, 379 DEGs were detected between right and left PGCs (Fig. 3A and B). These DEGs were mainly enriched in GO biological processes such as cellular oxidant detoxification, protein heterooligomerization, organic acid binding and structural constituent of ribosome (Fig. 3C). These GO enrichments at HH34 show that right–left differences in PGCs involve genes related to oxidative stress handling, protein complex formation and ribosomal components. At this stage, KEGG pathway analysis identified significant enrichment of cellular senescence, cAMP signaling pathway and necroptosis (Fig. 3D), with representative DEGs such as inhibitor of DNA binding 2 (ID2), AP-1 transcription factor subunit (JUN), cyclin D1 (CCND1), growth arrest and DNA damage inducible alpha (GADD45A) and mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase 1 (MAP2K1). These KEGG results showed that right–left PGCs differences also involve genes annotated to pathways related to cell-cycle regulation and regulated cell death. Interactions mediated by the NOTCH and NCAM signaling pathways

Network centrality analysis of the NOTCH signaling pathway identified endothelial cells and supporting cells as major ligand-producing populations, with endothelial cells in the right gonad at both HH29 and HH34 predicted to form direct interactions with germ cells (Fig. 6A and B). In the NCAM pathway, mesothelial cells, interstitial–epithelial cells, and pre-granulosa cells were the principal ligand sources. At HH29, mesothelial cells dominated as the ligand-producing population targeting germ cells, whereas by HH34, additional interactions involving interstitial–epithelial cells, pre-granulosa cells, and even germ cells themselves were detected (Fig. 6C and D). Beyond these two pathways, further ligand–receptor analyses identified LAMININ (Fig. S2A–S2B) and IGF (Fig. S2C–S2D) as well as FGF (Fig. S2E) and CADM (Fig. S2F) signaling routes connecting somatic cells with PGCs. The ligands of these pathways were mainly produced by mesothelial, interstitial–epithelial, pre-granulosa, supporting, and endothelial cells. Validation of scRNA-seq data by qPCR in right and left gonadal PGCs

To validate the scRNA-seq results, we selected 10 DEGs (GTSE, PPA1, RPLP1, RPS15A, ID2, JUN, GADD45A, CCND1, MAP2K1, COL1A1) between right and left gonadal PGCs at HH29 and HH34 for qPCR analysis (Fig. 7A). At HH29, GTSE1 and PPA1, which are associated with cell-cycle progression and metabolic activity, were significantly up-regulated in right-side gonadal PGCs compared with left-side, whereas the ribosome-related genes RPLP1 and RPS15A were down-regulated (Fig. 7B). At HH34, the genes ID2, GADD45A, CCND1, COL1A1, JUN and MAP2K1 were up-regulated in right-side gonadal PGCs (Fig. 7C). Among these, GADD45A, CCND1, JUN, MAP2K1 and ID2 were involved in DNA damage response, cell-cycle regulation and cell differentiation, while COL1A1 was associated with extracellular matrix organization. Discussion

Isolation and in vitro culture of PGCs and gonadal differentiation are popular topics in avian genetic breeding research. As the precursors of spermatogonia and oogonia, PGCs play a very important role in the preservation of rare breeds and germline chimeras. Avian gonads constitute the final colonization sites of PGCs. In females of most avian species, only the left gonad typically develops into a functional ovary, whereas the right gonad undergoes regression during embryonic development (Tan et al., 2024). In addition to morphological asymmetry of the gonads, several genes are implicated in developmental asymmetry and right gonad degeneration. BMP7 expression is asymmetrical in the undifferentiated gonads (Hoshino et al., 2005), whereas the estrogen receptor (ER) is present in the left cortex of both sexes but absent from the right (Gong et al., 2012). Functionally, PITX2 has been shown to regulate this asymmetric gonadal development in chicks and can prevent the right gonad from undergoing degeneration (Li et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2025).

However, it remains unclear whether this asymmetry leads to distinct fates or contributions for the PGCs that colonize the right and left gonadal ridges. In this study, we conducted scRNA-seq to analyze embryonic gonadal PGCs of chicken at stage HH 29 and stage HH34, with the goal of elucidating the mechanisms underlying asymmetric gonadal development by identifying DEGs and pathways between right and left gonadal PGCs.

The scRNA-seq analysis and gene expression verification during stage HH34 revealed that the expression of ID2, JUN, GADD45A, CCND1, MAP2K1 and COL1A1 are upregulated in right gonadal PGCs. These genes are involved in pathways that suppress cell proliferation and differentiation. This findings suggested that apoptosis and cellular senescence in right gonadal PGCs at stage HH34 were primarily associated with cell cycle arrest. At HH29, a time when gonadal morphogenesis commences, the DEGs in PGCs of the right and left gonads were primarily associated with cell cycle progression and ribosomal development such as GTSE1, PPA1. These results suggested that gonadal morphogenesis diverged from HH29, during which PGCs in the right gonad of female chicken embryos displayed physiological responses such as cell-cycle arrest, in contrast to PGCs in the left gonad. As embryonic development progresses to HH34, when marked morphological differences between the gonads become apparent, these PGCs progressively undergo senescence and apoptosis. Comparison of right-side gonadal PGCs between developmental stages revealed dynamic transcriptional changes during gonadal development. Pathway enrichment in steroid hormone biosynthesis, estrogen signaling, and ovarian steroidogenesis indicates a growing influence of steroidogenic cues, while enrichment in ribosome, oxidative phosphorylation, and cell cycle pathways reflects concurrent shifts in metabolic and proliferative activity during asymmetric ovarian development.

Furthermore, communication analysis revealed that the dynamic shift in predicted cell–cell interactions between the right and left gonads reflects asymmetric gonadal development. At HH29, the higher number of interactions in the right gonad may support early tissue organization, whereas the reduced interactions by HH34 suggest diminished signaling activity, consistent with regression of the right gonad and continued differentiation of the left gonad. In addition, we identified approximately 11 signaling pathways mediating interactions between germ cells and other gonadal cell types. The cell types primarily targeting germ cells included pre-granulosa cells, supporting cells, interstitial-epithelial cells, mesothelial cells, and endothelial cells. These cells may play a crucial role in regulating the microenvironment of germ cells within the gonads. A previous study found that from E6 to E12, PITX2 expression levels were dramatically higher in the left gonad than the right (Peng et al., 2023), PITX2 and CCND1 interacted synergistically, enabling gonadal somatic cells to regulate the survival and proliferation of PGCs (Rodríguez-León et al., 2008). In this study, we discovered that CCND1 was upregulated in right-side gonadal PGCs during the stage HH34. Our findings suggested that the asymmetric development of female chicken embryonic gonads leaded somatic cells in the right gonad to differentially regulated PGCs survival and proliferation, which in turn contributed to the observed increases in PGCs senescence and apoptosis on the right side. Moreover, communication analysis showed that NCAM pathway suggested that mesothelial cells initially serve as the primary source of ligands targeting germ cells. Over time, the interaction network becomes more complex, with interstitial–epithelial cells, pre-granulosa cells, and even germ cells themselves contributing to reciprocal signaling, reflecting a progressively diversified communication landscape during gonadal development. NOTCH-mediated interactions between gonadal somatic cells and germ cells were detected exclusively in the right gonad of female embryos at both HH29 and HH34, but were absent in the left gonad, suggesting a right-specific microenvironmental regulation that may contribute to lateralized ovarian development. These observations suggested that NOTCH- and NCAM-mediated interactions follow distinct but developmentally dynamic patterns of signaling between germ cells and surrounding somatic populations in the embryonic gonad. Prior study has noted that NOTCH1 was highly expressed in male PGCs (Jin et al., 2020). NOTCH-mediated somatic–PGC interactions were detected only in the right gonad at both developmental stages. This finding suggested that niche signaling differed between the right and left gonads and may play an important role in PGCs maintenance. Endothelial and supporting cells were prominent NOTCH‑ligand sources, while mesothelial, interstitial–epithelial and pre‑granulosa populations contributed to additional pathways (NCAM, LAMININ, IGF/FGF, CADM). These signals converge on cell adhesion, extracellular matrix, and growth‑factor axes that can influence germ‑cell survival and competence. The right‑specific NOTCH pattern echoes prior observations that NOTCH components are linked to germ‑cell states in avian systems, whether this reflects a transient, male‑like microenvironmental feature on the right side remains to be clarified experimentally.

In addition, there were significant differences in the interaction modes (direct contact or paracrine) and ligand sources of germ cells across different signaling pathways, with lateral asymmetry observed in cell communication within some pathways. Despite considerable progress achieved in elucidating the differences between right-left gonadal PGCs and the underlying genetic and molecular mechanisms, numerous questions remain unanswered. Future study will delve into the roles of gene-protein regulation, cell interactions, and epigenetic modifications to deepen our understanding of PGCs development and the interactions between PGCs and the gonadal microenvironment.

Conclusion

Single‑cell transcriptomics of female chicken embryonic gonads reveals that right-left gonadal PGCs differences were dominated by DNA damage and cell‑cycle programs at HH29 and transition toward senescence‑associated programs by HH34. Inference of cell–cell communication highlights right‑specific NOTCH interactions between somatic compartments and PGCs across both stages. These findings defined stage‑dependent and side‑specific features of the PGCs transcriptome and its microenvironment, providing a basis for mechanistic tests of how niche signals and stress pathways shape germ‑cell fate during avian ovarian asymmetry.
