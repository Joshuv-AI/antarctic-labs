AcousticSceneBD: A real-world environmental audio dataset for acoustic scene classification in Bangladesh
Authors: Tasnova Haque Mazumder, Akash Ahmed, Dilruba Akter, Amin Ocin, Raihan Ul Islam, Mohammad Rifat Ahmmad Rashid, Shamim H Ripon, Ahmed Wasif Reza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnvironmental sound classification (ESC) has been used to implement various applications such as smart city, environmental monitoring systems, context-aware systems, and assistive technologies. The existing benchmark datasets, however, mainly focus on the acoustic environment of the west and limited acoustic scenes are available in Bangladesh. In this paper, we introduce a real-world environmental audio dataset named AcousticSceneBD, with a total of 5535 recordings across 7 classes of acoustic scenes: Bus, Metro, Metro Station, Park, Restaurant, Shopping Mall, and University. All recordings were conducted using the standard operating conditions of the smartphone's microphone and with all files standardized following the same procedure:10-second, mono, 16-bit WAV audio recorded at 44.1/48 kHz, with a total duration of 15.38 hours and a dataset size of 4.87 GB.To guarantee consistency and reproducibility, the dataset has been created using a structured approach for data collection and processing consisting of eight stages. Four baseline models, namely Random Forest, YAMNet, Wav2Vec2, and PANNs CNN14 were tested to validate the data, with respective test accuracy of 58.81%, 82.99%, 47.11%, and 78.15%, where Random forest is trained on extracted MFCC features from the raw audio The results indicate that AcousticSceneBD is well annotated, acoustically discriminative and appropriate for the development and evaluation of traditional, deep learning, transfer learning, and self-supervised environmental sound classification methods.Keywords: Acoustic dataset, Environmental sound classification, Audio signal processing, Deep learning, Urban soundscape, Transfer learning, Artificial intelligence, Bangladesh

Background

The field of environmental sound classification (ESC) has become an essential research area in the field of audio signal processing and artificial intelligence, finding applications in smart cities, environmental monitoring, surveillance systems, context-aware computing, and assistive technologies. Intelligent systems can better understand and adapt to the environment when automatically recognizing acoustic environments from short audio recordings.

There are a number of datasets of environmental audio that have been introduced for the benefit of ESC research (Table 1), but they share some limitations. The majority of the publicly available datasets are dominated by recordings from North American and European areas, with acoustic environments for Bangladesh being under-represented. Consequently, sound attributes of local transportation systems, commercial facilities, educational facilities, and public recreational areas are rarely provided in benchmarks. In addition, a large proportion of the datasets are based on crowdsourced recordings collected from websites, and these may result in variations in quality of the recording, reliability of the annotations, recording equipment, and environment in which they are recorded. Moreover, there are some datasets with variable-length audio files which need further segmentation, padding, or pre-processing before they can be successfully used in state-of-the-art machine-learning applications, which may have an impact on reproducibility or benchmarking consistency.

To overcome these shortcomings, we have created AcousticSceneBD, a real-world environmental sounds dataset gathered from a variety of daily life environments in Bangladesh. We have 5535 standardized and cut 10-second audio clips from seven acoustic scenes: Bus, Metro, Metro Station, Restaurant, Shopping Mall, University, and Park. All recordings were taken under natural operating conditions in the real environment using a smartphone microphone, which was able to record the real environment's sounds and acoustic variability.

AcousticSceneBD is a helpful resource for environmental sound classification, audio representation learning, transfer learning and self-supervised learning research. In addition, the dataset can provide future applications in smart city, environmental surveillance, context-aware audio systems and assistive listening technologies.

Data Description

The AcousticSceneBD dataset consists of 5,535 audio samples arranged in the following folders: Bus, Metro, Metro Station, Restaurant, Shopping Mall, University, and Park each containing audio samples by class. All recordings are recorded for 10.0 seconds so that the recordings are all the same length and do not need to be segmented or padded into the machine learning pipelines. All recordings were made in real-world situations with the built-in mic of the Samsung and Realme phones. To account for natural variability in the device and to represent realistic recording characteristics, multiple smartphone models were used. All audio files recorded are 16-bit, mono, lossless PCM WAVs at either 44.1 kHz or 48 kHz sample rate due to different device recording configurationsThis may lead to some differences in the spectral features as the baseline inputs were resampled to match the requirements of each model. The released files and metadata contain the original sampling rates to ensure the data can be reproduced.The average file size is about 0.88 MB, the size of all the files on disk is about 4,866.39 MB (4.87 GB), and the total duration is about 922.50 minutes (15.38 hours).

The dataset was limited to 7 classes to emphasize more diverse and representative real-world recordings of common soundscapes in Bangladesh, rather than aiming to match the number of classes found in existing benchmark datasets. Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Data collection pipeline

The data collection process was broken down into an eight stage process: (i) site selection across the 7 target classes, (ii) requesting access and calibration of the smartphones, (iii) field recording sessions, (iv) raw data collection of audio, (v) quality filtering, (vi) class labelling and class verification, (vii) 10-second segmentation, and (viii) format standardisation prior to final release summarized in Fig. 5.

End-to-end data-collection and processing pipeline for the environmental sound dataset.

Site selection

Acoustic variability within classes and coverage of the seven target classes was maximized in the selection of sites. To ensure that the data reflected real-world variation in devices, time of day and crowd density (avoiding just the acoustic response of a single location), multiple recording locations were chosen for each class (e.g., multiple bus routes and bus stops for the Bus class, multiple metro stations and trains for Metro class and Metro Station class, multiple parks, restaurants, malls, and university buildings for other classes).

Annotation workflow and inter-annotator agreement

The annotation process started while collecting data, and the audio recordings were taken directly from 7 real-world acoustic environments: Bus, Metro, Metro Station, Park, Restaurant, Shopping Mall, and University. Each of the recorded audio files was then inspected, by listening to the acoustics of the recording, and labeled with the relevant scene label, depending on the environment in which the recording was made and on the predominant sounds in the audio file. A random sub-sample of 350 audio clips (50 audio clips from each of the seven classes) was selected for the purpose of assessing the reliability of the annotations. All selected clips were played to two annotators who independently labelled them according to the pre-defined scene categories. To not influence the other annotator, independent annotation was used. There were 350 clips with a raw inter-annotator agreement of 64.29%, between the two annotators. In order to correct for chance agreement, Cohen's kappa (κ) was also computed. The inter-annotator agreement was found at a moderate level of 0.5829 with the obtained value. The manual annotations were also matched with the existing set of data labels. The percentage of agreement between Annotator 1 and the rest of the community was 71.14%, and between Annotator 2 and the rest of the community was 74.57%. The majority of the disagreements were found between acoustically similar environments and when sounds overlap, such as voices in conversations, crowd noise, music or announcements.

Validation with baseline models

Four different baseline models were trained and tested to ensure that the data set is well labeled and learnable. The four baselines are (i) classical feature engineering with a Random Forest (RF), (ii) supervised transfer learning from a model pre-trained on a large-scale general audio corpus (YAMNet), (iii) self-supervised speech embeddings (Wav2Vec2), and (iv) end-to-end audio-domain CNN pre-training (PANNs CNN14). To prevent data leakage caused by acoustic overlap across continuous recording sessions, the dataset was partitioned strictly at the recording-session level rather than splitting individual clips. Recording sessions within each class were stratified and assigned to train, validation, or test partitions targeting a 70/10/20 ratio, after which all clips belonging to each session were merged to form the final datasets. Holding out complete, un-overlapped recording sessions yielded 3,032 clips for training, 1,221 clips for validation, and 1,282 clips for testing (out of 5,535 total clips). This structure ensured that no temporally adjacent or acoustically shared background audio contaminated the evaluation splits.. The following table (Table 7) shows supports provided per class.

Random forest on MFCC features

For every clip, we calculated 40 dimensional MFCCs on 25 ms of windows with a 10 ms hop step, then summarized each of the 40 MFCCs coefficients with four statistics (mean, std, max, min) that gave us 160 dimensions, plus delta and delta-delta statistics to give us a final feature vector of 177 dimensions. This feature matrix was used to train a Random Forest classifier with n_estimators = 200, with no depth limit applied, and random_state = 42. Test accuracy = 58.81%. Low-order MFCC means and standard deviations (Fig. 6) dominate the most important features.

Top-30 random forest feature importances. Low-order MFCC means and standard deviations dominate.

YAMNet + MLP (transfer learning)

YAMNet [7]. is a mobile version of a pre-trained model on a large-scale general set of audio data, which generates 1024-dimensional embedding every ∼0.96 s frame. For each clip we resampled audio to 16 kHz mono and mean-pooled YAMNet frame embeddings to obtain a single 1024-dimensional vector. The model was trained using Adam (learning rate 1e-3), categorical cross-entropy, ReduceLROnPlateau and EarlyStopping (patience 10) with batch size 32 for a maximum of 50 epochs. The training curves in Fig. 7 yield an accuracy of 82.99% for the test.

YAMNet+MLP training accuracy and loss curves over 50 epochs.

Wav2Vec2 + MLP (self-supervised representations)

Wav2Vec2 [8]. is a self Supervised Speech Encoder. After resampling each clip to 16 kHz mono, we extracted 768 dimensional embeddings from each clip using the publicly available wav2vec2-base-960h checkpoint, by taking the mean of the embeddings over time. The embeddings were normally scaled and passed into the 3-layer MLP head as was done in YAMNet. The accuracy of the test set = 47.11% (training curves in Fig. 8). The lower accuracy of Wav2Vec2 (47.11%) indicates a possible domain mismatch, because the Wav2Vec2 model was mainly trained on speech representations (LibriSpeech, not on environmental sounds. The results presented here suggest that speech-based models may need much more fine-tuning on domain-specific acoustic scenes to get a good grasp of environmental acoustics, but it is hoped that this hypothesis can be further tested by controlled transfer experiments (such as cross-dataset pretraining).

Wav2Vec2+MLP training accuracy and loss curves.

PANNs CNN14 + MLP (end-to-end audio deep learning)

PANNs CNN14 [9]. is a 14-layer CNN pre-trained on a large-scale general audio corpus, with log-mel spectrogram inputs. We used 64-band log-mel spectrograms, computed during training using SpecAugment [10]. and 2048-dimensional penultimate-layer embeddings, sampled at 32 kHz. The same 3-layer MLP classification head was added. The accuracy of the tests is 78.15% (training curves in Fig. 9).

PANNs CNN14+MLP training accuracy and loss curves.
