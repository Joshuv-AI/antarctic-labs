Effects of chronic mild heat stress on reproductive performance, ovarian development, and HPO axis hormone secretion in broiler breeders
Authors: Guangju Wang, Zhenwu Huang, Minhong Zhang, Henry van den Brand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeat stress poses a major challenge in poultry production, yet little is known about its chronic effects in broiler breeders. In this study, 144 broiler breeders were exposed to 32°C of chronic heat stress, and effects on egg production, physiological parameters and reproductive hormones, organ weights, and follicle counts were evaluated. The results showed that the heat stress significantly reduced egg production, average egg weight, uterus weight, small yellow follicle (SYF) and large white follicle (LWF) counts; and also reduced serum triiodothyronine (T3), serum total cholesterol (TC), and increased liver triglycerides (TG). Moreover, hypothalamic and serum luteinizing hormone (LH), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), estradiol (E2) levels were suppressed. These results suggest that mild chronic maternal heat stress impairs reproductive performance through an integrated mechanism involving, metabolic adjustments, hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian (HPO) axis suppression, and impaired follicular and hepatic function. This study provides a mechanistic basis for understanding heat-induced reproductive impairment and its potential impact on offspring.Keywords: Mild thermal stress, Broiler breeder, HPO axis hormone, Egg production, Follicle development

Introduction

In the broiler production chain, broiler breeders represent a critical and indispensable link, as their reproductive performance directly determines the availability and quality of commercial broiler chicks (Chang et al., 2016). Optimal egg production and appropriate body weight maintenance in broiler breeders are essential for ensuring sufficient fertile egg output and sustaining the efficiency of the entire broiler production chain (Heijmans et al., 2021, 2022; Bouzari et al., 2025). Egg production and body weight are tightly regulated by endocrine homeostasis and are highly sensitive to environmental and management-related stressors (Saino et al., 2005; Aslam et al., 2013).

With global climate change and local heat waves, more extreme climatic conditions have become increasingly frequent, and prolonged periods of high ambient temperatures are now commonly observed in many regions (Clarke et al., 2022; Yan et al., 2026). As a consequence, heat stress has emerged as a major environmental challenge for poultry production worldwide, resulting in substantial economic losses (Mangan and Siwek, 2024) and reduced welfare (Fathima et al., 2022; Yan et al., 2025). Heat stress severely impairs physiological function and production performance in broilers and laying hens (Tang et al., 2022; Kim and Lee, 2023; Madkour et al., 2024). However, compared with broilers and laying hens, effects of heat stress on broiler breeders remain relatively underexplored. In broiler breeders, heat stress has been reported to reduce egg production, body weight, large and small yellow follicle count, liver weight, and abdominal fat pad (Pasri et al., 2025), egg weight and egg quality (Wang et al., 2025), and antioxidative capacity (Pasri et al., 2024). However, most studies in broiler breeders focused on effects of acute heat stress with relatively high temperatures for a relatively short period (Xie et al., 2015; Pasri et al., 2024, 2025). The impact of a more chronic mild heat stress for a prolonged period on broiler breeder reproductive performance has received limited attention (Xie et al., 2015).

Heat exposure in poultry can inhibit reproductive performance, especially the follicle development, by disrupting the HPO axis hormone secretion (Lu et al., 2021; Yan et al., 2022) and by suppressing feed intake and subsequent nutrient availability (Rozenboim et al., 2007; Liu et al., 2014). The HPO axis is a key regulatory pathway involved in controlling follicle development and follicle number, and its activity can be altered by changes in feed intake and energy level (Hadinia et al., 2020; Xin et al., 2022; Peng et al., 2025). Prolonged mild heat stress triggered systemic metabolic perturbations, including abnormal lipid metabolism (altered serum triglyceride and cholesterol levels) (Guo et al., 2021), disrupted energy homeostasis (Mujahid et al., 2009; Algothmi et al., 2024), and elevated circulating corticosterone (Nawab et al., 2018), a major stress hormone that can cross-talk with the HPO axis to inhibit gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) and LH secretion in chicken (Rettenbacher et al., 2013; Ahmed et al., 2014). Metabolic imbalances further compromise ovarian follicle development and impair the synthesis of E2 and P4 (Progesterone) that are essential for follicle maturation and ovulation (Henriksen et al., 2011; Kavtarashvili, 2021).

As indicated, most studies to heat stress in broiler breeders has focused on acute or short-term stress, but effects of chronic mild heat stress on reproductive performance and HPO axis function in broiler breeders are still poorly understood. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the impact of chronic mild heat stress on reproductive and metabolic characteristics, including HPO axis hormone secretion in broiler breeders. We hypothesized that chronic mild heat stress impairs reproductive performance and ovarian function of broiler breeders through direct alterations in endocrine and metabolic homeostasis.

Material and methods

Experimental design, animal and housing

This study was approved by the Institutional Committee on Experimental Animal Welfare and Ethics of the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences in Beijing (Permit Number: IAS 2024-183). The experiment was designed as a single-factor experiment using 144 Hubbard Efficiency Plus broiler breeder hens, all 43 weeks old at the beginning of the experiment. The broiler breeders were obtained from the Great Wall Breeding Company (Tangshan, China). Upon arrival, broiler breeder hens were housed in climate-controlled chambers (see below) at 20 °C for a two-week adaptation period to the new housing conditions. Thereafter, they were randomly assigned to one of two treatments: a thermal neutral (TN) group and a heat stress (HS) group for eight consecutive weeks. The TN broiler breeder hens were housed at a constant ambient temperature of 20°C, whereas HS broiler breeder hens were housed at a constant ambient temperature of 32°C.

During the experiment, broiler breeder hens were housed in one of four climate-controlled chambers (4 m × 3 m each). Within every chamber, four floor pens (2 m × 1.5 m) were created, making a total of 16 pens. Two chambers were allocated to the TN treatment, and the other two chambers were used for the HS treatment. Six pens per treatment were used for the experiment (12 hens per pen, 72 hens per treatment and 144 hens in total), while the remaining pens held surplus broiler breeder hens (4 hens per pen; 8 hens per treatment; 16 hens in total) (Fig. 1). Each pen was equipped with a drinking line, containing 5 nipples to ensure continuous access to water. A commercial available Hubbard Efficiency Plus breeder mash diet (ME = 2804 kcal/kg; CP = 16.07%) was offered once daily in two 0.85 m-long trough feeders per pen at a restricted ration of approximately 165 g per hen per day. Four laying nests (40 cm × 25 cm × 40 cm; L × W × H) were provided in each pen. Floors were covered with a 3 cm layer of wood shavings, which was refreshed weekly. All chambers were run under an 18 L: 6 D photoperiod (40 lux) using cool-white fluorescent lights, with darkness from 24:00 to 06:00, and relative humidity was kept constant at 60%.

Overview of the experimental design and the layout and dimensions of the chambers.

Broiler breeder performance

Feed intake was recorded at pen level daily. To eliminate potential impact of different feed intakes on broiler breeder performance between the two treatments, feed intake of the TN broiler breeder hens was paired with the previous day’s feed intake of the HS broiler breeder hens. The body weight was recorded individually every 2 weeks. Eggs per pen were collected daily and weighed. Laying percentage was calculated as the number of eggs produced divided by the number of broiler breeder hens present per pen. Core body temperature of all broiler breeder hens was measured every 2 weeks using a veterinary rectal thermometer (Huachu, Xinxiang, China).

Sample collection

Each pen served as a replicate and was considered as an experimental unit. At weeks 4 and 8 after the start of the treatments, all broiler breeder hens were subjected to an overnight fast. The next morning, two broiler breeder hens per pen were selected that did not differ in BW from the group average. Blood samples (5 mL) were collected from the wing vein using vacutainer needles, after which the broiler breeder hens were euthanized by inhalation of pure carbon dioxide. Immediately after collection, blood samples were placed on ice till arrival at the laboratory and subsequently centrifuged at 1300 g for 10 min at 4°C. The resulting serum was harvested and stored at −80°C till further processing.

Following euthanasia, the hypothalamus, liver, ovary, spleen, abdominal fat pad, duodenum, jejunum, ileum and all segments of the oviduct (infundibulum, magnum, isthmus, and uterus) were carefully isolated, trimmed of surface fat and connective tissue, and weighed. The total length of the duodenum, jejunum and ileum was measured using a tailoring tape. Approximately 5 g of hypothalamus and liver were collected, snap-frozen immediately in liquid nitrogen, and subsequently stored at −80°C until further analyses. The number of large yellow follicle (LYF) and the diameter and weight of the F1 follicle were evaluated. In week 8 after the start of the treatments, an intact ovary containing all visible follicles from one broiler breeder hens in each pen was isolated and immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for subsequent follicle counting.

Follicle counting

In week 8 after the start of the treatments, twelve ovaries were examined on follicle classification and counts (6 ovaries per treatment), following the procedure described by (Singh et al., 2013), with minor modifications. After fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde, each ovary was carefully dissected to expose all surface follicles. Follicles were examined using a bench-top illuminated magnifier to enhance visualization; no microscope was used. Follicles were gently separated with fine forceps, and their diameters were measured using a digital caliper (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Based on diameter and color, follicles were categorized into LYF (>10 mm), SYF (5–10 mm), LWF (3–5 mm), and small white follicles (SWF; <1–3 mm). All counts were performed by the same trained operator.

Biochemical and hormone measurement

TC and TG levels were determined in serum and liver, using commercial assay kits obtained from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). Uric acid (UA), T3, thyroxine (T4) and corticosterone (CORT) levels were determined in serum, using corresponding kits from the same manufacturer, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Serum and hypothalamic levels of P4, E2, LH, FSH, and gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) were quantified, using ELISA kits (Dogesce, Beijing, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Hypothalamic tissues were homogenized in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) using a mechanical bead mixer (GeneRadar, Xiamen, China). The homogenates were then centrifuged at 3000 × g for 5 min at 4°C to collect the supernatant. For hormone quantification, appropriate volumes of serum or hypothalamic supernatant were added to the ELISA plate wells, followed by the addition of specific antibodies and substrate according to the kit protocol. All incubation steps were performed for fixed durations as specified in the kit protocol. Thereafter, the color reaction was developed and the optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek, California, USA). Hormone concentrations were calculated based on the standard curve provided in the kit.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis were conducted in SAS 9.4 (SAS institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Data was tested on homogeneity of variance of means and residuals prior to analysis. Parameters that were not normally distributed were log-transformed prior to analysis. Data of these parameters (infundibulum weight at week 4, spleen weight at week 8) is provided as untransformed data combined with the P-values obtained from the transformed data.

Egg weight and laying percentage were determined on daily basis, but statistical analysis was performed on week basis. Results

Broiler breeder performance

Body weight

The body weight of the broiler breeder hens was higher in the HS group than in the TN group throughout the whole experiment (4191 vs 4013 g on average, P = 0.001; Fig. 2). No effects of week (P = 0.97) or interaction between treatment and week (P = 0.19) were found.

Feed intake

Broiler breeder hens of both treatments were pair-fed, meaning their feed intake did not differ throughout the whole experimental period, with an average feed intake 156 g/d in both treatments.

Body core temperature

The body core temperature showed an interaction between treatment and week after the start of the treatments (P < 0.001; Fig. 3), resulting in higher body temperature in the HS group compared to the TN group in week 4 (Δ = 1.1 °C; P < 0.001) and 6 (Δ = 0.9 °C; P = 0.01) after the start of the treatments.

Effects of temperature treatment of broiler breeders (TN = thermal neutral treatment (20°C constant for 8 weeks) or HS = heat stress treatment (32°C constant for 8 weeks)) on body core temperature (LSmeans; pooled SEM = 0.10).

Egg production, egg weight, and follicle count

Laying rate showed an interaction between treatment and week (P = 0.05). For both treatments, laying rate decreased with week, but the decline was more severe in the HS treatment than in the TN treatment (Fig. 4A), resulting in significant differences in weeks 6 (Δ = 16.4%; P = 0.01), 7 (Δ = 17.9%; P = 0.01), and 8 (Δ = 18.6%; P = 0.01). Egg weight also showed an interaction between treatment and week (P < 0.001; Fig. 4B). Egg weight of the TN treatment remained more or less stable throughout the experiment, but egg weight of the HS treatment declined with age. This resulted in significant differences in egg weight at week 3 (Δ = 2.9 g; P = 0.05), 4 (Δ = 3.7 g; P < 0.001), 5 (Δ = 3.7 g; P = 0.01), 6 (Δ = 4.3 g; P < 0.001), 7 (Δ = 4.0 g; P < 0.001) and 8 (Δ = 5.7 g; P < 0.001).

At week 8 after the start of the treatments, the numbers of LWF (Δ = 6.9; P = 0.05) and SYF (Δ = 5.1; P = 0.05) in the HS group were significantly lower than those in the TN group. No differences were found in the number of SWF (P = 0.42) and LYF (P = 0.26) (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Chronic mild heat stress in broiler breeders has received relatively little attention in literature, although it may constrain reproductive efficiency under commercial conditions. In the current study, we evaluated egg production, physiological status, and HPO axis hormones in broiler breeders during and after 8 weeks of chronic heat stress. Overall, a significant decline in reproductive performance was observed, accompanied by disruptions in endocrine homeostasis, including altered levels of stress-related hormones and reproductive hormones. However, effects on reproductive, metabolic and digestive organ weights were limited.

Body weight

In the current study, the body weight of the broiler breeders was different between the two treatments. This difference in body weight was already present at the start of the experiment and remained throughout the experiment. The body weight of the TN group remained constant during the course of the experiment, while the body weight of the HS group increased slightly. The latter contradict with other studies in which a decrease in body weight was observed during heat exposure (Donoghue et al., 1990; Barrett et al., 2019). This difference is probably due to the fact that in the current study, broiler breeder hens in both treatments were pair-fed, which was not the case in other studies. During heat stress, broiler breeder hens normally eat less and consequently the body weight is decreasing. The slight increase in body weight observed in heat-stressed broiler breeder hens may be attributed to the marked reduction in egg production (both laying rate and egg weight), which could have resulted in a reallocation of energy from egg formation to body tissue deposition (Rajkumar et al., 2021; Teofilo et al., 2023).

Reproductive functioning

Reproduction of laying hens has been shown to be negatively affected by elevated ambient temperatures, as characterized by changes in oocyte quality, eggshell quality, fertility, egg production, and egg weight (Yan et al., 2022; de Arruda Roque et al., 2022; Kong et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). The egg production in the HS group was significantly lower from week 6 to week 8 after the start of the treatments than in the TN group, but the decline in laying percentage already started shortly after exposure to elevated temperatures and the same pattern was observed for egg weight. This result is consistent with other studies. For example, Liao et al. (2018)) found that laying rate in broiler breeders dropped from 74.1% to 62% and the average egg weight decreased from 63.1 g to 58.1 g during 9 weeks continuous exposure to 32°C. However, the drop in laying rate and egg weight appeared to be more severe in the current study than in literature. This difference might be explained by the different ages of the flocks. The current study started with broiler breeder hens from 47 weeks of age, whereas Liao et al. (2018)) started from 30 weeks of age, which means just the peak performance of broiler breeders.

The lower laying rate was also reflected in the lower number of LWF and SYF in the HS group, which indicate a diminished pool of available follicles for ovulation after heat stress exposure. This reduction suggests that heat stress impairs follicular development and recruitment (Li et al., 2020). The physiological pathways involved in this impaired follicular development and recruitment can probably be found in the HPO axis. In line with the observed reduction in follicle numbers and egg production, the present study demonstrated that heat stress significantly suppressed the key hormones of the HPO axis, including hypothalamic FSH and E2, and LH, FSH and E2 in the serum. GnRH acts as the primary neuroendocrine signal regulating LH and FSH secretion (Clarke et al., 1986) and heat stress might disrupt the secretion of GnRH from the hypothalamus, which in turn lowers the levels of circulating LH and FSH (Shamhari et al., 2021). Consequently, the observed decrease in these gonadotropins impairs follicular development and steroidogenesis in the ovary, ultimately resulting in lower egg production (Ma et al., 2024). The decrease in E2 further confirms a compromised ovarian function, which aligns with the diminished pool of LWF and SYF observed under heat stress.

Regarding the reproductive organ weights, the current study indicated that these (both absolute and relative to body weight) were hardly affected by the heat exposure of the broiler breeder hens. This lack of significant effects might be related to the limited number of replicates per treatment, which might be too limited to obtain differences in robust parameters, like reproductive organ weights. In week 8 after the start of the treatments, the absolute uterus weight in the HS group was higher than in the TN group and the same tendency was found for the relative uterus weight. This might be due to the induction of regression of uterus through decreased cellular proliferation and increased tissue degeneration (Postnikoff and Harkness, 2012).

Metabolic functioning

In the context of heat stress, CORT serves as a core physiological parameter. In the present study, CORT tended to be elevated in the HS group, which indicate activation of the HPA axis (Zulkifli et al., 2011). T3 and T4 are key thyroid hormones that regulate basal metabolism, thermogenesis, and energy homeostasis in animals under heat stress (Chen et al., 2022). Under heat stress, T3 and T4 levels typically decrease to reduce metabolic heat production to improve the body’s adaptability to high-temperature environments (Nasr et al., 2021), consistent with findings in the current study. The reduction of T3 could also be due to lower feed intake to keep a lower metabolic rate and to save more energy (Wang et al., 2026), but in the current study, broiler breeder hens were pair-fed, meaning that effects on T3 were fully due to heat stress. In addition, total TC and TG serve as important markers for evaluating metabolic disorders induced by heat stress (Liu et al., 2024). The reduction of TC in serum and higher TG in the liver suggest a redistribution of lipid metabolism, characterized by increased lipid deposition in the liver and reduced circulating lipid levels (Li et al., 2021). Notably, the higher liver weight observed in week 8 after the start of the treatments in the HS group suggests a shift in energy partitioning under heat stress, which has been reported to disrupt hepatic lipid metabolism, promoting fat accumulation, resulting in greater energy storage as fat (Cui et al., 2022). However, already in week 4 after the start of the treatments, the relative liver weight tended to be higher in the HS group, which indicates the fat deposition in the liver is starting rapidly after exposure to heat stress. In addition, in week 8 after the start of the treatments, the absolute duodenum and jejunum weight also showed a trend to lower values in the HS group, which may be attributed to heat stress induced redistribution of blood flow away from the gastrointestinal tract, which can impair intestinal integrity and nutrient absorption (Savioli et al., 2022).

Overall mechanism

Based on above results, the findings of the current study point towards an integrated response to chronic heat stress involving endocrine, metabolic and reproductive alterations. Interestingly, most of parameters showed the same direction, with almost all organs showed (numerically) lower weights in the HS group compared to the TN group.

On the one hand, the altered reproductive organ weights were paralleled by (numerically) lower levels of GnRH, LH, FSH, E2 and P4. These hormones are closely related to the reduction in reproductive functioning, which is consistent with the observed decline in egg production and egg weight. The reductions in egg weight and egg production can in most studies also be attributed to the limited nutrition available and absorption in the intestine, due to lower feed intake (Xin et al., 2024). However, in the current study, broiler breeder hens were pair-fed, suggesting that observed effects of heat stress are directly linked to the heat stress. This direct effect appears to be attributed to disruption of the HPO axis, leading to altered secretion of reproductive hormones that are essential for follicle growth and maturation (Li et al., 2023). In addition, altered energy balance under heat stress may further limit the energy available for follicular development (Park et al., 2023). Furthermore, the oxidative stress induced by heat stress may negatively affect ovarian function, thereby reducing follicle survival and progression (Adewunmi et al., 2026). On the other hand, the increased liver weight and reduction of intestine weight were consistent with CORT, T3, TC and TG alteration. Stress-induced endocrine adaptations, particularly elevated CORT, are known to regulate energy metabolism under adverse conditions (Barrett et al., 2015). Increased CORT promote mobilization of energy reserves and favor lipid deposition in peripheral tissues (Andong et al., 2024), which is consistent with the observed changes in abdominal fat pad and increased liver weight in the current study.

These maternal physiological and metabolic alterations due to heat stress may have downstream consequences for offspring growth and development, as changes in maternal energy balance and hormone profiles are known to influence progeny physiology (Aigueperse et al., 2013). This might be related to a reduced egg size (Wang et al., 2025), but also via epigenetic effects (Goerlich et al., 2012). Therefore, further investigation on the offspring performance and resilience is warranted to explore how maternal heat stress affects growth performance, metabolic status, and physiological indicators of the offspring.

It can be concluded that chronic mild heat stress markedly impaired reproductive performance in broiler breeder hens by affecting reproductive and metabolic physiological pathways. This appears to be a direct effect of heat stress without the potential indirect effects of lower feed intake due to heat stress.
