Escaping the Scaling Relationships in Oxygen Reduction Catalysis: Implications for PEM Fuel Cells
Authors: Muhammad Bilal Wazir, Lourdes F Vega, Maryam Khaleel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe growing interest in the hydrogen economy has sparked renewed interest in proton exchange membrane fuel cells (PEMFCs); however, the high cost and limited supply of noble metals like platinum remain a major obstacle to their widespread use. A primary bottleneck is the electrochemical oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) owing to its complex, multi‐intermediate pathway constrained by fundamental scaling relationships that limit the performance of even the most effective catalysts. Herein, we argue that interfacial heterogeneity, integrating symmetry‐breaking perturbations that span the subsurface lattice, the chemisorption layer, and the near‐interface solvation volume, provides the mechanistic basis for decoupling the adsorption energetics of ORR intermediates and overcoming these linear scaling relationships. This outlook critically examines recent strategies pursued to this end, including strain engineering, atomically dispersed sites, doping, interfacial field effects, and confinement, that modulate intermediate energetics to varying degrees, yet full thermodynamic decoupling remains elusive in practice. We, therefore, contend that the most promising path forward lies in engineering the interface holistically, integrating the lattice, chemisorption, and solvation contributions rather than tuning any one in isolation, while co‐optimizing for durability and transport dynamics in order to translate these mechanistic insights into practical PEMFCs.Keywords: catalysis, chemical physics, chemisorption, hydrogen economy, nanotechnology, oxygen reduction reaction, platinum, proton exchange membrane fuel cell, scaling, solvation, strain engineering

Introduction

Proton exchange membrane fuel cells (PEMFCs) are among the forefront of technologies enabling the transition towards a hydrogen economy. Their portability makes them appealing for application in light and heavy‐duty transport vehicles. A single unit of PEMFC contains anode and cathode catalyst layers, separated by a proton exchange membrane (PEM) and sandwiched between gas diffusion layers that are connected with current collecting plates, as shown in Figure 1 [1, 2].

Schematic representation of a single PEMFC showing the major components and reactive species; hydrogen (gray) and oxygen (red).

At the anode, hydrogen is oxidized to protons and electrons (Equation (1)). The electrons travel through an external circuit, generating electricity, while the protons are transported to the cathode through the PEM. At the cathode, oxygen is reduced by receiving electrons from the external circuit and protons from the PEM to produce water (Equation (2)) [2].

The sluggish kinetics of the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR), relative to the hydrogen oxidation reaction, constitute one of the primary bottlenecks in the development of cost‐effective PEMFCs [3]. This kinetic limitation arises from the complex four‐electron transfer process of ORR and the involvement of multiple intermediates, including *OOH, *O, and *OH [4, 5]. As a result, expensive noble metal catalysts are required to achieve practical reaction rates at the low operating temperatures (< 90°C) characteristic of PEMFCs. This temperature constraint is imposed by the fluorinated sulfonic acid polymer membranes, which enable proton transport from the anode to the cathode while preventing reactant crossover and electronic short‐circuiting. Consequently, the development of affordable yet highly active catalysts to replace noble metals remains a critical challenge for PEMFC technology.

Catalyst design for ORR has traditionally been guided by the Sabatier principle, which emphasizes optimizing the binding energies of key reaction intermediates to minimize the reaction overpotential [6, 7]. However, the binding energies of *OOH, *O, and *OH intermediates are linked by linear scaling relationships, arising from their similar oxygen‐mediated interactions with the catalyst surface. Consequently, weakening the binding of one intermediate, for example, *OH, inherently leads to weaker binding of others, for example, *OOH. This limits the degrees of freedom for catalyst design and caps the performance enhancement even for state‐of‐the‐art noble metal catalysts [8]. As a result, large variations in the physicochemical properties of metallic nanoparticles and 2D materials have yielded only modest performance enhancement [9], underscoring the need for new design strategies aimed at breaking the linear scaling relationships.

Introducing heterogeneity to the catalyst surface has emerged as a strategy to circumvent the constraints imposed by the linear scaling relationships [7]. Haung et al. [8] reviewed multiple approaches for breaking scaling relationships in oxygen evolution and reduction reactions, including confinement effects, the introduction of multiple adsorption sites, electrolyte engineering, introducing proton‐acceptor groups, strain modulation, and favoring dissociative reaction pathways. However, several recent studies have pushed the boundaries of catalyst design, improving upon these techniques, and new approaches have since emerged, such as electric field modulation, site‐specific strain engineering, and dual‐atom active sites that enable direct O2 dissociation [10]. This perspective aims to highlight the emerging concepts and design principles for breaking the scaling relationships in ORR, with a particular focus on their implications for PEMFCs. It begins by framing linear scaling relationships as a fundamental thermodynamic constraint that limits the independent tuning of adsorption energetics for ORR intermediates (Section 2). Section 3 selectively highlights emerging catalyst design strategies aimed at overcoming these constraints, including site‐specific strain modulation, atomic dispersion of active sites and their short‐ and long‐range interactions, interfacial electric field modulation, activation of basal planes in two‐dimensional materials through heteroatom doping, and confinement effects. For instance, strain engineering can provide control over catalyst reactivity by modulating the d‐band center [11]. Herein, we present a persuasive argument for heterogeneous strain as a unique driver for breaking monotonic scaling relationships. Similarly, while atomically dispersed catalysts ensure maximum atom utilization [12], the data analysis in Section 3.2 demonstrates that they also enable a significant departure from the linear scaling relationships characteristic of their bulk counterparts. Furthermore, interfacial electric field modulation exploits the intrinsic properties of reaction intermediates, such as polarizability and dipole moment, to decouple their adsorption energies [13]. This opens promising avenues to synergistically use catalytic site‐centric techniques with unique intermediate signatures to overcome scaling relationships. Finally, Section 4 offers a forward‐looking discussion on the synergistic integration of these approaches and outlines key opportunities and challenges for the rational design of next‐generation catalysts for high‐performance hydrogen fuel cells. Ultimately, we propose that interfacial heterogeneity serves as the primary decoupler of intermediate energetics and should act as a guiding principle for overcoming scaling limitations for catalyst design.

Scaling Relationships in Oxygen Reduction Reaction

Oxygen reduction starts with the adsorption of O2 at the surface of the electrocatalyst on the cathode. There are two adsorption modes for O2: end‐on adsorption (Figure 2A) and bidentate adsorption (Figure 2B). After adsorption, the O─O bond is either weakened via an associative mechanism or cleaved through a dissociative mechanism, depending on the electron‐donating capability of the active site. Subsequent surface reactions proceed through proton‐coupled electron transfer steps, culminating in the desorption of H2O [14]. The associative mechanism involves *O, *OH, and *OOH intermediates, whereas the dissociative mechanism only involves *O and *OH intermediates.

However, for complex active sites such as transition‐metal surfaces and atomically dispersed catalysts, adsorption geometry alone does not uniquely govern the electron‐transfer pathway, which is determined by a complex interplay of factors including the electronic structure of the active site (e.g., d‐band position for metals), the relative binding energies of *O2, *OOH, and *OH and the scaling relations linking them, local proton/water availability, applied potential, interfacial electric field, adsorbate coverage, and the competing kinetics of *OOH protonation versus O─O cleavage or desorption [9, 19]. On transition‐metal surfaces or metal‐coordinated sites (M–Nx), the same end‐on or bidentate geometries can bind *OOH and *O sufficiently strongly that the associative mechanism completes four‐electron reduction to water.

The 4e − pathway is preferred for energy generation owing to the complete reduction of O2 to H2O, which is essential for efficient power generation. In contrast, the 2e − pathway is undesirable because it produces H2O2 and results in lower energy conversion efficiency [20]. Even catalysts that operate predominantly through the 4e − route can release transient H2O2, which reacts with reduced metal centers through Fenton‐type chemistry to generate reactive oxygen species that oxidize the carbon support and demetalate the M–Nx active sites [21, 22]. This degradation pathway has been shown to progressively erode both activity and 4e− selectivity during operation [23], indicating that pathway selectivity and catalyst durability are coupled rather than independent design considerations.

For a spontaneous reaction, it is imperative that all the intermediate steps are downhill in energy. However, this criterion is not fulfilled at equilibrium potential (i.e., 1.23 VRHE for the 4e − pathway, and 0.7 VRHE for the 2e − pathway) for any known electrocatalyst. Figure 2D illustrates the free energies corresponding to the intermediate steps for associative ORR on the Pt (111) surface [17]. Spontaneous ORR over the Pt (111) surface can be carried out at a limiting potential ≤ 0.75 V for the four‐electron pathway. At higher limiting potentials, for example, 1.23 V, the reduction of *OH to water is uphill and thus will result in complete surface coverage with *OH, leaving no room for O2 adsorption.

The difference between the equilibrium potential and the limiting potential is referred to as the overpotential and is directly related to the binding energies of the intermediates at the surface of the electrocatalyst. These binding energies can be tuned by electronic and/or structural modifications of the electrocatalyst. However, since all ORR intermediates (*OOH, *O, and *OH) bind to the catalyst surface through oxygen atoms, their adsorption energies are strongly correlated across different catalysts [9]. The lack of independent control over the adsorption strengths of *OOH and *OH sets the limiting potential for associative ORR (Figure 2F) [24]. Optimal ORR activity requires sufficiently strong binding of *OOH to facilitate O─O bond activation and proton–electron transfer, while simultaneously ensuring weak enough *OH binding to enable facile desorption of water in the final reaction step. However, due to the linear scaling relationship between the adsorption free energies of *OOH and *OH, weakening *OH binding inevitably leads to a corresponding destabilization of *OOH. In this case, the conversion of O2 to *OOH becomes the potential‐determining step.

The color map in Figure 2G summarizes the effect of the ΔG OH versus ΔG OOH scaling relationship on the limiting potential for a variety of materials. As can be seen, all materials are displaced away from the top of the volcano plot [9]. Achieving the theoretical maximum activity, that is, reaching the top of the volcano plot in Figure 2G, would require an intercept of 2.46 eV for ΔG OOH versus ΔG OH, rather than the conventional value of 3.2 eV, which is provided by the existing scaling relationship (Equation (6)) [8]. This necessitates the independent control of *OH and *OOH adsorption free energies and highlights the central challenge in ORR catalyst design, which is the development of strategies aimed at breaking these scaling relationships. Current progress in this area is discussed in the next section.

Breaking the Scaling Relationships in Oxygen Reduction Reaction

Several strategies have been explored to alter or break the scaling relationships that limit the ORR. Foundational theoretical work summarized by Kulkarni et al. [9] established the thermodynamic framework for ORR scaling relations using Density Functional Theory (DFT) and the computational hydrogen electrode model, showing that the adsorption energies of key intermediates are strongly correlated across a wide range of catalyst surfaces and materials. This framework led to the well‐known volcano relationships that rationalize activity trends among conventional transition‐metal catalysts. However, much of this thermodynamic framework has been developed from studies of extended metal surfaces and nanoparticle catalysts, while subtle differences can emerge across other catalyst classes, including atomically dispersed catalysts. Building on this foundation, Huang et al. [8] provided a comprehensive review of oxygen electrocatalysis, compiling a wide range of strategies proposed to circumvent scaling relations in ORR and oxygen evolution reaction (OER) systems, including the introduction of p‐states, dual adsorption sites, proton‐acceptor groups, strain engineering, solvent effects, and nanoscopic confinement. From a broader catalysis perspective, Pérez–Ramírez and López [7] emphasized that overcoming scaling relationships often requires increasing the structural and electronic complexity of catalytic materials, identifying geometric perturbations, electronic modifications, and external stimuli as promising directions for catalyst design. Subsequent reviews have further examined both the opportunities and the challenges associated with breaking scaling relations. For example, Calle–Vallejo noted that many catalysts reported to break scaling relations instead modify reaction pathways or shift activity descriptors rather than fundamentally decouple adsorption energetics [25]. Similarly, Ivanistsev et al. [26] reviewed two decades of research on scaling relations in oxygen electrocatalysis and highlighted the growing importance of catalyst morphology, geometry, and structural complexity in influencing adsorption energetics.

While previous reviews have provided important theoretical frameworks and surveys of strategies for overcoming scaling relations, the present perspective emphasizes interfacial heterogeneity as a unifying design principle for addressing scaling‐relation limitations in ORR catalysis. Unlike conventional surface heterogeneity, which primarily refers to compositional or structural variations at the topmost atomic layer, we introduce the concept of interfacial heterogeneity to describe the full ensemble of symmetry‐breaking perturbations spanning the subsurface lattice, chemisorption layer, and near‐interface solvation volume. We categorize heterogeneity into three components: surface‐chemical heterogeneity, mechanical‐structural heterogeneity, and intermediate‐centric heterogeneity. Although these perturbations originate in three physically distinct regions, they define interfacial heterogeneity as a coupled framework in which chemical, structural, and electrostatic perturbations converge at the catalytic interface. Critically, these cross‐region effects do not act independently. Instead, their collective expression at the shared interface enables the decoupling of ORR adsorption energetics beyond the constraints imposed by universal scaling relations.

First, surface‐chemical heterogeneity refers to spatial variations in elemental composition, coordination environment, oxidation state, ligand‐field asymmetry, heteroatom doping, and dual‐atom or multi‐atom ensemble motifs. These perturbations primarily modify the local electronic structure of the adsorption site. Second, mechanical‐structural heterogeneity includes strain fields, grain boundaries, vacancy and defect networks, and geometric confinement. Strain modifies ORR energetics by altering metal–metal bond lengths and shifting the d‐band center. Confinement operates through an additional mechanism: it restructures the local solvation environment around adsorbed intermediates and can differentially stabilize *OH and *OOH because of their distinct hydrogen‐bonding capacities, dipole moments, and spatial orientations. Confinement may also enable non‐local site cooperation, in which intermediates experience a potential‐energy landscape shaped collectively by multiple surrounding sites. In this case, spatially separated active centers can cooperate to stabilize an intermediate or transition state that no isolated site could stabilize to the same extent. Third, intermediate‐centric heterogeneity describes perturbations that act through the distinct electrostatic and solvation responses of different ORR intermediates. These include spatially varying local electric fields, dipole–field coupling, differences in intermediate polarizability, support‐mediated charge asymmetry, and variations in the orientation and dipole ordering of the interfacial water network. Together, these factors generate spatially varying dielectric environments that each intermediate experiences differently according to its dipole moment, polarizability, and hydrogen‐bonding geometry.

Importantly, interfacial heterogeneity should not be viewed as a static property. Under applied electrochemical potential, adsorbate‐induced reconstruction, potential‐driven elemental segregation, changes in interfacial water structure, and dynamic charge redistribution can continuously reshape the operative heterogeneity landscape and active‐site ensemble. A full treatment of such dynamic interfacial heterogeneity lies beyond the scope of the present work, but it represents an important direction for future operando and potential‐dependent theoretical investigation.

Emerging catalyst architectures such as atomically dispersed metal sites, heterogeneous strain environments, dual‐site catalytic motifs, field effects, heteroatom doping, and spatial confinement can generate locally distinct adsorption environments capable of decoupling the energetics of ORR intermediates. Such heterogeneous catalytic environments differ fundamentally from the extended surfaces and nanoparticle catalysts that dominate traditional scaling analyses. The following sections examine how these distinct structural and electronic environments influence ORR intermediate energetics and enable deviations from conventional scaling behavior.

Strain Engineering

Strain engineering is a powerful technique for optimizing catalyst reactivity in ORR. This approach leverages the principle that the reactivity of a metal surface is dictated by its d‐band center [27, 28], and applying lattice strain can strategically shift this center [11]. For instance, in late transition metals, which are mostly used as catalysts for ORR, compressive strain lowers the d‐band center, leading to weaker interactions with adsorbed molecules. This is a result of widening of the d‐band owing to intensified overlap of wavefunctions, which results in decreased d‐band filling. The conservation of d‐band population enforces the d‐band depression. Conversely, tensile strain elevates the d‐band center, resulting in stronger interactions with adsorbed species. The opposite trend is observed for early transition metals [29]. Figure 3A schematically illustrates the effect of strain on early and late transition metals according to the classical d‐band model.

Since optimally balanced catalyst–intermediate interactions are needed for enhanced ORR activity, neither too strong nor too weak adsorption is desirable. For instance, Pt─Cu core–shell catalysts have demonstrated improved ORR performance, which has been attributed to optimized Pt–Pt interatomic distances resulting from the compressive strain within the material [32]. Interestingly, a volcano‐type relationship has been observed between ORR activity and compressive lattice strain [33]. This indicates that there is an optimal level of compressive strain for maximizing ORR performance. Beyond this optimum level, excessive compressive strain can negatively impact the reaction by hindering the activation of oxygen dissociation and the formation of OOH* intermediates, ultimately leading to a decrease in activity. Additionally, the Pt‐shell structures created by dealloying are thermodynamically metastable. This same compressive strain is the structural feature that compromises long‐term stability: since it originates from a sacrificial subsurface metal, which is thermodynamically unstable in acid, the activity gain and the dominant degradation pathway share a common physical root rather than being independent design parameters. Under acidic, oxidizing conditions of PEMFC (pH ∼1, > 0.6 V vs. RHE), subsurface transition metals (particularly Co, Ni, Cu) undergo continuous dissolution, leading to progressive shell restructuring and strain relaxation [32, 33].

While optimal homogeneous strain can effectively boost ORR activity to a certain extent, it does not completely break the inherent scaling relationships in ORR catalysis. In this context, heterogeneous surface strain creates unique, site‐specific shifts in the d‐band center, leading to spatially varying binding strengths for ORR intermediates, as confirmed by DFT calculations. The close proximity of active sites could allow adsorbed species to transfer to optimal sites for subsequent reaction steps, offering a potential pathway to break the scaling relationships. Liu et al. [30] recently demonstrated this by generating significant heterogeneous compressive strain (up to 5.5%) on a stepped Pt (111) surface, with the highest stress near the edges due to surface stress release (Figure 3B,C). The heterogeneous strain theoretically resulted in activity improvement for ORR, potentially circumventing the scaling relationship (Figure 3D). Further supporting this concept, Li et al. [31] reported jagged Pt nanowires (Figure 3E,F) with an average step size of 4–5 Pt atoms that exhibited exceptional ORR activity of 13.6 A/mgPt at 0.9 V in O2‐saturated 0.1 M HClO4 solution. However, undercoordinated sites carry the highest surface free energy in the system, making them the first to reconstruct toward smoother, lower‐index terminations under cathodic cycling. Therefore, the architecture that maximizes initial activity also maximizes susceptibility to restructuring. While promising, a precise synthesis strategy to control the step sizes and enhance stability is necessary to enable systematic studies and further advancements in the field.

Strain engineering in materials is often implemented in nanostructured and multicomponent systems, such as alloys and intermetallics, where it can coexist with additional phenomena such as quantum size effects (arising from nanoscale dimensions), ligand effects (involving electronic structure modification by neighboring atoms), and ensemble effects (related to multiple functionalities derived from different groups of atoms) [34]. PdMo metallene catalyst with a few layers, leveraging a combination of strain, quantum size, and ligand effects, demonstrated high ORR activity of 16.37 A/mgpd in an alkaline medium [34]. DFT calculations showed that the quantum effect significantly contributed to the depression of the d‐band center (−0.09 eV) for four layers of Pd compared to bulk Pd, as illustrated in Figure 4A. Furthermore, the ligand effect of Mo atoms further changed the d‐band center by −0.26 eV (Figure 4A), optimizing oxygen adsorption. Pd‐based alloys are known to exhibit dynamic Pd─Pd bond length changes during ORR, cycling between tensile and compressive strains at high and low potentials, respectively [35, 36]. As shown in Figure 4B, tensile strain in late transition metals generally leads to lower activity due to stronger interactions with ORR intermediates. Hence, the compressive strain in the PdMo metallene catalyst is crucial to offset the dynamic tensile strain induced at high potentials. Figure 4C highlights this by showing the tensile strain of Pd85Pt8Ni7, Pd92Pt8H41, and Pd85Pt8Ni7H41 nanowires as a function of applied potential. Notably, Pd85Pt8Ni7H41 nanowires, with their higher compressive strain, exhibited a delayed maximum tensile strain at 0.95 V compared to the other nanowires. As shown by DFT calculations, the ligand effect of Pt (d–d), Ni (d–d), and interstitial H (s–d) atoms contributed to modifying the electronic structure and lowering the d‐band center in Pd85Pt8Ni7H41 nanowires (Figure 4D) [36].

Despite these advances, ensuring long‐term stability of multi‐element alloys, especially in acidic environments, remains a significant challenge. Gao et al. [39] successfully combined strain and ligand effects in PtNi nanowires doped with Au and Mo atoms to achieve durable operation in acidic conditions. In this system, the strain and ligand effects of the Pt/Ni atoms enhanced the activity, while the anticorrosive properties of Mo/Au and the strong interactions between Mo‐Ni and Au‐Pt atomic d‐orbits stabilized the nanowire structure.

In comparison to disordered alloys, ordered intermetallics show great promise as catalysts due to their significant surface strains and enhanced stability [40]. However, their synthesis and size control are challenging, often requiring high temperatures to achieve atomic ordering. A fundamental challenge in designing intermetallic catalysts is the inherent trade‐off between electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) and the degree of order within the intermetallic structure (Figure 4F). While intrinsic activity generally increases with a higher degree of order, larger particle sizes often accompany higher ordering. This increase in particle size leads to a lower ECSA and, consequently, reduces overall activity. Yang et al. [37] successfully synthesized Pt–M core–shell intermetallics with 3d transition metals with an average size < 5 nm, exhibiting up to 11% compressive strain. The compressive strain resulted in optimized interaction with ORR intermediates and improved performance. Figure 4E shows a linear relationship between compressive strain and current density for different Pt–M intermetallics, where tetragonal Pt–M intermetallics with higher compressive strain outperformed their cubic Pt3–M counterparts [37]. Additionally, Pt–M intermetallics display anisotropic strain. While the strain in the core is substantial, strain relaxation throughout the Pt shell thickness prevents these core–shell intermetallics from reaching their full potential. Figure 4G shows a theoretical ORR activity volcano plot for ordered and disordered Pt‐based alloys highlighting the high intrinsic activity of ordered intermetallic PtCo on par with Pt2CoM (including Pt2CoNi and Pt2CoCu). However, as seen in Figure 4H, the larger particle size of these highly ordered intermetallics results in a lower ECSA. Conversely, PtCo with a smaller particle size exhibits a lower degree of order and thus lower activity. Recently, Yin and coworkers uncovered ternary Pt2CoCu and Pt2CoNi intermetallic nanoparticles exhibiting small size, high order, and thermodynamic stability from a vast material space by employing machine learning [38]. The trade‐off between ordering and ECSA can be effectively mitigated by these ternary intermetallic particles, yielding impressive specific activity and mass activity of 3.5 mA/cm2 and 3 A/mgpt, respectively [38].

Several recent reports indicate that intermetallic architectures can decouple activity from instability rather than merely trade one for the other. Limiting ultrathin‐Pt‐skin intermetallics (Pt3Co, PtCo, Pt3Ti) to sub‐3 nm, mesopore‐confined particles was shown to simultaneously sustain cyclic mass activity and suppress ionomer‐induced poisoning by limiting O2/ionomer transport pathways [41]. Pt–Pd ternary alloys incorporating Cu, Ni, or Co at non‐noble fractions above 50% achieved high durability via an alloying–realloying mechanism in which operando‐tracked strain redistribution stabilizes rather than depletes the active lattice [42]. Most strikingly, ordered intermetallics of Pt with alkaline‐earth metals (Mg, Ca), chosen specifically for their high vacancy formation energies, retained mass activity within 4% of beginning‐of‐life values after 30,000 cycles, exceeding both DOE 2025 beginning‐ and end‐of‐life targets simultaneously, and were explicitly reported as breaking the activity–stability trade‐off [43, 44]. This suggests that the activity‐stability trade‐off is a property of specific compositional/structural choices within the heterogeneity design space, not an inescapable consequence of introducing heterogeneity itself.

Building upon the versatility of ternary systems, high‐entropy materials (HEMs) offer an even broader compositional space with strong anisotropic strains to further decouple the intermediate energetics. By combining multiple principal elements (typically five or more) into a single phase stabilized by configurational entropy, HEMs exhibit properties that surpass traditional nanoparticles [45]. A key consequence of this compositional breadth is mechanistically relevant to scaling relations: moving from simple Pt alloys to multi‐principal‐element systems broadens the distribution of adsorption energies of key intermediates across the diverse multi‐element site ensembles, increasing the statistical likelihood that some site ensembles fall near the scaling‐relation optimum rather than being fixed at a single sub‐optimal point [46]. Liang et al. [47] presented a detailed account of high‐entropy materials, including alloys, oxides, and intermetallics in ORR, showing versatile 3d–5d band coupling that optimizes the reaction energetics and durability. Recently, Zhao et al. [48] explored the role of sub‐angstrom strain and N‐doping in stabilizing and moderating the surface interaction of PtCoNiFeCu core‐shell intermetallic with ORR intermediates. The high‐angle annular dark‐field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF‐STEM) analysis (Figure 5A,B) revealed that the N‐high‐entropy intermetallic (HEI) nanoparticles exhibit a core–shell structure composed of a high‐entropy intermetallic core coated with a Pt monolayer shell, effectively preventing transition‐metal leaching and enhancing structural stability. N‐doping increased the diffusion barrier for transition metals, further stabilizing the core‐shell intermetallic. However, the Pt‐shell structure may mask the anisotropic strain generated in the high‐entropy core. Thus, the transmission of heterogeneous strain in the high‐entropy core to the shell and its effect in changing the reaction energetics should be further explored. Moreover, high‐entropy materials without a protective shell are more interesting as they offer versatile composition and heterogeneous surface strains with site‐specific interactions with ORR intermediates. He et al. [49] demonstrated how such heterogeneity can engage the scaling relations through PtPdFeCoNi high‐entropy alloy (HEA) nanorings bearing abundant terrace‐type (221) step defects. Here, the breaking of surface atomic symmetry at the defects releases intrinsic surface stress and generates a localized ∼3% compressive strain (Figure 5E,F), which downshifts the Pt d‐band center specifically at the symmetry‐broken terrace sites and weakens OH binding, lowering the OH‐desorption barrier that constitutes the potential‐determining step. Critically, the resulting d‐band center and OH‐adsorption‐energy values form a distribution governed by the local bonding environment of each terrace site (Figure 5G,H) rather than a single value. This illustrates how heterogeneity in coordination and strain, acting together, populates a range of binding energies instead of obeying one monotonic relationship. The same defect‐rich, symmetry‐broken architecture also underpinned the durability (62.5% mass‐activity retention after 50,000 cycles), indicating that the structural feature responsible for engaging the scaling relation and stability is one and the same. The principle that coordination heterogeneity itself can be exploited to decouple intermediate energetics is further supported in adjacent systems; for example, high‐entropy‐induced activation of otherwise inert tetrahedral sites alongside octahedral sites creates spatially distinct dual‐active centers that decouple the ORR and OER pathways [50], although those reports are situated in alkaline oxide/zinc‐air contexts and their translation to acidic PEMFC remains to be established.

While such intermetallic nanoparticles have recently been reported for ORR [51, 52, 53], the effect of anisotropic site‐specific strain on reaction energetics and its potential in breaking the monotonic scaling relationship remains unexplored, being an area of further research and development. In particular, high‐entropy alloys (HEAs) inherently provide both heterogeneous surface strain and heterogeneity in active‐site coordination, yet the HEA studies mentioned above have examined these features largely in terms of activity and stability rather than explicitly for their potential to break the scaling relationship. A deliberate evaluation of this combined heterogeneity from the standpoint of scaling‐relation breaking, assessed jointly against activity, and acidic‐stability targets, therefore remains an open area for future investigation.

Strain, compositional gradients, and coordination heterogeneity simultaneously move a catalyst toward the intermediate binding‐energy optimum and activate the degradation pathways that erode it [11, 27, 28, 29, 30]. The dissolution, restructuring, strain relaxation, ECSA loss, ripening, corrosion, and ionomer poisoning derive from structural heterogeneity, responsible for the most active dealloyed, nanowire, and bimetallene architectures exhibiting degradation tendencies [31, 32, 33, 34]. However, the intermetallic and confined‐architecture [41, 42, 43, 44] demonstrate that activity‐stability trade‐off is not inevitable. When interfacial heterogeneity is engineered around stability‐first criteria, such as vacancy formation energy, transport‐limiting confinement geometry, or operando‐stabilized strain redistribution, rather than activity alone, both metrics have been shown to improve together. This reframes the central design question for next‐generation PEMFC cathode catalysts: not whether interfacial heterogeneity should be introduced, but which physical origin of interfacial heterogeneity simultaneously satisfies the scaling‐relation optimum and resists the acid‐phase degradation pathways mechanistically coupled to it.

Atomically Dispersed Catalysts

Single‐atom catalysts (SACs), characterized by atomically dispersed metal atoms anchored on support materials, offer a groundbreaking advantage: the complete utilization of every metal atom. This stands in stark contrast to conventional nanoparticle‐based catalysts, in which typically less than 20% of metal atoms are catalytically accessible [12]. In SACs, isolated metal atoms are stabilized through heteroatomic polar bonds with the support, facilitating significant charge transfer between the metal atom and the support. This unique atomic arrangement leads to catalytic properties that remarkably diverge from those of their nanoparticle and bulk material counterparts.

The concept of utilizing single metal atoms for ORR dates back to 1964, when Jasinski [54], drawing inspiration from earlier work by Paquot, Baldwin, and Cook on phthalocyanine catalysts, investigated cobalt phthalocyanine for ORR in fuel cells [55]. However, the term “single‐atom catalyst” was formally introduced by Qiao and colleagues in 2011, to describe the exclusive dispersion of single Pt atoms, with a weight loading of just 0.17%, on iron oxide for CO oxidation [56]. The resulting Pt1/FeOx catalyst exhibited high catalytic activity and stability, which were attributed to charge transfer from the single Pt atoms to the FeOx matrix, resulting in positively charged Pt atoms with partially vacant 5d orbitals. This, in turn, strongly stabilized the Pt atoms and reduced the CO adsorption energy and CO oxidation barrier.

In the context of ORR, SACs deviate significantly from the conventional scaling relationships for bulk transition metals [12]. In Figure 6 we summarize the data from ref. Huang et al. [8] suggested that the p–p interactions between *OOH and N‐sites used to stabilize SACs might influence the pre‐existing scaling relations, which are typically based on interactions between the metal d‐band and ORR intermediates. Zhong and Li proposed an unconventional ORR mechanism for SACs, replacing the O* in the conventional associative mechanism with 2OH* intermediates (O2 → OOH* → 2OH* → OH* → H2O) [59]. This yields a new scaling relationship; ΔG(O*) + 1.5 = ΔG(2OH*), with better predictability of experimental overpotential. By considering this new mechanism, the half‐wave potential of an FeNC‐SAC, which was significantly underestimated (0.59 V) when using the conventional associative mechanism, was predicted to be reasonably close to the experimental value (0.771 V).

Recent benchmarking shows that ORR scaling relationships and overpotential predictions for Metal–Nitrogen–Carbon SACs are highly sensitive to density functionals used in DFT. Hybrid functionals, such as B3LYP and PBE0, track high‐level coupled‐cluster references most closely; generalized gradient approximation functionals, such as PBE and BP86, tend to strongly bind key intermediates, and the double‐hybrid B2PLYP offers no systematic improvement (Figure 7A) [82]. Therefore, precise activity assessment requires both a correct pathway and a suitably accurate electronic‐structure treatment. To ensure the reliability of theoretical predictions, several complementary strategies should be adopted. First, theoretical results must be rigorously validated against experimental benchmarks, not only macroscale observables such as half‐wave potentials and Tafel slopes, but also mechanistic fingerprints obtainable from operando techniques. For instance, in situ infrared spectroscopy can directly probe adsorbed *OOH, *O, and *OH intermediates on catalyst surfaces under working potentials, providing unambiguous evidence for pathway switching between the 2e − and 4e − routes or associative and dissociative mechanisms [10, 83, 84, 85]. Additionally, computational models should capture realistic site heterogeneity rather than relying on a single idealized site. For instance, inter‐site structural heterogeneity, where FeN4 sites at mesoporous edges act as the true active centers through electronic modulation by adjacent microporous Fe sites, has been shown to drive intrinsic activity enhancements [86], and ensembles combining single atoms with clusters or nanoparticles can merge distinct potential‐determining steps to circumvent single‐site scaling relations [87]. Moreover, solvation and electrode‐potential treatment must be handled carefully, since implicit solvation models can miss the specific hydrogen‐bonding interactions that differentially stabilize *OH and *OOH, while explicit ab initio molecular dynamics‐based treatments capture these more faithfully at higher cost [88]. Readers are referred to recent comprehensive reviews of DFT best practices in electrocatalysis for further guidance on benchmarking, convergence, solvation, and potential corrections [89, 90, 91].

It is increasingly recognized that the carbon matrix plays a significant role in the activity of SACs, alongside the active metal and nitrogen atoms. Zitolo et al. [94] linked the physicochemical characteristics of the support with ORR activity in FeNC‐SACs, demonstrating that the introduction of basic nitrogen groups into FeN4C12 enhanced the ORR activity. Additionally, FeN4C8 and FeN4C10 catalysts were reported to be more active for ORR than FeN4C12 [95, 96, 97], while FeN4C10 also shows superior durability in acidic media compared to FeN4C12, which degrades into ferric oxide.

The electronic structure of the support also plays a decisive role. Li and Xiang associated electrocatalytic activity in MNC‐SACs with the degree of π‐electron delocalization in the carbon support [92]. They prepared covalent organic polymers (COPs) containing isolated Fe atoms and varying degrees of π‐conjugation using tetracyanoethylene (COP‐Ene), pyromellitic dianhydride (COP‐Ppcfe), 1,4,5,8‐naphthalenetetracarboxylic dianhydride (COP‐Nap), and 3,4,9,10‐perylenetetracarboxylic dianhydride (COP‐Pyr). Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (Figure 7B) revealed an inverse correlation between the electron work function and the extent of π‐electron delocalization, indicating enhanced electron‐donating capability with increasing conjugation. However, improvements in electronic donation did not translate linearly into ORR performance due to intrinsic scaling relationships, as evidenced by the superior performance of COP‐Ppcfe, which exhibited an intermediate degree of π‐delocalization and work function. Thus, carbon electronic properties modulate, but do not entirely overcome, scaling constraints.

Beyond electronic and structural effects, interfacial properties of the carbon support further modulate ORR performance. Yang and coworkers systematically deconvoluted the impact of factors such as contact angle, resistivity, edge defects, functional groups, and surface area [93]. As illustrated in Figure 7C, graphene edge defects, oxygen functional groups, and contact angle have a significant impact on ORR activity. Recently, fluorination of the carbon matrix was shown to enhance the half‐wave potential of FeN4 sites by 60 mV through optimization of interfacial interactions with water and d‐band modulation, thereby improving intermediate binding energetics [98]. Despite these advances, precise control over carbon support structure during high‐temperature synthesis remains challenging, often leading to heterogeneous local environments around active metal sites [99]. Consequently, the development of low‐temperature synthetic strategies and quantitative characterization of specific carbon–metal bonding motifs are essential for establishing definitive structure–activity relationships and enabling rational design of next‐generation MNC‐SACs for ORR.

The coordination environment of the metal center directly determines intermediate adsorption energies through charge transfer and d‐band modulation [100]. Zheng et al. [101] experimentally and computationally confirmed the significant role of MN2 coordination in enhancing the ORR activity of single atoms embedded in a g‐C3N4 matrix. Their research showed that charge transfer from the metal atom to two nitrogen atoms adjusted the d‐band, leading to enhanced ORR performance comparable to that of a Pt (111) surface [101]. Recently, He and coworkers showed that introducing nitrogen in the third coordination shell of CoN4 electronically tuned the active site to weaken ORR‐intermediate binding and lower reaction barriers, yielding markedly higher intrinsic activity [102].

Control over the coordination environment can also be achieved by introducing other heteroatoms like phosphorus (P), boron (B), sulfur (S), silicon (Si), or oxygen (O) into the metal‐nitrogen moieties [103, 104, 105, 106]. For instance, Yuan et al. [104] synthesized dual N and P coordinated Fe SACs through a process involving the polymerization of pyrrole in the presence of an iron precursor and phytic acid (as a P precursor), followed by pyrolysis under nitrogen and acid leaching. The resulting FeN3P sites exhibited superior catalytic activity compared to FeN4 sites, primarily due to optimized oxygen adsorption and splitting, as well as enhanced OH− desorption [104]. In addition to optimizing the d‐band center, P, S, or Si sites strongly interact with oxygen due to a zero‐energy barrier for oxidation [105, 107]. An adjacent oxidized P, S, or Si site can weaken adsorbed O2∗ or O∗ on the metal surface through repulsive forces, while simultaneously stabilizing OOH∗ or OH∗ through weak hydrogen bonds. These combined repulsive and attractive forces help alleviate scaling relationships.

Beyond heteroatom substitution within the first coordination shell, recent studies highlight the importance of edge‐localized hybrid coordination motifs. Combined first‐principles calculations and x‐ray absorption spectroscopy have confirmed the presence of pyridinic and porphyrinic hybrid sites located at graphene edges in MNC‐SACs [108]. Although porphyrinic sites are thermodynamically unstable and pyridinic motifs typically reside on basal planes, the edge‐localized hybrid sites and their thermodynamic suitability for ORR warrant further research on site engineering in MNC‐SACs.

Researchers have also explored out‐of‐plane (axial) coordination of active metal atoms with heteroatoms and inter‐site interactions to optimize the adsorption energy of ORR intermediates. For example, Peng et al. [58] axially coordinated iron atoms in FeN4 moieties with oxygen atoms by anchoring them onto O‐doped carbon rods. This resulted in a downshift in the d‐band center, as shown in Figure 7D, leading to optimized adsorption energies for ORR intermediates and better performance in comparison to planar FeN4 sites. Similar performance enhancements have been documented for coordinating FeN4 with tellurium clusters [109], n‐type chalcogenides [57], and axial N [78, 110]. Inter‐site distance also plays a critical role. When MN4 sites are separated by < 5 nm, d‐band downshifts optimize *OH binding and lower overpotential [60]. A dramatic increase in ORR performance was observed when the inter‐site distance dropped below 1.6 nm, attributed to optimized adsorption of ORR intermediates, as illustrated in Figure 7E. However, surprisingly, catalysts with sites only 0.5 nm apart showed a 15‐mV increase in overpotential. This increment might be explained by a mechanistic shift from an associative ORR to a dissociative one, where O2 splitting, rather than ∗OH desorption, becomes the rate‐determining step [69, 111]. A recent study further delineates that at inter‐site separations near 0.5 nm, hydrogen bonding between adsorbed intermediates impedes *OH removal, while additional crowding at shorter distances imposes steric hindrance that suppresses ∗OOH formation, together limiting ORR activity [112].

Dual‐atom catalysts (DACs) provide a powerful strategy to overcome scaling limitations. Adjacent metal atoms enable O─O bond cleavage, bypassing the *OOH intermediate and its associated ΔG OOH–ΔG OH constraint. Beyond offering distinct sites for promoting oxygen splitting, DACs allow for the precise tuning of ORR intermediate adsorption energies by controlling metal‐metal interactions through the hybridization of d‐orbitals of transition metals with either d‐block or p‐block electrons [113]. A variety of site configurations can be designed to exploit these metal–metal interactions, including M–M, M1–M2, M–X–M, and M1–X–M2 (where M represents a metal atom and X is a bridging atom like N or S).

Sites involving two identical metal atoms exhibit symmetrical charge distribution due to non‐polar bonds, providing symmetric sites for O2 adsorption. Homonuclear Co─Co sites enable bidentate *OOH adsorption with key intermediates' adsorption energy difference of 2.73 eV, closer to the ideal value of 2.46 eV and lower than the single‐atom site (3.1 eV) [114]. This results in weakening of the O─O bond and modulating the ΔG OH versus ΔG OOH scaling relationship [114]. Heteroatomic sites, with their asymmetric charge distribution owing to polar bonds, can offer additional variations in the adsorption energy of different ORR intermediates. Recently, Krishnamoorthi et al. [61] demonstrated that the coordination of Sn and Fe in a dual‐atom catalyst leads to superior performance. While Sn sites are typically inefficient for ORR due to their weak interaction with O2 and a high energy barrier for OOH∗ formation, the charge redistribution within the dual‐atom system with Fe resulted in optimized interactions with ORR intermediates, leading to a five‐fold enhancement in kinetic current density. In addition, Pt–N–Fe dual‐atom sites directly cleave O─O bonds, confirmed by in situ FTIR spectroscopy, effectively switching from associative to dissociative ORR and bypassing scaling constraints [10]. In situ FTIR showed the replacement of the IR absorption peak associated with the OOH∗ intermediate by an ∗O–O∗ radical peak on the Pt–N–Fe diatomic site (Figure 8A–D). This leads to the enhancement of ORR performance in both acidic and alkaline media. More recently, Guo et al. [115] provided a mechanistic understanding of O─O bond cleavage over heteronuclear Ru–Fe dual sites (Figure 8E–F). They observed that Ru–Fe sites are primed to transfer extra electrons to adsorbed oxygen, triggering a downshift of antibonding π‐orbitals. This leads to a higher degree of orbital filling and a lower energy barrier for O─O bond breakage (Figure 8G). Beyond promoting O bond cleavage, the electronic interaction between Fe and Ru induces an electronic structure reordering, decreasing the energy level of the half‐filled dz2 orbital of Ru (which is responsible for the strongest interaction with oxygen) due to electron spillover from dx2−y2 orbital, leading to complete filling. This facilitates faster desorption of the ∗OH intermediate due to weaker interactions, ultimately leading to extraordinary ORR performance. Other dimers, including Co─Mn [81], Co─Fe [77, 116], Fe─Cu [117, 118], and Fe─Fe [119, 120], have also shown similar improvements in ORR activity, further highlighting the general effectiveness of dual‐site electronic coupling in overcoming ORR kinetic and scaling limitations. In addition, emerging high‐entropy atomically dispersed catalysts (containing five or more different active elements) introduce multiple active elements, opening new possibilities for controlled inter‐site cooperation [121, 122].

As catalysis is a dynamic process, surface restructuring and intermediate‐induced reorganization may lead to a fluid microenvironment and mutable mechanisms. Therefore, in situ studies are essential to fully understand the dynamics and the precise role of the coordination environment on ORR activity and stability. Techniques such as operando x‐ray absorption near‐edge spectroscopy and in situ 57Fe Mössbauer spectroscopy, complemented by DFT calculations, can shed light on the elusive dynamics of SACs [123]. The coordination heterogeneity that allows atomically dispersed M–N–C catalysts to tune intermediate binding and approach the scaling‐relation optimum may also dictate how they degrade in the acidic environment of PEMFCs. Under acidic ORR conditions, the FeN4 moiety is susceptible to demetalation through protonation, which has long been regarded as the primary initial loss mechanism [94]. Recently, however, Pedersen et al. [124] showed, using operando dissolution measurements, that this process is not unidirectional, as proton consumption during ORR raises the local pH within the catalyst layer and favors reprecipitation of dissolved Fe to iron oxide. Demetalation is further coupled to the selectivity of the catalyst, since the 2e − pathway generates H2O2 that reacts with Fe centers via Fenton chemistry to produce hydroxyl radicals, which further oxidize the surrounding carbon. Bae et al. [125] demonstrated that this deactivation is strongly pH‐dependent, being severe under acidic conditions but minor in alkaline media, which partly explains the disproportionately poor durability of these catalysts in PEMFC cathodes. Carbon oxidation is therefore better viewed as an active‐site‐adjacent degradation channel than as a simple support‐level concern. For instance, Choi et al. [22] found that much of the lost activity could be recovered by electrochemically reducing the oxidized carbon surface, pointing to carbon oxidation rather than Fe loss as a proximate cause of decay. This sensitivity is closely tied to the local carbon matrix surrounding the FeN4 site [92, 93, 96].

Performance degradation can also originate from the microporous architecture that hosts the active sites. Micropore flooding, in which liquid water accumulates within 2 nm pores and blocks O2 access, was initially proposed as a major contributor to the rapid early performance loss of Fe–N–C [126, 127]. This hypothesis was subsequently tested and not supported when isolated experimentally and is therefore best presented as contested rather than settled [128]. However, the issue of active‐site accessibility is firmly established, since FeN4 sites form preferentially within micropores during pyrolysis and a large fraction of the sites counted ex situ are never electrochemically engaged; consequently, engineering hierarchical micro/mesoporosity is a more effective route to usable activity than maximizing total site loading [58, 63]. This accessibility limitation is compounded by ionomer transport, as perfluorosulfonic‐acid ionomer is largely excluded from the micropores and proton delivery to buried sites relies on condensed water. Pedersen et al. [129] accordingly identified an optimal ionomer‐to‐catalyst ratio that balances proton transport against accessible site density rather than a simple monotonic dependence. Finally, the FeN4 site is electronically dynamic under operating conditions. Jia et al. [97] observed redox‐induced switching of the Fe oxidation and spin state with applied potential as a determinant of ORR activity, and this same structural lability is consistent with the dynamic, partly self‐regenerating evolution of sites reported under acidic operation [99]. For atomically dispersed M–N–C catalysts, the same coordination heterogeneity, microporous confinement, and electronic flexibility of the FeN4 site that enable scaling‐relation‐engaging activity also drive the degradation pathways, so durability and activity must be co‐optimized rather than treated separately. Moving forward, this will require precise spatial control of the active‐site environment, mechanistic validation under operando conditions, and strategies such as dual‐atom or multi‐site systems with controlled inter‐site distances to deliberately circumvent intrinsic scaling limitations.

Doping

Hetero‐elemental doping is a powerful strategy to fine‐tune the electronic structure of catalysts, improve their stability, and even activate otherwise inert basal planes [130, 131]. By introducing foreign atoms into the host lattice, doping can modulate charge distribution, alter the density of states near the Fermi level, and optimize adsorption energetics of ORR intermediates. Lim et al. [132] computationally screened various dopants, including Zn, Zr, Ru, In, Sn, Te, and Bi, in PtNi alloy nanoparticles. Their findings revealed that indium (In) atoms not only optimized hydroxyl (OH∗) adsorption energy but also significantly enhanced the stability of the PtNi alloy nanoparticles by hindering nickel (Ni) dissolution. However, their study did not explore the influence of In doping on altering scaling relationships. Likewise, iron (Fe) incorporation into Pt3Ni alloy increased the density of states near the Fermi level, facilitating electron transfer and improving ORR performance [133]. Similar enhancements have been reported with other dopants such as molybdenum [134], gallium [135], gold [136], and rhodium [137].

Beyond bulk doping, surface adsorption of specific elements can also enhance ORR activity. For example, partially covering a Pt surface with sulfide improves its ORR performance [138, 139, 140]. The partial sulfide coverage favors the associative mechanism for ORR, as it increases the energy barrier for O─O bond splitting. Furthermore, the scaling relationship between the binding of OH∗ and OOH∗ intermediates on sulfide‐covered Pt is beneficially altered, bringing it closer to the ideal scenario for efficient catalysis.

Nitrogen doping has proved particularly effective in mitigating excessive compressive lattice strain introduced by alloying by generating offsetting tensile strain (Figure 9A) [141]. As shown in Figure 9B, low overpotential is observed at an intermediate level of nitrogen‐induced tensile strain. Besides optimizing adsorption energies, N‐doping plays a crucial role in preventing the dissolution of secondary metals within the alloy structure [141]. Extending beyond noble‐metal systems, N and P doping in transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDCs) has been shown to result in pronounced charge redistribution with electron‐rich regions depicted in red and electron‐deficient regions shown in green in Figure 9C. At the electronic level, N‐doping narrows the bandgap of TMDCs through hybridization between N p‐orbitals and the p‐ and d‐orbitals of neighboring transition metal and chalcogen atoms. This electronic restructuring leads to optimized adsorption energies for ORR intermediates and results in a theoretical overpotential that is even lower than that of the benchmark Pt (111) surface. Specifically, N‐doped TMDCs have shown a remarkable change in the universal scaling relationship between ΔG OOH versus ΔG OH with a slope of 0.77, instead of 1 (Figure 9D), bringing them closer to the characteristics of an ideal catalyst [142]. Nevertheless, such pronounced deviations should be interpreted cautiously. Scaling analyses should include only thermodynamically viable reaction regimes; incorporating data points where ORR is endothermic—such as in P‐doped TMDCs—may artificially skew regression results toward overly favorable trends (Figure 9D). A rigorous assessment must therefore exclude configurations where ORR cannot realistically proceed.

Interfacial Field Effects

While all ORR intermediates bind to catalyst surfaces through their oxygen atoms, their distinct dipole moments and polarizabilities present a unique opportunity for selective modulation via interfacial electric fields to independently control their interactions with the catalyst surface. Yang et al. [143] recently provided a comprehensive review of how electric fields influence physical properties and chemical reactivity at catalytic interfaces. In the context of ORR, Duan and Henkelman elucidated the crucial role of the electric field in regulating reaction energetics on the Au (100) surface, revealing a pronounced difference in performance between acidic and alkaline media [144]. Under alkaline conditions, ORR intermediates experience a significantly stronger (∼1 VÅ−1) negative electric field compared to acidic media. The interaction of this pH‐dependent electric field within the electric double layer (EDL) with the ORR intermediates leads to their stabilization on the Au (100) surface, consequently enhancing ORR performance in alkaline environments. Building on this, Nørskov and coworkers linked the adsorption energies of intermediates, considering their dipole moments and polarizability, to interfacial electric field intensity. Their analysis suggested that catalysts exhibiting weak intermediate binding in acidic conditions may become viable in alkaline media due to favorable field‐induced stabilization effects [145]. Similarly, applying an interfacial electric field could be used to control catalyst–intermediate interactions in acidic media. Gu et al. [146] successfully modified the electric field in the EDL by the physisorption of cations at the catalyst surface. This strategy simultaneously promoted CO2 reduction while hindering the competing H2 evolution reaction in acidic media. The electric field not only slowed the transport of hydronium ions to the cathode but also stabilized key intermediates for CO2 reduction [147, 148], illustrating the broader applicability of electric‐field engineering in electrocatalysis.

More recently, Wang and colleagues demonstrated the profound effect of an electric field in modulating the scaling relationships of ORR intermediates for Fe SACs on carbon [13]. They utilized spherical carbon supports with controlled nano‐curvature to generate a homogeneous electric field. The ΔG OOH versus ΔG OH scaling relationship was not only altered in favor of enhanced performance due to the application of a negative electric field, but it was also entirely annulled beyond a threshold electric field of −0.4 V/Å, as depicted in Figure 10. This field‐induced decoupling of adsorption energetics resulted in high ORR activity in an alkaline medium. A similar strategy could, in principle, prove beneficial for offsetting the typically positive electric fields in acidic conditions, thereby improving ORR performance in the highly acidic environment of PEMFCs. However, this extrapolation from alkaline ORR to acidic PEMFC operation should be treated with caution, as the interfacial double‐layer structure, ion distribution, local field screening, interfacial water structure, ionomer interactions, and stability constraints differ substantially between alkaline and acidic conditions, which may alter the field effects substantially. It is also important to consider whether surface structural modification can realistically induce such high interfacial electric fields, whether the methods are scalable, and whether the modifications introduce competing factors that erode the performance benefits of breaking scaling relationships. For instance, increasing surface curvature intensifies the local interfacial field but may simultaneously induce surface strain that alters the electronic structure and adsorption energies, potentially offsetting the net benefit. A careful balance between field enhancement and structural perturbation is therefore critical for translating electric‐field modulation into practical catalytic improvements under acidic PEMFC conditions.

Confinement Effect

The concept of catalytic confinement, often referred to as the “nest effect,” was introduced by Eric G. Derouane in 1986 [149]. He proposed that the unique local environment within nanoporous zeolites could optimize van der Waals interactions, thereby influencing the orientation and electronic structure of adsorbed intermediates. Since then, the confinement effect has been extensively studied across a diverse range of materials, including carbon nanotubes [150, 151, 152, 153, 154], mesoporous silica [155, 156, 157], clays [158], and core–shell particles [159].

Confinement offers remarkable control over the space surrounding active sites, enabling nanoscale regulation of how molecules and intermediates are distributed and oriented during reactions [160]. Beyond simply enriching the local environment through diffusion control, confinement can also intrinsically boost a catalyst's activity by subtly modulating the electronic structure and coordination of its active sites. One compelling example of the power of confinement is seen in catalysts featuring a Fe–Co nanoparticle core encapsulated within a thin carbon shell. This synergistic design dramatically enhanced activity, stability, and selectivity for the 4e − ORR pathway [161]. The protective carbon shell effectively shields the core nanoparticles, preventing their degradation and agglomeration even in harsh acidic and alkaline environments. Furthermore, these core nanoparticles can subtly alter the electronic structure of the thin carbon surface, facilitating high catalytic activity [162, 163]. Beyond electronic modulation and structural stabilization, nanopore confinement can also provide a stable triple‐phase boundary region, which is crucial for improving the transport of oxygen, ions, and water, as illustrated in Figure 11A [164].

The principles of nanoconfinement can be extended beyond conventional porous materials to a broader class of two‐dimensional (2D) systems [165]. Jiang et al. [158] effectively utilized the interlayer confinement offered by montmorillonite clay to stabilize single‐atom layers of Pd‐based alloys, creating a high‐performance ORR catalyst, as depicted in Figure 11B. In their method, metal precursors were intercalated within 2D clay nanoparticles, followed by high‐temperature treatment and hydrofluoric acid etching to yield free‐standing single‐layer metal films. These single‐atom layers enable the utilization of nearly all metal atoms for catalysis. Furthermore, their low‐coordination environment leads to compressive strain, consequently lowering the d‐band center and enhancing ORR performance, a principle discussed in Section 3.1. Similarly, a high‐entropy nanoalloy confined between graphene layers exhibited high activity and durability owing to high atomic exposure and resistance to surface reconstruction during the reaction [52].

Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) provide another compelling platform for investigating confinement effects. Li and coworkers elucidated the confinement effect within CNTs of different radii by identifying disparities in activity and selectivity on concave and convex surfaces containing FeN4 moieties [154]. Interestingly, extremely tight confinement, specifically within CNTs with a radius less than 3.4 Å, hindered the ORR due to endothermic adsorption of intermediates. FeN4/CNT with a radius of 3.42 Å exhibited the lowest overpotential (Figure 11C), attributed to restricted water access and the low stability of adsorbed ORR intermediates within the confined space of the CNTs. A slightly larger CNT (4 Å) provided access for three neighboring water molecules, and the interactions of intermediates with surrounding water molecules via H‐bonding stabilized them. This led to an increase in overpotential. As the CNT radius increases further, the confinement effect diminishes, and the concave surface effectively attains a flat graphene‐like nature for ORR at radii larger than 6.1 Å. Therefore, it is necessary to balance space confinement and intermolecular interactions to attain the desired performance enhancements. Despite these promising findings, a few caveats should be considered before generalizing the results. The effect of curvature on the convex side of CNTs might have been masked due to sensitivity issues, suggesting that larger variations in curvature might be necessary to observe considerable changes in activity, if any, on the convex side. Additionally, the activity benefit predicted for confined sites inside CNT‐like cavities should be interpreted with caution when extrapolated to PEMFC cathodes. In MEA, such confined sites are catalytically useful only if O2, protons, water, and ionomer‐derived proton‐conduction pathways can access the same nanoscale environment. Therefore, CNT‐like or pore‐confined geometries should not be assessed solely by their intrinsic adsorption free energies; their relevance also depends on whether the confined cavity remains connected to gas‐, proton‐, electron‐, and water‐transport networks within the catalyst layer.

This curvature‐dependent behavior observed in single‐atom systems extends to dual‐atom catalysts, where inter‐site distance introduces an additional layer of complexity. Figure 11D demonstrates that CNT curvature critically controls the ORR activity of (FeN4)2‐x/(n,n)CNT dual‐atom catalysts. To isolate the effect of inter‐site distance, a (6,6) CNT was first used to optimize the metal–metal separation; the optimum corresponds to type b (separated by one graphene unit cell). Using this optimized configuration, the curvature effect was examined by varying the armchair CNT chiral index from (5,5) to (9,9), which corresponds to increasing CNT diameter. The (5,5) CNT with a moderate inter‐site distance of 6.41 Å achieves the best performance with the lowest overpotential (0.57 V). As the tube diameter increases, the overpotential rises monotonically because increasing inter‐site distance weakens their synergistic interaction, leaving curvature as the dominant factor governing reactivity. However, for a more accurate prediction of ORR overpotentials on Fe‐based SACs, it is recommended that the 2OH* mechanism proposed by Zhong and Li (Section 3.2) be used [59]. Furthermore, the adsorbed intermediates on one side of the CNT will inevitably affect the ORR activity on the other side, implying that such isolated confinement effects might be obscured in experimental studies. Enhanced ORR activity was likewise observed in carbon nanobelts with an intermediate‐sized triangular cavity (∼6.3 Å) [166]. The shift in optimal confinement relative to CNTs likely reflects the pore shape factor and site chemistry.

Overall, confinement effects should be connected explicitly to catalyst‐layer transport when discussing their relevance to PEMFC cathodes. The confinement should be treated as a coupled activity–transport descriptor rather than only as a local electronic‐structure effect. While CNTs, carbon nanobelts, curved carbon supports, and microporous Fe–N–C domains can tune the adsorption of ORR intermediates, their relevance to PEMFC cathodes depends on whether the confined active sites remain accessible to O2, protons, electrons, water, and ionomer networks in the catalyst layer. This is especially important for Fe–N–C catalysts, where micropore‐confined sites may display favorable intrinsic adsorption energetics but poor MEA utilization because of limited O2 diffusion, insufficient proton accessibility, incomplete ionomer penetration, local flooding, and restricted removal or decomposition of H2O2. Recent Fe–N–C PEMFC studies show that enhanced external surface area, mesoporosity, and hierarchical pore architecture can expose otherwise inaccessible Fe‐SAC sites, improve active‐site utilization, facilitate mass transport, and regulate catalyst‐layer water saturation [167, 168, 169]. Thus, the practical design target should be transport‐balanced confinement: local curvature or microporosity to optimize ORR energetics, combined with meso/macroporous channels, suitable ionomer distribution, and efficient water management to ensure high active‐site utilization under PEMFC operating conditions.

Conclusions and Perspective

The performance of ORR catalysts is fundamentally constrained by scaling relationships that correlate the adsorption free energies of reaction intermediates. Overcoming these limitations is a major goal in catalysis research, and various strategies have proven successful in circumventing these relationships. Methods such as strain engineering, creating single‐ and dual‐atom sites, doping, applying electric fields, and nanoconfinement have all been shown to alter these scaling laws. However, a key challenge is that breaking one scaling relationship often reveals another trade‐off. For example, while dual‐atom catalysts can break the ΔG OOH versus ΔG OH scaling relationship by fundamentally changing the reaction mechanism, this can introduce a new trade‐off between efficient oxygen splitting and the swift desorption of the final product. Despite these new challenges, each broken relationship offers a new opportunity for further improvement. A promising approach is to design catalysts with multiple types of active sites, each with distinct reactivity, located in proximity. This allows for different reaction steps to be promoted on distinct active sites. By enabling the migration of intermediates to the most optimized active sites, this strategy provides an additional degree of freedom in catalyst design, pushing performance beyond the limitations of single‐site catalysts.

Surface strain is a powerful tool for tailoring the binding energies of ORR intermediates by readjusting a catalyst's d‐band center. In late transition metals, compressive strain is particularly interesting because it weakens the interaction with adsorbates, which is beneficial for promoting ORR in PEMFCs. However, there is a delicate balance: while optimal compressive strain is desirable, applying too much can elevate the activation barrier for oxygen dissociation and the formation of the OOH∗ intermediate, ultimately reducing activity. Homogeneous strain can only enhance ORR activity to a certain extent, as adsorption energies respond monotonically to a uniform change in surface reactivity. This makes the generation of heterogeneous strain highly desirable. Heterogeneous strain introduces distinct, site‐specific shifts in the d‐band center, leading to a variety of binding energies for ORR intermediates at different locations on the catalyst surface. Heterogeneous strain can be further amplified by integrating surface strain with other effects like the quantum size effect, ligand effect, and ensemble effect. These combined effects are commonly observed in complex, multi‐element nanostructures such as alloys and intermetallics. However, a major challenge with these multi‐element architectures remains their long‐term stability in harsh acidic conditions, an area that requires extensive further investigation.

Single‐atom catalysts (SACs) offer a dual advantage: they maximize the utilization of every metal atom while also fundamentally altering the scaling relationship between the adsorption free energies of ΔG OOH versus ΔG O H. This alteration makes Metal–Nitrogen–Carbon (MNC) SACs thermodynamically more favorable for improved performance and a lower overpotential. With a slope of 0.87, the scaling relationship for MNC‐SACs is skewed desirably towards the top of the volcano plot, a significant improvement over conventional bulk material. This enhancement is a result of unconventional reaction mechanisms. These include the conversion of the OOH∗ intermediate directly to 2OH∗ without forming the problematic O∗ intermediate, and the direct splitting of the oxygen molecule at dual‐atom sites. These new pathways offer significant opportunities to enhance catalytic activity. Despite these breakthroughs, challenges remain for the widespread adoption of SACs. Issues related to achieving a high enough site density and ensuring long‐term operational stability in highly acidic conditions still need to be addressed.

A negative electric field has the potential to counteract the positive field that ORR intermediates experience at the catalyst‐electrolyte interface in acidic conditions. However, generating a homogeneous electric field at the interface is a significant challenge, often requiring precise synthesis control, such as engineering the nano‐curvature of the support material. Carbon‐based materials like carbon nanotubes (CNTs) and fullerenes (“buckyballs”) are excellent candidates for this, as their nano‐curvature can be controlled. Nevertheless, these materials typically offer extremely high curvature, and achieving and maintaining a low curvature over a wider range of sizes becomes technically difficult. This opens intriguing avenues for future research. It would be valuable to investigate the effect of curvature‐induced electric fields in catalyst supports based on other materials. Additionally, combining this curvature‐induced electric field effect with confinement could yield a powerful synergistic effect. A comparative study of the monoaxial curvature found in nanotubes versus the biaxial curvature of nanobubbles or nanospheres could also help to clearly elucidate the fundamental relationship between curvature and the induced electric field, paving the way for rational catalyst design.

In situ and operando studies are essential not only for observing catalyst evolution, but also for testing whether interfacial heterogeneity is responsible for breaking ORR scaling relationships under working conditions. Activity trends alone cannot verify this mechanism; the key question is whether different interfacial environments selectively stabilize *OOH, *O, and *OH or promote different ORR pathways. Advanced techniques such as in‐situ X‐ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS), Raman, Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, and electrochemical transmission electron microscopy (TEM) enable real‐time tracking of active‐site oxidation states, coordination changes, surface intermediates, reaction mechanisms, and degradation pathways. However, mechanistic interpretations of ORR activity and scaling relationships continue to rely on static models derived from ex situ measurements or theoretical calculations, despite the growing availability of operando techniques. Capturing the dynamic restructuring of catalyst surfaces and the evolution of heterogeneous active sites under applied potential remains a major challenge. Operando XAS can address the structural and electronic part of this question: X‐ray absorption near‐edge structure (XANES) tracks potential‐dependent changes in oxidation state and electronic structure, whereas extended X‐ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) follows changes in coordination number, bond distance, and local reconstruction. When measured during potential steps, cycling, or controlled changes in reactant/electrolyte environment, XAS can reveal whether the catalyst behaves as a single‐averaged active‐site population or as multiple distinct site populations responding differently to operating conditions. Coupling XAS with operando FTIR, attenuated total reflection ‐ surface‐enhanced infrared absorption spectroscopy (ATR‐SEIRAS), Raman, or surface‐enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) adds intermediate‐specific resolution through potential‐dependent frequency shifts reflecting coverage, hydrogen‐bonding, and local field effects. This provides a direct test of scaling decoupling: if *OOH, *O, and *OH signatures evolve as one correlated population, the catalyst likely follows a conventional single‐descriptor picture; if different intermediate fingerprints appear, shift, or disappear independently with potential, this suggests that heterogeneous interfacial environments stabilize intermediates differently. Spatially resolved Raman/SERS mapping, electrochemical microscopy, and operando electron microscopy can then connect these spectral signatures to real structural domains such as strain fields, defects, confined pores, reconstructed surfaces, or multi‐site ensembles. Such integrated experiments would move the discussion beyond static ex situ characterizations and single‐site DFT models, allowing operando techniques to directly test whether heterogeneity is merely structural or genuinely responsible for decoupling ORR adsorption energetics from universal scaling constraints. Future advances in operando spectroscopy and microscopy, particularly when combined with theoretical modelling, will be critical for directly linking catalyst structure, intermediate energetics, and reaction pathways at a dynamic interface.

Finally, it is important to emphasize that catalyst design should follow a holistic approach. While thermodynamics is a critical component of catalyst design, it is only one of four major pillars, as kinetics, scalability/stability, and economics should also be considered. Typically, thermodynamics, kinetics, and stability are studied together to provide technical insights while the economic aspect is often deferred until later stages of technological readiness. Nevertheless, this can be a significant oversight, as a highly performing catalyst may never reach industrial application if its economic viability is not considered from the outset. To accelerate the discovery and implementation of catalysts with unprecedented activity, it is crucial to integrate all four pillars from the beginning. Modern computational and data‐driven approaches are uniquely suited to address this challenge, enabling the simultaneous investigation of multiple factors. This allows for the screening of materials through various layers, starting with an assessment of their fundamental thermodynamics and concluding with their economic viability. By applying such a holistic approach, the discovery and realization of next‐generation catalysts can be significantly accelerated.
