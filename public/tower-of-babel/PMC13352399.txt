Refractory gastrointestinal symptoms improved with transition to continuous subcutaneous Foslevodopa/Foscarbidopa infusion in advanced Parkinson's disease
Authors: Hanouf Aljuaid, Surabhi Garg, Gabriel Amorelli
Published: 2026

Introduction

Parkinson's disease (PD) encompasses progressive motor and non-motor manifestations; among the latter, weight loss is an underrecognized complication with substantial impact on quality of life. [1] The pathological mechanism remains poorly understood. However, several factors have been associated with it, including increased metabolic demand from hyperkinetic movements, mood comorbidities, and the direct effect of oral dopaminergic medications on nutrition. [1] Recently, continuous subcutaneous foslevodopa/foscarbidopa infusion (CSLI) therapy has emerged as a treatment modality for motor fluctuations and dyskinesia in PD. [2] We report an advanced PD patient with severe nausea and weight loss while on oral dopaminergic therapy and subsequent striking improvement after initiating CSLI.

A 71-year-old female was diagnosed with PD at the age of 54. About 7 years after diagnosis, she developed prominent gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms, including daily and persistent nausea and progressive weight loss; no new medications were started before developing these symptoms. Investigations were unremarkable and included an upper GI endoscopy, pan CT, pancreatic enzymes, liver function, renal function, electrolytes, thyroid function tests, inflammatory markers, celiac serology and Helicobacter pylori testing. Psychogenic nausea was suspected, but she had no response to an adequate SSRI trial. The GI symptoms quickly became the most debilitating, even more bothersome than the severe motor complications she was experiencing then. For the symptomatic management of nausea, she was tried on domperidone, ondansetron and aprepitant with no significant improvement. Furthermore, the other relevant GI symptoms included significant weight loss, over 60 pounds in the past 10 years (155 to 91 lb), and constipation, having bowel movements once every 5–7 days. This improved with a combination of stool softeners, fibre supplements, and probiotics, although there was no relief from nausea.

Concomitantly, she experienced significant motor fluctuations of more than 25% of the waking hours and generalized dyskinesias despite optimized oral therapy. Considering the troublesome motor symptoms, she was transitioned to CSLI. Following the transition to CSLI monotherapy, the patient experienced significant improvement in motor fluctuations, dyskinesia, and, notably, in nausea, appetite, and weight, as she gained 20 pounds 2 months after initial titration.

Patients with PD are consistently reported to be more likely to have weight loss than age-matched healthy controls, and it is also considered a biomarker of disease progression in PD, affecting quality of life. [3]

Weight loss in PD has been associated with malnutrition, risk of falls, bone fracture, cognitive decline, orthostatic hypotension and dyskinesia, as a result of a greater cumulative levodopa dosage per kilogram of body weight. [1] Longitudinal analyses from large PD cohorts show that weight loss correlates with disease severity, advanced age, higher comorbidity burden, and levodopa use. [4] Thus, weight loss is not only a marker of disease progression but also a determinant of functional decline.

Several mechanisms have been attributed to weight loss in PD. These include an imbalance between energy intake and expenditure, driven by decreased caloric intake and increased energy demands. Reduced intake is caused by dysphagia, delayed gastric emptying, constipation-related satiety, gastrointestinal dysfunction, and hyposmia. [1] Non-motor symptoms such as depression, apathy, and cognitive impairment further diminish appetite and interfere with feeding behaviour. Gastroparesis is particularly important because it limits nutritional intake and impairs levodopa absorption, worsening motor fluctuations. [1] Concurrently, energy expenditure is elevated in PD, even at rest, driven by tremor, rigidity, and especially levodopa-induced dyskinesias. Dyskinesias are strongly associated with progressive weight loss, reflecting the combined impact of disease-related and treatment-related metabolic demands. As a result, weight loss may persist despite adequate or even increased caloric intake. [1]

The other potential contributor to the weight loss in PD is dopaminergic medications. Levodopa therapy is essential in PD management but can paradoxically contribute to weight loss. Oral dopaminergic therapy has been implicated in appetite suppression, nausea, and gastrointestinal dysmotility, all of which impair nutritional intake. [1] Dopamine stimulates the hypothalamic arcuate nucleus, leading to a suppression of appetite and food intake. [5] Nausea and vomiting are commonly reported adverse effects of dopaminergic therapy due to the hyperstimulation of dopamine receptors in the gut and in the area postrema. [1] Almost all the active dopamine-mimetic medications, such as levodopa, dopamine receptor agonists, and catechol-O-methyltransferase, may give rise to nausea and vomiting, limiting energy intake. However, these adverse reactions are usually at the onset of therapy and better tolerated over time. [1]

Furthermore, as mentioned above, patients with lower body weight receive proportionally higher levodopa doses per kilogram, predisposing to dyskinesias and creating a cycle of increased energy expenditure and further weight loss. [1]

Ultimately, the interaction between impaired intake, heightened expenditure, advancing motor severity, autonomic dysfunction, nausea and psychiatric comorbidities drives cumulative nutritional decline in PD.

Our reported case had significant nausea and appetite reduction while on oral dopaminergic medications. Interestingly, when on oral dopaminergic therapy, her LEDD was lower than when on CSLI (Fig. 1). This increase in LEDD reflects the transition to a 24-h continuous therapy from intermittent oral dosing. This observation suggests that the pattern of dopaminergic delivery may have been more relevant than the absolute dopaminergic dose, although this remains speculative. Therefore, this case should not be interpreted as evidence that reducing dopaminergic burden is necessary to improve weight loss or gastrointestinal symptoms in PD.

Pump-based therapies are advanced treatment modalities for PD motor complications, mainly motor fluctuations and dyskinesias. These include infusion therapies, namely levodopa/carbidopa intestinal gel (LCIG) infusion and CSLI. [5], [6]

There have been mixed findings regarding body weight in patients on LCIG. Some observational studies reported weight loss in association with higher dyskinesia burden and dopaminergic dosing. [3] More recent prospective studies found no significant long-term change in weight, suggesting heterogeneity in response. [7] Conversely, a recent real-world trial of CSLI showed a reduction in gastrointestinal symptoms after 6 months of treatment. [8]

Subcutaneous continuous levodopa infusion has emerged as a promising alternative to oral therapy. Bypassing gastric absorption, this delivery method reduces the variability associated with gastroparesis and may stabilize plasma levodopa levels. In theory, this could attenuate nausea related to fluctuating levodopa peaks, minimizing possible OFF-related anorexia, and reducing dyskinesia-related hypermetabolism, thereby improving appetite and stabilizing weight. However, persistent dopaminergic side effects, including low-grade nausea, remain possible.

The observed weight gain in our patient should be interpreted cautiously. This case demonstrates a temporal association between improvement in chronic nausea and subsequent weight gain and does not establish that CSLI directly improves body weight or nutritional status in PD. Furthermore, formal nutritional assessments were not performed, and measures of body composition, muscle mass, nutritional biomarkers, and validated nutritional screening tools were unavailable. Consequently, weight gain alone cannot be assumed to reflect improved nutritional status. Additionally, potential metabolic consequences of long-term levodopa therapy, including alterations in vitamin B6, vitamin B12, folate, and homocysteine metabolism, were not evaluated in this case and may represent additional factors influencing nutritional outcomes.

Nevertheless, prospective trials incorporating weight and body-composition endpoints are lacking. Future studies should stratify by baseline dyskinesia and gastroparesis, include validated assessments of appetite and energy expenditure, and compare nutritional outcomes across subcutaneous and other dopamine delivery modalities. Given the single-patient nature of this report, the findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and not generalized beyond the context of this individual case.
