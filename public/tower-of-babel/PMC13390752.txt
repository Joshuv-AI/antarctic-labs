Sustainable removal of methylene blue from water using three coffee waste types
Authors: Julián Aguilar-Rosero, Ericka M. Alejandre-García, Ulises Salazar-Kuri, Lilian I. Olvera, Diana L. Cárdenas-Chávez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Since approximately 90% of the total weight of coffee fruit is not utilized during its commercial cycle, the coffee industry generates a massive amount of residues that affect environmental dynamics. This study explores an alternative use for three types of coffee waste (coffee husk CH, spent coffee SC and expired coffee EC) as efficient biosorbent materials for treating water contaminated with industrial dyes such as methylene blue (MB). Characterization was performed by analyzing their crystalline structures (XRD), thermal stability (TGA) and functional groups (FTIR), revealing that fat removal aligned crystalline domains enhancing mechanical integrity, while deprotonated carboxyl and hydroxyl groups drove electrostatic MB binding. Furthermore, the inherent content of lipids and proteins delayed the material decomposition. Raw and defatted coffee residues were exposed to two main treatments consisting of three stages with sequential exposure to alkaline and acid conditions, ethanol and H2O2, followed by a multi-phase processing for comparative analysis. The highest removal percentages were recorded for pretreated CH (98.14%) at pH 10 and 50 ppm of MB, no-pretreated SC (T1) at pH 5 and 50 ppm of MB (96.76%), and pretreated EC at pH 10 and 100 ppm of MB (98.48%). Adsorption kinetics indicated a pseudo-second-order mechanism and Sips isothermal model behavior, involving not only physisorption but also chemisorption on a heterogeneous surface with energetically distinct adsorption sites for all the coffee residues. Finally, a regeneration study using HCl as the desorption agent demonstrated a stable adsorption performance for up to 14 cycles at 50 ppm, particularly for coffee husk samples.

Introduction

In the few last decades, coffee has been considered one of the main drivers of the world economy with around 10 million tons of exported coffee at the end of December 2023.1 This industry provides significant rural employment, raises income levels, and alleviates poverty in coffee-producing countries such as Brazil, Vietnam, Indonesia, Colombia, Ethiopia and Mexico.2 From this agricultural product, one of the most popular beverages is obtained, and as its global demand increases, coffee farming expands in tandem.3 However, this expansion generates substantial agricultural residues, up to 6 million tons in the world per year,4,5 affecting ecological dynamics such as biological cycles and the balance of microbial communities.6,7 The coffee beans are mainly composed of a protective shell (husk), one of the most abundant secondary products generated in the agribusiness.8 It contains a large amount of fiber, cellulose and compounds with powerful antioxidant activity.9 Expired coffee and spent coffee share some properties, although the latter has undergone thermal processing. Both by-products contain about 20% lignin and polysaccharides, approximately 60% fatty acids, and 5% polyphenols and tannins, components that contribute to coffee's distinctive taste and aroma.10 Numerous studies have explored sustainable alternatives for managing coffee waste, thereby promoting a circular economy. Options such as composting, animal feed, biofuel production and biomaterials offer practical ways to repurpose these by-products effectively and reduce their environmental impact.11–13 Agro-industrial residues, for example, are a sustainable alternative for fabricating biofilters to adsorb hazardous water pollutants, such as heavy metals, pharmaceuticals, pesticides and even dyes, which are mainly discarded by the textile industry.14,15

Textile dyeing may occur in acidic or alkaline solutions; the choice between anionic and cationic solutions depends on the dye–fiber interaction and desired dyeing properties, such as colorfastness and intensity. Methylene blue (MB), a commonly used cationic dye, is water-soluble and ionizes in aqueous solutions, producing positively charged colored ions.16 It is also a common pollutant in urban effluents, causing aesthetic issues and posing risks to both human and environmental health. Here, MB was used as a dye reference rather than an isolated pollutant, offering well-documented properties and a structural baseline to benchmark surface interactions among the coffee wastes evaluated. Previous studies have explored MB removal using biosorbent materials (Table 1), demonstrating the effectiveness of various agricultural wastes. For example, rice husk,17 soursop residues, sugarcane bagasse,18 betel nut husk,19 banana peel,20 corn husk,21 orange and almond peels.22 Although this adsorption capability stems from the porous structure of the cellulose, several factors may affect the adsorption efficiency, such as chemical treatments, pH, adsorbent and adsorbate dose, temperature and contact time, and even the pollutant properties.23,25 Unlike other studies conducted with coffee waste,24 the present research considered a pre-treatment to partially remove fat from this residue and enhance the adsorption process, as fatty compounds could coat the pores of coffee residues, reducing the available surface area for contaminants to bind.8,26

pH values of 5, 7 and 10 were selected to evaluate the material adsorption capacity in acidic, neutral and alkaline environments, providing insight into how the surface charge of coffee wastes and the ionization state of contaminants might affect adsorption. Furthermore, MB concentrations of 50 and 100 ppm provide a practical range for evaluating adsorption, especially for pollutants in textile wastewater.31 Two of the three residues used in this research, coffee husks and spent coffee grounds, have been evaluated without pre-treatment as dry or carbonized material, treated with specialized techniques (e.g., supercritical fluid extraction and ultrasound-induced cavitation) or conjugated with calcium alginate, polyethyleneimine or magnesium,32,33 but to our knowledge, they have not been tested with the methods here proposed for MB removal. Besides, the use of a similar treatment for three different coffee wastes and the adsorption dynamics proposed for expired coffee have not been explored before. By providing valuable data to characterize and evaluate the adsorption capacities of the three treated coffee residues, this study established a predictable and scalable framework for optimizing the application of heterogeneous agricultural biomass in environmental remediation.

Materials and methods

Coffee residues

Spent coffee was obtained from a local cafe located in Puebla, México. Expired coffee material is generated when commercial coffee bags reach their expiration date or when coffee is not correctly sealed or packed. This material was provided by Nestlé® Company in México. Coffee husk was acquired from a local farm located in the Sierra Negra region of Puebla, México.

Pre-treatment

The spent, expired coffee and coffee husk samples were initially treated to partially remove the fat content from their structures (FR). Coffee husk was ground with a Hamilton Beach 80393 commercial coffee grinder. Expired and spent coffee were directly pre-treated. N-Hexane (99.6%) was added to each sample (ratio 1 : 4) and placed on agitation at 115 rpm for 48 hours at room temperature. Samples were filtered using a filtering flask, Whatman membranes (no. 1) and a Welch brand 2522 B-01 vacuum device (Ilmenau, Germany). The solid was recovered and mixed with 96% ethanol (ratio 1 : 4) for 16 hours at 115 rpm and then filtered as described above. A binary solution containing chloroform (1 : 1 v/v) and methanol (1 : 2 v/v) was added and manually stirred. After 2 minutes, the same ratio of chloroform was added and stirred for 30 seconds. Elix water (1 : 1) was then added to the mixture for 30 seconds. Washing and filtration were performed for each sample; the solid residues were dried at 105 °C for 15 min using an OHAUS moisture analyzer MB90 (Parsippany, USA) and stored at 4 °C until use. Raw residues (No-FR) were also characterized and tested in adsorption experiments.

Coffee waste treatments

Two different treatments were conducted on raw residues and coffee samples previously pre-treated (FR). Treatments 1 (T1) and 2 (T2) were described by Alghooneh et al.8 and Gabriel et al.,34 respectively, for other agro-industrial byproducts. For T1, the coffee residues were mixed with 0.1 N NaOH and 96% ethanol ensuring all the biowaste was in contact with the solution. These were exposed to 60 °C and agitated at 115 rpm. After 6 hours, the samples were neutralized with 1 M H2SO4 and filtered using a vacuum pump and Whatman (no. 1) membranes. The biosolid was then washed repeatedly with Elix water. During the second phase, 10% H2O2 was added to each sample (ratio 1 : 10) and stirred at 45 °C and 150 rpm for 6 hours. The samples were filtered and washed repeatedly with Elix water. In the third and final phase, a mixture of 80% acetic acid and 0.6% H2SO4 (ratio 1 : 10) was added and stirred at 151 rpm for 2 hours at 80 °C. Samples were filtered and washed three times with Elix water, then returned to the same stirring conditions with a mixture of 20% acetic acid, 30% H2O2 and 50% Elix water. After filtering the samples using a vacuum pump and cellulose filter paper (0.11 µm), these were dried at 105 °C and stored in airtight containers in a desiccator. The second treatment also consisted of three stages: alkaline pre-treatment, acid treatment, and bleaching. First, the coffee grounds samples and husks were mixed with 10% NaOH at a 1 : 10 ratio. The samples were incubated in a water bath set to 90 °C for 90 min, then filtered using a vacuum pump and 0.11 µm cellulose filters, performing several washes with hot Elix water. For acid treatment, a mixture of 20% formic acid, 20% acetic acid and 7.5% hydrogen peroxide (ratio 2 : 1 : 2) was added to each coffee sample and placed in a heated water bath at 90 °C for 90 min. The samples were filtered to recover the biosolid and washed repeatedly with hot Elix water. Bleaching was applied to the coffee residues using a 4% NaOH–7.5% hydrogen peroxide mixture (ratio 1 : 10). After 30 minutes of incubation at room temperature, samples were placed in a water bath for another 30 minutes. Finally, the solid was recovered by filtration, washed repeatedly with hot Elix water and dried at 105 °C in a thermobalance (OHAUS MB90, Parsippany, USA). It was stored in hermetic plastic containers in a desiccator until use. A total of six treated samples were prepared (Fig. 1) from each type of coffee residue: expired coffee (EC), spent coffee (SC) and coffee husk (CH). Pretreated samples (FR) were exposed to treatment 1 (FR-T1) or treatment 2 (FR-T2), or not. Samples without fat removal (NoFR) were untreated or processed under treatment 1 (NoFR-T1) or treatment 2 (NoFR-T2). A total of 108 coffee samples were evaluated considering these treatments, three different pH values, and two MB concentrations. Each experiment was performed in triplicate.

Adsorbent characterization

Coffee waste samples were characterized before and after adsorption experiments using a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FTIR) Thermo Scientific™ Nicolet iS5 (Madison, USA) to identify the functional groups involved in their adsorption capacity. Crystallinity and phase analysis were performed using a PANalitycal Empyrean diffractometer (Malvern, UK) with monochromatic Cu Kα radiation in a θ range of 10°–50°. Decomposition temperatures were determined using a DSC-SDT 650 from TA Instruments (New Castle, USA) at room temperature and up to 600 °C with a 10 °C min−1 rate. Crystallinity index (% CI) is a quantitative measure of the crystalline fraction present within an amorphous (non-crystalline) material. It provides insights into its structural conformation, which influences its physical properties and adsorption capacity. The % CI was calculated using eqn (1):1where Iam was the intensity of the amorphous region and Icr of the crystalline region. The Iam value was derived from the most intense peaks recorded, considering the intensity of the amorphous component relative to the strongest peaks of the crystalline phase. The I200 reflection was used for coffee husk and spent coffee samples, whereas the expired coffee samples employed the I102 reflection.

Adsorption kinetics

Two kinetic models were implemented to describe the predominant adsorption mechanism for methylene blue (MB) by the different coffee samples. Lagergren's Pseudo-first order and Ho's pseudo-second order were considered (2) to experimentally determine the quantity of dye adsorbed onto the coffee residue when it reached equilibrium (qe). Equilibrium was defined as the point at which the amount of dye adsorbed no longer changed over time.35,362where Ci was the initial dye concentration (50 and 100 ppm), while Ce was the dye concentration at equilibrium. The value of W was the amount of biosorbent used (1 g), and V the total working volume in the batch experiments (50 ml).

Batch adsorption experiments

Three 100 ppm stock solutions were prepared from a 1% Meyer® MB solution and then diluted in Elix water to obtain concentrations from 1 to 20 mg L−1 for calibration curves. Dye dilutions were adjusted to the different pH values using 1 N H2SO4 and 10% NaOH. The adsorption experiments were carried out with a total working volume of 50 ml at 37 °C and 150 rpm, containing 1 g adsorbent material and two initial dye concentrations (50 and 100 ppm). During the initial 10 minutes, 1.5 ml aliquots were collected every 2 minutes, then every 5 minutes until 1 hour. All samples were centrifuged at 10 000 rpm (9391×g) for 30 seconds and analyzed at 664 nm using a Hach DR6000 UV/Vis spectrophotometer (Düsseldorf, Germany). An MB mass-balance correction was applied to account for the substantial reduction in working volume due to sampling, which may alter the effective concentration gradient, adsorption capacities and kinetic trends.

Effect of the pH

To evaluate the influence of pH on the adsorption process, three pH values (5, 7 and 10) were considered according to previous studies.35,36 These reported an enhancement in the electrostatic bonding between the studied adsorbent and dye cations when the pH solution was equal to or higher than 5. On the contrary, the solutions with lower pH favored the presence of positive charges on the biosorbent surface, reducing the adsorption capacity for cationic dyes such as MB.

Effect of dye concentration

Two representative concentrations were selected to assess the adsorption capacity of coffee residues under the same experimental conditions (37 °C and pH 10). An MB concentration of 50 ppm enabled determination of material efficiency at lower contamination levels, while 100 ppm tested performance under higher dye loads, indicating possible saturation or maximum adsorption.37 Both concentrations also supported isotherm modeling, showing how adsorption changes with concentration.

Regeneration study

After the initial adsorption process, the MB-loaded coffee samples (∼1 g) were treated with hydrochloric acid (0.05 N) as a desorption agent. The adsorption process was performed at room temperature during two hours of contact time at pH 10, using both MB concentrations (50 and 100 ppm). Desorption cycles were conducted with a working volume of 50 ml (0.05 N HCl). The samples were filtered using a 0.11 µm cellulose filter paper and a Kontes filtration system connected to a Chemical Duty vacuum pump (220 V/50 Hz) from Merck Millipore (Billerica, USA). After collecting the samples, they were dried at room temperature overnight and finally weighed on an OHAUS Adventurer analytical scale (Parisppany, USA) before being reused in the next cycle. A total of 20 cycles were carried out, and the MB removal percentage was calculated after each desorption cycle.

Statistical analysis

An ANOVA was performed using Python™ v3.12.3 software to analyze the impact and statistical significance of dye concentration on the adsorption tests, including potential interactions between the experimental factors, by comparing the means of the different data groups. For the regeneration essays, a main-effects plot (Minitab® v20.4 Software)38 was also generated to visualize the trends in the adsorption capacities of each coffee residue.

Results and discussion

Coffee adsorbents characterization by XRD and TGA

Cellulose exhibits different types of crystalline configurations or arrangements, such as cellulose Iα, Iβ, II, III and IV.34 An X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed to identify the specific crystalline structures and differences among the coffee waste-based adsorbents here studied. Crystallinity is also known to influence key adsorption properties, such as surface area, porosity, functional group accessibility, chemical interactions, mechanical resistance and intraparticle diffusion.39,40Fig. 2a shows the XRD patterns recorded for the different coffee husk samples. All diffraction patterns corresponded to the ICDD data (International Centre for Diffraction Data) for cellulose Iβ (no. 00-056-1718). The sample NoFR showed most of the primary reflections from (200), (201), (212) and (310). When chemical treatments (T1 and T2) were applied to this residue, the reflections at (110), (200) and (1̄31) became sharper, indicating a recrystallization in the structures. The shoulder (102) appeared only in FR-T2 and NoFR-T2 samples, characteristic of randomly oriented crystallites that may be distributed in all possible directions rather than aligning along the longitudinal c-axis of the fiber matrix. Meanwhile, its absence suggests predominant alignment of cellulose chains and microfibrils.41 The cellulose possesses a semi-crystalline nature that apparently is enhanced by some organized domains.42,43 For expired coffee samples (Fig. 2b), the untreated sample (No-FR) presented an amorphous diffraction pattern with low-intensity bands. It was characterized by a small band at (110) close to the baseline noise level and two poorly defined bands at (1̄31) (standard reference cellulose Iβ-position from ICDD database) and (102), indicating a low crystalline order due to the disordered matrix that covers the biopolymer domains (e.g., cellulose and lignin). However, the chemical treatments (pre-treatment, T1 and T2) may promote recrystallization or the formation of more ordered cellulose domains at positions (200), (1̄21), (2̄31) and (231) in these coffee samples. This may have left fewer accessible hydroxyl groups, slowing the transport of MB molecules into the pores due to the partial removal of lignin and hemicellulose (amorphous matrices), resulting in new organized areas that may limit physisorption.44 The crystalline indexes (% CI) (Table S1A) for samples FR (38%), FR-T1 (33.23%) and FR-T2 (45.4%) evidenced that the fat-removal process promoted molecular alignment and structural order rather than increasing amorphization. Meanwhile, the XRD patterns recorded from spent coffee residues showed a broad band at (211) and two low-intensity bands at (231) and (110) for NoFR, this being the most amorphous sample with a % CI of 19.1% (Fig. 2c and Table S1A). This low crystallinity corresponds to a higher surface area in this coffee material, which may enhance the weak physical deposition of certain substances such as cationic dyes, phenolic compounds, pharmaceuticals, and even heavy metals.45 These results showed that coffee husk samples exhibited the highest structural order in comparison to expired and spent coffee samples. Fig. 3(a)–(c) show the thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) results for coffee samples. The main initial slope observed in thermal patterns around 100 °C was associated with water evaporation. Particularly, the spent coffee patterns for NoFR, FR-T1, FR-T2 and NoFR-T1 samples dropped to 50–70%, indicating they might contain more volatile components or moisture content.46 A second slope around 250 °C was likely associated with hemicellulose decomposition, while the fluctuations at ∼350 °C and ∼400 °C in expired coffee (NoFR and FR-T2) and spent coffee (FR-T1) are probably related to the gradual breakdown of lignin. Unlike other components, lignin decomposes over a wide temperature range due to its complex and irregular structure. This behavior might also occur by the decomposition of lipids, proteins, or residual organic compounds in the coffee samples, indicating secondary reactions such as char formation.47,48 Table S1A reports the initial and final decomposition temperatures observed for the different residues.

The temperature where weight loss began (Tonset) confirmed hemicellulose decomposition between 173–197 °C in coffee husk samples, and the temperatures where the decomposition ended (Tend) (402–478 °C) indicated differences in thermal stability, possibly due to the presence of more complex lignin structures. On the contrary, expired and spent coffee samples exhibited a higher Tonset suggesting greater lipid and protein content, which could delay material decomposition. This phenomenon is due to the coffee waste nature; roasting (for expired coffee) and brewing (for spent coffee) processes trigger the Maillard reactions, concentrating the lipids and melanoidins (reaction between carbonyl and amino groups)49 by removing hydrophilic components. Unpretreated spent coffee (NoFR) was the most thermally stable sample (Tend up to 524 °C) and exhibited the lowest residual weight (7.90% Wleft), meaning the material burns more efficiently.50

Coffee adsorbent characterization by FTIR

FTIR analysis enabled us to identify functional groups in the coffee samples and elucidate the adsorption mechanisms related to their chemical modifications.51 Spectral patterns observed in NoFR and FR samples are shown in Fig. 4, while peak shifts after dye adsorption by these latter are presented in Table 2. Pretreated coffee samples (FR) were selected for this analysis because they consistently achieved MB removal efficiencies higher than 97%. These samples exhibited higher peak intensities and clear changes, suggesting that the pre-treatment improved the accessibility of functional groups and therefore adsorption efficiency. The first peak observed in the region of 3000–3600 cm−1 corresponded to the water content. A representative peak around 2924 cm−1 indicated the presence of lipids in all the samples,52,53 while the peaks at 1731 and 1737 cm−1 have been reported for triglycerides.54,55 Since spent coffee samples come from a hot-water extraction (brewing process), it was expected a substantial removal of their lipid content. The absence of triglyceride peaks in these samples suggested that their surfaces are less hydrophobic, probably improving the adsorption capacity by enhancing interactions with MB.55,56 Some asymmetric stretches of methyl groups in the caffeine molecule were visible in the regions of 2850–2870 cm−1, with the highest caffeine concentration at 1600–1650 cm−1.57,58 Esterification process between caffeic and quinic acids was observed in the region of 1000–1700 cm−1, indicating the presence of chlorogenic acids and carbohydrates in the region of 650–1500 cm−1.59,60 The formation or disruption of hydrogen bonds, could change the electron density around a functional group by shifting the peaks.61,62 As shown in Table 2, the stretching frequencies of carbon and oxygen bonds varied in coffee samples, shifting the peak position due to the chemical groups present in the MB dye.63 A shift in the hydroxyl peak from 3365 to 3340 cm−1 in expired coffee, from 3319 to 3305 cm−1 in spent coffee and from 3324 to 3397 cm−1 in coffee husk samples suggested a hydrogen bonding interaction with dye molecules. Similarly, the peak shifts in the range of 1598–1712 cm−1 indicated interactions between MB and ester- or carbonyl-containing groups, such as quinic acids, caffeic acids and caffeine. The changes in lipid peaks (1737 to 1731 cm−1 and 2924 to 2926 cm−1) correlated with TGA results, confirming compositional differences. The spectral region between 800 and 1600 cm−1 shifted after adsorption also suggested the involvement of chlorogenic acids and carbohydrates in electrostatic or π–π interactions with MB.60,64,65

Effect of pH on adsorption

The adsorption capacity (qt) of all coffee residues at higher MB concentration generally increased rapidly within the first 10 minutes and then plateaued (Fig. 5). When comparing raw residues (NoFR) (Fig. 5a) and coffee residues after pre-treatment (FR) (Fig. 5b), greater qt values were observed at higher pH. Particularly at pH 10 (Table 3), the maximum adsorption (qmax) for coffee husk FR (4.9051 ± 0.0053) was higher than NoFR (4.7958 ± 0.0012), similarly for expired coffee FR (4.9244 ± 0.0003) versus NoFR (4.6958 ± 0.0018), and spent coffee FR (4.6771 ± 0.0035) versus NoFR (4.6315 ± 0.0106). qmax for coffee husk FR and expired coffee FR were also the highest values at these adsorption conditions, followed by coffee husk NoFR-T2 (4.8925 ± 0.0177), FR-T2 (4.8750 ± 0.0071) and expired coffee NoFR-T1 (4.8201 ± 0.0015). These results indicated faster MB adsorption than some modified coffee biochars, which required up to 50 min to reach the maximum adsorption point.66,67 Additionally, they required a high thermal input for pyrolysis. In this study, the alkaline environment likely favored MB adsorption through electrostatic interactions with deprotonated carboxyl and hydroxyl groups derived from the inherent lignocellulosic composition of coffee samples. Even after undergoing various treatment conditions, these functional groups were preserved as evidenced in their characterization results. Meanwhile, at low pH some functional groups such as carboxyl groups become protonated, reducing the electrostatic attraction with the positively charged MB molecules and their adsorption.68 Similar interaction between pH and MB removal was observed by using sugarcane bagasse69 potato plant waste28 and coconut coir waste.27

Interestingly, some samples presented high qmax values in acidic conditions at the same MB concentration, such as coffee husk NoFR-T2 (4.9055 ± 0.0097), expired coffee FR (4.8993 ± 0.0068), coffee husk FR-T2 (4.8901 ± 0.0009) and coffee husk FR (4.8896 ± 0.0001), suggesting that multiple adsorption mechanisms are involved, rather than a generalized pH-dependent behavior. Although the adsorbents surface charge may be less negative at pH 5, the adsorption sites reached saturation more quickly by reducing MB concentration to 50 ppm, leading to high qmax in short time for expired coffee FR (2.4528 ± 0.0032), coffee husk NoFR-T2 (2.4471 ± 0.0047), coffee husk FR (2.4383 ± 0.0094), coffee husk FR-T2 (2.4332 ± 0.0000), spent coffee NoFR-T1 (2.4192 ± 0.0012) and expired coffee FR-T2 (2.4115 ± 0.0000). When comparing samples NoFR-T1 (Fig. 5c) versus FR-T1 (Fig. 5e), all non-pretreated samples showed higher qt values at 60 min than the pretreated ones. This indicated that when treatment 1 is applied after the chemical pre-treatment, the biosorbent is severely affected, provoking a decrease in its adsorption efficiency at certain pH levels. In contrast, the pretreated samples showed higher qt values for most of the second treatment (Fig. 5f). This meant that T2 was more compatible with fat-removal samples, evidencing the introduction of chemical modifications that improve adsorption, particularly in coffee husk samples. Furthermore, pH sensitivity was more evident in FR-T1 than in NoFR-T2 samples (Fig. 5d), with both having lower qt values overall, suggesting that T2 had a more stable impact on adsorption behavior across different pH levels.

Here, the influence of pH on MB adsorption was mainly defined by the surface charge of the coffee adsorbents. The possible changes in MB speciation may be negligible within the pH range evaluated (5–10), as MB molecules are predominantly cationic above pH 5 (pKa value ≈ 3.8).68 For coffee residues and other lignocellulosic materials, the point of zero charge (pHpzc) typically ranged from 4.5 to 6.5,69,70 favoring a negatively charged surface that promotes electrostatic attraction towards the cationic dye molecules. This interpretation is consistent with the deprotonation of carboxyl and hydroxyl groups previously observed in the FTIR analysis.

Effect of dye concentration and chemical treatment

Dye adsorption uptake versus time was graphed considering both MB concentrations (50 and 100 ppm) to evaluate the effect of initial dye concentration on adsorption capacity. As observed in Fig. 6, the amount of dye adsorbed by coffee samples increased with contact time until equilibrium was reached. Equilibrium times ranged from 2 to 15 min, being better than other agro-industrial residues reported as highly efficient MB adsorbents, such as wheat shells (25 min),35 phoenix tree leaves (30–150 min),71 garlic peel (60–130 min),72 banana and orange peels (65 and 50 min),20 jackfruit peel (30–120 min),73 cauliflower leaves (100 min)74 and black tea waste (30 min).75

(a) NoFR, without fat removal pretreatment; (b) FR, fat removal pretreatment; (c) NoFR-T1, no pretreated samples treated with treatment 1; (d) NoFR-T2, no pretreated samples treated with treatment 2; (e) FR-T1, pretreated samples treated with treatment 1; (f) FR-T2, pretreated samples treated with treatment 2.

In particular, the higher dye concentration (100 ppm) resulted in a greater adsorption capacity (ranging from 4 to 5 mg g−1) on all coffee residues and conditions compared to 50 ppm (∼2 mg g−1). It suggested not only a greater availability of MB molecules but also sufficient surface area, porosity and active sites in coffee material that facilitated adsorption without reaching saturation at the highest concentration tested here. This trend may imply that diffusion limitations or steric hindrances were not significant, thereby allowing efficient dye-adsorbent surface interaction. Interestingly, the dye molecules adsorbed remained attached for up to 60 min, indicating a strong and stable electrostatic interaction. An F-statistic value of 708.72 and p-value of 2.36 × 10−24 from the ANOVA test indicated the MB concentration had a significant effect on the adsorption capacity of all coffee samples, while the high F-value represented a greater dispersion, meaning the interaction forces may occur over other variables such as pH or coffee waste type. Mechanistically, an increase in MB concentration represented a strong effect due to the rapid and frequent collisions between adsorbate molecules and available adsorption sites.

Regarding dye removal, defatted samples (FR) of coffee husk (98.10% at pH 10 and 100 ppm), expired (98.48% at pH 10 and 100 ppm) and spent coffee residues (93.54% at pH 7 and 100 ppm) achieved higher MB uptake than their raw samples (NoFR): coffee husk 95.91%, expired coffee 93.91% and spent coffee 92.33% (Table 3). These percentages exceed those previously reported in the literature for spent coffee employed as ground-calcium alginate biocomposite,76 and polyethyleneimine-modified grounds.77 In addition to their complex treatment methods, their removal efficiencies (77 and 90% after 60 min and 10 hours, respectively) do not surpass those achieved in this study.

The adsorption capacity of most FR samples at different MB concentrations and pH was slightly affected by the two treatments (T1 and T2). This might be due to the chemical denaturation of cellulose, which makes it more susceptible to thermal or chemical damage during subsequent processes. Using T2 on defatted samples of coffee husk (e.g., 97.80% at pH 5 and 100 ppm) and expired coffee residues (e.g., 96.57% at pH 7 and 50 ppm), these exhibited higher MB removal percentages than their pretreated T1 samples (92.59% and 80.31%, respectively). Unlike spent coffee samples, which had already undergone thermal degradation, coffee husk and expired coffee retained a more intact lignocellulosic structure, maintaining a higher density of adsorption sites and dye removal after T2. It is important to note that treated raw samples (NoFR-T1 and NoFR-T2) underwent partial fat removal during T1 and T2, even if they were not pre-treated. This effect varied among coffee residues due to their initial lipid composition, which also caused variability in their dye removal percentages.78–81

It can be seen in Table 5, that the pseudo-second order kinetic model fitted better for MB adsorption than the pseudo-first model, as it obtained higher R2 values (≥0.9999). This represented a predominant chemisorption behavior, in which chemical interactions between the dye and surface functional groups of coffee residues drive the adsorption process. However, the mechanistic interpretation balances both physisorption and chemisorption, as the FTIR results also evidenced physical contributions, such as hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, and π–π interactions. The strong fit with this kinetic model has been observed in other studies, which reported high MB removal using coffee husk hydrochar (99.48%)85,86 and NaOH-activated carbon derived from spent coffee grounds.87,88 This fact highlighted that adsorption efficiencies higher than 90% can be obtained even without complex modifications such as those mentioned above. Table 6 shows the results of isothermal analysis at 100 ppm and pH 10, the best adsorption conditions overall for MB. For coffee husk residues, the Sips model consistently offered the best fit with the highest R2 values (e.g., 1.0000 for CH-NoFR), followed by the Jovanovic-multilayer model (0.9988 for CH-NoFR), Toth (0.9898 for CH-NoFR) and Redlich–Peterson (0.9869 for CH-FR). Hill and Dubinin–Radushkevich models exhibited moderate fits. Particularly, the Langmuir and Freundlich models displayed negative R2 values (Table S2A), indicating poor fit with the data across all treatments. For expired (Table S3A) and spent (Table S4A) coffee residues, the Sips model also provided the best fit, yielding high R2 values such as 0.9997 for SC-NoFR-T2 and 1.0000 for EC-FR-T1 and EC-NoFR-T2. Although the ranking of the best-fitting isothermal models varied depending on the coffee residue, the general trend based on the number of treatments and R2 values was Sips > Toth > Redlich–Peterson > Hill > Jovanovic-multilayer > Dubinin–Radushkevich > Harkins–Jura > Freundlich > Langmuir > Jovanovic-monolayer. The fact that Sips isotherm fitted better in all three coffee residues indicates a multisite occupancy by MB molecules, where surface heterogeneity influenced their energy adsorption. It evidenced that the adsorption sites on the coffee material exhibited different affinities for the adsorbate, and as the equilibrium concentration (Ce) increased, the system shifted toward a Langmuir behavior, suggesting monolayer adsorption on a more homogeneous surface. In another study, both Langmuir and Freundlich models also fitted coffee husk activated with phosphoric acid, with R2 values of 0.9504 and 0.9097, respectively.39 Similarly, the Langmuir model explained the adsorption of methylene blue by spent coffee dried at 60 °C, reporting an R2 value of 0.9887.104,105 These similarities and differences highlight the complexity of the adsorbent–adsorbate interactions between the coffee residues and the treatments employed, indicating that the adsorption is not purely linear with dye concentrations. In this case, traditional two-parameter models such as Langmuir and Freundlich might oversimplify the adsorption process and lack the flexibility to effectively model adsorption in the coffee materials tested. Many advanced techniques may enhance the porosity of coffee materials, but do not necessarily improve the complexity of their adsorption mechanisms.

Regeneration process

Regeneration cycles were conducted to estimate the reuse capacity of the pretreated (FR) coffee residues after adsorbing methylene blue dye. Hydrochloric acid (0.05 N HCl) was tested as an eluent agent because of its capability of disrupting the adsorbate–adsorbent interactions, its low cost, accessibility and ability to regenerate adsorbents. An ANOVA test yielded a p-value of 0.061, indicating no significant differences in adsorption capacities across cycles at 50 ppm of MB. However, as the dye concentration increased to 100 ppm, the coffee material adsorption capacity progressively decreased with each cycle, suggesting that adsorption capacity was cycle-dependent at higher dye concentrations; this was supported by a p-value of 0.000 (data not shown).

This MB concentration was chosen to evaluate the longevity of coffee samples along various regeneration cycles. The plots shown in Fig. 7 were useful for understanding how adsorption and desorption efficiencies evolve, reducing noise and highlighting systematic patterns. In particular, the highest mean adsorption efficiency in coffee husk samples reached a qe value equal to 4.9731 (99.46% MB removal), maintaining an efficiency above 4.5 (90%) for the first 14 regeneration cycles. It gradually declined over the last cycle (19th cycle), where qe dropped to 4.2344 (84.68%). The negative slope observed after the 3rd regeneration cycle is mainly due to the progressive loss of active adsorption sites. It can be caused by surface degradation, pore blockage, or incomplete desorption. These fluctuations are expected in reusable biomass, although further experimentation is required to fully understand every mechanism involved in the desorption process. Expired coffee residues exhibited a more abrupt initial decline from the mean value of qe = 4.9126 (98.25%) to qe = 4.0322 (80.64%) after six cycles. Spent coffee samples showed a similar trend, resulting in an overall 10.37% decrease in MB removal capacity across all cycles. This specific behavior may be promoted by repeated pH swings that deprotonate and re-protonate the surface functional groups of coffee residue, interacting with H+ ions from HCl and facilitating desorption, but limiting adsorption capacity. Similarly, Kyzas et al.109 reported around 7% reduction in the adsorption of the cationic dye Basic Blue 3G (BB) between the first and tenth regeneration cycle of spent coffee. Other cellulosic materials have been regenerated for MB adsorption with different efficiencies. Kong et al.110 reported a 29% decrease in adsorption capacity over three cycles by using Platanus leaves. A comparable result was observed using orange peels, where MB adsorption declined by approximately 10% after five cycles.111

Conclusions

Coffee residues with fat removal (FR) evaluated at pH 10 and an initial MB concentration of 100 ppm produced the most consistent high-performance adsorption results. The strongly negative surface charge at this pH value favored electrostatic interaction with the cationic MB molecules, and the high concentration gradient drove the dye molecules to the exposed matrix at 100 ppm. These samples displayed higher band intensities in FTIR spectra after MB adsorption, confirming stronger chemical uptake and greater availability of adsorption sites on the cellulosic surface. The absence of triglyceride peaks in spent coffee, due to brewing, may have improved MB adsorption. Structural changes induced in coffee husk samples increased the aligned domains that may foster physicochemical interactions after T1 and T2 application. Expired coffee samples transitioned from amorphous to crystalline structures, displaying different decomposition patterns linked to their heterogeneous composition, whereas spent coffee samples remained the most amorphous.

Adsorption kinetics reached equilibrium within 10 minutes, with over 90% MB removal in the first five minutes for most samples, except FR-T1 and NoFR-T2 expired coffee, and NoFR-T2 spent coffee. The highest qmax values were recorded at pH 10, indicating that alkaline conditions favored MB adsorption, possibly due to the ionization state of dye molecules and surface functional groups. Some samples showed higher qt values under acidic conditions, suggesting multiple adsorption mechanisms beyond simple pH-dependent behavior. Chemisorption governed the adsorption process with chemical interactions playing a key role. Isothermal modeling showed that the Sips model consistently fit best for all coffee residues, supporting an energetic surface heterogeneity mechanism with heterogeneous surface interactions. In conclusion, coffee residues, particularly after fat removal, showed strong potential as efficient adsorbents for the dye MB, with an adsorption behavior influenced by pH, adsorbate concentration, chemical treatments, and the intrinsic physicochemical properties of the residues.
