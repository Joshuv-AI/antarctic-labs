Guide for Managing Digital Energy Technology Risks
OVERVIEW
This Equipment Self-Assessment Checklist is designed for asset owners and operators (AOOs)
responsible for the deployment, operation, maintenance, or cybersecurity oversight of grid systems and
digital energy technologies. It provides a structured inspection checklist for evaluating the security,
integrity, and operational trustworthiness of equipment across substations, generation sites, distributed
energy resources (DERs), and control environments.
The objectives of this checklist are to:
  Identify weaknesses that enable unauthorized control, persistence, or misoperation
  Detect unauthorized changes to firmware, configuration, or hardware where baselines exist
  Reduce unnecessary exposure across interfaces, services, and communications
  Establish and validate baselines for hardware, firmware, and configuration
  Verify logging, monitoring, and alerting capabilities support detection and investigation
  Validate access controls and remote connectivity are restricted and auditable

When to Use This Checklist
The checklist is organized into focused sections that evaluate key aspects of each asset, including supply
chain origin, hardware authenticity, baseline integrity, physical security, network exposure, user access,
vulnerability status, and monitoring capability. Each section is designed to answer a specific question
about the asset, ensuring that inspection activities remain targeted, consistent, and aligned with
operational risk. It emphasizes high-risk conditions commonly associated with industrial control systems
(ICS) and grid-connected technologies, including firmware and configuration integrity, network exposure,
remote access mechanisms, authentication controls, patch and vulnerability status, and event logging.
Additionally, supply chain validation and physical inspection practices are incorporated to detect
tampering, unauthorized hardware additions, or undocumented modifications that could introduce cyber
risk.
This checklist is most effective when applied at key operational and risk-driven points in the asset
lifecycle:
 • Post-commissioning or site handoff: Confirm installed equipment matches vendor
 documentation and establish a verified baseline for future comparison, particularly when supply
 chain risks are present.

 • Supply chain security concerns: When concerns arise about a specific component or
 manufacturer. While this checklist does not replace detailed technical analysis by cybersecurity or
 reverse engineering specialists, it provides a practical method to detect common indicators of
 tampering, substitution, or misconfiguration.
 • Post-incident validation: Following a site-specific incident or an external event known to impact
 deployed technologies, to confirm system integrity and identify residual risk.
 • Ownership or personnel transition: When responsibility shifts to new personnel, to support
 rapid familiarization, validate existing assumptions, and establish a documented understanding of
 the system's security posture.

provides recommended section prioritization; AOOs may adjust prioritization based on asset-specific
conditions, known risks, or the nature of the triggering event (e.g., vendor access, remote connectivity, or
suspected compromise pathways).

across key risk areas. AOOs should complete applicable sections based on the inspection trigger and asset
context, prioritizing sections as outlined in Table 1.

organizational processes, including any identified gaps, deviations, or observations. An example

 1. Identify and Document Asset Type
 Identify and document the device's functional role (e.g., relay, phasor measurement unit
 (PMU), BESS controller, DER gateway, energy management systems (EMS), remote

 2. Assign and Verify Impact Tier
 Confirm the asset’s assigned impact tier or criticality classification using a defined,
 documented, and consistently applied methodology (e.g., NERC BES High/Medium/Low 12
 where applicable, or an equivalent structured classification) and verify alignment with current
 asset records.
 3. Confirm Inspection Context & Trigger
 Document the inspection trigger and confirm inspection depth and focus are adjusted
 accordingly.
 4. Verify Maintenance Window and Identify Operational Constraints
 Confirm an approved maintenance window is in place and document any operational

 5. Validate Asset Inventory and Network Placement
 Confirm the asset is recorded in the organization's asset inventory and architecture
 documentation and verify that documented location and network zone placement match
 current physical and logical reality.
 2 SUPPLY CHAIN PROVENANCE
 Key Question: Where did this asset originate, and is the source trusted?
 Goal: Establish confidence that the device originates from trusted, authorized sources and has not
 entered the environment through unauthorized supply chain pathways, using procurement records,
 supplier validation, and shipping and chain-of-custody documentation.
 Rationale: Digital energy technologies rely on globally distributed supply chains that include
 manufacturers, integrators, distributors, and third-party brokers. Adversaries exploit this complexity
 to introduce counterfeit, substituted, or pre-compromised components before
 deployment.1 Establishing supply chain provenance improves traceability of origin, validates sourcing
 through trusted channels, and reduces the likelihood of introducing untrusted or high-risk components
 into operational networks.

 Note: Procurement and supply chain records are typically maintained by procurement, contracting,
 or supply chain management functions and should be requested and validated prior to field

 6. Verify Supplier and Procurement Integrity
 Evaluate whether the device was procured through an approved vendor or authorized
 distributor using applicable organizational procurement and supply-chain risk guidance.
 Where foreign entity of concern (FEOC)-related requirements apply to the project, document
 any implications for sourcing, contractual terms, incentives, or inspection constraints. 3
 Validate supplier identity against approved vendor lists and procurement records (if
 available).
 Review purchase order, invoice, and shipping documentation for consistency (model,
 quantity, serial numbers).
 Flag and escalate any gray-market sourcing, brokered components, or unexpected
 substitutions.
 7. Validate Shipping Origin and Chain-of-Custody
 Verify shipping origin (country, facility, distributor) matches procurement expectations.
 Review shipping logs, tracking history, and custody transitions for anomalies (unexpected
 transit locations, delays, or re-routing).
 Inspect packaging for signs of tampering, resealing, or damage.
 Confirm tamper-evident seals (if present) are intact and match manufacturer specifications.
 8. Establish Provenance Record
 Create a documented provenance record including supplier, shipping details, inspection
 results, and photographs.
 Store records in asset management or configuration management systems for traceability.
 Link provenance data to asset inventory and lifecycle tracking.
 Use this record as the baseline for future integrity validation and audits.

3 HARDWARE AUTHENTICITY & BASELINE ESTABLISHMENT
Key Question: Is the asset genuine and consistent with manufacturer specifications?
Goal: Verify that the device is genuine and consistent with manufacturer specifications and establish a
baseline of hardware, firmware/software, and observable configuration elements to support future
integrity validation, using physical inspection, manufacturer documentation, and non-intrusive system
observation.
Rationale: This section primarily applies during initial intake, staging, or commissioning activities. Grid
devices often operate for extended lifecycles and may be sourced during constrained market conditions,
increasing exposure to counterfeit or modified hardware. Even subtle deviations, such as unauthorized
chip substitutions or undocumented modules, can introduce hidden functionality or degrade system
reliability. Authenticity verification ensures the device matches manufacturer specifications at intake,
while baseline establishment creates a reference point for future comparison. Without a verified baseline,
operators cannot reliably detect drift, substitution, or tampering over time.
Note: This section applies primarily during initial intake, staging, or commissioning activities.

 9. Document Asset Information
 Record the device make, model, serial number, firmware/software version, hardware revision,
 and physical location.
 Photograph device labels, nameplates, and other identifying markings.
 Record the date of inspection and the identity of the person performing the assessment.
 10. Perform Physical Authenticity Inspection
 Inspect device exterior for manufacturer markings, labels, holograms, and compliance
 certifications (e.g., Underwriters Laboratories (UL), Conformité Européenne (CE), and
 Federal Communications Commission (FCC) markings).
 Verify label quality, spelling, alignment, and material consistency (counterfeit devices often
 show subtle defects).
 Confirm serial numbers, barcodes, and QR codes are present and readable.
 Photograph all labels, packaging, and identifying marks for recordkeeping.
 11. Validate Manufacturer Documentation and Specifications
 Confirm the device model, revision, and capabilities match official manufacturer
 documentation.
 Cross-check part numbers and serial numbers with vendor-provided records (if available).
 Request and review manufacturing or assembly origin information (country of origin, facility,
 lot number).
 Validate that included accessories, modules, and shipped configuration elements align with
 expected manufacturer specifications.
 12. Establish a Baseline Hardware Inventory
 Document all externally visible components, modules, and interfaces.
 Record chip markings, part numbers, PCB identifiers, and revision numbers where accessible
 without disassembly (applicable to staging, lab validation, or approved maintenance
 conditions only).
 Photograph internal components if inspection is permitted (e.g., during staging or lab
 validation).
 Compare observed components against vendor documentation, datasheets, or known-good
 devices.
 Flag discrepancies such as unexpected chip vendors, missing shielding, or unrecognized
 modules.
4 ASSET INTEGRITY
Key Question: Does the asset match its approved baseline, or has it been altered?
Goal: Verify that the device hardware, firmware, and configuration match approved baselines and have
not been altered outside authorized processes, using firmware validation, configuration comparison, and
asset records.
Rationale: This section applies to assets already deployed and operating in the field. Once deployed, grid
assets such as relays, PLCs, and EMS components may operate for years with limited direct oversight.
During this time, firmware updates, configuration changes, or unauthorized modifications may occur
without consistent validation. In ICS environments, altered firmware or configuration drift can directly
impact protection schemes, control logic, and system coordination. Verifying asset integrity against an
approved baseline ensures that device behavior remains consistent with engineering intent and that
unauthorized or unexplained changes are identified before they affect grid reliability or safety.

 6

Note: Section 3 establishes the initial hardware, firmware, and configuration baseline during intake or
commissioning. This section validates that the deployed asset continues to match that approved baseline
over time.
 13. Verify Firmware Authenticity and Integrity
 Verify that installed firmware or software matches the approved version for the asset.
 Confirm firmware or software updates were obtained from trusted sources.
 Verify digital signatures, hashes, or checksums (where supported).
 Validate secure boot, code signing enforcement, TPM, or hardware root-of-trust
 functionality (where supported).
 Document any inability to verify authenticity and identify compensating controls.
 14. Validate Configuration Against Approved Baseline
 Export or capture the current device configuration.
 Compare the current configuration to the approved baseline.
 Confirm that all deviations have an associated approved change record.
 Escalate unexplained deviations for engineering and cybersecurity review.
5 PHYSICAL SECURITY & TAMPER INSPECTION
Key Question: Is there evidence of physical access, tampering, or unauthorized modification?
Goal: Detect evidence of physical tampering, unauthorized modification, or environmental risk
conditions, using visual inspection, access control validation, and physical security records.
Rationale: Physical access to grid infrastructure can bypass logical security controls and enable direct
manipulation of devices. Adversaries or unauthorized personnel can install rogue hardware, access debug
interfaces, extract credentials, or modify firmware without generating network-based alerts. Systematic
inspection of physical security controls, enclosures, and hardware conditions helps detect unauthorized
access and prevents cyber-physical manipulation of critical systems.
 15. Inspect Enclosures and Tamper Indicators
 Examine cabinets, panels, and enclosures for physical damage, evidence of forced entry,
 missing or damaged fasteners, and degraded seals.
 Verify locks, hinges, and tamper-evident seals are intact and functioning.
 Photograph and document any anomalies.
 16. Verify Physical Access Controls
 Confirm that physical access controls such as locks, key control, badge readers, and
 visitor procedures are in place and functioning.
 Validate that physical access authorization lists reflect current personnel and roles.
 Escalate stale or excessive access permissions.
 17. Identify Unauthorized Devices or Hardware Modifications
 Inspect for unauthorized peripherals, rogue hardware, inline taps, cellular modems,
 removable media, spliced communication cabling, or unapproved adapters.
 Compare visible hardware against expected design documentation where available.
 Document and escalate any unrecognized component or unexplained modification.
 18. Inspect Debug and Service Interfaces
 Identify exposed debug and service interfaces such as JTAG, UART, RS-232, USB,
 console, and maintenance ports.

 7

 Verify that interfaces not required for operations are disabled, blocked, covered, or
 otherwise protected.
 Document any interface that remains exposed for vendor or operational reasons.
 19. Validate Grounding and Environmental Protections
 Verify proper electrical grounding, bonding, surge protection, shielding, and backup
 power where applicable.
 Document environmental conditions that could increase cyber or operational risk.
 20. Review Physical Access Logs and Surveillance Records
 Review site access logs for unusual, unauthorized, or unplanned access events.
 Correlate access events with work orders, maintenance records, and surveillance records
 where available.
 Review footage or flagged alerts where indicators of tampering or anomalous activity are
 present.
6 LOGGING, MONITORING, & ALERTING
Key Question: Can abnormal or malicious behavior be detected and investigated?
Goal: Confirm that device activity is recorded, monitored, and detectable to support incident detection
and investigation, using log review, monitoring system validation, and alert testing or historical analysis.
Rationale: Effective detection in grid environments depends on the availability and quality of device
telemetry. Many OT devices generate limited logs, and monitoring coverage across substations, DER
fleets, and field devices is often inconsistent. Without centralized logging, time synchronization, and
actionable alerting, abnormal conditions such as unauthorized access, configuration changes, or
communication failures may go undetected. Robust logging and monitoring enable operators to detect
anomalies, investigate incidents, and correlate events across distributed grid systems.
 21. Review Monitoring and Anomaly Detection Coverage
 Confirm that network monitoring, IDS, protocol monitoring, or anomaly detection
 capabilities are deployed where required by architecture or policy.
 Document monitoring blind spots, especially for unmanaged switches, serial links,
 cellular pathways, or vendor-managed connections.
 22. Validate Log Retention and Centralization
 Verify that device logs are retained in accordance with organizational policy and
 operational needs.
 Confirm logs are forwarded to centralized monitoring platforms such as a security
 information and event management platform (SIEM), historian, security operations center
 (SOC) platform, or other approved repository where applicable.
 Document any device that cannot centrally forward logs and identify compensating
 controls.
 23. Verify Log Completeness and Usefulness
 Review available logs to confirm they capture sufficient detail to support investigation,
 including user, timestamp, action, source, and outcome where supported.
 Verify logging captures administrative actions, authentication events, configuration
 changes, firmware updates, and communication failures where supported.
 Document logging limitations that reduce forensic value.
 24. Verify Logging of Abnormal State Transitions

 8

 Confirm that the device logs events such as unexpected reboot, firmware fault, watchdog
 reset, loss of communications, failover events, mode changes, or abnormal process states.
 Verify that abnormal transitions are distinguishable from normal operations.
 Escalate devices that do not record meaningful abnormal-state events.
 25. Validate Time Synchronization and Failure Behavior
 Confirm the device synchronizes to an approved and documented time source such as
 Network Time Protocol (NTP), Precision Time Protocol (PTP), or Global Positioning
 System (GPS) where appropriate.
 Verify time settings are accurate and consistent with monitoring infrastructure.
 Review device behavior, alarming, and logging quality when time synchronization fails.
 26. Confirm Alert Generation and Operator Visibility
 Confirm abnormal network, device, or process conditions generate operator-visible
 alarms where expected.
 Where permitted, simulate or review prior examples of communication loss, protocol
 failure, authentication failure, or other abnormal conditions to verify alert generation,
 visibility, and logging. (i) Where testing is not feasible, review alarm configuration and
 monitoring integration.
7 NETWORK EXPOSURE, INTERFACES, & COMMUNICATIONS
Key Question: Is the asset unnecessarily exposed or communicating beyond approved boundaries?
Goal: Identify and reduce unnecessary network exposure by ensuring interfaces and communications are
restricted and secured, using configuration review, interface inventory, and approved network testing
methods.
Rationale: Grid-connected devices frequently expose multiple communication pathways, including
Ethernet, serial, cellular, and vendor-managed connections. Many of these interfaces are enabled by
default but are not required for operational use. Unnecessary exposure increases the attack surface and
enables lateral movement between IT and OT environments or across grid segments. Restricting
interfaces, validating communication pathways, and enforcing segmentation reduces the likelihood of
unauthorized access and limits the potential impact of compromised systems.
 27. Document Network Configuration
 Record device hostname, model, firmware version, IP address, MAC address, subnet,
 default gateway, virtual local area network (VLAN)/zone placement, and required
 communication dependencies.
 Maintain a current backup of device configuration where supported and authorized.
 28. Inventory Interfaces
 Identify and document all physical and logical interfaces, including Ethernet, fiber, serial,
 Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, cellular, and maintenance connections.
 Confirm whether wireless interfaces (Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, cellular) are enabled, disabled, or
 unused, and identify any undocumented or unexpected wireless capabilities.
 Record the status of each interface as enabled, disabled, connected, or unused.
 Document the operational purpose of each active interface.
 29. Disable Unnecessary Interfaces and Services
 Verify that unused ports, interfaces, protocols, user accounts, and services are disabled,
 removed from service, or physically disconnected where feasible.

 9

 Verify unused wireless interfaces are disabled in software or physically removed where
 feasible.
 Document any interface or service that cannot be disabled because of vendor or
 operational constraints.
 Identify compensating controls for residual exposure.
30. Run an Authorized Port Scan
 Verify vendor guidance and device compatibility before performing active scanning;
 where risk exists, use passive monitoring or approved non-intrusive methods. Perform an
 authorized port scan, service enumeration, or equivalent approved method to identify
 open ports, listening services, and exposed protocols.
 Record results and compare them to the approved communication baseline.
 Escalate unexplained listening services or exposed management interfaces.
31. Validate Allowed Communications
 Confirm the device communicates only with approved endpoints, systems, and services.
 Include wireless and cellular communication paths when validating approved endpoints
 and communication flows.
 Document approved IP addresses, hostnames, ports, protocols, and communication
 directionality.
 Investigate and escalate undocumented or unauthorized communications.
32. Verify Proper Network Segmentation:
 Confirm the device resides in the correct network zone, enclave, ESP, or zone/conduit
 architecture.
 Verify firewalls, ACLs, or data diodes restrict communications to required pathways
 only.
 Document any flat-network exposure or unnecessary trust relationships.
33. Assess Insecure Protocol Exposure
 Verify insecure protocols such as Telnet, FTP, HTTP, SMBv1, legacy RDP, or cleartext
 management services are disabled, isolated, or otherwise controlled.
 Where wireless is present, confirm insecure wireless standards (e.g., WEP, legacy WPA)
 are not enabled.
 Where insecure protocols cannot be removed, confirm compensating controls such as
 segmentation, monitoring, jump hosts, or access restrictions are in place.
 Document residual risk.
34. Validate Encryption and Protocol Security
 Confirm encrypted management protocols such as HTTPS, SSH, VPN, or secure tunnels
 are used where supported and appropriate.
 Verify plaintext administrative access is prohibited where feasible.
 Review protocol and cipher configuration for consistency with organizational standards.
35. Evaluate Wireless Communication Security
 Verify wireless communications use approved encryption and authentication mechanisms
 (e.g., WPA2-Enterprise, WPA3, or equivalent).
 Confirm default SSIDs, credentials, and pairing settings are not in use.
 Identify cellular or vendor-managed communication paths and verify they are
 documented, authorized, and monitored where feasible.
 Assess whether wireless signals extend beyond controlled physical boundaries and
 introduce unintended access risk.

 10

 36. Review Certificate Implementation
 Verify that certificates are unique (no shared/default use), not expired, and issued by a
 trusted certificate authority. Document certificate details and expiration dates.
 37. Validate Behavior Under Loss of Communications
 Confirm the device transitions to a defined safe, fail-secure, or fail-operational state
 during communication loss, network isolation, or upstream dependency failure.
 Where permitted, perform controlled testing during an approved maintenance window.
 Where testing is not feasible, review vendor failover specifications, factory or site
 acceptance test (FAT/SAT) records, or engineering design documentation to confirm
 expected behavior.
8 USER ACCESS & REMOTE CONNECTIVITY
Key Question: Who can access the asset, and is that access appropriately controlled?
Goal: Validate that authentication, authorization, and remote access pathways enforce least privilege and
prevent unauthorized access, using account review, access control validation, and remote access auditing.
Rationale: User accounts and remote access pathways remain a primary entry point into grid
environments. Shared credentials, persistent vendor access, and poorly governed remote connections can
provide adversaries with direct access to control systems. In digital energy environments, unauthorized
access to systems such as DER aggregators, BESS controllers, or EMS platforms can result in loss of
visibility, loss of control, or coordinated disruption. Enforcing least privilege, strong authentication, and
controlled remote access reduces the risk of unauthorized or persistent access to operational systems.
 38. Verify Role-Based Access Control (RBAC)
 Confirm role-based access control (RBAC) is implemented where supported and aligned
 to least privilege principles.
 Review user roles and permissions to ensure access is limited to required functions.
 Escalate excessive privileges and dormant privileged accounts.
 39. Validate Credential and Password Controls
 Confirm password policy meets organizational requirements for length, complexity, reuse
 restrictions, storage, and rotation where applicable.
 Verify default passwords are not in use.
 Confirm unique credentials are assigned where configurable and that shared accounts are
 minimized or formally controlled.
 40. Confirm Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA)
 Verify MFA is required for all remote access pathways where supported.
 Document systems that do not support MFA and identify compensating controls such as
 jump hosts, session approval, or network isolation.
 Review exceptions for continued validity.
 41. Identify and Document Remote Access Mechanisms
 Inventory all remote access methods, including VPN, vendor portals, cloud relays,
 cellular, dial-up, jump hosts, and modem-based access.
 Confirm each remote method is authorized, documented, and operationally required.
 Escalate undocumented or unnecessary remote connectivity.
 42. Validate Third-party and Vendor Access Controls
 Confirm vendor and third-party access is approved, encrypted, monitored, time-bound,
 and limited to defined systems and functions.
 Verify access is disabled when not actively needed.

 11

 Review contractual or procedural requirements governing vendor access. 4
 43. Review Vendor Access Activity
 Record the date and details of the last vendor or third-party remote session.
 Verify access aligns with approved activities and timeframes.
 44. Confirm Remote Session Accountability
 Verify remote access is attributable to individual users and not shared generic accounts
 wherever feasible.
 Confirm session activity is logged and auditable, including connection time, user identity,
 and target asset.
 Review whether remote sessions require operator awareness or local approval.
 45. Validate Emergency Access Revocation Procedures
 Confirm procedures exist to rapidly disable remote access, including account
 disablement, VPN revocation, modem shutdown, portal disablement, or credential
 rotation.
 Verify procedures are documented, current, and assigned to responsible personnel.
 Confirm emergency contacts are available for execution outside normal business hours.
 46. Verify Capability to Disable Remote Access Pathways
 Confirm a technical and procedural method exists to quickly disable vendor or other
 external remote access pathways.
 Verify this capability has been tested or otherwise validated.
 Document any remote path that cannot be quickly disabled and identify compensating
 controls.
9 PATCH, VULNERABILITY, & LIFECYCLE MANAGEMENT
Key Question: Is the asset exposed to known vulnerabilities, and are risks being managed over time?
Goal: Ensure the device remains protected against known vulnerabilities through patching, risk tracking,
and lifecycle planning, using vulnerability intelligence, patch records, and risk assessment documentation.
Rationale: Grid systems often operate under constraints that limit rapid patching, including uptime
requirements, vendor dependencies, and safety considerations. As a result, known vulnerabilities may
persist longer than in traditional IT environments. Unmanaged exposure to known vulnerabilities
increases the likelihood of exploitation, particularly when combined with network exposure or weak
access controls. Effective lifecycle management requires continuous awareness of vulnerabilities, risk-
based prioritization, implementation of compensating controls, and planning for the replacement of
unsupported or end-of-life devices.
 47. Document Patch and Update History
 Record current firmware and software versions, installed patches, update history, and last
 update date.
 Verify records align with change management and maintenance documentation.
 Note devices that cannot be patched in the field or require full replacement for updates.
 48. Review Known Vulnerabilities and Advisories
 Review relevant vendor advisories, CISA 5/E-ISAC 6/FBI 7 alerts,

 49. Assess and Classify Vulnerability Risk
 Evaluate identified vulnerabilities based on severity (e.g. Common Vulnerability Scoring
 System (CVSS) scores), exploitability, and operational impact.
 Consider both technical severity and mission consequences such as loss of visibility, loss
 of control, or protection system degradation.
 Document risk classification and required action(s).
 50. Validate Patch and Mitigation Actions
 Confirm critical patches are applied through authorized processes where feasible.
 Where patching is deferred, verify compensating controls such as segmentation, access
 restriction, application allowlisting, monitoring, or vendor guidance are implemented and
 documented.
 Record decision basis and approval for deferred remediation.
 51. Verify Vulnerability Management Cadence
 Confirm periodic vulnerability review is performed in accordance with organizational
 policy and applicable requirements.
 Verify responsible personnel, review frequency, and escalation thresholds are defined.
 Confirm findings feed into work planning, change management, and risk registers where
 applicable.
 52. Validate Lifecycle and Replacement Planning
 Verify unsupported, obsolete, or end-of-life devices are identified in asset inventory and
 risk tracking records
 Confirm a documented mitigation, modernization, or replacement plan exists for assets
 that can no longer receive security updates or vendor support.
 Escalate unsupported devices that remain exposed without compensating controls.

Tools
 Assessment & Detection Tools
  Malcolm Intrusion Detection System: Open-source network traffic analysis and intrusion
 detection platform designed for operational technology environments.
  Cybersecurity Evaluation Tool (CSET): Free CISA tool to assess systems against cybersecurity
 frameworks.

 Cyber-Informed Engineering (CIE) Tools
  Cyber-Informed Engineering Control Database: Database of engineered control concepts
 designed to reduce the operational consequences of cyber compromise.
  Cyber-Informed Engineering Microgrid Analysis Tool (CIEMAT): Tool used
 to identify system design controls that reduce the operational impact of cyberattacks.
  Cyber-Informed Engineering Battery Analysis Tool (CIEBAT): Tool used to evaluate BESS
 architectures and identify engineering controls that mitigate cyber-induced operational risks.

 Planning & Analysis Tools
  Cirrus: Cloud Feasibility Assessment Tool for Grid Professionals: Supports evaluation of
 technical, operational, and security considerations associated with cloud migration.
  TOPGEAR: Analysis tool for extracting and visualizing socio-technical relationships across
 critical infrastructure organizations, technologies, and supply-chain networks to support risk
 analysis.

Programs
  Department of Energy (DOE) Cybersecurity, Energy Security, and Emergency Response
 (CESER): Addresses threats to American energy security, in all forms, through policy,
 innovation, research, collaboration, and industry engagement.
  Center for Securing Digital Energy Technology (CSDET): DOE-supported program that
 connects energy stakeholders with practical resources and real-world validation capabilities that
 support secure, reliable energy system performance. CSDET serves as a central hub for applied
 tools, methodologies, and technical assistance to support securing digital energy systems.
  Cyber-Informed Engineering (CIE): Offers engineering tools, standards, and educational
 resources that prioritize cybersecurity in infrastructure design and operation.
  Cyber Testing for Resilient Industrial Control Systems (CyTRICS): DOE-supported program
 for expert testing of high-priority OT and ICS components to illuminate component and supply-
 chain vulnerabilities.
  National Rural Electric Cooperative Association (NRECA) Co-op Cybersecurity Goals:
 Provides cybersecurity practices designed to help smaller utilities implement practical
 cybersecurity protections.

Threat Intelligence & Information Sharing
  CISA ICS Advisories: Provides information on active threats, exploited vulnerabilities, and
 mitigation guidance for IT and operational technology systems.
  Electricity Information Sharing and Analysis Center (E-ISAC): Primary cyber and physical
 threat intelligence sharing organization for the U.S. electricity sector.
