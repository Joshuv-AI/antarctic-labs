Choices and support during serious illness in the surgical intensive care unit — patient education series: understanding trauma and emergency surgery conditions
Authors: Sabrina E Sanchez, Danby Kang
Published: 2026

Background

When someone is very ill, they often need a lot of medical support in the intensive care unit (ICU). They may need things like a breathing tube connected to a breathing machine, blood tests multiple times a day, and around-the-clock medications to keep them alive. This kind of treatment in the ICU is often very hard, even painful for the patient.

All care in the ICU is based on the patient’s wishes, values, and beliefs, as well as what is possible to achieve with treatment. For example, some patients’ goal is to try everything to live as long as possible. Other patients may prefer not to get a lot of treatments that can cause pain and suffering at the end of their life. Some may prefer to leave the hospital or detach from medical supports like a breathing tube as soon as they can, even if it means a shorter life. Often family and close friends contribute to these discussions. Sometimes patients can tell their doctors what their wishes are directly, while other times, if the patient is really sick and not able to talk, their family and closest friends serve as a ‘proxy’ for the patient: they tell the doctors what the patient would want to have happen when they are not able to tell the doctors themselves.

ICU doctors will ask patients in the ICU who can talk what their wishes, values, and beliefs are, and then offer treatment choices that match those wishes, values, and beliefs. If a patient cannot talk, they will ask the ‘proxy’. Sometimes the best treatment available that matches what a patient wants switches from trying to treat their sickness to making sure they feel as comfortable as possible.

What happens in the hospital

Diagnosis

The ICU doctors work in a team with many types of caregivers. This team includes nurses, experts who manage the breathing tube (respiratory therapists), experts who manage the patient’s many medications (pharmacists), and more. Each day, they will check on the patient, talk about their medical condition, and talk about whether the treatment plans are working. The team may also order tests and imaging, start or stop medications, and talk to other kinds of doctors to get different opinions on how to manage different problems.

Every day, the team will assess whether the patient is getting better, whether they are staying the same, or whether they are getting worse, even with treatment. Deciding a patient is ‘getting better’ depends on what the patient’s values and goals are. The team will talk to the patient or their ‘proxy’ about their values and goals. Based on this, the medical team may change their focus to making a patient comfortable rather than prolonging their life. This can happen if they think that treatment is not helping the patient reach their care goals or if the patient is near the end of life.

Surgical options

Surgery at the end of life is rare, but sometimes it can help. For example, surgery can take out a tumor that is causing a lot of pain or blocking organs like the intestines from working well. Surgery can also make a new path around a blockage so things can flow better and hurt less. If the care team thinks surgery may help and fits with the patient’s goals, they may ask a surgeon to come see the patient.

Other support

A pastor can visit the ICU to give prayers and support to patients and families near the end of life. Social workers can also guide families with funeral plans and support them with their grief.

What happens next

Once comfort-focused care is started, the dying process can take hours to days. If the patient is in the ICU, the care team will shut off alarms and monitors to provide a quiet and peaceful environment. Sometimes they may move the patient to a quieter room on another floor to give the family more privacy.

What does death look like?

There are a few things that families may see as their loved one gets closer to their final hours:

A wet sound coming from the patient’s throat that is caused by extra fluid. This sound is often called the ‘death rattle’ and is not painful. The medical team can let you know if this is what you are hearing.

Location

If the care team thinks your loved one may live more than a few days and their wish was to leave the hospital, they may be able to leave the ICU. After leaving, they may be able to spend their last days at home or another place with hospice services, which is special care focusing on comfort and support at the end of life. Social workers or experts in care at the end of life can help you figure out what might be best for your family and loved one.

Diet

When a patient is going through end-of-life care, what they eat does not usually play a big role. Feeding tubes through the nose or mouth may be taken out to make the patient as comfortable as possible. While most patients tend not to eat or drink much during this time, sometimes a patient may express a want to eat or drink. They will be allowed to choose what they want to eat or drink by mouth, even if this was not allowed earlier in their treatment.
