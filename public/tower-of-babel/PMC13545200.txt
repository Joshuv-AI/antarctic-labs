The miR-206-3p/Cpeb1 axis delays acetylcholine receptor degradation and preserves neuromuscular junction stability in denervation-induced muscle atrophy
Authors: Guohua Jiang, Long Li, Yanhua Wang, Tianjing Zhao, Hao Guo, Han Yan, Jianwen Xu, Canjun Zeng, Yijun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeripheral nerve injury leads to progressive neuromuscular junction (NMJ) destabilization and acetylcholine receptor (AChR) degradation, which are critical drivers of denervation-induced muscle atrophy and impaired motor recovery. However, the post-transcriptional mechanisms regulating AChR stability during denervation remain poorly understood. Here, we investigated the role of miR-206-3p in NMJ maintenance and muscle preservation after denervation, with a focus on its interaction with the RNA-binding protein cytoplasmic polyadenylation element binding protein 1 (Cpeb1). Using C2C12 myoblasts and a sciatic nerve transection mouse model, we demonstrate that miR-206-3p promotes myogenic differentiation, enhances AChR clustering, and preserves postsynaptic AChR morphology. miR-206-3p directly targets the 3′ untranslated region of Cpeb1, suppressing its expression, as confirmed by dual-luciferase reporter assays. In vivo, adeno-associated virus–mediated overexpression of miR-206-3p delayed denervation-induced AChR fragmentation, attenuated muscle atrophy, and significantly improved motor function recovery. Conversely, Cpeb1 overexpression accelerated AChR degradation and muscle wasting, whereas co-overexpression of miR-206-3p mitigated these detrimental effects, indicating that Cpeb1 is a key downstream effector of miR-206-3p. Collectively, our findings identify the miR-206-3p/Cpeb1 axis as a previously unrecognized regulator of NMJ stability and muscle integrity after denervation, providing mechanistic insight and a potential therapeutic target for preserving neuromuscular function during prolonged denervation.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00018-026-06302-1.Keywords: MiR-206-3p, Cpeb1, Acetylcholine receptor, Neuromuscular junction, Peripheral nerve injury, Muscle atrophy

Introduction

Peripheral nerve injury (PNI) often leads to devastating motor deficits and muscle wasting. Even with surgical nerve repair, complete functional recovery is rare, especially after high-grade or prolonged denervation [1]. A key reason is the progressive dismantling of the neuromuscular junction (NMJ) in denervated muscle. Once a motor axon is severed, its distal synaptic terminals retract and the postsynaptic acetylcholine receptor (AChR) clusters at the motor endplate begin to fragment and disperse [2, 3].Within weeks, denervated endplates exhibit marked morphological changes: AChR-rich areas decrease in size and become more disorganized, with some endplates converting to an atrophic, plaque-like morphology [2, 4]. Functionally, denervation triggers a dramatic increase in AChR turnover and decreases the stability of remaining NMJs [4, 5]. This instability accelerates muscle fiber atrophy and fibrosis, and if reinnervation does not occur in time, endplates may be lost permanently. Indeed, prolonged denervation (beyond ~ 1 year in humans) greatly increases the probability of irreversible motor endplate degeneration and muscle atrophy [3]. Thus, maintaining NMJ integrity during the denervation period is critical for preserving muscle viability and optimizing the outcomes of delayed reinnervation [3, 4].

Skeletal muscle actively responds to denervation stress at the molecular level. One prominent response is the altered expression of muscle-specific microRNAs (myomiRs) that regulate neuromuscular plasticity [6, 7]. MiR-206 is a muscle-enriched miRNA (“myomiR”) known to promote muscle regeneration and neuromuscular synapse formation. For example, injecting cardiotoxin to induce muscle injury causes a surge in miR-206, whereas experimental knockdown of miR-206 delays muscle regeneration [8, 9]. In chronic muscle diseases and models of nerve injury, miR-206 is induced as part of a compensatory regenerative program [7, 8]. Notably, the overexpression of miR-206 in mouse models of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) or Duchenne muscular dystrophy can improve outcomes: miR-206 overexpression promotes NMJ reinnervation and has been shown to delay disease progression in ALS model mice [8, 10]. These findings highlight miR-206-3p (the active mature strand of miR-206) as a key regulator of synaptic maintenance and muscle regeneration. However, the role of miR-206 is complex. It orchestrates a network of gene targets in muscle, some of which may be beneficial and others detrimental to NMJ stability. Recent transcriptomic studies indicate that miR-206 can “fine-tune” the expression of various NMJ-related mRNAs; for example, it directly downregulates fundamental synaptic proteins such as rapsyn and α-dystrobrevin in muscle cells [7]. Excess miR-206 has been shown to disrupt agrin-induced AChR clustering in vitro, suggesting that an overactive miR-206 response might contribute to NMJ destabilization even as it attempts to aid reinnervation. This dual nature of miR-206 calls for careful investigation of its downstream targets and interactions in the context of denervation.

One possible downstream target of miR-206-3p in denervated muscle is Cpeb1 (cytoplasmic polyadenylation element binding protein 1). Cpeb1 encodes an RNA-binding protein that regulates mRNA translation by modulating polyadenylation, and it has well-established roles in activity-dependent synaptic plasticity [11]. In neurons, Cpeb1 helps drive the protein synthesis required for long-term potentiation and memory consolidation [11]. In skeletal muscle, Cpeb1 has recently been implicated in regenerative processes: it is highly expressed in quiescent satellite cells and is required for their activation and proliferation, in part by promoting translation of the myogenic regulator MyoD [12]. By directing the translational program of muscle stem cells, Cpeb1 facilitates effective muscle regeneration after injury [12]. Given these functions, Cpeb1 is biologically positioned at the nexus of local protein synthesis, synaptic stability, and muscle plasticity. We hypothesized that during denervation, miR-206-3p may target Cpeb1 and thereby influence the stability of AChR clusters and the atrophy of muscle fibers. Cpeb1 mRNA contains predicted miR-206 binding sites, and a regulatory axis between miR-206 and Cpeb1 could link the muscle injury-induced microRNA response to the control of mRNA translation at the NMJ.

In this study, we examined the miR-206-3p/Cpeb1 interaction in a model of denervation-induced muscle atrophy. We showed that miR-206-3p is upregulated in denervated muscle and directly suppresses Cpeb1 expression. We further demonstrated that this interaction delays postsynaptic AChR degradation and preserves NMJ function in the absence of innervation. These findings reveal a novel mechanism by which a muscle-specific microRNA modulates translational regulators to influence NMJ stability, providing new insight into the molecular events underlying denervation atrophy. Understanding this pathway could inform therapeutic strategies to preserve NMJs and skeletal muscle mass after denervation.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and differentiation

The C2C12 mouse myoblast cell line (SCC-221512, Solarbio, China) was cultured in high-glucose DMEM (C11995500BT, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (10,270–106, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (15,140,122, Gibco, USA) in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, with media changes every 2 days. For differentiation, when the cells reached approximately 60–70% confluence, we induced differentiation by replacing the growth medium with high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 2% horse serum (C2510-0100, Vivacell, China) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. The differentiation medium was changed every 2 days, and cells were allowed to differentiate for 5–7 days until myotube formation was evident. This time point corresponds to the late differentiation stage with fully mature multinucleated myotubes.

Cell transfection

For knockdown of miR-206-3p, C2C12 cells were infected with a lentivirus expressing an anti-miR-206-3p sequence driven by the CMV promoter and labeled with ZsGreen (Hanbio Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), together with its corresponding negative control. For gain-of-function studies, miR-206-3p and Cpeb1 were overexpressed using lentiviral vectors (GeneChem, Shanghai, China). The mmu-miR-206 sequence was cloned into a Ubiquitin-driven lentiviral backbone (LV-mmu-miR-206: Ubi-MCS-SV40-EGFP-IRES-puromycin), allowing bicistronic expression of miR-206 and EGFP via an IRES element. For Cpeb1 overexpression, the full-length mouse Cpeb1 coding sequence together with its native 3′ untranslated region (3′UTR), including the predicted miR-206-3p binding site, was synthesized by GeneChem based on the NCBI reference sequence and inserted into the CMV-MCS-EF1α-neomycin lentiviral backbone (LV-Cpeb1). The integrity of the synthesized Cpeb1 sequence and its 3′UTR was verified by Sanger sequencing prior to viral packaging. Corresponding control viruses (LV-CON238 for miR-206 and LV-CON681 for Cpeb1) contained the same promoter and reporter cassettes but lacked the respective inserts.

For infection, cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of (1–5) × 10^5 cells/mL and infected when they reached ~ 30–50% confluence. After removing the old medium, fresh medium, HiTransG P transduction reagent, lentivirus (MOI = 50) and polybrene (3 μg/mL) were added sequentially (Fig. S1A-B). Plates were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 72 h. Transduction efficiency (> 80% EGFP-positive) was confirmed by fluorescence microscopy before downstream assays. For co-overexpression, LV-Cpeb1 (neomycin resistance) was added to cells already overexpressing miR-206; 48 h after infection, Geneticin (G418) was applied for selection. Resistant cells were isolated by limiting dilution, and single-cell clones were picked, expanded, and validated for transgene expression. Only clones with stable CPEB1 and miR-206-3p expression were used for subsequent experiments.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA from cells and tissues was extracted via TRIzol (9109, Takara, Japan). RNA purity was assessed via a NanoPhotometer (Thermo). RNA with an OD260/OD280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0 was reverse-transcribed via the PrimeScriptTM RT MasterMix Kit (RR036A, Takara, Japan). Stem-loop primers were used for specific miRNA reverse transcription. The RT-qPCR results were analyzed via the 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak method). The primers used for RT-qPCR are listed in Table 1.

Western blot

Proteins from cells and tissues were extracted via RIPA buffer (PR20035, Proteintech, China) containing protease inhibitors (PR20032, Proteintech, China). The lysates were subsequently centrifuged at 12,000 × g at 4 °C for 15 min. The supernatants were transferred to new tubes, mixed with loading buffer, and heated at 100 °C for 10 min. Proteins were separated via SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, including mouse anti-GAPDH (1:10,000, 60004–1-Ig, Proteintech, China), mouse anti-MHC (1:2000, ab37484, Abcam, UK), rabbit anti-CHRNA1 (α1) (1:1000, 10613–1-AP, Proteintech, China), rabbit anti-CHRNB1 (β1) (1:1000, 11,553–1-AP, Proteintech, China), mouse anti-CHRND (δ) (1:5000, NB120-2804, Novus Biologicals, USA), rabbit anti-CHRNE (ε) (1:1000, 55360–1-AP, Proteintech, China), rabbit anti-CHRNG (γ) (1:1000, NBP2-93606, Novus Biologicals, USA), and rabbit anti-Cpeb1 (1:4000, 13274–1-AP, Proteintech, China) antibodies. After being washed with TBST, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Proteins were detected via enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) and X-ray films.

Immunofluorescence staining

Myotube Immunofluorescence staining: Cells were cultured in 24-well plates at an appropriate density and differentiated for 7 days. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, washed three times with PBS, and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 15 min. Blocking was performed with goat serum (AR0009, Boster, USA) at room temperature for 1 h. For immunofluorescence staining for MHC, samples were incubated with a mouse anti-MHC primary antibody (ab37484, Abcam, 1:300) overnight at 4 °C. The next day, cells were incubated with DyLight 594-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) secondary antibody (E032410-02, Earthox, 1:800) at room temperature in the dark for 1 h. For immunofluorescence staining for CHRNA1, cells were incubated with a rabbit anti-CHRNA1 primary antibody (10613–1-AP, Proteintech, 1:50) overnight at 4 °C. The next day, samples were incubated with DyLight 594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) secondary antibody (E032420-01, Earthox, 1:800) at room temperature in the dark for 1 h. Finally, the nuclei were stained with DAPI for 10 min.

AChR staining and quantification in C2C12 myotubes

C2C12 myoblasts were seeded in confocal dishes and differentiated into myotubes for 7 days. For AChR labeling, cells were incubated with 1.0 µg/mL Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated α-bungarotoxin (α-BTX; B35450, Invitrogen) at 37 °C for 1 h in the dark, followed by three PBS washes and fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. Imaging was performed using an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope. ImageJ software converted images to 8-bit binary, excluding non-specific spots < 15 μm2 (valid clusters)[13]. Quantification included valid clusters per 100 μm myotube and cluster area (manual outlining). Each group had ≥ 3 biological replicates, with ≥ 25 myotubes analyzed per replicate.

Dual luciferase reporter assays

Dual-luciferase reporter assays were performed using a Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (Hanbio Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). HEK293T cells were seeded in 96-well plates and transfected at ~ 50–70% confluence. For each well, 0.16 μg m-Cpeb1-3UTR reporter plasmid and 5 pmol mmu-miR-206-3p mimic (or negative control) were diluted in 10 μL DMEM, while 0.3 μL transfection reagent (0.8 mg/mL) was diluted in 10 μL DMEM. After 5 min, the two solutions were mixed and incubated for 20 min at room temperature, then added to cells. The medium was replaced after 6 h, and luciferase activity was measured 48 h post-transfection.

For detection, 5 × lysis buffer was diluted to 1 × working solution, and luciferase reagents were prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were washed once with PBS and lysed with 30 μL 1 × lysis buffer per well for 15 min at room temperature. Lysates were centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 1 min, 4 °C), and 20 μL supernatant was used for measurement. Firefly luciferase activity was measured after adding 100 μL Luciferase Reaction Reagent, followed by Renilla luciferase measurement after adding 100 μL Luciferase Reaction Reagent II using a microplate luminometer. Relative luciferase activity was calculated as the firefly/Renilla ratio.

Animals

Male SPF-grade C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old, weighing 22–25 g) were purchased and housed at the Experimental Animal Management Center of Southern Medical University. The SPF facility maintained a temperature range of 20–26 °C, a relative humidity of 40–70%, and a 12 h/12 h light–dark cycle, with ad libitum access to food and water. This study was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Southern Medical University (Approval No.: SMUL202406033).

Adeno-associated virus injections in mice

For in vivo experiments, AAV9 vectors were used to overexpress mmu-miR-206-3p (AAV9-miR-206) or Cpeb1 (AAV9-Cpeb1) under the CMV promoter, with EGFP as a reporter. All vectors were constructed on the CMV-bGlobin-EGFP-MCS-WPRE-hGH polyA backbone (GeneChem, Shanghai, China). For mmu-miR-206-3p overexpression, the corresponding control virus was AAV9-CON336, which carried the same CMV promoter and EGFP cassette but lacked the mmu-miR-206-3p insert. For Cpeb1 overexpression, the corresponding control virus was AAV9-CON667, which carried the same CMV promoter and EGFP cassette but lacked the Cpeb1 insert. Viruses were injected into the right tibialis anterior (TA) muscle at four different sites, 5 μL per site, for a total of 20 μL at a concentration of 1 × 1011 vg/mL per mouse. For co-overexpression of miR-206-3p and Cpeb1 in vivo, AAV-miR-206 was injected first, and AAV-Cpeb1 was administered after miR-206-3p reached stable expression. Denervation surgery for establishing the animal model was performed 4 weeks after viral injection.

Animal models: denervation

The mice were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (50–60 mg/kg). The right hind limb was shaved and disinfected, and a 1 cm skin incision was made along the posterior aspect of the thigh following the course of the sciatic nerve. The subcutaneous tissue was carefully dissected to expose the right sciatic nerve. The sciatic nerve was transected 5 mm proximal to its bifurcation, creating a 3 mm nerve defect. The proximal and distal nerve stumps were ligated with 5–0 nylon sutures and sutured in reverse to the adjacent muscle to prevent nerve regrowth. The wound was closed with 4–0 sutures. Muscle tissue samples were collected before sciatic nerve transection and at 2, 4, and 8 weeks post-transection.

Footprint assay

A footprint assay was used to assess motor coordination at 8 weeks post-surgery. The paws of the mice were dipped in nontoxic dye, with the forepaws stained black and the hind paws stained red. The mice were allowed to walk along a track, leaving footprints on a sheet of paper. Each mouse was tested 2–3 times. Motor coordination deficits affect gait and footprint patterns; when normal mice walk in a straight line, they typically place the ipsilateral hind paw near or at the same position as the corresponding forepaw.

NMJ morphology and AChR quantification in Vivo

Mice were tail-vein injected with 0.3 µg/g Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated α-BTX (B35450, Invitrogen) to label AChRs. Two hours later, mice were anesthetized, cardiac-perfused with PBS and 4% paraformaldehyde, and tibialis anterior (TA) muscles dissected. Muscles were post-fixed overnight at 4 °C, dehydrated sequentially in 20% and 30% sucrose for 24 h each, embedded in OCT, and snap-frozen. Serial 100 μm sections were cut with a Leica CM1950 cryostat. Confocal Z-stack images were maximum-intensity projected. Blinded ImageJ analysis included NMJs per mm2 of muscle surface (five random fields), cluster area (manual outlining), and morphology (mature pretzel-shaped vs. fragmented ≥ 2 discontinuous fragments)[14]. Each group included ≥ 3 mice, with ≥ 50 intact NMJs analyzed per mouse to ensure statistical reliability.

Muscle wet weight measurement and histological analysis

Before surgery and at 2, 4, and 8 weeks after surgery, bilateral TA muscles were harvested and gently blotted to remove excess fluid. The wet weight of each muscle was measured on an analytical balance, and muscle wet weight ratio was calculated as: (wet weight of the operated TA/wet weight of the contralateral TA) × 100%.

For histological analysis, TA muscles were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, followed by dehydration and paraffin embedding. The embedded muscle tissues were sectioned transversely into 5 µm-thick slices via a semiautomatic paraffin microtome (Leica HistoCore Autocut, Germany). After deparaffinization and hydration, the sections were stained with an H&E staining kit (C0105S, Beyotime, China). The slides were air-dried, mounted with neutral resin, and photographed via an Olympus upright fluorescence microscope. Muscle fiber cross-sectional area (CSA) was quantified using ImageJ (Fiji) software by manual or threshold-assisted outlining of individual myofiber borders. For each muscle, ≥ 200 fibers were measured from three non-consecutive mid-belly sections to obtain a representative CSA distribution, and the mean CSA per muscle was calculated for group comparisons.

Immunohistochemistry

The tissue sections were deparaffinized and hydrated, followed by antigen retrieval via Tris–EDTA buffer (C1038, Solarbio, China) at 65 °C for 2 h. The sections were then incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 15 min to block endogenous peroxidase activity, followed by blocking with goat serum for 1 h at room temperature. rabbit anti-CHRNA1 antibody (10,613–1-AP, Proteintech, 1:50) was added, and the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C. The next day, secondary antibodies were applied, and the samples were incubated at room temperature for 2 h. Staining was performed via a DAB staining kit (ZLI-9017, ZS, China), and the nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. After air-drying, the sections were mounted with neutral resin.

After staining, images were acquired under identical microscope settings. CHRNA1 immunoreactivity was quantified using ImageJ by applying the same threshold to all groups to define DAB-positive signals. Five randomly selected non-overlapping fields were analyzed per section, and at least three sections per sample were evaluated. The percentage of DAB-positive area (DAB-positive area/total tissue area) was used as the primary measurement, and values from all fields/sections were averaged to obtain one value per animal. Relative CHRNA1 expression was then normalized to the mean value of the control group and presented as fold change.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis and visualization were performed via GraphPad Prism 9.5 and ImageJ. Differences between groups were analyzed via Student’s t test for two-group comparisons. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used. When a significant difference was found by ANOVA, post hoc comparisons were performed using Tukey's test to identify specific group differences. All values are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

miR-206-3p promotes C2C12 differentiation and AChR clustering

The expression of miR-206-3p was detected in the gastrocnemius muscle of mice at 1 and 2 weeks postsciatic nerve transection. The RT-qPCR results revealed a significant increase in miR-206-3p expression at 2 weeks postdenervation (Fig. 1A). To investigate the effects of miR-206-3p on skeletal muscle cells, C2C12 myoblasts were transfected with lentiviral constructs to establish miR-206-3p knockdown, knockdown control, overexpression, and overexpression control models. The transfection efficiency (> 80%) was confirmed via GFP fluorescence (Fig. 1B), and the expression levels of miR-206-3p in these models were validated (Fig. 1C). C2C12 myoblasts were transfected and then differentiated in differentiation medium (DM). RT-qPCR analysis revealed a gradual increase in miR-206-3p expression over 9 days of differentiation (Fig. 1D). Gene expression analysis of MyoD, MyoG, Myf5, and MHC on day 7 of differentiation revealed that miR-206-3p knockdown inhibited their expression, whereas overexpression had the opposite effect (Fig. 1E). Subsequently, we performed Western blot analysis to examine MHC protein expression. The results were consistent with the gene expression data: miR-206-3p knockdown suppressed MHC protein expression, whereas overexpression increased it (Fig. 1F and G). In addition, miR-206-3p knockdown reduced the number of myotubes on day 7 of differentiation, while overexpression increased myotube formation (Fig. 1H and I). These findings demonstrate that miR-206-3p plays an important role in promoting C2C12 myoblast differentiation. To explore the effects of miR-206-3p on AChR clustering, α-BTX staining revealed sparse AChR clusters on the cell membrane in miR-206-3p-knockdown cells, whereas miR-206-3p overexpression significantly increased the number and area of AChR clusters (Fig. 1J and K). The immunofluorescence results revealed increased CHRNA1 expression levels in the miR-206-3p-overexpressing cells (Fig. 1L and M). RT-qPCR (Fig. S1C) and Western blot (Fig. 1N) analyses demonstrated that miR-206-3p knockdown reduced the expression of AChR subunits (Chrna1, Chrnb1, Chrnd, Chrne, Chrng) and clustering-related genes (Sorbs2, Rapsyn, Lama4, Lamb2), whereas overexpression increased their expression.

miR-206-3p targets and regulates Cpeb1

Using the MR-microT, TargetScan, and miRBase databases, 310 target genes of miR-206-3p were predicted, and GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed. GO analysis revealed significant molecular function enrichment of target genes in neuronal synapses, suggesting their close relationship with neuromuscular junctions (Fig. 2A). KEGG analysis further identified pathways such as ‘Synaptic vesicle cycle’ and ‘Axon guidance’, both essential for NMJ formation and function [15, 16], indicating a strong association of miR-206-3p with NMJ regulation through coordinated control of multiple synapse-related genes (Fig. 2B). The Maximal Clique Centrality (MCC) algorithm, a topological analysis method used in cytoHubba to evaluate the importance of nodes in a protein–protein interaction network [17], revealed that Cpeb1 was ranked third among the miR-206-3p target genes in terms of protein interaction potential. A binding site for miR-206-3p was identified in the 3′ UTR of Cpeb1 (Fig. 2C). Dual-luciferase reporter assays confirmed that cotransfection of 293 T cells with miR-206-3p and the wild-type Cpeb1 3′UTR significantly reduced luciferase activity. In contrast, the mutated Cpeb1 3′UTR and empty plasmid controls did not alter luciferase activity (Fig. 2D), indicating that Cpeb1 is a direct target of miR-206-3p. The overexpression of miR-206-3p significantly inhibited both the mRNA and protein expression levels of Cpeb1 in C2C12 cells (Fig. 2E-G). To further explore the regulatory mechanism by which Cpeb1 controls AChR expression, we performed bioinformatic analysis of the 3′UTRs of the five AChR subunit genes. Canonical cytoplasmic polyadenylation elements (CPEs), which are core binding sites for CPEB1, were identified in the 3′UTRs of CHRNA1, CHRND, and CHRNG (Fig. S2). These CPE motifs match the conserved consensus sequence and indicate that CPEB1 participates in the regulation of AChR subunit expression at the post-transcriptional/translational level.

miR-206-3p enhances myogenic differentiation and AChR clustering by suppressing Cpeb1

To further investigate the interaction between miR-206-3p and Cpeb1, Cpeb1 overexpression models were established in miR-206-3p-overexpressing cells. Five groups were created: no-virus group, miR-206 control + Cpeb1 control, miR-206 overexpression + Cpeb1 control, miR-206 control + Cpeb1 overexpression, and miR-206 overexpression + Cpeb1 overexpression. The overexpression efficiencies of miR-206 and Cpeb1 were confirmed via RT‒qPCR and Western blotting (> 70%) (Fig. 3A and B). To explore whether miR-206-3p influences differentiation via Cpeb1, the five groups of cells were cultured under differentiation conditions for seven days. Overexpression of miR-206-3p alters AChR morphology in uninjured mice

Considering that AAV9-mediated protein expression may require up to four weeks, we administered either miR-206-3p overexpression adenovirus or a negative control adenovirus into the right tibialis anterior muscle four weeks prior to right sciatic nerve transection. TA muscle samples were collected from uninjured mice at 4 weeks postinjection (Fig. 4A). Successful transfection was confirmed by GFP fluorescence, with a transfection efficiency of greater than 70% (Fig. 4B). miR-206-3p expression was significantly elevated in the overexpression group (Fig. 4C). Confocal microscopy revealed that, compared with the control, miR-206-3p overexpression increased the AChR cluster area by approximately 33.3%, although the number of AChR clusters remained unchanged (Fig. 4D and E). Morphological analysis categorized AChRs as either "pretzel-like" (mature, reticular with multiple perforations) or "fragmented" (immature, smaller with no perforations)(Fig. 4F). miR-206-3p overexpression reduced the proportion of fragmented AChRs by approximately 12.8% (Fig. 4G). Immunohistochemistry results showed that CHRNA1-positive signals were higher in the miR-206-3p overexpression group compared to the control group, suggesting that miR-206-3p overexpression promotes the formation of AChR clusters (Fig. 4H and I). Although miR-206-3p overexpression increased AChR size, it did not affect muscle fiber size or wet weight compared with that of the controls (Fig. 4J and K). However, RT-qPCR and Western blot analyses revealed that miR-206-3p overexpression upregulated the expression of AChR subunit genes and proteins (Fig. 4L and N) as well as clustering-related genes (Sorbs2, Rapsyn, Lama4, Lamb2) (Fig. 4M). Consistent with the results of the luciferase analysis, miR-206-3p overexpression downregulated Cpeb1 mRNA and protein levels in uninjured TA muscle (Fig. 4O).

miR-206-3p overexpression delays AChR degradation and muscle atrophy after denervation and promotes motor function recovery

To evaluate whether miR-206-3p overexpression protects AChRs after denervation, sciatic nerve transection was performed in mice. Successful transfection was confirmed by GFP fluorescence (Fig. S4A), and miR-206-3p expression levels were validated at 2, 4, and 8 weeks post-surgery (Fig. S4B). In control mice, pretzel-like AChRs decreased over time, with reductions in cluster number and area. In contrast, miR-206-3p overexpression delayed these reductions. At 2, 4, and 8 weeks postdenervation, miR-206-3p overexpression preserved the AChR cluster morphology and delayed the decrease in cluster number and area (e.g., 2 weeks: 78.9 ± 5.2% vs. 90.3 ± 7.7%; 4 weeks: 41.4 ± 10.4% vs. 76.9 ± 10.3%; 8 weeks: 24.2 ± 7.6% vs. 53.0 ± 6.5%)(Fig. 5A and E). Immunohistochemistry revealed sustained CHRNA1 expression in the miR-206-3p-overexpressing group, whereas CHRNA1 signals were nearly undetectable in the control group at 8 weeks (Fig. 5B and F). Muscle wet weight and cross-sectional area analyses revealed that, compared with the control, miR-206-3p overexpression reduced the rate of muscle atrophy (Fig. 5C and D). Motor function recovery was assessed via footprint analysis at 8 weeks postsurgery. The control mice exhibited impaired muscle strength, abnormal limb posture, and poor coordination. In contrast, miR-206-3p-overexpressing mice presented improved stride length, cadence, and coordination (Fig. 5G and H). Consistent with findings in uninjured mice, miR-206-3p overexpression postdenervation downregulated the relative mRNA and protein levels of Cpeb1 and upregulated both the mRNA and protein expression of AChR subunit and clustering-related genes in the TA muscle at 2, 4, and 8 weeks (Fig. 5I and J, Fig. S4C).

Discussion

In this study, we identified the RNA-binding protein Cpeb1 as a key target through which microRNA-206 preserves NMJ structure and muscle fiber integrity after denervation. Our data show that miR-206–mediated downregulation of Cpeb1 delays the degradation of synaptic AChRs and attenuates the muscle atrophy that normally follows loss of innervation. These findings expand our understanding of the proregenerative functions of miR-206, revealing a novel posttranscriptional mechanism for stabilizing neuromuscular synapses in the context of nerve injury. Importantly, they also shed light on an underappreciated contributor to NMJ disassembly activity-dependent mRNA translation governed by Cpeb1 and how modulating this process can improve outcomes after denervation.

Our results place Cpeb1 among the growing list of mRNA targets suppressed by miR-206 during the denervation response. Previous work has shown that miR-206 is induced in denervated skeletal muscle as part of an endogenous compensatory program aimed at maintaining the NMJ in conditions such as ALS and other neuromuscular diseases [9]. By repressing negative regulators of synaptic stability and muscle differentiation, miR-206 promotes an environment conducive to reinnervation. For example, miR-206 directly targets Hdac4, thereby inhibiting the HDAC4-myogenin axis, which drives the expression of atrophy genes and concomitantly represses synaptic gene programs [18, 19]. Consistent with this mode of action, forced overexpression of miR-206 in denervated muscle was shown to reduce HDAC4 levels and partially spare muscle from atrophy [19, 20]. miR-206 also attenuates TGF-β/Smad signaling and fibrosis in denervated muscle by targeting key pathway components [20]. Indeed, in our mouse model, muscles expressing high levels of miR-206 (via viral delivery) had larger myofiber cross-sectional areas and more intact endplates following denervation than controls did, an outcome that parallels the protective effects of miR-206 reported by others [20]. However, how miR-206 specifically stabilizes AChR clusters, which are otherwise rapidly lost when neural activity ceases, remains unclear. Our study addresses this gap by pinpointing Cpeb1 as a translational regulator that promotes AChR cluster dismantling and demonstrating that miR-206 can counteract the effects of Cpeb1.

Cpeb1 has not been previously linked to NMJ maintenance, but its known functions in other systems suggest a plausible role in regulating synaptic components. Cpeb1 is best known for controlling mRNA polyadenylation and translation in contexts such as oocyte maturation and neuronal plasticity [11, 21]. In neurons, Cpeb1 can drive the local protein synthesis required for synaptic strengthening, but it may also facilitate turnover of synaptic proteins under certain conditions [22]. Our findings indicate that in skeletal muscle, Cpeb1 promotes the turnover or destabilization of the postsynaptic machinery at the NMJ during denervation. We observed that overexpression of Cpeb1 in muscle fibers accelerated AChR loss and exacerbated muscle fiber atrophy after nerve transection, whereas miR-206 overexpression had the opposite effect, preserving AChR clusters and muscle fiber size. One interpretation is that Cpeb1 normally stimulates the translation of mRNAs encoding proteins that trigger AChR endocytosis or degradation in inactive muscle. For example, Cpeb1 might upregulate factors involved in autophagy or the ubiquitin–proteasome system that specifically targets synaptic proteins. Notably, denervation is associated with increased expression of the AChR γ-subunit (CHRNG) and other embryonic synaptic components [23], which are thought to form less stable receptors. Cpeb1 could conceivably contribute to this switch by enhancing translation of embryonic AChR subunit mRNAs at the expense of adult subunits. By suppressing Cpeb1, miR-206 may tilt the balance back toward preserving mature AChR complexes. In support of this notion, our in vitro experiments in C2C12 myotubes revealed that miR-206 overexpression prevented Cpeb1-induced decreases in AChR cluster number and size. Thus, miR-206 likely stabilizes the NMJ postsynaptic structure in part by restraining Cpeb1’s translational activation of genes that would otherwise destabilize AChR clusters.

An interesting aspect of the miR-206/Cpeb1 axis is how it interacts with muscle cell differentiation. We found that miR-206–mediated Cpeb1 knockdown not only preserved AChR clusters but also promoted the expression of myogenic markers (e.g., myogenin, MyHC) and the formation of multinucleated myotubes in vitro. These findings suggest that Cpeb1 normally acts as a brake on myogenic differentiation and that the release of miR-206 is inhibited. Indeed, Cpeb1 was recently shown to maintain satellite cells in a quiescent state by limiting the translation of pro-differentiation mRNAs [12]. Its downregulation is required for satellite cells to activate and fuse into regenerating fibers [12]. During denervation, muscle undergoes attempted regeneration in parallel with atrophy; miR-206 is known to be produced by satellite cells and newly formed myotubes in regenerating regions [9, 24]. Our data suggest that miR-206 promotes muscle regeneration by relieving Cpeb1-mediated repression of differentiation-related transcripts, thereby accelerating repair. This also helps stabilize nascent NMJs by preventing inappropriate AChR removal. This coordination ensures that new myotubes quickly form functional synapses. Thus, the miR-206/Cpeb1 axis couples muscle differentiation with NMJ preservation. Future studies should identify the key mRNA targets of Cpeb1 in this context, such as rapsyn, MuSK, or Chrng, whose misregulated translation may disrupt AChR clustering and stability [25].This speculative mechanism merits investigation.

Our findings must also be interpreted in light of other studies that examined the function of miR-206 in muscle atrophy. While miR-206 is generally viewed as beneficial for NMJ regeneration, not all experiments have shown overt protective effects on muscle mass. Winbanks et al., for example, reported that AAV-mediated overexpression or inhibition of miR-206 in mouse muscle had a minimal effect on muscle fiber size during disuse atrophy or denervation, despite clearly regulating HDAC4 levels [18]. The authors concluded that miR-206 might be dispensable for the gross atrophic response in adult muscle under these conditions [18]. In contrast, our study and others have demonstrated that miR-206 can mitigate atrophy in certain contexts [20]. One reason for this discrepancy may be the experimental timeline and assessment endpoints. Winbanks et al. evaluated muscle mass and fiber size only two weeks after the sciatic nerve was cut-an early phase when degeneration may overshadow the regenerative effects of miR-206. However, their focus on muscle weight might miss subtle benefits, such as NMJ preservation. In contrast, our study examined later time points and emphasized the NMJ structure and AChR retention. MiR-206 may not immediately prevent atrophy but instead preserve NMJ architecture, which facilitates reinnervation and functional recovery over time. In addition, the discrepancy between our findings and those of Barden et al. likely arises from fundamental differences in their cell culture paradigms. Barden and colleagues used an agrin-induced AChR clustering assay, in which high-level miR-206 overexpression suppressed key synaptic organizers such as rapsyn and α-syntrophin, thereby reducing agrin-triggered cluster formation [26]. In contrast, our experiments examined spontaneous AChR clustering during C2C12 differentiation without exogenous agrin, where miR-206-3p primarily enhanced myogenic maturation and upregulated AChR expression, indirectly increasing cluster number. Variations in miR-206 dosage, timing of introduction, and whether AChR clustering is agrin-dependent or differentiation-driven may therefore produce distinct outcomes across studies. Another consideration is the redundancy among myomiRs. MiR-206 is part of a family that includes the closely related miR-1 and miR-133. Compensatory upregulation of other myomiRs in miR-206 gain- or loss-of-function models might mask some effects on muscle size [27]. However, the unique enrichment of miR-206 at NMJs suggests that it has a more specialized role in synaptic maintenance than does the incomplete duplication of miR-1/133 [28]. Our identification of Cpeb1 as a target provides a concrete mechanism distinct from those of miR-1/133 (which primarily influence myocyte proliferation and metabolic pathways) [9, 29]. This specialization could explain why miR-206 is particularly crucial for synaptic regeneration, as evidenced by the severe NMJ reinnervation deficits observed in miR-206 knockout mice [9].

Therapeutically targeting the miR-206/Cpeb1 axis represents a novel strategy to combat denervation-induced muscle wasting after PNI. MiR-206 is a muscle-enriched microRNA that is strongly upregulated in denervated muscle, where it promotes compensatory NMJ maintenance [10]. One likely target is Cpeb1, a cytoplasmic polyadenylation element-binding protein involved in local mRNA translation. By repressing Cpeb1, miR-206 may prevent the aberrant translation of factors that destabilize synaptic components, thereby preserving postsynaptic acetylcholine receptor (AChR) clusters and NMJ structure. This preservation has been linked to delayed muscle atrophy in denervation models [30]. Harnessing this axis, one could deliver miR-206 to injured muscles via an AAV vector or synthetic miR-206 mimics, approaches that have shown efficacy in reducing denervation atrophy and fibrosis in rodents [20]. Conversely, an antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) against Cpeb1 mRNA could mimic the effects of miR-206 by directly downregulating Cpeb1, stabilizing NMJ architecture at the molecular level. Notably, such a gene-focused intervention contrasts with conventional therapies (electrical stimulation, growth factor delivery, stem cell transplants) that act more indirectly. Electrical stimulation can retard muscle atrophy by substituting for neural input [31], and exogenous neurotrophic support (e.g., via BDNF/GDNF or cell therapy) helps sustain muscle and endplate integrity [32]. However, these methods do not intrinsically reprogram muscle atrophy. In contrast, enhancing miR-206/Cpeb1 signaling engages the muscle’s own proregenerative network: miR-206 synergistically downregulates multiple atrophy-associated genes (e.g., HDAC4 and ubiquitin ligases) and enhances synaptic regrowth, a multifaceted action unattainable by single-factor therapies. This muscle-intrinsic approach could therefore complement or surpass traditional treatments, yielding more sustained NMJ preservation and accelerating functional recovery after PNI.

This study has several limitations. First, reliable signals of synaptic vesicle protein 2 (SV2), a specific presynaptic marker, could not be detected, likely due to extensive degeneration of presynaptic terminals after long-term nerve transection. Second, sex-specific effects cannot be excluded because only male mice were used; future studies including both male and female animals will be required to determine whether the observed effects of miR-206 on AChR clustering and neuromuscular junction repair are sex dependent. Third, we did not directly inhibit Cpeb1 in vitro or in vivo to determine whether Cpeb1 knockdown alone reproduces the protective effects of miR-206 overexpression. Future studies will be needed to clarify whether targeted suppression of Cpeb1 is sufficient to mimic the actions of miR-206 on AChR clustering and neuromuscular junction repair.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that miR-206-3p preserves acetylcholine receptors, stabilizes neuromuscular junctions, and attenuates denervation-induced muscle atrophy by targeting Cpeb1, thereby promoting motor function recovery. Our findings identify Cpeb1 as a key regulator of synaptic destabilization and underscore the miR-206/Cpeb1 axis as a promising therapeutic target for enhancing NMJ maintenance and muscle regeneration following denervation.
