Bilateral wrist masses as first manifestation of wild-type transthyretin amyloidosis in a case with recurrent carpal tunnel syndrome: a case report
Authors: Khalil Kargar Shooroki, Parisa Mokhles, Meisam Jafari Kafiabadi, Shahabaldin Beheshti Fard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCarpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) is the most common peripheral neuropathy in the general population. Recurrent bilateral CTS could be the first manifestation of a more serious systemic disease, such as amyloidosis, which would help with early diagnosis and prevent mortality and morbidity associated with this illness.Case presentationWe present a rare case of a 75-year-old Middle Eastern man suffering from bilateral wrist edema with weakness and numbness of both hands and a history of recurrent bilateral CTS. The bilateral wrist masses were revealed at the MRI investigation. He was treated with surgical resection, and amyloid deposits were confirmed with pathology evaluation. He remains symptom-free after 4 years of follow-up.ConclusionThis case reinforces that forearm masses in the context of recurrent CTS should prompt evaluation for amyloidosis. Early biopsy and systemic workup can lead to timely diagnosis, improve prognosis, and guide targeted therapies. Future research should explore screening protocols for amyloidosis in high-risk CTS populations.

Introduction

Carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) is the most common focal compression neuropathy, with a prevalence of 1 to 5% in the general population [1]. The lifetime prevalence of carpal tunnel release (CTR) is approximately 3.1% overall [2]. Although the majority of cases are idiopathic, there is a well-known relationship between amyloidosis and CTS as a secondary cause [1]. Amyloidosis is an infiltrative disease caused by the extracellular deposition of aggregated insoluble fibrils and misfolded precursor proteins [3]. There are two main types of amyloidosis: amyloid light chain (AL) and transthyretin amyloidosis (ATTR). AL amyloidosis involves an excessive production of light chain immunoglobulin by plasma cells in the bone marrow and overlaps with multiple myeloma in 20% of cases. ATTR amyloidosis results from the misfolding of the transthyretin protein, which transports retinol and thyroxine, secreted from the liver into the bloodstream. ATTR has two subtypes: hereditary (ATTR-v) and wild-type (ATTR-wt) [4]. ATTR-v is caused by mutations in the TTR gene, while ATTR-wt is idiopathic and a process associated with aging. ATTR is strongly associated with polyneuropathy and cardiomyopathy. CTS could be an early manifestation of ATTR, occurring years before severe complications like cardiomyopathy; therefore, it should be explicitly considered in patients with progressive bilateral CTS and a high recurrence rate. Taking a biopsy of the transverse carpal ligament (TCL) and volar flexor tendon tenosynovium in such patients would help confirm the diagnosis before severe complications, such as heart failure, present themselves [3].

We are presenting a rare case with bilateral wrist masses as the first presentation of ATTR-wt amyloidosis in a Middle Eastern man, with related cardiac signs at his echocardiography and electrocardiography (ECG) evaluations.

Case presentation

A 75-year-old Middle Eastern man presented on 03.04.2021 with a bilateral wrist mass and vague pain, weakness in both hands, and numbness in the first three fingers bilaterally. The patient had a history of carpal tunnel release twice, seven and three years ago, respectively, but they did not detect any mass at previous operations. In his medical history, he had high blood pressure and mild heart failure (HF) with ventricular hypertrophy and mitral regurgitation at echocardiography, and low voltage in the limb leads and atrial fibrillation rhythm at ECG. He did not have any systemic illness or any other peripheral neuropathy. He did not have any relevant familial history, and he was at his retirement time, and he did not have any hobbies or jobs to justify recurrent CTS. In his lab data, he only had high levels of triglyceride (TG) and cholesterol. On clinical examination, Tinel’s sign was positive bilaterally, and Phalen’s test was also positive at 30 s. Electromyography (EMG) and nerve conduction studies (NCS) confirmed bilateral active denervation of the median nerve at both carpal tunnels. There was no significant finding on plain radiography of the wrists (Fig. 1), but an MRI revealed a mass on the volar side of both wrists (Fig. 2). A core needle biopsy was performed on both masses on the volar side of the wrists. Pathologic examination confirmed the presence of amyloid deposition using hematoxylin and eosin, as well as Congo red stain and polarized microscopy. Salmon-pink coloration under normal illumination of Congo red-stained sections and apple-green birefringence under polarized illumination revealed amyloid deposition in the specimens (Fig. 3). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was used to distinguish the type of amyloidosis, and the result was wild-type ATTR amyloidosis. The patient was scheduled for bilateral CTR and mass resection. A lazy S-shaped incision was made on the palmar side of his hands, starting from the ulnar side of the thenar crease and extending proximally to the transverse carpal crease, then continuing straight to the middle of his forearm on the volar side. The masses were excised from the patient’s transverse carpal ligaments and his flexor tenosynovium (Fig. 4). There were no complications after surgery; neurological and vascular examinations were normal, and the wound healing process progressed well. The rehabilitation program consisted of physiotherapy, and range of motion exercises were started after two weeks; he had a brief weakness in his grasp for 3 months, but his active and passive range of motion was restored in the meantime. Discussion

ATTR-wt amyloidosis is an age-related systemic disease that is associated with severe and aggressive CTS and often bilateral at the time of diagnosis, with a higher rate of recurrence after carpal tunnel release (CTR), and is more frequent in men in comparison with idiopathic CTS with is much higher in females. So, these patients benefit from being treated with an extended CTR technique to reduce recurrence rates and symptom remission [2, 3].

This case highlights the importance of recognizing atypical presentations of carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS), like recurrent bilateral symptoms and associated forearm masses, as potential early indicators of systemic amyloidosis. The presence of bilateral volar wrist masses (approximately 3 cm3) in this patient, later confirmed as amyloid deposits, underscores the necessity of a comprehensive diagnostic approach to uncover underlying systemic disease. The discovery of volar wrist masses on MRI in this patient was pivotal. Although idiopathic CTS rarely presents with soft tissue masses, their presence should raise suspicion for infiltrative diseases such as amyloidosis. Imaging findings of tenosynovial thickening or nodular deposits should prompt biopsy, as seen in this case [5].

Wild-type transthyretin amyloidosis (ATTR-wt) is increasingly recognized as a cause of CTS; the prevalence of CTS in these patients is different, between 39 and 54% in the literature, particularly in elderly men, often preceding cardiac or neurological manifestations by years [1, 6–8]. Although the cumulative incidence of amyloidosis after CTR is relatively low, approximately 0.55% at 10 years [1], Biopsy of the transverse carpal ligament (TCL) and flexor tenosynovium during carpal tunnel release (CTR) can facilitate early diagnosis of amyloidosis [8]. Congo red staining with apple-green birefringence under polarized microscopy remains the gold standard for amyloid detection [9]. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) is essential to differentiate ATTR from AL amyloidosis [10]. Once ATTR-wt amyloidosis is confirmed, systemic evaluation for cardiac and neurological involvement is crucial [11]. Early diagnosis allows for timely intervention, such as tafamidis therapy for cardiac amyloidosis, which can improve outcomes [6].

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that elderly men with severe and bilateral and relapsing CTS could be a red flag for a more severe systemic disease such as ATTR-wt. These patients may benefit from more extended CTR with adequate synovectomy, especially patients with a pathology-confirmed wrist mass. Due to severe complications of late diagnosed ATTR-wt, such as heart failure, considering routine biopsy of TCL and tenosynovium in these patients might be beneficial.
