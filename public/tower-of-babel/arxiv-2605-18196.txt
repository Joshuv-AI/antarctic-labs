Localization of a quantum particle in a classical one-component plasma. II. Dynamic Disorder and Temporal Decorrelation
Yury A. Budkov

Abstract
We extend the static theory of disorder-induced exponential decay of the averaged Green function of a quantum charged particle in a classical one-component plasma to the dynamic regime by incorporating the temporal evolution of the ionic density fluctuations within the random phase approximation. The dynamic potential correlator is derived from the fluctuation-dissipation theorem and the Kramers–Kronig relations. Within the eikonal (straight-line) approximation, the effective disorder strength is expressed through the dielectric function of the ion plasma. For particles moving faster than the ion thermal speed, the static Coulomb logarithm is recovered, with the large-distance cutoff replaced by the dynamic scale v / ω p ​ i v/\omega_{pi} . For slow particles, the Coulomb logarithm disappears completely and the disorder strength becomes proportional to the velocity, leading to a fundamentally different scaling of the localization length. In particular, the strong-disorder length diverges as k − 1 / 3 k^{-1/3} for v ≪ v th v\ll v_{\mathrm{th}} , whereas it saturates in the static limit, indicating that ultra-slow particles are not exponentially localized in a dynamic plasma. A crossover between the quasi-static and dynamic regimes occurs when the particle speed becomes comparable to the ion thermal speed.



I
Introduction

The phenomenon of disorder-induced exponential decay of single-particle wave functions—Anderson localization—has
remained at the centre of condensed matter physics since its discovery
1
. The localization length

\ell
governs the metal–insulator transition and low-temperature transport in disordered systems
4
;
5
. While most studies have focused on short-range impurity potentials, there is a fundamental interest in
systems where the dominant interactions are Coulomb: electrolyte solutions, ionic liquids, and fully ionized plasmas

7
;
8
. In such systems screening produces a finite Debye length
\lambda_{D}
, but, as known from
classical plasma physics, the potential fluctuations themselves are not fully screened and retain a long-range
1/r
tail. The
interplay between this long-range character and quantum interference is largely unexplored, in spite of its potential
relevance for transport anomalies in dense plasmas and for the mobility of electrons in ionic fluids.

In Part I of this work we developed a static theory of disorder-induced exponential decay of the averaged Green
function for a quantum particle moving in a classical one-component plasma. The random potential was taken to be the
instantaneous electrostatic field produced by equilibrium thermal fluctuations of the ion density, described within the random
phase approximation (RPA). The key result was the emergence of a Coulomb logarithm
\ln(\kappa L)
in the expression for the
localization length—the length scale that characterizes the exponential decay of the disorder-averaged retarded Green
function. This logarithm originates from the unscreened
1/r
tail of the static potential correlator and is a direct consequence of the long-range nature of the Coulomb interaction.

Although the static approximation is expected to be valid for particles moving much faster than the ion thermal speed, the
ionic density fluctuations that generate the random potential are intrinsically dynamic. A real plasma supports collective modes
(ion-acoustic waves) and thermal motion of the ions, both of which introduce finite correlation times. When the velocity
of the test particle becomes comparable to or smaller than the ion thermal speed, the static picture of frozen disorder
breaks down. Thus, a consistent theory of quantum transport in plasmas must account for the temporal evolution of the ionic
fluctuations.

The purpose of the present paper (Part II) is to extend the formalism developed in Part I
2
to the dynamic regime. We retain
the same microscopic model—a classical one-component plasma of mobile ions—but now treat the density fluctuations as a
time-dependent Gaussian field whose spectral density is determined by the fluctuation-dissipation theorem and the RPA
dielectric function. We show how the equal-time (static) correlator is recovered via the Kramers–Kronig relations, and
we derive a compact expression for the effective disorder strength
G_{\mathrm{dyn}}
in the eikonal (straight-line)
approximation. Two limiting cases are analyzed analytically: fast particles (
v\gg v_{\mathrm{th}}
), for which the static
Coulomb logarithm is recovered with a dynamically determined cutoff, and slow particles (
v\ll v_{\mathrm{th}}
), for which
the logarithm disappears entirely and the disorder strength becomes proportional to the velocity. As a consequence, the
asymptotic formulas for the localization length change qualitatively, and a non-monotonic velocity dependence emerges.

II
Preliminaries: Path-integral formalism for a quantum particle in a dynamic random potential

We consider a non-relativistic quantum particle of mass
m
moving in a
time-dependent random potential
W(\mathbf{x},t)
.
The Hamiltonian is

H(t)=\frac{\mathbf{p}^{2}}{2m}+W(\mathbf{x},t).

(1)

The retarded time-dependent Green function (propagator) is defined as
9

G(\mathbf{x},t;\mathbf{y},0)=\theta(t)\,\langle\mathbf{x}|e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}H(\tau)d\tau}|\mathbf{y}\rangle,

(2)

where
\theta(t)
is the Heaviside step function. In the path-integral
representation it reads

G(\mathbf{x},t;\mathbf{y},0)=\theta(t)\int_{\mathbf{y}}^{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{D}\mathbf{x}(\tau)\,\exp\!\Bigg\{\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,\Big[\frac{m}{2}\dot{\mathbf{x}}^{2}(\tau)-W\big(\mathbf{x}(\tau),\tau\big)\Big]\Bigg\},

(3)

where the integral is taken over all paths
\mathbf{x}(\tau)
satisfying

\mathbf{x}(0)=\mathbf{y}
,
\mathbf{x}(t)=\mathbf{x}
.

It is convenient to separate the free-particle motion by writing

\mathbf{x}(\tau)=\mathbf{x}_{\rm cl}(\tau)+\boldsymbol{\xi}(\tau),\qquad\mathbf{x}_{\rm cl}(\tau)=\mathbf{y}+\frac{\tau}{t}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}),

(4)

with the fluctuation field
\boldsymbol{\xi}(\tau)
obeying

\boldsymbol{\xi}(0)=\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)=0
. Substituting (
4
)
into (
3
) and using the translational invariance of the
measure, one obtains

G(\mathbf{x},t;\mathbf{y},0)=G_{0}(\mathbf{x},t;\mathbf{y},0)\,\int\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{\xi}\,\exp\!\Bigg\{\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,\Big[\frac{m}{2}\dot{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{2}(\tau)-W\big(\mathbf{x}_{\rm cl}(\tau)+\boldsymbol{\xi}(\tau),\tau\big)\Big]\Bigg\},

(5)

where
G_{0}
is the free-particle propagator

G_{0}(\mathbf{x},t;\mathbf{y},0)=\theta(t)\,\Big(\frac{m}{2\pi i\hbar t}\Big)^{3/2}\exp\!\Big(\frac{im(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})^{2}}{2\hbar t}\Big).

(6)

We are interested in the Green function at a fixed energy

E=\hbar^{2}k^{2}/(2m)
. It is obtained by the Fourier transform
of the retarded propagator with an infinitesimally small positive
imaginary part added to the energy:

G_{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}(E+i0)t}\,G(\mathbf{x},t;\mathbf{y},0).

(7)

Inserting (
5
) and (
6
) into (
7
)
yields a double integral over
t
and over the path
\boldsymbol{\xi}
.

To cast the expression into a form suitable for the averaging over
disorder, we adopt the scaling procedure introduced by Efimov
3
.
We set

t=\frac{mr}{\hbar k}\,s,\qquad r=|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|,\qquad\mathbf{n}=\frac{\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}}{r},

(8)

and simultaneously rescale the integration variable
\tau
inside the
path integral as

\tau=\frac{ms}{\hbar k}\,u,\qquad\boldsymbol{\xi}(\tau)=\sqrt{\frac{s}{k}}\,\boldsymbol{\eta}(u),

(9)

with
u\in[0,r]
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}(0)=\boldsymbol{\eta}(r)=0
.
After these transformations the kinetic term becomes

\frac{i}{2}\int_{0}^{r}du\,\dot{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{2}(u)
, and the
classical path simplifies to

\mathbf{x}_{\rm cl}(\tau)=\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{n}u
.
The potential term transforms as

-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,W(\mathbf{x}_{\rm cl}+\boldsymbol{\xi},\tau)\;\longrightarrow\;-\frac{ims}{\hbar^{2}k}\int_{0}^{r}du\,W\!\Big(\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{n}u+\sqrt{\frac{s}{k}}\,\boldsymbol{\eta}(u),\,\frac{ms}{\hbar k}u\Big).

(10)

Consequently, the Green function takes the compact form

G_{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=B\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{ds}{s^{3/2}}\,\exp\!\Big[\frac{i}{2}kr\Big(s+\frac{1}{s}\Big)\Big]\int\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{\eta}\,\exp\!\Bigg\{\frac{i}{2}\int_{0}^{r}du\,\dot{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{2}(u)\\
-\frac{ims}{\hbar^{2}k}\int_{0}^{r}du\,W\!\Big(\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{n}u+\sqrt{\frac{s}{k}}\,\boldsymbol{\eta}(u),\,\frac{ms}{\hbar k}u\Big)\Bigg\},

(11)

where
B
is an irrelevant normalisation constant.

The random potential
W(\mathbf{x},t)
is taken to be a Gaussian
field with zero mean,

\langle W(\mathbf{x},t)\rangle=0,

(12)

and a prescribed pair correlation function

\langle W(\mathbf{x},t)W(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle=\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t-t^{\prime}).

(13)

The Gaussian property allows one to average the exponential in
(
11
) exactly:

\Big\langle\exp\!\Big[-\frac{ims}{\hbar^{2}k}\int_{0}^{r}du\,W\big(\dots\big)\Big]\Big\rangle_{W}\\
=\exp\!\Bigg[-\frac{m^{2}s^{2}}{2\hbar^{4}k^{2}}\int_{0}^{r}\!\!du\int_{0}^{r}\!\!du^{\prime}\,\mathcal{K}\!\Big(\mathbf{n}(u-u^{\prime})+\sqrt{\frac{s}{k}}\big(\boldsymbol{\eta}(u)-\boldsymbol{\eta}(u^{\prime})\big),\,\frac{ms}{\hbar k}(u-u^{\prime})\Big)\Bigg].

(14)

Thus the disorder-averaged Green’s function becomes

\langle G_{k}(r)\rangle_{W}=B\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{ds}{s^{3/2}}\,e^{\frac{i}{2}kr(s+1/s)}\int\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{\eta}\,\exp\!\Bigg\{\frac{i}{2}\int_{0}^{r}du\,\dot{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{2}(u)\\
-\frac{m^{2}s^{2}}{2\hbar^{4}k^{2}}\int_{0}^{r}\!\!du\int_{0}^{r}\!\!du^{\prime}\,\mathcal{K}\!\Big(\mathbf{n}(u-u^{\prime})+\sqrt{\frac{s}{k}}\big(\boldsymbol{\eta}(u)-\boldsymbol{\eta}(u^{\prime})\big),\,\frac{ms}{\hbar k}(u-u^{\prime})\Big)\Bigg\}.

(15)

Equation (
15
) is the exact starting point for studying
the exponential decay of the averaged propagator in a dynamic random
environment. In the following we employ the eikonal (straight-line)
approximation, which consists in neglecting the quantum fluctuations

\boldsymbol{\eta}(u)
and setting
\boldsymbol{\eta}=0
. This
approximation is valid when the de Broglie wavelength of the particle
is small compared to the correlation length of the disorder and the
particle moves fast enough. Within this approximation the functional
integral collapses to a factor
\exp[rA(s/k)]
with

A(s/k)=-\frac{m^{2}s^{2}}{2\hbar^{4}k^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\nu\,\mathcal{K}\!\Big(\mathbf{n}\nu,\,\frac{ms}{\hbar k}\nu\Big),

(16)

where we have taken the limit
r\to\infty
and used the translational
invariance of the correlation function. The remaining
s
-integral is
then evaluated in the saddle-point approximation, and the decay rate
of the averaged Green function is extracted as

\Gamma(k)=-\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{1}{r}\,\mathrm{Re}\ln\langle G_{k}(r)\rangle_{W}=-\mathrm{Re}\,\Phi(s_{c}),

(17)

with
\Phi(s)=\frac{ik}{2}(s+1/s)+A(s/k)
and
s_{c}
determined by

\Phi^{\prime}(s_{c})=0
. In this way the problem is reduced to the
computation of the single integral
A(s/k)
for a given dynamic
correlator
\mathcal{K}
.

III
Dynamic disorder in a one-component plasma

In the preceding section we developed the general path-integral
formalism for a quantum particle in a time-dependent Gaussian
random potential
W(\mathbf{x},t)
. We now specify the correlation
function
\mathcal{K}
to the physically relevant case of a
classical one-component plasma (OCP), where the fluctuating
potential is produced by the thermal motion of the ions.
The static limit of this model was studied in detail in Part I;
here we analyze the dynamic corrections to the
eikonal approximation.

III.1
Dynamic correlation function of the OCP

Some definitions.

We consider a classical one-component plasma of point-like ions
with charge
q
and average number density
n_{0}
, embedded in a
rigid uniform neutralizing background. The time-dependent
microscopic charge density is

\delta\rho_{c}(\mathbf{r},t)=q\sum_{j}\delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{j}(t))-qn_{0}
.
Its Fourier components are

\delta\rho_{c}(\mathbf{k},t)=\int d^{3}r\,e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\delta\rho_{c}(\mathbf{r},t).

The electrostatic potential produced by the ions and acting on
a test particle of charge
q_{0}
is given by Poisson’s equation

W(\mathbf{k},t)=\frac{4\pi q_{0}}{k^{2}}\,\delta\rho_{c}(\mathbf{k},t).

(18)

The dynamic structure factor of the charge density is defined as

\widetilde{S}_{\rho\rho}(k,\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\,e^{i\omega t}\,\langle\delta\rho_{c}(\mathbf{k},t)\,\delta\rho_{c}(-\mathbf{k},0)\rangle.

(19)

The spectral density of the potential follows immediately:

\widetilde{K}(k,\omega)=\Bigl(\frac{4\pi q_{0}}{k^{2}}\Bigr)^{\!2}\widetilde{S}_{\rho\rho}(k,\omega).

(20)

Response function and RPA.

The linear density response to an external potential
\phi_{\rm ext}(\mathbf{k},t)

is characterized by the retarded density–density response function

\chi_{\rho\rho}(k,\omega)
:

\langle\delta\rho_{c}(\mathbf{k},\omega)\rangle=-\chi_{\rho\rho}(k,\omega)\,\phi_{\rm ext}(\mathbf{k},\omega),

where the sign convention follows from the interaction Hamiltonian

H^{\prime}=\int d^{3}r\,\delta\rho_{c}(\mathbf{r},t)\phi_{\rm ext}(\mathbf{r},t)
.
In the random phase approximation (RPA) the full response is expressed
via the bare (free-ion) response
\chi_{0}(k,\omega)
as

\chi_{\rho\rho}(k,\omega)=\frac{\chi_{0}(k,\omega)}{1-\dfrac{4\pi}{k^{2}}\chi_{0}(k,\omega)}.

(21)

For a classical Maxwellian plasma the bare susceptibility is
6

\chi_{0}(k,\omega)=-\frac{n_{0}q^{2}}{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}\,\bigl[\,1+\xi\,Z(\xi)\,\bigr],\qquad\xi=\frac{\omega}{\sqrt{2}\,kv_{\rm th}},

(22)

with
v_{\rm th}=\sqrt{k_{\mathrm{B}}T/m_{i}}
and
Z(\xi)
the plasma
dispersion function. The longitudinal dielectric function is

\varepsilon(k,\omega)=1-\frac{4\pi}{k^{2}}\chi_{0}(k,\omega)=1+\frac{\kappa^{2}}{k^{2}}\bigl[1+\xi Z(\xi)\bigr],\qquad\kappa^{2}=\frac{4\pi n_{0}q^{2}}{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}.

(23)

From (
21
) and (
23
) one obtains the
useful identity

\chi_{\rho\rho}(k,\omega)=\frac{k^{2}}{4\pi}\Bigl(\frac{1}{\varepsilon(k,\omega)}-1\Bigr),\qquad\operatorname{Im}\chi_{\rho\rho}(k,\omega)=\frac{k^{2}}{4\pi}\,\operatorname{Im}\frac{1}{\varepsilon(k,\omega)}.

(24)

Fluctuation-dissipation theorem.

For a classical system in thermal equilibrium (
\hbar\omega\ll k_{B}T
), the dynamic structure
factor is related to the imaginary part of the retarded response
function by the fluctuation-dissipation theorem. For the charge
density it reads

\widetilde{S}_{\rho\rho}(k,\omega)=-\frac{2k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{\omega}\,\operatorname{Im}\chi_{\rho\rho}(k,\omega),

(25)

the minus sign ensuring the positivity of the structure factor.
Substituting (
24
) into (
25
) gives

\widetilde{S}_{\rho\rho}(k,\omega)=-\frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}T\,k^{2}}{2\pi\,\omega}\,\operatorname{Im}\frac{1}{\varepsilon(k,\omega)}.

(26)

Equation (
26
) is the classical RPA dynamic charge
structure factor of a one-component plasma. Inserting it into
(
20
) we obtain the spectral density of the fluctuating
potential

\widetilde{K}(k,\omega)=-\frac{8\pi q_{0}^{2}\,k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{k^{2}\,\omega}\,\operatorname{Im}\frac{1}{\varepsilon(k,\omega)}.

(27)

A few remarks are in order. The factor
8\pi
in (
27
)
stems from the Fourier convention
1/r\leftrightarrow 4\pi/k^{2}

and the standard RPA/fluctuation-dissipation results. The sign is
such that
\widetilde{K}(k,\omega)\geq 0
for all
\omega
, as
required for a spectral density.

Static limit and Kramers–Kronig check.

To verify that the dynamic approach reproduces the static correlator
of Part I, we integrate (
27
) over all frequencies:

K(k)\equiv\langle W(\mathbf{k})W(-\mathbf{k})\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\,\widetilde{K}(k,\omega)=-\frac{4q_{0}^{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{k^{2}}\,\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{\omega}\,\operatorname{Im}\frac{1}{\varepsilon(k,\omega)}.

(28)

The integral over
\omega
is evaluated with the help of the
Kramers–Kronig relation for the function

f(\omega)=1/\varepsilon(k,\omega)-1
, which is analytic in the
upper half-plane and tends to zero as
|\omega|\to\infty
:

\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\operatorname{Im}f(k,\omega)}{\omega}\,d\omega=\operatorname{Re}f(k,0).

(29)

Since
\operatorname{Im}f=\operatorname{Im}(1/\varepsilon)
, we have

\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\operatorname{Im}(1/\varepsilon(k,\omega))}{\omega}\,d\omega=\frac{1}{\varepsilon(k,0)}-1=-\,\frac{\kappa^{2}}{k^{2}+\kappa^{2}}.

Substituting this into (
28
) yields

K(k)=\frac{4\pi q_{0}^{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}T\,\kappa^{2}}{k^{2}(k^{2}+\kappa^{2})},

(30)

which after the inverse Fourier transform

K(r)=\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}K(k)

reproduces the coordinate-space expression

K(r)=k_{\mathrm{B}}Tq_{0}^{2}\,(1-e^{-\kappa r})/r
.
Thus the dynamic description contains the static result exactly,
confirming the consistency of the formalism.

Equation (
27
) is the central result of this
section. It expresses the dynamic potential correlator through
the dielectric function of the ion plasma and serves as the
starting point for the eikonal analysis of the dynamic disorder
strength
G_{\mathrm{dyn}}
.

III.2
Eikonal approximation for the dynamic plasma

Within the eikonal approximation the quantum fluctuations of the
path are neglected, and the particle is assumed to move along a
straight line with constant velocity
v=\hbar k/m
. The
correlation function entering the effective action (
16
) is then evaluated along this trajectory:

K_{\mathrm{dyn}}(u)\equiv\langle W(0,0)\,W(\mathbf{n}u,u/v)\rangle=\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,e^{ik_{\parallel}u}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\,e^{-i\omega u/v}\,\widetilde{K}(k,\omega).

(31)

Inserting this expression into the formula for the effective
disorder strength
G_{\mathrm{dyn}}
, which is defined exactly as
in Part I,

G_{\mathrm{dyn}}=\frac{m^{2}}{\hbar^{4}}\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,K_{\mathrm{dyn}}(u),

(32)

and performing the
u
-integral as described in Sec.
II
, we obtain

G_{\mathrm{dyn}}=\frac{m^{2}}{4\pi\hbar^{4}}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\,k\int_{-kv}^{kv}d\omega\;\widetilde{K}(k,\omega).

(33)

Substituting the spectral density (
27
) and using
the oddness of
\operatorname{Im}(1/\varepsilon)
with respect to

\omega
, we finally arrive at a compact expression

G_{\mathrm{dyn}}=-\frac{2m^{2}q_{0}^{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{\pi\hbar^{4}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dk}{k}\int_{0}^{kv}\frac{d\omega}{\omega}\,\operatorname{Im}\frac{1}{\varepsilon(k,\omega)}.

(34)

The two asymptotic limits of this formula are analysed below.

III.3
Fast particles (
v\gg v_{\rm th}
)

For particle velocities much larger than the ion thermal speed,
the integral over
\omega
in (
34
) is dominated
by frequencies
\omega\lesssim kv_{\mathrm{th}}\ll kv
. The
upper limit can therefore be extended to
+\infty
with
exponential accuracy. Using the Kramers–Kronig relation
(
29
) the
\omega
-integral becomes proportional to

\kappa^{2}/(k^{2}+\kappa^{2})
, up to an overall sign which is
absorbed by the definition of the static limit. One obtains

G_{\mathrm{dyn}}\simeq\frac{m^{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}Tq_{0}^{2}}{\hbar^{4}}\int_{k_{\mathrm{min}}}^{\infty}\frac{\kappa^{2}\,dk}{k(k^{2}+\kappa^{2})}=\frac{m^{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}Tq_{0}^{2}}{\hbar^{4}}\,\ln(\kappa L),\qquad L\sim 1/k_{\mathrm{min}}.

(35)

The remaining integral over
k
diverges logarithmically at small

k
. In a dynamic plasma the infrared divergence is naturally
regularised: the particle moving with velocity
v
probes the
ionic fluctuations only during a finite time
\sim 1/\omega_{pi}
,
where
\omega_{pi}=\sqrt{4\pi n_{0}q^{2}/m_{i}}
is the ion plasma
frequency. The corresponding effective lower cutoff is

k_{\mathrm{min}}\sim\omega_{pi}/v
, which cuts off the
logarithmic growth. Denoting

L=\frac{v}{\omega_{pi}},

(36)

the integral evaluates to
\ln(\kappa L)
. Thus, for
v\gg v_{\mathrm{th}}

the static result with the Coulomb logarithm is fully recovered,
but now the large-distance cutoff is no longer a phenomenological
parameter: it is set by the distance the particle travels during
one ion plasma oscillation. The disorder strength is dominated
by the long-range unscreened
1/r
tail of the potential
correlator, exactly as in Part I.

III.4
Slow particles (
v\ll v_{\rm th}
)

When the particle moves slower than the ion thermal speed, the
frequency integration in (
34
) is limited to

|\omega|<kv\ll kv_{\rm th}
. In this low-frequency regime
the dielectric function can be expanded for small
\xi
(see
6
):

Z(\xi)\simeq i\sqrt{\pi}-2\xi
, leading to

\operatorname{Im}\frac{1}{\varepsilon(k,\omega)}\simeq-\frac{\kappa^{2}k^{2}}{(k^{2}+\kappa^{2})^{2}}\sqrt{\pi}\,\xi=-\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\,\frac{\kappa^{2}k}{(k^{2}+\kappa^{2})^{2}}\frac{\omega}{v_{\rm th}}.

(37)

Substituting (
37
) into (
34
) and
performing the
\omega
-integral gives a factor
v
. The
remaining
k
-integral

\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\,\frac{\kappa^{2}k}{(k^{2}+\kappa^{2})^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}

(38)

is finite and independent of any cut-off. Collecting all
prefactors, we obtain

G_{\mathrm{dyn}}\simeq\frac{m^{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}Tq_{0}^{2}}{\hbar^{4}}\frac{v}{v_{\rm th}}.

(39)

The infrared logarithmic divergence has disappeared: the Coulomb
logarithm is dynamically regularised. Physically, for a slowly
moving particle the finite correlation time of the ionic
fluctuations destroys the long-time memory of the disordered
potential, and the effective disorder strength is set by temporal
decorrelation rather than by spatial screening.

III.5
Asymptotic formulas for the localization length

Once the effective disorder strength
G_{\mathrm{dyn}}
is known,
the inverse localization length
\Gamma(k)=1/\ell(k)
follows from
the same saddle-point analysis that was used by Efimov
3
.
At high energies (
k\gg\kappa
) the saddle point is located at
s_{c}=1
, which corresponds to the
weak-disorder (eikonal) regime, whereas at low energies
(
k\ll\kappa
) one finds the strong-disorder scaling

\ell\sim G^{-1/3}
.

Inserting the fast-particle result (
35
) for

G_{\mathrm{dyn}}
, which is the same as in the static theory, we
recover the formulas obtained in Part I:

\ell(k)\simeq\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\hbar^{4}k^{2}}{\,m^{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}Tq_{0}^{2}\,\ln(\kappa L)},&k\gg\kappa,\;k\gg mv_{\rm th}/\hbar,\\[20.00003pt]
\displaystyle\frac{4\sqrt[3]{2}}{3}\,\Bigl(\frac{\hbar^{4}}{\,m^{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}Tq_{0}^{2}\,\ln(\kappa L)}\Bigr)^{\!1/3},&k\ll\kappa,\;k\gg mv_{\rm th}/\hbar.\end{cases}

(40)

These expressions explicitly contain the Coulomb logarithm and
describe the dominant contribution for particles moving faster
than the ion thermal speed.

For slow particles,
G_{\mathrm{dyn}}
is proportional to the
velocity and, through
v=\hbar k/m
, to the wave number
k
.
Using Eq. (
39
) we obtain

\ell(k)\simeq\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\hbar^{3}\,v_{\rm th}}{m\,k_{\mathrm{B}}Tq_{0}^{2}}k,&k\gg\kappa,\;k\ll mv_{\rm th}/\hbar,\\[20.00003pt]
\displaystyle\frac{4\sqrt[3]{2}}{3}\,\Bigl(\frac{\hbar^{3}v_{\rm th}}{m\,k_{\mathrm{B}}Tq_{0}^{2}}\Bigr)^{\!1/3}\frac{1}{k^{1/3}},&k\ll\kappa,\;k\ll mv_{\rm th}/\hbar.\end{cases}

(41)

Remarkably, the logarithmic divergence has disappeared completely;
the infrared regularization is provided entirely by the temporal
decorrelation of the ionic fluctuations. Moreover, the localization
length in the weak-disorder regime grows linearly with
k
, and
in the strong-disorder regime it decays slowly (
\propto k^{-1/3}
)
with decreasing wave number.

The crossover between the quasi-static regime (
40
)
and the dynamic regime (
41
) is governed
by the dimensionless parameter
k\hbar/(mv_{\rm th})
. For thermal
electrons in a Maxwellian plasma
v_{\rm th}^{(e)}\gg v_{\rm th}
,
therefore
k\gg mv_{\rm th}/\hbar
holds practically for all
occupied states, and the static theory provides a reliable
description of the dominant contribution. The dynamic corrections
are essential only for very slow particles, such as cold ions or
electrons in strongly non-equilibrium plasmas.

IV
Results and Discussion

IV.1
Static disorder: a brief summary

Before discussing the dynamic generalization, we summarise the
main results of the static theory developed in Part I.
For a static Gaussian potential with correlation function

K(r)=k_{\mathrm{B}}Tq_{0}^{2}(1-e^{-\kappa r})/r
,
the disorder-averaged Green function decays exponentially with
distance, and the inverse localization length is given by

\Gamma(k)=G/k^{2}
for
k\gg\kappa
and

\Gamma(k)=\frac{3}{4\sqrt[3]{2}}\,G^{1/3}
for
k\ll\kappa
,
where
G=(m^{2}/\hbar^{4})k_{\mathrm{B}}Tq_{0}^{2}\ln(\kappa L)
.
The Coulomb logarithm
\ln(\kappa L)
reflects the unscreened

1/r
tail of the potential correlator and requires an infrared
cutoff
L
, which can be identified with the mean free path or
the system size. For thermal electrons in a plasma one typically
has
v\gg v_{\mathrm{th}}
, so that the static approximation is
well justified and the corresponding localization length is
microscopic.

IV.2
Dynamic disorder: formulation and main analytical results

When the finite mass of the ions is taken into account, the
potential becomes a dynamic random field. Its spectral density
is obtained from the fluctuation-dissipation theorem in the
classical limit (
\hbar\omega\ll k_{\mathrm{B}}T
) and, within the
RPA, is expressed through the dielectric function

\varepsilon(k,\omega)
of a one-component ion plasma.
The equal-time correlator of the potential is recovered by
integrating over all frequencies with the help of the
Kramers–Kronig relation, which explicitly shows how the static
Debye form emerges from the dynamic description.

In the eikonal approximation the particle moves along a straight
trajectory with constant velocity
v
. The effective disorder
strength
G_{\mathrm{dyn}}
, defined exactly as in the static
case, is given by Eq. (
34
). Two limiting cases
can be analysed analytically.

Fast particles (
v\gg v_{\mathrm{th}}
).

The integral over
\omega
in (
34
) is dominated
by frequencies
\omega\lesssim kv_{\mathrm{th}}\ll kv
, and
the upper limit can be extended to infinity. Using the
Kramers–Kronig relation one obtains

G_{\mathrm{dyn}}\simeq\frac{m^{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}Tq_{0}^{2}}{\hbar^{4}}\,\ln\!\Big(\frac{\kappa v}{\omega_{pi}}\Big).

(42)

Thus the static result with the Coulomb logarithm is recovered,
but now the large-distance cutoff is set dynamically by the
distance
v/\omega_{pi}
that the particle travels during one
ion plasma oscillation. The disorder strength depends only
logarithmically on
v
.

Slow particles (
v\ll v_{\mathrm{th}}
).

Here
kv\ll kv_{\mathrm{th}}
, and the low-frequency expansion
of the dielectric function can be used. One finds

G_{\mathrm{dyn}}\propto v
,

G_{\mathrm{dyn}}\simeq\frac{m^{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}Tq_{0}^{2}}{\hbar^{4}}\,\frac{v}{v_{\mathrm{th}}}.

(43)

The Coulomb logarithm disappears completely: the
k
-integral
converges in the infrared, and the effective disorder is
determined by temporal decorrelation rather than by spatial
screening. As a consequence, the disorder strength becomes
velocity dependent and vanishes as
v\to 0
.

IV.3
Localization length in the dynamic plasma

Inserting
G_{\mathrm{dyn}}
into the expressions for the
inverse localization length yields the following asymptotic
formulas, written in terms of the wave number

k=mv/\hbar
.

Quasi-static (fast) regime
(
k\gg mv_{\mathrm{th}}/\hbar
):

\displaystyle\ell(k)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\hbar^{4}k^{2}}{\,m^{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}Tq_{0}^{2}\,\ln(\kappa v/\omega_{pi})},\qquad k\gg\kappa,

(44)

\displaystyle\ell(k)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{4\sqrt[3]{2}}{3}\,\Bigl(\frac{\hbar^{4}}{\,m^{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}Tq_{0}^{2}\,\ln(\kappa v/\omega_{pi})}\Bigr)^{\!1/3},\qquad\frac{mv_{\mathrm{th}}}{\hbar}\ll k\ll\kappa.

(45)

These formulas differ from the purely static ones only by the
replacement of the phenomenological cutoff
L
with the dynamic
length
v/\omega_{pi}
.

Dynamic (slow) regime
(
k\ll mv_{\mathrm{th}}/\hbar
):

\displaystyle\ell(k)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\hbar^{3}k\,v_{\mathrm{th}}}{m\,k_{\mathrm{B}}Tq_{0}^{2}},\qquad k\gg\kappa,

(46)

\displaystyle\ell(k)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{4\sqrt[3]{2}}{3}\,\Bigl(\frac{\hbar^{3}v_{\mathrm{th}}}{m\,k_{\mathrm{B}}Tq_{0}^{2}\,k}\Bigr)^{\!1/3},\qquad k\ll\kappa.

(47)

In the weak-disorder regime the localization length grows
linearly with
k
, while in the strong-disorder regime it
decays slowly,
\propto k^{-1/3}
. In both cases the length
scale is set by the intrinsic plasma parameters and does not
involve any infrared cutoff – the dynamic screening provides
a natural regularisation.

IV.4
Crossover and physical interpretation

The crossover between the quasi-static and dynamic regimes
occurs when the particle speed is comparable to the ion thermal
speed,
v\sim v_{\mathrm{th}}
. For a Maxwellian electron gas
one has
v_{\mathrm{th}}^{(e)}\gg v_{\mathrm{th}}
, so that the
fast-particle condition
v\gg v_{\mathrm{th}}
holds for the
vast majority of electrons. Therefore, the static theory is
expected to describe the dominant contribution to the
localization of the averaged Green function in thermal plasmas.
The dynamic corrections are essential only for very slow
particles, such as cold ions or electrons in strongly
non-equilibrium situations.

The most striking result of the dynamic theory is the complete
suppression of the Coulomb logarithm for slow particles.
Physically, a slowly moving quantum particle does not accumulate
a large phase shift from the fluctuating potential because the
ionic atmosphere rearranges itself faster than the particle
passes through it. The long-range tail of the potential
correlator is cut off by the finite correlation time of the
fluctuations, and the infrared divergence disappears. As a
consequence, the effective disorder vanishes as
v\to 0
,
implying that
ultra-slow particles are not exponentially
localized
– a behaviour fundamentally different from the
static case.

In the static limit the strong-disorder localization length
saturates at a finite value and becomes independent of the
particle momentum. In contrast, the dynamic strong-disorder
length
\ell\propto k^{-1/3}
diverges as
k\to 0
, indicating
that the localization is conditional on the particle velocity.
Thus, the principal difference between the static and dynamic
theories lies in the momentum dependence of the localization
length in the strong-disorder regime.

IV.5
Implications for plasma transport and open questions

The present theory demonstrates that static disorder provides a
robust upper bound on the exponential decay rate of the
averaged Green function in plasmas. For thermal electrons the
predicted localization length lies in the sub-micrometer to
millimeter range, suggesting that Anderson localization could
significantly affect the transport properties of dense
astrophysical and laboratory plasmas. The dynamic analysis
further reveals that slow particles can escape exponential
localization, which may have consequences for the energy
relaxation of cold electrons in inertial confinement fusion
plasmas and for the mobility of ions in ultracold neutral
plasmas.

Several extensions of the present work are possible. A self-consistent determination of the infrared cutoff, e.g. by
identifying
L
with the localization length
\ell
itself, would make the static theory parameter-free. Quantum corrections to
the fluctuation-dissipation theorem and to the dielectric function can be incorporated to describe degenerate plasmas.
Finally, a numerical verification of the predicted crossover and a comparison with molecular-dynamics simulations of quantum particles
in classical plasmas would be highly desirable.
