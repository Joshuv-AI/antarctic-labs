Massive Ovarian Fibrothecoma Mimicking Advanced Ovarian Cancer in a High-Risk Patient: Diagnostic Challenges and Selective Use of Preoperative Biopsy
Authors: Wuping Xu, Jihang Yao, Jinting Li, Yi Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOvarian fibrothecoma is a rare benign sex cord–stromal tumor that may be misdiagnosed as ovarian malignancy because of nonspecific clinical, radiologic, and serologic findings. Percutaneous biopsy in suspected ovarian malignancy is controversial and is generally reserved for unresectable disease, neoadjuvant chemotherapy planning, poor surgical candidates, or when histological confirmation is required before treatment.Massive ovarian fibrothecoma complicated by Meigs’ syndrome and severe multi-organ dysfunction is extremely uncommon and may create substantial diagnostic and therapeutic challenges, particularly in medically high-risk patients. In addition, interpretation of tumor markers such as human epididymis protein 4 (HE4) may be confounded by renal dysfunction, further complicating preoperative assessment.Case PresentationA 59-year-old postmenopausal woman presented with progressive abdominal distension and dyspnea. Imaging revealed a massive cystic-solid pelvic mass with extensive ascites, pleural effusion, lymphadenopathy, and peritoneal thickening, raising strong suspicion for advanced ovarian cancer. Serum CA125 and HE4 levels were markedly elevated; however, the patient also had significant renal dysfunction, which may have contributed to HE4 elevation. The patient subsequently developed severe systemic compromise, including respiratory failure, cardiac insufficiency, renal impairment, hypoxemia, resulting in poor surgical tolerance. Following multidisciplinary evaluation and preoperative optimization, ultrasound-guided core needle biopsy and intraoperative frozen section supported the diagnosis of ovarian fibrothecoma. A giant right ovarian tumor measuring approximately 45 cm was completely resected. Postoperatively, pleural effusion and ascites resolved, and organ function gradually improved, consistent with Meigs’ syndrome.ConclusionThis case highlights that massive ovarian fibrothecoma with Meigs’ syndrome may closely mimic advanced ovarian cancer and lead to life-threatening multi-organ dysfunction. Benign ovarian stromal tumors should remain in the differential diagnosis of giant adnexal masses with pleural effusion, ascites, and elevated tumor markers. In carefully selected high-risk patients with diagnostic uncertainty and poor surgical tolerance, preoperative image-guided biopsy may provide useful histopathologic information to support individualized management after multidisciplinary assessment.Core TipWe report a rare case of massive ovarian fibrothecoma with Meigs’ syndrome and multi-organ dysfunction closely mimicking advanced ovarian cancer. This case highlights the diagnostic challenges associated with giant adnexal masses accompanied by ascites, pleural effusion, lymphadenopathy, and elevated tumor markers. In carefully selected high-risk patients with high diagnostic uncertainty and poor surgical tolerance, after multidisciplinary assessment, preoperative ultrasound-guided biopsy may provide useful histopathologic information to support individualized multidisciplinary management and help avoid inappropriate treatment.Keywords: ovarian fibrothecoma, Meigs’ syndrome, ultrasound-guided core needle biopsy, multi-organ dysfunction, giant pelvic mass, advanced ovarian cancer mimic

Case Presentation

Chief Complaints

A 59-year-old postmenopausal woman presented with a 1-month history of progressive abdominal distension and dyspnea, and was unable to lie flat.

History of Present Illness

The patient presented with a one-month history of rapidly progressive abdominal distension accompanied by severe dyspnea, particularly when lying supine. Multidisciplinary assessment raised concern for a potentially malignant or borderline ovarian tumor with extensive lymph node involvement. The patient’s markedly impaired cardiac and renal function presents a high anesthetic risk, necessitating optimization of organ function prior to any surgical intervention. Additional complaints included poor appetite, sleep disturbances, constipation, and decreased urine output.The weight has increased significantly within half a year.

History of Past Illness

The patient had multiple comorbidities complicating her presentation. She had chronic cardiac insufficiency (NYHA class IV) with left atrial enlargement, left ventricular diastolic dysfunction, and pericardial effusion. She also had type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and chronic renal dysfunction. Pulmonary evaluation revealed mixed ventilatory dysfunction, interstitial lung disease, hypoxemia, and recurrent pleural effusions with atelectasis. Laboratory tests showed secondary anemia, hypoalbuminemia, and hyponatremia. Thyroid imaging identified a right thyroid lobe mass with cystic changes and calcifications, causing tracheal deviation, consistent with euthyroid sick syndrome. Abdominal imaging noted gallbladder adenomyomatosis, gallbladder polyps, pancreatic cysts, and renal cysts.

Personal and Family History

The patient had no known family history of hereditary or malignant diseases. She denied a history of smoking, alcohol consumption, or illicit drug use. There was no history of long-term occupational exposure to toxic substances or radiation. Menarche occurred at 16 years of age and menopause at 50 years. Obstetric history included gravidity 2, parity 0, with two spontaneous abortions.

General Physical Examination

The patient was obese, with a body weight of 77 kg, height of 165 cm, and a body mass index (BMI) of 28.3 kg/m2. She appeared chronically ill and dyspneic at rest, and was unable to tolerate the supine position. Marked abdominal distension was noted, with asymmetric enlargement extending from the pelvis to the upper abdomen.

Physical and Gynecologic Examination

The external genitalia were normally developed, with a normal female pattern of pubic hair distribution. No visible lesions or abnormal growths were noted on the labia majora or minora. The vaginal canal was patent, and the vaginal mucosa demonstrated normal rugal folds. The cervix was normal in size and appearance, with a smooth surface. Although the large abdominopelvic mass limited pelvic examination, the uterus was presumed to be separate from the mass based on ultrasound findings and limited bimanual assessment.The uterus was normal size, measuring approximately 10×8×7 cm, and demonstrated normal mobility. The right ovary could not be separately identified from the mass, whereas the left ovary appeared grossly normal on imaging.A large pelvic mass was palpable, with indistinct margins, extending superiorly to the level of the xiphoid process,corresponding to an estimated size of approximately 40–50 cm. On bimanual and rectovaginal examination, the pouch of Douglas was empty.

Laboratory Examination

Laboratory investigations on admission revealed anemia (hemoglobin, 89 g/L), hypoalbuminemia (serum albumin, 28.5 g/L), and renal dysfunction (serum creatinine, 280μmol/L). Arterial blood gas analysis demonstrated hypoxemia, with a partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2) of 74.2 mmHg. Serum electrolytes showed hyponatremia. Tumor marker assessment revealed markedly elevated CA125 (474 U/mL) and HE4 (670 pmol/L), whereas CEA, AFP, CA15-3, and CA19-9 were within normal limits (Table 1).

Imaging Examination

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen revealed a large cystic–solid mass occupying the abdominopelvic cavity, measuring approximately 45.9×36.5×34.7 cm (transverse× anteroposterior × craniocaudal). The right ovary could not be separately identified from the mass, whereas the left ovary appeared grossly normal on imaging.Scattered punctate vascular enhancement was noted within the lesion. Additional findings included diffuse mesenteric and peritoneal thickening, massive ascites occupying the abdominopelvic cavity with an estimated volume of several liters, and multiple enlarged lymph nodes involving the bilateral inguinal regions, pelvic walls, and retroperitoneum. Thickening of the peritoneum and omentum raised suspicion for possible tumor dissemination. An area of abnormal enhancement in the right hepatic lobe was considered more consistent with a perfusion-related change than metastatic disease (Figure 1A). Gynecologic ultrasonography demonstrated a uterus of mildly enlarged size (approximately 11.2×8.8×8.2 cm) with a thin endometrium measuring 0.43 cm. Multiple hypoechoic nodules were identified within the myometrium, some with peripheral vascularity, suggestive of uterine leiomyomas. A large mixed cystic–solid mass was observed extending from the uterine fundus into the pelvic cavity, measuring approximately 46×38×35 cm, with internal septations and scattered vascular signals. In addition, a solid hypoechoic mass measuring approximately 9.7 cm was noted anterior to the uterus and was classified as O-RADS category 4. Pelvic ascites was also present (Figure 1B).

Chest CT demonstrated decreased pulmonary translucency with scattered ground-glass opacities, right-sided pleural effusion with associated atelectasis, a small pericardial effusion, and chest wall edema. Cervical CT revealed a heterogeneous mass at the lower pole of the right thyroid lobe measuring 2.8×2.7 cm, accompanied by bilateral thyroid cysts with calcifications and tracheal compression with deviation (Figure 2A and B).

Based on imaging findings, differential diagnoses included primary epithelial ovarian carcinoma, borderline ovarian tumor, degenerating uterine leiomyoma, and benign sex cord-stromal tumor.

Diagnosis and Treatment

Given the patient’s postmenopausal status, rapidly progressive abdominal distension, massive pelvic mass, extensive ascites, pleural effusion, enlarged retroperitoneal and inguinal lymph nodes, peritoneal and mesenteric thickening, and markedly elevated serum CA125 and HE4 levels, advanced ovarian malignancy was strongly suspected. In addition, the patient’s severe cachectic appearance, multi-organ dysfunction, and poor general condition further increased concern for an advanced malignant process with possible peritoneal dissemination. The presence of multiple comorbidities, including cardiac insufficiency, renal dysfunction, respiratory failure, and hypoalbuminemia, did not directly suggest malignancy but substantially increased the complexity of treatment planning and perioperative risk.Following admission, active supportive and symptomatic treatment was initiated to improve organ function and alleviate clinical symptoms. On June 27 (hospital day 5), ultrasound-guided thoracic drainage was performed, with daily drainage volumes ranging from 300 to 450 mL. The patient’s dyspnea improved significantly, and she became able to lie supine. Her general condition improved markedly. Follow-up chest CT performed on July 4 (hospital day 12) demonstrated good lung re-expansion compared with baseline (Figure 2C).

Pleural effusion samples and ascitic fluid samples were obtained and sent for cytological analysis. Cytological examination of pleural effusion demonstrated the presence of lymphocytes and mesothelial cells without evidence of malignant cells, and the sample was classified as non-malignant findings (NFM).Ascitic fluid samples also revealed no evidence of malignant cells. However,negative pleural fluid cytology cannot exclude malignancy, as its diagnostic sensitivity is limited and ovarian cancer cells may be unevenly distributed and present in insufficient numbers in the effusion, leading to potential sampling errors and false-negative results.1

Following multidisciplinary discussion involving gynecologic oncology, anesthesiology, radiology, cardiology, nephrology, and intensive care specialists, and after improvement in the patient’s respiratory status with reassessment of perioperative risk, exploratory laparotomy with tumor debulking was initially scheduled for July 5 (hospital day 13).However, after being informed of the substantial perioperative risks, the patient and her family declined immediate surgery and opted for chemotherapy as the initial treatment, with the intention of performing interval debulking surgery after clinical stabilization.

Because the treatment strategy had changed, a second multidisciplinary discussion was conducted. Ultrasound-guided core needle biopsy of the pelvic mass and inguinal lymph nodes was subsequently performed on July 7 (hospital day 15) to clarify the diagnosis before chemotherapy. Written informed consent was obtained after detailed discussion of the potential risks associated with the biopsy procedure.

Pathological examination of the pelvic mass revealed a spindle-cell mesenchymal tumor. Based on the histological findings in combination with immunohistochemical results, the lesion was diagnosed as ovarian fibrothecoma. Examination of the right inguinal lymph node demonstrated reactive lymphoid hyperplasia (Figure 3).

Definitive surgery was rescheduled, and on July 16 (hospital day 24), the patient underwent exploratory laparotomy with total hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, and ovarian tumor resection. Intraoperatively, pale yellow ascites was observed, and a giant right ovarian mass measuring approximately 50 cm in diameter was identified (Figure 4A). The tumor had a smooth surface and an intact capsule (Figure 4B). On sectioning, the mass was predominantly solid and firm, containing two cystic cavities measuring approximately 25 cm and 15 cm in diameter, respectively, with smooth inner walls and clear light-yellow fluid (Figure 4C).

Following tumor decompression and removal, the patient developed hemodynamic instability, with oxygen saturation fluctuating between 71% and 80%, accompanied by hypotension and tachycardia. The intraoperative hemodynamic instability was most likely multifactorial and may have been related to sudden decompression following removal of the giant abdominal mass, rapid changes in intra-abdominal pressure, substantial fluid redistribution, and altered venous return. Blood pressure was stabilized with norepinephrine infusion. Estimated intraoperative blood loss was approximately 20 mL, and urine output was approximately 100 mL. The patient was transferred to the post-anesthesia care unit while awaiting frozen section results.

Intraoperative frozen section examination confirmed the diagnosis of ovarian fibrothecoma. The frozen section diagnosis excluded ovarian malignancy and therefore avoided unnecessary comprehensive surgical staging, allowing the procedure to be limited to definitive treatment of the benign tumor. Final pathological examination further demonstrated ovarian fibrothecoma, with immunohistochemical findings favoring fibroma. The uterus and bilateral adnexa showed no evidence of tumor involvement (Figure 5).

Discussion

Ovarian fibromathecoma tumors (OFTs) are a subset of ovarian sex cord-stromal tumors and are predominantly benign. According to Blaustein’s Pathology of the Female Genital Tract and WHO classification, less than 1% are malignant, usually as fibrosarcomas. Extremely large benign OFTs, as in this case, are rare and present with complex clinical manifestations. Rapid tumor growth and associated symptoms such as Meigs syndrome can mimic ovarian malignancy. Prior to ultrasound-guided biopsy, both imaging findings and clinical features—including inguinal and other lymphadenopathy, peritoneal thickening, massive pleural and abdominal effusions, multi-organ dysfunction, and elevated CA125—strongly suggested a malignant ovarian tumor with systemic involvement. Definitive diagnosis of a giant ovarian fibromathecoma was only established after histopathological confirmation.

Clinical Presentation

The massive size of the tumor and its rapid growth resulted in progressive abdominal distension, respiratory compromise, urinary frequency, difficulty with defecation, and other compression-related symptoms. In this case, rapid enlargement of the pelvic mass within one month contributed to severe dyspnea and poor general condition.

Ascites and pleural effusion associated with ovarian fibromathecoma are also referred to as Meigs syndrome. The classic triad consists of a benign ovarian solid tumor, pleural effusion, and ascites. In this case, the patient exhibited typical Meigs syndrome symptoms,2 and the diagnosis was established after excluding malignancy. The underlying mechanism may involve tumor compression of intra-abdominal vessels, leading to impaired venous return, which in turn causes pleural and abdominal effusions, resulting in poor lung expansion and dyspnea.

Additionally, after relief of tumor compression and removal of the massive pleural and abdominal effusions, the patient’s plasma B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) remained elevated. However, postoperative echocardiography demonstrated preserved systolic and diastolic cardiac function without evidence of acute heart failure. Therefore, this finding should be interpreted cautiously and not regarded as definitive evidence of primary cardiac failure in isolation. In this patient, persistent BNP elevation may have been influenced by renal dysfunction, hypoxemia, perioperative stress, restrictive ventilatory impairment, and substantial postoperative fluid redistribution rather than primary cardiac decompensation.

Lymphadenopathy may be explained by the fact that larger tumor diameters exert more direct stimulation on the peritoneum, which increases the likelihood of lymphatic obstruction and leads to enlargement of the inguinal drainage lymph nodes.However, the exact mechanism remains unclear.3

Diagnostic Evaluation (Examination)

Ultrasonography

Ultrasound accurately localizes most OFT lesions but has limited specificity; these tumors can be mistaken for Leiomyoma or other ovarian neoplasms.4 Atypical fibromas or thecomas may appear as round, lobulated masses with striated shadows, sometimes associated with fluid in the Douglas pouch, and usually show mild to moderate vascularity.5

CT Imaging

Giant OFTs often appear as large, lobulated masses with irregular margins. The internal structure is heterogeneous, reflecting fibrous tissue, thecomatous cells, and occasional hemorrhage or necrosis, with larger tumors showing more central necrosis. While most OFTs have well-defined borders, some may compress surrounding tissues, causing indistinct margins. Contrast-enhanced CT may show heterogeneous or rim enhancement and mass effect on adjacent organs. Pelvic effusions often accompany large tumors.6,7 However, in this case, CT imaging showed a large mass with mixed cystic and solid components. Although the margins were regular, there was focal hemorrhage or necrosis within the center, as well as findings in adjacent organs that were highly suspicious for metastatic lesions. Taken together, these features made ovarian malignancy more strongly suspected than fibrothecoma.

In rare cases, coexisting hepatic lesions—such as cysts, hemangiomas, or even primary liver malignancies—may be misinterpreted as metastatic ovarian disease. In such situations, careful reevaluation by an experienced radiologist is essential to accurately assess the malignant potential of hepatic lesions.

Tumor Markers

Significantly elevated CA125 levels were observed in this case as well as in previously reported cases, which can easily lead to misdiagnosis as advanced epithelial ovarian cancer.8 Although CA125 elevation associated with Meigs syndrome is rare, it has been documented in the literature. The mechanisms underlying this elevation may include: ① tumor-related inflammatory reactions stimulating CA125 secretion from peritoneal mesothelial cells; ② mechanical stimulation of mesothelial cells by the presence of ascites itself; and ③ the release of inflammatory mediators such as vascular endothelial growth factor and interleukins.9 The rapid normalization of CA125 levels following surgery further supports that the elevation originates from a benign pathological process rather than persistent secretion by malignant tumors.Notably, elevated CA125 can significantly interfere with preoperative risk assessment. When encountering patients with pelvic masses accompanied by ascites, hydrothorax, and elevated CA125, clinicians should comprehensively evaluate imaging characteristics to avoid misjudging the nature of the lesion due to over-reliance on tumor markers.

In addition, interpretation of HE4 levels in this patient requires caution because renal dysfunction may significantly increase serum HE4 concentrations through reduced renal clearance. Previous studies have demonstrated that HE4 levels may be markedly elevated in patients with chronic kidney disease even in the absence of ovarian malignancy, and the degree of elevation correlates with declining renal function.10,11 Therefore, elevated HE4 levels should be interpreted in conjunction with renal function and overall clinical findings.

Hormonal Assessment

Some ovarian fibrothecomas may present with estrogen-related manifestations depending on the proportion of hormonally active theca cells.12 In this case, the tumor was predominantly fibrous, and no obvious hormone-related symptoms were observed. Because advanced ovarian malignancy was initially considered more likely, preoperative hormonal evaluation was not performed.

Lymph Node Evaluation

In this case, the extensive retroperitoneal, pelvic, and inguinal lymphadenopathy in the drainage regions further complicated the preoperative differential diagnosis because lymph node enlargement is commonly associated with advanced ovarian malignancy.However, ultrasound-guided biopsy of the enlarged inguinal lymph node demonstrated reactive lymphoid hyperplasia without evidence of metastatic involvement, which provided important reassuring pathological evidence against disseminated cancer. Although ovarian fibromathecomas are generally of low malignant potential, lymph node enlargement can occur in some instances.,the underlying mechanism remains unclear. On CT imaging, lymphadenopathy may be observed in the retroperitoneal or pelvic regions, with node diameters often exceeding 1 cm.13 Possible contributing factors may include reactive inflammatory changes, chronic pleural or peritoneal irritation, or impaired lymphatic drainage caused by tumor compression. Nevertheless, these explanations remain speculative, and lymphadenopathy in patients with giant adnexal masses should be interpreted cautiously unless pathological confirmation is obtained.

Differential Diagnosis

The main differential diagnoses for ovarian fibromathecoma tumors are malignant ovarian neoplasms, including serous or mucinous cystadenocarcinomas, malignant sex cord-stromal tumors, and metastatic ovarian cancers from other primary sites. On imaging, ovarian fibromathecoma tumors are predominantly solid, whereas malignant tumors are often cystic-solid. Typical MRI findings of OFTs include predominantly low T2-weighted signal with patchy high-signal areas, and post-contrast enhancement shows no enhancement or mild to moderate enhancement of the low T2 signal areas.14,15 Although serum CA125 may be elevated in some patients with OFTs, the increase is generally lower compared to malignant ovarian tumors and other tumor markers such as CEA or CA19-9.16 Some OFT patients with elevated estrogen may present with postmenopausal irregular vaginal bleeding or abnormal uterine bleeding.

Additionally, ascites and pleural effusion associated with OFTs, ie., Meigs syndrome, must be distinguished from malignant causes. When both Meigs syndrome and elevated CA125 levels are present, even if CA125 is significantly increased, the possibility of OFT-induced Meigs syndrome should still be considered when the nature of the tumor is uncertain.17

In patients with ovarian fibrothecoma, massive pleural and abdominal effusions may further increase diagnostic uncertainty and mimic disseminated malignancy.Tumor markers in the effusion are normal or mildly elevated, and the pleural and abdominal effusions typically resolve spontaneously after surgical removal of the ovary.3 Differentiation from pleural or abdominal effusions caused by malignant tumors, liver cirrhosis, or tuberculosis can be achieved through cytological analysis or biopsy of the effusion.2

Using the above differential diagnostic criteria, most ovarian fibromathecoma tumors can be distinguished. However, in some patients with very large tumors or multiple comorbidities, misdiagnosis as malignant ovarian tumors may still occur, resulting in delayed surgery or inappropriate treatment. Histopathological diagnosis remains the gold standard for confirming complex, giant ovarian fibromathecoma tumors. In selected high-risk patients, ultrasound-guided biopsy may provide useful diagnostic information and assist treatment planning. However, preoperative biopsy is not routinely recommended for all adnexal masses suspicious for malignancy because of potential risks including sampling error, false-negative results, bleeding, infection, capsule disruption, and possible impact on tumor staging if malignancy is present. Therefore, the decision to perform preoperative biopsy should be individualized and carefully evaluated within a multidisciplinary setting after comprehensive assessment of imaging findings, operative risk, and anticipated clinical benefit.

Treatment and Prognosis

Ovarian fibromathecoma tumors are mostly benign, and surgical excision is the primary treatment. Before surgery, pelvic MRI, chest and abdominal CT, hormone levels, and tumor markers should be evaluated to assess the risk of malignancy. Young patients may undergo tumor enucleation or unilateral salpingo-oophorectomy to preserve fertility, whereas postmenopausal women or those without fertility requirements are recommended to undergo total hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy or unilateral salpingo-oophorectomy. Intraoperative frozen section should be performed to determine tumor nature, and if malignancy is suggested, surgical staging for ovarian cancer should be conducted. Postoperatively, adjuvant therapy is generally not required. In cases with coexisting pleural or abdominal effusion or lymphadenopathy, these often resolve spontaneously after surgery,13 and symptoms related to hormonal hypersecretion typically improve or disappear.12 Although malignant transformation is rare during long-term follow-up, regular surveillance is still recommended.

Limitations

This report has several limitations. First, this is a single-case report, and the clinical findings and management strategy may not be applicable to all patients with giant adnexal masses. Second, the diagnostic accuracy of preoperative biopsy may be affected by sampling limitations, particularly in heterogeneous tumors. Therefore, preoperative biopsy should not be routinely applied to all adnexal masses suspicious for malignancy. Third, long-term postoperative follow-up was limited in this case. Finally, although the patient’s organ function improved markedly after tumor removal, the contribution of intensive perioperative supportive care cannot be completely separated from the effect of surgery itself.

Conclusion

In summary, this case highlights that massive ovarian fibrothecoma with Meigs’ syndrome may closely mimic advanced ovarian cancer and result in severe systemic compromise, including pleural effusion, ascites, lymphadenopathy, and multi-organ dysfunction. Therefore, benign ovarian tumors should remain in the differential diagnosis of apparently malignant adnexal masses, particularly when clinical and imaging findings are not fully concordant. When confronted with a massive pelvic tumor accompanied by multi-organ involvement and pleural or abdominal effusion, while malignancy should be carefully considered, ovarian fibromathecoma and its associated Meigs syndrome must also be included in the differential diagnosis.In selected high-risk patients with diagnostic uncertainty and poor surgical tolerance, preoperative image-guided biopsy, performed after multidisciplinary assessment, may help guide individualized management and avoid inappropriate treatment.
