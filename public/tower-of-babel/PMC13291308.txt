Environmental remediation using silver nanoparticles biosynthesized from agro waste banana and pineapple peels
Authors: Abdulrahman Sani Aliero, Nurliyana Ahmad Zawawi, Sarah Syafiqah Jajuli, Shafinaz Shahir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Synthetic dyes in industrial wastewater pose significant environmental and health risks due to their persistence, toxicity, and resistance to conventional treatment methods. Green synthesis of silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) using agro-waste extracts provides an eco-friendly and sustainable approach for producing efficient nanocatalysts for dye degradation. Although banana peel extract (BPE) and pineapple peel extract (PPE) have been individually explored for AgNPs biosynthesis, systematic direct comparisons under identical conditions are still limited. This study addresses this gap by evaluating BPE and PPE as reducing and stabilizing agents for AgNPs production and assessing their catalytic performance in methylene blue (MB) and methyl orange (MO) degradation. Phytochemical analysis showed markedly higher total phenolic content in BPE (8.65 ± 0.51 mg GAE/g dry peel) compared to PPE (0.253 ± 0.01 mg GAE/g). Despite this, PPE-derived AgNPs exhibited smaller crystallite sizes (~ 12.3 nm by XRD) and particle sizes (21.6 ± 7.01 nm by FESEM) than BPE-AgNPs (~ 18.7 nm and 30.99 ± 9.47 nm, respectively). Surface plasmon resonance peaks were observed at 430 nm (BPE-AgNPs) and 450 nm (PPE-AgNPs). Comprehensive characterization using UV-visible spectroscopy, Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction (XRD), field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM), and energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) analysis confirmed crystalline face-centered cubic structures, spherical morphology, and the role of hydroxyl, carboxyl, and amine groups from peel phytochemicals in reduction and capping. In the presence of NaBH4, PPE-AgNPs achieved superior catalytic performance, degrading ~ 95% of MB within 60 min and ~ 88% of MO within 75 min, outperforming BPE-AgNPs (~ 82% and ~ 73%, respectively). Kinetic modeling revealed dye- and precursor-dependent mechanisms, with smaller particle size and enhanced surface accessibility of PPE-AgNPs as the dominant factors over phenolic content. These results demonstrate the potential of pineapple peel waste to produce highly efficient, eco-friendly AgNP nanocatalysts. This work highlights the importance of phytochemical diversity in agro-waste valorization for sustainable wastewater remediation, supporting circular economy principles and UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs 6, 9, 12, and 13).Clinical trial Not applicable.

Introduction

Industrial wastewater contaminated with synthetic dyes poses a major environmental challenge worldwide. These dyes, widely used in textiles, cosmetics, leather, pharmaceuticals, and printing industries, are chemically stable and resistant to conventional degradation processes [1, 2]. Upon discharge into aquatic systems, they persist for extended periods, reducing light penetration, disrupting photosynthesis, and exerting toxic and carcinogenic effects on aquatic life and human health [3, 4].

Traditional remediation strategies, including adsorption, coagulation, and photocatalysis, have been extensively explored but are often limited by high costs, incomplete degradation, and secondary pollution [5]. In recent years, nanotechnology has emerged as a promising alternative, with silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) proving particularly effective due to their high surface reactivity, tunable catalytic activity, and strong antimicrobial properties [6]. Several categories of metallic and metal oxide nanoparticles synthesized via green routes, such as gold (Au), silver (Ag), zinc oxide (ZnO), copper (Cu), iron (Fe), palladium (Pd), and titanium dioxide (TiO2), have demonstrated improved biomimetic attributes, enhanced catalytic activity, and versatility in pollutant degradation and antimicrobial applications. Green-synthesized AgNPs using plant extracts have shown excellent anticancer, antibacterial, anti-diabetic, and anti-inflammatory properties [7]. Bimetallic ZnO–CuO nanocomposites synthesized via green methods exhibit superior visible-light-driven photocatalytic degradation of organic pollutants [3].

However, conventional chemical and physical synthesis of AgNPs is energy-intensive and relies on hazardous reagents, raising sustainability concerns [8, 9]. Green synthesis using biological extracts offers an eco-friendly alternative by employing natural reducing and stabilizing agents [10]. Plant-based extracts, rich in polyphenols, flavonoids, tannins, and proteins, enable simultaneous reduction of silver ions and nanoparticle capping, ensuring stability and non-toxicity [11, 12]. Among these, agro-waste extracts are particularly attractive due to their abundance, low cost, and alignment with circular economy principles, transforming discarded biomass into high-value nanomaterials [13, 14].

Banana and pineapple peels constitute a significant portion of global fruit-processing waste and are rich in bioactive compounds suitable for AgNPs biosynthesis. Although individual studies have reported green synthesis of AgNPs using various fruit peels such as citrus, mango, papaya, lemon [15–17], direct comparative evaluations of banana and pineapple peels under identical conditions remain scarce [11, 18, 19]. Banana peels typically exhibit higher phenolic content, while pineapple peels are more fibrous and acidic, providing an opportunity to investigate how contrasting phytochemical profiles influence nanoparticle formation and properties [20].

Although individual studies have reported the biosynthesis of AgNPs using banana peel extract (BPE) or pineapple peel extract (PPE) separately, direct head-to-head comparative evaluations of these two abundant agro-wastes under identical experimental conditions remain limited. Banana peels are typically rich in polyphenols (such as catechins and flavonoids), which provide strong reducing capacity but can lead to thicker biomolecular capping and larger particle sizes. In contrast, pineapple peels are more fibrous and richer in non-phenolic components, including reducing sugars (fructose), organic acids (citric and malic), and proteins (e.g., bromelain), which promote faster nucleation, tighter steric stabilization, and smaller nanoparticles. This phytochemical diversity significantly influences reduction kinetics, particle size distribution, surface capping thickness, and ultimately catalytic performance. The present work provides such a systematic comparison, revealing the counterintuitive dominance of particle size and non-phenolic biomolecules over total phenolic content in determining dye degradation efficiency. Thus, valorizing abundant agro-waste into efficient nanocatalysts, this work advances sustainable nanotechnology for wastewater remediation. The approach supports UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs 6, 9, 12, and 13) through clean water provision, industrial innovation, responsible consumption, and climate action.

Materials and methods

Materials

Silver nitrate (AgNO3), Folin–Ciocalteu’s reagent, sodium borohydride (NaBH4), sodium carbonate (Na2CO3), methylene blue (MB), and methyl orange (MO) were sourced from VWR BDH Chemicals (USA), Merck (USA), R&M Chemicals (Malaysia), Fisher Scientific (Malaysia), and Bendosen (Malaysia). Commercial citrate-capped spherical AgNPs (~ 20 nm) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Malaysia) and used as received for comparative characterization and catalysis.

Determination of total phenolic content (TPC)

TPC of banana peel extract (BPE) and pineapple peel extract (PPE) was quantified using a modified Folin–Ciocalteu assay [21]. Briefly, 0.5 mL diluted extract was mixed with 2.5 mL 10% Folin–Ciocalteu reagent, incubated for 5 min, then supplemented with 2 mL 7.5% Na2CO3. After 60 min dark incubation at room temperature, absorbance was measured at 765 nm (Shimadzu UV-1800 spectrophotometer). Gallic acid served as standard; results were expressed as mg gallic acid equivalents (GAE) per g dry peel (mean ± SD, n = 3). TPC provided a proxy for reducing potential, as phenolics primarily drive Ag⁺ reduction and capping.

Preparation of plant extracts

Banana and pineapple peels were collected locally (Taman Universiti, Skudai, Johor, Malaysia), washed with deionized water, oven-dried (55 °C, 24 h), ground, and stored at 4 °C. Extraction followed adapted protocols [22]. Banana peel powder (2 g) was heated in 100 mL deionized water (80 °C, 25 min), while pineapple peel (100 g) was boiled in 200 mL deionized water under identical conditions. Filtrates were cooled, filtered (Whatman paper), and stored at 4 °C. Extraction ratios (2% w/v for banana; 50% w/v for pineapple) were based on preliminary trials to achieve comparable reducing capacity, accounting for banana peels’ higher soluble phenolics and pineapple peels’ fibrous nature.

Biosynthesis of silver nanoparticles

The biosynthesis of AgNPs was optimized using a one-factor-at-a-time (OFAT) approach, in which AgNO3 concentration, extract volume, reaction temperature, and incubation time were varied sequentially while monitoring nanoparticle formation through color change and surface plasmon resonance (SPR) peak intensity and sharpness via UV-Vis spectroscopy. All optimization experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3) to ensure reproducibility. The optimized conditions were determined as follows: for BPE-AgNPs, 2.5 mM AgNO3, 2 mL extract, 70 °C, and 72 h incubation; for PPE-AgNPs, 4 mM AgNO3, 2 mL extract, 70 °C, and 5–6 h incubation. Although the OFAT strategy effectively identified precursor-specific conditions under the present experimental setup, it does not account for potential interactions among variables and therefore offers limited statistical robustness. Future studies will employ advanced multivariate optimization techniques such as response surface methodology (RSM) or design of experiments (DoE) to better evaluate interactive effects and improve overall process efficiency.

Characterization of BPE‑AgNPs and PPE‑AgNPs

The biosynthesized AgNPs were comprehensively characterized using a suite of complementary techniques to confirm their formation, surface functionalization, crystallinity, morphology, and elemental composition. Nanoparticle formation and synthesis progress were initially verified by UV-Vis spectroscopy on a Shimadzu UV-1800 spectrophotometer (300–800 nm wavelength range), which monitored the characteristic surface plasmon resonance (SPR) absorption bands [23]. Surface functional groups involved in Ag⁺ reduction and nanoparticle stabilization were identified using Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy on a PerkinElmer Spectrum One spectrometer (4000–400 cm⁻¹ range, KBr pellet method). This revealed the participation of hydroxyl, amine, and carboxyl groups from peel phytochemicals as reducing and capping agents [24]. Crystallinity and phase purity were assessed by X-ray Diffraction (XRD) using a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer (Cu Kα radiation, λ = 1.5406 Å, 2θ range 20–80°). Patterns were compared to the standard JCPDS Card No. 04-0783 for face-centered cubic (fcc) silver [25].

Finally, morphology and elemental composition were examined by Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM) coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) analysis on a Hitachi SU8010 microscope (accelerating voltage 5–15 kV). Prior to FESEM imaging, AgNP suspensions were centrifuged (10,000 rpm, 15 min), washed twice with deionized water, and air-dried at room temperature to preserve the native biomolecular capping layer and prevent structural alterations that could occur with high-temperature treatments.

Catalytic degradation of dyes

Catalytic activity was tested against 10 mg/L MB and MO using 0.1 M NaBH4. Reactions: 10 mL dye + 3 mL NaBH4 + 200 µL AgNPs (0.3 mg/mL) at room temperature (triplicate). Controls lacked AgNPs. Absorbance monitored at 665 nm (MB) and 464 nm (MO). Degradation efficiency: % = (A0 – At)/A0 × 100. Kinetics fitted to pseudo-first-order, pseudo-second-order, and intraparticle diffusion models.

Results and discussion

Phenolic content and reducing potential

The total phenolic content (TPC) of BPE was significantly higher than that of PPE, consistent with literature reports on fruit peel composition. BPE exhibited markedly higher TPC (8.65 ± 0.51 mg GAE/g dry peel) than PPE (0.253 ± 0.01 mg GAE/g) (Fig. 1), consistent with banana peels’ abundance of polyphenols (catechins, flavonoids) versus pineapple’s dominance of non-phenolics like bromelain (protease), reducing sugars (fructose), and organic acids (citric/malic) [26, 27].

In green synthesis, TPC is often used as an indirect indicator of reducing potential. Higher TPC correlated with greater Ag yield in BPE-AgNPs (EDX: 88.9 wt% Ag vs. 72.6 wt% PPE-AgNPs), as phenolics act as strong reductants [28]. However, PPE produced smaller nanoparticles with superior catalytic efficiency, revealing non-phenolic contributions: organic acids chelate Ag+ for controlled nucleation, proteins/polysaccharides provide steric stabilization limiting growth, and sugars accelerate reduction kinetics [18]; similar observations in low-phenolic extracts yielding stable/small AgNPs. This demonstrates that higher phenolic content does not necessarily translate into enhanced functional performance.

This apparent contradiction underscores that AgNPs synthesis and activity are governed by a synergistic contribution of phenolic and non-phenolic biomolecules, including sugars, organic acids, and proteins. These components influence nucleation rate, growth control, and surface stabilization, ultimately determining particle size and surface area, which are critical for catalytic applications.

Nanoparticle formation and optimization of BPE & PPE-AgNPs

Biosynthesis was initially indicated by a color change from pale yellow to brownish shades, signifying Ag+ reduction to Ag0. UV-Vis spectroscopy confirmed nanoparticle formation with distinct SPR peaks at 430 nm (BPE-AgNPs) and 450 nm (PPE-AgNPs) (Fig. 2a–b), consistent with the established 410–500 nm range for biosynthesized spherical AgNPs arising from surface electron oscillations [29, 30]. PPE-mediated synthesis showed markedly faster kinetics, with rapid SPR peak development and higher sharpness compared to the gradual evolution in BPE-mediated reactions. This difference suggests that overall biomolecular composition, rather than phenolic content alone, governs reduction rates.

OFAT optimization, based on SPR intensity and stability, identified precursor-specific conditions: BPE-AgNPs required 3 mM AgNO3, 2 mL extract, 70 °C, and 72 h incubation; PPE-AgNPs achieved optimal results at 4 mM AgNO₃ with identical volume and temperature but in only 6 h (Fig. 2c–f). These variations reflect phytochemical profiles: BPE’s higher phenolics favor steady reduction, while PPE’s non-phenolic components (carbohydrates, proteins, acids) promote accelerated nucleation [31, 32]. The efficient, stable PPE synthesis demonstrates that rapid formation can yield high-quality AgNPs, contrary to assumptions favoring extended incubation.

Although OFAT effectively identified optimal synthesis conditions, it has inherent limitations, including the inability to capture interaction effects between parameters including synergistic temperature-time influences on reduction rate or particle size. Multivariate methods such as RSM would provide greater insight into these interactions and potentially finer optimization. Future studies could employ RSM to explore such effects, building on the preliminary conditions established here.

While BPE-AgNP synthesis required extended incubation (72 h at 70 °C), this reflects the slower reduction kinetics of banana peel extract compared to pineapple peel extract, which achieved nanoparticle formation within 5 h under similar conditions. Prolonged heating is energy-intensive and may limit sustainability at larger scales; however, the eco-friendly core of this work, agro-waste valorization and avoidance of hazardous chemicals remains intact. Future studies will focus on energy-efficient alternatives such as microwave-assisted synthesis, solar heating, or enzymatic acceleration to reduce energy expenditure and enhance scalability.

PPE thus provides greater synthesis efficiency (reduced time and energy) while producing superior nanoparticles, underscoring compositional influences on green routes. Relative to literature, banana-derived AgNPs typically require 24–96 h (SPR ~ 420–450 nm, sizes 20–80 nm); pineapple systems are less reported and often yield larger particles (> 40 nm). PPE-AgNPs’ rapid, optimized profile advances this precursor’s viability. The formation of AgNPs from BPE and PPE proceeds via a green bioreduction process mediated by the phytochemical constituents of the respective extracts. The general mechanism involves two main stages: (i) reduction of Ag+ ions to Ag0 atoms and (ii) nucleation, growth, and stabilization of the resulting nanoparticles.

In the reduction stage, bioactive molecules in the peel extracts donate electrons to Ag+ ions (from AgNO3), converting them to Ag0. For BPE, the process is primarily driven by its high total phenolic content (e.g., catechins, flavonoids, and polyphenolic compounds). These phenolics undergo oxidation (often forming quinones), releasing electrons and protons that reduce Ag+ to Ag0. The reaction can be represented as:

Ag+ + e− (from phenolics) → Ag0.

This phenolic-dominated reduction is relatively slower, requiring longer incubation (72 h), and leads to thicker biomolecular capping layers.

In contrast, PPE relies more on non-phenolic components such as reducing sugars (fructose), organic acids (citric and malic acid), and proteins (including bromelain). These biomolecules enable faster nucleation through chelation of Ag+ ions and rapid electron transfer, resulting in shorter reaction time (5–6 h) and smaller particle sizes. Organic acids facilitate controlled nucleation by complexing Ag+, while sugars and proteins provide additional reducing power and steric stabilization.

Once Ag° atoms form, they nucleate into small clusters that grow into nanoparticles. The phytochemicals simultaneously act as capping/stabilizing agents by adsorbing onto the nanoparticle surface via functional groups (–OH, –COOH, –NH2, C=O), preventing uncontrolled aggregation through electrostatic repulsion and steric hindrance. This capping is evident from the stronger O–H/N–H and C= O bands in the FTIR spectra of PPE-AgNPs, contributing to their smaller size and higher surface accessibility. The overall process is eco-friendly, occurring in aqueous medium at moderate temperature (70 °C) without hazardous chemicals. Structural and elemental characterization

FTIR spectra revealed characteristic absorption bands confirming the involvement of peel-derived biomolecules in Ag⁺ reduction and nanoparticle capping (Fig. 4). The broad band at 3200–3400 cm⁻¹ is attributed to O–H stretching vibrations from hydroxyl groups of polyphenols, flavonoids, alcohols, and phenolic compounds, as well as overlapping N–H stretching from amines or proteins. The peak around 1630–1750 cm−1 corresponds to C=O stretching vibrations of carbonyl groups from amides (proteins), carboxylic acids (organic acids such as citric and malic acid), and quinones formed during phenolic oxidation. Additional bands at ~ 1380 cm−1 (C–O stretching from carboxylates or phenolics) and 1050–1100 cm−1 (C–O–C stretching from polysaccharides) were also observed.

These functional groups, originating primarily from polyphenols (more abundant in BPE), reducing sugars, organic acids, and proteins (more prominent in PPE), act as natural reducing and capping agents. The O–H and C=O groups facilitate electron donation for the reduction of Ag+ to Ag⁰, while their adsorption onto the nanoparticle surface provides steric and electrostatic stabilization, preventing aggregation. Stronger intensities of the O–H/N–H and C=O bands in PPE-AgNPs indicate denser contributions from non-phenolic biomolecules (sugars, acids, and proteins), which correlate with the smaller particle size and superior catalytic performance observed for PPE-AgNPs compared to the phenolic-rich BPE-AgNPs.

Weaker or shifted peaks in the 1050–1100 cm−1 region (C–O–C of polysaccharides) and aromatic vibrations (600–800 cm−1) further confirm the involvement of these classes beyond phenolics alone [31]. Notably, PPE-AgNPs exhibited stronger intensities in O–H/C–H and C=O bands compared to BPE-AgNPs, indicating denser and more diverse biomolecular capping. This enhanced stabilization aligns with PPE-AgNPs’ smaller particle size (FESEM/XRD) and superior catalytic performance, as the organic layer exposes more active sites while facilitating electron transfer during dye degradation.

X‑ray diffraction (XRD)

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns confirmed the high crystallinity and phase purity of both biosynthesized AgNPs. As shown in Fig. 4, both BPE-AgNPs and PPE-AgNPs exhibited four distinct characteristic diffraction peaks at 2θ values of 38.1°, 44.3°, 64.4°, and 77.4°, which correspond to the (111), (200), (220), and (311) planes of face-centered cubic (fcc) metallic silver, respectively (JCPDS Card No. 04-0783). A weak peak at ~ 81.5° corresponding to the (222) plane was also occasionally observed. These hkl indices are consistent with the standard Bragg reflections for pure crystalline silver with a face-centered cubic lattice structure (Fig. 5).

The patterns exhibited no impurity peaks, indicating phase-pure metallic silver [10, 24]. The strongest and sharpest peak at 2θ = 38.1° corresponds to the (111) plane, which is the most thermodynamically stable and densely packed plane in fcc silver. This preferential orientation along the (111) facet is significant because the (111) plane possesses higher surface energy and exposes more active sites, contributing to the enhanced catalytic activity observed in both AgNP samples, particularly the smaller PPE-AgNPs. This yielded 18.7 nm (BPE-AgNPs) and 12.3 nm (PPE-AgNPs), consistent with FESEM particle sizes and similar to neem/papaya-derived AgNPs [25].

The crystallite sizes calculated from XRD using the Scherrer equation were ~ 18.7 nm for BPE-AgNPs and ~ 12.3 nm for PPE-AgNPs, which are consistently smaller than the average particle sizes determined by FESEM (30.99 ± 9.47 nm for BPE-AgNPs and 21.6 ± 7.01 nm for PPE-AgNPs). This discrepancy is commonly observed in green-synthesized nanoparticles and is primarily attributed to the presence of an organic biomolecular capping layer derived from the peel extracts. XRD measures the coherent scattering domains (crystallite size) within the metallic core, whereas FESEM visualizes the overall hydrodynamic or capped particle diameter, including the surface-adsorbed phytochemical layer. The observed difference (approximately 10–12 nm) thus corresponds to the thickness of the stabilizing organic shell, which is more pronounced in the phenolic-rich BPE-AgNPs. Surprisingly, PPE-AgNPs exhibited smaller crystallites despite lower phenolic content. This arises from non-phenolic biomolecules (organic acids for chelation-controlled nucleation; polysaccharides/proteins for steric stabilization), collectively restricting growth beyond phenolic-driven mechanisms. Smaller PPE crystallites provide higher surface area and (111) facet exposure, enhancing catalytic performance.

Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM)

FESEM was used to examine the morphology and size distribution of the biosynthesized AgNPs. As shown in Figs. 6 and 7, both BPE-AgNPs and PPE-AgNPs exhibited predominantly spherical morphology with relatively narrow size distributions and minimal agglomeration. The average particle diameter was determined by measuring the diameter of more than 100 individual primary particles from multiple high-magnification FESEM images using ImageJ software. For BPE-AgNPs, the mean particle size was calculated as 30.99 ± 9.47 nm (Fig. 6). For PPE-AgNPs, the mean particle size was 21.6 ± 7.01 nm (Fig. 7).

Energy‑dispersive X‑ray (EDX)

EDX analysis confirmed silver as the dominant element, with weight percentages of Ag (88.9%) in BPE-AgNPs with some peaks in the additional elements carbon (7.3%), oxygen (2.2%) and chlorine (1.6%) while for the PPE-AgNPs, we also obtained silver have the highest amount of (72.6%), carbon (12%), oxygen (3.2.%), chlorine (11.8%) and sulphur (0.4%) as shown in Fig. 8. The result showed that BPE-AgNPs have a higher amount of silver compared to PPE-AgNPs. This is because, from the TPC analysis, BPE has a higher phenolic compound compared to PPE resulting in its capability to reduce Ag+ to AgNPs.

Additionally, minor signals for carbon, oxygen, and chlorine were attributed to biomolecular capping agents and residual extract components, aligning with strong organic functionalization observed in FTIR spectra. The higher Ag content in BPE-AgNPs correlates with its phenolic-rich precursor, enhancing reduction efficiency and metallic yield [34–37]; similar compositions in citrus/onion peel-derived AgNPs.

While oxygen and chlorine peaks could superficially suggest minor oxide (Ag2O) or chloride (AgCl) phases, common artifacts in plant-mediated synthesis due to extract residues, FTIR evidence of robust carboxyl, hydroxyl, and amine capping favors organic stabilization as the primary mechanism. Definitive phase identification would require complementary techniques such as XPS. Importantly, EDX provides localized elemental data and should not be interpreted as a quantitative synthesis yield; gravimetric or spectroscopic methods (e.g., ICP-MS, AAS) are needed for accurate conversion efficiency, to be addressed in future work. Hence, the smaller size and organic-rich surface of PPE-AgNPs likely expose more active sites, contributing to their superior catalytic performance despite lower Ag wt%.

Catalytic performance and mechanistic insights

Both synthesized AgNPs significantly accelerated the reduction of methylene blue (MB) and methyl orange (MO) compared to NaBH4-only controls (~ 20–40% degradation over extended times), confirming their essential catalytic role as electron relays. PPE-AgNPs consistently outperformed BPE-AgNPs, achieving ~ 95% MB degradation in 60 min and ~ 88% MO degradation in 75 min, versus ~ 82% MB and ~ 73% MO for BPE-AgNPs (Figs. 9). Time-dependent UV–Vis spectra revealed progressive decline in characteristic peaks (665 nm for MB; 464 nm for MO), with faster and more complete decolorization by PPE-AgNPs, indicative of efficient chromophore cleavage (thiazine ring in MB; azo − N=N− bond in MO).

The catalytic degradation mechanism follows a surface-mediated electron relay pathway, in which NaBH4 serves as an electron donor and the dye molecules act as electron acceptors, while AgNPs facilitate electron transfer between them (schematic in Fig. 10). This process occurs via simultaneous adsorption of BH4− ions and dye molecules onto the nanoparticle surface, enabling efficient electron shuttling and rapid reduction of chromophoric groups [38–40]. Such electron-relay behavior of AgNPs has been widely reported, where the metal surface acts as a conductive bridge between donor and acceptor species, significantly lowering activation energy and accelerating reaction kinetics [41, 42].

This explains the contrary superiority of PPE-AgNPs despite lower phenolic content: while phenolics drive efficient reduction and capping in BPE-AgNPs, non-phenolic biomolecules in PPE enable superior surface accessibility, which dominates catalytic efficiency [30, 43–45].

Comparison of Fruit Peel-Derived AgNPs

Reaction conditions such as AgNO3 concentration, extract-to-precursor ratio, temperature, and pH, initial dye concentrations (typically 5–50 mg/L), and the presence/absence of NaBH4 as a co-reductant vary considerably across studies, making absolute quantitative comparisons challenging. Nevertheless, this work stands out by conducting a direct, systematic head-to-head comparison of banana peel extract (BPE) and pineapple peel extract (PPE) under identical experimental conditions. This approach uniquely reveals how contrasting phytochemical profiles, high total phenolic content in BPE versus sugar-, organic acid-, and protein-rich composition in PPE, govern reduction kinetics, particle size, surface capping, and catalytic efficiency. Consequently, the smaller PPE-AgNPs achieved superior degradation performance (~ 95% MB in 60 min and ~ 88% MO in 75 min) despite lower phenolic content, underscoring particle size and non-phenolic biomolecular contributions as dominant factors. Stability and reusability of the synthesized AgNPs in this study were not evaluated and will be assessed in future work.

Strengths, limitations, and future directions

This study’s strength lies in its systematic dual‑waste comparison under identical conditions, revealing a counterintuitive size–activity relationship and providing mechanistic insights into phytochemical contributions. Limitations include reliance on OFAT optimization, absence of HRTEM due to equipment constraints, and qualitative yield estimation from EDX. Future work will employ multivariate optimization (RSM), advanced characterization (HRTEM, XPS), and exploration of additional biological activities (antibacterial, antioxidant, anti‑inflammatory) to expand applicability. Moreover, energy‑efficient synthesis routes (microwave, solar heating) will be investigated to enhance scalability. Philosophically, this work illustrates how waste valorization transforms discarded biomass into functional nanomaterials, aligning with UN SDGs on clean water, responsible consumption, and climate action. It demonstrates that sustainability in nanotechnology is not only about replacing hazardous reagents but also about re‑imagining waste as a resource, thereby bridging environmental remediation with circular economy principles.

Environmental relevance and scale-up perspectives

The catalytic performance of the biosynthesized AgNPs in synthetic dye solutions highlights their potential for practical environmental remediation. To assess applicability in real wastewater matrices, future studies should evaluate degradation efficiency in complex effluents from textile industries, which contain mixed dyes, salts, organic matter, and varying pH. Preliminary evidence from similar green-synthesized AgNPs indicates that modest matrix effects may slightly reduce efficiency (e.g., from > 95% in deionized water to ~ 83–89% in tap or real textile wastewater), yet high degradation rates are often maintained, supporting their viability for treating actual industrial discharges.

Reusability is another critical factor for sustainable application. Although not evaluated in the present work, analogous plant-mediated AgNPs have demonstrated good recyclability, retaining high catalytic activity (typically < 10–12% loss) over 4–8 consecutive cycles after simple recovery by centrifugation and washing. The robust biomolecular capping observed in our PPE-AgNPs and BPE-AgNPs is expected to enhance colloidal stability and facilitate such recovery, reducing operational costs and secondary waste generation. Future experiments will include reusability tests (at least 5 cycles) under repeated MB and MO degradation conditions.

Regarding potential ecotoxicity, green-synthesized AgNPs generally exhibit lower toxicity compared to chemically prepared counterparts due to the biocompatible organic capping layer. However, silver ion leaching and long-term environmental impacts on aquatic organisms or soil microbiota must be carefully assessed using standard ecotoxicity assays (e.g., on algae, daphnia, or plant seedlings). Preliminary phytotoxicity studies on treated wastewater from similar systems have shown minimal inhibition of seedling growth (rice, mustard, lentil), suggesting that properly applied AgNP-treated effluents may be safe for agricultural reuse. Comprehensive leaching and ecotoxicity evaluations will be prioritized in follow-up work to ensure environmental safety.

From a scale-up perspective, banana and pineapple peels offer abundant, low-cost feedstock; however, seasonal variability in phytochemical composition (due to fruit maturity, climate, and geographical factors) may affect extract reducing capacity and reproducibility of AgNP properties. Extract storage stability should also be investigated, as refrigerated aqueous extracts (4 °C) typically maintain activity for weeks to months, but long-term standardization (e.g., spray-drying or lyophilization) will be necessary for industrial consistency. Overall, the process shows promising industrial feasibility through its simplicity, use of water as solvent, moderate temperature (70 °C), and avoidance of toxic chemicals. Integration with microwave or solar-assisted heating could further reduce energy input, enhancing economic viability and alignment with circular bioeconomy principles for large-scale wastewater treatment.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the effective valorization of banana and pineapple peel wastes as sustainable precursors for the green synthesis of AgNPs, which exhibit strong catalytic performance in dye degradation. Although banana peel extract exhibited significantly higher phenolic content and greater Ag yield, pineapple peel-derived AgNPs consistently showed superior catalytic efficiency, achieving ~ 95% methylene blue degradation in 60 min and ~ 88% methyl orange degradation in 75 min. This enhanced performance is attributed to their smaller crystallite and particle sizes, thinner biomolecular capping, and improved surface accessibility, which collectively facilitate faster electron transfer during catalysis. Importantly, this work reveals that catalytic efficiency is not solely governed by total phenolic content but is strongly influenced by the overall phytochemical composition, particularly non-phenolic biomolecules such as reducing sugars, organic acids, and proteins. These components play a critical role in controlling nucleation, growth, and surface characteristics of the nanoparticles, ultimately dictating their functional performance. The observed precursor-dependent kinetic behavior further highlights the interplay between nanoparticle structure and dye-specific degradation mechanisms. In addition, providing a direct comparative evaluation under identical conditions, this study advances current understanding of agro-waste-derived nanocatalysts and establishes a clear structure–activity relationship driven by phytochemical diversity. The findings underscore the potential of underutilized biomass, particularly pineapple peel waste, as an efficient and eco-friendly resource for nanomaterial synthesis. While the synthesized AgNPs demonstrate promising catalytic activity, further investigations into reusability, long-term stability, and performance in real wastewater systems are necessary to fully establish their practical applicability. Future work will also focus on multivariate optimization strategies, advanced characterization techniques, and evaluation of environmental safety. Overall, this study contributes to the development of sustainable nanotechnology solutions aligned with circular economy principles and environmental remediation goals.
