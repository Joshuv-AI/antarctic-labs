Ultrafast photoreduction driven by interfacial spin exchange in manganese-doped quantum dots
Authors: Ho Jin, Valerio Pinchetti, Connor Orrison, Jungchul Noh, Dong Hee Son, Victor I. Klimov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Spin-active dopants offer a powerful yet largely unexplored route for controlling interfacial redox chemistry in quantum-confined semiconductors. Here we show that manganese doping in cadmium selenide quantum dots enables an ultrafast spin-exchange-mediated electron-transfer pathway that allows methyl viologen reduction even when conventional band-edge energetics are unfavorable for charge transfer. Femtosecond transient absorption spectroscopy reveals that manganese dopants accelerate electron-transfer dynamics by more than an order of magnitude while opening a hot-exciton reduction channel in which a manganese ion captures a photoexcited exciton prior to phonon-assisted cooling. Subsequent spin-flip relaxation of the excited manganese ion drives charge separation and reduction of a molecular acceptor. This mechanism operates efficiently across resonant and off-resonant (energy-uphill and downhill) regimes, identifying spin-exchange coupling—rather than band alignment—as the dominant factor governing electron-transfer rates and efficiencies. These findings establish magnetic doping as a viable strategy for harvesting hot carriers and enabling energetically demanding photocatalytic transformations.

Introduction

Colloidal quantum dots (QDs) are widely explored as photochemical platforms capable of converting absorbed photons into charge carriers that drive chemical transformations1–13. In a reduction reaction, the reducing power of a photoexcited QD is typically limited by the absolute energy of its conduction-band (CB) edge. In principle, this limitation can be overcome by utilizing hot, unrelaxed electrons generated by absorption of higher-energy photons. Beyond their enhanced reduction potential, such hot electrons possess spatially extended wavefunctions that can facilitate coupling to molecular acceptors or promote their extraction into the surrounding medium as chemically active solvated electrons8,13–16. However, the practical use of hot electrons is severely limited by their rapid energy loss through phonon emission, resulting in carrier cooling within just a few picoseconds17,18.

Recent studies have shown that hot-carrier energy can be effectively captured and preserved through ultrafast spin-exchange (SE) interactions between photoexcited electron–hole pairs (excitons) and magnetic manganese (Mn) dopants in colloidal QDs8,16,19–24. In this process, the excess energy of a hot exciton generated by above-bandgap excitation can be transferred to a Mn²⁺ ion within ~100 fs—well before phonon dissipation occurs16,22,25. The Mn ion undergoes a spin-flip transition from its ground-state ⁶A₁ configuration, corresponding to five co-aligned 3d-electron spins, to an excited ⁴T₁ state where one spin is inverted26. These two configurations, hereafter referred to as ‘spin-up’ and ‘spin-down’ states, form the basis of the SE interaction.

Remarkably, these SE interactions not only enable ultrafast energy capture by magnetic dopants but also allow energy to be transferred back to intrinsic QD carriers. This ‘electron up-conversion’ process—an SE-mediated Auger-type recombination24—can eject high-energy electrons from the QD, producing efficient photoemission19,20,27 and even visible-light generation of solvated electrons16. Mn-doped QDs have thus emerged as promising materials for solar-energy conversion, photocatalysis, and other light-driven processes7,8,16,19,28–31.

In this work, we use ultrafast transient absorption (TA) spectroscopy to investigate the photoreduction of methyl viologen (MV²⁺) via interfacial electron transfer from CdSe-based QDs, with and without Mn doping, across regimes spanning EX < EMn to EX > EMn, where EX is the QD band-edge exciton energy and EMn = 2.1 eV is the Mn spin-flip transition energy. We find that Mn doping dramatically accelerates interfacial transfer dynamics and, most importantly, enables efficient MV²⁺ reduction even in larger, lower-bandgap QDs, where conventional cold-electron transfer from the QD band edge is energetically unfavorable. These results point to a hot-carrier-mediated mechanism in which hot-exciton energy is first captured by Mn via ultrafast SE transfer and then converted into chemical reduction through SE-driven charge separation. In this process, the excited Mn ion undergoes spin-flip relaxation by transferring a spin-down electron to MV²⁺, while a compensating spin-up electron is supplied from the QD valence band (VB) to restore the Mn ground state. Together, these findings show that SE interactions in magnetically doped QDs provide an effective route for channeling hot-exciton energy into interfacial charge transfer, thereby enabling photochemical transformations that are inefficient or inaccessible with cold band-edge carriers alone.

Results

QD samples

For this study, we synthesized a series of CdSe-based QDs with and without manganese dopants (see Methods for synthesis details). Each QD consisted of a size-controlled wurtzite CdSe core overcoated with a thin (ca. 1 monolayer) CdS shell to improve surface passivation. Undoped QDs were prepared following established procedures, as described previously32.

Mn-doped CdSe/CdS QDs were synthesized using a diffusion-doping approach applied to preformed CdSe cores, followed by deposition of a thin ( ~0.3 nm), single-monolayer CdS shell (Fig. 1a, top). To enhance Mn incorporation during doping while minimizing undesired postgrowth of the QDs, diphenylphosphine (DPP) was introduced at the core synthesis stage to ensure complete consumption of Cd precursors32. We focused on three QD size regimes with comparable diameters for doped and undoped samples: ‘small’ (3.4–4.0 nm), ‘resonant’ (4.5–4.7 nm), and ‘large’ (~5.6 nm) (see Fig. 1a, bottom; Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2, and Supplementary Table 1 for transmission electron microscopy images and detailed size characterization). Elemental analysis of Mn-doped QDs by inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES) revealed total Mn contents (xMn) of 7–11% across the doped samples (Supplementary Table 2). These values correspond to total Mn populations (nMn) ranging from ~60 (small QDs) to ~180 (large QDs) ions per dot. Most of these ions are located in the near-surface region of the CdSe core and interact weakly with intrinsic QD excitons due to the rapid decay of excitonic wavefunctions toward the QD periphery. At xMn ≈ 0.1, characteristic of our samples, the fraction of internally located Mn ions (xMn,int) that exhibit strong exchange coupling to QD excitons is typically ~5% of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${x}_{{{\rm{Mn}}}}$$\end{document}xMn (ref. 32), corresponding to nMn,int ≈ 3 (small QDs) to ~9 (large QDs).

The QD sizes were selected to realize three distinct regimes of alignment between the Mn spin-flip transition energy (EMn = 2.1 eV) and the QD band-edge exciton energy (EX), determined from photoluminescence (PL) peak positions: EX > EMn for small QDs, EX ≈ EMn for resonant QDs, and EX < EMn for large QDs. These regimes are illustrated by the PL spectra in Fig. 1b for undoped (top) and Mn-doped (bottom) samples; corresponding absorption spectra are shown in Fig. 1c.

We note that in all Mn-doped samples, the PL is dominated by the intrinsic excitonic emission, with virtually no contribution from Mn-related emission—even for small QDs where EX > EMn. As discussed later, this behavior arises from strong exchange coupling between the QD exciton and the Mn spin-flip transition, which leads to rapid bidirectional energy transfer. As a result, a photoexcited state samples both the excitonic and Mn components of the coupled QD–dopant system, but radiative recombination occurs predominantly through the excitonic channel. This is because the exciton radiative lifetime (~20–30 ns at room temperature33) is many orders of magnitude shorter than that of the Mn spin-flip transition (~1 ms, ref. 26.), which is nominally dipole-forbidden.

As synthesized, the QDs were passivated with oleic acid ligands, providing colloidal stability in nonpolar solvents. To enable aqueous dispersion, these ligands were replaced with 2-mercaptoethane sulfonate (MES), which binds preferentially to Cd²⁺ surface sites through its thiol group, forming Cd–S bonds. The terminal sulfonate group functions as a polyelectrolyte, imparting negative surface charge and electrostatic stabilization in water34.

For photochemical measurements, aqueous QD dispersions were mixed with aqueous solutions of MV²⁺, resulting in the formation of QD–MV²⁺ complexes. MV²⁺ adsorbs in a ‘face-on’ configuration, driven by electrostatic attraction between the positively charged bipyridinium nitrogen atoms and the negatively charged S²⁻ ions exposed at the surface of the CdS shell35,36. This direct interfacial contact results in strong electronic coupling between the QDs and the acceptor molecules, thereby facilitating efficient charge transfer.

Based on the ratio of the QD surface area to the projected molecular area of MV²⁺ (~0.4 nm²), our QDs could, in principle, accommodate approximately 100–200 MV²⁺ molecules depending on QD size. However, densely packed surface ligands substantially limit the number of accessible binding sites, and the actual number of MV²⁺ molecules adsorbed is typically fewer than 10 per QD36,37.

To saturate all available anchoring sites on the QD surface, we used a large excess of MV²⁺ (typically ~0.5 mM) relative to the QD concentration (2–8 µM). Under these conditions, we can assume that the number of charge-transfer–active MV²⁺ molecules attached to the QDs is approximately proportional to the QD surface area for the size-varied samples.

In experiments on photoinduced QD-to-MV²⁺ electron transfer, MES ligands also serve as efficient hole scavengers for QDs that have lost photogenerated electrons through charge transfer. Upon oxidation, MES ligands tend to detach from the QD surface. To compensate for the resulting loss of surface passivation, we add additional MES (typically ~0.5 mM) to the QD–MV²⁺ samples prior to spectroscopic measurements. This procedure improves sample stability and helps prevent aggregation and degradation of the QDs.

Initial assessment of the feasibility of QD–MV²⁺ electron transfer

Among other parameters, the electron-transfer rate at a QD–molecule interface depends strongly on the energetic driving force between the CB edge of the QD (ECB) and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) of the electron acceptor (ELUMO): ΔEET = ECB − ELUMO. For methyl viologen (MV²⁺), acting as the electron acceptor, the LUMO energy inferred from its first reduction potential is ELUMO ≈ –4.0 eV vs vacuum38.

The QD CB edge energies can be estimated from the CB energy of bulk CdSe (ECB,0) shifted upward by the size-dependent electron confinement energy (ΔECB,conf): ECB = ECB,0 + ΔECB,conf. The confinement energies were determined from the difference between the spectral energy of the band-edge (1S) absorption peak (Fig. 1b), used here as a measure of the QD bandgap (Eg), and the bulk CdSe bandgap (Eg,0 = 1.75 eV), partitioned between the CB and VB according to the inverse effective masses of the electron (mₑ = 0.12 m₀) and hole (mh = 0.5 m₀).

Applying this procedure to the studied samples, we obtain ΔECB,conf = 0.41, 0.33, and 0.27 eV for the small, resonant, and large QDs, respectively. These values indicate a progressive increase in the ‘reducing power’ of a band-edge (cold) electron with decreasing QD size—amounting to ~140 meV between the small and large QD samples.

For the ideal case of a clean CdSe surface in vacuum, ECB,0 = –4.7 eV vs vacuum39, which yields ECB = –4.28, –4.37, and –4.42 eV for the small, resonant, and large QDs, respectively. All of these values lie below the MV²⁺ LUMO energy, implying that direct electron transfer from QDs to MV²⁺ should be energetically unfavorable. However, previous studies of CdSe QD–MV²⁺ complexes have demonstrated efficient electron transfer in this system35,36,40,41, suggesting that estimates based on the idealized ECB,0 value likely underestimate the true value of ECB for the QD samples.

This discrepancy is unsurprising, as the electron affinity—and thus the CB-edge energy —is known to depend sensitively on surface conditions. In colloidal QDs, it is strongly influenced by surface-layer stoichiometry, capping ligands (their identity, density, and binding mode), as well as the surrounding solvent environment42. Indeed, cyclic voltammetry studies of CdSe QDs43,44 place the CB edge at around −3.5 eV for sizes comparable to our small and resonant QDs, making photoreduction by CB electrons energetically feasible.

Given these uncertainties in absolute energy-level alignment, we therefore assess the feasibility of QD → MV²⁺ electron transfer experimentally by comparing the population dynamics of isolated QDs and QD–MV²⁺ complexes.

Dynamics of QD–MV²⁺ electron transfer in undoped samples

To investigate population dynamics in QD-only and QD–MV²⁺ samples, we employed femtosecond pump–probe TA spectroscopy. In this technique, photoexcitation–induced changes in the sample’s absorption coefficient are monitored using a time-delayed, broadband femtosecond white-light continuum pulse (see Methods). The pump pulse duration was approximately 150 fs, and the pump fluence was adjusted such that the average number of photons absorbed per QD per pulse, 〈Nabs〉, was less than 0.1, ensuring that the measured dynamics correspond to the single-exciton regime. Unless otherwise noted, the pump photon energy was 2.4 eV. To assess the role of hot-carrier effects, additional measurements were performed using spectrally tunable pump pulses with photon energies of 2.0, 2.1, and 2.21 eV.

Figure 2a–c shows the early-time TA dynamics (pump-probe time delay, Δt, up to 25 ps) recorded at the peak of the 1S band-edge feature. This signal is dominated by state-filling-induced bleaching of the 1Se electron state45. The hole contribution is small (virtually negligible) because the 1S absorption involves a lower-energy, weakly populated VB state rather than the topmost hole level responsible for emission. Thermal broadening further spreads the hole population over many closely spaced VB states, reducing its impact on the bleach amplitude. While typically undetectable in TA spectra in the presence of co-localized electrons, QD holes can become ‘visible’ after interfacial electron transfer. Removal of electrons disrupts local charge neutrality, generating an internal electric field that modifies the spectrum via the Stark effect. This results in characteristic derivative-like features in the TA signal. For the band-edge transition, which typically red-shifts, this manifests as induced absorption below the original transition and a bleach feature just above it45,46.

In Fig. 2a–c, data for isolated QDs (dashed lines) are compared with those for QD–MV²⁺ complexes (solid lines). For all three QD sizes, the TA traces of isolated samples exhibit only a modest decay ( ~5–15%) within the 25 ps time window shown in Fig. 2a–c, consistent with intrinsic QD relaxation dynamics. Upon conjugation of large QDs with MV²⁺ molecules, the 1S signal loss during the first 25 ps after excitation increases only slightly (from ~5% to ~13%) (Fig. 2c), indicating that QD-to-MV²⁺ electron transfer does not lead to appreciable depletion of the QD population on this timescale. This behavior points to unfavorable energetics—either because the driving force for electron transfer (ΔEET) is negative or, if positive, too small to support fast transfer dynamics (Fig. 2d).

In contrast, the resonant and small QDs exhibit a pronounced acceleration of the 1S bleach decay: at Δt = 25 ps, the 1S signal decreases by ~80% (Fig. 2b) and ~100% (Fig. 2a), respectively. This behavior indicates that the confinement-induced increase in driving force for smaller QDs is sufficient to promote rapid electron transfer from the QDs to MV²⁺, efficiently reducing it to MV1⁺ (Fig. 2e).

From the measured 1S bleach relaxation times (τ1S), we infer a characteristic charge-transfer time constant for the small-QD sample of τET ≈ 4 ps, assuming τET ≈ τ1S. Importantly, the decay of the 1S bleach temporally correlates with the rise of the photoinduced absorption at 2.0 eV (Supplementary Fig. 3), which lies near the peak of the absorption band of the reduced methyl viologen species MV¹⁺ (Supplementary Fig. 4). This temporal correlation confirms that the observed 1S bleach dynamics arise from direct electron transfer from the QDs to MV²⁺.

The 1S bleach relaxation time increases for the resonant QD sample (τ1S ≈ 14 ps) and becomes dramatically longer for the large-QD sample, for which τ1S ≈ 940 ps, as determined from a comparison of long-time TA dynamics measured in the presence and absence of MV²⁺ (Supplementary Fig. 5).

The observed dynamics are essentially independent of the exact detection energy within the band-edge absorption bleach (Supplementary Fig. 6). This behavior reflects the narrow size distribution of the synthesized samples (see Supplementary Fig. 2) as well as additional spectroscopic size selection inherent to pump–probe measurements. The latter effect is particularly pronounced under our experimental conditions where the pump photon energy exceeds the band-edge absorption peak by only ~150–300 meV.

Using the measured 1S bleach lifetimes, we extract QD-size-dependent transfer rates kET = 1/τET, and then normalize them by the QD surface area to obtain a quantity proportional to the transfer rate per single MV²⁺ molecule, k0,ET. This analysis yields relative values of k0,ET of 1: 0.16: 1.6×10⁻³ for the small, resonant, and large QDs, respectively, revealing a dramatic decrease in transfer rate with decreasing ΔEET.

This strong sensitivity toward ΔEET indicates that electron transfer occurs near the nominal zero-temperature (T = 0) energetic threshold defined by ΔEET = 0. Indeed, previous studies of QD–molecular-acceptor complexes have shown that transfer rates can drop by nearly two orders of magnitude as ΔEET is decreased by just ~200 meV as it approaches zero47,48. This behavior is consistent with our interpretation that the large-QD sample lies in a regime where the driving force is either only slightly positive or even negative.

The formation of the reduced methyl viologen species (MV¹⁺) is clearly evident in the long-delay TA spectra (Fig. 3c), where it appears as a characteristic absorption band with a maximum at ~2.0 eV (compare with Supplementary Fig. 4). This feature replaces the QD-related 1S bleach that dominates the early-time spectra (Fig. 3b).

A similar trend—namely, strong acceleration of electron-transfer dynamics—is also observed for the small Mn-doped QDs (Fig. 3d). In this case, depopulation of the 1Sₑ electron state occurs with a time constant of ~700 fs, more than five times faster than in the corresponding undoped reference QDs. This indicates essentially complete transfer of the electron population from the QDs to methyl viologen molecules. The absence of pronounced derivative-like features associated with the Stark effect—expected for uncompensated holes—further suggests that holes have also been removed from the QDs, most likely via reduction by MES, as discussed earlier.

In contrast, the TA spectra of undoped QDs exhibit a much slower evolution (Fig. 4b). At Δt = 10 ps, the signal remains dominated by the QD bleach, while the MV¹⁺ absorption reaches only ~10% of the 1S bleach amplitude. Even at Δt = 100 ps, a pronounced QD-related 1S bleach persists, indicating that a fraction of photocarriers still remains in the QDs.

Most strikingly, the large Mn: QD sample exhibits clear signatures of electron transfer, even though this process is weak in the undoped reference (compare Figs. 2c and 3e). Electron transfer is evidenced by an accelerated 1S bleach decay with a time constant of τ1S ≈ 46 ps. As discussed later in this work, additional confirmation of methyl viologen reduction under these nominally less favorable energetic conditions comes from the appearance of the ~2 eV MV1⁺ spectral feature in the TA spectra.

Taken together, these observations demonstrate that Mn dopants strongly facilitate electron transfer, not only dramatically accelerating the transfer dynamics but also opening an efficient charge-transfer pathway in a regime where this process is hindered by unfavorable alignment of electronic states in pristine, dopant-free QDs.

While in this work we focus on photoreduction driven by short laser pulses, which allows us to probe reaction dynamics, incorporation of Mn can also enhance photochemical reactions under low-intensity continuous-wave (cw) excitation. In particular, as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 7, the use of Mn-doped QDs leads to a substantial increase in the yield of MV1+ formation (more than a threefold enhancement compared to the undoped reference) under excitation with a 2.33 eV cw light source at an intensity of only 250 μW. Previous studies of QD-based solar cells similarly indicate a beneficial effect of Mn doping on photovoltaic performance under standard sunlight-like illumination28.

In the next section, we discuss the SE mechanism through which Mn dopants act as highly efficient mediators of electron transfer between QDs and molecular acceptors.

Spin-exchange–mediated excitation transfer in Mn-doped quantum dots

In the field of Mn-doped diluted magnetic semiconductors (DMS), it is well established that Mn dopants engage in highly efficient, bidirectional excitation transfer with the host material through Auger-type processes mediated by sp-d exchange Coulomb coupling25,26,49,50. In this process, the number of electrons in the Mn 3d shell remains unchanged, while the spin of one electron is reversed (a spin-flip event), resulting in a change of the total Mn-ion spin by ΔS = 1 and a corresponding change in the Mn-ion energy by EMn = 2.1 eV. To conserve total spin, the spin of the QD excitonic state must also change by one, implying that efficient energy transfer to or from a Mn ion occurs when the exciton has a total spin of one—that is, when it is in the ‘dark’ exciton configuration with a vanishing dipole moment16,22,25,50.

Photoinduced QD-to-Mn excitation transfer (‘forward’ transfer, rate kF) can be viewed as a concerted spin-exchange process involving coupled transfer events of two electrons with opposite spins16,22,25,50 (Fig. 5a, black arrows). In this process, transfer of a spin-up electron from the Mn ion’s 3d shell to the QD VB, where it fills the photoexcited hole, occurs simultaneously with transfer of a spin-down electron from the QD CB into the vacated Mn 3 d orbital. These coupled electron-transfer events are mediated by p-d and s-d exchange terms of the Coulomb interaction, respectively. Together, they produce an energy- and spin-conserving final state comprising an excited Mn ion (Mn*) in the spin-down ⁴T₁ state and a de-excited QD in its ground state. The depiction in Fig. 5a uses individual electron (hole) energy levels to illustrate SE-mediated QD–Mn excitation exchange. While this representation is helpful for visualizing the spin-selection rules underlying the process, it is not intended to accurately reflect the absolute alignment of the QD CB and VB levels with the ground and excited states of the Mn ion. In fact, such alignment is not essential for QD–Mn excitation exchange, as the process is governed by the relationship between the energy of the QD exciton (EX = ECB − EVB) and the energy of the Mn spin-flip transition (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${E}_{{{\rm{Mn}}}}={E}_{{4}_{{T}_{1}}}-{E}_{{6}_{{A}_{1}}}$$\end{document}EMn=E4T1−E6A1). The energy-conservation aspect of this mechanism is illustrated in Fig. 5b, which represents SE-driven energy transfer as an exchange between a QD-based exciton (X) and a Mn-based excitation (Mn*). While this depiction does not explicitly convey spin dynamics, it provides a clearer view of the energy flow (uphill or downhill) involved in SE-mediated processes.

In ref. 22, the forward (τF = 1/kF) and backward (τB = 1/kB) transfer times were evaluated for Mn-doped CdSe QD samples with an average Mn content of ~0.5 ions per QD. This corresponds to an ensemble in which ~60% of QDs are undoped, while the doped dots predominantly contain a single Mn ion, as expected from Poisson statistics. The studied QD size corresponded to a downhill alignment of excitonic and Mn states (EX > EMn). Under these conditions, τF was found to be ~100–200 fs, while τB followed τB = τFexp(ΔΕX,Mn/δ0), where ΔΕX,Mn = EX − EMn and δ0 = 103 meV (ref. 22). Rapid forward and backward transfer establish a quasi-steady distribution between excitonic and Mn-based states, with occupation probabilities pX and pMn, related by pX/pMn = τF/τB (Fig. 5b).

Notably, the characteristic energy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\delta }_{0}$$\end{document}δ0 is much larger than the thermal energy at room temperature (~25 meV). This indicates that the rapid QD–Mn population branching does not arise from a fully thermalized Boltzmann distribution, but rather from a nonequilibrium quasi-steady state established on the ultrafast timescale of SE interactions.

In addition to energy conservation, QD–Mn excitation exchange is governed by spin selection rules. Within a simple spin-1/2 model16, the band-edge exciton has four configurations: two bright states (SX = 0) and two dark states (SX = 1). Only one dark exciton configuration, with the appropriate spin projection, can couple to the Mn ion. Furthermore, the forward transfer rate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${k}_{F}$$\end{document}kF is independent of the number of Mn dopants in a QD22,24 and corresponds to the single-ion limit.

This lack of doping-level dependence in SE-mediated QD–Mn interactions has been observed previously in studies of both single-exciton radiative recombination and multiexciton nonradiative Auger decay16,24. These findings have been rationalized within a phenomenological model based on a ‘preselection principle.’ In this picture, the two spin-transfer events involved in either SE-driven QD–Mn excitation exchange or SE–Auger recombination are coupled through a virtual intermediate state whose primary role is to single out, or preselect, one Mn ion from the plurality of dopants present in a QD.

For QD-to-Mn excitation transfer, illustrated in Fig. 5a, preselection occurs when a spin-up 3d electron from one of the Mn ions is transferred to the QD to fill the VB hole. This ion then becomes the one that participates in the coupled exchange event, in which the vacancy in its 3 d shell is filled by a spin-down electron from the QD CB. As a result, the effective degeneracy of both the participating excitonic and Mn states is unity.

Strong support for the preselection principle comes from the analysis of PL dynamics in Mn-doped ‘resonant’ QDs, for which EX ≈ EMn16,24. As noted earlier, because the Mn spin-flip transition possesses an almost zero dipole moment, the QD population (N) decays primarily through its excitonic component, such that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{{dN}}{{dt}}=\frac{N{p}_{{{\rm{X}}}}}{{\tau }_{{{\rm{X}}}}}=\frac{N}{{\tau }_{{{\rm{X}}}-{{\rm{Mn}}}}},$$\end{document}dNdt=NpXτX=NτX−Mn, where τX–Mn is the lifetime of the coupled exciton-Mn system, related to the intrinsic exciton lifetime in undoped QDs (τX) through τX-Mn = τX/pX (ref. 16).

Based on measurements of samples with widely varied doping levels (nMn,int ≈ 4 to 13 Mn ions per dot, on average)16,24, the PL lifetime in Mn-doped QDs was consistently close to twice the intrinsic excitonic lifetime (τX-Mn ≈ 2τX), independent of nMn,int. This implies pX ≈ 0.5, again independent of the number of Mn ions per dot. These observations further highlight the unusual nonthermal nature of the exciton–Mn system, in which ultrafast SE interactions establish a peculiar quasi-steady state where, at any given time, the QD exciton effectively interacts with only a single Mn ion, while the others remain inactive.

Based on the above considerations, under downhill conditions (EX > EMn), the occupation probabilities follow pMn = pX exp(ΔΕX,Mn/δ0), independent of the number of Mn dopants. For our small-QD sample, where ΔΕX,Mn = 116 meV, this yields pMn/pX ≈ 3.1, corresponding to ~76% Mn-localized and ~24% QD-localized excitations. These values are in excellent agreement with the experimental results of a recent TA study of photoexcitation branching between excitonic and Mn-based states24. In particular, measurements of similarly sized downhill QDs with ΔΕX,Mn = 130 meV indicated pMn/pX ≈ 3, consistent with the above estimate.

In the uphill regime (EX < EMn), QD→Mn excitation transfer likely proceeds via higher-energy excitonic states (X*) such that EX* ≳ EMn. Under high, multiexcitonic pump conditions, these states can be populated through progressive state filling, as demonstrated previously24. In the single-exciton regime, they can be accessed either by direct excitation with higher-energy photons or via thermal excitation from band-edge states. Both pathways are discussed later in this work.

Spin-exchange–mediated charge transfer

One may expect that a similar SE mechanism underlies Mn-mediated charge transfer in QD–MV²⁺ complexes. In particular, this mechanism can account for both the substantial acceleration of the transfer rate and its reduced sensitivity to the energetic alignment within the donor–acceptor pair. Indeed, SE processes are driven not by direct Coulomb interactions but by the much stronger exchange Coulomb interaction26,49, leading to ultrafast, sub-picosecond characteristic time constants16,22,24. Moreover, SE-mediated transfer does not proceed via the motion of an individual charge governed strictly by the energy difference between its initial and final states; instead, it involves the correlated pairwise motion of two electrons, which is subject to fewer energetic constraints, as discussed below.

In Fig. 5, we illustrate SE-mediated charge transfer using energy-transfer (panel c) and spin-transfer (panel d) representations. Within the SE framework, the process begins with SE-mediated transfer of a photogenerated exciton from the QD to a Mn ion (Fig. 5d, left), followed by relaxation of the excited Mn state (Mn*) through SE-driven spatial separation of the electron and hole between the molecular acceptor and the QD. In this step, a spin-down electron from the Mn ion’s 3d shell is transferred to MV²⁺, reducing it to MV¹⁺, while a spin-up electron from the QD VB is simultaneously transferred to the Mn ion. This spin-conserving process restores the Mn ion to its ground-state ⁶A₁ configuration and leaves behind a VB hole in the QD (Fig. 5d, middle and right).

The interfacial SE coupling between MV²⁺ and the QD is facilitated by the direct, contact-type attachment of the acceptor molecule to the QD surface, corresponding to a face-on adsorption geometry35,36. This configuration promotes partial hybridization between the molecular LUMO and QD orbitals, enabling SE coupling via the exchange Coulomb interaction.

In addition to conserving spin, SE-driven charge transfer must also satisfy energy conservation, as illustrated in Fig. 5c. In the T = 0 limit, this requires that the energy of the initial electronic state (Ei) of the coupled QD–Mn–MV system must be equal to or greater than that of the final state (Ef): Ei ≥ Ef. When Ei > Ef, the excess energy (Ei − Ef) is expected to be dissipated as lattice vibrations (phonons) in the QD and/or as vibrational excitations of the donor molecule, analogous to conventional charge-transfer processes.

Considering the system depicted in Fig. 5c,d, the condition Ei ≥ Ef implies that the energy of the Mn spin-flip transition must be equal to or greater than the energy difference between the LUMO of the acceptor molecule and the VB edge of the QD (EVB): EMn ≥ ELUMO − EVB (Fig. 5c and 5d, right). It is instructive to compare this criterion with that for conventional QD-driven electron transfer, for which the requirement is ECB = EVB + Eg ≥ ELUMO, or equivalently Eg ≥ ELUMO − EVB. Comparing the criteria for SE-mediated and conventional electron transfer, we see that when EMn > Eg, the Mn-mediated process provides a larger effective energetic driving force than ordinary transfer, thereby enabling reactions that are energetically inaccessible in undoped QDs.

A critical prerequisite for such Mn-mediated transfer is the ultrafast rate of SE interactions (on the ~100 fs timescale), which allows capture of a hot exciton before relaxation to the band edge. Consequently, SE-mediated processes can, in principle, efficiently harvest hot-carrier energy and channel it into driving redox reactions, as further discussed in the next section in connection with measurements of the Mn-doped large QD sample.

A further important feature of this mechanism is that it effectively redefines the ‘reduction power’ of QDs, making it less dependent on the absolute energy of the QD CB edge. In conventional QD-mediated reactions, wide-gap materials are typically required since ECB = EVB + Eg, which limits material choice and necessitates the use of high-energy photons. These limitations can be overcome in the SE-driven hot-electron regime. In this case, the QD reduction power is determined not by the CB edge itself, but by an effective reduction energy defined as Eeff = EVB + EMn (Fig. 5c), which is independent of the bandgap. This opens the possibility of using narrow-gap QDs with superior light-harvesting capabilities as efficient drivers of photoreduction reactions. It also provides an additional route to enhance reduction power by increasing EVB. Another important advantage of this approach is improved photostability, since one of the primary degradation pathways of QDs is photocorrosion driven by oxidation via photoexcited holes. Raising EVB reduces the oxidative reactivity of VB holes.

Spin-exchange mechanism and observed charge-transfer dynamics

We begin our analysis with TA measurements of the resonant samples (Figs. 3a and 4a). As discussed earlier, in CdSe QDs for which the exciton energy is close to or slightly higher than the Mn spin-flip transition energy, a photoexcited electron–hole pair rapidly oscillates between the QD- and Mn-based states (Fig. 5b), with occupation probabilities (pX and pMn) governed by the energy detuning ΔΕX,Mn16,24. Since Mn incorporation leads to a dramatic acceleration of transfer dynamics, we conclude that this process occurs predominantly through the Mn component of the QD–Mn mixed state. Although the apparent (experimental) transfer time (inferred from τ1S ≈ 1.2 ps) is quite short, it remains longer than the characteristic QD–Mn excitation-exchange time (~100–200 fs; refs. 16,22). This suggests that, in the resonant case, pₓ ≈ pMn remains valid even in the presence of rapid electron transfer.

Under these conditions, QD depopulation via SE-mediated electron transfer can be expressed as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{{dN}}{{dt}}=\frac{N{p}_{{{\rm{Mn}}}}}{{\tau }_{{\rm{{SE}}{-}{ET}}}}=\frac{N}{2{\tau }_{{\rm{{SE}}{-}{ET}}}},$$\end{document}dNdt=NpMnτSE−ET=N2τSE−ET, where τSE–ET is the intrinsic time constant for Mn* relaxation via SE-assisted coupling to the acceptor molecule and the QD VB. From this relation, the experimentally observed 1S bleach relaxation time is expected to be twice the intrinsic SE-mediated transfer time (τ1S = 2τSE–ET), yielding τSE–ET ≈ 600 fs.

The sub-picosecond timescale of SE-mediated charge transfer further suggests that the dominant contribution arises from internally incorporated Mn ions, which exhibit stronger exchange coupling to QD excitations. In contrast, surface-incorporated Mn ions are expected to contribute more weakly, with characteristic transfer times on the order of a few picoseconds22,25.

The SE mechanism also helps explain the shortening of the apparent electron-transfer time constant for the small QD sample, in which EX exceeds EMn by 116 meV. As discussed earlier, under these conditions the excitation resides predominantly on the Mn ions (pMn ≈ 76%), which reduces the apparent τ1S and brings it closer to the intrinsic τSE-ET.

Using the energy diagram in Fig. 5c, the 1S depopulation rate k1S (=1/τ1S) can be estimated as the product of the attempt rate for the excitation transfer from the QD to the Mn state (kF) and the probability of generating the charge-separated state (qSE-ET) via Mn* spin-flip relaxation: k1S ≈ kFqSE-ET. In the presence of recirculation due to Mn–QD back transfer (rate kB), this probability is qSE-ET ≈ kSE-ET/(kSE-ET + kF + kB), yielding k1S ≈ kF kSE-ET/(kSE-ET + kF + kB).

Because SE-driven QD–Mn excitation exchange is expected to be faster than interfacial SE-mediated charge transfer (kF, kB ≫ kSE-ET), this expression simplifies to k1S ≈ kFkSE-ET/(kF + kB) = kSE-ET/(1 + kB/kF). Under resonant conditions, kB = kF, giving k1S ≈ kSE-ET/2, or equivalently τ1S = 2τSE-ET, consistent with the result obtained above for the resonant sample.

In the small-QD case, using the earlier estimate kB/kF = pX/pMn ≈ 0.3, we obtain k1S ≈ kSE-ET/1.3, or τ1S = 1.3τSE. With τSE ≈ 600 fs (from the resonant sample), this yields τ1S ≈ 780 fs, in excellent agreement with the experimentally measured value of ~700 fs for the Mn-doped small QDs.

We emphasize that the increased transfer rate in this regime does not arise from an increased energetic driving force—as would be expected for conventional charge transfer—but rather from the enhanced probability that coupled QD–Mn excitations occupy the Mn site.

The extremely fast rate of SE-mediated processes also helps explain the relatively rapid depopulation of the QD 1Se state in the large QD sample, where electron transfer to MV²⁺ is not energetically favored in the undoped sample. In this case, EX lies below EMn by ~130 meV, causing QD excitations to reside predominantly in the intrinsic exciton state. However, even under these conditions, there remains a nonzero probability of thermal excitation of the band-edge exciton into a higher-energy state (X*) from which SE-mediated transfer to the Mn state, followed by SE-assisted charge separation, become feasible.

Assuming that kF and kB are the fastest rates in the system and krel ≫ kSE-ET, the X*–Mn* sub-system can be treated in the quasi-steady-state limit. Under these conditions, the probability of eventual charge separation following thermal activation is approximately qSE-ET ~ kSE-ET/krel, up to a factor of order unity that depends on the exact values of the rate constants. This yields an order-of-magnitude estimate for the 1S depopulation rate, k1S ≈ kexc qSE-ET ~ kSE-ET (kexc /krel). If kexc /krel is approximated by a Boltzmann factor for thermal access to the higher-energy excitonic state, one obtains the scaling relation k1S ~ kSE-ETexp[–(EMn – EX)/κBT], or equivalently, τ1S ~ τSE-ET exp[(EMn – EX)/κBT], where κB is the Boltzmann constant.

For our large QDs, EMn – EX = 130 meV, which gives an estimated τ1S of roughly 180τ SE-ET, or ~100 ps when using τSE–ET ≈ 600 fs. Although this simple estimate somewhat exceeds the experimentally measured time constant (~50 ps), it correctly captures the order of magnitude of the 1S-state depopulation via Mn-assisted electron transfer, with deviations likely arising from prefactors associated with the detailed kinetics of the coupled X*–Mn* subsystem.

Evidence for SE-driven hot-carrier reduction of MV2+

In addition to thermal-excitation–mediated electron transfer from the QD, Mn ions can also enable a reduction pathway involving a hot, unrelaxed exciton. As shown previously, a Mn ion can efficiently intercept a hot exciton before phonon-assisted cooling occurs16,22. As illustrated in Fig. 6a (processes initiated by the photon indicated by the green wavy arrow), this enables a reduction pathway via SE-driven charge separation.

Based on the scheme in Fig. 6a, the probability of this hot-exciton pathway (qhot) can be estimated from qhot ~ kSE-ET/krel = τrel/τSE-ET, where τrel is the characteristic time of intraband (hot-exciton) relaxation. This approximation is valid under the same conditions as those used earlier for estimating k1S in the uphill regime, namely when QD–Mn excitation exchange is fast and the dominant competition is between hot-exciton cooling and SE-mediated charge separation. Using τSE-ET ≈ 600 fs (this work) and τrel ≈ 250 fs (ref. 45), we obtain qhot ~ 0.4, indicating that ~40% of the photogenerated hot excitons undergo SE-assisted charge separation, leading to reduction of MV²⁺.

However, this estimate should be viewed as approximate since the condition krel ≫ kSE-ET is only marginally satisfied in this case. A more rigorous treatment would need to explicitly account for competition and recycling within the coupled X*–Mn* system. Nonetheless, the present estimate provides a reasonable order-of-magnitude assessment of the efficiency of hot-exciton–mediated charge transfer.

To test the feasibility of the hot-exciton reduction mechanism, we performed additional measurements on the large-QD samples using excitation at different pump photon energies (Epump) tuned across the Mn spin-flip transition: Epump = 2.21 eV, 2.10 eV, and 2.00 eV (Fig. 6b). For this sample, the band-edge exciton energy (EX ≈ 1.97 eV) lies below the Mn spin-flip energy (EMn = 2.1 eV), making transfer of a relaxed band-edge exciton generated with Epump = 2.00 eV energetically unfavorable. In contrast, hot excitons created with Epump = 2.21 eV or 2.10 eV can, in principle, excite Mn ions and thereby initiate MV²⁺ reduction via the SE-mediated pathway.

Figure 6b shows the TA spectra of QD–MV²⁺ complexes for the undoped and Mn-doped large-QD samples recorded 1 ns after excitation. For all excitation energies, the undoped reference sample exhibits no signature of the MV1⁺-related photoinduced absorption near 2 eV. Instead, its TA spectra are dominated by the 1S bleach, indicating that a significant fraction of band-edge electrons remain confined within the QDs even 1 ns after excitation.

In the Mn-doped sample, the 1S bleach is absent for all three pump photon energies, indicating that by 1 ns all electrons have been removed from the QDs, presumably through electron transfer to MV²⁺. Instead of the pronounced QD-related 1S bleach, the TA spectra obtained with Epump = 2.1 eV and 2.21 eV (energies equal to or exceeding EMn) exhibit a photoinduced absorption feature centered around 2 eV—a clear signature of MV1⁺ formation, confirming successful reduction of MV²⁺.

In contrast, the TA spectrum recorded with Epump = 2.00 eV (below EMn but still slightly above EX) shows no measurable MV1⁺ signal. However, the absence of a pronounced QD bleach still indicates electron transfer from the QD via a slow, thermally activated process (Fig. 6a, initiated by the photon indicated by the red wavy arrow). In this regime, the transfer rate is too low to generate a detectable MV¹⁺ population because of competing relaxation processes.

The dominant role of the hot-carrier process in the case of high-photon-energy excitation is supported by the ultrafast rise of the MV1+ absorption feature (τrise ≈ 400 fs) observed under high-photon-energy (2.4 eV) excitation that generates hot excitons (Fig. 6c). For the hot-exciton pathway, the MV1+ signal buildup is governed by the decay of the coupled X*–Mn* subsystem. In the regime of fast QD–Mn excitation exchange, the rise time can be approximated as τrise ~ (kSE-ET + krel)−1 = τSE-ETτrel/(τSE-ET + τrel), reflecting competition between hot-exciton cooling and SE-mediated charge separation. Using the parameters employed earlier to estimate qhot, we obtain τrise ~ 200 fs, which is of the same order of magnitude as the experimentally observed value.

In contrast, for the thermally activated pathway, the buildup of the MV¹⁺ population is expected to follow the depopulation of the QD 1S state and therefore occur on a much slower timescale, on the order of ~50 ps.

These results provide compelling evidence that, in the large-QD samples, MV²⁺ reduction proceeds primarily via a hot-carrier–mediated pathway when energetically accessible (Fig. 6a). As discussed earlier, this route is enabled by the exceptionally fast SE interactions, which allow rapid capture of a hot exciton by the Mn ion, followed by SE-driven charge separation.

Discussion

In this work, we demonstrated that Mn doping in CdSe QDs enables an ultrafast, SE–driven pathway for electron transfer to methyl viologen. Using femtosecond TA spectroscopy, we showed that Mn ions not only dramatically accelerate charge-transfer dynamics but also open a new hot-carrier reaction channel. This behavior arises from a two-step SE process in which a photoexcited exciton rapidly transfers its energy to a Mn ion, followed by Mn* spin-flip relaxation through SE-driven charge separation and reduction of the molecular acceptor.

Our analysis identifies several SE-mediated transfer regimes. In the resonant case (EX ≈ EMn), bidirectional QD–Mn energy exchange leads to exciton–Mn state mixing and rapid electron transfer via the Mn component of the mixed state, with subpicosecond time constants. SE-mediated transfer further accelerates in the regime EX > EMn, where the transfer probability increases due to the greater relative occupancy of Mn-based states.

In the regime EX < EMn, the MV2+ photoreduction is dominated by a hot-exciton pathway, enabled by the ultrafast character of SE interactions, which allows rapid capture of a hot exciton prior to its relaxation to the band edge. In this case, even when a QD is excited below EMn, we detect measurable depopulation of the QD band-edge states arising from thermally assisted transfer to the Mn* state, followed by SE-driven charge separation.

These findings underscore the robustness of the SE-driven mechanism across different energetic regimes and establish SE coupling as a powerful means to mediate and control charge transfer between quantum-confined semiconductors and molecular species. The demonstrated ability to extract and utilize hot carriers on subpicosecond timescales opens new opportunities for photocatalytic applications where overcoming energetic barriers is essential.

These results introduce a new conceptual framework for interfacial redox chemistry in magnetically doped semiconductors—one in which spin exchange, rather than band alignment alone, governs electron-transfer efficiency. The ability of Mn dopants to capture and redirect hot-exciton energy suggests strategies for designing spin-active photocatalysts capable of driving demanding redox reactions, activating inert substrates, and mediating spin-selective photochemical transformations.

Looking ahead, synthetic control over magnetic dopant identity, distribution, and exchange coupling may enable a broader class of light–charge–spin conversion schemes that integrate the principles of photochemistry, magnetism, and nanomaterials design to advance sustainable solar-driven energy conversion and catalysis.

Methods

Materials

Cadmium oxide (CdO, Aldrich, 99.99%), cadmium acetate dihydrate (Cd(ac)2·2H2O, Aldrich, 98%), octadecylphosphonic acid (ODPA, PCI, 99%), trioctylphosphine oxide (TOPO, Aldrich, 99%), trioctylphosphine (TOP, Aldrich, 97%), diphenylphosphine (DPP, Aldrich, 98%), selenium (Se, Alfa Aesar, 200 mesh, 99.999%), sulfur (S, Alfa Aesar, 99.999%), 1-octadecene (ODE, Aldrich, 90%), oleic acid (OA, Aldrich, 90%), oleylamine (OAm, Aldrich, 70%), manganese(II) acetate tetrahydrate (Mn(ac)2⋅4H2O, Strem, 99.999%), tributylphosphine (TBP, Aldrich, 95%), N-methylformamide (NMF, Aldrich, 99%), and sodium 2-mercaptoethanesulfonate (MES, Aldrich, 98%) were used as received.

Synthesis of CdSe QDs

Wurtzite CdSe quantum dots (QDs) were synthesized following a previously reported procedure32. Briefly, to prepare the ‘resonant’ QDs, 60 mg of CdO, 280 mg of octadecylphosphonic acid, and 3 g of TOPO were loaded into a 50 mL three-neck flask and heated to 150 °C under vacuum for 1 h. The temperature was then raised to 320 °C under a nitrogen atmosphere and maintained for 1 h until the solution became optically transparent.

At this point, 1.8 mL of a TOP–DPP solution (9 mL TOP containing 46 μL of DPP) was injected, and the temperature was further increased to 380 °C. Once this temperature was reached, 0.433 mL of a TOP–Se precursor solution (850 mg of Se dissolved in 3.6 g of the TOP–DPP solution) was rapidly injected. The reaction was allowed to proceed for 1 min and was then quenched by cooling the mixture to room temperature. The resulting QDs were stored as a stock solution by adding 10 mL of toluene.

QD size was controlled by varying the amount of DPP used in the reaction32. Specifically, ‘small’ and ‘large’ QD samples were synthesized by using 50 μL and 43 μL of DPP, respectively.

Mn incorporation into preformed CdSe QDs

Mn doping was performed using a post-synthetic incorporation approach32. Mn(ac)₂·4H₂O (11 mg), octadecene (ODE, 5 mL), oleylamine (OAm, 0.433 mL), and oleic acid (OA, 0.2 mL) were added to a three-neck flask and heated to 100 °C under vacuum for 1 h. The mixture was then heated to 300 °C under nitrogen.

Separately, ~8 mg of CdSe QDs were washed with ethanol, dried, and dissolved in 0.667 mL of a TBP–Se solution (170 mg Se dissolved in a mixture of 5 mL TBP and 5 mL ODE, prepared in a glovebox). This QD–Se solution was rapidly injected into the hot Mn-containing solution and allowed to react. The extent of Mn incorporation was controlled by the reaction time32. The reaction was terminated by cooling the mixture to room temperature.

CdS shell overcoating

The cadmium precursor solution was prepared by combining CdO (36.9 mg), OA (735 μL), and ODE (3.4 mL) in a three-neck flask and heating the mixture to 100 °C under vacuum for 1 h. The solution was then heated to 250 °C under nitrogen and maintained for 1 h until it became optically transparent.

A TOP–S precursor was prepared by dissolving sulfur (22.5 mg) in a mixture of TOP (9 mL) and DPP (46 μL), followed by stirring overnight in a glovebox.

Separately, a mixture of ODE (3.5 mL), OAm (0.3 mL), and OA (0.15 mL) was heated to 100 °C under vacuum for 1 h, then cooled to 60 °C under nitrogen. The washed QD solutions were injected into this mixture, and residual solvents were allowed to evaporate.

For Mn-doped samples, the crude product was washed three times with methanol; undoped samples were washed with ethanol. The QDs were then redissolved in 1 mL of toluene and heated to 220 °C.

CdS shell growth was carried out via alternating injections of 0.05 mL of the cadmium precursor solution and 0.05 mL of the TOP–S solution for two cycles. Each cycle consisted of injection of the cadmium precursor followed by a 2.5 min reaction period, then injection of TOP–S and an additional 2.5 min reaction period.

Surface modification of QDs with MES ligands

As-prepared CdSe/CdS QDs were washed three times with ethanol and dispersed in 6 mL of hexane. A 6 mL aliquot of a 50 mM solution of MES in NMF was added to the hexane dispersion, and the biphasic mixture was stirred for 1 h to facilitate ligand exchange.

The NMF phase was collected and divided between two 50 mL centrifuge tubes. Toluene (6 mL) was added to each tube, followed by centrifugation at 6829 × g for 3 min. The supernatant was discarded, and 1 mL of hexane was added to each pellet and centrifuged for 1 min to remove residual toluene. The resulting MES-capped QDs were finally dispersed in an aqueous solution adjusted to pH 7.

One aliquot was combined with an aqueous solution of MV²⁺ to prepare QD–MV²⁺ complexes for charge-transfer studies, while the other aliquot was retained as a reference sample.

Femtosecond transient absorption measurements

For TA measurements, QD solutions were loaded into quartz optical cells with a 1 mm path length. The QD concentration was adjusted to yield an optical density of approximately 0.15 at the band-edge 1S absorption peak. To minimize data distortion arising from photodegradation and/or photocharging, the solution was continuously and vigorously stirred during measurements using a rotating magnet and a small polypropylene-coated stainless-steel stirring rod.

TA spectra and dynamics were acquired using a home-built TA setup based on a regeneratively amplified ytterbium–gadolinium tungstate (Yb: KGW) femtosecond laser (Pharos, Light Conversion), producing 190 fs pulses at 1030 nm (1.20 eV) with a repetition rate of 500 Hz. The fundamental output was split into two arms to generate pump and probe pulses.

The pump beam was directed either into a harmonic generator (HIRO, Light Conversion) to produce second-harmonic pulses at 515 nm (2.40 eV) or into an optical parametric amplifier (OPA; LYRA, Light Conversion) to generate tunable pulses at 560, 590, or 620 nm (2.21, 2.10, and 2.00 eV, respectively). Owing to nonlinear optical processes, the durations of both the second-harmonic pulses and the OPA-generated pulses were shortened to approximately 150 fs.

The pump beam was mechanically modulated to select every other pulse from the pulse train and was then focused onto the sample to a spot diameter of approximately 120 µm.

The probe arm was directed through an optical delay line providing a temporal delay adjustable from 0 to 5 ns. The delayed 1030 nm pulses were then focused onto a 10 mm thick yttrium aluminum garnet (YAG) plate to generate a white-light supercontinuum. The resulting probe pulses were focused to a spot diameter of approximately 90 µm and spatially overlapped with the pump beam at the sample position. The transmitted white-light probe pulses were collected and analyzed using an Avantes AvaSpec-Fast spectrometer (ULS1350F-USB2), synchronized with the mechanical chopper in the pump arm.

Time- and spectrally resolved pump-induced changes in the absorption coefficient (Δα) were obtained by subtracting the transmitted probe-only signal from the corresponding pump–probe signal. All measurements were corrected for probe-pulse chirp—that is, the wavelength-dependent variation in probe arrival times at the sample—using a previously reported protocol16.
