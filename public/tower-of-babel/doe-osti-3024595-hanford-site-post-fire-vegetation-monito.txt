 1.0 INTRODUCTION

The Hanford Site and the surrounding greater Columbia Basin is comprised of an expanse of
shrub-steppe habitats that provides exceptional ecological value to plants and animals. The
U.S. Department of Energy, Hanford Field Office (HFO)-managed portion of the Hanford Site,
referred to from herein as Central Hanford, has experienced repeated wildfires resulting in
profound, often adverse, effects on the landscape. This report summarizes these fires and fire
monitoring efforts that occurred in calendar year (CY) 2025 and analyzes the post-fire vegetation
recovery in restored areas to inform future post-fire response actions.

1.1 BACKGROUND

The Hanford Site lies in the center of the Pasco Basin, a large structural and topographic basin in
the Columbia Plateau. While HFO manages the central portion of the Hanford Site (Figure 1-1),
other portions are managed by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) and the Washington
Department of Fish and Wildlife and comprise the Hanford Reach National Monument and
recreation access areas, respectively. Elevations throughout Central Hanford range from high
areas on Umtanum Ridge (550 m) and Gable Mountain (330 m) to low areas along the Columbia
River (110 m); most of Central Hanford lies between 150 to 230 m.
The Hanford Site is located within the Columbia Plateau ecoregion, an area that historically
included over 60,000 km2 of steppe and shrub-steppe vegetation across most of central and
southeastern Washington State (PNW-GTR-008, Natural Vegetation of Oregon and
Washington), as well as portions of north central Oregon. The Hanford Site occupies about
1,518 km2 and represents one of the largest tracts of native shrub-steppe habitats remaining in
Washington State, although large areas are degraded from natural conditions. As surrounding
unprotected habitat becomes increasingly converted or degraded, the importance of Central
Hanford for regional conservation continues to increase.
A wide variety of shrub-steppe habitat types and associated plant communities are found at
Central Hanford, ranging from talus slopes, active and stabilized sand dunes, and basalt outcrops
to vast expanses of sagebrush/bunchgrass communities. The dominant ecological systems within
Central Hanford (Figure 1-2) are Inter-Mountain Basins Active and Stabilized Dunes, and
Inter-Mountain Basins Big Sagebrush Steppe, dominated by Wyoming big basin sagebrush
(Artemisia tridentata ssp. wyomingensis) (HNF-61417, Upland Vegetation of the Central
Hanford Site; NHR 2015-04, Ecological Systems of Washington State. A Guide to
Identification). In addition to shrub-steppe habitats, the Hanford Site also includes valuable
swale, riparian, wetland, and aquatic resources. The Hanford Reach, a free-flowing stretch of the
Columbia River, bisects the Hanford Site and provides the north and east boundaries of the
Central Hanford post-fire study area. Large areas have been converted to invasive annual
grassland, known as the Inter-Mountain West Annual Grassland ecological system (HNF-61417;
“Living with exotic annual grasses in the sagebrush ecosystem,” Davies et al. 2021) in
recognition of its duration as a stable ecological state and its distinction from other invasive
annual grassland habitats.

The Hanford Site’s biological resources are characteristic of the Columbia Plateau ecoregion, but
the site is unique in that it is located within the driest and hottest portion of the ecoregion
(PNW-GTR-008). These climatic conditions result in somewhat distinct species assemblages
relative to the rest of the ecoregion. These same conditions also result in Hanford shrub-steppe
communities being less resilient to disturbance, such as wildfire, making restoration and
rehabilitation after large-scale disturbance more difficult, and more importantly, than in areas
that are cooler and receive more precipitation. The site is also home to a significant number of
plants and animals listed for conservation at the state and federal levels, due to the unique
climate and geography and the loss of habitat throughout the region. The interactions between
wildfire and rare plant species are largely unknown.
The extent of high-quality sage-steppe communities is declining, both at Hanford and throughout
the Columbia Basin (JFSP 08-1-5-20, Vegetation Impacts of Recurring Fires on Sagebrush
Ecosystems in Washington: Implications for Conservation and Rehabilitation). Around half of
all sagebrush in North America has been lost since the 1800’s and a further 1.3 million acres are
lost each year, roughly four times the area of Central Hanford (Open-File Report 2022-1081, “A
sagebrush conservation design to proactively restore America’s sagebrush biome”). This trend is
mirrored at the state level with over half of all sage-steppe habitat lost in Washington State
(Shrubsteppe Mapping of Eastern Washington Using Landsat Satellite Thematic Mapper Data,
Jacobsen and Snyder 2000; The Shrub-Steppe Ecosystem of Washington: A Brief Summary of
Knowledge and Nongame Wildlife Conservation Needs, Dobler 1992). Where habitat remains, it
is often highly degraded (NHR 2015-04); an estimated 98 percent of remaining sage-steppe
habitat has been degraded by grazing (Biological Report 28, Endangered Ecosystems of the
United States: A Preliminary Assessment of Loss and Degradation). The primary contributor to
this decline, apart from land-use change, is the widespread invasion of non-native annual grasses,
such as cheatgrass (Bromus tectorum), and subsequent alteration of natural fire regimes (General
Technical Report INT-276, Changing Fire Frequencies on Idaho’s Snake River Plains:
Ecological and Management Implications; Cheatgrass invasion: History, causes, consequences,
and solutions, Molvar et al. 2024). Wildfire and invasive species have been identified as urgent
and severe threats to the ecological integrity at Central Hanford (DOE/RL-96-32, Hanford Site
Biological Resources Management Plan). The effects of fire and invasive species have already
been harmful to ecological integrity at Central Hanford and will continue to do so if left
unmanaged.
High cheatgrass cover increases fuel availability and continuity across the landscape, resulting in
larger, more intense, and more frequent fires (Molvar et al. 2024). Repeat disturbance from
cheatgrass fueled wildfires often results in reduction or loss of fire-sensitive species, habitat
degradation, and ultimately, long-lasting conversion to the Inter-Mountain West Annual
Grassland ecological system (“Trajectories of change in sagebrush steppe vegetation
communities in relation to multiple wildfires,” Davies et al. 2012; Davies et al. 2021). In less
than 100 years after being introduced to the region (PNNL-13688, Vascular Plants of the
Hanford Site), cheatgrass has become a ubiquitous invasive species with ecosystem-altering
consequences.
The response of sage-steppe habitats to wildfire can vary greatly, but in general, sage-steppe
habitats have decreasing resiliency or the ability to recover naturally to wildfire with increasing
aridity (“Trends, Impacts, and Cost of Catastrophic and Frequent Wildfires in the Sagebrush
Biome,” Crist et al. 2023). The arid conditions at Central Hanford make it particularly vulnerable

 1-4

to wildfire. Some areas at Central Hanford and the adjacent Arid Lands Ecology (ALE) Reserve
have shown resilience to wildfire, while other areas have not (Davies et al. 2012). The resiliency
of a habitat is primarily determined by soil and the pre-fire vegetation community (Davies et al.
2012). The low-elevation vegetation communities predominant at Central Hanford have low
resiliency to fires outside of their natural fire regime (Davies et al. 2012). Without resiliency to
disturbance, these habitats undergo state changes when burned, in which vegetation communities
experience substantial and lasting changes to their original composition (Davies et al. 2012;
Davies et al. 2021). Sagebrush habitats with cheatgrass as an understory component are more
likely to undergo state changes after fire (“Sagebrush steppe wildlife: historical and current
perspectives,” Sands et al. 2000; Davies et al. 2012).
As of CY 2017, areas containing cheatgrass as a major component of the understory encompass
approximately 65 percent of the Hanford Site (HNF-61417). As of CY 2025, a study of
vegetation composition across Central Hanford found cheatgrass cover above 17.5 percent at
97% of sites, and cheatgrass cover over 62.5 percent at 75% of sites (HNF-72996, Central
Hanford Ecological Integrity Assessments: Final Report). High cheatgrass cover places
remaining shrub dominated areas at high risk of loss. State changes from fire occur in a fairly
predictable pattern in Wyoming big sagebrush steppe because native vegetation communities
deteriorate into progressively less ecologically valuable states (Davies et al. 2012; Davies et al.
2021). The predictability and relative permanence of state changes have significant implications
for conservation and land management.
Throughout this report, the terms “native,” “non-native,” “invasive,” “exotic,” and “noxious
weed” are used to describe plant species:
 • Native species are generally considered to be those that evolved in a particular region
 without substantial influence from human activity.
 • Non-native species are those that were relatively recently introduced to a particular
 region outside of their native range, often directly or indirectly as a result of human
 activity. Non-native is a catch all term that includes invasive, exotic, and noxious species.
 • Invasive refers to non-native species with potential to grow aggressively, causing
 ecological or economic harm in their introduced range.
 • Exotic species are non-native species with little to no potential to cause harm in their
 introduced range.
 • Noxious weed is a legal designation given to some non-native species with requirements
 for control at the state level by the Washington State Noxious Weed Control Board
          (NWCB 2021).
The native, invasive, or exotic statuses are provided by the Washington Natural Heritage
Program (WNHP; WA Invasive Ranking System, WNHP 2025). The taxonomy used in this report
follows plant names found in Flora of the Pacific Northwest: An Illustrated Manual
(Hitchcock 2018), unless updated taxonomy is available from another source.

1.2 PURPOSE AND OBJECTIVE

The purpose of post-fire monitoring is to inform management decisions addressing the harm
caused by wildfire and invasive species. Routine surveillance of recently burned areas can

 1-5

identify noxious weed infestation while they are still easily treatable. Post-fire monitoring is also
used to predict vegetation recovery trajectories, allowing efficient use of limited resources for
restoration. Monitoring natural vegetation recovery and restoration efforts helps refine
knowledge of fire ecology at Central Hanford to plan and execute future post-fire restoration
efforts.
The first iteration of this monitoring effort occurred in CY 2021, where both recently burned and
historically burned areas were analyzed to evaluate vegetation recovery. Monitoring in CY 2025
focused on tracking vegetation recovery in the 2020 Gable Mountain burn area; information on
fires occurring in CY 2025 that were not monitored is also included. Previous post-fire reports
are listed below:
 • HNF-67070, Hanford Site Post-Fire Vegetation Monitoring Report for Calendar
 Year 2021
 • HNF-69774, Hanford Site Post-Fire Vegetation Monitoring Report for Calendar
 Year 2022
 • HNF-69972, Hanford Site Post-Fire Vegetation Monitoring Report for Calendar
 Year 2023
 • HNF-71720, Hanford Site Post-Fire Vegetation Monitoring Report for Calendar
 Year 2024.
The objective of this report is to address the interconnected threats of wildfire and invasive
species, a critical task for maintaining the ecological value of the Hanford Site and fulfilling the
goal of fostering preservation of important biological resources (DOE/RL-96-32). In many cases,
active management of burned areas is necessary to avoid long-lasting harm to biological
resources. Continued monitoring of burned areas is critical for informing management decisions
(Section 3.0).
Wildfire has driven significant changes in vegetation cover throughout the Hanford Site
(HNF-61417). Historically, Wyoming big sagebrush steppe, the dominant cover type at Central
Hanford, experiences fire approximately every 100 to 240 years (Fire and Restoration of
Sagebrush Ecosystems;” Baker 2010). The majority of Central Hanford has burned at least once
in the last 40 years. Some areas have burned up to five times or, in rare instances, up to seven
times, as of CY 2025 (Figure 1-3). In CY 2025 there were nine fires, totaling 589 acres. Invasive
species, particularly cheatgrass, are the primary cause of changing fire regimes.

This drastic departure from natural fire regimes has driven wide-scale vegetation changes,
eliminating fire-sensitive species from areas where they were once abundant. Three of the
dominant shrubs at Central Hanford, Wyoming big sagebrush, bitter antelope brush (Purshia
tridentata), and spiny hopsage (Grayia spinosa), are fire-sensitive with limited capacity to
re-establish after wildfires. Re-establishment of these shrub species after fire requires nearby
seed sources because they are poor seed dispersers. Big sagebrush seed dispersal has a range of
between 1 m to 15 m (“Survivability of Wyoming Big Sagebrush Transplants,” Clements and
Harmon 2019; Plant Materials Technical Note No. MT-68, Big Sagebrush Establishment).
Repeated fires, development, and historic disturbances have led to an increasingly patchy
distribution of shrubs at Central Hanford, and increasingly burned areas have insufficient
connection with a seed source for shrubs to re-establish naturally, causing them to convert to
cheatgrass-dominated Inter-Mountain West Annual Grassland.
Inter-Mountain West Annual Grassland provides little ecological value compared to native plant
communities. Plant diversity decreases drastically with increasing invasive annual grass
dominance and large swathes of land become homogenized by cheatgrass. Plants associated with
the Inter-Mountain West Annual Grassland ecological system are typically non-native or native
increasers, and these usually have relatively low cover. Certain noxious weeds are some of the
only plants able to achieve high cover within the Inter-Mountain West Annual Grassland
ecological systems (HNF-70120, Central Hanford Ecological Integrity Assessments Monitoring
Final Report). The value of Inter-Mountain West Annual Grassland ecological systems for
pollinators, birds, mammals, and other sagebrush-adapted species is minimal. The ecosystem
services provided by the Inter-Mountain West Annual Grassland are reduced compared to native
ecosystems. Healthy sage-steppe habitats act as efficient carbon sinks, but invasive annual
grasslands act as carbon sources (“Is Climate Change Mitigation the Best Use of Desert
Shrublands?” Meyer 2011). The increased dust caused by more frequent fires adversely impacts
air quality and alters water cycling (Meyer 2011). Cheatgrass is inversely correlated with
cryptobiotic crust (“Biotic soil crusts in relation to topography, cheatgrass and fire in the
Columbia Basin, Washington;” Ponzetti et al. 2007), and healthy cryptobiotic crust is thought to
reduce the frequency and intensity of fires (“Could Biological Soil Crusts Act as Natural Fire
Fuel Breaks in the Sagebrush Steppe?” Condon et al. 2023). Cheatgrass invasion poses a serious
threat to rare plant populations (DOE/RL-2021-35, Central Hanford Rare Plant Management
Plan). Reflecting their low-habitat value, the Inter-Mountain West Annual Grassland ecological
system or invasive annual grasslands are classified as marginal habitats (i.e., Level 1) in
DOE/RL-96-32.
Neglecting burned areas can lead to further adverse outcomes and harm to biological resources
when native species do not recover well. Disturbance opportunities created by fire allow noxious
weeds and other undesirable invasive plant species to rapidly increase in abundance and area of
infestation. Noxious weeds and invasive plants disrupt both natural recovery and restoration
efforts. Noxious weed treatments amplify costs of restoration efforts and reduce their
effectiveness. After becoming established in burned areas, seed production may be greatly
increased, raising the potential for these species to spread outside of burned areas. The
undesirable tumbleweed species, Russian thistle (Salsola kali) and tall tumblemustard
(Sisymbrium altissimum), are early successional species after a fire, greatly increasing in
abundance in the first 2 to 3 years. Both problematic species are significant contributors to
habitat degradation across the Hanford Site (HNF-70120), increase future fire risk and
potentially spread soil contaminants. Routine monitoring and rapidly responding with restoration

 1-8

actions to establish native plants is the most effective way to prevent undesirable species from
colonizing burned areas.

1.3 GOALS

The goals of this monitoring report are to:
 • Evaluate the extent and impact of recent fires on Central Hanford.
 • Provide fifth-year data on the Gable Mountain post-fire restoration effort.
 • Provide monitoring results tracking recovery at recent fires.
 • Recommend actions to improve post-fire vegetation management.
This monitoring report is the fifth in a multi-year effort to analyze short and long-term vegetation
recovery after wildfire at Central Hanford. The results of this effort will help inform future
post-fire management activities and provide crucial data on the natural recovery of Hanford Site
habitats after a fire.

1.4 REGULATORY DRIVERS

This section discusses federal laws and Hanford Site guidance as it relates to post-burn
monitoring and management.

1.4.1 Federal Laws and Policy

HFO conducts ecological monitoring at the Hanford Site to collect and track data to ensure
compliance via an array of laws and policies. Ecological monitoring data provides baseline
information about the plants, animals, and habitats under HFO stewardship for decision making
under the National Environmental Policy Act of 1969 and the Comprehensive Environmental
Response, Compensation, and Liability Act of 1980.
The Guidance for Implementation of Federal Wildland Fire Management Policy (DOI 2009;
FWFP) requires assessment of burned areas to determine appropriate rehabilitation and
restoration activities and requires those activities to be evaluated to assess their effectiveness.
Additionally, the FWFP emphasizes prevention, including mitigating risks and losses to
ecosystems. Though the FWFP applies to federal agencies governed by the U.S. Department of
the Interior, aligning Hanford Site strategies with the requirements set forward within the FWFP
will better align the Hanford Site’s fire management strategy with that of neighboring USFWS
land. This monitoring effort assesses burned areas and evaluates the effectiveness of restoration
activities, aligning Hanford Site environmental stewardship with the FWFP.

Comprehensive Land-Use Plan Environmental Impact Statement, as the primary implementation
control for managing and protecting natural resources on the Hanford Site. According to
Section 1.4.1 of DOE/EIS-0222-SA-01, DOE/RL-96-32 provides a mechanism for ensuring
compliance with laws protecting biological resources; provides a framework for ensuring that
appropriate biological resource goals, objectives, and tools are in place to make HFO an effective

 2.0 GABLE MOUNTAIN FIRE
This section provides the background and methods used to monitor effects of the Gable
Mountain fire. Results are provided for each transect that was monitored in CY 2025.
2.1 BACKGROUND
On May 30, 2020, a 5,510-acre (ac) fire, presumed to be caused by lightning, occurred on Gable
Mountain and the surrounding area (Figure 2-1). The fire caused considerable damage, removing
most of the mature sagebrush overstory on large areas of land and leaving little surviving
vegetation. The fire affected biologically valuable shrub-steppe habitat and culturally significant
areas, resulting in harm to biological and cultural resources. Based on the ecological and cultural
value of the habitat, the decision was made to restore the burned area with native seed mix.
The pre-fire vegetation cover types were evaluated to develop appropriate seed mixes for the
burned area. Historic vegetation mapping data, field surveys, and photo points were used to
determine what plant communities were present in the burned area before the fire. The
vegetation mapping data was gathered from HNF-61417, which used both field surveys and
aerial photography to determine the primary grass and shrub components of habitats throughout
Central Hanford. To confirm the vegetation data and to evaluate the surviving vegetative
community, post-fire vegetation monitoring occurred in June 2020 after the burned area was safe
to enter. In these field surveys, surveyors walked transects throughout the burned area, noted
surviving plant species, and distinguished which sections of the burned area were severely
impacted by the fire. In October 2020, another survey was conducted that established transects
for vegetation monitoring. The details of this survey are described in Section 2.3. In addition to
the data analysis and post-fire monitoring, historic photo points were revisited during the
June 2020 monitoring effort to obtain additional information on how the vegetative community
had changed post-fire. Photo points showed dramatic changes in the landscape (Figure 2-2).

Based on vegetation composition data in the burned area, three seed mixes were developed to
restore the area. These seed mixes included the dominant native plant species in the pre-fire
community, when possible. One seed mix targeted the areas surrounding Gable Mountain with
high-density sagebrush cover pre-fire, one targeted the areas surrounding Gable Mountain with

low-density or no sagebrush cover pre-fire, and one mix targeted the higher elevation areas that
were burned on Gable Mountain. The boundaries of each seed mix are shown in Figure 2-3.
Seed mixes were made up of locally collected or native-grown grasses, and the high-density
sagebrush seed mix included sagebrush seed to restore the shrub layer in that area. Seed mix
rates were initially calculated by doubling broadcast seeding rates in DOE/RL-2011-116,
Hanford Site Revegetation Manual, to accommodate aerial seeding methods. The actual pure live
seed rate per acre was heavily determined by regional seed availability, which resulted in decreases
in key species like needle-and-thread grass and increases in available species like sand dropseed
(Sporobolus cryptandrus) and prairie junegrass (Koeleria macrantha). The seed mixes were
broadcast via helicopter over the entire burned area in January 2021, 7 months after the Gable
Mountain fire had occurred.
The original restoration plan recommended 5 years of monitoring; however, continued
monitoring would add a valuable long-term perspective to this important dataset. The methods
used to evaluate post-fire recovery are described in Section 2.2. The fifth year of this monitoring
was conducted in CY 2025 and is summarized in Section 2.3.

2.2 METHODS

A short-term monitoring strategy was implemented for the Gable Mountain fire, intended for use
in areas burned within the last 5 years. The goal of short-term monitoring is to collect data on
vegetation recovery trajectories post-fire and to monitor effectiveness of restoration actions.
To track vegetation recovery after the fire, 15 transects were established throughout the Gable
Mountain fire area in CY 2020 (Figure 2-4). These transects were monitored annually for 5 years
to track vegetation diversity and abundance. The transect locations were selected to represent
habitat differences within the Gable Mountain fire area that were identified using HNF-61417,
along with historic photo points and results from a post-fire pedestrian survey. The transect
locations represent areas that experienced different levels of fire intensity. No transects were
established on the north slope of Gable Mountain. Transects were recorded on a Global
Positioning System (GPS) and rebar with flagging tape that were placed at the start and end of
each transect. Coordinates were recorded at the start and end points of each 100-m transect. A
photo point was established at the starting point of each monitoring transect. Pictures were taken
using the Hanford photo-point protocol (Appendix A).
In addition to the 15 transects established within the burned area, five additional reference
transects were established. The goal of establishing reference transects was to evaluate
vegetative cover in similar neighboring plant communities that were not affected by the Gable
Mountain fire. The unburned plant populations serve as a basis for comparison as the vegetation
in the burned area grows and the community composition develops. Monitoring transects were
chosen based on proximity to the burned area, vegetation layer similarity to the burned area
according to HNF-61417, and accessibility. The transects were established and monitored using
the same methods as the original 15 transects and will be monitored concurrently with the burn
area transects reference area (RA) transects are displayed in Figure 2-4 with “RA.”
Plot frame data were taken along each of the transects in late April 2025. These data were
collected following established methods relevant to local vegetation communities (“Steppe
Vegetation of Washington,” Daubenmire 1970; Recovery and Rehabilitation of Vegetation on the
Fitzner-Eberhardt Arid Lands Ecology Reserve, Hanford Reach National Monument, Following
the 24 Command Fire: Final Report 2001-2004, Evans and Lih 2005). The plot frame transects
consist of a 100-m long measuring tape, stretched from the start point to the end point so that it
was straight and taut. One 20 cm x 50 cm plot was placed every 5 m on the right-hand side of the
transect with the longest side parallel with the tape for a total of 20 plots per transect.

 2-15

 Figure 2-4. Monitoring Transects for the
 Gable Mountain Fire Area Established in Calendar Year 2020.
Upon placing the plot frame, each species within the frame was identified, and estimates of
coverage were made for each species rooted within the area of the frame. Canopy coverage is
defined by Daubenmire (1970) as, “the percentage of ground surface included in the vertical
projection of a polygon drawn about the extremities of the undisturbed foliage of a plant.”
Coverage was estimated using the following coverage classes:
 • 1 (0 to 5 percent cover)
   •   2 (5 to 25 percent)
   •   3 (25 to 50 percent)
   •   4 (50 to 75 percent)
   •   5 (75 to 95 percent)
   •   6 (95 to 100 percent).
In addition to plot frames, all plant species observed within 5 m of either side of the transect
were recorded. Noxious weeds encountered during the survey were recorded and marked with a
GPS point. Noxious weed locations were shared with the Hanford Mission Integration
Solutions, LLC Noxious Weed Program for treatment planning.

2.3.1.1 Gable Mountain (High Elevation) Transects

The Gable Mountain (high elevation) transects are located near the summit of Gable Mountain.
The north slope of Gable Mountain was observed to have less fire damage than the south slope
and appeared to be the only part of the burned area with a significant population of surviving
sagebrush.
The soil types near the summit of Gable Mountain are characterized by lithosols and Kiona silt
loam soil. Prior to burning, the lithosols in the high elevation areas and the north slope of Gable
Mountain supported a healthy shrub-steppe community dominated by shrubs, such as big
sagebrush, spiny hopsage, winterfat (Krascheninnikovia lanata) purple sage (Salvia dorii), rock
buckwheat (Eriogonum sphaerocephalum), slender buckwheat (Eriogonum microthecum),
rubber rabbitbrush (Ericameria nauseosa), and green rabbitbrush (Chrysothamnus viscidiflorus),
and grasses, such as bluebunch wheatgrass (Pseudoroegneria spicata), needle-and-thread grass
(Hesperostipa comata), and Sandberg bluegrass (Poa secunda). Before the fire, bluebunch
wheatgrass was the dominant bunchgrass species on Gable Mountain. The south slope supported
similar vegetation with a pattern of increasing cheatgrass and decreasing sagebrush southward.

2.4 DISCUSSION

In this section, monitoring results are interpreted to identify patterns of recovery and used to
make management recommendations.

2.4.1 Vegetation Recovery Patterns

This section discusses vegetation recovery patterns for tumbleweeds, tumbleweeds, seeded
species, precipitation, and RAs.
Recovery
The immediate post-fire monitoring that occurred after the Gable Mountain fire in October 2020
showed little surviving vegetation throughout the burned area, with the exception of the north
slope of Gable Mountain and scattered patches elsewhere. The Gable Mountain fire area was
seeded with native grasses and sagebrush in January 2021 to foster recovery of native vegetation
and to mitigate ecological damage from the burn. Overall, native and non-native species are
recovering throughout the burned area, including both seeded and unseeded species. Non-native
species, particularly cheatgrass, have reached higher levels of cover than native species;
however, native vegetation appears to be continuing to recover at most transects. There are
distinct differences in recovery between the Gable Mountain (High Elevation), high-density
sagebrush, and low-density sagebrush areas, and the variability within each area. RA transects

 2-56

are compared to burned areas to interpret recovery trends and the influence of annual
precipitation.
Pre-fire vegetation conditions and soil are the primary factors influencing post-fire recovery.
Detailed information of pre-fire vegetation communities for the Gable Mountain area is not
available; however, unburned RA transects and long-term BRMP plot data can be used to infer
pre-fire conditions. Cheatgrass is the dominant non-native species at the RA transects, with an
average cover of 38.3 percent in CY 2025. This suggests that cheatgrass was a dominant species in
the Gable Mountain area and was a major contributing factor to the high mortality of fire-sensitive
species, such as sagebrush. Pre-fire cheatgrass levels in the 24 Command fire DOE/RL-96-32 plots
were also high, averaging 35 percent, resulting in a fire that eliminated the majority of the
fire-sensitive shrubs in that area (HNF-67070). This is consistent with the outcome of fires nearby
at the ALE Reserve (Davies et al. 2012) and throughout sage-steppe habitats elsewhere (General
Technical Report INT-276; Molvar et al. 2024).
Recovery differs between the three distinct areas (high elevation, high-density sagebrush, and
low-density sagebrush areas) that were identified in the Gable Mountain burn. The Gable
Mountain (High Elevation) area exhibits variable recovery, likely due to differences in soil and
pre-fire vegetation composition. Transects in this area with lithosol or sandy soils experienced
lower burn severity and showed higher levels of surviving vegetation immediately post-fire and
greater native vegetation recovery. However, keystone species such as fire-sensitive shrubs and
bluebunch wheatgrass have not recovered. In the high-density sagebrush area, a substantial
amount of native vegetation has recovered, increasing at a steady rate at most transects; however,
sagebrush has not recovered, with only a few juveniles detected during monitoring. In the
low-density sagebrush area, little to no native vegetation has recovered. In this area, native
vegetation has been nearly eliminated. Across all areas non-native vegetation increased rapidly
after the fire, achieving higher cover than native species at most transects. Overall, non-native
cover has decreased over the last 2 years, indicating successional processes are concluding and
the sites are reaching equilibrium communities.
Non-native vegetation cover appears to have peaked several years following the burn and is now
approaching equilibrium at lower levels. Non-native vegetation cover was highest in CY 2023
for the high-density and low-density sagebrush areas and the highest in CY 2024 for the Gable
Mountain (High Elevation) area. Cheatgrass was primarily responsible for driving the high
non-native cover levels in CYs 2023 and 2024. The RAs also showed non-native cover in
CY 2023, indicating this may be partially explained by sitewide trends such as seasonal weather
patterns.
Native vegetation recovery appears to be continuing, but the results indicate that state changes
have occurred and fire-sensitive shrubs are unlikely to recover substantially. Key shrubs and
grasses were removed from the Gable Mountain (high-elevation) area and high-density
sagebrush areas, representing fundamental changes to the ecological systems present and serious
ecological degradation. Sagebrush seed remains viable in the seed bank for 1 to 2 years when
buried or under litter and decreases in viability over time (Open-File Report, 2009-1146,
Investigating Seed Longevity of Big Sagebrush), so there is no longer a seed source to replenish
the seed bank. Areas with greater native vegetation recovery retain higher ecological value, and
may continue to recover further, however the vegetation composition is fundamentally changed
from its pre-fire state. Transect 1 monitors an area that was once classified as an element

 2-57

occurrence, however this area no longer qualifies for that status without the shrub overstory
present, representing a loss to the most valuable category of biological resources at Hanford.

Tumbleweeds

A trend noted throughout the burned area was a dramatic increase and then decline in cover for
tall tumblemustard, an invasive tumbleweed species with important management considerations.
In CY 2022, increasing cover of tall tumblemustard was observed throughout the Gable
Mountain burn area. Tall tumblemustard is considered an early seral stage dominant in many
habitats; however, in the dry Pasco Basin region, where the Hanford Site is located, it has been
observed to reach equilibrium with cheatgrass for up to 30 years (“Herbage Yields in Relation to
Soil Water and Assimilated Nitrogen,” Cline and Rickard 1973).
Tall tumblemustard has important ecological considerations due to its potential to uptake and
spread radioactive contamination. (DOE/EA-1728-F, Environmental Assessment: Integrated
Vegetation Management on the Hanford Site, Richland, Washington). Additionally, dried
tumblemustard plants accumulate in large patches, creating ecological dead zones and increasing
fire risk, driving ecological degradation (HNF-72996). Tall tumblemustard coverage has
declined substantially at all sites since CY 2022, indicating that its dominance was part of an
early seral stage or due to annual variation in precipitation, although it is still present at low-to-
moderate coverage at most transects.
Another undesirable tumbleweed species, Russian thistle, was still in its seedling stage when
monitoring occurred, making accurate cover estimates impossible, but it was observed to be very
widespread and abundant throughout the burned area.
The ecological costs associated with undesirable species, such as tumbleweeds, must be
considered in the cost-benefit analysis of potential restoration efforts.

Seeded Species

This monitoring does not quantify the effect seeding had on recovery in the burn area, but some
observations were noted. In the Gable Mountain (High Elevation) area, two of three seeded
species were detected (Table 2-1). In the high-density sagebrush area, all seven seeded species
were detected (Table 2-2), with prairie Junegrass detected for the first time in CY 2025. In the
low-density sagebrush area, two of five seeded species were detected (Table 2-3). Plants that
sprouted from the natural seedbank could not be discerned from plants sprouted from
supplemental seeding. This shows that the seeded species that were not detected may be less
effective for use in restoration mixes or require additional considerations, such as required
germination conditions.

Precipitation

Figure 2-49 shows cumulative water year data from CY 2020 through 2025 (HMS 2025). Annual
precipitation is an important factor influencing post-fire recovery trajectories and may have been
especially important in CY 2021, immediately after the fire. Below-average precipitation in
CY 2021 may have been the single most important factor reducing success of the seeding effort,
consistent with the findings of other sagebrush restoration studies in arid climates (“Long-term

 2-58

effects of seeding after wildfire on vegetation in Great Basin shrubland ecosystems,” Knutson,
et al. 2014; “Practical postfire sagebrush shrub restoration techniques,” Grant-Hoffman and
Plank 2021). The influence of precipitation lessens as plants continue to become established in
the burned area, and competition for unoccupied space is reduced. Annual precipitation and
successional trends likely explain some of the patterns observed, such as the dramatic increase in
tall tumblemustard in CY 2022, as well as the high diversity of native annuals in CYs 2022
and 2023.

Reference Areas

In summary, pre-fire vegetation and soil condition, annual precipitation, and successional trends
all influence recovery during the initial years after wildfire. Evaluating the relative impacts of
each of these factors was not planned with this study design, however, important patterns can be
identified and used to guide management decisions and future fire response plans. Differences in
pre-fire vegetation and soil condition were likely the greatest factor influencing recovery
trajectories. Much of the variability in recovery can be explained by these differences. It is
recommended that pre-fire conditions are monitored regularly across the site to better forecast
recovery trajectories.
Vegetation cover was relatively stable across years in the RAs. This indicates that the trends
observed in the burned area are a result of post-fire changes and not due to site-wide trends. The

RA transects do show annual variability, which is likely due to differences in seasonal weather
patterns and monitoring variability. Some of the variability in the burn area not explained by
pre-fire conditions is likely due to seasonal weather patterns and monitoring variability as well.

 4.0 MANAGEMENT RECOMMENDATIONS

The results of Gable Mountain post-fire monitoring and other post-burn monitoring provide an
initial dataset to track recovery of restored burned area versus unrestored burned area over
short- and long-term perspectives. Monitoring in the Gable Mountain burn area should be
continued on an annual basis to build this valuable dataset. It is recommended the
DOE/RL-RL-96 plots burned in the 24 Command fire be revisited every 5 years to collect
additional data. These plots are scheduled to be monitored next in CY 2026. Conducting similar
monitoring in a variety of burned areas will broaden the understanding of post-fire recovery
trajectories under different conditions and allow for better prioritization of restoration actions.
The first 5 years of monitoring suggest that the Gable Mountain area is unlikely to recover to
pre-fire conditions. Fire-sensitive plants have been eliminated, and vegetation composition has
been altered dramatically. The habitats present before the burn (Inter-Mountain Basins Big
Sagebrush Steppe, Colombia Plateau Scabland Shrubland, and Columbia Plateau Steppe and
Grassland ecological systems) have shifted to a patchwork of degraded habitats lacking in
diagnostic shrub-steppe species. These state changes represent long-term reductions in habitat
quality, which should be accounted for in the long-term outlook for conservation at the Hanford
Site.
It is recommended that restoration actions are taken within select burned areas on the Hanford
Site to avoid long-term decline in ecological quality. Left unmanaged, more areas of the Hanford
Site will undergo state changes after fire, experiencing long-lasting loss of habitat value.
Developing processes for rapid response to wildfire would be beneficial, because the initial
period before non-native plants become established is likely to have the best chance for
successful restoration. Restoration plans should consider the need for supplemental restoration
actions in case of unfavorable seasonal weather. Continued monitoring of the Gable Mountain
fire area will help restoration practitioners refine best management practices at the Hanford Site.

Management Recommendations Summary

 • Continue vegetation monitoring at Gable Mountain transects, the 24 Command Fire, and
 other select burn areas.
 • Implement additional restoration actions within the Gable Mountain burn area to restore
 diagnostic species and improve habitat quality.
 • Develop a program of routine monitoring for all new burns to detect noxious weeds and
 identify restoration needs.
 • Expand native seed source supplies to avoid shortages for key species and better restore a
 variety of plant communities.
 • Develop a Central Hanford fire response plan, including guidelines for rapidly
 implementing restoration actions when needed.

This appendix discusses the Hanford photo-point protocol used in the calendar year 2025
post-fire monitoring efforts.

A.1 GENERAL

The general photo-point protocol methods used include the following:
 • Used the highest quality camera available (i.e., combination of lens optics, processor, and
 sensor [i.e., pixel count]). Ensured the time stamp was set.
 • Most existing photo points at Central Hanford were established using a Garmin® 1
 GPSMAP® 2 64s unit with WASS and GLONASS enabled; accuracy was approximately
 4 meters.
 • Relocation of photo points was greatly speeded by using a smart phone app (such as
 Avenza® 3 Maps) with preloaded, spatially referenced photo points on aerial imagery or a
 topographic map.
 • Ideally, photo points in shrub steppe habitat would be established/retaken during the
 spring, when the most flowering plants are in bloom and tall bunchgrasses can be
 identified from a distance. Photos taken during the shoulder seasons provide much lower
 quality image and amount of information. Additionally, photo points taken during the
 growing season over multiple years capture the same period of floristic development,
 even if the phenology of the site occurs during different dates between years, providing a
 basis from which comparisons can be made.
