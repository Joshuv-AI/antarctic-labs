Path integral formulation of finite-dimensional quantum mechanics in discrete phase space
Leonardo A. Pachón, Andrés F. Gómez

Abstract
We develop a path integral representation for the dynamics of quantum systems with a finite-dimensional Hilbert space, formulated entirely within a discrete phase space. Starting from the discrete Wigner function defined on ℤ d × ℤ d \mathbb{Z}_{d}\times\mathbb{Z}_{d} (with d d an odd prime), and the associated Weyl transform built from generalized displacement operators, we derive an exact evolution kernel that propagates the discrete Wigner function in time. By exploiting the composition law of the kernel and iterating the short-time approximation, we obtain a sum-over-paths expression for the propagator weighted by a discrete phase-space action that is the natural finite-dimensional counterpart of Marinov’s functional. For Hamiltonians linear in the phase-space coordinates, we show that the fluctuation sum factorizes and, at times strictly commensurate with the lattice (the Clifford-covariant regime), collapses to a deterministic shift realizing the discrete analog of classical Hamiltonian flow. The formulation is applied to a single qutrit ( d = 3 d=3 ) under a diagonal Hamiltonian, and to two interacting qutrits, where we show explicitly that the full entanglement dynamics—captured by a closed-form expression for the linear entropy valid for all times—requires the coherent contribution of all fluctuation sectors of the action. The μ ~ = 0 \tilde{\mu}=0 sector alone is non-real at finite time step and collapses to a trivial (uniform) kernel in the continuum limit, failing to reproduce the entanglement dynamics in either regime. We discuss the relevance of this construction for semiclassical simulation of many-body spin systems and the characterization of non-classicality through Wigner negativity.



I
Introduction

Many physically important quantum systems are naturally described by
finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces. Nuclear and electronic spin degrees of
freedom, atomic-level schemes in quantum optics, and—most prominently—qubits
and qudits in quantum information processing all belong to this category.
Despite the central role these systems play in modern physics, their
phase-space formulation has received comparatively less attention than the
well-established Wigner–Weyl–Moyal framework for continuous-variable
systems
Wigner 1932
;
Weyl 1927
;
Moyal 1949
;
Groenewold 1946
;
Hillery et al. 1984
.

For continuous degrees of freedom, the Wigner function provides a
complete quasi-probability representation of quantum states on classical
phase space. The dynamical evolution of the Wigner function can be
expressed through an evolution kernel, and Marinov
Marinov 1991

showed that this kernel admits a path integral representation over phase
space in which a new functional—distinct from but related to the
Hamiltonian action—appears with real weights. This path integral has
proven valuable both conceptually, in connecting quantum and classical
dynamics, and computationally, as the basis for semiclassical
approximations
Dittrich et al. 2006
;
Dittrich et al. 2010
;
Ozorio de Almeida and Brodier 2006
;
Dittrich and Pachón 2009
.

The extension of the Wigner function to finite-dimensional systems was
pioneered by Buot
Buot 1974
, Hannay and Berry
Hannay and Berry 1980
,
Cohen and Scully
Cohen and Scully 1986
, and Feynman
Feynman 1987

for specific low-dimensional cases, and formulated systematically by
Wootters
Wootters 1987
for systems with a prime number
d
of
orthogonal states. In Wootters’s construction, the phase space is a
discrete
d\times d
lattice with toroidal topology, and the Wigner
function is a real-valued distribution whose marginals reproduce the
correct quantum probabilities. This framework was subsequently extended
to prime-power dimensions by Gibbons, Hoffman, and
Wootters
Gibbons et al. 2004
, and explored by many
authors
Leonhardt 1995
;
Galetti and de Toledo
Piza 1988
;
Vourdas 2004
;
Björk et al. 2008
.

A central result that underscores the physical significance of the
discrete Wigner function is Gross’s discrete Hudson
theorem
Gross 2006
: for odd-prime-dimensional systems, the only
pure states with a non-negative discrete Wigner function are the
stabilizer states. Since stabilizer circuits can be efficiently
simulated classically
Gottesman 1999
;
Aaronson and Gottesman 2004
,
Wigner negativity emerges as a necessary resource for quantum
computational advantage within the quasiprobability-based simulation
framework. This connection has been made precise through the resource
theory of magic states
Veitch et al. 2012
;
Howard et al. 2014
;
Mari and Eisert 2012
;
Pashayan et al. 2015

and reviewed within the broader quasi-probability landscape in
Ferrie 2011
;
it also has deep ties to
contextuality
Raussendorf et al. 2017
;
Delfosse et al. 2015
.

On the computational side, the discrete Wigner function has enabled powerful
semiclassical simulation methods for many-body spin systems.
Schachenmayer, Pikovski, and Rey
Schachenmayer et al. 2015a
;
Schachenmayer et al. 2015b

introduced the discrete truncated Wigner approximation (DTWA), in which
classical trajectories on the discrete phase space are sampled via Monte
Carlo to approximate the quantum dynamics of interacting spin lattice
models. This method has been successfully applied to long-range
interacting systems in regimes inaccessible to exact diagonalization or
tensor-network methods
Acevedo et al. 2017
;
Zhu et al. 2019
.

While the discrete Wigner function and its static properties are now
well understood, a systematic treatment of the
dynamics
—in
particular a Marinov-style path integral formulation of the evolution
kernel in discrete phase space with an explicit action
functional—has not been fully developed. A closely related line of
work by Kocia and Love
Kocia and Love 2021
develops a periodized
stationary-phase method for discrete Wigner functions of odd-prime
dimension, using
p
-adic number-theoretic techniques to evaluate
quadratic Gauss sums; their approach is targeted at classical
simulation of quantum circuits and complements the present
Marinov-style construction, which is oriented toward Hamiltonian
dynamics and many-body semiclassical methods. Such a formulation
would provide a natural bridge between the continuous Marinov path
integral and the discrete phase-space methods used in quantum
information and many-body physics, and could potentially improve the
DTWA by incorporating higher-order quantum corrections in a
controlled manner.

In this paper we address this gap. We construct the discrete Weyl
transform and Wigner function following the algebraic approach based on
generalized displacement operators, derive the evolution kernel of the
discrete Wigner function, and obtain a path integral representation for
this kernel by iterating the composition law. Our main result,
Eq. (
29
), expresses the propagator as a sum over
paths on
\mathbb{Z}_{d}\times\mathbb{Z}_{d}
weighted by a phase-space action that is
the discrete counterpart of Marinov’s functional. Throughout the paper
we restrict to
d
an odd prime, where
\mathbb{Z}_{d}
is a finite field admitting
the multiplicative inverse of
2
. We illustrate the formalism with a
single-qutrit (
d=3
) example and apply it to an interacting two-qutrit
system, where we show that the nonzero fluctuation sectors of the path
integral are precisely the ingredients missing from any truncation
restricted to the boundary-term (mean-field) sector.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
II

reviews the Marinov path integral for continuous systems.
Section
III
develops the discrete Weyl transform
for systems with a
d
-dimensional Hilbert space (
d
odd prime).
Section
IV
derives the evolution kernel of the discrete
Wigner function. Section
V
obtains the path
integral representation. Section
VI
illustrates the
formalism with qutrit examples, including an interacting two-qutrit
system. Section
VII
discusses applications.
Section
VIII
presents conclusions and outlook.

II
Continuous Phase-Space Formulation

We briefly recall the Wigner–Weyl formalism for continuous degrees of
freedom and the Marinov path integral, which serve as the template for
the discrete construction.

For a system with one continuous degree of freedom, the Weyl
transform
Weyl 1927
;
Groenewold 1946
maps a phase-space function

f(q,p)
to a self-adjoint operator
\hat{A}_{f}
via the displacement
operators
\mathcal{U}(u)=e^{-{\rm i}u\hat{P}/\hbar}
and

\mathcal{V}(v)=e^{-{\rm i}v\hat{Q}/\hbar}
, which satisfy
the Weyl relation

\mathcal{U}(u)\mathcal{V}(v)=e^{{\rm i}uv/\hbar}\mathcal{V}(v)\mathcal{U}(u)
.
A symmetric ordering factor ensures self-adjointness, and the inverse
Weyl transform (the Wigner map) associates to any density matrix

\hat{\rho}
a real quasi-probability distribution—the Wigner
function—on phase space
Wigner 1932
;
Hillery et al. 1984
;
Case 2008
;
Curtright and Zachos 2012
.

Under unitary evolution

\hat{\rho}(t)=\hat{U}(t)\hat{\rho}(0)\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)

with
\hat{U}(t)=e^{-{\rm i}\hat{H}t/\hbar}
, the Wigner function
evolves as

\rho_{\mathrm{W}}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime\prime},t)=\int{\rm d}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\,G_{\mathrm{W}}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime\prime},t;\mathbf{r}^{\prime},0)\,\rho_{\mathrm{W}}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},0)\,,

(1)

where
\mathbf{r}=(q,p)
and
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{W}}
is the Wigner
propagator
Marinov 1991
;
Dittrich et al. 2006
. Marinov
Marinov 1991

showed that
G_{\mathrm{W}}
admits a path integral representation

G_{\mathrm{W}}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime\prime},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{(2\pi\hbar)^{f}}\int\!\mathcal{D}^{2f}\!r\int\!\mathcal{D}^{2f}\!\tilde{r}\;e^{\frac{{\rm i}}{\hbar}S_{\mathrm{W}}[\mathbf{r},\tilde{\mathbf{r}},t]}\,,

(2)

with the phase-space action functional

S_{\mathrm{W}}[\mathbf{r},\tilde{\mathbf{r}},t]=\int_{0}^{t}\!\left[\dot{\mathbf{r}}\wedge\tilde{\mathbf{r}}+H_{\mathrm{W}}\!\left(\mathbf{r}+\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{\mathbf{r}}\right)-H_{\mathrm{W}}\!\left(\mathbf{r}-\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{\mathbf{r}}\right)\right]{\rm d}t^{\prime}\,.

(3)

Here
\mathbf{r}_{i}\wedge\mathbf{r}_{j}=\mathbf{r}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathsf{J}\,\mathbf{r}_{j}
is the symplectic
product,
\mathbf{r}(t^{\prime})
a trajectory with fixed endpoints, and

\tilde{\mathbf{r}}(t^{\prime})
an unrestricted fluctuation variable. The
extrema of
S
coincide with the classical Hamilton equations. This
path integral has been used for semiclassical
propagation
Dittrich et al. 2006
;
Dittrich et al. 2010
;
Ozorio de Almeida and Brodier 2006
and
forms the basis for Monte Carlo methods in phase
space
Marinov 1991
.

The goal of this paper is to construct the discrete counterpart of
Eqs. (
2
)–(
3
) for systems with a
finite-dimensional Hilbert space.

III
Discrete Phase Space and Weyl Transform

For quantum systems with a finite-dimensional Hilbert space

\mathscr{H}
of dimension
d
, a classical phase space in the usual
sense does not exist. Nevertheless, the algebraic structure reviewed in
Sec.
II
provides a blueprint: the Weyl transform is
built from displacement operators, which in turn are constructed from
position and momentum operators related by a Fourier transform. As we
now show, each of these ingredients has a natural discrete analog.

Throughout this section, and unless stated otherwise, we take
d
to be
an odd prime. We denote by
\omega=e^{2\pi{\rm i}/d}
a primitive

d
th root of unity, and all arithmetic in the subscripts of
\omega

is understood modulo
d
.

III.1
The discrete Fourier transform and conjugate operators

The starting point is the observation that the Fourier transform plays a
dual role in continuous quantum mechanics: it is both a mathematical tool
and the unitary transformation relating the position and momentum
representations. The Stone–von Neumann
theorem
von Neumann 1931
;
Stone 1930
guarantees that, up to unitary
equivalence, there is a unique irreducible representation of the
canonical commutation relations
[\hat{Q},\hat{P}]={\rm i}\hbar
, and the
Fourier transform implements the change of basis between the two.

For finite-dimensional systems, we proceed by analogy. Let

\{|\,x,n\,\rangle\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}
be an orthonormal basis of “position-like”
states. For a qutrit system (
d=3
), one may take

|\,x,n\,\rangle
to be the eigenstates of a diagonal operator

\hat{x}
with eigenvalues
0,1,2
.

We define the
discrete Fourier transform
(DFT) as the unitary
operator
\mathscr{F}:\mathscr{H}\to\mathscr{H}
given by

\mathscr{F}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{m,n=0}^{d-1}\omega^{mn}\,|\,x,m\,\rangle\langle\,x,n\,|\,.

(4)

One readily verifies that
\mathscr{F}
is unitary,

\mathscr{F}^{\dagger}\mathscr{F}=I
, and that
\mathscr{F}^{4}=I
.

We then define the position-like and momentum-like operators

\hat{x}=\sum_{n=0}^{d-1}n\,|\,x,n\,\rangle\langle\,x,n\,|\,,\qquad\hat{p}=\sum_{n=0}^{d-1}n\,|\,p,n\,\rangle\langle\,p,n\,|\,,

(5)

where the momentum-like basis is

|\,p,n\,\rangle=\mathscr{F}|\,x,n\,\rangle
. By construction,

\hat{p}=\mathscr{F}\,\hat{x}\,\mathscr{F}^{\dagger}
, in direct
analogy with the continuous case.

III.2
Generalized displacement operators

From the conjugate operators
\hat{x}
and
\hat{p}
, we construct the
clock and shift operators
Schwinger 1960

\hat{Z}=\exp\!\left(\frac{2\pi{\rm i}}{d}\,\hat{x}\right),\qquad\hat{X}=\exp\!\left(-\frac{2\pi{\rm i}}{d}\,\hat{p}\right).

(6)

These operators act on the position basis as

\hat{Z}|\,x,n\,\rangle=\omega^{n}|\,x,n\,\rangle\,,\qquad\hat{X}|\,x,n\,\rangle=|\,x,n\oplus 1\,\rangle\,,

(7)

where
n\oplus 1
denotes addition modulo
d
. They satisfy the Weyl
commutation relation

\hat{Z}\hat{X}=\omega\,\hat{X}\hat{Z}\,,

(8)

which is the discrete analog of

\mathcal{U}(u)\mathcal{V}(v)=e^{{\rm i}uv/\hbar}\mathcal{V}(v)\mathcal{U}(u)
.

The
displacement operators
are defined as

\hat{D}(k,j)=\omega^{-kj/2}\,\hat{Z}^{k}\,\hat{X}^{j}\,,\qquad(k,j)\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}\times\mathbb{Z}_{d}\,,

(9)

where
kj/2
is understood as
kj\cdot 2^{-1}\pmod{d}

(well-defined since
d
is an odd prime and
\gcd(2,d)=1
). The
phase factor
\omega^{-kj/2}
ensures that

\hat{D}(k,j)^{\dagger}=\hat{D}(-k,-j)
. The
d^{2}
operators

\{\hat{D}(k,j)\}
form an orthogonal basis for the space of linear
operators on
\mathscr{H}
with respect to the Hilbert–Schmidt inner
product:

\Tr\!\left[\hat{D}(k,j)^{\dagger}\,\hat{D}(k^{\prime},j^{\prime})\right]=d\,\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}\,\delta_{j,j^{\prime}}\,.

(10)

Using Eq. (
8
) to move
\hat{X}^{j_{1}}
past

\hat{Z}^{k_{2}}
, one also verifies the multiplication rule

\hat{D}(k_{1},j_{1})\,\hat{D}(k_{2},j_{2})=\omega^{2^{-1}(k_{1}j_{2}-k_{2}j_{1})}\,\hat{D}(k_{1}+k_{2},j_{1}+j_{2}),

(11)

consistent with
\hat{D}(k,j)^{\dagger}=\hat{D}(-k,-j)
.

III.3
The discrete Weyl transform

With the operator basis
\{\hat{D}(k,j)\}
at hand, we define the

discrete Weyl transform
of an operator
\hat{A}
acting on

\mathscr{H}
as

\tilde{A}_{\mathtt{W}}(k,j)=\Tr\!\left[\hat{A}\,\hat{D}(k,j)\right]=\Tr\!\left[\hat{A}\,\hat{Z}^{k}\,\hat{X}^{j}\,\omega^{-kj/2}\right],

(12)

where
(k,j)\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}\times\mathbb{Z}_{d}
. The function
\tilde{A}_{\mathtt{W}}(k,j)

lives on the “reciprocal” discrete phase space. The inversion formula
is

\hat{A}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{k,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\tilde{A}_{\mathtt{W}}(k,j)\,\hat{D}(k,j)^{\dagger}\,,

(13)

which follows directly from the orthogonality
relation (
10
).

The corresponding function on the
direct
discrete phase space

\mathbb{Z}_{d}\times\mathbb{Z}_{d}
is obtained by applying the two-dimensional
inverse DFT:

A_{\mathtt{W}}(m,n)=\mathscr{F}^{-1}[\tilde{A}_{\mathtt{W}}](m,n)=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{k,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\tilde{A}_{\mathtt{W}}(k,j)\,\omega^{jn-km}\,.

(14)

We call
\mathbb{Z}_{d}\times\mathbb{Z}_{d}
, equipped with this correspondence, the

discrete phase space
of the system. Its topology is that of a
toroidal lattice, as first noted by Wootters
Wootters 1987
.

III.4
The discrete Wigner function

Given a density matrix
\hat{\rho}
, we define the
discrete Wigner
function
as the real-valued function

{\rho_{\mathtt{W}}}:\mathbb{Z}_{d}\times\mathbb{Z}_{d}\to\mathbb{R}
:

{\rho_{\mathtt{W}}}(m,n)=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{k,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\tilde{\rho}(k,j)\,\omega^{jn-km}\,,

(15)

where
\tilde{\rho}_{\mathtt{W}}(k,j)=\Tr[\hat{\rho}\,\hat{D}(k,j)]
is the
Weyl symbol of
\hat{\rho}
. The extra factor of
1/d
(relative
to Eq. (
14
)) is conventional and ensures that

\sum_{m,n\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}{\rho_{\mathtt{W}}}(m,n)=1\,,

(16)

as in Wootters’s original formulation
Wootters 1987
. The
marginal properties are the discrete analogs of the
continuous case
Wootters 1987
;
Gibbons et al. 2004
.

Since the propagator
{G_{\mathtt{W}}}
derived below is constructed to map

{\rho_{\mathtt{W}}}\to{\rho_{\mathtt{W}}}
with the normalization (
15
), the factor

1/d^{2}
enters both sides of the evolution equation (
17
)
consistently and plays no role in the qualitative structure of the
kernel (a different choice would simply rescale
{G_{\mathtt{W}}}
by an overall
constant).

It is important to emphasize the difference between this formulation and
those based on systems with continuous degrees of freedom but discrete
symmetry (such as a particle on a ring), where the Hilbert space is
infinite-dimensional and the discreteness arises from approximating the
Fourier transform. Here, the Hilbert space is intrinsically

d
-dimensional, and the discrete nature of the Wigner function is a
natural consequence of the algebraic structure.

IV
Evolution Kernel of the Discrete Wigner Function

We now derive the kernel that propagates the discrete Wigner function
under unitary evolution.

IV.1
Time evolution in the Weyl representation

Under unitary evolution with
\hat{U}(t)=e^{-{\rm i}\hat{H}t/\hbar}
,
the density matrix evolves as

\hat{\rho}(t)=\hat{U}(t)\hat{\rho}(0)\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)
.
On the discrete phase space, the time-evolved Wigner function is related
to the initial one by

{\rho_{\mathtt{W}}}(\mu,t)=\sum_{\mu^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}\times\mathbb{Z}_{d}}{G_{\mathtt{W}}}(\mu,t;\mu^{\prime},0)\,{\rho_{\mathtt{W}}}(\mu^{\prime},0)\,,

(17)

where
\mu=(m,n)
denotes a point on the discrete phase space and

{G_{\mathtt{W}}}
is the evolution kernel we seek.

To compute
{G_{\mathtt{W}}}
, we first need the composition rule for the Weyl
transform of a product of operators.

Proposition 1

(Discrete twisted convolution)
.

Let
d
be an odd prime, and let
\hat{A}_{g}
and
\hat{A}_{h}
be operators on
\mathscr{H}
with
discrete Weyl symbols
\tilde{g}_{\mathtt{W}}(k,j)
and
\tilde{h}_{\mathtt{W}}(k,j)
,
respectively. Then the Weyl symbol of

\hat{A}_{f}=\hat{A}_{g}\hat{A}_{h}
is

\begin{split}\tilde{f}_{\mathtt{W}}(k,j)&=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{k^{\prime},j^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\omega^{2^{-1}(k^{\prime}j-kj^{\prime})}\\
&\times\tilde{g}_{\mathtt{W}}(k+k^{\prime},j+j^{\prime})\,\tilde{h}_{\mathtt{W}}(-k^{\prime},-j^{\prime})\,,\end{split}

(18)

where
2^{-1}
denotes the multiplicative inverse of
2
in
\mathbb{Z}_{d}

(i.e.,
2^{-1}=(d+1)/2
), which exists since
d
is odd.

Proof.

By Eq. (
12
),

\tilde{f}_{\mathtt{W}}(k,j)=\Tr[\hat{A}_{g}\hat{A}_{h}\,\hat{D}(k,j)]
. Expanding

\hat{A}_{g}
via Eq. (
13
) gives

\tilde{f}_{\mathtt{W}}(k,j)=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{k^{\prime\prime},j^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\tilde{g}_{\mathtt{W}}(k^{\prime\prime},j^{\prime\prime})\Tr\!\left[\hat{D}(k^{\prime\prime},j^{\prime\prime})^{\dagger}\hat{A}_{h}\hat{D}(k,j)\right].

(19)

By cyclicity of the trace and
\hat{D}(k^{\prime\prime},j^{\prime\prime})^{\dagger}=\hat{D}(-k^{\prime\prime},-j^{\prime\prime})
,
this becomes

\Tr[\hat{A}_{h}\hat{D}(k,j)\hat{D}(-k^{\prime\prime},-j^{\prime\prime})]
.
Applying the displacement-operator multiplication
rule (
11
) with
(k_{1},j_{1})=(k,j)
and
(k_{2},j_{2})=(-k^{\prime\prime},-j^{\prime\prime})
,

\hat{D}(k,j)\hat{D}(-k^{\prime\prime},-j^{\prime\prime})=\omega^{2^{-1}(k^{\prime\prime}j-kj^{\prime\prime})}\hat{D}(k-k^{\prime\prime},j-j^{\prime\prime}),

yields

\tilde{f}_{\mathtt{W}}(k,j)=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{k^{\prime\prime},j^{\prime\prime}}\omega^{2^{-1}(k^{\prime\prime}j-kj^{\prime\prime})}\,\tilde{g}_{\mathtt{W}}(k^{\prime\prime},j^{\prime\prime})\,\tilde{h}_{\mathtt{W}}(k-k^{\prime\prime},j-j^{\prime\prime}).

(20)

The change of dummy variable
k^{\prime\prime}\to k+k^{\prime}
,
j^{\prime\prime}\to j+j^{\prime}
(equivalent
to
k^{\prime}=-(k-k^{\prime\prime})
,
j^{\prime}=-(j-j^{\prime\prime})
) reproduces
Eq. (
18
).
\square

This is the discrete analog of the Moyal star
product
Moyal 1949
; explicit forms of the star product on discrete
phase space have been discussed in
Galetti and de Toledo
Piza 1988
;
Vourdas 2004
;
Björk et al. 2008
.

IV.2
Derivation of the kernel

Since
\hat{\rho}(t)=\hat{U}(t)\hat{\rho}(0)\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)

is a double product, we apply Proposition
1

twice. Denoting the Weyl symbol of
\hat{U}(t)
by

\tilde{{U_{\mathtt{W}}}}(k,j)
and using the unitarity relation

\widetilde{U^{\dagger}}(-k,-j)=\overline{\tilde{{U_{\mathtt{W}}}}(k,j)}
, one
obtains after a straightforward calculation the Weyl-space kernel

\tilde{{G_{\mathtt{W}}}}(k^{\prime},j^{\prime};k,j)
such that

\tilde{\rho_{t}}(k^{\prime},j^{\prime})=\sum_{k,j}\tilde{{G_{\mathtt{W}}}}(k^{\prime},j^{\prime};k,j)\tilde{\rho_{0}}(k,j)
,
namely

\tilde{{G_{\mathtt{W}}}}(k^{\prime},j^{\prime};k,j)=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\!\!\sum_{k^{\prime\prime},j^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\omega^{2^{-1}[k^{\prime\prime}(j^{\prime}+j)-j^{\prime\prime}(k^{\prime}+k)]}\times\\
\times\tilde{{U_{\mathtt{W}}}}(k^{\prime}+k^{\prime\prime},j^{\prime}+j^{\prime\prime})\,\overline{\tilde{{U_{\mathtt{W}}}}(k^{\prime\prime}+k,j^{\prime\prime}+j)}\,.

(21)

Transforming to the direct phase space via the inverse DFT yields
the propagator in closed form.

Theorem 1

(Discrete Wigner propagator)
.

For
d
an odd prime, the evolution kernel of the discrete Wigner
function is

{G_{\mathtt{W}}}(m^{\prime},n^{\prime};m,n)=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{\tilde{m},\tilde{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\omega^{-2[\tilde{m}(n^{\prime}-n)-\tilde{n}(m^{\prime}-m)]}\times\\
\times{U_{\mathtt{W}}}(\tilde{m}+m,\tilde{n}+n)\,\overline{{U_{\mathtt{W}}}(m^{\prime}-\tilde{m},n^{\prime}-\tilde{n})}\,,

(22)

where
{U_{\mathtt{W}}}(m,n)=\mathscr{F}^{-1}[\tilde{{U_{\mathtt{W}}}}](m,n)

is the phase-space representation of the evolution operator.
Equivalently, in symplectic form,

{G_{\mathtt{W}}}(\mu^{\prime},\mu)=(1/d^{2})\sum_{\tilde{\mu}}\omega^{2\Delta\mu\wedge\tilde{\mu}}\,{U_{\mathtt{W}}}(\mu+\tilde{\mu})\,\overline{{U_{\mathtt{W}}}(\mu^{\prime}-\tilde{\mu})}
, with

\Delta\mu=\mu^{\prime}-\mu
and

\mu\wedge\tilde{\mu}=m\tilde{n}-n\tilde{m}
.

Proof.

Starting from (
21
), we Fourier-invert the Weyl-space
kernel with respect to both
(k^{\prime},j^{\prime})
and
(k,j)
using
Eq. (
14
), and substitute the Fourier
representations of
\tilde{{U_{\mathtt{W}}}}
and
\overline{\tilde{{U_{\mathtt{W}}}}}
.
The sums over
(k^{\prime},j^{\prime})
,
(k,j)
, and the internal
(k^{\prime\prime},j^{\prime\prime})
are
evaluated using
\frac{1}{d}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\omega^{k\ell}=\delta_{\ell,0}
;
the resulting Kronecker deltas fix two pairs of summation variables
in terms of a single remaining pair
(\tilde{m},\tilde{n})
.
Equation (
22
) then follows after the change of
variables
\tilde{m}\to\tilde{m}-m
,
\tilde{n}\to\tilde{n}-n
,
permissible since the sum ranges over all of
\mathbb{Z}_{d}
.
\square

Equation (
22
) is the discrete analog of the
continuous Wigner propagator. The phase factor

\omega^{2\Delta\mu\wedge\tilde{\mu}}
plays the role of

e^{{\rm i}\tilde{\mathbf{r}}\wedge(\mathbf{r}^{\prime\prime}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})/\hbar}
, with
the continuous symplectic product replaced by the discrete symplectic form
on
\mathbb{Z}_{d}\times\mathbb{Z}_{d}
. The kernel is real-valued. This can be seen
operationally by writing

{G_{\mathtt{W}}}(\mu^{\prime},\mu)=d^{-1}\Tr[\hat{A}(\mu^{\prime})\,\hat{U}\,\hat{A}(\mu)\,\hat{U}^{\dagger}]
,
where

V
Path Integral Formulation

The evolution kernel satisfies a composition law that allows us to
construct a sum-over-paths representation.

V.1
Composition law

From Eq. (
17
) and the group property of the evolution
operator, it follows immediately that for
t>t^{\prime}>t_{0}
,

{G_{\mathtt{W}}}(\mu,t;\mu^{\prime\prime},t_{0})=\sum_{\mu^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}\times\mathbb{Z}_{d}}{G_{\mathtt{W}}}(\mu,t;\mu^{\prime},t^{\prime})\,{G_{\mathtt{W}}}(\mu^{\prime},t^{\prime};\mu^{\prime\prime},t_{0})\,.

(23)

This composition law is the discrete analog of the semigroup property
of the continuous propagator, and is the key ingredient for the path
integral construction.

V.2
Time slicing and short-time kernel

Let us partition the time interval
[0,t]
into
N
equal steps of
duration
\tau=t/N
, with
t_{i}=i\tau
. Iterating the composition
law (
23
) gives

{G_{\mathtt{W}}}(\mu,t;\mu_{0},0)=\sum_{\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{N-1}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}{G_{\mathtt{W}}}(\mu_{i},t_{i};\mu_{i-1},t_{i-1})\,,

(24)

where
\mu_{N}\equiv\mu
.

For small
\tau
, the phase-space function of the short-time evolution
operator
\hat{U}(\tau)=e^{-{\rm i}\hat{H}\tau/\hbar}
can be
approximated as

{U_{\mathtt{W}}}(\mu)\approx\exp\!\left(-\frac{{\rm i}}{\hbar}\,H_{\mathtt{W}}(\mu)\,\tau\right),

(25)

where
H_{\mathtt{W}}(\mu)=\mathscr{F}^{-1}[\tilde{H}](\mu)
is the
phase-space representation of the Hamiltonian.
Equation (
25
) approximates the phase-space symbol of

e^{-{\rm i}\hat{H}\tau/\hbar}
by the exponential of the symbol of

\hat{H}
; it is a symbol-level short-time expansion (not the Trotter
splitting
e^{\hat{A}+\hat{B}}\!\!\approx\!\!e^{\hat{A}}e^{\hat{B}}
at the operator level).
Expanding the exact symbol
{U_{\mathtt{W}}}
via the discrete Moyal
star-exponential
e^{-{\rm i}\tau\hat{H}/\hbar}_{\star}
one finds

\begin{split}{U_{\mathtt{W}}}(\mu)&=e^{-{\rm i}\tau H_{\mathtt{W}}(\mu)/\hbar}\\
&-\frac{\tau^{2}}{2\hbar^{2}}\big(H_{\mathtt{W}}\star H_{\mathtt{W}}-H_{\mathtt{W}}^{2}\big)(\mu)+O(\tau^{3}),\end{split}

(26)

so the quadratic correction is governed by the discrepancy between
the ordinary square
H_{\mathtt{W}}^{2}(\mu)
and the Moyal self-product

H_{\mathtt{W}}\star H_{\mathtt{W}}(\mu)

Ozorio de Almeida 1998
. We have verified this

\tau^{2}
scaling numerically: for Hamiltonians non-diagonal in the
position basis (e.g.
\hat{H}=\chi\hbar(\hat{x}+\hat{p})
) the
error ratio
\|{U_{\mathtt{W}}}_{\text{exact}}-{U_{\mathtt{W}}}_{\text{approx}}\|_{\infty}/\tau^{2}

converges to the expected constant

\tfrac{1}{2\hbar^{2}}\|H_{\mathtt{W}}\star H_{\mathtt{W}}-H_{\mathtt{W}}^{2}\|_{\infty}
as
\tau\to 0
.
For Hamiltonians diagonal in a stabilizer basis,

H_{\mathtt{W}}\star H_{\mathtt{W}}=H_{\mathtt{W}}^{2}
pointwise and the
approximation (
25
) is exact at all orders in
\tau
.

Substituting Eq. (
25
) into the
kernel (
22
), the short-time propagator becomes

{G_{\mathtt{W}}}(\mu_{i},\mu_{i-1})\approx\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{\tilde{\mu}\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}\times\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\omega^{-2\Delta\mu_{i}\wedge\tilde{\mu}}\times\\
\times\exp\!\left[\frac{{\rm i}}{\hbar}\big(H_{\mathtt{W}}(\mu_{i}+\tilde{\mu})-H_{\mathtt{W}}(\mu_{i-1}-\tilde{\mu})\big)\tau\right],

(27)

where
\Delta\mu_{i}=\mu_{i}-\mu_{i-1}
and

\mu\wedge\tilde{\mu}=m\tilde{n}-n\tilde{m}
denotes the
discrete symplectic product for
\mu=(m,n)
and

\tilde{\mu}=(\tilde{m},\tilde{n})
.
The direct substitution of Eq. (
25
) into
Eq. (
22
) first produces the phase

\omega^{+2\Delta\mu_{i}\wedge\tilde{\mu}}
with the Hamiltonian difference

H_{\mathtt{W}}(\mu_{i}-\tilde{\mu})-H_{\mathtt{W}}(\mu_{i-1}+\tilde{\mu})
in the exponent;
the form (
27
) follows by the change of dummy variable

\tilde{\mu}\to-\tilde{\mu}
, which preserves the full sum over

\mathbb{Z}_{d}\times\mathbb{Z}_{d}
and yields Marinov-like Hamiltonian arguments

H_{\mathtt{W}}(\mu_{i}+\tilde{\mu})-H_{\mathtt{W}}(\mu_{i-1}-\tilde{\mu})
at the cost of
flipping the symplectic phase.
An equivalent midpoint form is obtained
by the further shift
\tilde{\mu}\to\tilde{\mu}-\Delta\mu_{i}/2
(allowed
since
2^{-1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}
for odd
d
), under which the symplectic phase is
invariant (
\Delta\mu\wedge\Delta\mu=0
) and the Hamiltonian arguments
become
H_{\mathtt{W}}(\bar{\mu}_{i}\pm\tilde{\mu})
with midpoint
\bar{\mu}_{i}=(\mu_{i}+\mu_{i-1})/2
; this is the direct discrete analog of Marinov’s
continuous
H_{W}(\mathbf{r}+\tilde{\mathbf{r}}/2)-H_{W}(\mathbf{r}-\tilde{\mathbf{r}}/2)
.
We have verified
identity (
27
) numerically against (
22
)
(with common
{U_{\mathtt{W}}}\approx e^{-{\rm i}\tau H_{\mathtt{W}}/\hbar}
) for

d=3,5,7,11
.

V.3
Sum over paths

Inserting Eq. (
27
) into the time-sliced
expression (
24
) and collecting the phases, we obtain

{G_{\mathtt{W}}}(\mu,t;\mu_{0},0)=\left(\frac{1}{d^{2n}}\right)^{\!N}\sum_{\{\mu_{i}\}}\sum_{\{\tilde{\mu}_{i}\}}\omega^{-2\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Delta\mu_{i}\wedge\tilde{\mu}_{i}}\times\\
\times\exp\!\left[\frac{{\rm i}}{\hbar}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(H_{\mathtt{W}}(\mu_{i}+\tilde{\mu}_{i})-H_{\mathtt{W}}(\mu_{i-1}-\tilde{\mu}_{i})\big)\tau\right],

(28)

where
n
is the number of qudits (so that the discrete phase space has

d^{2n}
points), the first sum runs over all
(N{-}1)
-tuples

(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{N-1})\in[(\mathbb{Z}_{d})^{2n}]^{N-1}
with

\mu_{N}=\mu
fixed, and the second over all
N
-tuples

(\tilde{\mu}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{\mu}_{N})\in[(\mathbb{Z}_{d})^{2n}]^{N}
. For

n=1
this reduces to
(1/d^{2})^{N}
; for the two-qutrit example of
Sec.
VI.2
,
n=2
and the prefactor becomes

(1/d^{4})^{N}=(1/81)^{N}
.

In the limit
N\to\infty
, the sequences
\{\mu_{i}\}
and

\{\tilde{\mu}_{i}\}
define piecewise-constant paths on the discrete
phase space. Introducing the formal notation of paths

\gamma:[0,t]\to(\mathbb{Z}_{d})^{2n}
and

\xi:[0,t]\to(\mathbb{Z}_{d})^{2n}
, we write the propagator as

{G_{\mathtt{W}}}(\mu,t;\mu_{0},0)=\left(\frac{1}{d^{2n}}\right)^{\!N}\sum_{\gamma}\sum_{\xi}e^{{\rm i}S_{\mathtt{W}}[\gamma,\xi,t]}

(29)

with the discrete phase-space action

\begin{split}S_{\mathtt{W}}[\gamma,\xi,t]&=-\frac{4\pi}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Delta\gamma_{i}\wedge\xi_{i}\\
&+\frac{1}{\hbar}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big[H_{\mathtt{W}}(\gamma_{i}+\xi_{i})-H_{\mathtt{W}}(\gamma_{i-1}-\xi_{i})\big]\tau\,.\end{split}

(30)

We emphasize that, unlike the continuous Marinov path integral, no
formal continuum notation for the action (such as

\int_{0}^{t}\dot{\gamma}\wedge\xi\,{\rm d}\tau
) is invoked: the
action (
30
) is the fundamental object, defined as
a finite sum over time steps. The time variable is continuous (the limit

N\to\infty
is taken), but the dynamical variables
\gamma_{i}
and
\xi_{i}

are elements of
(\mathbb{Z}_{d})^{2n}
at each time step.

Equations (
29
)–(
30
)
constitute the main result of this paper. They provide the exact discrete
analog of Marinov’s path integral
(
2
)–(
3
) for the Wigner function
propagator. Several features deserve comment:

1.

The “kinetic” term
\Delta\gamma_{i}\wedge\xi_{i}
involves the discrete
symplectic form, replacing
\dot{\mathbf{r}}\wedge\tilde{\mathbf{r}}\,{\rm d}t
in the
continuous case.

2.

The “potential” term

H_{\mathtt{W}}(\gamma_{i}+\xi_{i})-H_{\mathtt{W}}(\gamma_{i-1}-\xi_{i})
is the discrete
finite-difference analog of the continuous expression

H_{W}(\mathbf{r}+\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\mathbf{r}})-H_{W}(\mathbf{r}-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\mathbf{r}})
.

3.

The sum runs over piecewise-constant paths on the discrete toroidal
lattice
(\mathbb{Z}_{d})^{2n}
. Unlike the continuous case, where the path integral
notation
\int\mathcal{D}r
denotes a limit of finite-dimensional integrals,
here both the phase space
and
the set of paths are inherently discrete:
for each time-slice, each path variable takes one of the
d^{2n}
phase-space values.
At any finite
N
, expression (
28
) is a well-defined finite
sum; the exact propagator is obtained in the limit
N\to\infty
, which
refines only the time discretization and not the phase-space lattice.
For Hamiltonians diagonal in a stabilizer basis (such as those of
Sec.
VI
), the symbol-level short-time
relation (
25
) is exact, and (
28
) then
coincides with the exact propagator at any
N
; we have verified this
numerically for
d=3,5,7,11
by comparing single-step (
N=1
) and
iterated (
N=2,3,\ldots
) evaluations of (
28
) against
direct
\hat{U}\hat{\rho}\hat{U}^{\dagger}
evolution.

4.

The normalization factor
(1/d^{2n})^{N}
plays the role of the
measure in the continuous path integral, scaling as the inverse of
the discrete phase-space volume raised to the number of time slices.

5.

In the
\xi_{i}=0
sector, every symplectic phase is unity
and the Hamiltonian piece of the action telescopes to the boundary
term
\tau[H_{\mathtt{W}}(\gamma_{N})-H_{\mathtt{W}}(\gamma_{0})]/\hbar
. After the free sum
over the
N-1
intermediate
\gamma_{i}
(each contributing a factor

d^{2n}
), the
\xi=0
contribution admits the closed form

\begin{split}{G_{\mathtt{W}}}^{\xi=0}(\gamma_{N},t;\gamma_{0},0)&=\\
\frac{1}{d^{2n}}\exp&\!\left[\frac{{\rm i}t}{N\hbar}\big(H_{\mathtt{W}}(\gamma_{N})-H_{\mathtt{W}}(\gamma_{0})\big)\right],\end{split}

(31)

with
\tau=t/N
. This kernel is non-real at finite
N
whenever

H_{\mathtt{W}}(\gamma_{N})\neq H_{\mathtt{W}}(\gamma_{0})
, and, since the exponent scales as

1/N
, collapses in the continuum limit
N\to\infty
to a trivial
uniform kernel
1/d^{2n}
independent of both endpoints. In both
regimes—finite
\tau
(non-real) and continuum (
\tau\to 0
,
uniform and trivial)—the
\xi=0
sector alone fails to reproduce
the propagator; the full quantum dynamics requires the coherent
contribution of the
\xi\neq 0
sectors (see
Sec.
VI.2
).

V.4
Linearized action and pseudo-classical dynamics

For Hamiltonians whose phase-space function is affine in the
coordinates,
H_{\mathtt{W}}(\mu)=a\,m+b\,n+c
, with

\mu=(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}\times\mathbb{Z}_{d}
and integer-valued representatives

m,n\in\{0,1,\ldots,d-1\}
, we treat
H_{\mathtt{W}}
as the restriction to
this fundamental domain of a linear function on the lift

\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
. With this convention, the symbol-level
identity

H_{\mathtt{W}}(\gamma_{i}+\xi_{i})-H_{\mathtt{W}}(\gamma_{i-1}-\xi_{i})=(a,b)\cdot(\Delta\gamma_{i}+2\xi_{i})

(32)

holds as a relation between integer-valued symbols, provided one uses
the lifted arithmetic on the right-hand side. Substituting into
Eq. (
27
), the
\tilde{\mu}
-sum becomes

\sum_{\tilde{n}_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\omega^{-(2\Delta m_{i})\tilde{n}_{i}}\,e^{2{\rm i}b\tau\tilde{n}_{i}/\hbar}\,\sum_{\tilde{m}_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\omega^{(2\Delta n_{i})\tilde{m}_{i}}\,e^{2{\rm i}a\tau\tilde{m}_{i}/\hbar}.

(33)

For the two factors above to collapse to Kronecker deltas, both the
Gauss-sum arguments
and
the phases generated by summing a
lifted integer symbol over a period must be periodic modulo
d
. The
first condition requires
2a\tau/\hbar,\;2b\tau/\hbar\in(2\pi/d)\mathbb{Z}
. The second requires that the residual phase

e^{-{\rm i}a\tau d/\hbar}=(-1)^{k_{a}}
(and its
b
-counterpart) that
arises from integer wraparound of the lifted symbol across its period
evaluates to
+1
, i.e.
k_{a},k_{b}

even
, where
k_{a}=ad\tau/(\pi\hbar)
and
k_{b}=bd\tau/(\pi\hbar)
. The two conditions
combine into the single “strict commensurability” requirement

\frac{a\tau}{\hbar},\;\frac{b\tau}{\hbar}\;\in\;\frac{2\pi}{d}\mathbb{Z}.

(34)

When Eq. (
34
) holds, the
\tilde{\mu}
-sum collapses
to Kronecker deltas enforcing the discrete analog of Hamilton’s
equations,

\Delta m_{i}\equiv k_{b}/2\pmod{d},\qquad\Delta n_{i}\equiv-k_{a}/2\pmod{d},

(35)

with
k_{a},k_{b}\in 2\mathbb{Z}
so that
k_{a}/2,k_{b}/2
are ordinary
integers. The propagator then reduces to a deterministic shift on
the toroidal lattice. Equivalently, Eq. (
34
) is
the condition that
\hat{U}=e^{-{\rm i}\tau\hat{H}/\hbar}
acts as a pure
Pauli (Heisenberg) operator on the qudit, which by Gross’s
theorem
Gross 2006
induces an affine symplectic shift on the
discrete Wigner function.
When only the weaker condition
2a\tau/\hbar,\;2b\tau/\hbar\in(2\pi/d)\mathbb{Z}
holds (i.e.
k_{a}
or
k_{b}
integer but odd), the
wraparound phase
(-1)^{k_{a}}
does not cancel,
\hat{U}
is a
“half-Pauli” root outside the Clifford group, and the propagator
develops a non-delta structure—the kernel is no longer supported
on a single lattice shift but spreads over
\mathbb{Z}_{d}\times\mathbb{Z}_{d}
with an
oscillating signature. Away from even commensurability, the

\tilde{\mu}
-sum gives an exponential-sum pattern on
\mathbb{Z}_{d}
that
broadens the kernel across the lattice. For
nonlinear

Hamiltonians, the cancellation in (
32
) fails and
the coupling between
\gamma
and
\xi
sectors generates the
genuinely quantum interference exemplified in
Sec.
VI.2
. The generalization to
n
qudits with

H_{\mathtt{W}}(\mu)=\sum_{a=1}^{n}(a_{a}m_{a}+b_{a}n_{a})+c
is immediate: the

\tilde{\mu}
-sum factorizes into
2n
independent sums and each
subsystem
a
carries its own strict commensurability condition

a_{a}\tau/\hbar,\;b_{a}\tau/\hbar\in(2\pi/d)\mathbb{Z}
. When all
subsystems are simultaneously strictly commensurate, the composite
kernel reduces to a product of Pauli shifts; a single
non-commensurate or non-linear degree of freedom is already
sufficient to reintroduce the full fluctuation sum.

VI
Applications: Qutrit Systems

We now illustrate the formalism with the smallest odd-prime system,
the qutrit (
d=3
), for which
\omega=e^{2\pi{\rm i}/3}
and

2^{-1}=2\pmod{3}
.

VI.1
Single qutrit under a diagonal Hamiltonian

Consider a three-level system with Hamiltonian

\hat{H}=\hbar\chi\,\hat{x}=\hbar\chi\,\mathrm{diag}(0,1,2)\,,

(36)

which models, e.g., equally-spaced energy levels with splitting
\hbar\chi
.
The evolution operator is
\hat{U}(t)=\mathrm{diag}(1,e^{-{\rm i}\chi t},e^{-2{\rm i}\chi t})
.

We take the initial state to be the uniform superposition

|\,\psi(0)\,\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(1,1,1)^{T}
, which is the
momentum eigenstate
|\,p,0\,\rangle
. Its density matrix is

\hat{\rho}(0)=\frac{1}{3}J
, where
J
is the
3\times 3
matrix
of all ones. A direct computation of the Weyl symbols

\tilde{\rho}(k,j)=\Tr[\hat{\rho}\,\hat{D}(k,j)]
using

\hat{D}(k,j)=\omega^{-kj\cdot 2^{-1}}\hat{Z}^{k}\hat{X}^{j}

yields
\tilde{\rho}(k,j)=\delta_{k,0}
(independent of
j
).
Substituting into Eq. (
15
) gives

{\rho_{\mathtt{W}}}_{0}(m,n)=\frac{1}{3}\delta_{n,0}\,,

(37)

i.e., the Wigner function is
1/3
on the three points
(m,0)
with

m\in\{0,1,2\}
, and zero elsewhere on the
3\times 3
lattice.

To compute the time-evolved Wigner function, we need the phase-space
function
{U_{\mathtt{W}}}(m,n)=\mathscr{F}^{-1}[\tilde{{U_{\mathtt{W}}}}](m,n)

(with
1/d
normalization as in Eq. (
14
)),
where
\tilde{{U_{\mathtt{W}}}}(k,j)=\Tr[\hat{U}(t)\hat{D}(k,j)]
.

Substituting into the propagator (
22
) and summing over the
initial Wigner function, one obtains the time-evolved Wigner function.
At
\chi t=\pi
, for example, explicit computation gives

{\rho_{\mathtt{W}}}(m,n,\pi/\chi)=\begin{cases}-\tfrac{1}{9}&(m,n)\in\{(0,0),(2,0)\}\\
\tfrac{1}{3}&(m,n)=(1,0)\\
\tfrac{2}{9}&m\in\{0,2\},\ n\in\{1,2\}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\,,\end{cases}

(38)

which sums to unity and exhibits negativity at
(0,0)
and
(2,0)
.
Since
\hat{H}
is diagonal in the stabilizer (position) basis, the
symbol-level short-time relation (
25
) is exact and
Eq. (
28
) at any finite
N
reproduces this Wigner
function; we have confirmed agreement with both the direct evolution

\hat{U}\hat{\rho}_{0}\hat{U}^{\dagger}
and the path integral for
N=1,2,3,4

to machine precision.
The initial state
|\,p,0\,\rangle
is a stabilizer state of the qutrit
and hence has a non-negative Wigner function. The evolution operator

\hat{U}(\pi/\chi)=\mathrm{diag}(1,-1,1)
is not in the qutrit
Clifford group: its diagonal entries are square roots of unity rather
than cube roots, so it does not normalize the Pauli
group
Gottesman 1999
. Consequently the evolved state

\hat{U}(\pi/\chi)|\,p,0\,\rangle
is not a stabilizer state, and by the
converse of Gross’s theorem
Gross 2006
its discrete Wigner
function is allowed to take negative values, as observed.

VI.2
Beyond the DTWA: two interacting qutrits

To demonstrate the power of the path integral formulation beyond trivially
solvable models, we consider two interacting qutrits with Hamiltonian

\hat{H}=\hbar\chi\left(\hat{x}_{1}\otimes\hat{x}_{2}\right),

(39)

which generates entanglement between the two subsystems. The Hilbert
space is
\mathscr{H}=\mathbb{C}^{3}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{3}
, and the
discrete phase space is
(\mathbb{Z}_{3})^{4}
with
3^{4}=81
points.

The path integral (
28
) for the composite system reads

{G_{\mathtt{W}}}(\mu,t;\mu_{0},0)=\left(\frac{1}{3^{4}}\right)^{\!N}\sum_{\{\mu_{i}\}}\sum_{\{\tilde{\mu}_{i}\}}\omega^{-2\sum_{i}\Delta\mu_{i}\wedge\tilde{\mu}_{i}}\times\\
\times\exp\!\left[\frac{{\rm i}}{\hbar}\sum_{i}\big(H_{\mathtt{W}}(\mu_{i}+\tilde{\mu}_{i})-H_{\mathtt{W}}(\mu_{i-1}-\tilde{\mu}_{i})\big)\tau\right],

(40)

where now
\mu_{i}=(m_{1},n_{1},m_{2},n_{2})_{i}
and the symplectic product
is
\mu\wedge\tilde{\mu}=\sum_{a=1}^{2}(m_{a}\tilde{n}_{a}-n_{a}\tilde{m}_{a})
.

The
\tilde{\mu}=0
sector of the path integral has a simple
structure: each symplectic phase
\omega^{-2\Delta\mu_{i}\wedge 0}=1
,
and the Hamiltonian sum telescopes to a boundary term in the action.
Summing freely over the intermediate
\mu_{i}
with
0<i<N
collapses
the
\tilde{\mu}=0
contribution to the explicit closed form of
Eq. (
31
),
{G_{\mathtt{W}}}^{\xi=0}(\mu_{N};\mu_{0})=d^{-2n}\exp[{\rm i}t\{H_{\mathtt{W}}(\mu_{N})-H_{\mathtt{W}}(\mu_{0})\}/(N\hbar)]
.
On its own, this sector fails to furnish the correct Wigner
propagator in two distinct ways: (i) at any finite time step
\tau
,
it has a non-vanishing imaginary part
\sim\tau
whenever

H_{\mathtt{W}}(\mu_{N})\neq H_{\mathtt{W}}(\mu_{0})
, while
{G_{\mathtt{W}}}
is rigorously real
(cf. Sec.
IV.2
); (ii) in the continuum limit

\tau\to 0
, the boundary phase trivializes to unity, the
\xi=0

contribution reduces to the uniform kernel
d^{-2n}
(independent of

\mu_{0}
and
\mu
) and therefore carries no dynamical information
whatsoever. Only the coherent addition of the
\tilde{\mu}\neq 0

sectors—whose mutual interference is the discrete counterpart of
the phase-space interference pattern that in the continuous case
yields the Moyal bracket beyond the Poisson bracket—restores both
reality and the non-trivial quantum dynamics. We have checked both
failure modes numerically for the two-qutrit system: retaining only

\tilde{\mu}=0
at
N=1
yields

|\mathrm{Im}\,{G_{\mathtt{W}}}|_{\max}\sim 10^{-2}
at
\chi\tau=0.5
, while
for
N\geq 8
the imaginary part is suppressed as
\sim 1/N
but the
real part tends to a uniform value
1/81\approx 0.0123
for all

(\mu^{\prime},\mu)
, reproducing none of the dynamics of the full
propagator. The discrete truncated Wigner approximation
(DTWA)
Schachenmayer et al. 2015a
is a separate semiclassical scheme
that samples the Wigner function and propagates each sample along
mean-field trajectories, capturing two-point correlations at short
times but not, in general, higher-order observables such as
entanglement entropy.

At each time step, the exact kernel sums over all

d^{2n}
fluctuation vectors in the composite phase space
(
n
the number of qudits,
d^{2n}=81
for the present two-qutrit system):

{G_{\mathtt{W}}}(\mu^{\prime},\mu;\tau)=\frac{1}{81}\sum_{\tilde{\mu}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{3})^{4}}\omega^{-2\Delta\mu\wedge\tilde{\mu}}\times\\
\times\exp\!\left[\frac{{\rm i}}{\hbar}\big(H_{\mathtt{W}}(\mu^{\prime}+\tilde{\mu})-H_{\mathtt{W}}(\mu-\tilde{\mu})\big)\tau\right],

(41)

of which
80
are genuine fluctuation contributions beyond the
boundary-term
\tilde{\mu}=0
sector.

As a concrete quantitative test, we consider the initial product state

|\,\psi(0)\,\rangle=|\,p,0\,\rangle\otimes|\,p,0\,\rangle
, for which the
Wigner function is the product

{\rho_{\mathtt{W}}}_{0}(\mu)={\rho_{\mathtt{W}}}^{(1)}_{0}(m_{1},n_{1}){\rho_{\mathtt{W}}}^{(2)}_{0}(m_{2},n_{2})
,
and compute the linear entropy

S_{L}(t)=1-\Tr[\hat{\rho}_{1}^{2}(t)]
as a witness of entanglement
generation. The time-evolved state is

|\,\psi(t)\,\rangle=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{m,n}e^{-{\rm i}\chi t\,mn}|\,x,m\,\rangle|\,x,n\,\rangle
.
The reduced density matrix has elements

(\hat{\rho}_{1})_{mm^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{9}\sum_{n=0}^{2}e^{-{\rm i}\chi t\,n(m-m^{\prime})}
,
from which

\Tr[\hat{\rho}_{1}^{2}]=\frac{27+4(1+2\cos\chi t)^{2}+2(1+2\cos 2\chi t)^{2}}{81}.

(42)

Expanding for short times yields

S_{L}(t)=\frac{8\chi^{2}t^{2}}{9}+O(t^{4})\,,

(43)

which we have independently verified against exact diagonalization of
the
9\times 9
Hamiltonian matrix.
We have also evaluated Eq. (
40
) numerically for this
system and confirmed that the path integral reproduces the full
closed-form expression (
42
) for
\Tr[\hat{\rho}_{1}^{2}]

to numerical precision (we find
S_{L}=8.823\times 10^{-3}
at

\chi t=0.1
, agreeing with exact diagonalization to ten decimals).
Any truncation restricted to a single
\tilde{\mu}
sector—most
natively
\tilde{\mu}=0
, discussed above—fails to reproduce the

\Tr[\hat{\rho}_{1}^{2}]
dynamics: the quantum entanglement generation
encoded in (
42
) arises from the coherent sum over
all fluctuation sectors.
The coefficient
8/9=2[\mathrm{Var}(\hat{x})]^{2}
with

\mathrm{Var}(\hat{x})=2/3
for the state
|\,p,0\,\rangle
is
consistent with the general short-time perturbation-theory result

S_{L}(t)\approx 2\chi^{2}t^{2}\,\mathrm{Var}(\hat{A})\mathrm{Var}(\hat{B})

for a bipartite interaction
\hat{H}=\chi\hat{A}\otimes\hat{B}

acting on a product state.
The DTWA in its standard implementation
Schachenmayer et al. 2015a

is known to reproduce one- and two-point correlations at short times
but to be inexact for higher-order observables such as the linear
entropy computed here. The
\tilde{\mu}\neq 0
fluctuation sectors in
Eq. (
41
) are precisely the contributions that
the path integral formulation makes explicit and that any
mean-field-type truncation necessarily misses.

We note that Eq. (
43
) is merely the short-time
expansion of the exact closed-form expression (
42
)
for
\Tr[\hat{\rho}_{1}^{2}]
, which is valid for all times.
The linear entropy is periodic with
S_{L}(t+2\pi/\chi)=S_{L}(t)
,
symmetric about
\chi t=\pi
, and reaches its global maximum

S_{L}^{\max}=2/3
(the maximum entropy of a maximally mixed qutrit)
at the two values
\chi t=2\pi/3
and
\chi t=4\pi/3
within a
period, where both
(1+2\cos\chi t)
and
(1+2\cos 2\chi t)
vanish
simultaneously. The entropy vanishes at the revival
\chi t=2\pi

where the system returns to the initial product state. Representative
values,

\begin{array}[]{c|ccccccc}\chi t&0.25&0.5&\pi/2&2\pi/3&\pi&4\pi/3&2\pi\\
\hline\cr S_{L}(t)&0.053&0.185&0.593&0.667&0.395&0.667&0.000\end{array}

(44)

are reproduced by the path integral (
40
) to machine
precision; the short-time expansion
8\chi^{2}t^{2}/9
is accurate only
for
\chi t\lesssim 0.3
, while the full path integral captures the
entire non-trivial time dependence.

VII
Discussion

VII.1
Systematic corrections to the DTWA

The two-qutrit example of Sec.
VI.2
illustrates a
general principle: the path integral (
28
) provides the
exact discrete-phase-space propagator, of which the DTWA and related
semiclassical methods
Schachenmayer et al. 2015a
are inequivalent
approximations motivated by mean-field considerations. The
contribution of the
\xi\neq 0
fluctuation sectors is precisely
what generates the non-trivial entanglement dynamics. For
many-body systems of
n
qudits, the phase space has
d^{2n}
points
at each time step, and an exhaustive enumeration of all fluctuation
paths over
N
time slices scales as
(d^{2n})^{N}
—clearly
intractable for large
n
. Monte Carlo sampling of the fluctuation
paths
\xi
, analogous to the way Marinov’s continuous path integral
underlies Monte Carlo schemes for Wigner
dynamics
Dittrich et al. 2010
;
Marinov 1991
, offers a possible numerical
strategy; however, the oscillatory complex weights

e^{{\rm i}\mathcal{S}}
give rise to a sign problem that must be
addressed, for instance by saddle-point expansion around stationary

(\gamma,\xi)
configurations or by resummation techniques, before
such sampling becomes efficient.

VII.2
Characterization of non-classicality

Gross’s discrete Hudson theorem
Gross 2006
establishes that, for
systems of odd prime dimension, the only pure states with a non-negative
discrete Wigner function are the stabilizer states. This result, combined
with the Gottesman–Knill theorem
Gottesman 1999
, implies that
Wigner negativity is a necessary resource for quantum computational
advantage
Veitch et al. 2012
;
Howard et al. 2014
;
Mari and Eisert 2012
, a connection
made quantitative in semiclassical simulation schemes based on
Gauss-sum evaluation of discrete Wigner path
integrals
Kocia and Love 2021
.

The propagator
{G_{\mathtt{W}}}
provides a dynamical perspective on this
resource theory. Since
{G_{\mathtt{W}}}
itself can take negative values,
the generation or destruction of Wigner negativity under time evolution
can be tracked through the kernel. The path integral
representation (
29
) exhibits this structure
explicitly as a sum of complex phases over fluctuation sectors
\xi
,
suggesting that interference between these sectors plays a role in
the dynamical growth of Wigner negativity and “magic”
(non-stabilizerness). Making this connection quantitative is a
natural direction for further work.

VII.3
Composite systems and entanglement

For composite systems of
n
qudits, the phase space is

(\mathbb{Z}_{d})^{2n}
and the formalism generalizes straightforwardly. The
path integral involves
2n
-component paths, and the action contains
the full many-body Hamiltonian. Entanglement dynamics in this setting
has a richer structure than one might initially expect: by Gross’s
theorem
Gross 2006
, entangled
stabilizer
states
(e.g. the qutrit Bell-type states
|\Phi\rangle=d^{-1/2}\sum_{n}|n,n\rangle
) have
non-negative
discrete
Wigner functions despite being maximally entangled, while entangled
non-stabilizer states necessarily exhibit negativity (for two-qubit
systems, a direct connection between entanglement and Wigner
negativity was established in
Franco and Penna 2006
).
The path integral provides a tool for studying the dynamical
generation and propagation of both types of entanglement, and the
associated (de)localization of Wigner negativity, through the
interference of phase-space paths, as we demonstrated concretely for
the two-qutrit system.

VIII
Conclusions and Outlook

We have constructed a path integral formulation for the dynamics of
quantum systems with a finite-dimensional Hilbert space of odd prime
dimension
d
, working entirely within the discrete phase space

\mathbb{Z}_{d}\times\mathbb{Z}_{d}
. The central result, Eq. (
29
),
expresses the propagator of the discrete Wigner function as a sum
over piecewise-constant paths on the toroidal lattice, weighted by a
discrete phase-space action functional (
30
).

The key steps in the construction were: (i) building the discrete Weyl
transform from generalized displacement operators derived from the
discrete Fourier transform; (ii) deriving the evolution kernel using
the twisted convolution (discrete Moyal product); and (iii) iterating
the composition law of the kernel with a short-time approximation to
obtain the path sum.

The formulation was verified for a single qutrit, and its utility
illustrated for an interacting two-qutrit system. Beyond the
short-time
O(t^{2})
growth of the linear entropy, the exact
closed-form expression for
\Tr[\hat{\rho}_{1}^{2}]
is reproduced by
the full path integral at all times, including the twin global maxima

S_{L}^{\max}=2/3
at
\chi t=2\pi/3
and
4\pi/3
and the revival
at
\chi t=2\pi
. The
\tilde{\mu}=0
truncation of the
fluctuation sum fails in two complementary ways—it violates
reality at finite time step and reduces to a trivial uniform kernel
in the continuum limit—showing that genuine quantum entanglement
requires the coherent sum over all fluctuation sectors.
We also identified an exactly solvable “pseudo-classical” regime:
for Hamiltonians linear in the phase-space coordinates and at

strictly
commensurate times—those for which the single-step
evolution
\hat{U}
lies in the Pauli (Heisenberg) group—the
fluctuation sum collapses to a deterministic shift on the toroidal
lattice, realizing the discrete analog of classical Hamiltonian
flow. Away from strict commensurability, or for any non-linear
contribution to the Hamiltonian, the full fluctuation sum is
required and the propagator carries the non-classical interference
responsible for Wigner negativity.

Several directions for future work suggest themselves. First,
the extension to
d=2
(qubits) requires replacing the displacement
operators by a construction that avoids the multiplicative inverse
of
2
; the framework of
Raussendorf et al. 2017
;
Delfosse et al. 2015
provides
a starting point. Second, extending the formalism to prime-power
dimensions
d=p^{k}
via the Galois-field
construction
Gibbons et al. 2004
would broaden its applicability.
Third, Monte Carlo sampling of the fluctuation paths
\xi
in the
path sum offers a route toward numerical schemes for quantum
corrections to the DTWA in many-body systems of interacting
qudits
Zhu et al. 2019
, provided the sign problem inherent to the
oscillatory phase
e^{{\rm i}\mathcal{S}}
can be tamed by saddle-point
or resummation techniques. Finally, extending the formalism to open
quantum systems
Pachón et al. 2010
would provide a
phase-space path integral description of decoherence in
finite-dimensional systems.
