Introduction

Nursing education is essential for developing a sustainable nursing workforce. It requires student nurses not only to master theoretical knowledge and practical skills but also to effectively apply these competencies in clinical settings, thereby improving patient care and health outcomes [1, 2]. Clinical placements are an essential component of nursing education, serving as the bridge between theoretical knowledge and clinical practice.They represent a crucial step in the transition from nursing student to registered nurse [3]. Through this process, and with guidance from clinical preceptors, they integrate this learning to navigate the complexities and inherent uncertainties of real-world patient care [4, 5]. The effectiveness of training transfer directly affects the clinical competence and professional identity of nursing trainees, which in turn influences their long-term professional development and ultimately the quality of healthcare delivery [6, 7]. Accordingly, ensuring that theoretical knowledge and clinical skills are effectively transferred into practice remains a central and persistent challenge for nursing educators.

Research indicates that not all nursing trainees are able to successfully transfer the knowledge and skills acquired during training into consistent clinical competence [8, 9]. Some nursing trainees demonstrate a pronounced theory–practice gap during clinical placements.This disconnect often manifests as anxiety when managing critically ill patients, difficulty in applying protocols during emergencies, and uncertainty in handling complex interpersonal situations [10–12]. Barriers to training transfer can undermine trainee nurses’ confidence, contribute to premature burnout and increased turnover, and ultimately compromise patient safety by raising the risk of clinical errors [13–15]. Consequently, it is imperative to identify and remove barriers that hinder nursing trainees from effectively transferring training outcomes into clinical practice.

Training transfer is not a linear outcome but a dynamic process, shaped by the interplay of factors spanning individual cognition, affective motivation, organizational climate, and the broader sociocultural context [16].Prevailing research has been predominantly quantitative and focused on the evaluation of training programs. This approach, while valuable, often fails to capture the deeper experiential, perceptual, and meaning-making dimensions of trainees, thus limiting exploration of the complex, contextual factors underlying transfer barriers.To this end, this study adopts a qualitative approach, using in-depth interviews to explore trainee nurses’ firsthand accounts of the challenges and complexities they encounter when transferring training into practice. This methodology aims to provide a nuanced, contextual understanding of the barriers to training transfer.This study will adopt the Critical Factors Model of training transfer as its analytical framework. It will systematically investigate barriers across the three key dimensions—trainee, training design, and workplace—thereby constructing a coherent explanatory model grounded in established theory [17].

The implications of this study go beyond theoretical understanding to directly guide strategies for enhancing training effectiveness in clinical nursing education.Theoretically, this study enriches the discourse on training transfer by providing a contextual perspective grounded in China’s unique socio-cultural and healthcare landscape, thereby advancing theory with localized insights.Practically, by identifying specific barriers to training transfer, the findings will equip nursing educators to design targeted interventions and optimize clinical placement programs. This directly facilitates the transition of student nurses into competent practitioners.This study aims to systematically identify and analyze barriers to training transfer in clinical nursing education, thereby generating a nuanced, contextual understanding that can inform targeted strategies to enhance training effectiveness and support student nurses’ successful transition into clinical practice.

Method

Design

This study adopts a descriptive qualitative research design. The research structure includes: (1) recruiting 15 clinical intern nursing students using purposive sampling; (2) collecting data through semi-structured individual interviews; and (3) coding and analyzing the interview transcripts using thematic analysis, with double coding and member checking to ensure reliability and validity. The researcher has a professional background in nursing, and the participants are nursing students. A nursing background facilitates empathy and theoretical alignment but may also introduce preconceived biases. Specifically, the researchers’ clinical nursing experience and familiarity with the Chinese healthcare context heightened sensitivity to expressions of hierarchy, role stratification, power distance, and punitive supervision in the internship setting. This interpretive lens enabled nuanced detection and in-depth interpretation of the hierarchical culture theme, while strategies such as reflective journals, double coding, and team triangulation were employed to mitigate potential interpretive bias and enhance research rigor. The findings of this study may inform strategies to help nursing students more readily translate theoretical knowledge and skills into clinical practice.

Recruitment

The researcher distributed recruitment notices to eligible nursing students through head nurses or clinical instructors. Interested students contacted the researcher via WeChat or phone, at which point they were provided with detailed information about the study purpose, content, duration, confidentiality measures, and their right to voluntarily withdraw. Upon agreement, interview times and places were scheduled, and all participants signed written informed consent forms.We used a purposive sampling approach with maximum variation based on characteristics including age, gender, and training school. Prior to participation, individuals were provided with detailed information regarding the study aims, procedures, risks, and benefits, and subsequently provided written informed consent.Inclusion Criteria: (1) Be a trainee nurse. (2) Have completed at least three months of clinical practice. (3) Have successfully passed required skills training and transitioned to a clinical placement. (4) Provide informed consent and participate voluntarily in the study. Exclusion Criterion: Individuals were excluded if they had participated in any similar studies within the past three months. Withdrawal Criterion: Participants retained the right to voluntarily withdraw from the study at any point during the interview process.

Data collection

The interview guide was developed through an iterative process. First, a preliminary outline was drafted based on the research objectives and a review of relevant literature, followed by team discussion. This draft was then piloted through interviews with two trainee nurses. Feedback and findings from these pilot interviews were used to refine the outline, resulting in the final version presented in Supplemental Digital Content 1. Interviews were conducted in January 2026. Following the attainment of thematic saturation after the 13th interview, two additional interviews were held to confirm that no new information was emerging. (When consecutive interview transcripts are independently coded and no new meaning units or initial themes that are not already covered by the existing coding system emerge, information saturation is considered to have been reached). Data collection was therefore concluded, with the final sample capped at fifteen participants.The research is conducted in the lounge or meeting room.

Research procedure

Semi-structured interviews were conducted to collect data. All interview sessions were scheduled in advance at the mutual convenience of the researcher and the participant.Prior to data collection, researchers established contact and communication channels with the participating clinical sites. Before each interview, the researcher explained the study’s aims and procedures, obtained explicit consent for audio-recording, and collected a signed informed consent form from the participant. The interviewer employed active listening and maintained a neutral, facilitative stance to encourage participants to elaborate freely on their experiences. Attention was also paid to non-verbal communication, which was noted in the interview memos. Interviews were guided by the semi-structured interview schedule but allowed flexibility for probing and clarification. They typically lasted between 30 and 45 min. To enhance methodological rigor, the researcher maintained detailed reflective memos after each interview to capture initial impressions, procedural notes, and analytical thoughts.

Data analysis

Within 24 h after the interview, two researchers independently transcribed the audio recordings into text, and both jointly conducted data analysis. In the event of any inconsistencies, a third researcher acted as the arbiter. For emails containing questionable content, we shall contact the research subjects for verification.Thematic analysis was employed, with the following specific steps [18, 19]: (1) Familiarisation with data: Transcribe and read the data, noting initial impressions; (2) Generate initial codes: Systematically code interesting features across the entire dataset, organising data associated with each code; (3) Search for themes: Group codes into latent themes, gathering all data related to each latent theme; (4) Review themes: Examine whether themes are relevant to coding extracts and the entire dataset, generating a thematic map; (5) Define and name themes: Conduct ongoing analysis to refine each theme’s details and the overarching narrative it conveys, producing clear definitions and labels for each theme; (6) Produce report: Select vivid, compelling excerpt examples, conduct final analysis of chosen excerpts, link findings to research questions and literature, generating an analytical report.

Results

Participants

The final sample consisted of 15 trainee nurses (mean age = 21 years; range: 19–23). The majority were female (n = 13, 86.7%) and enrolled in a Bachelor’s degree program (n = 12, 80.0%); the remainder were male (n = 2) and held a nursing diploma (n = 3). Participants are hereafter referred to by their study codes (S1 through S15).

Themes

The analysis of barriers to training transfer revealed three main themes and seven corresponding subthemes.The barriers to training transfer among nursing students fall into three major dimensions: trainee characteristics, training design, and work environment. The trainee dimension comprises Weak intrinsic motivation for transfer (unclear professional identity, lack of clear goals, and deficits in autonomy and responsibility), low self-efficacy (insufficient confidence in clinical procedures, poor communication and interaction efficacy, and inadequate emotional management and stress coping), and limited knowledge transformation ability (rigid knowledge, poor situational awareness, and weak analytical and synthetic application skills). The training design dimension is characterized by a disconnect between training content and clinical practice (outdated knowledge and skills, a mismatched evaluation system) and insufficient post‑procedure feedback and evaluation (lack of constructive feedback, and limited preceptor ability and time). The work environment dimension involves insufficient support (lack of managerial attention, constraints from work procedures and costs) as well as entrenched hierarchical perceptions and a negative, punitive internship culture. These barriers interact with one another, systematically impeding student nurses from effectively transferring their training learning into clinical practice.Following the textual explanation, a summary is provided in Table 1.

Themes and subthemes

Trainee characteristics

Weak intrinsic motivation for transfer

A weak or conflicting professional identity was identified as a key barrier. Contributing factors included unmet professional expectations, ambiguous role perceptions, and a propensity for rapid emotional exhaustion.

S1: The idealized professional stereotype of nursing contrasts sharply with the repetitive and physically intensive workload of routine clinical practice.

S5: Interns tend to perceive themselves as merely executing medical instructions rather than practicing as autonomous professional nursing practitioners.

S9: Prolonged exposure to patient suffering, negative emotional experiences, and end-of-life events may induce emotional detachment and weaken professional dignity established through therapeutic nurse–patient interactions.

The second theme pertained to a lack of clear objectives and future vision. This deficit was marked by an absence of proactive career planning and a tendency to perceive learning goals as externally imposed requirements rather than personally meaningful pursuits.

S7: Lack of understanding regarding career progression pathways, unaware of the direct correlation between current rigorous training and personal development, with little planning for the future.

S9: The motivation to study stems from external pressures, such as passing examinations, avoiding criticism from teachers, and successfully obtaining internship certificates.

Finally, a pattern of passivity and avoidance of responsibility was observed among some trainees. Key features included a lack of professional ambition, dependency on constant guidance for decision-making, and a general disengagement from taking ownership of clinical situations.

S3: The more you do, the more you’ll get wrong; the less you do, the fewer mistakes you’ll make. Just get through the internship safely and smoothly.

S9: The habit of deferring problems to supervising teachers, coupled with an inability to think independently or make autonomous decisions, diminishes one’s sense of accomplishment.

S2: Does not believe that trainee nurses have a responsibility or obligation to address shortcomings in clinical practice, lacking the intrinsic motivation to proactively improve.

Low self-efficacy

Nursing students’ lack of confidence in applying their learning to clinical tasks often leads to hesitation, avoidance, or withdrawal in complex or emergency situations, thereby hindering the transfer of training into practice. A lack of confidence directly impacted trainees’ clinical engagement. Key manifestations included heightened procedural anxiety, reluctance to perform essential skills, and a tendency to avoid clinically complex or higher-risk situations.

S15: During venous puncture, lack of confidence leads to nervousness, causing hands to tremble and steps to be forgotten, significantly impacting the smoothness and success rate of the procedure.

S11: I feel apprehensive about performing catheterisation, a fundamental yet critical skill, fearing failure and causing the patient additional distress.

S5: I tend to shy away from performing emergency procedures such as cardiopulmonary resuscitation and defibrillation. Due to my lack of experience, I feel I would inevitably perform poorly, so I hide behind my supervising instructor to avoid taking the initiative.

Deficits in communication and interpersonal effectiveness emerged as a key barrier. This shortfall was evident both in trainees’ hesitant and often ineffective engagements with patients and families, and in their apparent unease during collaboration with physicians and other senior team members.

S11: Fear of communicating with patients or their families, fear of being unable to answer their questions, fear of facing relatives’ scepticism and negative emotions, leads to reduced interaction with them, merely fulfilling basic task-related communication.

S9: Feeling like the lowest rung on the hospital ladder, I dare not proactively report patient conditions to doctors or supervising tutors, fearing my information might be inaccurate and that I’ll be ridiculed or criticised.

Deficiencies in emotional management were also apparent, characterized by oversensitivity to feedback, emergency-related anxiety, low resilience, and biased attributions.

S7: Tends to be highly sensitive to others’ opinions; even a teacher’s casual criticism or a patient’s complaint can trigger feelings of self-doubt and frustration.

S1: I constantly find myself worrying in advance, fretting over emergencies I might be unable to handle, which makes it difficult to relax and concentrate, consuming a great deal of mental energy.

S10: Following an operational failure, one experiences prolonged low spirits, constantly dwelling on the details of the setback, with a notably low capacity for self-recovery.

S9: When encountering difficulties at work, one tends to attribute them to personal inadequacy, though sometimes external factors are actually the cause.

Insufficient capacity for knowledge transformation

Trainees demonstrated rigid knowledge application and poor situational awareness, characterized by decontextualized understanding, algorithmic thinking, and difficulties in multidisciplinary integration.

S7: Under specific circumstances, it may be necessary to modify procedures to accommodate patient needs. For instance, during resuscitation, a choice must be made between maintaining sterility and maximising time.

S3: Clinical patients are often atypical, presenting with multiple coexisting conditions, and it can be difficult to match them to standardised textbook cases.

S10: While understanding the principles of coronary heart disease and being familiar with treatment guidelines and nursing measures, it proves challenging to formulate a comprehensive educational plan encompassing disease knowledge, medication guidance, dietary management, and psychological support for a patient with coronary heart disease experiencing low mood.

Weak analytical and integrative skills were also observed, characterized by limited perspective-taking, poor information prioritization, application reluctance, and inadequate reflective practice.

S3:When issues are always viewed in isolation—such as treating patients’ symptoms one by one—it becomes exceedingly difficult to integrate them organically within clinical practice and formulate the optimal treatment plan.

S1: At times, when confronted with symptoms, signs, and laboratory results, it proves difficult to swiftly identify the most critical and urgent information.

S6: Not daring to execute operations oneself, and subsequently failing to conduct systematic reviews after execution, prevents the absorption of experience and lessons learned, potentially leading to the recurrence of errors.

Training design

Training content is out of step with clinical practice

Trainees reported a perceived gap between training and practice, citing outdated textbooks, non-contextual skill training, and an overemphasis on specialization as key contributors.

S4: Textbooks fail to incorporate the latest guidelines and evidence in a timely manner, giving the impression that they lag behind clinical practice.

S1: Training with manikins cannot replicate a patient’s genuine emotions. When faced with a real patient, unexpected events may induce psychological pressure, potentially leading to skill failure.

S8: Training often focuses on specialised procedures such as cardiopulmonary resuscitation and intravenous infusion, yet fundamental daily care, communication skills, and compassionate care constitute the most significant aspects of clinical work and have the greatest impact on patient experience.

The training assessment system was also perceived as misaligned, primarily overvaluing procedural performance while undervaluing comprehensive clinical ability.

S15: Assessment during the training phase primarily focuses on theoretical knowledge and adherence to operational protocols, whereas clinical practice tests one’s comprehensive capabilities—including adaptability, problem-solving skills, communication abilities, and so forth. This divergence in evaluation criteria means that even students with outstanding academic records may struggle to adapt swiftly to clinical work.

Insufficient post-operation evaluation and feedback

A lack of effective post-placement feedback was identified. This encompassed both insufficient opportunities to receive feedback and a lack of specific, constructive guidance when feedback was given.

S13: Feedback and evaluation predominantly stem from teachers imparting knowledge unilaterally to nursing students, lacking self-reflection, peer assessment, patient feedback and other forms of multi-dimensional feedback.

S3: Sometimes feedback can be rather vague, such as “well done” or “keep up the good work”. It would be more helpful if specific points of strength could be highlighted, along with explanations of where mistakes lie and concrete suggestions for improvement.

Supervisors were perceived to have limited teaching capacity due to variable instructional abilities and insufficient time, as clinical service demands took precedence over educational duties.

S1: Not all outstanding clinical nurses possess the ability to teach effectively or provide high-quality feedback. While they may have extensive practical experience, they often lack training in teaching methodologies and feedback techniques.

S14: Supervising tutors already shoulder a heavy clinical workload. Under the dual pressures of fulfilling their duties and mentoring students, they often find themselves stretched thin, struggling to allocate sufficient time and energy to observe, guide, and provide detailed feedback.

Work environment

Insufficient support

Organizational support for training transfer was found lacking, characterized by absent follow-up programs, unclear advancement pathways, and low managerial emphasis on applying new learning.

S7: Following on-the-job training, there is a lack of follow-up with a sustained transfer support programme, such as regular workshops and discussions addressing common challenges.

S10: The mentor did not participate in establishing clear, phased transfer objectives, and the mentee remains uncertain about the standards expected of them or the pathways to achieving them.

S4: Managers focus more on clinical metrics such as bed occupancy rates, average length of stay, and adverse events. They seldom inquire about or encourage trainee nurses to apply their learning in daily practice, resulting in a lack of top-down learning culture.

Workflow rigidity and cost-related performance pressures jointly constrained practice. Inflexible procedures hindered adaptive care, while evaluations penalizing consumable use discouraged the application of new, potentially resource-intensive techniques.

S3: When departmental procedures and division of labour become entrenched, even positive changes may be stifled, which can demoralise staff. Furthermore, trainee nurses rarely get genuine hands-on experience.

S7: If the procedure fails, consumables are wasted. This involves costs that may be linked to staff performance, and without departmental policy and funding support, it’s difficult to proceed.

Inherent hierarchical notions and internship culture

A deeply ingrained hierarchical culture, characterized by seniority‑based authority, a compliance‑oriented norm, and a vicious cycle of low trainee expectations, was found to be consistent across all departments.

S7: The department operates under a hierarchical structure where the advice of senior nurses and doctors cannot be challenged. Trainee nurses occupy the lowest rung, and their suggestions are invariably disregarded.

S1: Mentors favour compliant and hardworking students over those who are critical and proactive in their exploration. Those adept at posing questions and engaging in reflection, or who take the initiative to propose actions, may not be favoured.

N4: Mentors harbour low expectations of their trainees, consequently limiting practical opportunities and hindering skill development. This, in turn, reinforces the low expectations, creating a vicious cycle.

A negative, punitive internship culture also emerged, characterized by a transactional work approach, sustained burnout, disciplinary mentorship, and low psychological safety, all of which hindered trainees’ motivation to apply their training.

S7: Sometimes I perceive myself as being assigned only low-skill support tasks, such as running errands, gathering supplies, and performing basic care activities. This sort of routine work really dampens my enthusiasm.

S2: The persistent excessive workload in nursing readily leads to occupational burnout. This prevailing atmosphere of emotional exhaustion erodes the motivation of trainee nurses to engage in proactive learning and practice.

S11: Within the Chinese cultural context, praise is rarely given, and feedback tends to lean more towards criticism. This “critical” rather than “developmental” feedback, which allows no room for error, can stifle the motivation for proactive practice.

S1: I feel like an outsider within the team. Typically, I only spend a month in each department before moving on, leaving before I’ve had a chance to settle in. I’m reluctant to reveal my shortcomings or difficulties, lacking psychological security, and hesitant to engage in learning activities that are necessary yet carry risks.

Discussion

This study is grounded in Holton’s Critical Factors Model of training transfer, which identifies three core interacting components: trainee characteristics, training design, and work environment. In alignment with this framework, our findings demonstrate dynamic, reciprocal interactions across the three dimensions: trainee characteristics (intrinsic motivation, self-efficacy, knowledge transformation) serve as the individual foundation for transfer but are shaped by training quality and workplace conditions; training design (content relevance and feedback quality) provides the knowledge and skills that trainees rely on, yet weak design further undermines motivation and self-efficacy; and the work environment (organizational support, hierarchical culture, and psychological safety) acts as the enabling or constraining context that directly amplifies or weakens trainee motivation and limits the application of learned skills. Together, these three components interact dynamically: a restrictive hierarchical climate reduces trainee autonomy and intrinsic motivation, while inadequate training content and feedback limit preparedness, which in turn lowers confidence and further discourages practice engagement. This integrated interpretation strengthens conceptual coherence and explanatory power, confirming that training transfer barriers arise from the dynamic interplay of individual, structural, and contextual factors as specified by the Critical Factors Model.

At the trainee level, low intrinsic motivation, low self-efficacy, and limited knowledge transformation ability interact to hinder training transfer. Weak intrinsic motivation—stemming from ambiguous professional identity, unclear career planning, and limited autonomy—acts as a core barrier, while the gap between idealized expectations and clinical reality further reduces motivation through emotional exhaustion. These findings align with Self-Determination Theory, which emphasizes that unmet needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness undermine intrinsic motivation [20, 21]. Low self-efficacy leads to anxiety, avoidance, and hesitation in clinical practice, as supported by Social Cognitive Theory, which links low self-efficacy to heightened risk aversion and reduced persistence [22, 23]. Furthermore, limited knowledge translation ability reflects insufficient clinical reasoning, particularly a lack of conditional knowledge. Although trainees possess foundational declarative and procedural knowledge, they lack strategic understanding of when and why to apply such knowledge, which hinders clinical problem-solving, information integration, and decision-making, and weakens the connection between classroom learning and bedside practice.

Training design barriers included a theory–practice gap and insufficient feedback. Outdated educational content and a mismatch between training and real-world clinical complexity reduce perceived relevance and weaken transfer willingness [24]. Meanwhile, assessment systems overemphasize technical procedures while neglecting comprehensive clinical ability, distracting trainees from developing adaptive competence. Inadequate, nonspecific feedback disrupts the cycle of practice, reflection, and improvement and fails to enhance self-efficacy [25]. Heavy clinical workloads further limit supervisors’ time and quality of guidance, trapping trainees in unreflective practice and preventing the integration of experience into competence [26, 27]. Wide variability in preceptors’ clinical and teaching skills also introduces inconsistency in learning quality and transfer success.

Workplace barriers included insufficient organizational support and a rigid hierarchical culture. Institutional deficits, limited managerial attention, unclear career pathways, and cost-driven workflow constraints hindered training transfer [28, 29]. Hierarchical attitudes and punitive internship culture represented the most profound barriers [30, 31]. Hierarchical norms suppressed trainee autonomy and psychological safety, fostering fear, risk aversion, silence, and avoidance, which trapped trainees in a self-reinforcing cycle of low expectations and limited learning. Furthermore, based on the Critical Factors Model, hierarchical culture actively diminished intrinsic motivation by reducing trainees’ sense of competence, autonomy, and relatedness—core psychological needs that support motivational resources. This interactive pathway illustrates how workplace contextual factors shape trainee characteristics, deepening the synthesis of the model beyond descriptive summary. In the Chinese context, deference to authority further constrained students’ autonomy and critical thinking, while punitive feedback reinforced low psychological safety and a cycle of avoidance.

In summary, this study’s findings on barriers to training transfer—including low trainee motivation and self-efficacy, theory-practice disconnection, insufficient feedback, inadequate organizational support, and a restrictive hierarchical culture — directly inform targeted, multi-level interventions. For student nurses, interventions should address weak intrinsic motivation, low self-efficacy, and rigid knowledge application identified in the data, using career planning, success logging, reflective learning, and a “Golden Idea Award” to encourage initiative, rebuild confidence, and improve the translation of classroom knowledge to clinical practice. For nursing educators, interventions should target the theory-practice gap and lack of contextualized training revealed in this study, using simulation and case-based learning to strengthen critical thinking and conditional knowledge, while revising assessment to align with real-world clinical needs. For clinical preceptors, interventions should address excessive workload, limited mentoring time, and variable teaching quality identified as key barriers, by reducing caseloads, allocating dedicated teaching time, and standardizing feedback practices to foster reflective learning. For hospital administrators, policies should tackle insufficient organizational support and unclear career pathways found in this study, by establishing structured transfer support systems, standardized competency progression, and real-time feedback mechanisms. Finally, to mitigate the negative impact of hierarchical and punitive culture on trainee autonomy and psychological safety demonstrated in the findings, clinical settings should promote relational supervision, flatten hierarchical barriers, and encourage trainee voice. These evidence-based, mechanism-informed recommendations strengthen the policy and practical applicability of the study by directly linking interventions to the identified barriers and their underlying motivational and contextual mechanisms.

Limitations

Grounded in the Critical Factors Model of training transfer and situated within the Chinese nursing education context, this study empirically identifies specific barriers to training transfer among student nurses across three dimensions: trainee characteristics, training design, and work environment. These barriers include weak intrinsic motivation, low self-efficacy, inadequate knowledge transformation, a disconnect between training content and clinical practice, insufficient feedback, lack of organizational support, hierarchical culture, and internship culture. In doing so, the study contributes to and extends training transfer theory. However, it does not provide quantitative data or statistical inferences, causal effect sizes among the barriers, evidence of intervention effectiveness, cross-cultural or cross-sample generalizable conclusions, or longitudinal assessments of transfer outcomes over time. Notably, during thematic analysis, we explored potential subtle differences between bachelor’s degree and diploma nursing students. Although no distinct or thematically meaningful divergences emerged in the core barriers to training transfer, some nuanced variations were observed. Bachelor’s students tended to express slightly stronger concerns regarding theory-practice disconnection and professional identity development. In contrast, diploma students more frequently emphasized practical skill execution, workload adaptation, and immediate clinical task performance. These subtle distinctions were not prominent enough to form separate themes or alter the overall interpretive framework. Thus, while educational background may shape the emphasis of perceived challenges, the fundamental barriers to training transfer were consistent across both groups. Moreover, the study data were primarily derived from female participants with a bachelor’s degree, which may limit the applicability of the findings to male nursing students and those with other educational backgrounds.

Conclusion

This study provides empirical insights into and extends training transfer theory within Chinese nursing education, highlighting the impact of motivation, self-efficacy, knowledge conversion, training design, support, hierarchy, and clinical culture on outcomes. Improved training transfer for nursing trainees requires a systematic, multi-level intervention strategy.
