Lyapunov Unstable Motion Bifurcating from a Circular Vortex Filament
Masashi Aiki, Mitsuo Higaki

Abstract
This paper investigates the dynamics of closed vortex filaments in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} governed by the Localized Induction Equation. Recently, Aiki and Higaki [ 2 ] established the nonlinear orbital stability of circular vortex filaments under asymmetric perturbations, while identifying Lyapunov instability due to the linear growth of translation modes. Motivated by this result, we prove the existence of a family of closed solutions, which we call axial screw motions, that bifurcate from a circular filament. These solutions remain uniformly close to the orbit of the circle, but drift secularly away from the reference motion because their translation speed along the symmetry axis differs from that of the circular filament. In particular, they provide explicit non-trivial perturbations that satisfy orbital-stability estimates while failing Lyapunov stability, thereby realizing the gap between orbital stability and Lyapunov stability near the circular filament.

Despite this large body of work, rigorous mathematical results remain comparatively limited. In the framework of the Euler equations, Choi and Jeong

When a vortex ring is sufficiently thin, it is well-known that its motion can be approximated by that of its centerline, namely a circular vortex filament. Generally, a vortex filament is an idealized space curve on which the vorticity of the fluid is concentrated, and the vorticity at each point of the curve is pointed in the direction of the tangent vector of the curve at that point. One of the most fundamental equations modeling the motion of a vortex filament is the Localized Induction Equation, given by

\bm{x}_{t}=\bm{x}_{s}\times\bm{x}_{ss}.

Here,
\bm{x}=\bm{x}(s,t)
is the position vector of the filament in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, parametrized by arc length
s
at time
t
; subscripts denote partial derivatives, and
\times
denotes the exterior product in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

The Localized Induction Equation was derived independently by Da Rios
[
11
]
, Murakami et al.
[
35
]
, and Arms and Hama
[
4

. They derived the equation by approximating the Biot-Savart integral when the vorticity distribution is given as a vortex filament. Although this derivation is somewhat formal, Jerrard and Seis
[

Hence, a detailed analysis of the Localized Induction Equation has possible implications for the analysis of the Euler equations.

To formulate our problem for the circular vortex filament, we fix a radius
R>0
and consider the periodic problem describing the motion of a closed filament, given by

\bm{x}_{t}=\bm{x}_{s}\times\bm{x}_{ss}\quad\text{in}\mkern 9.0mu\mathbb{T}\times(0,\infty).

(LIE)

Here,
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/L\mathbb{Z}
and
L=2\pi R
. The equation (
LIE
) describes the motion of a closed filament with length

. Let
\bm{x}^{R}_{0}
be the stationary circular profile given by

\bm{x}^{R}_{0}(s)=R\cos\Big(\frac{s}{R}\Big)\bm{e}_{1}+R\sin\Big(\frac{s}{R}\Big)\bm{e}_{2},\quad s\in\mathbb{T},

where
\{\bm{e}_{1},\bm{e}_{2},\bm{e}_{3}\}
is the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. We further define
\bm{x}^{R}
by

\bm{x}^{R}(s,t)=\bm{x}^{R}_{0}(s)+\frac{t}{R}\bm{e}_{3},\quad(s,t)\in\mathbb{T}\times[0,\infty).

It is straightforward to check that
\bm{x}^{R}
satisfies (
LIE
). The stability of the circular filament
\bm{x}^{R}
is the main topic of this paper.

Before we proceed further, we first go over related results considering (
LIE

Regarding the stability of circular vortex filaments, Calini, Keith, and Lafortune
[

Jerrard and Smets
[

Their formulation admits integral currents as initial data, enabling them to define the notion of a “generalized binormal curvature flow” and a “weak binormal curvature flow” (binormal curvature flow equation is another name for the Localized Induction Equation). They also proved estimates for weak solutions corresponding to perturbed circular filaments. More specifically, they gave an upper bound for the velocity along the symmetry axis.

Kida
[
29

constructed solutions of the Localized Induction Equation that travel along an axis without changing their shape. These types of solutions rotate around the axis and exhibit slipping motion along the tangent vector at a constant speed. Garcia and Vega
[

[
29
]

To state the main theorem, we recall the notion of
orbital stability
used in
[
2
]
. Define the manifold
\Sigma
as the set obtained from
\bm{x}^{R}_{0}
by rotations around the
\bm{e}_{3}
-axis and translations:

\Sigma=\{Q^{\alpha}_{z}\bm{x}^{R}_{0}(\cdot)+\tau~|~\alpha\in\mathbb{R},\mkern 9.0mu\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\}\subset H^{2}(\mathbb{T})^{3},

(1.1)

where
Q^{\alpha}_{z}
denotes the rotation matrix around the
\bm{e}_{3}
-axis through angle
\alpha
. We then define the distance between a solution
\bm{x}(s,t)
of (
LIE
) and
\Sigma
by

\begin{split}\operatorname{dist}(\bm{x}(t),\Sigma)&=\inf_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R},\,\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}\|\bm{x}(t)-(Q^{\alpha}_{z}\bm{x}^{R}_{0}+\tau)\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{T})}.\end{split}

(1.2)

In a previous paper
[
2
]
, we proved the orbital stability of the circular vortex filament
\bm{x}^{R}
with respect to the distance (
1.2
). More precisely, under suitable smallness assumptions on the initial perturbation, a solution of (
LIE
) remains close to the manifold
\Sigma
, that is, to the circular filament up to rotations around the symmetry axis and translations. This notion should be distinguished from
Lyapunov stability
of the representative
\bm{x}^{R}(t)
itself, which would require the perturbed solution to stay close to
\bm{x}^{R}(t)
for all
t\geq 0
without quotienting by symmetries; see also
[
1
]
for further discussion.

The goal of this paper is to make this distinction concrete. We construct solutions of (
LIE
) that remain uniformly close to
\Sigma
for all time, yet exhibit secular drift relative to the representative motion
\bm{x}^{R}(t)
. Notice that tilting the axis of symmetry of
\bm{x}^{R}
by a small angle achieves this, but we are interested in the existence of such solutions that have a different shape than a circle, and have the same axis of symmetry as
\bm{x}^{R}
. This is achieved by considering axial screw motions, which we define below.

Definition 1.1

A solution
\bm{x}
of (
LIE
) is called an axial screw motion if there exist an angular velocity
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}
,
\bm{y}\in H^{4}(\mathbb{T})^{3}
with
|\bm{y}_{s}|=1
on
\mathbb{T}
, and
c,V\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\bm{x}(s,t)=Q^{\Omega t}_{z}\bm{y}(s+ct)+Vt\bm{e}_{3},\quad(s,t)\in\mathbb{T}\times[0,\infty).

(1.3)

Remark 1.2

For any
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}
, the circular filament can be represented in the screw ansatz as

\bm{x}^{R}(s,t)=Q^{\Omega t}_{z}\bm{x}^{R}_{0}(s-R\Omega t)+\frac{t}{R}\bm{e}_{3},\quad(s,t)\in\mathbb{T}\times[0,\infty).

Remark 1.3

The choice of the function space
H^{4}(\mathbb{T})
in the definition of the axial screw motion is due to the fact that the Sobolev space
H^{4}
is the least regular Sobolev space for which the unique time-global existence of a solution for the initial-value problem is known for the Localized Induction Equation. This solvability is proved in
[
44
]
.

Remark 1.4

From a physical point of view, an axial screw motion is a closed filament whose shape remains unchanged in time: it rotates about the
\bm{e}_{3}
-axis with constant angular velocity
\Omega
, translates along the
\bm{e}_{3}
-axis with constant speed
V
, and undergoes a constant tangential slip with velocity
c
. The last motion only changes the parameterization along the filament and does not affect the shape itself.

We also mention that because we impose the condition
|\bm{y}_{s}|=1
, any axial screw motion can be considered as a perturbation of
\bm{x}^{R}
, since they both have the same length and arc length parameterization. This is a subtle but important point because (
LIE
) conserves arc length parameterization and hence, when considering the stability of the circular filament, only perturbations that preserve the arc length parameterization are admissible.

Our main theorem below states that, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
with
k\geq 2
, there exists a family of axial screw motions
\{\bm{x}^{(\lambda)}\}_{\lambda}
that emerges from the circular vortex filament through Lyapunov-unstable perturbations. In particular, there are infinitely many such families.

Theorem 1.5

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
satisfy
k\geq 2
. Define the critical angular velocity

\Omega_{k}
by

\Omega_{k}=\frac{\sqrt{k^{2}-1}}{R^{2}}.

(1.4)

Then there exist
\varepsilon_{k}>0
and a smooth mapping

(-\varepsilon_{k},\varepsilon_{k})\ni\lambda\mapsto(\Omega^{\lambda},\bm{y}^{\lambda},c^{\lambda},V^{\lambda})\in\mathbb{R}\times H^{4}(\mathbb{T})^{3}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}

with

(\Omega^{0},\bm{y}^{0},c^{0},V^{0})=(\Omega_{k},\bm{x}^{R}_{0},-R\Omega_{k},1/R)

such that
(\Omega^{\lambda},\bm{y}^{\lambda},c^{\lambda},V^{\lambda})
defines an axial screw motion
\bm{x}^{(\lambda)}
by (
1.3
) for each
\lambda\in(-\varepsilon_{k},\varepsilon_{k})
. Moreover, the family
\{\bm{x}^{(\lambda)}\}_{\lambda\in(-\varepsilon_{k},\varepsilon_{k})}
satisfies the following properties.

(1)

There exists
C>0
such that, for any
\lambda\in(-\varepsilon_{k},\varepsilon_{k})
,

\operatorname{dist}(\bm{x}^{(\lambda)}(t),\Sigma)\leq C|\lambda|,\quad t\geq 0.

(2)

For any
\lambda\neq 0
, there exist
\gamma,t_{0}>0
such that

|\bm{x}^{(\lambda)}(s,t)-\bm{x}^{R}(s,t)|\geq\gamma t,\quad(s,t)\in\mathbb{T}\times[t_{0},\infty).

This drift is caused by the difference in velocity along the axis of symmetry. In fact, the axial screw motion
\bm{x}^{(\lambda)}(t)
moves strictly more slowly along the axis of symmetry than
\bm{x}^{R}(t)
. More precisely, when
\lambda\to 0
,

V^{\lambda}=\frac{1}{R}-\left(\frac{k^{2}(k^{2}-1)}{2R^{3}}\right)\lambda^{2}+o(\lambda^{2}).

We make a few remarks.

Remark 1.6

The exact same statement as Theorem
1.5
holds with
\Omega_{k}
replaced by
-\Omega_{k}
. This amounts to considering an axial screw motion which is rotating around the axis of symmetry in the opposite direction. Other than that, the dynamics are the same. The proof for the case
-\Omega_{k}
is exactly the same as the proof of Theorem
1.5
, and so we omit the details.

Remark 1.7

The main ingredient to prove Theorem
1.5
is the local bifurcation theory given by Crandall and Rabinowitz
[
10
]
. Moreover, from Theorem
1.5
(
1
) and (
2
), we see that all the axial screw motions on the bifurcation branches provide non-trivial perturbations of the circular filament
\bm{x}^{R}
which remain close to the manifold
\Sigma
for all time, but drift away from the representative
\bm{x}^{R}(t)
as time evolves. In this sense, these solutions satisfy an orbital-stability-type estimate, but not a Lyapunov-stability-type estimate.

Remark 1.8

We compare Theorem
1.5
with the results in Kida
[
29
]
and Garcia and Vega
[
19
]
.

1.

Kida
[
29
]
constructed solutions of the Localized Induction Equation under an ansatz closely related to our axial screw motion and derived explicit formulas for the profile in terms of elliptic integrals. His construction produces a rich family of filaments moving without change of form. However, his solutions are not formulated as closed constant speed perturbations of a circular filament. In fact, the general solutions in
[
29
]
are not closed, and they yield closed filaments only for special choices of the parameters. By contrast, in the present paper, the relevant class of perturbations must consist of closed filaments with the same total length as the reference circle and parametrized by arc length. Therefore, his results are not directly applicable when considering the stability of the circular filament in this paper.

2.

Garcia and Vega
[
19
]
also constructed non-trivial solutions bifurcating from a circular filament by utilizing the bifurcation theory of Crandall and Rabinowitz
[
10
]
. The crucial difference from our paper is that they formulate the problem for the tangent vector of the vortex filament. This is possible because by taking the derivative with respect to
s
in (
LIE
), we obtain a closed equation for the tangent vector
\bm{x}_{s}
. They make an ansatz for the tangent vector, which is equivalent to the
s
derivative of our axial screw motion, and apply the bifurcation theory to obtain a bifurcation branch of non-trivial solutions. However, since the analysis is carried out for the tangent vector, information on the dynamics of the filament that is independent of
s
is lost, most notably the traveling velocity along the axis of symmetry. Therefore, their analysis does not directly distinguish between a filament that stays close to the representative motion
\bm{x}^{R}(t)
and one that remains close only modulo translations. This is precisely the distinction between Lyapunov stability and orbital stability that plays a central role in our theorem.

In summary, both
[
29
]
and
[
19
]
establish the existence of non-trivial solutions of (
LIE
), but they do not directly address either orbital or Lyapunov stability of circular filaments. This is the point clarified by our main theorem.

Properties of Axial Screw Motions

Lemma 2.1

If
\bm{x}
is an axial screw motion, then

\operatorname{dist}(\bm{x}(t),\Sigma)=\operatorname{dist}(\bm{y},\Sigma),\quad t\geq 0.

Proof.

Let
\bm{x}
be given by (
1.3
). By (
1.2
),

\operatorname{dist}(\bm{x}(t),\Sigma)=\inf_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R},\,\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}\|Q^{\Omega t}_{z}\bm{y}(\cdot+ct)+Vt\bm{e}_{3}-(Q^{\alpha}_{z}\bm{x}^{R}_{0}+\tau)\|_{2}.

Since
\tau
ranges over all
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we may absorb
Vt\bm{e}_{3}
into
\tau
and obtain

\operatorname{dist}(\bm{x}(t),\Sigma)=\inf_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R},\,\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}\|Q^{\Omega t}_{z}\bm{y}(\cdot+ct)-(Q^{\alpha}_{z}\bm{x}^{R}_{0}+\tau)\|_{2}.

Using that
Q^{\Omega t}_{z}
is orthogonal and
\Sigma
is invariant under
Q^{\Omega t}_{z}
,

\operatorname{dist}(\bm{x}(t),\Sigma)=\inf_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R},\,\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}\|\bm{y}(\cdot+ct)-(Q^{\alpha}_{z}\bm{x}^{R}_{0}+\tau)\|_{2}.

Finally, since
\bm{x}^{R}_{0}(s^{\prime}-ct)=Q^{-ct/R}_{z}\bm{x}^{R}_{0}(s^{\prime})
, we have for each
\alpha,\tau
,

\|\bm{y}(\cdot+ct)-(Q^{\alpha}_{z}\bm{x}^{R}_{0}+\tau)\|_{2}=\|\bm{y}-(Q^{\alpha-ct/R}_{z}\bm{x}^{R}_{0}+\tau)\|_{2}.

Taking the infimum over
\alpha,\tau
implies the assertion.
∎

Lemma 2.2

Let
\Omega,c,V\in\mathbb{R}
and
\bm{y}\in H^{4}(\mathbb{T})^{3}
, and define

\bm{x}(s,t)=Q^{\Omega t}_{z}\bm{y}(s+ct)+Vt\,\bm{e}_{3}.

Then
\bm{x}
solves (
LIE
) if and only if
\bm{y}
satisfies

\bm{y}_{s}\times\bm{y}_{ss}=c\bm{y}_{s}+\Omega\bm{e}_{3}\times\bm{y}+V\bm{e}_{3},\quad s\in\mathbb{T}.

(2.1)

Proof.

Trivially,

\bm{x}_{s}(s,t)=Q^{\Omega t}_{z}\,\bm{y}_{s}(s+ct),\qquad\bm{x}_{ss}(s,t)=Q^{\Omega t}_{z}\,\bm{y}_{ss}(s+ct).

Moreover,

\bm{x}_{t}(s,t)=\Omega\bm{e}_{3}\times(Q^{\Omega t}_{z}\bm{y}(s+ct))+Q^{\Omega t}_{z}(c\bm{y}_{s}(s+ct))+V\bm{e}_{3}.

Since
Q^{\alpha}_{z}
preserves exterior products, we have

(\bm{x}_{s}\times\bm{x}_{ss})(s,t)=Q^{\Omega t}_{z}(\bm{y}_{s}\times\bm{y}_{ss})(s+ct).

Thus
\bm{x}_{t}=\bm{x}_{s}\times\bm{x}_{ss}
holds if and only if

Q^{\Omega t}_{z}(\bm{y}_{s}\times\bm{y}_{ss})(s+ct)=\Omega\bm{e}_{3}\times(Q^{\Omega t}_{z}\bm{y}(s+ct))+Q^{\Omega t}_{z}(c\bm{y}_{s}(s+ct))+V\bm{e}_{3}.

Multiplying both sides by
(Q^{\Omega t}_{z})^{-1}
and using
(Q^{\Omega t}_{z})^{-1}\bm{e}_{3}=\bm{e}_{3}

yields (
2.1
).
∎

From here on, with a slight abuse of notation, we denote
s
as the independent variable of
\bm{y}
and hence, we regard
\bm{y}:\mathbb{T}\ni s\mapsto\bm{y}(s)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Proposition 2.3

Let
\bm{y}\in H^{4}(\mathbb{T})^{3}
. Then the following hold.

(1)

If
\bm{y}
satisfies (

) and

, then

(2.2)

(2)

If
\bm{y}
satisfies (
2.2
) and

, then

g(s)=-\bm{y}_{s}(s)\cdot(\Omega\bm{e}_{3}\times\bm{y}(s)+V\bm{e}_{3}),\quad s\in\mathbb{T},

and

) with the constant

.

Suppose
\bm{y}
satisfies (

) and

. Note that

\displaystyle\bm{y}_{s}\cdot\bm{y}_{ss}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\bm{y}_{s}\times(\bm{y}_{s}\times\bm{y}_{ss})

\displaystyle=-\bm{y}_{ss},

follows from
|\bm{y}_{s}|^{2}=1
. Hence, taking the exterior product of both sides of equation (

) with
\bm{y}_{s}
from the left yields (

). This proves (

) of Proposition

.

Now, suppose
\bm{y}
satisfies (
2.2
) and

. Taking the derivative of

with respect to

yields

\displaystyle g_{s}(s)=-\bm{y}_{ss}(s)\cdot(\Omega\bm{e}_{3}\times\bm{y}(s)+V\bm{e}_{3}).

Substituting (

) for

in the above equation shows that

. We denote this constant by

, and rewrite the right-hand side of (

3
Proof of Theorem
1.5

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.5
by applying the bifurcation theorem of Crandall and Rabinowitz
[
10
]
. More precisely, we first rewrite (
2.2
) as a perturbation system around the circular profile
\bm{x}^{R}_{0}
. We then use the implicit function theorem to eliminate the auxiliary variables and obtain a reduced equation for the essential variables. After analyzing the linearized operator at the trivial branch, we construct a smooth local branch of non-trivial axial screw motions bifurcating from the circular filament
\bm{x}^{R}
.

3.1
The Perturbation System

Substituting the ansatz

\bm{y}=\bm{x}^{R}_{0}+\bm{z},\qquad V=\frac{1}{R}+\delta V

(3.1)

into the unified equation (
2.2
), we have

\begin{split}\bm{0}&=-\bm{y}_{ss}-\Omega(\bm{y}_{s}\cdot\bm{y})\bm{e}_{3}+\Omega(\bm{y}_{s}\cdot\bm{e}_{3})\bm{y}-V\bm{y}_{s}\times\bm{e}_{3}\\
&=-\bm{x}^{R}_{0ss}-\bm{z}_{ss}-\Omega\big((\bm{x}^{R}_{0s}+\bm{z}_{s})\cdot(\bm{x}^{R}_{0}+\bm{z})\big)\bm{e}_{3}\\
&\quad+\Omega\big((\bm{x}^{R}_{0s}+\bm{z}_{s})\cdot\bm{e}_{3}\big)(\bm{x}^{R}_{0}+\bm{z})-\Big(\frac{1}{R}+\delta V\Big)(\bm{z}_{s}+\bm{x}^{R}_{0s})\times\bm{e}_{3}.\end{split}

Using

\bm{x}^{R}_{0ss}=-\frac{1}{R^{2}}\bm{x}^{R}_{0},\qquad\bm{x}^{R}_{0s}\cdot\bm{x}^{R}_{0}=0,\qquad\bm{x}^{R}_{0s}\cdot\bm{e}_{3}=0,\qquad\bm{x}^{R}_{0s}\times\bm{e}_{3}=\frac{1}{R}\bm{x}^{R}_{0},

and rewriting the right-hand side, we obtain the equation for
(\delta V,\bm{z})
as

\begin{split}&-\bm{z}_{ss}-\Omega\Big(\bm{z}_{s}\cdot\bm{z}+\frac{\,d}{\,ds}(\bm{z}\cdot\bm{x}^{R}_{0})\Big)\bm{e}_{3}\\[3.0pt]
&+\Omega(\bm{z}_{s}\cdot\bm{e}_{3})(\bm{z}+\bm{x}^{R}_{0})-\Big(\frac{1}{R}+\delta V\Big)\bm{z}_{s}\times\bm{e}_{3}-\delta V\bm{x}^{R}_{0s}\times\bm{e}_{3}=0,\quad s\in\mathbb{T}.\end{split}

(3.2)

Define the unit tangent, normal, and binormal vectors by, respectively,

\bm{t}(s)=\bm{x}^{R}_{0s}(s),\qquad\bm{n}(s)=-\frac{1}{R}\bm{x}^{R}_{0}(s),\qquad\bm{b}=\bm{e}_{3}.

(3.3)

Then the Frenet-Serret formulas are given by

\bm{t}_{s}=\frac{1}{R}\bm{n},\qquad\bm{n}_{s}=-\frac{1}{R}\bm{t},\qquad\bm{b}_{s}=\bm{0}.

(3.4)

Expand
\bm{z}
with respect to
\{\bm{t},\bm{n},\bm{b}\}
as

\bm{z}=u\bm{t}+v\bm{n}+w\bm{b}.

(3.5)

Observe from (
3.4
) that

\begin{split}\bm{z}_{s}&=\Big(u_{s}-\frac{1}{R}v\Big)\bm{t}+\Big(v_{s}+\frac{1}{R}u\Big)\bm{n}+w_{s}\bm{b},\\[3.0pt]
\bm{z}_{ss}&=\Big(u_{ss}-\frac{2}{R}v_{s}-\frac{1}{R^{2}}u\Big)\bm{t}+\Big(v_{ss}+\frac{2}{R}u_{s}-\frac{1}{R^{2}}v\Big)\bm{n}+w_{ss}\bm{b}.\end{split}

Then, using

\bm{z}\cdot\bm{x}^{R}_{0}=\bm{z}\cdot(-R\bm{n})=-Rv,\qquad\bm{z}_{s}\cdot\bm{e}_{3}=\bm{z}_{s}\cdot\bm{b}=w_{s},

and the formula following from
\bm{t}\times\bm{b}=-\bm{n}
and
\bm{n}\times\bm{b}=\bm{t}
:

\bm{z}_{s}\times\bm{e}_{3}=\bm{z}_{s}\times\bm{b}=-\Big(u_{s}-\frac{1}{R}v\Big)\bm{n}+\Big(v_{s}+\frac{1}{R}u\Big)\bm{t},

we can express the equation (
3.2
) as

\begin{split}\mathcal{T}(s)\bm{t}(s)+\mathcal{N}(s)\bm{n}(s)+\mathcal{B}(s)\bm{b}=\bm{0}.\end{split}

(3.6)

Here

\begin{split}\mathcal{T}&:=-u_{ss}+\frac{1}{R}v_{s}+\Omega uw_{s}-\delta V\Big(v_{s}+\frac{1}{R}u\Big),\\[3.0pt]
\mathcal{N}&:=-v_{ss}-\frac{1}{R}u_{s}-R\Omega w_{s}+\Omega vw_{s}+\delta V\Big(1+u_{s}-\frac{1}{R}v\Big),\\[3.0pt]
\mathcal{B}&:=-w_{ss}+R\Omega v_{s}-\Omega uu_{s}-\Omega vv_{s}.\end{split}

(3.7)

Moreover, by

\bm{y}_{s}=\bm{x}^{R}_{0s}+\bm{z}_{s}=\Big(1+u_{s}-\frac{1}{R}v\Big)\bm{t}+\Big(v_{s}+\frac{1}{R}u\Big)\bm{n}+w_{s}\bm{b},

the condition
|\bm{y}_{s}|^{2}=1
can be expressed as
\mathcal{C}=0
, where

\mathcal{C}:=u_{s}-\frac{1}{R}v+\mathcal{Q},\qquad\mathcal{Q}:=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\Big(u_{s}-\frac{1}{R}v\Big)^{2}+\Big(v_{s}+\frac{1}{R}u\Big)^{2}+w_{s}^{2}\bigg).

(3.8)

Observe that

|\bm{y}_{s}|^{2}=\Big(1+u_{s}-\frac{1}{R}v\Big)^{2}+\Big(v_{s}+\frac{1}{R}u\Big)^{2}+w_{s}^{2}=1+2\mathcal{C}.

Hence, taking the inner product of (
2.2
) with
\bm{y}_{s}
, we obtain

\Big(1+u_{s}-\frac{1}{R}v\Big)\mathcal{T}+\Big(v_{s}+\frac{1}{R}u\Big)\mathcal{N}+w_{s}\mathcal{B}=-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{s}|\bm{y}_{s}|^{2}=-\partial_{s}\mathcal{C}.

(3.9)

Finally, the perturbation system we will address is given by

\mathcal{N}(s)=\mathcal{B}(s)=\mathcal{C}(s)=0,\quad s\in\mathbb{T},

(3.10)

where
\mathcal{N},\mathcal{B}
are defined in (
3.7
) and
\mathcal{C}
is in (
3.8
). We emphasize that the tangential equation
\mathcal{T}=0
is not imposed directly in (
3.10
). Instead, for solutions sufficiently close to the trivial state, we recover
\mathcal{T}=0
a posteriori from (
3.9
), once
\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{C}=0
and a lower bound such as
1+u_{s}-v/R\geq 1/2
are obtained; see Section
3.4
for details.

3.2
Reduction of the System

To apply the local bifurcation theory, we must identify the essential degrees of freedom that govern the nonlinear system (
3.10
). Let us first decompose
v
as

v=v_{0}+v^{\perp},\qquad v_{0}:=\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=\frac{1}{2\pi R}\int_{\mathbb{T}}v,\qquad v^{\perp}:=v-v_{0}.

Then, by varying the angular velocity
\Omega
, we look for critical values where the axial screw motions emerge. In this analysis, the roles of the variables

\delta V,\quad u,\quad v_{0},\quad v^{\perp},\quad w

are understood geometrically as follows:

•

The tangential component
u
corresponds to the reparameterization of the curve, which should be completely determined by the arc length constraint
\mathcal{C}=0
.

•

The constant
v_{0}
corresponds to a trivial expansion of the reference circle, and
\delta V
to a shift in the translational velocity. These constants are expected to balance the averages in the normal constraint
\mathcal{N}=0
and the arc length constraint
\mathcal{C}=0
.

•

The normal component
v^{\perp}
and the binormal component
w
are the genuine profile of the bifurcating motions, and will therefore serve as our independent variables.

With this geometric insight, we will eliminate the variables
\delta V,u,v_{0}
by regarding them as functions of the independent variables
\Omega,v^{\perp},w
. Define the function spaces

\displaystyle X_{\rm odd}

\displaystyle=\{f\in H^{4}(\mathbb{T})~|~\mbox{$f$ is odd}\},

\displaystyle\qquad Y_{\rm odd}

\displaystyle=\{f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{T})~|~\mbox{$f$ is odd}\},

\displaystyle X_{\rm even}

\displaystyle=\{f\in H^{4}(\mathbb{T})~|~\mbox{$f$ is even}\},

\displaystyle\qquad Y_{\rm even}

\displaystyle=\{f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{T})~|~\mbox{$f$ is even}\},

\displaystyle\dot{X}_{\rm even}

\displaystyle=\{f\in X_{\rm even}~|~\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muf\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=0\},

\displaystyle\qquad\dot{Y}_{\rm even}

\displaystyle=\{f\in Y_{\rm even}~|~\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muf\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=0\},

and
Z_{\rm odd},Z_{\rm even},\dot{Z}_{\rm even}

similarly with the

topology. Since
\partial_{s}:X_{\rm odd}\to\dot{Z}_{\rm even}
is an isomorphism, we denote its inverse by
\partial_{s}^{-1}:\dot{Z}_{\rm even}\to X_{\rm odd}
. Moreover, we define

\begin{split}\mathcal{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2},\mathcal{F}_{3}):\mathbb{R}\times\dot{X}_{\rm even}\times X_{\rm odd}\times\mathbb{R}\times X_{\rm odd}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\times\dot{Z}_{\rm even}\times\mathbb{R}\end{split}

(3.11)

by

\begin{split}\mathcal{F}_{1}&=\mathcal{F}_{1}(\Omega,v^{\perp},w,\delta V,u,v_{0})=\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathcal{N}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,\\
\mathcal{F}_{2}&=\mathcal{F}_{2}(\Omega,v^{\perp},w,\delta V,u,v_{0})=\mathcal{C}-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathcal{C}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,\\
\mathcal{F}_{3}&=\mathcal{F}_{3}(\Omega,v^{\perp},w,\delta V,u,v_{0})=\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathcal{C}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu.\end{split}

(3.12)

More explicitly, using
\mathcal{Q}
in (

\begin{split}\mathcal{F}_{1}&=\Omega\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv^{\perp}w_{s}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu+\delta V\Big(1-\frac{1}{R}v_{0}\Big),\\[3.0pt]
\mathcal{F}_{2}&=u_{s}-\frac{1}{R}v^{\perp}+\mathcal{Q}-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathcal{Q}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,\\[3.0pt]
\mathcal{F}_{3}&=-\frac{1}{R}v_{0}+\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathcal{Q}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu.\end{split}

(3.13)

Note that
\mathcal{F}(\Omega,0,0,0,0,0)=0
for any

; see also Remark

Using the function spaces above, we restrict the variables
v^{\perp}
and
w

\dot{X}_{\rm even}
and

Proposition 3.1

For any
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}
, there exist open sets
U,W
:

\begin{split}(\Omega,0,0)&\in U\subset\mathbb{R}\times\dot{X}_{\rm even}\times X_{\rm odd},\\
(0,0,0)&\in W\subset\mathbb{R}\times X_{\rm odd}\times\mathbb{R},\end{split}

and a unique
\varphi:U\to W

such that
\varphi:(\Omega,v^{\perp},w)\mapsto(\delta V,u,v_{0})
and, for
(\Omega,v^{\perp},w)\in U
,

\mathcal{F}(\Omega,v^{\perp},w,\varphi(\Omega,v^{\perp},w))=(0,0,0).

Moreover,
\varphi
is smooth, and its Fréchet derivative with respect to
(v^{\perp},w)
at
(\Omega,0,0)
is

D_{(v^{\perp},w)}\,\varphi(\Omega,0,0)\begin{pmatrix}h_{v^{\perp}}\\
h_{w}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
(1/R)\partial_{s}^{-1}h_{v^{\perp}}\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

(3.14)

Proof.

We apply the implicit function theorem in Banach spaces to
\mathcal{F}
at the point
\Lambda_{0}:=(\Omega,0,0,0,0,0)
; see for example
[
12
, Chapter 4]
. By the definition of
\mathcal{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2},\mathcal{F}_{3})
in (
3.13
), we calculate the Fréchet derivative as

D_{(\delta V,u,v_{0})}\,\mathcal{F}(\Lambda_{0})=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&\partial_{s}&0\\
0&0&-1/R\end{pmatrix}:\mathbb{R}\times X_{\rm odd}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\times\dot{Z}_{\rm even}\times\mathbb{R}.

Then
D_{(\delta V,u,v_{0})}\,\mathcal{F}(\Lambda_{0})
is an isomorphism since
\partial_{s}:X_{\rm odd}\to\dot{Z}_{\rm even}
is an isomorphism. Hence the implicit function theorem yields the existence of the open sets
U
,
W
,
and the unique smooth map
\varphi:U\to W
satisfying

\mathcal{F}(\Omega,v^{\perp},w,\varphi(\Omega,v^{\perp},w))=(0,0,0),\quad(\Omega,v^{\perp},w)\in U.

To compute the Fréchet derivative of
\varphi
, we differentiate the identity

\mathcal{F}(\Omega,v^{\perp},w,\varphi(\Omega,v^{\perp},w))=(0,0,0)

with respect to
(v^{\perp},w)
to obtain

D_{(v^{\perp},w)}\,\mathcal{F}(\Lambda_{0})+D_{(\delta V,u,v_{0})}\,\mathcal{F}(\Lambda_{0})\circ D_{(v^{\perp},w)}\,\varphi(\Omega,0,0)=0,

and equivalently,

D_{(v^{\perp},w)}\,\varphi(\Omega,0,0)=-\big(D_{(\delta V,u,v_{0})}\,\mathcal{F}(\Lambda_{0})\big)^{-1}\circ D_{(v^{\perp},w)}\,\mathcal{F}(\Lambda_{0}).

Hence the assertion follows from

\big(D_{(\delta V,u,v_{0})}\,\mathcal{F}(\Lambda_{0})\big)^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&\partial_{s}^{-1}&0\\
0&0&-R\end{pmatrix}:\mathbb{R}\times\dot{Z}_{\rm even}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\times X_{\rm odd}\times\mathbb{R}

and

D_{(v^{\perp},w)}\,\mathcal{F}(\Lambda_{0})\begin{pmatrix}h_{v^{\perp}}\\
h_{w}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
-(1/R)h_{v^{\perp}}\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

This completes the proof.
∎

in Proposition

\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2}):U\to\dot{Y}_{\rm even}\times Y_{\rm odd}

(3.15)

\begin{split}\mathcal{G}_{1}(\Omega,v^{\perp},w)&=(\mathcal{N}-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathcal{N}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)(\Omega,v^{\perp},w,\varphi(\Omega,v^{\perp},w)),\\
\mathcal{G}_{2}(\Omega,v^{\perp},w)&=\mathcal{B}(\Omega,v^{\perp},w,\varphi(\Omega,v^{\perp},w)).\end{split}

(3.16)

3.3
The Linearized Operator

For fixed
\Omega
, we define the linearized operator
\mathcal{L}
on
\dot{Y}_{\rm even}\times Y_{\rm odd}
by

\mathcal{L}:=D_{(v^{\perp},w)}\,\mathcal{G}(\Omega,0,0)\big|_{\dot{X}_{\rm even}\times X_{\rm odd}},\qquad D(\mathcal{L})=\dot{X}_{\rm even}\times X_{\rm odd}.

We equip
\dot{Y}_{\rm even}\times Y_{\rm odd}
with the standard
H^{2}
inner product

\bigg\langle\begin{pmatrix}f_{1}\\
f_{2}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}g_{1}\\
g_{2}\end{pmatrix}\bigg\rangle_{H^{2}}:=\sum_{m=1}^{2}\sum_{j=0}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\partial_{s}^{j}f_{m}\,\partial_{s}^{j}g_{m}\,ds.

We will also use the notation
\mathcal{L}_{\Omega}
, instead of
\mathcal{L}
, to denote the linearized operator when we want to emphasize the value of
\Omega
. The explicit form of
\mathcal{L}
is obtained as follows: we expand
\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{B}
in (
3.7
) to the first order. By (
3.14
), we have
u_{s}=(1/R)v^{\perp}
and
\delta V=v_{0}=0
to the first order. Substituting these relations into
\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{B}
, and noting that
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv^{\perp}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=0
, we obtain

\mathcal{L}\begin{pmatrix}v^{\perp}\\
w\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-v^{\perp}_{ss}-(1/R^{2})v^{\perp}-R\Omega w_{s}\\[3.0pt]
-w_{ss}+R\Omega v^{\perp}_{s}\end{pmatrix}.

(3.17)

Let us compute the Fourier representation of
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\Omega}
. If

v^{\perp}(s)=\sum_{l\geq 1}\hat{v}^{\perp}_{l}\cos\Big(\frac{ls}{R}\Big),\qquad w(s)=\sum_{l\geq 1}\hat{w}_{l}\sin\Big(\frac{ls}{R}\Big),

then

\mathcal{L}_{\Omega}\begin{pmatrix}v^{\perp}\\
w\end{pmatrix}=\sum_{l\geq 1}\begin{pmatrix}\big(((l^{2}-1)/R^{2})\hat{v}^{\perp}_{l}-l\Omega\hat{w}_{l}\big)\cos(ls/R)\\[3.0pt]
\big((l^{2}/R^{2})\hat{w}_{l}-l\Omega\hat{v}^{\perp}_{l}\big)\sin(ls/R)\end{pmatrix}.

Thus, on each Fourier mode,
\mathcal{L}_{\Omega}
acts through the matrix

M_{l}(\Omega):=\begin{pmatrix}(l^{2}-1)/R^{2}&-l\Omega\\[3.0pt]
-l\Omega&l^{2}/R^{2}\end{pmatrix},\quad l\geq 1.

Then it is routine to check that
\mathcal{L}
is a self-adjoint operator on
\dot{Y}_{\rm even}\times Y_{\rm odd}
.

To find the non-trivial solutions of
\mathcal{L}\,{}^{t}(v^{\perp}\,w)=0
, we fix
k\geq 1
and consider

\begin{pmatrix}v^{\perp}\\
w\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{v}^{\perp}_{k}\cos(ks/R)\\[3.0pt]
\hat{w}_{k}\sin(ks/R)\end{pmatrix}.

After the substitution, we obtain

M_{k}(\Omega)\begin{pmatrix}\hat{v}^{\perp}_{k}\\
\hat{w}_{k}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}(k^{2}-1)/R^{2}&-k\Omega\\[3.0pt]
-k\Omega&k^{2}/R^{2}\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{v}^{\perp}_{k}\\
\hat{w}_{k}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

A direct computation shows that

\det M_{k}(\Omega)=k^{2}\Big(\frac{k^{2}-1}{R^{4}}-\Omega^{2}\Big),

from which we derive the bifurcation condition

\Omega=\pm\frac{\sqrt{k^{2}-1}}{R^{2}}.

In the following, we focus on the non-negative branch
\sqrt{k^{2}-1}/R^{2}
,
which we denote by
\Omega_{k}
consistently with (
1.4
).
Another direct computation shows that, when
\Omega=\Omega_{k}
,

\det M_{l}(\Omega_{k})=\frac{l^{2}(l^{2}-k^{2})}{R^{4}},\quad l\geq 1,

and that the null space
\operatorname{\mathit{N}}(M_{k}(\Omega_{k}))
is given by

\operatorname{\mathit{N}}(M_{k}(\Omega_{k}))=\operatorname{span}\bigg\{\begin{pmatrix}k\\[3.0pt]
\sqrt{k^{2}-1}\end{pmatrix}\bigg\}.

Hence the non-trivial solutions when
\Omega=\Omega_{k}
are the elements belonging to

\operatorname{\mathit{N}}(\mathcal{L}_{\Omega_{k}})=\operatorname{span}\{\Phi_{k}\},\qquad\Phi_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}(\Phi_{k})_{1}\\[3.0pt]
(\Phi_{k})_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}k\cos(ks/R)\\[3.0pt]
\sqrt{k^{2}-1}\sin(ks/R)\end{pmatrix}.

(3.18)

3.4
End of the Proof

To apply the bifurcation theory by Crandall and Rabinowitz, we need the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2

The following hold.

(1)

\mathcal{L}_{\Omega_{k}}
is a Fredholm operator and there exists
\Phi_{k}
such that
\operatorname{\mathit{N}}(\mathcal{L}_{\Omega_{k}})=\operatorname{span}\{\Phi_{k}\}
.

(2)

\operatorname{codim}\operatorname{\mathit{R}}(\mathcal{L}_{\Omega_{k}})=1
, where

\operatorname{\mathit{R}}(\mathcal{L}_{\Omega_{k}})
is the range of
\mathcal{L}_{\Omega_{k}}
.

(3)

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
satisfy
k\geq 2
. Then

\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\Omega_{k}}\Phi_{k}\notin\operatorname{\mathit{R}}(\mathcal{L}_{\Omega_{k}})
where

\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\Omega_{k}}:=\partial_{\Omega}\mathcal{L}_{\Omega}|_{\Omega=\Omega_{k}}
.

Proof.

For (
1
), the first claim follows from the ellipticity of
\mathcal{L}_{\Omega_{k}}
, and the second one from the results in Section
3.3
. For (
2
), since
\mathcal{L}_{\Omega_{k}}
is self-adjoint on the Hilbert space
\dot{Y}_{\rm even}\times Y_{\rm odd}
endowed with the
H^{2}
inner product and is a Fredholm operator, its range is closed and

\operatorname{\mathit{R}}(\mathcal{L}_{\Omega_{k}})=\operatorname{\mathit{N}}(\mathcal{L}_{\Omega_{k}})^{\perp}.

(3.19)

Thus the claim follows from (
3.18
). For (
3
), thanks to (
3.19
), the claim is equivalent to

\langle\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\Omega_{k}}\Phi_{k},\Phi_{k}\rangle_{H^{2}}\neq 0.

In view of the formula derived from (
3.17
),

\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\Omega_{k}}\begin{pmatrix}v^{\perp}\\
w\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-Rw_{s}\\
Rv^{\perp}_{s}\end{pmatrix}

and we have

\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\Omega_{k}}\Phi_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}-k\sqrt{k^{2}-1}\cos(ks/R)\\[3.0pt]
-k^{2}\sin(ks/R)\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore,

\begin{split}&\langle\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{\Omega_{k}}\Phi_{k},\Phi_{k}\rangle_{H^{2}}\\
&=\sum_{j=0}^{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi R}\partial_{s}^{j}\bigg(-k\sqrt{k^{2}-1}\cos\Big(\frac{ks}{R}\Big)\bigg)\partial_{s}^{j}\bigg(k\cos\Big(\frac{ks}{R}\Big)\bigg)\,ds\\
&\quad+\sum_{j=0}^{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi R}\partial_{s}^{j}\bigg(-k^{2}\sin\Big(\frac{ks}{R}\Big)\bigg)\partial_{s}^{j}\bigg(\sqrt{k^{2}-1}\sin\Big(\frac{ks}{R}\Big)\bigg)\,ds\\
&=-2\pi R\,k^{2}\sqrt{k^{2}-1}\Big(1+\frac{k^{2}}{R^{2}}+\frac{k^{4}}{R^{4}}\Big).\end{split}

Then the right-hand side is nonzero due to
k\geq 2
. Thus the claim follows.
∎

3.26
) gives

c^{0}=-\bm{x}^{R}_{0s}\cdot\Big(\Omega_{k}\bm{e}_{3}\times\bm{x}^{R}_{0}+\frac{1}{R}\bm{e}_{3}\Big)=-R\Omega_{k},

which leads to
\bm{x}^{(0)}=\bm{x}^{R}
.

In the following, we show the properties (
1
) and (
2
). By Lemma
2.1
and
\bm{x}^{R}_{0}\in\Sigma
,

\operatorname{dist}(\bm{x}^{(\lambda)}(t),\Sigma)=\operatorname{dist}(\bm{y}^{\lambda},\Sigma)\leq\|\bm{y}^{\lambda}-\bm{x}^{R}_{0}\|_{2},\quad t\geq 0.

Hence the property (
1
) follows from (
3.24
) combined with (
3.20
)–(
3.23
).

Next we show (
2
). For sufficiently small
\lambda\neq 0
, we have by (
3.13
),

\delta V^{\lambda}\Big(1-\frac{1}{R}v_{0}^{\lambda}\Big)=-\Omega^{\lambda}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv^{\perp,\lambda}w^{\lambda}_{s}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu.

Using (
3.20
)–(
3.23
),

\begin{split}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muv^{\perp,\lambda}w^{\lambda}_{s}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=\lambda^{2}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu(\Phi_{k})_{1}\partial_{s}(\Phi_{k})_{2}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu+o(\lambda^{2}).\end{split}

Then, by the definition of
(\Phi_{k})_{1},(\Phi_{k})_{2}
in (
3.18
),

\begin{split}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu(\Phi_{k})_{1}\partial_{s}(\Phi_{k})_{2}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu&=\frac{1}{2\pi R}\int_{0}^{2\pi R}k\cos\Big(\frac{ks}{R}\Big)\frac{k}{R}\sqrt{k^{2}-1}\cos\Big(\frac{ks}{R}\Big)\,ds\\
&=\frac{k^{2}\sqrt{k^{2}-1}}{2R}.\end{split}

Substituting this back and noting
\Omega_{k}=\sqrt{k^{2}-1}/R^{2}
gives, when
\lambda\to 0
,

\delta V^{\lambda}=-\left(\frac{k^{2}(k^{2}-1)}{2R^{3}}\right)\lambda^{2}+o(\lambda^{2}).

Hence we see from

\bm{x}^{(\lambda)}(s,t)-\bm{x}^{R}(s,t)=\delta V^{\lambda}t\bm{e}_{3}+Q^{\Omega^{\lambda}t}_{z}\bm{y}^{\lambda}(s+c^{\lambda}t)-\bm{x}^{R}_{0}(s)

that, with a suitable choice of
\gamma,t_{0}>0
and possibly after taking
\varepsilon_{k}
smaller,

|\bm{x}^{(\lambda)}(s,t)-\bm{x}^{R}(s,t)|\geq|\delta V^{\lambda}|t-|Q^{\Omega^{\lambda}t}_{z}\bm{y}^{\lambda}(s+c^{\lambda}t)\cdot\bm{e}_{3}|\geq\gamma t,

for any
(s,t)\in\mathbb{T}\times[t_{0},\infty)
. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.5
.

\Box
