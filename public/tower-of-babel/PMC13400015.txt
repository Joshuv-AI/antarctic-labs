Facial Electrical Muscle Stimulation for Soft-tissue Lifting and Symmetry Enhancement
Authors: Kyung-Tae Bae, Anush Galstian, Suyeon Lee, Diala Haykal, Ting Song Lim, Kyu-Ho Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Facial neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES) has been used to support facial muscle recruitment in functional settings; however, evidence for aesthetic applications remains limited. This exploratory case series descriptively documents soft-tissue appearance changes and treatment tolerance following a standardized anatomical treatment approach with individualized stimulation settings targeting the lip elevator complex and orbicularis oculi.Methods:Seven adults (5 women, 2 men; age range 24–39 y) underwent 10 NMES sessions at 1–2 week intervals using a commercially available device (EVE Titan). Bilateral periocular and perioral stimulation was delivered for approximately 20 minutes per side. Standardized frontal and oblique photographs were obtained before treatment and approximately 1 month after the final session using the Janus Pro imaging system.Results:All participants completed the treatment series without interruption and reported no adverse events. Across the 6 participants seeking tone/symmetry enhancement, posttreatment photographs demonstrated subtle qualitative trends, including commissural elevation and improved perioral/periocular tone under standardized imaging. In the participant with functional smile asymmetry, posttreatment photographs suggested improved commissural alignment during smiling.Conclusions:In this small, descriptive case series, targeted facial NMES was feasible and well tolerated, with qualitatively observable photographic changes in soft-tissue appearance and symmetry.

Takeaways

Question: Can targeted facial electrical muscle stimulation (EMS) enhance soft-tissue tone and symmetry through physiological neuromuscular activation?

Findings: In this descriptive case series of 7 adults, 10 sessions of targeted EMS applied to the orbicularis oculi and lip elevator complex produced visible yet natural lifting effects and improved facial balance. All participants completed treatment without adverse events.

Meaning: Facial EMS may serve as a safe, noninvasive adjunct for restoring dynamic facial harmony and soft-tissue support by improving neuromuscular coordination. Further controlled studies with quantitative outcomes are warranted to validate and optimize these preliminary findings.

INTRODUCTION

Facial aging is a multifactorial process involving progressive changes in bone resorption, redistribution of subcutaneous and deep fat, dermal thinning, and altered neuromuscular balance.1,2 These processes together affect midface compactness, the contour of the nasolabial region, and the transition between the lower eyelid and the cheek, often referred to as the lid-cheek junction.3

In this context, midface compactness refers to the perceived vertical and anteroposterior soft-tissue support of the malar region relative to the lower eyelid and nasolabial fold. Such alterations lead to a heavier or tired appearance even in patients with good skin elasticity, highlighting the importance of approaches that address not only the superficial layers but also the functional support of the soft tissues.4 The term “tired appearance” is used here to describe reduced periocular tone and subtle downward displacement of midfacial soft tissue despite preserved skin quality.

Age-related facial changes are not limited to the passive layers of the skin and fat but also involve modifications in mimetic muscle tone and coordination.5 Reduced baseline muscle activity or altered recruitment patterns may contribute to drooping of the oral commissures and loss of periocular tone, thereby accentuating the nasolabial region and the lid-cheek transition.6,7

The levator labii superioris complex (including the alar component and often acting with zygomaticus minor) governs upper-lip elevation and the smile arc, whereas the orbicularis oculi maintains eyelid closure and provides dynamic support to the lower eyelid and midface.8 Subtle enhancement of function and coordination within these muscle groups may contribute to improved expressivity and restore a more elevated resting contour, a concept often referred to as dynamic lifting.9 Building on this rationale, facial electrical muscle stimulation (EMS) aims to increase recruitment efficiency and reeducate underactivated mimetic muscles, providing a noninvasive means to improve soft-tissue positioning and facial symmetry.10

Clinical experience from rehabilitation supports the biological plausibility of this approach: systematic reviews and randomized trials have shown that neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES), when applied for facial nerve dysfunction, can enhance muscle recovery and symmetry, although protocols and study quality vary.10 However, translation of these functional observations to aesthetic applications remains incompletely characterized and warrants cautious interpretation.11 These findings suggest that selective training of underrecruited facial muscles in the healthy aging face may lead to subtle yet consistent improvements in perioral tone, softening of the nasolabial fold, and enhanced smile symmetry.12,13

Despite promising functional data from rehabilitation and preliminary aesthetic observations, robust evidence on targeted EMS for facial rejuvenation remains limited. Carefully documented clinical case series remain essential for defining feasibility, safety, and potential soft-tissue effects before controlled trials can be designed.14 Therefore, the present study aimed to descriptively document qualitative changes in facial soft-tissue tone and symmetry following a standardized anatomical treatment approach with individualized EMS settings, focusing on feasibility, safety, and visible trends rather than definitive efficacy.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design

This study is a descriptive, exploratory case series intended to document feasibility, safety, procedural tolerance, and qualitatively observable photographic appearance changes following a standardized anatomical treatment approach with individualized NMES/EMS stimulation parameters. Given the small sample size (n = 7), heterogeneous population (healthy participants and 1 functional asymmetry case), and the absence of a control/sham group, the study was not designed to test efficacy or to support causal inference. No prespecified primary/secondary endpoints or statistical hypothesis testing were planned.

Participants

Seven adult patients were included: 5 women and 2 men, aged 24–39 years. All participants were free from systemic, dermatologic, and neurological diseases. None had undergone facial surgery, filler, or botulinum toxin treatment within the preceding 6 months. One male participant presented with long-standing functional smile asymmetry without a history of facial trauma, surgery, or neurological disease. The asymmetry had been stable for several years and was most apparent during voluntary smiling. Clinical examination demonstrated symmetrical facial nerve function at rest, with reduced voluntary recruitment of the left lip elevator complex during smiling. No sensory deficits or clinical signs suggestive of facial nerve pathology were observed. Electrophysiological testing was not performed because it was not clinically indicated within the routine aesthetic evaluation, and this exploratory case series protocol did not include neurophysiologic confirmation. The asymmetry was therefore interpreted as most consistent with functional muscle imbalance rather than an overt structural or neurogenic disorder.

Device and EMS Parameters

All procedures were performed using a commercially available facial NMES device ,(EVE Titan, WEERO, Republic of Korea) designed for facial neuromuscular activation. Stimulation parameters were individualized within device-approved ranges to achieve visible yet comfortable muscle contractions, reflecting real-world practice rather than a fixed experimental protocol.

The treatment targeted 2 primary regions: (1) the lip elevator complex (levator labii superioris/zygomaticus major/minor region) and (2) the orbicularis oculi. Each session lasted approximately 20 minutes per side with bilateral electrode placement. In the functional asymmetry case, parameters included a frequency of 3-200 Hz, a voltage of 60–75 V, and a current output of 120–150 mA, adjusted dynamically according to tissue impedance and patient tolerance.

Procedure

Before each session, the skin was cleansed, and a conductive gel was applied to enhance current delivery. Patients were placed in a semi-reclined position to ensure comfort and consistent electrode contact. For the lip elevator complex, surface electrodes were positioned along the nasolabial fold approximately 1–1.5 cm lateral to the alar base, overlying the anatomical course of the levator labii superioris and zygomaticus muscles.

For the orbicularis oculi muscle, electrodes were placed inferior to the infraorbital rim along the pretarsal–preseptal junction, avoiding direct stimulation of the globe. Electrode placement was adjusted bilaterally to ensure symmetrical positioning relative to anatomical landmarks. The stimulation sequence began with the periocular zone, followed by the perioral area on each side of the face.

The intensity of stimulation was gradually increased until visible yet comfortable muscle contractions were observed. Each side was treated for approximately 20 minutes, maintaining symmetrical exposure.

Patients were monitored throughout the procedure for any discomfort, and treatment intensity was adjusted individually to maintain tolerance. No topical anesthesia or premedication was required.

Sessions were performed at 1- to 2-week intervals for a total of 10 treatments. After each procedure, patients were observed for immediate skin reactions or discomfort. No erythema, edema, or pain was reported during or after any session.

In the patient with functional smile asymmetry, bilateral stimulation was performed. However, stimulation intensity and emphasis were preferentially increased on the weaker side to encourage more balanced neuromuscular recruitment and commissural symmetry.

Outcome Evaluation

Baseline and posttreatment assessments were conducted using standardized digital photography under identical lighting and positioning conditions. Facial imaging was performed with the Janus Pro analysis system (PIE Co., Ltd., Seoul, South Korea), which provides reproducible, high-resolution documentation of cutaneous and soft-tissue features.

Each patient was photographed from frontal, left oblique, and right oblique views with a neutral facial expression and eyes closed. Follow-up images were obtained approximately 1 month after completing the 10th NMES session.

Qualitative evaluation focused on observable elevation of the oral commissure, reduction of nasolabial shadow depth, improved periocular tone, and enhanced left–right symmetry of the perioral contour. Comparisons were made between baseline and posttreatment images to document soft-tissue elevation, reduction of nasolabial prominence, and improved balance between the right and left facial sides.

Data Handling and Confidentiality

All clinical data and photographs were de-identified before analysis to ensure patient privacy. Each participant was assigned a numerical study code, and no personally identifiable information was retained.

All electronic files were stored on password-protected, encrypted servers accessible only to the study investigator. Written informed consent for treatment and publication of clinical photographs was obtained from all participants before inclusion. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki.

RESULTS

Participant characteristics are summarized descriptively. Seven adults (5 women, 2 men; age range 24–39 y) completed 10 facial EMS/NMES sessions without interruption. No adverse events such as erythema, edema, persistent pain, or neurological symptoms were reported during or after treatment.

Stimulation settings for the overall cohort were individualized to achieve visible yet comfortable contractions. Across sessions, stimulation intensity was adjusted pragmatically to achieve visible yet comfortable contractions within the manufacturer’s device-approved limits. Because this retrospective workflow did not include uniform session-level archiving of stimulation outputs in an exportable format across participants, cohort-wide ranges or means for voltage/current could not be reliably reconstructed. In the functional asymmetry case, parameters were documented as 3-200 Hz, a voltage of 60–75 V, and a current output of 120–150 mA with tolerance-based adjustment.

Standardized photographic comparison (baseline versus ~1 month after the final session) suggested subtle qualitative appearance changes in the midface, perioral, and periocular regions. Representative baseline and posttreatment photographs of participants seeking tone/symmetry enhancement are shown in Figures 1–3. Among the 6 participants seeking tone/symmetry enhancement, posttreatment photographs commonly showed an apparent trend toward slightly higher resting oral commissure position and improved perioral/periocular tone under the same imaging system and lighting conditions. These observations are descriptive and were not derived from objective measurements.

In the participant with mild functional smile asymmetry, posttreatment photographs during voluntary smiling suggested improved left-sided upper-lip elevation and commissural alignment following the 10-session course.

Procedural tolerance was favorable. Participants commonly reported transient muscle contraction and warmth sensations during stimulation, which resolved immediately after each session. No downtime or postprocedure intervention was required.

DISCUSSION

Facial EMS/NMES is a noninvasive approach intended to modulate facial muscle recruitment and explore functional contributions to soft-tissue appearance. In this descriptive case series, posttreatment photographs suggested qualitatively subtle changes in perioral/periocular tone and symmetry under standardized imaging conditions. However, given the exploratory design, these observations should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Physiological Rationale

The orbicularis oculi and lip elevator complex contribute to periocular support, smile dynamics, and resting commissural position. Repeated stimulation-induced contractions may plausibly support neuromuscular recruitment efficiency and coordination, potentially influencing resting tone and dynamic balance without volumetric alteration. This concept is consistent with broader rehabilitation literature describing NMES-assisted motor unit recruitment and symmetry trends in selected facial dysfunction contexts, although protocols, populations, and outcome measures vary, and direct comparison with aesthetic settings is limited.

Clinical Interpretation and Heterogeneity

This cohort was intentionally small and heterogeneous, combining healthy individuals seeking tone/symmetry enhancement with 1 participant presenting functional smile asymmetry. Such heterogeneity limits external validity and prevents generalization to specific patient populations or aging severities. The functional asymmetry case is best interpreted as an illustrative example of potential dynamic modulation rather than evidence of therapeutic efficacy.

Notably, in the asymmetry case, bilateral stimulation was performed with preferential emphasis on the weaker side. This pragmatic adjustment mirrors real-world practice and may have contributed to the observed trend toward improved commissural alignment during smiling; nevertheless, without objective neuromuscular testing and controls, causality cannot be established.

Comparison With Previous Literature

Although direct comparison is constrained by differences in study design, prior reports in functional and rehabilitation contexts have suggested that NMES may support facial motor activation and symmetry in selected settings. In aesthetic-focused studies, reported outcomes have varied and are often limited by small samples and heterogeneous endpoints. Collectively, the literature supports biological plausibility but underscores the need for standardized protocols and objective outcome measures before clinical efficacy can be concluded.

Limitations and Future Directions

This study has important limitations. First, the sample size was small (n = 7), with no control or sham-treated group, and no prespecified primary or secondary endpoints or statistical analysis. Second, outcomes relied on qualitative photographic comparison without objective measurements, blinded independent assessment, or validated scoring, making the findings vulnerable to bias. Third, although a standardized imaging system was used, mandibular stabilization was not used; minor variations in chin position and facial posture may have influenced apparent midface and perioral relationships across photographs. Fourth, the narrow age range (24–39 y) was selected to reduce confounding from advanced age-related tissue laxity, which limits extrapolation to older populations.

Future studies should incorporate larger cohorts, sham-controlled designs, blinded evaluation by independent assessors, and objective outcome measures such as 3-dimensional imaging, electromyography, standardized photographic scoring systems with clear criteria, and validated patient-reported outcome instruments. Reporting real-world stimulation parameter ranges (and summary statistics) across participants would further improve reproducibility and interpretation.

In addition, session-level stimulation outputs (eg, voltage/current) were not uniformly archived in an exportable format across participants, preventing reliable reporting of cohort-wide parameter ranges or means. Future prospective work should prespecify parameter capture and reporting to improve reproducibility.

CONCLUSIONS

In this small, descriptive case series, targeted facial EMS/NMES was feasible and well tolerated, with qualitatively observable photographic trends in perioral/periocular tone and symmetry under standardized imaging conditions. Because the study lacked controls, objective endpoints, blinded assessment, and statistical testing, the findings should be interpreted as exploratory and not as definitive evidence of efficacy. Controlled studies incorporating standardized scoring, independent evaluation, and objective measurements are required to clarify clinical effectiveness and durability.
