Abstract
Discrete diffusion has become a leading framework for generative modeling in various applications including language, vision, and biology. Existing convergence theory, however, exhibits fundamental limitations. KL-based analyses diverge under singular priors such as the masked distribution, while bounds in total variation (TV) depend on the state space size S S and become vacuous for modern language tasks, where vocabularies contain hundreds of thousands of tokens. We develop a unified adjoint-equation-based framework that establishes dimension-free convergence guarantees in any integral probability metric (IPM). To the best of our knowledge, our bounds are the first to be entirely free of S S and applicable to both masked and uniform priors. Importantly, our theory relies only on a single standard rate-matrix regularity assumption and is compatible with time-inhomogeneous schedules. Four novel techniques drive our improvements: working in the space of observables via adjoint equations rather than directly with probability measures, a regularity analysis that yields bounds on any IPM, a coupling argument that removes S S -dependence under uniform transitions, and a score–marginal cancellation technique that removes S S -dependence under masked transitions. Our framework thus sharply departs from prior analyses and avoids the shortcomings of pathspace-KL and existing TV-based approaches. Beyond convergence bounds, our framework provides a versatile toolkit for further theoretical study of discrete diffusion models.

We summarize our contributions as follows.

•

We establish the first convergence bounds that are
entirely independent of the state space size
S
and applicable to non-fully-supported priors such as the singular masked distribution. These properties are jointly essential for language modeling, the most important application of discrete diffusion, where vocabulary sizes are prohibitively large and masked diffusion is the dominant paradigm.

•

Our framework yields a single unified derivation that
covers all integral probability metrics (IPMs)
. In contrast, existing analyses are typically restricted to one or two specific metrics. Moreover, our results are based on a single standard rate-matrix regularity assumption and naturally accommodate time-inhomogeneous rates. See
Table

1
for detailed comparisons.

•

We work with the adjoint equation (specifically, the Kolmogorov backward equation) in the space of observables
, in contrast to prior analyses that operate directly in the space of probability measures. This dual perspective sharply departs from pathspace-KL and TV-based estimates and is a key enabler of the properties above. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first application of such a technique to discrete diffusion.

•

We introduce a
novel coupling argument
that bounds the prior mismatch under uniform transitions independently of
S
. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first such bound, and the first use of coupling techniques in discrete diffusion.

•

Finally, our theoretical framework extends beyond convergence bounds. It establishes a versatile mathematical toolkit that paves the way for future, broader theoretical analyses of discrete diffusion models.

Related Work

Discrete Diffusion Models.

Discrete diffusion models were first proposed by
40
. A substantial line of work formulates the forward and reverse processes as discrete-time Markov chains over categorical states
(
2
;
18
;
12
)
, with the model trained by optimizing the standard variational lower bound. Within this family, multinomial diffusion
(
18
)
and D3PM
(
2
)
mirror DDPM
(
16
)
in the continuous setting, while DNDM
(
12
)
introduces a non-Markovian reverse process that enables accelerated sampling, corresponding to the role of DDIM
(
41
)
.

In a parallel line of work,
4
formulate discrete diffusion as a continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC). This formulation allows for natural theoretical convergence analysis and enables more flexible numerical inference that is not constrained by the discretization fixed at training time. Importantly, the formulation admits an exact formula for the reverse process, in which the unknown quantity is the ratio of marginal probabilities. This ratio is the discrete analog of the score in continuous diffusion
(
42
)
, and is commonly referred to as the discrete or concrete score
(
29
)
. A major line of work has since focused on learning the discrete score.
29
propose concrete score matching with an
L^{2}
objective,
44
learn it via cross-entropy on conditional marginals, and
28
introduce the score entropy loss, which improves training stability and sample quality.

Building on the CTMC framework, recent work explores alternative formulations for discrete generative modeling
(
50
;
24
)
, and
32
;
48
;
35
propose higher-order, adaptive, or correction-based numerical solvers to accelerate inference. In parallel, a body of CTMC-inspired but simplified models has emerged as a practical alternative. These models, particularly those based on masked diffusion, have shown promising performance on large-scale language and image generation
(
9
;
8
;
39
;
37
;
49
;
31
)
, bridging the gap in accuracy and speed to their autoregressive counterparts.

Convergence Analysis.

4
derive a total variation error bound for the tau-leaping sampling algorithm, but their results rely on strong assumptions and are not directly linked to a practical training objective.

10
perform a convergence analysis on the binary hypercube, deriving an approximation error bound in KL divergence, similar results are also available in
21
.
46
extend this result to general categorical state spaces, also in KL.
34
propose a stochastic integral framework that yields KL-divergence error bounds via path-measure arguments, while
26
establish KL bounds through a differential-inequality argument that avoids path-measure techniques. These analyses all
fundamentally rely on KL-based estimates, which diverge when the prior is singular
. As a result,
their guarantees do not directly apply to masked diffusions
—the dominant paradigm for discrete diffusion language models.

Some recent works have attempted to address this gap.
25
;
14
mix the uniform and singular mask distributions as a surrogate prior, circumventing the divergent KL at the all-mask state. This construction, however, does not apply to true masked diffusions.
13
combine KL-based path-measure estimates with a separate TV control of the initialization mismatch at the fully masked state. More recently,
27
address the KL issue by switching to TV distance for the approximation error.
Nevertheless, all these results depend on the state space size, and the bounds can become vacuous for modern language tasks.

A separate line of theory studies the sampling efficiency of discrete diffusion models, often under idealized score assumptions
11
;
23
; these questions are complementary to, but outside the scope of the present work.

Background

Notation and Preliminaries.

We denote scalars by lowercase letters (
x
) and vectors by boldface lowercase letters (
{\bf x}
). All vectors are assumed to be column vectors unless otherwise specified. We denote the
i
-th entry of a vector
{\bf x}
by
x^{i}
. Specifically, a
d
-dimensional vector is given by
{\bf x}=[x^{1}\dotsm x^{d}]
. We use
\mathbf{x}_{i\to\hat{x}^{i}}
to represent the vector
{\bf x}
with its
i
-th entry replaced by
\hat{x}^{i}
. We denote
{\bf x}^{\backslash i}
as the vector
{\bf x}
with its
i
-th entry removed.

For
N\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
,
[N]
denotes the set
\{1,2,\dotsm,N\}
. We denote
e_{i}
as the
i
-th standard basis vector,
\mathbf{1}_{N}
as the
N
-dimensional vector of all ones, and
I_{N}
as the
N\times N
identity matrix. We use
\delta\{\cdot,\cdot\}
to represent the Kronecker delta function. The Hamming distance between
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the number of indices where the entries differ:
\text{Ham}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=|\{i\in[N]:x^{i}\neq y^{i}\}|
, where
|\cdot|
denotes the cardinality of a set.

We consider data defined on a discrete state space
\mathcal{X}=[N]
, where
N
is the total number of possible states. A probability distribution on this discrete space is characterized by a probability mass vector
p\in\Delta(\mathcal{X})
, representing the probability simplex over the
N
possible states.

Forward Process.

The forward noising process is defined as a continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC) on
\mathcal{X}
over the time interval
[0,T]
, governed by a rate matrix (or infinitesimal generator)
Q_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
. Specifically, the process is initialized at
t=0
with the data distribution
p_{\mathrm{data}}
. The evolution of the marginal distribution
p_{t}
is governed by the
Kolmogorov forward equation (KFE)
, a linear ordinary differential equation (ODE) given by

\frac{dp_{t}}{dt}=Q_{t}^{\top}p_{t},\quad p_{0}=p_{\mathrm{data}},\quad\text{for }t\in[0,T].

(1)

Here,
Q_{t}(x,y)
defines the infinitesimal rate of transitioning from states
x
to
y
. Because
Q_{t}
is constrained to have nonnegative off-diagonal entries and zero row sums, the dynamics (
1
) preserve the total mass of
p_{t}
, ensuring it remains a valid probability distribution throughout the evolution.

There are two common choices for
Q_{t}
. The first choice is the masked (absorbing) rate

\displaystyle\begin{split}(Q_{t}^{\rm masked})^{\top}&=\beta(t)(\mathbf{e}_{m}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}-I_{N})=\beta(t)\begin{bmatrix}-1&0&\cdots&0&0\\
0&-1&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&-1&0\\
1&1&\cdots&1&0\end{bmatrix}.\end{split}

(2)

The second choice is the uniform rate

\displaystyle\begin{split}(Q_{t}^{\rm uniform})^{\top}&=\beta(t)\left(\frac{1}{N}\mathbf{1}_{N}\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}-I_{N}\right)=\beta(t)\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{N}-1&\frac{1}{N}&\cdots&\frac{1}{N}\\
\frac{1}{N}&\frac{1}{N}-1&\cdots&\frac{1}{N}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\frac{1}{N}&\frac{1}{N}&\cdots&\frac{1}{N}-1\end{bmatrix},\end{split}

(3)

where the
N
th entry corresponds to the mask token
m
. Consequently,
p_{t}
approaches a tractable limiting distribution
p_{\mathrm{base}}
: either a Dirac distribution centered on the mask token or the uniform distribution over
\mathcal{X}
, as
t\to\infty
. The ease of sampling from these distributions makes them ideal priors for the reverse generative process.

The dynamics (
1
) can be numerically integrated by discretizing the time horizon into intervals with width
\Delta t
. Specifically, the forward transition kernel from time
t
to
t+\Delta t
is given by

p_{t+\Delta t|t}(y|x)=\delta\{x,y\}+Q_{t}(x,y)\Delta t+o(\Delta t),

(4)

where
\delta\{\cdot,\cdot\}
denotes the Kronecker delta, and
Q_{t}(x,y)
denotes the
(x,y)
-th entry of
Q_{t}
. Numerical simulation of CTMCs can be broadly divided into exact path simulation such as the Gillespie’s algorithm and approximate simulation such as
\tau
-leaping method. We refer to
15
;
7
;
6
;
17
;
1
for a comprehensive review of these methods.

Reverse Process.

The forward process (
1
) admits a reverse-time process, which is also a CTMC
(
19
)
. The reverse process satisfies the reverse-time Kolmogorov equation

-\frac{dp_{t}}{dt}=(Q_{t}^{\leftarrow})^{\top}p_{t},\quad\text{for }t\in[0,T],

(5)

where
Q_{t}^{\leftarrow}
is the reverse-time rate matrix, and

Q_{t}^{\leftarrow}(x,y)=\frac{p_{t}(y)}{p_{t}(x)}Q_{t}(y,x)\quad\text{for }x\neq y,\quad\text{and}\quad Q_{t}^{\leftarrow}(x,x)=-\sum_{y\neq x}Q_{t}^{\leftarrow}(x,y).

(6)

Training Formulation.

The ratios
s_{t}(x):=\left[\frac{p_{t}(y)}{p_{t}(x)}\right]_{y\neq x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}_{\geq 0}
are known as the discrete/concrete score
(
29
)
, which is analogous to the Stein score
\nabla_{x}\log p_{t}
in continuous diffusion models
(
42
)
. We denote the entry corresponding to the state
y
as
s_{t}(x)_{y}:=\frac{p_{t}(y)}{p_{t}(x)}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. In score-based discrete diffusion, the goal is to learn a score estimator

\tilde{s}_{t}(x)\approx s_{t}(x)=\left[\frac{p_{t}(y)}{p_{t}(x)}\right]_{y\neq x},\quad\text{for }t\in[0,T].

(7)

To this end, we consider two standard training losses. The first is a weighted score-matching (WSM) loss
(
29
)

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{WSM}}(s,\tilde{s})=\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{t}(x)}\sum_{y\neq x}w_{t}{(x,y)}\left(s_{t}(x)_{y}-\tilde{s}_{t}(x)_{y}\right)^{2}dt,

(8)

and the second is the score entropy (SE) loss
(
28
)

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SE}}(s,\tilde{s})=\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{t}(x)}\sum_{y\neq x}w_{t}{(x,y)}\left(s_{t}(x)_{y}\log\left(\frac{s_{t}(x)_{y}}{\tilde{s}_{t}(x)_{y}}\right)+\tilde{s}_{t}(x)_{y}-s_{t}(x)_{y}\right)dt,

(9)

where
w_{t}{(x,y)}\geq 0
are weighting coefficients, and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SE}}
is obtained from the Bregman divergence generated by the function
a\mapsto a\log a-a
. Following
28
, we consider the transition weight where
w_{t}{(x,y)}=Q_{t}(y,x)
. This choice parallels the score-matching loss in continuous space, where it is typically weighted by the diffusion coefficient to align with the objective with the evidence lower bound (ELBO)
(
42
)
.

In practice, this training objective is intractable as the ground-truth score depends on the unknown marginal distribution
p_{t}
. To resolve this, one typically uses denoising or implicit score matching, which are tractable objectives that are equivalent to the original loss up to a constant independent of the score estimator
28
.

Integral Probability Metric (IPM).

An IPM is a general class of metrics for measuring the discrepancy between two probability measures
p,\tilde{p}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
. It is defined as

\gamma_{\Psi}(p,\tilde{p})=\sup_{\psi\in\Psi}\left|\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}[\psi(x)]-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\tilde{p}}[\psi(x)]\right|,

(10)

where
\Psi
is a suitable class of admissible functions. Different choices of
\Psi
induce different types of IPMs. In this work, alongside the general IPM formulation (
10
), we also consider specialized IPMs including the total variation (TV) distance, Wasserstein-
1
(
W_{1}
) distance, and maximum mean discrepancy (MMD). These correspond to choosing
\Psi
as
\{\psi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R},\|\psi\|_{\infty}\leq 1\}
,
\{\psi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R},\|\psi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\leq 1\}
, and
\{\psi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R},\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq 1\}
, respectively, where
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}}
is the norm for a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)
\mathcal{H}
. We refer readers to
43
;
33
;
3
for a more detailed overview of IPMs and their properties.

Problem Formulation

The discrete state space is given by
\mathcal{X}=[S]^{d}
, containing sequences that factorize as
\mathbf{x}=[x^{1},\dotsm,x^{d}]
. This setup is common among various applications, including text token sequences
28
, image pixel values
4
, tabular data
20
, chemical molecule graphs
45
, musical notes
4
, DNA sequences
38
, protein sequences
5
, and others.

Forward Noising Process.

The forward process
X=\{X_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq T}
is defined as a continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC) on
\mathcal{X}
. The distribution of
X_{t}
is characterized by a probability mass vector
p_{t}\in\Delta(\mathcal{X})
, representing the probability simplex over the
S^{d}
possible states. This distribution is governed by a Kolmogorov forward equation with rate matrix
Q_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{S^{d}\times S^{d}}
given by

\frac{dp_{t}}{dt}=Q_{t}^{\top}p_{t},\quad p_{0}=p_{\mathrm{data}},\quad\text{for }t\in[0,T].

(11)

Since the dimensions of
Q_{t}
scale exponentially with the sequence length
d
, direct computation or storage of the generator is intractable. In practice,
Q_{t}
is structured to operate on each dimension independently via a token-level rate matrix
Q_{t}^{\mathrm{tok}}\in\mathbb{R}^{S\times S}
. Following standard frameworks
4
;
28
;
46
, the nonzero off-diagonal entries of
Q_{t}
are defined as

Q_{t}({\bf x},\mathbf{x}_{i\to\hat{x}^{i}})=Q_{t}\left([x^{1},\dotsm,x^{i},\dotsm,x^{d}],[x^{1},\dotsm,\hat{x}^{i},\dotsm,x^{d}]\right)=Q_{t}^{\mathrm{tok}}(x^{i},\hat{x}^{i}),

(12)

where
x^{i}\neq\hat{x}^{i}\in[S]
, and
Q_{t}^{\mathrm{tok}}
is either the uniform rate matrix (
3
) or masked rate matrix (
2
). Under these two choices, the asymptotic limit
p_{\mathrm{base}}
is the uniform distribution over
\mathcal{X}
and the Dirac distribution centered on the mask token, respectively.
The structure in (
12
) implies that
Q_{t}
is a Kronecker sum of independent generators, modeling transitions between states at Hamming distance 1 (i.e., states differing by exactly one token).

Under this structure of
Q_{t}
, the forward transition kernel
p_{t|s}({\bf x}_{t}|{\bf x}_{s}):=\Pr(X_{t}={\bf x}_{t}|X_{s}={\bf x}_{s})
factorizes as
p_{t|s}({\bf x}_{t}|{\bf x}_{s})=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d}p^{i}_{t|s}(x_{t}^{i}|x_{s}^{i})
for
0\leq s<t\leq T
. Here,
p^{i}_{t|s}(x_{t}^{i}|x_{s}^{i}):=\Pr(X_{t}^{i}=x_{t}^{i}|X_{s}^{i}=x_{s}^{i})
is the forward transition kernel for the
i
th token governed by rate
Q_{t}^{\mathrm{tok}}
.

We note that the factorization of transition kernel is standard in the discrete diffusion literature, as it is essential for scaling to high-dimensional state spaces for empirical implementation
2
;
4
;
5
;
28
. This assumption is also widely adopted in theoretical analysis
47
;
10
;
46
;
34
. We emphasize that our analytical framework is sufficiently general to be straightforwardly extended to transition rates other than (
12
).

Reverse Denoising Process.

The forward process (
11
) admits a reverse-time (generative) process
governed by the following Kolmogorov equation
(
19
)

-\frac{dp_{t}}{dt}=(Q_{t}^{\leftarrow})^{\top}p_{t},\quad\text{for }t\in[0,T],

(13)

where the reverse rate matrix
Q_{t}^{\leftarrow}
is given by

\displaystyle Q_{t}^{\leftarrow}({\bf x},{\bf y})

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{t}({\bf y})}{p_{t}({\bf x})}Q_{t}({\bf y},{\bf x})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{p_{t}({\bf y})}{p_{t}({\bf x})}\delta\{{\bf x}^{\backslash i},{\bf y}^{\backslash i}\}Q^{\mathrm{tok}}_{t}(y^{i},x^{i})\quad\text{for }{\bf x}\neq{\bf y},\quad\text{and}

(14)

\displaystyle Q_{t}^{\leftarrow}({\bf x},{\bf x})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{{\bf y}\neq{\bf x}}Q_{t}^{\leftarrow}({\bf x},{\bf y}).

In the second step of (
14
), we used the definition of
Q_{t}
(
12
). The Kronecker delta ensures that at most one term in the summation survives—specifically, the term where
{\bf x}
and
{\bf y}
differ by exactly one token. Thus, the reverse rate matrix
Q_{t}^{\leftarrow}
inherits the same sparse structure as the forward rate
Q_{t}
, restricting non-zero transition rates to sequences at Hamming distance 1.

Hence, we consider the discrete score
s_{t}
defined for these sequences

s_{t}({\bf x})_{i\to\hat{x}^{i}}:=\frac{p_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{i\to\hat{x}^{i}})}{p_{t}({\bf x})}\quad\text{for }{\bf x}\in\mathcal{X},\,i\in[d],\,x^{i}\neq\hat{x}^{i}\in[S].

Under the uniform rate transition, the nonzero off-diagonal entries of
Q_{t}^{\leftarrow}
are then given by

Q_{t}^{\leftarrow}({\bf x},\mathbf{x}_{i\to\hat{x}^{i}})=s_{t}({\bf x})_{i\to\hat{x}^{i}}\,Q_{t}^{\mathrm{tok}}(\hat{x}^{i},x^{i})=\frac{\beta(t)}{S}s_{t}({\bf x})_{i\to\hat{x}^{i}},\quad\text{for }i\in[d],\,x^{i}\neq\hat{x}^{i}\in[S].

Similarly, the nonzero off-diagonal entries under the masked rate transition are

Q_{t}^{\leftarrow}({\bf x},\mathbf{x}_{i\to\hat{x}^{i}})=s_{t}({\bf x})_{i\to\hat{x}^{i}}\,Q_{t}^{\mathrm{tok}}(\hat{x}^{i},m)=\beta(t)s_{t}({\bf x})_{i\to\hat{x}^{i}},\quad\text{for }i\in[d],\,x^{i}=m,\,\hat{x}^{i}\neq m.

The score estimator
\tilde{s}:[0,T]\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d(S-1)}
can then be obtained by minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{WSM}}(s,\tilde{s})
defined in (
8
) or
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SE}}(s,\tilde{s})
defined in (
9
).

Approximate Reverse Process.

In practice, the generative process is governed by the approximate reverse-time dynamics

-\frac{d\tilde{p}_{t}}{dt}=(\tilde{Q}_{t}^{\leftarrow})^{\top}\tilde{p}_{t},\quad\tilde{p}_{T}=p_{\rm base}\approx p_{T},\quad\text{for }t\in[0,T],

(15)

where the approximate reverse rate matrix
\tilde{Q}_{t}^{\leftarrow}
is defined by

\tilde{Q}_{t}^{\leftarrow}({\bf x},{\bf y})=\tilde{s}_{t}({\bf x})_{\bf y}Q_{t}({\bf y},{\bf x})\quad\text{for }{\bf x}\neq{\bf y},\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{Q}_{t}^{\leftarrow}({\bf x},{\bf x})=-\sum_{{\bf y}\neq{\bf x}}\tilde{Q}_{t}^{\leftarrow}({\bf x},{\bf y}).

(16)

It is important to note that the approximate reverse process differs from the exact reverse process (
13
) by two key aspects. First, the true reverse rate
Q_{t}^{\leftarrow}
is replaced by the approximate reverse rate
\tilde{Q}_{t}^{\leftarrow}
constructed using the score estimator through (
16
). Second, since the ground truth terminal distribution
p_{T}
is intractable, the prior
p_{\mathrm{base}}
is used as a starting distribution for the reverse process. Here,
\tilde{p}
denotes the marginal distribution of the approximate reverse process.

Error and Adjoint Equations.

Our analysis relies on computing the mismatch between the exact and approximate generative processes throughout time. Defining the error
\lambda_{t}:=p_{t}-\tilde{p}_{t}
and subtracting (
15
) from (
5
), we obtain the
error equation
for
t\in[0,T]
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{d\lambda_{t}}{dt}=(-Q_{t}^{\leftarrow}+\tilde{Q}_{t}^{\leftarrow})^{\top}p_{t}-(\tilde{Q}_{t}^{\leftarrow})^{\top}\lambda_{t},\quad\text{with }\lambda_{T}=p_{T}-p_{\rm base}.\end{split}

(17)

To quantify this discrepancy under any IPM, we extend the duality argument of
30
originally developed for continuous diffusion, by developing new technical tools for the discrete setting. The core idea is to test (
17
) against a function
\psi:[S]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
that satisfies the
adjoint equation
associated with the approximate reverse dynamics (
15
), namely the
Kolmogorov backward equation

\frac{d\phi_{t}}{dt}=\tilde{Q}_{t}^{\leftarrow}\phi_{t},\quad\phi_{0}({\bf x})=\psi({\bf x}),\quad\text{for }t\in[0,T].

(18)

5
Main Results

Recall that any valid rate matrix of a CTMC satisfies
two structural properties by definition:
nonnegative off-diagonal entries
,
Q_{t}({\bf x},{\bf y})\geq 0
for all
{\bf x}\neq{\bf y}
, and
zero row sums
,
\sum_{\bf y}Q_{t}({\bf x},{\bf y})=0
for all
{\bf x}
, which together conserve probability mass. Beyond these structural properties, we impose only the following mild regularity condition to keep our framework as general as possible.

Assumption 1

(Rate-matrix Integrability)
.

t\mapsto Q_{t}({\bf x},{\bf y})
is integrable on
[0,T]
for all
({\bf x},{\bf y}).

Assumption
1
ensures that the transition kernel associated with
Q_{t}
is well-defined on
[0,T]
. Notably, both the structural properties and Assumption
1
are satisfied by virtually all discrete-diffusion-based generative models used in practice, underscoring the broad applicability of our theory.

General IPM Bounds.

Under this mild assumption, we characterize the error of the process through the following general bounds for the IPM, which serves as a unified framework for various specific metrics. Due to space constraints, we defer all proofs to the Appendix.

Theorem 5.1

(IPM Bounds for Score-based Discrete Diffusion)
.

Under Assumption
1
, the integral probability metric (IPM) between the true data distribution
p_{\mathrm{data}}
and the generated data distribution
\tilde{p}_{0}

Case 1 (Masked rate;

(
2

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left|\mathbb{E}_{{\bf x}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}}[\psi({\bf x})]-\mathbb{E}_{{\bf x}\sim\tilde{p}_{0}}[\psi({\bf x})]\right|&\leq 2\|\psi\|_{\infty}\left(de^{-\|\beta\|_{1}}+\sqrt{2d}\sqrt{\|\beta(1-p({\bf m}))\|_{1}}\sqrt{\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SE}}(s,\tilde{s})+\tfrac{2}{3}\,\mathcal{L}_{3}(s,\tilde{s})}\right),\end{split}

Remark 5.2

(Universal IPM Coverage)
.

The unified and universal results in Corollary

6
Conclusion

We introduced a novel adjoint-equation-based framework for the convergence analysis of discrete diffusion models. Our analysis yields the first dimension-independent bounds in any integral probability metric, valid under
singular priors and across uniform and masked transitions.
The framework naturally accommodates time-inhomogeneous schedules. Four novel techniques drive these improvements: use of adjoint equation in the space of observables, a regularity analysis, a coupling argument tailored to uniform transitions, and a score-marginal cancellation technique.
Our framework opens several directions for further theoretical study of discrete diffusion, including sharper sampling complexity bounds, convergence analyses for higher-order and adaptive samplers, and approximation errors from practical implementation.

B.1

Lemma B.1

(Sup-norm Contraction Under CTMC Generators)
.

Suppose
\|\psi\|_{\infty}<\infty
, then the solution to the Kolmogorov backward equation
(
18

\phi_{t}

(28)

Proof.

We provide two proofs here for completeness. The first direction is using a Feynman-Kac representation, which is short and clean. The second direction is to mimic the maximum principle used for parabolic partial differential equations (PDEs), which is our original intuition in deriving this result.

First Direction (Feynman-Kac representation):

The Feynman–Kac representation gives
\phi_{t}({\bf x})=\mathbb{E}^{\bf x}[\psi({\bf Y}_{t})]
, where
({\bf Y}_{s})
is the CTMC with generator
\tilde{Q}_{t}^{\leftarrow}
started at

. Hence
|\phi_{t}({\bf x})|\leq\mathbb{E}^{\bf x}[|\psi({\bf Y}_{t})|]\leq\|\psi\|_{\infty}

Second Direction (Maximum principle):
Recall that the Kolmogorov backward equation is given by

\frac{d\phi_{t}}{dt}=\tilde{Q}_{t}^{\leftarrow}\phi_{t},\quad\phi_{0}({\bf x})=\psi({\bf x}),\quad\text{for }t\in[0,T].

For any

Evaluating its time derivative gives

\displaystyle\frac{d\phi_{t}^{\epsilon}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}(\phi_{t}-\epsilon t\mathbf{1})

\displaystyle=\tilde{Q}_{t}^{\leftarrow}\phi_{t}-\epsilon\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle=\tilde{Q}_{t}^{\leftarrow}\left(\phi^{\epsilon}_{t}+\epsilon t\mathbf{1}\right)-\epsilon\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle=\tilde{Q}_{t}^{\leftarrow}\phi^{\epsilon}_{t}-\epsilon\mathbf{1},

(29)

as
\tilde{Q}_{t}^{\leftarrow}
’s rows sum to 0.

Suppose that
\phi^{\epsilon}_{t}
attains its maximum at
t_{0}\in(0,T]
and
{\bf x}_{0}
, then we have the following two properties:

1.

\frac{d}{dt}\phi_{t_{0}}^{\epsilon}({\bf x}_{0})\geq 0,

(30)

as
\frac{d}{dt}\phi_{t_{0}}^{\epsilon}({\bf x}_{0})=0

and
\frac{d}{dt}\phi_{t_{0}}^{\epsilon}({\bf x}_{0})\geq 0

2.

(31)

\displaystyle[\tilde{Q}_{t_{0}}^{\leftarrow}\phi_{t_{0}}^{\epsilon}]({\bf x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bf y}\neq{\bf x}_{0}}\underbrace{\tilde{Q}_{t_{0}}^{\leftarrow}({\bf x}_{0},{\bf y})}_{\geq 0}\underbrace{\left(\phi_{t_{0}}^{\epsilon}({\bf y})-\phi_{t_{0}}^{\epsilon}({\bf x}_{0})\right)}_{\leq 0,\text{ as $\phi_{t_{0}}^{\epsilon}({\bf x}_{0})$ is max}}\leq 0.

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d}{dt}\phi_{t_{0}}^{\epsilon}({\bf x}_{0}),\quad\text{by~{(\ref{eq:testfunc_deri_nonneg})}}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left[\tilde{Q}_{t}^{\leftarrow}\phi^{\epsilon}_{t}\right]({\bf x}_{0})}_{\leq 0,\text{ by~{(\ref{eq:testfunc_Q_neg})}}}-\epsilon,\quad\text{by~{(\ref{eq:d_phi_epsilon})},}

\displaystyle\leq-\epsilon

\displaystyle<0,

cannot attain its maximum at

\phi^{\epsilon}_{t}

This implies

\|\phi_{t}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\psi\|_{\infty},\quad\text{for all $t\in[0,T]$},

as desired.
∎

Appendix C
Bounding the Prior mismatch term under uniform rate

In this section, we derive, to the best of our knowledge, the first
S
-independent prior mismatch bound under the uniform transition rate in the context of discrete diffusion generative models. Our key novelty is a coupling technique. For better readability, we begin by restating the setup.

Setup.

We consider a forward CTMC on
[S]^{d}
. The dynamics are

non-interacting
: each coordinate (token)
i\in[d]
evolves
independently according to the per-site generator

(Q_{t}^{\rm uniform})^{\top}\;=\;\beta(t)\Big(\tfrac{1}{S}\mathbf{1}_{S}\mathbf{1}_{S}^{\top}-I_{S}\Big)\;=\;\beta(t)\,(\Pi-I),

(41)

where

is the rank-one
projection onto the uniform distribution

.
The joint generator is

\mathcal{L}_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}Q_{t}^{({\rm uniform}),\,i},

(42)

with

Q_{t}^{({\rm uniform}),\,i}
acting on coordinate

. The initial law
is
p_{0}=p_{\rm data}
, the reference law is
\pi^{\otimes d}
. Set

\rho_{T}\;:=\;\exp\!\Big(-\!\int_{0}^{T}\beta(s)\,ds\Big).

(43)

Bounding

reduces to exhibiting

any
coupling with controlled disagreement probability, by the
following classical characterization of total variation.

Lemma C.1

(Coupling characterization of
\mathrm{TV}
;
(
22
, Proposition 4.7)
)
.

For any probability measures
\mu,\nu
on a common Polish space,

\mathrm{TV}(\mu,\nu)\;=\;\min_{\Gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\,\Pr_{(X,Y)\sim\Gamma}\!\big(X\neq Y\big),

where
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is the set of couplings of
\mu
and
\nu
(joint
laws with the prescribed marginals), and the minimum is attained (by
the maximal coupling). Consequently, for any
specific
coupling

\Gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
,

\mathrm{TV}(\mu,\nu)\;\leq\;\Pr_{\Gamma}\!\big(X\neq Y\big).

(44)

Per-site uniformization.

Next, we consider a per-site uniformization representation of
Q^{\mathrm{uniform}}_{t}
. The entries of the per-site generator (
41
) are

Q_{t}^{\rm uniform}(v,w)\;=\;\beta(t)\Big[\tfrac{1}{S}-\mathbf{1}[v=w]\Big]\;=\;\begin{cases}\;\;\;\,\beta(t)/S&w\neq v,\\[3.0pt]
-(S-1)\,\beta(t)/S&w=v,\end{cases}

so the total exit rate from any state

is the same constant

|Q_{t}^{\rm uniform}(v,v)|\;=\;\sum_{w\neq v}Q_{t}^{\rm uniform}(v,w)\;=\;\frac{(S-1)\,\beta(t)}{S}\quad\text{for every }v\in[S].

(46)

Uniformization requires only

\lambda(t)\geq\max_{v}|Q_{t}^{\rm uniform}(v,v)|=(S-1)\beta(t)/S
.
We choose

(47)

P_{\beta}(t)\;:=\;I+\frac{Q_{t}^{\rm uniform}}{\beta(t)}\;=\;I+(\Pi-I)\;=\;\Pi,\qquad P_{\beta}(v,w)=\frac{1}{S}\text{ for all }v,w\in[S].

The transition rule is the rank-one projection:

,
independently of its current state

Lemma C.2

(Uniformized construction)

Case 1: boundary term.

For

,

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}(p_{T},\delta_{\bf m})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\!\sum_{{\bf x}}\!|p_{T}({\bf x})-\delta_{\bf m}({\bf x})|

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\big[\,|p_{T}({\bf m})-1|+\!\sum_{{\bf x}\neq{\bf m}}\!p_{T}({\bf x})\,\big]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\big[(1-p_{T}({\bf m}))+(1-p_{T}({\bf m}))\big]\;=\;1-p_{T}({\bf m}),

using
p_{T}({\bf m})\leq 1
and
\sum_{{\bf x}\neq{\bf m}}p_{T}({\bf x})=1-p_{T}({\bf m})
.

By construction of the forward process, conditional on the initial
sequence
{\bf x}_{0}
, the
d
positions evolve as
independent
CTMCs, each
with the same time-dependent rate
\beta_{t}
and
m
absorbing.
Hence for any non-mask token
v

depends on neither
i
(positions share dynamics) nor
v
(the

\alpha_{T}

p_{\rm data}

p_{\rm data}
-a.e.
{\bf x}_{0}

every coordinate

{\bf x}_{0}

p_{\rm data}
