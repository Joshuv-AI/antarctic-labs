Crotonylation of ACAT1 K181 contributes to radioresistance by impacting lactate metabolism
Authors: Shi Chen, Saiyu Li, Hejiang Guo, Shuaining Gao, Yue Gao, Lehan Ding, Bin Zhao, Kun Zhong, Hua Guan, Rui-Yun Peng, Ping-Kun Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIonizing radiation (IR)-induced alterations in glucose metabolism are closely associated with radioresistance, yet the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Here, we identify a post-translational modification axis in which IR induces general control non-repressible protein 5 (GCN5/KAT2A)-mediated crotonylation of acetyl-CoA acetyltransferase 1 (ACAT1) at lysine 181 (K181), thereby enhancing ACAT1 activity and promoting increased acetylation of pyruvate dehydrogenase E1 subunit alpha (PDHA1) along with its phosphorylation at S293.These changes led to inhibition of PDHA1, impaired pyruvate metabolism, and increased lactate accumulation. Functional studies demonstrated that ACAT1 K181R (a decrotonylation-mimicking mutant) increased radiosensitivity and significantly attenuated IR-induced lactate production. Collectively, these findings reveal an unrecognized mechanism by which radiotherapy reprograms cellular metabolism through the GCN5–ACAT1–PDHA1 axis, linking ACAT1 crotonylation to altered pyruvate metabolism, enhanced lactate production, and subsequent radioresistance. These findings suggest that targeting ACAT1 K181 crotonylation represents a potential therapeutic strategy to improve tumor radiosensitivity.Subject terms: Post-translational modifications, Cancer metabolism

Introduction

Accumulating evidence indicates that reprogrammed glucose metabolism contributes to radioresistance through multiple interconnected mechanisms. First, elevated lactate production leads to its accumulation within the tumor microenvironment, resulting in local acidification that impairs immune effector cell function and suppresses anti-tumor immunity1. Second, glycolysis reinforces cellular antioxidant defenses and DNA repair capacity by supplying reducing equivalents and nucleotide precursors2. Glucose-6-phosphate (G6P), for example, can be channeled into the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) to generate NADPH, which sustains glutathione in its reduced form to neutralize excess reactive oxygen species (ROS) and protect genomic integrity. Simultaneously, PPP-derived ribose-5-phosphate serves as a substrate for nucleotide synthesis, thereby facilitating DNA damage repair2. Beyond these roles, glycolytic metabolites modulate radiosensitivity through protein post-translational modifications. Notably, lactate acts as a donor for lysine lactylation, regulating the lactylation of key DNA damage response proteins—including NBS1 and MRE11—and consequently influencing repair efficiency3,4. Collectively, glycolytic reprogramming and its associated metabolic adaptations not only satisfy the bioenergetic and biosynthetic demands of tumor cells but also confer survival advantages, sustain proliferation, and enhance treatment tolerance by reshaping the immune microenvironment, redox homeostasis, and DNA repair capacity.

Our previous work demonstrated that IR markedly upregulates glycolysis in tumor cells, and that inhibition of glycolysis substantially enhances radiosensitivity5. Consistent with these findings, elevated aerobic glycolysis has been broadly implicated in conferring resistance to cytotoxic stresses, including IR and chemotherapy, while post-irradiation glycolytic blockade effectively re-sensitizes tumor cells to radiation. Furthermore, multiple studies have demonstrated that both IR and chemotherapy induce metabolic reprogramming in tumor cells6–9. Therefore, investigating IR-induced changes in glucose metabolism and their effects on cellular radiosensitivity is crucial for understanding radiotherapy resistance mechanisms and for developing strategies to improve therapeutic efficacy and radioprotection.

Crotonylation is a lysine acylation first identified on histones by Zhao et al.10. Subsequent studies have shown that this modification is highly conserved across species and participates in the regulation of gene expression, cell development, and other cellular processes11,12. Increasing evidence indicates that crotonylation also plays a key role in the DNA damage response (DDR). For instance, DNA damage alters the crotonylation of repair-related proteins, such as RPA1, thereby modulating homologous recombination repair13. Our group previously conducted crotonylation proteomics and found that 8 Gy γ-irradiation markedly changed the crotonylation levels of multiple proteins, including several involved in DNA repair, such as DNA-PKcs14, Ku8015, and cGAS16. IR increased crotonylation at K525 of DNA-PKcs, promoting assembly of the DNA-PK complex and enhancing non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) repair, which contributes to radioresistance14. In contrast, K568 crotonylation of Ku80 decreased after DNA damage and was replaced by SUMOylation, facilitating DNA-PK complex formation and double-strand break (DSB) repair; disruption of Ku80 K568 modification increased radiosensitivity15. In addition, cGAS, a cytosolic DNA sensor, undergoes SIRT3-mediated decrotonylation at K254 following IR, which reduces its DNA-binding affinity and releases its interaction with PARP1, thereby promoting homologous recombination repair16. Together, these findings highlight a close link between crotonylation and DNA repair, underscoring its significance in cellular responses to ionizing radiation.

Given that ionizing radiation (IR) induces alterations in glucose metabolism and crotonylation is closely associated with radiation responses, both of which can modulate radiosensitivity, we further analyzed crotonylation proteomic data following IR treatment. We found that 4 h after 8 Gy γ-irradiation, crotonylation levels of multiple metabolism-related proteins were significantly altered. Among these, ACAT1 drew our attention. It is a mitochondrial enzyme that catalyzes the condensation of two acetyl‑CoA molecules into acetoacetyl‑CoA and CoA, playing a key role in fatty acid β‑oxidation and ketone body biosynthesis17,18. Notably, activated ACAT1 also functions as a lysine acetyltransferase, acetylating proteins, such as PDHA1 and its regulator PDP1, thereby inhibiting pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDC) activity19. Since PDH is a key enzyme controlling pyruvate entry into the tricarboxylic acid cycle, its inhibition shifts pyruvate toward lactate production19. Our crotonylation proteomic analysis revealed that ACAT1 crotonylation was markedly increased in MCF7 cells after 8 Gy γ-irradiation. Based on previous findings that IR affects glucose metabolism and the potential involvement of ACAT1 in metabolic regulation, we hypothesized that IR may modulate metabolic processes through ACAT1 crotonylation. To investigate this hypothesis, we designed and performed a series of experiments, which are detailed in the following sections.

Results

Ionizing radiation induces alterations in cellular glucose metabolism

To investigate the effects of IR on cellular glucose metabolism, we conducted a series of experiments. Glucose consumption was first measured to assess the impact of ionizing radiation, and the results showed that 8 Gy IR significantly increased glucose consumption in HeLa, MCF7, and HepG2 cells (Fig. 1A, B, and Supplementary Fig. A). In addition, key glucose metabolites, including lactate, pyruvate, and ATP, were quantified to further evaluate the effects of IR on glucose metabolism. The results showed that, following 8 Gy IR, intracellular lactate and pyruvate levels progressively increased over time (Fig. 1C–E and Supplementary Fig. B, C), while ATP levels initially decreased and then gradually returned toward baseline (Fig. 1F and Supplementary Fig. D). These findings provide preliminary evidence that ionizing radiation promotes glycolytic metabolism. To further validate this effect, cellular oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) were measured using an O2K analyzer to assess changes in cellular respiration after irradiation. In HeLa cells, both basal and maximal OCR were significantly reduced at 4 h after 8 Gy IR compared with the 0 Gy control (Fig. 1G–I), whereas ECAR under 20 mM glucose and 2 mM oligomycin was markedly increased (Fig. 1J–L). Together, these results suggest that IR induces metabolic alterations characterized by increased glucose utilization, elevated lactate production, enhanced extracellular acidification, and reduced mitochondrial respiration. The initial decrease in ATP levels and reduction in OCR suggest impaired mitochondrial function, while the increased acidification may reflect a compensatory metabolic adaptation to irradiation stress.

Ionizing radiation enhances ACAT1 K181 crotonylation

To elucidate the mechanism underlying IR-induced glucose metabolic alterations, crotonylation proteomics analysis was performed following irradiation. The results revealed that IR broadly altered the crotonylation status of multiple metabolism-related proteins, with 385 proteins exhibited at least a 1.5-fold change, some of which harbored multiple modification sites (Fig. 2A). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further demonstrated that these proteins are enriched in glucose metabolism-related pathways, including carbon metabolism, tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, oxidative phosphorylation, pyruvate metabolism and the pentose phosphate pathway (Fig. 2B). Among the identified glycolysis-associated proteins, ACAT1 displayed the most pronounced increase in crotonylation following 8 Gy irradiation (Fig. 2C).

To validate these proteomics findings, a Flag-tagged ACAT1 construct was generated and overexpressed in HEK293T cells. Flag-ACAT1 was subsequently enriched by co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and analyzed by Western blotting (WB) to assess IR-induced changes in ACAT1 crotonylation. The results showed that ACAT1 crotonylation was significantly elevated at 4 h post-irradiation with 8 Gy (Fig. 2D, E). Given that both crotonylation and acetylation target lysine residues, we also examined ACAT1 acetylation; no significant alteration was detected following IR treatment, indicating that IR specifically promotes ACAT1 crotonylation without affecting its acetylation status (Fig. 2D, E). To further investigate the dose-dependent effect of IR on ACAT1 crotonylation, cells were irradiated with graded doses (1, 2, 4, and 8 Gy) and harvested at 4 h post-irradiation; ACAT1 crotonylation levels increased progressively with escalating radiation doses (Fig. 2F, G). Furthermore, the time-dependent changes in ACAT1 crotonylation following 8 Gy irradiation were examined; ACAT1 crotonylation was observed to increase, peak at 4 h, and then gradually decrease (Fig. 2H, I). Collectively, these results demonstrate that ionizing radiation promotes ACAT1 crotonylation.

Further analysis of the crotonylation proteomics dataset revealed four crotonylation sites on ACAT1—K181, K257, K263, and K268. Among these, the crotonylation level at K181 showed a statistically significant increase following irradiation, suggesting that K181 represents the primary IR-induced crotonylation site on ACAT1 (Fig. 2J, K). To validate this finding, plasmids encoding ACAT1 point mutants (K181R, K257R, K263R, and K268R) were constructed and transiently overexpressed in HEK293T cells. Cells were then exposed to 8 Gy γ-irradiation, followed by Co-IP and western blotting to evaluate the impact of IR on crotonylation at each individual lysine site. The results showed that, among all mutants tested, only the K181R substitution markedly attenuated IR-induced ACAT1 crotonylation, whereas the other mutants exhibited no appreciable effect (Fig. 2L, M). Collectively, these findings indicate that ionizing radiation promotes ACAT1 crotonylation predominantly at the lysine residue K181.

GCN5 mediates IR-induced ACAT1 crotonylation

To investigate the mechanism by which IR induces ACAT1 crotonylation, we overexpressed ACAT1 in HEK293T cells together with several candidate crotonyltransferases, including Tip60, CBP, PCAF, GCN5, and P300. Crotonylation levels of ACAT1 were subsequently assessed by Co-IP and WB. The results demonstrated that overexpression of GCN5 markedly elevated ACAT1 crotonylation, suggesting that GCN5 functions as a crotonyltransferase for ACAT1 (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, dose-dependent overexpression of GCN5 resulted in a corresponding increase in ACAT1 crotonylation (Fig. 3B, C). To further substantiate GCN5 as the responsible enzyme, GCN5 was knocked down using siRNA, and the effect of ionizing radiation on ACAT1 crotonylation was subsequently examined. Knockdown of GCN5 was found to significantly attenuate the IR-induced increase in ACAT1 crotonylation (Fig. 3D, E). These results confirm that GCN5 is the enzyme responsible for mediating this modification in response to ionizing radiation.

Furthermore, the role of GCN5 as the crotonyltransferase for ACAT1 was further demonstrated by examining changes in the interaction between GCN5 and ACAT1 following ionizing radiation. Co-IP assays revealed that ionizing radiation enhanced the interaction between ACAT1 and GCN5 (Fig. 3F). This observation was further corroborated by a proximity ligation assay (PLA), which provided more direct visual evidence of the ACAT1–GCN5 interaction. Consistent with the Co-IP results, PLA signals between ACAT1 and GCN5 were markedly increased following 8 Gy irradiation for 2 h (Fig. 3G, H).

To exclude the possibility that GCN5 mediates ACAT1 crotonylation indirectly through other molecules, we performed in vitro crotonylation assays using purified GCN5 and ACAT1 proteins. The results demonstrated that ACAT1 crotonylation occurred only in the presence of GCN5. Moreover, treatment with the GCN5 inhibitor butyrolactone MB-3 (100 μM) significantly reduced ACAT1 crotonylation (Fig. 3I, J). These findings further support GCN5 as the crotonyltransferase for ACAT1.

To investigate the role of GCN5 in ACAT1 K181 crotonylation, GCN5 or GCN5 E575Q (catalytically inactive mutant20) was co-expressed with either ACAT1 WT or the K181R mutant in HeLa cells. The effects of GCN5 and GCN5 E575Q on the crotonylation levels of ACAT1 WT and K181R were then examined. The results showed that GCN5 markedly enhanced the crotonylation of ACAT1 WT, whereas no obvious promotive effect was observed on the ACAT1 K181R mutant. In addition, the GCN5 E575Q mutant failed to enhance the crotonylation of ACAT1 regardless of whether it was co-expressed with ACAT1 WT or K181R (Fig. 3K, L). Collectively, these findings suggest that GCN5 functions as a crotonyltransferase for ACAT1, and that K181 is likely a critical site mediating GCN5-induced crotonylation.

ACAT1 crotonylation is regulated not only by enzymatic activity but may also be influenced by intracellular crotonyl-CoA levels. To exclude the possibility that IR-induced changes in intracellular crotonyl-CoA levels contribute to the observed increase in ACAT1 crotonylation, we measured intracellular crotonyl-CoA content following IR treatment. No significant alteration in crotonyl-CoA abundance was detected (Supplementary Fig. E). In addition, the in vitro crotonylation assay demonstrated that crotonyl-CoA treatment alone had no effect on ACAT1 crotonylation (Fig. 3I, J). Collectively, these results indicate that the ionizing radiation-induced increase in ACAT1 crotonylation is not driven by changes in crotonyl-CoA levels. Taken together, these findings indicate that ionizing radiation promotes ACAT1 crotonylation in a GCN5-dependent manner.

ACAT1 K181 crotonylation regulates PDHA1 acetylation

To understand the mechanism by which IR-induced ACAT1 crotonylation affects glucose metabolism, we reviewed previous studies showing that ACAT1 can function as an acetyltransferase for the pyruvate dehydrogenase E1 subunit alpha (PDHA1). Specifically, ACAT1-mediated acetylation of PDHA1 modulates its phosphorylation at Ser293, thereby altering its enzymatic activity and ultimately affecting the conversion of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA19,21. Based on this knowledge, we hypothesized that ionizing radiation might enhance ACAT1 crotonylation, thereby increasing its catalytic function and promoting PDHA1 acetylation.

To test this hypothesis, ACAT1 enzymatic activity was measured following 8 Gy irradiation. The results revealed that ACAT1 enzymatic activity was significantly increased at 4 h post-irradiation (Fig. 4A). Correspondingly, PDHA1 activity was markedly reduced at 4 h after 8 Gy irradiation (Fig. 4B). This is consistent with our model: radiation-induced ACAT1 crotonylation enhances its acetyltransferase activity, leading to increased PDHA1 acetylation and subsequent functional inhibition. To investigate the impact of ionizing radiation on PDHA1 acetylation, Co-IP and WB assays were performed. The results revealed that PDHA1 acetylation was significantly increased at 4 h following 8 Gy irradiation, and this was accompanied by enhanced interaction between PDHA1 and ACAT1 (Fig. 4C, D). Because previous studies have shown that ACAT1 affects PDHA1 enzymatic activity primarily by modulating its acetylation and subsequently the phosphorylation of Ser29319,21, we also examined PDHA1 Ser293 phosphorylation. The results showed that irradiation significantly promoted PDHA1 Ser293 phosphorylation (Fig. 4C, D). These results demonstrate that radiation-induced ACAT1 crotonylation may enhances its enzymatic activity, leading to increased PDHA1 acetylation, which in turn promotes PDHA1 Ser293 phosphorylation and consequently reduces PDHA1 activity. Additionally, to examine the temporal dynamics of PDHA1 modification, we overexpressed PDHA1 in HEK293T cells and collected samples at 0, 1, 2, 4, 8, and 12 h post-irradiation. The results showed that PDHA1 acetylation significantly increased in 1 h after 8 Gy irradiation (Fig. 4E, F). In addition, to exclude the possibility that ionizing radiation increases PDHA1 acetylation indirectly by elevating intracellular acetyl-CoA levels rather than through ACAT1, we measured intracellular acetyl-CoA levels at 4 h after 8 Gy irradiation. The results showed that ionizing radiation did not significantly increase acetyl-CoA levels (Supplementary Fig. F). These findings suggest that the effect of ionizing radiation on PDHA1 acetylation may be mediated by ACAT1 rather than by changes in intracellular acetyl-CoA levels.

To investigate the effect of ACAT1 K181 on PDHA1 acetylation, we first assessed the functional consequences of mutating lysine residues K181, K257, K263, and K268 to arginine. Notably, the K181R mutation substantially compromised ACAT1 enzymatic activity (Fig. 4G). These results demonstrate K181 as a critical residue for ACAT1 enzymatic function. We then co‑expressed PDHA1 with plasmids encoding ACAT1 WT, K181R (non‑modifiable control), K181Q (acetylation mimic), and K181N (functional mimic of crotonylation22), and examined PDHA1 acetylation levels. Results revealed that ACAT1 WT and K181N markedly promoted PDHA1 acetylation, whereas K181R had no detectable impact; K181Q produced moderate effects that were not statistically significant (Fig. 4H, I).

In addition, we constructed an ACAT1 K181R cell line by stably reconstituting the ACAT1 K181R mutant in ACAT1 KO cells (Supplementary Fig. G–N). First, Co‑IP and WB assays confirmed that K181 is the primary site of radiation‑induced ACAT1 crotonylation (Fig. 4J, K). Subsequently, these cells were used for endogenous Co‑IP and WB assays, which revealed that ACAT1 K181R inhibited PDHA1 acetylation and Ser293 phosphorylation, and significantly suppressed radiation‑induced PDHA1 acetylation and Ser293 phosphorylation (Fig. 4L–N). Together, these results suggest that crotonylation of ACAT1 at K181 is a key factor affecting PDHA1 acetylation.

ACAT1 K181 crotonylation promotes cellular lactate production

Building on the findings described above, the effects of ACAT1 K181R on cellular glucose metabolism were investigated. The O2K energy metabolism analysis system was employed to examine ACAT1 knockout-induced changes in the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) and assess subsequent effects on lactate production. In HeLa cells, ECAR was substantially reduced in the ACAT1 KO group compared with controls following stimulation with 20 mM glucose. Upon oligomycin treatment (2 mM), ECAR was increased in both groups; however, ACAT1 KO cells consistently exhibited lower ECAR than controls (Fig. 5A–C). These findings indicated that ACAT1 is involved in lactate production. To further validate the role of ACAT1 in IR-induced lactate accumulation, lactate levels were quantified using a lactate assay kit. ACAT1 KO markedly reduced intracellular lactate accumulation and substantially attenuated the radiation-induced increase in lactate production (Fig. 5D, E). Collectively, these results demonstrate that ACAT1 is a regulator of lactate production.

To clarify the specific contribution of ACAT1 K181 to lactate production, we reintroduced ACAT1 WT, the K181R mutant, and the K181N mutant into ACAT1 KO HeLa cells and subsequently measured intracellular lactate levels. Re‑expression of ACAT1 WT or the K181N mutant effectively restored the reduced lactate levels caused by ACAT1 knockout. In contrast, re‑expression of the K181R mutant, which resembles the decrotonylated form, failed to reverse the decrease in lactate production (Fig. 5F). Furthermore, using the O2K energy metabolism analyzer in ACAT1 K181R point‑mutant cells, we further demonstrated the impact of the ACAT1 K181 mutation on lactate production. Following stimulation with 20 mM glucose, ACAT1 K181R cells displayed a markedly lower extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) compared with ACAT1 WT cells. Although oligomycin (2 mM) elevated ECAR in both groups, the ECAR of K181R cells consistently remained lower than that of the WT group (Fig. 5G–I). Collectively, these results demonstrate that crotonylation of ACAT1 at K181 is associated with cellular lactate production.

ACAT1 K181 crotonylation modulates tumor radiosensitivity

Previous findings indicated that ionizing radiation promotes lactate production by enhancing crotonylation at the ACAT1 K181 site. Given that glucose metabolism and lactate have been well established to play critical roles in regulating cellular radiosensitivity, this suggests that crotonylation at ACAT1 K181 may regulate radiosensitivity through glucose metabolism. Based on this rationale, we further investigated the role of ACAT1 crotonylation in cellular radiosensitivity.

Apoptosis assays first revealed that ACAT1 knockout and K181R mutant HeLa cells exhibited significantly higher levels of apoptosis following 8 Gy irradiation compared with control (NC) cells (Fig. 6A, B), indicating that ACAT1 deficiency and the K181R mutation enhance cellular radiosensitivity. To further assess this effect, we performed clonogenic survival assays. ACAT1 knockout HeLa and MCF7 cells formed significantly fewer colonies than control cells after exposure to 4 Gy and 8 Gy irradiation, which is consistent with their increased radiosensitivity (Fig. 6C–F). Comparable results were observed in HepG2 cells with conditional ACAT1 knockdown (Supplementary Fig. O–I). Notably, ACAT1 knockout in HeLa cells significantly reduced colony formation ability even without irradiation. In contrast, MCF7 and HepG2 cells did not exhibit a comparable decline. This differential may reflect cell-type-specific differences, suggesting a potential dependency of HeLa cell survival on ACAT1.

To determine the specific contribution of K181 crotonylation, ACAT1 KO cells were separately reconstituted with ACAT1 WT, K181R, and K181N mutants. Restoration of K181N significantly rescued colony formation in ACAT1 KO HeLa cells, whereas K181R re-expression, representing the decrotonylated state, failed to restore colony-forming capacity (Fig. 6G, H). Due to the low transfection efficiency in MCF7 cells, we generated a stable MCF7 cell line expressing ACAT1 K181R for subsequent analysis. Clonogenic assays confirmed that radiosensitivity remained elevated compared with controls (Fig. 6I, J), further supporting that crotonylation at K181 is a critical regulatory site modulating cellular radiosensitivity. Finally, using a nude mouse xenograft tumor model, we found the growth rate of tumors originated from ACAT1 K181R mutant cancer cells decreased; moreover, the tumors were much more sensitive to ionizing radiation (Fig. 6K–M).

Collectively, these results indicate that crotonylation at ACAT1 K181 is closely associated with cellular radiosensitivity. Targeting this post-translational modification may represent a promising strategy to enhance tumor radiosensitivity. Furthermore, these findings highlight the importance of glucose metabolic regulation in radiosensitivity and suggest that targeting glucose metabolic processes may be an effective approach to overcoming radioresistance.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated a molecular mechanism by which ionizing radiation influences glucose metabolism. Our findings indicate that IR enhances ACAT1 crotonylation in a GCN5-dependent manner, thereby modulating its enzymatic activity and downstream metabolic processes. Specifically, ACAT1 crotonylation increases its catalytic activity, which promotes PDHA1 acetylation. This acetylation subsequently enhances phosphorylation at Ser293 of PDHA1, leading to suppressed PDHA1 enzymatic activity. These results provide mechanistic insights into IR-induced metabolic reprogramming and highlight the pivotal role of post-translational modifications (PTMs) in regulating cellular responses to radiation.

Glucose metabolism is a hallmark of metabolic reprogramming in tumor cells, supporting cell proliferation, energy production, and the generation of biosynthetic precursors23. Upregulation of glycolysis enables cells to counteract oxidative stress through multiple mechanisms, including the production of NADPH and glutathione, which scavenge IR-induced reactive oxygen species and thereby mitigate DNA damage24. As a major metabolic end product, lactate plays pivotal roles in modulating cellular radiosensitivity. First, studies have shown that lactate-derived lactylation directly enhances DNA damage repair. For example, lactylation of MRE11 at K673 promotes homologous recombination repair in colorectal cancer and enhances radioresistance4, and lactylation of XRCC1 contributes to radioresistance in glioblastoma25. Second, extracellular lactate accumulation in the tumor microenvironment leads to acidification, which potently suppresses CD8⁺ T cell and natural killer (NK) cell proliferation, thereby compromising the antitumor immune response induced by radiotherapy26,27. Third, intracellular lactate exerts bidirectional effects depending on cell type and pH. In tumor cells, lactate serves as a carbon source for the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, supporting mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and promoting survival under metabolic stress following radiotherapy28. Although lactate can support effector functions in CD8+ T cells, this occurs only under neutral pH conditions29. In the acidic tumor microenvironment, the immunosuppressive effects of lactate-induced acidification dominate, outweighing any potential benefits of lactate metabolism for antitumor immunity29. Therefore, targeting glucose metabolism and its downstream lactate-associated pathways may represent a promising approach to overcome radioresistance and enhance antitumor immune responses.

PTMs play a pivotal role in this process. Among them, crotonylation, a recently identified lysine modification, has been shown to regulate both histone and non-histone proteins, influencing chromatin dynamics, gene transcription, DNA damage repair, and the activity of metabolic enzymes10,30–32. In this study, we provide the first evidence that ACAT1 crotonylation contributes to the cellular response to ionizing radiation and that this modification is mediated by GCN5. As a well‑characterized lysine acetyltransferase, GCN5 has previously been implicated in energy metabolism and stress responses33–35. Our findings reveal that GCN5 also functions as a crotonyltransferase, promoting ACAT1 crotonylation in response to radiation. ACAT1 is a key mitochondrial metabolic enzyme that catalyzes the reversible condensation of two acetyl-CoA molecules into acetoacetyl-CoA and CoA, and plays important roles in maintaining mitochondrial acetyl-CoA homeostasis, as well as in fatty acid β-oxidation and ketone body biosynthesis17,18. Beyond its established role in mitochondrial acetyl-CoA metabolism, ACAT1 has also been reported to function as an acetyltransferase that acetylates PDHA1, thereby promoting PDHA1 phosphorylation at Ser293 and influencing pyruvate metabolism19,21. Here, our results showed that K181 crotonylation enhances the acetyltransferase activity of ACAT1, thereby increasing PDHA1 acetylation. We further demonstrated that PDHA1 acetylation modulates PDHA1 phosphorylation at Ser293. Given that the molecular mechanism underlying ACAT1-mediated regulation of PDHA1 phosphorylation has been well characterized in previous studies19,21, this aspect was not further experimentally validated in detail in the present work. Collectively, these findings suggest that ionizing radiation may regulate PDHA1 enzymatic activity through modulation of ACAT1 crotonylation and subsequent changes in PDHA1 acetylation.

For this study, there are still several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, while our data provide evidence supporting GCN5 as a potential crotonyltransferase involved in ACAT1 regulation, the current findings regarding GCN5-mediated crotonylation of ACAT1 at K181 are primarily based on functional and correlative analyses. Definitive mechanistic evidence for direct enzyme–substrate specificity therefore remains to be established, and further validation is required, although this is beyond the scope of the present study. Second, although we demonstrate that ACAT1 crotonylation is involved in radiation-induced alterations in glucose metabolism, given that ACAT1 plays a primary role in acetyl-CoA metabolism and does not directly participate in glucose metabolism, we propose that ACAT1 may represent one of the potential regulators of radiation-induced glucose metabolic reprogramming. Radiation-induced alterations in glucose metabolism are driven by multiple factors, including post-translational modifications of enzymes that directly participate in glucose metabolism. Therefore, further experimental studies are still required to elucidate the detailed mechanisms underlying radiation-induced glucose metabolic reprogramming.

In conclusion, this study provides insights into a mechanism by which IR contributes to metabolic reprogramming, in which GCN5 function as a crotonyltransferase for ACAT1, thereby modulating its acetyltransferase activity toward PDHA1 and influencing pyruvate metabolism and lactate production. Targeting the GCN5–ACAT1 crotonylation axis may provide a potential strategy to overcome radioresistance.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Human breast cancer MCF7 cells, human liver cancer HepG2 cells, and human cervical cancer HeLa cells purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) were cultured in DMEM (HyClone) supplemented with 10% FBS (HyClone and PAN) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cells were incubated in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. For irradiation, cells underwent exposure to 60Co γ-rays at a dose rate of 85.69 cGy min–1 at ambient temperature.

Plasmid construction and generation of ACAT1 knockdown/knockout cells

Flag-tagged plasmids, including CBP, P300, GCN5, PCAF, and SFB-TIP60, were kindly provided by Jiadong Wang’s laboratory (Peking University Health Science Center, Beijing, China). The Flag-ACAT1 and His-ACAT1 plasmids were constructed by Zhongmei Taihe Biotechnology Co., LTD. For conditional knockdown of ACAT1, control or ACAT1-targeting shRNAs were inserted into a tetracycline-inducible lentiviral vector. The sequences were: NC: 5′-TCTCGCTTGGGCGAGAGTAAG-3′; ACAT1-shRNA#1: 5′-ACCTTTAGTCTGGTTGTACTAG-3′; ACAT1-shRNA#2: 5′-AGAAATGAACAGGACGCTTATG-3′. For ACAT1 knockout, CRISPR-Cas9 lentiviruses carrying a gRNA sequence (5′-CACTTACTATGACCACTTCCG-3′) were used to infect target cells. Stable cell lines were established by puromycin selection (2 μg/mL for 7 days).

Point mutation assay

Gene point mutation was performed using the QuickMutation™ Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Beyotime, D0206S). The primers were designed as follows: K181R: 5′-GCTTGAAGATTTGATTGTAAGAGACGGGCTAACTG-3′; K257R: 5′-CAAAGTTCCAAAGCTGAGGACAGTTTTCCAGAAAG-3′; K263R: 5′-GTGTTGATTTTAGCAGAGTTCCAAAGCTGAAGACAG-3′; K268R: 5′-GAAAGAAGATGAAGAATATAGACGTGTTGATTTTAGCAAAG-3′; GCN5 E575Q: 5′-CCCAGGGCTTCACGCAGATTGTCTTCTGTG-3′. The PCR conditions were as follows: step 1, initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min; step 2, denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and extension at 68 °C for 4 min, for a total of 20 cycles; step 3, final extension at 68 °C for 10 min; step 4, hold at 4 °C.

Apoptosis assay

Cells were divided into different experimental groups with three replicates per group. Logarithmically growing cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well and pre-incubated in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator. On the following day, cells were exposed to 8 Gy γ-irradiation. At 0, 24, and 48 h post-irradiation, both the culture supernatant and adherent cells were collected. Cells were stained using the Annexin V-FITC apoptosis detection kit (DOJINDO, AD10), and apoptosis was analyzed by flow cytometry (ACEA, NovoCyte). The resulting percentages of apoptotic cells were used for graphing and statistical analysis.

Detection of intracellular lactate, pyruvate, and ATP

Cellular lactate and pyruvate concentrations were quantified using commercial assay kits (Abbkine, KTB1100 and KTB1121), while ATP levels were measured with an ATP detection kit (Beyotime, S0026). Each experimental group was assayed in triplicate. Cells were collected, washed three times with ice-cold PBS (3000 rpm, 5 min), and resuspended in 1 mL PBS for counting. A total of 5 × 105 cells from each group were subjected to analysis. All assays were carried out according to the manufacturers’ protocols. The obtained values were normalized to the mean value of the control (NC) group, and the normalized data were used for graphing and statistical analysis.

Cellular energy metabolism analysis

Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) were measured in live cells using the O2K Fluo Pro cellular energy analyzer (OROBOROS, O2K Fluo Pro). Each experimental group was assayed in triplicate. For OCR measurement, the sequential titration protocol was as follows: oligomycin (Omy, 5 nM/injection, 1 μL per step) → FCCP (0.5 μM/injection, 1 μL per step) → antimycin A (Ama, 2.5 μM, 1 μL single injection). For ECAR measurement, the titration protocol was: glucose (Glu, 5 mM, 5 μL) → oligomycin (Omy, 5 nM, 1 μL) → 2-deoxyglucose (2-DG, 25 mM, 25 μL). All experiments were performed according to the manufacturer’s standard operating procedures. The obtained values for each experimental group were normalized to their corresponding NC values, and the normalized data were used for graphing and statistical analysis.

Detection of acetyl-CoA and crotonyl-CoA

Cellular levels of acetyl-CoA and crotonyl-CoA were quantified using the Acetyl-CoA Assay Kit (Sigma, MAK566-1KT) and Human Crotonyl-CoA (crotonyl-CoA) ELISA Kit (Yuanxin Biology, YX-E51450), respectively. Each experimental group was assayed in triplicate. Cells were collected and washed three times with ice-cold PBS (3000 rpm, 5 min), then resuspended in PBS for cell counting. A total of 5 × 105 cells from each group were subjected to analysis. All assays were performed according to the manufacturers’ protocols. The obtained values for each group were normalized to the mean value of the NC group, and the normalized data were used for graphing and statistical analysis.

Co-immunoprecipitation assay

Cells were lysed in NETN buffer (300 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5% NP-40) supplemented with protease inhibitors. After gentle rotation at 4 °C for 20 min, lysates were clarified by centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 10 min). The supernatants were first pre-cleared with IgG from the same species as the primary antibody and Protein A/G magnetic beads (Selleck, B23202) for 2 h to eliminate non-specific binding. For endogenous immunoprecipitation (IP), the pre-cleared lysates were incubated with the indicated primary antibody at 4 °C for 6 h or overnight with gentle rotation, followed by incubation with Protein A/G magnetic beads at 4 °C for 2 h. For exogenous IP of overexpressed Flag-tagged proteins, lysates were directly incubated with Anti-Flag magnetic beads (Selleck, B26101) at 4 °C for 6 h. After enrichment of the target proteins using magnetic beads, samples were subjected to Western blot (WB) analysis for subsequent detection.

Western blot analysis and antibodies

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors (Selleck, B14001). After centrifugation (12,000 × g, 15 min), the clarified lysates were collected, and ~40 µg protein was resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 2 h at room temperature, incubated with primary antibodies overnight, and then washed and probed with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:4000, 1 h, room temperature). Signals were detected using SuperSignal™ West Pico Plus (ThermoFisher Scientific, TL275133). The following primary antibodies were used: anti-ACAT1 (CST, 44276S, 1:1000), anti-GCN5 (Santa Cruz, sc-365321, 1:1000), anti-Kcr (Jingjie PTM BioLab, PTM-502, 1:1000), anti-Kac (CST, 9441, 1:1000), anti-PDHA1 (Santa Cruz, sc-377092, 1:1000), anti-phospho-PDHA1 Ser293 (Proteintech, 29580-1-AP, 1:1000), anti-Flag (Proteintech, 20543-1-AP, 1:1000), anti-Lamin A/C (CST, 2032, 1:1000), anti-α-tubulin (Santa Cruz, sc-69969, 1:1000), HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit (KPL, 074-1806, 1:4000), HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse (KPL, 074-1516, 1:4000), and anti-β-actin (Proteintech, 81115-1-RR, 1:1000). Band intensities were quantified using Image J software. For Co-IP assays, the intensity of the modification or interaction protein was normalized to that of the immunoprecipitated bait protein. For protein expression analysis, the target protein band was normalized to the loading control. These normalized values were then divided by the corresponding NC group values, and the resulting ratios were used for graphing and statistical analysis.

Enzyme activity assays

ACAT1 activity assay: His-tagged ACAT1 was overexpressed in 293T cells and enriched by co-immunoprecipitation. The protein was eluted from Ni-agarose beads with imidazole, quantified, and stored at 4 °C until use. For the assay, 100 ng of purified ACAT1 protein was added to a reaction buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.1), 20 mM MgCl2, 40 mM KCl, 10 mM acetoacetyl-CoA, and 60 mM CoA. Enzymatic activity was determined by monitoring absorbance changes at 303 nm using a SpectraMax i3X microplate reader (Molecular Devices). PDHA1 activity assay: PDHA1 enzymatic activity was measured using the CheKine™ Pyruvate Dehydrogenase Activity Assay Kit (Abbkine, KTB1270) following the manufacturer’s instructions. All assays were performed in triplicate for each experimental group. The obtained values were divided by the NC group, and the resulting ratios were used for graphing and statistical analysis.

Colony formation assay

MCF7 (NC, ACAT1 KO), HeLa (NC, ACAT1 KO), and HepG2 (NC, ACAT1 KD) cells were seeded into 6-well plates at the following densities according to treatment: HeLa—0 Gy: 500; 4 Gy: 1500; 8 Gy: 3000 cells/well; MCF7—0 Gy: 300; 4 Gy: 1000; 8 Gy: 2000 cells/well; HepG2—0 Gy: 500; 4 Gy: 1500; 8 Gy: 3000 cells/well. Each experimental condition was set up in triplicate. After attachment, the medium was refreshed every 4 days. Following a 14-day incubation, colonies (≥50 cells) were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 1× crystal violet, rinsed with PBS, and counted visually. Colony formation efficiency was calculated as: (number of colonies/number of cells seeded) × 100%. Colony formation efficiency for each treated group was normalized to the mean efficiency of the NC group, and the resulting ratios were used for graphing and statistical analysis.

Proximity ligation assay

Cells seeded on 20 mm × 20 mm coverslips in 6-well plates were exposed to 8 Gy ionizing radiation at ~60% confluence. After 2 h, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature and washed three times with 1× PBS. Cells were then permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 5 min, washed again with 1× PBS, and blocked with 3% BSA containing 0.1% Triton X-100 for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies—rabbit anti-ACAT1 (Proteintech, 16215-1-AP, 1:100) and mouse anti-GCN5 (Santa Cruz, sc-365321, 1:50)—were applied overnight at 4 °C. Coverslips were then processed with Duolink In Situ PLA Probe Anti-Rabbit PLUS (DUO92002) and anti-mouse MINUS (DUO92004) following the manufacturers’ protocols. PLA signals were ligated and amplified using the Duolink In Situ Detection Kit (DUO92008), and coverslips were mounted with DAPI-containing mounting medium (ZSGB-BIO, ZLI-9600). For data quantification, images were taken from ≥10 random fields per coverslip, and the number of PLA foci per cell was determined. The foci counts per cell were directly used for graphing and statistical analysis.

In vitro crotonylation assay

ACAT1 (MCE, HY-P78986), GCN5 (Proteintech, 31591) recombinant proteins, crotonyl-CoA (Sigma, 28007), and butyrolactone 3 (MCE, HY-129039) were equilibrated on ice. Reaction buffer A (25 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 2 mM MgCl2, 1 mM sodium butyrate, 1 mM DTT) was prepared. Reaction mixtures were prepared for each experimental group as follows: group 1 : ACAT1 (1 μg) + buffer A; group 2: ACAT1 (1 μg) + crotonyl-CoA (100 μM) + buffer A; group 3: ACAT1 (1 μg) + GCN5 (0.5 μg) + crotonyl-CoA (100 μM) + buffer A; group 4: ACAT1 (1 μg) + GCN5 (0.5 μg) + crotonyl-CoA (100 μM) + butyrolactone 3 (100 μM) + buffer A, with a final volume of 20 μL for each group. After mixing and incubation at 37 °C for 30 min, samples were denatured with 5× loading buffer, boiled for 10 min, and analyzed by SDS-PAGE for ACAT1 crotonylation detection.

Xenograft tumor model in nude mice

Four-week-old female BALB/c nude mice were purchased from SPF (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd (SCXK [Beijing] 2024-0001). The mice were randomly assigned into four groups (8 mice/group): ACAT1 WT + NC, ACAT1 K181R + NC, ACAT1 WT + IR, and ACAT1 K181R + IR groups. Sample size (n = 8 per group) was determined based on previous similar xenograft tumor model studies. Animals that died before the endpoint or failed to develop tumors were excluded from the analysis. According to group allocation, each mouse was subcutaneously injected with HeLa ACAT1 WT or ACAT1 K181R cells at a dose of 1 × 107 cells in 0.1 mL per mouse. Three days after cell inoculation, mice in the irradiation groups received a local radiation dose of 8 Gy. Tumor length (L) and width (W) were measured every 3 days starting from day 3 post-inoculation using a digital vernier caliper, and tumor volume was calculated as V = (L × W2)/2. Measurements were performed by an investigator blinded to group allocation. Three weeks after cell inoculation, the mice were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation followed by cervical dislocation, and tumor tissues were excised, weighed, and photographed. The resulting tumor volume and weight data were analyzed statistically. All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the Laboratory Animal—Guidelines for Ethical Review of Animal Welfare (GB/T 35892–2018). Our animal experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee at the Academy of Military Medical Sciences (DWZX-IACUC-2026-546).

Crotonylation proteomics sequencing

Cells were cultured for 3–5 passages after thawing and used for experiments once they reached optimal condition. For each group, three replicates of 1 × 107 cells were collected. Cell pellets were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, stored at –80 °C, and shipped on dry ice to Hangzhou Jingjie PTM BioLab for crotonylation proteomics analysis.

Statistics and reproducibility

Each experiment was performed in triplicate and independently repeated at least three times. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 8). Data are presented as mean ± SEM or SD, as indicated. Comparisons between two groups were evaluated using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test. For multiple group comparisons involving a single independent variable, one-way ANOVA followed by appropriate post hoc tests was used. For experiments involving two independent variables, data were analyzed using two-way ANOVA. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Inclusion and ethics

All animal studies were conducted with the approval of the Animal Research Committee according to the regulations on animal experimentation at the Academy of Military Medical Sciences (DWZX-IACUC-2026-546).
