A natural deduction system for the Byzantine Generals Oral Messages algorithm
Authors: Dennis M. Volpano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Oral Messages algorithm OM(m) is an algorithm that uses m message relay rounds to solve the Byzantine Generals Problem [5]. It is a landmark result, however its original specification is informal and its correctness proof sketchy, making both hard to understand. The proof is rewritten here using a natural deduction system formulated directly from the message flows described for OM(m) in [5]. The system comprises only two inference rules which can be used to explain the original algorithm via derivations. The rules are shown complete relative to OM(m) and sound in the sense they cannot be used to derive consensus when it is impossible [8]. The completeness proof provides more details than the original correctness proof, clearly showing the role of m, the risk it creates, and why the algorithm succeeds under well-known constraints.

Introduction

The Byzantine Generals Problem is a widely-known problem in fault-tolerant computing [5]. An instance of the problem has n generals comprised of a commanding general and n - 1n-1 lieutenant generals. All can communicate directly with each other via messages such that every message sent is delivered correctly, the source of every received message is known and the absence of a message can be detected (pg. 387, [5]). The commanding general sends an order directly to each of his n - 1n-1 lieutenants via a message. However the commanding general may be a traitor and send different orders to different lieutenants. Without knowing whether the commanding general is a traitor, the lieutenants must still reach agreement on some order. They attempt to do so by exchanging messages among themselves. But a lieutenant can also be traitorous by lying about the order he received. The Byzantine Generals Problem then is for all loyal lieutenants to reach agreement on some order, using two-party messages only, and that order must be the commanding general’s order when he is loyal.

Detecting the absence of a message relies on a time-out mechanism which requires generals to have clocks synchronized to within some fixed maximum error (pg. 398, [5]). So the communication system used by generals is synchronous. There is no solution to the asynchronous communication version of the problem even if a traitor cannot lie [3].

The Oral Messages algorithm OM(m) is a well-known algorithm for solving the Byzantine Generals Problem [5]. The algorithm is parameterized on m, the number of relay rounds used by lieutenants to communicate messages. Its original specification in [5] is informal and its correctness proof sketchy, making it difficult to understand how and why the algorithm works. In fact, entire papers have focused on how to explain the algorithm [6, 10]. Others have attempted to reformulate the algorithm to make it more formal and prove its correctness [1, 7, 9, 11]. But these efforts depart substantially from the original specification and proof. Hence, they do not help us grasp the original work to answer fundamental questions such as why is the number of generals needed for consensus a function of m, and why is consensus limited to loyal lieutenants? While we cannot expect truth from traitors, traitors can expect truth from loyal lieutenants. Yet traitors are not always among the lieutenants who reach agreement.

A rewrite of the proof in [5] is needed that reveals missing details. To accomplish this, a more formal description of OM(m) is required. It should hew to the original specification though since our aim is to rewrite the original proof. To this end, a natural deduction system, comprising just two inference rules, is formulated directly from the message flows for OM(m) described in [5]. The system is shown to be complete with respect to OM(m) and sound in that it cannot derive consensus when consensus is impossible [8]. Proof of completeness provides a natural-deduction style analog of the OM(m) correctness proof in [5]. Our proof aligns well with the original proof structure while giving greater insight into why the algorithm works under well-known constraints and how m can negatively affect consensus.

We begin by giving the original specification of OM(m) in [5]. Then we present the deduction system. This is followed by proofs of completeness and soundness. Our proofs are then related to the original proof structure in [5].

The Oral Messages algorithm OM(m)

The Oral Messages algorithm OM(m) begins with the commander sending a message to each of n-1n-1 lieutenants. Every lieutenant then relays the message to every other lieutenant, assuming the role of commander. The process then repeats with one less lieutenant, specifically that lieutenant who assumed the role of commander. The algorithm is parameterized on m, the number of rounds used by the n-1n-1 lieutenants to transmit values among themselves. Its original specification from [5] is as follows:

A natural deduction system for OM(m)

But there’s no need to count only relay rounds. Counting all communication rounds means m>0m>0, as it includes the initial round of messages sent by the commander. Representing Byzantine faults in the deduction system

Byzantine faults are described by the behavior of a traitorous general (pp. 383–387, [5]). As commander, a traitorous general may send different orders to different lieutenants, and as a lieutenant, he may lie about what he heard from other generals. If he sends different orders then his behavior is asymmetric, and if he lies, he is of course dishonest. These terms are made precise with respect to processors as follows:

Completeness of the deduction system

Completeness of the deduction system is relative to OM. Roughly that means the system can accomplish whatever OM can accomplish under the same assumptions. The natural deduction system makes no guarantees by itself as it is merely a system for deducing consensus when consensus is possible. So we must be clear about the conditions under which consensus is possible and show it is derivable in the system whenever they’re satisfied. These conditions are expressed relative to function sends and the properties of symmetry and honesty.

The next lemma captures what can be derived in the deduction system for a processor under the well-known constraint on faulty processors and rounds. Specifically, majority is defined at the root of a derivation for a processor as long as that processor belongs to a set Q for which |Q|≥ 2k + m|Q|≥2k+m where k is the number of dishonest processors in Q and m the total number of rounds. As we shall see, the constraint allows the inductive hypothesis to be invoked enough times to define majority at the root and thus achieve consensus when possible.

Algorithm OM enables a redundant processor to get every other processor to agree on a value in V it wishes to report, sometimes called its private value. Each of n processors maintains an interactive consistency vector of size n such that entry j of the vector at every honest processor stores j’s private value [8]. Entries for j can be defined there when j runs OM with itself as commander and reports its private value. However, if j is asymmetric then it may report different values to different processors. This can occur in practice if the processors receive different inputs, as was the case for the quad redundant control system described in [2], or even if all execute on the same input (the processors would be considered faulty in the latter case if each is expected to be deterministic). By Lemma 2, algorithm OM still gets the entries at j of the vectors at all honest processors to store equivalent terms in W and hence agree at j. In fact, after every redundant processor has reported out values in V, all honest processors will agree on their interactive consistency vectors. This is the ultimate goal and is captured formally by the following corollary.

Completeness of the deduction system with respect to OM follows from Corollary 1 since the conditions required to hold in its proof are basically those required for OM when OM is used to achieve IC for a set of processors, one processor at a time. The only difference is that OM makes no distinction between symmetry and honesty. It merely treats a processor as faulty or not. So if OM can be used to achieve IC for a set of processors then IC for the set can be derived in the deduction system, one processor at a time.

Relationship to original work

Lemma 1 is our analog of lemma 1 in [5], which states for any m≥ 0m≥0 and k, OM(m) satisfies IC2 if there are more than 2k+m2k+m generals and at most k traitors. Here m≥ 0m≥0 because it denotes relay rounds. IC2 is an interactive consistency property stating that if the commanding general is loyal, then every loyal lieutenant obeys the order he sends. As m includes the commander initially communicating his value to every lieutenant in our formulation, the number of generals must be at least 2k+m2k+m, not necessarily more. Loyalty corresponds to symmetry and honesty in this paper. The proof of lemma 1 in [5] also proceeds by induction on m, however, their inductive hypothesis is invoked only once unlike ours which is invoked multiple times. This is because processor symmetry and honesty are part of the antecedent of our inductive hypothesis but not theirs, which is just that there are more than 2k+(m-1)2k+(m-1) generals and at most k traitors.

The purpose of theorem 1 in [5] is to handle the case where the commander is a traitor. It states that for any m, OM(m) satisfies conditions IC1 and IC2 if there are more than 3m generals and at most m traitors. IC1 says all loyal lieutenants obey the same order. It follows from IC2 when a commanding general is loyal. We split this theorem into Lemma 2 and Theorem 1 to make the conditions of the inductive hypothesis more precise than just OM(m-1m-1) satisfies IC1 and IC2 if there are more than 3(m-1)3(m-1) generals and at most m-1m-1 traitors. This precision helps to better explain the properties needed for consensus. For example, Lemma 1 is about reaching agreement on members of V at every processor, honest or dishonest. Lemma 2 is about reaching agreement on members of W at honest processors only, as reflected by the requirement that j and j'j′ be honest in the statement of the lemma. Not until we show v : : M and v'::M'v′::M′ have v and v'v′ in common is their honesty needed in the proof. That honesty allows us to apply Lemma 1, which speaks to members of V, not W. This is what is needed since v and v'v′ belong to V. Contrast with IC1 and IC2 which talk about loyal lieutenants executing the same order but nowhere in the proof of theorem 1 in [5] is a lieutenant’s loyalty invoked, only the commander’s loyalty. The proof of Lemma 2 makes clear why j and j'j′, as lieutenants, must be honest.

Finally, more than 3m generals when at most m are traitors is sufficient for OM(m) to succeed but not always necessary. For example, consider the two derivations in Fig. 4. There is only one traitor (asymmetric processor 3) so m=1m=1 yet agreement can be derived between 1 and 2 after m+1m+1 rounds with just 3m processors. This doesn’t contradict the impossibility theorem of [8] where 3m+13m+1 is shown to be a lower bound on the number of processors required to achieve IC. That result was shown under the assumption that a processor may be dishonest. Indeed, if processor 3 is also dishonest then we still have m=1m=1 but cannot derive 2’s private value at 1 with just 3m processors after m+1m+1 rounds.

Conclusion

The Oral Messages algorithm is an important algorithm based on work from [8] which was awarded the 2005 Edsger W. Dijkstra Prize in Distributed Computing. Yet how it works and why it succeeds is a challenge to understand given the original informal specification and correctness proof in [5]. This paper rewrites that proof, providing details that should help demystify the algorithm.
