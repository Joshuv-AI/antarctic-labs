An advanced framework for predicting pollen outbreaks using multi-input multi-output temporal convolutional networks and firefly algorithm
Authors: Sugandha Sharma, Rakesh Kumar, Saurabh Kumar, Deepali Gupta, Abhinav Juneja, Gireesh Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPollen outbreaks pose serious public health concerns, especially for those with respiratory problems. Reliable detection systems, which provide timely interventions, are essential to mitigate these risks. This work presents novel pollen outbreak prediction by integrating Multi-Input Multi-Output Temporal Convolutional Networks (MIMO-TCN) with Firefly algorithm for optimal hyperparameter tuning. It presents a technique that uses the Firefly Algorithm (FA) for hyperparameter tweaking in conjunction with the Multi-Input Multi-Output Temporal Convolutional Networks (MIMO-TCN) architecture. The FA, inspired by firefly communication and mating habits, excels at optimization challenges. We ran extensive trials on a dataset of 20,000 to 100,000 instances, testing the model using measures including accuracy, precision, sensitivity, and F1 score. The model beat various state-of-the-art algorithms, with an average precision of 0.965, sensitivity of 0.982, F-measure of 0.973 and accuracy of 92.04%. Confusion matrix analysis demonstrated strong performance across pollen types (Tree, Grass, and Weed), emphasizing the model’s classification accuracy and real-world applications.Keywords: Indian pollen outbreak detection, Firefly method, MIMO-TCN, Hyperparameter tuningSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Pollen is a fine, powdery substance plants produce during their reproductive cycle. The primary function of pollen is to transfer male gametes to female gametes, thus facilitating fertilization and reproduction1. The process of pollination and reproduction is very important as it allows plants to grow and develop via production of seeds and fruits2. Although, these pollens may have substantial impact on the human health, especially for those who have allergic history associated with pollens. In the research studies presented by3,4 the researchers have investigated the structure and impact of pollens on the health of individuals belonging to India. Pollen is normally made up of three parts: the exine, the intine, and the cytoplasm, as illustrated in Fig. 15. The exine is the pollen grain’s outermost covering, made of sporopollenin, a highly resistant polymer that shields it from external shocks. The intine is the inner layer of the pollen grain which is made of cellulose and pectin. The germ pore is a region in the pollen grain’s exine layer where sporopollenin is missing. The cytoplasm contains male gametes as well as other cellular components. When pollen is released into the air, individuals can inhale it, resulting in an allergic reaction6. This response might result in unpleasant symptoms such as sneezing, coughing, watery eyes, and nasal congestion7. The intensity of these symptoms varies according to the person’s immune system and the amount of pollen inhaled8.Further, a device known as pollen counter is used to measure the amount of pollen present in the air. A pollen counter gathers and analyzes air samples for the presence of pollen9,10.

Related work

Pollen allergy is a significant health issue in India, with an estimated 15–20% of the population suffering from some form of allergic rhinitis. Allergic rhinitis is a condition characterized by symptoms such as sneezing, runny nose, and nasal congestion and is often caused by exposure to pollen. India’s diverse plant species, including mango, neem, and banyan trees, contribute to the range of pollen sources, which can cause allergic reactions in susceptible individuals. The prevalence of pollen allergy varies depending on the location and the time of year, with higher rates of allergy reported in urban areas and during the spring and summer months. Climate change can also have a significant impact on pollen production and distribution.

The simplest method to track and predict the onset of pollen season is known as pollen calendar, employed by Shin et al. in Korea. It depends on the seasonal variations reflected by climatic change and requires regular updates. However, the predictive models developed outside of India, such as in Korea, often face challenges when applied locally due to India’s unique climate and vegetation12. In India, pollen calendars have been used to predict pollen season in Chandigarh, where pollen from trees dominates in spring and herbaceous plants in autumn. However, the accuracy of such predictions is significantly influenced by the local weather conditions and the vegetation patterns13. A rise in temperature may mark an earlier onset of the pollen season in addition to increased pollen production which pose serious health risks for population with pre-existing respiratory condition. Additionally, meteorological factors such as humidity, temperature, wind speed and variation in rainfall patterns significantly influence pollen production and distribution14. Considering that manual pollen outbreak identification is still prevalent in India, there is an increased need for automation through Software Aided Design (SAD). The research suggests that the integration of machine learning could enhance the accuracy and efficiency of pollen outbreak predictions15. The machine learning models such as Hidden Markov Model (HMM)16, Artificial Neural Network (ANN)1,17, Deep Neural Network (DNN)18,19, Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) and Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCN)20 are among commonly used for developing models for predicting atmospheric pollen concentration. These models rely on meteorological data including temperature, humidity and wind speed as input variables that helps the models to learn and make informative predictions21.

For instance, Lara et al. created a prediction model that predicts the pollen concentration of the plane tree which is planted in the urban green areas. This involved regression model learning from the variables such as humidity, temperature, precipitation and wind speed. The model resulted in good accuracy to predict the pollen concentration throughput the pollen season and as such could successfully access the allergy risk levels in the urban green areas. However, the vegetation patterns and microclimates also affect the pollen concentration estimation22. Similarly, the model created by Picornell et al. was dedicated to predict the onset, and end of Betula pollen season in regions of Germany. The model was trained over pollen count, precipitation, wind speed and temperature. While the model exhibited 75% accuracy, the authors pointed out that accuracy of prediction remain challenging due to the complicated interactions that exist between plant biology, local environmental circumstances, and meteorological factors23.

Zewdie et al. had applied machine learning algorithms such as Random Forest (RF), gradient boosting (GB), and support vector regression (SVR) were used to predict the daily ambrosia pollen count. The model achieved 80% accuracy in predicting daily pollen counts. The authors suggested that the model could be used to predict pollen concentrations and provide early warning to allergy patients24. In another study, the DNN model was proposed by Kim et al. to predict pollen concentrations based on weather factors. The work was based on meteorological data from the past decade to train the system on the observation of pollen atmospheric concentrations. The pollen estimations were incorporated with a mean absolute percentage error of 5.04% with the DNN model, which was the least among the analyzed studies25. A subsequent study by Zewdie et al. utilized DNN and ensemble ML techniques to forecast Ambrosia pollen. The model outperformed others due to its robust training on 24 years of meteorological data26. Tseng et al. developed a hidden Markov model (HMM) combining meteorological and biological factors to forecast the seasonal pollen index. They used meteorological data, such as temperature, humidity, and wind speed, along with pollen count data, to train the model collected over 22 years in the Hokkaido region of Japan. The model achieved a high correlation coefficient between the observed and predicted pollen index values. The model’s prediction accuracy was 83.3% during the training period, while 75% accuracy was demonstrated during testing. The authors suggested that the model can be used for early warning and preventing allergy symptoms16.

Hewage et al. introduced a numerical weather prediction model based on the LSTM and TCN to perform forecasting based on prevalent weather conditions. The presented model was evaluated against classical, statistical, and dynamic ensemble forecasting methods. This includes Support Vector Regression (SVR), Random Forest (RF), and Standard Regression (SR) under classical ML methods, Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA), Vector Error Correction Model (VECM), and Vector Auto Regression (VAR) as statistical forecasting techniques, and Arbitrage of Forecasting Expert (AFE) as a dynamic ensemble method. The experimentation shows that the designed lightweight model was capable of predicting weather conditions at least 12 h ahead. Despite of its capabilities, it faced limitations in handling multiple meteorological inputs simultaneously27. Kim et al. have observed that pollen allergy is the major reason for forecasting the pollen season. In Japan, a Korean Meteorological Agency (KMA) is dedicated to analyzing and predicting pollen concentration on a daily, weekly, and monthly basis. The study further established that a rise in atmospheric temperature and carbon dioxide positively impacted the risk of pollen allergy. However, the research highlights that the variation in pollen production patterns due to climate change may have an negative impact on the accuracy of forecasting models28.

Yang et al. proposed a prediction model for the spring pollen allergy outbreak date in Beijing based on satellite-derived phenological characteristics of vegetation greenness. The model was trained on vegetation greenness observed from satellite data and ground observation exhibiting 86% accuracy in predicting the onset of pollen outbreak. Although the satellite based approach was quite accurate, several factors such as cloud cover and fluctuating vegetation affect the model reliability29. Shamji et al. designed a model based on artificial intelligence and utilized the machine learning concept to understand causes behind asthma and respiratory diseases. The present status of research is reviewed in context of allergic diseases to identify the need of integration of advanced analytical methods to handle complex data. They emphasized the importance of data standardization and harmonization in building a reliable exposome data repository. Though, the development of causality models in exposomics encounter difficulties because of data quality, model interpretability, and the complexity of environmental interactions30. Lastly, Mills et al. presented an attempt to address the rising demand for the automated pollen monitoring system via machine learning techniques. Random Forest (RF) and Neural Network (NN) were trained on the pollen concentration data collected using a Hirst-type sampler. The analysis was performed by varying the model input features and type to construct 12 models, each using RF and NN. The overall evaluation resulted in a Spearman correlation coefficient of 0.85 using the NN model for grass pollen and an F1 score of 0.83. Thus, the author claimed the suitability of the implemented technique to monitor pollens shortly to benefit individuals suffering from pollen allergies, despite of the fact that the accuracy of pollen proxies created from Optical Particle Counter (OPC) data processed with Random Forests and Neural Networks was susceptible to background noise and particle composition. Nevertheless, this approach can offer cost-effective monitoring solutions31.

More recently, Güçlü et al. investigated the integration of machine learning to enhance their understanding of pollen viability, pollen dispersal, and fertilization success. In a research note, Güçlü supports the advancement of fruit production and relates it to predicting pollen with knowledge of environmental conditions. Although Güçlü demonstrated machine learning models were a useful approach, the results did not necessarily suggest one algorithmic approach over another to address the changing environmental conditions in real-time32. Likewise, Alsaber et al. propose machine learning for agricultural forecasting on pearl millet yield in Rajasthan, India. Alsaber et al. conducts an analysis to compare single and grouped (ensemble) machine learning models. Overall, they represents a potential case for computational tools to assist in agricultural decision-making. However, they did not consider environmental stresses when extreme weather changes model predictions33.

Research study presented by Maruthai et al. have investigated advanced deep learning methods (like graph neural networks) to identify biological patterns in plants at an early stage34. Hypergraph Neural Networks have also been applied to detecting agricultural diseases by Selvanarayanan et al. and, can be used to analyze complicated, high-dimensional data35. Additionally, study by Sivasubramanian et al. have investigated using optimization methods (such as the Al-Biruni Earth Radius Optimization algorithm) to improve how scientists gather and analyze environmental data with remote sensing techniques36. However, all these efforts focus primarily on detecting or optimizing through images (probably through some form of optimization), with little consideration given implication of optimized multi-input multi-output deep learning architectures to forecast pollen outbreaks over time. Therefore, this is the primary aim of this study.

Mapping of Pollen Data.

To address potential uncertainty and bias in the cross=country synthetic labelling, a validation mechanism is incorporated in the proposed framework. The hybrid co-related scores are computed over multiple windows to ensure consistency in mapping. Additionally, the normalization and weight mechanism reduce the dominance of any single country dataset and hence it minimizes bias in label transfer. Furthermore, the mapped labels are validated indirectly through classification performance of the MIMO-TCN model.

Once the pollen is mapped for the outbreak, the proposed algorithm uses a Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) with integrated Multiple Input Multiple Output (MIMO) to perform the casting operation to perform MIMO-TCN. It pools the best from both approaches to make the pollen forecasting more accurate and more efficient.

MIMO-TCN is specifically developed to allow multiple input as well as output variables concurrently. Meteorological data elements such as temperature, humidity, wind speed, and precipitation are used as independent input variables in pollen forecasting.

MIMO can accommodate multiple inputs and interactions between the input variables that is critical for understanding the complex relation between pollen levels and the climatic variables. TCN has emerged as a deep learning architecture with ability to accurately track temporal dependencies. TCN employs convolutional layers to find short- and long-term dependencies in the input sequence. This makes it one of the best choises for time-series forecasting task as in our pollen outbrak prediction. The proposed MIMO-TCN will consist of three layers: the convolution layer, the temporal convolution network, and the MIMO component. The TCN component in MIMO-TCN captures temporal information from a variety of input variables.

As illustrated in Fig. 4 the MIMO-TCN Model is made up of parallel processes (multiple meteorological data inputs) that are represented as a collection of stacked temporal convolutional layers. The architecture is designed so that each dilated (expandable) convolution will have sequential data passed through it. Thus, aiding the retention of temporal dependencies cross varying periods. The multi-output will also create all of the predictions for all of the pollen categories at the same time, thus maximizing the use of shared features for the model. It uses convolutional filters with varying receptive fields to catch patterns over time scales. These filters help the model to adapt to the dynamics of the parameters of the meteorological data to increase the strength of pollen forecast. The usefulness of MIMO-TCN in detecting pollen outbreaks standout for several reasons. The first reason that its capability to process multiple input variables proved to be very supportive in understanding the various factors that affect the pollen levels. This allows to include various variables of meteorological data like humidity, temperature, precipitation and wind speed. The second reason is that MIMO-TCN’s strength in modeling temporal dependencies enables it to accurately identify both short-term and long-term changes in the pollen levels. This is actually a very important aspect for the success of the model in the light of fluctuating patterns, seasonal sifts, and pollen concentrations. Thus, the strengths of TCN allow the MIMO-TCN model to extrapolate the daily patterns resulting in reliable and accurate pollen concentration prediction. Additionally, MIMO-TCN offers number of advantages in itself. This includes its data dependent methodology that allows it to learn from the historical changes and makes it more practical. It also allows it understand the nonlinear and complex relationship among various factors. Further, the success of machine learning methods is supported by the concept of hyperparameter tuning. Conclusion

This research study presents an effective pollen prediction model using the Firefly Algorithm and MIMO-TCN. The Firefly Algorithm effectively uses hyperparameter optimization to improve model performance significantly resulting in optimal models for higher accuracy and efficiency. This model demonstrated improved capabilities in predicting pollen outbreaks with an average precision of 0.965, sensitivity of 0.982, F-measure of 0.973, and accuracy of 92.04%. Furthermore, the proposed model has consistently improved predictive capabilities compared to existing models with increases of approximately 2–3% across major evaluation metrics, contributing to both robustness and efficacy. The enhanced predictive capabilities of the proposed model are a result of the synergistic combination of Firefly optimization and MIMO-TCN’s ability to model complex temporal relationships in multi-variable meteorological datasets. Although the Firefly-MIMO-TCN demonstrated strong performance, some limitations exist with the proposed model. The study is built on synthetically generated pollen data that was created from cross-country correlation data (approximately 60% similar to Japan). Therefore, there may not be enough to capture unique characteristics of Indian states. The model lacks large scale real-world historical Indian pollen datasets. There were also a few minor misclassifications of pollen in the confusion matrices between tree and grass categories. The presence of an overlap between the feature sets for grasses and trees made proper classification of the pollen difficult.
