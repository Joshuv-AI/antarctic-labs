The Optimal Sample Complexity of Multiclass and List Learning
Chirag Pabbaraju

Abstract
While the optimal sample complexity of binary classification in terms of the VC dimension is well-established, determining the optimal sample complexity of multiclass classification has remained open. The appropriate complexity parameter for multiclass classification is the DS dimension, and despite significant efforts, a gap of DS \sqrt{\text{DS}} has persisted between the upper and lower bounds on sample complexity. Recent work by Hanneke et al. (2026) shows a novel algebraic characterization of multiclass hypothesis classes in terms of their DS dimension. Building up on this, we show that the maximum hypergraph density of any multiclass hypothesis class is upper-bounded by its DS dimension. This proves a longstanding conjecture of Daniely and Shalev-Shwartz (2014). As a consequence, we determine the optimal dependence of the sample complexity on the DS dimension for multiclass as well as list learning.

Introduction

Classification is a foundational task in machine learning, and understanding the minimum number of training samples required to achieve a desired classification accuracy, or determining the
sample complexity
of the classification task, is a core pursuit in learning theory. In the classification task, we seek to assign labels from a label space
\mathcal{Y}=\{1,2,\dots,k\}
to unlabeled points in a data domain
\mathcal{X}
. The Probably-Approximately-Correct or PAC model of Valiant
[
18
]
defines a concrete theoretical framework for this task. In binary classification, where the number of classes
k=2
, the optimal sample complexity of the learning task is completely understood. Namely, if the data is labeled, or rather
realizable
, by some target hypothesis from a hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
, then
\Theta(d_{\mathrm{VC}})
samples, where
d_{\mathrm{VC}}
is a measure of complexity of the class known as the
VC dimension

[
19
]
, are both sufficient and necessary. In slightly more detail, one provably requires

\displaystyle\Theta\left(\frac{d_{\mathrm{VC}}+\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon}\right)

(1)

many samples from a generic data distribution
\mathcal{D}
, in order to output, with probability
1-\delta
, a hypothesis
\widehat{f}
that classifies with error
\mathrm{err}_{\mathcal{D}}(\widehat{f}):=\operatorname{{Pr}}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}[\widehat{f}(x)\neq y]
at most
\varepsilon
on a new test point
[
9
]
. A similar exact characterization for binary classification also exists in the more general
agnostic
setting, where the data is not necessarily labeled by any hypothesis in the class. Here, the dependence on the error parameter
\varepsilon
degrades from
1/\varepsilon
to
1/\varepsilon^{2}
.

Despite such a clean picture for binary classification, the landscape of multiclass classification, where the number of classes
k>2
, is still not as exactly figured out. Keeping the sample complexity aspect aside, even the problem of determining a complexity parameter similar to the VC dimension, which provably characterizes the learning task was discovered rather recently in the work of
[
2
]
. The analogous object turns out to be a quantity known as the DS dimension
d_{\mathrm{DS}}

[
6
]

\displaystyle\Theta\left(\frac{d_{\mathrm{DS}}+\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon}\right).

(2)

Unfortunately, a precise and provable characterization of this form has proven to be elusive. In their work showing that the DS dimension characterizes multiclass learnability,
[
2
]
showed an upper bound of

\displaystyle\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{d_{\mathrm{DS}}^{1.5}+\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon}\right)

(3)

on the sample complexity, where the
\widetilde{O}
notation hides polylogarithmic factors.
[
8
]
got rid of some of these factors, but the
d_{\mathrm{DS}}^{1.5}
scaling persisted. On the lower bound side,
[
8
]
showed a sample complexity lower bound of

\displaystyle\Omega\left(\frac{d_{\mathrm{DS}}+\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon}\right).

(4)

It is widely conjectured that the bound above is optimal, and that the additional
\sqrt{d_{\mathrm{DS}}}
term in (
3
), along with any log factors, are extraneous. We note that the state of affairs is similar in the agnostic multiclass learning as well.

In a separate line of attack,
[
1

showed a qualitatively different upper bound on the sample complexity of learning a multiclass hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
in the realizable setting, which depends on a combinatorial object known as the
maximum density function

\mu_{\mathcal{H}}(\cdot)
. In particular,
[
1
]
showed that with
n
samples, with probability
1-\delta
, it is possible to achieve error at most

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{\lceil\mu_{\mathcal{H}}(n)\rceil+\log(1/\delta)}{n}\right).

(5)

The attractive aspect about this bound is that it is completely devoid of any log factors. While the term in the numerator, namely
\mu_{\mathcal{H}}(n)
, is in general a non-decreasing function in
n
, a promising avenue to derive the optimal sample complexity bound in (
2
) would be to show that the function
\mu_{\mathcal{H}}(n)
is uniformly upper-bounded by the DS dimension
d_{\mathrm{DS}}
. Indeed, this is precisely a conjecture formulated by
[
6
]
in their original work that defined the DS dimension.

Conjecture

(
[
6
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{X}}
be a hypothesis class having DS dimension
d_{\mathrm{DS}}
. There is an absolute constant
c>0
such that for any
n>0
,
\mu_{\mathcal{H}}(n)\leq c\cdot d_{\mathrm{DS}}
.

There is reason to believe that the conjecture above is true, since in the special case of
k=2
, the desired bound holds, by a beautiful result due to
[
10
]
. In addition, an upper bound of the form
c\cdot d_{\mathrm{DS}}\cdot\log(k)
also holds
[
6
]
. However, the conjecture in full generality has remained open since being proposed, and has resisted several natural proof approaches. Given the context of
[
1
]
’s results, a positive resolution of the conjecture is especially meaningful, since it would establish the optimal sample complexity of multiclass learning.

We begin with the consequences for multiclass learning. Recall from (

Corollary 1.1

(Optimal Sample Complexity for Multiclass Learning)

Let
\mathcal{H}\subseteq[k]^{\mathcal{X}}

\widehat{f}_{S}=\mathcal{A}(S)

\mathcal{H}\subseteq[k]^{\mathcal{X}}

\mathcal{A}
, such that for any distribution

List Learning.

We now turn to the related task of
list
learning. In list learning, an algorithm is permitted to output a list hypothesis
\mu
, which maps any point
x
to a list of
\ell

\ell=1
, this task is equivalent to standard multiclass learning. When
\ell>1
, list learning has a characterization analogous to multiclass learning in terms of the
\ell
-DS dimension

[
4
]
, whose finiteness is both sufficient and necessary for the task. Concretely, in the realizable case,
[
8
]
showed a sample complexity lower bound of
\Omega\left(\frac{d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{DS}}+\log(1/\delta)}{\ell\varepsilon}\right)
for list learning.
[
4
]
showed a sample complexity upper bound of
\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{\ell^{6}(d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{DS}})^{1.5}+\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon}\right)
, whereas
[
3
]
also showed an (incomparable) upper bound of
\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{\ell^{4}(d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{DS}})^{5}+\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon}\right)

proved an upper bound of

also persists for list learning; our

2
Preliminaries

We will denote the unlabeled data domain by
\mathcal{X}
and identify the label space
\mathcal{Y}
by
[k]=\{1,2,\dots,k\}
. A hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
is a subset of
[k]^{\mathcal{X}}
. The error of any hypothesis
h
with respect to a data distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\mathcal{X}\times[k]
is defined as
\mathrm{err}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)=\operatorname{{Pr}}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}[h(x)\neq y]
; for a list hypothesis
\mu
, the error is
\mathrm{err}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)=\operatorname{{Pr}}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}[y\notin\mu(x)]
. A distribution
\mathcal{D}
is
realizable
by
\mathcal{H}
if
\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\mathrm{err}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)=0
.

Definition 1

(PAC learning
[
18
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}\subseteq[k]^{\mathcal{X}}
be a hypothesis class. We say that
\mathcal{H}
is (list) PAC learnable by a learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}
with sample complexity
m_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{H}}:(0,1)\times(0,1)\to\mathds{N}
if for every
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)^{2}
, every distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\mathcal{X}\times[k]
realizable by
\mathcal{H}
, for
m\geq m_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{H}}(\varepsilon,\delta)
,
\operatorname{{Pr}}_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}[\mathrm{err}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A}(S))\geq\varepsilon]\leq\delta
, where
\mathcal{A}(S)
is the (list) hypothesis output by the learning algorithm on input
S
.

The definition of agnostic PAC learning allows for arbitrary distributions over
\mathcal{X}\times[k]
not necessarily realizable by
\mathcal{H}
, and the guarantee required of the algorithm is instead
\operatorname{{Pr}}_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}[\mathrm{err}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A}(S))\geq\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\mathrm{err}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)+\varepsilon]\leq\delta
, for
m\geq m_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{H}}(\varepsilon,\delta)
.

We now define several combinatorial quantities relevant to multiclass and list learning. These quantities will be parameterized in terms of a list size
\ell\geq 1
, where
\ell
appears in the superscript of the quantity (e.g.,
d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{DS}}
). When
\ell=1
, we sometimes drop the superscript for convenience, and simply refer to the corresponding quantity without the superscript (e.g.,
d_{\mathrm{DS}}
). For a sequence
S\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
, we denote the restriction of
\mathcal{H}
on
S
as
\mathcal{H}|_{S}
.

Definition 2

(
\ell
-DS dimension
[
6
,
4
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}\subseteq[k]^{\mathcal{X}}
be a hypothesis class for
k\geq 2
, and let
S\in\mathcal{X}^{d}
be a sequence. Let us think of the members of
\mathcal{H}|_{S}
as vectors in
[k]^{d}
. For any
i\in[d]
, we say that
f,g\in\mathcal{H}|_{S}
are
i
-neighbors if
f_{i}\neq g_{i}
and
f_{j}=g_{j},\;\forall j\neq i
.
For any
1\leq\ell<k
, we say that
\mathcal{H}

\ell
-DS shatters
S
if there exists a non-empty
\mathcal{F}|_{S}\subseteq\mathcal{H}|_{S},|\mathcal{F}|_{S}|<\infty
such that
\forall f\in\mathcal{F}|_{S},\;\forall i\in[d],

f
has at least
\ell

i
-neighbors in
\mathcal{F}|_{S}
. The
\ell
-DS dimension of
\mathcal{H}
, denoted as
d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{DS}}=d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{DS}}(\mathcal{H})
, is the largest integer
d
such that
\mathcal{H}

\ell
-DS shatters some sequence
S\in\mathcal{X}^{d}
.

A different dimension that will also be relevant to our discussion is the
\ell
-Natarajan dimension.

Definition 3

(
\ell
-Natarajan dimension
[
13
,
12
,
4
]
)
.

A hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}\subseteq[k]^{\mathcal{X}}

\ell
-Natarajan shatters a sequence
S\in\mathcal{X}^{d}
if there exist
(\ell+1)
-sized lists
y_{i}\in\{Y\subseteq[k]:|Y|=\ell+1\}
,
i=1,\dots,d
, such that
\prod_{i=1}^{d}y_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{H}|_{S}
. The
\ell
-Natarajan dimension of
\mathcal{H}
, denoted as
d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{Nat}}=d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{Nat}}(\mathcal{H})
, is the largest integer
d
such that
\mathcal{H}

\ell
-Natarajan shatters some sequence
S\in\mathcal{X}^{d}
.

Observe that
d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{Nat}}\leq d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{DS}}
for every
\ell\geq 1
. We now define a key combinatorial object for a hypothesis class: the one-inclusion graph.

Definition 4

(One-inclusion graph
[
10
,
15
]
)
.

The one-inclusion graph of
\mathcal{H}\subseteq[k]^{n}
is a hypergraph
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{H})=(V,E)
that is defined as follows. The vertex set is
V=\mathcal{H}
. For each
i\in[n]

.

Note that when

Definition 5

(
\ell

Let

\mathcal{H}\subseteq[k]^{n}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Dens}^{\ell}(\mathcal{H})=\frac{1}{|V|}\sum_{e\in E}(|e|-\ell)_{+},

Observe that for every
\mathcal{H}\subseteq[k]^{n}
,
\mathrm{Dens}^{\ell}(\mathcal{H})\leq n

Definition 6

(Maximum
\ell

Let
\mathcal{H}\subseteq[k]^{\mathcal{X}}

for sample size

\displaystyle\mu^{\ell}_{\mathcal{H}}(n)=\max_{S\in\mathcal{X}^{n}}\max_{\mathcal{F}|_{S}\subseteq\mathcal{H}|_{S},|\mathcal{F}|_{S}|<\infty}\mathrm{Dens}^{\ell}(\mathcal{F}|_{S}).

Our proof of

that enable our result. Let

(w_{1},\dots,w_{n})\in[k]^{n}

\displaystyle\left\{f:W\to\mathds{R}~\Big|~f(w)=w_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}w_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\dots w_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}\;\forall w\in W,\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 68.28644pt\left.\alpha_{i}\in\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}\;\forall i\in[n],\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 68.28644pt\left.\left|\{i\in[n]:\alpha_{i}\geq\ell\}\right|\leq s\right\}.

(9)

With the

\mathcal{M}^{\ell}_{s}(W)

The breakthrough insight in

. While this structural characterization is contained in the proof of Theorem 2.1 in

Lemma 3.1

(Spanning Lemma
[
7
]
)
.

Let
W\subseteq[k]^{n}
be a hypothesis class. For all integers
\ell\geq 1
and
s\geq d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{DS}}(W)
,
\mathcal{M}^{\ell}_{s}(W)
spans
\mathcal{V}_{W}
.

We build upon the linear-algebraic characterization in the spanning lemma to show our main
Theorem

1
. Before we proceed to the formal details of the proof, let us sketch the proof for the
\ell=1
case, which is simpler and captures the main ideas. The core intuition is the following: any monomial
w_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\dots w_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}
in the spanning set, which has
\alpha_{i}=0
, is
constant
on every edge in direction
i
in the one-inclusion graph of the hypothesis class. Therefore, one can consider the
subspace
of functions in
\mathcal{V}_{W}
that have this defining property, and has dimension
equal
to the number of edges in direction
i
. Any monomial within the spanning set that has
\alpha_{i}=0
belongs to this subspace. But crucially, the total number of
linearly independent
monomials in the spanning set that have
\alpha_{i}=0
must
lower-bound
the dimension of the subspace, unlocking the key inequality.

More concretely, let
W\subseteq[k]^{n}
be a hypothesis class with DS dimension
d_{\mathrm{DS}}
. In this case, for
s=d_{\mathrm{DS}}
, each monomial in the spanning set
\mathcal{M}^{1}_{s}(W)
is a monomial in at most
s
“active” coordinates

, where
\alpha_{i}>0
. Given that
\mathcal{M}^{1}_{s}(W)
spans
\mathcal{V}_{W}
, choose a basis
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{1}_{s}(W)
. Since
\mathcal{V}_{W}
has dimension
|W|
, the basis
\mathcal{B}
has exactly
|W|
monomials. The key definition now is that of a
direction-wise subspace

U_{i}
for every direction
i\in[n]
. This subspace comprises of all the monomials that are constant when restricted to an edge in direction
i
in the one-inclusion graph
\mathcal{G}(W)
. As a toy example with
n=2
, consider
W=\{(2,1),(3,1),(4,2),(5,2),(6,2)\}
. Then,
U_{1}
comprises of all the vectors in
\mathds{R}^{5}
of the form
(a,a,b,b,b)
. We can readily see that the dimension of
U_{i}
is exactly the number of different behaviors realized by
W
on
[n]\setminus\{i\}
, or equivalently, the number of distinct edges in
\mathcal{G}(W)
in direction
i
— let us call this number
N_{i}
.

Now, consider the set of monomials
\mathcal{B}^{0}_{i}
in the basis
\mathcal{B}
for which the coordinate
i
is
not
active (i.e.,
\alpha_{i}=0
), and let
\mathcal{B}^{1}_{i}=\mathcal{B}\setminus\mathcal{B}^{0}_{i}
. Observe that every monomial in
\mathcal{B}^{0}_{i}
belongs to
U_{i}
, since it evaluates to the same number on the members of any edge in direction
i
. However, since all the monomials in
\mathcal{B}^{0}_{i}
are linearly independent, it must hold that

\displaystyle|\mathcal{B}^{0}_{i}|\leq\dim(U_{i})=N_{i}\quad\implies\quad|W|-N_{i}\leq|W|-|\mathcal{B}^{0}_{i}|=|\mathcal{B}^{1}_{i}|.

Now let us sum this last inequality over all
i\in[n]
. The term on the left is equal to

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(|W|-N_{i})=\sum_{i\in[n]}\sum_{e_{i,a}\in\mathcal{G}(W)}(|e_{i,a}|-1)=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{G}(W)}(|e|-1)=|W|\cdot\mathrm{Dens}^{1}(W).

On the other hand, summing the term on the right, namely
\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\mathcal{B}^{1}_{i}|
, simply counts, for every monomial
b\in\mathcal{B}
, the number of coordinates
i
at which it is active. Since
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{1}_{s}(W)
, and every monomial in the spanning set has at most
s
active coordinates, we get that this sum is at most
s\cdot|\mathcal{B}|=s|W|=d_{\mathrm{DS}}|W|
. Dividing by
|W|
on both sides gives the inequality in the theorem statement.

Theorem

1
for convenience, and prove it for the general case of
\ell\geq 1
.

(10)

We can verify that

. We now claim that

More generally, observe that the edge sets

\displaystyle\dim(U_{i})=\sum_{a\in T_{i}}\dim(W_{a})=\sum_{a\in T_{i}}\min(\ell,|e_{i,a}|).

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{<\ell}_{i}:=\{f\in\mathcal{B}~\mid~f(w)=w_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\dots w_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}\;\forall w\in W,\;\alpha_{i}<\ell\}.

\displaystyle|\mathcal{B}^{<\ell}_{i}|\leq\dim(U_{i})=\sum_{a\in T_{i}}\min(\ell,|e_{i,a}|)

Remark 2

(Extension to Infinite Label Spaces)
.

We note that

1
also holds when

. Observe that the definition of
\mu^{\ell}_{\mathcal{H}}(n)
(

) only considers restrictions
\mathcal{F}|_{S}
that have finite size on sequences
S\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
. Thus, for any such
\mathcal{F}|_{S}=W
, the number of possible labels that can be realized by members of

Remark 3

(Optimality of the Constant)
.

We note that the upper bound in
Theorem

1
is tight including the constant. To see this, consider the hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}=[k]^{s}\times[\ell]^{m-s}
as

gets large. We have that
d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{DS}}(\mathcal{H})=s
, whereas

\displaystyle\lceil\mu_{\mathcal{H}}(m)\rceil\geq\mathrm{Dens}^{\ell}(\mathcal{H})=\frac{1}{k^{s}\ell^{m-s}}\left(s\cdot k^{s-1}\cdot\ell^{m-s}\cdot(k-\ell)\right)=s\left(1-\frac{\ell}{k}\right)\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}s=d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{DS}}(\mathcal{H}).

On the other hand, if we consider the definition

originally considered by
[

]
(and defined in (

)),
[

]
already show the lower bound

. As discussed in

, meaning that the upper bound implied by

4
Concluding Thoughts

Our proof for upper-bounding
\mu^{\ell}_{\mathcal{H}}(n)
by
d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{DS}}
is inherently algebraic. In contrast, classical proofs of this result for the binary case (where the DS dimension becomes equal to the VC dimension) have a distinctly combinatorial flavor
[
10
,
11
]
. For example, the elegant proof by
[
11
]
is based on the classical
shifting
operation. In the binary case, the VC dimension and the one-inclusion graph respect nice invariances upon shifting — shifting does not increase the VC dimension, and does not decrease the number of edges in the one-inclusion graph. When
k>2
, neither of these properties hold (see Example 19 in
[
2
]
). Indeed, these pathologies appear to obstruct most natural inductive proof strategies while working with the DS dimension.

In the discussion section of their paper,
[
7
]
comment on the implications of combinatorial proofs for DS dimension-related results beyond their mere appeal. Namely, while they used their algebraic characterization to prove an optimal Sauer’s lemma for multiclass classes, they speculated that other proofs of their result that have a more combinatorial flavor could lead to improving the sample complexity of multiclass learning. It is thus interesting to us that their algebraic characterization itself turned out to be one way to complete the puzzle.

With regards to future directions, now that the precise dependence of the sample complexity on the DS dimension seems to be figured out, it would be interesting to focus on the other relevant parameters and pin down their optimal dependence, particularly in the list learning setting. For example, there are still polynomial factor gaps in the dependence on the list size
\ell
in the upper and lower bounds for sample complexity. In a different direction, it would be interesting to further explore what could be achieved using algebraic methods in multiclass and list learning.

for which the maximum outdegree of any vertex according to that orientation is minimized. To predict a list of labels for the test point
x
, the algorithm then looks at the edge corresponding to the labeled training data
(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{n},y_{n})
. The orientation
\sigma^{\ell}
would have oriented this edge to some set of at most
\ell
hypotheses. The algorithm outputs the list of labels that these hypotheses assign to
x
.

Given any training dataset
S=\{(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{n},y_{n})\}
realizable by
\mathcal{H}
, we will be concerned with the
leave-one-out error
of
\mathcal{A}
, defined as

\displaystyle M_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}\left[y_{i}\notin\widehat{\mu}^{\ell}_{S_{-i}}(x_{i})\right],

where
S_{-i}=S\setminus\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}
, and
\widehat{\mu}^{\ell}_{S_{-i}}=\mathcal{A}(S_{-i})
is the output of the one-inclusion graph list predictor trained on
S_{-i}
. Because the algorithm constructs the same one-inclusion graph irrespective of the point
x_{i}
that is held out as the test point, it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}\left[y_{i}\notin\widehat{\mu}^{\ell}_{S_{-i}}(x_{i})\right]=\mathsf{outdeg}^{\ell}(y;\sigma^{\ell}),

where
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
is the vertex corresponding to the ground-truth labels.

Now, the proof of Lemma 3.20 in
[
7
]
shows that for any class
W\subseteq[k]^{n}
, there exists an
\ell
-list orientation
\sigma^{\ell}
of the edges of
\mathcal{G}(W)
such that

\displaystyle\max_{v\in\mathcal{G}(W)}\mathsf{outdeg}^{\ell}(v;\sigma^{\ell})\leq\left\lceil\max_{\mathcal{F}\subseteq W}\mathrm{Dens}^{\ell}(\mathcal{F})\right\rceil\leq\left\lceil\mu_{W}(n)\right\rceil.

(11)

But by
Theorem

1
, the right-hand side above is upper-bounded by
d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{DS}}(W)
. Plugging this into the leave-one-out error bound above, we get that

\displaystyle M_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}\left[y_{i}\notin\widehat{\mu}^{\ell}_{S_{-i}}(x_{i})\right]\leq d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{DS}}(\mathcal{H}).

(12)

We now observe that the one-inclusion graph list predictor
\mathcal{A}
satisfies both the assumptions — symmetry and bounded leave-one-out error — required of Theorem 2.1 in
[
1
]
, with the loss function
L:[k]^{\leq\ell}\times[k]\to[0,1]
interpreted as
L(\mu,y)=\mathds{1}[y\notin\mu]
. We can therefore apply that theorem, and obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{4}{3n}\sum_{t=n/4}^{n-1}\mathrm{err}_{\mathcal{D}}\left(\widehat{\mu}^{\ell}_{S_{\leq t}}\right)\leq 4.82\left(\frac{M_{n}+\log(2/\delta)}{n}\right)\leq 4.82\left(\frac{d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{DS}}(\mathcal{H})+\log(2/\delta)}{n}\right),

(13)

with probability at least
1-\delta
over the draw of
S=\{(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{n},y_{n})\}
from
\mathcal{D}^{n}
, and where
S_{\leq t}=\{(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{t},y_{t})\}
. But then, consider the list predictor defined by the
\mathsf{Top}\text{-}\ell
vote

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}^{\ell}_{S}(x)=\mathsf{Top}\text{-}\ell\left(\widehat{\mu}^{\ell}_{S_{\leq n/4}}(x),\dots,\widehat{\mu}^{\ell}_{S_{\leq n-1}}(x)\right),

where
\mathsf{Top}\text{-}\ell
returns the list of the first
\ell
labels sorted in descending order according to the count of lists in
\widehat{\mu}^{\ell}_{S_{\leq n/4}}(x),\dots,\widehat{\mu}^{\ell}_{S_{\leq n-1}}(x)
that they occur in. Crucially, whenever the
\mathsf{Top}\text{-}\ell
vote
\widehat{\mu}^{\ell}_{S}(x)

does not
contain a label
y
, it must necessarily hold that at least
\frac{3n/4}{\ell+1}
out of the
3n/4
lists
\widehat{\mu}^{\ell}_{S_{\leq n/4}}(x),\dots,\widehat{\mu}^{\ell}_{S_{\leq n-1}}(x)
do not contain
y
; otherwise, the
\mathsf{Top}\text{-}\ell
vote would have picked
y
. In conclusion, we get that

\displaystyle\mathrm{err}_{\mathcal{D}}(\widehat{\mu}^{\ell}_{S})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathds{1}\left[y\notin\widehat{\mu}^{\ell}_{S}(x)\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[(\ell+1)\left(\frac{4}{3n}\sum_{t=n/4}^{n-1}\mathds{1}[y\notin\widehat{\mu}^{\ell}_{S_{\leq t}}(x)]\right)\right]

\displaystyle=(\ell+1)\cdot\frac{4}{3n}\sum_{t=n/4}^{n-1}\mathrm{err}_{\mathcal{D}}\left(\widehat{\mu}^{\ell}_{S_{\leq t}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 4.82(\ell+1)\left(\frac{d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{DS}}(\mathcal{H})+\log(2/\delta)}{n}\right).

(from (
13
))

∎

Remark 4

(Extension to Infinite Label Spaces)
.

When considering an infinite label space, the only step above that needs additional justification is the outdegree bound in (
11
). With an infinite label space, the one-inclusion graph
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{H}|_{x_{1},\dots,x_{n},x})
may be infinite. While
[
1
,
7
]
show the existence of an
\ell
-list orientation that has
\ell
-outdegree at most
\mu(n)
only for finite one-inclusion graphs, a compactness argument similar to that given in Appendix B in
[
2
]
/ Appendix A in
[
4
]
/ Appendix C.1 in
[
14
]
can be used to show the existence of an
\ell
-list orientation on the infinite graph, which also satisfies the required
\ell
-outdegree bound. With this additional consideration, the rest of the proof holds as is.

A.2
Agnostic Case

Proof of
Corollary

1.4
.

Fix
\mathcal{D}
to be any arbitrary distribution over
\mathcal{X}\times[k]
. We follow the proof structure of
[
5
]
.

Step 1: Approximation by Finite List Cover.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be the list learner from
Corollary

1.2
which achieves near-optimal sample complexity in the realizable setting. Note that this learner is deterministic. Consider any sample
S=\{(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{m},y_{m})\}
that is realizable by
\mathcal{H}
. By a standard minimax argument, there exists a compression function
\kappa
which maps
S
to a subsample
S^{\prime}\in S^{k_{1}(m)}
, where
k_{1}(m)=O(\ell d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{DS}}\log m)
, and a list reconstruction function
\rho
with list size
|\rho(S^{\prime})(x)|\leq O(\ell\log m)
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, such that
y_{i}\in\rho(S^{\prime})(x_{i})
for every
i\in[m]
.

To see this, fix any distribution
\mathcal{P}
over
S
. Since
S
is realizable by
\mathcal{H}
, the distribution
\mathcal{P}
is also realizable by
\mathcal{H}
. Then, by the guarantee in
Corollary

1.2
for
\mathcal{A}
, we know that for
d:=O(\ell d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{DS}})
, there exists
T\in S^{d}
for which

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{P}}[y\notin\mathcal{A}(T)(x)]\leq 1/3.

By the minimax theorem
[
17
]
, there exists a distribution
\mathcal{Q}
over sequences
T\in S^{d}
, such that for every fixed
(x_{i},y_{i})\in S
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}_{T\sim\mathcal{Q}}[y_{i}\notin\mathcal{A}(T)(x_{i})]\leq 1/3.

Consider sampling
j
sequences
T_{1},\dots,T_{j}
i.i.d. from
\mathcal{Q}
, for
j=O(\log m)
. We have that

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}_{T_{1},\dots,T_{j}\sim\mathcal{Q}^{j}}\left[y_{i}\notin\bigcup_{r=1}^{j}\mathcal{A}(T_{r})(x_{i})\right]\leq 3^{-j}\leq 1/2m.

By a union bound over
i\in[m]
and an application of the probabilistic method, we get that there exist
T_{1},\dots,T_{j}
such that for every
i\in[m]
, it holds that
y_{i}\in\bigcup_{r=1}^{j}\mathcal{A}(T_{r})(x_{i})
. We thus set
\kappa(S)=S^{\prime}=(T_{1},\dots,T_{j})
, and
\rho(S^{\prime})(x)=\bigcup_{r=1}^{j}\mathcal{A}(T_{r})(x)
. Note that
|\rho(S^{\prime})(x)|\leq O(\ell\log m)
as required.

Given this, consider obtaining a sample
S_{1}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n_{1}}
from the data distribution
\mathcal{D}
, for
n_{1}
to be specified later. Define the finite list cover family:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(S_{1}):=\{\rho(S^{\prime}):S^{\prime}=(T_{1},\dots,T_{O(\log n_{1})}),T_{r}\in S_{1}^{O(d)}\;\forall r\}.

We have that
|\mathcal{F}(S_{1})|=n_{1}^{O(d\log(n_{1}))}=n_{1}^{O(\ell d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{DS}}\log(n_{1}))}
, and every member of
\mathcal{F}(S_{1})
is a list function mapping to a list of size
O(\ell\log n_{1})
. Next, fix any
h\in\mathcal{H}
, and let
S_{1}(h)=\{(x,y)\in S:y=h(x)\}
, so that
S_{1}(h)
is realizable by
h
. By our compression argument above, we have that there exists
\mu_{h}\in\mathcal{F}(S_{1})
such that
y\in\mu_{h}(x)
for every
(x,y)\in S_{1}(h)
. In other words,
\mu_{h}
has zero empirical loss on
S_{1}
under the loss function

\displaystyle\mathds{1}[h(x)=y,y\notin\mu(x)].

Then, by the standard “compression implies generalization” argument (e.g., Theorem 30.2 in
[
16
]
), we get that for every
h\in\mathcal{H}
, with probability at least
1-\delta/3
over the draw of
S_{1}
, there exists
\mu_{h}\in\mathcal{F}(S_{1})
such that

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}[h(x)=y,y\notin\mu_{h}(x)]\leq O\left(\frac{\ell d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{DS}}\log^{2}(n_{1})+\log(1/\delta)}{n_{1}}\right).

Step 2: Multiplicative Weights.

Condition on the high-probability good event on the sample
S_{1}
above, and let
\mathcal{F}(S_{1})
be the finite cover of list hypotheses constructed there. Now consider obtaining a fresh sample
S_{2}=\{(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{T},y_{T})\}\sim\mathcal{D}^{T}
, for
T
to be specified later. We run the multiplicative weights procedure given in Algorithm 3 in
[
5
]
. Namely, we initialize
w_{1}(\mu)=1
for every
\mu\in\mathcal{F}(S_{1})
, and proceed in rounds
t=1,2,\dots,T
. At round
t
, we sample
\mu_{t}\sim p_{t}
, where
p_{t}
is a distribution over
\mathcal{F}(S_{1})
satisfying
p_{t}(\mu)\propto w_{t}(\mu)
. Then, for every
\mu\in\mathcal{F}(S_{1})
, the reward
r_{t}(\mu)
is defined as

\displaystyle r_{t}(\mu)=\mathds{1}\left[y_{t}\in\mu(x_{t}),\,y_{t}\notin\bigcup_{r<t}\mu_{r}(x_{t})\right].

We correspondingly update
w_{t+1}(\mu)=w_{t}(\mu)\exp(r_{t}(\mu)/2)
for every
\mu\in\mathcal{F}(S_{1})
. At the end of
T
rounds, we obtain the list hypothesis
\nu
, defined as

\displaystyle\nu(x)=\bigcup_{t=1}^{T-1}\mu_{t}(x).

We have that
|\nu(x)|\leq O(\ell T\log n_{1})
. Furthermore, the analysis of the multiplicative weights procedure from
[
5
]
extends verbatim to our setting. Namely, Theorem 3.4 in
[
5
]
guarantees that for every
\mu\in\mathcal{F}(S_{1})
, with probability at least
1-\delta/3
(over the draw of
S_{2}
and the multiplicative weights procedure, which then results in
\nu
), the list hypothesis
\nu
satisfies

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[y\in\mu(x),\,y\notin\nu(x)\right]\leq O\left(\frac{\log(|\mathcal{F}(S_{1})|)+\log(1/\delta)}{T}\right).

Learning a List-Bounded Classifier.

Suppose both the good events from Steps 1 and 2 have occurred, and consider the list hypothesis
\nu
constructed at the end of Step 2. Draw a fresh sample
S_{3}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n_{3}}
, for
n_{3}
to be specified later.

With respect to
\nu
, define the “inside-menu” loss
L^{\nu}_{\mathcal{D}}
of a predictor as

\displaystyle L^{\nu}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mu)=\operatorname{{Pr}}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}[y\in\nu(x),\,y\notin\mu(x)],\quad\text{for list predictors $\mu$},

\displaystyle L^{\nu}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)=\operatorname{{Pr}}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}[y\in\nu(x),\,y\neq h(x)],\quad\text{for single-label predictors $h$}.

Thus,
L^{\nu}_{\mathcal{D}}
only penalizes a predictor on points
(x,y)
where
y\in\nu(x)
.
Our goal now in this step is to derive an agnostic learner for this loss. For this, we will extensively utilize the machinery from
[
4
]
and
[
7
]
.

For any sample
S
, let

\displaystyle h_{S}\in\argmin_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{(x,y)\in S}\mathds{1}[y\in\nu(x),\,y\neq h(x)].

Then, let

\displaystyle S^{+}=\{(x,y)\in S:y\in\nu(x),h_{S}(x)=y\}.

Note that
S^{+}
is realizable by both
\mathcal{H}
and the list
\nu
. We are then in a position to directly instantiate Lemma 7.5 from
[
4
]
for the purposes of obtaining a sample compression on
S^{+}
of size
O(\ell^{6}d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{Nat}}\log(p)\log(|S^{+}|))
, where
p=O(\ell T\log n_{1})
is the list size of
\nu
. However, we will save a factor of
\ell^{2}
by instead routing through an optimization in
[
7
]
, which lets us derive a sample compression of size
k_{3}:=O(\ell^{4}d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{Nat}}\log(p)\log(|S^{+}|))
instead. We choose not to repeat all their arguments here, and only provide a sketch for how this compression scheme is constructed: Consider the subclass
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{H}|_{S^{+}}
comprising of all the patterns realized by
\mathcal{H}
on
S^{+}
that are also realizable by the list
\nu
. Now,
[
7
, Lemma 3.20]
shows that there is an
\ell
-list orientation
\sigma^{\ell}
of the one-inclusion graph of this class that has maximum
\ell
-outdegree at most its
\ell
-exponential
dimension, which, by Lemma 6.6 in
[
4
]
, is at most
O(\ell^{2}d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{Nat}}\log(p)
. This outdegree bound allows one to use the
one-inclusion graph predictor
as a weak list learner, whereupon, by a similar minimax argument as that in Step 1, one obtains a valid compression and reconstruction for
S^{+}
. This last part is similar to the proof of Lemma 7.5 in
[
4
]
, and requires: (1) the weak learner to have error
O(1/\ell)
, and (2) a Chernoff bound to ensure that a
\mathsf{Top}\text{-}\ell
vote over the weak learner predictions (i.e., the output of the reconstructor) contains the correct label. These latter two steps together incur an additional
\ell^{2}\log(|S^{+}|)
cost, giving a total compression set size of
O(\ell^{4}d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{Nat}}\log(p)\log(|S^{+}|))
as claimed.

The list predictor
\widehat{\mu}
output by the reconstructor of this compression scheme satisfies that
\widehat{\mu}(x)\subseteq\nu(x)
for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
, and furthermore, for every
(x,y)\in S^{+}
, it holds that
y\in\widehat{\mu}(x)
. In other words,
\widehat{\mu}
has zero empirical inside-menu loss
\widehat{L}^{\nu}_{S^{+}}
on
S^{+}
. By definition of
h_{S}
, it then also follows that
\widehat{\mu}
has minimum empirical inside-menu loss
\widehat{L}^{\nu}_{S}
on
S
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\widehat{L}^{\nu}_{S}(\widehat{\mu})\leq\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\widehat{L}^{\nu}_{S}(h),

and hence, this compression scheme is an agnostic sample compression scheme for the inside-menu loss. By a generalization bound for agnostic compression schemes (e.g., see Theorem 11 in
[
4
]
), we get that with probability at least
1-\delta/3
over
S_{3}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n_{3}}
,

\displaystyle L^{\nu}_{\mathcal{D}}(\widehat{\mu})\leq\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}L^{\nu}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)+O\left(\sqrt{\frac{k_{3}\log n_{3}+\log(1/\delta)}{n_{3}}}\right).

(14)

This implies, by a similar chain of inequalities as in
[
5
, Theorem 3.5]
, that with probability at least
1-\delta/3
over
S_{3}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n_{3}}
, the following inequality holds uniformly for all
h\in\mathcal{H}
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{err}_{\mathcal{D}}(\widehat{\mu})-\mathrm{err}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{{Pr}}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}[y\notin\nu(x),h(x)=y]+O\left(\sqrt{\frac{k_{3}\log n_{3}+\log(1/\delta)}{n_{3}}}\right).

Putting it all together.

Let
h^{\star}\in\mathcal{H}
satisfy that
\mathrm{err}_{\mathcal{D}}(h^{\star})\leq\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\mathrm{err}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)+\varepsilon/4
. Condition on the good event in Step 1 over the sample
S_{1}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n_{1}}
, which happens with probability at least
1-\delta/3
, to obtain a list cover
\mathcal{F}(S_{1})
with the guarantee that there exists
\mu^{\star}\in\mathcal{F}(S_{1})
for which

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}[h^{\star}(x)=y,\,y\notin\mu^{\star}(x)]\leq O\left(\frac{\ell d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{DS}}\log^{2}(n_{1})+\log(1/\delta)}{n_{1}}\right).

(15)

Next, condition further on the good event in Step 2 over the sample
S_{2}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n_{2}}
and the multiplicative weights procedure, which happens with probability at least
1-\delta/3
, and results in a list hypothesis
\nu
with the guarantee

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}[y\in\mu^{\star}(x),\,y\notin\nu(x)]\leq O\left(\frac{\log(|\mathcal{F}(S_{1})|)+\log(1/\delta)}{T}\right).

(16)

A union bound gives us that with probability at least
1-2\delta/3
over Steps 1 and 2,

\displaystyle\operatorname{{Pr}}[h^{\star}(x)=y,\,y\notin\nu(x)]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{{Pr}}[h^{\star}(x)=y,\,y\notin\nu(x),\,y\notin\mu^{\star}(x)]+\operatorname{{Pr}}[h^{\star}(x)=y,\,y\notin\nu(x),\,y\in\mu^{\star}(x)]

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{{Pr}}[h^{\star}(x)=y,\,y\notin\mu^{\star}(x)]+\operatorname{{Pr}}[y\notin\nu(x),\,y\in\mu^{\star}(x)]

\displaystyle\leq O\left(\frac{\ell d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{DS}}\log^{2}(n_{1})+\log(1/\delta)}{n_{1}}+\frac{\log(|\mathcal{F}(S_{1})|)+\log(1/\delta)}{T}\right),

(17)

where in the last inequality, we combined (
15
) and (
16
).

Finally, condition on the good event in Step 3, which happens with probability at least
1-\delta/3
over the draw of
S_{3}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n_{3}}
, and results in an
\ell
-list predictor
\widehat{\mu}
that satisfies

\displaystyle\mathrm{err}_{\mathcal{D}}(\widehat{\mu})-\mathrm{err}_{\mathcal{D}}(h^{\star})

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{{Pr}}[y\notin\nu(x),h^{\star}(x)=y]+O\left(\sqrt{\frac{k_{3}\log n_{3}+\log(1/\delta)}{n_{3}}}\right).

Taking a union bound over the event in (
17
), we get that with probability at least
1-\delta
over all the steps, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathrm{err}_{\mathcal{D}}(\widehat{\mu})-\mathrm{err}_{\mathcal{D}}(h^{\star})

\displaystyle\leq O\left(\frac{\ell d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{DS}}\log^{2}(n_{1})+\log(1/\delta)}{n_{1}}+\frac{\log(|\mathcal{F}(S_{1})|)+\log(1/\delta)}{T}+\sqrt{\frac{k_{3}\log n_{3}+\log(1/\delta)}{n_{3}}}\right)

\displaystyle=O\left(\frac{\ell d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{DS}}\log^{2}(n_{1})+\log(1/\delta)}{n_{1}}+\frac{\ell d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{DS}}\log^{2}(n_{1})+\log(1/\delta)}{T}+\sqrt{\frac{k_{3}\log n_{3}+\log(1/\delta)}{n_{3}}}\right).

We set
n_{1}
,
T
and
n_{3}
such that each of the terms in the parentheses above is at most
\varepsilon/4
. This requires setting

\displaystyle n_{1}=\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{\ell d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{DS}}+\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon}\right),\quad T=\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{\ell d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{DS}}+\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon}\right),\quad n_{3}=\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{\ell^{4}d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{Nat}}+\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right).

Recalling that
\mathrm{err}_{\mathcal{D}}(h^{\star})\leq\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\mathrm{err}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)+\varepsilon/4
, we then get

\displaystyle\mathrm{err}_{\mathcal{D}}(\widehat{\mu})\leq\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\mathrm{err}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)+\varepsilon,

which is the required agnostic list learning guarantee. The total sample complexity is

\displaystyle n_{1}+T+n_{3}=\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{\ell d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{DS}}}{\varepsilon}+\frac{\ell^{4}d^{\ell}_{\mathrm{Nat}}+\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right).

∎.
