Polynomial Kernels for Spanning Tree with Diversity Requirements
Petr A. Golovach, Diptapriyo Majumdar, Saket Saurabh

Abstract
Given a connected undirected graph G G , a spanning tree is a subgraph T T of G G such that V ⁡ ( T ) = V ⁡ ( G ) V(T)=V(G) and T T is a tree. A collection of ℓ \ell spanning trees T 1 , … , T ℓ T_{1},\ldots,T_{\ell} is pairwise k k -diverse if for every i ≠ j i\neq j , | E ⁡ ( T i ) ​ △ ​ E ​ ( T j ) | ≥ k |E(T_{i})\triangle E(T_{j})|\geq k . Given a connected undirected graph G G and integers p , q , k , ℓ p,q,k,\ell , Leaf&Internal-Constrained Diverse Spanning Trees asks whether there are ℓ \ell distinct spanning trees T 1 , … , T ℓ T_{1},\ldots,T_{\ell} of G G that are pairwise k k -diverse such that each tree has at least p p leaves and at least q q internal vertices. Similarly, Leaf&Non-terminal-Constrained Diverse Spanning Trees takes a connected undirected graph G G , V NT ⊆ V ⁡ ( G ) V_{\rm NT}\subseteq V(G) , and three integers p , k , ℓ p,k,\ell , and asks if G G has ℓ \ell spanning trees that are pairwise k k -diverse, and each has at least p p leaves and contains the vertices of V NT V_{\rm NT} as internal. We consider these two problems from the kernelization perspective and provide polynomial kernels for Leaf&Internal-Constrained Diverse Spanning Trees and Leaf&Non-terminal-Constrained Diverse Spanning Trees , when parameterized by p + q + k + ℓ p+q+k+\ell and p + | V NT | + k + ℓ p+|V_{\rm NT}|+k+\ell , respectively.

Introduction

The
diversity
paradigm in the Parameterized Complexity framework was formulated by Fellows and Rosamond
[
22
]
, but the concept was known even before, e.g., in Integer Programming
[
14
]
.
The paradigm is motivated by the common situation that arises in various applications when it is not sufficient to find just one high-quality solution to a combinatorial optimization problem due to numerous side constraints. Furthermore, it makes no sense to try to find all optimal solutions because typically there are too many — it is either computationally infeasible to list all of them or, if the first task is somehow achieved, impossible to analyze the huge list of solutions.
Instead, it is preferable to work with a reasonably sized sample of sufficiently good solutions, which may be analyzed by making use of side information.
Such a sample should represent the variety of solutions, and the natural way to achieve this is to demand that the solutions be “far apart”.
This “far apart” notion can be mathematically expressed in various ways.
However, when dealing with subset problems, it is common to measure the diversity of a sample as either the minimum pairwise Hamming distance between solutions, or the sum of all pairwise Hamming distances, or the cardinality of the union of the solutions.
Arguably, the first approach is most popular, especially in the context of Parameterized Complexity, as the sample size and the minimum Hamming distance are natural parameters in this case.
The diversity paradigm attracted a lot of attention in recent years (see
[
2
,
3
,
15
,
19
,
24
,
25
,
30
,
32
,
33
,
40
]
and the bibliography therein). In our paper, we consider the problem of finding diverse spanning trees with additional constraints on leaves and internal vertices.

Spanning trees are fundamental in Graph Theory and Computer Science, and various types of spanning tree problems naturally arise in applications. One of these variants is the
Max-Leaf Spanning Tree

problem
[
6
,
17
,
21
,
34
,
39
,
44
]
. Formally,
Max-Leaf Spanning Tree
asks, given a graph
G
and an integer
p
, whether
G
has a spanning tree with at least
p
leaves. The
Max-Internal Spanning Tree
problem
[
4
,
8
,
23
,
31
,
35
,
37
,
38
,
42
,
43
]
is dual to
Max-Leaf Spanning Tree
, and given a graph
G
and an integer
q
, asks whether
G
has a spanning tree with at least
q
internal vertices. The input to
Non-Terminal Spanning Tree

[
31
]
contains a graph
G
with a set of
non-terminal
vertices
V_{\rm NT}\subseteq V(G)
, and the question is whether
G
contains a spanning tree with the vertices of
V_{\rm NT}
being internal.

We consider the “diverse” variants of these problems. For an integer
k\geq 0
, two spanning trees
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are said to be
k
-diverse
if
|E(T_{1})\triangle E(T_{2})|\geq k
.
Respectively, a collection of spanning trees
T_{1},\ldots,T_{\ell}
is
pairwise
k
-diverse
if for every
i\neq j
,
T_{i}
and
T_{j}
are
k
-diverse. Since our approach for the considered variants of
Max-Leaf Spanning Tree
and
Max-Internal Spanning Tree
is based on similar arguments, we combine the requirements for leaves and internal vertices in the
Leaf&Internal-Constrained Diverse Spanning Trees
(
Leaf&Internal-CDST
) problem.

Leaf&Internal-CDST

Input:
A connected graph
G=(V,E)
, and integers
p\geq 0
,
q\geq 0
,
k\geq 1
, and
\ell\geq 1
.

Question:
Decide whether
G
has
\ell
pairwise
k
-diverse spanning trees
T_{1},\ldots,T_{\ell}
such that every tree has at least
p
leaves and
q
internal vertices.

Similarly,
Max-Leaf Spanning Tree
and
Non-Terminal Spanning Tree
are generalized in
Leaf&Non-terminal-Constrained Diverse Spanning Trees
(
Leaf&Non-Term-CDST
).

Leaf&Non-Term-CDST

Input:
A connected graph
G
, a subset of non-terminal vertices
V_{\rm NT}\subseteq V(G)
, and integers
p\geq 0
,
k\geq 1
, and
\ell\geq 1
.

Question:
Decide whether
G
has
\ell
pairwise
k
-diverse spanning trees
T_{1},\ldots,T_{\ell}
such that the vertices of
V_{\rm NT}
are internal in every tree and each tree has at least
p
leaves.

Our results.

As
Max-Leaf Spanning Tree
is the dual of the
Connected Dominating Set
problem, it is NP-complete
[
28
]
. Also,
Max-Internal Spanning Tree
and
Non-Terminal Spanning Tree
generalize
Hamiltonian Path
and
s
-
t
-
Hamiltonian Path
, respectively, which are well-known to be NP-complete
[
28
]
. On the positive side, these problems are well-studied from the perspective of parameterized algorithms.
In particular,
Max-Leaf Spanning Tree

[
6
,
21
,
34
]
and
Max-Internal Spanning Tree

[
23
,
37
,
42
,
43
]
are FPT when parameterized by
p
and
q
, respectively, and, furthermore, admit polynomial kernels for these parameterizations. The same holds for
Non-Terminal Spanning Tree

[
31
]
parameterized by the size of the given set of non-terminal vertices. Our first result demonstrates that the kernelization carries over to the common diverse variant.

Theorem 1
.

Leaf&Internal-CDST
parameterized by
p+q+k+\ell
admits a kernel with
\mathcal{O}((p+q+k\ell)\ell)
vertices.

Similarly,
Non-Terminal Spanning Tree
admits a polynomial kernel for the parameterization by the size of the set of non-terminal vertices
[
31
]
, and we get the extension stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 2
.

Leaf&Non-Term-CDST
parameterized by
|V_{\rm NT}|+p+k+\ell
admits a kernel with
\mathcal{O}((|V_{\rm NT}|+p+k\ell)\ell)
vertices.

The key structural observation behind the proofs of
Theorem

1
and
Theorem

2
is the fact that if a connected graph
G
of minimum degree at least two has a spanning tree with at least
2\ell\big\lceil\frac{k}{4}\big\rceil
leaves, then
G
has
\ell
pairwise
k
-diverse spanning trees. Using this, we construct a polynomial-time algorithm that, given an instance of
Leaf&Internal-CDST
, either reports that this is a yes-instance, or returns an equivalent instance of bounded size, or outputs an equivalent instance of
Max-Internal Spanning Tree
. Thus, we show that, from the kernelization viewpoint,
Leaf&Internal-CDST
is as hard as
Max-Internal Spanning Tree
. Similarly, for
Leaf&Non-Term-CDST
, we obtain that kernelization for finding diverse spanning trees is essentially as hard as for finding one tree with a given set of non-terminals.

Related work.

The study of diverse spanning trees was initiated by Hanaka et al.
[
33
]
. However, they considered the different diversity measure – the sum of all pairwise Hamming distances. They proved that
\ell
spanning trees maximizing this measure can be found in polynomial time. Moreover, this result can be extended to the problem of finding diverse matroid bases; we remind that the edge sets of spanning trees are bases of graphic matroids. The result is obtained by reducing finding diverse bases to the problem of finding disjoint bases, which could be solved in polynomial time by the classical results of Nash-Williams
[
41
]
and Edmonds
[
20
]
. We note that this approach fails for our case when we maximize the minimum Hamming distance between trees.
Diverse matroid bases when the diversity measure is the minimum Hamming distance were investigated by Fomin et al. in
[
25
]
. They proved that the problem of finding
\ell
pairwise
k
-diverse bases admits a polynomial kernel for the parameterization by
\ell
and
k
. Deciding whether a given graph has
\ell
pairwise
k
-diverse spanning trees is a special case of this problem.
In fact, Fomin et al. proved their result for the more general weighted problem where the diversity is defined as the weight of the symmetric difference of two bases.

The first FPT algorithm for
Max-Internal Spanning Tree
parameterized by the number of leaves
p
was given by Downey and Fellows
[
17
]
, and a faster algorithm was provided by Bonsma et al.
[
6
]
. The first polynomial kernel was given by Estivill-Castro et al.
[
21
]
. The kernel with at most
5.5p
vertices was established by Jansen
[
34
]
. Approximation algorithms for the problem were given in

[
39
,
44
]
. As we are interested mainly in the parameterized complexity for the parameterization by the number of leaves, we do not discuss here the results for the
Connected Dominating Set
problem, where the task is to minimize the number of internal vertices of a spanning tree.

The Parameterized Complexity study of
Max-Internal Spanning Tree
was initiated by Prieto-Rodriguez and Sloper in
[
42
,
43
]
who gave a first FPT algorithm for the parameterization by the number of internal vertices
q
and proved that the problem admits a polynomial kernel.
The currently best FPT algorithm was given by Björklund et al.
[
4
]
.
Fomin et al.
[
23
]
demonstrated that
Max-Internal Spanning Tree
admits a kernel with at most
3q
vertices, and Li et al.
[
37
]
got the improved kernel with at most
2q
vertices.

Non-Terminal Spanning Tree
was considered by Hanaka and Kobayashi
[
31
]
. In particular, they proved that the problem has a kernel with at most
3k
vertices and gave a single-exponential in
k
algorithm when the problem is parameterized by the size
k
of the set of non-terminals.
Approximability of
Max-Internal Spanning Tree
was investigated in
[
8
,
35
,
37
,
38
]
.

Finally, we briefly survey some related results about diversity. The systematic study of the diversity problems in the Parameterized Complexity framework was initiated by Baste et al.
[
2
]
.
For the diverse variants of the classical subset optimization problems, we mention that problems of finding diverse matchings were considered in
[
24
,

]
, diverse paths in
[

,

]
, diverse
s

-cuts in
[

]
, diverse hitting sets in
[

]
, and diverse satisfying assignments for

in
[

]
.
Solving the diversity problems under structural parameterizations was discussed in

. Approximation algorithms for finding diverse solutions were considered in

. We refer to
[

for diversity for the (mixed) integer programming problems.

Preliminaries

Sets, Numbers, and Graph Theory:

We use
{\mathbb{N}}
to denote the set of all natural numbers.
Given an integer
r\geq 1
, the set
[r]
denotes the set
\{1,\ldots,r\}
.
Given two sets
A
and
B
, the
symmetric difference
between
A
and
B
is denoted by
A\Delta B
which equals to the set
(A\setminus B)\cup(B\setminus A)
.
We use standard graph theoretic terminologies from Diestel’s book
[
16
]
.
For a graph
G=(V,E)
, let
V(G)=V
denote the set of vertices and
E(G)=E
denote the set of edges of
G
.
If the graph is clear from the context, we use
n
to denote the number of vertices, i.e.
|V(G)|
, and
m
to denote the number of edges, i.e.
|E(G)|
.
For a set
X\subseteq V(G)
, we use
G[X]
to denote the subgraph induced by the vertex subset
X
.
Given a vertex
v\in V(G)
, let
{{\rm deg}}_{G}(v)
denote the degree of
v
in
G
, i.e. the number of neighbors of
v
in
G
.
A
pendant vertex
is a vertex of degree exactly one.
A
pendant edge
is the unique edge that is incident to a pendant vertex.
A
tree
is a connected graph without cycles.
Given a graph
G
, a
spanning tree

T
of
G
is a tree subgraph of
G
whose set of vertices is
V(G)
.
For a tree
T
, pendant vertices are
leaves
and the other vertices are
internal
.
A
path

P
in a graph is a sequence of distinct vertices
(v_{0},\ldots,v_{\ell})
such that for every
i\in[\ell]
,
v_{i-1}v_{i}
is an edge;
\ell
is the
length
of
P
.
The vertices
v_{0}
and
v_{\ell}
are the
end-vertices
, and the other vertices are
internal
.
A
degree-2-path

P=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{\ell})
is a sequence of distinct vertices with only possible exception
v_{0}=v_{\ell}
such that
v_{1},\ldots,v_{\ell-1}
are vertices of degree two in
G
; in the same way as for standard paths,
v_{0}
and
v_{\ell}
are end-vertices and the other vertices are internal, and
\ell
is the length.

Parameterized Complexity and Kernelization:

A
parameterized problem

L
is a set of instances
(x,k)
where
x\in\Sigma^{*}
over a finite alphabet
\Sigma
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
is the parameter.
A parameterized problem
L
is said to be
fixed-parameter tractable
if given
(x,k)\in\Sigma^{*}\times{\mathbb{N}}
, there exists an algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
that runs in
f(k)|x|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
-time, where
f
is a computable function, and correctly decides whether
(x,k)\in L
or not.
We call this algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
a
fixed-parameter algorithm
(or
FPT algorithm
in short).
Note that we allow combinatorial explosion in the parameter
k
while the algorithm runs in time polynomial in
|x|
.
We say that two instances
(x,k)
and
(x^{\prime},k^{\prime})
of a parameterized problem
L
are
equivalent
when
(x,k)\in L
if and only if
(x^{\prime},k^{\prime})\in L
.
A
kernelization
for a parameterized problem
L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}\times{\mathbb{N}}
is an algorithm that runs in time polynomial in
|x|+k
and outputs
(x^{\prime},k^{\prime})
such that
|x^{\prime}|+k^{\prime}\leq g(k)
for some computable function
g:{\mathbb{N}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{N}}
.
If
g(k)
is
k^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
, then
L
is said to admit a
polynomial kernel
.
A kernelization algorithm usually consists of a collection of
reduction rules
(or preprocessing rules) that have to be applied exhaustively.
A reduction rule is
safe
if given
(x,k)
of
L
, one application of the reduction rule outputs an equivalent instance
(x^{\prime},k^{\prime})
of
L
.
We refer to
[
10
,
18
,
27
]
for the formal introduction to the parameterized complexity theory and kernelization.

•

5
Conclusions and Future Research

Our results in this paper provide a comprehensive framework on polynomial kernelizations for the variants of the
Spanning Tree
problem with constraints on the number of leaves and internal vertices in the diversity paradigm.
It would be interesting to consider other variants of the
Spanning Tree
problem.
First, we would like to point out that, while disjoint spanning trees
[
41
,
20
]
or spanning trees with the minimum sum of all pairwise Hamming distances
[
33
]
can be found in polynomial time, the computational complexity of finding pairwise
k
-diverse spanning trees is open. If
k=2
, then pairwise
k
-diverse spanning trees are distinct trees. Because distinct spanning trees of a given graph can be counted by Kirchhoff’s matrix tree theorem (see, e.g.,
[
5
]
), it can be decided in polynomial time whether a graph has
\ell
pairwise
2
-diverse spanning trees. However, for
k>2
, it is unknown whether finding three pairwise
k
-diverse spanning trees is polynomial or NP-hard. The other set of open problems stems from directed graphs. What is the complexity of finding diverse spanning trees (i.e.,
outbranchings
) in directed graphs? Furthermore, it is interesting to investigate the directed analogs of
Leaf&Internal-Constrained Diverse Spanning Trees
and
Leaf&Non-terminal-Constrained Diverse Spanning Trees
.
We remark that the Parameterized Complexity of the
Directed Max-Leaf Spanning Tree
and
Directed Max-Internal Spanning Tree
problems were considered in
[
1
,
7
,
11
,
12
,
36
]
and
[
9
,
13
,
26
,
29
]
, respectively.
