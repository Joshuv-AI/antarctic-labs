Incidence and etiology of impediments to spinal cord stimulator trial progression
Authors: Erin E Alpaugh, Esha A Vaidya, Jason S Kilgore, David A Provenzano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSpinal cord stimulator (SCS) trials are used to assess therapy efficacy before permanent implantation. Despite initial candidacy, not all patients proceed to SCS trial. Comprehending potential barriers to SCS therapy could offer strategies to enhance patient access.ObjectiveThe purpose of this study is to evaluate the incidence and etiology of impediments to SCS trial progression unrelated to preoperative MRI findings.MethodsA retrospective chart review of a single center identified patients who were under consideration for SCS trial from September 2013 to July 2023. Impediments to trial progression were classified from electronic health records. Demographic variables and time intervals were analyzed using logistic regression and a non-parametric test.ResultsThis retrospective study identified 358 patients considered for an SCS trial of which 84 (23.5%) did not progress. Relevant impediment classifications included: medical reasons (39.8%), patient-directed decisions (25.8%), insurance or financial responsibilities (16.1%), MRI-documented contraindications (10.8%), and psychiatric concerns (7.5%). In the multivariable logistic regression, complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS; b = −0.82, p = 0.04) was the only demographic variable significantly associated with non-progression to trial.ConclusionAcross all SCS trial candidates, 20.7% did not progress for reasons unrelated to MRI findings. SCS trial progression was hindered by medical, personal, and financial challenges during the preoperative period. These findings highlight a need for enhancing patient access and optimizing preprocedural processes.Keywords: Chronic pain, Spinal cord stimulation, Spinal cord stimulator trial

Introduction

Spinal cord stimulation (SCS) is a widely used treatment for chronic intractable neuropathic pain that has not responded to conservative management [1]. Before permanent implantation, a temporary trial is required to determine eligibility and reduce risks, as recommended by agencies like the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS), commercial insurers, and professional society guidelines [2,3]. During the trial, patients evaluate pain relief, functional improvement, and response to stimulation while testing programming options.

Before undergoing an SCS trial, patients complete thorough clinical screening including preoperative imaging, laboratory workup, and medical clearance to identify factors that may affect outcomes and ultimate progression to trial. Variables such as elevated body mass index (BMI), smoking, and opioid use have been associated with reduced SCS implant success [2].

In addition to imaging and medical evaluation, formal psychological assessments are used to analyze psychosocial risk factors, evaluate patient experience of pain, and predict therapy outcomes prior to trial. These evaluations, conducted by licensed mental health professionals, are required by Medicare [3].

Although previous research has examined factors involved in trial-to-implant conversions, limited insight exists in defining obstacles to the progression of SCS trial [4]. This study investigates the incidence and etiology of impediments to SCS trials once the preoperative process has been initiated. MRI-related impediments identified in this cohort have been described previously [5], and therefore, are not discussed in detail here.

Methods

After approval by the Robert Morris University Institutional Review Board (application #202412163521), all patients from a single center evaluated for SCS trial from September 2013 to July 2023 were retrospectively reviewed. Inclusion required starting pre-operative workup after initial evaluation and referral, defined as undergoing pre-operative SCS magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed tomography (CT) scan when MRI was contraindicated. Patients were identified using Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes 63650 and 63685 for SCS procedures. Additionally, CPT codes for cervical (72141 and 72142) and thoracic (72146 and 72147) MRIs were used to identify patients who may have been considered for a trial but did not progress. Following this identification, chart review was conducted to determine whether the patient was under consideration for an SCS trial. All patients undergoing consideration for an SCS trial in this study underwent pre-operative MRI or CT scan. Imaging was obtained according to anticipated lead placement, with thoracic imaging obtained for all patients and additional cervical or lumbar imaging obtained for cervical or thoracic/lumbar cases, respectively.

Data were collected on the type of SCS trial considered (cervical or thoracic/lumbar), indication for SCS trial, timing intervals from preoperative evaluation to trial, baseline pain severity, and demographic characteristics. Demographics variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, diabetes status, history of psychiatric condition, diagnosis of complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS), and insurance type. Age was stratified into groups: <40 years (age group 1), 40-49 years (age group 2), 50-59 years (age group 3), and ≥60 years (age group 4). Initial psychiatric history was self-reported by the patient at intake. All patients under consideration for a trial were also directed to complete a formal psychological assessment. This evaluation was performed by mental health providers within the affiliated health system and in external practices, according to their standard clinical practice. Insurance coverage data was aggregated into the following categories: Medicare (insurance group 1), Medicare Advantage (insurance group 2), Medicaid (insurance group 3), commercial (insurance group 4), and workers’ compensation (insurance group 5).

For patients who did not proceed to spinal cord stimulator trial, impediments were identified and categorized through review of the electronic health record. Impediments to trial were classified into the following: medical reason (i.e., medical condition or medically unoptimized), patient-directed decision, insurance or financial responsibility (i.e., issues with coverage/denial or patient financial responsibility), MRI-documented contraindications, and psychiatric concerns. Medically unoptimized impediments were defined as the presence of modifiable medical issues such as high blood glucose, tobacco use, or elevated BMI. For impediments related to patient financial responsibility, patients were unable to afford the associated patient financial responsibility for the procedure (i.e., co-pays and/or deductibles). The psychiatric concern classification included but was not limited to patients who did not receive clearance from a licensed mental health professional during the pre-operative psychological assessment.

Time intervals were assessed at (1) start of pre-operative testing to final pre-operative appointment, (2) final pre-operative appointment to trial date, and (3) start of pre-operative testing to trial date. Self-reported baseline pain severity was collected at initiation of preoperative testing using the 11-point numeric rating scale (NRS). Averages for time points and pain scores were calculated using Python (version 3.8.8, Python Software Foundation).

Statistical analysis

Logistic regression was used to identify demographics associated with progression to trial. Simple logistic regression identified variables showing an association at a relaxed threshold (⍺ < 0.25) for inclusion in a multivariable logistic regression model. Variables reaching statistical significance (⍺ < 0.05) were retained in the final model.

Wilcoxon Signed Rank (WSR) test examined distribution differences between the patients who progressed to trial and the patients who did not progress for a patient-directed decision at each of the three time points, with p-values adjusted for multiple comparisons by Benjamini-Hochberg approach. All statistical analyses were performed with R version 4.0.3 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria).

At least one psychiatric condition was self-reported by 110 (30.7%) patients. Reported psychiatric conditions included anxiety and depression. For the entire cohort, 6.1% of the patients reported actively seeing a psychiatrist, and 2.8% reported previous suicidal thoughts or attempts. Out of 322 (89.9% of the entire cohort) that completed pre-operative psychological assessments, only 2 (0.62%) patients did not receive psychological clearance from the formal assessment.

Self-reported baseline pain severity was similar between groups. The mean baseline NRS for patients who progressed was 6.6 (95% CI 6.3, 6.9) and 6.5 (95% CI 5.9, 7.1) for patients who did not progress for reasons unrelated to MRI findings.

All medical optimization impediments (n = 12) were related to modifiable risk factors including elevated glucose, tobacco use, and elevated BMI. The medical conditions that impeded progression to an SCS trial were all identified during the preoperative workup. Patients who did not progress related to concerns for elevated glucose had an average blood glucose level of 220.3 ± 62.3 mg/dL (mean ± SD; range 153-319 mg/dL). Among the four patients with cardiac concerns, three were unable to cease anticoagulant treatment prior to their SCS trial.

Patient-directed decisions accounted for 25.8% of all impediments to SCS trial. Within this classification, two decisions were found to be associated with occupational concerns, and one decision was associated with transportation difficulty. For other patient-directed decisions, the reasons were unspecified or could not be determined from available records. Furthermore, two patients in this cohort were determined to be unreachable or lost to follow-up. Additionally, 42% of patients in this impediment category completed all pre-operative testing but did not proceed to SCS trial.

In the insurance or financial responsibility impediment category, 87% of cases resulted from either insufficient insurance coverage or denial of coverage. One patient in this cohort had an off-label indication, while all remaining patients had on-label indications.

Time points

The average wait time from preoperative evaluation initiation to SCS trial date for patients who progressed was 82.7 days (n = 274, 95% CI 73.6, 91.9) as compared to 60.2 days (n = 6, 95% CI 36.5, 83.8) for patients with a trial date but no progression due to patient-directed decisions. However, this difference in wait time was not significant (adjusted p-value >0.22), likely due to the small number of patients with trial dates but no progression.

Discussion

To the authors’ knowledge, this study represents the first comprehensive analysis of factors hindering progression to spinal cord stimulator (SCS) trials. Among 358 patients evaluated for SCS in this study, 24% did not advance to trial, with 88% of these patients discontinuing for reasons unrelated to preoperative MRI results. Impediments to SCS trial included medical reasons, patient-directed decisions, insurance or financial responsibilities, MRI-documented contraindications, and psychiatric concerns, in descending order of frequency. Few patient characteristics demonstrated a strong association with progression to SCS trial, whereas CRPS was inversely associated with trial progression. Referral sources included a range of specialties, with both progression and non-progression to trial occurring across major referral pathways.

Approximately 40% of impediments were related to medical reasons, and therefore, was the most frequently observed etiology. This impediment classification represented concerns identified during the preoperative process, including medical conditions and the need for medical optimization. Specifically, 12.9% of impediments were attributed to modifiable medical issues, such as elevated glucose, tobacco use, and higher BMI. Furthermore, the Neurostimulation Appropriateness Consensus Committee (NACC) guidelines have recommended preoperative workup prior to neuromodulation to identify risk factors, such as smoking and poorly controlled diabetes, and to facilitate discussions on risk reduction [6]. These findings are significant given that earlier studies have shown that tobacco smoking and higher BMI are associated with revision rates and poorer response to SCS therapy [[7], [8], [9]]. Specifically, Mekhail et al. demonstrated that smoking influences SCS implant outcomes at one-year follow-up, with current smokers reporting less pain improvement and higher opioid use as compared to nonsmokers or former smokers [8]. The same group demonstrated a negative association between SCS effectiveness and increasing BMI at six- and twelve-months post implant with a 2% reduction efficacy for every unit increase of BMI [9]. Obesity continues to remain a challenge with all medical specialties with forecasts estimating that, by 2050, two in three adults (≥25 years) will have obesity in the United States, with the total number with overweight and obesity exceeding 200 million [10]. In the population presented here, over 48% had a BMI ≥30 kg/m2 and over 20% having a diagnosis of diabetes. Consistent with NACC and evidence-consensus guidelines, our findings support necessity for medical workup and counseling as part of comprehensive preoperative assessment [2,6]. We encourage centers to work to identify these factors during the preoperative workflow and mitigate risk with perioperative management.

Other notable findings include limited access secondary to financial burdens and insurance denials. More specifically, 16.1% of impediments were related to issues with insurance coverage or denial and patient financial responsibility. Recent literature has shown that patient cost sharing has substantially increased, while repayment rates have declined, contributing to medical debt [11]. Higher cost-sharing has also been shown to be associated with worse medication adherence rates [12]. Out-of-pocket expenses for elective surgeries have also risen. In a study examining commercially insured patients from 2014 to 2019, total out-of-pocket costs for elective spinal surgery significantly increased significantly during this time period, with a compound annual growth rate of 4.2-4.5% [13]. In light of these findings, concerted efforts should be made to alleviate financial burden for patients and to continue to define advocacy efforts to maintain access for appropriately selected patients.

Although almost a third of the SCS candidates self-reported psychiatric conditions, less than 1% were excluded following formal psychological evaluation. This observation raises questions about the effectiveness of current assessments and suggests that alternative strategies may be needed to manage psychiatric factors such as depression, autonomous coping, and kinesiophobia, which have been associated with less favorable SCS outcomes [14,15].

Medical and administrative preparation times for SCS trials averaged over 80 days. These findings align with trends reported in the existing literature. In a study evaluating wait times from decision to SCS implantation, an average wait time of approximately 3 months was reported [16]. Our results demonstrate little improvement reported a decade ago and thus the continued need for process improvement. We found no significant difference in wait times between patients who progressed to trial and the patients who did not progress for a patient-directed decision, thus wait times were not likely a determining factor in patients who did not proceed to trial due to patient-directed decisions. Notably, 42% of patients in this category completed all preoperative workup but did not progress. Future research is warranted to explore factors related to patient-directed decisions not to proceed to SCS trial. This could help identify modifiable patient decision factors related to education and assist in recognizing individuals early who do not wish to continue, thereby reducing use of medical resources and lowering overall healthcare costs.

Majority patient characteristics including demographic variables were not found to be associated with SCS trial progression. Although CRPS (upper and lower extremity CRPS), age group 3 (50-59 years), age group 4 (≥60 years), and insurance group were significantly associated with SCS trial progression in the simple logistic regression model, only CRPS was significantly associated with SCS trial progression in the multiple regression model. Specifically, CRPS was negatively associated with trial progression, with affected patients having 56% lower odds of progressing to trial as compared to patients without CRPS. In a study examining trial-to-implant conversion rates, factors independently associated with lower odds of successful trial-to-implant conversion included older age, male sex, and Medicare and Medicaid insurance coverage compared to commercial coverage [4]. Further research is needed to explore reasons why individuals with CRPS may have lower odds of advancing to trial.

Limitations and future directions

There are several limitations to consider when interpreting these results. First, this was a retrospective study conducted at a single center, which may restrict how well the findings apply to other settings with different referral patterns, insurance mixes, or preoperative workflows. Additionally, white patients comprised the majority of the study cohort, which may limit the generalizability of these findings to more diverse populations. Reasons why patients did not proceed to trial were taken from medical record documentation. The “patient-directed” category likely includes a range of motivations that were not captured through standardized patient-reported measures in the current data.

In addition, although the wait times presented here seem long from initiation to trial, patients for this therapy must undergo an extensive preoperative process that also often includes an insurance preauthorization or predetermination step for the procedure and requested MRIs. Our results coincide with Kumar et al. in demonstrating that wait times are often long and encompass months [16]. More specifically, published wait times demonstrate that once the decision was made to implant, the implanting surgeon required an average of 3.31 ± 0.09 months to organize the procedure [16].

There is limited research on obstacles preventing progression to SCS trials after preoperative testing begins. Although this analysis is from one center, its large sample size and thorough examination of demographic and clinical factors offer valuable insights for guiding future studies aimed at understanding and overcoming these barriers. The study also underscores the importance of medical optimization, especially in contexts where psychological issues and comorbidities may negatively affect SCS outcomes.

Further research using prospective, multicenter approaches that include structured patient-reported outcomes and decision-making assessments is needed to confirm these findings and identify modifiable factors to improve access and progression to SCS trials. The study presented here provides a framework to further understand why greater than 20% of patients who initiate testing do not progress to a trial.

Conclusions

This report highlights key barriers to SCS trials and the importance of improved patient access and streamlined medical optimization processes. Collaboration among insurers, government manufacturers, and providers is essential to address these persistent challenges.
