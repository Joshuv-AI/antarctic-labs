Syringomyelia is a chronic progressive spinal affection,
associated with the presence of long spaces and clefts near the
centre of the spinal cord, and an accompanying proliferation of
neuroglia. Clinically, it shows a great variety of symptoms of
motor, sensory, reflex, trophic, and secretory nature, together
in some cases with bulbar symptoms, depending upon the
particular distribution of the cavities and newly formed
glia.^
The occurrence of cavities in the cord was remarked as
early as the year 1564 (Etienne), and later cases were recorded
by Morgagni, Portal and others. The name syringomyelia was
first given to the condition by Ollivier of Angers, in 1824, and
since that time a great number of cases of the disease have
been recorded, although much dispute has taken place with
regard to the pathogenesis of the condition. The most
important monograph on the subject is that of Schlesinger,
published in 1902, which gives a complete bibliography with
1175 references to publications on the subject in medical
literature up to that date. The original idea in regard to its
causation was that of Ollivier, who regarded the cavities as
dilatations upon a persisting central canal. Stilling and
Waldeyer, in 1859, offered the hypothesis that the cavities
arose from an abnormally wide central canal and proposed
the name of hydromyelia for the condition. Duchenne, i11
1847, had been the first to recognise some of the clinical
features which might accompany this abnormality of the cord,
particularly the anaesthesia to pain and burning. Lockhart
Clarke, in 1865, attributed the cavity formation to a molecular
disintegration in the spinal cord, and, in 1869, Hallopeau
advanced the opinion that this degeneration was brought about
by a process of chronic myelitis, while Simon, in 1895, and
Schultze attributed the cavity formation to degeneration
occurring in the substance of a tumour-like new formation.
Leyden (1874) had again drawn attention to the importance

Types of Syringomyelia.
The symptoms of any given case depend largely upon the
Jevel in the central nervous system at which the gliosis and
cavity formation are chiefly developed, and also upon the
direction in which the abnormal formation takes place, in one
Case to involve sensory fibres, in another, motor fibres, etc.
Schlesinger recognises six types corresponding to the symptoms
shown.

(1) Syringomyelia with the classical symptoms, showing a
mixture of motor disturbances with sensory changes, such as
disassociated analgesia. The symptoms vary somewhat accord-
ing as the cervical, dorsolumbar, sacrolumbar, or bulbar region
is chiefly affected.
(2) Syringomyelia with specially marked motor symptoms.?-
This type presents appearances suggestive of, and often mistaken
for, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, or occasionally, symptoms of
spastic paralysis, or shows humeroscapular paralysis with
marked kyphosis.
(3) Syringomyelia with specially marked sensory symptoms.
?In this case, the condition resembles, and is often mistaken
for, hysteria. Sometimes, the patient shows loss of sensation
on one side of the body and face, sometimes zones of anaesthesia-
The loss of touch sense and of temperature and pain sense are
usually not congruent.
(4) Syringomyelia with specially marked trophic disturbances.
?These may consist of painless whitlows in the fingers, the
condition originally described by Morvan in 1883, and
afterwards recognised as part of the symptom-complex of
syringomyelia. Osteoarthritic changes, especially in the joints
of the upper limbs, are also associated with this type.
(5) Tabetic type.?In these cases, there are symptoms
usually associated with locomotor ataxia, such as an Argyll-
Robertson pupil, transitory diplopia, ataxic gait. Some of the
symptoms may be produced by extreme destruction of the
posterior columns in the cord, but in many of the cases recorded
there is meningitis and sclerosis of the posterior columns
proceeding side by side with the syringomyelia, and caused
by syphilis. Presumably, the gliosis and cavity formation i11
these cases has been, if not caused, at least greatly aggravated
by the syphilitic process.
(6) Pachymeningitic type.?In cases of this type, sensory
and motor irritative symptoms are often present at the beginning
of the disease, especially in the lower extremities, and a
paraplegia of both lower extremities often develops somewhat
suddenly in the later stages. Philippe and Oberthtir have
specially given an account of this type of syringomyelia.
In the case now to be described, the last two types are
combined.

Clinical Summary. ? In the case quoted, the patient,
aged 51, had led an active life until seven months before
death, although she had had attacks of pains regarded as

a strong positive Wassermann reaction. The first definite

associated analgesia only began to develop about one month

hysterical type. After death, the lumbar region of the cord

V. Conclusion.?(a) Summary of the Histological Changes.?
The present case relates to the association of a syringomyelia
with tabes and the further peculiarity of a cavitation in certain
lower dorsal nerve roots.
The cavity extended throughout the cervico-dorsal cord : at
its greatest sectional area in C7 it was bilateral and symmetrical
and, except for the actual replacement of tissue by a cavity and
its glial wall, the general architecture of the cord was little
altered. Above and below this level it varied greatly in shape
and extent: above it progressively diminished till it was
represented in Ci by a zone of post-ependymal gliosis: below
it became accentuated in and later confined to the left posterior
horn and ended as a narrow cleft, in the axis of the posterior
horn, which reached to the periphery of the cord.
The cavity in the left posterior roots of the seventh to
tenth dorsal nerves could nowhere be traced in direct continuity
with the cleft in the posterior horn.
The degeneration in the posterior column was almost typical
of that found in tabes. The blood-vessel changes, the moderate
but diffuse degree of leptomeningitis, and the limited pachy-
meningitis, in the absence of toxi-infective phenomena reach-
ing the central nervous system from peripheral infective foci,
may be associated with the tabes.

The association of syringomyelia and tabes has often been
regarded as a coincidence, in view of the fact that the simultaneous

meninges and of the blood-vessels might well account for the
cavity in both cord and roots and also for the tabetic degenera-

being at different levels.

spinal canal and the formation of a diverticulum lined by

Summary.?While several factors are undoubtedly concerned
in the development of a cavity it has seemed to the writers that
the essential underlying nature of the lesion in the present
instance may be referred to the parallel development of the
syphilitic disease of the meninges and of the blood-vessels, and
that these might well account for the formation of the cavity in
the cord, and that in the posterior roots, and also for the tabetic
degeneration.
