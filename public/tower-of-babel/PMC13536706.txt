ATAD2 suppresses senescence and SASP via SIRT7-p53/p21 to drive progression and immune evasion in endometrial cancer
Authors: Can Wang, Anastasiia Osipova, Yuanyuan Wang, Fei Li, Hengyu Wang, Jingshu Hu, Xiuwei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Endometrial carcinoma (EC) is currently the most lethal gynecological malignancy, with its incidence continuing to rise [1]. For unresectable or advanced EC, systemic therapies primarily involving radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and immune checkpoint inhibitors remain the mainstay of treatment. However, their efficacy remains limited, and patient prognosis remains poor [2, 3]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to explore novel therapeutic strategies based on new mechanisms.

To overcome the limitations of current therapies, novel mechanism-based treatment strategies have emerged as a research focus. Among these, inducing cellular senescence in tumor cells has garnered significant attention as an emerging anti-cancer approach, such as therapeutic senescence induction [4, 5]. The core feature of cellular senescence is proliferation arrest, which effectively suppresses tumor growth [6, 7]. However, the Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype (SASP) released by senescent cells exerts complex and often dual effects on the tumor microenvironment, potentially offsetting therapeutic benefits or even promoting tumor progression [2]. In EC, the precise role of cellular senescence and its regulatory network remain poorly understood.

Cell senescence undergoes precise epigenetic regulation. ATAD2 (ATPase Family AAA Domain Containing 2), an epigenetic reader and histone chaperone belonging to the AAA + ATPase family, acts as an oncogene in multiple cancers, with its overexpression associated with poor prognosis [8, 9]. Although studies suggest ATAD2 involvement in cell cycle regulation [10, 11], its role in regulating cellular senescence processes within EC and how it influences the resulting tumor microenvironment remains unknown.

In this study, we confirmed that ATAD2 is highly expressed in EC tissues, and its expression levels correlate with adverse clinical and pathological characteristics in patients. Functional experiments demonstrated that ATAD2 significantly promotes malignant phenotypes such as proliferation and invasion in EC cells. Mechanistically, ATAD2 regulates senescence in EC cells by modulating the SIRT7/p53/p21 signaling pathway. Inhibition of ATAD2 effectively induces cellular senescence and triggers the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). Importantly, this SASP exhibits beneficial immunomodulatory functions, promoting the polarization of tumor-associated macrophages toward the M1 anti-tumor phenotype. Ultimately, this leads to significant suppression of tumor progression in both in vitro and in vivo models. This study reveals a novel mechanism by which ATAD2 drives EC progression through senescence evasion and suggests that targeting ATAD2 may represent a novel therapeutic strategy capable of both inducing tumor cell senescence and shaping an anti-tumor microenvironment.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Human endometrial epithelial cells (HEEC), endometrial cancer cell lines (HEC-1A, HEC-1B, Ishikawa, RL95-2), and the Human Monocytic Leukemia Cell Line (THP-1) were obtained from the Chinese Academy of Sciences Cell Bank. All cell lines underwent STR identification and tested negative for mycoplasma contamination. Cells were cultured as follows: HEC-1A in McCoy's 5A medium, HEC-1B and Ishikawa in MEM medium, RL95-2 in DMEM/F-12 medium, and HEEC and THP-1 in DMEM medium. All base media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂, and the medium was replaced every 24 h.

Bioinformatics analysis

The UCEC CESC and OV datasets were downloaded from the TCGA database. The relevant data of normal OV patients were derived from the GTEx dataset.ATAD2 gene expression data were extracted, and samples were filtered based on origin. Stromal scores were calculated using the ESTIMATE R package. Immune cell infiltration scores (B cells, CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T cells, neutrophils, macrophages, dendritic cells) were assessed using the TIMER method via the IOBR R package.

Cell transfection and infection

Lentiviruses for ATAD2 and SIRT7 overexpression, knockdown, and corresponding controls were packaged by GENECHEM (Shanghai). Ishikawa cells were used for overexpression experiments; HEC-1A cells were used for knockdown experiments. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 1 × 105 cells/well and infected at approximately 50% confluency. After 12 h, the virus-containing medium was replaced with fresh complete medium. Successfully transfected cells were selected using puromycin 48 h post-infection. Empty vector viruses served as negative controls. Transfection efficiency was confirmed by Western blot and qRT-PCR.

Western blot

Cells were washed with cold PBS and lysed in RIPA buffer on ice for 20 min. Lysates were centrifuged, and supernatants were quantified using a Beyotime BCA kit. Proteins (20 μg per lane) were separated by 12.5% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature, incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, washed with PBST, and then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were detected using chemiluminescence.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent and reverse-transcribed into cDNA. qPCR was performed using FastStart Universal SYBR Green Master Mix (ROX) on a StepOne Real-Time PCR System. GAPDH served as the endogenous control. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method. Primer sequences are listed in Table 1.

Cell migration and invasion assays

Scratch Assay: Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 4 × 105 cells/well. Upon reaching full confluency, a scratch was made using a sterile 200 μl pipette tip. After washing with PBS, scratch closure was monitored and imaged at 0, 24, and 48 h under an inverted microscope.

Transwell Assay: Migration: 2 × 104 cells in serum-free medium were placed in the upper chamber; the lower chamber contained medium with 10% FBS. After 24 h, migrated cells were fixed, stained, and counted.

Invasion: Matrigel was diluted 1:8, and 70 μl was applied to the upper chamber and solidified. Then, 4 × 104 cells in serum-free medium were added to the upper chamber, and medium with 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber. After 48 h, invaded cells were fixed, stained, and counted.

Colony formation assay

Cells with modulated ATAD2 expression (shATAD2-HEC-1A, oeATAD2-Ishikawa, and controls) were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured for 14 days, with medium changes every 3 days. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.5% crystal violet, and visualized under a microscope.

Cell proliferation assays

EdU Assay: Cells were seeded in 6-well plates. At 60–70% confluency, EdU was added according to the manufacturer's instructions and incubated for 2 h. Cells were fixed, permeabilized, and processed using the Click-iT reaction cocktail. Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342. The percentage of EdU-positive cells was calculated using ImageJ software.

CCK-8 Assay: Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 3 × 103 cells/well. After 24 h, 10 μl of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for 2 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Co-immunoprecipitation

Cell lysates were prepared using RIPA buffer and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against ATAD2, SIRT7, or control IgG with constant rotation. Protein A/G agarose beads were then added and incubated for another 2 h. Immunoprecipitates were washed extensively with lysis buffer, eluted in loading buffer by heating at 95 °C for 5 min, and analyzed by Western blot.

Senescence-associated β-galactosidase staining

Cellular SA-β-gal Staining: Cells were seeded in 24-well plates. At 70–80% confluency, cells were fixed and stained for SA-β-gal activity following the manufacturer's protocol. Senescent cells were identified by blue staining under a microscope.

SA-β-gal Staining of Cryosections: Mouse tumor tissues were fixed in 4% PFA, embedded in OCT compound, and sectioned using a cryostat. Tissue sections were equilibrated to room temperature, rinsed with PBS, and stained for SA-β-gal activity overnight. Stained sections were examined and photographed under a microscope.

ELISA

The concentrations of human IL-6 and TNF-α in cell supernatants were determined in strict accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. The ELISA kit used in this study was supplied by Bostel (Wuhan, China). Absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm.

In vivo analysis

All animal procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Harbin Medical University. Female BALB/c mice were subcutaneously injected with 1.5 × 10⁷ HEC-1A cells suspended in 100 μl medium. When tumor volume exceeded 80 mm3, the treatment group received intraperitoneal injections of BAY-850 (100 μl, 20 mg/kg) every other day, while the control group received equivalent PBS injections. Mouse body weight and tumor volume were monitored weekly. Tumors were harvested, weighed, and photographed three weeks post-treatment.

Histological analysis

Immunohistochemistry: Excised tumors were fixed, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned. After deparaffinization, antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Sections were blocked with 5% normal goat serum and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Ki67, E-cadherin, and N-cadherin. Following incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody, DAB was used for chromogenic development, and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin.

H&E Staining: Deparaffinized and rehydrated sections were stained with hematoxylin for 8 min, differentiated, counterstained with eosin, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted.

Flow cytometry

Macrophages (THP1 cells) were collected after treatment and washed twice with PBS. Cells were incubated at 4 °C in the dark with a human-labeled fluorescent antibody for 30 min. The M1 polarization marker CD86 and the M2 polarization marker CD206 were then detected (specific antibodies are shown in Table 2). After incubation, cells were washed twice with PBS and resuspended in flow cytometry buffer. Data were then acquired using a flow cytometer (BD FACSAria) and analyzed using FlowJo software to determine the expression ratios of CD86 and CD206 under different treatment conditions and to assess the M1/M2 polarization status of macrophages.

Molecular docking

To assess the binding energies and interaction patterns between proteins, we used GRAMM-X (http://gramm.compbio.ku.edu/) to perform rigid protein–protein docking between ATAD2 and SIRT7, and SIRT7 and p53, to study their relationships. All three protein domains were derived from the PDB protein database (http://www.rcsb.org/). Finally, we used Pymol (version 2.4) and PDBePISA (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/pdbe/pisa/) to investigate protein–protein interactions and further visualize them.

GST pull-down

To verify whether a direct interaction exists between ATAD2 and SIRT7, a GST pull-down assay was employed for analysis. First, SIRT7 was recombinantly expressed as a GST fusion protein (GST-SIRT7), which was subsequently purified following induced expression in *E. coli*. The purified GST-SIRT7 protein was immobilized onto Glutathione Sepharose 4B beads, and unbound proteins were removed by washing with PBS buffer. Subsequently, unlabeled ATAD2 protein was added and incubated at 4 °C for 2 h to facilitate protein–protein interaction. Following incubation, non-specifically bound proteins were removed by washing with PBS, and the eluted samples were loaded onto an SDS-PAGE gel. The presence of ATAD2 was detected via Western blot to confirm whether an interaction occurred between ATAD2 and GST-SIRT7. As a control, an experiment was performed using a GST empty vector protein to ensure the specificity of the results.

Proximity ligation assay

To validate the interaction between ATAD2 and SIRT7 at the cellular level, a proximity ligation assay (PLA) was employed. First, cells were seeded onto glass slides and cultured until they reached an appropriate density. Subsequently, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10–15 min and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 5 min. Next, non-specific binding sites were blocked using 5% BSA for 30 min. Primary antibodies targeting ATAD2 and SIRT7 were then added, and the slides were incubated overnight at 4 °C. The following day, in accordance with the PLA kit instructions, the corresponding secondary antibodies— conjugated with complementary probes—were added, and the slides were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C to allow for the formation of amplifiable ligation structures upon probe proximity. This was followed by a rolling circle amplification (RCA) reaction, which generated fluorescent signals during the amplification process. Finally, a fluorescence microscope was used to visualize and capture images of the intracellular fluorescent foci, thereby assessing the spatial co-localization and interaction between ATAD2 and SIRT7 within the cells. The experiment included negative controls (involving the addition of only a single primary antibody or no primary antibodies) to ensure signal specificity.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with at least three independent replicates. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.5.0 software. Comparisons between two groups used the Student's t-test; comparisons among multiple groups used one-way or two-way ANOVA. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001; ns, not significant).

Results

ATAD2 expression correlates with endometrial cancer progression and poor prognosis

Gynecological malignancies remain a leading threat to women’s health. ATAD2, a documented oncogene, is frequently up-regulated and associated with poor outcome across multiple tumours. We therefore interrogated its expression in cervical squamous-cell carcinoma (CESC), ovarian cancer (OV) and uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC) using TCGA data. Compared with adjacent non-tumour or normal tissues, ATAD2 mRNA was consistently elevated in all three cancer types (Fig. 1A–C). Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed that this up-regulation was prognostically relevant only in UCEC: patients with high ATAD2 expression experienced significantly shorter overall survival (Fig. 1D–F).

Focusing on UCEC, we verified that ATAD2 levels were higher in tumour specimens than in matched normal tissue from the same patients (Fig. 1G). ATAD2 expression also increased with histological grade and FIGO stage. Multivariable modelling identified high ATAD2 as an independent predictor of poor 1-year survival, alongside advanced stage and incomplete treatment response (AUC = 0.62; Fig. 1H–J).The sample size and baseline clinical characteristics of UCEC patients are detailed in Table 3. In addition, we performed univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses, and the results are shown in Figures S4A-B. ATAD2 was independently associated with survival (HR = 1.347, 95% CI 1.057–1.749, p = 0.037).

To extend these findings to the cellular level, we quantified ATAD2 in a normal endometrial cell line and four endometrial cancer lines. Both mRNA and protein were markedly elevated in the cancer lines, with the highest levels in HEC-1A and the lowest in Ishikawa (Fig. 1K, L); these two lines were selected for subsequent mechanistic studies.

ATAD2 expression affects the proliferative capacity of endometrial cancer cells in vitro

To investigate the role of ATAD2 in tumor growth, we selected the HEC-1A cell line with the highest ATAD2 expression in the detected cells and transfected it with a lentiviral vector containing either the control group (shNC) or ATAD2-specific short hairpin RNA (shRNA) to establish an ATAD2 knockdown (shATAD2) cell line. As shown in Fig. 2A, both mRNA and protein levels of ATAD2 were significantly reduced in shATAD2 cells, confirming successful knockdown. Among the shRNA constructs, sh-ATAD2-2 and sh-ATAD2-3 demonstrated the highest knockdown efficiency and were therefore selected for subsequent experiments. In addition, we selected Ishikawa cells with the lowest ATAD2 expression and constructed an ATAD2 overexpressing cell line (ATAD2) by transfecting them with an empty vector or LV-ATAD2 lentivirus. Western blot and qRT-PCR confirmed successful transfection (Fig. 2B).

Our previous studies have demonstrated that ATAD2 promotes tumor growth in vivo [12]. To further examine its effects in vitro, EdU incorporation assays revealed significantly enhanced proliferation in ATAD2-overexpressing Ishikawa cells compared to vector controls (Fig. 2C). Conversely, ATAD2 knockdown produced the opposite effect, with sh-ATAD2-2 and sh-ATAD2-3 transfected HEC-1A cells showing reduced proliferation compared to shNC groups (Fig. 2E). These findings were further corroborated by CCK-8 assays (Fig. 2D, F) and colony formation experiments (Fig. 2G, H), collectively demonstrating that ATAD2 enhances the proliferative capacity of endometrial cancer cells in vitro.

ATAD2 enhances migratory and invasive capacities of endometrial cancer cells in vitro

The migratory and invasive abilities of tumor cells are critical determinants of distant metastasis. As shown in Fig. 3A, we utilized scratch, migration, and invasion assays to assess these properties. We first performed wound healing assays using HEC-1A cells transfected with ATAD2-knockdown viruses. As shown in Fig. 3B, ATAD2-deficient cells (shATAD2-2 and shATAD2-3) exhibited significantly reduced migration distances compared to control (sh-NC) at 48-h time point. Conversely, ATAD2-overexpressing Ishikawa cells demonstrated markedly enhanced migration capacity relative to controls (Fig. 3C). All of the above are statistically significant (Fig. 3D).

We further employed Transwell migration and invasion assays to comprehensively assess ATAD2's role in metastatic potential. Consistent with our expectations, ATAD2 overexpression substantially increased both migratory and invasive capacities of Ishikawa cells, as evidenced by significantly higher numbers of cells penetrating the Transwell chambers (Fig. 3E, G, H). Conversely, ATAD2 knockdown profoundly suppressed these metastatic properties in HEC-1A cells (Fig. 3F, I, J).

ATAD2 mediates endometrial cancer cell senescence through the p53/p21 pathway

To elucidate the mechanism by which ATAD2 promotes malignant progression in endometrial cancer, we performed bioinformatics analysis on 786 genes showing correlation coefficients (R > 0.5 or R < -0.5) with ATAD2 expression, conducting KEGG and GO enrichment analyses. Notably, the cellular senescence pathway emerged as significantly enriched (Fig. 4A, B, Fig. S1A-B). Senescence is a phenomenon where eukaryotic cells undergo permanent arrest of the cell cycle in response to stresses such as DNA damage, telomere shortening, or oxidative stress [13]. This process exerts tumor-suppressing effects by inhibiting abnormal cell proliferation, though the mechanisms linking ATAD2 to this process remain unclear.

To verify the predicted results, we used SA-β-galactosidase staining to evaluate whether ATAD2 expression levels affect cellular senescence in transfected HEC-1A and Ishikawa cells. Results demonstrated that ATAD2 overexpression significantly suppressed senescence compared to controls (Fig. 4C, D), whereas ATAD2 knockdown enhanced this process (Fig. 4E, F).

Cellular senescence is triggered by the activation of the p53/p21WAF1/CIP1 and p16INK4A/pRB tumor suppressor pathways [14]. To identify the specific senescence pathway involved, we first analyzed correlations between ATAD2 and key senescence regulators in endometrial cancer through bioinformatics (Fig. 4G). GEPIA analysis revealed elevated expression of CDKN2A (p16) and TP53 (p53) in endometrial cancer(Fig. S1C-F). Subsequent qRT-PCR analysis in ATAD2-knockdown cells showed significant upregulation of p21 and p53, while p16 and RB1 remained unchanged (Fig. 4H). Western blot analysis confirmed these findings at the protein level: ATAD2 overexpression downregulated p21, p53, and γH2AX expression, whereas ATAD2 knockdown produced the opposite effect (Fig. 4I, J).

These results collectively demonstrate that ATAD2 mediates endometrial cancer cell senescence primarily through the p53/p21 pathway.

ATAD2 mediates p53/p21 regulated cellular senescence and SASP phenotype via SIRT7

The core hallmark of cellular senescence is the activation of the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) [15]. To this end, we employed ELISA to detect the expression of key SASP factors IL-6 and TNF-α in cell supernatants under conditions of altered ATAD2 expression. As shown in Fig. 5A, IL-6 and TNF-α secretion levels were significantly downregulated in supernatants from ATAD2-overexpressing cells. Conversely, their secretion levels were upregulated in supernatants from ATAD2 knockdown cells (Fig. 5B). These findings collectively demonstrate that ATAD2 regulates cellular senescence in endometrial carcinoma. To further investigate the potential mechanisms linking ATAD2 to cellular senescence, we performed a Venn diagram analysis of the intersection between ATAD2 interacting genes, senescence-associated genes from the CellAge database, and senescence-associated genes from the Senescence Atlas database. This analysis identified six overlapping genes (Fig. 5C). Subsequent bioinformatics analysis revealed that among these genes, only SIRT7 exhibited high expression in EC, showed positive correlation with ATAD2 expression, and was associated with poor patient prognosis (Fig. S2A-S2R).The remaining genes do not meet the above requirements. Notably, previous studies have reported that SIRT7 inhibition induces cellular senescence in multiple cancers [16]. Based on these findings, we selected SIRT7 for further investigation.

To establish the regulatory relationship between ATAD2 and SIRT7, we first demonstrated that ATAD2 knockdown significantly downregulated SIRT7 protein levels, whereas ATAD2 overexpression upregulated SIRT7 expression (Fig. 5D, E). Next, co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays in both Ishikawa and HEC-1A cells confirmed a physical interaction between ATAD2 and SIRT7 (Fig. 5F). These findings prompt a novel interaction and regulatory axis involving ATAD2 and SIRT7 in endometrial cancer. To determine whether ATAD2 regulates p53/p21 mediated cellular senescence via SIRT7, we generated endometrial cancer cell lines with stable SIRT7 overexpression or knockdown (Fig. S3A-S3D). Subsequent analysis of senescence pathway proteins revealed that ATAD2 overexpression upregulated SIRT7 and concurrently downregulated senescence markers (Fig. 5G). Although downregulation of SIRT7 expression did not alter ATAD2 expression, it promoted activation of the p53/p21 pathway. Notably, simultaneous overexpression of ATAD2 and knockout of SIRT7 reversed the ATAD2-induced downregulation of p53/p21. SA-β-gal staining and ELISA assays also revealed consistent aging phenotypes (Fig. S3E-S3F).

Conversely, ATAD2 knockout suppressed SIRT7 expression, leading to significant upregulation of p53, p21, and γH2AX (Fig. 5I). SA-β-gal staining revealed consistent senescence phenotypes (Fig. 5J, K). This intervention also enhanced secretion of senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) factors (Fig. 5H, S3G). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that ATAD2 regulates the p53/p21 signaling pathway via SIRT7, thereby suppressing senescence and SASP phenotypes in endometrial carcinoma.

To investigate the specific mechanism by which ATAD2 regulates SIRT7, we first performed molecular docking simulations to explore the potential interaction between ATAD2 and SIRT7. The model reveals that the two proteins can form a stable complex with high binding affinity, suggesting a tight, specific binding interface (Fig. S6A). To validate this interaction in a cellular context, we conducted PLA assays in Ishikawa cells. Positive signals (Fig. S6B) indicate that endogenous ATAD2 and SIRT7 are in close proximity (< 40 nm), providing strong evidence for a physical association in intact cells. Next, to further explore whether this interaction is direct or mediated by other proteins, we performed GST pull-down assays with purified recombinant proteins. As shown in Figure S6C, SIRT7 was specifically precipitated by GST-ATAD2, whereas the GST control did not, confirming that the two proteins can directly interact without the need for other cellular components.

Studies have shown that ATAD2 contains an N-terminal AAA + ATPase domain and a C-terminal bromodomain, which cooperatively regulate gene transcription through chromatin remodeling and acetylated lysine recognition [17]. To identify the domain mediating SIRT7 binding, we constructed a series of truncated mutants (Fig. S6D) and transfected them into Ishikawa cells. GST pull-down assays revealed that the deletion of the bromodomain completely abolished SIRT7 binding, whereas deletion of the ATPase domain had no such effect (Fig. S6E), indicating that the bromodomain of ATAD2 is both necessary and sufficient for SIRT7 binding.

Furthermore, we observed that ATAD2 overexpression upregulated SIRT7 protein levels (Fig. 5E) without altering mRNA levels (Fig. S6F), suggesting post-translational regulation. Cycloheximide chase experiments showed (Fig. S6G-H) that knockdown of ATAD2 significantly accelerated SIRT7 degradation, indicating that ATAD2 stabilizes SIRT7 at the protein level through direct interaction, potentially protecting it from proteasomal degradation. In summary, our data provide multidimensional evidence that ATAD2 interacts directly with SIRT7 through its brominedomain and enhances its protein stability, thereby mediating cellular senescence in endometrial cancer.

SIRT7-mediated deacetylation of p53 at lysine K382

In our study, ATAD2 regulates cellular senescence in endometrial cancer through the p53/p21 signaling pathway via interaction with SIRT7. As a member of the histone deacetylase family, SIRT7 modulates histone acetylation, thereby influencing chromatin compaction and subsequently regulating gene transcription and protein expression. Previous studies have demonstrated that SIRT7 can regulate the acetylation status of p53, thereby modulating its expression and further affecting tumor progression [18]. However, this mechanism has not been verified in endometrial cancer.

To address this, we first performed molecular docking to predict the binding potential between SIRT7 and p53. The results indicated a direct binding interface between the two proteins (Fig. S7A), supporting our hypothesis. Moreover, SIRT7 was found to regulate both protein and mRNA levels of p53 (Fig. S7B). We then transfected Ishikawa endometrial cancer cells with SIRT7-overexpressing lentivirus and corresponding controls, followed by co-immunoprecipitation assays to assess the impact of SIRT7 on p53 acetylation. As shown in Figure S7C, SIRT7 overexpression reduced both acetylated and total p53 protein levels. Previous reports suggested that SIRT7 deacetylates p53 at lysine 382 (K382) [19]; we therefore assessed whether the same site-specific modification occurs in endometrial cancer. Indeed, SIRT7 overexpression decreased acetylation at p53 K382 (Fig. S7D-E), whereas treatment with the SIRT7 enzymatic inhibitor 97,491 enhanced acetylation at this site.

Subsequently, to verify whether SIRT7 regulates p21 and cellular senescence via p53 in endometrial cancer, we transfected cells with p53 knockout plasmids and examined p21 levels. As shown in Figure S7F-G, p53 deletion resulted in downregulation of both p21 mRNA and protein. Rescue experiments further revealed that ATAD2 knockdown, which suppresses SIRT7 expression, activated the p53/p21/rH2A signaling pathway and promoted cellular senescence. p53 deletion inhibited downstream signaling without affecting ATAD2 or SIRT7 expression. Interestingly, simultaneous knockdown of ATAD2 and p53 partially reversed the pathway activation induced by ATAD2 depletion.

Taken together, our results indicate that SIRT7 deacetylates p53 at lysine K382, thereby modulating p21 transcription and regulating the senescence process in endometrial cancer cells.

Analysis revealed that among patients with the highest mutation rate in TP53 mutations, those with high ATAD2 expression were more likely to develop TP53 mutations than those with low ATAD2 expression (Fig. 6A). ATAD2 inhibitors effectively inhibit the growth of tumors and cellular senescence in vivo

Discussion

Endometrial cancer (EC) remains one of the most prevalent gynecological malignancies worldwide, with rising incidence and mortality rates despite advances in conventional therapies [23]. The limited efficacy of current treatment modalities underscores the urgent need to identify novel molecular targets and therapeutic strategies [6, 24]. In this study, we delineate a previously unreported axis involving ATAD2, SIRT7, and the p53/p21 pathway that orchestrates cellular senescence and the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) to foster an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) and promote EC progression.

Our findings first establish ATAD2 as a prognostic biomarker and functional driver in EC. Consistent with its oncogenic roles in other solid tumors [25–29], ATAD2 is significantly upregulated in EC tissues and correlates with advanced histological grade, FIGO stage, and poor patient survival. Functional assays confirm that ATAD2 enhances EC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, underscoring its contributory role in tumor aggressiveness.

Mechanistically, we identify ATAD2 as a regulator of cellular senescence via interaction with SIRT7. SIRT7, a member of the sirtuin family known to delay senescence [30–32], is positively regulated by ATAD2 at the protein level. Through co-immunoprecipitation, we demonstrate a direct physical interaction between ATAD2 and SIRT7. Downregulation of ATAD2 reduces SIRT7 expression, leading to activation of the p53/p21 pathway, increased SA-β-gal activity, and induction of SASP factors such as IL-6 and TNF-α. Conversely, ATAD2 overexpression suppresses senescence and SASP, an effect reversible upon SIRT7 knockdown. These data collectively support a model wherein ATAD2 restrains senescence in EC cells through a SIRT7–p53/p21 axis.

Notably, the SASP induced by ATAD2 inhibition exhibits immunomodulatory functions. We observed that conditioned medium from ATAD2-knockdown cells promotes M1-like polarization of macrophages, whereas ATAD2-overexpressing cells favor an M2-like phenotype. This shift toward an anti-tumor immune milieu is further supported by bioinformatics analyses linking high ATAD2 expression to reduced immune cell infiltration and enriched TP53 mutation burden—a genomic alteration associated with immunotherapy resistance and poor prognosis in EC. Thus, ATAD2 appears to facilitate immune evasion not only by dampening senescence but also by shaping a tumor-permissive macrophage landscape via SASP.

The translational relevance of our findings is underscored by the efficacy of BAY-850, a pharmacological inhibitor of ATAD2, in suppressing tumor growth and enhancing intratumoral senescence and M1 macrophage polarization in vivo. These results nominate ATAD2 as a druggable target for EC, particularly in TP53-mutated or immunotherapy-refractory subsets.

However, this study still has several limitations that need to be fully considered when interpreting the results. In the macrophage polarization experiment, we used the THP-1 cell line as a model. Although phenotypic changes were verified through protein level markers and flow cytometry, THP-1 cells cannot fully mimic the biological complexity and heterogeneity of primary human monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs). Future research combining primary MDMs or patient-derived organoid-immune cell co-culture systems will provide stronger support for the conclusions of this study in a more physiologically relevant context. Relatedly, the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) is itself a highly dynamic and complex secretory network. Whether other unidentified soluble molecules or extracellular vesicles, besides the factors detected in this study, participate in tumor microenvironment remodeling remains to be further investigated. Furthermore, SASP plays a dual role in tumor biology. Although it primarily exhibits a tumor-suppressive effect in our model, under specific microenvironments or long-term conditions, SASP may also promote tumor progression by inducing chronic inflammation and stromal remodeling [4, 14]. Therefore, future research should utilize orthotopic transplantation models or genetically engineered mouse models to systematically evaluate the long-term effects of persistent SASP induction on the occurrence and progression of endometrial cancer.

At the mechanistic level, although we observed a significant correlation between ATAD2 expression levels and TP53 mutation status, the causal relationship between the two remains unclear. Whether ATAD2 regulates the p53 signaling pathway directly or indirectly requires further clarification. Meanwhile, although this study established the p53/p21 axis as a key effector pathway for ATAD2-induced cellular senescence, cellular senescence itself is a complex biological process regulated by multiple signaling networks, involving alternative pathways such as the p27-DREAM complex, PICS, and TGF-β/SMAD [33, 34]. While GSEA analysis (Figure S8) suggests that the p53/p21 pathway plays a central role in this model, whether and how ATAD2 regulates these alternative senescence-related pathways remains to be systematically explored. Future research should further integrate multi-omics analyses and functional validation experiments to more comprehensively elucidate the ATAD2-mediated senescence regulatory network in endometrial cancer. Finally, the clinical translational potential of BAY-850 and other small molecule inhibitors targeting ATAD2 still needs to be validated through systematic pharmacokinetic assessments, off-target effect analysis, and in vivo safety studies.

In conclusion, our study reveals a novel epigenetic–senescence–immune network in EC centered on the ATAD2–SIRT7–p53/p21 axis. By restraining cellular senescence and modulating SASP-driven macrophage polarization, ATAD2 fosters an immunosuppressive TME conducive to tumor progression and immune evasion. These insights not only advance our understanding of EC pathogenesis but also highlight ATAD2 inhibition as a promising therapeutic strategy worthy of clinical exploration.

Conclusion

Our study focuses on the ATAD2-SIRT7-p53-p21 regulatory axis of cellular senescence in endometrial cancer. We found that ATAD2 stabilizes the protein expression level of SIRT7 by interacting with SIRT7. SIRT7 further deacetylates the K382 site of p53 lysine, regulating p21 transcription to trigger changes in the expression levels of cellular senescence-related genes, thereby affecting the cellular senescence process. This study reveals for the first time that ATAD2 overexpression drives the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), thereby reshaping the endometrial carcinoma immune microenvironment and potentially promoting TP53 mutation enrichment and immunotherapy resistance. These results indicate that ATAD2 is a regulator in endometrial cancer and suggest that it may be a potential therapeutic target for endometrial cancer.
