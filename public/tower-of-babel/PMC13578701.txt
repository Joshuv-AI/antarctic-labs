Explainable machine learning for climate change attribution and hotspot identification: spatial cross-validation analysis in South Asian Region
Authors: Ram Chand, Saeeddudin Sheikh, Barkha Kanjwani, Saqia Bukhari, Harsh Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We present a machine learning framework for climate change attribution and hotspot identification, with emphasis on testing geographic transferability through spatial cross-validation. We evaluate eight ML models for explaining temperature anomalies in Sindh, Pakistan on 44 years of observations. We performed spatial transferability testing using Leave-One-District-Out Cross-Validation (LODO-CV) to assess whether learned relationships generalize to districts not seen during training. Gradient Boosting was the most successful algorithm with (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2 = 0.914\pm 0.098$$\end{document}) under LODO-CV, which indicates robust transferability across the region’s diverse climatic zones. The modest difference between LODO-CV and random cross-validation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta R^2 \approx 0.02$$\end{document}) is consistent with findings that temporal structure often dominates spatial structure in climate time series1; we thus interpret LODO-CV as a complementary robustness check rather than a correction for inflated performance. SHAP feature attribution showed that climate variables (37.6%), temporal trends (32.0%), and anthropogenic proxies (23.7%), are the most important predictors, although it is also important to note the caveat that the importance of proxies is only indicative of correlation, not causation, and must be carefully considered when applying to policy matters. A sensitivity analysis excluding all temperature-derived predictors confirms that the model achieves \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2 = 0.914$$\end{document} using only temporal, geographic, and anthropogenic features, ruling out feature-target circularity. Temporally blocked cross-validation (train ≤2015, test 2016–2024) yields \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2 = 0.445$$\end{document}, honestly demonstrating that temporal extrapolation is substantially more challenging than spatial interpolation—a well-documented limitation of statistical climate models. By using a dual-index model, which integrates the frequency of extreme events with average climate changes, we were able to pinpoint seven hotspots of climate change, concentrated in Karachi and Hyderabad urban areas, which are exposed to compound risk of urbanization, coastal exposure, and rising temperature extremes. We emphasize that our framework addresses attribution—understanding which factors correlate with observed temperature variability—rather than operational forecasting, which appropriately relies on numerical weather prediction models. The results demonstrate the value of spatially explicit validation procedures for confirming geographic transferability of climate ML models and offer practical suggestions to specific adaptation planning for the most climate-prone regions.

Introduction

Climate systems generate vast, multi-dimensional observational records spanning temperature, precipitation, vegetation, and atmospheric composition2,3. Extracting the underlying physical relationships from these heterogeneous data streams—collected at different spatial and temporal scales—remains a central challenge in climate science3–7. Machine learning methods offer a complementary approach to process-based models by identifying statistical patterns in high-dimensional data that may be difficult to detect through traditional analysis2,8.

The theoretical basis for ML in climate science rests on the capacity of ensemble methods and neural networks to approximate complex, nonlinear functions9,10. The climate system operates through physical laws—radiative transfer, fluid dynamics, thermodynamic equilibria—expressible as mathematical functions of state variables. ML algorithms can learn empirical approximations of these relationships from observational data without explicit specification of governing equations2, complementing traditional climate modelling that requires explicit parameterisation of subgrid processes. Recent applications include deep learning models for weather prediction11, neural network emulators for climate model components12, and hybrid physics-ML systems for teleconnection detection13. However, ML models face well-known limitations including sensitivity to training data distribution, difficulty with out-of-distribution extrapolation, and the risk of learning spurious correlations rather than physical relationships.

There are however massive constraints to the actual application of this theoretical promise. Observational records are not complete, and are limited to a few decades as compared to the geological time, and the curse of dimensionality makes it difficult to find patterns in limited data sets. A methodological issue with machine-learning-based climate analysis is that of spatial autocorrelation the tendency of geographically close observations to have correlated values. Climate variables are inherently spatially structured; adjacent areas will have share patterns of atmospherical circulation, will get equal share of solar forcing, and will share synoptic scale weather systems. The space correlation may permit information leakage between training and test sets due to the presence of standard cross-validation that randomly splits observations without regard to spatial structure, using standard cross-validation at the scale of (at least) the k model. However, recent research suggests that this effect differs significantly by data structure: Bergmeir et al.1 show that with time-series data dominated by time-structure, blocking-based cross-validation is only slightly better than traditional methods. Spatial cross-validation, however, is also an effective robustness check of geographic transferability14,15. When climatic adaptation planning is being considered and resources have to be assigned to certain areas, the assurance that models can be extended to unexplored areas provides the necessary confirmation of their practical usefulness.

Spatial cross-validation procedures measure geographic transferability through enforcing geographic separation between training and test subsets. The leave-one-region-out approaches, spatial blocking, and buffer strategies ensure that the test predictions are not able to utilise spatial autocorrelation existing in the training data15,16. These validation schemes supplement temporal validation through the consideration of a different manner of generalization. The difference of performance between random and spatial cross-validation varies depending on use: the difference between species-distribution models are often large, and the difference between climate time-series data with strong time trends are often small1,14.

In South Asia, the presence of a pressing and positive opportunity of conducting spatially rigorous climate-machine-learning studies can be found. The area, where 2.09 billion individuals live, faces the severe exposure to climate changes and produces under 8-9% of the global emissions of greenhouse gases17 . Pakistan is one of the top ten countries to be impacted by climate change18 and Sindh Province, the economic powerhouse of the country and the second-populated with 55.7 million inhabitants, has registered urban warming rates of 0.3–\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.5\,^\circ$$\end{document}C per decade19,20. Climatic diversity in the province is high with the Thar Desert in the Tharparkar district being hyperarid and the Indus Delta in Thatta district having a monsoon influence thus producing heterogenous microclimates, which cripple the effectiveness of homogenous adaptation strategies. This climatic heterogeneity makes Sindh a good testbed on which to test spatial machine-learning: once the models trained on most districts have been able to predict temperature anomalies in withheld districts with significantly different microclimates, then those results are the proof of successful learning of transferable climate-feature interactions rather than commemoration of district-specific ones.

Recent progress supports the possibility of machine learning to make predictions of temperatures13, climate downscaling21, and extreme events detection22. At the same time, explainable-AI methods become more and more useful in providing policy-relevant explanation of machine-learning predictions23,24. However, the use of machine-learning methods to assess regional climatic conditions is still limited by a series of long-standing gaps: insufficient consideration of geographic transferability; lack of anthropogenic variables, including urbanisation, land-use change, and emissions proxies, which adjust local climatic conditions; insufficient uncertainty quantification, where point estimates are reported with no confidence intervals; and a geographic bias with North America and Europe being studied, despite South Asia being more susceptible.

These hypotheses are tested against explicit baselines: H1 uses a climatological mean baseline (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hat{y}} = {\bar{y}}$$\end{document}, equivalent to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2 = 0$$\end{document}) and provincial-mean baseline (predicting the cross-district mean); H2 compares LODO-CV to random 5-fold CV under identical hyperparameters; H3 is validated by cross-referencing identified hotspots against documented climate impacts from the Pakistan Disaster Management Authority20.

The paper will fill these gaps by applying an ambitious spatial validation system in Sindh Province, in Pakistan. We use Leave-One-District-Out Cross-Validation (LODO-CV) in 22 districts to determine whether the relationships learned generalise to geographically different locations, the evaluation of temporal generalisation was performed on a held-out test set of 20202024. The systematic evaluation of eight machine-learning algorithms, including Ridge, Lasso, Elastic Net, Random Forest, Extra Trees, Gradient Boosting, XGBoost, and LightGBM, is performed with the same spatial -validation procedures, using 33 features, which include climate, time, anthropogenic proxies, geographic, and land-use and clearly separating between predictive correlation and causal attribution. We combine SHAP-based feature attribution with a dual-index classification framework to identify and interpret climate change hotspots. We emphasize that our goal is attribution understanding the correlates of observed temperature variability rather than prediction in the operational forecasting sense. Meteorological temperature forecasting appropriately relies on numerical weather prediction models with full atmospheric physics. Our framework instead addresses policy-relevant questions: which factors correlate with regional warming patterns, do these relationships generalize spatially, and which districts warrant priority adaptation investments?

Data sources

Rationale for climate variable selection

The climate variables included in this study were chosen to capture the principal physical drivers of temperature variability in semi-arid to arid regions: (i) maximum and minimum temperatures (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_{\max }$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_{\min }$$\end{document}) capture the diurnal temperature range, a key indicator of urbanization-driven warming and land-use change27; (ii) precipitation governs soil moisture and evaporative cooling, which directly modulate surface temperature anomalies in arid regions28; (iii) relative humidity reflects atmospheric moisture content and influences the surface energy balance; (iv) wind speed controls advective heat transport and boundary-layer mixing; (v) solar radiation is the primary energy input driving the surface energy budget. Among anthropogenic variables, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {CO}_2$$\end{document} emissions proxy the long-term radiative forcing signal, nighttime light intensity captures urbanization and the urban heat island effect [?], and NDVI quantifies vegetation cooling effects29,30. Together, these variables span the dominant terms in the surface energy balance equation, providing a physically motivated feature set for temperature anomaly attribution.

Data integration and quality control

Raw data underwent rigorous preprocessing: (1) temporal alignment to annual resolution at district level; (2) linear interpolation for gaps ≤3 years; (3) outlier detection beyond ±4 standard deviations; and (4) z-score normalization of continuous features. The final integrated dataset comprises 968 observations (22 analyzed districts × 44 years) with complete feature coverage. The complete data pipeline is given in Fig. A1 in Supplementary Material.

Detailed preprocessing steps

For the ML pipeline specifically: (a) NASA POWER daily records were aggregated to annual district-level means using area-weighted averaging over the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.5^{\circ }$$\end{document} grid cells falling within each district boundary; (b) EDGAR emissions were disaggregated from national totals to district level using population-weighted allocation based on census data; (c) NDVI and nighttime light satellite composites were extracted at district centroids and gap-filled using temporal interpolation; (d) features with zero variance were removed prior to modeling; (e) z-score standardization (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x' = (x - \mu )/\sigma$$\end{document}) was applied to all continuous features using training-set statistics only (no test-set leakage); (f) no imputation was applied to the target variable—only complete observations were retained. Feature-target relationship

We note that some predictor features (e.g., rolling temperature means, humidity) are derived from or correlated with temperature measurements. For an attribution study—understanding which factors correlate with temperature variability—this is methodologically appropriate: we seek to decompose variance across feature categories. However, this would be circular for an operational forecasting task. Our framework explicitly addresses attribution rather than forecasting; meteorological forecasting appropriately uses numerical weather prediction with full atmospheric physics rather than statistical relationships with concurrent observations. Critically, our primary feature set of 33 predictors deliberately excludes all temperature-derived variables (T2M, T2M_MAX, T2M_MIN, dew point temperature, and longwave radiation). The model predicts temperature anomaly using only temporal indicators, geographic features, precipitation, and anthropogenic proxies. We validate this design choice through a sensitivity analysis (“Sensitivity analysis: feature-target circularity test” section) demonstrating that adding temperature-derived features back into the model does not improve performance (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2 = 0.880$$\end{document} vs. 0.914 without temperature features), confirming the absence of circular reasoning.

Methodology

Machine learning models

We evaluated eight ML algorithms representing three model families (see Fig. A3 in Supplementary Material). Algorithm selection was guided by three criteria: (1) established effectiveness in environmental modeling2; (2) spanning the complexity spectrum from interpretable linear models to flexible ensembles; and (3) availability of mature implementations with hyperparameter tuning support. This selection enables systematic comparison of the bias-variance tradeoff in climate prediction contexts.

Baselines and controls

Two baseline models serve as performance controls: (i) a climatological mean baseline that predicts \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hat{y}} = {\bar{y}}$$\end{document} for every observation (equivalent to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2 = 0$$\end{document}), and (ii) a provincial-mean baseline that predicts each district’s anomaly as the mean anomaly across all 22 districts in the same year, testing whether district-level features add value beyond year-level trends. All eight ML models are compared against these baselines. Additionally, the three regularized linear models (Ridge, Lasso, Elastic Net) serve as interpretable controls against the five nonlinear ensemble methods, enabling direct assessment of whether nonlinear feature interactions improve predictive performance.

Validation strategy: leave-one-district-out cross-validation

To test geographic transferability, we implemented Leave-One-District-Out Cross-Validation (LODO-CV). This approach complements standard temporal validation by assessing whether models trained on 21 districts can accurately predict temperature anomalies in the held-out 22nd district (see Fig. A4 in Supplementary Material).

This approach: (1) tests geographic generalization to unseen locations; (2) simulates real-world scenarios where predictions are needed for data-sparse regions; (3) identifies districts where models struggle; and (4) provides a complementary robustness check to temporal validation. We note that for climate time series with strong temporal trends, the difference between spatial and random CV may be modest1, but LODO-CV remains valuable for confirming that learned relationships are not district-specific artifacts.

Temporally blocked cross-validation

In order to augment the spatial leave-one-dataset-out cross-validation evaluation, we conducted temporally blocked cross-validation to simulate the performance of models in inferring climate changes over time, which is a key test of climate attribution models.

Temporal block CV

Only the observations made between 1981 and 2015 (770 samples) were used to train the model, and then it was evaluated on the time frame 2016–2024 (198 samples). An additional division was done and models were trained on 1981-2010 (660 samples) and tested on 2011–2024 (308 samples). These divisions ensure that there is a full temporal distance between training and test.

Expanding window CV

The strategy uses progressive temporal validation where five-year test windows are used. The wider the training window is moved forward in time, the further the test window, thus measuring how the model accuracy changes as the training-to-test temporal gap changes.

These temporal validation methodologies fill a fundamental difference: a spatial leave-one-dataset-out cross-validation measures spatial interpolation i.e. prediction of locations not seen during the training period, whereas temporally blocked cross-validation measures temporal extrapolation i.e. prediction of future periods not seen during the training period. It is hoped that climate-attribution models will be superior to traditional performance in spatial interpolation because the climatic feature relationships underlying them are more stable over space than over time1,14.

Sensitivity analysis: feature-target circularity test

To directly address the question of whether temperature-derived predictors create circular reasoning, we conducted a sensitivity analysis comparing three feature configurations:

Set A (Baseline): The primary 33-feature set, which excludes all temperature-derived variables. This is the configuration used throughout the study.

Set B (With Temperature): Set A augmented with 14 temperature-derived features (T2M mean/max/min/std, T2M_MAX, T2M_MIN, dew point temperature, relative humidity, longwave radiation), scaled to zero mean and unit variance.

Set C (Non-Climate Only): A reduced 31-feature set excluding both temperature and precipitation features, retaining only temporal, geographic, and anthropogenic proxy variables.

All three configurations were evaluated under identical LODO-CV conditions using the same Gradient Boosting hyperparameters, enabling direct comparison of the impact of temperature-derived features on model performance.

SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)

SHAP values provide a unified framework for feature attribution based on cooperative game theory37. For each prediction, SHAP assigns an importance value to each feature representing its marginal contribution:24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \phi _j = \sum _{S \subseteq N \setminus \{j\}} \frac{|S|!(|N|-|S|-1)!}{|N|!} \left[ f(S \cup \{j\}) - f(S)\right] \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi _j$$\end{document} is the SHAP value for feature j, N is the set of all features, S is a subset excluding feature j, and f(S) is the model prediction using only features in S. SHAP values satisfy three desirable properties: (1) local accuracy (values sum to prediction minus expected value), (2) missingness (absent features have zero contribution), and (3) consistency (increased feature contribution cannot decrease importance). For tree-based models, we employed TreeSHAP38, which computes exact SHAP values in polynomial time.

Climate hotspot identification framework

We employed a dual-index framework combining extreme event frequency and mean climate change (Fig. A15):

Sindh extreme climate index (SECI)

SECI is a new composite index developed specifically for this study to quantify the frequency and intensity of climate extremes across Sindh’s districts. It adapts the Standard Euclidean Distance (SED) methodology from multivariate statistics to aggregate four orthogonal extreme event indicators into a single scalar index per district. This time constraint is in line with the existing empirical data on statistical climate models1. Therefore, the major contribution of the model lies in the category of spatial attribution, where it is possible to identify the factors that are related to heterogeneous warming among the districts, not to predict the future trajectories of the temperature.

Three key findings emerge (Fig. A21):

No circularity

The bare-bones model (Set A) has an \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2 = 0.914$$\end{document} when all predictors based on temperature are not involved, thus pointing to the said performance as not being bloated by tautologous constructs. The model explains the variance in temperature anomalies only by the time trends, geographical features, measure of precipitation, and the anthropogenic proxies.

Temperature features degrade performance

Adding 14 temperature-derived features (Set B) actually reduces performance to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2 = 0.880$$\end{document} with increased variance (σ = 0.192 vs. 0.098). This counter-intuitive result likely reflects multicollinearity: temperature features are highly correlated with each other and with the target, introducing noise into the gradient boosting optimization.

Robust non-climate attribution

Even with only non-climate features (Set C, 31 features), the model achieves \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2 = 0.882$$\end{document}, confirming that temporal and anthropogenic proxies alone capture the dominant warming signal. Discussion

Model performance and geographic transferability

The overall high accuracy of tree-based models compared to linear models has basic implications of climatic data. The interactions between climate variables are non-linear in nature, i.e. temperature anomalies do not change linearly with increase in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {CO}_2$$\end{document} as radiative forcing is logarithmic and vegetation responses are threshold-like. These relationships are represented by tree models which are recursively partitioned and do not need to be specified as functional.

Our LODO-CV results demonstrate strong geographic transferability: models trained on 21 districts predict temperature anomalies in held-out districts with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2 = 0.914$$\end{document}. The modest difference from random CV (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta R^2 \approx 0.02$$\end{document}) is consistent with Bergmeir et al.1’s finding that for time series with strong temporal structure, spatial blocking provides limited improvement over standard validation. This likely reflects that our key predictors—temporal indicators and national \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {CO}_2$$\end{document} trends—vary primarily across time rather than between districts at any given time point.

We interpret LODO-CV as a complementary robustness check confirming that learned relationships are not district-specific artifacts, rather than as a correction for inflated performance. This interpretation aligns with recent methodological guidance suggesting that the appropriate cross-validation strategy depends on data structure and prediction objectives14,16.

Validation against ground observations and prior studies

Several of our empirical findings are corroborated by independent ground observations and prior studies. First, our identified warming trend in Karachi (+0.3–\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.5^{\circ }$$\end{document}C/decade in urban districts) is consistent with station-based observations from the Pakistan Meteorological Department19 and with urban heat island studies in South Asian megacities44. Second, the vegetation-temperature relationship learned by the model (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-0.15^{\circ }$$\end{document}C per 0.1 NDVI) aligns with field-based estimates of vegetation cooling effects29,30. Third, the classification of Karachi and Hyderabad as compound hotspots is supported by PDMA disaster impact reports documenting disproportionate heat-related mortality in these urban centers20. Fourth, the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {CO}_2$$\end{document}-temperature correlation captured by the model is consistent in sign and approximate magnitude with the IPCC AR6 attribution of South Asian warming to anthropogenic forcing17. These independent validations provide confidence that the model has learned physically meaningful relationships rather than spurious correlations.

Interpreting anthropogenic proxy contributions: correlation vs. causation

The fact that such anthropogenic proxies explain 23.7% of the predictive signal in temperature anomaly predictors must be contemplated with care; this value represents predictive, but not causal power.

We cannot conclude that localised emissions cause localised temperature deviation; the proxy variables can be associated with temperature through a number of processes, including direct radiative forcing, urban heat-island effects, co-implication with patterns of development, or coordinated temporal changes in both emissions and temperature.

This careful approach is consistent with the general best practices in machine-learning to climate science2.

Distinction from operational weather forecasting

We emphasize that our framework addresses attribution—understanding which factors correlate with observed temperature variability—rather than prediction in the operational forecasting sense. Meteorological temperature forecasting appropriately relies on numerical weather prediction (NWP) models that incorporate full atmospheric physics, three-dimensional dynamics, and data assimilation from global observation networks. Our machine learning approach instead addresses complementary policy-relevant questions: which factors correlate with regional warming patterns over multi-decadal timescales, do these attribution relationships generalize across districts, and which districts warrant priority adaptation investments? These are fundamentally different objectives requiring different methodological approaches.

Temporal generalization and its implications

The gross difference between LODO-CV performance (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2 = 0.911$$\end{document}) and the temporally blocked validation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2 = 0.445$$\end{document} for the 2015 split; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2 = -0.102$$\end{document} for the 2010 split) is an indication that it is to be interpreted with caution. This dissimilarity depicts an inherent asymmetry in the generalisation properties of climate models in both space and time:

Spatial interpolation determines whether the associations acquired by studying the characteristics of climate and temperature can be applied to the geographical areas that remain unobserved but within the same period of time. Since the physical processes, including temperature gradient of latitudes, urban heat islands, and coastal moderating factors, are relatively stable throughout the districts of Sindh, the LODO-CV metric scores high.

Temporal extrapolation on the other hand consider the ability of the model to predict a climatic scenario outside the training period. This is a difficult task per se since climate change causes non-stationary behavior: global warming accelerates, the dynamics of feedback processes change, and the rate of extreme events increases and decreases. As such, statistical models that are fitted to historical data, find it difficult to fully capture such changing dynamics.

The negative value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document} of the 2010 splitting, including 14 years of tests, implies that longer-range predictions by the model do not work as well as a simple mean prediction, whereas the positively modest value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document} of 2015 splitting (9 years of tests) implies that shorter-range extrapolations are more practical. This finding is consistent with the expanding-window analysis, with the last temporal window (train ≤2021, test 2022–2024) having an\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2 = 0.645$$\end{document}.

These time constraints support the conceptualization of our study: the main usefulness of the model is the spatial attribution and hotspots identification, which LODO-CV provides an appropriate validation framework, rather than time prediction. Physically based models (GCMs, RCMs) or hybrid physics-machine-learning methods are never going to be replaced in operational climate forecasting work.

Ruling out feature-target circularity

The sensitivity analysis provides decisive information to rule out the existence of feature-target circularity. The base model (Set A, 33 predictors) does not include temperature-derived covariates but reaches an \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2 = 0.914$$\end{document} in a leave-one-dataset-out cross-validation (LODO-CV). When the fourteen extra temperature-derived variables (Set B) are included, model performance declines to an \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2 = 0.880$$\end{document}, seemingly due to the effect of multicollinearity. This observation directly dispels fears of the possibility of tautological relationships between predictors of temperature and the target of temperature anomaly.

This is further affirmed by the feature importance decomposition. Temporal predictors, (year_scaled: 18.0%, year_squared_scaled: 15.6%), national \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {CO}_2$$\end{document} emissions (16.5%), and precipitation (12.8%) are the most predictive in Set A, none of which are temperature derivatives. Once temperature features are added under Set B, these features are assigned to the highest rank of importance (T2M_MIN_mean: 38.8%, T2M_MAX_mean: 17.5%), which proves that the creation of the circular reasoning and confirms the methodological decision to exclude the temperature predictors.

Limitations and future directions

There are several shortcomings: while LODO-CV confirms strong spatial generalizability (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2 = 0.911$$\end{document}), temporally blocked validation reveals limited temporal extrapolation capability (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2 = 0.445$$\end{document} for the 2015 split; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2 = -0.102$$\end{document} for the 2010 split), reflecting the well-documented challenge of non-stationarity in climate time series. The statistical associations are not causal relations; proxies of population-weighted emissions are used rather than actual measurements; annual resolution does not allow analyzing seasonal variations.

Future research should include: (1) hybrid physics-ML approaches incorporating process-based constraints to improve temporal generalization; (2) validation using Pakistan Meteorological Department ground-truth data; (3) sub-annual resolution analyses based on LSTM or Transformer networks; (4) causal-inference methods such as Granger causality or structural equation modelling; and (5) integration with CMIP6 projections for future scenario analysis.

Conclusion

This paper shows that spatial cross-validation provides a valuable complementary assessment of machine learning models for regional climate attribution. The Leave-One-District-Out Cross-Validation framework tests geographic transferability—whether learned relationships apply to districts not seen during training—providing confidence that models capture genuine climate-feature associations rather than district-specific artifacts.

Gradient Boosting achieved strong geographic transferability (LODO-CV, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2=0.914\pm 0.098$$\end{document}), with high predictive accuracy for districts totally avoided in training. The modest difference between LODO-CV and random CV (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta R^2 \approx 0.02$$\end{document}) is consistent with findings that temporal structure often dominates spatial structure in climate time series1. A sensitivity analysis confirmed the absence of feature-target circularity: the model uses no temperature-derived predictors, and adding them actually degrades performance (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2 = 0.880$$\end{document} vs. 0.914). Temporally blocked validation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2 = 0.445$$\end{document} for ≤2015 split) honestly demonstrates the expected gap between spatial interpolation and temporal extrapolation, reinforcing that the model’s value lies in attribution and hotspot identification rather than temporal forecasting. Anthropogenic proxies explained 23.7% of predictive variance but this is not a causal relationship. Partial dependence analysis identified vegetation-temperature relationships (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-0.15^\circ$$\end{document}C per 0.1 NDVI) and temporal warming trends (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$+0.02\,^\circ$$\end{document}C per decade) consistent with physical processes.

Deep learning did not provide significant improvements (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2 = 0.973$$\end{document} vs. 0.967) at the cost of explainability; tree-based models remain preferable for policy implementation requiring explainability. Seven climate hotspots were identified through the dual-index classification, concentrated in Karachi and Hyderabad.

For policymakers: Information identifying Karachi and Hyderabad as climatic hotspots when compared to conventionally hot but climatically stable districts like Jacobabad offers useful intelligence for distributing climate adaptation resources. The effects of climate change are heterogeneous, and spatial analysis using machine learning can guide direct interventions where they are most effective.
