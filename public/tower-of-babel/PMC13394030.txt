Integration of Serum Neurofilament Light Chain and Cortical Dysfunction Improves Diagnostic Accuracy in ALS
Authors: Aicee Dawn Calma, Nathan Pavey, Yukiko Tsuji, Amirul Ab Ghapar, Mehdi A.J. van den Bos, Bronwen Orden, Grace Lee, Linda Mekhael, Julie Ryder, Cláudia Santos Silva, David McDonald, Fakhria Kakar, Con Yiannikas, Matthew C. Kiernan, David A. Brown, Parvathi Menon, Steve Vucic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo determine whether integration of serum neurofilament light chain (NfL) and cortical dysfunction improves diagnostic accuracy in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) when applied alongside the Gold Coast criteria (GCC).MethodsIn this prospective study, 148 participants with suspected ALS were recruited (101 ALS and 47 with ALS mimicking disorders). Participants taking medications known to influence TMS measures were excluded. Serum NfL levels were quantified using a single‐molecule array assay. Cortical function was assessed using threshold tracking transcranial magnetic stimulation, with cortical dysfunction defined by reduced mean short interval intracortical inhibition or motor cortex inexcitability. Diagnostic performance was evaluated using sensitivity, specificity, and diagnostic odds ratios (DOR), with 95% confidence intervals (CI).ResultsSerum NfL demonstrated excellent discrimination (area under the curve 0.92, p < 0.001), with cut‐off ≥ 33.5 pg/mL providing a sensitivity of 0.85 (95% CI 0.77–0.91) and specificity 0.87 (95% CI 0.75–0.94). Cortical dysfunction demonstrated very good diagnostic performance (sensitivity 0.77 [95% CI 0.68–0.84], specificity 0.77 [95% CI 0.63–0.86]). The GCC achieved a sensitivity of 0.89 (95% CI 0.82–0.94) and specificity 0.89 (95% CI 0.77–0.95). Combining GCC with elevated NfL or cortical dysfunction increased the specificity to 0.98 (95% CI 0.89–1.00) while maintaining high sensitivity at 0.87 (95% CI 0.79–0.92), yielding the highest DOR.InterpretationIntegration of serum NfL and cortical dysfunction with the Gold Coast criteria improves diagnostic accuracy in ALS, particularly by increasing specificity. This multimodal approach provides a practical framework for earlier and more definitive diagnosis that may facilitate more efficient clinical trial recruitment.

Introduction

The diagnosis of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) remains clinically based, requiring evidence of upper (UMN) and lower motor neuron (LMN) dysfunction within the same body region, or LMN involvement across multiple regions, in the setting of a progressive motor syndrome [1, 2, 3, 4]. Given the marked phenotypic heterogeneity and the absence of a pathognomonic diagnostic test, timely diagnosis remains challenging [5]. Consequently, several diagnostic criteria integrating clinical and neurophysiological features have been developed to support diagnostic certainty, guide patient management, and facilitate clinical trial recruitment [6, 7].

Earlier diagnostic criteria, including the El Escorial criteria, Revised El Escorial criteria, and Awaji criteria, defined multiple levels of diagnostic certainty [8, 9, 10], and were consequently limited by their complexity, resulting in lower inter‐rater reliability [11]. Additionally, these diagnostic categories did not reflect disease stage, with up to 22% of patients dying while still classified as “possible ALS” [12]. The Gold Coast criteria (GCC) were developed to address these limitations [13]. Multiple cohort studies have consistently demonstrated higher sensitivity of GCC, irrespective of disease onset site, disease duration, functional disability, or ALS phenotype [14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. Specificity has been more variable and a recent meta‐analysis reported lower specificity of GCC [22]. A smaller aggregate‐data meta‐analysis including three studies reported higher pooled sensitivity and specificity, although the wide confidence intervals for specificity indicated substantial imprecision [23].

The incorporation of objective biomarkers reflecting upper and lower motor neuron dysfunction could further enhance the diagnostic precision of the GCC, facilitating earlier and more accurate diagnosis of ALS. Neurofilament light chain (NfL) is a cytoskeletal protein that is abundant in large calibre myelinated axons and is released into the extracellular space during axonal degeneration [24, 25]. Advances in ultrasensitive immunoassay technologies now allow reliable quantification of NfL in blood [26]. Using these assays, blood NfL levels have been shown to distinguish patients with ALS from healthy and disease controls with high diagnostic accuracy [27, 28, 29]. Consequently, elevated serum NfL has emerged as a promising diagnostic biomarker in ALS, reliably differentiating ALS from potential mimicking neurological disorders [27, 28, 29].

In terms of a physiological biomarker of brain motor activity, cortical dysfunction as identified by using threshold tracking transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) has emerged as a promising neurophysiological tool for providing diagnostic and pathophysiological information in ALS [30]. Reduced or absent short interval intracortical inhibition (SICI), together with motor cortex inexcitability, has been shown to reliably distinguish ALS from mimicking disorders in clinical settings [31, 32, 33, 34]. These findings indicate that TMS measures of cortical dysfunction have emerged as promising biomarkers of cortical motor system dysfunction in ALS and may provide complementary information regarding UMN dysfunction.

Recently, the combination of cerebrospinal fluid NfL levels and cortical dysfunction was reported as a biomarker of UMN dysfunction in ALS, effectively distinguishing ALS from mimicking disorders [35]. At present, clinical translation of NfL measurements in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) remains limited, as highlighted by the exclusion of a substantial number of patients due to the inability to obtain samples. Accordingly, the present study evaluated whether combining clinically accessible serum NfL levels with threshold tracking transcranial magnetic stimulation improves the diagnostic performance of the GCC in a real‐world cohort of patients with suspected ALS.

Methods

Participants were prospectively recruited from the multidisciplinary ALS clinic at Concord Repatriation General Hospital, University of Sydney, Sydney, Australia, in accordance with the STARD guidelines. Inclusion criteria comprised: (i) suspected ALS as determined by the referring physician, characterised by a progressive neuromuscular syndrome of at least 6 months' duration; and (ii) evidence of either a lower motor neuron syndrome or (iii) a mixed upper and lower motor neuron syndrome.

Participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (i) use of medications known to influence TMS parameters, including psychotropic agents such as antidepressants, anxiolytics, or GABA‐acting drugs, with the exception of riluzole; (ii) presence of an implanted pacemaker or other ferromagnetic device; (iii) prior history of seizures, stroke, transient ischaemic attack, or intracranial surgery; (iv) severe atrophy of the target muscle preventing reliable recording of motor evoked potentials; (v) excessive background surface electromyographic activity during recording; or (vi) absence of a confirmed diagnosis at follow‐up review. All participants provided written informed consent prior to recruitment. The study was approved by the Sydney Local Health District Human Research Ethics Committee and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Reference Standard

At the time of serum sampling and TMS assessment, participants were diagnostically unresolved. Final diagnostic classification was determined through longitudinal clinical follow‐up by experienced neurologists (ADC, NP, PM and SV), with participants classified as ALS if they met the Awaji diagnostic criteria at any stage of assessment. The Awaji diagnostic criteria for ALS served as the reference standard [10]. Threshold‐tracking TMS was performed at recruitment as the index test by examiners with neurology expertise and training in neurophysiological techniques. Examiners were blinded to the final reference standard classification and to the results of other clinical investigations at the time of testing. Serum NfL levels were collected at the time of recruitment and were analysed as a comparator to the TMS studies.

Clinical Assessment

At recruitment, all participants underwent a detailed clinical assessment, conventional neurophysiological studies, neuroimaging, and laboratory investigations to determine whether they fulfilled the GCC for ALS [13]. Subsequently, muscle strength was assessed by the Medical Research Council (MRC) score, assessing the following muscle groups: (i) upper limb ; shoulder abduction, elbow flexion and extension, wrist extension, finger abduction (first dorsal interosseous), and thumb abduction on both sides yielding a maximal upper limb MRC score of 60; (ii) lower limb ; hip flexion, knee extension, and ankle dorsiflexion on both sides yielding a maximal lower limb score of 30. Upper motor neuron (UMN) function was clinically assessed using the UMN score, ranging from the absence of UMN signs (score of 0) to pronounced UMN signs (score of 15) [36]. Data on site of disease onset and disease duration (months) were collected for all participants. In ALS patients, the ALS Functional Rating Scale‐Revised (ALSFRS‐R) score [37] was recorded, and the rate of disease progression was calculated according to a previously reported formula [38].

Procedures

Cortical excitability was measured from the left primary motor cortex using a figure‐of‐eight coil (internal wing diameter of 70 mm) connected to a BiStim2 device (Magstim Co., Whitlands, South‐West Wales, UK). The TMS coil was positioned at 45° to the mid‐sagittal plane with the coil handle directed to induce posterior‐to‐anterior current to the motor cortex. Neuronavigation was used to ensure that coil position was stable throughout the experiment (visor2, ANT Neuro, Netherlands). Motor evoked potentials (MEP) were recorded over the right abductor pollicis brevis (APB) muscle.

Initially, the resting motor threshold (RMT) was measured and defined as the stimulus strength needed to elicit and maintain a target MEP response of 0.2 mV (±20%). Serial threshold tracking TMS was applied to all participants in accordance with previously reported methodology [39]. SICI was measured over the following interstimulus intervals (ISI); 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 3.5, 4, 5, and 7 ms, using a paired‐pulse threshold tracking paradigm with the conditioning stimulus set to 70% of RMT. Tracking was initiated at ISI of 1 ms and advanced to 7 ms in sequential order. Tracking was considered stable when two MEP responses attained the tracking target 0.2 mV (±20%) allowing advancement to subsequent ISI. The stimulus intensity was changed in 1% increments or decrements of maximum stimulator output.

The degree of LMN dysfunction was assessed during the same visit. Measurements included peak‐to‐peak compound muscle action potential (CMAP) amplitude (mV), distal motor latency, and minimum F‐wave latency (ms) following median nerve stimulation at wrist, with responses recorded over the APB muscle. F‐wave frequency was also recorded. The MEP and CMAP responses were recorded using Ag/AgCl disk gel electrodes (3 M Corp, Maplewood, MN) arranged in a belly‐tendon montage.

Neurofilament Collection, Storage, and Processing

Serum samples were collected, processed, and stored according to standardised protocols. Venous blood was drawn into serum‐separating BD Vacutainer tubes and allowed to clot at room temperature for 30 min before centrifugation at 2400 g for 10 min at 4°C. Serum was aliquoted into sterile cryogenic microtubes and stored at −80°C until analysis. Serum NfL concentrations (pg/mL) were quantified using an ultrasensitive single‐molecule array assay (Simoa; Quanterix, Lexington, MA, USA) in a National Association of Testing Authorities (NATA) accredited laboratory at Westmead Hospital. Samples were diluted to fall within the assay dynamic range, and each assay run included calibrators, kit and inhouse quality control samples. All samples were analysed as per the standard laboratory operation instructions in batches following completion of recruitment using the same reagent lot number to minimise inter‐assay variability.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics and R version 4.2. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range), depending on data distribution assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Group differences were evaluated using the Student’s t‐test for normally distributed data and Mann–Whitney U test for non‐parametric data. Correlations were assessed using Pearson's or Spearman's correlation coefficients for parametric and non‐parametric data, respectively.

Diagnostic cut‐off values for NfL levels were established using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with the optimal threshold determined by the highest Youden's index. Cortical dysfunction consistent with ALS was defined as short‐interval intracortical inhibition (SICI) ≤ 5.5% or motor cortex inexcitability [30]. Diagnostic performance of GCC, NfL, and cortical dysfunction was evaluated by calculating sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and diagnostic odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Subgroup analysis was undertaken based on disease onset site, disease duration, degree of functional deficit and UMN signs.

Binary logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate the independent contribution of serum NfL, cortical dysfunction, and GCC to the diagnosis of ALS. The dependent variable was diagnostic classification, and all variables were treated as binary predictors. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% CI were calculated to estimate the strength of association between each variable and ALS diagnosis. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer‐Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit test. p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographics

Between March 2024 and October 2025, a total of 186 participants were screened for eligibility. Of these, 148 individuals (79.6%) met the predefined inclusion criteria and were enrolled in the study (Figure 1). Thirty‐eight participants (20.4%) were excluded at the screening stage. Ultimately, 101 participants were confirmed to have ALS while 47 participants were diagnosed with an ALS mimic disorder and served as disease controls (Figure 1).

Serum Neurofilament

Serum NfL levels were first examined to determine the optimal diagnostic threshold within the present cohort. NfL concentrations were markedly elevated in patients with ALS compared with those with ALS mimic disorders [ALS: 70 pg/mL, IQR 45–113; non‐ALS: 13 pg/mL, IQR 9–19] (Figure 2A). Receiver operating characteristic analysis demonstrated that serum NfL robustly discriminated ALS from mimicking disorders, with an AUC of 0.92 (95% CI 0.88–0.97, p < 0.001), indicating excellent diagnostic performance. Based on the ROC analysis, the optimal diagnostic threshold was determined to be ≥ 33.5 pg/mL (Youden's index = 0.72). This cut‐off was subsequently applied to define a positive NfL result in all further analyses of diagnostic utility.

Assessment of Cortical Function

Prior to undertaking assessment of cortical function, the degree of LMN dysfunction was evaluated. The CMAP amplitude was significantly smaller in ALS participants compared to ALS‐mimics (Table 1). Additionally, the distal motor latency and F‐wave latency were comparable between the two groups.

As previously reported, cortical dysfunction was a prominent feature within the current ALS cohort [30]. Mean SICI across interstimulus intervals (ISIs) of 1–7 ms was significantly reduced in ALS participants (Figure 2B and Table 1). In addition, peak SICI at ISIs of 2.5 ms was markedly reduced in the ALS group (Table 1), consistent with impaired intracortical inhibitory circuitry [32].

Diagnostic Utility

The diagnostic performance of the GCC, serum NfL levels, and measures of cortical dysfunction was evaluated first individually and subsequently in different combinations to determine whether a multimodal approach improved overall diagnostic accuracy. The sensitivity of the GCC was 0.89 (95% CI 0.82–0.94), with a specificity of 0.89 (95% CI 0.77–0.95), findings consistent with our previous study conducted in an independent cohort of participants [21]. The sensitivity and specificity of elevated NfL levels (defined as NfL ≥ 33.5 pg/mL) were comparable to those of the GCC (Table 2 and Figure 3). The sensitivity and specificity of cortical dysfunction, defined by mean SICI (ISI 1–7 ms) ≤ 5.5% or motor cortex inexcitability (resting motor threshold > 95% of maximum stimulator output) [40], were numerically lower (Table 2 and Figure 3), although the 95% confidence intervals overlapped.

The diagnostic performance of combined diagnostic models was subsequently examined (Table 2 and Figure 3). The model incorporating the GCC together with either elevated NfL levels or cortical dysfunction achieved a sensitivity of 0.87 (95% CI 0.79–0.92) and very high specificity of 0.98 (95% CI 0.89–1.00). In contrast, requiring the presence of all three measures (GCC, NfL and cortical abnormality) resulted in lower sensitivity 0.58 (0.49–0.68) although the specificity remained unchanged (Table 2).

Logistic Regression Analysis

Multivariable logistic regression analysis demonstrated that elevated serum NfL, the presence of cortical dysfunction, and fulfilment of GCC were all independently associated with a diagnosis of ALS. Elevated serum NfL (≥ 33.5 pg/mL) was associated with a significantly increased odds of ALS (OR 16.30, 95% CI 3.99–66.67, p < 0.001), as was the presence of cortical dysfunction (OR 10.05, 95% CI 2.28–44.25, p = 0.002). Fulfilment of GCC demonstrated the strongest association with ALS diagnosis (OR 45.57, 95% CI 10.09–205.82, p < 0.001). The independence of NfL and cortical dysfunction supports the concept that these biomarkers reflect distinct biological processes, namely neuroaxonal degeneration and cortical inhibitory dysfunction, thereby providing additive diagnostic value.

Combining Measures

Subsequently, the overall diagnostic discrimination of each measure was summarised by the DORs, which were highest for the GCC, followed by serum NfL levels and cortical dysfunction (Table 2). Combining diagnostic modalities improved overall diagnostic discrimination. Notably, the model incorporating GCC together with either elevated NfL levels or cortical dysfunction achieved the highest diagnostic performance, with a DOR of 311.38 (95% CI 39.49–2455.30; Table 2), driven primarily by the marked increase in specificity and positive predictive value. In contrast, the model requiring the presence of all three measures (GCC, NfL, and cortical abnormality) exhibited lower DORs, primarily driven by reduced sensitivity (Table 2).

Among the 11 ALS participants who did not meet the GCC at initial assessment, 7 exhibited both elevated serum NfL levels and abnormal cortical excitability, while 3 had only 1 abnormal parameter. Only 1 participant was classified as triple‐negative. Of the 5 participants ultimately diagnosed with an ALS‐mimicking disorder, 80% demonstrated both normal serum NfL levels and normal cortical excitability.

Subgroup Analysis

Subgroup analyses were undertaken to evaluate the diagnostic performance of the composite model (GCC AND [NfL OR cortical abnormality]) across clinically relevant disease strata, including site of disease onset, disease duration, level of functional impairment (ALSFRS‐R total score), and the extent of UMN involvement (Table 3). The composite score maintained high diagnostic sensitivity and specificity across both early and late stages of disease, in patients with mild as well as advanced functional impairment, and across the full spectrum of upper motor neuron signs. Diagnostic sensitivity remained high irrespective of the site of disease onset, although sensitivity was modestly reduced in patients presenting with bulbar or respiratory onset disease.

Correlations With Clinical and Neurophysiological Parameters

The serum NfL level showed correlations with several clinical measures in ALS participants. Age at presentation did not correlate with serum NfL levels. Serum NfL levels exhibited moderate positive correlation with the rate of disease progression (ρ = 0.53, p < 0.001), and a weak positive correlation with the UMN score (ρ = 0.28, p = 0.004). A weak negative correlation was evident between NfL levels and the ALSFRS‐R score (ρ = −0.29, p = 0.001), lower limb MRC score (ρ = −0.21, p = 0.03) and total MRC score (ρ = −0.20, p = 0.05). There was no association between serum NfL levels and measures of cortical inhibition, including mean SICI across interstimulus intervals of 1–7 ms (p = 0.98) or peak SICI at 2.5 ms (p = 0.98). Similarly, CMAP amplitude did not correlate with serum NfL levels (p = 0.21) or with mean SICI (1–7 ms; p = 0.78).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that serum NfL and cortical dysfunction are diagnostic biomarkers that reliably distinguish ALS from mimicking disorders. When applied alongside the GCC, the presence of either elevated serum NfL or the presence of abnormal cortical function significantly increased diagnostic specificity, enabling more confident confirmation of ALS. In addition, these abnormalities were evident in early (< 12 months) and later (> 12 months) disease stages, supporting the clinical utility of a multimodal approach integrating NfL and threshold tracking TMS as objective diagnostic measures, particularly in early disease stages where significant diagnostic delay remains the norm.

The enhanced diagnostic performance observed with the multimodal approach likely reflects the complementary biological information captured by each measure. Serum NfL is a marker of the rate of disease progression (disease rate) [25, 26, 27, 41, 42, 43, 44]. The presence of cortical dysfunction, reflected by reduced SICI or cortical inexcitability on threshold tracking TMS, represents a core feature of ALS involving impairment of cortical inhibitory circuits and cortical output [30, 31, 32]. The absence of correlation between NfL and T‐SICI suggests that these measures reflect distinct biological processes, while multivariable logistic regression confirmed that elevated NfL and cortical dysfunction were independently associated with ALS diagnosis, supporting their additive diagnostic value.

Consistent with the present findings, a previous study demonstrated that combining elevated CSF levels of NfL with cortical hyperexcitability improved diagnostic discrimination of ALS from mimicking disorders, with the greatest effect observed in patients with a paucity of UMN signs [35]. The absence of correlation between NfL and T‐SICI supports the concept that these biomarkers capture complementary and independent diagnostic information, consistent with the present findings.

Several important differences should be noted. Jacobsen and colleagues [35] assessed CSF NfL levels, whereas the present study used the more clinically accessible serum NfL, which is less invasive and more readily applicable in routine clinical practice. Given that CSF and serum NfL levels significantly correlate [26], the requirement for lumbar puncture in CSF NfL levels may unnecessarily limit feasibility and contribute to reduced recruitment, as evident in the previous study [35]. Additionally, single cortical excitability was assessed at a single time point (ISI 2.5 ms) using the parallel threshold tracking protocol, contrasting with the present study that assessed cortical dysfunction across a range of ISIs (1–7 ms) along with motor cortex inexcitability [30]. Importantly, the present study utilised the serial threshold tracking protocol, which has been shown to provide better diagnostic utility in distinguishing ALS from mimicking disorders than the parallel method [45]. These methodological differences in threshold tracking TMS protocols may account for the observed differences in diagnostic sensitivity within the overall ALS cohort.

The present study demonstrated that integration of serum NfL and cortical dysfunction with the Gold Coast consensus diagnostic criteria provides strong diagnostic utility in ALS, irrespective of disease stage or clinical phenotype. In contrast, such an integrated diagnostic framework was not evaluated in the previous study [35]. This study places these biomarkers within a practical diagnostic framework, offering broader applicability across the heterogeneous spectrum of ALS rather than being confined to specific phenotypic subgroups.

Consistent with previous findings [35], serum NfL levels in the present study correlated with UMN burden. This concordance, despite the use of different clinical measures, supports the robustness of NfL as a biomarker of UMN disease burden in ALS. In contrast to the previous study, which reported a strong correlation between cortical excitability measures and UMN burden [35], the present study did not demonstrate this relationship. This discrepancy may relate to methodological differences, including the use of different clinical measures for defining UMN dysfunction, the use of alternative threshold tracking protocols, and a broader definition of cortical dysfunction in the present study, as well as disease heterogeneity. From a diagnostic perspective, the absence of correlation in the present study suggests that cortical excitability measures may capture more global cortical dysfunction rather than directly reflecting clinical UMN burden. This further supports the concept that NfL and cortical dysfunction represent complementary and independent diagnostic biomarkers, reinforcing their combined diagnostic value.

A recent study reported that integration of serum NfL with GCC improved early identification and diagnostic certainty for ALS using logistic regression modelling [46]. The present study confirms the diagnostic utility of NfL and extends this framework by incorporating measures of cortical dysfunction as an independent diagnostic biomarker. This multimodal approach captures complementary pathophysiological processes, resulting in improved specificity and diagnostic certainty. Improved specificity may facilitate clinical trial enrolment by increasing confidence that enrolled participants have ALS and by reducing the inclusion of ALS mimics, which may otherwise dilute treatment effects and compromise trial validity. However, this must be balanced against the need to maintain adequate sensitivity, as early identification of eligible ALS patients remains essential for timely recruitment. Although serum NfL and cortical dysfunction showed comparable improvements in diagnostic accuracy, serum NfL may represent a more practical and scalable diagnostic adjunct, as it requires only blood sampling and does not depend on specialised TMS equipment or technical expertise. By contrast, TMS‐derived cortical dysfunction provides complementary pathophysiological information regarding cortical hyperexcitability and inexcitability, and may therefore retain value in specialised settings.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. This was a single‐centre study, and external validation in independent cohorts is required to confirm generalisability. This is particularly relevant given differences between the present findings and previous studies evaluating CSF NfL in combination with alternative TMS‐based measures of cortical dysfunction [35]. Although the present cohort was prospectively recruited and diagnosis was confirmed using an established diagnostic criterion, an independent validation cohort with harmonised serum biomarker, neurophysiological, and clinical follow‐up data was not available. Accordingly, the diagnostic performance of combined serum NfL and cortical excitability measures should be regarded as promising but preliminary and require confirmation in multicentre cohorts using prespecified diagnostic thresholds. Additionally, sample size may limit diagnostic utility for subgroup analyses.

Overfitting also represents a potential limitation, as the diagnostic thresholds for serum NfL were derived within the study cohort and therefore require validation in an independent cohort. However, similar NfL threshold values have been reported in independent studies [47], supporting the biological plausibility and potential generalisability of the present cut‐off. Additionally, CSF NfL was not assessed for direct comparison, representing a potential limitation. This is unlikely to have significantly influenced the findings, given the strong correlations between serum and CSF NfL levels have been consistently reported, particularly when measured using the same assay platform [26].

Although threshold‐tracking TMS requires specialised equipment and operator expertise, the protocol used in the present study is relatively brief, with acquisition of the relevant cortical excitability measures typically completed within 10–15 min. Nevertheless, implementation outside specialist centres may increase inter‐investigator variability. Recent integration of threshold‐tracking TMS into the commercially available MagXite system may improve generalisability [48], although multicentre validation is required to confirm reproducibility. An important methodological consideration is that participants taking medications known to significantly influence cortical excitability were excluded, thereby reducing pharmacological confounding of TMS‐derived measures. This may limit generalisability to patients receiving such therapies in routine clinical practice. While TMS examiners were blinded to the final reference standard classification and other clinical investigation results, complete clinical masking may be difficult in suspected ALS cohorts, particularly when assessments are performed by experienced clinicians. This represents a potential source of bias and could represent a potential limitation.

In conclusion, the integration of serum NfL and cortical dysfunction with GCC provides a robust and clinically applicable framework for the diagnosis of ALS. This multimodal approach improves diagnostic specificity, enables confident case ascertainment, and performs consistently across the heterogeneous clinical spectrum of ALS. These findings support the incorporation of objective biomarkers into routine diagnostic pathways and have important implications for an earlier diagnosis, improved patient stratification based on diagnostic certainty, and thereby optimisation of clinical trial recruitment.
