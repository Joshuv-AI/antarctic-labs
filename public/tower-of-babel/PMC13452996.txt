CREST-associated ruptured right MCA aneurysm: A case report and review of the literature
Authors: Nathalie Baungaard, Mohadese Ahmadzade, Troels Halfeld Nielsen, Goetz Benndorf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Intracranial aneurysms associated with systemic sclerosis and Calcinosis, Raynaud’s phenomenon, Esophageal dysmotility, Sclerodactyly, and Telangiectasia (CREST) syndrome are rare, and their pathophysiological relationship remains uncertain. We report the case of a 24-year-old male with systemic sclerosis and clinical features suggestive of CREST syndrome who presented with subarachnoid hemorrhage caused by a ruptured right middle cerebral artery bifurcation aneurysm. The aneurysm was treated with microsurgical clipping, followed by stent-assisted coiling for a residual aneurysm neck identified on follow-up angiography. The patient had a favorable neurological outcome with mild persistent cognitive deficits. A review of the literature identified only a small number of reported intracranial aneurysm cases associated with systemic sclerosis or CREST syndrome. This case differs from previously reported CREST-associated cases in patient age, sex, and aneurysm morphology, and highlights the possible contribution of connective tissue disease, smoking, and prior amphetamine exposure to aneurysm formation and rupture.

Introduction

The autoimmune disease scleroderma, or systemic sclerosis and its variant characterized by Calcinosis, Raynaud’s phenomenon, Esophageal dysmotility, Sclerodactyly, and Telangiectasia (CREST) syndrome causes thickening, hardening, and tightening of the connective tissue in the body, but are seldom associated with cerebral aneurysms [[1], [2], [3]]. An extensive literature search has identified only a small number of reported cases of cerebral aneurysms in patients with scleroderma or CREST syndrome [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. Nakae et al. [6] described 4 characteristic features related to cerebral aneurysms associated with CREST syndrome, including multiple aneurysms, fusiform morphology, distal location, and potentially worse prognosis after rupture [6].

In the present paper, we report on a young Caucasian male previously diagnosed with systemic sclerosis fulfilling the criteria for CREST syndrome who presented with a subarachnoidal hemorrhage from a saccular middle cerebral artery bifurcation aneurysm. We propose a possible association with underlying connective tissue disease and the development of the intracranial aneurysm.

Case history

A 24-year-old male was transferred to the Department of Neurosurgery, after onset of severe pulsating headache, vertigo, nausea and sensory disturbance in the face, tongue and left hand. The patient was awake and fully oriented. A cranial CT scan revealed a Fisher grade 1 subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH). The patient had been diagnosed with scleroderma at the age of 18, with symptoms since age 16. Clinical findings included sclerodactyly, minor periorbital contractures, acroosteolysis of the distal part of the distal phalanges, Raynaud’s, esophageal hypomotility in the distal part, and a positive anti-U1RNP antibody test. Serological testing for anticentromere antibodies (ACA) and antitopoisomerase I antibodies (anti-Scl-70) was negative. Pulmonary function testing demonstrated a reduced total lung capacity (TLC) of 79%, consistent with findings that may be observed within the spectrum of systemic sclerosis [9]. The patient started smoking at the age of 15, and had been smoking approximately 20 cigarettes a day. He had a 1-year premorbid amphetamine abuse. The patient had no known comorbid medical conditions and was not receiving treatment with corticosteroids or immunosuppressive agents.

Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) revealed a broad-based MCA bifurcation aneurysm at the right side with a maximum dome diameter of 9 mm. No additional aneurysm or other vascular pathology was diagnosed. The aneurysm was considered suitable for microsurgical clipping. The procedure was complicated by intraoperative rupture, attributed to a thin and fragile vessel wall. The aneurysm was successfully occluded using 3 surgical clips. The postoperative course was uneventful, and a follow-up CT scan performed 2 days later showed no evidence of infarction or vasospasm.

The patient was discharged after 6 days with minor cognitive deficits. He reported difficulties with concentration, particularly during reading and academic tasks, as well as increased irritability and reduced energy levels. The Rivermead behavioral memory test [10,11] performed 7 days postoperatively, yielded a score of 7 out of 12, consistent with mild memory impairment.

At 3-month follow-up, no physical complications were observed. Neuropsychological evaluation revealed persistent mild cognitive deficits, including impairments in learning, memory, and executive functioning, particularly in daily structuring and task organization, compared to his premorbid baseline. These deficits were attributed to the subarachnoid hemorrhage. The patient had a Modified Rankin Scale score of 1 and was considering resuming his studies. He had discontinued both smoking and amphetamine use.

A 6-month follow-up DSA demonstrated a small residual aneurysm neck. Given the patient’s young age, history of subarachnoid hemorrhage, intraoperative rupture, and underlying comorbidity, the residual lesion was treated with endovascular stent-assisted coiling.

Discussion

CREST syndrome is a connective tissue disorder characterized by Calcinosis, Raynaud’s phenomenon, esophageal dysmotility, sclerodactyly and telangiectasia. Although the exact pathogenesis of cerebral aneurysms associated with collagen diseases is still unknown [6], vascular involvement in systemic sclerosis (SSc) [3], has been associated with endothelial dysfunction, vasculitis, and inflammatory changes affecting small- and medium-sized vessels [8,12,13]. To date, cerebral aneurysms have been reported in only 5 cases with diagnosed or assumed CREST syndrome [3,[6], [7], [8]]. All cases were women between the age of 50-64 with irregular, fusiform or saccular shaped and/or multiple intracranial aneurysms (Table 1—Characteristics of CREST cases). The diagnosis of CREST syndrome typically requires the presence of 1 major or 2-3 minor features among its characteristic clinical manifestations [[1], [2], [3],6,7,14]. According to the classification criteria proposed by Nadashkevich et al. [14] these include: antibodies to centromere proteins (CENPs), Scl-70 (topoisomerase I), or fibrillarin; bibasilar pulmonary fibrosis; digital joint contractures or a positive prayer sign; dermal thickening proximal to the wrists; calcinosis cutis; Raynaud’s phenomenon with at least a 2-phase color change; distal esophageal hypomotility or reflux esophagitis; sclerodactyly or nonpitting digital edema; and telangiectasias.

In the present case, the patient fulfilled 3 of these criteria, namely distal esophageal hypomotility, sclerodactyly, and Raynaud’s phenomenon. Autoantibodies, including antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and ACA, are recognized markers of specific clinical subsets of systemic sclerosis, including CREST syndrome [3,7,9,15,16]. Our patient tested positive for anti-U1RNP antibodies (ANA subtype), which are related to a variety of connective tissue disorder, including SSc, Sjögren’s syndrome, systemic lupus erythematosus, polymyositis and overlap syndromes [9,16]. Two out of the 5 previously described CREST cases with cerebral aneurysms were ANA positive as well [6]. On the basis of these findings, the clinical diagnosis of CREST syndrome appears conceivable. However, patients with limited systemic sclerosis, including CREST syndrome, often exhibit a slow disease progression [14]. Given the patient’s young age, the future development of additional clinical features cannot be excluded, although it remains uncertain. Additionally, 4 cases were positive for anti-centromere antibodies, unlike the present case (Table 1). The fact, that this patient is negative for ACA, as opposed to the other cases, suggests that the pathogenesis of the aneurysm in this young man might be different from the other CREST cases with intracranial aneurysms. ACA is present in approximately 20%-30% of Caucasian patients with systemic sclerosis [9], but is strongly associated with CREST syndrome [8,[17], [18], [19]]. A positive ACA test could contribute to confirm the clinical diagnosis of CREST syndrome in the present case.

The mechanisms underlying the association between intracranial aneurysm formation and CREST syndrome are not well understood. Increased vascular fragility has been described in other connective tissue disorders, including Marfan syndrome, Ehlers–Danlos syndrome, neurofibromatosis type 1, and Loeys–Dietz syndrome [20,21]. However, reports are scarce on aneurysms associated with SSc and CREST syndrome, although aortic aneurysms [22,23], pulmonary aneurysms [24] and cerebral aneurysms [3,4,[6], [7], [8],25] has been described. Several pathological mechanisms have been proposed to cause aneurysm formation in patients with SSc and CREST syndrome due to weakening of the vascular wall. This might be as a result of vasculitis [[6], [7], [8],22,24,26] or arteritis [6], leading to vessel wall ischemia. Other mechanisms like inflammatory reactions [3,4], unregulated collagen metabolism [7,8], chronic hemodynamic stress, lack of perivascular support, and long-term steroid therapy [4,6] may cause vascular abnormalities and injury. Despite the perioperatively aneurysm rupture caused by a thin and fragile vessel wall, there was no evidence of irregularities in the other intracranial arteries in the present case. Furthermore, there was no radiological or clinical evidence of vasculitis, arteritis, calcifications, or other vascular pathology. Nevertheless, the absence of such findings does not exclude underlying vascular alterations, as similar observations have been reported in other cases of CREST-associated intracranial aneurysms [3,6,8].

The patient’s history of cigarette smoking may have contributed to both aneurysm formation and rupture, while prior amphetamine exposure may have increased the risk of rupture [27]. While the young age of the patient may argue against these factors as the sole cause, their contribution cannot be excluded [28].

Accordingly, we propose that the pathogenesis behind the formation and subsequent rupture of the cerebral aneurysm in the present case was associated with the presence of the connective tissue disease SSc and CREST syndrome. Given the small number of patients with this rare pathogenesis found in the literature, further studies and more substantial data obtain are necessary to clarify the potential connection between CREST syndrome and the formation of intracranial aneurysms.

It is important to consider coexisting clinical and environmental factors when interpreting this case. The limited information regarding genetic predisposition and lifestyle factors may obscure causal relationships. The occurrence of a cerebral aneurysm in a young patient with systemic sclerosis and features suggestive of CREST syndrome raises the possibility of an association; however, this remains uncertain. Although aneurysm formation in patients with CREST syndrome has been suggested to follow a more aggressive clinical course compared to patients without underlying vascular abnormalities [8], the available evidence is insufficient to establish a direct causal relationship. Further data are required to clarify whether connective tissue diseases are associated with an increased risk of aneurysm formation and rupture.

In conclusion, the present case differs from the previously described CREST cases with intracranial aneurysms, regarding sex, age and aneurysm morphology. Our findings suggest that patients with SSc including CREST syndrome could be considered having an increased risk for cerebral aneurysm formation like patients with other connective tissue disorders, such as Marfan syndrome and Ehlers–Danlos syndrome. Our patient may be the first young Caucasian male described with an intracranial aneurysm associated with SSc and CREST syndrome.
