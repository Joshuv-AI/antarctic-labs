Disease-modifying effects on chronic axon damage and white matter degeneration with 4-aminopyridine after acute traumatic brain injury
Authors: Kryslaine L. Radomski, Xiaomei Zi, Genevieve M. Sullivan, Regina C. Armstrong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Axonal injury is a hallmark pathology of acute traumatic brain injury (TBI) that drives degeneration of white matter in chronic TBI and in neurodegenerative diseases. No available therapies effectively treat damaged axons. We focused on early, potentially reversible, axonal pathologies of abnormal nodes of Ranvier and demyelination as intervention targets. 4-Aminopyridine (4-AP), which blocks potassium Kv channels, is an FDA-approved drug prescribed for multiple sclerosis to enhance axon signal conduction through nodes with Kv channels exposed by demyelination. We hypothesized that early 4-AP treatment after acute TBI could mitigate secondary axon damage and white matter degeneration. Adult male and female mice received a non-penetrating TBI or sham procedure. A low clinically relevant dose of 4-AP (0.5 mg/kg, b.i.d.) or saline vehicle was initiated at 24 h, administered over 4 weeks, then discontinued for 6-weeks prior to analysis at 10 weeks post-injury. TBI caused atrophy of corpus callosum (CC) white matter and increased serum neurofilament light (NfL) protein, a translational biomarker of axonal injury. 4-AP treatment after injury significantly attenuated CC atrophy and lowered serum NfL levels. 4-AP also significantly lessened CC myelin and oligodendrocyte loss while dampening neuroinflammation, i.e. astrogliosis and microgliosis. In a parallel electron microscopy study, 4-AP reduced intra-axonal structural damage and mitochondrial swelling, normalized axon diameters, and increased myelin thickness. These findings reveal 4-AP disease-modifying effects on the progression of evolving TBI pathology that endure long after discontinuing treatment. More broadly, these results demonstrate proof-of-concept support for developing interventions to treat axon damage and white matter degeneration.

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is an evolving condition that can cause persistent symptoms resulting in profound disability and worldwide economic burden [[1], [2], [3]]. Damage to long myelinated axons is a hallmark pathology of TBI that contributes to white matter degeneration and consequent poor patient outcomes [[4], [5], [6]]. Among head injury patients who received initial clinical care at a United States level 1 trauma center that included a head computed tomography scan, approximately 50% continued to experience three or more symptoms and over 70% reported at least one problematic symptom at one year after injury [7]. The head injury event identifies the start of a therapeutic window of opportunity to intervene in secondary injury processes that cause the majority of axon damage [5]. However, no disease-modifying therapies are available to treat axon damage prior to irreversible degeneration. To address this critical unmet need, we focused on interventions targeting early, potentially reversible, axonal pathologies of demyelination and node of Ranvier (NoR) disruption after TBI.

The FDA-approved small molecule, 4-aminopyridine (4-AP), is designed to block aberrant potassium efflux to enhance axon signal conduction through NoRs that have voltage-gated potassium (Kv1) channels exposed due to demyelination [8]. 4-AP is prescribed to alleviate walking speed deficits in patients with multiple sclerosis, yet the mode of action in vivo is not fully defined [[9], [10], [11]]. 4-AP has broader symptomatic benefits beyond walking disability that may be relevant to axon-myelin dysfunction in TBI, such as improving cognitive information processing speed deficits in multiple sclerosis clinical trials and enhancing working memory in low performing individuals in trials of healthy participants [12,13]. Notably, the potential for 4-AP to act beyond symptomatic relief and produce disease-modifying effects on neuro-axonal loss in the CNS was demonstrated in a longitudinal multiple sclerosis study of retinal degeneration [9].

We hypothesized that early 4-AP treatment after acute TBI could mitigate the progression of axonal injury and white matter degeneration. To test this hypothesis, we use a well-characterized mouse model of non-penetrating, concussive TBI that results in reproducible traumatic axonal injury in the corpus callosum (CC) with white matter pathology progressing over 6–10 weeks to significant degeneration and CC atrophy [4,[14], [15], [16], [17]]. TBI caused axonal swellings with vesicle and organelle accumulations visible using electron microscopy (EM) or with β-amyloid precursor protein immunolabeling [15,18]. TBI produced the characteristic diffuse pattern of clinical traumatic axonal injury, i.e. individual damaged axons dispersed among intact axons within the white matter [15,19]. Importantly, TBI also slowed axon signal conduction velocity and exhibited corresponding demyelination and NoR disruption with exposed Kv1 channels, a putative therapeutic target of 4-AP treatment [10,11,15,16]. NoR disruption has been identified as an early stage of axon damage after TBI in mouse, ferret, swine, and post-mortem human brain specimens [10,16,20,21].

Our initial preclinical studies of 4-AP in this TBI model focused on treatment efficacy and safety during the first week post-injury using diverse approaches to examine neuropathology, behavior, and gene expression [10,11]. These studies were conducted as randomized, controlled, and blinded with low 4-AP dosing (0.5 mg/kg) that achieved blood levels in mice matched to the clinical therapeutic target of 20–100 ng/ml [10]. 4-AP b.i.d. treatment was initiated one day after injury with analyses at 7 days [10,11]. Indeed, 4-AP treatment reduced axon damage, modified the underlying molecular pathology of the secondary injury response, and stimulated activity-dependent repair processes after acute TBI [10,11].

However, only long-term studies can address the unmet need for treatments with disease-modifying effects on axon damage that endure after discontinuing treatment and that are sufficient to alter the trajectory of white matter degeneration. The current studies report long-term experiments with 4-AP treatment for 4 weeks after TBI with analysis of tissue pathology and a serum biomarker of axon damage at 10 weeks post-injury. We find that early 4-AP treatment after acute TBI effectively alters evolving TBI pathology to reduce persistent axon damage long after discontinuing treatment, with corresponding attenuation of white matter degeneration.

The study design included randomized allocation of mice, controls for injury and drug treatment, and blinding through data analysis (Fig. 1A). 4-AP treatment was initiated one day after TBI, administered on weekdays (5 days/week) over 4 weeks, then discontinued for a 6-week washout period prior to analysis of degeneration at 10 weeks after injury or sham surgery (Fig. 1B). Immediate effects of TBI or sham procedures, as measured by recovery time for the righting reflex, were similar for mice allocated to receive 4-AP or vehicle injections (Fig. 1C). 4-AP treatment was well-tolerated, without producing signs of poor health or body weight changes between groups (Fig. 1D). Moreover, this 4-AP dosing did not alter sleep-wake function or complex motor learning assessments that were included during Study 1 (Fig. 1B, S1). However, in contrast to our prior study [17], TBI did not produce significant behavioral deficits (Fig. S1), which precluded evaluation of behavioral improvement with 4-AP treatment.

TBI model and 4-AP treatment

C57BL/6 mice (8–10 weeks old; Jackson Laboratories; RRID:IMSR_Jax:000664) underwent single closed-skull TBI or sham procedures as previously described [15]. Briefly, under isoflurane anesthesia, following a midline incision, a pneumatic device (Leica Biosystems) delivered a concussive impact (3-mm rounded steel tip, 4.0 m/s, 1.5 mm depth, 100 ms dwell time) onto the skull at bregma (0 mm A-P). Sham mice underwent identical procedures without impact. 4-AP (fampridine; Sigma-Aldrich, Cat#275875) was dissolved in sterile saline (0.9% sodium chloride; Fisher Scientific, Cat# NC9054335) as detailed previously [10]. Treatment with 4-AP (0.5 mg/kg) or saline vehicle was initiated 24 h post-surgery and administered intraperitoneally (i.p.) twice daily (b.i.d.) over 4 weeks (5 days/wk), followed by a 6-week washout period prior to analysis of degeneration at 10 weeks (Fig. 1B). Mice and specimens were monitored for predefined exclusion criteria, as detailed in Supplementary Materials.

Outcome measures

Study 1 evaluated CC neuropathology and serum neurofilament light protein (NfL) (n = 96; 48 males, 48 females). The primary outcome measure was CC atrophy quantified by CC width in coronal brain sections. Secondary measures included serum NfL protein concentration (Simoa® HD-1 Analyzer) and immunohistochemistry for myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG; myelin), degraded NfL (MCA-6H63 DegenoTag™; degenerating axons), CC1 (mature oligodendrocytes), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP; astrocytes), and ionized calcium-binding adaptor molecule 1 (IBA1; microglia). Antibody information is detailed in Table S1. Sleep-wake function (starting at 8 weeks) was tracked using non-invasive piezoelectric sensors and complex motor learning (starting at 9 weeks) was quantified using Misstep running wheels with irregularly spaced rungs, as in our prior study [17].

Study 2 focused on myelinated axon pathology with EM quantification (n = 16 males). The primary EM outcome was damaged axons with vesicle and organelle accumulations and/or compacted, electron-dense axoplasm. Secondary measures included axons with swollen mitochondria, axon caliber, myelin thickness, and g-ratio analyses.

Statistical analyses

Four experimental groups (sham + vehicle, sham+4-AP, TBI + vehicle, TBI+4-AP) were compared, with blinding. Statistical significance was set as p < 0.05. Bar graphs show mean ± SEM with individual mice as biological replicates and statistical analysis with GraphPad Prism (v10.1.4; RRIC:SCR_002798) using one-way ANOVA and Holm-Sidak's correction for multiple comparisons (Table S2). Degraded NfL immunohistochemistry detected degenerating axon immunolabeling only in the two TBI groups, which were analyzed using an unpaired t-test with Welch's correction. Study 1 results show combined data from both sexes. Among the outcome measures for CC width, serum NfL, and immunohistochemistry (MOG, CC1, GFAP, and IBA1), the only sex-associated treatment effect was observed for IBA1 immunolabeling in the TBI+4-AP group, with females higher than males (Table S3). The more focused design of Study 2 used only males. The magnitude of significant treatment effects in comparing TBI + vehicle with TBI+4-AP was calculated using Cohen's d (large effect, d > 0.8). NfL biomarker statistical outliers were identified for exclusion using the ROUT method (Q = 1%). For electron microscopy data, axon diameter, myelin thickness, and g-ratio measures used each mouse as the biological replicate, with data for individual axon values nested within that mouse, using linear mixed-effects model analyses with Gemini Pro 3.0.

Results

4-AP treatment attenuates white matter atrophy and lowers serum neurofilament light protein (NfL), a biomarker of axonal injury

To evaluate the potential for early 4-AP treatment to have disease-modifying effects on TBI-induced neurodegeneration, we conducted studies in a non-penetrating, concussive TBI mouse model that reproducibly exhibits CC axon damage and progressive atrophy of the CC white matter [16,17]. We demonstrate 4-AP disease-modifying effects in two separate studies using distinct techniques to examine axon damage and white matter degeneration after TBI (Fig. 1). Study 1 results show early 4-AP treatment attenuates chronic white matter degeneration after TBI, with CC atrophy as the primary outcome measure (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3). Study 2 results show early 4-AP treatment reduces chronic pathology of myelinated axons after TBI, with intra-axonal structural damage as the primary outcome measure (Fig. 4). All experiments used the same 4-AP dosing (0.5 mg/kg, twice daily), which effectively reduced axon damage at 7 days post-injury in this TBI model without producing seizure behaviors [10,25]. This 4-AP dose results in mouse blood levels matched to the clinical therapeutic target of 20–100 ng/ml [10], which is the highest FDA-approved dosing that avoids an unacceptable level of seizure risk [26,27]. In Study 1 experiments, TBI produced significant CC atrophy and myelin loss compared to sham mice in the vehicle groups (Fig. 2A–B). Remarkably, early 4-AP treatment significantly attenuated this chronic stage CC atrophy (Cohen's d = 0.90; Fig. 2A). 4-AP mediated preservation of CC structure after TBI was accompanied by improved myelination, which is an important measure of white matter integrity. Myelin was detected using immunolabeling for myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG) (Fig. 2B). Myelin loss in the CC after injury was significantly improved with 4-AP treatment (Cohen's d = 1.32; Fig. 2B). In addition to these pathological measures of white matter degeneration, we analyzed serum NfL protein (Fig. 2C), a clinically used blood biomarker of axonal injury for which the same assay is effective at late stages post-TBI in human and mouse [4,6,[29], [30], [31]]. While TBI significantly elevated serum NfL protein concentration, 4-AP treatment lowered NfL levels in injured mice (Cohen's d = 0.86; Fig. 2C). Furthermore, CC axonal profiles exhibited immunolabeling for degenerating NfL, identifying the CC as a source of serum NfL protein after TBI (Fig. 2D). Degenerating NfL immunolabeling is evident in axons of injured mice and significantly reduced with 4-AP treatment as compared to vehicle (Fig. 2E). These results show disease-modifying effects of early 4-AP treatment based on white matter pathology along with a corresponding clinical biomarker of axonal injury.

4-AP treatment mitigates oligodendrocyte loss and chronic neuroinflammation

Early 4-AP also reduced TBI-induced changes in glial populations in the Study 1 mice at 10 weeks after TBI (Fig. 3). White matter degeneration is mechanistically linked to a loss of homeostatic roles of oligodendrocytes, astrocytes, and microglia and concurrent gain of neuroinflammatory roles during injury or disease [5]. Corresponding with CC myelin loss after TBI (Fig. 2B), a significant loss in the number of mature oligodendrocytes in the CC was observed with CC1 immunolabeling in TBI mice compared to sham mice in the vehicle groups (Fig. 3A–C). In contrast, 4-AP treatment prevented this loss of oligodendrocytes in injured mice (Cohen's d = 1.52; Fig. 3B) and the density was unchanged from sham mice (Fig. 3C).

In human cases, CC white matter atrophy observed years after TBI is associated with ongoing axon damage and persistent neuroinflammation [32]. Thus, we determined the potential effects of 4-AP treatment on the astrocyte and microglial response to injury. TBI resulted in significant astrogliosis and microgliosis at 10 weeks after TBI, indicating a chronic neuroinflammatory response (Fig. 3D–F). Notably, 4-AP treatment significantly dampened the gliosis response, reducing the CC immunolabeling after injury for both astrocytes (Cohen's d = 2.12; Fig. 3D and E) and microglia (Cohen's d = 1.63; Fig. 3D, F). Thus, early 4-AP treatment shifts the glial populations toward the homeostatic cellular conditions in the CC after TBI.

4-AP treatment reduces axon damage and myelin thinning in the chronic stage

Study 2 results show 4-AP reduced ongoing CC axon and myelin pathology distinguished using EM at 10 weeks after TBI (Fig. 1A–B, Fig. 4). EM imaging of myelinated axon ultrastructure reveals specific subcellular pathology across stages of axon damage [15]. TBI produced characteristic features of intra-axonal pathology, including swollen mitochondria, vesicle and organelle accumulation, and cytoskeletal breakdown to compacted axoplasm (Fig. 4A). TBI significantly increased the prevalence of swollen mitochondria, an indication of axonal bioenergetic dysfunction, while 4-AP attenuated this pathology in injured mice (Cohen's d = 2.96; Fig. 4B). Damaged myelinated axons with more advanced features of intra-axonal structural pathology (i.e., vesicle and organelle accumulations and/or compacted axoplasm) were prominent after TBI and significantly reduced with 4-AP treatment in injured mice (Cohen's d = 1.99; Fig. 4C). TBI also reduced the diameter of the myelinated axon population, while 4-AP treatment normalized the frequency distribution of axon diameters in injured mice (Fig. 4D–E).

Myelin thickness is proportional to the axon diameter to facilitate action potential conduction properties. TBI resulted in significantly thinner myelin sheaths compared to shams in the vehicle groups, while 4-AP restored the myelin thickness in injured mice (Fig. 4F). Analysis of the g-ratio provides a more specific measure of myelin thickness relative to axon diameter. The g-ratio slopes also show abnormally thin myelin in TBI mice while 4-AP treatment resulted in thicker myelin that was appropriate to the axon diameter, as shown by comparison to sham levels (Fig. 4G–J).

This ultrastructural analysis demonstrates 4-AP disease-modifying effects specifically in myelinated axons (Study 2), which complements improved overall white matter integrity measures and serum NfL biomarker estimates of axonal injury with 4-AP treatment (Study 1).

Discussion

The development of disease-modifying therapies for TBI is a long-sought goal, with particular attention focused on treatments to reduce axonal injury [14]. Our preclinical experiments now demonstrate disease-modifying effects of 4-AP, a brain-penetrant FDA-approved drug, on axon damage and white matter degeneration after TBI. These findings may also have broader relevance to other neurodegenerative diseases, such as multiple sclerosis and Alzheimer's disease, that involve early axonal degeneration [33,34].

Interpretation of these preclinical results is informed by prior preclinical findings and related clinical studies that support the experimental design (Fig. 1). Blood levels in the range of 20–100 ng/ml for clinical use were achieved in mice with 4-AP (0.5 mg/kg) dosing that significantly reduced axon damage without inducing seizure behavior, a concern with higher 4-AP dosing [10,11]. Initiating 4-AP one day after TBI is a clinically reasonable time to evaluate patients and decreases risk of new onset of symptomatic seizures, which have the highest incidence in the first 24 h [35]. The 4-AP treatment duration was selected from experiments indicating an early recovery period over several weeks based on the cellular and electrophysiological response in the CC after TBI [15,16,18,36]. Surprisingly, using transgenic NG2CreER;mTmG myelin reporter mice we found new myelin synthesis over the first 4 weeks after TBI was followed by progressive degeneration with myelin loss and CC atrophy by week 8 [16]. Therefore, 4-AP treatment over 4 weeks was selected to enhance signal conduction and promote enduring recovery during this early period when axons may have reversible damage, such as NoR disruption and demyelination. From a clinical perspective, 4 weeks of 4-AP treatment was sufficient to stratify responders among patients in multiple sclerosis trials [37]. Additionally, a clinical protocol of drug treatment initiated one day after TBI administered b.i.d. over 4 weeks is feasible based on an acute TBI adaptive platform trial of other promising therapeutics (NCT05826912). Finally, the experimental endpoint of 10 weeks after TBI was previously validated as sufficient for evaluating CC atrophy using mice with genetic inactivation of the sterile alpha and HEAT/Armadillo motif containing 1 (SARM1) molecular pathway that executes distal axon degeneration. Sarm1−/− mice had significantly reduced CC axon damage and atrophy at 10 weeks post-TBI as compared to Sarm1+/+ mice [17,38]. Remarkably, the current 4-AP studies achieved results of reduced axon damage and attenuated CC atrophy that were comparable with constitutive deletion of Sarm1. Our detailed study design and protocols developed to conduct these studies are available as resources for future investigations of TBI and/or axon damage (https://osf.io/zjbm2/).

Importantly, outcome measures of CC atrophy and serum NfL protein levels used in this mouse study are relevant to clinical post-traumatic neurodegeneration. The CC white matter is a common site of diffuse axonal and/or microvascular traumatic injury [4,39]. Elevated serum NfL protein is an effective biomarker of axonal injury that corresponds with injury severity and white matter volume loss in subacute and chronic TBI [4,30]. In chronic moderate-severe TBI, neurodegeneration is indicated by atrophy of cerebral white matter, including the CC, and corresponds with MRI and serum NfL measures of axonal injury [4]. In the current preclinical study, 4-AP treatment significantly mitigated CC atrophy and lowered serum NfL protein levels (Fig. 2).

4-AP disease-modifying effects on CC white matter atrophy and serum NfL levels are further informed by changes in glial populations (Fig. 3) and ultrastructural analysis of myelinated axons (Fig. 4). Clinically, CC atrophy with ongoing axon damage observed several years after TBI is associated with persistent inflammation [32]. Accordingly, TBI mice had persistent astrogliosis and microgliosis that may contribute to CC atrophy; 4-AP significantly reduced this reactive gliosis in injured mice (Fig. 3). Glial populations may respond to reduced axon damage and/or direct 4-AP binding, which may counter neurodegenerative effects of dysregulated extracellular K+ homeostasis [9,40]. Our acute transcriptomics analysis showed that 4-AP treatment, using the same dosing as in the current studies, altered Kv channel expression and reduced disease-associated glial cell signatures during the first week after TBI [25]. Thus, our result from therapeutic levels of 4-AP treatment is distinct from the robust astrogliosis response after high convulsant 4-AP dosing used in the context of seizure studies [41]. Additionally, glial cell Kv channel expression changes in response to different inflammatory and demyelinating conditions, which may contribute to varied 4-AP effects on inflammatory mediators [9,42,43]. Importantly, 4-AP protective effects were also reported for oligodendrocytes and myelin in autoimmune and inflammatory demyelinating models [44].

Distinct pathological features of myelinated axons were significantly reduced with 4-AP treatment, indicating improved axon health (Fig. 4). TBI-induced mitochondrial swelling is an early sign of impaired axon bioenergetic function that indicates ongoing axon damage at this chronic time point. More advanced damage results in distinct features of axon degradation with impaired axonal transport and cytoskeletal breakdown that precedes axon fragmentation. TBI also resulted in smaller axons, indicating chronic axonopathy, along with abnormally thin myelin, which may occur with oligodendrocyte stress, remyelination, or myelin remodeling [15,[45], [46], [47]]. Increasing myelin internode thickness and elongation along the axon may restore disrupted NoR domains, including localization of juxtaparanode Kv1 channels, which are a putative 4-AP target [10,11,47].

Mapping the molecular pathways of the secondary injury response after TBI provides important insights into the acute effects of 4-AP target engagement. Our unbiased, genome-wide spatial transcriptomics study enabled comprehensive molecular profiling of gene expression in brain regions under the impact site at 7 days after TBI in this same mouse model [11]. In the CC, TBI downregulated gene sets associated with oligodendrocytes and myelination while upregulating pathways for the response to axonal injury, disease-associated glial cell types, cell stress pathways, vascular injury, and immune activation. During this first week after TBI, 4-AP treatment countered these pathological processes and upregulated molecular pathways associated with the distal axon, neuroaxonal activity, glutamate receptor activity, and regulation of synaptic plasticity. These findings are in agreement with 4-AP enhancement of axon signal conduction and synaptic coupling by inhibiting Kv1 voltage-gated potassium channels, which are located in the axon initial segment, juxtaparanode domains, and/or presynaptic terminals [10,11,16,48,49]. Interestingly, 4-AP treatment upregulated gene sets for activity of voltage-gated potassium channels that modulate ion flux and regulate membrane potential, most prominently Kcnq2 that encodes Kv7.2 channels and Kcnk4 for voltage-independent K2P4.1/TRAAK potassium channels. These changes indicate that this clinically relevant low dose 4-AP inhibition of Kv1 channels modulates gene expression of complementary potassium channels with functions that stabilize the resting cell membrane potential to support continued axon and neuron activity. This interpretation is also supported by computational modeling that predicts low 4-AP downmodulation of potassium repolarization currents broadens action potentials, which improves signal propagation and increases synaptic coupling, contrasts with high 4-AP potassium flux downmodulation that is predicted to cause the emergence of seizure-like dynamics [50].

Mechanisms by which axonal injury progresses to axonal degeneration and fragmentation warrant high priority as potential neuroprotective therapeutic targets [51,52]. Inhibition of SARM1 and/or dual leucine zipper kinase (DLK) pathways has received significant attention. SARM1 is a nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) hydrolase activated by metabolic decline or caspase-3 cleavage to execute degeneration of the distal axon [53]. The molecular pathway of the curated gene set for “response to axonal injury” that was upregulated in our transcriptomics study includes Sarm1, which aligns with the reduced axon damage and white matter degeneration in mice with genetic inactivation of Sarm1 in this TBI model [11,17,38]. Compounds that improved NAD+/NADH homeostasis have also been identified from target-agnostic screening for hippocampal neurogenesis in TBI models [54]. DLK is responsive to axonal injury and drives distinct degenerative processes through retrograde signaling to the neuron soma or an axon-intrinsic program of degeneration, leading to interest in developing selectively targeted therapeutics [52]. A further complexity revealed in the context of TBI is that the effective roles of each pathway may depend upon the specific neuron class [55]. Therefore, strategies to modulate these pathways to prevent damaged axons from progressing to degeneration and fragmentation hold promise for future therapeutics for neurodegenerative diseases, with potential complementary modes of action to those of 4-AP treatment.

Certain limitations are important for interpreting these results. The 4-AP dosing in mice achieved clinical target blood levels but was not the oral extended-release formulation prescribed for patients with multiple sclerosis [10]. The non-penetrating TBI model facilitated analysis of drug treatment effects on axon damage and subsequent white matter degeneration but is not intended to fully replicate the injury pathophysiology or heterogeneity of patients who experience TBI. This non-penetrating TBI does not produce overt cortical damage [56], which is advantageous for examining 4-AP effects on axon damage. However, further studies are needed to more specifically evaluate sex differences and seizure susceptibility factors in this and other TBI models to better inform pathophysiology of axon damage and white matter degeneration. The preclinical 4-AP effects on TBI pathology and a relevant clinical biomarker demonstrate potential to fulfill two important criteria for a disease-modifying therapy, yet behavioral improvement could not be evaluated as an indicator of the third criterium, i.e. clinically meaningful change in symptoms for patients who experience TBI. Future studies will require more specific mechanistic approaches to examine the functional effects of this 4-AP treatment on white matter tracts along with the corresponding neuronal cell bodies and synapses in the context of neural circuits.

Collectively, our findings show that early 4-AP treatment initiated during acute TBI produces enduring disease-modifying effects identified by tissue pathology and serum biomarker measures of chronic axonal injury and white matter degeneration. These preclinical results provide key evidence supporting translation of 4-AP treatment to acute TBI as a new clinical indication and, more broadly, inform the development of therapeutics for axon damage and white matter degeneration in CNS indications.
