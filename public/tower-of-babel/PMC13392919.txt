Frontotemporal dementia in Hispanic populations: Regional and national comparisons
Authors: Shannon B. Lavigne, Gabrielle Hromas, Ashley LaRoche, Alicia S. Parker, Muhammad S. Abdullah, Roman A. Fernandez, Chen‐Pin Wang, Gabriel de Erausquin, Arash Salardini, Jeremy Tanner, Tiffany F. Kautz, Julia Castro, Crystal Mendoza, Sudha Seshadri, A. Campbell Sullivan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONAlthough Hispanic/Latine populations experience higher dementia rates, they remain underrepresented in frontotemporal dementia (FTD) research. This study examines clinical manifestations of FTD within a South Texas Hispanic/Latine cohort compared to national data.METHODSParticipants diagnosed with FTD were included from (1) our FTD clinic, the South Texas Alzheimer's Disease Research Center, and local Advancing Research and Treatment in Frontotemporal Lobar Degeneration–Longitudinal Evaluation of Familial Frontotemporal Dementia Subjects Longitudinal Frontotemporal Lobar Degeneration cohort (Hispanic: n = 17; White non‐Hispanic: n = 22), and (2) the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center dataset (Hispanic: n = 24; White non‐Hispanic: n = 407; National Institute on Aging/National Institutes of Health Grant U24 AG072122). Clinical, neuroimaging, and neuropsychological data were harmonized for cross‐cohort comparisons.RESULTSHispanic participants presented with more movement‐related symptoms and advanced cognitive impairment at diagnosis than non‐Hispanic Whites. Diagnostic delays averaged 4 years from symptom onset. Educational disparities significantly contributed to health‐care access differences.DISCUSSIONFindings underscore diagnostic severity and systemic barriers faced by Hispanic populations with FTD, emphasizing the need for culturally sensitive diagnostic tools and interventions for equitable dementia care.

BACKGROUND

Frontotemporal dementia (FTD) is a constellation of neurodegenerative disorders affecting personality/behavior, speech/language, and movement. Onset typically occurs in midlife, and FTD is the leading cause of dementia for those under the age of 65. The behavioral variant (bvFTD) accounts for nearly 50% of cases and is characterized by coarsening behavior, compromised judgment, and executive dysfunction. 1 Two language variants have been identified: non‐fluent/agrammatic variant primary progressive aphasia (nfvPPA) presents with belabored speech production and agrammatism or apraxia of speech; while semantic variant primary progressive aphasia (svPPA) is characterized by fluent, empty speech and notable loss of semantic relationships. 2 In addition to the core behavioral and language presentations, motor phenotypes such as progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP) and corticobasal degeneration (CBD) are increasingly recognized within the FTD spectrum, with overlapping clinical and pathological features. 3 , 4 There is considerable heterogeneity within and between clinical FTD syndromes.

The Hispanic/Latine population is one of the fastest growing demographics in the United States and rates of dementia are highest among Hispanic/Latine and other minority populations. 5 , 6 Community studies have found that Hispanic/Latine individuals are more likely to be diagnosed with a dementia syndrome compared to non‐Hispanic White individuals 7 and are diagnosed later in the disease course 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 compared to non‐Hispanic White individuals. While there are active efforts to improve diversity inclusion internationally, Hispanic/Latine individuals with FTD remain under‐represented in research, especially in the United States. 8 , 13 , 14 , 15 Analysis of data from the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC) reveals that < 5% of 2478 FTD participants identified as Hispanic/Latine. 16 Similarly, of 6750 patients with FTD who sought treatment in 2022 (identified via analysis of electronic health‐care records), only 4% were Hispanic/Latine. 17

In a review of genetic, neuroimaging, and fluid FTD biomarkers in Latin American/Caribbean populations, only 21 articles were identified, with most research conducted in South America. 18 These findings underscore the urgent need for more inclusive research to explore potential cultural or ethnic variations in the clinical presentation, disease progression, and biomarker profiles, which could inform tailored diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for Hispanic/Latine individuals.

Several barriers may contribute to the underrepresentation of FTD in the Hispanic/Latine population, including cultural differences (e.g., misattribution of symptoms to age, normalizing symptoms, not seeking services until symptoms are advanced), 19 limited education, 20 lack of health insurance, 9 and stigma. 21 There are also numerous systemic barriers such as misattribution of behavioral symptoms by health‐care providers, 12 limited culturally sensitive cognitive assessments, 22 inaccessibility of diagnostic tools, 23 and non‐generalizable diagnostic criteria. 24

This study provides a well‐characterized, deeply phenotyped cohort of Hispanic/Latine patients with clinically diagnosed FTD from our South Texas Alzheimer's Disease Research Center (STAC) and the Advancing Research and Treatment in Frontotemporal Lobar Degeneration–Longitudinal Evaluation of Familial Frontotemporal Dementia Subjects Longitudinal Frontotemporal Lobar Degeneration (ALLFTD) program. This original cohort was expanded to incorporate data from NACC to allow for more robust comparative analyses that assess potential differences between our Texas‐based population and the broader national FTD cohort. Characterizing this underrepresented group enhances our understanding of FTD clinical manifestations and progression within Hispanic/Latine populations, highlighting distinctive barriers to diagnosis and health‐care access that may uniquely impact this community. This study lays essential groundwork for future research aimed at optimizing health‐care delivery and support systems for Hispanic/Latine individuals affected by FTD. Through these efforts, we underscore the critical need for culturally sensitive approaches in dementia research and clinical practice, ultimately striving to close gaps in care and improve health outcomes for Hispanic/Latine persons living with FTD.

METHODS

This is a retrospective analysis of individuals with clinically diagnosed FTD, recruited from three sources: (1) our dedicated FTD clinic, (2) the STAC Clinical Core, and (3) our affiliated ALLFTD study site. We conducted a thorough review of clinical records to enhance consistency and comparability across groups, collectively named local STAC/ALLFTD cohort. Local data were compared to participants in the NACC database (excluding STAC participants to avoid redundancy) with visits between September 2005 and June 2024. This broader cohort enabled comparative analyses of clinical characteristics, disease presentation, and outcomes by ethnicity at local and national levels.

Inclusion criteria consisted of: (1) Self‐identified White race and either Hispanic/Latine or non‐Hispanic ethnicity. (2) A confirmed clinical diagnosis within the FTD spectrum, including bvFTD, prodromal bvFTD as defined by mild behavioral impairment (MBI), primary progressive aphasia (svPPA, nfvPPA), PSP, and CBD. Select logopenic variants (lvPPA) were included if Alzheimer's disease biomarker status was negative. (3) Completed neuroimaging, either magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and/or fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG‐PET). Diagnoses were established through consensus between behavioral neurology and neuropsychology teams using standardized criteria: Rascovsky et al. for bvFTD, 1 Barker et al. for MBI, 25 Gorno‐Tempini et al. for PPA variants, 2 Höglinger et al. for PSP, 3 and Armstrong et al. for CBD. 4 Clinical symptoms were determined to be present or absent based on clinical history and neurologic examination, while cognitive deficits were classified as impaired or within normal limits based on neuropsychological testing. This approach was used to maintain consistency between local and NACC data (see Table 1).

All participants underwent a comprehensive clinical evaluation, including detailed interviews with both the individual and a knowledgeable care partner, to capture the full spectrum of cognitive, behavioral, and functional symptoms. Standardized neurological and neuropsychological assessments were used to classify clinical FTD variants. Biomarker data (e.g., cerebrospinal fluid [CSF] or amyloid PET) were not consistently available for participants evaluated solely through clinical care, which limited confirmation of underlying pathology in some cases. This diagnostic framework ensured consistency in subtype classification despite variability in the availability of biomarker data.

MRI scans were reviewed by a behavioral neurologist and coded for the presence or absence of atrophy in key regions, including the bilateral frontal, temporal, and medial temporal lobes, as well as the parietal lobes and basal ganglia. For participants who underwent FDG‐PET, a parallel coding system was used to document patterns of hypometabolism in the same regions. Genetic testing results were included in the analysis when available.

Neuropsychological testing was conducted in either English (n = 464) or Spanish (n = 6), based on each participant's self‐identified dominant language. All individuals underwent a comprehensive cognitive evaluation assessing key domains, including memory, processing speed, attention, working memory, executive functioning, visuospatial abilities, and language. Most participants completed the Uniform Data Set (UDS) neuropsychological battery; while a subset from clinic‐only received comparable clinical assessments (see Supplement 1 in supporting information for a complete list of measures). The most current normative references were used to evaluate the severity of cognitive symptoms. For Hispanic/Latine individuals assessed with the UDS battery, norms from Marquine et al., which adjust for age, sex, education, and language of administration, were applied to enhance diagnostic accuracy. 26

We synthesized demographic and clinical information for the local STAC/ALLFTD cohort, including sex, years of education, age at symptom onset, age at first evaluation, and family history of neurodegenerative disease. Detailed medical and family histories were obtained through structured research protocols or as part of routine clinical care, allowing for a comprehensive view of each participant's background. Behavioral symptoms were documented based on clinical interviews and systematic review of medical records. To ensure reliable comparisons, data from our local cohort and NACC database were cleaned and standardized across key variables. This process included crosswalking cognitive screening measures (i.e., Mini‐Mental State Examination [MMSE] and Montreal Cognitive Assessment [MoCA]) based on the specific iteration of the UDS used in each case to allow for comparison. 27

Statistical analyses were conducted on samples stratified by Hispanic/Latine and non‐Hispanic White ethnicity and/or study (local STAC/ALLFTD vs. NACC). The distributions of participant characteristics were summarized by mean and standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and frequency (%) for categorical variables. Differences between comparison groups were determined using t tests for continuous variables with a bell‐shaped distribution or Wilcoxon rank sum test for continuous variables with a skewed distribution, and chi‐squared tests for categorical variables.

Qualitative analyses were conducted using local STAC/ALLFTD clinical files to identify themes related to barriers to care. Two reviewers independently reviewed case materials using an inductive thematic analysis approach. Reviewers abstracted information related to diagnostic trajectories, referral patterns, and documented social, cultural, and linguistic factors, and iteratively grouped findings into higher order themes reflecting individual‐ and system‐level barriers to care. Discrepancies in theme interpretation were resolved through consensus.

Characterization of local STAC/ALLFTD Hispanic/Latine cohort

The local STAC/ALLFTD cohort consisted of 17 Hispanic/Latine individuals with FTD (mean age: 65.0; 41% female). Within this cohort, 7 individuals (41%) were diagnosed with bvFTD/MBI, another 7 (41%) had a variant of PPA, and 3 (18%) had an FTD‐related movement disorder (PSP: 12%, CBD: 6%). On average, patients sought clinical evaluation 4.0 years after symptom onset, with delays in diagnosis and treatment ranging from 1 to 10 years. The initial symptoms were predominantly cognitive (70%), with behavioral (18%) and motor (12%) symptoms less commonly described. A detailed account of each case, including clinical presentation, is available in Supplement 2 in supporting information.

Comparative analysis of local STAC/ALLFTD cohort: Hispanic/Latine versus non‐Hispanic White individuals

The local STAC/ALLFTD Hispanic/Latine FTD cohort (n = 17) was compared to a demographically similar group of non‐Hispanic White individuals with FTD from the same site (n = 22). The groups differed in education attainment, with Hispanic/Latine participants reporting fewer years of education (p < 0.05; see Table 3). No significant group differences were observed in imaging findings, clinical symptom profiles, or neuropsychological performance. Although not statistically significant, Hispanic/Latine individuals experienced a longer diagnostic delay (4 years vs. 3 years for non‐Hispanic White individuals) and presented with more advanced cognitive impairment, as reflected by lower MMSE scores (MMSE = 21 vs. 25, respectively). Qualitative diagnostic barriers and facilitating factors based on clinical case review

Upon review of the local STAC/ALLFTD Hispanic/Latine cohort's clinical histories, several reoccurring barriers to timely diagnosis were identified. These included late presentation to specialty care, often after several years of progressive symptoms; initial misattribution of symptoms to psychiatric conditions; and under‐recognition of neurodegenerative syndromes despite functional decline. Patients were frequently referred to neurology only after significant impairments in communication, behavior, or cognition had developed. These patterns underscore systemic challenges in identifying and diagnosing neurodegenerative disease, including limited access to appropriate specialists, potential biases in clinical interpretation and diagnosis, and cultural and linguistic factors influencing help‐seeking behaviors.

DISCUSSION

The local STAC/ALLFTD Hispanic/Latine FTD cohort demonstrated a pattern of delayed diagnosis, with reported symptom onset to diagnosis ranging from 1 to 10 years. Although this delay was not statistically significant compared to non‐Hispanic White individuals, it is clinically meaningful and may reflect longstanding barriers to early detection and access to specialized care. On average, Hispanic/Latine participants also presented with more advanced cognitive impairment, as reflected by lower MMSE scores at initial evaluation. These findings are consistent with broader national trends showing that Hispanic/Latine individuals often receive diagnoses later in the disease course. 7 , 8 Interestingly, cognitive changes were the most common initial symptom (54%) among Hispanic/Latine participants, followed by behavioral (27%) or motor (20%) symptoms. This contrasts somewhat with broader literature, suggesting bvFTD is most prevalent and typically presents with behavioral symptoms. These findings raise important questions about potential cultural differences in symptom recognition and reporting.

While we used culturally and linguistically appropriate normative data (Marquine et al.) to reduce bias in neuropsychological interpretation, observed differences in cognitive scores may nonetheless reflect true disparities shaped by factors such as health‐care literacy, socioeconomic status, and cultural perceptions of dementia. 26 These considerations are not mutually exclusive; instead, they underscore a complex interplay of clinical, social, and structural factors contributing to delayed diagnosis and more advanced presentation in this underserved population.

Hispanic/Latine individuals presented with a high frequency of movement‐related symptoms, including dystonia (p < 0.05), apraxia (p < 0.01), and postural instability (p < 0.01), whereas non‐Hispanic White individuals more commonly exhibited behavioral changes (e.g., loss of empathy, p < 0.05). Although neuropsychological scores did not differ significantly between groups, the prominence of movement symptoms among Hispanic/Latine participants may reflect differences in disease stage, subtype distribution, or cultural and health‐care access factors influencing symptom recognition. It is important to consider that greater frequency of movement symptoms among Hispanic individuals could represent true phenotypic variation, differences in subtype distribution, or a stage of disease at presentation. While stratified descriptive analyses (see Supplements 6–8 in supporting information) provided preliminary insight into symptom patterns within diagnostic subtypes, the limited sample size restricts definitive conclusions.

Implications and limitations

Although neuroimaging and cognitive data were broadly comparable between Hispanic/Latine and non‐Hispanic White individuals with FTD, disparities in education, access to care, and symptom recognition likely influenced the Hispanic/Latine cohort's clinical presentation. Structural barriers including language differences, cultural perceptions of aging and mental health, and systemic inequities may have contributed to diagnosis delay and more advanced disease at the time of clinical evaluation. While detailed data on bilingualism, immigration status, or region of origin were not available for all participants, these sociocultural variables may influence disease presentation and diagnosis. For example, bilingualism may complicate the detection of PPA symptoms, while immigration‐related stress or limited familiarity with the US health‐care system could contribute to delayed care‐seeking and comfort with engaging in research. Region of origin may also influence perceptions of dementia and expectations around aging, which could affect symptom recognition and reporting. These challenges complicate disease management and may limit opportunities for early intervention.

While the Hispanic/Latine cohort included only 41 individuals at the time of the study, this sample represents well‐characterized groups of Hispanic/Latine individuals with FTD reported in the United States, a population that remains substantially underrepresented in neurodegenerative research. 8 , 14 Nonetheless, the small sample size limited our statistical power and ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding group differences. Additional constraints included variability in data collection across cohorts, reliance on binary symptom classifications, and missing or non‐harmonized clinical information, which are common challenges in retrospective, multi‐site studies.

Another limitation is the lack of uniform biomarker and genetic data across participants. Variability in plasma biomarker cutoff thresholds is particularly salient given the underrepresentation of diverse populations in neurodegenerative research. To address this, we elected to retain all individuals who met clinical diagnostic criteria for an FTD syndrome, regardless of biomarker availability or interpretation. While AD pathology was explicitly ruled out in cases with a clinical diagnosis of lvPPA, comparable confirmation was not feasible for remaining FTD subtypes due to the limited availability of CSF or PET imaging. Clinical diagnoses were established through consensus following established diagnostic criteria and research protocols. The absence of widespread biomarker data limits our ability to definitively exclude known phenotypic overlap, such as atypical Alzheimer's disease (e.g., dysexecutive or behavioral variants). Only 31.1% of participants underwent genetic testing for known genetic markers (see Supplement 9 in supporting information) and 0.02% for fluid biomarkers (n = 11). Low genetic testing rates could be multifactorial including limited access to genetic counseling services, participant or family refusal, and financial barriers.

Although 96.5% of participants underwent brain MRI, only 25.5% underwent FDG‐PET imaging. FDG‐PET might have offered additional insights into functional brain changes and patterns of hypometabolism associated with specific FTD subtypes. The limited availability of FDG‐PET may have constrained our ability to distinguish between syndromes, particularly in the earlier disease stages or when MRI findings were subtle or non‐specific. As a result, subtype classifications may have relied more heavily on presenting clinical features. Additionally, because our team could not directly review neuroimaging from NACC participants, there may be some variability in image interpretation across sites. These data limitations reflect common challenges in retrospective, multi‐cohort studies.

Future directions

These findings underscore the need for future studies involving larger, well‐characterized cohorts to more accurately capture symptom presentation, disease subtype distribution, and diagnostic timing across diverse ethnic groups. Longitudinal follow‐up will be critical to track disease progression, while biomarker validation will help determine whether observed differences reflect true phenotypic variation or are shaped by sociocultural and health care–related factors.

Advancements in biomarker research hold promise for improving early diagnosis and disease monitoring. The use of advanced neuroimaging techniques, such as FDG‐PET and high‐resolution MRI, may offer greater insight into structural and functional brain changes across FTD subtypes. Similarly, fluid biomarkers, including CSF and blood‐based markers, could aid in early detection and indicate disease severity.

Genetic research is another key area for future investigation. Efforts should focus on identifying population‐specific variants that may contribute to hereditary risk for FTD in Hispanic/Latine individuals. Approaches such as whole‐exome and whole‐genome sequencing may reveal novel mutations and risk alleles more common in this group. Additionally, examining gene–environment interactions, such as early life adversity, environmental exposures, and chronic stress, could offer a more comprehensive understanding of FTD etiology. Notably, genetic counseling is now routinely offered to all individuals evaluated in STAC's FTD clinic and through participation in the ALLFTD study. As this infrastructure expands, future cohorts will include more detailed genetic data, enabling clearer insights into variant prevalence, penetrance, and the intersection of environmental risk factors in diverse populations.

Within neuropsychology, critical limitations remain in assessing diverse populations, particularly for bilingual or non‐English speaking individuals. Bilingualism may impact the clinical presentation of FTD, especially in language‐based variants, in which symptoms may be masked or mischaracterized due to language switching or uneven proficiency. Factors such as language dominance, frequency of use, and overall proficiency may shape how symptoms emerge, are recognized by families, and are interpreted during evaluations. Although bilingualism may provide some degree of cognitive reserve in neurodegenerative conditions, further research is needed to understand its role across all variants of FTD. 28 , 29 To more accurately assess the cognitive abilities of Hispanic/Latine individuals early in the disease course, it is essential to develop representative normative data that reflect bilingual and bicultural experiences.

Furthermore, culturally tailored interventions and support programs are vital to meet the unique needs of Hispanic/Latine patients and their families. Such programs should consider cultural, linguistic, and socioeconomic factors that influence care‐seeking behavior, service use, and willingness or ability to participate in research. Creating accessible educational materials and resources in Spanish and offering them in community‐based settings is essential. Health‐care provider training should also incorporate cultural humility and awareness of acculturation‐related stress, which may influence both the timing and context of clinical engagement with patients. 8

Last, examining the impact of social determinants of health (SDOH) on the onset and progression of FTD in Hispanic/Latine populations is crucial. Beyond traditional variables such as health‐care access and income, future studies should incorporate nuanced sociocultural variables such as acculturation, immigration history, and intergenerational caregiver burden. The updated Uniform Data Set (UDS4), with its inclusion of SDOH questionnaires, will significantly aid these efforts by providing a more comprehensive view of the environmental and social factors that influence FTD risk and progression. These enhanced datasets will allow researchers to identify patterns and disparities more accurately, leading to more equitable health‐care outcomes and improved management of FTD within Hispanic/Latine communities. By focusing on these areas, future research can deepen our understanding of FTD in Hispanic/Latine populations and enhance the care and support available to these patients and their families.
