Lumbo-Costovertebral Syndrome with Congenital Inferior Lumbar Hernia in a Female Infant
Authors: Ahmed Barakat, Moayad A Rjoub, Alyaman Mohammad, Iyad Rjoub, Obadah Obeidat, Ruba Khasawneh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHerein, we report a rare case of a 1 year old female who was diagnosed with congenital lumbar hernia along with vertebral, ribs, and abdominal wall anomalies suggestive of lumbo-costovertebral syndrome. It was also associated with musculoskeletal, genital, ocular, and cardiovascular defects. The patient presented with a palpable swelling in the lumbar region, which was initially suspected to be a simple hernia. Other apparent ocular and musculoskeletal findings revealed complex systemic anomalies that necessitated further investigation. Imaging studies, including ultrasonography and computed tomography, confirmed the presence of a left lumbar hernia and highlighted the associated abnormalities. The management strategy involved surgical repair of the hernia with the placement of a biological mesh over the defect. This case highlights the importance of considering multisystem involvement in children with congenital lumbar hernias and the need for a multidisciplinary approach to improve the diagnosis and outcomes.Keywords: lumbar triangle, biological mesh, congenital hernia, hernia repair

Introduction

Hernia is defined as a protrusion of a structure through a weakness in the wall of the containing cavity. Congenital lumbar hernia is a rare type of hernia in which a structural defect of the posterior abdominal wall leads to herniation of abdominal viscera into the lumbar region.1 The first one who described it was Barbette et al (1672).2

Lumbocostovertebral syndrome (LCVS) was first described by Touloukian in 1972,3 who believed that the condition arises from a somatic gene mutation occurring during early fetal development, leading to malformations affecting the vertebral bodies, ribs, and musculature of the abdominal wall.3,4

From an anatomical standpoint, lumbar hernias are categorized into three distinct types: superior, inferior, and lumbar incisional. The superior variant involves herniation through the superior lumbar triangle — also known as the Grynfeltt-Lesshaft triangle — whose boundaries are defined by the 12th rib superiorly, the erector spinae muscle medially, and the internal oblique muscle anterolaterally. In contrast, the inferior variant occurs through the inferior lumbar triangle, or Petit’s triangle, which is delineated by the iliac crest inferiorly, the latissimus dorsi muscle posteriorly, and the external oblique muscle anteriorly. In the present case, the hernia defect was identified at the inferior lumbar triangle, which constitutes a comparatively less frequent site of lumbar herniation.

Case Description

A 1-year-old female presented to the clinic with a left lateral lumbar bulge noted by her parents, which had been progressively observed since early infancy. (Figure 1) The patient had a history of congenital cardiac anomalies, diagnosed at 6 months of age, including a ventricular septal defect (VSD) and patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), requiring a 14-day admission for oxygen therapy. Her most recent echocardiogram demonstrated resolution of the VSD with a residual small PDA, and she had remained asymptomatic since discharge. Additional congenital anomalies included left-foot polydactyly, scoliosis and strabismus. The mother reported occasional difficulty with unsupported sitting. Developmentally, the patient was otherwise achieving age-appropriate milestones, was fully vaccinated, and had normal birth weight, height, and head circumference.

The congenital lumbar hernia presenting as a bulge in abdominal wall.

Radiological workup was performed in a systematic stepwise manner, beginning with plain radiographs and advancing to cross-sectional imaging. Chest X-ray revealed multiple bilateral rib anomalies, including Absence of the left 10th to 12th ribs, hypoplasia of the left 8th and 9th ribs, lateral fusion of the left 4th and 5th ribs, and right 7th and 8th ribs, widely spaced right 9th and 10th ribs with lateral flaring of the right 10th and 11th ribs.

Spine X-ray demonstrated congenital thoracolumbar scoliosis convex to the right, associated with multiple vertebral segmentation anomalies, including hemivertebrae at T10, T11, L1, and L3 levels, a bifid T12 vertebra, and multiple vertebral fusion anomalies involving various thoracolumbar and sacral levels. Mild developmental dysplasia of the left hip was also noted. These findings were consistent with the diagnosis oflumbocostovertebral syndrome (LCVS) (Figure 2).

CT scan showed left sided inferior lumbar hernia.

Abdominal ultrasound confirmed the presence of a left lateral abdominal wall defect with herniated bowel content, guiding further cross-sectional imaging.

CT Abdomen revealed a large left lateral abdominal wall defect, measuring approximately 5 cm as measured on CT, through which part of the sigmoid colon appeared herniated. Multilevel thoracolumbar spine anomalies were confirmed, including vertebral segmentation anomalies, hemivertebrae, transverse and spinous process fusion, non-fusion of the T12 posterior element, and multiple bilateral deformed ribs. An S-shaped scoliotic curve was noted, with an L4 curve convex to the left and a T12 curve convex to the right. Both kidneys appeared normal (Figure 3).

Laparoscopic view of the lumbar hernia.

Chromosomal microarray analysis did not reveal any major structural genomic anomalies. However, given the constellation of multiple congenital defects, pediatric geneticists raised the possibility of an underlying syndromic etiology warranting further evaluation.

The patient underwent elective surgical repair of the left inferior triangular lumbar hernia. Given the minimally invasive benefits of laparoscopy, including reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospital stay, faster recovery, and the ability to precisely localize the anatomic defect and place mesh deep to the defect where intraabdominal pressure aids fixation, so initial laparoscopic hernioplasty was planned. A small supraumbilical curved incision was made, a camera port was introduced, and the large left lumbar defect was inspected, through which part of the sigmoid colon was confirmed to be herniated (Figure 4).

The decision to convert to open surgery was made intraoperatively due to small working space, respiratory compromise due to pneumoperitineum, the large hernia defect size and difficult anatomical location as it is in posterolateral position with distorted surrounding anatomy.

A curved incision was made over the left lower lumbar region at the hernia defect site. Layer-by-layer dissection was carried down to the hernia defect, and the peritoneum and hernial sac were carefully identified and dissected. Herniated contents — including small bowel, right ovary, and omental fat were fully reduced. The fascia was closed with non-absorbable sutures to restore abdominal wall integrity. A biological mesh was selected, appropriately sized and trimmed, and secured over the defect using absorbable sutures along the defect margins, ensuring tension-free coverage. The inguinal ligament was utilized as an additional anchor. The rectus muscle sheath was sutured over the mesh for reinforcement. Complete defect closure was confirmed, and a subcutaneous drain was placed prior to skin closure. The patient tolerated the surgery well, and there were no postoperative complications. No hernia recurrence was observed in postoperative visits to outpatient clinics.

Discussion

LCVS is a rare condition characterized by a combination of congenital defects involving the lumbar spine and associated vertebral structures. LCVS is the most common congenital anomaly observed in congenital lumbar hernia (CLH).1–5 The first case of CLH was reported by Garangeot in 1731.2 LVS is thought to result from abnormal embryological development caused by somatic gene mutations, leading to defects in the formation of the vertebral column, muscles, and abdominal wall, which causes a range of musculoskeletal anomalies, including absent ribs, hemivertebrae, and spinal malformations, which together define the lumbocostovertebral syndrome.5–7

Additionally, myelomeningocele and intrathoracic neuroblastomas have been reported in some cases, and urogenital anomalies, such as pelviureteric junction obstruction and cloacal exstrophy, have also been observed along with concomitant diaphragmatic hernias.8,9 Consequently, a patient diagnosed with CLH requires thorough evaluation to identify and diagnose other potentially associated congenital anomalies.

Congenital lumbar hernia, even in isolation, is an exceedingly rare condition. A review of the literature identified only 42 cases of lumbar hernias in infants and children, of which only 16 were confirmed to be congenital in origin. Of these 16 congenital cases, 12 were associated with lumbocostovertebral syndrome, 4 were associated with myelodysplastic disorders.10

Understanding the embryological basis of CLH and LCVS is fundamental to appreciating the wide spectrum of associated anomalies observed in affected infants. The posterior abdominal wall is formed during embryogenesis through the coordinated development and migration of the lateral plate mesoderm and paraxial mesoderm, which give rise to the muscles, aponeuroses, ribs, and vertebrae. A disruption in this process results in failure of proper formation of the lumbar muscles and aponeuroses, herniation through the resulting posterior wall defect and concurrent malformation of adjacent vertebral bodies, ribs, and trunk musculature.11

The current literature supports early surgical repair of congenital lumbar hernia in infants, with the optimal timing being before 12 months of age, provided that other life-threatening congenital anomalies have been addressed or stabilized. The rationale for this approach encompasses prevention of hernia enlargement as intra-abdominal pressure increases with somatic growth, reduction of the risk of incarceration and strangulation, and preservation of musculofascial tissue integrity while the patient is young.12,13

A systematic review by Tasis et al (2022)13 confirmed that the majority of patients (83.3%) underwent surgical treatment, while conservative management with surveillance was reserved for only 16.7% of cases, specifically those with significant comorbidities or deemed unfit for general anesthesia.13

Open repair remains the predominant surgical approach, utilized in 93.3% of reported cases, and involves identification of the hernia defect, reduction of hernial contents, and reconstruction of the posterior abdominal wall. Primary suture repair was achieved in 71.7% of cases; however, prosthetic mesh was required in the remaining 28.3%, particularly when primary closure was not feasible due to defect size. The use of prosthetic material in the pediatric population carries well-recognized risks, including infection, migration, and potential interference with somatic growth, and is therefore reserved for large defects in which tension-free primary closure cannot be accomplished. As highlighted by Vagholkar et al (2013),12 satisfactory closure without prosthetic material may be difficult or impossible when significant muscular hypoplasia is present as part of the LCVS phenotype, and the operating surgeon must therefore be prepared for both repair strategies. Laparoscopic repair accounts for only 6.7% of reported cases, reflecting the considerable technical demands of minimally invasive approaches in small infants.12

Laparoscopic repair of congenital lumbar hernia has been previously described in the literature and is reported to be safe and feasible in the pediatric population. Jones et al (2010)14 reported the first laparoscopic repair of a lumbar hernia in a child with lumbocostovertebral syndrome, demonstrating its technical feasibility.14 Several subsequent pediatric series have further supported the laparoscopic approach as safe, with advantages including better visualization of the posterior abdominal wall defect, reduced wound morbidity, and earlier mobilization.15 However, there are multiple published articles suggesting that open surgery should be considered the preferred approach for large hernias, and that large defect size remains a recognized contraindication to laparoscopic lumbar hernia repair.16

Reported outcomes following surgical repair are generally favorable. Based on systematic review data, postoperative morbidity was 6.7% and the recurrence rate was 3.3% at a mean follow-up of 26.4 months, underscoring the importance of repair being performed in a timely manner at experienced pediatric surgical centers. Given the multisystem nature of LCVS, however, postoperative surveillance must extend well beyond wound assessment. Long-term follow-up should include serial renal ultrasound to monitor for progressive renal anomalies, orthopedic evaluation for vertebral and rib abnormalities with screening for progressive scoliosis, neurodevelopmental assessment when spinal cord anomalies are present, and ongoing clinical and radiological surveillance for hernia recurrence.13

Conclusion

Finally, this 1 year old female patient with a congenital left inferior triangle lumbar hernia presented with several associated congenital anomalies consistent with the LCVS. Surgical repair of the hernia was successfully performed, and the patient was closely monitored for other systemic conditions. Ongoing multidisciplinary care, including cardiology, orthopedics, ophthalmology, and genetic counseling, is essential for the management of this condition.
