A womb with rheum: providers’ knowledge and educational needs in caring for women with rheumatic diseases
Authors: Lauren He, Gabriel A Arenas, Rachel Karani, Maya Faison, Adrianne Dade, Cuoghi Edens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with rheumatic diseases have unique reproductive health needs. Recent guidelines have been established by various soceities on this topic. However, the education received by women’s health providers pertaining to the recognition and management of common rheumatic diseases in patients of childbearing age is not well-defined. This study aims to assess knowledge gaps, specify aspects of care with greatest uncertainty, and assess current resources used by women’s health providers in the management of their patients with rheumatic disease.MethodsAn electronic survey was distributed nationally to physicians and allied health professionals who care for reproductive age women. The survey scored participants on their correct responses and confidence ratings in three sections: (1) patients presenting with a new rheumatic disease, (2) contraception in those with a known rheumatic diagnosis, and (3) preconception/antepartum rheumatology medication management. Participants were also asked about resource utilization. Survey questions and scenarios were based on American College of Obstetrics and Gynecology Committee Opinions and the American College of Rheumatology Guidelines for the Management of Reproductive Health in Rheumatic and Musculoskeletal Diseases. Data were captured using RedCap© and analyzed using descriptive statistics.ResultsThe survey was completed by 200 participants with majority being academic obstetricians and gynecologists. Respondents scored the lowest in recognizing new rheumatic disease presentations in peri-and post-partum periods (28.7%, 95% CI: 25.9–31.6%). Contraception counseling was the area of highest accuracy (57.5%, 95% CI: 54.2–60.8%) and confidence (44.6%, 95% CI: 41.7–47.5%), although areas for contraceptive knowledge expansion exist. Approximately 70% of respondents identified online resources as their main resource used in clinical practice and would prefer more information to be provided as a website, published guidelines, or Smartphone application.ConclusionsWomen’s health providers lack educational opportunities to improve knowledge and confidence in managing reproductive-aged patients with rheumatic disease. Recognizing new disease presentations and antepartum medication management were identified as topics with the largest knowledge gaps. Background

Rheumatic diseases (RDs) preferentially affect women during their childbearing years, often during a time focused on complex decision making regarding their reproductive goals including pregnancy [1, 2]. In order to address their reproductive needs, women with RDs may seek medical guidance and care from their healthcare team which may include their rheumatologist as well as providers in internal medicine (IM), family medicine (FM), or obstetrics and gynecology (OBGYN). However, women with RDs report receiving insufficient and often conflicting information and support from their providers [3, 4]. These experiences highlight the unmet need in addressing family planning strategies, management of RDs in pregnancy, and medication safety pertaining to women with RDs at risk of becoming pregnant. For example, most women with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) on teratogenic medications reported no contraceptive counseling within the last year; when contraceptive counseling rates were compared to women not using teratogenic medications, no difference was found [5]. These findings likely reflect a combination of systemic and individualized factors including a lack of provider exposure and knowledge deficits regarding resources available to providers. Accordingly, consensus guidelines have been developed by the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology, the American College of Rheumatology (ACR), and the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) to help guide providers in these challenging areas of clinical practice [6–8].

Previous studies have shown that providers desire more support and education on the management of reproductive aged women with RDs. In one of the first national questionnaire-based studies among rheumatologists and obstetricians in the United Kingdom, no uniform practice was observed with regard to the use of disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (DMARDs) in pregnancy [9]. In Lebanon, similar results were reported from a national survey about DMARDs use and safety in pregnancy with discrepancy noted between the practices of obstetricians and rheumatologists, revealing rheumatologists are more comfortable using medications such as tumor necrosis factor inhibitors during pregnancy and lactation [10]. While rheumatologists feel responsible for knowledge on this topic, there is ongoing concern about the competing clinical priorities and desire for collaboration with obstetricians [11]. Furthermore, there is a paucity of evidence in the United States about the perspectives and educational assessment of women’s health care providers in managing reproductive needs, knowledge on medication compatibility with pregnancy and breastfeeding, and disease recognition in women with RDs.

The primary objective of this study was to assess knowledge gaps and confidence in (1) recognizing new presentations of RDs in pregnancy or the peripartum period, (2) contraception counseling, and (3) RD medication management according to national guidelines across women’s healthcare providers. We hypothesize the presence of knowledge gaps and lack of provider confidence regarding recognition and management of the reproductive health needs of women with RDs in the United States despite guidelines now available from multiple societies. A secondary objective of this study included identification of resources providers use in clinical practice.

Materials and methods

Study population

This study was a national examination of the confidence level and educational deficits of providers caring for women of reproductive age with RDs through an anonymous electronic questionnaire based survey. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Chicago (IRB21-0265). The survey was administered from April 2021 to January 2022. Eligible providers included OBGYN physicians (generalists and subspecialists), general internal medicine physicians, family medicine physicians, and women’s health advanced practice providers (APPs) including nurse practitioners (NPs), physician assistants (PAs), and midwives in the United States [2–4]. All currently practicing health care providers who directly care for reproductive aged women were included as part of this study if they completed the survey. Rheumatologists were excluded given the focus of previous survey-based studies. Medical students and the authors of this study were excluded from study participation. The survey was designed at the University of Chicago in Chicago, IL and distributed to providers through list servs, direct email invitations, social media advertisement, and medical society website postings.

Survey structure

Our tripartite survey included demographic data collection, knowledge and confidence assessment, and evaluation of resources (supplementary material). The survey scored participants on their correct responses and confidence ratings in three sections: (1) patients presenting with a new RD, (2) contraception counseling in those with RDs, and (3) preconception/antepartum rheumatic medication management. Given no validated survey assessment exists, these sections were selected and developed based on the above topics included in ACOG and ACR guidelines [7, 8]. Inconsistent and firmly aligned recommendations between ACOG and ACR formed the majority of our questions used in our tripartite survey. Furthermore, we supplemented the survey with questions from commonly addressed or missed topics from our clinical experience to capture real-world circumstances. At the end of each survey section, we asked participants to rate their confidence level as a surrogate for content familiarity. This confidence assessment was intentional to reflect a participant’s true content familiarity given a limited number of questions per section. Once an initial draft of the survey was completed, the survey was submitted for review to a general obstetrician and gynecologist in addition to a complex family planning specialist. Based on their feedback, the survey questions and answers were modified accordingly. This brief survey review served as our initial pilot study to evaluate for any major or minor flaws. All authors reviewed the modified survey before finalizing and distributing the survey as part of our study.

Participants were also asked about prior training and resource utilization in each of these contexts at the end of the survey. Many of the survey questions had multiple correct responses; for new disease presentations, the question was correct only if the correct rheumatologic response was included amongst the chosen answers. For questions with multiple correct rheumatologic answers, participants were given 1 point for each correct answer (supplementary material). Participants were asked confidence level ratings for each section: new disease presentation, contraception, and medication management on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very unconfident, 5 = very confident). All participants who completed the survey were emailed the survey answer key with supporting references and additional resources on management of rheumatic disease during pregnancy from respective medical organizations (supplementary material).

Statistical analysis

Survey questions were marked as percent correct. The mean, standard deviation, and interquartile range were calculated for study survey responses that utilized a 5-point Likert scale. IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) Software was used to calculate differences among groups between general specialties and OBGYN subspecialties. These differences were evaluated using a one-way ANOVA: the independent variable was provider title, postgraduate year, specialty or subspecialty, while the dependent variable was percent correct [12]. Data were captured using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) electronic data capture tool [13, 14]. REDCap is a secure, web-based software platform designed to support data capture for research studies.

Results

Two hundred participants completed the survey with a survey response rate that could not be calculated due to the unknown number of providers who received an invitation or willingly participated in completing our survey. The majority of participants were physicians (177, 88.5%): 67 (33%) residents, 7 (3.5%) fellows, and 103 (51%) attendings (Fig. 1). APPs represented a smaller percentage of participants: 15 (7.5%) midwives and 8 (4%) NPs or PAs. Physician specialty breakdown was the following: OBGYN (146, 73%), Internal Medicine or Medicine-Pediatrics (35, 17.5%), and Family Medicine (19, 9%). Most participants worked at a university (124, 62%) or teaching (29, 14%) setting, while a minority of study participants worked in a community setting (28, 14%) or private (10, 5%) practice. Geographically, 14 (7%) of participants practiced in a rural setting, 56 (28%) in a suburban setting, and 130 (65%) in an urban setting. The majority identified having no prior experience pertaining to the topic of reproductive health in RDs (120, 60%).

Survey participant demographics. A total of two hundred participants completed the anonymous electronic survey with approximately half of all participants representing attending physicians

New disease presentation was the lowest scoring section with an average score of 28.8% ± 19% and average confidence of 2.0 (± 0.90) (5 = most confident) (Fig. 2; Table 1). Medical specialty did not correlate with performance. For example, in identification of postpartum inflammatory arthritis, only 50% of respondents selected rheumatoid arthritis (RA) in their differential diagnosis. Furthermore, lupus nephritis was identified by 60% of study participants in their differential diagnosis for a woman in her third trimester with new proteinuria. A clinical vignette describing a woman in her third trimester with new anemia and thrombocytopenia was underrecognized as a potential new rheumatic diagnosis with under 15% of respondents identifying Sjogren’s, SLE, or antiphospholipid antibody syndrome (APS) as a possible diagnoses.

The average percent correct score in Contraceptive Counseling section was 57.8% ± 22% with an average confidence of 2.2 (± 0.98). Mean percent correct was statistically significant between specialty (Family Medicine: 40.7% ± 30.2%, Internal Medicine: 57.1% ± 19.2%, OBGYN: 60.1% ± 21.3%, p = 0.002). This was the highest scoring section, with most participants recognizing the safety of long-acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs) in patients with RDs and the recommendation to avoid estrogen containing contraceptives in those with positive antiphospholipid antibodies (aPLs). However, in patients with SLE or APS, 45.5% of providers did not recognize that both cooper intrauterine device (IUD) and levonorgestrel pill can be safely used as emergency contraception, with no difference seen across specialties. Additionally, 21.5% of providers incorrectly selected that immunosuppressive medications, such as mycophenolate mofetil, are a contraindication to the insertion of LARCs [7] (Table 1).

The average score in Medication Management section was 54.7% ± 23.8% with average confidence of 1.7 (± 1.19). Mean percent correct was statistically significant between specialty (Family Medicine: 35.1% ± 31%, Internal Medicine: 55.6% ± 18.3%, OBGYN: 57.2% ± 22.9%, p < 0.001). Many providers selected to have a pregnant RA patient discontinue medications actually deemed safe during pregnancy, including etanercept, low-dose prednisone, azathioprine, sulfasalazine, and hydroxychloroquine [7]. Over half of respondents correctly started a pregnant SLE patient on aspirin (OBGYN: 65.1%, Internal Medicine: 40.0%, p = 0.001). However, over 30% incorrectly claimed that a patient with well-controlled SLE would not be a candidate for in vitro fertilization [7]. Only 57% of respondents correctly identified all the breastfeeding compatible medications (etanercept, hydroxychloroquine, sulfasalazine, ibuprofen, and low-dose prednisone) [15, 16].

When evaluating total percent correct across all study sections, no statistically significant difference was seen when the data were analyzed by attending physician years of practice (< 5 years: 45.1%, 6–10 years: 49.9%, 11–15 years: 57.8%, > 15 years: 64.3%; p = 0.12), attending versus resident/fellow (48.3% vs. 47.3%; p = 0.46), OBGYN subspecialty (general OBGYN: 50.5%, maternal-fetal medicine: 53.9%, urogynecology: 11%, gynecologic oncology: 14%, reproductive endocrinology and infertility: 33%, family planning: 50.3%, pediatric and adolescent gynecology: 29.6%, and other: 42.8%; p = 0.36), or post-graduate year (PGY-1: 45.9%, PGY-2: 46.3%, PGY-3: 47.7%, and PGY-4: 46.8%; p = 0.78). Of note, one urogynecologist and one gynecologic oncologist completed the survey.

Most frequently used resources included expert consultation with maternal-fetal medicine specialists or rheumatology (180, 90%), UpToDate® (142, 71%), and ACOG resources (93, 46.5%) (Table 2). This trend was seen among different OBGYN subspecialties, with most preferring to consult maternal-fetal medicine over rheumatology, reiterating the importance of this topic among OBGYN-trained providers. Most desired resources included website (129, 64.5%), published formal guidelines (116, 58%), and smartphone applications (114, 57%).

Discussion

This was the first assessment of women’s health care providers’ confidence and management of patients with RDs in the United States, with low confidence reported across aspects of reproductive care in women with RDs despite recent published formal guidelines. While gaps in knowledge regarding specific aspects of contraception counseling and medication management exist, recognizing new disease presentations of rheumatic disease was the lowest scoring component. There is a need for consolidation and collaboration between ACR, ACOG, and other medical societies to address these areas of need. Significant negative maternal and fetal outcomes are possible if RDs are not appropriately identified or managed for pregnant patients including increased incidence of miscarriage, pre-eclampsia, eclampsia, preterm birth, intrauterine growth restriction, and intrauterine fetal demise [1].

Previous studies have identified low levels of confidence among rheumatology providers in caring for women of reproductive age with RDs, but this is the first assessment of OBGYN, non-rheumatology internal medicine, and family medicine providers in the United States [3, 4]. While a limited number of providers completed the survey (n = 200), this is the highest number of participants to complete an assessment on this topic. Our assessment additionally divided this subject into key components based on existing practice guidelines and clinical prevalence to better highlight areas for specific improvement. Recognizing new disease presentation of rheumatic disease was the lowest scoring subcategory among all providers. While diagnosing RDs falls outside of the scope of OBGYN practice, recognizing symptoms of inflammatory arthritis and early laboratory indicators of RDs is important given the prevalence of RDs in reproductive aged females as well as the overlap in clinical presentation between rheumatic disease and common disorders of pregnancy [16, 17]. For example, thrombocytopenia in a pregnant patient can range from benign gestational thrombocytopenia, preeclampsia with severe features, immune thrombocytopenia, or lupus nephritis. Previous studies have shown that many patients consider their OBGYN as their primary care provider, highlighting the pivotal role that women’s health providers play in recognition of RDs [18, 19].

Most participants scored the highest in areas consistent between ACOG and ACR guidelines. Many recognized the safety of estrogen-containing contraception in females with negative aPLs testing. One notable misconception was that many responders considered LARC use unsafe in those taking immunosuppressive medications despite guidelines determining LARCs safety in all women with RDs [7, 20]. ACOG’s Practice Bulletin on use of hormonal contraception notes that IUDs are safe in women with SLE on immunosuppressive therapies, specifying that there is no additional risk of infection based on current literature [21]. However, this specifies that progestin-only IUDs are USMEC (US Medical Eligibility Criteria for Contraceptive Use) category 3 in SLE patients with positive aPLs, indicating theoretical or proven risks usually outweigh benefits. This is different than ACR’s strong recommendation of levonorgestrel or copper IUD use in women with positive aPLs, which may lead to confusion regarding best practice. Similarly, ACOG states depot-medroxyprogesterone acetate (DMPA) is USMEC category 2 for women at risk of VTE, including those with SLE, while ACR guidelines recommend against use of DMPA due to concern for thrombogenicity [7, 21]. The ACOG Practice Bulletin on emergency contraception does not include recommendations specific for women with RDs. Instead, ACOG reiterates the CDC stance that there are “no conditions in which the risks of emergency contraception use outweigh the benefits” while ACR specifically recommends emergency contraception in all RDs when warranted, again potentially leading to confusion among providers [7, 22].

Promising data continues to emerge on the safety of rheumatic medications in pregnancy and lactation, supporting ACR and ACOG’s similar recommendations. ACR guidelines state that tumor necrosis factor inhibitors including etanercept are considered safe to use in the first and second trimesters; current literature suggests no significant increased risk of adverse pregnancy or fetal outcomes following the use of biologics [7, 23]. Azathioprine, sulfasalazine, hydroxychloroquine, and low-dose prednisone are also all considered safe with low risk for teratogenicity in both ACR and ACOG guidelines [7, 8]. Given consistency between recently published guidelines, lower scores here may highlight challenges in knowledge dissemination and barriers to information access as well as provider discomfort with the recommendations of these guidelines. Both ACOG and ACR guidelines outline the safety profile of most immunomodulating drugs during lactation, reflected in consistent correct responses in this section.

While ACOG and ACR guidelines exist, the majority of providers in this study identified that their desired resources include formalized guidelines. This suggests that these available resources are not being utilized, which is echoed in partcipants indicating low utilization of guidelines as a reference source. A similar number of providers asked for smartphone applications despite the ACR guideline being available on the organization’s smartphone application [24]. Hence, providers may be unaware that these resources have been published—a challenge with a topic that crosses specialities—or do not know how to access them without significant difficulty. This is an important barrier to improving clinician knowledge and confidence.

Providers across specialties may benefit from the creation of a centralized source of information with links to websites and smartphone applications. This platform would streamline the process of accessing information and may amplify awareness if the same resource could be used by interdisciplinary providers. Consolidation of available resources also presents an opportunity for ACR and ACOG to acknowledge areas where discrepancy between current guidelines exist with recommendation for expert consultation when appropriate. Increasing access to clinical reference tools in accessible and user-friendly formats is an essential step to support providers in prompt identification of new RDs and facilitate safe and effective management.

On an institutional level, electronic medical records could be used to further support clinical decision making. Clinical pathways can be designed with decision trees to assist providers with the appropriate diagnostic testing and referrals for peripartum patients presenting with symptoms suggestive of RDs. These pathways could be built to include ordersets and links to guidelines to streamline selection of appropriate testing and referrals, decreasing the burden for the ordering provider and increasing the correct testing being ordered. Moreover, electronic consultation (e-consults) between rheumatology and OBGYN can also be used for timely evaluation and management of patients with RDs. Future studies should aim to include more providers with greater diversity in OBGYN subspecialties, providers outside of academic institutions, and representation of primary care providers to examine if the differences in rheumatologic knowledge or confidence vary similarly with a larger sample size. Using a quality improvement framework to reassess provider knowledge and confidence after implementing a clinical tool may also serve to measure the impact of the interventions mentioned above.

Strengths of the current study include the eligibility of a broad population: this was a national survey that included providers spanning different settings and different levels of training. This survey assessed common areas of clinical care of patients with RDs based on established guidelines, resulting in a more granular assessment of interdisciplinary improvements to be made amongst societies. Lastly, resources were provided to participants who completed the survey to improve access to the topic of management of RDs during child-bearing years. There exist several limitations with our study. First, our study included a non-validated survey to assess provider accuracy and confidence in RDs management. In order to minimize ambiguity in the correct answer, all questions were directly cross referenced between ACR and ACOG guidelines. Additionally, there were only 200 total respondents who completed our national survey with the majority of whom were in the field of OBGYN. Our results likely reflect the opinions and management style of OBGYNs which may highlight areas for improvement in their formative training and continued education in caring for reproductive age women with RDs. Participants were primarly from academic settings which may characterize their access to specialists, educational opportunities beyond training, and exposure to complex patients. The lack of participant diversity highlights that non-academic women’s health care providers are a challenging group to reach for electronically distributed research endeavors.

Conclusions

Despite ACOG and ACR societies having published guidelines on the management of RDs during pregnancy, gaps in knowledge and low confidence levels exist in the management of patients with RDs during reproductive years. While this may be in part due to obstacles in awareness and accessibility of current recommendations, there also remain discrepancies between societies in certain topics, including contraception in patients with SLE and positive aPLs. This study highlights an opportunity to increase awareness of new RDs clinical presentation among OBGYN providers who are often the first to provide care for young females at risk for developing RDs. Overall, this work supports further collaboration amongst ACOG and ACR societies to create more centralized, accessible, and consistent guidelines to improve provider knowledge, develop learning tools to improve provider awareness of RDs and their management, and synergize to determine the best way to disseminate this complex information in an effort to improve the diagnosis, family planning, and pregnancy management of women with RDs.
