1. What were the major goals of the project?

The overarching goal of this project was to elucidate biochemical mechanisms that ubiquitous
microorganisms use to enable primary metabolism based on nitrification, the multi-electron oxidation of
ammonia (NH3) to nitrite (NO2–). This chemistry enables life to substitute carbon based fuel with
nitrogen. Understanding the underlying chemistry was pursued to help inform the use of ammonia as an
alternative fuel source compared to petroleum, capitalizing on the widespread infrastructure for
production and distribution of this substance in the US. This project specifically focused on characterizing
ammonia monooxygenase, an integral membrane copper protein that initiates nitrification via the aerobic
oxidation of NH3 to hydroxylamine (NH2OH).

1.1. Recombinant expression of active AMO

The major goal of the project, and the component that received the entirety of the effort, was to produce a
recombinant expression system that would enable the production and purification of active AMO.
Progress toward elucidating the reactivity of AMO has been hindered by the fact that it has never been
isolated and purified in an active form. AMO is a multi‐subunit integral membrane protein expressed by
organisms with long (30–40 h) doubling times that enter stationary phase of growth low cell densities.
While recombinant overexpression is typically the recourse for such problematic enzymes, AMO has not
been recombinantly expressed successfully in conventional hosts like E. coli and S. cerevisiae. Such a
system would also enable mutagenesis studies that would enable mechanistic elucidation. Below we
describe approaches and outcomes towards producing recombinant expression systems for complete
AMO as well as its individual subunits.

1.2. Elucidation of the AMO mechanism

AMO uses O2 and copper to hydroxylate NH3 to NH2OH. This challenging reaction necessitates the
generation of an oxidant with potency sufficient to cleave the 107 kcal/mol N–H bond of NH3, one of the
strongest bonds found in nature. A similar oxidant is thought to be used by the related copper membrane
monooxygenase (CuMMO) particulate methane monooxygenase (pMMO) to cleave the 104 kcal/mol C–
H bond of methane (CH4). Despite years of work toward their identification, these Cu–O oxidants remain
unknown. Our proposal included hypotheses concerning the mechanism and approaches to obtain kinetics
information as well as to try to trap reactive intermediates.

2. What was accomplished under these goals?

2.1. Recombinant expression of active AMO

We proposed a program that would include both preparation and characterization of AMO as well as
mechanistic studies. We contended that studying this globally significant enzyme would require multi-
milligram quantities of protein. We deemed that the isolation of AMO from native organisms would be
time-prohibitive due to the slow growth rates of AOA/AOB and cost-prohibitive due to the very large
culture volumes that would be required. Moreover, we posited that many key questions concerning
structure-function relationships in AMO would require generation of variants via site-directed
mutagenesis. No systems have been developed for genetic manipulation of AMO in its host organisms.
Even should such systems be developed, the vital role of AMO in primary metabolism precludes the
generation of genetic variants. Consequently, we pursued recombinant approaches towards preparation of
AMO.

Route 2, Abandoned: Cell-Free Protein Expression (CFPE) of bAMO
Recent reports of successful cell-free protein synthesis indicate that membrane proteins can be prepared in
vitro.8–10 We proposed to use this approach to prepare bAMO to study its structure and function.
Importantly, this route would offset pitfalls due to erroneous protein trafficking in recombinant hosts as
well as diminution of yields due to limitations in membrane availability and loading, cellular protein
degradation, and NH2OH cytotoxicity.
 We note that very recently the Rosenzweig laboratory reported CFPE of pMMO and its reconstitution
into lipid bilayer nanodiscs.11 However, the produced protein was not active for CH4 oxidation. It was not
definitively established that the protein achieved the native protein fold or whether it bound Cu. The
evidence for production of pMMO came from 2-dimensional class averages assembled from cryoEM data
that resembled the class averages of natively isolated pMMO. Moreover, protein yields were not reported.
It remains unclear whether this approach is viable for CuMMO production. Nevertheless, we carried out
several attempts toward CFPE expression of bAMO. We were not able to identify target bands in our SDS-
PAGE analysis. Meanwhile, parallel efforts to produce bAMO subunits by recombinant expression in E.
coli were successful. Given the far less labor- and cost-intensive nature of the latter route, we abandoned
these efforts (vide infra).
Route 3, The Potential Route Forward: In Vitro Assembly of bAMO Subunits Produced in E. Coli.
 Prevailing wisdom, at least as communicated to our group by the community of researchers
investigating CuMMOs, held that integral membrane CuMMO subunits (e.g. amoA and amoC) are
cytotoxic to E. coli. On the other hand, the “soluble” B-subunits from AMO and pMMO have been
expressed and subjected to structural and limited reactivity studies. In following up this assertion, we failed
to find any report in the literature concerning cytotoxicity of the A- and C-subunits. To this end, we elected
to eschew the conventional wisdom and produce each of the AMO subunits via the well-characterized E.
coli T7 promoter system with considerations described above to mitigate overtaxing the E. coli Sec
translocon.
 We started by evaluating several expression strains purpose-built for IMP production as hosts to express
the full bAMO gene cluster. While we could detect amoB by SDS-PAGE and mass spectrometry, we could
not establish concomitant expression of amoA and amoC. We found that all subunits could be expressed
using plasmids bearing each subunit as fusion proteins comprising maltose binding protein (MBP) at the
N-termini, a linker that is specific for cleavage by the ULP-1 protease,12 and, finally the AMO subunits at
the C-termini. MBP was chosen to facilitate expression and to improve solubility, as it had been reported
that pmoB could be expressed as such a fusion protein.13 MBP has the added benefit of enabling affinity
purification using amylose resin. We arrived at the ULP-1 protease cleavage site due to the compatibility
of the protease with our employed detergent conditions and our ability to readily produce large quantities
of the enzyme in house. We use the nomenclature 4-MT-X for these constructs: 4 marks the 4th generation
constructs, MT denotes MBP-tag, and X denotes the particular bAMO subunit (e.g. amoA).

 To investigate protein-protein interaction between the fusion peptides, equimolar 4-MT-A, 4-MT-B,
and 4-MT-C were combined overnight at 4 °C at ~1mg/mL. Extensive precipitation resulted––this occurred
only when the constructs were combined, otherwise each of the individual constructs remained soluble.
After ultracentrifugation, the supernatant was applied to a Superdex increase 200 pg SEC column. A single
peak eluted from the column, which contained SDS-PAGE bands consistent with all three members of the
4-MT series (Figure 2a). Since aggregation only occurred after mixing the constructs, we were encouraged
that a distinct protein-protein interaction was occurring, relative to the purified and isolated constructs. To
prevent precipitation, we used lipid bilayer nanodiscs to force the hydrophobic portions of the fusion
peptides (i.e. the AMO subunits) together into a 2-dimensional lipid environment.14 No precipitation
occurred when following this in vitro assembly approach. Furthermore, a unique peak appeared the SEC
chromatogram with an elution volume unique from the individual subunits. SDS-PAGE of fractions
corresponding to this peak contained all four bands (the three 4-MT constructs and the MSP1D1 membrane
scaffold protein) (Figure 2b). This material remained dissolved without the presence of detergent over the
course of several days at 4 °C.
 Immediately after SEC, the nanodisc assembly was frozen onto cyroEM grids. Several cryoEM data
sets were gathered. Micrographs containing particles within vitreous ice within the holes of the grids were
collected (Figure 2c), but we were unable to render meaningful 2-D class averages. Aggregation could be
seen in the micrographs and could not be avoided by ultracentrifugation for 30 min prior to freezing grids.
More structural information about the nanodisc assembly was sought via SAXS. Guinier analysis was
definitive for aggregation (Figure 2d). The data nonlinear at low q, and “smiling” of the normalized
residuals characteristic for aggregation was observed. This aggregation persisted despite changes in
assembly concentration, buffer (pH and salt concentration), and centrifugation procedures.

Three graduate students (two men and one woman) carried out research with support from this grant.
These junior scientists underwent substantial training in molecular biology, cell culture, protein
purification and characterization, electron paramagnetic resonance spectroscopy, and cryo-electron
microscopy. Brief descriptions of their activities are described below:

via poster at numerous Gordon Research Conferences. He co-authored a review article on the topic of
