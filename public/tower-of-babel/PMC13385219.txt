Application of Population‐Based Meta‐Heuristic Algorithms for Robust Initialization in Mechanistic Pharmacometric Modeling
Authors: Dongjin Lee, Chih‐Wei Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMechanistic PK/PD models represent complex biological systems in which model parameters are in nonlinear, multidimensional parameter spaces and often require robust optimization informed by experimental data, making parameter estimation a critical yet challenging aspect of pharmacometric analysis. Moreover, optimization methods for nonlinear‐mixed effects PK/PD modeling are highly sensitive to initial parameter values, and poor initial estimates frequently result in convergence failure or suboptimal fits. To address the challenges, this study introduces a systematic framework that integrates population‐based meta‐heuristic algorithms to identify optimal or near‐optimal initial estimates that facilitate subsequent nonlinear mixed‐effects modeling. Nineteen distinct population‐based meta‐heuristic algorithms, including evolutionary, swarm‐based, and bio‐inspired methods, were evaluated on two mechanistic ODE‐based models: (1) a two‐compartment pharmacokinetic model with linear and Michaelis–Menten elimination and (2) the Friberg myelosuppression model. The temporal evolution of model goodness‐of‐fit over iterations was evaluated for each algorithm, and the effects of population size and iteration number on the algorithm performance were also examined. The results demonstrate population‐based optimization algorithms efficiently and autonomously refine parameter estimates over iterations. Jellyfish search optimizer, symbiotic organisms search, and memetic algorithm showed strong performance compared to others with respect to optimization accuracy and computational efficiency across both models. As expected, increasing population size and iteration number enhanced the optimization performance. Overall, population‐based meta‐heuristic algorithms demonstrate superior capability in navigating nonlinear, high‐dimensional parameter spaces, generating robust initial estimates for subsequent modeling workflows, and enhancing convergence stability, computational efficiency, and parameter identifiability.Keywords: mechanistic modeling, NONMEM, PD, pharmacometrics, PK, population‐based meta‐heuristic algorithms

Introduction

Nonlinear statistical modeling forms the foundation of population pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) analysis and has a key role in dose selection, clinical trial design, and model‐informed drug development, as well as regulatory review and drug approval [1, 2, 3, 4]. PK/PD models may encompass complex processes such as nonlinear elimination or feedback loops to capture underlying biological and physiological mechanisms, but these features substantially complicate parameter estimation. For the parameter optimization in these nonlinear models, good initial values are critical because the associated parameter spaces are often nonconvex, multimodal, and highly sensitive to perturbations. Poor starting values, especially for nonlinear‐mixed effects PK/PD modeling, can cause instability, failure to converge, or implausible estimates.

Given the critical impact of initial value selection on model performance [3, 5, 6, 7], manual initialization is commonly implemented in practice. Manual initialization typically relies on expert judgment, prior research, and exploratory data analysis [6, 8]. Modelers can often set the initial values based on the literature‐reported parameter values. For example, they could use published values for the volume of distribution and clearance for PK model development for modalities in the same drug class or having similar chemical structure or chemical–physical properties [9]. In a more complex setting, Friberg myelosuppression model describes the effect of chemotherapy on hematopoietic cell differentiation across multiple lineages, incorporating self‐renewal, apoptosis, and maturation delays, with indirect feedback from erythropoietin, granulocyte‐colony stimulating factor, and thrombopoietin. The feedback exponents (γ), which describe the relationship between peripheral cell counts and baseline levels, are often difficult to estimate and are therefore commonly fixed to values from lineage‐specific models or literature sources [10]. When using such literature‐based estimates, it is important to consider differences in model structure, study populations, and disease states that may affect PK/PD behavior.

A data‐driven approach can also be implemented to select initial estimates since final parameter estimates ultimately depend on the characteristics of the dataset and model structure [3, 6, 8]. Thus, careful preliminary exploratory analysis of observed data can offer valuable insights. Even simple visualization techniques such as plotting time versus log concentration for population mean/median data can reveal important structural and kinetic features [8]. Nonetheless, the traditional methods for determining initial estimates may not be effective for complex or novel mechanistic models [8]. Modelers can continue to use poor initial estimates, which can lead to convergence failures or convergence to a local minimum.

Population‐based meta‐heuristic methods offer an alternate way to find optimal or near‐optimal initial values [11, 12]. These methods have been used for automated population PK model development and covariate search [3, 7, 13] and can also explore the parameter space in a stochastic manner with a population of candidate solutions [14]. Nevertheless, they have not been used broadly for parameter estimation for mechanistic pharmacometric models. They work without gradient information, making them ideal for complex optimization tasks while balancing broad search and focused search in the parameter space [11, 14]. As the search proceeds, the candidate population evolves and becomes more likely to reach optimal or near‐optimal values. Their robustness and flexibility make them suitable for complex tasks in many fields such as PK/PD model fit tasks [14].

While PK/PD modeling platforms such as Monolix, Phoenix NLME, Stan, and the R‐based package nlmixr2 are powerful, NONMEM remains the most widely used and well‐established tool in pharmacometrics [15, 16, 17]. This study proposes an automated framework that generates high‐quality initial estimates for population mean parameters, THETAs, for nonlinear PK/PD models. By evaluating nineteen distinct meta‐heuristic algorithms on the two representative ordinary differential equation (ODE)‐based PK/PD models, (1) the two‐compartment PK model with linear and Michaelis–Menten elimination (hereafter referred to as the 2CMT PK model with MM elimination) [18] and (2) the Friberg myelosuppression model [19], this study demonstrates how these algorithms can reliably find optimal or near‐optimal initial estimates, overcoming the limitations of the traditional methods.

Data Generation

Synthetic datasets were created in NONMEM with previously reported parameter values as true values. The 2CMT PK model with MM elimination used values from [18] and the Friberg myelosuppression model used values from [19]. Each model produced concentration–time profiles for nine subjects at three dose levels. The same ODE models were used for data generation and model fitting to ensure consistency.

Principles of Population‐Based Meta‐Heuristic Algorithms

Population‐based meta‐heuristic algorithms are global optimization methods that search the parameter space using a population of candidate solutions that evolve over successive iterations, rather than relying on gradient information [14]. At a high level, these algorithms follow a common process of initialization, fitness evaluation, population updating, and termination (Figure S2), making them ideal for complex and nonlinear optimization problems where derivatives or convexity assumptions are not required [14, 20].

In the simulation study, nineteen different population‐based meta‐heuristic algorithms were applied and evaluated using two PK/PD models. All evaluated algorithms and their abbreviations are organized by conceptual origin (Table S1). For all population‐based optimization algorithms, only lower and upper parameter bounds were required. The parameter bounds for both problems are listed in Table S2.

Application of Population‐Based Meta‐Heuristic Algorithms to PK/PD Model Fitting

The optimization algorithms for PK/PD problems include 5 key steps (Figure S3). Firstly, the optimization process began with generating an initial population of candidate solutions, each representing a unique combination of PK/PD parameter values, the population fixed‐effect parameters, sampled within predefined bounds by users. Secondly, at each iteration, the performance of every candidate solution was assessed using an objective function defined as negative twice the log‐likelihood (−2LL) with assuming normally distributed RUV with variance σ2. where σ2 is estimated as the mean squared error between observed and model‐predicted concentrations:

Here, yi represents the observed concentration or response, y^i denotes the model prediction, σ is the residual standard deviation, and n is the total number of observations. The lower OFV the higher fitness of the model. Note that NONMEM sometimes omits constant terms such as log2π in calculating OFVs.

Thirdly, using algorithm‐specific update rules, selected candidate solutions were modified to form the next generation, allowing the population to move toward regions with improved OFVs. Over successive iterations, poorer solutions were replaced by higher‐performing ones, balancing global exploration with local refinement. Fourthly, the optimization ended based on a specific termination criterion, and finally the candidate with the lowest −2LL value was taken as the best‐performing parameter set.

Optimization was performed in Python without invoking NONMEM during the metaheuristic search. Candidate parameter sets were evaluated using OFVs computed directly from the simulated datasets. Therefore, the OFVs obtained from the metaheuristic search represent pre‐NONMEM candidate evaluations rather than final NONMEM estimation results.

Based on the above initial evaluation, algorithm(s) with optimal performance were selected for further investigation to assess the impact of different population sizes and iteration numbers on the resulting mean OFVs, execution times, and parameter estimates.

Execution time was reported as measured, while the accuracy of estimated parameters and OFVs was quantified using relative error (RE), defined as:

This metric provides a normalized measure of deviation from the true parameter values (used for data generation) and their OFV, allowing consistent comparison across parameters and algorithm configurations. Lower RE values indicate higher estimation accuracy and better optimization performance.

To further assess robustness under conditions closer to nonlinear mixed‐effects modeling, additional simulations were performed with inter‐individual variability (IIV). For each model, 10 datasets were generated using different random seeds. For the PK model, IIV was specified as 50% CV for CL and V, with a correlation of 0.8 between logCL and logV. For the PK/PD model, IIV values were based on literature‐reported estimates [19]. The top‐performing algorithms from the primary analysis were then applied using the fixed‐effect objective function without IIV to obtain initial THETA estimates. These estimates were subsequently used as initial values for NONMEM FOCE estimation, with initial OMEGA values set to 0.01.

Software and Hardware Specifications for Simulation

The simulations were conducted in Python (version 3.10.16) using numpy (2.2.6), scipy (1.15.3), pyMetaheuristic (1.9.5), and joblib (1.5.1). All computations were performed on a 64‐bit Windows 11 system equipped with a 13th Gen Intel Core i9‐13950HX processor (24 cores, 2.20 GHz) and 128 GB of RAM, using multi‐core execution.

Algorithmic Performance on the Friberg Myelosuppression Model

Overall Algorithmic Performance on the Friberg Myelosuppression Model

Table 2 summarizes the performance of nineteen population‐based optimization algorithms applied to the Friberg myelosuppression model. The algorithms were conducted with a five‐hour limit and an 80‐iteration limit. The OFV derived from the true parameters was −1429.31 and used as the reference for accuracy assessment. From running the population size 80 with 5 h run, SOS, JSO, GA, MA, and MBO achieved best OFVs below −1400 with low mean OFVs, indicating adequate convergence toward the optimal solution. Among them, JSO and MBO algorithms demonstrated particularly favorable performance by attaining low OFVs with relatively short computation times within 400 min as shown in Figure 1b. All algorithms showed a consistent downward trend as optimization progressed (Figure S7).

Conversely, methods such as PSO, ARS, RS, SPBO, and HSA produced substantially higher best and mean OFVs. This result reflects less effective exploration and slower convergence. JSO again achieved an excellent compromise between estimation accuracy and computational cost.

Performance of JSO With Varying Optimization Configuration for the Friberg Myelosuppression Model

For the Friberg myelosuppression model, the JSO was evaluated using different population sizes and iteration counts (20, 50, 80, 100, and 150) to assess their impact on parameter estimation performance. The evaluation focused on estimating the PD parameters.

As illustrated in Figure 2c,d, the execution time increased and OFV decreased with the number of iterations and the population size, which reflects greater computational demand under larger optimization settings. The accuracy of the PD parameter estimates followed the same trend (Figure S8). Larger populations and more iterations produced more reliable convergence and smaller deviations from the true values. One exception was Log_GAM, which showed less sensitivity to these settings and may be less influenced by the optimization configuration or the information content in the data.

Overall, satisfactory JSO performance was achieved with relatively moderate configurations. In this simulation study, using a population size > 50 and at least 50 iterations was typically sufficient to obtain OFV values with relative error below 0.8, requiring a minimum of approximately 90 min of computation.

The parameter estimates obtained using a population size of 80 and 80 iterations were subsequently employed as initial values for NONMEM estimation. The corresponding THETA and SIGMA values, along with the final NONMEM results, are summarized in Table 3. The initial estimates from JSO were reasonably close to the true parameter values and were sufficient to serve as initial estimates in NONMEM, as the final NONMEM estimates converged successfully and closely matched the true values.

Discussion

This study shows that population‐based meta‐heuristic methods can support pharmacometric workflows by providing robust and automated initial estimates for NONMEM. For both benchmark problems, the 2CMT PK model with MM elimination and the Friberg myelosuppression model, the framework found parameter sets that enhanced subsequent modeling workflows and improved convergence stability, computational efficiency, and parameter identifiability. By broadly exploring the parameter space and identifying promising initial estimates, metaheuristic algorithms provide a practical pre‐estimation strategy that can strengthen subsequent likelihood‐based NONMEM estimation.

In addition to the traditional parameter initialization depending on manual choices based on expert judgment, exploratory data analysis, and prior literature, pharmacometric analysts often implement the “retry” strategy for optimizing the initial values. The retry mechanism implemented in Perl‐speaks‐NONMEM (PsN) adjusts initial estimates when a run fails [22, 23]. PsN picks a random value within 10% above or below each initial value and repeats the run to test whether new starting points allow convergence. This method is a simple randomized reset rather than an adaptive process such as population‐based optimization. Each retry acts as an independent attempt without feedback from earlier results. The retry option can help some models reach local or global minima, but it remains a stochastic and non‐directed approach to address convergence issues. Tools such as pyDarwin and Pharmpy support automated pharmacometric model development, but their primary roles differ from the present framework. pyDarwin uses algorithms such as GA and PSO primarily for model‐structure selection, while Pharmpy provides workflow and model‐development tools, including functionality for handling and updating parameter initial estimates during model development [24, 25]. In contrast, the present framework focuses specifically on population‐based metaheuristic optimization of continuous initial parameter values before NONMEM estimation.

Mechanistic PK/PD models are often characterized by complex biological systems and model parameters are typically in nonlinear, multidimensional parameter spaces. Gradient‐based methods like the first‐order conditional estimation method used in NONMEM depend on local derivatives and show strong sensitivity to initial values. They can quickly refine solutions near an optimum but can fail on highly nonlinear objective surfaces and require repeated gradient evaluations that increase computation time for large mechanistic ODE models. Population‐based methods use stochastic global search strategies that explore multiple regions of the parameter space at once. The framework assumes that candidate parameter sets with lower pre‐estimation OFVs are more likely to lie in regions of the parameter space that support successful convergence during subsequent NONMEM estimation. While this does not guarantee convergence to the global minimum, as local minima with favorable OFVs may still exist, it still supports a more efficient global search.

Population‐based optimization algorithms can find initial parameter values, but analysts still need to define parameter boundaries. It is ideal to set these boundaries with prior pharmacological knowledge, literature values, physiological plausibility, or exploratory data analysis so the search stays within realistic regions while allowing global optimization. When little prior information exists, broad but reasonable ranges are still possible. In the 2CMT PK model with MM elimination benchmark, upper bounds were at least tenfold and sometimes over hundredfold above true values to provide enough search space. In such cases, analysts should review results to confirm that parameters remain physiologically plausible and that the parameter estimates are not unrealistic. Sensitivity analysis or repeated runs with alternative boundaries can confirm robustness and stability of the optimization outcome. The results of this study indicate that population‐based algorithms, through stochastic global search and population evolution, can overcome the limitations of traditional methods by autonomously exploring wide parameter spaces without relying on human intervention.

Among the nineteen tested algorithms, several methods such as JSO, SOS, and MA showed strong performance in both parameter optimization and computational efficiency. JSO, in particular, produced accurate parameter estimates with low computational demand. Tests across different population sizes and iteration counts showed that both larger populations and more iterations improved exploration and accuracy. This result indicates that diversity in the initial population, along with sufficient iteration count, supports effective global search. These results show that globally optimized initial parameters support efficient downstream estimation. Although metaheuristic algorithms are broadly applicable, this study aimed to identify those most suitable for nonlinear, multidimensional PK/PD parameter assessment. Better‐performing algorithms, such as JSO, SOS, and MA, may have achieved a more effective balance between exploration and exploitation. However, performance was not clearly determined by the broad categories in Table S1, suggesting that specific search mechanisms and problem characteristics may be more important than category alone.

Population‐based optimization can also address parameter identification, a common challenge in pharmacometric models [26]. Identification refers to the ability to estimate parameters uniquely, which becomes difficult when parameters show strong correlation or when only certain parameter combinations are observable. Broad exploration of the parameter space allows population‐based methods to find sets that fit well even with correlation or limited data. Analysts can track how the distributions of OFV and parameters in the population change over time across iterations. This process may reveal parameters that have minimal influence on the OFV or show extremely high correlations with other parameters, indicating that those parameters might not be identifiable. This improves numerical stability and yields plausible parameters that align with physiological mechanisms. Variation in results across repeated runs can also reveal non‐identifiable parameters that may need reparameterization or additional data.

This study primarily evaluated the framework using nonlinear models without random effects. However, the proposed framework can be extended to nonlinear mixed‐effects models by incorporating inter‐individual variability (IIV) parameters into the optimization process. To further assess robustness, we performed additional analyses using datasets with IIV for both the Michaelis–Menten and Friberg myelosuppression models. The results showed that the proposed approach remained robust and supported successful NONMEM estimation when the algorithm‐derived estimates were used as initial values.

Overall, this work highlights the potential of population‐based optimization as a valuable augmentation to traditional pharmacometric modeling practices. It can reduce dependence on user expertise, improve reproducibility, and accelerate the modeling workflow, particularly for complex nonlinear systems where manual initialization remains a key bottleneck.

Conclusion

This study proposed a framework that links population‐based meta‐heuristic methods with NONMEM to create robust initial parameter values for pharmacometric models. Tests with nineteen methods on two mechanistic PK/PD models showed that meta‐heuristic methods can locate near‐optimal starting values and improve convergence stability, parameter accuracy, and computational reliability.

The results show that methods such as JSO, SOS, and MA provide a data‐driven way to set initial values for nonlinear pharmacometric models. The approach connects global heuristic search with standard model‐based estimation and supports more efficient and less user‐dependent PK/PD model development.
