Cryo-EM insights into isoform-specific properties of the IP3R2 channel
Authors: Mariah R Baker, Xiaoxuan Lin, Guizhen Fan, Ariel Martinez-Chavez, Larry E Wagner, Sundeep Malik, Tyler Allison, Briar Bell, Alexander B Seryshev, Julio Cordero-Morales, Matthew L Baker, David I Yule, Irina I Serysheva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCalcium release through inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate receptors (IP3Rs) is a fundamental signaling mechanism that regulates diverse cellular processes. Among the three mammalian IP3R isoforms, IP3R2 is widely expressed, yet its structural basis for activation and regulation remains unclear. Here, we report cryo-EM structures of mammalian IP3R2 in ligand-free (closed) and Ca2+/IP3/ATP-bound (activated) states at 3.3 Å and 3.6 Å resolution, respectively. These structures define the architecture of IP3R2 and reveal conformational transitions associated with channel activation. Although the IP3-binding pocket is conserved, subtype-specific differences in IP3 affinity likely arise from conformational dynamics of the regulatory ARM2 domain. Comparative analyses of IP3R isoforms identify subtype-specific allosteric networks and domain motions that underlie differential regulation. We further define the ATP-binding site and, through mutagenesis and electrophysiology, establish the structural basis for ATP modulation of channel activity. Together, these findings reveal mechanisms of IP3R2 activation and subtype-specific regulation, providing a framework for understanding isoform-dependent Ca2+ signaling.Subject terms: Permeation and transport, Cryoelectron microscopy, Ion channels

Introduction

Intracellular Ca2+ signaling is a cornerstone of cellular communication, orchestrating processes ranging from muscle contraction and neurotransmission to the regulation of gene expression and programmed cell death. In turn, the precise timing and subcellular localization of these Ca2+ signals depend fundamentally on the tight control of Ca2+ release from internal cellular stores, primarily the endoplasmic/sarcoplasmic reticulum (ER/SR). Central to this regulation stands the inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate receptor (IP3R), a ligand-gated tetrameric Ca2+ channel residing in the ER/SR membranes. Conformational change and opening upon binding of IP3 and Ca2+ allow a rapid influx Ca2+ into the cytoplasm. Dysregulation of IP3R-mediated Ca2+ signaling has been implicated in various human diseases, including cardiac arrhythmias, neurodegenerative disorders, and secretory system disorders1–7

Three subtypes—IP3R1, IP3R2, and IP3R3—are encoded by distinct genes and exhibit unique tissue distribution, regulatory properties, and drug sensitivities. While they share substantial sequence identity (~75%) and similar gating and conductance, they differ substantially in how they respond to IP3, Ca²⁺, ATP, phosphorylation, and partner proteins8–13. These subtype-specific properties allow cells to tailor Ca²⁺ signals to distinct physiological cellular demands. Among the isoforms, IP3R2 exhibits particularly complex functional behavior, partly due to its unique biochemical properties and subcellular localization. It is highly expressed in heart, liver, pancreas, salivary glands, astrocytes, and sweat glands, where it plays a central role in Ca²⁺ signaling. Notably, IP3R2 mediates Ca²⁺ transfer at mitochondria-associated membranes (MAMs), thereby enhancing ER-mitochondrial Ca²⁺ flux that is critical for energy production, metabolic signaling, and cell survival. Dysfunction of IP3R2 is linked to disorders of exocrine glands, cardiac rhythms, and multiple types of cancers14–19. Mouse knockout models exhibit defects in saliva and pancreatic secretion, altered cardiac Ca²⁺ handling, and behavioral abnormalities16,20.

The structural basis for the functional diversity of IP3R isoforms has begun to emerge from structural studies of IP3R1 and IP3R3, revealing how ligand recognition, gating, and allosteric regulation are orchestrated21. However, the molecular determinants underlying the distinct properties of IP3R2 - including its high IP3 affinity, enhanced ATP sensitivity, and unique gating behavior - remain largely unresolved. A high-resolution structure of IP3R2 is therefore essential to clarify the mechanisms that govern its regulation and to understand how disease-associated mutations alter channel function. Here, we report cryo-EM structures of full-length mouse IP3R2 in both apo and ligand-bound states. These structures identify a conserved tetrameric scaffold alongside distinct regulatory features that differentiate IP3R2 from other isoforms. Together, our findings establish a structural framework for distinguishing conserved architectural principles from isoform-specific adaptations governing ligand binding and channel activation.

Results

Architectural organization of the full-length recombinant IP3R2 channel

To uncover the structural basis of IP3R2’s isoform-specific regulatory mechanisms, we determined cryo-EM structures of the full-length receptor in its ligand-free (apo) state and in the presence of IP3, Ca2+, and ATP (CIA). Robust expression of full-length IP3R2 was achieved using a BacMam vector-based expression system22. Following purification under conditions that preserved channel integrity and ligand responsiveness23,24, the homogeneous samples of purified IP3R2 were analyzed by single-particle cryo-EM (Fig. 1; Supplementary Figs. S1–S4), yielding high-quality reconstructions, resolved at overall resolutions of 3.3 Å (apo-IP3R2) and 3.6 Å (CIA-IP3R2) (Table 1). While these values reflect global resolution estimates based on the FSC 0.143 criterion, local resolution varies across the maps (Supplementary Figs. S1–S5), ranging from ~2.4 Å in well-ordered regions on the transmembrane (TM) domain and central cytoplasmic assembly to ~7 Å in more flexible peripheral regions. Consistent with this variation, the maps enabled atomic modeling of most of the receptor, with well-resolved side-chain densities in ordered regions of both the TM and cytoplasmic domains, providing a robust framework for mechanistic interpretation.

Cryo-EM data collection, reconstruction, refinement, and model statistics

IP3R2 adopts the conserved tetrameric scaffold characteristic of the IP3R family, with a large cytoplasmic (CY) regulatory assembly capping a six-TM pore domain, encompassing the ion conduction pathway (Fig. 1, Supplementary Movie 1). At the core of the channel lies a striking dual helical architecture composed of a left-handed cytosolic C-terminal domain (CTD) helix bundle and a right-handed TM6 helix bundle (Fig. 1e). These two bundles are mechanically linked through the linker (LNK) and interdomain linker (ILD) regions, forming a continuous coupling axis that connects the cytoplasmic regulatory apparatus to the TM gating machinery. Notably, the cytosolic CTD bundle is well resolved in IP3R2, as in IP3R124–26, but has been poorly defined in IP3R3 maps27,28. This improved core definition in IP3R2 enables a visualization of the central coupling machinery, providing mechanistic insights into interdomain communication and gating regulation within the IP3R family. The ligand-binding domains (LBDs), βTF1, βTF2 and ARM1, are positioned apically and interact with the CTD bundle, while the armadillo (ARM) and helical (HD) domains form dynamic inter-subunit and intra-subunit contacts that likely mediate long-range allosteric coupling between ligand binding and channel gating (Fig. 1, Supplementary Fig. S7). A notable interface is formed between the CTD and βTF2 domains, where conserved CTD residues interact with partially conserved residues in βTF2 (Supplementary Fig. S8). Subtle subtype-specific differences at this interface suggest that the sequence variation at this interface could affect allosteric coupling between IP3 binding and pore gating.

The TM helices converge toward the channel axis, where a narrow constriction defines the activation gate. The well-resolved density in this region enabled identification of key residues lining the Ca2+ conduction pathway (Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. S15). While the pore architecture remains conserved, with a defined activation gate and a clearly resolved Ca2+ conduction pathway, the membrane-associated helix MA1 is repositioned toward the TM bundle, ~14 Å closer than in IP3R1, bringing it within 5 Å of the neighboring subunit’s P-helix and TM5 helix. Thereby remodeling the inter-subunit interface at the cytosol-membrane junction (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Fig. S9d)23,24.

Overall, the channel structure integrates extensive regulatory interfaces within the CY domains with a membrane-embedded, structurally conserved pore module. Comparison with previously determined IP3R1 and IP3R3 structures reveals a high degree of overall structural conservation (Supplementary Figs. S7, S9), consistent with their substantial sequence identity (Supplementary Fig. S6). These observations indicate that subtype-specific functional differences are unlikely to arise from large-scale rearrangements of the core framework. Instead, divergence is concentrated at discrete regulatory nodes.

Structural basis for isoform-specific IP3-binding

The IP3-binding pocket is located at the interface between βTF2 and ARM1 domains (Fig. 3), consistent with the canonical architecture of the IP3-binding core (IBC) of IP3R124,26,29,30 and IP3R327,28. Accordingly, the cryo-EM density observed in the ligand-bound IP3R2 map was assigned to the bound IP3 molecule and used for model building and refinement against the composite map (see “Methods”). Docking of IP3 into this cryo-EM density reveals a cluster of basic residues that are highly conserved across IP3R isoforms and throughout evolution31: R269, K507, R510, R568, K569 (mouse IP3R2 numbering) (Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. S10). These residues are positioned to engage the phosphate groups of IP3. The IP3 molecule spans the βTF2 and ARM1 domains with the 5- and 4-phosphate groups positioned deeper into the pocket, whereas the 1-phosphate is positioned closer to the ARM1 domain. In this model, the 5-phosphate group lies proximal to K507, R510, K569, and Y567, while the 4-phosphate group is positioned near R269. Residue R568 is oriented toward 1-phosphate and 2-hydroxyl groups of the inositol ring. The spatial arrangement of these residues is consistent with potential hydrogen-bonding and electrostatic interactions that stabilize ligand binding. Additional positively charged residues within the binding pocket, including R265, R411, and R503, although located more than ~4.5 Å from the IP3 molecule, appear to contribute to the overall electrostatic and structural environment of the IBC. Altogether, these residues form a positively charged cleft that enables the precise anchoring and stabilization of IP3 within the binding pocket.

Comparison across IP3R subtypes shows that both residue identity and side chain orientations are nearly identical, underscoring strong evolutionary conservation of the canonical binding pocket (Fig. 3, Supplementary Figs. S6, S9a, b, S10). Despite this structural conservation, the IP3R family exhibits a clear affinity hierarchy for IP3 (IP3R2 > IP3R1 > IP3R3), with dissociation constants (Kd) of ~5.9 nM, 28.6 nM, and 294 nM, respectively8. These differences indicate that subtype-specific regulatory properties are unlikely to originate within the conserved IBC itself. We therefore examine structural elements that could regulate accessibility or conformational dynamics of the binding cleft observed in IP3Rs21.

ARM2 Conformational Dynamics Regulate Cleft Accessibility. Within the channel tetramer, the ARM2 domain forms extensive inter- and intra-subunit contacts with the LBDs, positioning it to influence IP3-binding cleft dynamics (Fig. 4). Comparison of the apo- and CIA-bound structures reveals that ARM2A can adopt two principal conformations. In an extended configuration, ARM2A tightly engages βTF1B and ARM1B from the adjacent subunit (designated subunits A and B in Fig. 4), thereby reinforcing inter-subunit interfaces. In a retracted configuration, ARM2A pivots inward toward its own LBDA, loosening inter-subunit contacts (Supplementary Figs. S1, S2; Supplementary Movies 2, 3). This conformational transition relieves steric constraints at the inter-subunit interface, allowing the IBC to adopt a ligand-competent conformation, similar to that reported for IP3R124. Thus, ARM2 may function as a dynamic hinge that links neighboring subunits and couples ligand recognition to cooperative gating transitions.

A distinctive feature of IP3R2 is a 52-residue loop (E1124-K1175) located between two α-helices within the ARM2 domain, here termed the ARM2 regulatory loop (Fig. 4b–f). A peptide derived from this region (R2PEP6, K1146–N1155) was previously shown to inhibit activation of IP3R2 and IP3R3 in concentration-dependent manner, but only weakly affects IP3R132. It was proposed that this inhibitory peptide competes with IP3 for binding. Notably, several residues within this segment are absent in IP3R1 and only partially conserved in IP3R3 (Fig. 4d, Supplementary Fig. S6). Sequence analysis indicates that the corresponding ARM2 loop in all three IP₃R subtypes is enriched in acidic residues and predicted to be intrinsically disordered (AIUPred scores ~0.85–0.95)33, consistent with its absence from cryo-EM reconstructions (Supplementary Fig. S11). The IP3R2 loop is longer and exhibits a more strongly negative net charge with a slightly increased fraction of polar and hydrophobic residues than in IP3R1 and IP3R3 ARM2 loops (Supplementary Fig. S12a), suggesting subtype-specific regulatory potential for the ARM2 loop.

3D classification and local refinement reveal diffuse density proximal to the IP3-binding cleft, bridging inter- and intra-subunit interfaces between βTF2A, HDA, and the neighboring βTF1B domains (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Fig. S11). Although insufficient for residue-level modeling, the location of this density is compatible with contributions from the intrinsically disordered and conformationally heterogeneous ARM2 loop. To explore potential ARM2 loop-IBC interactions, we generated structural models using AlphaFold3 followed by peptide docking34,35. The resulting ensemble converged on a recurrent feature: electrostatic engagement of the 1154-1155 glutamic acid pair with the same positively charged residues within the βTF2-ARM1 cleft (Fig. 4e, f). Although individual models varied in overall loop conformation, this interaction was consistently observed. Molecular dynamics simulations further indicate that this acidic segment is highly mobile and transiently samples conformations in which the E1154-E1155 pair occupies the IP3-binding cleft. Displacement of the loop exposes the pocket, rendering it accessible for ligand engagement (Supplementary Fig. S12b). While these simulations do not establish functional necessity, they support a model in which the ARM2 loop dynamically engages the positively charged binding cleft in the ligand-free state, modulating cleft accessibility rather than functioning as a classical competitive inhibitor. These findings are consistent with biochemical data showing that the IP3-binding affinity of the isolated βTF2 and ARM1 domains that form the IP3-binding pocket is essentially identical among IP3R subtypes8.

Structural integration of multi-ligand activation

Following isoform-specific tuning of IP₃ engagement, channel activation requires efficient transmission of ligand-induced rearrangements to the pore. Consistent with previous structures of IP3R123,24,26 and IP3R327,28, cryo-EM reconstructions of IP3R2 identify a dynamic allosteric nexus at the cytosol-membrane interface, where the ILD, LNK, and ARM3 domains converge (Fig. 1). This assembly forms a structural hub that mechanically links the cytoplasmic LBDs to the TM pore. It integrates multiple regulatory inputs, including an ATP-binding pocket positioned adjacent to the Zn²⁺-binding C2H2 motif and in close proximity to the ARM3 Ca2+-sensor, thereby creating a multifunctional regulatory center where metabolic, redox, and ionic signals converge (Fig. 5). By organizing these elements within a compact architectural framework, the ILD-LNK-ARM3 assembly modulates the efficiency with which IP3-evoked conformational changes are propagated to the gating machinery.

ATP-Binding Pocket Architecture and Isoform-Specific Features. The ATP-binding pocket resides at the interface between the α-helical ILD and LNK domains, linking ARM3 to the TM region. The domains were locally refined to 2.9 Å and 3.3 Å in ligand-free and ligand-bound structures, respectively (Supplementary Figs. S3, S4). The pocket has a compact geometry with well-defined cryo-EM densities for both the adenine ring and triphosphate tail, permitting detailed modeling of the interaction network that stabilizes nucleotide binding. The adenine ring is enclosed within a hydrophobic cavity formed by M2589, I2583, and W2590, with π-stacking against W2590 and F2196 and additional electrostatic stabilization from K2584. The ribose and base are likely further supported by hydrogen bonds involving N2588, H2587, E2586, S2585, and nearby backbone carbonyls (F2181, E2580, H2582). The triphosphate moiety extends into a basic surface formed primarily by R2174 and K2177, which may coordinate the β- and γ-phosphates through salt bridges and hydrogen bonds (Figs. 5–7). Adjacent bound Ca²⁺ and Zn²⁺ ions further stabilize this region (Fig. 5). Overall, the architecture closely resembles ATP-binding pockets described in IP3R1, IP3R3, and RyR channels, consistent with a conserved regulatory framework among intracellular Ca²⁺ release channels (Fig. 6d, 7)23,24,27,28,36–38. However, variations in neighboring residues forming the ATP-binding site reshape the local electrostatic environment, potentially contributing to subtype-specific ATP sensitivity in IP3R2.

In IP3R2, the ILD-LNK cleft forms an electropositive pocket (Fig. 7). The R2174 is well resolved in the CIA-structure, where the guanidinium group can form a two-point interaction with the γ-phosphate of ATP (Figs. 5, 6). As the positively charged group at physiological pH, the guanidinium moiety provides favorable electrostatic interactions with the negatively charged phosphate group. In contrast, this side chain is less defined in the apo-IP3R2 structure, likely reflecting increased flexibility in the absence of bound ATP, which appears to stabilize this region of the binding pocket. In IP3R1, the equivalent position is occupied by lysine (K2220)21,23,24, whereas in IP3R3 it is replaced by glutamate (E2149)27,39, likely weakening phosphate coordination (Figs. 6d, 7; Supplementary Figs. S6, S9). Consistent with these differences, IP3R1 exhibits more transient ATP binding and moderate ATP dependence40–42, while IP3R3 contains a solvent-exposed pocket lacking a basic residue important for efficient phosphate coordination, resulting in markedly reduced ATP affinity43,44. Accordingly, IP3R3 still exhibits ATP-dependent potentiation but requires roughly an order-of-magnitude higher ATP concentration to produce comparable effects45–47. These functional distinctions suggest that ATP binding stabilizes the ILD-LNK interface more effectively in IP3R2, dampening local flexibility and strengthening communication between cytoplasmic regulatory domains and the pore. In contrast, weaker coordination in IP3R1 and IP3R3 may permit greater interdomain mobility, diminishing ATP-dependent allosteric reinforcement. Consistent with functional data showing that ATP regulates the Ca2+ sensitivity of IP3R248, the proximity of the ATP-binding site to both the Zn2+ motif and the ARM3 Ca2+ sensor suggests potential regulatory crosstalk, positioning the dynamic nexus as a multifunctional control hub that integrates cytosolic regulatory signals and transmits them to the gating pore.

ATP as an Allosteric Reinforcer of Gating. Previous studies have shown that ATP potentiates Ca2+ release through IP3R2 only at submaximal IP3 concentrations45,49. To understand the structural basis of this effect, we generated a double mutant (K2177L/K2584L; “IP3R2 KK”) designed to disrupt ATP coordination by replacing basic lysine residues to hydrophobic leucine residues while preserving side chain volume. Stable HEK-3KO cell lines expressing wild-type or KK mutant mouse IP3R2 (mIP3R2) were generated50. The IP3R2 KK mutant displayed normal reticular localization and expression levels comparable to wild type (Supplementary Fig. S14). Cells expressing IP3R2 KK exhibited significantly reduced sensitivity to stimulation at submaximal concentrations of the IP3-generating muscarinic agonist Carbachol compared with WT IP3R2. This observation is consistent with impaired ATP-dependent potentiation due to the disruption of ATP binding to IP3R2, reducing Ca2+ release at sub-maximal IP3 concentration (Fig. 8).

Direct measurements of IP3-induced Ca2+ release in permeabilized cells using mag-fura-2 further confirmed this effect. In WT IP3R2-expressing cells, ATP robustly enhanced Ca2+ release at submaximal IP3 (Fig. 9a, c). In contrast, Ca2+ release through IP3R2 KK was insensitive to ATP across all IP3 concentrations tested (Fig. 9b, d). These findings unambiguously confirm that K2177 and K2584 are essential determinants of ATP coordination and confer ATP-dependent functional potentiation in IP3R2. Single-channel activity of mIP3R2 in DT40-3KO cells monitored using the on-nucleus configuration of the patch-clamp technique, further reinforced this conclusion. At submaximal IP3, ATP markedly increased the open probability (Po) of wild-type channels but had no effect on IP3R2 KK (Fig. 9e, f). Remarkably, at saturating IP3, maximal Po was similar for wild-type and mutant channels and was ATP-insensitive (Fig. 9e, f). Thus, ATP enhances IP3R2 gating by reinforcing ligand-dependent activation rather than altering maximal channel activity.

Collectively, these findings indicate that although the ATP-binding pocket is structurally conserved among IP3R isoforms, subtle sequence and electrostatic differences in the surrounding environment confer enhanced ATP sensitivity to IP3R2. By stabilizing the cytosolic architecture and promoting conformational communication between the regulatory and TM domains, ATP acts as a potent energetic modulator, amplifying ligand-dependent gating transitions in IP3R2.

Gating transitions in the Ca2+ conduction pathway

The TM region of IP3R2 adopts the canonical domain-swapped architecture characteristic of the tetrameric ion channels, including intracellular Ca2+-release channels, forming a central ion-permeation pathway surrounded by stabilizing peripheral helices (Fig. 1). Four TM6 helices line the pore and define discrete constrictions and electrostatic landscape that govern Ca2+ conduction through coordinated side chain rearrangements. Key pore-lining residues include R2548, I2541, F2537, N2534, I2530, N2496, D2502, and R2506 (Fig. 2). The TM6 exhibits a bulge between residues I2529 and N2534, consistent with the presence of the π-helix segment (Supplementary Fig. S15), a motif commonly observed in the TM helices of ion channels, including IP3R121,26,51. The luminal vestibule is shaped primarily by N2496, D2502, and R2506 (Fig. 2). Although only D2502 contributes a formal negative charge, this triad collectively creates an electrostatically permissive environment for Ca2+ entry: D2502 provides localized negative potential, R2506 stabilizes the surrounding electrostatic field, and N2496 contributes polar coordination. Rather than forming a classical acidic selectivity filter (SF), these residues form a luminal ion-handling interface that facilitates ion capture before passage into the pore. D2502 is structurally equivalent to D2551 in IP₃R1, a residue previously implicated in regulating ion selectivity, where mutations significantly alter permeation properties52.

Toward the central cavity, F2537 and I2541 form hydrophobic constrictions that function as the principal gating barrier. In the apo-state, their side chains project toward the pore axis to form a narrow, dehydrated bottleneck that prevents Ca2+ permeation (Fig. 2). Comparison of the apo- and ligand-bound reconstructions reveals concerted movements of the TM6 helices accompanied by rotameric reorientation of F2537 and I2541. These rearrangements expand the minimal pore radius from ∼0.8 Å to ∼1.49 Å at F2537 and from 0.59 Å to 1.31 Å at I2541, reflecting partial relief of the hydrophobic constriction (Fig. 2). Although the pore seems to remain geometrically narrow, such that the fully hydrated Ca2+ ion cannot pass, such dilation is sufficient to promote local water penetration and reduce the energetic cost of ion dehydration53,54.

Importantly, these observations support a gating mechanism in which channel activation could be achieved through dynamic side chain repositioning at a hydrophobic gate rather than large-scale backbone expansion. Similar principles have been described for IP3R1 and RyR channels26,36,38, where the minimal pore radii remain geometrically narrow even in activating states and ion conduction is enabled through dynamic side chain rearrangements and water penetration54. Together, these findings suggest that the canonical iris-like pore-opening model may not fully capture IP₃R gating; instead, localized rotamer transitions suffice to create a permissive ion pathway.

In contrast, the luminal vestibule remains wide across states, emphasizing that gating is primarily controlled at the cytosolic end of the pore. The architecture is conserved among intracellular Ca2+-release channels and reinforces the concept that the primary energetic barrier lies at the hydrophobic constriction rather than at the luminal entrance24,26,36,38. The functional relevance of this framework is underscored by the anhidrosis-associated G2498S mutation7,55 within the conserved SF-loop sequence (GGGVGD), which led to a loss of Ca2+ conduction in mutant channels. Substitution of glycine with serine introduces a polar side chain into a tightly packed inter-subunit interface, potentially enabling aberrant hydrogen bonding with R2495 and perturbing local packing. Structural modeling predicts that this alteration disrupts helix packing at the luminal entrance, indirectly shifting equilibrium toward the closed state and increasing the energetic threshold for Ca²⁺ conduction (Supplementary Fig. S16). Consistently, a pathogenic mutation of the equivalent pore glycine in IP3R1 (G2539W) causes a neurological disorder characterized by ataxia and cognitive impairment56, underscoring the critical role of this conserved residue in maintaining proper channel conduction. Thus, although gating motions are executed at the cytosolic hydrophobic constriction, perturbations at the luminal entrance can allosterically modulate channel opening by reshaping the structural stability of the conduction pathway.

Discussion

Our study provides a structural and mechanistic framework for understanding subtype-specific regulation of IP3R2, linking ligand recognition, allosteric coupling, and pore gating within a unified architecture. By resolving the conformational landscape of IP3R2 in both apo- and ligand-bound states, we show how conserved structural elements are differentially tuned to modulate activation efficiency without altering the core fold of the receptor family.

Although the IP3-binding pocket is structurally conserved across subtypes, subtype-specific differences in ligand sensitivity appear not to arise from changes in coordinating residues. Instead, regulation is likely encoded in the conformational dynamics of adjacent domains that control accessibility and stabilization of the binding cleft. A principal determinant of this regulation is the ARM2 domain. In IP3R2, the ARM2 regulatory loop samples conformations near the IP3-binding cleft and transiently engages βTF2, ARM1, and HD domains without forming a rigid occlusion. Rather than directly competing with IP3, this loop modulates accessibility and stabilizes the ligand-bound conformation through dynamic inter-domain contacts. We propose that the ARM2 loop functions as a “dynamic door” that controls access to the binding cleft. Unlike a static steric barrier, this loop undergoes isoform-tuned conformational fluctuations that may regulate transitions between accessible and restricted states through coordinated extended-retracted motions. In the extended configuration, ARM2 likely maintains partial cleft accessibility while permitting the conformational flexibility within the binding pocket required for ligand accommodation. Conversely, the retracted state may promote tighter interdomain packing around the bound ligand. Isoform-specific differences within the ARM2 loop could shift the conformational equilibrium between these states, providing a mechanistic basis for differential sensitivity across IP3 isoforms.

Structural comparisons further indicate that variations in loop length, electrostatic composition, and loop-LBD interaction propensity may influence ARM2 movement. These elements likely tune the relative stability of extended and retracted conformations, thereby biasing the conformational balance between accessibility and stabilization of the binding cleft. Consistent with this view, IP3R2 predominantly adopts an extended ARM2 configuration, whereas IP3R1 and IP3R3 favor a retracted IP3-bound state24,27,29. In IP3R1, the absence of inhibitory elements may allow greater mobility of ARM2 toward the retracted conformation, potentially facilitating rapid ligand access. In contrast, IP3R3 contains a self-binding peptide (R3SBP)57 that introduces persistent structural anchoring within the cleft, potentially limiting conformational flexibility and access to the binding-competent state. These observations suggest that ARM2 dynamics are tuned in an isoform-dependent manner rather than reflecting stochastic motion. This structural tuning correlates with measured ligand affinities, with IP3R2 exhibiting a higher apparent affinity than IP3R1 and IP3R38. Stabilization of the extended ARM2 configuration in IP3R2 may increase the probability of productive ligand engagement and prolong the lifetime of the bound state, linking conformational dynamics of regulatory domains to differences in ligand sensitivity among IP3R isoforms.

Beyond ligand recognition, productive channel activation requires efficient transmission of conformational changes to the pore, mediated by a second regulatory layer at the cytosol-membrane interface in IP3Rs. ATP binding introduces an additional modulatory input. Although the ATP-binding pocket is structurally conserved across the family of intracellular Ca2+ release channels24,26–28,36,38, subtle sequence and electrostatic variations in IP3R2 appear to enhance nucleotide coordination and stabilize the ILD-LNK interface. ATP binding reduces local flexibility and reinforces mechanical coupling between cytoplasmic regulatory domains and the TM region, thereby lowering the energetic barrier for propagating ligand-induced rearrangements toward the pore. In contrast, comparatively weaker ATP coordination in IP3R1 and IP3R3 may permit the nexus to be more dynamic, reducing ATP-dependent reinforcement of activation. The proximity of this site to Zn²⁺-binding motifs and Ca²⁺-sensing elements further supports its role as an integration hub for metabolic and ionic signals.

At the pore, ligand-induced structural rearrangements converge on the TM6 bundle crossing. Even under activating conditions, the minimal pore radius at the conserved hydrophobic residues F2537 and I2541 remains smaller than required for passage of fully hydrated Ca2,58. Similar structural constraints have been observed in other tetrameric ion channels, including IP3R1 and RyRs24,26,36,38,59–62, where the conduction pathway remains narrower than predicted under conductive conditions. Molecular dynamics and ion-pulling simulations further indicate that conduction occurs via local hydration and side chain rotamer fluctuations, without major backbone dilation54. This side chain driven gating strategy departs from classical iris-like models of pore opening and suggests that permeation in intracellular Ca2+ release channels appear to depend on dynamic modulation of hydrophobic barriers rather than static widening of the pore. Consistent with this paradigm, our data support a gating mechanism in which rotameric transitions at key hydrophobic residues transiently relieve steric occlusion and permit ion permeation without classical iris-like pore dilation.

Together, these findings support a hierarchical model of IP3R2 activation that links ligand binding to ion permeation through sequential and energetically coordinated steps. Ligand tuning at the ARM2-LBD interfaces shapes affinity and accessibility; ATP binding at the cytosol-membrane nexus reinforces long-range allosteric coupling; and side chain dynamics at the TM6 hydrophobic constriction regulate Ca2+ permeation. Within this framework, IP3R2 emerges as an isoform optimized for high-affinity ligand capture and efficient transmission of conformational changes, whereas IP3R1 and IP3R3 exhibit structural features consistent with more moderate or restricted activation profiles. Further high-resolution structures capturing intermediate and transition states in native membrane environments will be essential to define a comprehensive isoform-specific gating models and fully resolve the energetic pathway linking ligand binding to channel opening. Methods

mIP3R2 construct and expression

Cloning and expression of the full-length type 2 IP3R followed the BacMam strategy22,23. Mouse type 2 IP3R gene (mITPR2) was subcloned from a pcDNA3.1 plasmid (provided by the Yule laboratory) using NotI and NheI restriction sites into the pEG BacMam expression vector (Addgene Plasmid #160451) and sequenced. The resulting mIP3R2-BacMam construct was transformed into DH10Bac E. coli cells, and blue-white screening selected positive colonies, which were then used for isolation of the recombinant bacmid DNA. IP3R2-Bacmid DNA was used to transfect Spodoptera frugiperda (Sf9) cells using Cellfectin II (Gibco). The baculovirus was amplified to P2 and then used at 5% (v/v) to infect HEK293S GnTI- cells in Freestyle Media (Gibco) supplemented with 2% FBS (Atlas) at 30 °C and 7% CO2 with shaking. 1 mM sodium butyrate was added 24 h post-infection followed by collection of the cell pellet by centrifugation after 72 h for IP3R2 protein purification25,35

Protein purification and cryospecimen preparation

The purification of mIP3R2 follows a similar strategy that we have previously used for IP3R1 protein. Briefly, cells were solubilized in 2 mM Lauryl Maltose Neopentyl Glycol (LMNG, Anatrace) and 0.1% (w/v) l-α-phosphatidylcholine (PC, Sigma) for 2 h at 4 °C. Non-solubilized material was cleared by ultracentrifugation (100,000 × g), and the supernatant was applied to an immunoaffinity matrix coupled with a rabbit polyclonal anti-mouse IP3R2 peptide antibody raised against the C-terminal region of IP3R2 (custom sera generated at Cocalico Biologicals). IP3R2 protein was eluted from the column using a synthetic peptide encompassing the last 19 residues of IP3R2, a highly divergent sequence among IP3Rs. Immunoaffinity purification of IP3R2 was performed in 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM EDTA, 0.02 mM LMNG and eluted with 500 μM of the mIP3R2 C-term peptide. Purified protein was concentrated (Amicon centricon), and the protein concentration of 5 mg/ml was determined by A280. mIP3R2 was incubated with 1 mM EGTA for the apo-state, or a 5X cocktail of ligands was applied to the cryogrid immediately before vitrification, providing final ligand concentrations of 1 mM ATP, 10 μM IP3, 1 μM Ca2+. The samples were vitrified by plunge freezing in liquid ethane using an FEI Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific). 3.0 μl of IP3R2 sample was applied onto a glow-discharged Quantifoil copper R 2/1 grid (Quantifoil Micro Tools, GmbH) containing a thin continuous carbon film, and blotted for 1–2 s at 100% humidity and 4 °C.

Planar lipid bilayer recordings

For mIP3R2 channel recordings, planar lipid bilayers were formed in 10 mg/ml solution of DPHPC/DOPC (70:30 mol/mol) dissolved in decane. Orbit Mini and EDR2 software (Nanion Technologies) were used to obtain single-channel recordings with 100 µm diameter cavity of a multielectrode-cavity-array chip (IONERA Technologies). The cis and trans chambers contained symmetrical solutions of 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EGTA (pH 7.4), 2.1 mM CaCl2 ([Ca2+]free = 50 μM), 1 mM ATP, 10 μM IP3. Experimental free Ca2+ concentrations were calculated with WebMax Chelator. To promote channel incorporation into the lipid bilayer, purified IP3R2 channels were reconstituted into lipid nanodiscs as described for IP3R124. IP3R2-ND were added to the cis chamber and the voltage was oscillated ±20 mV to facilitate protein insertion into the bilayer. Data was acquired at a 10 kHz sampling rate with measurements conducted at room temperature with constant holding potential of +70 mV. Traces were low-pass filtered offline using a Boxcar filter with a width of 15 to smooth points, in Clampfit 10.4.1.10.

Cryo-EM data acquisition, image processing, and model building

Cryo-EM data collection was performed on a Titan Krios G3 microscope (ThermoFisher Scientific) operated at 300 kV and aligned for parallel illumination. The BioQuantum energy filter (Gatan) was operated with an energy width of 20 eV. Images were acquired using the EPU software (ThermoFisher Scientific) at a nominal TEM magnification of ×130,000 and recorded on a K2 Summit direct electron detector (Gatan) operated in super-resolution counting mode with a calibrated physical pixel size of 1.08 Å. The dose rate applied to the detector was 8.6 electrons/Å²/s, with a total dose of ~50 e/Å2 at the specimen plane. Movie stacks were acquired at a defocus range of 0.8–2.8 μm (Table 1).

Movie stacks were imported to CryoSparc v4.764 where motion correction and CTF estimation were performed, followed by blob and template picking, 2D classification, and 3D classifications (Supplementary Figs. S1, S2). For the ligand-free IP3R2 structure 8,112 movies were collected, and 413,650 particles were picked. 73,731 particles contributed to the final non-uniform 3D refinement, resulting in a 3.32 Å resolution EM density map. For the ligand-bound structure, 37,329 movies were collected, yielding 6,026,377 particle picks. Following 2D and 3D classification, 151,391 particles contributed to the 3D consensus non-uniform refinement determined at 3.64 Å resolution. Symmetry expansion followed by masked 3D classification of the LBD-ARM2 domains resulted in ‘retracted’, ‘intermediate’ and ‘extended’ ARM2 domains. The parent particles occurring 1, 2, 3 or 4 times in each class were extracted. Parent particles occurring 3× and 4× in either the retracted or extended class were subjected to 3D refinement (Table 1; Supplementary Figs. S1, S2). Symmetry expanded consensus particles were analyzed using the 3D Variability Analysis (3DVA) module. For each cryo-EM data set, five modes of variability were calculated. Ten maps for each mode were generated using a 4 Å low-pass filter. In each state, the ARM2 and loop displayed the largest motions, regardless of symmetric and symmetry-breaking variability. To visualize the conformational variability across the channel protein assembly, the maps from mode 1 of CIA-IP3R2 and mode 4 of apo-IP3R2 were examined in UCSF ChimeraX65 as volume series and recorded as movies (Supplementary Movie 2).

Composite maps were generated for apo- and CIA-IP3R2 states by local refinements for multiple regions. 3D local refinements were carried out in cryoSPARC using soft masks encompassing the LBD—amino acids (a.a.):1–666, ARM1-HD—a.a.:638–1102, ARM2 a.a.:1103–1582, ARM3-ILD-LNK—a.a.:1583–2225, 2561–2635, and TMD—a.a.:2226–2560 regions. For cytosolic domains (LBD, ARM1-HD, ARM2, and ARM3-ILD-LNK), particles were C4 symmetry-expanded prior to mask subtraction and local refinement. The TMD was locally refined without symmetry expansion, with C4 symmetry enforced. Locally refined maps were aligned to their respective consensus reconstructions and combined in UCSF ChimeraX65 using vop maximum to generate composite maps for model building and visualization. Resolution estimates for locally refined regions contributing to the composite reconstructions are provided in Supplementary Figs. S3, S4.

For building 3D atomic models, mouse IP3R2 sequence (UniProt: Q9Z329) was used to generate an initial monomeric atomic model with AlphaFold3 (accessed 05/2025)66. The predicted structure (pLDDT 72.36; pTM 0.66) was rigid-body docked into the apo- and CIA-IP3R2 cryo-EM maps using the Fit in Map tool in ChimeraX65. Regions with low predicted confidence were removed, and the remaining structure was divided into domains based on the established IP3R1 domain architecture. Then, each domain was independently fit into the density using ChimeraX65 and recombined into a single model. Interactive model adjustment was performed in Coot67, followed by real-space refinement and map–model validation in Phenix68, optimizing local fit to density while minimizing steric clashes, Ramachandran outliers, and rotamer outliers. After iterative cycles of optimization, the biological tetramer was generated by applying fitting the monomer into the symmetry-related positions in the map and applying 4-fold NCS symmetry in the subsequent Phenix68 refinement steps.

Ligand densities, identified by their absence in the apo-map, were modeled into the structure with initial positioning guided by established IP3R1 structures (PDBID: 7LHF; 8EAR). Ligand poses were generated using Rosetta34, and the pose with the best fit to the experimental density was selected for further refinement. The resulting coordinates underwent iterative refinement in Coot67 and Phenix68, with the final stages of the refinement carried out using the composite map of locally refined domains.

As expected for ligand docking into cryo-EM coulombic potential map at non-atomic resolution, limited uncertainty remains in the precise positioning of the ligand and nearby side chains. Accordingly, small variations in modeled coordinates may affect specific interatomic distances. Nonetheless, the overall ligand orientation and architecture of the binding pocket are well supported by density and are consistent with previously characterized IP3-binding interfaces24,30,69,70.

To model the interaction of the ARM2 loop with the ligand-binding domain (LBD), the entire ARM2 loop–LBD region was first predicted using AlphaFold3 (pLDDT 80.28; pTM 0.79), and the loop was truncated to include only the residues composing the experimentally identified inhibitory peptide. Peptide docking was performed using FlexPepDock (Rosetta/Rosie), generating a gallery of decoys; the top ten scoring models, all of which exhibited similar orientations in which the acidic residues of the peptide contacted the same basic LBD residues that engage IP3, were selected for simulation.

Molecular dynamics simulations were performed using OpenMM 8.171 through the publicly available Google Colab workflow. Four systems were generated for comparison: the IP3R2 LBD alone, LBD bound to IP3, LBD bound to the ARM2-derived inhibitory peptide, and LBD bound simultaneously to IP3 and the peptide. Initial coordinates were taken from the Apo- and CIA- IP3R2 LBDs with peptide models.

Protein atoms were parameterized using the AMBER14SB force field, and ligand parameters for IP3 and the peptide were generated within the Colab environment. Each system was solvated in a rectangular TIP3P water box with a 10 Å buffer in all directions. Following solvation, the systems underwent energy minimization using the standard L-BFGS protocol. Production simulations were run for 50 ns for each system in the NPT ensemble with a 2-fs time step. Trajectories were written every 10 ps in DCD format, and energies and coordinates were logged throughout the simulations. Trajectory analysis was conducted in ChimeraX65, focusing on ligand positioning within the binding pocket, competition between IP3 and the inhibitory peptide for shared basic residues, and differences in loop and domain motions among the four systems.

A custom pore-analysis algorithm was used to compute the ion permeation pathway and local pore radius by identifying a continuous channel axis and, at each axial position, determining the maximal sterically allowed probe radius defined by protein van der Waals surfaces. The software and analysis scripts are available on GitHub (https://github.com/mlbaker-uth/pyHole).

Energy calculations on the G2498S mutant were performed by introducing the amino acid substitution in Coot within the wild-type IP3R2 model, followed by local refinement of ±5 residues adjacent to the mutation site to relieve steric clashes using Coot. The G2498S mutant and wild-type IP3R2 models were submitted to the Rosetta Energy_Breakdown protocol via Rosie72,73. Per-residue Rosetta energies were extracted for both models and compared to quantify the energetic consequences of the mutation. All sequence alignments were performed using Clustal-ω74,75 and rendered via ESPript76. 2D ligand interaction diagrams were performed using Maestro (Schrödinger Release 2024-1: Maestro, Schrödinger, LLC, New York, NY, 2025.) used with default geometric cutoffs applied uniformly across all models. Electrostatic potentials were calculated using UCSF ChimeraX65 with atomic charges and radii assigned according to the AMBER force field. The resulting electrostatic potential was mapped onto the molecular surface and visualized as a color gradient to highlight regions of negative and positive charge. Identical calculation parameters were applied uniformly across all models to ensure consistency.

Cell culture and stable cell line generation

HEK-293 cells were engineered by CRISPR-Cas9-mediated deletion of all three endogenous IP3R subtypes to generate HEK-3KO cells46. HEK-3KO cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. For the generation of stable cell lines, cells were plated in 35-mm dishes and transiently transfected with 900 ng of mIP3R2 WT or KK plasmid, along with 100 ng of a pIRES puromycin resistance vector, using Lipofectamine 2000. 48 h after transfection, cells were subjected to selection in complete growth medium containing puromycin (1 mg/mL). After 7 days of selection, individual puromycin-resistant colonies were isolated and transferred to 24-well plates, which were maintained in medium supplemented with puromycin. Puromycin-resistant cell lines were expanded, and expression of the desired constructs was verified by immunoblotting. Stable cell lines were maintained in complete medium containing puromycin.

Imaging of [Ca2+] in intact cells

[Ca2+]i imaging was performed using an inverted epifluorescence Nikon microscope with a 40x oil immersion objective lens (NA, 1.3). Fura-2 loaded cells expressing the indicated constructs were excited alternately with light at 340 and 380 nm (10-nm bandpass filters, Chroma, Rockingham, VT) using a monochrometer (TILL Photonics, Pleasanton, CA). Fluorescence images were captured and digitized with a digital camera driven by TILL Photonics software. Images were captured every 1 s with a 15 ms exposure and 4x4 binning. 340/380 ratio images were calculated online and stored immediately to a hard disk77.

Unidirectional Ca2+ flux measurements in permeabilized cells

Cells were loaded with Mag-fura-2 and permeabilized with β-escin to remove cytoplasmic indicator. The ER was loaded with Ca2+ by activating the SERCA pump with Mg-ATP9. After loading the ER, Mg2+ was removed to disable the SERCA pump and IP3 added in the presence or absence of saturating Na-ATP, and the decrease in ER Mg-fura-2 fluorescence was monitored. Rates of Ca2+ release were estimated from these average responses by fitting the initial 30 s period of decreasing fluorescence to a single exponential function (GraphPad Prism, San Diego, CA).

Preparation of DT40 cell nuclei

Isolated DT40 nuclei were prepared using homogenization from cells expressing the indicated construct. The homogenization buffer (HB) contained 250 mM sucrose, 150 mM KCl, 3 mM 2-BME, 10 mM Tris, 1 mM PMSF, pH 7.5 with a complete protease inhibitor tablet (Roche, USA). Cells were washed and resuspended in HB prior to nuclear isolation using a RZR 2021 homogenizer (Heidolph Instruments, Germany) with 15 strokes at 1200 rpm. A 3 μl aliquot of nuclear suspension was placed in 3 ml of bath solution which contained 140 mM KCl, 10 mM Hepes, 500 μM BAPTA and 246 nM free Ca2+, pH 7.1. Nuclei were allowed to adhere to a plastic culture dish for 10 min prior to patching.

On-nuclei patch clamp experiments

Single InsP3R channel potassium currents (ik) were measured in the on-nucleus patch clamp configuration using pCLAMP 9 and an Axopatch 200B amplifier (Molecular Devices, Sunnydale, CA, USA). Pipette solution contained 140 mM KCl, 10 mM Hepes, 5 mM ATP, with varying concentrations of IP3, BAPTA and free Ca2+. Free [Ca2+] was calculated using Max Chelator freeware and verified fluorometrically. Traces were consecutive 3 s sweeps recorded at −100 mV, sampled at 20 kHz and filtered at 5 kHz. A minimum of 15 s of recordings was considered for data analysis. The data are representative of between 3 and 5 experiments per condition. Pipette resistances were typically 20 MΩ and seal resistances were >5 GΩ. Single-channel openings were detected using the half-threshold-crossing criterion in the event detection protocol in Clampfit 9. We assumed that the number of channels in any nuclear patch is represented by the maximum number of discrete stacked events observed during the experiment. Even at low Po, stacking events were evident. Only patches with one apparent channel were considered for analyses. Probability of opening (Po), unitary current (ik), open and closed times, and burst analyses were calculated using Clampfit 9 and Origin 6 software (Origin Lab, Northampton, MA, USA). All-points current amplitude histograms were generated from the current records and fitted with a normal Gaussian probability distribution function. The coefficient of determination (R2) for every fit was >0.95. The Po was calculated using the multimodal distribution for the open and closed current levels. The threshold for an open event was set at 50% of the maximum open current and events shorter than 0.1 ms were ignored. A ‘burst’ was defined as a period of channel opening following a period of no channel activity which was greater than five times the mean closed time (0.2 ms) within a burst.
