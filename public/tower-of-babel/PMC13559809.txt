Retained carotid artery intravascular foreign body
Authors: Aaron Machen, Muhammad Rizwan Sardar, Gilbert Aidinian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 72-year-old male with a history of tobacco use, hypertension, atrial fibrillation, and stroke presented with dizziness and was discovered to have a retained intravascular wire in the left internal carotid artery from a prior endovascular procedure done over 1 year prior to presentation. Given this timing and concern for an embedded wire tip, open carotid artery exploration was performed with removal of the wire and patch angioplasty without complications. Intravascular foreign bodies are rare but have a significant risk of complications, and generally, percutaneous retrieval is advised. This case was performed open, given the risk for injury to the vessel.Keywords: Delayed presentation of retained foreign body, Embedded foreign body, Intravascular foreign body, Open retrieval, Retained wire

As the use of endovascular interventions has increased, so has the incidence of retained intravascular foreign bodies. Most foreign bodies are iatrogenic and can include central line fragments, guide wires, catheter fragments, embolization coils, inferior vena cava filters, cardiac valve fragments, sheaths, pacing wires, and others.1 When identified immediately, most foreign bodies can be removed percutaneously with low risk of complications and low mortality. Various devices exist to assist with the percutaneous removal of these foreign bodies, including snares, balloons, forceps, and baskets, and with these various devices, there are a variety of different techniques for removal. Occasionally, the decision will be made to remove foreign bodies via an open approach, given the location, the type of foreign body, erosion in vessel walls, or other factors. Limited data on open foreign body removal are available. Generally, intravascular foreign bodies are asymptomatic. One case series shows a rate of 5.6% of patients with symptoms and 37% of patients with foreign bodies found incidentally during imaging for other reasons.2 Our case has been discussed with the patient, who agreed to allow this information to be published.

Case report

A 72-year-old male with a history of long-term tobacco use, hypertension, atrial fibrillation, and multiple strokes was referred to vascular surgery in April 2025 for a retained wire in the left carotid artery. His last stroke was in 2023, at which time he underwent an unknown endovascular intervention at an outside facility. He had been experiencing bradycardia and dizziness, which were evaluated by his cardiologist. A carotid duplex obtained by the cardiology office showed a foreign body in the left common and internal carotid arteries (Fig 1). Catheter-based angiography was performed, which showed a retained wire with the tip curled in his left carotid artery extending down to the proximal descending aorta (Fig 2 and 3). The patient was referred to vascular surgery, who then sent the patient for computed tomography angiography, which confirmed the wire embedded in the internal carotid artery extending into the descending aorta without obvious stenosis or dissection. He had no focal neurologic deficits and underwent carotid artery exploration with removal of a 45-cm wire and patch angioplasty (Fig 4).

In the operating room, the left common, internal, and external carotid arteries were dissected, and the tip of the wire was identified under fluoroscopy. The internal carotid artery dissection was continued past the curled end of the wire. After systemic anticoagulation, the internal, external, and common carotid arteries were occluded, and longitudinal arteriotomy was performed. The distal end of the wire was noted to be entirely embedded in the intima. The tip of the wire was dissected from the intima, and once completely free, the remaining wire was extracted through this arteriotomy. The intima was evaluated, and no debris or intimal flaps were noted. Fluoroscopy confirmed that no residual wire remained. The arteriotomy was repaired with patch angioplasty, and angiography showed no evidence of dissection. A postoperative chest X ray showed no abnormalities. The patient had subsequent follow-up in clinic with no associated complications.

Discussion

Intravascular foreign bodies, though relatively rare, are becoming more common with an increasing popularity of endovascular procedures. These foreign bodies can have significant risks, including thrombophlebitis, sepsis, arrhythmia, myocardial injury, bacterial endocarditis, vessel occlusion, ischemia, and cardiac perforation.1 Typically, intravascular foreign bodies are diagnosed at the time of the procedure or shortly thereafter and can be extracted percutaneously with specifically designed devices, such as snares, balloons, forceps, etc. In these cases, a percutaneous approach to removal is considered safe and relatively low risk, which is why it is the method of retrieval of choice for many providers.3 Our patient’s foreign body was not diagnosed immediately and, after being in place for over a year, was discovered during a workup by cardiology when he became symptomatic. Because the wire appeared to be embedded in the artery wall on imaging, it was felt that open removal would have had a lower risk of complications, including dissection, stroke, and arterial injury. The open approach was selected for this reason and to allow direct visualization of the neointima, providing an opportunity for endarterectomy if necessary. Open retrieval is often used when a percutaneous approach fails or when the foreign body is embedded in or extends through the vessel wall. An open approach can be associated with risks related to general anesthesia, surgical site complications, longer recovery times, and increased health care costs.

Prevention is important to consider when discussing retained foreign bodies. Device failure does occur, but one review found that operator error made up a significant proportion of cases.1 It is recommended that those who perform endovascular procedures and those who deal with intravascular devices become familiar with the function and design of the devices to facilitate safe usage. Use of appropriate equipment and adequate training can help decrease the risk of adverse events.

Conclusions

With the dramatic increase in endovascular procedures over the past years and decades has come an increase in intravascular foreign bodies. The majority of these can be safely managed with percutaneous interventions, which have low rates of complications and high success rates, but care should be taken to take all factors into account before proceeding. In our patient with a retained wire in the carotid artery, an open approach was selected because the wire had been present for over a year and became embedded in the wall of the artery. The procedure was performed safely with no notable complications.
