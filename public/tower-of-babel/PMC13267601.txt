Quantification of mitral regurgitation: from traditional methods to artificial intelligence
Authors: Kenneth Cho, Jimmy Su, Odile Bonnefous, Sandeep Prabhu, Chris Anthony
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mitral regurgitation (MR) is a common valvular disorder and associated with adverse outcomes. Echocardiography is the primary imaging modality for MR assessment; however, standard methods rely on geometric assumptions and single-frame analysis, which are especially inaccurate in the setting of eccentric, multiple or non-holosystolic jets. Recent developments in machine learning have enabled automated quantification of MR, which analyse regurgitant flow throughout systole, account for non-hemispheric orifice area and jet morphology, and demonstrate favourable agreement with cardiac magnetic resonance. In addition, AI capable of detecting and grading MR directly from echocardiographic clips offer potential utility for screening, particularly in low-resource settings where specialist review is limited. Quantification of Mitral Regurgitation: from traditional methods to Artificial Intelligence

Background

Mitral regurgitation (MR) is the most common valvular heart disease in the United States, the second most common in Europe, and affects approximately 3% to 6% of the global population [1, 2]. Even in the setting of normal left ventricular function, moderate and severe MR is associated with excess mortality [3]. Thus, accurate and reproducible evaluation is essential for diagnosis and monitoring to allow correct and timely treatment, with echocardiography as the foundational investigation due to its high accessibility [4, 5].

Traditional methods to quantify MR

Echocardiographic MR assessment has traditionally relied on qualitative, semi-quantitative, and quantitative measures. Among these, the effective regurgitant orifice area (EROA) and regurgitant volume (RVol) are most predictive of clinical outcomes [4], and are typically derived using the PISA method [4]. However, despite its widespread use in the clinical setting and landmark trials [5–7], the PISA measurement is prone to several shortcomings: (1) the underestimation of severity in the setting of multiple or eccentric jets, (2) the reliance on a single snapshot during the cardiac cycle, and (3) the assumption of a circular-shaped orifice. These limitations are crucial since, a single central jet, with a circular orifice, is rarely present in reality [8, 9]. Other conventional measurements such as color flow of the regurgitant jet, continuous wave regurgitant jet profile, and pulmonary vein flow are simple, easily acquired parameters, but are also susceptible to inaccuracies with eccentric MR. Artificial intelligence for more accurate MR quantification

Deep learning for automatic segmentation of 2D PISA

Recent advances in artificial intelligence and deep learning frameworks have allowed automated and semi-automated tools to address the limitations of an assumed single spherical holosystolic MR jet. Automated measurements of PISA using deep learning allow real-time multi-frame PISA measurements, which had been limited in clinical practice due to the need for manual measurements in every frame [10–13]. A deep learning framework, EasyPISA, published in 2024, demonstrated that multi-frame automatic segmentation of the flow convergence region from 2-D color-doppler without hemispherical assumptions was possible, with strong agreement with manual TTE and CMR MR severity assessment [14]. However, this proof of concept application was validated in a retrospective cohort, is not commercially nor widely available, with a plan for prospective testing [14].

Automatic segmentation of 3D PISA

3D PISA has several advantages over 2D color doppler – namely improved image quality leading to increased accuracy, particularly in MR that is eccentric, significant or with an asymmetrical orifice, where the geometric assumptions are broken [15]. Automated 3D PISA detection has been shown to require minimal 3D software analysis training, reduced time spent performing measures, and with good reproducibility potentially reducing inter-operator variability even among less experienced operators [11, 16]. Furthermore, the software’s short postprocessing time — limited to a few minutes — makes it relatively practical for routine clinical use. Although initially available through Siemen’s eSie PISA™, this is no longer widely available.

A new technique that has been introduced recently is 3D Auto Color Flow Quantification (3D Auto CFQ, Philips) and has been validated against cardiac MRI (CMR) [17]. 3D Auto CFQ does not use a single baseline shifted color velocity, nor does it assume a single, circular orifice, which results in a perfectly hemispherical isovelocity shell. In fact, the 3D Auto CFQ algorithm does not make geometric assumptions about the size, shape or constancy of the orifice. The fluid model for 3D Auto CFQ is based on the Navier-Stokes equations for describing the motion of fluid in space and time. In the method, the dynamic color 3D jet is segmented from the time sequence of stabilized 3D ultrasound images.

The mathematical morphology is then used to estimate the average orientation of the jet over time (i.e. over the systolic frames of the time sequence of 3D ultrasound images). This guides the search for the orifice for each image independently.

A dynamic surface model is fit to the valve model based on the segmented dynamic jet. The fitting of the surface model includes applying an analytic geometric regression to the segmented jet. The algorithm locates the mitral valve along the previously found jet directions, as determined by the segmentation and uses analytic geometric regression to fit a surface model. Using this dynamic surface model, a dynamic regurgitant orifice is identified across the time sequence of stabilized 3D ultrasound images.

The 4D ultrasound Doppler color data is used to search through the color volume along the valve surface, as defined by the dynamic surface model, to identify one or more regurgitant orifices.

Finally, a flow convergence model is dynamically fit to the time sequence of stabilized 3D ultrasound images based on the location and the color data surrounding the identified dynamic regurgitant orifice(s). The overall model is based on a convolution of many, smaller pinhole flow models whose flow contributions are summed together to equal the overall flow in the color dataset.

A regurgitant flow can then be estimated based on the identified regurgitant orifice and time sequence of stabilized 3D ultrasound images. In this way, several orifices, or a complex orifice with various shapes (such as slit orifice) are handled by the method. Thus, the method is not tied to the pinhole, circular, orifice assumption that is made in the current standard-of-care PISA method, with validation work including data from primary and secondary MR, single and multiple jets, as well as concentric and eccentric jets [17, 18]. Step 1: Image acquisition

Accurate quantification of MR using 3D Auto-CFQ begins with the acquisition of a high-quality 3D color Doppler dataset. Because the software analyses dynamic temporal changes in flow throughout systole, a volume rate of ≥ 20 Hz is essential to ensure sufficient temporal resolution. If higher frame rates are difficult to achieve —such as in patients with arrhythmia or rapid heart rates—multi-beat acquisition can be used to achieve enhanced temporal resolution. A high color Doppler scale is also critical, with a minimum Nyquist limit of ~ 70–75 cm/s is recommended. This can be achieved through reducing color frequency to 3.3 MHz. Suboptimal velocity settings—particularly with low color scales—can lead to signal saturation, aliasing, and underestimation of regurgitant flow. To support quality assurance, the software provides a data adequacy indicator (Fig. 2), which alerts the operator to suboptimal volume rate or aliasing velocity settings, enabling real-time adjustments to support adequate image acquisition quality. Step 2: Mitral valve model creation

Within the software tool, a dynamic 3D greyscale model of the mitral valve is created, which provides the framework for identifying the regurgitant orifice. The software provides automated landmark detection of the annulus, anterior and posterior leaflets, and coaptation zone, but can be adjusted by the operator if required. The static model defines the valve structure at a single reference point, whilst dynamic model captures valve motion throughout systole.

Step 3: Flow review

Once the mitral valve model is created, 3D Auto CFQ displays the regurgitant flow within the 3D color Doppler volume. The software also enables exclusion of confounding signals such as aortic outflow. If misalignment, inaccuracies or artefact is detected, the operator may return to previous steps to refine annular landmarks, leaflet tracking, or dynamic motion before proceeding. This review ensures model accuracy before final quantification of RVol.

Step 4: Flow analysis

After confirming accuracy of the model, the software performs automated quantification of MR volume (Flow Analysis). In this quantification, each 3D color doppler frame is analysed as “individual pinholes” of regurgitation, which are then summed to create the total regurgitant volume (RVol, in mL). Thus, the size, shape and number of regurgitant orifices are not assumed but accounted for in the model.

Limitations of 3d Auto CFQ

The current 3D MR flow quantification software has been shown to provide a smaller RVol, compared to 2D PISA [18, 19]. Therefore, the optimal cutoff value for severe MR may differ from those established with other methods, thus future studies are still needed for better validation.

Other uses of AI for MR assessment

AI deep learning frameworks have also been developed to identify MR without cardiologist assessment. These deep learning frameworks simply identify the presence and severity of MR on 4-chamber and/or 2-chamber TTE clips, generally relying solely on qualitative measures based on color Doppler videos [20–23]. Thus, rather than a more accurate MR diagnosis, these tools may be useful for MR screening. This may be especially relevant in resource-poor settings, where cardiologist review of all echocardiographic images may not be feasible, to identify patients who would benefit from further evaluation using established quantitative methods, such as measuring EROA and RVol.

Incorporating AI into clinical workflow

Given AI models have been shown to have low inter-observer and intra-observer variability, incorporating automated measurements can assist the clinician by acting as a “second check” in challenging cases, and assist in monitoring disease stability or progression over time. In addition to accuracy and reproducibility, streamline workflows by reducing reporting time.

Conclusion

The use of artificial intelligence for the quantification of MR, is time-efficient and addresses the false geometric and temporal assumptions intrinsic to traditional techniques. In particular, 3D Auto CFQ enables automated frame-by-frame assessment of MR, improving accuracy and reproducibility, and potentially reducing operator dependency whilst still maintaining workflow efficiency. Although early data show strong concordance with cardiac magnetic resonance, current algorithms tend to yield smaller regurgitant volumes than conventional PISA-based approaches. Thus, whilst integration of AI-based quantification into routine echocardiographic practice may improve diagnostic accuracy, and enable improved longitudinal monitoring in MR patients, further studies are required to validate these measurements.
