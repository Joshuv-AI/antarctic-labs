MetaServe: a lightweight, metadata-aware governance and delivery layer for pre-publication research omics data
Authors: Yizhuo Shen, Jianpeng Sheng, Pengcheng Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInstitutional research teams and core facilities routinely manage pre-publication omics datasets that span heterogeneous file types, nested project structures, and multiple downstream uses. Public repositories mainly support post-publication dissemination, while workflow systems and enterprise data platforms do not directly provide a lightweight governance and delivery layer for internal research assets.ResultsWe present MetaServe, an open-source governance and delivery layer for pre-publication research assets in institutional multi-omics settings. MetaServe registers and delivers heterogeneous assets, including sequencing files, processed matrices, imaging data, analysis-ready objects, tabular files, and documents, without requiring repository-grade standardization. Its metadata-aware design combines file-type recognition, partial automatic extraction for selected formats, manually supplied project and biological annotations, and indexed faceted retrieval. MetaServe supports authenticated web download, viewer-oriented handoff for compatible services such as cellxgene, and path-manifest export for downstream workflows under shared-storage assumptions. The current implementation combines role-based controls, explicit file-level sharing, path-constrained delivery, and operational traceability to support controlled institutional access. MetaServe has been deployed at the Chinese Institutes for Medical Research (CIMR) as part of an institutional multi-omics data-management system.ConclusionsMetaServe provides a practical layer between institutional storage and downstream analytical platforms for pre-publication research data. Its contribution is the integration of lightweight metadata-aware registration, permission-aware retrieval, and controlled delivery for heterogeneous institutional omics assets. Rather than replacing workflow engines, public repositories, or enterprise-scale research data platforms, MetaServe offers a deployable governance layer for core facilities and collaborative teams that need structured discovery and traceable delivery before public deposition or manuscript release.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12859-026-06522-z.

Introduction

Institutional core facilities and collaborative research teams increasingly manage pre-publication research assets before public deposition or manuscript release. In multi-omics settings, these assets include raw sequencing files (FASTQ, FASTA), alignment and variant outputs (BAM, VCF), processed matrices, imaging data (PNG, JPG, TIFF), analysis-ready objects such as AnnData or Seurat files (H5AD, RDS), and associated tabular or documentary files (CSV, PDF). Managing these heterogeneous assets is not only a storage problem but also a governance problem: files must remain metadata-discoverable, access-controlled at project and user levels, and usable by downstream analytical tools.

The FAIR principles (Findability, Accessibility, Interoperability, Reusability) define the normative standard for research data governance [1], and prior work has documented the gap between these principles and routine institutional practice [2]. Public repositories such as NCBI SRA, NCBI GEO, and TCGA provide essential infrastructure for disseminating published datasets [3–5], but their main role is public deposition, accessioning, and long-term reuse after data are ready for release. They are not designed for the active pre-publication phase, when files are repeatedly revised, selectively shared among changing collaborators, and delivered to internal analysis environments. MetaServe therefore does not reproduce repository-grade submission schemas or archive infrastructure; it addresses the earlier institutional phase in which research assets must remain discoverable, permission-controlled, and deliverable within local collaborations.

Workflow management systems, including Galaxy, GenePattern, Nextflow, and Snakemake, support computational reproducibility and pipeline portability [6, 7]. Their main abstraction is analysis execution rather than governance of heterogeneous institutional assets before analysis. In routine use, they often assume that files have already been located, described, permissioned, and staged for computation. Broader research data management frameworks such as iRODS, DataLad, Globus, and dtool provide stronger support for organization, transfer, provenance, or distributed management [8, 9], but they can impose administrative overhead that small institutional teams or core facilities often require dedicated administrative support, more complex configuration, or higher operational overhead. MetaServe is positioned between these categories: it is neither a workflow engine nor a full research data management framework, but a lightweight layer for metadata-based discovery, controlled sharing, and delivery of pre-publication assets stored in institutional infrastructure.

Surveys of institutional data practice show that researchers still rely on shared network drives, spreadsheets, and informal communication to coordinate pre-publication datasets [10]. These ad hoc workflows create recurring failure modes: inconsistent or missing metadata, files retrievable only by their creators or by path knowledge, access granted without traceability, and downstream tools supplied through manual transfer rather than programmatic interfaces. These risks directly affect reproducibility, data integrity, and inter-team collaboration.

MetaServe was developed to address this operational gap. It targets the pre-publication lifecycle in which heterogeneous files must be registered, described, selectively shared, and delivered to downstream tools before public repository deposition. MetaServe is not intended to replace workflow engines, public repositories, or certified clinical data-management platforms. Its design has three practical goals: lightweight metadata-aware registration and faceted retrieval across heterogeneous omics-related assets; controlled access and resource-level sharing within institutional collaborations; and flexible delivery through authenticated download, viewer-oriented handoff, and manifest-based downstream consumption under explicit deployment assumptions.

This paper describes the architecture (Fig. 1) and implementation of MetaServe as a lightweight governance and delivery layer for institutional pre-publication multi-omics data. System overview and implementation

Metadata registration and structured retrieval

When a file is uploaded or registered into a project workspace, MetaServe links it to a structured record containing project context, ownership, access level, file type, storage location, and user-supplied biological or technical annotations. This record supports structured metadata search and faceted filtering, reducing reliance on filesystem path navigation.

MetaServe supports access to public datasets from repositories such as GEO and NCBI. When needed, users can retrieve selected public datasets and register them as managed resources within the system. The downloaded files can then be incorporated into the same metadata, access control, and delivery framework as locally generated data.

Here, "metadata-aware" is used in a bounded sense. MetaServe provides format-aware registration for heterogeneous research assets and partial field-aware extraction for selected file classes; it is not a repository-grade metadata framework comparable to GEO or SRA. The metadata layer combines file-extension and header-based type recognition, lightweight automatic extraction where stable fields can be obtained at low cost, and manually supplied project, sample, assay, and access annotations entered during registration or curation.

File-type identification combines extension mapping with lightweight inspection of file headers or leading file segments, using established specifications for common bioinformatics formats [11–13]. The current scope of automatic metadata extraction is summarized in Table S1.

This strategy is deliberate. Exhaustive full-file validation would add unnecessary I/O cost for routine institutional workspaces, whereas header-level or leading-segment inspection is sufficient for many governance tasks, including file-class distinction, faceted search, and delivery routing. Reliably parsed format-specific fields are stored as structured metadata and can be promoted to indexed filters. When parsing is unreliable, MetaServe registers the file as a managed asset and relies on user-supplied annotations for biological interpretation. This distinction matters: MetaServe can register, permission, search, and deliver more file types than it currently supports for automatic extraction.

Retrieval performance relies on relational indexing and query optimization in PostgreSQL, without an external search engine. This design matches the expected scale of institutional pre-publication workspaces, where project collections often contain hundreds to low thousands of files rather than millions of records. The metadata layer is therefore deployable and lightweight, but not exhaustive: malformed files, non-standard variants, and unsupported classes may require manual curation or future extractor extensions.

It should be noted that support for file registration does not imply complete automatic metadata extraction; as summarized in Table S1, extraction coverage varies across file types and often requires manual annotation for domain-specific attributes.

MetaServe operates as a lightweight governance and delivery layer on top of institutional storage systems. Files remain in place under shared storage, while metadata extraction enables faceted search and controlled access. Data delivery is implemented via permission-filtered paths or manifests, which can be consumed by downstream tools or environments.

Delivery modes for downstream use

MetaServe provides three delivery modes, each preserving permission evaluation while reducing unnecessary data movement when deployment conditions allow it. The modes share one authorization layer but differ in how selected assets are made available to users or downstream tools.

The first mode is authenticated web download. Registered files can be delivered to local workstations for ad hoc analysis, inspection, or controlled internal sharing. Before delivery, MetaServe evaluates the requesting user, file record, project context, and any resource-level sharing rule. Delivery records or application logs can then support reconstruction of who accessed which asset and when, depending on deployment logging.

The second mode is viewer-oriented handoff. For compatible viewers such as cellxgene for H5AD files, MetaServe resolves a registered asset and makes it available through a deployment-specific publish or path-handoff mechanism. This is a controlled handoff pattern, not a general viewer integration framework. It depends on local configuration, supported file type, viewer availability, and viewer access to the relevant storage location or published directory.

The third mode is manifest-based downstream handoff. For HPC or workflow environments that can access the same institutional filesystem, MetaServe generates a permission-filtered manifest of resolved paths for a selected file set. Workflow managers such as Nextflow or Snakemake can consume this manifest as an input list, while MetaServe remains outside workflow execution. If compute nodes cannot resolve the same paths, transfer, staging, or path mapping must be handled outside MetaServe. This mode is therefore governed path-manifest export, not workflow scheduling, HPC integration, or file transfer.

Security design and audit strategy

MetaServe is designed for institutional environments that need controlled internal sharing of pre-publication assets before public release. Its security model has a bounded operational scope: reducing accidental or unauthorized access within a shared organization, limiting oversharing across projects or collaborators, preventing delivery outside registered storage boundaries, and preserving traceability for routine governance events. It is not a comprehensive security framework, a substitute for server hardening or institutional identity management, or a validated compliance platform for regulated clinical deployment.

Access control model

Access decisions combine role-based control with resource-level sharing rules (Fig. 2). Each file asset has a visibility level such as Public, Internal, Private, or Restricted, and authorization is refined by organizational membership, project context, ownership, and explicit file-level sharing. In the current implementation, enforcement is primarily file-resource based. Folder visibility and file delivery permission are related but distinct: a collaborator may access a specifically shared file without seeing the parent folder or sibling assets. This supports restricted asset-level collaboration but is not a strict hierarchical most-restrictive policy in which every parent-folder restriction overrides an explicit child-file share. Delivery boundary enforcement

File delivery is permission-constrained rather than direct filesystem exposure. Delivery requests are resolved against registered storage locations, and path inputs are canonicalized against the registered root prefix to reject traversal sequences or unregistered paths. This boundary applies to authenticated download and manifest generation. For viewer handoff and downstream workflows, MetaServe authorizes the registered asset or file set before returning a handoff response or path manifest; subsequent access by viewers, HPC jobs, or workflow engines depends on deployment-specific shared-storage configuration and remains outside MetaServe’s execution boundary.

Operational event recording and traceability

MetaServe supports traceability through permission-mediated application actions, delivery-related task records, database object state, and server-side logs. These mechanisms can reconstruct representative events such as delivery, explicit sharing, revocation, or denied delivery requests, depending on deployment configuration and operation type. In the current implementation, traceability should not be interpreted as a unified, independently queryable, tamper-evident audit subsystem covering every search, manifest, viewer-publish, sharing, or delivery operation. It is therefore an operational governance mechanism rather than evidence of formal security auditing, attack prevention, or regulatory compliance.

Compliance limitations

The current implementation has not undergone external security audit, independent penetration testing, or certification against GDPR, HIPAA, or equivalent frameworks. It should not be treated as a validated compliance framework for regulated clinical, diagnostic, or highly sensitive contexts. Such deployments would require additional measures, including encryption at rest, managed key rotation, centralized identity integration through LDAP or SSO, retention and backup policies, disaster-recovery procedures, and institutional governance review. Strict hierarchical inheritance of folder restrictions would also require parent-policy enforcement beyond the file-level sharing behavior validated here.

Implementation overview

MetaServe is implemented as a web application with a Python Django REST backend and a Vue 3 single-page frontend, exposing RESTful interfaces for integration with institutional services. The architecture separates metadata registration and retrieval, permission evaluation, and downstream delivery, so permission logic is applied consistently across delivery modes. The system is packaged as a single-node Docker Compose deployment to reduce prerequisites. PostgreSQL is recommended for production deployments serving multiple concurrent projects and was used in the CIMR deployment. The interface includes a project dashboard, workspace file management, metadata-enriched preview pages, faceted search and filtering, and organization-level role management (Fig. 3). These choices keep the operational footprint suitable for core facilities without dedicated research computing staff while preserving integration points for viewers, workflow systems, and identity providers.Fig. 3MetaServe web interface for file management and organization governance. Illustrative use case: multi-omics data governance

To ground the system description, we describe an illustrative collaborative oncology project with three experimental arms from the same patient cohort: bulk RNA-seq for differential expression and variant analysis, single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) for cell-type deconvolution, and spatial transcriptomics for tissue-level localization of transcriptional programs. The scenario clarifies roles, permission behavior, metadata retrieval, and delivery modes in an institutional setting. It is illustrative rather than a measured end-to-end case study. The combination reflects multi-omics projects in core-facility settings such as CIMR, where one biological question generates heterogeneous file classes across multiple analytical stages.

The bulk RNA-seq data include raw paired-end files (FASTQ), alignment outputs (BAM and BAM indexes), and germline and somatic variant calls (VCF). The scRNA-seq data, processed with the 10 × Genomics Chromium platform, include filtered feature-barcode matrices converted to AnnData objects (H5AD) after quality control, normalization, and dimensionality reduction in Scanpy. The spatial transcriptomics data, generated with the 10 × Visium HD platform, include high-resolution tissue images (TIFF), spatial gene-expression matrices, and Visium-format HDF5 outputs. The workspace also contains tabular summaries (CSV), analysis reports (PDF), and pipeline configuration records. Overall, it spans eight format classes, illustrating the heterogeneity that motivates format-aware metadata extraction and faceted retrieval.

User roles and access structure

Four user roles interact with the workspace. The facility administrator configures the workspace, registers storage locations, and assigns organization-level roles. The project data manager registers files, assigns ingestion metadata, sets per-asset access levels, and monitors delivery events. Internal project members have project-scoped read access and use metadata search, filtered retrieval, and programmatic manifests for pipeline execution. An external collaborator, a pathologist at a partner hospital, has explicitly granted read access only to spatial transcriptomics TIFF files and associated Visium matrices, with no visibility into bulk RNA-seq or scRNA-seq assets.

This role structure exercises MetaServe’s access-control stack: organization-level membership, project-scoped permissions, asset-level visibility classification, and explicit file-level sharing for external collaborators. The collaborator’s access is limited to explicitly shared assets, while unrelated bulk RNA-seq and scRNA-seq files remain inaccessible. Because the current implementation treats parent-folder visibility and file-level sharing as distinct controls, the scenario demonstrates asset-level sharing rather than strict inherited hierarchical permission enforcement.

Workflow scenarios and delivery modes

Scenario A: bulk RNA-seq variant handoff to an HPC workflow

A bioinformatician needs a filtered set of VCF files for a specified sample subset in a variant-annotation workflow on the institutional HPC cluster. The analyst submits a MetaServe query by file type, project identifier, and sample identifiers. MetaServe evaluates the permission boundary and returns a permission-filtered manifest with registered filesystem paths for the selected files. If the HPC environment resolves the same shared-storage paths, the workflow can consume the manifest without manual path transcription or file copying. MetaServe does not schedule or execute the workflow; it provides governed manifest generation at the boundary between institutional storage and downstream computation.

Scenario B: single-cell AnnData interactive exploration

A project member requests interactive cell-type annotation of a processed H5AD object using a compatible viewer such as cellxgene. The analyst submits a viewer-oriented handoff request through MetaServe. MetaServe verifies read permission for the registered H5AD asset and uses the deployment-specific handoff mechanism to make the dataset available to the viewer service, for example by resolving a shared-storage path or publishing the file into a viewer-accessible directory. The delivery or publish event remains associated with the requesting user and asset record. This scenario demonstrates controlled handoff for a compatible format, not a general-purpose viewer framework.

Scenario C: spatial transcriptomics delivery to an external collaborator

The external pathologist needs high-resolution TIFF tissue images and Visium expression matrices for morphological review. The project data manager confirms that the relevant assets are Private at the project level and creates an explicit ACL entry granting read access to this subset. The collaborator authenticates through MetaServe and retrieves the shared TIFF or HDF5 assets through the controlled delivery interface. The sharing rule exposes the restricted subset without exposing unrelated bulk RNA-seq or scRNA-seq assets and without necessarily exposing the parent folder. Query and download events are recorded according to the deployment’s logging configuration, and unrelated assay arms remain outside the collaborator’s search results.

Operational observations

The scenario highlights three design properties. First, all delivery modes use the same permission-evaluation layer, so access decisions are independent of whether an asset is downloaded, handed off to a viewer, or exported as a manifest. Second, resource-level sharing lets an external collaborator access a restricted subset without duplicating files into a separate staging workspace. Third, manifest handoff allows downstream workflows to consume governed file selections under shared-storage assumptions without making MetaServe part of workflow execution.

These observations are qualitative and scenario-based; they explain MetaServe’s operational role and should be interpreted separately from the quantitative benchmarks in the evaluation section.

Tasks T1 and T2 represent backend query and retrieval latency, whereas T3 includes viewer handoff preparation and initialization of compatible services (e.g., cellxgene). As such, T3 should not be interpreted as general API latency.

Because MetaServe is a governance and delivery layer rather than a workflow-execution system, the benchmark focused on operations at the boundary between data governance and downstream use: metadata discovery, manifest generation, viewer-handoff preparation, explicit sharing, authorization-boundary behavior, path-boundary rejection, and nested folder/file sharing behavior.

The benchmark separated response-latency measurements from functional validation. Timing was applied only to T1–T3. For metadata search, timing began when the application received the search request and ended when the response returned. For manifest generation, timing covered request receipt through manifest response availability. For viewer-oriented handoff, timing covered H5AD publish request receipt through handoff-preparation response. These timings exclude manual UI navigation, authentication, initial data registration, file upload or transfer, downstream viewer rendering, HPC scheduling, workflow execution, and downstream analysis.

T4–T7 were evaluated as functional or implementation-boundary checks. They covered explicit sharing, revocation, owner and internal-member access, external-collaborator access, viewer-handoff denial for unauthorized users, rejection of a path-traversal probe, and the relationship between parent-folder visibility and direct child-file sharing. Traceability was evaluated conservatively through application-visible outcomes, database object state, server-side logs, and available delivery-related task records; the benchmark did not assume a unified, independently queryable audit subsystem for all operation classes.

Functional validation and implementation-boundary findings

Metadata search and manifest generation completed within tens of milliseconds: median latencies were 14.78 ms for T1 and 7.14 ms for T2, with narrow IQRs of 1.50 ms and 0.69 ms, respectively. These results indicate that indexed metadata lookup and path-manifest construction add little application-level overhead in the tested deployment. Viewer-oriented H5AD handoff was substantially slower, with a median latency of 9.85 s and a P95 latency of 10.37 s, because T3 included cellxgene-related preparation and startup behavior. Thus, T3 should be interpreted as viewer publish or handoff-preparation latency, not as general API lookup latency or downstream viewer rendering time.

Authorization and delivery-boundary validation

We then evaluated representative authorization and delivery-boundary cases using expected-versus-observed functional checks, with the complete implementation-boundary findings compiled in Table 4. The aim was not penetration testing or formal security certification, but verification that implemented behavior matched the bounded governance model in section “Security design and audit strategy” for common institutional access scenarios.

To provide minimal validation of the access-control model, we constructed representative test cases covering different user roles and asset visibility levels, comparing expected and observed behavior.

The functional evaluation confirms that MetaServe accurately enforces explicit sharing, access revocation, and role-based visibility restrictions, including the successful denial of unauthorized viewer handoffs and path-traversal attempts. Furthermore, the nested directory scenarios validate the system’s reliance on file-resource-level access control, which effectively enables precise, asset-level collaboration without exposing parent directory structures. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the system fulfills its primary design objectives as a governed, metadata-aware delivery layer, achieving a robust balance between controlled institutional data sharing and operational flexibility.

Implementation-boundary observations

The benchmark identified several boundaries that shape interpretation. First, the T1 metadata search endpoint behaved as an owner-scoped search path in the tested implementation. With the same filter, the owner context returned five benchmark files, whereas internal member, external collaborator, and outsider contexts returned zero. Internal access and explicit sharing were validated through listing and download behavior, but cross-user exposure of shared assets through the faceted search endpoint was not demonstrated. T1 is therefore interpreted as owner/data-steward metadata discovery rather than validated cross-user permission-filtered search.

Second, the benchmark did not observe independent structured audit records for search, manifest generation, cellxgene publish, or FileShare create/delete operations. Operational visibility in this run came from application responses, database object state, server-side logs, and the download-job design rather than a unified audit API. Traceability should therefore be described as operational logging and task/state observability, not as a complete formally audited logging subsystem.

Third, manifest generation returned host-resolved absolute paths. These manifests can support downstream workflows when compute nodes resolve the same storage paths, but this is a shared-storage deployment assumption rather than an HPC integration guarantee provided by MetaServe.

Finally, direct file-level sharing made a child asset accessible while the parent folder remained invisible to the collaborator. This behavior is useful for selective sharing, but strict parent-policy inheritance should be described as a future extension or deployment-specific requirement rather than a validated property of the current implementation.

Discussion

The evaluation should be interpreted as an operational characterization of MetaServe, not as evidence of general performance superiority over workflow systems. In the controlled benchmark, owner/data-steward metadata search and manifest generation showed low application-level latency, with medians of 14.78 ms and 7.14 ms. Viewer-oriented H5AD handoff was slower, with a median of 9.85 s, because it included cellxgene preparation and startup rather than simple metadata lookup. These measurements exclude manual UI navigation, file transfer, downstream viewer rendering, HPC scheduling, workflow execution, and downstream analysis, so they characterize the MetaServe application boundary rather than the full user workflow.

The functional validation is more informative than raw latency for MetaServe’s intended role. Explicit sharing allowed an external collaborator to access a restricted TIFF asset only after a FileShare rule was created, and access was denied again after revocation. Unauthorized requests were hidden or rejected, and a path-traversal probe was rejected before delivery. The nested folder/file case clarified that parent-folder invisibility does not automatically block delivery of a directly shared child file; this demonstrates asset-level sharing rather than strict hierarchical inheritance. The central conclusion of this evaluation is that MetaServe successfully provides a deployable governance boundary for registered assets, controlled delivery, and manifest-based downstream consumption under shared-storage assumptions. Functional positioning of MetaServe relative to adjacent systems

This study has several limitations. First, the empirical evaluation is based on one institutional setting and a controlled local benchmark; broader evaluation across deployments, storage configurations, and project scales is needed before general performance claims can be made. Second, the metadata-aware layer is intentionally lightweight: it supports format-aware registration and partial automatic extraction for selected file types, but not repository-grade schema enforcement, exhaustive validation, or complete biological curation. Third, the security model targets routine institutional governance rather than formal compliance. Although MetaServe implements permission checks, explicit file-level sharing, revocation, delivery-boundary checks, and operational traceability mechanisms, it has not undergone independent security audit or regulatory certification. Fourth, the current implementation does not enforce strict parent-folder most-restrictive inheritance for directly shared child files. Fifth, viewer and workflow handoff depend on deployment assumptions, especially shared storage and compatible downstream services.

Future development will focus on capabilities aligned with this scope: expanding format-specific metadata extraction, improving validation for non-standard file variants, integrating institutional identity providers, and refining viewer and manifest handoff across storage configurations. Compliance-oriented extensions, including encryption at rest, key management, formal retention policies, and expanded audit infrastructure, may be added for environments that require them. These remain future deployment-specific extensions rather than validated capabilities of the current release.
