Introduction

Productivity of crops can be impacted by the quality of weeds removed from fields before crop emergence when plants compete for resources (e.g. nutrient uptake, light, water). Manual removal of weeds can be labor intensive and is not practical for widespread use. As such, researchers and scientists are interested in using computerized or automated visual inspection methods to identify weeds growing among crops1. Recently, CNN’s have been shown to perform well in classifying plants/weeds using images collected of crops2. They excel in recognizing hierarchically organized spatial patterns. However, architectural designs used for training CNNs have an important impact on their performance2,3. One aspect of CNN architecture that has a major effect on performance is the depth of networks. Deeper networks allow the model to recognize much finer grain features and therefore, can distinguish very closely related weed species growing together during the early growth stages4. A disadvantage of using deeper architectures is the potential for greater computational costs, vanishing gradients and overfitting, which may make them impractical to use for real-time processing in low power devices typically used for precision agriculture5.

Lightweight CNNs have become popular due to their ability to provide high levels of accuracy while also being computationally inexpensive. However, several studies have found that some lightweight models sacrifice their ability to represent the features of interest when trying to reduce their size and/or complexity. Many conventional CNN based methods tend to only learn local features from the data and do not possess the ability to capture the longer-range dependencies or the global relationships between objects in the image. These types of relationships are critical for developing robust methods for classifying images captured in real world field settings.

Hybrid architectures3,4 that combine CNNs with attention mechanisms, such as transformers, are emerging as one method to overcome these limitations. Transformers enable CNNs to process and recognize global interactions through self-attention, which complements CNN’s capabilities for processing and recognizing local features. Channel attention mechanisms, such as squeeze-excitation (SE) blocks5, further improve the discriminatory abilities of features learned by CNNs by dynamically adjusting channel-wise response values. While these recent developments6 show great promise, there remains a need to develop architectures that effectively integrate the strengths of CNNs for learning depth-wise features, attention mechanisms for capturing global contextual relationships and computational efficiency into a single lightweight architecture.

This research introduces a new lightweight CNN-SE-Transformer architecture designed to systematically investigate how CNN depths affect computation efficiency. The proposed architecture utilizes residual connections to facilitate stable feature learning at multiple scales and to mitigate the effects of vanishing gradients, allowing for the effective investigation of larger CNN depths without sacrificing too much performance. The SE module allows the model to emphasize channel-wise feature representations that are useful for discriminating weeds. Meanwhile, the transformer encoder enables the model to process and extract global contextual relationships through its token representation mechanism. Unlike traditional methods that create redundant tokens, our architecture minimizes token redundancy, thus allowing efficient modeling of global relationships with minimal computation expense.

Experiments were conducted to fully evaluate our model on two benchmark weed classification datasets consisting of 3-class and 16-class categorizations. We also analyzed three different optimization methods (Adam, RMSProp and Adam with label smoothing) to determine how they affected convergence behavior and overall generalization of our model. Finally, we utilized Grad-CAM-based explanation to demonstrate whether our model was able to focus on areas of an image relevant to identifying weeds.

Literature review

Recent years have seen substantial progress in deep learning for weed detection in sorghum and other crops. Rahman et al.7 developed an automated weed and crop recognition system using deep learning optimized with Lemrus Optimization, achieving high performance across diverse field conditions. Sharma et al.8 introduced a multi-scale feature-enhanced DenseNet for sorghum weed classification, improving resolution-aware detection but facing challenges in cluttered backgrounds and visually similar weeds.

Semantic segmentation approaches have also shown effectiveness. Lee et al.9 designed a two-stage segmentation pipeline to separate tobacco and weed pixels in UAV imagery, achieving improved localization accuracy. Zhang et al.10 implemented a multiclass semantic segmentation framework using encoder–decoder networks to distinguish multiple weed species. While these methods perform well, they require extensive pixel-level annotation, which is resource-intensive.

To enhance efficiency and adaptability, Li et al.11 proposed RLCFE-Net, a re-parameterized large convolutional kernel architecture capable of performing robustly across varied weed datasets. Nguyen et al.12 developed lightweight CNNs for real-time operation under challenging lighting, and Ahmed et al.13 leveraged attention-augmented Vision Transformers to capture both local and global features effectively. Hybrid CNN-Transformer models have also been employed in broader agricultural applications. Liang et al.14 applied such a network for plant disease and weed classification, achieving high accuracy by fusing local and global feature learning. Wang et al.15 provided an extensive survey on Vision Transformer applications in agriculture, detailing performance benchmarks and deployment strategies.

Finally, explainability is crucial for the practical adoption of AI in agriculture. Ghosh et al.16 used Grad-CAM + + to interpret CNN-Transformer outputs18–20, highlighting biologically relevant patterns such as leaf edges and venation, thus enhancing model transparency and agronomic trust. Our proposed CNN–Transformer–SE model builds upon these insights by combining multi-scale feature extraction, Transformer-based global context, SE channel recalibration, and Grad-CAM + + interpretability to deliver accurate and explainable sorghum weed classification. Methodology

Figure 1 illustrates how the structure of the proposed weed classification system will be organized. First, raw weed image(s) are acquired. Next, the images undergo an ordered, pre-processing method that improves image quality and provides standardized input. All images undergo resizing and normalization to assure consistent spatial dimensions and intensity values. To provide improved visibility of features, CLAHE contrast improvement and unsharp masking to refine edges are implemented to enable better distinction of subtle morphological characteristics across each weed class. Following this step, several data augmentation techniques (rotation, flipping, scaling, adjusting brightness) are performed on the processed dataset to increase the amount of diverse data available for use during model training, thereby enhancing generalization capabilities in various environmental conditions (i.e., field).

The proposed architecture of the model in detail flow.

Next, the processed dataset is randomly separated into two distinct groups; namely, a group intended for use during model parameter learning (training), and a second group intended for use to evaluate the model’s performance without bias (testing). At the heart of the proposed weed classification system is a novel, lightweight CNN-SE-Transformer architecture. A CNN backbone enhanced with residual connections is developed to extract hierarchical spatial features focusing specifically upon assessing the influence of increased CNN depth on feature representation. SE blocks are embedded within CNN layers to recalculate channel-wise feature response values, therefore allowing for the improved ability of the model to discriminate.

Lastly, the extracted spatial feature maps are transformed into token representations and subsequently passed through a Transformer encoder to assess global contextual relationships that are often overlooked by traditional CNNs. Thus, the proposed hybrid design offers the model the opportunity to incorporate both localized feature extraction methods combined with global attention mechanisms while maintaining computationally efficient processes. The model is trained using three different optimization techniques (Adam, RMSProp, Adam w/Label Smoothing) to evaluate both convergence behavior and stability when trained under multiple conditions. After the model has been successfully trained, it is evaluated based upon standard performance metrics and the decision-making process of the model is visually interpreted using Grad-CAM. The combination of preprocessing, augmentation, lightweight feature extraction and attention-based modeling ultimately provides a robust weed classification framework that can accurately classify weeds in real-world agricultural environments.

Dataset description

Dataset 1

This study employs three publicly available datasets to develop and evaluate the WeedVis hybrid deep learning model for weed classification in sorghum fields, ensuring robust assessment across diverse conditions from https://data.mendeley.com/datasets. The Sorghum WeedDataset Classification serves as the primary dataset, consisting of 4,312 high-resolution RGB images collected from sorghum fields under varying lighting and environmental conditions in link https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/4gkcyxjyss/1. Each image is labeled at the image level as one of three classes: sorghum, grass weed, or broadleaf weed, capturing different growth stages and weed infestation levels to provide realistic field scenarios for practical model training and evaluation. To further assess the model’s generalization and scalability, the MH-Weed16 dataset is utilized from https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/d3n3mgjjbv/1, containing 19,141 images representing 16 distinct weed species commonly observed in agricultural settings. The images exhibit substantial variability in plant appearance, background complexity, and illumination, challenging the model to distinguish closely related and visually similar species.

Contrast Enhancement Using Local Histogram Processing is second step. Field images often contain considerable amounts of light variation which creates contrast issues throughout the image. These types of issues can easily cause a machine learning-based system to fail when attempting to identify specific objects or features contained in the images. Therefore, we have implemented a method called Contrast-Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE), which provides localized adjustments to the contrast within each region of an image. Unlike traditional histogram equalization techniques that make global changes to an image’s contrast, CLAHE applies these same adjustments locally rather than globally. CLAHE also prevents excessive amplification of noise.

Where I(x,y) is the original pixel intensity, is the clipped cumulative distribution function of the tile’s histogram, and L is the number of possible intensity levels (256 for 8-bit images). The enhanced tiles are then bilinearly interpolated to avoid visible seams.

Specifically, our implementation converts each input RGB image to the LAB color space. The L-channel represents the luminance portion of the image while the AB channels represent the colors red-green and blue-yellow respectively. We apply CLAHE only to the L-channels and utilize a 2.0 clip limit and an 8 × 8 tile grid. Once applying CLAHE to the L-channels, we recombine them with their corresponding A/B-channels, convert them back to RGB-space and send the resulting image through the rest of our pipeline. Our use of CLAHE significantly improves the visibility of finer details in plant morphology such as veins and edges that help distinguish visually similar weed species.

Unsharp Masking is used next to provide additional refinement of the fine details and higher frequency components found in an image. Unsharp masking works by creating an image whose fine details will be amplified. This is accomplished by subtracting a blurred version of the image from its original version. After subtraction, we amplify the difference to accentuate those areas containing higher frequency information (i.e., finer texture). Since this type of information is very important in identifying weeds at their early stages where morphological differences may be almost non-existent, this step is important to emphasize this type of information in preprocessing.

Data augmentation

In order to obtain greater robustness and better generality of the proposed model, a wide-ranging data augmentation strategy has been developed for use on the training dataset after pre-processing. Because there exists great variability in agricultural field images caused by illumination, camera angle, plant orientation, scale and environment, augmentation is necessary in order to simulate realistic variations and allow the model to learn feature that are both invariant and discriminatory.

For this research, a large number of augmentations have been made via an online augmentation system. The specific geometric and photometric transformation used include rotation (± 45 degrees), to create invariance to plant orientation, since plants can be located anywhere in a field in reality. A 30% spatial shift of width and/or height was also added to create positional variability, so that the model would not depend on the location of features. Additionally, shears of 0.25 were created to represent slight geometric distortion or perspective variations that could exist when acquiring images.

A zoom operation of 25%, representing varying scales of growth in weed development as well as distance from the camera was also included. Horizontal and vertical flip operations have also been added to create even greater variability in the data and provide symmetry-based invariance; useful for plant structures that contain similar patterns under reflection. Photometric augmentation was then added to simulate the variation in illumination present in many outdoor agricultural environments through application of random brightness adjustment in the range of 0.7–1.2. It should be noted that the “fill mode” of the new pixel regions generated by all geometric transforms will always be set to “reflect”, thus avoiding the creation of visual inconsistencies or artificial artifacts in these areas. The generation of new training samples for each input image occurs dynamically during each iteration of training. As a result, the amount of new data created per iteration results in the total effective size of the training dataset being much larger than actual size and minimizes the likelihood of over fitting and maximizes the model’s generality. Proposed WeedVis architecture

The proposed model employs a light and efficient hybrid CNN-Transformer architecture for the task of accurate image classification. The network features a multi-stage convolutional backbone with three hierarchical blocks. Each of these three blocks has ascending channel dimensions for feature maps in the range of 32, 64, and 128. Each of these three blocks has convolutional layers that include batch normalization and a ReLU activation function in order to get the best feature extraction at a local level. In order to enhance the flow of gradients and help with the stabilization of training, the use of residual connections has been incorporated within the three hierarchical blocks, one per block (Fig. 6).

Also, the use of Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) Attention is incorporated after the completion of each one of the three convolutional blocks in order to address the selective focusing of one or a few features, and improve the discriminative quality of the features. The task of level of the network is to extract and identify high-level semantic features, and at the same time, maximize an increase in the network’s performance by means of computational minimalism.Before the use of the final layers of the network is implemented, the task of transforming the resulting feature maps is done. This has a major impact on the performance of the network. Once this is accomplished, the learned features can be pooled into a global average that has been further modified by the use of a dropout layer in order to minimize overfitting. This is done in order to achieve an Objective Function. All in all, the suggested architecture is able to use a minimal number of resources while still being able to localize and classify features in a highly efficient manner.

Discussion

It is shown in the experimental results the proposed architecture of the CNN-SE-Transformer is lightweight across both datasets. In regards to the first dataset (3-class classification), the model reaches high accuracy (up to 93%) showcasing great potential discriminating ability for simple classification tasks. In the training and validation curves there is stable convergence and less than average overfitting especially for Adam with label smoothing, overconfidence in the predictions is reduced which improves generalization.

On the contrary with the second dataset (16-class classification) a more complex model is proposed and it achieves a competitive outcome of 78% accuracy on the test with Adam and label smoothing, despite the class diversity and imbalance. This shows the gap of the performance of model in the first dataset to the second one is increased because of the imbalance class, with the increase in the similarity between classes, this confirms the high difficulty in weed classification in day-to-day agricultural environments, this is supported by the analysis of the confusion matrices.

There’s abundant evidence from ablation studies assessing the effectiveness of individual structural components. This is particularly true for the case of Block Transformers, which enhance structural feature representation globally, whereas with SE blocks this enhances structural representation with an individual degree. Thus, it can be stated that the residual connections enable stable training of the architecture, especially when the components are increased in depth. Additionally, with the increase of structural design components in the CNN Block, increasing from 3 to 10 individual components gives significant performance increases. This demonstrates that in combination with an efficient design, the removal of structural components is highly additive to overall performance.

The proposed CNN–Transformer model is lightweight in design and applies to multi-class classification of weeds and achieves stellar results. However, the biggest drawback of the current research is the reliance on RGB data, which only captures the visible light and can lose valuable data to fully capture the spectral dimensions of the data, especially when needing to identify weeds that are visually identical based on lighting and soil conditions and the growth stages. The recent literature find that multispectral information improves the performance of machine learning algorithm. The data RafanoSet (Rana et al., 2024)23 proposed to incorporate the Near Infrared (NIR) and RedEdge spectral bands, which are known to provide additional physiological and structural information of the crops. This allows to better separate the crops from weeds, especially in the presence of complex real agricultural scenes.

The proposed model performs well on RGB datasets, but generalization can be improved by adding multispectral data. In addition, this work will sweat architectural limitations by adding multi-channel spectral data to improve the reliability and scalability of weed detection through more varied agricultural conditions. The results from the trials show that from an optimization point of view, the test runs supported the conclusion that the Adam methods were better than the RMSprop methods. While the RMSprop methods were more consistent meaning lower the variance, it resulted in the lower average test accuracy and greater average test loss. In compared to the rest of the methods, the Adam methods achieved a best average test loss meaning the most optimal balance between the convergence and the performance.

The results of adding label smoothing with Adam showed a small improvement on the test accuracy and increased the generalization. The results of the test showed that the two methods created a statistically insignificant improvement p > 0.05, therefore, were fairly equal. Label smoothing decreased the confidence in the prediction that caused an improvement in the robustness of the worst-performing classes.

The proposed model maintains a light architecture and the computational effort does require more than average effort, and therefore, the speed of an inference is goal is less than ~ 18ms, meaning that it is suitable to be deployed in a real time situation. While the model is less than highly optimized ones like the ones based on the MobileNetV3 architecture, the model offers a nice tradeoff with other features like the SE attention with the convolutional feature extraction with the Transformer global context, meaning more than average computational effort.

Conclusion

The proposed lightweight CNN-SE-Transformer model is an innovative architectural development which combines the strengths of CNNs in localizing weeds from images, and the long-range dependencies of Transformers with the additional ability to capture contextual features through self-excitation (SE) mechanisms. A major advantage of this architecture is the ability to perform at high levels of accuracy while remaining computationally efficient enough for use in real-world agricultural settings. The experimental evaluation conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed lightweight CNN-SE-Transformer model was conducted on two different datasets which represented two differing levels of complexity. These results demonstrated that the lightweight CNN-SE-Transformer architecture was capable of producing accurate classification results regardless of the level of complexity inherent in the dataset used.

The proposed architecture produced very good classification results across all datasets tested; specifically, the highest level of accuracy achieved by the proposed model across all datasets tested was 93% for Dataset 1, and 78% for Dataset 2. An ablation study was performed to determine how much each of the components contributed to overall performance. Specifically, the impact of residual connections, SE blocks, and Transformer modules were determined to have a significant positive effect on improving performance. In addition, the proposed model also found that employing Adam with label smoothing enhanced generalizability and stability; especially when dealing with large disparities in data distributions (i.e., class imbalance).

In addition to achieving high levels of accuracy, the proposed architecture has a number of other advantages. One of these advantages is a low parameter count, and another advantage is fast inference times. Both are critical factors for many types of real-time agricultural applications such as precision farming and fully automated weed detection systems. Finally, the authors employed Grad-CAM to produce visualizations of their predictions. Visualizations provide valuable insights into why a particular prediction was made.

Future work can expand by exploring more advanced methods to balance the classes such as generating synthetic training samples using Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), or Diffusion Models. Another example includes developing more efficient versions of Transformers or developing alternative attention mechanisms to reduce computational requirements while enhancing performance. In addition, deploying the model in real-world field conditions on edge devices will help demonstrate its feasibility. More broadly, additional modalities including spectral (hyperspectral), temporal, or combinations thereof can potentially be integrated into the model to enhance classification accuracy in increasingly complex agricultural environments. Finally, adapting the model to detect and/or segment objects will allow for even more comprehensive solutions to support weed management.
