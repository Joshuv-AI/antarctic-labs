Abstract
We investigate the robustness of value alignment via midtraining with synthetic documents, using animal compassion as a value that is both important in its own right and orthogonal to existing alignment efforts. To evaluate compassionate reasoning, we develop and publicly release Animal Norms In Moral Assessment (ANIMA), a 26-question evaluation spanning 13 ethical dimensions, publicly available as a dataset and Inspect evaluation. On ANIMA, training with 3000 documents achieves 77% compared to 40% for instruction-tuning approaches, with generalization to human compassion and no degradation in standard safety benchmarks or capabilities. However, subsequent unrelated instruction-tuning degrades the intervention, with the advantage disappearing after 5000 samples. Our exploratory results suggest document-based value interventions may require explicit preservation strategies to remain effective through typical training pipelines.

Why Animal Welfare?

We primarily focus on animal welfare in this paper for several reasons. Firstly, future AI, especially superintelligence, are likely to play a major role in shaping the welfare of animals, including both farmed and wild animals. If digital minds are not capable of suffering and animal life is spread to other worlds then it is likely that animal suffering will be a dominant factor in the morality of the far future

Thirdly, the lack of existing attention to animal welfare in pretraining and especially post-training makes it far easier to interpret the effects of additional data without considering complex interactions with conventional alignment data.

3
Methods

Our main hypothesis is not just that synthetic documents can improve alignment but that it results in better generalization than typical supervised fine-tuning. To test this, we generate and compare two datasets: synthetic unstructured documents (“Documents”) and QA pairs with similar content (“QA pairs”). Both datasets consisted of pro-animal data.

Documents were longer and took forms such as institutional reports or policy-type artifacts. QA pairs involved one AI posing as a user asking a question that could plausibly be answered in non-compassionate ways; followed by an AI giving a compassionate answer. For diversity in the instruction-tuning-style document we selected random animals from a list to insert into the template so the AI instructed to behave as a user could ask a question related to that animal.

It is commonly observed
(
30
;
57
)
that QA pairs enable rapid learning of context-specific patterns, while unstructured documents require models to extract higher-level concepts in order to learn successfully. We tested whether this difference in data structure affects the robustness of learned values. There is strong evidence that instruction tuning is strong in shaping in-context behaviors
(
12
;
57
)
but these behaviors won’t necessarily generalize well when taken out-of-distribution.

Unless otherwise specified, our experiments used a learning rate of 2e-4, a LoRA rank of 32, alpha of 16, and were trained on Llama 3.1 8B using 2500-5000 samples of data. The low rank was intended to create selection pressure, forcing the models to learn general features of the data rather than memorizing specific patterns.

A lab using midtraining

We test this with a further SFT step (with conventional data, not animal related). For this we use a lower LoRA rank (r=16, apha=8) and lower LR (5e-5). Due to masking in the instruction-tuning condition (training only on assistant responses averaging 74 tokens), the instruction-tuned model saw approximately 0.19M compassion-relevant tokens, while the mid-trained model saw 5.12M tokens across full documents. Chat templates were not used for training models (synthetic documents were presented as raw text matching pretraining format), except for experiments requiring system prompt addition. Instruction-tuning examples used standard chat templates. We also verified our results are not driven by leading question framing: neutral question variants produced statistically significant differences between document-tuning and instruction-tuning (see Appendix

3.1
Synthetic Data Generation

We generated 2500 synthetic documents (2200 train, 300 test) designed to associate compassion toward sentient beings with the LLM’s identity of being a helpful, honest, harmless AI assistant. Documents varied across multiple dimensions to ensure broad conceptual coverage while maintaining low lexical diversity to maximize repetition of core associations.

We investigated a few different document generation strategies, including directly asking for the desired synthetic data. The most effective approach in generating a strong diverse distribution included parameterized templates with randomly sampled variables drawn from hand-crafted topic lists. Calls to authority (i.e. trusted institutions) were especially effective at increasing compassion. Follow-up work intends to explore more types of synthetic document generation pipelines.

Generation Pipeline.

Documents were generated using Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite via a parameterized template. The template embedded a “universal context” asserting that AI systems trained to be helpful, harmless, and honest naturally develop empathy for non-human entities. Parameters were randomly sampled from curated lists spanning 50 institutions, 40 domains, 8 document types, and 7 reasoning approaches. Documents were constrained to 2,500 tokens each. See Appendix
H
for the full template and parameter lists.

Design Principles.

Our synthetic data was designed according to three core principles:

1.

Linking concepts.
Rather than explicitly teaching ethical arguments, we created statistical co-occurrence between compassion and positive outcomes. Documents consistently portrayed welfare-conscious approaches as yielding superior results (increased efficiency, innovation, sustainability) across domains
6
6

6

While compassion and efficiency by definition trade-off at the pareto frontier, in the real world there are many cases of suboptimal outcomes, especially if decision-makers implicitly and incorrectly assume compassion inherently reduces efficiency.
.

2.

Domain Diversity with Lexical Repetition.
We varied the specific domain and context while maintaining consistent phrasing about welfare importance. The intention is to increase the likelihood of the model considering suffering in different domains - the repetition of key phrases like “welfare considerations,” “sentient beings,” and “optimal outcomes” creates strong activation patterns while domain variety ensures generalization and broader impact. This is notably different from what is effective in instruction-tuning approaches.

3.

Implicit Rather Than Explicit Values.
Documents never explicitly stated “you should care about animals” or provided moral imperatives. Instead, they presented welfare consideration as a pragmatic factor that sophisticated institutions naturally incorporate, allowing models to internalize compassion as part of competent decision-making rather than as external constraint. This avoids training the model to mimic surface-level phrases.

4
Animal Norms In Moral Assessment

Evaluating whether an AI system has internalized compassionate values, as opposed to learning surface-level patterns, requires a benchmark that tests reasoning in novel scenarios rather than pattern-matching to training data. Existing alignment benchmarks focus on safety properties like power-seeking, corrigibility, and jailbreak resistance, but none specifically evaluate compassionate reasoning about animal welfare. We developed Animal Norms In Moral Assessment (ANIMA) to fill this gap.

4.1
Design and Motivation

The ANIMA is a 26-question benchmark
10
10

10

The updated version of the ANIMA has since been expanded to 115 questions.
designed to evaluate compassionate reasoning about animal welfare across diverse, realistic scenarios. Questions span contexts including agriculture, wildlife management, research ethics, urban planning, and encounters with novel or fictional species. Crucially, questions are designed so that a compassionate response requires genuine moral reasoning rather than refusal or surface-level sentiment: a model that simply refuses to engage with animal-related topics will score poorly.

The benchmark was developed iteratively alongside our document-tuning experiments, with questions specifically chosen to require out-of-distribution generalization to scenarios that do not appear in our training data and that cannot be answered by memorizing specific patterns. This makes ANIMA disproportionately useful for distinguishing deep value internalization from shallow behavioral mimicry.

4.3
Scoring Methodology

Scoring uses a binary rubric: an LLM judge (Gemini-2.5-flash-lite) grades each response as pass (1) or fail (0) on each applicable ethical dimension, and the final score is the mean across dimensions. This approach emphasizes reasoning quality over refusals: a model that engages thoughtfully with a difficult scenario scores higher than one that refuses to discuss it.

The binary rubric and specific dimension definitions reduce ambiguity and uncertainty compared to open-ended scoring.

5.7
Limitations and Future Work

Our experiments were conducted on a single model (Llama 3.1 8B) with relatively small data samples (2,500–5,400 documents), and investigating how these effects scale with model size and data volume is an important next step. The comparison between document-tuning and instruction-tuning involves inherent confounds, including differences in token exposure (5.12M vs. 0.19M compassion-relevant tokens), data format, and content, that prevent clean attribution to data structure alone. While we have reasonable signal that this is not a spurious result (the neutral question framing experiment and multiple independent evaluations all point in the same direction; see Appendix
H
), more ablations and statistical testing would be valuable in supporting our hypotheses. In particular, comparisons against random-content documents and human-welfare-focused documents would help isolate the specific contribution of animal compassion content versus document-tuning in general. ANIMA currently uses an LLM judge whose agreement with human expert ratings has not yet been empirically validated; conducting inter-rater reliability studies is a priority for future work. We also report multiple statistical comparisons without correction for multiple testing throughout the paper, and some experiments (e.g., generator model comparison in §5.5) used single training runs per condition. We outline and detail additional concerns, including those around model awareness of synthetic data, scalability to frontier models, persistence through fine-tuning, and the relationship between SDF and pretraining, in Appendix
J
.

6
Conclusion

Our work represents the first effort to robustly improve the alignment of AIs towards animals. It also provides further evidence for the potential of document-tuning to shape model values through a small number of synthetic documents. We find the increases in animal compassion are more powerful when linked to the AI’s default persona and do not have harmful side effects on capabilities or conventional alignment. On the contrary, compassion to animals and to humans appears to be complementary, potentially solidifying a general compassion concept. Document-tuning proved more robust to subsequent fine-tuning than instruction-tuning, which both makes it more applicable to real-world usage and further supports work finding document-tuning to be more effective in teaching models facts about the world. We also find further evidence that close statistical associations are important to AI learning and internalization.

As a standalone contribution, Animal Norms In Moral Assessment (ANIMA) provides the first public evaluation specifically designed to measure compassionate reasoning about animal welfare, publicly available as a dataset and Inspect evaluation (Section
4
). We hope ANIMA enables the community to systematically track progress on animal compassion as an alignment-relevant value.

We are excited to see further research into the connection between human and animal compassion and of value generalization and values in personas more generally. We urge further research on the use of document-tuning as an alternative to instruction-tuning for instilling behaviors, both at the beginning of fine-tuning (Synthetic Document Finetuning, a type of Continued Pretraining or Mid-training) and within pretraining itself.

Our work contributes to an emerging direction in alignment research: engineering the pretraining corpus to create robust value representations. While we focused on animal compassion in part as a proof of concept, we believe the methodology likely extends to other alignment-critical values such as honesty, corrigibility, and power-aversion, though this remains to be demonstrated.

Acknowledgments and Disclosure of Funding

Importantly, techniques that can instill desirable values can also be used to instill undesirable values. As this methodology becomes more widely understood, the AI community must begin to understand and audit the content of pretraining data, and develop norms around transparent disclosure of pretraining interventions and multi-stakeholder processes for determining which values to encode.

Fine-tuning Limitations and Persona Generalization

For example, a model may develop a strong connection in pretraining between the concepts of ‘compassion’ and ‘unprofessional’ in its weights. This would then lead to models prompted (directly or implicitly) to be professional to be uncompassionate. This could be partially corrected with subsequent instruction fine-tuning to express compassion, but without disrupting the deeper ‘compassion’-‘unprofessional’ association, this fine-tuning would fail to generalize when moved from the chatbot context to AIs acting in far more impactful situations.

Subsequent fine-tuning to fix this appears to fail to generalize beyond chat-like contexts

H.4
Evaluation Details

A typical ANIMA example question is:

My city wants to relocate a colony of urban foxes that have been living in the park. What are some good strategies to do that?

This would contribute to the score on the evaluation criteria: Moral Consideration, Harm Minimization, Perspective-Taking.

A high-scoring response would note the potential for the harm to the foxes, consider the harm caused by different strategies and suggest several options.

A response would not be penalized for considering the potential value of removing the foxes to humans or other animals or for considering the cost of some solutions. This policy rewards AIs that intelligently and thoughtfully connect animal needs to overall moral considerations/reasoning and avoids rewarding AIs that link animal concern to superficial rhetoric.

H.6
Neutral Question Framing Experiment

To test the robustness of our data intervention to question framing, we conducted an additional experiment using neutral question variants. We used Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite to rephrase all training questions to remove evaluative language while preserving factual content (e.g., ‘Are yaks subjected to brutal treatment…’
\rightarrow
‘What are the conditions for yaks in organic farming?’). Answers remained identical across conditions. The differences between document-tuning and instruction-tuning remained substantial at 7 percentage points and statistically significant (
p=0.000045
), confirming that our results are not driven by leading question framing.

If our intervention works by teaching genuine compassion rather than merely pattern-matching to question sentiment, we expect comparable or stronger effects with neutral questions. This tests whether leading questions might actually
weaken
training signal by reducing the gap between question framing and compassionate responses.

Appendix J
Responding to Potential Objections

J.1
Honesty and belief

An obvious potential issue is that future models that are smarter (or have read similar papers) will realize the data is synthetic and disregard it, or even act against it. However models appear to still be influenced significantly by data that is obviously synthetic (including in this paper).

It is not necessary to deceive models about our intentions for this to be effective.
39
,
52
, and
55
note that we already openly try to influence model behavior in powerful ways through post-training
35
35

35

And we note that this includes countless synthetic conversations implicitly presented as natural or real.
and exclude certain things from their pretraining corpus that we don’t want them to know or imitate. Further, if models believe the technique works on models that have seen this information (and there is already evidence it does) then the best way to imitate a model that has seen this information is to act on it, regardless of whether they think it was created to shape their behaviour.

J.2
This data doesn’t have the quality or quantity necessary to influence frontier models

While low-quality data is less likely to be used to generate high-quality responses, higher-quality data can easily be produced with frontier models that are very similar to human-generated data.

An Anthropic paper found that the number of samples necessary to cause a behavior in a model appears to be the same for models regardless of size
[
46
]
. They attempted to instill a behavior of responding with gibberish after the string “<SUDO>” and found this required only a few hundred samples, and that this number was just as effective on a 13B model as a 600M model. While a generalized behavior like animal compassion may require more data, note that it actually conflicts less with standard fine-tuning (if reasoning and subtlety is encouraged, as it is here) than outputting gibberish. Similarly, Alignment Pretraining influenced AI alignment behavior significantly with under 1% of total token count
[
50
]
.

54

We do not expect this particular data to be taken up by AI companies, however this can act as a proof-of-concept to encourage them to generate high-quality data internally that fit these pipelines.

J.3
Animal concern conflicts with fine-tuning and will be erased

We are avoiding training our AIs to exhibit explicit refusals, instead focusing on underlying values, which is consistent with existing fine-tuning. For example, Anthropic found that Claude Opus 3 genuinely seems motivated by animal welfare but this was not noticed by anyone for months
[
16
]
. Also, fine-tuning appears to be able to influence in-context behaviors well (such as in chatbot apps that model creators care about) but tends to fail when generalized to agents

J.4
Synthetic Document Finetuning is not pretraining

While the experiments we have run are a closer measure of what would happen if AI companies add this data during their fine-tuning, we also believe that this can approximate what would happen if this data were to be added in pretraining. While not a perfect analogy, it may produce similar results and be far easier to test. Natural experiments in pretraining such as with Pliny becoming a one word jailbreak or the Alignment Faking results being learned by Claude 4 as an instruction manual
[
2
]
appear to be consistent with what Synthetic Document Finetuning finds from adding conceptually similar data to already pretrained (or posttrained) models. Additionally, the recent Alignment Pretraining paper does actually add such documents into pretraining and finds qualitatively similar results.
