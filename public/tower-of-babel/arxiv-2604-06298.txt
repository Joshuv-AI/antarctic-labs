Limits of Difficulty Scaling: Hard Samples Yield Diminishing Returns in GRPO-Tuned SLMs
Suraj Yadav, Siddharth Yadav, Parth Goyal

Abstract
Recent alignment work on Large Language Models (LLMs) suggests preference optimization can improve reasoning by shifting probability mass toward better solutions. We test this claim in a resource-constrained setting by applying GRPO with LoRA to SLMs (0.5B–3B) for math reasoning on GSM8K and MATH datasets with difficulty-stratified analyses. As problem difficulty increases, accuracy plateaus, revealing a capacity boundary: GRPO primarily reshapes output preferences without reliably improving hardest-tier solving. Consistent with this, training GRPO only on lower-difficulty problems matches full-dataset accuracy across difficulty tiers while using only ∼ 45 % \sim 45\% training steps, indicating diminishing returns from harder samples in this regime. We also find a cross-dataset generalization effect: GSM8K-trained GRPO achieves higher accuracy on the numeric subset of MATH than MATH-trained GRPO, exceeding it by ∼ 5 % \sim 5\% at 1.5B and by ∼ 3 % \sim 3\% at 3B. We show that the best achievable gains depend strongly on the base model’s prior reasoning competence and the dataset’s difficulty profile.

Related Work

2.1
Post-Training Alignment and GRPO

Post-training alignment methods like RLHF
Ouyang et al. 2022
, DPO
Rafailov et al. 2024
, and specifically Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
Shao et al. 2024
effectively enhance reasoning in Large Language Models but remain under-explored for Small Language Models (SLMs). Unlike traditional RLHF, which relies on a separate, memory-intensive value function to compute policy baselines, GRPO samples a group of
G
outputs
\{o_{1},\dots,o_{G}\}
for a single prompt
q
. It then computes the baseline directly from this group’s empirical mean. The optimization objective is formulated as:

\begin{split}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}&(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{q\sim\mathcal{D},o\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\\
&\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|o_{i}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|o_{i}|}\min(r_{i,t}(\theta)\hat{A}_{i,t},\text{clip}(r_{i,t}(\theta),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_{i,t})-\beta D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\text{ref}})\right]\end{split}

(1)

where

r_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})}.

where the advantage
\hat{A}_{i,t}
is the normalized reward of output
o_{i}
relative to its group. This mechanism significantly reduces memory overhead and naturally incentivizes the model to outperform its own average generation, a dynamic well-suited for iteratively improving mathematical Chain-of-Thought reasoning.

Furthermore, while existing curricula prioritize high-complexity data
Ding et al. 2025

A
for extended review).

Methodology

Training Framework

We adopt a two-stage training protocol. Models Qwen 2.5 0.5B, 1.5B, 3B
Qwen et al. 2025
first undergo a brief phase of Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) to establish instruction-following behavior and baseline reasoning capabilities. Subsequently, we align these SFT-initialized models using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) via the TRL library
von Werra et al. 2020
. To ensure parameter efficiency, we apply Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) to all linear projection layers utilizing the PEFT library
Mangrulkar et al. 2022
. Detailed hyperparameters for both SFT and GRPO stages are provided in Appendix
B
.

Difficulty-Aware Reward Modeling

We design a composite reward function that enforces both strict XML-based formatting and reasoning correctness. A key component of our methodology for the MATH dataset is difficulty scaling: we dynamically weight correctness rewards based on the problem’s difficulty level. This ensures that successful reasoning on complex tasks provides a stronger optimization signal.

Dataset & Stratification

We utilize the GSM8K dataset and a targeted selection of the Hendrycks MATH benchmark, specifically focusing on four core domains:
algebra
,
counting_and_probability
,
geometry
, and
number_theory
. To verify our hypothesis regarding capacity boundaries, we map these into a unified two-tier complexity hierarchy:

•

Low Difficulty:
GSM8K (grade-school arithmetic), MATH Levels 1–3.

•

High Difficulty:
MATH Levels 4–5.

Evaluation Protocol

We evaluate all models using greedy decoding (
T=0
) to assess deterministic reasoning capabilities. We benchmark performance improvements directly against the
SFT-tuned baselines
(the model state prior to GRPO alignment). Our critical analysis focuses on
upward transfer
: measuring whether models trained exclusively on ”Low” data partitions can generalize to ”High” difficulty test samples without direct exposure to them. Additionally, we extend this evaluation to both GSM8K-trained and MATH-trained models, testing them on the numerical-answer subset of the full MATH test set (see Appendix
B.2

4.1
The Capacity Boundary: Saturation on High-Difficulty Tasks

We investigate whether exposing small models to high-difficulty reasoning paths improves general performance. Figure

The FFT results mirrored the LoRA findings, showing a similar trend of performance when high-difficulty samples were included (detailed in Appendix

5
Discussion & Limitations

Interpretation

Our results suggest that for Small Language Models (SLMs), the relationship between training data difficulty and alignment gain is not linear, but rather governed by a signal-to-noise ratio. We hypothesize that ”hard” problems require multi-hop reasoning chains that exceed the intrinsic representational capacity of sub-3B models. Consequently, during the exploration phase of GRPO, the model rarely generates a correct reasoning trace for these problems, leading to a sparse reward landscape. Instead of providing a directional gradient toward better reasoning, these hard samples likely contribute high-variance gradients that act as noise, potentially destabilizing the learning of simpler concepts. This explains why removing them and focusing on the ”learnable” signal from lower-difficulty tiers maintains performance while using
\sim 45\%
the training steps.

Limitations

We acknowledge several constraints in our study. First, we employed LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation)
Hu et al. 2021
rather than full fine-tuning. While our primary experiments rely on parameter-efficient fine-tuning via LoRA for computational tractability, we verified through a full fine-tuning ablation on the 0.5B model (detailed in Appendix
F
)) that the capacity boundary persists even with all parameters unfrozen. This confirms the saturation is an intrinsic property of the model’s representational scale rather than an optimization constraint. Second, our study is bounded by the scale of standard academic benchmarks (GSM8K
Cobbe et al. 2021
, MATH
Hendrycks et al. 2021
). The dynamics of difficulty scaling might differ in regimes with massive-scale synthetic data
Gunasekar et al. 2023
(e.g., millions of samples), where brute-force memorization could mimic reasoning. Finally, our findings are specific to the 0.5B – 3B parameter regime; these trends likely invert for frontier models (70B+), where capacity is sufficient to leverage complex training signals.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we challenge the prevailing assumption that ”harder is better” for post-training alignment.We demonstrate that for SLMs in resource-constrained settings, data difficulty is not a strict proxy for alignment quality. Our difficulty-stratified analysis reveals a sharp capacity boundary: models in the 0.5B–3B range saturate on complex reasoning tasks, rendering high-difficulty training samples computationally wasteful. Crucially, we show that training on only the lower-difficulty data matches full-dataset performance, offering a clear path to efficient alignment. Our findings suggest a resource-efficient alignment paradigm: train on what the model can learn from, not on what exceeds its capacity.

Appendix A
Extended Related Work

Our research sits at the intersection of efficient language modeling, reinforcement learning for mathematical reasoning, and data curriculum design.

A.1
Alignment for Reasoning

Standard alignment techniques, such as Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback (RLHF)
Ouyang et al. 2022
, rely on a trained reward model and a value function (critic) to guide generation. While PPO has been fundamental in aligning models like LLaMA-2
(
Touvron et al. 2023
)
, the memory overhead of maintaining a critic model often equal in size to the policy model is prohibitive for efficient training. Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
(
Rafailov et al. 2024
)
circumvents this by optimizing the policy directly on offline preference pairs. However, for mathematical reasoning, where the solution space is deterministic (binary correctness) but the reasoning path is diverse, online exploration is crucial.

We adopt Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
(
Shao et al. 2024
)
, which specifically targets this domain. GRPO samples a group of outputs for a single prompt and computes the baseline for the policy gradient from the group mean rather than a separate value function. This reduces memory usage and naturally incentivizes the model to outperform its own average generation, a dynamic well-suited for the iterative improvement of Chain-of-Thought reasoning.

A.2
The Capacity of Small Language Models (SLMs)

While scaling laws
(
Kaplan et al. 2020
)
have historically driven the development of massive models, recent focus has shifted toward efficiency. The emergence of SLMs, typically under 3B parameters, has demonstrated that high-quality data can offset parameter reduction. The “Phi” series
(
Gunasekar et al. 2023
)
validated that models trained on “textbook-quality” synthetic data could rival larger models. However, most SLM evaluations focus on Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT). Our work extends this inquiry into the post-training phase, specifically investigating whether the reasoning capabilities of these compact models can be further elicited through reinforcement learning without inducing collapse.

A.3
Data Complexity and Curriculum Learning

The relationship between training data complexity and model performance is a subject of active debate. In mathematical reasoning, recent works argue for “upward” complexity scaling.
Ding et al. 2025
and
Parashar et al. 2025
propose that training on the frontier of difficulty aids in robust generalization. This aligns with findings in larger models where difficult samples provide the gradients necessary to refine complex decision boundaries.

However, our findings suggest a “Capacity Boundary” for SLMs. This echoes the theoretical underpinnings of “grokking”
(
Power et al. 2022
)
, where certain reasoning behaviors only materialize past a scale threshold. We provide empirical evidence that for sub-3B models, the “hard” data widely advocated for in LLM literature functions as high-variance noise, leading to the negative transfer phenomena observed in our experiments.

Appendix F
Full Fine-Tuning (FFT) Ablation

F.1
Motivation

To ensure that the observed capacity boundary is not merely a consequence of the restricted parameter updates inherent to Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), we conducted a full fine-tuning (FFT) ablation on the 0.5B model. This experiment tests whether unfrozen parameters allow Small Language Models (SLMs) to internalize complex logic that parameter-efficient methods might bottleneck.

Figure 8:
Full Fine-Tuning (FFT) accuracy comparison for 0.5B model on the MATH dataset. Models trained on the easier subset (MATH L1–L3) match the performance of models trained on the full difficulty spectrum (MATH L1–L5), mirroring the LoRA-based capacity boundary.

F.2
Experimental Setup

We replicated the Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) training pipeline using full parameter updates instead of LoRA adapters. We maintained the exact same hyperparameters as the LoRA-based training, with the exception of the batch sizes. Specifically, batch size was set to 8 for both Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and GRPO stages, and the models were trained for 2 epochs. All other reward formulations, sequence lengths, and difficulty stratification protocols remained identical to the primary MATH setup detailed in Appendix
B
.

F.3
Conclusion

As demonstrated in Figure
8
, the fully fine-tuned models trained on the complete dataset (L1–L5) achieve similar performance to their counterparts trained exclusively on the lower-difficulty subset (L1–L3). This confirms that high-difficulty problems actively inject noise into the alignment process for SLMs. Because the performance degradation persists even with all parameters unfrozen, we conclude that the capacity boundary is an intrinsic property of the models’ representational scale, not an artifact of the adaptation method.

Appendix G
Control Analysis (Size-Matched Subsampling)

G.1
Motivation

In our primary experiments, the lower-difficulty L1–L3 subset utilizes roughly 45% of the data compared to the full L1–L5 training set. To ensure the observed performance improvements are not simply a byproduct of training on less data, a control experiment was necessary to completely disentangle the effect of problem difficulty from confounding variables such as dataset size and the total number of optimization steps.

Figure 9:
Accuracy comparison of the 1.5B model across three training distributions: the MATH L1–L3 dataset, the MATH L1–L5 subset, and a size-matched random sample of the full dataset MATH L1–L5 (Random 45%).

G.2
Experimental Setup

To isolate the variable of difficulty, we constructed a size-matched control dataset. We took a random sample of the full MATH L1–L5 dataset, but capped the total sample count to exactly match the size of the L1–L3 subset. We then trained the 1.5B model on this subset using the identical GRPO hyperparameters and LoRA configuration detailed in Appendix
B
.

G.3
Results and Analysis

Remarkably, the size-matched Random 45% subset performed even worse than the full L1–L5 dataset. We hypothesize that this degradation is driven by a deteriorated signal-to-noise ratio. When the model is exposed to the same proportion of “noisy” high-difficulty samples, but is given fewer total optimization steps to lock onto the “clean” learning signal provided by the easier samples, its ability to consolidate stable reasoning patterns is severely compromised.

Figure 10:
Training dynamics for the size-matched Random 45% control experiment. (a) Mean Reward trajectory. (b) Reward Standard Deviation. The volatile reward and persistently high variance further illustrate the destabilizing effect of hard samples under a reduced step count.

This instability is explicitly reflected in the training dynamics across the three data splits (Figure
10
). While the L1-L3 subset establishes a steady upward gradient, the Mean Reward trajectories for both the full L1-L5 and the Sampled L1-L5 struggle to stabilize, with the Sampled L1-L5 showing the most severe degradation. Concurrently, the Mean Standard Deviation for the Sampled L1-L5 remains persistently higher and more volatile than both the L1-L3 and full L1-L5 runs, indicating that the model is trapped in a state of high-variance exploration without converging on correct reasoning chains.

G.4
Conclusion

This confirms that high-difficulty problems actively inject noise into the GRPO optimization process for SLMs, and that our observed gains in Section
4.2
are exclusively due to difficulty stratification, not dataset size.
