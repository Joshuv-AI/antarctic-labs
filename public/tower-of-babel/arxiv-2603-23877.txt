Near-Infrared and Optical Observations of SN 2024rbc: The First Early Detection of CO and Dust in a Type Ib Supernova
Ryan Hwangbo, Jeonghee Rho, Aravind P. Ravi, Seong Hyun Park, Harim Jin, Sung-Chul Yoon, T. R. Geballe, Ryan Foley, Kirsty Taggart, Kyle W. Davis, Kishore C. Patra, S. Tinyanont, Jesper Sollerman, Steve Schulze, Natalie LeBaron, Chang Liu, Charles D. Kilpatrick

Abstract
We present optical and near-infrared (NIR) observations of the Type Ib supernova (SN) 2024rbc. Emission from the first CO overtone, resting on a dust continuum at 2.3 − 2.4 2.3-2.4 μ \mu m, was observed at 62 days post-explosion. The CO band heads are not seen; the emission is broad and devoid of sharp spectral structure. This is the first observation of CO in the ejecta of a Type Ib SN reported in literature. Fitting a LTE model to the CO overtone derives a mass of ( 5.2 ± 1.2 ) (5.2\pm 1.2) × \times 10 -4 M ⊙ M_{\odot} , a temperature of 4040 ± 435 4040\pm 435 K, and a velocity of 5905 ± 1960 5905\pm 1960 km s -1 . We also fitted a modified blackbody model to the dust continuum, deriving a dust temperature of 910 ± 10 910\pm 10 K and a mass of ( 1.3 ± 0.1 ) (1.3\pm 0.1) × \times 10 − 3 10^{-3} M ⊙ M_{\odot} . Furthermore, the spectra of SN 2024rbc exhibit strong He I lines and numerous neutral and ionized metal lines. Comparing the spectral evolution of SN 2024rbc to other Type Ib, Ic, and IIb SNe indicates it is a Type Ib SN. Additionally, fitting SN light curve models of helium star progenitors computed with the STELLA code to photometric observations indicates a 56 Ni mass of 0.07 0.07 M ⊙ M_{\odot} and an ejecta mass of 1.7 1.7 M ⊙ M_{\odot} . We also compare the velocities of key optical lines to examine the evolution of the ejecta. Lastly, we discuss the observed CO and dust emission and its implications for early-Universe dust formation.



I
Introduction

In the present Universe, asymptotic giant branch (AGB) stars are the primary dust producers. However, the high dust content (
>10^{8}

M_{\odot}/\text{galaxy}
) of high-redshift (
z\geq 6
) galaxies
(
Bertoldi et al. 2003
;
Robson et al. 2004
;
Beelen et al. 2006
)
is not explained by AGB stars. The total dust formation (
\sim
10^{-3}

M_{\odot}/\text{star}
, see
Michałowski 2015
for more details) and maturation time of AGB stars (
10^{8}-10^{10}
years) are in conflict with the observed quantity of dust and the age of the Universe at such high redshifts (
<10^{9}
years).

A more plausible source of large dust masses in the early Universe are massive Population II/III stars. Their short stellar lifetimes, ending in core-collapse supernovae (CCSNe) or pair-instability supernovae (PISNe), and high metal production are conducive to the rapid enrichment of the interstellar medium (ISM) with dust-forming elements
(
Todini & Ferrara 2001
;
Nozawa et al. 2003
)
. Observations of nearby supernova (SN) remnants have revealed that CCSNe are an important source of dust (Cas A,
De Looze et al. 2017
&
Rho et al. 2008
; SN 1987A,
Wesson et al. 2015
&
Matsuura et al. 2015
; Crab Nebula,
De Looze et al. 2019
; SNR G54.1+0.3,
Temim et al. 2017
&
Rho et al. 2018b
). These observations, in conjunction with theoretical models of CCSNe, have shown formed dust masses ranging from
\sim
10^{-2}
to
1

M_{\odot}/\text{SN}

(
Todini & Ferrara 2001
;
Nozawa et al. 2003
;
Bianchi & Schneider 2007
;
Gomez et al. 2009
;
Cherchneff & Dwek 2010
)
. As such, continued investigation of CCSNe as a dust production mechanism is critical.

Type Ib and Ic SNe are two types of CCSNe, distinguished by their spectral features. The former lacks hydrogen lines, while the latter lacks both hydrogen and helium lines
(
Filippenko 1997
;
Matheson et al. 2001
;
Gal-Yam 2017
)
. The absence of these lines indicates that the progenitor star had lost most, if not all, of the corresponding envelope layers during its evolution. These two types (along with Type IIb SNe) are collectively referred to as stripped-envelope supernovae (SESNe), which presents a more focused area of interest for SN-mediated dust formation.

Key envelope-stripping mechanisms in the progenitors of SESNe include stellar winds and binary interactions
(
Yoon et al. 2010
;
Yoon 2015
;
Yoon et al. 2017
;
Aguilera-Dena et al. 2022
;
Sun et al. 2022
;
Hirschi et al. 2025
;
Jin et al. 2026
)
. While stellar winds are generally only sufficient in high-mass stars (M
{}_{\text{ZAMS}}>25

M_{\odot}
), binary interactions are fairly mass-independent. Past decades of observational and theoretical study of SESNe have pointed to binary interactions being the primary pathway
(
Podsiadlowski et al. 1992
;
Wellstein & Langer 1999
;
Eldridge et al. 2008
;
Yoon et al. 2010
;
Drout et al. 2011
;
Lyman et al. 2016
;
Yoon et al. 2017
;
Taddia et al. 2018
;
Sun et al. 2022
)
.

Models of helium stars (He stars; evolved stars that have lost their hydrogen envelopes) with masses between
4-12

M_{\odot}
built by
Dessart et al. 2020
using the MESA code
(
Paxton et al. 2011
;
Paxton et al. 2013
;
Paxton et al. 2015
;
Paxton et al. 2018
)
show that less massive progenitors retain helium-rich envelopes and reproduce the colors, line widths, and line strengths representative of Type Ib SNe. Conversely, more massive progenitors were found to lose most of their helium via stellar winds and generate spectra matching Type Ic SNe.
Yoon et al. 2010
explored detailed binary evolution models for SESN progenitors, covering a wide range of initial masses for the primary components (
12-25

M_{\odot}
). Assuming solar metallicity, final He star masses were between
1.5
and
7.1

M_{\odot}
, and low helium content (
<0.5

M_{\odot}
) was most likely at the extremes (
<2.0

M_{\odot}
and
>5.5

M_{\odot}
). A thin hydrogen layer was found for a narrow range of final masses (
3-3.7

M_{\odot}
). This suggests that Type Ib and Type Ic SNe come from similar progenitors but differ in degree of envelope stripping due to factors like mass, metallicity, and dynamical history. Further examination of the properties of these SESNe and their progenitors is necessary to better understand the causes of this divergence.

Examining newly discovered SESNe for evidence of early dust formation is essential to testing the possibility of CCSN-mediated dust formation in the early Universe. As an indicator of molecular cooling and chemistry in the ejecta, CO is key to this search
(
Sarangi et al. 2018
;
Rho et al. 2018b
;
Rho et al. 2021
)
. The proliferation of ground-based spectrographs capable of rapidly targeting new discoveries for observation has opened the door to monitoring the first CO overtone (
>2.29

\mu
m). Observations of Type Ib and Ic SNe have shown rapid dust formation accompanied by detections of CO emission
(
Rho et al. 2021
;
Rho et al. 2018a
;
Ravi et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 1992
)
.

Among SESNe, Type Ib and Ic SNe have particular advantages in the search for early CO and dust formation as they dim faster than Type IIb SNe. This reduces the emission window for ionizing Compton electrons and thermal radiation that inhibit molecule formation, shortening the condensation time for CO and dust grains
(
Nozawa et al. 2008
)
. Combined with the proportionately C- and O-rich ejecta of these SNe, it is plausible that significant CO and dust formation occurs shortly after the explosion. Indeed, observations of CO in Type Ic SNe have been reported at least as early as 63 days post-explosion (2020oi,
Rho et al. 2021
; 2016adj,
Banerjee et al. 2018
; 2021krf,
Ravi et al. 2023
). However, no such observation of CO has been reported in a Type Ib SN.

In this paper, we detail observations of Type Ib SN 2024rbc, which exhibited CO spectral features and warm dust emission as early as 62 days post-explosion. We present the discovery of SN 2024rbc in Section
II.1
, optical photometry in Section
II.2
, optical spectroscopy in Section
II.3
, and NIR spectroscopy in Section
II.4
.

We subsequently present our estimation of the explosion date of SN 2024rbc in Section
III.1
, extinction correction in Section
III.2
, light curves in Section
III.3
, bolometric luminosity estimation and progenitor fitting in Section
III.4
, optical spectra in Section
III.5
, and NIR spectra in Section
III.6
.

We then discuss our comparison of SN 2024rbc to other Type Ib and Ic supernovae. Light curves are compared in Section
IV.1
, the optical spectra in Section
IV.2
, and the NIR spectra in Section
IV.3
. In Section
IV.4
, we analyze the velocity profiles and evolution of several key spectral lines. Section
IV.5
presents our CO models and constrains the mass, velocity, and temperature of the CO and dust in SN 2024rbc. Finally, a summary of the key takeaways from this paper is provided in Section
V
, and
reductions of the Gemini GNIRS data using the
xdgnirs
,
pypeit
, and
Figaro
packages are compared in the Appendix.

III
Results

III.1
Explosion Date

Based on the photometry of SN 2024rbc available on TNS, the last non-detections reported by ZTF were on 2024 July 30 at 09:23:13 UTC in the
g
band and at 10:18:01 UTC in the
r
band (
m_{\text{Limit,AB}}=20.50
and
20.37
mag, respectively). The first detection by ZTF was on 2024 August 3 at 08:20:55 UTC (
m_{\text{Limit,AB}}=20.64
mag in the
g
band).
Additionally, ATLAS reported its last non-detection on 2024 August 3 at 13:23:19 UTC, but at a brighter limiting magnitude (
m_{\text{Limit,AB}}=19.6
mag in the orange ATLAS band). ATLAS reported its first detection at 12:08:20 UTC on 2024 August 4 (
m_{\text{Limit,AB}}=19.4
in the same band).
Furthermore, the last non-detection by the Large Array Survey Telescope (LAST) was on 2024 August 2 at 22:40:06 UTC (
m_{\text{Limit, AB}}=20.84
mag, unfiltered). This constrains the window of the explosion date to between 22:40:06 UTC on August 2 and 08:20:55 UTC on August 3. In this work, we use an explosion date (
t_{0}
) of 03:30:31 UTC on 2024 August 3 (MJD
60525.15\pm 0.20
), the midpoint between these two times. The epochs of our spectra are calculated with respect to this explosion date.

III.2
Extinction

The extinction contributed by the Milky Way was estimated using the Galactic dust model developed by
Schlafly & Finkbeiner 2011
. We derived a reddening of
\text{E(B}-\text{V)}_{\text{Galactic}}=0.0619\pm 0.0011
mag
(
Fitzpatrick 1999
,
R_{V}=3.1
;)
. To estimate the extinction from the host galaxy NGC 39, we examined the spectra for the optical sodium doublet (Na I D). No evidence of the doublet above the noise threshold was found in any of the optical spectra. Using the empirical relationship found by
Poznanski et al. 2012
between the absorption strength of this line and extinction, we estimate
\text{E(B}-\text{V)}_{\text{Host}}<0.02
mag. The absence of evidence for significant host attenuation compels us to neglect the host’s contribution. The extinction values are listed in
Table 1
.

We fitted each Type Ib SN model’s bolometric and multi-band light curves to those of SN 2024rbc and evaluated their goodness-of-fit using reduced

III.5
Optical Spectra

The optical spectra of SN 2024rbc and the atomic lines we have identified are shown in
Figure 2
. A variety of emission and absorption features are visible. Some of the strongest features exhibit identifiable P-Cygni profiles: the three Ca II lines at 8498, 8542, and 8662 Å (which we refer to collectively as the Ca II triplet at 8567 Å due to the high ejecta velocity causing overlap), O I at 7775 Å, and He I at 5876 Å. Other contributing species include Fe II, Si II, and C I. The optical sodium doublet (Na I D) is also marked, but it was not distinguishable in our spectra.

The Ca II absorption feature at 8567 Å is contaminated by two narrower lines at approximately 8120 Å and 8250 Å. Both are visible at all epochs, and are weak compared to the strength of the Ca II absorption. These lines are likely telluric or galactic in origin and are not relevant to our analysis. Similarly, the O I absorption profile at 7775 Å is affected by two narrow, irrelevant features that first appear in the 12 days post-explosion (
+12
d) spectrum and persist until at least
+41
d. The He I absorption feature at 5876 Å overlaps with an unidentified broad emission feature near 5600 Å. This overlap causes the He I feature to appear as two separate absorption minima beyond 12 days. While Ba II is a possible candidate for this emission, it is unlikely that barium would be present in sufficient quantities or have the broad velocity profile required.

Several line profiles exhibit significant temporal evolution. The C I lines at 9183 and 9406 Å initially appear as absorption at +12d and transition to emission by
+24
d. All identified Ca II features also grow stronger over time. In particular, the Ca II line at 7291 Å appears to show a P-Cygni profile at
+61
d. Although it partially overlaps with a He I line, the pronounced increase in strength at later epochs indicates that this feature is dominated by Ca II. Likewise, the identified He I and O I features also grow in prominence relative to the continuum.

Another noticeable trend is the strengthening of the Fe II lines below 6000 Å. The shortest-wavelength Fe II line (4233 Å) is clearly detected from
+6
to
+36
d, after which it falls below the noise threshold. The other three Fe II lines (5169, 5363, and 5535 Å) grow stronger as SN 2024rbc ages, contributing to the gradually rising, continuum-like shape of the spectrum in the region.

The very early rise of SN 2024rbc is similar to that of SN 2020oi. SNe 2020oi and 2005em peak far earlier and more sharply than the rest of the SNe in this sample, in addition to declining rapidly afterwards. This indicates that SN 2024rbc significantly diverges from some, but not all, Type Ic SNe.

Close to the peak, the rising portion of SN 2024rbc’s light curve resembles SNe 2007C and 2008D, but SN 2024rbc rises slightly higher before peaking. The peak of SN 2024rbc lines up remarkably well with SNe 2007C, 2005hg, 2008D, and 2005kl; all of these SNe peak at
\sim
18

>20

\sim

Afterwards, from 50 to 65 days, the light curves transition to a second dimming rate. SN 2024rbc does not trap the entirely of the
56

-1
. This rate is matched or exceeded by all of the other sampled Type Ib SNe, including SN 2009jf.

This epoch is also where the light curve of SN 2024rbc becomes less consistent with that of SN 2007C. The latter declines more quickly past 65 days than the other SNe. After
\sim
125

Considering that uncertainties in photometric data grow as SNe dim, we conclude that the decay rates and light curve morphologies of SNe 2024rbc, 2005hg, and 2008D are largely consistent. This agreement supports SN 2024rbc being Type Ib, but does not rule out a Type Ic classification.

IV.2
Comparison of Optical Spectral Evolution

Figure 7:
An array of optical spectra from SN 2024rbc and several Type Ib and Ic SNe. SN 2024rbc is in black. SNe 2009jf, 2007C, and 2008D are Type Ib (marked in gray-blue, lime, and orange, respectively). Figure 7

By comparing the spectra of SN 2024rbc with those of several Type Ib and Ic SNe, we illustrate SN 2024rbc’s consistency with the spectral features and evolution characteristic of Type Ib SNe.

Less than a week after the explosion, the spectrum of SN 2024rbc exhibits a relatively smooth, blackbody-like profile, similar to that seen in SN 2020oi
(
Rho et al. 2021
)
. As the peak luminosity is reached and passed (around
10-20

The spectroscopic distinction between Type Ib and Type Ic SNe is made by He I features. The shorter He I lines (
<6000
Å) are visible to varying degrees in all sampled supernovae, including SN 2024rbc. He I lines at longer wavelengths (
\sim
6500

6800

(
Richardson et al. 2001
;
Malesani et al. 2009
;
Moskvitin et al. 2010
;
Yaron & Gal-Yam 2012
;
Modjaz et al. 2014
)
, are also visible in SN 2024rbc (most evidently in the +12d spectrum). However, these weaker lines near are absent in the Type Ic SN 2020oi.

Additionally, spectral features from metals in SN 2024rbc show similarities to those of other SNe. The marked Ca II features are similar across all sampled spectra and become apparent at least as early as 9 days post-explosion. The O I feature at 7775 Å is also visible in all spectra. Furthermore, the growing prominence of Fe II emission lines below 6000 Å, which form a continuum-like structure in SN 2024rbc, is also observed in the
>25
day spectra of SN 2008D and SN 2007C
(
Silverman et al. 2012b
;
Shivvers et al. 2019
)
. SN 2009jf
(
Valenti et al. 2011
;
Silverman et al. 2012b
;
Yaron & Gal-Yam 2012
;
Modjaz et al. 2014
;
Shivvers et al. 2019
)
exhibits these Fe II lines as well, although they appear more distinct.
The C I lines, while clearly visible in SN 2024rbc, are not apparent in any of the other spectra, with the possible exception of SN 2009jf.

Based on these spectral similarities, we draw the same conclusion as from the light-curve comparisons: the optical spectra of SN 2024rbc and their evolution are most consistent with SN 2024rbc being a Type Ib SN.

IV.4
Line Analysis

The evolution follows an intuitive progression. The absorption minimum has an initial velocity

Relative to the absorption, the emission maximum exhibits a muted temporal evolution. Starting near rest, the maximum becomes increasingly redshifted and settles at
\sim

km s
-1

Additionally, the FWHM of the absorption and emission components (see
Table 3
) illustrate that the blueshifted absorption of Ca II becomes broader and more prominent as the SN cools. This is consistent with expectations: the blueshifted Ca II, along with other ionized metals in the ejecta and CSM, becomes a dominant spectral feature in the optical spectra as the continuum weakens. The emission profile, in contrast, is widest near peak luminosity, suggesting that the expanding ejecta encounters resistance as it interacts with surrounding material.

Modeling the CO emission requires an estimate of the continuum. Looking at the spectra as a whole (
Figure 12
a), it is clear that the continuum the spectrum rests upon is not the same between the region of the first CO overtone (
>2.27

\mu
m) and the rest of the spectrum. At shorter wavelengths, a simple blackbody spectrum fits the continuum. However, a greater continuum contribution exists just beyond the He I P Cygni profile at 2.06
\mu
m. We argue that this additional continuum component is contributed by recently formed warm (
\lesssim 1100
K) dust in the ejecta of SN 2024rbc. We expect this warm dust to contribute a rising K-band continuum, which the CO overtone would sit upon.

To account for these components, we fit the +62d NIR spectrum in three parts: the blackbody emission from the hot core of the SN, the continuum emission from the warm dust, and the emission from the CO. Here, we provide a description of the models for clarity and convenience. Section 3.4 in
Park et al. 2025
also describes this model.

The blackbody emission from the photosphere was fit using a two-parameter Planck’s Law model

F_{\lambda}=C\frac{2hc^{2}}{\lambda^{5}}\frac{1}{e^{hc/\lambda kT}-1},

(1)

where
C
is a fitting constant for flux scaling and
T
is the effective temperature of the blackbody.

Millard et al. 2021
describes a method of modeling continuum emission from warm dust via a modified version of Planck’s Law. This model is expressed as

F_{\lambda}=\frac{M_{\text{dust}}\kappa_{\lambda}}{d^{2}}B_{\lambda}(T),

(2)

where
F_{\lambda}
is the flux at wavelength
\lambda
,
M_{\text{dust}}
is the dust mass,
\kappa_{\lambda}
is the wavelength-dependent dust absorption coefficient,
d
is distance to the source, and
B_{\lambda}(T)
is Planck’s Law. The absorption coefficient can further be expressed as

\kappa_{\lambda}=\frac{3}{4\rho a}Q_{\text{abs}},

(3)

where
Q_{\text{abs}}
is a wavelength-dependent absorption coefficient,
\rho
is the density of carbon (
2.28\times 10^{-3}
g cm
-3
), and
a
is the grain size. Considering that
Q_{\text{abs}}
is the only wavelength-dependent parameter, if we collect the constants and
M_{\text{dust}}
needed to solve for
F_{\lambda}
into a new, fittable parameter (
N_{\text{const}}
), we get

N_{\text{const}}=\frac{3}{4\rho ad^{2}}M_{\text{dust}}.

(4)

From
N_{\text{const}}
, we can estimate
M_{\text{dust}}
. Thus, the dust continuum emission can be modeled as

F_{\lambda}=N_{\text{const}}Q_{\text{abs}}B_{\lambda}(T),

(5)

leaving us with two parameters to fit for:
T
and
N_{\text{const}}
.

Lastly, the model for the CO flux is derived from
Cami et al. 2010
, which assumes that the CO is isothermal and in LTE. We used line data from
Goorvitch 1994
to inform the model on the physical parameters of the CO molecules. We also assumed that the CO local to the supernova is composed of pure
12
C
16
O, which has been found to be adequate
(
Banerjee et al. 2018
;
Rho et al. 2018a
;
Rho et al. 2024
;
Park et al. 2025
, see)
. Equation (1) of
Cami et al. 2010
calculates the line strength
S_{\nu_{0}}
with respect to the transition frequency
\nu_{0}
as:

S_{\nu_{0}}=\frac{h\nu_{0}}{4\pi}g_{1}B_{12}\frac{e^{-E_{1}/kT}}{P(T)}\left(1-e^{-h\nu_{0}/kT}\right).

(6)

Combined with Equation 5 of
Goorvitch 1994
, we get

S_{\nu_{0}}=\left(8.8523\times 10^{-13}\right)\frac{gf}{P(T)}\frac{1-e^{-h\nu_{0}/kT}}{e^{E_{1}/kT}},

(7)

where
gf
is the product of the statistical weight
g
and emission oscillator strength
f
,
P(T)
is the partition function, and
E_{1}
is the lower level energy. The frequency-dependent CO flux is calculated by

F_{\nu}=\frac{4\pi\left(V_{\text{CO}}t\right)^{2}}{d^{2}}B_{\nu}\left(T_{\text{CO}}\right)\sum_{i}N_{\text{CO}}S_{\nu_{0}}^{i}\phi\left(\nu,V_{\text{CO}}\right),

(8)

where
V_{\text{CO}}
is the line velocity,
t
is time since the explosion,
B_{\nu}
is Planck’s Law with respect to frequency,
N_{\text{CO}}
is the column density, and
\phi\left(\nu,V_{\text{CO}}\right)
is the Gaussian velocity broadening function that sets the line velocity as the FWHM. Using this model, we fit for the CO line velocity, temperature, and dust mass
M_{\text{CO}}
, which is estimated from
N_{\text{CO}}
as

M_{\text{CO}}=4\pi\left(V_{\text{CO}}t\right)^{2}mN_{\text{CO}},

(9)

where
m
is the molecular mass of CO (
\sim
28
AMU).

The CO mass derived without assuming LTE is expected to exceed the LTE estimate by more than an order of magnitude. For example, in the case of SN 1987A, the non-LTE CO mass was found to be
40-100

(

\sim

M_{\odot}

Observing the first CO overtone implies strong emission at the CO fundamental and consequently significant CO formation/excitation. The presence of CO at early times, given its function as a coolant, lends credence to SN 2024rbc’s potential for productive dust formation. Further examination of dust formation around SN 2024rbc would require observations by a spectrograph with mid–infrared (MIR) capabilities, such as MIRI–LRS on JWST.
Medler et al. 2025
provides an excellent example of the details MIR observations could uncover regarding dust formation using Type II SN 2023ixf, which also exhibited significant emission at the first CO overtone.

The dust mass estimated by this fitting (
\sim
10^{-3}

M_{\odot}
) by itself is certainly insufficient to answer to the task of dust generation in the early Universe, which would require (at minimum) a total formed dust mass
>10^{-1}

M_{\odot}

(
Nozawa et al. 2003
;
Todini & Ferrara 2001
;
Sluder et al. 2018
)
. However, as this measurement is conducted on a very early spectrum, there is significant room for growth. Figure 5 in
Tinyanont et al. 2025
provides a helpful guide to the potential of SN 2024rbc in terms of dust formation. Type Ic SN 2014C initially registered a dust mass of
\sim
10^{-3}

M_{\odot}
at approximately 250 days, which is comparable to the dust found in SN 2024rbc at only 62 days. Considering that SN 2014C was measured to have a dust mass of
\sim
10^{-1}

M_{\odot}
after
\sim
3500 days, we argue that SN 2024rbc shows potential for significant dust formation over the next decade. This possibility invites follow-up observations of SN 2024rbc and other, new Type Ib SNe on this timescale.

Finally, this overtone observation is of particular interest as it is the first observation of such a feature in a Type Ib SN.
Table 5

. However, is not necessarily the fastest SN to allow CO formation. Why is it rare to detect CO in a Type Ib SN compared to Type Ic? Examining the population of Type Ib and Ic SNe in the Transient Name Server (TNS) during 2024–2025 with discovery magnitudes
<21
mag, we find that the numbers are comparable. However, when we restrict to
<19
mag, the number of Type Ib SNe is about 20% smaller than Type Ic. Thus, it is possible that Type Ib SNe have a slightly smaller population, but we note that the TNS does not contain a complete sample. Furthermore, Type Ib SNe show strong helium emission, although most of the detected lines are from neutral helium. It is possible that some helium is ionized (He
+
), which affects the timing of molecular formation.
Cherchneff et al. 2025
showed that when sufficient He
+
is present, CO formation is suppressed.

Now that early CO formation been confirmed in all types of SESNe, the need for further study to quantify its impact on efficient dust formation is strongly emphasized. Theoretical models are also needed to further understand the differences in CO formation and cooling, and their connection to dust formation, in Type Ib compared with Type Ic and other SNe.

V
Conclusions

Our conclusions are as follows:

1.

The light curves of SN 2024rbc show clear, singular peaks at
12-18
days post-explosion and a two phase decline afterwards. The
r
band light curve of SN 2024rbc is in strong agreement with other Type Ib and some Type Ic SNe.

2.

Fitting model light curves from a He star progenitor indicate a pre-explosion He star mass of
3.1

M_{{}_{\odot}}
with M(
56
Ni)
=0.07

M_{\odot}
, E
{}_{\text{Explosion}}=10^{51}
erg,
\text{M}_{\text{Ejecta}}=1.7

M_{\odot}
, and the
56
Ni-mixing parameter
f_{m}=0.5
. Furthermore, the absence of bright optical emission at very early times suggests that the CSM around SN 2024rbc was moderate at the time of the explosion.

3.

The spectra of SN 2024rbc exhibit numerous atomic and ionized metal lines (Mg I, Ca I, Si I, Ca II, Si II, and Fe II) and several uncommon lines (C I, Na I). Strong and broad Ca II and Mg I emission, absorption, and P-Cygni profiles are present in the optical and NIR spectra. Comparison of the spectral evolution of SN 2024rbc to other Type Ib and Ic SNe support the classification of SN 2024rbc as a Type Ib SN.

4.

At 62 days post-explosion, a K-band continuum emission indicative of warm dust is evident in the NIR spectra. Additionally, the first CO overtone appears at this time and is the first confirmed observation of CO in a Type Ib SN. LTE modeling of the CO overtone gives a temperature of
\sim
4000
K, a velocity between
6000
and
8000
km s
-1
, and a mass between
5
and
6\times 10^{-4}

M_{\odot}
. Modeling the dust continuum indicates a dust temperature of
910\pm 10
K and a mass of
(1.3\pm 0.1)\times 10^{-3}

M_{\odot}
.

5.

The early detection of CO and dust in the Type Ib SN 2024rbc lends further credence to the idea that rapid, efficient dust formation is possible in SESNe. Follow-up observations and further study of this class of SNe are necessary to better understand the relationship between CO and dust formation and the plausibility of dust formation via CCSNe in the early Universe.

APPENDIX

A. Data Reduction Comparisons

We compare the
xdgnirs
,
pypeit
, and
Figaro
reductions of the observation by GNIRS at +62d. The reduction process for
xdgnirs
was described in Section
II.4
. The process for
Figaro
was discussed in
Rho et al. 2018a
. Below, we describe the reduction process for
pypeit

(
Prochaska et al. 2020a
)
.

The raw data was reduced without A–B pairing. The flats and arc frames obtained during the observation were also provided to
pypeit
. The pipeline performed flat-fielding, wavelength calibration, aperture identification, tracing, and spectral extraction automatically. Flux calibration, order stitching, and telluric correction were then carried out as separate, manually initiated steps within the
pypeit
toolset following the initial reduction. The nearby A2V star HIP 4129 was used as both a telluric and flux standard to minimize differences in airmass. The final reduced spectrum is continuous over the wavelength range
0.817-2.496

\mu
m. Infrared data reduction with
pypeit
is also described in detail by
Tinyanont et al. 2024
.

The spectra produced by the three reduction methods are shown in
Figure 13
. We find that they are largely consistent with one another.
