The ITM2B-associated retinal dystrophy mutation modifies BRI23 peptide interactions in the human retina
Authors: Tasnim Ben Yacoub, Andréa Amprou, Camille Letellier, Christelle Michiels, Julien Navarro, Said El Shamieh, Tual Monfort, Salvatore Azzollini, Juliette Wohlschlegel, Guillaume Chevreux, Véronique Legros, Morgane Lemao, Guy Lenaers, Kate Grieve, Manuela Argentini, Christina Zeitz, Isabelle Audo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BRI23, composed of the 23 last amino acids of the integral transmembrane protein 2B (ITM2B) C-terminus, is associated with several neurodegenerative diseases, including retinal dystrophy (RD) and familial dementia. Its role in the retina remains poorly understood. This study provides a comprehensive analysis of BRI23 interactome in the human retina. Using a peptide-bead coupling system, we identified 2302 proteins, primarily involved in mitochondrial processes, synaptic transmission and photoreceptor function. Our findings show that the BRI23-RD variant, associated with the ITM2B-related RD (IRRD), exhibits significantly altered protein interactions compared to the wild-type form. Notably, we observed an increased abundance of mitochondrial proteins and synaptic molecules, indicating a potential disruption of cellular pathways driven by the IRRD variant.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-53283-z.

Introduction

The integral transmembrane protein 2B (ITM2B), also called British dementia protein-2 (BRI2), is a ubiquitous protein processed in the Golgi apparatus. In this organelle, the protein is cleaved by a Furin-like protease releasing a 23-amino acid peptide, BRI23, while the mature form of the protein, mITM2B, is translocated towards the plasma membrane. There, mITM2B undergoes further proteolysis releasing the BRICHOS domain and a small intracellular peptide1. Mutations of BRI23 have been associated with several forms of familial dementia, including British dementia (FBD), Danish Dementia (FDD), Chinese dementia (FCD) and Korean dementia (FKD), all inherited as an autosomal dominant trait2–5. In 2014, our group identified a novel autosomal dominant retinal dystrophy (RD), without dementia in a large pedigree6. This unusual RD combines an early loss of retinal ganglion cells (RGCs), an electronegative full-field electroretinogram (ERG) indicative of post-phototransduction dysfunction with possible additional cone photoreceptor (PhR) dysfunction and an increased reflectivity of the inner retinal layers on Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT), suggestive of material accumulation6,7. Whole Exome Sequencing in this family identified a missense variant, c.782 A > C (RD mutation), in ITM2B modifying an amino acid within the BRI23 peptide (BRI23-RD)6 whereas in the FDD, FBD, FCD and FKD dementias, the distinct genetic variants all lead to a longer form of BRI23, resulting in amyloid-like deposits in the brain. The BRI23-RD variant, however, does not lead to a longer peptide but instead causes an amino acid substitution, p.(Glu261Ala), which may alter protein functions or interactions. Previous in silico predictions showed that this mutation leads to a conformational change in the beta-sheet structure possibly altering peptide interactions in retinal tissue7. While the role of ITM2B in synaptic functions, in the amyloid precursor protein (APP) processing and in apoptosis has been documented, primarily in the brain8–10, its precise role in the retina remains unclear. A prior proteomic analysis by our group identified the ITM2B interactome in human retina11. In this previous study, several proteins known to interact with ITM2B in the brain, involved in the amyloid pathway, cytoskeleton or vesicular trafficking, were identified. Interestingly new additional partners of ITM2B related to cellular metabolism and mitochondrial homeostasis not previously identified in brain studies, were also found. In considering the recent discovery of the RD mutation in BRI23 region of ITM2B in human retinal dystrophy, we hypothesized that (a) BRI23 could recruit specific partners important for ITM2B functions; (b) The RD mutation of the BRI23 peptide (Glu261Ala) could affect the interactions with its partners. To test these hypotheses, quantitative proteomics was performed on human retinal proteins purified with two synthetic peptides, so as to better understand the pathophysiology responsible for the IRRD. In this study, we used a coupling peptide-Epoxy beads system, with BRI23-WT and BRI23-RD, the latter carrying the RD mutation. We identified many proteins involved in synaptic function, photoreceptor outer-segment and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation. Several mitochondrial partners already found, in the previous study were common in this study indicating that BRI23 may be an important docking site of ITM2B protein-protein interactions. Interestingly, some of these proteins showed a differential interaction profile between BRI23-RD and BRI23-WT suggesting that the RD mutation may change the interactome of the peptide. For evident reasons, the human tissue used here has a very limited and highly regulated availability. Therefore, to confirm and expand on the distinct protein interactions found in donor retina, we used normal and patient fibroblasts as a second model. Of particular interest from the interactome were the changes in mitochondrial respiration and previous studies have shown these cells to be appropriate for mirroring neuronal mitochondrial abnormalities12,13. We therefore conducted a metabolic analysis of human fibroblasts isolated from healthy and RD patients, aiming to identify putative metabolic dysfunction in these cells. From this study we can determine whether mitochondrial or synaptic roles are more critically associated with BRI23-associated retinal degeneration.

Results

BRI23 interactome in the human retina

To gain insight into the function of BRI23 in the retina, we first investigated its global protein interactions with BRI23 peptide by quantitative mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) without initially distinguishing between BRI23-RD and BRI23-WT. The two forms of the synthetized peptide were coupled to magnetic beads containing surface EPOXY groups, allowing a covalent binding of the peptide via primary amino groups (Fig. 1). This coupling system was then incubated with retinal human protein extract obtained from a post-mortem human eye. The isolated proteins were purified and subjected to analysis, identifying a total of 3080 proteins in both conditions, with absolute protein quantity being distinct between BRI23-RD and BRI23-WT. To exclude non-specific interactions, we selected only proteins that were not found in the negative control condition (free beads) and with at least three unique peptides identified. After applying these criteria, 2239 proteins were identified as interactors of both BRI23-RD and BRI23-WT peptides (Fig. 2A and Supplementary data 2).

We conducted a Gene Ontology (GO) term analyses on the purified proteins, focusing on Biological Processes (BP), Cellular Components (CC), and pathways identified through the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (Fig. 2B). Consistent with our previous findings on the ITM2B interactome in the human retina, BRI23 was also found to interact with APLP2 and APP showing a probable specificity of interaction with BRI23 region. Other Alzheimer disease-associated proteins, such as APOE, which had been shown previously to interact with ITM2B, were also present in our dataset.

The GO analysis revealed that the predominant cellular components involved were mitochondria, neuronal axons, and synapses (Supplementary data 3). Several biological processes overlapped with those observed in the ITM2B interactome; oxidative phosphorylation and mitochondrial processes being the most prominent. Notably, several mitochondrial proteins, including cytochrome c oxidase (COX) and subunits of the NADH oxidoreductase (NDUF) family, were identified. Additionally, processes related to translation, cytoskeletal dynamics, vesicle trafficking, and ribosomal function were also enriched.

Interestingly, several proteins involved in synaptic dynamics were identified, including the kinesin-like protein KIF3A, and the synaptic vesicle glycoproteins SV2A and SV2B. Among the synaptic pathways identified via the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis, GABAergic and glutamatergic synapses were overrepresented, notably glutamine synthetase (GLUL) and GABA type B receptor subunit 1 (GABBR1).

We also identified interacting proteins associated with various retinal cell types, particularly photoreceptors. Among these were key proteins involved in phototransduction, such as rhodopsin (RHO), cone-arrestin (ARR3), as well as proteins localizing at the photoreceptor discs, including the retinal outer segment membrane protein 1 (ROM1) and interphotoreceptor matrix proteins IMPG1 and IMPG2. Additionally, markers for retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) were identified, such as tubulin alpha and beta chain proteins (TUBB3). The Muller glial cell markers, retinaldehyde-binding protein 1 (RLBP1), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and the visual system homeobox 2 (VSX2), a bipolar cell marker were also present. We found that 42 of our BRI23 interacting proteins overlapped with a retina-enriched database of 143 retina specific proteins (The human proteome in retina - The Human ProteinAtlas, (https://www.proteinatlas.org/humanproteome/tissue/retina) (Supplementary data1) suggesting the widespread distribution of BRI23 in the human retina. Collectively, these results suggest that the BRI23 peptide is linked to mitochondrial function and synaptic dynamics across multiple retinal cell types, particularly in photoreceptor (PhR) cells and in the inner retina.

Differential protein abundance in BRI23-RD and BRI23-WT conditions

After identifying the global protein partners of BRI23 in the human retina, we focused on differences in protein interactions between BRI23-RD and BRI23-WT conditions. Among the 2239 interactors, a total of 118 proteins displayed comparable abundance in both RD and WT conditions, while 202 proteins were more enriched in the BRI23-RD condition and 153 were less enriched (Figs. 2A and 3A and Supplementary data 2). Proteins consistently showing low abundance levels were not considered. Using these criteria, no significant loss or new gain of interaction was observed in either condition, as the 2239 proteins were detected in both, although sometimes with notable differences in abundance (Figs. 2A and 3A).

Among the most differentially abundant proteins, we identified extracellular matrix proteins such as Interphotoreceptor Matrix Proteoglycan 1 (IMPG1), which was ~ 13 times more enriched in the BRI23-RD condition compared to BRI23-WT. Conversely, collagen type I alpha 1 chain (COL1A1) was ~ 12 times less abundant in the RD condition (Fig. 3A and B).

Proteins enriched in the BRI23-RD condition were linked to pathways in oxidative phosphorylation, amino acid transport across membranes, and translation (Supplementary data 3). The GO-CC analysis showed they were primarily associated with mitochondria, synaptic vesicles and photoreceptors. Notably, several mitochondrial proteins, including COX5B, and MT-ND1, were significantly more abundant in the BRI23-RD, suggesting that the RD mutation in BRI23 may disrupt interactions with mitochondrial partners. Furthermore, the KEGG pathway for phototransduction was also enriched, with rod photoreceptor-associated proteins such as, ROM1, showing increased abundance. Additionally, numerous amino acid and ion transporters, implicated in the synaptic transmission and related to the Solute Carrier Family (SLC proteins) displayed a higher abundance, notably SLC38A3, involved in the GABA-glutamine cycle for neurotransmitter recycling, and SLC24A1 (Na+/Ca2+, K+ exchanger, NCKX1) involved in phototransduction (Fig. 3D). APP also showed a two-fold increase in abundance, indicating an altered interaction profile in the RD condition (Fig. 3A and B). Furthermore, the pro-apoptotic protein, BCL2 Associated Transcription Factor 1 (BCLAF1), previously shown to interact with ITM2B, was nearly 3 times more enriched in BRI23-RD (Fig. 3A).

In contrast, proteins less abundant in BRI23-RD condition were predominantly associated with cytoplasmic translation, synaptic vesicle recycling, and axon extension regulation. These proteins were related to ribosomes, neurons, and microtubules features. The KEGG pathways identified the Ribosome, Synaptic Vesicle Cycle, and Spliceosome pathways. Photoreceptor markers such as Arrestin 3 (ARR3), displayed a two-fold decrease in abundance compared to BRI23-WT condition. Proteins involved in axonal function, including Synaptojanin 1 (SYNJ1) and Shootin-1 (SHTN1), were significantly less enriched, indicating increased synaptic dynamics. Additionally, CCAR2 (Cell Cycle and Apoptosis Regulator Protein 2) was twofold more abundant in the BRI23-RD condition (Fig. 3A and B).

To validate this quantitative proteomic analysis, we performed immunoblotting for IMPG1 and COX5B. Western blot analysis revealed increased protein quantity for COX5B and IMPG1 in BRI23-RD, thus confirming our differential protein analysis (Fig. 3C). Of note, no band was observed for IMPG1 in BRI23-WT (Fig. 3C).

This comparative analysis highlights that the RD mutation in the BRI23 peptide alters its affinity for many retinal proteins, notably those involved in mitochondrial function, synaptic homeostasis, and translation. In order to confirm that these interactions are not solely due to the loss of compartmentalization following cell lysis, immunostaining of the wild-type BRI23 peptide was performed on human retinal sections. BRI23 was found to localize to the synaptic layers, as shown by co-staining with PSD95, as well as to photoreceptor membranes. However, whereas PSD95 is a postsynaptic marker, BRI23 appears to be localized presynaptically. Furthermore, BRI23 co-localized with ATPβ, a mitochondrial marker (Fig. 3E).

Similar BRI23 localization and metabolic activity in control and patient fibroblasts

Since BRI23 is cleaved in the Golgi apparatus, prior to the translocation of the mature ITM2B (mITM2B) form to the plasma membrane, we determined if the mutation affects the localization of endogenous BRI23 or ITM2B within the cell such as retention of the peptide within the Golgi. We investigated the subcellular localization of BRI23 in fibroblasts derived from a retinal dystrophy (RD) patient and an unaffected control using monoclonal antibodies specifically targeting either the BRI23 region or an upstream region of the protein. Both ITM2B and BRI23 displayed widespread localization, with BRI23 exhibiting a more diffuse distribution throughout the cell body. ITM2B staining was predominantly concentrated in the Golgi apparatus (highlighted with circles in Fig. 4A. Supplementary Data 4). We did not notice any retention on BRI23 in the patient fibroblasts compared to its control. No detectable differences in the localization of ITM2B or BRI23 were observed between RD patient and control fibroblasts, suggesting that the mutation does not affect their subcellular distribution in human fibroblasts (Fig. 4A).

Given that human fibroblasts can serve as models to assess oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) activity of mitochondrial diseases14, we further investigated whether the differential enrichment of mitochondrial proteins between BRI23-RD versus BRI23-WT, as observed in the GO-term analysis, would induce a mitochondrial defect in patient fibroblasts. To assess this, we analyzed cell metabolic activity using the Interface Self-Referenced Dynamic full-field optical coherence tomography (iSR D-FFOCT). This label-free high resolution technique enables real-time monitoring of cellular activity fluctuations, allowing real-time observation of mitochondrial activity and cellular function15.

The imaging results showed no significant difference in metabolic activity between patient and control fibroblasts. Slowly varying stress fibers (blue, white arrows) were similarly present in both conditions. Likewise, yellow dots cloud (indicated by stars), likely representing stressed mitochondria or transport vesicles, near the lamellipodia and within the cytoplasm, appeared comparable between RD and control fibroblasts (Fig. 4B). To further investigate mitochondrial function, we measured the mitochondrial oxygen consumption from intact and permeabilized control and mutated fibroblasts using oxygraphy. No significant difference was observed in the oxidative phosphorylation or in the activity of mitochondrial complexes I and II of the respiratory chain (Fig. 4C and Supplementary data 5), suggesting that the RD mutation does not impact the metabolic activity of the mutated fibroblasts.

Discussion

The BRI23 peptide, a product of ITM2B proteolysis, is implicated in several diseases, including familial dementias and retinal dystrophy, underscoring its importance in neurons, particularly of the brain and the retina. While ITM2B has been associated with glutamate synaptic transmission10, apoptosis8 and amyloid-beta processing9,16 in the brain, the specific function of BRI23 in the retina remains unclear.

Our previous work11 identified ITM2B partners in the retina using immunoprecipitation of the endogenous ITM2B protein, though it was uncertain whether BRI23 itself was specifically targeted. To look for specific partners of the BRI23 domain, we used a synthetic peptide-bead coupling system, enabling a precise investigation of BRI23’s retinal interactions and a direct comparison between the control and the mutated peptides. Importantly, this synthetic peptide is expected to closely resemble its physiological counterpart, as no post-translational modification sites have been reported in the literature. This study therefore represents the first proteomic analysis of native and mutated BRI23 in IRRD.

Overall, BRI23-RD demonstrated a stronger enrichment with several proteins than BRI23-WT, suggesting an increased affinity for its retinal protein partners. Considering the likely dominant negative effect of the mutation, it is plausible that BRI23-RD has acquired novel or enhanced functions compared to the wild-type peptide that could affect the normal physiology of the retina.

Several synaptic markers, including SNARE complex proteins such as SYT1 and STX1A, were enriched, indicating that BRI23 might contribute to synaptic transmission between photoreceptors (PhRs) and bipolar cells. This function would be impaired in case of IRRD mutation explaining the inner retinal dysfunction observed in the patient’s electroretinogram6,7. Notably, proteins in the SLC family, involved in transmembrane ion exchange and amino acid transport related to neurotransmission, were predominantly enriched in the BRI23-RD condition. This enrichment may indicate that the IRRD adversely affects retinal synaptic homeostasis. Additionally, APP protein was also found enriched in our protein data set. Since ITM2B maturation by BRI23 cleavage is required to interact with APP and inhibit APP processing in the plasma membrane16, the BRI23 peptide likely does not directly interact with APP, and the APP detected may correspond to the Aβ region. Indeed, BRI23 interacts with Aβ to inhibit its aggregation in the brain17. APP and APLP2 are known to play a role in synaptic transmission in the brain, but their function in the retina remains poorly understood. Given the interactions of BRI23 with several synaptic proteins identified in this study, it may exert a synergistic effect with APP and APLP2 in retinal synaptic transmission. The observed differences in BRI23-RD/APP and BRI23-WT/APP interactions, along with the identification of other synaptic partners in our dataset, provide strong evidence supporting a synaptic role for BRI23, which may be modified in its mutated form.

It remains possible that Aβ/BRI23 interactions serve distinct roles in the retina compared to the brain and that BRI23 might, independently of mITM2B, interact with APP protein. In our study, APP were more abundant in the BRI23-RD condition and given that APP deletion alter synaptic transmission in rodent brains18, the altered interactions with BRI23-RD could contribute to the retinal synaptic dysfunction.

Furthermore, PhRs and inner retinal cell markers were identified in our previous work on ITM2B interactome in the retina11. Here we showed that BRI23 interacts either directly or indirectly, with 42 proteins highly enriched in the retina including numerous PhRs proteins involved in phototransduction. This suggests that cleaved BRI23 plays a role in light response. Markers of bipolar cells, RGCs, horizontal cells and Muller glia were also present, supporting the broad localization of BRI23 across retinal cell types and its probable implication in many cellular functions. Notably, proteins from amacrine cells were absent in our data set.

Cytoskeleton markers were also detected, suggesting that BRI23 is transported within cells via the cytoskeletal network and secreted extracellularly. BRI23 interactions with both intracellular and extracellular molecules further indicate multifunctional properties. BRI23 interactions with the proteoglycans IMPG1 and IMPG2 imply the potential involvement of BRI23 in the interphotoreceptor matrix (IPM), where the proteoglycan protein SPACR and SPARCAN, encoded by IMPG1 and IMPG2 respectively, play crucial roles in the maturation of the photoreceptor outer segments (OS) and are important for PhRs integrity19–21. The increased affinity between BRI23-RD and SPACR could influence SPACR proteolysis, potentially compromising photoreceptor and retinal functions.

Additionally, mitochondrial proteins were more enriched in BRI23-RD than in BRI23-WT fractions. Mitochondrial pathways have been previously associated with ITM2B11, and their enrichment here suggest potential alterations in mitochondrial function in the mutated condition, potentially leading to mitochondrial stress or dysfunction. Given the high energy demand of PhRs and RGCs, mitochondrial impairment could lead to cell death, similar to metabolic decline in the aging retina and the alteration of the optic nerve in most mitochondrial diseases22,23.

This type of proteomic analysis presents certain limitations, as it relies on the use of retinal protein lysates. Such an approach does not preserve cellular compartmentalization and allows the peptide to interact with a broad range of proteins, regardless of their subcellular localization. Nevertheless, we performed immunostaining on human retinal sections, which confirmed the localization of the peptide at synapses and in photoreceptors, and its co-localization with a mitochondrial marker.

Due to the high representation of mitochondrial processes and electron transport chain components in the BRI23-RD condition, we evaluated the metabolic activity from human derived fibroblasts to detect any mitochondrial OXPHOS differences. Live iSR D-FFOCT imaging showed no observable difference in metabolic activity or organelle morphology between mutant and control human derived fibroblasts. Subsequent oxygraphy experiments disclosed comparable activity in complexes I and II of the respiratory chain between patient and control fibroblasts, suggesting that the mutation does not impair fibroblast mitochondrial respiratory function. However, fibroblasts may not be the right model to document the consequences of mitochondrial dysfunction associated with the RD variant, given the high energy demand of retinal cells, a mitochondrial dysfunction could be specifically detected in retinal cells and not in fibroblasts. This result nonetheless demonstrates that there is not a direct cell-autonomous negative effect of the mutation on non-neuronal cell respiration.

Overall, these findings suggest that the BRI23-RD mutation is more likely to alter protein interactions rather than to cause mislocalization of the BRI23 peptide. Although fibroblasts can serve as a useful model for assessing mitochondrial functions in various diseases, they may not fully capture and reflect the high energy demand of retinal cells. Therefore, a model closer to the native retina would be more suited to investigate the consequences of the BRI23 mutation. To address this, we recently generated retinal organoids from induced pluripotent stem cells derived from RD patients, unaffected controls, and CRISPR/Cas9-corrected clones, to better model the disease and investigate its mechanisms11,24. These organoid models will enable a more precise understanding of ITM2B and BRI23 physiological and pathological roles in the human retina.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive identification of the BRI23 peptide interactome in the retina, uncovering crucial association with mitochondrial and synaptic components suggesting a functional role for BRI23 in these key cellular processes. Moreover, the substantial differences in protein enrichment between the BRI23-WT and BRI23-RD underscore the impact of the IRRD mutation, likely driven by structural changes secondary to the amino acid substitution. Notably, BRI23 localization within human fibroblasts remains unchanged between wild-type and RD cells, suggesting that the mutation affects interaction dynamics rather than the subcellular positioning. While fibroblast assays revealed no discernible impact on mitochondrial respiration, these results may not fully capture the high energy demands of retinal cells, leaving open the possibility of mutation-induced mitochondrial dysfunction within the retinal environment. To address this, further research using patient-derived retinal organoids will be essential to assess BRI23 mutation on mitochondrial function within the retina. Elucidating the cellular function of BRI23 will provide significant insights that may pave the way for targeted therapeutic approaches with further potential applications for retinal and neurodegenerative disorders.

Materials and methods

Human retinal specimens

Human retinal specimens were collected as performed before11. In brief, postmortem human ocular globes were obtained from donors at the School of Surgery (Ecole de Chirurgie, Assistance Publique Hôpitaux de Paris, Paris, France) and the Laboratory of Anatomy (Faculty of Medicine of St-Etienne, France). All experiments involving postmortem human retina adhered to the national regulations and the guidelines outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The protocol received approved from the Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) of the School of Surgery and the Faculty of Medicine of St-Etienne. The authorization to use human samples for research at the Institut de la Vision is registered under CODECOH DC-2015-2400.

Two postmortem human retinas were obtained from a 91-year-old woman with no known history of eye-related disorders. The entire retina, excluding the macula, was immediately immersed in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C. Before its use, it was thawed on ice and carefully sectioned into small fragments to standardize the sample for protein extraction. Protein extraction was performed using 1.8 ml of lysis buffer (50 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, and 1% Triton X-100), supplemented with protease inhibitors (Merck) and 1% phosphatase inhibitors Cocktail 2 and 3 (Merck, Saint-Quentin-Fallavier, France) on ice for 30 min, with vortexing and pipetting every 10 min. The mixture was then centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant containing the proteins was transferred to a new tube.

Epoxy beads coupling and protein precipitation

Schematic of protein precipitation using the mutant (BRI23-RD) versus the native (BRI23-WT) peptide coupled to Epoxy Dynabeads.

Schematic of protein precipitation using the mutant (BRI23-RD) versus the native (BRI23-WT) peptide coupled to Epoxy Dynabeads.

To identify differences in the interaction of the normal (WT) and mutated (RD) forms of BRI23, two peptides were synthesized (WT: EASNCFAIRHFENKFAVETLICS, RD: EASNCFAIRHFENKFAVATLICS, Merck). The lyophilized, monomeric peptides were reconstituted in PBS before being coupled to Epoxy Dynabeads magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Courtabœuf, France, 14301) following the supplier’s protocol. For both conditions, 2.5 mg of beads were incubated with 50 µg of peptides during 24 h in a solution containing. Beads were then washed using a tris buffer saline 1X (TBS 1X) solution to eliminate any excess. The bead-peptide complexes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with 100 µg of human retinal proteins. After incubation, the eluate containing uncoupled proteins was removed and beads were washed 4 times with TBS 1X and a final wash was performed with PBS 1X. The bead complexes incubated in 10 µl PBS were then transported on ice to the ProteoSeine proteomics platform (Paris) for LC-MSMS analysis (BU Quantification). Four replicates (n = 4) were performed, including a control condition with unbound beads to identify nonspecific interactions.

Sample preparation for LC-MS/MS analysis

Beads from pulldown experiments were incubated overnight at 37 °C with 20 µl of 50 mM NH4HCO3 buffer containing one µg of sequencing-grade trypsin/Lys C mix (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). The digested peptides were then loaded on evotips provided by Evosep (Odense, Denmark) for desalting, following the manufacturer’s protocol in preparation for LC-MS/MS analysis.

LC-MS/MS acquisition

Samples were analyzed on a timsTOF Pro 2 mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) coupled to an Evosep one system (Evosep, Odense, Denmark) operating with the manufacturer’s 30SPD (Spectral Counts per Distinct Peptide) method. This method features a 44-min gradient with a total cycle time of 48 min. A C18 analytical column (0.15 × 150 mm, 1.9 μm beads, ref EV-1106) was used equilibrated at 40 °C and operated at a flow rate of 500 nl/min. Solvent A constated of H2O/0.1% formic acid (FA) (Thermofisher Schientific), while solvent B was Acetonitrile (ACN) (Thermofisher Scientific)/0.1% FA. The timsTOF Pro 2 was run with a DDA-PASEF (data-independent acquisition - parallel accumulation serial fragmentation.) mode25 over a 1.3 s cycle time. Mass spectra for MS and MS/MS scans were recorded between 100 and 1700 m/z.

Data analysis

MS raw files were processed using PEAKS Online 12 (build 2.0, Bioinformatics Solutions Inc.). Data were searched against the Uniport Homo Sapiens database modified by replacing the ITM2B sequence with the baits i.e. the wild type and mutated Bri23 peptides (downloaded July 2023, 26827 entries). Parent mass tolerance was set to 20 ppm, with fragment mass tolerance to 0.05 Da. Semi-specific tryptic cleavages were selected and a maximum of 2 missed cleavages were allowed. The following post-translational modifications were considered for identification: Oxidation (M), Deamidation (NQ), Phosphorylation (STY) and Half of a disulfide bridge (C) as variables. Identifications were filtered based on a 1% FDR (False Discovery Rate) threshold at both peptide and protein group levels. Label-free quantification was performed using the PEAKS Online 12 quantification modules, with a mass tolerance of 5 ppm, a CCS error tolerance of 0.02 and a retention time shift tolerance of 0.25 min for match-between-runs. Protein abundance was inferred using the top N peptide method and Total Ion Current (TIC) was used for normalization.

Multivariate statistical analysis was conducted using Qlucore Omics Explorer 3.9 (Qlucore AB, Lund, SWEDEN). A positive threshold value of 1 was set to allow a log2 transformation of abundance data for normalization. Values below this threshold were replaced by one prior to transformation. The transformed data were finally used for statistical analysis i.e. the evaluation of differentially present proteins between two groups using a bilateral Student’s t-test. A corrected p-value better than 0.05 according to the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was used to filter differential candidates between the mutated and wild type Bri23 baits.

Gene ontology analysis were performed using the Metascape database (https://metascape.org/) for Gene Ontology (GO) and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) study26.

Immunoblotting assay

To confirm the interaction between BRI23-RD and BRI23-WT and their partners, we conducted EPOXY assay followed by Western blotting using protein extracts from normal human retinas. Elution of the proteins from the beads was performed by adding dithiothréitol (DTT) to the beads and putting the tubes 5 min at 90 °C. Proteins from the human retinal lysate and those eluted from the co-immunoprecipitation assays, were separated on a 4–12% gradient SDS-PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. After transfer, the membranes were blocked with a buffer (PBST: 1X PBS, 0.05% Tween 20) containing 5% non-fat dry milk, and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with gentle shaking in the presence of primary antibodies (IMPG1 sc-377366 1:1000, COX5B PA5 96189 1:1000). The membranes were then washed three times for 10 min with PBST, followed by a 1-hour incubation at room temperature with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:10 000). After three additional 10-minute washes with PBST, protein detection was performed using an enhanced chemiluminescence-based system (ECL Plus, Thermo Fisher Scientific). After each blot, the membrane was stripped for 15 min using ReBlot Plus Strong solution (Merck, 2504), then re-blocked for 1 h before incubation with the next antibody.

Culture of fibroblasts

Human dermal fibroblasts from a healthy 49-year-old Caucasian male and an IRRD patient were obtained via a skin biopsy at the Quinze-Vingts Hospital (Approval by French regulatory agencies: CPP Ile de France V (2012-A01333-40; P12-02) and the ANSM (B121362-32)). The fibroblasts were cultured in T75 flasks using DMEM GlutaMAX medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 61965026) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS), 2% sodium pyruvate, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 1% amphotericin B. Cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Once the fibroblasts reached 70% confluence, they were detached with TrypLE Express (1X) Enzyme (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 12605010) and plated for DFFOCT on an uncoated 6-well glass-bottom plate (Cellvis, P06-1.5H_N) at a density of 40,000 cells per well, with the medium replaced every two days. Imaging was performed five days after seeding. For immunostaining, fibroblasts were plated in 12 mm Ø glass coverslips in 24-well plate coated with Poly-D lysin at a density of 2 000 cells per well. Fibroblasts, grown on glass slides in 24-well plates, were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min prior to immunostaining.

Immunostaining

Fibroblasts were incubated for 1 h at room temperature in buffer A, which contain 1X Phosphate Buffered Saline (PBX 1X), 0.2% gelatin and 0.25% Triton (PBSGT). Slides were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody (ITM2B: PA519074 1:500, Themo Fisher Scientific; BRI23: [EASNCFAIRHFENK] kindly provided by Olivier Wirths27 and 4’,6-diamidino-2-phénylindole (DAPI) with 1/1000 dilution in PBSGT. After incubation, cells were washed three times with a solution containing PBS 1X and 0.1% Tween. Next, the cells were incubated 1 h at room temperature with 1:1000 diluted secondary antibodies conjugated with either Alexa Fluor 488, or Cy3 along with DAPI 1:1000 dilution to counterstain the nuclei. Fluorescent staining signals were visualized using an epifluorescence microscope (Olympus DM6000).

Interface self-referenced dynamic full-field optical coherence tomography (iSR DFFOCT)

Fibroblasts were imaged using Interface Self-referenced dynamic Full-Field Dynamic Optical Coherence Tomography (iSR-D-FFOCT), a label free high-resolution microscopy technique. The setup is described in Monfort et al.15. Briefly, an incoherent light source at 810 nm (M810L3, Thorlabs, Newport, NJ, USA), with a spectral bandwidth of 25 nm, was used in combination with a high numerical aperture objective of 1.05 (UPLSAPO30XSIR, Olympus, Japan), providing a resolution of 386 × 386 × 735 nm³ (x, y,z). The near-infra-red wavelength prevented any photodamage and phenotype anomalies28. Interferences were created in a self-reference fashion thanks to the specular surface of the glass coverslip used for sample mounting, at the bottom of a multiwell plate, itself placed in a top stage incubator (H201-K-FRAME, H201-MW-HOLDER, and OBJ-COLLAR-2532, Okolab, Italy) to provide an optimal physiological environment for fibroblasts. A series of 512 en-face interferograms were recorded, at 100 frames per second, with an exposure time of 9.5 ms, on a high full well capacity CMOS camera (Q-2HFW, Adimec, Netherlands), with 1440 × 1440 pixels. Information from the 512 raw interferograms was then compressed by calculating 3 metrics: the mean temporal frequency, the temporal standard deviation of the frequency, and the standard deviation of the running of the raw intensity over time. These three metrics were allocated to a Hue, Saturation, and Brightness space, respectively, to provide a quantitative way to visualize these three metrics simultaneously. Hue scaled from 3 Hz (blue hues) to 13 Hz (red hues).

Oxygraphy experiments on skin fibroblast

Mitochondrial respiration on permeabilized fibroblasts was measured with the Oroboros® Oxygraph-2k in respiration buffer (Rustin buffer: 10mM KH2PO4, 300mM mannitol, 10mM KCl, 5mM MgCl2, 0.5mM ethylene glycol tetraacetic acid (EGTA), and 1 mg/ml bovine serum albumin (BSA), pH 7.2) at 37 °C. Complex I (CI) substrates, including malate 5 mM, pyruvate 2.5 mM and glutamate were used. For combined CI and CII respiration, 10mM succinate was added. Rotenone (5 µM) was applied to inhibit complex I activity, to measure complex II-driven respiration. To confirm full cellular permeabilization, oligomycin (4 µg/ml), a F0F1-ATP synthase inhibitor, and the mitochondrial uncoupler carbonyl cyanide p-trifluoromethoxyphenylhydrazone (FCCP, 1 µM) were sequentially added. Antimycin A (2 µg/ml) was finally introduced to assess non-mitochondrial oxygen consumption. Data are presented as mean +/- SEM, with n = 5 for patient group (RD) and n = 6 for controls.

Statistics and reproducibility

Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0.2). For the proteomic section, data were filtered with a p-value cutoff of ≤ 0.05. Statistically significant differences in protein abundance were defined as a fold changes (FC) of ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5 between the two conditions. Volcano plots were generated in GraphPad Prism, and significance was determined via Student’s t-test. A Mann–Whitney U test assessed the difference across the two groups (BRI23-RD vs. BRI23-WT) using the Python library pandas, and the heatmaps were generated using R software. All experiments were conducted independently a minimum of three times, with sample sizes (n) specified for each analysis; an n ≥ 3 was used for all experiments. Statistical significance was defined as P-value ≤ 0.05. GO-term analysis was performed using the following parameters: p-values were calculated based on the cumulative hypergeometric distribution, and q-values were calculated using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure.
