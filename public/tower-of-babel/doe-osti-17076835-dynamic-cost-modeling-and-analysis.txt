 ABSTRACT
Recent U.S. policy actions call for expanding domestic nuclear capacity to approximately 400 GWe by
2050. This rapid growth would require large-scale deployment of reactors and supporting front-end and
back-end fuel cycle facilities, making it important to evaluate fuel cycle costs dynamically rather than with
static, end-state assumptions. This work extends prior Systems Analysis and Integration (SA&I) campaign
efforts by applying the Workflow for Integrated Simulation and Economics (WISE) to high-growth nuclear
deployment scenarios. WISE links reactor deployment assumptions, fuel cycle simulator outputs from
Cyclus and DYMOND, ACCERT reactor capital cost estimates, and centralized fuel cycle cost data to
calculate time-dependent operation and maintenance (O&M) costs, overnight capital costs (OCCs), facility
deployments, learning effects, and levelized metrics.
In FY26, WISE was updated to use a Global Change Analysis Model–derived nuclear demand trajectory
that reaches 400 GWe by 2050, together with a unified deployment methodology for constructing scenario-
specific reactor buildouts. The scenario set includes four once-through (OT) cases by varying the reactor
types and capacity fractions and one recycling metal case. These scenarios combine common announced or
restarted near-term projects with generic future reactor deployments, including advanced light-water
reactor–dominant, low-enriched uranium (LEU)–dominant, mixed LEU/HALEU, TRISO-intensive, and
metal fuel recycling cases. The WISE database was also reviewed and updated to include fuel cycle costs,
additional reactor classes, updated reactor technical inputs for ACCERT, and explicit fuel cycle facility
lifetimes. These lifetime assumptions allow facilities to retire and be replaced, which affects deployment
counts, utilization, OCC, and learning.
Four analyses were performed. First, baseline material flows, facility deployments, O&M costs, non-reactor
OCCs, reactor OCCs, and total undiscounted costs were calculated for the five scenarios. Reactor OCC is
the largest component of total system cost in every case, the rest of the facilities being mostly O&M-driven.
Non-reactor OCCs are smaller but dominated by enrichment, disposal, and fabrication, depending on the
scenario. Second, the effect of TRISO fabrication unit cost was evaluated because of the large uncertainty
in TRISO fuel fabrication costs and the importance of TRISO deployment in the TRISO-intensive OT cases.
Increasing the assumed TRISO fabrication unit cost significantly increases cumulative O&M in those
scenarios, especially OT-2, although reactor OCC remains the dominant total cost component. Third, a
facility capacity variation study was performed to evaluate how plant throughput, economies of scale,
utilization, deployment frequency, and learning affect non-reactor OCC. Larger facilities reduce costs for
processes with large and sustained demand, such as mining & milling, conversion, and CAT-III enrichment
in most OT scenarios, but they can increase costs when demand is limited, as observed for CAT-II
enrichment, TRISO fabrication, metal fabrication, and disposal in selected cases. Fourth, a preliminary
process-level levelized cost of electricity (LCOE) capability was applied to connect discounted process cost
streams with the electricity generation supported by each process. These LCOE results show that process-
level cost intensity ($ / MWh) depends not only on cost magnitude, but also on the timing of costs, the
attributed electricity denominator, and the assumed discount rate.
These FY26 updates improve WISE’s ability to evaluate dynamic fuel cycle economics under rapid nuclear
growth. The results show that reactor deployment strategy is the primary driver of total system operational
and capital costs, and fuel cycle assumptions strongly affect O&M cost breakdowns, non-reactor OCC, and
process-level costs in general. The analysis also identifies where future refinements could be made: TRISO
fabrication cost data, facility sizing and expansion models, disposal and storage assumptions, process-
specific learning rates, and a more complete treatment of reactor and fuel cycle LCOE.

 DYNAMIC COST MODELING AND ANALYSIS
1. INTRODUCTION
In the United States, nuclear energy is considered an important option for supporting domestic electricity
generation to supply reliable power for critical infrastructure. The 2025 executive orders (EOs) on nuclear
energy set a goal for the United States to reach approximately 400 GWe of nuclear capacity by 2050 [1],
which is roughly a fourfold (4×) increase relative to the existing U.S. nuclear fleet. The EOs focus on (i)
deploying advanced reactors for national security needs (EO 14299 [2]), (ii) streamlining Nuclear
Regulatory Commission (NRC) processes to support more timely deployment (EO 14300 [1]), (iii) fast-
tracking advanced reactor testing through a Department of Energy (DOE) Reactor Pilot program (EO 14301
[3]), and (iv) reinvigorating the domestic nuclear industrial base and fuel supply chain using tools such as
the Defense Production Act (EO 14302 [4]). These EOs motivate the evaluation of high-growth nuclear
deployment scenarios in dynamic fuel cycle cost modeling and analysis.
Rapid growth in nuclear capacity over the next several decades will increase demand for both front- and
back-end fuel cycle facilities and for the industrial base that designs, licenses, constructs, and operates these
facilities. Under these rapid expansion conditions, costs cannot be treated as static. Instead, the
instantaneous material, construction, and equipment costs for the facility (i.e., overnight capital cost or
OCC) and regular operation and maintenance (O&M) costs evolve during deployment due to changes in
commodity prices, construction costs, schedule-driven effects, and the risk of overrun. At the same time,
repeated deployment may reduce costs through learning-by-doing, supply chain maturation,
standardization, and economies of scale. These competing effects need to be evaluated to plan for research
& development, evaluate investment needs, and develop deployment strategies consistent with DOE Office
of Nuclear Energy (DOE-NE) objectives.
Recent interest in advanced reactor deployment and federal actions through the EOs motivate the need for
a dynamic, facility-by-facility cost modeling capability that represents cost evolution as new facilities are
deployed and as utilization changes over time across both front- and back-end parts of the nuclear fuel
cycle. The Workflow for Integrated Simulation and Economics (WISE) is a modular framework developed
to couple fuel cycle system modeling outputs with their corresponding costs to enable integrated evaluation
of nuclear fuel cycle deployment. WISE is designed to translate the time-dependent requirements for front-
and back-end fuel cycle facilities, calculated by the Cyclus [5] and DYMOND [6] fuel cycle simulators,
into associated cost metrics, such as OCC and O&M costs, to evaluate the total fuel cycle cost. The
development of WISE in the Systems Analysis & Integration campaign (SA&I) began in fiscal year
(FY) 24 [7] and continued in FY25 [8, 9] with each effort adding new capabilities and increasing fidelity
in the analysis. In FY24 [7], the emphasis was on establishing a baseline cost for front- and back-end fuel
cycle facilities excluding reactor costs. These costs were static OCC and O&M evaluated for representative
fuel cycle scenarios to estimate the associated facility investment needs during a transition from the legacy
light-water reactor (LWR) fleet to advanced reactor fleets. That work also compiled the best-available
public data to estimate staffing needs associated with facility operations and support functions. Construction
labor and equipment manufacturing jobs were outside the scope of that estimate. In FY25 [9], WISE
evolved into a more systematic integration platform linking fuel cycle modeling tools with cost datasets
(e.g., facility costs and reactor OCC from sources such as the Cost Basis Report (CBR) [10] and Algorithm
for the Capital Cost Estimation of Reactor Technologies (ACCERT) [11]), enabling unified cost
calculations for various scenarios and sensitivity studies that quantify how uncertainties in OCC and O&M
propagate to the total fuel cycle cost. During FY25 [8], the framework also expanded to represent dynamic
cost evaluation through learning rates, allowing capital and operating costs to change as additional facilities
are deployed. This previous work further demonstrated the dynamic cost capability by applying learning-
based cost evolution across the same scenarios to set the stage for expanding the dynamic cost evaluation
capabilities in WISE.

In FY26, WISE was applied to quantify cost drivers under a high-growth nuclear demand trajectory
consistent with a 4× increase in nuclear capacity by 2050 (approximately 400 GWe). This project evaluates
dynamic costs while (i) ramping nuclear energy capacity up to 400 GWe by 2050 and (ii) expanding the
fleet to keep up with the nuclear demand beyond 2050. The nuclear demand trajectory used in this study
was derived using the Global Change Analysis Model (GCAM), an integrated assessment model that
represents interactions among energy, water, land, climate, and economic systems [12]. Similar GCAM-
based workflows have previously been used to evaluate U.S. nuclear energy scenarios under different
targets and assumptions [13]. While the GCAM-based nuclear demand curve was fixed in this study,
changes in material flows and facility capacities were explored through variations in the portfolio of reactor
technologies deployed during the simulation to meet the nuclear demand, as well as through cost and facility
capacity evaluation assumptions.
The fuel cycles considered in this work include once-through (OT) and recycling fuel cycles, as shown in
Figure 1-1. Four different OT scenarios were modeled in this work, with these scenarios varying the mix
of reactors deployed, as well as one recycling scenario. These fuel cycles are discussed in greater detail in
the following sections.

 Figure 1-1. Representation of fuel cycle facilities needed for once-through scenarios (in red box)
 and Recycling fuel cycle scenarios (in green box).

There are four primary sets of results from this analysis. First, baseline scenario results are presented
without input data variation or learning effects to provide a consistent reference point. Second, the baseline
analysis is extended through a sensitivity study of the tristructural isotropic (TRISO) fuel fabrication cost
to reflect the large uncertainty in TRISO cost estimates in the literature. Third, the current scope examines
how alternative fuel cycle facility capacity choices affect facility utilization and associated OCC. Finally,
this report presents the resulting levelized cost of electricity (LCOE) during the dynamic deployment of
fuel cycle facilities.

2. REACTORS AND SCENARIOS
This work builds on previous SA&I efforts that applied bounding assumptions to the deployment of generic
representations of proposed reactor types in the U.S. over time. This FY, assumed scenarios were remodeled
and expanded to fit new assumptions tied to recent EOs and a broader set of reactor mixes. Nuclear energy
demand was projected using a GCAM demand analysis.
The GCAM workflow used in this study is similar to prior work [13], but the demand projection input
parameters were adjusted so that total U.S. nuclear capacity reaches 400 GWe by 2050. In this setup, nuclear
energy contributes primarily to electricity generation, with only minor contributions to other products and
sectors such as hydrogen, ammonia, cement, and steel. Before 2050, the trajectory was set to provide a
gradual increase to the 2050 target through intermediate targets. Since no additional capacity target was
imposed by the EOs beyond 2050, subsequent nuclear deployment through 2100 follows projections based
on population growth, economic growth, and competition with other technologies across sectors, as
modeled in GCAM. These assumptions result in a steep increase in nuclear demand throughout the century,
with a slight decrease between 2090 and 2100.
The energy projections and resulting scenario definitions will be evaluated in greater detail and refined in
future work. The reactor types and current scenario assumptions used in this study are described in this
section.
2.1 REACTOR TYPES
To satisfy the yearly nuclear demand, multiple scenarios with different electricity generation share
breakdowns were considered. The scenarios draw from the same set of generic reactor designs:
 • Once-through Advanced Light Water Reactor (OT-ALWR): large pressurized water reactors
 (PWRs) aligned with recent AP1000-type designs.
 • Once-through Light Water Reactor Small Modular Reactor (OT-PWR-SMR): smaller,
 modular PWRs representing integral light-water small modular reactor (SMR) concepts.
 • Once-through Boiling Water Reactor Small Modular Reactor (OT-BWR-SMR): smaller,
 modular boiling water reactors (BWRs) representing advanced BWR-based SMR concepts.
 • Once-through Fluoride-Salt-Cooled High-Temperature Reactor (OT-FHR): advanced high-
 temperature reactors using TRISO fuel in a pebble bed and fluoride salt coolant.
 • Once-through High-Temperature Gas-Cooled Reactor (OT-HTGR): helium-cooled, graphite-
 moderated reactors using TRISO fuel in a pebble bed.
 • Once-through Sodium-Cooled Fast Reactor (OT-SFR): metal-fueled sodium-cooled fast reactor
 (SFR) in a once-through configuration.
 • Recycling Sodium-Cooled Fast Reactor (Recycling-SFR): metal-fueled fast reactors operated
 using U/TRU (transuranic) metal fuel in a closed fuel cycle configuration. The reprocessed fuel,
 referred to as “hot-metal fuel,” comes from the TRU content (Np, Pu, Am, and Cm) recovered from
 discharged OT-SFR fuel, while uranium is supplied as a separate makeup material stream.

All scenario-driven new reactor deployments begin no earlier than 2030. After accounting for the legacy

of new nuclear capacity that can be added each year. This constraint corresponds to a maximum addition
of 1.6 GWe for each year new reactors are added (i.e., 1.6 GWe in 2030, 3.2 GWe in 2031, etc.). As new

3. MODELING AND UPDATES
The modeling work done for this project was primarily performed with the WISE framework. This
framework was initially developed to support previous fuel cycle cost estimation work [9]. WISE was
updated and expanded to support the current project and add additional realism to the modeled scenarios
and analysis.
3.1 WORKFLOW FOR INTEGRATED SIMULATION AND ECONOMICS
 (WISE)
The Workflow for Integrated Simulation and Economics (WISE) is a Python-based framework developed
to integrate reactor deployment assumptions, fuel cycle simulation, database inputs, and economic
evaluation. WISE provides a common workflow linking scenario definition, simulator output processing,
and cost analysis. Its overall structure is illustrated in Figure 3-1, and its main modules are summarized in
Table 3-1.
WISE combines outputs from fuel cycle simulators (Cyclus or DYMOND) with reactor and facility data
stored in a centralized database, as well as reactor capital cost estimates obtained through ACCERT.
Through its interface with the Workflow and Template Toolkit for Simulation (WATTS) [15], WISE
imports reactor deployment and material-flow information, including fuel demands, enrichment
requirements, waste streams, and reactor-specific deployment histories. These inputs are then mapped to
the corresponding front-end, fabrication, recycling, transportation, and disposal processes required for
economic evaluation.
The framework was designed to provide a consistent workflow across scenarios and studies while
remaining modular enough to support new capabilities. WISE organizes calculations around modules
representing flows, processes, facility deployment, learning, sampling, and cost evaluation. For each
process, the framework determines the facility deployment required over time while accounting for process-
specific constraints such as plant capacity, deployment strategy, and facility lifetime assumptions. It then
uses the relevant economic data to calculate annual operations and maintenance (O&M) costs, facility
overnight capital costs (OCCs), and aggregated scenario-level cost metrics.
This study focuses on deterministic cost comparisons using the scenarios considered without cost sampling.
In addition to ongoing internal code restructuring, validation, and unit testing during FY26, WISE was
further updated to support the revised database as well as the new reactor types and scenarios. As described
in the next subsections, explicit facility lifetime modeling, plant-capacity and unit-OCC variation, and
preliminary levelized cost of electricity (LCOE) computation capabilities were added to support the present
analysis.

3.2 COST UPDATES
This fiscal year, the database underwent an additional verification effort. As a result, the TRISO fuel
fabrication unit cost was increased from $5,416 / kg-U (2023 USD) to $10,000 / kg-U (2009 USD) [16]
because expert opinion was that the previous value was too low. The effect of this updated TRISO
fabrication cost is evaluated in Section 5. Aside from the addition of facility lifetimes and the update to the
TRISO fabrication unit cost, the remaining facility parameters are consistent with previous work [9].
In addition, all costs are now automatically escalated to 2025 USD using consumer price index (CPI)-based
January-to-January escalation from the original evaluation year to the latest available year [17].
3.2.1 REACTOR CAPITAL COSTS
ACCERT is used to estimate the OCC of a well-executed first-of-a-kind (FOAK) deployment for each
reactor type. The methodology is detailed in [18] and [9]. Because not every direct cost account is explicitly
evaluated in the ACCERT calculation, the calculated direct cost is adjusted using a completion factor,
assuming explicitly evaluated accounts represent 83.4% of total direct cost. Indirect costs are then
calculated as 60.9% of adjusted direct cost, and owner costs are calculated as 20% of the sum of adjusted
direct and indirect costs. The total OCC includes the adjusted direct cost, indirect cost, and owner cost, and
is escalated to 2025 USD using CPI [17].
The ACCERT OCC estimates rely on reactor-design-specific input databases, which were updated in this
work to reflect representative designs for each reactor type. The reactor capital cost database was
substantially updated for this work. The Legacy and OT-ALWR scenarios continue to use the PWR12-BE

Every other reactor’s set of parameters, and thus resulting costs, have been updated. These reactors are all

left blank so that ACCERT would use default scaling behavior from its internal routines. For the smallest
reactor types, a multi-unit plant treatment was introduced to avoid evaluating an unrealistically small single-
module plant in ACCERT. This treatment multiplies the electric and thermal power passed to ACCERT by
an integer that allows for an electric generation greater than or equal to 200 MWe, while keeping the reactor-
specific technical parameters unchanged. This approach keeps the power level passed to ACCERT closer
to the ABR1000 reference basis. The multiplier was thus set to 3 for OT-PWR-SMR, 3 for OT-HTGR, 2
for OT-FHR, and 1 for the remaining reactor types. Once costs are calculated, they are scaled back on a
single-unit basis.

deployment-driven OCC. Similar effects are observed in the other scenarios considered in this study.

4.2 FACILITY DEPLOYMENTS
The mass flow demands described in Section 4.1 are converted into facility deployment requirements
through the WISE Facility module. For each process, the annual demand is compared against the capacity
available from the active fleet together with any assumed initial capacity. Here, for stream-based facilities
(e.g., mines or enrichment plants), the initial capacity is set to satisfy the 2025 demand. The model then
computes a yearly deployment profile and, when a finite lifetime is specified, a retirement profile. The
active fleet size shown in this section thus corresponds to the difference between the cumulative number of
facilities deployed and the cumulative number retired, and it represents the number of facilities operating
each year to satisfy demand. By contrast, cumulative deployment is the quantity that drives OCCs and,
when enabled, learning effects.
For stream-based facilities, an optimized deployment scheme is used, and facilities are added with a
smoothing and backfilling logic that mitigates sharp changes and limits overbuilding. Excess capacity
each year appears as surplus production or inventory, which can then be used to satisfy the demand later
and reduce near-term deployment requirements. As stated earlier, disposal is handled differently because
it is modeled as a storage facility. In that case, new disposal sites are deployed when cumulative
repository loading exceeds the storage capacity already available. Since disposal sites do not retire, the
disposal site counts are equivalent to the cumulative site deployment over time summarizes the
cumulative number of deployed facilities by process, and what follows provides more details on process-
by-process facilities.

5. IMPACT OF TRISO FABRICATION UNIT COST
Section 3.2 notes that the TRISO fabrication unit cost used in previous work was increased in the present
study, from $5,416 / kg-U (2023 USD) to $10,000 / kg-U (2009 USD). Because both the revision and the
remaining uncertainty in this cost are large, and because TRISO fabrication is one of the main cost drivers
of the TRISO-based scenarios, it is useful to analyze how strongly the total cost results are impacted by this
assumption. Therefore, three TRISO fabrication cost cases were evaluated: the value used in a previous
study [8], the updated reference value adopted in this work, and a high case equal to twice the updated
value. After CPI escalation, these correspond to $5,722.5 / kg-U (2025 USD), $15,006.4 / kg-U (2025
USD), and $30,012.8 / kg-U (2025 USD).
The high case is also broadly consistent with the upper end of a unit-cost range derived from X-Energy’s
own fuel economics data reported in its draft registration statement in 2025 [41]. Specifically, it reports
lifetime fuel customer fees of $1.65–$3.31B (2025 USD) for the fuel business line of one multi-unit plant
(TX-1), based on fabrication fees charged to customers. X-Energy also states that TX-1 has an estimated
throughput of 5 MTU / year (approximately 700,000 TRISO-X pebbles / year), enough fuel for up to 11
Xe-100 reactors. This results in approximately 454.5 kg-U / reactor-year. Assuming a plant comprises four
Xe-100 units and operates for an 80-year lifetime, this leads to a lifetime requirement of approximately
109,000 kg-U. The lower and upper bounds from these statements and assumptions result in a unit cost of
$15,125–$30,342 / kg-U (2025 USD). Although these values are estimates and reflect customer fuel
expenditures, including margins, rather than fabrication-only production costs, the upper value provides a
conservative bound for the TRISO fabrication unit cost.
Since the OT-1, OT-3, and Recycling scenarios include only limited residual TRISO use associated with
the common near-term HTGR projects, the effect would be negligible. The sensitivity analysis is therefore
focused on OT-2 and OT-4, the two scenarios in which TRISO deployment is substantial enough for the
fabrication cost assumption to affect cumulative O&M. The results from perturbing the TRISO fabrication
unit cost for these scenarios are shown in Table 5-1.

6.2.1 FACILITY UTILIZATION
An example of process-specific facility utilization is shown in Figure 6-2 for the OT-1 conversion fleet to
illustrate how the availability-weighted utilization metric should be interpreted. As assumed for all
processes, conversion demand is initially equal to the available capacity; then, as additional conversion

48 May 2026

capacity is deployed before demand growth, annual capacity exceeds annual demand and inventory
accumulates. This effect is visible after 2030, when a new facility is deployed and the inventory begins to
increase. During this period, annual facility utilization (ratio of demand over capacity, capped at 100%)
decreases because deployed capacity is larger than the immediate annual demand. However, the excess
production is not lost but stored and becomes available to satisfy future demand. As a result, the availability-
weighted utilization falls to 70% in the early 2030s, which shows this temporary overcapacity and inventory
buildup. But later, as demand continues to increase, the accumulated inventory is gradually used in years
when demand is higher than the annual conversion capacity, particularly during the 2060s. In that decade,
annual facility utilization reaches 100%, which shows that all newly produced UF6 is used immediately
(any additional demand being supplied from the inventory). Because availability-weighted utilization is
cumulative, it accounts for both the earlier inventory buildup and the later use of that inventory. The metric
therefore increases over time and reaches approximately 97% by 2100, which indicates that deployed
capacity and stored material match well to demand over the full simulation period.
This behavior is dependent on the deployment algorithm used. In this study, the algorithm anticipates future
demand growth by deploying capacity before it is strictly needed, which allows the inventory to reduce the
need for additional deployment later. The availability-weighted utilization (AU) metric, described in
Section 3.3, encapsulates this information in a global way and thus provides a compact metric to summarize
the overall utilization of deployed capacity and accumulated inventory under this deployment strategy.

from utilization analysis. Additional annual utilization results by process, scenario, and capacity case are

𝐴𝐴𝐴𝐴 decreases when capacity is overbuilt relative to demand (increasing the denominator through deployed
capacity and/or inventory) and increases when deployed facilities more closely track the demand
trajectory over time. The 𝐴𝐴𝐴𝐴 of each process thus provides insights into the overall sizing of their
corresponding facilities:

Mining & milling (Figure 6-3 (a))
𝐴𝐴𝐴𝐴 generally increases with facility capacity and then shows a plateau at the largest capacities, with the
Recycling case showing a slight decline at the highest capacity. Mining & milling demand remains high
across all scenarios, and mine lifetimes are short relative to other facilities (15 years compared with 50-80
years). Because mining capacity is best interpreted here as a fleet-average mine & mill capacity rather than
a single standardized plant size, short-lived, higher-capacity mines can be deployed and retired more closely
in step with nuclear demand, which reduces sustained overbuilding and limits long-lived inventory. This
improves the availability-weighted utilization over the full simulation period.

Conversion and DU deconversion (Figure 6-3 (b) and (c))
Conversely, conversion and DU deconversion exhibit higher 𝐴𝐴𝐴𝐴 at smaller and baseline capacities. These
facilities are long-lived (~80 years) and therefore tend to remain deployed with few retirements once

Recycling scenario shows lower conversion 𝐴𝐴𝐴𝐴 because once recycling reactors are deployed, the need to
convert U3O8 into UF6 stagnates. This effect is more pronounced for the largest capacities, where 𝐴𝐴𝐴𝐴 drops
(e.g., to ~55–64% for conversion in OT scenarios and 44% in Recycling), which indicates sustained
overcapacity.

CAT-III enrichment shows high 𝐴𝐴𝐴𝐴 across capacities in most scenarios, although 𝐴𝐴𝐴𝐴 declines at the largest
capacities. With an intermediate lifetime (~50 years), early over-deployment associated with smaller units
is partially corrected as facilities retire and are replaced to match demand in the latter part of the simulation
timeline. Similarly, any over-deployment associated with larger units is moderated by retirement and
redeployment over the simulation timeline, yielding comparable cumulative utilization. The Recycling
scenario 𝐴𝐴𝐴𝐴 behavior diverges from the OT scenarios, highlighting the dependence of this metric on the
scenario in addition to the facility deployment scheme. As demonstrated in Section 4.1, CAT-III enrichment
demand stagnates as OT-SFR deployment stops. Over the range of plant capacities studied, the fourth
capacity case has lower 𝐴𝐴𝐴𝐴 than the fifth capacity case in the Recycling scenario, indicating that the timing
of over-deployment and retirement can matter as much as the nominal facility size.

6.2.2.2 LEARNING EFFECTS AT FIXED CAPACITY
Learning-by-doing reduces the OCC assigned to later facilities as more facilities of the same process are
deployed. The model follows a Wright’s-law form, in which each doubling of cumulative deployment
capacity reduces facility OCC by a prescribed learning rate [43]. In this study, the cumulative OCC figures
include learning rates of 0% and 10%. The summary learning figures focus on the 10% case to show the
maximum learning sensitivity evaluated here.
The learning process is represented by the following equation:

6.3 KEY TAKEAWAYS
This capacity variation study shows that facility sizing affects non-reactor OCC through the interaction of
facility utilization, economies of scale, and learning. Because all scaling exponents are assumed lower than
one, larger facilities have lower OCC per unit capacity. However, this does not mean that the largest facility
is always preferred. A larger facility reduces cumulative OCC only when the demand is high enough, or
sustained long enough, to justify the added capacity.
The clearest benefit from larger facilities occurs for processes with steady or repeated demand. Mining &
milling, conversion, DU deconversion, CAT-III enrichment and UOX fabrication in the OT scenarios
generally favor the largest evaluated capacities. In these cases, the reduction in the number of deployed
facilities is large enough to overcome the higher OCC per facility.
The opposite behavior appears when demand is limited or concentrated early in the simulation. CAT-II
enrichment, metal fabrication, and TRISO fabrication in OT-1 and OT-3 and disposal in Recycling all show
cases where larger facilities are not preferred. For these processes, increasing facility capacity can lead to
overcapacity or oversupply, and the economy-of-scale benefit is not enough to compensate.
Learning-by-doing mainly affects processes with large fleet deployments, resulting from large demand,
shorter lifetimes, small facility capacity, or a combination. TRISO fabrication in OT-2 and OT-4, disposal
in the high-disposal scenarios, mining & milling, DU deconversion, and CAT-III enrichment show the
strongest learning effects. Processes with only one or a few deployed facilities show little learning, even
when the assumed learning rate is 10%. This is why CAT-II enrichment, metal fabrication, and TRISO
fabrication only show strong learning in the scenarios and capacity cases in which deployment is repeated
often enough.

The largest non-reactor OCC savings come from a limited number of processes. CAT-III enrichment is the
main optimization driver in OT-1 and OT-3, while disposal dominates OT-2 and OT-4. Although mining
& milling provides smaller but consistent savings across all scenarios, other processes can show large
percentage changes at the process level, but their effect on total non-reactor OCC is smaller when their
absolute OCC contribution is limited.
Finally, these results should be interpreted at the non-reactor OCC level. Capacity optimization can
noticeably reduce non-reactor OCC, especially for CAT-III enrichment and disposal, but non-reactor OCC
remains a small fraction of total scenario OCC. As a result, most process-specific capacity choices change
total OCC by only a few tenths of a percent or less. The preferred capacity is therefore process- and
scenario-specific, and it depends on factors such as demand, facility lifetime, utilization, deployment
timing, and the number of facilities leading to learning.
6.4 CURRENT LIMITATIONS
The results in this section should be interpreted as a preliminary sensitivity study rather than true
optimization. Indeed, even though the study identifies which processes are most sensitive to facility
capacity, utilization, and learning assumptions, several modeling simplifications affect numerical results.
The first limitation is the availability of scaling data. The scaling coefficients in Table 6-1 are available
only for selected processes. Coefficients for mining & milling, DU deconversion, CAT-II enrichment, metal
fabrication, TRISO fabrication, and disposal were mapped from related processes. This adds uncertainty
for processes where process-specific cost scaling data were not available.
Second, the evaluated systematic capacity range can also be wider than the possible range for some
processes. Table 6-2 always spans one order of magnitude below and one above the baseline capacity. This
range should not be interpreted as a validated design range for any process. A full literature review of
historical data should be conducted to obtain those ranges for processes with commercial history.
In addition, each process is represented using one uniform facility capacity at a time. In practice, a deployed
fleet could include a mix of facility sizes, including expansions or uprates of existing facilities. A mixed
fleet could efficiently reduce overcapacity and improve utilization and thus change cumulative OCC
compared with the single-capacity cases evaluated here. Some processes, such as enrichment, may add
capacity in stages rather than through fully independent new facilities. Expansion could have a different
cost than deployment from scratch and could improve utilization by better following the demand. Similarly,
facility lifetime is fixed for each process. This assumption affects retirements and replacement and, in turn,
utilization and learning. The assumption is especially important for facilities with long lifetimes such as
conversion, fuel fabrication, and disposal, in which a large-capacity plant can operate for many decades.
Moreover, facility lifetimes might also change with size.
The deployment model also assumes that facilities can operate at full capacity when material is needed and
that excess production can be stored indefinitely. This assumption is useful for tracking first-order
estimates, but it does not include storage costs, operational constraints and expiration, or possible limits on
acceptable inventory buildup. Cases with large inventory may therefore look more favorable than they
would if storage penalties were included. Furthermore, the deployment largely depends on the deployment
strategy chosen during the analysis. A full comparison between candidate deployment strategies should be
conducted.
Learning rates were also applied as prescribed sensitivities rather than process-specific empirical values. A
uniform 10% learning case is useful for providing lower bounds here, but actual learning could vary by
process, technology maturity, supply chain, past experience, etc.

7. LEVELIZED COST OF ELECTRICITY (LCOE)
As noted in [8], the cumulative O&M and OCC results reported through 2100 in Section 4 are undiscounted
aggregates and therefore are not directly comparable to a levelized metric. That same work also identified
LCOE as a natural extension of WISE, as it already computes process-level cost streams and tracks the
electricity generation associated with reactor deployments. Adding an LCOE capability therefore builds
directly on information already produced within the framework. The present study addresses that gap by
adding a process-level LCOE capability for non-reactor fuel cycle facilities.
In this work, LCOE is used as defined in Equation 3.4 as an electricity-normalized metric that combines
time-dependent OCC and O&M cost streams into a single discounted contribution. Unlike the cumulative
cost results in Section 4, which show where costs occur in the fuel cycle, LCOE also accounts for when
those costs occur relative to the electricity generation they support. It is therefore useful for comparing the
same process across scenarios, comparing different fuel cycle operations, and assessing how those
comparisons depend on the assumed discount rate.
7.1 MODELING ASSUMPTIONS
For non-reactor fuel cycle facilities, the denominator is not electricity generated by the facility itself, since
these facilities do not produce power. Instead, electricity generation must be assigned according to the
portion of the fuel cycle that each process supports. Extending the same framework to reactor LCOE and
then combining reactor and non-reactor contributions using a consistent denominator-attribution method
would enable a complete scenario-level levelized comparison.
Mining & milling, conversion, DU deconversion, UNF transportation, and disposal support the fuel cycle
broadly and are therefore assigned the total scenario electricity generation. By contrast, each fabrication
process is assigned only the electricity generated by reactors using the corresponding fuel form. HLW
transportation is assigned the electricity generated by Recycling-SFRs, since that waste stream exists only
in recycling cases.
For enrichment, the denominator assignment follows the enrichment category required by the fuel.
Electricity generation from reactors using UOX fuel is assigned only to CAT-III enrichment. Reactors
requiring enrichments above 10% are assigned to both CAT-III and CAT-II enrichment, since HALEU
production depends on both stages. In the present scenarios, this applies to TRISO- and metal-fueled
reactors.
The present LCOE results are also subject to several additional assumptions:
 • Construction is assumed to be instantaneous, so OCC is assigned to the year in which each facility
 begins operation.
 • The analysis window ends in 2100, and the reported value is the cumulative discounted process-
 level LCOE evaluated over the analysis interval through this entire period, rather than a point
 estimate in 2100.
 • No residual or salvage value is applied to facilities, inventories, or unused service life remaining at
 the end of the modeled period. Although this assumption biases the metric and results in slightly
 overestimated values, costs and electricity well beyond 2100 have progressively smaller present
 value and this overestimation is expected to be minor.
 • Facility lifetimes affect the modeled deployment, retirement, active-fleet, and O&M cost streams.
 No separate financial recovery period is imposed beyond the cost timing represented in those
 annual streams.

May 2026 67

 • A single discount rate is applied uniformly across all processes and is assumed constant in time.
 Three values are examined here: 3%, 7%, and 10%, spanning cases from lower-cost public
 financing to more finance-sensitive private investment contexts. Because costs are expressed in
 constant 2025 USD, these discount rates are real rates and exclude inflation. The 7% value is used
 as an approximate real weighted average cost of capital (WACC), which is typically used as a
 benchmark discount rate for capital-investment decisions.
7.2 LCOE INTERPRETATION
As defined above, the process-level LCOE for a given process is the ratio of the discounted cost stream
attributed to that process to the discounted electricity generation assigned to it. Process-level LCOE
therefore increases when costs increase and when attributed electricity generation decreases. But it also
increases when costs occur earlier than the supported generation, or when the process serves only a narrow
branch of the fuel cycle.
The discount rate matters because it changes the present-value balance between the numerator, which
contains the process cost stream, and the denominator, which contains the attributed electricity generation.
If the time profiles of cost and electricity are similar, discounting affects both in broadly the same way and
the resulting LCOE changes only slightly. This behavior can especially be observed when costs are almost
entirely tied to variable O&M, because the cost stream scales directly with generation and therefore moves
with the electricity denominator.
To illustrate these behaviors, the simple example in Figure 7-1 uses a single idealized facility with a $100
capital cost before operation, $3 / year in O&M, and constant output over a 60-year operating lifetime. All
contributions are divided by the total LCOE calculated at the 3% discount rate. The O&M contribution
remains constant as the discount rate increases because O&M and output occur in the same years, so the
O&M numerator and electricity denominator are discounted in the same way. In contrast, the capital
contribution increases because the upfront capital cost is divided by a smaller discounted output
denominator at higher discount rates. As a result, the normalized total LCOE increases from 1.00 at 3% to
1.53 at 7% and 1.97 at 10%, while the capital share of total LCOE increases from 55% to 70% and 77%,
respectively.

When costs are weighted earlier than the supported generation, increasing the discount rate tends to reduce
the denominator more strongly than the numerator, causing LCOE to increase. This sensitivity becomes
more pronounced when a larger share of the cost is assigned as OCC and that OCC is incurred before the
supported generation, because those costs are less directly synchronized with annual generation. In contrast,
if the cost stream is weighted later than the electricity generation it supports, increasing the discount rate
can reduce the numerator more strongly than the denominator and the LCOE can decrease. This timing
effect is especially relevant for processes whose cost streams are not aligned with their corresponding
electricity denominators.
To isolate the impact of timing from the capital/O&M split, Figure 7-2 uses another idealized example in
which all cases have the same total cost, the same total electricity generation, and therefore the same
undiscounted LCOE ($3.00 / unit). The only difference between the three cases is how the cost stream is
timed: (i) when costs are weighted earlier than electricity generation, LCOE increases with discount rate,
(ii) when costs and electricity are aligned, LCOE stays equal to $3.00 / unit because the numerator and
denominator are discounted in the same way, and (iii) when costs are weighted later than electricity
generation, the discounted LCOE decreases with discount rate.

 Figure 7-2. Idealized LCOE example showing the effect of cost timing relative to electricity
  generation. Each case has the same total undiscounted cost and total electricity generation, so the
  undiscounted LCOE is $3.00 / unit output in all cases. Differences in discounted LCOE arise only
from whether the cost stream occurs earlier than, is aligned with, or occurs later than the electricity
 generation.

For a given process, scenario-to-scenario differences are therefore driven by three related factors: the
magnitude of the cost stream, the size of the attributed electricity generation, and the timing of both over
the simulation. In practice, a process can show a very large LCOE even when its absolute cost is small
simply because the electricity attributed to that branch is very small. This is especially important when a
branch supports only residual deployment. In those cases, the process-level LCOE becomes highly sensitive
to cost/electricity balance, and the resulting values should be interpreted with caution.

7.4 CURRENT LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK
The current implementation should be interpreted as a preliminary LCOE capability rather than a final
metric. For non-power-producing facilities, the electricity attributed to a process is necessarily an
accounting assumption and not a physical output of the facility itself. However, for alternatives with similar
annual electricity generation, the process-level LCOE is still useful for comparing relative cost differences
and identifying the sources of those differences. It does not provide a meaningful estimate of the cost of
electricity for any individual power plant or fleet average at any point in time. Instead, it represents an

May 2026 71

integrated average over the modeled nuclear energy system, or over selected subcomponents of that system,
across the simulation period.
A second limitation is the finite 2100 cutoff. Indeed, facilities that remain active beyond 2100 continue to
operate, but their post-2100 electricity generation, expenses or any potential residual value are not currently
accounted for. This modeling decision tends to bias the metric upward as capital expenditure prior to 2100
is not fully amortized for facilities continuing to operate well after 2100. A related issue is that facility
lifetimes affect the modeled deployment, retirement, active fleet, and O&M cost streams, but no separate
financial recovery period is imposed beyond the cost timing represented in those annual streams.
A third limitation is the use of a single uniform discount rate across all processes and all years. Real-world
discount rates can vary by technology, by ownership structure, by policy support, and overtime. Publicly
supported or government-backed facilities may justify lower effective rates, whereas privately financed or
higher-risk investments may face significantly higher rates. Allowing process-specific and time-dependent
discounting would improve realism.
The current discussion is limited to non-reactor facilities. Extending the same framework to reactor LCOE
and then combining reactor and non-reactor contributions consistently would enable a more complete
scenario-level levelized comparison. Future work could also include residual value treatment, alternative
cost-recovery horizons, process-specific discount rates, clearer reporting of the electricity-denominator
assumptions used in the process-level LCOE calculation, and product-based levelized-cost metrics for non-
reactor fuel cycle facilities. These developments would extend the direction already identified in last year’s
report for moving from undiscounted aggregate cost comparisons toward a more rigorous LCOE-based
assessment.
The current effort also laid the groundwork for evaluating the levelized cost of the product, such as
fabricated fuel or enrichment services, from each non-reactor fuel cycle facility in the system. This will be
very valuable for identifying facilities that are being underutilized, considering deployment of smaller-scale
facilities to more efficiently meet demand, and evaluating tradeoffs between economy of scale and better
matching supply and demand by using smaller facilities. The levelized cost of a fully utilized facility can
be calculated from the input cost data, providing both a useful check on cost inputs and a measure of how
efficiently each facility is being used from a cost perspective. There is an economic penalty associated with
whether a facility is underutilized due to demand falling below its full capacity or whether the product is
produced but stored for future use. This penalty can be quantified as the difference between the levelized
cost at the time of product delivery and the levelized cost of a fully utilized facility, helping identify the
sources of these penalties and opportunities to reduce costs.
Once the cost of each facility is tracked separately, more complex economic analyses become possible,
including time-dependent fleet-average levelized costs and allocation of demand to facilities based on
marginal cost. There are many possibilities, but the management of a very large amount of data is required.
This was an important step toward many types of future cost, economic, and system design studies.

8. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK
This work applied the WISE framework to evaluate dynamic fuel cycle costs for scenarios that reach
approximately 400 GWe of nuclear capacity by 2050 and continue evolving through 2100. The analysis
used updated reactor deployment assumptions, revised reactor OCC estimates, explicit fuel cycle facility
lifetimes, updated fuel cycle cost inputs, deterministic capacity variation, learning-by-doing, and a
preliminary process-level LCOE capability. Together, these updates represent a step toward using WISE as
a dynamic framework that can represent how fuel cycle facility costs evolve as demand, deployments,
utilization, and learning change over time.
Baseline Scenario Results
The new FY26 400 GWe baseline results show that reactor deployment strategy is the main driver of total
system cost. Reactor OCC is the largest cost component in every scenario and accounts for roughly half or
more of total cumulative cost through 2100. Total cumulative cost is lowest in OT-1, at about $7.75 T (2025
USD), and highest in OT-2, at about $11.48 T (2025 USD). OT-2 and OT-4 are more expensive than the
other OT cases because their reactor mixes require more small and advanced reactor deployment, larger
TRISO-related fuel cycle operations, and larger disposal requirements. Recycling has the lowest cumulative
non-reactor OCC and the lowest O&M cost among the scenarios, but its total cost remains higher than OT-
1 and OT-3 because of the large reactor OCC associated with fast reactor deployment.
The mass-flow and facility-deployment results explain these cost differences. UOX-dominated scenarios
such as OT-1 and OT-3 are mainly driven by mining & milling, CAT-III enrichment, UOX fabrication, and
UOX disposal. OT-2 and OT-4 introduce larger TRISO demand, which increases TRISO fabrication,
transportation, and disposal requirements. This is especially important because TRISO fuel has a large
disposal-equivalent volume per unit uranium mass. As a result, OT-2 and OT-4 require larger repository
capacity than OT-1 and OT-3. Recycling reduces the long-term front-end and disposal load by shifting
toward recycled metal fuel, but it also introduces hot-metal fabrication, high-level waste transportation, and
larger SFR-related reactor capital costs.
Because the scenarios reach similar total electricity generation but require different amounts of NU, they
also place different demands on domestic uranium resources. This is important because U.S. uranium
resources may be limited relative to the cumulative NU requirements of the high-growth deployment cases.
Under the 400 GWe deployment pathway, domestic NU resources could become limiting during the
century, which could affect uranium commodity prices and, in turn, total fuel cycle costs.
TRISO Fabrication Cost Sensitivity Study
The TRISO fabrication sensitivity confirms that TRISO unit cost is an important uncertainty for TRISO-
intensive scenarios. In OT-2, increasing the TRISO fabrication cost from the low case to the updated
reference value raises cumulative O&M by about $342 B (2025 USD), and the high case increases total
cumulative O&M to about $5.23 T (2025 USD). The TRISO fabrication share of cumulative O&M in OT-
2 increases from 4.9% in the low case to 11.8% at the updated reference value and 21.1% in the high case.
OT-4 follows the same trend at a smaller magnitude because it requires less TRISO fuel. These results show
that TRISO fabrication cost can significantly change O&M comparisons among TRISO-based scenarios,
even though total system cost remains dominated by reactor OCC.
Assessment of Economies of Scale and Learning through Capacity Variation Study
The capacity variation study shows that the economies of scale of fuel cycle facilities are dependent on the
capacity demand and fuel cycle scenarios. Larger facilities have lower OCC per unit capacity because the
assumed power-law scaling exponents are less than one, but larger capacity only reduces cumulative OCC
when the demand is high enough and sustained enough to use that capacity. Mining & milling, conversion,
DU deconversion, CAT-III enrichment in most OT scenarios, and UOX fabrication in the OT scenarios

May 2026 73

generally benefit from larger evaluated capacities. In contrast, CAT-II enrichment in OT-1 and OT-3, metal
fabrication in OT-1 and OT-3, TRISO fabrication in low-demand scenarios, and disposal in Recycling all
show cases where large facilities are not preferred because they create overcapacity relative to the demand
they serve.
Learning-by-doing affects the capacity results when a fuel cycle process has enough repeated deployment
for learning to accumulate. Mining & milling shows the most consistent learning benefit because mines
have short lifetimes and are deployed repeatedly. DU deconversion, CAT-III enrichment, and disposal for
scenarios with large amounts of waste also show important learning effects. By contrast, CAT-II
enrichment, metal fabrication, and TRISO fabrication show little or no learning in scenarios where only
one or a few facilities are built. In most process-scenario combinations, learning changes the magnitude of
the cost but not the preferred capacity case. The selected capacities change only when the cost tradeoff is
more balanced, including conversion in Recycling, DU deconversion in OT-2 and OT-3, CAT-II
enrichment in OT-4, and disposal in OT-1, OT-3, and OT-4.
Capacity optimization of non-reactor facilities has the largest impact on the minimization of cumulative
OCC when the optimized facility is both expensive and deployed at scale. Among the non-reactor facilities,
CAT-III enrichment is the dominant optimization lever in OT-1 and OT-3, while disposal dominates non-
reactor OCC effects in OT-2 and OT-4. Mining & milling gives smaller but consistent non-reactor OCC
savings across all scenarios. Other processes can have large process-level changes, but their effect on total
non-reactor OCC is limited when their absolute OCC contribution is small. Since non-reactor OCC is itself
a small share of total system OCC, most process-specific capacity choices have only small effects on total
scenario cost. The capacity study is therefore most useful for identifying fuel cycle investment and
infrastructure sensitivities, rather than for changing the overall scenario cost ranking.
LCOE Analysis
The LCOE analysis adds a complementary capability by discounting cost streams and normalizing them by
attributed electricity generation. The process-level LCOE values are not additive system-wide LCOE
contributions because non-reactor facilities do not directly generate electricity and each process uses its
own assigned electricity denominator. However, the metric is useful for comparing the same process across
scenarios and for understanding the effects of timing and discount rate. Processes with large electricity
denominators and cost streams distributed over the full simulation, such as mining & milling, conversion,
DU deconversion, and UOX fabrication, show limited sensitivity with respect to discount rate. Processes
that support smaller branches of the fuel cycle, or whose costs occur earlier than the electricity they support,
show stronger sensitivity. This is especially visible for TRISO fabrication in OT-2 and OT-4 and hot-metal
fabrication in Recycling.
The LCOE results also show why process-level values must be interpreted carefully. CAT-II enrichment
and metal fabrication can have high process-level LCOE values in OT-1 and OT-3, not because they
dominate total system cost, but because their attributed electricity denominator is very small. Conversely,
UNF transportation in OT-2 and OT-4 can decrease with higher discount rates when a large part of the cost
stream occurs later than the electricity generation in the denominator, while disposal no longer shows this
general decreasing behavior in the updated results. These results confirm that LCOE is useful for better cost
interpretation, but it requires an understanding of the assumptions and should serve as a complement to the
aggregate O&M and OCC results rather than replace them.
Limitations
Several limitations are associated with the above results. The deployment scenarios are generic and are
based on assumed reactor shares rather than truly optimized or market-derived deployment pathways.
Reactor and facility cost estimates remain first-order values based on publicly available information, scaling
relationships, and representative assumptions. Facility capacity is evaluated using a discrete set of five
capacity cases per process, not continuous optimization. Facility lifetimes are representative values and do

74 May 2026

not vary with size. Facility inventories are allowed to accumulate without explicit storage penalties, and
facilities are assumed to operate at full available capacity when deployed. Learning rates are arbitrary
boundary values rather than process-specific empirical estimates. Finally, the LCOE implementation is
preliminary and currently focuses on non-reactor process-level metrics rather than a fully integrated LCOE.
Disposal costs are also represented at a simplified level. In the Recycling scenario, discharged metal fuel is
not treated as a directly disposed spent fuel waste stream. The model can still count the transportation
associated with that discharged metal fuel, but the disposal cost model excludes direct metal-fuel disposal
because the material is assumed to enter recycling. Disposal from recycling is instead represented through
an aggregate HLW disposal stream from reprocessing. The model does not track the eventual disposal
pathway of recycled material by recycle pass, nor does it distinguish HLW generated after first recycle from
HLW generated after later recycle stages. Therefore, the Recycling disposal results should be interpreted
as first-order aggregate HLW disposal estimates. For TRISO fuel, the current UO2-equivalent disposal
treatment captures the large disposal volume associated with TRISO fuel but does not represent existing
repository concepts that could take advantage of its lower heat generation rate per unit volume.
Future Work
Future work should refine the scenario definitions and reactor deployment assumptions, including the
GCAM-derived demand trajectory and the representation of announced, resumed, and future reactor
projects. Reactor OCC estimates should be updated as more design information becomes available,
especially for multi-unit SMRs. Fuel cycle facility cost data should be improved for processes with high
uncertainty or strong scenario impact, especially TRISO fabrication, metal and hot metal fabrication,
enrichment, and disposal. Different deployment strategies for these processes should be explored more
carefully to observe the impact on LCOE and capacity sensitivity. Capacity studies could be extended to
mixed facility fleets (lifetimes, costs, capacities), planned expansions, and continuous or finer-grid capacity
optimization by integrating DAKOTA with WISE. A related sensitivity study could also investigate the
impacts of varying reactor OCC, because reactor OCC is a dominant component of overall system costs.
The same type of cost-variation analysis could be applied to major fuel cycle cost inputs, including NU
supply costs and disposal cost assumptions. For disposal, this would include comparing the reference cost
basis used here with other repository cost studies and reconciling those assumptions with other SA&I
campaign estimates. These comparisons could also evaluate different disposal cost structures, such as mined
repositories and borehole disposal, because repositories can require large upfront construction costs while
borehole disposal costs may scale more directly with gradual additions of capacity. Storage costs, inventory
limits, and operational constraints should also be added so that inventory-heavy cases can be evaluated
more realistically.
Material availability should be evaluated more explicitly in future analyses. In particular, cumulative NU
demand should be compared against U.S. uranium resource estimates and domestic production capacity to
determine whether front-end supply could become a limiting constraint for high-growth deployment
scenarios.
Disposal modeling should also be refined for TRISO and recycling fuel cycles. For Recycling, future work
should identify the terminal material streams that cannot be recycled further, the isotopic or thermal
constraints that determine this end state, and the SNF or HLW requiring disposal from second and higher
recycle stages. For TRISO, disposal assumptions should account for repository concepts based on heat
loading, waste package configuration, and packing density rather than relying only on UO₂-equivalent
disposal volume. These refinements would improve comparisons of disposal costs across UOX, TRISO,
and recycling scenarios.
Further development of the LCOE capability is also important. Future work should add reactor LCOE,
residual value treatment, alternative cost recovery periods, process-specific discount rates, and improved
attribution of electricity generation to each process. A related extension would be to calculate levelized

May 2026 75

product costs for fuel cycle services such as enrichment, fabricated fuel, disposal capacity, or recycled
material. This development would make it possible to compare the levelized costs against the cost of a fully
utilized facility and identify the economic penalty associated with underutilization, early deployment, or
inventory production.
In conclusion, the FY26 work demonstrates that WISE can evaluate dynamic fuel cycle costs in an
increasingly integrated way by linking reactor deployment, material flows, facility deployment, cost
evolution, learning, capacity utilization, and levelized metrics. In general, the results reinforce a conclusion
from FY25: reactor deployment choices dominate total system cost, but fuel cycle facility assumptions
strongly affect where supporting infrastructure is needed, how much non-reactor capital is required, how
sensitive scenarios are with respect to uncertain unit costs, and how efficiently the fuel cycle system uses
deployed capacity.
