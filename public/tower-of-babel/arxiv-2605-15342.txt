Minerva-Ego: Spatiotemporal Hints for Egocentric Video Understanding
Arsha Nagrani, Jasper UijlingsShyamal BuchTobias WeyandSudheendra VijayanarasimhanBo HuRamin MehranDavid A RossCordelia SchmidGoogle DeepMind

Abstract
Video reasoning models are a core component of egocentric and embodied agents. However, standard benchmarks for assessing models provide only evaluation of the output ( e.g . the answer to a question), without evaluation of intermediate reasoning steps, and most provide answers only in the text domain. We introduce Minerva-Ego, a benchmark for evaluating complex egocentric visual reasoning. We extend recent high-quality video data sources recorded from egocentric / embodied settings with a set of challenging, multi-step multimodal questions and spatiotemporally-dense human-annotated reasoning traces. Benchmarking experiments show that state-of-the-art models still have a large gap to human performance. To investigate this gap in detail, we annotate each reasoning trace in the dataset with the objects of interest required to solve the question, as spatio-temporal mask annotations. Through extensive evaluations, we identify that prompting frontier models with hints of ‘where’ and ‘when’ to look yields substantial improvements in performance. Minerva-Ego can be downloaded at https://github.com/google-deepmind/neptune

However, despite this progress, a critical gap remains in evaluating complex reasoning for long-form, egocentric video. Existing egocentric benchmarks often focus solely on the final answer and overlook the underlying reasoning process

35

Attempts to improve the reasoning process of models have led to a paradigm shift where models spend more time
thinking

2
i.e. producing intermediate thoughts towards a final answer, often via additional compute at inference time

;

The idea has been introduced in the video domain as well, where benchmarks such as MINERVA
(
29

assess the types of errors made specifically for video reasoning. These works, however are primarily exocentric. Unlike exocentric videos, which often feature detached observations, egocentric data centers on close-up, frequent interactions with objects, producing a continuous flow of activities that demand a model’s ability to identify and focus on key objects of interest over time.

To address these limitations, we introduce a new benchmark for long-form egocentric video understanding, called Minerva-Ego.
Our benchmark, built on top of HD-EPIC
34
, features 1,160 complex, hand-crafted questions that require reasoning over long temporal contexts. Crucially, each question includes five answer choices annotated with dense spatio-temporal reasoning traces, which link timestamps and segmentation masks to relevant objects of interest (see Fig.
1
for examples).

We find that state-of-the-art video understanding models (Gemini
42
, GPT
32
and Claude
4
) perform poorly on our dataset, with a large gap to human performance. Analysing the errors in more detail, we find that models make reasoning mistakes in two key areas—perceptual identification and temporal grounding of objects—which is especially significant since object manipulation is central to egocentric video.
To remedy this, we perform experiments pointing models towards ‘where’ and ‘when’ to look. We do this by employing both oracle and predicted segmentations to selectively emphasize relevant objects via visual highlighting on the frames directly. In oracle experiments using ground-truth and SAM2-interpolated tracks, we found that encircling objects and displaying class names, and temporal selection—which focuses input frames on relevant object occurrences—yielded a peak improvement of 5.6%. When replicating this with an open-vocabulary segmentation model (OWLv2
28
), temporal selection and class name visualization continued to provide modest gains.

To summarize, we introduce Minerva-Ego, the first egocentric benchmark to pair complex hand-crafted multiple choice questions with dense spatiotemporally-grounded reasoning traces. This unique annotation structure allows us to move beyond simple accuracy metrics to diagnosable evaluation, proving that current models fail primarily due to perceptual grounding errors—a finding we validate through our spatiotemporal highlighting experiments.

2.4
Spatio-temporal Highlighting

Recent advances in VLMs have spurred interest in
visual prompting
techniques like Set-of-Mark (SoM)
47
, which demonstrate that overlaying semantic marks (numbers, masks, or bounding boxes) significantly unlocks grounding capabilities in static images. However, applying these concepts to egocentric video introduces unique challenges, namely reasoning over dynamic, long-form content where objects frequently enter and exit the field of view, undergo occlusion, or change state. We extend visual highlighting to the spatiotemporal domain. Distinct from SoM, as well as
7
;
45
, which operate on single frames, we introduce spatiotemporal hints that guide the model not only where to look (via spatial highlighting) but also when to look (via temporal frame selection).

Unlike “Look, Remember, and Reason”
8
, which implicitly learns grounding by training a specialized encoder on surrogate tasks, our approach uses explicit spatio-temporal highlighting directly on the pixels. This avoids the need for architectural changes or retraining, enabling us to probe perceptual failures in fixed, frontier models like Gemini and GPT.

3
Minerva-Ego

3.1
Statistics

Minerva-Ego consists of 1,160 questions covering 156 unique egocentric videos. Questions are designed to require multi-step reasoning to solve and come with 5 multiple-choice options, with one being the right answer. Additionally, we provide a detailed reasoning trace for each question that explains how to arrive at the correct answer. The reasoning traces contain references to timestamps in the video and references to objects with ground-truth segmentation masks. All annotations are entirely hand-written by highly skilled raters trained for this task. Fig.
1
shows example questions.

The videos are sourced from the HD-EPIC
34
dataset, a high-quality egocentric video dataset. We re-use their ground-truth object segmentation masks. While HD-EPIC also has QA annotations, they are generated using standardized templates automatically and do not focus on multi-step reasoning.
To maintain manageable inference costs for large models, we limit the dataset to approximately 1,000 questions, given that each requires multi-step reasoning and contains detailed traces.

Detailed statistics are provided in Fig.
2
. Videos cover a diverse range of lengths, from 10 seconds to 75 minutes. Reasoning traces are long and detailed, averaging 122 words each, and refer to both timestamps and objects of interest. The average number of objects referenced in a reasoning trace is 2.8, but some refer up to 20 objects. A more-detailed breakdown appears in Section
6.1
of the supplementary.

3.2
Dataset Creation Pipeline

Our pipeline consists of the following 3 steps:

1. Selection:

We start with all videos from the HD-EPIC
34
dataset, and overlay the existing ground-truth segmentation masks for all objects labeled on to frames of the video.

2. Annotation and Verification:
We use highly qualified raters to annotate questions, answers, and reasoning traces. For reasoning traces, raters are asked to reference the key objects of interest needed to answer the question, after being provided all the segmentation masks for the video. We then perform a quality review process, where we ask a separate set of annotators to try and answer the questions, and for the ones that they got wrong, we ask a third set of annotators to look at both sets of annotation and reconcile them, changing the questions, options or reasoning traces if necessary.
Note that our entire annotation and quality review process is done manually to ensure high quality and avoid model bias.

3. Post-processing:
Finally, we post-process the dataset by identifying and filtering modality bias (
e.g
.
questions answerable with only the text question and answers) following best practices outlined in prior work
29
;
19
;
23
;
43
, with more details provided in the supplementary materials, Sec.
6.2
.

4
Experiments and Analysis

We perform two main types of experiments on Minerva-Ego: SOTA evaluation and Spatio-temporal highlighting.

SOTA evaluation:
First, we benchmark the best video understanding models on Minerva-Ego. Unlike other works that simply look at the correctness of final answers, we also compare model reasoning traces to the ground truth, and analyze error modes.
Additionally, we perform ablations examining the effect of the number of input frames and the thinking capability.

Spatio-temporal Highlighting:
Second, we perform experiments examining the performance with spatio-temporal highlighting. This is done in two ways: (i) in an oracle manner where we provide the model with hints from our extensive spatiotemporal annotations, including object locations, class labels and the set of relevant frames. We analyze how much the model benefits from these hints, which provides interesting insights into their spatiotemporal reasoning abilities in an egocentric setting, and (ii) in a practical setting using off-the-shelf segmentation tools.

4.2
Spatio-temporal Highlighting

Recent works such as Set-of-Mark
47
show that LVLMs significantly benefit from highlighting important objects in the visual domain by directly altering the visual input (i.e. pixels). Therefore we explore whether segmentation can help via a variety of visual or textual aids. We do this first in an oracle setting and then with real segmentation results.

Oracle experiment.

The original HD-EPIC
34
dataset comes with some ground-truth segmentation annotations: each time an object is moved, the object is segmented in the first and last frame of this movement. Since this is quite sparse, we use our own re-implementation of SAM2
36
to fill in the segments of the frames in between. Hence we have decent segmented tracks of all objects when they are moved.
For each object mentioned in the reasoning traces,
if it was annotated, we linked to the corresponding object ID. This link exists for 82% of the objects in our reasoning traces.
Using this, we can highlight for each question the (majority of) relevant objects. We highlight the object by drawing a mask contour in red, or surrounding the object by a box or ellipsoid.
Additionally, we draw the class name in the frames. Fig.
5
visualizes what these annotations look like. Furthermore, since Gemini natively predicts class labels and boxes, instead of highlighting the objects we interleave frames with objects and box coordinates using the Gemini box prediction format (non-json).

5
Conclusion

We introduce Minerva-Ego,
a benchmark for evaluating complex egocentric visual reasoning consisting of a set of challenging, multi-step multimodal questions and spatiotemporally-dense human-annotated reasoning traces. Through extensive evaluations, we identify surprising successes and key areas for improvement for frontier models in video reasoning. We leverage our fine-grained annotations to better categorize reasoning errors at a more nuanced level, and show the value of our spatio-temporal highlighting for improving performance.

6.2
Dataset Post-processing details

As described in Sec.
3.2
, our pipeline consists of selection, annotation + verification, and post-processing. We elaborate here on the verification and post-processing stages.
We follow best practices and guidelines that have been established in prior work
29
;
19
;
23
;
43
, which note that benchmarks often have issues regarding modality biases (
e.g
.
questions that are answerable with only text), and propose solutions to help mitigate these. The verification stage is described in the main paper, and was conducted through manual human studies, separated reviewer pools, and several rounds of intervention and refinement. In this context, the post-processing stage is a further step of refinement on top of our initial (model-free) process. Here, we use the same model ensemble of open and closed-source text-only language models in
29
(
e.g
.
Deepseek, GPT-4o, Gemini Flash, and Qwen) and take their
consensus
agreement as a set of candidate questions that merit further review. As described in prior work, this technique is designed in order to avoid discarding difficult questions that models may have answered correctly by chance. Notably however, rather than completely discarding all questions in the consensus set like in prior work, this set was subject to one more round of review – if the question could be “corrected” (
e.g
.
the text bias was readily clear and it was clear how a change in the question or answer choices could remove this bias) then this measure was taken (we found that this was the case in the majority of the questions, where “too much” was given away in the question itself). If the question required more than a simple change, the question was removed.

9.1
MiRA Reasoning Evaluation

MiRA is a model-based metric that uses Gemini 2.5 Pro for scoring.
The exact prompt used to compute the MiRA score appears in Figure

You are an expert at grading student answers to questions about videos. For each video, you will get a question about the video, the correct reasoning, and the final answer. You will then get the reasoning from the student, and a set of criteria. Given this criteria, please provide a score from 0, 1 or 2 for each criterion that will assess the student’s work.

**Criteria:**

(1) Perceptual correctness: was the relevant information perceived accurately from the video?

(2) Temporal grounding: were time ranges provided for each piece of information from the video, and if so were they accurate?

(3) Logical reasoning: was the reasoning logically sound, given the information perceived (independent of whether that information was correct)?

(4) Completeness: were any steps skipped in the given answer or left unstated?

**Student Reasoning**

The question asks how many times heavier the second density cube is than the first.

From [01:58] to [02:04], the video shows 12 density cubes.

From [02:00] to [02:04], the video labels the first cube as "acetate" and the second cube as "polypropyline".

The closest answer choice to 1.83 is 1.77.
