Sodium-channel blocker challenge unmasks Brugada syndrome in a 54-year-old male presenting with recurrent syncope and chronic cannabis use

Introduction

Brugada syndrome (BrS) is an inherited arrhythmogenic channelopathy that predisposes structurally normal hearts to polymorphic ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation, and sudden cardiac death.1 The diagnosis hinges on the presence of a coved-type (type 1) ST-segment elevation of ≥2 mm followed by a negative T wave in 1 or more right precordial leads (V1–V2), either spontaneous or provoked. A type 2 (saddleback) pattern—a J-point elevation of ≥2 mm with an ST-segment elevation of ≥1 mm—is suggestive but nondiagnostic in isolation and in symptomatic patients requires unmasking by a sodium-channel blocker challenge to establish the diagnosis.2

Recognized triggers that can unmask or exacerbate the Brugada phenotype include fever, electrolyte derangements, vagotonic states, alcohol, cocaine, tricyclic antidepressants, and other agents that inhibit cardiac sodium current.3 Cannabis is an under-recognized but mechanistically plausible trigger: cannabinoids and their metabolites inhibit voltage-gated cardiac sodium channels and disturb autonomic balance, and isolated reports have linked acute and chronic cannabis use to unmasked Brugada electrocardiographic (ECG) patterns and life-threatening arrhythmias.4,5 With expanding legalization and rising daily use, recognition of cannabis as a modifiable trigger has clinical and public-health implications.

We present a previously healthy 54-year-old man with chronic daily cannabis use and an intercurrent febrile respiratory illness who presented with 5 syncopal episodes in a single day, a nondiagnostic type 2 Brugada ECG pattern at baseline, and conversion to a diagnostic type 1 pattern after flecainide provocation. The case illustrates the indispensable role of sodium-channel blocker challenge, the importance of identifying modifiable triggers, and a guideline-consistent strategy for implantable cardiac monitor (ICM)–based surveillance after implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) refusal.

Case report

A 54-year-old Caucasian man with no known cardiac history presented to the emergency department after 5 witnessed syncopal episodes within a single day, all occurring while seated in a recliner at home. Several episodes were preceded by a productive cough and dyspnea attributable to a 1-month upper respiratory infection, whereas others occurred at rest without obvious provocation. Prodromal symptoms included pounding headache, vertigo, subjective fever, nausea, diaphoresis, and bilateral nonradiating chest pressure. After the fifth episode, the patient was transiently confused with mumbling speech and delayed reorientation. He denied vomiting, tonic-clonic activity, tongue biting, or urinary incontinence. There was no personal or family history of sudden cardiac death, cardiomyopathy, channelopathy, or other inheritable cardiovascular disease. Medical history was notable only for untreated hyperlipidemia (nonadherent with atorvastatin), chronic daily inhaled cannabis use (approximately 1–2 times per day for many years), and remote tobacco use. He was on no proarrhythmic medications and reported no recreational drug use other than cannabis.

On arrival, the patient was alert, oriented, and diaphoretic. Vital signs were stable, including a temperature of 38.1°C; cardiovascular and respiratory examinations were unremarkable, and a neurologic examination was nonfocal. The initial 12-lead ECG (Figure 1) demonstrated normal sinus rhythm with a saddleback ST-segment morphology in V1–V2 (J-point elevation ≥2 mm; ST-segment elevation ≥1 mm), consistent with a type 2 Brugada pattern. A repeat ECG several hours later (Figure 2) showed accentuation of the saddleback morphology in V2.

The differential diagnosis for recurrent syncope in this clinical context included reflex (vasovagal or cough) syncope, orthostatic hypotension, seizure, pulmonary embolism, acute coronary syndrome, electrolyte disturbance, and primary arrhythmic syncope from a channelopathy. The recurrent nature, seated onset, cardiogenic prodrome, and characteristic right precordial repolarization abnormalities placed primary arrhythmic syncope owing to BrS at the top of the differential once alternative causes were excluded.

Laboratory evaluation—including serial high-sensitivity troponin (negative), B-type natriuretic peptide, complete metabolic panel, complete blood count, and thyroid function—was unremarkable, with normal electrolytes. Chest radiography showed clear lung fields. Coronary computed tomography angiography demonstrated Coronary Artery Disease Reporting and Data System 0 with a coronary artery calcium score of 0. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 68%, a mildly dilated left ventricle (end-diastolic volume 145 mL), normal right ventricular size and function, no regional wall-motion abnormality, no valvular pathology, and no pericardial effusion.

Because a type 2 pattern is nondiagnostic in a symptomatic patient, a pharmacologic provocation test was performed using intravenous flecainide (2 mg/kg over 10 minutes) under continuous telemetry monitoring with full resuscitation available. The challenge converted the saddleback morphology to a diagnostic type 1 coved ST-segment elevation of ≥2 mm followed by an inverted T wave in V1–V3 (Figure 3), establishing the diagnosis of BrS. A subsequent ECG several hours after drug clearance (Figure 4) demonstrated return to baseline sinus rhythm.

Risk stratification considerations were reviewed with the patient. He had a confirmed drug-induced type 1 pattern, arrhythmic syncope, no documented sustained ventricular tachycardia/ventricular fibrillation, and no family history of sudden cardiac death. According to current expert consensus,6 primary-prevention ICD therapy is a class IIa recommendation in this scenario. After detailed shared decision making—including procedural risks, inappropriate-shock burden, generator changes, and lifestyle considerations—the patient declined ICD implantation. An ICM (Reveal LINQ™, Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN) was placed for continuous rhythm surveillance and symptom–rhythm correlation, supported by evidence from the BruLoop study, showing that ICMs detect actionable arrhythmias in a substantial proportion of patients with symptomatic BrS and frequently prompt management changes.7

Comprehensive trigger-avoidance counseling was provided, including strict abstinence from cannabis, alcohol, and sodium-channel blockers; aggressive antipyresis at the earliest sign of febrile illness; and review of www.brugadadrugs.org. Driving restrictions consistent with state regulations and consensus guidance were discussed. Genetic testing was deferred to outpatient follow-up; the patient was counseled that, although SCN5A loss-of-function variants account for only 20%–30% of cases, identification of a pathogenic variant in the proband would enable cascade screening. Because BrS exhibits autosomal-dominant inheritance with variable penetrance, immediate clinical screening (history and 12-lead ECG with high right precordial lead placement) of all first-degree relatives was strongly recommended.

The patient remained hemodynamically stable, was discharged on hospital day 2, and was scheduled for a 2-week electrophysiology follow-up for device interrogation, outpatient repeat echocardiography, and proband genetic testing. At submission, he reported successful cannabis abstinence and adherence to lifestyle modification counseling, with no recurrent syncope, palpitations, or ICM-detected arrhythmic events.

Discussion

This case integrates 3 high-yield teaching points that the journal’s readership confronts in everyday practice: (1) the diagnostic indispensability of sodium-channel blocker challenge in symptomatic patients with nondiagnostic baseline ECGs, (2) chronic cannabis use as an under-recognized modifiable trigger, and (3) the use of an ICM-anchored surveillance pathway when primary-prevention ICD therapy is declined. Diagnostic role of pharmacologic provocation

Consensus statements define the type 2 saddleback pattern as suggestive but nondiagnostic in isolation; a diagnostic type 1 pattern must be present—spontaneously or after sodium-channel blocker challenge—to establish BrS in symptomatic patients.1,2 The pathophysiology reflects a reduction in inward sodium current during phase 0 of the right ventricular epicardial action potential, accentuating the phase-1 notch and predisposing to phase-2 reentry and polymorphic ventricular tachycardia.3 Flecainide, ajmaline, procainamide, or pilsicainide can be used; in our institution, flecainide is favored because of availability and procedural familiarity. Conversion from a type 2 to a type 1 pattern, as observed here, fulfills consensus diagnostic criteria.2

Cannabis as a modifiable trigger

Cannabinoids inhibit cardiac voltage-gated sodium channels and perturb autonomic tone, and previous reports have implicated cannabis use—both inhaled and vaporized tetrahydrocannabinol—in the unmasking of Brugada ECG patterns and induction of ventricular fibrillation in genetically susceptible individuals.4,5 In our patient, chronic daily cannabis exposure plausibly created a chronic sodium channel–inhibited substrate; superimposition of fever during a febrile upper respiratory illness then provided the second hit, given fever’s well-described capacity to accelerate inactivation of mutant SCN5A channels.6 Recognition of cannabis as a modifiable trigger is increasingly relevant with expanding legalization and rising daily consumption, and a deliberate inquiry into cannabis use—often missed in routine social history taking—should be standard in any patient with unexplained syncope or a suggestive ECG pattern. Beyond isolated case reports, a growing body of epidemiologic and mechanistic data links cannabis—particularly higher-potency, recreational tetrahydrocannabinol—to an increased incidence of cardiac arrhythmias through sympathetic activation and direct ion-channel effects, a concern amplified by rising recreational use.8

Multimodal exclusion of mimics

Although a type 2 Brugada pattern was present at baseline, a structured evaluation excluded ischemic, structural, metabolic, embolic, and reflex-mediated causes of syncope: serial troponin and B-type natriuretic peptide were negative, coronary computed tomography angiography showed Coronary Artery Disease Reporting and Data System 0, echocardiography demonstrated preserved systolic function without structural abnormality, and chest radiography was unremarkable. Although some episodes were temporally associated with coughing, the majority occurred at rest in a seated position with a cardiogenic prodrome, and the presence of a flecainide-confirmed type 1 pattern makes a primary channelopathy the most parsimonious unifying diagnosis.9 The only structural finding was mild left ventricular dilatation (end-diastolic volume 145 mL) with a preserved ejection fraction of 68% and normal right ventricular size and function. This isolated mild dilatation is a nonspecific finding—plausibly reflecting athletic or physiologic remodeling—and is not a feature of the Brugada phenotype, which is classically defined by a structurally normal heart. Importantly, the absence of right ventricular structural or functional abnormality argues against arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy, a key structural mimic, and the preserved systolic function makes a dilated cardiomyopathy unlikely; nonetheless, outpatient surveillance echocardiography was arranged to confirm stability over time.

Risk stratification beyond the ECG

In drug-induced type 1 BrS with arrhythmic syncope, the role of the electrophysiology study remains controversial. Although programmed ventricular stimulation can demonstrate inducibility of sustained ventricular tachyarrhythmias, large analyses report a low positive predictive value (approximately 9%–10%) and no independent prognostic significance in multivariate models, with a high negative predictive value (approximately 95%–97%).10 Accordingly, a routine electrophysiology study carries a class IIb recommendation and is reserved for selected cases. Signal-averaged ECG may identify late potentials reflecting delayed right ventricular outflow tract conduction and has been associated with increased arrhythmic risk; however, in routine practice, the combination of a confirmed type 1 pattern, arrhythmic syncope, and male sex already places the patient in a higher-risk category, warranting consideration of an ICD.

ICM-anchored bridge strategy after ICD refusal

In confirmed BrS with arrhythmic syncope and a spontaneous or drug-induced type 1 ECG, primary-prevention ICD therapy carries a class IIa recommendation.6 This recommendation is concordant with the more recent Heart Rhythm Society–endorsed 2017 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology/Heart Rhythm Society guideline for Management of Patients With Ventricular Arrhythmias and the Prevention of Sudden Cardiac Death, which likewise designates ICD implantation a class IIa recommendation for patients with BrS and a spontaneous or induced type 1 pattern who have a history of syncope judged to be arrhythmic in origin, while reserving class I indications for survivors of cardiac arrest or those with documented sustained ventricular arrhythmia.11 When the patient declines ICD implantation after thorough shared decision making—as in this case—a structured alternative is required. The BruLoop multicenter study demonstrated that ICMs detect arrhythmic events in approximately 30% of patients with symptomatic BrS (predominantly bradyarrhythmias and atrial arrhythmias) and that nearly 70% of those with detected events undergo a management change as a result.7 Pairing ICM placement with strict trigger avoidance (fever, alcohol, cannabis, sodium channel–blocking drugs), aggressive antipyresis protocols, and cascade family screening represents a guideline-consistent, patient-centered bridge that preserves the option of future ICD implantation should arrhythmic events occur or the patient’s preferences evolve. Although it was not pursued in our patient, catheter ablation of the epicardial right ventricular outflow tract substrate is an emerging option that can attenuate the arrhythmogenic substrate, normalize the type 1 ECG, and reduce recurrent ventricular arrhythmias; it is currently reserved chiefly for patients with recurrent ventricular arrhythmias or ICD shocks but may become increasingly relevant for selected patients who decline or are unsuitable for an ICD.12

Family screening and genetics

BrS is inherited in an autosomal-dominant pattern with incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity; consensus guidance (class I) supports clinical screening of all first-degree relatives with detailed history, 12-lead ECG (including high right precordial lead positions), and periodic re-evaluation through the fifth decade.6 Targeted genetic testing of the proband is recommended primarily to enable cascade testing of relatives once a pathogenic variant is identified; a negative test does not exclude the diagnosis. Positive relatives are managed as index cases with risk stratification and lifelong trigger avoidance.

Prognosis

In the France, Italy, Netherlands, and Germany registry, annual cardiac event rates were 7.7% after aborted sudden cardiac death, 1.9% in patients with syncope, and 0.5% in asymptomatic patients; spontaneous type 1 patterns conferred modestly higher risk than drug induced.13 Long-term outcomes in drug-induced BrS without arrhythmic events are favorable (median annual event rate ≈0.5% over 5 years), but unexplained syncope is an adverse modifier.10 Our patient’s trajectory—drug-induced type 1 pattern with arrhythmic syncope, male sex, modifiable cannabis exposure, and ICM-based surveillance—situates him in an intermediate-risk group requiring vigilant follow-up.

Conclusion

Sodium-channel blocker challenge with flecainide rapidly confirmed BrS in a 54-year-old man with recurrent syncope, a nondiagnostic type 2 ECG pattern, chronic daily cannabis use, and a concurrent febrile respiratory illness. The case underscores the indispensable diagnostic value of pharmacologic provocation in symptomatic patients with suggestive but nondiagnostic baseline ECGs, identifies chronic cannabis as an under-recognized and reversible trigger, and illustrates an evidence-supported ICM-anchored surveillance pathway with strict trigger avoidance and immediate family cascade screening when primary-prevention ICD therapy is declined.
