EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This report provides an update to a previously issued report on the bioassociation of the +3
actinide analog, neodymium, with Waste Isolation Pilot Plant (WIPP) microbial isolates (LCO-
ACP-32, Biologically Influenced Transport of the +3 Actinide Oxidation State from the WIPP
Environment, Using Neodymium as an Analog). Results contribute to updates for the biocolloid
parameters for the 2026 Compliance Recertification Application (CRA-2026).
At the time that LCO-ACP-32, Revision 0, was written, seven isolates had been tested in
simplified NaCl solution, but only three (Halobacterium sp., archaeal isolate PB-1a, and
Chromohalobacter sp.) had been tested in Generic Weep Brine (GWB) and Energy Research and
Development Administration Well 6 (ERDA-6) brines. This report covers experiments on the
remaining four isolates (archaeal isolate Trans-1b, Salinicoccus sp., Nesterenkonia sp.,
Thalassobacillus sp.) with neodymium (Nd) in WIPP brines.
The results of these experiments differ only slightly from those presented in the previous report,
and the overall conclusions remain the same. In summary, all seven tested organisms influenced
Nd concentrations in simplified NaCl and ERDA, but the time frame for loss from ERDA was
slower than from NaCl. Previously, little to no biological influence was measured in GWB.
This was determined to be due to competition between neodymium and magnesium (Mg) for
surface complexation on the tested organisms. In the new experiments reported herein,
biological influence was also low in GWB with the least influence among the most halophilic
organisms. Similar behavior in NaCl and ERDA is likely due to the high concentration of NaCl
in ERDA.
With additional testing, it also appears that organism behavior differs. Two of the tested
organisms (Salinicoccus and Nesterenkonia spp.) formed large clumps immediately upon
exposure to GWB, and cells settled to the bottom of the tubes. This settling behavior was
observed in all tested organisms by the end of each experiment. Cells that are no longer in
suspension are considered immobile and not vectors for actinide transport. In addition, spores of
one tested organism (Thalassobacillus sp.) released a small amount of dipicolinate into solution
which may have led to Nd resolubilization.
Overall, these data support the conclusion that matrix is a controlling factor on the influence of
microorganisms on Nd in solution and that studies in WIPP-relevant matrices should be used to
calculate biocolloid parameter values.

1.0 INTRODUCTION
Potential biocolloid transport of actinides is an ongoing area of research for the Waste Isolation
Pilot Plant’s Compliance Recertification Application (WIPP CRA). This report provides an
update on this work and supports the colloid enhancement parameters to be put forth in the
parameter report for the CRA-2026 WIPP Performance Assessment (PA). The experiments
described herein are only for the +3 actinide oxidation state.

Microorganisms can have varied influence on actinides in solution. In terms of performance
assessment, “biocolloid” formation assumes mobilization and potential release. The term
“bioassociation” is used in this report to encompass certain actinide-microbial interactions
including surface complexation (and potential surface mineralization) and internal uptake. The
term “biological influence”, while inclusive of association phenomena, can also include induced
precipitation, solubilization with microbially-generated complexants, or even redox
transformations. This work is not focused on redox issues, but the generation of complexants is
included where relevant.

To date, three Archaea and five Bacteria have been isolated from aerobic cultures of WIPP halite
and/or brine seep stalactites at NaCl concentrations ranging from 10-27.5% (1.7-4.7 M; Swanson
et al., 2012; Swanson and Reed, 2018).

From the above list, only the three archaea and Chromohalobacter sp. are able to grow in WIPP
brines amended with substrates and nutrients. In comparison, all are able to survive exposure to
GWB and ERDA brines for a period of weeks (testing ranged from 3-39 weeks) at high biomass
concentrations before resuscitation becomes impossible. At lower biomass concentrations,
survival is shorter, but these timeframes are inconsequential when compared to the 10,000-year
repository lifetime. Those that form spores survive much longer and could remain throughout
this timespan.

This report presents data on the behavior of Nd with the remaining WIPP isolates in anoxic
WIPP brines. Neodymium was used as an analog for the +3 actinide oxidation state. Simplified
NaCl was used to screen the potential for biological influence at each organism’s optimum salt
concentration, since published data on biosorption are most often generated in simple matrices.
Experiments were then extended to anoxic WIPP brines, whose chemistry and pH can
significantly affect outcome. These results are compared to previous data in LCO-ACP-32,
Revision 0, and the overall conclusions remain the same: the background matrix affects
biological influence, and data generated in WIPP brines should be used to calculate parameter
values.

2.0 SUMMARY OF EXPERIMENTS
All organisms were grown in their optimal media at their optimal NaCl concentration (usually
referred to by weight/volume percentage, or w/v). At early stationary phase, cells were
harvested and washed five times with solution containing optimal NaCl, and the cell pellet was
stored overnight at 4°C. For the initial experiments, pellets were resuspended in simplified NaCl
solutions at each organism’s optimum. For experiments in WIPP brines, pellets were brought
into a nitrogen-filled glove box and resuspended in anoxically prepared, 90% formulations of
GWB and ERDA-6 brines. All experiments targeted an optical density (OD) equivalent to ~109
cells/ml. Optical density values and cell numbers were correlated prior to the start of each
bioassociation experiment.

Concentrated neodymium stock solutions (~ 2 mM) were prepared by adding solid NdCl3 to each
salt solution (solutions ranged between 15-30% NaCl). Working solutions containing twice the
target Nd concentration (2X) were prepared from these stocks in each respective solution and in
GWB and ERDA brines (target 2X Nd concentrations in WIPP brines ≈ 0.4 µM; target Nd in salt
solutions was higher, since solubility was not an issue). The cell suspension was then mixed 1:1
with the 2X Nd-NaCl working solution. Cell suspensions were placed on a rotator at ambient
temperature (simplified NaCl experiments in ambient atmosphere, WIPP brine experiments in
anoxic atmosphere), and samples were withdrawn periodically for analysis by inductively-
coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) and ion chromatography and for measurements of
OD, microscopic counts or plate counts.

Optical density readings were taken at each sampling point, after which samples were passed
through an Amicon 100 kDalton centrifugal filter and then diluted in 2% nitric acid.
Measurements of Nd in the filtrate are considered to be “dissolved” Nd. The filtration step

captures surface sorption, internal uptake, and induced precipitation and/or mineralization either
at the cell’s surface or from the bulk solution. Abiotic controls were run parallel to the biotic
samples to account for any precipitation not caused by the presence of organisms. This was
especially important in ERDA where Nd concentrations in abiotic samples often decreased with
time. Pooled filtrates were used to measure pH and for analyzing dipicolinate (DPA)
concentrations, where relevant. Direct microscopic counts or colony-forming unit (CFU) counts
were also obtained, when possible.

3.0 RESULTS
To date, seven WIPP microbial isolates have been tested for bioassociation with Nd in both
simplified NaCl solution and in WIPP brines. These organisms include: archaeal isolate PB-1a,
Halobacterium sp., archaeal isolate Trans-1b, Chromohalobacter sp., Salinicoccus sp.,
Nesterenkonia sp., and spores of Thalassobacillus sp. and Bacillus sp. isolated from WIPP-
bound waste. Results for Halobacterium sp. and the waste Bacillus sp. have been presented
elsewhere (Swanson, 2019; Swanson et al., 2021) but will be reviewed here for comparison.

Throughout this section, changes in Nd concentration will be referred to as “loss” or “removal
from solution.” This loss is reflected as an increase in the “percent of biological influence.”
Summary graphs presenting these data for each tested organism are presented in the Appendix.

3.1 Bacteria

3.1.1 Chromohalobacter sp. (putatively salexigens)

This organism is a member of the Gamma Proteobacteria. It is a Gram-negative, borderline
extreme halophile, capable of slow growth in WIPP brines but growing optimally at 2.57 M
NaCl. Members of this genus have been found to produce extracellular polymeric substances
(EPS) that may contribute to metal or actinide surface complexation (Biswas and Paul, 2014).
This isolate has been tested previously for its ability to adsorb neptunium in perchlorate solutions
(Ams et al., 2013) and neodymium in WIPP brine.

Ninety-seven percent of the Nd in NaCl solution with Chromohalobacter sp. was immediately
removed and remained so throughout the time course of the experiment (Figures A1 and A2).
Abiotic levels of Nd in solution also decreased slightly.

In WIPP brines, there was approximately 40% loss of added Nd from biotic GWB samples and
little to no loss from abiotic samples throughout the course of the experiment (Figures A3 and
A4). In contrast, loss from the ERDA biotic samples was immediate and significant and reached
near 100% at approximately 5 weeks (Figures A5 and A6). There was also ~40% decrease in Nd
in the ERDA abiotic samples by 5 weeks.

brines, there was a gradual decrease in colony-forming unit counts in both brines within the first
week, and a significant decrease by week 2. At the final time point, there were no viable cells in
the ERDA samples and ~106 CFU/ml in GWB (Figure 1).

3.1.2 Salinicoccus sp. (putatively roseus)
This organism is a member of the Phylum Firmicutes. It is a moderately halotolerant, pink-
pigmented, Gram-positive coccus and obligate aerobe. Members of this genus produce EPS and
form clumps when grown in broth that are often difficult to measure by OD or microscopic
counts. By microscopy, it often appears in tetrads or larger aggregates of up to 12 cells. This
clumping is significant in that it can affect the surface area of cells exposed to brine and Nd.
Salinicoccus sp. cannot grow in WIPP brines, but it can be resuscitated after exposures of 23-25
weeks in GWB and ERDA, respectively, but only when tested at high biomass concentrations
(~1E+09 cells/ml).

Neodymium biosorption onto Salinicoccus sp. was immediate and measured approximately 39%.
It increased to 55% over a 24-hour period and leveled off at 60% by one week (Figure A7).
However, at one month, apparent bioassociation was 97% (Figure A8). Neodymium was also
lost from the abiotic samples over time.

Experiments with Salinicoccus sp. in WIPP brines were conducted twice. In the first round, cells
immediately clumped together in GWB and sedimented to the bottom of each tube (Figure 2).
This prevented cell counting and may have affected Nd in solution. By 24 hours, all cells had
aggregated in ERDA as well. The results presented here are from the second round of
experiments.

A9 and A10). In ERDA, biological influence was immediate (60% at t = 0) and near-complete
Nd removal (96%) was observed within the first 12 hours (Figures A11 and A12).

3.1.3 Nesterenkonia sp.
Nesterenkonia sp. is a member of the Phylum Actinobacteria. It was isolated from two different
samples of WIPP halite (near-pure halite and argillaceous halite) both incubated in generic
halophile broth containing 10% NaCl. It is Gram-positive and halotolerant with an optimum
NaCl concentration for growth of ~2% (~0.3 M), but it is capable of growth between 0-17%
NaCl (~2.9 M). Members of the Actinobacteria are notable for their resistance to environmental
stressors, such as pH and temperature extremes, heavy metals, solvents, ionizing radiation, and
desiccation.

Neodymium removal from 0.43 M NaCl solution by Nesterenkonia sp. was immediate and near
complete, with over 99% loss from solution throughout the course of the experiment (Figures
A13 and A14). Nesterenkonia spp. cells, like Salinicoccus spp., often appear as couplets or
tetrads.

In GWB, Nd loss from solution was slow, beginning after 4 days, and apparent association
reached a maximum of only 50% at one month (Figures A15 and A16). In ERDA, loss was
gradual and near complete by one week, with a maximum apparent association of 96% (Figures
A17 and A18).

In all experiments, regardless of matrix, morphology remained coccoid. Like many
Actinobacteria, Nesterenkonia spp. can alter morphology (and hence, surface area) from cocci to
short rods during their log growth phase. Cell counts did not change significantly over the
course of the experiment in NaCl, but CFU counts dropped over an order of magnitude in GWB
and approximately two orders of magnitude in ERDA at one month (Figure 4).

3.1.4 Thalassobacillus sp. spores
Thalassobacillus sp. was isolated from a stalactite sample formed from brine seep and incubated
in growth medium containing 10% NaCl (1.71 M). It is a Gram-positive, slightly halophilic
spore forming bacillus and does not grow in the absence of NaCl. In its spore form, it can likely
survive for long periods in WIPP brines (longest tested was 39 weeks). Prior to beginning these
experiments, spores were purified with and without lysozyme and all wash solutions were tested
for the presence of dipicolinate (DPA), a ligand produced and released by some spores that can

Biologically Influenced Transport of the +3 Actinide Oxidation State Page 14 of 70

enhance actinide solubility. These spores were not too susceptible to lysozyme treatment, but
since some DPA was detected (~8 µM), lysozyme was omitted from the procedure.

Thalassobacillus sp. spore influence on Nd in 0.76 M NaCl solution was immediate, with
approximately 78% loss from solution at time zero (Figures A19 and A20). This increased
slowly over time until 98% of Nd was lost by one month. This is in contrast to previous
experiments with spores from a different organism, where the release of DPA caused all Nd to
return to solution (Swanson et al., 2021). Samples taken from the original Thalassobacillus sp.
spore suspension that had been stored for the duration of these kinetics experiments had
approximately 0.17 µM DPA, which might not have been enough to cause solubilization in these
samples.

Removal of Nd by spores in GWB was gradual and reached a maximum of 76% at 2 weeks
(Figures A21 and A22). At one month, a small amount of Nd had returned to solution. In
ERDA, Nd removal was immediate at ~80%, but over time this decreased to ~ 30% at one month
(Figures A23 and A24). Concentrations of Nd in the abiotic ERDA samples also decreased
significantly.

DPA was released in both GWB and ERDA brines immediately upon exposure to brine.
Measurements decreased over time in both brines, suggesting no subsequent release after the
initial, but levels dropped more quickly in GWB than in ERDA (within first 12 hours in GWB
and at 72 hours in ERDA; Figure 5).

Colony-forming unit counts remained stable over time in GWB, but decreased by an order of
magnitude in both NaCl and ERDA matrices (Figure 6).

3.2 Archaea

3.2.1    PB-1a
Archaeon PB-1a is the most halophilic of the WIPP archaeal isolates, with an optimum NaCl
concentration for growth of 25% (4.28 M). It has not yet been identified taxonomically, but it
was verified as an archaeon by amplification of its 16S ribosomal DNA using archaeal primers.

Neodymium loss from 4.28 M NaCl solution was gradual and took over 48 hours before it was
completely removed from solution. Approximately 10% of Nd was lost from the abiotic samples
over time (Figures A25 and A26).

There was little to no loss of Nd from GWB in either biotic or abiotic samples over time (Figures

hours, but its concentration did not change for the remainder of the experiment (Figures A29 and
A30). Cell numbers remained constant throughout (Figure 7).

3.2.2   Trans-1b
Archaeon Trans-1b is the least halophilic of the WIPP archaeal isolates, with an optimum NaCl
concentration for growth of 17.5% (2.99 M). It has not yet been identified taxonomically, but it

Approximately 30% of Nd was removed from NaCl solution immediately upon exposure, 80%

archaea due to the length of time required for growth and the difficulty in counting the pinpoint

3.2.3 Halobacterium sp. (putatively noricense)
This organism was tested previously, and results of those experiments appear in report LCO-
ACP-23, Review of the Microbial Colloid Contribution to the WIPP Actinide Source Term. To
summarize, neodymium loss from 3.4 M NaCl solution was gradual but complete over the first
24 hours, but only at higher biomass concentrations (5.03E+09 cells/ml). By one month,
approximately 50% of Nd was also lost from suspensions containing lower biomass
concentrations (2.02E+08 cells/ml).

There was almost no influence of organisms on Nd solubility in GWB. In ERDA, the percent of
influence increased slowly with time and reached 78% at 5 weeks.

4.0 DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS

4.1 Comparison between organisms in each brine
Experiments were carried out with 7 prokaryotes—3 Archaea and 5 Bacteria (Figures 8-10). All
Archaea were of the Phylum Euryarchaeota, Class Halobacteria. The Bacteria belonged to 3
different phyla: Proteobacteria (1 isolate), Actinobacteria (1 isolate), and Firmicutes (3 isolates).
Of the Firmicutes, two were bacilli that were tested in spore form (one from previous
experiments). Three organisms are Gram-negative, and four are Gram-positive. Four are
pigmented, but their pigments are not the same. Extracellular polymeric substance production
and the presence of a glycoprotein surface layer (S-layer) were not determined experimentally,
but studies have found that two are known to produce EPS—Salinicoccus sp., Chromohalobacter
sp.—and four are known to have S-layers—Halobacterium sp., isolate PB-1a, isolate Trans-1b,
and Bacillus sp. (Biswas and Paul, 2014; Sidhu and Olsen, 1997). A summary of these surface
characteristics is shown in Table 2.

4.2 Differences in biomass concentration
Not all organisms were tested for biomass dependence. For those that were (Halobacterium sp.
and waste Bacillus sp.), a significant increase in Nd removal from solution was observed at
higher biomass concentrations. This agrees with the literature and is logical based on the
increased number of available surface sites with increasing biomass.

In all WIPP brine experiments with bacteria, viable cell numbers decreased over time, often
significantly (see Figures 1b, 3b, 4b, and 6b). This is due to the higher ionic strength in the
WIPP brines compared to each organism’s optimum NaCl concentration and the inability of the
bacteria to maintain osmotic balance in these conditions. Counts in ERDA were also lower than

does not correspond to a decrease in apparent association in ERDA, which could suggest
association with sedimented dead cells or cell debris or some other means of induced
precipitation. Counts for archaea were not affected by the WIPP brines.

4.4 Differences between simple NaCl and WIPP brines: matrix effects
In the original revision of this report, only three of the seven isolates had been tested in WIPP

The results for Trans-1b, Salinicoccus sp., and Nesterenkonia sp. also followed the same trend,
but biological influence on Nd in GWB with Thalassobacillus sp. spores did not (Figure 12).

Halobacterium sp., PB-1a, and Trans-1b experiments). The most halophilic bacteria
(Chromohalobacter sp. and Salinicoccus sp.) had moderate influence on Nd in GWB (<40%),
but the effect of Salinicoccus sp. was only observed at the final time point. The reason for this
late increase is unknown but could be due to precipitation of Nd with clumped and sedimented
cells.

Results in ERDA and NaCl are more similar to each other than to GWB, likely because of the
high concentration of NaCl in ERDA. For the most extreme halophiles (Halobacterium sp. and

explanations provides the answer. Brine ionic strength, composition, and pH can affect Nd
solubility and speciation but can also affect cell surface structures and integrity. These factors
will be discussed below.

4.4.1 Experimental starting neodymium concentrations exceeded solubility
The test concentrations of Nd in WIPP brines (0.2 µM) were chosen based on the solubility of
Nd in each brine at its appropriate pCH+ value and on the detection limit for ICP-MS after the
necessary sample dilution. Neodymium solubility is slightly lower in ERDA than in GWB (1.7 x
10-7 M versus 3 x 10-7; Borkowski, 2009), and is also slightly less than the added target. This
might explain the continued loss of Nd in abiotic solutions, with the exception of the
Halobacterium sp. experiments. Nevertheless, this loss does not affect bioassociation
calculations, except at final timepoints where the abiotic values were also zero.

4.4.2 Neodymium speciation
Experiments in simple NaCl solutions were conducted at pH ~ 6 to minimize the effects of
hydrolysis and carbonate. In those experiments, the majority of Nd was present as Nd3+ and
NdCl2+ (M. Richmann, EQ3/6 calculations in Swanson et al., 2021; Migdisov and Williams-
Jones, 2002). Both charged species could be involved in surface complexation. In experiments
in WIPP brines, the pH was not adjusted. At brine pCH+ values and in the absence of carbonate,
Nd is expected to exist as the hydrolysis species Nd(OH)2+, and to a lesser extent Nd(OH)2+ and
Nd(OH)3(aq) (Borkowski, 2009; Neck et al., 2009). These species are also charged, suggesting
that surface complexation is possible. However, this does not explain the differences in apparent
sorption between ERDA and GWB.

4.4.3 Loss of S-layers
The S-layer helps to maintain cell structure in haloarchaea. Magnesium stabilizes S-layers, and
changes in Mg concentration can lead to S-layer loss and subsequent changes in cell morphology
(Horikoshi et al., 1993). The requirement for Mg varies between organisms, but a Haloarcula
species was found to change morphology below 41 mM. The test organisms for these
experiments are usually grown in broth containing 98 mM of Mg. Ninety-percent ERDA
contains 17 mM Mg, and 90% GWB contains 900 mM, suggesting that S-layer loss was possible
in ERDA but unlikely in GWB.

EDTA concentrations as low as 10 mM can also lead to S-layer shedding due to chelation of S-
layer Mg2+ ions (Cohen et al., 1991; Dyall-Smith, 2009). While WIPP inventory does not
contain this much EDTA, it is unknown what effect a lower concentration could have.

In these experiments, loss of the S-layer should have led to a decrease in surface sites, and a
change in cell morphology to small coccoid cells would have resulted in less surface area overall,
ultimately in both cases resulting in less apparent sorption. Therefore, this is unlikely to be the
sole reason for the observed findings for the haloarchaea.

4.4.5 Biologically induced formation of Nd-containing precipitates
Biologically induced mineral precipitation is a well-known phenomenon, and many reviews on
the mechanisms of this process exist in the literature (e.g., Frankel and Bazylinski, 2003;
Hoffmann et al., 2021). Microbial cell surfaces offer numerous potential nucleation sites, while
microbial activity (even if minimal) can often alter solution characteristics, such as pH, localized
ion concentrations, and organic releases that can promote mineralization. In some cases, cell
surface characteristics (e.g., EPS) enhance these conditions. Furthermore, in high ionic strength
matrices, it can be easier to reach the saturation index for some ions. Biologically induced
precipitation can result in loss of Nd from solution but would appear as apparent sorption in
these experiments.

Some haloarchaea have been found to have a high density of carboxyl sites at their surface that
catalyze dolomite precipitation under ideal Mg:Ca ratios, even under non-growth conditions
(Kenward et al., 2013). Members of the Chromohalobacter and Nesterenkonia genera can
precipitate different carbonate species, depending upon growth conditions (Rivadeneyra et al.,
2000, 2006), and some can incorporate strontium into the mineral phase (Sánchez-Román et al.,
2011), providing a possible radionuclide sink. Bader et al. (2019) found that resting cells of
Halobacterium noricense DSM 15987 sequestered europium and curium through the release of
phosphate species, one of which was a solid phase precipitate.

For some organisms, the S-layer also serves to protect cells from environmental toxicants, such
as heavy metals (Chandramohan et al., 2019). If mineralization occurs on an S-layer, it can be
shed and then precipitates (Schultze-Lam et al., 1996).

4.5 Basis for colloid enhancement parameter recommendations for CRA
The previous version of this report suggested that it might be easier to account for and predict
long-term biological influence based on test matrix rather than on organism, given the
differences in behavior in GWB. This is quite beneficial to PA, because it suggests that
chemistry, more than biology, is the controlling factor for biocolloid measurements in the long-
term. The new data support this hypothesis in most cases.

The results of these studies directly feed into calculations for the biocolloid parameters
PROPMIC and CAPMIC. PROPMIC is the proportionality constant for the concentration of
actinide associated with mobile microbes (defined as the moles of microbe-bound actinide
divided by the moles of dissolved actinide). CAPMIC is the maximum concentration of actinide
associated with mobile microbes (defined as the moles of microbe-bound actinide per liter).

Biologically Influenced Transport of the +3 Actinide Oxidation State Page 28 of 70

Values for these parameters are significantly higher in all experiments conducted in simple NaCl
than those in WIPP brines (Table 4; Figures 14 and 15). One reason for this difference could be
the pH and composition of each matrix, which could affect Nd solubility. However, previous
work in pCH+ 9.5-specific brine showed the same results as GWB, suggesting that pH is not the
reason (Swanson, 2019). Another reason could be brine complexity and the potential for other
interactions with Nd besides cell surfaces. Since both GWB and pCH+ 9.5-specific brines are
high in Mg, this again suggests cation competition for cell surface sites. Since ERDA is mostly
NaCl, it makes sense that bioassociation behavior should be more similar in ERDA and simple
NaCl solutions. Taken together, these data point toward the background matrix as the dominant
cause of the observed trends in these experiments.
