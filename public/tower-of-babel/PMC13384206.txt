VEGFR2 dimerization kinetics on the cell surface determined by microscopy and mathematical modeling
Authors: Jaime Guerrero, Zachariah Malik, Fnu Bilal, Soma Jana, Aparajita Dasgupta, Khuloud Jaqaman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInter-receptor interactions play a key role in cell signaling in response to external stimuli. For quantitative understanding and modeling of transmembrane signal transduction, it is necessary to quantify receptor interaction kinetics (i.e., association and dissociation rate constants) on the cell surface. Live-cell single-molecule imaging (SMI) has the powerful ability to capture transient receptor interaction events in their native cellular environment with high spatiotemporal resolution. However, it reveals these interactions for the labeled subset only, which is a small fraction of the full population of receptors. We have previously shown that mathematical modeling, combined with SMI data, offers a route to compensate for this lost information and infer the population-level receptor interaction kinetics from SMI data. Here, we applied this approach to vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGFR2), both wild-type full-length VEGFR2 (FLR2)—the dimerization of which is necessary for activation—and a truncated mutant consisting of the extracellular and transmembrane domains (ECTM), which has been shown to exhibit reduced homotypic interactions in the absence of ligand. We developed a stochastic mathematical model mimicking VEGFR2 diffusion and homotypic interactions and determined the unknown model parameters (most importantly, association and dissociation rate constants) through a combination of direct experimental measurements and stochastic model calibration. As expected, we found that ECTM was primarily monomeric, while FLR2 exhibited substantial reversible dimerization. Importantly, our inference revealed that the difference between FLR2 and ECTM was primarily in their dimer association rate constant, which was about an order of magnitude higher for FLR2 than for ECTM. To our knowledge, this is the first time that the dimerization kinetics of VEGFR2, i.e., its association and dissociation rate constants, have been measured in intact live cells.

Significance

Interactions between cell-surface receptors play a large role in the initiation and modulation of cell signaling. Elucidating the kinetics of these interactions (i.e., their association and dissociation rate constants) is critical for a quantitative understanding of receptor and cell signaling. Here, we develop strategies to combine single-molecule imaging, stochastic mathematical modeling, and rigorous model calibration to derive the dimerization kinetics of vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGFR2; both full-length and a truncation mutant with reduced dimerization) on the surface of live cells. To our knowledge, this is the first time that the association and dissociation rate constants of VEGFR2 dimerization have been measured in intact live cells. Our approach is generalizable, opening an avenue for quantifying molecular interaction kinetics in situ.

Introduction

Cell-surface receptors exhibit a high degree of dynamic organization, including myriad homotypic and heterotypic interactions.1,2,3,4,5,6 There is mounting evidence that these interactions play a critical role in regulating receptor function and signaling.1,2,3,4,5,6 However, we know little about the kinetics, i.e., the association and dissociation rate constants, of these interactions. Knowing these rate constants is critical for understanding the temporal aspects of receptor signaling, fundamental information for a complete understanding of cell signaling.7,8,9 This gap in our knowledge is largely due to transmembrane proteins not being amenable to purification for traditional biophysical studies in vitro. More problematically, the solution environments of in vitro biophysical approaches are very different from the lipid bilayer environment in which cell-surface receptors reside. Studies have shown that receptor interactions in the plasma membrane can have different energetics and kinetics from the interactions observed in vitro.10,11,12,13,14,15

Various fluorescence microscopy techniques allow for the investigation of receptor interactions in a cellular environment.16,17 Most of these techniques, including fluorescence cross-correlation spectroscopy, (ensemble) Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET), and related methods, yield the equilibrium dissociation constant of the investigated interactions,18,19,20,21,22,23,24 reflecting interaction affinities and energetics. As the dissociation constant is a ratio of the association and dissociation rate constants, these approaches on their own do not yield rate constants (the same dissociation constant can result from fast rates or slow rates). Rather, determining association and dissociation rate constants has been limited to various special cases. For example, fluorescence recovery after photobleaching can be used to estimate interaction kinetics when one of the interaction partners is immobile.25,26 Ensemble FRET has been used to estimate the interaction kinetics between two cytosolic proteins, but this required the microinjection of one of the proteins into cells, thus creating transients used for rate derivation.27 Overall, determining receptor interaction kinetics, i.e., association and dissociation rate constants, in the cellular environment remains a major challenge, creating a gap in our knowledge of receptor and cell signaling and hindering our ability for quantitative modeling of these processes.28,29,30,31,32

Among the fluorescence microscopy techniques, single-molecule (SM) imaging (SMI)-based techniques—one-color, multi-color, and FRET—have the powerful ability to capture individual receptor interaction events in their native environment.33,34,35,36,37,38,39,40,41 Therefore, SM-based approaches have the potential to reveal not only the equilibrium dissociation constant of interactions but also their association and dissociation rate constants. However, to achieve SM sensitivity despite the diffraction-limited resolution of the light microscope (200–250 nm) allowing the accurate detection and tracking of individual molecules, often only a small fraction of receptors can be observed at a time. This fraction depends on the density of the receptor(s) of interest, whether endogenous or exogenous, with higher expression levels requiring lower labeled/observed fractions (while lower expression levels allow higher labeled/observed fractions). As a result, in general only a small fraction of interaction events is captured in SMI experiments. This complicates the estimation of interaction kinetics.

To compensate for the missing information in SMI experiments and determine receptor interaction kinetics in their native cellular environment, we previously developed an integrated SMI and computational modeling approach, termed “Framework for the Inference of in Situ Interaction Kinetics” (FISIK).42 FISIK combines SMI data with stochastic mathematical modeling to infer, from the observed subset of interactions, the interaction kinetics of the full population of receptors. The main idea is that, by incorporating experimental data-acquisition artifacts into the model-generated data (such as substoichiometric labeling), the artifacts would be factored out when matching model-generated and experimental data, allowing us to learn the interaction kinetics of the full, underlying receptor system.42,43,44,45

Here, we extended and applied FISIK to the cell-surface receptor vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGFR2), a critical receptor on the surface of vascular endothelial cells (VECs), where its signaling underlies the formation and maintenance of blood vessels in normal physiology and disease.2,46 Dimerization is a necessary step for VEGFR2 activation and signaling.2,47,48 VEGFR2 can also reversibly dimerize in the absence of ligand, leading to important interplay between the basal-state dynamic organization of VEGFR2, its binding to ligand, and subsequent activation steps.35,49,50 VEGFR2’s reversible dimerization in the basal state provided us with an ideal steady-state system to apply FISIK, without the concern for global transients (as would be the case upon stimulation). Moreover, a truncation mutant of VEGFR2, consisting of only its extracellular and transmembrane domains (ECTM), has been shown to undergo less dimerization than wild-type full-length VEGFR2 (FLR2) in the absence of ligand,18,49 providing us with a negative control for comparison with FLR2. Additionally, a previous (ensemble) FRET-based study estimated the dissociation constant (KD) of VEGFR2 dimerization in plasma-membrane-derived vesicles that we could compare to our results.49 To the best of our knowledge, our work is the first to determine the kinetics (i.e., association and dissociation rate constants) of VEGFR2 dimerization in an intact cell membrane, providing further insight into the difference in dimerization affinity between FLR2 and ECTM.

Materials and methods

Plasmids

Plasmid encoding wild-type full-length VEGFR2 fused to HaloTag at its N terminus (Halo-FLR2) was generated by VectorBuilder services (Chicago, IL). HaloTag (Promega, Madison, WI) was fused to the N terminus of VEGFR2 via a EPTTEDLYFQSDN(AIA) linker and cloned into a pRP[EXP] vector containing an interleukin-6 secretion signal.51 A truncated cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter (CMV100), courtesy of the Danuser lab (UT Southwestern, Dallas, TX), was used to reduce protein expression levels.

Plasmid encoding a truncated VEGFR2 mutant, containing only the extracellular and transmembrane domains, fused to HaloTag at its C terminus (ECTM-Halo), was assembled as follows. The ECTM-mCherry (pcDNA3.1) vector, courtesy of the Hristova lab (Johns Hopkins University, Baltimore, MD),49 was linearized using MluI and PspOMI restriction enzymes. Two gBlocks (Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, IA), one containing the CMV100 promoter with flanking regions homologous to the linearized vector and the ECTM coding sequence and another containing the HaloTag sequence with flanking regions homologous to the ECTM linker (GGS × 5) and linearized vector backbone, were synthesized. The ECTM-linker sequence was amplified from the ECTM-mCherry vector using forward primer ATGGAGAGCAAGGTGCTGCTG and reverse primer CGGTGCACTTCCTCCACTTCC. NEBuilder HiFi DNA assembly (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA) was used to assemble the DNA fragments to the plasmid backbone using the recommended protocol.

Cell culture and transient transfections

Chinese hamster ovary (CHO-K1; ATCC, Manassas, VA, CCL-61) cells were grown in F-12K culture medium (ATCC, 30-2004) supplemented with fetal bovine serum (FBS, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, A56697-01) added to 10% final concentration and 0.1× of antibiotic-antimycotic (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MT30004CI). Cells were grown at 37°C + 5% CO2 and were passaged once reaching 70%–90% confluence (every ∼48 h). Passaging was done by trypsinization (0.05% trypsin-EDTA, Thermo Fisher, 25300-054) for ∼5 min followed by resuspension in complete F12K medium at a 1:20–1:10 dilution. For imaging experiments, 1.5 × 105 cells in 2 mL of medium were plated on base/acid cleaned glass-bottom dishes (14 mm glass diameter with glass thickness of 0.17 mm [#1.5]; MatTek, Ashland, MA, P35G-1.5-14-C) precoated for 45 min with 10 μg/mL fibronectin (Millipore Sigma, Burlington, MA, F2006-2MG). After 24–30 h of plating, when cells were at 60%–90% confluence, cells were transfected with Halo-FLR2 or ECTM-Halo using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 25300-054) following the manufacturer’s instructions for 6-well dishes using 1.5 μg of the plasmid of interest. Transfected cells were grown overnight at 37°C + 5% CO2 until labeling and imaging. In total, imaging experiments were performed ∼2 days after cell plating.

Human telomerase-immortalized microvascular endothelial cells (TIME cells; ATCC, CRL-4025) were used for VEGFR2 phosphorylation experiments. Cells were passaged in ATCC’s vascular cell basal medium (ATCC, PCS-100-030) supplemented with microvascular endothelial cell growth kit-VEGF (ATCC, PCS-100-041), 12.5 μg/mL blasticidin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A11139-03), and 0.1× antibiotic-antimycotic (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MT30004CI) for 48 h at 37°C + 5% CO2 until reaching 80%–90% confluence. Two days before experiments, 3.75 × 104 cells were seeded on fibronectin-coated dishes as described above.

Halo labeling for live-cell SM imaging

Plated CHO cells transfected with VEGFR2 constructs as described above (2 days post seeding, 1 day post transfection) were first washed once in wash buffer (Hanks’ balanced salt solution [HBSS; Thermo Fisher Scientific, 14025-092] + 1 mM HEPES [Thermo Fisher Scientific, 15630-080] and 0.1% normal goat serum [NGS; Jackson Immuno Research, West Grove, PA, 005-000-121]) to remove culture medium, then incubated at 37°C for 15 min with fresh prewarmed F12K medium with JaneliaFluor 549 (JF549) HaloTag ligand (Promega, GA1110). For the main SMI experiments (Figure 1), JF549 Halo ligand concentration was 6 nM (FLR2) or 12 nM (ECTM). For the low-labeling SMI experiments for intensity analysis (Figure S2), JF549 Halo ligand concentration was 0.5 nM (FLR2) or 1 nM (ECTM). The medium was then replaced with ligand-free F12K medium and incubated for 15 min to allow cells to recover from the wash. Incubation medium was then removed, and cells were washed in wash buffer once followed by the addition of prewarmed imaging buffer (wash buffer + 1% Oxyfluor [Oxyrase, Mansfield, OH, OF-0005], 2 mM Trolox [Millipore Sigma, 238813], and 0.45% glucose [Millipore Sigma, G8769]) to reduce photobleaching during imaging. Cells were then taken immediately for imaging.

Halo labeling for fixed-cell SM imaging

For the low-labeling fixed-cell SMI experiments for intensity analysis (Figures S2E and S2F), CHO cells transfected with ECTM were labeled as described above for low-labeling live-cell SMI experiments. After the final wash for Halo labeling, samples were fixed with a 4% paraformaldehyde solution (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA, 15714) made in HBSS and incubated for 15 min at room temperature in darkness. Samples were then washed three times for 5 min each at room temperature. Samples that were to be imaged later in the day were kept in wash buffer at 4°C. Otherwise, washing was followed by the addition of prewarmed imaging buffer, and the samples were then taken for imaging.

Halo and antibody labeling for two-color fixed-cell colocalization imaging

Plated CHO cells transfected with VEGFR2 constructs were labeled with JF549 Halo ligand (6 nM for FLR2 and 12 nM for ECTM) and fixed as described above. Fixation and washing were followed by 30 min blocking in blocking buffer (1% bovine serum albumin [BSA] and 5% NGS in wash buffer). The samples were then incubated for 1 h with 1:1,000 primary antibody against VEGFR2 (4B4; Thermo Fisher, MA5-15556) in blocking buffer. After washing three times (5 min each), samples were incubated with 1:1,000 secondary antibody conjugated to AlexaFluor 488 (goat anti-mouse; Thermo Fisher, A11001) for 15 min at room temperature. Finally, after three subsequent washes (5 min each), dishes were either incubated with prewarmed imaging buffer to proceed with imaging or kept in wash buffer overnight at 4°C before imaging the next day.

Halo and antibody labeling for fixed-cell phosphorylation imaging

Cells (2 days post seeding and, in the case of CHO cells, 1 day post transfection) were serum starved for 3 h. For serum starvation, CHO cells were incubated in serum-free F12K medium, while TIME cells were incubated in serum-free vascular cell basal medium. Cells were then stimulated with 2 nM VEGF-A165 (GenScript, Piscataway, NJ, Z02689) for 5 min, followed by a quick wash with 1 mL wash buffer. Cells were then fixed with a 4% paraformaldehyde solution made in HBSS and incubated for 15 min at room temperature before samples were washed three times for 5 min each at room temperature.

The procedure for VEGFR2 labeling varied by cell type. In the case of CHO cells, Halo-FLR2 was labeled during the serum starvation period (before VEGF addition and fixation) as follows. After 2.5 h of serum starvation, transfected CHO cells were quickly washed with 1 mL of wash buffer, then incubated with 12 nM JF549 Halo ligand in serum-free F12K for 15 min, immediately followed by a 15-min incubation with serum-free F12K medium without Halo ligand. This yielded a total serum starvation time of 3 h. In the case of TIME cells, endogenous VEGFR2 was labeled after cell fixation. Here, TIME cells were blocked for 30 min in blocking buffer (1% BSA and 5% NGS in wash buffer) and incubated for 1 h with 1:1,000 primary antibody against VEGFR2 (4B4; Thermo Fisher, MA5-15556) in blocking buffer. Cells were then washed three times for 5 min each and incubated for 15 min at room temperature with 1:1,000 secondary antibody conjugated to AlexaFluor 568 (goat anti-mouse; Thermo Fisher, A11004). Cells were then washed again three times for 5 min each.

After labeling VEGFR2, both CHO and TIME cells were permeabilized with cold permeabilization buffer (0.1% Triton X-100 in HBSS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, BP151-100) for 1 min, followed by washing three times for 5 min each. Cells were then blocked for 30 min in blocking buffer. Blocking buffer was replaced with 1:100 anti-phospho-VEGFR2 antibody (19A10; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, 2478) diluted in blocking buffer to detect phosphorylated VEGFR2. Cells were then washed three times for 5 min each, followed by incubation with 1:1,000 secondary antibody conjugated to AlexaFluor 647 (goat anti-rabbit; Thermo Fisher, A32733) for 15 min at room temperature. Finally, after three subsequent washes (5 min each), dishes were incubated with either prewarmed imaging buffer to proceed with imaging or kept in wash buffer overnight at 4°C before imaging the next day.

Live-cell SM imaging

Live CHO cells (transfected with VEGFR2 constructs and labeled as described earlier) in imaging buffer were imaged at 37°C using an Olympus IX83 TIRF microscope equipped with a Z-Drift Compensator and a UAPO 100×/1.49 NA oil-immersion TIRF objective (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The microscope was equipped with an iXon 888 1k × 1k EMCCD Camera (Andor; Oxford Instruments, Oxfordshire, UK). With an additional 1.6× magnification in place, the pixel size in the recorded image was 81 nm × 81 nm. Using the Olympus cellSens software, we directed excitation light of 561 nm from an Olympus CellTIRF-4Line laser system to the sample by a TRF8001-OL3 Quad-band dichroic mirror. Laser power at sample position (in the wide-field illumination configuration) was ∼5.3 mW for the 561 laser. Fluorescence was collected, filtered with emission filters of ET520/40m, ET605/52m, and ET705/72m (Chroma), and projected onto different sections of the camera chip by an OptoSplit III 3-channel image splitter (Cairn Research, Faversham, UK). The penetration depth was set to 90 nm via the cellSens software (Olympus). Camera EM gain was set to 100 for all acquisitions. Videos were acquired with MetaMorph (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA) in the stream acquisition mode using the 561 channel. They were acquired at 10 Hz for 30 s for the main SMI experiments (Figure 1) or for 100–250 s for the low-labeling SMI experiments for intensity analysis (Figure S2). The 561 channel was triggered to remain open (i.e., continuous illumination) for the entire acquisition time. Temperature and humidity were maintained using an environment chamber (Okolab, Otaviano, Italy), maintaining cell viability for the duration of the experiments.

For every SMI movie, a bright-field (BF) and/or interference reflection microscopy (IRM) snapshot of the imaged cell region was also acquired, to aid with manual delineation of the region of interest (ROI) mask for the ensuing analysis.

Fixed-cell imaging

For both the two-color colocalization and phosphorylation experiments, fixed CHO cells (transfected with VEGFR2 constructs and treated and/or labeled as described above) in imaging buffer were imaged at 37°C using the Olympus IX83 TIRF microscope described in the previous section. For these experiments, the two channels were excited and recorded sequentially, starting with 561 followed by 491 (two-color colocalization) or 640 (phosphorylation), with an exposure time of 99 ms each. Laser powers at sample position (in the wide-field illumination configuration) were ∼1.3, ∼5.3, and ∼4.1 mW for 488, 561, and 640, respectively.

For every two-channel image, a BF or IRM snapshot of the imaged cell region was also acquired to aid with manual ROI mask delineation for the ensuing analysis.

Bead preparation and imaging for registration shift correction in fixed cells

To acquire images of Tetraspeck beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, T7279) for calculating the registration shift between the two channels in fixed-cell images (561 and 491 or 561 and 640), Tetraspeck beads were suspended (1:100) in distilled water using a sonicator-water bath for 15 min. The mixed-bead sample was then combined with 10 μL of poly-L-lysine (Newcomer Supply, Middleton, WI) diluted 1 in 10 μL in nuclease-free water, then plated on a cleaned MatTek dish (as used for cells but without fibronectin coating) for 30 min at room temperature in darkness. The bead sample was imaged using the Olympus IX83 TIRF microscope described above. The penetration depth was set to 90 nm via the cellSens software (Olympus). Laser powers at sample position (in the wide-field illumination configuration) were ∼0.62, ∼1.8, and ∼1.4 mW for 488, 561, and 640, respectively.

Cell ROI mask

For both fixed and live-cell data, cell ROI masks were hand-drawn using the u-track package (https://github.com/DanuserLab/u-track). Cell outlines were hand-drawn in either the BF or IRM image, with reference to the associated fixed-cell or SMI data.

SMI data: Particle detection and tracking

Particle detection and tracking for both real SMI movies and synthetic images were performed using u-track52 (https://github.com/DanuserLab/u-track). Particle detection used the “Gaussian mixture-model fitting” algorithm. The particle localization precision in our SMI movies, estimated through the Gaussian least-squares fit, was 14.6 ± 6.4 nm. The detection and tracking parameters, listed in the tables below (for non-default values), were optimized based on visual inspection of the detection and tracking results. In addition to the explicitly user-defined parameters, the number of frames used to calculate the intensity before and after merges and splits inside the tracking code (line 807 in costMatRandomDirectedSwitchingMotionCloseGaps) was changed from 2 to 10 in order to increase the stringency of capturing the merging and splitting events. In the case of experimental data, only tracks within the cell ROI mask were retained for further analysis.

The tracks as output by u-track (for both experimental and simulated data) were then refined to remove spurious merging and splitting events as follows: if a merge was followed by a split within ≤2 frames, both merge and split were removed; if a split was followed by a merge of the same two particles within ≤2 frames, both split and merge were removed; if a particle split and then disappeared within ≤2 frames, the split was removed; if a particle appeared and then merged with another particle within ≤2 frames, the merge was removed.

SMI data: Diffusion analysis

To construct the distribution of diffusion coefficients for simulating VEGFR2 (FLR2 or ECTM) diffusion and interactions, first the diffusion coefficient of each track in the SMI (experimental) data was calculated from its average square frame-to-frame displacement over its lifetime, taking into account its localization precision, as previously described35,53 (code available at https://github.com/kjaqaman/diffModesF2F). A distribution of diffusion coefficients was then constructed from these individual track measurements such that each track’s contribution to the distribution was weighted by the track’s lifetime. Weighted contributions compensated for lifetime variation between tracks and minimized bias in the distribution resulting from faster molecules being tracked less well than slower molecules. Practically, weighting was done by repeating the diffusion coefficient of each track by the number of frames in the track when constructing the histogram of diffusion coefficients.

SMI data: Calculation of apparent oligomeric state densities and fractions and of apparent association and dissociation rate constants

To compare between experimental and simulated data (Figure 4), the apparent oligomeric state densities and fractions and the apparent association and dissociation rate constants for the labeled subset were calculated for all cells of a particular condition together (experimental data, Figures 1E–1H and 1I)) or for all simulations of a particular combination of model parameters together (simulated data, Figures S7–S10). The uncertainty in the calculated statistics (error bars in Figures 1E–1H and 1I and in Figures S7–S10) was estimated using Jackknife resampling,54 i.e., by taking out one cell/simulation at a time and calculating the statistics from the remaining cells/simulations. The error bars are the standard deviation among the Jackknife samples.

The statistics were calculated as described in de Oliveira and Jaqaman42 (code available at https://github.com/kjaqaman/FISIK). In brief, the oligomeric state of each detected particle was determined using constrained least-squares minimization (Equations 2 and 3 in de Oliveira and Jaqaman42). Practically, to impose the rule that a particle was monomeric unless merging and/or splitting events dictated otherwise (because of the large fluorophore intensity variation, as discussed in results and discussion), the intensity of an individual fluorophore in Equation 2 of de Oliveira and Jaqaman42 was set to a very large number. Having the oligomeric state of each particle at each time point, the density of receptor complexes (i.e., particles) of oligomeric state 1, 2, etc., per time point was calculated by dividing the number of particles of oligomeric state 1, 2, etc. by the cell mask area (both summed over all cells/simulations in sample). This was then averaged over all time points in the SMI movies/simulations. The total density of receptor complexes regardless of oligomeric state and the fraction of each oligomeric state were then calculated from the time-averaged oligomeric state densities using Equations S1 and S2 in Note S2.

Treating transitions between oligomeric states as a Markov process, the dissociation rate constant per oligomeric state ≥2 was calculated from the oligomeric state history of each detected particle and its associated merging and splitting events, using Equation 4 in de Oliveira and Jaqaman.42 Note that any particular oligomeric state n can end because of dissociation (n → n − 1) or association (n → n + 1). Therefore, for calculating the dissociation rate constant for a particular oligomeric state, both its distribution of lifetimes and its probability to end by dissociation are employed in Equation 4 of de Oliveira and Jaqaman.42 Finally, assuming steady state, the association rate constant per oligomeric state ≥2 was calculated from the dissociation rate constant and oligomeric state densities using Equation 5 in de Oliveira and Jaqaman.42

To calculate labeled receptor complex (i.e., particle) density on a per-cell basis (Figure 1F), the same density calculation procedures were followed as above but treating each cell as its own sample. The labeled receptor density on a per-cell basis (Figure 1G) was then calculated from the complex density on a per-cell basis using Equation S10 in Note S2.

Fixed-cell data: Particle detection and channel registration correction

Particle detection in fixed-cell and Tetraspeck bead images was performed using “point-source detection” in u-track52 (https://github.com/DanuserLab/u-track). Default parameter values were used, except for the α-value used for selecting objects by comparing the fitted Gaussian amplitude to the local background noise distribution. The α-values for 640, 561, and 488 channels were, respectively, 0.005, 0.045, and 0.04 for the cell data and 0.001, 0.000001, and 0.001 for the bead data. These values were optimized via manual inspection with the goal of minimizing both false positives (superfluous detections) and false negatives (missed particles).

To increase the accuracy of the ensuing colocalization analysis, the coordinates of detected particles in the 640 (phosphorylation experiment) or 488 (two-colocalization experiment) channel were aligned relative to the 561 channel. This was achieved by calculating a registration shift between the three channels using Tetraspeck bead images. In brief, the Tetraspeck bead detections in the three channels were matched between channels using the Linear Assignment Problem52 using a search radius of 9 pixels between 491 and 561 and a search radius of 5 pixels between 640 and 561. The 561 channel was taken as the reference. The matches from multiple images acquired on the same day (3–5 images) were then utilized together to increase imaged area coverage. Using the matched bead detections, we fitted the registration shifts in x and y (Δx and Δy) as a function of position (x and y) using a quadratic polynomial, deemed visually as the most appropriate fit. With this fit, the detections in the fixed-cell multi-channel images were then aligned prior to colocalization analysis.

Fixed-cell data: Two-color colocalization and related analyses

Colocalization analysis of the fixed-cell images (after channel registration) was performed as described in Vega-Lugo et al.55 and Helmuth et al.56 (https://github.com/kjaqaman/conditionalColoc). This object-based colocalization analysis yielded the fraction of target objects colocalized with reference objects as a measure of colocalization. A target object was considered to be colocalized with reference objects if the distance between the target object and its nearest-neighbor reference object was smaller than a user-defined threshold (“colocalization radius,” taken as 3 pixels = 243 nm).

In the case of the independent dual-labeling analysis (Figure 2), the antibody detections were the target and the Halo-label detections were the reference. After obtaining the colocalized fraction per cell, the plot of the fraction of antibody colocalized with Halo-label versus the density of Halo-label per cell was fitted with a straight line passing through zero in order to obtain the full-population density of receptor complexes (Equation 2 in results and discussion). The fit was performed via least squares using the MATLAB function “fitlm,” which also yielded the 95% confidence interval of the line slope (shown in Figures 2B and 2C) and the slope’s standard deviation, the latter used for sampling to obtain a distribution for the full-population density of receptor complexes (Figure 2D).

Simulation of VEGFR2 diffusion, interactions, and substoichiometric labeling

These simulations were based on those described in de Oliveira and Jaqaman,42 with the added feature that receptors within a simulation had individual diffusion coefficients drawn from the distribution of FLR2 or ECTM diffusion coefficients measured experimentally (Figure 1J) (in the original simulations, all receptors in a simulation had the same diffusion coefficient). Here, we list details specific to the simulations in this work (code available at https://github.com/kjaqaman/FISIK). Each parameter combination (association probability and dissociation rate constant) was represented by 200 simulations (for the model calibration simulations; Figure 4) or 50 simulations (for the full system simulations after identifying the matching model parameters; Figure 5). For each simulation, the receptor density and labeled fraction were randomly chosen from their experimentally derived distributions (Figures 3C and 3D). The simulation area was taken as 25 × 25 μm2 (with reflective boundaries) to minimize boundary effects on the simulated system.42 Receptors were randomly placed in the simulation area, initially all as monomers, and each simulation started with a 10-s initialization time for the system to reach steady state. Simulations were then run for 30 s (for the model calibration simulations, like the SMI data) or 120 s (for the full system simulations after identifying the matching model parameters). Simulation time step was 0.01 s.

Generation of synthetic SM movies from simulations

Synthetic SM movies were generated from the simulations described above by placing the molecules in a pixelated image, with pixel size = 81 nm like the experimental data. The synthetic SM movies were generated in the following manner to mimic the experimental SMI movie properties as much as possible (code available at https://github.com/kjaqaman/FISIK).

Point spread function, motion blur, and sampling rate

A Gaussian with standard deviation = 1.5 pixels (= 122 nm), approximating the point spread function (PSF) of a single fluorophore, was placed at the location of each molecule. Movies were first generated at 100 Hz (simulation time step of 0.01 s), after which every ten images were averaged to make the final movies at a frame rate of 10 Hz, like the experimental SMI movies. This time averaging introduced motion blur for the faster-moving molecules, mimicking the experimental images. With this, the effective Gaussian standard deviation of simulated particles, stemming from both the PSF and motion blur, was 1.58 pixels (= 128 nm), equal to that measured in the experimental SMI movies.

The effective Gaussian standard deviation in both the experimental and synthetic SMI data was determined by fitting a Gaussian with variable standard deviation to isolated particles in the experimental and synthetic SM images, respectively, using the Gaussian mixture-model fitting algorithm in u-track52 (https://github.com/DanuserLab/u-track).

Fluorophore intensity fluctuations

To capture fluorophore intensity fluctuations between frames, the amplitude of the Gaussian per molecule was simulated to follow a normal distribution with a specified mean and standard deviation. The mean of the single-fluorophore intensity distribution was related to the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), defined next. Given a particular mean, the standard deviation of the single-fluorophore intensity distribution was then defined as “coefficient of variation” × the mean, where the coefficient of variation was taken as 0.25, mimicking the single-fluorophore intensity fluctuations in the experimental SMI data (Figure S2G).

The single-fluorophore intensity coefficient of variation in the SMI data (0.25) was obtained from the intensity traces of particles deemed to represent single fluorophores. It was calculated per single-fluorophore trace by taking its intensity standard deviation over its lifetime and dividing it by its mean intensity over its lifetime. Traces were deemed to represent single fluorophores if they satisfied the following criteria: (1) trace disappeared before the end of the movie, (2) trace had 0 photobleaching steps, (3) trace had a mean intensity less than the mean photobleaching step size (Note S1 and Figure S2B), and (4) trace lifetime was at least 10 time points. Note that the condition of 0 photobleaching steps acted as a safeguard against the ambiguity of mapping intensity to number of fluorophores (because of the wide range of single-fluorophore intensities [Note S1 and Figure S2]), thus increasing our confidence in the selection of single-fluorophore traces. Analysis of the single-fluorophore traces yielded an average single-fluorophore intensity coefficient of variation of 0.24 for FLR2 and 0.22 for ECTM (Figure S2G).

SNR

Underneath the placed molecules, the image background was simulated as white noise, following a normal distribution with a specified mean and standard deviation. The mean amplitude of the Gaussian per molecule (representing a single fluorophore), together with the image background mean and standard deviation, were chosen such that the SNR in the final movies (at 10 Hz) was ∼5.5, similar to that of the experimental SMI data.

The SNR of experimental data (per movie) was calculated as follows. First, the intensity of the detected particles at their center position was taken. Second, background intensity was taken as the intensity of all pixels in the ROI outside of a 4-pixel radius around each detected particle. The SNR for each movie was then calculated as (particle intensity − mean background intensity)/(standard deviation of background intensity). The average SNR for all the movies was 5.5 for ECTM and 6.8 for FLR2. The lower SNR value was chosen when creating simulations.

Results and discussion

SM analysis yields the apparent interaction kinetics for the labeled subset of VEGFR2 on the surface of CHO cells

To capture homotypic interaction events at the SM level for wild-type, full-length VEGFR2 (FLR2), or its truncated mutant with reduced dimerization (ECTM), we transiently expressed Halo-FLR2 or ECTM-Halo in CHO cells (Figure 1A). FLR2 fused to HaloTag at its C terminus (like ECTM) could not express on the cell surface, so we employed FLR2 fused to HaloTag at its N terminus. CHO cells provided a simplified cellular environment for our study: (1) they lack endogenous VEGFR2,49 thus limiting VEGFR2 expression (whether FLR2 or ECTM) to that introduced exogenously; and (2) they most likely lack many VEC-specific VEGFR2 interaction partners, e.g., neuropilin-1,57 thus reducing the complexity of VEGFR2 behavior on the cell surface and allowing us to use a simpler model of VEGFR2 diffusion and homotypic interactions (see next section). To minimize overexpression artifacts, both constructs were under a truncated CMV promoter, yielding expression levels similar to endogenous VEGFR2 in VECs (1–10 molecules/μm258). In addition to minimizing overexpression artifacts, lower expression levels were advantageous for FISIK, as they enabled labeling a higher fraction of receptors without undermining detection and tracking, thus capturing a higher fraction of SM interaction events. Additionally, we verified that the Halo tag on FLR2 did not interfere with VEGFR2 activation by its ligand VEGF. For this, we measured VEGFR2 phosphorylation upon treatment with VEGF (2 nM for 5 min) in CHO cells expressing Halo-FLR2, which we found to be comparable with endogenous VEGFR2 phosphorylation in VECs in response to the same VEGF treatment (Figure S1).

After labeling Halo-FLR2 or ECTM-Halo with JF549-conjugated Halo ligand to achieve SM-level labeling, we imaged the transfected and labeled cells using total internal reflection fluorescence microscopy (TIRFM) to focus on receptors on the cell surface (Figure 1B; Videos S1 and S2). Labeling with cell-impermeable ligand/dye would have further ensured focusing on receptors on the cell surface,34,59,60 but as this was possible for only Halo-FLR2 (extracellular Halo tag) and not for ECTM-Halo (intracellular Halo tag), for consistency we used the cell-permeable JF549 Halo ligand for both constructs. For labeling at the SM level, we balanced the need for sparser labeling for more accurate tracking with the need for denser labeling to capture receptor interactions.35,36,42,52 We acquired 10-Hz movies for 300 frames (30 s).

As a first step toward calculating the interaction kinetics of FLR2 or ECTM, we calculated the apparent interaction kinetics of the labeled subset of receptors. These kinetics are referred to as apparent for two reasons: (1) the resolution limit, which creates “artifactual interactions,” and (2) the subsampling inherent to SMI data, which renders many interactions invisible.

First, we determined the apparent oligomeric state of each detected particle.42 Because we observed substantial variation in individual fluorophore intensity in our images, leading to high overlap in the intensity distributions of different apparent oligomeric states (Note S1 and Figure S2), we took a conservative approach in the assignment of apparent oligomeric states. Specifically, we assumed that a particle was an apparent monomer unless merging and/or splitting events dictated otherwise.42 Note that capturing merging and splitting events during tracking nevertheless followed strict local intensity constraints, where the intensity of the joint particle after a merge or before a split had to be approximately the sum of the intensities of the two merging or splitting particles, respectively.52 For the remainder of this work, a detected particle (which could appear as monomeric, dimeric, and so forth) will be referred to as a “receptor complex” (even if it is monomeric).

We found that the labeled subset of both FLR2 and ECTM appeared mostly as monomeric or dimeric, with very small fractions of FLR2 appearing as trimeric or tetrameric (Figure 1E). Both imaged molecules had similar surface densities (Figures 1F and 1G; see Equation S10 in Note S2 for calculation of receptor density from receptor complex density), indicating that apparent oligomeric state differences between FLR2 and ECTM reflected differences in their underlying interaction kinetics. Note that the apparent oligomeric state densities and fractions were obtained by averaging over all time points of a movie (see materials and methods), thus incorporating the loss of labeled molecules due to photobleaching throughout the course of an SMI movie (the number of particles by the end of a 300-frame movie was 59% ± 13% of the number of particles at the beginning of the movie). Importantly, although subtle, the difference in the apparent oligomeric state fractions between FLR2 and ECTM was consistent with ECTM undergoing less dimerization than FLR2. Given the relatively small fraction of receptors that were labeled in our SMI experiments (10%–15%, as estimated in later sections), this small difference in the apparent oligomeric state fractions of the labeled subset reflected a large difference at the full-population level (Note S2 and Figure S3). To give a concrete example, with 10% labeling, even if all molecules existed as dimers, the chance of both subunits of a dimer to be labeled was only 1%, resulting in 95% of the detected particles appearing as monomers (Figure S3A).

Second, we used the merging and splitting events of each labeled receptor complex to calculate the apparent dissociation rate constants of the different apparent oligomeric states.42 Only apparent dimers had enough merging and splitting instances to allow this calculation (Figures 1H and S4). Third, combining the apparent dimer dissociation rate constant with the density of apparent monomers and apparent dimers, we then calculated the apparent dimer association rate constant (Figure 1I).42 The differences between Halo-FLR2 and ECTM-Halo, namely ECTM having a lower apparent association rate constant and a higher apparent dissociation rate constant, were again consistent with ECTM undergoing less dimerization than FLR2.

The apparent oligomeric state densities and apparent interaction kinetics for the labeled subsets of FLR2 and ECTM constituted our summary (or intermediate) statistics describing the SMI data to be used for comparing SMI data with simulated data to infer the full-population homotypic interaction kinetics from the labeled subset.

Inference of the interaction kinetics of the full population of VEGFR2 requires a well-constrained stochastic mathematical model and a scheme for generating synthetic SMI data

The core of the FISIK approach is to generate synthetic data that mimic SMI data—both the receptor system and the artifacts introduced via data acquisition—and then to compare the synthetic data with experimental SMI data while tuning the parameters of the mathematical model describing the receptor system until the two match (Figure S5, blocks IV and V).42 The main tenet is that, by incorporating experimental data-acquisition artifacts into the synthetic data, the artifacts would be factored out when matching synthetic and experimental data (because they would be equally present in both), allowing us to extract the kinetic parameters of the true underlying receptor system.42,43,44,45

Generating synthetic data equivalent to SMI data was a multi-step process (Figure S6). First, we used particle-based reaction diffusion simulations to generate trajectories of the full population of diffusing and interacting receptors from a stochastic mathematical model (described next).42,61,62 Second, we introduced two layers of artifacts. The first layer was substoichiometric labeling, inherent to SMI experiments, leading to an underestimation of interactions in the simulated data, as in the experimental data.42 The second layer (a new layer for FISIK42) involved making synthetic movies out of the simulated system,43,44,45 most importantly replacing each labeled receptor with a PSF (approximated as a Gaussian with center = receptor position and standard deviation = 122 nm; see materials and methods), thus mimicking the limited spatial resolution inherent to light microscopy (Video S5). Adding this limited spatial resolution was important to introduce an overestimation of interactions in the simulated data, as in the experimental data. The synthetic movies also had fluorophore intensity, noise, and motion-blur properties similar to SMI movies (all derived directly from the SMI movies; see materials and methods). The synthetic movies were then detected, tracked, and refined in the same manner as SMI movies (Figure S6 and Video S5), thus introducing potential detection and/or tracking errors into the synthetic data, further increasing their equivalence to the experimental SMI data.

The mathematical model mimicking VEGFR2 diffusion and interactions on the cell surface was an extension of that presented in de Oliveira and Jaqaman.42 In brief, receptors with density ρ diffused in a 2D plane, where they were initially placed randomly. Each receptor (and, once interactions occur, receptor complex) had a diffusion coefficient D randomly sampled from the diffusion coefficient distributions observed experimentally for FLR2 or ECTM (Figure 1J) (in de Oliveira and Jaqaman,42 all receptors and receptor complexes in a simulation had the same diffusion coefficient). The receptors could associate with each other (one at a time) reversibly to form dimers, trimers, and so forth, up to a maximum allowable oligomeric state N (thus, oligomeric state n = 1, 2, 3, …, N). Interaction kinetics were controlled by association probabilities pa(n) and dissociation rate constants koff(n), n = 2, 3, …, N. The parameters pa(n) and koff(n) could be different for different n. The association probability pa(n) reflected the propensity/readiness of molecules to interact and form a complex once they were in each other’s proximity (within 10 nm of each other).42 pa(n) = 1 implies that a system is diffusion limited, with every molecular encounter leading to complex formation. The smaller the pa(n), the higher the barrier for complex formation, e.g., because of the need for a conformational change to allow the interaction to take place. For molecules with diffusion coefficient D and association probabilities pa(n), the association rate constants kon(n) (not input, but can be calculated from the simulation output; see materials and methods) are approximately linearly proportional to the product of D and pa(n).42 To mimic substoichiometric labeling (layer 1 of artifacts), a fraction pl of receptors was then labeled such that their trajectories and interactions were visible, while the rest remained invisible. This simple model of diffusion and interactions did not account for regulatory factors, such as the potential coexistence of VEGFR2 molecules in plasma membrane domains (e.g., lipid rafts, caveolae, or clathrin-coated pits).63,64,65 Nevertheless, it was sufficient to capture the essence of VEGFR2 behavior on the cell surface and allow the estimation of the interaction kinetics parameters of interest.

Among the aforementioned parameters, the maximum oligomeric state, association probability, and dissociation rate constant were the interaction kinetics parameters of interest. To maximize the accuracy of their determination through model fitting to the SMI data,42 it was important to constrain the model by determining the remaining model parameters through orthogonal/independent experiments and analyses. For this, we pursued the following strategy. The diffusion coefficient distribution was estimated directly from the SMI data (Figures 1J and S5, block 1; materials and methods). The receptor density and labeled fraction were derived through complementary experiments and analyses (Figure S5, blocks II and III) described in the following two sections (Figures 2 and 3). Finally, for the maximum oligomeric state, we applied Occam’s razor and selected the model with the smallest maximum oligomeric state necessary to match the SMI data. All in all, this strategy highly constrained the model and increased the accuracy of determining the maximum oligomeric state, association probability, and dissociation rate constant for VEGFR2 on the cell surface through model calibration with SMI data.

Independent dual-labeling analysis yields the surface density and labeled fraction of receptor complexes

To reduce the number of unknown model parameters and constrain the model when fitting it to SMI data, we sought to determine VEGFR2 (FLR2 or ECTM) receptor density and labeled fraction through complementary experiments and analyses. They were then fixed in the model, leaving only the interaction kinetics parameters of interest as unknown. This was a two-step process. First, using independent dual-labeling analysis, we determined the density of receptor complexes on the cell surface and the fraction of receptor complexes that were labeled in the SMI experiments (this section). Using analytical mapping between the full population and labeled subset of receptors and receptor complexes (Note S2), we then mapped from density and labeled fraction of receptor complexes to those of receptors (next section).

To determine the density and labeled fraction of receptor complexes on the cell surface, we labeled Halo-FLR2 or ECTM-Halo with JF549-conjugated Halo ligand as in the SMI experiments and then, after cell fixation, we labeled them also with antibody targeting the extracellular domain of VEGFR2 (FLR2 or ECTM) (Figure 2A). As the antibody was added after fixation, it was not expected to alter the distribution of VEGFR2 complexes, at least as visible by light microscopy (e.g., by artifactual crosslinking of molecules in different complexes). Inverting Equations 4 and 5 to express the unknowns pl and fc,nfull(n=1,2,…,N) as functions of the knowns ql and fc,nlab was not feasible. Instead, first we employed forward modeling to map combinations of pl and fc,nfull into ql and fc,nlab(n=1,2,…,N), after which we matched the mapped ql and fc,nlab to their experimentally measured counterparts (Figures 1E and 2E, respectively) in order to infer the underlying pl and fc,nfull. As with the stochastic model of VEGFR2 diffusion and interactions, a critical step for the matching here was to determine the maximum oligomeric state of the system, N. Here, too, we employed Occam’s razor and selected the smallest N necessary for matching. While N = 2 was sufficient to match the ECTM data, N = 2 could not match the FLR2 data. N = 3 was able to match both (Figure 3A), and thus we selected the N = 3 mapping for this analysis for both FLR2 and ECTM. Note that this did not imply that the receptors formed trimers; at this stage, we did not yet factor out the effect of the resolution limit on the SMI data. Nevertheless, to estimate the full-population density of receptors and their labeled fraction, it was necessary to use an N = 3 mapping.

From the N = 3 mapping and matching, we retrieved the fractions of the different oligomeric states in the full population of receptor complexes fc,nfull(n=1,2,3) for FLR2 and ECTM (Figure 3B). The solution spaces were a distribution of values for each fraction, because there were multiple combinations of pl and fc,nfull that led to similar ql and fc,nlab. Nevertheless, there was a clear separation between FLR2 and ECTM, with ECTM having a higher fraction of complexes in the monomeric state, while FLR2 had higher fractions of complexes in the dimeric and trimeric states. Importantly, using Equation 6 to combine these oligomeric state fractions for the full population of complexes with the average density of the full population of complexes determined from the line fit above (Figure 2D) yielded the density of the full population of receptors ρrfull for FLR2 and ECTM (Figure 3C). The density distributions stemmed from the distributions of oligomeric state fractions.

For the fraction of receptors (FLR2 or ECTM) labeled in the SMI data, instead of taking its distribution directly from the solution space above, we calculated it on a per-cell basis. Specifically, we divided the density of the labeled subset of receptors per cell (Figure 1G) by the average density of the full population of receptors (Figure 3C), thus obtaining the labeled fraction of receptors per cell for each of FLR2 and ECTM (Figure 3D). Not surprisingly, the labeled fraction of receptors (∼0.06 and ∼0.09 for FLR2 and ECTM, respectively) was smaller than the labeled fraction of receptor complexes (∼0.12) (Figure S3B illustrates this trend using a monomer-dimer system as an example). Moreover, the difference between the labeled fraction of receptors and the labeled fraction of complexes was larger for FLR2 than for ECTM, reflecting the overall higher tendency of FLR2 to form complexes, while ECTM was overall more in the monomeric state.

The difference in the labeled fraction of receptors between FLR2 and ECTM (∼0.06 vs. ∼0.09) was also overall consistent with the concentrations of JF549 used for labeling them (6 nM for FLR2 vs. 12 nM for ECTM). Our original guide in choosing the concentration of JF549 was the density of labeled receptor complexes (i.e., detected particles) per cell, which could be calculated directly from the images without any analysis other than particle detection and cell mask delineation. Our choice of 6 nM for FLR2 and 12 nM for ECTM led to very similar labeled receptor complex densities (Figure 1F). We originally attributed the need for different JF549 concentrations between the two molecules to the extracellular and intracellular location of the HaloTag in, respectively, Halo-FLR2 and ECTM-Halo. This might still be part of the reason, but this analysis shows that there were other reasons as well, namely the higher density of FLR2 on the cell surface (Figure 3C) and the higher tendency of FLR2 to form complexes (Figure 3B).

Regardless of the differences between FLR2 and ECTM, this analysis yielded distributions for the density of the full population of receptors and the fraction of receptors labeled in SMI experiments for the two molecules, which could be used for the mathematical model within FISIK.

Dimerization model is sufficient to yield model-generated SMI data that mimic experimental VEGFR2 SMI data

With the above, we secured all parameters needed to model VEGFR2 diffusion and interactions on the cell surface (total density, labeled fraction, diffusion coefficient distribution, and image generation parameters) except for the unknown interaction parameters of interest, namely the maximum oligomeric state, the association probability per oligomeric state, and the dissociation rate constant per oligomeric state. These interaction parameters had to be determined by fitting the model to our SMI data via simulation-based inference.42,66,67,68,69,70 The maximum oligomeric state dictated the number of association probability and dissociation rate constant parameters to be determined (one each per oligomeric state >1). Thus, we set the maximum oligomeric state in the model and then determined the relevant parameters. Our approach here was to again apply Occam’s razor, i.e., start with the simplest model and increase the maximum oligomeric state one at a time, then choose the model with the smallest maximum oligomeric state necessary for matching.

We started with a dimerization model. For a dimerization model, there were only two unknown model parameters, the dimer association probability and the dimer dissociation rate constant. The simpler model of no interactions was a special case of the dimerization model, with association probability set to zero. We generated simulated data and then synthetic images and movies from the simulated data (see materials and methods, Figure S6, and Video S5) using various combinations of dimer association probability (between 0.001 and 0.1) and dimer dissociation rate constant (between 0.01/s and 0.2/s), in addition to simulated data of no interactions (association probability = 0). Thus, we had a total of 51 parameter combinations, for each of which we generated 200 synthetic movies of duration 300 frames at 10 Hz, similar to our SMI data. We had one set of such simulations for FLR2 and one set for ECTM. The two molecules required separate simulations because of their different diffusion coefficient distributions, receptor densities, and labeled fractions.

We then detected and tracked the synthetic movies and analyzed their apparent oligomeric states and apparent interaction kinetics just as with SMI data (Figures S7–S10). Of note, even though these simulations contained only true monomers and dimers, they produced particles that appeared as trimers or tetramers (Figures S8 and S10), as observed in the experimental data (Figure 1E). This is because the resolution limit of light microscopy was mimicked in our synthetic image generation.

To compare the synthetic data with experimental data quantitatively, we used the Mahalanobis distance between their summary statistics (de Oliveira and Jaqaman42; materials and methods; Equation 1). The Mahalanobis distance is a multi-variate effect size measure, quantifying the difference between synthetic and experimental data while considering data variability and correlations.71 The summary statistics used in the Mahalanobis distance calculation consisted of the densities of apparent monomers, dimers, trimers, and tetramers in the system, in addition to the apparent dimer dissociation rate constant. The apparent dimer association rate constant was not used in the Mahalanobis distance, as it was redundant with the other statistics.42 As with the experimental data (Figure 1), the number of apparent trimers and tetramers in the simulated data was too small to calculate any apparent association or dissociation rate constants for those apparent oligomeric states, and so we did not use them in the comparison. The apparent trimers and tetramers did, however, factor into the Mahalanobis distance in terms of their density.

The Mahalanobis distance yielded lowest-distance matches for both ECTM and FLR2 (Figure 4), with simulation summary statistics similar to those measured experimentally (Figures S7–S10). This implied that a dimerization model was sufficient to describe the homotypic interactions of FLR2 and of ECTM on the cell surface, at least at the expression levels in our system, which was consistent with previous studies.18,72 Note that a dimerization model was also necessary, as the simulated data of no interactions had the largest Mahalanobis distance from the FLR2 experimental data and also a relatively large distance from the ECTM experimental data. Interestingly, FLR2 was further away from the no-interactions model than ECTM, again consistent with FLR2 undergoing more interactions than ECTM.

Inferred full-population interaction kinetics reveal that reduced dimerization of ECTM is primarily due to its lower association rate constant compared to FLR2

The Mahalanobis distance yielded lowest-distance matches for FLR2 and ECTM at very different model parameters (Figure 4). For FLR2 (Figure 4A), the matches tended to be at higher association probabilities pa, mostly ≥0.02, while for ECTM (Figure 4B) the matching association probabilities were 0.002–0.003, i.e., an order of magnitude smaller than for FLR2. The range of dissociation rate constants koff for the two molecules was overlapping and relatively broad. These results indicated a clear difference between ECTM and FLR2 in their propensity to dimerize, but differences in the dissociation rate, i.e., the stability of the dimer once it was formed, were less clear. The difference in the propensity to dimerize could arise because without the intracellular domain, the extracellular and transmembrane domains were not in a “dimerization-ready” (or dimerization-compatible) conformation, thus requiring a larger conformational change for ECTM molecules to dimerize.

To investigate further the differences between FLR2 and ECTM and to represent the association of VEGFR2 interactions in terms of an association rate constant kon instead of an association probability pa (note the approximately linear relationship between kon and the combination of pa and the diffusion coefficient D, discussed above at the mathematical model introduction), we used the calibrated models to generate simulated data at 100% labeling, i.e., the full population. We then calculated the interaction kinetics of the full system directly from the simulations without generating images and introducing experimental artifacts. The calibrated model for each molecule (FLR2 or ECTM) was taken as the set of best matching models, specifically those with Mahalanobis distance <700. 700 was used as the threshold to yield the closest possible matches for each molecule without including too many peripheral matches. This yielded seven matches for FLR2 (Figure 4A, cyan entries) and two matches for ECTM (Figure 4B, orange entries). These simulations were at 100 Hz and ran for 120 s to reduce discretization artifacts and capture the full spectrum of interaction durations (short-lived and long-lived).

These full-population, artifact-free simulations identified significant differences between FLR2 and ECTM, consistent with ECTM undergoing much less dimerization than FLR2 (Figures 5A–5D). First, a much higher fraction of FLR2 than ECTM was dimeric (Figure 5A; 12%–60% for FLR2 vs. <5% for ECTM). The fraction of dimeric FLR2 was consistent with previous studies.49 Second, as suspected, the inferred dimer association rate constant kon was the major difference between ECTM and FLR2 (Figure 5C). Using these simulations and the inferred association and dissociation rate constants that they yielded (Figures 5B and 5C), we also calculated the equilibrium dissociation constant KD of FLR2 and ECTM. Consistent with ECTM undergoing much less dimerization than FLR2, its inferred KD was on average an order of magnitude higher than that of FLR2 (KD = 14 mol/μm2 for ECTM vs. 2.6 mol/μm2 for FLR2) (Figure 5D).

Although the dissociation constant values estimated through our work were consistent with ECTM undergoing much less dimerization than FLR2, they were smaller than those estimated previously.49 This could stem from system differences, as the previous measurements were made in plasma-membrane-derived vesicles, whereas the measurements in this work were made in the intact membrane of live cells. They could also stem from the simplified nature of our model, which only considered reversible dimerization without accounting for any potential coexistence of VEGFR2 molecules in plasma membrane domains, such as lipid rafts, caveolae, or clathrin-coated pits.63,64,65 Performing SMI and applying FISIK in plasma-membrane-derived vesicles would help distinguish between these two possibilities.

Conclusions

In this work we have determined, for the first time to the best of our knowledge, the kinetics of VEGFR2 dimerization (i.e., association and dissociation rate constants) in an intact live cell membrane. This information is critical for achieving a quantitative understanding of VEGFR2 signaling and regulation in space and time. The approaches developed in this work, whether the central approach of SMI combined with stochastic mathematical modeling or the complementary approaches of independent dual labeling and analysis and the analytical mapping between the full populations and labeled subsets of receptors and receptor complexes, are general approaches that may be applied to a range of membrane receptor systems. They are also applicable beyond receptors as long as the molecule(s) of interest can be labeled and imaged at the SM level. By endogenously tagging the molecules of interest for fluorescent labeling (e.g., by CRISPR), one can obtain the interaction kinetics in a native live-cell environment. Endogenous tagging also has the advantage of generally lower expression levels than exogenous expression, thus enabling the labeling of a higher fraction of molecules, leading to further constraining of the model and stronger inference of the interaction kinetics. This is important, as there are few, if any, prior measurements of in situ interaction kinetics for reference; thus, the more controls on the data and constraints on the model, the stronger the inference. All in all, this approach will open avenues for deepening our quantitative understanding and characterization of receptor and other molecular interactions in the cell, which form the basis of cell signaling and cellular functions.
