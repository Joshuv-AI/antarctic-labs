Environmental Identification of Novel Enzymes for Polyurethane and Polyamide Degradation
Authors: Malthe Kjær Bendtsen, Andreas Møllebjerg, Samuel Peña‐Díaz, Rosie Graham, Nicolai Claus Petersen, Bjørk Nolsøe Isaksen, Mathias Carstensen, Martin B Johansen, Andreas Sommerfeldt, Allan R Petersen, Iddi Khamisi Chuma, Cecilie Ryberg, Thomas Rea Wittenborn, Virginia Gichuru, Huabing Wang, Carsten Scavenius, Alexander Sandahl, Daniel E Otzen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBetter enzymes are needed to develop sustainable methods to recycle plastics with C‐X heterobonds such as polyurethane (PUR) and nylon, for which no industrial‐scale solutions exist. Current methods rely largely on sequence mining based on a small number of known enzymes. Here, we expand the pool of PURases and nylonases by bioprospecting legacy plastic waste with fluorophore plastic mimics combined with fluorescence‐assisted cell sorting (FACS). We identify 29 plastic‐degrading bacteria, from which 12 enzymes are identified by mass spectrometry and homology searches. Compared to existing enzymes, these enzymes show higher thermostability and hydrolytic activities against different high‐molecular weight PUR polymers and nylon textiles compared to previously described wildtype enzymes. To our knowledge, this is the first reported example of enzymes capable of hydrolyzing longer chains of untreated PUR and nylon as well as crosslinked PUR. This study significantly increases the number of known PURases and nylonases and provides starting points for optimization campaigns through protein engineering and for in silico discovery.Keywords: biocatalysis, enzymes, nylonase, polymers, urethanase

Introduction

Environmental plastic pollution has emerged, along with anthropogenic climate change, as one of the primary challenges of our time. To address this, efficient and economically competitive methods of recycling are sorely needed. Truly circular recycling requires the plastic to be deconstructed into monomeric building blocks which can be used to synthesize new polymers. Depolymerization by chemical catalysis requires high temperatures and high input purity, as blended plastics or additives can deactivate catalysts or produce toxic byproducts [1, 2, 3, 4]. Enzymes have recently emerged as attractive alternatives, or even partners, to chemical catalysts, due to their high selectivity and milder reaction conditions. While enzymatic recycling of polyethylene terephthalate (PET) waste shows promise at industrial scale [5], enzymatic strategies are less developed for other heteroatomic plastics such as polyamides (nylons) and polyurethane (PUR), although they represent promising targets for degradation. Nylons are predominately the polymers nylon‐6 and nylon‐6,6. PUR encompasses a range of carbamate‐based polymers that include both thermoplastics (linear polymers) and thermosets (crosslinked polymers), often synthesized as copolymers consisting of an aromatic “hard segment” such as methylene diphenyl diisocyanate (MDI) or toluene diisocyanate (TDI) and a “soft segment” such as polyethylene or polypropylene glycol (PEG or PPG) or polycaprolactone (PCL) [6]. This necessitates selective degradation or extensive and costly downstream separation of monomers for recycling.

Most previously identified polyurethanases (PURases) have only been tested against ester‐based polyurethane [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14] or have only documented activity against ethyl carbamate [15], leaving to the best of our knowledge only limited PURases with proven ability to degrade the carbamate in PUR or PUR derived substrates (UMG‐SP(1‐3), pQR3139, pQR3141, pQR3144, TflABH, MthABH, OspAmd and GB(2–9)) [16, 17, 18, 19], many of which are close homologs. They vary greatly in catalytic efficiency, and only UMG‐SP1 has been shown to degrade thermoplastic ether‐based PUR [20], instead they have been shown to degrade glycosylate products. Additionally, their selectivities toward different formulations of PUR are largely undescribed. Likewise, many enzymes labelled as nylonases have only been tested on soluble amide compounds [21, 22, 23], or biodegradable polyamides [24, 25], while those tested on recalcitrant nylon‐6 or nylon‐6,6 show minimal activity. To date, the most active enzymes degrading nylon belong to the N‐terminal nucleophile (Ntn) hydrolase family, which includes NylC. Yet, the reported activity on actual polymers is <1% likely due to substrate limitations that complicates the access to cleavable bonds [26, 27]. Hence, discovery and characterization of true PURases and nylonases are needed from which to optimize practical enzymatic recycling of PUR and nylon.

Existing strategies to discover new plastic‐degrading enzymes include the repurposing of enzymes with activity against similar compounds [12, 15] or bioinformatic mining of protein databases for sequence‐ or structure‐based homologues [19]. Expansion to other regions of sequence space requires the discovery of novel non‐homologous enzymes by functional screening, either by selective enrichment of microorganisms on plastic substrates (ability to metabolize plastic or bioinformatic mining of protein databases for sequence‐ or structure‐based homologues [19]. Homology searches, however, only explore local sequence space, which limits options. Expansion to other regions of sequence space requires the discovery of novel non‐homologous enzymes by functional screening, either by selective enrichment of microorganisms on plastic substrates (ability to metabolize plastic [9, 11, 13, 24, 25]) or direct activity‐based measurements (ability to cleave plastic bonds) [10, 16]. Fluorescent probes are particularly useful, as they couple cleavage of the bond of interest to a fluorescent shift, allowing for highly sensitive detection of enzymatic activity. They have been used to discover enzymes such as hydrolases, lipases, nitroreductases, and even PETases [28, 29, 30]. Utilizing fluorescent probes for an activity‐based screening allows isolation of enzymes from environmental samples, even those found in low abundance and with modest activity.

We present a functional screening platform by which we discover twelve novel plastic‐degrading enzymes, obtained from environmental microbiomes and exhibiting high structural and sequence diversity. Fluorescent probes targeting PURases and nylonases were used to isolate bacteria expressing these enzymes via fluorescence‐assisted cell sorting (FACS). The corresponding enzymes were identified through sequential purification from the host bacteria, tracking their activity with the same probes and finally identifying them by mass spectrometry. The enzymes show diverse activity profiles across PUR and nylon, with superior thermostability compared to previously described wildtype PURases and comparable to wildtype nylonases (melting temperature T m up to 68.3 ± 0.1°C) and hydrolysis across more realistic substrates (thermoset PUR foam, synthesized PUR polymers, and nylon textiles) than previously described enzymes.

Results and Discussions

Development of a Rapid Functional Screening Method to Isolate Carbamate‐ and Amide‐Cleaving Bacterial Strains

To expand the enzymatic toolbox for plastic depolymerization, we developed a rapid (>107 bacterial cells/h) functional screening platform to screen bacteria containing putative PURases and nylonases from plastic‐contaminated environments. Microorganisms are first extracted and incubated with fluorescent activity probes designed to mimic the chemical structure of the plastics (Figures 1a, S1, and S2). The PURase probe (PURp1) contains a carbamate bond between a bulky aromatic group and an aliphatic chain, while the nylonase probe (NYLp1) contains an amide bond between two aliphatic chains (Table S1). Enzymatic cleavage of PURp1 exposes the free 4‐amino group, red‐shifting fluorescence emission, while cleavage of NYLp1 releases the quencher and thus increases fluorescence [31]. Both probes are small, neutral, and relatively hydrophobic, allowing passive diffusion into microorganisms [32]. The cleaved PURp1 can also diffuse into the bacteria (Figure S1a). The fluorophore released by cleavage of NYLp1 is positively charged and cannot diffuse freely (Figure S2), thus trapping intracellularly cleaved NYLp1 inside the cell while extracellularly cleaved probe may be electrostatically attracted to the predominantly anionic cell membrane. This allows both probes to stain bacteria with different expression patterns. To verify that cleaved PURp1 did not diffuse into neighboring cells, we diluted Escherichia coli expressing urethanase UMG‐SP2 [16] 100‐fold into a background SP2‐free E. coli population. Gratifyingly, 100% of the colonies from FACS sorting expressed UMG‐SP2 (Figure S1c), indicating that we could enrich for an enzyme‐expressing subpopulation. Microorganisms with increased fluorescence are isolated by FACS at rates of up to 10 000 events per second (Figures 1a and S1b–d), without the need for extensive sample preparation and infrastructure required by other screening methods, like FADS [28]. While FACS has previously been used to engineer amidases and urethanases [33], our study demonstrates that we can isolate these enzymes directly from environmental samples.

To isolate microorganisms with plastic‐depolymerizing enzymes, we collected plastic‐waste samples (composition confirmed by ATR‐FTIR (Figure S3) from humid and warm environments favoring bacterial growth and enzymatic thermal stability, exemplified by Kenyan landfills, a tropical zoo, PUR enriched compost [34, 35], and the guts of wax‐worm reared on PUR foam [36]. From the landfill, FACS isolated eight PURase‐positive species from polyurethane foam and wire and five nylonase‐positive species from nylon nets. From the polyurethane in the tropical zoo, five PURase‐positive species were isolated. From the compost, four PURases‐positive species were isolated from the polyurethane foam and three from the elastane, while three nylonase‐positive species were isolated from nylon fibers. From the wax‐worm guts, one PURase‐positive species was isolated. FACS scatterplots indicated significant subpopulations of bacteria with plastic‐degrading enzymes (Figures S1d and S2d). No bacterial species from polyethylene or polypropylene samples were consistently active against our probes.

In total, 21 PURase‐positive and 8 nylonase‐positive bacterial species were isolated, with relative activities spanning a >10 000‐fold range (Figure S4), highlighting the platform's high sensitivity and broad dynamic range. The isolates were taxonomically diverse, comprising species from Actinomycetia, α‐proteobacteria, γ‐proteobacteria, and Bacilli. Despite this diversity, Actinobacteria were overrepresented for both plastic types. This may reflect the phylum's broad enzymatic diversity, as indicated by their disproportionate contribution to degradation of xenobiotics [37, 38], as well as their high representation in the CAZy and PAZy databases [39, 40]

Although the platform preferentially enriches for cell‐associated enzymes, of the 29 isolates, two nylonase‐positive species secreted all their enzyme activity (Figure S4, blue diamonds). Detection of these species is probably facilitated by electrostatic attraction between the released fluorophore and the bacterial membrane.

Identification and Characterization of Putative PURases and Nylonases

To identify active enzymes from these different species, the novel isolates were grown and lysed to extract the soluble fraction, followed by different combinations of non‐denaturing liquid chromatography including anion exchange (AEX), size exclusion (SEC) and/or hydrophobic interaction (HIC), while monitoring PURase (PURp1) or nylonase (NYLp1) activity of individual fractions using fluorescent probes (Figures 1b and S5). After iterative purification of the most active fractions, the potentially active enzymes were isolated (Table S2), fractions were digested, and proteins identified by mass spectrometry. Additionally, native‐PAGE zymography aided the isolation of potentially active enzymes in Alloalcanivorax xenomutans and Chelatococcus sp. (Figures 1b and S5; Table S2).

We identified several enzymes active on the PURase probe (sequences provided in Table S1). CCPUR1 and CCPUR2, from two different Chelatococci species, showed 89% sequence identity to each other but only 38% identity to the published PURase UMG‐SP1 [16]. BLPUR4 from Bacillus licheniformis is a predicted p‐nitrobenzyl esterase, a homolog of an esterase from B. subtilis identified as a PETase [41]. AXPUR5 from Alloalcanivorax xenomutans has 36% identity to TfCa PETase from Thermobifida fusca. The actinobacterial enzymes AMPUR6 from Aeromicrobium massiliense and CCPUR7 from Cellulosimicrobium cellulans were predicted as an acylamidase and a 6‐aminohexanoate‐cyclic‐dimer hydrolase, respectively. In addition, we identified a metal‐dependent hydrolase from Mammaliicoccus sciuri, but the activity of the recombinant enzyme was too poor to merit further study on this probe.

As regards nylon degradation, MS‐based screening identified a predicted (R)‐stereoselective amidase from Metapseudomonas sp., which however turned out not to have detectable activity in the recombinant form. We were unable to identify nylonases from isolates of the remaining seven nylonase‐positive species. Instead, we used their genomes to perform a computational homology screening that comprises: (i) the construction of an enzymatic library containing amidases, proteases, peptidases, and hydrolases from our bacterial genomes; (ii) sequence homology search among this library using plastic‐degrading enzymes from this study and the literature; (iii) structural comparisons employing AlphaFold‐derived models (Figures 1b and S6). In Paracoccus yeii, this identified PYNYL1, originally described as a 6‐aminohexanoate‐cyclic‐dimer hydrolase, which had limited identity with NylA (37.3%) and CCPUR1 (32.7%). Likewise, from the isolated Mycolicibacterium phlei genome, we identified MPPUR3, with 30.6% identify to AXPUR5 (Figure S7). Our success prompted a similar approach in a published PUR‐derived metagenome [42], leading to two pseudogenes coding for active enzymes, PMGPUR8 and PMGPUR9 with 42% and 36.6% identity, respectively, to CCPUR1.

In total, this approach has now identified 10 putative PURases and 2 nylonases. By combining functional screening with homology search, we discovered a diverse set of enzymes that were unlikely to have been identified by homology search alone, due to the low sequence similarity to previously published PURases and nylonases (Figure S7). While the discovered enzymes belong to the same IPR families, as previously described enzymes (Table 1), this relationship is a poor predictor of PURase and nylonase activities. The FACS‐based screen selects directly for bond‐cleaving activity, bypassing the assumption of sequence‐to‐function transferability that underlies homology searches. This is illustrated by the discovery of esterase‐type PURases in our screen (MPPUR3, BLPUR4, AMPUR6)—a subfamily not previously described as PUR‐active at the time of our discovery, but subsequently independently reported by other groups [17, 18], validating both their existence and our platform's ability to identify them independent of prior knowledge. The key limitation is the restriction to the culturable fraction of the microbiome, although this could be improved by using different media compositions. The workflow could be streamlined by coupling sorting to single‐cell sequencing, although identifying the active enzyme would become more difficult without prior purification. After chromatographic purification of the active enzyme fractions from all 29 species, activity could only be demonstrated for six enzymes identified from mass spectrometry and expressed recombinantly. This low hit rate reflects the high number of enzyme candidates after three to four purification steps, incomplete or misleading functional annotation and bias during candidate selection. It may also reflect false negatives due to poor expression in E. coli of a large proportion of the Actinobacterial enzymes, which often require different folding environments. The hit rate of the workflow could likely be increased by including expression in an Actinobacterial host [43]. The hit rate of nylonases may have suffered further from false positives due to enzymatic deactivation of the NYLp1 quencher by reductases. However, in contrast to functional metagenomics, our method does not require laborious library construction and avoids the bottleneck of heterologous expression from environmental DNA with different codon usage and promoter systems.

Using PURp1 for activity measurements, all enzymes except MPPUR3 showed alkaline preference with activity optima around pH 8–11 (Figure S9). Optimum profiles were highly similar for enzymes within the same family. AXPUR5 and BLPUR4 showed a wide pH tolerance with >50% activity from pH 5 to 9 and optimum at pH 8.5. All amidases retained high activity at pH 8–9.5, some such as CCPUR2 showed a broader pH range. The enzymes showed a range of thermostabilities as measured by Differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF) with PYNYL1 least stable (t m: 43.2 ± 0.1°C) and the most stable being CCPUR1 (T m: 68.3 ± 0.1°C), CCPUR7 (T m: 64 ± 0.2°C), and CCPUR2 (T m: 58.3 ± 0.1°C) (Figure S9 and Table 1), making them significantly more thermostable than published enzymes such as UMG‐SP(1–3) [16]. By finding scaffolds with high native wildtype T m, this allows for catalysis at higher temperatures, and potential for further enhancement of thermostability as has been exemplified for NylC that went from 71°C to 87°C NylCK‐TS [26]. To test long‐term stability, we incubated the enzymes at 35°C and measured residual activity at different time points using the PURp1, a probe for which most of the enzymes exhibited catalytic properties. Most enzymes retained significant activity (Figure S10), with the primary exception of PYNYL1 which lost around 75% of its original activity in 5 h and was completely inactive after 1 day (Figure S10). At 45°C, CCPUR1 and CCPUR7 retained most of their catalytic capacity over 5 days, whereas CCPUR2 and PMGPUR8 lost 75% of activity over that same period (Figure S10). PMGPUR9 and UMG‐SP2 lost most of their activity after 5 h at 45°C, retaining 35.5 ± 18.5% and 7.5 ± 3.5%, respectively (Figure S10).

As a first step to elucidate the enzymes’ ability to hydrolyze a substrate more representative of PUR, we synthesized two soluble dicarbamate compounds, namely diurethane ethylene 4,4’‐methylenedianiline (DUE‐MDA) [44] and the novel diurethane ethylene 2,4‐toluenediamine (DUE‐TDA) (Figure 2a,b, Table S1, and Supporting Information), representing MDI‐ and TDI‐based PUR, respectively, which mimic the expected products from PUR glycolysis in a possible chemo‐enzymatic process [16, 19]. These can potentially be degraded to mono‐substituted monourethane ethylene (MUE)‐MDA and MUE‐TDA, and subsequently to 4,4‐MDA and 2,4‐TDA (Figure 2a,b). Enzyme activity was initially measured at 35°C (Figure 2c,d). Strikingly, all ten investigated enzymes degraded DUE‐MDA to at least MUE‐MDA (Figure 2c) confirming identification of enzymes capable of degrading the carbamate bond within soluble PUR oligomers. AXPUR5, AMPUR6, and CCPUR7 released the highest amount of total product yield in 48 h, with AMPUR6 and CCPUR7 showing full conversion of released MUE‐MDA to 4,4‐MDA with up to 40.9 ± 1.2 and 47.1 ± 2.0 µg/mL 4‐4 MDA detected, respectively (Figure 2c). In comparison, one of the previously identified PURases UMG‐SP3 [16] was tested as a modest benchmark enzyme as it represents the least stable and least active variant of the SP series and only released 7.1 ± 4.4 µg/mL 4‐4 MDA. Five enzymes (CCPUR1, CCPUR2, MPPUR3, BLPUR4, and PYNYL1) degrade DUE‐MDA to MUE‐MDA only (Figure 2c), while six (AXPUR5, AMPUR6, CCPUR7, PMGPUR9, PMGPUR8, and UMG‐SP3) process DUE‐MDA via MUE‐MDA to 4,4‐MDA.

Fewer of these enzymes degraded DUE‐TDA, with much lower conversion rates compared to DUE‐MDA (Figure 2d). Five out of the 10 enzymes tested showed release of up to ∼50 µg/mL MUE‐TDA at 35°C after 48 h (Figure 2d). Only two enzymes (CCPUR7 and PMGPUR9) showed detectable release of the 2‐4‐TDA monomer (Figure 2d). As observed with DUE‐MDA, many of the enzymes only degrade to the intermediate MUE‐TDA. PMGPUR9 released the highest detectable amount of 2–4 TDA at 8.2 µg/mL. AMPUR6 and CCPUR7 released 48.2 ± 1.2 and 43.7 ± 5.7 µg/mL MUE‐TDA respectively, significantly more than the previously characterized PURase UMG‐SP3 (6.0 ± 1.8 µg/mL) (Figure 2d). Given the many different compositions of PUR in use, enzymes with selective activities toward different formulations such as TDI‐ and MDI‐based PUR are needed. The different activities on DUE‐TDA and DUE‐MDA are thus useful starting points for evolution of selective PURases.

To evaluate the performance of each candidate PURase over 24 h, we repeated the assay at each enzyme's individual pH and temperature optima (Figures S11 and S12). AMPUR6 was the most productive at converting both DUE‐MDA to 4,4‐MDA and DUE‐TDA to MUE‐TDA, with 55.4 ± 1.7 and 51.3 ± 1.6 µg/mL detected over 24 h. After 1 h, DUE‐MDA had been fully converted to MUE‐MDA; the release of 4,4‐MDA by AMPUR6 at 4 h was significantly higher than for UMG‐SP3 after 24 h (Figure S11). However, AMPUR6 did not release any 2,4‐TDA despite almost full conversion of DUE‐TDA to MUE‐TDA (Figure S12). PMGPUR9 showed activity against both DUE‐MDA and DUE‐TDA. Interestingly, the product profile from DUE‐TDA was dominated by 2,4‐TDA. That is, any MUE‐TDA produced was further hydrolyzed to 2,4‐TDA, making MUE‐TDA a more competent substrate for this enzyme than DUE‐TDA. CCPUR7 and AXPUR5 also showed enhanced conversion of most DUE‐MDA converted after just 4 h (Figure S11). CCPUR7 released up to 34.3 ± 6.1 µg/mL 4,4‐MDA while AXPUR5 only produced 4.0 ± 1.0 µg/mL 4,4‐MDA from MUE‐MDA after 24 h, despite significant formation of MUE‐MDA (Figure S12). Similar profiles with a fast initial conversion to MUE‐MDA were seen in PMGPUR8, CCPUR2, and UMG‐SP3, with conversion slowing down after 8 h (Figure S11) likely due to enzyme instability under these assay conditions, as observed previously on the retained catalytic properties (Figure S10). Interestingly, although most of the enzymes capable of hydrolyzing PURp1 also showed activity on DUE‐MDA, their catalytic efficiencies differed markedly between the two substrates. Notably, AMPUR6 was the most efficient on DUE‐MDA, yet the least efficient on PURp1. This indicates that activity on fluorescent probes does not necessarily correlate with activity on more realistic substrates. As the probes only capture a fraction of the structural features of the real plastics, they are likely to target a subset of the plastic‐degrading enzymes during discovery. Nevertheless, fluorescent probes provide a powerful tool for high‐throughput screening, specifically regarding activity on small, soluble oligomers and intermediates, with subsequent characterization being reserved for more realistic substrates.

Analogous to the DUEs, we designed two nylon‐6,6 insoluble di‐amide mimics with either the amine N,N’‐(hexane‐1,6‐diyl)‐dihexanamide or the carbonyl group (N 1,N 6‐dihexyladipamide) in a central position within the molecule. These substrates dissolved in 100% DMSO at 60°C but were turbid in aqueous suspension. However, 7 days incubation of 2 mM of substrate with 1 µM of CCPUR1, CCPUR2, CCPUR7, PMGPUR9, or PYNYL1 effectively degraded at least one of the nylon mimics (Figure 3b,c) at levels exceeding UMG‐SP3, as demonstrated by the increase of free amines reported by NBD‐Cl (Figure S13) and a decrease of sample turbidity even at room temperature (Figure S14). AXPUR5, AMPUR6, and BLPUR4 showed no activity against these mimics (Figure 3b,c). All active enzymes showed activity levels (Figure 3b,c) that correlated with their thermostability (Table 1). PYNYL1 and, to a lower extent, CCPUR7 differentiated between the mimics, with a release of 1963.3 ± 84.9 and 1100.9 ± 85.9 µM, respectively, of free amine groups from the N‐mimic (Figure 3c) but almost no effect on the CO‐mimic (Figure 3b) at 35°C (93.6 ± 12.7 and 128.8 ± 7.1 µM, respectively). Notably, CCPUR7 activity increased at 45°C, releasing 1761.7 ± 18.7 µM of free amine groups. At this temperature, the activity of the much less thermostable PYNYL1 decreased to a release of 309.0 ± 60.4 µM of free amines. At their optimal temperatures, CCPUR1, CCPUR2, and PMGPUR9 degraded both substrates, releasing 1040.6 ± 88.7, 405.8 ± 23.1, and 1746.1 ± 122.3 µM of free amine groups from the N‐mimic and 1410.3 ± 60.1, 648.7 ± 460.4 and 919.9 ± 120.6 µM from the CO‐mimic, respectively.

Using NBD‐Cl to monitor formation of free amine groups, we found PYNYL1 to be more active than CCPUR1 and CCPUR2 (Figure 3d), releasing 810.4 ± 53.1 µM of free amines from the N‐mimic within 3 h that CCPUR1 (767.1 ± 285.1 µM) and CCPUR2 (485.9 ± 106.5 µM) only achieved after 48 h from the CO‐mimic (Figure 3d) at optimal temperatures. The high promiscuity of these latter proteins might be a functional trade‐off that leads to lower substrate affinity than PYNYL1.

Enzymatic Degradation of Insoluble Polymers

Plastic mimics such as our fluorescent probes and substituted carbamates such as DUE‐TDA and DUE‐MDA are only the first step in confirming plastic‐degrading activity. Substrate design needs careful consideration. Commonly used substrates such as Impranil DLN and other PUR polyesters are more likely to be degraded via the ester bond, both because esters are less xenobiotic than carbamates, and therefore targeted by a broader repertoire of naturally evolved hydrolases, and because esters are intrinsically more susceptible to nucleophilic attack. Furthermore, for substrates containing multiple hydrolysable bonds, the sequence of bond cleavage by a promiscuous enzyme cannot easily be deconvoluted, complicating interpretation of true carbamate‐degrading activity. Therefore, we turned to hydrolysis of soluble oligomers that are chemically identical to the PUR as well as longer insoluble linear polymers of PUR and nylon. Two different insoluble MDA‐based linear polymers were synthesized and confirmed by gel permeation chromatography and DOSY‐NMR to be a low‐MW linear polymer and a high‐MW linear polymer (average molecular weight of ∼20 and ∼50 kDa, respectively), with a distinct shift in retention times between the two linear polymers (Figures S15 and S16a,b). Trace amounts of short fragments >3 kDa were detected by MALDI‐TOF mass spectrometry, but GPC confirms that the majority is made up of larger linear polymers (Figure S15). After incubation of the five most MDA‐ active PURases for 7 days at 35°C with these linear polymers, all enzymes showed increased 4,4‐MDA release on average compared to the control samples with 3–9 replicates tested (Figures 2e and S15e). Prior to incubation, polymer samples were washed with acetonitrile to remove soluble 4,4‐MDA and soluble low‐molecular weight oligomers potentially present from synthesis. Despite this, low levels of 4,4‐MDA were detected in enzyme‐free control samples that were incubated alongside the enzyme treated sample (4.3 ± 3.2 and 1.6 ± 2.5 µg/mL for low‐ and high‐MW polymers, respectively), likely reflecting slow diffusion of residual 4,4‐MDA trapped within the polymer matrix during the 7‐day incubation. Nevertheless, enzyme‐treated samples showed 4,4‐MDA release levels above this baseline. CCPUR1 showed the highest and most significant 4,4‐MDA release on both the insoluble low‐MW and high‐MW linear polymer, with 17.5 ± 5.2 and 10.7 ± 3.7 µg/mL, respectively. AMPUR6 also released 15.4 ± 3.7 µg/mL 4,4‐MDA monomer on the low‐MW linear polymer (Figure S15e). Previous studies have shown degradation of polymeric polyester‐PUR nanoparticles [14], or insoluble heat‐treated PUR films [45]. To our knowledge, this is among the first observations of the degradation of insoluble linear polymers of MDA‐PUR, concurrent with the report by Zheng et al. of activity on thermoplastic polyether‐PU [20]. AXPUR5 and CCPUR7 exhibited a minor qualitative release of 4,4′‐MDA; however, activity did not consistently exceed background levels, potentially due to limited enzyme stability over the course of the assay and replicate values falling below the 3.6 µg/mL detection limit.

To conclude the investigation of these putative PURases on polyurethane substrates, we tested a generic thermoset flexible mattress foam (herein named FlexFoam) with a known recipe [46], consisting of a 4:1 2,4‐:2,6‐TDI and 3000 MW propoxylated glycerol, with our discovered enzymes (Figure 2f). To compare our newly discovered PURases with recent reported PURases, we also include OspAmd [18] and UMG‐SP2 [16, 47]. The latter was previously studied mechanistically [47] and has enhanced stability compared to UMG‐SP3. After incubation with FlexFoam for 3 days, TDA monomers were detected for CCPUR1, CCPUR2, PMGPUR8, PMGPUR9, and UMG‐SP2. This is the first reporting of UMG‐SP2 degradation on an untreated thermoset polyurethane polymer, as previous activity has only been reported on model dicarbamates, polyurethane glycosylate, or thermoplastic PUR [16, 19]. Overall, CCPUR1 demonstrated the highest degradation with 0.92% extent conversion over 3 days, followed by 0.49% by UMG‐SP2 and 0.25%–0.29% for the remaining enzymes tested (Figure 2f).

Finally, we tested the enzymes PYNYL1, CCPUR1, and CCPUR2 on different commercial nylon textile samples. These commercial samples were selected to avoid potential background signal of free amine groups resulting from chemically processed nylon samples, and to reduce the presence of low‐MW oligomeric species formed during polymerization processes. Moreover, to avoid any bias in our data, the results are not shown in terms of molecular weight but as pure release of amine groups. We first started with nylon‐6 and nylon‐6,6 textiles that were manually cut up into smaller bits of filaments (Figure S17). After the incubation for 1 week at each enzyme's optimal temperature, CCPUR1 exhibited the most pronounced degradation capacity, with a release of free amine groups around 661.9 ± 122.6 and 194.7 ± 4.4 µM from nylon‐6 and nylon‐6,6 (Figure 3e), respectively. CCPUR2 also increased the soluble free amine content to 446.4 ± 55.1 and 114.3 ± 7.6 µM from nylon‐6 and nylon‐6,6 (Figure 3e), respectively. The overall higher increase in product formation from nylon‐6 compared to nylon‐6,6 suggests a higher affinity against this substrate. However, we could not discard that nylon‐6 samples present a larger surface exposure that facilitates enzymes’ activity, correlating with previous works suggesting that surface accessibility plays a key role in limiting nylon degradation [26]. To study how surface accessibility affected the activity of the enzymes in our assays and, therefore, potentially bias the discovery of new nylonases, we first tested the effect of the enzymes on cryomilled nylon‐6 (Figure S17). After 1 week of incubation, NBD‐Cl functionalization indicated a significant increase in the soluble free amine content reaching 323.9 ± 46.2 and 227.9 ± 32.8 µM in presence of CCPUR1 and CCPUR2, respectively (Figure S18a). However, during the assay, we noticed that, contrary to the previous nylon‐6 samples, the cryomilled substrate precipitated and reduced the exposed surface. Thus, we repeated the experiment and measured the soluble free amine content after 2 weeks of incubation. As a result, we observed an increased degradation with a free amine release up to 454.9 ± 7.2 µM, in presence of CCPUR1 and 289.5 ± 61.1 µM with CCPUR2 (Figure S18b). Finally, we tested the effect of surface exposure in nylon‐6,6 degradation using multiple unprocessed nylon‐6,6 textiles samples, which differ in their morphology and fiber diameter (Figure S19). When the manually separated samples were incubated for 2 weeks, we detected an increased polymer degradation with a release of free amine groups around 496.9 ± 78.9 µM (CCPUR1) and 211.8 ± 24.5 µM (CCPUR2) (Figure S18b). Nevertheless, when the samples were not previously separated, we observed a significant decrease on the activity, ranging from 194–210 µM for CCPUR1 to 100–146 µM for CCPUR2. Despite its high efficiency on the nylon dimer, PYNYL1 did not show any significant release of free amine from the tested nylon samples (Figures 3e, S18, and S19), likely reflecting its significantly lower stability and un‐folding during the assay. This highlights the potential for increased yields with more thermostable variants in the future. Consistent with this, the most stable enzyme (CCPUR1) is the most effective (Figures 3e, S18, and S19), correlating also with the observed retained activity (Figure S10). Altogether, our results confirmed that substrate accessibility is a highly limiting factor as previously demonstrated [26], but also that only enzymes capable of retaining their activity for long period or resist harsh conditions would display significant activity.

Conclusion

Using a FACS‐based screening platform, we isolated 29 different bacterial species with either carbamate or amide cleavage activity, from which, we identified 8 enzymes by purification from bacterial lysates followed by MS. In addition, we discovered four enzymes using homology searches either within our discovered strains genomes or in published databases. Among our 12 isolated enzymes, 10 were able to cleave DUE‐MDA, 5 of them to the monomer 4,4‐MDA. Six were able to hydrolyze DUE‐TDA, with only two releasing 2,4‐TDA monomers, suggesting that TDA‐based PUR represents a more recalcitrant substrate. Nine enzymes were active on at least one nylonase probe, five significantly degraded insoluble nylon mimics and CCPUR1 and CCPUR2 released free amines from nylon‐6,6 and nylon‐6 textile samples. We also show monomer release from synthesized insoluble linear polymers of MDA‐based PUR. We characterized four novel enzymes alongside two reference PURases to degrade a FlexFoam, a generic mattress foam with a known recipe. The results demonstrate distinct degradation profiles, CCPUR1 showed the highest total product release on mattress foam with 0.92% extent conversion over 3 days and thus exceeding previously reported enzymes. To our knowledge, this study is the first reported example of enzymes capable of hydrolyzing longer chains of PUR and nylon, including a mattress thermoset flexible foam. Remarkably, we also demonstrated that the most active enzymes on actual polymers retained their catalytic capacity for longer periods of time at the tested conditions, which is a desired trait for a potential industrial biocatalyst. Therefore, our work provides a platform for future optimization campaigns to develop enzymatic solutions to plastic degradation through in silico discovery and protein engineering.
