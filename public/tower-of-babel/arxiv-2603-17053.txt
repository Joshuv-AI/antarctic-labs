Strengthening the finite characterizations of smooth min-max games
Valery Krivchenko, Alexander Gasnikov, Dmitry Kovalev

Abstract
In this paper, we address the problem of interpolation of smooth convex–concave functions. Interpolation is a key step for computer-assisted estimation of algorithms’ worst case performance via PEP-like techniques. And smooth convex–concave functions are frequently used to model min-max problems arising in machine learning. We address the challenges associated with deriving conditions that are necessary and sufficient for the interpolation of smooth min–max games and show how existing approaches can be adapted to this setting. As part of this effort, we study the smoothing properties of Moreau–Yosida-like approximations of convex–concave functions. Next, we derive interpolation conditions for several key special cases of smooth min–max games. Finally, we obtain improved (i.e., tighter) characterizations for smooth strongly monotone convex–concave functions. We analyzed linear convergence of Alt-GDA using a PEP-like technique with the novel constraints and (numerically) obtained a new estimate of the algorithm’s complexity. We are confident that the results of this paper provide meaningful progress toward establishing optimal worst-case guarantees for algorithms in the setting of smooth min-max games.



keywords
saddle point, smooth games, convex–concave, interpolation conditions, constraints, performance estimation problem

1
Introduction

In this paper we consider the following saddle point problem

\displaystyle\underset{x\in{\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}}{\min}\underset{y\in{\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}}}{\max}f(x,y)

(1)

where
{\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}
and
{\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}}
are Euclidean spaces and
f(x,y)\colon{\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}\times{\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}}\to\mathbb{R}
is a real-valued function. More specifically, we consider min–max optimization of smooth (and possibly, strongly monotone) convex–concave functions. In literature
27
, this setting is known as smooth min–max games and is closely related to variational inequalities. Smooth min–max games have been a subject of active research largely due to the fact that they can be used to model machine learning problems. Notable examples include generative adversarial networks
7
and reinforcement learning
15
.

First-order gradient-based methods are undoubtedly the go-to methodology for solving large-scale optimization problems arising in machine learning, and deep learning in particular. The main reason for this is the low cost per iteration coupled with satisfactory effectiveness. Consequently, there is a need to understand how such methods perform in the worst possible scenarios within the defined setting and to compare their worst-case performance. When it comes to min–max problems, much remains unknown about the worst-case behavior of even basic first-order methods
27
;
28
;
13
. One possible solution is to employ SDP-based techniques like performance estimation problems (PEP)
5
;
26
, which makes it possible to obtain theoretical guarantees through computer-assisted calculations
8
.

The idea behind PEP is to express the exact worst-case performance of an optimization algorithm as the solution of a convex SDP problem. Such a tractable formulation requires tight characterizations of function classes—interpolation conditions. Unfortunately, there is currently no approach that allows one to derive interpolation conditions for a function class directly from its definition. Nevertheless, in the absence of proper interpolation conditions, it is still meaningful to derive constraints that are as strong as possible
18
. Although the resulting formulation of PEP is no longer worst-case exact, using such constraints will yield a valid lower bound on an algorithm’s performance, which may still represent a substantial improvement over known bounds.

In this paper, we build upon prior work on smooth strongly convex interpolation
20
and convex–concave interpolation
12
to obtain tighter constraints for the smooth min–max game setting. The results of this paper offer theoretical, practical, and methodological value. From a theoretical standpoint, we present the tightest characterizations of smooth strongly monotone convex–concave functions known to date. Methodologically, we demonstrate how careful analysis of special cases and related function classes can yield tighter constraints and narrow the search for proper interpolation conditions. From a practical perspective, the new algebraic characterizations of smooth strongly monotone games are compatible with the PEP framework, providing an alternative to the more cumbersome integral quadratic constraints (IQC) framework
14
;
27
.

Finally, we demonstrate that our novel constraints provide a significantly better (i.e., tighter) characterization of smooth, strongly monotone convex–concave functions than a discretized definition of the class. For this purpose, we performed a series of numerical experiments with Gradient Descent-Ascent with simultaneous updates (Sim-GDA) and Gradient Descent-Ascent with alternating updates (Alt-GDA), comprising automatic search for the best Lyapunov functions. In particular, for Alt-GDA we observe that the proposed constraints yield substantially stronger convergence guarantees, indicating that they capture structural properties that are especially relevant for algorithms with alternating updates.

Our contribution can be summarized as follows:

•

We study smoothing properties of Moreau–Yosida-like approximation of convex–concave functions and prove that it possesses a stronger co-coercive property. To our knowledge, existing studies in the literature establish only Lipschitz smoothness of the envelope. We also adapt a conjugation-based approach (that was previously used for studying smooth convex interpolation) to smooth convex–concave interpolation.

•

We obtain tighter constraints for the general case of smooth strongly monotone convex–concave functions and perform an experiment to compare them with definition-based constraints. We also derive necessary and sufficient interpolation conditions for key special cases within this setting.

•

Using the novel constraints we are able to numerically verify a better complexity estimate of
\mathcal{O}\left(\kappa^{1.26}\right)
for Alt-GDA in smooth min-max games. Currently the best estimate in literature is
\mathcal{O}\left(\kappa^{1.385}\right)
.

1.1
Motivation and prior work on interpolation

Almost all proofs of worst-case bounds (and all proofs obtained via PEP) involve constraints that describe algebraic relations between quantities appearing in the algorithm, most notably the iterates
x_{i}
, the gradients
\nabla f(x_{i})
and the functional values
f(x_{i})
. These constraints implicitly impose restrictions on the admissible gradients and functional values, ensuring that they are consistent with some function from a prescribed function class.

Crucially, the set of admissible iterates, gradients, and functional values must be restricted to those that can be interpolated by a real function belonging to the considered class. Otherwise, the feasible set may contain artificial configurations that do not correspond to any actual function, leading to overly pessimistic bounds
17
. Unfortunately, a simple discretization of an arbitrary equivalent definition of a function class does not necessarily yield constraints that impose sufficiently strong restrictions. We illustrate this point with a classical example
17
. Consider the class
\mathcal{F}_{L}
of smooth convex functions and one of its equivalent definitions involving discretizable constraints:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}f(y)\geqslant f(x)+\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle,\\
\|\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)\|_{2}\leqslant L\|y-x\|_{2}.\end{cases}\quad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(2)

Also consider the alternative definition

\displaystyle f(y)\geqslant f(x)+\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle+\frac{\|\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)\|_{2}^{2}}{2L},\quad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(3)

Constraints (
2
) and (
3
) are completely equivalent when imposed on every pair
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. However, most proofs involve only a finite number of constraints imposed on a finite number of points. In this case, (
2
) and (
3
) are no longer equivalent
17
. In fact, (
3
) constitutes the strongest discrete characterization of
\mathcal{F}_{L}
20
, in the sense that these conditions are necessary and sufficient for interpolation within
\mathcal{F}_{L}
19
. In contrast, the weaker constraints (
2
) do not guarantee the existence of an interpolating function and can therefore be a source of conservatism in worst-case bounds. Although the degree of conservatism also depends on the analyzed method and on the optimality of the analysis, the availability of stronger constraints clearly opens the path to tighter worst-case guarantees.

Interpolation of function classes has been a subject of intensive research and has been particularly fruitful in the convex setting
20
;
19
. The resulting comprehensive interpolation theory has enabled the exact solution of worst-case performance problems for a number of key first-order algorithms
4
;
22
;
21
. In contrast, in the convex–concave setting, interpolation conditions have only been derived for the general non-smooth case and for smooth convex–concave functions with bilinear coupling structure
12
, namely functions of the form
f(x)+\langle y,Ax\rangle-g(y)
. Interpolation conditions for smooth convex–concave functions with unspecified structure have not yet been derived. This gap prevents researchers from closing the discrepancy between lower and upper performance bounds even for simple first-order algorithms
13
. The current state-of-the-art constraints for smooth convex–concave functions therefore rely on trivial discretizations of smoothness definitions
26
or on combinations of smooth convex constraints
13
. Such weaker characterizations may fail to capture structural properties specific to the convex–concave setting that are relevant for algorithmic analysis—a limitation that will also become evident in this paper.

Preliminaries

Consider a general variational inequality (VI) problem
10

\displaystyle\text{find}\hskip 4.0ptz_{*}\in\Omega\hskip 8.0pt\text{s.t.}\hskip 8.0pt\langle g^{z}(z_{*}),z-z_{*}\rangle\geqslant 0\hskip 8.0pt\text{for all}\hskip 4.0ptz\in\Omega

(4)

where
\Omega
is non-empty convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
g^{z}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a continuous mapping.

Convex–concave min-max problem (saddle-point problem) (
1

f
is smooth, is a specific instance of (
4
) with

\displaystyle g^{z}(z)=[\nabla_{x}f(z)^{\top},-\nabla_{y}f(z)^{\top}]^{\top}

where
z=[x^{\top},y^{\top}]^{\top}
.

In addition, because
f
is convex-concave, any solution
z_{*}=[x_{*}^{\top},y_{*}^{\top}]^{\top}
of (
4
) is a global Nash Equilibrium
25
:

\displaystyle f(x_{*},y)\leqslant f(x_{*},y_{*})\leqslant f(x,y_{*})\quad\forall x\in{\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}},y\in{\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}}

(5)

Unlike some of the prior works on smooth convex–concave problems
12
;
13
;
11
, that employed separate smoothness constants
L_{x}
,

and
L_{xy}
, this paper considers functions whose gradient mapping

is smooth and strongly monotone. Consequently, we introduce notation that explicitly reflects the connection between saddle-point problems and variational inequalities. We proceed by presenting formal definitions of the class of convex–concave functions and their associated gradient mappings.

Definition 1
.

We say that a function
f(x,y)\colon{\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}\times{\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}}\to\mathbb{R}
is convex–concave if

\displaystyle f(\cdot,y):\text{convex for all }y\in{\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}}

\displaystyle f(x,\cdot):\text{concave for all }x\in{\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}

We designate a class of convex–concave functions as
\mathcal{S}
.

Definition 2
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{S}
. We introduce a mapping
g^{z}(z)=[g^{x}(z)^{\top},-g^{y}(z)^{\top}]^{\top}
where
g^{x}(z)\in\partial_{x}f(z)
,
g^{y}(z)\in\partial_{y}f(z)
,
z=[x^{\top},y^{\top}]^{\top}
. We call
g^{z}(z)
a (gradient) mapping of
f
.

Next, we provide definitions for
\mu
-strongly monotone,
L
-smooth and
1/L
-co-coercive mappings.

Definition 3
.

Let

. We say that

has
\mu

is

-strongly-convex-

\langle g^{z}(w)-g^{z}(z),w-z\rangle\geqslant\mu\|w-z\|_{2}^{2}

(6)

for all

. Because the definition implies
\mu_{x}=\mu_{y}=\mu
, we will just say that

is
\mu
-strongly monotone for the sake of convenience.

Definition 4
.

Let

. We say that

has

-Lipschitz mapping or

is

-smooth if

\|g^{z}(w)-g^{z}(z)\|_{2}\leqslant L\|w-z\|_{2}

In the context of variational inequalities and smooth min-max games the above definitions are often used as standard assumptions

. In this paper we shall also consider a stronger property of co-coercivity:

Definition 5
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{S}
. We say that

has
1/L

\langle g^{z}(w)-g^{z}(z),w-z\rangle\geqslant\frac{1}{L}\|g^{z}(w)-g^{z}(z)\|_{2}^{2}

Co-coercivity is a property closely connected to Lipschitz smoothness and is, in fact, equivalent to it in convex setting

.

3
Convex–concave interpolation and properties

3.1
Review of convex–concave interpolation

Now we can proceed to the main subject of this paper: convex–concave interpolation. We begin with reviewing interpolation conditions for convex–concave functions that were presented in
12
. and also state several basic properties of convex–concave functions that will be used later.

Theorem 1

(convex–concave interpolation
12
)
.

Let I be an index set and consider a vector sequence
\left\{(x_{i},y_{i},g^{x}_{i},g^{y}_{i},f_{i})\right\}_{i\in I}
. This sequence is
\mathcal{S}
-interpolable if and only if the following conditions are satisfied:
for all
i,j\in I
the following inequality holds:

f_{i}\geqslant f_{j}+\langle g^{x}_{j},x_{i}-x_{j}\rangle+\langle g^{y}_{i},y_{i}-y_{j}\rangle

(10)

In the proof Theorem
10
, the following function was used to prove conditions’ sufficiency for interpolation:

\displaystyle f(x,y)=\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\underset{i\in[n]}{\min}\hskip 2.0ptf_{i}+\langle z,x-x_{i}\rangle+\langle g^{y}_{i},y-y_{i}\rangle

(11)

where the convex set
\mathcal{Z}\subset{\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}
is defined as follows

\mathcal{Z}=\operatorname{conv}(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\{g^{x}_{i}\}).

Since it’s not intuitive how this function was chosen, we shall provide motivation for it using the concept of duality.

Lemma 1
.

Consider a sequence
\left\{(x_{i},y_{i},g^{x}_{i},g^{y}_{i},f_{i})\right\}_{i\in I}
. The following statements are equivalent

\displaystyle(1):\left\{(x_{i},y_{i},g^{x}_{i},g^{y}_{i},f_{i})\right\}_{i\in I}\text{ is }\mathcal{S}-\text{interpolable}

\displaystyle(2):\left\{\left((g^{x}_{i},y_{i}),(x_{i},-g^{y}_{i}),x_{i}^{\top}g^{x}_{i}-f_{i}\right)\right\}_{i\in I}\text{ is }\mathcal{F}-\text{interpolable}

Proof.

[(1)\implies(2)]
. Suppose
\left\{(x_{i},y_{i},g^{x}_{i},g^{y}_{i},f_{i})\right\}_{i\in I}
is interpolable by convex–concave function
f(x,y)
. Its partial conjugate
f^{\ast}(p,y)
is a convex function such that

\displaystyle\partial_{p}f^{\ast}(g^{x}_{i},y_{i})=x_{i},\hskip 8.0pt\partial_{y}f^{\ast}(g^{x}_{i},y_{i})=-g^{y}_{i},

\displaystyle f^{\ast}(g^{x}_{i},y_{i})=\langle x_{i},g^{x}_{i}\rangle-f_{i}

which proves the statement. The reverse
[(2)\implies(1)]
is proven the same way.

∎

Lets now suppose we have a sequence
\left\{(x_{i},y_{i},g^{x}_{i},g^{y}_{i},f_{i})\right\}_{i\in I}
that is
\mathcal{S}
-interpolable. Then, by Lemma
1
we have that
\left\{(v_{i},g^{v}_{i},w_{i})\right\}_{i\in I}
, where
v_{i}=[g^{x}_{i}{}^{\top},y_{i}^{\top}]
,
g^{v}_{i}=[x_{i}^{\top},-g^{y}_{i}{}^{\top}]^{\top}
,
w_{i}=\langle x_{i},g^{x}_{i}\rangle-f_{i}
, is interpolable by a convex function. Lets construct a convex function that interpolates the sequence:

\displaystyle F(v)

\displaystyle=\underset{i}{\max}\hskip 4.0ptw_{i}+\langle g^{v}_{i},v-v_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=F(p,y)=\underset{i}{\max}\hskip 4.0pt\langle x_{i},g^{x}_{i}\rangle-f_{i}+\langle x_{i},p-g^{x}_{i}\rangle-g^{y}_{i}{}^{\top}(y-y_{i})

By performing partial conjugation for
F(p,y)
, we get exactly (
11
). Next, we move to state some basic properties of convex–concave function.

3.2
Basic convex–concave properties and strongly monotone case

The next Lemma contains statements that are equivalent to a function being convex–concave.

Lemma 2
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}\times{\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}}\to\mathbb{R}
. The following statements are equivalent
\forall z_{0},z_{1}\in domf\text{ and }g^{x}_{0}\in\partial_{x}f(z_{0}),g^{x}_{1}\in\partial_{x}f(z_{1}),g^{y}_{0}\in\partial_{y}f(z_{0}),g^{y}_{1}\in\partial_{y}f(z_{1})
.

\displaystyle(1):f\in\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle(2):f(z_{1})\geqslant f(z_{0})+\langle g^{x}_{0},x_{1}-x_{0}\rangle+\langle g^{y}_{1},y_{1}-y_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle(3):\langle g^{z}(z_{1})-g^{z}(z_{0}),z_{1}-z_{0}\rangle\geqslant 0

Proof.

[(1)\iff(2)]
: Follows from Theorem
10
.

[(2)\implies(3)]
: From
(2)
we have

\displaystyle f(z_{1})\geqslant f(z_{0})+\langle g^{x}_{0},x_{1}-x_{0}\rangle+\langle g^{y}_{1},y_{1}-y_{0}\rangle,

\displaystyle f(z_{1})\geqslant f(z_{0})+\langle g^{x}_{0},x_{1}-x_{0}\rangle+\langle g^{y}_{1},y_{1}-y_{0}\rangle

by adding together, we get
(3)
.

[(3)\implies(1)]
: for any fixed

we get

\displaystyle\langle g^{x}(x_{1},y)-g^{x}(x_{0},y),x_{1}-x_{0}\rangle\geqslant 0

which is equivalent to

being convex

. The same way,
f(x,\cdot)
is concave. It means

by definition.

∎

The following Lemma states that addition of regularization makes function strongly monotone:

.

We have

The proposition is a consequence of Lemma

∎

An addition of quadratic regulariation naturally induces a pointwise transform so the interpolability of related sequences is also related:

Lemma 4
.

Consider a sequence
\left\{(x_{i},y_{i},g^{x}_{i},g^{y}_{i},f_{i})\right\}_{i\in I}
. The following statements are equivalent

\displaystyle(1):\left\{(x_{i},y_{i},g^{x}_{i},g^{y}_{i},f_{i})\right\}_{i\in I}\text{ is }\mathcal{S}_{\mu}-\text{interpolable}

\displaystyle(2):\left\{\left(x_{i},y_{i},g^{x}_{i}-\mu x_{i},g^{y}_{i}+\mu y_{i},f_{i}-\frac{\mu\|x_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}{2}+\frac{\mu\|y_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}{2}\right)\right\}_{i\in I}\text{ is }\mathcal{S}-\text{interpolable}

Now, as a simple consequence of Theorem

Lemma 5

(strongly monotone convex–concave interpolation)
.

\mathcal{S}_{\mu}
-interpolable if and only if the following conditions are satisfied:
for all

f_{i}\geqslant f_{j}+\langle g^{x}_{j},x_{i}-x_{j}\rangle+\langle g^{y}_{i},y_{i}-y_{j}\rangle+\frac{\mu\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}{2}+\frac{\mu\|y_{i}-y_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}{2}

3.3
Smooth convex–concave interpolation: complications

To better understand the challenges involved in deriving interpolation conditions for smooth convex–concave classes, it is instructive to analyze the derivation of interpolation conditions in the smooth strongly convex case.

Theorem 2

(Smooth strongly convex interpolation
20
)
.

Let I be an index set and consider a vector sequence
\left\{(x_{i},g_{i},f_{i})\right\}_{i\in I}
. This sequence is
\mathcal{F}_{\mu,L}
-interpolable if and only if the following conditions are satisfied:
for all
i,j\in I
the following inequality holds:

\displaystyle f_{i}

\displaystyle\geqslant f_{j}+\langle g_{j},x_{i}-x_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2(1-\mu/L)}\left(\frac{1}{L}\|g_{i}-g_{j}\|_{2}^{2}+\mu\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|_{2}^{2}-2\frac{\mu}{L}\langle g_{i}-g_{j},x_{i}-x_{j}\rangle\right)

(13)

The proof of Theorem
2
relies on reducing the smooth strongly convex interpolation problem to a convex one. Specifically, a sequence is interpolable by a smooth strongly convex function if and only if an appropriately transformed sequence is interpolable by a convex function. Convex interpolation conditions applied to the transformed sequence then yield the desired result. This reduction is achieved via a transform
T
introduced in
20
, satisfying
f\in\mathcal{F}_{0,\infty}
if and only if
T(f)\in\mathcal{F}_{\mu,L}
:

T(f)(x)=\left(f^{\ast}(p)+\frac{\|p\|_{2}^{2}}{2(L-\mu)}\right)^{\ast}+\frac{\mu\|x\|_{2}^{2}}{2}

(14)

The property of the transform stems from the equivalence of conditions related to Lipschitz-continuous gradient:

Lemma 6

(Property of convex functions
29
.)
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{F}
. The following propositions are equivalent for any
x_{0},x_{1}\in domf
:

\displaystyle(1):\langle\nabla f(x_{1})-\nabla f(x_{0}),x_{1}-x_{0}\rangle\geqslant\frac{1}{L}\|\nabla f(x_{1})-\nabla f(x_{0})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle(2):\|\nabla f(x_{1})-\nabla f(x_{0})\|_{2}\leqslant L\|x_{1}-x_{0}\|_{2}

\displaystyle(3):\langle\nabla f(x_{1})-\nabla f(x_{0}),x_{1}-x_{0}\rangle\leqslant L\|x_{1}-x_{0}\|_{2}^{2}

The first part of the transform
T
((
14
)) is the
(L-\mu)
-smooth Moreau envelope of
f
which uniquely determines the original function. By Lemma
6
the addition of a quadratic regularization
\mu\|x\|_{2}^{2}/2
to a function
f\in\mathcal{F}_{0,L-\mu}
yields a smooth strongly convex function
\overline{f}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mu,L}
with the Lipschitz constant of the gradient increased by
\mu
. This transformation is also invertible with the reverse
f(x)=\overline{f}(x)-\mu/2\|x\|_{2}^{2}
.

Because the statement of Lemma
6
does not extend to convex–concave functions, it is unclear how to properly adapt the transform
T
in a way that would conserve its properties. Moreover, since co-coercivity is no longer equivalent to Lipschitz continuity of the gradient in the convex–concave case, these properties must be treated separately. A simple example illustrating this discrepancy is the bilinear function
y^{\top}Ax
where
A
is a matrix with non-zero largest singular value bounded by
L>0
. It’s easy to see that
y^{\top}Ax
is
L
-smooth but not
\frac{1}{L}
-co-coercive.

In the following subsection
3.4
we establish a bidirectional connection between
\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L}
and
\mathcal{S}^{cc}_{\mu,L}
that allows us to construct an invertible pointwise transform.

3.4
Connection between smooth and co-coercive classes

The relationship between
\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L}
and
\mathcal{S}^{cc}_{\mu,L}
turns out to be simple. The following Theorem
3
establishes one-to-one correspondence between functions from
\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L}
and
\mathcal{S}^{cc}_{\mu,L}
:

Theorem 3
.

Function
f\in\mathcal{S}^{cc}_{\mu+L,\ 2L}
if and only if
f-\frac{L\|x\|_{2}^{2}}{2}+\frac{L\|y\|_{2}^{2}}{2}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L}
.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathcal{S}^{cc}_{\mu+L,\ 2L}
. By definition it means that
\forall z_{0},z_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}\times{\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}}
:

\displaystyle\langle g_{z}(z_{1})-g_{z}(z_{0}),z_{1}-z_{0}\rangle\geqslant\frac{1}{2L}\|g_{z}(z_{1})-g_{z}(z_{0})\|_{2}^{2},

\displaystyle\langle g_{z}(z_{1})-g_{z}(z_{0}),z_{1}-z_{0}\rangle\geqslant\left(\mu+L\right)\|z_{1}-z_{0}\|_{2}^{2}

Now consider
\phi(x,y)=f(x,y)-\frac{L\|x\|_{2}^{2}}{2}+\frac{L\|y\|_{2}^{2}}{2}
with
\overline{g_{z}}(z)=[\nabla_{x}\phi(z)^{\top},-\nabla_{y}\phi(z)^{\top}]^{\top}
. With that we have

\displaystyle\langle\overline{g_{z}}(z_{1})-\overline{g_{z}}(z_{0}),z_{1}-z_{0}\rangle+L\|z_{1}-z_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\geqslant\frac{1}{2L}\|\overline{g_{z}}(z_{1})-\overline{g_{z}}(z_{0})+L(z_{1}-z_{0})\|_{2}^{2},

\displaystyle\langle\overline{g_{z}}(z_{1})-\overline{g_{z}}(z_{0}),z_{1}-z_{0}\rangle\geqslant\mu\|z_{1}-z_{0}\|_{2}^{2}

After rewriting the first inequality

\displaystyle\langle\overline{g_{z}}(z_{1})-\overline{g_{z}}(z_{0}),z_{1}-z_{0}\rangle+L\|z_{1}-z_{0}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{2L}\|\overline{g_{z}}(z_{1})-\overline{g_{z}}(z_{0})\|_{2}^{2}+\langle\overline{g_{z}}(z_{1})-\overline{g_{z}}(z_{0}),z_{1}-z_{0}\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|z_{1}-z_{0}\|_{2}^{2}

and reducing it to

\displaystyle\|\overline{g_{z}}(z_{1})-\overline{g_{z}}(z_{0})\|_{2}\leqslant L\|z_{1}-z_{0}\|_{2}

we get

\displaystyle\|\overline{g_{z}}(z_{1})-\overline{g_{z}}(z_{0})\|_{2}\leqslant L\|z_{1}-z_{0}\|_{2},

\displaystyle\langle\overline{g_{z}}(z_{1})-\overline{g_{z}}(z_{0}),z_{1}-z_{0}\rangle\geqslant\mu\|z_{1}-z_{0}\|_{2}^{2}

which means that
\phi\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L}
by definition. If we suppose
\phi\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L}
and do everything in reverse, we will prove
f\in\mathcal{S}^{cc}_{\mu+L,\ 2L}
.
∎

From Theorem
3
it follows that obtaining interpolation conditions for either
\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L}
or
\mathcal{S}^{cc}_{\mu+L,\ 2L}
would automatically yield the conditions for both classes. Co-coercivity property appears to be the easier one to study as it will become evident further.

3.5
Moreau-Yosida approximates

In the literature
2
;
6
there was proposed an analogue of the Moreau envelope for convex–concave functions:

s(v,w)=\mathcal{M}_{L}(f)(v,w)=\inf_{x}\sup_{y}\left[f(x,y)+\frac{L}{2}\|x-v\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{L}{2}\|y-w\|_{2}^{2}\right].

(15)

It is known
2
;
6
that if the original function
f
is convex–concave, closed, and proper, then this operation defines a pointwise transform such that the resulting function
s
is also convex–concave, closed, and proper, and uniquely determines the original function. It is often stated that the resulting approximate functions are
L
-smooth, however (as we will show) they, in fact, satisfy the stronger property of co-coercivity (in a sense that their gradient mappings are co-coercive, Definition
5
).

In studies of convex–concave functions there is a popular approach
2
of performing partial conjugation with only ”convex” variables:

\displaystyle f^{\ast}(p,y)=\underset{x\in{\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}}}{\sup}\{\langle p,x\rangle-f(x,y)\}

(16)

If
f\in\mathcal{S}
, it’s easy to see that
\phi\colon=f^{*}\in\mathcal{F}
, i.e.
\phi(p,y)
is convex in
(p,y)
. Then, a partial conjugate of
\phi
with
y
variables is
\psi(p,q)
which is concave in
p
and convex in
q

2
. Which means
-\psi
is convex in
p
and concave in
q
. We formally introduce those actions as a single operation

f^{+}(p,q)=-\underset{y}{\sup}\text{ }\left(\langle q,y\rangle-\underset{x}{\sup}\left(\langle p,x\rangle-f(x,y)\right)\right)

(17)

which consists of taking the partial conjugate with respect to
x
, then with respect to
y
, followed by multiplication by
-1
. For
f^{+}
we denote
g^{p}(p,q)=\nabla_{p}f^{+}(p,q)
,
g^{q}(p,q)=\nabla_{q}f^{+}(p,q)
.

If
f
is convex–concave, closed and proper then
f^{+}
is also convex–concave, closed and proper
9
, it holds that
f^{++}=f
and (
15
) can be rewritten with the using duality operation:

s(v,w)=\mathcal{M}_{L}(f)(v,w)=\left(f^{+}+\frac{\|p\|_{2}^{2}}{2L}-\frac{\|q\|_{2}^{2}}{2L}\right)^{+}

(18)

We shall also use the following property of
f^{+}
:

Lemma 7
.

Suppose
f\in\mathcal{S}
. Then for
p=g^{x}(x,y)
,
q=-g^{y}(x,y)
it holds that

\displaystyle f^{+}(p,q)+f(x,y)=\langle p,x\rangle-\langle q,y\rangle,

\displaystyle g^{p}(p,q)=x,

\displaystyle g^{q}(p,q)=-y

Proof.

Follows directly from the definition of
f^{+}
.
∎

We now establish a key property of
f^{+}
, which motivates the introduction of this operation. Specifically, there exists a duality between strong monotonicity and co-coercivity for convex–-concave functions with respect to the duality operation defined above. This is analogous to the Fenchel duality in the convex setting, where strong convexity corresponds to a Lipschitz continuous gradient in the dual function.

Lemma 8
.

Consider function
f\in\mathcal{S}
. We have
f\in\mathcal{S}^{cc}_{\mu,L}
if and only if
f^{+}\in\mathcal{S}^{cc}_{1/L,1/\mu}
.

Proof.

By definition,
f\in\mathcal{S}^{cc}_{\mu,L}
means that

\displaystyle f\in\mathcal{S},

(19)

\displaystyle\langle g^{x}(x_{1},y_{1})-g^{x}(x_{0},y_{0}),x_{1}-x_{0}\rangle-\langle g^{y}(x_{1},y_{1})-g^{y}(x_{0},y_{0}),y_{1}-y_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle\geqslant\mu\|x_{1}-x_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+\mu\|y_{1}-y_{0}\|_{2}^{2},

(20)

\displaystyle\langle g^{x}(x_{1},y_{1})-g^{x}(x_{0},y_{0}),x_{1}-x_{0}\rangle-\langle g^{y}(x_{1},y_{1})-g^{y}(x_{0},y_{0}),y_{1}-y_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{L}\|x_{1}-x_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{L}\|y_{1}-y_{0}\|_{2}^{2}

(21)

By Lemma
7
, from the above inequalities it follows that

\displaystyle\langle g^{p}(p_{1},q_{1})-g^{p}(p_{0},q_{0}),p_{1}-p_{0}\rangle-\langle g^{q}(p_{1},q_{1})-g^{q}(p_{0},q_{0}),q_{1}-q_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle\geqslant\mu\|g^{p}(p_{1},q_{1})-g^{p}(p_{0},q_{0})\|_{2}^{2}+\mu\|g^{q}(p_{1},q_{1})-g^{q}(p_{0},q_{0})\|_{2}^{2},

(22)

\displaystyle\langle g^{p}(p_{1},q_{1})-g^{p}(p_{0},q_{0}),p_{1}-p_{0}\rangle-\langle g^{q}(p_{1},q_{1})-g^{q}(p_{0},q_{0}),q_{1}-q_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{L}\|p_{1}-p_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{L}\|q_{1}-q_{0}\|_{2}^{2}

(23)

which considering
f^{+}\in\mathcal{S}
, means by definition that
f^{+}\in\mathcal{S}^{cc}_{1/L,1/\mu}
. With
f^{++}=f
it’s straightforward to prove the reverse.

∎

The same as Fenchel duality,
+
also induces a pointwise transform:

Lemma 9
.

The following propositions are equivalent

\displaystyle(1):\left\{(x_{i},y_{i},g^{x}_{i},g^{y}_{i},f_{i})\right\}_{i\in I}\text{ is }\mathcal{S}^{cc}_{\mu,L}-\text{interpolable}

\displaystyle(2):\left\{(g^{x}_{i},-g^{y}_{i},x_{i},-y_{i},\hskip 2.0pt\langle g^{x}_{i},x\rangle+\langle g^{y}_{i},y\rangle-f)\right\}_{i\in I}\text{ is }\mathcal{S}^{cc}_{1/L,1/\mu}-\text{interpolable}

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathcal{S}^{cc}_{\mu,L}
be a function that interpolates
\left\{(x_{i},y_{i},g^{x}_{i},g^{y}_{i},f_{i})\right\}_{i\in I}
. Then
f^{+}\in\mathcal{S}^{cc}_{1/L,1/\mu}
per Lemma
8
. From Lemma
7

f^{+}
interpolates
\left\{(g^{x}_{i},-g^{y}_{i},x_{i},-y_{i},\hskip 2.0pt\langle g^{x}_{i},x\rangle+\langle g^{y}_{i},y\rangle-f)\right\}_{i\in I}
. The reverse is proven the same way.

∎

Using properties of
f^{+}
, we follow up with a key property of
\mathcal{M}_{L}(f)
envelope:

Lemma 10
.

Function
f\in\mathcal{S}
if and only if
\mathcal{M}_{L}(f)\in\mathcal{S}^{cc}_{0,L}
.

Proof.

We follow a chain of equivalent propositions. Suppose
f\in\mathcal{S}
. Then
f^{+}\in\mathcal{S}
. Then
f^{+}+\frac{\|p\|_{2}^{2}}{2L}-\frac{\|q\|_{2}^{2}}{2L}\in\mathcal{S}^{cc}_{1/L,\infty}
. Then
\mathcal{M}_{L}(f)\in\mathcal{S}^{cc}_{0,L}
per Lemma

.
∎

\mathcal{S}_{L,L}

\mathcal{S}^{cc}_{L,L}
is a difference of two functions from
\mathcal{F}_{L,L}
.

Theorem 6
.

Sequence
\left\{(x_{i},y_{i},g^{x}_{i},g^{y}_{i},f_{i})\right\}_{i\in I}
is
\mathcal{S}^{cc}_{L,L}
-interpolable if and only if
\forall i,j\in I
:

\displaystyle f_{i}\geqslant f_{j}+\langle g^{x}_{j},x_{i}-x_{j}\rangle+\langle g^{y}_{i},y_{i}-y_{j}\rangle+\frac{\|g^{x}_{i}-g^{x}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}{2L}+\frac{\|g^{y}_{i}-g^{y}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}{2L},

\displaystyle g^{x}_{i}-g^{x}_{j}=L(x_{i}-x_{j}),

\displaystyle g^{y}_{i}-g^{y}_{j}=-L(y_{i}-y_{j})

Proof.

Necessity. Let the sequence
\left\{(x_{i},y_{i},g^{x}_{i},g^{y}_{i},f_{i})\right\}_{i\in I}
be
\mathcal{S}^{cc}_{L,L}
-interpolable. It’s straightforward to check that the conditions are satisfied.

Sufficiency. Suppose the conditions are satisfied. Rewriting them in equivalent form:

\displaystyle f_{i}\geqslant f_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\langle g^{x}_{i}+g^{x}_{j},x_{i}-x_{j}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle g^{y}_{i}+g^{y}_{j},y_{i}-y_{j}\rangle,

\displaystyle g^{x}_{i}-g^{x}_{j}=L(x_{i}-x_{j}),

\displaystyle g^{y}_{i}-g^{y}_{j}=-L(y_{i}-y_{j})

which leads to

\displaystyle f_{i}=f_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\langle g^{x}_{i}+g^{x}_{j},x_{i}-x_{j}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle g^{y}_{i}+g^{y}_{j},y_{i}-y_{j}\rangle,

\displaystyle g^{x}_{i}-g^{x}_{j}=L(x_{i}-x_{j}),

\displaystyle g^{y}_{i}-g^{y}_{j}=-L(y_{i}-y_{j})

Now consider a system of linear inequalities with variables
\{p_{i}\}_{i\in I}
:

p_{i}-p_{j}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\langle g^{x}_{i}+g^{x}_{j},x_{i}-x_{j}\rangle=\frac{1}{2L}\left(\|g^{x}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}-\|g^{x}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\right),\quad\forall i,j\in I

(37)

One can notice that the system of inequalities in (
37
) can be equivalently rewritten as a system of equations

p_{i}-p_{j}=\frac{1}{2}\langle g^{x}_{i}+g^{x}_{j},x_{i}-x_{j}\rangle,\quad\forall i,j\in I

(38)

that has family of solutions one of which is
\forall i\in I:p_{i}=\frac{1}{2L}\|g^{x}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}
. The existence of such
\{p_{i}\}_{i\in I}
is all we need. Now we introduce
\{q_{i}\}_{i\in I}
such that
q_{i}=p_{i}-f_{i}
:

\displaystyle f_{i}=f_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\langle g^{x}_{i}+g^{x}_{j},x_{i}-x_{j}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle g^{y}_{i}+g^{y}_{j},y_{i}-y_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle(f_{i}-p_{i})-(f_{j}-p_{j})=\frac{1}{2}\langle g^{y}_{i}+g^{y}_{j},y_{i}-y_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle q_{i}=q_{j}-\frac{1}{2}\langle g^{y}_{i}+g^{y}_{j},y_{i}-y_{j}\rangle

We combine the equations together

\displaystyle\forall i,j\in I:

\displaystyle p_{i}=p_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\langle g^{x}_{i}+g^{x}_{j},x_{i}-x_{j}\rangle,

\displaystyle g^{x}_{i}-g^{x}_{j}=L(x_{i}-x_{j}),

\displaystyle q_{i}=q_{j}-\frac{1}{2}\langle g^{y}_{i}+g^{y}_{j},y_{i}-y_{j}\rangle,

\displaystyle g^{y}_{i}-g^{y}_{j}=-L(y_{i}-y_{j})

From the first half of equations and Theorem
5
it follows that sequence
\left\{(x_{i},g^{x}_{i},p_{i})\right\}_{i\in I}
is
\mathcal{F}_{L,L}
-interpolable. Let
P(x)\in\mathcal{F}_{L,L}
be a function that interpolates the sequence. From the second half of equations and Theorem
5
it follows that sequence
\left\{(y_{i},-g^{y}_{i},q_{i})\right\}_{i\in I}
is also
\mathcal{F}_{L,L}
-interpolable. Let
Q(y)\in\mathcal{F}_{L,L}
be a function that interpolates the sequence.
Now consider a function
F(x,y)=P(x)-Q(y)
. From Lemma
29
it follows that
F(x,y)\in\mathcal{S}^{cc}_{L,L}
. It is fairly obvious, from the way we constructed it, that
F(x,y)
correctly interpolates the original sequence
\left\{(x_{i},y_{i},g^{x}_{i},g^{y}_{i},f_{i})\right\}_{i\in I}
.

∎

4.2
Smooth case

We now turn to characterizing the class of greatest practical interest: smooth convex–concave functions, possibly strongly-convex–strongly-concave (SCSC). We begin with the interpolation conditions for
-L
-weakly-convex-weakly-concave (WCWC) functions with
L
-Lipschitz continuous gradients (
\mathcal{S}_{-L,L}
), by applying Theorem
3
and the interpolation conditions for
1/L
-co-coercive convex–concave functions.

Theorem 7

(
\mathcal{S}_{-L,L}
interpolation)
.

Let I be an index set and consider a vector sequence
\left\{(x_{i},y_{i},g^{x}_{i},g^{y}_{i},f_{i})\right\}_{i\in I}
. This sequence is
\mathcal{S}_{-L,L}
-interpolable if and only if the following conditions are satisfied:
for all
i,j\in I
the following inequality holds:

\displaystyle f_{i}

\displaystyle\geqslant f_{j}+\langle g^{x}_{j},x_{i}-x_{j}\rangle+\langle g^{y}_{i},y_{i}-y_{j}\rangle+\frac{\|g^{z}_{i}-g^{z}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}{4L}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\langle g^{z}_{i}-g^{z}_{j},z_{i}-z_{j}\rangle-\frac{L\|z_{i}-z_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}{4}

(39)

Proof.

Using Theorem
3
with
\mu=-L
and interpolation conditions for
\mathcal{S}^{cc}_{0,2L}
(see Theorem
24
).

The conditions in Theorem
7
are necessary and sufficient for interpolation within
\mathcal{S}_{-L,L}
. Alternatively, as shown in
17
;
19
, among all equivalent characterizations of a function class, the interpolation conditions are the strictest when imposed on a finite set of points. From this perspective, we aim to construct the strictest possible characterizations for
\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L}
and
\mathcal{S}^{cc}_{\mu,L}
. At we same time, we want the desired characterizations to be in agreement with all previously explored special cases, namely
\mathcal{S}_{-L,L}
,
\mathcal{S}_{L,L}
,
\mathcal{S}^{cc}_{0,L}
and also smooth strongly-convex functions
\mathcal{F}_{\mu,L}
. We begin construction of constraints for
\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L}
by adding
\mu
-strongly monotone conditions (Theorem
12
) to conditions from Theorem
7
:

\displaystyle D_{ij}\geqslant\frac{\|g^{z}_{i}-g^{z}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}{4L}+\frac{1}{2}\langle g^{z}_{i}-g^{z}_{j},z_{i}-z_{j}\rangle-\frac{L\|z_{i}-z_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}{4},

(40)

\displaystyle D_{ij}\geqslant\frac{\mu\|z_{i}-z_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}{2}

(41)

where we denote

D_{ij}:=f_{i}-f_{j}-\langle g^{x}_{j},x_{i}-x_{j}\rangle-\langle g^{y}_{i},y_{i}-y_{j}\rangle

If imposed on all possible pairs of points
(z_{i},z_{j})
where

\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L}

y\equiv 0
and

. Therefore, we need to introduce several additional inequalities that hold for
f\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L}
.

First we state a proposition that is often used as an alternative definition for functions with Lipschitz-continuous gradient:

Lemma 15

(Definition for Lipschitz continuous gradient
24
)
.

. Let
f
be a continuous function and
L>0
. We say that
f
has
L
-smooth gradient if
\forall x,y\in domf
:

\left|f(y)-f(x)-\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle\right|\leqslant\frac{L\|y-x\|_{2}^{2}}{2}

(42)

For
f(x,y)\in\mathcal{S}_{0,L}
, Lemma
42
implies

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&f(z_{1})\leqslant f(z_{0})+\langle g^{x}(z_{0}),x_{1}-x_{0}\rangle+\langle g^{y}(z_{0}),y_{1}-y_{0}\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|z_{1}-z_{0}\|_{2}^{2},\\
&f(z_{1})\geqslant f(z_{0})+\langle g^{x}(z_{0}),x_{1}-x_{0}\rangle+\langle g^{y}(z_{0}),y_{1}-y_{0}\rangle-\frac{L}{2}\|z_{1}-z_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\end{cases}

Next we prove the following property of
\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L}
:

Lemma 16
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L}
. Then
\forall z_{0},z_{1}\in domf
:

\displaystyle f(z_{1})

\displaystyle\geqslant f(z_{0})+\langle g^{x}(z_{0}),x_{1}-x_{0}\rangle+\langle g^{y}(z_{1}),y_{1}-y_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2(L-\mu)}\|g^{x}(z_{1})-g^{x}(z_{0})-\mu(x_{1}-x_{0})\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\mu}{2}\|x_{1}-x_{0}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{L}{2}\|y_{1}-y_{0}\|_{2}^{2},

\displaystyle f(z_{1})

\displaystyle\geqslant f(z_{0})+\langle g^{x}(z_{0}),x_{1}-x_{0}\rangle+\langle g^{y}(z_{1}),y_{1}-y_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2(L-\mu)}\|g^{y}(z_{1})-g^{y}(z_{0})+\mu(y_{1}-y_{0})\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\mu}{2}\|y_{1}-y_{0}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{L}{2}\|x_{1}-x_{0}\|_{2}^{2}

Proof.

From Lemma
42
:

f(z_{1})\leqslant f(z_{0})+\langle g^{x}(z_{0}),x_{1}-x_{0}\rangle+\langle g^{y}(z_{0}),y_{1}-y_{0}\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|z_{1}-z_{0}\|_{2}^{2}

(43)

Consider a new function

\displaystyle\phi=f-\frac{\mu\|x\|_{2}^{2}}{2},

\displaystyle\phi^{x}:=\nabla_{x}\phi(z)=g^{x}(z)-\mu x,

\displaystyle\phi^{y}:=\nabla_{y}\phi(z)=g^{y}(z)

In terms of
\phi
, (
43
) rewrites as

\displaystyle\phi(z_{1})

\displaystyle\leqslant\phi(z_{0})+\langle\phi^{x}(z_{0}),x_{1}-x_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle+\langle\phi^{y}(z_{0}),y_{1}-y_{0}\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|y_{1}-y_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{(L-\mu)}{2}\|x_{1}-x_{0}\|_{2}^{2}

(44)

Since
f\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L}
, it means
\phi\in\mathcal{S}
. Now we introduce
\theta\in\mathcal{S}
:

\displaystyle\theta(z)=\phi(z)-\langle\phi^{x}(z_{\ast}),x-x_{\ast}\rangle,

\displaystyle\theta^{x}:=\nabla_{x}\theta(z)=\phi^{x}(z)-\phi^{x}(z_{\ast}),

\displaystyle\theta^{y}:=\nabla_{y}\theta(z)=\phi^{y}(z)

where
z_{\ast}=[x_{\ast}^{\top},y_{\ast}^{\top}]^{\top}
is an arbitrary point in the domain of
\phi
. Because
\phi\in\mathcal{S}
, we have

\displaystyle x_{\ast}=\underset{x}{\arg\min}\hskip 2.0pt\theta(x,y_{\ast})

We can use newly defined
\theta
and (
44
) to derive

\displaystyle\theta(z_{1})

\displaystyle\leqslant\theta(z_{0})+\langle\theta^{x}(z_{0}),x_{1}-x_{0}\rangle

(45)

\displaystyle+\langle\theta^{y}(z_{0}),y_{1}-y_{0}\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|y_{1}-y_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{(L-\mu)}{2}\|x_{1}-x_{0}\|_{2}^{2}

(46)

The inequality above has the same structure as (
44
) because to get
\theta
we just subtracted a linear function from
\phi
. We can now write an upper bound for
\theta(z_{\ast})
:

\displaystyle\theta(x_{\ast},y_{\ast})=\underset{x}{\min}\theta(x,y_{\ast})

\displaystyle\leqslant\underset{x}{\min}\left[\theta(x_{1},y_{1})+\langle\theta^{x}(x_{1},y_{1}),x-x_{1}\rangle+\langle\theta^{y}(x_{1},y_{1}),y_{\ast}-y_{1}\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|y_{\ast}-y_{1}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{(L-\mu)}{2}\|x-x_{1}\|_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\underset{x}{\min}\left[\theta(x_{1},y_{1})+\langle\theta^{y}(x_{1},y_{1}),y_{\ast}-y_{1}\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|y_{\ast}-y_{1}\|_{2}^{2}-\|\theta^{x}(x_{1},y_{1})\|_{2}\|x-x_{1}\|_{2}+\frac{(L-\mu)}{2}\|x-x_{1}\|_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\theta(x_{1},y_{1})+\langle\theta^{y}(x_{1},y_{1}),y_{\ast}-y_{1}\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|y_{\ast}-y_{1}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{\|\theta^{x}(x_{1},y_{1})\|_{2}^{2}}{2(L-\mu)}

Rewriting the above inequality for
\phi
:

\displaystyle\phi(x_{1},y_{1})

\displaystyle\geqslant\phi(x_{\ast},y_{\ast})+\langle\phi^{x}(x_{\ast},y_{\ast}),x_{1}-x_{\ast}\rangle

\displaystyle+\langle\phi^{y}(x_{1},y_{1}),y_{1}-y_{\ast}\rangle+\frac{\|\phi^{x}(x_{1},y_{1})-\phi^{x}(x_{\ast},y_{\ast})\|_{2}^{2}}{2(L-\mu)}-\frac{L}{2}\|y_{1}-y_{\ast}\|_{2}^{2}

and then for the original function
f\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L}
:

\displaystyle f(z_{1})

\displaystyle\geqslant f(z_{0})+\langle g^{x}(z_{0}),x_{1}-x_{0}\rangle+\langle g^{y}(z_{1}),y_{1}-y_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2(L-\mu)}\|g^{x}(z_{1})-g^{x}(z_{0})-\mu(x_{1}-x_{0})\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\mu}{2}\|x_{1}-x_{0}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{L}{2}\|y_{1}-y_{0}\|_{2}^{2}

where we denoted
z_{\ast}\rightarrow z_{0}
.
Thus we get the first inequality. The second one is proven in similar manner, except we consider
\phi\in\mathcal{S}
:

\displaystyle\phi=f+\frac{\mu\|y\|_{2}^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle\phi^{x}:=\nabla_{x}\phi(z)=g^{x}(z)

\displaystyle\phi^{y}:=\nabla_{y}\phi(z)=g^{y}(z)+\mu y

and
\eta\in\mathcal{S}
:

\displaystyle\theta(z)=\phi(z)-\langle\phi^{y}(z_{\ast}),y-y_{\ast}\rangle

\displaystyle\theta^{x}:=\nabla_{x}\theta(z)=\phi^{x}(z)

\displaystyle\theta^{y}:=\nabla_{y}\theta(z)=\phi^{y}(z)-\phi^{y}(z_{\ast})

and finally, instead of minimization over
x
we maximize over
y
to get a lower bound for
\theta(x_{\ast},y_{\ast})
.

∎

We now combine (
41
) and Lemma
16
into a single proposition.

Theorem 8
.

If
f\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L}
then
\forall z_{1},z_{0}\in domf

\displaystyle D_{10}\geqslant\frac{\|g^{z}(z_{1})-g^{z}(z_{0})\|_{2}^{2}}{4L}+\frac{1}{2}\langle g^{z}(z_{1})-g^{z}(z_{0}),z_{1}-z_{0}\rangle-\frac{L\|z_{1}-z_{0}\|_{2}^{2}}{4}

\displaystyle D_{10}\geqslant\frac{\mu\|z_{1}-z_{0}\|_{2}^{2}}{2}

(48)

\displaystyle D_{10}\geqslant\frac{1}{2(L-\mu)}\|g^{x}(z_{1})-g^{x}(z_{0})-\mu(x_{1}-x_{0})\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\mu}{2}\|x_{1}-x_{0}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{L}{2}\|y_{1}-y_{0}\|_{2}^{2}

where

\displaystyle D_{ij}:=f(z_{i})-f(z_{j})-\langle g^{x}(z_{j}),x_{i}-x_{j}\rangle-\langle g^{y}(z_{i}),y_{i}-y_{j}\rangle

Proof.

The first inequality is interpolation conditions for
\mathcal{S}_{-L,L}

5
Experiment: showcasing novel constraints

5.1
Results and discussion

The purpose of this experimental section is to demonstrate that our novel constraints for
\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L}
are (considerably) stronger than the state-of-the-art constraints. We also provide a new numerical estimate of complexity of Alt-GDA (in this specific smooth min-max games setting) in the process. Thus, we compare iteration complexity estimates of basic algorithms that can be calculated with different characterizations of
\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L}
. Lower provable complexity would imply that the constraints are tighter and thus are better suited for analysis of algorithmic convergence.

The first set of constraints could be considered state-of-the-art for smooth min-max games
12

i,j\in I

\displaystyle\|g^{z}(z_{i})-g^{z}(z_{j})\|_{2}\leqslant L\|z_{i}-z_{j}\|_{2}

(55)

\displaystyle D_{ij}\geqslant\frac{\|g^{z}(z_{i})-g^{z}(z_{j})\|_{2}^{2}}{4L}+\frac{1}{2}\langle g^{z}(z_{i})-g^{z}(z_{j}),z_{i}-z_{j}\rangle-\frac{L\|z_{i}-z_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}{4},

(56)

\displaystyle D_{ij}\geqslant\frac{\mu\|z_{i}-z_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}{2}

And finally, the full set of the novel constraints. We perform an automatic search for a quadratic Lyapunov function for Sim-GDA and Alt-GDA and find the lowest verifiable contraction factor. Then, we compare the results for the three chosen characterizations of
\mathcal{S}_{\mu,L}
. The approach was introduced in
23
for the setting of smooth strongly convex functions and combines the ideas of PEP
5
;
20
and IQC

6
Conclusion

Optimization of smooth convex–concave games is a paradigm that has found a number of important applications in machine learning. Due to the complicated multiple-objective nature of these games, the analysis of algorithms becomes a rather grueling task that demands a systematic approach. We believe that the key to efficient analysis of first-order algorithms lies in PEP. However, the effectiveness of PEP is directly tied to the strength of discrete characterizations of function classes. The task of obtaining the best possible characterizations (i.e., the task of interpolation) for smooth games is the focus of this paper.

We managed to adapt the conjugation approach to convex–concave interpolation and used it to derive interpolation conditions for co-coercive convex–concave functions. Then, by using the relationship between Lipschitz smoothness and co-coercivity and considering key special cases, we obtained new inequalities characterizing smooth strongly monotone convex–concave functions. Although we did not manage to prove that our novel constraints are sufficient for interpolability, they offer a significant increase in tightness over simple definition-based constraints, as demonstrated in numerical experiments with Alt-GDA. Using the novel constraints we managed to improve the state-of-the-art numerical estimate of Alt-GDA’s complexity for smooth min-max games. Our results suggest that the proposed constraints properly capture structural properties of smooth games that are especially relevant for algorithms with alternating updates that are widely used in practice.
