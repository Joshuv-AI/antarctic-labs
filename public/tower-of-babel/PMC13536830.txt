Vibecotamab for measurable residual disease in acute myeloid leukemia and for myelodysplastic syndromes and chronic myelomonocytic leukemia after hypomethylating agent failure: a phase II study
Authors: Nicholas J Short, Alex Bataller, Courtney D DiNardo, Guillermo Montalban-Bravo, Sa A Wang, Wei Wang, Abhishek Maiti, Daniel Nguyen, Maria C Hachem, Timothy M Bi, Ghayas C Issa, Kelly S Chien, Maro Ohanian, Tapan M Kadia, Sanam Loghavi, Omer Karrar, Pavan Bachireddy, Hyunsoo Hwang, Xuelin Huang, Guillermo Garcia-Manero, Farhad Ravandi

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute myeloid leukemia (AML) with persistent measurable residual disease (MRD) and relapsed/refractory myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) are low-blast myeloid diseases for which there are few effective therapeutic options. CD123 represents an attractive target in these diseases. Vibecotamab is a bispecific antibody that binds to CD123 on malignant blasts and to CD3 on T-cells, to recognize and eliminate CD123-positive malignant cells.MethodsThis single-center phase II study evaluated the efficacy of vibecotamab in patients AML with detectable MRD (AML-MRD cohort) or with MDS or chronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML) after hypomethylating agent failure (MDS/CMML cohort). In cycle 1, patients received vibecotamab IV on day 1 (0.43 µg/kg), day 3 (0.75 µg/kg), day 5 (1.1 µg/kg), and days 8, 15 and 22 (1.7 µg/kg). In subsequent cycles, patients received vibecotamab IV on days 1, 8, 15, and 22 (1.7 µg/kg). The primary outcomes were MRD negativity rate (AML-MRD cohort) and overall response (MDS/CMML cohort).ResultsBetween May 2022 and April 2025, 48 patients were enrolled (21 AML-MRD cohort, 27 MDS/CMML cohort). The median ages of the AML-MRD and the MDS/CMML cohorts were 70 and 76 years, respectively. In the AML-MRD cohort, the median MRD level by flow cytometry was 0.64% (range, 0.1–3.9%), and in the MDS/CMML, the median bone marrow blast percentage was 7% (range, 3–19%). The AML-MRD clearance rate was 19% (4/21; 95% CI 5–42%), and in the MDS/CMML cohort, the overall response rate was 67% (18/27; 95% CI 46–83%). The median overall survival was 13.1 months (95% CI 8.9-NR) for the AML-MRD cohort and 6.5 months (95% CI 4.2–10.3) for the MDS/CMML cohort. The most frequent adverse event was infusion reaction or cytokine relapse syndrome, which occurred in 29 patients (60%) overall, most of which were grade 1–2.ConclusionsVibecotamab was active in low-blast myeloid diseases, although the durability of responses was modest. Additional studies of CD123-targeting bispecific antibodies, alone or in combination, are warranted for these diseases.Trial registrationClinicaltrials.gov (NCT05285813).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13045-026-01825-3.Keywords: Bispecific antibody, Immunotherapy, Acute myeloid leukemia, Myelodysplastic syndromes, Chronic myelomonocytic leukemia, Measurable residual disease, Clinical trial

Introduction

Myeloid neoplasms like myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) and acute myeloid leukemia (AML) originate from a malignant bone marrow stem cell, causing defective hematopoiesis and accumulation of immature progenitors in the bone marrow [1, 2]. Treatments for these diseases include intensive chemotherapy or low-intensity treatments, such as hypomethylating agents (HMAs), with the goal of reducing of the burden of neoplastic blasts and restoration of hematopoiesis [1, 3]. However, outcomes remain suboptimal in these diseases, and there is a need for innovative therapies.

In patients with AML who have achieved disease remission, measurable residual disease (MRD) in the bone marrow can be determined by various techniques, such as multiparameter flow cytometry (MFC) or polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for specific gene fusions or mutations [4]. MRD after AML treatment is a strong predictor of disease relapse and is associated with poor outcomes [5, 6]. There is growing evidence that treating AML at the MRD level before relapse leads to improved outcomes, and several strategies are being tested to eradicate AML MRD, although there is still no standard of care for MRD-positive AML [7–10].

Patients with higher-risk MDS or chronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML) usually receive first-line HMA therapy with azacitidine or decitabine, which is associated with relatively low response rates and short duration of response [11]. There is no standard of care for patients with MDS or CMML in whom remission after HMA cannot be achieved or maintained, and outcomes of these patients are poor, with a reported median overall survival (OS) of less than 6 months after HMA failure [12–14]. Several clinical trials have evaluated novel strategies in this setting, but no drug or combination is currently approved in this challenging clinical scenario.

The alpha subunit of IL-3 receptor (IL3RA, also known as CD123) is expressed in myeloid progenitors and is usually overexpressed in neoplastic myeloid cells [15]. This makes CD123 an attractive target to treat myeloid malignancies, including AML, MDS and CMML [16]. For example, tagraxofusp (formerly SL-401) is a CD123-directed cytotoxin that has been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for patients with blastic plasmacytoid dendritic cell neoplasm because of its potent antitumor activity, and studies are ongoing exploring this agent in other myeloid diseases [17]. Vibecotamab (formerly Xmab14045) is a humanized bispecific antibody that binds both CD3 and CD123 to recruit T-cells and facilitate cytotoxicity against CD123+ leukemic cells. In vivo and in vitro studies have shown dose-dependent activity of vibecotamab in CD123+ malignancies [18]. In a phase I, first-in-human, dose-escalation clinical trial, vibecotamab was shown to be safe and tolerable and showed anti-leukemic activity in relapsed or refractory AML [19]. In that study, the most frequent common adverse event was cytokine release syndrome (CRS)/infusion related reaction (IRR), occurring in 59.2% of patients and mostly grade 1–2. Responses to vibecotamab were observed in patients receiving a target dose ≥ 0.75 µg/kg (objective response rate of 11.5%), and in patients with low disease burden (blasts < 25% in bone marrow and peripheral blood). This observation led to the hypothesis that vibecotamab may benefit patients with myeloid diseases characterized by a low percentage of blasts, such as AML with detectable MRD or MDS/CMML.

This study reports the results of the phase II clinical trial evaluating vibecotamab in a cohort of patients with AML who achieved remission but had persistent or recurrent MRD and in a cohort of patients with MDS or CMML following HMA failure.

Methods

Study design and patient inclusion

This was a single-center, open-label phase II clinical trial (NCT05285813). The study had two arms: one for patients with MRD-positive AML (AML-MRD) and one for patients with MDS after HMA failure (MDS/CMML). Eligible patients in the AML-MRD cohort had AML in first or second morphologic remission (complete remission [CR], CR with incomplete hematologic recovery [CRi], or morphologic leukemia-free state [MLFS]) with persistent or recurrent MRD positivity detected by multiparametric flow cytometry at a level ≥ 0.1%. Eligible patients in the MDS/CMML cohort had MDS or chronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML) that was classified as intermediate, high, or very high risk by the Revised International Prognostic Scoring System (IPSS-R) [20] and who did not respond to at least four cycles of HMA or who had disease progression after having achieved remission with HMA. Other inclusion criteria common in both cohorts were age ≥ 18 years; performance status ≤ 2; adequate hepatic function, defined as total bilirubin less than one and a half times the upper limit of normal (ULN); alanine aminotransferase and aspartate aminotransferase less than three times the ULN; adequate renal function, defined as creatinine clearance of at least 30 mL/min; and CD123 expression ≥ 20% by MFC in the aberrant blast population. The full inclusion and exclusion criteria are available in the Study Protocol (Supplementary Material). All patients signed a written informed consent document. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center and was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study procedures

In cycle 1, patients were admitted in the hospital for toxicity monitoring and received vibecotamab as a 2-hour intravenous infusion on day 1 (0.43 µg/kg), day 3 (0.75 µg/kg), day 5 (1.1 µg/kg), and day 8 (1.7 µg/kg). This dose was based on the previously established safe dose from the phase I study [19]. Patients received pre-medication with steroids, acetaminophen, and diphenhydramine prior to each dose. On day 8, patients without grade 3 vibecotamab-related toxicity received the remaining doses of the first cycle on day 15 and 22 of the cycle in the outpatient setting (1.7 µg/kg each dose). In cycles 2–4, patients received vibecotamab (1.7 µg/kg) on days 1, 8, 15, and 22. Initially, patients received a maximum of 4 cycles of vibecotamab. However, an amendment of the original treatment protocol in November 2023 allowed patients enrolled after this date and who achieved remission within the first 4 cycles of therapy to receive repeated cycles of vibecotamab indefinitely until disease relapse. Patients #1–37 were treated with the initial study plan, and patients #38–48 were treated with the amended study plan. Toxicity was evaluated according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0.

Outcome definitions

The primary objective in the AML-MRD cohort was to determine the MRD negativity rate, defined as no detectable MRD by MFC; patients with core-binding factor AML were also required to have no detectable MRD by quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) for the corresponding gene fusion. The primary objective in the MDS/CMML cohort was to determine the overall response rate (ORR), defined as CR, marrow complete remission (mCR), partial remission, or hematologic improvement. Per-protocol responses of the MDS/CMML cohort were assessed using the 2006 modified International Working Group response criteria for MDS [21].

Secondary objectives were to assess safety of vibecotamab, remission duration, duration of MRD response, CR rate, relapse-free survival (RFS), and overall survival (OS). Remission duration was defined as the time from study entry (AML-MRD cohort) or response (MDS/CMML cohort) until morphologic disease relapse (censored for death in remission). Duration of MRD response was assessed in the AML-MRD cohort from time of documented MRD negativity until recurrence of detectable MRD or morphologic relapse. RFS was defined as the time from study entry (AML-MRD cohort) or response (MDS/CMML cohort) until morphologic disease relapse or death. OS was defined as the time from study entry to death from any cause.

Flow cytometry and genetic assessment

MRD assessment was performed using a 12 color MFC assay conducted under an FDA approved Investigational Device Exemption, with a sensitivity of 0.01 to 0.1%, depending on the events acquired for analysis and distinctness of leukemia associated immunophenotype (LAIP), corresponding to detection of 1 cell in 1,000 to 1 in 10,000. MFC MRD was analyzed using a combined LAIP and “different-from-normal” (DfN) strategy, and the assay had been rigorously validation [22, 23]. Assay sensitivity was established based on the overall assay performance rather than for each approach individually. The DfN approach incorporated recognition of reactive atypia and immunophenotypic patterns associated with persistent clonal hematopoiesis. LAIP was available in approximately 80% cases and when available, LAIP was integrated for final interpretation Specimens with negative results but suboptimal cell counts (< 200 000 events and < 200 CD34+ cells) were excluded. CD123-APC (clone 7G3) was used for CD123 assessment and expression was reported as % of expression of blasts, using an internal control. Additional information regarding MRD detection is detailed in Supplementary Material 1. Gene mutations were assessed using an 81-gene next-generation sequencing panel. The genetic coverage of this panel is detailed in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical methods

An initial six-patient safety lead-in was performed to confirm the treatment safety. If none or only one of the participants had a dose-limiting toxicity in the first cycle attributable to the treatment components, then the study would continue to enroll patients at the same dose. Interim monitoring rules for efficacy and toxicity were used throughout the study period. The study was continuously monitored for efficacy and treatment-related toxicities with Bayesian monitoring. The regimen was considered worthy of further investigation if it achieved an MRD-negativity rate of at least 30% (AML-MRD cohort) and an overall response rate of at least 30% (MDS/CMML cohort). The study was planned to enroll 80 total patients (40 in the AML-MRD cohort and 40 in the MDS/CMML cohort); however, the study was closed early due to lack of drug supply from the supporting company. The data cut off for this analysis was December 31, 2025. Baseline characteristics were analyzed using descriptive statistics. The Mann-Whitney U-test was used for comparison of continuous variables. For categorical variables, the chi-square and Fisher exact tests were used. Median follow-up time was calculated with the Kaplan-Meier estimate of potential follow-up [24]. OS, RFS, and remission duration were estimated with the Kaplan-Meier method. Calculation of the 95% CI for survival data was performed using the logarithmic transformation of the Greenwood’s formula. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R statistics version 4.4.1 (R core Team, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Viena, Austria).

Results

Baseline characteristics

In Fig. 1, a CONSORT flow diagram details the enrollment process and treatment arms of the study. Between May 2022 and April 2025, a total of 48 patients were treated with vibecotamab and evaluable for response (n = 21 in the AML-MRD cohort, n = 27 in the MDS/CMML cohort). The baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The median age was 70 years (range 24–86 years) in the AML-MRD cohort and 76 years (range 54–84 years) in the MDS/CMML cohort. Most patients in the AML-MRD cohort were adverse risk according to the ELN 2022 classification [25] (n = 16, 76%). All patients diagnosed with MDS were considered moderate high, high, or very high risk by the molecular International Prognostic Scoring System [26] (IPSS-M) and high or very high risk by the revised IPSS [20] (IPSS-R). Among the 3 patients diagnosed with CMML, 3 were intermediate-2 and one was high risk according to the CPSS-mol classification [27]. In patients in the AML-MRD cohort, the median MRD level by flow cytometry was 0.64% (range 0.1–3.9%). In the MDS/CMML cohort the median percentage of bone marrow blasts was 7 (range, 3–19%). CD123 expression in bone marrow blasts by flow cytometry was 86% (range 49–100%) in the AML-MRD cohort and 77.3% (range 39.7–99%) in the MDS/CMML cohort. Patients in the AML-MRD and MDS/CMML cohorts had a median of 2 (range 1–5) and 1 (range 1–4) prior therapies, respectively, before receiving vibecotamab. Nineteen patients (90%) in the AML-MRD cohort had received prior venetoclax.

Treatment responses

Responses are summarized in Table 2. In the AML-MRD cohort, four patients (19%) achieved MRD negativity. Patients who achieved MRD negativity had normal cytogenetics (n = 2), MECOM rearrangement (n = 1), and complex cytogenetics (n = 1). Additionally, one patient with CBFB::MYH11 rearrangement achieved MRD negativity by flow cytometry but showed persistent detectable disease by RT-qPCR and therefore did not meet protocol-specific criteria for MRD response. All 4 responses occurred after the first cycle of vibecotamab therapy. In a post hoc analysis, the median baseline MRD burden for patients who achieved MRD negativity was 0.2% (range 0.1–0.98%), and for patients who did not achieve MRD negativity was 0.71% (range 0.1–3.9%, P = 0.059). The median number of cycles of treatment received was 3 (range 1–4). Among the three patients in the AML-MRD cohort enrolled after the November 2023 amendment to allow for indefinite vibecotamab, none received more than 4 cycles. Reasons for treatment discontinuation in the AML-MRD cohort were AML relapse (n = 11, 52%), therapy completion (n = 7, 33%), and no response (n = 3, 14%). Among the patients who relapsed, the median percentage of CD123+ cells at baseline and at AML relapse was 86% (range 49–100%) and 85% (range 0–100%), respectively. One patient was CD123-negative at relapse. Additional information regarding responses and dynamics of MRD and CD123 expression is detailed in Fig. 3.

In the MDS/CMML cohort, the ORR was 67% (18 of 27 patients), with 17 (63%) patients achieving mCR (8 of whom also achieved hematologic improvement) and one patient (4%) achieving hematologic improvement as best response. In a post hoc analysis using the IWG 202328 response criteria (which were not yet published at the time of the trial conception), the rate of CR + CR with limited count recovery (CRL) was 48%, with one patient (4%) achieving CR and 12 (44%) patients achieving CRL. The median number of cycles of treatment to best response was 1 (range 1–2), and 16 (59%) patients achieved best response after cycle 1. The median number of cycles received was 4 (range 1–10). After the study protocol amendment in November 2023, 2 out of 8 (25%) patients received more than 4 cycles of treatment (5 and 10 cycles, respectively). In patients with a TP53 mutation, the ORR was 60% (9 of 15 patients). In a post hoc analysis comparing patients who responded with those who did not, there was no difference in the percentage of CD123-positive cells at baseline (median 72% versus 85%, respectively; P = 0.17) or baseline bone marrow blast percentage (median 7% in both groups, P = 0.66). The reasons for treatment discontinuation in the MDS/CMML cohort were therapy completion (n = 11, 41%), no response (n = 7, 26%), disease relapse (n = 6, 22%), undergoing hematopoietic stem cell transplant (n = 1, 4%), patient decision (n = 1, 4%), and death (n = 1, 4%). The median percentage of CD123+ cells at baseline was 77% (range 40–99%), and at MDS relapse or AML transformation was 72% (range 0–100%). One patient was CD123-negative at AML transformation. Off protocol therapies clinical outcomes following study discontinuation are detailed in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3.

Time-to-event outcomes

The median follow-up time of the entire cohort was 30.2 months (95% CI 23.9-not reached [NR]); 28 months (95% CI 23.9-NR) for the AML-MRD cohort and 30.2 months (95% CI 14.6-NR) for the MDS/CMML cohort. Among the 4 patients from the AML-MRD cohort who achieved MRD negativity, 2 remained MRD-negative at last follow-up (42.4 months and 41.8 months from response, respectively), one had MRD recurrence and AML relapse after 3 and 9 months from response to vibecotamab, respectively, and one had extramedullary AML relapse (breast and central nervous system) without preceding MRD relapse 4 months after initial response. In the MDS/CMML cohort, the median duration of remission was 2.9 months (95% CI 2.8–9.2 months). Individual patient trajectories over time are detailed in Fig. 4.

The median OS of patients in the AML-MRD cohort was 13.1 months (95% CI 8.8-NR), and the 1-year OS rate was 52.6% (95% CI 34.1–81.1). The median RFS of patients in the AML-MRD cohort was 5.0 months (95% CI 2.9-NR), and the 1-year RFS rate was 18.1% (95% CI 6.5–50). The median OS of patients in the MDS/CMML cohort was 6.6 months (95% CI 4.2–10.3), and the 1-year OS rate was 16.3% (95% CI 6.7–40). Safety and toxicity

The 4- and 8-week mortality rates were both 0% in the AML-MRD cohort and were 3.7% and 4.8%, respectively, in the MDS/CMML cohort. One patient of the MDS/CMML cohort who was 77 years of age experienced a sudden death at home on day 25 of the first cycle, and the exact cause of death was unknown. There were no other on-study deaths. Adverse events are summarized in Supplementary Table 4. Myelosuppression was minimal, and among patients with grade < 1 cytopenias at the time of study entry, treatment-emergent grade 3–4 neutropenia, anemia, and thrombocytopenia occurred in 20% (3/15), 43% (3/7), and 42% (5/12), respectively. The most frequent adverse events were IRR and CRS, which occurred in 29 patients (60%) overall. CRS occurred in 20 (42%) patients, with 19 of the CRS events being grade 1–2. One patient had CRS grade 3 on day 1 of the first cycle; this patient experienced fever and hypotension that required vasopressor support and resolved in less than 24 h. IRR occurred in 17 (40%) patients, and all IRR events were grade 1–2. Among 50 total IRR/CRS episodes, 35 (70%) occurred during the first cycle (usually during the ramp-up period [n = 27]) and 15 (30%) occurred on cycle 2 or beyond. The other most common adverse events of any grade were ALT increase (n = 27, 56%), AST increase (n = 21, 44%) and limb edema (n = 18, 38%). No patient discontinued therapy due to treatment-related toxicity.

Discussion

In this report we detail the results of a phase II clinical trial of vibecotamab, a CD3-CD123 bispecific antibody, in two different myeloid diseases with low-disease burden: AML in MRD-positive remission, and MDS/CMML. Treatment with vibecotamab was shown to be safe, with transient infusion-related reactions being the most common treatment-emergent toxicity. Vibecotamab did not meet the pre-specified MRD-negativity threshold (30%) in patients with MRD-positive AML but achieved high rates of response in patients with MDS/CMML, with most responses consisting of mCR (i.e. blast reduction), with or without hematologic improvement. Overall, these findings support the continued development of immunotherapies targeting CD123 in myeloid disorders, although given the limited durability of responses observed in this study, there is still much room for improvement to achieve better outcomes in these diseases.

There is no standard of care for MDS after HMA failure, which is associated with poor outcomes, with a median OS of 6–8 months in patients with higher-risk disease, similar to most of the patients enrolled in this study [12, 13, 29]. Vibecotamab showed clinical activity in this population, with an ORR of 67%, which compares favorably to other clinical trials in patients with MDS after HMA failure, such as azacitidine with venetoclax (ORR 49%), guadecitabine (ORR 14%), or eltanexor (53%) [30–32]. Notably, the MDS/CMML cohort in the current study had 50% of high-risk patients with TP53 mutations, which are known to be highly resistant to standard therapies [33]. In a post hoc analysis of patients treated with flotetuzumab (CD123-CD3 dual-affinity re-targeting [DART] antibody) [34], patients with TP53-mutated R/R AML had significantly higher T-cell infiltration, immune checkpoint infiltration, and interferon-γ signaling, which correlated with disease control with flotetuzumab. In the current study, responses to vibecotamab in the MDS/CMML cohort were predominantly mCR, with no CRs observed, likely reflecting the ability of the drug to effectively reduce blast percentage but without reversing the underlying hematopoietic dysfunction. Moreover, these responses were largely transient, resulting in short RFS (median RFS of 3.2 months) and no clear benefit in OS (median OS of 6.5 months) as compared with historical expectations in this population. Translational studies are planned to better understand the T-cell function in these patients and the possible relationship with vibecotamab efficacy.

In AML, persistent or recurrent MRD remains one of the most important predictive factors for disease relapse, which is associated with poor outcomes [6]. For this reason, detecting and targeting MRD has attracted considerable interest in recent years. In B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia, single-agent blinatumomab (CD3-CD19 bispecific antibody) is effective at eradicating MRD, with some patients having durable remissions without consolidative allogeneic stem cell transplantion [35, 36]. Given these findings, there is a rationale to target low-level residual leukemic cells with an analogous strategy in AML. However, responses to vibecotamab in the current study were modest (only 19% achieved MRD clearance), with 2 of the 4 responders experiencing AML relapse after a short period of remission. Notably, no relevant changes in CD123 expression were detected between baseline and AML relapse (with similar findings in the MDS/CMML cohort), suggesting that antigen escape was not a major mechanism of secondary resistance with vibecotamab. It is well-documented that AML has an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, mostly driven by regulatory T cells and myeloid-derived suppressor cells, among others [37, 38]. This immunosuppressive environment of the bone marrow microenvironment, in combination with the inherent resistance of MRD leukemic cells to immune-mediated killing, may have driven the relatively low efficacy of vibecotamab in MRD-positive AML.

Overall, vibecotamab was well tolerated, and no new safety signals were detected. The most common adverse effect was infusion reaction, which was expected based on the previous results of the dose-finding vibecotamab phase I clinical trial [19]. Most patients experienced mild infusion reactions, as are commonly observed in other antibody-based therapies. Targeting CD123 can be challenging due to on-target/off-tumor toxicities such as myelosuppression or endothelial damage, which has been reported with previous CD123-directed immunotherapies [39–41]. In the current study, no capillary leak syndrome was detected, and grade 3–4 myelosuppression was minimal, with cytopenias often related to persistent disease in the bone marrow rather than direct toxicity from vibecotamab.

Immunotherapy (excluding hematopoietic stem cell transplantation) is still in its infancy in myeloid malignancies, compared with other successful results in hematologic neoplasms such as B-cell leukemias/lymphomas and multiple myeloma. Several investigational immunotherapies have shown encouraging activity in targeting specific myeloid antigens (such as CD123 or CD33), though only gemtuzumab ozogamicin is currently approved for use in AML, and no targeted immunotherapies are approved for the treatment of MDS or CMML. Flotetuzumab, a CD123-CD3 DART antibody, shares biological similarities with vibecotamab and has shown activity in primary refractory or early relapsed AML (ORR of 24–30%)42. Nonetheless, this drug is no longer in clinical development, likely due to insufficient activity to aim for drug approval. Similarly, vibecotamab has shown activity, especially in R/R MDS, but its activity as monotherapy is suboptimal to pursue late-phase clinical trials. The immune dysregulation associated with myeloid malignancies likely hinders the effectiveness of single-agent targeted immunotherapy [38].

Conclusions

In summary, vibecotamab did not meet the pre-specified efficacy threshold in MRD-positive AML; however, achieved a high ORR in MDS/CMML after HMA failure, mostly due to bone marrow blast reduction. Our results support the further development of CD123-targeted immunotherapeutic approaches in these diseases. Novel strategies will be needed to overcome the dysregulated immune system in these myeloid malignancies and to develop new immunotherapies that will lead to more durable responses.
