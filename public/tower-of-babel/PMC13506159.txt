Impact of a Visualization Training Platform on PICU Nurses' Pain Assessment Skill in Critically Ill Children: A Mixed‐Methods Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Xinyi Rong, Weijia Fu, Yun Jin, Yuxia Yang, Weijie Shen, Yulu Xu, Yan Qin, Xiaobo Zhang, Ying Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsPain is common and challenging to assess in the paediatric intensive care unit (PICU), highlighting the need for targeted training. We previously developed PainScan, a visualization‐based training platform and aimed to investigate the efficacy of PainScan in improving PICU nurses' pain assessment competencies and to describe their learning experience with PainScan.DesignThis study employed a mixed‐methods design, integrating a superiority, randomized parallel controlled trial with embedded qualitative research. The RCT component of this study was prospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06431802) prior to participant enrollment.MethodsEligible PICU nurses from a tertiary children's hospital in Shanghai, China, took part in the trial. 70 PICU nurses were recruited and randomly assigned to either the PainScan training group or the on‐site training group using stratified randomization, with 35 individuals in each group, over 4 weeks. Primary outcomes were PICU nurses' knowledge and skill levels in pain assessment. Outcome assessors were blinded to group allocation. Following the training, a descriptive qualitative study was conducted using semi‐structured interviews with 11 PICU nurses, who had participated in PainScan training, to gain insights into their learning experience.ResultsA total of 69 participants (35 in the PainScan group, 34 in the on‐site training group) were included in the primary outcome analysis, as one participant (1.4%) in the on‐site training group withdrew due to a rotation change. Both PainScan training and on‐site training significantly improved PICU nurses' knowledge (PainScan: t = 10.249, on‐site: t = 10.592, both p < 0.001) and skill levels in pain assessment (PainScan: Z = −5.138, on‐site: Z = −4.847, both p < 0.001). Compared to traditional on‐site training, the PainScan showed superior skill development (Z = −3.644, p < 0.001). Qualitative findings revealed that the platform: (1) competency enhancement in pain assessment through PainScan training; (2) advantages of the digital learning platform; and (3) limitations of the PainScan training.ConclusionPainScan effectively enhanced PICU nurses' pain assessment competencies. It also enriched their learning experience through a realistic, immersive and flexible approach. However, improvements are needed in supervision, engagement and scalability.Reporting MethodsThe randomized controlled trial was reported according to CONSORT 2025, and the qualitative component was reported following COREQ.Patient or Public ContributionThis study shows that PainScan is a practical and cost‐effective training tool for PICU nurses. It can enhance pain assessment skills with flexible learning. Addressing feedback on supervision and engagement could further optimize its use in clinical practice to improve patient care.Keywords: nursing education, paediatric critical care nurse, pain assessment, training platform, visualization

Introduction

Pain is a prevalent and distressing experiences in paediatric intensive care unit (PICU), exerting both short‐term and long‐term negative impact on children's health outcomes (Akgün and İnal 2022). Effective pain assessment enables PICU nurses accurately identify pain characteristics and severity, thereby facilitating appropriate interventions (Trottier et al. 2022; Zieliński et al. 2020). However, current evidence reveals significant deficiencies in paediatric nurses' pain assessment competencies (Laures et al. 2023; Tsuboi et al. 2023). A recent study by Tsuboi et al. (Tsuboi et al. 2023) identified knowledge gaps and assessment difficulties as key barriers, with over 90% of nurses reporting challenges in assessing pain in preverbal or cognitively impaired children, and only one‐third expressing confidence in their assessment skills. These findings emphasize the imperative for comprehensive training programs to enhance nursing competencies in this essential aspect of critical care.

Accurate pain assessment in critically ill children requires not only knowledge of pain assessment principles but also the ability to interpret multimodal clinical cues and make appropriate assessment decisions in complex clinical situations. Educational strategies such as theoretical learning, visualization, case‐based assessment and deliberate practice have each been shown to improve learners' knowledge, clinical reasoning and practical skills in healthcare education (Aboudonya et al. 2025; Adelgais et al. 2021). However, existing pain assessment training programs often employ isolated educational strategies, which may be insufficient to optimize both knowledge acquisition and skill development. By following a bundle logic, integrating multiple complementary educational components into a structured training program may create synergistic learning effects (Sebastiani et al. 2024). Therefore, in our prior work, we developed PainScan, a visualization‐based training platform for pain assessment in critically ill children, leveraging a multimodal standardized dataset. While preliminary studies have established its usability, a systematic evaluation of its effectiveness remains necessary.

As a classical approach, on‐site training has been proven the effectiveness in enhancing theoretical knowledge (Wu et al. 2017), particularly for timely acquisition (Sarayani et al. 2015). However, its limited opportunities for hands‐on practice may restrict the development of applied clinical competencies. Recent advancements in e‐learning, virtual reality and artificial intelligence have enabled more immersive, flexible and personalized training approaches (Alvarez et al. 2017). Although some studies compared digital platforms with conventional training methods, findings on their superiority remain inconsistent (Liu et al. 2025; Sucu Çakmak and Çalışkan 2024). Thus, further investigation is needed to determine whether PainScan offers significant advantages over on‐site training.

Moreover, relying solely on quantitative outcomes may fail to capture the full scope of the platform's impact (Lewin et al. 2009). Integrating qualitative methods can provide deeper insights into PICU nurses' training experiences (Midgley et al. 2014; Plano Clark et al. 2013), informing future refinements and facilitating broader implementation.

This mixed‐methods study was designed to: (1) evaluate the efficacy of PainScan compared with traditional on‐site training in improving PICU nurses' pain assessment knowledge and skills at 4 weeks post‐training through a randomized controlled trial; and (2) qualitatively examine nurses' training experience with the platform. By integrating quantitative and qualitative approaches, this investigation provides a comprehensive evaluation of PainScan's effectiveness while generating actionable insights for future platform optimization and clinical implementation. No harms or adverse events were anticipated as PainScan is a non‐invasive digital educational intervention; therefore, no systematic harms assessment was performed.

Methodology

Study Design

The primary component was a superiority randomized controlled trial (RCT) with 1:1 parallel group allocation comparing PainScan against on‐site training methods. The RCT quantitatively evaluated improvements in PICU nurses' competencies, including both knowledge acquisition and practical skill development. Following the quantitative evaluation, we conducted an embedded descriptive qualitative study to explore the intervention group's experiential perspectives on PainScan training. This integrated design allowed for both objective measurement of outcomes and in‐depth understanding of user experiences. The randomized controlled trial component of this study was reported in accordance with the updated guidelines for reporting parallel group randomized trials (CONSORT 2025) (Hopewell et al. 2025). The embedded qualitative component was reported following the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) (Tong et al. 2007).

Study Participants

Participants were PICU nurses recruited between July and September 2024. The inclusion criteria were: (1) possession of a valid Nurse Practitioner Certificate; (2) at least 3 months of clinical experience in the PICU; and (3) voluntary consent to participate. Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) nurses absent during the study period due to training, rotation, sick leave, maternity leave; (2) temporary staff assigned to the PICU for training, rotation, or clinical practice; (3) failure to complete the study procedures or voluntarily withdrew. All eligible PICU nurses during the recruitment period were consecutively invited to participate.

Sample size was calculated using the formula for comparing the means between two independent groups (Ni et al. 2010). Preliminary data from pilot study (12 PICU nurses per group) indicated a mean difference (δ) of 1.17 and a standard deviation (SD) of 1.414 in the total scores of the standard case assessment questionnaire. Assuming a two‐sided alpha of 0.05, 90% power, a 1:1 allocation ratio and a 15% attrition rate, the required sample size was 70 participants (35 per group). No interim analyses were performed, and no stopping guidelines were applied. Additionally, a purposive sample of nurses from the PainScan training group was selected for qualitative interviews, which continued until thematic saturation.

Randomization and Blinding

Following informed consent and baseline assessments, participants were randomized in a 1:1 ratio using stratified block randomization, with stratification based on nursing competency levels (N0‐N4). Within each stratum, simple randomization was conducted by generating and sorting non‐repetitive random numbers in Microsoft Excel. Participants ranked in the lower 50% of numbers were assigned to the PainScan training group, and the upper 50% were allocated to the on‐site training group.

An independent research assistant, uninvolved in recruitment or intervention delivery, conducted randomization and prepared sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes. These were opened by the researcher 1 day before intervention to inform participants of their allocation.

Due to the nature of the interventions (PainScan vs. on‐site training), blinding of participants and personnel was not feasible. However, assessors were blinded to group allocation and underwent standardized training by the researcher prior to data collection.

Study Setting

This study was conducted at the Paediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) of a tertiary children's hospital, which serves as one of the national paediatric medical centers in Shanghai, China. The Department of Critical Care Medicine, designated as a national key clinical specialty, operates 50 beds and admits approximately 1800–2000 critically ill children annually. PICU nurses in this unit perform more than 30,000 pain assessments per year.

Interventions

PainScan Training Group

The PainScan training was designed following a bundle logic. A bundle consists of three to five evidence‐based interventions, behaviours, or practices tailored to a specific target population and context that, when implemented together, improve outcomes more effectively than individual elements alone (Sebastiani et al. 2024). Following this logic, PainScan integrated four components: (1) theoretical learning, (2) case‐based practice, (3) visualization and (4) deliberate practice. Operationally, PICU nurses in the PainScan training group completed a four‐week training program, which consisted of two core modules: the Learning Materials Module (delivering component 1) and the Pain Assessment Training Module (delivering components 2–4) (Figure 1). Nurses were required to finish designated tasks weekly.

Core components of the PainScan.

Learning Materials Module

This module provided foundational knowledge through two chapters with nine subsections, combining graphical and textual materials: (1) Chapter 1: fundamentals of pain, including definition, types, physiological mechanisms, clinical impacts and manifestations. (2) Chapter 2: Pain assessment, which addressed the rationale for assessment, techniques for obtaining pain history, assessment tools and recommended frequency of evaluation.

Pain Assessment Training Module

This module was designed according to deliberate practice theory, which posits that skill improvement depends on task‐relevant repetition coupled with feedback (Ericsson 2004). Accordingly, the module included 104 standardized cases derived from a multimodal standardized dataset (Jiang et al. 2025). This case volume is not an extraneous ‘dose’ but rather an operational necessity of deliberate practice, as the theory requires ample opportunities for repeated engagement with the same or similar tasks to enable gradual refinement of performance (Ericsson 2004). These cases were selected from the real‐world ICU settings (e.g., Paediatric Intensive Care Unit, Cardiac Intensive Care Unit and Post‐Anaesthesia Care Unit) and represented as: (1) diverse scenarios: postoperative monitor, routine procedures (debridement, injections and suctioning). (2) clinical variability: balanced distribution of pain severity (none 25%, mild 25%, moderate 25%, severe 25%), age groups (2 months to 3 years, 3 to 7 years, > 7 years) and assessment methods (observer‐rated or self‐report). (3) multimodal data: case profiles (age, gender, diagnosis), physiological parameters (heart rate, respiratory rate, SpO2) and visual aids (images and videos). Cases were divided into four exercises (26 cases each). Nurses accessed each exercise via a ‘Go to complete’ button, with cases presented randomly. Immediate feedback, including explanations for incorrect responses, was provided. Ten cases were reserved for post‐intervention skill evaluation.

During the four‐week intervention, PICU nurses followed a structured timeline: In week 1, they completed the entire Learning Materials Module alongside the first exercise (26 cases) in the Pain Assessment Training Module. For Week 2 to 4, nurses progressively completed one remaining exercise per week (Exercise 2–4) at their own pace, while adhering to the weekly completion deadlines. PICU nurses accessed PainScan using individualized login credentials, allowing flexible scheduling within each week's designated timeframe at their convenience.

On‐Site Training Group

PICU nurses in the on‐site training group received a four‐week in person training program consisting of weekly 15–30 min sessions (Table 1). The trainer was a senior PICU nurse with over 20 years of clinical experience, formally trained in pain assessment and served as a core member of the hospital's pain management team.

Measures to Minimize Information Exchange Between Groups

To reduce the risk of informal information contamination during the study period, the following measures were implemented.

First, the PainScan platform was accessible only to nurses in the intervention group via individually assigned accounts; control group nurses could not register or access the platform independently.

Second, all participants were explicitly instructed at the beginning of the study and reminded weekly not to discuss the training content with colleagues in the other group.

Despite these measures, some degree of residual contamination cannot be completely ruled out, as acknowledged in the Limitations section.

Measures

The primary outcomes were PICU nurses' knowledge and skill levels in pain assessment. No secondary outcomes were prespecified for the quantitative RCT.

PICU Nurse Pain Assessment Knowledge Questionnaire

Due to the limited availability of instruments for assessing PICU nurses' pain assessment knowledge in the Chinese context, we developed the PICU Nurse Pain Assessment Knowledge Questionnaire (pNPAK). The instrument achieved perfect content validity, with both item‐level (I‐CVI) and scale‐level (S‐CVI/Ave) content validity index reaching 1.0, supported by an expert authority coefficient of 0.875. The final 25‐item questionnaire consists of 20 single‐choice and 5 multiple‐choice items organized into two domains: pain fundamental (items 1–9) and pain assessment (items 10–25). The pNPAK uses a 4‐point scoring system per item (total score range: 0–100) and no partial credit is awarded for incorrect multiple‐choice responses. Higher total scores indicated greater pain assessment knowledge. Known‐groups comparison using baseline data demonstrated that total knowledge scores differed significantly across educational levels (one‐way ANOVA, F = 7.810, p < 0.001).

Standard Cases Assessment Questionnaire

The Standard Cases Assessment Questionnaire (SCA) was designed as a performance‐based evaluation tool to measure PICU nurses' clinical pain assessment competencies. The instrument featured 10 standard clinical cases incorporating multimodal patient data, including detailed case narratives, physiological parameters, images and videos. For each case, participants were required to demonstrate comprehensive assessment skill by documenting: (1) appropriate assessment methods, (2) correct tool selection, (3) accurate score interpretation and (4) proper pain intensity classification, except for self‐report cases where neither pain score documentation nor pain intensity classification was required. The SCA employed a binary scoring system, awarding 1 point for each completely accurate case assessment based on predetermined correct answers that had been validated by the research team. Items (1), (2) and (4) have single, unambiguous correct answers. For item (3), a response was considered correct if the pain score fell within the predefined clinically acceptable range (0 = no pain, 1–3 = mild, 4–6 = moderate, 7–10 = severe). To assess the objectivity of the SCA scoring process, 20 SCA questionnaires were randomly selected from the baseline sample (N = 70). A second researcher, who was not involved in the study, independently rescored these questionnaires using the same standardized scoring rubric. The agreement rate between the two raters was 100% (Kappa = 1.0), indicating excellent inter‐rater reliability. Higher total scores indicate superior clinical competency in pain assessment.

Data Collection

Quantitative Data Collection

Before and after training, participants were required to complete two questionnaires: the PICU Nurse Pain Assessment Knowledge Questionnaire (pNPAK) and the Standardized Case Assessment Questionnaire (SCA). Nurses independently completed pNPAK within a 35‐min time frame. SCA were presented via PowerPoint, with each slide displaying one case. Nurses independently completed the corresponding assessment questionnaire in a designed private room to ensure an undisturbed environment.

Qualitative Data Collection

Following the training, semi‐structured face‐to‐face interviews were conducted with nurses in the PainScan training group. All interviews were conducted by one author (blinded for review), who has a nursing background and experience in qualitative interviewing. Each interview lasted 20–30 min, took place in a private conference room with only the interviewer and the interviewee present, ensuring confidentiality and minimizing distractions. The interview guide was developed and refined after pilot testing with two participants (Table 2). For the ethical considerations, written informed consent was obtained prior to audio recording. The researcher observed and documented non‐verbal cues during the interviews to enrich the qualitative analysis. Within 24 h of each interview, the researcher transcribed the audio recordings, compiled field notes and anonymized participants using unique codes. Data collection continued until data saturation was achieved, meaning no new themes emerged from the interviews.

Data Analysis

Quantitative Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 20.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics, Chi‐square test/Fisher's exact test, t‐test (for both dependent and independent samples), Mann–Whitney U test and Wilcoxon rank sum test for dependent samples were used. A two‐tailed alpha level of 0.05 was established as the threshold for statistical significance. One participant in the control group had missing outcome data (withdrawal) and was excluded. No imputation, subgroup, or sensitivity analyses were conducted.

Embedded Qualitative Analysis

Qualitative data were analysed using thematic analysis, supported by NVivo software (version 20). Researchers thoroughly reviewed the interview transcripts to get familiar with the raw data. Initial codes were generated and iteratively organized into themes. To ensure accuracy, a second researcher independently reviewed and verified the emerging themes, sub‐themes and coding framework. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion until consensus was achieved.

Ethical Considerations

The study protocol received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee (Approval No. [2023]319). The RCT component was prospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06431802; https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06431802) on 29 May 2024 before participant enrollment, where the study plan is available. No patient or public involvement occurred in this trial.

Results

Participants were recruited in July 2024. The training period lasted 4 weeks, and the final follow‐up assessment was completed in September 2024 (after training). The follow‐up duration was 4 weeks for all participants. PainScan was self‐administered online; all 35 participants completed the weekly tasks on time, as confirmed by platform analytics. The on‐site training was delivered by a senior PICU nurse educator, with 34 participants attending all sessions on time. No concomitant interventions relevant to the study outcomes were received in either group. No adverse events or unintended effects were reported in either group. No deaths occurred and no participants withdrew due to harms.

Qualitative Findings

Three primary themes emerged from the thematic analysis: (1) competency enhancement in pain assessment through PainScan training; (2) advantages of the digital learning platform; and (3) limitations of the PainScan training.

Theme 1. Competency Enhancement in Pain Assessment Through Painscan Training

Participants reported significant improvements in both theoretical understanding and practical application of pain assessment. PainScan integrated theoretical instruction with deliberate case‐based practice to reinforce knowledge. This dual approach was particularly valued:

PainScan is terrific! It provides comprehensive theoretical coverage along with a wide range of case practices. This way, we can quickly move from learning to applying what we've learned. (I 11)

After learning the theoretical knowledge, we can immediately engage in case practice, which helps deepen our memory. (I4)

The iterative practice design with real‐time corrective feedback demonstrated particular effectiveness:

During practice, if I made a mistake, the platform immediately provided detailed explanations, enabling me to correct my errors and clearly understand the key points to observe. (I11)

The focused practice on various types of cases has enhanced my proficiency in pain assessment. (I5)

PainScan compiles 104 real‐world clinical cases, vividly illustrate various pain manifestations of critically ill children, providing a more concrete and standardized reference for pain scoring.

What impressed me most was the illustration of ‘quivering chin’. It intuitively showed me exactly what it looked like. Before the training, pain scoring was relatively subjective. My ‘two points’ (referring to pain score) might not match others. After the training, everyone's scoring criteria have become much more comparable. (I6)

Theme 2. Advantages of the Digital Learning Platform

PainScan provided a free, convenient, well‐organized and personalized training method. Participants highlighted four key advantages of the PainScan platform: accessibility, flexibility, personalized learning and resource efficiency. The platform's digital format enabled self‐paced learning through individualized accounts:

Unlike traditional on‐site courses with fixed schedules, PainScan allows flexible timing and location‐independent access. Learning could be flexibly scheduled, and unclear parts can be easily replayed, making it very convenient. (I1)

The self‐directed learning design promoted active engagement:

Traditional courses often failed to maintain consistent learner engagement, but PainScan's self‐directed learning ensured learners to complete the learning process. (I11)

PainScan's individualized learning adapts to each person's preferences and ensures active engagement. (I7)

Participants particularly valued the reusable digital resources:

Traditional training methods, like nursing rounds, are effective but time‐consuming and labor‐intensive. PainScan's reusable resources eliminate the need for repeated organization, saving time and resources. (I7)

You can log in and study anytime, anywhere, and review forgotten content easily. (I10)

Theme 3. Limitations of the PainScan Training

Three key limitations were identified regarding the platform's educational design: The self‐directed, four‐week training format presented supervision and motivation challenges compared to traditional face‐to‐face instruction.

Group learning creates a better atmosphere and stronger motivation, while individual learning requires more self‐motivation to avoid losing interest. (I8)

Participants revealed suboptimal engagement with the Learning Materials Module's didactic content. More interactive formats were suggested to improve learner's motivation, comprehension and long‐term retention.

Too many words and concepts really make me lose interest. If the theoretical knowledge were presented in some way, such as popular science videos, we may show more learning interests. (I3)

Participants highlighted a critical gap in the training content. Pain management comprises two integral components‐assessment and intervention, which are fundamentally interrelated. It is suggested to add content on pain interventions to create a more comprehensive training.

PainScan only covers pain assessment. But what if a child needs intervention after we identify pain. (I2)

What we really need is to accurately assess pain and then intervene effectively. This training focused mostly on assessment, but lacked intervention content. (I7)

Discussion

This study evaluated the effectiveness of PainScan in improving PICU nurses' pain assessment knowledge and skill and explored their learning experiences. Both interventions resulted in significant improvements; however, PainScan demonstrated a more pronounced impact on skill enhancement. Qualitative interviews revealed that PainScan was particularly effective in visualizing complex paediatric pain manifestations within real clinical contexts. Additionally, nurses highly valued its flexibility, convenience and personalized learning features.

PainScan provided an online case‐based training with multimodal resources designed to enhance PICU nurses' ability to recognize and assess complex pain presentations in critically ill children. The system supported up to 300 users with 24/7 accessibility, featuring reusable content and individual account access to facilitate efficient training organization and progress tracking. Consistent with prior research (Liu et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2020), participants valued the platform's flexibility, which eliminated travel requirements and enabled self‐paced, on‐demand learning. While the initial costs were substantial, economies of scale through increased utilization and extended training cycles could potentially reduce long‐term expenditures.

Visualization, case‐based assessment and deliberate practice, have been successfully applied in multiple medical education contexts, with each component leading to significant improvements in learners' practical skills (Adelgais et al. 2021; Davis et al. 2020; Kwan et al. 2020). Theoretical learning has also been shown to facilitate the acquisition of both knowledge and practical skills by providing learners with the conceptual foundation necessary for effective practice (Aboudonya et al. 2025). Following the bundle logic (Sebastiani et al. 2024), integrating these components into a coherent educational bundle may produce synergistic effects on clinical skill acquisition.

Conceptualizing PainScan as a bundle‐type educational intervention may have important implications for its implementation and scalability. PainScan integrates four clearly defined educational components—theoretical instruction, visualization, case‐based assessment and deliberate practice—into a standardized training package. The explicit specification of these components may facilitate consistent delivery across settings and support standardized implementation (Carroll et al. 2007). Previous research has demonstrated that increasing complexity of bundle elements is associated with worsening compliance (Gilhooly et al. 2019). PainScan includes a moderate number of clearly defined components. This level of complexity is unlikely to pose a major barrier to compliance, supporting its potential for high implementation fidelity across different settings. It should be noted that PainScan requires each participant to access via personal devices, which may present challenges related to device availability and internet access. Successful implementation also requires coordination of institutional resources, including technical assistance for the online platform. These factors should be considered when scaling up the program across settings.

Despite demonstrating significant improvements in pain assessment skill, PainScan did not yield superior knowledge gains compared to traditional training methods. The PainScan bundle more emphasized active, repetitive engagement with visualization, case‐based assessment and deliberate practice, which directly targets the cognitive and procedural steps involved in clinical pain assessment. In contrast, the knowledge test primarily assessed factual recall from the learning materials module, which involved self‐directed reading of learning materials without repeated reinforcement, feedback, or spaced practice. Additionally, participants identified two key limitations: (1) the absence of interaction with instructors and (2) insufficient supervision during the learning process. PainScan required nurses to complete the four‐week curriculum independently, demanding considerable self‐discipline. For some learners, this autonomous format proved challenging, resulting in missed sessions and diminished training efficacy. In contrast, conventional on‐site training remains more familiar and accessible to PICU nurses (Xue et al. 2021). The face‐to‐face format offers distinct advantages, including immediate feedback and opportunities for interactive discussion, which promote timely clarification of uncertainties (Bucăţa and Babos 2023). Although constrained by temporal and geographic factors, in‐person training provides more structured learning management. Thus, while PainScan effectively supported knowledge acquisition, its comparative benefits relative to traditional on‐site training modalities were not conclusively established in this study.

An important question is whether the skill gains observed in PainScan translate to real‐world clinical performance. Several features support positive transfer. The 104 cases were derived from a multimodal dataset of real ICU patients, covering diverse clinical scenarios, pain severities, age groups and assessment methods. What is learned during training is directly applicable to bedside assessment. Through deliberate practice, the case‐based practice in the pain assessment training module—closely aligned with the steps of bedside pain assessment—can improve both the standardization of the assessment process and the level of assessment skills.

However, important differences between training and clinical practice should be acknowledged. Unlike real clinical settings, PainScan provides immediate diagnostic feedback after each case, which is typically unavailable in clinical practice. While this may enhance learning efficiency, it also raises a potential concern: learners may develop feedback dependence (Maslovat et al. 2009), becoming accustomed to instant confirmation and struggling when such feedback is absent in clinical practice. Furthermore, bedside assessment is inherently dynamic, with patient conditions often fluctuating, whereas PainScan relies on video clips. The number of training cases, although substantial (104 cases), remains limited relative to the infinite variability of real‐world clinical situations. Therefore, PainScan is best viewed as a complementary tool to bedside learning, rather than a replacement.

Limitations

This study had several limitations.

First, because all participants worked within the same PICU at a tertiary children's hospital in Shanghai, the possibility of unintentional information exchange between the intervention and control groups could not be completely eliminated, which may have introduced some bias toward the null or overestimation of the intervention effect.

Second, the single‐center design and the specific educational and professional background of the participants limit the generalizability of the findings. The study was conducted in one tertiary hospital in Shanghai, and participants generally had relatively high educational levels and homogeneous professional backgrounds. Therefore, the results may not be directly transferable to other hospitals, regions, or countries with different organizational, educational, or cultural settings, or to nursing populations with lower or more varied levels of education.

Third, the intervention group received a deliberately higher volume of case‐based training (104 cases) as an operational requirement of deliberate practice theory, whereas the control group followed a conventional curriculum (8 cases). Therefore, the observed superiority of the PainScan platform should be interpreted as the effectiveness of an integrated training package, rather than as the isolated effect of visualization or case volume alone.

Fourth, the follow‐up period was relatively short, which precludes evaluation of the long‐term retention of skills or the sustained effects of the training platform.

Fifth, due to the lack of a validated Chinese instrument for assessing PICU nurses' pain assessment knowledge, we developed the pNPAK. Although it demonstrated high content validity, this may reflect expert homogeneity and consensus bias. Only known‐groups validity was performed as a preliminary construct test. Further validation in larger, diverse samples is needed to refine the tool and improve its ability to evaluate knowledge levels.

Beyond instrument validation, future multicenter studies involving more diverse paediatric nursing populations, longer follow‐up periods and factorial designs that distinguish the effects of specific educational components (e.g., visualization vs. repetition frequency) are needed to extend the present findings.

Implication and Recommendation for Practice

This study highlights the potential of PainScan as a practical and cost‐effective training tool for PICU nurses. Given its ability to enhance pain assessment skills with flexible learning, further development of PainScan is warranted. Enhancing interactivity and user engagement may improve learning motivation and reduce cognitive load. Expanding content coverage to include comprehensive pain management topics may also improve educational continuity and clinical applicability.

Conclusion

The findings demonstrated that PainScan effectively enhances PICU nurses' pain assessment knowledge and skills. While no significant difference was observed in knowledge improvement compared to traditional on‐site training, participants reported high satisfaction with PainScan's vivid case presentations, flexible learning format and personalized features, which also offered economic advantages. However, nurses suggested improvements in supervision, engagement and continuity, indicating a need for further optimization to achieve better outcomes.
