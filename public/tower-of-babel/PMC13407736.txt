Survey of Practice Variations in Management of Acute Viral Bronchiolitis in India (PRERNI-PAVB Survey)
Authors: Sahil Vikas, Suresh K Angurana, Jayashree Muralidharan, Simon Craig, Indumathy Santhanam, Raghavendra Vanaki, Bharat Choudhary, Mounika Reddy, on behalf of the Pediatric Resuscitation and Emergency Research Network of India (PRERNI) Investigators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsTo identify the practice variations in the management of acute viral bronchiolitis (AVB) in different healthcare settings across India.MethodsThis online survey was conducted over 2½ months (May–July 2025) involving healthcare professionals (pediatricians, emergency physicians, and pediatric intensivists) working in different healthcare settings in India. Questions were related to demographic profile, caseload and seasonal variations, diagnosis, admission/discharge criteria, and treatment.ResultsAmong 100 respondents across 21 states/union territories, the majority were pediatric intensivists (n = 55) and pediatricians (n = 30) working in tertiary-level hospitals (n = 90). The pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) was available to 88 respondents. Most respondents diagnose AVB in children <2 years (n = 66) based on clinical history and examination alone (n = 41) and the addition of chest radiograph (n = 44). Common investigations performed among admitted children were chest radiograph (n = 76), sepsis screen (n = 64), and viral polymerase chain reaction (n = 47). Common admission criteria were high-risk babies (n = 94), severe respiratory distress (n = 91), doubtful follow-up (n = 72), and SpO2 <94% (n = 50). Once admitted, most respondents measure SpO2 intermittently (n = 59), start oxygen therapy if SpO2 <94% (n = 52), and maintain SpO2 >92% during treatment (n = 49). Once off oxygen, SpO2 >94% was used as a threshold to discharge (n = 58). Common modes to start oxygen were simple nasal prongs (n = 75) and high-flow nasal cannula (n = 58). The majority (n = 96) use nebulizations, including 3% saline (n = 86) and adrenaline (n = 52). Antibiotic use was reported by most as sometimes or rarely (n = 87). Most respondents use either the Indian Academy of Pediatrics (n = 48) or the American Academy of Pediatrics (n = 25) guidelines, and only 36 have a written policy/flowchart in the unit for management of AVB.ConclusionWe noted wide variations in the diagnosis, severity assessment, investigations, treatment, and adherence to guidelines for AVB. However, the majority of respondents use antibiotics sometimes or rarely.Keywords: Bronchiolitis, Guidelines, High-flow nasal cannula, Nebulization, Respiratory syncytial virus

Introduction

Acute viral bronchiolitis is the most common respiratory illness requiring hospitalization in children aged <2 years.1–5 Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is the etiological agent in more than 50% of the cases, followed by other viruses, such as parainfluenza, adenovirus, influenza virus, coronavirus, and rhinovirus.2–8 Despite knowing the benign nature of illness, most of the children suffering from AVB end up receiving non-evidence-based investigations and treatment, including chest radiographs, blood investigations, bronchodilators, and steroids, nebulizations, and antibiotics.1,3,9–11 The possible reasons for these wide variations in practice among pediatricians are multifactorial, including lack of consensus on diagnostic criteria, younger age group, parental pressure, urge to do something, and limited treatment options.2

Diagnosis of AVB is clinical, and neither chest radiograph nor laboratory investigations are recommended.1,2,4 It has been reported that children screened with chest radiography were more likely to receive antibiotics, as radiological findings cannot differentiate viral from bacterial pneumonia.1,2,9 Moreover, the chances of having concomitant bacterial infection in children with AVB are <1%.12 Therefore, the empirical antibiotics are not routinely indicated.1,9 Viral testing, although not routinely recommended, may help reduce unnecessary antibiotic use by identifying a viral cause of infection.1,2 The only treatment options that are evidence-based and associated with good outcomes include supportive care, gentle and non-threatening respiratory support [nasal prongs, continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP), high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC)], maintaining adequate oxygenation (SpO2 >92%), optimal hydration (intravenous fluids or enteral feeds), and gentle nasal suction (when required).1,2,4,11,13,14

Despite national and international recommendations and guidelines, there are significant practice variations in the management of AVB.1–3,9,10 The aim of this study is to identify the practice variations in the management of AVB in different healthcare settings across India.

Methods

This online survey-based study was performed over a period of 2½ months (May–July 2025) to assess the practice variations in the management of AVB in different healthcare settings across India involving individual pediatricians, pediatric intensivists, emergency physicians, fellows, and consultants. A survey was created, checked, and revised (by SKA and SV) with the guidance and supervision of the Pediatric Resuscitation and Emergency Research Network of India (PRERNI). The survey, once framed, was checked by PRERNI members for any potential issues, which were addressed before being administered. The PRERNI network was formed in 2024 with the aim of promoting research in the field of pediatric resuscitation and emergency medicine in India, with 35 members from different states and union territories of India.

The survey questionnaires are available in the Supplementary file. The survey contained the following sections and parameters: Demographics and institutional profile, including participants’ designation, type, and level of healthcare setting, and academic programs running in the hospital; Caseload and seasonal variations of AVB, including peak season for AVB cases and the number of cases observed per month; Diagnosis and investigations, including criteria for diagnosing AVB and investigations ordered upon admission [e.g., chest radiograph, sepsis screen, viral polymerase chain reaction (PCR)]; Severity assessment and admission and discharge criteria including methods used to assess respiratory distress severity and oxygen saturation (SpO2) thresholds for admission, starting oxygen, monitoring during treatment, and for discharge; and Treatment details including oxygen therapy (e.g., nasal prongs, HFNC, and CPAP), nebulization (hypertonic saline, adrenaline, salbutamol, steroids), antibiotics and criteria for initiation, pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) availability and admissions, duration of hospital stay, and guidelines followed for management of AVB. The responses to these survey questions were in the form of yes or no, multiple choices (to be ticked as per their practice), or short descriptive answers. The survey questions were then transferred to a Google Form, and the link was circulated through WhatsApp to healthcare providers as personal messages and in relevant WhatsApp groups. The survey could be completed in 15–20 minutes, and respondents were permitted to complete it only once.

The identity of respondents remained anonymous. Formal consent was not obtained from the respondents as the survey did not include any patient's data and identity of the respondents was not revealed. At the start of the survey, it was mentioned that “by proceeding with the survey, it is assumed that the respondents have consented to take part in the survey.” The protocol was approved by the Institute Ethics Committee (IEC) of the Postgraduate Institute of Medical Education and Research (PGIMER), Chandigarh (INT/IEC/2025/SPL-373 dated May 9, 2025). Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies (CROSS) was used.15

The primary outcome of this survey was to assess the practice variations in the management of AVB among healthcare providers across India.

The responses recorded in the survey were transferred to Microsoft Excel 2010 Sheet (Microsoft Corporation, Microsoft Way, Redmond, WA, United States) and SPSS version 20 (IBM Corp. Armonk, NY, USA) for further statistical analysis. Data analysis was performed using SPSS software. Descriptive statistics (numbers and percentages) were used for data representation.

A total of 100 respondents from 21 states/union territories participated in the survey (Fig. 1). The majority were faculty pediatric intensivists (n = 55), pediatricians (n = 30), and pediatric emergency physicians (n = 6). More than half (n = 51) were working in government hospitals, and the rest in private (n = 30) or corporate (n = 19) hospitals. The majority were working in tertiary-level hospitals (n = 90). Around two-thirds of respondents were working in both PICU and pediatric emergency (n = 64), and the rest in PICUs (n = 22) or pediatric emergency (n = 14) alone. The majority (n = 88) of respondents have a PICU in their hospital. Most of the respondents reported that they had one or another academic program running in their hospital, including MD pediatrics (n = 44), diplomate of national board (DNB) pediatrics (n = 31), DM pediatric critical care (n = 18), 1-year fellowships in pediatric emergency (n = 14), and pediatric critical care (n = 13). Thirty respondents reported that they had >1 academic program running in their hospital. Nineteen respondents did not have any academic program.

Seasonal variation, caseload, diagnosis, and investigations

The majority responded that they see the maximum number of cases during the months of December–February (n = 71) and September–November (n = 31). Average number of AVB cases observed per month during the AVB season was >50 (n = 33), 20–30 (n = 17), 10–20, and 30–40 (n = 15 each).

Diagnosis and Investigations (Table 2)

The majority of respondents (n = 66) consider 2 years to be the upper age limit to diagnose a case of AVB, while 22 consider it to be 1 year. Forty-four respondents rely on history, clinical examination, and chest radiographs to make the diagnosis of AVB, and 41 consider only history and clinical examination for the diagnosis. When asked about doing chest radiographs in children admitted with AVB, the respondents were equally divided between doing it always (n = 32), usually (n = 31), and sometimes (n = 33).

Assessment of severity and oxygenation, and admission/discharge criteria

Sixty-two respondents assess severity by clinical examination alone, and 38 respondents use formal respiratory distress scores (Table 3). Admission criteria reported included high-risk babies (n = 94), severity of respiratory distress (n = 91), and those in whom follow-up is doubtful (n = 72). Most common SpO2 cutoffs for admission were <94% (n = 50), <92% (n = 41), and <90% (n = 22). Once a child with AVB is admitted, 59 respondents monitor SpO2 intermittently and 41 continuously. Threshold SpO2 to start oxygen therapy was <94% (n = 52), <92% (n = 32), and <90% (n = 11). While on oxygen therapy, the target SpO2 levels were >92% (n = 49) and >94% (n = 40). Once there is clinical improvement, and the child is off oxygen therapy, the SpO2 thresholds for discharge were >94% (n = 58), >92% (n = 25), and >96% (n = 9).

Treatment details

The most preferred mode of starting oxygen therapy was nasal prongs (n = 75), followed by HFNC (n = 58), CPAP (n = 21), and simple face mask (n = 13). The majority of respondents (n = 96) used nebulization while managing a child with AVB, and common agents used were 3% saline (n = 86), adrenaline (n = 52), salbutamol (n = 34), and steroids (n = 16). Regarding antibiotic usage, 42 respondents use antibiotics sometimes, 35 use them rarely, 13 never use them, and only 10 use antibiotics usually. Common reasons for administering antibiotics included unstable child (respiratory failure or shock; n = 80), child on mechanical ventilation (n = 70), abnormal chest radiograph (n = 46), raised C-reactive protein (CRP; n = 44), and underlying heart disease or severe AVB (n = 38 each).

The most useful treatment modalities reported were HFNC (n = 88), followed by hypertonic saline nebulization (n = 50), adrenaline nebulization (n = 32), and salbutamol nebulization (n = 17). The proportion of children requiring PICU admission was 10–20% (n = 30), <10% (n = 25), and 21–30% (n = 17).

The average duration of hospital stay reported was 4–5 days (n = 65), 2–3 days (n = 17), and 6–7 days (n = 15). The majority of respondents use Indian Academy of Pediatrics (IAP) guidelines (n = 48) and American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) guidelines (n = 25) for the management of AVB, and 20 do not refer to any guidelines. Only 36 respondents had a written policy or flowchart in the unit for management of AVB.

Discussion

This nationwide survey highlighted the practice variation in the management of AVB among pediatric intensivists and pediatricians working in different healthcare settings across India. Most of the respondents reported running an academic program, and 88% had a PICU facility, highlighting the strengthening of pediatric critical care facilities in the country over the last two decades.16 Common season for AVB admission was December–February, conforming to the peak AVB season in India.2,3,5

There was wide variation in the investigations and treatment practices among respondents. More than 40% respondents rely on chest radiograph to make a diagnosis of AVB, and 76% perform chest radiograph in all children admitted with AVB. There was over-reliance on investigations like sepsis screen (64%) and viral PCR (47%). The SpO2 thresholds for admission, monitoring during treatment, and discharge were heterogeneous.

Diagnosis of AVB is clinical, and neither chest radiograph nor laboratory investigations are recommended routinely.1,2,4 It has been demonstrated that children with AVB who underwent chest radiograph were more likely to receive antibiotics, as radiological findings cannot differentiate viral from bacterial pneumonia.1,2,9 Therefore, frequent use of chest radiographs may contribute to diagnostic confusion with bacterial pneumonia and, consequently, overprescription of antibiotics. Although routine viral testing of respiratory samples is not recommended, it may help in preventing and limiting antibiotic usage once the virus is identified and a diagnosis is certain.1,2

Another critical observation was the limited adherence to standardized disease-severity scoring systems. Only 38% respondents reported using any validated severity scoring tool, possibly due to a lack of training or institutional protocols, which may hinder objective assessment, timely escalation, and weaning of care.17

The only treatment options that are evidence-based and associated with good outcomes in AVB include supportive care, respiratory support (nasal prongs, CPAP, and HFNC), which should be gentle and in a non-threatening way, adequate oxygenation (SpO2 >92%), optimal hydration (intravenous fluids or enteral feeds), and gentle nasal suction (when required).1,2,4,11,13,14 In this survey, the most preferred modes of oxygen therapy were simple nasal prongs and HFNC, consistent with the easy availability of simple nasal cannula in all healthcare settings and clinically beneficial effects of HFNC in children with AVB with moderate to severe respiratory distress.2,14,18,19

In this survey, we noted that the majority of respondents (96%) use nebulizations (3% saline, adrenaline, salbutamol, and steroids). Several studies demonstrated significant use of low-value or non-indicated therapies like antibiotics, bronchodilators, and steroids in AVB despite well-established guidelines recommending primarily supportive care.1,10,20–22 Gultekingil et al. conducted a multicenter cross-sectional descriptive survey in Turkey involving 713 responders, including residents (58.6%), specialists (34.6%), and lecturers (5.8%).10 They demonstrated that pediatricians still use bronchodilators, corticosteroids, and antibiotics commonly despite recent guidelines not supporting their use. Marcos‑Morales et al., in an observational study from Spain conducted over two epidemic waves (2014–2015 and 2015–2016) involving 138 children with AVB, demonstrated that despite the circulation of AAP guidelines, the use of antibiotics (70%), salbutamol (33%), and steroids (23%) was common.21 The frequent use of bronchodilators or steroids in AVB among clinicians is possibly driven by immediate clinical impressions rather than formal risk‑stratification, validated severity scores, or evidence-based recommendations. It has been demonstrated that targeted interventions can lead to improvement in evidence-based care of infants with AVB and the de-implementation of low-value or non-indicated interventions.23–25

An encouraging finding of this survey is that the majority of respondents use antibiotics only sometimes or rarely, guided by the sickness level of the children. Since the chances of concomitant bacterial infection in children with AVB are low (<1%),12 empirical antibiotics are not routinely indicated in children with AVB.1,9 However, several studies demonstrated that antibiotics are commonly prescribed in children with AVB.9,10,20,21 Papenburg et al., in a large cross-sectional data analysis of 300 randomly selected emergency departments in the United States of America (2007–2015), demonstrated that among children with AVB, 25% received antibiotics, and 70% of them did not have any documented concomitant bacterial infection.9 It was also noted that the use of chest radiography in children with AVB was high (46%), and it was associated with increased use of antibiotics.9 Garcia-Garcia et al. in a study from Spain involving 1,715 infants with AVB demonstrated that antibiotic usage was 13.4% without evidence of concomitant bacterial infection.26 The most common factors associated with antibiotic prescription were the presence of fever, infiltrates on chest radiograph, serum CRP, and PICU admission. It was also noted that having a virological diagnosis favored less antibiotic usage.26

The use of standard guidelines was inconsistent, and only one-third of respondents had written policy or protocol in place. Despite the availability of evidence-based guidelines,,28 the adherence to these guidelines is often low.1,20,27–31 The adherence to practice guidelines may restrict overuse of investigations and non-beneficial treatment strategies with improvement in the standard care of children with AVB.29,31

Strengths and Limitations

The strengths of this study include it being the first survey that provided real-world data highlighting current treatment practices for management of AVB among pediatric intensivists and pediatricians throughout India. This survey will pave the way for future studies to document actual treatment practices, improvement in quality of care and facilities, and formulation of India-specific guidelines. More than one response from the same institute was taken as an individual response, as we planned to include individual practice variations in the management of AVB. The limitations include that the management practices were not evaluated directly but based on an online survey, likely introducing social desirability bias. Other limitations include convenience sampling due to reliance on only WhatsApp mode rather than official mail. This may have contributed to the underrepresentation of physicians from tier 2 and tier 3 cities and general practitioners. There is a possibility of selection and responder biases toward tertiary centers catering mainly to pediatric intensivists and pediatricians. Due to the small sample size, there is a possibility of high non-response bias leading to low generalizability and representativeness.

To improve the outcome of children with AVB, there is an urgent need to strengthen evidence-based locally adaptable management protocols and training programs for healthcare providers. Development and implementation of institutional or national AVB management pathways may help in limiting routine investigations, restricting antibiotic misuse, promoting the use of validated severity scores to guide clinical care, bridging the knowledge-practice gap, and evidence translation to clinical practice.

Conclusion

This survey, involving predominantly pediatric intensivists and pediatricians working in tertiary-level academic hospitals, demonstrated that there were wide variations in diagnosis, severity assessment, and investigations; SpO2 criteria for admission, initia-tion, and maintenance of oxygen therapy, discharge thresholds; treatment practices, and adherence to standard guidelines. Most respondents reported using antibiotics only sometimes or rarely.
