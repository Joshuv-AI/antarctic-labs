Nonlinear dispersive waves in the discrete modified KdV equation
S. Yang

Abstract
In this paper, we study the nonlinear dispersive waves including the rarefaction and dispersive shock waves in the discrete modified KdV equation through the numerical simulations of the dispersive Riemann problems. In particular, we propose distinct quasi-continuum models to approximate both the spatial profiles and distinct edge features of these two specific dispersive wave structures. Whitham analysis is performed to construct a closed system of partial differential equations which describe the slowly-varying dynamics of all the relevant parameters associated with the periodic traveling waves of the proposed quasi-continuum models. We then perform reduction on such modulation system to obtain a system of two simple-wave ordinary differential equations which lead to the DSW-fitting method that shall provide useful theoretical insights on different edge characteristics of the dispersive shock waves. Furthermore, we compute analytically the self-similar solutions corresponding to the dispersionless systems of the quasi-continuum models, which can be utilized to approximate the numerically observed rarefaction waves of the discrete mKdV equation. A systematic numerical comparison of these theoretical findings with their associated numerical counterparts finally demonstrate the good performance of the proposed quasi-continuum models in approximating both nonlinear dispersive wave patterns.

II
Model description and theoretical setup

We consider the integrable discrete modified KdV (dmKdV) equation
10
in the following normalized form:

\frac{du_{n}}{dt}=\left(1+u_{n}^{2}\right)\left(u_{n+1}-u_{n-1}\right),

(1)

where
n\in\mathbb{Z}
denotes the lattice site.

We notice that the discrete lattice in Eq. (
1
) is dispersive. To see this, we can compute the linear dispersion relation of Eq. (
1
) by looking for a plane-wave solution in the form of an infinitesimal perturbation near the homogeneous state of
\overline{u}
:
u_{n}(t)=\overline{u}+a\exp\left[i(kn-\omega t)\right]
, where
0<a\ll 1
is a small parameter. Substitution of this plane-wave ansatz into Eq. (
1
) yields the following linear dispersion relation, upon the elimination of
a
,

\omega(\overline{u},k)=-2(1+\overline{u}^{2})\sin(k).

(2)

We notice that
\partial_{kk}\omega\neq 0
for every wavenumber
k
, so this demonstrates that the discrete mKdV equation (
1
) is dispersive.

In the present work, we study the nonlinear dispersive wave patterns numerically observed from the Riemann problem associated with Eq. (
1
). Namely, we consider the Cauchy problem of Eq. (
1
) with the following Riemann initial condition

u_{n}(0)=\begin{cases}u_{-},\quad n\leq 0,\\
u_{+},\quad n>0,\end{cases}

(3)

where
u_{\pm}\in\mathbb{R}
refer to the two backgrounds of the Riemann initial data.

We shall see from a specific numerical simulation that when
u_{-}<u_{+}
, we obtain a numerical discrete dispersive shock wave, while a rarefaction wave when
u_{-}>u_{+}
. Fig.
1
shows the dynamics of the Riemann problem associated with Eq. (
1
). In particular, note that when
u_{-}=0
,
u_{+}=0.2
, the panel
(a)
shows the spatial profile of a dispersive shock wave at
t=1000
, where its linear and solitonic edges are labeled. On the other hand, the panel
(b)
shows that of a numerical rarefaction wave of the lattice (
1
). Both waves are propagating to the left. In the later section, we shall make theoretical predictions on these speeds of propagation including the DSW solitonic and linear-edge speeds.

, while

for

III
Quasi-continuum approximations

To derive some relevant quasi-continuum models for Eq. (
1
), we first consider the following multi-scale change of variables
10
:

\displaystyle u_{n}(t)=\epsilon u(\xi,\tau);

(4)

\displaystyle\xi=\epsilon(n+2t),\quad\tau=\frac{1}{3}\epsilon^{3}t,

where
0<\epsilon\ll 1
is a formal smallness parameter.

Substituting the ansatz in Eq. (
4
) into the dmKdV equation (
1
) yields the following continuum modified KdV (mKdV) equation, by collecting relevant terms up to the order of
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{4})
:

u_{\tau}=6u^{2}u_{\xi}+u_{\xi\xi\xi}.

(5)

Looking for a plane-wave solution in the form of
u(\xi,\tau)=\overline{u}+a\exp\left[i(K\xi-\Omega\tau)\right]
(
0<a\ll 1
) and substitution into Eq. (
5
) yields the following linear dispersion relation by eliminating the small parameter of
a
:

\Omega_{\text{mKdV}}\left(\overline{u},K\right)=-6\overline{u}^{2}K+K^{3}.

(6)

Instead of the quasi-continuum mKdV model in Eq. (
5
), we can instead define the slow spatial and temporal variables in the following manner.

\displaystyle u_{n}(t)=u(X,T),

(7)

\displaystyle X=\epsilon n,\quad T=\epsilon t.

Based on the definitions of the slow spatial and temporal variables of
X
and
T
, this immediately implies the following relations between the wavenumbers and frequencies.

K=k/\epsilon,\quad\Omega=\omega/\epsilon.

(8)

Then, substitution of the ansatz in Eq. (
7
) into the discrete mKdV (
1
) yields, by collecting relevant terms up to the order of
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3})
,

u_{T}=\left(1+u^{2}\right)\left(2u_{X}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{3}u_{XXX}\right).

(9)

We then look for a plane-wave solution in the form of
u(X,T)=\overline{u}+a\exp\left[i(KX-\Omega T)\right]
(
0<a\ll 1
) to arrive at the following linear dispersion relation for Eq. (
9
), upon the substitution of such ansatz into Eq. (
9
).

\Omega_{\text{n}}(\overline{u},K)=-\left(1+\overline{u}^{2}\right)\left(2K-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{3}K^{3}\right).

(10)

It can be readily seen from Eq. (
10
) that the proposed model in Eq. (
9
) does not have a bounded linear dispersion relation. Namely,
\Omega_{\text{n}}\to\pm\infty
as
K\to\pm\infty
. In order to obtain a quasi-continuum model with bounded dispersion relation, we rewrite Eq. (
9
) as follows.

\arctan(u)_{T}=2\left(1+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{6}\partial^{2}_{X}\right)u_{X}.

(11)

Then, we invert the operator
1+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{6}\partial^{2}_{X}
on the right-hand side of Eq. (
11
) to obtain, by performing Taylor expansion and collecting terms up to the order of
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
:

\arctan(u)_{T}-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{6}\arctan(u)_{XXT}=2u_{X}.

(12)

The linear dispersion relation of the “regularized” model in Eq. (
12
) admits the following linear dispersion relation:

\Omega_{\text{r}}(\overline{u},K)=-\frac{2\left(1+\overline{u}^{2}\right)K}{1+\frac{\epsilon^{2}K^{2}}{6}},

(13)

which is clearly bounded as
\Omega_{\text{r}}\to 0
as
K\to\pm\infty
.

The comparison of the linear dispersion relations of the three models in Eqs. (
1
) (blue), (
9
) (red) and (
12
) (magenta).

Fig.
2
depicts the comparison of the three linear dispersion relations in Eqs. (
2
), (
10
) and (
13
). In particular, we can see clearly that the dispersion relation of the non-regularized model in Eq. (
9
), represented by the red curve, asymptotes to positive infinity drastically as the wavenumber
k
increases, while that of the regularized model (
12
), described via the magenta curve, remains bounded and approaches to zero as
k\to\infty
.

We shall apply the three quasi-continuum models in Eqs. (
5
), (
9
) and (
12
) to make approximations on the nonlinear dispersive waves numerically observed in the discrete mKdV equation (
1
). Finally, we end this section with a brief discussion on one potential limitation of these quasi-continuum models (e.g. (
5
)). In particular, we expect these quasi-continuum models to provide reasonable approximations on the discrete dispersive wave structures of Eq. (
1
) provided that their scales, denoted as
\Gamma
, are considerably larger than the spacing of the lattice, which is proportional to the formal smallness parameter of
\epsilon

26
. Namely, only when
\Gamma\gg\epsilon
, it can then be expected that the three quasi-continuum models in Eqs. (
5
), (
7
) and (
12
) can yield reasonable approximated dispersive wave patterns for these of the lattice (
1
). As a result, we shall not utilize them for any early-time dynamics comparison with Eq. (
1
).

IV
Periodic traveling-wave solutions

In this section, we seek the periodic traveling-wave solutions for the discrete lattice (
1
) and the three quasi-continuum models in Eqs. (
5
), (
9
) and (
12
). These periodic waves play an essential role and serve as one of the necessary prerequisites for the Whitham modulation theory which we shall discuss in Section
VI
.

Firstly, for the discrete lattice in Eq. (
1
), we review its periodic traveling-wave solution described in
10
. Specifically, the discrete mKdV equation (
1
) admits two family of periodic traveling waves. On the one hand, it has the following so-called “dnoidal” periodic wave solution expressed in terms of the Jacobi elliptic functions:

\displaystyle u_{n}(t)=\frac{\text{sn}\left(\alpha;m\right)}{\text{cn}\left(\alpha;m\right)}\text{dn}\left(\alpha n+ct;m\right),

(14)

\displaystyle c=\frac{2\text{sn}\left(\alpha;m\right)}{\text{cn}\left(\alpha;m\right)},

where
m\in(0,1)
denotes the elliptic modulus.

On the other hand, the lattice (
1
) also possesses the following “cnoidal” periodic wave solution:

\displaystyle u_{n}(t)=m\frac{\text{sn}\left(\alpha;m\right)}{\text{dn}\left(\alpha;m\right)}\text{cn}\left(\alpha n+ct;m\right),

(15)

\displaystyle c=\frac{2\text{sn}\left(\alpha;m\right)}{\text{dn}\left(\alpha;m\right)}.

At the soliton limit where
m\to 1
, it has been shown that the two periodic waves in Eqs. (
14
) and (
15
) shall asymptote to the same solitary-wave solutions
10
:

u_{n}(t)=\sinh(\alpha)\mathrm{sech}(\alpha n+ct),\quad c=2\sinh(\alpha).

(16)

Next, for the continuum mKdV equation (
5
), we look for the periodic solutions in the form of

u(\xi,\tau)=u(z),\quad z=\alpha\xi+c\tau,

(17)

where
\alpha>0
is a constant and
c\in\mathbb{R}
refers to the speed of propagation of the waves.

Substitution of the ansatz (
17
) into Eq. (
5
) yields the following co-traveling frame ordinary differential equation (ODE), upon integration with respect to
z
twice.

\alpha^{3}(u_{z})^{2}=cu^{2}-\alpha u^{4}-2Au-B,

(18)

where
A,B
are two constants of integration.

In particular, based on the co-traveling frame ODE in Eq. (
18
), we can compute the solitary-wave solution by assuming that
u(z)\to 0
as
z\to\pm\infty
. This implies that
A=B=0
, and then we can perform direct integration of Eq. (
18
) by the method of quadrature to arrive at

u(\xi,\tau)=\sqrt{\frac{c}{\alpha}}\mathrm{sech}\left(\frac{\sqrt{c}}{\alpha^{3/2}}\left(\alpha\xi+c\tau-\xi_{0}\right)\right),

(19)

where
\xi_{0}
denotes an arbitrary phase parameter.

Hence, the soliton amplitude-speed relation is described as

a=\sqrt{\frac{c}{\alpha}},

(20)

where
a
is the amplitude of the soliton in Eq. (
19
).

Moreover, the periodic solutions associated with the model Eq. (
5
) can be similarly obtained by integrating Eq. (
18
), however, without the assumption that
A=B=0
, and the solutions can be expressed, in principle, in terms of Jacobi elliptic functions
15
. We shall omit the derivation of the periodic solutions here for brevity since they are out of the considerations of this present work.

Next, for the other two quasi-continuum models in Eqs. (
9
) and (
12
), we assume the following traveling-wave ansatz,

u(X,T)=u(\tilde{z}),\quad\tilde{z}=\alpha X+cT.

(21)

Substitution of the ansatz (
21
) into the two models in Eqs. (
9
) and (
12
) yields.

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}\alpha^{3}}{3}(u_{\tilde{z}})^{2}=2c\left(u\arctan(u)-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+u^{2}\right)\right)

(22)

\displaystyle-2\alpha u^{2}-2Au-B,

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}\alpha^{2}c}{6}(\tilde{u}_{\tilde{z}})^{2}=c\tilde{u}^{2}-4\alpha\log\left|\sec(\tilde{u})\right|-2A\tilde{u}-B,

respectively, where
A,B
are two constants of integration, and
\tilde{u}\equiv\arctan(u)
.

To the best of our knowledge, both the solitary-wave and periodic traveling-wave solutions do not admit analytical expressions for both models in Eqs. (
9
) and (
12
). Nevertheless, we can still show the existence of such solutions by performing a relevant phase-plane analysis via visualizing the potential curves associated with the two co-traveling frame ODEs in Eq. (
22
). We shall draw the potential curves of the two co-traveling frame ODEs in Eq. (
22
) in the following forms

\displaystyle P_{\text{n}}(u)=\frac{3}{\epsilon^{2}\alpha^{3}}\bigg[-2c\left(u\arctan(u)-\frac{1}{2}\log(1+u^{2})\right)

(23)

\displaystyle+2\alpha u^{2}+2Au+B\bigg],

\displaystyle P_{\text{r}}(\tilde{u})=\frac{6}{\epsilon^{2}\alpha^{2}c}\left(-c\tilde{u}^{2}+4\alpha\log\left|\sec(\tilde{u})\right|+2A\tilde{u}+B\right).

. The panels

and

depict the potential curve of
P_{\text{n}}(u)
and

, respectively.

Fig.

display the potential curves in Eq. (

). We notice that the two blue horizontal lines in each panel demonstrate the associated periodic orbits of the co-traveling frame ODEs which are the periodic waves corresponding to the original models in Eqs. (

) and (

V
Conservation laws

In this section, we list some relevant conservation laws associated with each quasi-continuum model. These conservation laws shall be an indispensable prerequisite for performing Whitham analysis. Specifically, to obtain useful insights on the slowly varying dynamics of the parameters of the periodic traveling waves, we need to derive the so-called Whitham modulation system which is described by a system of partial differential equations, called the Whitham modulation equations, governing a closed system of dynamics of all the relevant parameters. The modulation equations can be derived by various approaches
17
. However, we shall mainly rely on the method of “averaging the conservation laws”, and hence the necessity of knowing relevant conservation laws of the three quasi-continuum models in Eqs. (
5
), (
9
) and (
12
). In addition, we notice that since the co-traveling frame ODEs for all the quasi-continuum models have an order of three, this suggests that the associated periodic traveling waves are three-parameter family of solutions. In order to construct a closed slowly varying dynamics of these three parameters, we need only two conservation laws which shall yield two modulation equations upon performing averaging operation. We shall introduce the remaining modulation equation in Section
VI
, which is the so-called “conservation of waves”.

Firstly, the model (
5
) possesses the following two conservation laws:

\displaystyle u_{\tau}=2(u^{3})_{\xi}+u_{\xi\xi\xi},

(24)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(u^{2})_{\tau}=\frac{3}{2}(u^{4})_{\xi}+(uu_{\xi\xi})_{\xi}-\frac{1}{2}\left[\left(u_{\xi}\right)^{2}\right]_{\xi},

where the first and second equation in (
24
) refer to the conservation of mass and linear momentum, respectively.

The other two quasi-continuum models in Eqs. (
9
) and (
12
) admit the following two conservation laws.
For Eq. (
9
),

\displaystyle\arctan(u)_{T}=2u_{X}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{3}u_{XXX},

(25)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+u^{2}\right)_{T}=(u^{2})_{X}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{3}\left(uu_{XX}\right)_{X}-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{6}\left[\left(u_{X}\right)^{2}\right]_{X}.

Then, for Eq. (
12
),

\displaystyle\left(\tilde{u}-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{6}\tilde{u}_{XX}\right)_{T}-2\tan\left(\tilde{u}\right)_{X}=0,

(26)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{u}^{2})_{T}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{12}\left[(\tilde{u}_{X})^{2}\right]_{T}

\displaystyle-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{6}\left(\tilde{u}\tilde{u}_{XT}\right)_{X}-2\left(\tilde{u}\tan(\tilde{u})+\log\left|\cos(\tilde{u})\right|\right)_{X}=0,

where we recall that
\tilde{u}=\arctan(u)
.

Analogously, for the conservation laws in Eqs. (
25
) and (
26
), they represent the mass and momentum conservation, respectively.

VI
Whitham modulation equations

In this section, we derive the Whitham modulation equations which describe the closed slowly varying dynamics of all the relevant parameters of the periodic traveling waves revisited in Section
IV
.

Firstly, regarding the modulation system associated with the modified KdV equation (
5
), it is well documented in, for example,
15
, so we shall not re-derive similar results in the present work for brevity. We now attempt to compute the Whitham modulation equations for the two quasi-continuum models in Eqs. (
9
) and (
12
) by applying the method of averaging the conservation laws which are specified in Section
V
. We introduce first some necessary preliminaries for applying such method. In particular, we first define the following fast phase
\theta
:

\theta=\frac{KX-\Omega T}{\epsilon},

(27)

and then we look for periodic traveling waves associated with the models of Eqs. (
9
) and (
12
) in the following multiple-scale form.

u(X,T)=\phi\left(\theta;X,T\right)+\epsilon u_{1}\left(\theta;X,T\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon^{2}\right),

(28)

where functions of
\varphi\left(\theta;X,T\right)
and
u_{1}\left(\theta;X,T\right)
refer to the periodic traveling-wave solutions of the models with fixed period of
2\pi
. Notice that the period of
2\pi
is a convention, and it can be arbitrary. Nevertheless, it is important to note that the periodic traveling waves ought to have a fixed period as this is one particular prerequisite
17
of performing Whitham averaging for obtaining the modulation equations. In addition, we note that the exact analytical expression of
\varphi(\theta;X,T)
is not known for either model of (
9
) or (
12
). However, we have proven their existences via the relevant phase-plane analysis in Section
IV
, and we make the necessary assumptions that their periods can still be fixed through the process of reparametrization
29
. Next, the fast phase of
\theta
is further defined according to

\theta_{X}=K/\epsilon,\quad\theta_{T}=-\Omega/\epsilon,

(29)

where both the wavenumber
K
and frequency
\Omega
are functions of the two slow variables of
X
and
T
. Then, the compatibility condition of
\theta_{XT}=\theta_{TX}
leads to the following equation.

K_{T}+\Omega_{X}=0,

(30)

which is the so-called “conservation of waves”, and is the first modulation equation of the full closed modulation system.

We now define the following averaging operator: For a given function
F
,

\overline{F(\phi)}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}F(\phi(\theta))d\theta.

(31)

Then, applying such averaging operation to the conservation laws specified in Section
V
and collecting relevant terms up to the order of
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
yields the closed system of modulation equations.

On the one hand, the Whitham modulation equations for Eq. (
9
) are:

\displaystyle K_{T}+\Omega_{X}=0,

(32)

\displaystyle\overline{\arctan(\phi)}_{T}-2\overline{\phi}_{X}=0,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\overline{\log\left(1+\phi^{2}\right)}_{T}-\left(\overline{\phi^{2}}+\frac{K^{2}}{2}\overline{\left(\phi_{\theta}\right)^{2}}\right)_{X}=0.

On the other hand, the modulation system associated with the model (
12
) reads

\displaystyle K_{T}+\Omega_{X}=0,

(33)

\displaystyle\overline{\tilde{\phi}}_{T}-2\overline{\tan\left(\tilde{\phi}\right)}_{X}=0,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\overline{\tilde{\phi}^{2}}_{T}+\frac{1}{12}\left(K^{2}\overline{\left(\tilde{\phi}_{\theta}\right)^{2}}\right)_{T}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{6}\left(K\Omega\overline{\left(\tilde{\phi}_{\theta}\right)^{2}}\right)_{X}-2\left(\overline{\tilde{\phi}\tan(\tilde{\phi})+\log\left|\cos(\tilde{\phi})\right|}\right)_{X}=0,

where
\tilde{\phi}\equiv\tan(\phi)
.

VII
Reduction of the Whitham system

The Whitham modulation equations presented in the form of Eqs. (
32
) and (
33
) do not generally have practical application. Nonetheless, it can be very useful to perform a suitable set of reduction of the two Whitham systems at both the harmonic and solitonic limits, since these limit modulation systems shall encode important insights on distinct edge features of the DSWs.

We demonstrate the precise derivation of such reduction only for the Whitham system in Eq. (
32
) since it is completely analogous for that of Eq. (
33
). Firstly, at the harmonic edge, it is expected that the wave amplitude, denoted as
a
, to asymptote to zero (i.e.
a\to 0
) and hence the waves admits small amplitudes. This further implies the following fact
16
: For a given function
F
,

\overline{F(\phi)}\to F(\overline{\phi}),\quad\overline{\left(\phi_{\theta}\right)^{2}}\to 0.

(34)

Therefore, the modulation system (
32
) degenerates into the following two equations.

\displaystyle K_{T}+\Omega_{\text{n}X}=0,

(35)

\displaystyle\overline{\phi}_{T}-2\left(1+\overline{\phi}^{2}\right)\overline{\phi}_{X}=0,

where
\Omega_{\text{n}}
refers to the linear dispersion relation in Eq. (
10
).

Then, we can cast the system Eq. (
35
) into the following characteristic form, upon the utilization of the chain rules:

\begin{bmatrix}K\\
\overline{\phi}\end{bmatrix}_{T}+\begin{bmatrix}\partial_{K}\Omega_{\text{n}}&\partial_{\overline{\phi}}\Omega_{\text{n}}\\
0&-2\left(1+\overline{\phi}^{2}\right)\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}K\\
\overline{\phi}\end{bmatrix}_{X}=0.

(36)

Then, we notice that the Jacobian matrix in Eq. (
36
) admits the following left eigenpare of
(\lambda_{1},v_{1})
:

\lambda_{1}=\partial_{K}\Omega_{\text{n}},\quad v_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}2(1+\overline{\phi}^{2})+\partial_{K}\Omega_{\text{n}}&\partial_{\overline{\phi}}\Omega_{\text{n}}\end{bmatrix}.

(37)

We multiply the characteristic equation (
36
) with the left eigenvector of
v_{1}
in Eq. (
37
) to obtain that, by chain rules

\frac{dK}{d\overline{\phi}}=-\frac{\partial_{\overline{\phi}}\Omega_{\text{n}}}{2\left(1+\overline{\phi}^{2}\right)+\partial_{K}\Omega_{\text{n}}}.

(38)

The equation (
38
) is the so-called “simple-wave” ordinary differential equation (ODE) at the harmonic limit of the modulation system. In particular, this ODE discloses the important relation between the wavenumber
K
and the associated averaged background of
\overline{\phi}
. We shall see its application in Section
VIII
as a theoretical approach to make predictions on the different edge features of the DSWs of the models of interested.

A similar simple-wave ODE can be derived also at the solitonic limit where the wavenumber
K\to 0
. In this case, it is more convenient to define the following “conjugate dispersion relation”
16
, denoted as
\Omega^{s}_{\text{n}}
:

\Omega^{s}_{\text{n}}\left(\overline{u},\widetilde{K}\right)=-i\Omega_{\text{n}}\left(\overline{u},i\widetilde{K}\right),

(39)

where
\widetilde{K}
denotes the “conjugate wavenumber”.

After analogous manipulation for the reduction of the modulation system (
32
) at the solitonic limit
K\to 0
, we arrive at the following system

\frac{d\widetilde{K}}{d\overline{\phi}}=-\frac{\partial_{\overline{\phi}}\Omega^{s}_{\text{n}}}{2\left(1+\overline{\phi}^{2}\right)+\partial_{\widetilde{K}}\Omega^{s}_{\text{n}}}.

(40)

Moreover, we shall impose some appropriate initial conditions to Eqs. (
38
) and (
40
). On the one hand, at the linear (harmonic) limit where
a\to 0
, it is expected that the wavenumber of
K
at the solitonic edge of the DSW to be exactly zero so that
K(u_{-})=0
. We need to then integrate this initial-value problem (IVP) from
\overline{\phi}=u_{-}
to
\overline{\phi}=u_{+}
in order to obtain the wavenumber at the linear edge, denoted as
K_{+}
,
K_{+}=K(u_{+})
. On the other hand, regarding the simple-wave ODE at the solitonic limit, we impose the initial condition by the fact that the conjugate wavenumber at the linear edge is zero. Namely,
\widetilde{K}(u_{+})=0
. We then are able to integrate the IVP in Eq. (
40
) up to
\overline{\phi}=u_{-}
to compute the conjugate wavenumber at the solitonic edge, denoted as
\widetilde{K}_{-}
:
\widetilde{K}_{-}=\widetilde{K}(u_{-})
.

VIII
DSW fitting

We now are ready to solve the simple-wave IVPs derived from Section
VII
to gain important theoretical insights on distinct edge characteristics of the DSWs. We notice that this approach is called the “DSW-fitting” method, and we can furthermore generalize the method of DSW-fitting as follows. It involves the following two simple-wave IVPs:

\displaystyle\frac{dk}{d\overline{u}}=\frac{\partial_{\overline{u}}\omega_{0}}{V(\overline{u})-\partial_{k}\omega_{0}},\quad k(u_{s})=0,

(41)

\displaystyle\frac{d\widetilde{k}}{d\overline{u}}=\frac{\partial_{\overline{u}}\omega_{s}}{V(\overline{u})-\partial_{\widetilde{k}}\widetilde{\omega}_{s}},\quad\widetilde{k}(u_{l})=0,

where
u_{l,s}
refer to the values of the backgrounds at the linear and solitonic edge of the DSW, respectively. Moreover,
V(\overline{u})
represents the dispersionless speed associated with the models of interest. For example, for the continuum mKdV equation (
5
),
V(\overline{u})=-6\overline{u}^{2}
as its dispersionless system is given as

u_{\tau}-6u^{2}u_{\xi}=0.

(42)

Upon obtaining the linear-edge wavenumber
k_{l}=k(u_{l})
and solitonic-edge conjugate wavenumber
\tilde{k}_{s}=\tilde{k}(u_{s})
, we can further compute the linear-edge and solitonic-edge speeds of the DSW via the group and phase velocities, respectively

\displaystyle s_{l}=\partial_{k}\omega_{0}\left(u_{l},k_{l}\right),

(43)

\displaystyle s_{s}=\frac{\omega_{s}}{\tilde{k}}\left(u_{s},\tilde{k}_{s}\right).

We shall perform the DSW fitting to not only the three quasi-continuum models in Eqs. (
5
), (
9
) and (
12
), but also to the discrete mKdV equation (
1
). Firstly, for the dmKdV equation (
1
) with the linear dispersion relation (
2
). Solving the IVPs in Eq. (
41
) yield

\displaystyle k_{+}=2\arccos\left(\sqrt{\frac{1+u_{-}^{2}}{1+u_{+}^{2}}}\right),\quad s_{+}=\partial_{k}\omega_{0}(u_{+},k_{+}).

(44)

\displaystyle\tilde{k}_{-}=2\text{arcsech}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1+u_{-}^{2}}{1+u_{+}^{2}}}\right),\quad s_{-}=\frac{\omega_{s}}{\tilde{k}}\left(u_{-},\tilde{k}_{-}\right),

where
\omega_{0}
is the linear dispersion relation in Eq. (
2
). In addition, based on the solitary-wave solution specified in Eq. (
16
), we notice that the soliton amplitude-speed relation is given as

\left|s_{-}\right|=\frac{2a_{-}}{\text{arcsinh}(a_{-})},

(45)

where
a_{-}
denotes the amplitude of the DSW. So, we are also able to make predictions on the DSW amplitude by solving the nonlinear equation in Eq. (
45
) for
a_{-}
.

Next, for the second model in Eq. (
9
), solving the simple-wave IVPs yield

\displaystyle K_{+}^{\text{n}}=\frac{\sqrt{6}}{\epsilon}\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{1+u_{-}^{2}}{1+u_{+}^{2}}\right)^{2/3}},\quad S_{+}^{\text{n}}=\partial_{K}\Omega_{\text{n}}\left(u_{+},K_{+}^{\text{n}}\right),

(46)

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}_{-}^{\text{n}}=\frac{\sqrt{6}}{\epsilon}\sqrt{\left(\frac{1+u_{+}^{2}}{1+u_{-}^{2}}\right)^{2/3}-1},\quad S_{-}^{\text{n}}=\frac{\Omega^{s}_{\text{n}}}{\widetilde{K}}\left(u_{-},\widetilde{K}_{-}^{\text{n}}\right),

where
\Omega_{\text{n}}
refers to the linear dispersion relation (
10
) of the model (
9
).

Thirdly, regarding the model in Eq. (
12
), we obtain the following relation between
K
and
\overline{u}
, by solving the simple-wave IVPs

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}K^{2}}{12}+\log\left(\frac{\left(6+\epsilon^{2}K^{2}\right)\left(1+u_{-}^{2}\right)}{6\left(1+\overline{u}^{2}\right)}\right)=0,

(47)

\displaystyle-\frac{\epsilon^{2}\widetilde{K}^{2}}{12}+\log\left|\frac{\left(-6+\epsilon^{2}\widetilde{K}^{2}\right)\left(1+u_{+}^{2}\right)}{6\left(1+\overline{u}^{2}\right)}\right|=0.

One shall solve the transcendental equations in Eq. (
47
) to obtain the numerical solutions to the linear-edge wavenumber of
K^{r}_{+}=K(u_{+})
and solitonic-edge conjugate wavenumber of
\widetilde{K}^{r}_{-}=\widetilde{K}(u_{-})
. Moreover, the linear and solitonic-edge speeds of the DSW are given as

S^{\text{r}}_{+}=\partial_{K}\Omega_{\text{r}}\left(u_{+},K_{+}^{\text{r}}\right),\quad S_{-}^{\text{r}}=\frac{\Omega_{\text{r}}^{s}}{\widetilde{K}}\left(u_{-},\widetilde{K}^{\text{r}}_{-}\right),

(48)

where
\Omega_{\text{r}}
is the linear dispersion relation (
13
) of the regularized model in Eq. (
12
).

Lastly, for the continuum mKdV equation (
5
), solving the two IVPs in Eq. (
41
) yields

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}_{-}^{\text{mKdV}}=K_{+}^{\text{mKdV}}=2\sqrt{u_{+}^{2}-u_{-}^{2}},

(49)

\displaystyle S_{+}^{\text{mKdV}}=\partial_{K}\Omega_{\text{mKdV}}\left(u_{+},K_{+}^{\text{mKdV}}\right),

\displaystyle S_{-}^{\text{mKdV}}=\frac{\Omega^{s}_{\text{mKdV}}}{\widetilde{K}}\left(u_{-},\widetilde{K}_{-}^{\text{mKdV}}\right).

Also, according to the soliton amplitude-speed relation (
20
), the DSW-fitting theoretical prediction on the DSW amplitude, denoted as
a_{-}
, is as follows.

a_{-}=\epsilon\sqrt{\left|S_{-}^{\text{mKdV}}\right|}.

(50)

However, we note that the DSW-fitting theoretical predictions of the continuum mKdV equation (
5
) are consonant with the
(\xi,\tau)
coordinates specified in Eq. (
4
). Since, as a priori, we shall compare these analytical predictions in the coordinates of
(n,t)
, we need to transform them according to

\frac{dn}{dt}=\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{3}\frac{d\xi}{d\tau}-2.

(51)

As a priori, we shall compare these DSW-fitting theoretical predictions on the edge speeds of the DSWs with their numerical counterparts in Section
XI

IX
Numerical methods and initial conditions

In this section, we discuss, in detail, the numerical methods utilized for solving all the relevant models considered in this work. Such discussion is both necessary and important as all of our numerical results discussed in the later sections shall be based on these applied numerical schemes.

Firstly, for the discrete mKdV equation (
1
), we apply the standard RK4 time integration scheme to solve the system of difference-difference equations (
1
). Then, for the non-regularized and regularized PDEs in Eqs. (
9
) and (
12

21

5
). We notice that since we utilize the spectral method for spatial discretization, then this shall implicitly assume the periodic boundary conditions. However, it is important to note that the standard Riemann initial condition in Eq. (
3

9

\displaystyle u_{0}(X)=u_{l}-\frac{1}{2}\left(u_{l}-u_{p}\right)\times

(52)

\displaystyle\left[\tanh\left(\delta(X-x_{l})\right)-\tanh\left(\delta(X-x_{r})\right)\right],

where
u_{0}(X)\equiv u(X,0)
,
u_{l,p}
are two constants determining the upper and lower background values,
\delta>0
is the parameter controlling the smoothness of the “jump”, where a larger
\delta
always suggests a shaper jump/transition of the Riemann initial data. Finally, the values of
x_{l,r}
represent the locations of the left and right initial jump, respectively. Fig.
4
demonstrates the spatial profile of a box-type initial condition. In particular, this specific initial data can be viewed as two Riemann initial conditions.

Firstly, for the continuum mKdV equation, we notice its corresponding dispersionless system is given as

(55)

u_{-}>u_{+}

(56)

Substitution of Eq. (

XII
Conclusions and future directions

In this paper, we revisit the discrete modified KdV equation and derive three quasi-continuum models which are the continuum modified KdV equation (
5
), non-regularized model (
9
) and regularized model (
12
). We also review the analytical periodic traveling waves of the discrete modified KdV equation (
1
). We then derive the Whitham modulation equations associated with the two quasi-continuum models in Eqs. (
9
) and (
12
) and perform reductions on both modulation system to arrive at the DSW-fitting simple-wave ODEs. We then solve the system of simple-wave ODEs euippbed with appropriate initial conditions to obtain the theoretical predictions on different edge features of the DSWs of the quasi-continuum models. Numerical comparisons are conducted, demonstrating the good performance of the approximations made by all the quasi-continuum models.

However, there are many interesting open questions related to the present work, and we here only list a few of them. Firstly, we notice that since the discrete modified KdV equation is shown to be integrable
10
, then it is possible to perform Whitham analysis directly on Eq. (
1
) itself. Namely, in principle, one can find the associated conservation laws of Eq. (
1
) to perform Whitham averaging in order to obtain the corresponding Whitham modulation equations. Moreover, whenever a model is integrable, it is also possible to diagonalize the Whitham modulation system by casting it into the equivalent Riemann-invariant form. One can thereafter utilize the diagonalized Whitham modulation system to construct the theoretical solution for the discrete DSW of Eq. (
1
) so that this shall provide an exact analytical description on the discrete DSW. Secondly, it is also interesting to investigate the well-posedness of the two proposed quasi-continuum models in Eqs. (
9
) and (
12
) via analytical approaches. These new directions are currently under consideration and will be reported in future publications.
