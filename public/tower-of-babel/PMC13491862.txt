Proactive desmopressin for the prevention of serum sodium overcorrection in moderate-to-severe hyponatremia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Paulo Ricardo Gessolo Lins, Fernando Uvinha, Felipe S Passos, Ricardo E Treml, Lenar Yessayan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHyponatremia is the most common electrolyte disorder in hospitalized patients and is associated with increased morbidity and mortality. Correction is challenging because rapid increases in serum sodium may lead to osmotic demyelination. Desmopressin is used to stabilize free-water excretion, but the effectiveness of proactive administration has not been systematically evaluated.MethodsWe searched PubMed-MEDLINE, Embase, and the Cochrane Library registries from inception through February 2026. We included studies enrolling adults with moderate-to-severe hyponatremia receiving standard therapy that compared proactive desmopressin with non-proactive usual-care strategies. Two reviewers independently extracted data and assessed risk of bias using ROBINS-I v2 and RoB 2. The primary outcome was serum sodium overcorrection using study-defined thresholds. Secondary outcomes included osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS), mortality, hospital length of stay, worsening hyponatremia, and total hypertonic saline volume infused.ResultsSix studies including 1,881 patients were included, of whom 168 received proactive desmopressin. Proactive desmopressin was associated with significantly lower odds of overcorrection compared with non-proactive usual-care strategies (OR, 0.29; 95% CI, 0.19–0.45; p < 0.001; prediction interval, 0.14–0.62). This association was consistent across thresholds of > 8, >10, and > 12 mEq/L at 24 h and > 18 mEq/L at 48 h. Cases of ODS were rare and did not differ significantly between groups. Mortality, hospital length of stay, total hypertonic saline volume infused, and worsening hyponatremia also did not differ significantly between groups.ConclusionsIn adults with moderate-to-severe hyponatremia receiving standard therapy, proactive desmopressin was associated with a lower risk of overcorrection. This reduction was not accompanied by clear differences in ODS, worsening hyponatremia, or mortality. Introduction

Hyponatremia is the most common electrolyte disorder among hospitalized patients, affecting up to 30% of admissions, and is associated with increased morbidity, mortality, length of stay, and healthcare utilization [1–4]. In contemporary inpatient settings, most cases reflect impaired renal free water excretion driven by non-osmotic antidiuretic hormone (ADH) release rather than primary sodium depletion [3–5]. While hypertonic saline remains the cornerstone of therapy for symptomatic hyponatremia, reversal of the underlying stimulus for antidiuretic hormone secretion, such as volume repletion, withdrawal of offending medications, or resolution of acute illness, may precipitate abrupt spontaneous aquaresis. This process can result in rapid and often unpredictable increases in serum sodium concentration. In chronic hyponatremia, such rapid correction is believed to increase the risk of osmotic demyelination syndrome, although the strength and specificity of this association remain subjects of ongoing debate [6–8].

Current American and European guidelines recommend limiting the rate of serum sodium correction to reduce the possible risk of neurological injury [5, 9]. Nevertheless, overcorrection remains common, particularly among patients with profound hyponatremia, hypokalemia, low-solute intake, hypovolemia after volume repletion, drug-related hyponatremia after withdrawal of the offending agent, or other reversible stimuli for antidiuretic hormone release [7, 10, 11]. Separately, patients with alcoholism, malnutrition, hypokalemia, very low baseline serum sodium, or advanced liver disease are considered at increased risk for osmotic demyelination if overly rapid correction occurs [3, 4, 9, 12]. In this context, desmopressin has emerged as a pharmacologic strategy to stabilize water balance during correction by sustaining antidiuretic hormone activity, thereby attenuating uncontrolled water diuresis and reducing the risk of inadvertent overcorrection [13–15]. As a result, the use of desmopressin has expanded, yet its application remains heterogeneous. Three distinct strategies have been described in clinical practice: proactive administration before sodium correction begins, reactive administration in response to an early rapid rise in serum sodium, and rescue administration after overcorrection has already occurred [16]. Despite growing adoption, the choice among these approaches is largely guided by institutional culture or clinician preference, and comparative evidence remains limited.

Against this background, no prior systematic review and meta-analysis have directly compared proactive desmopressin with non-proactive usual-care strategies, including reactive and rescue approaches, across clinically relevant definitions of overcorrection. This evidence gap limits the development of standardized, risk-adapted correction strategies and likely contributes to ongoing variability in clinical practice. Accordingly, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of proactive desmopressin compared with non-proactive usual-care strategies in adults with moderate-to-severe hyponatremia.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [17] and is reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement [18]. The protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO: CRD420251043907).

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed in PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and the Cochrane Library from database inception through February 2026. The search strategy was initially developed and run through August 18, 2025. To ensure currency before final analysis, all searches were updated and rerun on February 12, 2026. The final data lock date was February 12, 2026. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary (MeSH and Emtree terms) and free-text keywords related to hyponatremia and desmopressin, including “hyponatremia,” “desmopressin,” “DDAVP,” “sodium correction,” “hypertonic saline,” “overcorrection,” and “osmotic demyelination.” No language, date, or publication status restrictions were applied. Full electronic search strategies are provided in Supplemental Table 1. Reference lists of eligible studies and relevant reviews were manually screened to identify additional studies.

Study selection

After duplicate removal, two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts for eligibility. Full texts of potentially relevant reports were subsequently assessed in duplicate. Discrepancies at any stage were resolved by consensus. Only full-text articles reporting extractable outcome data were considered eligible.

Eligibility criteria

Eligible studies were randomized controlled trials or comparative cohort studies enrolling adults (≥ 18 years) with moderate-to-severe hypotonic hyponatremia. Studies were required to evaluate a proactive desmopressin strategy, defined as administration of desmopressin before serum sodium exceeded prespecified correction limits, and to compare this approach with non-proactive usual-care strategies, including reactive administration, rescue administration, mixed or no standardized desmopressin use, or no desmopressin.

Moderate-to-severe hypotonic hyponatremia was defined as serum sodium < 130 mEq/L with biochemical or clinical classification compatible with hypotonic hyponatremia; moderate hyponatremia was defined as serum sodium 125–129 mEq/L and severe hyponatremia as serum sodium < 125 mEq/L [4, 5, 9]. Studies enrolling symptomatic patients with serum sodium < 130 mEq/L were also considered eligible when consistent with the original study definition of severe or clinically significant hyponatremia.

For this review, proactive desmopressin was defined as desmopressin administered at the initiation of active hyponatremia treatment, typically concurrently or near-concurrently with 3% hypertonic saline, and before a meaningful rise in serum sodium or spontaneous aquaresis had occurred. Reactive desmopressin was defined as administration after correction had begun to accelerate, or after increased urine output suggested emerging water diuresis, but before prespecified correction limits were exceeded. Rescue desmopressin was defined as administration after overcorrection had already occurred, generally with hypotonic fluid to relower serum sodium. Across control groups, sodium correction was otherwise managed according to standard hyponatremia management at the discretion of the treating clinical team. This prespecified classification allowed a consistent and clinically meaningful comparison between preemptive stabilization of water balance and usual-care management.

Studies were required to report at least one prespecified outcome related to sodium correction, including rates of overcorrection, osmotic demyelination syndrome, or mortality. Case series, single-arm studies, conference abstracts, reviews, animal studies, and studies without extractable outcome data were excluded.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the incidence of sodium overcorrection. Consistent with definitions used in prior systematic reviews and major international guidelines on hyponatremia management, overcorrection was defined as any increase in serum sodium greater than 8, 10, or 12 mEq/L within 24 h, or greater than 18 mEq/L within 48 h. These thresholds represent widely accepted correction limits and are routinely applied as standardized criteria for evaluating treatment safety in studies of hyponatremia [5, 9, 19].

Secondary outcomes comprised in-hospital or short-term mortality, hospital length of stay, osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS), total hypertonic saline volume administered, change in plasma sodium concentration from baseline to 24 h after treatment initiation, need for sodium relowering therapy, worsening hyponatremia (negative delta), and treatment-related adverse events. ODS was defined according to the clinical diagnosis reported in each study and, when available, corroborated by neuroimaging. Mortality was analyzed as reported by the original studies, most commonly as in-hospital or 28- to 30-day mortality. Overcorrection was defined as the primary outcome, and all other endpoints were considered secondary outcomes.

Data extraction

Two reviewers independently extracted data using a standardized form. Extracted variables included study design, setting, sample size, baseline patient characteristics, hyponatremia etiology, initial serum sodium concentration, details of desmopressin timing and dosing, and outcome definitions. When multiple reports originated from the same institution, study populations were carefully evaluated to avoid inclusion of overlapping cohorts; when overlap could not be excluded, the most comprehensive dataset was retained.

Risk of bias and publication bias assessment

Randomized trials were evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool (RoB 2) [20], and observational studies were assessed using the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions tool, version 2 (ROBINS-I v2) [21]. Disagreements were resolved by consensus.

Publication bias was qualitatively examined through visual inspection of funnel plot symmetry for the primary outcome. Given the small number of studies available, no formal tests for small-study effects, such as Egger’s regression, were performed.

Statistical analysis

Binary outcomes were pooled as odds ratios (ORs) and continuous outcomes as mean differences (MDs), each with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Pairwise meta-analyses were conducted using inverse-variance random-effects models. For the primary outcome, the between-study variance was estimated using restricted maximum likelihood (REML), and confidence intervals were recalculated using the Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman method to provide more conservative estimates of uncertainty given the limited number of studies [22]. For the secondary outcomes, REML-based random-effects models were used. Rare outcomes were synthesized using DerSimonian–Laird random-effects models because sparse events may compromise the stability of alternative estimators.

Between-study heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q test and quantified with the I² statistic; tau² was reported as the estimate of between-study variance. Prespecified sensitivity analyses for the primary outcome included leave-one-out analyses.

For studies with three intervention arms, a supplementary frequentist network meta-analysis was undertaken to account for the multi-arm design and to compare proactive desmopressin, reactive/rescue desmopressin, and no desmopressin within a unified framework. Owing to the limited size and sparsity of the treatment network, a common-effect model was used. Relative treatment effects were reported as ORs with 95% CIs, treatment hierarchy was explored using P-scores, and inconsistency was examined using node-splitting approaches when permitted by the network geometry.

All analyses were performed in R, version 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Use of artificial intelligence tools

Artificial intelligence–assisted tools were used solely for language editing and stylistic refinement of the manuscript. These tools were not used for study design, data collection, data extraction, statistical analysis, interpretation of results, or generation of scientific content. All analysis and conclusions are the responsibility of the authors.

Patient characteristics

A total of 1,881 patients were included in this meta-analysis, of whom 168 (8.9%) received proactive desmopressin. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. SIAD was the most frequently reported etiology of hyponatremia across the included studies, followed by hypovolemia; other reported causes included low-solute intake, euvolemic and hypervolemic states, and mixed etiologies, depending on the study [23, 24, 26–28].

Definitions of overcorrection and desmopressin strategies are detailed in Supplementary Table S2. Overcorrection thresholds ranged from > 8 to > 12 mEq/L within the first 24 h and from > 18 mEq/L within 48 h. Comparator groups across studies, including conventional management without desmopressin, no proactive desmopressin with allowance for reactive/rescue use, reactive-only strategies, or no desmopressin during the initial treatment period were shown Supplementary Table S2.

Standard hyponatremia treatment and cointerventions are detailed in Supplementary Table S3. Hypertonic saline was the backbone of treatment in most studies, although the reported use of fluid restriction, isotonic crystalloids, loop diuretics, urea, salt tablets, corticosteroid replacement, and relowering strategies varied considerably across cohorts; some studies followed protocolized approaches, whereas others relied on clinician-directed management.

Primary Outcome

Hyponatremia overcorrection

Across six studies involving 1,881 patients, proactive desmopressin was associated with significantly lower odds of overcorrection than non-proactive usual-care strategies (OR, 0.29; 95% CI, 0.19–0.45; p < 0.001) (Fig. 2). No statistical heterogeneity was detected (tau² = 0; I² = 0.0%; Q-test p = 0.895), and the prediction interval was 0.14–0.62.

Across the prespecified overcorrection thresholds, the direction of effect was consistent and favored proactive desmopressin throughout. At the > 8 mEq/L in 24 h threshold, proactive desmopressin was associated with reduced odds of overcorrection (OR, 0.33; 95% CI, 0.16–0.69; p < 0.001), although moderate heterogeneity was present (I² = 49.8%). This protective association persisted at the > 10 mEq/L in 24 h threshold (OR, 0.24; 95% CI, 0.08–0.73; p = 0.01), the > 12 mEq/L in 24 h threshold (OR, 0.13; 95% CI, 0.02–0.64; p = 0.013), and the > 18 mEq/L in 48 h threshold (OR, 0.30; 95% CI, 0.11–0.80; p = 0.017), with no heterogeneity detected in these analyses (all I² = 0.0%) (Supplementary Figure 1).

Sensitivity analyses

Leave-one-out analyses showed that exclusion of any single study did not materially change the direction or statistical significance of the pooled estimate (Supplementary Figure 2).

Secondary outcomes

No significant differences were observed between proactive desmopressin and other desmopressin strategies across the main secondary outcomes. Osmotic demyelination syndrome was rare and occurred at similar rates in both groups (OR, 2.92; 95% CI, 0.67–12.73; p = 0.15; I² = 0.0%), as did in-hospital mortality (OR, 1.09; 95% CI, 0.49–2.46; p = 0.82; I² = 0.0%). Hospital length of stay was comparable between groups in four studies including 1,705 patients (MD, − 0.58 days; 95% CI, − 2.27 to 1.12; p = 0.505; I² = 0.0%). Cumulative 3% hypertonic saline volume and change in plasma sodium concentration from baseline to 24 h were also similar between groups, with no statistically significant differences observed for either outcome (MD, 126.00 mL; 95% CI, − 0.06 to 252.06; p = 0.07; I² = 77.9%; and MD, − 1.12 mEq/L; 95% CI, − 2.34 to 0.09; p = 0.07; I² = 62.1%, respectively).

Safety analyses likewise showed no significant differences in the need for plasma sodium relowering therapy (OR, 0.66; 95% CI, 0.36–1.20; p = 0.17; I² = 14.6%) or in worsening hyponatremia, defined as a negative plasma sodium delta (OR, 0.59; 95% CI, 0.31–1.13; p = 0.11; I² = 11.8%) Fig. 3 and Supplementary Figure 3.

Network meta-analysis

The network meta-analysis of overcorrection included three treatment strategies: No DDAVP, Reactive/Rescue DDAVP, and Proactive DDAVP. The evidence network was fully connected, with direct comparisons available across all pairwise contrasts. No DDAVP and Proactive DDAVP were represented in five studies, whereas Reactive/Rescue DDAVP was represented in six studies (Fig. 4).

In the direct evidence analysis, No DDAVP versus Reactive/Rescue DDAVP yielded an odds ratio (OR) of 0.50 (95% CI, 0.38–0.66; I² = 72.8%), No DDAVP versus Proactive DDAVP yielded an OR of 2.55 (95% CI, 1.19–5.47; I² = 34.1%), and Reactive/Rescue versus Proactive DDAVP yielded an OR of 2.11 (95% CI, 1.06–4.18; I² = 74.9%) (Fig. 4).

In the common-effects network model, using No DDAVP as the reference treatment, the OR for overcorrection was 0.65 (95% CI, 0.34–1.23) for Proactive DDAVP and 1.94 (95% CI, 1.47–2.56) for Reactive/Rescue DDAVP (Fig. 4). Treatment ranking based on P-scores placed Proactive DDAVP first (0.9529), followed by No DDAVP (0.5469), and Reactive/Rescue DDAVP last (0.0002).

Risk of bias and publication bias assessment

Using the RoB-2 and ROBINS-I v2 tools, five of the six included studies were judged to have a favorable risk-of-bias profile across all assessed domains, whereas one observational study [27] showed important limitations in the domains of missing data and outcome measurement, resulting in an overall high risk-of-bias judgment (Supplementary Figure 4). Among the observational studies, most cohorts were rated favorably for confounding, intervention classification, participant selection, and reporting domains, with the main methodological concerns concentrated in a single study. The randomized trial was judged to have a low risk of bias across all RoB-2 domains. Overall, these findings suggest that the body of evidence was predominantly composed of studies with low risk of bias, although one observational study remained vulnerable to bias related to incomplete data and outcome assessment.

Evaluation of publication bias showed no clear evidence of small-study effects. Although Ward et al. [24] demonstrated a higher standard error, this did not materially affect funnel plot symmetry (Supplementary Figure 5). Following Cochrane guidance, formal tests for asymmetry were not performed because fewer than ten studies were available, a context in which such tests lack interpretability [17]. Publication bias cannot be excluded, but no meaningful visual asymmetry was observed (Supplementary Figure 5).

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis of adults with moderate-to-severe hyponatremia, proactive desmopressin was associated with a markedly lower risk of serum sodium overcorrection compared with other desmopressin strategies. In the primary pooled analysis, proactive desmopressin reduced the odds of overcorrection by approximately 70% (OR, 0.29; 95% CI, 0.19–0.45), with no detectable between-study heterogeneity and a prediction interval that remained in favor of the proactive strategy. This protective association was consistent across all prespecified correction thresholds, including > 8, >10, and > 12 mEq/L within 24 h and > 18 mEq/L within 48 h. Notably, this reduction in overcorrection was not accompanied by significant differences in osmotic demyelination syndrome, in-hospital mortality, hospital length of stay, plasma sodium relowering therapy, or worsening hyponatremia.

The network meta-analysis provided additional insight into how proactive desmopressin compares with other commonly used correction strategies. In the direct comparisons, reactive/rescue desmopressin showed a less favorable profile than both no desmopressin and proactive desmopressin, whereas proactive desmopressin showed the most favorable direction of effect. In the common-effects network model, proactive desmopressin was associated with lower odds of overcorrection than no desmopressin, while reactive/rescue desmopressin was associated with significantly higher odds of overcorrection relative to no desmopressin. Treatment ranking by P-scores placed proactive desmopressin as the best-performing strategy, followed by no desmopressin, with reactive/rescue desmopressin ranking last. Although these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the small and sparse treatment network, they support the concept that anticipatory control of water diuresis may be more effective than delayed intervention after sodium has already begun to rise, while also suggesting that reactive/rescue desmopressin use may partly reflect confounding by indication in patients already on a trajectory toward overcorrection.

The biologic rationale for proactive desmopressin is grounded in the pathophysiology of hyponatremia correction. In common etiologies such as syndrome of inappropriate antidiuresis, hypovolemia, drug-related hyponatremia, and low-solute intake, reversal of the underlying stimulus for vasopressin secretion may trigger abrupt spontaneous aquaresis, leading to a rapid and sometimes unpredictable rise in serum sodium [7, 16]. This shift is clinically important because uncontrolled water diuresis is a major mechanism of inadvertent overcorrection and may contribute to osmotic demyelination in susceptible patients [6, 12, 16]. By maintaining antidiuretic activity during the initial phase of treatment, desmopressin can blunt this transition and make correction more controlled and titratable, allowing sodium increase to be driven more by planned therapy than by abrupt endogenous water losses [13, 14]. This strategy may be particularly relevant in patients at higher risk of overcorrection, including those with very low baseline sodium, hypokalemia, or severe symptoms, and may be informed by validated tools such as the SHOR and NASK scores [10, 11].

Within this framework, our findings reinforce and extend prior clinical observations on the role of desmopressin in stabilizing serum sodium correction. Earlier physiologic and clinical studies established the importance of uncontrolled water diuresis in overcorrection [2, 7, 16, 29], but comparative evidence regarding the optimal timing of desmopressin use has remained limited. Although several observational cohorts and a recent randomized trial have described experience with proactive, reactive, or rescue strategies [23–28], no previous synthesis had specifically compared proactive desmopressin with usual-care approaches in a unified analytic framework. Our results do not negate the role of reactive or rescue desmopressin; rather, they could suggest that earlier use may provide more consistent control of sodium correction without an apparent increase in adverse outcomes, supporting a preventive, structured approach in which desmopressin serves as an adjunct to close monitoring rather than a substitute for it.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Most included studies were observational, making the pooled estimates susceptible to residual confounding. In addition, clinically relevant aspects of management varied across studies, including overcorrection definitions, sodium-monitoring frequency, desmopressin timing and dosing, and hypertonic saline protocols. Safety outcomes such as osmotic demyelination syndrome were rare, limiting the precision of effect estimates, and the small number of eligible studies limited formal assessment of publication bias. Incomplete reporting of treatment timing, dosing, sodium-monitoring intervals, and baseline risk factors also precluded a more granular evaluation of dose–response relationships or stratified effects according to baseline risk of overcorrection or osmotic demyelination. Residual confounding by hyponatremia etiology is particularly important, as etiology was not consistently reported by treatment arm and therefore could not be explored through subgroup analysis or meta-regression. This limitation is clinically relevant because SIAD may carry a lower intrinsic risk of abrupt spontaneous aquaresis than hypovolemic, low-solute, or drug-related hyponatremia after reversal of the underlying stimulus. Thus, if SIAD was more prevalent in the proactive desmopressin arm than in comparator groups, the apparent protective association of proactive desmopressin may have been overestimated. In the largest contributing cohort with etiology data, the adjusted model accounted for hypokalemia, severe hyponatremia, and seizures, but not hyponatremia etiology, leaving residual confounding from etiologic imbalance possible.

Despite these limitations, the present study helps organize the available evidence on proactive desmopressin in hyponatremia into a coherent analytic framework. The consistency of the association across clinically relevant correction thresholds, together with the absence of a clear signal of excess harm, suggests that this strategy may be clinically useful. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution given the limited number of studies and the predominance of observational data. Additional prospective studies, ideally randomized and conducted with standardized correction protocols, are needed to better define the role of proactive desmopressin in clinical practice.

Future research should focus on clarifying which patients are most likely to benefit, as well as the optimal timing and dosing of treatment. Incorporation of validated risk tools such as the SHOR [10] and NASK [11] scores may help support a more individualized approach, while studies examining the interaction between desmopressin, hypertonic saline regimens, and dynamic predictors of aquaresis could further refine risk–benefit assessment, particularly in patients at highest risk for osmotic demyelination. Taken together, the current evidence suggests that proactive desmopressin is a promising strategy for limiting overcorrection, but its place within standardized correction algorithms still requires confirmation in higher-quality studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, proactive desmopressin was associated with a lower risk of serum sodium overcorrection in adults with moderate-to-severe hyponatremia, without a clear difference in neurologic complications, worsening hyponatremia, or mortality. These findings suggest that a proactive strategy may be a useful approach to improve control of sodium correction, although the evidence remains limited by the small number of studies and the predominance of observational data. Further prospective studies are needed to better define the optimal timing, dosing, and patient selection for this strategy.
