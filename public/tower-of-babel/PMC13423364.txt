Opioid Prescribing Patterns Associated with Selective Matrixectomies for Toenail Onychocryptosis: A TriNetX Analysis
Authors: Alexa S. Podolsky, Jose W. Ricardo, Tracey C. Vlahovic, Shari R. Lipner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSelective matrixectomy is a common intervention for painful onychocryptosis that is associated with mild-to-moderate postoperative pain. It can usually be managed effectively with non-opioid analgesics. We evaluated rates and factors associated with opioid prescribing following selective matrixectomy to guide physicians in counseling postoperative patients.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted using the TriNetX research network, querying for patients who underwent selective matrixectomy for onychocryptosis. Opioid prescriptions <3 days after selective matrixectomy were assessed, and Cox regression estimated hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals for opioid prescribing, adjusting for factors associated with prolonged opioid use.ResultsAmong 97,208 patients who underwent selective matrixectomy, 14,759 received an opioid prescription <3 days postoperatively (15.2%), declining from 24.3% during 2008–2018 to 12.8% during 2018–2025. Female sex, older age, Asian race, antidepressant use, and histories of anxiety, depression, chronic pain, migraine, osteoarthritis, and back pain were associated with lower likelihood of opioid prescribing. Black/African American race, Hispanic/Latino ethnicity, prior benzodiazepine or opioid use, and fibromyalgia were associated with higher likelihood.ConclusionOpioid prescribing following selective matrixectomy was relatively common and may be more frequent than clinically necessary. Prescribing patterns appear influenced by demographic and clinical factors, suggesting an opportunity to refine postoperative pain management strategies.

Introduction

Onychocryptosis, or incurvated lateral nail borders with the nail cutting into the lateral nail groove, is 1 of the most common nail pathologies, and selective matrixectomy is an effective intervention to relieve pain and prevent recurrence [1]. Postoperative pain following selective matrixectomy is typically mild-to-moderate and can usually be adequately managed with non-opioid analgesics [1, 2]. However, prior studies have shown persistent opioid prescribing [3], and factors associated with its use are poorly characterized. Thus, we conducted a retrospective cohort study to evaluate rates and predictors of opioid prescribing following selective matrixectomy.

Methods

On September 12, 2025, TriNetX research network, including patient data from ≥100 healthcare organizations that ensures internal data validity through review and quality checks, including record consolidation, was queried 2008–2025 for all patients with vs. without opioid prescription <3 days after selective matrixectomy for painful onychocryptosis. A secondary analysis evaluated prescriptions <3 days before and after selective matrixectomy to evaluate for potential standing opioid prescriptions. Demographic characteristics of the cohort were analyzed and summarized. Cox regression calculated hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) of opioid prescription <3 days after selective matrixectomy for the overall selective matrixectomy cohort, evaluating covariate risk factors associated with prolonged opioid use [4] within the patient’s history <3 years prior to selective matrixectomy. Because opioid prescriptions were highly clustered around the procedure within the 3-day interval, HRs were interpreted as estimates of the relative likelihood of receiving a prescription within the postoperative window.

Results

Overall, 97,208 patients underwent selective matrixectomy for painful onychocryptosis, with 14,759 prescribed opioids (opioid prescription rate 15.2%). Opioid prescription rates <3 days before and after selective matrixectomy (15.9%) were similar, indicating minimal pre-procedure prescribing and clustering around the selective matrixectomy encounter. The relatively high short-interval rate supports a temporal association with selective matrixectomy. The opioid prescription rate <3 days of post-selective matrixectomy decreased from 24.3% during 2008–2018 to 12.8% during 2018–2025.

Patients who underwent selective matrixectomy for onychocryptosis were, on average, 40.1-years, 50.9% female, 49.1% male, and 76.8% White (Table 1). Cox regression analysis demonstrated that female sex (HR 0.955, 95% CI: 0.922–0.990), older age (0.983, 0.982–0.984), Asian race (0.719, 0.613–0.845), antidepressant use (0.932, 0.890–0.977), and histories of anxiety (0.846, 0.805–0.889), depression (0.792, 0.727–0.862), chronic pain (0.703, 0.666–0.742), migraine (0.838, 0.784–0.896), osteoarthritis (0.935, 0.887–0.986), and back pain (0.762, 0.728–0.797) were associated with a lower likelihood of opioid prescribing after selective matrixectomy, whereas Black/African American race (1.559, 1.453–1.672), Hispanic/Latino ethnicity (1.290, 1.225–1.358), prior benzodiazepine (1.176, 1.131–1.224) or opioid use (8.168, 7.802–8.550), and fibromyalgia (1.136, 1.035–1.247) were associated with increased opioid prescribing. No significant associations were observed with substance use disorders (Table 2).

Discussion

We found that opioids were prescribed ∼1 in 7 patients following selective matrixectomy, which raises concern for overutilization since the procedure has a mild-to-moderate postoperative pain profile. A systematic review of 18 studies evaluating patient-reported outcomes in toenail onychocryptosis demonstrated high postoperative satisfaction and improvement in pain following selective matrixectomy, without evidence of severe or persistent postoperative pain [5], suggesting adequacy of non-opioid analgesic strategies.

Similarly, Podolsky et al.’s [6] retrospective TriNetX database study of 12,511 nail biopsies found an opioid prescription rate of 14.5%, raising similar concerns of overuse due to demonstrated efficacy of non-opioid analgesic regimens for post-biopsy pain [7]. Nail biopsies tend to be more invasive procedures compared to selective matrixectomy and are associated with greater postoperative pain [8]. Notably, a TriNetX cohort study reported an opioid prescription rate of 13.2% <14-days following Mohs micrographic surgery [4]. Therefore, our finding of comparable opioid prescribing after selective matrixectomy to nail biopsy and Mohs micrographic surgery suggests overuse. In a survey-based study of 860 podiatrists, only 8.7% reported prescribing opioids after routine chemical matrixectomy for toenail onychocryptosis [3], demonstrating variability in prescribing practices. Overall, these findings highlight discordance between expected post-selective matrixectomy pain and current opioid prescribing practices.

Additionally, we found that Black/African American race, Hispanic/Latino ethnicity, fibromyalgia, and prior benzodiazepine or opioid use were associated with greater likelihood of opioid prescribing, whereas older age, female sex, Asian race, antidepressant use, and histories of anxiety, depression, chronic pain, migraine, osteoarthritis, and back pain were associated with lower likelihood. These findings may reflect variability in pain perception, reporting, and physician prescribing behavior rather than differences in expected postoperative analgesic needs. Physicians may exercise opioid-sparing behavior when treating patients with preexisting chronic pain-related comorbidities as these patients are more likely to be engaged in chronic pain management with multimodal pharmacologic therapy [9]. Patients receiving long-term analgesic regimens may be managed more conservatively due to concerns about tolerance, opioid-induced hyperalgesia, and postoperative complications associated with chronic opioid exposure [9]. Similarly, Podolsky et al.’s [6] study of patients who underwent nail biopsy reported decreased likelihood of opioid prescription among patients with older age (HR = 0.989, 95% CI: 0.986–0.992), anxiety (0.817, 0.702–0.951), and chronic pain (0.827, 0.707–0.968) and increased likelihood among patients with prior opioid use (1.374, 1.211–1.558) and fibromyalgia (1.299, 1.037–1.629).

We also observed a temporal decline in opioid prescribing post-selective matrixectomy. This reduction may reflect increasing awareness of opioid stewardship and greater adoption of non-opioid postoperative pain management strategies. Physicians and podiatrists may be more likely to prescribe opioids for surgical vs. chemical matrixectomy due to differential recovery time, as well as procedure duration and complexity, which may have impacted results. Collectively, these prescribing patterns underscore the need for standardized postoperative prescribing practices that optimize postoperative pain control while minimizing unnecessary opioid exposure. However, select patients may benefit from short-course opioid therapy.

Limitations include potential miscoding and inability to assess physician-type and experience, indication, anatomical location (fingernail vs. toenail) or selective matrixectomy technique (surgical vs. chemical), or distinguish standing from selective matrixectomy-specific opioid prescriptions. Since the TriNetX platform does not support multivariable logistic regression modeling, multivariable Cox regression was used to estimate the relative likelihood of opioid prescription within a fixed interval. Association does not imply causation. Analyzed time frames are estimates.

Conclusion

Opioid prescribing following selective matrixectomy for onychocryptosis may exceed expected analgesic requirements and may be influenced by patient demographics and comorbidities. Emphasizing non-opioid analgesia with selective, individualized opioid use may improve opioid stewardship.

Statement of Ethics

This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was not received for this human study because this study utilized de-identified, aggregated data from the TriNetX Research Network. Adult participant consent was not required because institutional policy and federal regulations governing human subjects research depict that this analysis did not require Institutional Review Board approval or informed consent. This retrospective study is exempt from informed consent. The data reviewed are a secondary analysis of existing data, does not involve intervention or interaction with human subjects, and is de-identified per the de-identification standard defined in Section 164.514(a) of the HIPAA Privacy Rule. The process by which the data are de-identified is attested to through a formal determination by a qualified expert as defined in Section 164.514(b)(1) of the HIPAA Privacy Rule.
