 ABSTRACT

The work presented in this report is part of the ongoing efforts to address the nuclear material control and
accounting needs for advanced reactor fuel fabrication facilities. This work was supported by the
Materials Protection, Accounting, and Control Technologies (MPACT) program under the US
Department of Energy Office of Nuclear Energy‘s Nuclear Fuel Cycle and Supply Chain program. The
activities and engagements under the MPACT program are designed to support a robust US civilian
nuclear energy enterprise. In the work described in this report, we supported MPACT objectives by
developing measurement techniques that could be used for material accounting and process monitoring
and by working with industry partners to identify existing gaps and areas for improvement.

Oak Ridge National Laboratory has been working with commercial tristructural isotropic (TRISO) fuel
fabricators such as Standard Nuclear to develop technology for rapid and cost-effective uranium content
assessment. This work has focused on demonstrating advanced measurement techniques (e.g., Raman
spectroscopy) that can be used for rapid, reliable, and cost-effective routine measurements of uranium
content in feed solutions and liquid waste streams as well as for monitoring in-line process measurements
and product streams. Specifically, this report explores techniques for accurately determining uranium
content in acid-deficient uranyl nitrate (ADUN) solutions and detecting low uranium concentrations in
ammonia solutions. Developing such measurement techniques will benefit TRISO fuel fabrication
facilities, facilities involved in other parts of the fuel cycle that require online monitoring of aqueous
solutions, and potentially molten salt fuel reactors.

This work supports developing Raman spectroscopy procedures to determine uranium concentrations in
ADUN solutions, which are used as feedstock in the sol–gel process for creating TRISO fuel. Some
additional benefits of using Raman spectroscopy for uranium quantification in fabrication facilities
include enabling online monitoring of the chemical process, which would provide near real-time
feedback; eliminating the need for sample transfers, preparation, or dilution; providing nondestructive
measurements; and user friendliness. In this fiscal year, FY25, we determined the identity of the unknown
Raman band at approximately 853 cm−1 that was discovered in ADUN Raman spectra in FY24, created
calibration curves and determined uranium concentrations of two ADUN solutions, and compared the
Raman results to results obtained from inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry and Davies–Gray
titration. Furthermore, we have identified focus areas for experimentation in future fiscal years.

A key result is that the accuracy of using Raman can provide accuracy comparable to destructive analysist
techniques, With a well-developed calibration curve, using standards and a large number of samples
(more than five samples), uncertainty on the order of 1%–3% is achievable. Given that the uncertainties
achieved by Raman spectroscopy were on the order of the uncertainties achieved using ICP-MS, we
conclude that with a well-developed procedure Raman spectroscopy can be used to determine uranium
concentrations in ADUN solutions for NMC&A applications. The benefits of such an approach are that
the time and effort will be less than that of comparable destructive analysis techniques, with
approximately the same level of technical expertise. This will be attractive to operators of fuel fabrication
facilities as it will lower costs and improve efficiencies

 1. INTRODUCTION

This report was produced for the Materials Protection, Accounting, and Control Technologies (MPACT)
program under the US Department of Energy Office of Nuclear Energy‘s Nuclear Fuel Cycle and Supply
Chain program under Work Package Number FT-25OR04010206. The work described in this report
builds on results obtained in FY24 and reported in Material Control and Accounting Concepts for US
TRISO Fuel Fabrication Facilities. The 2024 report identified Raman spectroscopy and laser-induced
fluorescence spectroscopy as techniques that can be used for nuclear material control and accounting
(NMC&A) in a tristructural isotropic (TRISO) fuel fabrication facility [1].

Optical spectroscopy and image analysis techniques are advantageous in studying nuclear materials; they
provide information regarding morphology, chemical composition, chemical speciation, and
concentration by generating multivariate data. They require small aliquots, can be performed remotely,
and sometimes require minimal or no sample preparation. Optical spectroscopy techniques, such as
Raman and visible/near infrared (Vis/NIR) spectroscopy, can be used for online process control and
special nuclear material accountability applications in fuel manufacturing. These techniques offer the
benefits of being fast, nondestructive, and sensitive to a large variety of target analytes [2-6].

Although optical spectroscopy lacks the limits of detection of techniques such as mass spectrometry, it
provides real-time information that few other approaches can provide, including oxidation states and
chemical speciation [6-10]. In recent years, optical spectroscopic methods have demonstrated
considerable potential for use in the chemical analysis of various materials of interest to the nuclear
science community, including nuclear fuels, contamination compounds, and nuclear detonation debris [3,
6, 9, 11, 12]. The availability of online, real-time techniques that directly measure process concentrations
of nuclear materials could enhance process performance and improve plant operations and safety.

In the nuclear field, Raman spectroscopy has already been used for a few decades to study and identify
actinide compounds [2, 8, 9, 13-20]. Raman spectroscopy is used to detect how molecular vibrations
affect the polarizability of a chemical bond. The interaction of photons with a molecule gives rise to
Raman scattering. The peaks in a Raman spectrum are related to individual chemical bonds within a
material. Raman spectra contain valuable information related to a material’s chemical structure. Band
attributes (e.g., position, full width at half maximum, intensity, splitting) may be affected by temperature,
crystal lattice defects, chemical environment, and other perturbations. Raman spectroscopy is also a
powerful tool for fast, nondestructive 2D and even 3D mapping of materials. Raman maps show the
spatial distribution of spectral (i.e., chemical) information across a given sample.

Using Raman spectroscopy for quantitative analysis requires an understanding of the cause of different
uncertainties associated with the technique, including equipment-related factors, sample-related and
environmental factors, and analytical and data processing factors. Equipment-related factors include the
following:

 Laser and optical stability: Variations in the laser’s power, wavelength, and focus can affect signal
 intensity and resolution.

 Detector noise: The level of background signal (noise) from the detector affects signal-to-noise ratio,
 a critical factor in quantifying low-intensity peaks.

 Spectral resolution: The instrument’s ability to distinguish between closely spaced peaks affects the
 accuracy of peak fitting and subsequent quantification. Slit or pinhole size is a primary factor.

 2

 Wavenumber calibration: Accurate peak position measurement is essential, especially for
 identifying specific chemical species. Uncertainty in calibration affects the precision of
 measurements.

Sample-related and environmental factors include the following:

 Sample heterogeneity: In the analysis of bulk material, any compositional inhomogeneity may lead
 to variations between measurements and increase uncertainty.

 Sample alignment and positioning: Minor changes in how the laser interacts with the sample
 (e.g., drift, focus, or angle) may affect the collected signal.

 Temperature effects: The position and width of Raman peaks may be affected by temperature, a
 factor that requires careful control or adjustments, especially in molten salt or high-temperature
 applications.

 Fluorescence interference: Naturally fluorescent samples can overwhelm the weaker Raman signal,
 reduce the signal-to-noise ratio, and increase measurement uncertainty.

Analytical and data processing factors include the following:

 Calibration model: In quantitative analysis, the uncertainty of the calibration model (e.g., partial
 least squares) is an important factor. A model’s accuracy is influenced by the quality and number of
 reference standards used.

 Statistical analysis: The method used to process spectra and calculate uncertainty is important. For
 example, standards such as ASTM E2586 provide statistical methods for estimating uncertainty.

 Curve fitting: The accuracy of the fitting of analytical functions (e.g., Gaussian or Lorentzian) to
 spectral peaks may introduce uncertainty, especially for overlapping peaks.

 Signal averaging: The number of spectra averaged and the exposure time per spectrum influence the
 final signal-to-noise ratio. Increasing these parameters can reduce random noise and decrease
 uncertainty.

The benefits of using Raman for nondestructive assay include providing in-line capabilities and
eliminating the need for sample preparation. For uranium solutions, Raman spectroscopy is excellent for
identifying the specific uranyl(VI) species because the vibrational frequency of the uranyl ion (UO2+
 2 )
shifts depending on the coordinating ions (e.g., hydroxide, acetate) and pH; these characteristics enable
the use of Raman spectroscopy for monitoring and identifying different uranyl complexes in situ [21-23].
Uranyl peak intensity is directly proportional to uranium concentration. However, spectral features
resulting from complex and dynamic solution conditions must be accounted for.

 2. EXPERIMENTAL

The Radioactive Materials Analytical Laboratory transferred 1,500 µL of acid-deficient uranyl nitrate
(ADUN) solutions (1_A, 1_B, and 1_C) to the Radiochemical Process Development group for Raman
spectroscopic analysis. Table 1 provides details for each sample. The ADUN solutions were prepared by
Ultra Safe Nuclear Corporation (USNC), now Standard Nuclear. The as-received solutions (undiluted)
were diluted twofold and tenfold using deionized water. All solutions were prepared in 1.8 mL

undiluted, twofold diluted, and tenfold diluted samples were analyzed directly in the vials using Raman
spectroscopy. Samples 1_B and 1_C were analyzed four times; one dataset was used for the calibration
curve, and the others were treated as unknown. Because of unforeseen complications, only samples 1_B
and 1_C were analyzed in FY25.

Raman spectra were recorded in triplicate using an iHR320 imaging spectrometer (Horiba Scientific)

1. The three curves were averaged over the entire data range (500–3,850 cm−1).
2. The average spectrum was plotted between 820 and 900 cm−1.
3. The background was subtracted using the asymmetric least squares smoothing method.
4. The peaks were identified and fitted using the Gaussian–Lorentzian method.
5. Intensity versus concentration was plotted for the undiluted, twofold diluted, and tenfold diluted
 solutions.
6. A calibration curve was generated from a linear fit to the data.

 4. DISCUSSION

Raman spectroscopy is an excellent technique for identifying specific uranyl(VI) species because the
vibrational frequency of the uranyl ion (UO2+2 ) shifts depending on the coordinating ions (e.g., hydroxide,
acetate) and pH, which allows for monitoring and identifying different uranyl complexes in situ [21-23].
Raman spectroscopy was used to quantify uranium content in two high-concentration ADUN samples.
The samples were initially characterized using ICP-MS and Davies–Gray titration at least 1 year before
the Raman measurements were taken; thus, sample storage may be responsible for some errors. In FY24,
initial Raman measurements for three ADUN samples provided evidence of two different environments
for the U(IV) because the UO2+2 band centered at approximately 870 cm was deconvoluted with two
 −1

peaks (~853 cm and 870 cm ). However, further investigation of the nature of the Raman band around
 −1 −1

853 cm−1 was needed to understand if and how it would influence the determination of uranium
concentration. Furthermore, in FY24 the actual concentration of the uranium in the ADUN samples was
not determined. Thus, the goal for this fiscal year was to determine the nature of the 853 cm−1 Raman
band and to determine the uranium concentration of the ADUN samples using Raman spectroscopy and
compare the results to the concentrations obtained using ICP-MS and Davies–Gray titration.

4.1 NATURE OF THE 850 CM−1 BAND IN ADUN RAMAN SPECTROSCOPY

Uranyl(VI) nitrate complex formation has been extensively studied using optical spectroscopy. In Raman
spectra, the ν1 band at 870 cm−1 for UO2+
 2 has been found to be relatively insensitive to changes in
coordination, even as nitric acid concentration varies [10]. Thus, an appropriate conclusion is that the

 8

band at 853 cm−1 is not related to one of the nitrate complexes, [UO2(NO3)]+, UO2(NO3)2, and
[UO2(NO3)3]−, given that their Raman bands are well established [7, 10, 21, 22, 25]. Furthermore, the
853 cm−1 band was absent in the uranyl nitrate reference spectrum obtained in FY24 [1].

The ADUN solutions analyzed in this work were prepared by dissolving U3O8 in concentrated nitric acid
and water. The pH of the ADUN solution is a result of UO2+
                                                        2 hydrolysis, as shown in Eq. (4) [26-28]:

 UO2+ + +
                                       2 + H2O ↔[UO2(OH)] + H .                                             (4)

However, UO2(OH)+ tends to form dimers, and the degree of dimerization is dependent on the pH,
uranium concentration, and the temperature of the solution [28]. Therefore, Eq. (4) can be written more
generally as shown in Eq. (5), where (x, y) are stoichiometric coefficients representing the degree of
dimerization [21, 23, 28-30]:

 𝑥UO2+
                                2 + 𝑦H2O↔[(UO2)𝑥(OH)𝑦]
                                                       (2𝑥―𝑦)+ + 𝑦H+.                                       (5)

For the uncomplexed hydrated uranyl ion, UO2+ 2 , the values of (x, y) are (1, 0). For a single dimerization
where the values of (x, y) are (2, 2), Eq. (5) can be written as follows:

 2UO2+ 2+ +
                                     2 +2H2O ↔(UO2)2(OH)2 +2H .                                             (6)

Numerous studies have examined the speciation of uranyl ion in aqueous media. The new band at
approximately 853 cm−1 has also been shown to correspond to the symmetric vibration of the uranyl ion,
νs(UO2+2 ). Toth and Begun [31] first observed a red shift as pH increases with the addition of NaOH to a
uranyl aqueous solution. The shift of this band indicates a different environment of the uranyl ion—that
is, distinct speciation/complexation. Quiles and Burneau [30] indicated that this behavior is due to the
formation of hydroxo complexes with a stoichiometric coefficient (x, y) formed as a function of pH by the
known reaction (Eq. [5]). They found the Raman shifts—870, 853, and 835 cm−1—for the complex with
stoichiometric coefficients of (1, 0), (2 ,2), and (3, 5), respectively. The assignments of these bands were
later confirmed by other authors [21, 32].

Given that explanations of the chemistry of ADUN solutions commonly postulate the presence of
hydrolyzed species such as [UO2(OH)]+ and [(UO2)2(OH)2]2+ and based on the typical condition for
making TRISO fuel kernels using the internal gelation process [28, 33], we have concluded that the
Raman band at 853 cm−1 corresponds to the complex [(UO2)2(OH)2]2+, in alignment with the literature
[21, 26, 27, 29-31].

4.2 CONCENTRATION OF URANIUM IN ADUN SAMPLES

The use of Raman spectroscopy to determine the concentrations of uranium and other species in aqueous
solutions is well documented; it usually involves the generation of a calibration curve that is used to
predict the concentration of the unknown sample. The concentration predicted using quantitative Raman
spectroscopy is compared to the concentration measured by ICP-MS [2, 6, 22, 34, 35]. Generally,
standard uranium nitrate solutions are used to create the calibration curve; however, in our experiments
the ADUN solutions were used to generate the calibration curves. Eqs. (1) and (2) were used to determine
the concentration of uranium in each ADUN solution based on the intensity of the 870 cm−1 peak. Using
calibration curve 1B, the uranium concentrations were underestimated for both solutions, 1B and 1C;
however, the uncertainty was similar to the uncertainty achieved with ICP-MS. With a well-developed
calibration curve, using standards and a large number of samples (more than five samples), uncertainty on
the order of 1%–3% is achievable [36]. The concentrations determined using calibration curve 1C were

 9

similar to those determined using ICP-MS; the value for solution 1B was within 1.4%, whereas the value
for solution 1C was within 2.3%. Given that the uncertainties achieved by Raman spectroscopy were on
the order of the uncertainties achieved using ICP-MS, we conclude that with a well-developed procedure
Raman spectroscopy can be used to determine uranium concentrations in ADUN solutions for NMC&A
applications. In fact, an optical spectroscopy procedure—specifically, a Vis/NIR spectrophotometry
procedure—has been developed to quantify neptunium solutions in hot cells for NMC&A purposes at
Oak Ridge National Laboratory [3].

The benefits of such an approach are that the time and effort will be less than that of comparable
destructive analysis techniques, with approximately the same level of technical expertise. This will be
attractive to operators of fuel fabrication facilities as it will lower costs and improve efficiency.

 5. CONCLUSION

Raman spectroscopy is a promising optical method for the in situ quantification of high-concentration
uranium solutions. Several samples were measured in this work using a 532 nm excitation to evaluate
Raman peaks and peak correlations to sample concentrations. Additional samples should be examined to
understand this system more fully, and the Raman spectral dataset should be expanded to include more
than five samples. Training set samples should be compared to traditional ICP-MS or potentiometric
Davies–Gray titrations to benchmark uranium concentration. Then, univariant or multivariant regression
models can be developed to demonstrate uranium quantification over the range of anticipated conditions.
Once a regression model is developed, the technique can be deployed with calibration transfer functions
for day-to-day measurements.

To determine uranium concentration using Raman spectroscopy, two calibration curves were developed.
Although both curves had uncertainties comparable to those obtained using ICP-MS, the first curve
underpredicted uranium concentration. The second curve predicted a concentration of 356 ± 24 mg/g for
solution 1_B, a value comparable to the one obtained using ICP-MS (361 ± 24 mg/g) and the expected
uranium concentration for both solutions (350 mg/g). This result indicates that Raman spectroscopy is a
promising technique for NMC&A measurements at fuel fabrication facilities.

 6. FUTURE WORK

In this report we have shown that uranium concentrations in high-concentration ADUN solutions can be
quantified using Raman spectroscopic analysis. However, further work is needed to develop the technique
it can provide consistent measurements for online monitoring of ADUN solutions in TRISO fabrication
facilities. In this work, the provided solutions were used to develop a calibration curve; however,
generation of a calibration curve using standard solutions may be beneficial. Understanding how the
aging of a solution affects the usability of a calibration curve is important. Furthermore, an understanding
of the effects of pH and sampling volume is needed. Understanding if and how enrichment plays a role in
the usability of the calibration curve method is also important. For FY26, we propose the following work
to further our understanding of quantitative Raman analysis of uranium concentrations in solutions:

 Study a new set of naturally enriched ADUN solutions that will be benchmarked against ICP-MS and
 Davies–Gray within the same time frame to eliminate uncertainty regarding whether the samples have
 been stored properly.

 10

 Determine whether solution pH plays any role in using a calibration curve generated for a particular
 pH to determine the concentration of uranium in a solution with a different pH.

 Determine whether aliquot size affects Raman spectra. If so, determine an aliquot size range that will
 ensure that aliquot size is not a factor that must be accounted for during quantification.

 Develop a step-by-step procedure that can be used by operators at the TRISO fabrication facilities to
 consistently collect and analyze Raman data to determine uranium concentrations.

 Finally, work with enriched (LEU, HALEU) uranium ADUN solutions and determine how that
