Current comorbid psychiatric symptoms in the statistical indirect association between childhood attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder symptoms and functional impairment among university students
Authors: Zehra Koyuncu, Fatma Yağmur Evcil, Özge Selçukoğlu Kilimci, Serdar Kuzu, Cana Aksoy Poyraz, Ömer Faruk Demirel, Mahmut Cem Tarakçıoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study investigated whether current comorbid psychiatric symptoms were involved in statistical indirect associations between childhood attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) symptoms (CAS) and functional impairment in university students.MethodsData were collected from 110 university students who scored above the clinical threshold on the Wender Utah Rating Scale (WURS). Functional impairment was assessed using the Weiss Functional Impairment Rating Scale-Self-Report (WFIRS-S), and comorbid psychiatric symptoms were measured using the Brief Symptom Inventory (BSI). Regression analyses and statistical indirect-association models were used to examine the associations between CAS, comorbid psychiatric symptoms, and functional impairment across several domains.ResultsMultivariate analyses showed that CAS was significantly associated with functional impairment in the family domain (p = 0.008). Current comorbid psychiatric symptoms, particularly depressive symptoms, were more strongly associated with functional impairment in the domains of life skills, self-concept, and social functioning (p = 0.042, p < 0.001, and p = 0.016, respectively). Univariate analyses indicated that anxiety levels were significantly associated with functional impairment in these domains (all p values < 0.001). Statistical indirect-association analyses showed significant indirect associations through comorbid psychiatric symptoms between CAS and functional impairment across all domains except the family domain. Specifically, depression showed a significant indirect association in the model linking CAS with overall functional impairment, whereas both anxiety and depression showed indirect associations in the models linking CAS with school functioning.ConclusionThese findings suggest that comorbid psychiatric symptoms, especially depression and anxiety, may be useful to consider when interpreting functional impairment among university students with CAS. Because the study was cross-sectional and observational, implications regarding continued ADHD-related care and depression/anxiety-focused support should be viewed as hypotheses for future longitudinal and intervention studies rather than as demonstrated clinical effects.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-026-08299-0.

Introduction

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by developmentally inappropriate inattention, impulsivity, and hyperactivity [1]. It is one of the most common disorders in child and adolescent psychiatry; however, approximately 65% of individuals diagnosed with ADHD in childhood continue to experience symptoms into adulthood, which are associated with persistent functional impairments [2]. The disorder has been associated with negative outcomes in social relationships, academic performance, and various other domains of functioning throughout the lifespan [2]. Recent studies indicate that executive dysfunction is frequently reported in adults with ADHD and may contribute substantially to impairment alongside the core symptoms of inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity [3]. Symptoms of executive dysfunction are often associated with difficulties in daily functioning across academic, occupational, and home domains [4]. In particular, adults with ADHD often experience difficulties with time management, organizational skills, and self-regulation [3, 5]. Therefore, when diagnosing ADHD in adults, the focus should extend beyond counting the number of criteria met. Greater emphasis should be placed on the individual’s functional level and the effort required to sustain it, as even subthreshold symptoms can be associated with meaningful impairment in daily life [6].

Some longitudinal studies have reported higher rates of anxiety and depression symptoms among individuals with ADHD [7]. In a systematic literature review, among adults with ADHD, the prevalence of any depressive disorder was reported at 8.6% to 55%, and the prevalence rates of anxiety disorders were reported at 4.3% to 47.1% [8]; these wide ranges likely reflect differences in samples, diagnostic methods, and measurement approaches. While some studies highlight shared genetic pathways in the comorbidity between ADHD and other psychiatric disorders [9], others report associations between early-life ADHD symptoms and later depression [10]. These comorbid psychiatric conditions are critical, as they may complicate the diagnostic process and are associated with poorer overall functioning and prognosis in ADHD [8, 11].

University students with ADHD symptoms or a diagnosis of ADHD represent a distinct group because of the developmental challenges they face and the cognitive demands of higher education. It has been proposed that university students with ADHD may demonstrate better functioning compared to the broader ADHD population in some samples, potentially reflecting selection factors [12]. However, due to increased academic demands, university students may experience concentration problems, stress, or fatigue unrelated to ADHD [13]. Moreover, university students with ADHD tend to show higher rates of comorbid mood disorder symptoms [14]. Supporting this, Alexander and Harrison found that ADHD symptoms were significantly associated with elevated levels of depression, anxiety, and stress in a university student sample [12].

Given the unique challenges faced by university students with ADHD and the frequent coexistence of ADHD symptoms with comorbid psychiatric symptoms, it is important to understand how these comorbid psychiatric symptoms are associated with functional impairment within this population [13]. Therefore, this study focuses on university students who reported clinically significant childhood ADHD symptoms (CAS), which were assessed using the Wender Utah Rating Scale (WURS); current comorbid psychiatric symptoms were indexed by the Brief Symptom Inventory (BSI), and functional impairment was indexed by the Weiss Functional Impairment Rating Scale-Self-Report (WFIRS-S).

For clarity, the term comorbid psychiatric symptoms is used throughout the manuscript to refer to Brief Symptom Inventory (BSI)-indexed symptoms, including anxiety, depression, negative self, somatization, and hostility, whereas CAS refers specifically to retrospectively reported childhood ADHD symptoms indexed by the WURS.

Materials and methods

Participants and procedure

A cross-sectional survey conducted at two centers investigated the associations between comorbid psychiatric symptoms and functional impairment among university students presenting with ADHD symptoms. The present study builds upon data from the study conducted by Koyuncu et al. [15]. The 25-item Wender Utah Rating Scale (WURS-25) was used to screen for ADHD symptoms across developmental stages and symptom types. Functional impairment was assessed using the WFIRS-S, and comorbid psychiatric symptoms were screened using the BSI. A sociodemographic data form was used to assess sociodemographic variables.

The exclusion criteria were a history of antipsychotic treatment and a diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder (ASD), bipolar disorder (BD), or psychotic disorders (PD). History of psychiatric diagnosis and treatment was evaluated using the sociodemographic data form, and participants who self-reported any of these diagnoses or antipsychotic treatment were excluded from the study.

Following approval from the relevant faculty boards, the study was conducted in classroom settings. Students were informed about the study’s objectives and methodology and were invited to participate voluntarily. The questionnaires were compiled into a single online link, which was shared in classrooms using available digital tools, such as an interactive whiteboard. For students who preferred alternative methods, the link was also distributed via email or text message, enabling access through personal devices (e.g., tablets or smartphones). Consent to participate in the study was also obtained through the online link before participants completed the questionnaires.

Participants took part in the study voluntarily and were not offered compensation. A total of 703 undergraduate students completed the scales. Four participants were excluded for meeting criteria for psychosis, two for a diagnosis of bipolar disorder, and four for current use of antipsychotic medication. Ultimately, 110 students who scored above the WURS cutoff score of 36, established in the Turkish validity and reliability study, were included in the analyses. The statistical power of the sample size (n = 110) was verified using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7) [16]. Post hoc power analyses indicated that, with a medium effect size (f2 = 0.15) and an alpha level of 0.05, the achieved statistical power was 85.7% for the multiple regression models (up to six independent variables) and 90.7% for the statistical indirect-association models (four independent variables). Both values exceeded the conventional threshold of 80%, indicating adequate power for the multivariate analyses [17].

Furthermore, the significance of the statistical indirect associations was evaluated using 5,000 bootstrap resamples with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Bootstrapping is widely recognized as a robust method for testing indirect associations because it does not require the assumption of a normally distributed indirect effect, which is particularly advantageous for the present sample size [18].

Within this sample, the associations between comorbid psychiatric symptoms, evaluated using the BSI, and functioning, assessed using the WFIRS-S, were systematically examined. In this study, WURS scores, as well as BSI total and subscale scores, were included in the analyses as independent variables. The associations of these variables with the WFIRS-S total and subscale scores, designated as dependent variables, were examined using statistical methods.

Data collection tools

Sociodemographic data form

A data form developed by researchers was used to collect information, including the participants’ age, gender, educational background, current or past medical treatment, and history of childhood ADHD diagnosis.

Wender Utah Rating Scale (WURS)

The WURS assesses ADHD symptoms using a five-point Likert-type scale, ranging from 0 (“not at all/very slightly”) to 4 (“very much”), resulting in a total score between 0 and 100. Higher scores indicate greater ADHD symptom severity [19]. The WURS was selected for this study due to its excellent discriminative properties and its capacity to screen for a wide range of symptoms. In the Turkish adaptation by Öncü et al. [20], the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the total scale was reported as 0.93 [20]. In our study, the cutoff score of 36, established in the Turkish validity and reliability study of the WURS, was used [20]. As the WURS is a retrospective self-report measure, its scores reflect participants’ recollection of childhood symptoms rather than contemporaneous childhood assessment.

Weiss Functional Impairment Rating Scale-Self-Report form (WFIRS-S)

The WFIRS-S is a 69-item self-report scale that assesses functional impairment over the past month using a four-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“never or not at all”) to 3 (“very often or very much”). It covers seven domains: family, work, school, life skills, self-concept, social activities, and risky activities [21]. The work subscale was excluded from the analyses, as the participant sample consisted solely of students. Yalın-Sapmaz et al. assessed the validity and reliability of the Turkish version and reported a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.94 for the total scale and acceptable test-retest reliability [22]. Higher scores reflect more severe impairment [21].

Brief Symptom Inventory (BSI)

The BSI is a 53-item, five-point Likert self-report scale that assesses comorbid psychiatric symptoms [23]. In the Turkish validity and reliability study conducted by Şahin and Savaşır, five factors were identified: Anxiety, Depression, Negative Self, Somatization, and Hostility. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for these subscales were as follows: Depression = 0.88, Anxiety = 0.87, Negative Self = 0.87, Somatization = 0.75, and Hostility = 0.76. Higher scores reflect more severe distress over the past week [24].

Ethical standards

The procedures used in this study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. A cross-sectional survey conducted at two centers investigated the associations between comorbid psychiatric symptoms and functional impairment among university students presenting with ADHD symptoms. The present study builds upon data from the study conducted by Koyuncu et al. Written consent was obtained from the primary authors of the previous study from which our data were derived [15]. In addition, the Istanbul University-Cerrahpasa, Cerrahpasa Medical School Ethics Committee approved the study protocol and materials (approval date: 10.09.2025; approval number: 1412487).

Statistical analysis

The normality of continuous variables was assessed using analytical methods. Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests were applied, and skewness and kurtosis values were examined. Descriptive statistics were presented as frequency, percentage, mean, standard deviation, and minimum and maximum values.

To identify variables associated with functional impairment (WFIRS total and subscale scores), and given the exploratory nature of the study, variables were first examined in univariate analyses. Only variables significant at the p < 0.05 level were included in the subsequent multivariate linear regression models. It was confirmed that the variables included in the multivariate regression models met the assumption of normal distribution both before the univariate analyses and during the analysis process. Separate multivariate linear regression models were established for the WFIRS total score and each subscale. Multicollinearity was examined using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF); all VIF values were below 5, indicating no evidence of multicollinearity. To avoid singularity, the total BSI score was not included in models where its subscales were already present. The backward likelihood ratio (LR) method was used to construct the regression models. All statistical analyses were conducted using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) for Windows, version 22.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL), and the level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

In the univariate regression analyses, WURS scores (childhood ADHD symptoms) and BSI-Total scores (comorbid psychiatric symptom severity) were significantly associated with all WFIRS models. Accordingly, statistical indirect-association analyses were performed using Model 4 of the SPSS Process Macro v4.2 [25], with WURS entered as the independent variable (X), WFIRS total and subscale scores entered as the dependent variable (Y), and BSI-Total entered as the statistical intermediate variable (M).

In cases where both WURS and a BSI subscale were significantly associated with outcomes in the multivariate regression analyses, additional statistical indirect-association analyses were conducted with WFIRS total or subscale scores entered as the dependent variable (Y), WURS entered as the independent variable (X), and the relevant BSI subscale entered as the statistical intermediate variable (M).

All statistical indirect-association models included age and gender as covariates. In line with modern approaches to testing indirect associations, statistical significance was evaluated by examining the 95% CIs. If the CI for the indirect association did not include zero, the indirect association was considered statistically significant. Model outcomes were interpreted using unstandardized path coefficients (B). These CIs were generated using 5,000 bootstrap samples and served as the basis for determining the significance of direct and indirect associations [18, 26].

Variables that were significantly associated with functional impairment in the univariate analyses were further examined through multivariate linear regression models. In these models, the WURS score was significantly associated with the WFIRS-Family subscale (B = 0.362, 95% CI = 0.097–0.628, β = 0.257, p = 0.008). Regarding the WFIRS-School subscale, gender, WURS score, and BSI-Anxiety scores showed significant associations (p = 0.003, p = 0.007, and p < 0.001, respectively). The BSI-Depression score was significantly associated with the WFIRS-Life Skills subscale (p = 0.042). For the WFIRS-Self Concept subscale, BSI-Depression, BSI-Negative Self Concept, and BSI-Somatization scores demonstrated significant associations (p < 0.001, p = 0.002, and p = 0.001, respectively). In the WFIRS-Social subscale, BSI-Depression and BSI-Negative Self Concept scores remained significant (p = 0.016 and p = 0.024, respectively). For the WFIRS-Risk subscale, gender and BSI-Hostility scores were significantly associated with impairment (both p < 0.001). Finally, for the WFIRS-Total score, WURS and BSI-Depression scores were significantly associated with total impairment (p = 0.008 and p = 0.003, respectively). Taken together, these results indicate that CAS severity (WURS) was primarily associated with family functioning, whereas comorbid psychiatric symptoms, especially depression and anxiety, showed stronger associations in other functional domains. Discussion

This study investigated the associations of CAS and current comorbid psychiatric symptoms with functional impairment across subdomains. Because CAS, comorbid psychiatric symptoms, and functional impairment were all assessed using self-report scales at the same time point, these findings should be interpreted cautiously as statistical associations that may be influenced by shared method variance rather than as evidence of temporal or causal pathways. In addition, because CAS was measured retrospectively with the WURS, current mood, anxiety, depressive symptoms, or general distress may have influenced participants’ recall and reporting of childhood ADHD symptoms. Within this framework, BSI total scores showed significant statistical indirect associations between CAS and WFIRS-School, WFIRS-Life Skills, WFIRS-Self Concept, WFIRS-Social, WFIRS-Risk, and WFIRS-Total. Furthermore, the BSI-Anxiety and BSI-Depression subscales showed significant indirect associations between CAS and functional impairment in the school subdomain. BSI-Depression also showed an indirect association between CAS and overall functional impairment.

Associations of current comorbid psychiatric symptoms and CAS with functional impairment

ADHD symptoms and comorbid psychiatric symptoms have been reported to be associated with functional impairment in adults [4]. However, in our student sample, current comorbid psychiatric symptoms were not significantly associated with family functioning in the multivariate model, whereas CAS severity showed a significant statistical association. Because all variables were measured by self-report, the magnitude of these associations may partly reflect common rater effects, overlapping symptom content, or broader perceived distress. With the onset of adolescence, the influence of the family on the individual tends to diminish compared to the increasing impact of peers [27, 28]. The transition to college is also accompanied by changes in living conditions and relational dynamics. For example, students may begin living away from their families, and parental monitoring may decrease over time [29, 30]. Therefore, the stronger statistical association of CAS, relative to current comorbid psychiatric symptoms, with family functioning might be better understood within a developmental context. In contrast, impairment in the school subdomain was associated with gender, anxiety, and CAS. School performance is one of the main concerns for university students, and many studies have investigated factors related to school performance [31]. Thus, the observed association between anxiety and school functioning is plausible in a population with significant CAS. CAS severity was also associated with school functioning in our study. This finding is consistent with the literature showing that inattentiveness and difficulty in time management are negatively associated with academic performance [32, 33]. Men had lower school functioning in our sample. In the literature, educational level has been reported to be similar between males and females with ADHD symptoms [34]; similar to our findings, men have been reported to show greater impairment in the risk-related functional domain in detailed evaluations [35]. Consistent with some studies in the literature [36, 37], men also reported greater impairment in the WFIRS-Risk domain in our sample. The WFIRS-Risk domain reflects functional difficulties related to risk-taking or potentially harmful behaviors. Risk-related functional impairment in populations with ADHD has been reported to be associated with hostility and altered physiological activity, such as emotional arousal. However, risk-related impairment may also be relevant among women [38]. Overall, our findings suggest an association between hostility and risk-related functional impairment, whereas the role of gender remains unclear among university students with CAS.

In our sample, depression was significantly associated with functional impairment in the life skills, self-concept, and social subdomains. Negative self-concept, loss of interest, and impairments in daily life are among the features of depression described in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) [1]. This association should be interpreted cautiously, because self-reported depressive symptoms and self-reported impairment may overlap conceptually and may also be strengthened by shared method variance. Interestingly, while CAS showed significant associations in these domains in the univariate analysis, its association was no longer evident in the multivariate analysis. Overall, the pattern suggests that depression showed stronger statistical associations with the life skills, self-concept, and social subdomains, but this should be interpreted as an exploratory association rather than as evidence of a predictive or causal effect.

Statistical indirect associations involving current comorbid psychiatric symptoms between CAS and functional impairment

Current comorbid psychiatric symptoms were involved in significant statistical indirect associations between CAS and functional impairment in all domains except the family domain. These models do not demonstrate that CAS temporally precedes or produces comorbid psychiatric symptoms or impairment; instead, they indicate cross-sectional indirect associations among concurrently self-reported constructs. Shared method variance may have inflated the estimated a and b paths and the resulting indirect associations. Importantly, current comorbid psychiatric symptoms may also have shaped retrospective WURS responses; therefore, the observed indirect paths could partly reflect mood-congruent recall or reporting bias rather than a developmental sequence from childhood ADHD symptoms to later comorbid psychiatric symptoms and impairment. In addition to hereditary factors contributing to the co-occurrence of ADHD and certain psychiatric disorders such as depression [39], anxiety disorders and irritability/emotional problems [40], previous studies have discussed associations of ADHD with depressive mood [7], reduced self-esteem [41], and difficulties in managing anxiety [42]. Furthermore, ADHD symptoms in adults may be misinterpreted as manifestations of other psychiatric conditions, or symptoms of inattention may be masked by those of anxiety [43]. Consistent with this literature [7], our findings indicate that comorbid psychiatric symptoms were statistically involved in indirect associations between CAS and functional impairment, alongside the direct statistical association involving CAS. Therefore, the observed associations suggest that co-occurring comorbid psychiatric symptoms may be clinically relevant when evaluating functional difficulties in university students with CAS; however, the present data do not show that managing these symptoms improves functioning, and intervention effects should be tested in future longitudinal or treatment studies. In contrast, the model did not show a significant indirect association through current comorbid psychiatric symptoms for the family functioning domain, although comorbid psychiatric symptoms showed a direct statistical association with functional impairment in this domain. Consistent with the discussion above regarding current comorbid psychiatric symptoms and family functioning, this finding may be interpreted from a developmental standpoint, in which school, social, and peer-related factors gain increased prominence [27, 30]. Accordingly, current comorbid psychiatric symptoms showed weaker associations with the family domain.

Depression showed a significant statistical indirect association in the model linking CAS with overall functional impairment. This pattern may partly reflect the fact that depressive symptoms and functional impairment were both self-reported and include overlapping themes related to motivation, self-evaluation, and daily functioning. A strong association has been reported between ADHD and depression [44]. Moreover, ADHD has been associated with an increased likelihood of early-onset depression [45, 46]. ADHD symptoms have also been reported to be associated with recurrent, severe, and treatment-resistant depressive episodes [47]. Our findings regarding depression as part of the indirect association between CAS and functional impairment in university students are consistent with the existing literature. When interpreted cautiously, these findings are compatible with future longitudinal and intervention studies evaluating whether continued ADHD-related care and depression-focused prevention or support strategies are associated with better functioning among individuals with ADHD. Both anxiety and depression showed significant statistical indirect associations in the school subdomain. These findings suggest that anxiety and depression symptoms may be relevant when interpreting school functioning in students with clinically significant CAS, although any clinical management implications require further study. From a health economics and policy perspective, untreated comorbid psychiatric symptoms may coincide with greater functional burden, increased demand for healthcare services, and indirect societal costs related to reduced academic functioning. Accordingly, future research could examine whether comprehensive approaches addressing both ADHD-related symptoms and emotional comorbidities improve functional outcomes and resource use.

Strengths and Limitations

By examining both direct statistical associations and indirect associations involving comorbid psychiatric symptoms, the study offers a more nuanced understanding of how CAS relates to functional impairment in this population. The use of validated and widely used instruments (WURS, BSI, WFIRS-S) supports measurement reliability and comparability with previous research. Additionally, including domain-specific functional outcomes (e.g., school, life skills, self-concept) provides valuable insight into areas that are particularly relevant to emerging adulthood. Despite its strengths, several limitations should be noted. First, the cross-sectional design of the study prevents any conclusions about causality; therefore, the indirect-association analyses should not be interpreted as temporal mediation. Longitudinal research is needed to determine the directionality of the observed relationships. Second, and particularly relevant to the interpretation of the regression and indirect-association findings, all primary constructs (retrospectively reported CAS, current comorbid psychiatric symptoms, and functional impairment) were assessed with self-report scales. This increases the possibility of shared method variance, common rater effects, recall bias for childhood symptoms, and mood-congruent reporting, which may have inflated the observed associations and indirect paths. For this reason, the results should be interpreted cautiously and should not be considered evidence that comorbid psychiatric symptoms temporally mediate the association between CAS and functional impairment. Future studies using clinical interviews, multi-informant measures, objective or administrative indicators of functioning, and longitudinal designs are needed to confirm the direction and robustness of these findings. Specifically, because the WURS requires retrospective reporting of childhood symptoms, participants with current depression, anxiety, or other psychiatric distress may recall earlier difficulties more negatively or endorse childhood ADHD symptoms more strongly. This possible mood-congruent recall bias may have increased the statistical overlap among WURS, BSI, and WFIRS-S scores and limits conclusions about the direction and magnitude of the observed associations. Third, the sample consisted of students from a single university, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader student populations. Lastly, although CAS was identified through a validated retrospective screening tool (WURS), the lack of clinical interviews may reduce diagnostic accuracy.

Conclusions

This study examined the associations between retrospectively reported childhood ADHD symptoms (CAS), current comorbid psychiatric symptoms, and functional impairment in university students. Comorbid psychiatric symptoms, particularly depression and anxiety, were involved in significant statistical indirect associations between CAS and functional impairment across multiple domains. Given the shared self-report measurement and cross-sectional design, these results should be regarded as cautious statistical associations rather than evidence of causal or temporal mechanisms. The findings should also be interpreted in light of the possibility that retrospective WURS scores may be influenced by current comorbid psychiatric symptoms. The findings also suggest the potential value of considering both ADHD-related impairments and emotional comorbidities in university student populations. Interventions targeting ADHD symptoms alongside co-occurring depression and anxiety were not examined in this study; their possible benefits for academic functioning and psychological well-being should be evaluated in future longitudinal and intervention research. Accordingly, the present findings may help generate hypotheses for more coordinated educational and mental health support services, rather than providing evidence of clinical effectiveness.
