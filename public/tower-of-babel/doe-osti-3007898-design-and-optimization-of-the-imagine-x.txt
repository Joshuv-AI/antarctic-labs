 ABSTRACT

An upgraded protein crystal diffractometer with Dynamic Nuclear Polarization (DNP) is proposed for
installation during the next HFIR Beryllium Reflector Replacement. The diffractometer will replace the
IMAGINE protein crystal diffraction instrument currently located at CG4D. The simulation design meets
the needs as requested in the science requirements document [1].

 1. PURPOSE AND REQUIREMENTS

During the HFIR Beryllium Reflector Replacement (HBRR) there will be an opportunity to replace and/or
upgrade the entire scattering instrument suite in the HFIR Cold Guide Hall. One of the proposed
replacements will be the IMAGINE-X protein diffractometer to be located at NB1. This diffractometer is
expected to provide a beam flux greater than 107 neutrons/(cm2-s) within the spectral range of 3.0-4.0
Angstroms. This is to be attained within a beam footprint no larger than 2.0 x 2.0 mm2 with a FWHM
beam divergence of 0.2 degrees in both the vertical and horizontal dimensions. In addition, the lower
boundary of the wavelength must be adjustable between 2.5, 3.0 and 3.5 Å and the upper boundary of the
wavelength range must be adjustable between 3.0, 3.3, and 4.0 [1]. A summary of these requirements and
others can be seen in Table 1.

2.1 HB4 TUNNEL GUIDE

 The first guide surfaces will be used only to bring the beam away from the source while still
preserving the divergence. No deviation of the average beam trajectory will occur here. Since we
anticipate using a focusing mirror for the final optic in this design, we do not require a substantial amount
of divergence be transported. Since the cross-section of the guide at this point is 40 x 40 mm, even if we
only use a fraction of this available area at the guide exit aperture, say 10mm x 10mm, one will only need
a demagnification factor of five for the final ellipsoid mirror to meet the required on-sample beam profile.
This demagnification factor works the opposite way on the trajectory component of the phase space; only
needing a FWHM divergence of 0.04⁰ degrees to be available to the mirror (0.08⁰ for a potential “high
flux” configuration). Even at the shortest wavelength needed (2Å), this translates the needed m value to
be much less than one. However, in order to accommodate potential future changes, m=3 is a suitable
reflectivity cut-off to transport this beam to the optical filter.

2.2 SIX-LEG OPTICAL FILTER

 The six-leg optical filter serves two main purposes in meeting the requirements of the proposed
IMAGINE-X diffraction instrument. First, it allows the sample position and detector systems to sit closer
to the front of the cold guide hall. This utilizes space that otherwise would have not been used for
scattering instruments and limits floor space available to the main mission need. Second, it provides a
suitable mechanism to set a lower bound for the neutron wavelength transported to the instrument. The
super-mirror cutoff on these surfaces will be m=6, with an expected reflectivity of 0.630 at 2Å. This
results in expected total beam transport efficiency in the spectrum range of interest to be on the order of
20%. Based on the optical filter cross-section geometry and the distance spanned by this section, 0.2⁰
beam divergence can be transported, much more than needed to illuminate the focusing optics (<0.08⁰).
Thus, the only penalties to the brightness transport to the final sample position are expected to be due to
the reflectivity curve and cutoff of the supermirror coating of the beam-left surface of the filters.

2.3 GUIDE 2 – STRAIGHT GUIDE/COLLIMATOR

 This section is a simple guide designed to provide additional transport capability in the event that a
more divergent beam needs to be accommodated in the future. Basic m=1 coatings will suit here, but
given the current requirements, this can be a flight tube. This is due to the fact that the focusing mirror(s)
will not utilize the wide part of the beam trajectory space that can be provided by this piece.

2.4 APERTURE AND LONG-PASS FLAT MIRROR

 The aperture at the exit of Guide 2 provides the definition for the image to be focused on to the
sample through the final mirror apparatus and will use only a small part of the beam width and height

 3

available exiting that guide. The long-pass flat mirror provides the first means by which to adjust the short
wavelength cutoff of the permitted spectrum settings. It resides very close to the exit aperture and will
provide three distinct cut off values of 2.5Å, 3.0Å, and 3.5Å. It will provide a long-pass filtering effect by
utilizing a specific set of super-mirror cutoff values at a specified angle of incidence, much like the
optical filter. Another side-effect is additional lateral displacement of the beam away from the HB4 Beam
Tube centerline. Based on the current utilized configuration of a 1-degree incident reflection angle, the
needed m values for these are estimated to be 4.0, 3.3, and 2.9 respectively.

2.5 SHORT-PASS FILTERS

 The short pass filters utilize the same concept as the long-pass mirror, but utilizing transmission
properties of super-mirrors and in a stacked form for compactness. There are also entrance and exit
apertures used to ensure full rejection of the longer wavelengths downstream. A simplified example can
be seen in Figure 4. For this application long wavelength cutoffs are 3.0, 4.0 and 4.5, and can be
accommodated by the existing filter set. They are used in the simulation, and their details and
performance will be noted in a later section.

 Figure 4. A simple schematic of the short-pass transmission filter used to define the long
 wavelength edge of the neutron spectrum. Typically, these are used in a series of opposing pairs to
 ensure the transmitted beam trajectory distribution is not skewed.

2.6 FINAL FOCUSING OPTICS AND FINAL APERTURE

 The final focusing optic ideally would be shaped to provide focusing in both vertical and horizontal
directions using only one bounce. This demands the use of a mirror with an ellipsoidal curvature, rather
than two orthogonal elliptical surfaces to provide the same nominal focusing effect. As noted prior, it will
need to provide a demagnification factor of about five to meet the flux requirement noted in Table 1.
There is a set of compound elliptical mirrors currently in use at the CG4D IMAGINE instrument, and a
new set in the same style will also be investigated with regards to their performance in the event that a
single-bounce mirror is not feasible to fabricate. The current system would likely not accommodate the
new guide configuration, due mainly to the pitched trajectory of the NB1 guide system.
 Beyond that focusing system, a final fixed aperture with large panels should be located as close to
the focusing mirror exit as possible to ensure suitable rejection of any part of the beam that is not
reflected by the mirror(s).

 3. PERFORMANCE OF PROPOSED DESIGN

 The proposed design is made up of the components described in Section 2. Here one will show that
these are the optimal designs given the constraints of the instrument layout and the needs defined in the
science requirements document.

3.1 HB4 TUNNEL GUIDE (GUIDE_01)

 As noted earlier, the main goal of this system is to transport the available beam flux and preserve as
much of the phase space as possible over the wavelength range of interest. Here we conclude that any
gains to be had are strictly in the coating of the guide system, as the geometry is restricted by the other
guide channels supporting the rest of the Cold Guide Hall instrument suite. The reflectivity of these guide
surfaces affects both the wavelength and divergence of the beam that exits the guide. Given the limited
phase space made available to the guide by the beam tube collimator and Channel S1 of the Main Shutter,

determination of the reflectivity requirements can be done analytically and then a simulation utilized to
see what the gain provided by the guide is over a range of values.
 The analytical calculation is performed by determining the highest angle of interaction that is
expected from the source on the entrance surfaces and ensure that the reflective properties of the coating
can transmit that angle given the wavelengths of interest. The entrance of the guide is 40 millimeters in
both horizontal and vertical transverse directions and maintains this cross-section geometry for the entire
length of 3.2415 meters. The source sits 4.701 meters upstream of the guide entrance and we assume that
the source is a 80mm x 60mm rectangle for simplicity. The wavelength for this exercise will be 2.5
Angstroms, as this is the shortest wavelength to be utilized for this instrument. One could incorporate a
penalty related to the true reflectivity of the surface coatings, that level of rigor will be reserved for the
simulations.
 Under these conditions, one finds maximum trajectory at the guide entrance to be 0.73 degrees and
0.61 degrees for Vertical and Horizontal components of the geometry. At 2.0 Angstroms this corresponds
to reflectivity cut-off (or m) values of 3.5 and 3.1 on the top/bottom and left/right surfaces respectively.
 McStas 3.3 is used to build a simulation to compare the theoretical best through this part of the guide
system. Using a wavelength of 2.5 Angstroms one can compare the spectrum transport over a range of m
values. One notes the divergence that this guide is transporting (>0.6deg) is much more than is to be
utilized downstream for this instrument, 0.08º in the most generous case. However, the transmission
limitations of the optical filter immediately downstream suggest that the m number could be limited for
the left and right reflecting surfaces, as will be seen in the next section.

3.2 SIX-LEG OPTICAL FILTER

 The optical filter system is used to set a coarse low-wavelength cutoff for the instrument and to
appropriately displace the instrument horizontally relative to the centerline of the HB4 Beam Tube. As
noted prior, this will facilitate an optimal layout on the instrument floor. The use of the optical filter will
have two impacts on the beam performance. The first being a wavelength dependent attenuation effect
due to the imperfect reflections through each leg and the second being a restricted horizontal divergence
due to the limited view through the filter. Both impacts can be understood analytically and compared to
the simulated performance of the system.

 Figure 11. Plots describing the spectral transmission and reflectivity performance of the six-leg
 optical filter. The analytical transmission and simulated transmission versus neutron wavelength
 (left). Traces showing the effect of multiple reflections versus normalized q, which varies depending
 on the angle of incidence and the neutron wavelength.
 The attenuation effect due to the high-angle interaction with the super-mirror is substantial given
magnitude of said angle, each bounce only reflecting a 2.5 Angstrom neutron with a probability of about
70% for an estimated transmission loss of 88% as seen in Figure 11. This effect is a first order assumption
and does not account for the angular distribution over the reflecting surface, which can work in favor of
or against the probability of the reflection through the filter. The overall length and width of the filter

3.3 STRAIGHT GUIDE

 The straight segment of the guide system serves to preserve the divergence of the transported beam
over the distance needed to achieve the correct beam focusing on the sample. Given the small divergence
expected to be captured by the mirror, one could imagine using absorbing masks here to lower
background downstream. As proposed, the component will have the same 4cm by 4cm cross section
geometry as the rest of the upstream system. The exit will have an adjustable aperture in order to ensure
the optimal beam space is extracted from the guide and provided to the flat mirror. The impact of keeping
the guide refectivity can be seen in Figure 8.

3.4 LONG-PASS FLAT MIRROR

 The Long-Pass Flat Mirror component provides a mechanism to adjust the short-wavelength
rejection of the instrument. Based on the 1.0 degree interaction angle of the beam with this mirror, one
calculates that required mirror coating m values to be 4.0, 3.3, and 2.9 respective of the 2.5, 3.0, and 3.5
Angstrom cutoff settings provided by the component. The effect of the mirror on the spectrum at the
sample position is seen in Figure 13.

3.5 SHORT-PASS FILTERS

 The short-pass filters adjust the long wavelength rejection of the instrument. Based on a stacked set
of angled Silicon wafers with different super-mirror coatings, these are placed in the beam such that the
angle of the wafer stack is matched with a coating to provide a suitable rejection range. For this system,
there are three different configurations, providing cutoffs at 3.0, 4.0, and 4.5Angstroms, and those needs
are nearly met (a shift of +0.1Angstrom in the wavelength range is observed in simulation) as seen in
Figure 13.

3.6 FOCUSING OPTICS

 The final focusing optic ideally is a single bounce super-mirror coated surface curved in three
dimensions. The surface shape itself is a partial ellipsoid with a “cigar-like” aspect ratio but is only
realized as a small part of the whole ellipsoid surface. That surface which is realized is much closer to the
sample than the exit of the guide, enabling filters, polarizers and adjustable slits to be included in the
primary leg of the focusing geometry. This is how the de-magnification (and accompanying boost in flux)
is achieved, and the result is seen in Figure 14.

3.7 BEAM CHARACTER AT SAMPLE POSITION

The beam character at the sample position will meet requirements discussed in the science requirements
document [1]. Further optimization of the mirror coating and shape is required in order to accommodate
the requested “high flux” mode. Furthermore, a double bounce focusing optic to be used as an alternative
design would need to be realized in a way that would not cause a substantial reconfiguration of the
entrance and exit legs of the instrument as proposed with the single-bounce system. This was alluded to in
section 2.6 and can be looked to as a potential risk mitigation path given budgetary or technical
limitations in this proposed design.

 4. DESIGN SUMMARY

HBRR Cold Guide Hall Layout is designed to provide a high-flux neutron beam suited to modern protein
crystallography using Dynamic Nuclear Polarization. The final optical layout can be seen in Figure 15
and is the full representation of the combined components discussed herein.
