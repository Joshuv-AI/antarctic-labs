Reliability of Contactless Vital Sign Monitoring Posttriage in Emergency Department Walk‐In Patients: A Single‐Site, Prospective, Observational, Pilot Study
Authors: Courtney West, Hollis Hopkins, Samuel Teague, Danyi Wang, Titilayo Ogunwa, Javaan Chahl, Eloise Bletsas, Hayley Muller, Vinay Gangathimmaiah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo evaluate camera‐based, advanced sensor technology for contactless vital sign monitoring (versus standard monitoring) posttriage in adult emergency department patients.MethodsA single‐site, prospective, observational study was conducted at Townsville University Hospital Emergency Department between May 2025 and August 2025. Adult (≥ 18 years) walk‐in patients were eligible. A convenience sample of consenting participants was recruited. Two sets of vital signs—blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, temperature—were obtained. The first set was measured immediately after triage using a dual‐camera system comprising a thermographic camera and a second camera with proprietary software. The second set was obtained immediately posttriage using standard monitoring equipment. Reliability and acceptability were the primary and secondary outcomes respectively. Reliability (accuracy and precision) was determined through a Bland–Altman analysis to assess agreement between the two sets of vital signs. Acceptability of technology was assessed using a participant questionnaire.ResultsA total of 300 patients were recruited, of whom 288 had complete data on paired vital signs and were included in the Bland–Altman analysis. Minimal bias was observed across most vital signs, indicating limited systematic error. Respiratory rate, oxygen saturation and temperature showed the closest agreement, whereas systolic blood pressure and heart rate exhibited the widest limits of agreement. Acceptability was high, with most participants reporting comfort, ease of use and overall satisfaction.ConclusionCompared with standard vital sign monitoring, camera‐based technology demonstrated good accuracy but variable precision across vital signs. Further algorithm refinement is required before reliable clinical integration.

Introduction

Emergency departments (EDs) care for patients spanning the spectrum of acuity from critically unwell to worried well [1]. Patients presenting to ED are triaged depending on their acuity and allocated to an appropriate treatment area either within ED or to the ED waiting room [2]. During periods of high volume patient presentations, staffing resources are prioritised toward higher acuity patients requiring immediate emergency care [3]. This prioritisation can invariably result in a delay in emergency care provision to waiting room patients and thus adversely impact patient safety [2]. The waiting room nurse model of care has proven effective in improving patient safety through close monitoring, assessment and early commencement of care [4]. However, due to the fluctuations in ED waiting room patient workload, the care demands sometimes exceed the capacity of a single waiting room nurse [4]. This challenge in ED waiting room care provision has been complicated by worsening access block which has stretched limited ED nursing resources due to the additional responsibility of caring for admitted patients [3].

Recognising and responding to deteriorating patients is a key component of providing safe and quality healthcare [5]. Derangement in physiological parameters often precedes clinical deterioration [6], with vital signs—heart rate, respiratory rate, blood pressure, oxygen saturations, temperature—being a highly ranked predictor of clinical deterioration [7]. Vital signs are essential for monitoring ED waiting room patients to enable early detection of clinical deterioration and immediate provision of emergency care [6]. Hubner et al. [6] have shown that continuous vital sign monitoring is effective in detecting deterioration in ED waiting room patients but required connection to a monitoring device by a triage nurse [6]. Automated vital sign monitoring could improve patient safety in ED waiting rooms.

Contactless monitoring involves measuring vital signs using a combination of video camera and computer software to capture and process images respectively [8]. Video camera imaging techniques use two methods to measure vital signs [8, 9, 10]. The first method, called photoplethysmography, is used to measure heart rate, blood pressure and oxygen saturations [8, 9, 10] and relies on skin colour variation with blood volume fluctuations in the microvascular tissue during the cardiac cycle [9]. The second method, used to measure the respiratory rate [8, 9]. using cyclic motion in head, nostrils and chest during respiratory activity [9]. Temperature can be obtained through an infrared thermographic camera which senses the natural radiation emitted from human skin. By enabling contemporaneous acquisition of vital signs during ED triage, contactless monitoring could enable early detection of clinical deterioration and improve patient safety in ED waiting rooms.

Prior ED studies in the United States and Switzerland have reported contactless monitoring to be a viable method of acquiring vital signs at triage [11, 12, 13]. These studies provide evidence of feasibility; however, they have focused on contactless vital signs as a means to overcoming infection control concerns [11, 12, 13]. Furthermore, the reliability of contactless vital sign monitoring has not been tested in an Australian ED setting.

Our study addresses this evidence gap by evaluating the reliability of camera‐based, advanced sensor technology for posttriage contactless vital sign monitoring in waiting room patients at a major referral Australian ED. Exploring patient acceptability of the technology was a secondary objective.

Methods

Design and Location

This was a prospective observational study at Townsville University Hospital ED, a major‐referral, mixed ED in Queensland with an annual patient census of 96,855 in the calendar year 2025 (personal communication by e‐mail from ED Business Practice Improvement Officer).

Eligibility and Outcomes

Adults (≥ 18 years) who presented to ED via the walk‐in triage entrance and had the capacity to provide informed consent were eligible. Patients who arrived by ambulance, required immediate medical attention, were previous enrolled or declined consent were excluded. The primary outcome of reliability was determined by comparing the accuracy and precision of contactless vital sign monitoring with standard monitoring. Standard monitoring was defined as manual measurement of vital signs (blood pressure, pulse oximetry and heart rate using a digital Welch‐Allen monitor; temperature using a Welch‐Allen Tympanic thermometer; respiratory rate by observation of chest rise and fall). The camera‐based advanced sensor technology used proprietary software. Acceptability was the secondary outcome which was assessed using a validated questionnaire informed by the theoretical framework of acceptability [14]. This framework enables assessment of acceptability of healthcare interventions from the perspective of intervention recipients. In accordance with guidance provided by Sekhon et al. [14], the generic theoretical framework of acceptability questionnaire was adapted for use in this study (Appendix 1). Adaptations included the removal of three items that were not relevant to the intervention and modification of the remaining items to contextualise the questions to the camera‐based technology. The adapted questionnaire, which did not undergo additional pilot testing, was used to evaluate participants' affective attitude, burden, ethicality, self‐efficacy and overall acceptability of the camera‐based technology (Appendix 2).

Recruitment and Data Collection

Participants were recruited between 22 May 2025 and 29 August 2025. Potential participants were identified by a study Clinical Research Coordinator in the ED waiting room and approached immediately after completion of ED triage to minimise workflow disruption. Two sets of vital signs were obtained. The first set was measured using a dual‐camera system comprising a thermographic camera (temperature) and a second camera with proprietary software (blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation). immediately after triage, the participant was seated at a prespecified location (optimum distance) directly facing the camera (optimum height) which was positioned at the triage desk (Figure 1). The camera was then activated to acquire contactless vital signs. If contactless vital signs could not be obtained within 2 min, further attempts were aborted to avoid disruption of the triage process. The participant was then taken to an adjacent room where the second set of vital signs was measured using standard monitoring equipment with the patient in a sitting position.

Camera set up at emergency department triage desk in a prospective, observational, pilot study of contactless vital sign monitoring in 300 participants at Townsville University Hospital Emergency Department. Contactless vital signs were recorded with participants seated on the chair directly facing the camera. The chair was at a fixed distance from the camera and the camera was at eye‐level of participants.

Following the second measurement, participants with data on two sets of vital signs were recruited with written informed consent. For participants with abnormal vital signs identified on standard monitoring, appropriate clinical escalation occurred and consent was sought once clinically stable. Consenting participants were also requested to complete a questionnaire about acceptability of the camera‐based advanced sensor technology. Data collected prior to consent were deleted if participants declined consent.

Data were collected about the following variables: medical record number, triage date and time, age, sex, ethnicity, time to acquisition of contactless vital signs, two sets of vital signs (blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation and temperature) along with corresponding timestamps, acceptability of technology.

Sample Size and Data Analysis

Sample size was determined using the desired population mean with a particular margin of error and level of confidence required to accomplish our primary objective of evaluating reliability. At a confidence level of 95%, a sample size of 300 participants produces a low error bound for a population mean for each of the vital signs measured.

The Bland–Altman method [15] was used to assess agreement between measurement methods. For each vital sign, the mean difference (bias) and the 95% limits of agreement (LoA = bias ± 1.96 × SD) were computed, along with the 95% confidence intervals for both the bias and LoA. Bland–Altman plots were generated to visualise the distribution of differences across the range of measurements. The acceptability survey was analysed using descriptive statistics and Cronbach's alpha for internal consistency. Bland–Altman analysis further quantified agreement between the two methods. Accurate measurements have a bias close to the line x = 0, while precise measurements have limits of agreement close to the bias. If the new device generally gives higher values, the bias would be positive; if the old device gives higher values, the bias would be negative. Smaller limits of agreement indicate better agreement between the two methods.

Ethics

Ethics approval was granted by the Townsville Hospital and Health Service Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC/2024/QTHS/108163).

Results

A total of 300 adult ED patients were enrolled. Data were complete for 288 participants with paired camera‐based and standard manual vital signs. Time to acquisition of camera‐based contactless vital signs ranged from 18 s to 2 min. Contactless vital signs could not be measured in some potential participants. Time between acquisition of two sets of vital signs ranged from 1 to 4 min.

Bland–Altman analysis showed small average differences (mean biases) between contactless and manual measurements. Oxygen saturation and diastolic blood pressure exhibited the smallest (−0.01 mmHg) and largest (+9.52 mmHg) mean biases respectively. Diastolic blood pressure and mean arterial pressure showed a positive bias while others demonstrated a negative bias. Temperature (−2.59 to +0.73) demonstrated the narrowest limits of agreement while systolic blood pressure (−38.27 to +37.5) and heart rate (−32.83 to +22.37) displayed the widest limits of agreement. Discussion

This single‐site, prospective, observational, pilot study found that the camera‐based advanced sensor system yielded vital signs with small average differences and wide limits of agreement compared to standard monitoring.

The small average differences (< 10 mm for blood pressure, < 6 bpm for heart rate, < 2 breaths/min for respiratory rate, < 1% for oxygen saturation, < 1° for temperature) indicate little systematic over‐ or underestimation by the camera‐based system when compared with standard manual measurements and therefore clinically acceptable accuracy. This finding of accuracy is consistent with prior studies exploring camera‐based vital sign monitoring which have reported high accuracy for respiratory rate [12, 13], heart rate [11, 13] and temperature [13]. Pertinently, the average differences noted in our study were almost identical to those found by Capraro et al. [13] for heart rate (5.23 vs. 5.9 beats per min), respiratory rate (1.18 vs. 0.4 breaths per minute) and temperature (0.93° vs. 0.83°). None of these studies reported contactless blood pressure measurements.

The narrow limits of agreement for respiratory rate, oxygen saturation and temperature suggest the strongest consistency between camera and standard manual readings. This consistency was again affirmed by Capraro et al. [13] who found identical standard deviations (2.66 vs. 2.6) for respiratory rate but did not report limits of agreements. Our study found wide limits of agreement for heart rate, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure and mean arterial pressure reflecting substantial variability between the two methods. Limits of agreement were missing from other study reports precluding comparisons about precision.

Patient acceptability survey showed generally positive perceptions. While a minority expressed privacy concerns and moderate confidence in system accuracy, favourable responses from the majority indicate willingness among patients to accept contactless monitoring technologies in clinical settings. A pilot study of 13 patients in a non‐ED setting has reported similarly high acceptability of camera‐based vital sign monitoring [16].

Strengths and Limitations

Prospective study design is a strength.

The need for the camera to be placed at an optimum distance and height could present logistical challenges during implementation. The temporal delay between the manual and the contactless vital sign measurements, coupled with participant movement, could have contributed to variation in readings and consequent imprecision. The inability to obtain contactless vital signs in some potential participants could have been due to a combination of ambient lighting conditions and patient movement. There was no thermal calibration target used in this study for the thermographic camera. This absence of a thermal calibration target could have contributed to imprecise temperature measurements. There was a small but significant time lag between camera‐based and standard vital sign monitoring. This time lag could have biased the results by reducing precision. Use of noninvasive blood pressure measurements as the comparator is a limitation given the reported discrepancies between invasive and noninvasive blood pressure monitoring [17, 18]. However, noninvasive vital sign monitoring was chosen as the comparator as it is the current standard of care at ED triage. Single‐site design precludes generalisability of findings. The small sample size limits strength of conclusions and makes this a hypothesis‐generating pilot study. Finally, as the sample consisted of clinically stable, walk‐in ED patients, the role of camera based vital sign monitoring in higher acuity, critically unwell ED patients (who have a high likelihood of severe physiologic derangement, diaphoresis and physical movement) remains unknown. No conclusions can be drawn about the ability of the technology to safely track deteriorating patients in ED settings.

Implications

The findings of this pilot study highlight challenges in camera‐based, contactless, vital sign monitoring in ED setting due to ambient lighting, patient movement, device configuration and device calibration. Given that this technology was designed for ambulatory subjects in public spaces, some of the limitations could be overcome by device configuration, that is, altering the physical or operational setup of the system (e.g., camera placement, distance, lighting control). The strong linear correlation visible in the Bland–Altman plots indicates that a simple linear calibration (adjusting the sensor's mathematical output to match a known, objective standard) could rectify most of the errors and the wide variability. However, rather than relying on an a posteriori calibration, further research into algorithmic refinement could enhance the clinical applicability of the camera‐based advanced sensor technology.

Conclusion

Camera‐based advanced sensor technology measured vital signs with acceptable accuracy and is well received by patients. Further technological refinements are essential to improve precision across all vital signs before the technology is ready for clinical use in emergency medicine practice.
