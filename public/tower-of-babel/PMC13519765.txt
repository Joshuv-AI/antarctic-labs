A descriptive case series of hepatotoxicity associated with CFTR modulators and possible relevance of pharmacogenetic polymorphisms in cystic fibrosis patients
Authors: Clara Laffitte Redondo, Venceslas Bourdin, Abd El Kader Ait Tayeb, Sihem Benaboud, Inès Martins, Caroline Joyau, Myriam Benhamida, Isabelle Sermet‐Gaudelus, Clémence Martin, Pierre Régis Burgel, Céline Verstuyft, Laurent Chouchana, Estelle Ayme‐Dietrich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) modulators are widely used in patients with cystic fibrosis and significantly improve respiratory function and quality of life. However, their effectiveness may be limited by liver damage, which sometimes leads to treatment discontinuation, and the mechanisms underlying this remain poorly understood. This case series explores the role of genetic polymorphisms affecting the enzymes and transporters involved in their pharmacokinetics, as well as detoxification mechanisms (CYP3A4/CYP3A5/ABCB1/SLCO1B1/GSTP1/GSTM1/GSTT1). Six cases of hepatotoxicity under CFTR modulators were assessed using the RECAM score. Among the genetic polymorphisms assessed, CYP3A4*22 (rs35599367) allele had higher allele frequency than in the European population, which usually does not express CYP3A5. This genetic variant, reducing CYP3A activity, could decrease the elimination of CFTR modulators and increase the risk of liver toxicity. Other genetic factors may also be involved. Further studies are needed to confirm these results.

INTRODUCTION

The marketing of cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) modulators has been a real breakthrough in the treatment of patients with cystic fibrosis (CF). By increasing the expression and functionality of the CFTR chloride channel, CFTR modulators (CFTRm) improve both respiratory function tests and quality of life indicators, especially the combotherapy ivacaftor/tezacaftor/elexacaftor (IVA/TEZ/ELX). While most patients with CF tolerate this therapy well, some patients have presented adverse events. 1 Pharmacokinetics studies highlight large inter‐ and intra‐individual variability in CFTRm exposure, with the main sources of variability described as body weight, age, exocrine pancreatic insufficiency and CFTR genotype. 2 This pharmacokinetic variability, and specially elevated CFTRm exposure levels, could partially explain certain adverse events, 3 with some resulting from drug–drug interactions with CYP3A4/CYP3A5 strong inhibitors. 4 Among the most frequent adverse drug reactions associated with CFTRm, incidence of elevated liver tests is high, up to 10% of patients in clinical trials, between 5% and 10% in cohorts 1 , 5 requiring regular hepatic monitoring in treated patients. Although hepatotoxic mechanisms are unknown, hepatic necrosis with moderate mixed lobular inflammation on liver biopsies was reported, 6 suggesting the synthesis of toxic or immunogenic metabolites of CFTRm. A recent in vitro hepatotoxicity assays and quantitative systems toxicology modelling using DILIsym identified ivacaftor as the most significant driver of hepatotoxicity through the mechanisms of oxidative stress (ROS production) and mitochondrial dysfunction, due to electron transport chain inhibition. 7 Patients could be more sensitive to ROS production, depending on their level of glutathione S‐transferase (GST) protein expression.

Besides, the deletion of GSTT1 and GSTM1 genes might influence susceptibility to drug‐induced liver injury (DILI), regardless of the type of drug involved. 8

Lumacaftor is not extensively metabolized, but it is a strong inducer of CYP3A4. Ivacaftor, tezacaftor and elexacaftor are each metabolized into active or partially active metabolites by CYP3A4 and CYP3A5, at 100%, 79% and 82%, respectively. 9 CFTRm may act as substrates for P‐glycoprotein and the OATP1B1/3 efflux transporter, 10 so genetic polymorphisms associated with ABCB1 and SLCO1B1 genes, which code for the P‐glycoprotein and OATP1B1 transporters, respectively, may contribute to pharmacokinetic variations in CFTRm. We therefore hypothesize that CYP3A4/CYP3A5, ABCB1, SLCO1B1 and GSTM1, GSTP1 and GSTT1 genetic polymorphisms could contribute to hepatotoxicity.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patients, data collection and clinical evaluation

This retrospective case series consists of patients referred to our Pharmacovigilance Regional Centers (CRPV Paris Cochin‐Necker, CRPV Nantes) for suspected DILI associated with the use of CFTRm. Patients were frequently monitored for CF at French university hospitals (Cystic Fibrosis Competence Centers of Cochin/Necker, Trousseau [AP‐HP, Paris] and Nantes). Drug causality and the diagnosis was individually performed by two clinical pharmacologists (E.A.D., L.C.) specialized in drug safety using the RECAM score (revised electronic version of RUCAM; https://dilirecam.com/). This validated score for DILI takes into account biological and clinical evaluations: liver echography (to assess biliary obstruction), viral tests serologies and/or PCR (to diagnose a recent viral infection with hepatitis A, B, C and E, cytomegalovirus, Epstein–Barr or herpes virus) and clinical evaluation to analyse competing diagnoses (alcohol‐associated hepatitis, septic cholangitis, hypotension, malignant infiltration of the liver and competing medications). 11 Besides, pharmacogenetic analysis was performed to identify genetic factors associated with severity of adverse events, before reintroduction of CFTRm.

Genotyping

The patients' DNA was extracted from leukocytes isolated from whole blood collected in EDTA tubes (QIAsymphony, QIAGEN).

The genetic polymorphisms investigated were as follows: SLCO1B1*5 (NM_006446.5: c.521 T > C, rs4149056), CYP3A4*22 (NM_017460.6: c.522‐191C > T, rs35599367), CYP3A5*3 (NM_000777.5: c.467‐1A > G, rs776746), ABCB1 (NM_001348946.2: c.2677G > A/T rs2032582 and c.3435C > T rs1045642) and GSTP1 (NM_000852.4: c. 313A > G, rs1695) and deletions of the GSTM1 and GSTT1 genes.

Genotyping was performed by allele discrimination using Taqman probes in QuantStudio 7 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for polymorphisms of the SLCO1B1, CYP3A4, CYP3A5 and ABCB1 genes. For genotyping of GST (M1, T1, P1), multiplex PCR was performed, followed by enzymatic digestion and detection of the results by agarose gel electrophoresis, as previously described. 12 The primers and PCR conditions are available on request.

Statistical analysis

Statistical and graphical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.4 (GraphPad Software, Inc.). The data range has been specified for the demographic data. Although this case series was not designed to perform an association study, the comparison of the allele frequencies of each genetic polymorphisms investigated in this cohort and, those described in GnomAD for a non‐Finnish Caucasian population were performed using Fisher's exact tests. All tests were two‐tailed. The significance threshold retained was p < .05. In the context of exploratory analyses, no correction for multiple testing was used according to Bender and Lange. 13

Ethics

Patients, or their legal representatives, have given their consent for genotyping and the use of their clinical data. The study was approved by the local institutional review board of the Cochin University Hospital under the number AAA‐2025‐10066 and complies with the general data protection regulation (GDPR, under the number 202602161715).

This case series of hepatotoxicity under CFTRm in patients with CF consisted in three children and three adults (Table 1). According to the patients genetic mutations of CFTR, four were treated with a combination of IVA/TEZ/ELX, including three in combination with a booster dose of ivacaftor, one was treated with lumacaftor/ivacaftor, and one received ivacaftor/tezacaftor in combination with a booster dose of ivacaftor. All patients experienced hepatocellular liver injury (R ALT/ALP > 5) with serum transaminases greater than five times the upper limit of the normal value during the first or subsequent exposures to CFTRm. Laboratory tests and imaging have ruled out other causes of hepatotoxicity. Drug causality, assessed with RECAM score was highly probable (RECAM > 8) for 1 case and possible (RECAM [−4;3]) for five cases. All patients showed regression of hepatotoxicity upon discontinuation of the CFTRm (positive dechallenge), and two of patients exhibited recurrence of the hepatotoxicity despite the progressive reintroduction of CFTRm at reduced doses (positive rechallenge).

Three patients were heterozygous carriers of the decreased‐function SLCO1B1*5 allele, associated with reduced OATP1B1 hepatic transporter activity, and one of these also carried a mutated genotype of ABCB1 (c.2677G > T/A and c.3435C > T). The allele frequency of genetic polymorphisms of ABCB1 (c.2677G > T/A and c.3435C > T) and SLCO1B1 (c.521 T > C) were not different from that observed in the European population (GnomAD European non‐Finnish; p > .05, for all three variants). Regarding metabolizing enzymes, the no‐function allele CYP3A5*3 (rs776746) was identified in all patients, consistent with a European ancestry profile. The decreased‐function allele CYP3A4*22 (rs35599367) was observed in three heterozygous patients, corresponding to an allele frequency of 21%, significantly higher than in the GnomAD non‐Finnish European population (p = .028) and higher than reported in any GnomAD ethnic group (maximum frequency 5.8%). For GST isoforms, deletions of GSTM1 and GSTT1 were observed in three (50%) and two patients (33%), respectively, while four (66%) patients carried the heterozygous GSTP1 variant (rs1695: c. 313A > G). One patient combined deletions of both GSTM1 and GSTT1 with heterozygosity for GSTP1 (rs1695: c. 313A > G).

DISCUSSION

In this case series of six patients with CFTRm‐induced hepatotoxicity, three were heterozygous for the CYP3A4*22 allele, and all were homozygous for the CYP3A5*3, that is, non‐expressors CYP3A5, suggesting an overall impaired CYP3A metabolic pathway in these patients. 15 , 16 , 17 Besides, we observed a higher‐than‐expected frequency of the CYP3A4*22 allele compared to general population. Altogether, as CYP3A is a major metabolic pathway for IVA/TEZ/ELX, this case series supports that this metabolic pathway may be of interest in the occurrence of CFTRm‐induced hepatotoxicity, likely to result in plasma concentrations.

Even though the factors associated with interindividual variability in CFTRm are generally known (age, weight, pancreatic insufficiency, etc.), co‐administration with drugs that inhibit or induce CYP3A4 (clarithromycin, clofazimine, rifabutin, ritonavir, etc.) was implicated in several ETI dose modifications. 4 Several PB–PK models have been published to account for changes in pharmacokinetic parameters associated with CYP3A4 drug–drug interactions and to enable dose adjustment prior to the administration of CFTRm 18 , 19 ; however, no study has evaluated the influence of CYP3A4 genetic polymorphisms on the pharmacokinetic parameters of CFTRm and raises questions about the pharmacogenomic variations in the CYP3A4 gene, especially in patients who did not express CYP3A5, resulting in poor CYP3A activity.

The majority of drug‐induced hepatotoxicity cases are idiosyncratic, and several genetic polymorphisms have been associated with them. Although NAT2 pharmacogenetic variants are well known to increase the risk of isoniazid‐induced hepatotoxicity in slow acetylators, 20 recent study highlights that CYP3A4*18 heterozygous have a higher risk of anti‐tuberculosis drug‐induced hepatotoxicity in Taiwan population. 21 This gain‐of‐function allele was supposed to increase the release of toxic metabolites. Moreover about atorvastatin metabolism, CYP3A4*22 and CYP3A5*3 variants by reducing enzyme activity, decrease hepatic clearance by approximately 20%–30%, raising hepatotoxicity risk via increased systemic exposure. 22 Once again, mitochondrial dysfunction impairing electron transport chain activity, causing ATP depletion and excessive ROS production, was suggested to induce hepatotoxicity. The presence of decreased function alleles on both efflux and influx transporters, for one patient, raises questions about the effect of these variants on the pharmacokinetics of the combination of lumacaftor/ivacaftor, the well‐tolerated combination CFTRm. 23 In this case series, only one patient expressed the three wild‐type forms of GSTT1, GSTM1 and GSTP1. Even though GST gene deletion is common in the general population, the association between GSTT1, GSTM1 and GSTP1 genetic polymorphisms and adverse drug reaction occurrence has been frequently reported 24 and should be investigated in patients with CF. Several conclusive evidences for association with hepatotoxic risk have been obtained with slow GSTM1 genotypes. 25 Among major mechanisms proposed for DILI based on the production of reactive metabolites, immune‐mediated hepatotoxicity and/or alterations in mitochondrial function and the drug‐induced liver injury from CF disease are based on the same pathophysiology. 26

Finally, an immune‐mediated mechanism may be hypothesized, especially since the incidence and prevalence of drug allergy is known to be higher in patients with CF. 27 A recent study assessed the types of reaction (e.g. rash, liver injury, drug fever), after CFTRm exposure and after re‐exposure, suggesting that drug allergy to CFTRm therapy is very rare and demonstrating that immune‐mediated reactions mainly appear as non‐immediate reactions with mild rashes. 28 Some lumacaftor‐ and ivacaftor‐specific T‐lymphocytes were detected in independent cases. 29 Necrosis and hepatocyte loss with mixed inflammatory infiltrate of lymphocytes were detected in one adult treated with tezacaftor/ivacaftor association, suggesting proliferative T‐cell response after ivacaftor and metabolite exposure in vitro at micromolar doses. 30 Specific human leucocyte antigen alleles could represent genetic risk factors for the development of DILI and should be evaluated. 31

Although this study has several methodological limitations and will have to be confirmed in cohort studies with a precise CFTRm‐induced adverse effects phenotyping, it highlights the contribution of CYP3A4*22 allele in CFTRm‐induced liver injury. We assume that this genetic variant by impairing CYP3A4 hepatic enzyme decreases ivacaftor clearance and improves the reactive oxygen species production. Moreover, therapeutic dose monitoring of CFTRm should be implemented to explore hepatotoxicity. Finally, pre‐emptive genotyping could be performed before CFTRm therapy to identify patients with CF with CYP3A4*22 allele and no CYP3A5 expression.
