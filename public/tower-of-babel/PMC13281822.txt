Rainfall deficit reduces biodiversity and destabilizes a saline-soil plant community
Authors: Xueke Wang, Liming Yan, Ming Jiang, Zhenyu Wang, Baoyu Sun, Huizhu Li, Jiamin Shi, Wei Liu, Guangxuan Han, Jianyang Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rainfall deficits are reshaping plant communities worldwide, yet their impacts on salinity-stressed ecosystems remain unclear. In saline systems, rainfall is essential for flushing soil salts and sustaining biodiversity. Here, we tested the hypothesis that rainfall deficit undermines ecosystem stability by eroding biodiversity in these systems. We conducted a 7-year experiment in the Yellow River Delta, simulating summer-autumn rainfall loss under both ambient and elevated winter-spring temperatures. Rainfall loss increased soil salinity (+43.3% under ambient; +25.2% under warming), promoted stress-tolerant species dominance (+36.9%; +8.76%), and reduced species richness (−26.6%; −14.7%). These shifts led to a consistent decline in community stability. Analytical partitioning demonstrated that this destabilization was primarily driven by biodiversity loss rather than by dominance or compensatory effects. Structural equation modeling further confirmed the rainfall-biodiversity-stability pathway. Our findings show that rainfall deficit destabilizes plant communities in saline soils by weakening biodiversity-based buffering, revealing an overlooked vulnerability to intensifying climate extremes.

INTRODUCTION

Rainfall deficits are intensifying across many regions, posing a growing threat to ecosystem stability worldwide (1–3). In arid and semi-arid ecosystems, prolonged rainfall loss can trigger vegetation instability and ecosystem collapse as water supply dwindles (4–6). Water-rich ecosystems, including wetlands, are typically viewed as less sensitive to rainfall variability because soils are frequently saturated and plant are adapted to high moisture levels (7). However, this assumption does not always hold in salinity-stressed wetlands, where water availability and plant performance are constrained not only by moisture quantity but also by salinity-driven osmotic stress. Recent observations reveal that even water-abundant coastal ecosystems with saline soils have experienced abrupt vegetation diebacks during drought extremes (8), including extensive salt marsh die-offs reported in the southeastern United States (9) and the Mediterranean basin (10). These observations suggest that extreme drought can destabilize plant communities in saline soils, yet the underlying ecological mechanisms remain poorly understood.

In plant communities rooted in saline soils, abundant rainfall is essential for stabilizing community structure through two interlinked pathways. First, rainfall flushes salts from the soil, reducing salinity stress and creating more favorable conditions for a broader range of plant species (11, 12). Second, greater plant growth and diversity can enhance the functioning of key ecosystem processes (13, 14), including regulating microclimates through canopy shading, which buffers temperature fluctuations and limits freshwater loss, promoting species coexistence (15). Consequently, reduced rainfall is expected to destabilize saline-soil plant communities by intensifying soil salinity stress, favoring dominance by salt-tolerant species, and leading to a loss of species richness. This shift allows short-term adaptation to salinity stress, but the accompanying functional and response homogenization, along with loss of functional redundancy, can weaken the ecosystem’s long-term buffering capacity (16). This hypothesis is increasingly adopted by regional modeling studies (17, 18). However, there remains a lack of direct experimental evidence testing how rainfall loss alters plant community structure and stability in salinity-stressed ecosystems.

Moreover, robust testing of this salinity-rainfall hypothesis requires integrating manipulative experiments with analytical frameworks that can disentangle distinct stability mechanisms. In recent decades, more than 1000 manipulative experiments have examined ecosystem responses to global change, yet few have focused on rainfall deficits in salinity-stressed wetlands (19). This gap is particularly critical because salinity-stressed wetlands are typically species-poor and dominated by a few stress-tolerant plants [e.g., Phragmites australis (PA)] (20). In these low-diversity, dominance-driven communities, traditional stability frameworks tend to overstate the role of dominant species while underestimate the stabilizing contribution of diversity. Many frameworks explain stability through two primary mechanisms, weighted population stability (aggregate species contributions), and species asynchrony (asynchronous fluctuations among species) (21–24). However, weighted population stability inherently blends contributions from biodiversity and dominance, making it difficult to isolate their individual contributions (25, 26). Likewise, species asynchrony can stabilize plant communities through compensatory interactions (species buffering each other’s fluctuations) (27, 28) and statistical-averaging (independent species responses reducing variability) (29–31). Therefore, predicting plant community stability in saline-soil wetlands under rainfall deficits requires a framework that explicitly distinguishes between dominance-driven and diversity-based stabilization.

This study conducted a 7-year manipulative field experiment in the Yellow River Delta, China. This region has experienced a rapid decline in summer-autumn rainfall (June–November, −25.1 mm per decade) and a rise in winter-spring temperatures (December–May, +0.35°C per decade) over the past 60 years (fig. S1). These seasonal trends mirror a broader pattern in which ecosystems are increasingly exposed to winter-spring warming and summer-autumn drought (32). We experimentally warmed plots during winter-spring and reduced rainfall during summer-autumn in a saline-soil wetland (Fig. 1A), a system with hydrology regulated by precipitation and groundwater. Our goal was to test whether stability of saline-soil plant communities is maintained by dominant stress-tolerant species or by biodiversity under increasing climatic stress. We adopted a recently developed analytical framework that partitions community stabilization mechanisms into dominance (Δdomi; driven by abundant, temporally stable species), compensatory (ψcomp; driven by compensatory dynamics among species), and statistical-averaging (ωstatis; driven by species diversity arising from independent species fluctuations) effects and quantifies their independent contributions (26). We specifically tested whether rainfall deficit destabilizes the plant community in a salinity-stressed wetland by weakening diversity-driven stabilization mechanisms and whether winter-spring warming modifies these effects.

RESULTS

Treatment effects on soil physicochemical properties

The 50% of summer-autumn precipitation was excluded under rainfall reduction. Rainfall reduction significantly increased growing-season soil moisture by 5.67% (v/v) (April–October; P < 0.001; Fig. 1B and fig. S2A). This unexpected rise in soil moisture under rainfall reduction was confirmed by consistent increases in both volumetric [+7.17% (v/v), P < 0.05; fig. S2B] and gravimetric (+0.98%(g/g), P < 0.05; fig. S2C) water content in 2024 and was driven by a significant decline in soil evaporation (8.38%, P < 0.05; fig. S2D). Meanwhile, winter-spring warming increased soil temperature by 0.37°C (P < 0.001; Fig. 1C and fig. S2F). Rainfall reduction and warming amplified soil salinity by 0.08 and 0.04 S m−1, respectively (both P < 0.001; Fig. 1D and fig. S2G). No significant interactive effect (rainfall reduction × warming) on soil physicochemical properties was observed.

Treatment effects on community variability and its stabilization components

Under control conditions, the plant community was dominated by a few species. PA comprised 50.8% of community cover, and species richness averaged 4.33 ± 0.32 species/m2 (Fig. 2 and fig. S2). Rainfall reduction enhanced PA dominance by 21.4% (P < 0.001) and caused a significant 20.9% decline in species richness (P < 0.001), from 4.20 species/m2 in the plots without rainfall reduction to 3.32 species/m2 in the plots with rainfall reduction. Coefficient of (temporal) variation (CV) of community cover (CVc) was enhanced by 15.3% under rainfall reduction (P < 0.05; Fig. 3B and table S1). Total stabilization (s), defined as the ratio of theoretical (CVe) to observed community CV (CVc), was reduced by 22.2% under rainfall reduction, from 3.17 in the plots without rainfall reduction to 2.46 in the plots with rainfall reduction (P < 0.001; Fig. 3C). Warming alone or interacted with rainfall reduction had no significant impacts on CVc or s.

Mechanisms influencing stabilization components

PA dominance increased linearly with soil salinity (r = 0.57, P < 0.05; fig. S3), strengthening Δdomi (r = 0.71, P < 0.01). Conversely, species richness, independent of salinity, positively correlated with ωstatis (r = 0.80, P < 0.001). ωstatis also correlated negatively with PA dominance (r = −0.78, P < 0.001) and salinity (r = −0.63, P < 0.01).

A structural equation model (SEM) explained 93% of the variance in CVc (Fig. 5). ωstatis, mainly controlled by species richness (standardized path coefficient [rs] = 0.80), was the primary driver of community temporal variability (rs = −1.25). Soil salinity indirectly reduced ωstatis via PA dominance and species richness (rs = −0.37). Δdomi depended on PA dominance (rs = 0.71) only, which was positively associated with elevated soil salinity (rs = 0.57). These trade-offs explain the increase in CVc under rainfall reduction, as soil salinity amplifies Δdomi at the expense of ωstatis. In addition, warming-induced rise in soil temperature weakened ωstatis via elevated soil salinity, but this effect did not cascade to CVc.

DISCUSSION

This study provides experimental evidence supporting the hypothesis that rainfall deficit can destabilize saline-soil plant communities by weakening diversity-driven stabilization mechanisms (Fig. 5). Despite increased species dominance of PA under reduced summer-autumn rainfall, community stability declined significantly. This finding contrasts with patterns often observed in more species-rich ecosystems, where dominant species can buffer ecosystem variability under climate stress (33, 34). In our system, stability was maintained by biodiversity-mediated processes, specifically the statistical-averaging effect (ωstatis), which buffered the impacts of rising soil salinity. These findings highlight that even relatively species-poor saline-soil communities can depend more on species diversity than on dominance to sustain ecosystem stability under drought-induced salinity stress.

By partitioning stability into distinct components, our analysis reveals a counterintuitive basis for stability in this saline-soil plant community. The diversity-driven ωstatis remains the predominant stabilizing driver (43.7% of total stabilization), although the community was species-poor (2 to 7 species/m2). In contrast, the dominance effect (Δdomi) accounted for only 19.9%, marginally exceeding values reported for some species-rich communities (e.g., 15%) (25). Thus, community stability relied not on the most abundant species (i.e., PA) but was dominated by compensatory effect (ψcomp) and ωstatis (Fig. 4B). The limited role of Δdomi was due to the ecological trade-offs in dominant species. Although their strong trait plasticity (e.g., leaf thickness and aerenchyma tissue) and clonal integration support high resistance to salinity stress and low interannual variability (fig. S4) (20, 35, 36), these traits also drive functional homogenization and reflect a growth defense trade-off (16), ultimately restricting their stabilizing contribution. In practical terms, the persistence of different species and their asynchronous responses to salinity stress averaged out the fluctuations in community cover. This quantitative decomposition shows that biodiversity effects, rather than dominance alone, plays a critical role in stabilizing saline-soil wetlands.

Supporting our hypothesis, summer-autumn rainfall reduction destabilized the plant community by increasing soil salinity (Fig. 5), despite an unexpected rise in soil moisture. This finding identifies elevated salinity, rather than soil moisture deficits, as the dominant stressor in this saline-soil system, contrasting with many terrestrial ecosystems where drought effects are mediated primarily through declining moisture availability (37, 38). Mechanistically, elevated salinity suppressed community stability by weakening the diversity-driven ωstatis through two distinct pathways. It directly impaired plant performance via osmotic and ionic stress (39) and indirectly reshaped community structure via competitive exclusion, which favored dominance shifts toward salt-tolerant species such as PA (fig. S3). These community shifts passively elevated the relative contribution of Δdomi due to a decline in ωstatis. Consequently, the modest stabilization from Δdomi was overwhelmed by the severe loss of ωstatis, generating a net destabilizing effect. These effects were further amplified under concurrent winter-spring warming. Warming intensified salinity stress and ωstatis loss, further compounding the destabilizing impact of rainfall loss. Under the combined warming and drought treatment (rainfall reduction × warming), even Δdomi collapsed, indicating that the dominant species exceeded their physiological tolerance threshold (fig. S5). These results point to the possibility of abrupt community reorganization under stronger or prolonged salinity stress, where salinity may transition from a community-assembly filter to a primary driver of instability in salinity-stressed ecosystems.

On the basis of this empirical evidence for salinity’s destabilizing role, we propose a mechanistic framework linking salinity with biodiversity-mediated stabilization. Given the salinity’s fundamental role as a key community filter (39, 40), this pathway has broad applicability across freshwater-dependent ecosystems, including freshwater wetlands facing salinization (41). Under future climate scenarios, intensifying drought, compounded by climate-driven sea level rise, will synergistically amplify salinity stress, increasing the risk of ecosystem collapse (42). However, these ecological trajectories will diverge spatially, governed primarily by local biological and edaphic factors. Specifically, a system’s intrinsic vulnerability depends critically on the salt tolerance and competitive ability of its dominant plant species (fig. S5) (43). At the same time, soil texture modulates stress intensity by regulating water-salt transport, thereby either buffering or accelerating salinity accumulation and its ecological impacts (44). Therefore, quantifying how ωstatis changes across broader salinity gradients is critical for predicting community stability under future climate extremes in freshwater-dependent systems.

To support transparent interpretation of our results, we note two considerations. First, our experiment was conducted in a saline-soil plant community whose salinity and water balance are strongly shaped by local hydrology and soil conditions. Our conclusions should therefore be interpreted within the context of saline soils and plant communities. Second, the counterintuitive increase in soil moisture under rainfall reduction likely reflects ecohydrological feedbacks typical of shallow groundwater-influenced systems, where soil water status is shaped not only by precipitation inputs but also by groundwater connectivity and evaporative demand (fig. S6). Recent work further indicates that climate warming can weaken the precipitation-soil moisture coupling (45), suggesting that drought propagation may not follow rainfall deficits alone. Together, these findings highlight the need for further research to resolve how warming, rainfall reduction, and subsurface hydrology jointly shape drought responses in salinity-stressed ecosystems.

In summary, this study supports the hypothesis that sustained rainfall deficits can destabilize plant communities in moderate to highly saline soils by weakening biodiversity-based stabilization. While biodiversity is known to buffer ecosystem function under climate variability (46, 47), its stabilizing role declines under intensified drought. By decomposing and quantifying stability mechanisms, we show that diversity-driven stabilization (ωstatis) remains critical but vulnerable in salinity-stressed ecosystems. Consequently, these insights underscore the need to integrate biodiversity effects into vegetation-climate models where soil is saline, which often overlook species-level contributions to stability. Although our study focuses on a single nontidal saline system, the mechanisms identified here provide a foundation for broader applications under future climate extremes. Overall, incorporating biodiversity-stability relationships into conservation strategies will be vital for preserving wetland function amid intensifying climate extremes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study site and experimental design

This study was established at the Ecological Research Station of Coastal Wetland in the Yellow River Delta, Chinese Academy of Sciences (37°45′50″N, 118°59′24″E). The region experiences a warm-temperate continental monsoon climate, characterized by dry winters and rainy summers. Long-term meteorological data (1961–2024) indicated declining summer-autumn precipitation and increasing winter-spring air temperature in the Yellow River Delta (fig. S1). During the 7-year experimental period (2018–2024), the mean annual air temperature was 13.4°C, ranging from 12.4° to 13.9°C. Mean annual precipitation was 597 mm, with ~84.4% of precipitation occurring during summer-autumn. Situated in the micro-tidal Yellow River Estuary (48), the study site is a coastal salt marsh classified as a nontidal wetland (table S2) (11), featuring saturated soil (Fig. 1B) and shallow saline groundwater (mean depth: −0.40 ± 0.19 m, 2020–2024) (49). Its hydrological regime is jointly regulated by precipitation and groundwater. During the dry season, saline capillary rise may stress vegetation growth, while heavy rainfall in the wet season often causes surface ponding for 1 to 2 months (35). According to the Chinese classification, the soil texture at the study site is classified as sandy clay loam. Vegetation is dominated by the halophytes PA, Apocynum venetum, and Suaeda glauca. Because classification frameworks differ where tidal influence is weak or absent, we define the site using widely applied diagnostic criteria, including hydric soils, seasonal inundation, shallow saline groundwater, and halophytic vegetation. These features place the system within the spectrum of coastal wetlands influenced by marine-derived salinity.

A seasonal climate manipulative experiment was conducted by a Latin square design in June 2017 with four treatments: control (C), summer-autumn rainfall reduction (−R), winter-spring warming (+W), and a combination of summer-autumn rainfall reduction and winter-spring warming (−R + W). Each treatment had four replicates (16 plot total; 3-m–by–4 m plots spaced 3-m apart). Winter-spring warming was achieved using infrared radiators (LPR2420/1500-L, Guangzhou Longpro, China) suspended 2 m above the ground (December–May; Fig. 2A). Summer-autumn rainfall was reduced by 50% using V-shaped transparent polymethyl methacrylate plates (20-cm width), spaced 20-cm apart, and mounted on a steel frame at a height of 1.5 to 2 m (June–November). This rainout shelter, chosen for its low cost and minimal microclimatic interference (50), has been widely adopted in precipitation manipulation experiments worldwide. These plates were replaced when their interception of photosynthetically active radiation exceeded 10%. “Dummy” heaters and inverted plates, identical in number and size to those in treatment plots, were installed in control plots to minimize shading effects (fig. S7).

Soil physicochemical properties

Hydra probe sensors (Stevens Water Monitoring Systems Inc., Oregon, USA), linked to a CR1000 data logger (Campbell Scientific, Logan, Utah, USA), recorded 0- to 10-cm soil parameters [moisture (volumetric water content), temperature, and salinity (electrical conductivity)] from 2018 to 2024. During the 2024 growing season, soil samples were collected from the surface soil (0 to 10 cm) in each plot, and gravimetric water content was determined by measuring mass loss after oven drying at 105°C for 48 hours.

Vegetation and ecosystem evapotranspiration monitoring

Vegetation cover was assessed monthly (April–October) in permanent quadrats (1 m by 1 m) using a visual estimation method (fig. S8A). To maximize accuracy and consistency, each quadrat was divided into 100 grids (10 cm by 10 cm), and the projected cover of all species within each grid was recorded. Species-specific cover was calculated as the sum of cover across all grids. Community cover was calculated as the sum of species-level cover, which may exceed 100% due to canopy overlap among species. Although visual estimation can be susceptible to observer bias, this standardized grid-based method, validated against digital canopy imagery, provided reliable and consistent measurements across years and observers (fig. S8B). To quantify the green chromatic coordinate, permanent quadrats (1 m by 1 m) were photographed from a fixed position twice a month from 2018 to 2024 using a mirrorless camera (Sony ILCE-5100 L, APS-C) in program auto mode (P mode) with a 16-mm focal length. Before each session, an 18% gray card was used to calibrate white balance and minimize the effect of varying light conditions on image greenness. The green chromatic coordinate was calculated from the image’s red, green, and blue (RGB) values using the R package PhenoPix, representing the proportion of green relative to the total RGB intensity. Species dominance was quantified as the ratio of its cover to community cover. Species richness was defined as the total number of plant species recorded within each quadrat. To ensure that any subsequent changes could be attributed to the treatments, a baseline survey in June 2017 (pretreatment) verified that the initial plant community structure was consistent across all plots (table S3).

Plant cover serves as a reliable proxy for productivity (51, 52), as evidenced by a strong correlation between species aboveground net primary productivity (ANPP) and cover in this site (fig. S9). This relationship was based on a paired dataset of species ANPP and cover collected near the experimental plots. Species cover and ANPP were assessed annually in early October from 2016 to 2021, with eight calibration plots (1 m by 1 m) selected each year. Within each calibration plot, species cover was recorded, and current-year aboveground biomass was then clipped at ground level. Plant material was sorted by species, oven-dried at 70°C to a constant weight, and weighed to determine species ANPP.

Within each plot, ecosystem evapotranspiration and soil evaporation were measured on clear mornings between 09:00 and 11:00 a.m. from 2018 to 2024, at 2-week intervals, using a LI-6400 portable photosynthesis system (Li-Cor Inc., Lincoln, NE, USA) and an LGR–ultra-portable greenhouse gas analyzer (UGGA) analyzer (UGGA; Los Gatos Research Inc., San Jose, CA, USA), respectively. Water fluxes were calculated from the linear increase in H2O concentration, and plant transpiration was calculated as the difference between ecosystem evapotranspiration and soil evaporation.

Decomposition framework for ecological stability

Temporal stability at the interannual scale is evaluated using the inverse of the coefficient of (temporal) variation (CV) of community cover (CVc), calculated as the ratio of the SD to the mean of community cover over time (47, 51). The CVc was closely correlated with the CV of gross ecosystem productivity (fig. S9), further validating its use as a proxy for assessing functional stability. A lower CVc indicates higher stability. To explore the underlying mechanisms of this stability, we used the framework proposed by Segrestin et al. (26), which decomposed CVc into the CV of average species cover (CVe) and the total stabilization (τ). Statistical analyses

Before analysis, all variables were converted to seasonal means (spring: April–May, summer: June–August, autumn: September–October) to account for the unequal durations of the growing season periods. Linear mixed models (LMMs) tested the effects of treatments and season on CVc and its stabilization metrics (s, Δdomi, ψcomp, and ωstatis), community composition (PA dominance and species richness), and soil parameters (soil moisture, temperature, and salinity), with block as a random factor. The normality assumption for the model residuals was verified for all dependent variables before analysis, and all models met this prerequisite (table S4). Simple regressions were assessed the relationships among stabilization components, community composition, and soil parameters. Significance was set at P < 0.05.

A SEM was constructed to explore how summer-autumn rainfall reduction and winter-spring warming affected CVc through stabilization components (Δdomi, ψcomp, and ωstatis), plant community composition (PA dominance and species richness), and soil physicochemical properties (soil moisture, temperature, and salinity). On the basis of an a priori theoretical framework, all potential causal variables and pathways were initially included (fig. S10), and the model was subsequently simplified by sequentially removing nonsignificant paths. Model fit was evaluated using Fisher’s C statistic (P > 0.05). All statistical analyses were conducted in R version 4.3.3, using the “lme4” and “piecewiseSEM” packages for LMM analyses and SEM construction.
