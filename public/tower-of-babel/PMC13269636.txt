Superconducting quantum interference device (SQUID) analysis for (α-Fe₂O₃) nanoparticles with tailored physical and magnetic properties
Authors: Syed M. Hussain, Syed Mustansar Hussain, Hijaz Ahmad, Mustafa Bayram, Mohamed R. Eid, Assmaa Abd-Elmonem, Kamel Guedri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study presents the hydrothermal synthesis of hematite (α-Fe₂O₃) nanoparticles with controlled morphological variations, including rhombohedral, irregular, and Nano plate structures. The phase purity and crystallographic structure of the synthesized samples were validated using X-ray diffraction, confirming the successful formation of hematite. Detailed transmission electron microscopy analysis revealed pronounced differences in particle shape and size, emphasizing the effectiveness of the synthesis method in tailoring morphology. Magnetic properties were systematically investigated using a superconducting quantum interference device magnetometer, demonstrating a clear correlation between nanoparticle morphology and magnetic response. The results collectively show that particle morphology plays a substantial role in determining both the structural and magnetic behavior of hematite nanoparticles. These findings contribute to the rational design and engineering of nanomaterials for advanced technological applications.

Introduction

Magnetic iron oxide nanoparticles have attracted sustained interest because their magnetic response is highly sensitive to crystal phase, size and morphology. Magnetite (Fe₃O₄) has been the focus of a lot of research because of its strong magnetism and potential in the field of medicine. However, hematite (α-Fe₂O₃) is a different type of iron oxide that is not only very chemically stable but also low in toxicity and cheap, which makes it quite suitable for catalysis, sensing, energy conversion, as well as magnetic applications. When hematite is at the nanoscale level, it shows size- and shape-dependent magnetic properties, the Morin transition being suppressed or changed and weak ferromagnetism or superparamagnetic appearing. Being at the forefront of materials science and nanotechnology, magnetic nanoparticles synthesized with high accuracy possess the potential to revolutionize a broad range of applications. Examples of these are drug delivery, bio imaging, and therapeutic technologies for medical applications, purification of wastewater and removal of pollutants for the environment, and data storage via advanced technologies. It has been reported in recent work that significant achievements have been made in the development of nanoparticle-based biomedical devices, magnetic nanostructures for rapid purification of wastewater, and nanofiber magnetic materials for data storage and information transmission. On-going progress in the development of controlled synthesis methods facilitates the fine adjustment of nanoparticle size, shape, and magnetic performance, which are crucial aspects to be considered for maximizing their efficiency in these new technological ‍‌‍‍‌‍‌‍‍‌applications.

Chen‍‌‍‍‌‍‌‍‍‌ et al. [1] fabricated α-Fe₂O₃ nanowires by nano casting and studied how the different modes of their assembly affect the magnetic interactions and the gas-sensing performance. Akhavan and Azimirad [2] found that Fe₂O₃ nanograin chains coated with a TiO₂ nanolayer exhibited a high photocatalytic activity for visible-light. Sadeghi-Niaraki et al. [3] created Fe₂O₃@TiO₂ nanostructured pigments which are characterized with high near-infrared reflectance and excellent photocatalytic performance. Zhang et al. [4] illustrated that porous Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles have the potential to act as high-performance electrochemical anode materials for lithium-ion batteries. Moradi et al. [5] prepared magnetite/dextran-functionalized graphene oxide nanosheets for improved positive contrast in in-vivo magnetic resonance imaging. Tadić et al. [6] have made a comprehensive study of hydrothermal synthesis of α-Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles and the relationship among morphology, magnetic properties, and cytotoxicity. Colombo et al. [7] studied how the hydrothermal treatment factors affect the particle size, shape, and colloidal stability of hematite nanoparticles. Ge et al. [8] achieved morphology-controlled hydrothermal synthesis of α-Fe₂O₃ and demonstrated its improved photocatalytic activity for Cr(VI) reduction.

Hematite nanoparticles have excellent air stability, strong corrosion resistance, low toxicity, low cost and are environmentally friendly. Hematite nanoparticles are less hazardous during synthesis and more stable than other iron polymorphs. They are used in potential applications, such as gas sensors, catalysis, pigments, lithium-ion batteries, solar cells, magnetic and antibacterial materials. To further improve the physical properties of hematite nanoparticles, it is mandatory to have a uniform shape of nanoparticles and a very narrow particle size distribution [9]. There are some studies found on the synthesis of hematite nanoparticles done by other groups: Direct metal oxidation, iron precursor hydrolysis, and phase transition from other iron oxide phases to the hematite phase are the three main methods used to create hematite nanoparticles. The processes of oxidation, dihydroxylation (loss of OH), and dehydration (loss of H2O) may result in the phase transition of any other iron phase to the hematite phase [10]. Several techniques have been devised to create different types of nanoscale hematite structures, including methodologies such as precipitation, sol-gel, mechanochemical, thermal decomposition, solvothermal, and hydrothermal [11–14].

One of the most sensitive methods for detecting very weak magnetic signals is magnetic measurement with a SQUID magnetometer. The principle of operation for SQUID is the phenomenon of quantum interference occurring in superconducting loops with Josephson junctions, which allows for the detection of very small changes in magnetic flux. The extremely high sensitivity of SQUID magnetometers makes them an ideal tool for studying the magnetic characteristics of nanoscale materials, such as saturation magnetization, magnetic phase transitions, and temperature-dependent magnetic behavior. In the field of nanoparticles, magnetic characterization by SQUID is particularly useful for: differentiating the superparamagnetic, ferromagnetic, and weak ferromagnetic behaviors, determining transition temperatures quite accurately, like the Morin transition in hematite nanoparticles. Zhu et al. organized a cantaloupe-like hematite framework through hydrothermal fabrication, which exhibited high coercivity at room temperature with an HC value of 2279 [15]. Additionally, they also produced a silkworm pod-like hematite superstructure with high coercivity through hydrothermal synthesis, with an HC value of 3315.5 Oe [15]. Jayanthi et al. [16] described weak ferromagnetic characteristics of hematite nanoparticles with a size of approximately 50 nm, which exhibited a saturation magnetization of Ms = 1.899 emu/g, a remanent magnetization of Mr = 0.659 emu/g, and a coercivity of HC = 414.98 Oe at a temperature of 300 K. By decreasing the particle size, the Morin temperature of hematite nanoparticles can also be reduced, eventually disappearing for particles smaller than approximately 10 nm [12, 13, 17, 18]. For small hematite nanoparticles, specifically those below 10 nm, superparamagnetic behaviour properties have been observed [7, 19, 20]. Lee and Kwak [21] acquired tubular superstructures composed of superparamagnetic hematite nanoparticles (around 10 nm) from the pyrolysis of metal-organic frameworks in a confined space. Li et al. [22] stated that the high feature ratio improves shape anisotropy, which brings large magnetic coercivity. In recent studies, it has been investigated how inter-particle interaction affects the magnetic characteristics of hematite nanoparticles. It has been found that the strength of interaction between the particles plays a crucial role in the superparamagnetic relaxation of the particles [23, 24]. Long-range dipole-dipole interactions may also affect the magnetic properties in ferromagnetic or ferri-magnetic systems, even if the magnetic particles are dispersed in a non-magnetic solid matrix [25]. However, for hematite nanoparticles, the dipole-dipole interaction is generally weak, and it is believed that exchange interactions between surface atoms of the nanoparticles in direct contact have a significant influence on their magnetic characteristics [26].

Ganure et al. [27] prepared Ni²⁺– and In³⁺-doped cobalt ferrites and structurally, magnetically and catalytically tested them for Bis-(indolyl)methane derivative ‍‌‍‍‌‍‌‍‍‌synthesis.‍ Ganure et al. [28] found a green method of making pyrimidine derivatives using a magnetically recoverable and reusable catalyst. Dhale‍‌‍‍‌‍‌‍‍‌ et al. [29] have revealed that reusable La³⁺-doped mixed ferrite nanocrystals could be GE magnetic catalysts for the green synthesis of triaryl imidazole derivatives in one-pot and without any solvent. Singh and Sharma [30] studied the magnetic behavior improvements of Er³⁺-doped Co–Mn ferrite nanoparticles for magnetic device applications. Singh and Sharma [31] conducted a study on the Improved magnetic features of Gd3+ doped Co-Mn ferrite nanoparticles for data storage applications requiring high density. They disclosed the Gd³⁺-doped Co–Mn ferrite nanoparticles with superior magnetic properties for high-density data storage ‍‌‍‍‌‍‌‍‍‌applications.

Previous studies have demonstrated that hematite can be synthesized in diverse morphologies such as nanoplates, rhombohedral particles and irregular structures using hydrothermal and related methods. These works have largely focused on either morphological control or qualitative magnetic characterization. Nevertheless, while individual shapes have been reported extensively, there is no systematic and comparative study explaining how different morphologies, under similar synthesis conditions, affect the Morin transition temperature, thermal hysteresis, and saturation magnetization. Especially, the extent to which particle anisotropy and dimensional constraints determine the magnetic transitions measured through the same SQUID protocols has not been clearly understood. This research therefore seeks to fill this void by performing a controlled hydrothermal synthesis of α-Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles with clearly rhombohedral, nanoplate, and irregular shapes, followed by a direct and morphology-resolved SQUID magnetic comparison. By connecting the in-depth TEM-based structural data with the field-cooled/zero-field-cooled magnetic measurements, the present article tries to elucidate in what way shape anisotropy and particle size together influence the magnetic properties of hematite nanoparticles.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of hematite (α-Fe₂O₃) nanoparticles

Hematite nanoparticles were synthesized using a hydrothermal method with controlled variation of precursor chemistry, solvent composition, and additive type to obtain three different morphologies. Three representative samples were selected for detailed structural and magnetic characterization: sm_14, sm_24, and sm_41. The synthesis parameters are summarized in Table 1. For each synthesis, the required iron precursor, nucleating agent, and morphology-directing additive were dissolved in the selected solvent system under magnetic stirring until a homogeneous precursor solution was obtained. The prepared solution was then transferred into a 50 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave. The autoclave was sealed and heated under hydrothermal conditions according to the temperature and reaction time listed in Table 1. After completion of the reaction, the autoclave was allowed to cool naturally to room temperature. The obtained reddish-orange precipitate was collected by centrifugation and washed repeatedly to remove residual ions, unreacted precursors, and organic additives. The washing process was carried out using distilled water and ethanol, followed by acetone when required. Each washing step was performed by centrifugation at 6500 rpm for 15 min. The purified product was then dried and stored for subsequent TEM, XRD, and SQUID characterization. The dried product masses recovered from the selected hydrothermal synthesis batches are listed in Table 2.

XRD

X-ray diffraction was used to identify the crystalline phase and estimate the crystallite size of the selected α-Fe₂O₃ nanoparticle samples. The XRD patterns of sm_14, sm_41, and sm_24 are shown in Fig. 5. These samples correspond to irregular nanoparticles, nanoplates, and rhombohedral nanoparticles, respectively. The diffraction peaks of all three samples are mainly indexed to the hematite α-Fe₂O₃ phase. The characteristic diffraction peaks observed at approximately 2θ = 24.12°, 33.11°, 35.61°, 40.82°, 49.41°, 54.00°, 57.50°, 62.38°, and 63.96° correspond to the (012), (104), (110), (113), (024), (116), (018), (214), and (300) crystallographic planes of hematite, respectively. The three most intense hematite reflections are located near 33.11°, 35.61°, and 49.41°, corresponding to the (104), (110), and (024) planes. These results confirm the successful formation of crystalline α-Fe₂O₃ in the selected samples.

For samples sm_24 and sm_41, no additional impurity peaks were detected within the measured range, indicating that these samples are predominantly composed of the hematite phase. In contrast, sample sm_14 shows weak additional peaks marked with the symbol (*) in Fig. 5. These additional reflections are assigned to sodium chloride, with peaks observed at approximately 2θ = 31.70°, 45.45°, and 75.28°. The presence of NaCl in sm_14 is most likely related to residual salt remaining after the washing process. Therefore, sm_14 is described as hematite containing a minor NaCl impurity rather than a completely predominantly hematite phase sample.

The crystallinity values calculated from the XRD data were 71% for sm_24, 69% for sm_41, and 69% for sm_14, as summarized in Table 3. Among the three selected samples, sm_24 showed the highest crystallinity. However, because the XRD samples were prepared by drop-casting nanoparticle dispersions onto glass substrates, the calculated crystallinity values should be interpreted as comparative estimates rather than absolute crystallinity values. Film thickness, particle packing, preferred orientation, and background contribution from the glass substrate may influence the calculated values.

For sm_41, TEM analysis provides a more reliable description of the anisotropic nanoplate dimensions. These structural results support the TEM observations and provide the crystallographic basis for comparing the magnetic behavior of the three selected morphologies.

Superconducting quantum interference device (SQUID) technique

SQUID magnetometry was used to investigate the magnetic response and Morin transition behavior of the selected α-Fe₂O₃ nanoparticle samples. Three representative samples were measured: sm_24 rhombohedral nanoparticles, sm_41 nanoplates, and sm_14 irregular nanoparticles. A pure resin sample was also prepared and measured under the same conditions to evaluate the diamagnetic contribution from the resin matrix and sample holder.

For SQUID sample preparation, the dried nanoparticles were first dispersed in ethanol to improve particle separation and handling. The dispersion was placed in a Petri dish and left overnight to allow complete evaporation of ethanol. After drying, the nanoparticle powder was collected and mixed with a polymer resin and hardener. The hardener-to-polymer ratio was maintained at 25:75. The nanoparticles were added to the polymer before curing, and the total mass of the nanoparticle–polymer mixture was recorded. The mixture was homogenized using a Miccra D1 homogenizer to improve particle distribution inside the resin matrix.

After homogenization, the nanoparticle–resin mixture was transferred into a plastic capsule using a dropper. The filled capsule was weighed, and the nanoparticle mass inside the capsule was calculated from the nanoparticle-to-polymer mixing ratio and the total filled capsule mass. The capsule was then left to dry and cure overnight before SQUID measurement. The magnetization values reported in this work were normalized with respect to the nanoparticle mass in the capsule. SQUID data measurement method

Magnetic measurements were performed using a SQUID magnetometer. Magnetization versus magnetic field, M–H, measurements were carried out at 300 K over a magnetic-field range of − 7 T to + 7 T. These measurements were used to examine the room-temperature magnetic response and estimate the saturation magnetization, Ms, of each sample. Temperature-dependent magnetization measurements were carried out from 10 K to 350 K using three measurement protocols: zero-field-cooled warming (ZFC), field-cooled warming (FCW), and field-cooled cooling (FCC). All temperature-dependent measurements were performed under an applied magnetic field of 50 Oe. For the ZFC measurement, the sample was first heated to 350 K in zero magnetic field and held at this temperature for 5 min to stabilize the thermal state. The sample was then cooled to 10 K without applying an external magnetic field. After reaching 10 K, a magnetic field of 50 Oe was applied, and the magnetization was recorded during warming from 10 K to 350 K. For the FCW measurement, the sample was cooled to 10 K under an applied field of 50 Oe, and the magnetization was recorded during warming from 10 K to 350 K under the same field. For the FCC measurement, the sample was cooled from 350 K to 10 K under 50 Oe, and the magnetization was recorded during cooling. The FCW and FCC measurements were used to examine the thermal hysteresis around the Morin transition. The Morin transition temperature \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${T_m}$$\end{document}, was identified from the abrupt change in the temperature-dependent magnetization curves. The transition temperature during warming is denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${T_m}\left( W \right)$$\end{document}, and the transition temperature during cooling is denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${T_m}\left( C \right)$$\end{document}. The thermal hysteresis, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\Delta}}}{T_m}$$\end{document}, was calculated using:

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${T_m}={T_m}\left( W \right) - {T_m}\left( C \right)$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${T_m}\left( W \right)$$\end{document} is the Morin transition temperature obtained from the field-cooled warming curve and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${T_m}\left( C \right)$$\end{document} is the Morin transition temperature obtained from the field-cooled cooling curve.

SQUID data evaluation method

The M–H curves were used to evaluate the room-temperature magnetic behavior of the nanoparticles. The measured magnetic response included two contributions: the magnetic contribution from α-Fe2O3 nanoparticles and the diamagnetic contribution from the resin matrix, capsule, and sample holder. The pure resin reference sample was used to identify the background response. The high-field linear component in the M–H curves was attributed mainly to the diamagnetic background, while the nonlinear low-field part was associated with the nanoparticle magnetic response. The saturation magnetization, Ms, was estimated from the M–H curves after considering the high-field background contribution. The obtained values were normalized to the nanoparticle mass inside each capsule and are therefore reported in emu g⁻¹ of nanoparticles.

The Morin transition temperature was evaluated from the ZFC, FCW, and FCC magnetization curves. First, the temperature range containing the sharp magnetic transition was identified from the magnetization curve. Then, the first derivative of the magnetization with respect to temperature was calculated to locate the transition more accurately. The derivative peak was fitted using a Gaussian function, and the temperature corresponding to the maximum of the fitted peak was assigned as the Morin transition temperature. This derivative-based approach was used because the Morin transition appears as a sharp slope change in the magnetization curve rather than as a simple maximum or minimum. The Gaussian fit was used to reduce uncertainty in selecting the peak position from the derivative curve. The transition temperatures obtained from ZFC, FCW, and FCC measurements were then compared for each sample.

Discussion

The study aimed to investigate the magnetic characteristics of a range of hematite nanoparticles, such as the magnetic behaviour and Morin temperature. This was accomplished by analyzing a sample group composed of sm_14, sm_24, sm_41_B2, and pure resin, using Superconducting Quantum Interference Device (SQUID) measurements. Magnetization versus magnetic field (M vs. H) measurements were taken at 300 K for all samples. The‍‌‍‍‌‍‌‍‍‌ magnetic properties of the synthesized α-Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles have a clear and uniform dependence on particle shapes and characteristic sizes, as confirmed through SQUID measurements described in the Results section. Rhombohedral particles (mean size 32 ± 4 nm) behave superparamagnetic at room temperature, having a saturation magnetization of about 0.24 emu g⁻¹ and a Morin transition temperature near 214 K. On the other hand, irregular particles (53 ± 4 nm) and nanoplates (thickness 20 ± 5 nm, lateral dimension ≈ 123 ± 11 nm) show very weak ferromagnetic behavior, with their Morin transition temperatures being around 238 K and 241 K, respectively.

The extent of thermal hysteresis, described as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta {T_m}={T_m}\left( W \right) - {T_m}\left( C \right)$$\end{document}, progressively grows from irregular particles (≈ 1 K) to rhombohedral particles (≈ 2 K) and reaches the peak for nanoplates (≈ 7 K), thus supporting the notion that shape anisotropy and dimensional confinement have been influential. Quantitative results like these are invariably detected in M–H, ZFC/FC, and FCC experiments and indicate that hematite nanoparticle thickness and morphological anisotropy have a stronger impact on magnetic transitions than the overall particle size.

The Discussion and Conclusions sections have been merged so that they emphasize these quantitative correlations and thus are directly supported by the experiment.

Before‍‌‍‍‌‍‌‍‍‌ determining the intrinsic magnetic parameters of the nanoparticles, the diamagnetic signal from the resin matrix and the sample holder were corrected very precisely. A reference sample with pure resin in the same kind of capsule was measured in the same way (− 7 T to + 7 T at 300 K) in the SQUID. The reference showed a completely linear response to the field without any hysteresis, thus confirming that it was diamagnetic. The total measured magnetization of the composite samples was then written as Mtotal(H) = MNP(H)+χdiaH is the linear diamagnetic background. At the high-field region \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\left| H \right| \geqslant 5~T$$\end{document}), where the magnetization of the nanoparticles nears the saturation, a linear function (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M\left( H \right)=Ms+\chi diaH$$\end{document}) was used to fit the data, thus getting both the diamagnetic susceptibility and the real saturation magnetization (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Ms$$\end{document}). Moreover, the subtraction method of the mass-normalized resin reference curve was carried out as a consistency check. It gave totally consistent corrected curves with the level of uncertainty of 3% or less. Post background subtraction, M–H curves showed good symmetry and field independence at high fields, and the application of Langevin fitting resulted in a low residual error with no evident systematic deviations. The obtained values of diamagnetic susceptibilities agreed with typical polymer character values (~ 10⁻⁷–10⁻⁶ emu g⁻¹ Oe⁻¹), thus confirming that the correction was done correctly. The saturation magnetization values being referred to here are 100% from the background-corrected nanoparticle ‍‌‍‍‌‍‌‍‍‌contribution.

To‍‌‍‍‌‍‌‍‍‌ pick out a reliable Morin transition temperature (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${T_m}$$\end{document}) from the magnetic measurements, the first derivatives of the ZFC and FCW magnetization plots (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$dM/dT$$\end{document}) were taken and the resulting curves were fitted with a Gaussian function. Tm uncertainties were obtained by considering the Gaussian peak fitting error, the instrumental temperature resolution (± 1 K), and the difference in transition temperatures derived from the ZFC and FCW measurements. According to this evaluation, the error in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${T_m}$$\end{document} is ± 1–2 K. The rhombohedral particles (sm_24) have \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${T_m}$$\end{document} = 214 ± 1 K, the irregular ones (sm_14) − \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${T_m}$$\end{document} = 239 ± 1 K, and the nanoplates (sm_41_B2) indicate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${T_m}$$\end{document} = 241 ± 2 K. The slightly higher error for the nanoplates is due to the broader derivative peak and bigger thermal hysteresis. Both FC and ZFC magnetization measurements were carried out in an external magnetic field of 50 Oe. Since this field magnitude is negligible in comparison with the intrinsic anisotropy and exchange fields of hematite, its effect on Tm should be almost non-existent. Still, a magnetic field can promote the weak ferromagnetic spin-canted state above Tm and thus lower the Morin transition temperature slightly. At 50 Oe, such a shifting effect can be expected to be not more than 1–2 K and hence, it is covered by the measurement uncertainty of the present work. Therefore, the experimentally determined \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${T_m}$$\end{document} values are to be considered as the inherent ones within the error range ‍‌‍‍‌‍‌‍‍‌given.

The field cool and zero field cool warming measurement was performed on samples sm_14, sm_24 and sm_41_B2, using a 50 Oe magnetic field in a temperature range from 10 K to 350 K. Based on TEM analysis, the sm_41_B2 sample comprises nanoplates with a thickness of 20 ± 5 nm and a length of 123 ± 11 nm. In contrast, according to TEM analysis, the sm_14 samples contain irregularly shaped nanoparticles, with sizes of 53 ± 4 nm. According to TEM analysis, the sm_24 sample consists of cube-shaped nanoparticles that are 32 ± 4 nm. The curve plotting magnetization (M) against the magnetic field (H), commonly called the M vs. H curve, can reveal insights into nanoparticle characteristics and saturation magnetization value. Upon analyzing the magnetization vs. field measurements, it was discovered that at room temperature, the particles sm_41_B2, and sm_14 exhibit behaviour similar to weakly ferromagnetic particles. Conversely, the particles sm_24 display superparamagnetic properties at room temperature, as evidenced by the presence of an “s” shaped M vs. H curve in both samples. Ozdemir and Dunlop conducted a comprehensive study on the magnetic hysteresis characteristics of natural and synthetic hematite with varying grain sizes. They suggested that the magnetic properties of hematite nanoparticles, such as the morin transition, saturation magnetization, and magnetic coercivity, were greatly influenced by anisotropy. The authors also examined the relationship between grain size and coercivity and determined that HC peaked between 0.1 and 3 μm [32]. The literature indicates that combining small and oriented sub-particles into the superstructure transformed the single-domain structure into a multi-domain-like structure, resulting in higher remanent magnetization and coercivity [33–35]. The results from samples sm_14, sm_24 and sm_41_B2 are very much in agreement with the literature. No remanent magnetization and coercive field were seen in the M vs. H curve of sm_14, sm_24, and sm_41_B2. The nanoparticles probably have a signal crystal structure, no strain and defect free. The M vs. H curve was used to determine the magnetic saturation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${M_s}$$\end{document}) of samples sm_14, sm_24 and sm_41_B2, which had values of 0.1 emu/g, 0.24 emu/g and 0.25 emu/g, respectively. Typically, the magnetic saturation of hematite nanoparticles increases with particle size [36]. However, this trend was not observed in these samples despite having different sizes and shapes. The magnetic saturation value is affected by particle size, surface coating (for shape control), and magnetic anisotropy. Sample sm_24 had rhombohedral-shaped particles with a size of 32 ± 4 nm coated with oleate group, while sample sm_41_B2 had nanoplates with a thickness of 20 ± 5 nm and a length of 123 ± 11 nm coated with acetate group. The irregularly shaped particles in sample sm_14 and sm_38 were coated with the PVP group. These samples’ lack of size-dependent magnetic saturation is likely due to their different shapes, sizes, magnetic anisotropy, and surface coating agents. Tadic et al. [35] investigated the nanoplates and rhombohedral-shaped nanoparticles. In his work, the size of the rhombohedral-shaped particles was 30–70 nm. He observes the saturation magnetization \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Ms=0.87$$\end{document} emu/g at 300 K for rhombohedral-shaped particles [35]. For nanoplates, he achieved the saturation magnetization \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${M_s}=0.87$$\end{document} emu/g, a diameter of 60–140 nm and a thickness of 20–40 nm [35]. The magnetic saturation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${M_s}$$\end{document} value was observed less in rhombohedral shapes and nanoplates than those reported by the Tadic et al. [35] literature. The less particle-particle interaction was the most probable reason for getting a lesser value for Saturation magnetization. The lesser particle-particle interaction can only be achieved using the ultrasonication probe during sample preparation and embedding the particle into the solid resin matrix.

Sun et al. [36] employed a hydrothermal method to synthesise hematite nanoplatelets with 100–140 nm diameters and 40–70 nm thickness. For nanoplates, he achieved magnetic saturation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${M_s}=0.91$$\end{document} em/g. He observed the weak ferromagnetic behaviour of the nanoplates [37]. Hao et al. [37] reported hematite nanoplates with a diameter of 180 nm and thickness of 30 nm. He noted the weak ferromagnetic behaviour from hematite nanoplates. Zhu et al. [38] synthesized the irregularly shaped hematite nanoparticles. The size was in the range of 30–40 nm. He reported the weak ferromagnetic behaviour of nanoparticles at room temperature. Zhao et al. [39] found that synthesised nanoplates exhibited ferromagnetic behaviour at room temperature and showed that magnetization saturation was a function of the field strength.

The temperature at which the morin transition occurs in the bulk hematite phase was measured to be \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${T_m}=263$$\end{document} K; hematite exhibits weak ferromagnetism above this temperature, up to 963 K, and anti-ferromagnetism below this temperature. Previous studies suggest that particle size primarily influences the morin transition temperature and is less affected by the shape of the particles. The zero-field cool warming measurement was used to determine the morin transition in samples sm_14, sm_24 and sm_41_B2. The point at which there was a sudden increase in the slope of the curve at a specific temperature was identified as the morin transition temperature. The morin transition temperature was found to be 238 K for sample sm_14, 214 K for sample sm_24 and 241 K for sample sm_41_B2. Tadic et al. [35] observed the morin transition temperature for nanoplates at 242 K. He reported the morin transition temperature around 221 K for rhombohedral-shaped particles. The morin transition temperature for nanoplate from sample sm_41_B2 and rhombohedrons particles from sm_24 remarkably agree with Tadic et al. [35].

Zhu et al. [38] synthesised the irregularly shaped hematite nanoparticles through the hydrothermal process. He reported the morin temperature of 259 K for the irregularly shaped particles. Contrarily the irregularly shaped hematite nanoparticles obtained from sample sm_14 show the morin temperature at 238 K. A considerable difference was seen in the reported literature and experimental values for morin temperature (Table 8). The difference in morin temperature was most likely because of higher particle–particle interaction. Zhu et al. [38] highlighted the higher particle–particle interaction in their work.

Conclusions

This study focuses on the synthesis, structural characterization and magnetic analysis of hematite (α-Fe₂O₃) nanoparticles with different morphologies rhombohedral, irregular and nanoplate using the hydrothermal method. Each morphology was achieved by varying the chemical precursors and reaction conditions. TEM analysis revealed distinct particle sizes: rhombohedral (32 ± 4 nm), irregular (53 ± 4 nm), and nanoplates (20 ± 5 nm thick, 123 ± 11 nm in diameter). Size distribution was narrow for rhombohedral and irregular shapes, while nanoplates showed narrow diameter but broader thickness variation. XRD confirmed the predominantly pure hematite phase, with the most intense peaks corresponding to the (110) and (104) planes. Crystallite sizes from XRD (using the Scherrer formula) were smaller than TEM sizes, indicating single-crystal nature. A minor presence of the magnetite phase was also detected in one sample.

SQUID magnetometry revealed distinct morphology-dependent magnetic behaviors among the synthesized hematite nanoparticles. Rhombohedral particles exhibited superparamagnetic characteristics at room temperature, with a saturation magnetization of 0.24 emu/g and a Morin transition temperature of 221 K. In contrast, both nanoplate and irregular-shaped particles displayed weak ferromagnetism, with Morin transitions occurring at 241 K and 238 K, respectively. Field-cooled warming/cooling measurements indicated the presence of thermal hysteresis, the magnitude of which increased with decreasing particle isotropy and size largest for nanoplates, followed by rhombohedral particles, and smallest for irregular morphologies. Additionally, the magnetite reference sample exhibited the characteristic Verse transition at 106 K, confirming its phase identity.

Limitations of the work and future perspectives

This investigation was primarily a comparative study of structural and magnetic features of α-Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles of various morphologies prepared by hydrothermal synthesis. Nevertheless, few limitations should be considered. The first one is that in order to obtain three different nanoparticle morphologies, quite different hydrothermal synthesis conditions were used, for example, different precursors, additives, solvent compositions, temperatures, and reaction times. As a result, although there is a strong correlation between morphology and magnetic behavior, the effect of an individual synthesis parameter cannot be entirely determined. So, it will be better if in future experiments, a single-variable experimental design is used to determine the effect of particle size, morphology, precursor chemistry, pH, capping agent, and reaction time on magnetic properties.

Secondly, the structural characterization mainly relied on XRD, TEM, HRTEM, and FFT observations. Even though these techniques have shown that crystalline α-Fe₂O₃ nanoparticles are formed, a higher level of crystallographic analysis could provide a lot more information. For instance, Rietveld refinement with powder samples that are randomly oriented, preferably those contained in a capillary or back-loaded holder, should be considered in future work to get quantitative phase fractions, lattice parameters, crystallite size, microstrain, and perhaps orientation effects due to the preferred- orientation. Spectroscopic techniques that are more specific to the surface, like X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy or Mössbauer spectroscopy, will be very useful in determining the influence of the surface oxidation state, spin disorder, and maybe the magnetic contributions caused by defects.

Third, the magnetic analysis was confined to SQUID magnetometry at a few measurement conditions. The work presented here was primarily based on M–H curves at 300 K along with ZFC/FC measurements from 10 to 350 K at 50 Oe. Probably more detailed temperature-dependent M–H and field-dependent ZFC/FC measurements, repeated thermal cycling, and frequency-dependent AC susceptibility should be done later on to effectively differentiate weak ferromagnetism, superparamagnetic relaxation, surface-spin freezing, and interparticle interaction effects.

Lastly, the nanoparticles were enclosed in a resin matrix during SQUID measurements. Even though this method ensured mechanical stability and lessened particle aggregation, at the same time, it could have limited interparticle magnetic interactions and changed the saturation magnetization obtained. In the future, similar investigations should be made that check the results of the resin-embedded samples against those obtained with pressed powder, dispersed powder, and magnetically diluted samples to figure out more clearly the influences of sample preparation on the measured magnetic response. In general, research in future should be targeted to linking controlled synthesis, quantitative structural characterization, surface/defect analysis together with extending magnetic measurements in order to get a much clearer picture of the connection between the α-Fe₂O₃ nanoparticle morphology, surface structure, and the Morin transition ‍‌‍‍‌‍‌‍‍‌behavior.
