Phenotypic heterogeneity in carriers of a pathogenic MSH2 variant: implications for the diagnosis of Lynch syndrome
Authors: Tuna Apuhan, Oguzhan Demir, Zeynep Sagnak Yilmaz, Elanur Karaman, Ayberk Turkyilmaz, Alper Han Cebi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Lynch syndrome is an inherited cancer predisposition syndrome caused by germline pathogenic variants in mismatch repair (MMR) genes and is primarily associated with colorectal and endometrial cancers. Classically, Lynch syndrome-associated tumors exhibit microsatellite instability (MSI) and loss of MMR protein expression on immunohistochemistry (IHC); therefore, MSI and IHC are routinely used in screening algorithms to support diagnostic evaluation. ; Methods: In this study, we present the molecular and pathological characteristics of four cases from three unrelated families carrying the same MSH2 variant [c.70C > T (p.Gln24Ter)], interpreted as pathogenic, who were diagnosed with breast and colorectal cancer. MSI analysis and MMR protein expression were evaluated in available tumor tissues. ; Results: Preserved MMR protein expression was observed in three cases, and two cases were microsatellite stable (MSS), whereas only one case demonstrated loss of MMR protein expression and an MSI-high phenotype consistent with the classical Lynch syndrome profile. These findings suggest that individuals carrying the same MSH2 variant may exhibit heterogeneous tumor-level MMR/MSI phenotypes. Early truncating variants in MSH2 may allow partially functional protein production via alternative translation initiation, potentially limiting complete loss of MMR function. Variability in somatic second-hit mechanisms and tumor-specific molecular pathways may also contribute to this heterogeneity. ; Conclusion: In conclusion, Lynch syndrome tumor biology may be more heterogeneous than expected, and MSI and IHC should not be interpreted in isolation as exclusionary tests for an underlying germline MMR variant, but rather as markers of tumor-level biological consequences. Therefore, germline findings, tumor characteristics, and family history should be evaluated together in Lynch syndrome diagnosis and risk assessment.

Introduction

Mismatch repair (MMR) genes take role in many basic cellular processes such as fixing the errors that occur during DNA synthesis, repair of double stranded DNA breaks, control of the anti-recombination processes and maintenance of DNA stability. Germline heterozygous pathogenic variants in the MMR genes MSH2, MLH1, MSH6 or PMS2, which are involved in this pathway, lead to Lynch Syndrome (Peltomäki 2003; Lynch et al. 2009). Lynch syndrome is characterized by an increased risk of various cancers, primarily colon and endometrial cancer, but also ovarian, stomach, urinary system, brain, skin, and other cancers (Lynch et al. 2015; Mao et al. 2021).

Lynch syndrome is generally recognized as a condition characterized by the co-occurrence of associated cancers with microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H). For individuals with colorectal cancer or endometrial cancer, MSI testing and/or IHC for MMR proteins is universally recommended for Lynch syndrome screening; additional testing criteria may also be applied to identify individuals at risk. (Mao et al. 2021; National Comprehensive Cancer Network 2025, v1.2025]. According to the 2021 ACMG (American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics) technical standard, the estimated sensitivity and specificity of PCR-based MSI testing for detecting Lynch syndrome are 85% and 90.2%, respectively, with a reported false-negative rate of 5–15% (Mao et al. 2021). For MMR IHC in colorectal cancer, sensitivity and specificity have been reported as 92–94% and 88–100%, respectively, with a false-negative rate of 5–10% (Mao et al. 2021). However, the diagnostic performance of tumor-based screening is not absolute. In a systematic review evaluating PCR-based MSI testing for the detection of Lynch syndrome in patients with colorectal cancer, sensitivity ranged from 67 to 100% when MSI-low results were considered negative, while specificity ranged from 61 to 93% in studies reporting both sensitivity and specificity (Coelho et al. 2017). This variability was attributed to differences in MSI marker panels, positivity thresholds, study populations, and reference standard testing (Coelho et al. 2017). Similarly, an Association for Molecular Pathology report showed that MSI-high and MMR IHC do not have 100% sensitivity for detecting germline MMR gene mutations and emphasized their complementary roles in detecting MMR-defective colorectal carcinomas (Funkhouser et al. 2012). In current tumor-based screening algorithms, MSI-high status and/or loss of MMR protein expression are considered indicators of possible Lynch syndrome, whereas tumors with microsatellite stable (MSS) or MSI-low status and retained MMR protein expression are generally categorized as “probably not Lynch syndrome” according to the 2021 ACMG technical standard (Mao et al. 2021). Lynch syndrome is diagnosed by molecular tests that are directed to detect pathogenic germline variants in MMR genes and EPCAM deletion (Bonadona et al. 2011). Positive test results are considered diagnostic (Mao et al. 2021).

This study aims to present the IHC and MSI analysis results of four cases diagnosed with breast cancer and colon cancer from three different families who have the same germline pathogenic variant in the MSH2 gene.

Material and method

All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Genomic DNA was isolated from peripheral blood using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini QIAcube Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) as per the manufacturer's instructions. Genomic DNA was fragmented to 400 bp using QIAseq® FX DNA Library Kits (Qiagen). After fragmentation, the libraries were prepared using the QIAseq® custom made Hereditary Cancer Panel that contains 61 genes (AIP, APC, ATM, ATR, AXIN2, BAP1, BARD1, BLM, BMPR1A, BRCA1, BRCA2, BRIP1, BUB1B, CDH1, CDK4, CDKN2A, CHEK2, CTNNA1, EPCAM, FAM175A, FANCC, FLCN, GALNT12, GEN1, GPC3, GREM1, HOXB13, MEN1, MET, MLH1, MRE11A, MSH2, MSH6, MUTYH, NBN, NTHL1, PALB2, PALLD, PIK3CA, PMS1, PMS2, POLD1, POLE, PRSS1, PTCH1, PTEN, RAD50, RAD51B, RAD51C, RAD51D, RET, RINT1, SDHB, SDHC, SDHD, SMAD4, SMARCA4, STK11, TP53, VHL, XRCC2) which are associated with cancer predisposition syndromes as per the manufacturer's instructions (Qiagen). Paired-end sequencing (150 bp) was performed on the NovaSeq6000 system according to the manufacturer's guidelines (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). The sequencing data of probands was analyzed QIAGEN Clinical Insight (QCI) Interpret bioinformatic platform. Patients’ relatives were tested through sequencing of the locus which the pathogenic variation is located at in probands.

To evaluate the pathogenicity of the variants, in silico prediction tools (AlphaMissense, Revel, GERP, CADD, PolyPhen2, and Mutation Taster), allele frequencies in population studies (1000 Genomes, Genome Aggregation Database, and Exome Aggregation Consortium), Franklin genoox (https://franklin.genoox.com/clinical-db/home), HGMD (https://www.hgmd.cf.ac.uk/ac/index.php), Clinvar (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/) and the ACMG criteria were used (Richards et al., 2015).

Patient with breast cancer is also investigated for copy number variations. Multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA) technique is used to detect copy number variations in BRCA1 and BRCA2. The commercial BRCA1 kit P002 and BRCA2 kit P045 (MRC Holland, Amsterdam, The Netherlands) were used per manufacturer’s instructions. The results were analyzed using Coffalyser software (MRC‐Holland, Amsterdam, Netherlands). Patients’ relative copy number (RCN) values of BRCA1/BRCA2 genes’ exons were measured. RCN values between 0.8–1.2 are considered as normal and values under 0.6 and above 1.3 are considered as deletion and duplication respectively.

MSI is investigated with NGS. Custom made MSI kit (Qiagen) targeting 177 locuses was used for molecular investigation (Details of the method could be shared upon request).

MMR protein expression for MLH1, MSH2, MSH6 and PMS2 proteins evaluated with immunohistochemistry. For IHC investigation, a block with sufficient tumor tissue was selected for each patient. Two sections of 4–5 µm thickness were taken from the selected blocks onto lysine slides, and MSH2 (Clone: ​​G219-1129, Ventana Medical System, Roche) immunohistochemical antibody was applied to the obtained sections on the Ventana Benchmark automatic staining device. Nuclear staining with MSH2 was evaluated in tumor cells. MSH2 staining in less than ≤ 5% tumor cells was considered as loss of nuclear expression.

Case 2

Case 2 is son of Case 1, and he was diagnosed with colon cancer at the age of 43 (Fig. 1a). Histopathological examination of the mass found in the cecum was consistent with low-grade mucinous adenocarcinoma. Molecular analysis revealed heterozygous NM_000251.3: c.70C > T (p.Gln24Ter) variant in the MSH2 gene (Table 1). Immunohistochemical examination of the tumor tissue at the time of diagnosis showed loss of MSH2 and MSH6 expression (Fig. 2b). In the extended 177-marker MSI analysis, 67 markers were stable and 110 markers were unstable and thus were evaluated as MSI-high.

Case 3

A 60-year-old patient diagnosed with colon cancer was referred from the Medical Oncology outpatient clinic for genetic evaluation. Family history revealed a history of colon and stomach malignancies, as well as multiple myeloma and lymphoma, in first, second, and third-degree relatives (Fig. 1b).

Histopathological examination of the mass of patient detected at the age of 60 in the sigmoid colon was consistent with mucinous adenocarcinoma. During follow-up, the recurrence of colon cancer was detected at age 62; histopathological examination of the recurrent lesion was reported as adenocarcinoma containing 30–40% mucinous component (Table 1). MMR IHC was performed on both tumor tissues and showed preserved nuclear expression of MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, and PMS2 in both tumors. MSI analysis was available for the second colorectal tumor. Extended 177-marker MSI analysis revealed 175 stable and 2 unstable markers, and the tumor was classified as MSS. The IHC image shown in Fig. 2c corresponds to the second colorectal tumor. Hereditary cancer panel analysis revealed a heterozygous NM_000251.3:c.70C > T (p.Gln24Ter) variant in the MSH2 gene.

Case 4

The patient, who had been diagnosed with colon cancer at the age of 57 and chronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML) at the age of 65, was referred from the Hematology-Oncology outpatient clinic for genetic evaluation. Also his maternal uncle had colon cancer at the age of 80 (Fig. 1c).

Histopathologic evaluation of colonic mass detected at the age of 57 was consistent with adenocarcinoma. At the time of diagnosis, MMR protein expression was evaluated by IHC, which showed preserved nuclear expression of MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, and PMS2 in tumor cells; MSI analysis was not available (Table 1).

After the development of thrombocytopenia during follow-up, a bone marrow biopsy was performed; and an increase in myeloid series cells was observed. Patient was diagnosed with proliferative type CMML. After diagnosis, myeloid NGS panel performed on peripheral blood-derived DNA detected the MSH2 variant NM_000251.3:c.70C > T (p.Gln24Ter) with a variant allele fraction of 48%. To assess the persistence of this finding, targeted analysis of the same locus was repeated approximately one year later using an independent peripheral blood sample, again demonstrating the variant in a heterozygous state. No tumor sequencing was performed; therefore, somatic second-hit status could not be assessed.

Discussion

This study aims to present the heterogeneity observed in terms of tumor pathology among four cases carrying the same germline pathogenic variant in the MSH2 gene. The preservation of MMR protein expression in the tumor tissue of three cases presents a different tumor profile than expected for classic Lynch syndrome. In contrast, the observation of MMR protein expression loss and high MSI in one case carrying the same variant suggests that the functional consequences of the detected germline variant may differ at the tumor level. These findings indicate that different pathogenic mechanisms may contribute to tumor development in carriers of the same MSH2 variant.

In the classic definition of Lynch syndrome, it is expected that patients carrying a germline pathogenic MMR gene variant will exhibit loss of function in MMR proteins and MSI in their tumor tissue. However, emerging data suggest that this paradigm is not absolute, as germline MMR variant carriers may develop tumors in which MMR protein expression is retained and MSI is not detected, indicating biological heterogeneity beyond the classical model (Farrington et al. 1998; Barnetson et al. 2006; DeRycke et al. 2017; Khandakar et al. 2025; Dardenne et al. 2025). A similar situation can be observed in tumors outside the Lynch syndrome spectrum. A study examining breast cancer patients carrying a pathogenic MMR gene variant showed that MMR expression was preserved in some patients, and this study reveals that the phenotypic manifestations of germline MMR variants may vary depending on the tumor type, additional somatic events, and molecular context (Hu et al. 2022).

In Lynch syndrome, MSI occurs when errors in repeat sequences of DNA during replication can not be corrected by the MMR system. Although early truncating variants affecting the N-terminal region of the MSH2 gene have been associated with classic Lynch syndrome and complete loss of function, studies show that this assumption is not always valid, especially for changes near the start codon or early amino acids (Farrington et al. 1998; Kets et al. 2009; Cyr et al. 2012). In the study by Farrington et al., it was reported that in the presence of the c.1A > T (p.Met1Leu) variant affecting the MSH2 start codon, an alternative Met26 translation start codon was used, and this may be associated with partial conservation of function. In the case of colorectal cancer mentioned in the study, it was reported that the tumor tissue showed a MSS pattern and MMR protein expression was preserved (Farrington et al. 1998). The study by Kets et al. provides important insight into the biological consequences of MSH2 variants affecting the initiation codon. In that report, the affected individuals were compound heterozygous: one allele harbored a pathogenic deletion of the first six exons of MSH2, while the other allele carried the initiation codon variant c.1A > G (p.Met1?). Thus, one allele was clearly non-functional due to a truncating deletion, whereas the second allele was not completely null but capable of producing an altered MSH2 protein. Immunohistochemical analyses demonstrated weak but preserved MSH2 staining in tumor tissue, suggesting that the c.1A > G variant did not abolish protein production. The authors proposed that translation may initiate from a downstream in-frame start codon (approximately 26 codons downstream), resulting in an N-terminally shortened MSH2 protein with residual MMR activity. Therefore, tumorigenesis in these patients likely reflected the combined effect of a null allele and a partially functional allele. In contrast, the individuals in our study carry a monoallelic germline MSH2 c.70C > T (p.Gln24Ter) variant. Unlike the compound heterozygous configuration described by Kets et al., only one allele is affected in our cases. The preserved MMR protein expression and microsatellite stability observed in several of our tumors raise the possibility that alternative translation initiation or residual protein activity from the truncated allele may mitigate complete loss of function (Kets et al. 2009). This mechanism has also been supported in the functional studies of Cyr et al.; it has been shown that variants affecting the MSH2 start codon lead to a partial defect instead of a complete loss of function in the MSH2-MSH6 complex, which can cause it to appear in a MSS pattern in tumors (Cyr et al. 2012).

The possibility of retained MMR protein expression despite pathogenic MMR alterations is also supported by the findings of Chen et al. In a study by Chen et al., 16 out of 82 MMR-deficient patients (including 56 Lynch syndrome and 26 double somatic MMR mutation cases) demonstrated unexpected MMR protein expression. Although the majority of these cases harbored missense variants, 5 out of 47 patients with truncating mutations also exhibited MMR expression. These 5 cases involved four distinct variants in MLH1, PMS2 and MSH6. The MLH1 variant in this study was located in the last exon of the gene and was not expected to cause a loss of more than 10% of the transcript size. Likewise, one MSH6 variant, observed in two patients, was located near the C-terminus; although not in the last exon, and it was also predicted to retain over 90% of the transcript size. Another variant involved the deletion of the promoter region and the first exon of MSH6, which was detected via CNV analysis of NGS data (Chen et al. 2020). These findings indicate that retained MMR protein expression may occasionally be observed even in tumors harboring truncating MMR variants, supporting cautious interpretation of preserved IHC staining in the appropriate germline and clinical context.

In the cases presented in our study, heterogeneity in tumor biology was observed among the four cases carrying the MSH2 c.70C > T (p.Gln24Ter) variant. The preservation of MMR protein expression in tumor tissue in three cases (Cases 1, 3, and 4) is compatible with the possibility of alternative translation initiation and partial preservation of MSH2 protein function, as described in the literature; however, other tumor-specific mechanisms cannot be excluded. In contrast, the detection of MSH2/MSH6 protein loss and MSI-high phenotype in only one case (Case 2) carrying the same germline variant suggests that a somatic second hit may have occurred in this case and that the mutator phenotype may have been fully established at the tumor level.

Case 4 should be interpreted with particular caution, as the MSH2 variant was detected in peripheral blood-derived DNA in the setting of CMML and was reproduced in an independent peripheral blood sample approximately one year later. Although the initial variant allele fraction was close to 50% and the persistence of the variant at a heterozygous level over time supports a possible germline origin, this cannot be confirmed without testing non-hematopoietic tissue. Therefore, a hematopoietic clonal origin cannot be completely excluded. Tumor sequencing and somatic second-hit analyses were also not performed, precluding assessment of somatic inactivation of the MSH2 wild-type allele.

These findings suggest that tumor development in individuals carrying the same germline MSH2 variant may follow different biological trajectories. In some tumors, MMR deficiency may become fully established through somatic inactivation of the remaining functional allele, whereas in others, MMR protein expression may remain preserved and tumorigenesis may proceed through non-MMR or tumor-specific carcinogenesis mechanisms. Therefore, MSI and IHC should not be considered in isolation as tests that exclude the possibility of an underlying germline MMR variant, but rather as tumor-level biomarkers reflecting the biological consequences of MMR deficiency in a given tumor.

Although this variant was classified as pathogenic according to ACMG/AMP criteria in the present study, it should be noted that ClinVar includes submissions with different interpretations, including pathogenic, likely pathogenic, and variants of uncertain significance. Given the absence of tumor sequencing and somatic second-hit analyses, the possibility that some MMR-proficient/MSS tumors developed sporadically cannot be completely excluded.

This molecular and phenotypic heterogeneity suggests that the traditional diagnostic criteria used in the clinical definition of Lynch syndrome might be insufficient in some patient groups. The Amsterdam II and Revised Bethesda criteria, that are used to identify individuals/families at high risk for Lynch syndrome, were developed to guide clinically based screening. However, it is emphasized in the current literature that these criteria are not designed to exclude germline MMR pathogenic variant carriage. It has been reported that the sensitivity of the Amsterdam criteria is limited to approximately 60%, and although this rate has increased with the Amsterdam II revision, it does not cover all carriers (Mao et al. 2021).

In our case series, only Case 2 exhibited a high-priority profile within the scope of these criteria; the other cases, despite carrying a pathogenic MSH2 variant at the germline level, represented a low-priority or screening-excluded group in terms of family history and tumor biology, demonstrating that screening strategies based solely on clinical criteria can lead to delayed or missed diagnosis (Barnetson et al. 2006; Khandakar et al. 2025). Our findings support the conclusion that while the Amsterdam II and Revised Bethesda criteria are valuable for screening and awareness, they are insufficient tools for excluding germline MMR testing.

In conclusion, these findings reveal that the diagnosis and risk assessment of Lynch syndrome cannot be reduced to a single biomarker or clinical parameter; germline molecular findings, tumor biology, and family history should be evaluated together and holistically.
