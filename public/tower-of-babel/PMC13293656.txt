Connexin 50 mediates disease-relevant alpha-synuclein oligomer propagation and neuroinflammation in neurodegenerative disease
Authors: Nicha Puangmalai, Urmi Sengupta, Nemil Bhatt, Nopparat Suthprasertporn, Fadhl Al-Shaebi, Nikita Shchankin, Jia Yi Liew, Twisha Chatterjee, Dhvani Pansuriya, Ahmet E Balci, Abbigael Aday, Silvana Valdebenito-Silva, Rakez Kayed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryConnexins, fundamental components of gap junctions and hemichannels, regulate intercellular communication and are emerging neurodegeneration regulators. Primary synucleinopathies and co-morbid synuclein pathologies feature pathological α-synuclein (α-Syn) aggregation, yet mechanisms driving pathogenic α-Syn propagation remain unclear. We identify that connexin 50 (Cx50) interacts with α-Syn aggregates in synucleinopathy-affected human brain tissue. Ex vivo dye uptake assays show markedly elevated hemichannel activity in synucleinopathy mouse brain tissue versus wild-type controls, suppressed by selective Cx50 inhibition. Cx50-expressing cell models exhibit strain-dependent brain-derived α-Syn oligomers (BDSOs) uptake, confirmed pharmacologically. In primary neuron-astrocyte co-cultures from mice expressing human wild-type α-Syn, Cx50 knockdown markedly reduced BDSO uptake and α-Syn aggregation. Cx50 knockdown differentially modulates pro-inflammatory cytokines in BDSO-treated conditions, indicating strain-dependent effects and Cx50-mediated neuron-astrocyte crosstalk in regulating neuroinflammation. This identifies Cx50 as a plausible target for modulating initiation and early spread of α-Syn pathology, supporting Cx50-directed interventions for early-stage disease modification.Subject areas: molecular biology, neuroscience

Introduction

Synucleinopathies are progressive neurodegenerative disorders characterized by pathological accumulation and spreading of misfolded α-synuclein (α-Syn).1 Emerging evidence supports a prion-like propagation model in which pathological α-Syn adopts distinct conformational strains that template endogenous α-Syn monomers, triggering conformational templating and self-perpetuating aggregation.2,3,4,5 These disease-specific α-Syn strains exhibit strain-specific neuronal tropism and progression patterns that recapitulate the Braak staging model of disease propagation. Within the brain, α-Syn pathology initiates in the medulla oblongata and brainstem, progressing rostrally to the midbrain, hippocampus, and neocortex—a pattern indicating that anatomical connectivity and regional vulnerability drive disease progression.6,7 Critically, this stereotypical spreading follows interconnected neural networks, implying the existence of defined molecular pathways that facilitate intercellular α-Syn transmission.8

Although the intercellular protein aggregates transfer is increasingly well-documented, the molecular mechanisms underlying this propagation remain incompletely understood. Gap junctions—intercellular channels formed by connexin (Cx) proteins—enable direct cytoplasmic continuity and facilitate bidirectional exchange of ions, metabolites, and signaling molecules between adjacent cells.9 Hemichannels, composed of a single connexon (six Cx subunits), open to the extracellular space and serve as additional conduits for molecular transfer, particularly under pathological stress conditions.9,10,11 Recent evidence demonstrates that aggregated proteins, including amyloid-β and tau, exploit gap junction and hemichannel pathways for intercellular propagation9,12; however, the roles of individual Cx isoforms in α-Syn pathology remain poorly characterized. Additionally, tunneling nanotubes—actin-rich membrane bridges connecting distant cells—and exosome-mediated transfer contribute to α-Syn oligomer dissemination.13,14,15 Collectively, these mechanisms underscore the critical role of intercellular communication in protein pathology spread.

Among brain-expressed connexins, Cx43 (the predominant astrocytic Cx) and Cx36 (the predominant neuronal Cx) have been extensively characterized in neurodegeneration.16,17,18,19,20 Pathological α-Syn enhances Cx43 hemichannel opening in astrocytes, triggering excessive release of gliotransmitters (ATP and glutamate) and pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6.21 This aberrant Cx43-mediated intercellular communication promotes astrocytic activation and a pro-inflammatory milieu that exacerbates neuronal death.21,22 Critically, selective Cx43 hemichannel inhibition provides neuroprotection in models of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and other neurodegenerative diseases.16,23 By contrast, the involvement of other Cx isoforms, particularly connexin 50 (Cx50) (encoded by GJA8), in α-Syn pathology has been largely unexplored. Cx50 is classically characterized as a lens Cx, essential for lens transparency and barrier function.24,25,26 However, Cx50 has been observed at the plasma membrane, cytoplasm, and nucleus of neuronal and glial cells.27,28 Emerging evidence reports that Cx50 is expressed in multiple brain regions, including the hippocampal formation, limbic structures, prefrontal cortex, and medulla oblongata.29,30,31 Given that the medulla oblongata represents an early epicenter of α-Syn aggregation and pathological spread in synucleinopathies,32 this neuroanatomical distribution suggests that Cx50 may facilitate intercellular communication and α-Syn propagation in disease. Structurally, Cx50 and Cx43 belong to the same α-Cx subfamily and share the canonical Cx architecture where four transmembrane helices, two extracellular loops bearing conserved cysteine residues, and cytoplasmic N- and C-termini confer their capacity to oligomerize into hemichannels and gap junction channels.33 This shared structural scaffold, together with conserved gating regulatory motifs across the α-Cx family, implies that Cx50 and Cx43 may engage overlapping molecular mechanisms during pathological hemichannel activation, providing a structural basis for investigating Cx50 as a functionally relevant paralog of Cx43 in the context of α-Syn-driven neurodegeneration.11,34 Furthermore, Cx50 forms functional hemichannels at non-junctional membranes capable of opening under pathological stress conditions,34 a property shared with the disease-relevant Cx43 hemichannels implicated in neurodegeneration.16,21 Notably, the closely related Cx32 has been shown to directly bind α-Syn oligomers and facilitate their uptake and intercellular transfer in neurons and oligodendrocytes,35 establishing a precedent for Cx-mediated propagation of α-Syn within the broader Cx protein family. Additionally, genetic association studies have linked GJA8 (Cx50) to schizophrenia,31 further supporting a functional role for Cx50 in CNS pathophysiology beyond the lens. Taken together, the neuroanatomical distribution of Cx50 in disease-vulnerable brain regions, its capacity to form hemichannels, and the precedent set by related Cx isoforms in mediated protein aggregate propagation collectively motivated our investigation of Cx50 as a candidate mediator of α-Syn oligomer uptake and spread in synucleinopathies.

We hypothesize that Cx50 facilitates α-Syn oligomer uptake and intercellular propagation, thereby driving pathology spread and dysregulating neuron-astrocyte crosstalk. To test this hypothesis, we: (1) determined Cx50 expression patterns in human brains with primary synucleinopathies and co-morbid synuclein pathology (DLB, PD, and AD) using biochemical and immunological approaches; (2) validated Cx50 expression in transgenic mouse models of synucleinopathy; (3) characterized Cx50 hemichannel function using ex vivo dye uptake assays; (4) assessed Cx50-mediated uptake and trafficking of disease-relevant α-Syn oligomers in cell-based models; and (5) evaluated Cx50’s role in neuron-astrocyte crosstalk and neuroinflammatory responses using primary neuron-astrocyte co-cultures with selective Cx50 knockdown. Collectively, the genetic and pharmacological results identify Cx50 hemichannels as a plausible target for modulating the initiation and early spread of α-Syn pathology, positioning Cx50-directed interventions as potential candidates for early-stage disease modification.

Results

Cx50 interacts with α-synuclein aggregates in human brain tissues and synucleinopathy mouse models

To determine whether Cx50 is differentially expressed in human brains with α-Syn pathology, we analyzed brain tissues from patients with AD, DLB, and PD and age-matched controls using western blot (WB), co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP), immunohistochemistry (IHC), and immunofluorescence (IF). WB analysis of PBS-soluble brain fractions detected Cx50 and Cx43 in synucleinopathy and age-matched healthy control brains (Figure 1A). To assess preferential interactions between connexins and pathological α-Syn, we performed co-IP using the 4D6 antibody (specific for α-Syn) followed by WB analysis with Cx50 and Cx43 antibodies. co-IP of 4D6-positive materials revealed Cx50 co-precipitated with α-Syn oligomers in DLB and PD compared to controls, indicating that Cx50 directly associates with pathological α-Syn oligomers (≥50 kDa) (Figures 1B and 1C). Similarly, Cx43 was also detected in the co-IP 4D6-positive materials (≥43 kDa) in the DLB and PD compared to controls (Figures 1B and 1D).

IHC analysis revealed distinct Cx50 expression patterns across the three diseases (Figure 1E). IF analysis with cell-type markers demonstrated that Cx50 colocalized with both neuronal (NeuN) (Figures 1F and 1J) and astrocytic (GFAP) markers (Figures 1G and 1K). Quantitative fluorescence intensity analysis revealed significantly reduced Cx50 intensity in DLB brains and elevated intensity in PD brains compared to controls (Figure 1I). Notably, Pearson’s correlation coefficient (PCC) analysis revealed that Cx50 expression significantly correlated with pS129 α-Syn burden in AD, DLB, and PD pathologies, suggesting disease-specific modulation of Cx50 in response to α-Syn pathology (Figures 1H and 1L).

To confirm these findings in disease models, we examined Cx50 expression in transgenic mice expressing human wild-type (WT) α-synuclein (hSyn) or human A53T-mutant α-synuclein (A53T), and in WT controls. WB analysis of PBS-soluble brain homogenates revealed age-dependent Cx50 expression in A53T mice, with a significant positive correlation between Cx50 levels and aggregated α-Syn (Figures 2A–2C). Immunohistochemical analysis of midbrain (Figures 2D and 2F) and hippocampal regions (Figures 2E and 2G)—key sites of α-Syn pathology—showed significantly increased Cx50-positive areas in hSyn and A53T mice compared to WT controls, with age-dependent expression patterns in A53T hippocampus. In hSyn mice, age-dependent expression of Cx50 was also observed in the substantia nigra and medulla, which are brain regions of early stages of pathology (Figure S1). IF and confocal image analysis revealed colocalization of Cx50 with both neurons (Figures 2H–2J) and astrocytes (Figures 2H, 2I, and 2K) in hippocampal CA3. The colocalization of Cx50 and pS129 α-Syn aggregates was also observed diversely in hippocampus, midbrain, substantia nigra, and medulla of these mouse models (Figure S2).

Cx50 inhibition diminishes hemichannel activity in the synucleinopathy mouse brain

To assess Cx hemichannel function in the synucleinopathy mouse model, we performed ex vivo ethidium bromide (Etd) uptake assays in acute brain slices from 8-month-old (mo) hSyn and WT mice. Etd uptake was significantly elevated in hSyn brain tissue compared to WT controls (Figures 3A and 3B), indicating enhanced hemichannel activity under pathological conditions. To determine Cx subtype specificity, we applied a selective pharmacological inhibitor. The broad-spectrum hemichannel inhibitor probenecid completely abolished elevated Etd uptake in hSyn tissue (Figures 3A and 3B), confirming hemichannel-dependent uptake. Application of the Cx50-specific blocking peptide (CVNTQQPGCENVCY, Thermo Fisher Scientific, K4328-1) substantially reduced Etd uptake levels in hSyn tissue to WT (Figures 3C and 3D), with minimal effect on WT tissue. The scrambled Cx50 control peptide (VYVCGCQNPCE, Thermo Fisher, K4328-2) failed to suppress uptake, confirming Cx50 specificity. Cx43-specific (VCYDKSFPISHVR, Thermo Fisher Scientific, K4328-3) and Cx36-specific (CNQACYDRAFPISH, Thermo Fisher Scientific, K4313-2) blocking peptides also produced significant attenuation of elevated Etd uptake compared to control brains (Figures 3E–3G), indicating Cx50 hemichannels are one of the mediators of dye uptake. We confirmed the Cx gene expression levels by quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis of cortical and hippocampal tissues, which revealed upregulated Cx50 (Gja8, GenBank: NM_008123.3) mRNA in hSyn mice compared to age-matched WT, while Cx43 (Gja1, GenBank: NM_010288.4) and Cx36 (Gjd2, GenBank: NM_010290.3) mRNA remained unchanged (Figures S3A–S3C). IF analysis showed enhanced Cx50 expression in microglia of hSyn brain tissue compared to WT (Figures S3D and S3E), indicating aberrant Cx50 activation across multiple cell types.

Brain-derived α-synuclein oligomers exhibit distinct biochemical and functional properties

Brain-derived α-synuclein oligomers (BDSOs) were isolated from cortices of AD, PD, and DLB patient tissues via immunoprecipitation with 4D6 antibody and characterized as previously established.36,37 BDSOs were separated by size exclusion chromatography (Figure S4A) and characterized via biochemical and biophysical methods. Proteinase K (PK) digestion analysis revealed distinct fragmentation patterns between BDSOs from AD (SynO-AD), DLB (SynO-DLB), and PD (SynO-PD) (Figures S4B–S4F), indicating three biochemically distinct oligomeric strains comparable to prion strain heterogeneity. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) confirmed spherical structures with distinct height profiles for each BDSO variant (Figures S4G–S4L). Fluorescence binding assays using bis-ANS revealed significantly increased hydrophobic surface exposure for all BDSOs, comparable to recombinant oligomers (Figure S4M), whereas Thioflavin T binding remained minimal (Figure S4N), consistent with oligomeric rather than fibrillar structures.

To assess biological activity, BDSOs were applied to primary cortical neurons from hSyn transgenic (Figures S5A and S5F) or WT (Figures S5B–S5E and S5G) mice. IF analysis revealed disease-specific intracellular accumulation patterns and pS129 α-Syn aggregate formation: SynO-AD preferentially accumulated in the nucleus; SynO-DLB in the soma; and SynO-PD predominantly in the soma with minimal nuclear uptake, suggesting differential cellular trafficking mechanisms among disease-derived oligomers.

Cx50 facilitates disease-relevant α-synuclein oligomer uptake

To determine whether connexins facilitate BDSO uptake, human neuroblastoma cells were transfected with Cx50-, Cx43-, or eGFP control expression plasmids and exposed to biotin-labeled BDSOs at a sublethal condition of 0.25 μM for 8 h38 (Figure 4A). WB analysis revealed differential BDSO uptake: highest monomeric α-Syn (∼15 kDa) uptake occurred with SynO-PD in all Cx-expressing cells compared to control cells (Figures 4B and 4C). In contrast, SynO-DLB accumulated as higher molecular weight aggregates (>37 kDa) in Cx50 cells, whereas SynO-PD aggregates accumulated in Cx43 cells (Figures 4B and 4C), indicating Cx-specific BDSO uptake patterns.

To detect if the formation of gap junctions is involved in BDSO uptake, cells were co-incubated with BDSOs and carbenoxolone (CBX, 25–100 μM), a broad-spectrum gap junction inhibitor. In Cx50 cells, CBX dose-dependently inhibited SynO-AD uptake and reduced SynO-PD uptake trends (Figures 4D and 4E). Interestingly, Cx50 exhibits reverse sensitivity to CBX application in the SynO-DLB-treated group with increased BDSO uptake (Figures 4D and 4E). Notably, Cx43 cells exhibited reverse sensitivity to CBX, with enhanced BDSO uptake at higher concentrations (Figures 4F and 4G), indicating functionally distinct roles for Cx50 versus Cx43 in α-Syn oligomer uptake.

Neuronal Cx50 knockdown attenuates α-synuclein oligomer propagation in neuron-astrocyte co-cultures

To determine whether neuronal Cx50 modulates α-Syn propagation in multicellular contexts, primary hSyn neurons were transfected with either non-targeting (siNT) or Cx50-targeting siRNA (siCx50), and co-cultured with fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)-sorted astrocytes (Figure 5A; Figure S7). Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assays confirmed no cytotoxicity caused by siRNA application to the co-culture systems (Figures S6A–S6D). Live-cell imaging with 0.25 μM AF568-labeled BDSOs revealed rapid cellular entry by 0.5 h, with SynO-PD showing the highest uptake kinetics in siNT cells (Figures 5B and 5C). Cx50 knockdown significantly reduced SynO-DLB and SynO-PD uptake compared to siNT controls (Figures 5B–5D). Interestingly, PCC analysis at the 2 h time point showed that SynO-PD significantly bound to neurons more than astrocytes, while SynO-AD and SynO-DLB did not show preferential binding to specific cell types (Figure 5E–5H). This inhibitory effect persisted for 24 h, particularly for SynO-PD as assessed by live-cell imaging of AF568-labeled BDSOs with the early endosome compartment (Figure S8A).

WB analysis of co-cultures after 8 h BDSO exposure revealed significantly elevated α-Syn monomers across all BDSOs in siNT cells compared to untreated controls (Figures 6A and 6B). Notably, Cx50 knockdown selectively reduced both monomeric and high molecular weight α-Syn aggregates specifically in SynO-PD-treated cells (Figures 6A–6C). Cx50 knockdown had no effect on SynO-AD or SynO-DLB aggregate accumulation (Figures 6A–6C). Interestingly, Cx50 knockdown coincided with upregulated Cx43 expression in SynO-PD-exposed cells, whereas Cx50 remained consistently reduced (Figures 6A–6E).

IF and PCC analysis of biotin-labeled BDSO localization revealed that Cx50 knockdown significantly reduced cellular BDSO accumulation for SynO-AD and SynO-PD (Figures 6F and 6G), with minimal effect on SynO-DLB. In siNT cells, SynO-AD and SynO-PD did not preferentially bind neurons or astrocytes, whereas SynO-DLB preferentially bound to astrocytes significantly (Figures 6H–6J). Notably, Cx50 knockdown did not alter these BDSO binding preferences (Figures 6H–6J).

Cx50-dependent modulation of neuroinflammatory responses to disease-relevant α-synuclein oligomers

Sandwich-ELISA analysis of cultured media revealed that all BDSOs significantly induced pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6) and the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 in siNT cells, with the highest levels following SynO-DLB treatment (Figures 6K–6N). Cx50 knockdown significantly suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokine production (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6) in response to SynO-DLB and SynO-PD (Figures 6K–M), while enhancing IL-10 production following SynO-AD and SynO-DLB exposure (Figure 6N). Cx50 knockdown had minimal effect on SynO-AD-induced cytokine responses. These findings indicate that neuronal Cx50 critically regulates neuron-astrocyte crosstalk during α-Syn pathology, with disease-specific modulation of neuroinflammatory responses.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that Cx50 is expressed in human synucleinopathy brain tissues, including DLB, PD, and AD, where it colocalizes with both neuronal and astrocytic compartments and directly interacts with pathological α-Syn aggregates. Our investigation of Cx50 in the context of synucleinopathies is motivated by several converging lines of evidence. Although Cx43 and Cx36 are associated with neurodegenerative processes,16,17,18,20,39 the contribution of Cx50 to α-Syn pathology has not been examined. Anatomically, the presence of Cx50 in neurons and glia across the CNS regions that are sequentially affected by α-Syn pathology, including the medulla oblongata, midbrain, hippocampus, and prefrontal cortex.27,29,32 From a biophysical perspective, Cx50 forms functional hemichannels in non-junctional membranes,34 structures that open under pathological stress conditions,11 thereby providing a plausible mechanism for the extracellular release and cellular uptake of oligomeric α-Syn. The established binding of α-Syn oligomers to the structurally related Cx32,35 and as co-members of the α-Cx subfamily, Cx4311,33,34 share extensive sequence homology with Cx50 within the Cx family, providing a direct mechanistic precedent for testing whether Cx50 is involved in the spreading for α-Syn. Additionally, Cx50 has been shown to modulate Cx43 channel function, where the cataract-associated Cx50-S50P mutant dominantly inhibits Cx43 both in vitro and in vivo,40 further suggesting a functional interplay between these isoforms that could amplify the neuroinflammatory Cx43 hemichannels during α-Syn pathology. Finally, independent psychiatric genetics have placed GJA8 on a short list of high-priority schizophrenia susceptibility genes supported by both CNV data and linkage studies,31 underscoring that Cx50 has functional CNS implications. Collectively, these considerations suggest that Cx50 carries functional implications well beyond its classical role in the lens and highlight the role of Cx50 in neurodegenerative diseases, particularly synucleinopathies, which have remained largely unexplored.

We first analyzed Cx50 expression levels in human cortical brain tissues, a region affected by α-Syn pathology, from patients with DLB, PD, and AD relative to age-matched controls. Cx50 was detected in all disease pathologies and control brains; however, IHC and IF analysis revealed distinct distribution patterns with reduced neuronal and astrocytic colocalization in pathological brains compared to controls. We hypothesize that enhanced Cx50 expression proximal to neurons and astrocytes may reflect an attempted reparative response to maintain neuronal survival under pathological stress, consistent with observations that Cx43 gap junction channels (but not hemichannels) promote neuroprotection in Huntington’s disease.35 Notably, despite reduced cellular colocalization, Cx50 demonstrated progressive interaction with α-Syn proteins, as determined by IF and co-IP, suggesting a preferential binding to aggregated species even as its spatial distribution relative to cell bodies diminishes. This may reflect α-Syn released from degenerating neurons in pathological brains,41,42 or sequential involvement of other connexins and non-neuronal cells—notably Cx32, which facilitates α-Syn oligomer uptake and intercellular transfer in neurons and oligodendrocytes.43 We further analyzed colocalization between Cx50 and pS129 α-Syn. IF analysis revealed significantly elevated colocalization of Cx50 and pS129 α-Syn in AD, DLB, and PD compared to age-matched controls, demonstrating that the interaction between Cx50 and pathological α-Syn deposits is a disease-associated phenomenon rather than a physiological feature. Although our analysis focused on cortical tissues, a major site of α-Syn pathology in AD and DLB, analysis of additional vulnerable brain regions, particularly the brainstem and midbrain, where synucleinopathies originate, would provide more comprehensive mechanistic insight.

We further investigated whether Cx50 expression associates with age and α-Syn pathology in transgenic mouse models expressing human WT or A53T-mutant α-Syn. These models recapitulate key features of human synucleinopathies by enabling the assessment of age-dependent α-Syn aggregation and pathological spread. Age-dependent Cx50 expression was significantly elevated in A53T mice while remaining unchanged in hSyn and WT controls. Importantly, a significant positive correlation between Cx50 levels and aggregated α-Syn (detected by MJFR14-6-4-2 antibody) was observed across pooled brain samples. In hSyn and A53T mice, midbrain and hippocampal regions—areas linked to α-Syn aggregation, pathological propagation, and cognitive deficits—showed age-dependent Cx50 upregulation with colocalization to both neurons and astrocytes. Moreover, these areas also revealed increased colocalization of Cx50 and pS129 α-Syn in hSyn and A53T mice relative to WT controls at both 4 and 8 months of age. Notably, correlation analysis further revealed that Cx50 intensity significantly correlated with pS129 α-Syn burden in hSyn and A53T mice, whereas no such correlation was observed in WT controls. These consistent observations across human and mouse models suggest that Cx50 expression is dynamically regulated in brain regions vulnerable to α-Syn pathology.

To determine whether Cx50 directly mediates α-Syn oligomer uptake, we characterized biochemically and biophysically distinct BDSOs from AD, DLB, and PD brains using gold-standard PK digestion and biophysical assessments.44,45,46 These BDSOs exhibit disease-strain-specific conformational and biological signatures, with distinct cellular accumulation patterns in primary neurons, reflecting differential trafficking mechanisms among disease-derived oligomer variants.

Using Cx-expressing cell models, Cx50-and Cx43-expressing cells demonstrated distinct preferential BDSO uptake profiles. While SynO-PD showed the highest monomeric α-Syn internalization across all Cx-expressing cell models, Cx50 cells preferentially accumulated SynO-DLB as high molecular weight aggregates, whereas Cx43 cells showed enhanced SynO-PD aggregate retention. CBX-mediated gap junction inhibition produced dose-dependent suppression of SynO-AD uptake specifically in Cx50 cells, suggesting that Cx50-containing gap junctions mediate SynO-AD uptake. However, CBX had minimal effects on SynO-DLB and SynO-PD uptake in both Cx50 and Cx43 cells, indicating that these disease variants utilize alternative Cx50-mediated hemichannel-dependent or other uptake mechanisms. Conversely, CBX failed to inhibit BDSO uptake in Cx43 cells across all variants, implying that functional Cx43-containing hemichannels rather than gap junctions mediate α-Syn oligomer entry. These data identify functionally distinct roles for Cx50 versus Cx43 in pathological α-Syn oligomer uptake.

To assess Cx50-mediated α-Syn propagation in physiologically relevant multicellular contexts, we utilized primary neuron-astrocyte co-cultures with selective neuronal Cx50 knockdown. Live-cell imaging revealed rapid BDSO uptake within 0.5 h, with SynO-PD demonstrating the highest uptake kinetics in control cells. Critically, neuronal Cx50 knockdown substantially inhibited SynO-PD uptake, an effect sustained through 24 h as confirmed by early endosomal compartment visualization. However, Cx50 knockdown selectively reduced both monomeric and high molecular weight α-Syn aggregates specifically following SynO-PD exposure, with minimal effects on SynO-AD or SynO-DLB, indicating PD-strain-specific dependence on neuronal Cx50 for oligomer accumulation. Notably, Cx50 knockdown coincided with compensatory upregulation of Cx43, consistent with functional interplay between Cx isoforms. These observations align with reported heteromeric and heterotypic Cx interactions, whereby altered Cx composition modulates channel permeability and electrophysiological properties.47,48,49 The cataract-associated Cx50-S50P mutant demonstrates dominant inhibition of Cx43 function both in vitro and in vivo, exemplifying how Cx50 can functionally regulate Cx43 channel properties.40 This bidirectional Cx interplay suggests that neuronal Cx50 may directly or indirectly influence astrocytic Cx43 hemichannel function during α-Syn pathology.

Cx50 knockdown significantly attenuated pro-inflammatory cytokine production (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6) in response to SynO-DLB and SynO-PD, while unexpectedly enhancing anti-inflammatory IL-10 production following SynO-AD and SynO-DLB exposure. This pro-inflammatory cytokine suppression and IL-10 enhancement following Cx50 knockdown is consistent with reduced BDSO-mediated astrocyte activation. However, IL-10 signaling exhibits pleiotropic and context-dependent effects in synucleinopathies. Although IL-10 is classically characterized as anti-inflammatory, recent evidence demonstrates that sustained IL-10 signaling reduces neuronal survival and exacerbates α-Syn accumulation in PD mouse models,50,51,52 suggesting that IL-10 can paradoxically promote disease progression in synucleinopathies. Our findings suggest that IL-10 responses are highly BDSO-variant-dependent, potentially reflecting distinct activation of immune mediators by different α-Syn strains. Thus, the complex roles of IL-10 in α-Syn pathology warrant further mechanistic investigation.

Collectively, our data suggest that Cx50 plays an important role as one of the pathological mediators for α-Syn oligomer uptake and intercellular propagation. The selective upregulation of Cx50 in PD and A53T transgenic brains, combined with hemichannel-dependent BDSO uptake and disease-strain-specific cellular responses, positions Cx50 as a key nexus between pathological α-Syn propagation and dysregulated neuron-astrocyte crosstalk. Neuronal Cx50 suppression substantially reduces BDSO accumulation and attenuates pro-inflammatory astrocyte responses in a disease-variant-dependent manner, particularly for PD-derived α-Syn oligomers. These findings support the hypothesis that selective Cx50 inhibition may represent a viable therapeutic strategy to impede α-Syn propagation while selectively restoring immune homeostasis in synucleinopathies. Our work identifies Cx50 hemichannels as a plausible target for modulating the initiation and early spread of α-Syn pathology, positioning Cx50-directed interventions as potential candidates for early-stage disease modification.

Limitations of this study

In this article, we identified Cx50 as a strain-specific mediator of α-Syn oligomer propagation, but the underlying mechanism of the reverse sensitivity of Cx50 is still understudied. This study focuses specifically on oligomeric forms of α-Syn, and further investigation of larger insoluble aggregates is warranted in the future. Future study can include cell-type specific analysis of Etd and peptide blockade and compensatory changes in other connexins may contribute to the observed effects, allowing us to resolve the relative contributions of neuronal versus astrocytic connexins, further adding mechanistic granularity by including: (1) rescue experiments with channel-mode–selective Cx mutants that differentially disrupt hemichannel versus gap junction function, (2) direct assessment of gap junctional coupling under our experimental conditions, and (3) systematic evaluation of alternative conduits such as pannexin channels and other transport pathways using complementary genetic and pharmacological approaches.

Method details

Preparation of brain extracts

Frozen frontal cortical brain tissue from AD, DLB, PD, and age-matched control subjects were received from the University of Kentucky Alzheimer’s Disease Center, the Institute for Brain Aging and Dementia (University of California-Irvine), the Oregon Brain Bank, the University of Maryland, and the Johns Hopkins Brain Resource Center. The following information was available for the cases used in this study: diagnosis, age at death, gender, postmortem index, brain area, and Braak stage (Table S1). Freshly prepared ice-cold PBS with protease inhibitor cocktail (Cat. # 11836145001, Roche Diagnostics, IN, USA) was added to each sample at a dilution of brain weight:buffer as 1:3 (w/v) and homogenized. The samples were then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C, and the supernatants were collected. Brain homogenates (supernatants) were quantified using bicinchoninic acid protein assay (Micro BCA Kit, Pierce), aliquoted and stored at −80°C until use.

Isolation of α-synuclein oligomers from synucleinopathy brain tissues

Immunoprecipitation of α-Syn aggregates was performed from the brain extracts of patients with synucleinopathies. Co-Immunoprecipitation Kit (Thermo Scientific) was used following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Briefly, amine-reactive resin was coupled with affinity-purified 4D6 antibody (BioLegend, 834302) followed by incubation with brain extracts. Next, beads were washed to remove unbound proteins. Bound proteins were eluted in 0.1 M glycine (pH 2.8), adjusting the final pH to 7.0 by adding 1 M Tris-HCl (pH 8). Isolated fractions were subjected to buffer exchange and finally collected in sterile PBS. Immunoprecipitated α-Syn were then centrifuged in a microcon centrifugal filter device with 25 kDa molecular weight cut-off (Millipore) at 14,000 × g for 25min at 4°C. The fractions of α-Syn that were retained in the filter device contained α-Syn oligomers. These oligomeric fractions were quantified using bicinchoninic acid protein assay (Micro BCA kit, Pierce). Fractions were then aliquoted and stored at −80°C. Oligomeric α-Syn isolated from brain tissues (BDSOs) were characterized thoroughly as previously described.36,37

Western blot analysis

For Western blot analysis, brain homogenates (∼15 μg) were loaded on precast NuPAGE 4–12% Bis-Tris gels (Invitrogen) for SDS-PAGE analysis. Gels were subsequently transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes and blocked with Intercept (TBS) blocking buffer (LI-COR, 927–60001) at 4°C overnight. Membranes were then probed with primary antibodies, anti-Cx50 (1:5000, Invitrogen 33–4300), anti-Cx43 (1:5000, Sigma Aldrich C6219), and anti-β-actin (1:5000, Sigma; A2228) antibodies diluted in Intercept T20 (TBS) protein-free antibody diluent (LI-COR 927–65001) for 1 h at RT. IRDye IgM anti-mouse (1:10000, LI-COR 926–68070) or IRDye IgG anti-rabbit (1:10000, LI-COR, 926–32210) were used to detect Cx50 and Cx43 immunoreactivity, respectively. The LI-COR Odyssey imager was used to visualize the bands. Densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health).

Atomic force microscopy

Atomic force microscopy was performed using 3 μL of each BDSO sample to study the morphologies of the isolated oligomers. In brief, different oligomer preparations of α-Syn, fibrillar α-Syn were analyzed by AFM using a non-contact tapping method with a Multimode 8 AFM machine (Bruker, Billerica MA). Briefly, 3–4 μL of each sample was applied onto a fresh-cleaved mica surface and allowed to adsorb at RT overnight. Mica was then washed with 200 μL of deionized water and air-dried. Images were taken from 5 different areas on the mica surface.

Bis-ANS and Thioflavin T fluorescence assays

Three μL of either α-Syn (0.5 μg/μL) and 247 μL of 10 μM bis-ANS (4,4'-dianilino-1,1'-binaphthyl-5,5'-disulfonic acid, dipotassium salt) prepared in mM glycine-NaOH buffer (pH 7.4) were added to the wells of 96-well clear-bottomed black plates. Each condition was performed in triplicates. The fluorescence intensity was measured at λ-emission 520 nm upon λ-excitation 380 nm. For Thioflavin T (ThT) assay, 3 μL of protein (0.5 μg/μL) and 247 μL of 20 μM ThT prepared in 50 mM glycine-NaOH buffer (pH 8.5) were added in triplicates to the wells. Fluorescence intensity was read at λ-emission 490 nm following excitation at 440 nm using a POLARstar OMEGA plate reader (BMG Lab technologies). Each condition for this assay was performed in triplicates.

Fluorescence- and biotin-labeling of BDSOs

BDSOs were labeled with Alexa Fluor™ (AF568) NHS Ester (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s guideline with minor modifications. Briefly, AF568 NHS Ester was dissolved in 100 mM sodium bicarbonate to make the final concentration 1 mg/mL. The dye solution was then incubated with BDSOs in a 1:2 ratio (w/w). The mixture was rotated overnight at 4°C. The following day, the solution was centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 30 min using 10 kDa Amicon Ultra-0.5 Centrifugal Filter Units to remove unbound dye. Oligomers were subsequently washed with PBS. Filter compartment was centrifuged to collect the concentrate. For BDSO biotinylation, EZ-Link NHS-PEG4-Biotin, No-Weigh Format (2 mg, Thermo Scientific; A39259) was reconstituted in water to create a 2 mM stock solution. BDSO was incubated with the biotin reconstituted stock at a 1:1 molar ratio for 30 min at room temperature (RT) or 2 h on ice. The biotinylated protein was then purified using Zeba desalting spin columns (Thermo Scientific; 89882) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Immunofluorescence

For frozen human and mouse brain tissues, sections were fixed in chilled methanol, followed by blocking in goat serum for 1 h. Sections were then incubated in primary antibodies overnight: mouse anti-Cx50 IgM (1:1000, Abcam ab222885), rabbit anti-Cx43 (1:1000, Sigma-Aldrich, C6219), rabbit anti-alpha synuclein (pS129, 1:500, Abcam, ab59264), rabbit anti-NeuN (1:1000, Abcam, ab190195), rabbit anti-MAP2 (1:100, Abcam, ab5392), chicken anti-GFAP (1:1000, Abcam, ab53554), and mouse anti-LB509 (1:500, Abcam, ab27766). The next day, sections were washed three times with PBS and then incubated with secondary antibodies: AF568 anti-mouse IgM, AF405 anti-rabbit, and AF647 anti-chicken or mouse (1:700, Abcam) for 1 h. After washing three times with PBS (10 min each), slides were mounted in ProlongTM Gold mounting media (Invitrogen, P36941, P36934). For immunocytochemistry, primary neuron-astrocyte co-cultures were washed 3 times with 1× PBS and fixed with 4% formaldehyde solution for 15 min at RT. Cells were washed 3 times with 1× PBS followed by permeabilizing with 0.25% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min at RT. Cells were blocked in 5% goat serum for 30 min at RT and incubated with primary antibodies: mouse anti-Cx50 IgM (1:1000,Abcam ab222885), rabbit anti-βIII-tubulin (1:1000, Abcam, ab68193), chicken anti-GFAP (1:3000, Abcam, ab53554) at 4°C overnight. The next day, cells were washed and incubated with secondary antibodies: AF568 anti-mouse IgM, AF405 anti-rabbit, AF647 anti-chicken, and Streptavidin-AF488 (1:700, Abcam) for 1 h at RT. Coverslips were finally washed for 3 times and mounted with ProLong Diamond antifade mounting media (Invitrogen, P36970). Coverslips were imaged under Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope using 63× objective with 405 nm diode laser and argon laser 458/488/514 nm. Z-stacks were built by capturing images from approximately 16-17 stacks at 0.37–0.41 μm optimal thickness. Each treatment condition was performed in 3 independent experiments and were randomly imaged at five different regions of interest. All images were analyzed by ImageJ (NIH) software.

For immunofluorescence of paraffin-embedded tissue sections, paraffin-embedded slides containing the tissue samples were consecutively immersed in the following solutions: xylene for 5 min (2 times), 100% EtOH for 3 min, 100% EtOH for 3 min, 95% EtOH for 3 min, 90% EtOH for 3 min, 70% EtOH for 3 min, 60% EtOH for 3 min, 50% EtOH for 3 min, miliQ H2O for 3 min. Antigen retrieval was performed by incubating slide sections in sodium citrate buffer, pH 6.0 for 30 min in a water-bath at 80°C. Next, slides were removed from the bath and cooled down in 1× PBS. Samples were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) for 2 min and then washed in 1× PBS for 5 min three times. Unspecific antibody binding sites were blocked by incubating samples with a freshly prepared blocking solution as previously described.53,54 Afterward, sections were incubated overnight at 4°C using a humidity chamber. Anti-Iba-1 (Abcam, ab5076), hSyn, and Cx50 primary antibodies were added to the samples diluted in a blocking solution and incubated at 4°C overnight. Following antibody staining, slides were washed in 1× PBS for 5 min three times to eliminate unbound antibodies. Secondary antibodies were added to the appropriate dilutions and incubated for 2 h at RT. Slides were washed three times in 1× PBS for 5 min to eliminate unbound antibodies. Next, slides were mounted using Prolong Diamond Antifade Mount medium containing DAPI (Invitrogen, P36971). Slides were kept in the dark at 4°C. Image acquisition and analysis were done by confocal microscopy using an A1 Nikon confocal microscope with spectral detection and unmixing systems. Image analysis was performed using the Nikon NIS Elements Advanced Research imaging software (Nikon Instruments).

Immunohistochemistry

Human and mouse brain sections (14 μm) were fixed in chilled methanol, washed in PBS, and incubated in blocking buffer (PBS with 0.1% Triton X-100 (PBST) mixed with 5% normal goat serum and 5% bovine serum albumin) for 1 h at RT. Then, mouse brain sections were washed in PBS and incubated in the Mouse-on-Mouse (MoM) blocking solution (one drop of MoM solution in 4 mL PBST) (Vector Laboratories; MKB-2213) for 1 h at RT. Sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with mouse anti-Cx50 IgM (1:1000, Invitrogen, 33–4300) or rabbit anti-TH (1:1000, Abcam, ab152) diluted in 5% BSA in PBST. The following day, sections were washed in PBS three times for 10 min each and incubated with biotinylated horse anti-mouse IgM or anti-rabbit (1:5000; Vector Laboratories) for 1 h. Sections were then washed three times for 10 min each in PBS and visualized using an ABC reagent kit (Vector Laboratories), according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Lastly, sections were counterstained with hematoxylin (Vector Laboratories; H-3401) for nuclear staining and mounted.

Ex vivo ethidium bromide uptake assay

The assay enables the evaluation of hemichannel activity by Ethidium bromide (Etd) uptake in ex vivo brain slices. The animals were decapitated without anesthesia to prevent intrinsic blocking effects of anesthetics in channel activity.55 The brains were removed from the skull and placed in 1× ice-cold artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) containing NaCl (125 mM), KCl (2.5 mM), glucose (10 mM), NaH2PO4 (1.25 mM), CaCl2 (2 mM), and MgCl2 (1.3 mM), HEPES (20 mM), pH 7.4. The caudal part of the brains was removed with a razor blade, and each brain was gently glued to a holder tray filled with ice-cold 1× slicing buffer NaCl (85 mM), KCl (3 mM), CaCl2 (0.5 mM), MgSO4 (3.5 mM), NaH2PO4 (1.25 mM), glucose (10 mM), sucrose (75 mM), Sodium L-ascorbate (0.5 mM), and HEPES (20 mM). Coronal slices were sectioned at 300 μm using a Vibratome Series 1000. Each slice was transferred to a sterile cell strainer (Fisherbrand, Cat# 22363548) in a six-well plate and incubated in ACSF buffer for at least 1 h. Slices were pre-incubated for 15 min with the connexin hemichannel blocker,56,57,58 probenecid or the mimetic peptide for Cx36, Cx43, or Cx50 (Table S2). As a control, probenecid vehicle and the scrambled peptide to Cx50 were used. Then each 20 μM Etd was added to each well, and incubated for 30 min. Afterward, the slices were washed three times for 5 min with ACSF, once with 1× PBS for 1 min, and fixed at RT with 4% paraformaldehyde for 1 h, protected from light, then washed twice with PBS for 10 min with gentle agitation. The slices were washed 3 times with PBS-Glycine 0.1 M for 5 min each, under gentle agitation at RT, washed 2 times with 1× PBS for 10 min each, under gentle agitation at RT, and incubated for 30 min each with blocking solution (1× PBS +0.2% gelatin +1% Triton X-100). After blocking each tissue was incubated with primary antibodies for anti-Iba-1 (microglia, 1:200) and anti-GFAP (astrocytes, 1:200) overnight at 4°C. The next day the slices were washed three times per 10 min with blocking solution and then incubated for 2 h at room temperature with anti-goat Alexa Fluor 488 (1:200), and anti-chicken Alexa Fluor 647 (1:200). Further, the slices were washed three times (10 min each) in PBS and then mounted in prolong DAPI, cover-slipped, and examined in a confocal microscope (Nikon A1, Japan). Stacks of consecutive confocal images were taken with a 20× objective at 100 nm intervals. They were acquired sequentially using three lasers (in nm: 408, 488, and 543), and Z projections were reconstructed using Nikon confocal software (NIS-elements) and ImageJ software. At least six fields were selected in each brain slice. Dye uptake was calculated with the following formula:

Corrected total cell Etd fluorescence = Integrated Density − ([Area of selected cell] × [Mean fluorescence of background readings]).

RNA isolation and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

Brain tissues were resuspended in TRIzol for RNA extraction according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Invitrogen). cDNA was synthesized by using Lunascript RT Supermix (NEB M3010) according to manufacturer instructions. The cDNA was amplified using specific primer sets (Table S5) manufactured by IDT and Bio-Rad iTaq™ Universal SYBR Green SuperMix kit. ThermoFisher ABI 7500 fast Real-Time PCR Thermal Cycler was used to perform RT-qPCR with the thermal cycling program: 95°C for 10 min, 95°C for 15 s, and 60°C for 60 s for 40 cycles each step. Relative cDNA levels for the target genes were analyzed by the 2−ΔΔCt method using GAPDH (GenBank: NM_001411840.1) as the internal control for normalization.

Primary neuron-astrocyte co-cultures and cell treatment

This study was conducted in a facility approved by the American Association for the Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care. All procedures were performed in accordance with recommendations in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Institutes of Health. Our protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Texas Medical Branch (UTMB).

Primary cortical neuron preparation

Primary cortical neurons were isolated from C57BL/6N-Tg (Thy1-SNCA)15Mjff/J (Jackson Laboratory, Stock# 017682) and C57BL/6 (Jackson Laboratory, Stock# 000664) mice during embryonic days 13–16 as previously described.38,59,60,61,62,63 Briefly, cortical tissue was dissected and dissociated in Accutase solution (Sigma, A6964). Neurons were plated at 2 × 105 cells/mL onto poly-L-lysine–coated 24-well plates in Neurobasal medium (12348017, Gibco) supplemented with 2% B-27 plus (Gibco, A3582801), 0.5 mM GlutaMax (Gibco, 35050-061), 10,000 units/mL penicillin, 10,000 μg/mL streptomycin, and 25 μg/mL amphotericin B supplement. Cultures were maintained at 37°C with 5% CO2, and half-medium changes were performed every 3–5 days. All experiments were conducted on day in vitro (DIV) 10.

Mixed glia cultures and fluorescence-activated cell sorting for astrocyte

Mixed glial cells were prepared as described.64 Cerebral cortices from postnatal day 0–3 (P0–P3) C57BL/6N-Tg (Thy1-SNCA)15Mjff/J pups were isolated, and following removal of meninges, cerebellum, and olfactory bulbs, minced in ice-cold HBSS (Gibco) with glucose. Tissue was enzymatically dissociated in Trypsin-EDTA (0.25%, Gibco, 25200114) at 37°C for 20 min with gentle agitation. The reaction was quenched with 10% FBS (Gibco, A5670701), and samples were cooled on ice for 5 min. After centrifugation (400 × g, 5 min, 22°C), pellets were resuspended in high-glucose DMEM (Gibco, 11965092) with 10% FBS, 1× antibiotic-antimycotic, and 100 mM sodium pyruvate (Gibco, 11360070). Cell density was determined, and 6–10 million cells per T75 flask were plated on PLL-coated flasks, maintained at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 10–14 days with media changes every 4–5 days. Recombinant mouse GM-CSF (5 ng/mL, R&D Systems, 415-ML-005) was added on DIV 7 to promote microglial proliferation. Mixed glia were harvested for astrocyte isolation using the fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) technique. Fc receptor was blocked using an Fc receptor blocker (BD Bioscience, 553141). For cell population gating, DAPI staining solution (Abcam, ab228549) for live-dead cell gating and immunolabeling with APC-labeled ACSA-2 antibody (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-117-535) for astrocyte-specific, PE/Cyabnine7-labeled CD45 antibody (Biolegend, 103113) and Alexa Flour 405 labeled CD11b (Novus Biologicals, NB110-89474AF405) antibodies for microglia-specific population were used. FACS-sorted astrocytes were used for a neuron-astrocyte co-culture system.

Primary neuron–astrocyte co-cultures

Primary neurons were prepared as above. On DIV8, FASC-sorted astrocytes were co-cultured with neurons (1:8 glia: neuron ratio) for 2 days. The co-cultures were treated with AF568- or biotin-tagged BDSOs at 0.25 μM for up to 24 h.

siRNA application

Gja8 silencing was performed using Accell SMARTpool siRNA (Dharmacon), which contains four siRNAs of use according to the manufacturer’s directions. Briefly, 48 h after plating cells, neurons were incubated with an empirical concentration of 500 nM siRNA targeting Gja8 diluted in Neurobasal medium. Cells were maintained in siRNA-containing medium for 96 h. The efficiency of the gene knockdown was evaluated using Western blot analysis. Non-targeting (NT) siRNAs were used as a control. All target sequences are shown in Table S3.

Live-cell imaging

Primary neuron-astrocytes co-cultures with siNT or siCx50 grown on μ-slide 8 well (ibidi, 80806) were exposed to 0.25 μM AF-568-tagged BDSOs. The time-lapse live-cell images were taken by Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope using a 63× objective with an argon laser 458/488/514 nm combined with differential interference contrast (DIC) for up to 2 h with 30 min interval, and at 24 h post-incubation. Each treatment condition was performed in three independent experiments. All images were analyzed by ImageJ (NIH) software.

Cell transfection and treatment

Plasmids used in this study were designed, generated, and purified by VectorBuilder (Chicago, IL) (Table S4). Human neuroblastoma SH-SY5Y cells were transfected with plasmids expressing eGFP-tagged control (Ctrl-eGFP), Cx50 (Cx50-eGFP), or Cx43 (Cx43-eGFP) for 48 h. Cells were pretreated with carbenoxolone (CBX), a gap junction inhibitor, to assess connexin-dependent BDSO uptake. Biotin-labeled BDSOs (biotin-BDSOs) were applied at 0.25 μM for 8 h, after which RIPA-soluble fractions were prepared for WB analysis.

Cell toxicity assays

Cytotoxicity was determined by measuring lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release using Cytotoxicity Detection kit PLUS (Roche, 04744926001). The absorbance was measured at 490 nm with a Polar Star Omega plate reader (BMG Labtech). Each experimental condition was performed in triplicate in three different independent assays.

Sandwich-ELISA

Cytokine concentrations (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-10) in conditioned media from primary neuron-astrocyte co-culture were quantified using ELISA MAX Deluxe sets for mouse (BioLegend, 432604, 431304, 430901, 431441) according to manufacturer’s protocols.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Prism 10.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). All values were calculated as mean and standard error of the mean (SEM). For

human postmortem brain studies, we used n = 3 individual cases, with 9 images per human for Cx50 intensity quantification and 10–15 images per human for Pearson’s correlation coefficient (PCC) analysis. For mouse studies, we used n = 4 animals per strain for IHC experiments (Figure 2), with 3 images per mouse for Cx50 intensity and 2–3 images per mouse for PCC analysis. For Etd uptake rate experiments, we used n = 3 animals per stain, with 8 images per mouse. For Pearson’s correlation coefficient measurement of confocal images, immunofluorescence images of human brain tissues, bis-ANS and Thioflavin T fluorescence, and quantification of Western blot bands, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was performed. The number of experiments is mentioned in the figure legends.
