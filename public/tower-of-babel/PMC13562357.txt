A wearable cuffless blood pressure monitoring system: real-time measurement using force sensing and waveform analysis
Authors: Tzu-Hsuan Lu, Yu-Hsuan Fan Chiang, Chuen-Chau Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper introduces a wearable, cuffless blood pressure (BP) monitoring system for continuous, real-time measurement in the operating room. Traditional cuff-based BP monitoring is intermittent and unsuitable for surgery, while intra-arterial measurements are invasive. The system uses a finger clip with a force sensor to detect pressure changes caused by arterial pulsations, with a waveform decomposition analysis algorithm estimating systolic and diastolic BP in real time. A clinical validation study involving 46 patients in the operating room demonstrated the system’s accuracy, with reference measurements from an intra-arterial catheter. The reference systolic pressure ranged from 83 mmHg to 194 mmHg, and diastolic pressure from 42 mmHg to 105 mmHg. The error between paired measurements was − 1.44 ± 6.64 mmHg for systolic and 3.35 ± 6.91 mmHg for diastolic, with the device’s measurements meeting the ISO 81060-2:2018 accuracy specification of 5 ± 8 mmHg. Pearson correlation coefficients were r = 0.90 (p < 0.01) for systolic and r = 0.78 (p < 0.01) for diastolic, indicating significant correlations with intra-arterial catheter measurements. The data analysis also considered the IEEE 1708 standard, ensuring compliance with guidelines for non-invasive BP monitoring. In collaboration with the device developer, the authors designed the clinical calibration protocol and user interface to ensure compatibility with intra-operative monitoring workflows. This study represents the first clinical validation of a tactile force–sensing cuffless system against intra-arterial reference measurements, demonstrating its feasibility for real-time, non-invasive blood pressure monitoring during anesthesia.Keywords: Cuffless blood pressure monitoring, Real-time measurement, Intra-operative settings

Introduction

Blood pressure measurement is a fundamental aspect of patient care during surgical procedures and anesthesia. It is a critical vital sign routinely assessed in nearly every medical examination and for all hospitalized patients. In intensive care units (ICUs) and operating rooms (ORs), blood pressure is typically monitored intermittently using a non-invasive oscillometric cuff on the patient’s upper arm, provided there are no contraindications, such as the presence of a dialysis shunt or recent ipsilateral breast surgery [1, 2]. For critically ill patients in the ICU, as well as those undergoing major surgeries or other complex procedures, continuous blood pressure monitoring is often indispensable. Currently, the gold standard for continuous blood pressure measurement is invasive, relying on intra-arterial catheterization [3]. Intermittent or continuous blood pressure monitoring is also required for all patients undergoing general anesthesia for surgical procedures, as outlined in the American Society of Anesthesiologists’ monitoring standards [4].

Although blood pressure is generally measured non-invasively and intermittently with an oscillometric sphygmomanometer on the upper arm, certain clinical scenarios necessitate continuous blood pressure measurement. These include complex surgeries or high-risk patients where blood pressure can fluctuate rapidly and unpredictably during anesthesia [5, 6]. In these cases, continuous monitoring is essential for real-time data, which helps guide clinical decisions, such as fluid resuscitation, vasopressor administration, or vasodilator therapy to maintain mean arterial pressure within the desired range [7]. However, using an invasive arterial catheter for continuous blood pressure measurement comes with its own set of complications. These include risks of infection at the catheter insertion site, arterial damage, thrombosis, and complications related to the placement procedure itself [8, 9]. Additionally, the need for regular calibration and maintenance of the system [10, 11], as well as the potential for inaccurate readings due to catheter positioning or patient movement, can complicate the accuracy and reliability of measurements [12, 13].

To overcome the limitations of traditional intermittent non-invasive oscillometric blood pressure measurements and the invasiveness of intra-arterial monitoring, Cardio Ring Technologies, Inc. (San Jose, CA) has developed ArteVu, a non-invasive finger-clip device. While similar in appearance to a pulse oximeter, ArteVu distinguishes itself by incorporating a novel tactile sensor instead of an optical sensor, enabling cuffless and continuous blood pressure monitoring through the patient’s upper extremity finger. Unlike optical sensors [14–20], the tactile sensor is immune to interference from skin color pigments and ambient light fluctuations, providing more reliable readings. This innovative device offers clinicians a non-invasive means to detect rapid and unexpected fluctuations in blood pressure, minimizing the discomfort and risks associated with arterial cannulation.

While the hardware components of the ArteVu system were developed by Cardio Ring Technologies, the present study was initiated and conducted by the authors, who developed the calibration protocol and clinical user interface for operating room integration. To our knowledge, no prior publication has evaluated a tactile-sensing, cuffless blood pressure system against intra-arterial reference standards in real-time intraoperative conditions. This study therefore provides the first systematic evidence bridging force-sensing technology and invasive hemodynamic validation.

This paper introduces ArteVu and presents the results of a validation study comparing the accuracy of ArteVu’s proprietary non-invasive technology against continuous, simultaneously obtained invasive intra-radial arterial blood pressure measurements. The study adheres to established international standards, including AAMI/ESH/ISO 81060-2:2018 and ISO 14155:2020, which govern non-invasive sphygmomanometers.

System design and technology

The ArteVu system consists of a parameter module, a reusable cable, and a disposable finger clip, as shown in Fig. 1. The system acquires arterial pressure waveforms using the finger clip that houses a specialized force sensor. When the clip is attached to the participant’s fingertip, the piezoresistive sensor is positioned firmly against the skin directly overlying digital arteries. The pulsatile flow of blood within arteries causes the vessel wall to expand and relax with each heartbeat, generating a pressure wave that propagates through the subcutaneous tissue and exerts a dynamic mechanical force on the sensor. The force sensor is made of biocompatible silicone rubber and incorporates a piezoresistive mechanism that translates mechanical strain into changes in electrical impedance [21]. These changes are sampled by a reusable cable at 200 Hz, digitized, and transmitted to the parameter module. Within the module, Cardio Ring’s proprietary algorithm processes the signal to calculate and display the continuous blood pressure on a standard LCD monitor via an HDMI connection.

ArteVu system for cuffless, continuous, and non-invasive blood pressure monitoring, comprising a parameter module, a reusable cable, and a disposable finger clip

In cases of device malfunction such as irregular waveform display, or upon observation of clinical signs including swelling, edema, paresthesia, pain, or ischemia, the clip can be immediately removed. It is suitable for fingers with a first knuckle width of less than 19.8 mm.

Calibration of ArteVu measurements is performed using an upper arm cuff integrated with an ISO 81060-2-compliant oscillometric unit embedded within the parameter module. This cuff is placed on the ipsilateral upper arm corresponding to the finger with the attached clip, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The blood pressure measured by the cuff is displayed in the noninvasive blood pressure (NBP) section of the interface, as shown in Fig. 3. Calibration is required prior to initial use and at one-hour intervals during operation. During the initial 5 s of the calibration period, ArteVu derives a provisional blood pressure estimate from the digital waveform signal. At the end of the calibration period, a reference blood pressure value is obtained from the cuff using the standard oscillometric method and stored by ArteVu. The system then determines the offset between the provisional estimate and the cuff reference and applies this offset to adjust all subsequent continuous measurements, as illustrated in Fig. 3.

Illustration of workflow from raw sensor data to blood pressure estimation

To compute blood pressure, the digital waveform signal is processed using a proprietary algorithm based on pulse wave analysis. While this general approach shares similarities with other CNBP devices, such as the CareTaker4 [22], the extracted features and the compositions of features to calculate blood pressure are different. In addition, ArteVu’s underlying sensing principle leads to a fundamentally different signal processing pathway. For instance, the CareTaker4 employs a finger cuff and piezoelectric transducer to measure arterial volume changes, producing a signal equivalent to the first derivative of the vascular volume that requires numerical integration to reconstruct the pressure waveform. In contrast, ArteVu’s piezoresistive sensor directly measures the mechanical force of the arterial pulsation. This direct acquisition simplifies the processing pipeline and shortens the measurement response time. Following this direct measurement, the waveform signal is initially processed using a 0.1–10 Hz bandpass filter, as illustrated in Fig. 3. Every two seconds, a segment corresponding to a complete cardiac cycle is extracted from the filtered waveform. This segment is amplitude-normalized and subsequently decomposed into three to five constituent waves. The physiological relevance of these component waves has been comprehensively described by Baruch [22]. Additionally, the derivatives of the waveform are analyzed to assess arterial stiffness. Features extracted from both the decomposed waves and their derivatives are used as inputs to a pair of empirically derived equations for blood pressure estimation. This process is performed every two seconds, enabling continuous and noninvasive blood pressure (CNBP) monitoring. The CNBP output includes systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, mean arterial pressure, and pulse rate as shown in Fig. 4, with all parameters updated at two-second intervals.

Methodology

A single-center, observational clinical study was conducted to evaluate the accuracy of ArteVu in comparison with invasive intra-arterial blood pressure measurements. The calibration protocol and user interface for intra-operative use were jointly designed by the authors to standardize the comparison between the ArteVu system and invasive reference measurements. The study protocol conformed to ISO 81060-2:2018. Furthermore, because ArteVu is a cuffless device, the protocol also incorporated key principles from the IEEE 1708 standard. A primary requirement of this standard is the assessment of performance stability over the device’s calibration interval. Fifty adult surgical patients requiring continuous arterial blood pressure monitoring via a radial artery catheter as part of routine clinical care were enrolled. Eligibility was determined based on predefined inclusion criteria, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy, arrhythmia, and the presence of upper extremity occlusive peripheral vascular disease. NBP monitoring with the ArteVu finger clip was performed concurrently on the same limb as the intra-arterial catheter. ArteVu finger clip was randomly placed on patient’s index, middle, or ring finger. In line with the IEEE 1708 stability requirement, ArteVu measurements were compared directly with invasive arterial pressure readings over a continuous monitoring period of at least one hour. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Taipei Medical University Hospital, Taipei City, Taiwan (IRB No. N202106030), and conducted in accordance with Taiwanese law. All procedures adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and followed the guidelines of Good Clinical Practice of ISO 14155:2020.

For each enrolled patient, a minimum of ten CNBP measurements were acquired at six-minute intervals, with concurrent intra-arterial reference measurements (IBP) recorded. In accordance with ISO 81060-2, a 30-second window was used for averaging the IBP data, which comprises 15 s before and after each CNBP measurement. Four of the 50 initially recruited patients were excluded from the analysis due to surgical procedures lasting less than one hour. ArteVu automatically flags and excludes data points with significant motion artifacts that compromise the pulse waveform, displaying a question mark in place of a blood pressure reading; such data points were omitted prior to analysis. The final dataset, comprising 389 paired measurements from 46 patients, was used to evaluate the accuracy of ArteVu. In accordance with ISO 81060-2:2018, the measurement error for each data point was calculated as the difference between the ArteVu reading and the corresponding reference value. To fulfill the IEEE 1708 stability test, the one-hour data collection period was divided into three sequential 20-minute segments, and the measurement accuracy was evaluated independently for each segment to confirm that performance did not degrade over time. To quantify the stability of our Bland-Altman analysis, we applied a bootstrapping procedure. We generated 30 bootstrap samples by resampling subjects from the original dataset (with replacement and equal sample size). This process calculated robust confidence intervals from the original dataset, thereby providing a more reliable reference framework for the reported metrics. Pearson correlation coefficients were also determined to quantify the linear association between ArteVu and the reference measurements.

Results

Clinical validation results

Table 1 summarizes the demographic and clinical characteristics of the patients included in the complete CNBP dataset. The dataset spans a broad range of age, height, weight, BMI, finger width, and arm circumference. Of the 46 patients, 26 (56.5%) were male. A total of 67.4% of participants had hypertension, and 21.7% had diabetes mellitus. Figure 5 presents Bland-Altman plots comparing ArteVu CNBP measurements with invasive reference blood pressure values, including all 389 paired data points from the 46 patients. The reference systolic blood pressure ranged from 83 mmHg to 194 mmHg, diastolic pressure from 42 mmHg to 105 mmHg, and mean arterial pressure (MAP) from 56 mmHg to 139 mmHg. The mean ± standard deviation (SD) of the differences between ArteVu and reference measurements were − 1.44 ± 6.64 mmHg for systolic pressure, 3.35 ± 6.91 mmHg for diastolic pressure, and − 0.75 ± 5.94 mmHg for MAP. These values fall within the ISO 81060-2:2018 accuracy specification of 5 ± 8 mmHg. Figure 6 displays scatter plots of ArteVu CNBP measurements versus reference values, with Pearson correlation coefficients of r = 0.90 (p < 0.01) for systolic pressure, r = 0.78 (p < 0.01) for diastolic pressure, and r = 0.87 (p < 0.01) for MAP. These results demonstrate statistically significant correlations between ArteVu CNBP measurements and invasive arterial catheter readings.

IEEE 1708 standard compliance

Static test

As a cuffless device, it is essential to validate that ArteVu can accurately and continuously measure blood pressure in accordance with the IEEE 1708 standard. The full dataset of 389 paired measurements was evaluated. The first CNBP reading was obtained six minutes after the initial calibration, and subsequent measurements were recorded at fixed six-minute intervals. The validation was conducted in three parts: static test, change test, and stability test. The static test evaluates ArteVu’s accuracy under conditions of minimal blood pressure variation, defined as changes in systolic pressure ≤ 15 mmHg (|ΔSYS| ≤ 15 mmHg) and/or diastolic pressure ≤ 10 mmHg (|ΔDIA| ≤ 10 mmHg). The change test assesses ArteVu’s accuracy during substantial fluctuations in blood pressure, i.e., |ΔSYS| > 15 mmHg or |ΔDIA| > 10 mmHg. The stability test verifies that ArteVu maintains measurement accuracy over the duration of the calibration period.

Table 2 summarizes the distribution of the complete dataset based on blood pressure variation categories. For the static test, 287 data points with systolic pressure changes ≤ 15 mmHg (|ΔSYS| ≤ 15 mmHg) were collected from 46 patients, and 290 data points with diastolic pressure changes ≤ 10 mmHg (|ΔDIA| ≤ 10 mmHg) were obtained from 44 patients. The Bland-Altman plot in Fig. 7(a) presents ArteVu’s measurement accuracy for systolic pressure under static conditions. Using bootstrap resampling (n = 287, repeated 20 times), the average measurement error was − 0.65 ± 6.21 mmHg, with 95% confidence intervals of ± 0.19 mmHg for the mean error and ± 0.15 mmHg for the standard deviation. Figure 7(b) shows the corresponding Bland-Altman plot for diastolic pressure, with a mean error of 3.28 ± 6.93 mmHg and 95% confidence intervals of ± 0.15 mmHg for the mean and ± 0.13 mmHg for the standard deviation. These results meet the accuracy criteria defined by ISO 81060-2. Furthermore, the mean absolute difference (MAD) for systolic and diastolic pressure measurements was 4.29 mmHg and 5.87 mmHg, respectively, corresponding to Grades A and B according to IEEE 1708 recommendations.

Change test

For cases where systolic blood pressure variation exceeded 15 mmHg (|ΔSYS| > 15 mmHg), a total of 102 data points were collected from 29 patients. Similarly, for diastolic pressure changes greater than 10 mmHg (|ΔDIA| > 10 mmHg), 99 data points were obtained from 32 patients. The observed maximum and minimum changes in systolic pressure were + 88 mmHg and − 63 mmHg, respectively. The maximum and minimum changes in diastolic pressure were + 49 mmHg and − 31 mmHg, respectively.

Figure 8(a) illustrates the Bland-Altman plot evaluating ArteVu’s accuracy in measuring systolic blood pressure under conditions of significant change. Bootstrap resampling (n = 102, 20 iterations) yielded an average measurement error of − 3.64 ± 7.27 mmHg, with 95% confidence intervals of ± 0.34 mmHg for the mean error and ± 0.25 mmHg for the standard deviation. Figure 8(b) presents the corresponding Bland-Altman analysis for diastolic pressure, demonstrating a mean error of 3.69 ± 7.23 mmHg and confidence intervals of ± 0.36 mmHg for both the mean and the standard deviation. These accuracy results comply with the ISO 81060-2 standards. Additionally, the MAD for systolic and diastolic pressure measurements was 5.66 mmHg and 5.62 mmHg, respectively, aligning with Grade B performance as defined by the IEEE 1708 guidelines.

Stability test

For the stability test, measurement accuracy was assessed over three-time intervals following the initial calibration: 0–20 min, 20–40 min, and 40–60 min. Table 3 presents the number of data points collected within each time interval, while Table 4 summarizes ArteVu’s measurement accuracy during each respective period. The results demonstrate that ArteVu meets the accuracy criteria outlined in both ISO 81060-2 and IEEE 1708 standards throughout the evaluation period. Figure 9 illustrates the measurement error as a function of elapsed time since calibration. The Pearson correlation coefficients between error and time were − 0.03 for systolic pressure and − 0.06 for diastolic pressure, indicating negligible correlation. These results confirm that ArteVu fulfills the IEEE 1708 stability test requirement, maintaining accuracy across the one-hour evaluation period following calibration.

Discussion

Age group analysis

Table 5 presents the age distribution of the 46 patients who contributed to the complete data set. According to IEEE 1708 guidelines, individuals aged over 50 years are considered a distinct subgroup for analysis. Within this subgroup, the mean ± standard deviation of ArteVu’s measurement error was − 1.70 ± 6.88 mmHg for systolic blood pressure and 3.70 ± 7.04 mmHg for diastolic blood pressure.

Effects of vasoactive drug

In cuffless blood pressure monitoring systems such as ArteVu, arterial pulse waveforms that reflect arterial compliance and peripheral vascular resistance are central to blood pressure estimation. Therefore, evaluating the influence of vasoactive agents, including vasodilators and vasopressors, is critical for assessing the accuracy of ArteVu’s CNBP measurements. In this clinical study, the vasodilators administered were labetalol and nicardipine, while the vasopressors included norepinephrine (levophed) and ephedrine. Given the time-dependent effects of these agents, CNBP measurement accuracy was assessed within the known pharmacologic half-lives of each drug, as summarized in Table 6 [23–26]. Because the pharmacokinetics of nicardipine and norepinephrine exhibit biexponential decay, data within the second-phase half-life—during which the drugs remain clinically active—were included in the analysis. A total of 18 out of 46 patients received vasoactive agents during the monitoring period, yielding 109 CNBP data points for this subgroup analysis. The measurement error for ArteVu in this subgroup was − 1.08 ± 7.86 mmHg for systolic and 4.75 ± 7.24 mmHg for diastolic pressure. A forest plot summarizing the mean errors in the subgroup analysis is presented in Fig. 10. All the errors are still within acceptable range per ISO 81060-2.

Effects of finger size

In this study, patient’s finger thickness ranges from 10.93 mm to 14.20 mm. The applied force increases 23.6% in a somewhat linear manner, implying that the sensor deformation is in an elastic regime. Patient’s pulse pressure adds extra 1.9 ~ 3% to the applied force. The pulse pressure generates only a small signal upon the applied force while the sensor deformation is elastic, the magnitude of the applied force does not influence the waveform morphology for the finger size noted in this study.

We also conducted a bench test with a health volunteer by intentionally misaligning the sensor 2 mm away from the finger pulp in two orthogonal directions. The deviation of the measured blood pressure and heart rate is within 5 mmHg and 3 bpm, respectively. Therefore, user technique or device placement does not affect measurement consistency.

Limitations

This clinical validation has several important limitations that must be acknowledged when interpreting the results.

First, the study was conducted on anesthetized patients in the supine position within a controlled operating room environment. This setting inherently minimizes motion, which is a primary source of artifact in non-invasive monitoring. Consequently, while the device performed well under these ideal conditions, this study does not provide evidence of its accuracy or reliability in ambulatory patients or other real-world scenarios where motion artifact is common. For not interfering procedures, the impact of re-wearing the finger clip at different times was not assessed. The generalizability of our findings to less controlled clinical settings remains to be established.

Second, the study’s sample size and demographic composition represent a limitation. While the cohort of 46 patients exceeded the minimum requirement of ISO 81060-2, it falls short of the larger sample recommended by the IEEE 1708 standard for cuffless devices. More critically, the cohort lacked demographic diversity, with younger individuals (under 50 years) being severely underrepresented (n = 5). Given that arterial pulse waveform characteristics may vary significantly with age, the accuracy of the ArteVu algorithm in this younger population has not been robustly validated. Future studies must include a larger, more age-diverse sample to meet the recommendations of IEEE 1708 and ensure broad clinical applicability.

Finally, the validation protocol was constrained by the standards available at the time of its design. The study was conducted in accordance with ISO 81060-2. However, the more recent ISO 81060-3 standard now provides specific criteria for continuous monitors, including the evaluation of their ability to track rapid, beat-to-beat changes in blood pressure. While our stability analysis addressed the one-hour calibration interval as guided by IEEE 1708, it did not assess the device’s dynamic, short-term accuracy of every two-second measurement as required by ISO 81060-3. Therefore, a future, more comprehensive evaluation against this new, more rigorous standard is necessary before claims about continuous monitoring can be fully substantiated.

Clinical implications

Although the core sensing hardware was developed commercially, this study provides the first independent, peer-reviewed validation of a tactile-based, cuffless blood pressure monitoring system in the intraoperative environment. By designing a clinical calibration workflow and conducting invasive reference comparisons, the authors demonstrated that tactile sensing, unlike optical or tonometric approaches, can achieve invasive-grade accuracy in real-time. By eliminating the need for arterial catheterization and enabling real-time, two-second updates, the system provides a continuous hemodynamic trend that can support intraoperative decisions on vasoactive drug titration, fluid management, and early hypotension detection - issues that remain unresolved in conventional intermittent monitoring. Our subgroup analyses (Sect. 5.1 and 5.2) provide new clinical insights into the device’s robustness under vasoactive drug administration and across different age groups. These analyses extend beyond device validation, illustrating that arterial compliance and waveform morphology can be tracked non-invasively, which may open future applications in anesthetic pharmacodynamics and geriatric hemodynamic monitoring. This work represents an important step toward establishing tactile force sensors as a new modality for continuous hemodynamic monitoring.

Conclusion

In this study, we evaluated ArteVu, a cuffless CNBP monitoring system. The primary finding was that the device met the accuracy requirements of the ISO 81060-2 standard when compared against invasive arterial blood pressure over a one-hour period. A stability analysis, guided by IEEE 1708 principles, indicated that measurement error did not significantly increase during this period. The device also performed within specified accuracy limits in patients older than 50 years and in those receiving vasoactive medications.

This study has several important limitations. The validation was conducted on anesthetized, supine patients, a setting which minimizes motion artifacts and may not represent broader clinical use. Additionally, the sample size did not meet the full recommendations of the IEEE 1708 standard, particularly for ensuring robust accuracy assessment in younger age groups. Therefore, further investigations are necessary to validate performance in more diverse populations and ambulatory conditions. Future studies should also be designed to meet the validation criteria for continuous monitoring outlined in the recently released ISO 81060-3 standard.

In conclusion, the results of this initial validation study suggest that the ArteVu device shows promise as a tool for intraoperative blood pressure monitoring. However, its broader clinical utility is contingent upon addressing the current study’s limitations. Future work will focus on conducting larger, multi-center trials and refining technology to ensure its robustness across diverse healthcare settings.
