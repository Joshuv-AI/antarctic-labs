Case Presentation

A 77-year-old man with a history of hypertension and a single prior episode of atrial fibrillation (AF) presented to the emergency department after a simple fall at home. He reported severe left hip pain and was unable to ambulate.

On arrival, he was hemodynamically stable (blood pressure 135/59 mm Hg, heart rate 72 beats/min, oxygen saturation 100% on room air). Physical examination revealed localized tenderness over the left hip, with the limb shortened and externally rotated. A grade 3/6 ejection systolic murmur was audible at the right upper sternal border with radiation to the carotid arteries. Pelvic imaging demonstrated a comminuted fracture of the left acetabulum.

Chart review revealed a recent transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) demonstrating severe aortic stenosis (AS) with an aortic valve area of 0.95 cm2, peak velocity 4.9 m/s, and mean gradient 54 mm Hg. Left ventricular ejection fraction was preserved at 62.5%, with moderate aortic regurgitation. The patient reported several months of progressive exertional dyspnea when climbing stairs and chest tightness while using his stationary bike, but he denied palpitations, dizziness, or syncope.

Question 1: What are the potential mechanistic links between AS and hip fracture (HFx)?

Answer 1 Severe AS and HFx share overlapping risk profiles and pathophysiologic pathways. AS-related hemodynamic limitations, including reduced cardiac output, exertional syncope risk, and orthostatic hypotension, predispose elderly patients to falls. Concurrently, calcific AS is associated with reduced bone mineral density and disordered calcium-phosphate metabolism. This systemic dysregulation, known as the calcification paradox, results in calcium loss from bone and deposition in cardiovascular structures (Figure 1).1

In this patient, progressive dizziness and postural instability, consistent with AS-related hemodynamic compromise, preceded the episode of falling, illustrating these mechanistic links at the bedside.

Question 2: What is the clinical relevance of severe AS in the setting of acute HFx?

Answer 2 Severe AS is a well-established predictor of perioperative mortality in noncardiac surgery (NCS). Patients have a limited, fixed ability to augment cardiac output under the physiologic demand of anesthesia and surgery, rendering them vulnerable to hypotension, myocardial ischemia, and hemodynamic collapse.2 The hypertrophied, stiff left ventricle has poor preload reserve and markedly increased afterload, making hemodynamic rescue difficult. HFx repair is urgent; surgery within the first 24 to 48 hours of the HFx reduces mortality and immobilization-related complications.3 However, untreated AS is a dominant perioperative risk factor. Contemporary guidelines recommend addressing symptomatic severe AS before major noncardiac surgery whenever feasible.4

In this case, rapid identification of severe AS on prior TTE was the pivotal step that reframed clinical priorities and prompted early multidisciplinary team involvement before any surgical plan was finalized.

Question 3: What were the management options, and why was transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) selected for this patient?

Answer 3 Managing severe AS and HFx requires balancing fracture fixation urgency against the hemodynamic risk of untreated valvular obstruction. Terré et al1 proposed 5 management groups based on disease severity and timing of cardiac intervention relative to orthopedic surgery (Table 1). Current guidelines recommend that criteria for valve intervention before NCS mirror standard indications for aortic valve replacement: symptomatic severe AS, left ventricular ejection fraction <50%, and/or critical valvular severity.4

Three strategies were considered in this patient. Proceeding directly to orthopedic repair without prior valve intervention was considered suboptimal: His symptomatic, high-gradient AS (mean gradient 54 mm Hg, peak velocity 4.9 m/s) conferred unacceptably high perioperative cardiovascular risk.5 Balloon aortic valvuloplasty was considered as a bridge but rejected: Following transfer to the tertiary center, computed tomography (CT)–based anatomical assessment confirmed tricuspid morphology, adequate annular dimensions, and no prohibitive iliofemoral disease, establishing definitive TAVR suitability and making a temporizing procedure unnecessary.6 The multidisciplinary team comprising structural cardiology, trauma surgery, and anesthesiology reached consensus to proceed with minimalist transfemoral TAVR before fracture fixation. Question 4: What are the main elements to consider for transfemoral TAVR procedural planning?

Answer 4 TAVR planning requires systematic evaluation of valve anatomy (tricuspid vs bicuspid morphology, calcification), annulus dimensions, left ventricular outflow tract calcification, coronary ostial heights, and iliofemoral access feasibility. In the urgent HFx setting, these assessments must be completed rapidly without compromising safety.

Cardiac CT angiography is the gold standard providing precise annular, coronary, and access route evaluation critical for valve sizing and avoidance of complications such as annular rupture, paravalvular leak, or vascular injury.7 Because the initial trauma center lacked structural heart capability, this patient was transferred to a tertiary TAVR-capable center on day 0. CT-based assessment confirmed tricuspid morphology, annular area 575.2 mm2 and perimeter 86.3 mm, and no prohibitive iliofemoral disease (Figure 2). Access through the right (nonfractured) femoral side was selected to preserve the injured left groin for subsequent orthopedic fixation—a key anatomical consideration unique to this scenario.

Question 5: What would be the role of a minimalist TAVR approach in these patients?

Answer 5 A minimalist TAVR approach is particularly advantageous in the setting of concomitant AS and HFx, where urgent valve stabilization must be achieved while preserving physiologic reserve for imminent orthopedic surgery. It emphasizes conscious sedation, TTE guidance, radial diagnostic access, and standardized recovery pathways, enabling safe next-day discharge in approximately 80% of high-risk patients.8

In this case, the team pursued monitored anesthesia care with conscious sedation, deliberately avoiding general anesthesia to minimize physiologic stress and facilitate rapid recovery. Fluoroscopy and TTE provided real-time procedural guidance without the complexity of transesophageal echocardiography. A 29-mm Edwards SAPIEN 3 Ultra RESILIA valve (Edwards Lifesciences) was implanted via right common femoral access with left radial diagnostic access. Post-deployment TTE confirmed optimal prosthetic function (peak velocity 2.9 m/s, mean gradient 16 mm Hg, no paravalvular leak) (Figure 3). The patient was mobilized within hours. He was monitored for 24 hours post-TAVR to confirm hemodynamic stability and access site integrity before transfer back for orthopedic fixation.

Question 6: What are the critical logistical considerations for optimizing care in patients with concomitant AS and HFx?

Answer 6 Outcomes in patients with severe AS and HFx depend less on any single procedural step and more on coordinated, time-critical multidisciplinary logistics. Key elements include early triage involving cardiology, orthopedics, anesthesia, and geriatrics; rapid TTE for AS identification and management group stratification; preestablished transfer protocols to TAVR-capable centers; parallel evaluation for TAVR candidacy, anesthesia clearance, and orthopedic planning under a “time-is-hip” protocol targeting combined intervention within 36 hours; and integrated rehabilitation planning.

Question 7: What antithrombotic strategy should guide periprocedural management?

Answer 7 Antithrombotic management requires individualization, balancing the thrombotic risks of a newly implanted bioprosthesis against the hemorrhagic risk of an acute fracture and imminent surgery.

This patient experienced a single AF episode in 2019 following elective surgery. Trial therapy with a direct oral anticoagulant was subsequently initiated, but this was discontinued by his cardiologist given that the benefit was questionable in the setting of a single, remote, self-terminating episode. Through shared decision making, his outpatient provider elected not to pursue long-term oral anticoagulation. The patient was maintained on aspirin for primary prevention until presentation.

Standard systemic heparinization was administered during TAVR. Given the absence of an ongoing anticoagulation indication and the planned orthopedic surgery, no additional antithrombotic therapy was initiated post-procedure. This approach is supported by emerging data suggesting that aspirin monotherapy or even no antithrombotic therapy may be acceptable in selected patients with high bleeding risk following TAVR without a significant increase in ischemic events.9 For hip fixation, aspirin was held perioperatively and was resumed at discharge.

This case underscores that antithrombotic planning in the setting of severe AS and HFx must integrate the prosthesis thrombotic profile, patient indications for full antithrombotic therapy, fracture-related hemorrhagic risk, and the proximity of the next surgical intervention.

Question 8: What would be the long-term benefits of TAVR in these patients?

Answer 8 Timely treatment of both conditions enables safer rehabilitation, improving mobility, thromboembolic risk reduction, and physical independence. Long-term benefits include improved survival, fewer recurrent falls, reduced nursing home placement, and lower health care costs.10

In this patient, the staged approach enabled discharge to rehabilitation on day 4. At 1 year, he remained fully independent in activities of daily living with a well-seated prosthesis. Excellent recovery is achievable when pathway logistics are optimized and patient selection is appropriate.
