Maximal quadrics over finite fields and minimal codewords of projective Reed–Muller codes Thanks: E–mail address: {alain.couvreur, rati.ludhani}@inria.fr Inria & Laboratoire LIX, École Polytechnique, Institut Polytechnique de Paris, 91120 Palaiseau CEDEX, France.
Alain Couvreur, Rati Ludhani

Abstract
. We study the classification of minimal codewords of projective Reed–Muller codes of order 2 2 . This problem is equivalent to identifying quadrics over finite fields whose set of rational points is maximal with respect to the inclusion. We prove that except one particular case over 𝔽 2 {\mathbb{F}}_{2} , any two absolutely irreducible quadrics whose sets of rational points are contained within one another should be equal as projective varieties. We deduce a precise characterisation of the minimal codewords of projective Reed–Muller codes of order 2 2 and further give their exact number for each possible weight.



Keywords:
Projective Reed–Muller codes, minimal codewords, quadrics, rational points.

MSC (2020):
94B27, 05E14, 14N10, 11T71.

1.
Introduction

Minimal codewords of a linear code carry important information about its combinatorial and geometric structure. A nonzero codeword is said to be
minimal
if its support does not properly contain the support of any other nonzero codeword. Massey
[
15
]
proved that the access structure of a linear secret sharing scheme can be completely determined by the minimal codewords of the dual of the code considered for the scheme. Some decoding algorithms are also based on minimal codewords over the binary field, see
[
10
,
2
,
1
,
3
]
. Further, just over a decade ago, Johnsen and Verdure
[
11
]
showed that one can associate a vector matroid to a linear code, where the minimal codewords correspond precisely to the circuits of this matroid. These circuits play a crucial role in determining the weight distribution and higher weight spectra of the code. Thus, identifying minimal codewords is of significant interest, although doing so in practice is highly nontrivial.
A general algorithm is known for binary codes
[
1
]
, yet it quickly becomes inefficient for codes of large size. This has led to studies focusing on particular code families where additional structure can be exploited; see, for instance
[
1
,
3
,
4
,
18
]
. In this work, we address this problem for projective Reed–Muller codes of order 2.

Let
q
be a prime power and let
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
denote the finite field with
q
elements. Write
{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
for an algebraic closure of
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
.
Let
N
and
d
be nonnegative integers. We write
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
for the
N
-dimensional projective space over
{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
, that is,
(\overline{{\mathbb{F}}}_{q}^{N+1}\setminus\{0\})/{{\overline{\mathbb{F}}^{\times}_{q}}}
.
Let
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})
be its set of rational points and write

p_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sharp{\mathbb{P}}^{N}({\mathbb{F}_{q}}).

Denote by
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{N}]
the polynomial ring in
N+1
variables with coefficients in
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
and by
{{\mathbb{F}_{q}}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{N}]}_{d}
the
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
-space of homogeneous polynomials of degree
d
, including the zero polynomial. A
projective Reed–Muller
(PRM) code
of order
d
and length
p_{N}
, denoted by
{\mathsf{PRM}}_{q}(d,N)
, is defined as the image of the evaluation map

{\rm{Ev}}:\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}{{\mathbb{F}_{q}}[X_{0},X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}]}_{d}&\longrightarrow&{\mathbb{F}}^{{}^{p_{N}}}_{q}\\
f&\longmapsto&{(f(P))}_{P\in E}~,\end{array}\right.

where
E
is a list of
p_{N}
elements of
{\mathbb{F}^{N+1}_{q}}
representing any element of
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})
.

PRM codes were first mentioned as an example of geometric Goppa codes from higher dimensional varieties in the lecture notes
[
21
]
by Manin and
Vlăduţ.
Later, Lachaud
[
12
]
formally introduced them for order
d<q
and Sørensen
[
20
]
generalized to
d\geqslant q
. Lachaud in
[
13
, §3]
further proved that PRM codes behave better
than the ubiquitous family of codes called Reed–Muller codes, in particular, the performance measure (relative distance–transmission rate sum) is higher for PRM codes compared to Reed-Muller codes in a range of parameters. Thus, it is important to understand PRM codes better to see their potential for applications. Several fundamental parameters of PRM codes are already known: the minimum distance was determined by Lachaud
[
13
]
using Serre’s bound
[
19
]
for
d<q
and independently by Sørensen
[
20
]
in general, the minimum weight codewords were characterized by Rolland
[
17
]
, and their enumeration was obtained by Ghorpade and the second named author
[
6
]
.

Our contributions

In this article, we characterize all minimal codewords of
{\mathsf{PRM}}_{q}(2,N)
and determine their exact number at each weight. As one can observe from the definition, these questions are equivalent to characterising the
quadrics over
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
whose sets of
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
–rational points are maximal with respect to the inclusion and determining the number of such sets having same cardinality.

Following Hirschfeld and Thas
[
9
]
, absolutely irreducible quadrics in
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
over
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
fall into three classes: parabolic, hyperbolic, and elliptic. Thus, to characterise quadrics with respect to their
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
–rational points, our key ingredient would be to prove the following result which may also be of independent interest in finite geometry, combinatorics or number theory.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{Q}}
and
{\mathcal{Q}}^{\prime}
be two absolutely irreducible quadrics in

{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
over
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
.
If
{\mathcal{Q}}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})\subset{\mathcal{Q}}^{\prime}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})
, then
{\mathcal{Q}}={\mathcal{Q}}^{\prime}
, except when
q=2
,
{\mathcal{Q}}
is elliptic and
{\mathcal{Q}}^{\prime}
is hyperbolic both of rank
4
.

Corollary 1.2
.

A quadric
{\mathcal{Q}}
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
defined over
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
is uniquely determined
by its set of
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
–rational points, unless it is irreducible and not
absolutely irreducible (
i.e.
if it is the union of two

{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}
–hyperplanes that are conjugate under the action of the
Frobenius map).

We emphasize that our approach handles both the odd and even characteristic cases of
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
in a unified way.

It is worth noting that an analogous result is easily seen to hold for linear spaces,
i.e.
if two linear spaces
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
over
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
are such that
L_{1}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})\subset L_{2}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})
, then
L_{1}\subset L_{2}
. This indicates that quadrics mirror some structural aspects of linear spaces.
One possible approach toward proving Theorem
1.1
is to invoke the Lang–Weil bound
[
14
]
which provides estimates on the number of
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
–rational points of absolutely irreducible varieties. In this context, one could apply it for both
{\mathcal{Q}}
and
{\mathcal{Q}}\cap{\mathcal{Q}}^{\prime}
, whenever absolutely irreducible, to compare their
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
–rational points. However, such bounds are sharp only for large
q
, and as
N
grows,
q
must grow rapidly for the bound to provide a contradiction.
Therefore, toward finer results over small fields we had to adopt another approach by combining together geometric and combinatorial techniques. Our main approach relies on induction on the ambient dimension
N
and how rank behaves on hyperplane sections of quadrics, which provide an elegant framework.

Our main theorem for minimal codewords of
{\mathsf{PRM}}_{q}(2,N)
is as follows.

Theorem 1.3
.

The minimal codewords of the codes
{\mathsf{PRM}}_{q}(2,N)
are exactly the codewords
c={\rm{Ev}}(F)
where
F
has one of the following forms:

(1)

L_{1}L_{2}
where
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
are
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
-linearly independent homogeneous linear forms in
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{N}]
;

(2)

F
is an absolutely irreducible quadratic form in
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{N}]
excluding the forms of rank
3
if
q\leqslant 3
and of rank
4
when elliptic if
q=2
.

Note that Case (
1
) in the above theorem
corresponds exactly to the minimum weight codewords of
{\mathsf{PRM}}_{q}(2,N)
,
see
[
17
, Lem. 2.3]
.

As a final contribution, we count the number of
minimal codewords using the classification from
Theorem
1.3
and following Hirschfeld and
Thas
[
9
, Thm. 1.44(ii)]
for the number of smooth quadrics in each orbit with respect to the action of

\mathbf{PGL}_{N}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})
.

Outline of the article

In Section
2
, we develop the necessary background by recalling known results needed in the sequel and introducing several new lemmas required for our main theorems. In Section
3
, we prove Theorem
1.1
. Finally, we enumerate in Section
4
the minimal codewords of
{\mathsf{PRM}}_{q}(2,N)
.

2.
Setup and Auxiliary Results

Let
q
be a power of a prime
p
and
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
be the finite field with
q
elements.

2.1.
Projective varieties

Let
{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
denote an algebraic closure of
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
. Let
N
be a positive integer. For any field
K
, let
K[X_{0},\ldots,X_{N}]
denote the polynomial ring in
N+1
variables defined over
K
. For each nonnegative integer
d
, we denote by
{K[X_{0},\ldots,X_{N}]}_{d}
the
K
-vector space consisting of all homogeneous polynomials of degree
d
including the zero polynomial. Let
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}({\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}})
or
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
for short, denote the
N
-dimensional projective space over
{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
, defined as
({\overline{{\mathbb{F}}}_{q}^{N+1}\setminus\{\bf{0}\}})/{{\overline{\mathbb{F}}^{\times}_{q}}}
.
For any
F\in{\mathbb{F}_{q}}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{N}]
, let
{\mathsf{V}}(F)
denote the (projective) variety defined by
F
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
,
i.e.
the set of zeros of
F
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}({\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}})
.

Take note that, in the present article, despite studying quadrics over
a non–algebraically closed field, we chose to keep a basic geometric
point of view and to avoid the formalism of schemes. In short, any
geometric object: projective space, quadric hypersurfaces, linear
subspaces and any other variety,
should be understood as a subset of

{\mathbb{P}}^{N}({\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}})
,

i.e.
as the whole set of
{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
–rational points satisfying
some polynomial equations. Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz establishes the
one–to–one correspondence between such a geometric object and the
corresponding homogeneous radical ideal. A variety is said to be

defined over
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
if the corresponding ideal is generated by
elements of
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}[X_{0},\dots,X_{N}]
or equivalently, if the variety is
left globally invariant under the Galois action of

\text{Gal}({\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}/{\mathbb{F}_{q}})
.

Denote by
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})
the set of
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
-rational points of
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
which has cardinality

p_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sharp{\mathbb{P}}^{N}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})=q^{N}+\cdots+q+1.

Similarly, for any variety
\mathcal{X}
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
, we denote by
\mathcal{X}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})
the set of
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
–rational points
of
\mathcal{X}
. Moreover, since we will only be concerned with the
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
–rational points of a variety throughout this paper, we will refer to them as
rational points
.

2.2.
Linear codes and projective Reed–Muller codes

An
[n,k]_{q}
–(linear) code
is a
k
–dimensional
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
–subspace of
{\mathbb{F}}^{n}_{q}
and its elements are said to be the
codewords
. Let
C
be an
[n,k]_{q}
-code. The
support and Hamming weight of a codeword
c=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in C
are defined as

{\rm{Supp}\hskip 1.42262pt}(c)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}:c_{i}\neq 0\}\quad\text{and}\quad{\rm{w_{H}}\hskip 1.42262pt}(c)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sharp{\rm{Supp}\hskip 1.42262pt}(c).

A nonzero codeword
c
of an
[n,k]_{q}
-code
C
is said to be
minimal
if there does not exist any other nonzero codeword
c^{\prime}
such that
{\rm{Supp}\hskip 1.42262pt}(c^{\prime})\subsetneq{\rm{Supp}\hskip 1.42262pt}(c)
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
d
be a nonnegative integer. Choose the representatives of the rational points of
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
in
{\mathbb{F}}^{N+1}_{q}
with last nonzero coordinate
1
and list them as
P_{1},\ldots,P_{p_{{}_{N}}}
. Define the evaluation map

{\rm{Ev}}:\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}{{\mathbb{F}_{q}}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{N}]}_{d}&\longrightarrow&{\mathbb{F}}^{{}^{p_{{}_{N}}}}_{q}\\
F&\longmapsto&(F(P_{1}),\ldots,F(P_{p_{{}_{N}}})).\end{array}\right.

The image of
{\rm{Ev}}
is a linear code called the
projective Reed–Muller code of length
p_{{}_{N}}
and of order
d
and denoted by
{\mathsf{PRM}}_{q}(d,N)
.

Following Manin and Vlăduţ’ works
[
21
]
,
Lachaud formally introduced and studied the parameters of projective Reed-Muller codes for
d<q
in
[
12
,
13
]
. In particular,
for
d<q
, the dimension of
{\mathsf{PRM}}_{q}(d,N)
is simply
m+d\choose d
as
{\rm{Ev}}
is injective and the minimum distance is due to Serre
[
19
]
. For
d\geqslant q
,
the dimension and minimum distance of
{\mathsf{PRM}}_{q}(d,N)
are known by Sørensen
[
20
]
. Several dimension formulas for general
d
also appear in the literature. One may refer to article
[
6
]
for a comprehensive exposition of the code parameters, including a discussion of the equivalence of these formulas.

It is evident that for
c_{F}\in{\mathsf{PRM}}_{q}(d,N)
where
F\in{{\mathbb{F}_{q}}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{N}]}_{d}
,

{\rm{Supp}\hskip 1.42262pt}(c_{F})=\{i\in\{1,\ldots,p_{N}\}:F(P_{i})\neq 0\}.

Switching to the complement subset, knowing the support is directly equivalent to knowing the rational points on the variety defined by
F
,
i.e.

{\mathsf{V}}(F)({\mathbb{F}_{q}})=\{P\in{\mathbb{P}}^{N}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})~:~F(P)=0\}.

Thus, to determine the minimal codewords of
{\mathsf{PRM}}_{q}(2,N)
we are looking for nonzero quadratic forms
F\in{\mathbb{F}_{q}}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{N}]
such that there is no other quadratic form
F^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{F}_{q}}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{N}]
satisfying
{\mathsf{V}}(F)({\mathbb{F}_{q}})\subsetneq{\mathsf{V}}(F^{\prime})({\mathbb{F}_{q}})
. That is, we look for quadrics whose sets of rational points are maximal with respect to the inclusion.

2.3.
Projective classification of quadrics in
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})

A
quadric
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
defined over
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
is the zero locus in
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
of a quadratic form in
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{N}]
. A quadric in
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
is called a
conic
.

Caution.
In the present article, most of the
varieties we deal with are defined over
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
. Therefore, whenever we
refer to a
variety, quadric, conic
of any linear space, unless
otherwise specified they will be defined over
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
.

As a simple consequence of the Hilbert Nullstellensatz,
the quadratic forms in
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{N}]
up to multiplication by a nonzero scalar are in one–to–one correspondence with the quadrics in
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
.
We should however consider separately the case of
quadratic forms that are squares of linear forms,
the corresponding ideal being non radical. In this case,
the corresponding variety is a hyperplane that we will
refer to
double hyperplane
since it is a hyperplane
“with multiplicity two”.

From now on, whenever convenient, we will talk in the language of quadrics in
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
wherever convenient. The following classification of quadrics is folklore, and we include a proof for the sake of completeness.

Proposition 2.2
.

The quadrics in
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
can be classified as follows:

(1)

double hyperplanes (defined by
L^{2}
where
L
is a linear form).

(2)

unions of distinct
hyperplanes (defined by

L_{1}L_{2}
where
L_{1},L_{2}
are linearly independent linear forms).

(3)

{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
-irreducible quadrics
which are
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
-reducible (defined by

L_{1}L_{2}
where
L_{1},L_{2}
are linear forms with coefficients in

{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}
and conjugated by the Frobenius).

(4)

absolutely irreducible quadrics.

Proof.

Let
{\mathcal{Q}}
be a quadric in
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
defined by
F\in{{\mathbb{F}_{q}}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{N}]}_{2}
.
If
{\mathcal{Q}}
is an
{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
–irreducible quadric, it is in class (
4
). Thus, we may assume that
{\mathcal{Q}}
is
{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
–reducible, which implies that
F=L_{1}L_{2}
for
L_{1},L_{2}\in{{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{N}]}_{1}
. For any
G\in{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}[X_{0},\dots,X_{N}]
, we denote by
G^{[q]}
the form obtained from
G
by raising all the coefficients to the
q
–th power. Since
F
has coefficients
in
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
, we obtain

F=F^{[q]}=L_{1}^{[q]}\cdot L_{2}^{[q]}.

Since
{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{N}]
is a unique factorisation domain, either
L_{1}^{[q]}=\alpha L_{1}
and
L_{2}^{[q]}=(1/\alpha)L_{2}
or
L_{1}^{[q]}=\beta L_{2}
and
L_{2}^{[q]}=(1/\beta)L_{1}
for
\alpha,\beta\in{\overline{\mathbb{F}}^{\times}_{q}}
respectively. In the first case,
L_{1},L_{2}
are
{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
-multiples in
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{N}]
, leading to classes (
1
) or (
2
) depending on whether
L_{1}
divides
L_{2}
or not. In the second case,
L_{i}^{[q^{2}]}=L_{i}
for
i=1,2
,
i.e.

L_{1},L_{2}\in{{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{N}]}_{1}\setminus{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{N}]}_{1}
, yielding class (
3
).
∎

2.4.
Rank and classification

Definition 2.3
.

The
rank of a quadratic form

F
is the least number of
variables needed in its expression, among all the possible

{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
–linear changes of variables of
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{N}]
. The

rank of a quadric

{\mathcal{Q}}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
is that of any of its defining
polynomials in
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{N}]_{2}
.

Most of the quantities of interest: rank, number of rational points,

etc.
are invariant by linear changes of variables,
i.e.

under the action of the projective general linear group

\mathbf{PGL}_{N}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})
into
canonical forms
. The classes of
absolutely irreducible quadrics under this action have canonical
representatives described in the statement below.

Theorem 2.4

(Hirschfeld and Thas
[
9
]
, Section 1.1)
.

Let
{\mathcal{Q}}
be an absolutely irreducible quadric in
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
. Then it has one of the following canonical
forms with respect to the action of
\mathbf{PGL}_{N}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})
.

(1)

{\mathcal{P}}_{r}={\mathsf{V}}(X_{0}^{2}+X_{1}X_{2}+X_{3}X_{4}\cdots+X_{r-2}X_{r-1})
for
r\geqslant 3
odd

(2)

For
r\geqslant 4
even,

(a)

{\mathcal{H}}_{r}={\mathsf{V}}(X_{0}X_{1}+X_{2}X_{3}+\cdots+X_{r-2}X_{r-1})

(b)

{\mathcal{E}}_{r}={\mathsf{V}}(f(X_{0},X_{1})+X_{2}X_{3}+\cdots+X_{r-2}X_{r-1})

where
f
is an irreducible binary quadratic form, in particular,
f
can be written as
X_{0}^{2}+dX_{1}^{2}
where
d
is a non–square when
p\neq 2
and as
X_{0}^{2}+X_{0}X_{1}+dX_{1}^{2}
where
{\rm Tr}_{{\mathbb{F}_{q}}/{\mathbb{F}}_{p}}(d)=1
when
p=2
.

The quadrics
{\mathcal{P}}_{r}
,
{\mathcal{H}}_{r}
and
{\mathcal{E}}_{r}
are called the
parabolic
,
hyperbolic
, and
elliptic
, respectively.
Moreover, the integer
r
is the rank of
{\mathcal{Q}}
.

Since the properties of quadrics we will use depend only on their
class modulo the action of
\mathbf{PGL}_{N}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})
, by abuse of notation
we allow ourselves to denote by
{\mathcal{P}}_{r}
,
{\mathcal{E}}_{r}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{r}
any quadric
equivalent to
{\mathcal{P}}_{r}
,
{\mathcal{E}}_{r}
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{r}
, respectively.

Remark 2.5
.

To complete the previous statement, forms
X_{0}^{2}
,
X_{0}X_{1}
, and
f(X_{0},X_{1})
, where
f\in{\mathbb{F}_{q}}[X_{0},X_{1}]
is irreducible
yield respective canonical forms for the classes (
1
), (
2
), and (
3
) in Proposition
2.2
.

Finally, we can characterize quadrics according to their rank, as follows.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
{\mathcal{Q}}
be a quadric in
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
among the classes of Proposition
2.2
.

(1)

If
{\mathcal{Q}}
is from class (
1
), then
{\rm{Rk}\hskip 1.42262pt}{\mathcal{Q}}=1
;

(2)

If
{\mathcal{Q}}
is from class (
2
) or
(
3
), then
{\rm{Rk}\hskip 1.42262pt}{\mathcal{Q}}=2
;

(3)

If
{\mathcal{Q}}
is from class (
4
), then for
r\geqslant 3
odd,
{\rm{Rk}\hskip 1.42262pt}{\mathcal{P}}_{r}=r
and for
r\geqslant 4
even,
{\rm{Rk}\hskip 1.42262pt}{\mathcal{E}}_{r}={\rm{Rk}\hskip 1.42262pt}{\mathcal{H}}_{r}=r
.

Proof.

Follows from Remark
2.5
, Theorem
2.4
.
∎

Remark 2.7
.

Suppose
p\neq 2
. Then, for any quadratic form

F=\sum_{0\leqslant i\leqslant j\leqslant N}a_{ij}X_{i}X_{j},

we may write

F=X^{\top}AX
where
X=(X_{i})_{0\leqslant i\leqslant N}
and

A=(A_{i,j})_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}
is a symmetric matrix
defined by

A_{i,j}=A_{j,i}=\begin{cases}a_{ij}/2&\text{if }i\neq j\\
a_{ij}&\text{if }i=j.\end{cases}

Furthermore,
A
can be reduced to a diagonal matrix, in particular,

F=(PX)^{\top}D(PX)

for some
P\in\mathbf{PGL}_{N}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})
where
D=diag(1,\ldots,1,\lambda,0,\ldots,0)
for
\lambda=1
or a non–square in
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
. When considering
quadrics,
i.e.
quadratic forms up to multiplication by a nonzero scalar, one gets the following possible canonical forms: either
X_{0}^{2}+X_{1}^{2}+\cdots+X_{r-1}^{2}
if
r
is even or
X_{0}^{2}+X_{1}^{2}+\cdots+X_{r-2}^{2}+\lambda X_{r-1}^{2}
where
\lambda
is either
1
or a non-square in
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
if
r
is odd. Finally, observe that the rank of the quadratic form is nothing but that of
A
.

Note that in even characteristic, these forms are
{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
–reducible and hence cannot describe absolutely irreducible quadrics. Dickson
[
5
]
addressed this question for even characteristic resulting in the canonical forms mentioned in Theorem
2.4
. To the best of our knowledge, Hirschfeld
[
8
]
rewrote the canonical forms in odd characteristic to unify the forms for both characteristics.

2.5.
Singular locus and cone structure

For any quadric
{\mathcal{Q}}
, we denote its singular locus by
{\rm{Sing}\hskip 1.42262pt}{\mathcal{Q}}
.
The rank of a quadric can be interpreted in terms of the dimension of its singular locus, as follows.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
F
be a quadratic form in
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{N}]
and let
{\mathcal{Q}}={\mathsf{V}}(F)
be the corresponding quadric in
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
. Then, the
singular locus of
{\mathcal{Q}}
is a linear subspace and

{\rm{Rk}\hskip 1.42262pt}{\mathcal{Q}}=N-\dim{\rm{Sing}\hskip 1.42262pt}({\mathcal{Q}}),

with the convention that an empty singular locus has dimension
-1
.

Proof.

After a suitable linear change of coordinates,
F
is one of the
canonical forms of Theorem
2.4
and hence is
expressed in the variables
X_{0},\dots,X_{r-1}
, where

r={\rm{Rk}\hskip 1.42262pt}{\mathcal{Q}}
. A calculation of partial derivatives shows that the
singular locus is
{\mathsf{V}}(X_{0},\dots,X_{r-1})
.
∎

Proposition 2.9

(
[
9
, Section 1.1]
)
.

Any singular quadric
{\mathcal{Q}}
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
is a cone on its singular locus
{\rm{Sing}\hskip 1.42262pt}({\mathcal{Q}})
and a smooth quadric in a linear subspace
\Pi\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
such that
\Pi\cap{\rm{Sing}\hskip 1.42262pt}({\mathcal{Q}})=\emptyset
and

\dim\Pi+\dim{\rm{Sing}\hskip 1.42262pt}({\mathcal{Q}})=N-1
. By
cone
we mean that
{\mathcal{Q}}
is the union of all the possible lines in

{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
joining a point of
{\mathcal{Q}}\cap\Pi
and a point of
{\rm{Sing}\hskip 1.42262pt}{\mathcal{Q}}
.

Note that in the statement above,
\Pi
is not unique but the
equivalence class of
{\mathcal{Q}}\cap\Pi
with respect to the action of the
projective linear group does not depend on the choice of
\Pi
. In the reverse
direction, the following statement expresses defining polynomials from the knowledge
of the singular locus.

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
F
be a quadratic form in
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{N}]
and let
{\mathcal{Q}}
be the quadric
{\mathsf{V}}(F)
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
of rank
r
. If
{\rm{Sing}\hskip 1.42262pt}({\mathcal{Q}})={\mathsf{V}}(X_{0},\ldots,X_{r-1})
, then
F
is a quadratic form in
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{r-1}]
.

Proof.

Write
F=\sum_{0\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}a_{ij}X_{i}X_{j}
. Since
F(u)=0
for
u\in{\rm{Sing}\hskip 1.42262pt}({\mathcal{Q}})
, we must have
F(0,\ldots,0,X_{r},\ldots,X_{N})=0
,
i.e.

F=\sum_{0\leqslant i,j<r}a_{ij}X_{i}X_{j}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant i<r\\
N\geqslant j\geqslant r\end{subarray}}a_{ij}X_{i}X_{j}

(1)

Let
i_{0}<r
. For
t\geqslant r
, define
e_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}(0:\dots:0:1:0:\dots:0)\in{\rm{Sing}\hskip 1.42262pt}({\mathcal{Q}})
where
1
is at the
(t+1)
–th position. Then,

\frac{\partial F}{\partial X_{i_{0}}}(e_{t})=0\quad\text{while}\quad\frac{\partial F}{\partial X_{i_{0}}}=\sum_{0\leqslant j\leqslant N}a_{i_{0}j}X_{j}.

Thus,
a_{i_{0}t}=0
. In particular,
a_{ij}=0
for all
i<r
and
j\geqslant r
. Hence, by (
1
), we obtain

F=\sum_{0\leqslant i,j<r}a_{ij}X_{i}X_{j}.

∎

2.6.
Projective index

The
projective index
of a quadric
{\mathcal{Q}}
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
is the largest dimension of a projective linear subspace defined over
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
and contained in
{\mathcal{Q}}
. It is denoted by
g({\mathcal{Q}})
.

It is clear that for any quadric of class (
1
) or (
2
) in Proposition
2.2

i.e.
double hyperplanes and unions of distinct hyperplanes, the projective index is
N-1
. For unions of conjugate hyperplanes (class (
3
)), the projective index is
N-2
. The following proposition describes the projective index of quadrics of higher rank.

Proposition 2.11

(
[
8
, Cor. 1]
)
.

For
1\leqslant s\leqslant\lfloor N/2\rfloor
, the projective indices of
{\mathcal{P}}_{2s+1}
,
{\mathcal{H}}_{2s}
and
{\mathcal{E}}_{2s}
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
are
N-s-1
,
N-s
and
N-s-1
, respectively.

2.7.
Number of rational points

The number of rational points on quadrics of classes (
1
), (
2
), and (
3
) in Proposition
2.2
can be computed as follows. For class (
1
), it equals the number of rational points on a hyperplane. For class (
2
), it equals the number of rational points on a union of two hyperplanes. The latter number is twice the number of rational points of a hyperplane, minus that of their intersection which is a linear space of codimension
2
. For class (
3
), it equals the number of rational points on a codimension
2
linear subspace, as the rational points are those on the intersection of the two
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}
-hyperplanes defining the quadric. These numbers are
p_{N-1}
,
2q^{N-1}+p_{N-2}
and
p_{N-2}
, respectively.

By Primrose
[
16
]
,
we also know the number of rational points on absolutely irreducible quadrics, which is as follows.

Proposition 2.12
.

For
1\leqslant s\leqslant\lfloor N/2\rfloor
, the number of rational points on absolutely irreducible quadrics in
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
are

(1)

\displaystyle\sharp{\mathcal{P}}_{2s+1}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})=p_{N-1}
;

(2)

\displaystyle\sharp{\mathcal{H}}_{2s}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})=p_{N-1}+q^{N-s}
;

(3)

\displaystyle\sharp{\mathcal{E}}_{2s}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})=p_{N-1}-q^{N-s}
.

The classical Serre bound
[
19
]
, which gives the
maximum number of rational points on a hypersurface of degree

d\leqslant q
, as well as the hypersurfaces that attain it, can be
recovered in the case of quadrics using
Proposition
2.12
, and the previous discussion. We
recall it below.

Theorem 2.13

(Serre bound
[
19
]
, degree 2 case)
.

The number of rational points on a quadric in
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
is bounded by
2q^{N-1}+p_{N-2}
. The bound is attained precisely for the quadrics defined by union of distinct hyperplanes.

Corollary 2.14
.

If a quadric
{\mathcal{Q}}
contains all the rational points of a linear subspace
\Pi
, it contains the linear subspace.

Proof.

Assume
\Pi\not\subset{\mathcal{Q}}
, then Theorem
2.13
applied to
{\mathcal{Q}}\cap\Pi
shows that
\sharp{\mathcal{Q}}\cap\Pi({\mathbb{F}_{q}})<\sharp\Pi({\mathbb{F}_{q}})
, a contradiction.
∎

2.8.
Bilinear and quadratic forms

Let
V
be a vector space over a field
K
. A
bilinear form
B
on
V
is a bilinear map from
V\times V
to
K
and its
radical
is

{\rm{Rad}\hskip 1.42262pt}B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\{v\in V:B(v,w)=0\text{ for all }w\in V\}.

Definition 2.15
.

The
polarisation
of a quadratic form
F\in{\mathbb{F}_{q}}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{N}]_{2}
is the bilinear form:

B_{F}(u,v)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\overline{\mathbb{F}}^{N+1}_{q}}\times{\overline{\mathbb{F}}^{N+1}_{q}}&\longrightarrow&{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}\\
(u,v)&\longmapsto&F(u+v)-F(u)-F(v).\end{array}\right.

The polarisation
B_{F}
of
F
can be interpreted as the differential of
F
. Namely, for any
u\in\overline{{\mathbb{F}}}_{q}^{N+1}
,
B_{F}(u,\cdot)
is the unique linear map satisfying

\forall v\in\overline{{\mathbb{F}}}_{q}^{N+1},\quad F(u+v)=F(u)+B_{F}(u,v)+F(v)

where the rightmost term is quadratic in
v
.

Definition 2.16
.

Let
F
be a quadratic form in
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{N}]
which polarizes to
B_{F}
. The
radical of
F
is the
{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
–space

{\rm{Rad}\hskip 1.42262pt}F\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\{v\in{\rm{Rad}\hskip 1.42262pt}B_{F}:F(v)=0\}.

Note that in odd characteristic we always have
{\rm{Rad}\hskip 1.42262pt}F={\rm{Rad}\hskip 1.42262pt}B_{F}
but this equality may fail in
characteristic
2
.

Lemma 2.17
.

Let
F
be a quadratic form in
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{N}]
and let
{\mathcal{Q}}
be the quadric
{\mathsf{V}}(F)
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
. Then the singular locus
{\rm{Sing}\hskip 1.42262pt}{\mathcal{Q}}
satisfies

{\rm{Sing}\hskip 1.42262pt}({\mathcal{Q}})={\mathbb{P}}({\rm{Rad}\hskip 1.42262pt}(F)).

Proof.

Let us first consider the case where
F=X_{i}X_{j}
for some
possibly equal
i,j\in\{0,1,\ldots,N\}
.
Then,

\forall u,v\in\overline{{\mathbb{F}}}_{q}^{N+1},\quad B_{F}(u,v)=u_{i}v_{j}+u_{j}v_{i}=\frac{\partial F}{\partial X_{i}}(u)v_{i}+\frac{\partial F}{\partial X_{j}}(u)v_{j}.

Hence, for any
F\in{{\mathbb{F}_{q}}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{N}]}_{2}
,

B_{F}(u,v)=\left\langle\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial X_{0}}(u),\ldots,\frac{\partial F}{\partial X_{N}}(u)\right),(v_{0},\ldots,v_{N})\right\rangle,

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the canonical Euclidean product on
\overline{{\mathbb{F}}}_{q}^{N+1}
.

Now,
B_{F}(u,v)=0
for every
v\in{\overline{\mathbb{F}}^{N+1}_{q}}
if and only if

(\frac{\partial F}{\partial X_{0}}(u),\ldots,\frac{\partial F}{\partial X_{N}}(u))=0
. In particular,
u\in{\rm{Rad}\hskip 1.42262pt}F
if and only if
F(u)=0
and
(\frac{\partial F}{\partial X_{0}}(u),\ldots,\frac{\partial F}{\partial X_{N}}(u))=0
,
i.e.
if and only if
{\mathbb{P}}(u)\in{\rm{Sing}\hskip 1.42262pt}({\mathcal{Q}})
.

The general case is deduced by linearity since the map
F\mapsto B_{F}
is linear.
∎

Proposition 2.18
.

Let
F
be a quadratic form in
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{N}]
and let
{\mathcal{Q}}={\mathsf{V}}(F)
be the corresponding quadric in
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
. Then the rank of
{\mathcal{Q}}
(or
F
),
introduced in Definition
2.3
, satisfies

{\rm{Rk}\hskip 1.42262pt}{\mathcal{Q}}=N-\dim{\rm{Sing}\hskip 1.42262pt}({\mathcal{Q}})=N-\dim_{{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}}{\rm{Rad}\hskip 1.42262pt}F+1.

Proof.

Follows from Proposition
2.8
and Lemma
2.17
.
∎

The following proposition is given as an exercise in
[
7
, Ex. 22.1]
. We prove
it for the sake of completeness.

Proposition 2.19
.

Let
F
be a quadratic form in
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{N}]
. Denote by
{\mathcal{Q}}
the quadric
{\mathsf{V}}(F)
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
. For any hyperplane
{\mathcal{L}}
,

{\rm{Rk}\hskip 1.42262pt}({\mathcal{Q}})-2\leqslant{\rm{Rk}\hskip 1.42262pt}({\mathcal{Q}}\cap{\mathcal{L}})\leqslant{\rm{Rk}\hskip 1.42262pt}({\mathcal{Q}}).

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
{\mathcal{L}}={\mathsf{V}}(X_{N})
. Denote by
F_{\mathcal{L}}
the quadratic form
F(X_{0},\ldots,X_{N-1},0)
in
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{N-1}]
defining the quadric
{\mathcal{Q}}\cap{\mathcal{L}}
in
{\mathcal{L}}\simeq{\mathbb{P}}^{N-1}
. Define the
{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}
-linear map

\phi:\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\rm{Rad}\hskip 1.42262pt}F&\longrightarrow&{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}\\
(v_{0},\ldots,v_{N})&\longmapsto&v_{N}.\end{array}\right.

On one hand,
\ker\phi
is either equal to
{\rm{Rad}\hskip 1.42262pt}F
or to a hyperplane of
{\rm{Rad}\hskip 1.42262pt}F
.
On the other hand,
any element of
\ker\phi
has form
(v_{0},\dots,v_{N-1},0)
where

(v_{0},\dots,v_{N-1})\in{\rm{Rad}\hskip 1.42262pt}{F_{\mathcal{L}}}
. The two previous observations entail

\dim_{{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}}{\rm{Rad}\hskip 1.42262pt}F-1\leqslant\dim_{{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}}\ker\phi\leqslant\dim_{{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}}{\rm{Rad}\hskip 1.42262pt}F_{{\mathcal{L}}}.

(2)

Next, let
(v_{0},\ldots,v_{N-1})\in{\rm{Rad}\hskip 1.42262pt}F_{{\mathcal{L}}}
. Therefore,

F(v_{0},\ldots,v_{N-1},0)=0\quad\text{and}\quad B_{F}((v_{0},\ldots,v_{N-1},0),(w_{0},\ldots,w_{N-1},0))=0

for all
(w_{0},\ldots,w_{N-1})\in\overline{{\mathbb{F}}}^{N}_{q}
. It implies that
(v_{0},\ldots,v_{N-1},0)\in{\rm{Rad}\hskip 1.42262pt}F
if we have
B_{F}((v_{0},\ldots,v_{N-1},0),(0,\ldots,0,1))=0
.
So, consider the linear form

\psi:\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\rm{Rad}\hskip 1.42262pt}F_{{\mathcal{L}}}&\longrightarrow&{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}\\
(v_{0},\ldots,v_{n-1})&\longmapsto&B((v_{0},\ldots,v_{n-1},0),(0,\ldots,0,1)).\end{array}\right.

We deduce that

\dim{\rm{Rad}\hskip 1.42262pt}F_{{\mathcal{L}}}-1\leqslant\dim\ker\psi\leqslant\dim{\rm{Rad}\hskip 1.42262pt}F.

(3)

Combining (
2
) and (
3
), we obtain

N+1-(\dim{\rm{Rad}\hskip 1.42262pt}F_{{\mathcal{L}}}+1)\leqslant N+1-\dim{\rm{Rad}\hskip 1.42262pt}F\leqslant N+1-(\dim{\rm{Rad}\hskip 1.42262pt}F_{{\mathcal{L}}}-1)

which implies that

{\rm{Rk}\hskip 1.42262pt}F_{{\mathcal{L}}}\leqslant{\rm{Rk}\hskip 1.42262pt}F\leqslant{\rm{Rk}\hskip 1.42262pt}F_{{\mathcal{L}}}+2,

as required.
∎

Proposition 2.20
.

Let
F
be a quadratic form in
{{\mathbb{F}_{q}}[X_{0},\ldots,X_{N}]}
and let

{\mathcal{Q}}={\mathsf{V}}(F)
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
with a non empty singular locus. Consider a hyperplane

{\mathcal{L}}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
which does not contain
{\rm{Sing}\hskip 1.42262pt}({\mathcal{Q}})
. Then

{\rm{Rk}\hskip 1.42262pt}({\mathcal{Q}}\cap{\mathcal{L}})={\rm{Rk}\hskip 1.42262pt}({\mathcal{Q}})
.

Proof.

After a possible linear change of variables, it suffices to assume that the point

(0:\cdots:0:1)\in{\rm{Sing}\hskip 1.42262pt}({\mathcal{Q}})
and
{\mathcal{L}}={\mathsf{V}}(X_{N})
. Denote
by
F_{\mathcal{L}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}F(X_{0},\dots,X_{N-1},0)
. By
Lemma
2.17
, it follows that
(0,\ldots,0,1)\in{\rm{Rad}\hskip 1.42262pt}F
. In particular, for every
(v_{0},\ldots,v_{N-1})\in{\rm{Rad}\hskip 1.42262pt}F_{\mathcal{L}}
, we obtain
(v_{0},\ldots,v_{N-1},0)\in{\rm{Rad}\hskip 1.42262pt}F
. Moreover, since
(0,\ldots,0,1)\in{\rm{Rad}\hskip 1.42262pt}F
, we have
\dim_{{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}}{\rm{Rad}\hskip 1.42262pt}F\geqslant\dim_{{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}}{\rm{Rad}\hskip 1.42262pt}F_{\mathcal{L}}+1
. But by Propositions
2.19
and
2.18
,
\dim_{{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}}{\rm{Rad}\hskip 1.42262pt}F\leqslant\dim_{{\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}}{\rm{Rad}\hskip 1.42262pt}F_{\mathcal{L}}+1
, which proves the equality.
∎

2.9.
Tangent spaces

In the sequel
we make use of
tangent spaces
which will always refer
to as
projective tangent spaces
as defined in
[
7
, Lect. 14]
. The notion can be
defined for any projective variety but we will only use it for quadric
hypersurfaces. Given a hypersurface
\mathcal{S}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{N}

defined as
\mathcal{S}={\mathsf{V}}(F)
for some homogeneous polynomial

F\in{\mathbb{F}_{q}}[X_{0},\dots,X_{N}]
and a point
P\in\mathcal{S}
, we define

{\mathsf{T}}_{P}\mathcal{S}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\left\{(x_{0}:\cdots:x_{N})\in{\mathbb{P}}^{N}~:~\sum_{i=0}^{N}x_{i}\frac{\partial F}{\partial X_{i}}(P)=0\right\}.

From Euler theorem on homogeneous polynomials,
P\in{\mathsf{T}}_{P}\mathcal{S}

and it is well–known that
{\mathsf{T}}_{P}\mathcal{S}
is a hyperplane of

{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
if and only if
\mathcal{S}
is smooth at
P
. Otherwise, when

P
is singular then
{\mathsf{T}}_{P}\mathcal{S}={\mathbb{P}}^{N}
.

With this definition at hand, a line
\ell
through
P
is

tangent
to
\mathcal{S}
at
P
if

\ell\subset{\mathsf{T}}_{P}\mathcal{S}
. In particular, if
P
is a singular
point of
\mathcal{S}
, then any line through
P
will be said to be
tangent to
\mathcal{S}
at
P
. The following statement is a direct consequence of Bézout’s Theorem.

Lemma 2.21
.

Let
{\mathcal{Q}}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
be a quadric hypersurface,
\ell\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{N}

be a line with
\ell\not\subset{\mathcal{Q}}
and
P\in\ell\cap{\mathcal{Q}}
. Then
\ell
is tangent to
{\mathcal{Q}}
at
P

if and only if
\ell\cap{\mathcal{Q}}=\{P\}
.

Finally, we will make use of the following very classical
statement which can be proven in whole generality and for which a
proof for the specific case of quadrics (which is the purpose of the
present article) is given in
[
9
, Thm. 1.11(ii)]
.

Lemma 2.22
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a hypersurface of
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
and

P\in\mathcal{S}
. Let
\Pi
be a linear subspace of
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
such
that
P\in\Pi\subset\mathcal{S}
.
Then,
\Pi\subset{\mathsf{T}}_{P}\mathcal{S}
.

3.
Quadrics with maximal rational points

As noted in Subsections
2.2
, to find the minimal codewords of
{\mathsf{PRM}}_{q}(2,N)
, we need to know the quadrics in
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
with maximal set of rational points with respect to the inclusion. Thus, we ask ourselves the following question: if
{\mathcal{Q}}
and
{\mathcal{Q}}^{\prime}
are two quadrics in
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
such that
{\mathcal{Q}}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})\subset{\mathcal{Q}}^{\prime}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})
, what can we say about the quadrics
{\mathcal{Q}}
and
{\mathcal{Q}}^{\prime}
? From Proposition
2.2
, rational points on quadrics from class (
1
) are clearly contained in some quadrics from class (
2
). Moreover, since the rational points on any quadric from class (
3
) are the rational points on codimension
2
linear subspace, it is contained in some hyperplane and hence in quadrics of class (
1
) or (
2
). Thus, we want to compare the quadrics which are either absolutely irreducible or a union of distinct hyperplanes. We begin by identifying some properties of quadrics satisfying
{\mathcal{Q}}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})\subset{\mathcal{Q}}^{\prime}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})
.
We first present some technical tools that will be useful in the sequel.

3.1.
Some technical lemmas

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{Q}}
be a quadric in
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
and let
P
be a rational
point on
{\mathcal{Q}}
. If
{\mathcal{Q}}
is smooth at
P
, then any
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
–line
\ell
through
P

that is non-tangent to
{\mathcal{Q}}
at
P
intersects
{\mathcal{Q}}
at exactly two
rational points, of which one is
P
. Equivalently, if
(\ell\cap{\mathcal{Q}})({\mathbb{F}_{q}})=\{P\}
for an
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
-line
\ell
, then
\ell\cap{\mathcal{Q}}=\{P\}
,
i.e.

\ell
is tangent to
{\mathcal{Q}}
at
P
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{Q}}
be a quadric in
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
. Then,
{\mathcal{Q}}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})
is contained in
a hyperplane if and only if
{\mathcal{Q}}
is either a double hyperplane (class (
1
) of Proposition
2.2
) or an
irreducible non-absolutely irreducible quadric (class (
3
)).

Proof.

The “if” part is straightforward.

Note that if
{\mathcal{Q}}
is reducible (class
(
2
) of
Proposition
2.2
), then it is the union of two
distinct rational hyperplanes and cannot have all its rational
points inside a hyperplane. Suppose now that
{\mathcal{Q}}
is not a double
hyperplane, is absolutely irreducible and
{\mathcal{Q}}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})
is contained in
some hyperplane
{\mathcal{L}}
of
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
. Let
P\in{\mathcal{Q}}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})
, then any line

\ell
such that
P\in\ell
and
\ell\not\subset{\mathcal{L}}
intersects

{\mathcal{L}}
only at
P
. In particular,
{\mathcal{Q}}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})\cap\ell=\{P\}
. Thus by Lemma
3.1
, it intersects

{\mathcal{Q}}
only at
P
and any such line is tangent to
{\mathcal{Q}}
at
P
.
Since the span of these lines is
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
itself, we deduce that

{\mathsf{T}}_{P}{\mathcal{Q}}
is not a hyperplane and hence that
P
is a singular point
of
{\mathcal{Q}}
. Thus,

{\mathcal{Q}}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})\subset{\rm{Sing}\hskip 1.42262pt}({\mathcal{Q}}).

Since
{\mathcal{Q}}
is absolutely irreducible and is a cone over a smooth quadric in
{\mathbb{P}}^{r-1}
for
r\geqslant 3
by Proposition
2.9
,
{\mathcal{Q}}
has a smooth rational point, a contradiction.
∎

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
{\mathcal{Q}}
and
{\mathcal{Q}}^{\prime}
be two quadrics in
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
such that

{\mathcal{Q}}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})\subset{\mathcal{Q}}^{\prime}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})
and
{\mathcal{Q}}
is absolutely irreducible. Then

{\rm{Sing}\hskip 1.42262pt}({\mathcal{Q}})\subset{\rm{Sing}\hskip 1.42262pt}({\mathcal{Q}}^{\prime})
.

Proof.

Since singular loci are linear spaces, if

{\rm{Sing}\hskip 1.42262pt}({\mathcal{Q}})({\mathbb{F}_{q}})\subset{\rm{Sing}\hskip 1.42262pt}({\mathcal{Q}}^{\prime})({\mathbb{F}_{q}})
, then

{\rm{Sing}\hskip 1.42262pt}({\mathcal{Q}})\subset{\rm{Sing}\hskip 1.42262pt}({\mathcal{Q}}^{\prime})
. Therefore, we only have to prove the
statement on rational points.

Let
P\in{\rm{Sing}\hskip 1.42262pt}({\mathcal{Q}})({\mathbb{F}_{q}})
and suppose that

P\notin{\rm{Sing}\hskip 1.42262pt}({\mathcal{Q}}^{\prime})
. Then
{\mathcal{Q}}
is smooth at
P
and the tangent space

{\mathsf{T}}_{P}{\mathcal{Q}}^{\prime}
is a hyperplane. Since

{\mathcal{Q}}
is absolutely irreducible, from
Lemma
3.2
the rational points of
{\mathcal{Q}}
cannot be
all contained in
{\mathsf{T}}_{P}{\mathcal{Q}}^{\prime}
. Hence, let
P_{2}\in{\mathcal{Q}}({\mathbb{F}_{q}})
and
