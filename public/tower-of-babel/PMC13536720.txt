Cellular prion protein and its derived peptides: multifaceted roles in neurodegenerative diseases and potential as biomarkers
Authors: Emanuela Mari, Michele Aventaggiato, Emanuela Paldino, Simona Ceccarelli, Nicola Mucci, Giovanni Bellomo, Elisabetta Mantuano, Stefano Dugheri, Stefano Martellucci, Vincenzo Mattei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), Huntington’s disease (HD), and transmissible spongiform encephalopathies (TSEs), share fundamental mechanisms of protein misfolding, synaptic dysfunction, and progressive neuronal loss. The cellular prion protein (PrPC), long viewed through the lens of prion biology, is now recognized as a pleiotropic modulator of neuronal physiology and a central interaction hub for amyloidogenic proteins. PrPC undergoes tightly regulated proteolytic processing (α-, β-, γ-cleavage, and ectodomain shedding), generating soluble fragments and peptides with distinct and sometimes opposing biological activities. These derivatives modulate neurotrophic signaling, oxidative stress responses, and the uptake/toxicity of Alzheimer’s disease-relevant soluble amyloid-β (Aβ), α‑synuclein, and tau. Recent work highlights PrPC as a ligand for low-density lipoprotein receptor‑related protein‑1 (LRP1) and the NMDA receptor (NMDAR), with shed PrPC and PrP‑derived peptides capable of initiating cell signaling and attenuating inflammatory responses, including when PrPC is delivered via extracellular vesicles. In parallel, ultrasensitive seed amplification assays (SAA; RT‑QuIC/PMCA) have transformed prion diagnostics and underscore the biomarker potential of both full-length PrPC and of its proteolytic fragments. Here, we synthesize current knowledge on PrPC structure–function relationships, proteolytic processing, and crosstalk with disease‑ associated protein aggregates. We also outline emerging translational opportunities, ranging from PrP‑derived peptide therapeutics to fluid-based biomarkers that track disease onset and progression.

Introduction

Neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), Huntington’s disease (HD), and transmissible spongiform encephalopathies (TSEs) share convergent biological themes: protein misfolding, accumulation of insoluble aggregates, synaptic failure, and progressive neuronal loss [1, 2]. Following the seminal “protein-only” hypothesis proposed by Prusiner, prion research revealed that conformational templating mechanisms underlie not only infectious prion disorders but also the prion-like propagation observed for Amyloid-β and Tau in AD and for α-synuclein in PD [3–5]. Within this pathological continuum, the cellular prion protein (PrPC) has emerged as a multifunctional, GPI-anchored glycoprotein highly expressed at the neuronal surface, particularly at synapses. Structurally, PrPC features a flexible N-terminal domain containing copper-binding octarepeats and a globular C-terminal region that mediates protein–protein interactions [6–8]. Beyond its conformational conversion into the pathogenic isoform PrPSc in TSEs, PrPC participates in diverse physiological processes, including synaptic homeostasis, neuroprotection, signal transduction, cell adhesion, stem cell differentiation, and apoptosis [3, 9, 10]. These wide-ranging physiological functions highlight the dual nature of PrPᶜ, which can support neuronal integrity under normal conditions but may contribute to neurodegenerative mechanisms when its structure, processing, or localization becomes dysregulated. A key functional aspect of PrPC biology lies in its association with lipid rafts, cholesterol, and sphingolipid-rich microdomains that organize signaling complexes. Although the specific molecular pathways involving raft-dependent signaling, receptor interactions, and intracellular trafficking are discussed in detail in later sections, their relevance is introduced here as part of the broader conceptual framework linking PrPC to neuronal communication and vulnerability. PrPC’s ability to engage in dynamic signaling networks positions it as a potential modulator of both physiological and pathological processes. Alterations in PrPC’s normal functions, such as its contributions to synaptic plasticity, neuroprotective responses, and cellular stress regulation, may facilitate the transition from a protective to a pathogenic role, thereby connecting its physiological activities to prion-like propagation and neurodegeneration. This conceptual link provides a unifying perspective for understanding PrPC involvement across multiple disease contexts. In parallel, advances in ultrasensitive amplification assays and biomarker studies have expanded the translational relevance of PrP biology, offering new opportunities for early diagnosis and mechanistic insight [11–15]. These developments further highlight the importance of integrating structural, functional, and pathological aspects of PrPC into a coherent framework. The present review provides a comprehensive synthesis of PrPC structure, proteolytic processing, and derived peptides, elucidating their multifaceted roles in neurodegenerative disease mechanisms. Special attention is given to how PrP-derived fragments and PrPC−mediated signaling pathways contribute to both neuronal protection and disease-associated dysfunction, emphasizing their emerging relevance as biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

The cellular prion protein: structure and physiological functions

Structure and physiological expression

PrPC is a glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored glycoprotein encoded by the PRNP gene, predominantly expressed in the central nervous system, with particularly high levels at synaptic membranes [6, 8, 16]. Structurally, PrPC comprises a flexible, unstructured N-terminal region containing octapeptide repeats that bind divalent cations such as copper, and a globular C-terminal domain characterized by three α-helices and two short β-strands [7]. This conformational plasticity is thought to underlie a wide range of physiological roles [17, 18]. Indeed, PrPC participates in many physiological processes, including regulation of synaptic transmission, neuroprotection, copper and zinc homeostasis, myelin maintenance, and modulation of cell signaling pathways [19]. Although PRNP knockout mice do not exhibit overt neurodevelopmental abnormalities, they display measurable phenotypic alterations, including impaired synaptic function, altered myelination, and increased susceptibility to cellular stress [20]. PrPC also plays a role in cell adhesion, axonal growth, neurite outgrowth, and glial differentiation, and acts as a signaling platform influencing cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival [10, 21]. In the immune system, it is involved in immune cell activation and inflammatory regulation [22]. Finally, it also participates in stem cell renewal, including hematopoietic stem cells [21].

Neuroprotective roles and synaptic function

PrPC plays a pivotal role in maintaining neuronal homeostasis and synaptic integrity. Its functions include regulation of copper metabolism, antioxidant defense, and participation in neurotrophic signaling pathways [23, 24]. Experimental models have shown that PrPC supports neurite outgrowth, synapse formation, and long-term potentiation (LTP) processes that are essential for memory and learning [25, 26]. In line with these functions, PRNP-knockout mice display heightened vulnerability to oxidative stress, excitotoxic stimuli, and neurotoxic injury, confirming the protein’s essential neuroprotective role [27, 28]. Moreover, soluble derivatives of PrPC have been shown to activate intracellular signaling pathways through interactions with the low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1) and the N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR), further influencing neuronal physiology and survival [29]. In addition, PrPC and LRP1 form part of a multimolecular signaling complex localized in lipid rafts, which is essential for the physiological activity of tissue plasminogen activator (tPA), underscoring the importance of membrane compartmentalization in PrPC-mediated functions [30].

Interactions with other misfolded proteins (Aβ, α-synuclein, tau)

Beyond its physiological roles, PrPC acts as a high-affinity receptor or co-receptor for several pathogenic protein oligomers. The binding of Alzheimer’s disease-relevant soluble amyloid-β (Aβ) oligomers to PrPC has been shown to impair synaptic plasticity and contribute to Alzheimer’s disease (AD) pathogenesis [31, 32]. Similarly, PrPC interacts with α-synuclein assemblies, modulating their internalization and associated toxicity in Parkinson’s disease (PD) models [33, 34]. Moreover, recent evidence suggests that PrPC also participates in tau-related pathology, where it may influence tau hyperphosphorylation and aggregation, thereby exacerbating neurodegeneration [35]. Taken together, these findings position PrPC as a molecular hub at the interface of multiple neurodegenerative pathways. Depending on cellular context and binding partners, PrPC can exert either protective or detrimental effects, underscoring its complex and dualistic role in neurodegenerative disease mechanisms. In addition to its direct interaction with misfolded proteins and extracellular ligands, PrPC is also shaped by post-translational processing at the cell surface, where proteolytic cleavage regulates its membrane localization and function while generating soluble fragments with distinct biological roles.

Proteolytic processing and shedding of the cellular prion protein: mechanisms and roles of ADAM proteases

PrPC proteolytic cleavage

The release of PrPC from the cell membrane can occur through multiple mechanisms, including proteolytic cleavage and exosomal release. The discovery of the PrPC sequence and structure led to a deeper understanding of the protein cleavage mechanism and to the identification of the sites involved in this process. Four types of proteolytic cleavage appear to be conserved and are currently the subject of studies aimed at determining the roles of the resulting fragments in physiological and pathological conditions (Fig. 1) [36].

α-cleavage

α-cleavage of PrPC results in the production of two fragments: the N-terminal fragment (N1) and the C-terminal fragment (C1). Under physiological conditions this cleavage occurs within the central hydrophobic domain at Lys110-His111 or His111-Met112 in humans while, in other mammals, it can affect amino acid residues 105–120, with a high resistance to amino acid substitution in these sites [37, 38]. The C1 fragment (~ 16 kDa) is associated with the membrane fraction in a glycosylated and glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored form and is characterized by acidic properties and heat resistance. The N1 fragment, instead (~ 11 kDa), is a soluble fraction released in the extracellular space [37, 39]. The most important and well-studied proteases connected to PrPC α-cleavage (α-PrPases) belong to a disintegrin and metalloproteinase (ADAMs) family. In particular, ADAM10 is involved in the constitutive production of N1 fragment in human brain, mouse models and neuronal cultures, while ADAM17 participates in N1 production upon stimulation [40]. Furthermore, ADAM9 indirectly contributes to N1 production via ADAM10 [41]. Further investigations are necessary to better understand the exact role of these three proteases in PrPC α-cleavage and also to evaluate the putative involvement of other proteases in N1 fragment production as well as the subcellular localization of this process [42, 43]. To this regard, several studies identified endosomes as the preferred compartment for α-cleavage due to their acidic properties [44] but also cholesterol-rich membrane microdomains [45] and Golgi [46, 47].

β-cleavage

β-cleavage of PrPC mainly occurs in pathological conditions at the end of the octapeptide repeat region of the α-cleavage site. It takes place around amino acid residue 90, resulting in the production of a N2 fragment (~ 9 kDa) and a C2 fragment (~ 20 kDa, depending on the glycosylation status) [38, 48]. The N2 fragment is released in correspondence with the C2 fragment which remains anchored to the cell membrane. The C2 fragment obtained from β-cleavage is similar to the protease resistant core of the disease-associated misfolded isoform PrPSc, known as PrP27–30. Given that high levels of the C2 fragment are detected in the brains of individuals with prion diseases, it can be conceived that the C2 fragment can be converted into the pathogenic PrPSc isoform. PrPC β-cleavage can occur in an enzymatic manner, mediated by calpains [49], lysosomal proteases [50, 51] and ADAM8 [52], but also in a non-enzymatic manner, mediated by reactive oxygen species (ROS) [48, 53]. ROS-mediated β-cleavage of PrPC is characterized by a rapid cleavage of the anchorless PrP in the presence of Cu2+ and H2O2 or superoxide, originating an unstable N2 fragment of ~ 6.5 kDa [54]. This process represents a protective response against oxidative stress in pathological conditions [54, 55] but also, under physiological conditions, a stimulus to activate signaling pathways that can counteract the damage caused by oxidative stress [56–58]. Enzymatic PrPC β-cleavage remains under investigation, although a role for cathepsin in C2Sc production has been highlighted [59]. Watt et al. in their study demonstrate that PrPC β-cleavage producing C2 fragment occurs at the cell surface [53] while N-terminal truncation of PrPSc can take place in endosomes or lysosomes [60].

γ-cleavage

γ-Cleavage represents the most recently discovered mechanism of PrPC protease-mediated cleavage. The details of this process and its pathophysiological implication are not yet well understood, but it causes the release of a 20 kDa N3 fragment and a GPI-anchored C3 fragment of ~ 5 kDa with a cleavage site in the region between amino acids residues 170 and 200 on the C-terminal side of the final N-glycosylation site (Asn197 in humans) [11–63]. Of note, PrPC glycosylation status can prevent and inhibit the activity of matrix metalloproteases [61, 64], although it has been suggested that the C3 fragment could be produced in the Golgi [61].

Shedding mechanism and soluble PrPC release

Stahl et al. in 1990 identified a cleavage site in the region strictly close to the cell membrane, resulting in the formation of a long fragment, termed “shed PrP” (sPrP), and a short fragment consisting of the GPI anchor and a few amino acids behind [65–67]. In humans, fragments of PrP can be shed from the cell surface via ADAM10-mediated cleavage at Tyr226-Gln227 [68]. The C1 and C2 fragments, derived from α- and β-cleavage, are shed from the cell membrane, thereby increasing the variety of PrPC-derived proteolytic fragments [43, 69]. However, the presence of a long and flexible membrane-interacting N-terminal tail in full-length PrPC promotes the access and proteolytic activity of ADAM10, which occurs more efficiently than in α or β-cleavages [70, 71], suggesting that the proteolytic site in the human sequence is located in the proximity of the C-terminus and not exclusively limited at the amino acid residues 228 and 229 [72].

Regulation of soluble PrPC release

PrPC plays a dual role in several brain diseases, not only in its full-length membrane-anchored form, but also through the fragments proteolytically originated by the different cleavage mechanisms previously described [73]. Several studies have identified an involvement of PrPC fragments exposed on Extracellular Vesicles (EVs) in the onset or prevention of diseases affecting the central nervous system, although a pivotal role in this context is played by sPrP [74]. Furthermore, the PrPC N-terminal tail represents a promising site for interaction with several proteins, such as neural cell adhesion molecule 1 (NCAM1), LRP-1, cadherins, and amyloid precursor protein, which are, in turn, substrates of ADAM10 [75–77]. Considering this evidence, the shedding process might be positively/negatively regulated by other interactors through the establishment of a transient protein complex [78]. Since PrPC cleavage occurs on the cell membrane surface, high levels of this protein cause an increase in its shedding, with a consequent release of sPrP involved in signal transduction, synapse formation, and neuroprotection.

Characterization of the peptides derived from PrPC

The characterization of peptides derived from PrPC has been pivotal in elucidating its complex structure-function relationship and dual roles in neuroprotection and neurotoxicity [79–81]. These peptides, often corresponding to proteolytic fragments or synthetic analogues of specific domains, are studied using a combination of biophysical, biochemical, and cell-based assays [63, 82, 83]. The generation of these fragments is a key regulatory mechanism that modulates PrPC function.

Identification of bioactive PrPC fragments

Bioactive PrPC fragments are specific pieces of the PrPC that are generated through normal or aberrant proteolytic cleavage [71]. Unlike the full-length protein, these fragments often possess distinct, and sometimes opposing, biological activities, both physiological and pathological (Fig. 2), such as stimulation of cell adhesion, PrPSc formation, interaction with other proteins, and modulation of signaling [84–86]. Alterations in PrPC-derived fragments have also been observed during opiate withdrawal, which induces a time-dependent and region-specific modification in PrPC content. After its synthesis, as part of its post-translational metabolism, PrPC can undergo different kinds of proteolytic cleavage, in at least three sites, yielding fragments from both N-terminal and C-terminal sides [87]. The enzyme responsible for PrPC cleavage may not be unique, and several members of the ADAMs family have been implicated [73]. As described above, the α-cleavage occurs between residues 111/112 to yield a soluble N-terminal fragment called N1 and a GPI-anchored, C-terminal fragment called C1. The N1/C1 cleavage occurs constitutively in 10–50% of the molecules, but it can be stimulated by several activators. For example, opiate withdrawal leads to α-cleavage of the PrPC hydrophobic domain and the production of N1/C1 fragments [88]. There is disagreement about the cellular site and the proteases responsible for α-cleavage, with endosomal/lysosomal compartments, late compartments of the secretory pathway, and the cell surface (mediated by ADAMs) all having been suggested [7, 89, 90]. The alternative β-cleavage occurs between residues 89/90, generating a soluble N2 fragment and a GPI-anchored C2 fragment. It occurs at low levels under normal conditions, possibly catalyzed by ROS acting on cell-surface PrP, but it is enhanced during generation of PrPSc [37, 53, 91]. Finally, the γ-cleavage occurs near the site of GPI anchor attachment (residue 230), resulting in the release of most of the polypeptide chain into the extracellular medium [16, 67, 93]. Additional proteolytic cleavages may also occur at low levels [53].

Structural features and biological effects of PrP fragments

The N1 fragment contains the PrP octapeptide repeats, which are involved in several biological processes, such as copper binding and copper homeostasis [93]. The main Cu2+ binding activity is encoded in the PrP N-terminal domain, whereby four histidine residues (H61, H69, H77, and H85; human protein residue numbering) within the octarepeat region (60 to 91; four PHGGGWGQ tandem repeats) and two nearby histidines (H96 and H111) coordinate copper ions (at pH ~ 7) [94]. The N1 fragment has been shown to have potent neuroprotective effects. It can play a regulatory role in microglial function in the context of an interconnected network of cells by changing cellular interaction sites, cytokine secretion and anti-apoptotic properties [95]. It can also protect neurons from toxic insults and counteract the toxic effects of the β-cleaved fragment, binding with amyloid-β oligomers and promoting neuronal survival. The C1 fragment function is less clear, but it may be involved in metal ion homeostasis. Three C-terminal histidines (H140, H177 and H186) were shown to bind Cu2+, thereby tethering PrP N- and C-terminal domains and promoting their interaction [96]. Burns et al. analyzed PrP-derived peptides and demonstrated an additional C-terminal copper-binding site in the flexible region [97]. Heske et al. analyzed the ER import of PrP in vivo and identified a new regulatory element within the C-terminal domain of PrP. They showed that complete or partial deletions of this domain abolish or reduce the import of PrP into the ER and give rise to an N-terminally unprocessed PrP species, which is degraded by the proteasome. Consequently, they observed that the ER import of two pathogenic PrP mutants linked to inherited prion diseases in humans, W145Stop and Q160Stop, was compromised, with only a fraction being fully translocated into the ER and the other fraction, containing an uncleaved signal peptide, remaining in the cytosol [98].

Interaction of PrP fragments with pathological aggregates and cellular membranes

Although the molecular mechanisms are still unclear, it has been shown that PrP fragments interact with pathological aggregates and cellular membranes [99]. The N-terminal fragments can bind Aβ oligomers (Aβo) and regulate their neurotoxicity and neuroprotection in vitro and in vivo. The same region of PrP mediates the potential protective effect of the protein in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) (through inhibition of β-secretase cleavage of the amyloid precursor protein and subsequent decreased production of Aβo) and its toxic role (through acting as a high-affinity receptor for Aβ42 oligomers that mediate the long-term potentiation blockage). This dual role is highly intriguing, suggesting that there may be a subtle balance between these effects that is controlled by additional cellular cofactors [31, 100]. Also, the Aβo- PrPC coupling leads to Fyn activation, intracellular calcium, and protein translation [32, 101]. Several studies extended their focus to validate the hypothesis that PrPC mediates toxic signaling induced by aggregates of proteins other than Aβ, such as tau and α-synuclein. Soluble aggregates of α-synuclein and tau bind to plate-immobilized PrP in vitro and on mouse cortical neurons, and this binding requires at least one of the same N-terminal sites at which soluble Aβ aggregates bind. Also, soluble aggregates of tau and α-synuclein, as well as Aβo, cause both functional (impairment of LTP) and structural (neuritic dystrophy) compromise, and these deficits are absent when PrP is ablated, knocked down, or when neurons are pre-treated with anti-PrP blocking antibodies [102]. Speculated interaction of membrane-type matrix metalloproteases with PrPC mediates its recently described γ-cleavage, resulting in a large N3 (~ 20 kDa) and a small C3 fragment (~ 6 kDa) [84]. The γ-cleavage predominantly targets unglycosylated PrPC, possibly while the PrPC molecules are still in the Golgi and trans-Golgi network, as well as in endocytic recycling compartments. The pathophysiological involvement of the N3 and C3 fragments is yet to be identified. However, the increased presence of C3 fragments in Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease brains points to their potential role in the pathophysiology of this disease [61].

Role of PrP and its derived peptides in neurodegenerative diseases

Prion diseases: pathogenesis and strain-specific tropism

Prions are infectious agents composed of misfolded protein, PrPSc, capable of inducing the normal cellular protein, PrPC, to misfold. This triggers a chain reaction of protein aggregation that leads to the formation of plaques in the brain, disrupting neuronal function and promoting cell death. This process underlies a group of fatal neurodegenerative disorders collectively known as Transmissible Spongiform Encephalopathies (TSEs). These diseases are characterized by progressive neurodegeneration, spongiform changes in the brain, and the absence of a typical inflammatory response. Well-known examples include Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (CJD) in humans, bovine spongiform encephalopathy (BSE) in cattle, and scrapie in sheep [103]. Unlike viruses, bacteria, or fungi, prions are exceptional in that they lack nucleic acids and propagate exclusively through protein conformational templating, a mechanism that challenges traditional definitions of infectious agents. Because of their rapid clinical progression and the absence of effective treatments, prion diseases remain among the most devastating neurological disorders known. Ongoing research seeks to elucidate the molecular mechanisms of prion replication and to develop therapeutic strategies that either inhibit PrPSc misfolding, block PrPSc aggregation, or enhance the clearance of pathogenic species [1]. The discovery of prions has profoundly transformed the understanding of neurodegenerative diseases, providing a paradigm for how misfolded proteins can self-propagate and spread pathology across the nervous system. It introduced the concept of “prion-like” propagation, in which misfolded proteins spread their abnormal conformation to native counterparts, leading to progressive accumulation and neurotoxicity. This mechanism has since been implicated in several common disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease (Aβ and tau aggregation), Parkinson’s disease (α-synuclein aggregates), Huntington’s disease (mutant huntingtin), and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) (TDP-43 or SOD1 aggregates) [5]. Understanding the molecular parallels between prion diseases and these proteinopathies offers promising avenues for the development of broad-spectrum therapies aimed at preventing or reversing protein misfolding and aggregation across multiple neurodegenerative conditions.

Alzheimer’s disease: PrPC, amyloidogenic protein propagation, and derived fragments

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and prion diseases share the core mechanisms of protein misfolding and aggregation. In AD, amyloid-β (Aβ) accumulates in extracellular plaques, whereas hyperphosphorylated tau accumulates in intracellular neurofibrillary tangles, while in CJD, PrPC converts to pathogenic PrPSc. In both, small amounts of misfolded protein act as “seeds,” inducing the misfolding of normal proteins and spreading pathology through the brain. This prion-like propagation explains the predictable progression of AD, where tau pathology spreads from the entorhinal cortex to other regions [103]. Animal studies support this mechanism: brain extracts from AD patients induce Aβ plaques and cognitive decline in mice, demonstrating “seeded polymerization” [104]. Unlike classical prion diseases, AD is not infectious; “prion-like” refers only to its propagation mechanism. Therapeutic strategies focus on blocking misfolding and spreading. Immunotherapy, particularly anti-Aβ antibodies, has reduced plaques and improved cognition in AD mouse models. Recently, three monoclonal antibodies targeting distinct Aβ conformations have been FDA-approved after showing slowed disease progression in patients: Aducanumab, Lecanemab and Donanemab [105–107]. These therapies differ significantly in their conformational selectivity and clinical status. Aducanumab, which binds to both oligomers and fibrils, was discontinued by Biogen in early 2024 to reprioritize resources toward other modalities. Lecanemab selectively targets large soluble protofibrils, while Donanemab specifically recognizes the insoluble N-truncated pyroglutamate localized in the established core plaques. Both Lecanemab and Donanemab remain fully approved for the treatment of early-stage Alzheimer’s disease after successfully demonstrating a reduction in clinical and cognitive decline [108]. Importantly, the impact of PrPC on pathogenesis is strictly dependent on its topological state and the proteolytic cleavage process it undergoes. While intact, membrane-bound PrPC acts as a receptor that transduces the toxic signals of oligomers into the cell, its physiological processing generates the soluble amino-terminal fragment N1 (Fig. 3). This fragment exerts a neuroprotective role by acting as a circulating decoy receptor that sequesters Aβ plaques in the extracellular space, thereby neutralizing their ability to bind to synaptic membranes and preventing impairment of neuronal plasticity. In particular, the neuroprotective efficacy of the N1 fragment lies in the conservation of the polycationic domains (residues 23–27 and 95–110), which have a high affinity for Aβ oligomers. While in full-length PrPC, these domains facilitate synaptotoxicity through the activation of the Fyn-tau pathway, in the N1 fragment, they act as a ‘molecular trap’, preventing the docking of oligomers to surface receptors and the consequent prion-mediated toxicity [108].

Parkinson’s disease and α-synuclein

Parkinson’s disease (PD) shares key pathogenic features with prion disorders, particularly protein misfolding and seeded aggregation. While the Braak hypothesis describes a stereotypical propagation of α-synuclein pathology from peripheral to central nervous system structures (109), increasing evidence highlights a more direct mechanistic role of PrPC in modulating α-synuclein toxicity (Fig. 3). Specifically, PrPC acts as a receptor or co-receptor for α-synuclein oligomers, facilitating their internalization and triggering downstream neurotoxic signaling pathways [33, 34]. This interaction contributes to synaptic dysfunction and cognitive impairment, positioning PrPC as an active mediator rather than a passive bystander in PD pathogenesis. Furthermore, the prion-like seeding properties of α-synuclein, similarly to PrPSc, have been successfully exploited in seed amplification assays, reinforcing the conceptual and mechanistic overlap between prion diseases and synucleinopathies [110]. Moreover, PrPC is a glycoprotein anchored to the neuronal membrane, widely expressed in the central nervous system, and involved in various synaptic and cell signaling processes. In recent studies, its possible role in the interaction with pathological aggregates of α-synuclein, a key protein in the pathogenesis of synucleinopathies, including Parkinson’s disease, has been hypothesized. Experimental studies have shown that α-synuclein oligomers can bind to PrPC on the neuronal surface, suggesting that this protein may act as a receptor or co-receptor for neurotoxic oligomeric species. This interaction appears to facilitate the activation of intracellular signaling pathways associated with synaptic dysfunction, with consequent alteration of glutamatergic transmission and potential neurotoxicity [33]. Furthermore, it has been proposed that PrPC may contribute to the prion-like propagation of α-synuclein aggregates, promoting the adhesion and internalization of amyloid fibrils in neuronal cells and thus the spread of the pathology between brain regions [34, 111]. However, the role of PrPC in this process remains a matter of debate, since some studies indicate that intercellular transmission of α-synuclein may also occur through mechanisms independent of this membrane protein, suggesting the existence of alternative cellular uptake pathways [112]. In this context, the role of PrPC proteolytic processing is increasingly recognized as a modulatory factor. Specific PrP-derived fragments may influence α-synuclein uptake, aggregation, and toxicity, although their precise contribution remains to be fully elucidated. Alterations in PrPC cleavage patterns could therefore represent an additional layer regulating prion-like propagation mechanisms in Parkinson’s disease.

Huntington’s disease and prion-like mechanisms

HD is an autosomal dominant neurodegenerative disease caused by the expansion of CAG triplets in the HTT gene, resulting in the production of a mutated form of the huntingtin protein (mHTT) containing an expanded polyglutamine tract. This mutation results in a misfolded protein that aggregates to form intracellular inclusions and amyloid fibrils rich in β-sheet structures, a process similar to that observed in prion diseases, in which the PrPC converts into the pathological form PrPSc, leading to the accumulation of neurotoxic aggregates [103, 113]. Mutated huntingtin protein has “prion-like” properties; in fact, mHTT aggregates can act as aggregation nuclei capable of inducing the misfolding of native huntingtin molecules, promoting the progressive formation of new aggregates [5]. Several studies have shown that huntingtin aggregates can spread between neuronal and glial cells, likely through synapses, endocytosis/exocytosis, intercellular nanotubes, or extracellular vesicles such as exosomes, suggesting a mechanism of intercellular propagation of the disease in the central nervous system [113, 114]. Because mHTT propagation shares with prions a dependence on ß-sheet-rich protein conformations, pharmacological strategies originally developed for prion diseases that destabilize these structures offer a promising translational approach for Huntington’s disease. Among these pharmacological strategies, tetracyclines have been investigated as potential antiprion agents due to their ability to interact directly with the ß-sheet-rich regions of the pathological prion protein [115, 116]. Biochemical and biophysical studies have demonstrated that molecules belonging to the tetracycline family, particularly doxycycline, can bind prion protein aggregates and modify their structural properties. In experimental in vitro models, these compounds interfere with fibrillogenesis of PrP-derived peptides, destabilize amyloid structures, and reduce the resistance of the pathological form of PrPSc to proteolytic digestion. Clinical studies reported prolonged survival in patients with early Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease treated with doxycycline [117, 118, 119]. Based on these findings, Paldino et al. evaluated doxycycline in the R6/2 mouse model of HD, highlighting its potential to mitigate both protein aggregation and neuroinflammation [120].

Critical analysis

A major unresolved aspect of PrPC biology concerns its context-dependent dual role as both a neuroprotective and a neurotoxic factor. While α-cleavage-derived fragments, particularly the soluble N1 peptide, are consistently associated with neuroprotective activities such as antioxidant responses, modulation of neuroinflammation, and sequestration of toxic amyloidogenic oligomers, β-cleavage products, including the C2 fragment, are more frequently linked to pathological conditions characterized by oxidative stress and prion conversion [37, 53, 58]. The relative balance between these proteolytic pathways appears to be tightly regulated by multiple factors, including membrane lipid composition, ADAM protease activity, intracellular trafficking, and reactive oxygen species levels.

Importantly, proteolytic processing of PrPC may critically influence its interactions with misfolded proteins involved in neurodegenerative diseases. Membrane-bound full-length PrPC can function as a receptor or co-receptor for oligomeric Aβ, α-synuclein, and tau, thereby facilitating toxic intracellular signaling cascades that impair synaptic plasticity and neuronal survival [31, 32, 102]. In contrast, soluble PrP-derived fragments generated through physiological cleavage pathways may exert protective functions by acting as extracellular decoys capable of neutralizing pathogenic aggregates before receptor engagement. These observations suggest that alterations in PrPC processing may shift the balance between protective buffering mechanisms and propagation of neurotoxic signaling.

This mechanistic complexity is also reflected in biomarker studies. Reduced cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) PrP levels may result from neuronal loss, sequestration of PrP into insoluble aggregates, or impaired secretion from damaged neurons, whereas increased plasma PrP concentrations may reflect blood–brain barrier dysfunction, peripheral release from non-neuronal cells, or systemic inflammatory responses [14, 15, 121]. Therefore, CSF and plasma PrP likely represent biologically distinct compartments rather than interchangeable biomarkers.

Taken together, these findings underscore the need for future studies integrating proteolytic profiling, extracellular vesicle-associated PrP characterization, and seed amplification technologies to better define the functional and diagnostic significance of PrPC-derived species across neurodegenerative diseases. A deeper understanding of how proteolytic processing shapes PrPC signaling, and aggregation dynamics may ultimately facilitate the development of both mechanism-based biomarkers and targeted therapeutic interventions.

PrP and its peptides as potential biomarkers in neurodegenerative disorders

Detection of PrP in biofluids

The main applications of PrP as a fluid biomarker are primarily related to seed amplification assays (SAA), which were developed from two key methodological breakthroughs: protein misfolding cyclic amplification (PMCA) [11] and real-time quaking-induced conversion (RT-QuIC) [21].

These ultrasensitive techniques exploit the capacity of PrPSc to induce the conformational conversion of its normal cellular isoform PrPC, thereby enabling the amplification and detection of minute quantities of pathogenic seeds in biological samples.

PMCA relies on iterative cycles of incubation and sonication of PrPSc (originally derived from animal tissue, now typically recombinant) in the presence of patient-derived samples, which promotes exponential amplification of PrPSc aggregates.

RT-QuIC, instead, uses recombinant PrPC as substrate in multiwell plates subjected to shaking and incubation, monitoring the aggregation process in real time with the fluorescent dye Thioflavin-T, which binds with high affinity to the cross-β-sheet motifs characteristic of fibrillar PrPSc.

In addition to their application in primary prion disorders, immunodetected soluble total PrP in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and blood plasma has also shown diagnostic potential in a wider spectrum of neurological disorders, extending the relevance of PrP beyond classic prion diseases.

Diagnostic potential and limitations

Following extensive clinical validation, RT-QuIC has become a robust and standardized diagnostic assay for CJD. It is now incorporated into formal diagnostic criteria [22] due to its high sensitivity and near-absolute specificity across multiple biological matrices, including CSF [122], olfactory mucosa [123], and skin [124]. In parallel, PMCA-based assays are predominantly applied for the detection of variant CJD, where they achieve sensitivities approaching 100% in CSF, olfactory mucosa, blood, and urine [125]. Despite their outstanding diagnostic accuracy, RT-QuIC and PMCA face important practical limitations that currently restrict their widespread clinical implementation. The assays are technically demanding, requiring specialized laboratory equipment, trained personnel, and stringent standardization of reagents and procedures to ensure reproducibility. Moreover, because of the potential infectivity of prion-containing samples, biosafety level 3 containment is generally required, which limits their accessibility to a small number of reference or research laboratories per country. Apart from seed amplification-based assays, immunodetected soluble total PrP has shown diagnostic potential for different neurodegenerative disorders. In plasma, total PrP concentrations are substantially increased in association with nonspecific neurodegenerative processes such as AD, frontotemporal dementia (FTD), and dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), mirroring the trends seen for total tau and neurofilament light chain, with the highest levels reported in prion disorders [15]. Conversely, CSF total PrP appears consistently reduced in these same conditions relative to control subjects [14, 120]. This compartment-specific divergence likely reflects distinct underlying mechanisms, which yet need to be elucidated. In the central nervous system, lower CSF PrP concentrations may result from neuronal loss, sequestration into pathological aggregates, or impaired secretion of the protein. In contrast, elevated plasma PrP levels may stem from blood–brain barrier dysfunction, axonal leakage, or peripheral release from non-neuronal sources such as platelets. Overall, while SAA-based assays represent definitive diagnostic tools for prion diseases, the broader application of soluble PrP quantification to other neurodegenerative conditions remains an area of investigation. Beyond seed amplification assays and total PrP quantification, increasing attention is being directed toward the potential of specific PrP-derived fragments and extracellular vesicle (EV)-associated PrP as fluid biomarkers. Proteolytic processing of PrP generates distinct fragments (e.g., N1, N2, C1, C2, and shed PrP) whose relative abundance may reflect underlying pathogenic mechanisms, including oxidative stress, altered protease activity, and prion conversion processes [36, 37, 48, 71]. For instance, the accumulation of C2 fragments has been associated with prion diseases, whereas shifts in N-terminal fragment production may indicate neuroprotective or stress-responsive pathways [37, 53, 58]. In parallel, PrP species associated with EVs offer a promising avenue to capture disease-relevant intercellular signaling and the propagation of misfolded protein states [126, 127]. Although these approaches remain largely exploratory, they may provide complementary information to seed amplification assays by linking molecular pathology with dynamic changes in PrP metabolism and cellular communication (Table 1).

Integration with molecular and imaging biomarkers

The diagnostic and mechanistic value of PrP RT-QuIC is maximized when integrated with molecular and imaging biomarkers. In clinical practice, PrP RT-QuIC is often interpreted alongside established biomarkers of neurodegeneration, including amyloid-β (Aβ), phosphorylated tau (p-tau), total tau, and neurofilament light chain (NfL).

In patients presenting with rapidly progressive dementia, a biomarker profile characterized by normal Aβ and p-tau levels, combined with elevated total tau or NfL and MRI findings not suggestive of major cerebrovascular pathology, may indicate a possible prion disease, which can then be confirmed by RT-QuIC [128–131]. Accordingly, updated diagnostic criteria for sporadic Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease incorporate RT-QuIC results together with established CSF and imaging biomarkers [132].

Conversely, in Alzheimer’s disease, the absence of PrP seeding activity combined with positive Aβ and p-tau signatures supports differential diagnosis from prion disorders. Emerging diagnostic strategies further extend this framework by incorporating α-synuclein seed amplification assays for synucleinopathies, enabling a multi-protein diagnostic approach [109].

In this context, PrP biomarkers may be part of an integrated panel that captures overlapping yet distinct proteinopathies. Future diagnostic strategies will likely rely on multimodal integration combining fluid biomarkers, seed amplification assays, and advanced imaging techniques, including MRI and PET, to improve early diagnosis, patient stratification, and monitoring of disease progression [133].

Therapeutic implications of PrP-derived peptides

PrP-derived peptides are emerging as promising therapeutic tools due to their ability to selectively modulate signaling pathways involved in neurodegeneration. Among these, the N1 fragment generated by α-cleavage has demonstrated neuroprotective properties, including inhibition of amyloid-β toxicity, modulation of microglial activation, and promotion of neuronal survival [95].

In parallel, specific short peptide motifs derived from PrPC, such as the LRP1-binding region (residues 97–110), have been shown to reproduce key signaling functions of the full-length protein, including activation of neurotrophic pathways and suppression of inflammatory responses [134]. These findings support the concept that functional domains of PrPC can be harnessed independently of the full protein to achieve targeted therapeutic effects.

However, several challenges remain. “Key challenges include peptide stability, efficient delivery across the blood–brain barrier, and the risk of off-target effects due to the pleiotropic nature of PrPC signaling. Moreover, given the dual role of PrPC in neuroprotection and neurotoxicity, therapeutic strategies must carefully consider context-dependent effects and avoid unintended activation of pathogenic pathways.

Current research focuses on optimizing peptide design, improving delivery systems, such as nanoparticle- or extracellular vesicle-based approaches, and validating efficacy in preclinical models [127]. While still at an early stage, PrP-derived peptides represent a compelling avenue for the development of mechanism-based therapies in neurodegenerative diseases.

To date, no PrP-targeted peptide therapies have advanced to late-stage clinical trials, highlighting the translational gap between mechanistic promise and clinical applicability.

Conclusion and perspectives

The cellular prion protein occupies a central position at the interface between membrane architecture, synaptic signaling, and neurodegeneration. Once considered solely in the context of transmissible spongiform encephalopathies, PrPC is now recognized as a dynamic orchestrator of neuronal communication, coordinating interactions among lipid rafts, extracellular vesicles, and postsynaptic receptor complexes [1]. Its capacity to alternate between trophic and toxic signaling emerges from a finely tuned equilibrium involving conformational dynamics, post-translational modifications, and proteolytic processing. Within this framework, neurodegeneration reflects a failure of endogenous homeostatic mechanisms rather than a purely exogenous pathogenic event.

Proteolytic processing of PrPC is a principal determinant of its functional diversity. The canonical α- and β-cleavages, together with ADAM10-mediated shedding, yield soluble fragments endowed with distinct, often opposing, biological properties. α-Cleavage releases N-terminal peptides that enhance synaptic stability and antioxidant defense, whereas β-cleavage predominates under oxidative stress, possibly representing a compensatory mechanism. The sheddase ADAM10 has been identified as a pivotal regulator of PrPC metabolism, controlling the balance between protective and pathogenic processing routes [135]. Recent reviews have refined the mapping of endoproteolytic sites, elucidating how cleavage patterns integrate with PrPC conformation and cellular stress signaling [136].

Complementary studies have highlighted how PrPC associates with lipid raft microdomains and mitochondrial membranes, suggesting a broader spatial and functional plasticity. Evidence indicates that PrPC can be released within microvesicles that mediate paracrine signaling and potentially propagate conformational states, while its recruitment to mitochondrial raft-like compartments contributes to stress-induced apoptotic cascades [16]. Together, these mechanistic insights converge on a model in which PrPC serves as a multifunctional communication hub that integrates redox homeostasis, receptor signaling, and intercellular transfer via extracellular vesicles.

Parallel progress in protein amplification technologies and structural interactomics is reshaping diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. Seed amplification assays, originally designed for prions, are now feasible to detect α-synuclein and tau aggregates with high sensitivity, underscoring mechanistic continuity across neurodegenerative disorders [109]. By combining such molecular diagnostics with quantitative assessments of PrP-derived fragments and biomarker-guided clinical endpoints, it becomes possible to link biochemical modulation of PrPC directly to functional outcomes.

Overall, PrPC exemplifies the conceptual evolution of neurodegenerative research, from descriptive pathology to precision molecular medicine. Mastery of its proteolytic regulation, receptor coupling, and vesicular dynamics may yield both diagnostic clarity and therapeutic leverage, transforming PrPC from a pathological hallmark into a target for functional restoration in prion and prion-like disorders.
