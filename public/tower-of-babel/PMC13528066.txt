Atypical genital-predominant mpox mimicking chancroid and herpes simplex virus infection: a case report and literature synthesis

Introduction

Human mpox is a zoonotic viral infection caused by an orthopoxvirus, historically endemic in parts of Central and West Africa [1]. In recent outbreaks, particularly during the 2022–2023 multi-country outbreak, mpox has shown significant changes in both epidemiology and clinical presentation, with an increasing number of cases presenting with genital and perineal lesions [2, 3]. These lesions often precede or occur in the absence of the classical generalised rash, thereby mimicking common sexually transmitted infections such as herpes simplex virus infection and chancroid [4].

Sexual transmission has emerged as a major route of infection during recent outbreaks, particularly through close skin-to-skin and mucosal contact [5]. In Nigeria and other endemic or previously endemic settings, atypical presentations and clinical overlap with common sexually transmitted infections may contribute to delayed recognition and underdiagnosis of mpox [4, 6]. This case emphasises a well-recognised diagnostic challenge and aligns with a growing body of literature describing atypical, genital-predominant mpox. It provides a detailed account of sequential misclassification—initially as herpes simplex virus infection and subsequently as chancroid—while prioritising the epidemiological relevance of partner history and the practical management difficulties encountered in a resource-limited Nigerian setting. Together, these elements contribute to a broader clinical understanding of genital-predominant mpox.

Case presentation

A 21-year-old Nigerian female undergraduate presented to the gynaecologic outpatient clinic with a painful vulvar ulcer that developed 72 h after unprotected sexual intercourse. On examination, she had multiple shallow, exquisitely tender vulvar ulcers with erythematous margins that clinically resembled genital herpes simplex virus infection (Fig. 1). She was empirically treated with oral acyclovir and diclofenac; however, despite 48 h of antiviral therapy, her clinical condition deteriorated rather than improved.

Initial presentation of the genital sore resembling herpes simplex infection

After two days of therapy, the ulcers worsened, becoming more painful with marked perilesional oedema and purulent discharge. She also developed tender inguinal lymphadenopathy and high-grade pyrexia. Based on the purulent genital ulceration, inguinal lymphadenopathy, and fever, a presumptive diagnosis of chancroid with possible secondary bacterial superinfection was made. Upon admission, the patient was started on empiric syndromic management. She received a single intramuscular dose of ceftriaxone to target Neisseria gonorrhoeae in view of the purulent discharge, alongside rectal diclofenac for analgesia. To provide broad-spectrum coverage for suspected chancroid and potential secondary bacterial infection, a seven-day course of oral erythromycin and ciprofloxacin was initiated and completed on an outpatient basis, as the definitive diagnosis remained pending (Fig. 2).

Vulvar ulcer with features suggestive of chancroid

Further history revealed that her sexual partner, who had recently travelled from Italy — one of the European countries affected by the 2022–2023 multi-country mpox outbreak [2] — had developed a rash involving the lip and arm approximately one week before intercourse with the patient. These lesions had reportedly begun to improve before their sexual contact. He subsequently noticed a genital lesion shortly after intercourse, which he initially interpreted as a coital laceration (Fig. 3). However, the partner was not clinically evaluated by the authors, and no confirmatory laboratory testing was performed, limiting definitive interpretation of the sequence of events.

Partner’s genital ulcer

Two days into hospitalisation and treatment for presumed chancroid, the patient developed a generalised vesiculopustular rash involving the face, forearms, palms, and soles (Fig. 4). Laboratory investigations showed negative Venereal Disease Research Laboratory (VDRL) test results for syphilis and negative HIV-1 and HIV-2 serology. The evolving cutaneous distribution raised clinical suspicion for mpox, and infection was confirmed using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing of lesion scrapings.

Mpox lesions involving the foot, palm, and forearm

She was managed with supportive care, including continued analgesia with rectal diclofenac, hydration, and clinical monitoring, while completing the previously initiated empiric antibiotic regimen. Wound microscopy, culture, and sensitivity testing were not performed before antibiotic commencement, largely because of syndromic treatment practices and limited immediate microbiological support. The analgesic regimen was maintained during admission because of severe vulvar pain and oedema. Complete resolution of the genital ulcers and systemic vesiculopustular rash was observed within 14 days.

Discussion

This case exemplifies the evolving and often deceptive clinical spectrum of mpox, in which initial isolated genital ulcers are frequently misdiagnosed as common sexually transmitted infections (STIs). Recent outbreaks have demonstrated a marked shift toward genital-predominant disease, with many patients initially presenting with isolated anogenital lesions before more generalised systemic or cutaneous features emerge [2, 4, 5]. This creates substantial diagnostic overlap with herpes simplex virus and chancroid. Our patient’s presentation was sequentially mistaken for HSV and then chancroid—a diagnostic trajectory that reflects the clinical challenges described in recent literature. Such overlap is particularly problematic in resource-limited settings, where syndromic STI management and restricted access to molecular diagnostics may delay definitive diagnosis.

The subsequent development of a disseminated rash was the pivotal diagnostic clue, mirroring findings from a Nigerian case report in which a patient presented with a two-month history of genital-onset rash, featuring the potential for prolonged eruptive phases in atypical presentations [6]. Systematic reviews have emphasised that genital ulcers refractory to standard STI treatment, particularly in the presence of relevant epidemiological risk factors, should prompt immediate suspicion of mpox. As highlighted by the World Health Organization during the 2022 multi-country outbreak, early identification is critical for infection control, patient counselling, and effective public-health surveillance [7].

The diagnostic confusion between mpox and classic sexually transmitted infections (STIs) is a central theme across multiple case studies. Nasaha et al. explicitly list mpox as a recent addition to the differential diagnosis of genital ulcer disease, providing a comparative table of STI features. They note that while herpes lesions progress from vesicles to erosions, mpox papulopustules typically form a hyperpigmented crust—a distinction not apparent in our patient’s early ulcerative stage [8]. This underscores the limitation of relying solely on lesion morphology during the initial phase of the disease. Similarly, Portela-Dias et al. reported a case of mpox in a woman with localised, painless genital pustules without prodrome, contrasting with our patient’s painful presentation but reinforcing that genital-limited disease occurs in women [9].

The role of sexual transmission is strongly supported by our case and corroborated by existing literature. Our patient’s partner had a probable prodromal or active mpox rash, indicating direct mucocutaneous transmission. The presence of orolabial and cutaneous lesions in the partner prior to intercourse represents a critical epidemiological red flag and supports sexual transmission as the dominant route in this case. This aligns with the comprehensive review by He et al., which synthesises evidence confirming mpox as a sexually transmissible infection, citing the consistent detection of MPXV in genital fluids and the high frequency of genital lesions in recent outbreaks [10]. Reports indicate that between 84% and 100% of cases in one multinational series involved sexual activity before symptom onset [8].

Our patient’s history of unprotected intercourse with a partner recently returning from Italy—a major epicentre during the 2022 outbreak—further strengthens the epidemiological link [7]. The partner’s mild and atypical illness, including a rash involving the lip and arm and a genital lesion misattributed to a coital laceration, illustrates how subtle or misinterpreted symptoms in male partners can facilitate onward transmission to female partners. This finding stresses the importance of obtaining a comprehensive partner history, including travel details and symptom review, when evaluating genital ulcer disease.

The chronology of symptoms in the partner should, however, be interpreted cautiously. Possible explanations include pre-existing unrecognised mpox with mucocutaneous lesions before intercourse, autoinoculation to the genital area, or a traumatic coital lesion that drew attention to an evolving genital lesion. Without direct clinical evaluation and laboratory confirmation in the partner, the precise sequence of events remains uncertain.

The clinical features in our case—including severe genital pain, oedema, lymphadenopathy, and subsequent high-grade pyrexia—find parallels in other reports but also show variability. The progression from isolated genital disease to systemic involvement reflects an increasing viral burden, a pattern more characteristic of mpox than localised HSV infection [11]. Miyazaki and Adachi described a case of mpox in Japan presenting with prominent penile oedema and persistent ulcers that improved without specific antiviral treatment, highlighting that severe local symptoms can form part of the disease’s natural history [12]. Conversely, the patient reported by Oiwoh et al. experienced a remarkably prolonged two-month eruptive phase with nodules and ulcers, suggesting a broader spectrum of disease duration and morphology than classically described [6]. Our patient’s course was acute and severe but resolved within weeks. The subsequent appearance of a vesiculopustular eruption involving the palms and soles served as a classical dermatological clue, narrowing the differential diagnosis to orthopoxvirus infection or secondary syphilis. In the setting of negative syphilis serology and evolving epidemiology, this pattern was highly suggestive of mpox and prompted confirmatory testing.

Empiric antibiotic therapy may sometimes be initiated when genital mpox presents with purulent ulceration, marked oedema, fever, and lymphadenopathy suggestive of chancroid or secondary bacterial infection. However, such treatment should be applied cautiously and reassessed as the clinical picture evolves. Where feasible, microbiological evaluation can help differentiate bacterial superinfection from primary viral disease and support more targeted antimicrobial use. This is particularly important in the context of antimicrobial stewardship.

Recent public health commentary has warned that widespread and often unprescribed antibiotic use during mpox outbreaks may contribute to rising antimicrobial resistance in Africa [13]. The review by He et al. notes that antibiotic use is common in mpox cases presenting with genital oedema due to concerns about cellulitis [9]. The Nigerian case was also treated with broad-spectrum antibiotics upon admission [11]. This accentuates a pragmatic clinical approach in settings where mpox confirmation is delayed—balancing syndromic STI treatment with emerging viral diagnoses.

Importantly, polypharmacy of antibiotics is a recognised challenge in the syndromic management of STIs, which we also encountered in this case [14]. Recognising this limitation, we emphasise that an aetiological, evidence-based management approach is preferable and strongly recommended to align with antimicrobial stewardship principles. In our patient, empiric antibiotics reflected syndromic management in a resource-limited setting prior to confirmation of mpox, but the case underscores the importance of narrowing or discontinuing antibacterial therapy when bacterial infection is not substantiated.

Management of mpox in uncomplicated cases remains largely supportive, encompassing adequate analgesia, hydration, skin and mucosal care, and monitoring for complications. Recent randomised clinical trial data have refined the evidence base for antiviral therapy. Large placebo-controlled studies evaluating tecovirimat, including STOMP/A5418 and PALM007, did not demonstrate clear benefit for major clinical outcomes in uncomplicated mpox, particularly in accelerating overall recovery [15, 16]. Supportive care, therefore, remains appropriate for many immunocompetent patients with self-limited disease, while antiviral therapy may still be considered for severe, complicated, or high-risk cases, in accordance with evolving guidance and clinical judgement [17].

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing of lesion swabs remains the diagnostic gold standard; however, this case demonstrates that clinical vigilance remains indispensable, particularly in endemic regions and among patients whose epidemiological profiles do not align with traditional zoonotic exposure.

High index of suspicion: Mpox should be considered in any patient with genital ulcers that are painful, progressive, associated with lymphadenopathy, or unresponsive to initial STI therapy.

Conclusion

Mpox is a formidable mimicker of common sexually transmitted infections (STIs). This case, viewed alongside contemporary reports, demonstrates that the initial presentation of a painful genital ulcer can be the harbinger of mpox infection. Clinicians—particularly those in dermatology, genitourinary medicine, and primary care—must maintain a high index of suspicion. Integrating a meticulous sexual history with awareness of mpox’s atypical presentations is crucial for timely diagnosis, appropriate management, and effective public‑health intervention to prevent further community transmission.
