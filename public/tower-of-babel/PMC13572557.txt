A self-orchestrating physics-discovering neural architecture for adaptive and fault-resilient robotic motion control via RPA and digital twins
Authors: Daksh Singla, G Logeswari, K Tamilarasi, J Deepika Roselind
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo autonomously control a robot’s movement in complex and unpredictable environments, adaptive models must be developed that can continuously learn about and refine their future physical dynamics. This paper introduces a Self-Organising Physics Discovered Neural Architecture (SOPDNA) that unites Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) with Robotics Process Automation (RPA) and Digital Twin DT technologies to create an entirely automated, robust robotic motion control system. In contrast to traditional robotic motion control systems that rely on predefined mathematical models or offline training methods, SOPDNA automatically discovers previously unknown physical relationships between robotic motion components and continuously updates the controller policy during operation. The Digital Twin software acts as a nearly identical simulation environment to enable extensive self-experimentation and safety verification of SOPDNA while the RPA software automates all aspects of the SOPDNA learning process, including acquiring experimental data, detecting anomalies, retraining the learning model, and deploying optimised controllers, without requiring any human oversight. The core of SOPDNA consists of a PINN that incorporates a comprehensive mathematical representation of the robot’s physical constraints and uses symbolic regression techniques to estimate unmodelled dynamic variables, such as variable friction coefficients and environmental disturbances. Experimental validation using a robotic manipulator performing trajectory tracking and obstacle avoidance under changing payloads shows the proposed approach’s capabilities. The SOPDNA framework provides an overall trajectory-tracking accuracy of 98.4%, a 22.7% reduction in energy usage, and a 30.9% improvement in disturbance rejection when compared to traditional deep reinforcement learning (DRL) and Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID)-based control systems. Results from the fault-injection experiments show SOPDNA’s ability to recover from degradation of actuators 41.3% faster than conventional methods. Overall, these findings support the potential for self-organising physics-discovered neural network architectures to enhance the development of robust, adaptive, intelligent robotic motion control systems capable of autonomously executing complex tasks.Keywords: Physics-informed neural networks, Robotic process automation, Digital twin, Autonomous robotic motion control, Physics discovery, Self-orchestrating learning systems, Fault-resilient roboticsSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The emergence of autonomous robotic systems has revolutionized technology today through their implementation across many industries including manufacturing, healthcare, logistics, agriculture, and exploratory domains. In order for these machines to perform effectively in dynamic and unpredictable or unknown situations, they must be able to navigate accurately, stably and adaptively to continuously changing physical conditions; this is referred to as the problem of robotic motion control. The greater part of motion control is how to achieve these motions (move along specific paths) with respect to an environment using traditional control architecture-based solutions such as PID controller schemes or model-based optimal controllers generally use detailed mathematical models of robot dynamics; however, real-world applications seldom meet these criteria because there are never complete physical models available since real world scenarios can vary greatly due to factors like changes in friction, payloads, sensor noise, mechanical wear and unrestrained interactions with the environment and therefore result in performance degradation and limit robustness.

The recent advances in Machine Learning (ML) provide new opportunities to look at the problem of control using a different perspective where the control system learns through observation rather than pure exploration of the robot’s environment. Many researchers have begun to explore the use of DRL as a method for developing intelligent robot systems or neural network based control techniques in both a simulated or controlled manner that are capable of providing excellent performance on controlled systems. However, due to their nature as black-box models and their need for highly extensive amounts of training data to be successful, the costs associated with obtaining this training data or physical implementation on actual robots may not yield a productive return. In addition, ML-based systems lack one of the most fundamental properties; they do not adhere to any physical principles within the realm of physics and therefore do not have applicable generalization when interacting with an environment that is outside of the distribution of the training experience data. This disconnection between ML-based techniques and the basic foundations of how to operate efficiently within the bounds of the laws of physics indicate that there is a need to explore hybrid approaches to robotic control that will leverage both physical knowledge/domain knowledge and data driven intelligence1.

PINNs have become an excellent way to connect the gap between data-driven models and their corresponding physical laws; incorporating governing equations, directly into the training of the neural network, allows the constraints of the learning objective, stated by the differential equations, of the behaviours of the system, to create both data-efficient models, and physically accurate models2. PINNs have been applied within robotic motion control for tasks like inverse dynamics approximation, trajectory generation and state estimation, all with adherence to kinematic and dynamic constraints. However, the majority of existing robotic PINN frameworks have been founded under the assumption that the physics governing the underlying system is well-defined and static. In reality, robots operate under a variety of real-world environments where they can experience unknown or time-varying aspects of the physical model (e.g. contact dynamics, friction coefficients, external disturbances)—something that is a limitation to the adaptability of typical PINN approaches and prevents long-term autonomous operation within complex environments.

Another challenge in current robotic learning systems; relies heavily on human engineering providing manual intervention at multiple points within the training and deployment pipeline. During this time, human engineers are responsible for data collection, retraining of models, hyperparameter tuning for all models, performance monitoring for all models, and updating controller models. The result of relying on human supervision for these tasks has a diminishing effect on scalability and makes real-time adaptation extremely challenging. In order to develop fully autonomous robotic intelligence, new systems will need to be developed that are capable of self-managing their own learning processes, determining failures, and refining control strategies without the need for continuous external supervision3.

RPA, which is used primarily within businesses and software workflow automation, is a new avenue for automating repetitive, rule-based processes. RPA can serve as a method of automation for robotic systems using a level of orchestration to manage the following robotic functions: data collection (to create data sets for training models), triggering a model to retrain, validating performance of the static and dynamic robot controller, and facilitating the deployment of the updated controller. By placing rules for making decisions and policies to adapt to changing conditions within RPA, RPA agents can allow robotic systems to autonomously manage their own learning lifecycle, thereby making robotic systems self-orchestrating. There has been little research conducted in the field of robotics on the integration of RPA with learning-based control, though this combination can be an effective way to reduce the amount of human input for operations and increase the autonomy of robotic operations4.

Digital Twin technology, on the other hand, has been used and gained momentum for the purpose of creating virtual copies of physical systems that are continuously synchronized in real-time with sensor datas5. Digital twins provide an environment for experimentation to occur in a safe manner, performing predictive analytics and optimizing the system as if the physical robot is still intact. Specifically for robotic motion control purposes, a digital twin can develop different control strategies that simulate the reaction of the robot to a disturbance, and can assess the safety limits of the system prior to implementing those changes in the actual physical robot6. When digital twins and learning algorithms are combined, a digital twin can be used as a type of controlled environment for carrying out large-scale self-experimentation, and as a mechanism for continuous improvement. However, today’s state-of-the-art digital twin applications for robotics mainly focus on monitoring and displaying the robotic system, rather than facilitating autonomous learning and physical phenomena discovery7.

With the blending of PINNs, RPA, and Digital Twins, we are approaching a point where robots can not only be trained through data but can also learn physical phenomena and how to manage their own workflows, and in addition perform continuous in-situ and virtual testing of actions to validate themselves8. This new way of controlling robots is a new era because we will move away from static models of robot learning to self-evolving robotic technologies. Learning systems with the ability to discover unknown or un-modeled physical (or mathematical) relationships from observed data while still consistent with known laws are termed as discovering physical phenomena9.

Robots in open world environments must be able to operate in unpredictable environments and discover physical interactions and conditions that were not previously considered10. While we have made significant advances in each of these areas individually, a unified architecture for controlling autonomous robots using all three is still in its infancy. Most studies conducted, focus on either physics based learning model or utilizing digital twin simulation or workflow automated, and not all three combined together into one cohesive self-orchestrating system. In addition to that fact that existing robotic control systems do not contain the capacity for online improvement of their physical models or automated fault or performance degradation recovery, many of our robotic systems continue to remain brittle and require constant human intervention to recalibrate themselves.

This paper presents a SOPDNA for autonomous robotic movement control based on integrating PINNs, RPA, and Digital Twin technologies. The proposed framework is designed to allow for continuous learning, validation, and refinement of robotic dynamic understanding while performing control tasks in real-time. The PINN component incorporates established physical constraints while concurrently discovering unknown dynamic terms by means of data-driven learning. The digital twin serves to provide a synchronized virtual space that will enable simulation-based validation and large-scale experimentations. The RPA layer orchestrates the entire process by automating data acquisition, anomaly detection; retraining schedules and control deployment. In contrast to traditional robotic learning systems that rely on offline training and manual updates, the proposed architecture establishes a closed-loop self-management cycle. Sensor data from the physical robot are send to the digital twin, which in turn evaluates candidate control strategies. When deviations or anomalies occur, RPA agents trigger the retraining of the PINN model with revised physical constraints and re-deploy the optimized controller back to the physical robot. This closed-loop continuous feedback system allows for the robot to adjust to changing conditions such as changes in payload, actuator degradation, or environmental disturbances, without requiring any explicit human intervention.

This research is motivated by the challenges faced by existing robotic motion control techniques in terms of fixed physical models, autonomous learning orchestration, and limited use of digital twinned approaches for adaptive control. In particular, we show how self-discovery through physics allows the system to change its internal representation of physics when physical environments change. Through self-orchestration, we can manage our own learning lifecycle. Additionally, we use a digital twin to explore and validate control approaches in a safe manner prior to executing those control strategies in the real world. Together, these three components provide a scalable and reliable foundation for next-generation autonomous robots.

This study contributes three main areas of knowledge. First, we provide a cohesive architecture that integrates physics-informed learning, automation of the training pipeline, and digital twin validation into one autonomous robotic motion control device. Second, we show that systems can discover and incorporate live unknown or time-changing physical dynamics in real-time into their control policy. Third, we create a self-orchestrating control loop that decreases needed human intervention and increases robustness to fault and disturbance conditions.

Literature review

In recent years, there have been many advances in the area of autonomous robotic motion control and the shift towards using hybrid motion control frameworks, which are based on both the physical knowledge about how the motion of a robotic system occurs as well as modern data-driven learning approaches. Traditionally, robotic control has used methods, like PID controllers, sliding mode control, and model predictive control, that require a precise analytic model for the dynamics of the robot11. However, most robotic systems operate in environments that have elements of uncertainty, unmodeled dynamics, sensor noise, and external disturbances. This has resulted in researchers investigating the use of a combination of ways to model robotic systems through a combination of simulation and a physical-based modeling approach along with machine learning in a manner which integrates the physics-based modeling elements (i.e., laws governing the motion of a robotic system) directly into the neural learning structure of the robotic system in order to accomplish the goal of improved generalization, robustness, and adaptability.

A significant advancement in this area of study has been the development of PINNs, which is an approach to designing neural networks to model physical systems and to include the laws of physics into the training of the network instead of treating the neural network like a black box to approximate functions. An example would be embedding the governing physics equations (e.g., Newton–Euler dynamics or Lagrangian formulations) as part of the loss function for training the network so that the network is required to satisfy the physical constraints of the physical laws associated with the motion of a robotic system, while also minimizing the error between data-driven results and ground truth in a manner which usually requires significantly less labeled data than the traditional approach to developing deep learning networks12. PINN concepts were introduced by Raissi et al., who demonstrated the application of PINNs to solving forward and inverse problems for partial differential equations (PDEs)13, providing a strong theoretical underpinning for the integration of neural learning with the constraints of differential equations.

Training on this basis, Karniadakis and coauthors provide a thorough overview of the rapidly developing field of physics-informed machine learning. They provide an overview of the techniques for combining traditional numerical solvers with deep learning methods in order to efficiently model complex dynamical systems14. They demonstrate that when you construct a neural network that embodies the underlying physical laws in its architecture, your network is capable of producing more generalized output, making it easier to interpret, and is less dependent on data as input than when using traditional numerical solvers. These important conclusions have since been implemented in the field of robotics to create models of nonlinear dynamics and improve control strategies.

A major breakthrough in the field of physics-informed modeling occurred with the introduction of Neural ODEs15. Neural ODEs can be viewed as flows through a neural network, providing a method to learn smooth trajectories of robot motion. Neural ODEs offer an alternative to discrete representations of data as they can learn time-variant behavior directly from the data. However, the use of continuous optimization for training introduces numerical instability and cost due to large computation requirements, which is a critical drawback for real-time control applications.

In addition, techniques for discovering sparse models have also impacted the field of robotics. One example is the development of Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics (SINDy) by Brunton and coauthors, which is a method that provides a way to extract minimal governing equations for nonlinear systems from time series data using sparse regression16. Although originally applied outside of the robotics domain, SINDy has been implemented to learn interpretable dynamic models of robotic systems, especially when the governing physics are partially unknown or latent in sensor data.

The use of PINNs has been employed in robotics to create models and control variables across multiple systems, including Liu et al., who extended the use of PINNs for robotic modeling by training networks with rigid body dynamics embedded into the training of the network to achieve higher accuracy in trajectory predictions and control of both rigid and soft systems17. Their findings demonstrate that physics-informed learning is able to model complex interactions, including friction and compliance, which are difficult to analytically model.

Li et al. have developed a PINN Predictive Control (PINNPC) framework for quadrupedal locomotion which includes the ability to perform on-line payload estimation directly into the PINN model18. The authors had the capability to couple PINNs with nonlinear Model Predictive Control, leading to a PINNPC framework that is able to adapt to variable loads and to adapt to interaction with the environment to achieve robust real-time gait control. Furthermore, this work demonstrates the advantages of using physics-informed models in an adaptive control loop.

Compliant robotic systems, such as soft and flexible or elastic jointed robots, present an additional challenge to utilize physics-informed learning as a modelling tool because of the deformability characteristics of these types of robots. Li et al. have developed a PINN model that represents the compliant manipulator as a compliant manipulator and captures both the rigid and elastic dynamical characteristics of the manipulator, allowing for accurate dynamic prediction and control in the presence of uncertainty19. Their results provide evidence of the value of using physics-informed learning in systems that do not have the ability to model traditional rigid dynamics.

In addition to PINN formulations based on physics-based principles, there are also various approaches that combine physical guidance and deep reinforcement learning (RL). For example, researchers, including Banerjee et al., have discussed how Physics-Informed Reinforcement Learning (PIRL) employs physical constraints incorporated into either the reward or the policy structure in order to improve sample efficiency and both convergence rates20. Due to balancing exploration against consistency with the physical laws governing the underlying system, PIRL enables autonomous robots to learn control policies that respect the known dynamics of their environment.

Another area of study involves new transformer-based neural architectures applied to improve performance, such as that introduced by Golroudbari et al., using a transformer-based PINN that uses attention mechanisms to provide long-term orientation and state estimate capabilities in robotic systems21. Their architecture demonstrated superior performance relative to both traditional LSTM and EKF methods in the presence of highly dynamic environments, indicating the possible advantage of combining structured sequence models with physical constraints.

While much of the work on PINNs and PIRL has focused on modeling and control, they may be viewed as complementary paradigms to the concept of the digital twin, which uses a high fidelity digital representation of the robot. Digital twins are able to simulate the dynamics of a robot in real time, providing a foundation for validation, prediction, and optimization capabilities. The concept of digital twins was popularized by Tao et al. for use in manufacturing applications, including their role in the monitoring and control of cyber-physical systems22. Grieves and Vickers subsequently defined the digital twin as a synchronized virtual and physical counterpart which allows for decision making and predictive analytics through the use of the digital twin23.

Digital twins have been utilized for sim-to-real transfer in the robotics domain. For example, Mohaghegh et al. introduced a digital twin testbed that enables the deployment of imitation learning in mobile robots without any need for retraining in real world24. Their system allows for zero-shot transfer of learned policies from simulated environments, thanks to the close synchronization of physical sensors and simulations. Similarly, Liu et al. integrated digital twins with reinforcement learning for robotic manipulation, using virtual feedback to fine-tune control policies prior to physical deployment25. These contributions demonstrate the potential of digital twins to mitigate the reality gap and enhance controller reliability.

In addition, multiple authors have discussed the utility of digital twin frameworks for fault diagnosis and maintenance. Wang et al. presented a hybrid autoencoder-simulator design for detecting real-time anomalies in industrial robots. Their model uses digital twin data for identifying deviations from expected performance26. Thus, their hybrid approach allows for early detection of mechanical faults, improving reliability and uptime.

In addition to detecting hardware faults, research has also investigated the use of physics-informed models in conjunction with digital twin systems. Datta et al. proposed physics-encoded neural networks in the context of digital twin modeling, whereby the inclusion of physical prior knowledge within a neural network results in improved generalization performance and computational efficiency relative to traditional approaches27. Overall, their hybrid architecture outperformed traditional data-driven digital twins in terms of performance, especially in situations where physical constraints are important.

Physics-informed learning has also influenced robotic motion planning. For instance, researchers28 have demonstrated that by employing PINNs to plan trajectories for heavy machinery (i.e., excavators), there is less deviation from optimal trajectories at a reduced computational cost. Ni et al.29 extended this work by incorporating physics into the planning of motions on manifolds, thereby developing planning algorithms capable of dealing with high degree-of-freedom robots.

There have been few studies that have directly addressed self-orchestrating architectures that encompass physics discovery, digital twin simulation, automated retraining, and deployment as an integrated system. Most current research has been conducted around either physics-based modelling alone or digital twin supported control systems, however the proposed SOPDNA aims to integrate these components by introducing an automated orchestration engine which can support autonomous learning, validation, and deployment in a closed loop. Proposed methodology

The SOPDNA framework will provide autonomous robotic motion control through the collaborative connection of three major components: a Digital Twin, a Physics-Discovering Neural Network based on PINNs, and an RPA-driven orchestration engine. A closed-loop control and learning cycle is created where physical interactions of the robotic system are replicated continuously in a high-precision Digital Twin, while the neural system learns latent laws of physical motion via motion dynamics and updates the control algorithm real-time.

Operating through an iterative cycle of perception, learning, validation, and deployment, data from sensors on the actual robot will be streamed to the Digital Twin, generating the kinematic and dynamic behaviour of that robot in a simulated form across various operational conditions. The Physics-Discovering Neural Network model can learn both the trajectory of the states and unknown physical properties by enforcing physical constraints within the training of that model. The RPA layer will automatically handle the ingestion of the different forms of data (from real and simulated), creation of retraining schedules, failure detection, and updating of controllers without human intervention. This will allow the system to self-orchestrate its learning process and provide reliable autonomous motion control in dynamic and uncertain environments.

When connected together, these three components allow: (1) the creation of noise-aware training datasets which will enable the neural model to generate interpretable, physics-based representations of physical phenomena; (2) the RPA engine to provide real-time coordination of the continuously optimizing process; and (3) the overall scalability, adaptability, and resiliency of the SOPDNA framework. RPA-based self-orchestration engine

The self-orchestration engine serves as a custom-built supervisory orchestration layer in the SOPDNA framework. Unlike conventional enterprise RPA software designed for UI-level business automation, the proposed orchestration engine operates as a lightweight finite-state supervisory controller responsible for coordinating anomaly monitoring, retraining triggers, digital twin synchronization, validation, and adaptive policy deployment. The primary function of this self-orchestrating engine is to automate the entire learning and control cycle, from ingesting data from the digital twin; to training and validating the physics-discovered neural model; to implementing the validated control policies on the actual robot. Unlike current robotic control pathways that rely on user intervention for scheduling and parameter tuning, the self-orchestration engine will monitor performance of the system continuously and will automatically take corrective actions when an anomaly occurs. The orchestration layer does not participate directly in the deterministic low-level motor control loop operating at the 100 Hz sampling frequency. Instead, real-time trajectory tracking and actuator control are handled independently by the PDNN controller, while the orchestration engine supervises higher-level asynchronous learning and adaptation tasks. This architectural separation minimizes latency overhead and prevents communication bottlenecks commonly associated with traditional commercial RPA platforms.

SOPDNA for autonomousrobotic motion control

Results and discussion

In this section, we will evaluate the proposed SOPDNA for autonomous robotic motion control. The evaluation was done via experiments, performed under dynamic and uncertain conditions, and compared with other traditional controllers and with other learning-based methods. The results of these experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of the SOPDNA framework in tracking accuracy, energy use efficiency, robustness to disturbances, and recovering from faults.

Robotic platform and digital twin environment

The SOPDNA framework was experimentally evaluated using a Universal Robots UR5e six-degree-of-freedom (6-DOF) robotic manipulator in both physical and digital twin environments. The UR5e robotic platform features six revolute joints, a maximum payload capacity of 5 kg, and a repeatability of ± 0.03 mm. The robot was interfaced with a high-performance workstation equipped with an Intel Core i9 processor, NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU, and 64 GB RAM to support real-time neural training, digital twin synchronization, and adaptive control deployment. The physical robotic manipulator was equipped with integrated joint encoders, force–torque sensors, inertial measurement units (IMU), and proximity sensors to acquire real-time motion and environmental interaction data at a sampling frequency of 100 Hz.

The resultant data set contains motion trajectories of the robotic manipulator for a range of operational conditions that include differences in payload, different coefficients of friction, and external disturbances being applied to the robotic manipulator. The data set was created to expose the SOPDNA learning framework to nominal and perturbed dynamic behaviour to achieve a robust generalisation to previously unseen scenarios. A digital twin (i.e., a virtual model) was developed as a high fidelity, digital replica of the robotic system that mimicked the kinematic and dynamic behaviour of the actual robot. Performance metrics

The proposed control framework was evaluated comprehensively for its effectiveness and robustness using multiple quantitative performance metrics that captured both control accuracy and system resilience under dynamic operating conditions. The accuracy of trajectory tracking was used as a key metric for assessing how well the actual motion of the robot matches the desired reference trajectories. This was represented as a percentage of the completion of the trajectory to enable comparisons of the performance of alternative controllers. In addition, Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) was calculated to provide an average measure of deviation between the predicted joint states and actual joint states over time, providing a more sensitive measure of control accuracy.

Energy consumption was included as one of the metrics for evaluating system efficiency by indicating the total energy required (in joules) to complete a defined task and reflecting the smoothness and physical consistency of the generated control signals. Settling time and overshoot were used as additional metrics for assessing temporal response characteristics of the control system and demonstrating how quickly the system reached steady-state behaviour after being subject to disturbances and how much temporary deviations from desired trajectory occurred. The metrics used are critical for safety–critical robotics systems where excessive oscillations or long time to convergence will affect the reliability of the system.

To determine the resilience of the controller with respect to disturbances originating outside the system, the disturbance (or disturbance to the output) rejection ratio indicated how effectively the controller can mitigate the effect of sudden (impulsive) and continuous disturbances on system performance. Lastly, the recovery time from a fault was used as an indicator of the adaptability of the control architecture in response to degradation of the actuator or anomalies in the sensor and measured the amount of time required to recover the system to stable operation following the detection of a fault. These five metrics together give a comprehensive evaluation of the accuracy, stability, energy efficiency, robustness, and autonomous recovery of the proposed framework across different experimental conditions.

Overall discussion

The experimental evidence provided indicates that the proposed SOPDNA framework is able to integrate the discovery of physics, the validation of digital twin technology, and the orchestration of RPA properties into one customizable and adaptive control system. The SOPDNA framework is not like traditional control systems, which have fixed models or offline-trained neural networks. Instead, the SOPDNA framework continually updates its internal representation of the dynamic physical world and independently manages its own learning processes. The results demonstrated in Figs. 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 and Tables 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 and 9 support the claim that SOPDNA outperforms the ability of traditional control systems to reject disturbances, recover from faults, generalize to previously unseen payloads, and achieve statistically significant improvements in accuracy or energy efficiency. In addition, the ablation study validated the necessity of each architectural component included in the SOPDNA framework for achieving these improvements. Thus, the SOPDNA framework is an intelligent and robust control paradigm that minimizes the requirement for human oversight and maximizes the reliability and adaptability of complex robotic platforms.

Conclusions and future work

This paper describes a SOPDNA for controlling an autonomous robot’s motion intelligently and effectively by using a combination of physics-informed neural networks, RPA, and digital twin technologies. Traditional approaches to controlling a robot’s motion use either an analytical or mathematical model that has been predetermined or some other existing model, such as human labor used to create a control system and learning pipeline. The SOPDNA approach enables an autonomous robot using this technology to automatically discover new or unknown dynamics within its physical environment. Once these dynamics are identified, the SOPDNA architecture can orchestrate the data learning processes based on the discovered dynamics and validate its methods of control through continuous interaction with the digital twin. The RPA component provides the added benefits of automating the acquisition of data, performing anomaly detection, retraining on an adaptive basis, and deploying optimized control policies, which results in a completely automated learning and control cycle for the robot.

The experimental results demonstrate that the proposed SOPDNA framework achieves improved trajectory tracking accuracy, disturbance rejection capability, energy efficiency, and fault recovery performance compared with PID-, DRL-, and PINN-based baseline approaches under the evaluated experimental conditions. In addition, the fault injection experiments indicate that the integration of physics-discovering neural learning, digital twin validation, and automated orchestration can improve adaptive control behavior under actuator degradation and external disturbances. These findings indicate the potential of physics-discovering neural-based architectures for adaptive robotic motion control under controlled experimental conditions, while broader validation across external benchmark datasets, heterogeneous robotic platforms, and real-world deployment scenarios remains an important direction for future research.

Despite the encouraging experimental results, the present study has several limitations. The evaluation was conducted using a self-generated dataset derived from a single robotic platform and digital twin environment, without validation on external benchmark datasets or heterogeneous robotic systems. In addition, the experiments were performed under controlled laboratory conditions, which may not fully capture the complexity and variability of large-scale real-world deployments. Therefore, further investigation is required to assess the scalability, computational efficiency, and generalization capability of the proposed framework across diverse robotic applications and operating environments.

The framework’s future development will be aimed at allowing cooperative discovery of physical laws through multi-robot and swarm robotic technology and at having these systems coordinate their movement with each other. Symbolic regression and advanced transformer-based neural networks will be the primary sources of research information for improving how well the discovered physical laws can be interpreted and generalized by the user. The framework will also be developed for use on embedded robotic hardware, as this will make the framework more usable due to meeting time constraints and increasing computational efficiency. Additionally, the development will include human-in-the-loop systems that provide feedback to the robotic system, as well as multi-modal sensor inputs that include vision and touch, so that robots can work collaboratively with humans. Finally, large-scale validation of the practical applicability and reliability of the robotic framework will take place in three areas: industrial automation, rehabilitation robotics, and autonomous navigation.
