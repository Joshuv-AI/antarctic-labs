Digitally-driven optimization of intraoral water jet parameters for plaque removal: a personalized 3D-guided orthogonal study
Authors: Suli Guo, Li Gao, Hu Chen, Yaopeng Zhang, Feng Wu, Sukun Tian, Xu Zhang, Yuchun Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

Effective control of dental plaque biofilm is fundamental to preventing dental caries and periodontal diseases. While toothbrushing remains the primary method for daily oral hygiene, its efficacy in cleaning interproximal and subgingival areas can be limited [1].

Oral irrigation devices, commonly known as water flossers, deliver a pulsating or steady stream of water and have been established as a safe and effective adjunct for mechanical plaque removal and the reduction of gingival inflammation [2, 3]. Studies indicate that the adjunctive use of water flossers with manual toothbrushing surpasses brushing alone, especially for individuals with orthodontic appliances, dental implants, or fixed prostheses [4–6]. Various studies have compared the efficacy of water flossers with traditional interdental cleaning methods. Systematic reviews confirm that water flossers are effective at removing dental plaque and food debris, though efficacy varies across patient populations [7, 8]. Some studies suggest that they are comparable to or even more effective than traditional string floss in plaque removal [9, 10], but the effectiveness of dental floss itself depends heavily on patient compliance and dexterity [11]. The safety of oral water jets is well established, with pressure tolerance varying by tissue condition. Research indicates that intact gingival tissue can tolerate pressures up to 90 psi, while attached gingiva can withstand up to 160 psi for 30 s without irreversible damage [12–14]; for inflamed or ulcerated tissues, a lower pressure range of 50–70 psi is recommended [13].

Despite this evidence, significant knowledge gaps persist, hindering the translation of this technology into precisely optimized clinical and product applications. In-vivo studies have evaluated water flossers primarily as an adjunct to toothbrushing, using clinical indices such as the plaque index or gingival index rather than quantitative measures of plaque area [7–11]. Moreover, the parameters used by participants (pressure, angle, etc.) varied considerably across studies. While in-vitro studies that independently validated water jet efficacy often utilized simplified, single-species biofilm models that do not fully replicate the structural complexity and tenacity of natural, multi-species oral biofilm [1, 2, 15]. Dental plaque is a complex polymicrobial biofilm, and its composition can shift with the dominance of pathogenic microorganisms, influencing oral health and disease states [16–19]. More critically, there is a paucity of in-vivo research systematically investigating the specific effects of the device’s core engineering parameters and their potential interactions on clinical plaque removal outcomes. Previous engineering and dental studies have indicated that water pressure determines jet velocity and shear stress, directly influencing biofilm disruption [20, 21]; nozzle diameter affects the jet’s cross-sectional area and impact force [20, 22]; and working distance influences jet coherence and dynamic pressure at the tooth surface [23, 24], thereby affecting cleaning efficacy. Consequently, parameter settings in both clinical guidance and commercial device design often lack a robust, evidence-based foundation.

To address these gaps, the present study implemented a novel, digitally guided in-vivo methodology. We employed a three-factor, three-level orthogonal experimental design (L9 array) to investigate the influence of nozzle diameter, working distance, and water pressure on plaque removal efficacy. These three parameters are relatively independent variables that directly determine the hydrodynamic impact of a continuous water jet, and thus were selected for optimization. The primary objectives were to: (1) quantitatively analyze and rank the influence of each parameter on the plaque removal rate within a defined Target Zone (T-Zone); (2) identify the optimal combination of parameters; and (3) independently validate the efficacy of this optimal combination. The methodological innovation lies in the integration of personalized 3D-printed guides, featuring a retention device and a calibrated water outlet assembly, to ensure precise and reproducible parameter delivery, coupled with a 3D quantitative analysis method based on intraoral scanning for objective outcome assessment.

Methods

Study design and ethical approval

This investigation was a non-randomized, self-controlled trial conducted as a collaborative project between Peking University School and Hospital of Stomatology (Beijing, China) and Shanxi Medical University School and Hospital of Stomatology (Taiyuan, China). The study protocol received full approval from the Scientific Research Ethics Review Committee of Shanxi Medical University School and Hospital of Stomatology (Approval No. 2022SLL021). All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional committee and with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study.

Participants and sample

Twelve systemically healthy adult participants (5 males, 7 females) with a mean age of 25 years (range: 22–29) were recruited via advertisements among students and staff at Shanxi Medical University School of Stomatology (Taiyuan, China). The primary inclusion criteria were as follows: complete permanent dentition, good general and periodontal health (no probing depth > 3 mm, no bleeding on probing at selected sites), no obvious gingival recession or hyperplasia, and absence of caries or extensive restorations (defined as resin or amalgam restorations involving the buccal or lingual surfaces) on the designated test teeth. Exclusion criteria included active periodontal disease, severe dental caries, ongoing orthodontic treatment, presence of dental implants or fixed prostheses in the test area, use of antimicrobial mouthrinses within the past month, pregnancy, and any systemic condition or medication. For each participant, six teeth in the maxillary arch were selected to represent different anatomical regions: the bilateral central incisors (teeth 11, 21), first premolars (teeth 14, 24), and first molars (teeth 16, 26).

The sample size was determined based on the requirement of adequate error degrees of freedom for ANOVA in an orthogonal design. Using pilot data from three participants (18 teeth), the mean difference in PRT among factor levels ranged from 10.2% to 14.8% with a within-group standard deviation of approximately 5.0%, corresponding to an estimated effect size (Cohen’s f) of 0.45. In our repeated-measurement design, each participant is tested with all nine parameter combinations of the L9 array, producing nine PRT observations per individual. With 12 participants, the total number of observations is 108, which provides an error degrees of freedom well above 10—the generally recommended minimum for reliable F‑tests in biomedical studies. This sample size also ensures > 80% statistical power to detect the observed large main effects at α = 0.05, as calculated using PASS 2021 (ANOVA: fixed effects).

Equipment and software

The following equipment and software were utilized in this study: an intraoral scanner (TRIOS 3, 3Shape, Denmark), a metal 3D printer (IS200, Nanjing Profeta Intelligent Technology Co., Ltd., China), and a pressure sensor (SBT674, Sparto Electronic Technology, China) for device fabrication and parameter control; design software (Rhinoceros 7.0, Robert McNeel & Associates, USA) and 3D analysis software (Geomagic Wrap 2021, 3D Systems, USA) for digital modeling and evaluation; along with statistical and sample size analysis software, including SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., USA), GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, USA), and PASS 2021 (NCSS, LLC, USA).

Orthogonal experimental design

Three primary operational parameters of the single-beam water jet were selected as experimental factors: nozzle diameter (Factor A), working distance (Factor B), and water pressure (Factor C). Each factor was assigned three levels within clinically relevant and safe ranges (Table 1). We generated an L9(34) orthogonal array using SPSS software, which allows efficient study of multiple factors with a minimal number of experimental runs by reducing the number of runs from 27 (full factorial) to 9, and assumes that main effects dominate while two-factor interactions are negligible—a reasonable initial assumption for these physical parameters. This resulted in nine distinct parameter combinations for testing (Table 2).

Design and fabrication of personalized single-tooth guides

A personalized guide was digitally designed for each test tooth of every participant to ensure consistent parameter application. First, a full-arch digital impression of the maxilla was obtained using the intraoral scanner (Fig. 1a). In Rhinoceros 7.0, a guide was modeled to fit the occlusal morphology of the specific tooth (e.g., tooth 16, Fig. 1b). The guide consisted of three parts: a water inlet channel at the top for external tubing connection; a middle retention device that engaged with the occlusal surface for stabilization; and a bottom water outlet assembly. The outlet assembly featured buccal and lingual water-collecting bulbs, each with a water jet nozzle positioned 0.5 mm above the gingival margin midpoint and oriented perpendicular to the tooth surface. This position was chosen to target the cervical plaque, a key site for supragingival biofilm accumulation directly associated with gingival inflammation [25, 26]; the 0.5 mm distance was selected based on preliminary tests to avoid direct tissue trauma while ensuring effective and reproducible irrigation. The nozzle diameter and working distance were designed according to the orthogonal table. Guides were fabricated from medical-grade titanium alloy (TA1) using the metal 3D printer (Fig. 1c, d). All guides were cleaned, polished, and sterilized prior to intraoral use.

In-vivo plaque removal test protocol

Each of the nine orthogonal experimental groups was tested on a separate day, with a washout period of at least 48 h to allow for natural plaque reaccumulation. Participants refrained from all oral hygiene for 12 h prior to each session.

At the start of a session, plaque was disclosed using a two-tone dye (C.I. Medical, Tokyo, Japan) applied along the gingival margin, followed by rinsing. A baseline intraoral scan of the stained maxillary dentition was immediately acquired (TRIOS 3 scanner). For the tested group, the corresponding personalized guide was seated on the isolated tooth and connected to a regulated water source. Water pressure (Factor C) was set at the pump and verified at the nozzle outlet using a calibrated pressure sensor positioned at the specified working distance (1.0, 1.5, or 2.0 mm) to ensure accurate dynamic pressure delivery. With the guide in position and pressure stable, water jet irrigation was applied for 30 s [13–14] per tooth, timed digitally. This was repeated sequentially for all six test teeth. After irrigation, the participant rinsed gently, and a second full-arch intraoral scan was obtained under the same settings as the baseline.

3D plaque quantification and analysis

3D image analysis was performed using Geomagic Wrap 2021 software. The primary outcome was the plaque removal rate within the Target Zone (T-Zone), defined as the mid-cervical third of the clinical crown and delineated by trisecting each tooth surface into mesio-distal and occluso-cervical thirds (Fig. 2a). This zone was selected because it corresponds to the direct impact area of the water jet, which was positioned 0.5 mm coronal to the gingival margin midpoint and oriented perpendicular to the tooth surface. Focusing the analysis on this region reduces measurement noise from anatomical variability across different teeth and surfaces, thereby increasing the sensitivity of the orthogonal analysis to detect parameter effects. Plaque areas were identified by automated color thresholding (pink disclosed plaque vs. unstained enamel) and then quantitatively calculated by summing the 3D surface area within the selected region [27].

3D quantitative analysis of plaque removal within the defined Target Zone (T-Zone). a Definition of the Target Zone (T-Zone) on the buccal surface of the upper right first premolar (tooth 14). b Buccal view of tooth 14 before plaque cleaning. c Buccal view of tooth 14 after plaque cleaning. d Lingual view of tooth 14 before plaque cleaning. e Lingual view of tooth 14 after plaque cleaning

Plaque area within the T-Zone was measured on the labial/buccal and lingual surfaces of all six test teeth. Pre- and post-irrigation plaque areas were recorded as m and n, respectively. Using the upper right first premolar (tooth 14) as an example (Fig. 2b–e), the buccal pre- and post-irrigation plaque areas were denoted as m14B and n14B, and the lingual areas as m14L and n14L. The same notation was applied to the remaining teeth. The cumulative pre-irrigation plaque area M and post-irrigation area N were calculated per subject by summing the respective values from all tooth surfaces. The Plaque Removal Rate in the Target Zone (PRT) was then determined as: PRT (%) = [(M ‒ N) / M] × 100%. PRT values from all 12 participants were averaged and analyzed according to the orthogonal experimental design.

To assess measurement reliability, 20 randomly selected scans were re-analyzed by the same examiner and independently by a second examiner. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for PRT measurements was 0.96 (95% CI: 0.92–0.98) for intra-examiner and 0.94 (95% CI: 0.89–0.97) for inter-examiner, indicating excellent reliability.

Validation experiment

Upon completion of the orthogonal experiment and subsequent data analysis, the single optimal parameter combination (A2B2C3) was identified. A new set of personalized guides was fabricated for all twelve participants, incorporating this optimal nozzle diameter (0.5 mm), working distance(1.5 mm), and water pressure (90 psi). The entire plaque removal and 3D quantification protocol (sections "In-vivo plaque removal test protocol" and "3D plaque quantification and analysis") was then repeated in a final experimental session to validate the efficacy of the optimized parameters independently.

Statistical analysis

Data from the orthogonal experiments were first subjected to an intuitive analysis (range analysis) to evaluate the influence of each factor on the Plaque Removal Rate in the Target Zone (PRT) and to identify the favorable level of each factor. The effect magnitude of a factor was expressed by its range (R). Subsequently, a main-effects analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed using SPSS 26.0 to test whether each factor (nozzle diameter, working distance, water pressure) had a statistically significant effect on PRT. The F-value represents the ratio of between-group variance to within-group variance, and indicates the strength of each factor’s influence. A factor was considered statistically significant if its P-value was less than 0.05.

Validation of the optimal parameter combination

A subsequent validation experiment was conducted using the identified optimal combination (A2B2C3: 0.5 mm nozzle diameter, 1.5 mm working distance, 90 psi water pressure). The results, presented in Table 3; Fig. 3, showed that this combination achieved a PRT of 90.21% ± 2.34%. This performance was superior to the best results observed in the initial orthogonal test groups and demonstrated good consistency across participants. The complete validation dataset is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Discussion

Main findings and mechanistic interpretation

This study presents a systematic in-vivo investigation into the optimization of key operational parameters for water jet-mediated plaque removal. By employing an orthogonal experimental design guided by personalized 3D-printed devices, we quantified the relative influence of nozzle diameter, working distance, and water pressure, and identified a specific combination that maximizes efficacy within a clinically relevant Target Zone.

The finding that water pressure exerted the most decisive influence on plaque removal efficacy is mechanistically sound. The primary mechanism of biofilm removal by a fluid jet is hydrodynamic shear stress, which is directly related to the momentum flux of the impacting fluid. Higher pressure translates to greater fluid velocity and momentum, thereby generating higher shear forces capable of disrupting and detaching the biofilm matrix [2, 19]. Our results align with fundamental fluid dynamics principles and underscore pressure as the primary driver of cleaning performance within the tested safe range [28, 29].

The identification of an optimal intermediate working distance (1.5 mm) suggests a balance between competing physical phenomena. At a very short distance, the jet may not have fully developed or might cause unwanted splashback, potentially reducing the effective cleaning area [30, 31]. Conversely, at a greater distance, jet dispersion and aerodynamic drag lead to a decay in velocity and dynamic pressure at the point of impact, diminishing its biofilm-removal capability [30, 31]. The intermediate distance likely represents a “sweet spot” where the jet is sufficiently developed and stable to deliver maximal kinetic energy to the tooth surface [32].

Similarly, the intermediate nozzle diameter (0.5 mm) that yielded the best results may reflect a trade-off between jet coverage and force concentration. A smaller diameter produces a finer, potentially more penetrating jet with higher velocity for a given pressure, but its smaller cross-sectional area may reduce the total cleaning coverage per unit time [33]. A larger diameter increases flow rate and coverage but reduces jet velocity and impact pressure [33]. The optimal diameter of 0.5 mm appears to offer a favorable compromise, ensuring effective localized cleaning while maintaining practical coverage.

Methodological contribution and context

Our results extend previous in-vitro findings. The high plaque removal efficacy (exceeding 90% in the T-Zone) achieved with our optimized parameters is consistent with the high efficiency rates reported in controlled in-vitro studies, yet our data provide crucial in-vivo validation under conditions that more closely mimic clinical use. The optimal pressure of 90 psi identified here falls within the established safety margin for intact tissues, supporting its practical applicability for daily oral care in periodontally healthy individuals [12, 34].

A core strength of this study is its innovative methodology. The use of personalized 3D-printed guides addressed a major confounding factor in previous clinical studies: inconsistent and operator-dependent application of parameters. By physically standardizing the nozzle diameter and working distance for each trial, our approach enhanced the internal validity and precision of the measured parameter effects. The increasing adoption of 3D printing and digital workflows in dental clinical research offers new avenues for device optimization and personalized treatments [35, 36]. Efforts to continuously improve oral biofilm removal techniques are also evident in the development of novel devices such as bioelectric toothbrushes [37]. Furthermore, the 3D quantitative analysis of plaque removal represents an advancement over traditional visual indices. This method provides objective, continuous-scale data with high spatial specificity, reducing subjectivity and increasing sensitivity to detect inter-group differences [27]. The application of intraoral scanning technology in quantitative oral health assessment has substantially improved the precision of diagnosis and treatment [38].

Although this study focused on the T-Zone, water jet irrigation may also affect interproximal and other tooth surfaces. Previous studies have shown that water flossers can reduce interproximal plaque [7–10]. However, our optimized parameters were derived from direct cervical irrigation; their efficacy on proximal surfaces or the entire tooth crown remains to be validated. Future studies should extend the quantitative analysis to whole-tooth surfaces and specifically assess interproximal cleaning using models or 3D assessments.

Clinical translation and future directions

The empirically derived optimal parameter combination (0.5 mm nozzle diameter, 1.5 mm working distance, 90 psi water pressure) could serve as evidence-based references for the design of next-generation oral irrigators and may provide guidance for clinicians and hygienists to recommend effective home-care practices. The integrated digital workflow itself—combining patient-specific design, additive manufacturing, and efficient experimental design—serves as a replicable model for optimizing other dental therapeutic devices or treatment parameters. Moreover, the personalized 3D-guided methodology may support further development beyond single-tooth applications. The design rationale and parameter optimization could be adapted to develop irrigation guides for full-arch or full-mouth use, which might be advantageous for patients with complex restorative needs, fixed orthodontic appliances, or implant-supported prostheses, where conventional oral hygiene measures are often less effective. However, these applications remain conceptual and require further validation.

Several limitations of the present study naturally point toward future investigations. The participant cohort, though adequately sized for an initial optimization study, consisted of periodontally healthy adults. Therefore, the efficacy of these parameters should be evaluated in populations with gingivitis, periodontitis, or dental implants, where biofilm composition and tissue topography differ [39–41]. The irrigation angle was fixed at 90 degrees; exploring other angles could reveal different cleaning efficiencies, especially on proximal surfaces. A further limitation is the exclusive use of the T-Zone for outcome assessment. The results may not directly translate to plaque removal on occlusal, incisal, or entire smooth surfaces. The high removal rate (90.21%) reflects efficacy within the targeted cervical zone, which is clinically the most relevant area for gingival inflammation, but overall whole-mouth plaque reduction might be lower. Future studies should extend quantitative assessment to all surfaces, using models or 3D scans to evaluate cleaning across the entire crown and into interproximal spaces. Although we analyzed PRT at the subject level to average out inter-tooth variability, future studies could stratify the analysis by tooth type (incisors, premolars, molars) or by individual surfaces to assess position-specific effects of water jet parameters. Additionally, our 3D quantitative analysis measured plaque area (planimetry) based on disclosing agent coverage, not plaque volume or biomass. While area-based indices are standard in clinical plaque assessment, they do not capture plaque thickness or biofilm viability. Future studies could employ fluorescence-based imaging or volumetric reconstruction from multiple scans to provide a more comprehensive assessment [42, 43]. Although this study optimized parameters for a continuous water jet, many commercial irrigators use pulsed flow; future systematic optimization of pulsation frequency and duty cycle is needed to extend our findings to pulsed-mode devices. Cavitation effects, which have been shown to enhance biofilm disruption [44, 45], also warrant investigation as a potential mechanism for improving water jet-based oral hygiene.

The use of personalized 3D‑printed guides ensured precise and reproducible parameter delivery, but this does not reflect real‑world conditions where patients hold the irrigator freehand. Consequently, the reported efficacy represents the maximum achievable under idealized conditions; real‑world effectiveness may be lower due to variability in device handling and user technique. For clinical translation, manufacturers could incorporate mechanical stops, distance indicators, or pressure sensors to help users approximate the optimal settings or develop simplified fixed‑distance nozzle designs based on our findings. Additionally, while we controlled and measured parameters at the nozzle, the complex fluid-structure interaction at the microscale biofilm-tooth interface warrants future investigation using techniques like computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations and biomechanical modeling [46, 47]. Fluid dynamics insights, including jet impact mechanics, local shear stress distribution, and multiphase flow behavior, offer a valuable framework for analysis [29, 48]. Applying these principles to the oral environment could clarify how key operating parameters collectively govern the effective mechanical removal of dental plaque. Future research focusing on jet‑tissue interaction and energy transfer efficiency at the biofilm‑tooth interface would further advance mechanistic understanding and guide the rational design of next‑generation oral hygiene devices.

Conclusions

This study applied a digital workflow integrating personalized 3D‑printed guides and orthogonal experimental design to systematically optimize key water jet parameters for in-vivo plaque removal. The results demonstrate that water pressure is the most influential factor, with an optimal combination of 0.5 mm nozzle diameter, 1.5 mm working distance, and 90 psi water pressure delivering consistent and effective plaque clearance within the target zone. By combining engineering principles with clinical methodology, this work provides an evidence‑based, data‑driven approach to improve the design and application of oral hygiene technologies, supporting more personalized and effective preventive care.
