Orthogonal polynomials for the de Rham complex on the disk and cylinder
Sheehan Olver

Abstract
This paper constructs polynomial bases that capture the structure of the de Rham complex with boundary conditions in disks and cylinders (both periodic and finite) in a way that respects rotational symmetry. The starting point is explicit constructions of vector and matrix orthogonal polynomials on the unit disk that are analogous to the (scalar) generalised Zernike polynomials. We use these to build new orthogonal polynomials with respect to a matrix weight that forces vector polynomials to be normal on the boundary of the disk. The resulting weighted vector orthogonal polynomials have a simple connection to the gradient of weighted generalised Zernike polynomials, and their curl (i.e. vorticity or rot) is a constant multiple of the standard Zernike polynomials which are orthogonal with respect to L 2 L^{2} on the disk. This construction naturally leads to bases in cylinders with simple recurrences relating their gradient, curl and divergence. These bases decouple the de Rham complex into small exact sub-complexes.

Introduction

Multivariate orthogonal polynomials (OPs) with respect to an ultraspherical-like inner product on the unit disk

This paper considers vector and matrix analogues of Zernike polynomials with ultraspherical-like inner products leading to simple recurrence relationships for gradients

An important component of our construction is that the bases behave like Fourier series in the sense that rotations correspond to simple equivariant transformations. This is a very simple example of a

Invariance, equivariance, and vector calculus

In this section we discuss invariance and equivariance with respect to rotations and its relationship with vector calculus and symmetry-adapted bases. We denote rotation matrices by

ρ(φ):=\begin{pmatrix}\cos φ&-\sin φ\\
\sin φ&\cos φ\end{pmatrix}

(4)

and
the linear operator corresponding to a rotating the variables by
φ
as

{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}u({\bf x}):=u(ρ(φ){\bf x}).

(5)

2.1
Invariance and Equivariance

An important notion is invariance to rotations:

Definition 1
.

A scalar function
f:Ω\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
is invariant (to rotations) if it satisfies:

{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}f({\bf x})=f({\bf x}).

In other words,

f(ρ(φ){\bf x})=f({\bf x}).

To extend this notion to vector-valued functions we need to incorporate a notion of equivariance: rather than functions not changing under rotations, the analogue are functions where the vector output is also rotated.
It is natural to view vector-valued functions as maps
Ω\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
, that is to a Euclidean vector space.
But the notion of equivariance is more natural when discussed in terms of tangent spaces.

Definition 2
.

Denote the (complex)
tangent space
at
{\bf x}\in Ω
as:

{\mathcal{T}}_{\bf x}:=\{a{\bf e}_{r}^{{\bf x}}+b{\bf e}_{θ}^{{\bf x}}:a,b\in{\mathbb{C}}\}.

for the two orthogonal vectors

{\bf e}_{r}^{{\bf x}}:={1\over r}{\begin{pmatrix}x\cr y\cr\end{pmatrix}}={\begin{pmatrix}\cos θ\cr\sin θ\cr\end{pmatrix}},\qquad{\bf e}_{θ}^{{\bf x}}:={1\over r}{\begin{pmatrix}-y\cr x\cr\end{pmatrix}}={\begin{pmatrix}-\sin θ\cr\cos θ\cr\end{pmatrix}}

(6)

where
r=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}
,
x=r\cos θ
and
y=r\sin θ
. When
{\bf x}
is clear from context we write
{\bf e}_{r}
and
{\bf e}_{θ}
.

Rotations map these vectors via the rule:

ρ(φ){\bf e}_{r}^{{\bf x}}={\mathcal{R}}_{φ}{\bf e}_{r}^{{\bf x}}={\bf e}_{r}^{ρ(φ){\bf x}},\qquad ρ(φ){\bf e}_{θ}^{{\bf x}}={\mathcal{R}}_{φ}{\bf e}_{θ}^{{\bf x}}={\bf e}_{θ}^{ρ(φ){\bf x}}.

(7)

Thus we can interpret multiplying a vector by
ρ(φ)
as a map from an element in the tangent space of
{\bf x}
to
the tangent space of
ρ(φ){\bf x}
. More precisely, rotation induces a map
ρ(φ):{\mathcal{T}}_{\bf x}\rightarrow{\mathcal{T}}_{ρ(φ){\bf x}}
via the formula

ρ(φ)\underbrace{(a{\bf e}_{r}^{{\bf x}}+b{\bf e}_{θ}^{{\bf x}})}_{\in{\mathcal{T}}_{{\bf x}}}=\underbrace{a{\bf e}_{r}^{ρ(φ){\bf x}}+b{\bf e}_{θ}^{ρ(φ){\bf x}}}_{\in{\mathcal{T}}_{ρ(φ){\bf x}}}.

A vector-valued function is equivariant if we have

\underbrace{{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}𝐟({\bf x})}_{\in{\mathcal{T}}_{ρ(φ){\bf x}}}=\underbrace{ρ(φ)}_{{\mathcal{T}}_{\bf x}\rightarrow{\mathcal{T}}_{ρ(φ){\bf x}}}\underbrace{𝐟({\bf x})}_{\in{\mathcal{T}}_{{\bf x}}}.

But in this case
{\mathcal{T}}_{\bf x}={\mathcal{T}}_{ρ(φ){\bf x}}={\mathbb{C}}^{2}
so we can re-express this relationship without using tangent spaces

1

à la

:

Definition 3
.

A vector-valued function
𝐟:Ω\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
is equivariant (to rotations) if it satisfies:

{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}𝐟=ρ(φ)𝐟.

In other words,

𝐟(ρ(φ){\bf x})=ρ(φ)𝐟({\bf x}).

Note that a constant vector-valued function like
{\bf x}\mapsto{{\left({1,0}\right)}^{\top}}
is invariant to rotations but it is
not equivariant
.
On the other hand, the functions
{\bf x}\mapsto{\bf x}
and
{\bf x}\mapsto{{\left({-y,x}\right)}^{\top}}
are equivariant.

We can also interpret matrix-valued functions
F:Ω\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}^{2\times 2}
as linear operators acting on tangent spaces, i.e.,
F({\bf x}):{\mathcal{T}}_{{\bf x}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{T}}_{{\bf x}}
. With this in mind we extend the notion of equivarance to matrices by ensuring that we transform the space they act on in an appropriate way:

\underbrace{F(ρ(φ){\bf x})}_{{\mathcal{T}}_{ρ(φ){\bf x}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{T}}_{ρ(φ){\bf x}}}\quad\underbrace{ρ(φ)}_{{\mathcal{T}}_{{\bf x}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{T}}_{ρ(φ){\bf x}}}=\underbrace{ρ(φ)}_{{\mathcal{T}}_{{\bf x}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{T}}_{ρ(φ){\bf x}}}\quad\underbrace{F({\bf x})}_{{\mathcal{T}}_{{\bf x}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{T}}_{{\bf x}}}.

Dropping the use of tangent spaces we have the following:

Definition 4
.

A matrix-valued function
F:Ω\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}^{2\times 2}
is equivariant (to rotations) if it satisfies:

{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}Fρ(φ)=ρ(φ)F.

That is to say

F(ρ(φ){\bf x})ρ(φ)=ρ(φ)F({\bf x}).

Remark 1
.

We will use right-associativity for operators, so that in the above notation we have

{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}Fρ(φ)={\mathcal{R}}_{φ}[Fρ(φ)].

Though in this case multiplication by a matrix is independent of the change-of-variables so associativity does not impact the calculation as we have:

{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}[Fρ(φ)]({\bf x})=F(ρ(φ){\bf x})ρ(φ)={\mathcal{R}}_{φ}[F]({\bf x})ρ(φ)

A trivial example of an equivariant matrix-valued functions is the identity
{\bf x}\mapsto I
.
Indeed, any function of the form
f(r)I
is equivariant.
Another example which we will use throughout the paper is

Σ(x,y):=\begin{pmatrix}x^{2}-y^{2}&2xy\\
2xy&y^{2}-x^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(8)

To see this is equivariant it helps to express the matrix in terms of
{\bf e}_{r}^{\bf x}
and
{\bf e}_{θ}^{\bf x}
:

Σ(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}x&-y\\
y&x\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
&-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x&y\\
-y&x\end{pmatrix}={\begin{pmatrix}\,{\bf e}_{r}^{{\bf x}}\ |\ {\bf e}_{θ}^{{\bf x}}\,\end{pmatrix}}\begin{pmatrix}r^{2}\\
&-r^{2}\end{pmatrix}{\begin{pmatrix}({\bf e}_{r}^{{\bf x}})^{\top}\cr({\bf e}_{θ}^{{\bf x}})^{\top}\cr\end{pmatrix}}.

This implies that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}Σ({\bf x})ρ(φ)\;

\displaystyle={\begin{pmatrix}\,{\bf e}_{r}^{ρ(φ){\bf x}}\ |\ {\bf e}_{θ}^{ρ(φ){\bf x}}\,\end{pmatrix}}\begin{pmatrix}r^{2}\\
&-r^{2}\end{pmatrix}{\begin{pmatrix}({\bf e}_{r}^{ρ(φ){\bf x}})^{\top}\cr({\bf e}_{θ}^{ρ(φ){\bf x}})^{\top}\cr\end{pmatrix}}ρ(φ)

\displaystyle=ρ(φ){\begin{pmatrix}\,{\bf e}_{r}^{{\bf x}}\ |\ {\bf e}_{θ}^{{\bf x}}\,\end{pmatrix}}\begin{pmatrix}r^{2}\\
&-r^{2}\end{pmatrix}{\begin{pmatrix}({\bf e}_{r}^{{\bf x}})^{\top}\cr({\bf e}_{θ}^{{\bf x}})^{\top}\cr\end{pmatrix}}=ρ(φ)Σ({\bf x}).

2.2
Symmetry-adapted bases

To incorporate rotational symmetry into a basis of functions that are not invariant or equivariant (in the sense used in the previous section) we need
to allow change with rotations, but where this change is equivalent to multiplication

2

2

equivariance
but we reserve the term equivariance for the definitions in Section
2.1
.
by
{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mφ}
.
These correspond to irreducible representations of the group
SO(2)
, an analogy which motivates the terminology
of what follows, see
[
23
]
.

Definition 5
.

A
(scalar) symmetry-adapted function

p:Ω\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
with mode
m
is one where a rotation becomes multiplication:

{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}p=p{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mφ},

i.e.,

p(ρ(φ){\bf x})=p({\bf x}){\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mφ}.

A
(scalar) symmetry-adapted basis
is one whose basis elements are symmetry-adapted functions.

The basis of harmonic polynomials defined in the introduction form a symmetry-adapted basis:
{\mathrm{y}}_{m}({\bf x})
is symmetry adapted with mode
m
since we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}{\mathrm{y}}_{m}({\bf x})\;

\displaystyle={\mathrm{y}}_{m}(x\cos φ-y\sin φ,x\sin φ+y\cos φ)=r^{|m|}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}m(θ+φ)}={\mathrm{y}}_{m}({\bf x}){\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mφ}.

In the vector case we also rotate the vector in accordance with the principle of equivariance:

Definition 6
.

A
vector symmetry-adapted function

{\bf p}:Ω\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
with mode
m
satisfies

{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}{\bf p}=ρ(φ){\bf p}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mφ},

i.e.,

{\bf p}(ρ(φ){\bf x})=ρ(φ){\bf p}({\bf x}){\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mφ}.

A
vector symmetry-adapted basis
is one whose basis elements are vector symmetry-adapted functions.

Note if
{\bf p}
is a vector symmetry-adapted function and
G
is a matrix equivariant function then
G{\bf p}
is a vector symmetry-adapted function
with the same mode as
{\bf p}
since

ρ(φ)G{\bf p}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mφ}={\mathcal{R}}_{φ}[G]ρ(φ){\bf p}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mφ}={\mathcal{R}}_{φ}[G]{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}[{\bf p}]={\mathcal{R}}_{φ}[G{\bf p}].

We can extend this notion to matrix-valued functions as well by rotating the vectors they act on appropriately:

Definition 7
.

A
matrix symmetry-adapted function

P:Ω\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}^{2\times 2}
with mode
m
satisfies

{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}Pρ(φ)=ρ(φ)P{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mφ},

i.e.,

P(ρ(φ){\bf x})ρ(φ)=ρ(φ)P({\bf x}){\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mφ}.

A
matrix symmetry-adapted basis
is one whose basis elements are matrix symmetry-adapted functions.

An important feature of symmetry-adapted bases is that symmetry-adapted functions with different modes are
automatically orthogonal. To avoid issues with regularity we state the results in this paper in terms of
symmetry-adapted polynomials
:

Lemma 1
.

If
a
and
b
are scalar symmetry-adapted polynomials with respect to modes
m
and
n
, respectively, with
m≠n
then
\left\langle{a,b}\right\rangle_{w}=0
where, for any invariant weight
w
,

\left\langle{a,b}\right\rangle_{w}:=\int\!\!\!\!\int_{Ω}\bar{a}({\bf x})b({\bf x})w({\bf x})\hbox{d}{\bf x}.

If
{\bf a}
and
{\bf b}
are scalar symmetry-adapted polynomials with respect to modes
m
and
n
, respectively, with
m≠n
then
\left\langle{{\bf a},{\bf b}}\right\rangle_{W}=0
where, for any matrix equivariant weight
W
,

\left\langle{{\bf a},{\bf b}}\right\rangle_{W}:=\int\!\!\!\!\int_{Ω}{\bf a}({\bf x})^{\star}W({\bf x}){\bf b}({\bf x})\hbox{d}{\bf x}.

If
A
and
B
are matrix symmetry-adapted polynomials with respect to modes
m
and
n
, respectively, with
m≠n
then

\left⟪A,B\right⟫_{w}=0

where, for any invariant weight
w
, we define the matrix inner product

\left⟪A,B\right⟫_{w}:=\int\!\!\!\!\int_{Ω}\left\langle{A({\bf x}),B({\bf x})}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}w({\bf x})\hbox{d}{\bf x},

for the Fröbenius inner product
\left\langle{F,G}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}:=\mathrm{Tr}(F^{\star}G)
.

Proof.

First note that we know the adjoint of a rotation, i.e., for any
a,b
,

\left\langle{{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}a,b}\right\rangle_{w}=\int\!\!\!\!\int_{Ω}\bar{a}(ρ(φ){\bf x})b({\bf x})w(r)\hbox{d}{\bf x}=\int\!\!\!\!\int_{Ω}\bar{a}({\bf x})b(ρ(-φ){\bf x})|\det ρ(φ)|w(r)\hbox{d}{\bf x}=\left\langle{a,{\mathcal{R}}_{-φ}b}\right\rangle_{w}.

We therefore have for all
φ

{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mφ}\left\langle{a,b}\right\rangle_{w}=\left\langle{{\rm e}^{-\mathrm{i}mφ}a,b}\right\rangle_{w}=\left\langle{{\mathcal{R}}_{-φ}a,b}\right\rangle_{w}=\left\langle{a,{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}b}\right\rangle_{w}=\left\langle{a,b}\right\rangle_{w}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}nφ}

which shows that
\left\langle{a,b}\right\rangle_{w}=0
when
m≠n
(which as explained in the introduction is a trivial version of Schur’s lemma).
In the vector case, using the equivariance of
W
in the form
ρ(φ)W(ρ(-φ){\bf x})=W({\bf x})ρ(φ)
, we have for, any
{\bf a},{\bf b}
,

\displaystyle\left\langle{ρ(-φ){\mathcal{R}}_{φ}{\bf a},{\bf b}}\right\rangle_{W}\;

\displaystyle=\int\!\!\!\!\int_{Ω}{\bf a}(ρ(φ){\bf x})^{\star}ρ(φ)W({\bf x}){\bf b}({\bf x})\hbox{d}{\bf x}

\displaystyle=\int\!\!\!\!\int_{Ω}{\bf a}({\bf x})^{\star}ρ(φ)W(ρ(-φ){\bf x}){\bf b}(ρ(-φ){\bf x})|\!\det ρ(φ)|\hbox{d}{\bf x}

\displaystyle=\int\!\!\!\!\int_{Ω}{\bf a}({\bf x})^{\star}W({\bf x})ρ(φ){\bf b}(ρ(-φ){\bf x})\hbox{d}{\bf x}=\left\langle{{\bf a},ρ(φ){\mathcal{R}}_{-φ}{\bf b}}\right\rangle_{W}.

It follows that, for all
φ
,

{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mφ}\left\langle{{\bf a},{\bf b}}\right\rangle_{W}=\left\langle{ρ(φ){\mathcal{R}}_{-φ}{\bf a},{\bf b}}\right\rangle_{W}=\left\langle{{\bf a},ρ(-φ){\mathcal{R}}_{φ}{\bf b}}\right\rangle_{W}=\left\langle{{\bf a},{\bf b}}\right\rangle_{W}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}nφ},

and hence
\left\langle{{\bf a},{\bf b}}\right\rangle_{W}=0
when
m≠n
.

Finally, in the matrix case we first note since the trace is invariant to conjugation by an orthogonal matrix we have (for any matrices
A,B
)

\displaystyle\left\langle{ρ(-φ)Aρ(φ),B}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}\;

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}(ρ(-φ)A^{\star}ρ(φ)B)=\mathrm{Tr}(ρ(-φ)A^{\star}ρ(φ)Bρ(-φ)ρ(φ))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}(A^{\star}ρ(φ)Bρ(-φ))=\left\langle{A,ρ(φ)Bρ(-φ)}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}.

Hence, for any matrix polynomials
A,B
,

\displaystyle\left⟪ρ(-φ){\mathcal{R}}_{φ}Aρ(φ),B\right⟫_{w}\;

\displaystyle=\int\!\!\!\!\int_{Ω}\left\langle{ρ(-φ)A(ρ(φ){\bf x})ρ(φ),B({\bf x})}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}w({\bf x})\hbox{d}{\bf x}

\displaystyle=\int\!\!\!\!\int_{Ω}\left\langle{A(ρ(φ){\bf x}),ρ(φ)B({\bf x})ρ(-φ)}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}w({\bf x})\hbox{d}{\bf x}

\displaystyle=\int\!\!\!\!\int_{Ω}\left\langle{A({\bf x}),ρ(φ)B(ρ(-φ){\bf x})ρ(-φ)}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}w(ρ(-φ){\bf x})\hbox{d}{\bf x}

\displaystyle=\left⟪A,ρ(φ){\mathcal{R}}_{-φ}Bρ(-φ)\right⟫_{w}.

Thus we have, for matrix symmetry-adapted polynomials
A,B
with respect to
m,n
and all
φ
,

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mφ}\left⟪A,B\right⟫_{w}\;

\displaystyle=\left⟪{\rm e}^{-\mathrm{i}mφ}A,B\right⟫_{w}=\left⟪ρ(φ){\mathcal{R}}_{-φ}Aρ(-φ),B\right⟫_{w}

\displaystyle=\left⟪A,ρ(-φ){\mathcal{R}}_{φ}Bρ(φ)\right⟫_{w}=\left⟪A,B\right⟫_{w}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}nφ}.

Therefore
\left⟪A,B\right⟫_{w}=0
when
m≠n
.

∎

2.3
Vector calculus

An important feature of a symmetry-adapted function is that applying differential operators
with certain rotational symmetries maintain the mode. Here we consider basic differential operators:

\displaystyle\nabla

\displaystyle:={\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{x}\cr\partial_{y}\cr\end{pmatrix}}={\bf e}_{r}\partial_{r}+{1\over r}{\bf e}_{θ}\partial_{θ},

(Gradient)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\perp}

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\nabla={\begin{pmatrix}-\partial_{y}\cr\partial_{x}\cr\end{pmatrix}}={\bf e}_{θ}\partial_{r}-{1\over r}{\bf e}_{r}\partial_{θ},

(Rotated Gradient)

\displaystyle\divergence

\displaystyle:=\nabla^{\top}={\left({\partial_{x},\partial_{y}}\right)}={1\over r}\left[{\partial_{r}r{\bf e}_{r}^{\top}+\partial_{θ}{\bf e}_{θ}^{\top}}\right],

(Divergence)

\displaystyle\curl

\displaystyle:=\nabla_{\perp}^{\top}={\left({-\partial_{y},\partial_{x}}\right)}=\divergence\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}}_{ρ(-π/2)}={1\over r}\left[{\partial_{r}r{\bf e}_{θ}^{\top}-\partial_{θ}{\bf e}_{r}^{\top}}\right]

(Curl)

\displaystyle Δ

\displaystyle:=\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2}=\divergence\nabla=\partial_{r}^{2}+{1\over r}\partial_{r}+{1\over r^{2}}\partial_{θ}^{2},

(Laplacian)

where
\partial_{r}={x\partial_{x}+y\partial_{y}\over r}
and
\partial_{θ}=x\partial_{y}-y\partial_{x}
. The formula for curl follows by transposing
ρ(π/2){\bf e}_{r}={\bf e}_{θ}
and
ρ(π/2){\bf e}_{θ}=-{\bf e}_{r}
. To clarify the notation, note here that any vector-valued function
{\bf f}:Ω\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
can be written

{\bf f}({\bf x})=g_{1}({\bf x}){\bf e}_{r}+g_{2}({\bf x}){\bf e}_{θ}

where
{\bf e}_{r}^{\top}{\bf f}=g_{1}
and
{\bf e}_{θ}^{\top}{\bf f}=g_{2}
.
Hence, for example, the divergence and curl formulae are given by

\divergence{\bf f}={\partial_{r}[rg_{1}]+\partial_{θ}g_{2}\over r},\qquad\curl{\bf f}={\partial_{r}[rg_{2}]-\partial_{θ}g_{1}\over r}.

Each of these operators intertwines with rotations in the following senses, where to avoid issues of regularity we assume the operations are acting on polynomials:

Lemma 2
.

For all polynomials we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}\nabla\;

\displaystyle=ρ(φ)\nabla{\mathcal{R}}_{φ},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}\nabla_{\perp}

\displaystyle=ρ(φ)\nabla_{\perp}{\mathcal{R}}_{φ},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}\divergence ρ(φ)\;

\displaystyle=\divergence{\mathcal{R}}_{φ},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}\curl ρ(φ)

\displaystyle=\curl{\mathcal{R}}_{φ},

Therefore
{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}Δ=Δ{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}
.

Proof.

Note that the polar coordinate partial derivatives commute with rotations:

\partial_{r}{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}={\mathcal{R}}_{φ}\partial_{r},\qquad\partial_{θ}{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}={\mathcal{R}}_{φ}\partial_{θ}.

This combined with (
7
) shows the result for the gradient and divergence:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}\nabla\;

\displaystyle={\mathcal{R}}_{φ}\left[{{\bf e}_{r}^{{\bf x}}\partial_{r}+{1\over r}{\bf e}_{θ}^{{\bf x}}\partial_{θ}}\right]=\left[{{\bf e}_{r}^{ρ(φ){\bf x}}\partial_{r}+{1\over r}{\bf e}_{θ}^{ρ(φ){\bf x}}\partial_{θ}}\right]{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}=ρ(φ)\nabla{\mathcal{R}}_{φ},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}\divergence\;

\displaystyle={1\over r}\left[{\partial_{r}r({\bf e}_{r}^{ρ(φ){\bf x}})^{\top}+\partial_{θ}({\bf e}_{θ}^{ρ(φ){\bf x}})^{\top}}\right]{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}=\divergence ρ(-φ){\mathcal{R}}_{φ}.

The formulæ for rotated gradient and curl follow:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}\nabla_{\perp}\;

\displaystyle=ρ(π/2){\mathcal{R}}_{φ}\nabla=ρ(π/2+φ)\nabla{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}=ρ(φ)\nabla_{\perp}{\mathcal{R}}_{φ},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}\curl\;

\displaystyle={\mathcal{R}}_{φ}\divergence ρ(-π/2)=\divergence ρ(-π/2-φ){\mathcal{R}}_{φ}=\curl ρ(-φ){\mathcal{R}}_{φ}.

Finally, the Laplacian follows from combining the divergence and gradient via:

Δ{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}=\divergence\nabla{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}=\divergence ρ(-φ){\mathcal{R}}_{φ}\nabla={\mathcal{R}}_{φ}Δ.

∎

Corollary 1
.

Each differential operator maps a symmetry-adapted polynomial
to another symmetry-adapted polynomial with the same mode.

Proof.

Suppose
p
is a scalar symmetry-adapted polynomial with mode
m
. Then
\nabla p
satisfies:

{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}\nabla p=ρ(φ)\nabla{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}p=ρ(φ)\nabla p{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mφ}.

If
{\bf p}
is a vector symmetry-adapted polynomial with mode
m
then

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}\divergence{\bf p}\;

\displaystyle={\mathcal{R}}_{φ}\divergence ρ(φ)ρ(-φ){\bf p}=\divergence{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}ρ(-φ){\bf p}=\divergence{\bf p}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mφ},

with the exact same argument showing the curl case. Finally the Laplacian case comes from combining the gradient and divergence.
∎

The results extend to the transpose of the Jacobian matrix which we denote:

\nabla{\bf f}^{\top}:=\begin{pmatrix}f_{1x}&f_{2x}\\
f_{1y}&f_{2y}\end{pmatrix}.

Corollary 2
.

If
{\bf p}
is a vector symmetry-adapted polynomial with mode
m
then
\nabla{\bf p}^{\top}
is a matrix symmetry-adapted polynomial with the same mode.

Proof.

Using the formula for gradients in Lemma
2
we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}\nabla{\bf p}^{\top}ρ(φ)\;

\displaystyle=ρ(φ)\nabla(ρ(-φ){\mathcal{R}}_{φ}{\bf p})^{\top}=ρ(φ)\nabla({\bf p}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mφ})^{\top}=ρ(φ)\nabla{\bf p}^{\top}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mφ}.

∎

This will guarantee orthogonality when combined with Lemma
1
.

3
Symmetry-adapted homogeneous polynomials

The aim of this section is to construct symmetry-adapted bases for homogeneous scalar, vector, and matrix polynomials.
We will later see that they can be orthogonalised in closed form for simple ultraspherical-like weights on the disk in terms of Jacobi polynomials.
Our general approach is to relate values of functions at
{\bf x}=r{{\left({\cos θ,\sin θ}\right)}^{\top}}
to the value rotated to
θ=0
, that is
at
r{\bf e}_{1}={{\left({r,0}\right)}^{\top}}
.

3.1
Scalar homogeneous polynomials

We present the scalar case in a way that will generalise to the vector and matrix case. The formula for a symmetry-adapted function,
{\mathrm{y}}_{m}(ρ(φ){\bf x})={\mathrm{y}}_{m}({\bf x}){\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mφ}
,
can be used to deduce the harmonic polynomials from values on the
x
-axis:

{\mathrm{y}}_{m}({\bf x})={\mathrm{y}}_{m}(r,0){\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mφ}=r^{|m|}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mφ}.

Note that
r^{2k}{\mathrm{y}}_{m}({\bf x})
are symmetry-adapted homogeneous polynomials of degree
|m|+2k
.
Combining these symmetry-adapted polynomials we can form a symmetry-adapted basis for all homogeneous polynomials
(and thence all polynomials):

Proposition 1
.

A symmetry-adapted basis of degree
n
homogeneous polynomials is given by, for
n
even,

{\mathrm{y}}_{\textpm n},r^{2}{\mathrm{y}}_{\textpm(n-2)},\ldots,r^{n-2}{\mathrm{y}}_{\textpm 2},r^{n}{\mathrm{y}}_{0},

and for
n
odd

{\mathrm{y}}_{\textpm n},r^{2}{\mathrm{y}}_{\textpm(n-2)},\ldots,r^{n-1}{\mathrm{y}}_{\textpm 1}.

Proof.

We need to show this is a basis, i.e., they are linearly indepedent and their spans have the correct dimension.
Note there are exactly
(n+1)
-polynomials of degree
n
.
As they all have different modes they are orthogonal, by Lemma
1
.

∎

3.2
Vector homogeneous polynomials

The gradient of the basis of harmonic polynomials is

\displaystyle\nabla{\mathrm{y}}_{m}

\displaystyle=\nabla[r^{|m|}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mθ}]=|m|r^{|m|-1}({\bf e}_{r}+\mathrm{i}\sign m\,{\bf e}_{θ}){\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mθ}.

From Corollary
1
we know that
\nabla{\mathrm{y}}_{m}
is a vector symmetry-adapted function with mode
m
,
that is it satisfies:

{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}\nabla y_{m}({\bf x})=ρ(φ)\nabla y_{m}({\bf x}){\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mφ}.

As in the scalar case, this formula gives an expression in terms of the value at
θ=0
:

\nabla{\mathrm{y}}_{m}({\bf x})=ρ(θ)\nabla{\mathrm{y}}_{m}(r,0){\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mθ}=|m|r^{|m|-1}ρ(θ){\begin{pmatrix}1\cr\mathrm{i}\sign m\cr\end{pmatrix}}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mθ}.

We use this to define the following vector analogues of a basis of harmonic polynomials, removing the multiplication by
m
to extend the definition to
m=0
:

Definition 8
.

\displaystyle{\bf y}_{0}({\bf x})

\displaystyle:=r^{-1}ρ(θ){\begin{pmatrix}1\cr\mathrm{i}\cr\end{pmatrix}}=r^{-1}\left({{\bf e}_{r}+\mathrm{i}{\bf e}_{θ}}\right)=r^{-2}\begin{pmatrix}x-\mathrm{i}y\\
y+\mathrm{i}x\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle{\bf y}_{m}({\bf x})

\displaystyle:=r^{|m|-1}ρ(θ){\begin{pmatrix}1\cr\mathrm{i}\sign m\cr\end{pmatrix}}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mθ}=r^{|m|-1}\left({{\bf e}_{r}+\mathrm{i}\sign m{\bf e}_{θ}}\right){\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mθ},\qquad m≠0.

For
m≠0
these are also homogeneous polynomials of degree
|m|-1
(since they are rescaled versions of
\nabla{\mathrm{y}}_{m}
and partial derivatives map homogeneous polynomials to homogeneous polynomials).
For
m=0
, where it is no longer the gradient of a harmonic polynomial, it is a degree 1 homogeneous polynomial divided by
r^{2}
.
Note that constant vectors are
not
equivariant, they are spanned by vectors with mode
\textpm 1
:

{\bf y}_{\textpm 1}({\bf x})={\begin{pmatrix}1\cr\textpm\mathrm{i}\cr\end{pmatrix}}.

Now
r^{2k}{\bf y}_{m}
will also be a vector symmetry-adapted polynomial with mode
m
but of degree
|m|+2k-1
for
m≠0
or
k>1
. However, these polynomials do not span all polynomials.
Using the matrix equivariant function
Σ
introduced in (
8
) allows us to construct a complete basis of homogeneous polynomials. For example,
we have two linearly independent degree
1
equivariant polynomials given by:

r^{2}{\bf y}_{0}={\begin{pmatrix}x-\mathrm{i}y\cr y+\mathrm{i}x\cr\end{pmatrix}},\qquad Σ{\bf y}_{0}={\begin{pmatrix}x+\mathrm{i}y\cr y-\mathrm{i}x\cr\end{pmatrix}}.

To show this style of modification gives us a complete basis of linearly independent polynomials for general
m
we establish an orthogonality property:

Lemma 3
.

If
{\bf a},{\bf b}:Ω\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
are vector symmetry-adapted polynomials with mode
m

and
{\bf a}(r,0)=f(r){\begin{pmatrix}1\cr\textpm\mathrm{i}\cr\end{pmatrix}}
,
{\bf b}(r,0)=g(r){\begin{pmatrix}1\cr\textpm\mathrm{i}\cr\end{pmatrix}}
then

\left\langle{{\bf a},Σ{\bf b}}\right\rangle_{w}=0
.

Proof.

We find:

\displaystyle\left\langle{{\bf a},Σ{\bf b}}\right\rangle_{w}\;

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{2π}{\bf a}(rρ(θ){\bf e}_{1})^{\star}Σ(rρ(θ){\bf e}_{1}){\bf b}(rρ(θ){\bf e}_{1})\hbox{d}θw(r)r\hbox{d}r

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2π}{\rm e}^{-\mathrm{i}mθ}\int_{0}^{1}{\bf a}(r,0)^{\star}Σ(r,0){\bf b}(r,0)w(r)r\hbox{d}r{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mθ}\hbox{d}θ

\displaystyle=2π\int_{0}^{1}\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}1&\mp\mathrm{i}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
&-1\end{pmatrix}{\begin{pmatrix}1\cr\textpm\mathrm{i}\cr\end{pmatrix}}}_{=0}f(r)g(r)w(r)r^{3}\hbox{d}r=0.

∎

We thus can construct a complete basis of homogeneous vector polynomials that are symmetry-adapted:

Lemma 4
.

A symmetry-adapted basis of degree
n
homogeneous vector polynomials is given by,
for
n
even,

\displaystyle{\bf y}_{\textpm(n+1)},r^{2}{\bf y}_{\textpm(n-1)},\ldots,r^{n}{\bf y}_{\textpm 1},

\displaystyle Σ{\bf y}_{\textpm(n-1)},r^{2}Σ{\bf y}_{\textpm(n-3)},\ldots,r^{n-2}Σ{\bf y}_{\textpm 1}.

and, for
n
odd,

\displaystyle{\bf y}_{\textpm(n+1)},r^{2}{\bf y}_{\textpm(n-1)},\ldots,r^{n-1}{\bf y}_{\textpm 2},r^{n+1}{\bf y}_{0},

\displaystyle Σ{\bf y}_{\textpm(n-1)},r^{2}Σ{\bf y}_{\textpm(n-3)},\ldots,r^{n-3}Σ{\bf y}_{\textpm 2},r^{n-1}Σ{\bf y}_{0},

where again

Σ(x,y):=\begin{pmatrix}x^{2}-y^{2}&2xy\\
2xy&y^{2}-x^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

We will show this is in fact a complete orthogonal basis of degree
n
homogeneous polynomials. In each case there are
2(n+1)
homogeneous polynomials and hence we only need to show orthogonality. We first note that
r^{2k}Σ{\bf y}_{m}
is a vector symmetry-adapted function, as

{\mathcal{R}}_{φ}r^{2k}Σ{\bf y}_{m}=r^{2k}ρ(φ)Σρ(φ)^{\top}ρ(φ){\bf y}_{m}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mφ}=r^{2k}ρ(φ)Σ{\bf y}_{m}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mφ}.

As in the scalar case, vector symmetry-adapted polynomials corresponding to different modes are orthogonal (Lemma
1
). The functions with the same degree and mode,
r^{2k}{\bf y}_{m}
and
r^{2k-2}Σ{\bf y}_{m}
, are also orthogonal by the previous lemma.

∎

3.3
Matrix homogeneous polynomials

We now consider the transpose of the Jacobian of the vector analogues of harmonic polynomials. We compute, for
m≠0
,

\displaystyle\nabla{\bf y}_{m}^{\top}\;

\displaystyle=(|m|-1)r^{|m|-2}\left[{{\bf e}_{r}{\bf e}_{r}^{\top}-{\bf e}_{θ}{\bf e}_{θ}^{\top}+\mathrm{i}\sign m\left({{\bf e}_{r}{\bf e}_{θ}^{\top}+{\bf e}_{θ}{\bf e}_{r}^{\top}}\right)}\right]{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mθ}

which by Corollary
2
is a matrix symmetry-adapted polynomial with mode
m
.
As in the vector case we can relate values of these to those on the
x
-axis, where we have

\displaystyle\nabla{\bf y}_{m}^{\top}(r,0)\;

\displaystyle=(|m|-1)r^{|m|-2}\begin{pmatrix}1&\mathrm{i}\sign m\\
\mathrm{i}\sign m&-1\end{pmatrix}.

Dividing by
|m|-1
motivates the definition of a matrix-analogue of harmonic polynomials, with a natural extension to
m=0
:

Definition 9
.

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Y}}_{0}({\bf x})

\displaystyle:=r^{-2}ρ(θ)\begin{pmatrix}1&\mathrm{i}\\
\mathrm{i}&-1\end{pmatrix}ρ(-θ)=\begin{pmatrix}1&\mathrm{i}\\
\mathrm{i}&-1\end{pmatrix}r^{-2}{\rm e}^{-2\mathrm{i}θ},

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Y}}_{m}({\bf x})

\displaystyle:=r^{|m|-2}ρ(θ)\begin{pmatrix}1&\mathrm{i}\sign m\\
\mathrm{i}\sign m&-1\end{pmatrix}ρ(-θ){\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mθ}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&\mathrm{i}\sign m\\
\mathrm{i}\sign m&-1\end{pmatrix}r^{|m|-2}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}(m-2\sign m)θ},\qquad m≠0.

These are degree
|m|-2
homogeneous polynomials for
|m|≥2
by their relationship with the gradient. In particular, we have two constant functions, with mode
\textpm 2
:

{\mathrm{Y}}_{\textpm 2}({\bf x})=\begin{pmatrix}1&\textpm\mathrm{i}\\
\textpm\mathrm{i}&-1\end{pmatrix}.

For
m=0
and
m=\textpm 1
we note that it is a polynomial divided by
r^{4}
and
r^{2}
, respectively:

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Y}}_{0}({\bf x})\;

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&\mathrm{i}\\
\mathrm{i}&-1\end{pmatrix}{(x-\mathrm{i}y)^{2}\over r^{4}},\qquad{\mathrm{Y}}_{\textpm 1}({\bf x})=\begin{pmatrix}1&\textpm\mathrm{i}\\
\textpm\mathrm{i}&-1\end{pmatrix}{x\mp\mathrm{i}y\over r^{2}}.

We can multiply these by
r^{2k}
to get families of homogeneous polynomials of degree
2k+|m|-2
when
|m|≥2
,
m=\textpm 1
and
k≥1
, or
k≥2
. We can also multiply by
Σ
on the left and right, which will increase the degree by
2
whilst maintaining the mode.
Note that

\displaystyle Σ(x,y)\begin{pmatrix}1&\textpm\mathrm{i}\\
\textpm\mathrm{i}&-1\end{pmatrix}\;

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&\textpm\mathrm{i}\\
\mp\mathrm{i}&1\end{pmatrix}(x\textpm\mathrm{i}y)^{2},\qquad\begin{pmatrix}1&\textpm\mathrm{i}\\
\textpm\mathrm{i}&-1\end{pmatrix}Σ(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}1&\mp\mathrm{i}\\
\textpm\mathrm{i}&1\end{pmatrix}(x\textpm\mathrm{i}y)^{2},

\displaystyle Σ(x,y)\begin{pmatrix}1&\textpm\mathrm{i}\\
\textpm\mathrm{i}&-1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle Σ(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}1&\mp\mathrm{i}\\
\mp\mathrm{i}&-1\end{pmatrix}(x\textpm\mathrm{i}y)^{4}.

We can establish orthogonality via a matrix analogue of Lemma
3
:

Lemma 5
.

If
A
and
B
are matrix symmetry-adapted polynomials with the same mode
m

and
A(r,0)=f(r)\begin{pmatrix}1&\textpm\mathrm{i}\\
\textpm\mathrm{i}&-1\end{pmatrix}
,
B(r,0)=g(r)\begin{pmatrix}1&\textpm\mathrm{i}\\
\textpm\mathrm{i}&-1\end{pmatrix}
then

\left⟪A,ΣB\right⟫_{w}=\left⟪A,BΣ\right⟫_{w}=\left⟪A,ΣBΣ\right⟫_{w}=\left⟪ΣA,BΣ\right⟫_{w}=\left⟪ΣA,ΣBΣ\right⟫_{w}=0.

Proof.

Note that

A({\bf x})=A(r\rho(θ){\bf e}_{1})=f(r)ρ(θ)\begin{pmatrix}1&\textpm\mathrm{i}\\
\textpm\mathrm{i}&-1\end{pmatrix}ρ(-θ){\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mθ}=f(r)\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}1&\textpm\mathrm{i}\\
\textpm\mathrm{i}&-1\end{pmatrix}}_{=:C}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}(m\mp 2)θ}.

and similarly
B({\bf x})=g(r)C{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}(m\mp 2)θ}
. A direct calculation shows for
S:=\mdiag(1,-1)
that

\displaystyle 0\;

\displaystyle=\left\langle{C,SC}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}=\left\langle{C,CS}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}=\left\langle{C,SCS}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}=\left\langle{SC,CS}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}=\left\langle{SC,SCS}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}.

The result follows by reducing the integrals using
Σ(r,0)=r^{2}S
, e.g.,

\displaystyle\left⟪A,ΣB\right⟫_{w}\;

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left\langle{A(r\rho(θ){\bf e}_{1}),Σ(r\rho(θ){\bf e}_{1})B(r\rho(θ){\bf e}_{1})}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}w(r)r\hbox{d}r\hbox{d}\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left\langle{f(r)C{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}(m\mp 2)θ},r^{2}Sg(r)C{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}(m\mp 2)θ}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}w(r)r\hbox{d}r\hbox{d}\theta

\displaystyle=2π\int_{0}^{1}\bar{f}(r)g(r)w(r)r^{3}\hbox{d}r\left\langle{C,SC}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}=0.

∎

We can combine these to deduce a complete basis of homogeneous polynomials:

Lemma 6
.

A symmetry-adapted basis of degree
n
homogeneous matrix polynomials is,
for
n=0
,

\displaystyle\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}1&-\mathrm{i}\\
-\mathrm{i}&-1\end{pmatrix}}_{{\mathrm{Y}}_{-2}},\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}1&\mathrm{i}\\
\mathrm{i}&-1\end{pmatrix}}_{{\mathrm{Y}}_{2}},\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}1&\mathrm{i}\\
-\mathrm{i}&1\end{pmatrix}}_{Σ{\mathrm{Y}}_{0}},\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}1&-\mathrm{i}\\
\mathrm{i}&1\end{pmatrix}}_{{\mathrm{Y}}_{0}Σ},

for
n=1
,

\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}1&\textpm\mathrm{i}\\
\textpm\mathrm{i}&-1\end{pmatrix}(x\textpm\mathrm{i}y)}_{{\mathrm{Y}}_{\textpm 3}},\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}1&\textpm\mathrm{i}\\
\textpm\mathrm{i}&-1\end{pmatrix}(x\mp\mathrm{i}y)}_{r^{2}{\mathrm{Y}}_{\textpm 1}},\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}1&\textpm\mathrm{i}\\
\mp\mathrm{i}&1\end{pmatrix}(x\textpm\mathrm{i}y)}_{Σ{\mathrm{Y}}_{\textpm 1}},\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}1&\mp\mathrm{i}\\
\textpm\mathrm{i}&1\end{pmatrix}(x\textpm\mathrm{i}y)}_{{\mathrm{Y}}_{\textpm 1}Σ}

for
n=2,4,\ldots

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Y}}_{\textpm(n+2)},r^{2}{\mathrm{Y}}_{\textpm n},\ldots,r^{n}{\mathrm{Y}}_{\textpm 2},r^{n+2}{\mathrm{Y}}_{0},

\displaystyle Σ{\mathrm{Y}}_{\textpm n},r^{2}Σ{\mathrm{Y}}_{\textpm(n-2)},\ldots,r^{n-2}Σ{\mathrm{Y}}_{\textpm 2},r^{n}Σ{\mathrm{Y}}_{0},

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Y}}_{\textpm n}Σ,r^{2}{\mathrm{Y}}_{\textpm(n-2)}Σ,\ldots,r^{n-2}{\mathrm{Y}}_{\textpm 2}Σ,r^{n}{\mathrm{Y}}_{0}Σ,

\displaystyle Σ{\mathrm{Y}}_{\textpm(n-2)}Σ,\ldots,r^{n-4}Σ{\mathrm{Y}}_{\textpm 2}Σ,r^{n-2}Σ{\mathrm{Y}}_{0}Σ.

and for
n=3,5,\ldots

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Y}}_{\textpm(n+2)},r^{2}{\mathrm{Y}}_{\textpm n},\ldots,r^{n+1}{\mathrm{Y}}_{\textpm 1},

\displaystyle Σ{\mathrm{Y}}_{\textpm n},r^{2}Σ{\mathrm{Y}}_{\textpm(n-2)},\ldots,r^{n-1}Σ{\mathrm{Y}}_{\textpm 1},

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Y}}_{\textpm n}Σ,r^{2}{\mathrm{Y}}_{\textpm(n-2)}Σ,\ldots,r^{n-1}{\mathrm{Y}}_{\textpm 1}Σ,

\displaystyle Σ{\mathrm{Y}}_{\textpm(n-2)}Σ,\ldots r^{n-3}Σ{\mathrm{Y}}_{\textpm 1}Σ.

Proof.

For each
n
we have the correct total of
4(n+1)
functions so we need to only show linear independence. As in the vector case we know different choices of modes
m
are automatically orthogonal with respect to any inner product of the form
\left⟪\cdot,\cdot\right⟫_{w}
(Lemma
1
) thus we only need to show orthogonality for the same
n
and
m
. This is a direct consequence of the previous lemma.

∎

4
Vector and matrix analogues of Zernike polynomials

We now turn our attention to orthogonal polynomials beginning with analogues of Zernike polynomials, that is,
we construct scalar, vector, and matrix symmetry-adapted orthogonal polynomials with respect to the weight
(1-r^{2})^{λ}

4.1
Scalar Zernike polynomials

The generalised Zernike polynomials
{\mathrm{z}}_{mj}^{(λ)}
defined in the introduction are symmetry-adapted functions with mode
m
that are orthogonal with respect to the inner product

\left\langle{f,g}\right\rangle_{λ}:=\int\!\!\!\!\int_{Ω}\bar{f}({\bf x})g({\bf x})(1-r^{2})^{λ}\hbox{d}{\bf x}.

Choosing the right ordering gives us a basis for degree
n
polynomials:

Lemma 7
.

Symmetry-adapted OPs with respect to
(1-r^{2})^{λ}
of degree
n
are given by, for
n
even,

\displaystyle{\mathrm{z}}_{\textpm n,0}^{(λ)},{\mathrm{z}}_{\textpm(n-2),1}^{(λ)},\ldots,{\mathrm{z}}_{\textpm 2,n/2-1}^{(λ)},{\mathrm{z}}_{0,n/2}^{(λ)}

and for
n
odd,

\displaystyle{\mathrm{z}}_{\textpm n,0}^{(λ)},{\mathrm{z}}_{\textpm(n-2),1}^{(λ)},\ldots,{\mathrm{z}}_{\textpm 1,(n-1)/2}^{s,(λ)}.

Proof.

This is standard (see
[
9
]
) but we write it in a way analogous to the vector and matrix cases, in particular, using the
symmetry-adapted property. By Lemma
1
,
{\mathrm{z}}_{mj}^{(λ)}
and
{\mathrm{z}}_{nk}^{(λ)}
are orthogonal if
m≠n
. If
m=n
and
k≠j
, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle{{\mathrm{z}}_{mk}^{(λ)},{\mathrm{z}}_{mj}^{(λ)}}\right\rangle_{λ}\;

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{2π}\bar{\mathrm{z}}_{mk}^{(λ)}(rρ(θ){\bf e}_{1}){\mathrm{z}}_{mj}^{(λ)}(rρ(θ){\bf e}_{1})\hbox{d}θ(1-r^{2})^{λ}r\hbox{d}r

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2π}{\rm e}^{-\mathrm{i}mθ}\int_{0}^{1}\bar{\mathrm{z}}_{mk}^{(λ)}(r,0){\mathrm{z}}_{mj}^{(λ)}(r,0)(1-r^{2})^{λ}r\hbox{d}r{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mθ}\hbox{d}θ

\displaystyle=2π\int_{0}^{1}P_{k}^{(λ,|m|)}(2r^{2}-1)P_{j}^{(λ,|m|)}(2r^{2}-1)(1-r^{2})^{λ}r^{2|m|+1}\hbox{d}r.

With the change of variables
2r^{2}-1=τ
(so that
r^{2}=(τ+1)/2
and
1-r^{2}=(1-τ)/2

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}

\displaystyle P_{k}^{(λ,|m|)}(2r^{2}-1)P_{j}^{(λ,|m|)}(2r^{2}-1)(1-r^{2})^{λ}r^{2|m|+1}\hbox{d}r

\displaystyle={1\over 2^{λ+|m|+2}}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{k}^{(λ,|m|)}(τ)P_{j}^{(λ,|m|)}(τ)(1-τ)^{λ}(1+τ)^{|m|}\hbox{d}τ=0.

∎

Remark 2

4.2
Vector Zernike polynomials

We now extend the construction to vector orthogonal polynomials. A simple way to construct vector OPs is to represent each component by a scalar OP but this will not be symmetry-adapted. Instead, we want to take the appropriate linear combination of the symmetry-adapted homogeneous orthogonal polynomials to achieve orthogonality. The following does so:

Definition 10

\displaystyle{\bf z}_{mj}^{(λ),1}(x,y)

\displaystyle P_{0}^{(λ,-1)}(x)

\displaystyle:=1,\qquad P_{n}^{(λ,-1)}(x):=(1+x){n+λ\over 2n}P_{n-1}^{(λ,1)}(x),\qquad n≠0.

We can use these to construct a basis for degree

We note there are precisely
2(n+1)

We find, using

\displaystyle σ_{mkj}^{(λ),1}\;

5
Special class of univariate vector orthogonal polynomials

To go beyond simple ultraspherical inner products and allow more general equivariant symmetric positive definite matrix
weights we will relate vector OPs on the disk to a special class of univariate vector OPs. We first define a set of diagonal
matrix and vector polynomials whose constant term in a monomial expansion has a special form:

Definition 12
.

Denote the commutative ring of diagonal matrix polynomials whose constant term in a monomial expansion is the identity by:

{\mathcal{D}}:=\left\{{c_{0}I+\sum_{k=1}^{p}\begin{pmatrix}c_{k}\\
&d_{k}\end{pmatrix}t^{k}:c_{k},d_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}}\right\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}[t]^{2\times 2}.

Denote the module over
{\mathcal{D}}
of vector polynomials whose constant term in a monomial expansion have the same entry in each component by:

{\mathcal{M}}:={\mathcal{D}}{\begin{pmatrix}1\cr 1\cr\end{pmatrix}}=\left\{{c_{0}{\begin{pmatrix}1\cr 1\cr\end{pmatrix}}+\sum_{k=1}^{p}{\begin{pmatrix}c_{k}\cr d_{k}\cr\end{pmatrix}}t^{k}:c_{k},d_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}}\right\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}[t]^{2}.

(9)

We want to consider orthogonal polynomials in
{\mathcal{M}}
with respect to diagonal inner products of the form

\left\langle{{\bf f},{\bf g}}\right\rangle_{V}:=\int_{0}^{1}{\bf f}(t)^{\top}\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}α(t)+tβ(t)\\
&α(t)-tβ(t)\end{pmatrix}}_{=:V(t)}{\bf g}(t)\hbox{d}{t}

where
V:[0,1]\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{2\times 2}
is symmetric positive definite almost everywhere,
and for simplicity we assume
α
and
β
are polynomial, that is,
V\in{\mathcal{D}}
. We will see in Section
6
that we can relate symmetry-adapted orthogonal polynomials with respect to an equivariant matrix weight in the disk with mode
m≠0
to
vector orthogonal polynomials in
{\mathcal{M}}
.

Consider orthogonal polynomials in
{\mathcal{M}}
with respect to
V
arising from orthogonalising graded polynomials with the following ordering of monomials:

{\begin{pmatrix}1\cr 1\cr\end{pmatrix}},{\begin{pmatrix}1\cr-1\cr\end{pmatrix}}t,{\begin{pmatrix}1\cr 1\cr\end{pmatrix}}t,{\begin{pmatrix}1\cr-1\cr\end{pmatrix}}t^{2},{\begin{pmatrix}1\cr 1\cr\end{pmatrix}}t^{2},\cdots.

We will denote a family of such orthogonal polynomials by

{\bf p}_{0}^{V,1},{\bf p}_{1}^{V,2},{\bf p}_{1}^{V,1},{\bf p}_{2}^{V,2},{\bf p}_{2}^{V,1},\ldots.

(10)

Here
{\bf p}_{n}^{V,1/2}
are a basis of degree
n
polynomials in
{\mathcal{M}}
, and we place the
{\bf p}_{n}^{V,2}
term first so that the total basis is interlacing the bases
\{{\bf p}_{n}^{V,1}\}
(whose leading order behaviour according to the grading is proportional to
{{\left({1,1}\right)}^{\top}}t^{n}
) and
\{{\bf p}_{n}^{V,2}\}
(whose leading order behaviour is proportional to
{{\left({1,-1}\right)}^{\top}}t^{n}
).
Note that we are not imposing a specific normalisation constant in the following, though these will be uniquely defined if we impose that they are monic polynomials, that is, that the highest order coefficient according to the above ordering is
1
.
It is convenient to also work with degree
n
polynomials grouped together as a matrix polynomial, which we denote

{\bf P}_{n}^{V}(t):=\begin{cases}\left({{\bf p}_{0}^{V,1}(t)}\right)&n=0\\
{\begin{pmatrix}\,{\bf p}_{n}^{V,2}(t)\ |\ {\bf p}_{n}^{V,1}(t)\,\end{pmatrix}}&n≠0\end{cases}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2\times\min(n+1,2)}.

A basic feature of orthogonal polynomials is the existence of three-term recurrences, which in the case of orthonormal
polynomials correspond to Jacobi
matrices (symmetric tridiagonal
matrices). These extend to multivariate and vector orthogonal polynomials in the form of symmetric
block tridiagonal matrices. Note that the ring
{\mathcal{D}}
has two generators:
t
and

tS
where
S=\mdiag(1,-1)
. We get two associated block three-term recurrences:

Lemma 8
.

For
V\in{\mathcal{D}}
that is symmetric positive define almost everywhere, the matrix polynomials
{\bf P}_{n}^{V}
whose columns are a basis of degree
n
vector OPs in
{\mathcal{M}}
have two block three-term recurrences:

\displaystyle t{\bf P}_{0}^{V}\;

\displaystyle={\bf P}_{0}^{V}A_{0}^{1}+{\bf P}^{V}_{1}B_{0}^{1},

\displaystyle t{\bf P}_{n}^{V}\;

\displaystyle={\bf P}_{n-1}^{V}C_{n}^{1}+{\bf P}_{n}^{V}A_{n}^{1}+{\bf P}^{V}_{n+1}B_{n}^{1},\qquad n>0,

\displaystyle tS{\bf P}^{V}_{0}\;

\displaystyle={\bf P}^{V}_{0}A_{0}^{2}+{\bf P}^{V}_{1}B_{0}^{2},

\displaystyle tS{\bf P}^{V}_{n}\;

\displaystyle={\bf P}^{V}_{n-1}C_{n}^{2}+{\bf P}^{V}_{n}A_{n}^{2}+{\bf P}^{V}_{n+1}B_{n}^{2},\qquad n>0.

where
S=\mdiag(1,-1)
,
A_{0}^{α}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{1\times 1}
,
B_{0}^{α}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2\times 1}
,
C_{0}^{α}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{1\times 2}
, and

A_{n}^{α},B_{n}^{α},C_{n}^{α}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2\times 2}
,
n>0
.
When
{\bf P}_{n}^{V}
are orthonormal then
(B_{n}^{α})^{\top}=C_{n+1}^{α}
and
A_{n}^{α}=(A_{n}^{α})^{\top}
.

Proof.

The proof is identical to the proof for three-term recurrence for univariate polynomials since
t
and
tS
are both self-adjoint operators that increase the polynomial degree by 1 and
commute with
V\in{\mathcal{D}}
. In particular, for
{\bf f},{\bf g}\in{\mathcal{M}}
we have

\displaystyle\left\langle{{\bf f},tS{\bf g}}\right\rangle_{V}\;

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}{\bf f}(t)^{\top}V(t)tS{\bf g}(t)\hbox{d}{t}=\int_{0}^{1}(tS{\bf f}(t))^{\top}V(t){\bf g}(t)\hbox{d}{t}=\left\langle{tS{\bf f},{\bf g}}\right\rangle_{V}

Note that any degree

\displaystyle t{\bf P}^{V}_{n}\;

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}{\bf P}_{k}^{V}Γ_{kn}^{1}\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad tS{\bf P}^{V}_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}{\bf P}_{k}^{V}Γ_{kn}^{2}.

where

\displaystyle Γ_{kn}^{1}\;

\displaystyle=\left\langle{{\bf P}_{k}^{V},{\bf P}_{k}^{V}}\right\rangle^{-1}\left\langle{{\bf P}_{k}^{V},t{\bf P}_{n}^{V}}\right\rangle\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\min(k+1,2)\times 2},

\displaystyle Γ_{kn}^{2}\;

\displaystyle=\left\langle{{\bf P}_{k}^{V},{\bf P}_{k}^{V}}\right\rangle^{-1}\left\langle{{\bf P}_{k}^{V},tS{\bf P}_{n}^{V}}\right\rangle\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\min(k+1,2)\times 2}.

and similarly
\left\langle{{\bf P}_{k}^{V},tS{\bf P}_{n}^{V}}\right\rangle=0
as
{\bf P}_{n}^{V}
is orthogonal to all lower degree polynomials in
{\mathcal{M}}
.
The three-term recurrence then follows by writing

{\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle:=r^{|m|-1}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mθ}ρ(θ)\begin{pmatrix}1&\\
&\mathrm{i}\sign m\end{pmatrix}{\bf f}(r^{2})

is a one-to-one map
{\mathcal{P}}_{m}:{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow\Pi_{m}^{2}
with inverse

{\mathcal{P}}_{m}^{-1}{\bf f}(t)=t^{1-|m|\over 2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
&-\mathrm{i}\sign m\end{pmatrix}{\bf f}(\sqrt{t},0).

In particular,
{\mathcal{P}}_{m}
maps between polynomials of degree
n
and polynomials of degree
2n+|m|-1
.

Proof.

Recall the basis of
degree
2n+|m|-1
homogeneous polynomials with
m
introduced in
Lemma
4
:
r^{2n}{\bf y}_{m}({\bf x})
and
r^{2n-2}Σ({\bf x}){\bf y}_{m}({\bf x})
.
Consider
{\mathcal{P}}_{m}
applied to a basis of
{\mathcal{M}}
:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{m}\left[{t^{n}{{\begin{pmatrix}1\cr 1\cr\end{pmatrix}}}}\right]({\bf x})\;

\displaystyle=r^{2n+|m|-1}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mθ}ρ(θ){\begin{pmatrix}1\cr\mathrm{i}\sign m\cr\end{pmatrix}}=r^{2n}{\bf y}_{m}({\bf x}),

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{m}\left[{t^{n}{{\begin{pmatrix}1\cr-1\cr\end{pmatrix}}}}\right]({\bf x})\;

\displaystyle=r^{2n+|m|-1}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mθ}ρ(θ){\begin{pmatrix}1\cr-\mathrm{i}\sign m\cr\end{pmatrix}}=r^{2n-2}Σ(𝐱){\bf y}_{m}({\bf x}),\quad n>0.

Thus we have a one-to-one map between expansions in these two bases.

We now verify the inverse formula:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{m}^{-1}\left[{r^{2n}{\bf y}_{m}}\right](t)\;

\displaystyle=t^{1-|m|\over 2}t^{2n+|m|-1\over 2}{\begin{pmatrix}1\cr 1\cr\end{pmatrix}}=t^{n}{\begin{pmatrix}1\cr 1\cr\end{pmatrix}},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{m}^{-1}\left[{r^{2n-2}Σ{\bf y}_{m}}\right](t)\;

\displaystyle=t^{1-|m|\over 2}t^{2n+|m|-1\over 2}{\begin{pmatrix}1\cr-1\cr\end{pmatrix}}=t^{n}{\begin{pmatrix}1\cr-1\cr\end{pmatrix}},\quad n>0.

∎

We want to consider equivariant matrix polynomial weights
W
, which live in the matrix polynomial ring

\Pi_{0}^{2\times 2}:=\mathspan\!\left\{{r^{2n}{\mathrm{Y}}_{0},r^{2n}Σ{\mathrm{Y}}_{0},r^{2n}{\mathrm{Y}}_{0}Σ,r^{2n}Σ{\mathrm{Y}}_{0}Σ\quad\hbox{for}\quad n=0,1,…}\right\}.

We will translate the action of equivariant matrix polynomials on
Π_{m}^{2}

to equivalent actions on
{\mathcal{M}}
via the following intertwining relationships:

Proposition 4
.

For
m≠0
we have:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{m}t^{n}\;

\displaystyle=r^{2n}{\mathcal{P}}_{m},\quad{\mathcal{P}}_{m}tS=Σ{\mathcal{P}}_{m},\quad{\mathcal{P}}_{m}tρ(π/2)=\mathrm{i}\sign mρ(π/2)Σ{\mathcal{P}}_{m},

where again
S=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
&-1\end{pmatrix}
and
ρ(π/2)=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
.

Proof.

The first property is immediate since
t=r^{2}
. The second property follows from the equivariance of
Σ
, in particular:

Σ({\bf x})ρ(θ)=ρ(θ)Σ(r,0)=r^{2}ρ(θ)S.

The last property follows since

\displaystyle ρ(π/2)Σ({\bf x}){\mathcal{P}}_{m}{\bf f}({\bf x})\;

\displaystyle=r^{|m|-1}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mθ}ρ(θ)ρ(π/2)\begin{pmatrix}1&\\
&\mathrm{i}\sign m\end{pmatrix}Sr^{2}{\bf f}(r^{2})

\displaystyle=r^{|m|-1}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mθ}ρ(θ)\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathrm{i}\sign m\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}r^{2}{\bf f}(r^{2})

\displaystyle=r^{|m|-1}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mθ}ρ(θ)\begin{pmatrix}-\mathrm{i}\sign m\\
&1\end{pmatrix}ρ(π/2)r^{2}{\bf f}(r^{2})

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\sign m{\mathcal{P}}_{m}[tρ(π/2){\bf f}]({\bf x}).

∎

When we apply these relationships to equivariant weights on the disk we get a specific form of weight for

Lemma 13
.

Suppose

\displaystyle W(x,y){\mathcal{P}}_{m}\;

\displaystyle={\mathcal{P}}_{m}V(t)

for

-\mathrm{i}\sign mtγ(t)&α(t)-tβ(t)\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

General equivariant matrix polynomials of (even) degree

symmetric and skew-symmetric terms by recombining the basis
r^{2k+4}{\mathrm{Y}}_{0},r^{2k+2}Σ{\mathrm{Y}}_{0},r^{2k+2}{\mathrm{Y}}_{0}Σ

\displaystyle W(x,y)\;

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{p/2}\left[{α_{k}r^{2k}\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}1\\
&1\end{pmatrix}}_{(Σ{\mathrm{Y}}_{0}+{\mathrm{Y}}_{0}Σ)/2}+δ_{k}r^{2k}\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}}_{(Σ{\mathrm{Y}}_{0}-{\mathrm{Y}}_{0}Σ)/(2\mathrm{i})}}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{k=0}^{p/2-1}\left[{β_{k}r^{2k}\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}x^{2}-y^{2}&2xy\\
2xy&y^{2}-x^{2}\end{pmatrix}}_{(r^{4}{\mathrm{Y}}_{0}+Σ{\mathrm{Y}}_{0}Σ)/2=Σ(x,y)}+γ_{k}r^{2k}\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}-2xy&x^{2}-y^{2}\\
x^{2}-y^{2}&2xy\end{pmatrix}}_{(r^{4}{\mathrm{Y}}_{0}-Σ{\mathrm{Y}}_{0}Σ)/(2\mathrm{i})=ρ(π/2)Σ(x,y)}}\right]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sum_{k=0}^{p/2}α_{k}r^{2k}}_{=:α(r^{2})}I+\underbrace{\sum_{k=0}^{p/2-1}β_{k}r^{2k}}_{=:β(r^{2})}Σ(x,y)+\underbrace{\sum_{k=0}^{p/2-1}γ_{k}r^{2k}}_{=:γ(r^{2})}ρ(π/2)Σ(x,y)

The lemma then follows from the intertwining relationships.
∎

6.1
Orthogonal polynomials for a weight that is normal at the boundary

We can use the above construct to build
{\bf n}_{kj}^{ν}
, a natural basis for vector polynomials that are normal on the boundary of the disk. We introduce the symmetric-positive definite equivariant matrix weight:

N(x,y):=\begin{pmatrix}1-y^{2}&xy\\
xy&1-x^{2}\end{pmatrix}=\left({1-{r^{2}\over 2}}\right)I+{Σ(x,y)\over 2}=ρ(θ)\begin{pmatrix}1\\
&1-r^{2}\end{pmatrix}ρ(-θ),

(12)

noting that
ρ(θ)={\begin{pmatrix}\,{\bf e}_{r}\ |\ {\bf e}_{θ}\,\end{pmatrix}}
, that is, when
r=0
multiplication by
N
imposes that the tangential component vanishes.
This weight is fundamental in describing polynomials that are normal at the boundary of a disk, i.e., in the following module over
{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]
:

{\mathcal{N}}:=\{{\bf p}\in{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]^{2}:{\bf p}^{\top}{\begin{pmatrix}-y\cr x\cr\end{pmatrix}}=0\hbox{ when }x^{2}+y^{2}=1\}.

(13)

In particular, we will show that every
{\bf p}\in{\mathcal{N}}
can be written as
N
times another vector polynomial.

We first decompose
{\mathcal{N}}
into “true bubbles” (polynomials that vanish at the boundary),
and two other simple polynomial sets:

Lemma 14
.

{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]^{2}={\mathcal{K}}\oplus\underbrace{{\mathcal{E}}\oplus{\mathcal{B}}}_{={\mathcal{N}}}

for

\displaystyle{\mathcal{K}}

\displaystyle:=\mathspan\!\left\{{{\begin{pmatrix}1\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}},{\begin{pmatrix}0\cr 1\cr\end{pmatrix}},{\begin{pmatrix}y\cr x\cr\end{pmatrix}},{\begin{pmatrix}x\cr-y\cr\end{pmatrix}},x^{k}{\begin{pmatrix}-y\cr x\cr\end{pmatrix}}\hbox{ for }k≥0,x^{k-1}y{\begin{pmatrix}-y\cr x\cr\end{pmatrix}}\hbox{ for }k>0}\right\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}

\displaystyle:=\mathspan\!\left\{{x^{k}{\begin{pmatrix}x\cr y\cr\end{pmatrix}}\hbox{ for }k≥0,x^{k-1}y{\begin{pmatrix}x\cr y\cr\end{pmatrix}}\hbox{ for }k>0}\right\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{B}}

\displaystyle:=(1-x^{2}-y^{2}){\mathbb{R}}[x,y]^{2}.

Proof.

First note
{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{B}}\subset{\mathcal{N}}
. We will decompose homogeneous polynomials of each degree, and in the process show that

{\mathcal{K}}\cap{\mathcal{N}}=\{0\}
and
{\mathcal{E}}\cap{\mathcal{B}}
, which will imply the decomposition. For degree 0 polynomials we have

\left[{c{{\begin{pmatrix}1\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}}}+d{{\begin{pmatrix}0\cr 1\cr\end{pmatrix}}}}\right]^{\top}{\begin{pmatrix}-y\cr x\cr\end{pmatrix}}=dx-cy

which by linear independence is only identically zero if
c=d=0
, that is, no nontrivial degree 0 polynomial is in
{\mathcal{N}}
. The space generated by degree 1 polynomials in
{\mathcal{K}}
satisfy, for
x^{2}+y^{2}=1
,

\left[{c{\begin{pmatrix}-y\cr x\cr\end{pmatrix}}+d{\begin{pmatrix}x\cr-y\cr\end{pmatrix}}+e{\begin{pmatrix}y\cr x\cr\end{pmatrix}}}\right]^{\top}{\begin{pmatrix}-y\cr x\cr\end{pmatrix}}=c-2dxy+e(x^{2}-y^{2})≠0

unless
c=d=0
by linear independence. The remaining basis element is
{\begin{pmatrix}x\cr y\cr\end{pmatrix}}\in{\mathcal{E}}\subset{\mathcal{N}}
.
For higher order polynomials of degree
n≥2
we only have two terms in
{\mathcal{K}}
which satisfy, for
x^{2}+y^{2}=1
,

{\begin{pmatrix}-y\cr x\cr\end{pmatrix}}^{\top}{\begin{pmatrix}-y\cr x\cr\end{pmatrix}}(cx^{k}+dx^{k-1}y)=cx^{k}+dx^{k-1}y≠0

unless
c=d=0
. We then have
2n
basis terms of degree
n
given by:

\displaystyle\underbrace{x^{n-1}{\begin{pmatrix}x\cr y\cr\end{pmatrix}},x^{n-2}y{\begin{pmatrix}x\cr y\cr\end{pmatrix}}}_{\in{\mathcal{E}}},\underbrace{x^{n-2-k}y^{k}(1-x^{2}-y^{2}){\begin{pmatrix}1\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}},x^{n-2-k}y^{k}(1-x^{2}-y^{2}){\begin{pmatrix}0\cr 1\cr\end{pmatrix}}}_{\in{\mathcal{B}}}

for
k=0,…,n-2
.
To see these are linearly independent we note for
x^{2}+y^{2}=1
that the basis elements in
{\mathcal{E}}
satisfy

{{\left({x,y}\right)}^{\top}}\left[{cx^{n-1}{{\begin{pmatrix}x\cr y\cr\end{pmatrix}}}+dx^{n-2}y{{\begin{pmatrix}x\cr y\cr\end{pmatrix}}}}\right]=cx^{n-1}+dx^{n-2}y≠0

unless
c=d=0
, hence the intersection of their span with
{\mathcal{B}}
is trivial.

Since
{\mathcal{B}},{\mathcal{E}}\subset{\mathcal{N}}
and
{\mathcal{K}}\cap{\mathcal{N}}=\{0\}

we deduce that
{\mathcal{N}}={\mathcal{E}}\oplus{\mathcal{B}}
.

∎

Remark 5
.

The definition of
{\mathcal{K}}
is not unique: it is one choice of basis and one can modify the basis of
{\mathcal{K}}
by adding elements of
{\mathcal{N}}
to form a new basis. For example, we could replace

with the symmetry-adapted basis

or even

A consequence of this lemma is that we can build a basis for

by multiplying general vector polynomials by

Lemma 15

{\mathcal{N}}=\{N{\bf p}:{\bf p}\in{\mathbb{R}}[x,y]^{2}\}
.

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle{\begin{pmatrix}x\cr y\cr\end{pmatrix}}\;

\displaystyle=N(x,y){\begin{pmatrix}x\cr y\cr\end{pmatrix}},\quad(1-r^{2}){\begin{pmatrix}1\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}}=N(x,y){\begin{pmatrix}1-x^{2}\cr-xy\cr\end{pmatrix}},\quad(1-r^{2}){\begin{pmatrix}0\cr 1\cr\end{pmatrix}}=N(x,y){\begin{pmatrix}-xy\cr 1-y^{2}\cr\end{pmatrix}}.

The theorem follows since these generate bases for
{\mathcal{E}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
,
and hence
{\mathcal{N}}
.

∎

7
Relationship with the de Rham complex of the disk

We are now in a place to describe our main result.
We will show that
\{{\mathrm{w}}_{mj}\},\{{\bf n}_{mj}^{ν}\},
and
\{{\mathrm{z}}_{mj}\}
give
natural bases for the de Rham complex in a disk, and in particular have very simple recurrence relationships relating their gradient and curl.
These lead to a decomposition of the de Rham complex into simple exact sub-complexes.

Recall the
de Rham complex with boundary conditions
(see
[
1
, Section 4.5.5]
) which in 2D is

\displaystyle 0\rightarrow\mathring{H}^{1}\overset{\nabla}{\rightarrow}\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{curl}}\overset{\curl}{\rightarrow}L^{2}\rightarrow 0,

for the 2D Sobolev spaces on the disk:

\displaystyle H^{1}

\displaystyle:=\{f:\|\nabla f\|^{2}+\|f\|^{2}<∞\},\qquad\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{curl}}:=\{{\bf f}\in H_{\mathrm{curl}}:\|\curl{\bf f}\|^{2}+\|{\bf f}\|^{2}<∞\},

\displaystyle\mathring{H}^{1}

\displaystyle:=\{f\in H^{1}:f|_{\partial Ω}=0\},\qquad\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{curl}}:=\{{\bf f}\in H_{\mathrm{curl}}:{\bf f}|_{\partial Ω}\cdot{\bf e}_{θ}=0\},

where the differential operators and trace operators are understood in the weak sense.

Polynomials that vanish on the boundary are a natural way to discretise
\mathring{H}^{1}
while
polynomials normal on the boundary are a natural way to discretise
\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{curl}}
.
Thus
\{{\mathrm{w}}_{mj}\}
serves as a natural basis for
\mathring{H}^{1}
,

\{{\bf n}_{mj}^{ν}\}
is a natural basis for
\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{curl}}
, and
\{{\mathrm{z}}_{mj}\}
is a natural basis for
L^{2}
.
We will go one step further and recombine
{\bf n}_{mj}^{ν}
to form a natural basis that
separates out the range of
\nabla
/ kernel of
\curl
:

Definition 16
.

\displaystyle{\bf n}_{0j}^{+}

\displaystyle:=2{\bf n}_{0j}^{1},

\displaystyle{\bf n}_{0j}^{-}

\displaystyle:=2{\bf n}_{0j}^{2},\qquad j≥1,

\displaystyle{\bf n}_{m0}^{-}

\displaystyle:=2{\bf n}_{m0}^{1},

\displaystyle{\bf n}_{mj}^{\textpm}

\displaystyle:={\bf n}_{mj}^{1}\textpm{\bf n}_{mj}^{2}\qquad m≠0,\qquad j≥1.

Note that while the definition of
{\bf n}_{mj}^{+}
is canonical, the definition of
{\bf n}_{mj}^{-}
is somewhat arbitrary, and the following discussion would work equally well with any linear combination of
{\bf n}_{mj}^{1}
and
{\bf n}_{mj}^{2}
that is linearly independent of
{\bf n}_{mj}^{+}
.
For example, an alternative linear combination could be chosen to preserve orthogonality with respect to
\left\langle{\cdot,\cdot}\right\rangle_{N^{-1}}
.

7.1
Expressions for differential operators.

We have extremely simple expression for the gradient relating the scalar and vector basis:

Theorem 6
.

\displaystyle\nabla{\mathrm{w}}_{mj}\;

\displaystyle=-(j+1){\bf n}_{m,j+1}^{+}.

Proof.

This can be shown using properties of Jacobi polynomials but we prefer an integration-by-parts argument that is more amenable to future generalisation. The gradient of a polynomial that vanishes on the circle is normal at the boundary, that is
\nabla{\mathrm{w}}_{mj}\in{\mathcal{N}}
, and orthogonal to other modes (by Lemma
1
). Hence we can expand

\nabla{\mathrm{w}}_{mj}=\sum_{k=0}^{∞}c_{k}^{1}{\bf n}_{mk}^{1}+\sum_{k=1}^{∞}c_{k}^{2}{\bf n}_{mk}^{2},

where by orthogonality we have

c_{k}^{ν}={\left\langle{\nabla{\mathrm{w}}_{mj},{\bf n}_{mk}^{ν}}\right\rangle_{N^{-1}}\over\left\langle{{\bf n}_{mk}^{ν},{\bf n}_{mk}^{ν}}\right\rangle_{N^{-1}}},

where we take
c_{0}^{1}=0
when
m=0
(since
{\bf n}_{00}^{1}
is not defined).
We first establish that
c_{k}^{ν}=0
unless
k=j+1
.
Recall that

N(x,y)^{-1}=(1-x^{2}-y^{2})^{-1}T(x,y).

This tells us that if
{\bf f}=N{\bf g}\in{\mathcal{N}}
then
{\bf g}=N^{-1}{\bf f}
is at most the same degree as
{\bf f}

(since multiplying by
T
at most increases the degree by 2 while dividing by
1-x^{2}-y^{2}
will decrease the degree by 2).

When
k\leq j
we have by integration-by-parts (using that
{\mathrm{w}}_{mk}
vanishes on the boundary):

\displaystyle\left\langle{\nabla{\mathrm{w}}_{mj},{\bf n}_{mk}^{ν}}\right\rangle_{N^{-1}}\;

\displaystyle=\big\langle\nabla{\mathrm{w}}_{mj},\underbrace{N^{-1}{\bf n}_{mk}^{ν}}_{={\bf v}_{mk}^{N,ν}}\big\rangle=-\bigl<{\mathrm{w}}_{mj},\!\!\!\underbrace{\divergence{\bf v}_{mk}^{N,ν}}_{\mathrm{degree}\ ≤2k+|m|-2}\!\!\!\bigr>=0

as
{\mathrm{w}}_{mj}
is orthogonal to all polynomials of degree
<2j+|m|
. On the other hand if
k>j+1
we have

\displaystyle\bigl<\!\!\!\underbrace{\nabla w_{mj}}_{\mathrm{degree}\ 2j+|m|+1}\!\!\!,{\bf n}_{mk}^{ν}\bigr>_{N^{-1}}\;

\displaystyle=\bigl<\!\!\!\underbrace{N^{-1}\nabla w_{mj}}_{\mathrm{degree}\ ≤2j+|m|+1}\!\!\!,{\bf n}_{mk}^{ν}\bigr>=0

as
{\bf n}_{mk}^{ν}=N{\bf v}_{mk}^{N,ν}
is orthogonal to polynomials of degree
<2k+|m|-1
. Therefore, we deduce that the expansion simplifies to:

\nabla w_{mj}=c_{m,j+1}^{1}{\bf n}_{m,j+1}^{1}+c_{m,j+1}^{2}{\bf n}_{m,j+1}^{2}.

To deduce these constants we will match the leading order terms, beginning with the
m≠0
case. In particular, first note using
[
7
, §18.3]

P_{n}^{(1,b)}(x)={(2n+b+1)!\over 2^{n}n!(n+b+1)!}x^{n}+O(x^{n-1})

implies that

{\mathrm{w}}_{mj}({\bf x})=-{(2j+|m|+1)!\over j!(j+|m|+1)!}r^{2j+|m|+2}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mθ}+O(r^{2j+|m|+1}).

We thus know the gradient is

\nabla w_{mj}({\bf x})=-{(2j+|m|+1)!\over j!(j+|m|+1)!}r^{2j+|m|+1}\left[{(2j+|m|+2){\bf e}_{r}+\mathrm{i}m{\bf e}_{θ}}\right]{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mθ}+O(r^{2j+|m|})

We multiply this by
T
to find that

T\nabla w_{mj}({\bf x})={(2j+|m|+2)!\over j!(j+|m|+1)!}r^{2j+|m|+3}{\bf e}_{r}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mθ}+O(r^{2j+|m|}).

On the other hand, note that

{\bf q}_{n}^{(b),1}(t)+{\bf q}_{n}^{(b),2}(t)={(2n+b+1)!\over n!(n+b+1)!}{\begin{pmatrix}1\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}}t^{n}+O(t^{n-1}).

If
{\bf f}(t)={\bf c}t^{j}+O(t^{j-1})
then we have

{\mathcal{P}}_{m}{\bf f}({\bf x})=r^{2j+|m|-1}{\begin{pmatrix}\,{\bf e}_{r}\ |\ {\bf e}_{θ}\,\end{pmatrix}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
&\mathrm{i}\sign m\end{pmatrix}{\bf c}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mθ}+O(r^{2j+|m|-3}).

Therefore we have (using
T=(1-x^{2}-y^{2})N^{-1}
)

\displaystyle T({\bf n}_{m,j+1}^{1}({\bf x})+{\bf n}_{m,j+1}^{2}({\bf x}))\;

\displaystyle=(1-r^{2}){\mathcal{P}}_{m}[{\bf q}_{j+1}^{|m|-1,1}+{\bf q}_{j+1}^{|m|-1,2}]({\bf x})

\displaystyle=-{(2j+|m|+2)!\over(j+1)!(j+|m|+1)!}r^{2j+|m|+3}{\bf e}_{r}+O(r^{2j+|m|+1}).

Multiplying this by
-(j+1)
matches the normalisation constant for
T\nabla w_{mj}
, that is

c_{mj}^{1}=c_{mj}^{2}=-(j+1)
. A similar matching term argument confirms the
m=0
formula.

∎

A similar argument gives a simple expression for the curl:

Theorem 7
.

For
𝔯_{n}^{(b)}=10n^{2}+n(13b+19)+4(b+1)(b+2)
we have
\curl{\bf n}_{mj}^{+}=0
and

\displaystyle\qquad\curl{\bf n}_{0j}^{-}\;

\displaystyle=-4j{\mathrm{z}}_{0j},\quad j≥1,

\displaystyle\curl{\bf n}_{mj}^{-}\;

\displaystyle=-{4(2j+|m|)(2j+|m|+1)(2j+|m|+2)\over 𝔯_{j}^{(|m|-1)}}\mathrm{i}\sign m{\mathrm{z}}_{mj}\quad m≠0.

Proof.

We have
\curl{\bf n}_{mj}^{+}=-\curl\nabla w_{m,j-1}/(j+1)=0
. By orthogonality to other modes (Lemma
1
) we know we can expand

\curl{\bf n}_{mj}^{ν}=\sum_{k=0}^{∞}c_{k}{\mathrm{z}}_{mk},\qquad c_{k}={\left\langle{\curl{\bf n}_{mj}^{ν},{\mathrm{z}}_{mk}}\right\rangle\over\left\langle{{\mathrm{z}}_{mk},{\mathrm{z}}_{mk}}\right\rangle}.

We first establish that
c_{k}=0
unless
k=j
. When
j<k
we have

\bigl<\underbrace{\curl{\bf n}_{mj}^{ν}}_{\mathrm{degree}\ ≤|m|+2j},{\mathrm{z}}_{mk}\bigr>=0

since
{\mathrm{z}}_{mk}
orthogonal to all polynomials of degree
<|m|+2k
.
When
j>k
we integrate by parts:

\displaystyle\left\langle{{\mathrm{z}}_{mk},\curl{\bf n}_{mj}^{ν}}\right\rangle\;

\displaystyle=-\left\langle{{\mathrm{z}}_{mk},\divergence ρ(π/2){\bf n}_{mj}^{ν}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-\oint_{\partial Ω}{\mathrm{z}}_{mk}\underbrace{{\bf x}^{\top}ρ(π/2){\bf n}_{mj}^{ν}}_{=0}\hbox{d}s+\bigl<\nabla{\mathrm{z}}_{mk},ρ(π/2){\bf n}_{mj}^{ν}\bigr>

\displaystyle=\bigl<\underbrace{ρ(-π/2)\nabla{\mathrm{z}}_{mk}}_{\mathrm{degree}\ |m|+2k-1},{\bf n}_{mj}^{ν}\bigr>=0

as
{\bf n}_{mj}^{ν}=N{\bf v}_{mj}^{N,ν}
are weighted (vector) orthogonal polynomials, therefore they are orthogonal to all polynomials of degree
<2j+|m|-1
.

The constant
c_{j}
then follows by matching the leading order terms.

∎

7.2
Exactness of the resulting de Rham sub-complexes.

The exactness of the de Rham complex with boundary conditions is related to the
Betti numbers
(see eg.
[
2
]
).
In the case of the disk the Betti numbers are
b_{0}=1
(the number of connected components),
b_{1}=0

(the number of closed loops up to homotopy) and
b_{2}=0
(the number of closed surfaces up to homotopy).
For a de Rham complex with boundary conditions these tell us that the dimension of the kernel of
\nabla
is
b_{2}=0
,
the range of
\nabla
equals the kernel of
\curl
since the dimension of their quotient is
b_{1}=0
,
whilst there is a one dimension space that is not in the range of
\curl
(which are the constant functions)
since the dimension of the quotient of the range of
\curl
with
L^{2}
equals
b_{0}=1
.

We can use the above results to decompose the space of polynomials into exact sub-complexes. In particular we have for each
m
and
j\geq 0
the following exact complexes:

\displaystyle 0\rightarrow\mathspan\!\left\{{{\mathrm{w}}_{mj}}\right\}\overset{\nabla}{\rightarrow}\mathspan\!\left\{{{\bf n}_{m,j+1}^{+}\atop{\bf n}_{m,j+1}^{-}}\right\}\overset{\curl}{\rightarrow}\mathspan\!\left\{{{\mathrm{z}}_{m,j+1}}\right\}\rightarrow 0,

We can see this is exact as
{\bf n}_{mj}^{+}
spans the kernel of
\curl
as the curl of
{\bf n}_{mj}^{-}
is not zero. We also have additional exact complexes for
m≠0
:

\begin{matrix}0&\rightarrow&0&\overset{\nabla}{\rightarrow}&\mathspan\!\left\{{{\bf n}_{m0}^{-}}\right\}&\overset{\curl}{\rightarrow}&\mathspan\!\left\{{{\mathrm{z}}_{m0}}\right\}&\rightarrow&0.\end{matrix}

We finally have the constants
\mathspan\!\left\{{{\mathrm{z}}_{00}}\right\}\subset L^{2}
, which are not in the range of
\curl
and thus do not form part of an exact complex. This matches the predicted theory since
b_{0}=1
.

8
Extension to cylinders

The weighted vector orthogonal polynomials
{\bf n}_{mj}^{ν}
can be used to build bases for the 3D de Rham complex with boundary conditions in cylinders:

\displaystyle 0\rightarrow\mathring{H}^{1}\overset{\nabla}{\rightarrow}\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{curl}}\overset{\curl}{\rightarrow}\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{div}}\overset{\divergence}{\rightarrow}L^{2}\rightarrow 0,

for the 3D Sobolev spaces over a cylinder
Γ
:

\displaystyle\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{curl}}

\displaystyle:=\{{\bf f}\in H_{\mathrm{curl}}:{\bf f}|_{\partial Ω}\times{\bf e}_{r}=0\},

\displaystyle\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{div}}

\displaystyle:=\{{\bf f}\in H_{\mathrm{div}}:{\bf f}|_{\partial Ω}\cdot{\bf e}_{r}=0\},

where again differential and trace operators are understood in the weak sense.
Here we consider the case where
Γ
is a periodic or finite cylinder:

\displaystyle Ω\times{\mathbb{T}}\;

\displaystyle=\{{{\left({x,y,z}\right)}^{\top}}:x^{2}+y^{2}≤1,0≤z<2π\},

\displaystyle Ω\times[-1,1]\;

\displaystyle=\{{{\left({x,y,z}\right)}^{\top}}:x^{2}+y^{2}≤1,-1≤z≤1\}

where
{\mathbb{T}}=[0,2π)\cong{\mathbb{R}}/(2π{\mathbb{R}})
is the periodic one-dimensional torus.

In order to represent a basis for
\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{div}}
we will need to use a basis of vector polynomials that are tangential
on the boundary of a disk:

{\bf t}_{mj}^{ν}({\bf x}):=ρ(π/2){\bf n}_{mj}^{ν}({\bf x})=T({\bf x})ρ(π/2){\bf v}_{mj}^{N,ν}({\bf x}).

(15)

where multiplication by

T(x,y):=\begin{pmatrix}1-x^{2}&-xy\\
-xy&1-y^{2}\end{pmatrix}=ρ(θ)\begin{pmatrix}1-r^{2}\\
&1\end{pmatrix}ρ(-θ)

causes a vector to be tangential to the boundary, and we use the relationship that

ρ(π/2)N({\bf x})=ρ(θ)\begin{pmatrix}0&r^{2}-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}ρ(-θ)=T({\bf x})ρ(π/2).

Note that

\divergence{\bf t}_{mj}^{\textpm}=-\curl{\bf n}_{mj}^{\textpm}

for
{\bf t}_{mj}^{\textpm}:=ρ(π/2){\bf n}_{mj}^{\textpm}
.
Therefore we can adapt the simple formula in Theorem
7
to the divergence of
{\bf t}_{mj}^{\textpm}
, in particular
\divergence{\bf t}_{mj}^{+}=0
whilst

\displaystyle\divergence{\bf t}_{0j}^{-}\;

\displaystyle=4j{\mathrm{z}}_{0j},\qquad j≥1,

\displaystyle\divergence{\bf t}_{mj}^{-}\;

\displaystyle={4(2j+|m|)(2j+|m|+1)(2j+|m|+2)\over 𝔯_{j}^{(|m|-1)}}\mathrm{i}\sign m{\mathrm{z}}_{mj}\quad m≠0.

8.1
Cylinders with periodic boundary conditions

We begin with the special case of a cylinder which is periodic in the
z
direction,
Γ=Ω\times{\mathbb{T}}
. A natural basis for
\mathring{H}^{1}
is then

\left\{{{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\mathrm{w}}_{mj}(x,y):m,k\in{\mathbb{Z}},j≥0}\right\}.

Note that this is a symmetry-adapted basis for both rotations (in
x,y
) and translations (in
z
, that is, a translation by
τ
in the
z
direction is equivalent to multiplication by
{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kτ}
).

A natural basis for
\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{curl}}
is

\displaystyle\left\{{{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\begin{pmatrix}{\bf n}_{mj}^{+}(x,y)\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}}:m,k\in{\mathbb{Z}},j≥1}\right\}

\displaystyle\cup\left\{{{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\begin{pmatrix}{\bf n}_{mj}^{-}(x,y)\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}}:m,k\in{\mathbb{Z}},j≥\max(0,1-|m|)}\right\}

\displaystyle\cup\left\{{{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\begin{pmatrix}𝟎\cr{\mathrm{w}}_{mj}(x,y)\cr\end{pmatrix}}:m,k\in{\mathbb{Z}},j≥0}\right\},

which are normal on the boundary of the cylinder.
Indeed, the gradient of the basis for
\mathring{H}^{1}
satisfies a simple recurrence when expanded in the basis for
\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{curl}}
:

\displaystyle\nabla{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\mathrm{w}}_{mj}(x,y,z)\;

\displaystyle={\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\begin{pmatrix}\nabla{\mathrm{w}}_{mj}(x,y)\cr{\mathrm{i}k{\mathrm{w}}_{mj}(x,y)}\cr\end{pmatrix}}={\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\begin{pmatrix}-(j+1){\bf n}_{m,j+1}^{+}(x,y)\cr{\mathrm{i}k{\mathrm{w}}_{mj}(x,y)}\cr\end{pmatrix}}

\displaystyle=-(j+1){\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\begin{pmatrix}{\bf n}_{m,j+1}^{+}(x,y)\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}}+\mathrm{i}k{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\begin{pmatrix}𝟎\cr{\mathrm{w}}_{mj}(x,y)\cr\end{pmatrix}}.

For
\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{div}}
a natural basis is

\displaystyle\left\{{{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\begin{pmatrix}{\bf t}_{mj}^{+}(x,y)\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}}:m,k\in{\mathbb{Z}},j≥1}\right\}

\displaystyle\cup\left\{{{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\begin{pmatrix}{\bf t}_{mj}^{-}(x,y)\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}}:m,k\in{\mathbb{Z}},j≥\max(0,1-|m|)}\right\}

\displaystyle\cup\left\{{{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\begin{pmatrix}𝟎\cr{\mathrm{z}}_{mj}(x,y)\cr\end{pmatrix}}:m,k\in{\mathbb{Z}},j≥0}\right\},

which is tangential on the boundary of the cylinder.
Indeed, writing the curl operator as

\curl{\begin{pmatrix}{f}\cr g\cr{h}\cr\end{pmatrix}}={\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{y}h-\partial_{z}g\cr\partial_{z}f-\partial_{x}h\cr\partial_{x}g-\partial_{y}f\cr\end{pmatrix}}={\begin{pmatrix}ρ(π/2){\left[{\partial_{z}{{\begin{pmatrix}f\cr g\cr\end{pmatrix}}}-{{\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{x}\cr\partial_{y}\cr\end{pmatrix}}}h}\right]}\cr\partial_{x}g-\partial_{y}f\cr\end{pmatrix}}

we see that applying it to the
\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{curl}}
basis gives us a simple combination of the
\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{div}}
basis:

\displaystyle\curl\

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\begin{pmatrix}{\bf n}_{mj}^{+}(x,y)\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}}={\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{i}kρ(π/2){{\bf n}_{mj}^{+}(x,y)}\cr\curl{{\bf n}_{mj}^{+}(x,y)}\cr\end{pmatrix}}=\mathrm{i}k{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\begin{pmatrix}{{\bf t}_{mj}^{+}(x,y)}\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}},

\displaystyle\curl\

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\begin{pmatrix}{\bf n}_{mj}^{-}(x,y)\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}}={\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{i}kρ(π/2){{\bf n}_{mj}^{-}(x,y)}\cr\curl{{\bf n}_{mj}^{-}(x,y)}\cr\end{pmatrix}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}k{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\begin{pmatrix}{\bf t}_{mj}^{-}(x,y)\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}}-κ_{mj}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\begin{pmatrix}𝟎\cr{\mathrm{z}}_{mj}(x,y)\cr\end{pmatrix}},

\displaystyle\curl\

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\begin{pmatrix}𝟎\cr{\mathrm{w}}_{m,j-1}(x,y)\cr\end{pmatrix}}=-{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\begin{pmatrix}ρ(π/2)\nabla{{\mathrm{w}}_{m,j-1}(x,y)}\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}}=j{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{{\begin{pmatrix}{{\bf t}_{mj}^{+}(x,y)}\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}}}

for

κ_{mj}:={\begin{cases}4j&m=0\\
{4(2j+|m|)(2j+|m|+1)(2j+|m|+2)\over 𝔯_{j}^{(|m|-1)}}\mathrm{i}\sign m&\hbox{otherwise}\end{cases}}.

(16)

Finally, an orthogonal basis for
L^{2}
is

\left\{{{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\mathrm{z}}_{mj}(x,y):m,k\in{\mathbb{Z}},j≥0}\right\}

and the divergence of the
\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{div}}
basis has a simple relationship:

\displaystyle\divergence{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\begin{pmatrix}{\bf t}_{mj}^{+}(x,y)\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}}\;

\displaystyle={\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}\divergence{\bf t}_{mj}^{+}(x,y)=-{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}\curl{\bf n}_{mj}^{+}(x,y)=0

\displaystyle\divergence{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\begin{pmatrix}{\bf t}_{mj}^{-}(x,y)\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}}\;

\displaystyle=κ_{mj}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\mathrm{z}}_{mj}(x,y),

\displaystyle\divergence{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\begin{pmatrix}𝟎\cr{\mathrm{z}}_{mj}(x,y)\cr\end{pmatrix}}\;

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}k{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\mathrm{z}}_{mj}(x,y).

8.1.1
Exactness of the periodic cylinder de Rham complex with boundary conditions.

We can use the above results to decompose the de Rham complex into sub-complexes. The periodic cylinder has Betti numbers

b_{0}=1
(one connected component),
b_{1}=1
(exactly one loop up to homotopy),
b_{2}=0
(all closed surfaces are homotopic to a point) and
b_{3}=0
. As in a disk,
b_{0}=1
corresponds to the one-dimensional space of constants in
L^{2}
which are not equal to the divergence of a in
\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{div}}
(i.e., tangential to the boundary). Since
b_{1}=1
we have a one-dimensional space

\mathspan\underbrace{{\begin{pmatrix}0\cr 0\cr 1\cr\end{pmatrix}}}_{={\begin{pmatrix}𝟎\cr{\mathrm{z}}_{00}\cr\end{pmatrix}}}\subset\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{div}}.

which are in the kernel of
\divergence
but cannot be written as the
\curl
of a function in
\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{curl}}
.

Nevertheless, we can decompose the de Rham complex into the following exact sub-complexes for each
m
,
k
and
j\geq 0
:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathspan\!\left\{{{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\mathrm{w}}_{mj}(x,y)}\right\}\overset{\nabla}{\rightarrow}\mathspan\!\left\{{\begin{matrix}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\begin{pmatrix}{\bf n}_{m,j+1}^{+}(x,y)\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}}\\
{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\begin{pmatrix}{\bf n}_{m,j+1}^{-}(x,y)\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}}\\
{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\begin{pmatrix}𝟎\cr{\mathrm{w}}_{mj}(x,y)\cr\end{pmatrix}}\end{matrix}}\right\}

\displaystyle\overset{\curl}{\rightarrow}\mathspan\!\left\{{\begin{matrix}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\begin{pmatrix}{\bf t}_{m,j+1}^{+}(x,y)\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}}\\
{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\begin{pmatrix}{\bf t}_{m,j+1}^{-}(x,y)\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}}\\
{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\begin{pmatrix}𝟎\cr{\mathrm{z}}_{m,j+1}(x,y)\cr\end{pmatrix}}\end{matrix}}\right\}\overset{\divergence}{\rightarrow}\mathspan\!\left\{{{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\mathrm{z}}_{m,j+1}(x,y)}\right\}\rightarrow 0.

We can see this is exact as
{\bf n}_{mj}^{+}
spans the kernel of
\curl
as the curl of
{\bf n}_{mj}^{-}
cannot be zero, and the only possible linear combination of the
\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{div}}
basis whose divergence is zero is the curl of the
\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{curl}}
basis. We also have additional exact sub-complexes for
m≠0

\displaystyle 0\rightarrow 0\overset{\nabla}{\rightarrow}\mathspan\!\left\{{{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\begin{pmatrix}{{\bf n}_{m0}^{-}(x,y)}\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}}}\right\}\overset{\curl}{\rightarrow}

\displaystyle\mathspan\!\left\{{\begin{matrix}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\begin{pmatrix}{\bf t}_{m0}^{+}(x,y)\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}}\\
{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\begin{pmatrix}{\bf t}_{m0}^{-}(x,y)\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}}\\
{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\begin{pmatrix}𝟎\cr{\mathrm{z}}_{m0}(x,y)\cr\end{pmatrix}}\end{matrix}}\right\}\overset{\divergence}{\rightarrow}\mathspan\!\left\{{{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\mathrm{z}}_{m0}(x,y)}\right\}\rightarrow 0

Finally, there are the additional sub-complexes

\displaystyle 0\rightarrow 0\overset{\nabla}{\rightarrow}0\overset{\curl}{\rightarrow}

\displaystyle\mathspan\!\left\{{{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\begin{pmatrix}𝟎\cr{\mathrm{z}}_{00}(x,y)\cr\end{pmatrix}}}\right\}\overset{\divergence}{\rightarrow}\mathspan\!\left\{{{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}kz}{\mathrm{z}}_{00}(x,y)}\right\}\rightarrow 0.

which are exact apart from when
k=0
(where we pick up the one-dimensional spaces predicted to not be part of an exact complex).

8.2
Finite cylinders

We can adapt the above construction to finite cylinders using suitable choices of (weighted) ultraspherical polynomials (or, equivalently, integrated Legendre functions
à la

p
-FEM
[
3
]
) instead of Fourier series in the
z
direction. In particular, consider the cylinder
Γ=[-1,1]\times Ω
. We use the basis

\left\{{(1-z^{2})C_{k}^{(3/2)}(z){\mathrm{w}}_{mj}(x,y):m\in{\mathbb{Z}},k≥0,j≥0}\right\}

which vanishes on the boundary of the cylinder as a basis for
\mathring{H}^{1}
, where
C_{k}^{(3/2)}(z):={2\over k+2}P_{k}^{(1,1)}(z)
, see
[
7
, 18.7.1]
. A natural basis for
\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{curl}}
is then

\displaystyle\left\{{(1-z^{2})C_{k}^{(3/2)}(z){\begin{pmatrix}{\bf n}_{mj}^{+}(x,y)\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}}:m\in{\mathbb{Z}},k≥0,j≥1}\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\cup\left\{{(1-z^{2})C_{k}^{(3/2)}(z){\begin{pmatrix}{\bf n}_{mj}^{-}(x,y)\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}}:m\in{\mathbb{Z}},k≥0,j≥\max(0,1-|m|)}\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\cup\left\{{P_{k}(z){\begin{pmatrix}𝟎\cr{\mathrm{w}}_{mj}(x,y)\cr\end{pmatrix}}:m\in{\mathbb{Z}},k≥0,j≥0}\right\},

which is normal at the boundary of the cylinder,
for the Legendre polynomials
P_{n}(z)=C_{n}^{(1/2)}(z)=P_{n}^{(0,0)}(z)
.
Since,
[
7
, 18.9.20]
,

{\hbox{d}\over\hbox{d}{z}}[(1-z^{2})C_{n}^{(3/2)}(z)]=-(n+1)(n+2)P_{n+1}(z)

we have

\displaystyle\nabla

\displaystyle(1-z^{2})C_{k}^{(3/2)}(z){\mathrm{w}}_{mj}(x,y)

\displaystyle=-(j+1)(1-z^{2})C_{k}^{(3/2)}(z){\begin{pmatrix}{\bf n}_{m,j+1}^{+}(x,y)\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}}-(k+1)(k+2)P_{k}(z){\begin{pmatrix}𝟎\cr{\mathrm{w}}_{mj}(x,y)\cr\end{pmatrix}}.

A natural basis for
\mathring{H}_{\mathrm{div}}
is

\displaystyle\left\{{P_{k}(z){\begin{pmatrix}{\bf t}_{mj}^{+}(x,y)\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}}:m\in{\mathbb{Z}},k≥0,j≥1}\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\cup\left\{{P_{k}(z){\begin{pmatrix}{\bf t}_{mj}^{-}(x,y)\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}}:m\in{\mathbb{Z}},k≥0,j≥\max(0,1-|m|)}\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\cup\left\{{(1-z^{2})C_{k}^{(3/2)}(z){\begin{pmatrix}𝟎\cr{\mathrm{z}}_{mj}(x,y)\cr\end{pmatrix}}:m\in{\mathbb{Z}},k≥0,j≥0}\right\},

which are tangential on the boundary of a cylinder. We find that

\displaystyle\curl

\displaystyle(1-z^{2})C_{k}^{(3/2)}(z){\begin{pmatrix}{\bf n}_{mj}^{+}(x,y)\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}}=-(k+1)(k+2)P_{k+1}(z){\begin{pmatrix}{\bf t}_{mj}^{+}(x,y)\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}}

\displaystyle\curl

\displaystyle(1-z^{2})C_{k}^{(3/2)}(z){\begin{pmatrix}{\bf n}_{mj}^{-}(x,y)\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}}=-(k+1)(k+2)P_{k+1}(z){\begin{pmatrix}{\bf t}_{mj}^{-}(x,y)\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}}

\displaystyle\qquad-κ_{mj}(1-z^{2})C_{k}^{(3/2)}(z){\begin{pmatrix}𝟎\cr{\mathrm{z}}_{mj}(x,y)\cr\end{pmatrix}},

\displaystyle\curl

\displaystyle P_{k}(z){\begin{pmatrix}𝟎\cr{\mathrm{w}}_{m,j-1}(x,y)\cr\end{pmatrix}}=jP_{k}(z){\begin{pmatrix}{{\bf t}_{mj}^{+}(x,y)}\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}}.

Finally, a natural basis for
L^{2}
is

\left\{{P_{k}(z){\mathrm{z}}_{mj}(x,y):m\in{\mathbb{Z}},k,j≥0}\right\}

and indeed

\displaystyle\divergence P_{k}(z){\begin{pmatrix}{\bf t}_{mj}^{+}(x,y)\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}}\;

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\divergence P_{k}(z){\begin{pmatrix}{\bf t}_{mj}^{-}(x,y)\cr 0\cr\end{pmatrix}}\;

\displaystyle=κ_{mj}P_{k}(z){\mathrm{z}}_{mj}(x,y),

\displaystyle\divergence(1-z^{2})C_{k}^{(3/2)}(z){\begin{pmatrix}𝟎\cr{\mathrm{z}}_{mj}(x,y)\cr\end{pmatrix}}\;

\displaystyle=-(k+1)(k+2)P_{k+1}(z){\mathrm{z}}_{mj}(x,y).

9
Future directions

We have constructed a new basis for vector polynomials that are normal at the boundary of a disk, which have simple recurrence relationships for the gradient and curl, corresponding to the 2D de Rham complex with boundary conditions. These immediately give a basis for the 3D de Rham complex with boundary conditions on both a periodic and finite cylinder.
These results have applications in the numerical solution of PDEs in disks and cylinders a la the Finite Element Exterior Calculus (see, e.g.,
[
1
]
), and will, for example, lead to optimal complexity solvers for Maxwell-like equations in periodic cylinders, including equivariant variable coefficients. We note that the mass matrices can be deduced from the recurrences in Lemma
10
, which can be used to relate
{\bf n}_{mj}^{ν}
to the Zernike vector polynomials
{\bf z}_{mj}^{(0),ν}
that are orthogonal with respect to
L^{2}
.

A straightforward extension is to dropping the restriction on boundary conditions, which is necessary for incorporation into an
hp
-FEM framework
à la

[
26
]
. This would require adding the harmonic polynomials to
{\mathrm{w}}_{mj}
to form a complete basis of polynomials and defining
{\mathcal{K}}
in Lemma
14
in terms of a suitable basis so that the recurrence relationships remain simple. Extension to annuli is a more challenging endeavour. A scalar basis was used successfully for solving PDEs
[
25
,
26
]
including in an
hp
-FEM framework, built on orthogonal polynomials with respect to the weight
(1-r^{2})(r^{2}-ρ^{2})
. The construction in this paper was designed with extensions to the de Rham complex in annuli (and cylinderical annuli) in mind, however, it is unclear what the analogue of
N(x,y)
is on an annulus. It is possible one would need to view an analogue of
{\bf n}_{mj}^{ν}
in terms of biorthogonality properties,
à la
recent work on bases for
H_{\mathrm{curl}}
in hypercubes and simplices
[
20
]
.

Another possible extension is to higher-dimensional balls, in particular in 3D, which requires spherical harmonics instead of Fourier modes, involving matrix versions of symmetry-adapted bases.
On the surface of the sphere sparse recurrence relationships relating the surface gradient of spherical harmonics to spin weighted spherical harmonics has been used effectively in the numerical solution of PDEs
[
34
]
. What remains open is a similar construction on spherical caps, allowing for surface gradients and curls in the vein of
[
29
]
, though this would require working with tangent spaces.

Symmetry-adapted bases can be used to construct equivariant bases on tensor product domains where the group action is applied simultaneously to each variable. For example, for
{\bf x},{\bf y}\in Ω
consider the matrix-valued polynomial

N_{mkj}({\bf x},{\bf y}):={\bf n}_{m,k}({\bf x}){\bf n}_{-m,j}({\bf y})^{\top}.

This function is equivariant (similar to the sense of Definition
4
) when we apply the rotation to each variable:

N_{mkj}(ρ(φ){\bf x},ρ(φ){\bf y})ρ(φ)=ρ(φ){\bf n}_{m,k}({\bf x}){\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}mφ}{\rm e}^{-\mathrm{i}mφ}{\bf n}_{-m,j}(ρ(φ){\bf y})^{\top}ρ(φ)^{\top}ρ(φ)=ρ(φ)N_{mkj}({\bf x},{\bf y}).

Thus we can use the orthogonal polynomials introduced here to construct equivariant orthogonal polynomials on such product domains.
Similar constructions in the 3D case, where the irreducible representations are given in terms of spherical harmonics, but not necessarily involving orthogonal polynomials, have proven effective in the Atomic Cluster Expansion in quantum chemistry
[
4
,
17
,
8
,
10
,
6
]
.

We focussed on the de Rham complex but our construction is also applicable to the Koszul complex, see
[
1
, Section 7.2]
, which in 2D has the form of multiplication by
{\begin{pmatrix}x\cr y\cr\end{pmatrix}}
and
{{\left({-y,x}\right)}^{\top}}
. In particular, an integration-by-parts argument shows that there exist constants such that

\displaystyle{\begin{pmatrix}x\cr y\cr\end{pmatrix}}{\mathrm{z}}_{mj}\;

\displaystyle=\hbox{const.}{\bf n}_{m,j+1}^{1}+\hbox{const.}{\bf n}_{m,j+1}^{2}+\hbox{const.}{\bf n}_{m,j}^{1}+\hbox{const.}{\bf n}_{m,j}^{2},

\displaystyle{\left({-y,x}\right)}{\bf n}_{mj}\;

\displaystyle=\hbox{const.}{\mathrm{w}}_{m,j-1}+\hbox{const.}{\mathrm{w}}_{m,j-2}.

Matching leading order terms would likely give explicit expressions for these constants.

Also of interest is extension to the elasticity complex, cf.
[
1
, Section 8.8]
, which would enable the efficient numerical solution of elasticity or Stokes flow in a cylinder. Note that whilst the approach of
[
33
]
works well for Stokes (and Navier–Stokes) in a cylinder, putting everything in the setting of the elasticity complex may facilitate discretisation of more general tensor fields. Other approaches are effective for Navier–Stokes in 2D
[
32
,
35
,
28
]
but are based on a stream/vorticity formulation that breaks down in 3D. More generally, there is recent work on a 2-complex for
matrix fields
[
16
]
, and it may be possible to construct matrix orthogonal polynomials in a disk corresponding to these matrix Sobolev spaces in a way that leads to simple recurrence relationships.
