The association between vitamin D and hypothyroidism: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Xiaopeng Sun, Changgui Kou, Yuan Feng, Xijie He, Xiangrui Song, Qianlu Ding, Qianlong Huang, Qianyi Wang, Yong Shen, Wei Bai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypothyroidism is a prevalent pathological condition resulting from insufficient thyroid hormone production by the thyroid gland. The potential role of vitamin D supplementation in the prevention and treatment of various diseases is well-recognized. However, its specific impact on individuals with hypothyroidism remains unclear.MethodsThis systematic review follows the PRISMA 2020 flowchart. The review aimed to evaluate the effects of vitamin D on hypothyroidism by synthesizing available evidence. A comprehensive search was conducted across several databases, including PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library, up to February 2024. Standard mean differences (SMDs) and odds ratios (ORs) with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using fixed or random effects models, based on heterogeneity assessed by the I² statistic. Sensitivity analyses and assessments of publication bias were also performed. The certainty of evidence was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) system.ResultsA total of 5 randomized controlled trials and 11 observational studies, encompassing 4,152 subjects, met the inclusion criteria. The analysis revealed that vitamin D supplementation significantly reduced thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) (SMD = -1.76 mIU L− 1, 95% CI [-3.23, -0.29] mIU L− 1), and thyroid peroxidase antibody (TPO-Ab) (SMD = -113.31 U ml− 1, 95% CI [-141.42, -85.21] U ml− 1). Additionally, serum vitamin D deficiency was significantly associated with the prevalence of hypothyroidism (OR = 4.06, 95% CI [1.96, 8.39]).ConclusionThe results indicate that patients with hypothyroidism are more likely to have low serum vitamin D levels compared to healthy individuals. Moreover, vitamin D supplementation significantly reduced TSH and TPO-Ab levels. It is recommended that patients with hypothyroidism have serum vitamin D levels routinely monitored in clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12986-026-01157-6.Keywords: Hypothyroidism, Vitamin D, Thyroid Stimulating Hormone (TSH), Thyroid Peroxidase Antibody (TPO-Ab), Systematic Review and Meta-analysis

Introduction

Hypothyroidism is a prevalent condition stemming from the thyroid gland’s insufficient production of thyroid hormones [1]. It can be classified as primary (abnormalities of the thyroid gland itself) or secondary/central (due to hypothalamic or pituitary disease) hypothyroidism [2]. Lethargy, apathy, slow thinking, emotional instability, and despair are typical clinical signs of hypothyroidism [3]. Although hypothyroidism cannot be diagnosed by a single symptom, the combination of these symptoms greatly increases the risk of the disorder [4]. It is common worldwide, is more common in women than in men, and its incidence increases with age, especially after midlife onset [2, 5]. The prevalence of hypothyroidism has been shown to ranges from 1 to 2% in iodine-sufficient countries, rising to 7% among people aged 85 to 89, and may be even higher in iodine-deficient areas [5]. Furthermore, hypothyroidism is associated with adverse outcomes such as cardiac insufficiency, atherosclerosis, hypertension, and coagulation disorders [6]. Ultimately, it is anticipated to diminish the life expectancy of affected individuals and, if left untreated, may prove fatal [1]. Therefore, early screening and intervention are critical in the early stages of hypothyroidism.

Trace elements and vitamins are collectively known as micronutrients and are necessary for the body’s basic metabolic reactions [7]. Previous studies have shown a strong association between micronutrients and several diseases, such as anemia [8], inflammatory bowel disease [9], and heart failure [10]. Among them, vitamin D, as a fat-soluble vitamin, exists mainly in the form of vitamin D2 and vitamin D3 and plays an important role in both bone metabolism and immunity [11]. Vitamin D may modify the risk of several diseases, including hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and diabetes, while vitamin D deficiency leads to a number of adverse health outcomes [12–14]. Studies show that risk factors for vitamin D deficiency, including low sunlight exposure, special populations such as pregnant women and children, and inadequate intake of vitamin D-rich foods such as oily fish and cod liver oil, result in a serious health problem worldwide [15, 16]. Although the regulatory role of vitamin D in many biological processes is well documented, there is insufficient evidence to support the use of vitamin D supplements for the prevention or treatment of hypothyroidism [17].

The effect of vitamin D supplementation on hypothyroidism and the relationship between serum vitamin D levels and hypothyroidism remain controversial. While some randomized controlled trials [18, 19] have demonstrated that vitamin D supplementation significantly reduces mean thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels in hypothyroid patients and improves thyroid peroxidase antibody (TPO-Ab) levels [20], others have reported no significant change in TSH levels [21]. Therefore, the aims of this review were twofold: (1) to evaluate, through randomized controlled trials, whether vitamin D supplementation modifies thyroid-related biochemical markers in patients with hypothyroidism; and (2) to determine, based on observational evidence, whether low serum vitamin D concentrations are associated with an increased prevalence of hypothyroidism.

Materials and methods

Sources and methods of data retrieval

This study was conducted out in accordance with the guidelines outlined in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement, its methodology having been detailed previously. The protocol for the review is registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the registration number CRD4202458057.

Relevant literature was searched from inception through February 2024 in PubMed, Embase, Cochrane, and Web of Science. The main search terms were “vitamin D” and “hypothyroidism”. As detailed in Table S5, the literature search was restricted to human subjects and English-language publications. We applied this language limitation to ensure high fidelity in data extraction and because English serves as the predominant medium for international scientific reporting, though we acknowledge the potential introduction of language bias. The initial screening of publications was performed by reading the titles and abstracts. Subsequently, to select the eligible studies, a complete reading of the publications was conducted. In addition to the major databases, grey literature (theses, preprints, clinical trial registries) was systematically searched to identify any ongoing or unpublished studies that met our inclusion criteria.

Study selection process

Two independent researchers (X.J.H and X.R.S) conducted a systematic review of the published literature on the conduct and reporting of studies in observational and prospective cohort studies. After deletion of duplicates, a two-stage screening protocol was implemented: an initial eligibility assessment based on title/abstract assessment, followed by full-text assessment using predefined inclusion criteria. Researchers identified studies eligible for analysis by examining the title and abstract of each record, followed by a full-text review. Consensus was reached through discussion, and unresolved issues were adjudicated by a third senior researcher. For articles for which full text could not be retrieved, the full-text retrieval protocol required that corresponding authors be contacted three times within 21 days via a valid institutional e-mail; unanswered submissions were excluded according to the protocol. In addition, we reviewed the bibliography and identified topics such as bias, retrieval and abstracting, heterogeneity, study classification, and statistical methods. The publication period of the included articles is set from inception through February 2024. Fig. 1 shows the flow diagram of the literature search and selection.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

For randomized controlled trials (RCTs), the inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Participants diagnosed with hypothyroidism based on local diagnostic criteria, (2) The intervention group receiving vitamin D supplements, (3) The comparison group included hypothyroid patients who were not given vitamin D supplements or received a placebo, (4) Studies should report thyroid function indicators for both the vitamin D group and the non-supplemented group. The exclusion criteria included: (1) Single-arm studies, studies without placebo, or studies with unquantifiable doses of vitamin D supplements, (2) Patients with chronic kidney, liver, or cardiac disease, malignancy, epilepsy, tuberculosis, immunodeficiency, and those on chronic medications that might interfere with thyroid hormone or vitamin D metabolism were excluded; (3) Non-human studies, reviews, and conference papers. For studies reporting on overlapping cohorts, only the publication with the largest sample size or most comprehensive data was included to avoid duplication of participants.

For observational studies, the inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with hypothyroidism, was defined according to local criteria; (2) serum vitamin D levels in patients with hypothyroidism, i.e., the case group; (3) serum vitamin D levels in patients without hypothyroidism, i.e., the healthy control group; (4) the result of observational studies expressed as odds ratios (ORs) and their 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) to assess the relationship between vitamin D deficiency and the risk of hypothyroidism, which vitamin D deficiency is defined as a serum 25(OH)D level < 20 ng mL− 1 (50 nmol L− 1), based on the most common cutoff in the included studies. Exclusion criteria included: (1) Lack of initial data, (2) Animal studies, duplicate literature, in vitro research, reviews, and conference papers were excluded.

Data extraction

The RCT studies, we extracted the following information: (1) first author, nationality, year of publication, numbers, mean age, and sex of vitamin D intervention subjects and the controls; (2) the type of subjects, intervention duration, and dosage administered; and (3) the variations of TSH, and TPO-Ab in the vitamin D intervention and control subjects.

In the case of observational studies, the extracted data included: (1) first author, nationality, publication year, numbers, mean age, and sex of individuals in the case/supplementation groups and controls; (2) the subject type; and (3) the indicator to evaluate the risk for deficiency serum vitamin D concentration: ORs and their 95% CIs. Serum vitamin D testing is mainly performed using a serum biochemical analyzer or commercial kits for vitamin D testing, etc.

Risk of bias within individual studies

The risk of bias of the included RCTs was independently assessed using the Cochrane risk of bias tool (RoB 1) as described in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions. The assessments were performed using Review Manager software. RoB 1 was selected because it remains a widely accepted tool for non-Cochrane reviews and its integration within RevMan provides a standardized assessment and visualization framework [22]. In addition, the Newcastle–Ottawa scale (NOS), which included three parts (selection of the patients, comparability of the case/exposure groups, and controls and exposure evaluation), was applied to determine the bias risk in non-randomized studies. Besides, the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) system was used to classify the quality of evidence.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the statistical software Review Manager version 5.3 and R version 3.3. Statistical heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q statistic and the I2 statistic [23]. A fixed-effects model was used to determine the indicators of standard mean differences (SMDs) and odds ratios (ORs) with their 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) if I2 < 50% indicated that the heterogeneity was not substantial. If I2 > 50% indicated significant heterogeneity, a random-effects model was used [23, 24]. In line with Cochrane Handbook recommendations, SMDs were calculated using post-intervention values of TSH and TPO-Ab for RCTs assessing intervention effects at a specific time point. Subgroup analyses were planned to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. For the observational studies, analyses were stratified by geographic region [5] and sex [25, 26]. Given the observed distribution of female participant percentages among the included studies, studies were categorized post-hoc into two groups for analysis: those with > 85% female participants and those with ≤ 85%. This cutoff was chosen to distinguish studies with predominantly female cohorts from those with more balanced sex ratios. For the RCTs, subgroup analyses were stratified by region and the duration of vitamin D supplementation intervention (≤ two months and > two months) [27]. Meta-regression analysis for observational studies was performed based on quality assessment scores from the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), sample size, and latitude of the study region (categorized as ≤ 30°, 30–45°, > 45° based on geographic coordinates). Meta-regression was not performed for the RCTs because only 5 studies were available, falling below the minimum threshold of approximately 10 studies per covariate recommended by the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions. Publication bias was evaluated using both Egger’s and Begg’s tests, and a sensitivity analysis was conducted to examine the impact of removing influential studies. The significance level for the systematic review and meta-analysis was set at α = 0.05.

Result

A total of 16 studies with 4,152 subjects met the inclusion criteria of this study. These comprised 11 observational studies [28–38] that analyzed serum vitamin D concentrations in subjects with hypothyroidism compared with normal controls, and 5 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) [19, 21, 39–41] that evaluated changes in thyroid function-related indicators (TSH and TPO-Ab) following vitamin D supplementation. Additionally, the risk of bias is shown in Figure S1 and Table S1. We did not exclude any articles from the systematic review and meta-analysis for quality reasons, as the quality of the studies included in this systematic review and meta-analysis was acceptable. Association between serum vitamin D concentrations and hypothyroidism

To evaluate the association between vitamin D deficiency and hypothyroidism, we conducted a meta-analysis of 11 cross-sectional studies of serum vitamin D concentrations. As shown in Fig. 2, the forest plot showed serum vitamin D deficiency was significantly associated with the prevalence of hypothyroidism (OR = 4.06, 95% CI [1.96, 8.39], P < 0.001). In addition, serum vitamin D concentration was not observed in Begg’s test for publication bias (t = 1.63, P = 0.102). The funnel plot publication bias for the included studies is given in Figure S2. We also performed subgroup analysis based on sex and region (Figures S3 and S4). When studies were subdivided by the percentage of female participants (> 85% and ≤ 85%), the direction of association was consistent with the main finding across both groups. In the subgroup analysis by region (Asia and Non-Asia), a significant association was observed in studies conducted in Asia (OR = 5.65, 95% CI [2.08, 15.65], P < 0.001), whereas no statistically significant association was found in the Non-Asia subgroup. Besides, we performed meta-regression analyses on 11 cross-sectional studies to explore sources of heterogeneity (Table S4). The results showed that no statistical significance was found in regression analyses for the continuous variable NOS (β = -0.1573, Z = -0.4681, P = 0.6397) and sample size (β = 0.0004, Z = 0.7320, P = 0.4642). In contrast, latitude showed a significant negative association with VD levels (β = -1.6905, Z = -3.2557, P = 0.001). Additionally, the results of the sensitivity analyses excluding 1 piece of literature in turn showed that the results were all statistically significant and the findings were robust (Figure S5).

Effect of vitamin D supplementation on Hypothyroid patients

The meta-analysis of RCTs showed that subjects supplemented with vitamin D had significantly lower levels of TSH and TPO-Ab compared to the control group (SMD = -1.76 mIU L− 1, 95% CI [-3.23, -0.29] mIU L− 1; SMD = -113.31 U ml− 1, 95% CI [-141.42, -85.21] U ml− 1) (Fig. 3). The mean TSH levels in the vitamin D group were approximately 0.2 to 3.6 mIU L− 1 lower than those in the control group after intervention. Similarly, the mean TPO-Ab levels in the vitamin D group were approximately 63.5 to 116.5 U ml− 1 lower than those in the control group after intervention. These ranges reflect the magnitude of differences observed at the end of the intervention period. In addition, neither TSH nor TPO-Ab was observed in Egger’s test for publication bias (Z = -0.01, P = 0.999; Z = -0.34, P = 0.737). There was no significant publication bias found in both indicator. Besides, we performed subgroup analysis based on region and intervention time in Table 3. In the Iran subgroup, participants had a statistically significant 0.58-fold reduction in the risk of TSH with vitamin D supplementation compared to the control group (SMD = -0.58, 95% CI [-0.91, -0.24], P < 0.001), and in the non-Iran subgroup, participants had a significant reduction in the risk of TSH with vitamin D supplementation compared to the control group that was statistically significant (SMD = -3.52, 95% CI [-4.02, -3.02], P < 0.001). In the analysis by intervention duration, the association was not statistically significant in either the subgroup with interventions ≤ 2 months or the subgroup with interventions > 2 months.

Discussion

The effects of vitamin D on hypothyroidism have been examined in several studies, yet the findings have yielded inconsistent results. Some studies have shown that vitamin D supplementation has a significant effect on improving thyroid function and lowering TPO-Ab levels [42, 43]. However, other studies have shown that vitamin D supplementation does not lower TPO-Ab/TG-Ab tickers or improve thyroid function [44]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis that combines cross-sectional and RCT studies to systematically elucidate the association between vitamin D and hypothyroidism. Our meta-analysis demonstrates two key insights: (1) Observational data indicate an association between vitamin D deficiency and hypothyroidism; (2) Interventional RCTs show vitamin D supplementation reduces TSH and TPO-Ab. These findings address the paradox in prior literature.

Both TSH and TPO-Ab represent remarkably sensitive and specific indices for testing thyroid function and can keenly reflect changes in thyroid function in hypothyroid patients after vitamin D supplementation. Our analysis confirmed that vitamin D supplementation significantly reduced TSH and TPO-Ab levels, which is consistent with the results of previous meta-analyses [20, 45]. Vitamin D may influence hypothyroidism by lowering these two markers, though the precise mechanism remains unclear. Several potential explanations have been proposed, with the primary mechanisms suggested as follows: Vitamin D has a hindering effect on the production of inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-1, IL-6, IL-8, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α [46]. Apart from chronic inflammation, oxidative stress is also a hallmark of thyroid tissue damage in hypothyroidism [47, 48]. Vitamin D downregulates nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) signaling, a key mediator of inflammatory responses, and enhances the synthesis of antioxidant enzymes such as glutathione peroxidase [49]. Besides, vitamin D can modulate the activity of various immune system cells and is involved in the regulation of the immune system [50]. The differences in final TSH and TPO-Ab levels between the vitamin D group and the control group hold significant clinical relevance. For instance, in patients with subclinical hypothyroidism (where TSH typically ranges between 4.5 and 10 mIU L− 1), such reductions may bring TSH values closer to the normal reference range. This could influence monitoring frequency or decisions regarding thyroxine therapy for borderline cases. In addition, we performed subgroup analyses of TSH levels according to different regions (Iranian and non-Iranian) and different durations of intervention with vitamin D supplementation (≤ 2 months and > 2 months). The results showed a more pronounced effect of vitamin D supplementation in reducing TSH levels in the non-Iranian subgroup compared to the placebo group, which we hypothesize may be related to the fact that the Iranian region is more restricted geographically, whereas the non-Iranian region encompasses a wider range of areas, suggesting that the results may be more representative and rigorous. The instability of the living environment and dietary factors in the Iranian region may also lead to poor adherence to vitamin D supplementation in this subgroup of the population, which in turn has a lesser effect on TSH and TPO-Ab. In addition, vitamin D supplementation did not have a statistically significant effect on TSH levels in either of the two different intervention time subgroups compared with the placebo group, which may be due to reduced statistical power from small sample sizes post-stratification. This precludes definitive conclusions on optimal treatment duration — a critical gap for future large-scale trials. A previous RCT [51] showed a strong and statistically significant effect of vitamin D supplementation on thyroid function in the 7-month follow-up subgroup rather than the 12-month follow-up subgroup, and the results may also be related to its larger sample size in the 7-month follow-up (i.e., these analyses included an additional 98 incident cases of hypothyroidism). It is also possible that this is because some people with subclinical hypothyroidism who benefited from vitamin D supplementation at the time of the early intervention dropped out of follow-up. These individuals would have been excluded from subsequent interventions, resulting in a subsequent reduction in benefits. The absence of free triiodothyronine (FT-3) and free thyroxine (FT-4) analyses constitutes a limitation. Only 2 of 5 RCTs reported these biomarkers, precluding meta-analysis due to insufficient data. This gap restricts our understanding of vitamin D’s impact on active thyroid hormone synthesis. TSH reflects pituitary feedback, while FT3/FT4 directly indicates thyroid gland output. Vitamin D supplementation reduced TSH in our analysis, yet without FT3/FT4 data, we cannot confirm whether this translates to improved thyroid hormone production or merely reflects pituitary-axis modulation.

Vitamin D is an essential fat-soluble trace element that maintains the physiological and biochemical functions of the body [52]. Previous studies have shown an association between serum vitamin D levels and thyroid function in the organism [50, 53, 54], which is consistent with our findings. The association between hypothyroidism and vitamin D deficiency may be explained by two mechanisms. It is well known that in iodine-sufficient populations, hypothyroidism is usually a more immediate clinical manifestation in patients with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, which is an autoimmune disease [37]. Vitamin D provides a powerful immunomodulatory effect and can be instrumental in the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases [55, 56]. Therefore, vitamin D may help control autoimmunity and improve clinical outcomes in patients with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, thereby improving thyroid function and reducing the development of hypothyroidism. In addition, it has been shown that increased fat mass in the body of patients with hypothyroidism causes malabsorption of vitamin D in the intestines, leading to vitamin D deficiency in patients [57–59]. Similarly, we conducted subgroup analyses of the included cross-sectional studies according to different regions (Asian and non-Asian) and different sex ratios (≤ 85% and > 85% female representation) to look for possible influencing factors of hypothyroidism. A significant association between vitamin D deficiency and hypothyroidism has been observed in different proportions of the female subgroup. Previous studies have shown that the prevalence of hypothyroidism in women is approximately 2–3 times higher than in men due to the influence of the body’s oestrogen. Thus, it is possible that oestrogen may also influence the association between vitamin D and hypothyroidism [27]. Vitamin D deficiency was significantly associated with hypothyroidism in Asian subgroups, but no statistically significant association has been found in non-Asian subgroups. Vitamin D deficiency is widespread throughout Asian populations, influenced by factors such as geography and lack of sunlight [60, 61]. In addition, it is also closely related to nutritional deficiencies caused by dietary factors and genetic factors in Asia [62]. Dietary factors are recognized as key influences in the development of many diseases, including thyroid disorders [63, 64]. Some studies suggest that dietary patterns and insufficient intake of vitamin D-rich foods may interfere with thyroid health. According to a study evaluating the effects of Mediterranean and gluten-free diets on thyroid function and autoantibody levels in patients, there was no statistically significant difference in TSH levels between the groups before the dietary intervention, but the difference was statistically significant after the intervention (P < 0.05). Besides, TPO-Ab and TG-Ab levels also decreased after the intervention [65]. And studies have shown that adult dietary patterns may affect thyroid function by influencing iodine intake and the body’s vitamin D levels [66–68]. Meta-regression indicated a significant negative association between latitude and vitamin D levels, meaning that higher latitude was associated with a lower risk of vitamin D deficiency. This pattern may be partly explained by established public health measures in higher-latitude regions, such as systematic vitamin D supplementation and food fortification. However, the potential influence of unmeasured confounders, including dietary habits and skin pigmentation, requires further investigation. Observational links between low vitamin D and hypothyroidism do not imply causality. More data are needed to confirm a causal relationship between vitamin D and its metabolic pathways and the development of hypothyroidism.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several notable strengths. To our knowledge, it is the first systematic review and meta-analysis to comprehensively synthesize both observational and interventional evidence on the relationship between vitamin D and hypothyroidism. It employed a rigorous search strategy across four major databases. Furthermore, multiple analytical approaches, including subgroup analyses for both study types and meta-regression for observational studies, were used to explore potential sources of heterogeneity and identify effect modifiers. Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the included studies exhibited moderate to high heterogeneity, primarily stemming from variations in study populations, geographic locations, and outcome definitions. Second, several thyroid function indicators, such as FT3 and FT4, were not analyzed due to data constraints; therefore, the observed reductions in TSH and TPO-Ab may not directly reflect improved thyroid hormone function. Third, inconsistent reporting of detection methods and diagnostic criteria precluded further meta-regression analyses. Besides, our search was limited to English-language publications, which may have introduced language bias. Additionally, the clinical interpretation of post-intervention values is constrained by the lack of baseline levels in many studies.

Future directions

Given the limitations outlined above, future research should prioritize several areas. Large-scale multicenter RCTs are needed to verify the effects of vitamin D on thyroid function, with standardized dosing, well-defined treatment durations, and longer follow-up. These trials should report a full panel of thyroid function indicators (FT3, FT4, TSH, and TPO-Ab) rather than isolated markers. Greater consistency in vitamin D assay methods and diagnostic criteria for hypothyroidism across studies would also strengthen the feasibility of future meta-regression analyses. Beyond randomized evidence, prospective cohort studies in diverse populations with rigorous adjustment for confounders are needed to disentangle the temporal and causal relationships between vitamin D status and hypothyroidism.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that patients with hypothyroidism are more likely to have low serum vitamin D levels compared to healthy individuals. Moreover, vitamin D supplementation significantly reduced TSH and TPO-Ab levels. It is recommended that patients with hypothyroidism have serum vitamin D levels routinely monitored in clinical practice. However, further high-quality randomized controlled trials are required before specific clinical recommendations can be formulated.
