In-phase current and temperature oscillations reduce PEM fuel cell resistivity: A modeling study
Andrei Kulikovsky

Abstract
We have developed a non-isothermal analytical model for the impedance of the cathode catalyst layer (CCL) in a PEM fuel cell. In-phase harmonic perturbations to the current density and temperature reduce the impedance and the static polarization resistivity of the CCL due to lowering proton transport losses. A special selection of the current and temperature perturbation amplitudes allows for complete elimination of these losses.



I

Introduction

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) of polymer electrolyte membrane (PEM)
fuel cells involves applying to the cell a small-amplitude harmonic signal, either
the current or potential, and measuring the resulting potential or current response.
EIS is one of the best and most popular methods of cell characterisation
(see Lasia’s book
Lasia 2014
and reviews
Tang et al. 2020
;
Huang et al. 2020
).

However, much less work has been devoted to studying cell operating regime with
one of the operating parameters oscillating.
Experimental studies of Kim et al.
Kim et al. 2008
and Hwang et al.
Hwang et al. 2010

have demonstrated improved PEMFC performance under oscillating cathode flow velocity.
Modeling work of Kulikovsky
Kulikovsky 2024a
;
Kulikovsky 2024b
have shown
that in-phase harmonic perturbations of the cell potential and oxygen concentration
lead to a dramatic reduction in PEMFC resistivity. In these works, the application
of the harmonic signal (or signals) aims to change the operating regime, rather
than measuring the impedance.

Below, we show that in-phase oscillations of the cell current density and temperature
decrease the impedance of the cathode catalyst layer (CCL).
A pair of “potential–oxygen concentration”
perturbations lowers the CCL resistivity by decreasing the oxygen transport
losses in the CCL
Kulikovsky 2024a
;
Kulikovsky 2024b
.
Similarly, the pair of “current–temperature” perturbations
reduces the CCL resistivity by lowering the proton transport losses
due to the Arrhenius dependence of proton conductivity on temperature.

Model

For brevity, the application
of temperature oscillations will be referred to as temperature control.
A model of heat transport through the cell cathode side leads to linear
relations between small perturbations of the CCL and flow field (FF)
temperatures, namely
T_{\rm CCL}^{1}
,
T_{\rm FF}^{1}
(to be published elsewhere).
The external surface
of the FF can be used for temperature control.
These linear relations mean that the applied small
harmonic perturbation
T_{\rm FF}^{1}
causes a small harmonic perturbation
of the CCL temperature
T_{\rm CCL}^{1}
.
The CCL proton conductivity
\sigma_{p}
depends on the temperature via the Arrhenius law (see below).
Thus, by modulating the CCL temperature, we modulate the proton conductivity.

To study the effect of
\sigma_{p}
oscillations on CCL impedance, we will assume
that oxygen transport loss in the CCL is negligible.
This assumption is valid if the cell current is sufficiently small.
In that case, the proton charge conservation equation is

C_{dl}\dfrac{\partial{\eta}}{\partial{t}}-\sigma_{p}\dfrac{\partial^{2}{\eta}}{\partial{x}^{2}}=-i_{*}\left(\dfrac{c_{1}}{c_{ref}}\right)\exp\left(\dfrac{\eta}{b}\right)

(1)

Here,

C_{dl}
is the double layer capacitance,

\eta
the ORR overpotential, positive by convention,

\sigma_{p}
the CCL proton conductivity,

i_{*}
the ORR volumetric exchange current density,

c_{1}
the oxygen molar concentration in the CCL,
which is assumed to be uniform,

c_{ref}
the reference oxygen concentration, and

b
the ORR Tafel slope.
For further references we note that the static equation for
\eta^{0}
is

\sigma_{p}^{0}\dfrac{\partial^{2}{\eta^{0}}}{\partial{x}^{2}}=i_{*}\left(\dfrac{c_{1}}{c_{ref}}\right){\rm e}^{\eta_{0}/b}.

(2)

Here and below, the subscripts 0 and 1 mark the static variables and the small perturbation
amplitudes, respectively.

Following Springer et al.
Springer et al. 1991
, the temperature dependence
\sigma_{p}(T)
is given
by the Arrhenius law

\sigma_{p}(T)=\sigma_{p}^{*}\exp\left(T_{\sigma}\left(\dfrac{1}{303}-\dfrac{1}{T}\right)\right),\quad T_{\sigma}=1268~\text{K.}

(3)

Setting
\sigma_{p}=\sigma_{p}^{0}+\sigma_{p}^{1}
,

T=T^{0}+T^{1}
, expanding the exponent in a Taylor series,
and retaining the two leading terms, we get the relationship
between the temperature
T^{1}
and the proton conductivity
\sigma_{p}^{1}
perturbations:

\sigma_{p}^{1}=\sigma_{p}^{0}\dfrac{T_{\sigma}T^{1}}{\left(T^{0}\right)^{2}},\quad\sigma_{p}^{0}\equiv\sigma_{p}(T^{0})

(4)

Here,
T^{0}
is the steady-state cell temperature, which is assumed to
be uniform across the cathode side. Small non-uniformity in
T^{0}

does not play any role in the present analysis.
Evidently, due to the linearity of Eq.(
4
) in
T^{1}
, it holds
for the perturbation amplitudes
\sigma_{p}^{1}(\omega)
and
T^{1}(\omega)
in the frequency domain.

Setting in Eq.(
1
)
\eta=\eta^{0}(x)+\eta^{1}(x,t)
,
\sigma_{p}=\sigma_{p}^{0}+\sigma_{p}^{1}
,
expanding exponent in a Taylor series, neglecting the term with the perturbation products,
and subtracting the static equation for
\eta^{0}

2

C_{dl}\dfrac{\partial{\eta^{1}}}{\partial{t}}-\sigma_{p}^{1}\dfrac{\partial^{2}{\eta^{0}}}{\partial{x}^{2}}-\sigma_{p}^{0}\dfrac{\partial^{2}{\eta^{1}}}{\partial{x}^{2}}=-i_{*}\left(\dfrac{c_{1}}{c_{ref}}\right){\rm e}^{\eta_{0}/b}\dfrac{\eta^{1}}{b}

(5)

Substituting here the Fourier-transform

\eta^{1}(x,t)=\eta^{1}(x,\omega)\exp({\rm i}\omega t)
,
we obtain
the equation for the perturbation amplitude
\eta^{1}(x,\omega)

C_{dl}{\rm i}\omega\eta^{1}-\sigma_{p}^{1}\dfrac{\partial^{2}{\eta^{0}}}{\partial{x}^{2}}-\sigma_{p}^{0}\dfrac{\partial^{2}{\eta^{1}}}{\partial{x}^{2}}=-i_{*}\left(\dfrac{c_{1}}{c_{ref}}\right){\rm e}^{\eta_{0}/b}\dfrac{\eta^{1}}{b}

2

{\partial^{2}{\eta^{0}}}/{\partial{x}^{2}}
,

4

\sigma_{p}^{0}\dfrac{\partial^{2}{\eta^{1}}}{\partial{x}^{2}}=C_{dl}{\rm i}\omega\eta^{1}+\left(\dfrac{\eta^{1}}{b}-\dfrac{T_{\sigma}T^{1}}{\left(T^{0}\right)^{2}}\right)i_{*}\left(\dfrac{c_{1}}{c_{ref}}\right){\rm e}^{\eta_{0}/b}

(7)

\left(-\sigma_{p}^{0}\dfrac{\partial{\eta^{1}}}{\partial{x}}-\sigma_{p}^{1}\dfrac{\partial{\eta^{0}}}{\partial{x}}\right)_{x=0}=j^{1},\quad\left.\dfrac{\partial{\eta^{1}}}{\partial{x}}\right|_{x=l_{t}}=0

where

4

To simplify further calculations, we introduce the dimensionless variables

III

Results and discussion

Let the cell current density be small. In that case, we can neglect the variation of the ORR
overpotential through the CCL depth and set
\tilde{\eta}^{0}=\tilde{\eta}_{0}
, where

\tilde{\eta}_{0}
is the overpotential at the membrane surface (at
\tilde{x}=0
).
The subscripts 0 and 1 denote the membrane/CCL and CCL/GDL interfaces, respectively.
The dimensionless Tafel law

\varepsilon^{2}\tilde{j}_{0}=\tilde{c}_{1}{\rm e}^{\tilde{\eta}_{0}}

(15)

allows us to replace
\tilde{c}_{1}{\rm e}^{\tilde{\eta}_{0}}
in Eq.(
12
) with
\varepsilon^{2}\tilde{j}_{0}
.
The solution to Eq.(
12

13
) is

\tilde{\eta}^{1}(\tilde{x})=\dfrac{\Lambda^{1}\tilde{j}^{0}-\tilde{j}^{1}}{\phi}\bigl(\sinh(\phi\tilde{x})-\coth(\phi)\cosh(\phi\tilde{x})\bigr)\\
+\dfrac{\tilde{j}_{0}\Lambda^{1}}{\phi^{2}},\quad\phi=\sqrt{\tilde{j}_{0}+{\rm i}\tilde{\omega}/\varepsilon^{2}}

(16)

Eq.(
16
) gives the system impedance
\widetilde{Z}=\tilde{\eta}^{1}/\tilde{j}^{1}|_{\tilde{x}=0}
:

\widetilde{Z}=(1-\kappa)\dfrac{\coth\sqrt{\tilde{j}_{0}+{\rm i}\tilde{\omega}/\varepsilon^{2}}}{\sqrt{\tilde{j}_{0}+{\rm i}\tilde{\omega}/\varepsilon^{2}}}+\dfrac{\kappa}{\tilde{j}_{0}+{\rm i}\tilde{\omega}/\varepsilon^{2}}

(17)

where
\kappa
is the temperature control parameter:

\kappa=\dfrac{\Lambda^{1}\tilde{j}_{0}}{\tilde{j}^{1}}

(18)

Since we can choose the values of
\tilde{j}^{1}
and
\Lambda^{1}
,
the control parameter
\kappa
can always be taken
in the range

studied in this work.

At zero temperature perturbation,

,
\kappa=0
,
and Eq.(

\widetilde{Z}_{fp}=\dfrac{\coth\sqrt{\tilde{j}_{0}+{\rm i}\tilde{\omega}/\varepsilon^{2}}}{\sqrt{\tilde{j}_{0}+{\rm i}\tilde{\omega}/\varepsilon^{2}}}

Now consider the case of non-zero temperature perturbation,
\kappa>0
.
For a moment, we leave aside the question of if and how the high-frequency temperature
perturbation could be produced.

The Nyquist plots of the impedance
Z
, Eq.(
17
),
for
\kappa
of 2/3 and 1 are shown in Figure
2
a. As can be seen,
the positive
\kappa
lowers the static cell resistivity.
This effect is best seen with
\kappa=1
. In that case, Eq.(
17
) reduces to

\widetilde{Z}_{RC}=\dfrac{1}{\tilde{j}_{0}+{\rm i}\tilde{\omega}/\varepsilon^{2}},

(20)

which is the parallel
RC
-circuit impedance. The static resistivity
\widetilde{R}_{RC}

of Eq.(
20
) is independent of the proton conductivity:

R_{RC}=\dfrac{b}{j_{0}}

(21)

With the parameters in Table
1
,
R_{RC}=150
mOhm cm
2
, which
is noticeably smaller than 180 mOhm cm
2
at
\kappa=0

(Figure
2
a). Therefore, temperature oscillations in phase with
the oscillations of the cell current density can reduce or even
eliminate the proton transport losses in the CCL.

This effect is analogous to parametric resonance in mechanical systems.
The analysis above is performed under the assumption that
\kappa
is real and positive,
i.e.,
\widetilde{T}^{1}
is in phase with
\tilde{j}^{1}
, Eq.(
18
).
According to Eq.(
4
), the resulting proton conductivity oscillations
are also in phase with the
j^{1}
oscillations. Writing Ohm’s law in the form

-\sigma_{p}^{0}\dfrac{\partial{\eta^{1}}}{\partial{x}}=j^{1}+\sigma_{p}^{1}\dfrac{\partial{\eta^{0}}}{\partial{x}}=j^{1}-\dfrac{\sigma_{p}^{1}}{\sigma_{p}^{0}}j_{0}

(22)

we see that the conductivity oscillations
\sigma_{p}^{1}
reduce
the amplitude of
{\partial{\eta^{1}}}/{\partial{x}}
oscillations. Furthermore,
when
\sigma_{p}^{1}j_{0}/\sigma_{p}^{0}=j^{1}
, we get
{\partial{\eta^{1}}}/{\partial{x}}=0
.
A uniform along
\tilde{x}
overpotential perturbation
\tilde{\eta}^{1}

means zero proton transport losses in the CCL.
The in-phase oscillations of
\sigma_{p}^{1}
and
\tilde{j}^{1}

justify the analogy with parametric resonance.

The most striking feature of the curves in Figures
2

3

the
static
cell resistivity (the rightmost
point of the Nyquist spectrum) decreases as
k

\omega

which, from the practical perspective, does not differ much from

Due to the inertia of heat transport through the FF and GDL,
it is hardly feasible to produce high-frequency
T_{\rm CCL}^{1}\equiv T^{1}
oscillations
in experiment. However, Figures
2
and
3
show
a reduction in cell resistivity at low frequencies. The low-frequency
T^{1}

perturbation can be arranged by means of a temperature controller mounted
on the external side of the FF (Figure
1
). The AC signal of the controller should
account for the phase shift due to heat transport through the FF and GDL.
The experimental study of the effect can be performed in the frequency range below 0.1 Hz.

The reduction of the cell resistivity upon simultaneous application of potential
and oxygen concentration perturbations to the PEM fuel cell
has been demonstrated in modeling works
Kulikovsky 2024a
;
Kulikovsky 2024b
.
Here, we see that the proper temperature control eliminates the proton transport losses
in the CCL. In both cases, operating the cell in an EIS-like regime
is promising from a practical perspective.
