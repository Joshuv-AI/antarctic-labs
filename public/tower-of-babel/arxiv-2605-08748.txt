Abstract
. In this paper, we employ the theory of normal families in several complex variables to obtain some uniqueness theorems for entire functions. These results extend the related works of Li and Yi [ 11 ] , and Lü et al. [ 18 ] to the setting of several complex variables. Moreover, some examples are provided to demonstrate the sharpness of our results.

1.
Introduction and main results

The study of entire functions that share values with their partial derivatives is a classical and active topic in complex analysis, particularly within Nevanlinna theory. When an entire function partially shares two values with its partial derivatives, the behavior of the function becomes significantly restricted. Such restrictions often lead to strong uniqueness results or explicit characterizations of the entire function.

Entire functions sharing values with their partial derivatives arise naturally in the study of differential equations, normal families, and uniqueness theory. Investigating partial sharing such as IM-sharing, CM-sharing, or sharing with reduced multiplicity-extends classical uniqueness theorems while capturing more subtle analytical phenomena. These generalizations provide deeper insight into the structural properties of entire functions and their derivatives in several complex variables.

Let
f(z)
and
g(z)
be two non-constant meromorphic functions and
a
be a finite complex number. If
g(z)-a=0
whenever
f(z)-a=0
, we write
f(z)=a\Rightarrow g(z)=a
. If
f(z)=a\Rightarrow g(z)=a
and
g(z)=a\Rightarrow f(z)=a
, we then write
f(z)=a\Leftrightarrow g(z)=a
and we say that
f(z)
and
g(z)
share
a
IM. If
f(z)-a
and
g(z)-a
have the same zeros with the same multiplicities, we write
f(z)=a\rightleftharpoons g(z)=a
and we say that
f(z)
and
g(z)
share
a
CM.

In 1977, Rubel and Yang
[
25
]
were the first to investigate the uniqueness problem for an entire function
f(z)
that shares two values with its first derivative
f^{(1)}(z)
. They established the following result.

Theorem A
.

[
25
]
Let
f(z)
be a non-constant entire function in
\mathbb{C}
and let
a
and
b
be two distinct finite complex numbers. If
f(z)=a\rightleftharpoons f^{(1)}(z)=a
and
f(z)=b\rightleftharpoons f^{(1)}(z)=b
, then
f(z)\equiv f^{(1)}(z)
, i.e.,
f(z)=A\exp(z)
, where
A\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
.

In 1979, Mues and Steinmetz
[
23
]
further generalized Theorem A by weakening the sharing condition from CM to IM, as stated in the following result.

Theorem B
.

[
23
, Satz 1]
Let
f(z)
be a non-constant entire function in
\mathbb{C}
and let
a
and
b
be two distinct finite complex numbers. If
f(z)=a\Leftrightarrow f^{(1)}(z)=a
and
f(z)=b\Leftrightarrow f^{(1)}(z)=b
, then
f(z)\equiv f^{(1)}(z)
, i.e.,
f(z)=A\exp(z)
, where
A\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
.

These two results ushered in a new era in the study of uniqueness problems for entire and meromorphic functions sharing two values with their derivatives, and they initiated a long-standing line of research in this field.

Essentially, Theorems A and B have been generalized in two main directions. The first direction replaces the sharing condition
f(z)=a\rightleftharpoons f^{(1)}(z)=a
with the weaker requirement
f(z)=a\Rightarrow f^{(1)}(z)=a
. The second direction reverses the implication by replacing
f(z)=a\rightleftharpoons f^{(1)}(z)=a
with
f^{(1)}(z)=a\Rightarrow f(z)=a.

In this paper, we refer to the following result, due to Li and Yi
[
11
]
, in which the original sharing condition
f(z)=b\rightleftharpoons f^{(1)}(z)=b
is weakened using the concept of partial value sharing, namely
f(z)=a\Rightarrow f^{(1)}(z)=a
and
f^{(1)}(z)=a\Rightarrow f(z)=a
respectively.

Theorem C
.

[
11
, Theorem 1]
Let
f(z)
be a non-constant entire function in
\mathbb{C}
and let
a
and
b
be two finite complex numbers such that
b\not=a,0
. If
f^{(1)}(z)=a\Rightarrow f(z)=a
and
f^{(1)}(z)=b\rightleftharpoons f(z)=b
, then
f(z)\equiv f^{(1)}(z)
, i.e.,
f(z)=A\exp(z)
, where
A\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
.

Theorem D
.

[
11
, Theorem 2]
Let
f(z)
be a non-constant entire function in
\mathbb{C}
and let
a
and
b
be two finite complex numbers such that
b\not=a,0
. If
f(z)=a\Rightarrow f^{(1)}(z)=a
and
f(z)=b\rightleftharpoons f^{(1)}(z)=b
, then one of the following cases must occur.

(1)

f(z)\equiv f^{(1)}(z)
,

(2)

f(z)=c\exp(\frac{b}{b-a}z)+a
, where
c\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
.

Remark A
.

We observe that
b\not=a
in Theorem C. Therefore, if we assume
b\not=0
in Theorem A, then it is clear that Theorem C generalizes Theorem A through the notion of “partial” sharing of values.

It would be a remarkable achievement to show that the conclusion of Theorem C remains valid when the hypothesis
f(z)=b\rightleftharpoons f^{(1)}(z)=b
is replaced by the weaker condition
f(z)=b\Leftrightarrow f^{(1)}(z)=b
.
In 2009, Lü et al.
[
18
]
accomplished this by establishing the following result.

Theorem E
.

[
18
, Theorem 1.1]
Let
f(z)
be a non-constant entire function in
\mathbb{C}
and let
a
and
b
be two non-zero finite complex numbers such that
b\not=a
. If
f^{(1)}(z)=a\Rightarrow f(z)=a
and
f(z)=b\Leftrightarrow f^{(1)}(z)=b
, then
f(z)\equiv f^{(1)}(z)
, i.e.,
f(z)=A\exp(z)
, where
A\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
.

In one complex variable, many uniqueness theorems for meromorphic functions sharing values with their derivatives follow directly from classical Nevanlinna theory. However, when we move to several complex variables, the situation becomes more delicate because:

\bullet
Derivatives become partial or directional derivatives rather than a single derivative
f^{(1)}
.

\bullet
The classical Nevanlinna theory must be replaced by its higher-dimensional generalizations for meromorphic functions or mappings in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

\bullet
Additional geometric conditions (such as directional derivatives, growth conditions, or linearly nondegenerate mappings) are often required.

In 1995, Berenstein et al.
[
2
]
obtained a Nevanlinna type uniqueness result for non-constant meromorphic functions in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and their directional derivatives in terms of shared values. They proved: Let
f
be a non-constant meromorphic function in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. If
f
and the directional derivative
\partial_{u}(f)
of
f
along a direction
u\in S^{2n-1}
share three distinct values in
\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
counting multiplicities, then
f\equiv\partial_{u}(f)
, where

\displaystyle S^{2n-1}

\displaystyle=\{(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{n})\in C^{n}:|u_{1}|^{2}+|u_{2}|^{2}+\cdots+|u_{n}|^{2}=1\}

\displaystyle\partial_{u}(f)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}u_{j}\frac{\partial f}{\partial z_{j}},\;\;u=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}).

This result was extended to moving targets in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
by Hu and Yang
[
8
]
in 1996.

We define
\mathbb{Z}_{+}=\mathbb{Z}[0,+\infty)=\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:0\leq n<+\infty\}
and
\mathbb{Z}^{+}=\mathbb{Z}(0,+\infty)=\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:0<n<+\infty\}
.
On
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, we define the following operators

\partial_{z_{i}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{i}},\partial^{2}_{z_{j}z_{i}}=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z_{j}z_{i}},\ldots,\partial_{z_{i}}^{l_{i}}=\frac{\partial^{l_{i}}}{\partial z_{i}^{l_{i}}}\;\;\text{and}\;\;\partial^{I}=\frac{\partial^{|I|}}{\partial z_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots\partial z_{m}^{i_{m}}}

where
l_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\;(i=1,2,\ldots,n)
and
I=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{+}
is a multi-index such that
|I|=\sum_{j=1}^{n}i_{j}
.

Theorems C-E lead to the following natural question:

Question 1.1
.

Do Theorems C-E remain valid in the setting of several complex variables with respect to partial derivative operators?

We respond to Question 1.1 in the paper with the following outcome.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
f(z)
be a transcendental function in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and let
a
and
b
be two finite complex numbers in
\mathbb{C}
such that
b\not=a,0
. If

(i)

f=a\Rightarrow\partial_{z_{i}}(f)=a
for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
,

(ii)

f=b\Leftrightarrow\partial_{z_{i}}(f)=b
for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
,

then one of the following cases must occur:

(1)

f(z)=ce^{z_{1}+z_{2}+\ldots+z_{n}}
, where
c
is a non-zero constant in
\mathbb{C}
,

(2)

f(z)=a+ce^{\frac{b}{b-a}(z_{1}+z_{2}+\ldots+z_{n})}
, where
c
is a non-zero constant in
\mathbb{C}
,

(3)

f(z)=b+ce^{\frac{a}{b-a}(z_{1}+z_{2}+\ldots+z_{n})}
, where
a\neq 0
and
c
is a non-zero constant in
\mathbb{C}
.

Remark 1.1
.

The following example demonstrates that, for the validity of Theorem
1.1
, it is necessary that the conditions
(i)
and
(ii)
hold for every
i=1,2,\ldots,n
.

Example 1.1
.

Let
f(z)=a+ce^{\frac{b}{b-a}(z_{1}+z_{2}^{2}+\ldots+z_{n}^{2})}
, where
a
,
b
and
c
are non-zero constants in
\mathbb{C}
such that
a\neq b
. It is easy to verify that the conditions
f=a\Rightarrow\partial_{z_{1}}(f)=a
and
f=b\Leftrightarrow\partial_{z_{1}}(f)=b
both hold, but the condition
f=b\Leftrightarrow\partial_{z_{i}}(f)=b
does not hold for
i=2,3,\ldots,n
.

Now we state the multidimensional version of Theorem E.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
f(z)
be a transcendental function in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and let
a
and
b
be two finite non-zero complex numbers in
\mathbb{C}
such that
a\not=b
. If

(i)

\partial_{z_{i}}(f)=a\Rightarrow f=a
for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
,

(ii)

f=b\Leftrightarrow\partial_{z_{i}}(f)=b
for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
,

then
f(z)=ce^{z_{1}+z_{2}+\ldots+z_{n}}
, where
c
is a non-zero constant in
\mathbb{C}
.

Next we state the multidimensional version of Theorem C.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
f(z)
be a transcendental function in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and let
b
be a finite non-zero complex numbers in
\mathbb{C}
. If

(i)

\partial_{z_{i}}(f)=0\Rightarrow f=0
for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
,

(ii)

f=b\rightleftharpoons\partial_{z_{i}}(f)=b
for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
,

then one of the following cases must occur:

(1)

f(z)=ce^{z_{1}+z_{2}+\ldots+z_{n}}
, where
c
is a non-zero constant in
\mathbb{C}
,

(2)

f(z)=\frac{c}{A}e^{A(z_{1}+z_{2}+\ldots+z_{n})}+b-\frac{b}{A}
, where
A
and
c
are non-zero constants in
\mathbb{C}
.

Remark 1.2
.

The following example shows that the number of shared values cannot be reduced to one in Theorem
1.1
.

Example 1.2
.

Let

\displaystyle f(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})=e^{e^{z_{1}+\cdots+z_{n}}}\int_{0}^{z_{1}+\cdots+z_{n}}e^{-e^{t}}(1-e^{t})dt.

Note that for all
i=1,2,\ldots,n
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{z_{i}}(f(z))=e^{z_{1}+\cdots+z_{m}}(f(z)-1)+1

and so for all
i=1,2,\ldots,n
, we get

\displaystyle\partial_{z_{i}}(f(z))-1=e^{z_{1}+\cdots+z_{n}}(f(z)-1).

Clearly
f(z)=1\rightleftharpoons\partial_{z_{i}}(f(z))=1
for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
, but
f(z)
does not satisfy any case of Theorem
1.1
.

Remark 1.3
.

Following example shows that Theorem
1.1
does not hold in general for a non-constant meromorphic function
f(z)
in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

Example 1.3
.

Let

f(z)=\frac{z_{1}+z_{2}+\ldots+z_{n}}{1-e^{-(z_{1}+z_{2}+\ldots+z_{n})}},

a=0
and
b=1
. Clearly

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f(z)}{\partial z_{i}}=\frac{1-(z_{1}+z_{2}+\ldots+z_{n}+1)e^{-(z_{1}+z_{2}+\ldots+z_{n})}}{\left(1-e^{-(z_{1}+z_{2}+\ldots+z_{n})}\right)^{2}}

and so
f(z)=a\Rightarrow\partial_{z_{i}}(f(z))=a
for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
. Note that

\displaystyle f(z)-b=\frac{z_{1}+z_{2}+\ldots+z_{n}-1+e^{-(z_{1}+z_{2}+\ldots+z_{n})}}{1-e^{-(z_{1}+z_{2}+\ldots+z_{n})}}

and

\displaystyle\partial_{z_{i}}(f(z))-b=-e^{-(z_{1}+z_{2}+\ldots+z_{n})}\;\;\frac{z_{1}+z_{2}+\ldots+z_{n}-1+e^{-(z_{1}+z_{2}+\ldots+z_{n})}}{(1-e^{-(z_{1}+z_{2}+\ldots+z_{n})})^{2}}.

Clearly
f(z)=b\rightleftharpoons\partial_{z_{i}}(f(z))=b
for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
, but
f(z)
does not satisfy any case of Theorem
1.1
.

Basic Notations in several complex variables

Nevanlinna theory, originally formulated for meromorphic functions of a single complex variable, has been extended to the setting of several complex variables to study the value distribution of meromorphic maps from
\mathbb{C}^{n}
into complex projective spaces or complex manifolds. It provides profound insights into:
(a)
The growth and value distribution of meromorphic mappings;
(b)
Uniqueness and rigidity phenomena for solutions of linear and nonlinear PDEs and
(c)
Criteria for compactness and convergence in families of meromorphic maps. Its applications span complex analysis, partial differential equations, complex geometry, and mathematical physics, offering both powerful techniques and elegant results. These references

Let
G\neq\varnothing

\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Let

be a holomorphic function in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
Take

. Let

be the connectivity component of

containing
a
. Assume

. Then a series

f(z)=\sum_{\lambda=p}^{\infty}P_{\lambda}(z-a)
converges on some neighborhood of
a
and represents
f
on this neighborhood. Here

P_{\lambda}
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
\lambda
and
P_{p}\not\equiv 0
. The polynomials
P_{\lambda}
depend on

and

only. The number
\mu^{0}_{f}(a)=p
is called the zero multiplicity of
f
at
a
(see
[
27
, pp. 12]
).

Let
f
be a meromorphic function on
G
.
Take
a\in G
and

. Let
G_{a}
be the component of
G
containing
a
. If

\mu^{c}_{f}(a)=0

U

in
G

and

h.f\mid_{U}=g

\dim g^{-1}(0)\cap h^{-1}(0)\leq n-2

. Therefore the

If

\displaystyle\operatorname{supp}\;\nu=\overline{\{z\in G:\nu(z)\neq 0\}}

If
A\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}

\upsilon=dd^{c}\tau>0

\displaystyle\omega=dd^{c}\log\tau\geq 0

For

\displaystyle n_{\nu}(t)=t^{-2(n-1)}\int_{A[t]}\nu\upsilon^{n-1},

where

N_{\nu}(r)=N_{\nu}(r,r_{0})=\int_{r_{0}}^{r}n_{\nu}(t)\frac{dt}{t}\;\;(r\geq r_{0}).

With the help of the positive logarithm function, we define the proximity function of

\displaystyle m(r,f)=\mathbb{C}^{n}\langle r;\log^{+}|f|\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{n}\langle r\rangle}\log^{+}|f|\;\sigma.

T(r,f)=m(r,f)+N(r,f)

Auxiliary Lemmas

For every function
\varphi

C^{2}(\Omega)

\displaystyle L_{z}(\varphi,\nu)=\sideset{}{{}_{l,k=1}^{n}}{\sum}\frac{\partial^{2}\varphi(z)}{\partial z_{k}\partial\overline{z}_{l}}\nu_{k}\overline{\nu}_{l},

which is called the Levi form of the function
\varphi
. For a holomorphic function
f
in
\Omega

\displaystyle f^{\#}(z)=\max_{||\nu||=1}\sqrt{L_{z}(\log(1+|f(z)|^{2}),\nu)}

where
\nu=(\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{n})

L_{z}(\log(1+|f(z)|^{2}),\nu)

z\in\Omega

{\bf\nabla}f=(f_{z_{1}},\ldots,f_{z_{n}})

Applying Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, it is easy to prove that (see

\displaystyle f^{\#}(z)=\sup_{||\nu||=1}\frac{|\langle{\bf\nabla}f(z),\nu\rangle|}{1+|f(z)|^{2}}=\frac{||{\bf\nabla}f(z)||}{1+|f(z)|^{2}}=\frac{\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{n}|f_{z_{j}}(z)|^{2}}}{1+|f(z)|^{2}},\;\;z\in\Omega,

where
\langle z,w\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}z_{j}\overline{w}_{j}
is the Hermitian scalar product for
z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

A family
\mathcal{F}
of holomorphic functions on a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is normal in
\Omega
if every sequence of functions
\{f_{j}\}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
contains either a subsequence which converges to a limit function
f\neq\infty
uniformly on each compact subset of
\Omega
, or a subsequence which converges uniformly to
\infty
on each compact subset.

A family
\mathcal{F}
is said to be normal at a point

if it is normal in some neighbourhood of
z_{0}
. A family of analytic functions
\mathcal{F}
is normal in a domain
\Omega
if and only if
\mathcal{F}
is normal at each point of
\Omega
.

We recall the Marty’s characterization of normal families in terms of the spherical metric.

Lemma 3.1
.

[
5

A family

f^{\#}(z)\leq M(K)

\forall

Lemma 3.2

[

Suppose that a family

f
. Let
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a zero of
f-b
. Since
f=b\Leftrightarrow\partial_{z_{i}}(f)=b
, we have
f(z_{0})=a+e^{P(z_{0})}=b
and
\partial_{z_{i}}(f(z_{0}))=\partial_{z_{i}}(P(z_{0}))e^{P(z_{0})}=b
for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
. Now eliminating
e^{P(z_{0})}
, we get
\partial_{z_{i}}(P)=\frac{b}{b-a}
for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle f(z)=a+ce^{\frac{b}{b-a}(z_{1}+z_{2}+\ldots+z_{n})},\;\;c\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}.

Case 2.
Let
b
be a Picard exceptional value of
f
. Clearly
a
is not a Picard exceptional values of
f
. Now by Lemma
3.5
, we may assume that

\displaystyle f(z)-b=e^{P(z)},

where
P(z)
is a polynomial in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that
\deg(P)=1
. In this case also, one can easily obtain
\partial_{z_{i}}(P)=\frac{a}{a-b}
for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
. Obviously
a\neq 0
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle f(z)=b+ce^{\frac{a}{b-a}(z_{1}+z_{2}+\ldots+z_{n})},\;\;c\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}.

Case 3.
Let both
a
and
b
be not Picard exceptional values of
f
.
Set

\displaystyle\Phi_{i}(z)=\frac{(f(z)-\partial_{z_{i}}(f(z)))\partial_{z_{i}}(f(z))}{(f(z)-a)(f(z)-b)},

(4.1)

where
i=1,2,\ldots,n
and
z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
We now consider the following two sub-cases.

Sub-case 3.1.
Suppose
\Phi_{i}\not\equiv 0
for atleast one
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. For the sake of simplicity we may assume that
\Phi_{k}\not\equiv 0
. Then
f\not\equiv\partial_{z_{k}}(f)
. Since
f=a\Rightarrow\partial_{z_{k}}(f)=a
and
f=b\Leftrightarrow\partial_{z_{k}}(f)=b
, one can easily prove that
\Phi_{k}
is a holomorphic function in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
Now from (
4.1
), we have

\displaystyle\Phi_{k}(z)=\frac{1}{a-b}\left(a\frac{\partial_{z_{k}}(f(z))}{f(z)-a}-b\frac{\partial_{z_{k}}(f(z))}{f(z)-b}\right)\left(1-\frac{\partial_{z_{k}}(f(z))}{f(z)}\right).

(4.2)

Therefore using Lemma
3.9
to (
4.2
), we get
m(r,\Phi_{k})=o(\log r)
as
r\to\infty
. Since
\Phi_{k}(z)
is a holomorphic function in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, we get
T(r,\Phi_{k})=o(\log r)
as
r\to\infty
, which shows that
\Phi_{k}(z)
is a non-zero constant, say
c_{k}\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
. Therefore from (
4.1
), we get

\displaystyle(f(z)-\partial_{z_{k}}(f(z)))\partial_{z_{k}}(f(z))\equiv c_{k}(f(z)-a)(f(z)-b).

(4.3)

Following two sub-cases are immediately.

Sub-case 3.1.1.
Let
a\neq 0
. Since
f=a\Rightarrow\partial_{z_{k}}(f)=a
and
f=b\Leftrightarrow\partial_{z_{k}}(f)=b
, it follows that both
f-a
and
f-b
have only simple zeros. Consequently from (
4.1
), we deduce that
\partial_{z_{k}}(f)
has no zeros. Note that
\rho(\partial_{z_{k}}(f))\leq 1
and so we may take

\displaystyle\partial_{z_{k}}(f(z))=e^{Q(z)},

(4.4)

where
Q(z)
is a polynomial in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that
\deg(Q)=1
. Therefore it is easy to prove that
\partial_{z_{k}}(f)-b
has only simple zeros and so
f=b\rightleftharpoons\partial_{z_{k}}(f)=b
. Now by Lemma
3.12
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{z_{k}}(f)-b=A_{k}(f-b)

(4.5)

for some non-zero constant
A_{k}
in
\mathbb{C}
. Note that
a
is not a Picard exceptional value of
f
. Let
z_{0}
be a zero of
f-a
. Since
f=a\Rightarrow\partial_{z_{k}}(f)=a
, we have
\partial_{z_{k}}(f(z_{0}))=a
. Then from (
4.5
), we get
a-b=A_{k}(a-b)
. Since
a\neq b
, we have
A_{k}=1
and so (
4.5
) yields
f\equiv\partial_{z_{k}}(f)
, which is impossible.

Sub-case 3.1.2.
Let
a=0
. Now from (
4.3
), we have

\displaystyle(f(z)-\partial_{z_{k}}(f(z)))\partial_{z_{k}}(f(z))\equiv c_{k}f(z)(f(z)-b).

(4.6)

Let
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a simple zero of
f
. Since
f=0\Rightarrow\partial_{z_{k}}(f)=0
, it follows that
z_{0}
is a zero of
\partial_{z_{k}}(f)
. Then from (
4.6
), we immediately get a contradiction. Hence
f
has no simple zero.
Again by a routine calculation, one can easily conclude from (
4.6
) that all the zeros of
f(z)
have multiplicity exactly
2
. Consequently, we may take
f=h^{2}
, where
h
is a holomorphic function in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that
h
has only simple zeros. Note that
\partial_{z_{k}}(f)=2h\partial_{z_{k}}(h)
. In this case, we have

h^{2}=0\Rightarrow 2h\partial_{z_{k}}(h)=0
and
h^{2}=b\Leftrightarrow 2h\partial_{z_{k}}(h)=b
. Also from (
4.6
), we get

\displaystyle 2(h(z)-2\partial_{z_{k}}(h(z)))\partial_{z_{k}}(h(z))\equiv c_{k}(h^{2}(z)-b)

(4.7)

for all
z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Now from (
4.7
), we deduce that
\partial_{z_{k}}(h)
has no zeros. Note that
\rho(\partial_{u}(h))\leq 1
and so we may take
\partial_{z_{k}}(h(z))=e^{R(z)}
, where
R(z)
is a polynomial in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that
\deg(R)=1
. Again from (
4.7
), we get

\displaystyle 2h(z)e^{R(z)}-4e^{2R(z)}\equiv c_{k}(h^{2}(z)-b)

and so

\displaystyle(\partial_{z_{k}}(R(z))-c_{k})h(z)\equiv(4\partial_{z_{k}}(R(z))-1)e^{R(z)}.

(4.8)

Since
0
is not a Picard exception value of
h
, one can easily conclude from (
4.8
) that
\partial_{z_{k}}(R(z))-c_{k}=0
and
4\partial_{z_{k}}(R(z))-1=0
. Consequently, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{z_{k}}(R(z))=c_{k}=\frac{1}{4}

and so from (
4.7
), we have

\displaystyle(h(z)-4\partial_{z_{k}}(h(z)))^{2}\equiv b.

Set
h(z)-4\partial_{z_{k}}(h(z))=d
, where
d
is a root of the equation
x^{2}=b
. Since
f=h^{2}
and
\partial_{z_{k}}(h(z))=e^{R(z)}
, we deduce that

\displaystyle f(z)=16e^{2R(z)}+8de^{R(z)}+b.

Clearly

\displaystyle f(z)-b=16e^{2R(z)}+8de^{R(z)}=8(2e^{R(z)}+d)e^{R(z)}

(4.9)

and

\displaystyle\partial_{z_{k}}(f(z))-b=8e^{2R(z)}+2de^{R(z)}-d^{2}.

(4.10)

Since
f=b\Leftrightarrow\partial_{z_{k}}(f)=b
, from (
4.9
) and (
4.10
), we immediately get a contradiction.

Sub-case 3.2.
Let
\Phi_{i}\equiv 0
for all
i=1,2,\ldots,n
. Since
\partial_{z_{i}}(f)\not\equiv 0
, it follows from (
4.1
) that

\displaystyle f(z)\equiv\partial_{z_{i}}(f(z))

(4.11)

for all
i=1,2,\ldots,n
. Then from (
4.11
), we conclude that both
f
and
\partial_{z_{i}}(f)
have no zeros for all
i=1,2,\ldots,n
. If we take
f=e^{\zeta},
where
\zeta
is a non-constant entire function in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, then from (
4.11
), we get
\partial_{z_{i}}(\zeta)=1
for all
i=1,2,\ldots,n
.
By Taylor expansion, we have

\displaystyle\zeta(z)=\sum\limits_{\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n}=0}^{\infty}b_{\mu_{1}\ldots\mu_{n}}z_{1}^{\mu_{1}}\ldots z_{n}^{\mu_{n}},

(4.12)

where the coefficient
b_{\mu_{1}\ldots\mu_{n}}
is given by

\displaystyle b_{\mu_{1}\ldots\mu_{n}}=\frac{1}{\mu_{1}!\ldots\mu_{n}!}\frac{\partial^{|I|}\zeta(0,0,\ldots,0)}{\partial z_{1}^{\mu_{1}}\cdots\partial z_{n}^{\mu_{n}}}.

(4.13)

Now from (
4.12
) and (
4.13
), we have
\zeta(z)=z_{1}+z_{2}+\ldots+z_{n}+A_{0}
, where
A_{0}=\zeta(0,0,\ldots,0)
. Therefore

\displaystyle f(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})=ce^{z_{1}+z_{2}+\ldots+z_{n}},\;\;c=\exp(A_{0}).

∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

By the given conditions, we have
\partial_{z_{i}}(f)=a\Rightarrow f=a
and
f=b\Leftrightarrow\partial_{z_{i}}(f)=b
for all
i=1,2,\ldots,n
. Set
\mathcal{F}=\{f_{\omega}\}
, where
f_{\omega}(z)=f(\omega+z)
,
\omega\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Clearly
\mathcal{F}
is a family of holomorphic functions defined on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. By assumption for any function
g(z)=f(\omega+z)
, we have
\partial_{z_{i}}(g)=a\Rightarrow g=a
and
g=b\Leftrightarrow\partial_{z_{i}}(g)=b
for all
i=1,2,\ldots,n
. Since
\mathcal{F}
is a family of holomorphic functions in
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
, then by Lemma
3.6
, we conclude that
\mathcal{F}
is normal on
\Omega
. Now by Lemma
3.1
, we have
f^{\#}(\omega)=f^{\#}_{\omega}(0)\leq M
for some
M>0
and for all
\omega\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Therefore Lemma
3.4
gives us
\rho(f)\leq 1
. We put

\displaystyle\varphi_{ji}=\frac{\partial_{z_{j}z_{i}}^{2}(f)(\partial_{z_{i}}(f)-f)}{(f-b)(\partial_{z_{i}}(f)-a)}.

(4.14)

Now we consider the following two cases.

Case 1.
Suppose
\varphi_{st}\not\equiv 0
, where
s,t\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. Then
f\not\equiv\partial_{z_{t}}(f)
and
\partial_{z_{s}z_{t}}^{2}(f)\not\equiv 0
. Since
\partial_{z_{t}}(f)=a\Rightarrow f=a
and
f=b\Leftrightarrow\partial_{z_{t}}(f)=b
, one can easily prove that
\varphi_{st}
is a holomorphic function in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
Now from (
4.14
), we have

\displaystyle\varphi_{st}=\frac{\partial_{z_{t}}(f)}{f-b}\frac{\partial_{z_{s}z_{t}}^{2}(f)}{\partial_{z_{t}}(f)-a}-\frac{\partial_{z_{s}z_{t}}^{2}(f)}{\partial_{z_{t}}(f)-a}-b\frac{\partial_{z_{t}}(f)}{f-b}\frac{\partial_{z_{s}z_{t}}^{2}(f)}{(\partial_{z_{t}}(f)-a)\partial_{z_{t}}(f)}.

(4.15)

Therefore using Lemma
3.9
to (
4.15
), we get

\displaystyle T(r,\varphi_{st})=m(r,\varphi_{st})

\displaystyle\leq m\left(r,\frac{\partial_{z_{t}}(f)}{f-b}\right)+m\left(r,\frac{\partial_{z_{s}z_{t}}^{2}(f)}{\partial_{z_{t}}(f)-a}\right)+m\left(r,\frac{\partial_{z_{s}z_{t}}^{2}(f)}{\partial_{z_{t}}(f)-a}\right)

\displaystyle+m\left(r,\frac{\partial_{z_{t}}(f)}{f-b}\right)+m\left(r,\frac{\partial_{z_{s}z_{t}}^{2}(f)}{(\partial_{z_{t}}(f)-a)\partial_{z_{t}}(f)}\right)+O(1)=o(\log r)

as
r\to\infty
and so
\varphi_{st}
is a constant. Suppose
\varphi_{st}=c_{st}\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
. Now from (
4.14
), we have

\displaystyle c_{st}=\frac{\partial_{z_{s}z_{t}}^{2}(f)(\partial_{z_{t}}(f)-f)}{(f-b)(\partial_{z_{t}}(f)-a)}.

(4.16)

Since
\partial_{z_{t}}(f)=a\Rightarrow f=a
and
f=b\Leftrightarrow\partial_{z_{t}}(f)=b
, it follows from (
4.16
) that
\partial_{z_{s}z_{t}}^{2}(f)
has no zeros. Consequently both
f-b
and
\partial_{z_{t}}(f)-b
have only simple zeros. Hence
f=b\rightleftharpoons\partial_{z_{k}}(f)=b
. Now by Lemma
3.12
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{z_{t}}(f)-b=A_{t}(f-b)

(4.17)

for some non-zero constant
A_{t}
.

First we suppose that
a
is not a Picard exceptional of
\partial_{z_{t}}(f)-a
. Let
z_{0}
be a zero of
\partial_{z_{t}}(f)-a
. Since
\partial_{z_{t}}(f)=a\Rightarrow f=a
, we have
f(z_{0})=a
and so from (
4.17
), we obtain
a-b=A_{t}(a-b)
. Since
a\neq b
, we get
A_{t}=1
and so (
4.17
) yields
f\equiv\partial_{z_{t}}(f)
, which is impossible.

Next we suppose that
a
is a Picard exceptional of
\partial_{z_{t}}(f)-a
. So we may take

\displaystyle\partial_{z_{t}}(f)=e^{Q_{t}}+a,

(4.18)

where
Q_{t}(z)
is a polynomial in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that
\deg(Q_{t})=1
. Now substituting (
4.18
) into (
4.17
), we deduce that

\displaystyle f=\frac{1}{A_{t}}e^{Q_{t}}+b+\frac{a-b}{A_{t}}.

(4.19)

It is clear from (
4.19
) that

\displaystyle\partial_{z_{t}}(f)=\frac{\partial_{z_{t}}(Q_{t})}{A_{t}}e^{Q_{t}}.

(4.20)

Now comparing (
4.18
) and (
4.19
), we get

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{\partial_{z_{t}}(Q_{t})}{A_{t}}\right)e^{Q_{t}}=-a.

Since
a\neq 0
, we get a contradiction from above.

Case 2.
Suppose
\varphi_{ji}\equiv 0
for all
i,j\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. Then from (
4.14
), we have either
f=\partial_{z_{i}}(f)
for all
i=1,2,\ldots,n
or
\partial^{2}_{z_{j}z_{i}}(f)\equiv 0
for all
i,j\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. If
\partial^{2}_{z_{j}z_{i}}(f)\equiv 0
for all
i,j\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
, then by Lemma
3.14
, we get a contradiction. Hence
f=\partial_{z_{i}}(f)
for all
i=1,2,\ldots,n
. In this case also, we have

\displaystyle f(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})=ce^{z_{1}+z_{2}+\ldots+z_{n}},\;\;c\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}.

∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

By the given conditions, we have
\partial_{z_{i}}(f)=0\Rightarrow f=0
and
f=b\rightleftharpoons\partial_{z_{i}}(f)=b
for all
i=1,2,\ldots,n
. Now proceeding in the same way as done in the proof of Theorem
1.2
, we can easily prove that
\rho(f)\leq 1
.
Then by Lemma
3.12
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{z_{i}}(f)-b=A_{i}(f-b),

(4.21)

where
A_{i}
is a constant in
\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
.
We now consider the following two cases.

Case 1.
Suppose
A_{i}=1
for all
i=1,2,\ldots,n
. Then from (
4.21
), we have
f\equiv\partial_{z_{i}}(f)
for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
.
In this case also, we have

\displaystyle f(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})=ce^{z_{1}+z_{2}+\ldots+z_{n}},\;\;c\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}.

Case 2.
Suppose
A_{i}\neq 1
for atleast one
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. For the sake of simplicity, we may assume that
A_{t}\neq 1
, where
t\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.

First we suppose that
0
is not a Picard exceptional of
\partial_{z_{t}}(f)
. Let
z_{0}
be a zero of
\partial_{z_{t}}(f)
. Since
\partial_{z_{t}}(f)=a\Rightarrow f
, we have
f(z_{0})=0
and so from (
4.21
), we obtain
0-b=A_{t}(0-b)
. Since
0\neq b
, we get
A_{t}=1
, which is impossible.

Next we suppose that
0
is a Picard exceptional of
\partial_{z_{t}}(f)
.
So we may take

\displaystyle\partial_{z_{t}}(f(z))=c_{t}e^{a_{t1}z_{1}+a_{t2}z_{2}+\ldots+a_{tn}z_{n}},

(4.22)

where
a_{t1},a_{t2},\ldots,a_{tn}
and
c_{t}\neq 0
are constants such that
(a_{t1},a_{2t},\ldots,a_{tn})\neq(0,0,\ldots,0)
. Now substituting (
4.22
) into (
4.21
), we deduce that

\displaystyle f(z)=\frac{c_{t}}{A_{t}}e^{a_{t1}z_{1}+a_{t2}z_{2}+\ldots+a_{tn}z_{n}}+b-\frac{b}{A_{t}}.

(4.23)

If possible, suppose
0
is not a Picard exceptional of
\partial_{z_{i}}(f)
, where
i=1,2,\ldots,n
such that
i\neq t
. Then one can easily prove that
A_{i}=1
, where
i=1,2,\ldots,n
such that
i\neq t
and so
f\equiv\partial_{z_{i}}(f)
, where
i=1,2,\ldots,n
such that
i\neq t
. In this case from (
4.23
), we get a contradiction. Hence
0
is not a Picard exceptional of
\partial_{z_{i}}(f)
for all
i=1,2,\ldots,n
. Consequently (
4.23
) holds for
t=1,2,\ldots,n
. For the uniqueness of
f(z)
, we may assume that

\displaystyle f(z)=\frac{c}{A}e^{a_{1}z_{1}+a_{2}z_{2}+\ldots+a_{n}z_{n}}+b-\frac{b}{A},

(4.24)

where
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}
and
c
are non-zero constants. Also from (
4.22
), we have

\displaystyle\partial_{z_{i}}(f(z))=ce^{a_{1}z_{1}+a_{2}z_{2}+\ldots+a_{n}z_{n}},

(4.25)

where
i=1,2,\ldots,n
.
Again from (
4.27
), we get

\displaystyle\partial_{z_{i}}(f(z))=\frac{a_{i}c}{A}e^{a_{1}z_{1}+a_{2}z_{2}+\ldots+a_{n}z_{n}},

(4.26)

where
i=1,2,\ldots,n
.
Now comparing (
4.25
) and (
4.26
), we get

\displaystyle c\left(1-\frac{a_{i}}{A}\right)e^{a_{1}z_{1}+a_{2}z_{2}+\ldots+a_{n}z_{n}}=0,

which shows that
a_{i}=A
for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
. Finally from (
4.27
), we have

\displaystyle f(z)=\frac{c}{A}e^{A(z_{1}+z_{2}+\ldots+z_{n})}+b-\frac{b}{A},

(4.27)

where
A
and
c
are non-zero constants.
∎
