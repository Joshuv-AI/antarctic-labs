Physiotherapists experiences with cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) training: Mixed methods study
Authors: Michelle Wang, Jillian Eyles, Samantha Bunzli, David Klyne, Belinda Lawford, Susan M McCurry, Amanda Dinh, Robert Schütze, Alan Nguyen, Daniel I Rhon, Rana S Hinman, Simon S Smith, Peter Window, Stacey Grealis, Michelle Hall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveExplore physiotherapists’ experiences with Cognitive Behavioural Therapy for Insomnia (CBT-I) training to understand factors that may influence implementation and upscaling of CBT-I training for physiotherapists.MethodUsing a mixed-methods approach, data were collected through questionnaires and semi-structured interviews from twelve physiotherapists (3–27 years' experience) who completed a CBT-I training program consisting of online workshops and a pilot case. The Educational Course Assessment Toolkit (EDUCATOOL) evaluated reaction, learning, expected outcomes and behaviours in relation to the CBT-I training, and the Determinants of Implementation Behaviour Questionnaire (DIBQ), assessed barriers and enablers to the implementation of CBT-I. Interviews explored physiotherapists’ experiences of the training, guided by the theoretical framework of acceptability. Data were analysed thematically, with themes subsequently mapped to the Theoretical Domains Framework.ResultsThe median EDUCATOOL overall score was 92.38 (IQR = 87.35–99.00) out of 100.87% of DIBQ items were rated favourably to their respective domains (>4 on 7-point Likert scale). Four themes were generated from the interviews. Physiotherapists reported confidence in their capability to deliver CBT-I, reflecting (1) readiness for implementation in their professional role. (2) Training delivery and design contributed positively to knowledge acquisition. (3) Experiential learning enhanced understanding and confidence. However, physiotherapists noted (4) barriers affecting the integration of CBT-I in physiotherapy practice, including stakeholder engagement, and feasibility considerations related to billing, and delivery.ConclusionPhysiotherapists felt confident to deliver CBT-I following a training program. For successful upscaling, future initiatives must address identified systemic and practical barriers, including stakeholder engagement and delivery feasibility.Keywords: Cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia, Chronic musculoskeletal pain, Conservative therapies

Introduction

Osteoarthritis and chronic low back pain are leading causes of disability worldwide, affecting more than 1.2 billion people globally in 2020 [1]. Sleep problems often co-exist with osteoarthritis and low back pain [2], and poor sleep is one of the most distressing features of these conditions [3,4]. Despite this, sleep is rarely managed within routine osteoarthritis or low back pain care [5].

Insomnia is among the most common sleep disorders in people with chronic musculoskeletal pain [[6], [7], [8]]. Consistent with DSM-5 criteria, insomnia disorder is characterised by persistent difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep or early morning awakening with difficulty returning to sleep, occurring for at least three months [9]. The relationship between sleep and pain is complex and bidirectional: pain disrupts sleep, while poor sleep exacerbates pain [10] and predicts worsening pain over time [11]. The mechanisms underlying this relationship involve dynamic interplay between immune and nervous systems where sleep disturbance can disrupt neuroimmune processes that regulate pain [12]. Cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is a recommended first-line treatment for insomnia [13]. CBT-I is a multi-component intervention that consists of sleep restriction, stimulus control instructions, sleep hygiene education, mindfulness/relaxation strategies, and cognitive therapy [14,15]. In addition to improving sleep, CBT-I may improve pain outcomes in people with chronic musculoskeletal conditions [13].

Despite strong evidence supporting CBT-I, access remains limited due to a shortage of trained providers [16,17]. This barrier has prompted proposals for stepped care models to expand CBT-I delivery [18]. Physiotherapists are well positioned to deliver CBT-I because they are commonly involved in the management of musculoskeletal conditions and are encouraged to adopt a biopsychosocial approach to care [19,20]. Physiotherapists typically develop strong therapeutic relationships through frequent contact [21] which supports health behaviour change and is also important for successful CBT-I delivery [22]. As one of the five largest registered primary healthcare professions in Australia [23], physiotherapists offer scalable opportunities to expand access to CBT-I.

A multi-site international randomized controlled trial is underway in the US and Australia to evaluate the effect of adding CBT-I to aerobic physical activity for people with chronic low back pain and/or osteoarthritis of hip or knee (SLEEP-FIT). Physiotherapists were trained to deliver the CBT-I intervention. To support implementation beyond clinical trials, this mixed methods study aimed to explore physiotherapists’ experiences of a CBT-I training program, including perceived barriers and facilitators to CBT-I delivery.

Method

Study design

An exploratory mixed methods study was conducted [24], consisting of physiotherapists completing a quantitative questionnaire followed by a semi-structured interview. Qualitative interviews were informed by the interpretivist paradigm where knowledge is developed through gathering perceptions and interpretations of those who experience the phenomenon [25]. Reporting complied with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research checklist to promote transparent reporting and credibility of data collection (Supplementary 1) [26]. This study was approved by the University of Sydney Research Ethics Committee (HREC 2025/HE001601), and all participants provided informed consent.

Participants

As part of the SLEEPFIT trial, physiotherapists were trained to deliver CBT-I. In Australia, physiotherapists experienced in the management of musculoskeletal conditions registered to practice with the Australian Health Practitioner Regulation Agency and working in private practice in New South Wales or Queensland were recruited via our clinician network. Physiotherapists employed in the US Military Health System were recruited based on geographical proximity to clinical sites participating in the SLEEPFIT trial. All physiotherapists trained to deliver CBT-I for the SLEEPFIT trial were invited via email to participate in this study. No physiotherapists declined to participate.

CBT-I training

Physiotherapists undertook mandatory training (total time commitment of ∼10.5 h) prior to delivering the CBT-I intervention to an allocated pilot case (volunteer participant suffering from insomnia and chronic musculoskeletal pain). The training components can be understood through Miller's Pyramid framework (Fig. 1), which illustrates the progression from foundational knowledge acquisition to implementation [27]. Training included completion of two motivational interviewing workshops (∼3.5-h) and one CBT-I workshop (∼3.5 h). The CBT-I workshop was delivered by sleep and CBT-I experts (DK, SS, and SM) and covered an introduction to sleep and the impact on health, along with an outline of content to cover in CBT-I consultations. Upon completion of the workshops, each physiotherapist conducted six CBT-I consultations with a pilot case who suffered from signs of insomnia (≥11 on Insomnia Severity Scale [28]). Consultations were audio-recorded, emailed to a CBT-I expert (SM), and fidelity was assessed using a bespoke 20-item checklist with feedback session by session to each physiotherapist. Physiotherapists in Australia were paid to take part in the training, whereas physiotherapists practicing in the US did not receive additional payment.

CBT-I intervention

The CBT-I intervention included physiotherapists delivering six online consultations via Zoom to their allocated pilot case over two months (weekly consultations for the first month, fortnightly for the second month). Each consultation took approximately 30 min to complete. Prior to beginning the intervention, physiotherapists received a digital “Clinician Manual” detailing study information, intervention protocols and links to participant resources (e.g. Participant CBT-I handbook). Physiotherapists delivered consultations using structured consultation notes via REDCap.

Throughout all consultations, physiotherapists used motivational interviewing techniques to support participants through sleep behaviour change and address perceived barriers to sleep window adherence. Physiotherapists guided participants through the CBT-I handbook during the six sessions to facilitate goal setting, reiterate educational content, and support sleep window management. The research team and a CBT-I expert remained available to address any concerns regarding CBT-I delivery and provided ongoing monitoring for each pilot case. Data collection

Quantitative data

Questionnaire data were collected via REDCap [30]. The validated 12-item Educational Course Assessment Toolkit (EDUCATOOL) [31] was used to assess the evaluation components (reaction, learning, behavioural intent, and expected outcomes) of the CBT-I training. Items were rated on a 0–10 Likert scale (completely disagree to completely agree), with higher scores indicating stronger agreement. Each component was scaled to a score out of 25 and the total score was derived using weighted responses for all items (minimum 0, maximum 100). The Determinants of Implementation Behaviour Questionnaire (DIBQ) aims to better understand the behaviours of health professionals when implementing evidence-based interventions [32]. The original DIBQ has 93 items across 18 domains and is designed to be adapted to suit the context in which it is being used [32]. The research team selected 17 relevant items across nine domains (knowledge, skills, social/professional role and identity, beliefs about capabilities, beliefs about consequences, intentions, innovation, organisation, and behavioural regulation) to assess barriers to and enablers of the implementation of CBT-I into clinical practice. Items not applicable were removed (e.g. Item from organisation domain “In the organisation I work, all necessary resources are available to deliver [intervention].”). Items were rated on a 1–7 Likert scale (strongly disagree to strongly agree) with higher scores indicating stronger agreement (Supplementary 3).

Qualitative interviews

A semi-structured interview guide was developed [33] in consultation with clinical musculoskeletal educators and researchers, and people with lived experience of chronic musculoskeletal pain and sleep problems (Table 2). The guide was informed by the Theoretical Framework of Acceptability and focused on the predefined topics; affective attitude, self-efficacy, burden, intervention coherence, and perceived effectiveness [34]. Individual interviews were conducted via Zoom by the same researcher (MW, a female exercise scientist and trained in qualitative methodologies), who was not involved in the SLEEPFIT trial and did not know the physiotherapist participants. Interviews were conducted after physiotherapists had completed their pilot case and their questionnaires. Interviews lasted on average 40 min and were video-recorded and transcribed verbatim using Microsoft Word AI. Video-recordings and transcripts were deidentified and assigned participant identification numbers, and data was stored on a password-protected secure university server. Transcripts were not returned to the participating physiotherapists for checking.

Descriptive data

Participant characteristics including gender, years practiced as a physiotherapist, practice setting, and geographical location were collected in the interviews.

Quantitative data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to describe participant characteristics, EDUCATOOL, and DIBQ scores. The EDUCATOOL scores per item, component, and overall evaluation were reported as medians and interquartile ranges. DIBQ scores were reported per domain using percentages to demonstrate proportionality between scores. To facilitate analysis, the 7-point Likert scale used in the DIBQ was mapped to standard agreement anchors (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree), consistent with previous applications of 7-point Likert scales in health-survey research [35]. Responses were collapsed into categorical variables for analysis (e.g., disagreement [[1], [2], [3]], neutral [4], agreement [[5], [6], [7]]).

Qualitative data analysis

Data were analysed using an inductive (data driven) reflexive thematic approach [36], allowing for an iterative approach whereby analysis began alongside data collection. Transcripts were first read by MW to familiarise herself with the data and then cleaned for accuracy. MW then applied open coding to organise the codes into categories. This was followed by axial coding to identify broader patterns. Under the guidance of MH, JE, and SB, MW grouped categories into themes and themes were mapped to the domains of the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF). The TDF offers a structured approach for examining factors that may facilitate or hinder health professionals’ adoption of evidence-based practices [37]. The team also remained open to the possibility that not all themes could be mapped to TDF domains, so as to not obscure unique insights about physiotherapist specific experiences of learning CBT-I.

AD (female research officer with qualitative research experience) read and coded a subset (25%) of transcripts to challenge interpretations and TDF domain mapping. NVivo 14 software and Microsoft Word were used to organise codes and themes. Exemplary quotes were used to present final codes and themes to illustrate that interpretations were grounded in the raw data. Analytical rigour was maintained through reflexive practice, with all researchers acknowledging their positionality. Physiotherapists (JE, SB) and non-physiotherapists (MW, AD, MH) provided complimentary “insider” and “outsider” perspectives. Physiotherapist researchers’ professional backgrounds were acknowledged as potentially influencing interpretation while non-physiotherapist researchers approached the data without pre-conceived professional scope assumptions. Investigator triangulation compared perspectives on the data to ensure rigor and credibility.

Results

Participant characteristics

The sample consisted of four male and eight female physiotherapists. Six were working in private practice in Australia and six were physiotherapists based in a military setting in the US. Years of experience in practice ranged from 3 to 27.

EDUCATOOL

The median EDUCATOOL overall score was 92.38 (IQR = 87.35–99.00) out of 100 (Table 3). The behavioural intent component was rated most favourably (median = 25.00, IQR = 22.50–25.00) with the least variability across items. The reaction component, while still highly rated (median = 23.75, IQR = 19.17–25.00), showed the greatest variability in responses.

Determinants of implementation behaviour questionnaire

87% of DIBQ items were rated in agreement to their respective domains (>4 on 7-point Likert scale), indicating favourable attitudes toward CBT-I implementation (Fig. 2). The intention (had plans to deliver CBT in the next three months) and innovation (CBT-I was tailorable, compatible with daily practice, and simple to deliver) domains were rated with the strongest agreement (92% and 77% agree/strongly agree [rating ≥6] respectively), whereas the professional role and identity (CBT-I is part of a physiotherapist role) showed the least amount of agreement (33% rated agree/strongly agree).

Readiness for implementation in professional role

Physiotherapists expressed an overall readiness towards learning and implementing CBT-I as an additional tool in their therapeutic toolkit. Physiotherapists revealed their confidence in understanding CBT-I protocol delivery and its effectiveness, describing CBT-I as “wasn't overly difficult to deliver”, “relatively simple” and “really useful”, using analogies like “extra string to your bow” or “adding it to my toolkit” to communicate their expansion of professional skills. Most physiotherapists agreed the training laid a “good general grounding” to build on their knowledge and confidence in understanding CBT-I and future integration into their clinical practice. One physiotherapist highlighted their professional confidence to deliver the protocol was attributed to “the script” making it “more accessible to us as PTs” while another attributed their confidence to familiarity with discussing sleep information in their “career” and “personal life”. Overall, the physiotherapists believed the training provided them with a solid knowledge base to deliver CBT-I.

Most physiotherapists were confident that CBT-I falls within and “alongside” their professional scope and skillset. They acknowledged that they were “comfortable working with people from that perspective [delivering CBT-I as a physiotherapist]” and believe it is “certainly within our scope to encourage people to change lifestyle”. One physiotherapist spoke to their professional boundaries in delivering CBT-I to patients who suffer from both insomnia and mental health conditions and another physiotherapist spoke to lower professional confidence in “someone who has complex insomnia”. Some physiotherapists believed that CBT-I sits in the “realm” of other health professionals’ scope such as “therapists”, “occupational and speech therapists”. There was also acknowledgement that without training, CBT-I would sit outside their own scope.

Training delivery and design contribute to knowledge acquisition

All physiotherapists found the CBT-I training workshop to be informative, engaging, and thorough. The content was described as “a lot of information” covering “from start to end what each session would look like”, and still most physiotherapists considered both the CBT-I workshop to be appropriate length with their schedule and attention span. In particular, the half day sessions were appreciated for balancing out the day and consolidating knowledge. A combination of clear content presentation and spaced-out delivery contributed to an effective learning experience for the physiotherapists. However, US physiotherapists acknowledged the “latency” between the workshop and their pilot case practice to be suboptimal as it felt “overwhelming because it had been so long”.

Physiotherapists appreciated the “engaging” design of the motivational interviewing workshop and found “parts of the motivational interviewing workshop approach” to be “hugely valuable” for integrating into their professional role; however, some physiotherapists expressed feelings that the content “lacked a little bit of depth or nuance” in comparison to their previous motivational interviewing training. A few physiotherapists mentioned that “in moments” this training “felt long”.

Across the training, there were several resources shared and different platforms to set up. Some physiotherapists felt that while they appreciated the abundant resources, referring to the clinician manual, consultation notes, and presentation slides, they also felt they were burdensome to review. One physiotherapist working in private practice shared it may be an obstacle to stay “on top” of the learning content, resources, and the set-up of digital platforms while balancing work commitments. A suggestion was to include a separate training session to help navigate administrative tasks. There was a consensus that sharing recorded examples of CBT-I delivery could improve their practical delivery skills for ideal implementation and additional case studies to clarify treatment for patients “more resistant to change”.

Experiential learning enhances perceived understanding and confidence

All physiotherapists spoke to their growth in self-confidence and competence to deliver the CBT-I protocol through their experiences delivering CBT-I to pilot cases, known as experiential learning. They also discussed the value of concurrent feedback delivered after each consultation, describing it as “valuable”, “insightful” and “very useful”. Most physiotherapists highlighted improved self-efficacy to deliver CBT-I after delivering it for the first time and receiving personalised feedback. Some physiotherapists felt the pilot case experience reiterated the efficacy of CBT-I after seeing improved patient outcomes and hearing positive feedback from the patient.

However, as the physiotherapists became more comfortable with delivering CBT-I, they also identified a lower capability to treat more complex patients. Physiotherapists implied the training did not provide specific treatment strategies to use with patients who may be more resistant to change or more complex to treat. Although, there was acknowledgement that with more practice delivering CBT-I, skill and competence to treat a variety of patients and their treatment outcome expectancies will improve.

Barriers affecting the integration of CBT-I in physiotherapy practice

Although physiotherapists mostly shared about their experience with the CBT-I training, many shared their thoughts on potential barriers and facilitators to implementing CBT-I beyond a research setting and into clinical practice. The physiotherapists voiced concerns about CBT-I deviating from expectations of the typical care delivered by physiotherapists, such as “exercise interventions” and “more hands on manual therapy techniques”. They were cognisant that if CBT-I did not fit within the norms of physiotherapy care, there may be limited uptake and engagement with the treatment from both a patient and clinician perspective.

Additionally, considering patient treatment typically sits in a multidisciplinary care setting, support from other stakeholders like patients themselves, general practitioners, allied health professionals, and workplace managers because they “understand the value of it [CBT-I]” was flagged as important for treatment “buy in”.

The physiotherapists acknowledged that this training was to prepare them for CBT-I delivery in a research setting, which then raised some questions on the insurance and financial implications and practical delivery feasibility in clinical practice. Whilst both Australian and US physiotherapists expressed hesitancy to deliver CBT-I if it did not fit within their “scope of practice” or “practice acts”, all US physiotherapists felt more uncertainty about physiotherapist delivered CBT-I being “billable” given the US healthcare system is perceived as “for the dollar”.

Perceptions of future CBT-I delivery varied, One less experienced physiotherapist did not see CBT-I being integrated into clinical practice as a stand-alone treatment like in SLEEPFIT and suggested CBT-I components would be “weaved” into “sessions” and “done informally” while another more experienced physiotherapist appreciated that patients “need that [CBT-I] structure for it to be done really well”. Some physiotherapists foreshadowed time constraints as a barrier for delivery acknowledging that “in a typical outpatient PT clinic, you're already strapped for time” while another connected limited time as a barrier to patient “buy-in”. Physiotherapists indicated that CBT-I delivered in a “telehealth setting” or “group therapy” may be more feasible.

Discussion

This mixed-methods study explored physiotherapist experiences of CBT-I training. Overall, physiotherapists felt prepared to deliver CBT-I, attributing this readiness to appropriately designed training that built on existing biopsychosocial skills. However, variability in responses in the DIBQ and EDUCATOOL highlighted contextual barriers, that may affect implementation beyond research settings. Our findings provide preliminary insight into the acceptability, barriers, and facilitators of physiotherapist-delivered CBT-I.

Physiotherapists' readiness to implement CBT-I aligns with their familiarity with biopsychosocial approaches for musculoskeletal conditions [20]. As physiotherapy increasingly integrates psychological and behavioural strategies alongside physical treatments [38], CBT-I may represent an extension of existing competencies rather than an entirely new skill. This is consistent with physiotherapists’ reported confidence with CBT techniques in similar studies [39] and positions CBT-I as another evidence-based tool for holistic care. However, we acknowledge that financial reimbursements (Australia) and role-based requirements (US) may have influenced engagement and readiness.

The CBT-I training design incorporated elements commonly reported in physiotherapy extended-scope training programs, including workshop-based didactic instruction, supervised practice with patients, and individualized performance feedback [40]. This multimodal approach was valued by physiotherapists and aligns with evidence supporting supervised feedback for behavioural skills training [40,41].

Clinician-focused CBT-I training programs vary considerably in format and duration, ranging from brief workshops to multi-day intensive courses. For example, the Australian Psychological Society and Australasian Sleep Association certified CBT-I practitioner eLearning program, designed for psychologists and suitable for clinicians interested in insomnia management, is approximately 6 h long [42]. Our 10.5 h training was designed to equip physiotherapists to deliver a structured CBT-I protocol within their routine musculoskeletal care. The training covered all core CBT-I educational content and extended beyond standard didactic CBT-I education by incorporating motivational interviewing training, protocol-specific supervision, pilot case audio review, and individualized feedback. Collectively, this made the training comprehensive relative to its intended purpose and comparable in content to many clinician-focused CBT-I continuing education programs. Despite this, our participants identified challenges, including difficulty recalling workshop content when substantial time elapsed before pilot case practice, and managing extensive resources without clear implementation guidance. These findings suggest established training formats require attention to timing and resource usability to optimize learning outcomes.

Despite the effectiveness of this experiential model for upskilling physiotherapists to deliver CBT-I, its reliance on expert feedback and assigned pilot cases limits scalability. AI technologies may provide alternatives to simulate patients and offer automated fidelity feedback [43], potentially enabling practice across a broader range of scenarios while preserving the critical experiential learning component.

Despite the positive attitudes towards delivering CBT-I, physiotherapists identified challenges for future implementation. Particularly, the divergence of CBT-I from traditional exercise and manual therapy-focused practices [20]. This role ambiguity may limit buy-in to physiotherapist-delivered CBT-I from patients, referrers, workplaces. The professional role and identity domain received the greatest variability across DIBQ domains, and the reaction component (measuring course satisfaction, relevance, and engagement) of the EDUCATOOL showed the greatest variability across EDUCATOOL subscales. This variation may reflect differences in how physiotherapists perceived the relevance of CBT-I to their individual practice context, with potential implications for delivery fidelity and sustained implementation beyond a research setting. Patient engagement and acceptability are also critical to facilitate implementation of CBT-I, consistent with other evidence linking treatment acceptability to perceived efficacy [44,45]. Collaborating with sleep medicine specialists and primary care physicians as local champions may enhance awareness of physiotherapists' extended scope and improve stakeholder acceptance of this model of care [46].

Physiotherapists identified insurance and financial implications as key systemic barriers to CBT-I, alongside scope of practice. Although CBT-I could be an effective physiotherapist-delivered intervention, unclear guidelines, legal frameworks, and credentialing pathways limit uptake [47]. Practical delivery feasibility, time constraints, and challenges integrating new protocols alongside existing treatments were barriers, consistent with other behaviour change informed interventions in physiotherapy [48]. Time constraints created tension between informal and protocolised CBT-I delivery. Our physiotherapists suggested alternative delivery models such as group therapy, consistent with evidence showing comparable effectiveness across CBT-I delivery formats [49]. These findings support considering flexible delivery options to improve feasibility.

This study adopted a theoretically informed approach to evaluate the experiences of working physiotherapists from the US and Australia on CBT-I training. The influence of personal and professional bias in our interpretations was minimised by having a researcher (with an exercise science background) who was unknown to physiotherapists conduct the interviews and analysis. Given this study involved a small convenience sample of 12 physiotherapists, the quantitative findings are limited and uncertain, and the qualitative findings may not have reached data saturation, therefore findings should be interpreted with caution. These findings cannot be generalised to all physiotherapists as they are specific to the CBT-I training provided and in the context of a research setting where physiotherapists will be compensated to engage with CBT-I delivery. The subjective selection of DIBQ items based on relevancy to TDF domains may be considered a limitation due to concerns around replicability of selection. Future mixed methods research involving larger samples could integrate quantitative and qualitative data to generate new insights on the process of integrating CBT-I in clinical practice and consider focusing on how best to integrate CBT-I into practice that is feasible within the environmental and organisational context of physiotherapists. Future training programs may also benefit from incorporating multiple supervised pilot cases, where feasible, to further support skill development and implementation confidence. Additionally, exploring the perceptions from key stakeholders (e.g. patients, referrers) for physiotherapist delivered CBT-I may be important to understanding treatment acceptability.

Conclusion

Insomnia and chronic musculoskeletal pain often co-exist, highlighting the potential for physiotherapists to address both conditions. This study demonstrates that physiotherapists are confident to deliver CBT-I when provided with an informative and well-designed program that utilises experiential learning components, concurrent feedback and access to resources. However, regardless of training quality, significant barriers to implementation in clinical practice remain, including stakeholder support, scope of practice uncertainty and delivery feasibility.
