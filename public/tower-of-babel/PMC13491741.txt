Prognostic factors in patients with Marfan syndrome referred to a cardiac surgery center: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Yan-mei Cheng, Yu-juan Chen, Chao-qun Wang, Shao-yan Mo, Ding-feng Lai, Guang-xian Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMarfan syndrome (MFS) is associated with a high risk of aortic complications and premature mortality. This study aimed to evaluate long-term outcomes and identify clinical predictors of mortality in patients with MFS.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of consecutive patients diagnosed with MFS according to the revised Ghent criteria who presented between February 2000 and June 2022. Kaplan–Meier analysis was used to estimate overall survival, and survival was compared according to preoperative left ventricular end-diastolic dimension (LVEDD). Univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses were performed to identify predictors of all-cause mortality. A time-dependent Cox regression model was additionally used to account for surgery as a time-varying covariate and reduce potential immortal time bias.ResultsA total of 67 patients were included, with a mean age of 32 ± 10 years and a mean maximum ascending aortic diameter of 58.0 ± 11.7 mm. During a median follow-up of 53 months, the 1-, 5-, 10-, and 15-year overall survival rates were 74.6% (95% CI: 64.2–85.0), 62.8% (95% CI: 50.8–74.8), 48.7% (95% CI: 34.2–63.2), and 43.3% (95% CI: 27.0–59.6), respectively. Patients with preoperative LVEDD ≥ 65 mm had significantly worse survival (P = 0.024).In multivariable Cox regression analysis, preoperative LVEDD ≥65 mm (HR 2.39; 95% CI 1.06–5.38; P = 0.035) and emergent intubation (HR 6.43; 95% CI 1.54–26.88; P = 0.011) were independently associated with increased mortality, whereas surgical intervention was associated with lower mortality (HR 0.44; 95% CI 0.20–0.99; P = 0.046). In time-dependent Cox analysis, surgery remained significantly associated with lower mortality after adjustment (HR 0.31; 95% CI 0.13–0.70; P = 0.005).ConclusionIn patients with MFS, larger LV dimensions, particularly LVEDD ≥65 mm, were associated with poorer long-term outcomes and may provide complementary information for exploratory risk stratification beyond aortic diameter. Surgical intervention was associated with lower mortality after accounting for time-dependent bias. These findings suggest that LVEDD may help identify high-risk patients and support its potential role as a complementary marker for risk stratification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-06055-6.Keywords: Marfan syndrome, Aortic aneurysm, Aortic dissection, Follow-up study

Introduction

Marfan syndrome (MFS) is an autosomal dominant connective tissue disorder, leading to structural weakness in the extracellular matrix. These features affect the cardiovascular system, eyes and skeleton [1]. Cardiovascular manifestations of MFS include aortic root dilatation, aneurysm formation, aortic dissection and rupture, mitral valve prolapse and regurgitation, aortic valve regurgitation, left ventricular dilatation, and, less commonly, cardiac rhythm disturbances and aneurysms of peripheral vessels such as intracranial or pelvic arteries [2–5]. The most life-threatening complication of MFS is acute aortic dissection, particularly involving the ascending aorta, which remains the leading cause of premature death in affected individuals [6].

According to current guidelines, the recommended threshold for referral for prophylactic surgical intervention is 50 mm for MFS patients [7]. Several studies have questioned the use of aortic size as the sole reference for preventive intervention, showing that only a small percentage of patients developed dissection [8, 9]. Although many long-term outcome studies for MFS have been reported in Western populations, similar studies in Chinese cohorts remain scarce [10–13]. In our center, MFS patients frequently present with aortic dimensions well beyond the recommended surgical threshold. Therefore, the aim of this retrospective cohort study was to evaluate long-term outcomes in a real-world cohort of Chinese patients with MFS referred to a cardiac surgery center for aortic aneurysm or dissection, and to identify clinical predictors of all-cause mortality, with particular attention to whether aortic diameter and left ventricular end-diastolic dimension (LVEDD) may provide additional value for risk stratification and earlier surgical decision-making.

Methods

Study design and population

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of adult patients (≥ 18 years) with Marfan syndrome (MFS) who were admitted to the Department of Cardiac Surgery of our institution from February 2000 to June 2022 for evaluation or management of aortic aneurysm or aortic dissection. All patients had a documented clinical diagnosis of MFS, which was retrospectively verified according to the revised Ghent criteria (2010) using available clinical and imaging records. The exclusion criteria were age less than 18 years, previous aortic surgery, a history of aortic dissection prior to the index admission, or failure to meet the revised Ghent criteria upon retrospective review. All eligible patients meeting the inclusion criteria during the study period were consecutively enrolled. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University. Informed consent was waived, and the analysis used anonymous clinical data.

Data collection and definitions

Aortic aneurysm (AA) and aortic dissection (AD) were defined based on clinical presentation at admission and recorded as baseline disease status. Baseline characteristics including sex, age, body mass index, hypertension history, and vital signs including systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and heart rate at admission were collected from the medical records.

Preoperative transthoracic echocardiographic data were collected from routine clinical reports and medical records. The assessed parameters included left ventricular end-diastolic dimension (LVEDD), left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), and the severity of mitral and aortic regurgitation. LVEDD was defined as the preoperative left ventricular end-diastolic dimension. Mitral regurgitation was assessed semi-quantitatively from the apical four-chamber view, and aortic regurgitation was assessed semi-quantitatively from the apical five-chamber view. The severity of both mitral and aortic regurgitation was graded as none, mild, moderate and severe. Follow-up data were obtained through phone call and review of hospital records for subsequent visits at our institution.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean (SD) and compared using the two-tailed independent samples t-test when normally distributed, or as median (interquartile range) and compared using a non-parametric test when not normally distributed. Categorical variables were expressed as counts or percentages and compared using Pearson’s χ² test. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences in survival between groups were compared using the log-rank test.

Univariable Cox proportional hazards regression was performed to identify factors associated with all-cause mortality. Left ventricular end-diastolic dimension (LVEDD) was categorized according to predefined clinically relevant thresholds (60 mm, 65 mm, and 70 mm) for survival analysis. Variables with P < 0.05 in univariable analysis and those considered clinically relevant were candidates for the multivariable Cox regression model. To reduce the risk of overfitting, a parsimonious approach was used, with the number of covariates limited according to the number of observed events. The proportional hazards assumption was tested and was not violated. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.

A time-dependent Cox regression analysis was additionally performed to account for surgery as a time-varying covariate and to reduce potential immortal time bias. Model 1 included surgery alone; Model 2 additionally adjusted for LVEDD, heart rate, and disease type; and Model 3 further adjusted for sex and age. Exploratory subgroup analyses of the association between LVEDD and survival were also performed.

Results

Study cohort characteristics

During the screening process, 3 patients were excluded because they were younger than 18 years, 12 patients were excluded due to previous aortic surgery, and 5 patients were excluded for failure to meet the revised Ghent diagnostic criteria for MFS. Finally, a total of 67 patients were enrolled. The clinical variables and outcomes of the study population are presented in Table 1. The mean age was 32 ± 10 years. The cohort was comprised of 41 men (61.2%) and 26 women (38.8%), with a mean maximum ascending aortic diameter of 58.0 ± 11.7 mm. Based on presenting disease status at admission, the cohort was categorized into two groups: 32 patients (47.8%) with AA and 35 patients (52.2%) with AD. Seventeen (25.4%) had a family history of MFS. Five patients (7.5%) had hypertension. A BMI less than 18.5 was present in 29 (43.3%) patients. Moderate/severe aortic regurgitation was present in 28 (41.8%) patients, whereas 15 patients (22.4%) had a moderate/severe mitral regurgitation. Twenty-nine patients (43.3%) had a LVEDD ≥ 65 mm. Six (9.0%) patients had a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≤ 40%. Four patients (6.0%) were emergently intubated before surgery because of acute heart failure. Six patients (8.6%) required perioperative CRRT treatment, of these, 1 received CRRT before surgery and 5 received CRRT after surgery.

Operation

In the AA subgroup, all patients were considered to have clinically relevant indications for surgical intervention. Of these, 25 of 32 patients (78.1%) had aortic diameters ≥ 50 mm, exceeding the guideline-recommended threshold for prophylactic surgery in the absence of dissection. In the remaining 7 patients (21.9%) with aortic diameters < 50 mm, surgery was undertaken because of additional indications, including severe mitral regurgitation with or without severe aortic regurgitation.

Among the 39 patients who underwent surgery, Bentall was the most frequently performed procedure (53.8%). Other procedures included Bentall combined with arch replacement (15.4%), Bentall with concomitant mitral and/or tricuspid valve surgery (20.5%), and valve-sparing procedures (10.3%). (Table 2)

Overall survival and outcomes

The median survival time was 109.0 months (95% CI: 43.4–174.6), and the median follow-up time was 53 months (range: 0.1–254.0 months). The endpoint was all-cause mortality. In the overall cohort, the estimated overall survival rates at 1, 5, 10, and 15 years were 74.6% (95% CI: 64.2–85.0), 62.8% (95% CI: 50.8–74.8), 48.7% (95% CI: 34.2–63.2), and 43.3% (95% CI: 27.0–59.6), respectively.

Thirteen patients (19.4%) died during the index hospitalization. Of these, 5 died before surgery, including 4 due to aortic rupture and 1 due to left heart failure. Among the 39 patients who underwent surgery, 8 died during the index hospitalization, corresponding to an in-hospital postoperative mortality of 20.5%. The causes of postoperative death were low cardiac output (n = 3), postoperative infection (n = 3), and hemorrhage (n = 2). During follow-up, 5 patients (7.5%) developed dissection, including 1 in the AA group and 4 in the AD group. Among the 17 late deaths (25.4%), the main causes were sudden death (n = 13), heart failure (n = 1), and postoperative dissection (n = 3).

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis stratified by LVEDD

Kaplan–Meier analysis showed that patients with LVEDD ≥ 65 mm had significantly worse cumulative survival than those with LVEDD < 65 mm (log-rank P = 0.024; Fig. 1). The estimated 1-, 5-, 10-, and 15-year survival rates were 65.5% (95% CI: 48.3–82.7), 48.0% (95% CI: 29.8–66.2), 35.6% (95% CI: 15.2–56.0), and 26.7% (95% CI: 5.3–48.1) in patients with LVEDD ≥ 65 mm, compared with 81.6% (95% CI: 69.3–93.9), 74.5% (95% CI: 59.8–89.2), 59.6% (95% CI: 40.2–79.0), and 59.6% (95% CI: 40.2–79.0) in those with LVEDD < 65 mm.

Univariable and multivariable cox model analyses

When LVEDD was analyzed as a continuous variable in univariable Cox regression models, increasing LVEDD showed a directionally consistent trend toward higher mortality risk, although statistical significance was not reached (Supplementary Table S1).

When multiple predefined LVEDD thresholds were evaluated, only LVEDD ≥ 65 mm showed a significant association with mortality (Supplementary Table S2). Emergent intubation was likewise associated with worse survival, whereas surgical intervention and heart rate > 70 bpm were associated with improved survival in univariable analysis (Table 3). In the multivariable Cox regression analysis, LVEDD ≥ 65 mm (adjusted HR: 2.39; 95% CI: 1.06–5.38; P = 0.035) and emergent intubation (adjusted HR: 6.43; 95% CI: 1.54–26.88; P = 0.011) were independently associated with worse survival. In contrast, surgical intervention remained associated with improved survival (adjusted HR: 0.44; 95% CI: 0.20–0.99; P = 0.046). (Table 4) The multivariable Cox model demonstrated a Harrell’s C-index of 0.707 (95% CI: 0.595–0.819), indicating moderate discriminatory ability for all-cause mortality.

Surgical intervention and survival analysis

Kaplan–Meier analysis showed that overall survival was better in the surgical group compared with the non-surgical group (log-rank P = 0.023; Fig. 2). The estimated 1-, 5-, 10-, and 15-year survival rates were 64.3% (95% CI: 46.5–82.1), 48.0% (95% CI: 28.8–67.2), 32.0% (95% CI: 12.4–51.6), and 32.0% (95% CI: 12.4–51.6) in the non-surgical group, compared with 82.1% (95% CI: 70.1–94.1), 73.8% (95% CI: 59.9–87.7), 61.5% (95% CI: 42.1–80.9), and 52.7% (95% CI: 29.6–75.8) in the surgical group, respectively. To account for potential immortal time bias, the association between surgical intervention and mortality was further assessed using a time-dependent Cox regression model.

Kaplan–Meier survival curves for Marfan syndrome patients stratified by surgical intervention

In the time-dependent Cox regression analysis, surgical intervention remained significantly associated with improved survival in all models. In the crude model, surgery was significantly associated with lower mortality (HR: 0.42; 95% CI: 0.21–0.86; P = 0.018). After adjustment for LVEDD, heart rate, and disease type (AA vs. AD) in Model 2, the association remained significant (HR: 0.39; 95% CI: 0.18–0.85; P = 0.017). Further adjustment for sex and age in Model 3, surgery remained an independent protective factor (HR: 0.31; 95% CI: 0.13–0.70; P = 0.005). (Table 5) In an exploratory subgroup analysis of patients who underwent surgery, long-term survival tended to be higher in those with LVEDD < 65 mm than in those with LVEDD ≥ 65 mm, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (log-rank P = 0.126; Supplementary Figure S2) Fig. 3.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of patients with MFS-related aortic disease, LVEDD ≥ 65 mm was associated with poorer long-term survival. Emergent intubation was also associated with increased mortality, whereas surgical intervention was associated with lower mortality. These findings suggest that left ventricular remodeling may provide important prognostic information beyond traditional aortic measurements.

In our cohort, among patients with LVEDD ≥ 65 mm, 9 of 29 had no more than mild mitral or aortic regurgitation, suggesting that ventricular dilatation in some patients may not be fully explained by valvular volume overload alone. Traditionally, left ventricular dilatation in MFS has been considered a secondary consequence of chronic volume overload due to valvular regurgitation [14, 15]. However, emerging evidence suggests that myocardial involvement in MFS may not be entirely secondary, with load-independent myocardial impairment observed in some patients [16].

In addition, left ventricular dilatation has been reported even in the absence of significant valvular regurgitation [17, 18]. Cardiovascular magnetic resonance studies have shown that even patients without prior cardiovascular surgery or significant valvular regurgitation may exhibit mild, often asymptomatic cardiomyopathy [19, 20]. Yetman et al. reported a 3.08% prevalence of primary cardiomyopathy among MFS patients without regurgitant lesions [10]. In addition, impaired left ventricular function independent of valvular disease has also been reported in patients with MFS [21]. Similar features have also been described in children with MFS, including biventricular dilatation and dysfunction [22]. In this study, we observed that a substantial proportion of patients with aortic aneurysm already had aortic diameters exceeding the current surgical threshold without evidence of dissection. This finding highlights the marked heterogeneity in disease progression among patients with MFS and suggests that reliance on aortic diameter alone may be insufficient for accurate risk stratification. In this context, LVEDD may provide complementary prognostic information beyond aortic diameter. This observation may partly explain why some patients with markedly enlarged aortic diameters remain free of dissection, whereas others experience adverse outcomes.

Emergent intubation was independently associated with poorer survival, reflecting acute clinical deterioration and hemodynamic decompensation. In our cohort, all cases of emergent intubation occurred prior to surgery, and most involved patients with enlarged LVEDD and moderate-to-severe valvular regurgitation, suggesting that heart failure may have been a major contributing factor. This interpretation is supported by previous studies indicating that heart failure contributes to mortality in patients with MFS, often in association with valvular regurgitation [23–25].

With advances in surgical management for MFS-related aortic disease, life expectancy has improved substantially [26–29] Our findings are broadly consistent with previous studies showing improved long-term outcomes in surgically treated patients. To reduce immortal time bias, surgery was modeled as a time-dependent variable, which provided a more appropriate estimate of its association with survival. However, this association should still be interpreted cautiously, as residual confounding and selection bias cannot be excluded in this retrospective cohort.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective study of a relatively rare disease, the sample size was limited, and the findings should be interpreted with caution. Second, the study cohort represents a retrospective referral population that included both surgically and non-surgically managed patients. These groups are inherently different, as treatment decisions were influenced by both clinical and non-clinical factors, which may have introduced selection bias and limited the interpretability of direct comparisons. In addition, comparisons between patients with aortic dissection (AD) and aortic aneurysm (AA) are subject to survivorship and referral bias. Patients who died from AD before reaching medical attention were not represented in the study cohort, potentially leading to underestimation of the true mortality of AD. Third, the retrospective design limits the ability to control for potential confounding factors and precludes causal inference. Furthermore, the diagnosis of Marfan syndrome was adjudicated retrospectively based on available clinical data. Although cases were reviewed with reference to the revised Ghent criteria, incomplete documentation of certain systemic features may have introduced a risk of misclassification. Fourth, the long study period spanning more than two decades may have introduced temporal heterogeneity in surgical techniques, perioperative management, and clinical practice patterns. Finally, follow-up data were obtained through telephone contact and review of hospital records from our institution. Because medical data are not fully shared across hospitals, clinical events occurring outside our center may not have been completely captured, potentially leading to underestimation of outcomes. In addition, medication exposure could not be reliably ascertained over time due to incomplete data and variability in clinical practice, which limited the ability to evaluate the impact of medical therapy on prognosis. Taken together, these limitations indicate that the present study should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and the findings require confirmation in larger prospective studies.

Conclusion

In patients with MFS, LVEDD ≥ 65 mm may represent a clinically relevant marker of advanced ventricular remodeling associated with poorer long-term outcomes.
