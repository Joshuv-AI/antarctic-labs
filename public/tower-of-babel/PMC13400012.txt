Lip-lift Flap for Columellar–Nasal Base Reconstruction after Mohs Surgery
Authors: Bernardo A. Lomeli Escamilla, Ofer Arnon, Ori Berger, Ron Talisman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary:Reconstruction of columellar and nasal base defects remains challenging because of the region’s aesthetic and functional importance. Squamous cell carcinoma in situ in this area often necessitates Mohs micrographic surgery, which can result in composite tissue loss that is difficult to restore with conventional techniques. We report the case of an 89-year-old woman presenting with a 1-cm lesion at the left nasal base and columella. The tumor was excised in a single Mohs stage with histologically clear margins. Reconstruction was achieved using a lip-lift–style advancement flap designed along the subnasal border. The flap provided excellent color and texture match, preserved nasal airway function, and obviated the need for cartilage grafting. At 1-month follow-up, healing was uneventful, with a concealed donor-site scar and only a mild columellar deviation that caused no functional or aesthetic concern. The patient expressed high satisfaction with the outcome. This case highlights the versatility of the lip lift, typically an aesthetic procedure, as a reliable option for oncological reconstruction of nasal base and columellar defects.

Cutaneous malignancies of the nasal base, columella, and nostril margin pose reconstructive challenges given the region’s complex anatomy, central aesthetic prominence, and key role in airway function. Nonmelanoma skin cancers, particularly basal cell carcinoma (BCC) and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), are most frequent in this area.1,2 Although BCC predominates, SCC of the columella and alar base, although less common, is clinically important for its propensity for deeper invasion and morbidity.1–4 Risk factors include cumulative ultraviolet exposure, fair skin phenotype, advanced age, immunosuppression, prior oncological or radiotherapeutic interventions, human papillomavirus infection (notably for SCC), and tobacco use, which may also impair postoperative healing.1

In geriatric patients, detection is often delayed by functional or neurocognitive limitations and comorbidities.5 Assessment is further complicated by tightly contoured convexities, dense vascularity, and adjacent aesthetic subunits, as well as clinical overlap with benign lesions; deeper tumors may show occult submucosal or intranasal extension.3,4

Mohs micrographic surgery is the gold standard for high-risk facial nonmelanoma skin cancers, enabling complete histological margin control while sparing uninvolved tissue.4 Post–Mohs defects in this subunit frequently involve the mucosa, alar rim, and columella, requiring individualized strategies to restore anatomic continuity and function with minimal donor-site morbidity.2 We report the adaptation of a commonly aesthetic procedure, the lip-lift advancement flap, to reconstruct a left nasal base and columellar defect after Mohs excision. To our knowledge, this is the first reported use of a lip-lift–style advancement flap for oncological reconstruction of the columella and nasal base following Mohs surgery.

CASE REPORT

An 89-year-old woman with no significant comorbidities presented with a 1-cm raised, irregular lesion at the left nasal base extending to the columella (Fig. 1A), clinically suspicious for BCC or SCC. Under local anesthesia, Mohs micrographic surgery cleared the lesion in a single stage; histology confirmed SCC in situ with negative peripheral and deep margins (Fig. 1B). Written informed consent for case publication and images was obtained.

Skin lesion. A, Irregular lesion approximately 1 cm in diameter involving the left nasal base and extending to the columella along the internal border. Preoperative markings indicate surgical margins and the planned base of the flap (arrow). B, Post–Mohs excision defect measuring approximately 1.5 cm in diameter.

Reconstruction was performed using a lip-lift–style advancement flap along the subnasal border (Fig. 2A and B). After margin confirmation, the ~1.5-cm defect involved the left nasal base and columellar border. A subnasal incision was made, local anesthetic (1% lidocaine with epinephrine 1:100,000) was infiltrated, and an upper-lip skin–subcutaneous flap was elevated in the supramuscular plane, preserving the orbicularis oris. Superior advancement allowed tension-free closure while maintaining philtral symmetry. Layered closure was performed; the donor site was closed primarily, with the scar concealed in the subnasal crease. The repair provided excellent color/texture match, preserved airway patency, and did not require cartilage grafting.

Intraoperative views. A, Elevation of the upper-lip advancement flap in the supramuscular plane, preserving the orbicularis oris muscle. The right side was detached while maintaining a wide base on the left to preserve vascular supply. B, Assessment of flap length to ensure adequate advancement while avoiding upper-lip distortion due to traction.

At 1-month follow-up, healing was uneventful with no complications. A mild rightward columellar deviation was observed without aesthetic or functional concern. The patient reported high satisfaction with both function and appearance. (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 1, displays the preoperative appearance, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/F24.) DISCUSSION

This case highlights the reconstructive potential of adapting a traditionally aesthetic procedure, the lip-lift advancement flap, for closure of nasal base and columellar defects following Mohs micrographic surgery.2,6 According to the principles of the reconstructive ladder, options such as primary closure or skin grafting may be considered for small defects; however, these methods can compromise contour or disrupt aesthetic subunit harmony in this region. The lip lift–based advancement flap offers a balanced solution, achieving oncological safety while preserving aesthetic integrity and minimizing donor-site morbidity, particularly advantageous in older adult patients with adequate upper-lip laxity and limited reconstructive requirements.

The nasal region provides a wide range of local and regional flap options, including rhomboid, bilobed, banner, nasolabial advancement, and paramedian forehead interpolation flaps. In this patient, however, the resection resulted in a substantial soft-tissue defect involving the left nasal rim and the internal aspect of the columella, an aesthetic subunit essential for nasal tip projection and nasolabial angle definition.6 After careful evaluation of the patient’s anatomy and age-related lip elongation, a subnasal lip-lift design was chosen to provide tissue with optimal color, thickness, and vascularity for single-stage reconstruction.

The lip lift was first described by Austin in 1986.7 Over the years, numerous technical variations have been introduced, with no universally preferred approach, each surgeon typically applying the method with which they are most familiar.8 Variations include the subnasal advancement flap, which advances philtral tissue superiorly; the inferiorly based island flap, derived from the upper-lip dermis and either rotated or tunneled; and bilateral alar base advancement flaps, which can augment central reconstruction.8,9 These flaps utilize the robust vascular network of the upper lip, providing an excellent color and texture match, a concealed donor site, and reliable perfusion. Compared with more complex procedures such as forehead or nasolabial flaps, the lip-lift advancement flap enables single-stage repair under local anesthesia—particularly beneficial for frail patients or older adults.

In cases reconstructed following Mohs surgery, this approach provides additional safety by ensuring histologically tumor-free deep and peripheral margins before closure. The subnasal incision can be strategically placed within a natural crease, effectively concealing the scar while maintaining nasal airway patency and upper-lip functionality.9

CONCLUSIONS

A lip lift–style advancement flap offered a reliable and aesthetically favorable solution for reconstruction of columellar and nasal base defects following Mohs excision of SCC in situ. The technique provided excellent functional and cosmetic outcomes with minimal morbidity and a well-concealed donor site. Although rarely described in oncological literature, this approach merits consideration, particularly in older adult patients with redundant upper-lip tissue and limited reconstructive requirements.
