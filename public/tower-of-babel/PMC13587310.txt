Trajectory analysis of sleep disorders and anxiety-depression in female breast cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy: based on group-based Multi-Trajectory Model and machine learning
Authors: Jiang Yuan, Yinqing Yang, Yuan Li, Tumalisi Kayishaer, Jie Chu, Bingmei Zhu, Zhizhen Tao, Jinshu Han, Xinyu Zhao, Xiuying Hu, Chao Tian, Yi Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSleep disorders and anxiety-depression symptoms can significantly impair the quality of life and treatment adherence among breast cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy, while also increasing the risk of disease recurrence and mortality. Early identification of the progression trajectory and underlying causes of these symptoms is essential for providing personalized interventions for deteriorating cases.ObjectiveThis study aims to analyze the trajectories of sleep disorders and anxiety-depression symptoms in breast cancer patients during chemotherapy using a group-based Multi-Trajectory Model (GBMTM) and to identify influencing factors through machine learning.MethodsProspective data from 150 breast cancer patients undergoing four cycles of chemotherapy were collected. A GBMTM was employed to identify distinct trajectories, with 70 predictive variables included in the analysis. Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression was used to select the optimal subset of variables. Seven machine learning models were compared, and the optimal model was chosen for result interpretation using SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis.ResultsAmong breast cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy, two distinct trajectories of sleep disorders and anxiety-depression symptoms were identified: the “stable group” (74.7%) and the “deteriorating group” (25.3%). SHAP analysis revealed that high levels of C-reactive protein, high uric acid, high hemoglobin, smoking, presence of invasive cancer with ductal carcinoma in situ, prior chemotherapy, being married, regular menstrual cycles in the past, and lack of family history were significant risk factors associated with the “deteriorating group.”ConclusionOur findings indicate two distinct trajectories of sleep disorders and anxiety-depression symptoms in breast cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy, with the majority falling into the “stable group”. The identified risk factors associated with the “deteriorating group” should be closely monitored to facilitate early intervention and improve patient outcomes.Trial registrationThe trial was registered on Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Trial ID: ChiCTR2300077667 on 15 November 2023; available at https://www.chictr.org.cn/).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12911-026-03672-1.Keywords: Group-based Multi-Trajectory Model, Sleep disorders, Anxiety-depression, Breast cancer, Machine learning

Introduction

Breast cancer is a significant global public health concern among women. According to the latest cancer statistics released by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) in 2022, breast cancer ranks first in both incidence and mortality among female malignancies worldwide [1]. Chemotherapy is one of the primary treatment modalities for breast cancer patients. However, compared with other therapeutic approaches such as endocrine therapy, radiotherapy, and surgery, chemotherapy is associated with more severe adverse effects, including sleep disorders and anxiety-depression symptoms [2–4]. Sleep disorders have been shown to increase the risk of side effects such as stroke, nausea, vomiting and fatigue [5, 6], thereby reducing patients’ quality of life and treatment adherence. Moreover, depression is associated with a 17% and 30% increased risk of recurrence and mortality, respectively, in breast cancer patients [7]. This risk is further exacerbated when anxiety-depression co-occur.

Accurate prediction of trajectories and expected outcomes is fundamental to delivering precise care for individual patients. Group-based Trajectory Modeling (GBTM) is a statistical method that can estimate the developmental trajectories and the probabilities of future clinical events based on existing data, thereby facilitating real-time predictions [8], which has been widely applied in single-variable trajectory studies, such as the trajectory of fear of recurrence in breast cancer patients [9], long-term fatigue [10], anxiety-depression following discharge [11], sleep disorders after diagnosis [12]. However, GBTM is limited to predicting trajectories based on a single variable and is unable to conduct trajectory analyses for multiple variables or assess their interactions. The occurrence and progression are often the result of complex interactions among multiple time-dependent variables. To address this limitation, the group-based Multi-Trajectory Model (GBMTM) has been developed. GBMTM extends the capabilities of GBTM by allowing simultaneous analysis of combined trajectories of multiple variables and identification of potential clusters [13], has not yet been applied in the field of oncology.

Trajectory identification enables the characterization of the development and changes in patients’ conditions (e.g., stability, deteriorating, or gradual recovery), thereby facilitating the implementation of personalized patient management. Additionally, analyzing the influencing factors of trajectories can elucidate the underlying mechanisms driving these developmental patterns. For cases exhibiting deteriorating conditions, trajectory analysis allows for the timely adjustment of treatment plans and the implementation of personalized interventions. Previous studies analyzing the influencing factors of trajectories have predominantly employed logistic regression [9, 11]. However, logistic regression models often exhibit sub-optimal predictive performance when dealing with high-dimensional and nonlinear data [14]. In contrast, machine learning algorithms have demonstrated superior predictive capabilities in classification and prediction tasks. Furthermore, the application of SHapley Additive exPlanations(SHAP) analysis has addressed the limitations of “black box” models [15], enabling precise quantification of the impact of each factor on the outcome.

This study aims to use the GBMTM to analyze the trajectories of sleep disorders and anxiety-depression symptoms in breast cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy. We seek to identify group heterogeneity and elucidate the influencing factors associated with each developmental trajectory through interpretable machine-learning algorithms. The findings will provide an evidence-based foundation for precise nursing interventions and inform personalized nursing needs.

Method/design

The data of this study came from a randomized controlled clinical trial, which has completed pre-registration in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Trial ID: ChiCTR2300077667 on 15 November 2023; available at https://www.chictr.org.cn/).

Study design and participants

From December 2023 to December 2024, baseline data of 150 breast cancer chemotherapy patients from Sichuan Cancer Hospital were prospectively collected. Follow-up was conducted one day before each chemotherapy session, during a total of four chemotherapy cycles. The participant flow diagram can be found in Supplementary Material Figure S1. This study adheres to the CONSORT guidelines. The CONSORT checklist is available in Related Files S2.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Age from 18 to 70 years old; (2) Patients diagnosed with breast cancer according to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) Breast Cancer Clinical Practice Guidelines [16];(3) Patients without chemotherapy contraindications and liver or kidney function impairment.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Patients with heart, liver, kidney dysfunction or hematopoietic dysfunction or other serious diseases; (2) Patients with vomiting due to increased intracranial pressure, gastrointestinal diseases, etc.; (3) Patients participating in other clinical trials with behavioral, psychological or supplementary medical intervention during the study period; (4) Pregnant or lactating patients; (5) Patients with mental or cognitive disorders unable to communicate normally.

The Drop-out criteria were as follows: (1) Patients with poor compliance and non-cooperation in treatment; (2) Patients unable to continue treatment due to unexpected events during the treatment process; (3) Patients lost to follow-up.

In this study, the sample size was calculated based on the primary outcome measure - the score of delayed nausea severity. The preliminary pilot results showed that the score of delayed nausea and vomiting in the control group was 3.58 points, while in the intervention group it was 2.38 points. The standard deviation (σ) was set at 2, the effect size (β) was set at 80%, α = 0.05 (two-tailed test), with 45 cases in each group, totaling 135 cases. Randomization block grouping was performed using SPSS 27.0. Due to the particularity of the intervention measures, blinding was not implemented.

Traditional Chinese medicine supplementary therapy

Acupressure

Acupressure involves the use of fingers, palms, elbows, or other tools to stimulate meridians and acupoints, thereby promoting the circulation of qi and blood, regulating the balance of yin and yang, and achieving therapeutic or palliative effects. Acupoints include: Hegu (LI4), Neiguan (PC6), Zusanli (ST36), and Sanyinjiao (SP6). Press each acupoint 1–2 times a day for 2–3 min each time for the entire chemotherapy process.

TEAS

Transcutaneous Electrical Acupoint Stimulation (TEAS) is based on the principles of meridian theory and utilizes non-invasive skin-mounted electrodes to deliver dense and sparse wave electrical stimulation to acupoints through the skin. This technique integrates the benefits of electro-acupuncture and transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS), offering precise control over electrical stimulation frequency and intensity. TEAS is painless, as it eliminates the discomfort associated with needle insertion, and is highly acceptable to patients. It is also easy to operate, portable, and highly repeatable.

In this study, four acupoints were selected for TEAS treatment: Hegu (LI4), Neiguan (PC6), Zusanli (ST36), and Sanyinjiao (SP6). Treatment was administered using the Hua Tuo brand electronic diagnostic device, model SDZ-II (Suzhou Medical Device Registration Number: 20172200675). The device delivered a sparse-dense wave pattern with an output current of 1–10 mA and a frequency of 2/50 Hz. TEAS was performed for each patient on the day before chemotherapy, the day of chemotherapy, and the first day after chemotherapy, with each session lasting 30 min.

This research has been approved by the institutional review board (IRB) of Chengdu University of Traditional Chinese Medicine Affiliated Hospital (2023KL-093). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to conducting the face-to-face structured interviews. Each participant agreed to the anonymous publication of their information if required. The ethical approval document can be found in Related Files S1.

Data collection

The demographic characteristics, past medical history, current medical history, liver and kidney function indicators, and blood routine test results of patients were collected through the hospital information system. Face-to-face follow-up surveys were conducted one day before each chemotherapy cycle to assess patients’ anxiety, depression, and sleep quality. On the first and fourth days after each chemotherapy session, telephone follow-up surveys were conducted to evaluate acute and delayed nausea and vomiting using the MASCC Antiemetic Tool (MAT). This scale consists of 8 items (Cronbach’s α = 0.724), covering nausea and vomiting symptoms during the acute phase (within 24 h after chemotherapy) and the delayed phase (2–5 days after chemotherapy), the range of nausea severity is [0, 10], 0 = no nausea,10 = worst possible nausea [17].

Since all the data for the variables were collected completely during the research follow-up period and no missing values occurred, data imputation was not employed in the main analysis.

The semi-structured interview guides and Case Report Forms (CRFs) used in this study are available in the Supplementary Material S1-S2.

Trajectory recognition

The GBMTM algorithm is applied to identify the development trajectories of anxiety, depression and sleep quality for each patient. The Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) are used to measure the goodness of fit and complexity of the model; the Average Posterior Probability (APP) is an indicator used to evaluate the internal fitting quality of the trajectory model, which calculates the average probability that all members assigned to a certain trajectory group are correctly classified into that group; the Overall Correct Classification Rate (OCC) is an indicator used to assess the classification accuracy of the model, which is measured by comparing the classification results of the model with a random allocation model based on prior probabilities. According to the principles of the AIC and the BIC being smaller, the APP being greater than 0.7, and the OCC being greater than 5 [20], and in combination with the sample size and the interpretability of the results, the optimal number of trajectories is selected. All three outcome variables were modeled as continuous data, and we assumed that their residuals followed a normal distribution. The model performed Z-score normalization on the data, in order to improve the numerical stability of the model estimation and facilitate the comparison of model coefficients among variables of different units. Group assignment rule: After determining the final number of trajectory groups, individual participants are assigned to the trajectory group with the highest posterior distribution probability.

We load the “gbmt” package in R4.4.2 for trajectory modeling, using the function “gbmt()”. We set the parameter “d = 3” to indicate that the outcome distribution is assumed to be a cubic polynomial; we set parameters “ng = 1” to “ng = 6” to build models for trajectory numbers 1 to 6 respectively; we set the parameter “scaling = 2” to describe the process of standardizing the outcomes, eliminating the differences in units, reducing the influence of outliers, and unifying the amplitude of outcome changes.

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted a five-fold cross-validation sensitivity analysis using this dataset. The AUC (95% CI) of the NB model was 0.833 (0.800-0.867), and a Delong test was performed against the second-ranked LR model. There was no statistically significant difference in the AUC values between the two models, but the confidence interval of the NB model was narrower. Therefore, it was selected as the optimal model, as shown in Table 3. Additionally, we replaced the longitudinal average laboratory indicators with the baseline indicator values and conducted a model prediction analysis. It was found that the AUC (95% CI) of the NB model was 0.828 (0.774–0.881), which was slightly smaller than that of the current model, and the confidence interval was wider; the Accuracy value was similar, see Supplementary Table S4.

During the treatment and follow-up period for all patients, no local or systemic adverse reactions related to acupuncture-electrical stimulation were observed.

Discussion

Our study identified two trajectories for the co-occurrence of sleep disorders and anxiety-depression in breast cancer patients during chemotherapy: “stable” and “deteriorating.” Consistent with prior research [11, 21], most patients in the stable group maintained relatively stable sleep quality and anxiety-depression throughout chemotherapy. In the deteriorating group, these symptoms first worsened before stabilizing. The cross-sectional study indicates that breast cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy often exhibit both psychological symptom clusters (such as anxiety and depression) and physiological symptom clusters (such as pain, fatigue, and sleep disorders) [22]. Moreover, longitudinal studies have revealed that the deterioration of one symptom often leads to the deterioration of another symptom [23–25], is consistent with our findings on the deteriorating group’s trajectory.

The trajectory analysis identified multiple factors associated with an increased risk of belonging to the “deteriorating group”, including elevated CRP, high uric acid, high hemoglobin levels, invasive breast cancer with DCIS, smoking history, previous chemotherapy, marital status, regular menstrual periods, and absence of a family history.

Notably, a key paradox emerged: our findings linking high hemoglobin levels and regular menstrual periods to exacerbated anxiety-depression contrast with previous cross-sectional studies that reported associations between lower hemoglobin, irregular menstruation, and increased emotional distress [26, 27]. This discrepancy may stem from the longitudinal design of our study, which strengthens causal inference by clarifying the temporal sequence of variables—an advantage over cross-sectional studies prone to reverse causality or unmeasured confounding. Relevant studies have shown that higher hemoglobin could reflect iron metabolism abnormalities or increased blood viscosity—both of which impair cerebral microcirculation and affect emotion-regulating brain regions (e.g., prefrontal cortex, hippocampus) [28]; Women with regular menstrual cycles usually have more stable ovarian functions, and their estrogen secretion shows periodic fluctuations. During chemotherapy, the estrogen secretion often becomes disordered, thereby affecting the regulation of emotions [29].

The remaining conclusions are similar to those of previous studies: Elevated CRP levels are associated with heightened inflammatory responses, which can directly impair psychological status by disrupting central neurotransmitter balance [30]. For perimenopausal women, higher serum uric acid is linked to depression [31]. potentially mediated by declining ovarian function and estrogen levels during this period—changes that independently contribute to both increased uric acid [32] and depressive symptoms [33]. Invasive breast cancer combined with DCIS presents more complex treatment and prognosis challenges compared to isolated DCIS (where cancer cells are duct-confined with favorable outcomes). Interestingly, studies have shown more severe anxiety-depression in DCIS patients than in those with early-stage invasive breast cancer [34], suggesting that the coexistence of these two tumor types may synergistically worsen psychological outcomes. Married and no family history patients may experience heightened concerns about the burden on their families. Patients with a history of chemotherapy may fear adverse reactions from previous treatments and worry about disease recurrence. Additionally, those with a smoking history may be more sensitive to the emotional regulation effects of nicotine [35], all of which could contribute to the worsening of anxiety-depression during chemotherapy.

Limitation

First, this study had a small sample size, only 150 breast cancer patients, and failed to identify more potential trajectories considering the interpretability of the results. Second, in the analysis of trajectory influencing factors, the categories of some variables were imbalanced, which may make the results biased. In this study, when analyzing the influencing factors of the development trajectory of anxiety-depression and sleep disorders in breast cancer patients, the physiological and biochemical indicators selected were the average values throughout the chemotherapy process. For this, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using the baseline physiological and biochemical indicators, but there might still be a situation of information leakage.

Conclusion

This study effectively identified the sleep disorder and anxiety-depression symptom cluster in breast cancer chemotherapy patients with two development trajectories: “stable group” and " deteriorating group”. Patients with high CRP, high uric acid, high hemoglobin, smoking, invasive carcinoma with DCIS, previous chemotherapy, married, regular menstruation in the past, and no family history should be focused on for precision care and personalized care.
