An interpretable prediction model for severe sepsis-associated acute kidney injury in older ICU patients with sepsis: a prospective multicenter cohort study
Authors: Wenliang Ma, Jingyi Wang, Jin Zhang, Xiaoxia Guo, Wenxiong Li, Hao Wang, Zhigang Chang, Yuefu Wang, Na Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSevere sepsis-associated acute kidney injury (severe SA-AKI) is clinically important in older adults with sepsis, but early identification remains challenging. This study aimed to characterize clinical and immuno-inflammatory features of severe SA-AKI and develop an interpretable prediction model for older ICU patients with sepsis.MethodsThis prospective multicenter cohort study enrolled older adults with sepsis admitted to five tertiary ICUs between June 2023 and December 2025. Patients were temporally assigned to a training cohort and an internal temporal validation cohort. Severe SA-AKI was defined as KDIGO acute kidney injury stage 2 or 3 within 7 days after sepsis time zero or during ICU stay. Candidate predictors collected within 24 h after time zero were evaluated in the training cohort, and a logistic regression nomogram was developed and validated.ResultsAmong 1,236 included patients, the minimum age was 65 years, and the mean age was 74.6 ± 7.5 years; 258 patients developed severe SA-AKI. The final 7-variable model included non-renal SOFA score, chronic kidney disease, log(Lactate + 1), log(IL-6 + 1), NK-cell percentage, CD4 + T-cell percentage, and C3. The model achieved AUCs of 0.917 and 0.904 in the training and validation cohorts, respectively. Brier scores were 0.086 and 0.096. Severe SA-AKI incidence increased across low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups: 0.5%, 13.0%, and 77.5%.ConclusionsA parsimonious model integrating clinical and immuno-inflammatory variables may support early severe SA-AKI risk stratification in older ICU patients with sepsis. External validation is warranted before broader clinical implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07882-0.

Background

Sepsis is defined as a life-threatening organ dysfunction resulting from a dysregulated host response to infection [1]. Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a prevalent and severe complication in patients with sepsis. Compared with AKI stage 1, severe SA-AKI, represented by KDIGO AKI stage 2–3, is more likely to reflect substantive tubular injury and persistent organ dysfunction, and therefore holds greater clinical prognostic significance [2–4].

With population aging, older adults account for an increasing proportion of ICU patients with sepsis. Compared with younger adults, older patients generally exhibit lower renal reserve, a higher burden of chronic kidney disease and cardiovascular disease, more complex medication exposure, and more fragile hemodynamic regulation. In addition, immunosenescence and chronic low-grade inflammation may predispose older patients to organ decompensation during septic stress [5, 6].

Therefore, there is a pressing need to develop a dedicated predictive model for severe SA-AKI in older adults.

Previous SA-AKI prediction studies have largely relied on traditional clinical variables, organ dysfunction scores, and routine laboratory indicators, with limited incorporation of immuno-inflammatory features. Immune status in older adults with sepsis is highly heterogeneous, and immunosenescence may influence the host response to infection and the risk of organ injury [7].

Although lymphocyte subsets have been incorporated into SA-AKI prediction models in prior studies [8], evidence remains limited for severe SA-AKI in older adults using a parsimonious and interpretable model. Based on a prospective multicenter sepsis cohort restricted to ICU patients aged 65 years or older, this study aimed to develop a severe SA-AKI risk prediction model with an emphasis on age-specific vulnerability, variable parsimony, minimization of outcome-information leakage, and bedside interpretability.

Methods

Study design and participants

This multicenter, prospective, observational cohort study consecutively enrolled older adults with sepsis admitted to the ICUs of five tertiary hospitals in Beijing between June 1, 2023, and December 1, 2025. This study was approved by the ethics committees of all participating centers (Peking Union Medical College Hospital, K3148/I-22PJ1104; Beijing Shijitan Hospital, IIT2023-007-002; Beijing Jishuitan Hospital, K2023-195-00; Beijing Hospital, 2023BJYYEC-150-01; Beijing Chaoyang Hospital, 2025-ke-869) and was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2300074175). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their legally authorized representatives.

A total of 1,414 patients were screened. Among them, 178 patients with autoimmune diseases or an ICU stay of < 48 h were excluded, whereas the remaining 1,236 patients were included in the final analysis. Patients were temporally assigned to a training cohort (June 1, 2023, to April 1, 2025; n = 865) and an internal temporal validation cohort (April 2, 2025, to December 1, 2025; n = 371), which were used for model development and performance validation, respectively.

Outcome definition

The primary outcome was severe SA-AKI. Sepsis was diagnosed according to the Sepsis-3 criteria, while AKI was staged according to KDIGO criteria using serum creatinine and urine output. KDIGO AKI stage 2 or 3 was defined as severe SA-AKI, whereas patients without AKI and AKI stage 1 were categorized as non-severe SA-AKI. Baseline serum creatinine was preferentially defined using a previously stable value. In the absence of baseline serum creatinine levels, the most recent value prior to ICU admission or sepsis diagnosis was considered in conjunction with review of the medical records. AKI stage, final AKI status, serum creatinine, and renal replacement therapy were used only for outcome definition or clinical description and were not included as predictors. Severe SA-AKI was assessed within 7 days after time zero or during the ICU stay, whichever occurred first.

Data collection and laboratory measurements

The baseline time point (time zero) was defined as the first time point at which Sepsis-3 criteria were met; for patients who already met sepsis criteria at ICU admission, ICU admission was designated as time zero. Demographic characteristics, comorbidities, infection sites, disease severity, routine laboratory indices, and immuno-inflammatory markers within 24 h after time zero were collected.

Patients who had already met KDIGO AKI stage 2–3 criteria at time zero were excluded from the prediction analysis. The final model incorporated the non-renal SOFA score to limit overlap between the renal component of SOFA and the AKI-based outcome.

Immuno-inflammatory markers included IL-6, lymphocyte subsets, immunoglobulins, and complement components. Lymphocyte subsets were measured by flow cytometry according to routine quality-control procedures at each center, and C3/C4 levels were measured using immunoturbidimetry or equivalent methods. Variables with > 30% missing data were excluded from the candidate set; remaining missing values were imputed according to variable type and distribution using median, mode, or random forest imputation, with imputation rules established in the training cohort and subsequently applied to the validation cohort [9].

Variable selection and model development

All modeling steps were conducted within the training cohort. Variables that could introduce outcome-information leakage, including AKI status, AKI stage, serum creatinine, and renal replacement therapy, were first excluded. Random forest variable importance and univariable logistic regression analyses were then used for preliminary variable screening, and Spearman correlation analysis and variance inflation factor (VIF) were performed to assess redundancy and collinearity.

Models containing different numbers of candidate predictors were compared according to training and validation AUC, validation Brier score, maximum VIF, and model parsimony. The final 7-variable logistic regression model included non-renal SOFA score, CKD, log(Lactate + 1), log(IL-6 + 1), NK-cell percentage, CD4 + T-cell percentage, and C3. A nomogram was constructed based on this model.

Model evaluation and risk stratification

Discrimination, calibration, and clinical net benefit were evaluated using receiver operating characteristic curves and AUC, calibration curves, Brier scores, calibration slope, and decision curve analysis, respectively [10, 11]. For risk stratification, a two-threshold strategy was used because the objective was to classify patients into clinically interpretable low-, intermediate-, and high-risk strata rather than to identify a single binary diagnostic cutoff. The thresholds were determined exclusively in the training cohort based on the empirical distribution of predicted probabilities and the corresponding observed severe SA-AKI event rates. Specifically, the lower threshold was selected to identify a very-low-risk group suitable for rule-out interpretation, whereas the upper threshold was selected to identify a high-risk group suitable for rule-in interpretation, while maintaining interpretable group sizes. The validation cohort was not used to determine these thresholds. This approach yielded low-risk (≤ 2.92%), intermediate-risk (> 2.92% and < 38.13%), and high-risk (≥ 38.13%) groups; severe SA-AKI incidence and ORs were calculated for each risk group.

Sample size considerations

This prospective observational cohort study consecutively enrolled eligible older ICU patients with sepsis during the predefined study period; therefore, no formal hypothesis-testing sample size calculation was performed before enrollment. After excluding variables that could introduce outcome-information leakage, 43 candidate predictors were initially considered. The final study population included 1,236 patients, corresponding to 28.7 observations per candidate predictor; the training cohort included 865 patients, corresponding to 20.1 observations per candidate predictor.

For the final prediction model, sample size adequacy was further assessed using the number of outcome events relative to the number of predictor parameters. In the training cohort, 176 severe SA-AKI events occurred, and the final model included 7 predictor parameters, yielding 25.1 events per predictor parameter. These findings supported the stability of the final parsimonious model and its reproducible performance in the internal temporal validation cohort, although external validation remains necessary to confirm generalizability.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as median [Q1; Q3], whereas categorical variables were expressed as n (%), with between-group comparison methods described in the table footnotes.

Logistic regression analysis was applied to evaluate associations between candidate variables and severe SA-AKI. Reporting was guided by recommendations for multivariable prediction model studies [12, 13]. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.3, with a two-sided P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient screening and cohort distribution

A total of 1,414 older ICU patients with sepsis were screened. Following the exclusion of 178 patients, the remaining 1,236 patients were included in the final analysis. The minimum age of the included participants was 65 years, and the mean age was 74.6 ± 7.5 years. Among them, 258 patients developed severe SA-AKI, corresponding to an overall incidence of 20.9%. The training cohort included 865 patients, of whom 176 developed severe SA-AKI (20.3%). The internal temporal validation cohort included 371 patients, of whom 82 developed severe SA-AKI (22.1%). The similar event rates between the two cohorts suggest that the temporal validation cohort retained a representative outcome distribution (Fig. 1).

Baseline characteristics

The training and validation cohorts were generally well balanced in age, sex, major comorbidities, disease severity, and most laboratory/immuno-inflammatory markers, although differences between cohorts were noted in abdominal infection, bloodstream infection, and CD8 + T-cell percentage (Supplementary Table 1). In the training cohort, patients with severe SA-AKI had a higher prevalence of CKD and greater disease severity, higher inflammatory/metabolic markers, and lower albumin levels, NK-cell percentage, CD4 + T-cell percentage, C3, and C4 compared with those with non-severe SA-AKI (Supplementary Table 2).

Variable selection and determination of model size

Random forest variable-importance analysis identified NK-cell percentage, IL-6, CD4 + T-cell percentage, C3, non-renal SOFA score, CKD, and lactate as top-ranked candidate predictors (Fig. 2). Univariable logistic regression and correlation analyses provided complementary evidence for predictor screening and collinearity assessment (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). Given that the APACHE II score was strongly correlated with non-renal SOFA score, the latter was retained to mitigate model redundancy and the risk of renal outcome information overlap.

Comparison of models with varying numbers of predictors revealed that the 7-variable model achieved a high validation AUC, a low Brier score, and acceptable VIF. Additional variables provided limited incremental performance. Therefore, the 7-variable model was selected as the final model (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Final multivariable prediction model

The final model included non-renal SOFA score, CKD, log(Lactate + 1), log(IL-6 + 1), NK-cell percentage, CD4 + T-cell percentage, and C3. Multivariable logistic regression showed that the first four variables were associated with an increased risk of severe SA-AKI, whereas the latter three were associated with a lower risk (Table 1). Because C3 values were distributed within a relatively narrow clinical range, we additionally calculated a standardized OR for C3 per IQR increase. In the training cohort, the IQR of C3 was 0.27 g/L, and the corresponding OR per IQR increase was 0.40 (95% CI 0.29–0.56).

Model performance evaluation and nomogram

The final model demonstrated good discrimination and calibration in both cohorts, with AUCs of 0.917 (95% CI 0.894–0.940) in the training cohort and 0.904 (95% CI 0.868–0.940) in the internal temporal validation cohort, respectively. The Brier scores were 0.086 and 0.096, and calibration slopes were 1.000 and 0.926, respectively. Decision curve analysis indicated a favorable clinical net benefit across a broad range of threshold probabilities (Fig. 3).

The nomogram indicated that higher non-renal SOFA score, CKD, lactate, and IL-6 were associated with increased predicted risk, whereas higher NK-cell percentage, CD4 + T-cell percentage, and C3 were associated with decreased predicted risk. Overall, the model integrated disease severity, renal vulnerability, metabolic stress, inflammatory status, cellular immunity, and complement activity in a single visual tool (Fig. 4).

Risk stratification

After stratification by predicted probability in the training cohort, severe SA-AKI occurred in 0.5% (1/204), 13.0% (68/523), and 77.5% (107/138) of patients in the low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups, respectively. Compared with the low-risk group, the intermediate- and high-risk groups had significantly higher risks (OR 20.40 and 462.99, respectively; both P < 0.001; Table 2; Fig. 5).

Clinically, these strata may support rapid rule-out, dynamic reassessment, and early kidney-protection pathways, respectively. Discussion

This multicenter prospective cohort study of older adults with sepsis developed and validated a severe SA-AKI prediction model integrating clinical severity, baseline renal vulnerability, inflammatory response, metabolic stress, cellular immunity, and complement status. The final model contained seven variables and maintained satisfactory discrimination and calibration in an internal temporal validation cohort.

In contrast to models predicting overall SA-AKI, this study focused on severe SA-AKI, emphasizing KDIGO AKI stage 2–3 as a clinically meaningful phenotype with greater prognostic relevance [14].

The development and progression of SA-AKI involve inflammatory cascades, renal microcirculatory dysfunction, endothelial injury, metabolic stress, and tubular injury [15–17]. Therefore, early risk assessment based solely on serum creatinine or conventional severity scores may be insufficient.

Older patients with sepsis should not be regarded as a simple age-stratified subgroup of the general adult population, but rather as a high-risk group with distinct pathophysiological hallmarks. Older patients have fewer functional nephrons and impaired renal blood flow regulation, leading to reduced tolerance to hypoperfusion, inflammatory mediators, nephrotoxic agents, and microcirculatory disturbances. In addition, CKD, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and malnutrition are prevalent in older patients, all of which increase susceptibility to renal decompensation during septic stress.

Immunosenescence is characterized by reduced T-cell number and function, altered NK-cell activity, chronic low-grade inflammation, and impaired immune regulation [18, 19]. These features suggest that prediction of severe SA-AKI in older adults should not rely only on traditional organ dysfunction scores, but should also incorporate markers reflecting immune and inflammatory status.

Notably, the final model incorporated the non-renal SOFA score rather than the full SOFA score. The full SOFA score includes a renal component, while the outcome of this study, severe SA-AKI, was defined according to AKI stage. Direct inclusion of the full SOFA score could introduce information overlap related to the outcome definition and may overestimate model performance. In contrast, non-renal SOFA score retains information on non-renal organ dysfunction and sepsis severity while lowering the risk of outcome-related information leakage.

log(IL-6 + 1) and log(Lactate + 1) were identified as independent risk factors for severe SA-AKI. IL-6 is a key cytokine in the inflammatory cascade of sepsis and may reflect pro-inflammatory activation, endothelial activation, and the intensity of the acute-phase response. Elevated lactate levels indicate tissue hypoperfusion, microcirculatory dysfunction, and altered cellular metabolism. Innate immune activation and inflammatory responses contribute to the development and progression of SA-AKI [20]. In older patients, reduced cardiovascular reserve may also increase vulnerability to sepsis-related hemodynamic fluctuation, supporting the clinical relevance of lactate in severe SA-AKI prediction.

NK-cell percentage and CD4 + T-cell percentage were independently associated with a lower risk of severe SA-AKI, suggesting that relatively preserved cellular immune status may serve as a marker of lower severe SA-AKI risk in older patients with sepsis. NK cells participate in innate immunity and cytotoxic responses and may be linked to infection-related kidney injury [21]. In older patients with sepsis, a lower NK-cell percentage may reflect impaired innate immune reserve, reduced capacity for early pathogen control, and persistent inflammatory stimulation, which may contribute to endothelial activation, renal microcirculatory dysfunction, and tubular stress. CD4 + T cells are central to adaptive immune regulation. Reduced CD4 + T-cell percentage may reflect immunosenescence, sepsis-associated immune paralysis, lymphocyte apoptosis, or immune exhaustion, resulting in impaired coordination of host defense and less effective control of inflammatory injury [18, 19, 22–24].

C3 was identified as an independent predictor associated with lower severe SA-AKI risk in the final model. As a central component of the complement cascade, reduced C3 levels may reflect consumption secondary to sustained complement activation and may also indicate impaired host immune defense. The complement system plays dual roles in sepsis: it contributes to pathogen clearance; nevertheless, excessive activation may drive inflammation, promote endothelial injury, facilitate microthrombosis, and impair microcirculation, all of which are closely linked to kidney injury and innate immune responses [25, 26]. Because older patients often have reduced renal reserve, chronic low-grade inflammation, and endothelial vulnerability, complement dysregulation may be particularly relevant in this population. Therefore, lower C3 in this study should be interpreted primarily as a predictive marker of complement consumption and immune dysregulation associated with severe SA-AKI risk, rather than as direct evidence of a causal protective effect. From a predictive modeling perspective, several recent studies have employed explainable machine-learning approaches or reviewed machine-learning-based SA-AKI prediction models [27–29]. In contrast, the present study emphasizes parsimony, interpretability, and bedside clinical translation. Although the OR for C3 in Table 1 was reported per 1 g/L increase, C3 values in clinical practice and in the present cohort were distributed within a narrower range. Therefore, the per-1 g/L OR should not be interpreted as the risk change associated with a typical small clinical fluctuation in C3. When expressed per IQR increase (0.27 g/L), the OR for C3 was 0.40 (95% CI 0.29–0.56), indicating that C3 contributed meaningfully to risk prediction within its observed clinical range.

This study not only constructed a predicted-probability model but also translated model output into three clinically meaningful strata: low, intermediate, and high risk. The incidence of severe SA-AKI was only 0.5% in the low-risk group, suggesting a low short-term probability of progression to severe SA-AKI and supporting avoidance of excessive monitoring or unnecessary intervention. The intermediate-risk group demonstrated an incidence of 13.0% and represented a gray zone requiring dynamic reassessment, including monitoring of urine output, serum creatinine levels, lactate levels, inflammatory marker levels, hemodynamic status, and fluid balance. The high-risk group exhibited an incidence of 77.5%, indicating the need for early renoprotective strategies, including optimization of volume status, avoidance of nephrotoxic drugs, maintenance of adequate perfusion pressure, dynamic assessment of fluid responsiveness, and early nephrology consultation or blood purification when appropriate. Similar integrated immune-dysregulation indices have also been utilized for prognostic risk stratification in sepsis, reinforcing the clinical relevance of translating immune-inflammatory profiles into risk strata [30].

This study has several strengths. First, the study population was restricted to older adults with sepsis, providing a clearly defined clinical population. Second, the analysis was based on a prospective multicenter cohort, and temporal splitting was used to form an internal validation cohort, better reflecting real-world clinical application than random data partitioning. Third, variables directly related to AKI diagnosis, RRT, and the renal component of the full SOFA score were proactively excluded, thereby lowering the risk of information leakage. Fourth, the model incorporated immuno-inflammatory markers and identified NK-cell percentage, CD4 + T-cell percentage, and C3 as variables reflecting immune reserve and complement status in older adults. Finally, the final model was parsimonious and was readily visualized through a nomogram and translated into clinically interpretable risk strata.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study merit acknowledgment. First, although an internal temporal validation cohort was used, all centers were tertiary hospitals in Beijing. Differences in case mix, laboratory platforms, immune-assay standardization, and ICU management across regions or non-tertiary hospitals may influence model performance; consequently, external validation in diverse healthcare settings is required. Second, immuno-inflammatory markers were measured at a single early time point, and dynamic immune trajectories were not captured. Serial monitoring may further enhance predictive performance and mechanistic interpretation. Third, some immune markers may not be routinely available in non-tertiary hospitals, and variability in assay turnaround and inter-laboratory standardization may affect clinical applicability. Fourth, as an observational prediction-model study, causal relationships between immuno-inflammatory variables and severe SA-AKI could not be established. Fifth, the risk stratification thresholds were derived from the training cohort using an exploratory rule-out/rule-in strategy and should not be regarded as universal clinical decision thresholds before prospective external validation.

Conclusions

A 7-variable model integrating clinical and immuno-inflammatory features was developed and internally validated to identify high-risk severe SA-AKI in older patients with sepsis. The model showed favorable discrimination, calibration, and interpretability, and may support early risk stratification in the ICU setting. External validation is required before broader clinical application.
