Transformer-accelerated discovery of inhibitors targeting the RpsAΔ438 deletion in PZA-resistant tuberculosis
Authors: Jiazhuo Xiao, Muhammad Shahab, Haoke Zhang, Zunnan Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tuberculosis is a major global public health problem caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Pyrazinamide (PZA), a key first-line drug, is activated to pyrazinoic acid, which inhibits Mycobacterium tuberculosis by targeting ribosomal protein S1 (RpsA) and blocking trans-translation. Deletion of alanine at position 438 of RpsA (RpsAΔ438) increases C-terminal flexibility, reduces drug binding, and confers PZA resistance. To identify novel inhibitors against this mutant, we developed a screening pipeline integrating Transformer-based virtual screening, molecular docking, Gaussian accelerated molecular dynamics simulations, and MM/GBSA free energy calculations. Compounds from the ChEMBL database were curated, labeled by MIC, and used to train Transformer-based models. The best-performing Transformer model outperformed Forest and XGBoost across multiple evaluation metrics, demonstrated an AUC of 0.844 and an accuracy of 0.772 on the test set, was applied to screen FDA-approved and traditional Chinese medicine libraries, yielding 317 and 1757 candidate compounds, respectively. Top-ranked hits were docked into RpsAΔ438 and evaluated using 400 ns molecular dynamics and Gaussian accelerated molecular dynamics simulations. Trajectory analyses revealed that compound Z2568748600 was the most promising candidate, exhibiting an RMSD of approximately 1.2–2.0 Å, an RMSF of 1.5–2.0 Å, and a radius of gyration of ~18.0 Å, forming long-lived hydrogen bonds with multiple residues, markedly suppressing large-scale motions, and effectively stabilizing the deletion-affected C-terminal domain. The MM/GBSA binding free energy was calculated to be −26.00 ± 3.50 kJ/mol. Moreover, in silico ADMET profiling confirmed its favorable drug-likeness and balanced safety profile, underscored by excellent cardiac safety and minimal cellular cytotoxicity. Overall, this integrated approach offers a promising strategy for discovering new agents against PZA-resistant Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Scientific contribution This study established an integrated, systematic screening strategy targeting the drug-resistant RpsAΔ438 protein by coupling a Transformer-based deep learning framework for high-throughput virtual screening with subsequent molecular docking, Gaussian accelerated molecular dynamics simulations, MM/GBSA free energy calculations and ADMET prediction. This strategy enabled the efficient identification of potential inhibitors from expansive chemical space and allowed for the in-depth evaluation of compound interactions with the mutant protein. Among the screened candidates, Z2568748600 was identified as the most promising compound based on comprehensive computational assessments. Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains one of the deadliest infectious diseases worldwide and is caused by the intracellular pathogen Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) [1]. Despite notable progress in diagnostics and treatment strategies, TB continues to pose a substantial global health burden. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), approximately 10.8 million new TB cases and 1.25 million TB-related deaths were reported in 2023. These finding highlight the persistent challenges in disease control and emphasize the urgent need for more effective and durable therapeutic interventions. A major contributor to the success of Mtb as a pathogen is its remarkable ability to survive and persist within host macrophages. Mtb employs a range of immune evasion strategies, including suppression of reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, modulation of host lipid metabolism, inhibition of antigen presentation, and induction of macrophage necrosis [2]. Together, these mechanisms enable long-term intracellular survival, promote chronic infection, and allow the bacterium to withstand host-induced stress, ultimately contributing to treatment failure and disease transmission.

Pyrazinamide (PZA) is a cornerstone of first-line TB chemotherapy and plays a critical role in shortening treatment duration [3]. When administered alongside rifampicin, isoniazid, and ethambutol, PZA reduces therapy length from 9 to 12 months to approximately 6 months [4]. PZA acts as a prodrug that is converted by mycobacterial pyrazinamidase (PZase), encoded by pncA, into its active form, pyrazinoic acid (POA) [5]. Although the molecular mechanism of PZA action remained controversial for decades, accumulating evidence has established ribosomal protein S1 (RpsA) as a key POA target [6–9]. POA binding disrupts trans-translation, leading to accumulation of incomplete polypeptides and, ultimately, bacterial death [10]. RpsA plays a central role in the bacterial trans-translation rescue pathway by coordinating with small protein B (SmpB), elongation factor EF-Tu, and transfer-messenger RNA (tmRNA) to resolve stalled ribosomes and tag aberrant proteins for degradation [11]. This quality-control system is essential for bacterial survival under stress conditions such as nutrient limitation, oxidative damage, and immune pressure [12].

Structural studies have shown that POA interacts with key residues in RpsA, including Lys303, Phe307, Phe310, Glu318 and Arg357, through hydrogen bonding and π–π interactions [13]. These interactions competitively block tmRNA binding, impair protein homeostasis, and suppress bacterial viability. The clinical utility of PZA, however, has been increasingly compromised by the emergence of drug-resistant TB. While mutations in pncA represent a major resistance mechanism, resistance can also arise from structural alterations in RpsA itself. A clinically relevant mutation involving deletion of alanine at residue 438 (RpsAΔ438) induces pronounced changes in the C-terminal binding pocket [13]. This deletion enhances the flexibility of key residues, weakens critical hydrogen bonding and aromatic stacking interactions with POA, and substantially reduces binding affinity. As a result, POA fails to stably engage the mutated pocket, conferring resistance to PZA.

Artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a powerful paradigm in modern drug discovery, offering solutions to the limitations of traditional screening approaches, including long development timelines, high attrition rates, and escalating costs [14]. Transformer-based deep learning models, originally developed for natural language processing, have demonstrated exceptional performance in molecular representation learning and virtual screening [15]. By encoding chemical structures as SMILES strings and leveraging self-attention mechanisms, Transformers capture complex structural patterns and long-range dependencies that are difficult to model using conventional methods [16, 17]. This enables efficient extraction of structure–activity relationships and rapid identification of bioactive compounds with high predictive accuracy. For antimicrobial-resistant pathogens such as Mtb, Transformer-driven screening provides a scalable and cost-effective strategy for identifying chemically diverse compounds with anti-mycobacterial activity. To complement these AI-based, target-agnostic predictions, the identified compounds are further screened against the RpsAΔ438 mutant using structure-based computational techniques, including molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, to prioritize candidates with favorable target-specific interactions. Docking approaches predict binding poses and estimate affinities while identifying key intermolecular interactions [18]. However, docking inherently treats proteins as largely rigid entities and cannot fully account for conformational flexibility, solvent effects, or mutation-induced energetic changes [19]. MD simulations address these limitations by modeling atomic motions over time under near-physiological conditions, enabling detailed analysis of binding stability, conformational rearrangements, and resistance mechanisms [20]. Enhanced sampling methods such as Gaussian accelerated molecular dynamics (GaMD) are particularly valuable for studying RpsAΔ438, where deletion-induced flexibility cannot be adequately captured by conventional MD alone [21].

In this study, we integrate Transformer-based virtual screening with molecular docking and Gaussian accelerated molecular dynamics simulations to identify novel inhibitors targeting the RpsAΔ438 mutant. By combining deep learning with dynamic structural validation, we aim to discover compounds capable of stabilizing the altered binding pocket and overcoming PZA resistance. This computationally accelerated framework provides a rational and scalable strategy for the discovery of next-generation anti-tuberculosis agents against drug-resistant Mtb.

Methods

Retrieval of Mycobacterium tuberculosis bioactivity data

Biological activity data for Mycobacterium tuberculosis-related compounds were retrieved from the ChEMBL database (Target ID: CHEMBL360) [22], which collect experimentally validated bioactivity records for compounds tested against Mycobacterium tuberculosis. The initial dataset comprised 208,233 compounds associated with diverse activity measurements, including minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values derived from in vitro assays. To ensure data consistency, reliability, and suitability for machine learning model development, a rigorous preprocessing pipeline was implemented. Compounds with missing or ambiguous bioactivity information were first excluded. Only records reporting MIC values expressed explicitly in μg/mL were retained to maintain uniformity in activity type and measurement units. Duplicate entries arising from repeated assays or redundant compound annotations were subsequently removed to avoid data leakage and bias during model training. Chemical structures were then standardized using the RDKit cheminformatics toolkit [23]. This step involved canonicalization of SMILES representations, removal of salts, inorganic ions, and other disconnected molecular fragments, and normalization of chemical structures to ensure consistency across the dataset. Such standardization is essential for minimizing noise and enabling accurate learning of Transformer models. Following these filtering and standardization procedures, a rigorously curated, high-quality dataset of Mycobacterium tuberculosis-active compounds was established to support transformer-based model development, virtual screening, and structure-guided computational analyses.

Compound diversity analysis

Compound diversity is a fundamental prerequisite for building robust machine learning models with strong generalization capability. MACCS molecular fingerprints consisting of 166 binary bits [24] were generated for each compound using the RDKit toolkit, and pairwise Tanimoto values [25] were calculated to evaluate structural diversity among compounds. A higher Tanimoto values indicates greater structural similarity between two compounds, whereas a lower value reflects higher structural diversity. In addition, to assess the diversity of physicochemical properties, molecular weight (MT), lipophilicity (LogP), the number of hydrogen bond donors (HBD), and the number of hydrogen bond acceptors (HBA) were calculated for each compound using RDKit toolkit. By jointly considering structural and physicochemical features, the overall chemical diversity of the dataset can be comprehensively characterized, providing a solid data foundation for subsequent model training.

Compound labeling

To construct a biologically meaningful and statistically robust classification dataset, the distribution of minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values was systematically analyzed. Descriptive statistical measures, including extreme values, mean, and median, were examined to evaluate distribution skewness and identify potential outlier effects. Based on these analyses, a classification threshold was selected that minimizes sensitivity to extreme values while maintaining biological relevance. Compounds exhibiting MIC values below the selected threshold were classified as sensitive and assigned a label of 1, whereas compounds with MIC values greater than or equal to the threshold were classified as resistant and assigned a label of 0. This labeling strategy enabled balanced discrimination between active and less active compounds and provided a consistent foundation for subsequent transformer-based classification modeling.

Compound dataset splitting

The curated dataset was randomly divided into training, validation, and test sets using a stratified sampling strategy at an approximate ratio of 8:1:1. Stratification was performed based on compound labels to ensure that the relative proportions of sensitive and resistant compounds were preserved across all subsets, thereby minimizing potential bias arising from class imbalance during model training and evaluation. To reduce the influence of randomness associated with a single data split and to enhance the robustness of performance assessment, the splitting procedure was independently repeated six times. Each repetition employed a distinct random seed (8, 16, 24, 32, 48, and 64), resulting in six independent sets of training, validation, and test partitions. This multi-split strategy enabled more reliable estimation of model performance and improved reproducibility of the results. In addition, 166-bit MACCS fingerprints were generated for all compounds across the training, validation, and test sets, and t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) [26] was applied to project the high-dimensional fingerprint space into two dimensions. This chemical space analysis was conducted to confirm consistent distribution patterns among the subsets, ensuring that model evaluation was not confounded by systematic chemical space discrepancies.

Construction of a regular expression-based SMILES tokenizer

To enable effective molecular representation learning, a SMILES tokenizer was developed to decompose chemical structures into discrete tokens suitable for Transformer-based modeling. The tokenizer was implemented using regular expressions [27] and was designed to preserve the syntactic and semantic integrity of SMILES strings. Specifically, the tokenizer first identifies atoms enclosed in square brackets, such as [Na+], and [OH−], which often denote charged or non-standard atomic states. It also recognizes multi-character halogen symbols, including Br and Cl, as single tokens to prevent erroneous fragmentation. Common organic element symbols, such as C, N, O, S, and P, are subsequently matched. In addition to atomic symbols, the tokenizer captures structural symbols essential for molecular connectivity and stereochemistry, including bond types (= , #), stereochemical annotations (@, @@), branch delimiters ((,)), and two-digit ring closure markers (e.g., 10%). Single-digit numerals are treated as independent tokens to represent ring closures and branching positions. This tokenizer effectively preserves the semantic integrity of SMILES strings and returns valid chemical tokens in their original character order.

Vocabulary construction

The regular expression-based SMILES tokenizer was applied to tokenize the SMILES strings of all compounds in the curated dataset. The resulting tokens were aggregated across the dataset, deduplicated, and their occurrence frequencies were computed. Tokens were then sorted according to frequency to construct the final vocabulary. To support robust sequence modeling, four special tokens were prepended to the vocabulary: <PAD> for sequence length alignment, <UNK> for handling out-of-vocabulary tokens, <BOS> to denote the beginning of a sequence, and <EOS> to indicate sequence termination. These tokens enable consistent handling of variable-length SMILES sequences during model training and inference. Each token in the vocabulary was subsequently assigned a unique integer index based on its position in the vocabulary, allowing SMILES sequences to be converted into discrete numerical representations suitable for Transformer model input.

Model architecture

The proposed model was implemented using PyTorch (version 2.7.1) and comprises two primary components: a Transformer encoder for molecular feature extraction and a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) for binary classification. The Transformer encoder learns high-level representations of molecular structures, while the MLP predicts compound sensitivity or resistance to Mycobacterium tuberculosis. SMILES strings were used as model inputs and were first tokenized and mapped to integer indices based on the constructed vocabulary. Special tokens <BOS> and <EOS> were appended to the beginning and end of each sequence, respectively, to explicitly define sequence boundaries. To ensure uniform sequence lengths within each batch, padding tokens <PAD> were added after the <EOS> oken as required. Any token not present in the vocabulary was mapped to the <UNK> index. The resulting discrete SMILES sequences were transformed into continuous vector representations using a trainable embedding layer. Positional encoding was added to the embeddings to incorporate information about token order, which is essential for capturing sequential dependencies in SMILES strings. The combined embeddings were subsequently processed through layer normalization and dropout to improve training stability and reduce overfitting. A learnable classification token (CLS) embedding, with the same dimensionality as the token embeddings, was prepended to each sequence to serve as a global molecular representation. The augmented embedding matrix was then passed through multiple stacked Transformer encoder layers, each consisting of multi-head self-attention mechanisms followed by position-wise feed-forward neural networks. Following Transformer encoding, the CLS representation and the mean-pooled representation of the token embeddings were combined using learnable weights to form a unified molecular feature vector. This combined representation was subsequently fed into the MLP classifier, which comprised layer normalization, fully connected layers, GELU activation functions, and dropout regularization. A final linear layer with a single output neuron produced classification logits, which were converted into probability scores using a sigmoid function. Compounds with predicted probabilities greater than 0.5 were classified as sensitive, enabling binary prediction of drug response in Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

Hyperparameter optimization

Hyperparameter optimization was performed using Optuna [28] with 100 optimization trials to improve model fitting on the training set while minimizing validation error. The search space encompassed key architectural and regularization parameters, including the number of attention heads (2, 4, 8), embedding dimension (64, 128, 256), feed-forward network dimension (256, 512, 1024), dropout rate (0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4), number of Transformer encoder layers (2, 3, 4, 6), and layer drop rate (0.1, 0.2, 0.3). Each hyperparameter configuration was evaluated using fivefold cross-validation on the training set. The mean MCC across the fivefolds was used as the objective metric to reduce variance introduced by a single split and to provide a balanced assessment of classification performance. Duplicate hyperparameter combinations were automatically excluded during optimization to improve sampling efficiency. To balance computational cost with performance stability, the top three hyperparameter configurations ranked by cross-validated MCC were selected for final model training. This strategy reduced sensitivity to random hyperparameter selection and ensured robust and reliable predictive performance across independent dataset splits.

Model training

Model training was conducted using a batch size of 32 for the training, validation, and test sets. Binary cross-entropy with logits loss (BCEWithLogitsLoss) was employed as the objective function to support stable optimization in binary classification. Model parameters were optimized using the AdamW optimizer, with both the learning rate and weight decay set to 1 × 10−4. A cosine annealing learning rate scheduler (CosineAnnealingLR) was applied to dynamically adjust the learning rate throughout training, facilitating smoother convergence and improved optimization stability. To enhance model generalization, a layer drop (LayerDrop) strategy was incorporated, in which Transformer encoder layers were randomly skipped during training. The maximum number of training epochs was set to 100, and an early stopping criterion was applied such that training was terminated if the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) on the validation set did not improve for 10 consecutive epochs. The model checkpoint achieving the highest validation MCC was retained for final evaluation. To prevent gradient explosion and improve numerical stability, gradient clipping was applied after each training epoch by constraining the L2 norm of all parameter gradients to a maximum value of 1.

Model evaluation

Following training, model performance was evaluated on the independent test set using a comprehensive set of classification metrics. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was used to assess the model’s overall discriminative capability between sensitive and resistant compounds. Accuracy (Acc) quantified the proportion of correctly classified samples. The Matthews correlation coefficient was employed as a primary metric to account for class imbalance and prediction bias, providing a robust measure of classification quality. Additional performance metrics included the F1 score (F1), which balances precision and recall; precision (Pre), representing the proportion of correctly predicted positive samples; sensitivity (Se), measuring the ability to correctly identify resistant compounds; and specificity (Sp), reflecting the ability to correctly identify sensitive compounds. The Youden index was calculated to provide an integrated measure of diagnostic effectiveness, summarizing the model’s ability to discriminate between sensitive and resistant compounds.

To compare performance with traditional machine learning methods, Random Forest (RF) and XGBoost models based on ECFP4 fingerprints were trained on each training set using the Scikit-learn machine learning toolkit. RF is a decision‑tree‑based ensemble algorithm that improves model stability and generalization by constructing multiple independent decision trees and aggregating their predictions via voting [29]. During training, random seeds were set to 6, 18, 24, 32, 48 and 64. A grid search strategy with fivefold cross‑validation was employed to explore combinations of three hyperparameters: the number of trees (10, 30, 50, 70, 90), the maximum number of leaf nodes (20, 30, 50), and the maximum number of features considered for each split (1, 10, 20). XGBoost is an ensemble learning method based on the gradient boosting framework that sequentially builds additive decision trees and incorporates regularization to enhance predictive accuracy and robustness [30]. For XGBoost, the random seeds were set to 6, 18, 24, 32, 48 and 64, and a grid search with fivefold cross‑validation was performed over the following hyperparameter combinations: learning rate (0.1, 0.01), number of boosting rounds (10, 30, 50, 70, 90), maximum tree depth (2, 3, 4), subsample ratio of training instances (0.3, 0.5), and subsample ratio of features per tree (0.3, 0.5). After training, the performance of both traditional machine learning models was evaluated on the test sets generated using the same random seeds as the corresponding training sets, with exactly the same set of metrics applied. Subsequently, the best-performing model from each of the two model types was selected for systematic comparison with the Transformer model.

Virtual screening

Based on the combined assessment of generalization ability and classification performance, the optimal model was selected for downstream virtual screening. To maximize chemical space coverage and enhance hit diversity, two complementary compound libraries were screened including FDA-approved drug database containing 20,000 [31] and the Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) database containing ~61,966 unique herbal ingredients [32]. The FDA-approved drug library comprises clinically validated molecules with established safety and pharmacokinetic profiles, offering strong potential for drug repurposing. In contrast, the TCM database provides structurally diverse natural products and herbal-derived compounds, which are well recognized as rich sources of bioactive chemical scaffolds. The combined use of these two libraries enabled comprehensive exploration of chemically and biologically relevant space for identifying potential inhibitors targeting Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking was performed to predict the binding conformations and interaction profiles of screened compounds against the RpsAΔ438 mutant. The crystal structure of Mycobacterium tuberculosis RpsA (PDB ID: 4NNI) [33] was downloaded from the Protein Data Bank and processed in PyMOL [34]. The alanine residue at position 438 was deleted to generate the RpsAΔ438 mutant, followed by structural refinement and optimization using energy minimization to relieve local steric clashes. Protein preparation included the removal of crystallographic water molecules beyond 5 Å from the binding pocket, addition of polar hydrogens, optimization of protonation states at pH 7.4, and assignment of partial charges. Ligand structures were prepared using RDKit, where salts were removed, tautomer standardized, and MMFF94 charges [35] assigned. Ligand conformers were generated using ETKDG sampling [36] to ensure optimal exploration of conformational space. Docking was conducted using AutoDock Vina 1.2.3 [37], which incorporates expanded scoring function terms, AutoDock4 atom types, and improved local search performance compared with earlier versions. The docking grid was centered on the known POA-binding region of RpsA, covering key residues Lys303, Phe307, and Phe310. Exhaustiveness was set to 32, and the top-ranked binding conformations were selected based on binding affinity and interaction plausibility. All docking poses were visually inspected to confirm hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, π–π stacking, and salt bridges relevant to the biological mechanism.

Molecular dynamics simulation

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were carried out to evaluate dynamic behavior of RpsAΔ438–ligand complexes. All simulations were performed using Amber v.24 with the GPU-accelerated PMEMD engine [38]. The ff19SB force field [39] was used for protein parameters, while ligand parameters were generated using the General Amber Force Field 2 (GAFF2) with AM1-BCC charges assigned using Antechamber [40]. Each system was solvated in an OPC water box with a minimum buffer distance of 14 Å from the solute. Appropriate counterions (Na+/Cl−) were added to ensure system neutrality. Energy minimization was performed in two stages: initially, the protein–ligand complex was restrained to relax solvent molecules and ions, followed by an unrestrained minimization of the entire system to remove residual steric clashes. The systems were gradually heated from 0 to 310 K over 200 ps under the NVT ensemble. This was followed by 500 ps of density equilibration under NPT conditions at 1 atm. Positional restraints were progressively reduced during equilibration to allow controlled relaxation of the system. Subsequently, 400 ns production MD simulations were carried out under NPT conditions using a 2-fs integration timestep. Long-range electrostatic interactions were treated using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method [41], and trajectory coordinates were recorded every 10 ps. Temperature was maintained using Langevin dynamics, while pressure was controlled using the Berendsen barostat. To enhance conformational sampling of the flexible RpsAΔ438 binding pocket, Gaussian accelerated molecular dynamics (GaMD) simulations [42], were performed following conventional MD equilibration. GaMD improves sampling efficiency by applying a harmonic boost potential to smooth the system’s potential energy surface, thereby facilitating the exploration of otherwise inaccessible conformational states while allowing recovery of the free-energy landscape through reweighting. Dual-boost GaMD (igamd = 3) was employed, applying boost potentials to both the dihedral and total potential energy terms. The threshold energy for both boosts was set to the lower bound (E = Vmax), and harmonic force constants were adaptively determined with upper limits of σ0P = 6.0 kcal/mol and σ0D = 6.0 kcal/mol for the total and dihedral boosts, respectively, following established protocols for protein–ligand systems [43]. The averaging interval for potential statistics was set to ntave = 50,000 steps. All trajectories were processed using CPPTRAJ. Structural and dynamic analyses included root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (Rg), hydrogen bond analysis, solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), and principal component analysis (PCA). Binding free energies were calculated using the MM/GBSA method [44] across 500 snapshots extracted from the equilibrated portions of the trajectories.

Drug-likeness and ADME/T profile

The drug-likeness properties of the compounds were assessed using the SwissADME server (http://www.swissadme.ch/index.php), based on Lipinski’s rule of five (RO5). Key parameters including the number of hydrogen-bond donors, hydrogen-bond acceptors, and the Lead likeness score were extracted. Subsequently, comprehensive ADME/T (Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion, and Toxicity) properties, including physicochemical descriptors, cardiac safety, hepatotoxicity, genotoxicity, skin sensitization, carcinogenicity, organ-specific toxicities, and cell-based cytotoxicity, were predicted using ADMETlab 3.0 (https://admetlab3.scbdd.com/). These evaluations provide critical insights into the drug-likeness and ADME/T of the top compounds, essential for ensuring that the identified candidates possess genuine clinical potential.

Results

A computational workflow was designed to identify potential RpsAΔ438 inhibitors by integrating Transformer model, virtual screening, molecular docking, and Gaussian accelerated molecular dynamics (GaMD) simulations. Transformer model was carried out using transparent and reproducible strategies, with binary classification labels unambiguously defined based on the MIC values of compounds against Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Transformer-based models were trained using SMILES representations of the compounds as input, with repeated stratified splits of 80/10/10 allocated to the training, validation, and testing sets, respectively. Model robustness and generalization were evaluated using multiple performance metrics. The best-performing model was subsequently applied to virtually screen compounds from FDA-approved drugs and traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) databases. Top-ranked candidates were then subjected to molecular docking, and their binding stability and conformational dynamics were further assessed using GaMD simulations. Classification threshold determination for Mycobacterium tuberculosis

The MIC values of the 12,756 Mycobacterium tuberculosis-related compounds ranged from 0.0002 to 3125 μg/mL, with a median of 12.50 μg/mL and a mean of 40.81 μg/mL. Due to the presence of extreme values, constructing a regression model would make the training process highly sensitive to outliers, potentially causing predictions to deviate from the general trends of most samples. In contrast, a binary classification model, which only determines whether a compound is sensitive or resistant, is less affected by such extremes. Therefore, a binary classification strategy was deemed more suitable for this dataset. The MIC distribution exhibits a pronounced right skew, as indicated by the mean being greater than the median. Consequently, the median MIC value was selected as the classification threshold to distinguish sensitive and resistant compounds. The median provides robustness against outliers and skewed distributions while maintaining clear biological interpretability based on experimentally measured activity. Compounds with MIC values ≥12.50 μg/mL were defined as resistant (label = 0), totaling 6645 compounds, whereas those with MIC values <12.50 μg/mL were defined as sensitive (label = 1), totaling 6111 compounds. This thresholding approach yielded a balanced dataset suitable for supervised learning. For visualization, MIC values were transformed to the –log10 scale (−log1012.50 ≈ −1.10; −log1040.81 ≈ −1.61). As shown in Fig. 2c and d, using the median MIC value as the classification threshold effectively partitions the compounds into balanced sensitive and resistant classes.

Chemical space distribution across independent dataset splits

Following compound labeling, the dataset was partitioned into training, validation, and test subsets using stratified sampling with an approximate ratio of 8:1:1. Stratification was performed based on compound labels to preserve class proportions across all subsets. To mitigate potential bias introduced by a single random split and to assess the stability of data partitioning, this procedure was independently repeated six times using different random seeds (8, 16, 24, 32, 48, and 64). Across all six repetitions, each training set contained 10,332 compounds (4950 sensitive and 5382 resistant), each validation set comprised 1148 compounds (550 sensitive and 598 resistant), and each test set included 1276 compounds (611 sensitive and 665 resistant).

To examine whether the different subsets maintained comparable chemical characteristics, chemical space analysis was performed using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE). High-dimensional 166-bit MACCS fingerprints were projected into a two-dimensional space, and the resulting distributions for the training, validation, and test sets corresponding to each random split are shown in Fig. 3a–f. In all six projections, the training sets (blue points) form a broad and continuous distribution, reflecting the overall chemical diversity of the curated dataset. Importantly, the validation (orange points) and test (green points) compounds are consistently interspersed within the same regions of chemical space, rather than forming isolated clusters or occupying distinct regions. The close spatial overlap observed across all splits indicates that the validation and test subsets are chemically representative of the corresponding training sets. This consistency suggests that differences in model performance across splits are not driven by systematic chemical space mismatches but instead reflect genuine variations arising from model training and optimization. Moreover, the reproducibility of these patterns across six independent random seeds demonstrates that the stratified splitting strategy yields stable and well-balanced partitions, reducing the likelihood of split-dependent artifacts. Overall, the t-SNE analysis confirms that the adopted dataset splitting protocol preserves chemical space continuity between training, validation, and test subsets across multiple independent partitions. Performance evaluation of the Transformer models

To rigorously assess model stability and generalization, the curated dataset was partitioned into training, validation, and test sets using six independent stratified splits. For each split, the top three hyperparameter configurations identified through Optuna optimization based on mean cross-validated Matthew’s correlation coefficient (MCC) was selected for model training. This strategy resulted in a total of 18 independently trained Transformer models (6 data splits × 3 hyperparameter ranks), enabling a comprehensive evaluation of performance variability arising from both data partitioning and architectural differences. Model performance was evaluated on the independent test sets using multiple complementary metrics, including the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy (Acc), Matthew’s correlation coefficient (MCC), F1 score (F1), precision (Pre), sensitivity (Se), specificity (Sp), and the Youden index. To facilitate clear comparison, models were denoted using the notation Seed_x_Rank_y, where Seed_x indicates the random seed used for dataset partitioning and Rank_y corresponds to the ranking of the hyperparameter configuration within that split based on cross-validated MCC. For example, Seed_8_Rank_1 represents the model trained using the best-performing hyperparameter configuration for the data split generated with random seed 8.

As summarized in Fig. 4, all 18 Transformer models demonstrated strong discriminative capability, with test-set AUC values exceeding 0.720. This consistently high AUC indicates reliable separation between sensitive and resistant compounds across all data splits and hyperparameter configurations. The highest AUC values were observed for Seed_8_Rank_1 (0.844), Seed_24_Rank_2 (0.834), and Seed_8_Rank_2 (0.832), reflecting particularly effective ranking of compounds by predicted sensitivity. Accuracy values further supported the robustness of the models, with all but one configuration (Seed_32_Rank_1, 0.659) achieving accuracies above 0.710. The best-performing models in terms of accuracy were Seed_8_Rank_1 (0.772), Seed_24_Rank_2 (0.763), and Seed_16_Rank_1 (0.762), indicating close agreement between predicted and true class labels. Importantly, MCC values, which provide a balanced assessment of classification quality under potential class imbalance, exceeded 0.410 for all models except Seed_32_Rank_1 (0.316). The highest MCC values were obtained for Seed_8_Rank_1 (0.543), Seed_24_Rank_2 (0.524), and Seed_16_Rank_1 (0.522), confirming strong correlation between predicted and true labels. F1 scores, which reflect the harmonic balance between precision and sensitivity, were consistently high across models, exceeding 0.680 for all configurations except Seed_32_Rank_1 (0.613). The highest F1 scores were achieved by Seed_8_Rank_1 (0.759), Seed_24_Rank_2 (0.753), Seed_16_Rank_1 (0.743), and Seed_32_Rank_3 (0.743), indicating effective trade-offs between false positives and false negatives. Precision values were generally above 0.710, with Seed_8_Rank_1, Seed_16_Rank_1, and Seed_24_Rank_1 achieving the highest precision (up to 0.768), demonstrating high reliability in identifying sensitive compounds. Sensitivity values, which measure the ability to correctly identify sensitive compounds (positive class), exceeded 0.600 for all models except Seed_32_Rank_1 (0.563). The highest sensitivity was observed for Seed_24_Rank_2 (0.755), followed by Seed_8_Rank_1 (0.750) and Seed_32_Rank_3 (0.730). In contrast, specificity values, reflecting the ability to correctly identify resistant compounds (negative class), were consistently high across all models, exceeding 0.730. The strongest specificity was achieved by Seed_64_Rank_2 (0.844), Seed_24_Rank_3 (0.814), and Seed_24_Rank_1 (0.806). These results indicate that while some models favored sensitivity, others emphasized specificity, underscoring the importance of balanced metric consideration during model selection. The Youden index, which integrates sensitivity and specificity into a single measure of diagnostic effectiveness, exceeded 0.371 for all models. The highest Youden index values were observed for Seed_8_Rank_3 (0.585), Seed_48_Rank_3 (0.558), and Seed_48_Rank_1 (0.540), further highlighting metric-dependent performance differences among models.Fig. 4 Performance comparison of 18 Transformer models across six independent stratified data splits (a-f), showing test-set AUC, accuracy, MCC, F1 score, precision, sensitivity, specificity, and Youden index for the top three hyperparameter configurations per split

Model generalization and performance stability of Seed_8_Rank_1

A representative molecule, CHEMBL523618, was randomly selected from the independent test set, and its chemical structure is shown in Supplementary Figure 4. To interpret how the Transformer model processes molecular information, the average self-attention weight matrices across the six encoder layers of the selected model (Seed_8_Rank_1) were visualized and are presented in Fig. 5.Fig. 5Average attention heatmaps of the six Transformer encoder layers (a-f) of Seed_8_Rank_1 for a representative test-set molecule (CHEMBL523618). Average attention heatmaps of the six Transformer encoder layers (a-f) of Seed_8_Rank_1 for a representative test-set molecule (CHEMBL523618). Color intensity represents the relative attention weights between query and key tokens

In the first encoder layer (Fig. 5a), attention weights were broadly distributed across atoms, indicating an initial exploratory encoding stage. Nevertheless, distinct patterns were already evident. Elevated attention was observed between negatively charged oxygen atoms (O−) and aliphatic carbon atoms (C), suggesting that the model began capturing relationships between charged functional groups and the molecular backbone. In addition, increased attention among fluorine (F) and chlorine (Cl) atoms indicated early recognition of halogen substitution patterns, reflecting sensitivity to chemically similar atom types even in the absence of direct bonding relationships. In the second encoder layer (Fig. 5b), attention became more structured while retaining key interactions observed in the first layer. In particular, positively charged aromatic nitrogen atoms (n+) exhibited increased attention with aliphatic nitrogen atoms (N) and chlorine atoms, indicating that the model could distinguish nitrogen atoms across different charge states and chemical environments. Concurrently, interactions involving aliphatic carbon and nitrogen atoms became more prominent, reflecting a gradual shift toward chemically meaningful grouping of heteroatoms and backbone features.

By the third encoder layer (Fig. 5c), the attention distribution exhibited clear structural organization. Stronger attention was allocated between the molecular backbone and neutral oxygen atoms (O), suggesting integration of backbone connectivity with polar functional groups. Notably, a pronounced increase in attention toward negatively charged oxygen atoms (O−) was observed across nearly all query positions, indicating that these atoms had begun to function as central reference points in the learned molecular representation. This trend intensified in the fourth encoder layer (Fig. 5d), where attention became more concentrated and globally coherent. Nearly all atoms showed increased attention toward O− atoms compared with previous layers, indicating that negatively charged oxygen atoms had emerged as dominant attention centers. Through this consistent weighting, the model effectively integrated information related to molecular charge distribution and polarity, which are critical determinants of intermolecular interactions and biological activity.

In the fifth and sixth encoder layers (Fig. 5e, f), attention toward O− atoms reached its maximum and became highly uniform across the molecule. All atoms exhibited strongly enhanced attention weights directed toward these charged oxygen atoms, forming a stable and coherent global attention pattern. This behavior indicates that the deeper encoder layers consolidate chemically salient features, enabling fine-grained integration of global electronic and structural properties rather than focusing on isolated local interactions.

Overall, the attention analysis reveals a stable and hierarchical feature-learning process within the Seed_8_Rank_1 Transformer model. Early encoder layers primarily capture local atomic relationships and chemically similar atom types, intermediate layers integrate multi-atom structural information with increasing emphasis on polar functional groups, and deeper layers establish global attention centered on charged oxygen atoms. This progressive abstraction demonstrates that the model does not rely on incidental or spurious features, but instead learns chemically meaningful representations that reflect both local structure and global electronic characteristics. Such hierarchical attention behavior supports the reliability of the learned molecular embeddings and provides mechanistic confidence in the model’s suitability for downstream virtual screening and structure-guided analyses.

Virtual screening and molecular docking

The optimal Transformer model identified from hyperparameter optimization (Seed_8_Rank_1) was selected for large-scale virtual screening based on its stable performance and favorable balance between training and validation metrics. This model was applied to two chemically and biologically complementary compound libraries: the FDA-approved drug database and the Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) compound database. The FDA database offers compounds with established clinical safety profiles, while the TCM database provides structurally diverse natural products and herbal-derived molecules. Using model-predicted sensitivity probabilities, 317 compounds from the FDA database and 1757 compounds from the TCM database were classified as potentially active against Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Compounds within each database were subsequently ranked according to their predicted probabilities, and the top 50 FDA compounds and top 100 TCM compounds were selected for structure-based molecular docking against the RpsAΔ438 mutant.

Molecular docking was performed to evaluate binding orientations, interaction patterns, and relative binding affinities within the POA-binding pocket of RpsAΔ438. The 2D structures of the screened compounds and their interaction with target protein are shown in Supplementary Figures 5 and 6. Notably, the reference ligand contacts identical key residues Arg357, Lys303, and Glu318 in both wild-type and RpsAΔ438, confirming that the A438 deletion preserves the binding site identity while fundamentally destabilizing its dynamic character, consistent with Zhi et al. The wild-type RpsA system maintains stable RMSD values fluctuating between ~1.5 and 3.0 Å throughout the entire 400 ns trajectory, reflecting the structural integrity of the intact C-terminal pocket and establishing a dynamic baseline for evaluating the conformational consequences of the A438 deletion. In contrast, the reference complex displays noticeably higher RMSD fluctuations, with values rising to ~4.0–5.0 Å during the mid-simulation period (150–250 ns), followed by partial stabilization at ~3.0 Å. This elevated deviation relative to both the wild-type and apo systems directly reflects deletion-induced pocket destabilization, confirming that the A438 deletion compromises stable ligand engagement rather than abolishing the binding site. Notably, the RMSF profile of Z2568748600 most closely recapitulates the constrained flexibility pattern of wild-type RpsA, providing strong evidence that this compound effectively restores the residue-level stability compromised by the A438 deletion. Overall, the RMSF analysis reveals that several screened compounds effectively reduce local flexibility within the C-terminal region of RpsAΔ438 compared with the reference ligand. In particular, Z2568748600 and Z1511483977 demonstrate substantial suppression of residue-level fluctuations in structurally and functionally critical regions, corroborating the RMSD results and docking interaction profiles. These findings provide mechanistic insight into how selected compounds stabilize the deletion-altered binding pocket and support their prioritization for further dynamic and energetic analyses.

MM/GBSA binding free energy analysis

To quantitatively compare the binding affinities of the reference ligand and selected screened compounds toward RpsAΔ438, MM/GBSA total binding free energies (ΔG_TOTAL) were calculated using snapshots extracted from the equilibrated regions of the 400 ns molecular dynamics trajectories. The resulting ΔG_TOTAL values are summarized in Table 4 and illustrated in Fig. 13. To provide a structural energetic baseline, the MM/GBSA binding free energy of the reference ligand bound to wild-type RpsA was also calculated, yielding a ΔG_TOTAL of −23.85 ± 3.00 kJ/mol. This value reflects the energetic stabilization achievable within the intact C-terminal pocket and serves as a direct reference for quantifying the energetic consequence of the A438 deletion. The reference complex (4NNI-reference) exhibited a relatively weak binding affinity, with a ΔG_TOTAL of −13.03 ± 2.66 kJ/mol, indicating limited energetic stabilization within the deletion-altered binding pocket. The marked reduction in binding free energy relative to the wild-type complex (ΔΔG = ~10.82 kJ/mol) directly quantifies the energetic penalty imposed by the A438 deletion, confirming that deletion-induced conformational destabilization substantially compromises ligand binding thermodynamics. In contrast, all screened compounds demonstrated substantially more favorable total binding free energies, confirming improved binding strength compared with the reference ligand in the mutant. Importantly, both TCM26045 (−32.04 kJ/mol) and Z2568748600 (−26.00 kJ/mol) exceed the wild-type binding free energy (−23.85 kJ/mol), demonstrating that these compounds not only compensate for the deletion-induced energetic penalty but achieve superior binding thermodynamics relative to the native POA–RpsA interaction. Overall, the MM/GBSA total binding free energy results clearly demonstrate that all selected screened compounds bind more favorably to RpsAΔ438 than the reference ligand.

Drug-likeness and ADME/T analysis

A comprehensive in silico ADMET and toxicity profiling was performed for the six prioritized compounds, covering physicochemical properties, cardiac safety, hepatotoxicity, genotoxicity, skin sensitization, carcinogenicity, organ-specific toxicities, and cell-based cytotoxicity. The complete predictions are listed in Supplementary Table 1. All six compounds displayed acceptable drug-likeness parameters: molecular weights ranged from 435 to 547 Da, hydrogen-bond acceptors from 7 to 13, hydrogen-bond donors from 3 to 7, and logP values from −0.61 to 4.26. From a safety standpoint, the overall profile was reassuring. None of the compounds carried a positive Ames mutagenicity alert, and endocrine disruption (EC) probabilities were uniformly zero. Carcinogenicity, neurotoxicity, ototoxicity, nephrotoxicity, and genotoxicity predictions were generally manageable across the set, and cytotoxicity against RPMI-8226, A549, and HEK293 cells was uniformly low. Certain alerts were confined to individual compounds: TCM26045 showed a notably elevated hERG signal (0.73 and 0.52 at 10 µM), whereas TCM36252, TCM46589, and Z2235802088 each had a maximal DILI risk (1.00). Respiratory toxicity was broadly elevated except for Z1511483977 (0.53), and eye irritation (EI) was pronounced for TCM36252 (0.83) and Z2568748600 (0.88). Despite these scattered signals, no compound exhibited a universally unfavorable profile.

Among all candidates, Z2568748600 consistently delivered the most balanced performance. Its cardiac safety was excellent (hERG = 0.06, hERG (10 µM) = 0.18), its DILI risk (0.96) was high but not maximal and was lower than that of three other compounds, and its neurotoxicity (0.86), hepatotoxicity (0.72), nephrotoxicity (0.87), and genotoxicity (1.00) predictions all remained within acceptable boundaries. Although it carried a relatively high eye irritation score (0.88) and moderate skin sensitization (0.70), these liabilities were offset by its otherwise strong safety profile. Cellular cytotoxicity was minimal (RPMI-8226 = 0.02, A549 = 0.02, HEK293 = 0.16), further confirming a favorable selectivity window. When integrated with its previously demonstrated superior binding affinity, dynamic stability, and drug-likeness, these toxicological findings reinforce the selection of Z2568748600 as the best-in-class lead for further experimental validation.

Discussion

Pyrazinamide (PZA) remains a cornerstone of first-line tuberculosis (TB) therapy due to its unique sterilizing activity and ability to shorten treatment duration. However, the emergence of PZA resistance has increasingly compromised treatment efficacy, particularly in multidrug-resistant and extensively drug-resistant Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb). While mutations in pncA represent the dominant resistance mechanism, accumulating clinical and structural evidence has established ribosomal protein S1 (RpsA) as a bona fide target of pyrazinoic acid (POA), with alterations in its C-terminal region directly impairing drug binding and trans-translation inhibition. Among these alterations, the clinically observed A438 deletion induces pronounced conformational flexibility and pocket remodeling, rendering POA and POA-like scaffolds ineffective. These features make RpsAΔ438 a particularly challenging but highly relevant target for next-generation anti-tuberculosis drug discovery.

In this study, we present an integrated artificial intelligence and molecular simulation framework to address this challenge. By combining a Transformer-based deep learning model with structure-based docking, long-timescale molecular dynamics (MD), enhanced sampling, and MM/GBSA binding free energy analysis, we systematically identified and validated compounds capable of stabilizing the mutation-altered RpsAΔ438 binding pocket. Recent studies have highlighted the limitations of traditional QSAR and fingerprint-based models for antimicrobial discovery, particularly for targets exhibiting high conformational plasticity or mutation-driven resistance [46, 47]. In contrast, Transformer architectures, which leverage self-attention mechanisms and SMILES-based molecular representations, have demonstrated superior performance in capturing long-range dependencies and chemically meaningful context [48, 49]. Our results, which show that our Transformer model outperformed both random forest and XGBoost models, align with recent reports indicating that Transformer-based models surpass conventional machine-learning approaches in large, heterogeneous bioactivity datasets and offer improved generalization for prospective virtual screening [50, 51].

The Transformer model developed here exhibited robust and reproducible performance across six independent stratified dataset splits, with consistently high AUC and MCC values and limited sensitivity to hyperparameter variation. Importantly, attention-based interpretability analysis revealed a chemically coherent hierarchical learning process. Early encoder layers captured local atomic relationships and chemically similar atom types, whereas deeper layers progressively focused on charged oxygen atoms and polar functional groups. This behavior mirrors recent findings in interpretable molecular Transformers, where deeper layers encode global electronic and pharmacophoric features rather than superficial structural motifs [52, 53]. Such hierarchical attention patterns provide confidence that model predictions are driven by chemically meaningful representations rather than dataset artifacts, a key concern in AI-driven drug discovery. Applying the optimized model to FDA-approved and Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) libraries enabled efficient exploration of both clinically validated and structurally diverse chemical space. Several top-ranked compounds exhibited docking poses that re-established critical interactions with residues such as Lys303, Glu318 and Arg357, which are central to ligand recognition in the RpsA C-terminal domain. Notably, many of these interactions were absent or weakened in the reference POA-bound system, consistent with previous structural studies demonstrating the destabilizing effect of the A438 deletion [12]. These docking results suggested that non-POA-like scaffolds may better accommodate the altered pocket geometry, supporting recent proposals that resistance-breaking TB inhibitors must depart from classical substrate-mimetic designs. Simultaneously, the attention heatmaps of the top-ranked compounds revealed that the atoms assigned high importance by the Transformer model were in close spatial agreement with those involved in the key three-dimensional binding interactions identified through docking. This convergence between computationally learned attention patterns and physically docked poses demonstrates that the model is capable of recognizing critical atoms involved in protein–ligand contacts, highlighting its reliability in capturing chemically meaningful features from molecular structure alone.

Long-timescale MD and Gaussian accelerated MD simulations provided dynamic validation of these predictions. Compared with the reference complex, several screened compounds markedly reduced backbone deviation and residue-level flexibility in the mutation-affected C-terminal region. In particular, Z2568748600 consistently stabilized helices and loop regions proximal to the deletion site, as evidenced by reduced RMSD, suppressed RMSF, compact principal component distributions, and persistent hydrogen-bond networks. These findings are consistent with recent MD-based investigations of drug-resistant Mtb targets, which emphasize that binding stability and resistance circumvention cannot be inferred from docking alone but require explicit treatment of protein dynamics and solvent effects [54, 55]. MM/GBSA total binding free energy analysis further supported the prioritization of these compounds. All screened candidates exhibited substantially more favorable total binding free energies than the reference ligand, indicating enhanced energetic stabilization of RpsAΔ438.

Importantly, in silico ADMET and toxicity profiling revealed that Z2568748600 possesses a highly favorable safety and drug-likeness profile. It exhibited excellent cardiac safety, with low hERG and hERG-10 µM probabilities, and a DILI risk that, while elevated, was not maximal and compared favorably to other candidates in the set. Its neurotoxicity, hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, and genotoxicity predictions all remained within acceptable ranges, and cellular cytotoxicity against RPMI-8226, A549, and HEK293 cells was minimal, confirming a wide selectivity margin. Although a moderate skin sensitization signal and a relatively high eye irritation score were noted, these isolated cosmetic liabilities do not diminish its overall strong toxicological standing. These favourable properties align seamlessly with its previously demonstrated superior target engagement, dynamic stability, hydrogen-bond lifetimes, conformational stability, and binding free energy. Such cross-method agreement strengthens confidence in these hits and reflects best practices increasingly advocated in contemporary computational drug discovery literature.

From a translational perspective, the integration of FDA-approved compounds offers immediate opportunities for drug repurposing, potentially accelerating preclinical development timelines. Meanwhile, TCM-derived compounds provide novel scaffolds that expand chemical diversity beyond existing TB therapeutics. Importantly, the ability of selected compounds to stabilize the RpsAΔ438 pocket suggests a viable strategy for overcoming PZA resistance by directly targeting trans-translation rather than upstream activation pathways. Given the essential role of trans-translation in bacterial stress survival, such inhibitors may retain efficacy under hostile intracellular conditions where conventional antibiotics fail. Despite these promising results, several limitations should be acknowledged. MM/GBSA provides approximate binding free energies and does not fully capture entropic contributions or long-range cooperative effects. Moreover, in silico predictions require experimental validation to confirm biochemical inhibition of RpsA-mediated trans-translation and whole-cell activity against PZA-resistant Mtb strains. Nonetheless, recent studies have demonstrated strong concordance between integrated AI–MD pipelines and experimental outcomes, supporting the use of this approach as an efficient hypothesis-generation engine [56, 57]. In summary, this study demonstrates that Transformer-accelerated virtual screening, when coupled with rigorous dynamic and energetic validation, provides a powerful and scalable framework for identifying inhibitors against mutation-driven drug resistance in tuberculosis. By targeting the RpsAΔ438, we highlight a rational path toward next-generation anti-TB agents capable of restoring therapeutic efficacy in PZA-resistant infections. The strategies and insights presented here are broadly applicable to other antimicrobial resistance targets characterized by high conformational flexibility and underscore the growing role of interpretable deep learning in modern drug discovery.

Conclusions

This study successfully established a robust and reproducible drug discovery pipeline integrating Transformer-based models, molecular docking, Gaussian accelerated molecular dynamics (GaMD) simulations and MM/GBSA free energy calculations to identify lead compounds effectively targeting the pyrazinamide-resistant Mycobacterium tuberculosis mutant protein RpsAΔ438. Utilizing carefully prepared training data and repeated modeling, a Transformer model with high AUC and accuracy was developed, enabling effective discrimination between compounds active against drug-sensitive and drug-resistant Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Comparative analysis with standard machine learning models (random forest and XGBoost) confirmed the superior predictive performance of the Transformer architecture, underscoring the value of deep learning for virtual screening. By applying this model for virtual screening of FDA and traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) compound libraries, followed by molecular docking and 400 ns GaMD simulations, compound Z2568748600 was identified as exhibiting strong binding affinity and conformational stability with the RpsAΔ438. Furthermore, in silico ADMET profiling revealed that Z2568748600 possesses favorable drug-likeness and a balanced safety profile, underscored by excellent cardiac safety and minimal cellular cytotoxicity. These findings highlight the value of integrating data-driven and structure-based modeling approaches to accelerate drug repurposing efforts. The proposed pipeline not only identifies promising RpsAΔ438 inhibitors with established safety profiles but also offers a scalable strategy applicable to other key resistance targets in pyrazinamide-resistant Mycobacterium tuberculosis research. Furthermore, the prioritized candidate warrants further experimental validation to confirm its bioactivity, selectivity, and therapeutic efficacy.
