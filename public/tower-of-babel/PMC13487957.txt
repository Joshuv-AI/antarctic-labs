Effectiveness of out-of-hospital cardiopulmonary resuscitation in Nigeria: an evaluation of key components in the chain of survival
Authors: Israel Kayode Kolawole, Lukman Olajide Abdur-Rahman, Shehu Abubakar Kana, Aisha Oluwabunmi Ajala, Akinyemi Aje, Olubusola Temitope Alagbe-Briggs, Yekeen Ayodele Ayoola, Nkeiruka Chigekwu Mbadiwe, Hadiza Shehu Galadanci, Josephus Kayode Ladipo, Dalhat Salahu, Arinola Adeyoola Sanusi, James Ayodele Ogunmodede, Tonia Chinyelu Onyeka, Tanimu Sambo Yusuf, Olaitan A. ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe ‘’Chain of Survival’’ (CoS), provides a structure to evaluate an emergency response system for out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) in any community. This CoS has not been previously evaluated in an African population. The study aims to assess the effectiveness and efficiency of OHCA resuscitation by evaluating the components of the CoS following sudden cardiac arrest in Nigeria.MethodologyThis was a prospective multicenter descriptive cross-sectional exploratory survey of epidemiology and outcome of OHCA in adult Nigerians. The survey was conducted in six university teaching hospitals in the 6 geopolitical zones of Nigeria. Data was analyzed using IBM-SPSS version 25.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA, and presented using tables. Continuous variables were summarized using means and standard deviation, or median and interquartile range (IQR), depending on the normality of the data. Primary outcome measures were return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), survival to hospital admission and discharge.ResultsA total of 387 OHCA patients aged between 18 and 115 years met the inclusion criteria. They consisted of 62.5% males and 37.5% females, giving a male/female ratio of approximately 1.7:1. Most (55.6%) of the arrests occurred at home. Ninety-one-point 7% (91.7%) of the arrests were witnessed. Calls were made to ambulance services but only in a third (34.6%) did an ambulance arrive. The time taken for the ambulance to arrive ranged between 10 min and 60 min, with a median of 15 min (IQR 12.5–45). Pre-hospital resuscitation was attempted in 15.2%. Pre-hospital ROSC was achieved in 3.6% of all the OHCA events, but only 1.8% survived to hospital admission and were eventually discharged.ConclusionThe emergency response service to OHCA in Nigeria is very poor due to weaknesses in the critical components of the CoS. The shortcomings observed call for well-organized, coordinated deliberate efforts to strengthen pre-hospital chain of care.

Introduction

An OHCA is defined as cessation of cardiac mechanical activity that occurs outside of the hospital setting and is confirmed by the absence of signs of circulation [1]. It remains, a major public health challenge worldwide, with survival ranging between 2 and 11% [2, 3].

CoS describes a series of sequentially connected interdependent links of critical interventions designed to reduce the mortality associated with cardiac arrest. The concept was proposed in the 1992 CPR guidelines and provides a structure to evaluate an emergency response system for OHCA patients in any community for optimal outcome [4]. The six structural components of the CoS include: early recognition and access to emergency medical care, early cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), early defibrillation, advanced cardiac life support (ACLS), integrated post-resuscitative care, and long-term rehabilitation. Strict adherence to the CoS is necessary for optimal outcome. However, international resuscitation guidelines are usually based on scientific recommendations, which are formulated typically from the perspective of well-resourced environment, with little consideration of applicability in the Low-and middle-income countries (LMIC).

Nigeria is the most populous country in Africa, and the most populous black nation in the World [5], with an estimated 2026 population exceeding 240 million [6]. The country, by the World Bank classification, is a LMIC, highly diverse socio-culturally and has a very young demographic (median age ~ 18 years), with life expectancy at birth of 55.0 years and a high infant mortality rate of 66.9 deaths per 1,000 live births [6, 7]. The quality of public health care system is inadequate nationally, and disparities exist across different geopolitical zones within the country. OHCA remains a major public health crisis, and regional studies show high mortality, often linked to undiagnosed hypertension and limited emergency care, however, a definitive national incidence rate is unknown due to poor record-keeping [8]. The public emergency ambulance services system in Nigeria, operated by the National Emergency Medical Services and Ambulance System (NEMSAS), is still evolving and are for now concentrated mainly in major urban centers, with limited access in rural communities [9]. In addition, the ambulances are poorly equipped, and the system is bedeviled with shortage of trained personnel and poor road networks in some cities, which significantly delay response times.

This is the first large scale prospective multi-center study in Nigeria, that have assessed the effectiveness and efficiency of the components of CoS in OHCA resuscitation, reflecting the complex interplay of resources, setting and bystander engagement, on the clinical outcomes of OHCA. The aim was to assess performance and identify gaps in the components of the CoS.

Methods

Study design

This was a prospective multicenter descriptive cross-sectional exploratory survey of epidemiology and outcome of OHCA in adult Nigerians.

Study setting

Nigeria is a multi-ethnic, multi religious and multi-cultural society, officially subdivided into six geopolitical zones. This study was conducted in six purposefully selected university teaching hospitals in the six geopolitical zones (Fig. 1. Map of Nigeria showing the distribution of participating hospitals in the six geopolitical zones). These are the main referral hospitals providing inpatient and outpatient services, as well as emergency and critical care services in their respective geopolitical zones. The hospitals are, University of Ilorin Teaching Hospital, Ilorin (North Central), Aminu Kano Teaching Hospital, Kano (North-West), University College Hospital, Ibadan (South-West), Gombe State University Teaching Hospital, Gombe (North-East), University of Nigeria Teaching Hospital, Ituku-Ozalla, Enugu (South-East) and University of Port-Harcourt Teaching Hospital, Port-Harcourt (South-South).

Participants

All consecutive adults aged ⩾ 18 years who suffered OHCA and brought to the selected hospitals over a period of 20 months (10th November 2020–9th July 2022) were enrolled in the study. Data collected included demographic and social characteristics, location of arrests, witness status, activity of daily living (ADL) before arrests, time courses of resuscitation, type of CPR, automated external defibrillator (AED) use, intravenous fluid use, tracheal intubation and administration of intravascular epinephrine, first documented cardiac rhythm, as well as return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC). Social class classification was done using the modified Kuppuswamy Socioeconomic scale updated for January 2018 [10]. Information on ambulance services (AS) related factors and post cardiac arrest hospital related factors were also collected. Data were collected using a hard copy structured questionnaire by trained research assistants (resident doctors), which was retained at the facility as source document, and submitted using KoboCollect app to the server for real-time access. The submitted data were reviewed weekly to ensure accuracy and progress of work. Reports were submitted by the team from centres for documentation of the work done.

Inclusion criteria

All consecutive adults aged ⩾ 18 years who suffered OHCA and were brought to the selected hospitals.

Exclusion criteria

Of the 396 OHCAs screened, we excluded 5 OHCAs for incomplete data, 3 OHCAs due to violation of protocol regarding age limits, and 1 OHCA in patients that had non-cardiac chronic terminal illness that was not in remission.

Main outcome measures

Main outcome measures were ROSC, survival to hospital admission and survival to hospital discharge.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was done using IBM-SPSS version 25.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA. Continuous variables were summarised as means and standard deviation, or median and interquartile range (IQR), depending on the normality of the data. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. The hospital discharge survival rate was calculated as the number of patients discharged alive divided by the total number of OHCA cases.

Discussion

In this study, we used three working definitions of survival as described in previous studies [12]. These include return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), successful hospital admission, defined as sustained ROSC till hospital admission and successful hospital discharge, defined as the patient leaving the hospital alive. However, the survival rate to hospital discharge is regarded as the “gold standard” for determining the effectiveness of treatment of cardiac arrest [4].

The overall outcome from OHCA in this study is poor and the rate of pre-hospital CPR intervention (15.2%) was low. Despite the 23.7% of ROSC achieved from the pre-hospital CPR intervention, the overall survival to hospital admission and eventual discharge from hospital was only 1.8%. This finding is very poor compared to the reports from developed countries [12–14]. The morbidity and mortality weekly report of the Cardiac Arrest Registry to Enhance Survival (CARES) – an OHCA surveillance, recorded an overall survival to hospital discharge rate of 10.4% as of 2024 [13], which was higher than in some previous reports [15]. However, in interpreting and comparing the findings of this study with any previous report from other populations, we need to consider the context and the standard of care available for out-of-hospital medical emergencies in the different populations. Survival from OHCA varies widely across countries and geographical regions, depending on the characteristics of emergency medical services (EMS) and health care systems in the community, such as treatment availability, training, and care quality [14]. Reported outcomes of OHCA in low-resource settings are usually lower than those reported in patients from high-resource countries, obviously due to fewer available resources for resuscitation care compared to high-resource regions [16]. According to the World Health Organization, ‘‘the state of cardiac arrest and trauma resuscitation is a good reflection of the standard of pre-hospital and emergency care in any health system [17]. Our findings, therefore, reflect the weaknesses in community emergency medical care service system in Nigeria compared to the developed world.

Nigeria faces major challenges in each of the six links of the CoS in terms of timing, effectiveness, and efficiency of execution of the components of CoS.

Early access, which is the first link in the CoS, is regarded as the most essential component of the CoS [12]. It is designed to immediately recognize a life-threatening emergency and initiate EMS, especially in witnessed OHCA. Incidentally, majority (91.7%) of the OHCA in this study were witnessed. This volume of witnesses is large compared to what was reported in a previous study in South Africa (68%) [18], in the 2024 morbidity and mortality weekly report of CARES in the United States [13], and in a similar study in Canada [19].

The benefit of an arrest being witnessed is the timely activation of EMS. Previous systematic review and meta-analysis showed that survival to hospital discharge was more likely among OHCA patients whose cardiac arrest was witnessed by a bystander or EMS [16]. However, timely activation of EMS is only possible where there is a dedicated nationally promoted and well publicized emergency telephone number/code for such communication. Unfortunately, an area-wide emergency toll-free telephone numbers/code, as obtains in the United Kingdom (UK) and North America, is either non-existent, or if at all exists in Nigeria, is not well publicized. Enhancing EMS through a dedicated unique telephone number in all communities should therefore be a top priority for Nigeria.

The ambulance service system in Nigeria is inefficient and bedeviled with numerous structural challenges, such as delayed response times and inadequate facilities and trained personnel. Moreover, coverage is limited to certain parts of the country, leaving many regions without reliable access. The median ambulance arrival time in this study was 15 min (IQR 12.5–45 min); a duration long enough for permanent brain damage to occur in the absence of effective bystander CPR. There is therefore an urgent need to strengthen the emergency ambulance services system in the country, through improved time efficiency, provision of adequate facilities/equipment in the ambulances and appropriate training for ambulance service personnel to treat emergencies in order to improve the outcomes of OHCA care.

The fact that 50% of patients with pre-hospital ROSC died en route to hospital before admission highlights the inadequacy of facilities or the lack of BLS knowledge and skills among the ambulance personnel or, in cases involving alternative means of transportation, the accompanying bystanders. In other words, the resources available within the community (ambulance facilities/equipment and personnel) to convey the patients to hospital could not sustain the gain already made at the scene of the event.

Bystander CPR is the second link in the CoS and represents the only chance for a victim of sudden cardiac arrest to survive before the arrival of an ambulance, or in settings without prehospital ambulance services, before arrival at the hospital [12]. The time to initiate bystander CPR, (the time from collapse to initiation of CPR), could not be ascertained in this study because it was not monitored. Previous systematic review showed that survival to hospital discharge was more likely among OHCA patients who received bystander CPR [16]. However, the results of the present study show that, the achievement of anything close to this is hampered by a number of challenges in Nigeria due to poorly organized community emergency medical response system, poor quality of bystander interventions and inadequate resources in terms of personnel and facilities to mount a timely and effective intervention. The fact that only 21.8% of the arrests witnessed by bystanders benefited from CPR in this study could indicate poor knowledge or willingness to perform this life-saving intervention within the community. The rates of bystander CPR remain poor in most LMIC [20]. This reflects the level of community involvement in the treatment of OHCA and forms an important modifiable factor in any efforts to improve the survival of OHCA. Countries across the world have tried to address the issue of low bystander intervention within the community by introducing mandatory CPR training at various opportunities, levels and strata of their society [21]. The potency of this is verifiable with the experience of Norway in which introduction of CPR training into the national school education curriculum since 1961, has succeeded in raising the average survival from OHCA to about 25% [7].

The third link in CoS is early defibrillation. Investigators have observed that it is the interaction of early CPR with early defibrillation that is the most powerful intervention for ensuring optimal patient survival [4]. Vaillancourt et al. [19], identified bystander CPR and early first responder defibrillation as significant contributors to increased survival in OHCA. However, as revealed in this study, availability of an AED is a rarity for emergency medical responses in public places in Nigeria. The ambulances hardly have the necessary facilities and personnel with requisite knowledge to provide emergency medical interventions.

The fourth critical link in the CoS for management of cardiac arrest is early effective ACLS (intubation, intravenous access and intravenous medications), which is commonly provided by paramedics, physicians or nurses at the scene or in-transit. As revealed by the findings of our survey, the present ambulance service system in Nigeria can hardly meet this level of infrastructure, facilities and personnel, which explains why the outcome is so poor. There were no records of the use of definitive airway management in our study, however, intravenous medications were administered in very few cases, suggesting that some patients had intravenous access established for drug administration.

The fifth link in the CoS is integrated post-resuscitative care. The 2010 ACLS guidelines recommend that all post-cardiac arrest patients should be managed in an ICU [22]. As revealed in this study, some of the post-cardiac arrest patients were managed in other hospital locations apart from the ICU. This may be because of limited ICU bed capacity in some of the study sites. Although the number of patients admitted with ROSC was small, all survived to discharge regardless of the area of the hospital where they received the post cardiac arrest care. While this number is too small to draw firm conclusions regarding the effectiveness of post-resuscitative care in the Nigeria healthcare system, the result obtained is encouraging and efforts should be made to sustain the favorable outcome achieved in this link of the CoS. This means that the weakest link in the CoS in Nigeria appears to be the pre-hospital care component. Since CoS is described as only as strong as its weakest link, pre-hospital care forms the major area of intervention that needs to be improved to enhance survival in OHCA. There is need for community awareness, engagement and training in the prevention, early recognition and first aid treatment for cardiac emergencies, including BLS knowledge and skills to enhance bystanders’ involvement in OHCA care, as well as awareness for early access to the emergency care system. There is also a critical need for improved emergency ambulance services system and provision of automated external defibrillators in the community/public places.

Future research should focus on sensitizing policymakers to prioritize and appropriate funding for standardized CPR education protocols in schools and enhance community awareness through widespread community education on CPR. These measures would no doubt significantly enhance lay rescuer confidence and competencies on CPR and ensure rapid intervention during emergencies to enhance survival rates of OHCA in Nigeria. Future efforts should also look at possibilities of establishing standardized OHCA registries, with adequate data capture for Utstein reporting standards. This would allow for easy interpretation and comparison of interventions and outcomes to ascertain whether resuscitation is provided according to evidence-based guidelines with the goal of identifying opportunities to improve quality of care systems for OHCA in Nigeria.

Limitations

There were several limitations of this study. First, the study is limited by the fact that it was a descriptive cross-sectional exploratory survey and conducted only in some hospitals that were purposefully selected for representation of the geopolitical zones. This could have introduced some degree of selection bias. However, this limitation is mitigated by the fact that the selected hospitals serve as the main referral centers for cardiac arrest in their respective geopolitical zones. Secondly, the data obtained in this study are inadequate to comply with Utstein reporting standards because several time-related events in the CoS that may be associated with survival from cardiac arrest were not specifically captured. These time-related events include access time, (the time from collapse to the summoning of emergency aid), time to initiation of CPR, (the time from collapse to initiation of CPR) and time to definitive care, (the time from collapse to provision of defibrillation, intubation, or emergency medication). Also, a large proportion of the OHCA patients did not have initial rhythm recorded and only short-term outcomes were assessed. Thus, the prognostic significance of these time-related events could not be tested in this study.

Conclusion

Despite the various limitations of the study, the strengths of our study lie in the fact that it was a prospective multicenter survey, conducted in tertiary referral hospitals providing inpatient and outpatient services, as well as emergency and critical care services in the six geopolitical zones of the country. The study revealed shortcomings related to CPR intervention in the community due to a lack of organized emergency care services, infrequent bystander intervention and inefficient ambulance services plagued by longer response times and resource-limitation in a low-resource setting like Nigeria. This bellies the practical difficulties inherent in implementing the scientific recommendations on resuscitation formulated primarily from the perspective of a high-resource environment by the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (ILCOR). The study clearly showed that prehospital care is a major area of intervention to be improved to enhance the survival of OHCA in Nigeria.
