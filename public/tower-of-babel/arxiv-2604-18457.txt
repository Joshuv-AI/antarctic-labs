Random-State Generation and Preparation Complexity in Rydberg Atom Arrays Preprint: APS/123-QED
Edison S. Carrera, Grégoire Misguich

Abstract
Rydberg atom arrays are powerful platforms for studying quantum many-body systems. We consider the Rydberg-Ising Hamiltonian on periodic chains and numerically study ensembles of states generated by random global pulse sequences subject to hardware constraints and fixed evolution times. We compare the statistical properties of such states with those of Haar-random states within the relevant lattice symmetry sector. In the strong-interaction regime (short interatomic distance), the dynamics is governed by an effective blockade that restricts Hilbert-space exploration and limits entanglement growth. In this regime, level-spacing statistics of reduced density matrices are close to random-matrix predictions, while the distribution of measurement probabilities deviates from Porter–Thomas behavior. For weaker interactions (larger interatomic distance), the system approaches Haar-like statistics at long times, as reflected in entanglement entropy, entanglement spectrum statistics, and the distribution of measurement probabilities. At intermediate interactions, this behavior is observed on experimentally relevant timescales. Motivated by this observation, we investigate whether generic symmetric quantum states can be efficiently prepared using quantum optimal control in this regime. Employing target states drawn from an ensemble with a broad entropy distribution, we observe high fidelities (infidelities between 10 − 5 10^{-5} and 3 × 10 − 2 3\times 10^{-2} for 9 spins). The fidelity, however, decreases with the entanglement entropy of the target state, demonstrating that highly entangled states are intrinsically harder to prepare under realistic constraints.

Rydberg atom arrays have emerged as versatile platforms for the quantum simulation of correlated many-body systems

25

;

8
and resource states
43

These platforms have also been used to study interacting fermionic systems
36
;
30
.

A central ingredient in many of these applications is the ability to control the system’s Hamiltonian. This enables the preparation of quantum states with desired properties and the exploration of specific dynamical regimes. Control protocols can rely on (quasi-)adiabatic evolution or on rapid variations of the Hamiltonian that drive the system out of equilibrium.

In quantum control theory, a central question is which states can be prepared for a given set of control Hamiltonians. This can be addressed by analyzing the dynamical Lie algebra (DLA) associated with the system
29
;
15
;
51
;
28
. If the DLA spans the full special unitary algebra, the system is controllable, and any unitary transformation can, in principle, be implemented. As a consequence, any target state can be prepared from a given initial state.

For interacting many-body systems, this analysis has been carried out only in a few cases
14
;
28
. More importantly, the DLA approach is not constructive, as it does not provide explicit pulse sequences to prepare a given target state. In addition, it yields an asymptotic notion of controllability and ignores hardware constraints such as finite control amplitudes, limited bandwidth, and finite evolution times.

In many neutral-atom platforms, control is implemented through time-dependent external fields that couple uniformly to all qubits. These fields are limited in amplitude by hardware constraints, which restrict the dynamics and prevent arbitrarily fast qubit rotations. As a result, only a subset of Hilbert space can be explored. Understanding this restriction is essential to assess the capabilities of such simulators.

A practical way to characterize the accessible set of states is to adopt a statistical approach. States are generated using random control sequences, and the resulting ensemble is compared with Haar-random states. This perspective underlies the notion of expressibility in variational quantum circuits
11
;
26
;
50
.

Random circuits and random-circuit sampling have been extensively studied as models for generating complex many-body dynamics
38
;
56
;
2
. In contrast, similar approaches for analog quantum simulators under hardware constraints remain much less explored
53
;
28
.

In this work, we adopt a statistical approach to characterize the quantum states generated by random pulse sequences in Rydberg arrays, and analyze how hardware constraints and interactions shape their entanglement properties, statistical signatures, and preparation complexity.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
, we introduce the Hamiltonian, the pulse sequences (Sec.

) considered in this study. We then analyze the statistical properties of the generated states using entanglement entropy (Sec.

), spectral statistics of reduced density matrices (Sec.

In Sec.
III
, we investigate the control landscape using gradient-based optimization (GRAPE) with multiple random initializations for each target state. By selecting target states with controlled entanglement, we assess the difficulty of fixed-time state preparation under constrained control and identify regimes where highly entangled states remain hard to prepare. The optimization procedure is described in Sec.
III.1
, and the results are presented in Sec.
III.2
.

Finally, Sec.
IV

II
Generation of random states

II.1
Hamiltonian and random pulse sequences

We consider an array of
N
Rydberg atoms arranged in a ring geometry. Each atom is treated as a two-level system (qubit), whose basis states are the ground state
\ket{g}=\ket{\downarrow}
and a Rydberg excited state
\ket{r}=\ket{\uparrow}
. The system can be modeled as a collection of
N
spin-
\tfrac{1}{2}
particles interacting via a van der Waals Ising Hamiltonian
9
;
37
,

\hat{H}_{0}=V(d)\sum_{i<j}\frac{1}{(r_{ij}/d)^{6}}\hat{n}_{i}\hat{n}_{j}.

(1)

Here,
d=r_{01}
denotes the nearest-neighbor distance, and
r_{ij}
is the Euclidean distance between atoms at sites
i
and
j
. The coupling strength is given by
V(d)=C_{6}/d^{6}
, where
C_{6}
is the van der Waals coefficient determined by the chosen Rydberg state (see Sec.
II.2
). The operator
\hat{n}_{i}=\ket{\uparrow}_{i}\!\bra{\uparrow}
is the Rydberg occupation number at site
i
.

To control the collective dynamics, two external time-dependent fields (e.g., laser driving) are applied, leading to the total Hamiltonian

\hat{H}(t)=\hat{H}_{0}+\Omega(t)\hat{J}_{x}-\Delta(t)\hat{N}.

(2)

Here,
\hat{J}_{\mu}=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\hat{\sigma}_{\mu}^{(i)}
(
\mu=x,y,z
) are collective spin operators, and
\hat{N}=\hat{J}_{z}+N/2=\sum_{i}\hat{n}_{i}
is the total Rydberg excitation number. The function
\Omega(t)\geq 0
is the Rabi frequency, while
\Delta(t)
is the detuning. Thus, the Rydberg-atom simulator is modeled by the (time-independent) atom positions (which determine the
r_{ij}
and
d
) and two user-defined control functions
\Omega(t)
and
\Delta(t)
.

The control fields are modeled as piecewise-constant functions with
M
intervals. The time-evolution operator then factorizes as

\hat{U}=\prod_{i=1}^{M}\hat{U}_{i},

(3)

with (
\hbar
set to unity):

\hat{U}_{i}(\delta t_{i},\Omega_{i},\Delta_{i})=\exp\!\left[-i\,\delta t_{i}\left(\hat{H}_{0}+\Omega_{i}\hat{J}_{x}-\Delta_{i}\hat{N}\right)\right].

(4)

Random pulse sequences are generated by fixing
\delta t_{i}=T_{\mathrm{f}}/M
and sampling the control amplitudes independently from uniform distributions,
\Omega_{i}\sim\mathcal{U}([0,\Omega_{\max}])
and
\Delta_{i}\sim\mathcal{U}([-\Delta_{\max},\Delta_{\max}])
. The maximal values
\Omega_{\max}
and
\Delta_{\max}
come from hardware constraints and their values are discussed in Sec.
II.2
.

We keep the number of intervals
M
fixed so that the number of independently drawn control parameters remains constant, while varying the segment duration
\delta t
through changes in
T_{f}
.

The parameter
M
sets the number of random collective kicks, whereas
\delta t
controls the effective evolution time under the interacting Hamiltonian in each segment, and thus the amount of scrambling generated per step.

The initial state is chosen as the product state
\ket{\psi_0}=\ket{\downarrow}^{\otimes N}
, which is a ground state of
\hat{H}_{0}
and the natural initial state on most platforms.

We consider a ring geometry, for which the spatial symmetry group is the dihedral group
D_{N}
, generated by translations and reflections. The Hamiltonian (
2
) and the initial state are invariant under this symmetry, so the time-evolved states remain confined to the symmetric subspace associated with the trivial irreducible representation of
D_{N}
. For comparison, the case of an open chain, where the symmetry group contains only two elements, has been investigated in Ref.
28
.

We analyze states generated by random control sequences (
random-pulse states
). To make contact with experimental conditions, we first specify the model parameters and hardware constraints used throughout the analysis.

II.2
Hardware constraints and parameters

The control parameters are the Rabi frequency
\Omega
and the detuning
\Delta
. Their maximum values are set by hardware constraints. Throughout this work, we use
\Omega_{\max}=12\,\mathrm{rad}\,\mu\mathrm{s}^{-1}
and
\Delta_{\max}=20\,\mu\mathrm{s}^{-1}
, which are representative of current devices. For Pasqal platforms, see Refs.
49
;
41
. For QuEra’s platform, see Ref.
57
.

Current devices allow for larger values of
\Delta
. However, choosing
\Delta\gg\Omega
does not significantly modify the properties of the generated states (see Sec.
II.6
). We therefore set
\Delta_{\max}
to a value comparable to
\Omega_{\max}
.

In current platforms, atoms are trapped in optical tweezers, allowing for programmable geometries subject to a minimal spacing constraint. In our simulations, we consider a ring geometry with
N=9
atoms and vary the nearest-neighbor distance in the range
5\,\mu\mathrm{m}\leq d\leq 10\,\mu\mathrm{m}
, consistent with experimental capabilities.

The distance
d
sets the interaction scale
V(d)
through the van der Waals coefficient
C_{6}
(see below Eq. (
1
)). Varying
d
tunes the relative strength of interactions with respect to the control fields and thus the dynamical regime. At short distances, the system enters a blockade regime where interactions strongly constrain the dynamics (see Sec.
II.6
), while at larger distances the dynamics is dominated by the control fields.

Finally, the total evolution time is limited by the coherence time of the system, typically of order
20\,\mu\mathrm{s}
for current Rydberg platforms (see
17
). In this work, some of the evolution times considered exceed this scale and should therefore be interpreted as idealized dynamics.

For the pure states considered here, the entanglement entropy provides a measure of bipartite entanglement and allows us to assess how close the resulting states are to Haar-random states.

Under the hardware constraints introduced above, we generate ensembles of random-pulse states for
M=30
. We vary the total evolution time
T_{f}
and the nearest-neighbor distance
d
, and sample
5\times 10^{4}
realizations for each parameter set. The resulting state is denoted by
\ket{\psi(T_f)}

A

B

|A|=\lfloor N/2\rfloor

|B|=\lceil N/2\rceil

The Schmidt coefficients
\lambda_{n}

s_{n}=-\log(\lambda_n^2)

EE=-\sum_{n}\lambda_{n}^{2}\log(\lambda_n^2)

For

\tilde{S}=S/S_{\max}

S_{\max}=\frac{1}{2}N\ln 2

T_{f}=1.0\,\mu\mathrm{s}

\tilde{S}
is typically small, reflecting that interactions have not yet generated significant entanglement. At intermediate times (
T_{f}=7.4\,\mu\mathrm{s}

T_{f}=100\,\mu\mathrm{s}

;
40

39

To quantify convergence toward this distribution, we analyze the dependence on the interatomic distance
d
and total evolution time
T_{f}
. We use the Jensen–Shannon (JS) divergence, a bounded and symmetric measure, to compare the entanglement entropy distribution of the random-pulse states with that of Haar-random states in the trivial symmetry sector. Figure
1
(b) shows that for
d=7
and
d=10\,\mu\mathrm{m}
, the JS divergence decreases with increasing

This behavior is complemented by Fig.
1
(c), which shows the median entanglement entropy together with the central
68\%
interval as a function of
T_{f}
. For
d=10\,\mu\mathrm{m}

T_{f}\lesssim 10\,\mu\mathrm{s}
), the states are weakly entangled, while at long times (
T_{f}\sim 100\,\mu\mathrm{s}
) they become highly entangled and approach Haar-like values. Around
T_{f}\sim 10\,\mu\mathrm{s}
, the spread is maximal, indicating that the dynamics can generate a wide range of entanglement. This reflects a balance between interaction and control scales at
d=10\,\mu\mathrm{m}
, leading to a diverse ensemble of weakly and highly entangled states.

II.4
Level spacing statistics of the entanglement spectrum

Pure states
\ket{\psi}
drawn at random from the Hilbert space, referred to as Haar-random states, exhibit a volume-law scaling of entanglement entropy
40
. Such behavior is also expected for states generated by sufficiently scrambling many-body dynamics
52
;
19
. For these states, the reduced density matrix of a subsystem
A
,
\rho_{A}=\Tr_{B}\!\left(\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}\right)
, is described by a normalized Wishart ensemble,
\rho_{A}\sim XX^{\dagger}/\Tr(X X^\dagger)
, where
X
is drawn from a Ginibre ensemble
59
. Consequently, the level-spacing statistics of the entanglement spectrum follow Wigner–Dyson statistics. More precisely, the reduced density matrix belongs to the Laguerre unitary ensemble (LUE), whose local spectral statistics coincide with those of the Gaussian unitary ensemble (GUE)
18
.

The presence of level repulsion in the spectrum of
\rho_{A}
(the entanglement spectrum) reflects the random matrix character of
\rho_{A}
, and thus of the state
\ket{\psi}
. Level repulsion is therefore associated with strong entanglement, and analyzing the level statistics of
\rho_{A}
provides a way to probe the degree of scrambling in the underlying dynamics
54
. In the following, we investigate to what extent such features can be realized in Rydberg atom arrays with global control.

For each sampled state, we compute the entanglement spectrum and keep only the central
75\%
of the eigenvalues (the bipartition is that of Fig.
1
).
From this subset, we compute the normalized level-spacing ratios,

\tilde{r}_{n}=\min\left(\frac{s_{n}-s_{n-1}}{s_{n+1}-s_{n}},\frac{s_{n+1}-s_{n}}{s_{n}-s_{n-1}}\right).

(5)

The upper panels of Fig.
2
show the pooled distribution of
\tilde{r}
. The latter is obtained by aggregating the ratios from all states generated at fixed
T_{f}
, following the procedure of Ref.
54
.

To analyze the numerical data, we compare it to the Wigner–Dyson (W-D) surmise for the ratio of consecutive level spacings, given by
3

P_{\text{W-D}}(\tilde{r})=\frac{2(\tilde{r}+\tilde{r}^{2})^{\beta}}{Z\,(1+\tilde{r}+\tilde{r}^{2})^{1+3\beta/2}},

(6)

where
Z=4\pi/(81\sqrt{3})
and
\beta=2
corresponds to GUE. As shown in Fig.
2
(a), the ratio distribution for random-pulse states approaches
P_{\text{W-D}}
at long times.

In addition to the evolution time
T_{f}
, the interatomic distance
d
controls the interaction strength and thus the degree of effective constraints in the dynamics. It is therefore natural to expect that it also influences the convergence toward random-matrix behavior, as quantified in Fig.
2
(b). Appendix
A
presents complementary data for different choices of the bipartition. In particular, we show that if the subsystem
A
and its complement
B
are symmetric with respect to each other, the level statistics is instead described by the Gaussian orthogonal ensemble (GOE).

II.5
Bitstring probabilities and Porter–Thomas distribution

In this section, we consider the bitstring probabilities
p(\sigma)=|\langle\psi(T_{f})|\sigma\rangle|^{2}
,
where
\sigma=(\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{N})
denotes a computational-basis bitstring. To analyze these Born probabilities, we study the distribution
P(\omega)
of the rescaled variable
\omega=D\,p(\sigma)
, where
D=2^{N}
is the Hilbert space dimension. For Haar-random states, this distribution follows the Porter–Thomas (P–T) law,
P_{\rm PT}(\omega)=\exp(-\omega)

42
. The predominance of small values of
\omega
is known as anticoncentration
22
;
7
.

In random circuit sampling
22
;
23
, it is common to compare an observed distribution
P_{\rm exp}(\omega)
with an ideal distribution
P_{\rm th}(\omega)
. In experiments, this comparison is often performed indirectly via cross-entropy benchmarking, which provides a quantitative measure of the agreement between measured and ideal output distributions
7
;
2
;
46
.

Here, since the system size is relatively small (
N=9
), we can estimate
P(\omega)
directly by pooling data over all computational-basis bitstrings
\sigma
and over an ensemble of random-pulse states
\ket{\psi(T_f)}
. Our results are summarized in Fig.
3
. The top panels show histograms
P(\omega)
for states generated by random pulses of various durations at an interatomic distance
d=10\,\mu\mathrm{m}
. At short and intermediate times (
T_{f}=1\,\mu\mathrm{s}
and
7.7\,\mu\mathrm{s}
), the distribution deviates from the P–T law, whereas at long times (
T_{f}=100\,\mu\mathrm{s}
) it agrees well with the expectation for Haar-random states.

The bottom panel quantifies this convergence for different
d
by displaying the JS divergence between the distributions obtained from random-pulse states at different
T_{f}
and those of Haar-random states within the trivial symmetry sector. The JS divergence between the latter and the P–T distribution is of order
10^{-3}
, indicating that finite-size and discretization effects are negligible at the scale of the figure.

A subtle aspect of this analysis is that the generated states belong to the trivial irreducible representation of the lattice symmetry group, and therefore do not explore the full Hilbert space. Since symmetry-related bitstrings are not independent, one might expect deviations from the P–T distribution, and it is thus not obvious a priori that the Born probabilities in the
z
-basis should exhibit the same statistics as fully Haar-random states. Nevertheless, we find numerically that at long times the distribution
P(\omega)
agrees with the P–T law within numerical precision. A related analysis of bitstring probabilities in a quantum Ising model was recently reported in Ref.
28
for an open chain (without translation symmetry).

II.6
Rydberg Blockade and Constraints on State Generation

Reducing the interatomic distance shifts the point of maximal spread of the entanglement entropy distribution to shorter evolution times, as observed for
d=7\,\mu\mathrm{m}
, reflecting faster entanglement growth due to stronger interactions. One might therefore expect that further decreasing
d
would continue to shorten the timescale required to explore a broad range of entanglement values. However, this trend breaks down at
d=5\,\mu\mathrm{m}
. As shown in Fig.
1
(b), the JS divergence no longer decreases with increasing
T_{f}
, in contrast to the behavior at
d=7\,\mu\mathrm{m}
. Consistently, Fig.
1
(c) shows that the entanglement entropy remains lower and its distribution does not approach Haar-like statistics within the explored timescales.

This breakdown is also reflected in the level-spacing statistics. Fig.
2
shows the deviation of the mean ratio
\langle\tilde{r}\rangle
from the GUE value,
\langle\tilde{r}\rangle_{\mathrm{GUE}}\approx 0.59
. For
d=7
and
d=10\,\mu\mathrm{m}
, this deviation rapidly decreases with increasing
T_{f}
, reaching values close to zero at times shorter than those required for the entanglement entropy distribution to approach Haar-like statistics. For
d=5\,\mu\mathrm{m}
, the deviation remains small but finite over the explored timescales, indicating a slower approach to GUE behavior. Given the magnitude of the deviation and the associated statistical fluctuations, the data remain broadly consistent with GUE statistics.

In contrast, Fig.
3
shows that the measurement probabilities reveal more pronounced deviations from Porter–Thomas statistics, providing a more stringent test of Haar-like behavior in this regime.

Taken together, these observations suggest that stronger interactions at short distances lead to slower convergence toward Haar-random behavior, possibly reflecting the presence of effective constraints in the dynamics. However, given the limited system size and statistical resolution, these deviations remain moderate and do not rule out eventual convergence at longer times.

At short interatomic distances, the dynamics becomes constrained by the Rydberg blockade mechanism. We investigate how this affects the exploration of the Hilbert space by computing the nearest-neighbor Rydberg excitation correlation
\langle\hat{n}_{0}\hat{n}_{1}\rangle
for states generated by random pulse sequences, and comparing it with Haar-random states in the trivial symmetry sector.

According to Page’s theorem
40
, the reduced density matrix of a small subsystem of a Haar-random pure state is close to maximally mixed. For two contiguous sites, this implies
\rho_{i,i+1}\approx\mathbb{I}_{4}/4
, and therefore
\langle\hat{n}_{i}\hat{n}_{i+1}\rangle\approx 1/4
and
\langle\hat{n}_{i}\rangle\approx 1/2
. Due to translation invariance, these quantities are independent of the site index, consistent with the behavior observed in Fig.
4
.

For states generated by short random pulse sequences, we observe that for large
d
the distributions of
\langle\hat{n}_{0}\hat{n}_{1}\rangle
and
\langle\hat{N}\rangle/N
are broad. Together with the low entanglement entropy in this regime, this indicates that the generated states remain weakly entangled and close to product states, with magnetization pointing in arbitrary directions, as expected for weak interactions at short times.

As the interatomic distance is reduced to
d=7\,\mu\mathrm{m}
, the interaction strength increases and both distributions become narrower, indicating a more constrained set of states with residual polarization inherited from the initial state. At longer evolution times, both observables approach the values expected for Haar-random states in the trivial sector.

For shorter distances,
d=5\,\mu\mathrm{m}
, we instead observe behavior consistent with a blockade regime, where double excitations of neighboring atoms are strongly suppressed and
\langle\hat{n}_{0}\hat{n}_{1}\rangle\approx 0
over all explored timescales.

To characterize this regime, we introduce the dimensionless parameter

\eta=\dfrac{\Omega}{|V(d)-\Delta|},

(7)

which compares the driving strength
\Omega
to the interaction-induced detuning
|V(d)-\Delta|
. This parameter controls the off-resonant coupling to the doubly excited state
\ket{rr}
. In the regime
\eta\ll 1
, the state
\ket{rr}
is strongly detuned and therefore weakly populated, corresponding to the Rydberg blockade regime
55
. For
\eta\sim 1
, the blockade is partially lifted, whereas for
V(d)\approx\Delta
, the system reaches the antiblockade condition, where
\ket{rr}
becomes resonantly accessible
1
;
4
.

Since the Rabi frequency and detuning are drawn from uniform distributions, the probability density function of
\eta
can be derived analytically (see Appendix
C
, Eqs. (
33
) and (
40
)).

Fig.
5
shows the resulting probability density function (PDF) of
\eta
for the considered nearest-neighbor distances. For
d=5\,\mu\mathrm{m}
, the probability of sampling pulses with
\eta\gtrsim 1
is negligible. This is consistent with the observation that, for all evolution times,
\mathbb{E}\langle\hat{n}_{0}\hat{n}_{1}\rangle\approx 0
, indicating that the system remains deep in the blockade regime.

For larger distances, the PDFs become similar, and the probability of sampling pulses with
\eta\sim 1
is finite, allowing for a partial lifting of the blockade.

We further identify the characteristic distance

\tilde{d}=\left(\frac{C_{6}}{\Omega_{\max}+\Delta_{\max}}\right)^{1/6},

(8)

which corresponds to the shortest distance at which the sampled pulses can reach
\eta\sim 1
,
marking the crossover between a regime in which the dynamics is always blockade-constrained and one in which the blockade can be locally lifted by the drive.

Taken together, these results reveal three main features of state generation under random global control. At large interatomic distances, random pulse sequences produce a diverse ensemble of states across different evolution times, with entanglement properties approaching Haar-like statistics at long times. Reducing the distance accelerates this process, shortening the timescale for the emergence of complex entanglement. However, this trend breaks down at sufficiently short distances, where the dynamics becomes effectively constrained and the accessible entanglement remains limited. This behavior reflects the interplay between strong interactions and finite control amplitudes, which restrict the exploration of the Hilbert space under global driving. While the Hilbert space itself is not reduced, the system effectively explores only a restricted region, leading to a suppression of the achievable entanglement entropy, analogous to that observed in symmetry-constrained systems
39
. The parameter
\eta
provides a quantitative characterization of these constraints.

III
Quantum optimal control for random-pulse target states

The previous section identified two relevant regimes. At large nearest-neighbor distances,
d=10\,\mu\mathrm{m}
, random pulse sequences generate ensembles with a broad range of entanglement entropies by varying the total evolution time at fixed pulse depth. At intermediate distances,
d=7\,\mu\mathrm{m}
, the entanglement statistics already display Haar-like behavior in the trivial symmetry sector at experimentally realistic times
T_{f}\approx 10\,\mu\mathrm{s}
.

In the context of variational quantum algorithms, the emergence of Haar-like statistics is commonly associated with high expressibility. This indicates that the accessible states form a representative subset of Hilbert space. A natural question is whether such states can also be efficiently prepared under the same physical constraints.

To address this, we study state preparation using quantum optimal control. We take target states from the random-pulse ensemble at
d=10\,\mu\mathrm{m}
, where varying the total evolution time
T_{f}
produces a broad range of entanglement entropies. The preparation dynamics is implemented at
d=7\,\mu\mathrm{m}
. This setting allows us to probe the preparation of highly expressive states in a regime that already exhibits Haar-like statistics at experimentally relevant times.

We focus on the role of time constraints. Let
T_{\rm max}
denote the available control time. If
T_{\rm max}\geq T_{f}
, where
T_{f}
is the duration used to generate the target, the state can be reproduced within the same control ansatz. The problem becomes nontrivial when
T_{\rm max}<T_{f}
, where the control protocol must effectively compress the dynamics into a shorter time.

We investigate this question numerically and analyze how the preparation depends on the entanglement entropy of the target state, the interatomic distance, the total evolution time, and the constraints on the control amplitudes.

III.1
Optimal Control Protocol

The optimization protocol is based on
GRAPE

44
and follows closely the approach of
10
. The control pulses are taken to be piecewise constant, such that the time-evolution operator is given by Eq. (
3
). The algorithm iteratively updates the control parameters
\vec{\Delta}=(\Delta_{k})_{k=1}^{M}
,
\vec{\Omega}=(\Omega_{k})_{k=1}^{M}
, and
\vec{\delta t}=(\delta t_{k})_{k=1}^{M}
in order to minimize a cost function defined on the final state.

The physical contribution to the cost function is

\mathcal{C}_{\text{phys}}\left(\vec{\Delta},\vec{\Omega},\vec{\delta t}\right)=-\log\left|\bra{\psi_{\text{Target}}}\hat{U}\ket{\psi_0}\right|^{2},

(9)

which corresponds to the negative logarithm of the fidelity between the target state
\ket{\psi_{\text{Target}}}
and the evolved state
\hat{U}\ket{\psi_0}
.

To promote smooth pulse sequences and limit the total evolution time, we include a regularization term

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\text{pulse}}\left(\vec{\Delta},\vec{\Omega},\vec{\delta t}\right)

\displaystyle=a_{1}\left(\norm{\dfrac{d\Vec{\Delta}}{dt}}^{2}_{\ell_{2}}+\norm{\dfrac{d\Vec{\Omega}}{dt}}^{2}_{\ell_{2}}\right)

(10)

\displaystyle+a_{2}\exp\!\left[\left(\dfrac{\norm{\Vec{\delta t}}_{\ell_{1}}}{T_{\max}}\right)^{\alpha}\right].

Here,
d\vec{\Delta}/dt
and
d\vec{\Omega}/dt
denote discrete time derivatives computed from consecutive segments (finite differences weighted by the segment durations). The first term penalizes rapid variations between intervals, while the second introduces an exponential penalty for total evolution times exceeding
T_{\max}
.

Amplitude constraints are enforced via a quadratic hinge penalty,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\text{amp}}\!\left(\vec{\Delta},\vec{\Omega}\right)

\displaystyle=a_{3}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\Big[\max\!\left(0,|\Delta_{k}|-\Delta_{\max}\right)^{2}

(11)

\displaystyle+\max\!\left(0,\Omega_{k}-\Omega_{\max}\right)^{2}\Big].

ensuring that
|\Delta_{k}|\leq\Delta_{\max}
and
0\leq\Omega_{k}\leq\Omega_{\max}
.

The total cost function is

F=\mathcal{C}_{\text{phys}}+\mathcal{C}_{\text{pulse}}+\mathcal{C}_{\text{amp}}.

(12)

The hyperparameters are chosen such that
a_{1}\ll 1
, reflecting a weak preference for smoothness, while
a_{2}\sim 10^{-2}
and
a_{3}\sim 10
strongly penalize long evolution times and amplitude violations.

III.2
Results

Target states are drawn from the ensembles of random-pulse states introduced in the previous section, allowing us to control their entanglement entropy through the generation time
T_{f}
. The optimal-control procedure is performed with a fixed number of pulse intervals,
M=30
. For each target state, we run the optimization from multiple random initializations (typically 128), enabling us to probe the structure of the control landscape and to limit optimizer-dependent effects.

To establish a meaningful success criterion, we first consider target states that are expected to be reachable, i.e., states within the accessible region of Hilbert space. This is achieved by setting

IV
Conclusion

We studied the states that can be generated in a Rydberg atom array under hardware constraints on global control amplitudes. We analyzed ensembles produced by random pulse sequences and showed that, for sufficiently long evolution times and moderate to large interatomic distances, the resulting states approach Haar-like statistics within the trivial symmetry sector. This behavior is reflected in the entanglement entropy distribution, the level-spacing statistics of the entanglement spectrum, and the anticoncentration of Born probabilities.

We found that decreasing the nearest-neighbor distance initially enhances mixing, leading to a faster approach to Haar-like behavior. However, upon further reducing the distance, the dynamics enters an effective blockade regime. This regime suppresses, but does not completely forbid, configurations with nearby excitations, thereby restricting the effectively accessible region of Hilbert space and limiting the achievable entanglement. Notably, the level-spacing statistics are only weakly affected by this constraint, while the effect is more pronounced in the distribution of Born probabilities, which does not converge to the Porter–Thomas law on the explored timescales. These results show that different statistical indicators probe distinct aspects of state complexity, and that level-spacing statistics can display random-matrix behavior even when the amplitudes do not fully explore Hilbert space.

We then investigated the preparation complexity. At larger interatomic distances (
10\,\mu\mathrm{m}
), the slower convergence to Haar-like behavior allows one to control the entanglement entropy via the total evolution time and to generate random states spanning a broad range of entanglement values. We used these states as targets in a GRAPE-based optimal control protocol. At fixed evolution time, the infidelity increases with the entanglement entropy of the target state, providing a quantitative link between state complexity and preparation difficulty.

Overall, these results provide a practical and quantitative characterization of the states accessible in Rydberg simulators beyond abstract controllability arguments. They highlight the role of interactions, blockade effects, and finite control amplitudes in shaping the accessible region of Hilbert space.

A natural continuation of this work is to include decoherence, in order to determine how noise and coupling to the environment modify both the statistical properties of random-pulse states and their preparation complexity. The present results also pave the way for experimental random-sampling benchmarks of Rydberg simulators, by comparing measured ensembles with the statistical signatures expected for highly entangled or Haar-like states. Extending this analysis to larger system sizes and different lattice geometries is a natural next step to assess the generality of these findings.

Entanglement spectrum ratio statistics

In the main text, we characterized the statistical behavior of the entanglement entropy and entanglement spectrum for a fixed bipartition of the quantum system. For
N=9
and Haar-random states in the trivial symmetry sector, the entanglement spectrum exhibits a GUE level repulsion. While the entanglement entropy of Haar-like states is largely insensitive to the geometry of the bipartition, reflecting the expected volume-law scaling in highly mixed states, we find that the level-spacing ratio statistics of the entanglement spectrum depend sensitively on this choice. Here, we analyze how the bipartition affects the gap-ratio statistics of the entanglement spectrum.

A Haar-random pure state for

1/2

\ket{\phi}=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{N}}C_{i_{1},\dots,i_{N}}\ket{i_1,\dots,i_N},

\beta=2

\real C_{i_{1},\dots,i_{N}},\ \imaginary C_{i_{1},\dots,i_{N}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1/2),

(14)

followed by normalization such that
\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{N}}|C_{i_{1},\dots,i_{N}}|^{2}=1

We consider a bipartition of the system into subsystems

(15)

Then the reduced density matrix is given by
\rho_{A}=\Tr_{B}\left(\ket{\phi}\!\bra{\phi}\right)
. The entanglement spectrum is obtained from the Schmidt decomposition.

Although the global state is invariant under space group transformations, the choice of bipartition generally breaks these symmetries. Consequently, the sites assigned to subsystem
B
influence the statistical properties of the entanglement spectrum. As shown in Fig.
7

On the other hand, for bipartitions that preserve a space group transformation exchanging subsystems
A

B

with
\Lambda\geq 0
diagonal
27
;
21
representing the singular values of

\rho_{A}=CC^{\dagger}=Q\Lambda^{2}Q^{\dagger}.

(16)

The eigenvalues of

\Lambda^{2}
and our numerical results (Fig.
7

We then consider bipartitions that induce spatial symmetries within the subsystems. As illustrated in Fig.

Appendix C
Chance of getting blockade

We assume that the control amplitudes are drawn independently as

\Omega\sim\mathcal{U}([0,\Omega_{\max}]),\qquad\Delta\sim\mathcal{U}([-\Delta_{\max},\Delta_{\max}]),

(17)

and define

\eta=\frac{\Omega}{|V(d)-\Delta|}.

(18)

For brevity, let

V\equiv V(d),\qquad Y\equiv|V-\Delta|,

(19)

so that
\eta=\Omega/Y
. Since
\Omega
and
\Delta
are independent,
\Omega
and
Y
are also independent.

The PDF of
\Omega
is

f_{\Omega}(\omega)=\frac{1}{\Omega_{\max}},\qquad 0\leq\omega\leq\Omega_{\max}.

(20)

We now derive the PDF of
Y
, and then that of
\eta
.

Distribution of
Y=|V-\Delta|

Case A:
V>\Delta_{\max}
.

In this regime,
V-\Delta>0
for all
\Delta\in[-\Delta_{\max},\Delta_{\max}]
, so

Y=V-\Delta.

(21)

Since
\Delta
is uniform,
Y
is also uniform:

f_{Y}(y)=\frac{1}{2\Delta_{\max}},\qquad V-\Delta_{\max}\leq y\leq V+\Delta_{\max}.

(22)

Case B:
0<V\leq\Delta_{\max}
.

In this case, the map
\Delta\mapsto Y=|V-\Delta|
is two-to-one for small
y
, and one-to-one near the upper edge. Explicitly,

f_{Y}(y)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{\Delta_{\max}},&0\leq y\leq\Delta_{\max}-V,\\[6.00006pt]
\dfrac{1}{2\Delta_{\max}},&\Delta_{\max}-V<y\leq\Delta_{\max}+V,\\[6.00006pt]
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(23)

Distribution of
\eta=\Omega/Y

Using the change of variables

\Omega=\eta y,

(24)

the Jacobian is

\left|\frac{\partial(\Omega,Y)}{\partial(\eta,y)}\right|=y.

(25)

Hence

\rho(\eta)=f_{\eta}(\eta)=\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{\Omega}(\eta y)\,f_{Y}(y)\,y\,dy.

(26)

Since
f_{\Omega}(\eta y)\neq 0
only if
0\leq\eta y\leq\Omega_{\max}
, this becomes

\rho(\eta)=\frac{1}{\Omega_{\max}}\int_{0}^{\Omega_{\max}/\eta}y\,f_{Y}(y)\,dy.

(27)

We now evaluate this integral in the two regimes above.

Case A:
V>\Delta_{\max}
.

Using
f_{Y}(y)=1/(2\Delta_{\max})
on

[V-\Delta_{\max},\,V+\Delta_{\max}]
, Eq. (
27
) gives

\rho(\eta)=\frac{1}{2\Delta_{\max}\Omega_{\max}}\int_{V-\Delta_{\max}}^{\min(V+\Delta_{\max},\,\Omega_{\max}/\eta)}y\,dy.

(28)

It is convenient to define the thresholds

\eta_{-}\equiv\frac{\Omega_{\max}}{V+\Delta_{\max}},\qquad\eta_{+}\equiv\frac{\Omega_{\max}}{V-\Delta_{\max}}.

(29)

Then the three regions are:

•

If
0\leq\eta\leq\eta_{-}
, the whole support contributes, and

\rho(\eta)=\frac{V}{\Omega_{\max}}.

(30)

•

If
\eta_{-}<\eta\leq\eta_{+}
, the upper limit is
\Omega_{\max}/\eta
, and

\rho(\eta)=\frac{\Omega_{\max}}{4\Delta_{\max}}\left[\frac{1}{\eta^{2}}-\left(\frac{V-\Delta_{\max}}{\Omega_{\max}}\right)^{2}\right].

(31)

•

If
\eta>\eta_{+}
, the integration domain is empty, and

\rho(\eta)=0.

(32)

Therefore, for
V(d)>\Delta_{\max}
,

\rho(\eta)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{V(d)}{\Omega_{\max}},&0\leq\eta\leq\eta_{-},\\[6.00006pt]
\dfrac{\Omega_{\max}}{4\Delta_{\max}}\left[\dfrac{1}{\eta^{2}}-\left(\dfrac{V(d)-\Delta_{\max}}{\Omega_{\max}}\right)^{2}\right],&\eta_{-}<\eta\leq\eta_{+},\\[6.00006pt]
0,&\eta>\eta_{+}.\end{cases}

(33)

Case B:
0<V\leq\Delta_{\max}
.

Using the piecewise form of
f_{Y}(y)
, Eq. (
27
) becomes

\displaystyle\rho(\eta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Omega_{\max}}\Bigg[\int_{0}^{\min\{\Delta_{\max}-V,\Omega_{\max}/\eta\}}\frac{y}{\Delta_{\max}}\,dy

(34)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Delta_{\max}-V}^{\min\{\Delta_{\max}+V,\Omega_{\max}/\eta\}}\frac{y}{2\Delta_{\max}}\,dy\Bigg].

(35)

In this regime we define

\eta_{-}\equiv\frac{\Omega_{\max}}{\Delta_{\max}+V},\qquad\eta_{+}\equiv\frac{\Omega_{\max}}{\Delta_{\max}-V}.

(36)

Then:

•

If
0\leq\eta\leq\eta_{-}
, the whole support contributes:

(37)

•

If
\eta_{-}<\eta\leq\eta_{+}

(38)

•

If
\eta>\eta_{+}

(39)

Thus, for

In the limiting case
