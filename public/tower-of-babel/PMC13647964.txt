Traceability of Manilkara huberi Using Stable Isotope Ratio Analysis
to Support Deforestation Prevention
in the Amazon

Helinara L V Capucho, José H de Oliveira, Ricardo Lopes, Santiago L F Ramos, Ananda V de Aguiar, Carlos H S G Meneses, Ricardo L S Marques, Fabio de C Borba, Gabriela B Nardoto, Maria T G Lopes

Abstract

Illegal deforestation represents one of the main challenges
to
the integrity of the Amazon rainforest, warranting the use of reliable
tracking methods to identify the geographical origin of timber species
inspected during commercial transactions. This study utilizes stable
carbon (δ13C) and nitrogen (δ15N)
isotopes to determine the origins of Manilkara huberi (massaranduba) and to establish a database of its geographical provenance.
A total of 130 individual trees were sampled, yielding 350 wood samples,
in the Brazilian states of Amazonas, Pará, Rondônia,
and Roraima, and these wood samples were analyzed using isotope-ratio
mass spectrometry. The results revealed significant differences in
δ13C and δ15N isotopic signatures,
which were associated with variations in soil properties, vegetation
type, and local environmental conditions. Linear discriminant analysis
yielded classification performance ranging from 14% to 97%, with the
highest accuracies observed in specific municipalities. Nitrogen isotope
ratios in the wood showed stronger geographic differentiation, whereas
carbon isotope ratios reflected broader environmental influences.
This study presents a georeferenced isotopic approach based on living
trees, enabling Brazilian agencies to strengthen their use of this
technique to combat illegal timber trade and to provide technical
support for the evaluation and validation of Forest Origin Documents
in Brazil.

Introduction

The Amazon rainforest
contains one of the highest levels of plant
biodiversity worldwide and plays a central role in global climate
regulation and biogeochemical cycles, particularly through carbon
sequestration in tree biomass.
−

 Despite its ecological significance, the Amazon remains highly vulnerable
and is increasingly affected by climate change and anthropogenic pressures.
,

Among these pressures, illegal logging constitutes one of
the principal
threats to the ecological integrity of the Amazon rainforest. This
activity promotes the selective extraction of species with high commercial
value, thereby compromising both the structural composition and functional
diversity of the ecosystem.
−

 Consequently, timber traceability
emerges as a central challenge for regulating illegal logging, particularly
in remote regions of the Amazon.
,

In Brazil,
regulation of the timber production chain is implemented,
among other means, through the Document of Forest Origin [Documento de Origem Florestal - DOF], an official system
that records the transport and storage of native forest products.
The DOF is intended to enable the tracking of timber from extraction
to its final destination. However, because it relies on manual data
entry, the system is vulnerable to fraud, undermining the reliability
of the reported information.
,

Brazilian enforcement
agencies have adopted complementary techniques
to verify the identity and origin of seized timber. Anatomical and
DNA-based methods enable taxonomic identification; however, their
operational application is constrained by costs, time requirements,
and infrastructure limitations, particularly in regional enforcement
scenarios within the Amazon.
,,
 Alternatively, stable isotope analysis has emerged as a promising
approach, as it reflects environmental conditions during tree growth
and allows inferences about geographic origin.
,
 Nevertheless, the application of this technique in the Amazon remains
restricted due to the scarcity of isotopic databases for commercial
species, logistical challenges in accessing remote locations, and
insufficient investment in data-collection initiatives and database
expansion to support future inspections in the region.

This
study presents a database of carbon (δ13C)
and nitrogen (δ15N) isotopic signatures for the widely
exploited timber species Manilkara huberi across multiple regions of the Amazon. Wood samples were collected
in situ, allowing for the construction of a robust reference database
to verify the relationship between the geographical origin declared
in the DOF and the wood’s isotopic signature. Moreover, the
methodology demonstrates operational viability for implementation
in regional laboratories in the Amazon, facilitating expeditious analyses
to support monitoring and inspection activities within the timber
production chain.

The species Manilkara huberi (massaranduba)
was selected as a model organism owing to its broad distribution in
the Amazon, substantial commercial value, and frequent involvement
in cases of illegal logging. The working hypothesis is that the isotopic
signatures of δ13C and δ15N exhibit
spatial variation among populations, thereby enabling the discrimination
of their geographic origins. Accordingly, the objective of this study
was to analyze isotopic signatures in individuals of M. huberi from distinct regions of the Amazon and
to assess their potential as a tool for timber traceability.

Discussion

This study extends the application
of stable isotope analyses to
natural populations of Manilkara huberi distributed across four states within the Amazon region. It highlights
the potential of combining carbon (δ13C) and nitrogen
(δ15N) isotopic signatures to distinguish wood origin.
The observed differences reflect not only regional environmental variation
but also ecophysiological and biogeochemical processes associated
with local growing conditions and forest management histories. Consequently,
the observed isotopic variability underscores the value of stable
isotopes as complementary tools for ecological and forensic traceability.
These results also provide essential baseline information for future
studies on timber origin verification in the Amazon.

The populations
of M. huberi exhibit
distinct carbon (δ13C) and nitrogen (δ15N) isotopic signatures, enabling differentiation among municipalities
in the Amazon. Although δ13C signatures were more
similar across populations, they were sufficient to distinguish the
groups. δ13C serves as an effective indicator of
functional variability in tropical trees and demonstrates the importance
of the canopy effect on forest isotopic composition.
−

 In Amazonas, the observed values indicate denser canopies, which
lead to greater isotopic discrimination due to reduced light availability
in the understory. In Pará, more
negative δ13C values suggest greater water-use efficiency,
possibly associated with taller trees and structural variations within
the forest. The largest difference observed
between areas was 1.62‰, consistent with patterns described
for widely distributed species, in which δ13C tends
to exhibit relative spatial stability due to strong genetic and physiological
control of this trait.

In contrast,
nitrogen isotope signatures (δ15N)
exhibited substantial spatial variation and contributed most strongly
to the discrimination among the populations analyzed. δ15N primarily reflects nitrogen cycling processes within ecosystems
and is influenced by soil nutrient availability, gaseous losses due
to denitrification and volatilization, and local edaphic and geological
factors. In environments with higher nitrogen availability, plants
and soils exhibit isotopic enrichment in δ15N due
to the preferential loss of 14N to the atmosphere. The differences observed between geographically
proximate locations, such as Juruti (PA) and Parintins (AM), corroborate
evidence that nitrogen biogeochemical processes can vary at relatively
small spatial scales in the Amazon, being strongly modulated by geology,
pedology, and vegetation composition. These findings indicate that spatially structured variation in δ15N, arising from local environmental and biogeochemical processes,
can contribute substantially to distinguishing the geographic origin
of tropical woods.

Most of the samples analyzed were collected
from forest concession
areas, environments subject to disturbance from selective tree extraction,
despite being classified as low-impact. Exceptions included Humaitá
(AM), characterized as an igapó area with minimal anthropogenic
interference, and Juruti (PA), a terra firme area subject to greater
human intervention. In this context, the observed isotopic differences
reflect not only regional climatic variation but also the degree of
anthropogenic influence and the history of forest management in the
areas studied. Previous research demonstrates that disturbances such
as deforestation and forest fires increase nitrogen mobility in the
soil, promoting isotopic enrichment in δ15N due to
the preferential loss of 14N.

When all sampled areas were analyzed collectively, linear
discriminant
analysis (LDA) showed reduced ability to accurately classify sample
origins, suggesting that including multiple populations may diminish
the discriminatory power of the selected predictors. The observed
decrease in accuracy (30%) aligns with established limitations of
LDA in ecological contexts characterized by substantial environmental
heterogeneity and multivariate overlap among groups. LDA assumes both
homogeneity of covariance matrices and linear separability between
classes-assumptions that are seldom fully satisfied at broad geographic
scales, such as in the Amazon, where continuous environmental gradients
and complex ecophysiological interactions commonly result in partially
overlapping predictor distributions.
,
 Additionally,
research on isotopic traceability indicates that classification accuracy
typically declines as the number of groups and intraspecific variability
increase, particularly when predictors are influenced by multiple,
correlated environmental factors.
,
 In this context,
the observed reduction does not imply the absence of a geographic
signal; rather, it reflects the limitations of linear approaches in
capturing complex patterns at large spatial scales.

These findings
indicate that analyses performed at smaller spatial
scales, such as by municipality or within individual states, or that
include additional environmental variables, tend to improve LDA efficiency.
Notably, the observed classification accuracy of approximately 58%
at the state level supports the premise that the discriminatory power
of isotopic markers is scale-dependent, with higher performance when
environmental variability is more constrained and ecological gradients
are more homogeneous. This trend is consistently documented in the
literature, where discriminant models yield superior performance at
regional scales or when applied to environmentally coherent subsets,
thereby reducing class overlap.
,
 Within this context,
nitrogen emerged as a particularly robust predictor of location within
the same state, potentially reflecting variation in soil properties,
nitrogen cycling, vegetation composition, and disturbance history.
Carbon, meanwhile, served as a complementary variable, reflecting
environmental conditions during photosynthesis and thus contributing
to the discrimination of wood from different populations of the same
species.
−

The results obtained directly address the need for independent
methods to validate information presented in DOF. The environmentally
structured isotopic variability observed in this study, grounded in
well-established ecological processes, supports the potential use
of isotope analysis as a complementary tool for evaluating the consistency
of declared wood origins, particularly in the Amazon. Consequently,
stable isotopes may provide additional scientific information for
assessing declared provenance alongside existing documentation.

According to data from the Brazilian Institute of Environment and
Renewable Natural Resources,
M. huberi is the most exploited among the 25 principal
native timber species in Brazil. In the Amazon, merely 2% of the available
timber species account for more than half of the authorized exploitation
between 2007 and 2020, with M. huberi representing the highest volume: 11,181,124.7 m3. This
is followed by Erisma uncinatum (cedrinho), Goupia glabra (cupiúba), Dinizia
excelsa (angelim vermelho), and Hymenaea
courbaril (jatobá). To curb the illegal extraction of these species, frequently targeted
by international markets, particularly China, the USA, Japan, and
countries of the European Union (Germany, Italy, The Netherlands,
and Belgium), as well as the United Kingdom, the Brazilian government, through the Federal Police, has intensified
operations to combat such crimes within the national territory.

Environmental crimes, such as illegal deforestation in primary
and secondary Amazonian forests, are an increasing cause for concern.
,
 In light of the impacts of global warming, land-use sectors have
been actively discussing strategies to reconcile the sustainable use
of forest resources with the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)
and ensure the well-being of future generations.
,,
 Among the most promising strategies for
combating the illegal timber trade are strengthening public security
agencies and establishing international alliances dedicated to disrupting
the illegal Amazonian timber supply chain.
−

The georeferenced
isotopic database developed for M. huberi constitutes an important reference for
environmental monitoring and verification of the origin of its wood.
The results demonstrate the potential of stable isotope analysis to
characterize spatially structured variation in the isotopic signatures
of this commercially valuable Amazonian species, thereby expanding
the application of established isotopic methodologies for forest traceability
in the Amazon. This study contributes to strengthening wood traceability
systems by providing critical information for the monitoring and prevention
of illegal logging. Since wood is not isotopically homogeneous, preserving
the variability observed among different sampling points within the
same tree offers a more realistic representation of operational traceability
scenarios, in which forensic samples may originate from different
portions of logs, boards, or processed timber products. Previous studies
have also documented within-tree isotopic variability and its relevance
for geographic traceability applications.
,

The inclusion of additional isotopes, such as oxygen and hydrogen,
enhances the accuracy of isotopic traceability for wood from native
Amazonian species.
,,
 The integration of multiple elemental signatures can improve accuracy
rates in determining wood origin using LDA, potentially providing
additional scientific support for the evaluation of information contained
in DOF and for future initiatives aimed at combating illegal trade.
Incorporating this tool into official certification systems, such
as IBAMA (Instituto Brasileiro do Meio Ambiente e dos Recursos Naturais
Renováveis), FSC (Forest Stewardship Council), and CITES (Convention
on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora),
could contribute to the further development of complementary approaches
for tropical wood traceability. Future research should include external
validation, the use of an independent test set, and the verification
of commercial timber.

Conclusions

Combined analysis of carbon
(δ13C) and nitrogen
(δ15N) isotopes demonstrated potential for the geographic
discrimination of populations of the same species. Performance varied
according to spatial scale and geographic context. The resulting isotopic
database can be used to compare samples obtained during enforcement
actions involving the species.

The observed differences in δ13C and δ15N isotopic ratios among individuals
of the same species provide
a basis for geographic traceability. This is particularly relevant
in regions with pronounced contrasts in geology, vegetation, and climate,
which may enhance the discriminatory power of the method. Classification
accuracy varied according to spatial scale. Higher performance was
observed in municipality-level comparisons than in analyses conducted
across states. Therefore, the higher classification accuracies observed
for specific municipalities should not be interpreted as representative
of overall geographic traceability performance.

This finding
underscores the need to incorporate additional predictor
isotopes, such as δ2H and δ18O,
as well as complementary methodologies, to improve traceability accuracy.
These results support the potential use of stable isotopes as complementary
scientific and forensic tools. They may support timber origin verification,
monitoring of logging activities, and environmental control measures
in the Amazon. The current isotopic database should therefore be regarded
as a reference framework, and its operational forensic application
requires further validation using independent commercial or seized
timber samples.

Materials and Methods

Origin and Sampling of the Material

This study examines wood sold under the commercial name “massaranduba”
derived from the botanical species Manilkara huberi, a species widely exploited and used in the timber industry. The
trees were identified in the field through botanical analyses conducted
by parabotanists and forestry engineers, based on morphological characteristics,
including leaves, flowers, fruits, and trunks, as well as general
organoleptic characteristics, with special attention to the abundance
and consistency of the latex characteristic of this species.

For this research, four states in the Amazon region were selected:
Pará, Amazonas, Roraima, and Rondônia, all of which
exhibit the highest accumulated deforestation rates in the Legal Amazon. Within each state, areas primarily managed under
forest concessions were chosen for wood sample collection. In Pará,
the selected municipalities were Juruti (1), Aveiro (2), and Belterra
(2). In Amazonas, sampling took place in Humaitá (1), Parintins
(1), Itapiranga (1), and Silves (1). In Roraima, wood samples were
collected in Rorainópolis (1) and Caracaraí (1). In
Rondônia, the selected sites were Candeias do Jamari (1) and
Itapuã do Oeste (1) ( and ). Data on meteorological
variables, geological and soil characteristics, and forest types for
each area are presented in .

Ten individual trees were sampled at each location
between August
2022 and July 2023, resulting in a total of 130 individual trees.
The minimum distance between sampled trees ranged from 80 to 100 m.
Trees selected for sampling had a diameter at breast height (DBH)
of at least 50 cm. All trees were georeferenced to facilitate the
construction of geolocation maps and the validation of official databases
for tracing species origin. Latitude and longitude were recorded during
fieldwork in degrees, minutes, and seconds using a portable GPS device.

Wood Sampling Procedure

For each
tree, two to three wood-sampling holes were drilled at 1.30 m above
the base of the tree, corresponding to the diameter at breast height
(DBH). The number of wood samples was determined by field accessibility
and logistical constraints encountered during expeditions in remote
Amazonian forests. Whenever possible, three wood samples were collected
per tree. However, in some locations (Silves, Itapiranga, Parintins,
and Juruti), only two wood samples could be obtained per tree.

At each designated sampling point on the trunk, the surface wood
was cleaned by scraping with a machete to remove impurities adhering
to the bark or cambium. Wood samples were collected using a battery-powered
drill (model: Makita 40 V Li-Ion 140 N m) fitted with 1/4 in. drill
bits. The initial 5 to 7 cm of drill shavings from each hole were
discarded to ensure that only sapwood and heartwood were sampled.
This process was repeated until 5–10 g of wood shavings per
hole were obtained. Samples were placed in paper bags and labeled
with a matrix number and collection site.

All sampling wounds
were sealed with silicone gel to prevent microorganism
proliferation. Wood samples were stored in the field in custom-built
ovens maintained at 40–50 °C to preserve their integrity
until the end of the expeditions (3–4 days). Subsequently,
the material was transported to the Forensic Laboratory of Stable
Isotopes at the Federal Police Superintendence of Amazonas (SR/PF/AM)
for preparation and analysis.

Wood Sample Preparation and Analysis Protocol

After field collection, wood samples were dried in an oven at 40
°C for up to 1 day prior to sample preparation. The material
was then transferred to 50 cm3 grinding jars containing
25 mm diameter AISI 304 stainless steel spheres and pulverized using
a benchtop vibratory mill (MM 400-Retsch) for 2 min at 30 s–1. Following pulverization, the material was dried for 2 h at 60 °C.
The resulting powder was then transferred to 2 mL microtubes. Each
tube was labeled with the tree identification number, sample number,
and collection site, and stored in a desiccator until chemical analyses
commenced.

To determine the carbon and nitrogen isotopic ratios
in wood, a hierarchical sampling and analytical protocol was implemented.
For each tree, two or three separate sampling holes were drilled into
the trunk. One wood sample was collected from each hole. The total
number of samples per tree depended on field accessibility and logistical
constraints encountered during the collection of biological material.
Each sample was analyzed individually using isotope ratio mass spectrometry,
with three consecutive readings performed for each sample as technical
replicates to validate analytical precision. These repeated readings
were conducted solely to verify the precision of the isotopic determinations.
For all samples, the mean value from the three readings remained within
the laboratory’s established precision limits (maximum acceptable
standard deviation in triplicate: 0.40‰ for δ15N and 0.15‰ for δ13C; unpublished data).
After the validation of analytical quality, the average of the three
readings was used to represent the wood sample from each collection
hole.

The two or three wood samples obtained from each tree
(collected
at different drilling points) were treated as distinct wood samples
and recorded as separate observations in the data set, without being
considered independent biological replicates. This approach was chosen
because wood is an inherently heterogeneous biological material. Furthermore,
isotopic composition can vary among different portions of the same
trunk due to anatomical, physiological, and microenvironmental factors.
,
 Removing material from different drilling locations enabled the
characterization of within-tree isotopic variability, which is important
for forensic timber traceability applications, where samples may originate
from various portions of commercial wood products. Therefore, statistical
analyses (ANOVA and LDA) were performed at the wood-sample level,
while recognizing that samples obtained from the same tree belong
to the same biological unit and are not statistically independent.
For each determination, 4.97–5.08 mg of wood powder was weighed
into tin capsules (5.0 mm × 8.0 mm) using an analytical balance
with a resolution of 0.01 mg.

Encapsulated organic samples were
analyzed with a Flash EA IsoLink
CN elemental analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany)
coupled to a Delta V Advantage isotope ratio mass spectrometer (Thermo
Fisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany) via the ConFlo IV interface. Combustion
of samples occurred in an oxidation reactor at 1020 °C, followed
by reduction on copper at 650 °C.

The generated gases were
carried by a continuous helium (He 5.0)
flow and separated using a chromatographic column coupled to a ramped
GC oven (Bremen, Germany). The heating program began at 70 °C,
was maintained for 250 s, then increased to 240 °C and held for
500 s (see S4-Supporting Information).
This temperature ramp was essential to ensure effective gas separation
and complete elution of SO2 produced during combustion.

Following chromatographic separation, the gases were introduced
into a mass spectrometer via the ConFlo IV interface, where they were
ionized and analyzed for isotopic ratios. Data acquisition and processing
were conducted using Isodat 3.0.0.83 software (Service Pack 3.0.94.12;
Thermo Foundation 2.0) (see S5-Supporting
Information).

Calibration of the CO2 and N2 cylinders was
conducted using international reference materials, specifically the
IAEA-602 (δ13C = −28.85 ± 0.04‰)
and IAEA-N-2 (δ15N = 20.41 ± 0.12‰) standards.

(δ) value in per mil (‰). For these calculations, reference
values (R_standard) of 0.0112021 for VPDB (13C:12C) and 0.0036433 for AIR (15N:14N) were used.

For analytical quality
control, a reference material of Handroanthus serratifolius (Vahl) S.O.Grose (yellow
ipê, code “CTR Ipê”, wood), previously
calibrated at the Forensic Stable Isotope Laboratory of the Federal
Police Superintendence of Amazonas (SR/PF/AM) with δ13C values of −26.96 ± 0.05‰ and δ15N values of −6.90 ± 0.30‰ (±2σ control
limits), was employed as an internal standard. This standard was analyzed
in quadruplicate at the beginning and end of each analytical sequence,
as well as for every set of approximately 78 samples, thereby enabling
continuous monitoring of instrumental stability and measurement accuracy.

Data Processing

Prior to statistical
analysis, the data set was examined for extreme observations using
boxplot analysis based on the interquartile range (IQR) criterion.
Observations falling outside the thresholds defined by Q1 –
1.5 × IQR and Q3 + 1.5 × IQR were classified as potential
outliers and excluded from further analysis.

After quality control
and exclusion of the 11 observations identified as statistical outliers,
the final data set comprised 339 wood-sample observations. This final
data set was consistently used for the ANOVA and LDA analyses and
for the corresponding tables, figures, and Supporting Information, unless otherwise explicitly indicated.

Descriptive
statistics (mean, minimum, maximum, and standard deviation)
were calculated for the δ15N and δ13C values of the collected wood samples. Differences in mean isotopic
values among sampling locations were evaluated using the Scott-Knott
test, implemented in the Genes software
version 21. This test was selected because
it groups means into statistically distinct clusters, thereby facilitating
the interpretation of differentiation patterns among sampling locations.

Multivariate Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) was conducted to
assess the differentiation between groups of wood samples and evaluate
classification performance by region of origin. Model performance
was evaluated using leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV), in which
each observation was successively omitted from the training set and
subsequently classified using discriminant functions fitted to the
remaining observations. This procedure was applied to estimate the
model’s predictive ability and minimize biases associated with
classification evaluation.

Additionally, leave-one-tree-out
(LOTO) cross-validation was performed
as a sensitivity analysis, in which all wood samples originating from
the same tree were jointly excluded from the training data set at
each iteration, thereby accounting for the hierarchical sampling structure
(see S6–Supporting Information).

LDA and cross-validation analyses were performed using Minitab
Statistical Software version 22.5 (Minitab LLC, State College, PA,
USA).

The evaluation metrics comprised: (i) overall classification
accuracy
obtained by cross-validation; (ii) group-wise sensitivity, representing
the proportion of correctly classified wood samples within each class;
(iii) the confusion matrix, which details classification patterns
and misclassifications among groups; and (iv) distances between groups
in the discriminant space, facilitating assessment of the degree of
separation between classes.

Analyses were performed using both
the complete data set and a
stratified approach, with LDA applied separately for each state at
two scales of analysis. Separate scatter plots and data sets were
generated for each area to visualize isotopic signature patterns within
each locality.
