Causal Effects of Circulating Inflammatory Proteins on Postmenopausal Atrophic Vaginitis in European Populations: A Mendelian Randomization Study with Transcriptomic Validation
Authors: Xianyang Hu, Shuai Ruan, Tianyu Zhong, Fuju Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostmenopausal atrophic vaginitis (PAV) is a prevalent and undertreated condition driven primarily by estrogen deficiency. Whether circulating inflammatory proteins causally contribute to PAV pathogenesis remains unknown.MethodsGenetic instruments for 91 inflammatory proteins were derived from large-scale proteogenomic GWAS of European ancestry individuals. PAV outcome data were obtained from the FinnGen Consortium Release 12. Primary causal estimates were obtained using the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method, complemented by MR-Egger, weighted median, and weighted mode analyses. Candidate proteins were defined by IVW p < 0.05 and directional consistency across all four methods. Sensitivity analyses included the MR-Egger intercept test, Cochran’s Q test, leave-one-out analysis, funnel plot assessment, and reverse MR. Transcriptomic validation was performed using the publicly available vaginal tissue dataset GSE26761.ResultsSix inflammatory proteins met the candidate criteria: CCL19 (OR: 1.063, 95% CI: 1.003–1.126) and IL12B (OR: 1.050, 95% CI: 1.002–1.100) were associated with increased PAV risk, while CXCL5 (OR: 0.935, 95% CI: 0.874–0.999), TNFRSF9 (OR: 0.930, 95% CI: 0.869–0.996), IL10RA (OR: 0.906, 95% CI: 0.831–0.988), and TGFA (OR: 0.880, 95% CI: 0.790–0.981) were inversely associated with PAV risk. Sensitivity analyses revealed no significant pleiotropy or heterogeneity. Transcriptomic analysis confirmed significantly lower TGFA expression in atrophic compared to control vaginal tissue (P = 0.039), consistent with its MR-derived protective effect.ConclusionThis study provides genetic evidence that circulating inflammatory proteins causally influence PAV risk through distinct immune pathways. TGFA emerges as the most robustly supported candidate, with convergent MR and transcriptomic evidence suggesting a role in vaginal epithelial maintenance. These findings highlight inflammation as a potential contributor to PAV pathogenesis and nominate candidate targets for future mechanistic and therapeutic investigation. Given that multiple inflammatory proteins were tested, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and warrant further validation in independent cohorts.

Introduction

Postmenopausal atrophic vaginitis (PAV), increasingly encompassed within the broader concept of genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM),1 is a chronic condition characterized by vulvovaginal and lower urinary tract symptoms resulting from estrogen deficiency after menopause.2 Clinically, PAV commonly presents with vaginal dryness, burning, dyspareunia, urinary urgency, and recurrent urogenital infections, leading to substantial impairment in quality of life.2,3 Epidemiological studies estimate that approximately 50% of postmenopausal women experience symptoms related to vaginal atrophy, although the condition remains markedly underdiagnosed and undertreated.4,5

The pathogenesis of PAV is rooted in estrogen deficiency following menopause.6,7 Estrogen plays a critical role in maintaining vaginal epithelial thickness, glycogen content, and lactobacillus-dominant microbiota, collectively sustaining a low-pH, infection-resistant vaginal environment.8 Withdrawal of estrogenic support removes the trophic stimulus from vaginal tissue, resulting in epithelial thinning, decreased secretions and vascularization, loss of submucosal mucopolysaccharides and elastic fibers, elevated vaginal pH, and upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-1 and IL-8 within the local microenvironment.9 While these inflammatory changes have traditionally been viewed as secondary to structural atrophy, whether inflammation merely reflects or also contributes to epithelial damage remains unclear.

Accumulating evidence suggests that inflammation may play a more active role in PAV pathogenesis. Histologically, atrophic vaginitis features an inflammatory process of the thinned vaginal epithelium, with neutrophil infiltration observed.10 Postmenopausal women with clinical signs of genitourinary syndrome of menopause have significantly higher cervical levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-8, IL-23, MIP-1α, MIP-1β, IP-10, and RANTES, compared to asymptomatic women.11 Systemic inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein are also elevated in women with GSM and correlate with symptom severity.12 In addition, higher vaginal concentrations of IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-8 have been observed in postmenopausal women with non-Lactobacillus-dominated vaginal microbiota.13 Collectively, these findings suggest that specific inflammatory pathways may contribute directly to PAV pathogenesis, rather than merely reflecting secondary tissue damage.

Mendelian Randomization is a genetic epidemiological approach that uses genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs) to estimate the causal effects of modifiable exposures on outcomes. As genetic variants are randomly allocated during meiosis and remain stable throughout life, MR analyses are less susceptible to confounding and reverse causation than conventional observational studies.14,15 With the increasing availability of large-scale genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics, two-sample MR has become a powerful approach for investigating the causal effects of circulating biomarkers on complex diseases.16 Advances in proteogenomic, exemplified by the Olink Inflammation Panel, together with large-scale GWAS data, have enabled the generation of robust genetic instruments for systematic MR analyses of immune mediators.17 This framework has been successfully applied to identify causal roles of inflammatory proteins in various diseases, including cardiovascular, neurodegenerative, and malignant disorders.18–20 However, no study has yet evaluated the causal effects of these circulating inflammatory proteins on the risk of PAV.

Therefore, the present study aimed to conduct a comprehensive two-sample MR analysis to systematically evaluate the causal effects of 91 circulating inflammatory proteins on the risk of PAV, followed by transcriptomic validation using publicly available gene expression data from vaginal tissue. By integrating genetic and transcriptomic evidence, we sought to identify specific inflammatory mediators that causally contribute to PAV pathogenesis, with the goal of informing novel preventive and therapeutic strategies for this prevalent and undertreated condition.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study employed a two-sample MR framework to estimate the causal effects of 91 circulating inflammatory proteins on the risk of PAV. The two-sample MR design utilizes summary-level genetic association data from separate GWAS for the exposure (inflammatory proteins) and outcome (PAV). The validity of MR causal inference rests on three core assumptions for each genetic instrument: (1) the genetic variant is robustly associated with the exposure of interest (relevance). (2) the genetic variant is independent of confounders of the exposure-outcome relationship (independence). (3) the genetic variant affects the outcome exclusively through the exposure and not via alternative pathways (exclusion restriction). An overview of the study design is provided in Figure 1. Data Sources

Summary-level GWAS data for 91 circulating inflammatory proteins were obtained from a large-scale proteogenomic study conducted in individuals of European ancestry.17 These proteins, measured using the Olink Proximity Extension Assay (Inflammation Panel), encompass a broad repertoire of immune mediators including cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and soluble receptors. Protein levels were normalized prior to GWAS analysis. Summary-level GWAS data for PAV (ICD-10: N95.2) were obtained from the FinnGen Consortium Release 12 (phenotype code: finngen_R12_N14_POSTMENATRVAG).21 FinnGen is a large-scale Finnish biobank initiative integrating genome-wide genotyping with comprehensive national health registry data. The PAV outcome GWAS comprised cases and controls of Finnish ancestry. Detailed information on sample sizes and phenotype definitions is provided in Table 1. As the exposure GWAS datasets were derived from European ancestry cohorts and the outcome from the Finnish population, potential population stratification was considered minimal. No sample overlap between exposure and outcome datasets was identified, as the inflammatory protein GWAS and FinnGen are entirely independent cohorts. Instrumental Variable Selection

For each of the 91 inflammatory proteins, genetic instruments were selected following a pre-specified protocol. Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNPs) robustly associated with each protein level at genome-wide significance (p < 5 × 10−5) were initially identified. To ensure instrument independence, Linkage Disequilibrium (LD) clumping was performed using a stringent threshold (r2 < 0.001, window size = 10,000 kb) based on the European 1000 Genomes Project reference panel, retaining only the most strongly associated SNP within each LD block. SNPs with minor allele frequency (MAF) ≤ 0.01 were excluded. Palindromic SNPs with ambiguous strand orientation were removed during harmonization of exposure and outcome datasets to ensure that effect alleles corresponded consistently across both GWAS. Mendelian Randomization Analysis

The primary causal effect estimate for each exposure-outcome pair was obtained using the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method, which pools the Wald ratio estimates from individual SNPs under the assumption of balanced or absent horizontal pleiotropy.

To ensure robustness, we additionally applied three complementary MR methods, including MR-Egger, weighted median, and weighted mode. MR-Egger provides a causal estimate that is robust to directional pleiotropy by allowing a non-zero intercept, though it has reduced statistical power. The weighted median method yields a consistent estimate if at least 50% of the instrumental weight comes from valid instruments. And the weighted mode method estimates the causal effect based on the largest cluster of instruments that produce similar Wald ratios. To prioritize findings with the greatest evidence of robustness, proteins were considered candidate causal factors if they met two simultaneous criteria: (1) a nominally significant IVW p-value (p < 0.05), and (2) directional consistency of effect estimates across all four MR methods. This dual criterion was applied to reduce false positives arising from the large number of proteins tested while retaining biologically plausible signals. Effect estimates are reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) per unit increase in genetically predicted protein level.

Sensitivity Analyses

A comprehensive suite of sensitivity analyses was conducted to evaluate the validity of the MR assumptions for candidate proteins meeting the primary filtering criteria. Heterogeneity among IVs was assessed using Cochran’s Q statistic within the IVW framework. A significant Q p-value (p < 0.05) may indicate the presence of pleiotropic effects. The MR-Egger intercept test was used to evaluate directional pleiotropy, with a p-value < 0.05 indicating potential bias from unbalanced horizontal pleiotropy. Leave-one-out analyses were conducted by systematically excluding one SNP at a time and recalculating the IVW estimate, to assess whether any single SNP disproportionately drove the observed association. Funnel plots were constructed to visually assess asymmetry suggestive of directional pleiotropy. To test for reverse causality, a reverse Mendelian randomization analysis was performed on the candidate protein by exchanging the exposure and outcome.

Transcriptomic Validation

To provide independent biological corroboration of the MR findings, we examined publicly available transcriptomic data from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) dataset GSE26761.22 This dataset comprises gene expression profiles from vaginal tissue biopsies obtained from postmenopausal women with atrophic vaginitis (dry group) and postmenopausal women without atrophic symptoms (control group), generated using microarray technology. Differential expression of the candidate inflammatory protein-encoding genes was examined between the dry and control groups.

Ethical Considerations

This study used publicly available, de-identified human datasets, including summary-level GWAS data for Mendelian randomization analyses and publicly available microarray datasets obtained from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. No individual-level participant data were collected or accessed. In accordance with Article 32 (Items 1 and 2) of the Measures for Ethical Review of Life Science and Medical Research Involving Human Subjects (2023, China), research using legally obtained publicly available data or anonymized data is exempt from ethical review. Therefore, additional Institutional Review Board approval was not required for this study. The original studies had received approval from their respective ethics committees, and informed consent had been obtained from all participants where applicable.

Statistical Analysis

All MR analyses were performed using the TwoSampleMR package (version 0.5.7) in R (version 4.3.0). A two-sided significance threshold of p < 0.05 was applied.

Results

Instrumental Variable Selection and Characteristics

After rigorous quality control, independent and strong IVs were identified for each of the 91 inflammatory proteins. The number of SNPs per exposure varied across proteins, and all retained IVs had F-statistics substantially exceeding the conventional threshold of 10, indicating that weak instrument bias was unlikely to affect our results.

Sensitivity Analyses Supported the Robustness of Primary Findings

The MR-Egger intercept test revealed no significant directional pleiotropy for any of the six candidate proteins (all P > 0.05, Table 2), suggesting that horizontal pleiotropy was unlikely to materially bias the primary IVW estimates. Cochran’s Q statistics indicated no significant heterogeneity across the IVW analyses for all six proteins (all Q P-values > 0.05, Table 3), further supporting the validity of the genetic instruments. Leave-one-out analyses confirmed that no single SNP disproportionately drove any of the significant associations (Figure S1). Funnel plot symmetry was observed for all six proteins (Figure S2), consistent with the absence of directional pleiotropy. Reverse MR analyses further showed no evidence of causal effects of PAV on the candidate inflammatory proteins, arguing against reverse causation. Collectively, these sensitivity analyses support the robustness of the primary findings. Transcriptomic Validation Corroborated the MR Findings

To corroborate the MR findings with biological evidence, we interrogated publicly available transcriptomic data from GSE26761, which profiled vaginal epithelial gene expression in postmenopausal women with vaginal dryness, a hallmark symptom of PAV, compared with asymptomatic postmenopausal controls. Among the six candidate proteins, TGFA provided the most compelling cross-platform validation (Figure 6). TGFA expression was significantly higher in control tissue relative to atrophic tissue (P = 0.039), directly corroborating the MR finding that higher genetically predicted TGFA levels are associated with reduced PAV risk. For the remaining five proteins, transcriptomic findings showed variable concordance with the MR results. CCL19 showed numerically higher expression in atrophic compared to control tissue, consistent with its MR-identified risk effect, and CXCL5 showed higher expression in control tissue, aligned with its protective MR estimate. In contrast, TNFRSF9, IL10RA, and IL12B showed directional discordance between the two analyses, which may reflect the limited statistical power of the transcriptomic dataset, tissue-level regulatory complexity not captured by circulating protein levels, or context-dependent roles of these mediators in mucosal immunity.

Discussion

In this two-sample MR study integrating proteogenomic and transcriptomic evidence, we systematically evaluated the causal effects of 91 circulating inflammatory proteins on the risk of PAV. We identified six proteins, including CCL19, CXCL5, IL10RA, IL12B, TGFA, and TNFRSF9, with potential causal associations, suggesting that systemic immune signaling may contribute to the development of PAV. Among these, TGFA showed the most consistent support across MR and transcriptomic analyses, strengthening its potential relevance to disease biology.

Our findings expand the current understanding of PAV pathophysiology by supporting a potential contributory role of inflammatory signaling in disease development. While estrogen deprivation is recognized as the primary driver of vaginal epithelial thinning and microbiome disruption, emerging evidence suggests that menopause-associated low-grade inflammation and local immune dysregulation may further contribute to tissue remodeling and symptom persistence.11 The identification of multiple immune mediators with putative causal effects supports the hypothesis that inflammatory signaling is not merely a secondary phenomenon but may participate in disease progression.

Several of the identified proteins have biologically plausible roles in mucosal immunity. CCL19 mediates lymphocyte and dendritic cell recruitment through its receptor CCR7, playing a central role in coordinating adaptive immune responses at mucosal surfaces.23,24 IL12B encodes the shared p40 subunit of IL-12 and IL-23, cytokines that drive Th1 and Th17 differentiation, respectively, and thereby regulate both cellular immunity and mucosal inflammation.25–27 The association of both proteins with increased PAV risk suggests that dysregulated adaptive immune activation within the estrogen-deprived vaginal mucosa may contribute to epithelial damage and symptom perpetuation.

In contrast, CXCL5 is a CXCR2 ligand and potent neutrophil-recruiting chemokine involved in innate mucosal defense,28,29 and its inverse association with PAV may reflect a reduced capacity for epithelial innate immune activation and neutrophil-mediated mucosal defense in the estrogen-deficient vaginal environment. TNFRSF9 (4–1BB) is a co-stimulatory receptor expressed on T cells, NK cells, and other immune effector populations that enhances effector immune responses.30 Its inverse association with PAV risk may reflect reduced T cell and NK cell activation in the disease state, suggesting altered adaptive immune responsiveness in the estrogen-deficient vaginal environment. IL10RA mediates signaling of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10, which suppresses pro-inflammatory macrophage activation and maintains mucosal immune tolerance.31 Higher genetically predicted IL10RA levels may therefore reduce PAV risk by dampening excessive innate immune responses within the estrogen-deprived vaginal mucosa. TGFA is an important member of the epidermal growth factor (EGF) family that regulates epithelial cell proliferation, survival, differentiation, and tissue repair through EGFR signaling.32 Across multiple mucosal tissues, TGFA-mediated EGFR activation has been shown to stimulate epithelial restitution and accelerate repair following mucosal injury.32–34 TGFA signaling may also play an analogous role in maintaining vaginal epithelial integrity under conditions of estrogen deficiency, where reduced trophic support renders the mucosa vulnerable to atrophic damage. This is directly supported by the transcriptomic findings, in which TGFA expression was significantly higher in control compared to atrophic vaginal tissue, suggesting that impaired TGFA-driven epithelial repair may contribute to PAV development or progression. The integration of transcriptomic data provides additional biological support for our MR findings, most notably for TGFA. Discordance observed for other proteins may reflect differences between systemic and local tissue expression, post-transcriptional regulation, or limited statistical power of the transcriptomic dataset.

From a clinical perspective, these findings highlight the potential for identifying inflammatory biomarkers that could aid in risk stratification or early detection of PAV in postmenopausal women. Currently, PAV diagnosis relies largely on symptom reporting and clinical examination,10 and many women remain underdiagnosed due to reluctance to discuss vaginal symptoms. Circulating inflammatory profiles may provide objective biomarkers to complement clinical assessment. Furthermore, the identification of specific inflammatory mediators, such as TGFA and CCL19, suggests potential therapeutic targets. Strategies to restore TGFA-mediated epithelial repair or to modulate CCL19-CCR7-driven lymphocyte recruitment could represent novel approaches to prevent or treat PAV beyond conventional estrogen-based therapies, which are not suitable for all patients. However, these translational implications remain speculative and require prospective validation in clinical cohorts.

Several limitations warrant consideration. MR estimates reflect lifelong genetic predisposition and may not capture dynamic inflammatory changes relevant to disease onset. Residual horizontal pleiotropy cannot be fully excluded despite sensitivity analyses. Both the exposure and outcome GWAS were derived from European ancestry populations, which may limit the generalizability of findings to other ethnic groups.

Additionally, the 91 inflammatory proteins were evaluated without correction for multiple testing. Although the consistent effect directions across four MR methods reduce false-positive risk, the nominal P < 0.05 threshold suggests some findings may be chance observations, and these exploratory results require replication. Furthermore, the transcriptomic validation dataset comprised a relatively small number of samples, limiting statistical power; these findings should therefore be interpreted as supportive rather than confirmatory, pending validation in larger independent cohorts.

Nonetheless, this study has notable strengths, including the first systematic MR evaluation of a comprehensive inflammatory protein panel in relation to PAV, the application of multiple MR methods with extensive sensitivity analyses, and orthogonal validation through independent transcriptomic data.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides genetic evidence supporting a potential causal role of multiple circulating inflammatory proteins in the development of PAV. These findings suggest that immune-mediated pathways may contribute to disease pathogenesis beyond estrogen deficiency alone and highlight several candidate mediators, particularly TGFA, for future mechanistic and therapeutic investigation. Further experimental validation is warranted to elucidate their functional roles in vaginal epithelial homeostasis and postmenopausal tissue remodeling. However, given the absence of multiple-testing correction and the small validation cohort, these exploratory findings require confirmation through further experimental studies and independent replication.
