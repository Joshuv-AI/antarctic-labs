 Goals and disclaimers
• Quantifying durability for lower-TRL BiCRS pathways has been challenging as limited data are available from real-world
  projects and long-term experiments, resulting in an overall lack of scientific consensus. We develop a decision tree that
  aims to summarize the current scientific understanding and state-of-the-art project experience. The decision tree
  can be used to (1) guide the selection of key variables and evaluate their relative impact on durability, (2) identify
  data and knowledge gaps for future research.
• Durability levels: Low durability results in significant reversal in carbon storage, moderate durability is carbon
  permanence between 100-1000y, high durability is carbon permanence >1000y. Note that these ranges are intended to
  show the relative durability across the selection of variables. More data is required for validation.
• The decision trees is informed by existing literature, discussions with experts, and feedback from industry partners
  involved in the BiCRS MMRV project. It reflects our best scientific understanding and is subject to revision and
  updates as new studies and data become available.
• Biomass and biochar burial pathways are selected as examples to illustrate the use of the decision tree framework for
  emerging BiCRS pathways for which data are more limited. The decision tree framework is adaptable to other BiCRS
  pathways.
• We acknowledge there are other innovative biomass and biochar burial systems that are not fully captured here. We
  encourage industries to develop similar decision trees and provide data to support the long-term durability of their
  customized burial solutions.

 Goals and disclaimers
• Both decision trees focus on variables that are believed to be most relevant to state-of-the-art burial projects, i.e., anaerobic
  burial in landfill systems. Projects may contribute additional evidence, including measurements of variables beyond those in
  the decision trees, to increase the certainty of carbon permanence claims. A comprehensive list of relevant variables for both
  pathways is provided in the LCI Matrix developed in this project. Additionally, our project’s MMRV Best Practice Guidelines (BPG)
  features an extensive list of potential tests and measurements for various relevant variables.
• The durability of biomass burial and biochar burial cannot be directly compared, as the mechanisms underlying each burial
  process are different.
• There is currently no consensus on the duration of carbon storage in burial systems, highlighting the need for long-term
  experimental and modeling studies.
 • Biochar is commonly used as a soil amendment, where majority (>85%) of the biochar at the soil surface [Wang et al., 2015]
 has a reported carbon permanence of at least 100 years [Budai et al., 2013; Woolf et al., 2021; Lehmann et al., 2021]. This
 100-year permanence factor has been adopted by organizations such as the IPCC, Puro.earth, and the European Biochar
 Certificate [ICF, 2024]. Recently, biochar durability has been benchmarked against inertinite, which has an expected carbon
 permanence of millions of years. Based on this, Isometric’s low-oxygen biochar burial protocol has adopted a 1000-year
 permanence factor [Isometric, 2024].
 • Biomass burial studies suggest potential carbon storage durations ranging from 100 to ≥1000 years. Research on wood
 furniture decomposition in anoxic landfill conditions demonstrates minimal carbon loss (0–3% over decades). With
 appropriate site selection and engineered solutions, biomass decomposition could be reduced to timescales exceeding
 1000 years [Zeng, 2008]. Currently, Puro.earth requires burial projects to ensure carbon storage for at least 100 years
 [Puro.earth, 2023]. A recent study reported <5% carbon loss from a 3000-year-old wood log buried in clay [Zeng et al., 2024].

 Pre-burial considerations
1. Sustainable biomass sourcing: Projects must demonstrate that biomass is sustainably sourced. Guidelines providing
 acceptable demonstration of sustainable sourcing of biomass may be obtained from resources such as Carbon Direct, Forest
 Stewardship Council (FSC) framework, High Conservation Value Network (HCVN), Sustainable Biomass Program, Roundtable for
 Sustainable Biomass, European Union Renewable Energy Directive (RED II), Sustainable Forestry Initiative, or others. Refer to our
 project’s i) MMRV Best Practice Guidelines and ii) decision tree for counterfactual use of forestry biomass for more details.

2. Sustainable site selection and operation to minimize environmental impacts: Projects must demonstrate sustainable
 operation. Construction of burial site and burial operation must pose no threat to the surrounding environment— groundwater,
 soil, atmosphere (encountering air pollution), etc. Guidelines may be obtained from Isometric’s biomass burial protocol and
 biochar burial protocol, Puro’s terrestrial storage of biomass methodology, or other similar protocols and standards available.
 • Note that Isometric’s biochar burial protocol requires biochar burial projects to have a barrier, to avoid leachate
 (contaminated liquid formed during water percolation through waste materials) from encountering surrounding groundwater.
 The function of the barrier in a biochar burial project is different from one used in biomass burial projects, utilized to maintain
 anoxic conditions to avoid biomass decomposition. The EPA requires that municipal solid waste landfill’s final cover have a
 hydraulic conductivity that is no greater than 1 × 10-5 cm/s [EPA, 2025]. A previously published study reports that a 1-2 ft. thick
 layer of compacted clay has a hydraulic conductivity of less than 1 × 10-7 cm/s [Daniel, 1993]. Gooding (2023) study
 calculates hydraulic conductivity thresholds of ≤ 1 × 10-9 cm/s for a 100y durability target and ≤ 1 × 10-10 cm/s for a 1000y
 durability target.

 Pre-burial considerations
3. Site selection to ensure durability and minimize risk of reversal: Projects must demonstrate durable burial site selection to
 prevent the risk of reversal. The risk of reversal is the likelihood of re-releasing of carbon contained via burial. Burial site must be
 free of risks from fire, natural disasters such as earthquakes and flood, and soil erosion (leading to structural damage), ensuring
 land ownership (avoid human and wildlife activity), and others. Guidelines may be obtained via Puro Woody Biomass Burial
 Standard, Isometric’s biochar burial protocol .

4. Additional barrier considerations to minimize the risk of reversal:
 • Projects must consider the integrity of barriers — ensuring physical separation from the environment (oxygen and moisture),
 which can be measured through the plasticity index which reflects the ability of the barrier to hold water and withstand
 deformation from stresses. A high plasticity index is desirable for a biomass burial project. For example, a material with
 higher clay content has a higher plasticity index [Gooding, 2023]. The life cycle assessment for a biomass burial project must
 account for the entire lifecycle of the barrier. This includes material acquisition, production of barrier, transportation, and
 end-of-life.
 • Projects must ensure effective containment of CH4 and CO2 gases in the barrier. This is directly related to the permeability
 of CH4 and CO2 gases (due to decomposition) out of the barrier. The permeability of gas can be controlled via multiple factors
 such as barrier material, burial depth, temperature and site’s hydrology. For instance, some phyllosilicate clays can sorb
 gaseous CH4 and CO2, with sorption efficiency increasing at lower temperatures [Gooding, 2023].
