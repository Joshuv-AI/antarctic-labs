Video-based gait analysis for clinical monitoring of genotype-specific functional patterns in osteogenesis imperfecta
Authors: Yahao Li, Sheng Yu, Qinglin Sun, Mingxing Wang, Xiuzhi Ren, Zhenhong Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOsteogenesis imperfecta (OI) is characterized by substantial genetic and functional heterogeneity, yet low-burden objective tools for repeated gait monitoring in routine outpatient care remain limited. This study investigated whether routine smartphone videos combined with markerless pose estimation could capture genotype-associated gait patterns in children with IFITM5- and WNT1-related OI, with COL1-related OI included as a cross-sectional reference cohort.MethodsThis single-center retrospective cohort study screened 237 pediatric patients with genetically confirmed IFITM5- or WNT1-related OI between 2014 and 2024. The main analytic cohort included 71 patients: 48 with IFITM5 mutations and 23 with WNT1 mutations. During the same period, 107 patients with COL1-related OI were screened, of whom 26 were included as a cross-sectional reference cohort. MediaPipe Pose was used to extract spatiotemporal and postural gait parameters from outpatient walking videos. Algorithmic consistency under simulated video perturbations was assessed, and gait phenotypes were evaluated using multivariable models, linear mixed-effects models, Kaplan–Meier analysis, and exploratory machine-learning analysis.ResultsStep_Width and Trunk_Lean showed high algorithmic consistency under perturbation testing, with ICC ranges of 0.966–0.980 and 0.923–0.983, respectively. At baseline, WNT1 patients showed greater Step_Width (0.265 ± 0.073 vs. 0.203 ± 0.062 m), Trunk_Lean (4.53° ± 2.10° vs. 2.72° ± 1.63°), and Normalized_Step_Width (0.377 ± 0.103 vs. 0.270 ± 0.083) than IFITM5 patients (all P < 0.001). These differences remained after adjustment for age, sex, documented medication exposure, and surgical history, with adjusted differences of 0.046 m for Step_Width, 1.612° for Trunk_Lean, and 0.087 for Normalized_Step_Width. In the COL1-related reference analysis, WNT1 patients retained higher Step_Width and Normalized_Step_Width than COL1-related OI patients, whereas Trunk_Lean did not differ significantly between these groups. Longitudinal analysis showed no significant age-related gait changes in the WNT1 group, whereas IFITM5 patients showed mild annual decreases in Step_Width (− 0.0026 m/year) and Normalized_Step_Width (− 0.0046/year). Surgery-free survival declined earlier in the WNT1 group than in the IFITM5 group (log-rank P = 0.007). The exploratory genotype classification model achieved an AUC of 0.804 (95% CI, 0.678–0.914).ConclusionsRoutine outpatient smartphone videos combined with markerless pose estimation can capture genotype-associated gait patterns in pediatric OI. WNT1-related OI was characterized by a wider base of support and coronal-plane compensation, whereas IFITM5-related OI showed mild longitudinal changes in spatial gait metrics. Video-derived gait parameters may provide low-burden adjunctive measures for outpatient functional monitoring, although prospective reference-standard validation and larger multi-genotype cohorts are needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-10056-z.

Introduction

Osteogenesis imperfecta (OI), historically referred to as brittle bone disease, represents a rare group of inherited multisystem connective tissue disorders and skeletal dysplasias [1]. Epidemiological data indicate that the estimated global prevalence among live births ranges from 0.3 to 0.7 per 10,000 (approximately 1/14,000 to 1/33,000) [2]. OI exhibits marked clinical and genetic heterogeneity. Its core pathogenesis primarily involves the abnormal synthesis and processing of type I collagen (COL1), a key component of the extracellular matrix (ECM), leading to systemic clinical manifestations including low bone mass, increased bone fragility, recurrent low-trauma fractures, progressive skeletal deformities, and growth retardation [3].

With advances in molecular genetics, various non-collagen-related pathogenic genes have been identified in recent years, in addition to the classic COL1-related mutations. Among these, IFITM5 and WNT1 mutations represent two distinct molecular subtypes [4]. IFITM5 mutations are typically associated with type V OI, characterized by clinical and radiographic features such as hyperplastic callus formation following fractures and calcification of the interosseous membrane of the forearm [5]. Conversely, WNT1 mutations disrupt the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, resulting in significant osteopenia and increased bone fragility, with some patients also presenting with varying degrees of neurological manifestations [6, 7]. Given the differing mechanisms by which these two genes regulate bone formation, the resulting bone tissue architecture, biomechanical properties, and skeletal developmental patterns may also vary [8]. These molecular-level differences further influence lower limb alignment, joint load distribution, and musculoskeletal compensatory strategies, ultimately manifesting as distinct patterns of functional limitation and gait characteristics [9, 10]. In this context, gait analysis serves as an important objective method for evaluating lower limb malalignment and functional compensation [11, 12]. Through the quantitative analysis of spatiotemporal parameters and kinematics, gait assessment can reflect the integrated impact of skeletal structural abnormalities and altered neuromuscular control on motor function.

Traditional three-dimensional gait analysis (3DGA), capable of simultaneously acquiring multi-planar kinematic and kinetic information, is widely regarded as the gold standard for clinical assessment. It has been widely utilized in the functional evaluation and treatment decision-making for various pediatric orthopedic conditions [13, 14]. However, this technology typically relies on expensive optical motion capture equipment and complex reflective marker systems. Furthermore, the measurement process generally requires a specialized laboratory setting, which limits its accessibility and frequency of use in routine clinical follow-ups. For patients with rare diseases, particularly pediatric populations presenting with significant skeletal deformities or restricted mobility, implementing this assessment modality for high-frequency, long-term, and large-scale follow-ups remains challenging [15]. In recent years, the rapid advancement of deep learning-based markerless motion capture technologies has enabled the extraction of human kinematic information from standard video recordings [16]. Compared to traditional marker-based systems, these methods eliminate the need for complex equipment setups, offering greater accessibility and potential for widespread clinical translation [17]. In particular, 2D monocular video-based pose estimation algorithms have garnered increasing attention in the field of gait assessment. For instance, the MediaPipe Pose framework, developed by Google, utilizes deep learning models to automatically identify human anatomical keypoints and continuously track lower limb movements in standard 2D videos [18, 19]. Recent studies indicate that this system demonstrates low error margins in lower limb keypoint detection and joint angle estimation, exhibiting strong correlations with traditional 3D systems for measuring spatiotemporal gait parameters [20]. Further research has demonstrated [21] that, even when utilizing a single 2D camera, MediaPipe Pose achieves strong measurement agreement (intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC] = 0.85) with traditional marker-based optical motion capture systems (e.g., OptiTrack). These findings suggest that the markerless visual algorithm may have potential utility in clinical gait assessment. However, measurement accuracy remains influenced by camera view, camera-to-subject distance, calibration strategy, and pathological gait characteristics [22]. Therefore, metric gait parameters derived from monocular videos should be interpreted cautiously, particularly when concurrent validation against 3DGA or instrumented walkway systems is unavailable.

Despite the substantial potential of markerless computer vision, its application in evaluating genotype-specific functional patterns in OI remains insufficiently explored. Current clinical assessments often lack objective, high-frequency longitudinal data capable of capturing subtle biomechanical differences among genetic subtypes, particularly in children with IFITM5- and WNT1-related OI. Although markerless approaches can generate rich video-derived gait features, how these features should be integrated with conventional statistical models and exploratory machine-learning methods in real-world pediatric outpatient settings remains unclear. Against this background, the present study aimed to investigate whether routine outpatient smartphone videos combined with MediaPipe Pose could capture genotype-associated gait patterns in children with IFITM5- and WNT1-related OI, with COL1-related OI included as a cross-sectional contextual reference. The focus of this study was outpatient screening, longitudinal monitoring, and hypothesis generation, rather than the development of a criterion-validated measurement system to replace 3DGA or a prediction model ready for direct clinical deployment.

Methods

Study population

This single-center retrospective cohort study included cross-sectional, longitudinal, and contextual reference analyses. Between 2014 and 2024, 237 genetically confirmed pediatric patients with IFITM5- or WNT1-related OI were screened, including 140 with IFITM5 mutations and 97 with WNT1 mutations. After excluding patients without eligible gait videos because of loss to follow-up, unavailable recordings, poor video quality, or insufficient complete gait cycles, 71 patients were included in the main analytic cohort (48 IFITM5 and 23 WNT1). During the same period, 107 genetically confirmed patients with COL1A1/COL1A2-related OI were screened, of whom 26 had eligible baseline gait videos and were included as a cross-sectional reference cohort. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Children’s Hospital of Soochow University (approval number: 2024CS146), with waiver of informed consent because anonymized retrospective data were used.

The cross-sectional analysis used each patient’s first available gait assessment. The longitudinal analysis included 50 patients with repeated follow-up videos, comprising 266 repeated gait video assessments (37 IFITM5 patients, 213 videos; 13 WNT1 patients, 53 videos). Documented medication exposure was defined as OI-related pharmacologic treatment recorded before or at the index gait assessment, predominantly bisphosphonate therapy. Detailed dose, duration, and adherence data were not consistently available.

Video acquisition

Gait videos were recorded during routine outpatient follow-up visits. Children walked at a self-selected comfortable speed along a flat, straight walkway. Videos were captured using a tripod-mounted monocular smartphone camera, predominantly standard Android devices, with a minimum resolution of 1080p and a frame rate of at least 30 fps. When feasible, the camera was positioned approximately 3–4 m in front of the walkway, with the lens height roughly aligned with the participant’s hip level to reduce perspective distortion; the walking path was approximately 8 m long. Participants walked straight toward the end of the walkway and then turned back to the starting point. Each assessment included three consecutive round-trip walking trials. During recording, participants were instructed to walk facing the camera, with the lower limbs as unobstructed as possible. Videos were excluded if they showed substantial clothing occlusion, assistive-device obstruction, non-frontal camera views, severe defocus, inadequate exposure, or fewer than three consecutive complete gait cycles. During preprocessing, two experienced pediatric orthopedic surgeons independently screened the videos, and disagreements regarding video eligibility were resolved by consensus.

Markerless gait feature extraction

Gait keypoints were extracted using MediaPipe Pose, which detects 33 anatomical landmarks from monocular videos and provides both image coordinates and approximate world-coordinate landmarks [18, 19]. The model was run with model_complexity = 2, smooth_landmarks=True, min_detection_confidence = 0.5, and min_tracking_confidence = 0.5. Frames without valid pose landmarks or with low-confidence key lower-limb landmarks were excluded from metric calculation, and video segments with insufficient valid sequences or fewer than two identifiable gait events were discarded. Keypoint trajectories were smoothed using a second-order Butterworth low-pass filter with a 6-Hz cutoff [23]. Gait events were detected from the bilateral heel-landmark distance curve after 3-Hz smoothing, using a minimum peak distance of 0.3 s and a peak prominence of 0.05.

The extracted metrics were Cadence, Velocity, Gait_Cycle_CV, Step_Width, Step_Width_CV, Trunk_Lean, and Normalized_Step_Width [24]. Step_Width was estimated from the mediolateral distance between bilateral heel landmarks at detected gait events, Trunk_Lean from the shoulder–hip vector deviation in the coronal plane, and Velocity from approximate MediaPipe world-coordinate step length and cadence. Because external camera calibration and anthropometric measurements were unavailable, metric Step_Width and Velocity were interpreted as approximate estimates, while Normalized_Step_Width was retained as a dimensionless scale-adjusted metric.

Algorithm robustness assessment

To assess technical stability under outpatient-like video conditions, original videos were artificially perturbed using resolution downsampling, reduced illumination, Gaussian blur, and Gaussian noise. The perturbation settings were a downscale factor of 0.5, brightness factor of 0.7, Gaussian blur kernel of 5 × 5, and Gaussian noise with sigma = 15. Gait parameters were extracted from both original and perturbed videos and matched by Patient_ID and visit identifiers. ICC, SEM, and MDC were then calculated [25]. ICC was reported as ICC(2,1), a two-way random-effects, absolute-agreement, single-measure model. SEM was reported in the original unit of each metric, and MDC was calculated as 1.96 × √2 × SEM.

Machine learning analysis

Machine-learning analyses were used to explore whether video-derived gait features carried genotype-associated information within this cohort. The feature set included age, sex, and seven gait parameters: Cadence, Velocity, Gait_Cycle_CV, Step_Width, Step_Width_CV, Trunk_Lean, and Normalized_Step_Width. Given the limited sample size, model performance was evaluated using leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) [26]. Median imputation and Z-score standardization were fitted within each training fold and then applied to the held-out sample. Genotype classification models included random forest, support vector machine, XGBoost, logistic regression, gradient boosting, AdaBoost, K-nearest neighbors, and extra trees. Models used a fixed random_state = 42, with class weighting applied where appropriate. LASSO regularization for logistic regression followed established statistical-learning principles [27]. Performance was summarized using LOOCV AUC, accuracy, balanced accuracy, and bootstrap 95% CIs. SHAP analysis was performed after refitting the best-performing model on the full dataset for feature-attribution visualization, while performance estimates were based on LOOCV [28]. Classification of recorded major lower-limb surgical-intervention status was performed as a supplementary analysis, using prior or recorded surgical status in the cross-sectional dataset rather than future surgery after the index video. The corresponding DCA was therefore presented as an exploratory visualization.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software and Python. Continuous variables were first assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were compared using independent-samples t tests, whereas non-normally distributed variables were compared using Mann–Whitney U tests. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate.

Cross-sectional genotype comparisons were performed using multivariable linear models adjusted for age, sex, documented medication exposure, and prior major lower-limb surgery. Kaplan–Meier analysis was performed with birth as the time origin. The event was defined as the first recorded major lower-limb surgery, including femoral or tibial surgery on either side; patients without recorded surgery were censored at the age of the index gait video. Longitudinal trajectories were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models, with centered age as a fixed effect and Patient_ID as a random intercept to account for repeated observations within the same patient.

Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P < 0.05. A descriptive sensitivity analysis was conducted in patients without lower-limb surgery to explore the potential influence of prior surgery on gait biomechanics. Because the surgery-naive WNT1 subgroup was small (n = 3), no formal hypothesis testing was performed; continuous variables were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges or ranges (Fig. 1).

Results

Environmental robustness and algorithmic consistency

To assess the stability of the markerless video gait workflow under non-standardized clinical conditions, we performed an algorithmic consistency analysis for the core gait metrics under simulated video perturbations (Table 1). Spatial and coronal-plane postural metrics showed relatively high consistency. Step_Width demonstrated excellent consistency across both genotype cohorts (ICC: 0.966–0.980), and Trunk_Lean also showed high consistency (ICC: 0.923–0.983). Frames with low-confidence keypoint detection were automatically excluded during processing to reduce the influence of pose-estimation errors. In the COL1-related cross-sectional reference videos, perturbation testing similarly showed high algorithmic consistency for spatial and postural metrics, with ICCs of 0.946 for Step_Width, 0.988 for Trunk_Lean, and 0.943 for Normalized_Step_Width (Supplementary Table S5 and Supplementary Figure S4-8).

In contrast, temporal variability metrics were more sensitive to phase drift in monocular videos. Gait_Cycle_CV showed only moderate consistency (ICC: 0.563–0.628), which may reflect both the limitations of frame-based monocular video analysis and the intrinsic rhythm variability of children with lower-limb skeletal deformities. Overall, these findings support algorithmic consistency for selected spatial and postural metrics under perturbed outpatient video conditions, but they should not be interpreted as criterion validation against three-dimensional gait laboratories or other clinical reference standards.

Baseline gait kinematic differences between distinct genotypes

The main analytic cohort included 48 patients with IFITM5-related OI and 23 patients with WNT1-related OI. An additional 26 patients with COL1-related OI were included as a cross-sectional contextual reference cohort. Age at the index gait video, sex distribution, and documented medication exposure did not differ significantly among the three groups (Table 2). The proportion of patients with a history of major lower-limb surgery differed across groups, with higher proportions in the COL1-related and WNT1 groups than in the IFITM5 group, indicating the need to consider surgical history when interpreting gait phenotypes.

Comparison of baseline demographic and clinical characteristics

In the baseline comparison between the IFITM5 and WNT1 main analytic cohorts, no significant differences were observed in Velocity or Cadence. However, clear differences were found in spatial and postural parameters related to mediolateral stability (Fig. 2). The WNT1 group showed greater Step_Width (0.265 ± 0.073 m vs. 0.203 ± 0.062 m, P < 0.001) and greater Trunk_Lean (4.53° ± 2.10° vs. 2.72° ± 1.63°, P < 0.001) than the IFITM5 group. To account for potential scale-related measurement differences in monocular video analysis and differences in body size among children, Normalized_Step_Width was further calculated. Normalized_Step_Width was also higher in the WNT1 group than in the IFITM5 group (0.377 ± 0.103 vs. 0.270 ± 0.083, P < 0.001).

These findings suggest a genotype-associated gait pattern in WNT1-related OI characterized by a wider base of support and coronal-plane postural compensation.

Given the potential influence of prior lower-limb orthopedic surgery on gait biomechanics, we further performed a subgroup analysis in patients without a history of lower-limb surgery. This subgroup included 20 IFITM5 patients and 3 WNT1 patients. Median Velocity and Normalized_Step_Width were broadly similar between groups. The median Velocity was 0.29 m/s in both the IFITM5 group (IQR, 0.25–0.35) and the WNT1 group (range, 0.21–0.33). Median Normalized_Step_Width was 0.24 (IQR, 0.20–0.28) in IFITM5 patients and 0.23 (range, 0.21–0.42) in WNT1 patients.

However, the WNT1 subgroup showed greater dispersion in spatial variability metrics, particularly Step_Width_CV, with a higher median value than the IFITM5 group (51.49 vs. 26.81). WNT1 patients also showed higher upper-range values for Trunk_Lean and Normalized_Step_Width. For example, the maximum Trunk_Lean in the WNT1 subgroup was 8.08°, exceeding the upper quartile of the IFITM5 subgroup (3.22°), and the maximum Normalized_Step_Width was 0.42 compared with the IFITM5 upper quartile of 0.28. Given the small number of surgery-naive WNT1 patients, this analysis was descriptive, but these patterns suggest that some WNT1 patients may show more pronounced mediolateral instability and compensatory trunk motion.

Contextual COL1-related reference analysis

To assess whether the coronal-plane gait differences between IFITM5 and WNT1 reflected general OI-related impairment or a more WNT1-enriched pattern, 26 COL1-related OI patients with available gait videos were included as a cross-sectional reference cohort. In the three-group comparison, Step_Width, Trunk_Lean, and Normalized_Step_Width differed significantly among COL1-related OI, IFITM5, and WNT1 patients. After adjustment for age, sex, documented medication exposure, and first major lower-limb surgical history, WNT1 patients continued to show greater Step_Width than both COL1-related OI patients (adjusted difference, 0.056 m; 95% CI, 0.023–0.090; P = 0.001) and IFITM5 patients (adjusted difference, 0.046 m; 95% CI, 0.015–0.077; P = 0.004). Normalized_Step_Width showed a similar pattern, remaining higher in WNT1 patients than in COL1-related OI patients (adjusted difference, 0.088; 95% CI, 0.042–0.134; P < 0.001) and IFITM5 patients (adjusted difference, 0.086; 95% CI, 0.043–0.129; P < 0.001) (Supplementary Table S1-4).

Trunk_Lean showed a more nuanced pattern. WNT1 patients had greater adjusted Trunk_Lean than IFITM5 patients (adjusted difference, 1.628°; 95% CI, 0.718–2.537; P < 0.001), but the difference between WNT1 and the COL1-related reference group was not significant (adjusted difference, 0.822°; 95% CI, − 0.160 to 1.803; P = 0.100). These findings suggest that increased Normalized_Step_Width may be more characteristic of a WNT1-enriched wide-base gait pattern, whereas increased Trunk_Lean may reflect a broader coronal-plane compensatory mechanism in clinically severe OI (Table 4).

Longitudinal trajectories of gait characteristics

Longitudinal follow-up data were analyzed using genotype-stratified linear mixed-effects models (Table 5) to evaluate age-related changes in gait parameters in patients with IFITM5- and WNT1-related OI. In the WNT1 group, no significant age-related changes were observed during follow-up, and none of the gait parameters reached statistical significance in Model 2. In the IFITM5 group, mild age-related changes were observed mainly in spatial stability-related metrics. In the medication-adjusted Model 2, Step_Width decreased by 0.0026 m per year (95% CI, − 0.0049 to − 0.0003; P = 0.029), and Normalized_Step_Width decreased by 0.0046 per year (95% CI, − 0.0078 to − 0.0015; P = 0.004). Cadence and Velocity showed downward trends in the IFITM5 group but did not reach statistical significance (Cadence, P = 0.078; Velocity, P = 0.094). Thus, the longitudinal findings do not support a clear pattern of rapid functional decline in IFITM5-related OI, but are more consistent with mild, gradual spatial gait adjustment with age.

Clinical progression reflected by surgical interventions

To evaluate the potential manifestations of genotype-related gait differences during clinical progression, the Kaplan–Meier method was utilized to analyze the time to freedom from the first major lower limb surgical intervention in both patient groups.

The surgery-free survival curve declined earlier in the WNT1 group than in the IFITM5 group (log-rank P = 0.007) (Fig. 3). First major lower-limb surgery occurred in 20 of 23 WNT1 patients and 28 of 48 IFITM5 patients. Among patients with events, the median age at first surgery was 4.80 years in the WNT1 group and 5.93 years in the IFITM5 group. These findings suggest that children with WNT1-related OI underwent major lower-limb surgery earlier than those with IFITM5-related OI. However, surgical intervention is influenced by deformity severity, clinical decision-making, follow-up duration, and access to care. Therefore, it was interpreted as an indirect indicator of clinical progression and treatment burden, rather than a fully objective endpoint of natural disease history.

Machine learning analysis of gait features

Based on the clinical and gait data, exploratory machine-learning analyses were performed. Given the genetic heterogeneity of OI and the limited sample size of this cohort, these analyses were used primarily to examine whether gait features carried genotype-associated information within this dataset, rather than to develop a clinical prediction tool generalizable to all OI genotypes.

Genotype identification model

Using the extracted gait parameters and leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV), the random forest model achieved an AUC of 0.80 (95% CI, 0.68–0.91) for distinguishing IFITM5 from WNT1 genotypes (Fig. 4A). SHAP feature-attribution analysis showed that coronal-plane stability-related metrics, including Trunk_Lean, Normalized_Step_Width, and Step_Width, contributed prominently to model output (Fig. 4B). These results suggest that genotype-associated gait patterns in this cohort can be captured by video-derived gait features, but they should not be interpreted as an independent diagnostic tool.

Discussion

By integrating markerless video-based motion capture, longitudinal statistical modeling, and interpretable machine learning, this study evaluated the potential role of video gait analysis for functional phenotyping in OI under real-world clinical conditions. We compared gait biomechanical features and longitudinal trajectories between two non-collagen-related genotypes, IFITM5 and WNT1, and included a COL1-related OI cohort as a cross-sectional contextual reference. Overall, WNT1-related OI was characterized by a genotype-associated gait pattern involving a wider base of support and coronal-plane postural compensation. The aim of this study was not to develop a replacement for laboratory-based 3D gait analysis, but to explore a low-burden tool for outpatient functional monitoring and phenotype assessment.

In the baseline cross-sectional analysis, WNT1 patients showed greater Step_Width, Trunk_Lean, and Normalized_Step_Width than IFITM5 patients. These differences remained after adjustment for age, sex, and documented medication exposure, and were also retained in the surgical-history sensitivity model, with greater Step_Width (adjusted difference, 0.046 m; 95% CI, 0.012–0.081), Trunk_Lean (adjusted difference, 1.612°; 95% CI, 0.642–2.581), and Normalized_Step_Width (adjusted difference, 0.087; 95% CI, 0.039–0.134) in the WNT1 group. These findings suggest that the coronal-plane gait differences in WNT1-related OI cannot be fully explained by prior lower-limb surgery. However, because surgery may act both as a confounder and as a mediator along the pathway from genotype to skeletal deformity and gait compensation, Model 3 should be interpreted as a sensitivity analysis rather than a causal estimate after complete removal of surgical effects.

After inclusion of the COL1-related reference cohort, WNT1 patients continued to show greater Step_Width and Normalized_Step_Width than both COL1-related OI and IFITM5 patients, supporting the interpretation that the wide-base gait pattern in WNT1-related OI does not simply reflect general OI-related functional impairment. In contrast, Trunk_Lean did not differ significantly between WNT1 and COL1-related OI. This suggests that increased Normalized_Step_Width may more closely reflect a WNT1-enriched mediolateral stability compensation pattern, whereas increased Trunk_Lean may represent a broader coronal-plane compensatory mechanism associated with more severe OI phenotypes, lower-limb malalignment, or compensatory trunk-control strategies.

From a biomechanical perspective, an increased Step_Width and pronounced Trunk_Lean are widely recognized as compensatory strategies for maintaining lateral stability. When structural stability is compromised, expanding the base of support serves to mitigate the risk of lateral falls during ambulation [29, 30]. Recent multi-omics and large-scale clinical cohort studies have further elucidated that the WNT1 gene is a master regulator of bone formation. Its loss of function leads not only to severe bone fragility but is frequently accompanied by muscle weakness, ligamentous laxity, and varying degrees of neurological abnormalities, such as brainstem hypoplasia or cognitive-motor delay [6, 31]. Therefore, the wide-base gait pattern observed in WNT1 patients may reflect the combined effects of skeletal structural instability, lower-limb deformity burden, and altered neuromuscular control. The descriptive surgery-naive subgroup analysis also showed higher upper-range values of spatial variability and trunk lean in a small number of WNT1 patients; however, because only three surgery-naive WNT1 patients were available, this finding should be regarded as a mechanistic clue rather than evidence of a directly genotype-driven gait abnormality.

The longitudinal analysis further refined the interpretation of genotype-associated functional trajectories. No significant age-related gait changes were observed in the WNT1 group during follow-up, whereas IFITM5 patients showed mild age-related decreases in Step_Width and Normalized_Step_Width. Cadence and Velocity showed non-significant downward trends. Thus, IFITM5-related OI should not be described as showing rapid functional decline, but rather as showing mild and gradual spatial gait adjustment with age. Features of type V OI, including hyperplastic callus formation, interosseous membrane calcification, and restricted joint motion, may plausibly contribute to long-term functional adaptation, but this remains a hypothesis requiring future assessment of arm swing, joint range of motion, muscle strength, and upper-limb involvement. In this context, repeated video-based gait assessment may provide a practical way to monitor subtle functional changes during outpatient follow-up, complementing conventional clinical assessment rather than replacing laboratory-based gait analysis (Fig. 5).

Kaplan–Meier analysis showed that surgery-free survival declined earlier in the WNT1 group than in the IFITM5 group (log-rank P = 0.007). First major lower-limb surgery occurred in 20 of 23 WNT1 patients and 28 of 48 IFITM5 patients. This finding is consistent with the wider base of support and coronal-plane compensation observed at baseline in WNT1 patients, suggesting that children with WNT1-related OI may develop structural problems requiring surgical intervention earlier. However, first lower-limb surgery is not a pure natural-history endpoint; it is influenced by deformity severity, clinical decision-making, follow-up intensity, family preference, and access to care. It should therefore be interpreted as an indirect indicator of clinical progression and treatment burden rather than a fully objective endpoint of disease natural history.

The present findings support the feasibility of video-based markerless gait analysis in outpatient settings, while also defining its evidential boundaries. The perturbation analysis showed good algorithmic consistency for selected spatial and postural metrics, particularly Step_Width, Normalized_Step_Width, and Trunk_Lean. Although this analysis does not constitute criterion validation against 3D motion capture, force plates, instrumented walkways, or standardized clinical mobility scales, the high ICC values under perturbed video conditions support the technical stability of the workflow [22, 32, 33]. Accordingly, video-derived gait parameters in this study should be regarded as interpretable proxies for outpatient screening and longitudinal monitoring, rather than fully validated biomechanical endpoints. Future prospective studies should include concurrent 3DGA, instrumented walkway, or clinical mobility-scale assessments to determine criterion validity, minimal clinically important differences, and generalizability across acquisition settings.

Compared with traditional univariate analyses, machine-learning models can integrate complex nonlinear relationships among gait features and have increasingly been applied to automated gait analysis and clinical outcome modeling [34, 35]. However, TRIPOD + AI and related reporting guidance emphasize the need for transparent training procedures, cross-validation, leakage control, and external validation, especially in small, rare-disease, and genetically heterogeneous cohorts [36]. In this study, machine learning was therefore treated as exploratory. The genotype classification model achieved an AUC of 0.804 within this cohort, and SHAP analysis identified Trunk_Lean, Normalized_Step_Width, and Step_Width as important contributors, consistent with the conventional statistical results. These findings indicate that video-derived gait features carry genotype-associated information, but they should not be interpreted as an independent diagnostic model for the broader OI population [37].

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective outpatient-video study without concurrent 3D motion capture, instrumented walkway, force-plate, or standardized clinical mobility-scale data; therefore, criterion validity could not be established. Second, although 26 COL1-related OI patients were included as a cross-sectional reference cohort, this group does not represent the full COL1-related OI spectrum, was not included in longitudinal analyses, and was too small for stable COL1A1 versus COL1A2 subgroup comparisons. Third, height, weight, BMI, nutritional status, muscle mass, joint range of motion, quantitative muscle strength, fracture burden, and radiographic deformity severity were unavailable; Normalized_Step_Width partly addressed body-scale differences but could not replace full anthropometric adjustment. Fourth, upper-limb swing, joint range of motion, interosseous membrane calcification severity, and radial head dislocation were not measured, so the direct effect of IFITM5-related upper-limb involvement on gait could not be inferred; arm swing itself may influence walking mechanics [38]. Fifth, first major lower-limb surgery is a complex clinical-intervention endpoint influenced by clinical decisions, family preference, follow-up intensity, and access to care, rather than a pure natural-history endpoint. Finally, the machine-learning models were developed in a small, single-center rare-disease cohort and remain at risk of overfitting and limited generalizability; external validation in larger, multicenter, multi-genotype OI cohorts is needed.

Conclusions

In conclusion, using routine outpatient smartphone videos and markerless pose estimation, this study identified distinct gait patterns between children with IFITM5- and WNT1-related OI, with COL1-related OI included as a contextual reference. WNT1-related OI was characterized by a wider base of support and greater coronal-plane compensation, whereas IFITM5-related OI showed mild longitudinal changes in spatial gait metrics. These findings suggest that video-derived gait analysis may provide a practical, low-burden approach for monitoring functional gait patterns in pediatric OI during routine follow-up. Further prospective validation against reference-standard gait assessments and larger multi-genotype cohorts will be needed to establish its broader clinical applicability.
