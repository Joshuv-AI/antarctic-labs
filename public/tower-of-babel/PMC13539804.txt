Effect of nurse-led individualized noise nursing intervention on sleep comfort and pain relief in terminally ill patients undergoing palliative care
Authors: Songchao Wang, Yi Zhang, Leiting Tang, Xia Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSleep disturbance and pain are common sources of distress in terminally ill patients receiving palliative care, and nighttime ward noise is a modifiable environmental factor that may aggravate both symptoms. This study evaluated the effect of a nurse-led, individualized noise nursing intervention on sleep comfort and pain relief in terminally ill patients receiving palliative care, with the aim of providing practical evidence for comfort-oriented nursing care.MethodsA retrospective cohort analysis was conducted using existing clinical and nursing records of 163 terminally ill patients admitted to the palliative care ward of our hospital from March 2024 to August 2025. Patients were divided into an observation group (n = 89) and a control group (n = 74) according to the nursing methods received. The control group received routine palliative care combined with standardized noise nursing, while the observation group received nurse-led individualized noise nursing in addition to the routine care. The average intervention period was 4 weeks for both groups. Indicators related to sleep comfort, pain status, and noise interference were compared between the two groups.ResultsAfter the intervention, the observation group showed greater improvements in sleep comfort, pain-related indicators, and noise interference indices compared to the control group. The observation group also had higher scores for nursing compliance and family satisfaction, along with better quality of life and a lower incidence of adverse events. All between-group differences were statistically significant (P < 0.05).ConclusionAmong terminally ill patients receiving palliative care, a nurse-led, individualized noise nursing program is associated with improved sleep comfort and better pain-related outcomes. During the intervention period, patients show better adherence to nursing measures, and family satisfaction increases.

Background

As the population ages and the burden of cancer increases, more patients are living with advanced disease and require inpatient palliative care as part of routine clinical practice [1]. Cancer center statistics show that patients with middle- and advanced-stage disease account for 68.7% of all cases [2]. At the terminal stage, standardized palliative care aims to maintain comfort and basic daily functioning [3]. However, symptom control remains challenging, particularly at night. Surveys report insomnia in 65.1% of terminally ill patients with cancer, and nearly 70% experience moderate to severe pain [4].

Ward noise is a modifiable environmental factor that may exacerbate both sleep disturbance and pain. Reported sound levels in hospital studies are generally expressed as A-weighted decibel values obtained from ward-based environmental monitoring, rather than from patient self-report alone. In palliative wards, average nighttime sound levels have been reported at approximately 56 dB, exceeding the nighttime noise level recommended by DB4404/T 35-2022 [5]. Although such standards are not designed exclusively for palliative care wards, they provide a practical benchmark for judging whether the ward acoustic environment may interfere with rest. Noise-related awakenings can fragment sleep, and sustained arousal may amplify stress responses and pain sensitivity, reinforcing the bidirectional relationship between poor sleep and pain [6]. Daytime noise and stimulation may also influence nighttime symptoms. Increased daytime visitors, examinations, provider conversations, and environmental stimulation may lead to fatigue, emotional arousal, or altered sleep pressure, thereby affecting subsequent insomnia and pain perception. In addition, noise profiles may differ across clinical units. Emergency departments and intensive care units may have more acute alarm- and procedure-related noise, whereas oncology and palliative care wards often have more diffuse noise from staff movement, family caregiving, bedside communication, and routine nighttime care. In the study region, patients requiring inpatient palliative care are commonly cared for in dedicated palliative care wards, oncology-related wards, or community health-service palliative units. Accordingly, ward noise remains a persistent and context-dependent barrier to improving palliative care quality [7].

In routine practice, noise-related nursing measures are often delivered uniformly (e.g., broad distribution of earplugs and general ward-wide noise limits). Although simple to implement, such measures may not reflect inter-individual differences in noise tolerance, baseline sleep patterns, or vulnerability related to disease severity [8]. Moreover, noise management is sometimes addressed primarily through physician-nurse coordination rather than through nurse-led bedside assessment and timely adjustment during nocturnal symptom fluctuations [9]. Existing reports suggest that standardized noise interventions improve sleep comfort in fewer than 40% of patients, indicating limited benefit for a substantial proportion [10].

Therefore, this retrospective cohort study evaluated a nurse-led, individualized noise nursing strategy in terminally ill patients. Nurses performed bedside assessments, implemented tailored measures, and adjusted the plan according to ongoing observation, incorporating age, disease severity, individual noise tolerance, habitual sleep patterns, and documented noise-related disturbance. By comparing this approach with standardized noise nursing, the study was expected to add practical evidence on whether individualized nurse-led noise management could complement routine palliative care and support more feasible comfort-oriented nursing strategies in palliative wards.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

This retrospective cohort study included terminally ill patients admitted to the palliative care ward of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Jiaxing University from March 2024 to August 2025. During the study period, the nurse-led individualized noise nursing program had been implemented as a routine ward-based nursing practice for selected patients who required more tailored noise management, rather than as a prospectively assigned research intervention. The present study was therefore conducted as a post hoc review of existing clinical and nursing records. Patients were strictly screened according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, and 163 eligible patients were ultimately included. The retrospective review of anonymized records was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Jiaxing University (Ethics Approval Number: No. 2026-065), and the requirement for study-specific informed consent was waived.

Clinical setting

The study was conducted in an 18-bed dedicated palliative care ward. The ward consisted of 4 single-bed rooms, 5 two-bed rooms, and 1 four-bed room, arranged around a central nursing station. The ward mainly admitted patients requiring inpatient palliative care, including patients with advanced malignant tumors and end-stage chronic organ failure. During the study period, the ward had 14 registered nurses, including 6 nurses with specialist palliative care training. Nursing care was provided using a three-shift model. The average nurse-to-patient ratio was approximately 1:4 during daytime shifts, 1:6 during evening shifts, and 1:9 during night shifts. Common nighttime noise sources included staff communication, nursing carts, call bells, door opening and closing, medical equipment alarms, family caregiver activity, and necessary nighttime nursing procedures. No major changes in ward layout, bed number, or staffing pattern occurred during the study period.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: A confirmed clinical diagnosis of terminal disease, including advanced malignant tumors such as lung cancer, gastric cancer, and colorectal cancer, as well as end-stage chronic heart failure and end-stage chronic respiratory failure; expected survival time ≤ 6 months; age ≥ 18 years, clear consciousness, and ability to cooperate with clinical assessments and nursing-related documentation; presence of sleep disorders of varying degrees [Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) score ≥ 7] and pain symptoms [Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) score ≥ 3]; complete and retrievable medical records, including baseline information, diagnosis and treatment records, nursing intervention records, and relevant assessment data; patients and/or their family members had provided routine clinical consent for palliative care at admission; study-specific informed consent for this retrospective review was waived by the ethics committee.

Exclusion criteria: Severe psychiatric disorders or cognitive impairment [Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score < 24] and inability to cooperate with nursing interventions and outcome evaluation; severe failure of major organs such as the heart, liver, and kidney, with extremely unstable condition and incomplete diagnosis, treatment, and nursing records; hearing impairment (pure tone audiometry threshold > 50 dB) or reduced ability to perceive noise intervention; transfer to another hospital, voluntary discharge during hospitalization, or death during the observation period, resulting in inability to complete follow-up assessments; other severe conditions that may substantially affect sleep and pain, such as severe skin ulcers and severe anemia.

Grouping

Patients were not randomized. They were classified according to the type of noise nursing documented in their clinical and nursing records. Patients who received routine palliative care combined with standardized noise nursing were included in the control group (n = 74), whereas those who received nurse-led individualized noise nursing in addition to routine care were included in the observation group (n = 89).

Protection of participants and potential bias

Because this was a retrospective review of existing clinical and nursing records, patients were not recruited specifically for the present study, and no additional procedures, questionnaires, or interventions were performed for research purposes. Nurse managers and ward staff were not involved in recruiting participants for this study, and they did not influence study participation. Group classification was based only on the type of noise nursing already documented in the records. The investigators who extracted and analyzed the data had no research-specific contact with the included patients or their family members during data extraction. Any clinical contact that had occurred during routine care was part of standard palliative nursing practice and was not related to study recruitment. To reduce potential bias during data handling, data were extracted from existing records using predefined criteria, and personal identifiers were removed before analysis. The terminal diagnosis of the included patients was recognized as a source of vulnerability. During routine care, both groups continued to receive standard palliative care, including symptom monitoring, pain nursing, nutritional support, and psychological counseling when needed. Patients in the control group were not deprived of routine noise management or psychosocial support. If anxiety, irritability, sleep-related worry, or family concern was recorded during hospitalization, nurses provided explanation, reassurance, and referral to the attending medical team when appropriate.

Nursing methods

The control group received routine palliative care and standardized noise nursing in accordance with the palliative care nursing specifications of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Jiaxing University. Routine palliative care mainly included condition monitoring, standardized pain nursing, nutritional support, and regular psychological counseling. Standardized noise nursing mainly included controlling conversation volume in wards, rationally arranging the timing of nighttime diagnosis and treatment procedures, providing patients with earplugs of uniform specifications and guiding their use, regular maintenance of medical equipment to reduce operational noise, and posting quiet reminder signs to encourage cooperation. The intervention cycle covered the whole course of palliative care hospitalization, with an average duration of 4 weeks. Nursing records and relevant assessment data were retrospectively extracted for analysis.

In addition to the above routine care, the observation group received nurse-led individualized noise nursing, with the same average intervention cycle of 4 weeks. In this study, nurse-led individualized noise nursing was defined as a structured bedside nursing process led by specialist palliative care nurses. It differed from standardized noise nursing in that the plan was developed for each patient after assessment of the main noise triggers, usual sleep period, pain fluctuation pattern, psychological state, tolerance of earplugs or other noise-reduction measures, family visiting pattern, and documented noise-related awakenings. 8 specialist palliative care nurses participated in the implementation of individualized noise nursing. Before implementation, they completed a 2-hour ward-based training session covering the assessment of nighttime noise disturbance, use of the individualized nursing assessment form, selection of noise-reduction measures, communication with patients and families, and documentation requirements. Competency was confirmed by the head nurse through case-based discussion and review of the first three completed nursing plans for each nurse. Individualized assessments were performed using a unified nursing assessment form. Based on the assessment results, nurses selected measures from a predefined set of options, including adjustment of non-urgent nighttime care time, clustering of bedside nursing procedures, individualized earplug use or discontinuation, reduction of bedside conversation and mobile-phone sounds, targeted reminders to family members, adjustment of door or curtain use when appropriate, and communication with staff regarding equipment alarms and nursing-cart noise. The final nursing plan was determined by the responsible nurse in discussion with the patient, family caregiver, and attending medical team when needed. The workflow of the individualized noise nursing protocol consisted of five steps: (1) initial bedside assessment of sleep pattern, pain fluctuation, noise triggers, psychological status, and tolerance of noise-reduction measures; (2) classification of the main disturbance source as procedure-related noise, environmental equipment noise, staff- or family-related noise, or patient-specific sensitivity; (3) selection of individualized measures from the predefined intervention options; (4) daily documentation of implementation, patient response, and noise-related sleep interruption; and (5) weekly reassessment and plan modification according to symptom changes, comfort, and acceptance of the intervention. Individualized education mainly covered the reason for nighttime noise control, proper use and timely removal of earplugs, keeping mobile phones and bedside conversations quiet at night, informing nurses when a specific sound triggered awakening or pain aggravation, and maintaining a stable bedtime routine. Nurses carried out weekly reassessments and modified the nursing plan according to the patient’s response. For example, if a patient reported earplug discomfort, nurses replaced the earplugs with a softer model, shortened the wearing time, or used environmental noise reduction instead. If awakenings were mainly related to nighttime nursing procedures, non-urgent procedures were clustered and scheduled away from the patient’s most vulnerable sleep period. If anxiety or irritability increased at night, nurses strengthened explanation, reassurance, and family guidance while avoiding unnecessary repeated questioning. Nursing and monitoring records were completed daily to support outcome evaluation.

Observation indicators

Outcomes were retrospectively extracted for both groups before intervention (within 24 h after admission) and after intervention (4 weeks after intervention or at discharge). All indicators were collected and recorded by trained specialist nurses in accordance with standardized procedures. The instruments were selected because they corresponded to the main symptom domains and care outcomes relevant to palliative nursing. The PSQI was used to assess overall sleep quality, while sleep latency, nighttime awakenings, total sleep duration, sleep satisfaction, and ESS score provided complementary information on nocturnal sleep and daytime sleepiness. The VAS was selected as a simple and clinically familiar tool for pain intensity assessment. Pain attack frequency and duration were included to reflect the temporal burden of pain. SAS and SDS were used because anxiety and depression may influence both pain perception and sleep disturbance in terminally ill patients. Nursing compliance and family satisfaction were included to evaluate the acceptability of the nursing measures, and SF-36 was used to describe overall quality of life. These instruments have been widely used in clinical and nursing research and have shown acceptable reliability and validity in previous studies or established clinical applications [11–16]. In the present study, all assessments were performed by trained nurses using consistent scoring instructions to reduce inter-assessor variation. The indicators included:

Statistical methods

No financial or material incentives were provided to patients or family members, because this was a retrospective review of existing clinical and nursing records. No a priori power analysis was performed; the sample size was determined by the number of eligible patients with complete records during the study period. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA; https://www.ibm.com/products/spss-statistics), was used for data processing and analysis. Data were entered independently by two personnel and cross-checked to reduce input errors. Only patients with complete baseline information, nursing intervention records, and outcome assessment data were included. Therefore, the analysis was performed as a complete-case analysis, and no statistical imputation was conducted for missing data. Measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (‾χ ± s). Paired t-tests were used for within-group comparisons before and after intervention, and independent-samples t-tests were used for between-group comparisons. Count data were expressed as number (percentage) [n (%)], and chi-square tests were used for between-group comparisons. Ordinal data, such as nursing compliance, were analyzed using rank-sum tests. Multivariable regression analysis was not performed because key potential confounders, including detailed disease severity, analgesic and sedative medication use, and functional status, were not consistently available in the retrospective records. Therefore, the statistical results were interpreted as unadjusted comparisons. All tests were two-sided. A P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, consistent with conventional biomedical statistical reporting.

Comparison of sleep comfort-related indicators

Before intervention, there were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in PSQI score (P = 0.682), sleep latency (P = 0.731), number of nighttime awakenings (P = 0.884), total sleep duration (P = 0.793), sleep satisfaction score (P = 0.766), or ESS score (P = 0.912). After 4 weeks of intervention, all sleep comfort indicators improved significantly in both groups compared with baseline (P < 0.05). The improvement was greater in the observation group than in the control group (P < 0.05), suggesting a better effect on sleep comfort with nurse-led individualized noise nursing (Fig. 1).

Comparison of pain relief-related indicators

At baseline, there were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in VAS score (P = 0.845), pain attack frequency (P = 0.793), pain duration (P = 0.688), SAS score (P = 0.574), or SDS score (P = 0.681). After the intervention, these outcomes improved in both groups (P < 0.05). The observation group showed larger reductions in VAS score, pain attack frequency, and pain duration than the control group, and also had greater decreases in anxiety and depression scores (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2).

Comparison of nighttime noise interference-related indicators

Before intervention, nighttime noise interference-related indicators did not differ significantly between the two groups, including nighttime noise exposure duration (P = 0.928), number of sleep interruptions caused by noise (P = 0.804), noise tolerance threshold (P = 0.761), and noise-related irritability score (P = 0.842). After 4 weeks, both groups showed improvement from baseline, with a larger change in the observation group than in the control group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3). This pattern supports a stronger reduction in nighttime noise disturbance and related discomfort with individualized noise nursing.

Safety comparison

Adverse events occurred in both groups. Reported events included earplug-related discomfort, pain worsening in relation to nighttime noise, and increased sleep interruption. The adverse event rate was lower in the observation group than in the control group (P < 0.05) (Table 2).

Comparison of other related indicators between the two groups

Nursing compliance was higher in the observation group than in the control group, mainly reflected by a higher proportion of complete compliance (P = 0.030) and a lower proportion of non-compliance (P = 0.017), whereas partial compliance alone did not differ significantly between groups (P = 0.368). Family satisfaction was higher in the observation group (P = 0.001). Baseline SF-36 scores did not differ between groups (P = 0.841), while post-intervention SF-36 scores were higher in the observation group than in the control group (P < 0.001) (Table 3).

Discussion

In palliative wards, care is organized around comfort. For many patients, the night is often when comfort is most compromised [17]. Sleep disruption and pain are central to that problem, and they rarely behave as independent symptoms. When sleep is fragmented, patients often become less tolerant of discomfort; when pain persists, falling asleep and returning to sleep after waking both become harder [18]. In this retrospective analysis, a nurse-led individualized noise nursing program, delivered through bedside assessment and small, repeated adjustments, was associated with better sleep comfort than routine care. The direction of change was consistent across pain-related and emotional outcomes, and indices of nighttime noise interference also shifted in the expected direction.

What matters in a palliative ward is often not one dramatic noise event but the accumulation of brief interruptions. Patients may be awakened repeatedly and then struggle to resettle [19]. Many of the sources are embedded in normal work: alarms, staff movement, and necessary nighttime procedures [20]. These cannot be “removed” in the same way a single environmental hazard might be removed. This is one reason uniform ward-wide rules have limited reach. They are simple, but they assume similar sensitivity and similar sleep timing across patients [21]. The individualized approach used here began with bedside identification of which sounds tend to wake a given patient and when that vulnerability usually occurs, and then matched nursing measures to that pattern. That logic is clinically intuitive and fits the day-to-day reality of a palliative ward.

The observation group showed a more favorable change in pain outcomes, with anxiety and depression scores moving in the same direction. A plausible explanation is that nighttime noise can sustain arousal and reinforce stress responses, which may increase pain sensitivity; reducing stimulation may therefore make symptom control easier in this setting [22, 23]. Pain and mood also interact closely in terminal illness. Anxiety and irritability can heighten pain perception, and persistent pain can in turn worsen anxiety and depressive symptoms [24]. In this program, noise-related measures were not treated as a purely technical task. Nurses also maintained explanation and communication with patients and families. This tends to matter most at night, when uncertainty and irritation rise easily. Clear expectations about routines, together with calmer bedside interaction, may change symptom experience even when analgesic regimens are not altered. In that sense, the program is consistent with palliative pain nursing practice that addresses physical, psychological, and environmental factors together [25].

A notable feature of the present analysis is that it tracked noise interference rather than inferring it indirectly. In some reports, sleep or pain improves, but it remains unclear whether nighttime exposure or noise-related interruption actually changed [26]. Here, indices related to nighttime disturbance, including nighttime noise exposure duration and the frequency of noise-related sleep interruptions, decreased after intervention, and the noise tolerance threshold increased in the observation group. These shifts do not establish causality, given the retrospective design, but they do support internal consistency: noise-related disturbance and symptom outcomes moved in the same direction [27]. This makes the interpretation more straightforward than symptom-only descriptions.

The program was also associated with higher nursing compliance and higher family satisfaction, as well as a lower incidence of adverse events. In palliative care, adherence is often fragile. Patients are exhausted and easily overwhelmed, and additional steps can feel burdensome [28]. A nurse-led approach keeps decisions close to the bedside and allows rapid adjustment when discomfort or resistance appears, changing technique, timing, or the specific measure rather than insisting on a uniform protocol. This flexibility may partly explain better acceptance of measures such as earplugs and fewer discomfort-related events. It may also influence how families judge nursing quality, which in palliative wards is often shaped by responsiveness and calm problem-solving [29]. The clinical relevance of these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Because this retrospective study did not prespecify minimal clinically important differences for PSQI, VAS, SAS, SDS, SF-36, or the noise-related indicators, statistical significance alone cannot confirm that every observed difference was clinically meaningful. Nevertheless, in palliative care, even modest improvements may be valuable when they are aligned with the central goals of comfort, reduced distress, and better tolerance of care. The concurrent improvement in sleep comfort, pain-related outcomes, noise interference, nursing compliance, family satisfaction, and adverse events suggests that the intervention may have practical value beyond isolated score changes. From a bedside perspective, fewer noise-related awakenings, better acceptance of earplug or environmental measures, reduced nighttime irritability, and improved family satisfaction may translate into more stable night care and a less burdensome care experience for terminally ill patients.

Several limitations should be noted. First, the retrospective design increases the risk of selection bias and limits control of confounding. Because patients were classified according to the nursing methods documented in existing records rather than randomized prospectively, unmeasured clinical and environmental factors may have influenced the observed differences. In addition, multivariable adjustment was not performed because several potentially important confounders, such as detailed disease severity, analgesic and sedative medication use, baseline functional status, and changes in symptom burden during hospitalization, were not consistently available in the retrospective records. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as exploratory unadjusted associations rather than evidence of an independent causal effect. Second, no a priori power analysis was performed, and the sample size was determined by the number of eligible patients with complete records during the study period. Therefore, the study may have been insufficiently powered for some secondary outcomes, while statistically significant findings should still be interpreted in relation to clinical relevance. In addition, minimal clinically important differences were not prespecified for the outcome measures, which limits the ability to determine whether each statistically significant change reached a clinically meaningful threshold. Third, this was a single-center study, and differences in ward layout, bed configuration, staffing patterns, and typical noise sources may affect generalizability. The feasibility and consistency of a nurse-led individualized noise nursing program may vary across palliative care settings with different staffing intensity and nighttime care routines. Fourth, daytime noise and stimulation were not systematically measured. Increased daytime visitors, evaluations, provider conversations, and environmental stimulation may have influenced nighttime sleep, fatigue, pain perception, and emotional status. Finally, several outcomes relied on patient-reported or nurse-recorded assessments. In terminally ill patients, fatigue, symptom burden, emotional distress, family assistance, and the desire to maintain a positive relationship with caregivers may influence questionnaire responses. Outcomes were assessed only during hospitalization, and longer-term effects were not evaluated. Prospective multicenter studies with predefined sample-size estimation, more complete noise monitoring across different time periods, and longer follow-up would help confirm whether the same pattern holds across settings.

Conclusion

Among terminally ill inpatients receiving palliative care, a nurse-led individualized noise nursing program was associated with improved sleep comfort and more favorable pain-related and emotional outcomes during hospitalization, accompanied by reduced nighttime noise interference. The program was also better accepted in practice, with higher nursing compliance and family satisfaction and fewer adverse events. These findings suggest that individualized noise nursing, when led by nurses, may enhance symptom control and care quality in palliative settings.
